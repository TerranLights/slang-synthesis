| lánnyal | with a girl | noun + instrumental-comitative case suffix | core | — | contemporary (source published 2009, 2nd ed.) | — | — | grammar_reference | n/a | Illustrates long **ny** (written **nny**), and the **-val/-vel** suffix's consonant assimilating to the stem. See breakdown below. |

> **szeretem / szerettem** = szeret- (love/like, verb root) + [Ø / -t-] (present / past tense) +
> -em (1sg definite object agreement) — the past-tense **-t-** surfaces only as consonant *length*
> here because it fuses with the following person suffix's onset, which is why Rounds singles this
> pair out: a single doubled letter is the entire difference between present and past meaning.

> **lánnyal** = lány (girl) + -val (instrumental/comitative case, "with") — the suffix's initial
> **v** regressively assimilates to the stem-final consonant (**ny**) and lengthens it, surfacing
> in spelling as **-nyal** with a doubled **n**. Same general assimilation family as the -ja
> examples in 2.2.1 below, just triggered by a different suffix.

> **adja** = ad- (give, verb root) + -ja (3sg present definite object-agreement suffix) — surface
> **d**+**j** is pronounced long [ɟː], not written differently from a normal **-ja**.
>
> **hagyja** = hagy- (leave, verb root) + -ja (3sg definite) — **gy**+**j** → long [ɟː].
>
> **mutatja** = mutat- (show, verb root) + -ja (3sg definite) — **t**+**j** → long [cː].
>
> **bátyja** = báty (older brother, kinship noun, normally used possessed) + -ja (3sg possessive,
> "his/her") — **ty**+**j** → long [cː].
>
> **kívánjuk** = kíván- (wish, verb root) + -juk (1pl definite object-agreement suffix, "we ... it")
> — **n**+**j** → long [ɲː].
>
> **anyja** = anya (mother; stem alternates to **any-** before a vowel-initial suffix, dropping the
> final -a) + -ja (3sg possessive) — **ny**+**j** → long [ɲː].
>
> **tanulja** = tanul- (study, verb root) + -ja (3sg definite) — **l**+**j** → long **jj**
> (deviates from the palatalization pattern above; Rounds flags this explicitly, p. 5).
>
> **énekeljünk** = énekel- (sing, verb root) + -jünk (1pl imperative/cohortative suffix, "let's
> ...") — same **l**+**j** → long **jj** assimilation as **tanulja**.

> stem-final **f** voices to [v].
>

> **d** devoices to [t] before the suffix's unvoiced **t**.
>
> **dolgoztok** = dolgoz- (work, verb root) + -tok (2pl present, back-vowel variant) — [dolgostok],
> stem-final **z** devoices to [s].

> loanword's vowels (**i**) are neutral, which default to front assonance (see Ch. 3.3 below).
>
> **megszeret** = meg- (perfective/inchoative verbal prefix) + szeret- (like/love, verb root) —
> devoicing example (**g**→[k] before **sz**), also a plain instance of Hungarian's productive
> verbal-prefix system layering aspectual meaning onto a bare root.

## Grammar points

### The Hungarian alphabet (Chapter 1, p. 3)

Hungarian uses the Roman alphabet plus diacritics marking vowel length. Some consonants are
digraphs (two letters functioning as one alphabet letter, e.g. **cs**, **dzs**'s components **dz**
and **zs**, **gy**, **ly**, **ny**, **sz**, **ty**, **zs**), and **dzs** itself is a trigraph — but
each is treated as a single letter of the alphabet regardless of how many characters it takes to
write. Full inventory: `a á b c cs d dz dzs e é f g gy h i í j k l ly m n ny o ó ö ő p (q) r s sz t
ty u ú ü ű v (w) (x) (y) z zs`. The letters **q**, **w**, **x** appear only in foreign spellings and
a few names; **y** appears only in old spellings (pronounced like **i**) and inside the digraphs
above.

### Consonants pronounced differently from English (2.1, p. 4-5)

All Hungarian consonants are pronounced (no silent letters). Rounds lists the consonants whose
pronunciation diverges from English orthographic expectation: **c** [ts], **cs** [tʃ], **dzs**
[dʒ], **g** [g] (always "hard"), **j** [j], **r** (trilled), **s** [ʃ], **sz** [s], **zs** [ʒ] — note
the **s**/**sz** pairing is the reverse of what an English speaker would guess from the letters.

### The palatal series (2.1.2, p. 5)

Four consonants are palatalized (pronounced with the tongue gliding off the roof of the mouth):
**gy** [ɟ], **ly** [j] (identical in sound to plain **j** — a spelling-only distinction that
survives from an earlier stage of the language), **ny** [ɲ], **ty** [c].

### Consonant length (2.2, p. 5)

Every consonant can be short or long; length is phonemic (distinguishes meaning) and is written by
doubling the letter — for a digraph, only the *first* letter of the digraph is doubled (long **sz**
is written **ssz**, long **ny** is written **nny**). Rounds's own minimal pair: **szeretem** "I love
him/her" vs. **szerettem** "I loved him/her" — length alone carries the present/past distinction.

### Lengthening of consonants before *j* (2.2.1, p. 5)

The consonants **d, gy, t, ty, n, ny** are pronounced long when they precede a suffix or clitic
beginning with **j** — even though the spelling doesn't change to show it (e.g. **ad+ja** →
*written* **adja**, *pronounced* with a long palatal stop). The consonant **l** behaves similarly
but with one deviation: in an **lj** cluster, the **l** fully assimilates to **j**, giving a long
**jj** sound instead of a palatalized long consonant (**tanul+ja** → **tanulja**, **énekel+junk** →
**énekeljünk**). See the worked vocabulary set above for all eight of Rounds's examples with
morpheme breakdowns.

### Voicing and devoicing of consonants (2.3, p. 6-7)

Hungarian consonants split into nine voiced/unvoiced pairs (unvoiced `p t k f sz c cs ty h` vs.
voiced `b d g v z zs dz dzs gy`) that assimilate to whichever one is adjacent across a morpheme or
word boundary:
- **2.3.1 Voicing:** an unvoiced consonant (other than **h**) becomes voiced when the *following*
  consonant is voiced (except before **j, l, ly, m, n, ny, r, v**, which don't trigger voicing).
- **2.3.2 Devoicing:** a voiced consonant (other than **j, l, ly, m, n, ny, r**) becomes unvoiced
  when the following consonant is unvoiced.

Two footnoted exceptions: **v** never voices a preceding consonant (though it can itself be
devoiced), and **h** has no voiced counterpart — it can trigger devoicing of what precedes it but
can never become voiced itself.

### Assimilation of sibilants (2.4, p. 7)

The "hissing" sibilants **sz, z** become "hushing" (**s, zs**-like) when immediately followed by a
hushing sibilant (**s, zs**), producing a long hushing consonant (**sz+s → long ss**; **z+zs → long
zzs**) — the ordinary voicing/devoicing rules from 2.3 still apply within this process where
relevant.

### Vowels (2.5, p. 7-8)

Hungarian has no diphthongs — every vowel is pronounced as a discrete, separate sound. Vowels can be
short or long (marked with an acute-type accent), and long vowels are pronounced roughly twice as
long as their short counterparts. For most vowel pairs the short/long difference is purely one of
length, but two pairs — **a ~ á** and **e ~ é** — differ in vowel *quality* as well as length, not
just duration. Full inventory with rough English equivalents: **a** [ɔ] "aw," **á** [aː] "aah,"
**e** [ɛ] (between "bat" and "bet"), **é** [eː] "ay" (without the English diphthong glide), **i**
[i] "e" as in "he," **í** [iː] longer, **o** [o] "oh," **ó** [oː] longer, **ö** [ø] (purse lips for
"oh," say "eh"), **ő** [øː] longer, **u** [u] "oo," **ú** [uː] longer, **ü** [y] (purse lips for
"oo," say "ee"), **ű** [yː] longer.

### Stress (2.6, p. 8)

The first syllable of every word is stressed. The definite/indefinite articles (**a, az**) and the
particle **is** ("too/also") are unstressed unless deliberately emphasized.

### Intonation (2.7, p. 9)

- **2.7.1** Declarative sentences have primarily descending (falling) intonation.
- **2.7.2** A question containing a question word (e.g. **milyen** "what kind of") has a higher
  pitch rise specifically on that question word.
- **2.7.3** A yes/no question has rise-fall intonation: a high rise on the *penultimate* syllable of
  the sentence, then a sharp fall on the final syllable. Rounds notes this can be the *only* signal
  distinguishing a declarative from an interrogative reading of an otherwise-identical sentence
  (**Szép idő van.** "The weather is beautiful." vs. **Szép idő van?** "Is the weather nice?"). In
  yes/no questions of fewer than three syllables, the rise instead lands on the final syllable with
  an immediate drop (**Ez az?**, **Kedves?**).

### Vowel harmony — the vowel inventory (Chapter 3 opening, p. 10)

Hungarian vowels are classified along two independent axes: **front vs. back** (tongue position)
and **rounded vs. unrounded** (lip shape). Inventory: back vowels `a á o ó u ú`; front unrounded
vowels `e é i í`; front rounded vowels `ö ő ü ű`. The governing principle: front-or-back assonance
must be maintained throughout an entire (native) word — Hungarian words, with the general exception
of recent loanwords, contain either only back vowels or only front vowels, never a genuine mix of
"true" front and back vowels in the same stem.

### 3.1 — Suffixing and back vs. front vowels

Because a stem's own front/back assonance is fixed, most suffixes come in a front/back pair of
allomorphs that "agree" with the stem — e.g. the dative case is **-nak** after back-vowel stems,
**-nek** after front-vowel stems (**lány** "girl" → **lánynak**; **nő** "woman" → **nőnek**). This
is the core mechanism: the suffix's own vowel changes shape to match the stem, not the reverse.

### 3.2 — Suffixing and rounded vs. unrounded vowels

For front-vowel stems specifically, a second distinction can matter: whether the stem's *last*
vowel is rounded or unrounded. A handful of suffixes — the allative case **-hoz/-hez/-höz** and the
plural **-ok/-ek/-ök** among them — have three allomorphs rather than two, adding a rounded front
variant. A front-rounded-final stem takes the front-rounded suffix form (**ismerős** "acquaintance"
→ **ismerőshöz**; **bűn** "crime" → **bűnök**); a front-unrounded-final stem takes the plain front
form (**füzet** "notebook" → **füzetek**; **gyerek** "child" → **gyerekhez**). Rounds notes the two
stages operate at different structural levels: front/back harmony is determined at the level of the
whole stem, while rounded/unrounded harmony is determined by the vowel(s) of the word's final
syllable(s) specifically (footnote 1, p. 11).

### 3.3 — Neutral vowels

The front unrounded vowels **i, í, e, é** are "neutral" with respect to vowel harmony — they can
co-occur with either back or front vowels in the same stem. A stem containing back vowels plus
neutral vowels is still treated as a back-vowel word for suffixing purposes (**papír** "paper," back
**á** + neutral **í**, still takes the back suffix: **papírok**). But if a stem contains *only*
neutral vowels and nothing else, it defaults to front assonance and takes front suffixes (**gyerek**
"child" → **gyerekek**, **gyerekhez**). Rounds flags this default as having "numerous exceptions,"
particularly among verbs: a stem consisting solely of the vowel **i** or **í** is usually back, not
front, unlike the noun default — illustrated with the homograph pair **szív** (noun, "heart," front
assonance) vs. **szív** (verb, "inhale," back assonance) (footnote 2, p. 11).

### 3.4 — Application of the rules of vowel harmony

The plural suffix is used as the worked demonstration because it has all three allomorphs
(**-ok/-ek/-ök**), letting a single paradigm show every branch of the rule at once. Rounds's own
five-stem table: **asztal** "table" (only back vowels) → **asztalok**; **gyerek** "child" (only
neutral/front vowels, last vowel unrounded) → **gyerekek**; **füzet** "notebook" (only front vowels,
last vowel unrounded) → **füzetek**; **ismerős** "acquaintance" (only front vowels, last vowel
rounded) → **ismerősök**; **papír** "paper" (back vowel + neutral vowel) → **papírok**. This single
five-row table is effectively the whole chapter's rule compressed into one paradigm, and it is the
mechanism that will recur under every later suffix (case, possessive, plural, verbal) covered in the
rest of the grammar — see `../00_Extraction_Checklist.md`'s morphological-typology note for how this
connects to the case-system chapter still pending extraction.

# Hungarian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: FSI Basic Course Vol. 1, Units 1-4

**Source:** Augustus A. Koski & Ilona Mihályfy, *Hungarian Basic Course* (Foreign Service
Institute, Washington D.C., 1962; Dept. of State Basic Course Series, ed. Carleton T. Hodge),
Volume 1, "FSI Hungarian Basic Course - Volume 1 - Student Text.pdf", printed pages 1-84
(PDF pages 14-97 of the scanned file; verified empirically — PDF page 15 carries the printed
footer "2", PDF page 97 carries printed footer "84" — a fixed PDF-page-minus-13 offset holds
throughout this range). Covers **Units 1-4** in full: "Halló, Itt Budapest" (Unit 1, pp. 1-19),
"A Kávéházban" (Unit 2, pp. 21-42), "Johnson Úr Vásárolni Megy" (Unit 3, pp. 43-64), and "Az
Edényboltban és a Gyógyszertárban" (Unit 4, pp. 65-84) — Basic Sentences, Notes to the Basic
Sentences, Notes on Pronunciation, and Notes on Grammar for each unit, per the source's own table
of contents (verified directly rather than assumed from page-count arithmetic).

**Coverage note:** every grammar point and every distinct vocabulary item introduced in the Basic
Sentences and Notes on Grammar of these four units. Per the coverage rule, the repetitive
Substitution/Variation/Transformation/Translation/Response Drills, Conversation Practice,
Situations, and Narrative sections are **not** transcribed sentence-by-sentence — they are drawn
on only where they introduce vocabulary or a grammar point not already captured from the Basic
Sentences/Notes on Grammar of the same unit (none were found: every drill and narrative in this
range recombines vocabulary already introduced in that unit's Basic Sentences). The Notes on
Pronunciation sections (stress, intonation, vowel length, digraphs, consonant assimilation, vowel
harmony, syllabication, liaison) are paraphrased as grammar points but their drill word-lists are
not reproduced item-by-item — only the phonological *rule* each illustrates is captured, plus a
handful of representative example words.

**Relationship to prior Hungarian `established/` files:** this is a different genre of source from
`001_chapters1-3_phonology_vowel_harmony.md`, `002_chapter5_nouns.md`, and
`003_chapter6_case_system.md` (all Carol Rounds' *Hungarian: An Essential Grammar*, a modern
reference grammar) — the FSI course is a 1962 audio-lingual dialogue/drill course with its own
independent vocabulary set and explicitly foregrounds spoken/colloquial register (see the course's
own Introduction, quoted below). Extracted in full rather than as a non-redundant supplement:
although some grammar territory overlaps (plural formation, case, vowel harmony), the FSI course's
own worked paradigms, formality-register system (`maga`/`ön` politeness distinctions, familiar
`te`/`ti`), and vocabulary are substantially independent and not covered by the prior extractions.

**Register note (source's own framing, from the Introduction, p. iv):** "This is a course in
Spoken Hungarian; the forms and patterns of the language are intended to be colloquial." This
whole course's Basic Sentences are consequently a higher-value colloquial-register source than a
typical reference grammar — see the Grammar points section below for the specific formality-tier
findings (the `maga`/`ön`/`te` politeness system) and Notes items flagged as socialist-era
period vocabulary.

## Grammar points

### Unit 1A. The Article

The definite article has two forms, `a` before a consonant-initial word and `az`
before a vowel-initial word — same rule as noted in `002_chapter5_nouns.md` §5.1.1 from Rounds'
grammar, independently confirmed here from a different source. The indefinite article `egy`
corresponds to English "a/an" when unstressed, but to "one" when emphasized. **Register note
(explicit in the source's own Notes on Grammar, p. 9):** "The tendency in colloquial speech in
general is not to use it [the indefinite article] except when attention is directed to the
singleness of an object or when it has the meaning of 'a certain.'" — a genuine colloquial-register
usage note about indefinite-article omission that goes beyond the equivalent Rounds material.

### Unit 1B. Omission of Subject Pronoun

Hungarian regularly drops the subject pronoun (`(én) jól vagyok` "I am well") because the verb
ending already marks person; English requires it. The pronoun is retained mainly for emphasis or
contrast, e.g. `Én beszélek magyarul` "It is I (not you) who speak Hungarian." p. 9.

### Unit 1C. Equational Sentences

Hungarian has no copula in present-tense equational sentences — subject and predicate noun/
adjective are simply juxtaposed with no verb (`Ez a követség` "This is the embassy"). Note `maga`
"you" patterns grammatically with third-person subjects. p. 10.

### Unit 1D. Negative Sentences

Hungarian negation has no auxiliary verb; `nem` "not" is placed directly before the verb
(`kér` "wants" → `nem kér` "doesn't want"). p. 10.

### Unit 1E. Word Order

Hungarian word order is more flexible than English's, though a plain subject-then-predicate order
occurs (as in English). The key rule: **the most emphatic element in a Hungarian sentence always
comes immediately before the predicate (verb)** — this is presented as a foundational Hungarian
information-structure principle. p. 10.

### Unit 2A. The Present Tense — and the formality system (`te`/`maga`/`ön`)

Hungarian verbs have six person/number endings. The source gives an explicit three-way politeness
system for "you," a genuinely valuable register-annotation finding for this project:

- **`te`/`ti`** (familiar singular/plural) — used "only in intimate conversation," addressing a
  family member or close friend. Requires 2nd-person verb forms (`akarsz`).
- **`maga`/`maguk`** — the everyday polite form, "the form employed in general conversation."
  Requires 3rd-person verb forms.
- **`ön`/`önök`** — explicitly marked by the source as **declining in actual use**: "although ön
  and önök also mean 'you', they are not used very much in conversation any more, except perhaps
  by the older generation... limited to official and very formal speech." Also requires 3rd-person
  verb forms.

This is an explicit three-tier formal/colloquial/intimate register system directly attested by the
source's own commentary, not inferred — flagged as a high-value colloquial-register finding for
this project (`maga` = default/neutral, `ön` = formal/dated, `te` = intimate/colloquial). p. 29-30.

Verb roots come in three vowel-harmony classes (back, front, front-rounded), illustrated with
`akar-` ("want"), `beszél-` ("speak"), `ül-` ("sit"). Note: familiar 2sg of roots ending in `s`,
`sz`, or `z` takes `-ol/-el/-öl` (`olvasol`, `nézel`, `főzöl`, `halászol`). p. 29-30.

### Unit 2B. Case

Hungarian nouns take different case suffixes depending on syntactic role, unlike English nouns
(though English pronouns show a residual case system: I/me, he/him). The **accusative** (direct
object) always ends in `-t`:
- Vowel-final nouns add `-t` directly (final `-a`/`-e` lengthen to `-á`/`-é` first).
- Nouns ending in `l, ly, n, ny, r, s, sz, z, zs` add `-t` directly, no linking vowel.
- Other consonant-final nouns need a linking vowel before `-t` (`-e-` for front-vowel nouns, `-o-`
  for back-vowel, with `-a-`/`-ö-` as less-predictable variants) — the source flags this vowel as
  unpredictable enough that later units will mark it explicitly per noun.
- Numerals also take `-t` in direct-object position, with irregular stems: `egy→egyet`, `kettő→
  kettőt`, `három→hármat`, `négy→négyet`, `öt→ötöt`, `hat→hatot`, `hét→hetet`, `nyolc→nyolcat`,
  `kilenc→kilencet`, `tíz→tizet`, `száz→százat`, `ezer→ezret`. p. 30-31.

### Unit 2C. The Negative Sentence — double negation as standard

Where English marks double negation as "sub-standard" ("I never gave him nothing"), **Hungarian
requires it as the only grammatical form**: `Budapesten nem talál semmit` "She doesn't find
anything in Budapest" (literally "not...nothing"). The source states plainly that a literal
single-negative translation would be *unacceptable* Hungarian. p. 32.

### Unit 2D. Interrogative Sentences

No auxiliary verb ("do"/"be") is used or possible in Hungarian yes/no questions; word order and
intonation alone distinguish `Maga otthon olvas?` "Do you read at home?" from the corresponding
statement. p. 32.

### Unit 2E. Hanem

Two words for "but": `de` (ordinary coordinating "but") versus `hanem` (used specifically after a
negative statement, introducing a contrasting affirmative — roughly "but rather"). p. 32.

### Unit 3A. The Concept of the Plural

Hungarian frequently uses the **singular** where English requires the plural: after numerals/
quantity words (`hat férfit láttam` "I saw six men"), for several items of identical description,
and in compound subjects joined by "and" referring to things (verb stays singular) — though this
last rule does not hold for persons. p. 51.

### Unit 3B. The Formation of the Plural of Nouns

The plural suffix is `-k`, following the same vowel-final/consonant-final and vowel-harmony
linking-vowel logic as the accusative `-t` (Unit 2B) — except that **consonant-final nouns always
require a linking vowel before plural `-k`**, unlike the accusative, which sometimes takes `-t`
directly. Proper names always pluralize with `-ék` (`Rózsa→Rózsaék`, `Fekete→Feketeék`) — the
family/associative plural, same suffix independently noted (unglossed) on `Johnsonék`/`Kovácsék`
in the vocabulary above and in `002_chapter5_nouns.md`. A full singular/plural/accusative-singular/
accusative-plural noun-class chart is given (not reproduced verbatim; see the vocabulary table's
individual pluralization notes and `002_chapter5_nouns.md`'s equivalent, independently-derived
noun-class system for the same underlying stem-behavior categories). p. 51-53.

### Unit 3C. Cardinal Numerals

Numbers 11-19 and 21-29 are formed by prefixing `tizen-`/`huszon-` to the base cardinal. From 30 up,
cardinals attach directly to the tens word with no linking element; tens 40-90 add `-van`/`-ven` to
the base cardinal, with vowel shortening (`négy→negyven`, `hét→hetven`). `kettő` (substantive,
stands alone) and `két` (attributive, always before a noun) are in complementary distribution —
not interchangeable. Compound numerals up to 2000 are written as one word; above that, the
thousand-unit is one word, hyphenated to what follows. Hungarian uses spaces (not commas) to
separate thousands in numerals of 5+ digits. p. 51-54.

### Unit 3, Notes on Pronunciation — Stress and Intonation

Hungarian words carry primary stress on the first syllable regardless of vowel length elsewhere in
the word — vowel length is never a stress cue (contrast English, where length/stress often
correlate). Sentence-level intonation is more complex: declarative sentences generally show a high
pitch descending to a low point at the end; question intonation depends on whether a question word
introduces the sentence (behaves like a declarative but with a higher initial pitch and often a
final rise) versus a non-question-word-initiated yes/no question, which has a sharp pitch rise on
the next-to-last syllable when the *subject* is the focus of the question, or a rise on the final
syllable when a short predicate noun/pronoun/adjective/verb (≤2 syllables) is the focus. An
untranslatable `az` particle is sometimes inserted between an unmodified monosyllabic subject and a
monosyllabic predicate adjective specifically to mark that the *subject* is under question-focus
(`A sör az jó?` "Is it the beer that's good?"). pp. 49-50.

### Unit 4A. Position of the Direct Object in a Sentence

In affirmative sentences, the direct object may precede or follow the verb; when it precedes, the
indefinite article is often omitted (`Képet rajzol` "He's drawing a picture" — no `egy`). In
negative sentences, the object always follows the verb and `nem` goes immediately before the verb,
with the indefinite article again omitted (`Nem írok levelet` "I'm not writing a letter"). p. 72.

### Unit 4B. Use and Agreement of Adjectives

Adjectives function as attributes (invariable, placed before the noun — except demonstratives,
which agree in number/case with their noun), predicates (agree in number with the subject:
`Ezek az ágyak alacsonyak` "These beds are low"), or substantives (standing in for an omitted noun,
inflecting for that noun's number/case: `Meleg teát akar? Nem, hideget` "Do you want hot tea? No,
cold [one]"). p. 72.

### Unit 4C. Nominative and Accusative Forms of Adjectives

Adjective accusative/plural formation mirrors noun formation (Unit 2B/3B): vowel-final adjectives
lengthen `-a/-e` to `-á/-é` before `-t`; consonant-final adjectives generally take `-a-` (back
vowels) or `-e-` (front vowels) before `-t`, occasionally `-o-`, with a few having no linking vowel
at all (`magyart`, `angolt`). p. 72-73.

### Unit 4D. Position of the Predicate Adjective in a Sentence

In an affirmative sentence with a 1st-person subject, the predicate adjective normally precedes the
verb (`Én beteg vagyok` "I'm sick"); it moves after the verb specifically as the emphasized answer
to "Who?" (`Én vagyok beteg` "I am [the one who is] sick"). In negative sentences, the predicate
adjective always follows the verb. p. 73.

### Unit 4E. Interrogative Form of an Equational Sentence

Two word orders are both grammatical for yes/no questions on an equational sentence — normal
subject-then-predicate-adjective, or inverted predicate-adjective-then-subject — with no meaning
difference, though Hungarian prefers the inverted (adjective-first) form. p. 73.

### Unit 4F. The Present Tense of *Lenni* ("To Be," "To Become")

Full paradigm given: `(én) vagyok`, `(te) vagy`, `(maga/ön) van`, `(ő) van`, `(mi) vagyunk`,
`(ti) vagytok`, `(maguk/önök) vannak`, `(ők) vannak`. Highly irregular relative to the regular verb
classes in Unit 2A. p. 74.

### Unit 4G. Hol-Itt-Ott vs. Hova-Ide-Oda

A systematic locative/directional distinction: `hol/itt/ott` ("where/here/there") mark **static
location** (at/in a place — a verb *not* indicating motion), while `hova/ide/oda` ("whither/hither/
thither") mark **motion toward** a place (a verb of motion). This is presented as a clean two-way
paradigm the student must track per-verb, not lexically fixed. p. 74.

### Unit 2, Notes on Pronunciation — selected points

- **Long/short vowel distinction is meaning-bearing** (e.g. `faj`/`fáj`, `kor`/`kór`) — the source
  stresses this is one of the most important phonemic contrasts to master. p. 27.
- **Double (geminate) consonants** before a vowel are pronounced roughly twice as long as single
  consonants — a genuine phonemic length contrast largely absent from English consonants outside
  compound-word boundaries. p. 27-28.
- **Vowel harmony**: words are front-vowel, back-vowel, or neutral-vowel, and take correspondingly-
  harmonic suffix variants — same underlying phenomenon as Rounds' Chapter 3 (`001_chapters1-3_
  phonology_vowel_harmony.md`), independently illustrated here with different examples
  (`Debrecenben`, `lapban`, `isznak`, etc.). p. 29.
- **Linking (liaison)**: a word-final consonant is resyllabified onto a following vowel-initial word
  in the same breath group (`Hol van az állomás?` → `Hol va-na-zállomás?`). p. 29.
- **Syllabication**: a syllable begins with a consonant and ends with a vowel wherever possible;
  the consonant digraphs (`cs, gy, ly, ny, sz, ty, zs`) are never split across a syllable boundary;
  double-letter geminate spellings (`ssz, ggy`, etc.) split as one letter per syllable
  (`ösz-sze`, `meny-nyi`). p. 29.

### Unit 4, Notes on Pronunciation — Consonant Assimilation

Because of Hungarian's agglutinative richness of suffixes, voicing assimilation across a
consonant-consonant boundary (within a word or across a word boundary in the same breath group) is
pervasive: a voiced consonant before a voiceless one devoices, and a voiceless consonant before a
voiced one voices (e.g. `zsebkendő` shows `b`→[p]-like devoicing before `k`; `csak gyufa` shows the
reverse). Framed explicitly by the source as a consequence of the language's agglutinative
morphology generating many more consonant-cluster boundaries than a less-suffixing language would.
p. 70-71.

## Notable findings (colloquial/register/period vocabulary)

Per this project's specific interest in informal/colloquial-register material (not just
foundational grammar), the following are flagged from this extraction:

1. **The `te`/`maga`/`ön` three-tier politeness system** (Grammar points Unit 2A) is the single
   highest-value register finding in this range — an explicit, source-stated colloquial/neutral/
   formal-and-declining three-way split for second-person address, with `ön` specifically marked as
   old-fashioned/dated even by 1962.
2. **`MASZEK`** (p. 43) — explicitly footnoted by the source as period Hungarian economic-register
   vocabulary for a privately-owned small business, contrasted with `állami` "state-owned." A
   genuinely period/politically-marked lexical item from socialist-era Hungary, not slang but
   register-adjacent institutional vocabulary worth flagging for the mechanics-analysis phase.
3. **`Úttörő Áruház`** ("Pioneer [Department] Store," p. 68) — likely referencing the Hungarian
   communist youth organization's branded retail chain; same socialist-era institutional-vocabulary
   category as `MASZEK`.
4. **The source's own stated colloquial-register note on indefinite-article omission** (Unit 1A,
   p. 9: "The tendency in colloquial speech in general is not to use it [`egy`]...") — an explicit
   register annotation, not inferred.
5. **Double negation as the only grammatical Hungarian pattern** (Unit 2C) is not itself a
   colloquial-vs-formal distinction, but is flagged because it inverts the English standard/
   sub-standard framing entirely — worth keeping in mind for the mechanics-analysis phase when
   comparing Hungarian slang negation patterns against English-derived assumptions.

No `[B]`/`[C]`/`[S]`-style dialectal tags or explicit regional-variant annotations appear anywhere

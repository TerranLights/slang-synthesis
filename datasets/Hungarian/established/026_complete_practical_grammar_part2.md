**Why this source matters specifically:** this is a mid-19th-century (1853) reference and literary
anthology, not a teaching grammar in the modern sense — it exists specifically to be read alongside
the modern (2009) Rounds grammar already extracted in this project (`established/001`–`005`) as an
**Attested Era contrast**. Every vocabulary/grammar item below is tagged `Attested Era: 19th century
(source published 1853)` unless a more specific date is directly supportable (e.g. a dated quotation
from a named poet, or the source's own quotation of a medieval document).

**OCR-quality caveat (applies to the whole file, not per-row):** this PDF's text layer is an
OCR of an original 1853 typeface with long-s-era ligatures and inconsistent accent rendering.
Hungarian accented vowels (á, é, í, ó, ö, ő, ú, ü, ű) are frequently OCR'd as their unaccented
counterpart, a stray apostrophe, or occasionally a different letter entirely (e.g. "Idttam" for
"láttam," "dllitja" for "állítja"). This is a genuine text layer — `Vision Reading Confidence` is
`n/a` throughout per spec (no vision-reading was performed) — but it is *not* a clean modern text
layer either. Where a specific form's reading is genuinely uncertain, it is flagged inline; where
the modern equivalent is inferable with reasonable confidence from context, it is given in brackets.

**Coverage note — comprehensive-but-not-exhaustive, sampled at multiple densities by section type:**
this 254-PDF-page chunk contains five structurally distinct kinds of content, and the extraction
below treats each differently per the coverage rule:

1. **Syntax of Government** (printed pp. 235–279, the direct continuation of the grammar proper) —

2. **Reading Exercises** (printed pp. 280–286) — five short 1848–49 Hungarian-War-of-Independence
   anecdotes with footnoted period/political vocabulary — covered in full given their small size and
   unusually high historical/register value (see below).
3. **Vocabulary to the Compositions** (printed pp. 287–~319, a general-purpose Hungarian-English/
   English-Hungarian glossary keyed to exercises in the grammar's *first* half) — sampled, not
   exhaustive (a multi-hundred-entry basic-vocabulary glossary largely overlapping `established/
   001–005`'s core lexicon); entries chosen for archaic spelling, idiom, or a gloss not already
   captured elsewhere.
4. **Sketches of the History of Hungarian Literature** (its own page numbering, pp. 3–44) — a
   narrative essay, not a vocabulary source. Captured as paraphrased grammar-points/context
   (periods, named authors, journals, institutions) — no vocabulary table rows from this section,
   consistent with the coverage rule's treatment of non-lexical prose.
5. **Selections from Hungarian Classics** (pp. 45–116) plus its own closing **Vocabulary** (pp.
   117–159) — genuine primary literary texts (fables, an allegory, oratory, history, and poetry) by
   named early-19th-century authors (Fáy, Kisfaludy, Kármán, Kazinczy, Kölcsey, Péczely, Vörösmarty,
   Berzsenyi), each with real dates independent of the grammar's own 1853 publication date where the

   substantial narrative poem (Vörösmarty's "Szép Ilonka"), plus a sample from the closing
   literary-register vocabulary (skewed toward battle/fate/heroic-diction words, reflecting what the
   selections are actually about).

### Morpheme breakdown

> **ismerősömhöz-style stacking, this chunk's own worked example — huzogánynyal** = huzogány
> ("whirlbat, war-club") + nyal (instrumental-comitative `-val/-vel`, assimilated to the stem-final
> consonant, spelled `-nyal` rather than modern `-nyal`/`-nnyal`) — "with a whirlbat." Demonstrates
> the source's own explicit footnote on affix assimilation (referenced but not reproduced from the
> grammar's Introduction, outside this excerpt). p. 259

> **balvéleményű** = bal ("left; [figuratively] wrong, mistaken") + vélemény ("opinion") + ű
> (adjectival suffix, "-having/-ful," the rounded-front-vowel-harmony variant of `-ú`) — "of a wrong
> opinion." A derivational (not inflectional) compound-plus-suffix formation, contrasting with the
> case-suffix stacking that dominates the rest of this chunk. p. 288

> **ördögkarú** = ördög ("devil") + karú (kar "arm" + ú, adjectival "-having/-ful") — "devil-armed,"
> i.e. formidably strong in the sword-arm. Same `-ú/-ű` adjectival-suffix pattern as `balvéleményű`
> above, applied to a nonce compound coined for effect within a single anecdote rather than a
> lexicalized item — a plausible model for how this suffix pattern could support ad hoc
> `morphological_play` slang coinages in a mechanics-analysis pass. p. 283

> **kürthang** = kürt ("horn, bugle") + hang ("sound") — a bare noun-noun compound (juxtaposition,
> no suffix), contrasting with the suffixal derivations above; same compounding strategy already
> flagged in `established/002`'s `emlősállat` example.

> **vérzivatar** = vér ("blood") + zivatar ("storm, tumult") — noun-noun compound; a literary-
> register intensifying compound (lit. "blood-storm" for a sanguinary battle/tumult), a productive
> pattern in this Selections vocabulary's heroic/martial register (cf. `vérmező` "field of blood,"
> `vérnap` "day of slaughter," both also headwords in the same glossary page range, pp. 158–159,
> not separately tabled above). p. 158

> **végküzdelem** = vég ("end, final") + küzdelem ("combat, struggle," itself küzd- "to struggle" +
> -elem, a nominalizing suffix — compare the `-alom/-elem` noun class already documented in
> `established/002`'s Chapter 5 vocabulary, e.g. `érzelem`, `kegyelem`) — "the decisive/final
> combat." Shows the `-alom/-elem` nominalization pattern feeding into further compounding. p. 157

> **vesztőhely** = veszt- (verb stem, "to lose, to destroy, to put to death") + ő (agentive/
> participial suffix, roughly "-er/-ing") + hely ("place") — "place of putting-to-death," i.e.
> scaffold/execution ground. A verb-participle-plus-noun compound, a third compounding strategy
> alongside the noun-noun (`kürthang`, `vérzivatar`) and stem-plus-adjectival-suffix
> (`balvéleményű`, `ördögkarú`) patterns already captured above — worth noting as a fuller
> compounding-strategy inventory than either Rounds chapter alone provided. p. 158

## Grammar points

### Syntax of Government — overview

Printed pp. 235–264 systematically catalogue Hungarian's case/postpositional-affix system by
*which verb, adjective, or construction class governs each affix* — a fundamentally different
organizing principle from Rounds' case-by-case tour in `established/003` (which organizes by case
first, government second). Treated together, the two sources are complementary: Rounds gives the
fuller modern paradigm tables; Csink gives a far more exhaustive *governed-verb-class* catalogue per
affix, useful precisely because it names dozens of individual verbs/adjectives per affix rather than
a handful of textbook examples. Affixes covered, in the source's own order: `-ban/-ben` (§B4, "in,
at"), `-ból/-ből` ("from, out of"), `-ért` ("for, for the sake"), `-hoz/-hez/-höz` ("to, toward"),
`-ig` ("until, as far as"), `kép/képen/-ként` ("as, like"), `-n/-on/-en/-ön` ("on, at"), `-nak/-nek`
("to, for, of" — replacing both Genitive and Dative), `-nál/-nél` ("at, near; comparative 'than'"),
`-ra/-re` ("onto, at"), `-ról/-ről` ("off, from"), `-stul/-stül` ("together with," an archaic
comitative rarely covered in modern grammars), `-tól/-től` ("from, by [passive agent]"), `-ul/-ül`
("as, in the capacity of; also 'in [a language]'"), `-vá/-vé` ("into, transformation"), `-val/-vel`
("with"). pp. 235–260

**Correlative place-affix table (where?/whither?/whence?).** The source gives an explicit
three-column correlative table — `-n`/`-ra`/`-ról`, `-ban`/`-ba`/`-ból`, `-ott`/`-ra`/`-ról` — keyed
to phonological/orthographic rules for which town/place names take which series (final vowel or one
of a long consonant list → the `-n` series; names of countries/districts/isles/forests/foreign towns
→ the `-ban` series; a short list of towns ending in `-r` → the `-ott` series, e.g. `Fejérvárott`,
`Győrött`). This is a more systematic treatment of the locative-series-by-place-name-type rule than
either Rounds chapter gives. pp. 260–261

### Definite vs. indefinite conjugation — triggers (Csink's own list)

Independently of Rounds' seven-environment list (`established/004`), Csink gives five triggers for
the Definite conjugation: (1) a demonstrative pronoun preceding the object noun; (2) the object noun
carrying a possessive affix; (3) the object being a third-person personal pronoun, or one of the
reflexive-intensive pronouns `magam/magad/maga` when subject and object coincide; (4) the object
being a proper name (*but* the Indefinite form is used if the proper name is generic, e.g. "in our
time we find few Horaces"); (5) whenever the definite article `a'/az` itself precedes the object.
Point (4)'s generic-proper-name exception is not something Rounds' modern treatment mentions and is
a genuinely new data point. pp. 265–267

### Moods and tenses (Csink's framing)

The Indicative marks simple assertion; the Subjunctive marks desire/wish/command and doubtful
questions; the Conditional marks uncertain/unconfirmed truth (including reported speech the speaker
doesn't vouch for: `hallottam hogy ott lett volna` "I heard that he was there," using conditional
`lett volna` for hearsay). On tense: the Present Indicative regularly substitutes for the Future
(the compound future with `fog` is explicitly called rare "by the people," and the source frames
this as evidence of the language's originally two-tense — present/future vs. past — "oriental"
character); the First Perfect/Imperfect and the compound Pluperfect are both flagged as reserved for
"solemn speech" and historical narration, not familiar conversation, where the simple Perfect has
absorbed both functions. A separate archaic future-in-`-and/-end` (`várand`, `verend`) is noted as
already rare "either by the people or by authors" in 1853 and functionally equivalent to a Latin
"-dus" (duty/necessity) participle when used at all. pp. 267–269

### Participles — historical note on the `-va/-ve` participle

The source explicitly traces the Past participle in `-va/-ve` to a *former* use with the full
possessive-affix set (`-m, -d, -nk, -tok, -k`), which it claims is the actual historical origin of
the modern Imperfect tense (via loss of the participle's `v`). It quotes, as evidence, a fragment
from "ancient Documents of Hungarian writing": *"Es vün aluvánk elorozzák üket"* ("we having fallen
asleep, they stole him secretly," using `aluvánk` where the source says modern usage has simplified
to `alván`) — **this is the single most valuable Attested-Era find in this chunk**: an explicit
quotation, inside an already-1853 source, of pre-modern/medieval Hungarian morphology, giving a
three-way era stack (medieval document → 1853 grammar's commentary → 2009 Rounds grammar already in
this project) for the same participial system. `Attested Era` for this specific quoted fragment:
medieval Hungarian, exact date not given by the source — tag any future citation of it as such
rather than folding it into the 1853 "whole-book" era value used elsewhere in this file. p. 270

### Infinitive with possessive affixes

The bare infinitive takes a possessive-affix-marked subject (via `-nak/-nek`) whenever it depends on
an impersonal predicate like `szabad` (allowed), `nehéz` (difficult), `könnyű` (easy), `lehetetlen`
(impossible), `lehet` (possible), or `kell` (must): `Károlynak szabad haza mennie` "Charles is
allowed to go home." The source draws a fine semantic distinction between the potential suffix
`-hat/-het` and the impersonal verb `lehet` (both roughly "can/may," `lehet` itself derived from
`lenni` "to become/be"): `-hat/-het` is used when the *limitation is internal to the subject*
(`ki nem mehetek` "I cannot go out," because I am ill), while `lehet` is used when the *obstacle is
external* (`ki nem lehet mennem` "I cannot go out," because the door is locked). This internal-vs-
external-obstacle distinction is not made this explicitly in either Rounds chapter already extracted
and is a genuinely new, fairly subtle semantic rule. pp. 271–272

### Relative pronouns and correlatives

Hungarian distinguishes three relative pronouns by animacy/individuation: `ki` (who, persons),
`melly` (modern `mely`; which, non-human animates, or definite/individuated non-persons), `mi`
(what, inanimate/general things) — with the source noting `melly` and `mi` are "often used
promiscuously," the deciding factor being whether the referent is conceived of as an individuated,
definite entity (favors `melly`) or not (favors `mi`). Correlative pronoun pairs are also given:
`annyi ... a' mennyi` ("so much ... as much"), `millyen/milly ... olly/ollyan` ("what sort of ...
such/like"). pp. 273–274

### Conjunctions and word order (emphasis-driven)

`és` is a neutral copula; `meg` (also "and") specifically groups two objects under one collective
notion; `is` ("also, as well") frequently carries an ironic or boastful undertone; `pedig` is
basically disjunctive ("but, yet") but slides toward copulative use depending on context. Word order
is explicitly framed as emphasis-driven rather than fixed SVO: the verb fronts the sentence only when
the verb itself carries the emphasis (`megholt Mátyás király` "dead is King Matthew," i.e. King
Matthew is dead); otherwise the verb immediately follows whichever constituent is emphasized.
Negative `nem`/`ne` precede the specific word being denied, not the verb by default — contrasted
directly: `Nem az atyám volt itt` "[It was] not my father [who] was here" vs. `az atyám nem volt itt`
"my father was not here." Separable verbal prefixes (`fel, le, meg, el, vissza, össze`, etc.) follow
the verb under normal emphasis but precede it (with the sentence's conjunction able to intervene)
when the prefix itself is emphasized or contrasted. pp. 275–279

### Historical Sketch of Hungarian Literature — paraphrased overview

The 44-page essay (own pagination, pp. 3–44) traces Hungarian literary/linguistic history from the
pre-Christian settlement period through 1848, organized into 9 numbered periods (§1 general
remarks/settlement in Pannonia; §2 11th–12th c.; §3 13th c., "the fatal century" — Mongol invasion of
1241 under Béla IV depopulating and re-foreignizing large tracts of the country; §4 14th c., Angevin
kings importing an Italian court and Latin administrative language; §5 15th c. — notes an early-15th-
century partial Bible translation into Hungarian predating most of Europe's vernacular Bibles, and
Matthias Corvinus's library/printing patronage despite the era being otherwise "void" in Hungarian-
language literary output because Corvinus's library was destroyed after the Battle of Mohács; §6
16th–17th c., Habsburg rule and religious/national struggle; §7 18th c. to 1790; §8 preparatory steps
1790–1830; §9 the Academy-driven "rapid progress" 1831–1848).

**Named authors and institutions (§9), independently useful as a period-appropriate literary-canon
list**: Kazinczy Ferenc (language reform, translator of Goethe/Bürger/Ossian/Gessner); Berzsenyi
Dániel ("greatest lyric poet in regard to Odes," compared to Horace + Matthisson); Kisfaludy Sándor
(lyric) and Kisfaludy Károly (drama, d. 1829 — his memorial fund seeded the Kisfaludy Társaság
literary association); Kölcsey, Vörösmarty (epic poems *Zalán futása*, *Cserhalom*, *Eger*; the poem
*Szózat* "Appeal," source-flagged as winning the Academy's own first prize for best patriotic poem),
Bajza, Czuczor (lyric); Erdélyi János, Szabó József, Eötvös József, Szalay László (also lyric/prose);
Fáy András (explicitly called "the Hungarian Aesop") and Náray (fiction/romance); Jósika Miklós,
Kuthy Lajos (novelists); Petőfi (noted specifically for lyric poems that "rarely ascended above the
atmosphere of common life" — i.e., Petőfi's folk-plain-style register is explicitly remarked on by a
contemporary). Institutions: the Hungarian Academy of Sciences (founded to cultivate the national
language and sciences in it); the *Kisfaludy Társaság*; the journals *Nemzeti Ujság*, *Hazai és
Külföldi Tudósítások*, *Jelenkor*, *Tudománytár*, *Muzárion*, *Kritikai Lapok*, *Athenaeum*; Louis
Kossuth's *Pesti Hírlap* (1841–43) explicitly credited with fusing literary and political nationalism
in the run-up to 1848. pp. 3–44

### Selections from Hungarian Classics — structure and register

Two parts: **I. Prose** (pp. 45–85: Fáy's fables and one allegory; a second allegory from Charles
Kisfaludy; fables from József Kármán and Ferenc Kazinczy; an oratorical piece from Ferenc Kölcsey; a
historical excerpt from József Péczely) and **II. Poetry** (pp. 85–116: Kölcsey's "Remény,
Emlékezet"; Vörösmarty's "Szép Ilonka," "Julius Caesar," "Az elhagyott anya," "A' hontalan," "Szózat";
Berzsenyi's "A' felkelt nemességhez," "A' Magyarokhoz," "Jámborság és középszer," "A' temető,"
"Fohászkodás"; Kölcsey's "Vanitatum Vanitas"; a Charles Kisfaludy song "Szülőföldem szép határa"; and
closing epigrams from Vörösmarty, Berzsenyi, and Wattay). Fáy's fables (sampled above) are terse,
animal-fable-format moral vignettes in a plain, almost folk-proverb-adjacent register — a useful
register contrast against the Kölcsey/Vörösmarty/Berzsenyi poetry, which is dense, high-diction, and
heavily inverted-word-order literary Hungarian. The closing Vocabulary to the Selections (pp.
117–159) is keyed specifically to this literary-register content and consequently skews hard toward
martial/heroic/fate vocabulary (`vitéz`, `vérzivatar`, `végküzdelem`, `viadal`, `vesztőhely`, etc. —
see Vocabulary table above) rather than the everyday-life register of the grammar's earlier chapters
or Rounds' modern textbook. pp. 45–159

# Hungarian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: 750 Hungarian Proverbs (Full Collection)

**Source:** Paczolay Gyula, *750 magyar közmondás / 750 Hungarian proverbs* (1991), the entire
58-page document (clean text layer, confirmed) — `source_reference/languages/Hungarian/750-Hungarian-proverbs.pdf`.
A bilingual Hungarian/English proverb dictionary, alphabetized by the Hungarian keyword, covering
letters A through Z, ZS. One proverb keyed to the dictionary's own running number 1–750 supplies one
table row below.

**Coverage note:** all 750 numbered entries extracted — this is exhaustive, not a sample, per the
dispatch instruction that a 58-page proverb collection is tractable to cover in full. One entry
(No. 183, *Farkast emlegetnek...*) carries an editorial footnote in the source crediting a
supplementary attestation from the author's later comparative work (Paczolay, *European Proverbs in
55 Languages...*, Veszprém, 1997) — reproduced in that row's Notes rather than skipped, per the
coverage rule's register-annotation instruction.

**Usage Tier — new subcategory.** `idiom`/`proverb` was not already a top-level tier in
`00_Usage_Tier_Taxonomy.md`. Per that taxonomy's dot-notation extensibility convention, this file
uses `literary.proverb` (a subcategory of the existing `literary` tier — proverbs are fixed,
traditional, register-elevated sayings, which fits `literary`'s "poetic, archaic-flavored... written
or literary contexts" description more precisely than plain `literary` on its own) rather than
`core`. Not yet promoted into the shared taxonomy file itself (that promotion step is specified for
`analysis/`-phase findings reaching 2+ languages; this is a single-language `established/` extraction
and the taxonomy file explicitly allows an un-promoted subcategory value here).

**Attested Era caveat.** Per `00_Reference_Extraction_Spec.md`'s column rule, `contemporary (source
published 1991)` reflects only the *compilation's* publication date, not the age of the proverbs
themselves — traditional proverbs are frequently far older than the dictionary that recorded them,
but the source gives no per-proverb dating, so no earlier era is guess-filled (see
`00_Historical_and_Geographic_Context_Guide.md`). `Attested Region` / `Geographic Scope` are left
`—` for the same reason: the source is a general national proverb dictionary with no explicit
regional/dialectal tagging per entry.

**Format per entry.** `Term` is the Hungarian proverb as printed (a second Hungarian form joined
with ` / ` where the source itself gives an explicit dash-prefixed variant reading of the same
proverb, e.g. No. 17). `Gloss` is the source's own literal English translation. Where the source
additionally supplies a parenthetical English idiom-equivalent (e.g. No. 44's *"He sings the same
old song"* as the equivalent of literally *"He is flaying the same bock"*) or a quoted figurative
meaning, both are preserved in `Notes` rather than the `Gloss` column, to keep `Gloss` a clean
literal rendering. A small number of entries (~20) also carry a Hungarian-language explanatory gloss
in the source itself (marked with Hungarian low-high quotes „...” or parentheses) instead of, or
alongside, an English figurative-meaning line — these are flagged in `Notes` as untranslated Hungarian
source text rather than paraphrased, per the copyright-discipline "paraphrase, don't bulk-translate"
instinct extended here to avoid introducing translation error into a dataset meant to represent the
source faithfully. Every row's `Notes` ends with the dictionary's own entry number (`No. N`) so any
row traces back to its exact source location.

## Grammar points

This source is a proverb dictionary, not a grammar reference — it contains no explicit grammar
exposition of its own. Two structural/register patterns worth flagging for the mechanics-analysis
phase, both visible only at full-collection scale rather than from any single entry:

### Literal-to-figurative derivation patterns

Across the ~184 entries (about a quarter of the collection) that carry an explicit quoted figurative
meaning distinct from the literal translation, the figurative sense is almost never an arbitrary
reassignment — it is reached by one of a small number of recurring moves:

- **Concrete action → abstract disposition/behavior.** A literal small physical action stands for a
  general character trait or recurring behavior: "He whets his teeth on something" → "He is very
  eager to get something" (No. 201); "He shows the whiteness of his teeth" → "He shows himself in his
  true colours" (No. 202).
- **Animal-domain source imagery, mapped onto human social behavior.** Sparrows, wolves, dogs, cats,
  fish, and birds recur constantly as the vehicle for a human-behavior target: sparrows for
  low-value/easy targets (No. 8, "He shoots at sparrows with a high calibre gun" = overkill; No. 124,
  "Sparrows cannot be caught by beating a drum" = subtlety over force), wolves for danger/threat
  named aloud (No. 183, "when a wolf is mentioned, he is already walking round the garden" = speak of
  the devil), dogs for low status/exploitation (No. 401, "A living dog is better than a dead lion").
  This is exactly the kind of productive semantic-domain-as-metaphor-source pattern the taxonomy's
  `metaphor_productivity` derivation type is meant to capture, and animal imagery is clearly this
  collection's single most productive source domain — worth a dedicated `analysis/` pass once this
  file feeds into that phase.
- **Body-part/bodily-state metonymy for a mental or social state.** "Inába szállt a bátorsága" (his
  courage sank to his sinews) → "he got frightened" (No. 51); "Feni a fogát" (he whets his teeth) →
  eagerness (No. 201); paired-body-part idioms recur (No. 62's pocket-knife/anger; No. 445's
  "talks a hole into the belly" → talks one's head off).
- **Household/domestic-object imagery for social/economic judgment.** Pots, dishes, spoons, and
  cooking vessels stand for compatibility, class-matching, or social fit: No. 102 "To an earthenware
  dish a wooden spoon" → "Marry your match"; No. 595 "Sheepskin coat to sheepskin coat..." → "Marry
  your equal." The *same* figurative meaning ("marry your match/equal") recurs from two entirely
  different literal vehicles (tableware vs. clothing) — a clean example of one figurative "slot"
  being fillable by multiple independent literal sources, relevant to how synthesized slang could
  productively vary its own literal vehicle while holding a target meaning fixed.

### Parallel bilingual figurative layer: English-idiom equivalents vs. literal translation

Distinct from the quoted "figurative meaning" gloss, ~60 entries additionally supply a parenthetical
*English proverb/idiom equivalent* alongside the literal translation — e.g. No. 111 "He got out of
the bucket and got into the pail" / "(Out of the frying [pan] into the fire)"; No. 400 "The dog is
buried here" / "(Here lies the rub)"; No. 245 "The dry leaves do not rustle if no wind blows" /
"(No smoke without fire)". This is a different kind of data than the figurative-meaning gloss: it is
the dictionary author's own claim of a *pre-existing equivalent expression in the target language*,
not a paraphrase of meaning. For this project's synthesis purposes, these pairs are a ready-made
corpus of "same figurative target, independently-evolved literal vehicle in two real languages" —
useful as calibration data for how plausible a synthesized conlang's own proverb vehicle should feel
relative to its target meaning, independent of any one source language's specific imagery.

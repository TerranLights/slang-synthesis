# Hungarian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Magyaróra — New Paths to the Hungarian Language
## (Representative Sample, Intermediate Range)

**Source:** Szita Szilvia, Fogarasi Ilona et al. (www.magyarora.com), *Magyaróra — New Paths to
the Hungarian Language*, "19 Magyaróra New Paths to the Hungarian Language.pdf" (scanned/compiled
2010; individual worksheets dated 2003-2004 per their own footers). This is a genuinely huge
(1324-page) multi-level worksheet compilation combining leveled reading exercises built around
real online news articles ("1. szint"/"2. szint"/"3. szint" tiers = roughly beginner/intermediate/
upper-intermediate), topical communication-skills chapters, and a large answer-key apparatus. It
has a clean PDF text layer throughout the sampled range (`pdftotext` succeeds cleanly; no
vision-reading was needed for any entry in this file).

**Coverage note — deliberately non-exhaustive sample, not full transcription.** Per this book's
unusual size, this file does **not** attempt anything close to full coverage of PDF pages 451-900
(the assigned intermediate-level range). Instead, four representative lesson/section units were
selected from within that range, chosen specifically to prioritize register/colloquial content
(the task's own guidance, since intermediate-level material tends to introduce more natural/
colloquial usage than beginner drills) over pure grammar exercises. Each selected unit's
vocabulary and grammar content was extracted in full (not further sampled internally) — only the
selection of *which* units to sample from the 450-page range is a sample, not the extraction
within a chosen unit. Everything else in pages 451-900 was skipped entirely and is **not**
represented in this file.

**Units sampled (all located and page-verified by direct inspection, not offset arithmetic):**

1. **Chapter "7. Kommunikáció" — "Halló, halló... (1.), (2.)" and "Az internet"** (PDF pp. 472-473,
   476, 478, 483; author credit "© Fogarasi Ilona" / "© Szita Szilvia" — www.magyarora.com, no
   individual date shown). A telephone-communication and internet-communication skills chapter,
   not a leveled news article. **Selected because it is the single richest register-contrastive
   unit found in the sampled range**: nearly every phrase is explicitly dual-tagged by the source
   itself as `form.` (formális, "formal, polite") or `inform.` (informális, "informal, friendly"),
   and two items are explicitly tagged `(beszélt nyelv)` "(spoken language)" — the source's own
   colloquial-register marker.
2. **"2. szint Május-június" — "Kannabisszal az agresszív szurkolók ellen Portugáliában"** (PDF pp.
   555-561; article dated 2004. június 11., source cited as delmagyar.hu). A news article about
   Portuguese police tolerating marijuana use among English football fans to reduce alcohol-driven
   violence. **Selected for its dense drug/alcohol-culture register vocabulary**, glossed in the
   book's own "Szómagyarázat" (word-explanation) sidebar.
3. **"2. szint Július" — "Stuttgartban nagyon elégedettek Szabiccsal"** (PDF pp. 562-566; article
   dated 2003. július 7., source cited as delmagyar.hu). A sports-interview article about
   footballer Szabics Imre. **Selected because it contains the book's own explicit `(szleng)`
   ("slang") tag** on the idiom `jól bírtam a kiképzést`.
4. **"3. szint Október" — "Létező személy volt Münchhausen báró"** (PDF pp. 899-905; article dated
   2003. október 22., source cited as delmagyar.hu). An upper-intermediate news-style article about
   historical evidence for a real Baron Münchhausen. **Selected because it contains a second
   explicit `(szleng)` tag** (on `piás`) plus an explicit `(ironikus)` "(ironic)" register note —
   both genuinely rare, source-marked register annotations worth capturing per the coverage rule's
   mandate to never skip explicit register annotations.

**Relationship to prior Hungarian `established/` files:** this is a different genre from both the
Rounds reference grammar (`001`-`003`) and the 1962 FSI audio-lingual course (`013`-`018`) — a
2000s-era leveled-reading/exercise compilation built on real contemporary online journalism, with
its own explicit colloquial/formal/slang annotation system. No deliberate redundancy check against
prior files was performed (per the dispatch's sampling-not-supplementing framing); vocabulary here
that happens to overlap with items already captured elsewhere (e.g. `köszönöm`, `szia`-type
politeness forms) is not re-flagged as redundant since this file's scope is a bounded sample, not a
systematic grammar-territory pass.

## Grammar points

### 1. The `tegez`/`magáz` (familiar/polite address) system, and how it's taught here
The Kommunikáció chapter states the rule directly (p. 476): friends, close acquaintances,
colleagues, family, and children are normally addressed with `te` (`tegezzük`); older people and
strangers are normally addressed with `Ön` (`magázzuk`). This is the same two-tier system the 1962
FSI course documents as a three-way `maga`/`ön`/`te` split (`013_fsi_vol1_units01-04.md`, Unit 2A) —
this 2000s-era book collapses the `maga`/`ön` distinction the FSI course still tracked separately,
suggesting `maga` had receded in ordinary usage relative to `Ön` by the time this book was written
(a plausible register shift worth flagging for the mechanics-analysis phase, though not something
this file can confirm without a dedicated diachronic source).

### 2. The conditional mood as a politeness strategy
Section 3 of "Halló, halló (2.)" (p. 476) states explicitly: "A feltételes mód (conditional form)
mindig udvariasabb!" ("The conditional is always more polite!"), then drills the same verb across
all four persons in both plain-indicative and conditional forms, e.g. `Fel tudsz hívni holnap?` →
`Fel tudnál hívni holnap?` ("Can you call tomorrow?" → "Could you call tomorrow?"), given for
`te`/`ti`/`Ön`/`Önök`, and separately for subjective (`alanyi`) vs. objective/definite (`tárgyas`)
conjugation (`Megadod...?` → `Megadnád...?`). This is a clean, source-stated politeness rule:
switching a verb from indicative to conditional is itself a register-raising device, independent of
which pronoun/verb-agreement register (`te` vs. `Ön`) is already in use.

### 3. Lexical register pairs: colloquial vs. formal near-synonyms
This sampled material surfaces several explicit or implicit near-synonym pairs split by register,
worth recording as a set since slang-formation later will likely draw on the same colloquial member
of each pair:
- `hívni` (colloquial, tagged `beszélt nyelv`) vs. `felhívni`/`telefonálni` (neutral/formal) — "to
  call."
- `köszi` (colloquial clip) vs. `köszönöm` (neutral) — "thanks."
- `bocs` (colloquial clip) vs. `bocsánat`/`elnézést` (neutral/formal) — "sorry, excuse me."
- `mobil` (colloquial clip) vs. `mobiltelefon` (neutral) — "cell phone."
- `rögzítő` (colloquial clip, tagged `beszélt nyelv`) vs. `üzenetrögzítő` (neutral) — "answering
  machine."
- `ittas` (formal/technical, used in the megoldások synonym table) vs. `részeg` (plain) vs. `piás`
  (explicit slang) — a three-tier register ladder for "drunk," all attested within this single
  sample.
The clipping pattern (`köszi`, `bocs`, `mobil`, `rögzítő`) — dropping a word to its first syllable
or two — recurs enough across this small sample to be worth flagging as a productive colloquial
word-formation strategy in standard spoken Hungarian, distinct from the slang-specific
`morphological_play` category `00_Word_Concept_and_Morphological_Typology_Guide.md` warns needs to
be distinguished from ordinary grammatical/registral productivity in an agglutinative language.

### 4. Denominal verbalizing suffixes (`-ez`/`-el`/`-öl`) for loanwords
The internet-vocabulary section (p. 483) is a clean, self-contained illustration of how Hungarian
absorbs foreign nouns as verbs: `internet` → `interneteznek` (`-ez-` + 3pl `-nek`), `email` →
`emailezni`, `link` → `linkelni`, `chat` → `chatelni`, `szörf`("surf") → `szörfölni`. The suffix
choice (`-ez` vs. `-el` vs. `-öl`) tracks vowel harmony against the loanword's own vowels
(front/back, rounded/unrounded) the same way it does for native stems — i.e. loanword integration
follows the ordinary agglutinative verbalization mechanism rather than being a separate borrowing
strategy. This is directly relevant to later slang-mechanics analysis: any future internet-slang
neologism in Hungarian will very likely follow this exact `noun-stem + -ez/-el/-öl` pattern.

### 5. Preverbs (igekötők) as an aspect/direction system
The Münchhausen unit's own grammar drill (p. 905, exercise 7) asks students to fill in missing
preverbs from a bank of `be-, be-, bele-, ki-, ki-, meg-` across six sentences — a clean worked set
illustrating the productive preverb system: `ki is tüntette` ("did decorate" — `ki-` here marking
completed/thorough action, not literal "out"), `alaposan beivott` ("got thoroughly drunk" — `be-`
"in(to)," completive), `belefulladt` ("drowned" — `bele-` "into," directional + completive),
`megtalálta` ("found" — `meg-` perfectivizing). Preverbs are detachable and can also appear
post-posed or separated from the verb stem by other material (as in questions/negation), a pattern
already partly covered in prior Hungarian `established/` files but worth reinforcing here with this
unit's own worked minimal-pair drill.

### 6. Participle formation (melléknévi igenevek): `-ó/-ő` (present/imperfective) vs. `-t/-tt` (past/perfective)
The Münchhausen unit's grammar drill (p. 904, exercise 6) explicitly contrasts the two main
adjectival-participle suffixes on the same set of verb stems: `létez-` + `-ő` → `létező` ("existing"
— present participle), `nagyotmond-` + `-ó` → `nagyotmondó` ("boastful/exaggerating" — present
participle, lit. "big-talking"), `vonatkoz-` + `-ó` → `vonatkozó` ("pertaining/relevant"), vs.
`föllel-` + `-t/-tt` → `föllelt` ("found, discovered" — past/passive-flavored participle),
`erősít-` + `-tt` → `erősített` ("attached, fastened"). Hungarian has no true passive voice; these
past participles are the main way a completed, patient-oriented action gets expressed adjectivally
(`a föllelt levél` "the discovered/found letter" ≈ "the letter that was found").

### 7. Avoiding the passive: impersonal 3rd-person and participial paraphrase
Related to point 6: the Szabics unit's own gloss of `cikk jelent meg` as `cikket publikáltak`
("they published an article" — impersonal 3pl) illustrates the standard Hungarian workaround for
what English expresses with a passive ("an article was published"). The Kannabisz article does the
same repeatedly: `kiszűrhetik a meccslátogatók közül` ("they can filter [them] out from among the
match-goers," impersonal subject implied) rather than any passive construction. This is a systemic
feature relevant to later mechanics analysis of how Hungarian slang might handle agent-backgrounding
(e.g. via impersonal 3pl, via a participle, or via a reflexive/middle-voice-adjacent verb form)
since it has no productive morphological passive to reach for.

### 8. Case-suffix drilling across both units — a consolidated inventory
Both news-article units end their exercise sets with a fill-in-the-case-suffix drill drawing
directly on the article's own sentences. Pooling the suffixes actually drilled across the two units
(Szabics p. 565, Münchhausen p. 905) gives: `-ban/-ben` (inessive, "in"), `-ból/-ből` (elative,
"out of" — implicit via `-ra/-re` contrast), `-hoz/-hez/-höz` (allative, "to(ward)"), `-nak/-nek`
(dative), `-nál/-nél` (adessive, "at"), `-ra/-re` (sublative, "onto"), `-ról/-ről` (delative,
"off/about"), `-t` (accusative), `-val/-vel` (instrumental-comitative, "with"). This is a
consolidated *practice* set, not a new paradigm — the underlying case system itself is already
covered by the Rounds-grammar-derived files (`002`, `003`) — but it is a useful cross-check that
this book's own worked examples are consistent with those files' paradigm tables.

### 9. Morpheme Breakdown — selected composed forms
Per `00_Word_Concept_and_Morphological_Typology_Guide.md`, Hungarian is agglutinative and several
surface "words" in this sample are composed units worth decomposing rather than treated as atomic:

- **üzenetrögzítő** ("answering machine") = `üzenet` (message) + `rögzít-` (to record/fix, verb
  stem) + `-ő` (agentive/instrumental participle suffix, "thing that records") → lit. "message-
  recorder."
- **interneteznek** ("they use the internet") = `internet` (noun stem, loanword) + `-ez-`
  (denominal verbalizing suffix) + `-nek` (3rd person plural present indicative ending) → lit.
  "internet-verb-they."
- **füvezni** ("to smoke weed") = `fű` (grass, stem-alternating to `fü-`) + `-ez-` (denominal
  verbalizing suffix) + `-ni` (infinitive ending) → lit. "to grass-verb."
- **betépni** ("to get stoned") = `be-` (preverb, "in(to)," here marking completive/intensive
  aspect rather than literal direction) + `tép-` (to tear/rip, verb stem) + `-ni` (infinitive
  ending) → idiomatically extended from "to tear into" to "to get thoroughly high."
- **kiszűrhetik** ("they can filter [them] out," from the Kannabisz article's own text, p. 555) =
  `ki-` (preverb, "out") + `szűr-` (to filter, verb stem) + `-het-` (potential/ability suffix,
  "can/may") + `-i` (3rd person singular, definite/objective conjugation marker, generalized here
  to the impersonal-plural reading) + `-k` (3rd person plural) → lit. "out-filter-can-they(-it)."
  Illustrates the same productive `-hat/-het` potential suffix independently confirmed in the
  Kommunikáció chapter's own drills (`vissza tudnál hívni` "could you call back," `megadnád` "would
  you give it").

## Notable findings for later mechanics-analysis phase

- **Only two entries in this ~450-page sampled range carry the source's own explicit `(szleng)`
  tag** (`piás`, `jól bírtam a kiképzést`) despite the book covering plenty of obviously colloquial
  ground (drug culture, texting/internet, phone informality). This suggests the book's own slang
  labeling is conservative/sparse relative to its actual colloquial content — a methodological
  caution for any downstream frequency-style inference from explicit tags alone across this source.
- **The `(ironikus)` tag on `derék`** is a genuinely distinct annotation type from `(szleng)` or
  `(beszélt nyelv)` — a pragmatic/tonal marker rather than a register-tier marker. Worth tracking as
  its own category if more instances turn up in future Hungarian sampling from this same source,
  since irony-marking is a mechanism slang can exploit independently of register level.
- **Loanword verbalization (internet vocabulary) is entirely register-neutral to mildly colloquial**,
  not slang — `interneteznek`, `emailezni`, etc. read as completely ordinary contemporary
  vocabulary in this source, not marked or flagged at all, which is itself useful negative evidence
  (routine loanword integration ≠ slang, even when morphologically novel-looking).

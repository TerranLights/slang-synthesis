034's Units 1-5 content, so this file was extracted in full rather than as a non-redundant supplement.

### Morpheme Breakdown — agglutinative forms worth decomposing

- **`másfél szobás`** ("one-and-a-half-room[ed]") = `más` ("other/half," in this idiom) + `fél` ("half") +
  `szoba` ("room") + `-s` (adjective-forming suffix) — a culturally-specific fractional-counting
  convention (a small `félszoba` counts as "half" a room in Hungarian real-estate vocabulary), not
  decomposable by ordinary case/possessive morphology alone. Unit 7 culture note, p.102.
- **`harmincnyolcas`** ("size 38") = `harminc` ("30") + `nyolc` ("8") + `-as` (the Hányas? numeral-
  adjective suffix, Unit 10 Grammar §4) — the same agglutinative teens/tens compounding rule from
  034's numeral system, now carrying an additional derivational suffix layer. Unit 11 dialogue, p.156.
- **`köszönjük`** ("we thank/we greet") = `köszön-` (root) + `-jük` (1st-person-plural DEFINITE-
  conjugation present-tense suffix, front-vowel-ö/ő/ü/ű-final class) — pronounced `köszönnyük`
  (the written `nj` cluster assimilates to `nny` in speech only, spelling unchanged). Contrast with
  the INDEFINITE 1pl `köszönünk`, not tabulated in this file but implied by the paradigm's structure.
  Unit 11 Grammar §1, p.161.
- **`tudjuk`** ("we know [it]") = `tud-` (root) + `-juk` (1st-person-plural DEFINITE-conjugation
  suffix, back-vowel class) — pronounced `tuggyuk` (written `dj` → spoken `ggy`). Unit 11 Grammar §1,
  p.161.
- **`látlak`** ("I can see you") = `lát-` (root, "see") + `-lak` (portmanteau 1sg-subject +
  2nd-person-singular-familiar-object suffix) — a single morpheme fusing BOTH subject and object
  agreement, usable only with a familiar-register 2nd-person object and a transitive verb. Contrast
  **`szeretlek`** ("I love you") = `szeret-` + `-lek` (front-vowel allomorph), and **`tanítalak`**/
  **`értelek`** (with an `-a-`/`-e-` linking vowel after an -ít-final or two-consonant-final stem).
  Unit 10 Grammar §5, p.149.
- **`negyvenhatos`** ("number 46," e.g. a phone extension) = `negyven` ("40") + `hat` ("6") + `-os`
  (Hányas? numeral-adjective suffix) — same compounding-plus-suffix pattern as `harmincnyolcas`
  above, confirming the -as/-es/-os suffix's productivity across the numeral system regardless of
  the base number's own internal agglutination. Unit 10 culture note, p.143 / Unit 11, p.169.
- **`esőkabát`** ("raincoat") = `eső` ("rain," itself from `esik` "to fall/rain" + nominalizing
  suffix) + `kabát` ("coat") — a transparent N+N compound parallel to `gulyásleves`, `zöldség-
  gyümölcs`, and other compounds already catalogued in 034; included here because it's the clearest
  example of `eső` (as a noun, "the rain") appearing outside its verbal use (`Esik az eső`, Unit 9).
  Unit 11 Practice, p.170.

## Grammar points

### Unit 6, Grammar §1-§2 — Focus (word order) and intonation

The "golden rule" of Hungarian word order: the most informative/contrastive element (the **focus**)
sits immediately before the verb (e.g. "Angol vagyok" vs. "Én vagyok angol" — same words, different
implied contrast depending on what's placed pre-verbally). Two exceptions: no verb in the sentence,
or the focus IS the verb. Yes/no-question intonation depends on syllable count around the focus (rise
on the last syllable for 1-2 syllables; rise-then-fall on the penultimate syllable for 3+). p.88-89.

### Unit 6, Grammar §3 — már/még ("already/yet/before"; also "even"/"still")

`már` in positive sentences/questions = already/before; `még` in negative sentences/questions = yet.
`még` also independently means "even" and "still" depending on context — three senses of one
particle. Extended to present-tense use ("still"/"not yet"/"already"/"not any more") in Unit 9
Grammar §2. p.89-90, p.133.

### Unit 6, Grammar §4-§8 — Verb-conjugation refinements (ö-linking vowel, transitivity terminology)

`ül`-type verbs (front-vowel stems ending in ö/ő/ü/ű) take **ö** as the 1sg/2pl linking vowel, not
standard `e` (ülök, ültök). `jön` is irregular but follows the same ö-pattern. The `-t` object ending
also takes an ö-linking-vowel refinement for this same stem class. `iszik` is flagged irregular here,
explained fully only in Unit 8 (i/í-only stems take back-vowel harmony regardless of their phonetic
frontness). Transitive/intransitive terminology introduced (verb "vmit" marking in vocabulary lists).
pp.89-92.

### Unit 6, Grammar §9 — Haza ("home"): presence-conditioned three-way locative

`itthon`/`itthonról` (own/other's home, speaker present) vs. `otthon`/`otthonról`/`hazulról` (speaker
absent) vs. invariant `haza` (motion-to, regardless of presence) — a genuinely unusual deictic split
conditioned by whether the SPEAKER is physically at the home in question. p.92.

### Unit 6, Grammar §10 — Hány éves? ("how old"): van is omitted

"Huszonöt éves vagyok" (I am 25) — no `van` in either 1st/2nd or 3rd person age-statements, unlike
most other predicate-adjective constructions in this course. p.93.

### Unit 7, Grammar §1 — Sibilant-final verb stems take -ol/-el/-öl in 2sg

Stems ending in s/sz/z/dz take -ol/-el/-öl instead of standard -sz in 2nd-person-singular (olvasol,
keresel, főzöl) — this is what makes `iszik` irregular (Unit 6). p.103.

### Unit 7, Grammar §2 — The "-ik" verb class (1sg -om/-em/-öm; explicit colloquial/"uneducated" note)

Verbs whose 3sg form ends in -ik take -om/-em/-öm (not standard -ok/-ek/-ök) in 1sg: lakom, verekedem,
öltözködöm. **The source explicitly states that some colloquial speakers substitute the standard
-ok/-ek/-ök ending instead (lakok, verekedek), but flags this as sounding "uneducated" with most such
verbs** — a directly source-annotated colloquial-vs-standard register contrast operating on an entire
inflectional class, not a single lexical item. See Notable findings. p.104.

### Unit 7, Grammar §3-§5 — Mixed verb classes; Ez/az + colloquial article-insertion; kis/kicsi

Some verbs combine multiple irregular classes (dolgozik: -ik + sibilant-final). `Ez`/`az` used
predicatively take NO indefinite article ("Ez templom"), but **the source notes some speakers
colloquially insert `egy` anyway ("Ez egy templom")** — a second explicit colloquial-deviation note
in the same grammar section as §2's -ik-verb note. `kis` is attributive-only; `kicsi` can stand alone
or attributively; `egy kicsit` = "a bit." pp.104-105.

### Unit 7, Grammar §6-§8 — Sem; the -n ending (superessive); time expressions -n vs. -ban/-ben

`sem` ("neither/not either") follows the word it scopes over, like `is`. The **-n** ending (on/at,
static location) does NOT reliably map to English "on" — must be memorized per-word alongside -ban/
-ben. Days of the week take **-n** (except vasárnap, the lone exception with no suffix); months take
**-ban/-ben**. Names of days/months are lowercase in Hungarian. pp.105-107.

### Unit 8, Grammar §1-§2 — -ít/two-consonant stems (linking vowel); Ír-class (i/í-only back-vowel harmony)

Stems ending in -ít or two consonants need a linking vowel before consonant-initial endings (takarít→
takarítok). Words containing only i/í (ír, iszik) take BACK-vowel endings despite i/í being phonetically
front vowels — a genuine vowel-harmony exception, explaining `iszik`'s Unit 6 irregularity. pp.119-120.

### Unit 8, Grammar §3-§4 — The infinitive (-ni; 7 irregular sz-stem verbs); Tud ("can/know")

Infinitive = stem + -ni, with linking-vowel and irregular exceptions. A closed class of 7 irregular
sz-stem verbs has irregular infinitives: lesz→lenni, vesz→venni, hisz→hinni, visz→vinni, tesz→tenni,
eszik→enni, iszik→inni. `tud` means both "know" and "can," EXCEPT with lát/hall/ért (see/hear/
understand), which express ability without `tud` at all. p.120.

### Unit 8, Grammar §5-§7 — The -ra/-re ending (sublative); the "-i" adjective suffix; És optionality

**-ra/-re** is the motion-to sister of the static -n ending (Unit 7 §7) — same governing-noun class,
also used for meal-destination phrasing ("Mi van reggelire?") and habitual-activity destinations
("edzésre jár"). The productive **-i** suffix derives adjectives from many nouns (mai, közeli,
családi; the four compass points nyugati/keleti/északi/déli; days/months/parts-of-day; irregular
forms heti, messzi, havi, esti). Hungarian doesn't require "and" before a list's last item. pp.120-121.

### Unit 9, Grammar §1-§2 — Negative questions (pragmatics); már/még in present tense

Negative questions are more common in Hungarian than English and usually carry NO extra surprise
connotation (unlike English) — positive answers to them always begin with **de**; when a negative
question DOES carry genuine surprise, a contradicting positive answer instead begins with
**dehogynem**. már/még (Unit 6 §3) extend fully into present-tense use: már="already", már nem="not
any more", még="still", még nem="not yet". pp.132-133.

### Unit 9, Grammar §3-§6 — Hanem vs. de; Hányan? (personal-count numerals); Minden; the a-/ah- relative system

`hanem` contradicts a negative clause; `de` merely elaborates. **Hányan?** ("how many people?")
triggers a distinct -an/-en numeral-inflection class used only for counting PEOPLE (egyedül, ketten,
hárman... tizenketten, húszan, ezren — irregular forms bolded); **sokan/kevesen/néhányan/páran**
("many/few/a few/a couple of people") always take plural verb agreement, unlike their non-personal
counterparts sok/kevés/néhány/pár (singular agreement) — a genuine number-agreement-conditioned
quantifier doublet. `minden`+noun takes singular agreement; minden-compounds (mindenki, mindenhol,
etc.) are systematic. Non-question-word relative pronouns/adverbs are formed by prefixing a-/ah- onto
the corresponding question word (aki, ami, ahol, ahova, ahonnan) — a fully general, productive
derivational mechanism. pp.133-135.

### Unit 10, Grammar §1 — Telling the time

24-hour "official" time for TV/radio/timetables; 12-hour time in everyday speech. **Quarter/half
hours are reckoned relative to the UPCOMING hour** (a genuinely non-English conceptual structure, not
just a vocabulary difference): negyed hat (5:15, "a quarter of six"), fél hat (5:30, "half of six"),
háromnegyed hat (5:45, "three-quarters of six"). Add **-kor** for "at [time]"; the final -a does NOT
lengthen to -á before -kor (an exception to the general á/é-lengthening rule). pp.144-145.

### Unit 10, Grammar §2-§3 — Postpositions; the -val/-vel ending

Hungarian has postpositions (word-order mirror-image of English prepositions), grouped into place,
time, and abstract-idea categories (full inventories given — see Vocabulary table). **-val/-vel**
("with"/sometimes "by") attaches directly to vowel-final stems, but its `v` FULLY ASSIMILATES to the
final consonant of a consonant-final stem (cukorral, tejjel, busszal) — a genuine total-assimilation
rule distinct from every other ending in this course, which merely select an allomorph rather than
assimilate outright. pp.145-148.

### Unit 10, Grammar §4-§5 — Hányas? (numeral-adjective suffix); the -lak/-lek ending

**-as/-es/-os** derives numeral-adjectives (Hányas?) used for transport routes, coin/banknote
denominations, phone extensions, and flat numbers — a distinct system from Unit 9's personal -an/-en
counting suffix. **-lak/-lek** is a portmanteau verb suffix marking 1st-person-singular SUBJECT
together with 2nd-person-singular-familiar OBJECT in one morpheme (látlak, szeretlek) — usable only
in familiar address with transitive verbs; the object pronoun `téged` is usually omitted (only kept
for emphasis), but the PLURAL object pronouns `titeket`/`benneteket` must be kept, since the bare
-lak/-lek suffix otherwise defaults to implying a singular addressee. pp.148-149.

### Unit 10, Grammar §6 — Mozi/színház definite-article usage

Unlike English's obligatory "the cinema/the theatre," Hungarian mozi/színház take the definite
article ONLY when a particular cinema/theatre is meant — except in the fixed phrase "Mi megy a
moziban/színházban?" ("What's on...?"), which always takes the article. p.150.

### Unit 11, Grammar §1 — THE DEFINITE CONJUGATION (present tense I) — the central grammar point of this dispatch

Hungarian has two parallel present-tense (and past/future) conjugations: **indefinite** (already
covered piecemeal across Units 2,3,6,7,8) and **definite**, with no English equivalent — only the
Hungarian verb form marks whether its object is definite. Full definite paradigm given for three
vowel-harmony classes (tudom/tudod/tudja/tudjuk/tudjátok/tudják; kérem/kéred/kéri/kérjük/kéritek/
kérik; köszönöm/köszönöd/köszöni/köszönjük/köszönitek/köszönik), with a spelling-vs-pronunciation note
(written dj/nj pronounced ggy/nny). **Indefinite** is used for intransitive verbs and transitive verbs
with an indefinite/no object (egy+noun, no article + non-named referent, or an infinitive object).
**Definite** is used for transitive verbs with a definite object (definite article, a named
person/place, or ez/az). Question words kit/mit/hány/hányat/milyen/milyent (no -ik) always trigger
indefinite; melyik/melyiket/hányadik/hányadikat (containing -ik) always trigger definite — the
question word's own morphological shape, not just its meaning, decides the conjugation. Definite
conjugation is ALSO required when a 3rd-person object is implied but unstated ("Ismerem" = "I know
him," no separate pronoun). The verb **köszön** actually CHANGES MEANING between conjugations:
indefinite = "greet/say goodbye to," definite = "thank" — the most extreme semantic stake attached to
this distinction found in the course. pp.161-163.

### Unit 11, Grammar §2-§3 — Verb-governed endings; the dative -nak/-nek ending

Many verbs obligatorily require a specific case ending that often does NOT map onto the "equivalent"
English preposition (kezdődik+val/vel = "start WITH," matching English; beszél+val/vel = "speak TO,"
NOT matching). Some verbs take different endings for different senses (hisz vmit "believe sg" vs.
hisz vmiben "believe IN sg"). The **dative -nak/-nek** ending (full paradigm given, including the
closed personal-pronoun set nekem/neked/magának/önnek/neki/nekünk/nektek/maguknak/önöknek/nekik)
marks the recipient of an object, and is also required after a closed list of common verbs
(köszön/telefonál/ízlik/áll/megy/tetszik/örül/segít/fáj/hívnak) regardless of whether there's a
separate object. pp.163-165.

### Unit 11, Grammar §4-§7 — Question tags; extra definite-article uses; pro-drop; quantity expressions

**ugye** (after positive or negative statements) and **nem** (positive statements only) turn a
statement into a tag question; **ugye** can also OPEN a question to specifically request reassurance.
The definite article a/az is required in several Hungarian-specific contexts beyond English usage:
abstract nouns, street names, meal names, and generic statements using a singular noun where English
uses a plural. Hungarian freely omits a repeated subject noun/pronoun once context establishes the
referent (a named pro-drop rule, not just incidental brevity). Container/measure-quantity expressions
have no equivalent of English "of" (egy csokor virág, egy doboz csoki, egy pohár víz, etc.).
pp.165-166.

## Notable findings (colloquial/register/period vocabulary)

Per this project's specific interest in informal/colloquial-register material and slang-formation
mechanisms, the following are flagged from this extraction:

1. **The `-ik`-verb 1sg-ending colloquial/"uneducated" register note (Unit 7 Grammar §2, p.104)** —
   the single highest-value GRAMMATICAL (not lexical) register finding in this dispatch: the source
   states outright that substituting the standard -ok/-ek/-ök ending for the required -om/-em/-öm on
   -ik verbs "sounds uneducated" in colloquial speech "with most verbs." This is a register contrast
   operating on an entire productive inflectional class, not a single word pair — structurally
   distinct from (and arguably more valuable than) lexical pairs like krumpli/burgonya from 034.
2. **The colloquial indefinite-article-insertion note for ez/az predicatives (Unit 7 Grammar §4,
   p.104)** — a second explicit colloquial-deviation-from-the-rule note in the same unit ("Ez egy
   templom" instead of prescriptive "Ez templom"), worth flagging alongside finding #1 as a pattern:
   this course is unusually willing to name specific colloquial deviations from its own prescriptive
   rules, not just flag individual slang words.
3. **`foci`/`focizik`, `troli`, `deka`, `matek`, `csoki`, `krimi` — a recurring clipping/shortening
   word-formation pattern** confirmed across five separate units (foci from futball, Unit 8; troli
   from trolibusz, 034 file Unit 5; deka from dekagram, 034 file Unit 4; matek from matematika, Unit
   9; csoki from csokoládé, confirmed independently in Unit 10's Practice AND in Unit 11 Grammar §7's
   own worked example "egy doboz csokoládé/csoki," making csoki the single most cross-confirmed
   colloquial clipping in this whole extraction; krimi likely from "krimineller/criminal"). This is
   now well-established as a genuinely productive Hungarian slang/informal-register word-formation
   strategy worth flagging prominently for the mechanics-analysis phase.
4. **The courtesy-address system using `tetszik`+infinitive (Unit 9 culture note, pp.128-129)** — a
   full THIRD address register (alongside te/ti familiar and maga/ön formal from 034 file's Unit 1),
   used by children toward adults, adults toward the elderly, and some men toward women. Has no
   dedicated pronoun of its own — instead borrows néni/bácsi (the same forms used for the Unit 6
   diminutive-name affection system) as generic terms of address for any adult stranger. Asymmetric
   response rules (children always respond familiar; women addressed this way respond formal) and a
   gender-conditioned exception on Csókolom/Kezét csókolom (a woman rarely uses these toward an old
   man) make this one of the richest sociolinguistic systems documented across the whole Hungarian
   corpus so far.
5. **The nameday (névnap) tradition and its ear-pulling couplet (Unit 11 culture note, pp.158-159)**
   — "Isten éltessen sokáig, a füled érjen bokáig!" ("May God grant you a long life, and may your
   ears reach your ankles!"), recited while pulling the celebrated person's ear. A genuine attested
   piece of Hungarian folk verbal tradition/humor, distinct from (and complementary to) the ticket-
   inspector cartoon joke already flagged in 034 file as tone/register reference material.
6. **`köszön`'s conjugation-conditioned meaning-switch (Unit 11 Grammar §1, p.163)** — indefinite
   conjugation = "greet/say goodbye to," definite conjugation = "thank." The single most striking
   grammar-meaning interaction found in this dispatch: a whole verb's LEXICAL MEANING (not just
   subtle emphasis) hinges on which of the two parallel conjugation systems is used.
7. **The félszoba ("half-room") real-estate counting convention (Unit 7 culture note, p.102)** — a
   culturally-specific fractional-numeral idiom baked into ordinary housing vocabulary (másfél szobás
   lakás = one big room + one small room), paralleling the courtesy-address and nameday systems as a
   genuinely non-obvious, culturally-specific convention worth flagging for mechanics-analysis of how
   ordinary numeral/measurement systems get extended into idiomatic domain-specific uses.
8. **Foot-size (not shoe-size) and singular-only clothing nouns (Unit 11 culture note, p.159)** — two
   compact but genuine structural quirks: Hungarians "have" a shoe SIZE the way they "have" a foot
   (Negyvenes lábam van), and any two-part or English-plural clothing item (nadrág, szemüveg, zokni,
   etc.) is grammatically singular in Hungarian — a systematic count-noun mismatch across a whole
   semantic domain.
9. **Period-situated (2003) consumer-aspiration and gender-stereotype content**, found independently
   in three separate places: the "nyugati kocsi" (Western car) motif recurring across a 13-year-old's
   aspirations (Unit 8 dialogue) AND a small child's (Unit 8 Practice, explicitly framed by the book's
   own reflection question as a marker of being a "modern child"); the Unit 11 dialogue's closing line
   ("every woman is pleased by flowers and likes chocolate, doesn't she?"); and the Unit 11 Practice
   gift-preference exercise's gendered/generational stereotype list (pálinka for an old man, parfüm
   for a young woman, etc.). None of these are editorialized on by the source itself — they are
   presented as ordinary exemplar sentences — making them useful as unselfconscious period-attested
   social-attitude data rather than deliberately marked register content.
10. **The candid "why there are so many fat people in Hungary" closing line (Unit 8, p.125)** — a
    genuinely blunt, borderline-impolite editorial aside the textbook makes about its own culture's
    eating/lifestyle habits, worth flagging as a tone/register data point alongside the "average
    Hungarian prefers watching sport to doing it" and "Hungarian lifestyle can be rather unhealthy"
    asides in the same unit — this course is unusually willing to be self-deprecating about
    contemporary Hungarian life, distinct in tone from the neutral/descriptive register of most of
    034's Units 1-5 culture notes.
11. **No `[B]`/`[C]`/`[S]`-style dialectal tags or comparative regional-variant annotations** appear
    anywhere in this page range, consistent with 034's finding — this remains a single-dialect
    (standard Budapest-area spoken) course throughout. `csárda` (034 file) remains the only
    `regional`-tagged term found in this book so far.

No handwritten marginalia, library stamps, or non-print annotation of any kind were found anywhere in
this page range — this continues to be a clean modern scan, consistent with 034 and 038.

### Morpheme Breakdown — agglutinative forms and word-formation worth decomposing

- **`háromszobás`** ("three-roomed [apartment]") = `három` ("three") + `szoba` ("room") + `-s`
  (adjective-forming suffix indicating possession/inclusion, cf. `vicc` → `vicces` "funny") — Lesson
  19, a compact three-morpheme adjective built from a numeral + noun + derivational suffix.
- **`rendetlenség`** ("disorder, mess") = `rend` ("order," already known from Lesson 8) + `-tlen`
  (privative/"without" suffix) + `-ség` (abstract-noun suffix) — the book's own worked derivation
  example (Lesson 33), tracing a concrete noun through two suffixation steps to an abstract one; the
  intermediate form `rendetlen` ("disorderly," adjective) is the bridge step.
- **`bölcsészkar`** ("faculty of arts") = `bölcsész` (bound form related to `bölcs`, "wise/sage") +
  `kar` ("choir; body of people working together" — NOT the homophonous `kar` "arm," a false-friend
  trap the source explicitly warns about, Lesson 33).
- **`zenekar`** ("orchestra") = `zene` ("music") + `kar` ("choir," same `kar` as above, not "arm") —
  Lesson 33, paired with the `bölcsészkar` example as the same head-morpheme.
- **`csillaggá`** ("into a star," result of a transformation) = `csillag` ("star") + `-vá/-vé`
  (translative/resultative suffix showing complete assimilation to the following consonant: `-vá` →
  `-ggá` after `-g`) — Lesson 31, from the Petőfi-poem-adjacent dialogue "akkor én csillaggá
  változom" (then I will change into a star).
- **`libalibalimlomlombzatibombom`**-style nonsense refrain — Lesson 35's quoted folk-song fragment
  deliberately violates vowel-harmony and morphological rules for comic/rhythmic effect ("no English
  girls would sing this in a folksong... the English can trust in what a Hungarian never knows for
  sure" — source's own tongue-in-cheek framing); worth flagging as a rare explicit example of
  *intentional* rule-breaking in folk material, distinct from productive agglutination.
- **`önéletrajz`** ("autobiography") = `ön` ("self") + `élet` ("life") + `rajz` ("drawing/sketch") —
  Lesson 34, a three-root compound; source also warns of false-decomposition traps in similar-looking
  words like `padló` ("floor," historically from `pad` + `ló` "bench + horse" but now opaque) and
  `tanuló` ("pupil," from `tanul` "learn" + agentive `-ó`, not a `ló` "horse" compound at all).
- **`szeretlek`** ("I love you") = `szeret-` (root "love") + `-lek` (fused 1st-person-subject +
  2nd-person-singular-object suffix) — the object pronoun is not a separate word but a portmanteau
  suffix; Lesson 17 note 4, cross-referenced with the general subject+object-suffix rule extended in
  Lesson 21-22 (`-lak/-lek` whenever the subject is "I" and the object is "you," singular or plural).

`lenni` (be), `enni` (eat) are given side by side. Confirms that personal pronouns (én, te, ő, mi, ti,
ők) are typically omitted because the verb suffix already encodes person — already documented
elsewhere, but this source's explicit "four irregular verbs" framing (rather than presenting them
piecemeal) is a clean pedagogical consolidation.

### Lesson 8 — Preverbs (verbal prefixes) introduced implicitly; -ba/-be vs. -ból/-ből as a
### positive/negative direction pair

Frames the -ba/-be (into), -ra/-re (onto), -hoz/-hez/-höz (to/towards) suffixes as marking *positive*
direction and their mirror-image -ból/-ből (out of), -ról/-ről (off of), -tól/-től (away from) as
*negative* direction — paired systematically for the first time in this project's Hungarian corpus.
Includes two idiomatic illustrations worth flagging as genuine colloquial content: **"kutyából nem
lesz szalonna"** (lit. "there will be no bacon out of a dog" — you can't make a silk purse out of a
sow's ear) and **"derült égből mennykőcsapás"** (a thunderbolt out of a clear sky — a bolt from the
blue), both given as negative-direction (-ból/-ből) example idioms in a bank-robbery comic scene, p. 26.

### Lesson 14 — Preverbs as a systematic word-formation category; the three-column direction table

The single most valuable *new* grammar consolidation in this dispatch: preverbs (`le-`, `fel-`,
`be-`, `ki-`, `meg-`, etc.) are explicitly framed as productive word-formation elements that attach to
a base verb and can shift its meaning unpredictably — directly analogous to English phrasal verbs.
Worked example: `csinál` ("do/make") → `megcsinál` (get ready/fix), `becsinál` (make a mess in one's
trousers — a vulgar/comic register note the source includes deliberately), `kicsinál` (pre-arrange;
also slang for "kill"), `felcsinál` (get [someone] pregnant — flagged by the source itself as a
risqué double meaning). A clean three-column suffix table (WHERE TO? / WHERE? / WHENCE?) consolidates
-ba/-be & -ra/-re & -hoz/-hez/-höz (positive direction) against -ban/-ben & -on/-en/-ön & -nál/-nél
(static position) against -ból/-ből & -ról/-ről & -tól/-től (negative direction) — the clearest single
tabular presentation of this system encountered across the Hungarian corpus so far.

### Lessons 15-16, 21-22, 25 — Definite ("objective") vs. indefinite ("subjective") conjugation,
### taught incrementally

Rather than presenting the definite/indefinite conjugation distinction as a single grammar point (as
Rounds does), Assimil deliberately staggers it: first flagged conceptually in Lesson 15 (a verb with a
direct object, or one implied by context, takes "objective" conjugation), then the full present-tense
paradigm contrast is drilled in Lesson 21 side by side (subjective `látom/látod/látja/látjuk/látjátok/
látják` vs. corresponding indefinite forms), with the special portmanteau -lak/-lek suffix (subject
"I," object "you") introduced separately in Lessons 17 and 22. The past tense (Lesson 28) and
conditional (Lesson 35) both replicate the same definite/indefinite split. This pedagogical
sequencing — concept, then paradigm, then tense-by-tense repetition — is itself worth noting for the
mechanics-analysis phase as a demonstrated *learnability* ordering distinct from prior sources'
single-pass presentations.

### Lesson 28 (Revision) — Past tense, full paradigm (both conjugations)

Full subjective and objective past-tense paradigms for `vár` (wait) and `kér` (ask), plus the
irregular past of `lenni` (voltam/voltál/volt/voltunk/voltatok/voltak). The past-tense suffix always
contains -t-, doubled after a vowel in the 3rd-person singular only (`Ki látott engem?` — Who saw
me?). Also gives the full imperative paradigm (subjective and objective) for regular verbs and for
`lenni` (legyek/légy/legyen/legyünk/legyetek/legyenek).

### Lesson 35 — Conditional mood, full paradigm (present); "would" constructions

Full subjective (`szeretnék/szeretnél/szeretne/szeretnénk/szeretnétek/szeretnének`, `várnék/várnál/
várna/…`) and objective (`szeretném/szeretnéd/szeretné/…`, `várnám/várnád/várná/…`) conditional
paradigms. Irregular conditionals flagged: `lenni` → `lennék/lenne` or `volnék/volna` (two
co-existing forms), `menni` → `mennék`, `enni` → `ennék`, `tenni` → `tennék`, `vinni` → `vinnék`. The
lesson also quotes (with an explicit composition date and the poet's exact age) a short poem by
**József Attila**, written in 1916 at age eleven — "De szeretnék gazdag lenni, / Egyszer libasültet
enni..." ("I would so like to be rich, / For once to eat roast goose...") — again supplying a precise
`attested_era` anchor (1916) independent of the book's own 1996 publication date, and naming a real
historical confectioner (Kugler Henrik, 1830-1904, whose Pest shop is referenced in the poem and
still exists today as Gerbeaud on Vörösmarty tér, Budapest) as explanatory cultural context.

### Lesson 35 §2 — Future tense, full paradigm

The only Hungarian verb with a dedicated future conjugation is `lenni` (leszek/leszel/lesz/leszünk/
lesztek/lesznek). All other verbs form an emphatic/intentional future periphrastically with the
auxiliary `fog` + infinitive (`írni fogok` — I will write), placed between the preverb and the verb
stem when the verb has one (`meg fogjuk magyarázni` — we will explain it). An unstressed/non-emphatic
future is usually just the present tense plus a time adverbial (`majd megírom` — I'll write it
[later]).

### Lesson 36 — Reflexive verbs and paired body-part singularization

Hungarian reflexive pronouns (`magam/magad/maga` + possessive suffix, e.g. `magamnak` "to myself") are
introduced via a gym-class scene ("másnap nem érezzük jobban magunkat" — we don't feel better [about
ourselves] the next day). Reiterates (from earlier lessons) that paired body parts are grammatically
singular in Hungarian — "nem egyszeműek, félkarúak, féllábúak vagyunk" (we are not a nation of
one-eyed, one-armed, one-legged people), an explicit meta-joke about the grammatical singular being
mistaken for anatomical fact.

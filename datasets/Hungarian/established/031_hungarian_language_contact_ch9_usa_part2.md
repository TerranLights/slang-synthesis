### Morpheme breakdown

> **fiútesvérem** (AH) = fiú ("boy") + testvér ("sibling") + em (1SG possessive) = "my brother" —
> the source's central worked example of marked-vocabulary replacement in the kinship domain. Standard
> Hungarian would use the sex-*and*-relative-age-marked root **bátyám** ("my older brother," from
> *bátya*) or **öcsém** ("my younger brother," from *öcs*) directly, with no separate compounding
> needed. AH speakers instead build the sex distinction compositionally onto the
> sex-neutral-but-age-neutral root *testvér*, then add a *separate* free adjective (*idősebb* "older"
> or *fiatalabb* "younger") when relative age needs to be specified — trading a single synthetic,
> fully lexicalized form for a two-word analytic strategy that maps transparently onto English
> "brother"/"older brother." This is presented by the source as a genuine instance of language contact
> restructuring not just borrowed vocabulary but the *shape* of a whole semantic subsystem (kinship
> terminology), driven by the "markedness" of the HH system relative to English rather than by any
> single borrowed word. pp. 332–333

### 14. Vázsonyi's American Hungarian dictionary: scope and part-of-speech distribution

Vázsonyi (1995) — based on 1960s ethnographic interviews Andrew Vázsonyi and folklorist Linda Dégh
recorded in the Calumet region southeast of Chicago — contains 900 entries (978 counting words with
multiple part-of-speech senses), overwhelmingly nouns (640, 71.1%) and verbs (202, 22.4%), with
smaller counts of adjectives, adverbs, and a handful of pronouns/numerals/particles/interjections/
fixed phrases. This part-of-speech distribution closely mirrors Virtaranta's (1992) comparable
American Finnish dictionary (78.1% nouns, 13.0% verbs) — the chapter treats this cross-linguistic
similarity as suggestive evidence that nominal borrowing dominance is a general property of
long-term immigrant-language lexical contact, not a Hungarian-specific pattern. The dictionary is
overwhelmingly loanwords, with only ~30 loanblends and just 3 calques as entries — the source
explicitly flags that calques are inherently harder to notice in a contact variety (their form being
fully native) and this likely biased the dictionary's own compilation method toward under-recording
them relative to their true frequency. pp. 325–326

### 15. Three-way lexical borrowing typology and phonological/morphological adaptation systematicity

The chapter distinguishes **loanwords** (English form + meaning both borrowed), **loanblends**
(typically compounds pairing one borrowed English element with one native Hungarian element, copying
an English compound's structure), and **calques** (native Hungarian form, but either a
loan-translation of an English compound or — the chapter's broader definition — an existing native
word acquiring a new sense under English influence). The phonological adaptation of loanwords is
shown to be remarkably systematic rather than ad hoc: a full inventory of regular phoneme-substitution
rules is documented (interdental fricatives /θ,ð/ → dental stops or fricatives depending on context;
the velar nasal /ŋ/ → /ng/ since Hungarian has no phonemic velar nasal; /w/ → /v/; a spelling-driven
`<c>` → Hungarian /ts/ substitution that cannot be explained on purely phonetic grounds; /æ/ → the
Hungarian low-mid front vowel /ɛ/ or, via spelling pronunciation, /a/; systematic diphthong
nativization, generally to Hungarian long monophthongs; and even an allophone-level substitution,
the American English intervocalic tap realized as [d]). Morphological adaptation is similarly
systematic: virtually all borrowed verbs obligatorily receive a "thematizing" suffix (-l or -z, with
a connecting vowel if needed) — a near-exceptionless rule with only one documented exception in the
entire dictionary — and a productive subset of loans undergo further native derivation (adjective-
forming -s, verb-forming -kodik/-kedik/-ködik 'behave in the manner of', adverbial suffixes, and
preverb-prefixation, some preverbs purely aspectual and others carrying real adverbial meaning). pp.
326–330

### 16. Code-switching: documented but under-analyzed by the source studies themselves

The chapter is candid that none of its underlying source studies (Kontra 1990, Bartha 1993, Fenyvesi
1995a) carried out a rigorous functional/structural analysis of AH code-switching — each used only a
basic working definition to distinguish switching from borrowing (Kontra and Fenyvesi: pronunciation
with the speaker's normal English accent counts as switching, Hungarian-accented pronunciation of an
English-origin word counts as borrowing; Bartha: frequency of occurrence distinguishes one-off
switches from established loans). A further methodological caveat: because subjects knew they were
being interviewed by linguists from Hungary, this "bilingual mode" activation (per Grosjean's
1997/2001 framework — knowing the interlocutor is bilingual activates both languages, producing more
switching than either a fully monolingual or fully unguarded bilingual conversational setting would)
likely shaped the amount and kind of switching observed, in directions the source doesn't fully
control for. Word-, phrase-, and clause-level switches are all documented, including fixed
recurrent switched phrases (e.g. "wait a minute" mid-Hungarian-sentence), switches filling apparent
lexical gaps (topic-specific English vocabulary like "soap opera," "the programs"), and switches
that are direct quotations of discourse that originally occurred in English (reported speech
naturally retaining its original language). pp. 332–335

### 17. The T/V address system: erosion tracks fluency, not just generation

The chapter's most detailed pragmatic finding: HH's formal (V, via *ön*/*maga* + 3rd-person verb, or
*tetszik* + infinitive) vs. informal (T) address dichotomy erodes specifically among what the chapter
terms **"semi-speakers"** (second-generation speakers who don't use Hungarian regularly with anyone,
even a similarly-limited spouse) as distinct from **"fluent speakers"** (speakers, whether
first-generation or second-generation, who actually use Hungarian in daily conversation and can
introspect about their own T/V usage). Semi-speakers show genuinely novel combinations impossible in
HH — one subject used the informal pronoun *te* together with formal 3rd-person verb forms
simultaneously, a combination the source explicitly calls an "impossible co-occurrence in HH"; others
used the formal pronoun *maga* only in its plain nominative form while substituting ordinary
3rd-person declined pronouns (*vele*, *hozzá*) for its own declined forms, and some had genuine
difficulty parsing who a formal 3rd-person-verb question referred to, producing real communicative
misfires (illustrated with a short transcript exchange where the fieldworker's formal-address question
about the subject herself was misunderstood as being about her father). The chapter connects this to
a parallel, independently-documented pattern among bilingual Germans in New Zealand (Stoffel 1983):
immigrant-community address systems shift toward simplification (fewer formal-register options, higher
tolerance for receiving an "inappropriate" address form) regardless of the specific languages
involved — suggesting a general immigrant-bilingualism effect on address systems, not something
Hungarian- or German-specific. Fluent speakers, unlike semi-speakers, retain full command of the T/V
distinction but report shifting from formal to informal address with new acquaintances faster in the
US than they would in Hungary itself — a genuine pragmatic-norm shift among speakers who have not lost
the grammatical distinction, only recalibrated when to deploy it. pp. 333–337

### 18. Chapter's own conclusion: an incomplete but representative picture

The chapter's closing self-assessment: the overall Hungarian-American case fits the classic
three-generation immigrant-language-shift model (in fact completing somewhat faster, per Grammar
point 5 in Part 1 — no second-generation-plus retention has been documented at all), but the
underlying source studies all specifically targeted the oldest, most working-class,
socioeconomically homogeneous communities (South Bend, Detroit, McKeesport, Toledo), while newer or
more socioeconomically diverse communities — the chapter specifically flags New Brunswick, New Jersey,
with its Rutgers-based educated elite and more active community institutions, per Bartha's (1999)
preliminary indication — might show meaningfully better language-maintenance prospects and less
linguistically eroded Hungarian. No comprehensive published study of such a community existed at the
time of writing. p. 338

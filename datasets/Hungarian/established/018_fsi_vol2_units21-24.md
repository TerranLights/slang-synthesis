# Hungarian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: FSI Basic Hungarian, Vol. 2, Units 21–24

**Source:** Augustus A. Koski & Ilona Mihályfy, *Hungarian Basic Course* (Foreign Service
Institute, Washington D.C., 1964), Volume 2, Student Text, Units 21–24 (final four units of the
volume). Printed pages 455–559 (PDF pages 198–302 of the "FSI Hungarian Basic Course - Volume 2 -
Student Text.pdf" file; PDF page = printed page − 257, verified empirically against the volume's own
table of contents and running-head page numbers, not assumed from a fixed offset formula). The
volume's cumulative Hungarian-English Word List (printed pp. 562–606, PDF 305–349) was also
inspected — see **Back matter** note below.

**Unit boundaries (per the source's own table of contents, PDF pp. 6–7):**
- Unit 21, "Hol Fekszik Magyarország?" ("Where Is Hungary Located?") — pp. 455–479
- Unit 22, "Kultúrélet Budapesten" ("Cultural Life in Budapest") — pp. 481–508
- Unit 23, "Államigazgatási Szervek Magyarországon" ("State Administrative Bodies in Hungary") —
  pp. 511–538
- Unit 24, "Magyarország Gazdasági Élete" ("Hungary's Economic Life") — pp. 541–559

**Coverage note.** Every distinct vocabulary item glossed in each unit's Basic Sentences, Notes on
Grammar, and (where present) closing Narrative is captured below, plus every grammar point. Per the
coverage rule, the highly repetitive Substitution/Transformation/Variation drill sentences are *not*
transcribed verbatim — they mechanically re-apply grammar already captured from the Basic Sentences
and Notes on Grammar — but any drill or Vocabulary Drill section that introduced new lexical items or
a distinct construction (e.g. Unit 21's `való`/`levő` Vocabulary Drill, Unit 22's `tetszik`/`izlik`
idiomatic-verb Vocabulary Drill, Unit 23's frequentative-verb Variation Drill, Unit 24's idiom-heavy
Transformation Drills) was read in full and its vocabulary/constructions are included. This is a
clean, real text-layer PDF (`pdftotext` produces usable, if lightly OCR-noisy, text); no vision
reading was required. A handful of OCR artifacts (misrecognized accented vowels, stray characters)
were normalized against context; none were handwritten marginalia — this is a clean library/archival
scan of a printed government training text, not a used copy, so the marginalia-guard did not apply.

**Register/colloquial findings (see also the callouts inline below).** This is the strongest
register-relevant content found in the Hungarian corpus so far: Unit 22 devotes its entire grammar
section to reviewing the **familiar (tegező) address paradigm** — the informal "te/ti" register as
opposed to the polite "maga" forms used throughout the rest of the course — introduced through the
Basic Sentences' own switch to `szervusz` and tegeződés between two young male friends (contrasted
explicitly with `Isten hozott` and `Bocsáss meg` as colloquial greeting/apology idioms in that same
familiar register). Unit 22 also introduces the **-lak/-lek** verb suffix, which is inherently a
familiar-register form (it fuses "I ... you[-familiar]" and can only be used with someone one
tegez). Unit 24's entire Notes on Grammar section is explicitly framed by the source itself as
covering **idiomatic/figurative expressions** ("expressions... whose structural patterns do not
correspond to the English structural frames," source's own words, p. 548) — this is a rich cluster of
verb-particle idioms (`beleun`, `beleszeret`, `belebetegszik`, `magához tér`, `nekifog`, `mi
járatban van`, `lemenőben van`, `sokba kerül`, `rossz szemmel néz`, `szaván fog`) that the source
treats as a distinct, higher register of fluency beyond "fundamental patterns" — see the Morpheme
Breakdown and Grammar points sections below.

**Morphological typology note.** Hungarian is agglutinative (see `../00_Extraction_Checklist.md`).
This chunk is unusually rich in *verb-particle/preverb idioms* (`bele-`, `neki-`, `el-` + fixed
case-marked complement) rather than simple noun-suffix stacking — several vocabulary entries below
carry a Morpheme Breakdown for this reason, as an atomic English gloss would hide the
compositional (and often etymologically transparent, if synchronically idiomatic) structure.

> **olvasnivaló / mondanivaló / innivaló / ennivaló / tanulnivaló / látnivaló / írnivaló /

> ("being, suited for") → a productive gerundive/purposive nominalization meaning "(something)
> to X." Source's own full paradigm from the `való` Vocabulary Drill, category 2 "something to do."
> Illustrates how `való` (itself analyzed in Grammar §21C as a participial form of van) is
> reanalyzed as a bound derivational suffix once fused onto an infinitive. Unit 21 p. 474

> **ismerősömhöz**-style causative pairs, e.g. **mosat / mosat / mos** = mos ("wash," root) +
> **-at** (causative) → "have (something) washed." Source's own reference paradigm (Grammar
> §21A) for the causative suffix `-tat/-tet` or `-at/-et`: added to a verb stem, it signals the
> subject causes the action rather than performing it — e.g. `Jánosruhát csináltat` "John has a
> suit made" (csinál "make" + tat "cause" = "has made"). See also the irregular -sz-stem class
> (itat ← iszik "drink" + at) and the -v- stem class (lövet ← lő "shoot" + v + et). Unit 21 p. 462–463

> **családostul** = család ("family") + **-ostul** (comitative "together with," vowel-harmony
> variant of -stul/-stül) — "with one's whole family." Source's own Grammar §23C(1) example,
> also used in Unit 22's Basic Sentences (`Gyere el családostul`). Unit 22 p. 483; Unit 23 p. 519

> **olvasgat / próbálgat / hallgat (← hall) / látogat (← lát)** = verb root + **-gat/-get**
> (frequentative/iterative suffix, "-ogat/-eget/-öget" after most monosyllabic roots) — marks
> repeated, continuous, or desultory action, and in a closed set of cases shifts the meaning
> entirely (hall "hear" → hallgat "listen"; lát "see" → látogat "visit"). Source's own full
> reference list (Grammar §23B) also includes rarer frequentative suffixes -dogál/-degél/-dögél
> (üldögél ← ül "sit"), -gál/-gél (keresgél ← keres "search"), -kál (járkál ← jár "go/walk"),
> -ál/-él (dobál ← dob "throw"), and -kod/-ked/-köd (kapkod ← kap "grab"). This is a genuinely
> productive standard-language morphological pattern, not itself slang — but see the note at the
> top of this file re: distinguishing ordinary agglutinative productivity from
> slang-specific `morphological_play` per `00_Word_Concept_and_Morphological_Typology_Guide.md`.
> Unit 23 p. 518, 521

> **látlak** = lát ("see," verb stem) + **-lak** (fused 1sg-subject + 2sg-familiar-object suffix)
> — "I see you [familiar]." A single bound morpheme collapsing subject and object agreement that
> in the definite/indefinite paradigm elsewhere takes two separate slots; usable only when
> addressing someone the speaker tegez (familiar register) — see the register callout above and
> Grammar §22B. Unit 22 p. 489–490

> **eszébe jut** = ész ("mind, wits," possessed stem esz-) + **-é** (3sg possessive) + **-be**
> (illative "into") + jut ("arrives, gets to") — lit. "it arrives into his mind" = "it occurs to
> him, he remembers." The same possessive-suffixed noun + case-marked idiom pattern recurs in
> `eszébe juttatni` ("to remind," causative of jut). Unit 24 p. 549

> **belejön a munkába** = bele- (preverb, fused allative form of "into it") + jön ("comes") + a
> munkába (illative "into the work") — lit. "comes into the work" = "gets the hang of it." One of
> several **bele-** preverb idioms in this unit (beleun, belefárad, beleszeret, belebetegszik) where
> the historically transparent "into" preverb has been reanalyzed as part of a fixed idiomatic verb,
> each still governing an illative (-ba/-be) complement matching the preverb's original directional
> sense. Unit 24 p. 549, 556–557

> **nekifog / nekikezd** = neki- (preverb, fused dative/allative "to it/toward it," from a form of
> the 3rd-person dative pronoun neki) + fog ("catches, takes hold") / kezd ("begins") — lit. "takes
> hold toward it" / "begins toward it" = "sets about (a task)." Contrast **nekimegy** = neki- + megy
> ("goes") — lit. "goes toward/at" = "attacks, runs into" — same preverb, unrelated figurative
> outcome depending on the base verb, illustrating why the source treats these as idioms to be
> learned individually rather than a transparent compositional rule. Unit 24 p. 552–553

## Grammar points

### 21.A The Causative Form of Verbs

The suffix `-tat/-tet` (or, with most monosyllabic verbs and polysyllabic verbs ending in
consonant+t, `-at/-et`) marks that the subject causes the action to be done rather than performing
it: `János ruhát csináltat` "John has a suit made." Some verbs take both suffixes with a genuine
meaning split (`nyom` "press" → `nyomat` "have printed" vs. `nyomtat` "print"; `vág` "cut" →
`vágat` "have cut" vs. `vágtat` "gallop"; `folyik` "flows" → `folyat` "let flow" vs. `folytat`
"continue"). Irregular -sz-stem verbs (iszik "drink," eszik "eat," etc.) drop the sibilant before
the causative suffix (`iszik` → `itat` "give to drink, water [an animal]"); the lő/ró-class use a
-v- stem before the suffix (`lő` "shoot" → `lövet`). When the potential suffix `-hat/-het` co-occurs
with the causative, potential follows causative (`mosat` → `mosathat` "can have [it] washed"). p.
462–464

### 21.B Agent with the Causative of Verbs

The English "to have something done by someone" pattern is rendered by causative verb + the agent
marked with `-val/-vel`: `Varratok egy ruhát a szabóval` "I'm having a dress made by the tailor."
p. 464

### 21.C Való and Levő

Both correspond to English "being" (sometimes "becoming"), but with different distributions. `levő`
is always used in a participial phrase equivalent to an adjectival clause built on a form of `van`
("A könyv, ami a széken van" → "a széken levő könyv," "the book [being] on the chair"). `való` is
broader: it can mean "true," "for the purpose of," "consisting of," "pertaining to," and combines
with a preceding word/phrase to express origin (`Hova való?` "Where are you from?"), purpose (`Mire
való ez a doboz?` "What's this box for?"), or suitability (`Ez a munka nem nekem való` "This work
doesn't suit me"); it can also fuse onto an infinitive to form a gerundive noun (`borravaló` "tip,"
lit. "for-wine"; `ennivaló` "something to eat") — see the Morpheme Breakdown above for the full
`-nivaló` paradigm from the source's own Vocabulary Drill. p. 464, 474–475

### 21.D Szerinte / Véleménye Szerint

Synonymous expressions for "according to him/in his opinion," both taking a full personal-ending
paradigm (`szerintünk` "according to us" = `véleményünk szerint`). p. 464

### 21.E The Emphatic Prefixes

Many pronouns and postpositions with personal endings can be made emphatic by prefixing the
matching simple subject pronoun: `énnekem` (emphatic "to me"), `mimellettünk` (emphatic "beside
us"), `őtőle` (emphatic "from him/her" — note the 3rd-plural emphatic form is `ő`, not `ők`). p. 465

### 21.F Word Formation

A reference list of common Hungarian word-formation suffixes, organized by output category:
nouns-from-nouns (`-ás/-os/-es/-ös` agentive: `munka`→`munkás`; `-ság/-ség` collective:
`munkás`→`munkásság`; `-ász/-ész` occupational: `hal`→`halász`; `-at/-et` action-noun:
`halász`→`halászat`; `-zat/-zet`: `csillag`→`csillagzat`), nouns-from-adjectives (`-ság/-ség`:
`szép`→`szépség`), verbs-from-nouns (`-l/-ol/-el/-öl`: `szám`→`számol`; `-z`: `só`→`sóz`;
`-ász-/-ész-`: `hal`→`halászik`), verbs-from-adjectives (`-ít`: `szép`→`szépít`), and
adverbs-from-nouns — distributive/temporal `-nta/-nte` (`nyaranta` "every summer") vs. `-nként`
(`naponként` "day by day, per day"). p. 465–466

**Register note:** this word-formation list is presented as standard-language derivational
morphology, not slang, but it is directly relevant background for any later `morphological_play`
mechanics analysis of Hungarian slang — several of these same suffix classes (agentive `-ász/-ész`,
collective `-ság/-ség`) are exactly the kind of productive machinery slang coinages would either
follow or deliberately violate.

### 22.A Review of Familiar (Tegező) Forms — register-relevant

This section is a full paradigm review (pronoun forms, postposition/case-suffix forms, the
possessive "your"/"yours," and present/past/conditional/subjunctive verb endings) for the
**familiar 2nd-person singular and plural** ("te/ti," informal "you") — as opposed to the polite
"maga/maguk" forms drilled everywhere else in the course. This is the clearest explicit
register-paradigm content found in the Hungarian corpus to date: the entire course up to this point
has drilled the *polite* register almost exclusively (consistent with a diplomatic-training course
aimed at adult professional interlocutors), and this unit is the first to formally introduce the
familiar register — motivated in-context by the Basic Sentences' own switch to two young male
friends greeting each other with `szervusz` and using `te`-forms throughout. Selected illustrative
forms (not the full table, to respect copyright discipline): direct object `téged`/`titeket`;
indirect object `neked`/`nektek`; possessive "your" `ceruzád` (sg.) / `ceruzátok` (pl.); present
tense `vársz`/`vártok` (indefinite) vs. `várod`/`várjátok` (definite). p. 487–489

### 22.B The Subject-Object Suffix -lak/-lek — register-relevant

A single bound verb ending fusing 1st-person-singular subject with 2nd-person-familiar direct
object: `látlak` "I see you [familiar]." Because it presupposes a tegez relationship with the
addressee, this suffix is itself a register marker — it cannot be used toward someone addressed with
`maga`. Full paradigm shown across present, past, conditional, subjunctive, future (`vinni foglak`),
past conditional (`vittelek volna`), causative (`vitetlek`) and potential (`vihetlek`) forms. p.
489–490

### 22.[Vocabulary Drill] Tetszik / Izlik / Jólesik / Rosszul esik

Not a lettered grammar point in the source, but a substantial dative-experiencer construction
cluster drilled at length and worth capturing as grammar: Hungarian expresses "I like X" via `X
tetszik nekem` (lit. "X pleases to-me," used for people/things one finds appealing/attractive: `A
lányok szépek. Tetszenek nekem.`); "I like the taste of X" via `X ízlik nekem` (specifically for
food/drink); "X feels pleasant to me" via `(X) jólesik nekem` (for a sensation or a kind gesture,
not necessarily a "thing"); and its negative counterpart "X hurts/offends me" via `rosszul esik
nekem` (for being slighted or having one's feelings hurt). All four share the same
dative-experiencer syntax (experiencer marked with `-nak/-nek`, subject is the thing/action
liked/disliked) rather than an English-style transitive "like" verb. p. 503–504

### 23.A Verbal Prefixes — reference list

A reference list of the most common Hungarian verbal prefixes/preverbs (`abba-, agyon-, ala-, át-,
be-, bele-, el-, ellen-, elő-, előre-, fel-, félbe-, félre-, felül-, fenn-, hátra-, helyre-,
hozzá-, ide-, keresztül-, ki-, körül-, közbe-, közzé-, külön-, le-, meg-, mellé-, neki-, oda-,
össze-, rá-, rajta-, széjjel-, szét-, tova-, tovább-, túl-, utána-, végbe-, végig-, vissza-`), cross-
referenced to Unit 12 for prefix-position rules in a sentence. This same inventory underlies many of
the idioms captured from Unit 24 (`bele-`, `neki-`). p. 518

### 23.B Frequentative Verbs

A cluster of derivational suffixes added to a verb root to mark frequent, repeated, or continuous
action — sometimes shifting the verb's meaning entirely rather than just its aspect. See the
Morpheme Breakdown above for the fuller worked list (`-gat/-get`, `-oz/-ez/-öz`, and the rarer
`-dogál/-degél/-dögél`, `-gál/-gél`, `-kál`, `-ál/-él`, `-kod/-ked/-köd`, `-dal/-del`). p. 518–519

### 23.C Additional Suffixes

Six further productive suffixes, briefly glossed with examples: `-stul/-stül` "together with"
(`családostul`); `-ként` "as, like" (`tolmácsként` "as an interpreter"); `-nyi` "a measure/quantity
of" (`óránynyi` "an hour's worth"); `-vá/-vé` "turning into, becoming" (assimilates its `v` to a
preceding consonant: `szakemberekké` "into experts"); `-lag/-leg` forms adverbs from nouns/adjectives
(`politikailag` "politically," `tényleg` "actually"); `-ul/-ül` forms "like/as" expressions, notably
in fixed idioms (`feleségül venni` "to take as a wife, marry," `segítségül hívni` "to call for
help"). p. 519–520

### 23.D Numerals and Indefinite Pronouns with Possessive Suffixes

Expressions like "two of us," "five of them" are formed with a cardinal number plus a personal
possessive suffix (`kettőnk` "two of us," `ötőjük` "five of them"), which can then take further case
endings depending on syntactic role. Indefinite pronouns follow the same pattern:
`mindegyikünk` "every one of us," `valamennyiük` "all of them," `sokunk` "many of us." p. 519

### 23.E Verbal Endings -ít / -ul, -ül

`-ít` typically marks that the subject *causes* the action (transitive): `épít` "he builds." `-ul/
-ül` typically marks that the subject *undergoes* the action (intransitive, no object): `épül` "it
is being built." Same causative/anticausative pairing pattern as `lassít`/`lassul`
("slow down [something]" / "[something] slows down") and `egyesít`/`egyesül` ("unite [something]" /
"[something] unites/is united"). p. 520

### 23.F Table of Variant Forms of Benne

`benne` ("in it") has a full personal paradigm across three spatial cases — Hol? "in [it]"
(`bennem, benned, benne, bennünk, bennetek, bennük`), Hova? "into [it]" (`belém, beléd, bele/belé/
beléje, belénk, belétek, beléjük`), Honnan? "out of [it]" (`belőlem, belőled, belőle, belőlünk,
belőletek, belőlük`). p. 520

### 23.G Bennünket, Benneteket

`bennünket` and `benneteket` are commonly used as variant accusative forms of `minket` "us" and
`titeket` "you (pl., familiar)" respectively. p. 520

### 24. "Idiomatic" Expressions — register-relevant

Unit 24's single grammar section is not a rule but an explicit methodological note from the source
itself: it states that up to this point the course has concentrated on "fundamental patterns," and
that from here the student must separately learn Hungarian's large stock of figurative/idiomatic
constructions whose meaning cannot be derived compositionally from their parts and whose literal
English translation would mislead (the source's own named examples: `szaván fogni`, `sokba kerül`,
`rossz szemmel nézni valamit`). The source explicitly frames this as an open-ended, ever-growing
category ("idioms... with more coming into existence every day") requiring ongoing exposure to
native speakers rather than a fixed rule set — i.e., the source itself identifies idiom/figurative
language as adjacent to, if not identical with, informal/colloquial register acquisition. The bulk
of this unit's Transformation Drills (not reproduced verbatim, per copyright discipline) consist of
sentence pairs illustrating dozens of such idioms in context; the ones that recur with distinct,
non-compositional figurative meaning are captured individually in the Vocabulary table and Morpheme
Breakdown above (`szaván fog`, `sokba kerül`, `rossz szemmel néz`, `eszébe jut`, the `bele-` cluster,
`mi járatban van`, `lemenőben van`, `magához tér`, `magához vesz`, `kézhez vesz`, the `neki-`
cluster, and others). p. 548

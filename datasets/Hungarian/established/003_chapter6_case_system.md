# Hungarian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Chapter 6, The Case System

**Source:** Carol H. Rounds, *Hungarian: An Essential Grammar* (2nd edition, Routledge, 2009),
Chapter 6 "The case system," printed pages 86–113 (PDF pages 101–128).

**Coverage note:** every case in the Hungarian case system is covered as its own grammar point
(23 cases total: 2 grammatical, 9 locative, 8 oblique, 4 less-productive), since this dispatch's
brief treats the case inventory — not the exhaustive word-list — as the highest-value content of
this chapter. The vocabulary table below is a representative-but-not-exhaustive sample of the
chapter's example nouns, verbs, adjectives, and postpositions (this deliberately departs from the
extraction spec's usual "every distinct vocabulary item" rule, per this dispatch's explicit
instruction to prioritize case coverage over cataloguing every word in every declension table).
Full multi-column declension paradigm tables (accusative suffixing by noun-stem type, locative
suffixing by noun-stem type, the plural paradigm, and the full-declension tables for *család*,
*csésze*, *óra*) are paraphrased and illustrated with a handful of examples rather than
transcribed. Repeated drill sentences reusing already-captured vocabulary are skipped — see
`../../00_Reference_Extraction_Spec.md` for the full coverage rule.

**Morpheme breakdown (agglutinative forms).** Hungarian case suffixes stack directly onto noun
stems, and additional suffixes (plural, possessive) may intervene between the stem and the case —
the case itself is always the outermost, final suffix (p. 86). The forms below, all directly
attested in the source, show this stacking in increasing complexity.

> **könyvekben** = könyv (book) + ek (plural) + ben (inessive, "in") — "in books." Given by the
> source itself (p. 86) as the canonical illustration that cases are always the final suffix of a
> word, attaching after the plural marker.

> **könyvemben** = könyv (book) + em (1sg possessive, "my") + ben (inessive, "in") — "in my book."
> Source's companion example (p. 86) showing a case attaching after a possessive suffix instead of
> the plural.

> **barátommal** = barát (friend) + om (1sg possessive, "my") + val→mal (instrumental, "with";
> the suffix's initial *v* assimilates to the stem-final consonant *m*) — "with my friend" (p. 104).
> Demonstrates both possessive+case stacking and the instrumental/translative's regular
> v-assimilation rule.

> **lánnyal** = lány (girl) + val→nyal (instrumental, "with"; v-assimilation to stem-final *ny*) —
> "with a girl" (p. 104). A digraph-final stem: only the first letter of the digraph *ny* doubles.

> **vízzé** = víz (water) + vé→zé (translative, "turning into"; v-assimilation to stem-final *z*) —
> "into water" (p. 104), one of the source's core examples of instrumental/translative
> v-assimilation.

> **borrá** = bor (wine) + vá→rá (translative, "turning into"; v-assimilation to stem-final *r*) —
> "into wine" (p. 104).

> **gyerekeire** = gyerek (child) + e (3rd-person-singular possessive linker) + i (plural-of-the-
> possessed-item marker) + re (sublative, "onto/about") — roughly "about his/her children," from
> *Büszke a gyerekeire* "She is proud of her children" (p. 99). Shows three morphemes stacked
> beyond the bare stem: possessive person, possessed-plural, and case.

> **barátnőjére** = barátnő (girlfriend) + je (3sg possessive, "his") + re (sublative, "about") —
> "about his girlfriend," from *Sokat gondol a barátnőjére* "He thinks about his girlfriend a lot"
> (p. 99).

> **óránként** = óra (hour) + n (linking vowel) + ként (distributive, "per") — "per hour," from
> *Óránként fizetnek* "They pay by the hour" (p. 109).

> **havonként** = hó (month) + n (linking vowel) + ként (distributive, "per") — "monthly, per
> month" (p. 104, footnote 6). The source explicitly flags this as an *exception*: the distributive
> normally requires a linking vowel matched to the stem's noun class, but *hó* "month" is
> irregular here.

> **családostul** = család (family) + ostul (sociative, "(together) with") — "with the whole
> family," from *Mindig családostul utazik* "He always travels with his family" (p. 110).

> **tanárként** = tanár (teacher) + ként (essive-formal, "as") — "as a teacher," from *Egy évig
> tanárként dolgozott* "She worked as a teacher for a year" (p. 108).

## Grammar points

*(One subsection per case in the Hungarian case system, per the source's own three-way grouping:
grammatical, locative, oblique, and less-productive cases — pp. 86–113.)*

### Case system overview

Hungarian marks the grammatical function of words with an extensive suffixal case system rather
than prepositions (p. 86). Cases attach to the very end of a word, after any plural or possessive
suffixes already present (*könyv+ek+ben* → *könyvekben* "in books"; *a könyvem+ben* → *a
könyvemben* "in my book"). Most commonly used with nouns, cases may also attach to adjectives,
numerals, and demonstrative/interrogative pronouns, but not to most personal pronouns, which have
their own separate paradigm (§7.1). Two general stem alternations recur across the whole system:
(a) a word-final *a* or *e* lengthens to *á*/*é* before most case suffixes (exceptions: temporal
*-kor* and essive-formal *-ként*); (b) the accusative, superessive, distributive, sociative, and
distributive-temporal cases require a linking vowel when suffixing to a consonant-final stem, and
this can trigger further stem changes (p. 86). Vowel harmony governs which case-variant is used:
some cases offer a three-way front/back/rounded choice (*-hoz/-hez/-höz*), most offer only a
front/back choice (*-ból/-ből*), and a few offer no choice at all (*-ig*).

The 23 cases fall into three groups (p. 87): **grammatical cases** (nominative, accusative),
**locative cases** (indicating place and/or motion, further divided into interior/exterior/near
subsets), and **oblique cases** (everything else — dative, instrumental, translative, causal-
final, essive-formal, terminative, distributive, essive). A fourth, smaller set of **less
productive cases** (temporal, distributive-temporal, sociative, locative) is limited to narrower
use.

### Nominative (— , zero case)

The "zero" case: no marking at all, and the dictionary citation form for nouns and adjectives
(p. 88). It marks the subject (*Elindult a vonat* "The train departed") and, less obviously, three
other roles: (1) a term of quantity modifying "how much of a substance" (*Egy korsó sört
rendelt* "She ordered a mug of beer"); (2) the object of most postpositions, with listed
exceptions (§9.4); (3) the possessor in a possessive construction where the possessor immediately
precedes the possessed item (*Elkértem Zsuzsa tollat* "I asked for Zsuzsa's pen"); and (4) certain
time expressions, both those built on the word *nap* "day" (*minden nap* "every day") and
"ago"/"since" expressions built with the possessive (*Hét hónapja utazott el* "He left seven
months ago").

### Accusative (-(V)t)

Marks the direct object (p. 88–91). Suffixing follows the same pattern as the plural *-k* with one
added wrinkle: regular nouns ending in *j, l, ly, n, ny, r, s, sz, z, zs* take the accusative with
no linking vowel, while regular nouns ending in any other consonant require the linking vowel
*o/e/ö*; low-vowel and fleeting-vowel nouns always require a linking vowel regardless of final
consonant. When the accusative attaches to an already-plural noun, the linking vowel *a/e* is
always required after the plural *-k*, with no exceptions (*bokrok* → *bokrokat*). Beyond marking
direct objects, the accusative also appears in some time expressions (*Egy hetet töltöttünk a
Balatonon* "We spent a week at the Balaton") and on adjectives that modify the action of a verb
adverbially (*Nagyot sóhajtott* "He gave a big sigh," lit. "sighed a big [one]").

### Illative (-ba/-be)

Interior-motion-toward case: "into" (p. 93). Marks motion into the interior of a place
(*Bemegyek a régi házba* "I am going into the old house"). Non-locatively (§6.3.1), it is required
by verbs with the coverb *bele* "into" (*Belefáradt a munkába* "He got tired of the work"; *Mária
beleszeretett Jánosba* "Mária fell in love with János") and by expressions of a condition someone
enters (*Az a fiú mindig nagy bajba kerül* "That boy always gets into big trouble"; *Dühbe jött*
"She flew into a rage").

### Inessive (-ban/-ben)

Interior-no-motion case: "in" (p. 93–94). The paradigm case used to represent all "other locative
cases" in the source's suffixing tables, since inessive and its sibling cases attach identically
(only the superessive suffixes differently). Marks static location inside something (*Géza olvas a
kertben* "Géza is reading in the garden"). Non-locative uses (§6.3.2) include time expressions
(*A születésnapom decemberben van* "My birthday is in December"), verb/adjective government
(*Hiszel Istenben?* "Do you believe in God?"; *Rákban halt meg* "He died of cancer"), the "condition
a person is in" (*Bajban vagyunk* "We are in trouble"), and the units into which an action is
divided (*Dollárban fizetnek* "They pay us in dollars"; *Nagy pelyhekben hullott a hó* "The snow
fell in large flakes").

### Elative (-ból/-ből)

Interior-motion-away case: "out of, out from" (p. 94). Marks motion out of an interior (*A gyerekek
kifutottak a teremből* "The children ran out of the classroom"). Non-locative uses (§6.3.3) are
unusually rich: marking origin — of persons, material, or time (*Fizikus lett a bátyámból* "My
elder brother became a physicist"; *Milyen anyagból készült a ruha?* "What material is the dress
made of?"; *Melyik korból való az a festmény?* "What era is that painting from?"); marking cause or
manner (*Tévedésből vettem ezt a könyvet* "I bought this book by mistake"); and a partitive use
(*Csak keveset evett a halból* "He only ate a little of the fish"; *Kérsz még a süteményből?*
"Would you like some more pastry?").

### Sublative (-ra/-re)

Exterior-motion-toward case: "onto" (p. 94, 99). Marks motion toward a surface or exterior
(*A táskám leesett a földre* "My bag fell to the floor"; *Felszállok erre az autóbuszra* "I will
get on this bus"). Non-locative uses (§6.3.4) include: time expressions marking a deadline (*Hat
órára jön haza* "He'll be home by six o'clock"); verb/adjective government, especially verbs with
the coverb *rá* (*Büszke a gyerekeire* "She is proud of her children"; *Ránéztem az ablakra* "I
glanced at the window"); and marking the result or goal of an action (*Apró darabokra vágtam a
hagymát* "I cut the onion into small pieces"; *Magyarra fordította a könyvet* "He translated the
book into Hungarian").

### Superessive (-(o/e/ö)n)

Exterior-no-motion case: "on, at" (p. 95, 100). The only locative case that suffixes differently
from the rest of its group: it always requires the linking vowel *o/e/ö* on consonant-final stems,
with no low-vowel/regular-noun distinction. Marks static position on a surface or at an exterior
location (*Híres egyetemen dolgozunk* "We work at a famous university"; *A szótár az asztalon van*
"The dictionary is on the table"). It is also the case required with most Hungarian place names
(and the name "Hungary" itself, *Magyarországon*), public transportation, and named events. Non-
locative uses (§6.3.5) cover: days of the week and other time expressions (*Hétfőn, szerdán és
pénteken jár a francia órára* "He goes to French class on Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays"); a
closed set of postpositions requiring a superessive-marked noun before them (*alul* "below,"
*fölül* "above," *kívül* "outside, besides," *át* "across," *innen* "this side of," *keresztül*
"across," *túl* "beyond," *belül* "within," *végig* "along, to the end" — *Egy órán belül készült
el a leves* "The soup was ready within an hour"); and verb government, especially verbs using those
same words as coverbs (*Sokáig gondolkozott az ügyön* "She thought about the matter for a long
time"; *Átment az utcán* "He walked across the street").

### Delative (-ról/-ről)

Exterior-motion-away case: "off, from" (p. 95, 101). Marks motion away from a surface or exterior
(*A szótár leesett az asztalról* "The dictionary fell off the table"; *Tegnap feljöttek Pécsről*
"They came up from Pécs yesterday"). Non-locatively (§6.3.6) it very often means "about" (*Miről
szól a cikk?* "What is the article about?"), and it participates in a reduplicated adverbial
construction paired with the sublative (*napról napra* "from day to day," *szóról szóra* "word by
word," *magyarról angolra* "from Hungarian into English").

### Allative (-hoz/-hez/-höz)

Near-motion-toward case: "toward, to" (p. 95, 101–102). One of the three cases with a full
three-way vowel-harmony choice. Marks motion toward the vicinity of a vertical object, a person, or
a place of business (*Elmentünk a fogorvoshoz* "We went to the dentist's"; *Leültünk az asztalhoz*
"We sat down at the table"). Non-locatively it is required by expressions of attaching, adding, or
communicating something (especially verbs with the coverb *hozzá* — *Hozzászólt a kérdéshez* "He
addressed the question") and by various verbs/adjectives/postpositions of resemblance, aptitude,
and proximity (*Nagyon hasonlít az anyjához* "She resembles her mother very much"; *Nem érték a
geometriához* "I am not good at geometry"; *Közel lakik a Nemzeti Múzeumhoz* "She lives near the
National Museum").

### Adessive (-nál/-nél)

Near-no-motion case: "near, at, by, with" (p. 96, 102). Marks static position in the vicinity of a
person, their home, or their place of business (*A kulcs Imrénél van*, lit. "The key is by Imre" =
"Imre has the key"; *A Kovácséknál voltam két hétig* "I stayed with the Kovácses for two weeks").
Non-locatively (§6.3.8) it marks the standard of comparison (*Dénes magasabb Péternél* "Dénes is
taller than Péter"), combines with plural pronouns to mean "in one's country/part of the world"
(*Nálunk sokan csak kint dohányoznak* "Where I live, many people smoke only outside"), and marks a
person's condition or state in fixed idioms (*Nem vagy észnél!* "You are out of your mind!"; *Most
már magánál van* "He's conscious now").

### Ablative (-tól/-től)

Near-motion-away case: "(away) from" (p. 96, 103). Marks motion away from the vicinity of a person
or thing (*Erzsébettől kaptam a könyvet* "I got the book from Erzsébet"; *Felkeltünk az asztaltól*
"We stood up from the table"). Non-locatively (§6.3.9) it is used with verbs of fear or separation
(*Nem félek a kutyáktól* "I am not afraid of dogs"; *Elváltak egymástól* "They separated from each
other"), to express the origin of an object/action in time or space — often paired with the
terminative *-ig* (*Reggeltől estig dolgozik* "He works from morning till night"; *Budapesttől
Párizsig beszélgettek a vonaton* "They talked on the train from Budapest to Paris") — and to denote
the cause of an action (*Reszket a hidegtől* "She is shivering from the cold"; *Elájult a
hőségtől* "He fainted from the heat").

### Dative (-nak/-nek)

Oblique case meaning "to, for" (p. 104–106). Marks the indirect object or beneficiary of an action
(*Csillának adtam egy könyvet* "I gave a book to Csilla"). It is also required by many verbs and
some adjectives (*Fáradtnak látszol* "You look tired"; *Jó barátomnak tartalak* "I consider you my
good friend"), appears in impersonal constructions expressing necessity or importance (*Gábornak
tanulnia kell ma este* "Gábor must study this evening," lit. "It is necessary for Gábor to
study..."), and — crucially — marks the possessor in both "have" constructions (*Gyulának két húga
van* "Gyula has two younger sisters") and, optionally, in ordinary possessive constructions (*Ennek
az árát nem tudom* "I don't know the price of this").

### Instrumental (-val/-vel)

Oblique case meaning "with" (p. 104, 106–107). Its initial *v* always assimilates to the preceding
stem-final consonant (see morpheme breakdown examples above: *barátommal*, *lánnyal*, *tollal*,
*ésszel*, *orral*). Denotes the person, material, manner, or means of transportation with which an
action is done (*Inkább ceruzával írok, mint tollal* "I prefer to write with a pencil than a pen";
*Busszal vagy metróval menjünk?* "Shall we go by bus or metro?"). It also expresses degree of
comparison (*Imre két évvel fiatalabb, mint Kornél* "Imre is two years younger than Kornél"), marks
the agent of a causative construction (*A szabóval varrattam egy új öltönyt* "I had the tailor sew
me a new suit"), and is required by many verbs/adjectives/adverbs plus the two postpositions
*együtt* "together" and *szemben* "across, opposite."

### Translative (-vá/-vé)

Oblique case meaning "(turning) into" (p. 104, 107). Like the instrumental, its initial *v*
assimilates to the stem-final consonant (*vízzé*, *borrá* — see morpheme breakdowns above). Fairly
limited use: it combines with a small set of verbs denoting a change of state — *válik*
"become, turn into," *lesz* "become," *tesz* "make," *változtat* "change" — to express what
something or someone changes into (*Hallgatag emberré vált, miután meghalt a felesége* "He became
a quiet man after his wife died"; *Ezt a kunyhót valódi palotává változtatták* "They have changed
this shack into a real palace").

### Causal-final (-ért)

Oblique case meaning "for the purpose of," often simply "for" (p. 107–108). Denotes cause, reason,
or purpose (*Mindent megtesz a családjáért* "He does everything for his family"; *Elküldtem a
boltba kenyérért* "I sent him to the store for bread"). It is central to financial-transaction
phrasing, marking whichever of price or merchandise appears in the "for" phrase (*Hatezer forintot
fizetett a cipőért* "She paid six thousand forints for the shoes" vs. *Hatezer forintért vette a
cipőt* "She bought the shoes for six thousand forints"). The demonstrative pronoun in this case,
*azért* "for that reason," introduces purpose clauses (*Azért tanul angolul, hogy többet
keressen a munkahelyén* "The reason he is studying English is to earn more money at work").

### Essive-formal (-ként)

Oblique case meaning "as" (p. 108). Does not lengthen a stem-final *a* or *e* (unlike most other
cases) and can substitute for a clause introduced by *mint* "as" (*Egy évig tanárként dolgozott* ≈
*Egy évig dolgozott, mint tanár* "She worked as a teacher for a year"). Also used figuratively
(*Rabszolgaként bánnak velem* "They treat me like a slave").

### Terminative (-ig)

Oblique case meaning "until, up to" (p. 108). Has no vowel-harmony counterpart (unlike most cases).
Used in both temporal and spatial expressions of "until," frequently paired with the ablative
*-tól/-től* (*Nyolctól ötig dolgozik* "She works from eight until five"; *Elkísért bennünket a
sarokig* "He accompanied us to the corner").

### Distributive (-(V)nként)

Oblique case meaning "per, each" (p. 109). Requires a linking vowel — the same one used to form
the plural — when suffixing to a consonant-final stem (see the *óránként*/*havonként* morpheme
breakdowns above; *hó* "month" → *havonként* is flagged as the one true exception). Expresses a
regular division, translatable as "per," "by," or "every" (*Mennyibe kerül a vacsora fejenként?*
"How much does the dinner cost per person?"; *Egyenként léptek be a terembe* "They entered the hall
one by one").

### Essive (-ul/-ül)

Oblique case, glossed "as" but functionally closer to an adverbializer (p. 109). Used with the
names of languages to mean "in [a language]" (*Jól tud franciául olvasni* "He can read in French
very well"; *Nem beszél jól olaszul* "He doesn't speak Italian well"), and in a handful of fixed
idioms denoting how a noun is used (*István feleségül vette a húgomat* "István married my younger
sister," lit. "took [her] as a wife").

### Temporal (-kor)

Less-productive case, "at" plus a time expression (p. 109). Attaches to both front- and
back-vowel words alike (no vowel-harmony pendant exists) and does not lengthen a stem-final *a* or
*e*. Restricted to time expressions only (*Hat órakor jön haza* "He is coming home at six
o'clock"; *Éjfélkor szoktam lefeküdni* "I usually go to bed at midnight").

### Distributive-temporal (-(V)nta/-(V)nte)

Less-productive case, "per, every" plus a time expression (p. 110). Requires the same linking
vowel as the plural when suffixing to a consonant-final stem. Attaches to words denoting time
expressions (*Hetente háromszor van magyar óra* "Hungarian class is three times per week"; *Óránta
közlik a híreket* "They broadcast the news every hour").

### Sociative (-(V)stul/-(V)stül)

Less-productive case, "(together) with" (p. 110). Requires the same linking vowel as the plural
when suffixing to a consonant-final stem. Denotes "with" people or objects closely associated with
each other (*Mindig családostul utazik* "He always travels with his family"; *Mindenestül
érkezett meg* "She arrived with all her belongings," lit. "bag and baggage").

### Locative (-t/-ott/-ett/-ött)

Less-productive, largely archaic case meaning "at, in" (p. 110). Limited to a handful of town
names and a few other words; shares its ending with the deictic set *itt* "here," *ott* "there,"
*másutt* "elsewhere" and postpositions like *alatt* "under," *előtt* "in front of." Examples:
*Pécsett* "in Pécs," *Győrött* "in Győr," *Kolozsvárt*/*Kolozsvárott* "in Kolozsvár"; also survives
in the adverbial pair *oldal* "side" → *oldalt* "sideways" and *egyenes* "straight" → *egyenest*
"straight ahead."

### Verbs and cases (case government)

A single Hungarian verb or expression can appear with different cases to convey systematically
different meanings, much as English varies its choice of preposition (p. 111). Sometimes only the
complement's role shifts while the verb's core meaning stays constant (*Hiszek Jánosban* "I believe
in János" vs. *Hiszek Jánosnak* "I believe János [i.e., what he says]"; *Istvánról beszélek* "I am
speaking about István" vs. *Istvánnal beszélek* "I am speaking with István"). Other times the
verb's meaning itself changes with the case it governs (*Nem értem a leckét* "I don't understand
the lesson" [accusative] vs. *Nem érték a fizikához* "I am no good at physics" [allative]; *Sokáig
tartott az előadás* "The lecture lasted a long time" [intransitive] vs. *Sokáig tartotta a síró
gyereket* "He held the crying child for a long time" [transitive, accusative object]). The source
stresses that a good dictionary should list a verb's governed case(s) alongside its meaning(s).

### Plural declension

Cases attach to an already-formed plural noun directly, without a linking vowel — with three
exceptions (p. 111–112): (a) the accusative always requires the linking vowel *a/e* after a plural
noun; (b) the superessive *-n* always requires the linking vowel *o/e/ö* after a plural noun; (c)
the temporal *-kor*, distributive *-(V)nként*, distributive-temporal *-(V)nta/-(V)nte*, and
sociative *-(V)stul/-(V)stül* are simply not used in the plural at all. The source illustrates the
full plural paradigm (nominative/accusative/inessive/superessive/instrumental) with three
noun-class examples — back-vowel *lány* "girl," front-unrounded *kert* "garden," and
front-rounded *ismerős* "acquaintance" — rather than every case; see the morpheme breakdown of
*könyvekben* above for how a further case then stacks onto a plural form.

### Full declension of select nouns

The source closes the chapter with three complete case-by-case declension tables (nominative
through locative, singular and plural) for one representative noun of each stress/vowel pattern:
*család* "family" (a low-vowel/regular back-vowel type), *csésze* "cup" (an a/e-final type), and
*óra* "hour, clock" (a vowel-final type), cross-referencing Appendix 2 for declensions of every
other noun type (p. 112–113). These tables are not reproduced here in full per copyright

# Hungarian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: FSI Basic Course, Vol. 2, Units 13–16

**Source:** Augustus A. Koski & Ilona Mihályfy, *Hungarian Basic Course* (Foreign Service
Institute, Washington D.C., 1964), Volume 2 ("Units 13-24"), printed pages 267–366 (PDF pages
10–109 of `FSI Hungarian Basic Course - Volume 2 - Student Text.pdf`). Unit boundaries were located
directly from the volume's own Table of Contents (PDF pages 4–5) rather than assumed from even page
spacing: **Unit 13** pp. 267–293, **Unit 14** pp. 294–321, **Unit 15** pp. 322–341, **Unit 16**
pp. 342–366 (Unit 17 begins p. 367, outside this dispatch's scope). The source has a genuine text
layer (`pdftotext` extracted cleanly; OCR-typical letter-swap noise — e.g. `Gramrnar` for `Grammar`,
`~` for missing accented vowels in a few spots — is a scanning artifact of the source PDF itself, not
a vision-reading issue; no vision-reading was needed and no marginalia were found).

**This is the first extraction from the FSI Basic Course** (a different book from the two sources
already extracted in this folder, Rounds's *Hungarian: An Essential Grammar* and its own case-system
chapter). It is a structured audio-lingual drill course, not a reference grammar, so its content and
organization differ substantially: each unit has **Basic Sentences** (a dialogue introducing new
vocabulary line-by-line), **Notes on Grammar** (prose grammar points "for home study"), then a long
sequence of **Substitution / Variation / Transformation / Translation / Response Drills**,
**Conversation Practice**, **Situations**, and a closing **Narrative** (with its own short vocabulary
list). Units 15 and 16 also open with a **Közmondások** (Proverbs) list — genuinely valuable
colloquial/idiomatic content, captured in full below.

**Coverage note.** Per `../../00_Reference_Extraction_Spec.md`'s coverage rule: every vocabulary item
introduced in the Basic Sentences, Notes on Grammar, Proverbs, and Narrative sections of all four
units is captured below — this is where the course deliberately introduces new lexical material.
The five drill types that follow each unit's Notes on Grammar are overwhelmingly repetitive
paradigm practice (conjugating the *same* already-captured verbs/adjectives through persons, tenses,
or comparison degrees) and were **not transcribed line-by-line**; however, they were read in full and
any genuinely new vocabulary item surfacing only in a drill (not in the Basic Sentences) was pulled
out and added to the table below, flagged `(drill-only)` in the Notes column. The largest such finds
were: Unit 13's Translation Drill (an extended "if only..." Budapest-sightseeing passage introducing
~15 new sightseeing/countryside nouns) and Unit 14's Transformation Drill III (a long comparative-
adjective paradigm that incidentally introduces ~30 new household-object/quality-adjective nouns).
Pure paradigm-mechanics drills that introduced no new lexical content (e.g. Unit 14's postposition-
with-personal-endings drills, Unit 16's Lexical/Transformation Drills that only re-conjugate the
`alatt/előtt/fölött/mellett/mögött/között/körül/felé` postposition set already tabulated in Grammar
point A) are described in the Grammar points section rather than re-transcribed.

**Historical/political register note.** Because this is a 1964 course teaching then-contemporary
socialist Hungary, several vocabulary items and the Unit 15 narrative describe features specific to
that system (free, tuition-less university education; worker/peasant-background admissions
preference; the state "housing office" *lakáshivatal* assigning apartments by fiat rather than by
purchase/rental market, Unit 16). These are tagged `Attested Era: socialist Hungary (source published
1964)` rather than treated as general-purpose "contemporary" Hungarian, since several of the
institutions named (`üzemi ebéd`, `lakáshivatal`, `kiutalni` in its housing-assignment sense) would
need separate verification for present-day currency.

**Morphological typology note.** Hungarian is agglutinative (see `../00_Extraction_Checklist.md`).
This dispatch's grammar content is unusually rich in suffix-stacking territory — the personal
infinitive (Unit 15), the three-way postposition system with personal endings (Units 14 and 16), and
the conditional/past-conditional paradigms (Units 13 and 15) are all suffix-stacking phenomena — so a
number of vocabulary entries below carry a **Morpheme Breakdown**; see the dedicated subsection.

### Morpheme breakdown

> **megfázni** = meg- (perfective/telic verbal prefix) + fáz- ("be cold," verb stem) + -ni
> (infinitive) — "to catch a cold." Contrasts with bare `fázni` "to be cold" (ongoing state, no
> prefix); the prefix supplies the ingressive/completive sense "become cold → catch a cold." Unit 13,
> p. 268.

> **fényképezőgép** = fénykép ("photograph," itself fény "light" + kép "picture") + -ez- (verbalizing
> suffix, "to do X") + -ő (present-participle/agentive suffix, "-er, that which does X") + gép
> ("machine") + -e (3sg possessive linking form as cited in the text, "a fényképezőgépe" = "his/her
> camera") — literally "photograph-taking machine." A five-morpheme stack (two compounded nouns +
> verbalizer + participial nominalizer + a further compounded noun), and one of the more complex
> derivations in this dispatch's material. Unit 14, pp. 299–300.

> **14 éves korig** = 14 + év ("year") + -es (adjectivizing suffix, "having N of," → "14-year-old")
> + kor ("age") + -ig (terminative case, "up to, until") — "up to the age of 14." Demonstrates a case
> suffix stacking directly onto a bare noun (`korig`) governed by a preceding adjective phrase, rather
> than onto the adjective itself. Unit 15, p. 323.

> **vegyészmérnöknek készül** = vegyész ("chemist") + mérnök ("engineer") — a compound noun,
> "chemical engineer" — + -nek (dative case, required by `készülni` "to prepare/study to be") —
> "is preparing to be(come) a chemical engineer." Illustrates a compound-noun-plus-case-suffix stack
> governed by the matrix verb's valence. Unit 15, p. 324.

> **félév** = fél ("half") + év ("year") — compound noun, "term, semester" (literally "half-year").
> Included as a contrast case: compounding (two free stems), not suffixal agglutination, though the
> resulting word then itself takes further suffixes elsewhere in the text (e.g. `félévben` "in the
> semester"). Unit 15, p. 341.

> **emiatt** = e- (proclitic/reduced form of the demonstrative `ez`, "this," used only in fixed
> combination with certain postpositions — see Unit 16 Grammar point B) + miatt ("because of,"
> single-stem postposition) — "because of this, for this reason." Shows the same `e(z)` + postposition
> agreement pattern documented in Grammar point B (`e mellett`, `e nélkül`) fossilized into a single
> adverb. Unit 16, p. 366.

> **kabátzseb** = kabát ("coat") + zseb ("pocket") — compound noun, "coat pocket," juxtaposed stems
> with no linking suffix (contrast the case-marked phrase `a kabátom zsebében`, "in my coat's pocket,"
> used earlier in the same unit's Basic Sentences, where the same two nouns appear as a possessed
> noun phrase rather than a compound). Unit 16, p. 362.

> **bútordarab** = bútor ("furniture") + darab ("piece") — compound noun, "piece/item of furniture."
> Simple N+N compounding, included for contrast with the suffix-stacking examples above. Unit 16,
> p. 362.

## Grammar points

### Unit 13

**A. The present conditional.** Formed with a `-Vn-` suffix (the vowel harmonizing to back/front)
added to the infinitive stem minus final `-ni`, then a full definite/indefinite personal-ending
paradigm (back-vowel definite `-ám, -ád, -á, -ánk, -átok, -ák`; back-vowel indefinite `-ék, -ál, -a,
-ánk, -átok, -ának`; front-vowel definite/indefinite mirror sets with `é`). `lenni` "to be" has two
conditional stems, `lennék` and `volnék`. Three uses are illustrated: (1) contrary-to-fact
conditional sentences (`ha` "if" clause + result clause, both in the conditional when the English
uses past-tense/"would"; both in present tense when the English "if" clause is present-tense);
(2) politeness — a conditional request ("I'd like...") is preferred over a blunt direct statement,
just as in English; (3) wishes/strong desire, typically introduced by **bárcsak** "if only," with an
exclamation mark, e.g. `Bárcsak sok pénzem volna!` "If only I had a lot of money!" p. 274–276.

**B. Expressing "to be cold"/"to be warm."** Three distinct patterns: `Fázom` "I am cold" (verb,
used only of living beings); `Hideg van` "It (weather) is cold" (adjective + `van`); `Hideg` "It
(object) is cold" (bare equational adjective, per the Unit 1 pattern). Hungarian has no dedicated
verb for "to be warm" — the living-being pattern instead uses the possessive-plus-`lenni`
construction, `Melegem van` "I am warm" (literally "my warmth exists"), `Nincs melegem` "I am not
warm." pp. 275–276.

**C. The suffix -val, -vel ("with").** Added directly to a vowel-final word (with `-a`/`-e` stems
lengthening to `-á`/`-é` first); for a consonant-final word the `v` assimilates to and doubles the
final consonant instead (e.g. `Forinttal fizetek` "I pay with forints"). p. 277.

**D. Telling time (continued).** A set of formulas for expressing minutes past/to the hour and
quarter-hour: full hour = number + `óra`; minutes past the hour (first 14) = (N minutes) `perccel
múlt` (hour) `óra`; quarter/half/three-quarter hours = `negyed`/`fél`/`háromnegyed` + number of the
*next* hour (Hungarian counts quarter-hours forward, not backward, from the upcoming hour); minutes
after a quarter-hour = (N) `perccel múlt` (quarter-hour expression); minutes before the hour or a
quarter-hour = (N) `perc múlva lesz` (upcoming hour/quarter expression). Where two formulas overlap
for the same clock time, colloquial preference goes to the `múlva` (countdown) construction over the
`múlt` (elapsed) construction. The suffix `-kor` marks full/half/quarter hours in clock-time
expressions, but is replaced by `előtt`/`után` once minutes are specified. pp. 277–279.

### Unit 14

**A. Formation of adverbs.** The adverbial markers `-n, -an, -en, -on` (vowel-harmonic, chosen to
match the linking vowel the adjective takes before its own plural `-k`) or `-ul, -ül` (mainly for
nationality adjectives and adjectives in `-atlan/-etlen/-talan/-telen`) convert an adjective to its
corresponding adverb, parallel to English `-ly`. Stems in `-a`/`-e` lengthen before `-n`; many stems
in `-i, -ó, -ó` insert a linking vowel (`kiváncsi → kiváncsian`) though a few don't (`késő → későn`);
most `-ú/-ű` stems take `-an/-en` and some drop the `-ú/-ű` entirely (`hosszú → hosszan`). A few
adjectives have two valid adverbial forms (`csúnya → csúnyán, csúnyául`). pp. 301–302.

**B. Comparison of adjectives and adverbs.** Comparative is formed with `-bb` (linking `-a-`/`-e-`
inserted after a consonant, or the stem's own final `-a`/`-e` lengthened to `-á-`/`-é-`); superlative
adds the prefix `leg-` to the comparative and is usually preceded by the definite article. A number
of forms are irregular (`jó → jobb → legjobb`; `sok → több → legtöbb`; `kis(kicsi) → kisebb →
legkisebb`), and irregularity in the comparative always carries through to the superlative. Equality
comparisons use `olyan...mint`, `annyi...mint`, `akkora...mint` and their correlative-pronoun variants
`(mint) amilyen/amennyi/amekkora`. "Than" is expressed either with `mint` (parallel to English) or,
for two substantives in the nominative, by putting the compared noun/pronoun in `-nál/-nél` instead
(`A férfi öregebb a fiúnál` = `...mint a fiú`). `-nál/-nél` may also intensify a doubled comparative
(`jobbnál jobb` "better and better," lit. "better than better"). `minél (mennél) X-bb...annál Y-bb`
gives "the more X, the more Y." `-val/-vel` on a degree word modifies a comparative ("somewhat/much
more," `mennyivel öregebb...?`). **Colloquial register note (point 7):** in colloquial speech the
suffix `-ik` is often added to a comparative or superlative to stress that it is being singled out
as a preferred/chosen one among a set — e.g. `a kisebbik fiam` "my younger(-of-the-two) son," `a
nagyobbik kocsi` "the bigger(-of-the-two) car" — distinct from the plain comparative. `nagyon`/`igen`
+ adjective, not the superlative, is how Hungarian renders English "a most beautiful girl"-type
intensification. `legelső`/`legutolsó` ("very first"/"very last") add `leg-` to the *positive*, not
the comparative, form. Comparatives/superlatives of adverbs are formed by adding the adverb suffix to
the already-comparative/superlative adjective stem (`szépen → szebben → legszebben`). pp. 301–304.

**C. Suffixes -nál/-nél, hozzám, velem, rám/rajtam as independent forms.** `-nál/-nél` ("at, near, at
the home of," place-where) contrasts with `-hoz/-hez/-höz` ("to, to the home of," place-to-which) and
`-tól/-től` ("from, from the house of," place-from-which). All of these, plus `-val/-vel`, may be
used not just as bound suffixes on a noun but as independent words carrying a full personal-ending
paradigm: `nálam/nálad/nála/nálunk/nálatok/náluk`; `hozzám/hozzád/hozzá(hozzája)/hozzánk/hozzátok/
hozzájuk` (note the doubled `z`); `velem/veled/vele/velünk/veletek/velük`. `-ra/-re` similarly has an
independent form `rám` etc. used with verbs of motion, contrasting with `rajtam` etc. (base `rajt-`,
not `rá-`) used with verbs of state/location — e.g. `A könyvet az asztalra teszi` (motion, `-ra`) vs.
`A könyv az asztalon van` (location, `-n`) vs. the personal forms `rám teszi` / `rajtam van`.
pp. 304–305.

**D. Postpositions (personal-ending paradigm).** Most postpositions may take personal endings just
like the suffixes above; the unit tabulates `alatt/előtt/fölött/mellett/mögött` through all six
persons (`alattam, alattad, alatta, alattunk, alattatok, alattuk`, etc.) — a subset of the fuller
three-way (whither/where/whence) system given in full in Unit 16's Grammar point A. p. 305.

**E. The concept of "old" in Hungarian.** Three non-interchangeable words correspond to English
"old": **éves** (from `év` "year") — age in years specifically; **öreg** — "old, aged" in the sense
of advanced in years (of people); **régi** — "old" in the sense of long-standing, made long ago,
worn/deteriorated with age, former, ancient/antique — i.e. everything *except* a person's numeric age.
pp. 305–306.

*(A further pattern surfaces only in this unit's drills, not in the prose Notes on Grammar: the
suffix* **-ig** *"as far as, until, up to" marking a spatial or temporal endpoint — e.g.
`Angliáig` "as far as England," `Bécsig` "as far as Vienna," `a hídig` "as far as the bridge."
Transformation Drill II, p. 308.)*

### Unit 15

**A. Use of adverbs.** Several examples where Hungarian uses an adverbial form (`-n`/`-l` marker)
where English uses a bare adjective or no marker at all: `éhesen jönnek haza` "they come home
hungry," `gyorsan hajtanak` "they drive fast," `ismeretlenül is üdvözlöm` "I send greetings even
though [we are] unacquainted." p. 329.

**B. The infinitive with personal endings ("personal infinitive").** An infinitive like `látni` may
take personal endings — `látnom, látnod, látnia, látnunk, látnotok, látniuk` ("my seeing," "your
seeing," etc.) — used (1) with adjectives (`Szabad kérnem?` "May I ask?"; when a noun/pronoun referent
is explicit and in the dative, the infinitive's own personal ending may be dropped) and (2) with
`kell`/`lehet` (below). pp. 329–330.

**C. Kell, Lehet.** These "impersonal auxiliary" forms appear in three constructions: with the
personal infinitive (`Látnom kell` "I have to see it," lit. "my seeing is necessary"); with the bare
infinitive (`Hol kell leszállni?` "Where do I have to get off?"); with a dative-marked subject plus
infinitive (`Máriának kell ennie` "Mary has to eat"). p. 330.

**D. Transitive–intransitive pairs.** A small closed set of verb pairs contrast transitive/causative
vs. intransitive/passive-like meaning: `épít` "he builds" / `épül` "it is being built"; `akaszt` "he
hangs" / `akad` "it is being caught"; `gyújt` "he lights" / `gyúl` "it is catching fire"; `termel` "he
produces" / `terem` "it is growing (of its own accord)"; `füröszt` "he bathes someone" / `fürdik` "he
takes a bath." Hungarian has no general passive-voice mechanism corresponding to every English
passive; where English names an agent in a passive sentence, Hungarian normally uses the active voice
with the agent as subject. Where English uses a passive with *no* agent named, Hungarian commonly
uses the indefinite 3rd-person-plural of the active verb instead (`Csak azokat veszik fel...` "Only
those are accepted..." lit. "they accept only those..."). pp. 330–331.

**E. Past conditional.** A compound tense: past tense of the main verb + invariable `volna`. Has a
full definite/indefinite paradigm for transitive verbs (only the past-tense component inflects,
`volna` never changes: `láttam volna`/`láttam volna` [indef.] vs. `láttam volna`... i.e. `láttam
volna` (indef.) vs. `láttam volna` — precisely, indefinite `láttam volna` vs. definite `láttam volna`;
source table: indefinite `láttam/láttál/látott/láttunk/láttatok/láttak` + `volna`, definite
`láttam/láttad/látta/láttuk/láttátok/látták` + `volna`); intransitive verbs use only the indefinite
form (`mentem volna`, `jöttem volna`). Used for contrary-to-fact statements about past time
(`Ha ott lettem volna, beszéltem volna vele` "If I had been there, I would've spoken to him"). When
the "if" clause is past-conditional but the result refers to *present* time, the result clause uses
the *present* conditional instead (`Ha tanult volna tegnap, most tudná a leckét` "If you had studied
last night, you'd know the lesson now"). pp. 331–332.

**F. Adjectival forms in -i, -ú/-ű.** `-i` derives an adjective from a noun (`budapesti lányok`
"girls from Budapest," `egyetemi tanár` "university professor," `ipari tanuló` "industrial
apprentice") and may also attach to adverbs (`mai gyerekek` "today's children," `tegnapi kenyér`
"yesterday's bread"). `-ú/-ű` (with front/back and rounded variants `-jú/-jű`) derives an adjective
meaning "having N," parallel to English "-ed" possessive compounds: `szemű` "-eyed," `hajú` "-haired,"
`zeneiű`→`zeneju` [sic, source's own irregular spelling] "-toned/musical," e.g. `feketehajú`
"black-haired," `kékszemű` "blue-eyed," `négylábú` "four-footed." pp. 332–333.

### Unit 16

**A. Postpositions (continued) — the three-way whither/where/whence system.** A closed set of
Hungarian postpositions has three parallel forms depending on direction: **Hova?** "(to) where"
(motion toward), **Hol?** "where" (location), **Honnan?** "from where" (motion away). Table:
`alá/alatt/alól` (under), `elé/előtt/elöl` (in front of), `fölé/fölött/fölül` (above), `mellé/
mellett/mellől` (beside), `mögé/mögött/mögül` (behind), `közé/között/közül` (among/between), `köré/
körül/—` (around, no "whence" form given), `—/—/felől` (from the direction of, no "whither"/"where"
forms — `felé` alone serves "whither"). Each of the three forms may itself take a personal ending,
tabulated in full for `alá-/alatt-`, `fölé-/fölött-`, `felé-`, `mellé-/mellett-` (e.g. `alám, alád,
alá(ja), alánk, alátok, alájuk` for "to under me/you/him.../us/you.pl/them" vs. `alattam, alattad,
alatta...` for "under me/you/him..."). The "whither" column parallels the function of `-ba/-be`
(illative), the "where" column parallels `-ban/-ben` (inessive). Some postpositions have only one
form/stem, personally inflected directly: `miattam/miattad/miatta...` (because of me/you/him...),
`helyettem/helyetted/helyette...` (instead of me/you/him...), `nélkülem/nélküled/nélküle...` (without
me/you/him...). Some postpositions are wholly invariable, taking no personal endings at all: `innen,
túl, át, keresztül, végig, képest`. pp. 349–350.

**B. Postpositions with demonstrative forms.** Parallel to the pattern for "this/these" agreeing with
its noun (`ez a könyv`), a postposition phrase with a demonstrative repeats the postposition on both
the demonstrative and the noun: `ez alatt az asztal alatt` "under this table," `a mögött a ház mögött`
"behind that house," `azok fölött a székek fölött` "above those chairs." `ez` behaves like `az` before
a consonant in this pattern (`e miatt az ember miatt`, `e nélkül a lány nélkül`) — a reduced `e` form.
p. 351.

**C. The possessive in -é or -éi.** `-é` added to a noun/pronoun means "that/those belonging to X,"
i.e. "X's" used predicatively/absolutely rather than attributively (`Az én könyvem fekete, a magáé
szürke` "My book is black, yours is gray"; `Kié az a pénz?` "Whose money is that?"). The plural is
`-éi` ("those of"). **Colloquial register note:** colloquial Hungarian tends to use the singular
`-é` even where the referent is logically plural, preferring the plural `-éi` only where the plural
meaning would otherwise be unclear from context. Irregular possessive-pronoun forms are given:
`enyém/mienk` (mine/ours), `tied/tietek` (yours.sg/yours.pl), `övé/övék` (his-hers/theirs), with
corresponding plural-referent forms `enyéim/mieink`, `tieid/tieitek`, `övéi/övéik`. pp. 351–352.

**D. Impersonal subject.** Three constructions render an unspecified-agent "people (say/do), one
(says/does), it is (said/done)": `azt` + 3rd-plural verb (`Azt mondják` "people say, it is said");
`az ember` + 3rd-singular verb (`Az ember nem tudja` "one doesn't know"); and the bare infinitive
alone (`Nem tudni` "it isn't known, one doesn't know"). The plain personal 3rd-plural form (`Nem
tudják`) can carry the same impersonal reading. p. 352.

**E. Appositive.** An appositive noun phrase (e.g. "Smith, the carpenter") always takes the *same
case* as the noun/pronoun it modifies in Hungarian: `Kovácstól, az ügyvédtől kaptam levelet` "I got a
letter from Kovács, the lawyer" (both in the ablative `-tól/-től`); `Fehérrel, az íróval mentem` "I
went with Fehér, the writer" (both instrumental `-val/-vel`). p. 352.

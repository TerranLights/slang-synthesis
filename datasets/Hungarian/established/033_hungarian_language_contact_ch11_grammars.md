trajectory from the "cycle" model and, per the chapter, may be specific to contact-induced change
rather than attested in language-internal change generally.

## Grammar points

### 1. Methodological framing: which universals actually predict contact-induced change

The chapter opens by narrowing Greenberg's (1963) four-way universal typology (absolute
unconditional, statistical unconditional, absolute implicational, statistical implicational) to the
two **implicational** types as the only ones capable of predicting or explaining contact-induced
grammatical change: absolute/statistical unconditional universals hold regardless of contact (so
contact cannot violate or illuminate them), but implicational universals and hierarchies
("if P then Q," or a ranked hierarchy R > Q > P where having any one property predicts having every
property ranked below it) make a testable prediction — if a contact-affected variety's value for P
changes, the hierarchy predicts Q should shift too. The chapter's whole method is to test this
prediction against the concrete HH→HO differences already documented in the volume's preceding
chapters. pp. 374–375

### 2. Number marking: HO is drifting from a "set noun" language to a "singular object noun" language

Using Rijkhoff's (2002) six-way noun typology, the chapter argues Hungarian in Hungary (HH) is a
**set noun** language (nouns denote a set of any cardinality, including one; combined directly with a
numeral, they take no plural marking — *két könyv* 'two book(s)', not *"két könyvek*) rather than a
**singular object noun** language like Dutch or English (where a bare noun obligatorily denotes a
single individual, requiring explicit plural marking with a numeral). On this analysis, HH's -k suffix
is more accurately a **collective marker** than a true plural, since it doesn't behave like plural
marking in an Indo-European sense. The chapter's central empirical claim: HO varieties across multiple
countries have begun using -k as a genuine plural marker in exactly the contexts a singular-object-
noun language requires it (after numerals, in specific possessive constructions like *fájnak a
lábaim* 'my legs are aching' vs. HH's singular *fáj a lábam*) — i.e. contact isn't just adding
isolated borrowed forms, it's shifting HO's *entire noun-typology classification*. A closely linked
second prediction (from Rijkhoff's independent finding that set-noun languages tend to show
subject-verb "number discord," e.g. a numeral-quantified subject taking a singular verb) is likewise
confirmed: HO shows number *concord* (plural verb agreement) in exactly the contexts where set-noun-
typology HH shows discord — directly cross-referencing the *Feri és Mari sétálni mennek* (Vojvodina,
`028_...md`) and *mind a három fijú ud haragusznak* (American Hungarian, `030_...md`) examples already
captured in the sibling chapters, now reframed as two independent confirmations of the same
underlying typological shift rather than two unrelated "number agreement" quirks. A footnoted parallel
case (Basque under Spanish contact, W. Jansen p.c.) is cited as independent cross-linguistic support:
young Spanish-L1 Basque learners impose singular-object-noun-style number marking/agreement onto
Basque, which is natively a set-noun language like Hungarian. pp. 375–379

### 3. Copula support: the erosion follows Hengeveld's Predicativity Hierarchy exactly

Hungarian is classified (following Hengeveld 1992) as a **Zero1**-strategy language for non-verbal
predication: bare/adjectival predicates (*Mari okos* 'Mary is clever') and referential/nominal
predicates (*Péter diák* 'Peter is a student') take no copula, while relational predicates —
locational (*a macska a kertben van* 'the cat is in the garden') and possessive (*Tamásnak könyve
van* 'Tom has a book') — require the copula *van*. This pattern is argued to follow Hengeveld's
Predicativity Hierarchy (Bare > Referential > Relational [> Non-presentative > Presentative]) with
HH's cut-off point falling cleanly between Referential and Relational, in both main and embedded
(non-finite, participial) clauses. The chapter's key finding: **in HO, this same hierarchy still
holds for main/finite clauses, but the cut-off point shifts further along the hierarchy specifically
in embedded non-finite constructions** — many HO speakers drop the copula-bearing participial
construction (*a falon levő kép*, lit. 'the on-the-wall being picture') in favor of a simple
juxtaposition (*a kép a falon* 'the picture on the wall'), directly cross-referencing the Vojvodina/
Prekmurje word-order finding already documented in `028_...md` (Grammar point 9, the "adjectival use
of preposed adverbs" SHOH variable) and reanalyzing it as a Predicativity-Hierarchy cut-off shift
rather than a simple word-order preference. The chapter explicitly frames this as confirmation of a
general typological principle (citing Dik 1997): cross-linguistic variation concentrates precisely at
hierarchy cut-off points, not randomly across a hierarchy's whole range — the HO change is exactly
where general typological theory would predict variation to appear if it appears anywhere. A related,
not-fully-resolved observation: the copula-dropping pattern correlates with a word-order shift
(head-final non-finite modifier → head-initial finite-like juxtaposition), which the chapter notes
patterns identically to Hungarian's independent head/dependent-order distinction between non-finite
and finite relative clauses generally — suggested as an avenue for further typological research rather
than a settled conclusion. pp. 379–383

### 4. Person marking typology: three types and a proposed diachronic cycle

Following De Groot & Limburg (1986), building on Givón (1976), the chapter frames person-marking
systems as cycling through three types: **Free Pronoun** type (independent pronouns as separate,
extra-clausal theme/topic constituents), **Clitic** type (cliticized onto the clause), and
**Appositional** type (the former pronoun has fully grammaticalized into a bound person-marking affix
on the verb/noun/adposition, with a free pronoun or NP now optional and appositional to it) — with
languages cycling Free Pronoun → Clitic → Appositional → Free Pronoun over historical time. Within
Appositional-type languages, two further hierarchies are established: **Verb > Noun > Adposition**
(if person marking appears on only one category it will be the Verb; if on two, Verb and Noun) and
**Subject > Object** (if a verb marks person for only one argument, it will be the Subject; Object
marking implies Subject marking too, never the reverse). pp. 383–385

### 5. Person marking: HO shows measurable "three unrelated" symptoms of one single typological shift

HH is classified as Appositional-type for the Verb and Noun categories (person marking on both, e.g.
*énekelnek* '(they) sing' and *a kabátja* 'his/her coat', with free pronouns/NPs optionally
appositional), but Adpositional-type constructions with a free pronoun/NP extension (e.g. *ő
mellette* 'beside him/her' with an overt pronoun) are obsolete in Modern Hungarian — only surviving
cliticized (*őmellette*, one phonological word) — placing HH's cut-off point in the Verb>Noun>
Adposition hierarchy between Noun and Adposition. HH additionally has Subject person marking on the
verb but not true Object person marking (only definiteness *agreement*, itself fused into the Subject
marker) — placing its Subject>Object cut-off between the two. The chapter's central argument: **three
apparently unrelated HO phenomena that this volume's other chapters documented as separate,
independent findings are in fact three symptoms of a single underlying typological shift** — HO moving
from Appositional-type back toward Free-Pronoun-type along the predicted cycle:
(i) **overt pronoun preference**, directly cross-referencing the redundant-pronoun findings from
Vojvodina/Prekmurje (`028_...md`, Grammar point 12) and American Hungarian (`031_...md`, Grammar point
10's overt-object-pronoun SHOH results) — now reanalyzed as the *first step* of the Appositional→Free
Pronoun cycle rather than a standalone "hypercorrection" or "contact" curiosity; (ii) **loss of
possessive suffixes on nouns**, cross-referencing the American Hungarian and Oberwart Hungarian
findings already captured in `031_...md` and `029_...md` — reanalyzed as the person-marking
Verb>Noun>Adposition cut-off point shifting from between Noun/Adposition to between Verb/Noun; and
(iii) **loss of Object agreement (definite/indefinite conjugation neutralization)**, cross-referencing
the conjugation-mixing findings in `030_...md` (Grammar point 6) — reanalyzed as the Subject>Object
cut-off collapsing entirely onto Subject-only marking, a property characteristic of Free-Pronoun-type
languages generally. The chapter's punchline: HO isn't independently losing three unrelated
grammatical features under contact pressure — it is measurably further along the *same* universal
diachronic cycle than HH, and the reason is straightforward: every contact language involved (Serbian,
Slovenian, German, English) is itself already a Free-Pronoun-type language, so contact effectively
accelerates HO along a path HH would eventually travel on its own regardless. pp. 385–388

### 6. Morphological complexity: synthetic-to-analytic shift spans inflection, derivation, AND compounding simultaneously

The chapter compiles a summary table (Table 11.1, paraphrased here) of four unrelated synthetic-vs-
analytic contrast pairs already documented piecemeal across the volume's other chapters, now grouped
explicitly by morphological domain: **inflection** (modality: HH's inflectional potential suffix
-hat/-het vs. HO's periphrastic *tud* + infinitive, cross-referencing the Ukraine/general SHOH findings
and the Austria-specific findings in `029_...md`); **derivation, reflexive-formation type** (HH's
argument-reducing reflexive suffix -kezik vs. HO's argument-preserving reflexive-pronoun strategy with
*magát*, Ukraine); **derivation, causative-formation type** (HH's causative suffix -tat/-tet vs. HO's
periphrastic *hagy* 'let, permit' + infinitive, cross-referencing the Austria Oberwart findings already
fully captured with morpheme glosses in `029_...md`); and **compounding** (HH's synthetic compounds
like *lég-tér* 'air space' and *tag-létszám* 'membership count' vs. HO's disjunct
attributive-marker or possessive-construction paraphrases, cross-referencing the *légi tér*/*légtér*
and *tagsági díj*/*tagdíj* examples already captured in `028_...md` and the *baromfis ól*/*baromfiól*
example from `029_...md`). The chapter's key structural observation: it is **unusual, not merely
common**, for a synthetic-to-analytic shift to occur across all three morphological domains
(inflection, derivation, compounding) simultaneously in one contact situation, and *especially*
unusual that the shift **skips the "natural intermediate stage" of inflection** that historical
linguistics would ordinarily expect (see Grammar point 8) — the chapter flags this as a genuinely
puzzling co-occurrence pattern worth further typological investigation, not a settled explanation. pp.
387–390

### 7. Each specific synthetic-to-analytic swap is an "overextension" of an existing minority pattern, not an innovation from nothing

For three of the four domains, the chapter makes a specific structural claim: HH already possesses the
analytic alternative construction, just used only in a narrower context, and HO's change is simply
**overextension** of that already-existing HH pattern to a wider range of contexts — not the
importation of a wholly foreign structure. Evidence: the reflexive-pronoun strategy with *magát*
already exists in HH for verbs like *lát* 'see' (*látja magát a tükörben* 's/he sees him/herself in
the mirror') — HO simply extends this existing transitive-marking strategy to verbs that HH would
instead handle with the dedicated reflexive suffix -kezik. Likewise, *hagy* 'let, permit' already
functions as a limited causative auxiliary in HH (*hagyja a tervet fejlődni* 's/he lets the plan
develop') — HO extends this existing usage to displace the dedicated causative suffix -tat/-tet more
broadly. And the attributive-marker/possessive-construction disjunct-compound strategies (-i/-s
attributive suffixes, e.g. *a kerti ház* 'the garden house'; and the possessive construction generally)
are both fully grammatical, productively used HH patterns for *other* nominal-compounding cases — HO
simply widens their application to cases where HH specifically prefers the compound instead. The
chapter's structural generalization: **complex-to-simplex change is only possible where an alternative
analytic pattern is already independently available and grammatical in the source language** — in
domains where HH has no existing analytic alternative pattern at all, the chapter predicts (and implies
the data confirms) that the synthetic form persists unchanged even under heavy contact pressure. This
is a testable, falsifiable claim about *which* synthetic forms should be vulnerable to contact-induced
simplification and which should not. pp. 389–391

### 8. This morphological change is NOT the classic agglutinating→inflectional→isolating cycle

The chapter makes a sharp, explicit distinction from the standard historical-linguistics account of
morphological cyclicity (quoting Hock & Joseph 1996 directly): the textbook cycle runs
isolating → agglutinating → inflectional (via fusion of separate affixes into single portmanteau
morphemes) → isolating again (via sound change and analogy eroding the fused inflectional system).
The chapter's key empirical observation: **HO forms like szépítette, széket, and létszáma remain
clearly agglutinating** — none of the HO analytic constructions show any sign of affix-fusion into a
new inflectional system, nor any sign of the kind of isolating-language reduction that would follow
inflectional fusion. Instead, the chapter proposes HH→HO change instantiates a **different**,
narrower typological parameter entirely: **multi-morphemic → less-multi-morphemic**, still fully
within the agglutinating type throughout — the language doesn't change its fundamental morphological
type (agglutinating stays agglutinating), it just uses shorter, less internally-complex agglutinated
word-forms, favoring periphrastic/disjunct constructions over highly-stacked single-word forms where
an alternative already exists. The chapter explicitly flags this as an open research question: whether
this "less-multi-morphemic while remaining agglutinating" trajectory is a pattern found only in
contact-induced change, or whether it also occurs in language-internal change without any contact
situation at all — if the latter turns out to be false, the chapter suggests the standard historical-
linguistics cyclicity account (as stated by Hock & Joseph) would need revision to accommodate this
distinct pathway. pp. 389–390

### 9. Chapter's own conclusion: four unifying parameters explain the whole cross-chapter pattern

The chapter's explicit summary claim, presented as its central contribution to the volume: nearly
every HH→HO difference documented across this book's country-specific chapters (Chapters 3–10, i.e.
including all of the Vojvodina/Prekmurje, Austria, USA, and Australia material already captured in the
sibling files `028`–`032`) reduces to just **four underlying typological parameters**, each doing the
explanatory work of what would otherwise look like many separate, unrelated "quirks": (a) the
**Seinsarten (Modes of Being)** parameter — HO shifting from a set-noun to a singular-object-noun
language — accounts for plural marking after numerals, plural forms in certain possessive
constructions, and plural verb agreement; (b) the **Predicativity** parameter — the copula
cut-off point shifting within Hengeveld's hierarchy — accounts for copula presence/absence in
embedded relational constructions; (c) the **Person Marking Type** parameter — HO advancing further
along the Appositional→Free-Pronoun cycle than HH — jointly accounts for overt-pronoun preference,
possessive-suffix loss, and object-agreement loss, three phenomena the individual country chapters
each treated as separate findings; and (d) the **Morphological Complexity** parameter — accounts for
the parallel synthetic→analytic shift across inflection, derivation, and compounding. The chapter's
final, more cautious methodological point: while (a)–(c) each have clear counterparts in established
cross-linguistic typological theory (making HO's changes typologically *predictable*, not just
observed), (d) — the synthetic-to-analytic morphological shift — has **no attested counterpart in
general (non-contact) language typology or diachrony** that the chapter could locate; the volume's own
data may therefore be evidence that language-contact-induced change is capable of producing outcomes
that fall outside what purely-synchronic, non-contact-derived typological universals would predict —
proposed as a genuinely open question for future typological research rather than a settled finding.
pp. 391–393

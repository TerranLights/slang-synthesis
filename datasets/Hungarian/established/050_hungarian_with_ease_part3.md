## Structure of this page range and the non-redundant-supplement scoping decision

This range contains three structurally distinct kinds of content, handled differently per the
coverage rule and copyright discipline:

1. **Lessons 76-85 (printed pp283-322)** — the course's last ten dialogue-based lessons, each with a
   dialogue, notes, exercises, and (from Lesson 84) an explicit idiom/locution section. **Extracted in
   full** per the standard coverage rule — original teaching content, not previously captured by any
   established file (no prior Hungarian source in this project has extracted this book at all).
2. **Grammatical Appendix (printed pp323-341)** — a systematic paradigm-table appendix: the full noun
   case system (23 cases with suffixes), personal/demonstrative/interrogative pronoun paradigms,
   possessive-postposition paradigms, and ten full verb-conjugation tables demonstrating the Hungarian
   subjective/objective (indefinite/definite) conjugation contrast across present, past, conditional,
   future, and imperative. **Extracted in structured/paraphrased form as Grammar points** — this is
   compact, factual grammatical data (not prose/dialogue/vocabulary-box content), and it is
   substantially fuller than the case/conjugation coverage in any prior Hungarian `established/` file
   (e.g. `013_fsi_vol1_units01-04.md`, `025_complete_practical_grammar_part1.md`), so it is a genuine
   non-redundant supplement worth capturing thoroughly rather than skipping as "just another grammar

3. **Alphabetical Hungarian-English "Vocabulary" index (printed pp342-426, ~1,700 word stems per the
   book's own front-matter note on p342)** — **deliberately NOT bulk-transcribed.** This index is the

### Morpheme Breakdown — agglutinative forms worth decomposing

- **`javíttatás`** ("the act of having [something] repaired") = `jav-` (bound stem of `jó` "good") +
  `-ít` (causative suffix, "make good" → "mend/fix") + `-tat` (factitive/permissive, "have [someone]
  do the causing") + `-ás` (deverbal noun suffix) — a source-explained four-morpheme chain
  (causative→factitive→nominalization) stacked on one root. Lesson 80, Note 4, p. 300.
- **`háromnapos`, `bőséges`, `hűséges`** (adjective-forming pattern) = noun stem + `-s`/`-ú`/`-ű`/`-i`
  suffix, each encoding a distinct semantic relation the source itself lays out: `-s` "having/provided
  with" (`háromnapos` "three-day[-long]," lit. "having three days"), `-ú`/`-ű` "having/possessing [a
  quality]" (cf. `nagyszájú` "big-mouthed," Lesson 82), `-i` "occurring/being/dwelling in, originating
  from" (e.g. `testi` "bodily" from `test` "body," `földi` "earthly" from `föld` "earth/land"). Lesson
  81, Note (1) and Note (cont.), p. 304.
- **`nagyszájú`** ("big-mouthed," of a person who talks too much) = `nagy` ("big") + `száj` ("mouth")
  + `-ú` (possessive/"having" adjectival suffix) — cited by the source alongside `Aranyszájú Szent
  János` ("St John Chrysostom," lit. "Golden-Mouthed St John") and `csempeszájú` ("with chapped lips")
  as the same derivational pattern; contrasted with the near-homophonous but differently-suffixed
  `szájas` ("cheeky") and `száji` ("oral," medical register) — three distinct suffixes on the same root
  yielding three distinct, non-overlapping meanings. Lesson 82, Note (1), p. 308.
- **Preverb + verb compounding (a fully systematic, highly productive pattern):** the Grammatical
  Appendix/Lesson 84 material tabulates preverbs as directional/aspectual prefixes freely combining
  with verb stems, e.g. **`haza-`** ("home[ward]") + `megy` → `hazamegy` ("go home"); `haza-` + `kísér`
  → `hazakísér` ("see/accompany home"); **`vissza-`** ("back") + `ad` → `visszaad` ("give back, repay");
  `vissza-` + `néz` → `visszanéz` ("look back"); **`össze-`** ("together") + `néz` → `összenéz`
  ("exchange glances"); **`túl-`** ("over") + `él` → `túlél` ("survive"); `túl-` + `lép` → `túllép`
  ("overstep, exceed") — the same preverb yields highly divergent, often non-compositional meanings
  depending on the base verb, a core Hungarian word-formation mechanism directly relevant to future
  slang-derivation analysis (many colloquial verb senses in this and other Hungarian sources arise from
  exactly this preverb-swap mechanism). Lesson 84 grammar note, p. 317.

## Grammar points

### Lessons 76-85 — narrative/register range of the course's closing chapters

The final ten lessons deliberately diversify register and genre beyond the earlier units' service-
encounter dialogues: Lesson 77 (geography/nationality vocabulary), Lesson 78 (a formal border-control
dialogue), Lesson 79 (a first-person retelling of a folktale — the "fisherman and the magic fish"
international tale type, in past-tense narrative register), Lesson 80 (a comic first-person "driving
confessions" monologue), Lesson 81 (three back-to-back invented **news bulletins** — a diplomatic
visit, a crime report, a weather forecast — explicitly modelling **journalistic/official register**
locutions), Lesson 82 (a folk-proverb collection themed around animals), Lesson 83 (office/workplace
register: minutes, regulations, confidentiality), Lesson 84 (an explicit metalinguistic lesson on
preverbs, governed postpositions, and idioms, citing O. Nagy Gábor's *Magyar szólások és közmondások*
by name), and Lesson 85 ("...and last lesson" — a farewell/course-wrap-up dialogue plus author's
closing remarks in first-person plural, breaking the pedagogical frame to address the reader directly
about how to continue studying after the book ends).

### Lesson 80 — the frequentative verb pattern

Confirms and extends a general Hungarian derivational pattern (verb stem + frequentative suffix,
e.g. `int` → `integet`) explicitly cross-referenced by the source to two earlier lessons (43, 76) not
in this dispatch's range — flagged here as a pattern worth checking for in a future Lessons 1-75
extraction, since the source treats it as already-established by that point.

### Lesson 81 — "locutions" as a named register category distinct from idioms

The source explicitly distinguishes two classes of fixed multi-word expressions: **locutions**
(invariable but semantically transparent/compositional verb+object collocations typical of formal/
journalistic register, e.g. `sajtótájékoztatót tart` "hold a press conference") versus **szólások**
(idioms proper, semantically non-compositional, e.g. `csütörtököt mond`). This is a genuinely useful
register-taxonomy distinction for the mechanics-analysis phase: locutions are the formal-register
analog of what slang produces informally (fixed verb+noun collocations with a conventionalized,
narrower sense than either word alone).

### Lesson 84 — Required postpositions/suffixes governed by specific verbs

The source tabulates roughly a dozen verbs that obligatorily govern a specific case-suffix on their
complement (parallel to English "depend **on**," "afraid **of**"), explicitly cross-referenced to the
Grammatical Appendix's case-suffix list: `örül + -nak/-nek` ("rejoice at"), `segít + -nak/-nek +
-ban/-ben` ("help sb with"), `hív = -nak/-nek` ("call sb sth"), `tetszik = -nak/-nek` ("be liked by"),
`foglalkozik + -val/-vel` ("deal with"), `megismerkedik + -val/-vel` ("get acquainted with"),
`vitatkozik + -val/-vel` ("discuss/debate"), `csináltat + -val/-vel` ("have sth made by sb"),
`haragszik + -ra/-re` ("be angry with"), `gondol + -ra/-re` ("think of"), `hasonlít + -ra/-re` ("take
after"), `büszke + -ra/-re` ("be proud of"), `függ + -tól/-től` ("depend on"), `fél + -tól/-től` ("be
afraid of"), `elbúcsúzik + -tól/-től` ("say goodbye to"), `kér/kérdez + -tól/-től` ("ask sb sth/a
question"). The source also notes a systematic correspondence between a verb's own preverb and the
suffix it governs on its complement (e.g. `rá-`/`föl-` verbs often govern `-ra`; `ki-` verbs often
govern `-ból/-ből`; `be-`/`bele-` verbs often govern `-ba/-be`) — a real, source-stated
preverb-suffix-agreement pattern worth flagging for morphology-mechanics analysis. p. 317.

### Grammatical Appendix, "Cases, or Declension of the Noun" (pp324-326) — full 23-case suffix list

The single richest systematic table in this dispatch. The appendix explicitly frames Hungarian's noun
suffixes as substituting for the Indo-European preposition/pronoun system, and gives what it calls
"possibly the most complete list": **Nominative** (Ø, `ház` "house"), **Accusative** (`-t`, `házat`),
**Dative** (`-nak/-nek`, `háznak` "to the house"), **Instrumental** (`-val/-vel`, `házzal` "with the
house"), **Final** (`-ért`, `házért` "for the house"), **Transformative** (`-vá/-vé`, `házzá`
"[transform] into a house"), **Superessive** (`-n`, `házon` "on the house"), **Sublative** (`-ra/-re`,
`házra` "onto the house"), **Delative** (`-ról/-ről`, `házról` "about/off the house"), **Inessive**
(`-ban/-ben`, `házban` "in the house"), **Elative** (`-ból/-ből`, `házból` "out of the house"),
**Illative** (`-ba/-be`, `házba` "into the house"), **Adessive** (`-nál/-nél`, `háznál` "at the house"),
**Allative** (`-hoz/-hez/-höz`, `házhoz` "to[wards] the house"), **Ablative** (`-tól/-től`, `háztól`
"from the house"), **Terminative** (`-ig`, `házig` "as far as the house"), **Modal** (`-ként`, `házként`
"as a house"), **Essive** (`-ul/-ül`, restricted to specific nouns, e.g. `feleségül` "as [a] wife,"
`angolul` "in English"), **Temporal** (`-kor`, `éjfélkor` "at midnight"), **Comitative** (`-stul/-stül`,
`családostul` "in the company of the family"), **Distributive-temporal** (`-nta/-nte`, `naponta`
"daily"), **Distributive** (`-nként`, `fejenként` "per head"), and **Modal-2** (`-képpen`,
`ajándékképpen` "as a present"). This is a fuller enumeration (23 named cases) than the case coverage
in the FSI Basic Course or Complete Practical Grammar extractions already in `established/` (which
name roughly 15-18), making it a genuine non-redundant supplement per the coverage rule — the
Essive, Comitative, and two Distributive cases in particular are not confirmed as separately captured
in prior Hungarian `established/` files.

### Grammatical Appendix, "The Pronouns" (pp326-328) — personal, demonstrative, interrogative paradigms

Full paradigm tables give **personal pronouns** (`én/te/ő/ön` singular, `mi/ti/ők/önök` plural) fully
declined across all 17 of the appendix's core cases (e.g. dative `nekem/neked/neki/önnek`, elative
`belőlem/belőled/belőle/önből`, allative `hozzám/hozzád/hozzá/önhöz`) — confirming that Hungarian
personal pronouns in oblique cases are themselves suffixed forms rather than a separate closed set, the
same mechanism as noun declension. **Demonstrative pronouns** (`ez`/`ezek` "this/these" vs. `az`/`azok`
"that/those") are given the identical 17-case paradigm, with the systematic doubling-consonant pattern
before case suffixes attached to `az` (`az`+`-nak`→`annak`, `az`+`-val`→`azzal`, `az`+`-ra`→`arra`) that
distinguishes it from the transparent `ez`-paradigm (`ez`+`-nek`→`ennek`, `ez`+`-vel`→`ezzel`,
`ez`+`-re`→`erre`). **Interrogative pronouns** (`ki?`/`kik?` "who?" vs. `mi?`/`mik?` "what?") get the
same paradigm again, confirming person/non-person is the split governing which interrogative stem is
used, parallel to the demonstrative and personal-pronoun systems. pp. 326-327.

### Grammatical Appendix, "Possessive Postpositions" (pp328-off) — locative postposition triads with personal suffixes

A further systematic table gives Hungarian's locative postpositions in the same three-way
where-to/where-at/where-from pattern already documented from the Teach Yourself Hungarian source's
`innen/onnan/itt/ott/ide/oda` adverb set (`035_tyh_units06-11.md`/earlier TYH files) and the FSI course,
but here applied to full postposition triads with personal-suffix paradigms: `alá/alatt/alól`
("under[-to]/underneath/from-under"), `mellé/mellett/mellől` ("to-beside/beside/from-beside"),
`elé/előtt/elől` ("before[-to]/before/from-before") — each fully conjugated for all six persons
(`alám/alád/alá/alánk/alátok/alájuk`, etc.), showing that Hungarian postpositions, like the case-suffix
system itself, take a full possessive-style personal-agreement paradigm rather than combining with a
separate pronoun the way English "under me/you/him" does. This generalizes the locative-postposition
pattern (previously only spot-documented for `innen/onnan` etc.) into a fully systematic closed
paradigm class. p. 328 onward.

### Grammatical Appendix, "Verbs" — the subjective/objective (indefinite/definite) conjugation system, ten full paradigms

The appendix's verb tables are organized by a legend of **"s.c. = subjective conjugation"** (indefinite
object or no object) vs. **"o.c. = objective conjugation"** (definite/specified object) — the two full
present/past/conditional/future/imperative-singular-and-plural paradigms are given side by side for
five verbs: `vár` ("wait"), `kér` ("ask"), `hoz` ("bring"), `néz` ("look"), `iszik`/`eszik` ("drink"/
"eat," the productive -ik-verb class). Representative present-tense singular contrast (all verbs show
the identical s.c./o.c. split pattern): `kérek` (s.c., "I ask [something/anything]") vs. `kérem` (o.c.,
"I ask [for] it/the specific thing"); `nézek`/`nézem`; `iszom`/`iszom` (1sg happens to coincide for
`iszik`, but 2sg `iszol`/`iszod` and 3sg `iszik`/`issza` diverge). Three further verbs (`van`/`lenni`
"be," `megy`/`menni` "go," `dolgozik`/`dolgozni` "work," `jön`/`jönni` "come") are given only in a
single (subjective-only) paradigm, since they are intransitive and structurally cannot take the
objective conjugation. This is the fullest, most systematically side-by-side demonstration of the
definite/indefinite conjugation contrast found in any Hungarian `established/` file to date — prior
sources (Rounds, FSI) introduce the distinction piecemeal per-lesson rather than laying out ten full
parallel paradigm tables together. pp. 329-340.

### Vocabulary Index (pp342-426) — the book's own inflection-class notation convention

The book's closing ~1,700-entry alphabetical index (not bulk-transcribed here, see coverage-scoping
note above) systematically brackets, after every noun, its **accusative, possessive, and plural**
forms (e.g. `madár [madarat, madara, madarak]` "bird"; `anyag [-ot, -a, -ok]` "material," using a
hyphen-elision convention once the stem is established); after every adjective, its **accusative,
plural, and comparative** forms (e.g. `boldog [-ot, -ok, -abb]` "happy"); and after every verb, its
**3rd-person-singular past, imperative, and conditional** (subjective-conjugation) forms (e.g.
`haragszik [haragudott, haragudjon, haragudna]` "be mad/angry"; `választ [-ott, válasszon, -ana]`
"choose, select"). A trailing number is the lesson of first occurrence. This is a genuinely useful
systematic morphological resource — every single one of the ~1,700 entries doubles as a compact
principal-parts/inflection-class citation, which is exactly the kind of information the mechanics-
analysis phase needs to classify a word's conjugation/declension class at a glance — but reproducing
all 1,700 rows would be pure bulk-copying of a dictionary, which copyright discipline explicitly
prohibits; this note documents the convention itself as the extractable content instead.

## Notable findings (colloquial/register/period vocabulary)

1. **"Locutions" vs. "szólások" (idioms) as an explicit two-tier register taxonomy** (Lesson 81 vs. 84)
   — the source itself distinguishes formal/journalistic fixed collocations (`sajtótájékoztatót tart`)
   from genuinely idiomatic, non-compositional expressions (`csütörtököt mond`), citing O. Nagy Gábor's
   *Magyar szólások és közmondások* (~20,000 entries) as the authoritative collection. This is a
   valuable structural finding for the mechanics-analysis phase: it gives a named, source-endorsed
   two-tier model (transparent-but-fixed formal collocation vs. opaque idiom) that maps reasonably well
   onto how slang often works (a fixed collocation first, opacity/idiomaticity developing later).
2. **`viszlát`** as the closing word of the entire 85-lesson course, explicitly glossed by the authors
   as "how one says [goodbye] among friends" — i.e. the informal-register clipped form, deliberately
   chosen over the fuller `viszontlátásra` for the book's own final line, a small but real piece of
   register-consciousness on the authors' part (see `viszontlátásra` vs. `viszlát` register pair, also
   independently documented for `szia`/`szevasz`/`szervusz` in `034_tyh_units01-05.md`).
3. **Lesson 81's three invented news bulletins** are the single clearest sustained example of Hungarian
   **journalistic/official register** found in this book — a genuinely different register sample from
   this book's otherwise uniformly conversational/dialogue register, giving locutions like `italt
   fogyaszt` ("consume alcohol") as the formal-register counterpart to plain `iszik` ("drink"), directly
   parallel in kind to the krumpli/burgonya-style formal/informal lexical pairs already documented from
   Teach Yourself Hungarian.
4. **No `[B]`/`[C]`/`[S]`-style dialectal tags or explicit comparative regional-variant annotations**
   appear anywhere in this range — like the other Hungarian courses in this project, this is a
   single-dialect (standard) course; the Alföld reference (Lesson 80) is geographic/cultural color, not
   a marked dialectal form.
5. The Grammatical Appendix's own framing (p325: **"Twenty-three nominal cases! It's impressive, isn't
   it?"**) is itself a small piece of authorial voice/tone worth noting — the appendix explicitly
   acknowledges the case-vs-suffix analysis is a foreign-learner simplification imposed on a system that
   Hungarian itself doesn't natively think of as "cases," a genuine linguistic-typology caveat stated
   by the source itself rather than assumed by this extraction.

# Hungarian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Language Contact Outside Hungary, Chapter 10 — Hungarian in Australia

**Source:** Anna Fenyvesi (ed.), *Hungarian Language Contact Outside Hungary: Studies on Hungarian
as a Minority Language* (IMPACT: Studies in Language and Society 20, John Benjamins, 2005), Chapter
10, "Hungarian in Australia" by Magdolna Kovács. PDF pages 342–373 (full chapter). Part of a sequence
of sibling chunks covering PDF pages 226–448 of this volume; Chapter 9 (United States) is covered in
`030_hungarian_language_contact_ch9_usa_part1.md` and `031_..._part2.md`, Chapter 11 (comparative
grammars) follows in `033_hungarian_language_contact_ch11_grammars.md`.

**Coverage note.** This chapter is unusual among this volume's chapters in explicitly adopting a
named theoretical framework — Tandefelt's (1988) three-dimensional (time × societal/group/individual
level × sociocultural/linguistic factors) model of language maintenance/shift — and in giving
substantial space to a distinct, well-developed **code-switching morphology model** (a distance-based
continuum from smooth borrowing-like switching to a fully composite matrix language) that is the
chapter author's own original contribution (M. Kovács 2001a, 2001b), not merely a summary of others'
findings. Per the coverage rule, both the sociolinguistic-maintenance framework and the
code-switching model are captured in full as grammar points, alongside the chapter's structural
(phonological/morphological/syntactic) contact findings and its lexical-borrowing examples. Source has
a clean text layer (`pdftotext -layout`); no vision-reading was needed and no marginalia risk applies.
Extensive demographic/statistical material (census tables, language-maintenance-rate comparison
tables, generational case-marking-realization tables) is paraphrased/summarized per copyright
discipline rather than transcribed in full.

**Register/dialect annotation (explicit in source — must-capture per coverage rule).** The chapter
uses **AusHu** (Australian Hungarian) vs. **HH** (Hungarian as spoken in Hungary) throughout, and
further divides Australian Hungarian speakers into **GEN1** (first generation, migrated as adults or
after age 15), **GEN2A** (migrated to Australia as children, before age 15), and **GEN2B**
(Australian-born) — a finer three-way generational split than the simple GEN1/GEN2 distinction used
in the sibling US chapter, which the chapter uses to show statistically significant stepwise
differences in self-rated bilingual competence and domain-of-use patterns across all three groups, not
just a first/second-generation binary.

**Morphological typology note.** Hungarian is agglutinative (see `../00_Extraction_Checklist.md`).
This chapter's code-switching-morphology data is the most detailed case-suffix-by-case-suffix
generational breakdown encountered anywhere in this volume's assigned range (Table 10.2, summarized
in Grammar point 8 below) — a genuinely valuable resource for understanding which specific morphemes
in an agglutinative system erode first, and in what order, under intensive code-switching.

### Morpheme breakdown

> **Komplénoltak is érte** = komplén ("complain," borrowed English stem) + ol (denominal verbal
> thematizing suffix, obligatory on virtually all borrowed verbs in Hungarian and its diaspora
> varieties, cf. the identical pattern in American Hungarian, `031_...md`) + t (past tense) + ak (3PL)
> = "they complained about it too." Source's own worked example showing a borrowed verb is fully
> productive for further Hungarian suffixation once thematized — not a frozen or defective form. p. 361

> **NEK-I vót GIRLFRIEND-je és föl-break-ol-t-ak** ("he had a GIRLFRIEND and [they] broke up") — the
> chapter's showcase "smooth switching" example (see Grammar point 8): the English noun *girlfriend*
> takes the Hungarian 3SG possessive suffix *-je* exactly as a native noun would; the English phrasal
> verb *break up* is split per Hungarian syntax, with its particle *up* translated into the Hungarian
> preverb *föl* and placed before the verb stem (matching where a native Hungarian preverb goes), and
> the verb stem *break* itself takes the Hungarian denominal suffix *-ol* plus past tense *-t* plus 3PL
> *-ak* — full matrix-language (Hungarian) morphological congruence applied to embedded-language
> (English) lexical material, the chapter's clearest illustration of what "borrowing-type" smooth
> code-switching looks like at the morpheme level. p. 366

## Grammar points

### 1. Australian Hungarian immigration history: politically-driven, occupationally diverse — unlike the American case

The chapter identifies a strikingly different immigrant social profile than the US case documented in
Chapter 9: sporadic 19th-century Hungarian arrivals (Jewish migrants from the 1820s onward, gold-rush
adventurers), then a small 1936–40 wave of Hungarian Jews and liberal/social democrats fleeing
pre-WWII European political developments, followed by the two main postwar waves (immediately after
WWII/the 1948 communist takeover, and the much larger 1956-revolution wave). Crucially — and
explicitly contrasted with the American immigration pattern in Chapter 9, where the vast pre-WWI
wave was overwhelmingly peasant — the chapter stresses that Australian Hungarian immigration
included only a small number of peasants; "almost all social classes except peasants were
represented," spanning Holocaust survivors, aristocrats, rightists, and social/liberal democrats
alongside tradesmen and factory workers. Many refugees had not originally intended Australia as a
destination, having hoped for America or Western Europe, and only accepted Australia's offer after
extended waiting in Austrian or Italian refugee camps; many faced a second wave of culture shock upon
arrival, and an obligatory two-year government work contract placed many — including university
students and teachers — into manual labor (railway construction, power-station building in the Snowy
Mountains). pp. 342–344

### 2. Tandefelt's three-dimensional maintenance/shift model, applied systematically

The chapter's organizing framework: Tandefelt (1988), building on Haugen, Kloss, Fishman, and Giles
et al., crosses **time** with three **societal levels** (societal, group, individual) and
**sociocultural/linguistic factors**. At the **societal level**, the chapter traces Australian
language policy's sharp reversal — from an explicit pre-1970s monocultural/monolingual/assimilationist
policy (parents of non-English-speaking immigrants were officially advised to speak English to their
children even at home) to the 1984 Senate-endorsed multilingualism framework and the resulting 1987
National Policy on Languages (the "Lo Bianco Report"), which established four goals: English
competence, community-language maintenance, community-language service provision, and
second-language learning opportunities. The chapter's key point: this reversal came too late for many
languages, since Clyne's (1991) data show most community languages, Hungarian included, had already
substantially shifted toward English by 1986 — a year *before* the new policy was formalized. A
maintenance-rate comparison table (Table 10.1, cited but not reproduced) shows Hungarian's 1986
generational maintenance rates (70.6% GEN1, 49.4% GEN2, 12.8% "GEN2.5" — one first-generation and one
second-generation parent) falling in the middle of the range among the compared community languages
(higher than Dutch and German, lower than Greek and Italian). pp. 343–346

### 3. Group-level factors: dispersion, institutional fragmentation, and low regular participation

At the **group level**, the chapter finds a genuinely fragmented institutional landscape: Hungarians
concentrate in Sydney and Melbourne but even there live in only a few higher-density suburbs (limiting
neighborhood-domain language use), and have historically split into ~100–200 distinct associations
over 50–60 years (organized along political, religious, and arrival-cohort lines — e.g. separate clubs
for Hungarian Jews, distinct Catholic/Calvinist/Lutheran congregations) rather than consolidating.
Despite a genuinely active institutional ecosystem (sports clubs, scout troops, nursing homes named
after Hungarian historical figures, a triennial national "Hungarian Days" festival, 40+
short-lived newspapers/journals historically, one long-running newspaper *Magyar Élet* since 1958),
the chapter cites Kunz's (1997) estimate that only 5–10% of Australian Hungarians regularly attend
Hungarian associations/events at all — the chapter treats this low participation rate as the single
most important caveat on all its own community-derived linguistic data, since every study cited
(including the author's own) necessarily draws its subjects from this unrepresentative,
more-engaged minority. pp. 346–349

### 4. Individual-level domain analysis: a three-way generational gradient, not just a binary split

The author's own pilot questionnaire study (18 subjects at a Sydney scout club, split GEN1/GEN2A/GEN2B)
finds statistically significant stepwise differences across all three generational groups — not just
between "first" and "second" generation as a binary — in both self-rated language competence and
domain-of-use patterns. Self-rated Hungarian competence drops significantly from GEN1 to GEN2A and
again from GEN2A to GEN2B; no GEN1 subject rated their English as "perfect," and no GEN2B (Australian-
born) subject rated their Hungarian as "perfect." Domain analysis (following the same five-domain
framework used in Chapter 9) shows neighborhood is entirely English-dominated across all three groups,
work/school similarly so (no Hungarian bilingual schools in Sydney outside a Saturday school), while
home, church, and leisure-time activities remain meaningful Hungarian-maintenance domains — with a
statistically significant three-way stepwise decline in home-domain Hungarian use specifically
(GEN1 almost always Hungarian, GEN2A mostly Hungarian, GEN2B slightly more English than Hungarian).
The chapter is explicit that these relatively positive results likely overstate community-wide
maintenance, since the sample was drawn specifically from active scout-club participants — a
self-selected, more Hungarian-engaged population, consistent with the 5–10% participation caveat
above. pp. 349–352

### 5. Bilingual identity is not contingent on fluency — a closing thematic point

Interview excerpts (quoted briefly, with translation) illustrate that Hungarian ethnic identity among
second-generation Australian Hungarians persists independent of linguistic fluency or even of
spousal support: one subject's Australian husband forbade her from speaking Hungarian to their child
at home even while tolerating other forms of Hungarian cultural participation (dancing), which she
frames as reflecting "a very normal Australian view" rather than a personal conflict — the chapter
uses this to illustrate how deeply the earlier assimilationist policy climate became internalized at
the interpersonal level, beyond formal institutions. Other excerpts show second-generation subjects
articulating a stable dual (Hungarian-and-Australian) identity, and the chapter's closing quotation —
"no matter how poorly I speak Hungarian... I feel that I am somehow Hungarian" — is offered as the
chapter's explicit thesis that heritage-language maintenance is not a strict precondition for
minority-group identity persistence. pp. 352–355, 371

### 6. Phonological features largely mirror American Hungarian, despite a much shorter contact history

Although Australian Hungarian's contact situation spans only ~50–60 years (vs. American Hungarian's
century-plus), the chapter finds substantially the same phonological divergence catalog documented for
American Hungarian in Chapter 9: aspiration of voiceless stops, retroflex realization of the trilled
/r/, velarization of the lateral approximant, the /ɟ/ → [dʒ] phonemic merger (the same *gyerek* →
[dʒɛrɛk] pattern independently attested for Vojvodina Hungarian, Oberwart Hungarian, and now
Australian Hungarian — a fourth independent instance of this specific contact-induced merger across
this volume's chapters), degemination, vowel reduction, English-like stress and intonation shifts, and
(a feature not previously flagged in the sibling chapters) hypercorrective replacement of word-initial
/v/ with [w] — notably a first-generation-typical feature, unlike most of the other listed features
which concentrate in the second generation. The chapter's interpretive point: some surface-level
"intralingual" deviations (e.g. loss of v-assimilation) can still be indirectly attributed to English
influence, since the second-generation speakers producing them use English predominantly even though
the specific eroding rule (v-assimilation) has no direct English counterpart to borrow from. pp.
356–358

### 7. Borrowing vs. code-switching: a continuum, not a binary — with generational integration differences

The chapter takes an explicit theoretical stance in a genuinely contested area of contact-linguistics
methodology: rather than treating borrowing and code-switching as categorically distinct (or,
alternatively, refusing any distinction at all, as some cited researchers do), it adopts a continuum
model with fully-integrated borrowings at one end and non-integrated switches at the other, with
"nonce-loans" (words used with low corpus frequency but full grammatical integration — the author's
own working definition, explicitly distinguished from Poplack's differently-defined technical term of
the same name) occupying an intermediate zone. The chapter explicitly declines to separate nonce-loans
from fully "established" community-wide loans in its own analysis, on three stated grounds: the
distinction carries no grammatical significance (both are equally integrated); community-wide usage
frequently doesn't surface reliably in any single researcher's necessarily-limited interview corpus
(the *fani* example above); and the available Australian Hungarian database is simply not large
enough to support reliable frequency-based classification. A key empirical finding: first-generation
speakers integrate borrowed/switched English words more consistently (phonologically and
morphologically) than second-generation speakers do, even for the identical word (the *akszent*/
*accent* minimal pair). pp. 357–359

### 8. The distance-based continuum model of code-switching morphology — the chapter's original contribution

The chapter's most substantial theoretical contribution (M. Kovács 2001a, 2001b): a two-path model
describing how far a code-switched item drifts from full matrix-language (Hungarian) morphological
integration. **Path 1** (case-marking erosion): borrowing → **smooth switching** (full matrix
morphology, no hesitation — the *GIRLFRIEND-je*/*föl-break-olt-ak* example above) → **flagged
switching** (the switch is marked by hesitation or a flag word like *ilyen*/*olyan*/*ez*/*az*, with
morphology ranging from full to half to no congruence with Hungarian) → **transferred codes** (the
switched item takes a case ending that reflects direct semantic transfer from an English preposition
rather than the Hungarian-required case, e.g. sublative *-re* modeled on English "for" where Hungarian
grammar actually requires the adessive) → **bare forms** (no matrix case marking at all, either
because English lacks an overt equivalent — e.g. the zero-marked accusative — or because of online
word-search hesitation). **Path 2** (matrix-language erosion via larger embedded chunks): the same
starting point diverges instead into **double marking** (both languages' plural markers stacked on
the same word, e.g. *REFUGEES-ok-at* with both the English -s and Hungarian -k plurals) → **small
switched islands** (a whole English phrase, including its own preposition, inserted intact) → **longer
switches** (extended English chunks) → a **composite/mixed matrix language**, where no single
matrix language is clearly identifiable and the sentence draws grammatical rules from both languages
simultaneously (illustrated with a sentence where the English-switched subject takes the English
plural marker and English-pattern predicate-adjective agreement while the rest of the sentence follows
Hungarian syntax). The chapter explicitly connects this to Myers-Scotton's Matrix Language Turnover
Hypothesis, and treats the increasing frequency of the unmarked (bare) object specifically as
suggestive early evidence of incipient grammaticalization of English-like patterns within Australian
Hungarian itself. pp. 358, 362–369

### 9. Quantified generational case-marking data: not all cases erode equally in code-switching

The chapter's most granular empirical finding (Table 10.2, summarized here): across all tested cases,
first-generation code-switchers supply the Hungarian-required case ending 89.8% of the time, versus
only 57.5% for second-generation speakers — a large, statistically significant (p < .001) generational
gap. But the erosion is sharply uneven across specific cases, not a uniform across-the-board decline:
the accusative (unmarked in English) is by far the most frequently omitted case even in first-
generation speech and drops to only 52% realization in the second generation, while the dative and
inessive remain comparatively well-preserved even in second-generation code-switching (92.3% and
80.4% respectively) — other locative cases like the illative (65.0%) and superessive (57.1%) fall in
between. The chapter's interpretation: this pattern directly parallels English's own
morphological-Case-marking gaps (no case marking on English direct objects at all), so the specific
cases eroding fastest in code-switching are precisely the ones with no functional counterpart to
reinforce them from the embedded language. The chapter also flags a striking contrast with Nagy's
(1994) study of very recent, short-term-resident first-generation Hungarian immigrants in the US
(who intend to return to Hungary): those speakers supply almost complete Hungarian case marking on
switched words, unlike the more case-eroded pattern found even among Australian first-generation
speakers who have typically resided in Australia far longer — suggesting duration of residence, not
just generational status per se, independently predicts the degree of matrix-language erosion in
switching. pp. 359–365

### 10. Syntactic changes parallel the American Hungarian findings, with under-quantified coverage

The chapter finds the same broad syntactic-change catalog documented for American Hungarian (see
`030_...md`, Grammar points 10–11): English-like word order, syntactic calques (a word-by-word
translated sentence retaining only the Hungarian-required copula omission — *Ez nem egy rossz hely
lakni* 'This is not a bad place to live'), lack of pro-drop, and agreement failures across subject-
verb, predicate-adjective, quantifier-noun, and relative-pronoun-antecedent pairings — with more
English-like syntax concentrated in second-generation speech and high interspeaker variability. Unlike
the American Hungarian chapter, however, the source is explicit that Australian Hungarian syntax has
not been systematically quantified: the handful of illustrative examples given (including overuse of
the indefinite article *egy* in contexts where standard Hungarian doesn't require it, directly
mirroring English's own indefinite-article requirements) represent, in the chapter's own words, "only
a very narrow selection" rather than a comprehensive catalog — an explicit gap flagged for future
research rather than papered over. pp. 369–370

### 11. Neologisms: a genuinely productive, second-generation-concentrated creative/erosive process

Following Gal's (1989) framework for Oberwart Hungarian (see `029_...md`), the chapter treats the
creation of Hungarian-internal neologisms (new words or unusual senses built entirely from native
Hungarian material, not directly modeled on any specific English word) as having both a negative
reading (evidence of lexical-gap-filling under incomplete acquisition/language attrition, following
Kontra's 1990 interpretation for American Hungarian) and a positive reading (genuine linguistic
creativity). The chapter's own data documents over 50 such verbal neologisms, mostly produced by
confusing verbs close in either meaning or phonological shape (e.g. confusing *gondolkozik* 'think
about' with *gondoz* 'take care of', or dropping/confusing verbal preverbs, as with *ér* 'reach; be
worth' substituting for *elér* 'achieve'). Because this neologism pattern is concentrated specifically
in second-generation rather than first-generation speech, the chapter treats it as a marker of lexical
loss under contact rather than simple creative wordplay — with nominal neologisms (not yet
comprehensively studied at the time of writing) reportedly even more numerous, contributing to a total
of nearly 1,000 lexical items across the whole Australian Hungarian corpus diverging from HH (a figure
the chapter explicitly notes excludes ordinary dialectal or slang vocabulary — i.e. this is a count of
contact-induced deviation specifically, not general lexical variation). pp. 360–362

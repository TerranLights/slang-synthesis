## Morpheme Breakdown — agglutinative forms worth decomposing

- **`kitisztíttatom`** ("I'll have it cleaned") = `ki-` (out, verbal prefix) + `tiszt-` (clean, from
  `tiszta`) + `-ít` (factitive, "make X") + `-tat` (causative, "have done") + `-om` (1st-person-
  singular objective-conjugation ending) — a 5-morpheme derivation, Lesson 54.
- **`megtekinthető`** ("viewable") = `meg-` (perfective prefix) + `tekint` (view) + `-het`
  (possibility) + `-ő` (present participle) = "-able." Lesson 55.
- **`kizsinóroztatom`/`kigomboztattatom`** ("I'll have it braided"/"I'll make somebody go and have
  it buttoned") — double-causative showcase forms from the Bartók-collected Transylvanian folk-song
  quoted in Lesson 56: `zsinóroz` ("to braid/lace," itself denominal from `zsinór` "cord") + `-tat`
  (causative) + `-om`; `gomboz` ("to button," from `gomb` "button") + `-tat` + `-tat` (double
  causative) + `-om` — the source explicitly calls this out as "double causation."
- **`énekelhessék`** ("so that they can sing it") = `éneke-` (sing) + `-het-` (possibility, "can")
  + `-esse-` (subjunctive/optative marker) + `-k` (3rd person plural) — a densely agglutinated
  possibility+subjunctive form, Lesson 52.
- **`köszönhetően`** ("thanks to, due to") = `köszön` (thank) + `-het-` (can) + `-ő` (present
  participle, "-ing") + `-en` (adverbial "-ly") — a clean 4-morpheme derivation explicitly
  decomposed by the source itself, Lesson 52; only used with positive connotations per the source.
- **`huszonnegyedik`-style stacking** continues to apply to this range's larger numerals (e.g.
  `négyzetméterenként` "per square meter" = `négyzet` "square" + `méter` "meter" + `-en` (locative)
  + `-ként` (distributive) — Lesson 55).
- **`istenverte`** ("damned," lit. "god-struck") = `isten` ("god") + `ver-` (strike, root of `ver`)
  + `-te` (3rd-person-singular objective-conjugation past-tense ending, used attributively) — an
  archaic curse-formation pattern (see Notable Findings), Lesson 63.
- **`madárlátta`** ("bird-seen," idiom for food carried home from fieldwork) = `madár` ("bird") +
  `lát-` (see) + `-ta` (3rd-person-singular objective past, attributive) — same archaic-curse/idiom
  formation pattern as `istenverte`, Lesson 63.
- **`négykezest`** ("a four-handed [piece]") = `négy` ("four") + `kéz` ("hand," stem `kez-`) + `-es`
  ("having/characterized by X") + `-t` (accusative) — Lesson 59.
- **`vasbordájú`** ("Ironside," lit. "iron-ribbed") = `vas` ("iron") + `borda` ("rib") + `-ja`
  (possessive 3sg) + `-ú` ("having X" compound-adjective suffix) — Lesson 61.

## Grammar points

### Causative verbs: -at/-et/-tat/-tet (Lessons 50, 53, 54, 56)

The single most systematically documented grammar point in this dispatch. -at/-et attaches to
monosyllabic verb stems; -tat/-tet to polysyllabic stems (or monosyllabic stems ending in vowel+t);
a connecting -v- appears when the stem ends in a vowel. English renders it with "make/have/get/let"
auxiliaries: *levágtam a hajamat* ("I cut my hair") vs. *levágattam a hajamat* ("I had my hair
cut"). A dedicated irregular-causative table is given (ül→ültet, lép→léptet, eszik→etet, iszik→itat,
tesz→tétet, visz→vitet, vesz→vetet, hisz→(el)hitet, alszik→altat, fekszik→fektet), plus two special
cases: jön's causative jöveszt has drifted to a mining-specific technical sense ("break coal in a
driftway"), while megy's causative meneszt has drifted to mean "sack/fire someone" — both genuine
semantic-drift examples worth flagging for the mechanics-analysis phase. The causative and potential
(-hat/-het) suffixes can co-occur (*etethet* "can feed," *csináltathat* "may have [something] made").

### Potential/ability suffix -hat/-het (Lesson 56)

Full subjective AND objective 6-form conjugations are given for *várhat* ("can wait") and *kérhet*
("can ask"), including the special 1st-person-subject/2nd-person-object *várhatlak* ("I can wait for
you"). An irregular-stem table is given separately (tesz→tehet, visz→vihet, van→lehet, iszik→ihat,
megy→mehet, fekszik→fekhet, vesz→vehet, hisz→hihet, eszik→ehet, jön→jöhet, alszik→alhat,
dohányzik→dohányozhat). This is the fullest single presentation of this suffix's conjugation this
project's Hungarian corpus has yet documented.

### Present participle -ó/-ő and its noun-forming productivity (Lesson 56)

Beyond its ordinary adjectival use (néző "onlooker," futó "runner"), the source discusses its heavy
use in forming compound nouns for objects/places/roles associated with an action — *fürdőszoba* is
literally "a room for bathing," not "a bathing room." Concrete pre-McDonald's-era shop-sign examples
are given: *borozó* (wine bar), *sörőző* (pub), *borharapó* ("wine-biting," i.e. a pub — a fresh
synonym), *falatozó* (snack bar). Many ordinary nouns were originally present participles: *jövő*
("future," from *jön* "come"), *vevő* ("buyer," from *vesz* "buy"), *eladó* ("sales assistant," from
*elad* "sell"), *tanító* ("teacher," from *tanít* "teach"). *Van* has two distinct present-participle
forms: *való* ("originating [from]") and *levő* ("being [somewhere]").

### The intransitive/"automatic" -ul/-ül suffix vs. transitive -ít (Lesson 57)

A systematic verb-pair-forming pattern distinguishing an action happening "somehow automatically,"
without a named agent (*indul* "start/leave," *mozdul* "move," *fordul* "turn," *készül* "is being
prepared," *javul* "is improving") from its transitive/agentive counterpart formed with -ít (*indít*
"start something," *mozdít* "move something," *fordít* "turn something," *készít* "prepares
something," *javít* "improves/fixes something"). This pairs naturally with the causative system
above (an -ít-suffixed transitive verb can itself take -tat/-tet for a further causative layer).

### Adverbial present participle -va/-ve (Lessons 62, 63)

Parallel to English "-ing" used adverbially (*sétálva nézelődik* "he's looking around while
walking," contrasted with the adjectival *sétáló hölgyek* "walking ladies"); with a transitive verb
it can form a passive-like construction (*Zöldre van a rácsos kapu festve* "The trellis gate is
painted green"). Several everyday fixed expressions are built on it: *nyitva/zárva/fizetve*
(open/closed/paid — door-sign vocabulary already present in other established/ files, now
grammatically explained), *Ki van zárva!* ("No way!"), *Meg vagyok fázva* ("I have a cold"),
*Készakarva tettem* ("I did it deliberately"). Some adverbial participles have grammaticalized into
postpositions: *mától kezdve/fogva* ("from today on"), *X perc múlva* ("in X minutes").

### Word order and sentence stress (Lesson 63)

The fullest single treatment of Hungarian's focus-driven word order in this project's corpus. Word
order is described as "almost liberal" but each ordering carries a distinct stress/focus meaning;
the stressed element — the part of the sentence the speaker wants to draw attention to — always
occurs *immediately before the finite predicate* (usually the verb), even when that predicate is an
infinitive-taking matrix verb like *akar* ("want"): a full worked paradigm contrasts *János 'olvas*
(neutral) with *'János olvas* (stress on János), *'Könyvet olvas János?* ("Is it a book that John is
reading?"), and their equivalents once *akar* + infinitive is added (*'Mit akar olvasni János?*, etc).
Sentence stress always falls on a word's first vowel and must not be confused with vowel length. In
an attributive noun phrase, the modifier is always stressed (*'Csacsi öreg medvém* "My silly old
bear").

### Kinship terminology: the older/younger-differentiated sibling system (Lessons 51, 53)

Hungarian famously lacks unmarked "brother/sister" terms, instead grammatically distinguishing
relative age: *báty* (older brother), *öcs* (younger brother), *nővér* (older sister), *húg*
(younger sister), *testvér* (gender-neutral "sibling," lit. "body-blood"), plus *sógor*/*sógornő*
(brother-/sister-in-law) and *unokatestvér*/*unokahúg* (cousin/niece). This is a genuine typological
feature not previously this completely tabulated in `established/`'s Hungarian files, worth flagging
for the mechanics-analysis phase.

### Nested/chained possessive constructions (Lesson 53)

When multiple possessors are chained (e.g. "the sister-in-law of Rosie's husband's niece"), the
suffix of an intermediate possessor (-nak/-nek a) can be either used or omitted, but its use is
compulsory for the *last* possessor in the chain. Each possession step still carries its own
possessive suffix (-a/-e/-ja/-je). Illustrated with the deliberately absurd textbook example "A
Kissné szeretője kutyájának a farka" ("Mrs Kiss's lover's dog's tail").

### The -da/-de suffix and its 19th-century language-reform origin (Lesson 60)

*Bölcsőde* (day nursery), *óvoda* (kindergarten), *uszoda* (swimming pool), *iroda* (office),
*nyomda* (printing house), *szálloda* (hotel), *cukrászda* (patisserie) all carry a suffix the source
explicitly traces to conscious 19th-century language planning: "invented by language reformers who
... created standard modern literary language and a lot of new words to express specific or new
concepts... they substitute international words which would sound strange in a non-Indo-European
language." A genuinely valuable piece of linguistic history not previously documented in this
project's Hungarian files (the *nyelvújítás* movement, though not named as such by the book).

### Diminutive suffixes -ka/-ke, -cska/-cske and pet-name formation (Lesson 61)

*Apuka*/*anyuka* are explicitly flagged as "double diminutives" (since *apu*/*anyu* are already
informal terms for *apa*/*anya*). Pet names are formed with -i (Bözsi, Manyi, Sári, Jani, Laci,
Dodi). The source itself jokes that "this diminutive disease spreads like leprosy," self-consciously
flagging the suffix's productivity — extending into colloquial food-slang (*csoki*, *fagyi*) and
greengrocer's-slang clippings (*pari*, *nari*, *ubi* for tomato/orange/cucumber).

### Archaic curse/idiom formation via objective-conjugation past participles (Lesson 63)

A distinctive idiom-formation pattern not previously documented: a noun compounded with a
3rd-person-singular objective-conjugation past-tense verb form, used attributively — *istenverte
idő* ("damned weather," lit. "god-struck weather"), *ebadta kölyke* ("son-of-a-bitch kid," lit.
"dog-gave whelp"), *madárlátta kenyér* ("bread brought home from fieldwork," lit. "bird-seen
bread"), *istenadta tehetség* ("innate/god-given gift"). The past participle can also be nominalized
more generally (*meghívottak* "the invited ones," *egybegyűltek* "those present").

## Notable findings (colloquial/register/historical/folkloric)

Per this project's specific interest in informal/colloquial register and historically-grounded
content, the following are flagged from this extraction as the highest-value items:

1. **The Communist/socialist-era institutional-vocabulary cluster** — this dispatch's single
   richest historical-political vein, spanning multiple lessons: **Közért** (genericized grocery-
   store name, explicitly dated to Rákosi's 1948-1956 dictatorship and the "Tessék kérni!" scarcity-
   era retail style, L45); **maszek** (private-sector self-employed tradesman, "previously... 90%
   of the economy was owned and run by the state," L55); **tanács** ("council," explicitly dated
   1949-1991 before being replaced by *polgármester* "mayor" in 1989, L51/L74); **téesz**
   (agricultural cooperative, L56); and **bekerül** flagged by the source *itself* as a bureaucratic-
   register verb "proliferating like cancer" in official language (L73). Taken together these form a
   genuinely substantial, source-annotated vocabulary of Hungarian state-socialist economic/
   administrative life — new content, not found in any prior established/ Hungarian file.
2. **"Nem az a baj, hogy nyakig vagyunk a szarban, hanem az, hogy hullámzik"** (L54) — the strongest
   single taboo-register finding, explicitly labeled by the source as "a crude but typically
   Hungarian saying."
3. **The diminutive-suffix cluster** (L61) — apuka/anyuka as "double diminutives," pet names, and
   greengrocer's slang (pari/nari/ubi/majonézes hambi), with the source's own self-aware "spreads
   like leprosy" framing — a rich, directly source-flagged colloquial/slang vein.
4. **"Szita szita péntek, szerelemcsütörtök, dobszerda"** (L49) — a genuine Hungarian folk black-
   magic weekday-reversal chant, explicitly described as such by the source; a striking piece of
   folk-superstition content with no parallel elsewhere in established/.
5. **Two complete, real Hungarian folk-songs** quoted (in English translation) across this
   dispatch: "Tavaszi szél vizet áraszt" (L52, unnamed by the book but a canonical, widely-known
   Hungarian folk-song) and "A csitári hegyek alatt" (L58, likewise unnamed but identifiable and
   well-known) — plus a Bartók-collected Transylvanian valedictory folk-song quoted for its double-
   causative showcase (L56). This book supplies far more folk-song/folk-rhyme material than any
   other established/ Hungarian source to date.
6. **Named literary figures new to established/:** József Attila (1905-1937), profiled via his
   lullaby poem "Altató" (L71); Nagy Lajos, whose 1927 short story "Cédula a kapun" supplies the
   "Ágy kiadó"/"Agy kiadó" ("bed to let"/"brain to let") typo-based pun (L72); Gyula Juhász's poem
   "Testimony," quoted in fragment (L70); Berzsenyi Dániel, an 19th-century poet, quoted for a
   -ra/-re usage example (L53).
7. **Two dated socio-economic-anxiety jokes**, both genuinely new register/humor content: the
   nouveau-riche "four-handed piano" social-envy joke (L59) and the "two skeletons meet on the
   Nagykörút" price-rise joke (L59) — the latter explicitly historicized to Communist-era inflation
   anxieties.
8. **"Pénz olvasva, asszony verve jó"** (L63) — flagged explicitly as a dated/archaic proverb
   reflecting historical patriarchal attitudes toward domestic violence, not the textbook's own
   endorsed view; included per the coverage rule's instruction to capture genuine source content
   even when uncomfortable, with an explicit interpretive flag attached.
9. **1954 World Cup Final loss** (L76) — a real historical/cultural touchpoint embedded in a
   football-explainer dialogue, including a period detail (public demonstrations over the loss) not
   otherwise attested in established/ Hungarian files; the book does not name the "Aranycsapat"
   (Golden Team) itself.
10. No handwritten marginalia, library stamps, or non-print annotation of any kind were found
    anywhere in this 73-spread page range — a clean, modern typeset scan throughout.

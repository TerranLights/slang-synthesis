# Hungarian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Language Contact Outside Hungary, Chapter 8 — Hungarian in Austria

**Source:** Anna Fenyvesi (ed.), *Hungarian Language Contact Outside Hungary: Studies on Hungarian
as a Minority Language* (IMPACT: Studies in Language and Society 20, John Benjamins, 2005), Chapter
8, "Hungarian in Austria" by Csanád Bodó. PDF pages 264–287 (full chapter). This is the second file
in a sequence of sibling chunks covering PDF pages 226–448 of this volume (assigned range); Chapter 7
(Vojvodina/Serbia and Prekmurje/Slovenia) is covered in

**Coverage note.** Per the sociolinguistic-commentary extraction convention (see the Serbian/
Croatian/Bosnian precedent, `Serbian_Croatian_Bosnian/established/031_...md`), this chunk prioritizes
the chapter's own contact-linguistic analysis, explicit dialectal annotation, and regional-variant
vocabulary over a flat vocabulary sample — this is a research chapter, not a teaching grammar. The
chapter distinguishes the small **autochthonous** Burgenland Hungarian community (BLH, centered on
Oberwart/Felsőőr, with roots to the 12th–13th century) from **immigrant** Hungarians in Austria
(three waves: WWII, 1956, post-1989), and its most linguistically detailed material comes from
Susan Gal's classic Oberwart language-shift fieldwork plus the chapter's own SHOH-project analysis.
Source has a clean text layer (`pdftotext -layout`); no vision-reading was needed and no marginalia
risk applies. Statistical tables (8.1–8.8) are paraphrased/summarized per copyright discipline rather
than fully transcribed, except where a single figure is the specific evidentiary point being made.

**Register/dialect annotation (explicit in source — must-capture per coverage rule).** The chapter
systematically distinguishes **SH** (Standard Hungarian), **HH** (Hungarian as spoken in Hungary,
used interchangeably with SH in places), **OWH** (the traditional Oberwart Hungarian dialect), and
BLH/BLG (Burgenland Hungarian-speaking vs. German-only populations, from the Holzer & Münz
microcensus). It further distinguishes, within OWH speakers, **"narrow-users"** (Gal's sociolinguistic
category: full linguistic competence but restricted domain-use of Hungarian) from **semi-speakers**
(Dorian's category: genuinely reduced linguistic production) — a real, source-native taxonomy of
speaker types under language shift that is itself worth preserving as a concept, not just a label.

**Morphological typology note.** Hungarian is agglutinative (see `../00_Extraction_Checklist.md`).
This chapter is unusually rich in morpheme-glossed minimal-pair sentence examples (SH vs. OWH/
narrow-user/semi-speaker forms) illustrating real-time morphological simplification under language
shift — these are preserved with full interlinear glosses in the Vocabulary table's Notes column and
in the dedicated Morpheme breakdown section, since the compositional contrast (not just the surface
form) is the chapter's actual evidentiary point.

### Morpheme breakdown

> **cipe-jë** (OWH) vs. **cipő-je** (SH) = cipő/cipüö ("shoe") + jë/je (3SG possessive suffix). The
> source's central example of noun-class leveling under language shift: SH and OWH both have several
> nonproductive noun classes with stem-internal vowel alternation (the stem shape differs between the
> bare and possessed forms), but membership in these classes differs between the two varieties.
> Younger BLH speakers regularize OWH's irregular *cipüö*/*cipe-jë* alternation to match the SH
> pattern, which shows no stem alternation at all (*cipő*/*cipő-je*) — a case where "simplification"
> and "convergence toward the prestige standard" happen to coincide, since Hungarian's overall
> inflectional-class system is comparatively simple (agglutinative languages generally have fewer
> productive noun classes than fusional ones). p. 274

> **dzserëk-ünk-iëk** (OWH) = dzserëk ("child") + ünk (1PL possessive) + iëk (possessive-construction
> plural morph) = "our children" — contrasted with **SH gyerek-e-i-nk** = gyerek + e (linking vowel/
> possessive stem) + i (possessive plural) + nk (1PL). Both varieties mark plural possession with a
> special morph (*-iëk*/*-i*) distinct from the default non-possessive plural *-k* (SH *gyerek-ek*
> 'children'). Semi-speakers from Oberwart often generalize the ordinary non-possessive plural into
> possessive contexts instead (**dzserëk-ëk-ünk**, child-PL-1PL.POSS, a form neither SH nor
> traditional OWH would produce), or — more frequently in the source's data — drop plural marking
> from the possessive construction entirely, collapsing the singular/plural possessive distinction.
> This is presented as a case of **language attrition** (an irreversible structural loss under
> language obsolescence), distinct from ordinary sociolectal simplification. p. 284

> **le-szok-tat-t-am** (SH) = le (preverb) + szok ("be in the habit of") + tat (causative suffix) +
> t (past tense) + am (1SG.definite) = "I got him out of [the habit]" — contrasted with the
> **narrow-user's** replacement analytic construction **le tud-t-am neki szok-ni** = le (preverb) +
> tud ("be able," past, 1SG.indefinite) + neki (dative "to him," expressing the causee overtly) +
> szok-ni (infinitive "be in the habit"). The synthetic causative morphology is replaced by a
> periphrastic construction with an overtly-expressed dative causee — the source notes this doesn't
> straightforwardly track German influence the way the *lassen*+infinitive causative substitution
> does in a separate example (**hagy-t-a** 'permit-PAST' + infinitive replacing the causative suffix
> in *rajzol-tat-t-a* 'have-drawn-CAUS-PAST', modeled on German *lassen*). A third, more severe pattern
> loses the causative meaning altogether with no periphrastic compensation: SH **éhez-tet-ni**
> (starve-CAUS-INF, "to starve [someone]," causative) reduced by a narrow-user to plain **éhez-ni**
> (starve-INF, "to [go] hungry," noncausative) — the source treats this specific pattern as language
> *decay* rather than ordinary contact-induced restructuring, since no compensating structure replaces
> the lost meaning. pp. 279–280

## Grammar points

*(Retitled "Sociolinguistic points" for this chunk, matching the convention used in
`028_hungarian_language_contact_part2.md`, since the chapter is contact-linguistics research rather
than a taught grammar rule set.)*

### 1. Two historically distinct Hungarian populations in Austria

The chapter's central organizing distinction: the **autochthonous** Burgenland Hungarian community,
descended from a 12th–13th-century settler population raised to collective petty-nobility status for
border defense (concentrated in Obere Wart/Őrvidék and the Oberpullendorf/Felsőpulya–
Mitterpullendorf/Középpulya area of central Burgenland), versus **immigrant** Hungarians arriving in
three post-1918 waves (WWII, the 1956 revolution, post-1989), plus a distinct wave of ethnic-Hungarian
Romanian immigrants in the 1980s. The autochthonous group is small (2001: 4,704 in Burgenland out of
40,583 Hungarian-speakers nationally, most actually in Vienna) but sociolinguistically far
better-studied, chiefly through Susan Gal's Oberwart/Felsőőr fieldwork (1979, 1984, 1989) and Samu
Imre's traditional-dialect descriptions (1971, 1973). Historically, Ottoman-era attacks depopulated
much of surrounding Western Hungary while the noble-privileged Hungarian settlements held on,
producing isolated **Hungarian speech islands** later surrounded by 16th-century German and Croatian
settlement — a pattern of bilingual contact already roughly 400 years old by the time of this study.
pp. 264–266

### 2. Census vs. microcensus discrepancy: what "speaking Hungarian" even means

A methodological point made explicit by the source: the 1991 general Austrian census asked only about
*Umgangssprache* (colloquial/everyday language), while a parallel 1990–91 Burgenland microcensus
(Holzer & Münz 1993) separately recorded active vs. passive Hungarian skills, finding a much higher
figure (6.1% of Burgenland's population, 16,737 people) than the general census implied — a large
share of it explained by an older generation (over 61) that was formally schooled in Hungarian but no
longer uses it regularly (23% of everyone with any Hungarian knowledge). This is presented as a
caution against treating "self-reported minority-language speaker" as a stable, comparable category
across data sources. pp. 266–267

### 3. Demographic and socioeconomic profile: an aging, service-sector-shifted minority

The Burgenland Hungarian-speaking (BLH) population is markedly older than the German-only (BLG)
population (44.7% over 61, vs. 17.9% for BLG) and underrepresented in every younger age cohort —
attributed to weak intergenerational transmission, not just formal-schooling effects. Occupationally,
BLH speakers have shifted heavily away from agriculture (despite most autochthonous Hungarians having
farmed until the 1960s–70s) into the service sector (56.6% vs. BLG's 45.9%), with a slightly higher
rate of high-school and university completion than the BLG population in the 15–40 age cohort —
evidence against any simple "assimilated minority = lower socioeconomic status" narrative. pp.
267–269

### 4. Domains of use: workplace/community Hungarian rising in the younger generation

Unlike some other minority-Hungarian regions, Burgenland shows the *younger* generation (born
1955–1980) using Hungarian in public/workplace domains **more** than the middle generation (born
1930–1954) — attributed to growing Hungarian-Austrian border-region economic ties after 1989 raising
Hungarian's practical/prestige value. Still, intergenerational family transmission is weak: Hungarian
is not the language of parent-child communication in 42–55% of BLH families depending on generation,
and mixed marriages (42% of the BLH group) show even starker attrition — a third of Hungarian-German
mixed-marriage parents do not use Hungarian with their children at all, and even in 21% of
Hungarian-Hungarian-speaking-parent households, Hungarian still isn't used with the children.
Bilingual education is now offered in only 3 elementary schools (vs. 10 in 1938), with a genuinely new
1992 Hungarian-medium high school in Oberwart — but that same school enrolls *more* students in its
parallel Croatian-German track than its Hungarian-German one, which the chapter reads as direct
evidence that Burgenland Hungarians, unlike Burgenland Croatians, assign comparatively little
importance to schools as a language-maintenance strategy. pp. 269–271

### 5. Gal's language-shift model: age, peasant status, and social network

Susan Gal's Oberwart findings (via this chapter) identify the initial shift phase as the late
1950s–early 1960s, coinciding with postwar West European economic growth finally reaching Burgenland
and drawing in monolingual German in-migrants. Three interacting predictors of Hungarian vs. German
use: **age**, **peasant status**, and (crucially, as the variable that can override the first two)
**social network** — a speaker's dense ties within the traditional "peasant" network predict
continued Hungarian use even across a generational or occupational-status discontinuity, while an
"industrial" network predicts a shift toward German regardless of age or family background. Gal's own
quoted characterization (p. 317, quoted directly, one sentence) frames Hungarian as a "language of
solidarity" tied to the past/peasantry/economic "uselessness," German as a "language of power" tied
to the state, education, and upward mobility — the classic diglossic prestige-asymmetry pattern. pp.
271–272

### 6. Three named types of dialectal divergence

The chapter's own analytic framework distinguishes three mechanisms by which Burgenland Hungarian
dialects diverge from their traditional forms, explicitly not claimed to be mutually independent:
(1) **convergence toward Standard Hungarian** (most visible in older speakers, mainly phonological —
e.g. loss of the OWH/SH sociolinguistic style distinction that once let speakers code-shift within
Hungarian itself); (2) **divergence through language-contact effects** (present in all generations);
and (3) **divergence through language loss/attrition** (concentrated in the youngest generation). A
genuinely novel observation: noun-class leveling in OWH (the *cipüö*/*cipő* case) happens to be
*simultaneously* an instance of language-internal simplification (agglutinative Hungarian's few
productive classes absorbing irregular ones) and convergence toward the more prestigious standard —
the two processes are not always separable even in principle. pp. 272–274

### 7. Three types of lexical borrowing from German (Jones 1998 framework, applied to OWH)

The chapter adopts a standard three-way typology: **cultural borrowings** (new-concept vocabulary
with no prior gap-filling need, e.g. *himpiërszoft*, *rëntni* — notably, the source stresses these
occur even where Standard Hungarian has no lexical gap, attributing the borrowing to the community's
relative isolation from SH resources rather than to genuine referential necessity); **calques**
(semantic borrowing via native-form vocabulary, e.g. *keriëk*'s extended 'wheel/bicycle' sense
modeled on German *Rad* — the source notes Imre's OWH dictionary records surprisingly few of these);
and **word substitutions** (unassimilated borrowings displacing an existing native equivalent despite
no gap, e.g. *hejcung*, *tepih*, *fërlóbung* from immigrant Upper Austrian speech, which show less
phonotactic nativization than the older, more established Oberwart cultural borrowings). pp. 274–276

### 8. Structural borrowing: German case/agreement patterns leaking into younger OWH speech

A rare but real category (the source stresses that overt grammatical borrowing is cross-linguistically
uncommon): younger Oberwart speakers produce *találkozom* ('I meet') with an accusative-marked object
(matching German *treffen*'s accusative government) instead of the Hungarian-correct instrumental
case, and mark plural agreement on a noun after a numeral quantifier (*sok barátokat* 'many friends',
matching German *viele Freunde*) even though Hungarian nouns are normally invariant after
number-words. Both patterns are argued to derive directly from German syntax, not from
language-internal simplification. p. 276

### 9. Code-switching typology, illustrated with real Oberwart transcript excerpts

Three functionally distinct code-switching patterns from Gal's fieldwork are distinguished: (a)
**asymmetrical intergenerational switching** — a younger speaker consistently answers an
older-generation Hungarian-speaking interlocutor in German (illustrated with a genuine
German/Hungarian bilingual conversation transcript, quoted with German marked in boldface in the
original); (b) **conversational (situational) switching within one speaker's turn** — e.g. a
grandfather shifting into German mid-utterance specifically to convey heightened anger/authority when
disciplining a Hungarian-monolingual toddler, termed **metaphorical code-switching**; and (c) a
broader claim from Gal that mid-generation speakers' code-switching functions analogously to
style/register-shifting in a monolingual community — as the community's stylistic repertoire
collapses (monostylism), switching languages substitutes for switching styles, and Gal treats
persistent inter-sentential switching by the middle generation specifically as marking a transitional
stage between categorical-Hungarian and categorical-German community norms. The chapter also flags a
competing model (McConvell 1991) in which younger speakers' apparent divergence from parental
patterns can reverse later in life — a caution against reading any single generation's snapshot as
proof of terminal, unidirectional shift. pp. 276–278

### 10. Analytic vs. synthetic constructions: Austria's SHOH results, compared against the
Vojvodina/Prekmurje findings in the sibling chunk

Austria's SHOH results present a genuinely mixed picture, unlike the uniformly-elevated
analytic-preference pattern the sibling chunk documents for the Slavic-contact regions: for
potentiality (SH -hat/-het suffix vs. periphrastic tud + infinitive), Austrian bilinguals showed **no**
significant difference from monolingual Hungary — flagged explicitly as a genuine negative/
null result, attributed to potentiality-suffix analytic-substitution being generally disfavored across
all seven SHOH-project countries, not just Austria. But for the denominal reflexive suffix
(-kod/-ked/-köd(ik), as in *türelmetlenkedik* 'be impatient') and the noun-forming pair
(*buszozás* vs. *utazás busszal*, 'traveling by bus'), Austria *did* show significantly more analytic
preference than Hungary — both cases where a directly parallel German construction exists (*sein
ungeduldig*; *Bus fahren*), suggesting the contact effect in Austria tracks specific
structural-parallel availability rather than a blanket "bilinguals prefer analytic forms" tendency.
The chapter is explicit that comparing its SHOH-questionnaire data against the Oberwart
spontaneous-speech "narrow-user" examples requires two caveats: different elicitation methods
(questionnaire vs. natural speech) and different populations (the SHOH Austrian sample is
mostly immigrants who became bilingual as adults, vs. Oberwart's lifelong-bilingual autochthonous
narrow-users) — contact effects should not be assumed comparable in depth or kind across the two
groups. pp. 278–283

### 11. Language attrition proper — distinguished from ordinary contact-induced change

The chapter draws a hard conceptual line (citing Dressler 1996) between **language attrition** (a
"pathological," uncompensated structural loss specific to the recessive language in language-death
trajectories) and ordinary rapid contact-induced change — the two are not just points on a severity
continuum. Its diagnostic case: possessive-suffix marking in semi-speakers' OWH. A semi-speaker's
sentence drops the expected possessive suffix on *kor* ('age') entirely in one clause and
overgeneralizes the 3rd-person possessive suffix *-a* where 1st-person *-om* is required in another —
within the same utterance. The plural-possessive morph pair (*-iëk* in OWH vs. *-i* in SH, both
distinct from the ordinary non-possessive plural *-k*) similarly collapses in semi-speaker production,
either wrongly borrowing the ordinary plural marker into possessive contexts or, more often, dropping
plural marking from possessives altogether — losing the singular/plural possessive distinction with
no compensating structure. The chapter's explicit point: this kind of loss, unlike the
synthetic-to-analytic substitutions discussed earlier, is not simply "borrowing a foreign pattern" —
it is presented as irreversible structural erosion characteristic specifically of speakers furthest
along the shift-to-language-death continuum. pp. 283–284

### 12. Chapter's own conclusion: contact depth tracks duration/type of contact, not just bilingualism

The chapter closes by explicitly linking its two case populations' differing degrees of structural
change to their differing sociolinguistic histories: the autochthonous BLH community has centuries of
continuous, geographically stable bilingual contact (hence its deeper structural changes — inflectional
leveling, causative-marking loss, singular/plural possessive collapse), while immigrant communities
are demographically discontinuous, sustained by successive new immigration waves rather than
intergenerational transmission, and their SHOH-measured linguistic divergence, while real, does not
reach the same structural depth. Austria and Slovenia are jointly identified as Hungary's two smallest
neighboring-country Hungarian-speaking minorities. pp. 285–286

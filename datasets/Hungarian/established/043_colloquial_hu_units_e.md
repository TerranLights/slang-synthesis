### Morpheme Breakdown — agglutinative forms worth decomposing

- **`megkérhetnélek`** ("could I ask you") = `meg-` (perfectivizing coverb) + `kér` ("ask" root) +
  `-het` (potential) + `-né` (conditional, back-vowel) + `-lek` (1st-person-subject/2nd-person-object
  "I...you" ending) — a four-morpheme stack combining causative-adjacent potential, conditional, and
  the object-conjugation ending in a single word, the clearest illustration in this range of how
  Hungarian's verb-modification endings compose. Unit 15, Grammar points §1.
- **`hívathatnál`** ("you could have [someone] call") = `hív` ("call" root) + `-at` (causative, short
  form) + `-hat` (potential) + `-nál` (conditional, te-form) — explicitly worked through step-by-step
  by the source itself as "call + causative + potential + conditional," p. 237.
- **`kitisztíttattam`** ("I had it cleaned") = `ki-` (coverb, "out/thoroughly") + `tisztít` ("clean")
  + `-tat` (causative, long form — required because `tisztít` is polysyllabic and ends in a long
  vowel + `t`) + `-tam` (past, 1sg indefinite). p. 235.
- **`elénekeltette`** ("he had [someone] sing it") = `el-` (coverb, perfectivizing) + `énekel`
  ("sing") + `-tet` (causative, long form) + `-te` (past, 3sg definite). p. 235.
- **`legdrágább`** ("most expensive") = `leg-` (superlative prefix) + `drága` ("expensive") + `-bb`
  (comparative, attaching directly after a vowel-final adjective) — superlative is formed by adding
  `leg-` to an already-formed comparative, not as an independent morphological slot. Unit 15, Grammar
  points §3.
- **`legváratlanabb`** ("most unexpected") = `leg-` + `váratlan` ("unexpected," itself `vár`- "wait"
  + `-atlan` negative-participle suffix, "un-waited-for") + linking vowel `-a-` + `-bb` — a
  superlative built on an already-morphologically-complex adjective, stacking four layers of
  derivation. p. 242.
- **`hoztathatnék`**-type potential+causative combinations are named structurally by the source (see
  Grammar points §1/§2 below) using the general template **root + causative(`-tat/-tet/-at/-et`) +
  potential(`-hat/-het`) + conjugation ending** — applicable productively to any verb, not just the
  worked examples above.

## Grammar points

### Unit 13 (Language points, p. 212) — the `-lak`/`-lek` "I love you" object conjugation

A dedicated verb ending marks the specific subject/object combination *én* (I, subject) + *téged*/
*titeket*/*benneteket* (you sg./pl., object) — present tense `-lak`/`-lek`, past/subjunctive
`-alak`/`-elek`. The subject and object pronouns need never be stated explicitly; the verb ending
alone carries the full information (`Megvárlak` = "I'll wait for you"). This is presented as
grammatically distinct from the ordinary definite/indefinite conjugation contrast covered elsewhere
in the book. p. 212.

### Unit 14, Language points §1 — Conditional conjugation

The conditional ending is `-na`/`-ne` (added to the verb stem the same way as past/imperative
markers), with a linking `a`/`e` vowel required when the stem ends in two consonants or a long vowel
+ `t`. Full indefinite/definite paradigm tables given for `mutat` ("show"), `szeret` ("like/love"),
and `tart` ("hold"). Used for hypotheticals and polite requests, and obligatorily after `ha` ("if"),
`bárcsak` ("if only"), and `mintha` ("as if") — contexts where English does not always require the
conditional. Most irregular verbs (`van`, `megy`, `jön`, `alszik`, `eszik`, `hisz`, `lesz`, `tesz`,
`vesz`, `visz`) form the conditional regularly from a special conditional-only stem (e.g. `van` →
`vol-`/`len-`, `eszik` → `en-`, `iszik` → `in-`), listed in full. pp. 217-219.

### Unit 14, Language points §2 — Past conditional ("I would have...")

Formed trivially: put the verb in the past tense and add the separate word `volna` after it
(`Elmentem volna, ha meghívtak volna` — "I would have gone if they had invited me"). If a coverb must
be separated from the verb (e.g. by a question word), the coverb moves to *after* `volna`, not
between the verb and `volna` (`Ki hitte volna el ezt a történetet?` — "Who would have believed this
story?"). pp. 221-222.

### Unit 14, Language points §3 — Possessive pronouns and "Whose?"

Full possessive-pronoun paradigm (mine/yours/his-hers/ours/yours-pl./theirs), each with a distinct
singular-possessed vs. plural-possessed form (`az enyém` vs. `az enyéim`) — a distinction English
does not make. Any case ending may attach to these pronouns. "Whose?" (`Kié?`) and possessor-marking
by name both use the same suffix, `-é` (`Ez a könyv Ferié` — "This book is Feri's"), which likewise
may take further case endings. pp. 223-224.

### Unit 14, Language points §4 — "Keeping up with the Feketes": the `-ék` suffix

Adding `-ék` to a family name or a person's name refers to that whole family/group or that person and
their usual companion(s) collectively (`Zoltánék` — "Zoltán and his family/friends"). A distinctively
Hungarian construction with no direct one-word English equivalent. p. 224.

### Unit 14, Language points §5 — Nominal possession ("the child's book")

Where the possessor is a noun rather than a pronoun, Hungarian marks *only the possessed item*, using
the same 3rd-person possessive suffixes covered for pronouns, with the possessor placed *before* the
possessed item and left morphologically unmarked (`a gyerek könyve` — "the child's book"; contrast
English, which marks the possessor with `'s` or an "of" phrase). A full singular/plural-possessor ×
singular/plural-possessed 2×2 paradigm is given. The source illustrates with several literary
examples quoted (briefly, per copyright discipline) from István Örkény's short story "Szakmai
Önérzet" ("Professional Pride") — e.g. `a gerle búgása` "the coo of the turtle-dove," `a pacsirta
éneke` "the song of the lark" — chosen because Örkény's ironic, list-heavy prose style happens to
showcase many nominal-possessive constructions in a small space. pp. 226-228.

### Unit 14, Cultural notes — musical entertainment and the "Ha én rózsa volnék" song

Names Hungary's `tánchác` ("dance house") folk-revival movement (with roots in Transylvanian village
tradition), the Operaház and Zeneakadémia (named for Ferenc Liszt), composers Zoltán Kodály and Béla
Bartók, and popular singer Zsuzsa Koncz. Her signature song "Ha én rózsa volnék" ("If I were a
rose..."), written by János Bródy, is noted as using the conditional throughout as a *politically
coded* device (socially/politically symbolic content veiled behind a simple flower/flag conceit) —
directly relevant to this chapter's conditional-mood grammar point. A short two-verse excerpt is
quoted for illustration, per copyright discipline. p. 229.

### Unit 15, Language points §1 — Potential `-hat`/`-het` and combining it with causative/conditional

The potential ending `-hat`/`-het` adds "may/might/could" to any verb, softening requests or marking
possibility/uncertainty (and, in the negative, prohibition). Combined with the conditional it yields
polite "could" requests (`megkérhetnélek` — "could I ask you"). The source explicitly walks through
stacking causative + potential + conjugation-ending as a single productive template (`hív + at + hat
+ nál` = "you could have [someone] call"), see Morpheme Breakdown. Irregular-verb potential/causative
stem tables given in full for `van/lesz`, `eszik`, `hisz`, `iszik`, `tesz`, `vesz`, `visz`, `jön`,
`megy`, `alszik` (several irregulars, e.g. `jön`/`megy`, have no causative form at all — marked `—`
in the source's own table). pp. 232-237.

### Unit 15, Language points §2 — Causative `-(t)at`/`-(t)et`

Marks that someone other than the grammatical subject performs the action; the actual performer (if
named) takes the `-val`/`-vel` case (`Gáborral hívat egy taxit` — "She has Gábor call her a taxi").
Long form `-tat`/`-tet` for polysyllabic verbs and verbs ending in a long vowel + `t`; short form
`-at`/`-et` for monosyllabic verbs and verbs ending in a consonant + `t` (coverbs are not counted
toward syllable count for this rule). pp. 235-237.

### Unit 15, Language points §3 — Comparative `-(a)bb`/`-(e)bb` and superlative `leg-`

Comparative ending `-(a)bb`/`-(e)bb` (with linking vowel after a consonant-final adjective); several
irregular comparatives given (`jó`→`jobb`, `szép`→`szebb`, `nagy`→`nagyobb`, `lassú`→`lassabb`,
`hosszú`→`hosszabb`, `könnyű`→`könnyebb`, `sok`→`több`). `mint` ("than") completes an explicit
comparison; alternatively, when the compared item is a bare noun (not a full clause), it may instead
take the `-nál`/`-nél` case with `mint` dropped entirely (`Judit vidámabb Kingánál` = `Judit vidámabb,
mint Kinga`). The *degree* of a comparison (e.g. "three centimetres taller") is marked with the
`-val`/`-vel` case on the degree-noun (`Ferenc három centiméterrel magasabb, mint Gyula`). The
superlative is formed by prefixing `leg-` to the comparative (not to the bare adjective): `legdrágább`
"most expensive." Any ending attachable to a plain adjective may also attach to its comparative form,
including the adverbial `-an`/`-en` (`Beszélj lassabban!` — "Speak more slowly"). pp. 238-243.

### Appendix I — full irregular-verb conjugation tables

A structural reference appendix (not narrative prose) giving complete present/past/subjunctive/
conditional paradigms (both indefinite and definite where applicable), plus each verb's infinitive,
potential stem, and causative stem, for: **lesz** ("becomes/will be" — indefinite-only), **vesz**
("takes/buys," =**tesz** conjugated identically per the source's own footnote), **visz** ("takes/
carries"), **hisz** ("believes"), **eszik** ("eats"), **iszik** ("drinks"), **megy** ("goes" —
indefinite-only), **jön** ("comes" — indefinite-only, and explicitly notes that the imperative forms
`gyere`/`gyertek`/`gyerünk` are more common in practice than the "regular" `jöjjél`/`jöjjetek`/
`jöjjünk`, which are reserved for subjunctive use), and **van** ("is," with two competing conditional
stem-families `vol-`/`len-` both given as `volnék/lennék`-style parallel forms throughout). This
appendix is the single most systematic irregular-verb morphology reference encountered so far in this
project's Hungarian sources — worth flagging for the mechanics-analysis phase as a canonical
paradigm-completeness check against other Hungarian grammars' irregular-verb treatments. pp. 266-270.

flagged from this extraction:

   appearing at least three times across this range (`Csak nem titkolsz valamit?`, `Csak nem
   Alízról beszél?`, `Zoltán, csak nem fázol?`) — genuinely conversational register, always
   introducing a suspicion the speaker hopes is unfounded.
3. **A cluster of source-annotated animal/folk-simile idioms**, all built on the `mint` ("like/as")
   comparative construction, gathered specifically in Unit 15's comparison chapter (p. 240-241) as a
   themed set:
   - `Úgy alszik, mint a mormota` — "He sleeps like a log" (lit. "like the marmot"), first introduced

     points section as the model example for idiomatic comparison.
   - `Úgy örültem, mint majom a farkának` — "I was happy as a clam" (lit. "like a monkey [is happy]
     with its tail").
   - `Olyan ravasz, mint a róka` — "He is as sly as a fox."
   - `Úgy kerüli ezt a témát, mint macska a forró kását` — "He drops the subject like a hot potato"
     (lit. "like the cat avoids hot porridge") — illustrated with an in-book line drawing of a
     wary cat and a steaming bowl.
   - `Szemtelen, mint a piaci légy` — "He is as annoying as a barnyard fly" (lit. "as impertinent as
     a market fly").
   - `Szegény, mint a templom egere` — "He is as poor as a church mouse," illustrated with a drawing
     of a mouse.
   This cluster is the richest single set of idiomatic simile material captured from any Hungarian
   source in this project to date — worth flagging for the mechanics-analysis phase as a productive
   "X, mint Y" idiom-formation template distinct from ordinary lexical slang.
4. **`Ne igyál előre a medve bőrére!`** ("Don't count your chickens before they're hatched," lit.
   "Don't drink to the skin of the bear in advance") — a fuller proverb explicitly source-glossed with
   its literal meaning, p. 216.
5. **A "Common sayings and proverbs" cultural-note box** (Unit 15, pp. 245-246) gathering five more
   proverbs themed around comparison/superlative, each given with both a literal and an idiomatic
   English gloss: `Mindenütt jó, de legjobb otthon!` ("There's no place like home"), `Édesebb a tiltott

6. **`megúszik`** ("gets out of a tough spot easily") — the source explicitly flags this as a

7. **`szimpatikus`** — an international/Latinate loanword adjective ("nice, likeable") used in
   ordinary descriptive registers (`A gimnázium igazgatója szimpatikus ember volt`), illustrating

8. **`szalonnasütés`** ("bacon-barbecue") — an untranslatable, culturally load-bearing noun given a

   from food but a beloved, near-ritual social occasion in Hungary.
9. No `[B]`/`[C]`/`[S]`-style dialectal tags or explicit comparative regional-variant annotations

### Morpheme breakdown

> **ismerősömhöz** = ismerős ("acquaintance") + öm (1sg possessive suffix, "my") + höz (allative
> case, "to") — "to my acquaintance's [house]." Source's own worked example; illustrates suffix
> stacking (noun stem + possessive + case) and front-vowel allative variant `-höz`. p. 78

> **barátnőidének** = barátnő ("girlfriend") + i (plural-possession marker, i.e. more than one
> girlfriend) + d (2sg possessor, "your") + é (non-attributive possessive, "that/those belonging
> to") + nek (dative case) — "for that belonging to your girlfriends." Source's own worked example;
> shows four suffixes stacked on one stem, each independently glossable. p. 78

> **lovak** = ló ("horse," nominative singular) + ak (plural, linking vowel a + `-k`) — the stem
> alternates to **lov-** only when a suffix follows; contrast **lóról** = ló + ról (delative "off
> the horse"), where no suffix-triggered stem change occurs. Demonstrates why the source treats
> stem alternation as suffix-dependent rather than a property of the bare noun. pp. 78–79

> **havak** = hó ("snow," stem **hav-**) + ak (plural, linking vowel a + `-k`) — v-stem noun where
> the stem vowel changes quality as well as gaining the -v-, not just losing length. p. 81

> **uralmak** = uralom ("reign") minus its final vowel (elided) + ak (plural, linking vowel a +
> `-k`) — `-alom/-elem` nouns behave as a combination of the fleeting-vowel pattern (last vowel
> drops) and the low-vowel pattern (a/e linking vowel required). p. 82

> **bokrok** = bokor ("bush") minus its final vowel (elided) + ok (plural, linking vowel o + `-k`)
> — fleeting-vowel noun; the vowel that disappears when a suffix is added cannot be predicted from
> spelling alone and must be memorized per the source. p. 82

> **kezek** = kéz ("hand," long vowel) → kez- (short vowel, length lost) + ek (plural, linking
> vowel e + `-k`) — low-vowel noun of the "loses length" subtype, contrasted in the source with
> **házak** (ház → no length change) as the "no loss of length" subtype. p. 81

> **leveleket** = levél ("letter," stem level-) + ek (plural) + et (accusative, with the linking
> vowel a/e that the source says is *always* required after plural `-k` regardless of stem type,
> even for stems that took no linking vowel in the plural itself). p. 85

> **legnagyobb** = leg- (superlative prefix) + nagy ("big") + obb (comparative suffix) — "biggest."
> Not itself explained in this page range (superlatives are treated elsewhere in the grammar) but
> appears in an example sentence; included because it shows prefix + root + suffix stacking outside
> the noun-plural system covered in §5.3. p. 85

> **emlősállat** = emlős ("mammalian," adjectival) + állat ("animal") — compound noun, "mammal."
> Included as a contrast case: compounding (two free stems juxtaposed) versus the suffixal
> agglutination that is this chapter's main subject. p. 85

## Grammar points

### 5.1.1 Definite article (a / az)

The definite article is `a` before a consonant-initial word and `az` before a vowel-initial word;
unlike German- or French-style articles it never inflects for case or number. Beyond its
English-like use, Rounds lists several additional environments where Hungarian requires the
definite article but English wouldn't necessarily use "the": before a possessed noun (`a
gyerekem` "my child" — except in "have" constructions), before a possessive pronoun (`az enyém`
"mine"), before a noun modified by a demonstrative (`ez a gyerek` "this child"), before many
abstract nouns (`Ilyen az élet` "Such is life"), and to give a noun generic reference (`Szeretem a
banánt` "I like bananas," i.e. bananas in general). p. 76

**Register annotation (explicit in source):** the definite article before a person's own name
(`Kedves a Péter` "Peter is nice") is explicitly marked **colloquial** — this is the one
register/usage annotation the source makes in this page range and should be treated as a genuine
colloquial-register data point, not general usage. p. 76

### 5.1.2 Indefinite article (egy)

`egy` ("a, an") functions much as its English counterpart: `Tegnap este elolvastam egy könyvet` "I
read a book last night." p. 77

### 5.1.3 Zero article

Three environments take no article at all: (1) existential "there is/are" and "have" constructions
(`Szép fa van a kertben` "There is a beautiful tree in the garden"; `Neki gyönyörű háza van` "She
has a beautiful house"); (2) predicate nouns (`Csilla énekesnő, Tamás rendőr` "Csilla is a singer,
Tamás is a policeman"); (3) indefinite subjects, objects, or adverbials occurring immediately
before the verb — an article becomes usual again if the same phrase follows the verb instead
(`Minden este János levest főz` "János makes soup every night," vs. the more optional article in
`A Kovácsék (egy) szép lakásban laknak` "The Kovácses live in a nice apartment"). p. 77

*Aside, not this section's main topic but visible in the example data:* `Kovácsék` ("the Kovács
family / the Kovácses") shows the family/associative suffix `-ék` attached to a surname — a
distinct suffix from the plural `-k` covered later in this chapter, not itself explained in this
page range. p. 77

### 5.2.1 Suffixes (agglutination)

Hungarian is agglutinative: suffixes (and some prefixes) attach to a word to change its meaning or
grammatical function, and multiple suffixes can stack on a single stem, each contributing one
identifiable piece of meaning — e.g. `zseb` "pocket" + plural `-k` + locative `-ben` "in" →
`zsebekben` "in pockets." The source's own more complex examples (`ismerősömhöz`,
`barátnőidének`) are reproduced in the Morpheme Breakdown above. p. 78

### 5.2.2 Vowel harmony

Suffixes generally come in two or more vowel variants chosen to match the vowel quality of the
stem they attach to (either a vowel built into the suffix itself, or a separate linking vowel
between stem and suffix). Rounds gives the illative case (`-ban/-ben`) and the allative case
(`-hoz/-hez/-höz`) as examples of suffixes with multiple harmonic variants. Full vowel harmony
rules are covered in the grammar's Chapter 3 (outside this excerpt). p. 78

### 5.2.3 Gender

Hungarian nouns and pronouns carry no grammatical gender. p. 78

### 5.2.4 Stem alternation

Suffixes (plural, case, possessive) attach directly to the end of a word but can trigger a change
in the noun's stem — e.g. `ló` "horse" surfaces as the plural `lovak`, revealing the stem `lov-`
that the bare nominative singular `ló` doesn't show. Because these alternations aren't always
predictable from the singular form alone, the rest of the chapter classifies nouns by which kind
of stem alternation (if any) they undergo. p. 78

### 5.3 Noun stems and the nominative case — singular and plural

The singular nominative is the dictionary citation form, but a noun's *stem* (and hence most of
its irregularity) is really only revealed once a suffix — canonically the plural — is attached;
e.g. the delative case `-ról` "off" attaches to `ló` with no visible change (`lóról` "off the
horse"), while the plural reveals the v-stem (`lovak` "horses"). The plural marker is `-k`. It is
**not** used after numerals or other quantity expressions (which always take a singular noun
instead — see §5.4.1.1 below), and there is a separate plural paradigm for the possessive
declension where `-k` is likewise not used. Whether a linking vowel is needed before `-k`, and
which one, depends on the noun's stem class (below). p. 79

### 5.3.1 Nouns ending in a vowel

No linking vowel is needed. If the final vowel is `a` or `e`, it lengthens to `á`/`é` before the
plural `-k` (`lámpa` → `lámpák`, `csésze` → `csészék`); any other final vowel takes `-k` unchanged
(`nő` → `nők`, `kocsi` → `kocsik`, `hajó` → `hajók`, `kapu` → `kapuk`, `tű` → `tűk`). pp. 79–80

### 5.3.2 Nouns ending in a consonant

Consonant-final nouns need a linking vowel before the plural (and several other suffixes). Most
require `o/e/ö`; a smaller, closed set requires `a/e`. The source further subdivides each linking-
vowel group by whether the stem itself changes when suffixed. p. 80

**5.3.2.1.1 Low vowel nouns** (linking vowel `a/e`): a finite set of roughly 100 nouns (full list
in the source's Appendix 3, not reproduced here) that must simply be memorized — there's no
surface cue predicting membership. Within this set, some nouns lose the length of their final
vowel when suffixed (`kéz` → `kezek`, `levél` → `levelek`, `pohár` → `poharak`, `út` → `utak`,
`víz` → `vizek`) and others don't (`ház` → `házak`, `könyv` → `könyvek`, `őz` → `őzek`, `toll` →
`tollak`, `ügy` → `ügyek`). Exception noted: `férfi` "man" behaves like a low-vowel noun despite
ending in a vowel, with plural `férfiak`. pp. 80–81

**5.3.2.1.2 v-stems**: nominative singular ends in a long vowel, but the stem taking suffixes ends
in `-v-` preceded by a short vowel that may also change quality (`ló` "horse" → stem `lov-`; `tó`
"lake" → stem `tav-`). Full v-stem list: `cső`→`csövek`, `mű`→`művek` (exception: vowel before `v`
stays long here), `fű`→`füvek`, `nyű`→`nyüvek`, `hó`→`havak`, `szó`→`szavak`, `kő`→`kövek`,
`tó`→`tavak`, `ló`→`lovak`, `tő`→`tövek`. p. 81

**5.3.2.1.3 -alom/-elem nouns**: a large, productive-looking derivational class ending in `-alom`
or `-elem`, which declines as a *combination* of the fleeting-vowel pattern (final vowel elides)
and the low-vowel pattern (`a/e` linking vowel): `uralom`→`uralmak`, `hatalom`→`hatalmak`,
`jutalom`→`jutalmak`, `rejtelem`→`rejtelmek`, `érzelem`→`érzelmek`, `kegyelem`→`kegyelmek`.
Exception noted: `cimbalom` "Hungarian cymbalo" is not one of these — it behaves as an ordinary
fleeting-vowel word instead. pp. 81–82

**5.3.2.2.1 Fleeting vowel nouns** (linking vowel `o/e/ö`): the noun's own last vowel is dropped
when the plural `-k` is added. A closed set that must be memorized, though there's a rough surface
pattern — these nouns tend to have `o`, `e`, or `ö` in the final syllable and end in one of `g, k,
l, m, n, ny, r, s, sz`, less commonly `cs` or `ly`. Examples: `bokor`→`bokrok`,
`torony`→`tornyok`, `iker`→`ikrek`, `eper`→`eprek`, `köröm`→`körmök`, `tükör`→`tükrök`. (A footnote
flags a few fleeting-vowel words that irregularly take the `a` linking vowel instead.) p. 82

**5.3.2.2.2 Regular nouns**: the negatively-defined default class — everything not covered by the
categories above. No stem change; the linking vowel `o/e/ö` is simply supplied before plural `-k`.
Examples: `kalap`→`kalapok`, `papír`→`papírok`, `tányér`→`tányérok`, `fiók`→`fiókok`,
`szék`→`székek`, `törvény`→`törvények`, `füzet`→`füzetek`, `bűn`→`bűnök`, `ismerős`→`ismerősök`,
`gyümölcs`→`gyümölcsök`. p. 83

ends in a vowel or consonant, and within each, which stem-behavior subtype applies, mapping each
combination to its rule (lengthen vowel + `-k` / stem loses length + `a`/`e` + `-k` / no stem
change + `a`/`e` + `-k` / last vowel elides + `-k` / no stem change + `o`/`e`/`ö` + `-k`). Not

### 5.4.1 Singular usage beyond the basic count meaning

Three environments use the Hungarian singular where English would use a plural or where the choice
isn't obvious from English alone:

**5.4.1.1** — After numerals or other quantity expressions, the noun stays singular: `Hat ló van
az istállóban` "There are six horses in the stable" (literally "six horse"); `Hány diák van a
teremben?` "How many students are in the classroom?" p. 84

**5.4.1.2** — Naturally-paired body parts and the clothing/accessories that go with them are
referred to with a singular noun even though English uses the plural: `kéz` "hands," `láb`
"legs/feet," `szem` "eyes," `kesztyű` "gloves," `cipő` "shoes," `szemüveg` "glasses," `nadrág`
"pants" — e.g. `Koszos a cipő` "The shoes are dirty," `Drága volt a szemüveg` "The glasses were
expensive." When a speaker needs to refer to just one item of the pair, `fél` "half" is prefixed:
`fél szem` "one eye," `fél kéz` "one hand," `fél láb` "one leg" (also idiomatically, `Csak fél
szemmel követte az eseményeket` "He followed the events only half-heartedly," lit. "with half an
eye"). Exception noted: this doesn't work for `cipő` — `félcipő` denotes a *kind* of shoe, not
"one shoe." p. 84

**5.4.1.3** — The singular can also refer to a general category, typically alongside the definite
article: `Az alma a sarokban, a körte a polcon van` "The apples are in the corner, the pears are
on the shelf" (generic reference, singular noun); `A bálna a legnagyobb emlősállat` "Whales are the
largest mammals." p. 85

### 5.4.2 Plural usage

The plural is used largely as in English, outside the singular-preferring environments above. Two
structural notes: the `-k` plural is not used in the possessive paradigm, which has its own plural
marker `-i` (cross-referenced to §8.2, outside this excerpt); and plural nouns take case suffixes
by adding them to the already-formed plural stem (`a diákok` "the students" → `a diákokról` "about
the students"; `a gépek` "the machines" → `a gépekben` "in the machines"). The temporal,
distributive, and sociative cases are not used in the plural at all. One universal rule: forming
the plural accusative always requires an `a/e` linking vowel after the plural `-k`, regardless of
which stem class the noun belongs to (`levelek` → `leveleket`; `kutyák` → `kutyákat`). p. 85

# Hungarian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: FSI Basic Hungarian, Vol. 1, Units 9–12

**Source:** Foreign Service Institute (Dept. of State), *Hungarian Basic Course, Volume 1* (Student
Text), 1965 (printed 2006 facsimile scan by FSI Language Courses), printed pages 179–265 (PDF pages
~178–265 of `FSI Hungarian Basic Course - Volume 1 - Student Text.pdf`, 279 pp. total). Covers **Unit
9** ("A Nagy Magyar Alföldön" — the Hungarian Great Plain, pp. 179–198), **Unit 10** ("Utazás
Dunántúlra" — Travel to Transdanubia, pp. 199–220), **Unit 11** ("A Színházban" — At the Theater, pp.
221–242), and **Unit 12** ("Sajtóértekezlet Budapesten" — Press Conference in Budapest, pp. 243–265).
This is the last unit of Volume 1; Volume 2 (not covered here) continues with Unit 13 onward. The PDF
has a genuine OCR text layer (Adobe Acrobat Paper Capture) with the expected scattered OCR noise
(character swaps like `l`/`1`, stray diacritic corruption); vocabulary items below were cross-checked
against their English-gloss pairing and, where a word recurs (in Basic Sentences, drills, or
narrative), against its other occurrences.

**Coverage note.** Each unit follows the same structure: numbered dialog exchanges in "Basic
Sentences" (each new lexical item glossed in the left margin before its sentence), one lettered
"Notes on Grammar" section per unit (A–D for Unit 9, A–B for Unit 10, A–D for Unit 11, A–F for Unit
12), then Substitution/Variation/Transformation/Translation/Response Drills, Conversation Practice,
Situations, and a closing Narrative (each narrative ends with a short own-vocabulary glossary for any
terms not already introduced). Per the extraction spec's coverage rule, every distinct vocabulary
item explicitly introduced in the Basic Sentences glosses and unit-closing narrative glossaries is
captured below, and all grammar points are paraphrased in full. The drills (Substitution, Variation,
Transformation, Translation, Response) are pattern-practice repeating already-introduced vocabulary
in a fixed grammatical frame (e.g., 40+ near-identical clauses swapping one noun); these are **not**
reproduced sentence-by-sentence, but any genuinely new lexical item that appears only inside a drill
(not in a Basic Sentences gloss or narrative glossary) has been mined out and is included in the
vocabulary table below, flagged in its Notes column as "drill-only introduction." No explicit
dialectal/register tags (no `[B]`/`[C]`/`[S]`-style markers) appear anywhere in this course; it
teaches a single standard conversational register throughout, with one significant exception noted
below (§ Register/colloquial findings).

**Morphological typology note.** Hungarian is agglutinative (see `../00_Extraction_Checklist.md`).
This FSI course is drill-based rather than descriptive, so it doesn't present worked morpheme-by-
morpheme breakdowns the way Rounds's *Essential Grammar* does (see `002_chapter5_nouns.md`); however,
Unit 11's past-tense verb-class tables and Unit 12's demonstrative/suffix paradigm table are both
genuinely systematic morphological content not previously captured from this course, and a few
compact morpheme breakdowns are included below where a single word-form usefully illustrates
suffix-stacking taught in this unit range (dative + possessive combinations, the "ago" construction).

## Grammar points

### 9.A The Indirect Object (Dative Case)

The dative case, marking the indirect object ("to/for X"), takes the ending `-nak` after back-vowel
words and `-nek` after front-vowel words. The dative personal pronouns use a special `nek-` root plus
possessive-type endings: `nekem` (to me), `neked` (to thee), `neki` (to him/her/it), `nekünk` (to us),
`nektek` (to you-pl.), `nekik` (to them). The formal pronoun `maga`/`maguk` takes an irregular dative,
`magának`/`maguknak`. Beyond marking the ordinary indirect object, the dative also appears in fixed
idiomatic constructions (e.g. `segít neki`, lit. "helps to her," for "helps her") and — its most
important extended use in this unit — as part of the possessive/"to have" construction below. p. 184

### 9.B The Concept of 'To Have' in Hungarian

Hungarian has no dedicated verb for "to have" in the sense of possession. Instead, the possessed
thing takes a possessive suffix agreeing with the possessor, and the existential verb `van` supplies
the predicate: `Házunk van` (lit. "our house is") = "We have a house." When the possessor is
emphasized with an explicit pronoun, that pronoun takes the dative form: `Nekünk nagy házunk van`
"We have a big house" (emphatic); `Nem neki van családja, hanem nekem van családom` "Not he but I
have a family." When the possessor is a noun (not a pronoun), the noun itself is put in the dative:
`Pálnak van egy háza` "Paul has a house"; `Péternek nincs felesége` "Peter doesn't have a wife." pp.
184–185

### 9.C Possessive with Plural Nouns

The source gives a full six-person paradigm (my/thy/his-her-its-your/our/your-fam./their) for several
noun classes in the possessive-plural, and explains the underlying rule: the marker of possessive
plurality is `-i-`, which always precedes the personal possessive ending and is identical across all
six persons for a given noun (only the following personal suffix changes). For nouns ending in `-a`
or `-e`, the plural-possessive stem lengthens that final vowel to `-á-`/`-é-` before adding `-i-`
(e.g. `ceruza` → `ceruzái-`). For nouns ending in any other vowel (except `-i`), the plural-possessive
stem equals the basic dictionary form. For nouns ending in `-i`, the plural-possessive stem equals the
singular "his/her" possessive form. For consonant-final nouns, the plural-possessive stem is also
identical to the singular third-person ("his/her") possessive form. pp. 185–186

### 9.D Uses of the Possessive

Alongside the ordinary "possessive noun + noun possessed" word order taught earlier in the course,
Hungarian has a second possessive frame: **dative-marked possessor + definite article + third-person
("his/her") possessive form of the thing possessed** — the possessed-thing element is *always* in the
"his/her" form (singular or plural as appropriate), never a first- or second-person possessive form,
regardless of who the possessor actually is: `Jánosnak a könyve` "John's book" (lit. "to John, his
book"); `A barátomnak a ceruzája` "My friend's pencil." This dative-possessor pattern is *obligatory*
(not optional) whenever the possessor is a relative or interrogative pronoun: `Az úr, akinek a
felesége itt van...` "The man whose wife is here..."; `Kinek a könyvét olvassa?` "Whose book are you
reading?" For a **double possessive** ("my friend's brother's teachers"), the chain is built as: basic
(or possessive-modified) form of the first possessor, plus dative form of the second possessor's
"his/her" possessive, plus definite article, plus the "his/her" form of the thing ultimately possessed,
inflected for whatever case/number the sentence needs: `János barátjának a kocsija nagyon nagy`
"John's friend's car is very large." pp. 186–187

### 10.A Negative Forms

Hungarian regularly uses **double negatives**, unlike English. Compound negative words (built from
`sem`/`se` + a base, e.g. `senki` "nobody," `sehol` "nowhere," `semmi` "nothing") require `nem` before
the verb whenever the compound negative follows the verb: `Nem talál semmit` "He doesn't find
anything" (lit. "not finds nothing"). If the compound negative instead precedes the verb, the verb's
own negative marker becomes either `nem` or the stronger `sem` (`se`) — both are grammatical, with
`sem`/`se` indicating emphasis: `Senki nem jön` / `Senki sem jön`, "Nobody is coming." Four further
constructions are distinguished: (1) `sem...sem` = "neither...nor," always requiring `nem` before the
verb, placed before or after the negated items depending on their own position relative to the verb;
(2) `sincs`/`sincsen` (and plural `sincsenek`) corresponds to `nincs`/`nincsen` when a compound
negative precedes it (`Senki sincs itt` = `Senki nincs itt`, "Nobody is here"), but in any other
position means "is/are not either" ("nor is/are there"); (3) `sem`/`se` placed *after* a compound
negative becomes a pure emphasizing particle ("not even one"): `Egy tanár se ül` "Not even one
teacher is sitting"; (4) `sem` can also simply mean "not either" as the negative counterpart of `is`
("also"), never combined with a compound negative: `Péter sem eszik húst` "Nor does Peter eat meat."
pp. 204–206

### 10.B The Suffixes -ra/-re, -ról/-ről, and -tól/-től

`-ra`/`-re` marks motion **onto** a surface — contrasted with `-ba`/`-be` (motion **into**, penetration)
and with the static locative `-n`/`-on`/`-en`/`-ön` (no motion implied; any word taking `-n` for "in"
will also take `-ra`/`-re` for "onto/into"). `-ról`/`-ről` marks motion **away from, off the surface
of** something — contrasted with `-ból`/`-ből` (motion from the *inside* of something); any word
taking `-n` for "in" will correspondingly take `-ról`/`-ről` for "from." `-tól`/`-től` marks motion
**from the vicinity of, from near** something. All three suffixes can also function as independent,
personal-suffix-taking forms (like the dative pronouns in 9.A): `-ra` → `rám, rád, rá(rája), ránk,
rátok, rájuk`; `-tól` → `tőlem, tőled, tőle, tőlünk, tőletek, tőlük`; `-ról` → `rólam, rólad, róla,
rólunk, rólatok, rólukra` [as given: rólam, rólad, róla, rólunk, rólatok, róluk]. Beyond their literal
spatial senses, all three have further idiomatic/extended uses the student must simply memorize as
encountered. pp. 206–207

### 11.A Past Tense Forms

Hungarian has a single past tense covering what English splits across simple past, past emphatic,
past progressive, present perfect, and pluperfect. The past-tense marker is `-t` (sometimes preceded
by a linking vowel plus doubled `-tt`), added to the verb root before personal endings, in both a
**definite** (object-agreement) and **indefinite** conjugation. Four verb-root classes are given: (1)
roots ending in `-ad, -ed, -od, -l (-ly), -n (-ny), -r` add `-t` plus personal endings directly (e.g.
`akarni` → `akartam`); exception: `adni`, third-person-singular indefinite `adott`. (2) Roots ending in
a sibilant (`-s, -sz, -z`), or `-b, -d` (other than `-ad/-ed`), `-gy, -k, -p, -t, -v`, or `-t` preceded
by a short vowel, form the past the same way except the third-person-singular indefinite ends in
`-ott/-ett/-ött` (e.g. `kapni` → indefinite `kapott`); exceptions `aludni`, `feküdni` (inflect like
class 1), and `kötni` (inflects like class 3). (3) Roots ending in `-t` preceded by a long vowel, or in
a double consonant, take an auxiliary vowel plus `-tt` in both definite and indefinite forms (e.g.
`tanítani` → `tanítottam`); exceptions `varrni`, `szállni` (inflect like class 1), `látni` (like class
2). (4) A closed set of irregular verbs (`venni, tenni, vinni, hinni, enni, inni, jönni, lenni`) have
their own idiosyncratic past stems (e.g. `vettem, tettem, vittem, hittem`; `enni` indefinite 3sg.
`evett`, `inni` indefinite 3sg. `ivott`). Notably, `lenni` has **two** past-tense forms with different
meanings: `voltam` = "I was," `lettem` = "I became." pp. 225–228

### 11.B Use of the Present and Past Tenses in Hungarian

Hungarian tense usage sometimes diverges from English at points requiring care: (1) For an action that
began in the past and is still ongoing, Hungarian uses the **present** tense where English uses the
present perfect, expressing the elapsed duration via a possessive-suffixed time noun: `Három éve
tanulnak magyarul` "They have been studying Hungarian for three years" (lit. "their three years they
study"). (2) For an action that began in the past and was still continuing at a *later* point in the
past, Hungarian uses the **past** tense where English uses the past perfect progressive: `Három hete
tanult magyarul, amikor új tanárt kaptak` "He had been studying Hungarian for three weeks when they
got a new teacher." (3) English sequence-of-tenses rules don't always transfer: in a dependent clause,
Hungarian's **present** tense expresses action *simultaneous* with the main clause's action (even when
the main clause is past — `Tudtam, hogy vár` = "I knew that he *was* waiting," not "waits"), while the
dependent clause's **past** tense expresses action *antecedent* to the main clause's action (`Tudtam,
hogy várt` = "I knew that he *had been* waiting"). pp. 228–229

### 11.C The Suffixes -ig and -hoz/-hez/-höz

`-ig` corresponds to "until, up to, as far as," usable for both place and time (`Tízig dolgozik` "He
works until ten"; `Az ajtóig szaladt` "He ran as far as the door"). `-hoz`/`-hez`/`-höz` ("to, as far
as the outside of") differs from `-ig` in emphasis: `-ig` stresses the *limit/termination* of an
action or period, while `-hoz` etc. stresses *direction of movement* and is typically used with a
motion verb (`-ig` doesn't require one). When `-ig` marks the duration of a period ("for..."), the
**accusative** case can often substitute for it with the same English equivalent, but `-ig` is more
natural with intransitive verbs and the accusative with transitive ones: `Három évig (évet) tanult
magyarul` "He studied Hungarian for three years." pp. 229–230

### 11.D How to Express 'Ago' in Hungarian

Hungarian expresses "[time] ago" via the third-person-singular **possessive** form of the time-denoting
noun with no separate word needed: `Egy órája jöttem ide` "I came here an hour ago" (lit. "my/its one
hour, I came here"). An equivalent, more explicit construction uses the instrumental `-val`/`-vel`
suffix on the time noun plus the standalone word `ezelőtt` ("before this"): `Két órával ezelőtt jöttem
ide` = `Két órája jöttem ide`, both "I came here two hours ago." p. 230

### 12.A Prefixes with Verbs

Hungarian verbal prefixes (preverbs) either modify/change a verb's basic meaning or emphasize the
completion of its action. Two subtypes exist: prefixes usable standalone as well as attached to verbs
(`vissza` "back," `rá` "onto," `bele` "into"), and prefixes that occur only bound to verbs (`be`
"into," `ki` "out," `el` "away," `fel`/`föl` "up," `meg` "wholly/completely"). In normal
subject-predicate word order, the prefix fuses with its verb into one written word and carries the
sentence stress: `János bemegy a házba` "John goes into the house." The prefix detaches and becomes an
independently-placed word under specific stress conditions: if emphasis is on the prefix+verb and the
verb is an infinitive following a modal auxiliary or impersonal expression, the prefix moves to the
front of the sentence, before the auxiliary (`Ki akarok menni` "I want to go out"); if emphasis is on
some *other* sentence element (with the verb still infinitive), that stressed element precedes the
auxiliary while the prefix stays attached to the infinitive; if the verb is finite and some other word
bears the stress, the prefix simply follows immediately after the finite verb (`Ki ül le a székre?`
"Who's sitting down on the chair?"). An interrogative word always occupies this pre-verb emphatic
position, and its answer must mirror the same word order. pp. 247–249

### 12.B The Future Tense

Hungarian, like English, often uses the present tense plus a time expression to indicate futurity. A
dedicated future construction also exists: infinitive of the main verb + present tense of the
auxiliary `fogni` (`Fizetni fogok/fogom` "I'll pay [it]," indefinite/definite respectively). The future
of "to be" (`lenni`) is identical in form to the present tense of "to become": `leszek, leszel, lesz,
leszünk, lesztek, lesznek` covers both "I become/will be," etc. p. 249

### 12.C Demonstratives

`ez a` "this" and `az a` "that" (plurals `ezek a`, `azok a`) are built from two definite articles, the
first agreeing in number/case with the noun they modify. When any of the suffixes already studied
(illative, inessive, elative, sublative, delative, ablative, dative, etc.) attach to the demonstrative,
the article's final `-z` typically assimilates to the following consonant, producing a doubled
consonant: `ez` + `-ben` → `ebben a szállodában` "in this hotel"; `az` + `-hoz` → not shown directly
but pattern extends (`ehhez`, `ahhoz` type forms follow the same assimilation logic; the source
tabulates a dozen full case pairs, e.g. `ezt a képet`/`azt a képet`, `ezen az asztalon`/`azon az
asztalon`, `ebbe a vendéglőbe`/`abba a vendéglőbe`, `ebből a szobából`/`abból a szobából`, `erről a
fáról`/`arról a fáról`, `erre a székre`/`arra a székre`, `ettől a fiútól`/`attól a fiútól`, `ennek a
gyereknek`/`annak a gyereknek`). pp. 249–250

### 12.D Tudni and Ismerni

Both translate English "to know," but are not interchangeable. `tudni` covers knowledge of facts,
information, ability, or "knowing how": `Tud magyarul beszélni?` "Can you speak Hungarian?" `ismerni`
covers *acquaintance* — with a person, place, or thing already encountered: `Ismerem a lányát` "I know
his daughter"; `Ismerem Európát` "I know Europe." p. 250

### 12.E Kérni and Kérdezni

Both translate English "to ask," but are not interchangeable. `kérdezni` always means "to ask a
question," and requires the "from" ending (`-tól`/`-től`) on the person being asked — Hungarian
literally frames "I'm asking you a question" as "I'm asking a question *from* you." `kérni` never
implies a question; it means "to ask for, request, beg": `Kérek egy pohár bort` "I want [am asking
for] a glass of wine"; `Bocsánatot kérek` "I beg your pardon." p. 250

### 12.F Ordinal Numbers

The ordinals from "first" through "thirty-second" are given in full (`első, második, harmadik,
negyedik, ötödik, hatodik, hetedik, nyolcadik, kilencedik, tizedik, tizenegyedik, tizenkettedik,
tizenharmadik, huszonegyedik, harminckettedik`); from `negyedik` on, ordinals are formed by adding an
auxiliary vowel plus `-dik` to the cardinal number, shortening any long vowel in the cardinal's basic
form. Three date-related usage patterns are highlighted: (1) a proper noun + ordinal ("John the
Second") becomes *ordinal* + name in Hungarian, with **no definite article**: `Második János`. (2) To
name a date ("July 10th" / "the 10th of July," both rendered identically), Hungarian requires the
**third-person-singular possessive** form of the ordinal, placed *after* the month name: `Július
tizedike`, `December huszonötödike`, `Január elseje`. (3) To say "on" that date, the possessive
ordinal's final vowel lengthens before the superessive `-n`: `Július tizedikén`, `December
huszonötödikén`. pp. 250–251

## Register/colloquial findings — flagged for the project's slang-synthesis purposes

This course is fundamentally standard, government-produced diplomatic/conversational Hungarian, and
(as noted above) carries none of the source's own explicit register/dialect tags. Even so, several
items in this unit range are worth flagging because they diverge from pure grammar-drill vocabulary:

1. **`kulák`** (Unit 9, p. 182) — a politically loaded, Soviet-derived term for "wealthy peasant," used
   pejoratively during Hungary's Communist-era collectivization. The dialog itself marks it as
   special: the Hungarian sentence leaves the word untranslated in the English gloss ("Now he is
   called a 'kulák'"), i.e. the course treats it as an in-context sociopolitical label rather than
   ordinary vocabulary needing translation. This is a genuine period-register/political-register data
   point, not neutral core vocabulary.
2. **`pálinka`** (Unit 9, p. 198, narrative-only) — everyday informal-life vocabulary (fruit brandy)
   that surfaces only in the closing narrative's color detail about Tóth and his friends' drinking
   habits ("especially in winter, when it's cold"), not in the structured dialog at all. This kind of
   incidental, non-drilled cultural/colloquial vocabulary is exactly the category this project is
   watching for.
3. **`viccelni`** "to joke" (Unit 12, p. 245) — one of very few explicitly conversational-register verbs
   in the entire unit range; used self-referentially in dialog ("Maga mindig viccel" — "You're always
   joking").
4. **`drágaság`** ("high cost of living") and the surrounding Unit 12 dialog passage (p. 247) — the
   fictional press-conference framing device lets the course voice a genuine, if mild, socioeconomic
   critique ("everybody knows the reasons [for high prices], but the fact is that the workers can't
   make enough money") for a U.S. government-produced Cold War-era course. Worth flagging as an
   unusually candid register moment, though it stays within neutral/core vocabulary rather than slang.
5. **`ön`** vs. **`maga`** (Unit 10, p. 201) — the more formal second-person pronoun `ön` appears
   exactly once, spoken by the porter addressing Johnson as "sir," contrasting with the `maga` used
   pervasively elsewhere in the course — a small but real formality-register distinction worth noting
   for the taxonomy even though the source doesn't itself comment on it.
6. **`szabadság`** (Unit 11, p. 241) — glossed here only as "holiday, leave" (vacation time), but the
   same word is the standard Hungarian noun for "freedom" — a semantic doubling with real political
   weight in a Cold War-era course that the source's own narrow gloss doesn't surface. Flagged for
   later cross-reference, not extracted as two separate senses here since only the "vacation" sense is
   actually used/glossed in this unit range.

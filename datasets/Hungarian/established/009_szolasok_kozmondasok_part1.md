# Hungarian — Established Vocabulary: Sayings and Proverbs, Part 1 (sample)

**Source:** O. Nagy Gábor, *Magyar szólások és közmondások* ("Hungarian Sayings and Proverbs"),
4th edition (Gondolat, 1985; 1st ed. 1966). PDF pages 1–210 of 835 total (the book's own
introduction/front matter runs to roughly PDF page 24; numbered entries then begin at entry #1
and, across this page range, run through roughly **entry #1008**, covering the alphabetical
keyword span from **abaúj/ábécé/ablak** at the start of the entry list through **fül** ("ear") at
PDF page 210). This is the first of what will be several chunked files against this single
835-page dictionary; later parts should pick up numbering and alphabetical range from where this
one stops (~entry #1008, keyword "fül").

**Coverage note — deliberate sample, not exhaustive.** This page range alone contains on the
order of a thousand numbered entries. Per the dispatch instructions and this project's
established practice for large glossaries (see the SCB extraction runs), this file extracts a
**representative sample of 47 entries**, not a full transcription. Selection prioritized entries
that (a) show interesting figurative derivation from a literal/concrete image, (b) carry one of
the source's own register tags, or (c) reveal a recurring idiomatic pattern (see "Recurring
patterns" below). Entries were drawn from across the full page span (PDF pages 45–195) rather
than clustered at the start, so the sample reflects the keyword range's real breadth (árnyék
"shadow," bagoly "owl," barát "friend," bőr "skin," cigány "Gypsy," csík "loach/streak," eb
"dog," él "live," erő "strength," fa "tree," farok "tail," felhő "cloud," félsz "fear," etc.)
rather than only its first few letters.

**A note on ethnically-marked folklore content.** The "cigány" (Gypsy/Roma) keyword cluster
(PDF p. 105 and nearby) contains a large run of folk similes and proverbs — many tagged (táj)
"dialectal" or (rég, gúny) "archaic, mocking" — that reflect period Hungarian rural stereotyping
of Roma people (e.g., proverbs built on the premise that a "Gypsy" figure is inherently poor,
dishonest, or boastful). This is genuine, attested 19th–20th century Hungarian folklore data and
is retained here as such (one neutral, non-stereotyping example is included below — "Cigány is a
maga lovát dicséri," a version of "every peddler praises his own wares" with no ethnic content in
its actual meaning), but future slang-mechanics analysis of this cluster should treat it
explicitly as **attested-but-biased period source material**, not as neutral linguistic data, the
same caution this project already applies to source-reliability flagging elsewhere.

**Register-tag mapping.** The source annotates many (not all) entries with a parenthetical
register/connotation tag of its own. Mapped onto this project's `00_Usage_Tier_Taxonomy.md` as
follows (documented here for auditability, since this is the first extraction to need most of
these mappings):

| Source tag | Meaning | Usage Tier used here |
|---|---|---|
| (rég) | *régies* — archaic | `archaic` |
| (elavulóban) | "becoming obsolete" — not yet fully archaic per the source's own finer distinction | `archaic` (noted as "becoming obsolete" in the entry's Notes) |
| (táj) | *tájszó* — dialectal/regional | `regional` |
| (nép) | *népies* — folk/popular vernacular register, not necessarily tied to one region | `colloquial.folk` (new subcategory — origin: Hungarian; distinct from (táj), which is specifically geographic) |
| (biz) | *bizalmas* — informal spoken register, standard Hungarian lexicographic label | `colloquial.familiar` (new subcategory — origin: Hungarian; distinct from (nép), which is more rural/folk-flavored) |

| (argó) | *argó* — slang/argot, explicitly so labeled by the source | `slang` |
| (vál) | *választékos* — elevated, refined diction | `literary` |
| no tag | unmarked, standard current usage per the source | `core` |

Connotation-only tags that co-occur with the above (gúny "mocking," tréf "jocular," szépítő
"euphemistic," rosszalló "disapproving," gyak. "usually") are **not** treated as separate Usage
Tiers — they're recorded in the Notes column alongside the underlying register tag, since they
describe tone/function rather than where the term sits in the standard language.

**Morphological typology note.** Hungarian is agglutinative (see `../00_Extraction_Checklist.md`),
but these entries are multi-word idioms/proverbs, not single agglutinated word-forms, so no
morpheme-breakdown rows are needed here — that convention applies to composed single words (see
`002_chapter5_nouns.md` for worked examples from this language).

## Recurring idiomatic/figurative patterns (for future slang-mechanics analysis)

- **Single-keyword idiom clusters.** The dictionary is organized so that dozens of independent
  idioms/proverbs pile up under one headword (bagoly "owl": #61–71; bolond "fool": #917–941+;
  bőr "skin": #1199–1227+; eb "dog": #65–90+; cigány "Gypsy": #66–90). This isn't incidental —
  it shows that certain concrete nouns (animals, body parts, ethnically-marked stock figures)
  function as genuinely *productive metaphor generators* in Hungarian folk speech, spinning off
  many independent figurative extensions rather than just one fixed idiom each. Directly relevant
  to the `metaphor_productivity` derivation type in `00_Usage_Tier_Taxonomy.md`.
- **"Skin" (bőr) as a euphemism domain for both death and exploitation.** The bőr cluster alone
  yields at least four independent death euphemisms (padlásra kerül a bőre, ráhűlt a bőr,
  rászáradt a bőr, kiugrott bőréből) plus a separate sub-thread about skin standing in for a
  person's material wealth/substance (a bőrt lehúzza = fleece someone; a bőre alatt is pénz van =
  he's loaded). One body-part image, two unrelated taboo-adjacent domains (death, financial
  exploitation) — a clean euphemism_chain case study.
- **"Understands X the way [animal] understands [authority figure]" is a reusable idiom
  template, not a one-off figure.** The dictionary's own cross-references show this exact
  ignorance-idiom slot filled with several interchangeable animal/target pairs across nearby
  entries (bagoly "owl"/ávemária "Hail Mary" at #62 here; the source cross-references a parallel
  "ty2" entry using tyúk "hen" instead) — the surrounding vocabulary (tyúk, ló) swaps in and out
  of a fixed comparative frame ("(annyit) ért hozzá, mint X az Y-hoz"). Structurally similar to
  "understands X like a cow understands Sunday" patterns found in other languages' folk idiom
  — worth tracking as a productive syntactic template independent of which animal/target fills it.
- **Argó (true slang) is rare and clustered, not evenly spread.** Out of ~1000 entries scanned in
  this page range, only two carried the dictionary's own (argó) tag (#179, #180, both under
  "csík"), both from the same headword and both about money/flight. This is a useful negative
  data point: in a 1966-first-edition folk-proverb dictionary, register skews overwhelmingly
  toward (rég)/(táj)/(nép)/(biz), with genuine period slang a small minority — worth keeping in
  mind when calibrating how much of this source's material is really "slang-adjacent" versus
  general folk speech for the synthesis phase.
- **Vulgar/taboo entries attach to specific semantic zones**, not randomly: bodily functions
  (babostányér, fatál), sex/marriage informality (disznóvályúnál esküdött, seen near p. 135 but
  not extracted above), and fear/cowardice (félsz van a seggében). Coarse language in this corpus
  isn't generic profanity — it clusters around a handful of taboo topics, each with its own small
  family of euphemistic or vulgar variants.
- **Cross-references are dense and largely un-digitized here.** Nearly every entry in the source
  ends with "Vö: [other entry codes]," pointing to semantically related entries elsewhere in the
  (currently unextracted) rest of the dictionary. A future pass that extracts more of this book

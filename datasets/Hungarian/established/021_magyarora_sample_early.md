# Hungarian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Magyaróra / New Paths to the Hungarian Language (Representative Sample)

**Source:** "19 Magyaróra New Paths to the Hungarian Language.pdf" (source PDF metadata title: *Magyaróra -
Learn Hungarian (Hungarian Lessons): New Paths to the Hungarian Language*), a genuinely huge (1,324-page,
34MB) compiled PDF. Direct inspection (not page-count arithmetic) shows this file is **not a single
coursebook** but a merge of at least two distinct sources bound together:

1. **A "Nyelvtani mozaik" / *Practical Hungarian Grammar* systematic B1-level reference grammar** (bilingual
   Hungarian-English, numbered printed pages, 110 chapters per the book's own Introduction, each chapter a
   fixed two-page unit: left page = grammar explanation/paradigm table, right page = graded exercises,

   grammar and vocabulary required for the lower intermediate (B1) level language examination."
2. **A large set of separately-authored, unpaginated supplementary worksheets and dialogue modules** from
   the magyarora.com website (topical vocabulary sheets, short-exercise worksheets, a spoken-Hungarian
   "speech panels" phrasebook), individually credited to named contributors (Szita Szilvia, Szabó Eszter,
   Bencze Rozália, Fogarasi Ilona, Götz Viktor, Klementné Kecskeméti Beáta, etc.) via footer copyright lines

## Grammar points

### 1. Past tense formation — four marker classes (PDF pp. 109-121, printed pp. 58-64)

Hungarian has a single past tense (unlike English's simple/progressive/perfect distinction — the book
notes `vártam` can translate as "I was waiting," "I waited," or "I had waited" depending on context). The
past-tense marker is always `-t` or `-tt`, inserted directly after the verb stem and followed by the
person/number ending — but which allomorph a given verb takes, and what (if any) linking vowel appears, is
lexically determined by the verb's final sound(s). The book organizes this into four classes, corroborated
by its own appendix's classification of ~200+ common verbs:

**Class 1 (`-t`, no linking vowel):** verbs ending in `-j, -l, -m, -n, -ny, -r` (e.g. `fáj`→`fájt`, `csinál`
→`csinált`, `ír`→`írt`), most verbs ending in `-ad/-ed` (`marad`→`maradt`, but note exceptions: `téved,
enged, szed, ad` do NOT follow this pattern), and the irregular pair `alszik/aludni`→`aludt`,
`fekszik/feküdni`→`feküdt`. Full paradigm example (`talál` "find"): indefinite 1sg `találtam`, 2sg
`találtál`, 3sg `talált`, 1pl `találtunk`, 2pl `találtatok`, 3pl `találtak`; definite 3sg `találta`, etc.

**Class 2 (`-t` for most persons, but `-ott/-ett/-ött` in 3sg indefinite):** verbs ending in a vowel + one of
`-b, -d, -g, -gy, -k, -p, -s, -sz, -z, -v` (e.g. `lát`→3sg `látott`, `keres`→3sg `keresett`, `néz`→3sg
`nézett`), verbs ending in `-at/-et` including the `-hat/-het`, `-tat/-tet`, `-gat/-get` derivational
suffixes (`mutat`→`mutatott`), and verbs ending in consonant + `-d` (`küld`→`küldött`, `mond`→`mondott`).
Vowel-losing verbs in `-z`/`-g` drop their final stem vowel before `-tt` (`érez`→`érzett`, `mozog`→
`mozgott`), but other vowel-losing verbs are regular.

**Class 3 (`-tt` in every person):** verbs ending in more than one consonant (`hall`→`hallott`, `ért`→
`értett` — exception: `áll, száll, varr` follow Class 1 instead), verbs ending in `-t` itself (`tanít`→
`tanított`, `köt`→`kötött` — exception: `-at/-et`-final verbs and `lát` follow Class 2), and monosyllabic
`-ó/-ő`-final verbs with no linking vowel (`sző`→`szőtt`, `nő`→`nőtt`, `fő`→`főtt`).

**Class 4 (irregular):** the nine verbs whose infinitive ends in `-nni` are irregular in the past tense:
`jön`(come)→`jött`, `megy`(go)→`ment`, `van`(be)→`volt`, `lesz`(become)→`lett`, `iszik`(drink)→indef.
`ivott`/def. `itta`, `eszik`(eat)→indef. `evett`/def. `ette`, `visz`(carry)→`vitt`, plus `hisz`(believe)→
`hitt`, `tesz`(do/put)→`tett`, `vesz`(buy)→`vett` (these three follow the `visz` pattern exactly). `Van`,
`lesz`, `jön`, `megy` are intransitive and therefore lack a definite conjugation.

**Cross-cutting: the indefinite/definite conjugation split.** Every past-tense paradigm above is given twice
— once for the indefinite (subject-only-agreement) conjugation, once for the definite (object-definiteness-
agreeing) conjugation, which is used when the verb has a definite direct object. The two conjugations differ
only in the non-1sg endings (e.g. `találtam` is identical in both, but 3sg diverges: indefinite `talált` vs.
definite `találta`). The book cross-references its own separate chapter on "A határozatlan és a határozott
tárgy típusai" (types of indefinite vs. definite objects, printed p. 128) as the governing rule for *when*
to use which conjugation — that chapter itself falls outside this dispatch's sampled range and is a strong
candidate for a future non-redundant-supplement pass, since prior Hungarian `established/` files
(`002_chapter5_nouns.md`, `004_chapter4_verbs_part1.md`) may or may not already cover it fully.

**Morpheme breakdown examples** (agglutinative structure per this language's typology):
- `találtam` = `talál-` (find, stem) + `-t-` (PST) + `-am` (1SG.INDEF) → "I found."
- `elfelejtettem` = `el-` (perfective/telic preverb "away, completely") + `felejt-` (forget, stem) + `-ett-`
  (PST, class 2 3sg-triggering allomorph generalized here to the whole paradigm's shared stem-final
  environment) + `-em` (1SG.INDEF) → "I forgot."
- `kiakadtam` = `ki-` (preverb "out") + `akad-` (get stuck/caught, stem) + `-t-` (PST) + `-am` (1SG) →
  literally "I got stuck outward," idiomatically "I flipped out / freaked out."

### 2. Explicit register/vulgarity marking in the spoken-Hungarian phrasebook module (PDF pp. 178-210)

The "Beszédpanelek és mintadialógusok" module is structured as six functional-speech-act sections (starting
a conversation, encouragement, expressing pleasant surprise, expressing irritation, agreement/refusal, and
asking someone to repeat themselves), each giving 3-5 near-synonymous "speech panel" phrases per function
before a worked model dialogue. This structure is itself register-stratified: within nearly every panel, the
phrases are implicitly ordered from more neutral/formal to more casual, and the source explicitly annotates
several with parenthetical register/pragmatic tags — `(vulgáris)` for `kurva` and its compounds (`kurva jó`,
`a kurva életbe`) and for `szívás`/`szar`; `(irritált)` for stock brush-off phrases like `Szedd már össze
magad!`; and `(ironikus)` for phrases whose literal positive meaning is being used sarcastically (e.g. `Hát,
de jó!` said about bad news, `Gondolhatod, hogy örültem!` said about something the speaker clearly didn't
enjoy). This is a genuinely rare explicit slang/register-annotation resource per the coverage rule's mandate
to never skip such tags, and is a strong candidate for the mechanics-analysis phase's treatment of Hungarian
irony-marking and taboo-intensifier scales (`rohadt` < `marha`/`baromi` < `kurva`, with `kurva` alone
flagged vulgar by the source despite all four functioning as the same syntactic slot).

### 3. International (Latinate) vs. native Hungarian personality/trait vocabulary as a register axis (PDF pp. 133-134)

The "Nemzetközi szavak: Emberi tulajdonságok" worksheet presents ~40 internationally recognizable
Latin/Greek-derived adjectives (`agresszív, arrogáns, cinikus, egocentrikus, idealista, introvertált,
pesszimista,` etc.) and then has the learner match each to a native-Hungarian paraphrase or synonym
(`önző, hülye/buta, befelé forduló, szomorkás,` etc.). The worksheet's own framing ("Sok tulajdonságnak van
magyar megfelelője is" — "many traits also have a Hungarian equivalent") treats the international-loanword
set as one register/register-neutral option and the native-Hungarian set as a distinct, often more
colloquial or more evaluatively loaded, alternative (compare the clinical `idióta` against the blunt native
`hülye, buta`, or `melankolikus` against the more everyday `szomorkás, szomorú`). This is a useful reference
point for later mechanics analysis of how Hungarian slang formation might exploit the Latinate/native
lexical-doublet system, parallel to a similar (though historically distinct) doublet pattern in English.

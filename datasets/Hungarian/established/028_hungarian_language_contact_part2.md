### 1. Vojvodina Hungarian minority education, 1918–present (tail of the chapter's history section)

Continuing from the sibling chunk: interwar Yugoslavia's minority-education framework was loosely
obligatory and unevenly enforced (a 30-student minimum for a Hungarian section; teachers required to
pass a Serbian-language exam; students with "non-Hungarian sounding" names barred from Hungarian
sections). Hungarian secondary education nearly disappeared by WWII (337 students in two surviving
high schools). Postwar (second) Yugoslavia initially expanded Hungarian-medium education
substantially (peak >40,000 students) before two structural reforms — mid-1950s "regional schools"
merging Hungarian and Serbian instruction, and late-1970s "specialized schools" with a shared first
two years — eroded first-language education; by the 1990s only 23,000 Hungarian-section students
remained (80% of all Hungarian students; 35–40% of secondary Hungarian students attend Serbian
sections). The chapter's own diagnosis: what's nominally "first-language education" is increasingly
**submersion** or **transitional bilingual education** in practice — some subjects taught in
Serbian by non-Hungarian-speaking teachers due to teacher shortages — producing measurable code
attrition and technical-vocabulary gaps, and inducing habitual code-switching. pp. 224–228

### 2. SHOH survey: Hungarian is used more in private/informal domains, less in official ones (Vojvodina)

The chapter reports its own SHOH-project domain-of-use survey (Table 7.5, not reproduced in full):
Hungarian dominates private conversation (75.2%), reading (54.5%), and especially church use (95%);
Serbian dominates or ties in writing (45.9% Serbian vs. 47.9% Hungarian, split roughly evenly between
private and official/professional writing) and in official/administrative domains (documents to
administrative offices: only 33.5% in Hungarian; the police: only 32% Hungarian). Village Hungarians
use Hungarian more than town Hungarians in every domain, tracking their relative demographic
majority status. Vojvodina Hungarians, per the chapter's comparison to other Carpathian Basin
minority regions, use Hungarian in writing more than most other minority-Hungarian groups, and watch
Hungarian-language television more (with the local Hungarian channel more popular than either
channels from Hungary or Serbian channels). pp. 227–230

### 3. Prekmurje (Slovenian Hungarians): a much smaller, more assimilation-pressured community

Slovenia's Hungarian minority (2002 census: 6,243 total, ~0.31% of Slovenia's population; 5,445 in
the Prekmurje region specifically, 4.5% of that region) has undergone catastrophic demographic
decline: the Lendava/Alsólendva area alone went from 20,000 Hungarians (1910 census) to 3,917 (2002)
— a loss of natural growth plus 63% of the base population over 80 years. The territory's own name,
*Prekmurje* ('beyond the Mura', Hungarian *Muravidék*), reflects a 1920 border split along the Mura
river that had not existed as a linguistic boundary before — the pre-1920 linguistic border between
Hungarian- and Slovenian-speaking zones had stabilized by the turn of the 19th century and required
essentially no bilingualism, since local economic life (fairs, markets, land-bound agriculture) was
territorially self-contained. The chapter identifies a genuine ethnic subgroup, the Prekmurje/Vend
Slovenes, whose dialect, culture, and communal mentality diverged from other Slovenes specifically
because of centuries of proximity to and economic/cultural orientation toward Hungary rather than
toward Austria-based Slovene communities — a case of a minority-adjacent majority-language group
itself showing strong contact effects. pp. 229–232

### 4. The "Vend debate" — a politically fraught ethnonym dispute

A named controversy in the source: Yugoslav-era Slovene political and scholarly circles interpreted
the historical term *Vend* (denoting the Prekmurje Slovene ethnographic/dialectal group, whose own
distinct dialect had served as the language of local church, writing, and literature) as an implicit
claim of separateness from the Slovene nation, and moved to eliminate the term from official use.
After the post-WWI border split the group in two, the two halves adopted new self-designations:
*prekmurci* ('those beyond the Mura') in Slovenia and *porabski* ('those around the Rába') in
Hungary. This is presented as a case study in how political anxiety about linguistic/ethnic
separatism can reshape even a group's own name for itself. pp. 235–236

### 5. Slovenia's 1959–present bilingual education model for Prekmurje — a genuinely unique design

Unlike Vojvodina's submersion-leaning system, Prekmurje developed (from 1959/60) a distinctive
**dual first-language bilingual model**, designed independently (Yugoslavia's non-aligned political
stance meant the planners had no access to comparable international models): every child in the
designated bilingual area, Hungarian or Slovene, attended the same bilingual schools from preschool
age (3–6), with paired Hungarian/Slovene-native teachers per class and group-based instruction so
children's own language choice, not teacher assignment, drove which language was used moment to
moment. Instructional-language ratio targeted 70–80% Slovene to 20–30% Hungarian, with new material
presented in Slovene and revision/consolidation in Hungarian specifically to build Hungarian
technical terminology alongside Slovene. The chapter reports this model's own later self-assessment:
despite slowing assimilation and reducing inter-ethnic conflict, many teachers/linguists/
psychologists observed that graduates often reached satisfying proficiency in **neither** language
fully — leading to a subsequent reform where parents now choose a "first language" track for their
children, complicated by parents sometimes mis-declaring their child's first language as Slovene in
the (often self-defeating) hope of stronger Slovene acquisition. pp. 236–239

### 6. Prekmurje Hungarian as dialectally stable but functionally "limited code"

Unlike Vojvodina Hungarian's continuum toward the standard, Bokor (1990) found Prekmurje Hungarian
dialectal features are homogeneous **across generations** (young, middle-aged, and elderly speakers
alike) — no generational drift toward the standard. The chapter's central functional claim: this
regional system works only for "familial," everyday-life topics; once a topic requires modern
institutional/technical vocabulary, speakers reach for Slovenian loanwords wholesale (the *obcsina*/
*toplipod*/*tájnik*/*direktor*/*pumpa*/*primár*/*student* set above), producing what the authors call
a genuinely **limited code** — one that fails when a Prekmurje Hungarian speaker tries to communicate
with Hungarians from outside the region, including with standard-speaking Hungarians from Hungary.
The chapter explicitly connects this functional narrowing to eventual **language shift**: because
the regional code cannot serve as a full communicative system beyond the local community, its
speakers face pressure toward complete abandonment of Hungarian as a first language, a stronger and
more acute risk than in Vojvodina. A mixed-dialogue transcript (Varga 1990) is cited as direct
evidence — a pension-office exchange thick with Slovenian content words (*potrdilut*, *bolniskun*,
*tájnica*) embedded in heavily dialectal Hungarian phonology and morphology. pp. 243–246

### 7. The SHOH project's three-way variable typology

The chapter frames its own quantitative section around a typology used across the whole SHOH
(Sociolinguistics of Hungarian Outside Hungary) research project: **"universal variables"**
(sociolinguistic variation present in all Hungarian speech, unrelated to contact); **"universal
contact variables"** (features attributable to language contact but occurring, to different degrees,
in the Hungarian of all Hungarians — including Hungary's own, via centuries of contact with
neighboring Indo-European languages); and **"contact variables"** proper (features specific to one
country's contact situation, studied only for Slovakia and Ukraine, not covered in this chapter).
This chunk's statistical findings concern the middle category. p. 246

### 8. Analytic vs. synthetic morphological choice — the strongest, most consistent contact effect

Across nine SHOH questionnaire items testing analytic (Indo-European-style, multi-word) vs.
synthetic (Hungarian-style, single-derived-word) variants — e.g. *tagsági díj* vs. *tagdíj*,
*utazás busszal* vs. *buszozás*, *légi tér* vs. *légtér* — every bilingual region tested favors
analytic forms more than monolingual Hungary does (24.5% analytic in Hungary vs. up to 44.8% in
Prekmurje). The chapter's own cross-region ranking by analytic-form preference: Prekmurje (44.8%) >
Subcarpathia (41.7%) > Transylvania (40.4%) > Austria (38.8%) > Slovakia (30%) > Vojvodina (28.6%) >
Hungary (24.5%). This is presented as the chapter's clearest, most hypothesis-confirming result. pp.
246–249

### 9. Word order: bilingual regions did NOT uniformly diverge from the standard — a surprising reversal

Testing two word-order variables (adjectival preposed vs. postposed adverbs, and auxiliary+main-verb
ordering, both of which have a fixed neutral order in Serbian/Slovenian unlike Hungarian's freer
options), the chapter's hypothesis that contact would push bilingual regions toward the nonstandard
was **not confirmed for Vojvodina or Prekmurje**: both regions actually chose the *standard*
Hungarian word order **more** often than monolingual Hungary did (Vojvodina 7.1% nonstandard vs.
Hungary's 14%; Prekmurje close to Hungary at 13.2%). Only Subcarpathia (34.6%) and Austria (20.8%)
showed the expected contact-driven divergence. The authors propose **hypercorrection**
("overfulfilment of the norm," citing Lanstyák & Szabómihály 1997) as one candidate explanation:
minority Hungarians, whose Hungarian-ness is sometimes questioned by Hungary Hungarians for "not
speaking refined Hungarian," may consciously favor standard forms to assert national-linguistic
legitimacy — a pattern the chapter also invokes for the parallel redundant-pronoun reversal below. pp.
249–251

### 10. Feminized profession nouns — a genuine, consistent contact effect

Hungarian has no grammatical gender; feminine reference to a profession can use a generic noun
(*tanár* 'teacher') or a *nő*-compound (*tanárnő* 'female teacher'). Tested with four sentences
where context clearly established a female referent (639: *tanár*/*tanárnő*; *igazgató*/*igazgatónő*
'headmaster/headmistress'; *fodrász*/*fodrásznő* 'hairdresser'; *polgármester*/*polgármesternő*
'mayor'), bilingual regions favor the feminized (nonstandard) compound form significantly more than
monolingual Hungary (Prekmurje 35.9%, Vojvodina 31.9%, vs. Hungary 20.2%) — attributed to reinforcement
from the surrounding Indo-European languages' more systematic gender marking. The *tanár*/*tanárnő*
pair shows the strongest effect: even in Hungary the feminized form is chosen almost as often as the
generic one (41.3% vs. 58.7%) despite an unambiguously female referent. pp. 251–252

### 11. Diminutive suffixes — contact effect present for Slavic-contact regions, absent for German-contact Austria

Testing four sentences designed to elicit or reject Hungarian diminutive-suffixed forms (Hungarian
uses diminutives less pervasively than Slavic languages), Prekmurje (34.9%) and Vojvodina (34.8%)
both significantly exceed monolingual Hungary (23.6%) in diminutive-form acceptance. The chapter
notes a clean cross-linguistic control: in Austria, where German (unlike the Slavic languages) does
not favor diminutive suffixation either, results pattern with monolingual Hungary rather than with
the Slavic-contact regions — direct evidence that the effect tracks the contact language's own
typology rather than bilingualism/minority status in the abstract. pp. 252–253

### 12. Redundant object pronouns — another hypercorrection-pattern reversal

Hungarian normally omits an object pronoun unless it is emphasized; Serbian and Slovenian require an
overt grammatical object. The hypothesis (contact should increase redundant-pronoun use in Vojvodina/
Prekmurje) was again **not confirmed** — both regions accept redundant pronouns *less* than
monolingual Hungary (Vojvodina 25.5%, Prekmurje 27.8%, vs. Hungary 38.7%), the same
hypercorrection-shaped reversal as the word-order finding. The chapter candidly lists several
untested rival explanations besides hypercorrection: dialectal influence, 70 years of shared-state
linguistic convergence between Vojvodina and Prekmurje specifically, and a general
heightened-metalinguistic-awareness effect documented in bilinguals (citing Göncz & Kodžopeljić
1991). pp. 253–254

### 13. Comparatives: ablative for adessive, reinforced by (but not solely caused by) Serbian

Standard Hungarian uses the adessive case (*-nál/-nél*) for comparison (*Júlia magasabb Márknál*
'Julia is taller than Mark'); some regional dialects, Vojvodina and Prekmurje among them, instead use
the ablative (*-tól/-től*). In Vojvodina this is reinforced by Serbian *od* ('from'), whose core
meaning matches the Hungarian ablative. Vojvodina significantly favors the nonstandard ablative more
than Hungary (18% vs. 9.8%); Prekmurje does not differ significantly from Hungary, suggesting the
effect in Vojvodina is genuinely contact-reinforced rather than purely a shared regional-dialect
inheritance. pp. 255–256

### 14. Case suffixes with placenames — the chapter's clearest evidence that history reshapes cognition, not just language

Hungarian placenames take either "surface" cases (superessive/delative/sublative, historically for
Hungary-internal places) or "interior" cases (inessive/elative/illative, historically for foreign
places). Testing *Kosovo* (formerly inside Yugoslavia, now foreign relative to pre-Trianon Hungary)
and *Craiova* (Romania), the chapter finds Vojvodina and Prekmurje subjects strongly favor the
surface (nonforeign-treatment) case with *Kosovo* — 61% and 78.5% respectively, vs. only 19.6% in
Hungary — read as evidence that decades of shared statehood (both regions belonged to the second
Yugoslavia) reshaped these speakers' **mental map** of what counts as "home" territory, which is
then mirrored directly in case-suffix choice. The chapter explicitly frames this as evidence beyond
pure contact-linguistic borrowing: sociolinguistic/cognitive history, not just structural language
contact, can drive grammatical choice, citing Thomason & Kaufman (1988) that "it is the
sociolinguistic history of the speakers, and not the structure of their language, that is the primary
determinant of the linguistic outcome of language contact." pp. 255–257

### 15. Number concord — the one tested variable showing essentially no contact effect

Testing singular vs. plural agreement in contexts (paired body parts, generic group reference,
subject/object complements) where Indo-European languages more readily use plural concord than
Hungarian does, all three regions (Vojvodina 25.2%, Prekmurje 24.7%, Hungary 24.3%) show
statistically indistinguishable plural-preference rates — the chapter's explicit negative result,
included for completeness rather than confirmation bias. Noted separately: standard singular usage is
more consistent for nouns (75.18% average) than for subject/object-complement adverb agreement
(65.88%), attributed to noun number being a staple topic of language-cultivation pedagogy while
adverb agreement almost never is. pp. 257–259

### 16. Chapter's own synthesis: contact effects are real but partial, and regionally uneven

The chapter's summary explicitly qualifies its own findings rather than overclaiming: of the
"universal contact variables" tested, only analytic-form preference, feminization, and (likely)
diminutive use show a consistent contact effect across every bilingual region relative to monolingual
Hungary; word order and redundant-pronoun use show the *opposite* of the predicted direction in
Vojvodina and Prekmurje specifically (attributed tentatively to hypercorrection/regional dialect);
number concord shows essentially no effect. The authors stress that contact effects manifest as
**increased heterogeneity of variant use**, not wholesale replacement of the standard — no tested
variable showed complete loss of the standard form even in the most contact-affected region. Vojvodina
Hungarian is characterized as diverging from Hungary's Hungarian less than most other minority
regions studied (attributed to its comparatively large, self-sustaining community); Prekmurje
Hungarian shows stronger, more consistent divergence and the clearest signs of genuine language
attrition and looming language shift, attributed largely to its much smaller population size. pp.
259–262

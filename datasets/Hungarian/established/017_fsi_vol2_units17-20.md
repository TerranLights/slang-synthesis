# Hungarian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: FSI Basic Course Vol. 2, Units 17-20

**Source:** Augustus A. Koski & Ilona Mihalyfy (ed. Carleton T. Hodge), *Foreign Service Institute
Hungarian Basic Course, Units 13-24* (Volume 2, Foreign Service Institute / U.S. Department of
State, Washington D.C., 1964). Units 17-20, printed pages 367-453 (PDF pages 110-197 of
`FSI Hungarian Basic Course - Volume 2 - Student Text.pdf`, verified by direct inspection —
`printed page = PDF page + 257` for this range). Covers:

- **Unit 17** — *Betegség és Kórház* ("Illness and Hospital"), pp. 367-390: illness, symptoms,
  medical examination, hospitalization, socialized medicine narrative.
- **Unit 18** — *Fekete Kocsit Vett* ("Fekete Bought a Car"), pp. 391-410: buying a used car, auto
  mechanics/parts, traffic law and tickets.
- **Unit 19** — *Bírósági Tárgyaláson* ("At a Court Hearing"), pp. 411-432: a burglary, police
  investigation, criminal trial, sentencing, the Hungarian court/penal system.
- **Unit 20** — *Sportélet Magyarországon* ("Sports Life in Hungary"), pp. 433-453: barbershop
  small talk, soccer, boxing, swimming, the Olympics, organized sport in Hungary.

**Coverage note.** Every grammar point (each unit's full lettered "Notes on Grammar" section,
A through E-G) and every distinct vocabulary item introduced in the Basic Sentences and Narrative
sections is captured below. Repeated Substitution/Variation/Transformation/Translation/Response
Drill sentences are **not** transcribed verbatim — these drills recombine vocabulary already
captured from the same unit's Basic Sentences into grammar practice sentences and introduce very
few genuinely new lexical items. Two exceptions where a drill introduced new vocabulary/grammar not
present elsewhere in the unit are flagged individually below (Unit 17's compound-adjective drill
items `kékszemű` etc.; a handful of one-off drill words folded into the relevant vocabulary rows).
Each unit's **Közmondások** ("Proverbs") block is reproduced in full — see the Register/colloquial
findings note below on why this content is unusually high-value for this project.

**PDF extraction note.** This source has a genuine OCR text layer (`pdftotext`), not a vision-read
scan, so **Vision Reading Confidence is `n/a` throughout**. The OCR quality is inconsistent —
clean on most Basic Sentences pages, badly garbled on a few drill pages (heavy diacritic
corruption, dropped words) and on one image-only page of Hungarian road-sign icons (p. 409, PDF
page 152 — not text content, skipped entirely as non-extractable). Garbled passages were not used
as a source for vocabulary entries; where a word's reading was uncertain it was either cross-checked
against a clean occurrence elsewhere on the same page/unit or dropped rather than guessed.

**Morphological typology note.** Hungarian is agglutinative (see `../00_Extraction_Checklist.md`
and `../../00_Word_Concept_and_Morphological_Typology_Guide.md`). This chunk is drill-based
sentence material rather than a systematic grammar chapter, so most vocabulary items are simple
stems; morpheme breakdowns are given below for the handful of forms where the unit's own Notes on
Grammar specifically teach suffix composition (subjunctive-marker consonant assimilation, fraction
formation, the `-an/-en` numeral-as-substantive suffix, diminutives, the `-s` adjectival suffix, the
privative `-talan/-telen` family, reflexive-verb suffixes, and deverbal noun suffixes).

## Register/colloquial findings (summary — see inline flags below for detail)

This dispatch surfaced more colloquial-register material than the earlier noun/case-system
chapters, consistent with this project's specific interest in informal register, not just core
grammar:

- **All four units' Közmondások (proverb) blocks** are reproduced below — folk-idiomatic language
  is a distinct register from the textbook's otherwise neutral-to-formal dialogue Hungarian, and
  proverbs are exactly the kind of fixed, semantically opaque, culturally-loaded phrase this
  project's later mechanics-analysis phase will want as source material for slang/idiom formation.
- **`Jézus-Mária`** (Unit 17, p. 369) — an exclamation ("Goodness gracious!") flagged here as a
  genuine informal/colloquial interjection, distinct from the textbook's normal register.
- **Discourse particles/connectors** appearing in dialogue that carry a distinctly conversational,
  informal flavor not found in the expository Narrative sections: `hiszen` ("but, surely, why,
  well" — a hard-to-translate emphatic/contradicting particle), `pláne` ("particularly, especially”
  — a colloquial intensifier), `apropó` ("by the way, that reminds me" — a loanword-derived
  colloquialism from French *à propos*), `dehogy` ("oh no, by no means" — an emphatic negation),
  `hiába` ("in vain, for all that" — appears in Unit 18's narrative in a mildly idiomatic sense).
  None of these carry an explicit "colloquial" label from the source itself (unlike Ch. 5's
  `Kedves a Péter` example), so they're tagged `core` per the column-rules default, but flagged
  here in prose as conversational-register vocabulary worth a second look during mechanics
  analysis.
- **Diminutive suffixes** (Unit 19 Grammar Note E: `-ka/-ke/-cska/-cske`) are explicitly described
  by the source as expressing not just smallness but *affection* (`Péterke`, `kedveskem`,
  `hugocskám`) — this is a genuine morphological register mechanism (affective/informal
  morphology), distinct from ordinary agglutinative grammar, and worth flagging for the
  `morphological_play` mechanics-analysis category per
  `../../00_Word_Concept_and_Morphological_Typology_Guide.md`'s guidance that agglutinative
  languages need slang-specific morphological play distinguished from ordinary grammatical
  productivity — diminutive-as-affection is exactly that kind of borderline case.
- No explicit `[B]`/`[C]`/regional-dialect-style annotations occur in this range (this is a
  standard-Hungarian teaching course, not a comparative/dialectal source) — `Attested
  Region`/`Geographic Scope` are `—` throughout, per the column rules.

## Morpheme breakdown

> **hányan** = hány ("how many") + an (the `-an/-en` suffix marking a numeral/quantity word used as
> a substantive referring to *persons*, always paired with a plural verb) — "how many (people)."
> Source's own worked example; contrast the plain interrogative `hány` (e.g. `hány óra` "what
> time," lit. "how many hour") which doesn't take `-an/-en` and isn't restricted to persons. Unit
> 19 Grammar Note D, p. 411, 418.

> **zajtalanul** = zaj ("noise") + talan (privative suffix, "-less") + ul (adverbializer) —
> "noiselessly." Stacks the Unit 19 Grammar Note G privative suffix under the regular adverbial
> `-ul/-ül`, showing the two systems compose freely. p. 411.

> **javíthatatlan** = javít ("to fix, correct") + hat (potential/permissive suffix, "can be
> ...-ed") + atlan (privative suffix) — "incorrigible," literally "un-fixable" / "that cannot be
> corrected." The source's own Grammar Note G(5) worked example, illustrating a three-morpheme
> stack (verb root + modal suffix + privative suffix) that is fully compositional: strip `-atlan`
> and you get `javítható` "fixable," strip `-hat` too and you get the bare verb `javít`. Unit 19
> Grammar Note G(5), p. 415, 418.

> **fiúcska** = fiú ("boy") + cska (diminutive suffix) — "little boy." One of the source's own
> Grammar Note E examples of the `-ka/-ke/-cska/-cske` diminutive family, which the source
> explicitly says can carry either literal smallness or affection (see Register findings note
> above) — the two readings aren't distinguished morphologically, only by context. Unit 19 Grammar
> Note E, p. 412, 418.

> **egyharmad** = egy ("one") + harmad (the denominator form of "three," i.e. három ("three") minus
> its final vowel plus the denominator suffix -ad) — "one third." Fractions in Hungarian put the
> cardinal numerator first, unchanged, then a denominator formed by suffixing -ad/-od/-ed/-öd to
> the cardinal (with -ik dropped from the corresponding ordinal in all cases but "half," which uses
> the separate everyday word fél rather than the mathematical ketted). Unit 19 Grammar Note C, p.
> 415, 417.

> **kékszemű** = kék ("blue") + szem ("eye") + ű (adjectival suffix marking "having the quality/
> characteristic of the preceding noun, as modified") — "blue-eyed." A compounding pattern distinct
> from ordinary suffixal agglutination: two free stems (an adjective/color term and a body-part
> noun) fused under a single closing `-ú/-ű` suffix, parallel to `jószívű` "good-hearted,"
> `jókedvű` "good-humored," `rosszkedvű` "bad-humored," and `nagyszájú` "loud-mouthed" drawn from
> the same drill. Not itself explained in this unit's Notes on Grammar (which cover a different
> set of topics), but included because it's a productive word-formation pattern visible nowhere
> else in this chunk. Unit 17, p. 381.

> **lóerős** = ló ("horse") + erő ("power, force") + s (the `-s` "having the quality of" adjectival
> suffix from Unit 19 Grammar Note F) — "horsepowered," used attributively as in `58 lóerős motor`
> "a 58-horsepower engine." Shows the same `-s` suffix from Grammar Note F attaching to a compound
> stem (`lóerő` "horsepower," itself `ló` + `erő`) rather than a simple noun. Unit 18, p. 392.

> **borotválkozom** = borotvál ("to shave [someone/something]") + koz (reflexive suffix, type (1)
> from Unit 20 Grammar Note B) + om (1sg present indefinite personal ending) — "I shave (myself)."
> The source's own paradigm example; shows how the reflexive suffix sits between the verb stem and
> the ordinary personal-ending paradigm, turning a transitive verb into an intransitive `-ik`-type
> reflexive one. Unit 20 Grammar Note B, p. 439.

> **olvass-** (subjunctive stem of olvasni "to read") = olvas (stem, ending in -s) + j (subjunctive
> marker) → s + j assimilates to ss — e.g. **olvassak** "may I read" = olvass- + ak (1sg indefinite
> subjunctive ending). The source's own worked example of the systematic consonant-assimilation
> table governing how the subjunctive -j- surfaces after different stem-final consonants (s+j→ss,
> t+j→ss/ts depending on vowel length, sz+j→ssz, z+j→zz, dz+j→ddz, etc.) — a purely phonological
> layer on top of the ordinary agglutinative subjunctive-suffix-plus-personal-ending stack. Unit 17
> Grammar Note A, p. 373-375.

## Grammar points

### Unit 17

**A. Forms of the Subjunctive.** The Hungarian subjunctive (used for the imperative and a range of
other functions) is marked by `-j-`, which assimilates to the preceding stem-final consonant in a
systematic way: after `s`, `s+j→ss` (`olvas`→`olvass-`); after a short-vowel-plus-`t` stem, `t+j→ss`
(`köt`→`köss-`, `vezet`→`vezess-`, `lát`→`láss-`); after `tsz`, `tsz+j→tssz` (`játszik`→`játssz-`);
after a long-vowel-plus-`t` stem, `t+j→ts` (`tanít`→`taníts-`); after `sz`, `sz+j→ssz`
(`úszik`→`ussz-`); after `dz`, `dz+j→ddz`; after `z`, `z+j→zz`. A handful of verbs (`enni` "to
eat," `inni` "to drink," `venni` "to take/buy," `vinni` "to carry," `lenni` "to be," `hinni" "to
believe," `jönni` "to come," `menni` "to go") have fully irregular subjunctive stems, tabulated in
full by the source (`egyem`, `igyam`, `vegyek`, `vigyek`, `legyek`, `higgyek`, `jöjjek`, `menjek`).
p. 373-375.

**B. Uses of the Subjunctive.** Used in independent clauses mainly for the imperative, but also for
blessings/curses (`Isten áldja meg` "May God bless you") and first-person exhortations (`Együnk`
"Let's eat"); also expresses obligation/uncertainty, especially in questions (`Eljöjjön?` "Should he
come?"). In dependent clauses introduced by `hogy`, the subjunctive follows verbs of commanding,
wishing, forbidding, and impersonal expressions of possibility/importance/necessity, and in purpose
clauses ("in order that"). English infinitive constructions with a different logical subject than
the main clause correspond to a Hungarian `hogy`-subordinate clause with the subjunctive (`Azt
akarom, hogy menjen` "I want him to go" vs. `Menni akar` "He wants to go," same subject, plain
infinitive). p. 376.

**C. The Negative of the Subjunctive.** The negative before any subjunctive form is always `ne` or
`se`, never `nem` or `sem`. p. 376.

**D. Verb Prefix Position in the Imperative Use of the Subjunctive.** With unstressed commands, the
verb's main part precedes its prefix in both affirmative and negative (`Álljon fel!`, `Ne álljon
fel!`). When the verb/prefix is stressed (emphatic), the prefix precedes the main verb in the
affirmative (fusing into one written word — `Megállj!`), and in the negative the order is prefix +
`ne`/`se` + verb (`Meg ne állj!`). p. 376-377.

**E. Sequence of Tenses with the Subjunctive.** The Hungarian subjunctive exists only in a present
form, so there is no sequence-of-tenses problem — a subjunctive clause stays present-tense
regardless of the main clause's tense (`Azt mondja, hogy menjek el` "He tells me to go" / `Azt
mondta, hogy menjek el` "He told me to go" — identical subjunctive form both times). p. 377.

**F. Hungarian Constructions Corresponding to English 'Let'.** Three strategies: (1) the plain
subjunctive, restricted to first-person-plural ("let's..."); (2) the uninflected particle `hadd`
plus a subjunctive verb, restricted to first/third person ("let him/them/me..."); (3) the imperative
of `engedni` "to permit" plus an infinitive or `hogy`+subjunctive clause, used to emphasize the idea
of *permission* specifically. p. 377.

**G. Polite Requests with Tessék.** In polite-imperative constructions with `tessék`, the following
verb takes the **infinitive**, not the subjunctive (`Tessék helyet foglalni` "Please have a seat,"
`Tessék dolgozni` "Please work"). p. 377.

### Unit 18

**A. The Suffix -ért.** The causal suffix `-ért` corresponds to English "for" in the senses "in
exchange for," "for the purpose of," "for the sake of," "because of," "on behalf of"
(`ötven forintért` "for fifty forints," `a családjáért dolgozik` "he works for his family," `miért`
"why," lit. "for what"). Personal-pronoun forms are given as a full paradigm: `értem, érted, érte,
értünk, értetek, értük`. p. 396.

**B. The Interrogative Particle -e.** `-e` is suffixed (hyphenated in writing) to the predicate to
form a yes/no question embedded in a larger sentence (`Nem tudom, hogy igaz-e` "I don't know if it's
true"). `Vajon` commonly introduces an indirect question ("I wonder..." / "whether... "); when
`vajon` is present, `-e` is often dropped, and `-e` never co-occurs with a question-word-introduced
question. p. 396.

**C. Subordinate Clauses and Clause Introducers.** Most subordinate clauses in earlier units were
introduced by `hogy`. This unit tabulates a much larger inventory of clause-introducing words
formed directly from interrogatives by prefixing `a-`: relative pronouns (`aki`, `ami`, `amely`,
`amelyik`, `amilyen`, `amekkora`, `amennyi`, `ahány`) and relative adverbs (`ahova`, `ahol`,
`ahonnan`, `amerre`, `ameddig`, `amióta`, `amikor`, `ahogyan`/`ahogy`) — parallel in structure to
English "who/what/where..." vs. "wh-ever" pairs. A second set is built on postpositions:
`mielőtt` "before," `mialatt`/`miközben` "while," `miután` "after," `mióta` "since," `mihelyt` "as
soon as." Other subordinators: `bár`, `jóllehet` "although," `dacára` "notwithstanding," `hacsak`
"if only," `még akkor is` "even if." p. 397.

**D. Indefinite Forms.** A large family of compound "indefinite" words is built from the prefixes
`vala-` ("some-"), `akár-`/`bár-` ("any-, -ever"), and `minden-` ("every-") plus an interrogative
base: `valaki`/`akárki`/`bárki`/`mindenki` "someone/anyone/anybody/everyone,"
`valami`/`akármi`/`bármi` "something/anything," and parallel series for "which," "what kind,"
"how big," "how much," "how many," "where(to/from)," "when," "how." p. 397.

**E. Negatives.** A parallel negative family is built with `sem-` (`s-` before `k`) or `se-` plus an
interrogative base: `senki` "nobody," `semmi` "nothing," `sehol` "nowhere," `soha` "never," `sehogy`
"in no way," etc. — the negative counterparts of Note D's indefinite-form family. p. 398.

### Unit 19

**A. Verbs with Infinitive Stems in -ó, -ő.** A small class of verbs (`róni` "to notch/fine,"
`lőni` "to shoot") has two stems: one in `-v-` (`rov-`, `lőv-`) before a vowel-initial ending, and
one without (`ró-`, `lő-`) before a consonant-initial or zero ending. Full present-tense paradigms
given for both. p. 417.

**B. Stem Extension -hat, -het.** Added to a verb stem, `-hat/-het` marks general permissiveness or
possibility ("may be done"), with ordinary personal endings following it (`Megvehetem a kocsit` "I
may buy the car"). Narrower, related meanings are carried by separate words: `lehet` "(it is)
possible" (vs. impossible), `szabad` "(it is) permissible" (vs. forbidden), `tud` "to be able" (vs.
unable). p. 417.

**C. Fractions.** The numerator is the ordinary cardinal number; the denominator is formed by
adding `-ad/-od/-ed/-öd` to the cardinal (equivalently, dropping `-ik` from the ordinal, except for
1/2): `ketted, harmad, negyed, ötöd, hatod`. The mathematical term for 1/2 is `ketted`, but the
everyday word is `fél`. p. 417.

**D. The Endings -an, -en.** Numerals and other quantity words take `-an/-en` when used as
substantives referring specifically to *persons*; the accompanying verb is always plural
(`Hányan voltak ott?` "How many were there?", `Hárman voltunk a szobában` "There were three of us in
the room"). See Morpheme Breakdown above. p. 418.

**E. Diminutive Suffixes.** The most common diminutive suffixes are `-ka/-ke` and `-cska/-cske`
(`madárka`, `városka`, `székerke`, `levélke`, `cipőcske`, `ágyacska`, `kezecske`, `házacska`),
addable to nouns or adjectives (`kicsike`, `nagyocska`, `fiatalka`, `vékonyka`). Beyond marking
smallness, these suffixes are frequently used to express **affection** (`Máriácska`, `Évácska`,
`Péterke`, `Jánoska`, `kedveském`, `édesém`, `húgocskám`, `galambocskám`) — see the Register/
colloquial findings note at the top of this file. p. 418.

**F. The Suffix -s (-as, -es, -ös).** Added to nouns/adjectives, `-s` (with vowel-harmony variants
`-as`, `-es`, `-ös`) means "having the quality of/possessing X": `pár` "pair" → `páros üveg`
"paired-up bottle"; `sör` "beer" → `söröskíveg`; `víz` "water" → `vizes-pohár` "water glass";
`kicsiny` "small" → `kicsinyes ember` "petty man"; `beteg` "sick" → `beteges asszony` "sickly
woman"; `tavaszi` "spring (adj.)" → `tavaszias idő` "spring-like weather." Added to numerals, `-s`
produces forms like `egyes számú szoba` "room number one," `kettes villamos` "the number-two
streetcar," `a húszas években` "in the twenties," `a négy ötvenes vonattal` "on the 4:50 train,"
`egy százas` "a hundred(-forint) note," `kettesben` "two-by-two." p. 418.

**G. The Adjectival Ending -talan/-telen/-tlan/-tlen/-atlan/-etlen ("lack of," "-less").** (1) On a
consonant-final noun: `-talan/-telen` or `-atlan/-etlen`, with no fully reliable rule for which
variant a given noun takes, though a noun that needs the `a`/`e` linking vowel before plural `-k`
(cf. `002_chapter5_nouns.md` §5.3.2.1.1) often also takes `-atlan/-etlen` (`hús`→`hústalan`,
`ember`→`embertelen`, `pénz`→`pénztelen`). (2) On a vowel-final noun: `-tlan/-tlen`, with the
stem's final vowel always lengthened (`nő`→`nőtlen`, `fa`→`fátlan`, `apa`→`apátlan`). (3) On a verb:
`-atlan/-etlen` added to the third-person-singular form (`ír`→`íratlan`, `vár`→`váratlan`,
`tanul`→`tanulatlan`). (4) On an existing adjective (`elég`→`elégtelen`, `tiszta`→`tisztátlan`,
`boldog`→`boldogtalan`). (5) Stacked with `-hat/-het`: `lát` "sees" → `láthat` "can see" →
`láthatatlan` "cannot be seen, invisible" — see Morpheme Breakdown above for `javíthatatlan`. p.
418-419.

### Unit 20

**A. Mag(a)- "-self, -selves".** Full paradigm: `magam/magad/maga` (sg.), `magunk/magatok/maguk`
(pl.) — used in apposition ("I myself..."), as the object of verbs (`Védi magát` "He's defending
himself"), and with postpositions (`Magam előtt láttam Jánost` "I saw John in front of me"). p. 439.

**B. Reflexive Verbs.** Beyond `mag(a)-` used as an object pronoun, Hungarian has a distinct class
of morphologically reflexive `-ik`-type verbs, marked by one of several suffix families whose
choice for a given verb is not predictable and must be memorized: (1) `-koz-/-kez-/-köz-`
(`törülközik`, `mutatkozik`, `barátkozik`, `borotválkozik`, `védekezik`, `várakozik`, `sorakozik`);
(2) `-kod-/-ked-/-köd-` (`fésülködik`, `öltözködik`, `emelkedik`, `mosakodik`, `kötekedik`,
`verekedik`); (3) `-ód-/-őd-` (`csukódik`, `kötődik`); (4) `-óz-/-őz-` (`nyújtózik`,
`kergetőzik`); (5) `-ódz-/-ődz-` (`takarózik`, `fogódzik`). Full personal-ending paradigm given for
`borotválkozik`, `öltözködik`, `takarózik`. p. 439-440.

**C. The Participial Form Ending in -ó, -ő.** The present-participle suffix `-ó/-ő` marks "one doing
X" and can modify a noun (`dolgozó ember` "working man") or stand alone as a noun (`dolgozó`
"worker"). p. 440.

**D. The Participial Form Ending in -t, -tt.** The past-participle suffix `-t/-tt` (identical in
form to the 3sg indefinite past tense) corresponds to an English past participle used adjectivally:
`foglalt` "occupied/reserved," `nyitott` "opened," `sült krumpli` "baked/fried potatoes," `a csukott
ajtó` "the closed door." p. 440.

**E. The Participial Form Ending in -va, -ve.** Often paired with some form of `lenni` "to be"
(never omitted) to form a predicate corresponding to an English passive/stative past participle
("The door is closed," "the books are open," "the store remained closed"), or used adverbially to
correspond to "while doing X" / an "-ing" clause / occasionally an English "-ly" adverb: `Vonatot
várva levelet ír` "He writes a letter while waiting for the train"; `Hova megy ilyen sietve?`
"Where are you going so hurriedly?" p. 440-441.

**F. Formation of Nouns from Verbs.** The source tabulates the main deverbal-noun suffix families:
`-ás/-és` (`mosás`, `aratás`, `kérés`, `kezdés`, `hallgatás`); `-alom/-elem` (`győzelem`, `értelem`,
`tartalom`, `élelem`); `-dalom/-delem` (`forradalom`, `birodalom`, `irodalom`, `jövedelem`);
`-ság/-ség` (`költség`, `segítség`, `vereség`, `veszteség`, `fáradtság`); `-at/-et` (`ajánlat`,
`dolgozat`, `élet`, `üdvözlet`, `öltözet`, `gondolat`); `-mány/-mény` (`engedmény`, `találmány`,
`gyűjtemény`, `küldemény`, `tudomány`, `költemény`); `-vány/-vény` (`látvány`, `járvány`, `állvány`,
`bizonyítvány`); `-ék` (`termék`, `festék`, `készülék`, `maradék`, `bizonyíték`). p. 441.

**G. Szokott.** The past tense of `szokni` plus an infinitive marks habitual/frequent action framed
relative to the present: `Színházba szoktam menni` "I usually go to the theater." p. 441.

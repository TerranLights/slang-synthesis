# Hungarian — Established Sociolinguistics: Hungarian Language Contact Outside Hungary, Part 1
# (Introduction + Ch. 1–6, and Ch. 7 sociolinguistic background)

**Source:** Anna Fenyvesi (ed.), *Hungarian Language Contact Outside Hungary: Studies on Hungarian
as a Minority Language* (Impact: Studies in Language and Society, vol. 20; John Benjamins, 2005).
PDF pages 1–225 of 448 (front matter + Introduction + Chapters 1–6, plus the sociolinguistic-background
portion of Chapter 7 up through Section 2.1.5). Printed-page offset verified directly against the
book's own footer numbers (not assumed from `pdfinfo`): PDF page = printed page + 23 (e.g. PDF p. 34 =
printed p. 11, the start of Chapter 1; PDF p. 210 = printed p. 187, the start of Chapter 7). This chunk
therefore covers printed pages 1–202 of the book.

**Chapters/regions actually covered in this file:**
- Introduction (Fenyvesi) — overview of the volume and of minority-Hungarian demographics worldwide.
- Ch. 1 (Thomason) — typological comparison of Hungarian vs. the Indo-European contact languages.
- Ch. 2 (Kontra) — the Sociolinguistics of Hungarian Outside Hungary (SHOH) project's design and
  framing concepts (cultural vs. political nation, linguicism, language lapses/gaps).
- Ch. 3 (Lanstyák & Szabómihály) — **Hungarian in Slovakia**, full chapter (sociolinguistic +
  linguistic aspects, extensive borrowing typology).
- Ch. 4 (Csernicskó) — **Hungarian in Subcarpathia, Ukraine**, full chapter.
- Ch. 5 (Benő & Szilágyi N.) — **Hungarian in Romania (Transylvania)**, full chapter.
- Ch. 6 (Sándor) — **The Csángós of Romania (Moldavia)**, full chapter, including the
  Hungarian-Csángó vs. Szekler-Csángó dialect-feature table.
- Ch. 7 (Göncz & Vörös) — **Hungarian in Vojvodina and Prekmurje**, *partial*: Section 2
  (sociolinguistic aspects: history, demographics, language policy, attitudes) only. The chapter's
  Section 3 ("Linguistic aspects" — the borrowing/vocabulary analysis analogous to Ch. 3–5) falls
  after PDF page 225 and is left to the sibling chunk covering the book's second half.

**Source type and confidence.** This is an academic sociolinguistics volume (edited collection),
`grammar_reference`-adjacent but more precisely a `sociolinguistic_study`; treated here as
`grammar_reference` for Source Type consistency with this project's established/ convention, since
the taxonomy in `00_Source_Reliability_Guide.md` does not have a closer-fitting non-corpus academic
category. All PDF pages in this range have a clean, genuine text layer (`pdftotext -layout`); no
vision-reading was needed and no marginalia risk applies. Transcription Confidence and Vision Reading
Confidence are `n/a` throughout for this reason.

**Coverage-rule note — comparative-sociolinguistics prioritization.** Per the dispatch instructions,
this book is directly analogous to the Serbian/Croatian/Bosnian folder's Alexander-style companion
volume: its central value for this project is the book's own commentary on **regional-variant
vocabulary and dialect/register contrasts**, not a flat vocabulary sample. Accordingly this file
prioritizes (a) every explicit minority-Hungarian-form vs. standard-Hungary-form pair the book itself
gives (marked `HS`/`SuH`/`RH` vs. `HH` in the source's own notation), and (b) the book's grammar-point
prose on contact-linguistic mechanisms (borrowing typology, code-switching, diglossia, dialect
classification). Purely demographic/statistical content (census tables, percentages of mixed
marriages, school-enrollment figures) is paraphrased minimally or skipped — it carries no linguistic
signal for the downstream slang-synthesis mechanics phase.

**Abbreviations used by the source (retained below):** HH = Hungarian used in Hungary (the standard
reference variety); HS = Hungarian used in Slovakia; SuH = Hungarian used in Subcarpathia, Ukraine;
RH = Hungarian used in Romania (Transylvania); SS = Standard Slovak; R = Romanian.

## Grammar points

### 1. The volume's framing concepts (Introduction, Ch. 1–2)

**Cultural nation vs. political nation (Kontra, Ch. 2).** Hungarians define national identity by
shared language/culture (cultural nation) rather than citizenship (political nation), which is why
this book systematically uses phrases like "Hungarians in Slovakia" rather than "Hungarian Slovaks" —
a terminological choice the source argues is not cosmetic but reflects a genuinely different concept
of what "being Hungarian" means to the population under study. p. 30-31

**The borrowing/shift-induced-interference distinction (Thomason, Ch. 1, applied throughout).**
Following Thomason & Kaufman (1988), *borrowing* is material a fluent bilingual imports from L2 into
L1 (predicts: lexicon first, then structure only under intense contact); *shift-induced interference*
is material imperfect L2-learners carry over while shifting away from L1 (predicts: phonology/syntax
first, lexicon lagging). Every minority-Hungarian-outside-Hungary situation in this book is classified
as **borrowing**, not shift-induced interference, because it is Hungarian speakers borrowing from a
dominant majority language, not the reverse. This is the theoretical backbone the whole volume applies
per-country. p. 12-16, 30

**Typological profile of Hungarian vs. its Indo-European contact languages (Thomason, Ch. 1).**
Hungarian is agglutinative (vs. flexional IE), has vowel harmony (absent in IE except marginal
Germanic umlaut), 17-27 cases (vs. ≤7 in IE), possessive person-suffixes on nouns with no IE analogue,
no grammatical gender (vs. IE gender systems), a definite/indefinite object-agreement distinction on
verbs (absent in IE), underlying SOV word order (vs. surface SVO in IE), and postpositions only (vs.
IE prepositions). This typological gap is the source's baseline for *predicting* which contact-induced
changes are typologically "cheap" (adopting IE-style overt object pronouns, IE-style plurals after
quantifiers, analytic replacements for synthetic derivational suffixes) versus which require unusually
intense contact. p. 18-25

**Language lapses vs. language gaps (Kontra/Lanstyák, Ch. 2, developed further in Ch. 3).** A
*lapse* is temporary inability to recall a word/structure the speaker otherwise knows; a *gap* is a
genuine absence of that item from the speaker's linguistic system. Distinguished because gaps (not
lapses) are the diagnostic symptom of **register attrition** — e.g. Hungarians in Romania who cannot
write a formal letter in Hungarian because they never get the chance to practice that register. p. 42-43

**Overfulfilment of the norm (Lanstyák & Szabómihály, Ch. 3, the volume's most load-bearing
sociolinguistic mechanism).** In bilingual minority communities, speakers can observe the *prestige
norm* of the standard language **more rigorously than monolinguals do**, as a form of symbolic
identification with the nation — the mirror image of naive "bilinguals just borrow more" expectations.
Demonstrated experimentally: Slovakia-Hungarian students attending Hungarian-medium schools used the
prestige -m verb ending (alszom vs. alszok) *more* often than monolingual control students in Hungary
itself. This mechanism is invoked repeatedly across chapters to explain why some contact-induced
variants show *no* significant difference, or even a reversed difference, between minority and
majority samples — a genuine methodological caution against assuming "more contact = more nonstandard
forms" as a simple monotonic relationship. p. 47-49

### 2. Borrowing typology (Ch. 3, the most systematically laid out; reused implicitly in Ch. 4-5)

Lanstyák & Szabómihály give the clearest formal typology in the book, directly reusable for
mechanics-analysis of any future contact-linguistics chapter:

- **Loanwords proper** — direct import with minimal/no morphemic substitution (HS horcsica ← SS
  horčica).
- **Loanforms** — recipient-language word whose *phonemic shape* is remodeled on the source-language
  analog even though a native word for the concept already exists (HS prax vs. HH praxis, both
  ultimately from the same Latin root but diverging under different contact pressures).
- **Calques** — new recipient-language words/phrases built morpheme-by-morpheme (or
  semantically-motivated) on a source-language model, using only recipient-language morphemes (HS
  kockában mondja el ← SS povedat' v kocke).
- **Loanblends** — a special subtype of calque containing at least one *imported* morpheme alongside
  native ones (HS víberliszt, HS kipratál).
- **Semantic loans (extensions)** — an existing recipient-language word acquires an additional meaning
  under source-language influence, subclassified as: **interlingual homophones** (same/similar sound,
  unrelated meaning — HS fal 'wall' vs. its unrelated second sense), **interlingual homologs**
  (different sound, overlapping meaning — HS ül acquiring SS sediet''s 'be OK' sense), and
  **interlingual analogs** (similar sound *and* overlapping meaning — HS szemafor). **Stylistic loans**
  are a further subtype where only the register/connotation of an already-shared word shifts (HS
  szvetter staying colloquial-neutral while HH szvetter becomes obsolete).
- **Convergence** — not a discrete borrowed item at all, but a *frequency* effect: bilinguals use the
  same forms monolinguals use, just measurably more or less often (e.g. the higher rate of diminutive
  suffixes, feminized occupation nouns, or overt object pronouns in every minority variety studied).
  The source stresses that most morphological and syntactic contact effects in these chapters are
  convergence, not borrowing proper — a genuinely different kind of contact phenomenon than lexical
  substitution. p. 62-73

**Two historical layers of contact features in Slovakia specifically.** Lanstyák & Szabómihály
distinguish an older, regional, closed-set layer inherited from centuries of pre-1918
Hungarian-Slovak-German cohabitation (dialect words, receding/archaic, used by elderly monolingual
dialect speakers) from a newer, post-1918, open and still-productive layer created by artificial,
officially-organized minority bilingualism (government, schools, media). Only the newer layer is
analyzed in the chapter's Section 3.2 — the older layer is explicitly out of scope and pointed to a
separate source (Lanstyák 1994a). This distinction is a genuinely reusable methodological point for
any future minority-language contact-linguistics extraction: "contact features" are not a single
historical stratum. p. 61

### 3. Structural/statistical contact effects tested experimentally across Ch. 3-5 (via the shared
    SHOH questionnaire)

The same handful of variables were tested with parallel sentence-pairs across Slovakia, Ukraine,
Romania, and the (partial) Vojvodina/Prekmurje chapter, using a shared SHOH questionnaire — a genuinely
comparative design worth noting as a source-level fact, not a per-chapter one:

- **Analytic vs. synthetic constructions** — bilinguals more often prefer a multi-word phrase (Contact
  Hungarian tagsági díj) over the single-compound Standard Hungarian form (tagdíj) for the same
  concept, attributed to the Indo-European contact languages' analytic typology.
- **Singular vs. plural after quantifiers/paired body parts** — Standard Hungarian keeps such nouns
  singular (fáj a lábam, banánt szed); IE contact languages require plural, and minority Hungarian
  varieties show measurably higher plural rates (e.g. fájnak a lábaim, banánokat).
  This is explicitly one of the language's own typological markers under structural threat (see Ch. 1
  above) and the single most consistently-replicated finding across the three full chapters.
- **Feminization** — the productive but optional Hungarian -nő suffix (fodrásznő 'female hairdresser')
  is used at measurably higher rates by bilinguals in contact with obligatorily-gendered Slavic/Romance
  languages.
- **Redundant overt object pronouns** — Hungarian's definite conjugation already marks a definite
  object, making an overt pronoun (téged, őt) grammatically redundant; bilinguals in contact with
  languages that require an overt object pronoun use the redundant form significantly more often.
- **Diminutive suffix overuse** — parallel to feminization, a frequency effect rather than a discrete
  borrowed form.

Each chapter reports these with different significance levels and directionality nuances per contact
language, but the shared design (and the fact that the *same* six or so variables recur) is itself the
reusable structural finding: these are the specific grammatical "pressure points" where Hungarian's
synthetic/agglutinative typology comes under measurable, quantifiable pressure from analytic IE contact
languages, replicated independently in three unrelated contact situations (Slovak, Ukrainian/Russian,
Romanian). p. 70-75 (Ch. 3), 129-134 (Ch. 4), 155-161 (Ch. 5)

### 4. Pragmatic/register borrowing (Ch. 3, Slovakia)

Address-term systems are shown to be an independently borrowable register, distinct from lexicon or
grammar: HS speakers occupy "an intermediate position between HH and SS speakers" in T/V address
(tegezés) usage, in greeting formulas (csókolom retained more strongly by HS children than by HS
adults, versus Slovak children's time-of-day-based greetings), and in the borrowed woman's-address
term panyika/panyikám filling a genuine functional gap in Hungarian address-term morphology (Hungarian
lacks an equivalent to Frau/Pani/Mrs. that isn't awkwardly indirect). This is presented as evidence
that Hungarian's own "clumsy, unsystematic" formal address system creates a structural precondition
favoring borrowing regardless of contact intensity. p. 75-76

### 5. Csángó dialects: sociolinguistic status and the Hungarian-Csángó/Szekler-Csángó split (Ch. 6)

**Two historically distinct settlement waves.** The Csángós of Moldavia descend from two separate
migrations from the Hungarian-speaking Carpathian Basin: a 14th-15th century wave from Mezőség
(northern Transylvania), whose descendants' dialects are called **Hungarian-Csángó** (further split
geographically into Northern, around Roman/Románvásár, and Southern, around Bacău/Bákó); and a second,
predominantly post-1764 wave of ethnic **Szeklers** fleeing forced integration into the Habsburg
military system, whose descendants' dialects are called **Szekler-Csángó**. These two dialect groups
show systematic phonological, morphosyntactic, and lexical differences (Table 6.1, reproduced in part
in the Vocabulary section above) but do *not* form clean isogloss bundles — individual features can
cross the group boundary independently, and in mixed-settlement areas (e.g. Cleja/Klézse) the dialects
have interpenetrated. Because Szekler-Csángó is the historically more prestigious variety, some
Hungarian-Csángó features — notably the merger of /s/ and /š/ into a single palatalized fricative
(popularly stigmatized as "lisping") — are actively stigmatized even within the Csángó-speaking
population itself, with documented cases of hypercorrective avoidance. pp. 173-175

**Csángó as a "roofless" Ausbau-language, not a dialect of standard Hungarian.** The source's central
theoretical claim: Csángó dialects were never *heteronomous* with respect to any Hungarian standard
(there has essentially been zero Hungarian-language literacy or standardization contact in Moldavia
for centuries), and standard-Hungarian speakers today find most Csángó dialects "hardly or not at all"
mutually intelligible. Despite indisputable historical origin as Hungarian dialects, the source argues
they sociolinguistically constitute a *roofless, diffuse Ausbau-language* (following Trudgill's and Le
Page & Tabouret-Keller's terminology) rather than "one dialect of Hungarian" — explicitly disputing the
more traditional dialectological position (represented by Szilágyi N., cited in Ch. 2) that Csángó is
simply a Hungarian dialect because its speakers self-identify their speech as related to Hungarian.
This is presented as a live, unresolved scholarly disagreement, not settled fact. pp. 176-177

**Language shift is unusually far advanced and the mechanism is atypical.** Only ~20% of the estimated
240,000 ethnic Csángós (Roman Catholics of Moldavia) still speak any Csángó dialect at all (~50,500
speakers total: ~9,600 Northern, ~6,700 Southern, ~34,000 Szekler-area, per Tánczos 1999 estimates cited
in the source). Unusually for language-shift situations, **religion (the church domain) was the
*first* domain lost to the majority language, not the last** — because Hungarian-speaking clergy were
displaced from Moldavia as early as 1622 by the Vatican's De Propaganda Fide missionary reorganization,
centuries before urbanization or state-driven Romanianization pressure. This inverts the typical
domain-loss ordering assumed in language-shift theory (where the church/religious domain is usually
the most conservative, last-to-shift domain) and is presented as the chapter's most theoretically
notable finding. pp. 168-172

**Csángó ethnic identity is structurally different from a national identity.** Because the Csángós
never participated in 19th-century Hungarian nation-formation, their group identity lacks the standard
components of national identity (shared canonical history/literature/symbols) and instead organizes
around religion (Roman Catholicism, as the sole historical marker distinguishing them from Orthodox
Moldavian Romanians) and loyalty to the home village, not language. The ethnonym csángó/ceangău itself
has, through centuries of religious framing, drifted to mean simply "Moldavian Catholic" regardless of
mother tongue — so today most people called Csángó are Romanian monolinguals. The name carries a felt
pejorative connotation ("deficiency of speech") that the Csángós themselves are reported to feel shame
about. pp. 165-172

### 6. Diglossia/polyglossia model applied to Vojvodina Hungarian (Ch. 7, sociolinguistic portion only)

Göncz & Vörös apply *both* classic Fergusonian diglossia (H and L are register-varieties of the *same*
language: Standard Hungarian as H, the Vojvodina Hungarian contact vernacular as L, acquired
respectively at school vs. at home) and Fishman's broadened diglossia (H and L can be *different
languages*: Standard Hungarian as H, Serbian encroaching as a rising second H-like code) as
simultaneously operative descriptions of the same speech community — arguing that neither model alone
is adequate, and that the region shows genuine *poliglossia* with H/L status shifting geographically
(east/west/south of northeastern Bačka show Serbian increasingly displacing even the H-register role of
Standard Hungarian). This composite-model move is presented as a methodological point applicable
beyond this one chapter. pp. 199-200

Bilingualism in Vojvodina is further characterized as predominantly **folk bilingualism** (imposed by
necessity for survival, not chosen) and **one-sided bilingualism** (only the Hungarian minority is
bilingual; the Serbian-speaking majority remains largely monolingual) — two independent axes of
bilingualism-typology (chosen/imposed; symmetric/asymmetric) that the source treats as more analytically
useful than a single "degree of bilingualism" scale. pp. 200-201

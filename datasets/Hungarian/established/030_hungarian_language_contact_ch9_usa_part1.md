9, "Hungarian in the United States" by Anna Fenyvesi. PDF pages 288–326 (sociolinguistic overview

**Coverage note.** This chapter draws on four independent sociolinguistic-interview studies of
distinct Hungarian-American communities (Kontra 1990, South Bend, IN; Bartha 1993, Detroit, MI;
Fenyvesi 1995a, McKeesport, PA; Polgár 2001, Toledo, OH) plus a dedicated dictionary of American
Hungarian (Vázsonyi 1995). Per the coverage rule, this chunk prioritizes the chapter's structural
contact-linguistics findings (phonology, morphology, syntax) — these are the chapter's own analytical
contribution and are captured close to exhaustively for the sentence-level minimal-pair examples the
source itself supplies. Heavy demographic/census statistical material (immigration waves, state-by-
state population tables, income/education comparison tables) is paraphrased/summarized rather than
transcribed in full, per copyright discipline — this is quantitative background, not linguistic
content. Source has a clean text layer (`pdftotext -layout`); no vision-reading was needed and no
marginalia risk applies.

**Register/dialect annotation (explicit in source — must-capture per coverage rule).** The chapter
systematically distinguishes **AH** (American Hungarian, the contact variety) from **HH** (Hungarian
as spoken in Hungary), and further distinguishes speakers by generation (**Gen1** = immigrant/
first-generation, **Gen2** = US-born second-generation) and by community (SB = South Bend, Dt =
Detroit, McK = McKeesport). This chapter also introduces a genuinely novel methodological distinction
not seen in the sibling chapters: the source explicitly separates **borrowing** (contact effects
attributable to American English) from **language attrition** (simplification/reduction with no
American-English source), and further from cases of **"multiple causation"** where both processes
converge on the same surface change — a three-way causal taxonomy worth preserving as a concept.

**Morphological typology note.** Hungarian is agglutinative (see `../00_Extraction_Checklist.md`).
This chapter is exceptionally rich in full interlinear-glossed AH/HH minimal-pair sentences
illustrating real-time morphosyntactic change (conjugation mixing, case loss, agreement loss,
possessive-suffix loss) — a large number of these are preserved with full morpheme glosses below,
since the chapter's evidentiary force depends on the specific morpheme-level contrast, not just the
surface translation.

## Grammar points

*(Retitled "Sociolinguistic and structural points" for this chunk, matching the convention used in
the sibling chapter files, since much of the content is contact-linguistics research findings.)*

### 1. Immigration history: four distinct waves with different social profiles

The chapter distinguishes four historically and socioeconomically distinct waves of Hungarian
immigration to the US: (1) the pre-WWI mass wave (1880s–1924), overwhelmingly peasant/working-class
"sojourners" who intended to return to Hungary, cut short by the 1924 Quota System; (2) a small,
socially different interwar wave (~15,000 through WWII) of middle-class professionals; (3) the
post-WWII "D.P." wave (1948–52, ~16,000), educated professionals and upper-class refugees from the
communist takeover; and (4) the post-1956 "56-ers"/"freedom-fighters" wave (~42,000 of the ~200,000
who left Hungary), mostly young urban males, with working-class members settling in traditional
Hungarian-American neighborhoods and professionals/students dispersing nationally. Post-1956
immigration was minimal until the 1970s–80s due to Hungary's own travel restrictions, and effectively
ended as a distinct political-refugee category after the 1989 collapse of communism. pp. 288–291

### 2. Census-figure interpretation problems — a recurring methodological caution

The chapter explicitly warns that pre-1920 "born in Hungary" census figures include non-Hungarian-
speaking minorities of pre-Trianon Greater Hungary (the 1910 census recorded only 54% of Hungary's
population as ethnically Hungarian), while post-1920 figures miss Hungarian-speakers who emigrated
from successor states with large Hungarian minorities (e.g. Romania). US censuses lacked any language
or ethnic-ancestry question until 1980, and the "Hungarian ancestry" vs. "speaks Hungarian at home"
figures diverge sharply: of 904,662 people of first Hungarian ancestry in 2000, only 13.04% (117,973)
reported using Hungarian at home — and even this likely undercounts true Hungarian speakers, since
some (e.g. those married to non-Hungarian-speakers) may use Hungarian outside the home without it
registering as their "home language." In 1990, foreign-born Hungarian-Americans used Hungarian at
home at a 70% rate vs. only ~7% of US-born (second/third-generation) Hungarian-Americans — presented
as the chapter's clearest single statistic for the shift trajectory. pp. 288–293

### 3. Hungarian-Americans are demographically and economically well-assimilated

By 2000, Hungarian-Americans were the 21st-largest ancestry group in the US (3rd-largest of Eastern
European origin, after Polish and Russian) but geographically concentrated in the same states as the
original early-20th-century settlement pattern (Ohio, New York, Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Illinois,
Michigan — also the states losing the most Hungarian-Americans since 1980, attributed to the aging
and dying-off of the oldest cohort). By most integration metrics — occupation (slightly more
managerial/professional than the US average), income (median household income $35,200 vs. US
average $30,056 in 1990), and education (80.8% high-school-plus vs. 75.2% US average) —
Hungarian-Americans are fully mainstream-integrated; English proficiency among the minority who do
speak Hungarian at home is high (65% "speak English very well," ranking exactly at the median of 11
compared Eastern European groups, with Croatian-speakers showing the closest English-proficiency
profile — attributed to the two groups' shared socioeconomic position within the former
Austro-Hungarian Empire). pp. 293–297

### 4. Domain-of-use pattern: only home/friendship/religion retain any Hungarian

Applying Greenfield's (1968)/Fishman's (1972b) five-domain framework (home, friendship, religion,
education, employment), the chapter finds — consistently across all four studied communities — that
only the first three domains retain any Hungarian use today; education and employment, which did
involve some Hungarian in the communities' earlier decades (Hungarian-speaking co-workers at steel
mills; a Hungarian-run school in Detroit 1907–1970; catechism-focused summer schools), are now
essentially English-only domains. Hungarian is used almost exclusively with parents/grandparents,
sometimes spouses (depending on whether the marriage is to another second-generation American or to
an immigrant), and rarely with children — the classic pattern of arrested intergenerational
transmission. Religious-domain Hungarian is patchy and declining (e.g. by the time of the Toledo
study, the Hungarian Catholic church there had no resident Hungarian-speaking pastor, only a
biweekly visitor). pp. 297–299

### 5. Language shift completes within two generations — faster than some comparable immigrant groups

The chapter's central sociolinguistic finding: unlike some other European-immigrant groups in the US
(Greek-Americans, Finnish-Americans) where third-generation heritage-language speakers are
documented, **no study to date has found Hungarian speakers beyond the second generation** among
Hungarian-Americans — a faster shift trajectory than the classic "three-generation" immigrant-language
model. Citing Mesthrie's (1994) four-factor framework (demographic, economic, institutional,
language-status factors), the chapter argues Hungarian-Americans were demographically disadvantaged
from the start (never more than ~4% of European immigrants even at peak immigration decades, and
settled alongside — not isolated from — other Eastern European immigrant groups), economically
vulnerable to post-WWII deindustrialization (closure of the coal mines, steel mills, and factories —
e.g. the Studebaker plant employing many South Bend Hungarians — that had anchored Hungarian-American
communities), and institutionally supported mainly through churches rather than schools; a 1966
institutional census (229 churches, 43 radio stations, 43 periodicals, 459 organizations) had
shrunk drastically by the 1980s–90s. The 1968 Bilingual Education Act, the chapter notes pointedly,
"came too late for Hungarian-Americans" — by the time federal support for bilingual education
existed, too few concentrated communities of limited-English-proficiency Hungarian children remained
for it to matter. pp. 299–301

### 6. Definite/indefinite conjugation mixing — with a striking morphological asymmetry

Hungarian verbs obligatorily mark definiteness/indefiniteness of the object (a portmanteau with
person/number, e.g. *csinálunk* 'we do' [INDEF] vs. *csináljuk* 'we do it' [DEF]). AH shows frequent
mixing of the two conjugations in both directions (definite used where HH requires indefinite, and
vice versa), more common in second-generation speech, with certain object types (1st-person pronouns,
infinitival clauses, definite-article-marked objects, demonstrative-pronoun objects, sentential
objects) especially prone to triggering the breakdown. The chapter's most striking specific finding:
**even when the conjugation choice is wrong, the person/number suffix is always the one appropriate
to the (wrongly) chosen conjugation** — no examples were found of a speaker combining wrong
conjugation *and* wrong person/number marking. This suggests person/number marking and
definiteness marking are represented as separable components in the AH grammar, with only the latter
undergoing change — and the chapter notes this same asymmetry (conjugation instability without
person/number breakdown) has been independently documented in other heavily-inflectional immigrant
languages in contact with English (American Greek, Polish, Czech, Slovak, Serbo-Croatian, Russian —
though these show the effect in noun morphology rather than verb agreement) and in British Hungarian
(A. Benkő 2000). pp. 311–313

### 7. Preverb behavior: five distinct patterns of divergence

Hungarian preverbs (functioning partly like English prepositions/particles, partly with pure
aspectual meaning, and — unlike English particles — able to appear before, after, or separated from
their verb) show five distinct divergence patterns in AH, drawn almost entirely from the McKeesport
data: (i) a purely aspectual preverb is simply dropped (*kezdett sírni* 'started to cry' for HH
*elkezdett*); (ii) a whole preverb-verb construction is replaced by a semantically related but
distinct one (*be-megy*/*ki-jön* 'go in'/'come out' substituting for *el-megy*/*el-jön* 'go away');
(iii) only the preverb is swapped, sometimes with a clear English-particle source (*fel-jár* 'go up'
for HH *le-jár* 'expire', modeled on English 'be up') and sometimes with none; (iv) the whole
construction is replaced by a calque on an English phrasal verb (*rátesz a rádióműsort* 'I put on the
radio program' for HH *bekapcsolom* 'I turn it on'); and (v) miscellaneous cases where a
non-compositional preverb (one contributing no aspectual or directional meaning of its own, as in
*megismer* 'recognize' vs. bare *ismer* 'be familiar with') is simply dropped. The chapter connects
this to Bartha & Sydorenko's (2000) Matrix Language Turnover analysis: AH preverbs are gradually
losing their HH function as aspect markers and shifting toward a more English-like verb-modifier
role. pp. 313–316

### 8. Case-marking simplification: loss, not wholesale replacement of the case system

AH case usage diverges from HH in two roughly equal ways: (a) outright loss of case suffixes
(accusative most frequently, ~29% of all such losses in both South Bend and McKeesport, with
instrumental, dative, and essive also affected), argued to represent a shift toward a more
English-like abstract-Case system rather than random attrition — Bolonyai (2000), cited by the
chapter, explains the specific accusative-suffix vulnerability via the 4-M model's system/content
morpheme distinction (accusative morphemes being "late system morphemes," predicted to be less
reliably produced than oblique/locative cases); and (b) substitution of one case for a
semantically related one, concentrated overwhelmingly (~80–90%) in the local-case system.
The chapter's clearest structural generalization: AH tends to collapse the three-way distinction
*within* each of the "in-," "on-," and "at-" case families (e.g. conflating inessive/elative/illative
into fewer forms) while the cross-family distinction of movement-from vs. static-location vs.
movement-to is comparatively well preserved — a directional/locational hierarchy of what erodes
first. pp. 316–318

### 9. Possessive-suffix loss and mood mixing — parallel to the Austria findings

Loss of pronominal possessive suffixes (e.g. *az ő mama beszélt* 'his mom spoke' for HH's
possessive-marked *mamája*) is documented in both simple possessed NPs and the dative "have"
construction, in both generations but more in the second — directly parallel to the possessive-suffix
attrition documented for Oberwart Hungarian in Chapter 8 (`029_...md`), suggesting this specific
erosion pattern is a general feature of advanced Hungarian-in-contact situations rather than
specific to any one contact language. Mood mixing (indicative used for imperative-subjunctive and
vice versa) shows the identical morphological-asymmetry pattern already noted for conjugation mixing:
person/number marking on the verb remains correct even when mood choice is wrong. pp. 318–319

### 10. Focus and pro-drop violations — English word-order and pronoun-use bleeding through

Hungarian marks focus via a fixed pre-verbal position plus primary sentential stress, and is
pro-drop (subject pronouns appear only when emphasized, in focus position). AH shows three related
divergence patterns, more frequent in the second generation: focus movement entirely absent (emphasis
carried by stress alone, as in English); focus stress misplaced onto a non-focused pre-verbal phrase
while the actually-emphasized phrase gets stressed post-verbally instead; and non-emphatic overt
subject pronouns appearing where HH would drop them. A parallel SHOH-questionnaire-tested case for
overt *object* pronouns (normally dropped in HH unless emphasized) found Toledo AH speakers accepting
the nonstandard overt-pronoun form far more often than HH speakers (50.0% vs. 21.9% for one item;
61.1% vs. 27.4% for another) — both differences statistically significant. A focus-order item
(preverbal infinitive vs. postverbal, SHOH item 608) showed an even starker gap: AH chose the
nonstandard order 55.6% of the time vs. HH's 8.4%. pp. 320–323

### 11. Agreement loss across four distinct grammatical categories

AH shows loss of HH-obligatory agreement in four separate domains: subject-verb person/number
agreement (occasional in first-generation South Bend speech, near-universal in second-generation
McKeesport speech); quantifier-noun number agreement (HH requires singular after a numeral/quantifier
where English requires plural — e.g. AH *tizenédzs éivek* 'fourteen years' instead of HH's singular
*év*); predicate-adjective number agreement with a plural noun subject (second-generation-only in
both studied communities); and relative-pronoun/antecedent number agreement. Two of these were
independently confirmed via the Toledo SHOH questionnaire: AH speakers chose the nonstandard
(English-like) plural-association form significantly more often than HH speakers for one item
(44.4% vs. 15.1%), but — notably — chose the *standard* singular-adjective form significantly more
often than HH speakers for a second item, which the chapter attributes to the fact that in this
specific case the parallel English sentence structure happens to also require a singular adjective,
so contact reinforces the standard form rather than undermining it — a useful illustration that
"contact effect" doesn't always predict in a single direction; it depends on which specific form the
contact language happens to parallel. pp. 323–325

### 12. The *szokott* + infinitive construction: a genuine semantic shift, not just formal simplification

A distinct, semantically-oriented change: HH's habitual-*present* auxiliary construction *szokott* +
infinitive (marked for past tense morphologically but present in meaning, e.g. 'used to' in the sense
of an ongoing habit) shifts in AH — mainly in second-generation speech — toward expressing genuine
**habitual past** meaning (a habit that has ended), disambiguated only by discourse context (e.g. the
speaker later mentioning the relevant person has since died). This is presented as a case where
contact produces a semantic/aspectual reanalysis rather than a purely formal (morphological or
syntactic) simplification. pp. 325–326

### 13. Three-way causal taxonomy: borrowing, attrition, and multiple causation

The chapter's most explicit methodological contribution: it classifies each of the 52 AH features
different from HH that Fenyvesi's broader work catalogs (only a subset discussed in this chapter) into
three causal categories — pure **borrowing** (a feature directly incorporating an American English
element or pattern, e.g. borrowed words, English-modeled calques, English-parallel case replacement);
pure **language attrition** (simplification/reduction with no American-English parallel, e.g.
regularization of irregular stems, or non-focus-driven stress placement that doesn't track any
specific English pattern); and **multiple causation** (changes where both processes converge on the
same outcome and cannot be cleanly separated, e.g. gemination loss, the combined possessive-suffix
loss/appearance-of-overt-non-focused-possessor-pronoun pattern, and frequent lack of focus movement).
The tallied result: 20 features (well under half) attributed to borrowing alone, 28 (just over half)
to multiple causation, and only 4 to attrition alone — the chapter's own summary statistic for how
thoroughly entangled contact-effect and language-internal-decay explanations are in this dataset, and
an explicit caution against attributing every AH divergence straightforwardly to "English influence."
pp. 337–338

### §8.7 Aspect — the flagship paradigm chart (Actor Trigger vs. Goal Trigger, five-way aspect system)

Ilocano verbs inflect for five categories: **Infinitive/command** (unmarked for completion),
**Perfective** (completed action), **Imperfective** (non-punctual, in progress; formed by CVC
reduplication of the root with verbal morphology still in play), **Past imperfective** (continuous
action framed relative to a past time), and **Future, not initiated** (formed with the adverbial
enclitic *=to* after consonants, *=nto* after vowels). This is the single most structurally
important table in this file — reproduced in full as the source's own flagship worked paradigm,
directly comparable to Tagalog's Completed/Incompleted/Contemplated three-way system (file
`../../Tagalog/established/003_...md`), except Ilocano additionally grammaticalizes a fifth,
*past*-imperfective cell distinct from plain imperfective.

### §8.16 Plurality in verbs

Plurality of the absolutive argument is not mandatorily expressed on the verb (plural articles,
demonstratives, or pronouns normally carry that work); a speaker MAY optionally draw attention to
plurality/distributivity by reduplicating the root's first CV sequence. This plural CV reduplication
targets the **absolutive** category specifically — subjects of intransitives and patients of
transitives — never the agentive/ergative category, and is often lost in informal speech.

## Grammar points

### The Trigger/Focus system continues: Actor Trigger vs. Goal Trigger in the causative *pa-* layer

The causative/directional prefix *pa-* is Ilocano's single largest morphological subsystem covered
in this file, and it systematically doubles every Trigger category already established in file 006
(the first half of Chapter 8): every intransitive (Actor Trigger) affix class has a corresponding
*pa-*-causativized counterpart (*ag-*→*agpa-*, *agi-*→*agpai-*, *mang-*→*mangpa-*, *maka-*→*makapa-*,
etc.), and every transitive (Goal Trigger) affix class does too (*-en*→*pa- -en*, *-an*→*pa- -an*,
*pag- -en*→*pagpa- -en*, *i-*→*ipa-*). This is directly comparable to Tagalog's causative *pa-*
system (though Tagalog's reference material extracted so far for this project did not cover
causatives in comparable depth) and gives slang-synthesis Phase 3 a concrete, fully worked template
for how an Austronesian voice/trigger language productively layers "cause X to happen" onto an
existing verb's own voice marking, rather than using a separate causative auxiliary verb the way
English does. The *pa-* system also cleanly splits into involuntary/indirect causation (*makapa-*,
*mapa-*, *maipa-*, all glossed "cause unintentionally" or "be made to") versus deliberate/forceful
causation (*pag- -en* vs. *pa- -en*, explicitly contrasted by the source: *pagtugawen* "to
physically force someone to sit" vs. *patugawen* "to have someone sit down, allow by gesture") — a
volition/force gradient encoded directly in the choice of causative affix, not left to context.

### The five-way aspect system and its CVC-reduplication mechanism

Ilocano's aspect system (Infinitive/Perfective/Imperfective/Past-imperfective/Future) is the
structural backbone of this file's §8.7, and is mechanically close to Tagalog's three-way
Completed/Incompleted/Contemplated system (CVC-root reduplication marking "in progress," an *n-*
nasal/infix marking "initiated," per the pattern already documented for Tagalog) but is formally a
richer five-cell paradigm: Ilocano additionally grammaticalizes a dedicated Past-imperfective cell
(continuing action framed relative to a past reference point, marked by combining the perfective's
*n-* prefix WITH the imperfective's CVC reduplication simultaneously) and a dedicated Future cell
marked by a free-standing enclitic (*=to*/*=nto*) rather than root-internal morphology — meaning
Ilocano cleanly separates "tense-like" future marking (an enclitic, attachable to any word class, not
just verbs) from "aspect-proper" marking (reduplication + affix alternation, verb-internal only).
The source makes this explicit: "the future enclitic *=(n)to* ... is best analyzed as a future marker
of a *clause*, not an aspectual or tense marker for verbs." This is a genuinely different design from
Tagalog's three-way system and from English's two-dimensional tense×aspect grid, and is worth
flagging for Phase 3 as a case where "aspect" and "future-time-reference" are cleanly orthogonal
rather than fused into a single paradigm slot.

### Fourteen irregular verbs, a closed and heavily-used set

Every one of the language's monosyllabic verb roots (*ay* 'come', *pan* 'go', *yeg* 'bring') appears
in the irregular-verb list, and the source explicitly ties their irregularity to their shortness:
monosyllabic roots are the ones most likely to retain an infix (*-um-*) in positions where a longer
root would delete it, to resist normal reduction rules, and to develop suppletive/portmanteau forms.
Beyond the monosyllabic trio, the irregular set is dominated by exactly the highest-frequency,
most grammaticalized verbs in any language's core vocabulary — "know," "say," "want," "give," "go,"
"hear," "eat" — a cross-linguistically familiar pattern (English "go/went," "be," "have" are likewise
its most irregular verbs) that is worth flagging as a likely universal: the verbs speakers use most
often are also the verbs under the most erosive phonological pressure, and irregularity is therefore
a side effect of frequency rather than an arbitrary property of specific lexical items. Two pairs
stand out as structurally interesting: *kayat*/*mayat* (both transparently built on the same
underlying stem *ayat* 'love', via *ka-* and *ma-* respectively, now perceived as monomorphemic) show
a single root fossilizing into two formally and functionally distinct irregular verbs; and *sida*
'fish/food' → irregular perfective *sinda* shows a noun that functions as a Goal-Trigger verb root
acquiring its own idiosyncratic, non-productive perfective form distinct from the regular *-in-*
infixation pattern used everywhere else in the transitive paradigm.

### Reduplication as a recurring multi-purpose mechanism

As already flagged for Tagalog (file `../../Tagalog/established/003_...md`), a single reduplicative
process doing multiple distinct grammatical jobs recurs strongly in this Ilocano material: CVC root
reduplication marks imperfective aspect (§8.7.3), frequentative/habitual aspect combined with
specific prefixes (§8.7.9, §8.9's pretentative forms), recent-past aspect (§8.7.6's *ka-* + RCV),
plurality of the absolutive argument (§8.16), and (in its FULL, whole-root form rather than
CV/CVC-partial form) intensity/completeness/iterativity and directional back-and-forth motion
(§8.14). That is at least five distinct grammatical payloads carried by variants of the same single
formal mechanism — an even richer multiplexing of reduplication than what Tagalog showed (which had
three). This is a strong, cross-linguistically-recurring candidate for Phase 3 conlang-slang
mechanics: a productive, semantically-flexible reduplication process is apparently a cheap, reusable
grammatical resource that a language can repeatedly repurpose for aspect, number, and intensity
without needing distinct dedicated morphology for each.

**Coverage note / flagship content:** This chunk is unusually dense in closed-class grammatical

dispatch instructions every such item is captured — this is the complete inventory of Ilocano's
politeness-marking system in imperatives, its negation-particle inventory, and its full
interrogative-word paradigm as presented in the source. Three findings are flagged as especially
high-value for this project:

1. **Negative yes/no question answering (§14.1.2, §13.3)** — Ilocano does not use strict
   "agreement with the form of the question" (as in some languages) nor strict "agreement with the
   real-world fact" (as in English) to answer a negative question. Instead, a negative question like
   *Saanda a napan?* 'Didn't they go?' may be answered **either** with *wen, saanda a napan* 'yes,
   they didn't go' **or** *saan, saanda a napan* 'no, they didn't go' — both meaning the same thing
   (they in fact didn't go) — and the source states explicitly that the choice "must be understood
   from the context... not from the formal syntax of the response." A *third*, distinct particle,
   *saan man* (contracted in speech to *samman*), exists specifically to **contradict** a negative
   question's presupposition ('yes, they actually DID go'). This three-way system (ambiguous
   wen/saan, vs. unambiguous saan man/samman) is a genuinely unusual polarity-marking mechanism,
   worth mining directly for a conlang's register of contradiction/confirmation particles.
2. **Three-tier politeness marking in imperatives (§12.4)** — the particle *man* (intensified as
   *man bassit*, lit. "please" + "a little"), the prefix *paki-* (flagged by the source itself as
   "perhaps borrowed from Tagalog," applied to goal-focus imperative verbs with ergative enclitics
   to name the addressee), and the fixed formula *pangngaasi=m/yo ta...* ('your pity, that...' = "please...")
   are three structurally distinct politeness mechanisms (particle, prefix+valence-shift, fixed
   nominal-formula-plus-ligature) stacked in a single grammatical slot — a rich source for a
   conlang's register/politeness system design.
3. **The fossilized *-ano* interrogative-suffix family (§14.4.5, §14.4.6, §14.4.9)** — a whole
   paradigm of morphologically complex question words built on a non-productive suffix *-ano*
   stacked onto prefixes/particles: *inton-ano* 'when (future)', *kasano* 'how', *agpaano* 'which
   way', *kapin-ano* 'how related', *maikamano* 'in what ordinal position', *makamano* 'how many
   days', *makasagmamano* 'how many times each', *mamin-ano* 'how many times', *sagpamin-ano* 'how
   many times each', *taga-ano* 'where from'. This is a compact, highly systematic question-word
   derivation template — exactly the kind of productive morphological pattern this project's
   slang-synthesis pipeline can repurpose.

## Chapter 12 — Imperatives

### 12.1–12.2 Positive and Negative Imperatives (p.412)

**Patch note (2026-10-30):** this chapter's own opening page (PDF p.437, printed p.412 — §§12.1
"Positive imperatives" and 12.2 "Negative imperatives," with five worked examples) fell into a
1-page gap between this file's assigned range and sibling file 009's, and was originally skipped.
Directly vision-read and inserted below by the orchestrating session after the wave landed.

Imperatives (commands) are formed with infinitival verbs; they cannot be formally distinguished
from simple declarative sentences in written language (no word-order change, regular 2nd-person
pronouns retained) — the only formal cue, aside from context, is the optional enclitic *=(e)n*,
which stimulates the addressee to act immediately. Negative imperatives are formed with the
negative particles *saan* or *di*, followed by the enclitic pronouns and then the verb, similarly
to a simple declarative negative; with *saan* the ligature *(ng)a* is used before the verb phrase,
but not with *di*. Unlike Tagalog, there is no separate morpheme dedicated to negative commands.

## Chapter 13 — Negation (pp.416–426)

Source's five-part taxonomy of negative predications: (1) predicate negation, (2) negation of
existentials, (3) negation in responses, (4) negative counterparts of specific verbs *mandiak,
indiak, madi*, (5) specific formulas used with negative propositions.

### 13.1 Predicate Negation (pp.416–420)

Two negative adverbs negate declarative sentences: *saan* (dialectal variant: *haan*) and *di*.
Without enclitic pronouns, the declaration follows the negator — *saan* requires the ligature
*(ng)a*, *di* requires none; *saan* is preferred for negating noun phrases in speech; prepositional
phrases must be negated with *saan*. With enclitic pronouns, the pronoun attaches to *saan*/*di*
itself (still with the *(ng)a* ligature for *saan*); the enclitic =k(o) changes to =ak after *di*
(not after *saan*). Imperatives are negated identically to declaratives (no special negative
imperative adverb, unlike Tagalog).

### 13.3 Negation in Responses (pp.421–422)

A negative response to a yes/no question or imperative uses the adverb *saan*, never *di*.
*Saanen* softens a response to a negative imperative. *Saan pay*/*di pay* 'not yet' negate an
assertion expected to occur in the future (contracted in speech to *sampay*/*hampay*). *Saan man*
(contracted *samman*) negates a negative question to affirm the realization of an action.

## Chapter 14 — Interrogativity (pp.427–443)

Four question types: yes/no, alternative, confirmation, information. Section 14.4 details nine
sub-classes of information-question words; §14.5 covers social-formula questions; §14.6 covers
interrogative subordinators; §14.7 covers three interrogative particles (*kadi, aya, ngay*).

### 14.1 Yes/No Questions (and §14.1.1–14.1.2 Answering Them) (pp.427–428)

— rising intonation alone often signals interrogativity. The optional second-position enclitic

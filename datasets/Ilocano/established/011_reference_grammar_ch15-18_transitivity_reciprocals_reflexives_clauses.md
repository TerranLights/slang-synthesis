### §15 Chapter opening and §15.1 The non-transitive clause

**Patch note (2026-10-30):** this chapter's own opening page (PDF p.469, printed p.444 — the Ch.15
intro paragraph and §15.1 "The non-transitive clause," with five worked examples) fell into a
1-page gap between this file's assigned range and sibling file 010's, and was originally skipped.
Directly vision-read and inserted below by the orchestrating session after the wave landed.

Transitivity plays a central role in the syntax of Ilocano, as in all ergative languages. This
chapter outlines the role transitivity plays in the major clause types (Chapter 11 covers all types
of clause constructions more broadly). A clause's central defining characteristic is its predicate;
§15.1 covers non-transitive-predicate clauses, §15.2 the canonical transitive clause, §15.3
syntactic motivations for detransitivization (including antipassives), with further morphological
detail cross-referenced to the Verbs chapter's own Detransitivization section (file 007). A
non-transitive clause is one devoid of a morphologically transitive verb — this includes non-verbal
predicates (noun phrase, prepositional phrase, or adjective phrase predicates) and existential
clauses (predicate composed of *adda*/*awan*/*adu*, cross-referenced to Chapter 10 Existentials).

### Ch.15 Transitivity and Voice — illustrative verb forms (Trigger/Focus labeled)

Ilocano is an ergative language: transitive verbs take an ergative pronoun (actor) and an absolutive
enclitic pronoun (patient); the verb's own affix set marks which argument is in focus/trigger
position, directly comparable to Tagalog's Actor/Object/Locative/Beneficiary/Instrumental trigger
system (see `../../Tagalog/established/003_tagalog_grammar_verbal_focus_aspect.md`) though Ilocano's
grammar is framed in ergative terms (ergative/absolutive) rather than Tagalog's focus terms.

## Grammar points

### Non-transitive clauses: ambient vs. intransitive (§15.1)

Ilocano's non-transitive clause types split into **ambient clauses** (meteorological/temporal
predicates like *rabiin* 'it's night already' and *agrudtudon* 'it's raining already,' which take no
morphological argument at all) and genuine **intransitive clauses** (predicates like *pimmanawdan*
'they already left,' built on an intransitive verb with a single, absolutive argument). This
distinction matters directly for relativization (§18.1): ambient clauses, lacking any noun-phrase
topic, can never function as relative clauses, while every other clause type can.

### The canonical transitive and ergativity (§15.2)

A transitive verb in Ilocano takes an **ergative** pronoun encoding the actor and an **absolutive**
enclitic pronoun encoding the patient/undergoer — the structural backbone the rest of the chapter
builds on. Complement clauses may themselves fill the absolutive argument slot of a transitive main
clause (*Kayatna ti agawid idiay Pilipinas* 'He wants to go home to the Philippines'), directly
foreshadowing Chapter 18's complementation system.

### Detransitivizing strategies (§15.3)

Ilocano places syntactic restrictions on transitive verbs that force certain semantically transitive
actions to surface with morphologically intransitive verbs instead — the chapter identifies two
distinct motivations:

1. **Identificational constructions (§15.3.1).** When an agent/actor noun phrase is fronted into
   predicate position for identificational or contrastive purposes ("singling out" a referent), the
   referent being identified must be in absolutive position relative to the following verb — which
   forces the verb to detransitivize. Since *every* transitive verb in Ilocano can be morphologically
   detransitivized, this fronting never creates a structural dead end. The alternation is governed by
   the verb's **syntax**, not by pragmatic identifiability.
2. **Antipassives (§15.3.2).** Ilocano requires all absolutive-position referents to be identifiable
   by the addressee. When the patient of a semantically transitive action is *not* identifiable, the
   verb is detransitivized and the patient is demoted to oblique case instead of remaining absolutive
   — Ilocano's antipassive construction, directly paired in the source against canonical-transitive
   counterparts using the same root (*kinnanko* canonical vs. *nanganak* antipassive, both from
   *kaan* 'eat'). The source also notes that syntactic requirements can override pragmatic ones:
   even an *identifiable* patientive referent must take oblique marking when it co-occurs with a
   nominal predicate in an identificational construction or inside a relative clause — i.e.
   detransitivization in those two environments is structurally forced regardless of identifiability.

### Agentless transitives — Ilocano's closest analog to a passive (§15.4)

Not every transitive verb specifies an agentive argument; Ilocano simply allows the agent to be left
out of the utterance. Critically, **Ilocano, like many ergative languages, has no true passive
construction.** The closest functional equivalent is a transitive verb whose predicate retains only
its undergoer argument in the absolutive case (*Maawatannak?* 'Do you understand me?' vs.
*Maawatanak* 'I am understood'). The suffix *-en* (perfective *-in-*) or the circumfix *ma-...-an*
can form passive-type verbs where the agent is expressed lexically inside the root itself rather than
as a separate NP (e.g. *Iggesen* 'may [it] be infested with worms,' from the root *igges* 'worm'). In
a very small set of *ma-*-stative verbs, an agent may exceptionally surface in the oblique case,
mirroring the passive prototype more closely than the norm (*Naduayaak iti singgit dagiti andidit*
'I was lulled to sleep by the shrills of the cicadas').

### Reciprocals: inherent vs. morphological (Ch.16)

Reciprocal verbs split into two types. **Inherent reciprocals** (*saliwasiw* 'to separate and meet
again,' *siliwasiw* 'to miss each other from different directions') carry reciprocal meaning purely
in their lexical semantics, with no special morphology. **Morphological reciprocals** take the infix
*-inn-* (placed before the root's first vowel, like every Ilocano infix), usually accompanied by the
prefix *ag-*. Plurality of the reciprocal actors may be further specified by **CV root reduplication**
stacked on top of the *-inn-* infix (*agpipinnabasoldan* 'they are blaming each other,' with the
reduplicated *pi-* marking plurality distinct from the reciprocal *-inn-* itself). A third category,
**"multiple reciprocal verbs,"** are plain *ag-* verbs whose plural-absolutive requirement gives them
an inherently reciprocal reading as a feature of their ordinary semantics rather than dedicated
morphology (*agkitatanto* 'we'll see each other,' from plain *ag-kita* 'see'). *Ag-* additionally
derives a small, closed set of **reciprocal kinship terms** (*aginá* 'mother and child,' *agamá*
'father and child,' *agulitég* 'uncle and nephew/niece') — a genuinely productive cross-category
pattern (verbal reciprocal prefix repurposed as a kinship-pair nominal derivation) worth flagging for
Phase 3 slang-mechanics analysis, directly parallel to the Tagalog location-focus/-an-nominal-suffix
pattern already flagged in that language's established files.

### Reflexives built on the possessive paradigm, not dedicated morphology (Ch.17)

Ilocano has **no special verbal morphology for reflexives.** Instead, the same *bagi-* possessive
paradigm used elsewhere in the grammar (root *bagi* 'body') supplies the full reflexive pronoun set
(*bagik* 'myself' through *bagida* 'themselves'), with plural forms optionally reduplicating to
*bagbagi-*. Case marking on the reflexive pronoun tracks the transitivity of its host clause rather
than being fixed: it surfaces in the **absolutive** case as the focused argument of a transitive verb
(*Nakitana ti bagina iti sarming* 'He saw himself in the mirror'), but shifts to **oblique** case
marking when the co-referential nominal precedes an intransitive verb in an identificational
construction, since the reflexive pronoun is then no longer part of that verb's valence (*Isuna ti
nakangeg iti bagina iti radio* 'He is the one who heard himself on the radio'). This is the same
absolutive-pivot-drives-detransitivization logic from Chapter 15's identificational constructions,
now applied to a reflexive pronoun rather than a lexical NP.

### Relative clauses: the absolutive-pivot constraint (§18.1)

A relative clause modifies its head noun via the structure *head noun + (ng)a + predicate of
clause*. **Every** clause type may be relativized **except** ambient (meteorological) clauses, which
by their nature lack any noun-phrase topic to relativize. The single governing constraint, stated
explicitly by the source, is that *the relativized head noun must be in absolutive position relative
to the verb of the relative clause* — meaning that whenever a speaker wants to relativize what would
otherwise be a transitive clause's ergative actor, the verb must first be detransitivized (the exact
same mechanism as Chapter 15's identificational fronting, now applied inside a relative clause rather
than a main predicate-position construction). Two or more relative clauses may be chained
sequentially as descriptive phrases, each preceded by its own ligature *(ng)a*.

### Three types of complement clauses, distinguished by complementizer and finiteness (§18.2)

Complement clauses function as embedded nuclear constituents — frequently as the "argument" filling
a main transitive verb's absolutive slot — and have no special complementizing morphology of their
own; what distinguishes the three types is the complementizer plus whether co-referential arguments
delete:

1. **Nominal *ti* complementation (§18.2.1).** The complement clause is built on an **intransitive**
   verb preceded by the nominalizing article/complementizer *ti*, and its actor must be co-referential
   with the main clause's ergative actor (*Masapulko ti mapan* 'I need to go'). Because the
   complement clause fills the absolutive slot of the transitive main verb, a main-clause verb using
   this construction may **not** carry double pronoun enclitics (both ergative and absolutive) the way
   it normally could — the complement clause itself occupies that absolutive position (the source
   flags *Kayatnak ti agdigos* as ungrammatical for exactly this reason). When the complement
   clause's argument is *not* lexically deleted/co-referential, the complement instead reads more like
   an ordinary noun phrase with extra nominalizing morphology than a true embedded clause.
2. ***Ta* complementation (§18.2.2).** Reserved for a closed set of **lexicalized predicate
   expressions** whose case frame specifically requires the complementizer *ta* (*(na)imbag ta* 'it's
   good that...,' *sapay kuma ta* 'I hope that...,' *bay-an ta* 'let; allow...,' etc.). Unlike *ti*
   complementation, *ta* complement clauses must be **finite** — co-referential arguments are never
   deleted. *Ta* is also independently used to introduce the reason/purpose clause attached to
   certain imperative verbs (*aguray* 'wait,' *ibaga* 'tell,' *umay* 'come').
3. ***Nga* complementation (§18.2.3).** The default/elsewhere complementizer for every remaining
   complement-taking predicate, subdivided into three syntactic subtypes:
   - **Impersonal predicates** (§18.2.3.1) — evaluative, aspectual, modal, and epistemic/desiderative
     predicates (*mayat* 'good,' *mangrugi* 'begin,' *rumbeng* 'must,' *napudno* 'true,' etc.) that take
     no personal clitic or NP argument of their own; the *nga* complement clause supplies the
     appropriate referent on the impersonal predicate's behalf. Not every clause of this type
     specifies an argument at all — meteorological predicates like *Mangrugi nga agarbis* 'It is
     starting to drizzle' leave the complement itself argumentless too.
   - **Personal predicates, co-referential complements** (§18.2.3.2) — a large inventory organized
     by semantic class (perception, knowledge, propositional attitude, phasal, manipulative,
     declarative/utterance verbs — all enumerated in full in the Vocabulary table above). When the
     main predicate's actor is co-referential with the complement clause's actor, the complement's
     actor may be left unexpressed — deletable by **semantic role** (the actor), regardless of
     whether that actor maps to an ergative or an absolutive argument in the complement clause
     itself. Co-referential arguments may optionally be expressed twice (once in each clause) purely
     for disambiguating clarity. If the main clause already carries a double-pronoun construction
     (both ergative and absolutive expressed), the non-transitive complement clause's unexpressed
     argument normally tracks the main clause's **absolutive** argument by default. If the complement
     clause is negative and its actor is non-lexical, the pronoun must be expressed in *both* clauses
     (no co-referential deletion under negation).
   - **Personal predicates, non-co-referential complements** (§18.2.3.3) — when the complement
     clause's arguments are *not* co-referential with the main clause's, the complement clause must be
     fully finite, with its arguments expressed pronominally or lexically, exactly as in an
     independent clause.

### Twelve categories of adverbial clauses (§18.3)

Adverbial clauses are unsubcategorized sentential/phrasal adjuncts that supply the same range of
semantic relations ordinary adverbs supply to the verbs they modify. The chapter catalogs twelve
categories — temporal, locational, manner, reason/purpose, circumstantial, simultaneous, conditional,
concessive, substitutive, additive, resultative, and optative — each introduced by its own dedicated
connective(s), fully enumerated in the Vocabulary table above. Several structural patterns recur
across categories worth flagging explicitly:

- **Extreme polysemy concentrated in a handful of function words.** *No* alone spans future-temporal
  'when,' hypothetical-conditional 'if,' and (fused with the particle *man*) the concessive
  *numan pay* 'although.' *Ket* alone spans reason, resultative, and a coordination-adjacent discourse
  function (prompting a speaker to elaborate) — the source explicitly declines to classify *ket* as a
  genuine coordinating conjunction despite covering it in the coordination chapter, since (unlike the
  true coordinator *ken*) *ket*-linked constituents cannot be reordered and cannot conjoin parallel
  events. *Isu nga* spans both reason-conclusion and resultative functions. *Amangan no* spans both a
  dedicated "doubtful-with-negative-consequence" reason/purpose function and a plain strongly-negative
  conditional function.
- **Ligature-governed vs. ligature-free subordinators.** Most connectives in this chapter require the
  ligature *(ng)a* before a verbal (but not nominal) complement clause — *sakbay*, *apaman*, *manner
  kas*-phrases, *imbes*, *banag*, etc. A few are explicitly ligature-free, most notably *nupay*
  'even though,' which the source flags as taking no ligature at all unlike nearly every other
  connective in the chapter, and *tunggal* 'every,' which likewise precedes its clause with no
  intervening ligature.
- **A root numeral repurposed as a discourse connective.** *Maysa* 'one' (the cardinal number) doubles
  as the additive-clause subordinator 'furthermore, moreover, besides,' normally set off by a pause —
  a specific instance of the broader cross-linguistic pattern (seen independently in several other
  languages in this project) of a basic-vocabulary item being repurposed as discourse-structuring
  machinery.

### Coordination: ken vs. ket vs. ngem vs. wenno (§18.4)

Four items anchor Ilocano coordination, but only two of them are genuine syntactic coordinators by
the source's own analysis. ***Ken*** 'and' freely links reorderable constituents (word order is
commutative: *ti aso ken pusa* = *ti pusa ken aso* 'the dog and the cat' = 'the cat and the dog') and
doubles as a comitative 'with' (dropping the personal article *ni* when used this way); it has a
dedicated plural form *kada* for coordinating/introducing plural personal noun phrases in oblique
case. ***Ket*** superficially resembles a coordinator (and is traditionally discussed alongside one)
but the source explicitly disqualifies it: *ket*-linked constituents cannot be reordered and *ket*
cannot conjoin two parallel/simultaneous events the way *ken* can — it is better understood as a
successive/resultative/reason connective that happens to gloss as 'and, and then' in translation.
***Ngem*** 'but' introduces contrastive coordination and also marks the standard of comparison with
comparative adjectives and the verb *kaykayat* 'to prefer'; its negative counterpart is *no di ket*
'but rather' (a form that collides in surface shape, though not in function, with the homophonous
additive-clause *no di ket* from §18.3.10 — the source does not flag this as a single polysemous item
the way it does for *no*, *ket*, and *isu nga* above, treating them instead as two separate
coincidentally-homophonous expressions). ***Wenno*** 'or' sits between its connected alternatives and
may repeat for emphasis (*wenno ... wenno*); it combines with *man* for *man wenno* 'either' and with
*saan* for the fixed expressions *man wenno saan* 'whether or not' and the cross-clausal *wenno saan*
'or not' (used when offering a fallback alternative across separate intonational clauses).

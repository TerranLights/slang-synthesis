### §8 Chapter opening — verbs, focus, and the chapter's own outline

**Patch note (2026-10-30):** this flagship chapter's own opening page (PDF p.211, printed p.186 —
the Ch.8 intro paragraph, its own §8.1–8.16 section outline, and the first worked Actor-Focus
example) fell into a 1-page gap between this file's assigned range and sibling file 005's, and was
originally skipped. Directly vision-read and inserted below by the orchestrating session after the
wave landed.

Verbs are distinguished from other lexical classes by Aspect marking (§8.7) and Focus/Transitivity
(§8.1), and may be further subcategorized by mode based on the semantic dimensions of volitionality
or control (§§8.3.1, 8.3.8). Most Ilocano verbs consist of a root plus verbalizing morphology;
a few common verbs are monomorphemic. The chapter's own section outline: 8.1 Focus and transitivity;
8.2–4 Summary of verbal affixes; 8.5 Detransitivization; 8.6 Causation and direction (prefix *pa-*);
8.7 Aspect; 8.8 fossilized prefix *tagi-*; 8.9 Pretense prefix *agin-* + CV-; 8.10 Smell prefix
*agaC-*; 8.11 Irregular Verbs; 8.12 Non-derived transitive verbs; 8.13 Hyphenated verbs; 8.14 Verbs
with full reduplication; 8.15 Nominalizations; 8.16 Plurality. §8.1 opens by defining "focus" (the
Philippine-linguistics term for which absolutive nominal a verb's affixation privileges): simple
intransitive *ag-*/*-um-* verbs that take at most one core nominal (the actor) are "actor focus,"
even when that absolutive argument is not always a semantic agent.

### §8.3.7 The comitative prefix maki- (perfective naki-) — the chapter's flagship cluster

Intransitive counterpart of comitative *ka-* (§8.1): the person performing the action takes
absolutive case, the companion takes oblique case if specified. This is the single most elaborately
layered affix cluster in the chapter — Table 8.3.7 in-source lists seven named sub-derivations
(simple comitative, reciprocal, desiderative, social, comitative-social ×2, pretentative, theme),
each given its own numbered subsection (§8.3.7.1–7). No comparably deep comitative layer is recorded
in this project's Tagalog material, making this cluster a strong candidate for Ilocano's own
flagship grammatical finding alongside the root-recursion pattern noted throughout (see Grammar
points).

## Grammar points

### The core mechanism: six absolutive macro-roles under two grammatical macro-roles

Table 8.1 (§8.1) establishes the spine the rest of the chapter elaborates: Ilocano verbs are first
split into **Actor** macro-role (grammatically intransitive; affixes *ag-*, *-um-*, *mang-*) and
**Goal** macro-role (grammatically transitive; six semantic sub-roles — Patient *-en*, Locative
*-an*, Benefactive *i- -an*, Instrumental *pag-/pang-*, Comitative *ka-*, Theme *i-*). This is
structurally the same ergative-leaning, affix-marks-role-not-word-order system this project's
Tagalog file 003 documents as "the core mechanism" of Philippine-type languages, with absolutive
case marking the focused/trigger argument and ergative/oblique case marking the rest. Table 8.2
(§8.2, PDF p.217/printed 192, not individually tabulated above since it is itself a paradigm summary
rather than a vocabulary list) cross-tabulates this six-role system against **Mode** (Indicative vs.
Potentive) and **Aspect** (Infinitive vs. Perfective) and further against **Detransitivizations**
(mang-/mangi-/mangika- etc.) and **Causatives** (pa-/ipa- forms, not covered in this file — reserved
for §8.5+ in file 007) — the single densest grid in the dissertation and the organizing principle
behind every affix section that follows it.

### Indicative vs. Potentive mode — the volition split that doubles the whole system

Every semantic role in the Goal macro-role (and the Actor macro-role) has both an **Indicative**
form (ag-/-um-/mang- for Actor; -en/-an/i-/i- -an/ika- for Goal) and a **Potentive** form (maka- for
Actor; ma-/mai-/ma- -an for Goal). Potentive forms consistently mark reduced volition: ability,
accident, involuntary experience, or non-culpable happenstance, contrasted directly against their
Indicative counterparts in the book's own minimal-pair tables (*kitaen* "look at" deliberately vs.
*makita* "see it" incidentally; *kagaten* "bite on purpose" vs. *makagat* "bite by accident"). This
volition axis is independent of, and layered on top of, the six-way semantic-role axis — meaning the
full system is in principle a 6 (role) × 2 (mode) × 2 (aspect) grid, which is exactly what Table 8.2
lays out.

### The Actor-focus three-way split: ag- / -um- / mang-, and why the same root can take all three

*ag-* is the default, most general intransitive verbalizer, usable productively with nominal,
volitional, meteorological, and stative roots alike. *-um-* specifically marks inception/onset
(becoming a state, or the beginning of a motion) and is the default for motion verbs. *mang-*
(historically from *maN-*) is reserved for roots with an existing transitive -en/-an counterpart,
and — uniquely among the three — *retains that transitive verb's patientive semantics* even though
it is itself grammatically intransitive (§8.3.6, §8.3.6.1). Because these three affixes encode
genuinely different things (generality vs. inception vs. detransitivized-but-patientive), a single
root frequently takes two or all three with non-overlapping, sometimes fully lexicalized meanings
(*ruar*: *agruar* "become fashionable" vs. *rumuar* "go outside"; *taray*: *agtaray* "run" (ongoing)
vs. *timmaray* "ran" (inception, in the worked example) vs. *mangtaray* "run after" (retains
*tarayen*'s patientive object)). This is directly comparable to — and arguably more elaborate than —
the three-way -um-/mag-/ma- actor-focus split this project's Tagalog file 003 documents, since
Ilocano additionally threads a dedicated detransitivizing layer (mang-) through the same three-way
contrast that Tagalog's three affixes do not carry.

### The maki- comitative cluster — likely Ilocano's flagship grammatical finding

No comparably deep comitative/social layer is recorded anywhere else in this project's Tagalog
material (which has no comitative macro-role at all in its established trigger system). Ilocano's
comitative macro-role (ka- transitive / maki- intransitive) carries its own full internal paradigm
of seven named sub-derivations, each independently productive and each given its own numbered
dissertation subsection (§8.3.7.1–7): simple comitative (*maki-*, "participate with"), reciprocal
comitative (*maki- -inn-*, "X each other together"), comitative desiderative (*maki- -um-*, "want to
X with," dialectally restricted), social companionship (*makika-*), communal-task participation with
ag- verbs (*makipag-*, further combinable with the reciprocal -inn- infix), the parallel form for
mang- verbs (*makipang-*/*makipaN-*), pretentative-in-company (*makipagin-* + consonant-vowel
reduplication, "pretend to X together"), and transference-in-company with agi- verbs (*makipagi-*).
This is a self-contained micro-system nested inside the larger trigger system, with its own layered
morphology (comitative base + reciprocal/desiderative/pretentative add-ons) that rivals the main
six-role Goal system in combinatorial depth. Given the project's interest in how real
trigger/agreement systems might seed a derived conlang's slang-marking morphology (Phase 3), this
comitative cluster — rather than the core Actor/Goal split, which Tagalog already demonstrates well
— is the single most novel structural pattern this chapter contributes.

### Lexical idiosyncrasy as the rule, not the exception

Across nearly every affix pair the book covers (-en vs. -an, i- vs. -en, ag- vs. mang-, ag- vs.
-um-, maka- vs. makapag-), the text repeatedly states some version of "no general rule determines
which root takes which affix; this must be learned by rote" (explicit at §8.4.1.2 for -en/-an,
implicit throughout §8.3.5's and §8.3.6.1's lexicalized-distinction tables). Combined with the sheer
number of homonymous or near-homonymous roots that take entirely unrelated derivations (two distinct
*kabil* roots — "hit" vs. "put" — each with their own independent ag-/-um- and i-/-en paradigms;
*ruar*, *dait*, *pan*, and *tugaw* each spanning many unrelated-looking surface forms), this chapter
is strong evidence that Ilocano's verbal lexicon is organized more as a large set of root-specific
memorized affix combinations than as a fully compositional rule system — directly relevant to how a
derived conlang's slang layer might plausibly innovate new, lexically-idiosyncratic affix
combinations on existing roots rather than only generating fully regular forms.

# Ilocano — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: A Reference Grammar of Ilocano, Front Matter + Chapters 1–2

**Source:** Carl Ralph Galvez Rubino, *A Reference Grammar of Ilocano*, PhD dissertation, University
of California, Santa Barbara (UMI Microform 9823418), June 1997 (UMI copyright 1998). 549 PDF pages
total. This file covers the full front matter (title page through Curriculum Vitae and Abstract,
PDF pp. 1–11), a structural summary of the detailed Table of Contents (PDF pp. 12–26), and the full
text of Chapter 1 "Introduction" (printed pp. 1–9) and Chapter 2 "Phonology" (printed pp. 10–29),
ending immediately before Chapter 3 "Noun marking: Articles, Demonstratives and Case" (printed p.
30). This is the **first-ever Phase 1 extraction for Ilocano** — no prior `established/` content
exists to check for redundancy against, so this chunk is extracted fully and comprehensively.

**Printed-page-to-PDF-page offset — corrected empirically.** The dispatch instructions assumed PDF
page = printed page + 26. This was verified against the source's own footer page numbers and found
to be **off by one: the correct offset is PDF page = printed page + 25** (e.g. printed p. 1, the
start of Ch. 1, is PDF page 26, not 27; printed p. 29, the end of Ch. 2, is PDF page 54; printed p.
30, the start of Ch. 3, is PDF page 55 — confirmed by reading PDF page 55 itself, which shows the
header "3. Noun marking: Articles, Demonstratives and Case" and footer "30"). This file's content
range is accordingly **PDF pages 1–54** (front matter PDF 1–11 using roman-numeral folios, body
PDF 26–54 using the corrected offset for printed pp. 1–29; the Table of Contents, PDF pp. 12–26,
uses roman folios ix–xxii and is described structurally below rather than transcribed).

**Source condition:** confirmed pure image scan with zero text layer (no embedded font/ToUnicode
data — a 1997 UMI microform dissertation scan, consistent with the dispatch's description). Full
vision-reading was required for all pages in this range. **Vision-reading guard:** all pages read
are genuinely typeset dissertation content (IBM Selectric/daisy-wheel-style typewriter face
throughout the body, consistent with a 1990s UCSB dissertation) with one page of handwritten
committee signatures correctly identified as such (PDF p. 5, the approval page) and excluded from
vocabulary extraction. Scan quality was clean and legible throughout; no low-confidence readings
were required — all entries below are marked `verified`.

**Front matter (PDF pp. 1–7, printed roman i–vii):** title page, UMI "Information to Users" notice,
UMI copyright/microform notice, committee approval page (handwritten signatures: Wallace Chafe,
[illegible], Katia McClain, Lawrence Reid, Marianne Mithun as Chairperson), a second copyright page,
and a dedication page written **in Ilocano** (captured as vocabulary below, since it is substantive
glossed content, unlike the rest of the low-content front matter). PDF pp. 5–7 (printed v–vii) are
the Acknowledgments (Panagyaman - Thanks, partly in Ilocano) and Curriculum Vitae; per the dispatch
instruction these are summarized rather than exhaustively tabled, since they are biographical/thin
on vocabulary — key facts are given in prose below. PDF p. 11 (printed viii) is the Abstract, summarized
in prose; it contains no Ilocano-language vocabulary, only English linguistic terminology (ergative,
absolutive, oblique) describing the dissertation's analytical framework.

**Table of Contents (PDF pp. 12–26, printed roman ix–xxii), structural summary only (not tabulated,
per dispatch instruction — navigational, not vocabulary content):** The dissertation is organized in
two parts. **Part I: Phonology and Morphology (Words)** — Ch. 1 Introduction (p. 1), Ch. 2 Phonology
(p. 10), Ch. 3 Articles, Demonstratives and Case (p. 30), Ch. 4 Pronouns (p. 52), Ch. 5 Nouns (p.

pa- combinations; aspect), Ch. 9 Adverbs (p. 320), Ch. 10 Existentials (p. 385). **Part II: Issues in
Syntax (Clauses)** — Ch. 11 Sentence types (p. 395), Ch. 12 Imperatives (p. 412), Ch. 13 Negation (p.
416), Ch. 14 Interrogativity (p. 427), Ch. 15 Transitivity and Voice (p. 444), Ch. 16 Reciprocals (p.
452), Ch. 17 Reflexives (p. 454), Ch. 18 Clause combining (p. 456, including a Coordination section
with the conjunctions *ken*, *ket*, *ngem*, *wenno*). The dissertation closes with a Glossary (p.
498), References (p. 511), Map Appendix (p. 513), and Index (p. 517). **Flag for future Phase 1
waves on this book:** Chapter 8 (Verbs) is the single most important chapter for this project's
trigger/focus-system interest, directly analogous to the Tagalog verbal-focus flagship chapter
(`datasets/Tagalog/established/003_tagalog_grammar_verbal_focus_aspect.md`) — a strong candidate for
early priority in a future Ilocano extraction wave.

**Coverage note.** Chapter 1 is sociolinguistic/historiographic prose with light but real vocabulary
content (loanword examples, two interlinearized example sentences). Chapter 2 (Phonology) is, like
the Hawaiian grammar's Chapter 2, extremely vocabulary-dense — nearly every phonological rule
(consonants, vowels, diphthongs, stress, stress shift, stress regression, gemination, the glottal
stop, phonologically-conditioned affixation changes, and syllabification) is illustrated with
multiple glossed example words. Every distinct glossed item is captured below, organized by the
source's own subsection structure for navigability (all tables share the 12-column format required
for this file). Where the exact same word+gloss pair is used by the author to illustrate more than
one phonological point (e.g. *dagiti* 'plural article' appears in three different orthography
tables; *nasam-it* 'sweet' illustrates both the glottal stop and syllabification sections), it is
entered once at its first occurrence with a Notes-field cross-reference to the other page(s), per
the "no mechanical restatement" convention used in the Tagalog and Hawaiian established files — this
is not the same as the drill-repetition the convention is meant to exclude, since each reappearance
is doing genuine illustrative work for a different grammar point, so the cross-references are kept
rather than silently dropped. No Trigger/Focus column was needed for this file: this page range
(Ch. 1–2) is introductory/phonological and contains no verb forms whose gloss depends on a
trigger/focus affix choice (that system is the subject of Ch. 8, out of this file's range) — per the
dispatch's own expectation.

**Hawaii/Hawaii-diaspora flag (flagship finding for this project).** Chapter 1, Section 1.1 (printed
p. 2 / PDF p. 27) states explicitly: *"Ilocanos are perhaps the most migratory of the Philippine
ethnic groups. They have settled in many other parts of the world, forming sizable communities in
Brunei, Singapore, Hong Kong, Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, and most urban centers in the United States and
Canada. The largest concentrations of Ilocanos in the United States are in California, Alaska, and
especially Hawaii, where one fifth of the total population is composed of Ilocanos (Espiritu 1977,
Foronda 1978). In Hawaii, Ilocano has been taught in the schools in bilingual education programs as
the medium of instruction, and in the universities as a foreign language."* This is a substantial,
directly-stated Hawaii-contact data point — one fifth of Hawaii's total population given as
Ilocano-descended as of the cited 1977/1978 sources, with Ilocano (not just Hawaiian or Tagalog)
used as a medium of bilingual instruction in Hawaii schools and taught as a foreign language at the
university level. This sits alongside the existing Hawaiian-language-file University-of-Hawaiʻi
Ilocano/Tagalog pedagogical references already on record and should be cross-referenced in any
future Hawaii-contact synthesis work across this project's languages.

**Slang flag.** Section 2.1.3 (The affricates, printed p. 13) glosses *dióga* as 'breast, **slang**'
— the source itself explicitly marks this item as slang register, the only such explicit tag
encountered in this page range. Flagged per this project's slang-synthesis mandate even though this
is a grammar-reference (not slang-corpus) source.

### Front matter — Curriculum Vitae (PDF pp. 6–7, printed roman vi–vii), summarized

Not tabled as vocabulary (biographical, English-language, per dispatch instruction). Key facts: Carl
Ralph Galvez Rubino, born Dec. 16, 1968, Chicago. B.A. Spanish and Linguistics, UCLA (1987–91); M.A.
(Jan. 1995) and Ph.D. (June 1997) Linguistics, UCSB. Taught UCSB Ling 40 "Intensive Conversational
Tagalog" (1/96–3/96) and UCSB Ling 40 "Intensive Ilocano" (1/97–3/97). Fieldwork languages listed:
Akoue Baule (UCLA), Yup'ik (UCSB), Pangasinan (Philippines). Publications include Rubino 1994
("Against the notion of unidirectionality in lexeme genesis"), Rubino 1995 ("On the structure of a
polysynthetic mental lexicon... in Ilocano", his M.A. thesis), Rubino 1995 ("Preferred argument
structure in Central Alaskan Yup'ik"), and Rubino 1996 ("Morphological integrity in Ilocano",
*Studies in Language* 20:3:333-366). Colloquium titles of note for this project: "The lexicalization
of olfaction in Ilocano" (1994), "Transitivity and topicality in Ilocano" (1995), "Ilocano
lexicography" (1996), "Onomatopoetic morphology in Ilocano" (1997). Works and Publications also
lists an unpublished ms., "A root based dictionary of the Ilocano language" (UCSB ms.) — distinct
from the companion dictionary volume already present in this project's `source_reference/` folder
("Ilocano dictionary and grammar... (Rubino, Carl R. Galvez)"), which is plausibly a later, published
descendant of this same ms.

### Front matter — Abstract (PDF p. 11, printed roman viii), summarized

No Ilocano vocabulary; English prose only. States the dissertation's goals: a summary of modern
spoken and written Ilocano morphology/syntax usable by non-linguists and by Ilocano pedagogy/language
study; interlinearized glosses; discourse data from spoken and written sources; analysis organized
around the core/ergative/absolutive/oblique distinction rather than European subject/object
terminology.

**Dialectal note (Sec. 1.1, p.1/2):** the orthographic symbol *e* represents two contrastive phonemes
(mid front and high back unrounded) in the southern dialects — La Unión, Abra, Pangasinan, Tarlac,
Nueva Ecija — but only one phoneme in the northern dialects — Ilocos Norte, Ilocos Sur. This is a
region-level phonological (not lexical) distinction stated directly by the source, cross-referenced
in Chapter 2's vowel section (see below); no individual vocabulary item below is tagged with a
specific Attested Region, since the words themselves are used dialect-wide and only their
pronunciation differs.

**Genetic-affiliation note (Sec. 1.1, p.1):** a reproduced Reid (1989:57) genetic tree places Ilocano
within Northern Cordilleran > Meso-Cordilleran, as sister to Bontok (implied by the tree position
marked with an arrow), alongside Northern Cordilleran sister languages Gaddang, Ibanag, Itawis,
Malaueg, Yogad (Cagayan Valley branch) and Isneg, Atta, Kalinga, Agta, Paranan (Northeastern Luzon
branch); these are language names, not Ilocano vocabulary items, so not tabled as entries.

Ilocano* (front matter + Ch. 1 Introduction + Ch. 2 Phonology), corresponding to the dispatch's
assigned "pp. 1–55" range once the corrected printed-to-PDF offset (+25, not +26) is applied — PDF

project-wide tracking: (1) a direct, sourced Hawaii-diaspora statistic (one fifth of Hawaii's 1977/78
population Ilocano-descended, with Ilocano used as a school medium of instruction and university
foreign-language offering in Hawaii) in Ch. 1 §1.1; and (2) an explicit in-source slang tag on
*dióga* 'breast, slang' in Ch. 2 §2.1.3.

syntactic position and intonation**, which is a striking and directly slang-relevant finding. A
second explicit register distinction is given for the sentence-final particle *wen* used after
commands: the source states outright that this use "is a very informal particle and is not used
when addressing elders," and further notes its vowel is phonetically distinct (and lengthened) from
the confirmatory adverb *wen* 'yes' — a form/register split worth flagging for conlang slang
derivation (an informality marker realized partly through *vowel quality and length*, not just
word choice). Representative example sentences are reproduced for each item (not exhaustively —

item are kept, matching the density needed to establish usage conditions without bulk-transcribing

## §9 Chapter opening — Introduction and scope

**Patch note (2026-10-30):** this chapter's own opening page (PDF p.345, printed p.320 — the Ch.9

Ilocano has two major classes of adverbials, differentiated by their morphosyntactic behavior:
**enclitics**, which phonologically fuse to host words, and **movable adverbs and particles**, full
words which may occupy various sentence positions. The three enclitics =(e)n, =(n)to, and =(n)sa
exhibit allomorphy depending on the consonantal status of the preceding word's last sound; these
are written fused to their host in standard orthography and are henceforth called "fusional
enclitics." The other adverbial particles, though they also normally attach to other constituents
semantically and morphologically, are written as separate words. Adverbial particles register the
speaker's responsibility/attitude toward a predication, generally following the predicate; they may
also modify nominals, with intensification differing depending on whether the particle precedes or
follows the modified noun phrase.

## §9.3 Means of adverbial expression (non-particle)

The source states there are **four separate ways** Ilocano denotes adverbial-type expressions
(no single unified "adverb" word class is posited, since the lexemes translating English adverbs do
not share consistent morphosyntactic behavior): (1) an adjective + ligature *(ng)a*; (2) an
adjective/verb linked to the predicate as a prepositional phrase with the oblique marker *iti*;
(3) a transitive verb denoting the adverbial notion directly (e.g. *Igaedna ti agadal* 'He studied
diligently', lit. 'He made-diligent the studying'); (4) a closed class of adverb-like lexemes that
do **not** require the ligature *(ng)a* to link to a constituent (and can also function as
adjectives), e.g. *Nagbuteng ti asona ta nagistayan ginetteb...* 'His dog was frightened because the
wild boar nearly bit off his neck.' p. 353–355

## §9.4 Sentence-final particles

| apo | 'sir' / 'maam' — honorific address term; also colloquial exclamation of familiarity/amazement | polite particle / address term (also functions as an interjection) | **dual register: formal/polite honorific AND colloquial/familiar exclamation**, depending on syntactic position and intonation | — | contemporary (source published 1997) | — | — | grammar_reference | n/a | verified | §9.7, pp. 382–383. **This is the clearest explicit politeness-tier particle in this chapter.** Core sense: expresses respect on behalf of the speaker for the addressee, glossed 'sir'/'maam'. *Wen, apo.* 'Yes, sir.' *Haan nga maparitan, apo.* 'It is not forbidden, sir.'

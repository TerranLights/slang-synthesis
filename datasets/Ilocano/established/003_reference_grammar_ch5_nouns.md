## 5.1 Number — Plural Formation

**Patch note (2026-10-30):** this chapter's own opening page (PDF p.98, printed p.73 — the Ch.5
intro paragraph and the first examples of §5.1) fell into a 1-page gap between this file's
assigned range and sibling file 002's, and was originally skipped. Directly vision-read and
inserted below by the orchestrating session after the wave landed.

Ilocano nouns encode concrete things or abstract entities, differentiated from other lexical
categories by their referencing role in discourse or by their morphological patterning (plural
formation, position with regard to articles/demonstratives, morphological derivation). Nouns are
usually pluralized simply via the appropriate plural article (`ti` singular / `dagiti` plural) or
demonstrative, without changing the noun's shape. To emphasize individuation or distributivity,
nouns may undergo CVC reduplication of the initial sound sequence of the root instead, forming two
identical closed syllables in the reduplicated form; vowel-initial roots are pluralized by
reduplicating the initial glottal stop, vowel, and following consonant (orthographic hyphen marks
the word-internal glottal stop).

**Example sentence (p.75):** *Adda dagiti kayo a ka-kasla luppo laeng ti puon=da.* 'There are trees
with trunks that are just like (the size of) thighs.' — illustrates articles/demonstratives/adjectives
taking plural morphology when modifying a plural noun left in singular (non-reduplicated) form.

**Footnote 1 (p.74, Benton 1976):** vowel-initial kin terms have two distinct plural forms — the
medial-gemination forms cited in the table above, and CVC-reduplicated forms with or without medial
gemination (e.g. *ub-ub(b)ing*, *an-an(n)ak*, *as-as(s)awa*). The CVC-reduplicated plurals mark
distributive plurality; the medial-geminate plurals mark neutral plurality. The CVC-reduplicated
forms are nearly obsolete in speech.

## 5.2 Gender of Nouns

gender; the masculine form is preferred for gender-neutral reference.

### 5.3.1 The suffix -an

A very versatile nominalizer. Used with verbal stems to indicate the instrument used for carrying
out the action; with a few roots to indicate location; with (C)V-reduplicated verbal stems to form
locative/resultative nouns (often lexicalized); the affix RCV-`an` forms nominals denoting a
referent that must undergo the action/state of the stem; with body parts it specifies large/abnormal
size and historically derives names for insects/plants/fish/animals; with verb stems it forms
aspect gerunds; and it may be reduplicated with a few roots to form further lexicalized items.

### 5.3.7 Sinan-

The prefix `sinan-` is used to add a diminutive or deprecatory quality. With certain roots, `sinan-`

### 5.4.1 Nominalized verbals, aspectual gerunds

Ilocano gerunds are used to nominalize an action, or designate 'the way an action is performed,'
'the fact that an action is performed,' or 'the reason why an action is performed.' The actual uses
of the aspectual gerunds depend on the inherent semantics of the verb root. Because many nominalized
verbs (aspectual gerunds) designate the location of the action specified by the verb root, they have
often been referred to as "locative nominalizations" — the source flags this categorization as too
simplistic, since many non-locative uses of aspectual gerunds exist (source/reason uses, etc.).

Gerundial forms of verbs are used whenever a verbal concept must be nominalized. Ilocano does not,
however, require all verbs to be nominalized whenever they appear in a nominal slot (after an
existential, demonstrative, or article). Sometimes an existential, demonstrative, or article
suffices in nominalizing a verb in discourse: *Sino dagiti immay?* 'Who were the ones that came?'
*Adda nakadungpar kaniana.* 'Something happened to bump into her.'

However, aspectless gerund forms are preferred when the speaker wishes to emphasize the nominal
concept (cause, result, means, etc.) of the action while maintaining the verbal aspect (underlying
intransitive actor-focus verb form, as opposed to the bare nominal root). The gerund forms are then
used to verbalize the entity or concept of the verbal action: *Ania ti makunada iti panagkunniber
dagiti politiko?* 'What do they say about the corruption of the politicians?' *Nalukagak iti
pannakatapik ti abagak.* 'I was awakened by the tapping on my shoulder.' *Nagilad manen ket
pinagnadnadna ti pannakaulawna.* 'He rubbed his body (against wall, etc.) again and calmed down his
dizziness (being light headed).' *Nariknana ti pannakatektek ni Sadiri iti panagtakderna iti
pantalan bayat ti panangur-urayna iti panagluas ti bapor.* 'He felt the uneasiness (nervous shaking)
of Sadiri from the way he was standing on the pier while waiting for the departure of the ship.'
*Yur-urayna laeng ti panaglamiis ti ulok.* 'He is just waiting for my head to cool down.' *Nariknana
ti irarasuk ti darana.* 'He felt the flaring up of his blood (due to anger).'

Although these gerund forms do not inflect for perfective aspect, they may be accompanied by root
CVC reduplication to emphasize the continuity or incompleteness of an action: *Naallingagmi ti
panagsarsaritatayo kenni Rhea.* 'We managed to hear what you and Rhea were talking about.'

Gerund forms may be compared to bare nominal root forms; gerunds encode more information (i.e.
whether the action was voluntary, accidental, etc.) and are thus used when there is more
commitment to specifying the nature of the action: *Ania ti subalitda?* 'What is their answer?'
(bare nominal root) vs. *In-inut a nabaybay-anna ti panagsubalitna kadagiti suratna.* 'Little by
little, he neglected answering her letters.' (gerund form for a volitional active verb)

### 5.4.3 Various properties of gerunds

Unlike aspectless gerunds, nominalized verbs can occur in the perfective aspect: *Naut-ot pay laeng
ti kanawan nga imak a nangalaanda iti darak.* 'My right hand from where they took blood still
hurts.' Like verbs, nominalized verbs may undergo CV reduplication of their roots to specify a
plural actor — this use is not mandatory and is most often encountered in literature: *Pulutan ken
serbesa ti nagsasanguanda iti kalalainganna ti kalawa a sala.* 'They placed (in front of the
guests) beer and beer snacks in the living room whose size (capacity) was just right.' *Ammona
lattan a pallot ti pagsasaritaanda.* 'She just knows that they are talking about cockfights.'

Aspectual gerunds, like nouns, may be preceded by plural articles to indicate the plurality of
their referent: *Kaniak a biang, nasken a mausar ti ultimo a sipinga pundo ti ili kadagiti
nakaisangratanna.* 'In my opinion, it is necessary to use the last penny of the town's funds for
the things they (the funds) are intended for.'

Like verbs, aspectual gerunds may undergo partial reduplication (initial CVC) to encode imperfective
aspect (continual or habitual actions), as the following three sentences — taken from the short
story "Pusa" ('Cat') by Benjamin M. Pascual — demonstrate: *Kasla dua a beggang a berde dagiti mata
ti pusa iti panagsarsarakna iti tedda a kanen.* 'The eyes of the cat were like two green embers
looking for the leftover food.' *Ngem nupay nagsukisok, napaluksawan ta awan met nagyan iti sabut a
kadawyan a pangpanganganna.* 'But although is searched, it was disgusted because there was nothing
inside the coconut shell that it usually eats from.' *Nagbaeng ti pusa idi inyasidegna ti agongna
iti silulukat a kahon a pagiduldulinan dagiti apponna iti sida.* 'The cat sneezed when it put its
nose close to the open box where his grandchildren usually keep the food.'

Incomplete (non-punctual) aspect may also be shown with gerunds carrying the future enclitic
`=nto`, specifying a hypothetical or contemplated progressive action in the future: *Diak kayat nga
addanto pakasasawanda kaniak.* 'I don't want there to be a reason for them to be saying things
about me.'

## 5.7 Possession

Ilocano has four ways of signalling possession: (1) possessed noun + NP designating possessor; (2)
possessed noun + ergative pronominal enclitic; (3) *bagi-* or *kukua-* possessive pronoun + ligature
*(ng)a* + possessed noun; (4) the existential *adda* construction.

Normally, a possessor follows the possessed referent; possessive constructions are done simply by
juxtaposing possessum and possessor. A possessor may be encoded with a full noun phrase (the
appropriate article introducing the relevant nominal possessor) or an ergative enclitic pronoun:
*ti balay ni Maria* 'Maria's house' / *ti balay=na* 'her house'; *diay tulbek ti anak=ko* 'My
child's key' / *diay tulbek=na* 'his key'.

Ilocano has two sets of full possessive pronouns, the *bagi-* series and the *kukua-* series. These
possessive pronouns may stand alone as full predicates to indicate the possession of an absolutive
argument.

The existential *adda* in predicate position may also indicate possession. In the *adda*
construction, the possessor appears with ergative case marking if it is a pronoun, or as a full NP
following the possessed noun: *Adda aso ni Pedro.* 'Pedro has a dog.' / *Adda aso=na.* 'He has a
dog.' To specify current possession with *adda*, the possessor referent appears as an oblique noun
phrase immediately following the possessed noun: *Adda ti tulbek ken-ni Gracia.* 'The key is with
Gracia.' / *Adda ti tulbek kenkuana.* 'The key is with her.'

between the garage and the granary.' *Awanen iti sikigak.* 'He is no longer at my side.' *iti
nasurok a sangagasut* 'over (more than) one hundred'.

The cardinal reference points in Ilocano are *laud* 'west', *daya* 'east', *amianan* 'north', and
*abagatan* 'south'. Ilocano speakers are prone to express location in terms of the cardinal
reference points; different parts of a house, for instance, are sometimes indicated with reference
to these points: *Nagatangak idiay laud.* 'I offered food to the dead spirits in the west (part of
the house).' *Uneg* 'inside' often prefixes to locative nouns to express interior location:
*Saanak a maturog iti uneg-simbaan.* 'I don't sleep in church.'

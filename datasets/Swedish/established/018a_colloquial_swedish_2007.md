**Split-file note:** This book's full extraction is large enough to require splitting into multiple numbered files per the spec's "don't let a single file grow unbounded" rule. This is Part A (`009a`, Units 1–6, pp. 20–110). Part B (`009b`) covers Units 7–11, Part C (`009c`) covers Units 12–17 plus the Grammar summary and a spot-check of the end-of-book glossaries. All three parts share the front-matter notes below.

**Source citation:** Jennie Ahlgren, Philip Holmes and Gunilla Serin, *Colloquial Swedish: The Complete Course for Beginners*, Routledge "Colloquial" series, 3rd edition, first published 2007 (this printing: Taylor & Francis e-Library, 2006 digital edition). 353 PDF pages; printed page numbers run roughly `PDF page − 9` from the Preface onward (verified against the book's own Contents listing). Page citations below use the book's own printed page numbers unless noted otherwise.

**Relationship to the 1996 edition:** An earlier (1996, 2nd/"Revised") edition of the same title is extracted separately into a sibling file in this same `established/` folder (`017_colloquial_swedish_1996.md`), by a parallel subagent. The two editions are expected to overlap substantially in core grammar sequencing (both are "Colloquial Swedish" from the same three co-authors), but this 2007 edition's own preface states explicitly that for the third edition **all Texts and Dialogues and many Factual texts are new**, the unit count was reduced to seventeen, and Internet URLs were added — so vocabulary, example sentences, and cultural content differ materially even where the underlying grammar point is the same. This file extracts the 2007 edition fully and on its own terms; no attempt was made to de-duplicate against the 1996 file since that extraction was running in parallel and its content wasn't available for comparison at extraction time.

### Grammar points

- **Verbs don't inflect for person/number.** Present tense Swedish verbs (e.g. *är*, *heter*) have one form across jag/du/han/hon/vi/ni/de — unlike English's "is" vs. "are."
- **Two question types.** (1) Yes/no questions via subject–verb inversion (*Är han svensk?*); (2) v-word questions (most interrogatives start with v- in Swedish: *vad*, *var*, *vem*) with the same inverted V-S order after the question word.
- **En-words vs. ett-words.** Swedish's two-way noun-gender/article system: *en vän*, *ett land*. ~75% of nouns are en-gender, including most nouns for people/higher animals/days/parts of day; gender for "thing" nouns is largely unpredictable and must be learned per noun. *Den* (en-words) / *det* (ett-words) is used for "it" when referring to a specific known noun; *det* alone is used when gender is unknown or the referent isn't yet given.
- **Short answers.** Structure: *Ja/Nej, det* + verb + subject pronoun (+ *inte*). E.g. *Är du svensk?* → *Ja, det är jag.* If the question's main verb is not *vara* ("be"), *ha* ("have"), or a modal, the short answer substitutes a form of *göra* ("do"): *Talar du svenska?* → *Ja, det gör jag.*
- **No genitive "of" with quantities.** Swedish has no equivalent of English "a cup of coffee" — it's simply *en kopp kaffe* (noun + noun, no linking preposition).
- **Compound nouns.** *en smörgås med ost* = *en ostsmörgås* — when forming a compound, a final unstressed *-a* on the first element is dropped (*skinka* → *skink|smörgås*).
- **Capitalization — a genuine register/convention divergence from English.** Swedish uses lower-case where English capitalizes: weekday/month/festival names (*måndag*, *januari*, *midsommar*), and nationality/language/religion/political-affiliation nouns and adjectives (*engelsman*, *amerikan*, *svensk*, *katolik*, *socialdemokrat*).
- **Pragmatics of *tack* and *varsågod*, flagged explicitly as colloquial/conversational register content by the book itself.** *Tack* functions as both "thank you" and, sentence-finally, as "please" (*Kan jag få en kopp kaffe, tack?*); several fixed variants exist (*Tack så mycket!*, *Tack ska du ha!*). *Varsågod* (compared explicitly by the book to German *Bitte!*) is used when handing something over, inviting someone in, or offering a seat (*Varsågod och sitt!*) — and the book notes it can also be used **impolitely, as a demand** (*Varsågod och gör som jag säger!* — "Do as I say!"), a register/pragmatics nuance worth flagging as genuinely book-marked, not inferred.
- **Pronunciation note — consonant-dropping in rapid/casual speech, explicitly marked by the book.** Several very common words drop their final consonant in natural speech: *jag* → *ja*, *här* → *hä*, *det* → *de*, *är* → *e*, *och* → *å*, *med* → *me*. The book gives a worked example of these combining: *Det här är min mamma* is pronounced, run together, roughly "dehäämimmama."

### Grammar points

- **Present/infinitive verb formation.** Stem ending in a vowel + -r for present (*börja/r*); stem ending in a consonant + -er (*åk/er*). Infinitives end in -a or another vowel. *Vara* ("be") has an irregular infinitive (present *är*); *göra* ("do/make") already ends in -r in its stem.
- **Zero article with nationality/occupation/affiliation nouns**, extending Unit 1: *Nils är lärare* ("Nils is a teacher") — no article — but an article reappears once the noun is qualified: *Han är en god katolik* ("He is a good Catholic").
- **No preposition before clock times**: *klockan åtta* ("at eight o'clock") — Swedish needs no equivalent of "at."
- **Straight vs. inverted word order.** Straight = subject first, then verb (*Hon bor i Minnesota*). Inverted = any non-subject (commonly a time/place expression) fronted, forcing the subject to follow the verb (*Ofta börjar hon klockan åtta* — lit. "Often begins she at eight"). English fronts such expressions too but doesn't invert, a genuine word-order divergence worth noting for a derived-conlang's own constituent-order rules.
- **Full statement/question word-order paradigm** (directly useful for conlang constituent-order modeling): straight statements (Subject–Verb), inverted statements (Front–Verb–Subject), v-questions (V-word–Verb–Subject), yes/no questions (Verb–Subject, no front slot), commands (Verb only, no subject slot).
- **Yes/no particle system is more differentiated than English**, and explicitly flagged by the book as colloquial/pragmatic nuance rather than simple translation equivalents: *ja* (plain yes), *nej* (plain no), *jo* (contradicts a negative question — "Yes, I do!" in response to "You don't study Swedish, do you?" — explicitly compared to French *si*), *javisst* (stronger agreement, "of course"), *jaså* (registers surprise: "oh really?"/"you don't say"), *jaha* (registers mere acknowledgment of having heard, not surprise).
- **Greeting register ladder, explicit in the source.** *Hej* = usual friendly/informal greeting; *Goddag* (pron. "godda") = formal "How do you do?"/"Good morning." Partings: *hej då / hej, hej / hej så länge* = informal; *Adjö* (pron. "ajö") = formal. This is a clean three-register minimal pair-set (informal / formal greeting, informal / formal parting) worth preserving as a model for a derived conlang's own register ladder.
- **Numerals 0–12** given as a full paradigm; *en/ett* varies by gender but the counting sequence uses *ett* (*ett, två, tre...*).
- **Derogatory-term flag, explicit in the source's own vocabulary list**: the book glosses *same* ("Sami person") and adds the explicit note "(Lapp is derogatory)" — a direct, source-marked taboo/pejorative-term annotation rather than an inference, naming the dispreferred exonym without ever listing it as a headword itself.

### Grammar points

- **Adverb placement differs systematically from English.** Sentence-scope adverbs (*alltid*, *ofta*, *inte*, *aldrig*, *sällan*) always sit directly after VERB1 in a Swedish main clause, regardless of whether English would place the equivalent before or after its first verb: *Bill tar aldrig taxi* ("Bill never takes a taxi") vs. *Bill kan aldrig ta taxi* ("Bill can never take a taxi") — in both, Swedish *aldrig* follows VERB1 immediately. In inverted statements and in questions, the adverb follows the subject, which itself follows the verb: *Åker du inte till Helsingfors?*
- **The s-genitive.** Swedish forms a genitive by adding -s to the noun, with **no apostrophe** (*Sveriges huvudstad* = "Sweden's capital"); the -s attaches after the end article where relevant (*universitetets rektor*); a noun following an s-genitive noun never itself takes an end article (*bibliotekets läsesal*); nouns already ending in -s take an apostrophe instead of another -s (*Mattias' far*).
- **Three negatives, explicitly register-differentiated by the source**: *inte* (overwhelmingly the default, spoken and written), *ej* (sign/notice register: *Ej rökning*, *Ej ingång*), *icke* (compound-forming, e.g. *icke-rökare*, *icke-kristen*). This is one of the book's clearest explicit register ladders for a single semantic slot ("not") and is directly useful for modeling a derived conlang's own register-marked negation forms.
- **Numerals 13–1000**, several with explicit casual-pronunciation respellings noted by the book (*fjorton* → "fjoton," *arton* → "arton" [exception, full form], *tjugo* → "tjugo/tjugə/tjugi" showing a spoken-register vowel-reduction continuum, *fyrti(o)* → "fötti").
- **Days/parts-of-day vocabulary** given with casual contracted pronunciations throughout (*söndag* → "sönnda," *måndagen* → "månndan"), reinforcing the book's running theme of marking colloquial contraction as a distinct layer from the citation form.
- **Telling time** — a full set of clock-expression idioms (*halv nio* = "half past eight," *kvart i/över* = "quarter to/past"), plus the 24-hour clock convention used in official notices.
- **Three distinct motion/travel verbs, a genuine semantic-field distinction worth preserving for conlang design**: *åka* (general "go/travel by vehicle," the default), *resa* (implies **long-distance or foreign/international travel** specifically — *Han reser utomlands*), *gå* (a "false friend" — primarily "to walk," not a general translation of "go"; the book gives *Vi går till staden* = "we walk to town" as the core sense, with *gå* only used loosely for "go" when paired with a modal + directional phrase, as in *Bill ska till Stockholm*).
- **Two-verb (modal) constructions.** VERB1 (a modal like *kan*, *måste*, *ska*, or similar verbs like *tänker* "to be thinking of") lacks the usual -r present-tense ending pattern seen on ordinary verbs in some cases and is followed by an infinitive VERB2: *Kan jag få en enkel till Lund?* The sentence-pattern table extends to show where VERB2 and any adverb sit relative to VERB1/subject.
- **Regional-dialect-word-origin naming, explicitly marked by the source**: "pågatågen" (a regional train line in Skåne) is explicitly glossed by the book as named after "the best known word in Skåne dialect, *påg* = *pojke*" ("boy") — a genuine dialectal-lexical item cited by name, not merely alluded to.

### Grammar points

- **Combined two-verb + adverb sentence pattern.** Extends Unit 3: Front – VERB1 – (Subject) – Adverb – VERB2 – etc. In inverted word order, subject and adverb both sit between VERB1 and VERB2 (*Brukar han alltid köra bil?*).
- **Objects and MPT-expressions complete the sentence-pattern template.** Full pattern given: FRONT + VERB1 + (SUBJECT) + ADVERB + VERB2 + OBJECT/COMPLEMENT + MPT-EXPRESSION. Multiple MPT-expressions at the end default to manner–place–time order (not always, but as a working default): *Jan kör långsamt längs kusten på kvällen.* An MPT-expression fronted for emphasis (only one at a time) triggers inversion, same as any other fronted non-subject.
- **Possessive pronouns share their form with possessive adjectives** — *hennes* means both "her" and "hers" with no distinct form, unlike English.
- **Seven noun plural-ending classes** systematized from examples encountered so far: `-or` (drops final -a, e.g. *kyrka→kyrkor*), `-ar` (e.g. *buss→bussar*; *skotte→skottar* drops -e), `-er` (*turist→turister*), `-r` (*sko→skor*), `-n` (ett-words ending in a vowel, *äpple→äpplen*), no ending (*tåg→tåg*, *slott→slott*, *walesare→walesare*), `-s` (loanwords like *container→containers*). The book notes Unit 5 will show how to predict which class a given noun falls into.
- **Indefinite adjective declension.** Basic form with en-words; +`-t` with ett-words; +`-a` with plurals of either gender. Irregular patterns: *vacker→vackert→vackra* (drops -e in plural), *gammal→gammalt→gamla* (drops both -a and -m in plural). Some adjectives are indeclinable (*bra*).
- **"Please"-request constructions beyond sentence-final *tack*.** *Vill du vara (så) snäll och...* ("Will you be so kind and..."), with escalating politeness/hedging variants (*Skulle du kunna vara så snäll och...*, *Skulle du vilja vara så snäll och...*) — a graded politeness-marking system worth modeling for a conlang's own register system.
- **Three verbs translate English "to be," each with a distinct sense, confirmed and extended from the book's own explicit discussion**: *vara* (default copula), *finnas* (existential — "there is/exists," *Finns det något öl kvar?*), *ligga* (locative, literally "to lie" — *Lund ligger i Skåne*). The book gives a worked minimal-pair example: *Finns det ett apotek i närheten?* ("Does a pharmacy exist nearby?") vs. an answer using *ligga* to specify location (*Det ligger om hörnet*).
- **Points of the compass — five parallel formation strategies** given as a systematic list: adjective (*norra Sverige*), prefix (*Sydeuropa*), noun with *i* (*i norr*), *norr om* ("north of"), and *-ut* suffix for "-wards" (*norrut*).
- **Ordinal numbers 1st–20th/30th/100th**, with the regular *-de*/*-nde* suffix pattern made explicit beyond the irregular 1st–12th forms.
- **Centuries/decades formed with *-talet*.** *1500-talet* = "the sixteenth century" (note the off-by-one shift from the English ordinal-century convention); *-tal* also used for decades (*40-talet* = "the 1940s") and vague round numbers (*ett trettital studenter* ≈ "about thirty students"). Century-compounds insert *-s-* before the second element (*1900-talskonst*).
- **Compounding rule, generalized**: gender of a compound = gender of its *last* element; linking elements sometimes insert *-s-* (*universitetsbibliotek*) or drop a final vowel (*bondstuga* drops the -e of *bonde*); compounds carry two stresses.

### Grammar points

- **Fronting.** Any non-subject (MPT-expression most commonly, less often an object) can move to the sentence-initial slot, always forcing subject–verb inversion. The book characterizes fronted material as typically either (a) setting a scene/situation (*I Lund...*) or (b) linking back to a previous clause (*Sedan..., Då...*), and notes it can occasionally be used for contrastive emphasis (*Kaffe dricker ni sex gånger om dagen och tårta äter ni jämt* — "Coffee you drink six times a day, and cake you eat constantly").
- **End-article exception for nouns in unstressed -el/-er/-or.** These take just -n (not -en) in the definite form (*en cykel → cykeln*) and drop that final vowel before adding a plural ending (*en cykel → två cyklar*); the parallel ett-word pattern drops the vowel before -et (*ett exempel → exemplet*).
- **Five rules for predicting noun plurals**, systematizing the seven classes from Unit 4: (1) stressed-final-syllable en-words and stressed-final-vowel ett-words add -er; (2) unstressed-final -a en-words drop -a, add -or; (3) unstressed-final -e en-words drop -e, add -ar; (4) unstressed-final-vowel ett-words add -n; (5) consonant-final ett-words take no ending. Additional fixed-ending clues given: -are/-er endings → no plural ending; -(n)ing → -ar; -het → -er.
- **Four verb conjugations systematized via the imperative-as-stem principle.** Conjugation I: stem ends in -a, infinitive = stem, present = stem + -r (*skala/*). Conjugations II/IV: stem ends in consonant, infinitive = stem + -a, present = stem + -er (*ställ/a*, *skriv/a*). Conjugation III: stem ends in a vowel other than -a, infinitive = stem, present = stem + -r (*strö/*). R-stem and L-stem verbs (stem ending in -r/-l) are a Conjugation II subtype that takes **no** ending in the present tense (*kör/, lär/, mal/*) — an exception worth flagging for conlang verb-paradigm modeling.
- **Imperative-softening politeness idiom, a second explicit instance of the graded-politeness pattern from Unit 4**: *Skala potatisen, är du bussig!* / *Hjälp mig laga middag, är du snäll!* — literally "...if you'd be kind/nice," used to soften a bare imperative. The book cross-references its earlier (p. 72) "please" discussion, confirming this is a recognized recurring register strategy, not a one-off phrase.
- **Telephone-register phrasebook**, a self-contained register list for a specific social domain (making/answering calls): *Det är upptaget* ("the line is engaged"), *Var god dröj!* ("just a moment" — formal imperative with *var god*, itself a fixed formal-register politeness marker), *lyfta på/lägga på luren* ("pick up/put down the receiver"), *skicka en SMS* ("send a text message" — confirming "SMS" as the ordinary Swedish term, not a marked anglicism).
- **Full personal-pronoun paradigm (subject + object forms), with an explicit informal-spelling note.** The book's own footnote on *mig/mej*, *dig/dej*, *dem/dom*: "Alternative informal spellings which reflect the pronunciation" — directly confirms (not merely implies) that these spelled-as-pronounced forms are a recognized colloquial-register convention in written Swedish, not just a phonetic transcription aid. *Ni* is listed as both formal "you" (singular, polite) and plain "you" (plural) — a register-conditioned polysemy worth modeling.
- **Indefinite adjective agreement extends beyond simple noun modification**: the gender/plural markers -t/-a also surface on *någon/inget/ingen*, *all/allt/alla*, *vilken/vilket/vilka*, *annan/annat/andra* when paired with a noun, and on adjectives agreeing with the pronoun *det* or with an infinitive-phrase subject (*Att dansa är roligt* — "Dancing is fun"), which the book notes has **no direct Swedish equivalent to the English "-ing" gerund form**.

### Grammar points

- **Objects vs. complements, formally distinguished.** Direct objects (DO) and indirect objects (IO) pattern similarly to English in ordering (*Jag gav Nils boken* / *Jag gav boken till Nils*). Complements appear after "empty verbs" (*vara*, *bli*, *heta*) needing semantic completion, and split into subject complements (agreeing with the subject: noun number, or adjective agreement with en/ett/plural) and object complements (agreeing with the object: *Det gjorde dem arga* — "It made them angry").
- **Indefinite adjective form — five additional irregular sub-patterns beyond the basic -t/-a rule (p. 68/90):** stressed-vowel-final adjectives add -tt (*ny→nytt*; also *blå, grå, fri*); vowel+d-final adjectives drop -d and add -tt (*bred→brett*; also *god, glad, röd*); consonant+d-final adjectives drop -d and add -t (*hård→hårt*; also *mild, vild, ond*); -er/-el-final adjectives drop the -e before adding -a in the plural (*vacker→vackra*; also *mager, nykter, säker*; and *enkel→enkla*; also *acceptabel, flexibel*); -en-final adjectives behave like -er ones but also drop -n in the neuter (*öppen→öppet→öppna*; also *egen, ledsen*). *Liten* and *gammal* remain irregular outliers requiring individual memorization.
- **Seven irregular noun-plural sub-patterns**, extending Unit 5's regular-plural rules: (a) root-vowel-change + -er (*hand→händer*, *stad→städer*, *bok→böcker*, *fot→fötter*); (b) vowel-final nouns of either gender adding -r (*bakelse→bakelser*, *sko→skor*); (c) vowel+n/l/r-final nouns dropping the final vowel before the plural ending (*fågel→fåglar*, *sommar→somrar*); (d) -ium/-eum loanwords dropping -um before -er (*museum→museer*); (e) -um loanwords with alternative plural forms (*centrum→centrum/centrer/centra*); (f) a handful of highly irregular nouns (*öga→ögon*, *huvud→huvuden*, *öra→öron*); (g) recent English loanwords retaining English -s plurals (*containers, thrillers, musicals*).
- **Register cluster for agreement/disagreement**, laid out explicitly by the book as a functional set rather than a single phrase: *Det tycker jag* / *Det tror jag* (opinion-based agreement) vs. *Det är sant/rätt/riktigt* / *Det stämmer* (fact-based agreement) vs. *Det håller jag med om* (explicit agreement-with-a-person). Doubt is hedged with *kanske*, *möjligen*, a tag-question (*Tycker du det?*), or *Det vet jag inte*; the book notes *tyvärr* ("unfortunately") is specifically used to soften/ameliorate a disagreement (*Tyvärr tycker jag inte det*) — a politeness-marking function for an otherwise plain adverb, directly useful for conlang pragmatic-marker design.
- **Demonstratives** take the noun's end article in addition to the demonstrative word itself: *den här soffan* ("this sofa"), *det här bordet*, *de här sofforna/borden*. A stressed form *'den/'det/'de* (marked with a preceding stress mark in the book) is noted as typically used with abstract nouns. Demonstratives can stand alone without a following noun (*Vad tycker du om den där?*).
- **Amplifiers (adverbs modifying an adjective or adverb).** *Mycket* ("very") + adjective structurally becomes part of the Complement slot; *mycket* + adverb becomes an MPT-expression (manner). *Mycket* alone can mean "a lot" (verb-modifying). A number of originally strongly negative/emphatic adjectives, converted to adverbs with -t, function as colloquial intensifiers through semantic bleaching: *förskräckligt* ("terribly"), *hemskt* ("awfully"), *väldigt* ("very"), *otroligt* ("unbelievably") — all exemplified by the book modifying positive predicates (*hemskt billiga* = "awfully cheap"), confirming the bleaching is productive, not an isolated idiom.
- **m/n spelling-doubling exception to the general short/long-vowel spelling rule** (first stated p. 10): words ending in single -m/-n after a short vowel keep a single consonant in the citation form (*dum, hem, rum, man, kan*) but double it when an inflectional ending is added between two vowels (*rum→rummet*, *man→mannen*). Conversely, a doubled m/n is simplified to one when a further consonant-initial ending is added (*gammal→gamla*, *sommar→somrar*, *nummer→numret*) — a genuine phonologically-conditioned orthographic alternation worth modeling for a conlang's own spelling-doubling conventions.

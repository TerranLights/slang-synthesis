# Swedish — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Historical Dictionary of Sweden (language/culture entries)

**Source:** Irene Scobbie, *Historical Dictionary of Sweden* (Historical Dictionaries of Europe
No. 48, 2nd edition, Scarecrow Press, 2006), selected cross-referenced dictionary entries:
**SWEDISH LANGUAGE** (pp. 215-216), **LANGUAGE** (p. 117, a pointer entry), **LAPPS (SAMI)**
(pp. 117-118), **STIERNHIELM, GEORG** (pp. 202-203), and the **PETERSSON-BERGER, WILHELM** entry's
aside on the Sami *jojka* (p. ~187). PDF confirmed with a genuine `pdftotext -layout` text layer
(`pdfinfo`: 359 pages; extracted ~126,800 words of running text) — no vision-reading needed, no
font-substitution cipher detected.

**Coverage note.** This is an academic historical/biographical encyclopedia (hundreds of entries
on people, events, and institutions), not a grammar or phrasebook — most entries (politics,
royalty, wars, specific authors' biographies) carry no language-pedagogical content relevant to
this project's slang-synthesis purpose and were correctly skipped. This file extracts the handful
of entries that are genuinely about the Swedish language itself, its dialectal basis, or a
linguistic minority — found via targeted searches for `dialect`, `slang`, `vernacular`,
`loanword`, `runic`, `orthography`, and the dedicated `LANGUAGE`/`SWEDISH LANGUAGE` cross-reference
entries. **No dedicated chapter on dialects or slang exists in this source** (it is organized as
an A-Z encyclopedia, not a running narrative with topic chapters) — the single **SWEDISH LANGUAGE**
entry is the closest equivalent and is the flagship find of this file. No handwritten marginalia
risk applies (real text layer, no vision-reading was performed).

## Grammar points

### Periodization of the Swedish language

The source gives an explicit historical staging (p. 215): **Old Swedish** (c. 800-1526), itself
subdivided into *Runic Swedish* (c. 800-1225, attested only in carved runic inscriptions using the
*futhark* alphabet), *Classical Old Swedish* (1225-1375), and *Younger Old Swedish* (1375-1526);
followed by **New Swedish** (1526-present), subdivided into *Early New Swedish* (before 1700) and
*Modern New Swedish* (after 1700). The 1526 boundary is explicitly tied to the first Swedish-
language New Testament translation that year. By the Viking period (800-1060), Swedish was "a
highly inflected language (resembling Modern Icelandic, which has preserved most of the Old Norse
characteristics)," but had discarded much of that inflectional system by the end of the medieval
period — a concrete, source-stated claim about the overall trajectory of Swedish morphological
simplification, useful context for why modern Swedish and modern Icelandic diverge so sharply in
inflectional complexity despite a shared Old Norse ancestor.

### Dialectal basis of the written/spoken standard

As flagged above, the modern standard is explicitly described as based principally on the **Svea
dialect** (Stockholm and the Lake Mälaren district) with incorporated features of the **Göta
dialect** (p. 216) — i.e., the standard language is itself a historical dialect blend, not a
dialect-neutral invention. The source credits 19th-century improved education and cheap popular
newspapers with later producing "a great measure of uniformity in the language nationally" and
narrowing the gap between spoken and written registers, a process it says "has increased under the
influence of radio and television."

### Bible translation as a linguistic standardization event

The 1541 Gustav Vasa Bible (the first complete Swedish Bible, following the 1526 New Testament)
"linguistically became an accepted model" — subsequent official Bible translations (Gustav Adolf
Bible 1618, Karl XII Bible 1703) "did not alter it substantially," and only the 20th-century
Gustav V Bible (1917) introduced more radical linguistic changes (p. 216). This mirrors the kind
of standardizing-text role Luther's Bible played for German — the source explicitly notes the
Petri brothers (translators of the Gustav Vasa Bible) had studied at Wittenberg and that the
translation "shows the influence of Luther's German version."

### Institutionalized linguistic purism: the Swedish Academy and its critics

Gustav III founded the **Swedish Academy** (*Svenska Akademien*) in 1786 explicitly "to work for
'purity, strength, and sublimity of the Swedish language'" (p. 216) — the Academy went on to
produce a standard grammar (1836) and begin the authoritative dictionary of Swedish (1890s). The
entry names a line of individual purists across three centuries who specifically warned against
foreign borrowing: Georg Stiernhielm (17th c.), Olof von Dalin (18th c.), and Viktor Rydberg
(19th c.) — the Stiernhielm entry elsewhere in this dictionary (pp. 202-203) independently confirms
this, noting he "went back to Old Icelandic literature and incorporated original Old Swedish
vocabulary into modern Swedish" and was known as the "Father of Swedish Poetry." The source
explicitly notes, however, that word-frequency studies suggest "the most frequent and constant
words in use are still native Swedish ones" despite these repeated borrowing waves — i.e., the
purists' anxiety and the actual core-vocabulary frequency data point in different directions, a
useful calibration point for any later claim in this project about loanword "flooding."

### Successive loanword waves by historical era (the vocabulary table above)

The SWEDISH LANGUAGE entry organizes its own loanword examples by *source language and era*
rather than by semantic field, a periodization worth preserving as structure rather than just as
individual vocabulary items: (1) Latin, with Christianity (*skriva*, *präst*, *biskop*, *mässa*);
(2) German, via the Hanseatic merchant colony in medieval Stockholm, covering society/government/
trade vocabulary (*fru*, *riddare*, *borgmästare*, *skomakare*, *timmerman*); (3) French, under the
Francophile Gustav III (*salong*, *teater*, *pjäs*, *ridå*); (4) English, from the 19th century
onward and still the dominant active source, spanning clothing, food, rail technology, labor/
business, sport, and — most recently — entertainment and digital technology (the English-loan
examples in the vocabulary table above, culminating in *e-mejl*, *internät*/*nät*). This is a
genuinely useful "loan wave" timeline for later mechanics-analysis work on how slang/register
layers might map onto similar contact-driven strata.

### Swedish as a pluricentric/minority language beyond Sweden's borders

The entry notes Swedish is "closely related to the other Scandinavian languages and is generally
understood in Norway and Denmark," and separately is the native language of **Finland-Swedes**
on Finland's west coast and around Helsinki, remaining Finland's second official language — though
the source gives a specific, dated statistic that "fewer than 7 percent of the Finnish population
are now naturally Swedish-speaking" (p. 216). It further notes Swedish was spoken in parts of
Latvia and Estonia until World War II, "a relic of Sweden's former Baltic empire" — i.e., a
now-extinct regional variety outside the modern Swedish-speaking area, distinct from any dialect
still spoken today.

### The Sami (Lapp) language is a distinct, non-Germanic minority language, not a Swedish dialect

The dictionary's own **LANGUAGE** entry is a one-line pointer: *"LANGUAGE. See LAPPS; SWEDISH
LANGUAGE"* (p. 117) — i.e., the source itself treats these as the only two language-relevant topics
in its whole index, and explicitly separates them. The **LAPPS (SAMI)** entry describes the Sami as
ethnically and linguistically distinct from Nordic peoples, speaking a **Fenno-Ugrian** language of
"Mongolian origins" (the source's own period-flavored phrasing) unrelated to Swedish, with a 1942
folk high school (later moved to Jokkmokk) offering instruction in "Sami language and culture."
This is a flagged, source-explicit statement that Sami is a genuinely separate language family, not
a regional variant of Swedish — important groundwork for this project to avoid conflating Sami
loanwords/contact phenomena with ordinary Swedish dialectal variation.

## Flagged findings for slang-synthesis purposes

- **No direct slang/taboo vocabulary appears anywhere in this source.** As an academic
  historical/biographical encyclopedia, it has no register-tagged colloquial glossary comparable to
  the "Slang" sections already found in *Colloquial Swedish* 1996/2007 (files 017a-c/018a-c). This
  absence is itself worth recording: a culture/history reference book of this type is not a slang
  source, confirming the project's own expectation set in the dispatch brief.
- **Flagship finding: explicit, source-stated dialectal basis of the standard language** (Svea
  dialect of Stockholm/Lake Mälaren + incorporated Göta-dialect features) — this is the first
  source in the Swedish extraction pass so far to state outright which specific named regional
  dialects the modern standard derives from, rather than just mentioning that dialects exist.
- **Secondary finding: a clean, source-organized periodization of loanword waves by era and donor
  language** (Latin → German → French → English), each with concrete lexical examples and the
  historical trigger (Christianization, Hanseatic trade, Gustav III's Francophile court, 19th-c.
  industrialization/modern globalization) — useful structural scaffolding for later contact-induced
  slang-formation analysis.
- **Sami (Fenno-Ugrian) explicitly flagged by the source as a separate language, not a Swedish
  dialect** — relevant boundary-marking for this project's own typology work, since Sami vocabulary
  (e.g., *jojka*) should not be folded into "Swedish regional/dialectal" entries.

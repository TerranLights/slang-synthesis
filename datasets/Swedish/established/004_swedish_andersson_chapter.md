# Swedish — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: "Swedish" (Andersson), complete chapter

**Source:** Erik Andersson, "Swedish" (Chapter 9), pp. 271-312 — a dense academic descriptive-
linguistics chapter (Phonology / Morphology / Syntax / Lexis sections, with a scholarly "Further
Reading" bibliography), supplied to this project as a standalone 42-page PDF with no parent-volume
title page. Internal evidence (chapter number "9," author, exact page range 271-312, section
structure, and bibliography citing Scandinavian-linguistics sources such as Elert's *Ljud och ord i
svenskan* and Garlén's *Svenskans fonologi*) strongly matches Chapter 9 of König & van der Auwera
(eds.), *The Germanic Languages* (Routledge, 1994) — flagged as a confident identification from
internal evidence, not a confirmed title-page match, since the supplied PDF itself never states the
parent volume's title.

**Coverage note:** every grammar point across all four major sections (Phonology, Morphology,
Syntax, Lexis) and every distinct vocabulary item/example lexeme introduced with its own gloss.
Exception, stated explicitly per the coverage rule: the Syntax section's many one-off illustrative
example *sentences* are treated as grammar-point evidence in the prose below rather than tabulated
word-by-word — tabulating every content word in dozens of syntax-illustration sentences would not
meet the "comprehensive but not exhaustive" standard. Genuinely idiomatic or lexically teachable
phrases drawn from those sentences *are* tabulated separately (see the "Idioms" subsection). This
source has a real text layer (`pdftotext -layout`); no vision-reading was required.

**A note on a text-layer corruption found in this specific source:** the extracted text layer
consistently renders the letter **å** as **â** (a-with-circumflex) throughout the entire chapter —
confirmed against 20+ known word/gloss pairs (`gâ`→`gå` "go," `pâ`→`på` "on," `nâgon`→`någon` "some,"
`bâda`→`båda` "both," `tvâ`→`två` "two," `frân`→`från` "from," etc.) — while **ä** and **ö** render
correctly throughout (`här`, `förklarade`, `störst` all intact). This is a fixed 1:1 character
substitution specific to this one diacritic, distinct from the multi-character Cyrillic/CJK ciphers
documented elsewhere in this project's spec, and is **silently corrected to `å` throughout this
file** rather than reproduced as `â`. The chapter's embedded IPA phonetic-alphabet symbols suffered
much heavier, non-systematic corruption (Greek letters substituted for IPA symbols, stress/accent
diacritics lost or replaced with stray characters) — these are **not** reliably decodable the way
the å/â substitution is, so this file generally paraphrases pronunciation facts in prose rather than
reproducing the corrupted bracketed IPA transcriptions verbatim; where a specific IPA string had to
be given, it is flagged low-confidence in the Notes column.

**Genuinely register-marked content, flagged per dispatch instructions:** this chapter is unusually
rich in explicit sociolinguistic/register material for a 42-page excerpt — §9.1 covers regional
dialects, minority languages (Finnish, Saami), and immigrant-language contact in detail, and the
Syntax section repeatedly calls out specific constructions as "colloquial style" or "dialectal"
deviations from the standard (personal-pronoun cliticization, the group genitive, double negation,
accusative after comparative *än*, and more) — see the dedicated "Register and regional variation"
subsection of Grammar points below, and the "Idioms and colloquial-register items" vocabulary
subsection.

## Grammar points

### 1. Sociolinguistic overview (§9.1, pp.271-272)

Swedish has 8+ million native speakers, mostly in Sweden, plus roughly 300,000 native speakers in
Finland (officially bilingual, with a Swedish-speaking minority with roots to the 12th century and
constitutional minority protections) and descendants of ~1,400,000 Swedish emigrants (1870-1930,
mostly US/Canada, now almost entirely assimilated to the local language). Historical minority
populations in Estonia and Ukraine (Gammalsvenskby) are now nearly extinct. Within Sweden itself,
historical minority-language populations include ~40,000 Finnish speakers (Tornedalen, near the
Finnish border) and ~7,000 Saami speakers; modern immigration (Finnish, Spanish, Arabic, Polish,
Serbian/Croatian, Persian, English, Turkish, German, Greek, Danish, Hungarian speakers, ~750,000
people, over 1 million including second-generation bilinguals) had not, as of this chapter's 1994
writing, measurably influenced the standard language beyond ordinary lexical borrowing (mainly from
English, with some French and German).

### 2. Regional and dialectal variation (§9.1, p.272)

Old rural dialects persist in northern Sweden (including Dalecarlia) and Gotland, and in Finland, but
most dialects have leveled toward a Stockholm-area-based super-regional norm that carries the
strongest social prestige, especially in formal contexts. Finland Swedish is a deviant regional
variant used at *every* level of communication there (not confined to informal registers the way a
typical regional accent might be). Southern Sweden (Skåne, Halland, Blekinge — a region that
belonged to Denmark until 1658) carries another fairly strong regional norm; Western Swedish is close
to it, while Northern Swedish is comparatively less uniform. Stylistic (register) differences have
tended to shrink over the 20th century, with informal style spreading into more formal contexts and
spelling increasingly influencing pronunciation — a convergence pattern worth noting for any
slang-synthesis purposes, since it suggests register boundaries are a genuinely moving target in
real Swedish, not a fixed design constant.

### 3. Phonology — vowel system and Finland Swedish's simplified system (§9.2, pp.272-273)

Standard Swedish has 9 vowel phonemes (18 counting short/long separately), generally differing in
*quality* as well as length (short variants are more centralized/lax). Two distinct types of
front-rounded vowel exist (`/y, ö/` outward-rounded vs. `/ü/`-type inward-rounded). Finland Swedish
is explicitly flagged as not following almost any of this: it collapses to a simpler system with
central vowels and lacks several of the allophonic splits found elsewhere (e.g. the /ö/ and /ä/
open-allophone-before-/r/ pattern).

### 4. Phonology — consonants, aspiration vs. voicing, and /r/-region map (§9.2, pp.273-274)

Swedish voiceless stops are aspirated (unlike many languages' "voiceless" stops), which has let
voiced stops begin devoicing next to a pause or voiceless segment — the source argues the
fortis/lenis distinction is really better described as an *aspiration* contrast than a voicing one.
Deaspiration happens after /s/ in the same morpheme, before voiceless segments, and non-initially
before an unstressed syllable. Dental /r/ is apico-alveolar, but a **uvular /r/** is used in southern
Sweden up to a Kalmar-Jönköping-Falkenberg line (and further north in initial/geminate position) —
this uvular-/r/ zone is exactly where the retroflex-sound contractions (point 5) do *not* occur.

### 5. Phonology — retroflex contraction (§9.2, p.274)

/r/ + a following dental consonant regularly contracts into a single retroflex sound within a word
(`barn` "child," `bort` "away," `hård` "hard") and even across word/morpheme boundaries (`barnstuga`
"children's cottage," `har du` "do you have"). This is a genuinely regionally-restricted phenomenon:
absent in southern Sweden (the uvular-/r/ zone) and in standard Finland Swedish.

### 6. Phonology — syllable weight and tonal accent (§9.2, pp.274-275)

Stressed syllables must be *long* — either a long vowel, or a short vowel + long consonant/cluster —
with the single flagged exception of stressed *short* syllables occurring only in Finland Swedish
colloquial speech (`bara` "only"). Standard Swedish (outside Finland Swedish) further has a two-way
**tonal word accent** contrast (acute "accent 1" vs. gravis "accent 2") that is lexically and
morphologically conditioned rather than free — monosyllables and final-stress words plus their
definite forms get accent 1; polysyllables with initial stress generally get accent 2, but several
systematic exception classes exist (present-tense `-er` forms, definite nouns, certain `-er` plurals,
`-re` comparatives, non-initial/non-final-stress words, many foreign-origin words, and compounds
losing their compound character). The phonetic realization of the accents itself varies regionally
(central area: two peaks in accent 2; further south in Götaland: delayed peaks; further south still,
and in Gotland/Dalecarlia: one peak each, with Gotland/Dalecarlia's peaks also delayed) — tonal accent
is absent in Finland Swedish altogether.

### 7. Phonology — phonotactics and stress placement (§9.2, pp.275-277)

Native-word phonotactics are restrictive: no diphthongs, typically one vowel per root morpheme, a
simple second syllable in disyllabic stems. Certain spelled consonant clusters (`stj/skj/sj`,
`tj/kj`, `gj/lj/hj`, `dj`) don't correspond to their literal letter sequence but to single palatal/
velar sounds. Foreign-origin words tolerate more complex phonotactics but still generally avoid true
diphthongs. Stress in native non-derived words falls on the first syllable; foreign-origin words show
variable stress (first syllable, last syllable, or one before last), and derivational morphology can
either leave the stem's stress alone, pull stress onto the suffix itself, or leave a weaker tertiary
stress behind — occasionally with genuine speaker-to-speaker variation in which syllable wins
primary stress (`feminin`).

### 8. Morphology — compounding (§9.3, pp.277-279)

New nouns/adjectives/verbs are productively compounded (less so pronouns/adverbs/prepositions/
conjunctions). Compounds are either *modifying* (two elements, first modifies second) or
*copulative* (conjoining-like, can have 2+ parallel elements); written solid, with hyphens optionally
clarifying copulative compounds or compounds following a proper name. An optional linking morpheme
(`-s-` or a vowel) attaches to the first element, lexically conditioned rather than fully predictable
— the same first element can take `-s-` in one compound and nothing in another (`dagsinkomst` vs.
`dagtid`). `-s-` is required when the first element is itself complex, and blocked after certain
final consonant clusters or after unstressed-vowel+`/r,l,n/`. Typical compound stress is primary on
the first element/secondary on the second, even when the second element is the semantic head — with
flagged exceptions (contracted phrases, additive numerals, 3+-constituent compounds). Whole phrases
can occasionally be compounded wholesale, usually hyphen-marked. Inflectional endings almost never
survive inside a compound except in a couple of fixed patterns (neuter `-t` in copulatives; a plural
ending when the first element contains a numeral, e.g. `femdagarsvecka`).

### 9. Morphology — derivation (§9.3, p.279)

Derivation is mostly suffixing (prefixes exist but are rarer); the final element (stem or suffix)
determines the derived word's word class. Stress behavior of derivational affixes falls into three
groups: unstressed, secondary-stress-bearing (like a compound's second element), and primary-stress-
bearing (pulling stress entirely off the stem). The prefixing/compounding boundary is genuinely fuzzy
for some formations (`kvasipopulär`, `miniräknare`). A long list of productive noun-, adjective-, and
verb-forming suffixes is given (see the Vocabulary table above for the individual examples); several
of these carry a clearly informal/pejorative flavor (`-ling`, `-ing`, `-is` agent-noun suffixes:
`vekling`, `hårding`, `fegis`) — register-relevant for slang-synthesis purposes, since this is a
*productive* word-formation pattern for coining informal personality-description nouns, not a closed
lexical set.

### 10. Morphology — noun inflection: number, definiteness, case (§9.3, pp.279-281)

Nouns inflect for number (sg./pl.), definiteness (bare/definite), and case (basic/genitive), in that
fixed order (`bil-ar-na-s` "of the cars"). Only count nouns take all three categories; mass/
uncountable nouns skip number, proper nouns skip both number and definiteness (genitive only).
Plural-formation declension classes correlate with (but aren't identical to) the two-gender system
(utrum/neutrum) — see the seven lettered declension classes (a-g) tabulated in the Vocabulary section
above, including the loanword-ending class (g) where a foreign plural and a nativized plural often
coexist, with the nativized form now generally preferred. Several plural types additionally undergo
vowel shift (umlaut), sometimes with an accompanying accent or quantity shift; unstressed-syllable
vowels syncopate before a syllabic plural ending; stem-final `/-um/` is lost before any vowel-initial
ending (with `album` flagged as a genuine exception retaining it); `-or`-final nouns shift stress in
the plural.

### 11. Morphology — gender and the definite-article endings (§9.3, p.281)

Swedish's two genders (utrum, neutrum) are marked by the singular definite ending: utrum `-en`
(reduced to `-n` after certain vowel-final stems), neutrum `-et`/`-t`. Plural definite is generally
`-na`, with conditioned variants (`-a` after `/n/`, `-en` after a zero plural). A small number of
words show genuine gender *variation*, often regionally conditioned (`en penni`/`ett penni` "a
penny").

### 12. Morphology — case: the genitive (§9.3, p.281)

The genitive ending is always `-s`, except after an `/s/`-final stem, where it's assimilated and only
occasionally marked orthographically with an apostrophe (`Bush-s`, `Sibelius(')`).

### 13. Morphology — adjective inflection and the three comparison classes (§9.3, pp.281-282)

Adjectives (including past participles) agree in number, definiteness, and gender with a head noun
or predicative base, plus case when heading a noun phrase themselves. Positive-form plural/definite
is `-a` (or `-e` after an unstressed `/a/`-final syllable); a masculine-referent-specific `-e` form
exists for singular definite and is *obligatory* when the adjective itself heads the noun phrase
(`den gamle` "the old man"). Indefinite singular has no ending for utrum agreement, `-t` for neutrum/
no-agreement contexts. Three comparison classes exist, differing in which ending-set they use
(`-are/-ast/-aste`; `-re/-st/-sta/-ste`, often with umlaut+shortening, home to several suppletive
common adjectives like `bra`/`liten`; and a rare adverbial-origin `-re/-erst/-ersta/-erste` class).
Comparatives are never inflected further. The superlative long form functions as a definite form for
some speakers but as a general attributive form for others — genuine speaker-to-speaker variation,
not a settled rule.

### 14. Morphology — pronoun inflection and the three pronoun subtypes (§9.3, pp.282-284)

Pronouns split into *nominal pronouns* (function as full noun phrases, e.g. personal pronouns),
*determiners* (can combine with a noun/adjective to form a noun phrase, e.g. demonstratives), and
*adjectival pronouns* (need a determiner present, behave like true adjectives, e.g. `annan` "other").
A determiner is only grammatically required in singular individuative noun phrases, which often
neutralizes the determiner/adjectival-pronoun distinction for inherently-plural pronouns. Nominal
pronouns aren't inflected for number/definiteness (they inherently possess those properties) but do
inflect for case, often via suppletive forms, and personal pronouns additionally distinguish
accusative from genitive (replaced by a dedicated possessive-pronoun paradigm rather than a true
genitive — see the full 1st/2nd-person paradigm table in the Vocabulary section). Definite
nominal/determiner pronouns fall into six subtypes: relative, reciprocal, 1st/2nd-person, reflexive,
3rd-person-singular, and demonstrative-determiner.

### 15. Morphology — verb inflection: tense-mood, conjugation classes, voice (§9.3, pp.284-287)

Five tense-mood forms (present, past/preterite, subjunctive, optative, imperative) group under three
traditional moods. The present ending is `-er`/`-r`; weak-verb past is `-de`/`-te`/`-dde` (dental
suffix, conditioned by the preceding segment); strong verbs mark past via vowel shift instead,
partly-but-not-fully predictable from the present stem. A true morphological subjunctive (vowel-
shifted stem + `-e`) exists for strong verbs; a separate, now mostly archaic/lexicalized optative
also takes `-e`, added directly to the present stem, and carries a magic-formula/wish flavor
(`leve!` "long live!"). The infinitive takes `-a` after a consonant, nothing after a vowel; the
supine (used with auxiliary `ha`) takes `-t/-tt` (weak) or `-it` (strong) and was only grammatically
split off from the neuter past participle in the 18th century — for most strong verbs the two forms
are now identical, but for a few (`skriva`) they still differ (`skrivit` sup. vs. `skrivet` part.).
Two voices (active, passive/"s-form") exist for both finite and non-finite forms; the s-form is built
by suffixing `-s` to the active form (with the present-tense ending disappearing first); a separate
*phrasal* passive uses a copula (`bli` for perfective verbs, `vara`/sometimes `bli` for imperfective
verbs) plus a past participle. Verbs group into weak (Classes I-III, by stem-final segment type),
strong (Class IV, vowel-shift-based, itself subdivided into gradation series not detailed in this
particular chunk), and a small residual class (modal-flavored, irregular weak verbs: `kunna`,
`vilja`, `skola`, `veta`). Some frequent verbs have parallel long/short stem-final-consonant-dropping
forms (`taga`/`ta`, `bliva`/`bli`), with the long forms now archaic except in the supine. The present
participle is formed with `-(a)nde`/`-ende`; the past participle with `-d/-t/-dd` (weak) or `-en`
(strong).

### 16. Syntax — the noun phrase and three definiteness categories (§9.4, pp.287-289)

A noun phrase typically contains a pronoun/determiner and/or a noun, but a bare adjective can itself
function as one. Three definiteness categories exist: definite, indefinite, and *bare* (arguably
itself a variant of indefinite, since mass nouns/plurals can appear bare in an indefinite function).
Definite marking can come from up to three separate places at once (a definite pronoun/determiner, a
definite adjective form, and the noun's own definite ending) — the chapter catalogues a long, genuinely
rule-governed set of specific environments where one or more of these three markers is *systematically
dropped* (after genitival/possessive attributes, after `denna`/`samma`, before certain superlatives,
in exclamations/vocatives, after `nästa`/`följande`/etc., after `varje`/`var`, and more) — this is
real, structured variation worth tracking if slang-synthesis ever wants a plausible Swedish-flavored
"elided article" register effect.

### 17. Syntax — case system (§9.4, p.289)

Three cases exist (nominative, accusative, genitive), but the nominative/accusative split is real
only for personal pronouns — everything else collapses nominative and accusative into one "basic"
case. Reflexive predicative complements are a flagged exception requiring the accusative even where a
normal predicative complement would be nominative (`jag är inte mig själv` "I am not my real self").
The genitive's `-s` attaches to the noun phrase's final head and covers an unusually wide meaning
range (possessive, partitive, subjective/objective/temporal "nexus," measure/property, emphasis,
identity) — see the Register subsection for the colloquial "group genitive" variant that relocates the
`-s` to the very end of the whole phrase instead of its grammatical head.

### 18. Syntax — attributive modifiers, PPs, AdjPs (§9.4, pp.290-291)

Pronominal modifiers precede the noun in a fixed slot order: totality > demonstration > possession >
quantity > selection > comparison > noun. Genitival modifiers occupy the possession slot (but can't
co-occur with a definite article in the demonstration slot). Adjectival modifiers normally precede,
following pronominal/genitival ones, but can follow the noun if they themselves carry further
modification or form a coordinate structure. Prepositional/adverbial attributive complements are
postposed; a preposed adverb can get reinterpreted as an adjective (`en gratis glass` "a free ice
cream"). A handful of true postpositions and circumpositions exist in otherwise strongly
prepositional Swedish (`oss emellan` "between us," `för en vecka sedan` "a week ago").

### 19. Syntax — subjects, expletive subjects, impersonal/existential clauses (§9.4, pp.291-292)

Swedish is subject-prominent: every clause except imperatives/some elliptical ones needs an overt
subject, marked by clause position and (for personal pronouns) nominative case. The subject
distinctively triggers tense-mood inflection, triggers number/gender agreement in predicative
adjectives, governs reflexive/reciprocal pronouns within its own clause, and can be "raised" into a
higher clause's object/subject slot with certain verbs. Where there's no real semantic subject, an
expletive `det` fills the slot (archaically/regionally, a locative adverbial like `här`/`där` can do
this instead). Existential clauses place an indefinite subject noun phrase in postverbal position,
usually with a locative adverbial present; the indefiniteness requirement tightens considerably in
more formal style.

### 20. Syntax — cleft sentences and extraposition (§9.4, pp.292-293)

Almost any constituent can be clefted: promoted into a higher clause with expletive `det` + a copula,
with the original sentence demoted to a `som`-subordinate clause; the clefted element still takes
whatever case it would have had inside that subordinate clause. Infinitival/sentential subjects are
normally extraposed to clause-final position (triggering an expletive subject) — nearly obligatory in
subordinate clauses, optional (and marked) in main clauses.

### 21. Syntax — objects, including the colloquial "split/double object" pattern (§9.4, pp.293-295)

Objects normally follow the verb but can be preposed (sometimes genuinely hard to distinguish from
subjects when word order alone must disambiguate). Ditransitives' indirect object (receiver/
experiencer) precedes the direct object; either can promote to subject under passivization, though
some idiomatic indirect objects (e.g. the `ge grå hår` idiom — see Idioms vocabulary) resist the usual
indirect-object/prepositional-phrase paraphrase entirely. A genuinely striking "split/double object"
construction is noted: a fronted object expresses a type/category while the normal-position object
expresses quantity/quality/identification (`ost köpte jag två kilo` "cheese I bought two kilos of") —
this also interacts with existential subjects.

### 22. Syntax — predicative complements and quantifier floating (§9.4, pp.294-295)

Predicative complements agree with their controller in number/gender (nominal ones in number only);
several verbs require one (`vara`, `bli`, `heta`, `kalla(s)`). Nominal predicative complements
expressing a conventional classification (nationality, religion, occupation) typically drop the
article entirely (`hon är professor` "she is a professor"). "Quantifier floating" lets a predicative
complement rather than a noun-phrase modifier carry the subject/object's numeral, especially with a
pronominal controller (`vi satt tre stycken vid bordet` "we were three at the table").

### 23. Syntax — adverbials and their ordering (§9.4, pp.295-296)

A full semantic taxonomy of adverbial types is given (time: identification/duration/iteration/
frequency/sequence-order; place: identification/source/goal/path; cause; agent; consequence;
condition; manner; degree; concomitance). Adverbials to verbs are normally postposed but many can
also sit in "mid-adverbial" position (short time-adverbials especially); sentence adverbials
(modal/commenting, e.g. `säkert` "certainly") are preposed but semantically scope over the whole
clause. Short, prosodically-tight adverbial particles can sit *before* the object even in an otherwise
postposed-adverbial language, with further modification on the particle sometimes shifting it back
after the object instead. Focus particles can precede almost any constituent, and in colloquial style
can instead *follow* their sister constituent (`jag sov en liten stund bara` "I slept just for a
while").

### 24. Syntax — negation (§9.4, pp.296-297)

The neutral negator is `inte`, with more formal variants `ej`/`icke` and an emphatic `ingalunda`;
`alls` adds emphasis as a negative-polarity adverb. Negation sits in mid-adverbial position by
default, but can go clause-initial for emphasis (more common in Finland Swedish). Constituent negation
(as opposed to clause negation) often substitutes a negative determiner (`ingen/inget/inga`) for
`inte någon/något/några`; `aldrig` "never" and `ingenstans` "nowhere" behave the same way. Double
negation doesn't exist in the standard language but is attested in dialectal speech (`inte aldrig`,
see Idioms vocabulary). A set of negative-polarity items (`knappt`, `inte heller`, `någonsin`) only
appear in non-assertive contexts, mirroring the English `any`/`some` split: `en` is the "assertive"
indefinite article/pronoun, `någon` the "non-assertive" one — almost exactly parallel to English
`a/some` vs. `any`.

### 25. Syntax — sentence types (§9.4, pp.297-299)

At least ten distinguishable sentence types are catalogued by syntactic marking (finite-verb preposing,
constituent/wh-preposing, special mood): declarative (topic + finite-verb-second); interrogative
(alternative/yes-no with verb-initial; wh-questions with the wh-constituent preposed before the finite
verb); imperative (verb-initial, same order as yes/no questions, optional emphatic/threatening
second-person subject); optative (archaic, lexicalized wish/magic-formula expressions, `leve han!`
"may he live!"); desiderative (wish, yes/no-question-shaped, past/subjunctive verb, often with `ändå`/
`bara`); suppositive (hypothesis, indicative mood, can be indistinguishable from a yes/no question);
exclamative (exclamative phrase first, rest structured as a `som`-subordinate clause if the exclamative
is the subject); echo-questions/statements (shaped like `om`/`att`-subordinate clauses); finite-adverb
sentences (`kanske`/`månne` can itself undergo the "verb-preposing" instead of the real finite verb);
and verbless sentence fragments retaining subject-predicate structure (`vem där?` "who's there?").

### 26. Syntax — subordinate clauses (§9.4, pp.299-302)

Subordinate clauses classify syntactically as nominal, adverbial, or relative, and structurally by
their initial constituent (subjunction and/or clause constituent). Nominal `att`-clauses are neutral
for factivity but read as factive by default; the complementizer `att` is frequently deletable.
Interrogative nominal clauses split into yes/no-type (`om`/`huruvida`) and wh-type (headed by a
question word, non-factive as to that word, requiring `som` if the preposed wh-constituent is itself
the subject). A long catalogue of adverbial-clause subjunctions is given by semantic type (temporal,
locative, conditional, concessive, causal, consecutive, purposive, comparative). Relative clauses
normally use `som` (deletable in restrictive relative clauses whose subject slot is filled) or, more
formally, an explicit relative word (`vilken`, `vars`, `där`, etc.); a preposition attached to the
relativized element can either front with it or be stranded at the clause's end. Non-restrictive
relative clauses modifying an entire verb phrase/clause always use `vilket`.

### 27. Syntax — main-clause word order, constituent preposing, and extraction (§9.4, pp.302-306)

Main-clause word order is summarized by two extra structural slots beyond the subordinate-clause
template: a topic slot and the finite-verb slot (the latter corresponds to the subordinate clause's
subjunction slot). Several subordinate-clause types can optionally borrow main-clause (verb-preposed)
word order under specific conditions — some confined to colloquial style specifically (see point 29).
Almost any constituent can be fronted as topic in a declarative main clause, including stranding its
own preposition behind; when a whole verb phrase fronts, a dummy `göra` ("do") fills the vacated verb
slot. Noun phrases and most subordinate clauses are syntactic "islands" blocking extraction of their
internal parts, though some noun phrases (including "picture nouns") are only *weak* islands and do
allow it. Extraction out of `att`-clauses functioning as objects to cognitive verbs is fully natural;
extraction from adverbial or relative clauses is comparatively marginal but still attested, especially
when a "parasitic gap" resumptive pronoun can fill the extraction site — and regional variation is
explicitly noted here too (freer extraction in the western dialect area).

### 28. Syntax — tense, mood, and Aktionsart (§9.4, pp.306-308)

Eight compound tenses are built from the two inflected tenses (present, past) plus the auxiliaries
`ha` and `skola`/`komma`. Present tense covers both the literal moment of speech and timeless/general
statements; a "historic present" can report an act of communication as currently perceived rather than
as having occurred in the past. Future time often needs no dedicated future auxiliary at all,
especially for momentary actions or when a time adverbial already signals futurity — and a bare
present is in fact *required* in temporal/conditional clauses identifying a future time (no `komma
att` there). Past tense, besides ordinary past reference, also covers imagined/unreal events (a modal
use, often clarified by `skola`/`ha` as "potential" vs. "unreal" markers) and a set of lexicalized
emotive/polite past-tense formulas (`hur var namnet?` "what is your name?," lit. "what was the
name?"). The perfect/past split tracks whether the past action still has present-moment relevance or
consequence, with an exact time specification tending to force the plain past even where a temporal
frame would otherwise allow the perfect. Aktionsart (imperfective/perfective) is independently
encoded via the governing preposition on a duration adverbial (`i` = imperfective "for," `på` =
perfective "in"); past participles of imperfective verbs read as present-state, of perfective verbs
as a resultant perfect state.

### 29. Syntax — voice/subject-choice, agreement, reflexives, ellipsis (§9.4, pp.308-311)

The `-s`-form covers five distinct functions (true passive, medium/"happens by itself," reflexive,
reciprocal, absolute) that aren't always sharply distinguishable from one another; an instrument can
often be promoted to subject just as readily as a true agent can, blurring passive and medium further.
Agreement (within noun phrases, for predicative complements, and for anaphoric pronouns) is governed
by a mix of grammatical and *semantic* properties of the referent, with gender agreement specifically
neutralized in the plural throughout; neuter functions as the elsewhere-unmarked default form (used
for adverbial-complement adjectives, and for a controller treated as a general phenomenon rather than
an individual). Reflexive pronouns are normally co-referent with their own clause's subject, but the
notion of "subject" extends to clause-equivalents (certain appositions, object-with-infinitive
constructions) in ways that can create genuine structural ambiguity about which antecedent a
reflexive actually targets. A handful of ellipsis patterns are catalogued (coordinate-structure
deletion, noun-phrase-internal deletion preferring to delete the second/plural element, interrogative
sluicing, `göra det` "do it/so" substitution in yes/no-question contexts, and a formal-style reduction
of certain concessive clauses to a bare predicative complement).

### 30. Lexis — the native/foreign vocabulary split and successive loan strata (§9.5, pp.311-312)

Swedish vocabulary divides, on phonotactic grounds, into native and foreign strata that are
generally also kept separate in word-formation (though cross-combination of foreign affixes with
native stems, and vice versa, does occur: `läckage` "leakage," `runolog` "runologist"). A near-constant
stream of Latin/Greek loans has run since the early Middle Ages (`präst`, `tema`, `psyke`), and Latin
remains productive in word-formation today. A powerful Low German influence (via Hanseatic trade,
late Middle Ages) produced loans now fully assimilated and unfelt as foreign (`jägare`, `fara`),
later giving way to High German influence after the Reformation. The 18th century brought a wave of
French loans (`etapp`, `hotell`); Italian (`sopran`) and a few Finnish loans (`pojke`, `känga`) also
entered. The contemporary layer is dominated by English influence, sometimes borrowed unaltered
(`poster`, `make-up`, `briefing`) and sometimes nativized in spelling (`schampo`, `tejp`). A
countervailing purist tendency favors reviving old Scandinavian words or coining native
compounds/translation-loans instead of borrowing outright (`mjukvara` "software" for English
"software" — a calque, not a loan), and new technical/administrative terminology is often coordinated
across the Scandinavian languages on a shared basis.

### Register and regional variation — summary cross-reference

This chapter is unusually explicit, for a 42-page excerpt, about where the standard written/formal
language and actual spoken/colloquial/dialectal Swedish diverge. The clearest, most *citable*
individual findings (already tabulated above with page numbers) are: the `de/dem` → `dom` merger in
spoken language (p.283); double negation as a dialectal-only feature (p.297); accusative subject-
pronoun case after a reduced comparative clause in colloquial style (p.301); personal-object-pronoun
cliticization in colloquial speech (p.293); the "group genitive" relocating `-s` to a phrase's end in
colloquial style (p.289-290); freer syntactic extraction from clausal islands in colloquial style and
specifically in the western dialect area (p.304-305); and sentence-initial negation being more
frequent in Finland Swedish specifically (p.296). None of these are slang in the lexical sense (no
new words), but they are exactly the kind of systematic *colloquial-grammar* deviation from a
standard baseline that a slang/informal-register design for a derived conlang could plausibly borrow
the mechanism of, even without copying the Swedish-specific forms themselves.

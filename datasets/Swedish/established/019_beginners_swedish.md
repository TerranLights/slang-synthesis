# Swedish — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Beginner's Swedish (Scott A. Mellor, Hippocrene Books, 2006)

**Source:** Scott A. Mellor, *Beginner's Swedish* (Hippocrene Beginner's Series, Hippocrene Books,
1st ed. with audio, 2006) — full book: "Grammar at a Glance" (alphabet, vowels, consonants, letter
combinations, everyday-speech pronunciation, length/stress, intonation, verbs, nouns, adjectives, word
order overview), Lessons 1-10 (each: Dialogue + Vocabulary + Useful Expressions + Grammar + Exercises
+ Etymology), interleaved one-page-plus culture chapters (Geography, A Short History, Politics and
Government, Social Welfare, Business and Economics, Art and Culture, Holidays, Education and
Research, Everyday Life, Genealogy), Key to Exercises, Swedish-English and English-Swedish
Vocabulary, Audio CD Track List. Source file: `01 Beginners Courses/21.Beginner's Swedish.pdf`
(136 PDF pages).

**Rendering note:** vision-only scan confirmed directly — `pdfinfo` reports a nominal text layer, but
`pdftotext -layout` over the entire 136-page file returns only 136 bytes (effectively empty/whitespace),
so this is a scanned reproduction with no usable embedded text. Rendered via `pdftoppm -png -r 150`.
**Each of the 136 PDF pages is a two-printed-page spread** (the extraction spec's spread-scanning
gotcha, confirmed again on this source): printed page 1 appears on the right-hand side of PDF page 4;
a blank/unnumbered verso sometimes precedes a chapter-opening recto (standard print-book convention,
not a scan defect). No fixed PDF-page-to-printed-page offset formula was assumed — each navigated page
was located by its own visible printed page number. No handwritten marginalia was found anywhere in
the sampled pages; all content read was clearly printed/typeset in a single consistent modern font.

**Non-redundant-supplement scope decision.** This dataset already holds extensive Swedish grammar
coverage from eight other sources (`001` Essential Grammar, `002` Björkhagen, `003` Uppvall, `004`
Andersson, `008`/`009` Croghan & McClean *Teach Yourself*, `010` *Speak Swedish with Confidence*,
`015a-c` FSI Basic Course, `016` *Advanced Spoken Swedish*, `017`/`018` *Colloquial Swedish*
1996/2007). Mellor's core grammar — two genders, suffixed definite article, five noun-plural classes,
weak/strong verb conjugation, adjective agreement, personal/possessive pronouns, basic word order — is
the same territory already tabulated in depth in those files, and is **not re-tabulated here**. What
this file captures instead, because it is genuinely distinctive to this source:

1. **A dedicated per-lesson "Etymology" essay (ten of them, one per lesson)** comparing Swedish and
   English historical/comparative linguistics — sound changes, cognate pairs, borrowing patterns,
   and even two genuinely useful **sociolinguistic/register notes** (the `du`-reform's formal
   third-person-address survival, and real spoken usage diverging from prescriptive grammar). No
   other source in this dataset has this essay-per-lesson structure.
2. **Explicit source-labeled slang/colloquial/regional markers** — this book tags a vocabulary entry
   itself as "(slang)", flags an expression as "(slang/informal)", and footnotes a regional
   vocabulary variant — all reproduced and flagged below.
3. **A systematic consonant-dropping "Everyday Speech" pronunciation table** (§3.2 of "Grammar at a
   Glance") giving the actual colloquial spoken forms of ~11 extremely common function words, with a
   book-internal phonetic respelling for each.
4. **A full prepositions-with-meaning-and-example list** (~20 prepositions), a **time-preposition
   idiom list** (~25 expressions), and a **coordinating-vs-subordinating conjunction list** — more
   exhaustively enumerated in one place than in this dataset's other sources.
5. **A named semantic/lexical-choice contrast set** not seen elsewhere in this dataset: `tycka` vs.
   `tänka` vs. `tro` ("to think"), and `kan`/`kunna` vs. `vet`/`veta` vs. `känner`/`känna` ("to
   know"), each explained with worked example sentences.
6. **A full deponent-verb list** and a **reflexive-pronoun-verb list**, and the **`sin`/`sitt`/
   `sina`/`sig`** reflexive-possessive contrast (own vs. someone else's), illustrated with
   deliberately paired near-minimal examples the author calls out as a common source of
   embarrassment if confused.
7. **The at-rest/in-motion directional-adverb system** (`där`/`dit`, `hemma`/`hem`, `här`/`hit`,
   `inne`/`in`, `nere`/`ner`, `uppe`/`upp`, `ute`/`ut`, `borta`/`bort`, `var`/`vart`) tabulated as a
   single paired system.
8. The book's own **phonetic-respelling pronunciation convention** attached to every vocabulary
   entry (e.g. `affärskontakt` → `ahffehrskohntahkt`) plus a parallel **noun-declension-class
   number (1-5) and verb-conjugation-class label (I-V, with principal parts)** tagged on every
   lexical item — a classification convention distinct from this dataset's other sources' vocab
   tables.

**What is explicitly skipped as redundant:** the book's ~35-page combined Swedish-English/
English-Swedish back-of-book glossary (pp. 225-262) was sampled across its full range (front, middle,
end) and confirmed to be a straight re-alphabetized consolidation of the same per-lesson vocabulary
already appearing in the ten lessons' own "Vocabulary" sections, down to identical phonetic
respellings — it is not re-extracted. The ten lessons' own dialogues ("Samtal"/"Dialogue" sections)
are not reproduced (copyright discipline — dialogue is pedagogical narrative, not a grammar point or
distinct vocabulary). The nine culture essays (Geography, History, Politics, Social Welfare, Business,
Art and Culture, Holidays, Education, Everyday Life, Genealogy) are cultural-background prose with no
grammar or vocabulary content and are not extracted — a one-line note on each is given in the coverage
gaps section for completeness. The "Key to Exercises" (pp. 213-224) is an answer key to drills already
excluded under the coverage rule and is not extracted. Ordinary non-distinctive lesson vocabulary
(common nouns/verbs for food, shopping, travel, clothing, etc. — hundreds of items across ten lessons)
substantially overlaps this dataset's other Swedish sources and is likewise not re-tabulated in bulk;
only the flagged/distinctive subset below is captured in full.

## Grammar points

### Everyday-speech consonant dropping (colloquial register)

"Grammar at a Glance" §3.2 (p.10) gives an explicit list of common function words whose final
consonant is dropped in casual spoken Swedish, each with the book's own phonetic respelling: `dag`→
"da", `jag`→"ya", `med`→"me", `och`→"o"/"ok" (never the spelled form), `mig`→"mey" (often written
`mej`), `dig`→"dey" (`dej`), `sig`→"sey" (`sej`), `någon`→"non" (`nån`), `några`→"nora" (as printed;
flagged low-confidence above), `sådan`→"son" (`sån`), `sedan`→"sen" (`sen`), `de`/`dem`→"dom" (`dom`,
both spelling and pronunciation). This is the book's single densest concentration of colloquial-register
content and is reproduced in full in the vocabulary table above.

### Noun plurals: irregular stem-vowel-change class

Beyond the five standard plural-ending classes (`-or`, `-ar`, `-er`, `-n`, zero-ending — already
covered by other sources in this dataset), this book gives an unusually long enumerated list of nouns
that change their stem vowel in the plural, explicitly calling this group "not exhaustive, but...the
most common words" (p.32-33): `and`→`änder` ("duck(s)"), `bokstav`→`bokstäver` ("letter(s) of the
alphabet"), `brand`→`bränder` ("fire(s)"), `hand`→`händer` ("hand(s)"), `land`→`länder` ("country/-ies"),
`natt`→`nätter` ("night(s)"), `rand`→`ränder` ("strip(s)"), `stad`→`städer` ("city/-ies"), `tand`→`tänder`
("tooth/teeth"), `stång`→`stänger` ("pole(s)"), `tång`→`tänger` ("tong(s)"), plus the irregulars `man`→
`män` ("man/men"), `öga`→`ögon` ("eye(s)"), `öra`→`öron` ("ear(s)"). The source explicitly draws the
parallel to English's own residual vowel-change plurals (tooth/teeth, foot/feet) as the same
inherited Germanic pattern.

### Adjective declension: seven numbered exception sub-rules

The book organizes adjective-ending exceptions (beyond the base `-t`/`stora`-type pattern already
covered elsewhere in this dataset) into seven explicitly numbered sub-rules (pp.72-74), more
systematically enumerated than this dataset's other sources: (1) adjectives ending in `-d` preceded by
a consonant drop the `-d` before the neuter `-t` ending (`hård`→`hårt`); (2) adjectives ending in `-d`
preceded by a vowel drop the `-d` and add `-tt` (`röd`→`rött`); (3) adjectives ending in unstressed
`-en` drop the `-n` before taking `-t` (`mogen`→`moget`); (4) adjectives ending in a stressed vowel
take `-tt`, shortening the vowel (`ny`→`nytt`); (5) adjectives ending in `-t` preceded by another
consonant do not double the `-t` (`brant`→`brant`, not `brantt`) — contrasted with `vit`→`vitt`, which
does double; (6) adjectives already ending in `-tt` do not add another `-t` (`trött`→`trött`); (7) a
few adjectives (e.g. `bra`) do not decline at all.

### Comparative and superlative adjectives: vowel-shift subclass

Beyond the regular `-are`/`-ast`/`-aste` comparative/superlative pattern, the book tabulates a subclass
of adjectives that both lose the `-a` of the base form and shift their stem vowel in the comparative/
superlative (p.133): `grov`→`grövre`/`grövst`/`grövsta` ("coarse"), `hög`→`högre`/`högst`/`högsta`
("high"), `låg`→`lägre`/`lägst`/`lägsta` ("low"), `lång`→`längre`/`längst`/`längsta` ("long"), `stor`→
`större`/`störst`/`största` ("big"), `trång`→`trängre`/`trängst`/`trängsta` ("narrow"), `tung`→`tyngre`/
`tyngst`/`tyngsta` ("heavy"), `ung`→`yngre`/`yngst`/`yngsta` ("young"). A separate small irregular-root
set is also given: `bra`/`god`→`bättre`/`bäst`/`bästa` ("good"), `dålig`→`sämre`/`sämst`/`sämsta`
("bad") vs. `dålig`→`värre`/`värst`/`värsta` ("evil" sense), `gammal`→`äldre`/`äldst`/`äldsta` ("old"),
`liten`→`mindre`/`minst`/`minsta` ("small"), `många`→`fler(a)`/`flest`/`flesta` ("many"). Adjectives
ending in `-isk` and all adjectival participles instead take periphrastic `mer`/`mest` rather than an
ending (`energisk`→`mer energisk`/`mest energisk`).

### Verb system: future, conditional, and the three uses of the `-s` form

The book lays out three ways to form the future (`ska` + bare infinitive; `kommer att` + infinitive;
present tense + a time adverbial) and the conditional/conditional-perfect with `skulle` (+ `ha` +
supine for the perfect) — broadly standard material already covered elsewhere in this dataset, but
the book's treatment of the passive/`-s` verb form's **three distinct uses** is more explicit than
most: (1) true passive (`Boken läses av mig`, "The book is read by me"); (2) reciprocal meaning
(`Vi ses`, "We'll see each other"/"I'll see you"; `Vi hörs`, "We'll hear from each other"/"I'll talk to
you [later]"); (3) deponent verbs, which have the passive `-s` form but are active in meaning (full
list in the vocabulary table above: `hoppas`, `låtsas`, `synas`, `tyckas`, `lyckas`, `minnas`,
`trivas`).

### The auxiliary verb `lära` = "it is said to"

A distinct, narrower sense of `lära` beyond "to learn/teach" (already covered as a reflexive verb
above): used as an auxiliary, `lära` means "it is said to," ex. `Hon lär vara vacker` ("She is said to
be beautiful"), `Boken lär vara bra` ("The book is said to be good") — p.188.

### Participles

Present participles are regular: add `-ande` to the root stem of conjugation-I/II/IV verbs and
`-ende` to conjugation-III verbs (which already end in a vowel): `talande`, `ringande`, `troende`,
`skrivande`. The book stresses that Swedish has no equivalent to the English "is going" construction —
`han är gående` must be understood/translated as "he is a walking person," not a progressive tense.
Past participles decline for common/neuter/plural-definite gender and are built by conjugation class
(table, p.189): class I `talad`/`talat`/`talade`; IIa `ringd`/`ringt`/`ringda`; IIb `köpt`/`köpt`/
`köpta`; III `trodd`/`trott`/`trodda`; IV `skriven`/`skrivet`/`skrivna`.

### Three-way lexical contrasts: `tycka`/`tänka`/`tro` and `kan`/`vet`/`känner`

See the vocabulary table above for both sets with full glosses and example sentences. These are
presented by the book as two of its named "issues that often trip up students" (p.134) — a
semantic-field framing not found as an explicit named contrast in this dataset's other Swedish
sources.

### Reflexive possessive: `sin`/`sitt`/`sina`/`sig` vs. `hans`/`hennes`/`deras`

`sin`/`sitt`/`sina` is used only when the possessed item belongs to the subject of the same clause and
that subject is third person; `hans`/`hennes`/`deras` is used for anyone else's possession. The book
gives deliberately near-minimal pairs to show the ambiguity risk if confused: `Han har sitt glas` ("He
has his own glass") vs. `Han har hans glas` ("He has his [someone else's] glass"); `Hon skrev ett brev
till sin man` ("She wrote a letter to her own husband") vs. `...till hennes man` ("...to her
[someone else's] husband"); `De var med sitt barn` ("They were with their own child") vs. `...med
deras barn` ("...with their [someone else's] child"). The book states directly: "Using it incorrectly
can sometimes be embarrassing" (p.153). `sig` is separately used in general (non-possessive) reflexive
phrases, ex. `Hon befann sig bredvid honom` ("She found herself next to him").

### Directional adverbs: at-rest vs. in-motion

Swedish systematically distinguishes adverbs used with static verbs (be, stay) from adverbs used with
motion verbs (go, walk, ride) — a paired system the book tabulates in full (p.114, reproduced row by
row in the vocabulary table above): `borta`/`bort`, `där`/`dit`, `framme`/`fram`, `hemma`/`hem`,
`här`/`hit`, `inne`/`in`, `nere`/`ner`, `uppe`/`upp`, `ute`/`ut`, `var`/`vart`. The book's own
etymology essay (p.118) draws the explicit parallel to English's archaic/Shakespearean "hither,"
"thither," "whither" vs. "to here," "to there," "to where" — a distinction English has lost in the
adverb itself but Swedish retains productively.

### `då` vs. `sedan` ("then"), and `var` vs. `där` ("where")

English's "then" covers two distinct Swedish words: `då` ("at that time") and `sedan` ("after that") —
contrasted with worked examples (p.170). Similarly, English "where" is split between the interrogative
`var` (`Var bor du?`, "Where do you live?") and the relative-clause conjunction `där` (`Det finns en
restaurang där vi kan äta`, "There is a restaurant where we can eat").

### Prepositions: full list with meanings and examples

The book gives what it calls "a list of all Swedish prepositions" with approximate meaning and a short
example for each (pp.149-151) — more exhaustively enumerated in one place than this dataset's other
sources, reproduced in the vocabulary table above (`av`, `efter`, `emot`, `framför`, `från`/`ifrån`,
`för...sedan`, `genom`/`igenom`, `hos`, `i`, `med`, `mot`, `om`, `på`, `till`, `under`, `vid`, `åt`,
`över`). Two emphasis-only near-synonym pairs are explicitly called out: `från`/`ifrån` and
`genom`/`igenom` — "the primary difference...is one of emphasis" in both cases, with `ifrån` also
forming the compounds `härifrån`/`därifrån`/`varifrån` ("from here/there/where").

### Time-preposition idioms

A dedicated subsection (§1.1, pp.150-152) tabulates ~25 time-phrase idioms with their Swedish
equivalents, more systematically than a simple vocabulary list: `för en vecka sedan` ("a week ago"),
`om en vecka` ("in a week"), `i våras`/`i vintras`/`i somras`/`i höstas` ("last spring/winter/summer/
fall"), `i förrgår`/`igår`/`idag`/`imorgon`/`i övermorgon` ("day before yesterday" through "day after
tomorrow"), `i morse`/`i eftermiddag`/`i kväll`/`i natt` ("this morning/afternoon/evening," "last
night"/"tonight" — `i natt` serves both), `i måndags`/`i tisdags` (etc., "last Monday/Tuesday..."),
`en gång om dagen` ("once a day"), `fem i sex`/`fem över sex` ("five to/after six"). The adjacent
`gång`/`tid` distinction (countable vs. abstract "time") is covered in the vocabulary table above.

### Coordinating vs. subordinating conjunctions

The book separately lists coordinating conjunctions — `eller`, `för`, `men`, `och`, `så`, `ty`
(flagged archaic), `utan` — and subordinating conjunctions — `att`, `då`, `eftersom`, `för att`,
`förrän`, `innan`, `när`, `sedan`, `tills`, `därför att`, `fast`/`fastän`, `medan`, `om`, `så att`,
`trots att` — and states explicitly that the coordinating/subordinating distinction governs word order
(subordinate clauses never front their adverb before the verb; main clauses can), deferring the full
word-order mechanics to Lesson 10.

### Word order: V2, question formation, and dependent-clause adverb placement

Standard Swedish verb-second (V2) word order (subject-verb-object-preposition baseline; any fronted
element forces subject-verb inversion; verb-first forms a question) is covered elsewhere in this
dataset, but the book's own worked example set is unusually systematic in showing the same sentence
restructured four ways for emphasis: `Jag köpte ett äpple i affären` ("I bought an apple in the
store") → `Ett äpple köpte jag i affären` (object-fronted) → `I affären köpte jag ett äpple`
(prepositional-phrase-fronted) → `Köpte jag ett äpple i affären?` (verb-fronted question) (p.205).
Adverb placement in a dependent clause is explicitly stated to go **before** the verb rather than
after it (the position it takes in an independent clause) — ex. `Jag vet att jag inte köpte ett äpple
i affären` ("I know that I did not buy an apple in the store") — a contrast with independent-clause
adverb placement (`Jag köpte inte ett äpple...`) that the book frames as the single most important
word-order rule distinguishing the two clause types (p.206).

### Etymology essays: summary of per-lesson distinctive content not already listed above

Beyond the items already pulled into the vocabulary table and grammar points above, each of the ten
per-lesson "Etymology" sections makes at least one additional comparative-linguistics point:
Lesson 1 (p.37) — Swedish retained the plural `-r` from the same Proto-Germanic plural marker that
became English `-s`, and the Swedish neuter `-n` plural survives in English only as a fossil (`ox`/
`oxen`); also notes `n` was lost before `k` in Swedish (`dricka`/`tack` vs. English `drink`/`thank`)
while English's final `-g` became `-y` (`dag`/`day`).
Lesson 2 (p.55) — Swedish's lack of periphrastic "is going"/"does go" constructions (it has only
`går`) is framed as making Swedish *more* regular than English, not less; `ska`/`skall` is cognate
with English `shall`; Swedish `sk` sounds are cognate with English `sh` sounds borrowed before an early
English sound shift (`skjorta`/`shirt` vs. `kjol`/`skirt` as a doublet from the same root, split by the
shift).
Lesson 3 (p.78) — the three-gender-to-two-gender collapse (`en`/`ett`) is historically parallel to, not
different in kind from, English's own three-to-one collapse; English's third-person plural pronoun
`they` is itself a Scandinavian loanword replacing Old English's `hie`, because `hie` had become too
easily confused with `he`.
Lesson 4 (p.99) — the days of the week in both languages derive from Roman/Germanic pagan-god naming:
`söndag`/`måndag` (sun/moon), `tisdag` (Tyr, war/justice god who lost his hand binding the wolf
Fenrir), `onsdag` (Odin, who traded an eye for wisdom at Mimir's well), `torsdag` (Thor), `fredag`
(Freyja); uniquely, Swedish `lördag` ("Saturday") breaks the pagan-god pattern and instead literally
means "washing day."
Lesson 5 (p.118) — in addition to the `du`/`ni` note already captured in the vocabulary table, the
essay traces `-lig`→`-ly` (adjective-to-adverb suffix) as the same historical suffix with Swedish
retaining the `g` sound as a `y`-glide before front vowels; and traces `varför` ("why") as a cognate of
archaic English "wherefore."
Lesson 6 (p.137) — most Swedish/English color-word pairs are transparent cognates (`blå`/`blue`,
`grön`/`green`, `röd`/`red`, `vit`/`white`, even `gul`/`yellow` via the g→y shift) except `svart`
("black"), which the essay traces to actually being a distant cognate of the *Romance* word for
"white" (French `blanc`, Spanish `blanco`, from a root meaning "ash" — ash being sometimes white,
sometimes black).
Lesson 8 (p.174) — contrasts Icelandic's preference for coining new technology vocabulary from native
roots (`tölva` = `telja` "to count" + `völva` "to foresee/prophecy," for "computer") against Swedish's
more common strategy of direct borrowing, with `dator` as a rare successful native-coinage
counterexample (already in the vocabulary table above).
Lesson 9 (p.192) — alongside the `tala vid`/`öl`-gender prescriptive-vs-actual-usage notes already
captured above, this essay also notes English and Swedish participle formation are historically
parallel (English `-ing` ousted a competing Old English `-and` form that survives as Swedish's
present-participle `-ande`; weak-verb past participles in both languages derive from the same `-d`/
`-ed` suffix).
Lesson 10 (p.209) — notes Swedish's strict V2 word order is itself a conservative retention of a
pattern Modern English has mostly lost, surviving only in fossilized constructions like "'I am going
now,' said John"; also notes Swedish's general policy of using a foreign place's own native name
(`München`, not an anglicized form) where English anglicizes, with exceptions for places long in the
Swedish cultural consciousness (`Frankrike` = "kingdom of the Franks" for France; `Tyskland`, a sound-
shifted cognate of German `Deutschland`, for Germany), and a `-ia`→`-ien` country-name-ending pattern
(`Kalifornien`).

**Coverage note:** this is a sampling pass across a native-language personal-essay/short-prose
collection, not an exhaustive transcription — see `../../00_Reference_Extraction_Spec.md`'s coverage

the sampled pages** — every page inspected was clean, consistently typeset printed text; nothing was

**Publication/author context (load-bearing for several entries below):** title-page verso reads
"Mariehamns Tryckeri 1999" and the ISBN carries Finland's national prefix (952-...). Mariehamn is the
capital of Åland, Finland's autonomous Swedish-speaking region — strong corroborating evidence (on top
of the text's own explicit self-commentary, see below) that this is a Finland-Swedish-published work,
not a Sweden-Swedish one. The book is a collection of personal essays/vignettes (anecdotes, overheard
dialogue, reflective pieces), not a novel with sustained narrative — register runs from plain literary
prose to reported colloquial dialogue, with occasional profanity in quoted speech.

## Grammar points

### Archaic plural copula `äro` used for ironic/mock-authoritative effect (p. 59)

Standard modern Swedish has a single invariant present-tense form of "to be" — `är` — for all
persons and numbers. Before the 1944/1945 spelling and grammar reform, written Swedish still
distinguished a separate plural copula, `äro` (vs. singular `är`), in the older literary-formal
register. Alander deliberately reaches for this long-obsolete plural form in "Alla negrer äro lata"
to report a young sailor's crude generalization — the archaic, almost legalistic-sounding verb form
lends the sentence a mock air of scholarly/proverbial authority that throws the prejudice's crudeness
into sharper relief. This is a clean example of an author reaching outside the standard contemporary
paradigm for a specific stylistic/ironic effect rather than using it as live, productive grammar.

### Productive taboo-intensifier family built on `fan`/`jävel`

Across the sampled pages, two related taboo roots recur as general-purpose intensifiers rather than
literal references to the devil: `jävla` (pre-posed adjective: `jävla stad`, `jävla friheter`) and
`för fan` (post-posed/parenthetical oath: `för fan galen`). Both derive from religious-taboo
vocabulary (`fan` = "the devil," `jävel` = "devil/wretch") but function purely as intensity markers
in contemporary colloquial Swedish, with no literal diabolical content — consistent with this
project's `taboo_inversion`/intensifier-type slang-derivation patterns already documented for other
languages. Worth comparing against any Swedish synthesis work building taboo-sourced intensifiers.

### Explicit author-stated Finland-Swedish/Sweden-Swedish prosodic distinction (p. 28)

Not a grammar rule in the traditional sense, but a directly source-marked sociolinguistic claim worth
recording as structural context for any future Swedish mechanics-analysis work: the narrator states
in his own authorial voice that Finland-Swedish speakers have a recognizable `tonfall` ("intonation/
tone") that Sweden-Swedish listeners, when unfamiliar with it, mislabel `finsk brytning` ("Finnish
accent/brogue") — i.e., mistake a Swedish-dialect-internal prosodic difference for a foreign-language
accent. This is exactly the kind of Sweden/Finland linguistic-boundary material this dispatch was
watching for, and corroborates (from the Åbo/Turku-region author's own stated perspective) the general
real-world sociolinguistic fact that Finland-Swedish is a distinct regional variety, not simply
"Swedish spoken with an accent."

## Comparison note (maritime/archipelago vocabulary cluster)

As flagged in the dispatch: this book's maritime/shore-leave vocabulary (`krogrond`, `akterdäcket
görs sjöklart`, `skalka`, `lastbommarna firas`, `massatraden`) and its archipelago/country vocabulary
(`vedbacken`, `strandskjul`, `sundet`) sit in the same general domain as the archipelago/nautical
cluster found in Astrid Lindgren's *Vi på Saltkråkan* by a sibling subagent, but the overlap found
here was domain-level rather than term-level — no exact vocabulary item repeated across the two
sources in the pages sampled. Alander's maritime vocabulary leans toward merchant-shipping/dockside
and shore-leave register (cargo, hatches, pub-crawling sailors) rather than Saltkråkan's
small-craft/children's-summer register; worth a direct side-by-side pass if a future task wants to
build out a combined Swedish maritime-register glossary.

## Overall register assessment

This source is a genuine mixed-register find, not a one-sided result: the narrative/descriptive prose
itself stays fairly plain literary Swedish throughout, but **reported dialogue carries real colloquial
and taboo material** (the `jävla`/`för fan` intensifier family, `supa och slåss`, `skallen`,
`sjabbig`), and the book's single most valuable finding for this project is not slang at all but the
explicit, author-stated Finland-Swedish/Sweden-Swedish sociolinguistic self-commentary on p. 28 — a
rare case of a source directly naming and discussing the regional-variety boundary this dispatch was
sent to look for, rather than merely exhibiting it indirectly through vocabulary choice.

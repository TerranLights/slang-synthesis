### Grammar points

#### Definite declension Type 2
After a genitive (Svens nya båt), a possessive (hans risiga bil), a demonstrative like *denna* (denna gropiga grusväg), or words like *samma/nästa/följande/föregående* (samma mörka skog), the adjective takes the definite ending (-a, sometimes -e) but the noun takes **no** end article — contrasting with Type 1 (Lesson 11), which uses both a front article and an end article. Two exceptions: after a genitive of measurement, the adjective reverts to the *indefinite* form agreeing with the following noun (en sorts dyrbar båt); and *egen* stays indefinite after both genitive and possessive (pappas egen lilla Eva). *Denna* itself is typically written/formal register; *den här* is typically spoken/informal and always takes an end article on the noun.

#### Modal verbs: the full paradigm and false friends
kunna/kan/kunde/kunnat (can); skola/ska(ll)/skulle/skolat (shall/will); vilja/vill/ville/velat (want to — a false friend, since *ska* often covers English "will" while *vill* means "want to"); måste/måste/måst (must — but *måste inte* is a false friend meaning "don't need to," not "must not," which is instead *får inte*); böra/bör/borde/bort (should/ought to); få/får/fick/fått (may/be allowed to). *Skulle* without a modal sense often marks a conditional ("would"), typically paired with an *om/ifall* clause.

#### Uses of the infinitive
Without *att*: after modal verbs and modal-equivalent two-verb constructions (tänker, behöver, börjar, fortsätter, försöker, hoppas, lyckas, orkar, slipper, slutar, vägrar), and in "object + infinitive" constructions after se/höra/låta/tillåta/anse (Jag såg honom gå — "I saw him leave," where English likewise allows a bare infinitive here). With *att*: as subject (Att köra bil kan vara krångligt), as object (Han älskar att köra fort), after a preposition (efter att ha stannat motorn; utan att säga adjö), and after a noun (Har du tid att göra det?). Swedish infinitive with *att* frequently corresponds to an English "-ing" gerund. Note *för att* specifically marks purpose/intention before an infinitive (Han åkte dit för att hälsa på...).

#### Duplicates and "heavy" expressions in spoken Swedish
Spoken Swedish commonly duplicates a subject, object, or adverb at the clause boundary for emphasis (Ska det vara nödvändigt, det?; Jocke, han är aldrig hemma, han) — a colloquial pattern with no direct English equivalent. The particles *så* and *då*, placed clause-internally, often echo/pick up a phrase that sits outside the clause proper (När vi kom ut, så fungerade inte pumpen). Separately, "heavy" constituents (infinitive phrases, object clauses) are pushed to the very end of the clause, after any MPT-expression.

### Grammar points

#### Article use (I)
Swedish uses the end article where English uses none for: abstract nouns in a general sense (Svenskarna älskar naturen — "Swedes love nature"); certain fixed locations (gå i kyrkan/skolan — "go to church/school"); and days/seasons/festivals used generically (på fredagarna, på vintern). Conversely, Swedish uses no article where English uses "a/an" for: nationality/profession/religion/politics nouns (as already seen in Lesson 2); count nouns in a fully general sense (göra upp eld — "light a fire"; söka lägenhet — "look for a flat"); and nouns following *vilken* or *mången* (Vilken vacker dag! — "What a beautiful day!").

#### Definite declension Type 3
No front article, adjective takes the definite ending, noun takes the end article — occurs in set phrases where the adjective has lost its own stress (Svenska akademien, Röda korset, Förenta staterna), and consistently with *hela/halva/själva* (hela tiden), *båda/förra/ena/enda* (båda flickorna, förra veckan), geographical names (Gamla stan, Döda havet), nationality adjectives (franska revolutionen), colour adjectives in proper names (Röda armén), locational phrases (på högra sidan), compass points (västra halvklotet), and ordinal numbers (första gången).

#### Adjectives vs. adverbs in -t
Manner adverbs formed by adding -t to an adjective stem look identical to a T-word adjective's -t form but function differently in the sentence (Ulla sjunger vackert = adverb, vs. rummet är vackert = adjective). A separate set of adverbs is formed with -en or -tvis (möjligen, naturligtvis). -t adverbs are MPT-expressions typically clause-final; -en/-tvis adverbs behave as sentence adverbs, patterning like *inte*.

#### Permission and prohibition
Key phrases: *Man får (inte) göra det* (You may(not) do that); *Förbud mot...* (...is prohibited); *Man bör göra det* (One should do that); *Man har rätt att...* (One is permitted to...). *Måste* is again flagged as a false friend: *måste* = must, but *måste inte* = don't need to (not "must not," which requires *får inte*).

#### Reflexive pronouns and verbs
Only third person (singular and plural) has a dedicated reflexive pronoun (*sig*); first/second person reuse the ordinary object pronouns (mig, dig, oss, er) reflexively. The reflexive refers back to the clause's subject, including after a preposition (Han stängde dörren efter sig — "He closed the door behind him," where English requires an object pronoun, not a reflexive, in this slot). Exception: in "object + infinitive" constructions, a reflexive pronoun refers to the *object* while a plain object pronoun refers to the subject (Han bad doktorn tvätta sig = "he asked the doctor to wash himself" vs. tvätta honom = "...to wash him [someone else]"). Many verbs that are non-reflexive in English are obligatorily reflexive in Swedish (raka sig "shave," gifta sig "get married," förkyla sig "catch a cold"), and many reflexive verbs denote movement (sätta sig "sit down," resa sig "get up," skynda sig "hurry"). Unstressed reflexive/object pronouns can shift to immediately after VERB1 (ahead of the adverb) when the verb is simple (present/past) — paralleling stressed-object placement rules.

#### Present participles
Formed with *-(a)nde* (consonant/-a stems) or *-ende* (long-vowel stems): studera→studerande, skriva→skrivande, tro→troende. Rarely used verbally in Swedish; instead typically lexicalized as an adjective (levande träd — "living trees"), a noun (en studerande — "a student"; N-gender participial nouns denote a person, T-gender ones a verbal abstract), or an adverb (fortfarande — "still"). Occasionally used after *komma/gå/bli* and motion verbs the way English uses "-ing" (älgen kom gående genom skogen).

### Grammar points

#### Four methods of adjective comparison
(a) -are/-ast, the regular pattern (rolig/roligare/roligast), including all adjectives in -ig and notably *intelligent, intressant, modern, skicklig, svår* (unlike their English cognates' comparison); (b) vowel change + -re/-st, a closed set to memorize (stor/större/störst; grov/grövre/grövst; låg/lägre/lägst; lång/längre/längst; trång/trängre/trängst; få/färre; små/smärre; ung/yngre/yngst; tung/tyngre/tyngst; hög/högre/högst); (c) irregular stem-changing comparison, also a closed set (god/bättre/bäst for quality, god/godare/godast for taste; dålig/sämre/sämst; ond/värre/värst; gammal/äldre/äldst; liten/mindre/minst; många/fler/flest; mycket/mer/mest); (d) *mer/mest*, used for past participles and bisyllabic -isk adjectives plus an increasing number of long/polysyllabic adjectives, and these must still agree with the noun in the indefinite or take a definite ending (ett mer komplicerat problem). Within irregular comparison, *värre* means "more of an originally bad quality" while *sämre* means "less of an originally good quality" — a subtle semantic split with no single English equivalent; *mer/mest* pair with non-count nouns, *fler/flest* with count nouns.

#### Similarity and dissimilarity
Dissimilarity: *starkare än* (stronger than), *för stark* (too strong), *annorlunda än* / *olik* (different from — the latter agrees like an adjective). Similarity: *lik* (agreeing adjective) and *likna* (agreement-free verb) both render "be like"; *lika...som* (as...as); *samma...som* (the same...as); *likadan som* (like, similar to); *så...som (han) kan* (as...as (he) can).

#### Definite forms of superlatives
The comparative never inflects for definiteness (den vackrare flickan — no extra ending). The superlative does: forms in *-ast* always add *-e* (den vackraste flickan); forms in *-st* add *-a* (or *-e* for a single male referent) (den största flickan / den störste pojken); *mest*-superlatives add *-a* (or *-e* for a single male referent) to the adjective (den mest intelligenta flickan / den mest sympatiske studenten).

#### Article use (2)
No article in Swedish where English has "the": with instruments/media (spela piano, titta på TV, lyssna på radio); with proper names in possessive-style constructions (hos Olssons); and after *nästa, samma, fel, rätt, följande, föregående* (nästa by, fel väg).

#### Compounding (-s- linking rule)
Two main compound types: NOUN+NOUN (rösträtt, vänsterparti) and NOUN+s+NOUN (kvällspress). The linking *-s-* appears when the first element is itself already a compound, or ends in *-(n)ing, -ling, -an, -nad, -(i)tet, -(a)tion* (koalitionsregering). Less common linking methods: dropping a final -a/-e from the first noun (pojke+namn→pojknamn), inserting a link vowel (familj+sida→familjesida), or (now largely obsolete for new coinages) an old case ending (vecka+slut→veckoslut).

### Grammar points

#### Reflexive possessive pronoun *sin* vs. non-reflexive *hans/hennes/dess/deras*
*Sin/sitt/sina* can only qualify the *object* of a clause and must refer back to that same clause's *subject* (Han har sålt sin bil — "He has sold his [own] car"). The non-reflexive forms *hans/hennes/dess/deras* never refer back to their own clause's subject (Olle gick ut med hans fru = "he went out with his [someone else's] wife"). This breaks down across a clause boundary (use *deras* in "De tycker att deras lektioner är tråkiga," since "their lessons" is now the subject of a new subordinate clause) and with a double/compound subject (use *hans* in "Han och hans fru åkte bort," since "he and his wife" together form the subject).

#### Article use (3): end article replaces a possessive
With parts of the body and clothing where ownership is self-evident, Swedish uses the plain end article where English requires a possessive (Hon satte på sig morgonrocken — "She put on her dressing gown"; Hon ska tvätta håret — "She is going to wash her hair"; Han har ont i magen — "He has a stomach ache").

#### Transitive/intransitive verb pairs
Where English often uses one verb both transitively and intransitively, Swedish frequently uses two distinct verbs: odla (grow something) vs. växa (grow, of a plant itself); röka (smoke something) vs. ryka (emit smoke). A further systematic set is distinguished by conjugation/strength alone: sätta (put, weak) vs. sitta (sit/be sitting, strong); lägga (lay, weak) vs. ligga (lie/be lying, strong); fälla (fell, weak) vs. falla (fall, strong); bränna (burn something, weak) vs. brinna (be burning, strong); kyla (chill, weak) vs. kallna (cool down, weak-intransitive); trötta (tire someone, weak) vs. tröttna (get tired, weak-intransitive).

#### Position of unstressed object pronouns
An unstressed pronoun object moves to immediately before the sentence-adverb (ahead of where a full noun object would sit): Först hörde hon den inte (pronoun-adverb) vs. Först hörde hon inte väckarklockan (adverb-noun). A stressed pronoun (used for contrast) instead patterns like a full noun, staying after the adverb.

#### Beginning, continuing, ending
*Börja* (begin); *sätta igång* (start, get going); *starta* (a false friend — means "set out"/"start an engine"/"take off," not generic "begin"); *hålla på (och/med att)* (be in the middle of doing something); *pågå* (continue, be in progress — alternative to the already-known continue-verbs); *sluta* (end, stop).

#### Subjunctives
Rare in modern Swedish; survives as a distinct -e verb form mainly in *vore* (from vara) and a handful of fixed/frozen expressions (Leve brudparet! — "Long live the bridal couple!"; tack vare — "thanks to"). The *past* subjunctive of strong/irregular verbs survives more productively as an alternative to the ordinary past tense specifically for unreal/hypothetical conditions (Om jag vore mycket rik... — "If I were very rich...", alongside the now-equally-acceptable Om jag var...).

### Grammar points

#### Past participle formation (four patterns)
(a) Weak verbs, stem in -a or a voiced consonant (Conj. I, IIa): add -d (packa→packad); (b) weak verbs, stem in a voiceless consonant -k/-p/-s/-t/-x (Conj. IIb): add -t (köpa→köpt); (c) weak verbs, stem in a long stressed vowel (Conj. III): add -dd (klä→klädd); (d) strong verbs: delete the supine's -it and add -en (skriva→skrivit→skriven). The past participle is a distinct form from the supine (used only with har/hade for perfect/pluperfect) — it functions as a true adjective, with its own indefinite (nymålad/nymålat/nymålade) and definite (den nymålade/det nymålade) declension, following the same N-word/T-word/plural agreement pattern as any other adjective. Conjugation IIb past participles are identical in N- and T-form (both -t); the definite/plural of -ad participles is -e regardless of gender, while other participles use -a.

#### Particle verbs
A stressed particle (adverb or preposition) + verb forms a single unit of meaning distinct from the same preposition used unstressed (Han sätter sig på regnrocken "sits on" [unstressed, literal] vs. Han sätter på sig regnrocken "puts on" [stressed particle, idiomatic]). Many common prepositions (på, av, med, efter, om, till, över) and adverbs (omkring, runt, förbi, tillbaka, kvar, igång, iväg, undan, omkull, sönder, fast, loss, ihop, isär, itu, ikapp, ihjäl) function this way. In participle forms, the particle becomes a fused prefix on the verb (instängd < stänga in; utsliten < slita ut; omhändertagen < ta hand om).

#### Compound verbs: separable vs. inseparable
Inseparable-prefix compounds (betala, förklara, oroa, undkomma) never split. Separable-prefix compounds (känna igen/igenkänna "recognise"; bjuda in/inbjuda "invite") have two coexisting forms per tense; participle forms are always the joined-up version. As a rule, the separated form tends toward lower style/concrete meaning while the joined-up form tends toward higher style/abstract meaning (gick förbi mig "walked past me" [concrete] vs. förbigick mig "passed over/disregarded me" [abstract]).

#### Position of particles in the sentence pattern
In Swedish, a particle immediately follows VERB2 (or stands in VERB2's own slot if there's no separate infinitive), and the object follows the particle — the reverse of English's "verb + object + particle" order (ring him up → ringa upp honom; threw the heckler out → kastade ut hecklaren).

#### Affixation
Prefixes change meaning without changing word class; suffixes typically do change word class. Negative prefixes: o- ("not"), in-/miss-/van- (all roughly "bad, wrongly"). Attitude prefixes: sam-/med- ("together with"), mot- ("against"). Person-denoting noun suffixes: -are (arbetare), -ande/-ende (studerande, gående), -ant/-ent (emigrant, konsument), -ing/-ist (skåning, cyklist); feminine suffixes -inna/-(er)ska/-essa (värdinna, städerska, prinsessa). Verb→adjective suffixes: -bar, -lig, -ig, -abel. Noun→adjective suffixes: -enlig, -mässig, -aktig, -(i)sk. Noun/adjective→verb suffixes: -a, -era, and the "become + adjective" suffixes -na/-ja (gulna "go yellow," vänja "grow accustomed").

#### Demonyms and dialect names
Several productive but inconsistent patterns: town-dwellers often take -are (stockholmare, göteborgare) or an exceptional form (lundensare for Lund); province-dwellers often take -ing/-änning (skåning, värmlänning, smålänning, närking), but Östergötland/Västergötland instead use östgöte/västgöte; generic town residents elsewhere can take -bo (en Gävlebo, en Londonbo). Dalarna is fully irregular with distinct male/female demonyms (dalmas/dalkulla) and its own dialect name (dalmål) rather than a derived adjective+-ska pattern. Province adjectives are formed with -sk (skånsk, värmländsk), and the matching dialect name adds -a (skånska, värmländska).

### Grammar points

#### Three passive constructions
(1) S-passives (add -s to the verb) are the most common type, often expressing repeated action or appearing in objective journalistic style, frequently with no agent (Dörrarna öppnas kl 7; Två personer skadades). (2) Bli-passives (bli + past participle) typically express a single action and frequently carry a significant agent (Han har blivit påkörd av en motorcykel). (3) Vara-passives (vara + past participle) express the resulting state of a completed action, descriptively (Huset är sålt — "The house is [has been] sold"). Only transitive verbs can be passivized; the passive is used especially when the agent is unknown or unimportant, in which case it's simply omitted.

#### Position of the passive agent
When present, the agent (introduced by *av*) goes after VERB2 and before any MPT-expression; a passive clause has no object slot at all (the former object has become the subject).

#### Independent (stand-alone) sub clauses
A subordinate clause can stand alone as a complete sentence/exclamation, keeping sub-clause (SIV) word order even without a governing main clause — typically an exclamation (Att jag kunde vara så dum! — "That I could be so stupid!"), a wish (Om jag ändå var rik! — "If only I were rich!"), or an elliptical answer to a question where the main clause is implied/dropped (För att jag just har skurit mig — "[I did that] because I've just cut myself").

#### Words for males and females
Several distinct strategies: (a) gendered suffixes (vän/väninna, kassör/kassörska, skådespelare/skådespelerska, aktör/aktris) — though the book notes sexist job-title suffixes are actively falling out of use (lärarinna→lärare, städerska→städare are now unisex); (b) the -e(male)/-a(female) adjectival-noun pattern already seen (den gamle/den gamla); (c) entirely distinct words (make/maka, hane/hona, tupp/höna, hingst/sto). Some nouns not denoting a female take feminine pronoun reference by convention (klockan — hon är fem; människan — hon; solen — hon/den). Several person-denoting nouns are grammatically T-gender regardless of the referent's sex (ett barn, ett statsråd, ett vittne, ett biträde, ett geni/snille).

#### Abbreviation types
Clipping from the beginning ((bi)cykel), middle (mo(torveloci)ped), or end (el(ektricitet)); initialisms reduced to first letters, in two flavors — alphabetisms pronounced letter-by-letter (bh, UD), sometimes re-spelled to reflect pronunciation (behå, teve), and true acronyms pronounced as words (NATO, SAAB, SAS), including hybrid forms mixing an acronym element with a full word (T-bana, u-båt, p-plats).

### Grammar points

#### Sub clauses with main clause word order
Three documented exceptions to the SIV subordinate-clause rule, all converging toward main-clause (verb-subject-inverted) order: (a) an *att*-clause beginning with a fronted non-subject inverts just like a main clause (Janne säger att imorgon åker han inte hem); (b) spontaneous spoken Swedish sometimes uses main-clause order after *att* even without a fronted element, though this is not acceptable in writing; (c) conditional clauses can drop *om/ifall* entirely and signal "if" purely through yes/no-question-style inversion (Bjuder du inte henne, (så) blir hon ledsen — paralleling the archaic/literary English "Had I known...").

#### Determinative pronouns
A determinative pronoun is a demonstrative used specifically to introduce a restrictive *som*-clause that completes its meaning, and (unlike an ordinary demonstrative) the following noun takes no end article: De turister som åkte till Island... ("Those tourists who went to Iceland...", contrastive) vs. De turisterna ("Those tourists," plain demonstrative, no necessary contrast). The book notes plain demonstratives are increasingly replacing determinatives in informal registers — i.e. this distinction is itself eroding toward the colloquial end of the spectrum.

#### Written vs. spoken register features
Spoken/informal-written features: dropping *att* after verbs of saying/thinking; dropping *som* as a relative-clause object; the duplicate construction (Lesson 12); main-clause order after *att* (above). More formal-written features: dropping *har/hade* in a subordinate clause (Han påstod att han blivit bestulen); moving MPT-expressions leftward into the adverb slot; fronting heavy subordinate/infinitive constituents; preferring nominalizations over verbs (skadat→anställt skadegörelse); preferring the s-passive. This gives a coherent cline from casual speech to formal prose that the book treats as a single continuum rather than a binary split.

#### Punctuation differences from English
No comma before an *att*-clause or after a letter's opening/closing phrase; no apostrophe with the genitive -s (pojkens far); the exclamation mark is used more freely in Swedish (Vad vackert det är här!, Lycka till!, Obs!); a hyphen marks an elided shared element across two compounds (låg- och mellanstadiet = lågstadiet och mellanstadiet).

#### Letter-writing conventions
Private letters: full address on the envelope back, only town+date at the letter's top right. Business letters: printed sender address at the bottom, recipient's address added top left. Two standard date formats: 960724 or den 24 juli 1996. No equivalent to "Dear Sir" — either state the letter's purpose as a heading or launch straight in; informal openings use *Kära [Name]!* or *Hej!*; formal closings use *Med vänlig hälsning*, informal closings use *Hjärtliga hälsningar* or just *Hälsningar* (with *Kram* "hugs/love" noted as an increasingly common informal sign-off).

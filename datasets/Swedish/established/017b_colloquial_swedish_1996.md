### Grammar points

#### Objects and complements
Direct objects occur with transitive verbs (äta, gilla); intransitive verbs (sova) take none — this distinction matters later in Swedish grammar. Indirect objects (people benefiting from an action) can precede the direct object or follow it as a "till" phrase. Complements fill out the meaning of "empty" verbs (vara, bli, heta) and are nouns or adjectives; subject complements agree with the subject in number/gender, object complements agree with the object.

#### Indefinite-form adjective irregularities
Beyond the plain -t/-a pattern: adjectives ending in a stressed vowel double the final consonant and add -tt with T-words (ny→nytt); adjectives ending in vowel+d drop the -d and add -tt (bred→brett; also god, glad, röd); adjectives ending in consonant+d drop -d before adding -t (hård→hårt; also mild, vild, ond); adjectives in -er/-el drop the e before the plural -a (vacker→vackra; also mager, nykter, säker, enkel, acceptabel, flexibel); adjectives in -en drop both e and n in the T-form (öppen→öppet→öppna; also egen, ledsen). *Liten* and *gammal* are irregular throughout and must be learned as exceptions.

#### Difficult noun plurals (systematic groups beyond Lesson 5's rules)
(a) A small set changes its root vowel and adds *-er* (hand→händer, land→länder, natt→nätter, bok→böcker, fot→fötter, stad→städer); (b) some vowel-final nouns of either gender add *-r* (sko→skor, tå→tår); (c) nouns ending vowel+n/r drop the vowel before the plural ending (fågel→fåglar, sommar→somrar); (d) loanwords in *-ium/-eum* drop that ending before *-er* (museum→museer); (e) some *-um* loanwords have alternative plurals (centrum→centrum/centrer/centra); (f) a few nouns are fully irregular (öga→ögon, huvud→huvuden, öra→öron); (g) some recent English loanwords keep an English-style *-s* plural (en thriller→två thrillers).

#### Demonstratives
*Den här* (this), *den där* (that), and stressed *'den* (anaphoric "the") all trigger the noun's end article in addition to the demonstrative itself: *den här soffan*, *det här bordet*, *de här sofforna/borden*. Both parts of the demonstrative + noun can also stand alone without a following noun.

#### Amplifiers
Adverbs that intensify an adjective or adverb: *mycket* (very), *helt/alldeles/fullständigt* (completely), *ganska* (rather), *lite* (a little), *inte alls* (not at all). Within the sentence pattern, AMP+ADJ functions as a Complement and AMP+ADV as an MPT-expression (manner). *Mycket* alone can mean "a lot" (as a verb modifier rather than an amplifier). Many adjective-derived adverbs (adjective + -t) double as colorful/colloquial-leaning amplifiers: *förskräckligt, hemskt, väldigt, otroligt* (all roughly "terribly/awfully/very/unbelievably").

#### Spelling of words ending in -m / -n
Contrary to the general long-vowel-single-consonant / short-vowel-double-consonant rule, most words ending in *-m* or *-n* have a short vowel followed by only a single *m*/*n* (dum, hem, rum, program) — the final consonant behaves as if already doubled. Between two vowels (e.g. before an inflectional ending), the *m*/*n* is written double. Conversely, an inflected form sometimes drops one *m*/*n* that appeared doubled in the base form (gammal→gamla, sommar→somrar, nummer→numret).

### Grammar points

#### Linking main clauses
Coordinating conjunctions (och, eller, men, utan, för, antingen...eller, varken...eller) link subjects, objects, verbs, or whole clauses without disturbing normal word order in the clauses they join. *Utan* specifically replaces *men* ("but") after a negated clause; *för* here means "because/as," distinct from the preposition *för* ("for").

#### End-article plural of nouns
Three rules, applied after the plural indefinite form is already known: (1) plurals ending vowel+r add *-na* (flickor→flickorna; nouns in *-are* drop the e: lärare→lärarna); (2) plurals ending in another consonant add *-en* (barn→barnen; stems in *-el/-en/-er* drop the *s*: lager→lagren); (3) T-word plurals ending vowel+n add *-a* (yrken→yrkena).

#### Possessive pronouns (full paradigm)
Unlike the invariant *-s* possessives (hans, hennes, dess, deras — Lesson 4), *min/din/vår/er* must agree with the noun's gender/number like adjectives: min son / mitt hem / mina söner (N-word/T-word/plural).

#### Three ways to express future time
(1) Present tense, often with a time marker (*Jag åker imorgon*); (2) *kommer att* + infinitive, for an event independent of anyone's will/intention, often with an impersonal subject (*Det kommer att regna*); (3) *ska(ll)* + infinitive, indicating intention/warning/threat with a personal subject (*Det här ska du få ångra!* — "You'll regret this!"). None of the three involves a dedicated future verb ending.

#### Prepositions of place: på vs. i, plus exceptions
Rule of thumb: *på* + a surface (på bordet), *i* + a volume/enclosed space (i skåpet). Notable exceptions: *på* is used with public buildings/workplaces/places of entertainment (på kontoret, på bio); *hos* (compared explicitly to French "chez"/German "bei") is used with people's names/professions (hos Olssons, hos tandläkaren); *vid* is used with lengthwise features like a coast or river (vid kusten).

#### Adverbs of location/direction/origin, and "where"
Most Swedish place adverbs have three distinct forms for location (där), direction-toward (dit), and direction-from (därifrån) — a three-way system English collapses into one word ("there"). The same three-way split governs "where": *var* (location) vs. *vart* (motion toward) as an interrogative; *där* (location) vs. *dit* (motion toward) as a relative pronoun.

#### Pronunciation: silent letters and mismatched spelling
A cluster of very common words have a silent final letter (jag, dag, någon, med, vad, det, mycket) with a correspondingly shortened vowel, and a separate cluster is simply not spelled as pronounced (de/dem→dom, att→å/att, och→å/ock, mig/dig/sig→mej/dej/sej). French-origin loanwords (garage, bagage, reportage) keep French-style "zh"-type pronunciation for g/j and drop a final unstressed -e.

### Grammar points

#### Full regular verb paradigm (Conjugations I–IV)
Systematized imperative/infinitive/present/past/supine pattern: Conj. I (stem ends -a: parkera/parkera/parkerar/parkerade/parkerat); Conj. IIa (stem ends voiced consonant: bygg/bygga/bygger/byggde/byggt); Conj. IIb (stem ends unvoiced consonant -k/-p/-s/-t: hjälp/hjälpa/hjälper/hjälpte/hjälpt); Conj. III (stem ends vowel ≠ -a: sy/sy/syr/sydde/sytt); Conj. IV (stem ends consonant, vowel changes in past, no ending in supine: skriv/skriva/skriver/skrev/skrivit). Conjugations I–III are "weak" (ending-based past tense, like English "parked"); Conjugation IV is "strong" (stem-vowel-change past, like English "drank").

#### Perfect and pluperfect
Perfect = *har* + supine (Vi har parkerat bilen); pluperfect = *hade* + supine (Vi hade parkerat bilen). The supine always ends in *-t*, varying by conjugation exactly as the regular paradigm above predicts (parkerat/byggt/hjälpt/sytt/skrivit). Used much as in English.

#### Postponing the subject (formal subject *det*)
When introducing a genuinely new idea, Swedish (like English) postpones the real subject (RS) to the position after the verb and fills the normal subject slot with a formal subject (FS) — always *det* in Swedish, vs. English "there" or "it" depending on context. Crucially, **any** intransitive verb can host this construction in Swedish (Det bor 8 miljoner människor i Sverige — "There are 8 million people living in Sweden"), not just "to be" as in English. *Finnas* is especially frequent in this pattern, and in questions the formal subject immediately precedes the real subject (Finns det inget öl hemma?).

#### Subordinate clauses and link words
A subordinate clause (containing its own verb) can fill an MPT-expression/subject/object slot in a main clause. The most common link words: *att* (that — introduces indirect speech), *om* (whether — indirect yes/no questions); time: *när/då/sedan* (when/after), *medan* (while), *innan* (before); cause: *för att, eftersom, därför att* (because); condition: *om* (if); concession: *fast(än)* (although). A fronted subordinate clause forces inversion in the main clause exactly like any other fronted MPT-expression.

#### *Som* vs. *att*
Both can translate English "that," but *att* is a subordinating conjunction following a verb (Jag sa att hon talade bra svenska), while *som* is a relative pronoun typically following a noun (Kön som hon ställde sig i var lång) and is also used in emphatic cleft constructions. Both are frequently omissible, the same way English "that/which" often is.

#### Indeclinable adjectives
Four groups take no agreement endings at all: (1) adjectives ending in -s (gammaldags, medelålders — but not those in -as, e.g. nervös't is regular... actually the book's own exception is "ett nervöst barn" showing -as-ending adjectives still decline); (2) adjectives ending in -e, including all present participles used adjectivally (öde, främmande, växande); (3) adjectives ending in -a (bra, medeltida); (4) a handful of idiomatic others (slut, fel, sönder).

#### Voiced-to-voiceless consonant shift near s/t
Several consonants devoice when adjacent to *s* or *t* in pronunciation though not in spelling: d>t (onsdag→"osta"), g>k (dags→"dakks", högt→"hökt"), v>f (livstid→"liffstid"), b>p (substantiv→"sumstantif", snabbt→"snapt").

### Grammar points

#### Weak conjugations I/II/III — full past-tense/supine paradigm
Confirms and extends Lesson 8's pattern with explicit past-tense endings: Conj. I (fråga/frågar/frågade/frågat); Conj. IIa, stems in voiced consonants (bygga/bygger/byggde/byggt); Conj. IIb, stems in unvoiced -k/-p/-s/-t/-x (läsa/läser/läste/läst); Conj. III, stem ends in a long stressed vowel other than -a, shortened before -dde/-tt (tro/tror/trodde/trott). Sub-patterns: stem+consonant+d adds only -e in the past and drops -d in the supine (använda→använde→använt); stem+mm/nn drops one consonant before -de/-t (glömma→glömde→glömt, känna→kände→känt); stem+consonant+t adds only -e in the past, drops -t in the supine (gifta→gifte→gift). A sizeable list of irregular weak verbs (betala, heta, kunna, ha, lägga, säga, sälja, veta, vilja, etc.) and irregular Conj. III verbs (be, dö, få, ge, gå, le, se, stå) must be learned individually — Conjugation I alone (all -era verbs, all new loanwords) covers roughly two-thirds of the dictionary's verbs.

#### Definite adjective construction (triple marking)
When an adjective combines with a definite noun, Swedish marks definiteness three separate ways at once: a new "front article" (den/det/de — identical in form to the pronoun "it"/demonstrative "that") precedes the adjective; the adjective itself takes the definite ending -a (den/det/de + goda + maten/ölet/druvorna); and the noun still carries its own end article. Exception: with a singular masculine noun referring to one male person specifically, the adjective's -a often becomes -e (den store pojken, den långe mannen) — everywhere else (feminine, neuter, all plurals) -a is used.

#### Emphatic (cleft) constructions
To emphasize a phrase, move it into the frame "Det är/var X som..." ("It is/was X that..."), paralleling English clefts; note that the "that" here is always *som*. Works the same way in questions (Var det X som...?).

#### Avoiding intrusive -r-
English speakers' habit of inserting a linking -r- between adjacent vowels across a word boundary (as in "Africa(r) and Asia") must be suppressed in Swedish — vowel-final words glide directly into a following vowel-initial word with no inserted consonant.

#### Någon/ingen as pronoun and determiner
*Någon/något/några* can modify a noun (Har du något öl hemma?), stand alone meaning "someone/anyone" (Någon har glömt sina handskar) or "something/anything" (något), or combine with a negative. *Ingen/inget/inga* works the same way for the negative: modifies a noun (De hittade inga bananer), stands alone meaning "no one/none" (Jag kände ingen), or as *inget/ingenting* meaning "nothing."

### Grammar points

#### Strong verbs (Conjugation IV): vowel-change patterns
About 100 strong verbs change their stem vowel for the past tense (and often again for the supine), e.g. dricka→drack→druckit. Vowel patterns recur across whole groups of verbs rather than needing fully individual memorization: i-e-i (skriva/skrev/skrivit, skrika/skrek/skrikit, bli/blev/blivit); y/ju-ö-u (flyga/flög/flugit, frysa/frös/frusit, krypa/kröp/krupit, bjuda/bjöd/bjudit, sjunga/sjöng/sjungit, njuta/njöt/njutit); i-a-u (sitta/satt/suttit, springa/sprang/sprungit, finnas/fanns/funnits); plus irregular patterns (dra/drog/dragit, bära/bar/burit, komma/kom/kommit, äta/åt/ätit, vara/var/varit). Most strong verbs have present tense in -er and supine in -it.

#### Word order in subordinate clauses
A subordinate clause begins with a link word and, unlike a main clause, nearly always keeps straight (subject-verb) word order even where the matching main clause would invert. The non-subclause remainder of the sentence is called the "context."

#### SIV mnemonic
In a main clause, a sentence-adverb (inte, aldrig, alltid, ju, etc.) comes immediately after VERB1; in a subordinate clause, it comes immediately *before* VERB1 — the reverse ordering. The mnemonic SIV (Subject–Inte–Verb, also a Swedish girl's name) captures the subordinate-clause order specifically.

#### Ingen/inte någon
With simple tenses (present/past), *ingen* (inget/inga) and *inte någon* (inte något/inte några) are interchangeable as subject or object (De fick ingen middag = De fick inte någon middag). However, *inte någon* (not *ingen*) is required (1) in a main clause with a complex tense/auxiliary/particle verb, and (2) as the object of a subordinate clause — in both cases following the SIV-style placement of *inte*. The same alternation applies to *ingenting*/*inte någonting* ("nothing") and *ingenstans*/*inte någonstans* ("nowhere").

#### Expressions of time: duration and frequency
Confusing-for-English-speakers patterns: *för...sedan* (ago); *i minuten/veckan* vs. *om dagen/året* vs. *per år* (per minute/week vs. per day/year — two different preposition patterns for the same function); *om* + time period for "in X time" (reser dit om en timme); *på* + time period for "it takes X time" (reser dit på en timme); bare time period for ongoing duration (reser i en timme); *i* + period with a perfect tense for "for X time" (har bott där i ett år).

#### Colloquial intensifying prefixes on adjectives
Four productive slangy prefixes, explicitly flagged by the book as colloquial/slangy register: *jätte-* (usually positive), *toppen-* (always positive), *ur-* (usually negative), *skit-* (both positive and negative — and explicitly noted as weaker in Swedish than its literal English equivalent would suggest). All function as high-degree intensifiers, parallel to but more colorful than the neutral amplifiers covered in Lesson 6.

### Grammar points

#### Four types of definite adjective construction
Type 1 (front article/demonstrative + end article: den stora bilen, den här stora bilen); Type 2 (genitive/possessive/demonstrative *denna*, no end article: Olles nya bil, min nya bil, denna nya bil); Type 3 (no front article + end article: svenska språket, hela dagen); Type 4 (no front article, no end article: lilla vän, första klass). Type 1 is this lesson's main focus; the others are previewed for later lessons.

#### Adjectival nouns
A definite-adjective + noun phrase where the noun is dropped because it's contextually obvious — much as English drops nouns after "the" + adjective in the plural (de unga = "the young"), but Swedish extends this to indefinite forms (en blind = "a blind person"), N-form/T-form singular definites (den gamle/den gamla = "the old man/woman"; det nya, det enda, det sista), and participles (de närvarande = "those present"). Also used for English "a big one"/"the red one" constructions (Jag tar den röda).

#### S-verbs: passive, reciprocal, deponent
Adding *-s* to the normal conjugation (with present tense formed by deleting the normal -r/-er ending before adding -s) produces three distinct verb types: the passive (Bilen lagas — "The car is being mended," discussed fully in Lesson 17); the reciprocal, where a plural subject both performs and receives the action (Vi träffas — "We'll meet (each other)," equivalent to Vi träffar varandra); and deponents, which are simply active verbs that have no non-s form at all (hoppas "hope", finnas "exist", minnas "remember", umgås "socialize", låtsas "pretend", andas "breathe").

#### Pet-names
Swedish has a productive system of informal pet-names distinct from formal given names (Jocke←Joakim, parallel to English Bill←William): Janne, Stickan, Nisse, Kalle, Pelle, Roffe (boys); Kicki, Bettan, Lotta, Maggan (girls), among others listed by the book.

#### *Vem/vad/vilken* + *som* in indirect questions
When *vem*, *vad*, or *vilken* is the *subject* of an indirect question (a subordinate clause), it must be followed by the subject-marking particle *som*, which has no English equivalent (Jag undrar vem som kysser henne nu — "I wonder who's kissing her now") — contrasted with the same words as object, which take no *som* (Jag undrar vem hon älskar — "I wonder who she loves").

# Swedish — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Swedish Grammar, Modern (Björkhagen, 1923)

**Source:** Im. Björkhagen, *Modern Swedish Grammar* (P.A. Norstedt & Söner, Stockholm, 1923) — a
reference grammar for English-speaking learners, covering Pronunciation/Accentuation/Spoken
Language, then the Noun, the Adjective, the Numerals, the Pronouns, the Verb, the Adverb,
Conjunctions, Prepositions, and Order of the Words (pp. 1-208+).

**Non-redundant-supplement scope decision, per this project's standing dispatch convention.** By
the time this file was written, `001_swedish_an_essential_grammar.md` (Holmes & Hinchliffe, 2008)
had already comprehensively extracted a modern Swedish reference grammar covering almost exactly
the same chapter sequence (phonology, nouns, adjectives, pronouns, numerals, verbs, adverbs,
prepositions, conjunctions, word order). This 1923 source overlaps heavily with 001 in scope and
pedagogical structure. **Rather than re-extracting the shared territory, this file captures only
what is genuinely new, deeper, or historically distinct** — and there turns out to be a substantial
amount of exactly that, because this is a *different era* of standard Swedish, not just a different
author's restatement of the same modern grammar:

- A **four-gender noun system** (masculine/feminine/common/neuter) that the modern standard (001)
  has collapsed into two (non-neuter/neuter) — a genuine historical grammatical change, not just a
  different analytical framework.
- **Verb number agreement surviving in the written standard** (distinct singular/plural verb forms,
  e.g. `jag binder` "I bind" vs. `vi binda` "we bind"), explicitly marked by the source as already
  "gradually falling out of use" even in 1923 — directly datable evidence for *when* this leveling
  was in progress, which 001 (2008) simply presents as a settled fact with no historical note.
  Confirmed to still be alive specifically for `ni` (formal "you") as late as the 1920s (§129: the
  singular verb form is used with `ni` even for a plural addressee, in both speech and increasingly
  in writing) — a live snapshot of agreement actually collapsing in real time.
- The **archaic 2nd-person-plural pronoun `I`** ("ye"), already confined by 1923 to "elevated style
  (in the Bible, in poetry, etc.)" and still taking a distinct Verb ending in `-en`.
- A **three-point historical trajectory for `du`/`ni`** that this source's own evidence completes
  alongside 001's: this 1923 grammar states `ni` was "not yet generally accepted... but gradually
  coming into use" as a polite address form; 001 (2008) states `du` became "almost universally
  accepted" from the 1960s onward, leaving `ni` sounding "old-fashioned and stand-offish" by its own
  time of writing. Put together, the two sources bracket a full rise-and-fall arc for `ni` as a
  politeness form across the 20th century.
- The **1906 spelling reform**, described with concrete old/new spelling pairs — a level of detail
  001 never needed to cover since it only teaches the post-reform standard.
- **Genitive-avoidance patterns** (cases where Swedish structurally avoids the `-s` genitive that
  English would use) not covered in 001's genitive section.
- A second, independently-dated instance of the Chapter-1-style spoken/written divergence catalogue
  ("The Spoken Language," §14), confirming that several colloquialisms 001 documents as current in
  2008 (`dom`, `mej/dej/sej`, `ja` for `jag`, `ska`, short verb forms `ta'/ge/be/bli'`) were already
  established by 1923 — a ~85-year continuity data point for this project's historical/register
  tracking.

**Everything else — regular declension tables, regular adjective comparison, regular preposition/
conjunction usage, regular word order — is explicitly skipped as redundant with 001**, which covers
the modern system in full. This is a deliberate, auditable scope decision per the spec's
non-redundant-supplement convention, not an oversight.

**A note on this source's OCR quality.** This 1923 scan suffered severe, largely non-recoverable
OCR corruption specific to its embedded IPA phonetic transcriptions and several diacritic-bearing
Swedish letters (å/ä/ö render inconsistently as `to`/`se`/`0`/`9`/`o` and other garbage glyphs
depending on context; a schwa-marking superscript circle renders as a stray `c` or `f`). **Ordinary
orthographic Swedish example words are generally legible** (e.g. `bord`, `start`, `Karl`, `varna`)
and are the basis for every vocabulary entry below; genuinely illegible/unrecoverable spans are
simply not quoted rather than guessed. No vision-reading was attempted given the real (if degraded)
text layer and the non-redundant-supplement scope already limiting this file's ambitions.

## Grammar points

### 1. The four-gender system and its collapse into the modern two-gender system

This source teaches four grammatical genders (masculine, feminine, common, neuter), each
independently testable by which third-person pronoun (`han`/`hon`/`den`/`det`) could replace the
noun. Masculine and feminine are assigned almost entirely by real-world sex of a human or "higher"
animal referent (with named exceptions going each direction: `barn` "child" is neuter regardless of
sex; `sändebud`/`statsråd` take a neuter *article* but a masculine *pronoun*; `fruntimmer` "woman"
takes a neuter article but a feminine pronoun). Common and neuter between them cover everything
else, split in a way the source admits is "necessary to consult a dictionary" for — "no hard and
fast rules can be given" for whether an inanimate/animal noun is common or neuter. Crucially, **both
masculine and feminine nouns share the identical definite-article ending (`-n`) that common-gender
nouns also use** — meaning the surface morphology of masculine, feminine, and common nouns is
already identical in 1923, and only the *pronoun* that can replace them still distinguishes the
three. This is exactly the structural setup that collapses easily into a single "non-neuter/utrum"
category once the pronoun-replacement test for masculine/feminine humans and animals stops being
treated as a formal *grammatical gender* distinction and starts being treated as ordinary semantic
reference instead — which is precisely what 001's two-gender modern system reflects. In other
words, this source captures the Swedish gender system at the exact moment the fourth and third
categories (masculine, feminine) were already morphologically indistinguishable from the second
(common) and poised to be reanalyzed away, a genuine "snapshot of a change in progress" rather than
a stable four-way system the modern language simply discarded.

### 2. Genitive usage diverges from English in both directions

Section 45 states the Swedish `-s`-genitive is used *more freely* than English "of" for inanimate
possessors (`husets ägare` "the owner of the house," `ljusets hastighet` "the speed of light" — both
natural, unmarked Swedish where a genitive-avoiding English paraphrase would otherwise be needed).
Section 47 then gives the opposite-direction case: certain English "at X's"/"a relative of X's"
constructions that look genitive-shaped are *not* rendered with a genitive in Swedish at all, instead
using `hos` + possessive + noun (`hos sin faster` "at his aunt's") or a plain definite noun phrase
(`gick till bagaren` "went to the baker's"). Taken together, these two sections show that Swedish and
English genitive/possessive marking don't track each other along a single "more/less genitive-prone"
axis — the two languages simply carve up which possession-like relationships get genitive marking
differently, a more nuanced picture than either language's own native-speaker intuition alone would
suggest.

### 3. The ni/du politeness-register trajectory across three source generations

This grammar states (§128) that `ni` ("you," formal/plural) "is not yet generally accepted as a form
of address... but is gradually coming into use," while the older honorific alternative — addressing
someone by title + third-person verb (`Har professorn varit i London?`) — is presented as the more
genuinely polite option for someone whose title/name is known. By 001's time of writing (2008), that
trajectory has completed and then reversed: `du` has become the default, `ni` itself has become the
dated, "stand-offish" option, and the title-plus-third-person construction is "reserved only for very
formal occasions." §130 adds a third, older data point still: the inherited plural pronoun `I` ("ye"),
grammatically distinct from `ni` and carrying its own verb ending, was *already* confined to
scriptural/poetic register by 1923. The three-source arc (archaic `I` already dead > `ni` actively
spreading as the new polite form > `du` sweeping `ni` aside in turn) is a clean, well-evidenced
example of a politeness-pronoun system cycling through successive "new polite form ousts the old one,
then gets ousted itself" stages within a single language's documented history — directly useful as a
worked historical case study if the derived conlang's own slang-synthesis work ever wants to model a
believable politeness-register shift over in-universe time.

### 4. Verb number agreement: a leveling caught mid-collapse

Sections 216-217 show that in 1923, Swedish verbs still formally distinguished singular from plural
in the present and past tense — but only for Conjugation IV (strong verbs): `jag binder`/`vi binda`
(present), `jag kom`/`vi kommo` (past, with a dedicated `-o` plural ending). Conjugations I-III had
already lost the distinction in the past tense by this point (`jag talade`/`vi talade`, identical).
The Spoken Language section (§14.6-7) independently confirms the plural forms were already "not used
in colloquial speech" at all, even where the written standard still taught them, and that spoken
Swedish applied the *singular* verb form universally regardless of grammatical number — meaning the
leveling was complete in speech well before this book went to press, and the formal written standard
was already trailing behind actual usage by 1923. 001 (2008) presents the leveled, no-agreement
system as simply "modern Swedish" with no further comment, confirming the written standard caught up
with the spoken one at some point in the 20th century. This gives a precise three-stage model
(colloquial leveling first → written standard holds out longer, weak conjugations first → written
standard catches up completely) for how a grammatical agreement category can disappear from a
language over roughly a century — again, a reusable case study for modeling historical language
change in a derived-conlang design context.

### 5. The 1906 spelling reform as a dated orthographic-register marker

Section 6 documents the 1906 Swedish spelling reform concretely: the `hv-`/`-fv-`/`-f` spellings for
the `v`-sound were abolished in favor of plain `v` (`hvit`→`vit`, `sofva`→`sova`, `bref`→`brev`), and
`-dt` after a short vowel was replaced by doubled `-tt` (`godt`→`gott`). Because this book was
published in 1923 — close enough to the reform that the author still needs to warn readers about
"dictionaries with the old spelling" — it preserves a level of practical, dated detail about the
reform that no modern grammar (including 001) has any reason to include, since the old spellings are
now purely historical/antiquarian. Worth noting: three entirely independent sources in this project's
Swedish corpus (this one; 001's own phonology chapter; Andersson's academic chapter, 004) all
independently reach for a `bref`/`brev`-type example when illustrating this reform, suggesting it is
the canonical textbook-tradition illustration for this specific sound change across the whole
pedagogical lineage, not a coincidence of citation.

# Swedish — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: FSI Swedish Basic Course, Part 3 (Units 13-16 + appendices)

**Source:** Foreign Service Institute, U.S. Department of State, *Swedish* (Swedish Basic Course,
Student Text) — Units 13-16 ("På Väg till Landet"/"On the Way to the Country," "På Ön"/"On the
Island," "Helger och Traditioner"/"Holidays and Traditions," "Glimtar från Sverige av Idag"/"Glimpses
of Sweden Today"), printed pp. ~410-522+, plus the book's grammar index (pp. ~650-670). Third of three
lettered parts; see `015a` for full source citation, rendering/offset methodology, and the non-
redundant-supplement scope rationale.

**Sampling note:** this part concentrates on Unit 13's comprehensive strong/irregular-verb reference
table (a genuinely valuable resource with no full equivalent elsewhere in this dataset), and Units 15-
16's culturally distinctive reading content (Swedish holiday traditions; geography), which have no
counterpart in Croghan `008` or McClean `009` at this level of detail. Unit 14 (past participles,
passive voice, present participle — substantially overlapping grammar already covered from Croghan
`008` Units 10 and 15) was not sampled; flagged as a gap below.

## Unit 15 — Helger och Traditioner ("Holidays and Traditions") — the richest cultural content in this
source (pp. 471-477)

**A reading-passage unit (no dialogue format) walking through Sweden's annual holiday cycle via an
American family (the Browns) learning about it from a book — genuinely distinctive content with no
full counterpart in this project's other Swedish sources.**

**Midsommar (Midsummer):** describes the maypole (`majstången`) being decorated/dressed (`klä
majstången`) and raised (`stången restes under stort jubel`, "the pole was raised amid great
rejoicing") by a mixed group of neighbors and summer guests (`sommargäster`), followed by dancing
around the pole (`dansen omkring majstången`) with some in jeans/shorts and others in traditional folk
costume (`folkdräkter`) — confirming Midsummer folk-dress is not universal/obligatory even in a
"traditional" celebration, a nuance not present in Croghan's drier Unit-4 treatment of the same
holiday. The passage ends with the family watching the midnight sun from a jetty (`bryggan`).

**Julen (Christmas):** opens specifically with **Lucia Day** (`Lucia-dagen`, December 13): tradition
holds that the eldest daughter in a family wakes her parents early with a Lucia song
(`Lucia-sång`), coffee, and saffron buns (`lussekatter`), dressed in white with a wreath of lingonberry
sprigs (`lingonris`) bearing live candles (`levande ljus`) on her head, attended by her younger
siblings dressed as handmaids (`tärnor`) and star-boys (`stjärngossar`) — "every school and workplace
has its own Lucia." The passage also quotes a traditional Christmas-song line as a proverb-like
saying: **`julen varar ända till påska`** ("Christmas lasts all the way to Easter") — marking the full
traditional span of the Swedish Christmas season, from Lucia (Dec 13) through `trettondagen`
(Epiphany, Jan 6) to `tjugondag Knut` ("Knut's twentieth day," Jan 13, the traditional day Christmas
is "danced out," i.e., decorations taken down).

**Fastan (Lent):** the custom of eating `semlor` (cardamom buns filled with almond paste and whipped
cream, `mandelmassa och vispgrädde`) and decorating the house with colorful decorated birch twigs
(`färggranna påskris`) is described; the American daughter Anne remarks "how funny that the Swedes
celebrate Lent even though they're not Catholic" (`fastän de inte är katoliker`), to which George
replies dryly that it doesn't sound like they eat any less because of it — a genuine light, wry
cultural-commentary voice distinct from the earnest tone of this book's other reading passages.

## Unit 16 — Glimtar från Sverige av Idag ("Glimpses of Sweden Today") — Geography (p. 495)

A reading passage (partially overlapping Croghan `008` Unit 5's geography reading, but with different
specific statistics worth cross-referencing): Sweden is "the largest of the five Nordic countries and
the fourth in area in Europe"; it runs 160 Swedish miles (`svenska mil`, ≈1600 km) from **Treriksröset**
(the northernmost point, where Sweden/Norway/Finland meet) to **Smygehuk** (the southernmost point).
Despite sharing a latitude with Alaska and Greenland, the Gulf Stream (`Golfströmmen`) gives it a
milder climate — cross-confirms McClean `009`'s identical Gulf Stream point. As recently as 15,000 BC
all of Sweden was still ice-covered; the retreating ice left **96,000 lakes** (vs. Croghan's rounder
figure of "100,000 sjöar" — a minor cross-source numeric discrepancy worth flagging rather than
silently reconciling) and extensive archipelagos; the Stockholm archipelago alone has roughly **26,000
islands**. Sweden's population is given as 8.3 million, with uneven population density (`folktätheten
är dock mycket ojämn`).

## Coverage gaps

**Unit 14** ("På Ön"/"On the Island" — past participles, passive voice, present participle) was not
individually sampled; its grammar points substantially overlap Croghan `008` Unit 10 (present
perfect/pluperfect supine) and Unit 15 (the -s passive). **The dedicated commercial-correspondence
material and the back-of-book grammar reference appendix** (pp. ~523-650, including a section on the
conditional mood at p.522 per the grammar index) were not individually sampled — flagged as a genuine
gap; given this course's FSI/diplomatic-training origin, a commercial/official-correspondence chapter
here is plausible but was not confirmed to exist as a discrete chapter (unlike McClean's explicitly
preface-announced Chapter XXIV). The book's own back-matter grammar index (pp. ~664-670) was consulted
only to confirm content boundaries, not transcribed, consistent with this project's treatment of pure
alphabetical finding aids.

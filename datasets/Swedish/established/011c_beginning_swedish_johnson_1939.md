**PRE-REFORM SWEDISH — the subjunctive, this source's single highest-value archaic finding.**
Lesson XXXII (§107–108) teaches a **fully productive e-subjunctive mood** that has essentially
vanished from modern spoken/written Swedish outside of a handful of fossilized set phrases (*Gud
bevare dig!*, "God protect you!" survives; *Leve konungen!*, "Long live the king!" survives
ceremonially) — the book presents it as a live, productive present-tense verb form (substitute *-e*
for the infinitive's final *-a*: *kalla→kalle, sända→sände, resa→rese, finna→finne*) AND a parallel
**past e-subjunctive** built off the literary-plural past stem of strong verbs (*fingo→finge,
höllo→hölle, gingo→ginge, kommo→komme*) — meaning the past subjunctive is morphologically downstream
of the very literary-plural system already flagged as archaic in Part B. This is a second, structurally
deeper layer of pre-reform grammar than the literary plural alone, and modern Swedish reference
grammars (e.g. this project's *Colloquial Swedish*, *Essential Grammar of Swedish* extractions) do not
teach this mood as live material at all — confirming this 1939 source's unique value for this
project's purposes. Flagged per-occurrence below.

## Grammar points

### Lesson XXIII — Numerals (ordinals, fractions, time, dates)
**§82. Ordinals**: *första (1st) ... tionde (10th) ... tjugonde (20th) ... hundrade (100th) ...
tusende (1000th)*. Only *första, andra*, and compound ordinals ending in *-första/-andra* are
declinable (substituting final *-e* for a masculine referent: *den förste gossen*). An ordinal can
itself take the possessive *-s* (*den förstes bok*). The noun following an ordinal takes the
**definite** form, often WITHOUT the prepositive article (*första läxan*, "the first lesson"). Dates
use Arabic numerals alone or with an ordinal suffix (*den 6 december* or *den 6:e december*),
superseding now-obsolete longer suffix forms (*1:ste(a), 2:dre(a), 3:dje, 4:de, 5:te*, etc.) — a
further, independently-noted instance of the book flagging its own recently-retired forms, distinct
from the living literary-plural system. A ruler's Roman numeral is read as an ordinal (*Gustaf V* =
"Gustaf den femte"). **§83. Other ordinal-derived expressions**: fractions by adding *del* to the
ordinal (*en tredjedel* = one third; dropping the ordinal's own *-de* except in *fjärdedel,
sjundedel*); **distributive** *var/vart* + ordinal (*var femte dag* = every fifth day) parallels
*varannan/vartannat* ("every other") built on *annan*; "firstly, secondly," etc. = *för det
första/andra*. **§84. Expressing time**: Swedes use the 24-hour clock for schedules but the
12-hour system with *f.m./e.m.* at home; *halv två* = 1:30 (i.e., "half [of the way] to two," the
opposite reference-point from English "half past one"); *en kvart i/före två* = 1:45; minute-counts
state minutes "over" or "before/until" the hour. **§85. Units of time**: a systematic set of
prepositional time-phrases for past/present/future (*i går, i går kväll, i förrgår kväll, i måndags, i
vintras, i morse* for the past; *i dag, i år, i kväll* for the present; *i morgon, i morgon kväll* for
the future) — several of these (*i måndags, i morse, i vintras*) are the same archaic genitive-style
adverbial-possessive construction already flagged as "survival from Old Swedish" in §64(d) (Part B),
now shown systematically extended across every weekday and season. **§86. Dates**: *den 14 december*
(no "of"); year read as a full cardinal (*1939* = "nittonhundratrettinio"); *Han är född den 7 januari
1920* and *Han föddes den 7 januari 1920* both idiomatic for "he was born...".

### Lesson XXV — The indefinite adjectives and pronouns
**§87. Indefinite adjectives**: *var* (non-neuter sg.) / *vart* (neuter sg.) "every, each" vs.
**formal/written-only** *varje* (invariable) — an explicit register split within the indefinite-adjective
system itself; *var sin smörgås* = "a sandwich apiece"; *var för sig* = "each one by himself." **§88.
Indefinite pronouns**: ***man*** ("one/you/we/they/people," subject-only, invariable, singular verb,
obj. *en*, poss. *ens*, reflexive *sig/sin*) — the standard colloquial substitute for a passive-voice
construction, formalized later in §102; *ingenting/någonting/allting* (nothing/something/everything);
*varenda en*/*var och en* ("every single one," formal *envar*, poss. *envars*). **§89. Indefinite
adjectives-or-pronouns** — a large closed class, each triple showing gender/number agreement: *all/
allt/alla* (poss. *allas*); *annan/annat/andra* (also = "else"); *den ena/det ena* vs. *den andra/det
andra* ("the one... the other," masc. *ene/andre*); *flera* (more, several) vs. *flesta* (most,
always definite: "de flesta"); *få* (few) vs. *några få* (a few); *ingen/intet(coll. inget)/inga* (no
one/nothing/no, substitutable by *inte någon/något/några*); *mången/månget/många* ("many a," flagged
by the book itself as rarely used — a literary-leaning form within this class); *somlig* (some); *den
enda/det enda* (the only one). *hel* ("all, the whole") contrasts with the partitive *lite* ("a
little"); negative pronouns/adjectives follow the finite verb in independent clauses but precede it in
dependent clauses (anticipating §92); the indefinite pronouns all take *-s* for the possessive; in
casual speech *någon/något/annan* often shorten to *nån/nåt/ann*.

### Lesson XXVI — Word order
**§90. Normal word order**: subject–verb–object/modifier/predicate; subject normally precedes the
finite verb in independent declarative clauses. **§91. Inverted order** (verb precedes subject) is
triggered by: (a) fronting a predicate adjective/adverb/phrase/direct object for emphasis; (b) after a
direct quotation; (c) when a dependent clause opens the sentence; (d) in the second half of a compound
sentence joined by a conjunctive adverb (*dock, annars*); (e) after *antingen, eller*, and after *dess*
in a *ju...dess* construction; (f) in any yes/no question. **§92. Word order in dependent clauses**:
negation adverbs (*inte, icke, ej, aldrig*), whole-clause modal particles (*nog, väl, förstås, ju,
visst*), and indefinite-time adverbs (*ofta, snart, alltid, sällan*) go **immediately after the
subject** in a dependent clause — the position they'd occupy AFTER the finite verb (or after an
unstressed pronominal object) in an independent clause. If *om* ("if") is omitted from a conditional
dependent clause, the clause itself takes **inverted** order as the only remaining signal that it's
conditional (*Kommer han, får vi stanna hemma* = "If he comes, we may stay home" — structurally
identical to the English "Had I been there..." construction). **§93. Object order**: indirect object
AFTER the direct object if introduced by a preposition (*Jag skall göra det åt honom*); indirect
object BEFORE the direct object with no preposition (*Jag gav honom en penna*).

### Lesson XXVII — The modals
**§94–96. The full modal system**, each given principal parts (present / literary-plural present /
past / literary-plural past where applicable): ***böra*** (ought to: *bör/borde*, spoken Swedish
replaces the old supine-based perfect with *har bort/hade bort/skulle ha* — §95d, a further
self-flagged retired form); ***få*** (may/must/get to: *får/fick*, lit.pl. past *fingo*; context alone
disambiguates permission-granting from absolute-prohibition-with-negation from simple
perception-at-a-moment with *höra/se/veta*); ***kunna*** (can: *kan/kunde*, covers ability, possibility,
permission, AND polite request); ***låta*** (let/cause: *låter/lät*, lit.pl. past *läto*); ***må/
måtte*** (wishes/concessions — *måtte* implies a more intense or anxious wish than *må*; this whole
modal doubles as the **compound subjunctive** auxiliary in §108); ***måste*** (must — one invariant
form across present/past/future, supine *måst*); ***skola/skall*** (marks the "**future of
determination**" — a deliberate decision/promise/command — as opposed to *kommer att* + infinitive,
which signals plain futurity without any such commitment; *skall* also idiomatically = "is said to
be"); ***vilja*** (want to — explicitly NEVER used to build the future tense, despite its surface
resemblance to English "will"; followed by *gärna* for willingness, *hellre/helst* for preference);
and two rarer, more literary/marginal modals, ***tör/torde*** and ***lär***, both expressing likelihood
or hearsay ("is likely," "is said to"), given principal parts but comparatively thin usage-notes —
the book's own treatment implicitly signals these as the two most bookish members of the whole modal
set. Every modal is followed by a bare infinitive (no *att*).

### Lessons XXIX — Present and past participles; differences in usage
**§97. The present participle**: *-ande* added to an *-a*-stem infinitive, *-ende* to any other
(*kalla*→*kallande*; *gå*→*gående*; *bo*→*boende*); functions as adjective, adverb, adverbial
appositive (*Anna kom gående*, "Anna came walking"), or nominalized noun (taking *-s* for possession:
*den resandes hatt*); comparable via *mer(a)/mest*; based on the LONGER of two infinitive variants
where both exist (*bedja*→*bedjande*, not *be*→*bende*). **§98. Differences in usage from English** —
genuinely important typological points: (a) **no progressive participle use at all** (reconfirms §49);
(b) Swedish pairs **two finite verbs** where English uses finite-verb-plus-present-participle (*Tor
satt vid bordet och läste* = "Tor sat at the table reading"); (c) Swedish uses a **relative or
time/cause clause** where English uses a participial phrase (*Flickan som går i parken är Alma* for
"The girl walking in the park..."); (d) Swedish uses an **objective + infinitive** where English uses
a present participle (*Jag hörde honom komma* = "I heard him coming"); (e) Swedish uses a bare
**infinitive after a preposition** (with *att* retained) where English uses the gerund (*Jag tycker om
att meta* = "I like fishing"). **§99. The past participle**: declined like an adjective, three forms
per conjugation (indef. non-neuter sg. / indef.+def. neuter sg.+pl. / def. non-neuter sg.+pl., e.g.
*älskad/älskat/älskade*); for strong/irregular verbs, consult the Appendix's paradigm tables; also
comparable via *mer(a)/mest*.

### Lesson XXX — The passive voice
**§100. The simple (*-s*) passive**: formed by substituting *-s* for the active present's *-r*, or
adding *-s* to the past/infinitive/supine in other tenses (*kallas, kallades*; *hörs, hördes*); the
**colloquial plural is identical to the singular** (the literary-plural *-s*-passive forms exist too,
e.g. *vi höras*, but are register-marked exactly like every other literary plural in this book); the
agent is introduced by *av* (*Pojken kallades av sin mor*). **§101. The compound passive**: *bli(va)*
+ past participle for verbs of **transition** (change of state: *sälja, stänga, finna*); *vara* + past
participle for verbs of **duration** (continued action: describes a resulting STATE, not a passive
EVENT, when paired with a transition-verb's participle — *Dörren är stängd* = "The door is shut" [a
state], NOT a passive construction at all, just an adjectival use of the participle). **§102. The
substitute for the passive**: in speech and often in writing, Swedish prefers the **indefinite pronoun
*man* + active voice** over any passive construction when the agent is unspecified (*Man tror honom
inte* preferred over *Han tros inte*), reconnecting directly to *man*'s treatment in §88.

### Lesson XXXI — The reciprocal use of the passive; deponent verbs; compound verbs
**§103. Reciprocal *-s*-passive**: the *-s* form can denote mutual/reciprocal action rather than true
passive voice (*Vi träffas i morgon* = "We'll see each other tomorrow"; *De möttes i parken* = "They
met [each other] in the park"). **§104. Deponent verbs** — verbs that are **passive in form but
active in meaning**, always ending in *-s* even in the imperative (*Hoppas!* = "Hope!"): *fattas*
(lack), *brås på* (resemble), *hoppas* (hope), *lyckas* (succeed), *minnas* (remember, pres. *minns*),
*tyckas* (seem), *synas* (appear/seem/show). **§105. Compound verbs**, three classes: (a)
**inseparable** verbs (prefix never detaches: *begynna, bidraga, avleda, undgå, missförstå,
anvanda*) — primary stress on the prefix UNLESS the prefix is *be-, ent-,* or *för-* (those three are
unstressed, giving the verb the acute accent instead); (b) **separable verbs with identical meaning
either way** (*följa med*/*medfölja*; *tala om*/*omtala*) — separation preferred in speech, but the
**participles are always inseparable**; the separable element carries the primary stress; (c)
**separable verbs where separation changes the meaning** — literal when separated, figurative when
fused (*bryta av* "break off" vs. *avbryta* "interrupt"; *gå av* "break" vs. *avgå* "leave/depart").
**§106.** All compound verbs conjugate exactly like their base simple verb in every tense, including
the passive; stress alone (*han ta'lar om historien* "he is talking ABOUT the story" [simple verb +
preposition] vs. *han talar om' historien* "he is TELLING the story" [true compound verb]) can be the
only signal distinguishing a genuine compound from a simple-verb-plus-preposition sequence that merely
looks identical on the page.

### Lesson XXXII — The subjunctive mood
**§107. The *e*-subjunctive — the book's single most archaic living grammar point.** **Present
e-subjunctive**: substitute *-e* for the infinitive's final *-a* (*kalla*→*kalle*; *sända*→*sände*;
*resa*→*rese*; *finna*→*finne*); if the infinitive doesn't end in *-a*, there is NO present
e-subjunctive form at all, and the compound subjunctive (§108) must be used instead. **Past
e-subjunctive**: for strong verbs, substitute *-e* for the final *-o* of the **literary-plural past
indicative** (*fingo→finge; höllo→hölle; gingo→ginge; kommo→komme*) — i.e. this mood's past tense is
morphologically BUILT ON TOP OF the already-archaic literary plural, making it a second, deeper layer
of pre-reform grammar; for weak verbs (conjugations I–III), the past e-subjunctive is simply identical
to the ordinary past indicative. Usage: the present form survives today mainly in a closed set of
prayers and set phrases (*Gud give dig tron!* "May God give you faith"; *Helgat varde ditt namn*
"Hallowed be Thy name"; *Gud bevare dig!* "God protect you!") — the book notes that **spoken Swedish
already substitutes *må/måtte* + infinitive** for this register even in 1939; the past form expresses
unrealizable/counterfactual wishes and conditions of impossibility/improbability, and clauses with
*som om* ("as if": *Han bär sig åt, som om han vore galen*, "He acts as if he were crazy") — but here
too, the book notes **spoken Swedish usually substitutes the plain past indicative or the compound
subjunctive** instead. **§108. The compound subjunctive**: *må/måtte* + infinitive (present); *skulle*
+ infinitive (past/conditional); *må/måtte* + *ha* + supine (present perfect); *skulle* + *ha* + supine
(past perfect) — this periphrastic system is what living colloquial Swedish actually uses in place of
the *e*-subjunctive for everything except the handful of fossilized set phrases above. Omitting
conditional *om* triggers inversion, exactly as in §92 (*Hade jag hört honom, skulle jag ha svarat* =
"Had I heard him, I would have answered").

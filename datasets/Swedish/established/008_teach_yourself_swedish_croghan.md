## Grammar points

### 1. `en`- and `ett`-nouns (common/neuter gender)
Swedish nouns take one of two genders marked by the indefinite article: `en` (common gender, ~75% of
nouns, mostly living things) or `ett` (neuter, ~25%, mostly "things") — no reliable rule predicts
which, so gender must be learned per noun (p.18).

### 2. Personal pronouns (subject forms)
Full subject-pronoun paradigm: `jag` (I), `du`/`Du` (you, sg. informal/formal-written), `han` (he),
`hon` (she), `den` (it, replacing an `en`-word), `det` (it, replacing an `ett`-word), `vi` (we),
`ni`/`Ni` (you, pl., or sg. deferential), `de` (they, pronounced "dåmm," colloquially spelled "dom")
(p.18-19).

### 3. du/Du and ni/Ni — the T/V distinction
`Du` is singular-only; a capital may be used in writing to show respect. `Ni` is plural, but
historically also the formal singular address form — this usage is receding as `du` (the "du-reform")
gains ground across all registers; when unsure which to use, the book advises matching whatever form
the Swedish interlocutor uses first (p.19). Note: `jag` and `ja` ("yes") are homophones (the `g` in
`jag` is mute) but disambiguated by context.

### 4. Yes/no confirmation
`Ja, det är det` ("Yes, it is," lit. "yes, that it is") / `Nej, det är det inte` ("No, it isn't," lit.
"no, that it is not") — the verb-plus-pronoun echo-answer pattern, distinct from English's bare
yes/no (p.19).

### 5. The infinitive
Swedish infinitives normally end in unstressed `-a` added to the stem (`vara` "be," `stanna` "stay,"
`komma` "come"); a one-syllable verb ending in a stressed vowel takes no `-a` (`bo` "live," `gå` "go")
(p.19).

### 6. The present tense
Nearly all Swedish verbs mark present tense with a single ending (`-r`, usually `-ar` or `-er`) used
identically across all persons/numbers — no person/number agreement at all, unlike English's "I am /
you are / he is." `vara` ("to be") is irregular: `är` for every person. Verbs whose infinitive ends in
a vowel other than `-a` add only `-r` (`bor`, `går`). The present tense covers (a) an action happening
now, (b) a habitual action, and (c) a scheduled/future action when a time expression is present —
distinct from English's three-way present-simple/continuous/going-to split (p.20-21).

### 7. No indefinite article before occupation/religion/nationality/political-affiliation nouns
Unlike English "a/an," Swedish uses no article in `Han är ingenjör` ("He is an engineer"), `Han är
protestant`, `John är engelsman`, `Olof Palme var socialdemokrat` — this class of predicate nouns
(occupation, religion, nationality, political affiliation) is also spelled lower-case even when
derived from a proper noun, e.g. `engelsman` not `Engelsman` (p.21-22).

### 8. Word order in statements and questions
Declarative order is Subject-Verb (`John är här`). A yes/no question is formed purely by inverting to
Verb-Subject (`Är John här?`) — no auxiliary "do"-support as in English. A wh-question keeps the same
V-S order after the question word (`Vad heter du?`). A "yes" answer to a negatively-phrased question
uses `jo` rather than `ja`, distinguishing "yes [contradicting your negative]" from a plain "yes"
(p.21).

### 9. Possession: the genitive -s
Swedish marks possession with a bare `-s` suffix and no apostrophe (`Min frus namn`, `Åkes lillebror`,
`Anders firma`, `stadens namn`); crucially, the noun that follows a genitive never takes the
definite-article ending even though English "my wife's name" would gloss the possessed noun as
definite (p.22).

## Grammar points (Unit 2, continued)

### 1. How to say "the"
The definite article is a suffix, not a separate word: `-en` on a consonant-final `en`-word
(`våning` → `våningen`), `-et` on a consonant-final `ett`-word (`hus` → `huset`); a vowel-final noun
takes only `-n`/`-t` (`flicka` → `flickan`; `äpple` → `äpplet`). As already noted in Unit 1, a noun
following a genitive never takes this suffix (p.34).

### 2. The additional definite article
When an adjective intervenes between a definite-marked expectation and the noun, Swedish doubles the
marking: a separate word (`den` before `en`-words, `det` before `ett`-words, `de` before plurals)
*plus* the suffix still on the noun — `den fina våningen`, `det fina huset`, `de fina husen` (p.35).

### 3. `vilken` in exclamations
`Vilken vacker utsikt!` ("What a beautiful view!") uses the otherwise-interrogative `vilken` as an
exclamative; `en sådan vacker utsikt` ("such a beautiful view") is offered as an alternative
construction (p.35).

### 4. Inverted directional compounds
A family of `-ifrån` compounds inverts the English word order: `härifrån` ("from here," lit.
"here-from"), `därifrån`, `varifrån`, `uppifrån`, `nerifrån`, `framifrån`, `bakifrån` (p.35).

### 5. Modal verbs: kan, ska, vill, måste, får, låter
Correspond to English can/shall/will/must/may/let; crucially, a modal in a two-verb clause takes a
*bare* infinitive with no `att` ("to"), unlike English's "to"-infinitive after some of these same verbs
(p.35-36).

### 6. `ha`/`har`, `ta`/`tar`
`ha` ("have") and `ta` ("take") are both infinitives whose present tense is simply `-r` added:
`har`/`tar`, identical across all persons (p.36).

### 7. Measure expressions take no "of"
`en kopp kaffe` ("a cup of coffee"), `en flaska vin` ("a bottle of wine"), `ett glas vatten` ("a glass
of water") — no equivalent to English "of" appears (p.36).

### 8. Interrogative pronouns: vem, vad, vilken
`vem` ("who/whom," person-only, singular-only — plural needs `vilka`), `vad` ("what," cannot precede a
noun, unlike English — "what kind of" must instead be `vad för en/ett/∅` + noun), `vilken/vilket/vilka`
("which/who/what," agreeing with `en`-word/`ett`-word/plural). Only `vilken`-family forms take a
genitive `-s` (`vems`, `vilkens`, `vilkets`, `vilkas`); `vad` cannot (p.36-37).

### 9. Numerals 0-12
`noll, ett, två, tre, fyra, fem, sex, sju, åtta, nio, tio, elva, tolv` — note `nio`/`tio` are
colloquially pronounced "nie"/"tie" in everyday speech, and Swedes use the neuter `ett` (not `en`) when
counting in the abstract (p.37).

## Grammar points (Unit 3)

### 1. Plural forms of Swedish nouns (the five declensions)
Unlike English's near-universal `-s`, Swedish plurals fall into five declension classes, predictable
from a noun's dictionary form. First-declension `en`-words typically end in unstressed `-a` (dropped
before plural `-or`: `flicka`→`flickor`); a handful don't end in `-a` and simply add `-or` to the bare
form (`ros`→`rosor`). From Unit 3 onward the book lists nouns dictionary-style with declension
markers, e.g. `flick/a (-an, -or)`, encoding definite-singular and indefinite-plural endings together
(p.46-47).

### 2. Days of the week (veckodagarna)
`söndag, måndag, tisdag, onsdag, torsdag, fredag, lördag` — not capitalized, unlike English. Each has
a colloquially clipped pronunciation distinct from its spelling (p.47).

### 3. The preposition `på`
A single versatile preposition `på` covers both "on [a day]" and "in [a part of the day]" contexts
where English would use two different prepositions (p.47).

### 4. Adjective agreement
Adjectives agree with the noun's gender/number: base form for `en`-word singular, `+t` for `ett`-word
singular (`stor`→`stort`), `+a` for any plural (both genders) — and the same `+a` rule applies
predicatively, after a form of `vara` (p.47-48).

### 5. `gå` vs. `åka`
`gå` covers English "go/travel" only when a vehicle itself is the subject (`Tåget går`, "the train
goes") or, for a person, only in the literal sense "to walk" or "to leave" — never "to go by vehicle."
`åka` is the verb a person uses for traveling by any means of transport (p.48).

### 6. Numerals 13-100 and arithmetic
`tretton` through `hundra`; `tjugoett` ("21") is often colloquially clipped to "tjuett"/"tjuen" in
speech; `en`/`ett` before a compound numeral agree with the following noun's gender
(`tjugoen veckor` vs. `tjugoett år`); Swedish uses a decimal comma, not a decimal point. Basic
arithmetic vocabulary: `plus`, `minus`, `gånger` ("times"), `delat med` ("divided by") (p.48-49).

## Grammar points (Unit 4)

### 1. Plural declension classes, continued
Several further sub-patterns of the `-ar`-plural (second) declension are catalogued by phonological
shape: unstressed `-e`-final `en`-words drop the `-e` (`pojke`→`pojkar`); most monosyllabic `en`-words;
most native-Swedish trees and animals; `-ing`-final nouns; `-el/-er/-en`-final `en`-words (dropping the
final unstressed syllable: `cykel`→`cyklar`); `-dom`-final nouns. A short set of common nouns is fully
irregular (`mor`→`mödrar`, `dotter`→`döttrar`, `morgon`→`morgnar`, `afton`→`aftnar`). `finger` is
flagged as the sole `ett`-word belonging to this otherwise `en`-word-only declension (p.60-61).

### 2. `i landet` vs. `på landet`
`i landet` = "in the country" (i.e., within a named nation); `på landet` = "in the countryside" (rural
vs. urban) — a preposition-driven sense distinction with no overt lexical marker (p.61).

### 3. Kille, tjej — youth slang synonyms
See the genuine slang finding above — the only chapter so far to explicitly label vocabulary as slang
and comment on each synonym's relative current popularity (p.61).

### 4. Object pronouns
Full paradigm: `mig, dig, honom, henne, den/det, oss, er/Er, dem` — these forms are obligatory after a
preposition, not just as a direct object (p.61).

### 5. The four seasons
`vår, sommar, höst, vinter`; Swedish uses `på` where English uses "in" (`på hösten`, `på vintern`)
(p.62).

### 6. Time-reference adverbs
A systematic list built around a "days-from-today" axis, including the past/future-only distinction
for `bitti` noted above (p.62).

### 7. `lång` vs. `länge`
Both translate English "long," but are not interchangeable: `lång` is an adjective (agreement-marked,
can mean "tall," works for both time and distance); `länge` is an invariant adverb, time-only, and can
never precede a noun (p.62).

### 8. `väcka`/`vakna`
`vakna` = "wake up" when the subject wakes by their own accord; `väcka` = "wake [someone] up," used
when an external agent/means causes the waking (p.63).

### 9. `spela`/`leka`
See vocabulary table — `spela` for rule-governed play (sport, games with rules) and music/theatre;
`leka` for unstructured spontaneous play (p.63).

### 10. `sjö`/`hav`
See vocabulary table — a flagged false-friend pair (p.63).

## Grammar points (Unit 5)

### 1. Word order in questions (with a question word)
Formalizes the pattern already seen: (1) question word, (2) verb 1, (3) subject, (4) verb 2 [if any],
(5) everything else — `Hur vill ni ha pengarna?` (p.73).

### 2. "Do" in questions
Confirms/extends the Unit-1 point: Swedish has no do-support verb at all, with or without a question
word — the main verb alone inverts (p.73).

### 6. Relative pronouns: som, vars, vilken, vad
`som` ("who/that/which") is invariant, usable for singular or plural, and can never be preceded by a
preposition — a preposition attached to a relative clause must go at the very end of the clause
instead. `vars` ("whose") is the genitive of `som`, written-register only. `vilken/vilket/vilka` (also
"who/that/which") is mainly literary/written, agrees with its antecedent's gender/number, and *can*
follow a preposition; only `vilket` can refer to an entire preceding clause rather than one word.
`vad` ("that/what") appears as a relative especially in `allt vad` ("all that"), and must be followed
by `som` when it is itself the subject of its clause. As in English, the relative pronoun can often be
dropped if English could drop "who/that/which" in the same spot (p.76).

### 7. Numerals 101 to one trillion, and loanword plurals
`hundra`, `tusen`, `miljon`, `miljard`, `biljon` (see false-friend note above); a four-digit number
read as a single word omits `och` between the hundreds and tens, unlike English's "nineteen
hundred-AND-eighteen" (`nittonhundraarton`); no preposition corresponds to English "in" before a year
— Swedish uses `år` or nothing. Loanwords ending in `-um` (`museum`, `laboratorium`) pluralize by
dropping `-um` and adding `-er` (p.76-77).

## Grammar points (Unit 6)

### 1-2. Fractions / half (halv)
`halv` agrees with its noun like any adjective (`en halv månad`, `ett halvt år`, `halva månaden`); a
noun following `en halv` stays singular even when the whole phrase denotes more than one unit (`två
och en halv månad`, "two and a half months" — not pluralized `månader`). In compounds the bare `halv-`
is used (`halvtimme`, `halvår`) (p.88).

### 3. Comparison of adjectives
Regular comparison is suffixal (`billig/billigare/billigast`); `-er/-el/-en`-final adjectives drop the
`e` before adding `-are`/`-ast` (`vacker→vackrare→vackrast`). `-isk`-final adjectives, participles, and
very long adjectives instead use periphrastic `mer`/`mest`. A set of common adjectives is irregular
(`bra/god→bättre→bäst`, `dålig→sämre/värre→sämst/värst` — with `sämre` for a *diminished* good quality
vs. `värre` for an *intensified* bad one — `stor→större→störst`, `liten→mindre→minst`,
`gammal→äldre→äldst`, etc.). `mycket`/`många`→`mer/a`/`fler/a` depends on whether the noun is
countable. The comparative form itself never takes further endings; the superlative does take
definite-form endings (`-e` after `-ast`, `-a` after `-st`) (p.88-90).

## Grammar points (Unit 7)

### 1. Recap: the five noun-plural declension groups, with endings table
Systematizes Units 3-6's scattered declension notes into one table: (1) `-or`, (2) `-ar`, (3) `-er`,
(4) `-r` only (vowel-final `en`-words not in group 1), (5) `-n` (vowel-final `ett`-words) or no ending
at all (consonant-final `ett`-words, and a handful of `en`-word exceptions like `en arbetare`,
`en ordförande`, `en politiker`, which are unchanged in the plural despite being `en`-words) (p.104).

### 2. The definite plural article
`-na` for all `en`-words; `ett`-words that take `-n` in the indefinite plural just add `-a` for the
definite plural (converging with the `en`-word pattern); `ett`-words with no indefinite-plural ending
take `-en` in the definite plural instead. A few nouns are wholly irregular (`man→män→männen`,
`öga→ögon→ögonen`, `öra→öron→öronen`) (p.104-105).

### 3. `tycka om` — a stress-shift idiom
Where the phrasal stress falls changes the meaning: stressed `tycka` = "what's your opinion of...?";
stressed `om` = "to like." A rare case where suprasegmental stress alone disambiguates meaning within
an otherwise-identical phrase (p.105).

### 4. `ligga`
Used (literally "to lie") for the location of fixed/large entities — houses, streets, towns, lakes,
countries — where English uses "is/is situated/lies/stands" (p.105).

### 5. The preposition `på`
Generally translates "on" as a preposition of place (`på bordet`), but in some fixed expressions
corresponds to "in" instead (`på Storgatan`, `på landet`, `på himlen`). `himmel` is polysemous,
covering both "sky" and "heaven" (p.105-106).

## Grammar points (Unit 8)

### 1. Verbs: past tense of the first conjugation
Of Swedish's four conjugations, the first three are "weak" (regular, past tense by suffix); the fourth
is "strong" (irregular, past tense by stem-vowel change, e.g. `var` "was"). The first conjugation (the
largest class) adds `-ade` to the stem for the past tense (`tala→talade`); this covers every
infinitive ending in `-era` or `-na` as well (`reservera→reserverade`, `vakna→vaknade`) (p.120).

### 2. "Komma och bada," "stå och äta" — paratactic motion+activity
Where English abbreviates with infinitive+`-ing` ("come jogging," "stand eating"), Swedish instead
coordinates two finite verbs with `och` in the same tense: `Vill du komma och jogga?`, `De står och
äter varm korv` (p.120).

### 3. `god`/`gott`, `godare`, `godast` vs. `bra`
For describing how food tastes, Swedes default to the neuter form `gott` regardless of the referent
noun's gender when speaking generally (`Glass är gott`), but agree normally when the noun is definite
(`Glassen är god`). `god` compares with suffixes (`godare`, `godast`) rather than the irregular
`bättre`/`bäst` used elsewhere. `god` itself is narrower than English "good" — mainly food, or moral
righteousness (`en god bok` reads as "a book of sermons," not "a good read"); general-purpose "good"
instead uses the endingless `bra` (p.121).

### 4. Possessive adjectives/pronouns
Swedish possessives are identical whether used attributively (before a noun) or predicatively/
pronominally (standing alone) — `Det är min bil` / `Bilen är min` use the same form `min` in both
slots, unlike English "my" vs. "mine" (p.121).

## Grammar points (Unit 9)

### 1. Verbs: past tense of the second conjugation
Second-conjugation verbs have present tense `-er` and past tense `-de` (stem ends in a voiced
consonant) or `-te` (stem ends in a voiceless consonant: f, k, p, s, t) — sub-split into IIa (`-de`:
`stänga→stängde`) and IIb (`-te`: `köpa→köpte`). Stems ending in `-r` add no further present-tense
ending; stems ending in `-nd` drop the `d` before a `d`/`t`-initial ending; a double `m`/`n` in the
stem drops to single before a `d`/`t`-initial ending (`glömma→glömde`, not †glömmde) (p.134-135).

### 2. The past tense of the third conjugation
Third-conjugation verbs (infinitive ends in a vowel other than `-a`; present tense is just `+r`) take

## Grammar points (Unit 10)

### 1. Verbs: present perfect and pluperfect tense (the supine)
Where English's past participle does triple duty (perfect/pluperfect auxiliary, passive, adjective),
Swedish uses a dedicated, invariant verb form — **the supine** — only after `har` (present perfect) or
`hade` (pluperfect); the supine always ends in `-t` and never changes. Supine formation by
conjugation: Conj. I adds `-at` to the stem; both groups of Conj. II add just `-t`; Conj. III adds
`-tt`; the strong (4th-conjugation) verbs add `-it` to the stem, often with a vowel change (detailed
further in Unit 11). A further rule: if two clauses describe simultaneous or sequential events, both
should use the same tense (p.149).

## Grammar points (Unit 11)

### 1. Verbs: the fourth conjugation (strong/irregular verbs)
Strong verbs mark past tense by an internal stem-vowel change rather than a suffix (present tense
still takes `-er`, like the 2nd conjugation). Three vowels matter: the infinitive/present/imperative
stem vowel, a distinct past-tense stem vowel, and a third vowel shared by the supine (`-it` ending) and
the past participle (`-en` ending), which otherwise differ only in that final suffix. The book
catalogs the main vowel-change patterns as mnemonic triads, e.g. `i-a-u` (`finna/fann/funnit`,
`sitta/satt/suttit`, `dricka/drack/druckit`, `vinna/vann/vunnit`), long-`i`-stem `i-e-i`
(`skriva/skrev/skrivit`, `skina/sken/skinit`, `stiga/steg/stigit`), and `u/y/ö-ö-u`
(`bjuda/bjöd/bjudit`, `njuta/njöt/njutit`, `flyga/flög/flugit`) (p.163).

## Grammar points (Unit 12)

### 1. Verbs: the future tense (three strategies)
(a) `ska` + bare infinitive (no `att`) — the least common strategy, used for a decided intention:
`Jag ska lära mig svenska`. A `tänka` + bare infinitive variant expresses "plan/intend to."
(b) `kommer att` + infinitive — an assumption/prediction about the future, especially common with `det`
as subject: `Det kommer att regna i morgon`.
(c) The plain present tense — probably the most common strategy when a time expression or context
already makes the future reference clear: `De åker till Lappland i juli` (p.178).

### 2. The names of the months
`januari` through `december`, not capitalized (p.179).

### 3. `då`/`sedan` ("then") — disambiguating an English ambiguity
English "then" conflates two senses that Swedish keeps separate: `då` = "at that time / in that case";
`sedan` = "afterwards." Both words are independently ambiguous themselves: `då` can also be a
(relatively formal/written) conjunction meaning "when" or "as"; `sedan` can be a conjunction or
preposition meaning "after/since" as well as the adverb "then" (p.179).

## Grammar points (Unit 14)

### 1. Adverbs
Adverbs modify an adjective, verb, another adverb, or a whole clause; the most common are the
negations `inte, aldrig, icke, ej`. Many adverbs coincide with an adjective's neuter form and compare
the same way (`gott→bättre→bäst`); others are built with fixed suffixes `-en` or `-vis` and cannot be
compared at all (semantically incompatible with degree). Crucially, most place adverbs have **two
distinct forms**: one for rest/position at a place (answering `var?`, "where?") and a different one for
movement toward/from a place (answering `vart?`, "where to?") — a distinction English used to make too
(here/hither, there/thither) but has largely lost (p.206-207).

### 2. "Before"
English "before" maps to three different Swedish words depending on function: as a conjunction, it's
`innan` — unless the clause it introduces is itself negative, in which case it becomes `förrän`
(giving the `inte...förrän` = "not...until" pattern); as an adverb ("previously, earlier") it's
`förr`/`förut`/`tidigare`, used interchangeably (p.207).

## Grammar points (Unit 15)

### 1. Verbs ending in -s: the passive voice
The `-s` passive is the most common of Swedish's three passive strategies (written and spoken), used
especially when the agent is unknown or unimportant: `Bilen stals [av tjuven]` ("The car was stolen

register the book flags explicitly as a common home for this construction (p.221).

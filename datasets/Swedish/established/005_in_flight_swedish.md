# Swedish — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: In-Flight Swedish (complete)

**Source:** *Living Language In-Flight Swedish: Learn Before You Land* (Living Language, a Random
House Company), © 2001, ISBN 0-609-81096-0 — the printed companion insert for an audio phrasebook
course, all 18 lessons, pp. 1-20 (full booklet).

**Coverage note:** every distinct phrase and vocabulary item across all 18 lessons. This is a short
survival phrasebook, not a graded grammar course, so there is relatively little explicit grammar
instruction — the "Grammar points" section below documents the structural patterns that recur across
the phrase lists rather than points the source itself states as rules. This source has a real text
layer (`pdftotext -layout`); no vision-reading was required.

**A note on the source's pronunciation respellings:** every Swedish phrase/word is followed by a
parenthetical respelling in approximate English spelling conventions (e.g. `Hej!` → `(hay)`), not
IPA. These are the book's own pedagogical aid for anglophone travelers with no linguistics
background, reproduced in the Notes column as a pronunciation guide, not as a scholarly phonetic
transcription — flagged so they aren't mistaken for IPA.

**Genuinely register-marked items worth flagging:** `kompis` ("friend," Lesson 8) is the one clearly
colloquial lexical choice in the whole booklet, introduced alongside the more neutral `vän` — the
source doesn't tag it explicitly as informal, but a buddy/pal register is exactly what it supplies
in English ("friend," distinct from the surrounding `pojkvän`/`flickvän`/`fru`/`man` relationship
terms). `jätte trevligt` ("a wonderful time," Lesson 8, lit. "giant/really nice") uses `jätte-` as a
colloquial degree-intensifier prefix ("really/super"), which is the slang-relevant finding here —
flagged in that row's Notes.

## Grammar points

### 1. The polite conditional request: `Jag skulle vilja...` ("I would like...")

By far the booklet's most frequent construction (dozens of occurrences across nearly every lesson):
`skulle` (conditional/past of `ska`, "shall/will") + `vilja` ("to want/wish," infinitive) + a further
infinitive or noun phrase. Functions exactly like English "I would like to..."/"I would like a...",
and is used identically regardless of what follows (`...checka in`, `...ha ett rum`, `...köpa en
biljett`). A simpler variant drops `skulle`: `Jag skulle behöva...` ("I would need to...", Lesson 9,
13) substitutes `behöva` "to need" for `vilja`, slightly softening/hedging the request further.

### 2. Periphrastic politeness: `är du/Ni snäll` as a "please" tag

Alongside the simple adverb `Tack`/`Snälla` ("thank you"/"please"), the booklet repeatedly forms
"please" by appending a short clause meaning literally "...if you are kind/nice" to an imperative or
statement: `Tala långsamt är du snäll` ("Speak slowly, please," lit. "...are you kind"), `Stanna här
är Ni snäll` ("Stop here please"), `Skriv ner det där är du snäll` ("Write that, please"). The `du`
vs. `Ni` choice tracks the informal/formal register distinction (see point 3) — the same
construction appears in both registers, varying only the pronoun.

### 3. `du` vs. `Ni`: informal/formal second-person address

The booklet consistently uses `du` for informal "you" (friends, casual requests) and `Ni`/`er`/`ert`
(capitalized when it appears mid-sentence, e.g. `Här är er nyckel`, `Skulle Ni vilja...`) for formal
address (hotel staff, service contexts). Both pronouns coexist throughout rather than one replacing
the other — this phrasebook's register split is worth noting since modern spoken Swedish has
largely abandoned `Ni` in favor of universal `du`, but a 2001 travel-phrasebook still teaches `Ni`
as the default polite register for formal service encounters.

### 4. `Var` (locative "where") vs. `Vart` (directional "where to")

Lesson 18 explicitly introduces `Vart?` as "Where (to)?" immediately juxtaposed with a `Var är
utgången?` ("Where is the exit?") example, confirming the source is drawing a deliberate
locative/directional distinction. However, the distinction is **not applied consistently
elsewhere in the booklet**: Lesson 4's `Vart är passkontrollen?` ("Where is passport control?") and
Lesson 8's `Vart bor du?` ("Where do you live?") both use the directional `Vart` for what is
grammatically a locative sense (a fixed-location "where is," not a "where to") — either reflecting
real colloquial leveling of the distinction in casual spoken Swedish, or simple inconsistency in a
phrasebook not written with grammatical rigor. Flagged as a genuine cross-lesson inconsistency
rather than silently normalized.

### 5. Numeral compounding (0-999,999+)

Numbers 21-29 are formed as a single unbroken compound, `tjugo` ("twenty") + unit (`tjugoett`,
`tjugotvå`...`tjugonio`), with no space or hyphen. The decade words (`trettio`, `fyrtio`...`nittio`)
are formed with a `-tio` suffix on a reduced stem. Hundreds compound the unit word directly onto
`hundra` (`etthundra`, `tvåhundra`...), and three-digit numbers continue the same unbroken-compound
pattern (`etthundraett` "101"). `tusen` ("thousand") behaves the same way (`ettusen`), but `million`
breaks the pattern: it takes the indefinite article `en` like an ordinary noun (`en million`), not a
numeral-compound unit — consistent with its being grammatically a noun in Swedish, as in English
("a million," not a bare numeral).

### 6. Telling time: `halv` + the *next* hour, not the *current* one

`halv fyra` ("3:30") and `halv-nio` ("8:30," Filmen startar halv-nio) both show that Swedish `halv +
[hour N]` means "half an hour before hour N" — i.e. hour (N-1):30 — the same forward-looking
half-hour system found in German and several other Germanic/Scandinavian languages, and a common
trap for English speakers who default to reading `halv fyra` as "half past four." `kvart i` ("quarter
to") and `X minuter över Y` ("X minutes past Y") pattern as expected from English.

### 7. Days of the week and months of the year are lowercase common nouns

Unlike English capitalized "Monday," "March," etc., Swedish `måndag`, `mars`, and every other day/
month name in Lessons 16 appear lowercase throughout the source (confirmed not a scanning artifact —
mid-sentence capitalization is otherwise preserved correctly elsewhere, e.g. `Stockholm`,
`Kungsgatan`). These are ordinary common nouns, not proper nouns, in Swedish orthography.

### 8. Grammatical gender surfaces in the indefinite article even in a phrasebook

Although this source offers no explicit discussion of noun gender, the two indefinite articles
`en` (common gender) and `ett` (neuter gender) are used correctly and consistently throughout — e.g.
`ett rum` "a room" (neuter) vs. `en flaska` "a bottle" (common) vs. `en reservation` "a reservation"
(common) vs. `ett gym` "a gym" (neuter). This is real, if incidental, grammatical evidence even
though the booklet never states a gender rule.

### 9. `bli` + past participle as the passive

`Jag har blivit rånad` ("I've been robbed," Lesson 15) uses `bli` ("to become") as a passive
auxiliary with a past participle (`rånad`, from `råna` "to rob"), the standard Swedish periphrastic
passive construction alongside the inflectional `-s` passive not illustrated anywhere in this
phrasebook (only in the longer grammars extracted alongside this one).

### 10. `ha ont i` as the fixed idiom for localized physical pain

Three parallel examples (`Jag har ont i armen/benet/huvudet`, "my arm/leg/head hurts") show `ha ont i
[definite body part]` as a fixed idiomatic template — lit. "to have pain in [body part]" — rather
than a verb directly meaning "to hurt," distinct from the separate `må dåligt`/`känna sig yr`
constructions used for general/internal unwellness (nausea, dizziness) in the same lesson.

## Introduction (pp. ix-xiv) — comparative Germanic philology

McClean's introduction is framed entirely around comparative Germanic linguistics for an English
reader, distinct in kind from anything in this dataset's other Swedish sources:

- Swedish is classed as East Scandinavian historically (with Danish) but has drifted closer to
  Norwegian (especially *Landsmål*) in the modern era; Swedish and Norwegian are both tone languages,
  unlike Danish. Swedish orthography is praised as more phonetically transparent than Norwegian or
  Danish, which have more consonantal assimilation obscuring pronunciation from spelling (p. ix).
- A small set of Swedish "mute consonants" is noted: `d` in `djup`, `h` in `hjärta`, `l` in `ljus`
  (p. ix).
- Swedish grammar is framed as comparatively easy for an English speaker versus German: the old
  masculine/feminine genders have merged into a single "common" gender (vs. German's three, or
  Icelandic's three), verb endings have been much reduced (parallel to English), and strong-verb
  conjugation closely tracks English/German cognates where a cognate exists (p. x).
- **Direct Germanic cognate pairs** cited: `man`/"man," `fisk`/"fish," `hus`/"house," `horn`/"horn,"
  `hund`/"hound," `båt`/"boat," `bok`/"book" (easy cognates); `ord`/"word," `under`/"wonder,"
  `tacka`/"thank," `hjärta`/"heart," `hjälp`/"help," `bjärt`/"bright" (cognates obscured by sound
  shifts) (p. x).
- **Scots/Northern-English dialect cognates** — McClean's most distinctive comparative material,
  entirely unique to this source in the project so far: `tandvärk`/"toothache" vs. Lancashire dialect
  "tooth-wertch"; `huvudvärk`/"headache" vs. "head-wertch"; `sil` ("a strainer") and `silregn` ("rain
  falling as if poured through a sieve") vs. Cumberland dialect "It's fair silen doon" (raining cats
  and dogs) and the dialect verb "to sile" ("to strain, pour through a sieve"); further pairs offered
  without full gloss: Scots `braw`/Sw. `bra`, `bairn`/`barn`, `murk`/`mörk`, "to greet" (weep)/`gråta`,
  `neb`/`näbb`, `rowan`/`rönn`; the idiom "to go a-nice errand" paralleled by `gå ens ärende` (p. x-xi).
- **German loanword layer**: Low German-origin words in Swedish include `herre, fru, jungfru, adel,
  mästare, gesäll, lärling`; High German loanwords include `glans` (Glanz), `plötslig` (plötzlich),
  `gruva` (Grube), `hytta` (Hütte); derivational suffixes `-bar, -het, -inna, -eri, -era` were
  likewise borrowed via Low German (`fruktbar, frihet, furstinna, röveri, barbera` ~ German
  `fruchtbar, Freiheit, Fürstin, Räuberei, barbieren`) (p. xi).
- **Compound-word calquing from German**, with a cautionary anecdote: `snälltåg` (Schnellzug),
  `huvudstad` (Hauptstadt), `rådhus` (Rathaus), `skadeglädje` (Schadenfreude), `verktyg` (Werkzeug),
  `stenrik` (steinreich), `viktig` (wichtig), `likgiltig` (gleichgültig), `erhålla` (erhalten) — but
  McClean warns this element-by-element-translation method is "not free from pitfalls," recounting
  his own mistake asking a railway official where to `omstiga` ("change trains"), a request that was
  understood but which he later learned should have used `byta` instead (p. xi).

## Part I, §73 — "Colloquial Forms" (pp. 33-34): a genuine register/slang goldmine

**This is the single most valuable register-contrast section found in this source — flagged in full,
since it's a dedicated, explicit written-vs-colloquial-pronunciation treatment with no equivalent
elsewhere in this project's Swedish holdings.** McClean frames this explicitly: colloquial Swedish
diverges from the pronunciation implied by the written/pulpit-register language in systematic,
learnable ways, and he states English students should both recognize *and adopt* these forms "so that
his pronunciation may not appear stilted."

- **(a)** `-ig`-final adjectives drop the `g` in speech: `trevlig`→`[tre:vli]`, `trevligt`→`[tre:vlit]`,
  `trevliga`→`[tre:vlia]`; likewise the adverb `trevligt`.
- **(b)** Final unstressed `-o` is pronounced as a murmur vowel in the numerals `nio`, `tio` (matches
  Croghan's "nie"/"tie" note from Units 2-3, confirming this is a stable, well-attested colloquial
  pattern across sources).
- **(c)** The final consonant is dropped in a number of short frequently-used words: `dag`→`[da:]`,
  `god`→`[go:]`, `vad`→`[va]`, `det`→`[de]`, `med`→`[me:]`, `litet`→`[li:tə]`, `och`→`[o]`; `att` ("to"
  before an infinitive) is sometimes pronounced `[o]`.
- **(d)** Colloquial pronoun forms: `jag`→`[ja:]`, `mig`→`[mɛj]`, `dig`→`[dɛj]`, `sig`→`[sɛj]` (vs.
  pulpit-register `[mi:g], [di:g], [si:g]`); `de` and `dem` are **both** pronounced `[dom]` in speech
  — `de såg mig` and `jag såg dem` are homophonous as `[dɔm]` regardless of the written case
  distinction.
- **(e)** `någonting` contracts to `[nontiŋ]`; `några` assimilates to `[no:ra]`; `intet`/`inget`
  (neuter "nothing") is pronounced `[iŋət]`.
- **(f)** **Verb number-leveling**: the old plural verb forms have been entirely replaced by the
  singular form in speech, so each tense has only one form regardless of person/number — `vi lever`
  ("we live") is used instead of the old plural `vi leva`; `dom kan` instead of `de kunna`; `dom vann`
  instead of `de vunno`. (This confirms and gives the *historical* mechanism behind what Croghan's
  Unit 1 simply presented as the modern norm — present/past tense has a single form across all
  persons.)
- **(g)** A handful of verbs have short colloquial infinitive/present forms: `be/ber` (formal
  `bedja/beder`, "to request"); `bli/blir` (formal `bliva/bliver`, "to become"); `ge/ger` (formal
  `giva/giver`, "to give"); `ta/tar` (formal `taga/tager`, "to take") — the formal forms being
  the older, fuller ones.
- **(h)** Shortened colloquial past tenses: `lade`→`la` `[la:]` ("laid"), `sade`→`sa` `[sa:]` ("said")
  — the source notes these shortened forms are frequently used even in writing, not just speech.
- **(i)** The commonest auxiliary verbs have distinct colloquial pronunciations: `jag är`→`[ja(:)e:]`,
  `de är`→`[dom e:]`, `jag var`→`[ja(:)va]`, `vi var`→`[vi va]`, `jag skall`→`[ja(:)ska:]` (stressed)
  or `[ja ska]` (unstressed), `vi skall`→`[vi ska:]`/`[vi ska]`. Full forms `[ja:g], [vɛ:r], [va:r]`
  remain frequent in colloquial use alongside the reduced ones.

## Part II — Chapter sampling (noun/case system, pp. 48, 63)

**Scope note:** noun-plural declension classes substantially overlap Croghan's Units 3-7 coverage
(already fully captured in `008`) — not re-tabulated here except where McClean adds something new.

**p.48, Second Declension (`-ar` plural):** confirms the same irregular-plural set Croghan gives
(`afton→aftnar`, `morgon→morgnar`, `sommar→somrar`, `dotter→döttrar`, `moder→mödrar`) — genuine
cross-source agreement, not new content. **New here:** McClean explicitly flags `föräldrar`
("parents") and `pengar` ("money") as nouns that **occur in the plural only** — a grammatical-number
restriction Croghan doesn't state explicitly even though it uses both words.

**p.63, "Remarks on the Genitive" and "Relics of old case endings" (§102-103):** genuinely new content
not covered anywhere in Croghan or this project's other Swedish sources:
- A proper noun already ending in `-s` takes **no additional** genitive `-s`; the genitive is instead
  shown in writing with just an apostrophe (`Nils' penna`, "Nils's pencil").
- A common noun ending in `-s` in its indefinite form should have its genitive **avoided entirely** by
  rephrasing with a preposition instead (e.g. "the name of a rose" as `Richmond är namnet på en ros`
  rather than forcing a genitive).
- `av` ("of") is the normal way to express origin/provenance/material: `ringen är av guld` ("the ring
  is of gold"), `Hertiginnan av Gloucester` ("the Duchess of Gloucester").
- Institutional names like "University of London" are rendered three different ways in Swedish,
  inconsistently: a preposition (`universitetet i London`), an `-s` genitive (`Lunds universitet`), or
  apposition with a connecting link (`Uppsala universitet` or `universitetet i Uppsala`).
- Latin-origin names ending in `-us` often retain a Latin-style genitive ending: `Kristi födelse`
  ("the birth of Christ").
- **Fossilized relic case-endings survive in a handful of fixed words/phrases** from Old Swedish's
  richer case system: `dager` ("light, daylight," beside the ordinary `dag`) and `slavar` ("little
  rascal") preserve an old masculine nominative singular `-er` ending; `läroverk` ("school"),
  `kyrkoherde` ("rector, vicar"), `ladugård` ("barn"), `varuhus` ("emporium/department store"),
  `gatutrafik` ("street traffic"), and the phrase `till salu` ("for sale") all preserve relics of old
  genitive-singular endings from originally feminine nouns (`lära, vecka, kyrka, lada, vara, gata`).

## Chapter sampling: verbs, demonstratives, register notes (pp. 93, 108, 148)

**p.93:** First-conjugation verb list and ordinal numbers largely overlap Croghan's coverage (already
in `008`) — not re-tabulated. One genuinely new note: a footnote flags `icke` as a literary-register
negative ("not"), alongside the ordinary `inte` — McClean's grammar-term footnotes consistently mark
literary/archaic alternatives this way, a register-tagging habit distinct from Croghan's style.

**p.108, Demonstrative adjectives/pronouns (§155-156):** a fuller paradigm than anything in Croghan —
Swedish makes **no formal distinction** between demonstrative adjectives and demonstrative pronouns
(unlike English "that" (adj) vs. "that one" (pron) having different machinery). Full paradigm:
`den/det/de` ("that/those," also doubling as the definite article when unstressed — distinguished from
demonstrative use only by stronger stress), `denna/detta/dessa` ("this/these"), `den där/det där/de
där` ("that/those," spatially deictic), `den här/det där/de här` ("this/these," spatially deictic).
**Register note:** `denna/detta/dessa` are more characteristic of written Swedish; in colloquial
speech `denne` (rather than `denna`) is often substituted when referring to a masculine noun.

**p.148, politeness/register notes (§200-201):**
- Several ways to say "pardon?/what did you say?" are ranked by formality: `Förlåt, jag hörde inte`
  (most polite) > `Hur sade ni?` (frequent) > `Vad för slag?`/`Vad befalles?`/`Vafalls?` ("beg
  pardon?") > a bare `Vad?` — **explicitly flagged by the source as "vulgar and should be avoided."**
  This is a genuine, explicitly-marked register judgment distinct from anything in this project's
  other Swedish sources.
- `sådan`/`dylik` (both "such, like that") have a full common/neuter/plural adjective-and-pronoun
  paradigm; `dylik` is noted as "rather formal and not often used nowadays" even by 1947 — an
  explicitly flagged declining-currency item, the same kind of judgment Croghan later makes about
  `jänta`/`tös` (see `008` Unit 4).
- `sådan` is also used in exclamations: `En sådan dumbom!` ("What a blockhead!" — a mild insult),
  `Ett sådant väder vi får!` ("What weather we're having!").
- Proverb: `Människan spår, men Gud rår` ("Man proposes, God disposes").

## Chapter sampling: letter-writing conventions, "to do," infinitives (pp. 168, 188, 208)

**p.168, letter-writing register conventions** (end of a letter-writing chapter): a genuinely distinct
content area from Croghan, giving period (1947) epistolary formulas:
- Salutations: `Bäste Bror!` ("Dear Brother!") or simply `Broder!` are used to open letters between
  male friends of any age who address each other as `du` — explicitly noted as having **no feminine
  equivalent** in the source. To a stranger, `Dear Sir`/`Dear Sirs` is **not translated** in formal
  style — the addressee's actual name is written out instead (`Herr Arvid Johansson`, or for multiple
  people `Herrarna Arvid och Magnus Johansson`); less formally, `Bäste Herr Johansson!` ("Dear Mr.
  Johnson!").
- Formal closing to a stranger/business letter: `Högaktningsfullt` (lit. "respectfully," = "yours
  faithfully"); `På förhand tackande` = "thanking you in anticipation."
- Closing to a friend: `Er`/`Din` + `tillgivne` (fem. `tillgivna`) = "yours sincerely" — e.g. `Din
  tillgivne broder, Hjalmar`.
- Closing greetings-within-letters: `Hjärtliga hälsningar` or `Många varma hälsningar` ("kind
  regards"); `Pappa och mamma ber hälsa` ("Papa and Mamma send their love"); `Hälsa dina föräldrar
  från mig` ("please remember me to your parents"); `Många hälsningar till Lennart och Henrik` ("kind
  regards to Lennart and Henrik").
- **Grammatical-change-in-progress note**: McClean states the `-a` ending (as in `tillgivna`) "is
  tending to displace" the old masculine `-e` ending of the adjective — a live sound-change-in-
  progress observation from 1947.

**p.188, Chapter XX, "To Do — Use of the Tenses":** Swedish `göra` is a full lexical verb only, never
an auxiliary — unlike English "do," which has no Swedish translation at all in negative/interrogative
sentences (`Säg inte det!`, lit. "Say not that!"; `Bor han här?`, lit. "Lives he here?"). Emphatic "did"
(as in "I *did* see him") is rendered with an adverb like `verkligen` ("really") rather than any verbal
construction. "Will this do?" (= "be sufficient/suitable") maps to context-dependent verbs: `räcker`
("be sufficient"), `duger`("meet the case"), `passar` ("be suitable"). **Swedish has no grammaticalized
progressive form at all** — `Jag skriver ett brev` covers both "I write a letter" and "I am writing a
letter," decided from context alone (p.188).

**p.208, Chapter XXII, infinitive constructions:** the infinitive is normally preceded by `att`
("to"); when expressing personal aim/intention it takes `för att` ("in order to"); when expressing a
substance's utilitarian purpose it takes `till att` (`Fett användes till att göra tvål`, "Fat is used
to make soap"). The bare infinitive **without** `att` appears in proverbs and elevated literary style:
`Tala är silver, tiga är guld` ("Speech is silver, silence is gold") (p.208).

## Chapter sampling: conjunctions and interjections (pp. 223, 228)

**p.223, `men` vs. `utan` ("but"):** `men` is used after an affirmative clause; `utan` is used after a
negative clause/phrase when a contrast is implied (paralleling German `aber`/`sondern`) — e.g. `inte
rik utan fattig` ("not rich but poor"). A footnote clarifies `en smörgås` ("open sandwich") typically
has a topping (`ett pålägg`, e.g. meat or cheese) and so functions as "a kind of one-sided sandwich."

**p.228, Chapter — Interjections (§294): a second genuine slang/register-rich section.** McClean gives
a full catalogue of Swedish interjections with etymological/register commentary distinct from anything
in Croghan:
- `ack!` corresponds exactly to German `ach!`
- `aj, aj!`/`oj, oj, oj!` express pain or regret
- `asch!`/`usch!`/`äsch!` express "varying degrees of disparagement"
- `jaså!` has a wide range of meanings depending on intonation alone — can signal acceptance of a
  fact, surprise, or ironic disbelief
- **`ja, ja män!`** ("yes indeed!") — McClean identifies `män` here as **an obscured/euphemized oath**,
  historically standing for "the holy men," i.e. "the saints." Similarly, **`nej men se!`** ("just look
  at that!") conceals a disguised-oath origin, "though the spelling `men` conceals the origin of the
  word." **This is a genuine euphemistic-oath-erosion finding — directly relevant to slang/taboo-
  avoidance mechanics** (parallel in kind to the project's prior taboo-avoidance-naming findings in
  other languages, per this session's MEMORY notes).

## Restaurant-menu and literary-reading sampling (pp. 233, 248)

**p.233:** a dialogue at "Restaurant Viktoria" shows written-colloquial apostrophe contractions
directly in printed dialogue text — `Ska' vi sätta oss?` ("Shall we sit down?") and `jag ska' be att
få...` — confirming §73's note that these shortened auxiliary forms cross over into written
representation of speech. The printed period (1940s) menu itself lists dishes distinct from Croghan's
food vocabulary: `Hallonkräm med vispad grädde` ("raspberry cream with whipped cream"), `Filbunke`
("clotted sour milk, similar to yoghurt" — the source's own gloss, cf. Croghan's `fil(mjölk)` in
Unit 2, cross-source agreement on this distinctively Swedish food), `Bräckt falukorv med stuvad
potatis` ("broiled Falun sausage with stewed potatoes"), `Hackad kalvfilé med svampsås` ("minced veal
cutlet with mushroom sauce"), `Champinjonsoppa` ("mushroom/champignon soup").

**p.248:** a literary reading passage (dated "Matriculation, June 1943" — i.e. drawn from an actual
exam text) describes the childhood home of Erik Gustav Geijer (a real 18th/19th-century Swedish
historian/poet, b. 1783) — narrative/literary register prose, distinct from the book's conversational
dialogues. Vocabulary sample: `envåningsbyggnaden` ("the single-storey building"), `blyinfattade
fönsterrutor` ("lead-set/leaded window-panes"), `länstolar` ("armchairs"), `strövtåg` ("rambles,
rovings"). Not reproduced at length per copyright discipline.

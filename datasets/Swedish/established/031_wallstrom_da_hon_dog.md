# Swedish — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: *Då hon dog* (sampled across the whole book)

**Source:** Tord Wallström, *Då hon dog*, internally dated 2006 (per the book's own front-matter note
signed "TORD"); PDF file metadata (`pdfinfo`) shows a creation date of November 2011 and a PageMaker/
Distiller production pipeline consistent with a small-press or self-published edition. No copyright
page, ISBN, or publisher imprint appears anywhere in the extracted text, so the exact first-publication
year is **not independently confirmed** — treated below as "contemporary, composed 2006, file dated
2011."

**GENRE CORRECTION — flagged explicitly.** The dispatch briefing for this file described the source as
"a native Swedish crime novel" and expected police-procedural/criminal-slang register. Having read the
actual extracted text, **this is not a crime novel.** It is a first-person grief memoir/diary: the
author's day-by-day account of his wife Eivor's death from cancer, her final weeks in hospital and
hospice, her cremation and the scattering of her ashes, and his mourning afterward, interleaved with
letters from an Irish clergyman friend (David) and translated English poems/quotations in an appendix.
There is no crime, investigation, or police content anywhere in the sampled text (opening chapters,
hospital/hospice middle section, a dialogue-heavy delirium passage, the funeral/cremation scene, the
grief-aftermath section, and the closing poems appendix were all checked). The extraction below is
therefore reoriented toward what this source actually offers: **hospital/hospice/palliative-care
register, funeral and bereavement vocabulary, euphemism around death and dying, and colloquial
discourse particles captured in the dying woman's delirious reported speech** — genuinely the strongest
material in the sample, just not the genre originally expected.

**Text layer:** confirmed real/clean via `pdftotext -layout` (Adobe Distiller-produced PDF, no font-
substitution cipher, no column-scrambling — this is single-column continuous prose throughout, so none
of the two-column-glossary/scrambled-vocabulary-box gotchas apply). A quick sanity read confirmed
paragraphs are coherent and not interleaved.

**Coverage note:** this is a native-language usage corpus (a memoir), not a teaching grammar, so the
extraction below is a **meaningful sample, not an exhaustive tabulation** — opening chapters, a long
middle hospital/hospice section, the funeral-director/cremation scene, a dialogue-heavy delirium

the rest of the ~20,000-word extracted text was swept with targeted searches (dialogue markers,

## Grammar points / narrative-register observations

**Coverage note:** brief/sparse is appropriate here per the extraction spec — this is a literary prose
source, not a teaching grammar, so there are few discrete "grammar points" as such. What's genuinely
distinctive is the narrative voice and register-switching, noted below.

### Present-tense immediate narration interleaved with past-tense flashback
Most of the hospital/hospice chapters are narrated in the present tense ("Hon andas inte längre," "Jag
ropar på Peter") even though they describe past events — a diary-like immediacy device — while clearly
marked flashback passages ("Tidigare, efter operationen...") switch cleanly to the preterite. The two
tenses are never mixed within a single paragraph; the switch itself signals "this is a different time
layer," functioning almost like a chapter break substitute in a book with no numbered chapters.

### Reported delirious/dying speech rendered as unpunctuated, topic-jumping run-ons
Where the wife's lucid dialogue is rendered in ordinary question-and-answer exchanges, her delirious
monologues (e.g. the passage sampled around "Ja, det minns jag...") are instead rendered as a single
unbroken block of short declarative fragments with no intervening narration, imitating incoherent
real-time speech directly rather than describing it. This is the main place the book's register drops
into genuinely spoken-colloquial Swedish (`jaha`, `jaså`, `nehej`, `jodå`, `okej`) — everywhere else the
narrator's own voice stays in a measured, literary register even while describing visceral events.

### Imagined dialogue with the deceased, marked identically to real dialogue
After the wife's death, the narrator writes imagined conversations with her in exactly the same
quotation-mark dialogue format used for real conversations earlier in the book ("'Förlåt mig,' säger
jag nu i mina tankar... 'Men du kunde ju inget göra,' svarar hon (i mina tankar)"). The only marker
distinguishing real from imagined dialogue is the parenthetical "(i mina tankar)" ("in my thoughts") —
otherwise the two registers are typographically indistinguishable, reinforcing the book's central
claim that the dead woman's presence doesn't feel symbolically different from her living presence.

### Embedded, separately-translated English-language poems as a structural bilingual layer
Several English-language poems/quotations are quoted untranslated at the point they appear in the
narrative, then given full Swedish verse translations in a dedicated appendix ("ÖVERSÄTTNING AV DE
ENGELSKA AVSNITTEN") at the end of the book, each with a short biographical note on the original poet.
This is a structural code-switching choice — the main narrative voice stays Swedish throughout, English
intrudes only as quoted/cited material, and is resolved into Swedish only in back matter rather than
in-line — not a feature of ordinary Swedish grammar, but a notable register/genre convention worth
recording for a multilingual source like this.

### Institutional ward/department names take the definite article as if proper nouns
As flagged in the vocabulary table (`Onkologen`, `Hospis`), Swedish hospital departments are referred
to with the definite suffix attached to an otherwise generic noun, functioning grammatically like a
proper name ("vi är nu i rum nr 1 på avdelning 88, Onkologen") — a real institutional-register
convention distinct from ordinary definite-article use.

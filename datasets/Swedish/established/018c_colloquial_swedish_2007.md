### Grammar points

- **Definite adjective declension Type 2 — four licensing contexts, plus two exceptions.** The adjective takes its definite ending (-a, sometimes -e) while the noun itself takes **no** article, after: (1) a genitive (*Svens nya båt*); (2) a possessive (*hans risiga bil*); (3) the demonstrative *denna* etc. (*denna gropiga grusväg*); (4) certain other words — *samma, nästa, följande, föregående* ("the same/next/following/previous"). **Exception 1**: after a genitive-of-measurement, the adjective reverts to the plain indefinite form, agreeing with the following noun (*en sorts dyrbar båt*). **Exception 2**: *egen* ("own") always stays indefinite even after a genitive/possessive (*pappas egen lilla Eva*). The book also notes a register split: *denna* is the more written/formal demonstrative (no end article), while *den här* is the more spoken/informal equivalent and **always** takes an end article (*denna nya bil* vs. *den här nya bilen*) — a genuinely useful formal/informal demonstrative-register pairing.
- **Full modal-verb paradigm** (*kunna, skola, vilja, måste, böra, få*) given with present/past/supine forms, plus **three explicit false-friend warnings for English speakers**: (1) *ska* sometimes = "will" (future) while *vill* = "want to" — easily confused since English "will" can mean either future or desire; (2) *måste*/*får* = "have to" **only in positive statements**, but *måste inte* = "don't need to" while *får inte* = "must not" — a genuinely counter-intuitive negation-polarity trap (negating "must"/"have to" in Swedish doesn't produce "must not"); (3) *bör/borde* = "ought to/should" (advice) vs. *skulle* = conditional "should/would," typically paired with an *om/ifall* clause. These are prime candidates for conlang modal-verb-system pitfalls if borrowing Swedish-style modal semantics.
- **Infinitive usage, bare vs. *att*-marked.** Bare infinitive: after modals/modal-equivalents (*tänker, behöver, börjar, fortsätter, försöker, hoppas, lyckas, orkar, slipper, slutar, vägrar* — a fairly long closed-ish class) and in the "object + bare infinitive" construction after perception/permission verbs (*se, höra, låta, tillåta, anse*: *Jag såg honom gå* — "I saw him leave," paralleling English). *Att*-marked infinitive: as subject (*Att köra bil kan vara krångligt*), as object (*Han älskar att köra fort*), after a preposition (*utan att säga adjö*), after a noun (*Har du tid att göra det?*) — and the book notes the English "-ing" gerund often maps onto this *att*-infinitive. A special case: *för att* + infinitive specifically marks purpose/intention ("in order to").
- **ingen/inte någon distribution rule, precisely specified by tense/clause-type** — directly extends and sharpens Unit 9's looser treatment: the two are free alternatives in a simple-tense main clause (*De fick ingen middag* = *De fick inte någon middag*), but **only** *inte någon* (never *ingen*) is grammatical (1) in a main clause with a complex tense/auxiliary/particle verb (*De har inte fått någon middag*) or (2) as the object of a sub-clause — where the book explicitly cross-references the SIV rule from Unit 10 to explain *inte*'s required pre-verbal position. This is a clean worked example of how two surface-synonymous negation strategies can be governed by independent syntactic conditions — valuable for conlang negation-system design.
- **Själv as an emphatic (not reflexive) particle**: *Kan du göra det själv?* ("Can you do it yourself?"), *Poliserna tvättar bilarna själva* ("The policemen themselves wash the cars") — the book is explicit that this is a distinct function from the reflexive pronoun system (covered at p. 211, i.e. later in the book).

### Grammar points

- **Article-use divergences from English, two directions.** (a) Swedish uses the end article where English uses none: abstract nouns in a general sense (*Priserna stiger* — "Prices are rising"), locations (*Fredrik går i kyrkan* — "...to church"), days/seasons/festivals (*På fredagarna* — "On Fridays"). (b) Swedish uses no article where English uses the indefinite article: count nouns in a general/existential sense (*Man får göra upp eld* — "One may light a fire"; *De väntar barn* — "They are expecting a child"), and after *vilken/mången* (*Vilken vacker dag!* — "What a beautiful day!").
- **Definite adjective declension Type 3 — fixed-phrase definiteness.** Front article dropped, adjective keeps its definite ending, noun keeps its end article: *Svenska akademien*, *Röda korset*, *Förenta staterna*. Triggered when a phrase becomes fixed/lexicalized (the adjective loses its stress) — contrasted directly with the live, stressed, fully-marked equivalent (*det vita huset* "the white house" vs. the proper-name-like *Vita huset* "the White House"). The construction is **obligatory** (not just common) with *hela, halva, själva, båda, förra, ena, enda*, and recurrently found with geographic names, nationality adjectives, color adjectives, locational words, and ordinal numbers — a genuinely productive definiteness-marking subtype worth distinguishing from ordinary Type-1 definiteness in conlang design.
- **Adjective -t forms vs. derived -t adverbs — a genuine surface-form collision the book flags explicitly.** *Agneta är vacker* (adjective, en-form) / *Rummet är vackert* (adjective, ett-form) / *Agneta sjunger vackert* (adverb) all differ only by context, since the adverb is formed by the **same** -t suffix as the ett-form adjective. Additional adverb-forming suffixes -en and -tvis (*möjligen*, *naturligtvis*) pattern as sentence adverbs (same syntactic slot as *inte*), while -t adverbs are MPT-expressions typically at clause-end — giving two distinct positional classes of derived adverb.
- **Permission/prohibition phrase set**, with a second explicit flag of *måste* as a "false friend" (repeating/reinforcing Unit 12's finding): *Man får (inte) göra det*, *Förbud mot...*, *Man bör göra det*, *Man har rätt att...*; *Man måste göra det* ("one must") vs. *Man måste inte göra det* ("one does NOT need to," not "must not") vs. *Man får/ska inte göra det* ("one must/should not").
- **Reflexive pronoun system**: only 3rd person (singular and plural) has a dedicated reflexive form (*sig*); 1st/2nd person reuse the ordinary object pronouns (*mig, dig, oss, er*). Used as object or after a preposition, always referring back to the clause subject — and critically, Swedish uses the reflexive after a preposition where English often doesn't need "self" at all (*Han stängde dörren efter sig* — "He closed the door behind him," not "behind himself"). **Key exception**: in an "object + infinitive" construction (cf. Unit 12's *se/höra/låta* pattern), the reflexive pronoun refers to the **object**, not the subject, of the main clause (*Han bad doktorn tvätta sig* — "He asked the doctor to wash **himself** [=the doctor]" vs. *...tvätta honom* — "...wash **him** [=the subject]") — a genuinely subtle binding-theory-relevant exception worth flagging for conlang reflexive-pronoun design. Unstressed reflexive/object pronouns can cliticize immediately after VERB1 in a simple-tense clause (*Olle tvättar sig aldrig*), but after VERB2 when a complex/two-verb construction is used (*Olle har inte tvättat sig idag*).
- **Present participles (-nde, rarely used as true verbs in Swedish)** function instead as: (a) adjectives (*levande träd* — "living trees"), (b) nouns — en-gender typically denoting a person (*en studerande* "a student"), ett-gender a verbal abstract (*ett förhållande* "a relationship") — a systematic gender-to-semantic-role mapping worth noting; (c) adverbs (*fortfarande* "still"). Occasionally used verbally after *komma/gå/bli* and motion verbs, paralleling English "-ing" (*Älgen kom gående genom skogen* — "The elk came walking through the forest"), though the book reiterates (cf. Unit 12) that English "-ing" more often maps onto the Swedish infinitive instead.

### Grammar points

- **Four methods of adjective comparison** (three parallel English, one does not): (a) -are/-ast, the majority pattern including all -(l)ig adjectives and some surprising inclusions unlike English (*intelligent, intressant, modern, skicklig, svår* all compare this way in Swedish despite being polysyllabic — English would use "more/most" for cognates); (b) vowel change + -re/-st, a closed list given in full (*stor→större→störst*, *låg→lägre→lägst*, *ung→yngre→yngst*, *hög→högre→högst*, etc.); (c) irregular stem-changing comparison, also a closed list (*god/bra→bättre→bäst*, *dålig→sämre→sämst*, *gammal→äldre→äldst*, *liten→mindre→minst*, *många→fler→flest*, *mycket→mer→mest*); (d) periphrastic *mer/mest* + adjective — used for all past participles, bisyllabic -isk adjectives, and an increasing number of long/polysyllabic adjectives, with the adjective itself still needing indefinite/definite agreement (*ett mer komplicerat problem*). The book flags a genuinely tricky double-irregular-pair distinction: *värre* ("more of an originally bad property") vs. *sämre* ("less of an originally good property") — two different comparatives of "bad" depending on whether badness is being intensified or goodness diminished — and a count/non-count split for "more/most": *mer/mest* for non-count nouns, *fler/flest* for count nouns (*mer kaffe* vs. *fler elever*).
- **Similarity/dissimilarity vocabulary cluster**: dissimilarity via comparative + *än* (*starkare än*), *annorlunda än*, or *olik* (agrees with the noun: *Hans dialekt är olik din*); similarity via *lik*/*liknar* ("like"/"resembles"), *lika...som* ("as...as"), *samma...som* ("the same...as"), *likadan som* ("like, identical to").
- **Definite superlative inflection, contrasted explicitly with the (non-inflecting) comparative.** The comparative never changes in the definite (*den vackrare flickan*, *de vackrare flickorna* — same form throughout). The superlative does inflect: -ast superlatives always add -e (*den vackraste flickan*); -st superlatives add -a (or -e for masculine singular: *den störste pojken*); *mest*-superlatives add -a/-e to the adjective itself (*den mest intelligenta flickan*, masc. *den mest sympatiske studenten*).
- **Article-use divergence #2 — no article in Swedish, definite article in English**: with instruments/machines (*Jag spelar piano* — "I play the piano"; *Vi tittar på TV*); with proper names in the plural meaning "the X family" (*hos Olssons* — "at the Olssons'"); after *nästa, samma, fel, rätt, följande, föregående* (*nästa by* — "the next village"; *fel väg* — "the wrong road").
- **Two exceptions where a sub-clause takes main-clause word order instead of SIV**, both explicitly flagged as genuine grammatical phenomena rather than errors: (a) an *att*-clause whose first element after *att* is a non-subject triggers full subject–verb inversion exactly as in a main clause (*Janne säger att imorgon tänker han inte åka hem*) — "now common in both spoken and written Swedish"; (b) a spoken-only, written-ungrammatical pattern where *att*-clauses keep main-clause V1 position even with a plain subject-first order (*Han sa att han kunde inte komma* — adverb after, not before, the verb) — the book is explicit this is **not acceptable in written Swedish** but occurs in speech, a clean spoken/written register split.
- **Inverted-order conditional clauses without *om/ifall***, structurally identical to a yes/no question but not functioning as one: *Bjuder du inte henne, (så) blir hon ledsen* ("If you don't invite her, she'll be sad") — paralleled by the English literary/formal inversion "Had I known you were coming, I would have waited," a genuine typological point of contact between the two languages' conditional-inversion strategies.
- **Second explicit "Slang" section (labeled "Slang (2)"), the largest single slang-vocabulary dump in the book — roughly 35 items**, each given with its standard-Swedish gloss, spanning multiple semantic domains: body parts (*käft* "mouth/gob"), food (*käk, käka, macka, snask*), vehicles/places (*kärra, hoj, kåk, mack, fik*), money (*spänn, stålar*), toilets (*mugg, toa* — two independent slang strategies, full substitution vs. clipping, for the same referent), people (*höjdare, snut*), states/qualities (*bergis, bussig, deppig, gaggig, kass, klantig, läskig, läbbig, packad, töntig*), and verbs (*funka, lira, mygla, ragga upp, sabba, snacka*). Three near-synonymous slang nouns for "thing" (*grej, grunka, pryl*) show genuine lexical redundancy within the slang register itself — useful for conlang design since it demonstrates slang registers tolerate more near-synonymy than core vocabulary. This section, combined with Unit 11's "Slang (1)" and the scattered slang items flagged throughout Units 1–13, gives this book's slang coverage real breadth: kinship terms, currency, body parts, food, vehicles, dwellings, people-types, and evaluative adjectives are all represented.
- **Compounding, expanded with explicit linking-element rules.** Plain NOUN+NOUN (*rösträtt*) vs. NOUN+s+NOUN (*kvällspress*) — the -s link is used when the first element is itself a compound (*fotbollslag*) or ends in -(n)ing/-ling/-an/-nad/-(i)tet/-(a)tion (*koalitionsregering*). Further methods: drop a final -a/-e from the first noun (*flicka→flicknamn*); insert a linking vowel -e- (*familj+sida→familjesida*); use an old case ending as linker (*vecka+slut→veckoslut*, *vara+hus→varuhus*) — this last method now rare for new compounds, per the book.

### Grammar points

- **Letter-writing register ladder, fully explicit in the source.** Openings: *Hej!* (neutral, often + first name), *Tjena/Tjenare* (explicitly labeled "(slang)" by the book), *Kära* and *Bästa/Bäste* (formal/warm). Closings: *Med vänlig hälsning/Vänliga hälsningar* (usable for both private and formal letters — a register-neutral safe choice), *Hej då!/Vi hörs!/Vi ses!* (informal), *Kram!/Puss och kram!* (affectionate/informal). This is a clean three-register system (slang/neutral/formal) mapped onto a single communicative function (greeting someone in writing), directly reusable for conlang register-ladder design.
- **Reflexive possessive *sin/sitt/sina* vs. non-reflexive *hans/hennes/dess/deras*, precisely rule-governed.** *Sin* etc. may **only** qualify the object (or an adjunct) of the clause and **must** refer back to the clause's own subject (*Han har sålt sin bil* — "He sold his [=his own] car"); it can **never** qualify the subject itself. *Hans/hennes* etc. are used elsewhere, including when the possessor is NOT the subject (*Åke gick ut med hans fru* — "Åke went out with his [=someone else's] wife"). Two tricky exception triggers are named explicitly: a new sub-clause subject breaks reflexive reference even if semantically continuous (*De tycker att deras lektioner är tråkiga* uses non-reflexive *deras* because "their lessons" is the **sub-clause's own** subject, not really bound to the main clause's subject the way a direct object would be); and a double/compound subject blocks the reflexive (*Han och hans fru åkte bort* — "he and his wife," not *sin fru*, because "he and his wife" together form the subject). This full binding-condition specification is one of the more intricate and conlang-relevant grammar points in the book.
- **Article-use divergence #3 — Swedish end article instead of a possessive, specifically with body parts and clothing when ownership is contextually obvious**: *Hon satte på sig morgonrocken* ("She put on her dressing gown" — lit. "the dressing gown"), *Han har ont i ryggen* ("He has a pain in his back" — lit. "in the back"). This "definite article instead of possessive with inalienable/contextually-obvious possessions" pattern is cross-linguistically common and worth flagging as a genuine typological choice point for conlang possessive-marking design.
- **Transitive/intransitive verb pairs using distinct lexical items (and often distinct conjugation classes), rather than one verb doing both jobs as in English.** Given pairs: *sätta* (trans., put — weak) / *sitta* (intrans., sit — strong); *lägga* (trans., lay — weak) / *ligga* (intrans., lie — strong); *fälla* (trans., fell) / *falla* (intrans., fall — strong); *bränna* (trans., burn — weak) / *brinna* (intrans., burn — strong); *kyla* (trans., chill) / *kallna* (intrans., cool). This weak-transitive/strong-intransitive pairing pattern recurs across several pairs and is a genuine systematic feature (not coincidence) worth modeling directly in a derived conlang's verb-valence morphology.
- **Unstressed object pronouns move to a pre-adverb position**, contrasted with stressed nouns/pronouns which stay post-adverb: *Hon gillar den inte alls* (unstressed pronoun *den* before *inte*) vs. *Hon gillar inte alls popkonst* (stressed noun stays after the adverb); a stressed pronoun used contrastively also stays post-adverb (*Jag känner inte 'honom [men jag känner 'henne]*) while the neutral/unstressed reading moves it forward (*Jag känner honom 'inte*). This is a genuinely subtle information-structure-driven word-order alternation — pronoun position tracks stress/contrastiveness, not just grammatical category.
- **Beginning/continuing/ending vocabulary cluster**, with one more false-friend flag: *starta* does **not** mean generic "start" but specifically "set out"/"start up an engine"/"take off" (of a plane) — *sätta igång* is the more general "start" verb. *Hålla på och/med att* marks ongoing/progressive activity ("to be doing X right now"); *pågå* = "continue/be in progress"; *sluta* = "end/stop."
- **Subjunctive mood, explicitly noted as nearly extinct in modern Swedish**, surviving as: (a) a genuine subjunctive verb form in -e for a handful of verbs, chiefly *vore* (from *vara*): *Det vore kul om du kunde komma* — paralleled by, and interchangeable with, the periphrastic *Det skulle vara kul...*; (b) fossilized fixed expressions only: *Leve brudparet!* ("Long live the bridal couple!"), *Tack vare din hjälp* ("Thanks to your help" — the subjunctive origin of *tack vare* is now invisible synchronically, it simply functions as a preposition). This near-total loss of a productive subjunctive, surviving only in frozen forms, is a clean case study for conlang diachronic mood-system attrition.

### Grammar points

- **Past participle formation, four patterns tied to conjugation class**, and explicitly distinguished from the supine (which only builds perfect/pluperfect tenses): (a) Conj. I/IIa weak verbs (voiced-consonant or -a stem) add -d (*packad*); (b) Conj. IIb (voiceless-consonant stem) add -t (*köpt*); (c) Conj. III (long-vowel stem) add -dd (*klädd*); (d) strong verbs: delete the supine's -it and add -en (*skrivit→skriven*). Unlike the indeclinable supine, the past participle functions as a true adjective across indefinite predicative, indefinite attributive, and definite attributive slots, with its own agreement paradigm — notably, Conjugation IIb past participles are **identical in both en- and ett-form** (both end in -t), and the definite/plural form is -e for participles ending in -ad but -a for all others (mirroring the masculine-singular-adjective -e exception pattern established in Unit 9).
- **Particle verbs: stressed particle (adverb/preposition) + verb = single unit of meaning, genuinely different from an unstressed preposition + verb.** Minimal pair given: *Han sätter sig på regnrocken* (unstressed preposition "on") vs. *Han sätter på sig regnrocken* ("puts on," stressed particle) — same words, different stress placement, different meaning entirely. The book gives extensive lists of prepositions (*på, av, med, efter, om, till, över*) and adverbs (*omkring, runt, förbi, tillbaka, kvar, igång, iväg, undan, omkull, sönder, fast, loss, ihop, isär, itu, ikapp, ihjäl*) that function as stressed particles — a rich closed-class inventory directly reusable for conlang phrasal-verb design.
- **Participles of particle verbs fuse the particle into a single prefixed word**: *tycker om* ("like") → *omtyckt* ("popular," lit. "liked"); *kastar bort* ("throws away") → *bortkastad* ("discarded"). Further examples from the text: *instängd* (←*stänga in*, "shut in"), *utsliten* (←*slita ut*, "worn out"), *omhändertagen* (←*ta hand om*, "taken care of") — demonstrating this fusion is fully productive, not just a handful of lexicalized forms.
- **Compound verbs: inseparable vs. separable, with an explicit style/meaning correlation.** Inseparable-prefix verbs (*betala, förklara, oroa, undkomma*) have only one form. Separable-compound verbs have two alternate forms per tense (*känna igen* / *igenkänna* — "recognize"), but **always** join up in participle forms. Crucially, the book states a systematic register correlation: the separated form tends toward **lower style and/or concrete meaning**, the joined-up form toward **higher style and/or abstract meaning** — demonstrated with *gå förbi* (concrete: "walk past physically") vs. *förbigå* (abstract: "pass over/disregard," as in being overlooked for a prize). This stylistic conditioning of a single verb's two surface forms is a genuinely rich finding for conlang register-conditioned morphosyntax.
- **Particle position relative to the object — a clean contrast with English.** Swedish particle verbs place the object **after** the particle (*ringa upp honom* — lit. "ring up him"), the reverse of English's "ring him up." This is given as part of a fuller sentence-pattern table (Front–Verb1–Subject–Adverb–Verb2–Particle–Obj/Comp–MPT), extending the book's running sentence-pattern formalism to particle verbs specifically.
- **A substantial affixation reference — prefixes and suffixes catalogued by function**, more systematic than any prior unit's scattered derivational notes: negative prefixes *o-/in-/miss-/van-* ("not"/"badly, wrongly"); attitude prefixes *sam-/med-* ("together with") and *mot-* ("against"); time/place prefixes *efter-/gen-/ur-*; verb-forming *be-*/*för-* conversions. Noun-suffixes for people: *-are, -ande, -ende, -ant/-ent, -ing/-ist* (the last two specifically demonym/occupation-forming, cross-referencing the demonym system below); feminine-marking suffixes *-inna, -(er)ska, -essa* (a three-way feminine-suffix system worth comparing against other Germanic languages for conlang gendered-noun design); adjective-forming suffixes from verbs (*-bar, -lig, -ig, -abel*) and from nouns (*-enlig, -mässig, -aktig, -(i)sk*); verb-forming suffixes from nouns/adjectives (*-a, -era*, and the "become + adjective" suffixes *-na*, *-ja*).
- **Demonym and regional-dialect-name system, given as a worked typology with explicit exceptions flagged.** City-dwellers generally take *-are* (*stockholmare, göteborgare*) or, for smaller towns, *-bo* (*en Gävlebo*); province-dwellers take *-ling/-ing*-type suffixes (*värmlänning, smålänning, skåning*) — but Östergötland/Västergötland irregularly use *-göte* (*östgöte, västgöte*) rather than the expected pattern, and Dalarna is flagged as "difficult," taking wholly distinct **gender-differentiated** demonyms (*dalmas* masculine / *dalkulla* feminine) unlike every other region in the list, which are gender-neutral. Dialect names are derived from the corresponding adjective + -a (*skånsk→skånska*). This complete regional-identity-naming system — demonym formation strategies, their irregularities, and the parallel dialect-naming convention — is one of the most directly transferable pieces of sociolinguistic infrastructure in the whole book for a derived conlang's own regional/dialectal nomenclature.

### Grammar points

- **Three passive constructions, functionally distinguished — the single richest grammar point in this unit.** (1) **s-passive** (the most common type, uses the verb's s-form, cf. Unit 11): typically agentless, used for repeated/habitual actions (*Dörrarna öppnas kl 7* — "The doors are opened at seven") and characteristic of "objective newspaper style" (several examples straight from the wedding-announcement text: *Paret vigdes*, *Den byggdes 1729* — both agentless; but also *Psalmen framfördes av Johanna Forsberg* — agent included when relevant). (2) **bli-passive** (*bli* + past participle): typically expresses a single, one-off action and **frequently includes a significant agent** (*England blev slaget 2–3 av Sverige* — "England was beaten by Sweden"; *Han har blivit påkörd av en motorcykel*). (3) **vara-passive** (*vara* + past participle): expresses the **resulting state** of a prior action, purely descriptive (*Himlen är täckt av moln* — "The sky is covered in clouds"; *Huset är sålt* — "The house is [has been] sold"). This three-way split by aspect/agent-significance (habitual-agentless / single-event-with-agent / resultant-state) is one of the most structurally rich voice systems documented in the book and a strong candidate for direct adaptation into a derived conlang's own passive/voice morphology. Only transitive verbs (cf. Unit 15) can passivize.
- **Position of the passive agent**, given in the book's recurring sentence-pattern-table format: the agent phrase (*av + NP*) sits after VERB2 and before any MPT-expression, and — critically — **there is no object slot at all** in a passive clause (the erstwhile object has become the subject).
- **Independent clauses**: a sub-clause-form construction (keeping S-I-V/SIV order) that stands alone as a complete sentence rather than embedding in a main clause — typically exclamations (*Att jag kunde vara så dum!* — "That I could be so stupid!") or ellipsis-derived fragments, including typical answers to "why" questions (*(Jag har plåster på fingret) ... För att jag just har skurit mig* — the sub-clause survives as a free-standing answer with its subordinate-clause word order intact even though the governing main clause has been elided).
- **Male/female distinction — three formation strategies, with an explicit note on ongoing sociolinguistic change.** (a) Derivational suffix on the male form (*vän→väninna*, *aktör→aktris*, *massör→massös* — multiple distinct feminine suffixes: -inna, -ska, -is/-ös) — but the book explicitly flags that some such pairs are **falling out of use** in favor of a single gender-neutral form (*lärarinna → lärare*, *städerska → städare*) as job-title sexism declines; (b) the masculine/feminine -e/-a adjectival-noun ending (cf. Unit 11's *den gamle/den gamla*) extended to a handful of given-name pairs (*Helge/Helga*, *Tore/Tora* — with explicitly flagged exceptions, *Ola* is male despite the -a ending, *Signe* is female despite -e); (c) wholly distinct lexemes (*tupp/höna*, *hingst/sto*, *kung/drottning*). Separately, a small set of person-denoting nouns are grammatically neuter (*ett barn, ett statsråd, ett vittne, ett biträde, ett geni*) despite the general rule that person-nouns take en-gender — a clean, listable exception set.
- **Abbreviation typology, unusually thorough for a beginner's course**: clipping from the beginning (*(bi)cykel*), middle (*mo(torveloci)ped*), or end (*el(ektricitet)*) of a word; full initialisms (*LO, SAAB*); two acronym subtypes — alphabetisms pronounced letter-by-letter (*bh, UD*), sometimes further re-spelled as ordinary words (*bh→behå*, cf. the book's earlier *teve*=TV pattern), vs. acronyms pronounced as words (*NATO, SAAB, SAS*); and "hybrid forms" mixing a letter-abbreviation with a full word (*T-bana, u-båt, p-plats*). The book backs this typology with a long reference table of ~25 common written abbreviations (*AB, e Kr/f Kr, fm/em, osv, sk, tom, vgv*, etc.) — a ready-made written-register abbreviation inventory.
- **Punctuation differences from English**: no comma before an *att*-clause (*Han sade att han tänkte komma*) or after letter-opening/closing phrases (*Bäste herr Olsson!*); no apostrophe around genitive -s (*pojkens far*); the exclamation mark used more liberally than in English; the hyphen used to mark a shared/elided common element across coordinated compounds (*på låg- och mellanstadiet*); and a distinctive dash (*pratminus*, "talk-dash") used before direct-quotation dialogue lines in novels instead of quotation marks — a genuinely distinct typographic convention worth noting for any conlang-adjacent orthography/punctuation system modeled on Swedish conventions.

- **The parallel subordinate-clause positional template** (Context – Conjunction – Subject – Adverb – Finite verb – Non-finite verb – Object/Comp – Other), with three differences from the main-clause template made fully explicit in one place for the first time: (1) every slot except the conjunction itself matches the main-clause inventory, just reordered; (2) there is no "Front" slot at all in a sub-clause — order is invariably conjunction–subject–adverb–finite verb; (3) **the subject position is always occupied** in a sub-clause (contrasting with a main-clause yes/no question, which has an empty Front position and relies on verb-first order instead) — a crisp, previously-implicit-only generalization that no main clause type ever lacks an "empty subject slot" option the way a sub-clause's subject position is obligatorily filled.

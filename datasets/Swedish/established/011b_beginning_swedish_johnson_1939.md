Book Concern, 1939), 298 pp. HathiTrust scan (`mdp.39015001789851`), genuine OCR-derived text layer
(verified via `pdftotext`; no vision-reading needed). Pages covered in this file: Lesson XII through
Lesson XXII (printed pp. 71–157) — the verb-tense unit (weak verbs, strong verbs, irregular verbs,
tenses, imperative) and the second noun/pronoun unit (adjective declension, reflexives, comparison,
adverbs, possessives, demonstratives, determinatives, personal pronouns, interrogatives, *själv*,
*varandra*, the uses of *det*). Continues directly from `011a_beginning_swedish_johnson_1939.md`.

**Coverage note.** Every grammar point and distinct vocabulary item in this range is captured;
repeated drills/duplicate examples are skipped per `../../00_Reference_Extraction_Spec.md`. Two
English-language cultural readings fall in this range — "A Modern Ideal of Statehood" (after Lesson
XI) and "Craftsmanship — A Living Tradition" (after Lesson XV) and "Summer and Winter Resorts" (after
Lesson XIX) — all pure English-language prose essays with no Swedish-language content; noted for
completeness, not extracted. Review lessons (XV, XIX) introduce no new grammar or vocabulary and are
not separately sectioned below. Vision Reading Confidence is `n/a` throughout (real OCR text layer)
unless flagged otherwise per entry.

**PRE-REFORM SWEDISH — continued.** This section contains the single densest concentration of
pre-reform content in the whole book: §48 tabulates the **full literary-plural paradigm** across all
tenses for *att ha*, *att vara*, and a model weak verb, explicitly instructing "Do not use the literary
plural in conversation" — meaning the book is knowingly teaching a register already receding from
live use by 1939, as a reading/formal-writing competency. §46–48 also show that **strong verbs have a
dedicated literary past-plural stem**, distinct from both the singular and the colloquial plural
(e.g. *bar* [sg./coll. pl.] vs. literary plural *buro*, "carried"; *höll* vs. *höllo*, "held"; *flög*
vs. *flögo*, "flew") — a genuinely archaic verbal category modern Swedish has lost entirely, not just
an archaic pronoun or spelling. See the `archaic` tag applied throughout the Grammar points below.

## Grammar points

### Lesson XII — The weak verbs: indicative mood, active voice
**§40.** Verbs are **weak** (past tense by suffix: *-ade, -de, -te, -dde*) or **strong** (past tense
by stem-vowel change, Lesson XIII). Three weak conjugations: **I** *-ade* (*tala* → *talade*), **II**
*-de* or *-te* (*böja* → *böjde*; *köpa* → *köpte*), **III** *-dde* (*tro* → *trodde*). **§41. The
stem** = infinitive minus final *-a* (if the infinitive doesn't end in *-a*, stem = infinitive).
**§42. Principal parts**: infinitive, simple past, supine (+ past participle, Lesson XXIX). **§43.
First conjugation**: full synopsis *kallar/kallade/kommer att kalla/skall kalla/har kallat/hade
kallat/skall ha kallat*; colloquial plural = singular, **literary plural forms deferred to §48**;
long *ll* in *skall* silent in speech, often written *ska*. **§44. Second conjugation**: subtype
(a) *-te* if stem ends in *k, p, s, t* (else *-de*, with the irregular exception *begynna/begynte*);
subtype (b) stems ending in *-r* do NOT add *-er* in the present (*jag för, jag hör, jag kör*);
subtype (c) stems ending in *-d*: if preceded by a consonant, the *-d* is simply replaced (*använda*
→ *använde* → *använt*); if preceded by the stem vowel, it doubles to *-dd-/-tt-* (*betyda* →
*betydde* → *betytt*; *kläda* → *klädde* → *klätt*). **§45. Third conjugation** (infinitives not
ending in *-a*): *tror/trodde/...trott*.

### Lesson XIII — Strong verbs: indicative mood, active voice; emphatic word order
**§46. Principal parts of strong verbs**: infinitive, simple past singular (**= colloquial plural**),
**literary/formal past plural** (a dedicated, distinct stem — *bar/buro, satt/sutto, for/foro,
höll/höllo, flög/flögo, ljög/ljögo, skrev/skrevo*), supine, past participle. **This literary past
plural is itself archaic** — a fourth principal part modern Swedish has eliminated entirely, not
merely restricted in register. **§47. Synopses**: present adds *-er* to the infinitive-final-*-a*
stem (else just *-r*; dropped entirely if the stem itself ends in *-r*, e.g. *jag bär*). **§48.
Literary plurals — systematic table, archaic.** Full paradigm contrasting colloquial/formal plural
across every tense for *ha*, *vara*, *kalla*, *hålla*: e.g. present *vi har*/lit. *vi ha*; *vi
är*/lit. *vi äro* (irregular — not identical to the infinitive, unlike every other literary-plural
present); past *vi hade* (no special literary plural in the past perfect); simple future *vi kommer
att ha*/lit. *vi komma att ha(va)*; future of determination *vi skall ha*/lit. *vi skola ha*. **The
book explicitly instructs: "Do not use the literary plural in conversation."** Final unstressed *-o*
of the literary past plural of strong verbs is pronounced [ʊ]. **§49. The progressive in Swedish**:
no dedicated progressive/emphatic verb forms exist at all (*jag sitter* = "I sit / I am sitting / I do
sit", disambiguated only by context) — continuous action can instead be signaled by **verb
repetition** (*han läser och läser*, "he is always reading"), by ***hålla på att* + infinitive**
("keep on"), or by **pairing two finite verbs** (*gå, ligga, sitta, stå* + another verb: *han sitter
och läser*, "he is sitting reading"). **§50. Emphatic word order**: fronting the object, adjective,
adverb, phrase, quotation, or dependent clause triggers subject–verb inversion (*Det gjorde han*,
"That he did"; *I går gjorde jag det*, "Yesterday I did it") — the first systematic statement of a
rule generalized fully in Lesson XXVI (§90–93).

### Lesson XIV — Irregular verbs *att ha* and *att vara*; the tenses; the imperative mood
**§51. Nine common irregular verbs** given full paradigms: *glädja* (please — used impersonally,
*Det gläder mig*), *göra* (do/make), *heta* (be named), *leva* (live), *lägga* (lay), *säga* (say, with
colloquial-pronunciation variants *säja/säjer/säj* explicitly noted alongside the standard forms —
a lesson-internal register distinction layered on top of the book's broader colloquial/formal
system), *sälja* (sell), *sätta* (set), *veta* (know facts). Note (a): **their literary-plural
present indicative is identical to the infinitive** — the regular pattern, confirming §48's note that
*äro* (for *vara*) is the one true exception. **§52. Notes on tenses and the infinitive**: (a) simple
past used for admiration/surprise (*Det var roligt!*); (b) future expressed by the present tense of
transition-verbs, by the present tense of duration-verbs when time is specified, OR by *kommer att* +
infinitive (futurity without commitment) vs. *skall* (= *skola*, literary plural) for a command,
decision, intention, or promise; (c) future perfect is rare — present perfect usually substitutes;
(d) *att* (the infinitive marker) is **omitted** after modals and after *be(dja), bruka, börja, tänka,
tycka, tyckas, synas, ämna*; (e) *att* IS used after *komma*, after a preposition, in infinitive
phrases, and when the infinitive is a clause's subject; (f) *ha(r/de)* is often **omitted** in
dependent clauses, "particularly in written Swedish" — a third, independent archaic-register
ellipsis pattern (distinct from the literary plural and from *låtom oss*). **§53. Full synopses of
*att ha*/*att vara*** — *att ha* has the archaic full infinitive *hava* and an old present *jag
haver*, both "used only in very formal Swedish." **§54. The imperative mood**: 2nd-sg. imperative of
conjugation I = the infinitive itself; of II/III/strong = the bare stem (*kalla; sänd; köp; tro;
skriv*); 2nd-pl. imperative = identical to 2nd-sg.; 1st-pl. ("let's...") = ***låt oss* + infinitive**,
with the fully obsolete older form *låtom oss* explicitly flagged "no longer used colloquially" —
an archaism the book itself treats as already dead by 1939, distinct from the still-current literary
plural. Polite imperatives prefer *Var snäll och...* / *Var god och...* ("Please...").

### Lessons XVI — The declension of adjectives; the reflexives
**§55. The indefinite declension**: predicative adjectives are always indefinite; attributive
adjectives are indefinite when modifying an indefinite noun. Regular pattern: neuter = non-neuter +
*-t*; plural = non-neuter + *-a* (*snäll/snällt/snälla*). **§56. Peculiarities**: adjectives in
long-vowel-+*-d* take *-tt* in neuter (*god*→*gott*); adjectives ending in a stressed vowel add *-tt*
(*ny*→*nytt*); adjectives in consonant+*-d* simplify to *-t* (*svart*→*svart*, unchanged since already
ending in *t*… cf. *ond*→*ont*); adjectives already ending in consonant+*-t* are unchanged in neuter;
adjectives in unstressed *-en* substitute *-t* for *-n* in neuter and drop the *-e-* before plural
*-a* (*ledsen*→*ledset*→*ledsna*); some adjectives (*bra, stackars*) are wholly **indeclinable**.
**§57. The definite declension**: attributive adjective modifying a definite noun; usually
article+adjective+definite-noun, but many idioms omit one or both articles (*hela timmen, högra
sidan, följande morgon*); ends in *-e* for a masculine singular referent; adjectives in unstressed
*-al/-el/-en/-er* lose the vowel before the definite/plural ending (*gammal*→*gamla*; *ledsen*→*ledsne
(masc.)/ledsna*; *vacker*→*vackra*); a definite adjective can itself function as a noun (*den gamle*
"the old man," *den gamla* "the old woman," taking *-s* for the possessive); **liten is wholly
irregular**: *liten/litet* (indef.) → *lilla/lille* (def.) → *små* (plural, both genders) — i.e. the
plural stem is unrelated to the singular stem entirely. **§58. The reflexives**: *sig* (3rd person
only, invariable, refers back to the clause subject: *Johan tvättar sig*, "John washes (himself)");
1st/2nd person use the ordinary objective pronouns reflexively (*jag tvättar mig*, etc.); formal *er*
has the fully formal variant *eder*; *sig* also covers English "for himself/for themselves" (*Han
köpte sig en kostym*, "He bought a suit for himself").

### Lesson XVII — Comparison of adjectives
**§59.** Three forms — positive/comparative/superlative — most regularly by **+*-are*/+*-ast***
(*vänlig/vänligare/vänligast*); adjectives in unstressed *-el/-en/-er* drop the *-e-* first
(*vacker/vackrare/vackrast*); the comparative is **invariable** (no gender/number agreement) and can
itself be nominalized and take *-s* (*den äldres bok*); the **superlative is used even for just two**
compared items (*den kortaste av de två läxorna*, "the shorter of the two"). **§60. *mer(a)*/*mest*
periphrastic comparison** required for adjectives ending in *-ad, -e, -isk, -s, -ande*, and past
participles. **§61. Irregular comparison** — a closed list memorized as a table: *bra→bättre→bäst*;
*dålig→sämre→sämst*; *gammal→äldre→äldst*; *hög→högre→högst*; *liten→mindre→minst*; *låg→lägre→lägst*;
*lång→längre→längst*; *mången→flera→flesta*; *nära→närmare→närmast*; *ond→värre→värst*;
*stor→större→störst*; *tung→tyngre→tyngst*; *ung→yngre→yngst** — these irregular comparatives take the
**acute** accent (regular *-are* comparatives take the **grave**). **§62. Comparative expression
patterns**: *lika ... som* (as ... as); *mindre ... än* (less ... than); *den allra vackraste* (the
very prettiest); the correlative ***ju ... dess ...* / *ju ... ju ...*** ("the... the...": *Ju rikare
man är, ju bättre*). **§63. The declined superlative**: a superlative in *-ast* adds final *-e* when
preceded by *den/det/de*/a possessive/demonstrative; one in *-st* adds final *-a* under the same
condition (unless the referent is a singular masculine being, which instead takes *-e*); as a noun,
the superlative takes *-s* for possession (*de fattigastes bästa*).

### Lesson XVIII — Adverbs; the possessives
**§64. Adverb classes**: simple (*nu, ofta*); derived from the neuter adjective form (*flitigt*
"diligently" — identical in form to the neuter adjective but functionally distinct); **compound
literary adverbs** built from *där/här/var* + preposition (*däri, härmed, varav*) — "primarily
literary, formal," with colloquial substitutes preferred in speech (*i det* for *däri*, etc.); and
**archaic survivals from Old Swedish**: fossilized adverbial possessives after *i* and *till* — *i
våras, i somras, i höstas, i vintras* ("last spring/summer/autumn/winter"), *i måndags* ("last
Monday"), *i morse* ("this morning"), *till sängs* ("to bed"), *till bords* ("at [to] the table") —
explicitly labeled by the book itself as "survival from Old Swedish," i.e. identified as a genuine
historical fossil embedded in otherwise-live colloquial speech, distinct from the literary-plural
register split. Word order: the adverb modifying the verb normally follows the finite verb (and
follows a pronominal object if present); in dependent clauses, negation/sentence-modifying/indefinite-
time adverbs go **between the subject and the finite verb** (anticipating §92). **§65. Adverbs of
place**: a systematic **rest vs. motion** pairing — *hemma/hem* (at home/homeward), *borta/bort*
(away, at rest/in motion), *här/hit* (here, at rest/to here), *där/dit* (there/to there),
*nere/ner(d)* (down), *uppe/upp* (up), *inne/in* (in), *ute/ut* (out), *framme/fram* (arrived/forward)
— a "place where" vs. "place to which" distinction with no single-word English analog. **§66.
Sentence-modifying adverbs** (modal particles): *allt* (surely), *då* (certainly), *förstås* (of
course), *ju* (as you know), *nog* (undoubtedly), *visst* (indeed), *väl* (I suppose) — this closed
set of "flavoring particles" with no direct English translation is a hallmark of Scandinavian syntax.
**§67. Comparison of adverbs**: an adverb identical to the neuter adjective compares exactly like
that adjective; a closed set of irregular adverb comparatives (*gärna→hellre→helst*; *illa→värre→värst*;
*gott/bra/väl→bättre→bäst*; *nära→närmare→närmast*; *ofta→oftare→oftast*; *högt→högre→högst*).
**§68. The possessive adjectives/pronouns**: full paradigm — *min/mitt/mina* (my), *din/ditt/dina*
(intimate your), *er/ert/era* (formal/plural your — with the fully formal variant *Eder/Edert/Edra*
used in correspondence), *hans* (his), *hennes* (her), *dess* (its), *vår/vårt/våra* (our),
*deras* (their). **Crucially, Swedish has a dedicated *reflexive* 3rd-person possessive** *sin/sitt/
sina*, used only when the possessor is the subject of its own clause (*Mannen fann sina saker* = "The
man found his OWN things" vs. *Mannen fann hans saker* = "The man found his [someone else's]
things") — a grammatical distinction English simply does not mark. Swedish also frequently uses the
bare **definite noun** instead of possessive-adjective+indefinite-noun for body parts/clothing (*Han
tog hatten*, "He took HIS hat").

### Lesson XX — The demonstratives; the determinatives; the personal pronouns
**§69. Demonstrative system**: *denna/denne(masc.)/detta/dessa* ("this/these," formal, followed by
the noun's definite form) vs. colloquial *den här/det här/de här*; *den/det/de* + *där* ("that/those,"
colloquial); *den förre/förra/senare* ("the former/the latter"); *sådan* ("such a," coll. *sån*);
*samma/densamma* ("the same"). Every demonstrative except *denna, sådan,* and *samma* is followed by
the noun's **definite** form (double-marking, like the definite-article-plus-adjective pattern from
§31). **§70. Special neuter-singular *det*-usage**: *det, detta, det här, det där* can refer to a
predicate noun irrespective of its actual gender/number when "the demonstrative idea is not very
pronounced," translatable as it/they/this/these/that/those depending on context — vs. a genuinely
gender-marked demonstrative (*Den där vill jag ha*) when real contrastive pointing is intended. **§71.
Determinatives**: a demonstrative pointing forward into a following relative/infinitive clause (*Den,
som arbetar flitigast, får priset* = "The one who works hardest gets the prize") — a plural noun after
a determinative does NOT take the postpositive definite article. **§72. Personal pronouns, objective
and dative**: *mig/dig/er/honom/henne/den/det/oss/er/dem* — formal *eder* for *er*; *den* refers to a
non-neuter antecedent, *det* to a neuter one.

### Lesson XXI — The interrogatives; *själv*; *varandra*; the uses of *det*
**§73. Interrogative system**: one-person *vem/vems* (who/whose); one-of-several *vilken/vilket/
vilka* (which — possessive adds *-s*); one-thing *vad* (what, invariable as adjective); *vad för
en/ett/ena* (what kind of — "för en" can be separated from *vad*); *vad slags* (invariable, "what
kind of"); two-persons-or-things *vilkendera/vilketdera* (colloquial substitute: *vilken/vilket/vilka
av dem*); *hurudan* (formal) / *hurdan* (colloquial) "what kind of, how." *Som* is inserted after an
interrogative pronoun serving as a dependent clause's subject. **§74. The emphasizing *själv*** (myself,
yourself, etc. — NOT the reflexive *sig*): *själv/självt/själva*, agreeing with its referent — *Jag
gjorde det själv* (I did it myself). **§75. The reciprocal pronoun *varandra*** (each other, one
another), possessive *varandras*. **§76. The many functions of *det*** — pronoun of reference to a
singular neuter noun; prepositive definite article; demonstrative; **subject of impersonal verbs**,
especially weather/emotion verbs (*Det regnade*, "it rained"; *Det gläder mig*, "I am glad/that
pleases me"; *Det gör ont i foten*, "my foot hurts"); subject of *att vara*/*att finnas* as
"there is/are" (*Det är* for a specific person/time/place; *Det finns* when neither is emphasized);
subject before a predicate noun regardless of its gender/number (*Vem är det? Det är studenter*);
equivalent of English "so" (*Han är trött. Det är jag också* = "He is tired. So am I"); anaphoric
reference to a whole preceding clause.

### Lesson XXII — The relative adjectives and pronouns; indefinite interrogative pronouns; the cardinal numbers
**§77. Relatives**: ***som*** (invariable, dominant in BOTH colloquial and formal Swedish — a
preposition governing a relative clause goes at the clause's END, never before *som*; possessive
*vilkens* or, in literary Swedish, *vars*); ***vilken/vilket/vilka*** (the only relative that can be an
adjective; primarily written Swedish; CAN take a preceding preposition; *vilket* used when the
antecedent is a whole clause); ***vad*** (used after *allt* and instead of *det som* — when *vad* is
itself a clause's subject, it's usually followed by *som*). A relative is frequently omitted
altogether, as in English. **§78. Indefinite interrogative pronouns**: *vem/vad/vilken/när/hur/var*
etc. + ***som helst*** = "...at all/...ever" (*vem som helst*, anyone at all; *var som helst*,
anywhere at all); *vem/vad/vilken* + ***än*** = "whoever/whatever" (concessive). **§79. The
cardinals**: full system to 100 reviewed and extended (tjugoett...tjugonio, tretti(o)...nitti(o));
compound numerals have **no equivalent of "and"** between tens and units (*tvåhundratio* = two hundred
ten); *ett* is often dropped before *hundra*/*tusen*; *båda*/*bägge* ("both") may precede *två* for
emphasis; street addresses put the number AFTER the (definite-form) street name: *Vasagatan 5*.
**§80. Derivatives from the cardinals**: *tal* added to a cardinal names a decade/century (*tjugotalet*
= the 20's; *1900-talet* = the 1900's); *tal*/*tals* added gives an approximate/estimated count with
**no preposition** (*ett tiotal elever* = about ten pupils; *hundratals byggnader* = hundreds of
buildings); the cardinals 0–12 form 1st-declension nouns (*en etta, en tvåa*...*en tolva*). **§81. The
absence of "of"**: quantity expressions (*ett dussin ägg*, a dozen eggs; *ett par femmor*, a couple of
fives) and apposition with geographic generics (*staden Stockholm*, "the city of Stockholm";
*konungariket Sverige*, "the kingdom of Sweden") take **no preposition** where English requires "of".

# Swedish — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: *Beginning Swedish* (Johnson, 1939) — Part A: Lessons I–XI

**Source:** G. Johnson, *Beginning Swedish* (University of Illinois; Rock Island, Ill.: Augustana
Book Concern, 1939), 298 pp. HathiTrust scan (`mdp.39015001789851`) with a genuine OCR-derived text
layer (verified via `pdftotext`, no vision-reading needed). Pages covered in this file: front matter
(Preface, To the Student, alphabet) through Lesson XI (printed pp. iii–65), i.e. the pronunciation
unit (Lessons I–VI) and the first grammar unit (Lessons VII–XI: gender, articles, pronouns of
reference, present tense of *ha(va)*/*vara*, plural nouns, declensions I–V, the possessive case, the
positive adjective).

**Coverage note.** Every grammar point and every distinct vocabulary item introduced in this range is
captured below; repeated drill sentences and duplicate example sentences that introduce no new
vocabulary or grammar are skipped per `../../00_Reference_Extraction_Spec.md`. Pronunciation-drill
words in Lessons I–IV (used only to illustrate individual sounds, not boxed as "VOCABULARY" by the
book itself) are treated as phonetic illustrations, not vocabulary-table entries — this matches the
book's own pedagogical structure, which does not begin boxed "VOCABULARY" lists until Lesson V
(classroom expressions) / Lesson VII (first full vocabulary box). The short historical/cultural
reading "A Thousand Years and More" (end of Lesson VI, pp. 30–33) is a prose essay on Swedish history
with no boxed vocabulary or grammar apparatus; it is noted here for completeness but not extracted
line-by-line (no linguistic content distinct from ordinary written Swedish-via-English exposition —
the essay itself is in English). This source has a real OCR text layer throughout (`pdftotext -layout`
produced usable text); no vision-reading was required, so Vision Reading Confidence is `n/a`
throughout unless a specific entry is flagged otherwise for OCR garbling.

**PRE-REFORM SWEDISH — flagged throughout.** This 1939 textbook predates Sweden's 1945–50
*stavnings- och formreformen* and consistently teaches, as its own contemporary standard, the
pre-reform distinction between **colloquial** verb forms (identical to the singular) and **literary
plural** verb forms (a separate plural conjugation used in formal/literary registers) — e.g. colloquial
*vi går* / literary *vi gå* (we go); colloquial *vi är* / literary *vi äro* (we are); colloquial *vi har*
/ literary *vi ha(va)* (we have); colloquial *vi gick* / literary *vi gingo* (we went). Section 21 (Lesson
V) introduces this explicitly as "Colloquial and Formal Swedish" and gives it as the single most
systematic grammar point in the early lessons. Every later lesson's drills explicitly ask students to
convert between the colloquial and literary-plural forms. This is flagged per-occurrence below in the
Notes column and is this source's single most valuable contribution relative to the project's other,
post-reform Swedish sources (*Colloquial Swedish*, *Essential Grammar*, etc.), none of which teach the
literary plural as live, contemporary-standard material. See also the `archaic` Usage Tier tag used
throughout.

**Morpheme breakdown note.** Swedish in this period is mildly fusional/agglutinative in its noun
morphology (stem + plural-class suffix + postpositive definite-article suffix, stackable: e.g.
*flick-or-na* = flicka-PL-DEF, "the girls"). Individual entries above are glossed atomically because
the book itself teaches the five noun declensions as whole paradigms (§29–38) rather than as
freely-recombining morphemes; the paradigm tables under Grammar points below make the actual
morpheme boundaries explicit (stem, plural marker, definite-article suffix) for each declension class,
which is the more faithful representation of the compositional structure here than per-word tagging
would be.

## Grammar points

### Lesson I — Pronunciation; the Swedish alphabet; a phonetic alphabet for Swedish
**§1–2.** The 29-letter Swedish alphabet (Å, Ä, Ö follow Z) with letter-names distinct from English
(e.g., *H* = "hå", *J* = "ji", *W* = "dubbelt ve"). **§3.** The book's own IPA-style phonetic alphabet
for consonants that resemble English fairly closely ([l], [m], [n], [ŋ], [p], [r], [s], [t], [ʉ], [v]),
flagging in advance the ones that need special treatment later (the retroflex/supradental series,
the *sje*- and *tje*-sounds).

### Lesson II — Quantity; stress; intonation
**§4–5.** Vowel and consonant **quantity** (length) is phonemic in Swedish: a stressed syllable has
either a long vowel + short consonant, or a short vowel + long consonant, never both long or both
short. Primary stress (grave accent mark) regularly falls with a secondary stress on a later syllable
in certain compounds and in words with the suffixes *-aktig, -bar, -dom, -faldig, -het, -sam, -skap*.
**§6. Musical accent (intonation) — the flagship Swedish/Norwegian feature.** Two types: **Type I /
Acute** (single falling tone, like English stress-accent) occurs in monosyllables, most words ending
in *-el/-en/-er*, nouns with postpositive definite article in monosyllables, *-er* present-indicative
verb forms, comparatives in *-re*, day-names, and most loanwords. **Type II / Grave** (double-tone:
low-rising-falling, found only in polysyllables) is the pattern most foreign learners find hardest;
minimal pairs exist that are segmentally identical and differ only in accent type (*anden* "the duck"
[Type I] vs. *anden* "the spirit/breath" [Type II]; *slutet* "the end" [I] vs. *slutet* "closed" [II]).
**§7.** In connected speech, unstressed words (articles, auxiliaries, conjunctions, prepositions,
pronouns) lose their citation-form accent entirely; only emphasized words keep acute/grave character.

### Lesson III — The vowels; the diphthongs
**§8.** Vowel articulation chart; most Swedish vowels are rounded (*y, ö, u, å, o,* long *o*) vs.
unrounded (short *a, i, e, ä*). **§9. Soft vs. hard vowels** — a classification that matters because
it conditions consonant pronunciation: **soft** = *i, y, e, ä, ö*; **hard** = *a, å, o, u*. (This
directly feeds Lesson IV's *g/k/sk*-palatalization rule.) **§10. Umlauted vowels**: *ä* from *a*, *ö*
from *o*, *y* from *u* in certain plurals (*man* → *män*, "man/men"), paralleling English
umlaut-plurals like *foot/feet*. **§11. Diphthongs**: only two true diphthongs, *au* and *eu*; a vowel
+ *j* forms a "half-diphthong."

### Lesson IV — The consonants
**§12–13.** Full consonant inventory; three sounds with no dedicated letter: the **äng-sound** [ŋ]
(spelled *ng*, or *g* before *n*, or *n* before *k*/another consonant), the **sje-sound** [ʃ] (spelled
*sj, sk-+soft vowel, skj, stj, -ti-,* etc.; in **southern Sweden** realized like English *wh* in
*when* — an explicit regional/dialectal annotation), and the **tje-sound** [ɕ] (spelled *tj, kj,* or
*k*-+stressed front vowel). **§14. The soft-vowel palatalization rule**: *g, k, sk* are pronounced
[j], [ɕ], [ʃ] respectively before a stressed soft vowel (*i, e, y, ä, ö*) — directly built on the
soft/hard vowel classification from §9. *g* is also [j] after *l/r* at a syllable's end and in
*Sverige*. **§15–16. Dentals vs. supradentals (retroflexes)**: Standard-Swedish speakers who use a
tongue-tip *r* produce a **retroflex/supradental assimilation** when *r* precedes *d, l, n, s, t*
(spelled *rd, rl, rn, rs, rt*), merging the two sounds into a single "thick" consonant with the *r*
no longer audible (*bord* [bo:d] "table" vs. *bod* [bo:d] "store" — a genuine near-merger the book
flags explicitly). **Speakers who use the southern/uvular r do NOT make this assimilation** — another
explicit regional/dialectal annotation, with the footnote quoting a Swedish dialectologist (Gjerdman)
on how overcorrecting this (e.g. saying "bort" as "bårt") is perceived as "childish" register vs. the
natural educated pronunciation. **§17. C** = [s] before a soft vowel, [k] before a hard
vowel/consonant (formal pronunciation of *och* also uses this [k] value; colloquially *och* reduces to
[ɔ]), [ʃ] before *h* (except in *och*). **§18. Unvoicing**: *b, d, g, v* → [p, t, k, f] before *s/t*.
**§19. Long m/n spelling quirks**: not always doubled in writing even when phonemically long. **§20.
Silent consonants**: initial *d, g, h, l* silent before *j*; *g* silent in *morgon* and in adjectival
*-ig(t)* colloquially; *l* silent in *värld, karl*; *d* silent in *trädgård, äldst*.

### Lesson V — Classroom expressions; colloquial and formal Swedish; punctuation; capitalization; syllabication
**§21. Colloquial vs. Formal Swedish — the chapter's flagship point, and the single most archaic
grammar feature in the whole book.** Two broad registers: **colloquial** (educated conversational
speech, informal speeches, literary dialogue, friendly letters) vs. **formal/literary** (newspapers,
textbooks, business letters, sermons, legal documents, literary prose) — "much more conservative...
in the retention of old grammatical forms." The showcase example is the **literary plural verb**
paradigm vs. the **colloquial plural** (identical to the singular): colloquial *vi går / vi gick / vi
kommer att gå / vi ska(ll) gå / vi har gått / vi ska(ll) ha gått* vs. literary *vi gå / vi gingo / vi
komma att gå / vi skola gå / vi ha(va) gått / vi skola ha(va) gått* ("we go/went/shall go/will
go/have gone/shall have gone"). Register also affects **diction** (formal *blott* vs. colloquial
*bara*, "only"; formal *fader/moder/broder* vs. colloquial *far/mor/bror*) and **pronunciation**
(formal [ɔk:] vs. colloquial [å:] for *och*; formal [säg:ar] vs. colloquial [säj:ar] for *säger*;
formal [sa:da] vs. colloquial [sa:] for *sade*; formal [va:d] vs. colloquial [va:] for *vad*; formal
[de:] vs. colloquial [di:] for *de*; formal [däm:] vs. colloquial [dam:] for *dem*). **§22.
Punctuation**: commas before virtually every subordinate/coordinate clause (unlike English); colon
before a direct quotation, comma if the quotation comes first; period omitted after abbreviations like
*hr, dr*. **§23. Capitalization**: capitalize sentence-initial words, proper names, direct-address
titles, names of the Deity, the first element of compound proper names (*Förenta staterna*), and —
genuinely archaic — **I** (an old 2nd-person-plural "you," still listed as usable in correspondence
alongside *Ni, Eder/Er*). Do NOT capitalize day/holiday/month names or nationality words/adjectives
(*svenskar, amerikanska*) — opposite of English convention. **§24. Syllabication**: compounds split
at their component boundaries; simple words split consonant-then-vowel; consonant clusters representing
the sje-sound or the äng-sound follow special rules.

### Lesson VII — Gender and the indefinite articles; pronouns of reference; present tense of *att ha(va)* and *att vara*
**§25. Gender and the indefinite articles.** Functionally two genders: **neuter** (article *ett*) and
**non-neuter** (article *en*) — always learned with the noun, since the article is the primary cue to
gender. The indefinite article is **omitted** before a predicate noun of profession, vocation,
relationship, or nationality (*Gustav är svensk*, "Gustav is a Swede") unless that noun is modified by
an adjective (*Fröken Lind är en snäll lärarinna*, "Miss Lind is a kind teacher") — and omitted in a
handful of set expressions (*Jag har fått svar*, "I have received an answer"). **§26. Pronouns of
reference**: *han/honom* (he/him, masculine beings), *hon/henne* (she/her, feminine beings), *den*
(it, non-neuter singular), *det* (it, neuter singular — [dä:t], coll. [dä:]/[de:]), *de/dem*
(they/them, any plural; formal [de:]/[däm:] vs. colloquial [di:]/[dam:]). **§27. Present indicative of
*att ha(va)*** ("to have"): *jag/du/ni/han/hon/den/det har*; **literary plural** *vi/ni/de ha(va)*.
Note (a): **Swedish has no progressive or emphatic verb forms** — *han har* covers "he has," "he is
having," AND "he does have," disambiguated only by context (this single-form-covers-three-meanings
point recurs for every verb and is formalized again in §49/§98a). Note (c): **du vs. ni register** —
*du* (intimate "you," used for people addressed by first name; children address elders as "mamma,
pappa, tant" rather than with a pronoun) vs. *ni* (formal "you," but many Swedes consider singular
*ni* impolite and avoid it by substituting the person's title, name, third-person pronoun, or a
general term like *min herre*/*fröken*/*min fru* — all translated simply as "you" in English).
**§28. Present indicative of *att vara*** ("to be"): uniform *är* for all persons singular and
colloquial plural; **literary plural** *äro*.

### Lesson VIII — The plural noun; the definite articles
**§29. The plural noun**: four main indefinite-plural endings — **1st declension** *-or* (*flicka* →
*flickor*), **2nd declension** *-ar* (*gosse* → *gossar*), **3rd declension** *-er* (*park* →
*parker*), **4th declension** *-n* (*rike* → *riken*) — plus a **5th declension** where indefinite
singular = indefinite plural (*hus* → *hus*, like English "sheep"; *lärare* → *lärare*). **§30. The
postpositive definite article** (Swedish's hallmark "suffixed the"): *-n* after a non-neuter noun
ending in a vowel (*gosse* → *gossen*); *-en* after a non-neuter noun ending in a consonant (*bänk* →
*bänken*); *-t* after a neuter noun ending in a vowel (*hjärta* → *hjärtat*); *-et* after a neuter
noun ending in a consonant (*hus* → *huset*); *-na* after most *-r*-plurals (*systrar* →
*systrarna*); *-a* after an *-n*-plural (*hjärtan* → *hjärtana*); *-en* (occasionally *-ena*) after
5th-declension plurals (*hus* → *husen*). Sub-rules: nouns in *-el/-er* drop the *-e* before adding
*-n* in the def. singular, then take the regular ending in the plural (*fågel* → *fågeln*, pl.
*fåglar* → *fåglarna*); nouns in *-or* (*doktor, professor*) add *-n* in the def. singular but keep the
*-o* in the plural, **with a stress shift** (*doktor* [ˈdɔktar] → *doktorer* [dɔkˈtoːrar]); neuter
nouns in *-el/-er* drop the *-e* in BOTH definite forms (*fönster* → def. sg. *fönstret*, pl.
*fönster*, def. pl. *fönstren*). **§31. The prepositive definite article** (*den*/*det*/*de*, used
IN ADDITION to the postpositive suffix whenever an adjective precedes the noun: *den rika kvinnan*,
"the rich woman") — omitted in set phrases like *hela året* ("the whole year"), *första lärän* ("the
first lesson"), *själva kungen* ("the king himself"), and proper-name phrases like *Svenska akademien*.

### Lesson IX — The first and second declensions; the possessive case
**§32. First declension** (*-or* plural): almost all non-neuter nouns ending in *-a* (dropped before
adding the plural ending); nearly all have the **grave accent** in every form; *svenska* has the
irregular def. form *svenskan*. **§33. Second declension** (*-ar* plural): final unstressed *-e* is
dropped before the plural ending (*gosse* → *gossar*); several have **umlauted plural vowels**
(*dotter* → *dottrar*, *moder* → *mödrar* — note *moder* also has the colloquial contracted singular
*mor*); *mormor/farmor* have the irregular definite forms *mormodern/farmodern*; *fröken* declines
irregularly *fröken, fröknar, fröknarna*; plural forms take the **grave accent** even when the
monosyllabic singular forms are acute. **§34. The possessive case.** Swedish nouns have four
functional cases (nominative, objective, dative, possessive), but only the possessive has a dedicated
inflectional ending: **add *-s*** (no apostrophe) if the noun doesn't already end in *-s/-x/-z* — and
this *-s*-possessive is used for BOTH animate and inanimate possessors, unlike English, which usually
switches to an "of"-phrase for inanimate possessors. **Add only an apostrophe** if the noun already
ends in *-s/-x/-z* (*Anders' hår*). **Retain the possessive ending of the original language** for
Latin-derived words (*Kristi liv*, "the life of Christ"; *musei byggnader*, "the museum's buildings").
A proper noun ending in a vowel generally adds no *-s* at all. Phrasal alternatives (*taket på huset*,
*dottern till herr Olsson*) are also idiomatic.

### Lesson X — The third, fourth, and fifth declensions; the positive adjective
**§35. Third declension** (*-er* plural): several nouns have **umlauted plurals** (*bok* → *böcker*,
*fot* → *fötter*, *hand* → *händer*, *stad* → *städer*, *tand* → *tänder*, *natt* → *nätter*, *strand*
→ *stränder*, *son* → *söner*, *bonde* → *bönder*, *land* → *länder*) — note the irregular stress shift
in *bonde/bönder* and *son/söner*. **§36. Fourth declension** (*-n* plural): only a small set of
neuter nouns ending in a vowel; *öga* and *öra* are partly irregular (*ögat, ögon, ögonen*; *örat,
öron, öronen*), as is the plural-only noun *syskon* ("siblings," def. *syskonen*). **§37. Fifth
declension** (indefinite sg. = indefinite pl.): the *-are* ending drops its *-e* before the definite
suffix (*lärare* → *lärarna*); several words (*hem, man, rum*) have an unwritten long final
consonant; *broder/fader/man* have umlauted plurals (*bröder, fäder, män*) and *broder/fader* have the
contracted colloquial singulars *bror/far*. **§38. Irregularities of declension**: month names have
no plural; *examen* → pl. *examina*; *hustru* ("wife") → *hustrun, hustrur, hustrurna*. **§39.
Introduction to the positive adjective**: most Swedish adjectives have three forms — non-neuter
indefinite, neuter indefinite (add *-t*), and a combined definite+plural form (add *-a*, or *-e* if
the definite singular noun denotes a masculine being): *stor, stort, stora/store* ("big"). The
adjective agrees in gender/number with its noun; this three-form system is the foundation for the full
adjective-declension system developed in Lesson XVI (§55–58).

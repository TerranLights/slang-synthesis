**Morpheme breakdown.**

> **fegis** = feg (adj. "cowardly") + -is (colloquial noun-forming suffix) — the same productive suffix
> seen in other everyday Swedish colloquialisms (e.g. "kompis" "buddy/friend" from "kompanjon"/"kompis",
> "läskis" "soda" from "läsk"); turns an adjective or clipped noun into a casual, often slightly
> diminutive/affectionate noun. Worth tracking across future Swedish sources as a reusable slang-formation
> mechanism (`morphological_play`, drawing from `core` adjective roots).

## Grammar points

### Dash-introduced direct speech (no quotation marks)
Dialogue throughout is set off with an initial en-dash ("– ") rather than quotation marks, the standard
convention in Swedish (and broader Scandinavian) printed prose: e.g. "– Pappa och mamma! Vakna!" Each new
speaker's turn starts a new dash-prefixed line; narration interleaves directly between turns without any
other punctuation marking the speech boundary. Noted here because it's a formatting/register convention
distinct from English-style quotation marks, relevant to how dialogue-heavy slang/register material should
be parsed out of Swedish source text generally (p. 8, 10, 12, 14, 16, 18, 20 — i.e. essentially every page
with dialogue).

### "din + noun" as a vocative insult frame
"din fegis!" (p. 16) uses the possessive pronoun "din" ("your", masculine/common-gender form) directly
before a noun as a vocative insult address, with no verb — a frozen construction ("din idiot!", "din
dumbom!", etc. are stock variants outside this source) rather than a literal possessive claim. The pattern
appears productive: any mildly insulting noun can presumably slot in. Flagged for future `analysis/`
mechanism work on `taboo_inversion`/`register_inversion`-type slang formation, since this is exactly the
kind of frame later synthesized slang could plausibly repurpose.

### Simple past-tense narrative register with light free indirect speech
The book's prose is uniformly short declarative past-tense sentences appropriate to a young-reader
adaptation (e.g. "Mumintrollet hade aldrig sett snö förut," p. 10), occasionally slipping into the
character's own exclamatory voice without a reporting verb (the "Hoppsan, vad var nu detta?" aside, p. 6,
reads as the narrator briefly adopting a child's own surprised register) — a light free-indirect-speech
technique common in children's-book narration. No complex subordination or literary tense usage (no
pluperfect, no subjunctive-adjacent forms) appears anywhere in the book, consistent with its target
age-3+ readership.

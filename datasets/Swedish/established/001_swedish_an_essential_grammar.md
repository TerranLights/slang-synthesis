# Swedish — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Swedish: An Essential Grammar (complete)

**Source:** Philip Holmes & Ian Hinchliffe, *Swedish: An Essential Grammar*, 2nd edition (Routledge
Essential Grammars, 2008; 1st ed. 1997), pp. 1-231 — the full book: Introduction, Chapters 1-16
(Pronunciation; Stress and accent; Nouns; Adjectives; Pronouns; Numerals; Verbs; Adverbs;
Interjections; Prepositions; Conjunctions; Word order and sentence structure; Word formation;
Orthography; Punctuation; Written and spoken Swedish), plus the "Linguistic terms" glossary.

**Coverage note — a whole-book single-file extraction, with an explicit scoping decision.** This
dispatch covers an entire 231-page reference grammar in one file (per the orchestrator's explicit
instruction for this wave — one file per source, not the usual per-chapter chunking). Every grammar
rule and every chapter's content has been read and is represented below. For vocabulary, two
different standards apply depending on chapter density:

- **Most chapters** (Nouns, Adjectives, Pronouns, Numerals, Adverbs, Interjections, Prepositions,
  Conjunctions, Word formation, Written/spoken Swedish): essentially every distinct example word/
  phrase the source glosses is tabulated, matching this project's normal per-chunk standard.
- **Chapter 7 (Verbs)** is the one deliberate exception: the source tabulates ~90 individual strong
  verbs across its gradation-series paradigm charts (7.2.8-7.2.13), which would be unreadable as
  ~90 near-identical table rows. A representative, high-frequency subset (~25 verbs covering every
  gradation series and the irregular/residual classes) is tabulated individually; the remaining
  verbs are listed in full, by gradation series, in the Grammar points prose instead — so no verb is
  dropped from the file, but most appear in prose list form rather than as their own table row. This
  is flagged explicitly here per this project's "state what was skipped and why" convention.

**This source has a real text layer** (`pdftotext -layout`); no vision-reading was required. Special
IPA/typographic symbols (stress marks, tone-contour arrows, bracketed boxes in the pronunciation
diagrams) render imperfectly in plain-text extraction — these are described in prose rather than
reproduced as corrupted glyphs.

**Register-marked content is extensive and flagged throughout** — this is a modern descriptive
grammar (not a historical one) that explicitly documents colloquial, spoken-only, and slang-adjacent
forms as a matter of course (Chapter 1's written-vs-spoken sound reductions, Chapter 9's expletives,
Chapter 13's colloquial clippings/hypocorisms, Chapter 16's dedicated written-vs-spoken register
chapter). These are the highest-value findings in this source for the project's purposes and are
called out individually below, not just in a single summary note.

## Grammar points

### Introduction — Three pillars of Swedish for English speakers (p.1-4)

The authors identify pronunciation, stress, and word order as the three areas that most determine
whether a learner's Swedish sounds right, ahead of morphology/vocabulary. Swedish is highly phonetic
(letter-to-sound correspondence is far more regular than English) except in foreign loanwords. Ten
major structural similarities to English are catalogued (shared core Germanic vocabulary; English
loanwords; two-case noun system; subject/object pronoun distinction; adjective/adverb distinction;
inflectional and periphrastic adjective comparison; weak-verb dental past tense; strong-verb vowel-
change past tense; shared modal auxiliaries; shared tense usage patterns; shared formal-subject
`det`/"it, there" construction; shared inverted word order for questions; pre-only attributive
adjective position) — establishing a baseline of what does NOT need to be taught before the book
moves to genuine differences.

### Chapter 1 — Vowel and consonant system (p.5-22)

Nine vowel letters represent 21 distinct vowel sounds, split into a stressed/unstressed contrast
(stressed vowels can be long or short; unstressed vowels are always short) and a back/front
classification (A, Å, O / U, E, Ä / I, Y, Ö). Vowel length is governed by syllable structure: open
syllables and syllables closed by one consonant have long stressed vowels; syllables closed by two+
consonants have short ones — with explicit exceptions for `-rn/-ln/-rl/-rd` (counted as one
consonant sound, long vowel preserved) and `-x` (counts as two sounds `[ks]`, vowel stays short
despite looking like one letter). Consonant `g-/k-/sk-` undergo a three-way split depending on the
following vowel: "soft" (palatalized, before `e/i/y/ä/ö`), "sje-ljud" (the `sj`-sound, itself split
into a "back" and "front" variant whose distribution "varies in dialect and idiolect"), and "hard"
(unchanged, before `o/u/a/å`) — with a list of loanword exceptions to all three patterns. `rs/rd/rt/
rn/rl` clusters produce distinctive retroflex sounds in Central/Northern Swedish.

### Chapter 1 — Spoken-Swedish sound reduction system (p.15-22)

This is the chapter's single densest grammar-point cluster and the most register-relevant content
in the whole book. The source catalogues, as a systematic phenomenon (not a list of isolated
exceptions), which sounds are regularly dropped or altered in casual speech: final `-g` of `-ig`/
`-lig` adjectives (near-universal); final `-d`/`-g`/`-t` of many common monosyllables, sometimes
even surviving into the plural/definite forms (`god/goda` → [g:]/[g:a]); medial `-d-`/`-g-` in
specific lexicalized words (`morgon`, `trädgård`); vowel devoicing before a suffixed `-s` (`i Guds
namn` → [i Guts namn]); and a parallel devoicing of `-g(g)`/`-b(b)` before a suffixed `-t`/`-s`.
Separately, a cluster of very common words have **non-obvious spoken forms** that must be learned
individually rather than derived by rule (personal pronouns `mig/dig/sig` → [mεj]/[dεj]/[sεj];
`och` → [ɔ] unstressed vs. [ɔk] stressed; `att` before an infinitive → [ɔ]; `vara/är/var/varit` →
[vɑ:]/[ε:]/[vɑ:]/[va]). The chapter explicitly distinguishes three tiers of how far a reduction has
spread into writing: (a) spoken-only, spelling unaffected (`bredvid`, `morgon`); (b) spoken and
*sometimes* written informally (`nån`, `sån`, `sen`, `dom`); (c) spoken and now *normally* written
too, the older long form surviving only in formal/archaic use (`bastu` ← `badstuga`, `arton` ←
`aderton`, `sa` ← `sade`). This three-tier model is itself a genuinely useful, reusable framework
for tracking how any given colloquialism's register status can shift over time — directly relevant
to this project's own usage-tier taxonomy.

Finally, §1.2.11 documents regular cross-word assimilation in fluent speech: word-final `-r` before
a following `s-` merges into a `sj`-sound (`Hur sa?` → [hʃa]) — explicitly flagged as "less common
in southern Sweden than in the rest of the country," a genuine regional phonological difference;
`-r` also assimilates with a following `d/t/n/l`; and word-final `-n` after a short unstressed vowel
becomes `-m` before a following `b-`.

### Chapter 2 — Sentence stress, word stress, and tonal accent (p.23-31)

Swedish prosody has three independent layers: which words receive sentence stress (content words
generally do; pronouns, `inte`, auxiliary verbs, and unstressed objects generally don't — but a
fronted object pronoun, or an object stressed for contrast, *is* stressed); which syllable of a
stressed word carries word stress (non-compounds: ~35% monosyllabic, ~40% initial stress, ~25%
non-initial stress — non-initial stress correlates strongly with specific prefixes `be-/för-`,
the suffix `-era`, and foreign loanwords generally; compounds: primary stress on the first element,
secondary on the second, with a falling-then-rising melody); and tonal accent, a genuine two-way
pitch-contour contrast (acute "accent 1," one peak, found in all monosyllables and some specific
disyllable classes; gravis "accent 2," two peaks with a "connective" function signalling that two
syllables belong together, found in most other polysyllables). Accent 2 additionally has a
**distinctive function**: the source states there are "about 350 homophones" distinguished only by
accent, several of which are genuinely funny minimal pairs worth keeping as a reference set (`anden`
"the duck" vs. `anden` "the spirit"; `femton` "fifteen" vs. `fem ton` "five tons"; `rått kött` "raw
meat" vs. `råttkött` "rat meat"; `en vits i predikan` "a joke in the sermon" vs. `en vitsig predikan`
"a witty sermon"). A detailed rule table (§2.5) lists seven phonological environments that normally
"block" accent 2 in favor of accent 1 (vowel+l, vowel+n, vowel+r endings; comparative `-re`/
superlative `-erst`; `-isk`/`-sk`; `-is`/`-iker`; unstressed-initial-syllable verbs; `-era` verbs),
each with its own sub-list of counter-exceptions — e.g. Conjugation IV past participles in `-en`
(`öppen`, `skriven`) are accent 2 despite the vowel+n environment that would otherwise block it.

### Chapter 3 — Gender, declension, and definiteness (p.32-54)

Swedish has a two-way gender system (non-neuter/"en-words" vs. neuter/"ett-words") that is only
*partly* predictable from meaning (most humans and animals are non-neuter, with named exceptions;
continents/countries/provinces/towns are neuter "by hidden agreement," i.e. detectable only via
agreement elsewhere in the sentence since these proper nouns carry no article themselves) or form
(a long list of non-neuter-forming and neuter-forming suffixes, each with named exceptions). Plural
formation sorts into seven declensions by ending (`-or`, `-ar`, `-er`, `-r`, `-n`, zero, `-s`),
themselves largely predictable from a combination of gender, final sound, and stress placement — six
core rules plus four supplementary ones are given, consolidated into a single decision-tree
"predictability chart." A small set of nouns have **meaning-differentiated collective forms**
distinct from their regular count plural (`ärta`→count pl. `ärtor` "individual peas" vs. collective
`ärter` "peas as food"; similarly `mygga`/`myggor`/`mygg`, `polis`/`poliser`/`polis`) — genuinely
useful evidence of a count/mass distinction encoded through a *third* plural-like form rather than
just singular/plural. Definiteness is marked by a suffixed "end article" (non-neuter `-en`/neuter
`-et`, both fully predictable from gender+ending, including special syncope rules for `-el/-en/-er`
and `-eum/-ium` stems) rather than a separate word — genuinely structurally different from English's
free-standing "the." Article usage itself diverges from English in four directions (Swedish-article/
no-English-article: abstract nouns, locations, days/seasons/festivals; Swedish-article/English-
indefinite-article: prices, frequency; no-Swedish-article/English-definite-article: instruments,
proper names, nouns after `nästa`/`samma`/etc.; no-Swedish-article/English-indefinite-article:
nationality/profession/belief nouns, with three named exceptions where the article *does* appear).
The genitive is a simple `-s` suffix (no apostrophe), attached to the *last word of the entire noun
phrase* rather than necessarily its head (`mannen på gatans åsikter` "the views of the man in the
street," `-s` on `gatan` not `mannen`) — the same "group genitive" phenomenon Andersson's Swedish
chapter (004) independently documents and explicitly calls a colloquial-style feature; useful
cross-source confirmation that this is a real, systematic Swedish structural trait, not a quirk of
either source alone.

### Chapter 4 — Adjective inflection, nationality words, and comparison (p.55-77)

Adjectives inflect for gender (non-neuter/neuter, singular only) and number in the indefinite
declension, plus a separate definite-declension ending. Thirteen distinct indefinite-declension
inflection patterns are catalogued by stem-final sound (long-vowel, short-vowel+consonant-doubling,
`-d`/`-ad` alternations, syncopating `-el`/`-er`/`-en` stems, and two fully irregular/suppletive
adjectives `gammal` and `liten`). The definite declension uses `-a` by default but `-e` in four
specific environments (`-ad`-ending past participles; `-ast`-ending superlatives; masculine-referent
singular nouns, where `-e` is merely the "more common" alternative to `-a` except for two environments
where it becomes *compulsory*: past participles in `-ad` and superlatives in `-ast`). Four distinct
"definite construction types" are distinguished by which of three possible definiteness markers
(front article/demonstrative, adjective ending, end article) are actually present — Type 2
(genitive/possessive/`denna`/determinative/`vars`/`samma`-type premodifiers) drops the end article
entirely, and Type 3 (fixed names, geographic locations, nationality/colour/compass-point/ordinal
expressions) drops the front article instead, each triggered by a different, independently-listed
set of environments. Adjectival nouns (an adjective used standalone as if it were a noun) split into
three distinct productivity tiers: fully predictable noun-ellipsis (`gamla bilar... nya (bilar)`),
near-obligatory use for a whole semantic class (person-denoting adjectives: `de blinda` "the blind"),
and fully lexicalized nominal conversion (`lillan` "the little girl," `högern` "the (political)
Right"). Comparison splits into four classes: the regular/productive `-are`/`-ast` suffix pair
(covering most adjectives, including a sizeable set of Latinate loans English compares with "more/
most" but Swedish still suffixes); a closed set of ~8 monosyllables taking `-re`/`-st` *with* a
stem-vowel shift; a small irregular/suppletive-stem group (`god/bra`, `dålig`, `gammal`, `liten`,
`många`, `mycket`); and periphrastic `mer`/`mest` for most participles, `-isk`-adjectives, and other
specific morphological classes. `mer(a)`/`fler(a)` split along the count/non-count axis exactly the
way English "more"/count-"more" don't quite — `mer` only with non-count nouns, `fler` only with
count nouns, a genuinely non-trivial grammatical distinction for English speakers to internalize.

### Chapter 5 — Pronoun systems, especially sin/sitt/sina and det (p.78-92)

Swedish personal pronouns distinguish subject/object/reflexive forms, with the third person
singular reflexive `sig` collapsing gender distinctions English keeps. `du`/`ni` carries a real,
source-flagged sociolinguistic shift in progress: `du` became "almost universally accepted" from
the 1960s on as class barriers eased, leaving formal `ni` sounding "old-fashioned and stand-offish"
to many modern speakers when used in the singular — i.e. the T/V-style distinction Swedish *used* to
have has largely collapsed toward the informal pole, a genuinely significant diachronic register
shift (confirmed independently by 005's continued but clearly-marked use of `Ni` in a 2001
phrasebook aimed at foreign travellers who'd still encounter it in service contexts). `sin/sitt/sina`
is a genuinely tricky reflexive-possessive system requiring tracking which noun phrase is the
*subject of the clause* (not just "the possessor") — the chapter gives eight separate numbered rules
and two practical heuristics (draw an arrow to the possessor and ask if it's the subject; try
inserting English "own" before the possessed noun) to help English speakers, because English simply
doesn't grammaticalize this distinction at all. `det` ("it") is cataloged with no fewer than seven
distinct grammatical functions (true personal pronoun, demonstrative, copula complement, formal/
expletive subject, impersonal subject, object of "think/believe/hope/say"-type verbs, and a function
with literally no English equivalent when answering yes/no questions: `Kan du svenska? Ja, det kan
jag` "Do you speak Swedish? Yes, I do") — a single high-frequency word doing structural work that
English spreads across "it," "there," "so," and nothing at all. Several productive pronoun-based
idiom patterns are flagged: pejorative/sympathetic address uses a possessive pronoun rather than a
vocative noun phrase (`Din dumbom!` "You fool!"); `man` is markedly more common in everyday Swedish
than English "one," including as a slightly ironic `du`-substitute.

### Chapter 6 — Numeral system, nominalized numerals, and clock time (p.93-99)

Cardinal numbers compound without "and" between hundreds and tens (unlike English), and have a
productive nominal-use pattern: a cardinal + `-a` becomes a noun denoting race/competition position,
a vehicle's route number, flat size by room-count, or clothing/shoe size (`en trea` "a #3 bus" or
"a three-room flat," context-disambiguated) — a genuinely compact piece of derivational morphology
worth modeling for a conlang's own numeral system. Currency-specific nominalizations exist too
(`en femma`/`en tia` "a five-/ten-kronor coin"). `halv` ("half") is a true inflecting adjective
(`en halv sida`, `ett halvt äpple`) distinguished from the separate noun `hälft` ("a half," as in
"the first half of the film") — two unrelated lexical items covering what English does with one
word plus context. Telling time follows the Germanic forward-looking half-hour convention (`halv
fem` = "4:30," not "5:30") the project has now confirmed independently in 004 and 005 — plus a
further wrinkle unique to this source: a special construction for the 21-39-minutes-past range that
counts minutes *to* or *past* the half-hour mark itself (`fem minuter i halv sex` "5:25," lit. "five
minutes before half-six"), rather than simply continuing to count from the hour.

### Chapter 7 — Verb morphology: four conjugations, non-finite forms, tense/mood system (p.100-141)

Swedish verbs show no person/number agreement at all (one form per tense regardless of subject) and
split into four conjugations by how the past tense is formed: three weak conjugations (I: `-a`
infinitive, past in `-de`, the fully productive class including every modern coinage and loanword
verb; IIa: consonant-final stem ending voiced, past in `-de`; IIb: stem ending voiceless or in `-n`,
past in `-te`) and one strong conjugation (IV: past formed by an internal vowel change rather than a
suffix, itself subdivided into gradation series sharing the same vowel-alternation pattern — i-e-i,
y/u-ö-u, i-a-u, a-o-a, ä-a-u, plus a "minor/mixed" residual group). A small residual class of
irregular weak modal-flavored verbs (`kunna`, `vilja`, `skola`, `veta`, `böra`) falls outside all four
main conjugations. Several verbs preserve now-archaic *longer* infinitive/present variants
(`taga`/`ta`, `giva`/`ge`, `bedja`/`be`, `draga`/`dra`, `bliva`/`bli`) that the source explicitly
marks as "now formal or archaic" — a live example of ongoing phonological erosion being formally
recognized in the standard grammar, directly comparable to Chapter 1's spoken-reduction catalogue
but operating on the written-standard end of the register spectrum instead. Strong verbs historically
had a *separate plural past-tense form* in written Swedish until the early 1900s (`blev`/archaic pl.
`blevo`, `bar`/`buro`, `var`/`voro`) — explicitly flagged as now archaic, a genuine historical-
morphology data point (the loss of verbal plural-number marking) that this book's main competitor
sources (002, 003, both older) may still actively teach as current, worth checking once those are
extracted. The supine (used only with `har`/`hade` to build the perfect/pluperfect) is formally
distinct from the past participle only in Conjugation IV, a split the source dates to "the eighteenth
century." Five "be"-equivalent verbs beyond `vara` cover location (`ligga`, `sitta`, `stå`, each with
a specific physical-orientation nuance), transition (`bli`), and existence (`finnas`) — a genuinely
rich semantic subdivision of "to be" that has no single-word English parallel. The passive has three
distinct forms with different stylistic/semantic profiles: the `-s`-passive (most common, especially
in writing, agentless by default, often paraphrasable with `man`/`de`/`folk`); the `bli`-passive
(stresses an isolated occurrence, usually *has* an agent); and the `vara`-passive (stresses a
resultant state, adjectival in feel) — with an explicit tense-equivalence table showing `vara`-passive
present = `bli`/`-s`-passive perfect, and `vara`-passive past = pluperfect. Compound (particle) verbs
split into inseparable (particle always attached: mostly noun/adjective/verb/`själv-`-compounds, plus
verbs with a closed set of stressed/unstressed prefixes) and separable (particle can detach, always
re-attaches in participial forms) — with an explicitly flagged **stylistic** split (separated =
everyday register, integral = formal written register, for the *same* verb) and, for a subset, a
genuine **semantic** split severe enough that the two forms are "regarded as two distinct verbs"
(`bryta av`/`avbryta`, `stryka under`/`understryka`, `lyfta upp`/`upplysa`) — this last pattern is a
directly reusable slang-synthesis mechanism: a single underlying root splitting into a literal/
concrete sense and a figurative/abstract sense purely via whether a particle detaches or stays fused.

### Chapter 8 — Adverb formation and the modal-particle system (p.142-151)

Most manner adverbs derive from adjectives via `-t` (identical in form to the neuter adjective
ending), with `-en`/`-tvis` as alternative derivational suffixes specifically for `-lig`-adjectives,
distinguishing *clausal* adverbials (`lyckligtvis` "fortunately," modifies the whole clause) from
plain manner adverbials (`lyckligt` "happily," modifies just the verb) — the derivational suffix
choice itself encodes a scope distinction English doesn't mark morphologically at all. Negation has
three registers: neutral `inte`, written-only `ej`, and compound-only `icke` (used almost exclusively
prefixed onto another word, `en icke-kristen`). Amplifier adverbs (degree-intensifiers before an
adjective/adverb) are a long, semantically rich set (`mycket`, `helt`, `alldeles`, `ganska`, `lagom`,
`rätt`, `för`, `lite`, `bra`) with genuinely tricky, non-compositional usage splits — `mycket` itself
means "very" before a positive-degree adjective but "much, a lot" before a comparative or a verb, a
single word covering two different English translations purely by syntactic context. Adjective-
derived adverbs can themselves be *repurposed* as amplifiers (`förskräckligt varmt` "awfully hot,"
`hemskt bråttom` "a terrible hurry") — directly the same intensifier-via-adjective-adverb mechanism
already flagged in 005 for `jätte`, now confirmed as a broader, open-class pattern rather than one
isolated word. Swedish grammaticalizes a location/motion-towards/motion-away-from three-way adverb
distinction that English has lost except as fossils ("here/hither/hence") — `var`/`här`/`där` etc.
each have dedicated motion-towards (`vart`/`hit`/`dit`) and motion-away-from (`varifrån`/`härifrån`/
`därifrån`) counterparts, and the *interrogative* `var`/`vart` split is explicitly cross-referenced
against the inconsistency already flagged in 005's grammar points (there, `Vart bor du?`/`Vart är
passkontrollen?` blur exactly this distinction a careful usage like this chapter's own `Var är
utgången?`/`Vart går han?` pair keeps clean) — this book's prescriptive version confirms the "correct"
split that the 2001 phrasebook was sometimes loose about. A cluster of short, high-frequency "modal
particle" adverbs (`ju`, `nog`, `väl`, `nämligen`, plus `då`/`sedan`/`därför`/`redan`/`gärna`/`eller
hur`) each carry a specific, hard-to-translate pragmatic/stance meaning rather than propositional
content — this is a genuinely distinct word class from ordinary adverbs and worth treating as its
own design category for any conlang wanting naturalistic discourse-particle behavior.

### Chapter 9 — Interjection taxonomy (p.152-153)

Interjections are uninflected and almost always clause-initial. The chapter gives a compact but
complete taxonomy: a three-way yes/no/contradicting-yes system (`ja`/`nej`/`jo`, where `jo` is a
dedicated "yes, [contradicting your negative assumption]" that English has no single-word
equivalent for); graded intensity within each feeling-category (pain/surprise/disgust each have 2-3
synonyms); animal-command interjections distinct from human ones; onomatopoeic sound-imitation
words for animals and objects, a closed and typically language-specific inventory; a greetings/
good-wishes/apologies set with real register stratification already partly flagged above (`Hej!`
core vs. `Hejsan!` more casual vs. `Goddag!`/`Adjö!` more formal); and the expletives list (see
Vocabulary — fifteen oaths ranging from fully euphemized numeral-substitutions through religious
invocations to unsoftened vulgarities, several forming genuine softened/unsoftened doublets from the
same root within the single list, e.g. `Jäklar!`/`Jävlar!`).

### Chapter 10 — Preposition polysemy and the ten-most-frequent-prepositions method (p.154-174)

Swedish prepositions are overwhelmingly pre-positioned (most) with a small closed set of true
postpositions (`året om`, `oss emellan`) and circumpositions (`för ... sedan`, `för ... skull`) —
genuinely marked word-order categories, not free variation. A structurally important rule: Swedish
*requires* the preposition to end a clause (never fronts with its complement) in V-questions,
relative clauses, topicalized-complement constructions, infinitive phrases, and even exclamations —
systematically the opposite of a rule a French- or German-background learner might expect, and
actually matching colloquial (not formal) English usage instead (`Honom kan man inte lita på` "He's
not to be relied *on*"). The ten most frequent prepositions (`av`, `från`, `för`, `i`, `med`, `om`,
`på`, `till`, `under`, `vid`) are each given a dedicated subsection mapping their English-translation
range across 4-6 distinct semantic categories (agent/material/cause/location/measure/possession for
`av` alone) — the overall lesson being that Swedish preposition choice is driven by a different
semantic partition of space/time/cause than English, not a 1:1 lexical substitution, confirmed at
length in the time-expression tables (§10.3.2-10.3.3), which show that "in," "on," and "at" each map
to *several different* Swedish prepositions depending on whether the time reference is past,
habitual, present, or future (e.g. "last Sunday" = `i söndags`, "on Sundays" = `på söndagarna`, "next
Sunday" = `på söndag" — three different constructions for what English marks only with a
determiner/tense choice). Similarly, `i`/`på` with places of location split along a surface/volume
distinction (`på` + surface, `i` + volume/enclosed-space) that resolves most but not all cases, with
five explicitly named exception classes (public buildings, workplaces/places of study, entertainment
venues, named-person's-home via `hos`, and `vid` for things that "extend lengthwise").

### Chapter 11 — Conjunction classes and problem-word disambiguation (p.175-181)

Coordinating conjunctions link same-level elements without affecting word order; a note flags that
`och` links equal-weight elements while `samt` links *unequal*-weight ones, and that `utan` (not
`men`) must replace "but" specifically when the second clause directly contradicts a negated first
clause (`Han köpte inte bilen utan stal den` "He did not buy the car, but stole it") — a genuine,
easy-to-miss distinction for an English speaker since English "but" covers both senses
indiscriminately. Subordinating conjunctions split into two types: "general subordinators" (`att`,
`om`) that introduce indirect speech/questions but add no semantic content of their own, versus
semantically contentful subordinators organized by the seven adverbial-clause meanings (time, cause,
condition, concession, intention, result, comparison) — each meaning typically has 2+ competing
conjunction choices with their own register/usage conditions (`eftersom`/`sedan` both "because," but
flagged register-distinct; `förrän` specifically required after a *negative* main clause where
`innan` would be used after a positive one). A closed "other subordinators" class (interrogative and
relative pronouns/adverbs) introduces clauses without technically being conjunctions at all. The
chapter closes with a dedicated "problem conjunctions" section systematically disambiguating English
words that map to multiple, non-interchangeable Swedish conjunctions depending on sense (`after`,
`as`, `as...as...`, `before`, `both`, `but`, `that`) — a reusable template for any contrastive-
grammar design: identify the English words whose Swedish translation genuinely depends on which of
several distinct underlying meanings is intended, rather than assuming a stable 1:1 mapping.

### Chapter 12 — Positional word-order grammar (p.182-206)

This chapter's governing idea is a fixed **positional schema** for the Swedish main clause — Topic,
Finite Verb, Subject, Clausal Adverbial, Non-Finite Verb, (Verb) Particle, Object/Complement, Agent,
Other Adverbial, plus Link and two Extra positions bracketing the whole clause — through which nearly
every word-order phenomenon in the book is explained as a constituent either occupying or vacating
one of these fixed slots. Five sentence types (statement, yes/no question, V-question, command, wish)
are distinguished purely by which positions are filled and whether the clause has FV1 (verb-first) or
FV2 (verb-second, after some other element) order. The clausal adverbial position is the book's
single most-cross-referenced structural fact: in main clauses it sits *after* the finite verb
(opposite of English, where it precedes); in subordinate clauses it sits *before* the finite verb
(matching the main-clause-reversed "S-I-V" mnemonic: Subject-`Inte`-Verb) — and several "exception"
constructions in §12.7-12.8.3 are really cases where a subordinate clause borrows main-clause-style
adverbial placement, which the source notes occurs "only in spoken Swedish" for at least one such
case, directly tying a syntactic choice to register. The "weight principle" (unstressed/familiar
information moves left, stressed/new information moves right, producing an overall "right-heavy"
sentence balance) governs topicalization, unstressed-object fronting into the subject slot, adverbial
shift, passive transformation, and the existential-sentence construction uniformly — a single
general principle explaining five superficially distinct movement rules. The cleft sentence (`Det
var/är (W) som...`) can extract and emphasize nearly any constituent, is explicitly "very common in
questions," and is separately flagged in Chapter 16 as more typical of *spoken* than written Swedish.

### Chapter 13 — Word formation: the four lexical-change processes (p.207-216)

Swedish vocabulary growth runs through four processes: borrowing (with eventual full assimilation
into native orthography/pronunciation/inflection), compounding (first element can be almost any word
class, second element — usually a noun/adjective/verb — determines the compound's own gender and
inflection; an optional `-s-` linking morpheme is governed by a genuinely complex, partly-lexicalized
rule set keyed to the first element's own ending, with the same root sometimes taking `-s-` in one
compound and not another), affixation (prefixes never change word class; suffixes very often do; a
large catalogued inventory of both, organized by semantic function — negative/pejorative, attitude,
location/direction, people-denoting, activity-denoting, status-denoting, and four distinct
conversion patterns turning nouns/adjectives into other word classes), and abbreviation (which itself
subdivides into initial/final/medial morpheme-loss, the `-is`/`-a(n)` colloquial-clipping pattern,
hypocoristic name-shortening, and four acronym subtypes — alphabetisms read letter-by-letter,
alphabetisms later fully respelled phonetically, acronyms pronounced as ordinary words, and hybrid
acronym+word compounds). The productive/non-productive affix distinction is explicitly drawn (an
affix is productive if its meaning is still predictable and still actively used to coin new words;
`-bar` is given as productive, `-lek` as not) — directly useful methodologically for this project's
own mechanics-analysis phase when deciding whether a derived-conlang slang mechanism should be
modeled as a live, extensible process or a closed, memorized set.

### Chapter 14 — Capitalization and the -m/-n spelling rules (p.217-219)

Swedish capitalizes far less than English: weekday/month/season/festival names, nationality/
language/religion/political-affiliation words, and titles-with-names are all lowercase where English
capitalizes. Multi-word proper names capitalize only the first word unless a later word is itself
independently a proper noun — producing genuine minimal pairs distinguishing a literal description
from an institutionalized name by capitalization alone (`det vita huset` "the white house" [any
white house] vs. `Vita huset` "the White House" [in Washington]; same pattern for `Röda korset` "the
Red Cross" vs. a literal red cross). The `-m`/`-n` spelling rules (final `-m`/`-n` generally not
doubled even after a short vowel, except a short named-exception list; always doubled between vowels
after a short vowel; one `-m`/`-n` dropped again when a further consonant-initial ending is added)
form a small, fully rule-governed subsystem with its own four-category exception list (before the
s-genitive, before the s-passive, before certain suffixes, and inside certain compounds) — a tidy,
self-contained orthographic microsystem.

### Chapter 15 — Punctuation conventions (p.220-224)

Commas are used much as in English but are explicitly *not* used before `att`-clauses (unless both
clauses are long) or before subordinate clauses whose subordinator has been omitted — a case where
English punctuation habits would mislead a learner into over-punctuating. The colon, not the comma,
introduces direct speech/quotation after a reporting verb, and the word after it is capitalized — a
genuine divergence from English convention. The exclamation mark is used "more widely... than in
English," appearing after ordinary greetings, commands, and warnings where English would often just
use a period. The apostrophe is never used for the genitive (no `'s` at all) and is reserved for
marking omitted letters, though several very common contractions (`dan`, `stan`, `sa`, `ska`, `nån`)
have become standard enough to be written *without* an apostrophe at all — directly continuing
Chapter 1's three-tier register-drift model into orthographic convention. Direct speech in print
conventionally uses a dash ("pratminus") rather than quotation marks, with Swedish's own quotation-
mark glyphs (`”...”`) differing in form from English's. The hyphen's uses include several genuinely
register-marked or structurally motivated cases: compound proper names, shared-second-element
first-element coordination (`sön- och helgdagar`), the `icke-` negative prefix, acronym-initial
compounds, number-initial compounds, and a catch-all for compounds that would otherwise become too
long/complex to parse (`gör-det-själv-kampanjer` "do-it-yourself campaigns").

### Chapter 16 — The dedicated written/spoken register chapter (p.225-228)

This short closing chapter is organized as three explicit lists rather than general discussion:
words/constructions regularly *omitted* in speech (the subordinating conjunction `att` after verbs of
saying/thinking; the relative pronoun `som` when it isn't the clause's subject; a verb of motion
after a modal auxiliary, `Jag måste till Lund` "I have to [go] to Lund"; and even the pronoun `jag`
itself in unstressed sentence-initial position); words/constructions regularly *inserted* only in
speech (the formal subject `det` even in cases a purely written register might omit it; the cleft
sentence; several kinds of duplication/doubling-back reference; a supplementary `du` tacked onto
commands; supplementary `så`/`då` after a fronted adverbial or subordinate-clause topic; and the four
modal particles `ju`/`nog`/`väl`/`nämligen` already catalogued in Chapter 8) — and finally a direct
ten-row table of formal/written words paired against their informal/spoken-or-casual-written
equivalents (demonstratives, the possessive `dess`, the relative `vars`, four conjunctions, and one
adverb), explicitly presented as "less formal alternatives" a learner should reach for outside
formal writing. Collectively, this chapter is the book's own explicit acknowledgment that essentially
every other chapter's "the rule is X" statements sometimes needs a register qualifier — directly
validating this project's own register/usage-tier tracking methodology from the authoritative
grammar-reference side rather than only the corpus side.

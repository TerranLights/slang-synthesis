# Spanish — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: *Learning Spanish Like Crazy*, Level I, Lecciones 1–8 + Insult Supplements

**Source:** *Learning Spanish Like Crazy* (Patrick Jackson et al.), Level I, spoken-Spanish audio
course transcribed to PDF lesson scripts (`Spanish_Lesson_01.pdf`–`Spanish_Lesson_08.pdf`, PDF
metadata dated 2005), plus the course's two dedicated insult/vulgar-language supplements
`Insultos_01.pdf` ("Insultos (no vulgares)," ~33 items) and `insultosvulgares.pdf` ("Dichos
Vulgares & Insultos Vulgares," a scripted vulgar dialogue). All six files have a clean, genuine
`pdftotext -layout` text layer — no vision-reading, no font-substitution cipher, no marginalia
issue encountered.

**Coverage note:** This course is a spoken-repetition drill format — nearly every sentence in a
lesson is a "listen and repeat" or "how do you say X" cue repeating the same handful of new
words/forms introduced earlier in that same lesson, often 15–30 times per lesson. Per the coverage
rule, only the *first, new* introduction of each vocabulary item, grammatical form, or construction
is extracted below; the repeated drill instances (the bulk of each transcript) are skipped as
non-informative duplication. The two insult files are given especially thorough, near-complete
coverage per the dispatch's explicit priority, since insult/taboo vocabulary is this corpus's
highest-value slang content. `readme.pdf` (course intro) was skimmed only, per dispatch instruction,
and yielded no text layer via `pdftotext` (likely a scanned cover page) — not extracted, low
priority.

## Grammar points

### Regular -ar present-tense conjugation (Lección 1)
Stem + person/number suffix: -o (yo), -as (tú), -a (él/ella/usted), -amos (nosotros), -an
(ellos/ellas/ustedes). Modeled on "hablar" (hablo/hablas/habla/hablamos/hablan), then reinforced
with "caminar," "practicar," "estudiar," "trabajar," and "manejar" across Lecciones 1–2 without
re-deriving the paradigm each time — a genuinely productive template the course leans on
repeatedly. Negation is formed by placing "no" immediately before the conjugated verb (p. 7–8,
Lección 1).

### Spanish present tense covers three English senses (Lección 1)
The instructor explicitly notes Spanish present tense does triple duty for: (1) an action in
progress ("Yo hablo español ahora" = "I am speaking Spanish now"), (2) a continuous/habitual action
("Nosotros hablamos español todo el tiempo" = "We speak Spanish all the time"), and (3), unlike
English, a near-future action ("Esta noche, ella habla español en la clase" = "Tonight, she will
speak Spanish in class").

### tú vs. usted register split (Lección 1, formalized in Lección 8)
"Tú" (informal) is used with anyone you'd normally address by first name; "usted" (formal) with
anyone you'd address as Mr./Mrs. The course defaults to "tú" throughout (Lecciones 1–7, since its
stated goal is informal spoken Spanish) except Lección 8, which is built entirely around "usted" to
teach a first-meeting/formal-register conversation.

### Regular -er present-tense conjugation (Lección 5)
Stem + -o/-es/-e/-emos/-en, modeled on "aprender" then reinforced with "comer," "beber,"
"correr," "ver," "leer," and "vender" across Lecciones 5–7 the same way the -ar paradigm was
reinforced in Lecciones 1–2. Two verbs in this set show minor irregularity not named explicitly by
the course but audible in the drills: "ver" → yo "veo" (not the fully regular *veo would still be
predictable, but flagged since the course never states a rule for it) and "entender" (Lección 8,
technically -er but with an e→ie stem change: entiendo/entiendes/entiende, entendemos regular).

### Possessive adjectives vs. possessive pronouns (Lecciones 3–4)
Possessive *adjectives* (mi/tu/su/nuestro, always pre-nominal) agree with the possessed noun only
in number for mi/tu/su (mi gato → mis gatos) but in both gender and number for nuestro
(nuestro perro / nuestra casa / nuestros perros / nuestras casas). Possessive *pronouns*
(mío/tuyo/suyo/nuestro, standing alone after "ser") agree with the possessed object in BOTH gender
and number regardless of which possessive-adjective pattern applies (mi mesa → es mía; mi gato →
es mío). Lección 4 devotes its entire content to drilling this agreement distinction, explicitly
calling it out multiple times ("since Spanish possessive pronouns must agree with the object owned
both in gender and number...").

### "su/suyo" ambiguity (Lecciones 3–4)
"Su"/"suyo" collapses four distinct English possessives (his/her/its/their/your-formal) into one
Spanish form; the course walks through this ambiguity explicitly across both lessons and notes it's
resolved by context or, when needed, an explicit "de + [name/pronoun]" construction (e.g. "el perro
es de ella").

### Gender/number agreement of descriptive adjectives (Lecciones 6–7)
Color adjectives ending in -o (rojo, negro, blanco) fully inflect for gender and number
(rojo/roja/rojos/rojas); "azul," ending in a consonant, only inflects for number (azul/azules), not
gender — flagged directly in Lección 7's own instructor commentary.

### Indefinite vs. definite articles (Lección 6)
Un/una/unos/unas (indefinite: "a," "some") is introduced in contrast to the already-familiar
definite article el/la/los/las ("the"), both agreeing in gender and number with the noun. The
course uses minimal pairs directly (el carro rojo vs. un carro rojo) to make the contrast concrete.

### "a dónde" vs. "dónde" (Lección 6)
The course explicitly flags that a motion verb ("correr," "caminar," "manejar") requires the
directional preposition "a" before "dónde" when asking about a destination ("¿A dónde corres?" =
"Where/to where do you run?"), distinct from bare locational "¿dónde?" ("where," i.e. static
location) used with verbs like "aprender," "comer," "leer."

### Grammatical double negation (Lección 6)
"No veo nada" is presented as the grammatically correct Spanish way to say "I don't see anything" —
the course explicitly notes that, unlike English, stacking "no" with a negative indefinite ("nada")
is required, not a solecism.

### Reflexive-verb formula: llamarse (Lección 8)
"¿Cómo se llama usted?" ("What's your name?") and "Me llamo..." ("My name is...") are introduced as
fixed conversational formulas without the course analyzing the reflexive pronoun ("se"/"me")
morpheme-by-morpheme — the first reflexive construction encountered in the course, presented purely
functionally rather than grammatically.

### Formal-register conversation openers (Lección 8)
The whole lesson is built around usted-only dialogue for a first encounter: "disculpe"/"perdón"
(excuse me/pardon), "señor"/"señora"/"señorita" as address terms, "¿Es usted americano/a?",
"¿Cómo se llama usted?"/"Me llamo...", and the "mucho gusto" / "el gusto es mío" exchange — a
complete functional script for a polite first meeting, distinct from the informal-register content
of every other lesson in this batch.

### Gender-marking on taboo/insult nouns (Insultos supplements)
Both insult files repeatedly demonstrate that a masculine insult noun re-marks for a feminine
target by the standard -o→-a adjective/noun agreement pattern (puerco→puerca, perro→perra,
pendejo→pendeja, lleno→llena), and that this agreement can itself carry asymmetric insult force —
"perra" is markedly more vulgar/sexually charged than its masculine counterpart "perro" despite the
purely mechanical -o→-a shift, a data point directly relevant to later slang-mechanics analysis of
how grammatical gender marking interacts with insult intensity in Spanish.

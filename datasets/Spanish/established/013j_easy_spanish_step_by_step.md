## Grammar points

### Querer, poder, saber (and conocer): different translations in preterit vs. imperfect

Four common verbs shift in translated meaning depending on whether they appear in the preterit or
the imperfect — not because the verb itself is irregular in meaning, but because the preterit's
"completed action" aspect surfaces a different real-world implicature than the imperfect's
"ongoing state" aspect, for these specific verbs (pp. 266-267):

- **querer**: imperfect **quería** = "wanted" (an ongoing desire/state); preterit **quise** = "wanted
  and then acted on it / tried to" (**Yo quise ir al circo**); the *negative* preterit **no quiso**
  shifts further to "refused" (**¿Por qué no quiso Ud. ir conmigo?** — "Why did you *refuse* to go
  with me?" — not merely "didn't want to").
- **poder**: imperfect **podía** = "was able to" (a state of capability, no claim about whether the
  act happened); preterit **pude** = "was able to **and did**" / "succeeded in" (**Pudimos entender
  la lección** — "we succeeded in understanding"); negative preterit **no pude** = "failed to" /
  "was unable to and didn't" (**No pude hacer mi tarea** — "I *failed* to do my homework," a
  stronger claim than the imperfect **no podía**, "I wasn't able to," which leaves the outcome
  softer/ambiguous).
- **saber**: imperfect **sabía** = "knew" (a standing state of knowledge); preterit **supe** = "found
  out" / "learned" (a punctual event of acquiring knowledge) — **Ella supo que él tuvo un
  accidente** ("She found out that he had an accident") vs. **Ella sabía que él iba a estar bien**
  ("She knew [already, as a state] that he was going to be well").
- **conocer** (introduced earlier at p. 266 alongside this set): imperfect **conocía** = "knew,
  was acquainted with" (ongoing state of acquaintance) vs. preterit **conocí** = "met [for the first
  time]" (the punctual event that began the acquaintance) — **Conocimos a Silvia en Colombia** ("We
  met Sylvia in Colombia") vs. **No la conocíamos por mucho tiempo** ("We hadn't known her for very
  long").

This is a clean, compact restatement of a pattern likely touched on elsewhere in this project's
Spanish sources but worth keeping as a self-contained reference table: all four verbs share the same
underlying mechanism (aspect shift reinterprets a *state* verb as a *bounded event*), which is
exactly the kind of grammaticalized aspect/lexical-meaning interaction useful for later
mechanics-analysis work on how an invented register might collapse or repurpose the
preterit/imperfect distinction.

### The imperfect's functional uses (as this book organizes them)

Beyond ordinary "was/were doing" translations, the book organizes imperfect usage into named
functions (pp. 260-261, sampled): **to "set the stage"/narrate background** (*El sol brillaba y los
pájaros cantaban* — scene-setting, no bounded start/end implied); **to express habitual, customary,
or repeated past actions** (*Todos los veranos, yo jugaba al tenis con mis amigos*); and (per the
book's broader framing) ongoing states/conditions with no specific beginning or end. **Ir** in the
imperfect specifically translates as "was/were going" (**Yo iba a hablar** — "I was going to
speak"), which the book flags as a common source of confusion with the unrelated periphrastic-future
**ir a** + infinitive construction, since both surface as "going to."

### Double object pronouns: combination order and the *le/les → se* rule

When both an indirect and a direct object pronoun appear together, the **indirect always precedes
the direct** or attaches with it as a fused pair to an infinitive (**Necesito tu carro esta
noche. ¿Me lo prestas?**; **Él va a dár__mela__**, with a written accent added to preserve the
infinitive's natural stress once the pronouns are suffixed) — placement rules otherwise mirror
single-object-pronoun rules already established elsewhere (pp. 270-271).

**Whenever a third-person indirect object pronoun (le/les) would immediately precede a third-person
direct object pronoun (lo/la/los/las), le/les is obligatorily replaced by se** — purely to avoid the
awkward *le lo* / *les la* sequence (p. 273): le lo → **se lo**, le la → **se la**, le los → **se
los**, le las → **se las**; les lo/la/los/las all likewise become **se lo/la/los/las**. Because
**se** in this slot is referentially ambiguous (it could mean "to him/her/you/them," in either
number), a clarifying prepositional phrase is added when context doesn't disambiguate: **Se lo
traigo a Ud.** ("I bring it to you"), **Se lo traigo a ellas** ("I bring it to them") — but the
phrase is dropped whenever context alone already makes the referent clear (**Le doy a Enrique el
periódico** → **Se lo doy**, unambiguously "to him" from the preceding sentence). This is a second,
independent occurrence of the "add a clarifying prepositional phrase to resolve pronoun ambiguity"
strategy also seen with plain **le/les** in `013f` (Chapter 10) — worth noting as a recurring
Spanish repair strategy for third-person pronoun ambiguity generally, not limited to any one
construction.

# Spanish — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: The Big Red Book of Spanish Idioms

**Source:** Peter Weibel, *The Big Red Book of Spanish Idioms* (McGraw-Hill, 2004), full 401-page
volume — a true A-Z idiomatic dictionary (Spanish-English Idiomatic Dictionary, pp. 1-207; English-
Spanish Dictionary and Index, pp. 209-401), title page states "over 4,000 Spanish idiomatic phrases
and expressions ... and almost 8,000 English translations" (the filename's "4000 Idiomatic
Expressions" matches the title page's "4,000" figure; a different cover variant states "12,000
Spanish and English expressions" counting both directions together — not a discrepancy, just two
ways of stating the same count). PDF has a genuine text layer, but `pdftotext -layout` badly
interleaves this book's two-column print layout (lines from unrelated columns get merged onto the
same output line) — plain `pdftotext` (no `-layout` flag) was used instead and produces clean,
correctly-ordered single-column reading order; this is a layout gotcha worth flagging for future
two-column dictionary-style PDFs in this corpus. Transcription Confidence = n/a, Vision Reading
Confidence = n/a for every row below (no vision-reading needed).

**Coverage approach — dictionary-scale, deliberately sampled (per dispatch instruction to check
page count and decide full-vs-sampled with documented reasoning):** this is genuinely
dictionary-scale — ~4,000 Spanish headword entries across 207 pages of the Spanish-English section
alone, each with its own register tag. Full exhaustive extraction was not attempted; it would
amount to re-publishing the entire source book. Instead, this project's standard register-tag
priority was applied systematically: the book carries its own explicit register-abbreviation system
in its front matter (`fam.` = familiar/colloquial, `pop.` = popular/informal slang, `sl.` = slang,
`vulg.` = vulgar, `euph.` = euphemism, `pej.` = pejorative, `hum.` = humorous, `iron.` = ironical,
`fig.` = figurative, `prov.` = proverb). A systematic search was run for every line tagged `(vulg.)`
(148 occurrences in the plain-text extraction) and the great majority of these were captured below
in full entry context, since vulgar/taboo-register idioms are this project's single highest
priority and this book's `(vulg.)` tagging is itself a ready-made taboo-tier filter. A smaller,
curated sample of `(pop.)`/`(sl.)`-tagged entries (slang tier, not vulgar) and a handful of
especially vivid `(fam.)`/`(prov.)` idioms were also captured where they illustrate a productive
derivation pattern (e.g., testicle/body-part-rooted idiom families, euphemism chains, drug-slang
senses hidden inside innocuous headwords). The ~3,000 plain `(fam.)`-only entries (ordinary
colloquial vocabulary, not slang/vulgar) were NOT exhaustively extracted — at that register they
substantially overlap with general colloquial-Spanish content already well covered by this
language's established corpus, and exhaustive capture was judged not to justify the effort for this
wave. The English-Spanish index/reverse-lookup section (pp. 209-401) was not separately extracted —
it is a pure index pointing back into the Spanish-English dictionary already sampled, with no new
headword content of its own.

**Mapping to taxonomy:** `vulg.` → Usage Tier `taboo`; `pop.`/`sl.` (without `vulg.`) → `slang`;
`fam.` (without `pop.`/`sl.`/`vulg.`) → `colloquial`; `prov.` → kept as `colloquial` with a Notes
flag identifying it as a proverb. Region: the book marks `Esp.` for Spain-specific entries and
otherwise gives no explicit region (most entries are presented as general/pan-Hispanic); English
glosses occasionally carry their own `Am.E.`/`Br.E.` tags, which describe the English translation's
dialect, not the Spanish term's region — these are noted but not treated as Spanish geographic scope.

**Non-redundant-supplement note:** this book's taboo/vulgar-register idiom density goes well beyond
anything else in this wave or the existing Spanish corpus — its systematic `(vulg.)` tagging across
hundreds of entries (body-part-rooted insults/curses, explicit sexual-act verbs, scatological
idioms, drug slang hidden under innocuous headwords like "la mierda"/"el polvo") is new, dictionary-
depth content distinct from the conversational-register idioms in *Spanish Among Amigos*
(established/042), the personality/mood idioms in *Spanish in Your Face!* (established/043), and the
party-phrasebook content in *Hide This Spanish CD* (established/044). Some individual root words
recur across sources (cojones, huevos, joder, mierda, culo) simply because Spanish's core taboo
vocabulary is small and highly productive — this book's value is the sheer *number* of distinct
idiomatic expressions built from each root, which the other sources in this wave only sample a few
of.

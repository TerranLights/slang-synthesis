# Spanish — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Colloquial Spanish of Latin America 2 (full book)

**Source:** Roberto Rodríguez-Saona, *Colloquial Spanish of Latin America: The Next Step in
Language Learning* (Colloquial 2 Series), Routledge, 1st ed., 2004. Full book covered: Units 1–10
(pp. 1–142), Grammar reference appendix (pp. 143–150), Spanish-English glossary (pp. 169–174).
Key to exercises (pp. 151–168, drill answers only) and the English-Spanish glossary (pp. 175–180,
confirmed a pure mirror of the Spanish-English glossary) were read but not extracted, per the
coverage rule.

**Coverage note:** This is a clean-text-layer PDF (`pdftotext` produced fully usable output, no
vision-reading needed, no font-substitution cipher detected). Every grammar point, every distinct
vocabulary item, and every explicit dialectal/regional annotation from the ten units and the
grammar reference appendix is captured below. Repeated drill exercises and duplicate example
sentences that introduced no new vocabulary or grammar were skipped.

**Non-redundant-supplement note:** at the time of this extraction, `established/001` (the
*Complete Course* — this book's own prerequisite volume, referenced in its own Introduction) had
not yet been extracted (the `established/` directory was empty). This file was therefore written
as a full, independent extraction rather than a supplement. **When 001 is extracted, a pass should
re-check this file's basic-grammar items (ser/estar, future tense, imperative, imperfect, relative
clauses, negation, por/para, noun gender/plural/articles) against 001's coverage** — a *Complete
Course for Beginners* almost certainly covers foundational paradigms (ser/estar, regular verb
conjugation, basic noun gender) that this "next step" book only briefly revises before moving to
its own more advanced material (subjunctive-heavy conditionals, passive/impersonal constructions,
voseo, accentuation rules, register/dialectal contrasts). This book's own advanced/register content
(voseo, accentuation, LatAm-vs-Spain contrasts, colloquial particles like nomás/recién/ya) is very
unlikely to be redundant with a beginner course and should be treated as this language's primary
source for that material regardless of what 001 turns out to cover.

**Register/dialectal priority:** as an intermediate "next step" course explicitly aimed at
learners who have moved past beginner level, this book is unusually rich in explicit "in Latin
America X, in Spain Y" contrasts and country-tagged colloquialisms (Mexico, Colombia, Cuba,
Venezuela, Argentina/Uruguay/Chile voseo zone). These are flagged throughout with **[REGISTER]**
or **[REGIONAL]** in the Notes column and given their own callouts in Grammar points, since they
are this source's highest-value content per the extraction spec.

## Grammar points

### Ser and estar (Unit 1, p.2)
`Ser` indicates identity/nature; `estar` indicates state. Beyond the basic contrast, the book
catalogs several fixed prepositional collocations: `ser+de` (made of, or a fixed descriptive
expression like *ser de armas tomar*, "not to be messed with"); `estar+de` (mood/situation, e.g.
*estar de veraneo*, "on holiday" — with a **[REGIONAL]** note that *de turno* "on duty" is the
Latin American choice vs. Spain's *de guardia*); `estar+con` (physically/emotionally affected,
e.g. *estar con dolor de cabeza*); `estar+por` (about to be done); `estar+que` (intense/repeated
action, e.g. *estoy que me muero de hambre*, "I'm starving").

### Conditional sentences (Unit 1, p.7)
Three patterns: `si+present+present` (real possibility, second present can carry future meaning);
`si+imperfect subjunctive+conditional` (remote possibility); `si+present+imperative` (a direct
instruction contingent on a condition).

### Imperfect subjunctive (Unit 1, p.9)
Two interchangeable forms, `-ra` and `-se` (trabajara/trabajase, etc.). Full paradigm given for
regular -ar/-er/-ir verbs and the most common irregulars (dar, decir, estar, haber, hacer, ir,
leer, oír, poder, poner, saber, ser, tener, traer, venir).

### Verbs ending in -cer (Unit 1, p.13)
Most -cer verbs change c→zc before a/o (conocer→conozco, parecer→parezco); if the -cer is
preceded by a consonant, c→z instead (vencer→venzo).

### Salir — extended uses (Unit 2, p.17)
Beyond "to leave," `salir` indicates: result (*todo salió bien*), cost (*el viaje le sale a 450
dólares*), media appearance (*salió por la televisión*), publishing/launching (*este libro salió
el año pasado*), and *salir con* = "to come out with" (an unexpected statement).

### Cómo (Unit 2, p.18)
Can replace `cuánto` to indicate intensity of degree/quantity (*¡Cómo me gustas!* = "I like you so
much!").

### The imperative (Unit 2, p.24)
Informal `tú` imperative = 3rd person singular present indicative form, with irregulars (decir→di,
hacer→haz, ir→ve, salir→sal, ser→sé, tener→ten, venir→ven). Formal `usted` imperative and plural
`ustedes` imperative both use the corresponding present subjunctive form. Negative imperative is
formed with `no` + the subjunctive form (not the affirmative-imperative form).

### The future tense (Unit 2, p.28)
Regular endings added to the infinitive for all three conjugations; irregular stems for decir,
hacer, poder, poner, querer, saber, salir, tener, venir. Two extended pragmatic uses: (1) future
plan/promise/intention carrying an implication of doubt; (2) future of approximation/supposition
(*¿Qué horas serán?* "I wonder what time it is" — **[REGIONAL]** Spain says *¿Qué hora será?*
singular).

### Present tense with future meaning (Unit 3, p.35)
Used to convey immediacy/certainty about a near-future action (*el programa lo publicamos mañana*
= "will be published tomorrow"), distinct from the present's ordinary habitual-action use.

### Por — motive/cause (Unit 3, p.40)
One core use of `por` introduced here (motive/cause/reason); later contrasted systematically with
`para` in Unit 10 (see below).

### Pluperfect subjunctive + conditional perfect (Unit 4, p.45)
Pluperfect subjunctive = imperfect subjunctive of `haber` + past participle (hubiera/hubiese
viajado). Conditional perfect = conditional of `haber` + past participle (habría comprado). Used
together for a past condition that was not fulfilled and can no longer be fulfilled. **Register
note:** in spoken language, speakers commonly substitute the subjunctive for the conditional in
both clauses of this construction (*si hubieras venido, hubieras comido* instead of the
prescriptive *habrías comido*) — a genuine colloquial simplification worth tracking for slang-
mechanics purposes, since it collapses two distinct verb forms into one in informal registers.

### Seguir + gerund (Unit 4, p.51)
Indicates an action continuing/still happening (*sigo asistiendo a clases* = "I'm still
attending").

### Relative clauses (Unit 5, p.60)
`que` (that/which/who) is the default relative pronoun; `quien` (more formal, used for people)
and `cuyo` (whose, agrees in gender/number with the following noun) are alternatives. Restrictive
clauses limit the antecedent's reference; non-restrictive clauses (marked with a preceding comma)
do not.

### Conjunctions: o/y/mas/pero/sino (Unit 5, p.63)
`y`→`e` before a word starting with an i-sound; `o`→`u` before a word starting with an o-sound.
`mas` = "but," synonymous with `pero` but restricted to formal/poetic register — a genuine
register-marked conjunction, rare in speech.

### Conjunctions: como/porque/pues/ya que/puesto que (Unit 5, p.64)
All convey cause; distinguished mainly by formality and position rather than meaning.

### Aunque / a pesar de que (Unit 5, p.66)
Convey concession ("although" / "despite the fact that"). Colloquial variant `y eso que` ("despite
the fact that") is restricted to mid-sentence position and always takes the indicative, never the
subjunctive — a fixed colloquial-register exception worth flagging.

### Con tal de que / siempre que (Unit 5, p.67)
Convey precondition ("provided that/as long as"). `con tal de que` commonly abbreviates to `con
tal que`; `siempre que` is interchangeable with `siempre y cuando`.

### Passive construction (Unit 7, p.87)
`ser` + past participle, agreeing in gender/number with the affected subject. Full paradigm shown
across present, preterite, conditional, pluperfect, conditional perfect, imperfect, future, and
future perfect for *ser fotografiado*.

### Se instead of passive construction (Unit 7, p.88)
The impersonal `se` construction (*se inauguró la sucursal*) is noted as the more common spoken-
register alternative to the full passive.

### More impersonal constructions (Unit 7, p.89)
Generic `tú` (like English "you," addressing people in general); generic `uno` (avoids naming
`yo`/`nosotros` directly); third-person-plural verb with unspecified subject (*dicen que...* =
"they say that...").

### Negation (Unit 8, p.103)
`no` precedes the negated element; also used in tag questions (*¿no?*). **Double-negative rule:**
every negatable element in a Spanish negative sentence must itself be negated (unlike English) —
*ni...ni*, *no...tampoco*, etc. `nada` can function as an intensifier ("not at all"), not just
"nothing." `ni` is typically repeated before each negated element. `tampoco` ("neither/not...
either") requires a double-negative frame. `nunca` vs. `jamás` — both "never," but `jamás` is more
emphatic ("never ever"). `ninguno` apocopates to `ningún`/`ninguna` before a following noun,
agreeing in gender.

### Voseo (Unit 8, p.110) — [REGISTER/DIALECT FLAGSHIP]
The use of `vos` instead of `tú` as the second-person-singular informal pronoun. Attested in
Argentina, Uruguay, Paraguay, Chile, and parts of Central America and southern Mexico — presented
as a genuine parallel grammatical paradigm, not merely a lexical variant. Distinct verb forms
exist only with `vos`: present indicative (estudiás, comés, escribís — note the lack of the
diphthongization/stem-change found in the corresponding `tú` forms of irregular verbs), imperative
(estudiá, comé, escribí), present subjunctive (estudiés, comás, vivás). The verb `ser` has its own
distinct voseo present-indicative form, `sos` (*vos sos* = "you are"), rather than a simple
suffix change. This is the single highest-value dialectal-grammar finding in this book for
mechanics-analysis purposes — a fully worked, distinct pronoun-and-conjugation paradigm running
parallel to the standard `tú` system, not an inflectional footnote.

### Nomás (Unit 8, p.109) — [REGISTER]
A colloquial Latin American particle with several distinct senses depending on position and
context: "only" (*por esta semana nomás*), "as soon as" (*nomás que la vi, se fue corriendo*), and
a bare intensifying/reassuring filler after an imperative or short answer (*aquicito nomás* =
"right there," *siéntese nomás* = "please, sit down"). This particle recurs across Units 1, 2, 6,
and 8, making it one of the book's most persistent colloquial-register markers.

### More negative words: nada, nadie, nunca/jamás, ninguno (Unit 8, p.112)
Expanded treatment of the negation set introduced earlier in the same unit — `nada`/`nadie` can
mean either "nothing/nobody" (with `no`) or "anything/anybody" (in a question or after certain
verbs), `ninguno` agreeing in gender and apocopating before a masculine singular noun.

### Stress and written accent — agudas/graves/esdrújulas (Unit 9, p.119, restated in the Grammar
reference appendix p.143)
Agudas: stress on the last syllable, written accent required only if the word ends in a vowel, -n,
or -s. Graves/llanas: stress on the penultimate syllable, written accent required only if the word
ends in a consonant other than -n/-s. Esdrújulas: stress before the penultimate syllable, always
written with an accent.

### Acentuación diacrítica (Unit 9, p.123) — [full paired set]
A diacritic accent distinguishes otherwise-identical monosyllables by grammatical function: `mí`
(pronoun, "me") vs. `mi` (possessive, "my"); `tú` (pronoun, "you") vs. `tu` (possessive, "your");
`él` (pronoun, "he") vs. `el` (masculine definite article); `sí` (affirmative particle "yes," also
reflexive-pronoun-with-preposition use) vs. `si` (conditional "if"); `sé` (1sg. present indicative
of `saber`, "I know" — also imperative of `ser`) vs. `se` (personal/reflexive pronoun); `sólo`
("only") vs. `solo` ("alone"); `aún` ("still, not yet") vs. `aun` ("even, including"). **Live norm
dispute flagged explicitly by the source:** a spelling-reform ruling states that `sólo` should only
carry the accent when needed to avoid ambiguity, but the text notes many speakers still apply the
older blanket distinction — worth tracking as an example of a prescriptive/descriptive split within
the standard language itself, not just a LatAm/Spain contrast.

### Prepositions por and para, contrasted (Unit 10, p.133)
`para`: purpose/destination; "by" a deadline; "for" a future span of time. `por`: cause/reason/
motive; agent in a passive construction ("by"); substitution ("in exchange for/instead of");
distributive "per"; "to fetch" (*ir por algo*); manner; "throughout/all over" (place); "through."
The unit supplies a substantial set of fixed colloquial `por`-phrases (por casualidad, por un
pelo, por si acaso, por gusto, por temor, por orden alfabético, por lo pronto, por si fuera poco,
por correo) that function as idioms in their own right rather than compositional preposition use.

### Grammar reference appendix (pp. 143–150) — consolidated paradigm reference
This appendix restates the stress/accent rules (redundant with Unit 9, not repeated) and then adds
foundational paradigm material not given in narrative form elsewhere in the book:
- **Noun gender**: typical masculine endings -o/-l/-r/-y; typical feminine endings -a/-ión/-dad/
  -tad/-tud/-itis; regular masculine→feminine formation by adding -a to a consonant-ending noun
  (vendedor→vendedora); a small set of nouns take different meanings under each gender (*el
  capital* "capital, money" vs. *la capital* "capital city"; *el policía* "police officer" vs. *la
  policía* "the police force").
- **Plural formation**: unstressed-vowel or stressed-e nouns add -s; consonant/-y/other-stressed-
  vowel nouns add -es (exceptions: mamá→mamás, papá→papás); nouns ending in -z change z→c before
  -es (luz→luces).
- **Articles**: definite (el/la/los/las) and indefinite (un/una/unos/unas), agreeing in gender/
  number; the masculine `el` substitutes for `la` before a singular feminine noun beginning with
  stressed a-/ha- (*el agua*, *el haba*), despite the noun remaining grammatically feminine. The
  neuter article `lo` never precedes a noun — it forms abstract nouns from an adjective or past
  participle (*lo interesante*, *lo dicho*).
- **Possessive adjectives**: mi/tu/su/nuestro-nuestra/su; only `nuestro` has a distinct feminine
  form.
- **Personal pronouns**: full table (yo/tú/usted/él/ella; nosotros/nosotras/ustedes/ellos/ellas) —
  the appendix notes these are used far less often than their English equivalents, mainly reserved
  for emphasis or disambiguation.
- **Full regular-verb paradigm reference** across persons for: present indicative; present perfect
  (haber + past participle, -ar→-ado, -er/-ir→-ido, with irregular participles abrir→abierto,
  decir→dicho, escribir→escrito, hacer→hecho, morir→muerto, poner→puesto, romper→roto, ver→visto,
  volver→vuelto); present perfect continuous (haber + past participle + gerund); preterite
  (regular, plus irregular hacer/ir/tener/dar); future (regular, plus irregular decir/hacer/salir/
  tener/venir); imperfect indicative; imperfect continuous (imperfect of estar + gerund); present
  subjunctive; conditional (regular, plus irregular decir/haber/hacer/poder/poner/querer/salir/
  tener/venir).

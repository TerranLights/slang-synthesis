## Grammar points

### The Spanish alphabet and its pronunciation

Spanish has 30 letters (26 shared with English plus *ch*, *ll*, *ñ*, *rr*, each traditionally
counted as its own letter). Each vowel has exactly one sound (no English-style long/short/schwa
variation), and consonants are either always silent or always pronounced — no exceptions the way
English *gh* or silent *k* behave. *ll* is pronounced as *y* in Latin America but *j* in Spain —
the book's one explicit dialectal split in this section (p. 2–3).

### Stress and accent marks

Three rules govern stress placement, in contrast with English's lack of firm rules (illustrated via
English heteronyms like *record*/*record*, p. 3–4):
1. A word ending in a vowel, *n*, or *s* stresses the second-to-last syllable (*casa*, *hablan*).
2. A word ending in any other consonant stresses the final syllable (*hablar*, *ciudad*).
3. Any exception to rules 1–2 is marked with a written accent (´), which can also distinguish
   otherwise-identical one-syllable words by meaning alone (*el* "the" vs. *él* "he"; *tu* "your"
   vs. *tú* "you") — accents are never decorative or optional (p. 4–5).

### Plurals

Four pluralization rules, all more regular than English's: (1) vowel-final words add *-s* (*la
casa* → *las casas*); (2) consonant-final words add *-es* (*el árbol* → *los árboles*); (3) a word
ending in an accented vowel + consonant drops the accent when *-es* is added (*el camión* → *los
camiones*); (4) a word ending in *-z* changes *z* → *c* before adding *-es* (*la luz* → *las luces*)
(p. 5–6).

### Diphthongs

A diphthong forms when a "soft" vowel (*i*, *u*) combines with a "hard" vowel (*a*, *e*, *o*) or
another soft vowel, collapsing into a single syllable (*bien*, *bueno*). Two hard vowels together
stay as two separate syllables (*paseo*, *cacao*). A written accent on the soft vowel breaks the
diphthong back into two syllables regardless (*país*, *día*) (p. 6–7).

### Punctuation marks

Period, comma, colon, semicolon, and hyphen work as in English. Questions and exclamations get an
inverted mark (¿ ¡) at the start in addition to the standard mark at the end. Large numbers and
decimals invert English convention: Spanish uses a period where English uses a comma for thousands
groupings, and a comma where English uses a decimal point (English $5,000.00 = Spanish
$5.000,00). Quotation marks may be "  " or the classical « ». Spanish has no apostrophe at all —
English possessive 's and contractions are rendered with *de* + noun instead (*el coche de
Horacio* "Horatio's car") (p. 7–8).

### Cognates

Four categories, in descending order of similarity to English: pure cognates (identical spelling/
meaning), near cognates (similar spelling), false cognates/*falsos amigos* (similar spelling, wrong
meaning), and adopted words (borrowed unchanged, often food terms). English derives roughly 60% of
its vocabulary from Latin/French, 25% Germanic, 5% Greek — which the book gives as the reason
Spanish/English near-cognates are so abundant (p. 8–9).

### A note on suffixes

The book previews a running theme: many near-cognates differ from their English counterparts only
in a predictable suffix swap (Spanish *-ción* ↔ English *-tion*, *-ismo* ↔ *-ism*, *-dad* ↔ *-ty*,
etc.), tabulated for both common noun endings (15 pairs, e.g. *-ario*↔*-ary*, *-ista*↔*-ist*) and
adjective endings (11 pairs, e.g. *-able*↔*-able*, *-oz*↔*-ocious*) at p. 9–10. This is flagged here
as a genuine derivational-morphology pattern relevant to later mechanics analysis, not just a study
tip — a systematic suffix correspondence like this is exactly the kind of structural regularity slang
coinage could plausibly exploit or subvert.

## Non-redundant-supplement scope decision (read this before the tables)

This is the **third** independently-authored "Practice Makes Perfect" Spanish grammar in
`established/`, after `005_practice_makes_perfect_basic_spanish_grammar.md` (Dorothy Richmond,
2009, beginner-level, Lessons 1–50) and `006_practice_makes_perfect_advanced_spanish_grammar.md`
(Rogelio Alonso Vallecillos, 2008, Units 1–12, advanced/idiomatic). This book is by a **third,
different author** (Gilda Nissenberg) and is a 26-unit, more systematically-sequenced grammar
covering the full verb-tense system, all major word classes, and closed-class grammar — much of
which is confirmed heavy overlap with 005/006, per a keyword check run against both files before
vision-reading began (`grep` for reflexive/progressive/perfect-tense/subjunctive/command/

perfect, pluperfect subjunctive, cardinal/ordinal numbers as a system, and a full commands
paradigm — confirming these as genuine gaps before committing vision-reading effort to them).

Given the 386-page/no-text-layer scale of this source, vision-reading was **not applied
exhaustively across all 26 units**. Applying the spec's non-redundant-supplement pattern
aggressively, effort was concentrated on the units confirmed to be genuinely new or substantially
deeper than 005/006, and the remaining units were explicitly scoped out rather than silently
skipped:

## Grammar points

### Reflexive verbs: four distinct meaning-classes (Unit 8, pp. 81–105)

Beyond the basic "self-directed action" sense (daily-routine verbs: bañarse, vestirse, etc.), the
book distinguishes: (1) **reciprocal** reflexives, where se/nos means "each other," not "oneself"
(nos escribimos = we write each other); (2) **change-of-state** reflexives expressing an emotional
or physical shift (enojarse, aburrirse); (3) reflexive/non-reflexive **verb pairs whose core
meaning shifts**, not just gains reflexivity (ir "to go" vs. irse "to leave"; llevar "to carry" vs.
llevarse "to get along"; parecer "to seem" vs. parecerse a "to resemble"); (4) reflexive
constructions of **"to become"** that are not interchangeable: ponerse (sudden, involuntary
emotional/physical change), hacerse (achieved through effort or a gradual voluntary process),
llegar a ser (a long-term, ultimate change of state/identity), and volverse (a sudden, often
negative or unexpected change) — a four-way distinction with no single English equivalent.

### Progressive tenses: verbs of motion as aspectually distinct auxiliaries (Unit 9, pp. 95–115)

Beyond the standard estar + gerundio (happening right now), the book identifies four other verbs
that form progressive-like constructions with distinct aspectual meaning: seguir/continuar +
gerundio (an action that continues), andar + gerundio (wandering/going around doing something,
often colloquial), ir + gerundio (a gradual, incremental buildup — contrasted explicitly with
estar's "right now" reading), and venir + gerundio (an action that has been building toward the
present moment). Irregular gerunds insert -y- for vowel-final stems (leyendo, oyendo, trayendo,
yendo) and stem-changing -ir verbs keep their vowel change in the gerund (pidiendo, durmiendo,
sintiendo) rather than the present-tense pattern.

### The compound (perfect) tense system (Units 10–11, pp. 104–135)

Spanish's full compound-tense inventory, built from haber + past participle across every simple
tense: present perfect (he hablado), past perfect/pluperfect (había hablado), the now-rare literary
preterit perfect (hube hablado, confined to formal narration after apenas/en cuanto/después que and
replaced by the simple preterit in speech), future perfect (habré hablado — including its
"conjecture about the past" evidential use, roughly "must have"), and conditional perfect (habría
hablado — likewise doubling as past-relative conjecture). Object/reflexive pronouns are never split
from the participle in any compound tense; they always precede the conjugated form of haber.
Irregular past participles form a closed, memorizable list (abierto, cubierto, dicho, escrito,
hecho, muerto, puesto, resuelto, roto, visto, vuelto).

### The subjunctive mood — present, adverb/relative-clause uses, and past tenses (Units 13–15, pp. 131–174)

**This is the flagship gap this dispatch was assigned to find**, and the largest structural feature
of Spanish absent from both prior established/ Spanish files. Key points:

- **Formation**: present subjunctive is built from the yo-form of the present indicative (drop -o,
  add the "opposite vowel" set of endings: -ar verbs take e-endings, -er/-ir verbs take a-endings).
  Six verbs have irregular subjunctive stems: sea, esté, vaya, sepa, dé, haya.
- **Noun-clause triggers** (a WEIRDO-shaped list, though the book doesn't use that mnemonic):
  volition (querer que), emotion (alegrarse de que, sentir que), doubt/denial (dudar que, no creer
  que, negar que), and impersonal expressions (es necesario/importante/posible que). Verbs of
  communication split on subjunctive vs. indicative depending on whether they report a fact
  (indicative) or convey an order/influence (subjunctive) — e.g. decir que.
- **Adverb-clause conjunctions** requiring the subjunctive form a closed list: a condición de que, a
  fin de que, a menos que, antes (de) que, con tal que, en caso de que, para que, sin que. A second
  group (cuando, mientras que, hasta que, en cuanto, tan pronto como) takes the subjunctive only
  when the adverb clause refers to a future/not-yet-realized action; the same conjunctions take the
  indicative for habitual or already-completed actions — a real subjunctive/indicative minimal-pair
  distinction the book illustrates directly (Leemos las revistas cuando las compro, indicative,
  habitual, vs. a subjunctive equivalent for a one-off future event).
- **Adjective (relative) clauses**: the subjunctive is triggered when the antecedent is vague,
  indefinite, or not confirmed to exist (Buscamos una oficina que tenga vista al mar — and the
  personal a is dropped from the main-clause direct object in this case, since the sought person/
  thing isn't confirmed real); the indicative is used when the antecedent is real/definite (and the
  personal a is retained); a negative antecedent (nada, nadie, ninguno) also triggers the
  subjunctive.
- **Other/idiomatic uses**: tal vez/quizás/acaso + subjunctive when the speaker judges the statement
  improbable, vs. + indicative when judged probable (a genuine mood-as-degree-of-certainty system,
  not a fixed rule per adverb); que yo sepa ("as far as I know") as a subjunctive hedge; por (muy/
  mucho) que + subjunctive ("no matter how (much)"); cuandoquiera/dondequiera que ("-ever"
  constructions); sea como sea ("be that as it may").
- **Imperfect subjunctive** formation: take the 3rd-person plural preterit, drop -ron, add either
  the -ra set (hablara, pudiera, sintiera) or the less-common alternate -se set (hablase, pudiese,
  sintiese) — both sets carry identical meaning, and irregular preterit stems carry through
  unchanged. Triggered when the main-clause verb is imperfect, preterit, pluperfect, or conditional
  (the "sequence of tenses" principle).
- **Pluperfect subjunctive** (hubiera/hubiese + participle): the past-subjunctive equivalent of the
  pluperfect indicative, for an action prior to another past action under a past-tense subjunctive
  trigger.
- **Contrary-to-fact conditionals**: a three-way real/present-hypothetical/past-hypothetical system
  — real condition clauses use the indicative in both halves (si ganas..., puedes...); a
  present/future contrary-to-fact condition uses imperfect subjunctive in the si-clause and the
  conditional in the result clause (si ganaras..., podrías...); a past contrary-to-fact condition
  uses pluperfect subjunctive + conditional perfect (si hubieras ganado..., habrías ahorrado...). The
  -ra imperfect/pluperfect subjunctive forms can also substitute directly for the conditional/
  conditional perfect in the result clause. como si ("as if") always triggers the subjunctive.

### The full command (imperative) paradigm (Unit 16, pp. 175–189)

A more complete, systematically-organized command system than 006's narrow "polite request" forms:
Ud./Uds. commands are formed from the present subjunctive (with the same -gar/-ger,-gir/-guir/
-cer,-cir/-car/-zar spelling-change rules that apply throughout the subjunctive); regular tú
affirmative commands use the 3rd-person-singular present indicative form, with a small closed set
of irregulars (di, haz, ve, pon, sal, and by the same pattern ten/ven/sé, though only the first five
appeared on the specific pages read); negative tú commands always use the present subjunctive tú
form, and **no irregular negative tú forms exist** — the regular subjunctive-derived rule holds
even for verbs with an irregular affirmative form; vosotros affirmative commands (infinitive minus
-r, plus -d) are explicitly marked by the book as used "primarily in Spain (except the Canary
Islands)," with the rest of the Spanish-speaking world substituting the Uds. form for familiar
plural commands — this book's one explicit regional/dialectal note; nosotros ("let's") commands use
either the present subjunctive nosotros form or vamos a + infinitive (the latter more common in
affirmative); and the bare infinitive substitutes for a command in impersonal contexts — recipes and
public signage (No fumar).

### Indefinite/negative word system and pero vs. sino (Unit 24, pp. 293–300)

alguien/nadie are restricted to people; algo/nada are restricted to things or ideas (and can also
function as degree adverbs: algo mejor = "somewhat better," no me gusta nada = "I don't like it at
all"); algún(o)/ningún(o) can refer to either people or things and take the standard masculine-
singular apocope before a noun (algún día, ningún profesor). Spanish freely stacks two or three
negative words in a single sentence, provided the verb itself is also preceded by no whenever a
negative word follows it (Nadie viene nunca = "No one ever comes"). Negative words also appear in
affirmative-form questions to add rhetorical emphasis when a negative answer is expected. Beyond
006's brief pero/sino note, this book clarifies sino as an adversative conjunction usable only after
a negative statement, either contrasting a whole idea ("not X but rather Y") or restricting meaning
to "only" (no espera sino que... = "he only wants that..."), with sino que required when a full
clause (not just a phrase) follows.

### The Spanish number system: cardinals, telling time, ordinals, fractions, multiples (Unit 26, pp. 308–316)

Cardinal numbers 16–29 have two accepted spellings (dieciséis / diez y seis); cien (not ciento)
directly precedes mil or millones; millón/millones requires de before a following noun (cien
millones de dólares). Telling time uses the singular es exclusively for la una and the plural son
for every other hour, with y/menos marking minutes past/to the hour and media/cuarto for half/
quarter hours. Ordinal numbers run primero through décimo; cardinal numbers substitute for ordinals
above the tenth in ordinary usage (el Congreso 15, not decimoquinto); primero and tercero drop
their final -o (primer, tercer) before a masculine singular noun, mirroring the familiar
bueno/malo/grande apocope pattern from adjective placement. Fractions use the masculine ordinal
form except for the two irregulars medio ("half") and tercio ("a third"), and take the suffix -avo
for fractions finer than a tenth (un doceavo = "a twelfth"). Multiple numbers (doble, triple,
cuádruple, quíntuple) are mostly English-Spanish cognates.

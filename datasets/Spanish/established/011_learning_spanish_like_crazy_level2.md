## Grammar points

### Gendered agreement in predicate-adjective slang

Several slang/colloquial adjectives introduced in these lessons inflect for the gender of the person
described exactly like standard adjectives (agüitado/agüitada "down," enojado/enojada "angry,"
enamorado/enamorada "in love," cochino/cochina "dirty," moreno/morena, blanquito/blanquita), used
productively with estar/ser. The register is informal but the morphology is fully regular fusional
agreement — slang status here is lexical (word choice), not morphological (no special slang
inflection pattern). (Bonus Slang Lesson A, pp. 1-6; Bonus Slang Lesson B, pp. 1-4.)

### Apocope/clipping as a productive slang-formation pattern for kinship terms of address

"Mano"/"mana" (< hermano/hermana), "manita" (< hermanita), "compa" (< compadre), and "'ta bien"/
"'ta bueno" (< está bien/está bueno) all share a pattern: informal register is signaled by clipping
the initial syllable(s) of a standard word, with the truncated form re-lexicalized as its own fixed
slang item rather than treated as casual elision on the fly. "No'mbre" (< no, hombre) shows the same
mechanism fusing two words into one clipped interjection. This recurring apocope pattern is worth
flagging for the later mechanics-analysis phase as a distinct Spanish slang-derivation mechanism
(clipping/word-fusion), separate from the semantic-repurposing pattern below. (Bonus Slang Lesson B,
pp. 4-5.)

### Semantic repurposing of literal-sense words into slang function words

Several items are ordinary nouns whose literal, unrelated meaning has been repurposed for a slang
function distinct from that literal sense, flagged explicitly by the instructor in each case:
"naranjas" (oranges → "no"), "cochino" (pig → "dirty," of anything), "¿Qué onda?" (lit. "what wave"
→ "what's up"), "pedo" (fart → drunkenness), "estar de goma" (lit. "to be made of rubber" →
hungover), "tener resaca" (lit. "to have undertow" → hungover). This is a second distinct
slang-derivation mechanism (semantic extension/metaphor) worth tracking separately from the
apocope/clipping pattern above for the mechanics-analysis phase. (Bonus Slang Lesson A, pp. 4-6;
Bonus Slang Lesson B, p. 4.)

### Register-graded near-synonyms: querer vs. amar

The source explicitly teaches "te quiero" (lit. "I want you") as a lower-commitment romantic
declaration than "te amo," used for a relationship that "has not yet reached a more serious stage."
This is a native-speaker-taught register/pragmatics distinction rather than a simple synonym pair —
the two verbs (querer, amar) are not freely interchangeable in this romantic-declaration context.
(Bonus Slang Lesson A, p. 2.)

### Explicit regional variation flagged by the source itself

The source itself marks several items as regionally restricted rather than pan-Hispanic, which the
extraction spec calls out as high-value content: "guey" and "simón" (Mexico), "guagua" (Puerto Rico/
Dominican Republic/Cuba, vs. camión/autobús/bus elsewhere), "cruda"-family hangover terms (Mexico) vs.
"estar enguayabado" (Colombia), and "chulo" carrying an additional Venezuela-specific sense beyond its
general "good-looking" meaning. (Bonus Slang Lesson A, pp. 1, 5; Bonus Slang Lesson B, pp. 1, 2.)

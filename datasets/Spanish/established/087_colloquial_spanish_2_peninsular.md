## Non-redundant-supplement scope

**This file does NOT re-extract the book's basic grammar.** Ser/estar/hay, the present perfect
vs. preterite vs. imperfect contrast, reflexive verbs, weather expressions with `hacer`, basic
prepositions (a/de/en/con), object-pronoun placement with gerunds/infinitives, qué vs. cuál,
adjective position, and the present/past subjunctive mood itself are all already extensively
covered elsewhere in this corpus (001, 002, 005, 006, 074, 077, etc.) and this book's treatment of
them does not differ from the general-Spanish version already established. What follows is
specifically the material that is either (a) genuinely Peninsular-marked — a word, construction,
or institution either not used or used differently in Latin America — or (b) a distinctive idiom/
cultural item this book introduces that the rest of the corpus had not yet captured. Sampling was
not literally exhaustive across all 252 pages (a full pass of every unit's dialogue/vocabulary box
was not performed line-by-line past Unit 3), but every unit's dedicated "Vocabulary" box was
checked, plus the full Grammar reference appendix, specifically for this kind of content.

## Peninsular-Spanish findings worth flagging (existing corpus is LatAm-heavy)

1. **The `vosotros`/`vosotras` paradigm is fully conjugated across every tense in this book's
   Grammar reference appendix** (present, preterite, imperfect, present perfect, pluperfect,
   present continuous, future, future perfect, conditional, conditional perfect, imperative) —
   see the dedicated grammar point below. This is the single clearest grammatical marker
   separating Peninsular from Latin American Spanish (Latin America uses `ustedes` for 2nd-person
   plural in both formal and informal registers, with vestigial/regional exceptions; Peninsular
   Spanish keeps `vosotros/as` for informal 2nd-person plural and reserves `ustedes` for formal).
   No prior Spanish `established/` file had tabulated this full paradigm.
2. **`vale`** is used throughout the book's dialogues as a discourse-final tag meaning
   "OK?/alright?" or a standalone "OK" — a textbook Peninsular colloquialism; Latin American
   Spanish favors `¿listo?`/`¿de acuerdo?`/`¿okay?` instead (though `vale` is spreading via media).
3. **Spain's immigration-law and consumer-protection bureaucracy vocabulary** (work-permit types
   A–D, `Oficina de Extranjeros`, `Delegación`/`Subdelegación del Gobierno`, `hojas de
   reclamaciones`, `Servicio Territorial de Turismo`) is specific to the Spanish state apparatus
   and has no equivalent institutional vocabulary already in this corpus's Latin-American sources.
4. **Spain's two-surname-only (no maternal-surname-skip) naming convention and its explicit
   first-surname/second-surname terminology** (`apellido` 1/2 = father's/mother's) is stated
   explicitly in Unit 1 and is a genuinely cultural-grammatical item.
5. **Spain's political-party vocabulary** (PSOE, PP, IU) and the recurring-elsewhere-in-the-corpus
   subjunctive triggers (`ojalá`, `parece mentira que`, `no creo que`) appear here anchored to
   Spain's own post-Franco political history (the `transición pacífica`) rather than any Latin
   American country's politics — new real-world anchoring, not a new grammar point.
6. **Carnaval de Cádiz's performance-group vocabulary** (`chirigotas`, `comparsas`, `coros`,
   `cuartetos`) is a tight, culturally specific register cluster with no Latin American equivalent
   in this corpus (Latin American carnival vocabulary, e.g. Caribbean carnival terms, would be a
   wholly different register cluster — not covered by this book either).

## Grammar points

### `vosotros`/`vosotras` — full paradigm (the defining Peninsular/Latin-American split)

This book's Grammar reference appendix (pp. 201–215) tabulates the complete `vosotros/vosotras`
conjugation across every indicative and conditional tense, plus the imperative, for all three
regular conjugation classes (`hablar`/`beber`/`vivir`):

- Present: habláis / bebéis / vivís
- Preterite: hablasteis / bebisteis / vivisteis
- Imperfect: hablabais / bebíais / vivíais
- Present perfect: habéis hablado / habéis bebido / habéis vivido
- Pluperfect: habíais hablado / habíais bebido / habíais vivido
- Present continuous: estáis hablando / estáis bebiendo / estáis viviendo
- Future: hablaréis / beberéis / viviréis
- Future perfect: habréis hablado / habréis bebido / habréis vivido
- Conditional: hablaríais / beberíais / viviríais
- Conditional perfect: habríais hablado / habríais bebido / habríais vivido
- Affirmative imperative: trabajad, bebed, escribid (infinitive minus final `-r`, plus `-d`)
- Negative imperative: `no` + 2nd person plural present subjunctive

The pronoun table in the same appendix also confirms the full `vosotros/as` object/prepositional/
reflexive/possessive set (`os`, `vosotros/as` (prepositional), `os` (reflexive),
`vuestro/a/os/as`). This is the single most load-bearing grammatical fact distinguishing
Peninsular from (most) Latin American Spanish, which uses `ustedes` (with the 3rd-person-plural
verb paradigm) for 2nd-person plural in both formal and informal registers. No prior file in this
language's `established/` corpus tabulates the `vosotros` paradigm this completely; it is included
here in full precisely because this is the first genuinely Peninsular-sourced textbook in the
corpus and this is exactly the grammatical fact a LatAm-trained learner (or this project's
downstream slang-synthesis pipeline) would most need from a Peninsular-specific source.

### Two-surname naming convention

Stated explicitly in Unit 1's opening dialogue: Spanish nationals conventionally carry two
surnames, the first (`apellido 1`) inherited from the father and the second (`apellido 2`) from
the mother — illustrated with the author's own name, Untza **Otaola** (father's) **Alday**
(mother's), and again in the Unit 12 electoral-register form (Apellido 1: AGUIRRE; Apellido 2:
LÓPEZ). This is a cultural-administrative convention rather than a verb/syntax rule, but it is
load-bearing for any onomastic or bureaucratic-register slang-derivation work downstream.

### `qué`/`cuál` plus impersonal `se` for asking general questions

Not itself new (qué/cuál distinction is already covered elsewhere), but this book's specific
construction pattern — adding `se` directly before the verb to form an impersonal question — is
worth noting as a clean, explicit illustration: "¿Qué se celebra el 21 de septiembre?" (What is
celebrated on 21 September?), "¿Qué se come durante las fiestas?" (What does one eat during the
festival?). Useful as a model sentence pattern for any impersonal-register slang/register
synthesis.

## Relationship to other Spanish `established/` files

This file is a deliberate, register/dialect-focused supplement, not a competitor extraction of
001/002's Latin-American "Colloquial Spanish" series — all three books share a series format
(Dialogue → Vocabulary → Language points → Exercises → Grammar reference appendix) but cover
different national varieties. Where 002 already flags explicit LatAm-vs-Spain lexical contrasts
from the Latin American side (e.g. `plata` vs. `dinero`, `de turno` vs. `de guardia`), this file
supplies the missing other half: genuine Peninsular-sourced content, anchored in Spain's own
institutions, history, and regional culture (Basque Country, La Rioja, Cádiz) rather than treated
as a footnote to a Latin-American-centered course.

## Grammar points

### The five subjunctive-triggering contexts (chapter's own organizing structure)

The chapter states up front that the present subjunctive "cannot exist alone" — it is always caused
by some other element in the sentence — and organizes every trigger into five categories (p. 206),
each elaborated below with its own closed or illustrative list:

**1. Certain impersonal expressions** (p. 212) — any of the following, followed by **que** +
subjunctive, obligatorily (not optional) triggers the subjunctive in the dependent clause: es bueno
(que), es difícil (que), es dudoso (que), es fácil (que), es imposible (que), es importante (que), es
malo (que), es mejor (que), es necesario (que), es posible (que), es probable (que), es preciso (que)
("it is extremely necessary that"), es una lástima (que) ("it is a pity that"), es urgente (que).
Note: the present subjunctive here often carries an implied *future* sense in translation (**Es
posible que ella venga mañana** — "Is it possible that she will come tomorrow?"). If there is only
one subject overall (no dependent clause), these same expressions take a plain infinitive instead
(**Es importante comer bien** — "It is important to eat well") — no subjunctive is triggered.

**2. Certain verbs**, split into three functional subgroups (pp. 214-218):
   - *Expressing hope, happiness, sadness, or regret (about another person's action)*: alegrarse de,
     esperar, estar contento de, estar triste de, gustarle a uno, sentir, tener miedo de/temer.
   - *Expressing orders, requests, or advice*: aconsejar, decir (only when used to *order*, not to
     relate a fact — contrast **José nos dice que el tren viene** [indicative, a fact] vs. **Yo te
     digo que vayas** [subjunctive, a command]), dejar, insistir en, pedir, permitir, prohibir,
     mandar, sugerir. Four of these (**dejar, permitir, prohibir, mandar**) can alternatively take a
     plain infinitive with no meaning change: *Les dejo que entren* = *Les dejo entrar*.
   - *Expressing doubt or uncertainty*: dudar, no creer, no pensar.
   - In every case: if the sentence has only one subject, no dependent clause/subjunctive is used —
     an infinitive follows instead (*Yo quiero cantar*, not *Yo quiero que yo cante*).

**3. Certain conjunctions**, split into two behaviors (pp. 220-224):
   - *Subjunctive only when the clauses have two different subjects* (an infinitive follows if one
     subject): a pesar de que ("in spite of"), antes de que ("before"), después de que ("after"), en
     caso de que ("in case"), hasta que ("until"), para que ("in order that/so that"), sin que
     ("without").
   - *Subjunctive always, regardless of one or two subjects*: a menos que ("unless"), luego que ("as
     soon as"), tan pronto como ("as soon as").
   - **Cuando** is a special case with three distinct behaviors depending on what it introduces:
     subjunctive when future time is implied (*Vamos a viajar cuando tengamos tiempo y dinero*);
     indicative when **cuando** introduces a question (*¿Cuándo vas a estar en casa?*); indicative
     when **cuando** introduces a repeated action or general present-tense statement (*Cuando hace
     frío, los niños juegan en la nieve*).
   - **Por más que** / **por mucho que** ("no matter how much") always take the subjunctive (*Por más
     que ella limpie, su casa está siempre desordenada*).
   - **Ojalá** ("would to God that"/"I hope," an interjection of Arabic origin) always takes the
     subjunctive: *Ojalá que ella tenga suerte*.
   - **Acaso**, **quizás**, **tal vez** ("perhaps") all take the subjunctive: *Quizás ellos me digan
     la verdad*.
   - **Aunque** ("although") takes the subjunctive specifically when the action has *not yet
     occurred* (*Aunque Pedro se quede esta noche, yo voy a salir* — the staying is still
     hypothetical from the speaker's vantage).
   - **Compounds of *-quiera*** (a distinct closed set functioning like English "-ever" compounds)
     always take the subjunctive: quienquiera ("whoever"), cualquiera ("whichever"), adondequiera
     ("wherever," directional), dondequiera ("wherever," locational) — *Dondequiera que estén ellos,
     los voy a buscar*.
   - **Como** takes the subjunctive specifically when it means "however" (*Ellas van a preparar la
     comida como tú quieras*).

**4. Certain dependent adjective clauses** (p. 223): the subjunctive is used when the noun described
in the main clause is indefinite, hypothetical, or explicitly nonexistent — i.e., the speaker doesn't
know whether the described entity actually exists: *Busco un apartamento que sea grande y barato*
("I am looking for an apartment that is big and cheap" — no particular apartment is known to exist
yet); *¿Conoce Ud. a alguien que sepa hablar alemán?*; *No hay nadie que siempre tenga razón*. This
is a genuinely distinct trigger category from the other four — it's about referential
(in)definiteness of a noun rather than any lexical or conjunctional cue, and is a useful mechanism
to flag for mechanics-analysis work on how Spanish grammaticalizes epistemic status (known-to-exist
vs. not) directly into verb morphology inside a relative clause.

**5. Certain fixed expressions** — covered above under conjunctions/adverbs (por más que, ojalá,
acaso/quizás/tal vez) since the book treats them in the same run of subsections; grouped here per the
book's own five-way top-level split for completeness of the taxonomy.

This five-way (plus the internal cuando/aunque/como sub-conditions) taxonomy is, taken as a whole,
one of the more systematically enumerated subjunctive-trigger catalogs encountered in this project's
Spanish sources so far — worth treating as a reference checklist for later mechanics-analysis work
on which subjunctive triggers, if any, survive or collapse in an invented in-universe register.

## Grammar points

### How Spanish business-letter register is constructed: usted/ustedes plus syntactic avoidance strategies
This book's introductory essay gives an explicit, multi-part account of *how* formality is built in
Spanish commercial correspondence, worth preserving in more detail than a typical grammar reference:
(1) the primary formality marker is **usted/ustedes** (never tú/vosotros, even informally, in this
register); (2) **subject pronouns referring to the parties in the transaction are avoided**, with
**impersonal se constructions** (third-person verb forms) substituted in their place; (3) using
**first-person plural** verb/pronoun forms rather than singular projects corporate responsibility and
greater formal weight, even from a single writer; (4) the **subjunctive mood is unusually widespread**
in this register compared to ordinary spoken Spanish; (5) writers favor a **more abstract, often
longer** vocabulary item even where a perfectly acceptable shorter/plainer alternative exists — a
deliberate register-raising choice, not a neutral synonym swap. The combined effect, per the authors,
is sentences that can read as either appropriately grave/precise or, at worst, "pomposity or
obscurity" — an explicit acknowledgment that this register has a known failure mode of over-formality.

### Explicit Spain vs. Latin America contrast in business-correspondence conventions
The book opens by noting real regional variation: Latin American business correspondence tends to
**retain older/more outmoded formulae** that have fallen out of current use in Spain itself, alongside
a separate, more recent **American-English influence** on Latin American commercial style. It also
notes a grammatical-register point with a geographic split: **usted is "yet more widespread" in Latin
America** than in Spain (where vosotros survives for informal plural "you," never in correspondence
either way), and ustedes is the universal plural "you" in Latin America where Spain still has a
vosotros/ustedes split in spoken register. The authors' own translation-methodology note adds that the
parallel English/Spanish letters in the book are **not word-for-word equivalents** specifically
because letter-writing conventions differ enough between Spain, Latin America, and the English-
speaking business world that a literal translation would misrepresent authentic usage in either
direction.

### Punctuation after the salutation: colon, not comma
A small but systematic point: Spanish business letters use a **colon** after the opening salutation
(*Estimada Sra Ruiz:*) where English convention uses a comma — a simple but easy-to-miss orthographic
transfer error for an English-dominant writer.

### Conditional + imperfect subjunctive as a marked "commercial correspondence" verb complex
A footnote on the book's very first sample letter flags *le agradeceríamos nos enviara* (conditional
"we would be grateful" + imperfect subjunctive "[if] you were to send") as "a complex verb structure
typical of Spanish commercial correspondence" — i.e. the authors themselves identify this
conditional-governing-a-subjunctive-clause pattern as a register marker of the genre, not just a
generic subjunctive-trigger instance. This is a genre-specific observation about *which* mood/tense
combinations cluster in formal written register, beyond the general subjunctive mechanics already
covered in this project's other Spanish grammar extractions.

### Capitalization rules for job-role nouns
Spanish capitalizes a role/position noun (*el Presidente, el Jefe de Ventas, la Directora*) specifically
when it refers to a **named, specific individual** currently holding that role, but leaves it lowercase
when the same noun is used generically (referring to the role/position in the abstract, or to an
unspecified/future holder of it) — a courtesy-marking capitalization rule with no real English
equivalent (English capitalizes job titles mainly by position-in-sentence/formality convention, not by
this specific-person-vs-role distinction).

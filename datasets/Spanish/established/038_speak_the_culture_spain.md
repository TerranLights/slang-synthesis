## Grammar points

*(Genuinely thin, per the genre — this book is a cultural reference, not a teaching grammar, and
teaches almost nothing in the way of morphology/syntax. The handful of usage-level/orthographic
points it does make are below.)*

### Inverted question/exclamation marks (¿ / ¡)

The book notes that spoken Spanish marks a question or exclamation purely through tone of voice —
there is no grammatical change to the sentence itself. Written Spanish therefore uses an inverted
`¿`/`¡` at the *start* of the sentence as well as the normal mark at the end, a convention mandated
by the Real Academia Española since the 18th century specifically to signal the speaker's intent to
the reader before they've finished reading the sentence. Example given: "¿We are going to the
beach?" (p.59). This is a genuine orthographic/pragmatic point about why the convention exists, not
just a style note.

### "Al-" prefix as an Arabic-loanword marker

The book makes an explicit, generalizable claim: most Spanish words beginning with "al-" (aldea,
alcoba, alcázar) have Moorish/Arabic ancestry, part of a roughly 4,000-word Arabic substrate in
Spanish vocabulary, mostly concentrated in food/drink terms (arroz, naranja, albaricoque) and
specialist/scientific vocabulary (p.56). Useful as a recognizable derivational-pattern heuristic,
albeit stated at the level of a popular-reference generalization rather than a systematic etymological survey.

### Castellano vs. "español" as the language's own name

The book flags a real naming distinction: Spain's language is called Castellano by most Latin
American speakers (reflecting Castile's role in the language's origin) even though people within
Spain itself more casually say "español." This isn't a grammar rule but a genuine sociolinguistic
naming convention worth tracking for register/regional-usage modeling (p.56).

## Sociocultural findings (most relevant to slang-synthesis)

**Regional/dialectal structure — the book's single highest-value contribution.** Spain is treated
throughout as a genuinely plural linguistic space, not a single-language nation with regional
"accents." The book explicitly names and distinguishes: three official minority languages (Galego,
Catalá, Euskera — each with its own TV channels, press, and school instruction, actively suppressed
under Franco and revived since the 1980s devolution); two additional unofficial regional languages
with named speaker-count estimates (Aragonese, ~10,000 speakers; Bable/Asturian, ~100,000 speakers);
and a separate tier of Castilian *dialects* distinguished by name from the minority languages
themselves — Andaluz, Murciano, Extremeño, and the contested case of Valenciano (explicitly flagged
by the book as a live dispute: speakers insist it's a distinct language, outsiders call it a Catalan
dialect). The book even gives one phonological generalization usable for register/dialect modeling:
Andaluz is characterized as systematically dropping letters and syllables, "shortening any word they
can" — a genuine (if informally stated) lenition/elision claim, contrasted with Valladolid as
supposedly having the "best" (most standard) Castilian enunciation. Basque is flagged as the one
language on the peninsula with non-Latin origin and "doesn't resemble any other tongue on the
continent." Silbo Gomero (a whistled language on La Gomera, Canary Islands) is a genuinely unusual
paralinguistic-channel finding, revived onto the school curriculum in 1999.

**Swearing/register norms.** The book states directly that "the Spanish swear a lot," that foul/
abusive language doesn't carry the same social weight it does in Anglophone cultures, that the
connotation is usually sexual (sometimes blasphemous) but "usually taken lightly" — and cites Camilo
José Cela's *Diccionario Secreto* (1968/71), a full two-volume dictionary of commonly used Spanish
obscenities published by a Nobel-winning novelist, as evidence of how culturally mainstream this
register is (p.59). This is a strong, explicit source claim about taboo-register social cost being
lower in Spain than in comparison cultures — directly useful for calibrating a slang-synthesis
taboo-tier weighting.

**Conversational style as register marker.** Interruption during conversation is explicitly framed
as *not* rude in Spanish social norms but as "proof of interest in the debate" — a pragmatics point
(not lexical) worth carrying into any dialogue-generation layer that models politeness/register.
Close physical distance, sustained eye contact, and loud/expressive delivery are all framed as
normal-register rather than aggressive, with the book explicitly warning that what reads as
"confrontational" to outsiders is "usually more about posturing than genuine anger" (p.60).

**Youth culture / taboo-adjacent findings.** The botellón/macrobotellón phenomenon (public group
drinking by young people, often early-teens-upward, organized via text/email/chat room) is the
book's clearest contemporary youth-slang-adjacent finding, complete with a literal gloss ("big
bottle") and an explicit note that it's banned in 8 of Spain's 17 autonomous regions yet persists —
genuinely useful as a register/era-marker (source published 2008, describes a then-current youth
practice). The rap/hip-hop scene section names three contemporary Spanish-language artists (Tote
King, Junior Miguez, Mala Rodríguez) without quoting lyrics, noted as popular "particularly among
urban youth" — flagging where period-appropriate urban slang in Spanish would likely cluster, even
though the book itself doesn't quote any.

**Class/socioeconomic coinages.** Two idiom-level findings worth flagging for a conlang slang
layer: *mileuristas* (educated people stuck earning only ~€1,000/month — a coinage responding to a
specific economic squeeze) and *cultura del pelotazo* (an idiom for the shady get-rich-quick dealing
culture of the 1980s Transición years, still carrying enough stigma that even legitimately wealthy
businesspeople are "eyed with suspicion"). Both are explicitly framed by the book as colloquial
coinages with real social charge, not neutral economic terminology.

**Media-register slang.** Three TV-register colloquialisms (telebasura "trash telly," caja tonta
"idiot box," culebrones "serpents" for soap operas) are each given with an explicit literal gloss by
the book itself — a clean, ready-made register/slang trio for the Spanish corpus.

**Gender/family register markers.** The Franco-era term permiso marital (the legal requirement for
a wife to obtain her husband's authorization before working or banking) and machista (explicitly
glossed by the book as "chauvinism") are both flagged as socially loaded terms tied to a specific
historical period (pre-1981 divorce legalization, pre-1985 abortion legalization) — useful
historical/register anchoring if the conlang slang layer ever needs a "patriarchal-era formal/legal
register" analog.

copyright 1951, 1953, 1989; this trade paperback edition, Broadway Books, 2001), pp. 371–740 of 740

radical-changing verbs II/III, `-uir` verbs, impersonal verbs, `deber`, passive voice, negatives, an
idiomatic-expressions-and-slang section (pp. 624–629), and the book's closing tests. A sibling
subagent covers pp. 1–370 as `003_*`; this file does not duplicate that range.

**Morpheme breakdown (fusional diminutive suffixation — noted per this language's fusional
typology).** Spanish's `-ito/-ita/-itos/-itas` diminutive is a productive bound suffix that both
marks size *and*, independently, carries an affective/endearment reading not present in its literal
gloss — worth flagging for later mechanics analysis since slang-formation by affective suffixation is
a cross-linguistically common source of register-shifted vocabulary:

> **abuelito** = abuelo ("grandfather") + -ito (diminutive/endearment suffix) — here the suffix marks
> *not* smallness (a grandfather isn't literally small) but affection/endearment, distinct from
> **casita** = casa ("house") + -ita, where the same suffix can carry either a literal
> smaller-than-average sense *or* an endearment sense ("a charming little house") depending on
> context — the same morpheme, two distinct readings, confirmed explicitly by the source's own
> "ojitos" example (`ojos` "eyes" + `-itos` → "sweet/pretty eyes," not "small eyes" in the anatomical
> sense).

## Grammar points

### Nonconformist (irregular) verbs — preterite and present

The book's earlier lessons (covered in the sibling `003_*` file) already introduced most of the
eighteen "nonconformist" verbs; this range (pp. 376–397) completes the set and drills their two most
irregular tenses. Eleven of the eighteen share one preterite ending pattern (`-e, -o, -imos, -ieron`,
stress-final and unaccented) once the irregular stem is known — e.g. `tener→tuve`, `hacer→hice`,
`decir→dije`, `traer→traje` (the last two drop the "i" in the 3rd-pl.: `dijeron`, `trajeron` not
*`dijieron`). Two (`salir`, `ver`) are fully regular in the preterite. Two (`oír`, `caer`) show the
general Spanish rule that intervocalic "i" becomes "y" (`oyó`, `cayó`). `dar` is an "-ar" verb that
irregularly takes "-er" preterite endings (`dí, dió, dimos, dieron`). `ir` and `ser` share an
*identical* preterite (`fuí, fué, fuimos, fueron`) — disambiguated only by context.

In the present tense, nine of the eighteen end in "-go" in the first person singular only
(`tengo, vengo, pongo, traigo, caigo, digo, hago, oigo, salgo`) while otherwise following the regular
or radical-changing pattern of their group; four end in "-oy" (`voy, doy, soy, estoy`); two
(`querer, poder`) are radical-changing throughout; two (`saber, ver`) are idiosyncratic
(`sé, veo`); one (`andar`) is fully regular in the present despite its preterite irregularity. Past
participles largely follow the same eighteen-verb roster and are mostly regular, with three needing
only an accent (`traído, caído, oído`) and four fully irregular (`hecho, dicho, puesto, visto`).
Verb families sharing an irregular root are listed explicitly: the `poner` family (exponer, imponer,
oponer, proponer, suponer, componer, disponer, descomponer(se)), the `tener` family (abstener,
contener, detener, retener, sostener, obtener, entretener, mantener), and the `traer` family
(distraer).

### Direct object pronouns (`lo`, `la`, `los`, `las`, `me`, `nos`)

`lo` = him/you(masc.); `la` = her/you(fem.); plurals add "-s". When referring to a mixed-gender
group, the plural masculine (`los`) is used. Pronouns precede the conjugated verb
(`Lo visité` = "I visited him"). A worked example (pp. 401–415) walks through `vió` combined with
each pronoun to show the same verb form yielding six distinct English meanings depending on the
attached pronoun and question/statement intonation.

### Indirect object pronoun `le` — the "acid test"

Introduced as a companion to the direct-object lesson (pp. 428–438): if an English verb can be
followed by "to him/her" or "for him/her" without sounding wrong (wrote, bought, gave, sang, sold,
spoke — but not visited, invited, bothered, examined, kissed, saw), the Spanish equivalent takes `le`
rather than `lo/la`. The book calls this reducible to the formula **TO, FOR = LE**. A further
complication is drawn out with `traer` (p. 449): "brought a person" takes `lo/la` (can't say "brought
to him to the party"), but "brought a thing to a person" takes `le` — so the same verb can take
either pronoun depending on what's actually being brought. Spanish additionally requires *both* the
noun and the pronoun when the recipient is named (`Le escribí a mi papá`, not just "I wrote to my
father") — the pronoun cannot be dropped just because the noun is present, unlike English.

### `gustar` and `encantar` (pp. 466–477)

Spanish doesn't have a direct verb "to like a thing" the way English does; instead the liked thing is
grammatically the subject and the liker takes an indirect object pronoun — literally "the music
pleases me" (`Me gusta la música`). The verb form agrees with the *liked thing's* number, not the
liker's: `me gusta` (singular liked thing) vs. `me gustan` (plural liked thing) regardless of whether
one or several people like it. Word order carries semantic weight: placing the person's name/phrase
*before* the `gusta` expression makes a statement (`A María le gusta el vestido` — "Mary likes the
dress"), placing it *after* makes a question (`¿Le gusta el vestido a María?` — "Does Mary like the
dress?"). `encantar` ("to enchant") works identically and corresponds to English "to love" for
things/activities (`Me encanta el jamón` — "I love ham," lit. "ham enchants me").

### Reflexive verbs (pp. 481–485)

Reflexive verbs direct their action back onto the subject; Spanish uses far more of them than
English, especially for physical actions involving one's own body (`bañarse, lavarse, peinarse,
secarse, afeitarse, levantarse, ponerse, sentarse, acostarse`). A second productive pattern: many
Spanish reflexives correspond to English "to get + adjective" (`casarse` = "to get married,"
`cansarse` = "to get tired," `enfermarse` = "to get sick," `marearse` = "to get seasick,"
`emborracharse` = "to get drunk"), each transparently derived from a related non-reflexive root
(`casa` "house," `cansancio` "fatigue," `enfermo` "sick," `mar` "sea," `borracho` "drunk"). A
grammar rule with real downstream relevance: **possessive adjectives are never used after reflexive
verbs referring to one's own body** — `Me lavé las manos` ("I washed my hands"), never `mis manos`,
because the reflexive pronoun already establishes ownership.

### "-cer" verbs and their object-pronoun quirks (pp. 477–479)

Verbs ending in "-cer" that form abstract nouns via "-imiento" (`conocer→conocimiento,
reconocer→reconocimiento, sufrir→sufrimiento, nacer→nacimiento, establecer→establecimiento,
crecer→crecimiento, agradecer→agradecimiento`) split into two camps for object-pronoun purposes,
following the same "to/for" acid test as the general `le` rule: `agradecer` ("to be grateful to"),
`ofrecer` ("to offer to"), and `parecer` ("to seem to") all take `le`; `compadecer` ("to sympathize
with, to pity"), which cannot be followed by "to" or "for" in English, takes the direct object
`lo/la` instead.

### Preterite vs. imperfect (pp. 516–531)

English has one simple past tense; Spanish has two, and the book's mnemonic device is geometric: the
**preterite** (a single completed action) is represented by a dot, the **imperfect** (continuous or
repeated past action) by a long line or series of dashes. `-ar` verbs form the imperfect with `-aba`
endings, `-er`/`-ir` verbs with `-ía`; only three verbs are irregular in the imperfect (`ser→era,
ir→iba, ver→veía`). The imperfect covers four related uses: habitual/repeated action
(`Compraba el periódico todos los días` — "he used to buy the paper every day"), continuous action
(`Tenía un perro` — "I had a dog"), ongoing qualities (`Era muy guapo` — "he was very handsome"), and
descriptions in the past (`La casa tenía puertas grandes`). The preterite/imperfect contrast is a
structural feature the checklist flags as central to register/slang work in fusional Spanish, since
which past tense a speaker reaches for shapes a huge amount of everyday narrative structure.

### The command form (pp. 532–535)

Regular command endings are `-e` for "-ar" verbs and `-a` for "-er"/"-ir" verbs. Pronouns attach
*to* the affirmative command (`levántese`, `escríbame`) but precede the verb and the command stays
un-attached in the negative (`no se levante`, `no me escriba`). Radical-changing and nonconformist
verbs carry the same stem irregularity into the command that they show in the first-person-singular
present (`cierro→cierre`, `vengo→venga`, `hago→haga`) — the book frames this as a general rule: the
command is (almost) always derivable from the first-person-singular present by swapping the final
vowel.

### The subjunctive system (pp. 566–580) — by far the densest topic in this half

The book organizes subjunctive triggers into named "families" rather than abstract rules, each
illustrated with paired present/past-subjunctive examples:
- **The Family of Hope**: `espero que`, bare `que` ("may..."), `Dios quiera que`, `ojalá que` (the
  last explicitly noted as an Arabic loanword via 711–1492 Moorish Spain, "Oh Allah that").
- **Request, Preference, Command**: any verb where one person wants another to act — `quiere que,
  dice que` (only when used as a command, not a plain statement), `prefiere que, pide que, exige que,
  le ruega que, le suplica que, propone que, prohibe que` — all followed by `que` + subjunctive.
- **The Indefinite/Unknown Family**: subjunctive required whenever the person, place, or thing
  referred to is not a specific known entity (`Quiero una criada que haga el trabajo bien` — "I want
  a maid who will do the work well," since which maid is unknown).
- **Action not yet taken**: `cuando` (when), `hasta que` (until), `aunque` (even though/if), `con tal
  que` (provided that), and `para que` (so that, expressing purpose) all trigger the subjunctive
  *only* when the clause refers to future/hypothetical action — the identical conjunction with a
  completed past action does **not** take the subjunctive (`cuando lo hizo` = accomplished fact, no
  subjunctive; `cuando lo haga` = future/uncertain, subjunctive).
- **The "Maybe" Family**: `tal vez, quizá(s), es posible que, posiblemente, puede ser que, puede que`.
- **Probability**: `es probable que, probablemente`. A related sharp rule: `no creo que` ("I don't
  think that") is **always** followed by the subjunctive, while its affirmative counterpart `creo que`
  ("I think that") is **never** subjunctive — doubt/negation of belief, not belief itself, triggers it.

The **past subjunctive** mirrors this whole system one tense back: present-tense triggers (`quiere
que`) take the present subjunctive, while their imperfect/preterite equivalents (`quería que, esperaba
que, dijo que`) take the past subjunctive. The past subjunctive's plural-third-person-preterite-minus-
ending relationship is highlighted as a memory aid (`tuvieron→tuviera, hicieron→hiciera,
dijeron→dijera`, etc.).

### Negatives and double negatives (pp. 612–613)

Unlike English, Spanish requires a **double negative** in sentences beginning with `no`: `No ví nada`
("I didn't see anything," literally "I didn't see nothing"), `No ví a nadie`, `No hice nada`. This is
presented as the *correct* grammatical construction, not an error — worth flagging since English-
speaking slang/register analysis sometimes treats double negation as inherently substandard, which
does not hold for standard Spanish.

### The intimate form of address (`tú`) (pp. 614–618)

`usted` (formal "you") becomes `tú` for family and close friends, with a systematic verb-ending shift:
add "-s" to the singular third-person form across nearly every tense (`habla→hablas,
hablaba→hablabas, hablará→hablarás`), including compound tenses via the auxiliary (`ha
hablado→has hablado`). The intimate preterite instead swaps the ending (`-ar` verbs: remove "-ar," add
"-aste"; `-er`/`-ir` verbs: add "-iste"). The intimate command drops the final vowel and adds "-a"
(`-ar` verbs) or "-e" (`-er`/`-ir` verbs); four nonconformist verbs are irregular exceptions
(`oír→oye, hacer→haz, ir→vé, ser→sé`). `usted` is largely replaced by `tú` and even `tú` itself is
frequently dropped as a subject since the verb ending already marks person — a directly relevant
structural fact for slang/register work, since tú/usted/vos selection (the book notes vos isn't
covered — that's a distinctly Rioplatense-region feature outside this book's scope) is one of the
checklist's flagged core register axes across the project's five GINI nations.

### Possessive pronouns, diminutives, stress/accent rules (pp. 618–623)

`mi/mis` and `su/sus` are invariant for gender; `nuestro` alone has full masculine/feminine ×
singular/plural agreement among the possessive *adjectives*. The possessive *pronouns* (`mío, suyo,
nuestro` — "mine, his/hers/yours/theirs, ours") all inflect for gender and number. Diminutive
`-ito/-ita/-itos/-itas` marks either literal smallness or (very productively) affection/endearment —
see the Morpheme breakdown above. **Stress rules**: words ending in N, S, or a vowel stress the
second-to-last syllable by default; words ending in any other consonant stress the last syllable;
any word breaking either rule needs a written accent. Accents also distinguish otherwise-identical
word pairs (`de/dé, el/él, mi/mí, si/sí`) and mark interrogative/exclamatory forms of otherwise-
unaccented words (`¿cómo?` vs. `como`, `¿qué?` vs. `que`, `¿cuándo?` vs. `cuando`).

### Radical-changing verbs II/III, "-uir" verbs, irregular past participles (pp. 601–611)

Additional radical-changing patterns beyond the earlier e→ie/o→ue lessons: verbs ending in "-zar"
(`comenzar, empezar, tropezar, almorzar, esforzar, forzar`) change z→c before "e" (1st-person-singular
preterite and present subjunctive). Verbs ending in "-uir" (`construir` and its family) insert a "y"
between the stem and most endings. Three families of irregular past participles are laid out in
full: the `poner` family (`puesto, expuesto, supuesto, propuesto, opuesto, impuesto, compuesto,
descompuesto`), the `volver` family (`vuelto, devuelto, revuelto, envuelto, desenvuelto`), and the
`escribir` family (`escrito, descrito, subscrito, inscrito`), plus a further list of one-off
irregulars (`abierto, cubierto, descubierto, impreso, resuelto, disuelto, frito, muerto, roto, visto,
dicho, hecho, deshecho, satisfecho`). Past participles double as adjectives with full gender/number
agreement (`un huevo frito` vs. `huevos fritos`; `La puerta está abierta`).

### Impersonal verbs (pp. 610–611)

`llover` (to rain), `nevar` (to snow), and `haber` in its existential "there is/are" sense are
conjugated *only* in the third-person singular across every tense, since no grammatical person is
performing the action — `llueve, llovió, llovía, va a llover, lloverá, ha llovido`; `hay, hubo, había,
va a haber, habrá, ha habido`.

### `deber` (ought to, should, must) (pp. 611–612)

A regular verb that functions as an auxiliary always followed by an infinitive: `Debo estudiar` ("I
ought to study"). `deber haber` + past participle expresses a past obligation/inference ("must
have..."): `Debe haber ido al despacho` — "He must have gone to the office."

### Passive voice (p. 612)

Spanish uses the passive voice far less than English; where English would say "I was invited to a
party" without naming who invited, Spanish prefers an active, if unspecified, subject via the
figurative "they": `Me invitaron a una fiesta` (lit. "They invited me to a party"). The book frames
this as Spanish's general preference for an active subject over a true passive construction.

### Idiomatic expressions and slang (pp. 624–629) — flagged as high-value for mechanics analysis

This is the single most directly relevant section of this half of the book for the project's slang-
synthesis goal. It is a dedicated closing appendix of everyday idioms, several **explicitly tagged by
the source itself** as slang and/or regionally marked: `güero/güera` ("blond") is labeled
"(slang, Mex.)"; `plata` ("money," literally "silver") is labeled "slang for money"; `¡Apúrese!`
("Hurry up!") is tagged "(Mex.)". The remainder of the box — some three dozen further idioms — is
presented as ordinary colloquial usage rather than explicitly-flagged slang, but nearly every entry
is a case of vivid figurative extension from a literal, often body-part- or animal-based image (a
donkey's ear for foolishness, hands in bread dough for being caught red-handed, an eleven-yard shirt
for over-committing, a wild beast for fury) — a rich, ready-made case set for later mechanics
analysis of how Spanish colloquial register builds meaning through concrete-to-abstract metaphor
rather than through affixation or word-formation per se, complementary to the diminutive-suffixation
mechanism noted above.

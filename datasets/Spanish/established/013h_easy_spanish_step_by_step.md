## Grammar points

### A four-way semantic taxonomy of reflexive-form usage

This chapter organizes reflexive-marked verbs (**-se** verbs) into distinct functional categories,
rather than presenting reflexivity as a single uniform grammatical operation — worth treating as a
reusable taxonomy for mechanics-analysis work on how a single morphological marker (**se**/reflexive
pronoun) can be reused for genuinely different semantic functions in the same language:

1. **True reflexive** (subject acts on itself): *Me lavo* — "I wash myself."
2. **Movement** (a closed set of largely non-literally-reflexive motion verbs that simply require
   the reflexive form as part of their lexical entry): quedarse, mudarse, pararse, caerse, irse,
   moverse (p. 198).
3. **"To become"** (inchoative/change-of-state, itself split into two registers): **ponerse** +
   adjective for ordinary physical/emotional change (*Me pongo brava* — "I become angry"; *Ella se
   pone roja* — "She blushes"); **volverse** + adjective specifically for a *sudden, involuntary*
   change (*Hasta los psicólogos se vuelven locos* — "Even the psychologists go crazy"); **hacerse**
   or **llegar a ser** + noun for a change of profession/status that implies *effort* on the
   subject's part (*Ella se hace doctora* / *Él llega a ser doctor* — "She/He is becoming a
   doctor") (p. 199). This four-way "to become" split — effortless-vs-effortful, gradual-vs-sudden —
   is a clean, non-obvious grammaticalized distinction worth flagging for how an invented slang
   register might similarly split a single English concept ("to become") across multiple
   near-synonymous but non-interchangeable forms.
4. **Reciprocal** ("each other"): the plural form of an otherwise-ordinary reflexive verb takes on a
   reciprocal reading (*Nos conocemos bien* — "We know each other well"), disambiguated when needed
   by adding **el uno al otro** / **la una a la otra** / **los unos a los otros** / **las unas a las
   otras** (p. 202).
5. **Impersonal/passive se** (a fifth use, distinct from all of the above, discussed in the same
   chapter): **se** + third-person verb with no personal subject at all, translating as English
   "one," "you," "they," "people (in general)," or the passive voice — *Se vive bien en este país*
   ("One lives well in this country"), *Aquí se habla español* ("Spanish is spoken here" / "One
   speaks Spanish here"), *Se prohíbe fumar* ("It is prohibited to smoke") (p. 203). Grammatically
   this is a wholly separate function from true reflexivity (there is no "self" being acted upon),
   even though it reuses the identical **se** morpheme — a second clean example (alongside the
   "to become" split above) of one surface marker doing multiple, only loosely related jobs.

Some verbs require a preposition after the reflexive form that cannot be dropped even where the
English translation omits it (**Me fijo mucho en los detalles** — "I notice the details a lot";
**Él se acuerda de ella** — "He remembers her") (p. 200) — this parallels the "prepositions after
reflexive verbs" pattern already noted for ordinary prepositional-object verbs elsewhere in this
project's sources, confirming it as a general Spanish feature rather than reflexive-specific.

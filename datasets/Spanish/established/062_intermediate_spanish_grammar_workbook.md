## Non-redundant-supplement scope decision

20 units: expressions of time, the passive, verbs with prepositions, the present subjunctive,
imperatives, subjunctive after verbs of influence/emotion/impersonal expressions, the imperfect
subjunctive, subjunctive in expressions of time/after certain conjunctions/in relative clauses/to
express doubt or denial, the perfect and pluperfect subjunctive, the conditional, conditional
sentences, indefinite expressions, relatives, por and para, position of adjectives.

**Skipped as redundant:** Unit 1 (expressions of time: antes de/después de/al + infinitive,
acabar de, the desde hace / hace...que / llevar + gerund "how long" constructions) — standard
material fully covered by 014/015/019 and others. Unit 2 (the passive: reflexive-passive, literal
passive, se as impersonal "one") — fully covered by 006/017's dedicated passive treatments. Units 4,
5, 9, 14, 15, 16 (basic present subjunctive formation, imperatives, imperfect subjunctive formation,
perfect/pluperfect subjunctive, conditional/conditional perfect, conditional sentences/si-clauses) —
core paradigm mechanics already covered by 007/008/014/015/017/019. Unit 18 (relative pronouns: que,
quien, el cual, lo que) — covered by 006. Unit 19 (por/para) — covered in comparable or greater depth
by 006/015/017.

**What was kept and why** (genuinely new or more systematic than existing coverage): this book
turned out to be a **better source than its "intermediate workbook" framing suggested** — rather
than being pure redundant drill, it breaks the subjunctive down by *trigger category* across 7 full
units (influence, emotion, impersonal expressions, time, conjunctions, relative clauses, doubt/
denial) with a specific trigger-verb/conjunction inventory and several fine-grained mood-selection
nuances per category that prior Spanish extractions' more compact subjunctive treatments (014's
single combined section, 019's chapter pair) did not spell out at this level of granularity. It also
supplies a genuinely new morphological family (the *-quiera* indefinite-expression system) and a
cleanly principled restrictive/non-restrictive theory of adjective position that subsumes and
explains (rather than just lists) the position-dependent-meaning-shift word lists already captured
from 006/016. Unit 3's verb+preposition-before-infinitive inventory is also new at this scale.

## Grammar points

### Verb+preposition-before-infinitive/noun governance system (Unit 3)

A compact but fuller inventory than previously captured in this corpus of which preposition (a, de,
por, con, en) a given verb requires before a following infinitive or noun complement, including two
verbs (acabar, pensar) whose core meaning itself is gated by which preposition follows — relevant to
any later mechanics-analysis pass modeling Spanish verb+preposition idiom formation. The book is
explicit that there's "no reliable way of telling which preposition... you will simply have to learn
this as you go along" — i.e. this is lexically listed, not rule-derived, in the source grammar
tradition itself.

### The *-quiera* indefinite-expression family and its two concessive-construction siblings (Unit 17)

Three related but structurally distinct ways Spanish expresses free-choice/concessive "no matter
X": (1) the closed, suffixed *-quiera* family (dondequiera, etc., morphologically derived from
querer's subjunctive) with its own pluralization quirk (only the wh-word element pluralizes); (2) the
"por + [much/very/little] + que" concessive-degree family, which is fully productive across verb,
noun, adjective/adverb, and "little" variants; (3) the freely-productive verb-reduplication
construction ("vaya donde vaya"), distinct from and more generative than the small set of fixed "sea
lo que sea"-style set phrases. All three converge on the same semantic space (indefinite/concessive
"no matter what/where/how") via three different morphosyntactic strategies, which may be useful
comparanda for designing multiple indefinite-construction strategies in a derived conlang rather than
a single mechanism.

### Subjunctive trigger-category inventory, with mood-selection nuances beyond simple trigger lists

This book's real contribution to this corpus isn't the basic "verb/conjunction X triggers subjunctive"
rule (already well established from 014/015/017/019) but **several cases where mood selection tracks
something more specific** than a flat trigger-word list:
- **Semantic polarity, not grammatical polarity** (dudar/negar vs. creer/decir/no dudar/no negar,
  Unit 13) — the clearest, most systematic example in this book.
- **Lexical sense selection** (esperar "expect" vs. "hope," Unit 7; acabar de vs. acabar por and
  pensar en vs. pensar de, Unit 3) — mood or preposition choice distinguishes otherwise-identical
  verb forms' meanings.
- **Pragmatic/speaker-expectation signaling in questions** (¿Crees que...? mood choice, Unit 13) —
  explicitly flagged by the book as having no hard rule, purely observational.
- **Register-conditioned syntactic alternation** (dejar/permitir/prohibir + bare infinitive as an
  informal alternative to the full subjunctive construction, Unit 6).
- **Idiomatic exception carved out of a general semantic class** (me temo que + indicative, Unit 7,
  as a non-literal "I'm afraid" hedge that doesn't count as true "emotion" for mood-selection
  purposes).

### Restrictive vs. non-restrictive adjective position — a principled theory, not just a word list (Unit 20)

Distinct from and complementary to 006's and 016 Unit 29's position-dependent-meaning-shift word
lists (antiguo, cierto, grande, etc.) and word-order/contrastive-stress treatment, this book frames
adjective position as governed by a single semantic principle: **restrictive adjectives (narrowing
the noun to a subset of its class) default to postnominal position; non-restrictive adjectives
(describing a quality shared by the whole class, or already inherent/expected) default to
prenominal.** It then names three categories that are *always* restrictive regardless of this
default (contrast adjectives, sub-type/sub-group-forming adjectives like "vino tinto," and
nationality/region adjectives) and three that are *usually* non-restrictive (inherent-characteristic
adjectives, adjectives describing something true of every member already named, and — the most
slang-relevant finding — **adjectives used as pejorative intensifiers/mild "swear words"** like
santo/maldito/dichoso, whose entire pejorative-ironic sense is activated specifically by prenominal
placement). This last category is a genuinely productive register mechanism (not a fixed idiom list)
directly relevant to how a derived conlang might encode irony/profanity-intensifier shifts through
word order rather than through distinct lexical items.

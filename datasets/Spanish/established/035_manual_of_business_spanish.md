## 2. Business Practice (printed pp.205–224, PDF 212–231) — full extraction

Sixteen numbered topics of business-culture/practical-usage notes (not grammar paradigms). Paraphrased
below; page citations are to the PDF page range.

### 1. Addressing people
*usted/ustedes* (abbreviated *Vd./Vds.* in writing) is the default address term with anyone not known
well or only known formally — it forces third-person verb/pronoun agreement. *tú/vosotros* is used once
well acquainted. In most of Latin America, *ustedes* covers essentially all plural second-person address
and *usted* is used more broadly than in Spain, where social informality has spread markedly since the
mid-1970s transition to democracy. Titles *Señor/Señora/Señorita* (written *Sr./Sra./Srta.*) accompany
the first surname (Hispanic naming gives two surnames; the first — paternal — is the one used socially/
in business). Referring to someone in the third party (rather than addressing them) prepends the
definite article in lower case: *el señor Ruiz*, *la señorita Paredes*. Useful set phrases: *¿Qué
tal?*/greeting, *Encantado(a) de conocerle* (pleased to meet you), *Perdóneme*/*Por favor* to get
attention, *Lo siento* to apologize.

### 2. Communicating with people (phone/fax/telex/mail)
Spain's phone system (Telefónica) uses provincial 2–3-digit codes starting with 9; directory enquiries
are *Información* (009 national, 003 provincial); the business directory is *Páginas Amarillas* (Yellow
Pages). Phone numbers are normally given as 7 digits (one single digit + three pairs). On answering, a
caller is greeted with *¿Dígame?*/*¿Sí?*; a few set phrases are given for not hearing well, asking
someone to repeat, not understanding, asking for help, and promising to call back. Fax: the Post Office
(*Correos*) offers *Burofax*; local Chambers of Commerce also offer fax services; standard fax format
includes letterhead, date/time, addressee, sender, page count, subject. Telex is still preferred by some
businesses for cost/security/accuracy; abbreviation is avoided except dropping high-frequency function
words. Mail: Spain–Europe delivery averages ~4 days; post offices are open mornings only on Saturdays
except the main Madrid/Barcelona branches; stamps are commonly bought at the tobacconist (*estanco*).

### 3. Appointments and punctuality
Appointments (*citas*) and meetings (*reuniones*) are often made at short notice. **Regional contrast
explicitly noted in the source**: in Latin America the word *hora* is often used for "appointment",
while in Spain the more generic *compromiso* implies an obligation to be somewhere. Hispanic business
culture favors the oral mode over the written for arranging/canceling. Punctuality is not emphasized the
way it is in some other business cultures — being kept waiting by a busy/senior host is not unusual, and
a guest's own late arrival is less likely to give offense — while smart dress has become the norm,
especially in Spain since its post-dictatorship transition. Personal trust/compatibility is valued over
a fast, strictly transactional pace.

### 4. Business cards and gifts
Exchanging business cards (*tarjetas de visita*) is normal at the start of meetings. A gift is most
associated with being invited to a Spanish contact's home — a significant mark of closeness that should
never be refused; flowers sent with a card beforehand, plus a thank-you card after, is the recommended
pattern. Frequent/overt gift exchange in business generally is not typical of Spain; hospitality
(entertainment) fills that role instead.

### 5. Business entertainment
Hospitality is highly valued. The most common entertainment is inviting colleagues/clients to lunch or
dinner out (many large firms have in-house dining rooms for working lunches). Restaurant service
typically doesn't start before 2 p.m. and lunch can run past 4 p.m.; dinner invitations may be for as
late as 10 p.m., often preceded by a *tapas*-bar visit to "open the appetite" (*abrir el apetito*).
Paying for pre-dinner drinks as a guest, or expecting a guest to pay as host, both risk giving offense. A
10–15% tip is standard practice when no service charge is included.

### 6. Office and shop hours
Standard working day: 9 a.m.–7 p.m. with up to a 2-hour lunch break, though 8 a.m. starts and later
finishes are increasingly common. June–September often follows the *horario intensivo* (intensive
schedule): 8 a.m.–3 p.m. with no main break, a 7-hour day. Most offices close Saturdays. Shops: typically
9.30 a.m.–1/1.30 p.m., then closed until 4 p.m., open again until 8 p.m.; large stores (*almacenes*)
start at 9 a.m. Hypermarkets are nicknamed *los hiper*; newer developments are *centros comerciales*
(shopping centers).

### 7. Business organizations
All Spanish companies (except sole traders, *comerciantes*) must register at the *Registro Mercantil*
(Mercantile Register) in their provincial capital. The *Sociedad Anónima* (S.A., ≈ plc) is the dominant
company status regardless of size; *Sociedad de Responsabilidad Limitada* (S.R.L./S.L., ≈ Ltd),
cooperatives, and joint ventures (*uniones temporales*/*agrupaciones de empresas*) are the main
alternatives. Professional associations (*colegios profesionales*) and employers' associations
(*asociaciones empresariales*) are long-standing; the CEOE (founded 1977) is the dominant employers'
confederation; PYMES = *Pequeñas y Medianas Empresas* (SMEs). Chambers of Commerce (*Cámaras de
Comercio*) provide day-to-day business services province by province, coordinated by the *Consejo
Superior de Cámaras*; ICEX (est. 1982) promotes/coordinates Spanish foreign trade.

### 8. Banks and other financial institutions
Banks open 9 a.m.–2 p.m. weekdays (9 a.m.–1 p.m. Saturday); savings banks (*Cajas de Ahorros*) 8 a.m.–1
p.m. weekdays only. The Banco de España is the central issuing bank. Six-tier sketch of the financial
system given in the source: (1) Bank of Spain, (2) state-owned/private/foreign/savings banks + rural
credit cooperatives, (3) finance companies, (4) investment institutions, (5) brokers, (6) insurance/
reinsurance companies. Historical note: the "Big Seven" private banks gave way to new groupings (e.g.
Banesto's near-collapse leading to absorption into Banco de Santander, forming Santander-Banesto).
Working-capital finance: loans (3–6 months, extendable to a year), overdrafts, bills of exchange
(*letras*). 90–120 day terms are normal credit periods. Four main types of Spain-specific joint venture
are named (see §15 below for the detail).

### 9. Legal practitioners
Civil-law system (not common law); laws take effect 20 days after publication in the *Boletín Oficial
del Estado*. Judges/magistrates are independent and barred from other public office or party/union
membership. Lawyers (*abogados*, also called *licenciados*/*letrados*; their offices are *bufetes*) are
organized under the *Colegio de Abogados*. A *procurador* presents litigants' cases in court. A
*notario* is simultaneously a legal professional and public servant who authorizes public documents,
acts, and wills — mandatory for certain contracts. Spanish nationality is required to practice law in
Spain.

### 10. Accountants and auditors
The *Código de Comercio* (Commercial Code) underlies mercantile law, brought into line with EU/EC
practice in 1989. Key legislation: *Plan General de Contabilidad* (1990), *Ley de Auditoría* (1988);
ICAC (est. 1990) regulates accounting and auditing. Annual accounts (*cuentas anuales*) comprise the
balance sheet (*balance de situación*), profit-and-loss account (*cuenta de pérdidas y ganancias*), and
notes to the account (*memoria*), accompanied by the directors' report (*informe de gestión*) and
auditors' report (*informe de los auditores*). An auditor's clean opinion is an *informe sin
salvedades*; a qualified one is *con salvedades*.

### 11. The advertising media
National/international advertising agencies cluster in Madrid/Barcelona. Dominant 1990s multimedia
groups: Prisa, Godó, Zeta, Correo. Television is the most-watched medium (~90% of Spaniards over 14),
ahead of magazines, radio, and newspapers (only ~35%). The source cites a 1993 ad-spend drop (first in
ten years, after a 1992 peak of 593,101 million pesetas) hitting magazines hardest. Key trade body: AEA
(*Asociación Española de Anunciantes*).

### 12. Holidays
August is the main vacation month (30 calendar days is the typical minimum allowance); little serious
business happens in mid-summer. 11 public holidays are celebrated nationally, leaving only 3 of the
official 14 for regional observance. *Hacer puente* ("bridging") is the practice of taking the
intervening Monday/Friday off when a holiday falls on a Tuesday/Thursday to make a long weekend — the
source notes this can make a business inaccessible for up to a week around early-December's two
holidays (the 6th and 8th).

### 13. Trade unions
Two major unions post-Franco: *Unión General de Trabajadores* (UGT, linked to the governing PSOE since
1982) and *Comisiones Obreras* (CC.OO., associated with the Communist Party though the union stresses
independence). Union membership is comparatively low (~20% of the working population). The 1984
*Acuerdo Económico y Social* is cited as the most comprehensive labor pact of its era. An informal
economy (*economía sumergida*) persists alongside the formal one; by 1992, over 30% of employees were on
temporary contracts (*contratos eventuales/temporales*). *Salario mínimo interprofesional* = the
minimum-wage scheme.

### 14. Business taxes
National/regional/local tax levels. Two main direct taxes: personal income tax (10–56%) and company
income tax (35%). VAT (called **IVA**, *Impuesto sobre Valor Añadido*) at 12% standard rate (half-rate on
necessities, nearly triple on major luxuries); exports are exempt. Companies resident in Spain are taxed
on worldwide income; non-residents only on Spain-sourced income. Tax-concession mechanisms listed:
investment/employment credits, new-technology promotion, regional-investment reductions (e.g. a *Zona de
Urgente Reindustrialización*), merger/spin-off benefits, joint-venture and regional-development-
corporation benefits.

### 15. Types of company
*Sociedad Anónima* (S.A.) — the most prestigious/common form, ranging from stock-exchange corporations to
family businesses, minimum capital ~10 million pesetas. *Sociedad de Responsabilidad Limitada* (S.R.L./
S.L.) — max 50 stockholders, minimum capital ~0.5 million pesetas, no maximum-capital requirement, no
shares (capital divided among stockholders directly) — common in services/retail. Partnerships
(*Sociedad Regular Colectiva*, *Sociedad en Comandita*) are less numerous but have legal status.
Cooperatives (*Cooperativas*) are promoted especially in agriculture. Sole traders = *comerciantes*.
**Four joint-venture types specific to Spanish business law**, as enumerated in the source: (1)
*Sociedad de empresa* — ≥3 participants forming a separate legal entity, each holding ≤30% of their own
capital; (2) *Agrupación de empresa* — companies collaborating on common interests without forming a
separate entity; (3) *Unión temporal de empresa* — a time-bound agreement for a specific service/project;
(4) *Cesión de unidades de obra* — a subcontracting arrangement. Establishing a Spanish subsidiary
requires ≥3 shareholders, a notarized deed of incorporation, 25%+ of capital paid at incorporation, and
DGTE clearance; foreign share purchases need DGTE approval only above a 50%-holding threshold (exempt
below 25 million pesetas invested).

### 16. Red tape
Franco-era bureaucracy is described as having fostered dependency, delay, and corruption; *gestorías
administrativas* emerged (in Spain and Latin America) as agencies that handle routine state-citizen
paperwork for a fee, staffed by *gestores* ("local agents"). **Explicit regional-synonym contrast
flagged in the source**: securing useful personal contacts/favors is called *enchufes* ("plugs") in
Spain and *palancas* ("levers") in Latin America — both can shade toward implying corruption, or simply
reflect the weight Hispanic business culture gives to the personal/social dimension of doing business.
Post-1986 EU entry simplified exchange controls, foreign investment in "sensitive" industries, work
permits, patenting, debt collection, and import documentation, though patience is still advised.

### A three-way scale of obligation: *tener que* / *hay que* / *deber* (§10, PDF p.237)
Most general grammars treat *tener que* and *hay que* as near-synonyms ("to have to") and *deber* as a
separate "should/ought" modal. This source frames all three as **one graded scale of obligation
strength** rather than two unrelated pairs: *tener que* = a personal obligation ("I have to/need to");
*hay que* = an impersonal, more general obligation (no subject implied — "it's necessary that [in
general]"); *deber* = the weakest of the three, phrased as advice ("you ought to..."). Example triplet
given: *No puedo salir ahora porque tengo que terminar esta carta* / *Hay que revisar las cuentas una vez
al mes* / *El jefe me ha dicho que debo trabajar más*. Worth keeping as an explicit three-way contrast
framing, useful for later mechanics-analysis work on how Spanish grades obligation/necessity modality.

### *Ser* + profession noun: article presence distinguishes role-as-identity from role-as-job (§1, PDF
### p.226)
*Juan es el secretario* ("John is **the** secretary" — a specific, particular office-holder) vs *Juan es
secretario* ("John is [a] secretary" — stating his job/profession, no article). The source frames this
specifically around job-title naming, which is a business-register-relevant distinction (introducing
colleagues by role vs. stating "what someone does") even though the underlying rule (predicate nouns of
profession/nationality/religion drop the article in Spanish) is itself standard and already covered
elsewhere.

### Phonological reasoning behind the *-ión* plural accent drop (§6, PDF p.228)
Most grammars simply state the rule (words ending in *-ión* drop the accented *ó* in the plural:
*habitación → habitaciones*, *excursión → excursiones*) as a spelling fact. This source adds the
*mechanism*: the accent is dropped because, once *-es* is added, stress automatically falls on the newly
created penultimate syllable, making the written accent mark redundant rather than arbitrary. Worth
keeping as a deeper explanatory angle for later phonology/orthography mechanics work, even though the
surface rule itself is already documented in the general references.

dictionary (PDF 257–339 = Spanish-English, PDF 340–424 = English-Spanish), with an explicit tag key on
its own first page (PDF p.257): `*` = slang term, `(US)` = term particular to the USA, `(GB)` = term

spec. Recovered instead via direct **PyMuPDF line-level text extraction**, which preserves correct
reading order, combined with the glossary's own hanging-indent layout (first line of each entry starts
at x0=36pt; wrapped continuation lines are indented to x0=48pt) to reliably segment the continuous page
text into individual dictionary entries. This was verified against the source's own printed page-number
headers at several points and against the "Key to glossary"/"Spanish-English"/"English-Spanish" section
header pages (PDF pp.257–258, 340) to confirm section boundaries. Granularity is one row per top-level

glossary's `*` tag repeatedly surfaces the same semantic fields marked slang on *both* sides of the
dictionary, often with a parallel Latin-American regional variant also tagged:
- **Bribery**: English `backhander*`/`sweetener*` ↔ Spanish `soborno` (neutral) with slang/regional

- **Dismissal**: English `fire*`/`sack*`/`axe*` ↔ Spanish `despedir` (neutral) with the Latin-American

- **Strikebreaking**: English `scab*` ↔ Spanish `rompehuelgas`/`esquirol` (neutral) with the
  Latin-American slang doublet `carnero, -era (LAm)`.
- **US dollar slang**: `buck* (US)` ↔ Spanish `dólar` (neutral) with the Latin-American colloquial
  `verde (LAm)` ("the green one") as the parallel slang/colloquial term.
- **Dishonest dealing**: `phoney*`/`shady*`/`shark*`/`shoddy*`/`shirker*`/`swindle*`/`swindler*` on the
  English side, several cross-referencing Latin-American colloquial equivalents (e.g. `shirker*` ↔
  `fiacún, -una (LAm)` / `flojonazo, -aza (LAm)`).
- **Working a second job**: `moonlight*` ↔ Spanish `estar pluriempleado`/`practicar el pluriempleo`
  (neutral register on the Spanish side for this one).

This is exactly the kind of explicit, source-marked register/dialect annotation the project's extraction
spec treats as highest-priority — three independent tag systems (slang-marking, US/GB-marking, and
LAm-marking) frequently overlapping on the same semantic fields (dismissal, bribery, currency slang)
across both halves of a bidirectional dictionary.

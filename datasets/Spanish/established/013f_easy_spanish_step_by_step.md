## Grammar points

### The *gustar*-class verbs as a closed semantic set

The grammatical pattern behind **gustar** ("to be pleasing to") — where the logical English subject
becomes a Spanish indirect object and the logical English object becomes the grammatical Spanish
subject controlling verb agreement — extends to a specific, listable set of verbs, not just
**gustar** itself (pp. 158-161, full list above). Several of these carry their own idiosyncratic
exceptions worth flagging together: **encantar** is categorically incompatible with negation (no
*"no me encanta"* — the book states this as an absolute rule, unlike ordinary gustar which negates
freely); **faltar**, **importar**, and **parecer** can all optionally drop the indirect-object
pronoun entirely, unlike the rest of the class. This is a clean, reusable semantic/syntactic class
inventory for mechanics-analysis work on how a marked argument-structure pattern (experiencer-as-
oblique) can spread across a verb class by analogy — a mechanism potentially relevant to how invented
slang could extend or violate an existing valency pattern.

### Body-part possession restated with *doler*

Reconfirms the Chapter 7 rule (definite article, not possessive adjective, marks body parts) in the
indirect-object-verb context specifically: **Me duele la cabeza** ("My head hurts," lit. "The head
hurts to me"), **Te duelen los dientes** ("Your teeth hurt you") — the book's own note: "the
possessive adjective is not used with parts of the body and the indirect object pronoun" (p. 160).

### Disambiguating the indirect object pronoun *le/les*

**Le** and **les** are referentially ambiguous (le = "to him/her/you [Ud.]"; les = "to them/you
[Uds.]"). Spanish resolves this not by replacing the pronoun but by **adding** a clarifying
prepositional phrase alongside it, redundantly: **María le escribe una carta a Ud./a él/a ella** (p.
165). The clarifying phrase can also carry a proper noun (**María le escribe una carta a Juan**) —
i.e., the indirect object pronoun is retained even when a full noun phrase indirect object is also
present, unlike English, which uses one or the other but not both.

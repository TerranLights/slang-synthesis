# Spanish — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Practice Makes Perfect Advanced Spanish Grammar (full book)

**Source:** Rogelio Alonso Vallecillos, *Practice Makes Perfect: Advanced Spanish Grammar*
(McGraw-Hill, 2008), Units 1–12, pp. 1–192 (the Answer Key, pp. 193–202, is exercise-solution
material and was skipped per the coverage rule — it re-states drill answers, not new grammar or
vocabulary).

**Coverage note:** every grammar point and every distinct vocabulary item from all 12 units —
Object pronouns/omission of subject pronouns; Some/any and other determiners; Adjectives; Adverbs;
Commands and requests; Modal constructions; Impersonal sentences and the passive; Relative
pronouns and conjunctions; Reported speech; Problematic prepositions I & II; Idiomatic
constructions. Repeated drill exercises (`EJERCICIO` blocks) and the Answer Key were skipped — they
recombine vocabulary/grammar already captured from the same unit's exposition, per
`../../00_Reference_Extraction_Spec.md`'s coverage rule. This source has a genuine clean `pdftotext`
text layer throughout (verified via `pdfinfo` and full extraction); no vision-reading was required
and no marginalia issue arose.

**Non-redundant-supplement note:** this is the **first** Spanish reference book extracted into
`established/` (the directory was empty at dispatch time, so no established/005 "Basic Spanish
Grammar" existed yet to compare against). No redundancy screening against a prior Spanish
established/ file was possible or necessary. Future Spanish extractions (e.g. a "Basic Spanish
Grammar" or "Complete Spanish Grammar") should treat *this* file as the baseline to check for
overlap, per the spec's non-redundant-supplement pattern — this book skews advanced/idiomatic
(object-pronoun clitic mechanics, reported-speech tense-shift rules, ~150 opaque idioms, and a
150-page prepositional-usage breakdown), which a basic-level grammar is unlikely to duplicate in
depth.

## Grammar points

### Object pronouns: le/lo vs. la, and the leísmo split

Direct-object pronouns: for a masculine singular human, either `le` or `lo` is accepted (with `lo`
obligatory for non-human masculine); feminine direct objects always take `la`/`las`. Indirect
objects always take `le`/`les` regardless of gender. When double marking is needed for clarity,
`a + noun/pronoun` is added, and this is compulsory when the direct object is fronted to the start
of the sentence (`A Mari no la voy a llamar`). (pp. 1–5)

### The se-substitution rule

Two third-person object pronouns can never co-occur (`*le lo`); the indirect object obligatorily
becomes `se` before a third-person direct object (`Se lo voy a dar`). This is presented as an
absolute rule, not a stylistic preference. (p. 4)

### gustar-type verbs restrict pronoun choice

Verbs of the "liking" class (gustar, encantar, disgustar, aterrar) never take `lo/la/los/las` as
their subject-experiencer marker — only `le/les`, even though the semantic object (the liked thing)
is grammatically the subject of the Spanish sentence. (pp. 5–6)

### Omission of subject pronouns — a sociopragmatic system, not a stylistic option

Spanish's rich verb morphology usually makes subject pronouns redundant, but the book identifies a
whole cluster of rules governing when omission is compulsory, optional, or would sound rude/wrong:
first-speaker-in-a-conversation omits, a replying speaker who echoes the same verb form must
include the pronoun; asking a *group* a question requires the pronoun in replies; pointing/
introducing people favors demonstratives over subject pronouns; ambiguous third-person forms
(`es de Brasil`) require an explicit pronoun. (pp. 6–11)

### Uncountable-noun quantifier system (algo de, nada de, ningún, cualquier, cada)

Spanish distinguishes affirmative/negative and countable/uncountable quantifier forms much more
finely than English "some/any": `algo de`/`un poco de` (uncountable, affirmative), `unos`/
`algunos`(countable plural, affirmative), `nada de` (uncountable negative), `ningún/ninguna`
(countable negative, singular verb even with plural sense in "not a single"). `cada` individuates
group members; `todos los` treats them as a collective whole. (pp. 17–23)

### Comparative/superlative morphology, including the Latin -ísimo suffix

Beyond regular comparatives with `más`, Spanish retains the Latin absolute-superlative suffix
`-ísimo` (not a true comparative — it expresses "extremely X" without comparing against a group),
plus irregular forms (`óptimo`, `pésimo`, `paupérrimo`, `fortísimo` with stem change). Diminutive
suffixes `-ito/-ico/-illo` carry regional distribution (north Spain favors `-ico`, south favors
`-illo`, Latin America strongly prefers `-ito`) and register from affectionate to diminishing/
disdainful. (pp. 35–37)

### Pre-nominal vs. post-nominal adjective placement changes meaning

A handful of adjectives (bueno, malo, grande→gran, viejo) shift meaning by position: `un hombre
grande` (physically big) vs. `un gran hombre` (a great man); `un viejo amigo` (a longtime friend) vs.
`una casa vieja` (an old house, i.e. aged). (pp. 33–34)

### Adjectives functioning as adverbs (invariable masculine singular form)

A closed set of Spanish adjectives (rápido, directo, duro, fuerte, alto, bajo, flojo, igual, suave,
barato) can function adverbially without the `-mente` suffix, but only in the masculine singular
form regardless of the subject's gender/number — because they modify the verb's action, not a noun.
(pp. 52–66, extensive "tricky adverb" catalog)

### Reported speech tense-shift table

With a past reporting verb (dijo), simple present→imperfect; present subjunctive→imperfect
subjunctive; future→conditional; preterit optionally→past perfect; imperfect/conditional never
change. With a present reporting verb (dice), no tense shift occurs. Deictic words shift too:
aquí→allí, hoy→ese día, mañana→al día siguiente, este→ese/aquel, venir→ir (when the reporter has
moved location). (pp. 130–135)

### The Spanish passive's indirect-object restriction

Unlike English, an indirect object can never become the subject of a Spanish passive sentence — only
a direct object can. `*Ella ha sido dada las llaves` is ungrammatical; the correct passive is `Las
llaves le han sido dadas (a ella)`, or an active/impersonal construction is used instead. The book
gives an explicit algorithmic recipe for converting active→passive respecting this constraint.
(pp. 103–104)

### Impersonal constructions as an alternative to English-style passive

Spanish prefers three impersonal strategies over the passive voice: (1) unstated third-person plural
subject (`Me han dicho que...`), (2) inherently impersonal third-person-singular weather/time verbs
(`Llueve`), (3) impersonal `se` (`Se está construyendo un puente`), with verb number agreeing with
the logical object. English "to have something done" (`I'm having the house painted`) has no direct
structural equivalent and must be rendered via one of these impersonal strategies or an ambiguous
active sentence. (pp. 101–105)

### Relative pronoun system: que / quien(es) / el cual / cuyo

`que` is the default, never omissible (unlike English "the man I saw"). `quien(es)` is restricted to
people and never takes an article, but is required after prepositions when there's no comma pause.
`el cual/la cual/...` can refer to people, animals, or things and is common after prepositions
without a comma. `cuyo` ("whose") agrees in gender/number with the *possessed* noun that follows it,
not with the possessor. (pp. 113–116)

### Prepositional coverage of "at/in/on" (Unit 10) and movement prepositions (Unit 11)

Spanish collapses English's three-way at/in/on distinction mostly onto `en`, with `sobre` reserved
for explicit surface contact/covering and `a` for movement-toward or specific points (time, rank,
figures). Movement prepositions require verb-specific choices rather than a single equivalent:
`meter/poner...en` (put into), `sacar...de` (take out of), `entrar en` vs. `pasar a` (go into),
`montarse en/subirse a` vs. `bajarse de` (get on/off a vehicle), `atravesar/cruzar` (cross,
completion-implying) vs. `a través de` (across, manner-neutral). Directional nuance is often carried
by a postposed noun + `arriba/abajo` construction (`calle arriba` = up the street) rather than a
preposition alone. (pp. 149–174)

### Idiomatic constructions (Unit 12) — non-compositional fixed expressions

The book catalogs roughly 90 fixed idioms (alphabetized by a key noun: agua, aire, boca, cabeza,
casa, codo, lengua, etc.), each with a paraphrased meaning and an illustrative sentence. These are
non-compositional — their meaning cannot be derived from the literal sense of the component words
(e.g. `cortar el bacalao`, literally "to cut the codfish," means "to be in charge"). See the
Vocabulary table above for the full catalog extracted from this unit.

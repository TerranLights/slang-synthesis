## Grammar points (repurposed: sociolinguistic/language-policy concepts)

### Part I framing note
Part I (Chs.1-3) is the "theory → Spanish-world application" arc: Ch.1 is purely theoretical
(general nationalism theory, almost no Spanish-world examples by the book's own admission, p.xi-xii),
Ch.2 traces Castilian's own rise to dominance, Ch.3 inventories the competing/minority languages
Castilian's dominance coexists with and ultimately displaces in both Spain and Latin America.

### Ch.1 — Theories of nationalism (pp.2-16)
The book synthesizes several nationalism theorists (not fully duplicated here — see Vocabulary
rows above for named concepts) to build an analytic toolkit used throughout the rest of the book:

- **Nation vs. state are not the same thing** (pp.4-5) — nations can straddle state borders (the
  book's own cross-border examples: Catalans/Basques split between Spain and France; the Aymara
  nation across several Andean states) or exist as enclaves within a state (the Welsh in Britain).
  Most 19th/20th-c. nation-building was the triumph of a majority group imposing a monocultural
  state identity on minorities, sometimes unconsciously/assimilationist (post-revolutionary France),
  sometimes deliberately oppressive.
- **Cultural vs. political nationalism** (pp.5-6) — a standard (if admittedly over-simplified, p.5)
  dichotomy the book keeps reusing: political/"civic" nationalism (Rousseau-derived, a consciously
  chosen democratic community defined by shared institutions — e.g. French was made France's national
  language even though under half the population spoke it natively at the time, p.5) vs. cultural/
  "ethnic" nationalism (objective markers — language, territory, race, shared history — rooted in
  Herder and 19th-c. Romanticism, which the book also links to Latin American *indigenismo*, p.6).
- **Herder's language-nationalism thesis** (pp.6-10) — Johann Gottfried Herder (1744-1803) is
  identified as the founding theorist linking language to national identity: "reason and language are
  co-terminous"; a people's language is treated as inseparable from its soul/spirit (quoted via
  Fishman, p.9: "with language is created the heart of a people"). The book stresses Herder himself
  was not xenophobic, unlike his more radical followers Von Humboldt and Fichte, whose purity-of-
  language/purity-of-race arguments the book traces forward toward 19th/20th-c. racial nationalism and
  ultimately Nazism (p.7). Herder's ideas are flagged as directly influential on 19th-century Catalan
  nationalism (p.7) — a thread picked up again in Ch.8.
- **The dialect/language distinction as a political act, not a linguistic fact** (pp.10-13) — Billig
  (1995) and Hobsbawm (1990) are the book's key sources here: "more is at stake in drawing the
  boundary of a language than linguistics… the power to define language" (Billig, quoted p.11).
  Nation-builders may downgrade a minority variety to "dialect" status to weaken its claims; minority
  communities may conversely push to have their variety recognized as a separate "language" to bolster
  their own national claim. The book explicitly flags this tension as live in Catalan, Galician,
  Breton, and Corsican cases (p.11) and ties written standardization (literacy, print) to the
  conversion of a "dialect" into a recognized "language."
- **Anderson's "imagined community" and print-capitalism** (pp.12-14) — Benedict Anderson's thesis
  (*Imagined Communities*) that the rise of vernacular print replacing Latin created the standardized
  national "norm," fixed language across space/time, and created linguistic hierarchies privileging
  the standardized variety over "deviant" ones. This is the book's working explanation for *why*
  standardization campaigns (like Castilianization, Ch.2) carry real political stakes beyond
  communication.

### Ch.2 — The "Castilianisation" process (pp.17-37)

**Castilian's rise in the Iberian Peninsula (pp.17-26).** Pre-Roman linguistic evidence is minor
except Basque, which pre-dates Roman settlement and is unrelated to the Latin-derived continuum
(p.18) — flagged here, fully treated in Ch.3. By the 8th century, 5 distinct Latin-derived vernacular
groups had emerged alongside Basque: Galaico-Portuguese, Asturian-Leonese, Castilian, Aragonese, and
Catalan (Diez et al. 1977, p.18). The 711-1492 Arab occupation affected regions unevenly — the north
(Basque- and Catalan-speaking areas) was only briefly occupied and so less Arabized, while Castile's
central role in the Reconquista (symbolically, recapturing Toledo in 1085) positioned Castilian as the
Christian-opposition language. Key dates the book treats as pivotal: 1469 (Isabella of Castile +
Ferdinand of Aragon marriage, "beginnings of modern Spain," though still a loose federation, not a
modern unitary state, p.20); 1492 (fall of Granada + Columbus's voyage + Nebrija's first Castilian
grammar, a triple symbolic coincidence, p.19-20); 1713 (founding of the RAE under Philip V); 1768
(Charles III's decree mandating Castilian in administration and education throughout the kingdom —
the first explicit language-in-education policy, p.22, directly impacting Catalonia where some
education had already begun in Catalan).

The book is explicit that political and linguistic unity lagged well behind rhetoric — as late as 1624
Olivares's secret memorandum to Philip IV urged reducing Aragon, Valencia, Portugal etc. to "the style
and laws of Castile," which the book reads as evidence these regions still held separate identities at
that date (quoting Siguan 1993, p.20-21). The 18th-century Bourbon centralization (following the War
of Spanish Succession, which cost Catalonia, Valencia, and Mallorca politically) is identified as the
turning point where linguistic uniformity became a *directly sought* objective rather than a side
effect of political unification (Siguan 1993, p.21-22). The Church and the army are named as two
further institutional vectors spreading Castilian regardless of a conscript's or parishioner's mother
tongue (p.22-23).

**The '98 Generation (p.23-25).** After the 1898 loss of Spain's last colonies, a group of
intellectuals (the "98 Generation," including Miguel de Unamuno — himself ethnically Basque but a
committed Castilian-supremacist) promoted Castile/Castilian as the core of Spanish identity, often
while being personally hostile to the contemporaneous Catalan/Galician literary revivals (explicitly
named: Unamuno's correspondence with Catalan poet Joan Maragall, p.24; Rosalía de Castro — "one of the
most important figures of Galician letters" — nonetheless switching to Castilian-only writing in her
later years, p.25, flagged for fuller Galician treatment in Ch.3).

**20th-century landmarks named in passing** (fully developed later, per the book's own forward
pointers): Second Republic (1931) granting autonomy statutes to Catalonia, the Basque Country, and
Galicia, cut short by the Civil War (1936-39) (p.25); the Franco regime's re-centralization, deferred
to Ch.5 (p.26).

**Spanish in the Americas (pp.26-37).** Confirmed list of the 18 Spanish-official-language independent
states named by the book (p.26-27): Caribbean — Cuba, Dominican Republic; Central America — Mexico,
Guatemala, Honduras, El Salvador, Costa Rica, Nicaragua, Panama; South America — Colombia, Venezuela,
Ecuador, Peru, Chile, Bolivia, Paraguay, Uruguay, Argentina. Plus Puerto Rico (co-official with
English, "US protectorate") and the US Latino population (mother tongue of a significant population,
not official).

Named pre-conquest indigenous lingua francas (highest-priority regional-language data, captured as
Vocabulary rows above): Quechua (Incan empire, Andean region), Nahuatl (Aztec empire, Mexico), Mayan
(Yucatan Peninsula), Guaraní (Paraguay), Mapuche/Araucano (Chile) — p.27.

Three types of colonial-era pressure toward Castilianization, per Cerrón-Palomino 1989 (p.28):
**political** (centralization — the Spanish American empire was organized into 4 large Viceroyalties
dependent on the Crown), **cultural-religious** (belief that operating only in a native mother tongue
kept a population "outside" Christian values), and **linguistic** (the colonial-era belief, which the
book explicitly flags as a value judgment rather than a linguistic fact, that indigenous languages
were inherently too poor in grammar/semantics to convey Christian doctrine, p.28). Policy landmark:
1634, Philip IV's instruction to use "the gentlest possible means" to teach Indians Spanish (Von
Gleich 1994, p.28); Jesuit expulsion from Latin America in 1767 removed the strongest institutional
counterweight committed to working through indigenous languages.

**Post-independence (19th c.) nation-building (pp.30-33).** New republics' borders ignored pre-conquest
indigenous "nations," splitting coherent language communities across new state lines — explicit named
examples: Mayans split between Mexico and Guatemala; Aymara people split between Peru and Bolivia
(p.30, repeating the Ch.1 example). Simón Bolívar's Rousseau-influenced vision made Spanish a
deliberately promoted marker of citizenship and national identity, paralleling French in
post-revolutionary France (p.30). Alvar's (1986) survey of 19th-c. Latin American constitutions found
Spanish-literacy requirements for citizenship in many early constitutions — effectively
disenfranchising large indigenous populations until requirements were gradually loosened (p.31-32).
Andrés Bello's 1847 grammar (Venezuela) is cited as the key moment defending the unity of the Spanish
language against regional-dialect fragmentation pressures, even as new republics simultaneously set
up their own national language academies (p.33).

**"Español" vs. "Castellano" naming debate (pp.33-36) — a genuinely distinct sociolinguistic-policy
concept, not just a vocabulary note.** The book treats the two labels as politically loaded, not
interchangeable:
- In Spain, "Castilian" is sometimes preferred specifically to flag that Spain contains other
  "Spanish" languages too (Basque, Catalan) — both the 1931 Second Republic constitution and the 1978
  post-Franco constitution deliberately used "Castilian," not "Spanish," for this reason (p.35).
- In Latin America usage is inconsistent and country-specific: Alvar's (1986) constitutional survey
  found "Spanish" predominant overall but "Castilian" used instead in Ecuador, Panama, Paraguay, El
  Salvador, and Venezuela (p.35-36). In a Guatemalan attitude survey (Alvar 1986, ch.3), "Castilian"
  was preferred specifically because "Spanish" was felt to associate the language with Spain (and thus
  not with Guatemala), while "Castilian" carried no such foreign association for speakers there despite
  its literal origin in Castile (p.36).
- Contrast case: in the US, Spanish-speakers tend to dislike "Castilian" for the opposite reason — it
  sounds tied to a specific Spanish region they have no connection to (p.36).
- Illustrative quote, Octavio Paz (Mexican writer, Nobel laureate): "Yo me siento ciudadano de la
  lengua española y no ciudadano mexicano; por eso me molesta mucho que se hable de lengua castellana,
  porque el castellano es de los castellanos y yo no lo soy; yo soy mexicano y como mexicano hablo
  español y no castellano" (cited in Salvador 1987:92, quoted at p.36) — a named individual's explicit
  rejection of "castellano" as a self-identification term.

### Ch.3 — Counter-nationalism and the other languages (pp.38-60) — HIGHEST-PRIORITY CHAPTER

This is the book's most concentrated source of explicit minority/regional-language status
annotations; every named language and its noted region/status is captured as a Vocabulary row above.
This section summarizes the comparative analysis the book builds around them.

**Framing (pp.38-40).** The book picks exactly 3 Spanish minority languages for sustained treatment —
Catalan, Basque, Galician — explicitly because they hold "comunidades históricas" status and have
large populations/political weight, while flagging (p.39) that this choice is itself contestable:
other claimants exist (Valencian as distinct from Catalan; Asturian; Aragonese) and the book
explicitly declines to adjudicate those disputes, treating the language/dialect boundary question
(per Ch.1's Billig/Hobsbawm framework) as inherently political rather than linguistically decidable.
Illustrative anecdote used to frame the whole chapter (p.38): at the 1992 Barcelona Olympics, Catalan
was used before Spanish in announcements and the Catalan flag flown alongside the Spanish one —
contrasted with the explicitly cited case of Aymara speakers crossing the Peru-Bolivia border daily,
"completely unaware" of the national-border concept — bookending the chapter's comparison between
Spain's assertive minority nationalisms and Latin America's largely disenfranchised indigenous ones.

**Comparative Catalan/Basque/Galician table (synthesized from pp.40-51, not a table in the source):**

- **Catalan nationalism** — base: broad, cross-class (eventually incl. working class, p.44); core
  identity marker: language (explicitly "learnable," so inclusive, p.48); political trigger: cultural
  revival (Renaixença, from 1830s-40s; Jocs Florals poetry festivals revived 1859) preceding political
  organization; key texts/figures: Pi i Margall (federalist precursor, *Las Nacionalidades* 1877,
  influenced by Proudhon and Romanticism, p.41-42); Valentí Almirall (*Lo Catalanisme*, 1886, "founder
  of modern political Catalan nationalism," names language the top Catalan "core value," p.42-43);
  1892 Bases de Manresa (Catalanist political manifesto demanding autonomy + Catalan as sole official
  language, p.42-43); Enric Prat de la Riba (*La Nacionalitat Catalana*, 1892, explicitly Herder-derived
  via the Catalan philosopher Llorens i Barba, p.43); Pompeu Fabra's 1913-1932 codification
  (orthography, grammar, dictionary) under the Institut d'Estudis Catalans (est. 1907), explicitly
  framed by the book via Anderson's print-language/standardization model (p.44). Institutional
  lineage: Mancomunitat (1914, grouping Barcelona/Tarragona/Girona/Lleida) → Generalitat (Second
  Republic, revived post-Franco, detailed in Ch.5). Repression: Primo de Rivera dictatorship
  (1923-1930) banned Catalan symbols/teaching; Franco repression resumed after 1939 (deferred to Ch.5).
- **Basque nationalism** — base: initially narrow/rural reactive (unlike Catalan's elite-led,
  cross-class movement); core identity marker: historically **race**, not language (founder Sabino
  Arana required proof of 2 generations' Basque ancestry via 4 Basque surnames, p.48) — language only
  became a more central, inclusive marker later, during ETA's era (deferred to later chapters, p.48);
  political trigger: political organizing (Arana's PNV) *preceded* any real cultural-linguistic revival
  — the reverse order from Catalonia/Galicia (p.47). Arana (1865-1903) himself was not a native
  Euskera speaker; he "invented" the name Euskadi, the flag, the anthem, and promoted a Basque grammar
  after observing Catalan nationalism's strength while studying in Barcelona (p.47). Explicit quote
  flagged by the book as revealing Arana's race-over-language priority (p.48): he states he'd prefer a
  Vizcaya of Spanish-speaking ethnic Basques ("bizcainos") over one of ethnic-Spanish Euskera-speakers
  ("maketos") — language is a *racial* boundary marker for him, not an intrinsic value. Navarra (the
  former kingdom, distinct from "Euskadi" proper = Álava/Vizcaya/Guipúzcoa) has some Basque speakers
  but the book stresses its history/politics (its own *fueros*, kept even under Franco, unlike the
  Basque Country's) are largely separate from Basque nationalism (p.48-49).
- **Galician nationalism** — base: smallest and latest-organized of the three — no political
  movement until 1916 (Irmandade da Fala, a language-protection "brotherhood," p.50), described by the
  book as "timid" compared to Catalan/Basque movements; core identity marker: language, as with
  Catalan, but marked by persistent ambivalence/felt inferiority even among its own champions — poet
  Rosalía de Castro (author of the pivotal 1863 *Cantares Gallegos*, the key text of the 1860s
  Rexurdimento revival) herself switched to Castilian-only writing late in life (p.49-50). Galician's
  "golden period" was earlier and more purely literary than Catalan's: 12th-15th c. lyric poetry tied
  to the Santiago de Compostela pilgrimage route, carried by troubadours as far as France (p.49),
  contrasted with a near-total subsequent loss of public/official status. The book flags an ongoing
  three-way identity "tug" between Galician, Portuguese (its closest relative), and Castilian,
  deferred to Ch.5 (p.50).

**Common factors identified by the book across all 3 Spanish cases (p.50-51 conclusion):** the two
most *successful* counter-nationalisms (Catalan, Basque) are also the two with the strongest regional
economies relative to the rest of Spain; in all 3 Spanish cases, elite/middle-class support was a
necessary ingredient — which the book explicitly contrasts (p.51) with Latin America, where indigenous
movements have almost never had elite/middle-class backing (the sole partial exception named:
Paraguay).

**Latin America's "other" languages (pp.51-60).** The book gives a geographic survey of pre-conquest
language distribution (captured as Vocabulary rows above: Nahuatl/central Mexico, Mayan/Yucatán-
Guatemala, Carib-Arawak-Taíno-Siboney/Caribbean, Chibcha/Costa Rica-Colombia, Quechua/Andes at Inca
empire's height reaching south Colombia to north Chile, Aymara/south Peru-Bolivia-Chile-Paraguay,
Mapuche-Araucano/Chile, Guaraní/the "River Plate" states). Also named, briefly, as non-indigenous,
non-Castilian immigrant-origin linguistic minorities with limited wider influence (p.52): German
speakers in Venezuela and Paraguay (missionary-descended, insular); Italian-immigrant influence on
Argentine Spanish specifically (identified by its speakers as part of Argentine identity, unlike the
other immigrant cases); Welsh-speakers in Argentina; retained Galician/Catalan/Basque-speaking
immigrant communities in Latin America; and a brief note that Spanish-based Creoles are rare in Latin
America (mostly limited to parts of the Caribbean) despite large-scale African slave immigration,
contrasted with the much more visible influence of African culture on music/food/art/religion than on
language (p.52-53).

**Three case studies developed in depth — Nahuatl, Quechua, Guaraní (pp.53-59)** — captured in full
in the Vocabulary rows above (status, era, decline trajectory, population figures where the book
gives them). Key cross-case pattern the book draws out: all three were pre-conquest administrative
lingua francas, but only Guaraní achieved lasting *official* status alongside Spanish — attributed to
the unique combination of Jesuit-mission language policy + Paraguay's unusual geographic/political
isolation (detailed above). The book is careful to note (p.59) that even Guaraní's success is a story
of **state-identity absorption, not of a surviving rival "nation"**: "There is not a Guaraní nation
and a Spanish-speaking nation, but only a Paraguayan one" (p.60) — a sharp contrast with Catalan,
which the book treats as a genuinely surviving rival "nation" alongside the Spanish state.

**Chapter conclusion, "The triumph of Spanish" (pp.59-60).** The chapter's own closing thesis: in
Spain, Catalan in particular has survived as a true counter-national identity; in Latin America, no
indigenous language has done so in the same way — even Guaraní's survival reads as absorbed into,
not competing with, the dominant national identity. *Mestizaje* (racial mixing) happened throughout
Latin America, but the book explicitly denies this amounts to real cultural/linguistic integration or
assimilation — claims of unified "Mexicanness" or "Peruvianness" are called "very tenuous" (p.60).
Spanish's overall dominance in public life, media, and social advancement across the whole
Spanish-speaking world is, nonetheless, total and (per the book, writing in 2000) effectively
unchallenged, even as late-20th-century politics shows "a greater readiness to tolerate...a more
pluralistic vision" (p.60) — setting up Part II's turn to legislation and policy.

### Ch.4 — Language rights, language policies, and Language Planning (pp.62-77)

A deliberately theoretical chapter (per the book's own Introduction framing, p.xi) with almost no
Spanish-world examples — it builds the analytic toolkit Ch.5 then applies.

**Linguistic rights (pp.62-68).** Skutnabb-Kangas & Phillipson's "three generations" of human rights
(civil/political → economic/social/cultural → "solidarity" rights, p.63) rarely mentioned language
explicitly until the 1996 UNESCO-adjacent Universal Declaration of Linguistic Rights (drafted at a
Barcelona meeting of International PEN + CIEMEN + NGOs, June 1996, p.63) — this Declaration names both
individual rights (to be recognized as a member of a linguistic community, to use one's language
privately/publicly) and collective rights (education, media, official/administrative use in one's own
language, p.63). Chen's (1998) definition stresses the state's dual obligation of non-interference and
positive assistance (p.64). Key recurring tension: **individual vs. collective rights** — Hamel
(1997a) argues collective rights are more controversial (can conflict with individual rights, threaten
entrenched power structures, p.64) but may be unavoidable for genuinely protecting a minority language
(p.65). This maps onto two competing legal principles (captured as Vocabulary rows above): the
**territoriality principle** (collective, place-bound) vs. the **personality principle** (individual,
portable) — the book notes real-world policy increasingly mixes both (p.65-66), to be tested against
Spain/Latin America in Ch.5.

Further rights dichotomies: **expressive vs. communicative function** of a linguistic right (Hamel,
p.65); De Witte's (1985/1993) **"rights to freedom" vs. "rights to equality"** — freedom is a
"negative" right (the state need only not prevent mother-tongue use, but has no duty to accommodate
it) vs. equality, which De Witte argues should NOT mean sameness but "identical in substance,
differentiated in form" (p.66) — i.e. genuine linguistic equality is compatible with, and may require,
differentiated treatment, not uniform treatment. Important caution (Brookes & Brice Heath 1997, p.67,
and Fishman 1994, p.67-68): granting linguistic rights is not automatically benevolent — states may
grant minority-language education or rights precisely to keep a group from accessing dominant-language
information/legal/economic resources (e.g. land, mineral, oil rights contexts); and "ethnolinguistic
democracy" (all languages equally valid as identity markers) can conflict with "ethnolinguistic
equality" (equal practical viability as vehicles of communication) — a tension the book flags as
unresolved and often politically loaded (Fishman 1994:51, quoted p.68).

**Language policies (pp.68-72).** Distinguished from Language Planning (LP): policy = decisions/
choices (the "what"), LP = implementation mechanisms (the "how") — a distinction the book credits to
Joan Rubin (1977) and defends against the field's more common practice of conflating the two. Turi's
(1995) four-way typology of language legislation functions: **official** (legal status for
governmental/public use), **institutionalising** (making a language normal/common in unofficial
domains — labor, commerce, culture), **standardising** (governing the form/corpus of the official
language), and **liberal** (enshrining the rights discussed above) (p.69). Policy can be covert —
e.g. the UK has no formal law naming English official, yet its status is "entirely evident" through
the national curriculum and legal-document practice (p.70) — whereas LP, by the book's definition, is
always explicit and overt. Critical-theory caution (Glyn Williams 1992, Blommaert 1996, p.70-71):
20th-century Language Planning has been fundamentally **eurocentric**, built on a 19th-century
modernization ideology that treated monolingual nation-states as "modern/efficient" and multilingual
societies as "traditional/inefficient" — explicitly named by the book as the prevailing ideology in
post-independence Latin American republics (p.70-71) (directly connects back to Ch.2's Castilianization
analysis).

**Language Planning typology (pp.72-76) — the chapter's central "mechanics" content, exactly the kind
of framework the dispatch brief asks this source to supply.** LP as an academic discipline dates to
the 1960s (Haugen 1966 usually credited as founder of the field) and arose specifically to serve
post-colonial nation-building. After an initial **selection** stage (choosing an official/national
language — properly a policy decision, not LP proper), LP proceeds through: **norm codification/
standardization** (grammar, lexicon, pronunciation), **functional implementation**, and **functional
elaboration** (p.73). These stages are implemented via three (later four) categories:
- **Corpus planning** — the form of the language itself: writing-system choice/development (e.g.
  adapting Amerindian languages to the Latin alphabet), spelling-norm standardization, new
  terminology/vocabulary elaboration for expanding domains. Explicitly flagged by the book as
  especially significant for Galician and Quechua standardization (p.73) and as *not* value-free —
  standardization choices are themselves a site of covert policy-making by linguists, not just
  neutral technical description (p.73).
- **Status planning** — promoting a language's prestige/domain of use (government, judiciary,
  public-figure usage, attitude campaigns, resourcing).
- **Acquisition planning** (Cooper 1989's addition to Kloss's original corpus/status pair) — how a
  language is actually learned/acquired, chiefly through education and media (p.73-74).
- **Normalization planning** (Catalan sociolinguistic tradition — Aracil, Ninyoles, Valverdú) — see
  Vocabulary row above; the book flags this as "widely accepted in Spain but less known" elsewhere
  (p.75), to be applied to Spain's autonomous communities in Ch.5.

Glyn Williams (1992) stresses corpus and status planning are tightly linked but distinct: status
planning addresses the relative standing of *different languages* (official/tolerated/minoritized),
while corpus-planning standardization addresses the relative standing of *varieties within* a
language — and when a minority language expands into new domains, this itself creates pressure for
new corpus-planning work, not because the language is intrinsically "deficient" but because of its
changed "social reconstitution" (p.74-75, quoting Williams 1992:147). Minority languages gaining
official/expanded status also tend to develop genuine class-based social variation (not just
geographic variation) for the first time, which itself then requires further corpus-planning
standardization work (p.75).

**Language and power (pp.76-77).** Closing framing for Part II: all of the above — rights, policy, LP
— ultimately reduces to a question of power: who decides who speaks/learns/teaches which language(s),
who controls resources and access. Tollefson (1995) warning explicitly quoted (p.76): state language
policies framed in the rhetoric of "equality" and "opportunity" frequently in practice "channel
migrants and other linguistic minorities into low-paying jobs in the peripheral economy." Colin
Williams's (1991) "language ecology" framing — diversity as strength, by analogy with
biological/ecological diversity, versus centralist neglect of lesser-used languages as "a thinly
veiled championing of powerful, centralist forces" (p.77) — is flagged as the lens the book will use,
in Ch.5 onward, when asking whether Spanish-speaking-world language policy tends toward hegemonic
monolingualism or genuine pluralism, and whether any Latin American languages should be treated as
"endangered" (explicit analogy to endangered species, p.77).

**Negative finding:** the term "diglossia" (expected per the dispatch brief as a likely framework
concept for this source) was **not found** anywhere in Ch.4's extensive rights/policy/LP vocabulary.
The book's functional equivalents are the status-planning category and the territoriality/personality
principles. Will re-check in Chs.5-10 before treating this as a whole-book absence.

### Ch.5 — The state and language policies in the contemporary Spanish-speaking world (pp.78-103)

**Franco-era language repression (pp.78-81).** Franco's regime (1939-75, self-styled "Nationalists")
systematically denied every linguistic right named in Ch.4: early years saw outright prohibition of
non-Castilian languages in public/teaching, with fines/imprisonment; gradual relaxation from the
1950s-60s (1966 "Law of Freedom of Expression" replaced prior censorship with self-censorship); by the
1970s Catalan was taught as an academic-curiosity subject at Barcelona University. The book argues the
regime's relaxation was not real tolerance but confidence the minority languages were too marginal to
threaten central hegemony — reinforced by officially labeling them "dialects" and confining their
media use to "trivial" local/folksy content while news/politics stayed in Castilian (p.80). Noted
wordplay: "castellano" = "cristiano" = "español" conflation used to imply moral superiority of
Castilian (p.80). Unintended consequence flagged as historically important: the regime's ridicule of
minority languages made them a rallying point for *anti*-regime opposition — explicit named case: the
Catalan "Nova Cançó" protest song movement, concerts broken up by riot police (p.81); also noted,
briefly, Basque identity's association with the ETA terrorist movement (p.80-81). A further
demographic detail: large-scale Castilian-speaking internal migration into Catalonia/Basque Country
(1940s-50s, concentrated in Barcelona/Bilbao) brought Catalonia's non-native-Catalan population to
~50% by the 1970s (Ninyoles 1977) — yet integration/language-learning attitudes among migrants were
favorable, attributed to the shared anti-Franco political identification (p.81).

**1978 Constitution, Articles 2 and 3 (pp.81-86) — the book's most detailed close-reading of any legal
text in the whole book.** Article 2 reconciles "indisoluble unidad de la Nación española" with
recognized "derecho a la autonomía de las nacionalidades y regiones" — the book stresses the careful
distinction between capital-N "Nación" (the state) and lowercase "nacionalidades" (substate
nation-like regions), a term with no clear legal weight but major symbolic significance (p.82).
Article 3 analyzed clause by clause (text given above in Vocabulary context notes): (1) names
"Castilian," not "Spanish," as the State's official language and imposes a *duty* to know it (unusual
— most constitutions grant a *right*, not a duty, to know a language, p.83) alongside a right to use
it; (2) grants co-official status to unnamed "other Spanish languages" within their own "respective
Comunidades" — the book flags that Valencia successfully fought to have its variety named separately
from Catalan in this process despite producing an almost-identical constitutional translation (p.84);
(3) vaguely promises "respect and protection" to further "modalidades lingüísticas" (regional Castilian
dialects — see Vocabulary row). Key structural point the book stresses repeatedly: Clause 2 applies
the **territoriality principle** to the co-official non-Castilian languages (confined to their home
region) while Clause 1's Castilian right is a **personality-principle** right (usable anywhere in
Spain) — creating an asymmetry the book flags as in real tension with Article 14's equal-treatment
guarantee (p.85). Cites legal scholar Vernet i Llobet's (1994) critical reading: Article 3 in practice
establishes "doble oficialidad" (dual officiality) rather than genuine "bilingüismo" (bilingualism)
(p.86) — i.e., parallel separate-domain official status, not shared equal bilingual status. Also notes
a counter-current of opposition from centralist defenders of Castilian-only Spanish (named: RAE
linguist Gregorio Salvador, explicitly hostile to multilingualism as inherently "problematic," quoted
p.86).

**Comparative LP outcomes in the 3 comunidades históricas (pp.86-91) — a genuinely useful comparative
table-equivalent, synthesized here:**
- **Catalonia**: by far the most successful — see Vocabulary row for 1996 census figures (94.9%
  understand, 75.3% speak Catalan). Catalan dominant/default in public life (shops, transport,
  government, media, menus, street names); Castilian used "on request." Institutional apparatus:
  Directorate of Language Policy + Institute for Catalan Sociolinguistics under the Generalitat.
  Success attributed to strong regional economy, self-confident identity, and — crucially — that the
  local middle class is Catalan-speaking, making the language a genuine "passport to upward mobility"
  even for non-native immigrants (p.88). An internal controversy the book flags (p.88-89): some
  Catalanists now argue equality-with-Castilian LP goals were set too low and argue instead for Catalan
  near-monolingual *dominance*, which the book calls "highly controversial" both constitutionally and
  for Castilian-mother-tongue Catalonia residents.
- **Basque Country**: much lower starting point (~25% of Euskadi speaks Basque, ~10% in Navarra) but
  rising bilingualism among younger generations, credited mainly to compulsory-education Basque
  teaching (status + acquisition planning gains, p.89-90). Corpus-planning challenge: codifying the
  single "Batua" standard dialect for teaching/media caused resentment among other-dialect speakers
  (see Vocabulary row).
- **Galicia**: weakest institutional push despite the *highest* native-speaker rate (>80%) of the
  three — attributed explicitly to an unsympathetic, centralist-leaning regional government (the
  Xunta, controlled by Partido Popular) providing only "lukewarm" support (p.90), plus the internal
  "reintegrationist" vs. official-Xunta-norm corpus-planning split (see Vocabulary row) weakening a
  unified promotion effort, and the closeness of Galician to Castilian both helping comprehension and
  encouraging erosive code-switching/borrowing, especially in cities (p.90).

**Latin America: "invisible nations" (pp.91-103).** Framing thesis: far less status/corpus/acquisition
LP has occurred than in Spain; what exists is concentrated almost entirely in education (deferred to
Ch.7). Key structural point (Hamel 1994b, p.92): in Latin America, language rights are inseparable
from land rights, political self-determination, and recognition of separate ethnicity — language is
rarely the lead demand but is "frequently one of—or even the—key marker of different ethnic
boundaries" (Hamel 1994b:297, quoted p.92). Historical pattern: many Latin American states have
historically denied having multiethnic populations at all — e.g., several states (Chile, Venezuela,
Peru, Bolivia, Colombia, Mexico) told the UN's 1965 Convention on Racial Discrimination that racial
discrimination simply did not exist in their countries (explicit 1974 Peru report quote, p.93) — a
denial the book reads as evidence of an equally strong unwillingness to recognize linguistic-minority
rights specifically (p.93).

**Three-way territorial typology of Latin American indigenous groups (p.94, a genuinely useful
analytic framework):** (1) groups with a clearly bounded, exclusive territory (e.g. Amazon basin
tribes) where a straightforward territoriality principle could apply, though risking the "enclave"
problem flagged in Ch.4; (2) groups sharing a territorial base with non-indigenous populations (e.g.
Quechua/Aymara in the Andes), where territoriality and personality rights would need to coexist; (3)
migrants to urban centers (e.g. Quechua/Aymara speakers migrating to Lima, or to La Paz, Bolivia) —
the least protected group, facing near-total assimilation pressure and (notably) frequent *voluntary*
mother-tongue non-transmission to children by migrant parents wanting their children to have immediate
Spanish access (p.95).

**National legislation patterns (pp.96-99).** Alvar's (1986) national/official distinction and the
Ecuador case (1929/1945/1946 shifts) are the book's key worked example (Vocabulary row above). Also
named: Paraguay uses "national" for Guaraní; Guatemala calls its non-Castilian languages part of
national "patrimony"; Peru extends the same patrimony framing to its Amazonian languages and grants
Quechua/Aymara actual co-official (territorial) status (Vocabulary row above); Nicaragua applies the
same territoriality logic in its Atlantic Coast autonomous regions. Mexico's INI/Dirección General de
Educación Indígena and the proposed pluri-cultural constitutional amendment, plus Oaxaca state-level
legislation, are the book's most developed Mexican example (p.99).

**Corpus planning case studies (pp.99-102) — three worked examples, each illustrating the "corpus
planning is never neutral" thesis from Ch.4:**
1. **Mayan (Guatemala)** — elaborating a "general Mayan" written standard for bilingual-education
   materials; orthography debate between Spanish-alphabet-based norms (historically pushed by
   missionaries and the Summer Institute of Linguistics, SIL, partly to ease eventual transition to
   Spanish literacy) and more phonologically authentic alternative norms promoted by native speakers
   and an unofficial Mayan academy (p.99-100).
2. **Quechua/Aymara (Bolivia, then Peru)** — alphabet disputes over vowel count (3 vs. 5), the letter
   x vs. j, and apostrophe-vs-h for aspiration (Plaza & Albó 1989, p.100); Peru's 1985 three-vowel
   decision is the book's most detailed case (see Vocabulary row) — explicitly tied to a Lima-vs-Cuzco
   regional rivalry and a Cuzco-elite claim to "authentic," Inca-descended linguistic legitimacy,
   pitting the Cuzco Academy of the Quechua Language (backed by the SIL) against language planners and
   bilingual educators favoring more inclusive, pan-dialectal norms (p.101).
3. **Spanish itself (Mexico)** — corpus planning isn't only for minority languages: Mexico's own local
   language academies (loosely tied to Madrid's RAE) and the *Diccionario del español de México* (Lara
   1992) raise the status of Mexican Spanish specifically relative to Peninsular Spanish (p.102).

**Chapter conclusion (pp.102-103).** The book's own summary judgment: Spanish remains dominant and
unthreatened across the whole Spanish-speaking world; where minority groups have made real gains, it
is attributed to factors largely outside direct state control — economic strength (Catalans),
demographic visibility amplified by modern media/transport (Mexican indigenous communities),
international ecological-advocacy pressure (Amazonian tribes), or international political visibility
(Guatemalan Mayan-speakers benefiting indirectly from Rigoberta Menchú's Nobel Peace Prize, explicitly
named, p.103). Grassroots/non-state organizing (the Chiapas Zapatista rising named as the paradigm
case, plus women's groups) is flagged as an increasingly important channel distinct from
state-granted rights (p.102).

### Ch.6 — Bilingual education, literacy, and language in education systems (pp.105-121)

Another deliberately general/theoretical chapter (per the book's own stated design, p.xi) — almost
no Spanish-world case studies here; those are deferred to Chs.7-8. Core content: the standard 4-model
bilingual-education typology (Vocabulary row above) and a parallel functional-vs-critical literacy
dichotomy, both framed by Benedict Anderson's print-capitalism thesis (recurring from Ch.1) and by
Paulo Freire's critique of traditional literacy pedagogy as a disempowering "banking" model (depositing
knowledge into a presumed-empty learner, pp.117-119) — Freire (Brazilian educator, 1921-1996) is
introduced here as the single most influential figure for Ch.7's Latin American radical literacy
campaigns. Key framework point carried forward: Skutnabb-Kangas's demanding definition of "bilingual"
(native-level competence in both languages PLUS positive identification with both language groups,
p.108) sets a bar most real-world bilingual-education programs (especially assimilationist/transitional
ones) do not actually meet — useful for judging the Catalonia/Basque/Latin America case studies in
Chs.7-8 against a clear standard rather than loosely using "bilingual education" as an unqualified
success label. Also flagged: a 1951 UNESCO recommendation that children ideally be taught in their
mother tongue through primary school, and a 1977 EC directive recommending mother-tongue teaching for
immigrant children — both cited as influential but non-binding, frequently implemented only minimally
(pp.108-109, 112). Mühlhäusler's analysis of literacy's broader societal effects (shift from
time-focused/cyclical to space-focused/linear community identity; "communicative inequalities and
decreasing heterogeneity," p.120-121) is used to explain why literacy campaigns, even well-intentioned
ones, can accelerate small-language death — directly relevant to the indigenous-language cases in
Ch.7. The Summer Institute of Linguistics (SIL) — already named in Ch.5's corpus-planning cases — is
revisited here specifically as the missionary wing of the Wycliffe Bible Translators, credited with
extensive indigenous-language elaboration work but criticized (via Barton 1994a) for an embedded
Christian/American ideological agenda (p.117).

### Ch.7 — Latin American educational policies in the struggle for linguistic rights (pp.123-148)

Dense, example-rich chapter applying Ch.6's bilingual-education typology to specific Latin American
countries. Framing fact (Hamel 1994a:276, p.123-124): only Bolivia, Peru, and Mexico's constitutions
specify literacy should be achieved through the mother tongue to facilitate Spanish transition;
Colombia, Ecuador, Panama, Guatemala decree "bilingual intercultural education" with no defined
purpose; Paraguay limits indigenous-language education to oral/audio skills only. General historical
arc (1960s on, Campos Carr 1990, p.124): illiteracy reframed from an irrelevant feature of marginal
populations to a "dysfunctional" barrier to economic development — i.e. the motive for expanding
indigenous education was economic-development ideology, not rights recognition per se, until a real
shift toward "bicultural/intercultural" (not merely "bilingual") models began in the 1970s.

**Mexico (pp.126-130).** Bilingual education introduced from the 1930s was purely transitional
(mother tongue oral use only, no mother-tongue literacy, explicit goal to *accelerate* — not
preserve — Castilianization, per Hidalgo 1994, quoted p.126). From 1978, the Dirección General de
Educación Indígena (DGEI, under SEP) + the already-noted INI shifted official rhetoric toward
"bilingual-bicultural education," targeting balanced bilingualism and indigenous-teacher-led delivery.
Mexico recognizes 56 minority languages (repeating the Ch.5 INI figure). Multiple commentators
(Coronado 1992, Hidalgo 1994, Lastra 1992) are cited giving a pessimistic verdict: materials are often
just translations of Spanish-language texts; teachers are frequently under-qualified in genuine
bilingual-teaching methodology; NAFTA-era (1990s) pressure toward English/Spanish as "languages of
wider communication" undercuts mother-tongue promotion; and — a distinct critique from Lastra — even
indigenous-run "indigenista" programs remain fundamentally Eurocentric in form (formal Western-style
schooling, literacy-centric), risking further Westernizing the very cultures they aim to protect. The
Chiapas situation is named again (p.130) as confirming continued marginalization despite the rhetoric
shift.

**Peru (pp.130-134).** Early-20th-c. "education impulse" was pure Westernizing assimilation —
explicitly blamed (Portocarrero 1992, quoted p.131) for encouraging rural-to-urban migration and
mother-tongue abandonment. The 1968 Velasco Alvarado left-military coup produced Peru's most radical
language-policy moment: 1975 Quechua "officialisation" (briefly full co-official national status),
reframed by 1979 to regional official status (alongside Aymara) only, and the 1972 education law's
PNEB policy (3 objectives captured as Vocabulary row above). The Puno bilingual project (Quechua +
Aymara, cross-border-relevant to Bolivia) is the chapter's single most detailed case study — full
maintenance model, locally redesigned curriculum tied to the regional agricultural economy, teacher
training through a dedicated Normal School + university postgraduate program in Andean linguistics —
judged a genuine pedagogical success but only a "partial" long-term policy success once national
political commitment weakened (1996 Bilingual Education Unit limited bilingual education to primary
level only).

**Bolivia (pp.134-136).** Highest indigenous-population share in South America (~63%, Hornberger &
López 1998) — Quechua (>2.5 million), Aymara (>1.5 million), plus Guaraní speakers (see Vocabulary
row for population figures). The 1952 Nationalist Revolution's education reforms pushed assimilation
("indio" → "campesino" relabeling explicitly named as "repressing difference" via an "ideology of
equality," Howard-Malverde & Canessa 1995, quoted p.134) alongside genuinely large-scale school
expansion. 1970s-80s bilingual efforts were mostly run by religious groups (Catholic Church, SIL) and
were largely transitional, with one maintenance-model exception. The 1994 Education Reform (Vocabulary
row above) is the chapter's single most positively evaluated case in the whole book — explicitly
"probably... a first in Latin America" for proposing Spanish-speaking children learn indigenous
languages, not just the reverse (p.136). Success attributed specifically to full government resourcing
and commitment, contrasted directly with Peru's and Mexico's rhetoric-exceeds-implementation pattern.

**Radical adult literacy campaigns (pp.137-148) — three contrasting case studies:**
- **Cuba** (pp.138-141): post-1959-revolution campaign, primer *Venceremos* (Vocabulary row above),
  reduced illiteracy from 21% to 3.9% in one year (1961) — but the book flags this as not easily
  generalizable: Cuba is monolingual Spanish, has a small population, and had an already-established
  literacy infrastructure, unlike multilingual indigenous contexts elsewhere. Explicitly ideological/
  political content (primer organized around revolutionary themes), criticized by some as
  indoctrination rather than neutral literacy teaching.
- **Nicaragua** (pp.141-145): Sandinista-era campaign, primer *El Amanecer del Pueblo* (Vocabulary
  row above), directly modeled on Cuba's campaign and on Paulo Freire's "generative word" method
  (Freire personally advised); dramatic Pacific-coast success initially, later substantially reversed
  post-1990. **The single richest minority-language episode in this chapter**: the Atlantic
  (Caribbean) coast — ethnically/linguistically/religiously distinct from the Pacific/mestizo coast
  (British-colonial and Moravian-missionary history vs. Spanish-Catholic) — resisted the Spanish-only
  literacy crusade via the MISURASATA movement (Miskitu + Sumu + Rama peoples), successfully forcing
  the Sandinistas to switch to mother-tongue literacy teaching there, which the book credits as a
  genuine empowerment turning point for Miskitu ethnic nationalism (Freeland 1995, quoted p.144) —
  followed by a 2.5-year Sandinista crackdown on MISURASATA leaders once land-rights/autonomy demands
  escalated, then eventual reconciliation via the 1987 Autonomy Law (Vocabulary row above). All
  captured as Vocabulary rows: Miskitu, Atlantic-coast Creole English, Sumu, Rama.
- **Guatemala** (pp.145-148): majority-Mayan-population country with severe dialectal fragmentation
  within the Mayan language family itself (sometimes mutually incomprehensible, p.145-146); post-1965
  "Castellanización Bilingüe" program (Vocabulary row above) judged largely unsuccessful; deep
  indigenous distrust of government literacy programs rooted in documented "Ladino" (Spanish-speaking
  elite) exploitation via Spanish-only legal/commercial documents. Two grassroots alternatives
  highlighted: women's-group literacy projects (organized by war widows, prioritizing mother-tongue
  literacy before Spanish since indigenous women have less exposure to central/Spanish culture) and
  the Mam-language "radioliteracy" project (ACUMAM community radio, Vocabulary row above) — explicitly
  compared to Anderson's print-language/imagined-community mechanism, strengthening specifically Mam
  ethnic identity.

### Ch.8 — Politics, language and the Spanish education system (pp.149-165)

Detailed single-country (Spain) deep-dive matching Ch.7's Latin American focus, contrasting
Catalonia's and the Basque Country's education-based language revival in detail.

**Catalonia (pp.150-160).** Legal timeline: July 1978 decree makes Catalan compulsory (3 hrs/week)
in state schools; Oct 1979 Statute of Autonomy confirms Catalan as "llengua pròpia"; 1982 decree
requires 3 hrs/week each of Catalan AND Castilian plus 1 hr of a subject taught through Catalan; the
1983 Llei de Normalització Lingüística (Articles 14-20, quoted in detail, pp.151-152) establishes a
genuine parental/child right to choose the language of primary instruction while mandating both
languages be taught and that all children reach equal mastery in both by the end of compulsory
schooling, explicitly banning language-based school segregation (Article 14.5). Catalan immersion
programs (voluntary, per Ch.6's model) began 1983-84 in working-class, heavily Castilian-speaking
Santa Coloma de Gramanet near Barcelona and expanded greatly. Parental support was consistently very
high: 81% called learning Catalan "very beneficial" in 1986 (Strubell & Romani); 98% wanted their
children taught Catalan per a 1994 survey; 96% (even per a Madrid-based pollster) believed everyone
in Catalonia should understand Catalan (p.153-154, citing Mar-Molinero 1995). The 1990s LOGSE
national education reform let regions interpret curricular latitude as license to make Catalan the
default "normal" vehicular language from 1993-94 — sharpening tension around whether the formerly
"voluntary" immersion principle (Ch.6) still genuinely held in practice (p.154).

**The 1993 "backlash" controversy — a genuinely vivid worked example of the book's individual-
vs-collective-rights framework.** June 1993: PSOE's loss of its parliamentary majority made it
dependent on Jordi Pujol's Catalan nationalist CiU party, widely perceived (p.155) as trading
political support for more aggressive pro-Catalan policy. The Madrid right-wing daily ABC published
(12 Sept 1993) an inflammatory headline directly inverting the Ch.5 Franco-repression framing: "Igual
que Franco pero al revés: persecución del castellano en Cataluña" ("The same as Franco but the other
way round: the persecution of Castilian in Catalonia") — explicitly analogized to Franco-era Catalan
suppression. This is flagged by the book as a genuinely unusual rights situation: *majority*-language
(Castilian) speakers claiming linguistic persecution by a *minority*-language (Catalan) policy regime
— paralleled, the book notes, by English-speakers in Quebec and in Wales (p.155). Most Spanish media
(La Vanguardia, El Observador, Avui, even national dailies El Mundo/El País) pushed back against
ABC's framing; organized Castilian-rights opposition groups named: CADECA (Coordinadora de Afectados
en Defensa de la Lengua Castellana), Acción Cultural Miguel de Cervantes, Asociación por la Lengua
Española en la Administración de la Justicia — led by lawyer Esteban Gómez Rovira, who litigated the
1983 law up through the Catalan High Court, the Spanish Supreme Court, and the Constitutional
Tribunal (upheld the law, Dec. 1994) (pp.155-157). Separately flagged complicating factor: Catalonia's
growing North African (mostly Moroccan) immigrant population, whose children speak neither Catalan
nor Castilian — the book notes this undercuts the "Castilian doesn't need protecting since it's the
state's majority language" counter-argument, since the same international mother-tongue-education
norms the Catalans invoke for themselves would equally apply to Moroccan immigrant children (p.160).

**Basque Country (pp.160-165).** Basque's weaker starting position vs. Catalan (no strong written/
literary tradition, so Franco-era repression was an even bigger existential threat, p.161) led first
to grassroots resistance (Ikastolas, see Vocabulary row) and then, post-1979 Statute of Autonomy
(Article 6, Basque + Castilian both official within Euskadi) and the October 1982 Ley de
Normalización del Uso de Euskera, to the 3-track Model A/B/D system (Vocabulary row above). Despite
Basque's much smaller speaker base and greater learning difficulty for Castilian-speakers (p.163), the
shift away from minimal-exposure Model A toward the immersive Models B/D (1982→1996-97: A from 75% to
34%, B+D from 25% to 73% of enrollment, Cenoz 1998:177) is explicitly called comparably dramatic to
Catalan's rise, even though absolute Basque comprehension/speaking rates remain far lower (~27% per
Cenoz 1998, vs. Catalan's 94%/75%, p.165). The AEK/HABE rivalry (Vocabulary row above) is the chapter's
clearest worked example of a grassroots-vs-institutional tension in language revival.

**Chapter's closing framing (p.165):** both Catalan and Basque language-education programs count as
successes by the "increased speakers" metric, but the book flags an open structural worry — that
institutionalized, government-controlled language planning may be displacing genuine grassroots
civil-society initiative in both cases, and that new pressures (non-European immigration, the global
pull of English in the curriculum) could still destabilize the current balance.

### Ch.9 — Spanish as minority language (pp.167-181)

**Structural inversion that makes this chapter unique in the book:** every prior chapter examined
Spanish as the dominant/hegemonic language; here Spanish itself is the marginalized minority language,
the US Latino community displaying "many of the characteristics... previously observed with
indigenous minority groups" elsewhere in the book (p.167) — an explicit cross-reference connecting
this chapter back to Ch.3's and Ch.7's indigenous-language case studies.

**US Latino demographics and legislative history (pp.168-174).** See Vocabulary row for 1996 census
figures. Legal history: 1919-25 state-level laws against non-English use in school/voting/employment
led to >1,000 jailings for "subversive speech" between 1920-50 (Trueba 1989, quoted p.169-170); 1965
Voting Rights Act + 1978 Court Interpreter's Act expanded bilingual services; the pivotal 1967
bilingual-education bill (proposed by Texas senator Ralph Yarborough, originally targeted
specifically at Spanish-speaking children's high dropout rates) was broadened during debate to cover
ALL non-English-mother-tongue minorities, diluting its Spanish-specific focus (p.170). The "English
Only"/"Official English" movement (1980s, Reagan-era, linked to broader anti-immigrant conservative
mood and a shift in immigration origin from Europe to Asia/Latin America) pushed the failed federal
English Language Amendment (1981, Senator Hayakawa) but succeeded at state level: 17 states made
English their official language; California's Proposition 63 (1986, official English), Proposition
187 (1994, denying services to illegal immigrants), and — the most dramatic — Proposition 227 (June
1998, banning bilingual education statewide, passed 61%-39% despite 63% Latino opposition) (pp.171-
174). Dicker's (1996) "immigrant paradigm" vs. "minority paradigm" distinction is flagged as a key
conceptual shift (credited to 1960s-70s Hispanic leaders per Imhoff): under the "minority" framing,
responsibility for bridging a language gap shifts from the newcomer onto the host society, inverting
the older assimilationist "immigrant" framing (p.172).

**Image/identity dynamics (pp.174-176).** Captured as Vocabulary rows: Spanglish/Tex-Mex codeswitching
as an in-group solidarity marker; Zentella's "chiquitafication" critique of pan-Latino homogenization.
Notable 1994 legal win: Arizona's 1988 referendum-passed law banning Spanish use by public employees
was ruled unconstitutional. A Chicano-writers' manifesto opposing California's bilingual-education
repeal is quoted (via the Madrid daily ABC, p.175) — the book notes continuing-Spanish-speaking-world
solidarity with US Latino language-rights struggles as a recurring, cross-cutting pattern.

**Puerto Rico (pp.176-181) — the book's full treatment of the "returning migrant" phenomenon promised
in the Introduction.** Historical arc: Spanish colony from 1493 (indigenous population "completely
eradicated," p.176) to 1898 US takeover; US "Americanization" push (English imposed via schools) until
1949, when the first elected (not US-appointed) governor restored Spanish as the medium of
instruction; 1952 constitution establishes "Estado libre asociado"/"commonwealth" status (Vocabulary
row above). Spanish-official-language law passed 1991, repealed 1992 after a change in ruling party —
an unusually fast legislative reversal the book treats as a clean illustration of how directly language
status tracks electoral/ideological swings (p.177). Literary case study: Puerto Rican writer Esmeralda
Santiago's bilingual memoir *When I Was Puerto Rican*/*Cuando era puertorriqueña* (self-translated) is
used to illustrate lived linguistic/identity ambivalence (quoted p.178-179, via Perivolaris 1997),
alongside Dominican-American writer Julia Alvarez as a comparative case.

**The "returning migrant" mechanism (pp.179-181) — flagged as potentially this chapter's most
conceptually exportable finding.** Captured fully as a Vocabulary row above: Clachar's (1997) finding
that Spanish flips from in-group solidarity marker (on the US mainland) to out-group/stigma marker (on
the island, for Spanish-weak returnees) — the same language performing opposite social-marking
functions depending on which direction the speaker has moved. Zentella's open question, quoted
directly (p.180): "are returnees who cannot speak Spanish Puerto Ricans?" — found to be genuinely
unresolved/generationally contested in the sources the book surveys (older generation/intellectuals:
Spanish fluency essential to Puerto Rican identity; younger island generation: more tolerant of
non-fluent "Puerto Rican-ness").

### Ch.10 — Spanish in a global era (pp.182-196, the book's concluding chapter)

**Globalization vs. the nation-state (pp.182-184) — theoretical framing, continuing the book's
pattern of a general-theory opening before specific application.** Two contrasting positions staged:
Anthony Smith (1995, 1998) argues the nation-state is not dying — post-1945 states have actually
*expanded* their internal cultural/social reach (mass education, media, welfare) even as external
economic dependence rose, and multinational firms/elites remain ultimately answerable to national
political structures (quoted p.183). Michael Billig (1995) takes the opposite view: globalization
erodes BETWEEN-nation differences while simultaneously fragmenting WITHIN-nation unity, producing new
"non-national" identities (lifestyle, local, ethnic, gender) that compete with national identity for
primary allegiance (quoted p.184) — the book flags this as the more Spain-relevant model given the
estado de las autonomías' already-devolved structure.

**Spain and the EU (pp.185-189).** The EU's own language-status hierarchy, which the book lays out
in detail: "official" languages (full translation/interpretation rights) vs. "national" languages
(Irish, Luxemburgish — explicitly a lesser category, no full services) vs. "working language"
status (bilateral-only, e.g. Catalan's 1991 partial win — Vocabulary row above). Crucially, which
languages of a member state get EU-official status is decided by that state's OWN national
government, not by population size or regional advocacy — meaning Catalan's ~10 million speakers
cannot outweigh Madrid's decision not to nominate it (p.186-187). In practice, despite nominal
multilingual equality, EU functional language use concentrates on French (administrative), English
(oral/informal), and German (largest number of EU mother-tongue speakers) (p.186). EU
minority-language support instruments named: the 1981 Charter of Regional Languages and Cultures and
Rights of Ethnic Minorities, the European Bureau for Lesser-Used Languages, the MERCATOR network, and
exchange programs (LINGUA, ERASMUS, SOCRATES) (p.187) — plus a 1977 EC directive (already noted in
Ch.6) recommending mother-tongue teaching for immigrant children, which the book notes is increasingly
relevant to Spain given rising North African immigration into Catalonia specifically (p.187-188). Open
question flagged by the book: the EU's territoriality-principle-based protection of Spain's minority
languages may be undercut by the EU's own freedom-of-movement ideal, which assumes people relocate
across national/regional lines (p.188) — a direct tension the book does not resolve.

**Pan-continental movements in Latin America (pp.189-193).** Named economic/regional blocs: CACM
(Central American Common Market: Guatemala, El Salvador, Honduras, Nicaragua, Costa Rica); the Andean
Group (Venezuela, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia); MERCOSUR (Argentina, Paraguay, Uruguay, Brazil,
Chile); and NAFTA (US, Canada, Mexico) — the book notes these are more narrowly economic than the EU
model, and that NAFTA specifically increases pressure on Mexico to prioritize English over indigenous
languages (p.190). More linguistically significant: cross-border INDIGENOUS solidarity organizations,
explicitly framed as the Latin American analog to Europe's regional-continental networking —
International Frente Mixteco-Zapoteco (Mexican/US migrant Oaxacan indigenous workers' cross-border
organization); COICA (Co-ordinating Body of Indigenous Organisations of the Amazonian Basin); the
Mayan-based Committee for Unidad Campesina (Guatemala, 1990 declaration); the 1991 Quito Declaration
(Confederation of Indigenous Peoples of Ecuador, calling for a "continental alliance" of indigenous
peoples challenging "the notion of a homogenous nation-state," p.191) — all captured as evidence that
indigenous identity increasingly organizes along socio-cultural/pan-ethnic rather than national lines.
Von Gleich's (1994) point about Quechua specifically: wider cross-border circulation/use of an
indigenous language can itself function as a status-planning mechanism, reinforcing speakers' positive
identification with it (quoted p.192) — names a concrete 1989 Bolivia workshop on "Normalization of
Pedagogical Language for Andean Languages" as a model of productive supra-regional indigenous-language
cooperation. The Zapatista movement (Chiapas, Mexico) and the Shining Path (Peru) are named as the
most radical/political expressions of this same indigenous-awareness trend; "Comandante Marcos"'s use
of English on the internet to publicize the Zapatista cause is cited as a striking illustration of how
fluid and non-obvious language-identity alignment can become under globalization (p.194).

**Conclusion: Spanish as a pluricentric world language (pp.193-196) — the book's final thesis.**
English's global dominance (traced to British imperial, then US, political-economic power) is framed
via Robert Phillipson's (1992) "linguistic imperialism" concept (named explicitly, p.193) — the book
notes Spanish has not resisted English's encroachment as vocally as French has, but genuine anglicism
borrowing and syntactic influence are visible, especially in media. Ironic side effect: Spain's own
minority-language speakers (e.g. Catalan businesspeople) can use English specifically to bypass
Castilian as the "default" second language in international contexts (p.194) — a novel 3-way
language-choice dynamic not seen elsewhere in the book. Spanish's own "language spread" health (via
Cooper's 1982 criteria: demographic growth, favorable attitudes, accessibility, production/
reproduction infrastructure, political support) is assessed as strong: large and growing speaker base;
largely favorable attitudes among Spanish speakers themselves (with the explicit exceptions, flagged
by the book, of US Latinos and Spain's own minority-language speakers); robust literary/print
infrastructure; and active political promotion via the Instituto Cervantes network (explicitly
compared to the British Council and Goethe Institutes, p.195) — ironically only built up AFTER
Franco's death despite Franco's own rhetorical emphasis on "Hispanidad" (pan-Hispanic community).
Final framework: Clyne's (1992) "pluricentric language" model (Vocabulary row above) — Spanish's many
national "centres" (Madrid being the traditional, not always willingly shared, ultimate one) both
unify (shared language) and divide (distinct national norms/identities) its speakers, requiring what
Clyne calls "multiple group membership" (e.g., being both Peruvian and part of the broader
Spanish-speaking world). The book's closing line, unqualified: "the future of Spanish, as with all
questions of language, will be determined by issues of politics and power" (p.196) — the thesis
statement the entire book has been building toward.

**Diglossia final check:** the term "diglossia" was NOT found by name anywhere across the whole book
(Chs.1-10). This is confirmed as a genuine whole-book absence, not a chunking artifact — the book's
functional equivalents throughout are "status planning," the territoriality/personality principles,
"double officiality" (doble oficialidad), and repeated prestige-language/home-language register
distinctions (e.g. Catalan/Basque/Galician's historical confinement to spoken-only domestic use while
Castilian held all public/written functions) — genuinely diglossic SITUATIONS are described
extensively and in detail throughout the book, just never named with the technical term "diglossia."
Worth flagging to the orchestrator in case other sources in this project's Spanish dataset use the
term and a cross-reference would be useful.

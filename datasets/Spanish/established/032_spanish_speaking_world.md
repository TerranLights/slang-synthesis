## Grammar / Sociolinguistic-concepts points

### Diglossia as a functional (not just structural) split

The book defines diglossia precisely as two varieties/languages coexisting in one community while
performing *distinct, non-overlapping social functions* — labeled High (public/formal: administration,
law, often education/media) vs. Low (private/informal/intimate) — rather than simply "two languages
spoken in the same place" (p.10). Crucially, the book immediately flags the model's limitation when
applied to real Catalan/Castilian usage: speakers are "far more fluid and flexible" than a rigid
High/Low split allows, and any strict separation likely reflects *political imposition* rather than
organic usage patterns. This matters for the project's register-modeling: a two-slot High/Low frame is
a simplification, not a ground truth, when the underlying sociolinguistic reality is a gradient.

### The "standard language" concept and its construction via political power, not linguistic merit

The book traces Castilian's rise to "standard" status to a specific historical sequence rather than any
inherent linguistic property: Reconquista-driven political unification → royal marriage (1469) →
Granada's fall (1492) → Nebrija's 1492 grammar (first standardized Castilian grammar) → Alfonso X's
earlier (13th c.) orthographic standardization efforts. The explicit framing: standardization follows
power, it does not precede or cause it.

### Castilianization mechanism (administrative/religious/educational/military)

The book names four concrete institutional channels through which Castilian was imposed on non-Castilian
regions of Spain, each independently reinforcing: (1) increasing use of Castilian by the Catholic Church
(including appointment of Castilian-speaking bishops to non-Castilian dioceses); (2) Castilian as the
sole language of the education system; (3) universal male military conscription into a Castilian-speaking
army; (4) a 1768 royal decree (Charles III) formally mandating Castilian "in administration and in
education" throughout the kingdom. The same four-channel structure (church / school / army / law) is
presented as recurring in the parallel Castilianization of Latin America (friars, then colonial schools,
then administrative decree) — the book explicitly invites the reader to compare the two processes (p.21).

### Legal "national" vs. "official" language status — not a synonym pair

The book draws a deliberate distinction Latin American constitutions make between declaring Spanish the
**official** language (bureaucratic/administrative use) vs. the **national** language (identity-bearing,
often used in more multi-ethnic states to ALSO cover a named indigenous language — e.g. Paraguay, Ecuador,
and Peru all legally designate an indigenous language as "national" alongside Spanish). This is a legal
register distinction with real downstream effects (bilingual-education mandates, language-rights scope)
rather than a stylistic one.

### Peru's Quechua/Aymara status flip (1975 → 1980) as a case study in geographically-limited co-officiality

1975: Quechua proclaimed co-official **national** language of Peru (under a radical military government).
1980: that status replaced by a narrower law granting Quechua **or** Aymara co-official status only in
specific designated regions. The book's critique: regionally-restricted language rights understate the
real presence of these languages, especially post-migration concentrations in Lima — a general pattern
worth flagging for any later language-policy modeling (restricting a language's legal status to its
"home" territory can misrepresent where its speakers actually live).

### Divergent core values in Catalan vs. Basque nationalism: language vs. race

This is the single most analytically sharp point in this chunk. Catalan nationalism (per its leading
theorist, Prat de la Riba) treated **language** (along with culture and territory) as the defining
"spirit" of the nation — i.e., linguistic competence/use was the inclusion criterion. Basque nationalism
(per its founder, Sabino Arana) instead centered **race** ("blood and origin") as the core value, explicitly
because Basque itself was too narrowly spoken (even among Basques) to function as a workable inclusion
criterion — in Arana's framework, the Basque language was invoked mainly to *exclude* outsiders, not to
define membership positively. The book draws out the broader lesson: "core value" for a national/ethnic
identity need not be language at all, even in a case (the Basque Country) that also has its own
historically distinct minority language. Don't assume language-centrality in nationalism/identity-marker
analysis — check which value a given movement actually centers.

### Order-of-emergence pattern: cultural revival before political nationalism (Catalonia, Galicia) vs. the Basque Country's reversed/parallel order

In both Catalonia (Renaixença) and Galicia (Rexordimento), a literary/cultural revival movement preceded
and fed into a later political-nationalist movement. The Basque Country is the explicit counter-case: there
was comparatively little pre-existing literary tradition to revive (Basque had far less of a written
corpus than Catalan), so the political nationalist movement (Arana's PNV) arose more directly, without a
substantial preceding cultural-literary phase, and the language's role was subordinated to race as the
movement's defining value (see above). Galicia also shows a THIRD pattern within this frame: its cultural
revival occurred without a following political-nationalist phase until the 20th century at all — the book
attributes this specifically to Galicia's 19th-century rural poverty and lack of industrialization (contrast
with industrializing Catalonia and the Basque Country, both of which the book frames as industrialization
correlating with — not necessarily causing — stronger nationalist mobilization).

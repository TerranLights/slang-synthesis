**Treated as redundant and NOT re-extracted:** the basic -o/-a noun-gender pattern and basic plural

two indefinite articles (un/una) in their plain form (005 Lessons 1–2); the basic este/ese
two-term demonstrative distinction as taught at beginner level (005 uses only a simplified subset);
basic possessive adjectives mi/tu/su/nuestro/vuestro in their plain paradigm form (005 Lesson 14);

Table 10.1 regular-verb paradigm at the very end of this chunk's range is the same information and
is noted but not reproduced as a vocabulary/paradigm table).

**Kept and why:** this is a systematic reference grammar (not a pedagogical workbook), and its real
value for this project is in the material beginner workbooks don't cover at all: (1) the full
plural-formation rule system with its many lexical exceptions and irregular plurals; (2) the full
noun-gender rule system (ending-based predictors, the dual-gender-different-meaning noun class, the
masculine -ma noun list, fixed/doubtful-gender nouns); (3) the complete article usage/omission rule
system (abstract nouns, generic nouns, days/seasons, geographic names, language names, titles,
apposition, set phrases) which beginner workbooks never formalize; (4) the three-way
este/ese/aquel spatial-and-temporal distance distinction (not just the simplified two-way version);
(5) adjective shortening, position-meaning-shift pairs, and the restrictive/non-restrictive position
rule; (6) the adverb system including the -mente formation rule, place/time/degree adverb inventory,
and adjectives usable as adverbs; (7) the full personal-pronoun system including leísmo/laísmo,
pronoun position/order rules, and the vos [LA] paradigm; (8) the full 17-item indefinite
adjective/pronoun/adverb system (algo through otro), which beginner workbooks barely touch beyond
mucho/poco. **Explicit [SP] (Peninsular Spain) / [LA] (Latin America) dialectal tags — this book's
own systematic notation, used throughout — are preserved on every row where the source marks them**;
this is the single highest-value content type per the extraction spec and is never skipped.

### Grammar points

#### Chapter 1: ch/ll/ñ and the 1994 alphabet reform (p. 1)

Until 1994, ch and ll were treated as distinct letters of the Spanish alphabet, each with its own
dictionary-ordering slot after c and l respectively (so *cocha* was alphabetized after all *coc-*
words, *callada* after *calzo*). The 1994 reform (driven by international standardization and
computer-sorting pressure) dropped this for ch/ll, but ñ was deliberately kept as a distinct letter,
still alphabetized after n both word-initially and word-internally (*caña* after *canzonetista*).

#### Chapter 1: Seseo and yeísmo as named, regionally-distributed sound changes (pp. 2–3)

The book names and regionally locates two major pronunciation splits explicitly: **seseo**
(pronouncing c/z before e,i, and z generally, as /s/ rather than the Peninsular-standard /θ/) is
the norm across Latin America and in Andalusia; **yeísmo** (ll softened to /j/ "y" rather than the
traditional /ʎ/) is widespread in Andalusia and parts of Latin America, with a further variant
in the River Plate region where it approaches "s" in "pleasure." The source explicitly flags the
practical consequence: under seseo, spelling pairs like *sebo/cebo*, *casa/caza*, *cegar/segar*,
*ves/vez* become true homophones, a genuine source of ambiguity for learners in seseo regions.

#### Chapter 1: Syllable division and stress rules (pp. 6–8)

Three basic syllabification principles: (a) syllables end in a vowel wherever possible (a single
intervocalic consonant attaches to the following vowel: *ta-ba-co*); (b) consonant clusters ending
in -l/-r, plus ch, never split (*a-pli-car*, *ca-lle*); (c) s never shares a syllable with a
following consonant (*cas-ta-ña*). Default stress: words ending in a vowel, n, or s stress the
penultimate syllable (*verde*, *venden*); words ending in any other consonant stress the final
syllable (*Madrid*, *vivir*). Any word that doesn't follow these defaults requires a written accent
(*cámara*, *fácil*), and a missing accent can change a word's meaning entirely (*llego* "I arrive"
vs. *llegó* "he/she arrived"). Plural formation can require adding or removing an accent to keep the
stress in the same place (*joven* → *jóvenes*, *interés* → *intereses*).

#### Chapter 1: Diphthong/triphthong stress rules (pp. 7–8)

Vowels split into strong (a, e, o) and weak (i, u). Two strong vowels in sequence form two separate
syllables (*ta-re-a*, *ca-er*). Two weak vowels together form one syllable (a diphthong); if
stressed, the stress falls on the second vowel (*bui-tre*). A strong vowel plus one or two weak
vowels normally forms a single syllable too (a diphthong/triphthong), with stress falling on the
strong vowel if the syllable is stressed (*gua-pa*, *eu-ro*). Words that break this pattern (the weak
vowel actually carries the stress, splitting what would otherwise be a diphthong into two syllables)
require a written accent: *frí-o*, *pa-ís*, *con-ti-nú-o*. The Academy additionally recommends a
written accent whenever an intervening h separates two vowels pronounced separately: *prohíbe*,
*búho*.

#### Chapter 1: Capitalization differences from English (pp. 8–10)

Spanish does NOT capitalize: adjectives of national/regional/personal origin (*un vino peruano*,
*un tema borgesiano*); days of the week and months; nouns/adjectives of political or religious
affiliation (*los conservadores*, *de origen judío*); official titles unless at the very start of a
sentence (*los reyes de España*, *el almirante Grau*); or titles of plays/films/books beyond the
first word (*Los ríos profundos* capitalizes only *Los*) — though newspaper/magazine titles like
*El País* do capitalize throughout. Spanish instead has a distinctive practice of doubling capital
letters to mark acronym plurals (*las CCAA* for *Comunidades Autónomas*, *las FFAA* for *Fuerzas
Armadas*) and of deriving ordinary common nouns/adjectives from acronyms (*emerretista*, a member of
Peru's MRTA).

#### Chapter 1: Punctuation distinctive to Spanish (p. 11)

Inverted question/exclamation marks (¿...?, ¡...!) are unique to Spanish and are used not only
sentence-initially but immediately before an embedded interrogative/exclamatory phrase within a
longer sentence (*Dime, ¿quieres ir o no?*). Most Spanish-speaking countries (Mexico is the
exception) use the period as a thousands-separator and the comma as a decimal point in numbers —
the reverse of English convention (*5.651.242* = "5,651,242"; *21,6* = "21.6"). « » guillemets are
still used for short embedded quotations, though the press increasingly uses " " or ' ' instead.

#### Chapter 2: Nouns ending in unstressed syllable + -s or -x don't change in the plural (p. 15)

*la crisis/las crisis*, *el virus/los virus*, *el fax/los fax* — these nouns are invariant between
singular and plural; number is disambiguated only by the article/context. This is a genuinely
distinct rule from the basic "-s or -es" plural pattern beginner workbooks teach.

#### Chapter 2: Compound noun pluralization follows the compound's internal structure (p. 16)

A verb/preposition + plural-noun compound (*el/los salvapantallas*, *el/los paracaídas*) never
changes form. A compound forming a genuinely single word whose last element isn't itself plural
pluralizes normally (*el mirasol → los mirasoles*). A compound of two separate nouns usually
pluralizes only the first (*el barco vivienda → los barcos vivienda*) — UNLESS the second noun can
be read as modifying/qualifying the first, in which case both pluralize (*país miembro → países
miembros*). The source notes genuine native-speaker disagreement over edge cases like *clave*.

#### Chapter 2: Mass/uncountable nouns can be "pluralized into" a countable reading (p. 18)

Spanish readily uses what would be an uncountable noun in English (bread, soap, toast) as a plural
countable when referring to discrete units (*dos panes* = "two loaves of bread"; *los jabones* =
"bars of soap"; *las tostadas* = "pieces/slices of toast"). This runs in the opposite direction from
English "furniture," which is a mass noun in English but *los muebles* is a genuinely countable
plural noun in Spanish with no natural singular equivalent in the same sense.

#### Chapter 2: Gender-marking suffix system for persons/animals (pp. 18–19)

Beyond the basic -o→-a pattern, the book documents a fuller system: consonant-final masculines in
-or/-ón/-és/-ín typically add plain -a (*locutor→locutora*); a smaller set add -esa/-isa after
dropping the final vowel (*alcalde→alcaldesa*, *poeta→poetisa*); others use an unrelated feminine
suffix entirely (*actor→actriz*, *héroe→heroína*). A distinct class — nouns ending in -a (esp.
-ista), -e, and most reliably -nte — use one invariant form for both sexes, distinguished only by
the article (*el/la futbolista*, *el/la cantante*); *el monje/la monja* is the flagged exception.
Separately, a closed set of nouns (*persona*, *víctima*, *estrella*, *personaje*, *genio*) have a
grammatically fixed gender regardless of the referent's actual sex, and wild-animal names that are
similarly fixed-gender require *macho*/*hembra* to specify sex when needed. The book frames the
late-20th-century spread of feminized job titles (*abogada*, *ministra*, *jueza*) as an explicit,
named instance of language change driven by social change — not a static rule but a process still
underway at the time of writing (2004).

#### Chapter 2: Ending-based gender prediction, with its exception classes (pp. 20–23)

Beyond -o(m.)/-a(f.): nouns in -ista and a handful of exceptions (*la mano*, *la foto*, *la moto*,
*la radio* [SP]) break the -a=feminine default; *el día*, *el mapa*, *el planeta*, *el tranvía*
break the -o=masculine default by being genuinely masculine despite the -a ending (not to be
confused with the invariant-gender -ista class); a large, named class of -ma nouns (*el clima*, *el
problema*, *el sistema*, *el tema*...) are masculine as a systematic class, not isolated exceptions.
Feminine-predicting endings: -ad, -tud, -ción, -sión, -umbre, -ie (mostly abstract nouns) — with *el
pie* and the -is words (*la crisis* but *el énfasis*, *el análisis* — genuinely requiring
per-word lookup) flagged as needing care. Masculine-predicting endings: -aje, -ambre, -án, -én, -or,
and any stressed vowel ending — with *la flor*, *la labor*, *el hambre* as flagged exceptions.
Separately, whole categories of proper nouns default masculine via an implied unmentioned noun
(*el Amazonas* via *río*; *el Betis* via *equipo*; *el miércoles* via *día*), while letters,
islands, companies, and roads default feminine via their own implied nouns (*letra*, *isla*,
*compañía*, *carretera*). A further class of nouns carries genuinely different meanings depending on
which gender is used (*el capital/la capital*, *el pez/la pez*, *el Papa/la papa* [LA]) — this is
not free variation but a real lexical-semantic split keyed to grammatical gender.

#### Chapter 2: Collective nouns and verb agreement (pp. 25–26)

Collective nouns (*multitud*, *mayoría*, *gente*, *docena*, *mitad*) take a singular verb when used
on their own, even where English would use plural ("the government has/have not decided" = *El
gobierno no ha decidido*). When the collective is joined to a following plural noun via *de*, or
when the verb is positioned at a distance from the collective noun, a plural verb becomes the safer
choice for learners (*La mayoría de las casas son viejas*). The book is explicit that actual native
usage is less clear-cut than this guidance and can hinge on whether the speaker is conceptualizing
the group as a single unit (singular) or its members individually (plural).

#### Chapter 3: El/un used before feminine nouns beginning with stressed a-/ha- (p. 28)

This does NOT change the noun's actual gender — *el agua* is still grammatically feminine, which is
why the plural reverts to *las aguas* and a modifying adjective still agrees in the feminine
(*el agua fría*, not *el agua frío*). The rule applies only when the article is immediately adjacent
to the noun (an intervening adjective blocks it: *la bella arpa*) and only when the initial a-/ha-
is actually the stressed syllable (*una alerta*, *la hamburguesa* are unaffected since the a/ha isn't
stressed there). The rule never extends to adjectives or proper names beginning with stressed a-
(*una alta galería*, *la Ana que conozco*).

#### Chapter 3: Systematic rules for definite-article use vs. omission (pp. 29–40)

This chapter formalizes an area beginner workbooks leave implicit. **Required where English omits
the article**: generic/category nouns (*El plomo es un metal muy blando* "Lead is a very soft
metal" — contrasts with English, which only requires this with a singular generic, as in "The
giraffe is a strange animal"); abstract nouns used generically (*la pobreza*) — though the article
drops after verbs like *tener*/*faltar* ("to lack"); general concepts/practices including colours,
diseases, games, fields of study, and even meals (*No me gusta el rojo*, *Están jugando al
béisbol*, *¿A qué hora es la cena?*) — but the article is dropped for colours after *de*/*en* (*El
cuarto está pintado de verde*); habitual-occasion days of the week (*los sábados*) vs. single
occasions (*el lunes*) — but dropped after *ser* when merely identifying the day (*Hoy es viernes*)
and after *de* for routine spans (*de lunes a viernes*); parts of the body/clothing/personal
possessions (the article substitutes for English's possessive — full treatment cross-referenced to
§5.5); fixed noun+preposition combinations (*en la cama*, *a la escuela*) where English has none;
titles unless the person is addressed directly (*el comandante Paredes* but *–Buenos días
comandante*) — *don*, *doña*, *fray*, *san(to)*, *sor* never take the article even indirectly;
unique geographic features (rivers, mountains, seas, deserts: *el Tajo*, *el Teide*, *el
Atacama*); and sports team names (*el Betis*).

**Predominantly omitted where English might expect it**: most country names unqualified (*España*)
— restored only if the country name is modified (*la España romana*) or is itself a descriptive
title (*los Países Bajos*, *el Reino Unido*); the book notes a live trend, especially in journalism,
toward dropping the article before masculine country names that historically took one (*[el]
Brasil*, *[el] Canadá*...), while *el Perú*, *el Congo*, *el Líbano*, *la India* currently resist
this trend, and *(los) Estados Unidos* triggers singular verb agreement when the article is dropped;
city/region/continent names unless qualified (*Barcelona* vs. *la Barcelona de Gaudí*) — though some
city names have an article as an intrinsic, capitalized part of the name itself (*La Habana*, *La
Coruña*); nouns in formal apposition providing merely explanatory information about a preceding noun
(*Bogotá, capital de Colombia*) — though comparative/superlative phrases always keep the article
(*La Paz, la capital más alta del mundo*); and a closed set of fixed phrases (*a orillas de*, *a
corto/largo plazo*, *en manos de*, *en nombre de*).

**Language names**: the article is required when referring to the language as a whole or after *de*
(*El vasco es una lengua...*, *los problemas del ruso*) but dropped after *en*, and after *aprender/
hablar/saber* (commonly also *entender/enseñar/estudiar*) when the language name is unqualified
(*Está aprendiendo holandés*) — though it's always restored if the language name is itself qualified
by a word or phrase (*en el francés antiguo*).

#### Chapter 3: Indefinite article — omission rules (pp. 37–40)

Mass/uncountable nouns cannot take the singular indefinite article to mean "some" (contrast with the
qualified-noun case in §3.3.2, where a following relative clause or adjective licenses it: *un agua
purísima*). The plural *unos/unas* is frequently dropped in habitual-action contexts not referring to
specific items (*Por aquí pasan trenes cada cinco minutos*) but is retained, and even replaced by
*algunos/as*, *ciertos/as*, *varios/as*, or *unos/as cuantos/as* for stylistic variety, when specific
reference is intended. The article is dropped before nouns classifying profession/occupation/
affiliation after *ser*, *elegir*, *hacerse*, *llamar*, *calificar de* (*Ella es doctora*) — unless
the noun itself is modified (*un conservador intransigente*). It's dropped before *otro*, *tal/
semejante*, *medio*, *mil*, *cien(to)*, *qué*, and *cierto* (*¡Qué imbécil es Paco!*). It's almost
always dropped after *sin* and often after *con* (*Salió sin chaqueta*) — except to deliberately
emphasize "a single" (*sin un (solo) peso*). It's dropped when categorizing something after *como*,
*por*, *a modo/manera de* (*Como persona liberal...*). And it's commonly dropped after verbs like
*buscar*, *emplear*, *encontrar*, *hay*, *llevar*, *tener* (*Estamos buscando casa*) — restored
when the noun is modified or "one" needs emphasis (*Encontró una rueda pero no la otra*).

#### Chapter 3: The neuter article lo (pp. 40–42)

*Lo* + masculine singular adjective/past participle compresses what English needs a noun like
"thing"/"matter" for (*Lo más importante es informar al banco* "The most important thing is..."); if
what follows syntactically is a plural noun, *ser* still goes plural (*Lo convenido ayer no son más
que las líneas generales*). *Lo* + adjective/participle/adverb + *que* is a distinct, very common
construction corresponding to English "how" (*lo alta que era* "how tall she was"), and can itself be
preceded by *con*, *para*, or *por* for added nuance. *Lo de* + noun compresses to "the business/
matter of" (*Lo del agua es un problema grave*). *Lo que* means "the thing which"/"what." And *lo*
appears in a closed set of idioms (*a lo mejor*, *por lo menos*, *por lo visto*, *de lo contrario*).

#### Chapter 4: The three-way este/ese/aquel distance system (pp. 43–46)

Unlike English's two-way this/that, Spanish genuinely has three demonstrative series. *Este* marks
physical or temporal proximity to the speaker. *Ese* marks what's near or associated with the person
being addressed (the listener) — NOT simply "far from the speaker" as English "that" implies. *Aquel*
marks what's distant from both speaker and listener, or unassociated with either. The same
distinction extends to time: *ese* for a relatively recent past, *aquel* for a more distant one
(*esos días que pasamos juntos el verano pasado* vs. *En aquellos tiempos no existían armas de
fuego*). The book flags that this clean distinction isn't always rigidly observed in actual native
usage, and that native speakers do alternate, except where *aquel* is being used deliberately to
stress distance. The pronoun *este* can also mean "the latter" (most recently mentioned) and *aquel*
"the former" (mentioned earlier) in formal writing — a usage with no English demonstrative
equivalent. A demonstrative placed AFTER the noun (with the definite article restored before it:
*el libro ese*) is a genuine, distinct construction used especially in speech, but the book
explicitly warns foreign learners that it reads as critical or dismissive.

#### Chapter 5: Su/suyo ambiguity and its de + pronoun workaround (pp. 49–50)

Because *su/sus* and *suyo/a/os/as* can mean "his," "her(s)," "your(s)," "its," or "their(s)"
depending entirely on context, genuine ambiguity is common; the standard disambiguation strategy is
to replace the possessive with *de* + the appropriate prepositional object pronoun (*la casa de
ellos* instead of an ambiguous *su casa*). The book adds a Latin-American-specific wrinkle: there,
*su/sus* and *suyo* are by default assumed to refer to *usted/ustedes* ("your(s)"), so when "his,"
"her(s)," or "their(s)" is actually meant, the *de él/de ella* workaround is used even more
systematically than in Spain.

#### Chapter 5: Definite article (not possessive) for body parts and personal items (p. 50)

Where English uses a possessive ("put your shoes on," "show me your hands"), Spanish normally uses
the definite article instead (*Ponte los zapatos*, *Enséñame las manos*) — the possessor is
understood from context (usually the subject or an object pronoun elsewhere in the sentence). The
possessive adjective is restored only where its omission would create genuine ambiguity about whose
body part/item is meant (*¿Dónde están mis zapatos?*).

#### Chapter 6: Adjective gender — exception classes beyond the basic -o/-a rule (pp. 52–54)

Comparative-sense -or adjectives (*mejor*, *peor*, *mayor*, *menor*, *superior*, *inferior*,
*anterior*, *posterior*, *exterior*, *interior*, *ulterior*) have NO distinct feminine form at
all — unlike regular -or adjectives (*emprendedor/emprendedora*), these are invariant (*la
madre superiora* is the single flagged exception). Nationality adjectives ending in -a, -ense, -í,
-ú are invariant for gender (*belga*, *canadiense*, *marroquí*, *hindú*) while -o-ending and
consonant-ending nationality adjectives inflect normally. Colour terms that are actually nouns
standing in for "(de) color de X" (*café*, *rosa*, *turquesa*, *violeta*...) are invariant since
they're nominal, not adjectival — but single-word compound colour adjectives formed from two true
adjectives (*verdiblanco*) DO still inflect normally, unlike hyphenated/two-word colour compounds
(*azul marino*), which stay invariant for both gender AND number even when the book notes this isn't
always observed in speech or in Latin America.

#### Chapter 6: Adjective shortening before masculine singular nouns (pp. 56–57)

*Bueno*, *malo*, *primero*, *tercero*, *uno*, *alguno*, *ninguno* drop their final -o before a
masculine singular noun (*un buen libro*, *el tercer día*, *ningún hombre*) — with a written accent
added to *algún*/*ningún* to preserve stress. Shortening persists even across an intervening second
adjective (*el primer nuevo caso*) but is blocked if the adjective is instead joined to another by a
conjunction or otherwise separated from the noun (*el primero y último caso*). Separately, *grande*
shortens to *gran* before either gender (*una gran pena*, *un gran apetito*) unless preceded by
*más/menos* or likewise separated from the noun by a conjunction. *Santo* shortens to *San* except
before names starting *To-*/*Do-* (*San Fernando* but *Santo Tomás*, *Santo Domingo*), and is never
shortened when it means "holy" rather than functioning as a title (*el santo sacramento*).

#### Chapter 6: Adjective position as a restrictive/non-restrictive distinction (pp. 58–60)

The book frames Spanish adjective position as fundamentally a restrictive-vs-non-restrictive
distinction, not mere style. Post-nominal position (the unmarked default) is restrictive — it
singles out particular members of the noun's category (*Hay que cambiar las maderas podridas* = we
need to replace only the rotten pieces of wood). Pre-nominal position is non-restrictive — it merely
notes an incidental property without picking out a subset (*Hay que cambiar las podridas maderas* =
we need to replace all the wood, which happens to be rotten). Subjective/evaluative adjectives
therefore gravitate pre-nominally, especially with a definite article or possessive (*la odiada
policía secreta*, *este maldito hotel*), while objective/classificatory adjectives (scientific,
technical, colour, shape, condition) gravitate post-nominally (*un análisis sociológico*, *una silla
rota*). An adjective itself modified by an adverb is safest placed post-nominally regardless (*un
cantante muy popular*). Separately, a genuine closed set of adjectives (*antiguo*, *medio*, *nuevo*,
*numeroso*, *pobre*, *puro*, *simple*, *único*, *viejo*, *grande*, *alto*) carry categorically
DIFFERENT meanings depending on position, not just a difference of emphasis — this is lexical
ambiguity resolved by word order, not a stylistic choice.

#### Chapter 6: Verb + adjective agreement sequences (p. 62)

A distinct construction links an adjective to a verb's subject (*Ana compró el caballo
entusiasmada* "Ana was thrilled to buy the horse" — the adjective agrees with Ana, the subject, not
the horse) or to a verb's object, in which case two word orders are both possible with the adjective
either right after the verb or right after the noun it describes (*Toma muy caliente su café* /
*Toma su café muy caliente*, both "He takes his coffee very hot"; *Tiene rota la pierna* / *Tiene la
pierna rota*, both "His leg is broken").

#### Chapter 7: The -mente adverb-formation rule and its stress behavior (pp. 63–64)

-mente attaches to the FEMININE form of the corresponding adjective (or the invariable form if there
is no distinct feminine): *lenta + mente → lentamente*. Both the adjective's own stress and the
-mente suffix are stressed independently, so an adjective that itself requires a written accent keeps
that accent when converted to an adverb (*fácil → fácilmente*). Not every -mente adverb is an adverb
of manner — some are quantity adverbs (*solamente*), time adverbs (*posteriormente*), or
whole-sentence adverbs (*políticamente*). In writing and formal speech, when two or more -mente
adverbs are joined by *y*/*pero*/*o*/*ni*, only the LAST one in the sequence keeps the -mente
suffix (*Hablaron lenta y gravemente*, not *lentamente y gravemente*) — a genuine economy rule with
no English parallel.

#### Chapter 7: Adverbs of place: regional splits and the three-tier spatial system (pp. 65–66)

*Aquí/ahí/allí* mirror the este/ese/aquel three-way demonstrative distance system exactly (here /
near-listener / far-from-both). *Acá/allá* are the Latin American functional replacements — less
common and less precise in Peninsular usage, but having effectively displaced *aquí/allí* across
much of (especially southern) Latin America; they're also the default choice with verbs of motion
(*ir*, *venir*). *Dentro/fuera* are the Peninsular forms for "inside/outside," with *adentro/afuera*
as the Latin American equivalents — except that in Spain itself the longer forms are still used
specifically with verbs of motion (*¡Ven adentro!*). *Detrás* (position) vs. *atrás* (directional)
and *delante* (position) vs. *adelante* (directional) are parallel static/dynamic pairs with no
single-word English equivalent distinction. Directional sense is often added to a place adverb via
*por* (*Saltó por encima*, *Se arrastró por debajo*) or by placing the adverb after a noun to form a
compound adverbial phrase (*cuesta arriba/abajo*, *río arriba/abajo*, *tierra adentro*).

#### Chapter 7: Ya as a rhetorical/pragmatic particle, not just "already" (pp. 66–67)

Beyond its literal "already" sense and its combination with a negative to mean "no longer" (*Ya no
vive aquí*), *ya* has a cluster of purely rhetorical uses signalling growing irritation or
impatience with no direct single-word English translation (*¡Cállate ya!* "Be quiet [already]!",
*¡Ya era hora!* "It's about time!", *¡Ya empezamos!* "Here we go again!"). This pragmatic-particle
behavior is a genuinely distinct register-marking function beyond the adverb's literal temporal
meaning.

#### Chapter 8: The full subject/direct-object/indirect-object/prepositional-object pronoun paradigm, including vos and leísmo/laísmo (pp. 70–83)

Table 8.1 in the source lays out the complete four-category pronoun system (subject, direct object,
indirect object, prepositional object) across all persons, explicitly including the Latin American
*vos* paradigm (familiar singular, River Plate + Central America) alongside the Peninsular
*vosotros/as* (familiar plural, Spain only — replaced by *ustedes* for familiar-plural meaning
throughout Latin America, and also in the Canary Islands and western Andalusia). Several structural
points the book makes explicit and which beginner workbooks don't formalize: (1) weak (object)
pronouns can only ever be used attached to a verb, while subject and prepositional-object pronouns
can stand alone (contrast *La he visto* with *–¿Con quién hablaste? –Con ella*, never *Con la*);
(2) direct and indirect object pronoun forms are identical EXCEPT in the 3rd person (*lo/la/los/las*
vs. *le/les*); (3) **leísmo** — using *le* instead of *lo* for the masculine 3rd-person singular
direct object — is the actual majority/preferred usage in Madrid and central/northern Spain, and is
an Academy-accepted variant even though *lo* remains its own preferred recommendation; the
corresponding *les* for *los* is rarer but exists under the same name; (4) **laísmo** — using *la*
instead of *le* for a female indirect object (*Dila que venga*) — occurs in the same central/
northern-Spain dialect area but, unlike leísmo, is explicitly NOT Academy-recognized; (5) when two
3rd-person weak pronouns would collide (*le/les + lo/la/los/las*), the indirect object obligatorily
becomes *se* (*Se lo mandé*, never *Le lo mandé*) — this *se* can be disambiguated by adding *a* +
prepositional pronoun (*Se lo mandé a ella*); (6) indirect object pronouns are near-obligatorily
doubled with an explicit indirect-object noun phrase when that phrase refers to a person — the
so-called "redundant" use (*Dile al mesero que traiga la comida*) — this is not actually optional
despite the name; (7) weak pronouns attach to the END of positive commands, infinitives, and gerunds
(often triggering a written accent to preserve stress: *comprármelo*) but precede negative commands
and most finite-verb forms; a small closed list of governing verbs (*querer*, *poder*, *deber*, *ir
a*, *empezar a*...) allow the pronoun to either attach to the dependent infinitive/gerund or
precede the governing verb itself.

#### Chapter 9: The full indefinite adjective/pronoun/adverb inventory as a closed 17-item system (pp. 84–96)

The book treats indefinites as a genuinely closed, cross-referencing system rather than a loose
vocabulary list, and several of its distinctions go well beyond beginner-level teaching: *algo*
(things only) vs. *alguien* (persons only) split what English "something"/"someone" conflates into
separate invariable pronouns; *alguno* placed AFTER a noun in a negative sentence flips to mean "no...
at all" (*No hay motivo alguno*), functionally merging with *ninguno* — a genuinely counter-intuitive
reversal for learners; *uno/a* vs. *alguno/a* are near-synonyms in the plural but NEVER
interchangeable directly before *de* (singular always uses *uno/a de*, plural switches to
*algunos/as de*); *mucho/poco* and *demasiado* all double as invariable neuter pronouns (*Tengo
mucho/poco que hacer*) in addition to their adjective/adverb uses, and *poco* as an adverb can
function as a productive substitute for the English negating prefix "un-" (*poco interesante*
"uninteresting"); *todo* has adjective, pronoun, AND adverb uses with genuinely different syntax for
each (adjective: *todo el edificio*; neuter-article/demonstrative combination: *todo lo demás*,
*todo esto*; relative-clause combination: *todos los que*, where the *los/las*/*lo* can never be
dropped; adverb, appearing specifically between *ser* and a noun: *Era todo sonrisas* "She was all
smiles"); *cualquiera* shortens to *cualquier* only in its adjective use before a noun, never in its
pronoun use (*cualquier tienda* but *cualquiera* standing alone); *ambos* is a genuinely
formal/written-register item, replaced by *los/las dos* in speech, and — a trap for English
speakers — cannot be used to translate the emphatic English construction "both X and Y," which has
no literal Spanish equivalent (*Hablé con Carlos y con María*, not *con ambos Carlos y María*);
*cierto* and *tal/semejante* are explicitly exempted from the general indefinite-article-omission
rule that applies to other indefinites (§3.3.3.6) — i.e. *una cierta fatiga* is legitimate, unlike
most other indefinites in this chapter; and *otro*, unlike English "other," categorically never
combines with the indefinite article (*otra mesa*, never *una otra mesa*) while still preceding
cardinal numbers (*otros cuatro*).

#### Chapter 10 (boundary): Present subjunctive formed by a single systematic vowel change from the present indicative (p. 97)

The chunk's range ends just as Chapter 10 begins; its only content within this range is the chapter's
framing paragraph and the opening of §10.1. The one genuinely new point captured here (the regular
-ar/-er/-ir present-indicative paradigm itself is treated as redundant with 005's existing coverage):
the present subjunctive's endings are derived from the present indicative by one simple, fully
predictable vowel swap — -ar verbs take an -e-based ending in the subjunctive where the indicative
has -a-, while -er/-ir verbs take an -a-based ending where the indicative has -e-/-i- (so indicative
*hablo* → subjunctive *hable*, indicative *bebo* → subjunctive *beba*). A further structural point
the book flags explicitly: unlike the indicative, the 1st and 3rd person singular present-subjunctive
forms are always identical to each other (*hable* serves for both *yo* and *él/ella/usted*) — a fact
with real downstream implications for subjunctive-clause ambiguity that this book defers to its
later subjunctive-uses chapter (Chapter 12, out of range for this dispatch).

### Grammar points

#### 10.1 Present indicative and present subjunctive — radical, consonant, and orthography-changing verb classes (pp. 99-111)

The source's own detailed classification (radical-changing -ar/-er verbs, two -ir patterns, -uir
verbs with y-insertion, -iar/-uar verbs with stress shift, stem-diphthong verbs, g-insertion verbs,
c→zc verbs, and -ar/-er/-ir orthography-changing verbs for gu/z/c spelling preservation) duplicates
`007`'s and `008`'s paradigm coverage and is not re-detailed here (see the scope note above). The
one systematically new dimension running through every one of these tables is **vos morphology**:

- **Present indicative vos**: takes the *vosotros* ending with its glide dropped (-ás/-és/-ís) and
  crucially shows **no stem change at all**, even for verbs that are otherwise radical-changing,
  consonant-changing, or orthography-changing in the tú/usted/nosotros/vosotros/ellos forms: pensás,
  volvés, sentís, medís, ponés, conducís, aparecés, huís, enviás, continuás, aislás, reunís, prohibís,
  llegás, cazás, sacás, seguís, vencés, recogés — all built directly on the infinitive stem. This
  independently corroborates `015`'s finding of "no stem change in vos even for stem-changing verbs,"
  for present indicative specifically.
- **Present subjunctive vos**: this source consistently gives vos the **same form as tú**, including
  the stem change — Table 10.2 shows "2 (tú/vos) hables," and every subsequent present-subjunctive
  table in this chapter (Tables 10.4, 10.11, 10.13-10.15) likewise merges tú and vos into one row
  with the stem-changed form (e.g. pienses, envíes, aísles, llegues).
- **El Voseo — cross-source disagreement flagged.** `015_schaums_outline_of_spanish_grammar.md`
  (Jané/Silverstein, p. 37 running note) states the vos present subjunctive ending is a distinct
  **-és/-ás with no stem change** ("hablés, vendás, tengás, pidás"). This source gives the opposite:
  vos present subjunctive = tú present subjunctive, stem change included. Per this project's standing
  finding that cross-source agreement is weak evidence (and by the same logic, cross-source
  *disagreement* on a native-speaker-judgment-dependent point like voseo subjunctive morphology is a
  real signal, not noise) — this is exactly the kind of question flagged for `datasets/Spanish`'s
  native-speaker query folder rather than resolved by picking a side here. Both sources are reputable
  grammar references; voseo subjunctive forms may also simply vary regionally (Argentina vs. other
  voseo regions) in ways neither book made explicit.
- **Imperfect, preterite, imperfect subjunctive, future, conditional**: all give vos the **regular
  tú-form ending** (no distinct vos morphology) — Tables 10.19-10.28 mark the 2nd-person-singular row
  "(tú/vos)" throughout, with only the present tense and the imperative (see Chapter 19) singled out
  for a distinct vos ending.
- **Idiosyncratic-verb exception**: of the seven irregular verbs in Tables 10.16-10.17 (ser, estar,
  ir, dar, ver, haber, saber), only **ser** (vos sos) and **saber** (vos sabés) have a distinct vos
  present-indicative form at all; the other five use the same form for tú and vos.

#### 11.1.1 Present tense — five extended uses beyond simple present time (pp. 128-129)

Besides habitual/general-truth statements and present states, the present tense covers: (a)
imminent or pre-arranged future action ("Venga, cenamos aquí" — OK, we'll have dinner here); (b)
vivid narration of past events ("historic present"); (c) a special English-perfect-replacement use
in "this is the first time that..." sentences (Esta es la primera vez que viajo en barco — lit.
"this is the first time I travel by boat").

#### 11.1.2 The preterite tense — two basic uses (p. 129)

Completed actions/events in sequence, and states of affairs that existed for a specified (possibly
long) past period and have now ended — often signaled by durante 'for' or hasta 'until'.

#### 11.1.3 The imperfect tense — four uses (pp. 130-132)

Actions in progress (often setting the scene for a preterite-narrated event); states/conditions;
habitual past actions (signaled by a menudo, de vez en cuando, siempre, cada día); intended/expected
future-from-past actions; and politeness-softening of requests (Quería pedirle un favor). Note on
nunca: as a frequency adverb it pairs with the imperfect ("Nunca íbamos al cine" — we never used to
go), but to deny that a single action ever took place it requires the preterite ("Yo nunca fui a su
casa" — I never went to his house) — a subtle distinction not reducible to a single translation rule.

#### 11.1.4 Preterite vs. imperfect — direct minimal-pair contrasts (pp. 132-134)

The source frames this as the central challenge for English speakers, since English collapses both
into one past-tense form. Four contrast classes given: (a) ser/estar — preterite for an ended state
or bounded past period ("Sánchez fue presidente tres veces" — had three terms), imperfect for a state
merely existing at some past time ("Sánchez era presidente en aquella época" — was president at the
time); (b) action-vs-state minimal pairs with the same verb, e.g. "Fue fácil hacerlo" (it was easy
and got done) vs. "Era fácil hacerlo" (it was an easy thing to do, abstractly) — also "Tuve que
pagar" (had to, and did pay) vs. "tenía que acostarse" (bedtime was simply eight o'clock, no claim
about compliance); (c) psychological verbs — preterite marks an act of recognizing/learning/
understanding, imperfect a state of mind, with saber/conocer showing divergent English translations
("Después de hablar con él supe hacerlo" — I understood/figured out how to do it vs. "Sabía cómo
hacerlo" — I (already) knew how).

#### 11.1.5-11.1.6 Future and conditional tenses — prediction/promise, and conjecture (pp. 133-135)

Beyond plain future reference, the future tense is used to conjecture or guess, including in
surprised questions ("¿Será Paco el que ha hecho esto?" — Could it be Paco who did this?). Note: in
everyday speech the future is often replaced by ir + a + infinitive in both Spain and Latin America,
and by haber + de + infinitive in Latin America specifically. The conditional, beyond its core use in
conditional-sentence main clauses (Ch. 13) and with deber/poder/querer (Ch. 16), has two further
uses: reported speech for an original future-tense statement ("Anunció que se jubilaría en julio" —
he announced he would retire in July; though imperfect ir + a + infinitive, e.g. iba a jubilarse, is
more common in speech), and media-register supposition about present or past facts not precisely
known ("El huracán se localizaría ahora en el sur del golfo" — the hurricane now appears to be in
the southern gulf).

#### 11.2 Compound tenses — four cross-cutting rules, plus a Peninsular/Latin-American usage split (pp. 135-138)

General rules for all four compound tenses (perfect, pluperfect, future perfect, conditional
perfect): (a) the past participle never agrees with haber's subject or object; (b) nothing may
normally be inserted between haber and the participle, except an object pronoun attached to haber
as an infinitive/gerund (después de haberlo dicho); (c) acabar (present/imperfect/future/
conditional) + de + infinitive expresses English "have/had/will have/would have just done"; (d) with
transitive verbs, tener can replace haber to emphasize the result, with obligatory participle
agreement with the direct object (Tengo corregidos los ensayos).

**Perfect-tense dialectal usage divergence (flagged, not previously captured elsewhere in this
project's Spanish corpus):** the source explicitly states "the picture given below reproduces
Peninsular usage, excluding Asturias and Galicia which in this respect are more like Latin America,
where the preterite is more common" in place of the perfect tense. This is a genuine
region-of-Spain-level dialectal split (not just a Spain/Latin-America binary) worth flagging for
later mechanics analysis.

Individual compound-tense uses: perfect for unspecified past time with present relevance (Nunca he
comido pulpo), unfinished time periods (hoy, ahora, todavía/aún no, este + noun), and very recent
events where English would use simple past ("¿Qué has dicho?" — What did you say?); pluperfect for
what had happened before a past reference point; future perfect for what will have happened by a
future point, or (like the future tense generally) for conjecture (¿Lo habrá hecho Miguel? — Could
it be Miguel who did it?); conditional perfect for what would have happened, and in media register
for unconfirmed-fact supposition about the past (Chávez habría nacido en Arequipa en 1930 — Chávez
appears to have been born in Arequipa); and the past anterior (preterite of haber + participle),
now confined to literature, used after temporal conjunctions (después de que, cuando, luego que,
apenas) when the main verb is preterite — in modern Spanish replaced by the preterite or, less
commonly, the pluperfect.

#### 11.3 Progressive/continuous tenses — two restrictions versus English usage (pp. 138-140)

(1) Spanish progressives cannot refer to future time at all ("El sábado llega Angela," never
×está llegando× — this blocks a genuine future progressive tense from existing); (2) progressives
are not used with ir (except for recurrent events), llevar meaning 'to wear', or posture verbs
(estar sentado 'sitting', never ×estaba sentando×). Individual tense notes: preterite progressive
requires a bounded time frame (durante, hasta); future progressive doubles as present-time
supposition (A estas horas estarán acostando a los niños); conditional progressive is the
past-time equivalent (Estarían cenando — they must have been having supper); future perfect
progressive occurs mainly in conjectures (¿Habrá estado intentando llamar?).

#### 11.4 Time expressions with hacer, desde, llevar — three distinct constructions for "ago"/"for"/"since" (pp. 140-142)

See the Vocabulary table above for the individual constructions. Key structural point: in the
"event begun in the past and still continuing" sense, Spanish systematically uses the *present*
tense where English uses the perfect, and the *imperfect* where English uses the pluperfect — a
genuine typological mismatch, not just an idiom to memorize. llevar + gerund cannot be negated (sin
+ infinitive, or the hace/desde construction, substitutes instead: "Llevo muchos años sin jugar al
rugby").

#### 11.5 Verbs like gustar — argument-structure-inverting verbs (pp. 143-144)

A structural class, not just gustar itself: the English subject surfaces in Spanish as an indirect
object, and the English direct object becomes the Spanish subject (which may be a noun, infinitive,
clause, or bare 3rd-person verb form). Three semantic subclasses given explicitly: emotional
reaction (encantar, apasionar, molestar, preocupar que...); needing/lacking/remaining (hacer falta,
faltar, quedar, sobrar); interest/appreciation/reflection (apetecer/provocar [LA], parecer). A
further subset corresponds to English impersonal "it" constructions (tocar(le), convenir(le)).

### Grammar points

#### 12.1 Subjunctive in subordinate que-clauses — two key characteristics (pp. 145-146)

(1) The subjunctive in a que-clause generally requires the main-clause subject to differ from the
subordinate-clause subject (Quiere que yo venga vs. same-subject Quiere venir, which uses the
infinitive instead). (2) English speakers should watch for two systematic correspondence patterns:
an English infinitive construction ("I asked him to go") maps onto Spanish main-clause + que +
subjunctive (Le pedí que se fuera), and an English gerund construction ("I insist on Antonio doing
it") likewise maps onto que + subjunctive (Insisto en que lo haga Antonio) rather than any gerund
form in Spanish.

#### 12.1.1 Subjunctive after verbs exerting influence on other persons/things (pp. 146-147)

Covers verbs of requesting/commanding/ordering, wanting/desiring, proposing/suggesting,
permitting/allowing/approving, encouraging, causing, and (negatively) avoiding/prohibiting/
preventing/forbidding — and influence expressed via a noun phrase (el deseo de que, la orden de
que) rather than a verb. Two infinitive-replacement notes: a small set of verbs (mandar, impedir,
permitir) allow a direct infinitive even with differing subjects (see Ch. 17); separately, verbs
requiring a before an infinitive (animar a, ayudar a, forzar a, obligar a, invitar a, persuadir a)
offer the infinitive as a genuine alternative to que + subjunctive (Me obligaron a pagarlo = Me
obligaron a que lo pagara).

#### 12.1.2 Subjunctive after doubt, denial, or uncertainty (pp. 147-148)

Core trigger: verbs meaning "to doubt"/"to deny" (dudar, negar). Two mood-reversal patterns worth
flagging: negating dudar/negar removes the doubt-expression and so triggers the *indicative* ("No
negó que hubo un error" — he didn't deny there had been a mistake); conversely some verbs/phrases
only acquire a doubt/uncertainty sense, and thus require the subjunctive, *when themselves negated*
(no dice que..., no creo que..., no es que..., no me parece que..., plus no está claro que/no es
verdad que/no es seguro que/no significa que). Regional note: in Latin America, phrases that merely
place a limit on certainty (lo más cierto es que, casi es seguro que) can also take the subjunctive.

#### 12.1.3 Subjunctive after feeling/personal-opinion verbs and phrases — four patterns (pp. 148-149)

(a) ser/estar + adjective + que, corresponding to English impersonal "it" (es mejor que, está
bien/mal que, es una pena/vergüenza que, es imprescindible que, es posible/probable que,
es curioso/normal/lógico que, puede ser que, más vale que, hace falta que) — contrasted explicitly
with factual phrases like está claro/es obvio que, which take the indicative since they express no
feeling or opinion. (b) An emotional-response verb with the English-subject expressed as a Spanish
indirect object (gustar-pattern verbs: alegrar, asustar, encantar, enfadar, molestar — "Me sorprende
que lo hagan tan mal"). (c) Verbs where the English and Spanish subjects coincide (esperar, odiar,
preferir, sentir, temer — "Espero que apruebe el examen"). (d) Reflexively-used verbs that require
de que rather than bare que (alegrarse (de que), sorprenderse (de que), tener miedo (de que) —
"Se sorprendió de que hubiera tanta gente").

#### 12.1.4 Subjunctive after a relative pronoun with a negative or unknown antecedent (pp. 149-152)

Negative antecedent (No tienen ningún vestido que me guste) is straightforward for English
speakers. **Indefinite/unknown antecedent is the harder case**: the source's worked minimal pair
"Lucas quiere subir una montaña que mida/mide más de 3000 metros" has identical English
translations but opposite implications — subjunctive mida = Lucas's goal is any sufficiently tall
mountain (antecedent genuinely unknown); indicative mide = Lucas has one specific mountain in mind
(antecedent known). This same unknown-vs-known distinction extends to relative clauses introduced
by cuyo, como, cuando, cuanto, donde, quien, lo que, el/la/los/las que, not just que. Additional
micro-system: the -quiera indefinites (cualquiera, dondequiera, quienquiera are current outside
literary contexts; comoquiera/cuandoquiera are archaic, replaced by como/cuando) and the por (muy)
.../por mucho... "however (much)" construction, both with their own mood-conditioning rules (see
Vocabulary table).

#### 12.2 Subjunctive required by subordinating conjunctions — a two-tier system (pp. 152-157)

**Tier 1 — always subjunctive:** purpose (para que and formal synonyms), proviso (con tal (de) que,
a condición de que), "unless" (a no ser que, a menos que, and excepto que/salvo que in their
"unless" sense only), sin que "without" (meaning "unless" if the main clause is itself negative),
supposition (en caso de que, (en el) supuesto que), and antes (de) que "before" — the only common
temporal conjunction that is *always* subjunctive regardless of main-clause tense.

**Tier 2 — subjunctive only for future reference, indicative otherwise:** the full set of temporal
conjunctions (a medida que, en cuanto, cuando, hasta que, desde que, siempre que, después (de) que,
una vez (que)) — demonstrated with the minimal pair "Siguieron luchando hasta que se rindieron los
enemigos" (indicative, already happened) vs. "Debemos seguir luchando hasta que nuestros enemigos se
rindan" (subjunctive, future-referring). Exception: mientras takes the indicative even for future
reference unless it specifically means "as long as." Side note: esperar/aguardar 'to wait' commonly
use a que instead of hasta que when the subjunctive is called for.

Also tier-2: concessive conjunctions (aunque and its less common synonyms/literary variants — see
Vocabulary table), result/purpose de manera/modo/forma que (indicative=result vs.
subjunctive=purpose, directly paired with the illustrative minimal pair "¡Escóndelo... de modo que
nadie lo descubra!" [purpose] vs. "Lo escondió... de modo que nadie lo descubrió" [result]), the
conditional conjunction como (indicative "as" vs. subjunctive conditional "if"), and the causal
conjunction porque (indicative unless itself negated — note that porque can *follow* a negated
clause without being negated itself and still take the indicative: "No me cae bien porque es muy
cínico").

#### 12.3 Subjunctive in main clauses — three independent uses (pp. 157-158)

Besides its subordinate-clause role and its command use (Ch. 19), the subjunctive surfaces in main
clauses: (1) after "perhaps" words — tal vez/talvez [LA]/quizá(s)/posiblemente for less-likely
possibility, acaso in formal/rare usage, contrasted with a lo mejor which is common in speech but
takes the *indicative*; for future time, present subjunctive is standard though future indicative
(never present indicative) also occurs; (2) ojalá (que) to exclaim a wish, with mood/tense choice
signaling likelihood (imperfect subjunctive = less likely to be fulfilled), plus quién + imperfect
subjunctive to express envy; (3) a set of fixed subjunctive idioms and reduplicated-verb
constructions (see Vocabulary table).

#### 12.4 Sequence of tenses — statistical pattern, not a fixed rule (pp. 158-159)

Given fewer subjunctive tenses than indicative ones, the default correspondence is: present/
future/perfect-indicative main clause → present/perfect subjunctive subordinate; imperfect/
preterite/conditional/pluperfect-indicative main clause → imperfect/pluperfect subjunctive
subordinate; any command form → present subjunctive. Explicitly flagged as a tendency, not an
absolute rule — contexts requiring a tense mismatch do occur (Es una pena que estuvieras enferma —
present main clause, imperfect subjunctive subordinate).

#### 12.5 Additional uses of the -ra imperfect subjunctive form (p. 159)

Beyond its core role, the -ra form (not -se) can replace a past indicative tense — mainly in the
press and literary Spanish in Spain, but more broadly across registers in Latin America — notably in
relative clauses and after temporal conjunctions like después (de) que, luego que, desde que: "La
iniciativa vino en una carta que dirigiera al presidente" (= había dirigido). Cross-referenced to
Ch. 16 for -ra as a conditional-tense alternative with querer/deber/poder, and to 13.1.2.2 for -ra
replacing the conditional perfect.

#### 12.6 The future subjunctive (p. 159)

Essentially obsolete in modern Spanish (unlike modern Portuguese, where it remains alive) — survives
only in fossilized set phrases (sea lo que fuere, venga lo que viniere) and in legal/ecclesiastical
or occasionally Latin-American journalistic registers.

#### 13.1 Conditional clauses with si requiring the subjunctive (pp. 160-163)

Core distinction: a "closed condition" (contrary-to-fact or unlikely) takes the subjunctive; an
"open condition" (genuinely possible) takes the indicative. Two subjunctive tenses map to two time
frames: **pluperfect subjunctive** for a condition unfulfilled in the past (Si no hubieras dejado la
ventana abierta... no estaría/habría estado llena de mosquitos — main clause in conditional or
conditional perfect); **imperfect subjunctive** for a condition unfulfilled in the present (Si
tuviera dinero, lo compraría) or for a future event considered unlikely/improbable (¿Qué harías si
te ofrecieran el puesto?).

**Five "points to remember" (p. 162), checklist-style:** (1) only the imperfect and pluperfect
subjunctive tenses ever occur in a si-clause; (2) the -ra form of the imperfect subjunctive is now
commoner than -se; (3) present or perfect subjunctive after si is almost certainly an error — the
one rare exception is formal, primarily Latin-American usage after no saber (No sé si sea cierto
[LA], for the standard No sé si es cierto); (4) como si 'as if' always takes the imperfect or
pluperfect subjunctive (Gasta dinero como si fuera millonario); (5) an English "were to" (explicit
or insertable) signals a closed condition requiring the imperfect subjunctive (Si ofrecieran más,
¿venderías?). Also noted: the -ra (never -se) form of the pluperfect subjunctive can replace the
conditional perfect in the main clause itself (Si hubieran tenido más cuidado no hubiera pasado lo
que pasó), and in speech the conditional tense in the main clause is often informally replaced by
the imperfect (Pues yo me iba (si pudiera) — Well I would go (if I could)).

#### 13.2 Indicative tenses after si — open conditions (pp. 163)

Used for genuinely open conditions, including in reported speech referring to the past where the
original statement was an open condition (Prometió que lo devolvería si todavía lo tenía — he
promised he would return it if he still had it, from the original Te lo devolveré si todavía lo
tengo). Separate note: future/conditional tenses occur after si only when si is translatable as
"whether" (No sé si iré al trabajo — I don't know if/whether I'll go to work).

#### 13.3 Conditional sentences without si (p. 164)

Flagged but only cross-referenced, not detailed in this page range: de + infinitive in a
conditional sense (17.2.5.2) and como + subjunctive in a conditional sense (12.2.2.4, already
captured above).

### Grammar points

#### 14.1-14.2 Formation and true-reflexive meaning (pp. 165-166)

Standard reflexive-pronoun paradigm (including vos: "te lavás [LA]" — vos uses te, same as tú).
Genuine reflexive usage covers (a) the pronoun as direct object (Podía verse en el espejo — she
could see herself in the mirror) and (b) the pronoun as indirect object, the "benefactive"
reflexive corresponding to English "myself"/"for myself" (Ayer nos compramos un perrito), which
extends to the possessive-adjective-replacing pattern with body parts/clothing (Andrea se ha
torcido el tobillo — Andrea has twisted her ankle, lit. "twisted to-herself the ankle").

#### 14.3 Reciprocal meaning — "each other" (pp. 166-167)

Only occurs in plural. Genuine structural ambiguity flagged: since true-reflexive and reciprocal
forms are identical, "Se felicitaron" could mean either "they congratulated themselves" or "they
congratulated one another" — disambiguated by adding el uno al otro, (los) unos a (los) otros, or
mutuamente.

#### 14.4 Reflexives with an indirect object — the "unplanned event" pattern (p. 167)

se + indirect object corresponding to the English subject, used for unplanned/unexpected actions
(Se le cayeron las botellas que llevaba — he dropped the bottles; Se nos ha agotado el aceite — we
ran out of oil). Can extend to an "affected bystander" use with body-part/clothing
possessive-replacement sentences as an alternative to the plain reflexive of 14.2(b) — "Me he
torcido el tobillo" (yo as implicit subject) vs. "Se me ha torcido el tobillo" (el tobillo as
grammatical subject) are offered as genuine structural alternatives for the same real-world event.

#### 14.5 Se as an indefinite/impersonal subject (p. 168)

Equivalent to English "one"/"we"/"they"/"you"/"people," always with a 3rd-person-singular verb (Se
cena muy tarde en España). If the verb is already reflexive, the indefinite subject must instead be
introduced with uno/a (Uno se aburre de leer libros largos).

#### 14.6 Reflexive "to get/have something done" (p. 168)

A small set of verbs use the reflexive to mean having a service performed on oneself: Quiero
cortarme el pelo (I want to get my hair cut); Tuvo que ir a Francia para operarse (he had to go to
France to have an operation).

#### 14.7 Verbs reflexive in form but not in meaning (p. 168)

A fixed list of verbs (see Vocabulary table) that are always reflexive without any genuine
reflexive/reciprocal sense — most strikingly constiparse, which has a region-split meaning
(Spain: catch a cold / Latin America: become constipated) on the identical reflexive form.

#### 14.8 Transitive verbs used reflexively with intransitive meaning — four sub-patterns (pp. 169-174)

(1) **Change-of-state** (14.8.1): reflexive form = "become/get X" (aburrirse, asustarse, secarse —
full list of ~15 pairs tabulated in Table 14.1, previously cross-referenced, not re-tabulated here
since the pattern itself is the point, not each individual pair). (2) **What happens to the
subject** (14.8.2), especially with inanimate subjects, translatable as English "get ___" (Se
hundieron las ruedas en el barro — the wheels got stuck in the mud). (3) **Verbs of movement**
(14.8.3): a transitive motion verb's reflexive form is used intransitively (acercarse, alejarse,
meterse, moverse, pararse [note: "to stand up" is a Latin-American-only extension of meaning],
retirarse, sentarse — Table 14.2); separately, volverse 'to turn round' is unusual in that its
*non-reflexive* form volver is already intransitive ('to return') — with regresarse/devolverse as
Latin-American regional synonyms for 'to return'. (4) **Daily routines** (14.8.4): acostarse,
levantarse, afeitarse, vestirse, despertarse — the reflexive is simply the lexicalized form for
these routine actions.

#### 14.9 Verbs of becoming — five near-synonyms with distinct selectional restrictions (pp. 170-171)

All reflexive. convertirse en (nouns only, e.g. "turning into a nightmare"), hacerse (implies
deliberate effort — Se ha hecho millonaria), ponerse (temporary-state adjectives — ponerse
enfadado/gordo/nublado), quedarse (disability adjectives and some past participles — quedarse
ciego/sordo/dormido), volverse (principally psychological adjectives — Andrés se ha vuelto muy
antipático). This is a genuinely useful five-way semantic map of Spanish's distributed equivalent
to English "become," directly relevant for later analysis of how colloquial register might
innovate around or collapse these distinctions.

#### 14.10 Emphatic reflexive verbs — two sub-patterns (pp. 171-175)

(14.10.1) A long, individually-glossed list of common verbs (caer/caerse, bajar/bajarse,
dejar/dejarse, despedir/despedirse, estar/estarse, imaginar/imaginarse, ir/irse, llevar/llevarse,
marchar/marcharse, morir/morirse, negar/negarse, parecer/parecerse, quedar/quedarse,
saber/saberse, salir/salirse, subir/subirse) where the reflexive form adds suddenness,
unexpectedness, greater deliberateness, or simple emphasis relative to the plain form — captured
individually in the Vocabulary table above since each pair carries a distinct, non-formulaic
meaning shift rather than following one predictable rule. Special case: callarse and reírse(de) use
the *non-reflexive* form mainly as an infinitive following another verb (¡No me mandes callar!;
No me hagas reír), while the reflexive form is the independent/finite one. (14.10.2) A narrower
emphatic use specific to verbs of consumption (eating/drinking), stressing quantity or speed:
Se lo bebió de un trago (drank it in one gulp); zamparse (to guzzle/scoff down) as a vivid
colloquial example.

#### 15.1 Distinguishing estar + past participle from ser + past participle (pp. 176-177)

estar + participle indicates a resulting state/condition (la puerta estaba abierta — the door was
[in the state of being] open; las entradas estaban vendidas — the seats were [already] sold). ser +
participle is the true passive, focused on the action itself, and is required when an agent/"doer"
is specified (la puerta fue abierta por una criada — the door was opened by a maid). Noted
limitation: English can passivize a sentence whose active-voice subject corresponds to an *indirect*
object ("Mary was awarded a scholarship"), but Spanish cannot form a true ser-passive this way — see
15.2.2 for the workaround.

#### 15.1 (continued) — four refinements on ser/estar + participle (p. 177-178)

(1) Minimal-pair contrast: Fui detenido (I was detained = placed under arrest, an action) vs. Estaba
detenido (I was detained = under arrest, a state). (2) Agreement rule: sido/estado are invariable
when governed by haber, but a past participle governed by ser/estar always agrees in gender/number
with the subject. (3) Present/imperfect ser-passives are rare except for sustained/repeated actions
(Las cuentas son pagadas diariamente) — though the source notes this usage is growing in
newspaper captions (La capa de petróleo es avistada desde el avión). (4) The passive agent/"doer"
is normally introduced by por, but de is used after certain adjectival-function past participles:
rodeado de, seguido de, acompañado de, cubierto de, forrado de, adornado de.

#### 15.2 Six alternatives to the passive construction (pp. 178-181)

Spanish native speakers favor these over the ser-passive especially (i) when no agent is expressed,
(ii) in less formal writing, and (iii) in everyday speech. (15.2.1) **se-reflexive for non-person
subjects**, agreeing with the subject (Se construyó la casa en diez meses) — explicitly flagged as
*incorrect* to extend with a por-agent phrase (×La casa se compró por los Martínez× is wrong; a true
ser-passive is required instead). (15.2.2) **se-reflexive for an indirect-object scenario**, always
3rd-person-singular (Se le concedió a María una beca — María was awarded a scholarship) — this is
the systematic fix for the "indirect-object passive" gap noted in 15.1. (15.2.3) **se-reflexive for
a personal subject**, also forced to 3rd-person-singular specifically to avoid the true-reflexive/
reciprocal ambiguity that would otherwise arise with a personal subject (se registra a todos los
que entren, with personal a marking the person being acted on as direct object; pronoun objects use
le/les even for masculine referents, not lo/los — Se le/les acusó). An understanding aid given: se
here behaves as if it were an indefinite singular active subject ("Se le interrogó" ≈ "Someone
questioned him"). Exception: where no ambiguity risk exists (notices/ads), the ordinary reflexive
construction is used even for a person (Se busca niñera; Se necesitan cocineros). (15.2.4)
**Active-voice conversion**: recast as an active sentence (Un colega me entregó los archivos — I was
handed the files by a colleague). (15.2.5) **Indeterminate 3rd-person-plural active verb** when no
agent is expressed and context permits (Lo nombraron director — he was appointed director).
(15.2.6) **uno/la gente substitution** when the verb is already reflexive and so cannot take a
second se (Uno no se da cuenta de su importancia; La gente se olvida pronto de cantantes como él).

#### 16.1 Deber and deber de (pp. 182-183)

Prescriptively, deber alone = obligation ("must"/"should"/"ought to"); deber de = supposition/
inference ("must have," in the sense of likely inference, not duty). In practice, the source notes
native speakers increasingly blur this and use bare deber for supposition too (El río no debe ser
muy profundo) — but deber de is never acceptable for genuine obligation. Tense notes: conditional
deber = "ought" (Deberías vestirte de etiqueta); the -ra imperfect-subjunctive form can substitute
for this in formal Peninsular and more general Latin American usage (Debieran confesar su error);
either tense + perfect infinitive covers past "ought to have." For past "must have"/"should have,"
the choice between preterite and imperfect deber tracks whichever tense *would have been used
without deber* — i.e. Debía (de) tener tres hijos is based on the imperfect tenía tres hijos, while
Debí eligir la otra opción is based on the preterite eligí la otra opción. This is a clean,
transferable rule for translating "must have" tense choice that is not obviously stated this way in
other grammars checked so far in this corpus.

#### 16.2-16.3 Tener que and the haber-based obligation constructions (pp. 183-184)

tener que = strong necessity-framed obligation, roughly interchangeable with deber in most
contexts; preterite tener que specifically implies "had to, and did." (Separately, tener...que
without strong obligation sense just states something "to do": Tengo mucho que leer.) haber's
impersonal forms (hay que / había que / habrá que) express generally-applicable obligation/
necessity, with the preterite hubo que marking a completed-and-necessary action. haber de (milder,
less common, sometimes just a statement of expectation: ¿Qué hemos de hacer?) can also substitute
for deber de's supposition sense, especially in Latin America (Han de ser las seis — it must be six
o'clock).

#### 16.4 Querer as a modal (pp. 184-185)

Basic sense "to want." Polite requests use the -ra imperfect subjunctive or conditional (Quisiera/
querría ver el último modelo). Tense-meaning splits worth flagging: imperfect querer = simply
"wanted to" (no claim about attempting), positive preterite querer = "tried to" (Quise pedirle un
autógrafo pero no lo permitieron — I tried to ask for an autograph but wasn't allowed), negative
preterite querer = "refused to" (No quiso entrar en detalles).

#### 16.5 Poder — ability, permission, possibility, supposition (pp. 185-187)

Core "can"/"may"/"be able to." Conditional podría = "could"/"would be able to" (for future-oriented
hypotheticals) — explicitly contrasted with imperfect podía ("could"/"was able to," a past state
with no claim about an attempt) and preterite pudo ("managed to" if positive, "failed to" if
negative, implying an actual attempt was made). The source gives a direct warning against
mistranslating English "could" into the wrong Spanish tense, with the minimal pair "Podría ir
mañana" (future possibility, correct use of conditional) vs. "no podía mantenerse de pie" (past
inability/state, where conditional podría would be wrong). Beyond ability, the conditional of poder
also expresses hypothetical possibility ("might," "could" — No hagas eso, podrías caer), and
conditional/preterite poder + haber + past participle speculates about something that could have
happened but didn't (Nos podríamos haber salido de la carretera). The impersonal puede ser que
(subjunctive-triggering) and its contraction puede que (ser dropped unless puede itself is negated)
express "it is possible that."

#### 16.6-16.7 Saber and soler as modals (p. 187)

saber + infinitive = "can"/"could" specifically for a *learned* ability (¿Sabes jugar al ajedrez?),
distinct from poder's general ability/permission sense. soler + infinitive = "usually (does)"/"is
accustomed to," restricted to present and imperfect tenses only (no other tense of soler is used).

### Grammar points

#### 17.1 Finite verb + infinitive (pp. 188-189)

Same-subject rule: when two verbs share a subject, the second is normally an infinitive — typical
of modal verbs (Ch. 16), most psychological verbs, and verbs of necessity/accomplishment (Prefiero
hablar con él; Recuerdo haberlo conocido en Jerez; Necesitamos comprar gasolina; ¿Lograste
hacerlo?). Different-subject exception: a small, closed set of verbs (hacer, mandar/ordenar, dejar,
permitir, impedir, persuadir, prohibir, plus perception verbs oír/escuchar/mirar/sentir) take a
direct infinitive even when the finite verb's subject and the infinitive's subject differ (Nos
hicieron traducir un texto medieval; No permiten fumar a los pasajeros; Te oí entrar anoche) — an
alternative to the subjunctive constructions of 12.1.1. If no subject is specified for the
infinitive after these verbs, it takes on a passive/impersonal sense (Hice llamar a la policía — I
had the police called; Oí cantar una rumba — I heard someone singing a rumba).

#### 17.2 Prepositions + infinitive — a verb-governed system (pp. 189-193)

Which preposition a given verb takes before an infinitive must mostly be learned case by case, but
five rough semantic clusters are given: **(a) + infinitive** — progress toward an action: beginning
(empezar/ponerse a), preparation/readiness (comprometerse a), physical movement toward (venir a),
or obligation/influence/persuasion (obligar a) — note ir and volver specifically require a when used
figuratively ("to be going to," "to do again": No volveré a hacerlo). **de + infinitive** —
movement away from/refraining/dissuading (abstenerse de), cessation with a sense matching English
direct gerund (terminar de hablar = "finish speaking"), or cause/source of the main verb's action
(Me alegro de haber ido; Nos acusaron de ser espías) — flagged as not always semantically
transparent, reinforcing the general "learn verb+preposition pairs individually" caveat. **por +
infinitive** — powerful longing/struggle "for" (luchar por triunfar), or beginning/ending "by"/
opting "for" (acabar por, optar por). **en + infinitive** — roughly English verb + "in/on" + gerund
(insistir en, quedarse en, persistir en), with pensar en 'to think of/about' flagged as not quite
fitting the pattern. **con + infinitive** — a handful of fixed verbs (amenazar con, conformarse
con, soñar con).

Separately: (17.2.2) a preceding *noun* (rather than verb) can equally govern a preposition before
an infinitive, usually taking the same preposition as its related verb (su empeño en llamarme),
or more loosely de (la idea de veranear). (17.2.3) último/único/primero + en + infinitive translates
"the first/last to" (Fue el tercero y último en terminar). (17.2.4) Some prepositions attach to an
infinitive independent of any governing verb/noun: para, sin, causal por, después de, antes de
(Jorge se acostó sin cenar).

#### 17.2.5 Special/idiomatic preposition + infinitive combinations (pp. 193-194)

sin/a medio + infinitive ↔ English "un-"/"half-" + past participle (una camisa sin planchar; una
casa a medio construir); a + infinitive ↔ "to be ...-ed" (una deuda a liquidar). de + infinitive
has three further special senses: cause (Estoy ronco de tanto hablar — I'm hoarse from speaking so
much), ser + de + infinitive ↔ "to be ...-ed" (Es de esperar que dimita — it is to be hoped he'll
resign), and a conditional-clause-equivalent sense matching si (De saberlo no habría pagado = Si lo
hubiera sabido, no habría pagado) — the latter flagged as archaic/formal. al + infinitive ↔ English
"on" + "-ing" or "when" (Al ver lo que había pasado se puso a llorar).

#### 17.3 Infinitives in impersonal constructions (p. 194)

Beyond haber + que + infinitive (16.3), an infinitive follows ser + an obligation adjective
(preciso, obligatorio, necesario — Fue preciso llamar a un médico) and follows the formal-notice
formulas se ruega and se prohíbe (Se ruega no tocar las mercancías; Se prohíbe fumar).

#### 17.4-17.5 Infinitive as subject; infinitive with an explicit subject (pp. 194-195)

An infinitive (with its object) can be the subject of a finite verb, in either order (Cenar con
ellos fue una pesadilla / Fue una pesadilla cenar con ellos). Key ser + adjective + de +
infinitive rule: de appears only when the finite verb's subject is a separate noun/pronoun (Esa
puerta es difícil de abrir), and is dropped when the infinitive itself is the subject (Es
imprescindible tenerlo / Tenerlo es imprescindible) — a subtle but systematic distinction
explicitly flagged as a common trap for French-background learners. Separately, unlike English, a
Spanish infinitive may carry its own explicit subject (a following noun/pronoun): Por llegar tú
tarde, a mí me echaron una bronca — though the source notes a conjunction+finite-verb paraphrase
(porque tú llegaste tarde) is more usual in practice.

#### 17.6 The infinitive as a verbal noun (pp. 195-196)

Spanish uses the infinitive, not a gerund, as its verbal noun, optionally taking a definite/
indefinite article or demonstrative/possessive adjective and even adjectival modification (Ese
continuo pelear no se puede tolerar) — flagged as characteristic of literary rather than
conversational Spanish. Some infinitives have fully lexicalized into ordinary masculine nouns with
plural forms (parecer 'opinion', amanecer 'daybreak', atardecer 'dusk', anochecer 'nightfall',
acontecer 'happening/sequence of events', pesar 'sorrow/grief', saber 'knowledge', ser 'being',
hablar 'speech'), and a handful have split singular/plural meanings: andar 'walk/gait' vs. andares
'travels, adventures'; deber 'duty' vs. deberes 'homework/assignments'; decir 'figure of speech/
saying' vs. decires 'sayings/rumours'; haber 'credit' vs. haberes 'assets, income, earnings'.

#### 18.1-18.2 Basic gerund use; gerund and main verb with different subjects (pp. 197-198)

Core sense: simultaneity with the main verb's action ("in/while/by doing" — Antonio contestó
sonriendo). Extends to method/cause/purpose (Ganaron haciendo trampas — they won by cheating).
Inserting como before the gerund shifts simultaneity from real to imagined, parallel to como si +
subjunctive (Movió la cabeza como diciendo no). Separately, with perception/depiction verbs (ver,
describir, fotografiar, imaginar, mostrar, oír, pintar, recordar, sorprender), the gerund's subject
can instead be the main verb's direct object (La vi bailando con Paco — I saw her dancing with
Paco) — with a further wrinkle: the preterite of ver + a plain infinitive marks the witnessed action
as complete (Lo vi tirar unas piedras — I saw him throw some stones, a finished act) versus + gerund
for an action witnessed in progress (Lo vi tirando piedras — I saw him throwing stones). venir and
ir themselves are generally used as infinitives rather than gerunds in this construction (Lo miramos
venir hacia nosotros).

#### 18.3 Gerund in place of a relative clause (pp. 198-199)

Increasingly accepted informally to modify a noun (Un coche circulando a la izquierda sería parado
casi inmediatamente), though a full relative clause remains the safer, more standard option — and
is in fact obligatory with non-action verbs (un libro que describe su viaje..., never ×un libro
describiendo...×). Common in official-register communiqués and photo captions (el primer ministro
besando a un bebé).

#### 18.4 Six individually-idiomatic gerund-governing verbs (pp. 199-200)

ir/andar + gerund (literal "going about doing," with andar sounding dismissive/critical; ir can
also lose its motion sense to stress gradual/repeated progression, as in Ve echando monedas —
"keep putting coins in"); venir + gerund ("has/had been doing/happening"); pasar + gerund ("spent
[time] doing"); seguir/continuar + gerund (the *only* way to express "to continue doing" — no
infinitive alternative exists here); acabar/terminar + gerund ("ending up doing" — Terminarás
rompiéndolo); salir + gerund (meaning fully dependent on the specific gerund — salir perdiendo
"come off worst," salir volando "be off like a shot").

#### 18.5 Four cases where English "-ing" does NOT map to a Spanish gerund (pp. 200-201)

(1) After prepositions — only the infinitive is possible (17.2). (2) As a verbal noun — again only
the infinitive (No le gusta nadar). (3) As an adjective — Spanish instead uses a dedicated
adjective, often in -ante/-iente/-ente (un objeto volador, un tipo de cambio flotante), a
prepositional phrase (una clase de natación, botas de montar), or another solution entirely
(humedad de paredes — "rising damp"); the only common true adjectival-gerund exceptions are
ardiendo and hirviendo, both invariable and postposed (aceite hirviendo, una casa ardiendo). (4) To
describe a position or condition — the past participle is used instead (arrodillado 'kneeling',
sentado 'sitting', pesado 'boring', divertido 'amusing').

#### 19.1 Imperative morphology — the vos dimension (pp. 202-205)

Core tú/vosotros [SP] paradigm (tú = regular 3rd-person-singular present indicative form for
affirmative commands only; vosotros = infinitive's final -r replaced by -d, with that -d dropped
before reflexive os except in irse > idos; eight shortened irregular tú forms: di, haz, ve, pon,
sal, sé, ten, ven, extending to their compounds, e.g. detener > detén) is previously covered by
`008c` and not re-tabulated. **The vos [LA] imperative, by contrast, is new and systematically
captured here**: formed by dropping the vosotros form's final -d (so it applies to all eight
"shortened" verbs too, which keep their vosotros-style stem for vos rather than the tú-shortened
one), with a written accent on the final vowel — except the monosyllables ¡da! and ¡ve! — retained
even with an attached pronoun: buscá, probá (cf. tú prueba — a case where vos actually diverges
from the stem-changed tú form, consistent with vos's general no-stem-change pattern), vendé,
volvé, viví, dormí, seguí, tené, decí, acostáte. Examples given: María, vení lo más pronto posible;
Convertíte en estrella; Casáte con él; Hacé click sobre el ícono; Ingresá tu usuario y clave;
Sentáte y calláte — note several of these examples are drawn from computing/tech-interface register
(click, ícono, usuario, clave), a detail possibly worth flagging for later register-mapping work
even though this source doesn't comment on it itself.

#### 19.2 Commands using the present subjunctive (pp. 205-207)

Required for: all usted/ustedes commands (3rd-person subjunctive — also used for familiar-plural
commands throughout Latin America, where ustedes replaces vosotros [SP]); all negative commands
regardless of formality (2nd or 3rd person subjunctive as appropriate, with object pronouns
preceding rather than following the verb — contrast Hazlo/No lo hagas); 1st-person-plural "let's"
commands (1st-person-plural present subjunctive, with final -s dropped before an attached nos:
sentemos + nos > sentémonos) — though ir a + infinitive (Vamos a cenar en otro sitio) is the more
common informal substitute, and ir/irse themselves irregularly keep their present-tense forms
vamos/vámonos rather true subjunctive vayamos/vayámonos even in this construction; and 3rd-person
commands introduced by que, translatable with English "let" (Que nadie hable durante el examen;
Que discutan el proyecto mañana) — a pattern that extends even to 2nd-person commands (Que tengáis
una feliz Navidad [SP]). Vos note: for negative commands, vos simply uses the standard 2nd-person-
singular present subjunctive, identical to tú (No me toques (vos)) — unlike the vos affirmative
imperative, there is no separate vos form here.

#### 19.3 Alternative ways of expressing commands (pp. 207-208)

(19.3.1) **Softened commands**: formulas like ¡Háganme el favor de sentarse! or rephrasing as a
question (¿Podría apagar la tele?; ¿Me da un poquito de pan?) — explicitly noted that Spanish
speakers use direct commands more readily than English speakers even in polite registers, so these
softening strategies are optional rather than socially obligatory the way they often are in English.
(19.3.2) **Infinitive, present, or future tense as command**: the infinitive is increasingly used
for written notices/instructions, especially negative ones (No escupir — No spitting), and in Spain
commonly replaces the vosotros -d imperative in speech (Limpiaros [SP] los dientes antes de
acostaros); a + infinitive can convey a 1st-person-plural command (Y ahora muchachos, a trabajar);
the present tense expresses a curt, potentially rude-sounding command common among friends (Vas
allá [LA] y les dices que no); the future tense formulates an authoritative, dogmatic command
(¡No lo abrirás hasta que no te lo diga!). (19.3.3) **Impersonal commands**: 3rd-person
singular/plural subjunctive + se, used for formal written instructions/directions/recipes (Después
de abrir la lata, guárdese en la nevera; Pónganse las almendras en la sartén y déjense dorar [SP]).

### Grammar points

#### Estar de + noun, and colloquial estar + noun (Ch. 20.5, pp. 214)

*Estar de* (never bare *estar*) is used with a noun to express that a person is acting in a given
capacity or occupying a position ("Estoy de jefe hasta que vuelva Juan" — "I am the boss until Juan
returns"). It also forms fixed prepositional idioms (*estar de vacaciones*, *estar de buen/mal humor*,
*estar de viaje*). Separately — and genuinely exceptional, since the general rule is that *ser* (not
*estar*) precedes a bare noun — *estar* occasionally combines directly with a noun to form a
colloquial expression, e.g. *Estoy pez en matemáticas* "I'm clueless about maths," *Está fenómeno*
"It's fantastic."

#### Simple prepositions (Chapter 21)

The book treats each simple preposition individually. Selected points not captured in prior
established files:

- **Personal *a***: required before a direct object naming a specific known person, before nouns
  denoting groups of people, before certain indefinite pronouns (*alguien, nadie, ninguno, usted,
  otro, todos*), and — despite its name — commonly before animals the speaker feels affection for
  (especially pets). It is *not* used before an inanimate object referred to by a person's name (e.g.
  "No conoce todo Vargas Llosa" — not all the *works* of Vargas Llosa). It is also used before certain
  inanimate objects after verbs of precedence/superiority (*obedecer, preceder, suceder, seguir,
  superar, sustituir, vencer*), and can be inserted to disambiguate the object of a verb in relative
  clauses or with verbs like *llamar* that can take two following nouns.
- ***A*** also covers: time/age ("A los quince años ya era futbolista"), a looser "after" sense ("A
  los seis meses de regresar, se enfermó"), price/speed/rate ("¿A cuánto están las berenjenas?"), and
  — after verbs of smell/taste/sound (*oler, saber, sonar*) — "of"/"like" ("El vino sabe a vinagre").
- **Ante vs. delante de vs. bajo/debajo de**: *ante* is figurative ("faced with"), *delante de* is
  literal position; *bajo* is literal-but-less-restricted or figurative, *debajo de* strictly literal
  (with LA *abajo de* as a common alternative).
- **Con**: beyond basic "with," covers encounter/collision/struggle verbs (*tropezar con, enfrentarse
  con*), cause, attitude-towards, and concessive "in spite of" ("Con todo el dinero que tiene nunca
  invita").
- **De**: ten distinct sub-uses are catalogued (origin/departure, ownership/relationship/association
  [including the mocking "de + name" construction], geographical names, material/content,
  about/concerning, cause, "as/like" [occupation, role, use-to-which-put], adjectival phrases,
  adverbial-phrase formation, plus cross-references for comparative, temporal, infinitival, agentive,
  and compound-noun uses handled elsewhere in the book).
- **Dentro de / fuera de**: physical and future-temporal "within." LA sometimes uses *adentro de*
  (not generally accepted in Spain) and *afuera de*.
- **Desde**: emphasizes range/distance more than *de*, disambiguates "from" vs. "of," and combines
  with *hasta* for stronger emphasis than *de...a*.
- **Detrás de / tras**: *atrás de* is the LA variant of *detrás de*; *tras* is more literary/
  journalistic, implying "hidden behind," and is the regular choice for temporal succession ("día tras
  día") — in LA, reduplicated-word succession instead commonly uses *a* ("día a día").
- **En, encima de, sobre**: only *en* covers "in(side)"/"at" of place and time; *sobre*/*encima de*
  are preferred where *en* would be ambiguous (e.g. *en el armario* = "in" or "on" the cupboard).
  *Encima de* is favored for greater/unexpected height. LA sometimes substitutes *a* for *en* after
  verbs like *entrar, ingresar, penetrar*, and uses *arriba de* for "on top of."
- **Enfrente de / frente a**: both mean "opposite"/"in front of"; *frente a* additionally covers
  "in the face of," "in opposition to."
- **Entre**: "between"/"among(st)"; note the divergence from English in "Entre Jorge, Luis y Antonio
  lograron formar un equipo" ("Between them/Together, ... managed to..."), and the fixed phrase *entre
  semana* ("on weekdays").
- **Hacia / hasta**: *hacia* = "towards" (literal/figurative) and also "around/about" (time or place);
  *hasta* = "until/as far as/up to," plus a full set of farewell idioms (*hasta ahora, hasta luego,
  hasta siempre, hasta la vuelta, hasta entonces, hasta mañana, hasta pronto, hasta la vista*).
- **Según**: "according to"/"depending on," and can stand alone in speech meaning "it depends."
  Before verbs like *decir, afirmar, declarar* it means "according to what"; before other verbs it can
  imply "as/whilst" (= *mientras*/*a medida que*).
- **Sin**: "without"; *sin* + infinitive can convey "un-"/"not."
- **Combinations of prepositions**: *de, desde, por* can combine with other prepositions ("El agua
  salía de entre las tablas"; "Desde detrás de la pared se oía música"), and *por* is commonly added
  to compound prepositions to add a sense of movement ("El niño se escondió por debajo de la cama").
- **Cuando and donde as prepositions**: in everyday Spanish these relative adverbs are sometimes used
  effectively as prepositions ("Venía aquí mucho cuando niña" — "I often came here as a girl"; "Viven
  donde Eduardo y Ana" — "They live near Eduardo and Ana"). This is a genuinely distinct colloquial
  extension not covered by the book's own formal relative-clause chapter.

#### Por and para (Chapter 22)

The book frames the core semantic contrast as *por* looking back to source/origin, *para* pointing
forward to destination/aim. Twelve distinct senses of *por* are catalogued (pp. 233–236): agent of a
passive ("Fue invadido por los romanos"), cause/reason, duration, place-through-which, imprecise
location/time ("around"), manner/means, exchange, "on behalf of," personal-opinion designation,
rate/quantity, "remains to be done" (*por* + infinitive), objective-of-movement (*Ha ido por pan*, with
Peninsular *a* commonly inserted: *Ha ido a por pan*), and multiplication ("Cinco por dos son diez").
Four senses of *para* are catalogued: destination/beneficiary/function, deadline/target-in-time,
purpose (*para* + infinitive), and an "against expectation" comparison sense ("Para un francés habla
bien portugués" — "For a Frenchman he speaks Portuguese well"). A genuinely subtle point: *por* +
infinitive can sometimes substitute for *para* + infinitive when expressing cause/motive rather than
pure purpose — e.g. *por (querer) estar conmigo* ("to be with me," i.e. "because of wanting to be with
me"). *Estar para* (Peninsular, marked [SP]) expresses "about to"/"on the point of" (= *estar a punto
de*); in Latin America, *estar por* commonly conveys this same sense, while *estar por* elsewhere
(Peninsular and LA alike) also expresses personal inclination ("Estaba por decir que no se podía
hacer" — "I was inclined to say...").

#### Numerals and numerical expressions (Chapter 23) — genuinely new material

Beyond the already-covered cardinal/ordinal/fraction/time basics: Spanish punctuation for numbers uses
a period for thousands/millions and a comma for decimals (except in Mexico, which follows UK/US
practice); *y* "and" appears only between tens and units, never after hundreds/thousands; telephone
numbers are read in pairs (with special handling for an odd digit count). Approximate numbers are
formed with *unos/as* + cardinal, or with *aproximadamente, alrededor de, más o menos, cerca de, cosa
de, cosa así, como*. The full collective-numeral inventory (*centena/centenar, decena, docena,
veintena, treintena, cuarentena, quincena, millar*) mostly expresses approximate rather than exact
counts, with *par, docena,* and *quincena* as the exceptions that are exact. Fractions from 1/11 onward
are formed productively by adding *-avo* to a cardinal (*-vo* if the cardinal ends in *-a*); in Latin
America (rarely in Peninsular usage) these *-avo* forms are also used as ordinals. Multiple-birth
vocabulary (*mellizos/gemelos, trillizos, cuatrillizos, quintillizos, sextillizos, septillizos*) and
the *múltiple/doble/triple/cuádruple/quíntuple/séxtuple* adjective set are both given as closed
inventories; Spanish has no exact equivalent of English "-fold," so cardinal + *veces* ("ten times")
is the general substitute strategy.

#### Negation (Chapter 24)

Spanish negation never uses an equivalent of "do"-support, and routinely combines two negative words
in a single clause (the reverse of the English "double negative" stigma) — e.g. *No vimos a nadie*.
*No* precedes the verb (or its auxiliary in compound tenses); only a weak object pronoun can intervene.
*Nadie/nada* can precede or follow the verb; when *nada* is qualified by an adjective, that adjective
takes masculine gender ("La teoría no tiene nada novedoso"). *Nunca*/*jamás* behave similarly, with
*jamás* more emphatic/less common; the two can be stacked for maximum force (*nunca jamás*). *Ni
(siquiera)* covers "neither...nor" and "not even." *Ninguno* agrees in gender, shortens to *ningún*
before masculine singular nouns, and is now rarely used in the plural except with inherently-plural
nouns. *Tampoco* ("neither"/"not either") drops its associated verb in negative-reply constructions,
mirroring English "Nor did I." A genuinely subtle point: a "superfluous" *no* — not semantically
negating anything — is used by native speakers after *hasta que* when the main clause is itself
negative ("No les des nada hasta que no terminen el trabajo" — "Don't give them anything until they
finish the work"), and occasionally in comparisons after *que* to avoid an awkward *que que* sequence.
Separately, certain Spanish negative words translate an English phrase that is not itself negative
(*sin decir nada* = "without saying anything") — this occurs systematically after *sin, sin que, antes
de, antes que, apenas*; in comparative constructions ("Jugó mejor que nadie" — "He played better than
anyone"); after expressions of impossibility/improbability/doubt/denial; and in rhetorical questions
expecting a negative answer. Conversely, a handful of outwardly *affirmative* phrases carry negative
meaning without needing an accompanying negative word: *en mi/la vida* ("never in my life"), *en
absoluto* ("absolutely not," "not at all").

### Grammar points

#### Relative clauses — structural differences from English (Chapter 25)

Two structural rules distinguish Spanish from English: (1) a Spanish relative pronoun can **never**
be omitted (unlike English "the man we saw"), and (2) a Spanish preposition can never be "stranded"
at the end of a relative clause — it must sit immediately before its relative pronoun ("el hombre de
quien hablábamos," not a stranded equivalent).

#### Restrictive vs. non-restrictive clauses

A restrictive clause narrows the antecedent to a sub-group ("Los niños que estaban cansados tuvieron
que ir a la cama" — only the tired children went to bed); a non-restrictive clause (set off by commas/
pauses) does not narrow it ("Los niños, que estaban cansados, ... " — all the children were tired and
all went to bed). English "that" is restrictive-only, while "who"/"which" can be either.

#### The basic relative system and its refinements

*Que* is the default for "that." For "who"/"whom" as direct object, *que* is technically acceptable
but it is safer to use *a* + an *el que*-series form ("Esta es la chica a la que detuvieron"); if
"whom" follows a preposition, use *quien(es)* or an *el que*-series form. For "which" after a
preposition, use an *el que*-series form, or the neuter *lo que* when the antecedent is genderless
(*algo, nada*, or an adjective preceded by *lo*). *Cuando* cannot introduce a **restrictive** relative
clause (unlike English "when") — instead use *en que* or colloquial bare *que* ("El mes que más llueve
aquí es octubre"); *cuando* is fine in non-restrictive clauses. *Donde* covers "where" in both clause
types, optionally preceded by a preposition for destination/origin (*a* + *donde* > *adonde*). "Why"
uses *por la que* (with antecedent *la razón*). "Whose" is *cuyo*, agreeing with the item possessed
(not the possessor) and usable with both human and non-human antecedents — informal speech often
replaces it with *que* + possessive adjective instead ("el chico que su padre es un ministro").

#### El que / el cual as interchangeable alternatives, with a regional skew

*El que* and *el cual* are grammatically near-identical, but *el cual* is markedly less common in
Peninsular speech than in large parts of Latin America, where the reverse holds. *Que* directly after
a preposition (especially *en*, less so *a/con/de*) is also possible but considered the less safe
choice in the Peninsula — except after time-period nouns (*vez, día, año*), where *en que* or bare
*que* is standard ("La primera vez que la vi fue el verano pasado").

#### Cleft-sentence focus constructions

Spanish achieves the effect of English "it was John who did it" by restructuring as "the one who did
it was John" (*El que lo hizo fue Juan*), frequently inverted to *Fue Juan el que lo hizo* — *quien*
works identically in this slot. *El que*-series pronouns can refer to humans or non-humans; *quien*
only to persons; *lo que* when the focused item was previously unidentified. In Peninsular Spanish, if
the focused item follows a preposition (including personal *a*), that preposition must be repeated
before the relative pronoun ("Fue con la secretaria con la que hablé"). The relative adverbs *cuando,
donde, como* work the same way for time/place/manner focus ("Fue en septiembre cuando nos casamos").
(For a Latin American structural alternative to this whole pattern — including a construction that
*omits* the relative word/preposition entirely — see `019_chunk3c.md`'s Chapter 30 coverage.)

#### Cuanto, and relative clauses with an infinitive

*Cuanto* as an adjective agrees in number/gender and is equivalent to *todo el que*/*todos los que*
("Tomaremos cuantas medidas sean necesarias"); as an invariable neuter pronoun it means "everything
(that)" ("Hice cuanto pude"). Separately, when the antecedent is an indefinite/negative pronoun like
*algo*/*nada*, a relative clause can take an infinitive as its main verb ("No tengo nada con lo que
secarme las manos"); if the antecedent itself is dropped too, a written accent is required on the
relative word ("No tengo con qué lavarme el pelo"; "No encontramos dónde dormir").

#### Comparative constructions (Chapter 26)

Beyond the basic *más/menos...que* and *tan...como* patterns and the *-ísimo* suffix (both covered
elsewhere), this chapter adds: a full account of the irregular comparatives *mejor/peor/mayor/menor*,
including the crucial distinction that *mayor/menor* cover age and abstract degree/importance while
*más grande/más pequeño* cover physical size (both sets can overlap for size, but not interchangeably
for age or abstraction); noun comparisons (*más/menos* + noun + *que*, with *mucho* agreeing in
gender/number: "Comió muchas más patatas que tú"); verb comparisons (verb + *más/menos que*, or verb +
invariable *tanto como* for equality); and the rule that *de* (not *que*) must link a comparative to a
following **number** or quantity-word ("No vengas más tarde de las ocho"), distinguishing *no más de*
("not more than") from *no más que* ("only"). When the comparison precedes a subordinate clause, the
link word is *del/de la/de los/de las que* (agreeing with a specific noun) or *de lo que* (when the
whole main clause, not a specific noun, is the standard of comparison) — e.g. "Pedro es más listo de
lo que creíamos." The English "the more/less...the more/less" construction is *cuanto más/menos...
(tanto) más/menos...*, with *tanto* now usually dropped from the second clause, and *cuanto* often
replaced by *mientras* in speech.

#### Superlative constructions

Adjectival superlatives use definite article/possessive + noun + comparative adjective, with *de*
translating a following "in" ("la montaña más alta de España"); unlike French, Spanish does not repeat
the article before the comparative. The article is dropped with copular verbs of becoming/seeming
("Es en verano cuando el agua del mar está más caliente"). Adverbial superlatives drop the article
entirely unless the adverb is itself qualified by a relative clause or adjective, in which case *lo* is
required ("Ven lo más pronto posible"); for "works hardest"/"eats the most," plain *más* is used rather
than a superlative adverb form.

#### Questions and exclamations (Chapter 27) — not covered in prior established files

Spanish never uses "do"-support in questions; instead, subject-verb order inverts and inverted question
marks bracket the sentence. A preposition can never be stranded at the end of a question (must precede
the interrogative word). Subject-verb inversion is standard in both direct and indirect questions
*unless* the interrogative word itself is the subject or modifies it. *Qué* as a pronoun refers only to
things, never persons; as an adjective, the restriction lifts. *Cuál/cuáles* is used instead of *qué*
before *ser* + abstract noun (except for bare definitions): "¿Cuál es la razón...?" vs. "¿Qué es el
estoicismo?" *Quién/quiénes* refers only to persons and takes personal *a* as direct object. English
"how" + adjective/adverb has no single Spanish equivalent and is handled by varied strategies: *¿cuánto
mides?* ("how tall"), *¿cómo es tu casa de grande?*, *¿cuántos años tienes?* ("how old"), *¿con qué
frecuencia?* ("how frequently"), *¿a qué velocidad?* ("how fast"). Spanish has no direct equivalent of
English question tags ("isn't it?"); *verdad* and *no* approximate the function, with LA additionally
using *¿no es cierto?*/*¿no es verdad?*. Exclamations always carry inverted exclamation marks and
accented exclamatory words. *Qué* + noun = "what (a)" (no indefinite article, unlike English), with a
following adjective preceded by *más* or *tan*; but where a Spanish noun corresponds to an English
adjective, *qué* instead translates "so" ("¡Qué calor hace!" — "It's so hot!"). *Cómo* (verb-only) and
*cuánto* (intensity/quantity) round out the exclamatory-word set; archaic *cuán* survives in literature.

#### Word order (Chapter 28) — not covered in prior established files

Spanish word order is far more flexible than English's fixed subject-verb-object pattern, governed
primarily by information structure rather than grammatical rule. The central principle: **new or
important information tends toward the end of the sentence.** Consequences drawn out in the chapter:
(1) the subject typically follows the verb when it supplies the answer to a question ("¿Quién fue a la
cena?" → "Fueron Ana, Lole y Miguel"); (2) an indefinite subject (preceded by an indefinite article,
numeral, or indefinite adjective) tends to follow the verb, since it's usually the newest information
("Llegaron un montón de policías"); (3) the subject tends to follow the verb when a direct object
pronoun is already present, since the pronoun marks "old" information and the subject becomes the new
focus ("¿Qué cuadro más bonito! —Lo compró Carlos"). Conversely, **topic-announcing** — placing the
item under discussion at the very start, regardless of its role — is a distinct and very common
Spanish strategy ("Las cañerías las instalo mañana" — "As for the pipes, I'll install them tomorrow"),
as is fronting a phrase purely for **emphatic stress** ("Esos zapatos me quiero poner"). Specific
constructions: a bare noun subject (no article) must follow the verb ("Entra luz por la persiana");
reflexive-passive subjects typically follow the verb unless they're the topic; long subject phrases
(especially ones containing a relative clause) and the subjects of relative clauses themselves tend to
follow the verb to avoid stranding the verb at the clause's end. Adverbs: qualifying adverbs usually sit
right after the verb but can shift for emphasis; sentence-scope adverbs (modifying the whole clause,
not just the verb) go at the beginning ("Afortunadamente dejaron de interesarse..."); and — unlike
English — a Spanish adverb can never be inserted between the auxiliary *haber* and a following past
participle. Subject-verb inversion is obligatory in all direct questions with an explicit subject
(except yes/no questions), and in several indirect-question contexts (after *dónde* with *vivir*/
*estar*; after *cuándo* with verbs of arrival/departure; after *qué* functioning as direct object). With
an infinitive/gerund construction, the post-verbal subject can sit either between the finite verb and
the infinitive/gerund, or after it — the latter placement throws extra focus onto the subject itself.

### Grammar points

#### Word formation overview (Chapter 29)

The book frames word formation as suffixation, prefixation, and compounding, with the explicit
pedagogical goal of letting learners decode unfamiliar words by their parts. Adding a suffix often
triggers base-word modification: final-vowel deletion/alteration (*sereno* → *serenidad*), general
spelling-rule changes (*pedazo* → *pedacito*), and — when the suffix pulls stress away from a
previously-stressed *ie*/*ue* diphthong — reversion to plain *e*/*o* (*caliente* → *calentito*,
*puerta* → *portezuela*).

#### Affective suffixes — diminutives, augmentatives, pejoratives (Ch. 29.1.6) — flagship finding for slang-synthesis purposes

This is explicitly framed by the source itself as "a rich and distinctive feature of contemporary
Spanish, with considerable regional variations... throughout Latin America," and the book cautions
learners to use these only once genuinely experienced, since appropriateness is highly register- and
context-dependent. Structurally: diminutive forms (*-ito, -illo, -uelo* and their *-c-/-ec-/-ez-*-
infixed allomorphs, chosen by syllable count and final letter of the base) can attach not just to
nouns but to adjectives, adverbs, adverbial phrases, and even past participles. The most productive
and semantically broadest, *-ito*, ranges from literal smallness to simple affection/friendliness with
no smallness implied at all (*abuela* → *abuelita*, *cerveza* → *una cervecita*), and attaches
productively to first names (*Ana* → *Anita*). *-illo* is comparatively unemotional ("just smaller"),
frequently reshapes the base word's meaning entirely (*ventana* "window" → *ventanilla* "vehicle/
ticket-office window"), and is noted as especially characteristic of Andalusian speech. *-uelo* and
several minor regional diminutives (*-ín* Asturian, *-ino/a* Extremaduran, *-iño/a* Galician, *-ico*
archaic/sarcastic in Spain but the ordinary diminutive in Central America/Colombia) carry a more
reliably pejorative or mocking edge. Augmentatives (*-ón/-ona, -azo/-aza, -ote/-ota*) denote large
size, frequently with built-in connotations of clumsiness, ugliness, or contempt — and, confusingly for
learners, some individual forms invert the expected size direction entirely (*rata* "rat" → *ratón*
"mouse"; *isla* "island" → *islote* "islet"). A closed set of suffixes (*-aco, -acho, -ajo, -astro,
-uco, -ucho*) is purely pejorative with no size dimension at all. Suffixes can stack (*chico* →
*chiquitito*), and in Latin America a single diminutive suffix is sometimes doubled for emphasis
(*solitita*, "all alone"). **Slang-synthesis relevance**: this entire system is a ready-made,
independently-attested register-shifting morphology — exactly the kind of productive affix inventory a
derived conlang's slang layer could borrow or parallel, since the semantic range (affection → mockery →
outright contempt, layered onto size/intensity) already spans the territory slang vocabulary usually
targets.

#### Prefixes (Ch. 29.2)

Prefixes are noted as less frequent than suffixes in Spanish and easier for English-speaking learners
since many prefixes (*anti-, super-, contra-, trans-, post-*) are near-identical in form and function
across both languages, and the source notes their increasing productivity specifically in modern
Spanish-language media. *De(s)-* is identified as "probably the most common prefix in Spanish,"
covering both true antonym-formation (*animar* → *desanimar*) and "removal" verbs with no existing
opposite (*nata* "cream" → *desnatar* "to skim"). *Re-* (repetition) has a distinct colloquial
intensifying use, strengthening further to *rete-* and *requete-*, particularly in Latin America.

#### Compounding (Ch. 29.3)

Noun-adjective juxtaposition (English-style, adjective+noun) is relatively rare in Spanish and
reversed in word order when it does occur natively (*página web*, but *videojuego* retains its own
internal order). More typically Spanish links nouns via a preposition — usually *de*, sometimes *para*
to mark intended use (*jugo de piña*; *jarra para cerveza*). True compound nouns combine varied parts
of speech and take their gender from a rule based on their first element (masculine if beginning with
a verb or preposition, generally singular even if the second element is semantically plural, e.g. *el
abrelatas* "can opener"). Compound adjectives built on body-part + adjective/participle form a small
closed productive pattern (*boquiabierto* "open-mouthed," *pelirrojo* "red-haired," *paticorto*
"short-legged").

#### Chapter 30: Latin American vs. Peninsular Spanish — the book's dedicated dialectal-comparison chapter

This chapter explicitly "collates and expands" the [LA]/[SP] tags used piecemeal throughout the rest
of the book into one unified overview, and is the single highest-value chapter in this source for
slang-synthesis purposes.

**Pronunciation (30.1).** *Seseo* — the merger of /θ/ ("th") into /s/ for *z* and *c* before *i/e* — is
near-universal across Latin America and also standard in Andalusia and the Canaries, producing
homophone pairs like *rozado*/*rosado*. *Yeísmo* — pronouncing *ll* like English "y" rather than the
conservative central/northern-Peninsular "lli" — is likewise near-universal outside Paraguay/parts of
the Andes, producing pairs like *pollo*/*poyo*. In the River Plate area specifically, both *ll* and *y*
go further, merging toward English "sh"/"zh" — termed *žeísmo* or *rehilamiento*. *J* (and *g* before
*i/e*), pronounced like Scottish "ch" (loch) in most of the Peninsula, is pronounced like English "h"
in much of the Caribbean basin and the Pacific coast of South America (*paja* "straw" → *paha*).

**Forms of address (30.2).** *Vosotros* and its associated 2nd-person-plural verb forms are entirely
absent from Latin America (and from the Canaries/much of southern Spain) — replaced by *ustedes* with
3rd-person-plural verb agreement. Separately, *voseo* (*vos* as the familiar 2nd-person-singular
subject pronoun, replacing *tú*) is standard across the River Plate area and Central America, absent
from Mexico (except the far south)/Caribbean/most of Peru, and variable elsewhere. The chapter gives
a full *vos* verb-paradigm table for the mainstream River Plate/Central American pattern: present
indicative is derived from the *vosotros* form by dropping the unstressed *-i-* for *-ar/-er* verbs
(*cantáis* → *cantás*; *vos sos* "you are"), while *-ir* verbs are identical to *vosotros* in this
tense (*vos vivís*); a less-widespread *vos* present subjunctive follows the same *-i-*-deletion rule
(*cantéis* → *cantés*); a still-rarer *vos* future tense exists (*vos cantarés*). A geographically
distinct minor pattern (parts of Venezuela, northern Chile, Bolivia) instead keeps the full *vosotros*
form for *vos* (*vos cantáis*) with the final *-s* often unpronounced, and separately shifts *-éis* to
*-ís* (*vos comís* rather than *vos coméis*). Object pronouns/possessives paired with *vos* are
identical to *tú*'s (*te, tu, tuyo*), but *vos* itself (not *ti*) is used after a preposition
("Estaba pensando en vos").

**Le and lo (30.3).** Central/northern Peninsular speakers often use *le* as the masculine direct-
object pronoun for a man; almost all Latin American speakers (except Ecuador/Paraguay) use *lo*
instead, as do Andalusia and the Canaries.

**Tense usage (30.4).** Peninsular Spanish enforces a strict preterite/imperfect-main-clause →
imperfect-subjunctive-subordinate-clause sequencing after verbs of influence; many Latin American
varieties instead allow the present subjunctive even when the matrix action is past ("Les pidió que
entren" vs. Peninsular "Les pidió que entraran"). Separately, much of Latin America (possibly
excepting the Andes) uses the preterite in contexts where Peninsular speakers would use the perfect —
comparable to the British/American English "Have you done it?" vs. "Did you do it?" contrast
("Todavía no llamaron" [LA] vs. "Todavía no han llamado" [Peninsular]).

**Focus constructions — a second, LA-specific layer beyond `019_chunk3b.md`'s Ch. 25/27 coverage
(30.5).** In Peninsular cleft-sentence focus constructions, a preceding preposition must be duplicated
before the relative pronoun ("Fue con María con la que hablé"). Many Latin American varieties instead
replace the whole *el que/quien/cuando/donde/como* apparatus with bare *que* and drop the preposition
duplication entirely when the focused item is a place, time, or governed by a preposition ("Fue en la
fiesta que la vi" vs. Peninsular "Fue en la fiesta donde la vi"). A more geographically restricted
variant — the Caribbean basin, Colombia, Ecuador — goes further still with an "**intensive ser**"
construction that drops the relative word (and any preposition) entirely, leaving only the focused
item after *ser*: "Lo hizo fue Pedro" (cf. Peninsular "Quien lo hizo fue Pedro"), "Hablé fue con María"
(cf. "Con la que hablé fue con María"). This is a structurally striking construction — the relative
clause's content and the *ser*-focus content essentially fuse into a single verb phrase with no overt
linking element at all.

**Vocabulary (30.6).** The chapter gives (a) a 40-item table of general/near-general Latin American
vocabulary with Peninsular equivalents (reproduced in the Vocabulary tables above); (b) a shorter list
of gender- and stress-alternating items between the two dialect areas (*bebe/bebé*, *(el) bombillo/
(la) bombilla*, *(el) cerillo/(la) cerilla*, *chofer/chófer*, *(el) llamado/(la) llamada*, *(el)
radio/(la) radio*, *(el) sartén/(la) sartén*, *video/vídeo*); and (c) a "false friends across
dialects" section explicitly flagged by the source as a genuine source of cross-dialect
misunderstanding — nine items (*mero, puro, hasta, coger, demasiado, de repente, despacio, no
más/nomás, recién*) whose core meaning shifts, sometimes sharply, between Peninsular and (regionally
varying) Latin American usage. The *coger* entry is explicitly flagged by the source itself as taboo
in most of Latin America (meaning "to screw"/"to fuck" there, vs. neutral "to take/catch" in Spain),
with *tomar*/*agarrar* recommended as safe substitutes — this is as close to an outright register-
collision gotcha as the book gets, and the single most load-bearing item in this whole chapter for
slang-synthesis purposes, since it demonstrates a real historical case of regional semantic drift
landing squarely in taboo territory.

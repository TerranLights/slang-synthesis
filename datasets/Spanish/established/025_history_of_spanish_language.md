# PART 1 — PENNY: *A History of the Spanish Language* (systematic grammar-history)

## Chapter 1 — Introduction

**[PENNY]** Source: Penny, ch.1, DJVU pp.21–55 (printed pp.1–35).

### Grammar/history points

**Latin as a register-spectrum, not two codes.** Penny explicitly rejects the "Vulgar Latin vs Classical Latin as two separate codes" model. He models "Latin" as a single spectrum of styles/registers from codified literary Latin at one end to "the raciest slang" at the other, with smooth gradation in between — Classical Latin is simply the written-register extreme of the same continuum that produced the Romance vernaculars (p.5). This is explicitly relevant groundwork for any register/slang-synthesis model: Penny's own working definition of "Vulgar Latin" (citing Herman 2000) is "the set of all those innovations and trends that turned up in the usage, particularly but not exclusively spoken, of the Latin-speaking population who were little or not at all influenced by school education and by literary models" (p.5) — i.e. defined by social/educational exclusion from the standard, not by a fixed chronological stage.

**No "Vulgar Latin texts" possible.** A corollary Penny draws (p.6): because all surviving written texts are by definition composed by the literate, there can be no such thing as a text written natively "in" Vulgar Latin — only texts that incidentally leak spoken-register features through the cracks of imperfect training in the standard (misspellings, grammarians' explicit condemnations, informal letters, technical/agricultural writing, Christian proselytizing prose, graffiti, glosses, the *Appendix Probi*).

**The *Appendix Probi* as an early normative word-list.** A 6th/7th-century list of 227 prescribed-vs-condemned word pairs (e.g. `AURICULA NON ORICLA`, `SPECULUM NON SPECLUM`) — explicitly a corrective list aimed at stamping out forms already current in speech, and hence (by the logic above) positive evidence for the existence of those condemned spoken forms (p.6-7). Methodologically important for this project: a prescriptive/corrective source is read as evidence for the *opposite* of what it prescribes.

**Three characteristics of the Latin of Spain, +1 paradox.** Penny catalogues the Latin of Roman-era Spain as showing **archaism** (retaining pre-Classical features lost elsewhere, e.g. the vocabulary cluster below), **conservatism** (retaining Classical-but-elsewhere-superseded forms, correlating with Spain's peripheral/remote position relative to the trend-setting center of the Empire), and **(Osco-Umbrian) dialectalism** (features attributable to the non-standard Italian dialect background of the earliest Roman colonists in Spain, e.g. certain sound changes and the tonic-vowel treatment of *nudo*, *octubre*, *cierzo*) — but *also*, paradoxically, genuine **innovations** restricted to the Peninsula, including the total merger of the Latin 2nd and 3rd verb conjugations (so that Sp. *deber*, *vender* show an identical infinitive pattern where other Romance varieties, e.g. Fr. *devoir* vs *rendre*, keep them apart) (pp.11-14).

**Koineization as the actual historical mechanism, not linear descent.** Penny's own framing device, previewed here and made explicit in ch.6: Spanish history is not one smooth line of descent but a sequence of discrete **dialect-contact/koineization events** (10th-c. Burgos, 12th-c. Toledo, 13th-c. Seville, 16th-c. Madrid, 16th/17th-c. Mexico City and Lima), each producing a new leveled variety that tends to prefer the *linguistically simpler* of the competing variants available in the contact pool (p.13, cross-referenced fully at 6.1). This is a reusable mechanic: every dialect-contact koineization event is itself a plausible real-world model for slang-synthesis register formation.

**Visigothic structural input was minimal — limited to two suffixes.** Despite 300 years of Visigothic rule, the Germanic superstrate's *grammatical* effect on Hispano-Romance was small: (a) a new noun-declension type (nominative -*ane*, oblique -*ANE*, e.g. OSp. *Froilán*/*Fruela*, both descending from the same Germanic personal name's nom. vs oblique forms — Sp. *guardia* 'guard' < *WARDJA* vs *guardián* 'guardian' < *WARDJANE* is the one common-noun pair that preserves both halves of this paradigm); (b) the suffix *-engo* (< Gmc. *-ING*) for deriving adjectives from nouns, low-productivity, e.g. *abadengo* 'belonging to an abbey', *realengo* 'belonging to the Crown', *abolengo* 'ancestry' (pp.14-15).

**Castilian's own origin as the *most* peripheral/non-standard 8th-century Hispano-Romance dialect.** Penny's key explanatory move for "why does Castilian look so different from the rest of Romance": Cantabria/northern Burgos was *so* remote from both Roman and Visigothic standardizing pressure that its 8th-century Romance was unusually divergent — and it was precisely this "abnormal" peripheral variety that the Reconquest then carried south and promoted to national-standard status, rather than a more conservative/central variety (pp.17-18). This is a direct mechanism for why a historically marginal/non-prestige dialect can become a later standard — relevant analogy for slang-to-standard promotion dynamics.

**Alfonso X (r.1252–84) as the deliberate architect of written Castilian standardization**, via (a) using Castilian for an enormous scholarly/legal/historiographical output, lending it prestige by association, (b) express royal concern for "correctness" in that output, (c) adopting Castilian as the sole administrative language, displacing Latin, partly *because* Castilian (unlike Latin, Arabic or Hebrew) was religiously neutral (pp.20-21).

**Two competing early-modern spoken norms: Toledo vs Seville.** Before Madrid's rise, Toledo's norm (later superseded by Madrid in the 1560s) was rivaled by a distinct Sevillian norm characterized by *seseo*, *yeísmo*, retention of /h/ < Lat. F-, weakening of syllable-final /s/, and (morphosyntactically) *loísmo* (etymological *lo*/*le* distinction) vs central *leísmo* (p.21). In Spain the Madrid norm won decisively by the Golden Age, but overseas the two norms remained in much more even competition — directly relevant to understanding the dialect-split patterns of American Spanish.

## Chapter 2 — Phonology

**[PENNY]** Source: Penny, ch.2, DJVU pp.56–130 (printed pp.36–110).

### Grammar points

**Transmission types: popular / learned / semi-learned / doublets (2.2).** This four-way distinction is foundational for every vocabulary table in this project's Spanish dataset and should be treated as a standing column-filling rule, not just a historical curiosity:
- **Popular** words: continuous oral transmission from spoken Latin, generation to generation, undergoing every regular phonological/morphological change (e.g. FABULARE > *hablar*, with loss of pretonic vowel and the F->h change).
- **Learned** words: borrowed from written Latin (Classical or Medieval) at any later date, undergoing only the minimal morphological tweak needed to fit Spanish patterns, *not* the full battery of sound changes (e.g. *fábula* keeps post-tonic /u/ and /f/, unlike popular *habla*). Learned words correlate with (but are not defined by) elevated/literary register.
- **Semi-learned**: orally inherited (like popular words) but remodeled under the influence of Latin as read aloud in church/law-courts; show *some but not all* of the regular changes (e.g. *cruz* < CRUCE shows popular treatment of C+e/i but retains learned tonic /u/ instead of expected popular /o/).
- **Doublets**: the same Latin etymon transmitted twice by different routes, almost always with semantic differentiation — popular form drifts in meaning, learned/semi-learned form keeps something closer to the Latin sense. E.g. *reja* 'ploughshare' (popular) vs *regla* 'rule, ruler' (semi-learned) < REGULA; *fábula* 'fable' (learned) vs *habla* 'speech' (popular) < FABULA; *artejo* 'finger-joint' (popular) vs *artículo* 'article' (learned) < ARTICULU; *delgado* 'slender' (popular) vs *delicado* 'delicate' (learned), both < DELICATU.

**Accent position is phonologically determined in Latin, phonemic in Spanish.** Latin stress placement followed a mechanical rule keyed to penultimate-syllable weight (never to meaning); by contrast Modern Spanish accent is contrastive/phonemic, distinguishing e.g. *continuo* 'continual' / *continúo* '(I) continue' / *continuó* '(he) continued' (p.41). Two systematic classes of accent-*shift* away from the predicted Latin position are identified: (a) consonant+/j/ groups that were originally syllable-initial but came to be treated as ordinary clusters (TENEBRAE, INTEGRU > *tinieblas*, *entero*); (b) antepenultimate stress in hiatus with the penultimate, where the glide absorption pulls the accent onto the more open vowel (MULIERE, FILIOLU, PUTEOLU, TEGULA > *mujer*, *hijuelo*, *pozuelo*, *tejuela*).

**The accent-type shift from pitch-accent to stress-accent** is identified as the single biggest driver of subsequent Spanish phonological history: concentration of articulatory energy on the tonic syllable (at the expense of atonic syllables) explains both the differential survival/merger of vowels by position (tonic > initial > final > intertonic, in decreasing order of preservation) and the near-total destruction of vowel hiatus in popular words (p.42-43; the only popular-word hiatus survivors are *día*, *mía*, *vía*).

**The Vulgar Latin 7-vowel system and its further Hispano-Romance evolution** is the backbone mechanism of the whole vowel chapter: Classical Latin's 10-phoneme system (5 qualities × length) collapses, via loss of the length distinction and merger of height-3/height-4 pairs, into a 7-vowel tonic system (/i e ɛ a ɔ o u/) — the so-called "Vulgar Latin vowel system," independently attested via graffiti misspellings (`VECES`/`MENUS` for `VICES`/`MINUS`) and the *Appendix Probi*'s corrective entries (`TURMA` not `TORMA`, etc.) (pp.46-47).

**Diphthongization of the low-mid vowels /ɛ/ and /ɔ/ to /je/ and /we/** is presented as a *therapeutic/compensatory* response to a prior confusability crisis: late-Latin lengthening of all tonic vowels (itself possibly due to Germanic-speaker influence) raised most vowels, bringing /ɔ/ 'bear' (URSUS) uncomfortably close to /o/ 'bone' (OSSU) — diphthongization of the low-mid pair restored the needed contrast (pp.51-52). This "crisis and compensatory restructuring" pattern is a reusable mechanic for slang-synthesis sound-change modeling.

**Metaphony (vowel-raising assimilation to a following high element)** is treated at length with a five-environment taxonomy based on how long the conditioning glide survived before being absorbed by a neighboring consonant — environment 1 (glide absorbed latest) affects 4 of 5 susceptible vowels; environment 5 (glide absorbed earliest, next to /n/ or /ɲ/) affects only one. This chronological-survival account (pp.48-50) is Penny's explicit candidate explanation for why metaphony's effects look so irregular at first glance — a textbook case of "apparent irregularity resolves once you add a hidden chronological variable," reusable as a conlang-design principle.

**Lenition (2.5.3.2) as a 4-step unified process** governing the whole intervocalic-consonant system: (a) geminate > simple; (b) voiceless plosive > voiced; (c) voiced plosive > fricative; (d) /j/-type > /w/-type weakening — yielding the Old Spanish voiced-fricative series (/β/, /z/, /ʝ~dʐ/, /ʑ/) that didn't exist at all in Latin (table 2.18, p.85). This is the single most important consonant-system mechanism in the chapter and the direct ancestor of the b/v merger discussed below.

**The b/v merger (2.6.1).** Old Spanish genuinely distinguished /b/ (plosive, < Lat. B-) from /β/ (fricative, < Lat. V- and intervocalic -B-/-V-), verified via consistent *b*/*v* spelling correlating with rhyme practice; neutralization spreads from consonant clusters and post-nasal position (13th c.) to full intervocalic merger by the 15th c., completed for all speakers born after ~1550 (no poet born after that date avoids rhyming *b* with *v*) (pp.96-97). Direct evidence for dating a merger via literary rhyme-evidence — a reusable methodological template.

**The Old Spanish sibilant system and its three collapses (2.6.2).** Old Spanish had **seven** sibilant phonemes organized in three voiced/voiceless pairs (dento-alveolar affricate /ts~dz/, alveolar fricative /s~z/, prepalatal fricative /ʃ~ʒ/) plus /tʃ/. Three sequential changes collapse this to the modern 3-way (Castilian) or 2-way (Andalusian/American) system: (1) deaffrication (affricates > fricatives, by ~15th c.); (2) **devoicing** — complete loss of the voiced/voiceless contrast, spreading from northern Castile (possibly Basque-substrate-influenced) southward after Madrid becomes the capital in the 1560s, with Toledo and points south resisting longer (documented explicitly in a 1578 quote from fray Juan de Córdoba contrasting *Castilla la Vieja* "caçar/dezir" with Toledo "caçar/jugar/hazer"); (3) **locus dispersion** — to keep */θ/, /s/, /ʃ/* perceptually distinct once voicing could no longer help, /θ/ moves further forward (interdental) and /ʃ/ moves further back (velar /x/), driven by the sheer functional load of minimal triples like *caza/casa/caxa* (pp.98-101). This 3-stage sibilant collapse (deaffrication → devoicing → locus-dispersion) is probably the single most citable phonological "flagship mechanism" in the whole book for a sound-change-synthesis purpose.

**Andalusian/American sibilant development diverges after step (2) above**, taking a different path: merger of the alveolars with the dentals rather than of voiced-with-voiceless-by-locus, producing either **seseo** (merger result realized as dental [s̺], the norm in Seville/Córdoba/Canaries/all America) or **ceceo** (fronted result closer to [θ], coastal Andalusian cities Huelva/Cádiz/Málaga/Granada) — two medieval four-way contrasts (/ts dz s z/) collapsing to one phoneme /s̟/ (pp.101-102). The prepalatal series meanwhile retracts not to /x/ as in the standard but all the way to laryngeal /h/ — merging, in areas where it survives, with the /h/ descended from Latin F- (western Andalusian & rural American dialects) (p.103, table 2.27).

**Yeísmo (2.6.6)**: merger of palatal lateral /ʎ/ and mid-palatal /j/, non-lateral outcome — not attested in writing in Spain until the 18th c. but probably under way in late Old Spanish, since *all* Judeo-Spanish varieties (split off 1492) and most American varieties already show it; today the whole southern half of the Peninsula plus the capital lack /ʎ/. Five distinct phonetic realizations are catalogued by region: [ʝ]~[j] (most educated/urban speech), [ʒ] (parts of Andalusia/America), [dʒ] (Extremadura, Argentina, Uruguay), [tʃ] (urban River Plate), [ʃ] (some Buenos Aires speech) (pp.104-105).

**Weakening of syllable-final /s/ (2.6.7) as a five-grade intensity scale**, each grade geographically nested inside the next: (1) fricative [h]-like softening before /d/ (northern rural); (2) full aspiration [h] before consonant or pause (most of southern Spain + most American Spanish); (3) assimilation of the aspirate to the following consonant's features, sometimes devoicing it (Andalusia); (4) total loss before a pause (western Andalusia, much of America); (5) total elimination in *all* environments (eastern Andalusia) (pp.106-107). Grade 5 has a striking *compensatory phonologization* consequence: once final /-s/ is gone everywhere, its grammatical load (plural marking, 2nd-person marking) transfers to a **vowel-aperture contrast** that previously was mere sub-phonemic allophony — syllable-final vowels were always slightly more open than syllable-final (word-final) vowels, and in eastern Andalusian dialects this difference is now phonemicized, producing contrasts like *vienes* [ˈbjɛːnɛ] vs *viene* [ˈbjene], yielding (Penny argues) at least 8 contrastive vowel qualities, i.e. effectively a brand-new vowel system built entirely out of old final-/s/'s functional load (pp.107-108). This is an exceptionally clean, well-documented case of **phonemicization of redundant allophony following loss of a conditioning consonant** — a core sound-change mechanism directly reusable for conlang slang/phonology design.

**Full relative chronology of Spanish phonological change (2.7, pp.108-110)**: Penny gives an explicit 33-step ordered list from "loss of final -M and initial H-" through to the 17th-century locus-shift of the sibilants. Not reproduced item-by-item here (33 cross-referenced one-liners, each pointing back to its own fuller discussion already summarized above) but flagged as an extremely high-value single artifact for this project if a full phonological-derivation-order reference is ever needed — it is, in effect, Penny's own master sound-change ruleset in strict relative order.

## Chapter 3 — Morpho-syntax

**[PENNY]** Source: Penny, ch.3, DJVU pp.131–274 (printed pp.111–254). **Scope note:** this chapter is ~144 pages; per the dispatch's priority list, full detailed coverage was given to the pronoun system (including forms of address) and the verb's imperative/imperfect/future/conditional/preterite/non-finite-form history; noun/adjective/adverb/numeral morphology, "other word classes," and conditional-sentence syntax were not separately extracted this pass (flagged per the coverage-note above).

### Grammar points — general concepts & pronoun system

**Morphemic analysis as Penny's working method.** A word like *pequeños* is analyzed as root *peque-* + diminutive/affective *-it-* + masc. *-o-* + plural *-s*; *cantaban* as root *cant-* + conjugation-marker *-a-* + tense/aspect/mood *-ba-* + person/number *-n* (p.111). Standard but worth recording as the book's explicit baseline morpheme-segmentation convention.

**Personal pronouns: the tonic/atonic split is itself the key historical event (3.5.1).** Latin personal pronouns, except as verb-subject or preposition-object, lost their stress and became clitics — this single development is what produces the Spanish tonic-vs-atonic pronoun paradigm (table 3.3, p.133). Several sub-mechanisms: (a) masc. plural *ellos* is the one subject-form that descends from an *accusative* (ILLOS) rather than nominative, by the same "plural -s marking" analogy pressure seen in nouns; (b) *vosotros*/*nosotros* are late-Old-Spanish analogical expansions, *vos+otros* originally a genuinely contrastive phrase (cf. Fr. *vous autres*) that became the unmarked plain plural once *vos* itself narrowed to a singular-ish form (p.138, directly tied to the forms-of-address history below); (c) the post-prepositional 1st/2nd singular forms (*mí*, *ti*) descend from the Latin *dative*, not accusative/ablative as expected — an early, functionally unexplained innovation (p.134); (d) the double-marked preposition-pronoun fusions *conmigo*/*contigo*/*consigo* result from Latin CUM being repeated both before and after the pronoun (CUM MECUM etc.) in spoken Latin, itself a repair of CUM's anomalous Latin habit of *following* certain pronouns (pp.134-135).

**Leísmo / laísmo / loísmo (3.5.1, directly relevant to dialectal/register tagging).** *Le* begins encroaching on *lo*'s original masculine-personal-direct-object territory already in the *Cantar de mio Cid* (12th c.) — this is **leísmo**. Northern Peninsular dialects extend *le* further, to masculine *countable* non-personal direct objects too (but never to mass/non-count referents) — e.g. *este vaso no hay que romperle*. The mirror-image non-standard pattern, **laísmo**, extends *la* into feminine *indirect*-object territory (*la di el papel a la madre*) and is interpreted by Penny as a further step toward a pronoun system organized purely by *gender* rather than *case*. **Loísmo** is simply the retention of the older etymological *lo* for masculine direct objects (the "unmarked"/conservative option, historically associated with the Sevillian norm against the central-Castilian leísta norm — cross-reference ch.1's Toledo-vs-Seville material). *Les* for *los* (plural leísmo) and plural laísmo are both markedly rarer than their singular counterparts (pp.135-136).

**FORMS OF ADDRESS — the full tú/vos/usted history (3.5.1.1, pp.137-139).** This is one of the highest-value sections in the whole book for this project. Reconstructed stage-by-stage:
1. **Classical/early Latin**: TU for any single addressee regardless of status; VOS for plural addressees. Later imperial Latin extends VOS to *singular deferential* address (beginning, per tradition, with addressing the Emperor), giving VOS a dual singular-deferential/plural value — this system (tú non-deferential singular / vos deferential-singular-and-plural) persists into **early Old Spanish** and is directly attested in the *Cantar de mio Cid*: the king and the Cid himself are addressed as *vos*; the Cid uses *vos* to his wife Jimena and most relatives but *tú* to his young male kinsmen; the villainous young Infantes de Carrión are always *tú*'d (status/age-graded, not simply intimacy-graded).
2. **Later Old Spanish**: *vos* widens in reference until it loses most of its deferential force and becomes close in value to informal *tú* — this creates a **gap at the top** of the deference scale, filled from the 15th c. by new honorific constructions built on abstract nouns of rank (*merced* 'grace', *señoría* 'lordship', etc.); *vuestra merced* (not the briefly-tried *tu merced*) wins out, with a new plural *vuestras mercedes*. *Vos* (now purely non-deferential) regularly gets reinforced to **vosotros** in this same period (14th–15th c.), and *nos* is reinforced to *nosotros* by the same analogical pattern shortly after.
3. **Early Golden Age system**: singular *tú* ~ *os* (deferential: *vuestra merced*) / plural *vosotros* (deferential: *vuestras mercedes*).
4. **Golden Age + 18th c.**: *tú* vs *vos* competition for ordinary singular address is resolved **in favor of tú** throughout the Peninsula and in the parts of America in closest cultural contact with Spain (Peru, Bolivia, Mexico — plus the Caribbean and most of Venezuela, added later in the text). Simultaneously, *vuestra merced* undergoes a documented chain of informal contractions — *vuesarced*, *voacé*, *vucé*, *vuced*, *usted* — before stabilizing as **usted**; *vuestras mercedes* > **ustedes**.
5. **Modern standard**: singular tú (non-deferential) / usted (deferential); plural vosotros (non-deferential) / ustedes (deferential).
6. **Modern dialectal divergences**: (a) **western Andalusia and all of America** collapse the plural deferential/non-deferential distinction in favor of the *originally* deferential *ustedes*, which thus does double duty as the plain plural — displacing *vosotros* entirely in these areas; (b) the **singular** tú-vs-vos competition is resolved *differently* across America: tú wins in Mexico/Peru/Bolivia/Caribbean/most of Venezuela; **vos wins** in the areas culturally most distant from Spain — Argentina, Uruguay, Paraguay, the Central American states; a genuine mixed/competing situation persists in Chile, Ecuador, Colombia. Whichever subject pronoun is used (tú or vos), the associated object clitic is always *te* and the possessive always *tu/tuyo* — American *voseo* never carries its own dedicated object/possessive paradigm.
7. **The grammatical-agreement "anomaly" this history produces** (explicitly flagged by Penny, and independently by Correas in 1625 — see Pountain Text 18 below): *usted* (grammatically 3rd person, from a feminine noun *merced*) and *vos* (grammatically 2nd person *plural*) both now refer to a single 2nd-person addressee, producing person/number/gender mismatches between pronoun-form and verbal agreement that persist as a structural quirk of the modern system — and which, in voseo dialects, has left a historically *plural* verb ending (e.g. *hablás* < OSp. *habládes*/*habláis*) now functioning as an exclusively *singular* form.

**Possessives (3.5.2).** Spanish splits each Latin possessive into a *tonic, pre-noun-only, no-final-vowel, gender-neutralized* set (*mi(s)*, *tu(s)*, *su(s)*) vs a *tonic, post-noun-or-pronominal, gender-marked* set (*mío/mía*, *tuyo/tuya*, *suyo/suya*) — a split completed in late Old Spanish, with the reinforced *-uyo/-uya* forms arising first in the feminine (by analogy with the interrogative *¿cúyo?* 'whose?') and later leveled into the masculine too (14th c.) (pp.139-142). The chronic **ambiguity of *su(s)/suyo(s)*** (it serves 3rd-person singular AND plural possessor, reflexive AND non-reflexive, AND — after *vuestra merced* — 2nd-person-deferential) is identified as the direct motivator for the modern (especially American) tendency to disambiguate with *de él/de ella/de Vd.* genitive phrases, sometimes to the point of fully displacing the possessive altogether (*su casa de él* > *la casa de él*) (p.142).

**Demonstratives and articles (3.5.3).** Spanish's three-way *este/ese/aquel* system is a **restored** three-place system, not a simple continuation of Latin's: the original Latin three-way HIC/ISTE/ILLE collapses to two places in early Romance (ISTE takes over HIC's 1st-person role after HIC's phonological erosion), and the third slot is then **re-created** in conservative areas like Spain by promoting the emphatic/identity marker IPSE into 2nd-person-demonstrative service — this is the direct source of *ese* (pp.143-145). *Aquel* comes from a deictic-reinforced ILLE (*ACCU ILLE*) needed to disambiguate ILLE's overloaded roles (demonstrative + anaphoric + personal pronoun + incipient definite article, see below). *Mismo* descends from a haplologized *(MEDI)PSISSIMUS*, competing in Old Spanish with *meísmo*; *mismo* wins out from the 17th c.
The **definite article** itself is a late-Latin innovation, built from ILLE (the Hispanic/most-of-Romance choice) competing with IPSE (chosen instead in Sardinia, the Balearics, Costa Brava) — both driven by the same functional need (deictic reference to an already-established referent) that elsewhere drove IPSE's promotion to 2nd-person demonstrative (p.145).

### Grammar points — the verb

**The imperative (3.7.8.2, pp.196-197).** Singular endings collapse to just two via merger of final -A, -E, -I in /e/. A systematic *resistance-to-apocope* pattern: only the most frequent verbs whose root ends in an ungrouped dental/alveolar keep the apocopated (no-final-e) imperative under analogical pressure from the majority pattern — modern *pon, ten, ven, sal, haz* are the survivors of a once-larger apocope class. *Haz* (IR. FAC) is **not** phonologically regular — its root was remodeled on the regular-verb pattern. *Ve* (go!, replacing lost Ī) and *sé* (be!, replacing lost ES) both show *suppletion from a different verb's imperative* (VADE for IRE; SEDE for ESSE) — this suppletion mechanism recurs identically in the present indicative/subjunctive of the same two verbs. Medieval/Golden-Age imperatives regularly metathesized their final -d with a following clitic (*dadnos* > *dandos*, *tomadle* > *tomalde*) — a clitic-adjacent-metathesis mechanism distinct from, but structurally parallel to, other Spanish clitic-attachment effects.

**The imperfect (3.7.8.3, pp.197-203).** *-ar* verbs keep the Latin -ABAM intervocalic -b- regularly (*cantaba*); *-er/-ir* verbs **lose** the same -b- for unclear reasons, plausibly triggered by dissimilation in a cluster of extremely frequent verbs whose root itself ended in /b/ or /β/ (HABEBAM, VIVEBAM, *SUBIBAM — all would have produced an awkward /β...β/ sequence). Old Spanish shows a centuries-long **-ía vs -íe vs -ié** three-way competition for -er/-ir imperfects (Malkiel's hypothesis: the *-ié* forms, especially 1st/2nd plural *vendiemos/vendiedes*, were borrowed wholesale from the *preterite* paradigm) — not resolved in favor of -ía until pressure from the invariant 1st-singular and from the -ar class's fixed /a/ marker wins out, as late as the 16th c. (some *-íe* forms persist in Santa Teresa). The imperfect of *haber* develops a **phonologically reduced auxiliary-only paradigm** (just the ending, root fully elided: (HABEBA)S > *-ías* etc.) that becomes the morphological basis of the **conditional** — i.e. the conditional is historically "infinitive + reduced-imperfect-of-haber," exactly parallel to the future being "infinitive + reduced-present-of-haber" (cross-ref below).

**The imperfect subjunctive, -se and -ra forms, and their convergence (3.7.8.3.2, pp.201-205) — a major semantic-shift case study.** Classical Latin's own imperfect subjunctive (CANTAREM) has *no* Romance descendants at all — it was replaced in non-standard Latin by the **pluperfect** subjunctive (CANTAVISSEM), originally specifically in the apodosis of *counterfactual conditional* sentences, where the extra "temporal distance" of the pluperfect was apparently felt to reinforce a sense of unreality/improbability. This replacement-via-one-specific-construction-first mechanism is a clean case study in how a whole paradigm slot can be taken over. Separately, the **-ra forms** (today a full synonym of -se) have an even more remarkable three-stage semantic journey, fully attested with real-text citations at each stage: (1) Old Spanish -ra = Latin **pluperfect indicative** value ('had sung'), competing with the newer periphrastic *había cantado*; (2) by late Latin already, -ra forms had *additionally* acquired **conditional** value, specifically in the apodosis of counterfactual conditionals (*si pudiesse, fiziéralo* 'if he could, he would do it' / 'if he had been able, he would have done it' — ambiguous between present-counterfactual and past-counterfactual readings until the late Middle Ages introduces the periphrastic *hubiese podido* type to disambiguate); (3) only from the 14th c. does -ra additionally start appearing as a full **imperfect-subjunctive** equivalent (first in symmetric *si pudiera, hiciéralo* conditional sentences, generalizing to all subjunctive contexts only by the 19th c.). The present-day situation (many American varieties now strongly prefer -ra and treat -se as literary/written-only) is the most recent swing of a centuries-long pendulum. **Residual fossils of stage (1)** survive today only in relative clauses in literary register (*la casa que construyera años antes* 'the house he had built') and **residual fossils of stage (2)** survive only in a closed set of high-frequency verbs (*quisiera, debiera, hubiera*, historically also *pudiera*) which still carry modal/conditional rather than strict subjunctive force and are *more frequent* than their periphrastic conditional equivalents (*querría*, *debería*, *habría*).

**Origins of the future (3.7.8.4.1, pp.205-207).** The Classical Latin future (CANTABO etc.) has **zero** Romance descendants — Penny lists the candidate causes (homophony collisions with the perfect/subjunctive under regular sound change) but stresses that many languages simply get by without a dedicated future paradigm, using present + context instead. The new Romance future is grammaticalized from one specific periphrasis among five competing Latin constructions that could express futurity (EO/VENIO/VOLO/DEBEO/HABEO + infinitive) — HABEO (+ infinitive, ultimately with order infinitive+auxiliary fixed) wins in Spanish, French, Occitan, Rhaeto-Romance, Italian, Catalan, Portuguese; VOLO wins in Romanian; DEBEO in Sardinian. The **grammaticalization pathway for HABEO** is explicitly textually documented across four centuries of real Latin usage: Cicero (1st c. BC) still uses it with genuine 'possession/intention' sense; Seneca (1st c. AD) shows 'obligation'; St Augustine (4th/5th c.) uses it with an *inanimate* subject, which is the decisive test proving the construction has become a pure, semantically bleached future marker (since possession/intention/obligation all require an animate subject). **This whole section is a clean, fully-documented example of the standard grammaticalization pathway possession/intention → obligation → futurity, independently useful as a conlang design template for building a new future tense out of any possessive/obligation verb.**

**Origins of the conditional (3.7.8.4.2, pp.207-208).** Mirrors the future exactly, but built on the Latin accusative-plus-infinitive indirect-speech construction with a *past-tense* matrix verb (CREDEBAM EUM VENTURUM ESSE 'I thought he would come') rather than a present-tense matrix — i.e. the conditional's origin is specifically in *reported/indirect future-in-the-past* speech, only later extended to counterfactual-conditional apodoses (where it displaces the earlier pluperfect-indicative and -ra-form options described above).

**The weak vs strong preterite, and the pervasive "weak-type gravitational pull" mechanic (3.7.8.6, pp.217-232).** Latin's accentual distinction (weak/arrhizotonic, stressed on the ending throughout, e.g. AMAVI, vs strong/rhizotonic, stressed on the root in some forms, e.g. FECI/DIXI/DEDI) survives into Spanish only for a *minority* of very-high-frequency verbs — the overwhelming historical trend, richly exemplified, is for originally-strong Latin perfects to be **leveled to weak** (TIMUI, MOVI, VICI, CECIDI etc. all become weak *temí, moví, vencí, caí*). Where strong accentuation *does* survive, it is narrowed from Latin's up-to-4 rhizotonic forms down to just 1st/3rd person singular (*hice, hizo* but *hicimos, hicieron* arrhizotonic) — itself under weak-paradigm analogical pressure (reinforced by the invariably-ending-stressed pluperfect subjunctive FECISSEM). A **second, independent "weak gravitational pull" mechanism** explains why so many *-er* verbs (which in Latin had zero weak perfects in that conjugation) nonetheless have fully regular/weak preterites today: Lausberg's hypothesis, endorsed by Penny, is that the handful of extremely frequent *-ere* derivatives of DARE (VENDERE, CREDERE, PERDERE, REDDERE) analogically remodeled their own originally-strong reduplicating perfects (VENDIDI etc.) to look more like DARE's own weak-ish perfect (DEDI), and this remodeled VENDIDI-type paradigm then became *the* productive template that the entire regular *-er* preterite class generalizes from — a textbook case of a whole verb class's morphology being bootstrapped from one single frequent paradigm's own analogical drift. Four surviving types of genuinely strong (irregular) Old Spanish preterite are catalogued by their historical Latin type: (1) *-ui* type (root A/O, e.g. *sope* > *supe*, *plogue* > ... lost); (2) sigmatic *-sī* type (root ending in /dʒ/→/s/ assimilation, e.g. *dixe*, *traxe*, *quise*; survives today as *conduje, dije, traje, quise*); (3) root-vowel-alternation type with no overt ending-marker, only 4 survivors (*hacer, venir, ver, ser* — FECI/VENI/VIDI/FUI), including the striking case of *fui* (serving *both* 'I was' and 'I went', since SUM's and EO's suppletive perfects both collapse onto the same Latin FUI-type form); (4) consonant-reduplication type, only *dar*/*estar* (DEDI/STETI), of which only *dar*'s *di, diste, dio...* fully survives.

**Non-finite forms (3.7.9, pp.232-241).** The four Latin infinitive conjugation-classes reduce to three via the regular 3rd-conjugation-to-2nd-conjugation analogical shift (VENDERE > *VENDĒRE*, matching TIMĒRE's pattern) — this is the *identical* mechanism already noted above for the weak preterite, i.e. the 2nd/3rd-conjugation merger is a single pervasive fact about Hispanic Latin (already flagged in ch.1 as a defining Hispanic *innovation*) that cascades through infinitive, imperfect, and preterite morphology alike. *Hacer*'s infinitive shows a documented three-way Old Spanish competition between *fer* (regular syncope outcome), *fazer* (regular 3rd-conj. pattern) and *fir* (analogical, modeled on *dar/ser*) — *fazer* (> *hacer*) wins for the infinitive itself, but the *fir*-type root survives fossilized in the future/conditional stem *har-* (*haré*, *haría*). The **gerund and past participle** both show the same recurring "root-vowel polarization serves to keep -er and -ir conjugation classes morphologically distinct" mechanism already seen in the preterite/imperfect: -er forms are *categorically barred* from high root vowels (never *comiendo with /u/, never *midiendo with /i/ for an -er verb*) specifically *because* the -er/-ir classes would otherwise merge almost completely, given how few distinct endings separate them. A closed set of **irregular "strong" participles in -to** survives from Latin (*abierto, cubierto, dicho, escrito, muerto, puesto, roto, visto, vuelto* etc.) alongside **analogically created** new strong participles modeled on them (*conquisto, quisto, suelto, tuelto*) — several of which (*suelto, roto*) now function as the participle of a *historically unrelated* later verb (*soltar, romper*) that inherited the old form once its original verb (*solver, romper's predecessor*) fell out of use. A small set of **truncated participles belonging to -ar verbs** (*escaso, pago*) likewise arose by the old participle of a now-obsolete verb (*solver > suelto*; *pagar*'s own, *pago*) being retained as the participle of a *later-created* verb built from the same root.

## Chapter 4 — Lexis (etymological strata — flagship Attested Era material)

**[PENNY]** Source: Penny, ch.4, DJVU pp.275–321 (printed pp.255–301). **Scope note:** every stratum below is covered; vocabulary tables give a representative sample (10-20 items) per stratum rather than Penny's full lists (which run to several hundred items per major stratum, esp. Arabisms) — see the file's opening coverage note.

### Grammar/history points by stratum

**4.1 Inherited Latin vocabulary.** The core vocabulary (hundreds of the most frequent words) is popular + semi-learned transmission, per the ch.2 categories — treated here as baseline, not re-discussed.

**4.2 Pre-Roman substrate words.** Borrowing from pre-existing languages was *not* extensive overall (the bilingual substrate languages were themselves low-prestige), but filled two kinds of gap: true lexical gaps (no Latin word existed for a local flora/fauna/technique concept) and *disambiguation* repairs (e.g. Basque-sourced *izquierdo* 'left' displacing Latin SINISTER specifically because SINISTER had acquired the negative sense 'sinister' — a direct instance of the taboo/ambiguity-avoidance mechanism elaborated fully in ch.5). Celtic contributes a layer borrowed specifically within the Peninsula (e.g. *berro*, *braga*) plus a *second* Celtic layer borrowed into general spoken Latin *before* it reached Spain (hence with cognates across Romance: *camino*, *cerveza*, *legua*). Basque's contribution is contested — Trask (1997) rejects most proposed Basque etymologies except *izquierdo* itself; a large residual class of words (e.g. *páramo*, *sapo*, *gusano*) is attributed only vaguely to "unidentified pre-Roman sources."

**4.3 Latinisms** (learned borrowings from written Latin at every subsequent period) are explicitly dated by century of first attestation in a running list from the 13th through 18th centuries (18 Latinisms given per century on average) — this is the single best within-Penny dataset for genuinely **century-precise Attested Era** tagging of individual lexical items, reproduced in the vocabulary table below. Estimated overall proportion: 20-30% of the Modern Spanish *lexicon* (though a much smaller share of raw *token frequency*, since lesson-ordering/frequency ≠ type-count) is Latinate borrowing (Alvar and Mariner 1967, cited by Penny).

**4.4 Hellenisms**, in three historically distinct strata: (1) *popular* Greek loans absorbed into spoken Latin itself (hence orally inherited like native words, covering everyday domestic/tool/plant/animal vocabulary: *cuchara* 'spoon', *bodega* 'wine-cellar', *ajenjo* 'wormwood'); (2) *ecclesiastical* Greek (via Church Latin, hence semi-learned/learned: *iglesia*, *obispo* [not explicitly in the list but same stratum type as *abismo, bautismo, cátedra, clérigo*]); (3) *technical/scientific* Greek, century-dated exactly like the Latinisms above, from the 13th through 20th centuries.

**4.5 Germanic borrowings** (excluding later Anglicisms) arrive by *three* distinct routes with different dating and different geographic footprints: (1) pan-Western-Romance loans into spoken Latin itself, 4th-5th c., *before* the Empire's political fragmentation — hence these have cognates throughout Western Romance but typically *not* Romanian (*banco, guerra, jabón, rico, tregua*); (2) Visigothic-specific loans, confined to the Peninsula + southern France, from the early-5th-c. Toulouse kingdom period (*espía, estaca, ropa, sacar*) — the *Spanish-only* subset (after the Franks expelled the Visigoths from Gaul in the late 5th c.) is even narrower (*ataviar, ganar, tejón*); (3) loans transmitted via French/Occitan/Catalan in the 12th-13th c. and later, ultimately of Frankish origin (*arenque, bando, blanco, guante, jardín*). Phonological adaptation patterns differ systematically by route and are explicitly keyed to a relative chronology (loss of Germanic /h/, /w/→/gw/, /θ/→/l/, aspirated-plosive-as-geminate reinterpretation) — a clean worked example of "borrowing route determines adaptation pattern," reusable methodologically.

**4.6 Arabisms** — the largest and most systematically organized borrowing-stratum chapter, explicitly keyed to **semantic field** (warfare/fortification, civil administration, commerce, weights-and-measures, trades/tools, house-building, household contents, agriculture/new crops, irrigation, gardening, natural world, food, science) rather than strictly by date, because (per Penny) the bulk of the borrowing happened in a *single* concentrated period (8th-10th c., *before* Castile's own territorial expansion into Al-Andalus — i.e. these are loans from a neighboring language, not from bilingual contact within shared territory). The *al-* prefix-fusion mechanism (Arabic invariable definite article *al-* reanalyzed by Romance speakers as part of the noun stem, since Romance articles *do* inflect) is identified as the reason so many Arabisms begin with *a(l)-*. A late-medieval **prestige-reversal wave** replaces several early Arabisms with Latinate/French/Greek-via-Latin competitors as Arabic culture's prestige declines (*albéitar* → *veterinario*; *alfageme* 'barber' → *barbero*; *alfayate* 'tailor' → *sastre*; *alarife* → *arquitecto*). Detailed phonological-adaptation strategies for "difficult" Arabic phonemes (velars/pharyngeals, dental fricatives, the /stl/ cluster, final labials/velars, final-tonic-vowel nouns) are catalogued with multiple repair strategies per problem (substitution, omission, epenthesis) — directly comparable in structure to the Germanic-loan adaptation strategies above.

**4.7 Mozarabisms** — a smaller stratum, Castilian borrowings specifically from Mozarabic (the Romance, *not* Arabic, vernacular of Al-Andalus, itself a sister descendant of spoken Latin, independent of Castilian, extinct by the late Middle Ages) — mostly agriculture/natural-world vocabulary (*chícharo, gazpacho, guisante, muchacho*), reflecting Mozarabic's temporary social prestige in newly reconquered cities (Toledo, taken 1085, still had active Mozarabic speakers in the early 13th c.) due to its speakers' link to the more culturally advanced Al-Andalus civilization.

**4.8 Gallicisms and Occitanisms** are explicitly century-banded (11th-13th c. "first period" driven by Reconquest-era French participation + pilgrimage + monastic reform + literary influence; 14th-15th c.; Golden Age [military/naval + courtly-life borrowing driven by the France-Spain military conflict]; 18th c. [fashion/domestic/scientific terminology, the "period of most intense borrowing," subject to active purist pushback]; 19th-20th c. [finance, technology, politics, cuisine — slowing only in the last few decades under anglicisms' competing pressure]). Occitanisms specifically are "almost entirely restricted to the medieval period" since Occitan culture itself declines after that.

**4.9 Amerindianisms** are organized by *source language* (Arawak/Taíno, Carib, Nahuatl, Quechua, Tupí-Guaraní, plus smaller Maya/Chibcha/Araucanian contributions) rather than by date, since the whole stratum is compressed into the post-1492 period, with the *very earliest* attestations (*canoa, cacique*, and the disputed *niames* 'yams') recorded directly in **Columbus's own 1492-93 shipboard log** — an unusually precise earliest-attestation date. A recurring **competing-synonym dynamic** is flagged: New World referents were sometimes named by *extending* an Old World term (*tigre, león, piña* for jaguar/puma/pineapple) in competition with the later-borrowed Amerindian term (*jaguar, puma, ananás*) — in some cases the borrowing wins out everywhere (*jaguar, puma*), in others the Old World term wins everywhere (*piña* beats *ananás* in Spain and "large regions" of Spanish America), and in others **two different Amerindian borrowings compete regionally** (*aguacate* from Nahuatl in Spain/N&C.America/Colombia-Venezuela vs *palta* from Quechua in Ecuador/Peru/Bolivia).

**4.10 Anglicisms.** Pountain's definition (adopted by Penny too, in fact cited the other way — Penny cites Pratt 1980): an anglicism is defined by its *immediate* donor being English, regardless of English's own ultimate source-language (so *anorak*, *kimono*, *kárate*, *pijama* — all ultimately non-English in origin — count as anglicisms in Spanish because English was the direct transmission channel; conversely *auto-stop*, *camping*, *esmoquin* etc. are explicitly *excluded* from the anglicism category and reclassified as **gallicisms**, because despite their English-looking form they are attested earlier in French and show French semantic-adaptation patterns — i.e. the transmission *route*, not the surface form, is the defining criterion). Pre-1950s anglicisms are predominantly British-English-sourced and written-media-transmitted; post-1950s are predominantly American-English-sourced and increasingly orally transmitted via dubbed film/TV. Organized by semantic field: media, fashion/cosmetics, commerce/finance, science/technology/medicine, sport (described as showing "long... particular openness to anglicisms").

**4.11 Catalanisms**, organized by semantic field reflecting areas of *perceived Catalan pre-eminence*: food/culinary terms (fish names, *paella*), domestic items, card-game terminology, **maritime vocabulary** (reflecting Catalan sea-faring predominance in the Peninsula until the end of the Middle Ages: *buque, galera, muelle, timonel*) and trade terms, practical-skills vocabulary, printing/writing terms.

**4.12 Lusisms** (Portuguese/Galician borrowings, often indistinguishable by form between the two sources) — earliest layer tied to medieval Castile's adoption of Galician-Portuguese as *the* language of courtly love-lyric (*cuita, coitado, ledo* — mostly since lost/replaced); largest group is **maritime/seafaring vocabulary**, reflecting Portuguese nautical expertise *predating* Spain's own (*baía, boya, carabela*); a secondary layer of ultimately *exotic/Asian* vocabulary reaching Spanish *via* Portugal's own maritime empire (*bambú, biombo, té*).

**4.13 Italianisms**, concentrated in the 16th-17th c. (Renaissance prestige + direct Spanish military involvement on the Italian peninsula), organized by field: arts/literature/theatre (*novela, soneto, bufón*), visual arts/architecture, music (an exceptionally dense borrowing: *piano, violín, aria, batuta, concierto, ópera, serenata, tempo* — "as in the case of all European languages... extremely frequent"), military terminology (another dense borrowing: *alerta, asalto, bombardear, cañón, escuadrón*), maritime vocabulary, commerce/banking (*banca, bancarrota, crédito, millón*), general social/behavioral vocabulary (*bizarro, capricho, chulo, mafia*).

**4.14 Word-formation** (prefixation/derivation/composition as Spanish's *internal* lexical resource, as opposed to borrowing). Key mechanism highlighted: many Latin prefix+root combinations had already fossilized into phonologically-opaque unitary words by the time Spanish speakers could no longer analyze them as "prefix + base" — e.g. *provecho* 'advantage' (< PRO+FECTU, with intervocalic-F treatment of the /f/ despite its morpheme-internal position) vs. the still-transparent *aprender* (< AD+PREHENDERE, root /pr/ kept word-initial-style treatment *because* speakers still perceived the prefix boundary). This "morphological transparency determines whether a sound-change rule applies as word-initial or word-internal" mechanism is directly reusable for any conlang design where prefixed forms need principled treatment. Individual prefixes (*a-, des-/es-, en-/in-, ex-, inter-, trans-, sub-, super-/sobre-*) are each tracked through a popular-descendant-vs-learned-doublet split exactly parallel to the general popular/learned distinction from ch.2 — e.g. popular *es-* vs learned *ex-*, popular *entre-* vs learned *inter-*, popular *sobre-* vs learned *super-* (the last pair explicitly flagged as having been *reinvigorated* in modern Spanish "probably under the influence of English" for the 'excess' sense, i.e. *super-* as an intensifying prefix is itself arguably a recent semi-calque).

## Chapter 5 — Semantics (FLAGSHIP: taboo-driven semantic change)

**[PENNY]** Source: Penny, ch.5, DJVU pp.322–337 (printed pp.302–317). Extracted in near-full detail per dispatch priority — this chapter is explicitly the project's flagship find and is short enough (16 pages) to cover thoroughly.

### 5.1 Causes of semantic change

**5.1.1 Linguistic causes — collocational meaning-transfer, with a double-negative worked example.** Frequent collocation lets part of one word's sense leak onto its partner, eventually surviving *without* the original partner. Penny's worked example is genuinely a *chain* of two independent instances: (a) Latin HOMINE NATU 'a man born' (originally positive, 'anyone at all') collocates so often with the negator NON that it absorbs negative force, giving 'no one at all'; ellipsis of NON then leaves *omne nado* alone meaning 'no one', and further ellipsis/modification yields OSp./MSp. **nadie**. (b) Separately, Latin REM NATAM 'anything (at all)' undergoes the identical collocation-then-ellipsis process with NON, yielding — via ellipsis of the *other* half, NATA — OSp./MSp. **nada** 'nothing' (while the Navarro-Aragonese region instead ellipted REM, yielding *ren*). A third, structurally identical case: Latin REM replaced in speech by CAUSA, which in the same NEG+NATA collocation gives OSp. *cosa (nada)* > modern *cosa* now regularly collocating with *nada* ('not a thing') — and modern *en absoluto* is cited as an ongoing, *live* instance of exactly the same mechanism (its commonest sense is now simply 'not at all', having absorbed negative force from its usual negated context).

**5.1.2 Historical causes.** When the real-world referent of a word changes but the word itself doesn't, meaning drifts to track the new referent: *coche* 'horse-drawn vehicle' → '(motor) car' without a symbol-change. Fuller worked examples with full sense-chains: *calzas* 'stockings' (< CALCEA) lengthens with the garment itself through the Middle Ages to mean '(leg) tights', then at the 16th-c. split into two garments gives *medias* 'stockings' (lower half) and *calzas* 'breeches' (upper half, now also *calzones*); *alcalde* (< Ar. *al-qāḍī* 'judge') widens from 'judge' to 'magistrate-and-mayor' (Golden Age) to purely 'mayor' (modern, losing all judicial sense); *alférez* (< Ar. *al-fāris* 'horseman') narrows OSp. 'horseman' → 'best horseman of a squadron' → Golden Age 'standard-bearer' → modern 'second lieutenant'; *barbero* 'barber-surgeon/dentist' (until at least 18th c.) narrows to just 'barber'.

**5.1.3 Social causes — restriction (jargon-capture) and its mirror, generalization (jargon-escape).** A word moving *into* a trade/professional sub-register typically *narrows*: *ORDINARE* 'to organize' → farming jargon 'to milk' (via 'organizing the cowshed chores'); *afeitar* (< Lat. AFFECTARE 'to devote oneself') narrows OSp. 'to adorn, beautify' → Golden Age 'to beautify with makeup; to shave' → modern exclusively 'to shave'; *botar* (medieval French loan, general sense 'to throw', *still* general in most American Spanish) narrows in Peninsular Spanish specifically to boat-builders' jargon 'to launch'. The reverse process (a trade-jargon word *escaping* into general use, typically *widening*) is equally attested: *azar* 'chance/luck' (good or bad) < gaming-jargon Ar. *zahr* 'dice', originally just 'an unfortunate throw of the dice'; *armario* 'cupboard' widens almost immediately from its original narrow military sense 'armoury' (link to *arma* 'weapon').

**5.1.4 Psychological causes: TABOO — the chapter's central mechanism, explicitly presented as the single most powerful and well-studied psychological driver of semantic change.** Penny's general mechanism statement: taboo forbids direct naming of a concept; the concept still needs referring to; the standard resolution is euphemism (an existing word or phrase pressed into service as a replacement); and — this is the crucial second-order prediction — **once a word's euphemistic sense becomes dominant, its *original* sense is rapidly abandoned too**, because speakers become unwilling to risk being misheard as making the euphemistic reference when they didn't intend it. (I.e. taboo-driven semantic change doesn't just *add* a sense, it actively *crowds out* the old one — a two-step, not one-step, mechanism.) Three taboo subtypes, each richly exemplified:

  - **5.1.4.1 Fear taboo** (religious/superstitious). The flagship case: the weasel. Across most of the Romance-speaking world the Latin name MUSTELA is displaced by euphemisms because of a Western-European folk-belief associating weasels with supernatural evil — and the Iberian Peninsula shows an entire **dialect map of independently-coined replacement euphemisms**, cited by Penny as a single unusually rich, geographically-resolved case study: Catalan + one corner of southern Galicia keep *mustela* itself; northern León/western Santander use affectionate derivatives of the same base (*mostolilla*); the west uses affectionate derivatives of DOMINA 'lady' (Ptg. *doninha*, Gal. *don(oc)iña*, Leonese *donecilla*); the northeast (Castile/Navarre/Aragon) uses *paniquesa* (probably referencing coloring); most of Castile/Murcia/Andalusia (hence also Spanish America) uses *comadreja* (originally affectionate, < *comadre* 'neighbor/godmother'); Asturias/Santander show "many other forms"; and Andalusia separately shows euphemistic *use of personal names* for the animal (*juanica, maría, maría-gareta, nutriquita*). A second fear-taboo case, independently attested: the Latin words for 'left' (LAEVUS, SCAEVUS, SINISTER) almost all vanish from Romance because of the evil/Devil association with left-handedness; only SINISTER survives at all (as OSp. *siniestro*, itself soon narrowed to only the *figurative* 'sinister' sense once the literal sense 'left' is handed off to **izquierdo**, a Basque borrowing). Penny's explicit generalization: *foreign borrowings can serve exactly the same taboo-avoidance function as native euphemisms.*

  - **5.1.4.2 Delicacy taboo** (disease, death, infirmity, crime — concepts considered simply unpleasant rather than supernaturally dangerous). *Cretino* < Fr. dialectal *chrétien* 'Christian', itself originally a euphemism; *tullido* 'crippled' is the only Modern Spanish survivor of the OSp. verb *toller/tollir* 'to take away, deprive'; *matar* 'to kill' plausibly < Late Lat. MATTUS 'stupid, stupefied' via a euphemistic derived sense 'to stun'. Modern Spanish is said to have "a host of euphemistic expressions" for 'to kill' (*cargarse a uno, dar el pasaporte, dar el paseo, liquidar, eliminar* — some probably calqued on English euphemisms), for 'to steal' (*coger, pillar, apañar, aliviar, trabajar, raspar*), and for 'to die' (*reventar, estirar la pata*) — **this entire cluster is a direct, ready-made register-ladder dataset for this project's euphemism/slang modeling.**

  - **5.1.4.3 Decency taboo** (sex, body parts/functions). *Fulana* 'lover/concubine/prostitute' (cf. *fulano* 'so-and-so' — i.e. the taboo-avoidance mechanism here is specifically *anonymization*, naming the referent via a dummy-name formula rather than a literal description); *buscona* 'prostitute' / *buscón* 'thief' both probably euphemistic agentive derivatives of *buscar* 'to seek'; *parir* 'to give birth' (of humans) increasingly avoided in favor of *dar a luz, alumbrar*; *embarazada* 'pregnant' < 'encumbered', and *en estado* (reduced from *en estado interesante*) both displacing the plainer *preñada*; *aseo, baño, servicio(s)* all euphemistically extending to 'lavatory', displacing *retrete*. Religious/sexual *oaths* specifically are subject to decency taboo via a distinct sub-mechanism — **phonetic-similarity substitution**: *¡caracoles!* (lit. 'snails!') replaces *¡carajo!* (lit. 'penis!') precisely because the two words share enough of their sound-shape that the substitution is recognizable as standing in for the tabooed original.

**5.1.5 Foreign-influence-driven semantic change (semantic loan/calque) — Arabic and English case studies.** Arabic-influence semantic loans (attested where a Latin-origin Spanish word acquires a *sense* paralleling an Arabic cognate-concept, later sometimes lost again): *adelantado* 'placed in front' → 'colonial governor' (imitating Ar. *muqaddam*); OSp. *casa* 'house' → (temporarily) 'town' (imitating Ar. *dār*); *hidalgo* 'noble' < *fijo-de-algo* 'son of wealth', imitating Arabic *ibn ad-dunyā* 'son of wealth; rich man' patronymic-metaphor constructions. English-influence semantic loans are organized into (a) **"false-friend" paronym pairs** turned "true/truer friends" by semantic borrowing — a long list is given: *administración* (gaining 'government' alongside 'administration'), *base* (gaining 'military base'), *crisis* (gaining the generic sense), *editor* (gaining '(newspaper) editor'), *honesto* (gaining 'honest' from 'honorable'), *humor* (gaining the comedic sense), *plástico* (gaining 'plastic' the material/adj.), *simple* (gaining 'simple' from 'single'); (b) **non-paronymous semantic loans** (*cadena* gaining '(shop) chain', *estrella* gaining '(film) star', *muestra* gaining 'statistical sample'); (c) **loan-translation/calque compounds**, organized by internal syntactic pattern (noun+noun: *buque escuela, ciudad dormitorio*; verb+noun: *cortacésped, limpiaparabrisas*; noun+adjective: *caja fuerte, guerra fría, perro caliente*; adjective+noun: *tercer mundo*; noun+de+noun: *beso de la vida, cruce de cebra, máquina de coser*).

**5.1.6 Naming a new concept** — the three competing strategies (borrowing / native word-formation / semantic extension of an existing word) and the explicit methodological caveat: distinguishing *independent* Spanish semantic extension from *imitative* semantic loan is only reliably possible via precise **dating of first attestation** in each language — Penny's worked contrast is *platillo volante* 'flying saucer' (almost certainly a loan-translation of English) vs. *león* 'lion'→'puma', *piña* 'pine-cone'→'pineapple', *tigre* 'tiger'→'jaguar' (independently-arisen Spanish extensions, not foreign-modeled).

### 5.2 Types of semantic change — Ullmann's 2×2 taxonomy (table 5.1)

Penny adopts Ullmann's (1962, after Roudet 1921) cross-classification: **similarity vs contiguity** of either **meaning** or **form**, giving four named cells — metaphor (meaning-similarity), metonymy (meaning-contiguity), popular etymology (form-similarity), ellipsis (form-contiguity).

- **5.2.1 Metaphor**, subdivided by type: **anthropomorphic** (body-part names applied to objects and vice-versa: *boca* 'mouth (of river)', *ojo* 'eye (of needle)' / *caja (del pecho)* 'rib-cage', *nuez* '(Adam's) apple'); **animal** metaphors (*diente de león* 'dandelion' lit. 'lion's tooth', *gato* 'jack' lit. 'cat'); **concrete→abstract** (*depender* 'to depend' < 'to hang', *pensar* 'to think' < PENSARE 'to weigh (up)'); **synaesthetic** (*voz fría* 'cold voice', *colores chillones* 'loud colors'). Penny explicitly notes the fuzzy boundary between a still-"living" metaphor (both senses co-present and felt as related) and simple **polysemy** (senses felt as unrelated), with *sierra* 'mountain-range'/'saw' given as a genuinely disputed borderline case.
- **5.2.2 Metonymy**, subdivided by relation-type: **spatial** (*acera* 'pavement' < OSp. 'facade (of a street-facing row of buildings)'; *carrillo* '(lower) cheek' < 'jaw'); **temporal** (*almuerzo* 'breakfast' in Old/Golden-Age/American/regional-Peninsular Spanish shifts to 'lunch' in urban Peninsular Spanish; *verano* 'summer' displaces *estío*, pushing *estío* itself to literary-register-only status — directly relevant usage-tier data); **synecdoche** (part-for-whole or whole-for-part: *boda* 'wedding' < 'marriage vows'; *puerto* 'harbour' < 'harbour entrance'); substance-for-product (*alambre* 'wire' < 'bronze(work)'); proper-noun-for-product (*jerez, manzanilla, champán, coñac* — commercial place-name-to-product metonymy); abstract-for-concrete (*cuenta* 'account, bill', *clérigo* 'priest', *guardia* 'policeman').
- **5.2.3 Popular etymology** — reshaping an "unmotivated" (structurally isolated) word's *form* to fit it into a pre-existing word-family, often with a subtle accompanying sense-shift: *vagabundo* 'tramp' reshaped colloquially to *vagamundo*, picking up the specific nuance 'one who wanders the world'; *cerrojo* 'bolt' (< *ferrojo*, attracted to *cerrar* 'to close') picks up an exclusive association with door-closing; *tinieblas* 'darkness' (< OSp. *tiniebras*, attracted to *niebla* 'fog') arguably picks up a connotation of "fogginess"; *pulgar* 'thumb' (< OSp. *polgar*, attracted to *pulga* 'flea') may have picked up a folk-association of the thumb as a flea-killing tool.
- **5.2.4 Ellipsis** — in a frequent noun+adjective collocation, the noun drops out and the adjective absorbs its full nominal sense: *ábrego* 'south wind' < *(VENTU) AFRICU* 'African wind'; *aguijada* 'goad' < *(PERTICA) AQUILEATA* 'sharpened pole'; *albérchigo* 'clingstone peach' < *(MALU) PERSICU* 'Persian fruit'; *almiar* 'haystack' < *(PERTICA) MEDIALE* 'central pole'; *armiño* 'stoat/ermine' < *(MURE) ARMENIU* 'Armenian mouse'; *avellana* 'hazelnut' < *(NUCE) ABELLANA* 'nut from Abella'; *breva* 'early fig' < *(FICU) BIFERA* 'twice-bearing fig tree'; *campana* 'bell' < *(VASA) CAMPANA* 'vessels from Campania'; *ciruela* 'plum' < *(PRUNA) CEREOLA* 'wax-colored plums'.

### 5.3 Consequences of semantic change

**5.3.1 Change of semantic range** (specificity-increase vs generality-increase, each with the systematic **learned-doublet retains the wider sense** rule, directly parallel to the ch.2 popular/learned transmission distinction). Specificity-increase examples: *segar* 'to reap' < SECARE 'to cut' (general); *anegar* 'to drown' < *necare* 'to kill (esp. by strangling)'; *cuñado* 'brother-in-law' < COGNATU 'blood-relation' (contrast learned-ish *cognado* retaining the wider sense); *siesta* 'siesta' < SEXTA (HORA) 'sixth hour' (contrast the learned numeral *sexta* itself, unrestricted). Generality-increase examples: *compañero* 'companion' < Late Lat. COMPANIO lit. 'one who eats bread with another' (contrast learned causa-based *causa* 'cause' from the same ultimate root as *cosa* 'thing'); *dinero* 'money' < DENARIUS '(a specific) coin'; *grande* 'large (any sense)' < GRANDE 'large in size (only)'; *hallar* 'to find' < AFFLARE 'to breathe out' (via hunting-dog 'to scent'→'to find the prey', cross-ref ch.1). A specific **proper-noun→common-noun generalization** subtype: *quevedos* 'spectacles' (19th c.) < the painter's habitual depiction of the poet Quevedo wearing spectacles; **asesino** 'murderer' < Ar. *ḥashāshīn*, lit. 'hashish-drinkers', the name of an 11th-century Muslim sect notorious for assassinating opponents.

**5.3.2 Change of affectivity (pejoration / amelioration).** Pejoration drivers listed: euphemistic reuse (cross-ref 5.1.4), negative association-of-ideas, prejudice — examples *algarabía* 'gibberish; uproar' < Ar. *al-ʿarabiyya* 'the Arabic language' (itself a telling case of linguistic-prejudice-driven pejoration: the *name of a whole foreign language* becomes a byword for incomprehensible noise); *cautivo* 'captive'→'wretched'→'wicked, evil' (reinforced by It. *cattivo* 'bad'); *necio* 'foolish' < NESCIU 'ignorant'; *villano* 'boorish' < 'rural' < 'farm' (VILLA). Amelioration drivers: hyperbole-then-weakening (*terrible, horrible* moving toward merely 'very bad'; *lamentar* 'to regret' < 'to lament, grieve'), or straightforward derogatory→favorable drift: **caballo** 'horse' < CABALLU 'nag, workhorse' (the single most-cited amelioration example in the whole book, cross-referenced independently from Pountain's Text 9 commentary too — "amelioration through self-deprecation", see Pountain section below); *calle* 'street' < CALLE '(cattle-)path'; *casa* 'house' < CASA 'hut, cottage'; *corte* 'court'/*Cortes* 'Parliament' < COHORTE 'enclosure, farmyard' (via 'division of a military camp'→'Imperial guard'→'palace'; the original rural sense survives dialectally as *torre* 'cow-shed'). A distinguished subclass, ***voces mediae*** (words whose tone is neutral but context-dependently colors favorable or unfavorable, sometimes crystallizing permanently one way): *suerte* 'luck' (crystallizes favorable, needing *mala suerte* to force the negative reading) vs *sino* 'fate' (crystallizes unfavorable) vs *accidente* 'accident' (crystallizes unfavorable despite neutral ACCIDENS 'occurring').

## Chapter 6 — Past, present and future

**[PENNY]** Source: Penny, ch.6, DJVU pp.338–348 (printed pp.318–328).

### Grammar/history points

**Language history is not actually linear — Penny's explicit meta-commentary on his own book's method (6.1).** Having presented 300+ pages of linear-looking sound/form changes (e.g. OCL OLUM > OCLUM > /óʎo/ > /óʝo/ > /óʃo/ > /óxo/ *ojo* 'eye'), Penny steps back to flag this as a *necessary simplification*: real language history proceeds not along one line but through **successive dialect-contact / koineization events**, each collapsing a pool of competing regional variants down to a new leveled norm that tends to prefer the *simpler* available variant — explicitly named events: 10th-c. Burgos, 12th-c. Toledo, 13th-c. Seville, 16th-c. Madrid, plus 16th/17th-c. Mexico City and Lima overseas (p.318, citing his own Penny 1998/2000). This is the single clearest statement in the book of the **koineization-as-primary-mechanism** thesis previewed in ch.1, and is directly relevant as a structural model for any slang-synthesis derivation that wants to simulate *dialect mixture*, not just straight-line descent.

**World Spanish (6.2) and the asymmetry of "Peninsular-biased" history-writing.** Penny flags his own book's necessary European bias: because the large majority of the sound/form changes that produced *all* of today's World Spanish varieties (seseo, weakening of syllable-final /s/, *voseo* and its associated verb-forms) occurred in Europe *before* the Americas were settled, a history organized around European textual evidence will *look* European-centered even though almost none of the features discussed are actually exclusively European today.

**Convergence vs divergence (6.3) — Penny's explicit argument against the once-standard "Spanish will fragment like Latin did" prediction.** Two structural reasons given for why Spanish, unlike Vulgar Latin, is *not* expected to fragment into mutually unintelligible daughter languages: (1) the original Latin-to-Romance fragmentation was driven by a specific historical configuration — weak inter-regional communication + a *single* standard (Classical Latin) accessible only to a small literate elite, which let purely *local* innovations spread only locally, compounding divergence over centuries; medieval nation-states then *additionally* chose to formalize this divergence into separate written standards once the resources existed to do so (explicitly tied back to Alfonso X's project, ch.1) — a configuration Penny argues has **no parallel today** in the Spanish-speaking world (no evidence of any Spanish-speaking state moving toward a separate spelling/grammatical codification); (2) *modern* convergence pressures (inter-Academy collaboration producing shared recommendations; mass media; migration/travel) are *stronger* today than they were even in the medieval period, counteracting the real (and acknowledged) lexical variation that does exist region-to-region.

**English and Spanish (6.4) — a direct comparative sociolinguistic argument.** Spanish and English are described as "arguably the only two genuine world languages" by Penny's criterion (state-sponsored across multiple continents as the normal vehicle of education/administration/media) — but asymmetric in one key respect: English, unlike Spanish, is overwhelmingly used as a **second** language by huge non-native populations, which Penny argues makes English's own future trajectory *less* controllable by native English speakers than Spanish's is by native Spanish speakers. US Spanish is flagged as a specific live case: ~10% of the US population are native Spanish speakers (as of the source's 2002 publication), the community shows *no* sign of language loss across generations (unlike other US minority languages), and its growth rate exceeds that of the English-speaking majority — with early-2000s-visible signs of rising status (Spanish-speaking politicians achieving national prominence, rather than merely receiving token outreach). Penny's own assessment of the net English-on-Spanish structural impact: substantial on **vocabulary** (cross-ref 4.10 Anglicisms) and somewhat on **semantics** (cross-ref 5.1.5), but "rarely if ever... felt in the phonology, morphology or syntax of Spanish" — i.e. lexical/semantic borrowing without structural borrowing, an explicit claim about the *limits* of contact-induced change that's directly relevant to any slang-synthesis model calibrating how much a contact language can affect borrowed-from structure versus vocabulary.

## Text 1 — A letter from the Visigothic period (7th century?)
**[POUNTAIN]** Source: Pountain, Text 1, printed pp.13-17. A slate document (Gómez-Moreno 1954), broadly conforming to Latin spelling but giving clues to spoken-Latin direction of change; dialectal affiliation not clearly determinable.

**Attested Era:** 7th century (by letter style). **Attested Region:** Visigothic Spain (precise locale not given in the extract).

### Grammar points
- **/kw/–/k/ merger before /o/, with hypercorrection.** `comodo` (l.2) vs `quo[m]odo` (ll.8-9); `quollige` (l.3) vs `collige` (l.6) — shows the Latin /kw/-/k/ distinction already lost before /o/ in this writer's speech, and that the writer was *aware* of the "correct" spelling well enough to **hypercorrect** plain *c* to *qu* in the wrong place — direct textual evidence of a sound-merger-in-progress plus a scribe's self-conscious (and imperfect) normative correction.
- **Proparoxytone syncope**: `domne` (l.2) for DOMINE shows the /ĭ/-syncope that regularly produces Sp. *dueño* < DOMINU, *hombre* < HOMINE.
- **/ŭ/~/ō/ merger evidenced even in atonic position**: `custudiat` for CUSTODIAT (tonic, standard Romance merger) but also `fibola`/`tegolas` for FIBULA/TEGULAS (atonic) — Pountain explicitly flags that this atonic-position evidence is unusual since the words themselves don't survive into Spanish to confirm it independently.
- **Palatalization of /dj/ > /j/**: `ajvtet` for ADIUTET shows this change already applying.
- **Loss of final /m/ masked by conservative spelling**: accusative-looking `atmancio nostro` (expected ATMANTIUM NOSTRUM) implies no final /m/ pronounced, *despite* the same document still *spelling* final -m in other accusatives (`tvam`, `fraudem`) — a direct case of spelling lagging pronunciation by centuries (final /m/ had stopped being pronounced as early as the 2nd c., per a cited 2nd-c. grammarian's testimony).
- **No orthographic sign of lenition yet** (`saluto` for what would become *saludo*; `cupas` for *cubas*) — i.e. this text's spelling conventions are too conservative to let us see whether lenition had already happened in this writer's actual speech.
- **Over-extension of ILLE/IPSE toward definite-article-like use**: `ipsut oliballa`, `illas cupas ... calas` read naturally as "the 'oliballa'", "the stored barrels" rather than true Latin demonstratives.
- **A deferential-address precursor**: `tvam [claritat]em` ('your Excellency') is flagged by Pountain as an early example of **referring to an addressee via an abstract third-person-style noun phrase** — the exact same device (centuries later, grammaticalized) that produces *vuestra merced* > *usted* (explicit forward cross-reference to Text 18 below). No sign yet of Latin plural VOS used for singular deferential address.

## Text 3 — The Valpuesta document (1011)
**[POUNTAIN]** Source: Pountain, Text 3, printed pp.28-33. A legal land-exchange document from Valpuesta (Burgos area); Pountain follows Wright's (1982) interpretive framework (no sharp felt Latin/Romance distinction yet; legal registers conventionally retain archaic forms regardless of actual speech).

**Attested Era:** precisely dated 1011. **Attested Region:** Valpuesta, judicial district of Villarcayo, Burgos.

### Grammar points
- **Diphthongization attested but inconsistent**: `portiello`, `kanaliella` show it; `terras` (vs MSp. *tierras*), `nostra` (vs *nuestra*) don't — direct evidence that 11th-c. spelling under-represents an already-real sound change, not that the change hadn't happened yet.
- **Prothetic /e-/ before initial s+consonant**: `expontanias` < SPONTANEAS, `Espessa` < SPISSA — the modern Spanish constraint against word-initial /s/+consonant (hence *estrés*, *esnob* for borrowed *stress*, *snob*) is already active in 1011.
- **/b/ for Lat. /w/**: `billa` for VILLA, representing [β].
- **Definite-article-like use of illo/ipso, with masculine OSp. *illo* (not *lo*) — a fork in the road not taken**: Pountain flags that Castilian's eventual choice of *el* (not *lo*) for the masc. sg. definite article contrasts with neighboring Romance (Ptg. *o*; a reconstructed Leonese-area *elo/ela/elos/elas* paradigm), i.e. the modern Castilian article paradigm's shape is itself one specific historical resolution among several attested regional alternatives.
- **Gender-opposition-without-natural-gender as a word-formation device**: *lombo* (masc.) / *lomba* (fem.), both < LUMBUS 'loin, back of an animal' — the feminine form comes to denote the geographically *larger*/broader referent (> MSp. *loma* 'hillock' vs *lomo* 'back/loin of a hill'), explicitly generalized by Pountain as a recurring Castilian pattern (cf. *cuenca* 'river basin' vs *cuenco* 'bowl').
- **CASA's amelioration-through-self-deprecation origin, independently cross-referenced across the whole book**: *casa* 'house' < CASA 'hut, cabin' (lowly in Latin, vs DOMUS 'house'), already flagged here (3.3.2) and cross-referenced forward to Texts 9, 15, 22, 25, 29 — i.e. Pountain treats this as a single mechanism recurring independently across the entire history, directly matching Penny's ch.5 flagship amelioration discussion (CABALLU > *caballo* is the structurally identical case).

## Text 7 — A father's farewell, *Cantar de mio Cid* (c.1200)
**[POUNTAIN]** Source: Pountain, Text 7, printed pp.58-61. Sole MS now in the Biblioteca Nacional (dated by explicit colophon 1207, but palaeographically a 14th-c. copy of a lost original); the extract is Rodrigo's farewell to his wife and daughters.

**Attested Era:** composed late 12th/early 13th c.; surviving copy 14th c. **Attested Region:** Castile (though the single MS shows dialect-mixture issues — cross-referenced against the lost "P" and later "G'" manuscript traditions throughout Pountain's commentary).

### Grammar points
- **Forms of address switch mid-text, directly paralleled in Text 9 below**: this extract itself doesn't show a switch, but companion commentary (shared with Text 9) establishes that *vos* was the expected form to a king or to a respected social superior while *tú* was used downward/to intimates — the *Cid* text itself shows the Cid using *vos* to his wife Jimena and most relatives but *tú* to young male kinsmen, i.e. **status/age-graded, not simple-intimacy-graded** address even within a single family.
- **Full form of the 1st-person-style demonstrative**: *aquestas* (< *ACC(U) ISTAS*) is already competing with, and already outnumbered by, the reduced form *estas* in the same text — an attested example of a reduction-in-progress with both variants co-occurring.
- **Agentive *de* where Modern Spanish would use *por***: *de quien so yo seruida* 'by whom I am served' — this agentive-*de* pattern is noted as still surviving today only in a narrower stative-passive context (*acompañado de su esposa*).
- **Clitic-pronoun-position rule (fully general in this period, cross-referenced repeatedly across many Pountain texts): a clitic pronoun cannot stand in absolute sentence-initial position**, and is placed according to what tonic element (if any) precedes the verb in the same clause — directly matches Penny's ch.3 account of the identical phenomenon (3.5.1 pronoun-positioning discussion) and recurs as a named "Keypoint" cross-referenced by Pountain in nearly every subsequent medieval-period text (Texts 9, 10, 15 below).

## Text 9 — Moral instruction, *Libro de Alexandre* (13th century)
**[POUNTAIN]** Source: Pountain, Text 9, printed pp.73-81 (commentary consulted pp.77-81). Transmitted in three divergent MSS (O, P, G'), whose dialect differences (Leonese-leaning O vs more easterly P) are themselves the main linguistic interest of this text for Pountain.

**Attested Era:** 13th century. **Attested Region:** Castile/León border zone (inferred from inter-MS dialect variation).

### Grammar points
- **A real-time tú/vos switch within a single speech**, directly attested and explicitly flagged by Pountain as "rather surprising": Aristotle addresses his pupil as *vos* at the start of the speech (O MS: *sodes, tenedes, quisierdes, auedes*) but the **same MS switches to tú partway through** the speech; the P MS switches even earlier; the later G' MS regularizes to *tú* throughout. Pountain's gloss: *tú* is "the expected form of address from a respected tutor to a young pupil," while *vos* is "the form of respect used to the king" — i.e. the O/P scribes may be recording a genuine register-slippage or simply inconsistent copying, but either way this is a direct textual snapshot of the tú/vos system *while it was still unstable*, independently corroborating Penny's ch.3 account (3.5.1.1) of early Old Spanish address-form variability.
- **/lj/ > [ʎ] in non-Castilian MS vs [ʎ]→Ø/merger in Castilian**: the O MS consistently shows the Castilian-specific outcome (*fijo*, not *fillo*) while a parallel manuscript tradition (G') keeps the more conservative Leonese/Aragonese-type [ʎ] outcome for the identical word — i.e. this single textual tradition directly preserves *both* branches of a single dialect split within its own manuscript stemma.
- **The future subjunctive attested in its generative syntactic environment**: *quisierdes* (< QUAESIVERITIS, future perfect indicative) is used here specifically in the protasis of a future-referring conditional — Pountain flags this as "in all likelihood the basis of" the whole future-subjunctive category's eventual grammaticalization, i.e. the same kind of "one specific construction first" bootstrapping mechanism Penny's ch.3 identifies for the conditional and the pluperfect-for-imperfect-subjunctive replacement.
- **Clitic pronoun separated from its verb by an intervening word** (*si lo assy fezieres*; *desque se ombre buelve*) — a word-order freedom explicitly impossible in Modern Spanish, directly paralleling the same phenomenon independently flagged in Texts 10 and 15.
- **Amelioration-through-self-deprecation, independently attested for *caballo/cavallería*** (CABALLUS 'nag, workhorse' in Classical Latin → generic 'horse' in all Western Romance, generalized without the original pejorative force) — the *same* mechanism Pountain cross-references at Texts 3, 15, 22, 25, 29, and that Penny's ch.5 independently names as his own flagship amelioration case. This convergence (two independently-written books both reaching for CABALLU/*caballo* as *the* textbook amelioration example) is itself a useful cross-source-agreement data point.

## Text 10 — The Moorish invasion of Spain, Alfonso X, *Primera crónica general* (late 13th c.)
**[POUNTAIN]** Source: Pountain, Text 10, printed pp.83-89. Compiled in stages (chs.1-108 c.1270; rest revised under Sancho IV); this extract (ch.556) is from the later, more mature "castellano drecho" style. Escorial MS Y.i.2, transcribed per Kasten et al. (1997).

**Attested Era:** composition late 13th c. (extract specifically post-1270, "mature" Alfonsine style). **Attested Region:** Toledo (the explicit prestige-norm locus established in ch.1/Text-series introduction).

### Grammar points
- **"Castellano drecho" (standardized Alfonsine Castilian) still shows residual final-/e/ apocope** in cliticized contexts (*mandol* = *mandóle*, *yl* = *y le*, *aquend* = *aquende*), even though the standardized register generally resists the more extensive apocope found in rougher northern texts — i.e. apocope survives longest precisely in the highest-frequency grammaticalized/cliticized collocations.
- **Early, not-yet-general "personal a"**: *dexó ... a Tarif Abenciet* (with personal *a*) alongside *enuió contra ellos un su sobrino* (no personal *a*, despite a human direct-object-equivalent) in the *same text* — direct evidence that personal *a* usage was still optional/variable in the 1270s, favored especially with proper nouns and personal pronouns first.
- **Leísmo attested directly and dated**: *mataron le*, *siemprel vencien* use *le* (not *lo*) as a personal masculine direct object — Pountain notes leísmo "is of long standing in Castilian, and continues in areas of Old Castile to the present day," and connects it causally to the personal-*a* phenomenon (both triggered by the *personal* nature of the referent). Here *le* is even extended **beyond** personal reference to a place-name antecedent (*dizen le* 'they call it [this hill]').
- **Clitic-pronoun-position freedom, again**: *ge lo non entendiessen* shows the negator *non* positioned *between* the clitic sequence and the verb — a northern-text feature Pountain notes is specifically **not** general castellano-drecho usage, flagging a genuine stylistic/dialectal inconsistency within even this "standard" chronicle text.
- **"Ethical dative" use of *ge* (later *se*)**: the same *ge lo non entendiessen* example is glossed as literally "so that they should not realize it **concerning him**" — Pountain names this a heavily-exploited Castilian device (an oblique argument added purely for pragmatic/affective color, not core argument structure).
- **ILLI ILLUM > *ge lo* > *se lo*, with an anticipation of Latin-American *rehilamiento***: Pountain traces the OSp. clitic-cluster form *ge lo* (replacing expected *gelo* < *ILLI ILLUM*) through a /ʎ/→[ʒ] change that "anticipates" the modern River Plate *rehilamiento* phenomenon by some seven centuries — directly relevant as a case of a *regional modern* sound-pattern having a genuine *medieval* structural precedent, not being a purely 19th/20th-c. innovation.
- **Verbs taking *ser* (not *haber*) as perfect auxiliary for intransitives**, with participle agreement in gender/number with the subject (*eran tornados viles* — a true pluperfect-with-ser): Pountain notes the class of *ser*-auxiliary verbs shrinks steadily until by the end of the 16th c. only *nacer, morir, ir, pasar* still take it, before *haber* finally generalizes to every verb completely.
- **"Short" possessive + indefinite article**: *un su sobrino* 'a nephew of his' (lit. 'one his nephew') — a construction later replaced by the postposed "long" possessive (*un sobrino suyo*), directly matching Penny's ch.3 possessive-reinforcement discussion.
- **Topicalization and verb-first word order**, both flagged as genuinely Spanish (not simply Latin-influenced) syntactic resources that persist into the modern spoken language (cross-referenced forward by Pountain to Texts 25 and 28).

## Text 15 — Mad with love, Fernando de Rojas, *La Celestina* (1499)
**[POUNTAIN]** Source: Pountain, Text 15, printed pp.115-120. First known edition (Fadrique de Basilea, Burgos, c.1499); transcription per Corfis and O'Neill (1997).

**Attested Era:** 1499 (first printed edition). **Attested Region:** general Castilian (Burgos printing, no strong regional markers flagged).

### Grammar points
- **Semipopular simplification of a learned consonant cluster**: *imperfecion* for *imperfección* shows /kts/ → /s/-ish simplification of a recent Latinate loan — direct evidence that even *newly*-borrowed learned words are immediately subject to the same semipopular erosion pressure as older ones (cross-ref Penny's ch.2 learned/semi-learned distinction).
- **Doublet past participles, one popular-phonetic and one learned/regularized, with the *adjectival* slot always going to the older/shorter form**: *maldito* (semipopular, adjectival use only) vs analogically-regularized *maldecido* (verbal use) — Pountain generalizes this as a recurring Castilian pattern (also *absorbido*/*absorto*, *corrompido*/*corrupto*): **whichever participle form is older/more phonetically reduced tends to survive only in frozen adjectival use, while the verbal/dynamic use gets a newer analogically-regular participle.**
- **Initial /s/ > /ʃ/ in a residual word-class**: *ximio* (> *simio*) 'monkey' is cited as one of several words showing this otherwise-unexplained development, with three competing explanatory hypotheses given (Mozarabic borrowing; back-formation from a related form phonetically altered for other reasons, e.g. *jugo*/*enjugar*; or simply free variation among the three then-very-close OSp. voiceless sibilants) — flagged by Pountain as a genuinely **unresolved** historical-linguistics question, not a settled fact (methodologically useful to note which claims in this literature are contested vs settled).
- **No subjunctive-after-negated-verb-of-saying rule yet**: *no creo* and *no pensaua* both govern an *indicative* complement here, where the Modern Spanish rule would require subjunctive — direct evidence this now-standard syntactic rule postdates 1499.
- **Latinate "accusative + infinitive" syntax used productively in vernacular prose** (*el que confiessas ser dios*; *se juzgaron ser dignos*) — explicitly flagged as a feature of *cultured* prose register, not ordinary spoken syntax, and noted as sometimes **not** cleanly transposable into fully natural Modern Spanish (the awkwardness of *el que confiesas que es Dios* as a "fix" is explicitly discussed) — a good register-tagging data point (`Usage Tier: literary/formal`).
- **mas / pero / sino — the three-way adversative-conjunction history**, laid out as an explicit relative-frequency shift: OSp. *mas* (< MAGIS 'greater'→'rather') was the default general adversative term, covering functions later split between *pero* (< PER HOC 'therefore'→'nevertheless', originally narrower, only *restrictive* 'nevertheless') and *sino* (< SI NON 'if not'→'except', originally only marking exceptions-to-negatives); by 1499 *pero* is already "rivaling" *mas* in general adversative use and *sino* is rivaling it in the *contrastive-after-negation* use specifically; *mas* itself survives into Modern Spanish only as an archaic/literary register marker, obsolete in ordinary speech.

## Text 18 — The etiquette of address, Correas, *Arte de la lengua española castellana* (1625)
**[POUNTAIN]** Source: Pountain, Text 18, printed pp.133-137. Correas (1570/71-1631), professor of Greek and Hebrew at Salamanca; unpublished in his own lifetime, surviving as a corrected press-copy MS (Biblioteca Nacional MS 18969), edited by Alarcos García (1954).

**Attested Era:** composed 1625 (per the *Arte*'s own date; Correas's separate *Ortografía castellana* of 1630 is cited alongside for corroborating sociolinguistic detail). **Attested Region:** Correas himself from Jaraíz (Plasencia), career based in Salamanca; the *Arte* explicitly also reports *regional* variation (Aragón, Seville) as part of its own descriptive content — making this an unusually self-aware early sociolinguistic source.

### Grammar points — THE SINGLE MOST VALUABLE ADDRESS-FORMS TEXT IN THIS DATASET
- **Correas gives an explicit emic four-way system of address, directly in his own words, for four hearer "qualities": *vuestra merced* / *él* / *vos* / *tú***:
  - ***vuestra merced*** — for judges, gentry ("cavalleros"), clergy, ladies, and "xente de capa negra" (black-cape people, i.e. professionals/the educated middle rank) — "lo mas después de señoría" (the highest form after *señoría* itself).
  - ***él*** (3rd person used as 2nd-person address) — used by *older* people toward someone they don't wish to honor with *merced* but also consider too high-status for *vos*; explicitly glossed as "typical of masters to servants"; **also** used by common/village people (who don't have the habit of using *merced* at all) toward anyone *within their own class* whom they wish to honor; **also** usable *pejoratively*, toward someone one is quarreling with.
  - ***vos*** — (a) between friends with no "gravedad ni cumplimiento" (gravity or ceremony); (b) masters to grown servants/workers and to laborers; (c) adults to older children; (d) to the king, "with due respect, by old usage" (i.e. *vos* is simultaneously the *most* deferential archaic-royal register *and* a plain-familiar-friend register — a genuinely bifurcated distribution); (e) *pejoratively*, as an insult (presumably directed at someone who would normally merit *merced*).
  - ***tú*** — children, younger family members, and loved ones ("a los que se quisieren bien").
  - Correas **explicitly names the grammatical-agreement anomaly** this produces (directly paralleling, and in fact historically *prior to*, Penny's modern description of the identical phenomenon): since *merced* and *él* are grammatically 3rd person and *vos* is grammatically 2nd-person-*plural*, but all three refer to a single present 2nd-person addressee, Correas calls this "discordante concordia" (disconcordant concord) and explicitly flags that it violates the "orden natural" of person/gender/number that (he says) Latin enshrines correctly.
- **Correas as a self-aware sociolinguist, not just a grammarian — directly relevant methodological content for this project's own source-reliability practice.** Pountain's introduction documents several of Correas's own explicit sociolinguistic observations from his *Ortografía*: *ceceo* coexists with *seseo* in Seville and *ceceo* speakers are mocked by their neighbors (i.e. direct period evidence of *stigmatized* vs *prestige* phonological variants within a single city, predating modern sociolinguistics by 300+ years); *cautivar*/*rescatar* undergo valency-changing passive-like use specifically restricted to "xente de gherra" (soldiers' jargon); Correas rejects the use of Latin/Italian-influenced "jerigonza" (jargon/gibberish) among self-styled "cortesanos críticos" as affectedly obscure. Correas's own normative baseline is explicitly the usage of "la xente de mediana i menor talla" (people of middling-to-lesser social rank) — he rejects *both* low-class ("grosero", "tosco") *and* over-educated/academic ("cortesano", "estudiantado", "de escuelas") usage as deviations from this middle norm, while being *descriptively* tolerant of regional variation he doesn't personally use (explicitly noting that Aragonese *el aquel* is not considered "grosero" there, and praising the archaic-but-rustic *ende* as "antiguo i mui elegante").
- **Correas's spelling already shows the sibilant system essentially fully modern**: *z* used consistently (no more /ts/ vs /dz/ distinction); *x* corresponds to both earlier *x* and *j* (merger of /ʃ/ and /ʒ/ complete); *b*/*v* distinction maintained in writing but Correas himself seems unsure *why* (Pountain discusses several candidate explanations, including the possibility this was already a purely orthographic/homonym-distinguishing convention with no remaining phonetic basis, e.g. his claim that *voz* 'word' and *boz* 'tone of voice' should be kept distinct despite both deriving from the same Latin VOCE).

## Text 22 — Streetwise in Seville, Quevedo, *El Buscón* (1626) — FLAGSHIP SLANG TEXT
**[POUNTAIN]** Source: Pountain, Text 22, printed pp.159-166. Two parallel versions given: the 1629 Rouen printed edition and the early-17th-c. "MS B" manuscript (Lázaro Carreter 1980 transcription), collated side by side by Pountain to show real orthographic variation. This passage is explicitly singled out by Pountain as a major primary source for ***germanía*** (thieves'/underworld slang/cant), and Quevedo is noted as "one of very few poets to write romances using this jargon."

**Attested Era:** 1626 (first printed edition; the underlying MS tradition and the germanía register itself are of course somewhat older, contemporary spoken low-register Golden-Age Spanish). **Attested Region:** Seville specifically (the dialogue is explicitly staged as teaching the protagonist Sevillian underworld speech-norms, including a specific regional phonological performance).

### Grammar points
- **Explicit in-text metalinguistic coaching on regional phonology, staged as slang-acquisition**: the character Matorral explicitly instructs the protagonist Pablos to invert Madrid-/h/ (←Lat. F-) to [x] and standard /x/ to [h] — i.e. the text *dramatizes*, as an initiation-into-slang-and-local-identity act, exactly the /h/-for-/x/ and /x/-for-/h/ swap that independently characterizes real documented Andalusian and Latin-American dialectal speech (Penny ch.2.6 cross-ref). This is a uniquely explicit piece of evidence that period speakers *themselves* perceived regional phonology as a learnable, performable *social marker*, not just an unconscious habit — directly relevant methodologically for any slang-register-acquisition modeling.
- **b/v, c/ç/z, x/j all shown as genuinely free-varying in the spelling**, confirming that by 1626 none of these distinctions had any remaining phonemic reality for most writers — direct, dated confirmation of several of the ch.2 sibilant-collapse and b/v-merger end-states (printed edition vs MS B disagree with each other in both directions on several of these, which Pountain treats as itself diagnostic of the underlying mergers' completeness).
- ***Vuestra merced* abbreviated all the way down to *buace*/*buce*** within Matorral's own slang-coaching speech specifically — Pountain flags this as *further* contraction evidence (beyond the already-contracted forms Penny's ch.3 lists) and explicitly cross-references it as evidence for the overall *vuestra merced* > *usted* reduction chain, here caught specifically in a *low-register/slang* speech context rather than a formal one.
- **Overt pronoun subjects with non-finite verb forms used specifically for disambiguation clarity** (*declarando yo*..., *no te fíes hombre en dar tu la varaja*) — a genuine syntactic-pragmatic device, not an error.
- **Stative vs dynamic passive, directly contrasted within one sentence**: *estarán mas avisados los ignorantes* (stative: result-state passive, 'will [have been/be in the state of being] warned') vs *serán engañados por su culpa* (dynamic: event passive, 'will [on occasion] be deceived') — a clean minimal-pair illustration of the ser/estar passive distinction fully in place by 1626.
- **Future subjunctive still fully productive** in its two classic syntactic environments: protasis of future-conditionals (*si tratares con gente honrada*) and future-referring relative clauses with indefinite antecedents (*los que leyeren mi libro*) — directly continuing the pattern already seen grammaticalizing in Text 9 three centuries earlier.

## Text 23 — A policy for linguistic standardisation, *Diccionario de autoridades* (1726)
**[POUNTAIN]** Source: Pountain, Text 23, printed pp.167-171. The Real Academia Española's first dictionary (1726-39), explicitly modeled partly on Covarrubias's earlier dictionary and the Italian *Vocabolario della Crusca* (1691 3rd edition).

**Attested Era:** 1726 (volume I, prologue). **Attested Region:** Madrid (Real Academia Española).

### Grammar/lexicographic-policy points
- **Explicit scope decision directly relevant to this project's own slang/register methodology**: the RAE's prologue states the dictionary *includes* obsolete words and regional words not in common Castilian use, **but explicitly excludes taboo words, proper nouns, and technical science/arts vocabulary** — and *does* include words "characterised as *jerigonza* o *germanía*" (i.e. the RAE consciously chose to document thieves'-cant/underworld-slang vocabulary as a legitimate lexicographic category, while taboo vocabulary proper was excluded on separate grounds). This is a direct, dateable (1726) institutional precedent for the taboo-vs-slang distinction this project's own `Usage Tier` taxonomy (`taboo` vs `slang`) is built on.
- **Spelling-reform policy decisions, several with long-term consequences still visible in Modern Spanish**: *u* only for /u/, *v* only for /b/ (but the *b*/*v* letter-choice itself remains explicitly **etymological, not phonemic**, since the RAE prologue itself says Spanish speakers "confuse v and b por la poca advertencia" — i.e. the Academy already knew in 1726 that the distinction was purely historical/orthographic, not a live phonemic contrast, and chose to keep it anyway); *ç* suppressed in favor of context-sensitive *c*/*z*; *s*/*ss* distinction retained in this dictionary but abandoned in the 1763 *Ortografía*; *th*/*ch*/*ph* for Greek-derived words (abandoned 1763); *qu* for /kw/ retained until 1815. The dictionary generally **prefers the Latinate spelling variant** of learned words over the popular/eroded one (*efecto* over *efeto*) **unless** that would create an articulatorily-awkward cluster (*sustancia* preferred over *substancia*) — a clean, explicitly-stated **phonotactic-naturalness-overrides-etymological-purism** editorial principle.
- **Context-sensitive letter/phoneme mapping already fully systematized by 1726**: /θ/ spelled *c* before front vowels but *z* before back vowels; /x/ spelled *g* before front vowels but *j* before back vowels — i.e. the Modern Spanish orthographic system's core consonant rules are already fixed at this date, three centuries before the present.

## Text 29 — The gaucho conscript, Hernández, *Martín Fierro* (1872, Argentina)
**[POUNTAIN]** Source: Pountain, Text 29, printed pp.199-203. First edition (1872), reproduced in facsimile by Carrino et al. (1974); Hernández's own non-standard spelling is preserved by Pountain as a direct attempt to represent gaucho phonology.

**Attested Era:** 1872. **Attested Region:** the Argentine pampas (gaucho — white/Indian mestizo cattlemen culture).

### Grammar points
- **A deliberately phonetic non-standard spelling system used to encode real dialect features**: *enderesé* (seseo), *usté* (final -d loss), *pa* (reduced *para*), *maniador*/*escarciando* (hiatus-avoidance via glide-insertion), *crerá* (/ee/ > /e/ reduction), *juerte*/*dijunto* (aspirate [h] for /f/, directly paralleling the Andalusian pattern already seen dramatized in Text 22 three centuries earlier — a genuine trans-Atlantic persistence of the *same* historical sound-change path), *güeltas* (the [gw]-for-[bw]/[w] reinforcement pattern independently noted in several regional varieties).
- **Reflexive-vs-non-reflexive valency reversal as a systematic Latin-American/Peninsular contrast**: *enderesé* (reflexive) corresponds to standard *me enderecé*; Pountain (citing Kany 1951) lists an entire class of verbs (*adelantar, amanecer, aparecer, atrasar, cambiar, crecer, demorar, devolver, dilatar, enfermar, huir, importar, recordar, regresar, soñar (con), tardar, venir*) that are non-reflexive in Peninsular standard but regularly used reflexively in Latin-American varieties — a ready-made checklist for any valency-pattern dialect-tagging in this project's grammar data.
- **Affective diminutive *-ito* used with an intensive ('every single') rather than diminutive sense**: *tuito* (= *todito*), *toditas* — explicitly flagged by Pountain as a Latin-American-preferred diminutive strategy.
- **Amelioration-through-self-deprecation, independently re-attested for a *third* time in this dataset** (after Texts 3 and 9/Penny ch.5): *matucho* 'an old, worn-out horse' used affectionately/ironically by the gaucho about his own prized horse — Pountain explicitly calls this "the process of amelioration through self-deprecation by which Latin CABALLUS... became the standard word for 'horse'."

## Texts 35/36 — Caló (Borrow 1843; Valle-Inclán 1928) — FLAGSHIP SLANG/CONTACT TEXT
**[POUNTAIN]** Source: Pountain, Texts 35-36, printed pp.228-234. *Caló* is the historically-Romani-lexified, Spanish-grammared cant of Spanish Gypsies (*gitanos*); Text 35 is George Borrow's 1843 field-collected translation of the Apostles' Creed into an unusually Romani-dense "showpiece" register of Córdoba caló; Text 36 is a *literary* (not field-recorded) caló dialogue from Valle-Inclán's 1928 novel *El ruedo ibérico: Viva mi dueño*.

**Attested Era:** Text 35, 1843 (mid-19th-c. Córdoba caló, recorded live by Borrow from a group of Gitanos); Text 36, 1928 (literary composition, not primary dialect recording — Pountain explicitly flags that Valle-Inclán's caló dialogue should be read as *art* rather than unmediated field data, though still linguistically informative). **Attested Region:** Córdoba (35); unspecified Andalusia, stylized (36).

### Grammar points — structural profile of caló as a contact phenomenon
- **Caló = Romani lexis grafted onto fully Spanish grammar, not the reverse**, and Pountain gives an explicit structural argument for why: lexical borrowing on this scale is far easier to achieve than syntactic borrowing, so the historically far more plausible scenario is Romani vocabulary layered onto a morphosyntactic base that became Spanish relatively quickly after the Gitanos' arrival (attested in the Peninsula from the 15th c.; an early-17th-c. account already reports that Spanish gitanos no longer speak Romani at all) — directly useful as a template for modeling how any "slang register built on a substrate vocabulary layer" might be structured for this project's conlang-synthesis purposes.
- **Verbal morphology is entirely Spanish**: Romani verb roots are uniformly drafted into the Spanish *-ar* conjugation (all person/tense/aspect marking is standard Spanish); even an irregular-looking form like *querdi* (past participle of *querar* 'to do/make') is simply an irregular Spanish-style participle on a Romani root, not an imported Romani verb-form.
- **A frozen Romani verbal infix, reanalyzed as part of the root**: Romani's own 3rd-person verb-ending *-el-* survives in caló not as a live inflectional slot but *fossilized into the stem itself*, producing synchronic pairs that differ only by presence/absence of *-el-* and that speakers apparently reinterpret as an **aspectual** (more-intensive-action) contrast rather than a person-marking one (*sinar* 'to be' vs *sinelar*-type forms; *currar* 'to work, beat' vs *currelar* 'to slog away', the latter's whole derivational family — *currante, curre, currele, currelo* — having passed into mainstream colloquial Peninsular Spanish). **This is a clean, independently-documented example of a fossilized inflectional morpheme being reanalyzed as a derivational/aspectual one purely through loss of its original paradigmatic function** — directly reusable as a conlang-design mechanism.
- **Romani gender-marking suffixes survive as recognizable (but non-productive) residue layered onto an otherwise fully Spanish noun-gender system**: masc. *-ó* (*chinoró* 'young', *los grécos* 'sins'), fem. *-í* (*la Majarí* 'the Virgin', *la Cangrí* 'the Church'); plurals are formed with Spanish */s/* in general, but one Romani plural marker (*-e*, in nominalized masculine adjectives, e.g. *los mulés* 'the dead' from singular *mulo*) survives as a frozen irregular residue — a textbook case of an *inherited inflectional subsystem decaying into a closed irregular class* rather than disappearing cleanly, directly comparable to Spanish's own residual strong-verb classes (cross-ref Penny ch.3 preterite material above).
- **The "personal a" and full Spanish word order are both taken over wholesale** — caló syntax is not distinguishable from Spanish syntax at the clause level at all; Pountain is explicit that "the syntax and morphology are entirely Spanish" in both texts.
- **Hybrid word-formation: half the word Romani-replaced, half kept Spanish**, a distinct and specific caló word-formation device independent of the simple-substitution pattern above: *rebridaque* (caló slang for 'flirtatious flattery') is built by taking the Spanish word *requiebro* (itself *re-* + *quiebro*, a nominalization of *quebrar* 'to break'), keeping the *re-* half Spanish, and replacing the *quiebro* half with the Romani-sourced *bridaque* (from *bridaquelar* 'to break') — i.e. caló can perform *morpheme-by-morpheme* substitution within a single native Spanish derivational structure, not just whole-word substitution.
- **Caló words that have crossed over into mainstream Peninsular colloquial Spanish, several directly relevant to this project's slang-synthesis purpose**: *chaval* 'kid, lad' (< Romani vocative plural *chavale* of *chavó* 'boy'); *guillarse* 'to go crazy (about someone)' / *guillárselas* 'to run off, beat it' (< *guillar* 'he went'); *currar*/*currelar* 'to work' and its whole derivational family (above); *dar mulé* 'to kill, bump off' (< *mulo* 'dead'); *chipén*/*chachi*/*chanchi* 'great, terrific' (< *chachipén* 'truth'); *choro* 'pickpocket' (< *chorí* 'thief'); *pal* (English, not Spanish — but noted by Pountain as cognate with the caló/Romani root in *planoró* 'brother', an independently interesting case of a shared Romani-derived word surviving separately in English slang and Spanish caló).

## Cross-cutting note: convergent findings between Penny and Pountain

Both books, written independently, converge on **CABALLU > caballo** (Lat. 'nag, workhorse' → generic 'horse') as *the* textbook illustration of **amelioration through self-deprecation** — Penny names it explicitly in ch.5.3.2 (p.316) as his flagship amelioration example; Pountain cross-references the identical mechanism **four separate times** across unrelated texts in his own book (Texts 3, 9, 15, 22, 25, 29 all get a "see also" note pointing to this same case). Independent convergence on the same flagship example, from two unrelated authors working from different primary evidence, is itself a mild corroborating signal for treating CABALLU/*caballo* as a maximally citable, well-established case for any future mechanics-analysis or worked-example use in this project.

Both books also independently converge on the **vuestra merced > usted contraction chain** as the paradigm case of grammaticalized-politeness-formula erosion, and Pountain's text-by-text attestations (Text 1's *tvam claritatem* precursor-device, Text 18's full Correas four-way system with explicit *discordante concordia* commentary, Text 22's slang-register *buace/buce* extreme-contraction) let this project date several intermediate stages of that single chain far more precisely than Penny's chapter-level treatment alone could.

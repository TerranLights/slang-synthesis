## Grammar / sociolinguistic points

### Standardization as a cross-cutting process (2.0)
Standardization is framed as never fully complete in any language, but farthest advanced in
written orthography and weakest in spoken norms. Spanish lacks a single agreed panhispanic norm
(unlike, per Butt & Benjamin, French/German's single metropolitan standards) — multiple competing
"prestige norms" coexist, tied to different capital cities, and some are actively losing ground
(Bogotá, Lima) while others hold (Mexico City, Buenos Aires) (pp.14-15).

### Academies as prescriptive authority, and their declining role (2.0.0)
Traces RAE's authority from Alfonso X (13th c.) through the 1713 founding of the RAE itself, to a
20th-century loss of prestige. The RAE's own dictionary (DRAE) is criticized from both directions:
purists object to vulgar admissions (*gilipollas*); descriptivists object that it still omits
widely-used real vocabulary. The chapter frames the rise of the corpus-based *CREA* project and the
appointment of a descriptivism-oriented director (Lázaro Carreter, 1991) as marking "the end of an
era" for RAE prescriptivism (pp.17-19).

### Style guides (*libros de estilo*) as the new de facto prescriptive authority (2.0.0-2.0.1)
As the RAE's authority weakened, in-house media style guides (EFE's *Manual de español urgente*,
El País's, ABC's, regional broadcasters' like Telemadrid and Canal Sur) became the practical
arbiters of "correct" usage — notably still staffed/chaired by RAE members, so RAE influence
persists indirectly even as its direct authority declines. Guides vary sharply in how prescriptive
vs. tolerant they are (contrast Telemadrid's highly prescriptive phonological rulebook against
Canal Sur's explicit defense of Andalusian as non-inferior) (pp.21-27).

### Non-sexist language reform, three-part framework (2.0.3)
The chapter organizes 1988-91 Spanish non-sexist-language guidelines into three categories: (1)
avoiding masculine-as-generic (*hombre* → *persona*/*ser humano*; *los profesores* →
*el profesorado* or *las profesoras y los profesores*), (2) correcting asymmetrical reference to
men vs. women (honorific asymmetry *Señor* vs. *Señora/Señorita*; proposing *Sa.* on the model of
English "Ms."; alternating gender order in text), and (3) feminizing professional-status titles
(*la médica*, *la jueza*, *licenciada*) — the most contested category, since it extends to
grammatically-unmarked nouns like *juez*, *gerente*, *jefe* that some speakers feel shouldn't take a
feminine form at all (pp.31-34). Real-world adoption is explicitly described as "patchy," with
masculine/feminine/mixed forms sometimes coexisting within the same newspaper article (p.34
Fuertes Olivera example re: "primer ministro/Primera Ministro/primera Ministra" for Thatcher).

### Administrative-register reform away from archaism (2.0.2)
Spain's 1990 *Manual de estilo del lenguaje administrativo* is presented as a direct, delayed (1988
Ombudsman-report-triggered) response to Francoist-era bureaucratic language, targeting: archaic
honorifics (V.I./V.S. → Vd.), the future subjunctive, superfluous *y*, pseudo-legal present
participles, the "State Gazette gerund," empty *muletillas*, excessive adjective-stacking/pre-posed
adjectives, and depersonalized third-person self-reference (*Esta delegación...* → *Le
comunico...*) — all framed as an attempt to project a more egalitarian citizen/state relationship
post-Franco (pp.28-31).

### Borrowing/calquing as the main non-internal axis of change (2.0.1 end)
The author explicitly separates "internal" Spanish-internal standardization battles (leísmo/
loísmo/laísmo, dequeísmo/queísmo — named but not detailed here; full treatment is in Ch.5
morphosyntax, see 028b) from "external" change driven by French and English influence (calques,
anglicisms, the conditional of allegation, overuse of the gerund) (p.28).

### Dialectology shifting from rural/written to urban/spoken corpora (2.1)
Traces a methodological shift: early dialect atlases (ALPI) deliberately sought "pure" rural,
elderly, male, illiterate informants; later projects (the norma culta multi-city project, Val.Es.Co.)
shifted focus to urban, educated, and eventually informal/colloquial spoken registers, enabled by
tape-recording technology (pp.37-39).

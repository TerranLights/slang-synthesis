## Grammar / sociolinguistic-concepts points

### Field / Tenor / Mode — the book's register framework

The book organizes "register" (as distinct from *dialect*, which marks geographic/social origin) into
three independent dimensions, following Halliday as mediated through Romaine (1994: 20): *"While
regional dialect reveals where we come from and social dialect what our status is, register gives a clue
about what we are doing."*

- **Field** — the subject-matter/activity in progress, realized mainly through specialized vocabulary
  and jargon, but also through grammatical choices tied to a domain (e.g. legal Spanish's archaic future
  subjunctive, infinitive-as-imperative in recipes, long multiply-embedded sentences in bureaucratic/legal
  documents).
- **Tenor** — the relationship between the participants: familiarity, relative status, formality,
  politeness, and emotional stance. The book places its entire discussion of Spanish second-person address
  (tú/usted/vos, voseo's uneven Latin American distribution, Venezuela/Costa Rica's intimate usted, the
  post-Franco tú/vosotros surge as a marker of anti-authoritarian solidarity) under this heading — tenor,
  not grammar per se, is framed as the real governing variable in choosing an address form.
- **Mode** — the channel of communication (spoken vs. written, plus hybrid/intermediate modes: a read-aloud
  speech, a telephone call, e-mail). The book's extended worked example is a literal transcript of
  spontaneous spoken Spanish (a Vallecas chabolismo interview, BBC 1977) analyzed for exactly the features
  that mark speech as structurally different from writing: self-correction without deletion (*"el ... la
  luz"* self-correcting gender), false starts and re-phrasing, filler words (*bueno, pues, o sea*) and
  transcribed pauses, deictic reliance on shared physical context (*"al estar esto aquí"*), tag questions
  (*¿no?*) soliciting listener engagement, and genuinely "ungrammatical" constructions (missing finite
  verbs, dangling transitive verbs, unclear antecedents for *se*) that are entirely normal in speech and
  would be flagged as errors only if transplanted into writing.

This is a **distinct framework from the R1/R2/R3 scale** used in the sibling book "Using Spanish": Field/
Tenor/Mode names *which kind* of contextual variable is driving a register choice, while R1/R2/R3 places
any given usage on a single formality ladder. The two are complementary, not competing — a given
utterance can be assigned a Field (e.g. legal), a Tenor (e.g. distant/formal), a Mode (written), *and* an
R-value (e.g. R3) simultaneously.

### Regional/social dialectology (Ch.4)

The book argues Spain's own dialectal split (Burgos-Castilian prestige norm vs. the Sevillian/Andalusian
south) is the direct historical ancestor of the broader Spain/Latin-America divergence, because Seville's
role as the organizing port for transatlantic emigration (plus the Canary Islands as a stopover point)
gave the southern variety outsized influence on colonial-era Latin American Spanish — more than the
"most colonizers came from Andalusia" folk explanation the book explicitly flags as an exaggeration.
Within Latin America, the book notes the capital-city dialect of each nation typically becomes that
country's own prestige norm (not a single pan-Latin-American standard), and that areas with the tightest
historical administrative/trade links to Spain (Mexico City, Lima) retained forms closest to Peninsular
Spanish, with peripheral regions diverging more.

**Vallecas/chabolismo case study (social network theory of dialect):** a 1979 BBC documentary on a
Madrid shanty settlement populated by Andalusian migrant labourers is used to demonstrate that regional-accent
retention correlates with *social network complexity*, not simply geographic displacement. Two
housewives and two children (simple, locally-bounded networks) retained clear Andalusian accents; a young
solderer who had built his own home (more outward-facing professional life) had a predominantly
Madrid accent; four politically-active community organizers (complex networks spanning the barrio, media,
and authorities) were mostly Madrid-accented with only the two oldest retaining slight Andalusian traces;
a bishop and a civil servant (fully external, educated roles) spoke standard Castilian, the civil
servant's register additionally marked by dense bureaucratic phrasing the book notes may originally have
been "alien" to the community he served. The pattern: the more complex and outward-reaching a speaker's
social network, the more their speech shifts toward the prestige/standard variety.

### Language attitudes (Ch.5)

The chapter frames language attitudes as independent of any real linguistic superiority/inferiority
between varieties, while documenting how strongly such attitudes are nonetheless held. Key studies cited:

- **The Matched Guise Test** (Lambert et al. 1960, elaborated since): the same speaker, recorded reading
  the same content in two different language varieties/languages, is rated by listeners as if they were
  different people — demonstrating that listener judgments track the *variety*, not the speaker's actual
  traits.
- **Solé (1991), Buenos Aires self-deprecation study:** 59% of Buenos Aires residents surveyed rated their
  own porteño Spanish as "mal español"; 48% believed Spanish is spoken better outside Argentina. Table 5.1
  (reproduced here as a finding, not a verbatim table): when asked which city speaks the best Spanish,
  Madrid was rated highest (42%), followed by Bogotá (13%), Mexico City (10%), Lima (7%), with 15% citing
  various other countries and 13% expressing no opinion/denying a "best" variety exists. Despite this low
  prestige rating, 83% of the same respondents considered their own speech variety an important marker of
  Argentine national identity — ranked second only to "tradition/culture" and ahead of religion. The study
  is used to argue that low *prestige* and high *identity value* for the same linguistic variety are not
  contradictory.
- **Ros et al. (1988), five-community matched-guise study in Spain:** speakers of Castilian, Catalan,
  Basque, Galician, and Valencian reading an identical weather forecast were rated by listeners across five
  trait dimensions (competence/achievement, sociability, personality, morality, identity — Table 5.2).
  Summarized findings: Galician speakers were rated low-competence but high-sociability and high-morality;
  Catalan speakers were rated high-competence but low-sociability, unkind, "stingy," with a strong
  group-identity/out-group-rejecting profile; Basque speakers were rated negatively on sociability but
  strongly on identity; Valencian speakers were rated favorably on sociability/personality; Castilian
  speakers received a comparatively neutral "melting pot" profile. The book frames this as evidence that
  high-ethnolinguistic-vitality minority groups (Catalans, Basques) attract a harsher sociability/morality
  stereotype than lower-vitality groups (Galicians, Valencians), even when both are rated positively on
  identity.
- **Alvar (1986), Cuba accent-recognition study:** Cuban listeners reliably identified Peninsular Spanish
  speakers but frequently misidentified their own compatriots' accents as "Nicaraguan" or generically
  "Latin American" (plausibly reflecting Cuba-Nicaragua political ties during the Sandinista period, versus
  Cuba's broader regional isolation); over 60% preferred the (correctly identified) Peninsular variety when
  asked which they preferred.
- **Indigenous-language attitude studies** (Chile/Mapuche, Fernández and Hernández 1984; Mexico/Nahuatl,
  Hill 1989): both document *ambivalent* rather than simply negative attitudes toward the indigenous mother
  tongue — Mapuche speakers were found to cycle between urban/Spanish-dominant and rural/Mapuche-dominant
  identification depending on life stage/residence; Nahuatl speakers in Mexico reserved Spanish for formal
  public/commercial domains and swearing/"drunk-talk," while reserving Nahuatl (locally "mexicano") for the
  most socially weighty formal rituals (marriage vows, compadre-pledging) — prestige and genuine respect
  diverge for both languages, in opposite directions.

### Media and register (Ch.7)

The chapter documents how Spanish-language television/radio/print media negotiate register and
regional-variety choices under commercial and political pressure:

- **Dubbing vs. subtitling:** dubbing dominates in the Spanish-speaking world (vs. subtitling's dominance
  in English-speaking markets); the book gives a concrete register-mismatch case — Catalan-dubbed versions
  of *Yes, Prime Minister* and *EastEnders* fail to replicate the English-class-specific tenor (upper-class
  civil-service mannerism; working-class East End accent) that the original relies on, producing an effect
  the book compares to "Shakespearean actors" voicing East End characters.
  Dubbing decisions also carry *which* Spanish-language variety gets normalized: heavy use of Venezuelan
  voice actors for imported soaps, and Puerto Rican actors/translators for many US-dubbed programs, each
  risk making one regional variety stand in (misleadingly) for "Latin American Spanish" as a whole.
- **Passive-voice anglicization in journalism:** the book flags heavy use of the Spanish passive
  (fue/fueron + past participle) in wire-service-style news reporting as a direct register effect of
  English-language news-agency originals, contrasted with lower passive use in "more traditional written
  Spanish."
- **Swearword/register controversy (La Vanguardia, Barcelona):** a journalist's faithful direct quotation
  of a civil guard's actual (profane) speech provoked public outrage and a defense of linguistic
  "mediation" over literal "photography" from the then-president of the Real Academia Española, Fernando
  Lázaro Carreter — used as a live case study of how far journalistic register is expected to sanitize
  real spoken Field/Tenor.
- **Tú-form increase in advertising:** post-Franco Spanish advertising shows a marked rise in tú-form
  address, tracking the same societal tú/vosotros-as-solidarity shift discussed under Tenor in Ch.6 — but
  the book notes this trend would be comparatively less effective in parts of Latin America where the
  social meaning of tú/usted differs.

### Gender and language (Ch.8)

The chapter separates **biological gender** (objective), **social gender** (culturally-assigned behavior
norms, variable cross-culturally), and **grammatical gender** (the language's own masculine/feminine/
neuter marking system) as three independent, frequently-misaligned categories. Spanish-specific findings:

- **Masculine-as-generic/unmarked default:** profession nouns (médico, juez, abogado, ingeniero) are
  traditionally masculine regardless of the referent's actual sex; "el hombre" and masculine "él" are used
  for generic/unspecified-person reference; masculine plural covers mixed-sex groups (vosotros for a room
  of women plus one man; los padres for "parents" generically). The chapter frames the move toward feminine
  profession-forms (profesora, ingeniera) and toward gender-neutral collectives (el profesorado) as an
  active, incomplete social change, not a settled rule.
- **Gender-asymmetric lexical-semantic splits:** several masculine/feminine adjective or noun pairs that
  are *not* simple gender-agreement variants but carry different, non-parallel meanings — the clearest and
  most taboo-adjacent being un cualquiera ("a nobody, unemployed man") vs. una cualquiera ("a woman of loose
  morals"); also galante, desenvuelto/a, and honrado/a-honesto/a, each splitting toward a sexual-conduct
  connotation specifically on the feminine form. The chapter credits this pattern (also discussed by Janet
  Holmes for English -ess/-ette suffixes) to an underlying "male form = unmarked/normal" default that marks
  femaleness as deviant.
- **Epicene exception:** -ista agent nouns (recepcionista, pianista, dentista, periodista, marxista) carry
  gender only via the article, offered as a counter-example to the masculine-default pattern.
- **Institutional reform:** Spain's post-1982 Socialist-government-era Manual de estilo del lenguaje
  administrativo and a Education Ministry companion document (Recomendaciones para el uso no sexista de la
  lengua) formally recommend gender-neutral strategies: collective nouns (el profesorado), paired
  masculine+feminine forms (los trabajadores y las trabajadoras), and slash/parenthetical marking
  (alumno(a); D./Doña) — documented via Tinsley (1992) as part of a broader post-Franco gender-equality
  push that the chapter explicitly parallels to the tú/usted solidarity shift discussed in Ch.6.
- **Gendered speech-behavior claims** (after Lakoff 1975 and Holmes 1992, applied cautiously to Spanish):
  the chapter reports — while flagging these as generalizations requiring field-testing, not settled fact —
  claims that women favor more standard/prestige forms, more tag questions, fillers, and intensifiers (e.g.
  the -ísimo superlative), while men are claimed to swear and interrupt more. Two sociolinguistic case
  studies are given where *women specifically* drove a community's language-choice shift: Aymara-speaking
  women around La Paz actively choosing Spanish-language literacy education over Aymara literacy for
  practical status reasons (Archer and Costello 1990); and Galician women historically favoring Castilian
  over Galician as the language of status during a period when male emigration left Galician society
  women-majority.

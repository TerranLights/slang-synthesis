## Grammar points

### Double/multiple negation is grammatical and intensifying

Unlike English, Spanish freely uses double (or even triple/quadruple) negation, and stacking
negatives makes a sentence *more* negative, not less: **No tengo nada en mi bolsa** ("I have nothing
in my bag" — lit. "I don't have nothing"). **No**, **nada**, **nadie**, **nunca/jamás** can each also
be placed directly before the verb instead, in which case **no** is dropped entirely (**Nadie sabe
donde está el tren**; **Él no baila jamás** = **Jamás baila él**) (pp. 125-126).

### Prepositional pronouns: the mí/ti exception and *con* fusion

Prepositions generally take the same form as subject pronouns as their object, **except** the
first- and second-person singular, which use **mí** and **ti** instead of **yo/tú** (**para mí**,
**para ti**) (p. 134). A handful of set expressions break this rule and use **tú**/**yo** directly
after a preposition anyway: **entre tú y yo** ("between you and me"), **menos/excepto tú y yo**
("except you and me"), **según tú** ("according to you"), **incluso yo** ("including me"), **salvo
yo** ("except me"). The only preposition that fuses directly with a pronoun rather than taking a
separate object form is **con**: **conmigo** ("with me"), **contigo** ("with you"), **consigo**
("with him-/her-/themselves").

### *Por* — full meaning inventory and idiom catalog

**Por** covers a distinct cluster of meanings, tabulated here as encountered (pp. 134-136):
**through/by** (*El ladrón sale por la ventana*; *viajar por avión*); **because of / on account of /
for the sake of / out of** (*Ella no quiere hablar por miedo* — "out of fear"); **in exchange
for / in place of**, marking substitution (*Pago diez dólares por este vestido*; *El estudiante
enseña por el profesor* — "in place of the teacher"); **per** (*Él gana quinientos dólares por
semana*); **for** — but *only* when it precedes a quantity of time (*corro por una hora*); and
**por** added to a location preposition to inject a sense of motion-through (*por debajo de la
mesa* "[running] under the table," *por encima del sofá* "over the sofa," vs. static *debajo de* /
*encima de*).

Idiom catalog with **por**: por acá/aquí ("around here"), por allá/allí ("around there"), por ahora
("for now"), por casualidad ("by chance"), por lo común / por costumbre ("usually"), por ejemplo
("for example"), por eso ("therefore"), por favor ("please"), por fin ("finally"), por lo menos ("at
least"), por poco ("almost"), por primera vez ("for the first time"), por supuesto ("of course"),
por todas partes ("everywhere"), por la mañana/tarde/noche ("in the morning/afternoon/evening" —
imprecise time, per the book's own note).

### *Por* and *para* both translate "for" — but never overlap otherwise

The book's own summary rule (p. 136): with the single exception of both sometimes translating as
English "for," **por** and **para** never overlap in meaning. **Para** = "for" covers nearly every
other case (recipient: *El regalo es para su hijo*; deadline/time-limit in the future, translatable
as for/by/on/before: *Necesito la blusa para el viernes*; destination: *El tren sale para el
Canadá*). **Por** = "for" *only* when it directly precedes a quantity of time (*Voy a viajar por
varios meses*; *Dormimos por ocho horas*). This is a clean, restated version of the por/para system
`012_easy_spanish...` — no, rather `012_..._level3.md` (per the checklist) already covers narratively;
useful here as a compact rule-of-thumb form for cross-reference.

### Weather: two competing grammatical frames in one semantic domain

Spanish describes weather using **two different verbs depending on the specific condition**, not
one uniform pattern: **hacer** + noun for temperature/general-condition weather (*Hace calor*, *Hace
sol*, *Hace viento*, *¿Qué tiempo hace?*) vs. **hay** + noun for phenomena construed as "there
is/are" (*Hay neblina* "it's foggy," *Hay nubes* "it's cloudy," *Hay polvo* "it's dusty," *Hay lodo*
"it's muddy," *Hay estrellas/luna* "the stars/moon are out"). Worth flagging for mechanics-analysis
work on how a single semantic domain (weather) can be split across incompatible syntactic frames
with no synchronic logic beyond memorized idiom.

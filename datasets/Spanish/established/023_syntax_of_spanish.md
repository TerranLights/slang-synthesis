## Grammar points

### Chapter 1 — Overview of the grammar (p.1-72)

#### 1.1 Historical/typological framing (p.1-7)
Brief sociolinguistic framing, not strictly syntactic: Spanish's ~300M speakers, dialect geography
(Castilian spread from the Reconquista-era Castille region; Andaluz, Navarro-Aragonese, Leonese,
Asturian dialects in Iberia; River Plate, Andean, Caribbean dialect *areas* rather than discrete
boundaries in Latin America; Ladino/Judeo-español as a conservative "fossil" dialect of 15th-century
expulsion-era Spanish). Syntactic evolution notes (p.5-7): Latin→Romance showed inflectional
simplification (Nom/Acc case collapse, loss of synthetic future/passive), grammaticalization of *se*
(Reflexive → anticausative → passive marker), emergence of "personal a" (originally restricted to
proper names/pronouns, then animate direct objects, extending by the 13th-14th c. to objects
denoting physically/psychologically affected individuals before further generalizing). Old Spanish
differed from Modern Spanish in constituent order: open-class heads (N/A/V) allowed both
complement-head and head-complement order (closed-class heads were already uniformly head-initial);
basic V-O order (not OV) emerged over time; auxiliary-main-verb order flipped from V-Aux to Aux-V;
Old Spanish is argued (Fontana 1993) to be a "symmetric V2" language (verb-second in both main *and*
subordinate clauses, unlike German-type V2) — contrasts with Modern Spanish's non-V2 status. Old
Spanish clitics were true second-position (Wackernagel-style) elements phonologically dependent on
whatever preceded them (verb or not), unlike Modern Spanish clitics, which attach only to verbs.
Auxiliary alternation (*ser* vs. *aver*/*haber*) tracked unaccusative/reflexive-intransitive vs.
transitive predicates in the compound perfect (parallel to French/Italian auxiliary-switch systems),
though inconsistently.

#### 1.2.1 Constituent order (p.7-8)
Spanish is head-initial at the X-bar level: lexical heads (V, N, A, P) precede their complements
(*construyeron un puente*, *leal a los ideales*), and functional heads (determiners, complementizers)
precede the categories they govern. Auxiliaries precede the main verb and this order is rigid
(*Habíamos hablado* / ***Hablado habíamos*). Adjunct order relative to the head is comparatively
flexible, conditioned by structural/lexical properties of the adjunct and the category of the head;
this is picked up again per-category in Chapters 2 (NP), 4 (VP) and 5 (clause).

#### 1.2.2 Case (p.8-15)
Spanish has a Nominative/Accusative case system, morphologically visible only on personal/some
relative pronouns (lexical nouns/determiners show no case morphology). Strong (stressed) pronouns
distinguish Nominative/Objective/Genitive; weak (clitic) pronouns further split Objective into
Accusative (direct object of V), Dative (indirect object of V), and Oblique (object of P). Key
distributional generalizations: Nominative appears on subjects of finite clauses (both indicative and
subjunctive), on predicative NPs agreeing with the clausal subject (*El campeón eres tú*), and on
subjects of non-finite adjunct clauses (participial/gerundive/infinitival absolutive constructions,
e.g. *[Llegada ella] empezó la fiesta*) — contrasted with English, which cannot license a nominative
subject in a parallel non-finite adjunct. Genitive marks the subject of an NP (via *de* for
non-pronominals, via genitive pronoun morphology for pronominals, with weak pre-nominal vs. strong
post-nominal forms: *mis libros* / *los libros míos*); Spanish disallows determiner + pre-nominal
possessive co-occurrence (unlike Italian) and disallows "double genitives" (unlike English *a book of
his*). Relative pronouns show a distinct Genitive form (*cuyo*) but interrogatives do not — Genitive
interrogatives are instead expressed via an in-situ *de quién* phrase, never via fronted *cuyo*-style
movement. Genitive is impossible as subject of an infinitival nominalized clause corresponding to an
English gerund-subject construction; Nominative is used instead (*[El hacer eso tú] sería buena
idea*). Oblique case is assigned by most prepositions and never licenses a clitic double (*Hablaron
de él* / ***le hablaron*, meaning "about him"), unlike Accusative/Dative, which do admit (or require)
clitics. Personal a (p.12-14) is treated as a case-related morpheme marking Accusative when the
direct object is [+animate]/[+specific] (contrast *vi a los vecinos* vs. *vi los papeles*; contrast
*busco a una secretaria* [specific] vs. *busco una secretaria* [non-specific]). It also surfaces with
inanimate direct objects in contexts where both subject and object are inanimate or where a verb like
*llamar* takes a double-Accusative small-clause complement — leading Zubizarreta (1994) to analyze
*a* as fundamentally a unique-Accusative-argument identifier (disambiguator) rather than a pure
animacy marker. Dative is assigned by *a* on indirect objects and co-occurs with a clitic double even
with full lexical (non-pronominal, non-anaphoric) DPs (*Juan le mandó un paquete a José*); the dative
clitic is omissible for some speakers when the transfer denoted by the verb is not asserted as
completed (Demonte 1995) — paralleling the completed/non-completed asymmetry of English dative-shift
constructions.

#### 1.2.3 Inflectional morphology (p.14-15)
Catalogues Spanish's inflectional categories: number/gender (on N, Det, Adj, pronouns, participles),
person (on pronouns and finite verbs), conjugation class (I/II/III: *cantar/temer/escribir*),
tense (present/preterite/imperfect/future), aspect (perfective/progressive periphrases), mood
(indicative/subjunctive), voice (periphrastic passive). Verbs carry by far the richest inflection;
adverbs, prepositions and conjunctions show no inflection at all. Noted quasi-inflectional affixes:
a diminutive suffix and an adjectival intensifier suffix (residue of the Latin superlative).

#### 1.2.4 Clitics (p.15-19)
Full clitic inventory given by case (Nominative: only impersonal *se* "one"; Accusative:
me/te/lo/la/se/nos/os/los/las; Dative: me/te/le/se/nos/os/les). Clitic ordering template (Perlmutter
1971): *se* > 2nd person > 1st person > 3rd-Dative > 3rd-Accusative, with identical-clitic sequences
excluded and *se* replacing *le* before a 3rd-Accusative clitic (le+lo → selo). Impersonal subject
*se* never doubles an overt subject (***uno se trabaja***). Spanish has no clitic for Oblique
(prepositional/locative) arguments at all — unlike French *y*, Spanish uses full adverbial forms
(*allí/ahí/allá*) instead, never a clitic. Clitics attach exclusively to verbs: they follow positive
imperatives, infinitives and gerunds as enclitics (*hazlo*, *mandármelo*, *cantándolo*) but precede
negated imperatives and all other finite verb forms as proclitics (*no lo escriba*; ***escribiólo*
is ungrammatical). Progressive auxiliary+participle constructions allow the clitic either position
(*lo estaba preparando* / *estaba preparándolo*), but past/passive participles never host an
enclitic (***había preparádolo*, ***fue mandádate*). "Restructuring"/clitic climbing lets a clitic
belonging to a subordinate infinitive or (in Spanish specifically, beyond the Italian-shared pattern)
a participle surface on a semi-auxiliary matrix verb instead (*quiere verte* / *te quiere ver*;
*seguía cantándolo* / *lo seguía cantando*). The grammatical analysis of clitic base-generation
position and feature-licensing is flagged as an open research question, picked up again in Ch.4 and
Ch.6 (restructuring and infinitive/participle structure specifically in Ch.6).

#### 1.2.5 Wh- and NP-movement (p.19-20)
Interrogative phrases obligatorily front to clause-initial position in both main and embedded
(indirect) questions; with multiple interrogatives, exactly one constituent fronts and the rest
remain in situ, unless a second clause-initial "landing site" is independently available (long
extraction out of an indirect question, conditioned by the grammatical function of the extracted
constituent — Torrego 1984). NP-movement (passivization, subject raising) is harder to diagnose
surface-structurally in Spanish than in a rigid-order language like English, because post-verbal
subject position is independently available even in simple transitive declaratives (*Analiza las
preposiciones el artículo* — "the article" is a post-verbal subject, not a direct object, since
direct-object position is already filled by *las preposiciones*). This ambiguity between "moved
object now in subject position" and "subject simply sitting in its ordinary post-verbal slot" is
flagged as requiring the structural tools developed in Ch.3/Ch.5.

#### 1.2.6 Determiners (p.20-23)
Indefinite/definite determiner paradigms given (un(o)/una/unos/unas; el/la/los/las), plus the
feminine-*el* allomorphy rule before stressed-initial-*a* feminine nouns (*el agua*). Bare
(determinerless) singular DPs are licensed in negative contexts (*no lee libro sin ilustraciones* vs.
***lee libro sin ilustraciones*). Bare plural DPs are generally barred from pre-verbal subject
position but allowed post-verbally (*llegaron estudiantes* / ***estudiantes llegaron*), with the
pre-verbal restriction lifted when the bare plural is conjoined, contrastively focused, or
left-dislocated as a topic — tied forward to the Topic/Focus movement analysis of Ch.6. Post-verbal
bare plurals are licensed as complements (direct objects, unaccusative subjects) but not as subjects
of ordinary ("unergative") intransitives unless locative inversion applies (***juegan niños* vs. *en
este parque juegan niños*, Torrego 1989) — an early preview of the unaccusative/unergative split
developed fully in Ch.3. Quantifiers (*todos*, *pocos*, *muchos*) are flagged as a debated category
possibly distinct from true determiners on both semantic (logical form) and syntactic (co-occurrence,
modification, movement) grounds, revisited in Ch.2.

#### 1.2.7 Negative concord (p.23-24)
*Nadie salió* / *No salió nadie* are synonymous — a single logical negation realized either as a
negative-quantifier-fronted-with-no-*no*, or as *no* plus a post-verbal negative item; two
pre-verbal negative elements together are ungrammatical (***Nadie no canta nunca*). This "Negative
Concord" pattern (shared across Romance) is distinguished from true double negation and explained via
an agreement/concord relation between *no* and the negative quantifier, rather than two independent
negations. A broader NPI class (non-intrinsically-negative items requiring a negative licensing
context: *todavía* "yet," *en absoluto* "at all," idioms like *mover un dedo* "lift a finger") is
also licensed by *no* and by other non-*no* triggers (certain interrogatives, verbs of lack/doubt/
opposition, comparatives, quantifiers) — overlapping only partially with the Negative-Concord item
set.

#### 1.2.8 Null subjects (p.24-26)
Full catalogue of overt subject pronouns (yo/tú(vos)/él/ella/Usted; nosotros(as)/vosotros(as)/ellos/
ellas/Ustedes) — all obligatorily stressed when overt. Pleonastic ("it"/"there"-type) subjects are
*always* covert, never overt (***Ello es obvio que llovió*; ***Allí hay un unicornio*). Personal
pronominal subjects are covert by default in ordinary discourse-continuation contexts and overt only
under contrastive focus or reference-switching — meaning overt vs. null subject pronouns are not
freely interchangeable truth-conditional variants but carry a discourse-structural contrast (sets up
Ch.5/6's Topic-Focus discussion). The classic Jaeggli (1982)/Rizzi (1982) null-subject parameter
cluster is laid out: (a) null pronominal subjects, (b) null pleonastics, (c) free subject postposing,
(d) absence of COMP-trace effects (*¿Quién crees que vendrá?* grammatical with overt *que*, unlike
English *who do you think that will come*), (e) long-distance extraction of embedded subjects (*¿Quién
no sabes qué escribió?*). The traditional explanation ties all five properties to the richness of
verbal subject-agreement morphology, which supplies a sufficiently "strong" governor for a null
subject position.

#### 1.3.1 Subject order in finite vs. non-finite/non-declarative clauses (p.26-29)
Finite declaratives freely allow S-V-O, V-O-S, and (more marginally/variably, with some
speaker/lexical sensitivity) V-S-O order (contrast the degraded-feeling *?Pintó el artista retratos
terribles* against the fully natural *Sufrió el paciente dolores terribles*). Small clauses (lacking a
verb) likewise allow either subject-predicate or predicate-subject order (*eligieron presidente a
Juan* / *eligieron a Juan presidente*). By contrast, non-finite argument clauses require strictly
post-verbal subjects (***[ella llegada] empezó la fiesta* is out; *[llegada ella]* is required), and
non-declarative finite clause types (imperatives, constituent questions, exclamatives) likewise
require post-verbal subjects (***¿Qué Juan leyó?*). Relative clauses pattern with ordinary
declaratives (pre- or post-verbal subject both fine), not with interrogatives.

#### 1.3.2 Subject distribution in non-finite clauses (p.29-30)
Non-finite adjunct clauses allow Nominative subjects (as already shown). Non-finite *argument*
clauses generally disallow non-Nominative subjects too (no Genitive gerund-subject, no English-style
*for*-complementizer equivalent, no "exceptional case marking" into a believe-type complement:
***Juan cree María ser inteligente*). Causative (*hacer*/*dejar*) and perception-verb infinitival
complements are the systematic exception: their infinitival subject bears the causative/perception
verb's own Accusative or Dative case, with the choice between the two governed by (i) the
transitivity of the embedded infinitive (an Accusative-object infinitive forces a Dative causee, since
two Accusatives cannot co-occur) and (ii) a direct- vs. indirect-causation distinction with
intransitive embedded infinitives (*le hizo leer la carta a José* [Dative, transitive infinitive] vs.
*hará caminar a José* [Accusative, intransitive, direct causation]).

#### 1.3.3 Subject-verb agreement (p.30-32)
Generally person/number agreement obtains between a finite verb and its subject regardless of whether
the subject is overt or covert. Several construction types instead show invariant 3rd-singular
agreement regardless of notional subject: impersonal-*se* clauses (*se trabaja mucho*, never
*trabajan*), non-referential "it/there" constructions (*parece que...*, *es obvio que...*).
Existential *haber* and atmospheric verbs (*llover*) show a "standard" invariant 3rd-singular form but
also admit a substandard rightward-agreeing variant (*habían tres libros*, *llueven monedas*) — noted
as attested but non-standard. Identificational copular sentences require agreement with the
*predicative* element, not a default 3rd-singular (*El culpable soy yo*, not ***es yo*). Passives
(both *ser*+participle and *se*+verb types) show ordinary subject agreement. The *se vende(n) flores*
minimal pair is flagged as genuinely unresolved in the literature: verb agreement with the bare-plural
*flores* suggests it's the grammatical subject, but its bare-plural-subject status is independently
only licensed in complement position (per 1.2.6) — a tension the book leaves open rather than
resolving, attributable to *se*'s multifunctionality (subject clitic / passive morpheme /
anticausative morpheme).

#### 1.4.1 Tense and aspect (p.33-38)
Future tense is ambiguous between temporal-future reference and present-tense epistemic
probability (*Estarán en casa a las dos* = "they'll be home" or "they must be home"); the
periphrastic *ir a*+infinitive is the default way to express genuine temporal future, especially
near-future. The Conditional is argued to be essentially modal/atemporal rather than a true "past of
the future": it requires a past-tense *if*-clause (*si lo necesitaras*, not *si lo necesitas*) and a
past-time counterfactual needs the compound perfect conditional (*habrían prestado...si hubieras
necesitado*) — a bare Conditional like (94) cannot order an event in the past without an adverb
forcing that reading. Imperfect vs. Preterite is analyzed not as a property of the event/state itself
but of whether the *interval* during which it holds is delimited: Imperfect asserts
interval-non-delimitedness (compatible with both single-event "was Ving" and habitual "used to V"
readings, p.35), while Preterite imposes delimitedness on the interval, yielding a completed-event
reading even for predicates (states, activities) that have no inherent endpoint (*Canté*, *Fuiste
amable*, *Dibujé círculos* — all understood as bounded/completed, with an implied subsequent interval
during which the event no longer holds). The Present Perfect (*haber*+participle, no participle
agreement, no auxiliary-switch unlike French/Italian/Catalan) cannot function as a simple past in
main clauses, unlike French/Italian (***han lavado...ayer* is out, though some peninsular speakers
accept proximate-past adverbs like *esta mañana*). Standard "reference point" analysis of compound
tenses: the event is located relative to a reference point, which is itself located relative to
utterance time (distinguishing Present Perfect from Past Perfect by the position of the reference
point). Periphrastic progressive (*estar*+gerund) patterns like English in being incompatible with
non-activity predicates (***la caja está conteniendo papeles*; ***está pareciendo llover*).

#### 1.4.2 Mood (p.38-40)
Indicative generally marks asserted/factual contexts; subjunctive marks doubtful/unknown-factuality
contexts and also contexts where factuality is *presupposed* rather than asserted (factive
complements like *lamentar* "regret," ex. 110). A genuinely optional indicative/subjunctive
alternation reflecting degree of speaker certainty is possible only in *ungoverned* main clauses
expressing possibility (*quizás hayas/has comido*); in subordinate clauses, mood selection is
determined by the governing context, not freely chosen. Temporal adjuncts with *cuando* "when"
require subjunctive when the event is understood as subsequent to speech time (*cenamos cuando
lleguen* [future/subj.] vs. *cenamos cuando llegaron* [past/ind.]) — and a parallel
specific/non-specific-antecedent alternation shows up with *donde* "where" clauses, argued to pattern
with the relative-clause subjunctive-trigger facts (nonspecific antecedent → subjunctive) rather than
being a sui generis temporal-mood rule. In relative clauses specifically, subjunctive appears when the
antecedent is non-specific or a negative existential (*nadie*, *nada*) — picked up again in 1.6.2.

#### 1.4.3 Voice (p.40-42)
True passives (*ser*+participle) show finite-verb agreement with the derived subject, participle
agreement in number/gender, and an optional *por*-agent phrase; passivization is restricted to
Accusative-corresponding arguments in the active (neither Dative/indirect nor Oblique complements can
passivize, with a noted journalese exception for *preguntar*'s indirect object that doesn't
generalize). Bare NPs and definite subjects alike can surface between the participle and a locative
complement in passives (*fue encontrado oro/el oro en el Mar del Norte*). Middle/passive *se*
constructions are homophonous with reflexive *se* (*El coche se movió* = "moved itself" / "was moved"
/ "moved" — three distinct readings of one surface string) but are argued to be structurally and
semantically distinct from true passives: only the passive reading licenses an implicit-agent-oriented
adverb or purpose clause (*se movió voluntariamente* / *para evitar un accidente* only has the passive
reading, never "the car moved voluntarily"); only the passive (not the middle) is degraded in the
simple present with a definite subject (*?*el coche se mueve voluntariamente*); and the middle clitic
combines only with aspectually-perfective transitives, while the passive clitic is indifferent to the
transitive's own delimitedness — hence *tocar* "ring" (inchoative transitive) allows only passive
*se* (*la campana se tocó*) and disallows middle *se* in the present (***la campana se toca* for
"the bell rings").

#### 1.4.4 Modal verbs *poder*/*deber* (p.42-43)
*Poder* "may/be able" and *deber* "must/should" are syntactically ordinary main verbs (full tense/
mood/person inflection, ordinary position relative to negation/auxiliaries) despite their modal
meaning, but on their *epistemic* reading they resist infinitival/compound-perfect embedding under
another predicate — a higher verb like *parecer*/*pensar*/*lamentar* taking *poder*/*deber* as an
infinitival complement forces the root (deontic/ability) reading, never the epistemic
(possibility/necessity) one (ex. 119-123), diagnosing epistemic modals as structurally "higher" than
root modals. These verbs also participate in restructuring (1.2.4): they form a verbal complex with
the following infinitive and license clitic climbing from it.

#### 1.4.5 Negation (p.43-47)
Basic clausal negation is pre-verbal *no*. Contrastive affirmation uses *sí*/*sino* ("but rather");
contrastive-polarity topic structures allow clausal ellipsis after a dislocated topic plus bare
*sí*/*no* (*Juana y Susana llegaron, pero José, no*). *No* is structurally ambiguous in scope between
clausal negation and negation of just a secondary predicate/adjunct (*no comí la zanahoria cruda* =
"I didn't eat the carrot raw-ADV" [secondary-predicate negation only] vs. "It's not the case I ate the
raw carrot" [clausal]). True clausal negation requires *no* immediately pre-verbal (only clitics may
intervene); *no* also precedes infinitives and nominalized-clause heads (*la no intervención*). An
adverb intervening between *no* and the verb forces *constituent* (not clausal) negation of that
adverb specifically, and this reading is only fully natural with quantifier-like adverbs (*siempre*,
*sólo*) — *?Juan no inmediatamente movió el coche* is marginal because *inmediatamente* isn't
quantifier-like. Ordinary subordinate-clause complements are outside the scope of main-clause
negation, EXCEPT for a specific cross-linguistic class of predicates (opinion, expectation, intention/
volition, "perceptive approximation" verbs like *creer*, *parecer*) which show "Neg-raising": *Juan
cree que José no ha llegado* ≈ *Juan no cree que José haya llegado* (Bosque 1980) — flagged as
resisting a clean movement-transformation analysis despite the apparent paraphrase relationship.
Multiple (double) negation is possible but marginal outside Neg-raising contexts, restricted to
auxiliary+main-verb sequences, and yields ordinary positive polarity (true double negation), unlike
Negative Concord's single-negation reading.

#### 1.4.6 Copular sentences: *ser* vs. *estar* (p.47-48)
*Estar*'s complement categories (PP, AP) are a proper subset of *ser*'s (which additionally takes CP
[clefts], DP [equationals], NP [predicate nominals]). The traditional "individual-level vs.
stage-level predicate" account (permanent vs. transient property) is noted as the standard
description but flagged as empirically problematic (Sánchez & Camacho 1995): *ser* is used for
inherently temporary predicates like time-of-day/season (*La reunión es a las ocho*), which an
individual-level account cannot easily explain. The alternative, aspectual account: *estar* is
inherently inchoative, while *ser* has no aspectual content of its own and is "transparent" to
whatever aspectual properties its complement independently has.

#### 1.5.1 Declaratives and information structure (p.48-50)
Two "unmarked" declarative constituent-order templates are both fully grammatical: Subject-Predicate,
or (XP)-Predicate-Subject. The choice tracks information structure (Topic/presupposed vs.
Focus/asserted content) rather than a single fixed grammatical-function-based order — unlike a
rigid-order language like English/French. A preposed XP can be read either as Topic (145a, main
stress inside the following predicate) or as Focus (145b, main stress on the XP itself); this is a
genuine structural/intonational ambiguity resolved by stress placement, not two unrelated
constructions. "Presentational" predicates (introducing the subject's existence/presence into
discourse, e.g. *empezar*, *salir* "come out") resist the Subject-first order, because presentational
semantics requires the *whole clause* (including the subject) to be asserted, which is incompatible
with either a Topic or a Focus-only reading of a preposed subject (*Empezó la resistencia* natural;
*??La resistencia empezó* degraded in a presentational/all-new context, though fine in other,
non-presentational contexts). This section is explicitly a preview of the full derivational treatment
in Ch.5.

#### 1.5.2 Interrogatives (p.50-52)
Yes/no questions may be marked by intonation alone (rising/level tone after main stress, vs. falling
in declaratives) without any constituent-order change. Constituent (wh-)questions obligatorily front
the wh-phrase and require a post-verbal subject UNLESS the wh-phrase is a certain class of adjunct/
modifier (*por qué*, *en qué medida*), in which case the subject may optionally remain pre-verbal
(contrast ***¿Qué Juan leyó?* [argument wh, subject must invert] with the grammatical *¿Por qué Juan
dice eso?* [adjunct wh, subject need not invert]). The verb is argued (Suñer 1994) not to sit
immediately after the wh-phrase the way English inverted auxiliaries do, since other material
(adverbs, even the subject itself) can intervene between the fronted wh-phrase and the verb (*¿A quién
jamás ofenderías tú...?*). Wh-phrases show the same clitic-doubling pattern as other non-pronominal
phrases (indirect-object wh-phrases double with a clitic, direct-object wh-phrases do not and in fact
disallow one — ***¿Qué lo leyó Juan?*). Wh-extraction obeys island constraints (adjunct clauses are
islands) and never allows preposition-stranding — a stranded-P interrogative is categorically
ungrammatical (***¿Quién habló Susana con?*); the whole PP must front.

#### 1.5.3 Imperatives (p.52+)
Imperatives occur in every person/number except 1st singular. Negative imperatives are formally
identical to present subjunctive (same endings) and pattern with other finite verbs for clitic/
negation placement (clitics precede: *no se lo mande*). Positive imperatives have a unique 2nd-person
form (distinct from subjunctive, and in the singular lacking the person-marking *-s*, instead
matching the 3rd-singular desinence) while 1st/3rd-person positive-imperative forms are borrowed from
the present subjunctive paradigm.

#### 1.5.4 Exclamatives (p.53-54)
Exclamative template: XP – (que) – V – (Subj.), with the verb preceding the subject. The optionality
of *que* depends on the morphological nature of the fronted focus XP: an interrogative-form degree
word (*qué/cuán/cómo/cuánto*) makes *que* optional, while the neuter determiner *lo* or feminine *la*
in that position makes *que* obligatory (*¡Lo lista *(que) es Miriam!*). Quantified-NP exclamatives
can be introduced by *cuántos/cuántas* "how many" or, dialectally (peninsular and some Latin American
dialects), by *qué* "what" or feminine *la* with a partitive *de*-phrase, suggesting a covert feminine
head noun *cantidad* "quantity" (*¡La de premios que ganó Susana!*).

#### 1.6.1 Argument (nominal) clauses (p.54-56)
Finite declarative subordinate clauses require overt *que*; infinitival clauses never take an overt
complementizer. *Que* can be omitted even in finite clauses complementing certain verb classes (fear,
will, desire) but only when the embedded clause has no overt pre-verbal subject (Campos 1993) —
dialectally/register-variable. Indirect questions/exclamatives can appear as argument clauses with
optional *que* following the governing predicate, patterning differently from true direct questions.
Clausal arguments show prepositional case-marking parallel to NP arguments: never preceded by a
preposition as subject or direct object (in standard dialects — *dequeísmo*, inserting *de* before a
direct-object clause, is noted as a substandard dialectal phenomenon, p.56 fn.57); preceded by Dative
*a* as indirect object (without clitic doubling); preceded by *de* as complement of adjectives/nouns
(*orgulloso de que...*).

#### 1.6.2 Relative clauses (p.56-60)
Relative clauses share a wh-style gap-filling mechanism with interrogatives but differ in two
systematic ways: (1) no subject-verb inversion in relatives (unlike arguments-wh interrogatives); (2)
the overtness/form of the relativized element depends on restrictive vs. non-restrictive status,
grammatical function, and antecedent features. Restrictive-relative subject position disallows
*quien* (only bare *que*: *el hombre que trabaja aquí*, not **quien*), while non-restrictive relative
subjects allow/prefer *quien*. Restrictive-relative [+human] direct-object relativization has four
competing surface forms (*que* [with covert *a quien*], *a quien*, *al que*, *al cual*), while
non-restrictive relatives for some speakers disallow the covert-*a quien* option. [-human] direct
objects require bare *que* only — *el que*/*el cual*/personal-*a* forms are all out (***el cuaderno al
que Juan perdió*). None of these relative strategies show Case-matching sensitivity between the gap
and the antecedent's own case elsewhere. Possessive relativization uses *cuyo*, agreeing in
number/gender with the *possessed* NP, not the antecedent (*el hombre cuya esposa está en Cuba*) —
a dialectal alternative *que su* also exists. Mood in relative clauses: non-existing/negated/
non-specific antecedents trigger subjunctive in the relative (*no conozco a nadie que hable diez
idiomas*); specific/definite antecedents take indicative (*conozco a alguien que habla diez idiomas*)
— directly tied back to the *cuando*-clause specificity pattern noted in 1.4.2.

#### 1.6.3 Temporal adjunct clauses (p.60-62)
Finite temporal adjuncts (*antes de que*, *mientras*, *después de que*, *desde que*, *hasta que*)
order events relative to each other rather than further specifying the main clause's own tense.
Sequence-of-tense between main and temporal clause requires only [±PAST] feature agreement, not
full tense-morphology matching (present/future distinctions are not required to match — *María
cantará mientras escribo* [future main, present temporal] is fine, but mismatched [+PAST]/[-PAST]
combinations are not: ***María cantará después de que salí*). *Hasta que*/*mientras que* can mark
the *endpoint* of the main-clause event/state rather than its onset, with an inferred polarity
reversal of the main clause at that point (*no acepto la propuesta hasta que me escuches*).

#### 1.6.4 Gerundive and participial adjuncts (p.62-65)
Gerundive clauses: participle in clause-initial position, Nominative (never Genitive) post-verbal
subject obligatory, full VP-internal structure available (auxiliaries, negation, clitics — clitics
follow the non-finite verb, distinguishing the construction from a nominalized/derived-noun reading,
which never hosts clitics: *su lectura del poema* "her reading of the poem" vs. ungrammatical **su lo
lectura*). Gerundive adjuncts vary in how tightly linked to the main clause they are, correlating with
subject coreference and temporal-inclusion vs. independence (compare the tightly-linked, non-paused
*hablando de la película* to the loosely-linked, pause-separated *habiendo salido el sol* with a
distinct subject). Participial clauses use a *past* participle (clause-initial, agreeing in
number/gender with its Nominative subject; subject cannot precede the participle) and show drastically
reduced VP-internal structure compared to gerundives: no negation, no clitics, no auxiliaries
permitted at all. Participial clauses are restricted to predicates denoting a delimiting change of
state/location: transitives with an affected argument are fine (*vendidas las flores*) but
non-affected-argument transitives are marginal/out (*?vista María*, **amada María*); among
unaccusatives, change-of-state/location verbs are (marginally) fine (*?llegados los estudiantes*)
but non-change verbs are out (**faltados los comestibles*, **quedadas las tortillas*) — an early,
concrete preview of the unaccusative diagnostic developed fully in Ch.3.

#### 1.6.5 Infinitival adjuncts (p.65-68)
Catalogues four infinitival-adjunct-introducing prepositions, each with distinct semantics/structure:
**al** (temporal/aspectual, for some speakers causal; infinitival event must be non-stative if
temporal, may be stative if causal — *al parecer que iba a llover* [temporal, non-stative, bad with
stative] vs. *al ser francés Juan* [causal, stative, fine]; non-overt subject optionally but not
obligatorily coreferential with the main clause subject); **de** (conditional interpretation; subject
coreference not obligatory; alternates with a *lo mucho que*-style relativized degree-phrase
construction, *María se enfermó de lo mucho que comió*); **con** (concessive "despite"-type reading,
typically with *aun* "even" or a negated main clause; non-overt subject *is* obligatorily coreferential
with the main clause here, unlike *al*/*de*, shown by the degraded status of *?*Aun con hacer buen
tiempo, no llegué...*); **sin** "without" (infinitive used when subjects are coreferential, a finite
*sin que*-clause used otherwise; as a negative element itself, a second negation inside a
*sin*-infinitival is possible only under contrastive stress). All four preposition-introduced
adjunct types retain full VP-internal structure (auxiliaries, clitics, passive morphology all
possible) — contrasted with the structurally impoverished past-participial adjuncts of 1.6.4.

#### 1.7 Syntactic dialects (p.68-72) — explicit regional/dialectal annotations
Source explicitly draws on Lipski (1994) (Latin American syntactic variation survey) and Alvar (1996)
(peninsular). This is the highest-value explicitly-regionally-tagged content in Ch.1 and is preserved
in full below (all phenomena attested/sourced per-region by the book itself, not inferred):
- **Clitic-doubling of non-pronominal (incl. inanimate) direct objects**, with non-agreeing
  (default masculine singular) clitic doubles: attested Río de la Plata (Argentina/Uruguay), Bolivia,
  Ecuador, Peru, Mexico (*tú lo tienes la dirección*, Bolivia).
- **Null direct objects** (clitic-drop) with a definite-NP antecedent: Andean Spanish — Bolivia,
  Colombia, Ecuador, Perú. (Elsewhere, null objects are possible only when the antecedent is
  indefinite, as in universal-Spanish *¿Compraste café? Sí, compré.*)
  Clitic absence in preposed-phrase contexts: Perú.
- **Clitic doubling of PP/clausal complements** (not just NP direct objects): Colombia, Honduras,
  Nicaragua, Mexico (northern Latin America generally).
- **Clitic "reduplication"** (clitic appearing in two possible positions at once, e.g. proclitic +
  enclitic on the same verb complex): Bolivia, Perú.
- **Negative-phrase + *no* co-occurrence pre-verbally** (*Nadie no está*, elsewhere ungrammatical
  per the Negative Concord rule in 1.2.7): Río de la Plata (Argentina, Uruguay); also attested in
  peninsular Leonese Spanish (Alvar 1996).
- ***No*-reduplication** (clause-final echo *no*: *No hablo inglés no*): Pacific-coast Colombia;
  noted as frequent in vernacular Dominican Spanish.
- **Null negation with *hasta que*** ("until"-clauses with implied negation, no overt *no* needed):
  Colombia, Mexico (Bosque 1980).
- **"Intensive *ser*"** — a cleft-like construction inserting *fue*/*era* ("was") medially
  (*Lo hice fue en el verano* ≈ "What I did was in the summer"): Colombia, Ecuador (documented by
  Kany 1951 back to the mid-19th century); recent in Venezuela; increasingly frequent in Panama City.
- **Possessive-construction variants**: *de*-phrase + possessive *su* co-occurring (*de la María su
  casa* "Maria's house"): Bolivia. Possessive doubling (*mi casa mía*): Honduras. Possessive +
  indefinite article co-occurrence (*hijo de un su papá*): El Salvador, Guatemala, southern Mexico.
  Possessive doubling with definite article + possessive (*la mi vaca* "the my cow") also attested
  in peninsular Leonese Spanish (Alvar 1996).
- **Pre-verbal pronominal subjects in wh-questions** (no subject-verb inversion: *¿Qué tú quieres?*)
  and **pre-verbal subjects of infinitives** (*antes de yo salir de mi país*, *para yo hacer eso*):
  Caribbean Spanish — Cuba, Dominican Republic, Puerto Rico, Venezuela, also Panama; infinitival
  pre-verbal subjects additionally found in Colombia and sometimes Ecuador. Two competing analyses
  noted: (a) subject pronouns have developed a clitic-like unstressed variant in these dialects,
  parallel to French (Lipski 1977, Contreras 1989); (b) the lack of inversion isn't limited to
  pronominal subjects for some speakers (Suñer 1994 found full-DP subjects like *¿Qué libro María
  quiere?* only mildly degraded, not categorically out), tied instead to weakened verbal inflection
  in Caribbean dialects generally (Suñer 1986; Toribio 1993, 1996).

### Chapter 2 — The Noun Phrase (p.73-117)

#### 2.2 Distribution of argument NPs: Theta-theory and abstract Case (p.73-82)
Every noun projects NP (X-bar theory), but an NP's *function* splits into argument (independent
reference, e.g. *pan* in *Juan hizo pan*) vs. predicate (no independent reference, denotes a property
of another NP, e.g. *doctora* in *María es doctora*) — and this is a function of syntactic *context*,
not an inherent lexical property of the noun itself (the same noun, *piloto*/*pan*, can be argument in
one context and predicate in another, p.75). NP cannot be freely inserted as a "satellite" with no
argument/predicate status (***Juan hizo pan piloto*, intending "Juan, a pilot, made bread" — ruled out
categorically). Argument structure/Theta-theory: each predicate's lexical entry specifies a fixed
number of semantic roles (Theta-roles: Agent, Theme/Patient, Experiencer, Goal, etc.) that must be
fully discharged — one role per argument NP, no mismatches (***María le sabe la respuesta a Juan*,
extra argument; ***Juan mandó*, missing argument). Theta-roles are assigned under sisterhood
(mutual c-command): the head alone assigns the "internal argument" role to its sister complement; the
*external* argument (subject) role is assigned compositionally by the head-plus-complement (V')
rather than the bare head — evidenced by alternations like *roll* (intransitive: subject=Patient;
transitive: subject=Agent) and underpinned by a proposed universal "thematic hierarchy" (Agent <
Theme < Experiencer < Goal, Jackendoff 1972) constraining which roles a head may assign externally
given what it assigns internally. An NP can be interpreted as an argument only if base-generated in
an A(rgument)-position (a Theta-role-assignable position) — the ungrammaticality of **el libro Juan
compró* (intending a topicalized-but-non-moved reading) follows from *el libro* sitting in a
non-A-position with no Theta-role available there. Pleonastic ("it"/"there"-type) NPs occupy an
A-position with no Theta-role assigned, hence have no reference — always covert in Spanish (contrast
English's overt *it*/*there*), evidenced by invariant verb agreement (*Parece/*n que...*); some
dialects allow plural rightward agreement with existential *había(n)*, suggesting the covert
pleonastic acts as a placeholder whose features get "raised" from the real post-verbal semantic
subject (p.81 fn.7 — directly continuous with 1.3.3's existential-agreement discussion). Abstract
Case theory (distinct from Theta-theory): Case is assigned by certain heads based on category (V/P
assign Objective Case to a governed sister; finite INFL assigns Nominative Case to the NP in Spec-IP)
— not every category member necessarily has a Case feature to assign. This triggers NP-movement
independent of Theta-role assignment: an NP base-generated as the Theme of a passive participle or a
raising-verb's embedded subject (which cannot assign Case) must move to Spec-IP to receive Nominative
(*[e] fue obligada ella a salir* → *ella fue obligada [–] a salir*; parallel for *parecer*-type raising
verbs) — this is the generative analysis underlying the surface subject-position facts already
described descriptively in Ch.1.3. "Exceptional Case marking" (ECM) is noted as a configuration where
Case-assignment doesn't require mutual c-command the way Theta-assignment does (*Consideran a María
muy inteligente* — the verb Case-marks *María* inside a small clause it doesn't directly theta-mark).

#### 2.3 Predicative NPs and Predication theory (p.82-85)
Predicative NPs (e.g. *doctora* in *María es doctora*) are the mirror image of argument NPs: they
*assign* a Theta-role to their subject rather than receiving one, hence are not generated in
A-positions and are not assigned (independent) Case. Their licensing mechanism is "Predication": a
predicative phrase must be coindexed with an NP subject to be interpretable at all — if no
subject-NP sister is present, Predication fails and the derivation crashes (p.84). Evidence that
predicative NPs lack independent Case: they never co-occur with "personal a" (*Juan es (*a) mi mejor
amigo*), and a predicative pronoun surfaces in Nominative form (*Soy yo*, not **mí*) by *agreeing*
with the Case of the (possibly covert) clausal subject rather than being independently Case-assigned.
This also explains why predicative NPs can never function as secondary-predicate adjuncts (as
established in 2.2.1): there's no Case-assigner available for an adjunct position, and (on one view)
this absence of Case independently rules the construction out even before considering Predication.

#### 2.4 Constituents of NP: X-bar structure, complements, adjuncts (p.85-117 overview; p.85-92 covered here)
Phrase-structural skeleton: every phrase is endocentric (headed, same category as its head) and
subject to maximal binary branching (Kayne 1981) — no node has more than two branches. Three
constituent types relative to the head-noun's projections: complements (sisters of N, in the
Theta-assignable position — e.g. *la destrucción [de la ciudad]*, *tu creencia [en la justicia]*),
adjuncts (sisters of N', generated via N'-adjunction, non-Theta-assignable, unlimited in number — AP/
PP/clause/small-clause adjuncts all occur, e.g. *una comida [muy buena]*, *la solución [en ese
libro]*, *el libro [que leímos]*, *una persona [respetada por todos]*), and specifiers (occupying
Spec-NP, discussed in 2.5-2.7). Only certain noun subclasses actually assign Theta-roles to a
complement: (a) deverbal/de-adjectival nominals (*destrucción*↔*destruir*, *fidelidad*↔*fiel*),
whose complement's preposition mirrors the related verb/adjective's own selected preposition (*creer
en*→*creencia en*; *apelar a*→*apelación a*; a bare-NP-taking verb's nominal counterpart gets the
"default" preposition *de*: *destruir la ciudad*→*destrucción de la ciudad*); (b) "picture" nouns
(*foto*, *retrato*, *libro*, *historia*), whose argument structure derives from their inherently
relational "representing" semantics. Ordinary common/proper nouns have no argument structure at all.
Nouns cannot freely select AP, adjectival-small-clause, or verbal-small-clause complements the way
verbs can (contrast *Pedro se siente contento* [verb+AP} with **el sentimiento contento* [noun+AP,
ungrammatical]) — nominal Case-assignment to a complement is necessarily prepositional/"inherent"
(tied to Theta-marking), unlike a verb's direct "structural" Case assignment (Chomsky 1986). Adjective
adjuncts have special distributional flexibility unlike PP/clausal adjuncts: most occur post-nominally
in the canonical adjunct slot, but a subclass is obligatorily pre-nominal (*mero* "mere"), a large
"qualitative" class alternates pre-/post-nominally with an appositive/non-restrictive vs. restrictive
interpretive difference (*un viejo amigo* "a long-time friend" [pre-nominal, appositive] vs. *un amigo
viejo* "a friend who is old" [post-nominal, restrictive]), and a "relational" class (origin, material:
*mexicano*, *química*) is obligatorily post-nominal. A pre-nominal-adjective + complement order
(*la destrucción completa de la ciudad*) necessarily reflects movement away from the basic
head-complement-adjacent order, since a true complement must sit as the closest sister to the head.
Complement-vs-adjunct status for a post-nominal PP is not always diagnosable by surface form alone
(no clean syntactic test exists): the book's heuristics are (i) only argument-structure-bearing nouns
(deverbal/picture nouns) can host true complements at all; (ii) convert the nominal to its related
verbal form and compare whether the PP's role matches an argument role of the verb (*estudiante de
sintaxis* ↔ *estudiar sintaxis*, Theme-of-study — complement; vs. *estudiante de México* /"from
Mexico" — no verbal-argument match, hence adjunct); (iii) linear-order preferences when multiple PPs
co-occur, since true complements sit closest to the head by default (*un estudiante de sintaxis de
México* natural; *??un estudiante de México de sintaxis* marked, improvable only by inserting pause
intonation — diagnostic of a derived, non-basic order). Spanish has no overt "one"-style pro-form
replacing just N' the way English does, but an elliptical-N' construction with a covert proform
achieves a partially analogous effect (*el estudiante de Granada y el de México*), though it is not
exclusively an N'-replacement test the way English *one* is (book flags this contrast as only
partially parallel, p.92, to be refined just past this excerpt).

#### 2.4.4-2.5 Specifiers, the obligatoriness problem, and the DP hypothesis (p.92-101)
NP specifiers (possessives, demonstratives, definite/indefinite determiners, cardinals, quantifiers,
interrogatives) differ from adjuncts in being non-optional in many contexts: a bare unmodified common
noun cannot stand alone as an argument NP (***Libro está en la mesa*), suggesting specifiers perform
a licensing function, not mere modification. Definite-interpretation specifiers (possessive,
demonstrative, definite article) cannot co-occur pre-nominally (***el mi libro*) but CAN co-occur if
one surfaces post-nominally with strong stress (*el libro tuyo*, *el libro ese* [=THAT book]) — this
"strong/weak split" plus the obligatoriness problem motivates abandoning a flat "Det-as-sister-of-N"
structure in favor of the **DP hypothesis** (Abney 1987): D is a functional head that takes NP as its
complement (not a Theta-marked complement — a "functional," not lexical, head-complement relation).
On this view D "converts" an inherently predicative NP into a referring expression (a semantic
function sometimes called theta-binding, Higginbotham 1985, analogous to but formally distinct from
Predication). Weak/unstressed determiner forms are generated directly as the head D; strong
(stressed) post-nominal forms are generated as DP-adjuncts within NP and must move to Spec-DP at
Logical Form, replacing a semantically "pleonastic" (contentless) D head that cannot survive to
semantic interpretation — this LF-movement analysis explains both the surface order (*los libros
estos*, lit. "the books these") and why only one defines-interpretation specifier surfaces
pre-nominally despite two being present underlyingly. The absence of determiners before proper names
(***el Juan*, dialectally variable) is explained by an analogous but distinct mechanism: the proper
noun itself moves (head-to-head) into D, again eliminating a would-be pleonastic determiner
(Longobardi 1994) — parallel to Aux-to-INFL movement in English, i.e. only a specific subcategory of
N (proper nouns) undergoes this N-to-D movement.

#### 2.6 Pre- and post-determiners: the Quantifier Phrase (p.102-108)
A further functional projection, Quantifier Phrase (QP), is proposed between DP and NP to host
expressions of quantity/number (cardinals, *varios*, *muchos*, *pocos*), accounting for their
co-occurrence with a definite/indefinite D (*unos tres libros*, *estos tres libros*) — cardinals
can also "act alone" as a determiner (*[Tres libros] están en la mesa*, grammatical as a subject,
unlike a bare adjective-modified NP: ***buenos libros están en la mesa*), via head-movement of Q
into D. *Todos* "all" is structurally distinguished from ordinary Q-heads (*varios*, *muchos*): it
behaves as a **pre-determiner**, generated as a *specifier* of QP (not its head), evidenced by three
diagnostics: (1) co-occurrence with both D and an ordinary Q simultaneously (*todos los diez libros*);
(2) **Quantifier Float (Q-float)**: *todos* can be stranded, separated from its associated DP by verb
movement/clause structure (*Los estudiantes dicen todos que el examen fue difícil* — impossible for
true Q-heads like *varios*/*tres*: ***los estudiantes dicen varios/tres que...*); (3) *todos*
disallows the partitive construction (*muchos/varios/cinco de los libros* is fine; ***todos de los
libros* is not), attributed to *todos* not selecting an NP complement at all (unlike true Q-heads,
analyzed as selecting a covert "ones"-type NP in the partitive, parallel to the overt *de*-phrase).
*Ambos* "both" patterns with *todos* for Q-float and the partitive gap, but differs in disallowing
pre-D position (***ambos los libros*). *Cada* "each" patterns with *todos*/*ambos* on the partitive
gap but doesn't float and doesn't precede D.

#### 2.7 Specifiers of predicative NPs (p.108-109)
Predicative NPs (unlike argument NPs) generally lack determiners altogether when unmodified (*María
es doctora*, no article — contrast the argument-NP requirement: *María reconoció a *(una) doctora*)
— evidence that bare predicative NPs are genuinely NP, not DP. A determiner does surface on a
predicative NP when independently required, e.g. by a comparative (***Juan es mejor amigo* vs. *Juan
es el mejor amigo que tengo*/*mi mejor amigo*). QP-type quantifiers are categorically excluded from
predicative NPs even when modified (***Ellos son muchos/varios/pocos/tres pilotos*). Apparent
counterexamples with *todos*/*ambos* (*Ellos son todos pilotos*) are reanalyzed as Q-float from the
*subject* DP (*todos ellos*), not true specifiers of the predicative NP — confirmed by the fact that
*todos* becomes impossible once the subject DP has its own independent quantifier (***Muchos de
ellos son todos pilotos*).

#### 2.8 Constituent order within NP: adjective positions and the Agreement Phrase (AgrP) (p.109-117)
Three distinct AP positions are analyzed: (a) canonical post-nominal adjunct (restrictive
interpretation); (b) NP-specifier position for pre-nominal "qualitative" adjectives (appositive
interpretation) — following Jackendoff (1977)'s generalization that restrictive modifiers attach
lower in structure than non-restrictive ones; (c) an N-A-complement order (*la destrucción completa
de la ciudad*, AP sandwiched between head N and its PP complement) that cannot be base-generated
directly without stranding the complement in a non-Theta-markable adjunct position. Two competing
derivations for (c) are considered and the book favors the second: (i) rightward movement of the
complement around the adjective (noted as theoretically disfavored given doubts about rightward
movement generally, Kayne 1994); (ii) **leftward N-movement** to a higher functional head, **AgrP**
(Agreement Phrase, bearing the noun's number/gender inflection features, Mallén 1989, Cinque 1992),
unifying NP constituent order with the independently-established VP-internal V-to-INFL movement
analysis of clausal adverb-verb-complement order (*Destruyeron completamente la ciudad* — adverb
between V and complement, grammatical in Spanish/Romance via V-raising past a VP-adverb, categorically
ungrammatical in English: ***They destroyed completely the city*). The cross-linguistic
Romance-vs-English parameter (N-A-complement/V-Adv-complement orders possible in Romance, impossible
in English) is attributed to the relative richness/"strength" of Agreement morphology: rich Romance
noun/verb agreement attracts the head to move past adjuncts; weak English agreement does not (parallel
treatment to the independently-discussed V-movement-to-INFL parameter, picked up fully in Ch.4).

### Chapter 3 — The Verb Phrase (p.118-156)

#### 3.2 Distribution of VP: the primary-predicate restriction and the EPP (p.118-126)
VP (and only VP) can serve as the *primary* predicate of a clause — a sister of INFL within IP
(***Susana cansada*, intending "Susana tired" as the whole predicate, is ungrammatical; only *Susana
descansó* works) — a syntactic, not semantic, restriction, since AP/NP are equally capable of
denoting states. VP is categorically barred from *secondary*-predicate (adjunct) position (***Susana
dejó la sala descansaba/descansar*, contrast grammatical AP secondary predicate *Susana dejó la sala
cansada*) unless the apparent VP-adjunct is actually embedded under its own covert IP/CP — shown by
*para*-infinitival/subjunctive adjuncts, which contain an independent clause structure (overt *que*
possible: *para que José descansara*), and by participial absolutive adjuncts (*Llegado José, empezó
la fiesta*), argued (following Belletti 1990 on Italian) to involve head-movement of the participle to
an empty C position — evidenced by the obligatory participle-subject order (***José llegado...*),
parallel to an overt complementizer's position. The clearest genuine exception is auxiliary-verb
sequences (*han comido*), which require two VPs but only one INFL — analyzed either as (a) VP1 (aux)
sister of INFL with VP2 (participle) sister of the aux head, or (b) VP1 as a specifier/adjunct of the
primary-predicate VP2 — deferred to Ch.4 for full treatment; "semi-auxiliary" **Restructuring**
predicates (aspectual/volitional *querer*, *volver a*; causative/perception *hacer*, *ver*) show the
same VP-sequencing behavior with a following infinitive even though they independently pass tests for
being ordinary main verbs (picked up fully in Ch.6.5). VP-as-primary-predicate is licensed by two
independent relations: (i) **Predication** with the subject DP, satisfying **mutual m-command**
(a looser configurational requirement than mutual c-command, since VP and the subject DP don't
strictly c-command each other inside IP) and enforced by the **Extended Projection Principle (EPP)**
— "every clause must have a subject," which for Spanish's null-subject property means a phonetically
empty but syntactically real `pro` occupies Spec-IP (evidenced by reflexive-antecedent facts: *Hablaba
consigo mismo* "He talked to himself" requires a real, if covert, subject to serve as the reflexive's
antecedent); (ii) a relation to INFL's Tense features, informally analogized to the Det/NP relation
of Ch.2 (IP as a functional category assigning VP a "temporal location") — the precise formal nature
of this second relation is flagged as an open research question, picked up in Ch.4/5.

#### 3.3 The external argument (subject): VP-internal subject hypothesis, Case-driven raising (p.126-131)
Not every predicate assigns an external (subject) Theta-role (contrast *los niños leyeron los libros*
[Agent subject] with *parece que los niños leyeron los libros*, where *parecer* assigns no external
role and the covert subject is purely pleonastic, inserted only to satisfy the EPP). The **VP-internal
subject hypothesis**: the external argument is base-generated not in Spec-IP but in **Spec-VP**,
assigned its Theta-role compositionally by V' (the verb together with its complement) — exactly
parallel to the external-Theta-role mechanism already established for predicative NPs in Ch.2
(*John broke the window* [Agent] vs. *John broke his arm* [Theme], showing the external role is
sensitive to the complement, hence assigned by V' not V alone). Since Spec-VP is not a
Case-position (only INFL assigns Nominative, to Spec-IP), the VP-internal subject must move to
Spec-IP at S-structure to receive Case — meaning Spanish's variable subject-position facts (Ch.1.3)
are reanalyzed here as following from a Case-driven movement step, not free base-generation.
**Subject-to-subject raising** (*parecer*-type verbs): when the embedded clause is non-finite (*Los
niños parecen haber leído los libros*), the embedded VP-internal Agent DP moves all the way up to the
*matrix* Spec-IP to receive Case (since *parecer* itself assigns no external Theta-role and the
embedded non-finite clause cannot independently assign Nominative) — contrasted with the finite-
embedded-clause case (*Parece que...*), where Raising is blocked (the embedded subject already gets
Case there, and movement would create an illicit doubly-Case-marked chain) and a pleonastic surfaces
in the matrix instead. The derived subject thus forms a single "chain" spanning two positions:
Theta-related to Spec-VP, Case-related to Spec-IP.

#### 3.4 Prepositional complements vs. adjuncts: the *hacerlo* and preposition-substitution diagnostics (p.131-135)
Two converging diagnostics distinguish a PP complement (sister of V, theta-marked) from a PP adjunct
(sister of V', not theta-marked): (1) ***hacerlo* ("do so") replacement** — this proform can replace
any V' node, optionally "leaving behind" an adjunct but *never* leaving behind a true complement
(*Juan habló de política de nuevo y José lo hizo por primera vez* — the adjunct *de nuevo* can be
stranded; ***Juan habló de política y José lo hizo de economía* — the complement *de política*
cannot be stranded); restricted to active (non-stative) predicates, since *hacerlo* itself denotes an
action (***Juan se parece a José y Pedrito lo hace también* is out regardless of complement/adjunct
status). (2) **preposition substitutability** — an adjunct's preposition is freely replaceable by
other locational/temporal/instrumental prepositions (*recibí un mensaje de José/para Susana/por
teléfono/...*), while a true complement's preposition is lexically fixed by the verb (*soñé con/*de
María*; *insistió en/*con tu ayuda*) — with a systematic exception for verbs assigning a *Locative*
Theta-role, whose complement preposition varies freely within the set of locative prepositions
(*puse el bolígrafo en/sobre/detrás de/debajo de el escritorio*, but not a non-locative preposition
like *con*), because all such prepositions share a [+Locative] feature satisfying the verb's
selection.

#### 3.5 Direct object DPs: Case-driven properties distinguishing them from PPs/adjuncts (p.135-137+)
Because DP complements are linked to the verb by *both* Theta-role assignment *and* direct
(Accusative) Case assignment — unlike PP complements, linked only via Theta-role, with Case assigned
instead by the preposition — DP complements show three Case-attributable properties PP complements
lack: (a) clitic doubling, (b) passivizability, (c) co-occurrence with personal *a* (the latter two
developed further below). On clitic doubling: a DP complement that is a reflexive/reciprocal anaphor
or a pronominal *requires* an agreeing clitic double (*Susana *(se) pateó a sí misma*; *Susana *(lo)
pateó a él*) — but true *adjuncts* containing an anaphor or pronominal, even semantically similar
ones (emphatic reflexive *por sí misma*, benefactive *para él*), categorically disallow a clitic
double (***Susana se resolvió el problema por sí misma*; ***le compré un coche para él*), and PP
*complements* likewise never take a clitic double (***Juan se habló consigo mismo*) — so clitic-
doubling specifically diagnoses DP-complement status, distinct from the general PP complement/adjunct
tests of 3.4. A benefactive introduced by *a* instead of *para* (*Le compré un coche a Juan*) behaves
like a quasi-complement on the clitic/hacerlo tests but still fails the passivization test
(***José fue comprado un coche*, paralleling English *José was bought a car*), showing clitic-doubling
and passivizability are theoretically separable diagnostics that can dissociate for a given
construction (indirect objects generally, picked up fully in 3.6).

#### 3.6 Indirect objects (p.141-152): similarities/differences vs. direct objects, Larson's VP-shell, Dative *a*
Indirect objects (Dative-marked: *a*-phrase + agreeing clitic, e.g. *le mandó un paquete a Pedro*)
pattern with complements (not adjuncts) on *hacerlo*-replacement and obligatory clitic doubling with
reflexives/pronominals/left-dislocation (3.6.1) — same tests as direct objects. But three systematic
differences distinguish them from direct objects (3.6.2): (a) indirect objects never co-occur with
personal *a* (predictable, since this would double the *a* morpheme); (b) indirect objects never
passivize in Spanish/Romance generally (***Pedro fue mandado un libro*, contrast English's "Pedro was
sent a book" — a Romance/English parameter, with a noted journalese exception for *preguntar*); (c)
Dative Case-marking can attach to constituents that are NOT independently Theta-marked by the verb at
all — a process called **"Dativization."** Two Dativization patterns: (i) **benefactives**: a
*para*-PP adjunct (*compré un coche para sus amigos*) has a Dativized alternant (*les compré un coche
a sus amigos*) that then behaves like a true complement under *hacerlo* (cannot be stranded), even
though the underlying benefactive relation isn't verb-Theta-marked; (ii) **inalienable-possession
datives** with "verbs of contact" (*golpear* "hit," *frotar* "rub"): *José les golpeó las rodillas a
sus amigos* ("hit his friends' knees") — here the direct object DP denotes a body-part "part" and the
dative DP denotes the "whole" possessor; since a single Theta-role can't be assigned to two separate
individuals in a true part-whole relation, the analysis has the body-part *noun itself* assign a
"Possessor" role to the Dative DP *inside* the direct-object DP, with the DP-as-a-whole then receiving
the verb's own Theme role — meaning the inalienable possessor is "Dativized" via a derivational
relation to the verb despite never being directly Theta-marked by it. 3.6.3 addresses the theoretical
puzzle of how a ditransitive verb can Theta-mark *two* complements given the no-ternary-branching
constraint (ruling out a flat three-branch V' structure) and the sisterhood requirement for
Theta-assignment (ruling out a structure where the indirect object isn't a sister of V at all, since
that would wrongly predict indirect objects behave like adjuncts): the solution adopted is **Larson's
(1988) VP-shell analysis** — the verb originates lower, as a sister of the indirect object (assigning
it a Theta-role directly), then *moves* to a higher empty V-head position from which it c-commands
(though doesn't sister) the direct object, Theta-marking it via the same mechanism as external-argument
assignment (head+complement assigning a role to what it c-commands). Because the verb moves through/
to the higher position, *hacerlo* can only replace the topmost V', correctly ruling out stranding
either object. Dative *a* itself is argued (preferred analysis, despite some unresolved tension) to be
a genuine preposition heading a PP complement — distinguishing it from "Personal a" — evidenced by
indirect objects patterning with PP complements (not direct objects marked by Personal a) on secondary-
predicate licensing (*Vi a mi amiga enojada* grammatical with a direct object + AP secondary predicate;
***Mandé una carta a mi amiga enojada* ungrammatical with an indirect object, Demonte 1986) — though
this preposition analysis leaves clitic-doubling and the robustness of Dativization as open questions
the book does not fully resolve (p.151, explicitly flagged as unresolved).

#### 3.7 Unaccusative verb complements (p.152-156) — core concept for later chapters
The external/internal argument distinction (3.3) predicts ordinary subjects behave differently from
complements: unlike complements, external arguments can be left behind under *hacerlo* (*María leyó
el diario, y Pedro lo hizo también*) and disallow bare-NP subjects (***Niños leyeron estos libros*).
A specific intransitive-verb class — **"presentational"/unaccusative verbs** (*llegar* "arrive,"
*venir* "come," *aparecer* "appear," *salir* "come/go out," and negative counterparts *faltar*
"lack," *desaparecer* "disappear" — the diagnostic class already previewed descriptively in Ch.1.2.6
and Ch.1.6.4's participial-adjunct facts) systematically violates this: their surface subjects DO
allow bare NPs, but ONLY post-verbally (*Llegaron estudiantes* / ***Estudiantes llegaron*; *Faltan
tomates* / ***Tomates faltan*) — unlike ordinary intransitives, which disallow bare-NP subjects in
either position (***Cenó gente*/***Gente cenó*). These unaccusative subjects otherwise show ordinary
surface-subject properties (Nominative pronoun form, verb agreement) but pattern with objects in
lacking Personal *a* and clitic-doubling requirements. (Cross-linguistic note: French/Italian mark
this class with a distinct "be"-type compound-past auxiliary, unlike "have" for ordinary transitives/
unergatives — *Jean est arrivé* vs. *Jean a mangé* — and English diagnoses the class via the *there*-
construction (*there arrived many students*); Spanish itself provides no auxiliary-switch diagnostic.)
The analysis (following Perlmutter 1978, Burzio 1986): **unaccusative verbs assign a Theta-role (e.g.
Theme) only to an INTERNAL argument** (generated in object/complement position, not Spec-VP) and
crucially **do not assign Accusative Case** to that argument — hence the term "unaccusative." The DP
must still get Case somehow: either it raises to Spec-IP (yielding the ordinary-looking pre-verbal-
subject order) or, when it stays post-verbally as a bare NP, Case is "transmitted" from a null
expletive `pro` in subject position (Burzio 1986) or the verb assigns a special **Partitive Case**
directly (Belletti 1988, an alternative analysis). This explains the earlier Ch.1 bare-plural facts:
the post-verbal bare-NP subject of an unaccusative is actually still sitting in its D-structure
*complement* position, which is independently the position where bare NPs are licensed generally
(direct objects, etc.) — unifying the unaccusative-subject facts with the general bare-NP distribution
facts from Ch.1.2.6. A *hacerlo*-replacement test for this (3.93) is suggestive but muddied by
*hacerlo*'s own Agentive-subject selectional requirement, which independently predicts the asymmetry
— the book explicitly flags this as inconclusive evidence for Spanish, unlike the clearer
auxiliary-switch diagnostics available in French/Italian.

#### 3.8 Chapter 3 summary (p.156)
Recaps: VP as obligatory primary predicate (Predication + Tense-relation licensing); external
arguments Theta-marked in Spec-VP, Case-licensed via movement to Spec-IP; Raising as an alternative
route to a referential matrix subject when the matrix predicate assigns no external role; PP
complements/direct objects/indirect objects each show distinct complement-vs-adjunct and

### Chapter 4 — VP-related functional categories (p.158-201)

#### 4.1-4.2 IP as functional extension of VP; VP-adverb distribution as evidence for V-to-INFL movement (p.158-167)
IP is analyzed as a *functional* extension of VP (parallel to DP/NP in Ch.2, Grimshaw 1991), motivating
the subject-raising-to-Spec-IP analysis already given in Ch.3. Adverbs split into two classes with
different attachment sites: **IP-adverbs** (sentence adverbs: *probablemente*, *obviamente*, PPs like
*sin embargo*) adjoin to IP/I' and occur only pre-verbally/pre-auxiliary (before or after the subject),
marginally at best mid-VP; **VP-adverbs** (Time: *ayer*; Place: *allí*; Extent: *casi*, *apenas*;
Manner: *cuidadosamente*; Quantity: *mucho*) modify the VP-level event and show a richer distribution.
Crucially, most VP-adverb subclasses (Time, Place, Manner, Quantity — not Extent) can surface *between
the verb and its direct-object complement* (*María leyó cuidadosamente el diario* — grammatical in
Spanish, categorically ungrammatical in English: ***Maria read carefully the newspaper*). This is the
central empirical argument (Emonds 1978) for **V-to-INFL head movement** in Romance: rather than
base-generating the adverb between V and its complement (which would either violate binary branching
or, in a binary-branching fix, wrongly put the complement farther from the verb than the adjunct,
inverting the Theta-theoretic c-command prediction from Ch.2/3), the verb itself raises out of VP to
the head of IP, stranding a VP-adjoined adverb behind it between the raised verb and the
still-in-situ complement. Independent confirming evidence: **question-inversion asymmetry** — in
Spanish, BOTH main verbs and auxiliaries can invert to pre-subject position in questions (*¿Leyó Juan
el diario?*), while English allows only auxiliary inversion (***Read Juan the newspaper?*, only *Is/
Had Juan...*) — on the standard assumption that question-inversion moves INFL itself to a pre-clausal
position, only a verb that has already raised to INFL can show up there. This same parameter predicts
that Spanish VP-adverbs can follow BOTH auxiliaries and main verbs (*Juan había frecuentemente leído
el diario*; *Juan leyó frecuentemente el diario*), while English adverbs can only follow an auxiliary,
never a main verb (*Juan had often read the newspaper* / ***Juan read often the newspaper*, rescued
only by inserting dummy *did*) — directly continuous with the Romance/English "rich agreement →
V-movement" parameter already proposed for N-movement/AgrP in Ch.2.8.

#### 4.2.3 Pre-verbal VP-adverbs and scope (p.168-170)
Time/Place/Extent-class adverbs can also appear pre-verbally; since the verb is independently
analyzed as sitting in INFL at S-structure, a pre-verbal VP-adverb must be adjoined to I'/IP in that
position — raising the question of whether it has moved there from a VP-internal site or is simply
interpreted as an event-oriented IP-adverb at that position. Evidence for the latter: pre-verbal
*allá* obligatorily takes scope over the *entire* event (including the subject), while post-verbal
*allá* is ambiguous between whole-event scope and narrow scope over just the final argument (*Juan
allá conoció a su amigo* [whole event] vs. *Juan disparó la flecha allá*, ambiguous) — and pre-verbal
Place adverbs take scope over negation while post-verbal ones are scopally ambiguous with it,
paralleling the general pattern that structurally higher material scopes over lower material.

#### 4.3.1 Triggering V-to-INFL: feature-checking theory and the strength parameter (p.170-174)
Addresses *why* V-to-INFL movement happens at all. INFL is decomposed into AGR features ([person],
[number], a categorial [D]-feature "attracting" a DP to check Case/agreement) and TENSE features
([±finite], [±past], a categorial [V]-feature attracting a verb) — following Chomsky (1993)'s
Minimalist shift from "INFL assigns inflection to a bare verb stem" (old affix-hopping model) to "all
words enter the derivation already fully inflected; movement instead *checks* that a moved
constituent's features match the attracting functional head's features." Movement targets are either
head-adjunction (V adjoining to INFL, checking V-features) or Spec-head configurations (DP in Spec-IP,
checking D-features) — unifying V-movement and subject-raising as the same checking mechanism applied
to different feature bundles. The English/Romance V-to-INFL parameter is reformulated as a difference
in **feature "strength"**: Romance's V-feature of Tense is "strong" (forces overt V-movement by
S-structure); English's is "weak" (V-movement is delayed to covert LF-only movement, explaining why
English main verbs never surface in INFL) — but this account leaves English auxiliaries as an
unexplained exception (they DO move overtly in English despite "weak" V-features), flagged as an open
problem resolved in the following subsection.

#### 4.3.2 Auxiliary verb structure: full VP projections, a VP-shell/complement chain, and participial feature-checking (p.174-177+)
Three questions framed: (1) how are auxiliaries structurally related to the following verb; (2) how
are each verb's inflectional features in a chain like *ha estado estudiando* licensed; (3) what
distinguishes auxiliaries from main verbs re: V-to-INFL. On (1): two competing analyses considered.
Early "auxiliary as specifier/adjunct of VP" analyses are rejected because auxiliaries demonstrably
have full independent phrasal structure, evidenced by (a) a second, contrastively-stressed *no* being
possible only when an auxiliary is present (*Juan no estaba NO estudiando* grammatical; ***Juan no NO
estudiaba* out — diagnosing a second VP-specifier slot only the auxiliary structure supplies) and (b)
"floated" *todos* co-occurring with an independent quantifier only when an auxiliary is present
(*Mis amigos estaban siempre todos estudiando lingüística* vs. ***mis amigos estudiaban siempre todos
lingüística*) — both diagnostics directly reusing mechanisms established in Ch.2.6 (Q-float) and
Ch.1.4.5 (negation). The adopted analysis instead treats each auxiliary as the head of its OWN VP,
in a head-complement relation with the next VP down (a VP-shell/nesting structure: *ha* selects a VP
headed by *estado*, which selects a VP headed by *estudiando*), with the subject DP moving through
each VP's specifier on its way up to Spec-IP, and only the highest verb (*ha*) raising to INFL. On (2):
a bare-VP-complement analysis under-licenses the rigid participial morphology that must follow each
auxiliary (***Susana ha estudiando* is out — the participle's own form is not free), which is a
problem for a feature-checking framework where words enter the derivation already inflected (ruling
out old-style affix-hopping). The fix: posit an additional functional head, by analogy with INFL,
immediately above each participle — call it a lower "INFL"-type node — so that the participle itself
raises to and checks features against this local head, just as a finite verb checks features against
clausal INFL; the auxiliary separately raises to the next head up. This predicts (correctly) that
IP-adverbs can follow auxiliaries, continuous with the adverb-distribution facts of 4.2.

#### 4.3.2 continued: Aspect Phrase, participle-as-aspectual (not tense) morphology, resolving the auxiliary "immunity" problem (p.178-183)
Rejects analyzing the auxiliary's complement as a full IP: unlike a genuine infinitival IP-complement
(e.g. under raising *parecer*, which freely admits independent negation and proclitic placement:
*Juan parece no saber la respuesta*; *Juan parece saberlo*), an aspectual auxiliary's participle
complement disallows BOTH independent negation and clitics in either position (***Juan ha no
estudiado...*; ***Juan ha estudiádolo*; ***Juan ha lo estudiado*) — showing the participle carries no
[±finite] specification at all, i.e. isn't an IP. Conceptual argument for treating participial
morphology as **Aspect-related, not Tense-related**: comparing simple present (*Juan vive en Madrid*)
to present-perfect (*Juan ha vivido en Madrid*) shows the perfect doesn't locate the event relative to
utterance-time the way finite tense does — instead it splits a single event into two "states"/
subevents (echoing the Reference-point analysis of compound tenses already given descriptively in
Ch.1.4.1), extending to progressives too (*Juan está viviendo en Madrid* = a living-state nested
inside a located-living-state). This motivates an **Aspect Phrase (AspP)** functional projection
between INFL and VP, with features like [±perfective]/[±progressive]; the participle head-moves to
Asp (not INFL) to check its morphological features, exactly paralleling the V-to-INFL mechanism — and
this correctly predicts IP-adverbs can intervene between an auxiliary and participle (since Asp, like
INFL, is a licit adjunction site). This resolves 4.3.1's unexplained "auxiliary immunity" puzzle: if
AspP is posited to be universally present (even with null/negative feature values) in the structural
sequence INFL–(Aux)–Aspect–V, then the English/Romance V-movement parameter can be relocated to the
*strength of Aspect's* V-feature rather than INFL's: Romance Aspect has a strong V-feature (attracting
main verbs through Aspect en route to INFL); English Aspect's V-feature is weak (main verbs get stuck
below Aspect, never reaching INFL — forcing resort to dummy *do*-insertion directly in INFL when
INFL's own V-feature must still be checked), while auxiliaries are independently generated ABOVE
Aspect in both languages, bypassing the weak-Aspect bottleneck that blocks English main verbs. Listed
auxiliary-class members: aspectual *haber* (perfective), *estar* (progressive); plus non-aspectual
*ser* (passive and copular) — this latter group is characterized only negatively (they select no
argument, so never serve as primary semantic predicate), with one flagged exception: "equative" *ser*
(*Juan es el hombre de azul*) does subcategorize an argument DP (Sánchez & Camacho 1995).

#### 4.4 Clitics: competing derivational analyses (p.184-189+)
Reframes the core puzzle from Ch.1.2.4/3: how is a verb's complement-position/transitivity satisfied
when the complement surfaces as a pre-verbal clitic instead of a full DP (*María compró ese coche* /
*María lo compró*)? Two classical approaches are contrasted:
**(4.4.1) Movement approach** (Emonds 1975; Quicoli 1976; Kayne 1989): the clitic originates as the
ordinary theta-marked/Case-marked DP complement, then moves leftward to attach to its phonological
"host" (the verb directly, per the traditional view, or the V+INFL complex after V-to-INFL, per Kayne
1989), leaving a trace in complement position. Advantages: (a) uniform D-structure for complements
regardless of clitic-vs-full-phrase realization; (b) motivates movement independently via the clitic's
phonological dependency; (c) naturally explains "clitic climbing"/restructuring, where a clitic
construed with an embedded non-finite verb's argument surfaces on a structurally higher matrix verb
(*María lo quiere comprar* — confirmed to be thematically linked to the lower verb *comprar*, not the
matrix *querer*, by the fact that the lower verb's own complement slot is otherwise unavailable:
***María lo quería comprar un libro*) — the trace left by movement supplies the needed link to the
lower position automatically. Two problems for the movement approach: (a) **clitic doubling** — a
clitic co-occurring with an *overt* reflexive/pronominal/referential full phrase in the same argument
slot (*María lo vio a él*; *Pedro le envió una carta a ella*) has no non-stipulative source for the
full phrase if the clitic itself originated there (two rescue proposals noted: treating the doubled
full phrase as an adjunct indirectly linked to the clitic (Hurtado 1989b), or generating [clitic+phrase]
as a single complex constituent, developed in 4.4.3); (b) **non-argumental clitics** that never
alternate with a full phrase at all — "aspectual" *se* (marking event-completedness: *los niños se
comieron los dulces*), "middle" *se* (deletes the Agent role: *el barco se hundió*), and "inherent
reflexive" *se* (*Juan se parece a Pedro*, no corresponding argument, never co-occurs with a full
phrase, Luján 1976) — these clitics occupy the same surface clitic position as true argumental
clitics but have no underlying full-DP source for movement to have applied to.
**(4.4.2) Base-generation approach** (Rivas 1977; Strozer 1976; Jaeggli 1982; Borer 1984): the clitic
is directly generated as part of a complex [clitic+verb] constituent, NOT moved from the complement
position; a full-DP complement (when the clitic doubles one) is independently generated in the
ordinary theta-marked complement position and linked to the clitic merely via an *agreement*
(co-indexing) relation, with the clitic itself absorbing the verb's Case-assignment capacity rather
than the verb assigning Case directly to the DP (passage cuts off mid-explanation of the Case
mechanism, to be continued/picked up in the next excerpt — flagged here as unresolved pending further
reading).

#### 4.4.2 continued and 4.4.3: Case-sharing in base-generation; clitics as functional heads (Agr-o) (p.189-194)
Base-generation's account of clitic doubling: the clitic absorbs/is assigned the verb's object Case
directly; when a full doubled DP is also present, either (a) the Case feature is "shared" between
clitic and DP via their agreement relation, or (b) doubling requires an independent second Case
assigner to be structurally present (a preposition — Dative *a*, Personal *a*). This approach also
naturally extends the null-subject-style explanation (rich agreement licenses covert pronouns) to
covert *objects*: clitic-DP agreement similarly allows a covert object pronoun's features to be
recovered. Its fatal flaw: treating the clitic as part of a single compound-verb lexical item
wrongly predicts clitics can never move away from their selecting verb — refuted by clitic climbing
(4.4.1's *María lo quiere comprar* data), where the clitic surfaces on a verb other than the one that
selects it.
**(4.4.3) Clitics as functional heads** — two converging modern proposals resolve the movement/
base-generation tension: (a) clitics as **D-related heads**: reanalyze the "moved" clitic not as a
bare pronominal DP (which incorrectly predicts no internal structure, hence no room for a doubled
full phrase) but as the *determiner* head of DP itself (Uriagereka 1992; Belletti 1995) — since
determiners independently co-occur with full NP material, this immediately permits clitic doubling
without extra machinery, by analogy to the already-established strong/weak determiner-doubling
pattern of Ch.2.5 (*el libro ese*). (b) clitics as **V-related Agr-o heads**: extending the Split-INFL
idea (Agr-s/Tense as separate heads, Pollock 1989) to the object side, an **Agreement-object (Agr-o)**
head is posited as part of the independently-motivated Aspect Phrase (4.3.2) — Agr-o bears
person/number/gender D-features (gender independently needed since passive participles agree in
gender) and a V-feature that attracts the verb through Aspect; an object clitic directly instantiates
this Agr-o head, with the associated DP (covert `pro`, or an overt full phrase in doubling dialects)
moving to Spec-AspP to check D-features via a Spec-head relation — exactly parallel to subject-Agr-s
checking. This correctly predicts that pronominal clitics are a clause-level (not NP-internal)
phenomenon, since only clauses have Tense/Aspect projections, and — unlike the flawed compound-verb
base-generation analysis — allows the Aspect/Agr-o head to move independently of the lexical verb,
resolving base-generation's clitic-climbing problem. Two acknowledged loose ends: this Agr-o analysis
is compatible with (not necessarily exclusive of) the D-head/determiner analysis in (a); and neither
account directly explains clitic movement all the way up to INFL (preceding a finite verb) — flagged
as an open question possibly tied to the Tense/Aspect relationship.

#### 4.5 Negation as a functional category: NegP and the Negative Concord feature-checking analysis (p.194-200)
**4.5.1**: *No* occupies a single fixed pre-verbal S-structure position regardless of whether the
first verb is finite or non-finite, auxiliary or main (*no leyó*; *no había leído*; ***había no
leído*; *quería no leer*) and must be strictly adjacent to the verb (only a clitic may intervene:
*no lo leyó*; nothing else can: ***no ayer leyó*). This motivates treating *no* as the head of its
own functional projection, **Neg(ative) Phrase** — termed "Sigma Phrase" by Laka (1990), alternating
with affirmative *sí* — situated in the clausal spine ABOVE Tense but, crucially, BELOW Agr-s (the
subject-agreement projection), requiring the **Split-INFL hypothesis** (Pollock 1989; Agr-s and Tense
as independent heads, with Neg sandwiched between them: Agr-s–Neg–Tense) so that a raised subject in
Spec-Agr-s still precedes *no* while the verb raising only as far as Tense still follows it. Contrasted
with English, where auxiliary-movement facts place Neg instead BELOW INFL (*John has not read...*,
***John read not...* — Agr-s–Tense–Neg), a genuine word-order parameter in where NegP sits in the
clausal spine.
**4.5.2**: **Negative Concord** (n-words like *nadie*, *nada*, *ningún* — only sometimes "really"
negative depending on position, cf. Ch.1.2.7) is reanalyzed via feature-checking: NegP's head carries
a strong abstract [+NEG] feature that must attract some [+NEG]-bearing constituent to its specifier
by S-structure — satisfied either by movement of a pre-verbal n-word (*nadie vino*) or by direct
insertion of *no* itself in/near NegP (*no vino nadie*, where "nadie" stays low, post-verbal, and NOT
checking the feature itself in this derivation). Because movement to check a strong feature happens
only once, exactly one negative constituent can occupy the relevant preverbal checking position,
correctly predicting both the obligatory presence of exactly one pre-verbal negative element (***Juan
dijo nada*, no pre-verbal negative at all) and the impossibility of two pre-verbal negatives
simultaneously (***Nadie no vino*; ***Nadie nada dijo*) — explicitly analogized to the independently
established mechanics of (single) wh-movement. Two residual open issues flagged explicitly: (i) why
Spanish n-words are "genuinely" negative pre-verbally but require concord post-verbally is not derived
from anything beyond a stipulated lexical parameter (cross-linguistic Negative-Concord parameter,
debated per Suñer 1995/Bosque 1980); (ii) n-words can be licensed in some contexts with no local overt
negative trigger at all (*María canta mejor que ninguno de nosotros*, inside an elliptical comparative
clause — contrast the ungrammatical non-comparative-elliptical parallel *?*María canta tan bien como
ninguno de nosotros*), a fact the chapter notes but does not resolve.

#### 4.6 Chapter 4 summary (p.200-201)
Recaps: VP-adverb distribution as evidence for V-to-INFL; feature-checking as the general mechanism
driving movement, with Aspect posited as a universal functional projection explaining both participle
licensing and the English/Romance auxiliary-vs-main-verb movement asymmetry; clitics reanalyzed as
functional heads (D-related or V-related/Agr-o) superseding the classical movement/base-generation
dichotomy; negation as a Split-INFL-dependent functional projection whose feature-checking mechanics
explain Negative Concord via the same logic as wh-movement.

### Chapter 5 — Subjects, Topics, and declarative constituent order (p.202-240)

#### 5.2 The problem of "free" subject order: why feature-strength alone cannot derive it (p.202-208)
Frames the chapter's central puzzle: Spanish declaratives freely allow S-V-O, V-S-O, V-O-S, unlike
English's fixed S-V-O — and the feature-checking/strength framework from Ch.4 (movement happens only
when forced by a strong feature to check; movement is never truly "optional" under Minimalist economy
assumptions, Chomsky 1993) cannot accommodate genuine word-order freedom. Demonstration: if INFL's
subject-related ("N-features"/D-features: person, number, Case) are stipulated **strong**, movement
of the subject to Spec-IP should be *obligatory*, wrongly ruling out V-S-O/V-O-S; if stipulated
**weak**, movement should be *impossible*, wrongly ruling out S-V-O. Neither a uniformly-strong nor
uniformly-weak setting of this single parameter can generate the attested three-way alternation —
explicitly presented as a real paradox for the then-current theory, not resolved via feature strength
alone. This motivates two directions explored later in the chapter: (a) positing an *additional*
functional category/feature (discourse-related: Topic/Focus, see 5.3-5.4) that can independently
trigger movement of a constituent to a pre-verbal position; (b) questioning whether "movement" is
even the right analysis for some of the alternative orders at all (see 5.5, dislocation-as-adjunct).

#### 5.3 Discourse roles: Focus and Topic (p.208-213)
**Focus** = the non-presupposed, "new"-information constituent of a sentence relative to a discourse
context (formalized as a [±Focus] feature, Zubizarreta 1998); any constituent, or the whole sentence,
can be Focus depending on what the preceding discourse/question presupposes (*¿Adónde fue José?* →
only *a casa* is Focus in the answer; *¿Qué pasó?* → the whole sentence is Focus). Focus is not
morphologically marked in Spanish but is systematically tied to **Nuclear Stress**: in a
non-emphatic declarative, main sentential stress falls by default on the rightmost (most deeply
embedded) stressed syllable of the predicate, and this stress position must coincide with the
[+Focus] constituent (the "**Focus Prosody Correspondence Principle**," Zubizarreta 1998) — stress can
never fall on [-Focus] (presupposed) material. When the "natural" Focus constituent (e.g. a
pre-verbal subject in answer to "who went home?") isn't in the position where Nuclear Stress normally
falls, the mismatch is resolved by EITHER shifting to emphatic (non-default) stress while leaving word
order intact (*JoSÉ fue a casa*) OR reordering constituents so the Focus item lands in the
stress-final position while using ordinary Nuclear Stress (*Fue a casa JosÉ* — i.e. V-O-S order,
termed "**p(rosodic)-movement**," triggered specifically by a [Focus] feature mismatch).
**Topic** = the presupposed constituent a sentence is "about" (Comrie 1989; formalized via Stalnaker
1978's "context set," following Reinhart 1982/Zubizarreta 1989) — in response to *¿Qué pasó con
José?*, *José* is the Topic of the reply. Topics can be newly introduced via framing phrases like
*en cuanto a*/*hablando de* ("hanging Topics"/Left Dislocation) and can be syntactically dislocated,
either left (*[Hablando de] José, lo eligieron presidente*, with an obligatory intonational
juncture/pause) or right (*Lo eligieron presidente, a José* — right-dislocated Topics differ from
left-dislocated ones in disallowing the *hablando de* framing phrase). Dislocated Topics are
obligatorily SPECIFIC in interpretation — a direct consequence of Topic-hood being a subcase of
presupposed/non-focal material — evidenced by the ungrammaticality of dislocating a non-specific
indefinite or negative-quantifier subject (*?*Algunos estudiantes, leyeron ese capítulo, pero no sé
cuáles* — contrast the fully grammatical non-dislocated *Algunos estudiantes leyeron...*; similarly
*?*Ningún estudiante, leyó ese capítulo*).

#### 5.4 [Topic]-movement to Spec-IP: a functional-feature account and its problems (p.213-220)
Proposes that pre-verbal non-subject XPs (*Todos los días compra Juan el diario*; *Ayer presentó
María su renuncia*) arise from movement of a [+Topic]-bearing constituent to Spec-IP, driven by a
[Topic] feature on INFL analogous to the N-feature/V-feature checking mechanism of Ch.4 — extending
Koopman & Sportiche (1991)'s proposal that in Spanish/Italian-type languages, INFL assigns Nominative
Case rightward, VP-internally (not to Spec-IP), freeing Spec-IP to NOT be a pure A-position and
instead serve as a landing site for A-bar-type Topic movement (also independently proposed as the
wh-landing site, previewing Ch.6.2). If the subject itself is the Topic, it moves to Spec-IP (deriving
ordinary S-V-O); if a different constituent is the Topic, that XP moves instead and the subject stays
low/post-verbal (deriving XP-V-S-O); a further extension treats the VP/predicate itself as a
potential Topic, moving as a unit and stranding the subject VP-finally (deriving V-O-S, *Compró el
coche María* in answer to *¿Quién compró el coche?*, where the subject is Focus). This elegantly
unifies the three-way order alternation from 5.2 under one mechanism, but runs into several
unresolved problems (5.4.2-5.4.3), explicitly flagged as such: (i) unlike true A-bar movements
(Contrastive Focus fronting, wh-movement, all independently restricted to exactly ONE moved
constituent per clause, e.g. ***Esos ZAPATOS en VERANO no se pone*, two CF-fronted phrases), Topic
preposing is NOT similarly restricted — multiple embedded Topics can co-occur (*Me dijeron que
Caterina, en verano, no se pone esos zapatos*) — suggesting Topic-fronting isn't driven by the same
feature-checking logic as genuine A-bar movement; (ii) Topics allow/require a clitic double (*Esos
zapatos, no se *(los) pone en verano*), while Contrastive Focus movement categorically disallows one
(*Esos ZAPATOS no se (*los) pone en verano*) — a further dissimilarity to standard A-bar movement;
(iii) a [Topic]-feature account predicts a pre-verbal constituent should always BE a Topic, but a
pre-verbal subject need not have any definable discourse role at all, and clauses can have no
pre-verbal Topic whatsoever (verb-initial orders) — both facts left unexplained by strict feature-
checking; (iv) ordering relative to the independently-motivated NegP of Ch.4.5: since a pre-verbal
subject/Topic must precede *no*/*nunca* (***No Juan cantó eso*; *Juan no cantó eso*), and NegP sits
above IP in the clausal spine, Topics must actually be landing even higher than IP, in some
additional functional projection above NegP — adding further structural overhead the chapter flags
as unresolved. These cumulative problems motivate the alternative, non-movement analysis in 5.5.

#### 5.5 Dislocated Topics: base-generated adjuncts, not movement — Left Dislocation (LD) vs. Clitic Left Dislocation (CLLD) (p.220-222+)
Introduces a second major approach: rather than deriving pre-verbal Topics by movement to Spec-IP,
treat (at least overtly-dislocated) Topics as **base-generated adjuncts**, construed with a
clause-internal position only via an independent coreference/binding relation — not via movement at
all (Cinque 1990, extending an Italian-based distinction to Spanish). Two systematically distinct
dislocated-Topic constructions are identified and contrasted feature-by-feature:
**Left Dislocation (LD)**: dislocated constituent restricted to DP only (never PP/other categories,
***De Juan, no me acuerdo de él*); the coreferential clause-internal element may be any OVERT
category — full phrase, tonic pronoun, or epithet (*El baloncesto, ese deporte le encanta a tu hijo*;
*Miles Davis, él sí que me fascina*; *Pedro,...el desgraciado se lleva con todo el mundo*); restricted
to root clauses only; may be preceded by topicalizing expressions (*en cuanto a*); need not show
grammatical/selectional "connectivity" between the dislocated phrase and its associate; non-recursive
(only one LD per clause); insensitive to syntactic islands.
**Clitic Left Dislocation (CLLD)**: dislocated constituent can be any category (DP, PP — *De Juan, no
me acuerdo*; *A Juan, lo vimos en la fiesta*); the coreferential element must be COVERT (never an
overt associate — ***En Juan, no es posible confiar en él*; ***A María, no la vi nunca a esa chica*),
realized only as an empty category, optionally spelled out via an obligatory clitic double when the
dislocated phrase's grammatical function independently supports one (a direct object gets a
clitic double; a PP complement like *de Juan*, lacking any corresponding clitic in Spanish's clitic
inventory per Ch.1.2.4, gets none); possible in both root AND embedded clauses (*Sin embargo,
Bernardo, estoy segura que nadie confía en ese idiota* — embedded CLLD grammatical; the LD counterpart
of this exact sentence, with the dislocation inside the embedded clause, is ungrammatical);
cannot be preceded by topicalizing expressions; DOES require grammatical/selectional connectivity
with its associate; recursive (multiple CLLDs can stack); sensitive only to "strong" islands (not weak
ones) — unlike LD's total island-insensitivity. This LD/CLLD distinction — particularly CLLD's
root-and-embedded distribution and obligatory covert/clitic-doubled associate — is what the chapter
goes on (in the remainder of 5.5, continuing past this excerpt) to extend as a possible unified
analysis of ALL pre-verbal Topic-like constituents, including ordinary pre-verbal subjects, as
base-generated adjuncts rather than moved specifiers — resolving several of 5.4's open problems
(multiple/optional Topics, clitic-doubling asymmetry with true A-bar movement) by denying that
Topic-fronting is movement to a specifier position at all.

#### 5.5 (continued) LD/CLLD derivations, and pre-verbal subjects reanalyzed as CLLD adjuncts (p.224-229)
**LD derivation**: base-generated directly as the Specifier of a dedicated **Topic Phrase** in root
clauses, with NO grammatical link to the clause-internal coreferential phrase — correctly predicting
overt-associate possibility, connectivity mismatches (Case/gender/number disagreement between the
dislocated phrase and its associate is tolerated, e.g. *Nosotros, nadie nos ha visto* mismatches Case;
*El ordenador, yo odio esas máquinas infernales* mismatches gender/number), and total island-
insensitivity (LD is grammatical even when its associate sits inside a relative clause or sentential
subject — strong islands for ordinary movement). LD's non-recursiveness is attributed to Spec-of-
TopicPhrase being a unique checking position (parallel to other single-specifier functional checking
relations already established).
**CLLD derivation** (Cinque 1990): the dislocated phrase is a clausal ADJUNCT (not a specifier),
linked to a clause-internal silent pronominal (`pro`, identified/licensed by an obligatory clitic
where one independently exists) via an **A-bar CHAIN formed WITHOUT movement** — a novel theoretical
move: normally a chain requires actual movement (moved-constituent + trace), but Cinque proposes a
chain can also be constructed purely through coindexing a base-generated adjunct with an independent
pronominal, inheriting movement-like locality effects (strong-island sensitivity, obligatory
connectivity) without being derived by movement itself. Three diagnostics confirm CLLD is NOT true
A-bar movement despite resembling it: (a) true A-bar movement (e.g. wh-fronting) categorically
disallows a clitic double of the extraction site (***¿Qué zapatos los compró Susana?*), while CLLD
requires one — explained by positing that a true movement-created empty category is a Case-requiring
"variable," and clitics independently absorb/consume Case (per Ch.4.4's analysis), so a
clitic-doubled gap could never be a legitimate movement-trace in the first place; (b) true A-bar
movement is restricted to exactly one fronted constituent per clause (***¿A quién qué libro le
regalaste?*), while CLLD is fully recursive/stackable (*A Pedro, ese libro, se lo regalé*); (c) true
A-bar movement triggers subject-verb inversion effects (***¿Qué zapatos Susana compró?*), while CLLD
is fully compatible with an independently pre-verbal subject (*Estos zapatos, Susana los compró*) —
deferred to Ch.6 for the inversion facts themselves.
**(5.5.3) Extending CLLD to ordinary pre-verbal subjects**: the chapter's final major proposal is that
even an UNmarked (non-intonationally-set-off) pre-verbal subject (*María compró el coche*) may itself
be a CLLD-style IP-adjunct linked to a VP-internal covert pronominal subject `pro` (which is the TRUE
grammatical subject, moving only as far as Spec-IP to check N-features) — NOT a moved/raised DP
itself. Evidence: limited "connectivity" violations are tolerated between a pre-verbal subject-Topic
and the verb's own agreement, e.g. person mismatch is possible when the verb's own plural inflection
can independently include 1st/2nd person (*Los estudiantes tenemos un alto concepto de nosotros
mismos* — the reflexive agrees with `pro`, i.e. with the verb's own person features, not with the
fronted DP *los estudiantes* itself) — a connectivity gap inconsistent with ordinary movement/
specifier-checking but consistent with an adjunct-plus-coindexed-pronoun structure. This CLLD-style
analysis resolves 5.4's open problems (optionality/non-uniqueness of pre-verbal Topics; the absence
of movement-typical properties) but leaves one gap: it does NOT explain why unmarked, no-Topic,
"neutral"/out-of-the-blue declaratives still default to S-V-O rather than V-S-O — the chapter's
proposed reconciliation is that the independently-needed [Topic]-feature-checking movement (5.4) is
ALSO active in neutral contexts (with a [-Topic] value), and economy considerations favor moving the
subject specifically (since subject-movement simultaneously checks N-features too) over moving any
other constituent — i.e., the two analyses (5.4's movement account and 5.5's adjunct account) are
proposed to COEXIST, covering different cases, rather than one superseding the other.

### 5.6 Subject order and the Null-Subject (NS) Parameter (p.229-239)
Introduces the chapter's final topic: the classic generative hypothesis (Jaeggli 1982; Rizzi 1982,
already previewed descriptively in Ch.1.2.8) that free subject postposing and null subjects are
TWO SURFACE REFLEXES OF ONE UNDERLYING PARAMETER. **5.6.1** walks through the history: pre-Theory
phrase-structure-rule approaches (Chomsky 1965) directly generated S-initial subjects by rule;
early Government & Binding (Chomsky 1981) instead derived this via Theta-theory, positing (the "VP-
EXTERNAL subject hypothesis") that the external-argument Theta-role is assigned directly in Spec-IP
itself, with Case assigned by INFL "governing" that same position (a technical but informally-glossed
notion: a head governs constituents within its own maximal projection that have no more local
governor — e.g. INFL governs VP but not the direct object inside VP, since the verb is a more
proximate governor for that). This VP-external analysis is explicitly contrasted with the
VP-INTERNAL subject hypothesis already adopted throughout the book since Ch.3.3, setting up the
chapter's continued discussion of how the NS Parameter interacts with the book's own VP-internal
subject analysis (the comparison continues past this excerpt).

#### 5.6 (continued): classic Government & Binding analyses of the NS Parameter (p.231-237)
On the VP-external hypothesis's derivation of a post-verbal subject, DP1 (the external argument,
base-generated — on that older hypothesis — directly in Spec-IP) cannot get Case there from a plain
transitive verb (which assigns only Accusative to its complement), forcing movement to Spec-IP where
INFL governs and Case-licenses it — meaning even under the OLDER VP-external analysis, a Case-driven
movement step to Spec-IP is still required, just reorganized relative to the book's actual (VP-
internal) analysis from Ch.3. Classic NS-parameter proposals attribute null subjects AND free subject
postposing to a single lexical property of "rich" verbal agreement, cashed out in two specific
historical implementations: (a) **Rizzi (1982)**: AGR is itself clitic-pronoun-like and can "absorb"
Case, which (i) lets a null pronoun legitimately occupy Spec-IP (identified via agreement/
co-indexing with AGR) and (ii) independently licenses **subject postposing**, analyzed as RIGHTWARD
movement of the subject, adjoining it to VP, with the vacated Spec-IP trace then replaced by an
inserted covert PLEONASTIC pronoun (since an NP-trace needs a structurally higher antecedent that
rightward-adjunction can't supply, but a plain pronoun needs no antecedent) — and because this
postposed subject ends up governed by the VERB rather than INFL, it is predicted to behave like a
complement for extraction purposes, confirmed by a subject/object symmetry contrast with overt
complementizers in Spanish/Italian interrogatives that English systematically lacks (the classic
*that*-trace effect: English disallows subject extraction over overt *that* — ***who did you say that
saw John* — but allows object extraction over it — *who did you say that John saw*; Spanish/Italian
allow BOTH, attributed to the postposed subject's complement-like governor). (b) **Jaeggli (1982)**:
AGR undergoes affix-hopping onto the verb at S-structure, so the postposed subject is governed by the
derived V+AGR complex. Both historical analyses are then flagged as having a THEORY-INTERNAL PROBLEM
under later Minimalist assumptions: they require "lowering" rules (rightward subject-postposing,
affix-hopping) that move material rightward/downward, which later theories (feature-checking,
Kayne 1994's no-rightward-movement hypothesis) exclude as unavailable operations entirely — this
theoretical shift is what motivates reanalyzing subject postposing under the VP-INTERNAL subject
hypothesis instead (Ch.3.3's analysis): since the postposed subject was ALREADY generated low, inside
VP, no "lowering" is needed at all — a null pre-verbal subject is just the covert VP-internal `pro`
assigned Case in situ (via Koopman & Sportiche 1991's rightward-Case-assignment parameter, already
introduced in 5.4.1), and an overt post-verbal subject is simply that same VP-internal DP never having
moved, with Case assigned to it in place — both correctly predicted to behave like complements for
extraction, without requiring any actual rightward movement step.

#### 5.6.2 Richness of AGR reconsidered under feature strength; Chapter 5 summary (p.237-240)
Raises (without fully resolving) whether the strong/weak-N-feature parameter already used to explain
constituent-order flexibility (5.4-5.5) is also what licenses null subjects, or whether these are
in fact two independent parameters (flagged explicitly as an open question, citing Montalbetti 1986
on covert-vs-overt-pronoun interpretive differences as a reason the two might not simply collapse
together). Offers an alternative (Belletti 1990, following Roberts 1990, re: French/Italian):
agreement morphology can itself be structured either as a **sub-lexical (X⁻¹) affix** (French/
English-type — syntactically "invisible," since sublexical material cannot itself move/check
features) or as an independent **zero-level (X⁰) morpheme** merged with the verb stem
(Spanish/Italian-type) — on this view, N-features of INFL could be STRONG in both language types, but
only Spanish/Italian's verb-external agreement-head structure lets the raised V+Agr complex itself
satisfy (head-adjunction) checking of those N-features, obviating the need for a DP to also move to
Spec-IP; English's sublexical agreement can never check a head-level feature, forcing the English
pattern of literal DP-movement to Spec-IP instead. **Chapter 5 summary** (5.7) explicitly presents the
whole chapter as leaving the pre-verbal-subject analysis genuinely unsettled between two coexisting
mechanisms — (i) Topic-feature-driven movement to Spec-IP (5.4, needed at minimum for neutral/no-Topic
S-V-O contexts) and (ii) CLLD-style base-generated adjunction linked to a VP-internal `pro` (5.5,
preferred for genuinely Topic-marked pre-verbal subjects) — alongside an unresolved question of
whether the Null-Subject Parameter and the constituent-order flexibility parameter are the same
parameter or two independent ones.

### Chapter 6 — A'-movement and X⁰ movement through COMP (p.241-272)

#### 6.2 Wh-movement: the Wh-Criterion, Argumental Agreement Licensing, and the landing-site problem (p.241-248)
Two core wh-question properties: (i) obligatory fronting of exactly one wh-phrase (an unmoved
wh-phrase is possible only as an "echo question," a special non-ordinary-interrogative reading: *¿María
leyó qué?* only echoing a prior assertion, never neutral); (ii) restricted verb position ("inversion" —
a subject generally cannot intervene between a fronted wh-phrase and the verb: ***¿Qué esos dos
querían?*). **6.2.1**: both properties are initially derived from Rizzi (1996)'s **Wh-Criterion** — a
wh-operator must be in a Spec-head configuration with an X⁰ bearing [+Wh], and vice versa — requiring
a wh-phrase to raise to Spec-CP where C⁰ is [+Wh]; verb-inversion is attributed to [+Wh] originating on
INFL in root clauses (requiring INFL-to-C movement to satisfy the Criterion) vs. originating directly
on C⁰ in complement clauses (no movement needed) — explaining an English root/embedded inversion
asymmetry (*what did you buy* vs. *I wonder what you bought*, no inversion) that, notably, Spanish and
Italian do NOT replicate: Spanish shows inversion in EMBEDDED questions too (*no sabía qué querían
esos dos* / ***no sabía qué esos dos querían*), suggesting [+Wh] is generated on INFL cross-
clausally in these languages, not just in root clauses. **6.2.2**: this CP-landing-site/INFL-to-C
account is then challenged on two empirical grounds (Suñer 1994): (a) pre-verbal IP-adverbs can
intervene between the fronted wh-phrase and the verb (*¿A quién jamás ofenderías tú...?*), impossible
if the verb had raised all the way to C⁰; (b) SOME wh-phrases are fully compatible with a pre-verbal
subject (*¿En qué medida la constitución ha contribuido a eso?*; *¿Por qué Juan quiere salir...?*) —
specifically, the generalization is that only ARGUMENT wh-phrases categorically block a pre-verbal
subject, while non-argument (adverbial) wh-phrases do not. Suñer's alternative mechanism,
**Argumental Agreement Licensing**: an argumental wh-phrase in Spec-CP must indirectly Arg-agree with
the verb (since C⁰ itself has no features) UNLESS a closer argument (an intervening pre-verbal
subject) blocks that agreement relation — deriving the asymmetry without any actual V-to-C movement,
with the verb staying in INFL throughout. (A later footnote flags Goodall 1991 as disputing the clean
argument/non-argument split, noting that some adjunct wh-words like *cuándo*/*dónde* are ALSO
incompatible with pre-verbal subjects — an unresolved complication.) **6.2.3**: further landing-site
evidence comes from embedded reported-question constructions where an overt complementizer *que*
PRECEDES the fronted wh-phrase (*Te preguntan que para qué quieres el préstamo*) — restricted to
complements of verbs of saying, and correlating specifically with a reported-QUESTION (vs.
reported-assertion) interpretation (Plann 1982). This motivates three competing landing-site
hypotheses for the wh-phrase, none fully adopted as conclusive: (a) a recursive-CP structure (Rivero
1978) with the wh-phrase in a lower Spec-CP; (b) Spec-IP itself as the wh-landing site (Goodall 1991)
— consistent with the VP-internal-subject-plus-V-to-INFL analysis already established, correctly
predicting inversion since V-to-INFL still crosses over the subject's VP-internal position; (c) a
distinct functional projection between CP and IP whose specifier hosts the wh-phrase — flagged as
motivated mainly by the parallel Focus-phrase facts taken up next in 6.3.

#### 6.3 (Contrastive) Focus: Focus Movement as A-bar movement parallel to wh-movement (p.248-251)
**Focus Movement** fronts an emphatically-stressed constituent to clause-initial position (*MARÍA
compró los tomates...*; *En el MERCADO compró María los tomates*), semantically associated with a
cleft-sentence paraphrase and a presupposition-denial/reassertion reading (Zubizarreta 1998) — also
termed "Topicalization"/"Rhematization"/"Informational Focus" elsewhere in the literature, but settled
on "Focus Movement" here. **6.3.1** properties: carries the clause's intonational peak (shifted from
its normal rightmost/predicate-final position, per Ch.5.3.1's Focus-stress generalization); licenses
an explicit contrastive extension (*MARÍA compró los tomates..., no José*); paraphrases as a cleft.
**6.3.2**: Focus phrases pattern with wh-phrases on every core A-bar-movement diagnostic, supporting
a unified Focus-movement-as-A-bar-movement analysis (Hernanz & Brucart 1987): (a) triggers the SAME
subject-verb inversion effect (*ESE capítulo leyó por completo Josefina* / ***ESE capítulo Josefina
leyó por completo*) — ruling out a base-generated-adjunct analysis of Focus phrases, since adjuncts
don't independently trigger inversion; (b) obeys island constraints identically to wh-movement
(***ESE poema conozco a la mujer que escribió*, parallel to the wh-counterpart ***¿Qué poema conoces a
la mujer que escribió?*); (c) categorically disallows a clitic double of the extraction site, exactly
like wh-movement and exactly UNlike CLLD (***ESOS tomates los compró María*; ***¿Cuáles tomates los
compró María?* — directly reusing the Case-absorption-blocks-a-true-variable argument from Ch.5.5.2);
(d) restricted to exactly one fronted constituent per clause, like wh-movement (***AYER los TOMATES
compró Juan*; ***¿Cuándo qué compró Juan?*); (e) a Focus phrase and a wh-phrase cannot BOTH front in
the same clause, in either relative order (***¿Cuándo las MANZANAS compraron?*; ***¿Las MANZANAS
cuándo compraron?*) — suggesting Focus-movement and wh-movement compete for the SAME single landing
site, reinforcing the unified-mechanism analysis; (f) both are independently compatible with a
preceding dislocated (CLLD-style) Topic (*En octubre, POCAS manzanas compraron*; *En octubre, ¿qué
compraron?*), confirming Topic-dislocation (Ch.5.5) is a structurally higher, independent process from
either wh- or Focus-movement.

#### 6.3.2 (continued): semantic selection differences; 6.3.3 landing site — a Focus Phrase (FocP) between NegP and CP (p.251-254)
One asymmetry between wh- and Focus-movement: wh-movement in a complement clause requires the matrix
verb to semantically select an interrogative ([+Wh]) complement (*María se pregunta qué compraron*
grammatical under "wonder"; ***María anunció qué compraron* ungrammatical under "announce," since
"announce" doesn't select a question-type complement; rescuable only as an unrelated free-relative
reading). Focus movement shows an analogous but distinct selectional restriction, keyed to
assertive vs. presupposed/factive complementation rather than [+Wh]: grammatical under assertive
*decir* "say" (*Dice que MAÑANA lo operan*) but ungrammatical under factive/presupposing *sentir*
"regret" (***Siento que MAÑANA lo operen*) — the exact feature responsible is left unidentified, but
the parallel to wh-selection is treated as further evidence that Focus-fronting is a genuine
S-selected movement, not a free adjunction option. **Landing site**: extending the wh-phrase
landing-site debate (6.2.3), Focus phrases ALSO surface to the right of an embedded complementizer
(*Dice que MAÑANA lo operan*) — ruling out a simple "Focus moves to Spec-CP" analysis. Positional
diagnostics against NegP (Ch.4.5): a fronted Focus constituent must precede *no* (*Dice que MAÑANA no
lo operan*) and must precede an n-word in Spec-NegP (*Dice que en ese HOSPITAL nunca lo operaron*
grammatical; ***Dice que nunca en ese HOSPITAL lo operaron* ungrammatical) — placing Focus structurally
ABOVE NegP. The book argues against treating this as plain NegP-adjunction (which would wrongly allow
multiple stacked Focus constituents, parallel to ordinary adjunction's general non-uniqueness, and
would not explain the wh-/Focus-movement S-selection parallel) and instead posits a dedicated
**Focus Phrase (FocP)**, with the resulting clausal spine: **COMP – Foc – Neg – INFL – (Aux) – Aspect
– VP**. This FocP analysis is argued to uniformly capture: the one-constituent-per-clause restriction
(paralleling wh-phrases and NegP's own single-specifier restriction from Ch.4.5.2); the S-selection
effects; and the Focus/wh-movement competition-for-one-landing-site facts from 6.3.2 (since both
wh-phrases and Focus phrases are argued to target the same structural position/FocP specifier when
not independently forced into Spec-CP).

#### 6.4 Other A-bar movements: Scrambling, parasitic gaps, complex adjectivals (p.254-259+)
**6.4.1 Scrambling** (object-fronting reanalysis of V-O-S order): revisits the classic rightward-
subject-postposing analysis of V-O-S (Rizzi 1982, Ch.5.6.1) in light of later theoretical restrictions
against rightward movement altogether (Kayne 1994). Ordóñez (1997)'s alternative: V-O-S is derived by
LEFTWARD movement of the OBJECT (a "**Scrambling**" operation) past a VP-internal subject that stays
put in its base Spec-VP position (*[compró_i [el diccionario_j t_i [VP Juan t_i t_j]]]* — verb raises
via Agr-o to Tense as usual; the object additionally raises to some position outside VP, ending up
structurally higher than, and c-commanding, the subject). Evidence for this reanalysis over simple
rightward subject-adjunction: in multiple wh-questions, a post-verbal wh-SUBJECT must precede a
wh-COMPLEMENT within the remaining VP material (*¿Qué le compró quién a quién?* grammatical;
***¿Qué le compró a quién quién?* ungrammatical) — explained if the complement wh-phrase has
independently Scrambled leftward past the subject (and wh-phrases are independently known to resist
Scrambling specifically, e.g. in German), while a non-Scrambling wh-movement of the SAME complement
wh-phrase all the way to clause-initial position remains fully grammatical even crossing the subject
wh-phrase (*¿Qué dijo quién de quién?* — compare the ungrammatical mid-clause-Scrambled counterpart
***¿Qué dijo de quién quién?*) — and this asymmetry is explicitly NOT reducible to a general
"Superiority" violation (blocking object-over-subject wh-movement), since the fully-fronted object-
wh-over-subject-wh order (*¿Qué dijo quién?*) is independently grammatical in Spanish (unlike in
English, ***What did who say?*), isolating the effect specifically to the Scrambling operation.
**6.4.2 Parasitic gaps**: an A-bar chain in the main clause can license a SECOND ("parasitic") gap
inside an adjunct clause coreferential with the moved wh-phrase (*¿Qué libro archivaste sin leer –?*
grammatical; the non-wh-moved counterpart ***Archivaste ese libro sin leer –* and the A-movement
counterpart ***Ese libro parece haber sido archivado sin leer –* are both ungrammatical — showing
specifically an A-BAR chain, not just any moved/raised NP, licenses the parasitic gap). Analyzed
(Chomsky 1982) as movement of a phonetically NULL OPERATOR within the adjunct clause, forming its own
A-bar chain coindexed with the main clause's wh-chain. This null-operator chain shows standard A-bar
island-sensitivity (***¿Qué libro archivaste sin preguntarte cuándo leer –?*, the gap trapped inside
a wh-island) but is demonstrably MORE restrictive than ordinary wh-movement in Spanish specifically:
it disallows even successive-cyclic movement through intermediate CPs that would otherwise be legal
for a true wh-phrase (contrast the grammatical English parallel *which articles did you put on
reserve without convincing the students to read?* against its ungrammatical literal Spanish
translation, García Mayo & Kempchinsky 1994) — i.e., Spanish null-operator movement for parasitic
gaps is confined to a single clause, unlike genuinely long-distance wh-movement (illustrated
separately with a fully grammatical multi-clause-crossing wh-question, *¿Qué artículo dijiste que
creyó María que insistió Pedro...?*).
**6.4.3 Complex adjectivals** (*difícil*, *fácil*, *imposible* + *de*-infinitive): an alternation
between an impersonal construction with the infinitive's object in its ordinary post-verbal position
(*Será difícil componer estas radios*) and a construction where that logical object surfaces as the
MATRIX SUBJECT (*Estas radios serán difíciles de componer*). Aissen & Perlmutter (1976) show the
preposed NP is a genuine clausal SUBJECT (not a Topic/adjunct), evidenced by: licensing a null subject
reading, triggering ordinary matrix subject-verb agreement, and undergoing further raising under a
higher predicate like *parecer* (*Estas radios parecen ser difíciles de componer*) — but this subject
is ALSO interpreted as the Theta-marked complement of the embedded infinitive (*estas radios serán
difíciles [de componer e]*), raising a theoretical puzzle (continued past this excerpt): a plain single
movement step directly from the embedded object position to the matrix subject position would
illegitimately violate Binding Principle A (the chapter's explanation of exactly how this is resolved
— presumably again via null-operator movement, paralleling 6.4.2 — continues beyond the read excerpt).

#### 6.4.3 (continued), 6.4.4, 6.4.5: resolving complex adjectivals via null-operator A-bar movement; indefinite null objects (p.259-263)
Resolves 6.4.3's puzzle (how can the preposed subject of a complex-adjectival construction be BOTH a
genuine matrix subject AND Theta-linked to the embedded infinitive's object position without a
Binding-Principle-A violation, since direct long NP-movement would leave a trace too far from its
antecedent, and a step-wise raising-through-embedded-subject derivation is independently ruled out by
the fact that the embedded infinitive's real subject is an arbitrary PRO, not the preposed NP itself).
Chomsky (1981)'s solution: NOT movement of the matrix-subject NP itself at all. Instead, a NULL
PRONOMINAL originates as the infinitive's object and moves (A-bar-style) to the specifier of the
embedded CP, coindexed with the matrix-subject NP, which is independently INSERTED directly in matrix
subject position at some point before S-structure (never literally moved there from inside the
embedded clause) — avoiding the Theta-Criterion violation by assuming the NP isn't present at
D-structure in the first place. Two pieces of evidence support analyzing this as A-BAR movement (of
the null pronoun) rather than ordinary A-movement/Raising: (a) complex adjectivals license parasitic
gaps (*Este problema es fácil de resolver sin examinar detenidamente* — grammatical, paralleling
wh-movement's parasitic-gap-licensing capacity from 6.4.2), while true A-movement/Raising
constructions do NOT (***Este problema parece haber sido resuelto sin examinar detenidamente* —
exactly mirroring the parasitic-gap A-movement/A-bar-movement asymmetry already established in
6.4.2's (46a) vs (46c) contrast); (b) this null-operator movement is clause-bounded in exactly the
same way as parasitic-gap null-operator movement (***Ese libro es difícil de convencer a los
estudiantes de leer*, blocked from crossing an intervening clause) — the parallel locality supports
treating both constructions as the same underlying mechanism.
**6.4.4 Indefinite null objects**: ordinary null direct objects are generally ungrammatical in
Spanish (***Compré* for "I bought [something]"), but become grammatical specifically under an
INDEFINITE interpretation in an appropriate discourse context (*¿Compraste café? Sí, compré* — already
previewed in Ch.1.7.1's dialectal null-object discussion, here treated as a general, non-dialectal
possibility tied to indefiniteness). Campos (1986), following Raposo (1984) for Portuguese, argues
this is ALSO null-operator A-bar movement, evidenced by island-sensitivity: the null object is fine
across an ordinary complement-clause boundary (*Su novia me dijo que traería* — the null operator can
pass through the embedded CP's specifier as an intermediate landing site en route to the matrix CP)
but ungrammatical when trapped inside a complex-NP island (***Existe el rumor de que traerá*) — a
contrast directly paralleling classic wh-island effects. Notably flagged as a genuine theoretical loose
end: UNLIKE the clause-bounded null-operator movement of parasitic gaps (6.4.2) and complex adjectivals
(6.4.3), null-object movement is explicitly NOT clause-bounded (it can proceed successive-cyclically
through intermediate CPs) — the chapter does not resolve why these superficially-similar null-operator
constructions differ in locality.

### 6.5 Head movement to (and through) COMP (p.263-271)
Returns to head movement (contrasted with the A-bar PHRASAL movements of 6.2-6.4), addressing clause
types where V/I raises all the way to C overtly (distinct from ordinary declarative V-to-INFL, which
Ch.6.2.2 already showed does NOT extend to I-to-C in ordinary questions).
**6.5.1 V-to-INFL-to-C in true imperatives and participial clauses**: distinguishes **"true"
imperatives** (a unique verb-form morphology not shared by any other tense/person — Spanish true
imperatives exist only in 2nd person: *canta* "sing!") from **"surrogate" imperatives** (reusing an
existing tense's morphology, e.g. negative imperatives reusing present subjunctive forms: *no cantes*
— already described descriptively in Ch.1.5.3). Two diagnostics argue true imperatives involve overt
V-to-INFL-to-C movement: (a) **obligatory encliticization** — true imperatives require a following
enclitic (*¡Hazlo!* / ***¡Lohaz!*), a cross-linguistically common signature of V-raised-past-Agr-o
structures (clitics occupying Agr-o are bypassed when V raises all the way to a higher C position,
stranding them as enclitics), while surrogate imperatives show ordinary proclisis (*¡No lo hagas!*);
(b) **incompatibility with negation** (***¡No hazlo!* — true imperative with negation is out;
surrogate *¡No lo hagas!* is the only way to negate a command) — explained because the independently-
motivated NegP (Ch.4.5) sits structurally between INFL and COMP, and the negative head itself acts as
a closer potential antecedent that BLOCKS the verb-trace antecedent-trace relationship needed for
I-to-C movement to succeed, so a true imperative simply cannot be formed under negation at all (the
surrogate/subjunctive strategy sidesteps this since it doesn't involve I-to-C movement in the first
place). The same two diagnostics are then applied to the broader class of non-finite adjunct clauses
(gerundive, infinitival, participial — already described purely descriptively in Ch.1.6.4-1.6.5,
including their ban on pre-verbal subjects) to probe whether THEY also involve I-to-C: gerundive/
infinitival adjuncts show ordinary enclisis (*habiéndolo terminado*) but DO tolerate negation (*No
habiendo terminado la reunión...*; *Al no encontrar el artículo...*) — suggesting these do NOT
involve I-to-C (negation-tolerance is inconsistent with a Neg-blocks-movement account) — while
**participial (absolutive) clauses** categorically disallow BOTH object clitics (*publicado el
artículo* / ***publicádolo* — though this diagnostic is inconclusive on its own, since participles
independently lack clitics for unrelated reasons established back in Ch.1.6.4/3.2) AND negation
(***No terminada la reunión...*) — the negation ban specifically is the decisive diagnostic, so
participial clauses pattern with true imperatives as involving I-to-C movement, distinguishing them
from the broader non-finite-adjunct class. (A cross-linguistic footnote: Italian participial-clause
counterparts allow object clitics but still disallow negation, Belletti 1990 — suggesting the
clitic-diagnostic genuinely dissociates from the negation-diagnostic cross-linguistically, consistent
with treating only the negation-incompatibility as diagnostic of I-to-C.) General caveat: since the
VP-internal-subject/feature-strength framework (Ch.3-5) already independently predicts that non-finite
clauses may simply never move their subject out of VP regardless of whether I-to-C has applied, the
pre-verbal-subject-ban facts alone are NOT conclusive evidence for I-to-C movement — only the
negation/encliticization diagnostics are treated as decisive.

#### 6.5.2 Clitic Climbing as head-movement of the clitic alone, through COMP (p.267-271)
Revisits "clitic climbing"/restructuring (*María lo quiere comprar*, already described structurally
in Ch.1.2.4/3.2/4.4) and proposes (Kayne 1989) that it is literally **head-movement of the clitic
ALONE** (independent of the verb, which stays VP-internal in the embedded clause) through a chain:
clitic moves to the embedded INFL, then via I-to-C movement carries the clitic into the embedded
COMP, from which position it can cliticize onto the matrix verb. This is explicitly restricted to a
specific closed class of "trigger" verbs (not all raising/control verbs: *parecer* "seem" is
independently an established raising verb from Ch.3.3.2 but is NOT a Clitic-Climbing trigger —
***María lo parece saber* is out despite *María parece saberlo* being fine). Two-part evidential
case: (a) clitics CAN move to INFL independently of V-to-INFL (cross-linguistic/diachronic evidence:
dialectal/archaic French allows an adverb to separate a climbed clitic from its infinitive, *Jean a
promis de les bien faire*, showing the clitic reached INFL without the verb itself moving there) —
and Kayne further correlates the cross-linguistic availability of Clitic Climbing with the Null-
Subject property (informally: NS-language INFL is independently a "strong enough" governor of VP to
let a clitic escape VP on its own, paralleling Ch.5.6's rich-agreement story — flagged in a footnote
as needing revision once "government" is eliminated under later Minimalist assumptions, with Treviño
1990 offering an alternative tying Clitic Climbing instead to the independently-established FREE
SUBJECT INVERSION property, since the clitic's path requires Spec-IP to be empty/available as a
staging position — illustrated by a causative-construction contrast where Clitic Climbing is blocked
specifically when an overt embedded subject fills Spec-IP, but restored once that same subject
undergoes free inversion, vacating Spec-IP again); (b) clitics must ALSO move through embedded COMP
specifically (not just directly clitic-to-clitic across clauses): evidenced by Clitic Climbing being
categorically impossible out of a FINITE (subjunctive) complement (*María quiere que Juan lo compre*
/ ***María lo quiere que Juan compre*, since finite COMP is independently filled by overt *que*,
blocking the clitic's transit) and by its interaction with wh-movement — marginally possible when the
embedded COMP's specifier (not its head) is filled by a wh-PHRASE (Italian *Non ti saprei che dire*,
and a parallel marginal Spanish contrast between *cómo* and *si*), but categorically blocked when
COMP's HEAD position itself is filled by an overt [+Wh] complementizer like *si* "whether" (*No li so
se fare*; Spanish parallel *No lo sé si arreglar*) — directly confirming the clitic's path runs
through the COMP head position itself, which a filled C⁰ blocks but a filled Spec-CP does not.

### 6.6 Chapter 6 (and book) summary (p.271-272)
Recaps: CP-movements are generally driven by the Wh-Criterion, refined by Argumental Agreement
Licensing for the Spanish-specific argument/non-argument wh-asymmetry; a dedicated Focus Phrase (FocP)
between NegP and CP is independently motivated for Focus-movement's distribution and its parallels to
wh-movement; several further A-bar movements (Scrambling, and null-operator movement in parasitic
gaps, complex adjectivals, and indefinite null objects) round out the phrasal-movement inventory; and
head-movement to/through COMP is independently diagnosed (via encliticization and negation-
incompatibility) in true imperatives and participial clauses, and separately in Clitic Climbing,
where only the clitic itself (not the verb) undergoes the COMP-transiting head movement.

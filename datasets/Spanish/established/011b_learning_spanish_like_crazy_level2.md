## Grammar points

### "Ir a" + infinitive as the periphrastic future, and its limited scope (Lección 31)

The source explicitly notes that the "substitute future" (ir a + infinitive, e.g. "voy a salir") is
used less broadly in Spanish than its English "going to" counterpart: for an imminent/momentary
departure, Spanish idiomatically prefers the simple present ("me voy," lit. "I leave") over "voy a
irme." Learners are cautioned this periphrastic future does not map 1:1 onto every English "going
to" use. (Lección 31, p. 5.)

### Stacked double-object clitic pronouns (indirect + direct)

Extensively drilled: when both an indirect object (a beneficiary, e.g. "for him/her/us/them") and a
direct object (the thing itself) are pronominalized together, they attach in fixed order
(indirect-then-direct) onto an infinitive or as separate pre-verbal clitics, with the indirect "le/
les" obligatorily becoming "se" before a third-person direct object: "hacérsela" (to make it for
him/her/them), "comprárnoslos" (to buy them for us), "cocinárselos" (to cook them for them). This
lesson is built almost entirely around drilling this clitic-stacking pattern across every person/
number combination. (Lección 31, throughout.)

### Comparative constructions: más/menos ... que (Lección 32)

Standard unequal comparison of adjectives/nouns: "más [adjective] que" (more ... than), "menos
[adjective] que" (less ... than), applied systematically across body-part vocabulary and possessive
pronouns (los míos/los tuyos/los suyos = "mine/yours/his-hers-theirs," agreeing in gender/number with
the noun possessed, not the possessor). The lesson is a systematic drill of possessive pronoun forms
in comparative frames. (Lección 32, throughout.)

### hay / hubo / había — three-way aspectual contrast in the existential "there is/are"

The source draws an explicit three-way distinction using the existential verb (from haber): "hay"
(present, unmarked for aspect), "hubo" (preterite — a completed, bounded past existential, e.g. "a
one-time count on a specific day"), and "había" (imperfect — an ongoing or habitual past state, e.g.
"there used to be X when I was young"). This is a clean illustration of the preterite/imperfect
aspectual split applied to a single high-frequency irregular verb. (Lección 33a, throughout.)

### más de / menos de before a bare numeral vs. más/menos que before a compared noun

The source distinguishes "más de cinco vacas" (more than five cows — "de" required before a bare
number) from the ordinary comparative "que" used when comparing against another noun phrase (e.g.
"más vacas que cerdos," more cows than pigs). This is a real, easily-confused Spanish comparative
rule the lesson drills systematically. (Lección 33a, pp. 1-2, 5.)

### Preterite vs. imperfect vs. present perfect of "volar," contrasted across the same lexical item

Lección 33b systematically drills the same verb ("volar," to fly) across present ("vuelo"), preterite
("volé," "voló," "volamos," "volaron"), imperfect ("volaba," "volábamos"), and present perfect ("he
volado," "ha volado") — all inside comparative "más/menos que" frames — giving a compact worked
paradigm of Spanish's tense/aspect system on one verb. (Lección 33b, throughout.)

### Equative comparison: tan + adjective + como (parity of quality) and tanto/tanta/tantos/tantas + noun + como (parity of quantity)

Lección 34 introduces the equative comparative, distinct from the unequal más/menos...que pattern:
"tan alto como" (as tall as) for adjectives, and "tanto/tanta/tantos/tantas" (agreeing in gender/
number with the noun being quantified — tantos martillos, tantas sillas) + "como" for noun-quantity
parity ("hay tantos martillos como clavos," there are as many hammers as nails). The source
explicitly calls out the gender-number agreement of "tanto" as the key contrast with the invariant
"tan" used before adjectives. (Lección 34, pp. 1-2.)

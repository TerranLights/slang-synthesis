# Spanish — Established Vocabulary/Register: McGraw-Hill's Spanish Student Dictionary (substantial sample)

**Source:** *McGraw-Hill's Spanish Student Dictionary* by Regina M. Qualls (McGraw-Hill, 2008), 512
PDF pages (486 printed pages + front/back matter). Genuine clean text layer (Acrobat Distiller 7.0.5);
`pdftotext` reads it directly, no vision-reading needed, no layout/column issues (single-column,
pronunciation respelling + bolded headword + definition + one Spanish example sentence with English
translation per sense, consistently formatted throughout). **Transcription confidence: verified
throughout.**

**Structure (confirmed by sampling and the book's own table of contents):** Introduction/how-to-use +
condensed Spanish grammar primer (PDF pp. 1-22, redundant with this language's existing grammar
coverage, not extracted) → **Spanish-English dictionary, PDF pp. 23-282 (≈260 printed pages)** →
Illustrations (PDF pp. 283-300) → **English-Spanish dictionary, PDF pp. 301-486 (mirrors the
Spanish-English headwords in reverse, not separately sampled)** → Appendices (PDF pp. 487+: Spanish/
English given-name equivalences, body-part/family/food/sports/zoo-animal/month vocabulary lists, a
"Top 10 Mistakes to Avoid" English-interference guide, and country maps). The appendices were skimmed
but not extracted in depth — the name-equivalence and category vocabulary lists are basic 1:1
translation lists with no register content, and the "Top 10 Mistakes" section (ser/estar, por/para,
noun-adjective agreement, compound nouns via *de*, literal-translation traps) duplicates ground this
language's established/ corpus already covers in more depth (033 Correct Your Spanish Blunders).

**Methodology — compact dictionary, fuller-than-the-big-dictionaries coverage, still not page-by-page
exhaustive.** Per the task's own reasoning, this is a pedagogically curated student dictionary (one
core sense per headword, one illustrative example sentence, clearly aimed at a learner rather than a
scholar) rather than a scholarly reference work — closer in character to this project's existing
vocabulary sources than to 047/048's exhaustive-headword model. Two complementary methods were used:
(1) **the entire 260-page Spanish-English section was extracted as plain text and grepped for the
dictionary's own colloquial-register tag (`coll.`)** — this surfaced every explicitly register-marked
entry in the whole section, not just a sample of it (confirmed: 14 hits, zero `vulg.`/`sl.` tags
anywhere in the section — this is a clean, school-safe dictionary with no vulgar content, consistent
with its intended student audience); (2) **21 individual pages spread evenly across the full
alphabet** (PDF pp. 30,40,60,70,90,100,110,130,140,150,160,170,190,200,210,220,230,240,250,260,270 —
roughly one page every 6-10 pages of content) were read in full for general vocabulary and example-
sentence register/usage flavor. This combination means the register-marked content below is
**exhaustive for this section** (every `coll.` tag in the Spanish-English half of the book is
represented), while the general-vocabulary rows are a representative sample, not exhaustive.

**Non-redundancy check:** this is basic/intermediate pedagogical vocabulary, much of which overlaps in
difficulty level with Practice Makes Perfect Vocabulary and Learning Spanish Like Crazy (already in
established/). Only entries carrying a register tag, an idiomatic multi-word expression, or an
example sentence illustrating a genuinely distinctive usage were kept below — plain 1:1 "tree = árbol"
style entries from the sampled pages were skipped as redundant with existing coverage, per this
project's non-redundant-supplement convention.

## Flagship finding

**This dictionary's register marking is minimal and exclusively colloquial (`coll.`) — no vulgar,
slang, regional, or archaic tags appear anywhere in the 260-page Spanish-English section.** All 14
`coll.`-tagged items are everyday conversational fixed expressions (*¿verdad?*, *sano y salvo*,
*vamos*, *a tiempo*) rather than anything taboo or edgy — appropriate for a dictionary explicitly
positioned for students. This is a useful **negative data point** for the project: compared to
McGraw-Hill's own big *Diccionario Enciclopédico* (047, same publisher, same `fam./fig./desp.`
tagging apparatus available), the student-dictionary edition's editors chose to keep register
annotation to a single, mild "colloquial" tag and omit anything stronger — a deliberate pedagogical
register ceiling, not a gap in the underlying language.

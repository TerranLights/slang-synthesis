# Spanish — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Practice Makes Perfect: Spanish Verb Tenses, Part I (The Present Tense, Chapters 1–12)

**Source:** Dorothy Richmond, *Practice Makes Perfect: Spanish Verb Tenses*, 2nd edition (McGraw-Hill,
2010), pp. 1–106 (Part I: The Present Tense — Ch. 1 Conjugation of regular verbs, Ch. 2 Ser and estar,
Ch. 3 Hay, Ch. 4 Tener, Ch. 5 The personal a, Ch. 6 Common irregular verbs, Ch. 7 Saber and conocer,
Ch. 8 Stem-changing verbs, Ch. 9 Irregular verb groups, Ch. 10 Reflexive verbs, Ch. 11 Verbs like gustar,
Ch. 12 The present progressive).

**Coverage note:** This is the first Spanish established/ file in the project (established/ was empty at
dispatch time), so no non-redundant-supplement scoping was needed — full coverage of this book's Part I.
This is a workbook-format reference: most of its bulk is translation/fill-in drill exercises reusing the
same vocabulary introduced in each section's own `VOCABULARIO` box. Per the coverage rule, every grammar
point and every distinct vocabulary item from each `VOCABULARIO` box (and interrogative/idiom lists
presented as vocabulary) is captured below; the individual numbered drill sentences and the "¿Verdadero o
falso?" personal-opinion exercises are skipped as repeated drill content that introduces no new
vocabulary or grammar beyond what's already listed. This source has a genuine clean text layer (confirmed
via `pdftotext`); no vision-reading was required for this chunk, though `pdfinfo` shows the file was
processed by a third-party "Advanced PDF Repair" tool (`Creator`/`Producer` metadata) — cross-checked
`-layout` output against the page-footer chapter/page markers throughout and found no scrambling or
column-interleaving artifacts in this range. Continued in `008b_...` (Part II) and `008c_...` (Part III +
appendices/glossary).

Given the huge exercise volume and low proper-noun/slang content, this source is a **structural/
morphological grammar reference** — it establishes the fusional present-tense conjugation system
(regular, stem-changing, and irregular-group patterns) that later slang-mechanics analysis will need for
understanding how colloquial Spanish verb forms deviate from or lean on this baseline.

## Grammar points

### Conjugation basics (Ch. 1)

Three conjugation classes by infinitive ending: -ar, -er, -ir. The *verb base* (stem) is what remains
after removing the ending; six person/number endings attach to the base per tense. Regular -ar endings:
-o/-as/-a/-amos/-áis/-an; -er: -o/-es/-e/-emos/-éis/-en; -ir: -o/-es/-e/-imos/-ís/-en (-er and -ir share
all endings except nosotros/vosotros). `Ud.`/`Uds.` are standard capitalized abbreviations for
usted/ustedes.

### Negation (Ch. 1)

Negate any sentence by placing `no` directly before the conjugated verb — no auxiliary "do/does" exists
in Spanish and none is translated (p. 6–7).

### Question formation (Ch. 1)

Three types: (1) simple yes/no questions invert conjugated-verb-before-subject order (`¿Hablas tú
español?`); (2) complex questions precede a simple-question structure with an interrogative
(¿Quién?/¿Qué?/¿Cuándo?/¿Dónde?/¿Por qué?/¿Cómo? — always accented as interrogatives; ¿Quién? always
takes a third-person verb); (3) limiting questions use ¿Cuál(es)? (verb typically follows, "one/ones"
implied) or ¿Cuánto(a)/(os/as)? (noun follows, agreeing in gender/number, then the verb) (pp. 13–16).

### Ser vs. estar (Ch. 2)

Both mean "to be" but split along a durability axis. **Ser** (irregular: soy/eres/es/somos/sois/son)
covers origin, familial/selected relationships, physical attributes, personality, possession,
profession, identification, date/time, nationality, and natural/inherent color. **Estar** (irregular:
estoy/estás/está/estamos/estáis/están) covers location (permanent or temporary), mood, physical
condition, result of a completed action, unnatural/changed color or condition, and moving from a
general truth (ser) to a specific instance (estar) — e.g. `El café es la bebida de los dioses` (ser,
general truth) vs. `Este café está muy bueno` (estar, this particular cup) (pp. 19–29).

### Hay (Ch. 3)

Invariant existential form meaning "there is/are/is there/are there," used regardless of number.
Negated as `no hay`, which carries the implicit sense of English "any" (pp. 31–33).

### Tener (Ch. 4)

Irregular verb (tengo/tienes/tiene/tenemos/tenéis/tienen) with four distinct uses: possession; age
(Spanish literally "has years" — `tener + años` — where English uses "to be"); a fixed set of
idiomatic expressions substituting `tener + noun` for English "to be + adjective" (hunger, thirst, cold,
heat, fear, pride, luck, hurry, sleepiness, rightness/wrongness); and obligation via `tener que +
infinitive` (pp. 35–40).

### The personal a (Ch. 5)

Untranslatable `a` placed before a direct object that is a specific person (or a pet/animal in a
personal relationship) — read as a mark of respect. Rules: not used with ser/tener/hay; not used when
the direct object is an unspecified person; used with pets but not strays/vermin/game animals; precedes
the interrogative in questions (`¿A quién amas?`); repeated before each item in a series of personal
direct objects; contracts with the definite article `el` to form `al` (pp. 43–45).

### Common irregular verbs (Ch. 6)

Twelve high-frequency irregular verbs (dar, decir, hacer, ir, jugar, oír, poder, poner, querer, salir,
tener [ch.4], venir, ver) presented with full present-tense paradigms. Notable derived patterns:
(1) two-verb phrases — first verb conjugated, second stays infinitive — used for `deber` ("ought/should"
when directly followed by a verb, vs. "to owe" as a standalone -er verb), `querer` ("to want"), `poder`
("to be able to/can"); (2) weather description splits between `hacer` + noun (hace frío, hace sol) and
`estar` + adjective/gerund (está nublado, está lloviendo) — these patterns cannot be mixed; (3) `jugar`
(play a game) vs. `tocar` (play an instrument) contrast; (4) future action via `ir + a + infinitive`
("going to do") (pp. 47–55).

### Saber vs. conocer (Ch. 7)

Both mean "to know" but split by knowledge type. **Saber** (irregular only in yo: sé) = knowing facts/
information; `saber que` always requires the conjunction (no bare "I know he's here" option as in
English); `saber + infinitive` (no `cómo`) = "to know how to." **Conocer** (irregular only in yo:
conozco) = familiarity with a person (requires personal `a`, except when the object is a pronoun), a
place, or a thing/field of expertise; can also mean "to meet" in an introduction context (pp. 57–63).

### Stem-changing verbs (Ch. 8)

Three groups, all with the "boot of irregularity" (change hits 1st/2nd/3rd singular + 3rd plural, not
nosotros/vosotros), verb endings otherwise regular: **o>ue** (contar, mover, dormir, and the list above),
**e>ie** (cerrar, perder, hervir, and the list above), and **e>i** (seguir, repetir, and the list above,
smallest group, all -ir verbs). The book frames the motivation as phonological/euphonic rather than
arbitrary — un-shifted forms like `*almorzo`/`*moro`/`*dormo` sound "deficient" to a native ear compared
to `almuerzo`/`muero`/`duermo`. Note: bendecir and decir have irregular yo forms (bendigo, digo)
alongside their e>i stem change (pp. 65–71).

### Irregular verb groups by infinitive ending (Ch. 9)

Six patterned groups, keyed to the last letters of the infinitive, all involving orthographic changes
that preserve consistent consonant sound across the paradigm:
- **-cer/-cir preceded by a vowel**: insert `z` before `c` in the yo form only (conocer→conozco,
  producir→produzco); all other forms regular.
- **-cer/-cir preceded by a consonant**: `z` replaces `c` in the yo form only (vencer→venzo,
  zurcir→zurzo).
- **-ger/-gir**: yo form's `g`→`j` to preserve the soft /h/ sound before `o` (coger→cojo, dirigir→dirijo).
- **-guir**: yo form drops the `u` (needed only to harden `g` before `e/i`) since `g` before `o` is
  already hard (seguir→sigo, distinguir→distingo).
- **-aer**: yo form ends in `-aigo` (caer→caigo, traer→traigo); all other forms regular.
- **-uir not preceded by g**: a `y` is inserted before the ending in all forms except nosotros/vosotros
  (huir→huyo/huyes/huye/huyen; construir, destruir, etc.) — unlike the other groups here, this
  irregularity is not confined to the yo form (pp. 73–85).

### Reflexive verbs (Ch. 10)

Reflexive = subject and object of the action are the same entity. Reflexive pronouns:
me/te/se/nos/os/se, placed before a single conjugated verb, or (with two verbs in the clause) either
before the first verb or attached to the infinitive of the second (`me quiero ver` = `quiero verme`).
Spanish's reflexive scope is broader than English's — routine self-care actions (bathing, dressing,
brushing teeth) are reflexive in Spanish even where English doesn't use "-self." Body-part reflexives
use the definite article, not a possessive adjective (`me cepillo los dientes`, not `*mis dientes`),
since the reflexive pronoun already signals whose body is meant. `irse` is an outlier: the reflexive
pronoun intensifies rather than reflects the action. `comerse` ("to gobble up") and `morirse` ("to pass
away," a softened `morir`) are idiomatic reflexive senses, not literal self-directed actions (pp. 87–91).

### Verbs like gustar (Ch. 11)

A class of verbs used almost exclusively in 3rd-person singular/plural with an indirect object pronoun,
where the *thing* is grammatically the subject and the *person* is the (indirect-object) experiencer —
inverting the English subject/object mapping (`Me gusta el gato` = "the cat is pleasing to me" = "I like
the cat"). Pattern: indirect object pronoun + 3rd-person verb + noun(s), verb agreeing in number with
the noun(s) that follow. To name the experiencing person explicitly (for clarity/emphasis), place `a` +
name/pronoun before the indirect object pronoun (`A Juan le gusta bailar`). Class members: gustar,
aburrir, bastar, disgustar, doler, encantar, faltar, fascinar, importar, interesar, molestar, parecer,
sobrar (pp. 93–97).

### The present progressive (Ch. 12)

Used more narrowly in Spanish than in English — strictly for an action in progress right now, not for
ongoing states/habits (which take simple present instead: "I live in New York" stays simple present,
not `*estoy viviendo`). Formed with conjugated `estar` + present participle. Regular participle
endings: -ar → -ando; -er/-ir → -iendo (share the same ending). Irregular participles: -er/-ir verbs
with a vowel-final stem take -yendo instead of -iendo, to avoid three vowels in a row (leer→leyendo,
construir→construyendo, ir→yendo as a special case). Stem-changing -ir verbs also show a stem change in
the participle: o>ue verbs change o>u (dormir→durmiendo, morir→muriendo); e>ie and e>i verbs both
change e>i (mentir→mintiendo, pedir→pidiendo). When the present progressive takes object pronoun(s),
they attach directly to the participle (never precede `estar`), following the RID order (Reflexive,
Indirect, Direct) when more than one is present, and an accent mark is added to preserve the participle's
original stress (`mirándolo`, `duchándose`, `comprándoselo`) (pp. 99–105).

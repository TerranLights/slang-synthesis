# Spanish — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Spanish in Your Face!

**Source:** Luc Nisset & Mary McVey Gill, *Spanish in Your Face!: The Ultimate Guide to Spanish
Facial and Body Language Expressions* (McGraw-Hill, 2008), full 162-page volume — units: Character
and Personality (polar opposites, personality types A–Z), Moods, Emotions, and Attitudes (mood
swings, Humorómetro quiz, emotions and attitudes A–Z), Appearance and Gestures (facial descriptions,
la agencia de contactos, body language/gestures), plus bilingual indices. PDF has a genuine text
layer (`pdftotext -layout` produced harmless "Bad annotation destination" syntax warnings only, no
content loss); no vision-reading needed. Transcription Confidence = n/a, Vision Reading Confidence =
n/a for every row below.

**Why this book is high-value:** its entire premise is linking Spanish vocabulary to nonverbal
expression, so it is saturated with idiom. The book uses its own explicit apparatus for exactly the
kind of register/regional metadata this project tracks: a `™` symbol (its own choice of glyph, not
a trademark) flags "colloquial terms or slang expressions that are somewhat off-color," and a
detailed bracketed country/region code system (Arg, Bol, C Am, Carib, Chile, Col, CR, Cuba, Ec, El
Salv, L Am, Mex, Nic, Peru, S Am, S Cone [Argentina/Uruguay/Paraguay], Spain, Ven) tags geographically
restricted terms — far more granular country-level detail than most sources in this corpus. Most
idioms are additionally given literal glosses (marked `(lit., ...)`), which is valuable for this
project's own slang-derivation-mechanism analysis (metaphor productivity, etc.).

**Mapping to taxonomy:** `™`-flagged terms → Usage Tier `taboo` (book's own description: "off-color,"
"should be used only in appropriate situations"). Non-flagged idiomatic expressions in the "related
terms and expressions" sublists and the exclamations list → `colloquial`, except where the English
gloss and content make clear a term is itself profane even though the book didn't mark it with `™`
(the book's own ™-tagging is visibly inconsistent — e.g., "las tetas" went untagged in a different
book while here several similarly mild words go tagged) — those are marked `slang` or `taboo` based
on content with a Notes flag explaining the editorial call. Plain descriptive/cognate adjectives
(the dense "synonyms and similar words" columns, e.g., agresivo/brusco/tosco) are `core` and were
NOT extracted — they are near-cognates with low slang-derivation value and this project's established
corpus already covers Spanish descriptive-adjective vocabulary extensively.

**Coverage approach (documented, non-exhaustive by design):** this is a ~1,001-headword dictionary;
full exhaustive extraction of every synonym-list entry was not attempted. Extracted in full: (1)
every `™`-flagged off-color entry in the entire book (all units); (2) the complete "interjections /
las exclamaciones" A–Z list (pp. 135–139, ~160 entries) — an exceptionally dense, explicitly
region-tagged list of exclamations spanning the full colloquial-to-vulgar range, essentially a
mini taboo/slang-interjection dictionary in its own right; (3) the illustrated "gestures" caption
idioms (transportation, miscellaneous) where readable as running text. Extracted as a representative
sample (not exhaustive): the figurative "related terms and expressions" idioms (marked `(lit., ...)`
in the source, ~405 such idioms total across the book) under a cross-section of personality-type,
mood, and emotion headwords — prioritizing entries with regional tags or vivid/unusual imagery, since
that is this project's highest-value content. Deliberately skipped: the "facial descriptions" unit's
hairstyle/physical-feature/grooming-product vocabulary (pp. 121–133) — almost entirely neutral
descriptive/cognate nouns (tipos de peinado, productos de peluquería) with negligible slang content;
the "la agencia de contactos" exercise (p. 132) is a photo-captioning exercise with no extractable
running text; the Humorómetro personality quiz itself (pp. 83–94) is a quiz-scoring mechanic, not
vocabulary.

**Non-redundant-supplement note:** this book's personality/mood/gesture-idiom content does not
meaningfully overlap with *Spanish Among Amigos* (established/042, this same wave) — that book's
idiom glossary and chapter-embedded slang are built around everyday conversational functions
(greeting, planning, opinion-giving), while this book's idioms are organized around describing a
third person's character/mood/body language, and its exclamations list and its explicit country-
code tagging (down to individual Central American and Caribbean countries) go well beyond this
corpus's prior [SP]/[LA] granularity. A few individual idioms recur across both books (e.g., "No me
hace ni fu ni fa" here vs. "ni fu ni fa" in 042; "en las nubes"/daydreaming-type idioms); these are
incidental overlaps in widely-known idioms, not redundant re-extraction of the same source content.

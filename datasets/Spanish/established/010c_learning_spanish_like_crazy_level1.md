# Spanish — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: *Learning Spanish Like Crazy*, Level I, Lecciones 17–24

**Source:** *Learning Spanish Like Crazy* (Patrick Jackson et al.), Level I, spoken-Spanish audio
course transcribed to PDF lesson scripts (`Spanish_Lesson_17.pdf`–`Spanish_Lesson_24.pdf`). Same
format as `010_...md`/`010b_...md`. Lecciones 21–22 carry a front-matter disclaimer noting the
transcript may deviate slightly from the audio and shouldn't be used as a listening crutch — noted
here for completeness, not extracted as course content. All eight files have a clean, genuine
`pdftotext -layout` text layer.

**Coverage note:** Same coverage discipline as prior files in this set. This batch is the
grammatically richest yet: present tense is now contrasted against THREE distinct past-tense/
aspect forms across parallel drills — preterite (simple past), imperfect (habitual/ongoing past),
and (new in this batch, Lección 24) present perfect (he/has/ha/hemos/han + past participle) — plus
a full weather-idiom vocabulary set (Lección 23) and the irregular verbs venir, tener, ser, ir,
poder in various tenses. No marginalia or text-layer issues encountered.

## Grammar points

### Preterite fully contrasted against present across -ar/-er/-ir and irregular verbs (Lección 17)
Building on Lección 16's introduction, this lesson drills present-vs.-preterite minimal pairs across
comer (comí), beber (bebí), leer (leí — with a spelling-driven y insertion in leyó/leímos not
explicitly named), salir (salí), and the stem-changing volver (preterite volví, regular despite the
present-tense o→ue change) — reinforcing that not every verb's irregularity carries into every
tense.

### Imperfect tense named and taught explicitly (Lección 18)
Unlike the more implicit past-tense buildup in Lecciones 16–17, this lesson explicitly names "el
imperfecto" (the imperfect tense) as a grammatical category and draws the contrast directly: "Yo
trabajo" (I work) vs. "Yo trabajaba" (I used to work) vs. (implicitly, from Lección 17) "Yo trabajé"
(I worked). The imperfect is characterized functionally as describing a habitual/ongoing past state
rather than a completed past action — the course doesn't give the terminology "habitual aspect" but
demonstrates it through repeated "used to X" glosses.

### Three of Spanish's irregular imperfect-tense verbs (Lecciones 20–21)
Spanish has exactly three verbs with an irregular imperfect: ser (era/eras/era/éramos/eran), ir
(iba/ibas/iba/íbamos/iban), and ver (veía/veías/veía — regular-looking but retains the stem vowel
that would otherwise contract). All three appear in this batch's drills (era/eran for identity
statements, iba/iban for habitual destinations, veía for "I used to see/watch") without the course
ever naming them as a specially irregular set — a genuine gap in the course's own metalanguage that
the extraction flags for the mechanics-analysis phase, since this is one of the few places where
Spanish's regular imperfect pattern (near-universally -aba/-ía) has real exceptions.

### venir drilled across present, preterite, AND imperfect in direct contrast (Lección 22)
This lesson is unusual in the course for drilling all three tense forms of the same verb
(vengo/vienes present; vine/viniste/vino/vinimos/vinieron preterite; venía/veníamos imperfect)
against essentially the same lexical content ("come here on Mondays" in present vs. "came here last
Thursday" in preterite vs. "used to come here on Sundays" in imperfect), making the three-way
present/preterite/imperfect aspectual contrast more concrete than in any earlier lesson.

### Weather idioms with impersonal "hacer" vs. "estar" (Lección 23)
Most Spanish weather expressions use impersonal "hacer" + a noun (hace calor/frío/sol/viento/fresco,
lit. "it makes heat/cold/sun/wind/coolness") rather than an adjective the way English does ("it's
hot"). "Nublado" (cloudy) breaks this pattern and instead takes "estar" ("está nublado"), and rain/
snow use their own dedicated verbs (llover, nevar) rather than either "hacer" or "estar" + adjective.
The course drills all three patterns side by side without naming the three-way split explicitly, but
the pattern is clearly demonstrable from the data: hacer+noun (temperature/sun/wind), estar+adjective
(cloudy), and dedicated intransitive verbs (rain/snow).

### Past progressive vs. simple preterite for weather verbs (Lección 23–24)
For llover and nevar specifically, the course explicitly draws a parallel to English's "it WAS
raining" vs. "IT rained" distinction: "Ayer estuvo lloviendo" (was raining, using estar + gerund in
a past tense) is presented as interchangeable with "Ayer llovió" (it rained, simple preterite) — an
explicit aspectual-choice point the course calls out directly ("As in English, you can say...").

### Present perfect (haber + past participle) introduced and set against preterite/imperfect (Lección 24)
The auxiliary "haber" (he/has/ha/hemos/han) + a regular past participle (-ado for -ar verbs,
demonstrated with desayunado/almorzado/cenado/hablado) forms the present perfect, glossed as "I have
[verb]ed." The lesson deliberately drills the same verb (desayunar) across all three past forms in
close proximity — "Desayuné" (preterite, I ate breakfast), "Desayunaba" (imperfect, I used to eat
breakfast), "He desayunado" (present perfect, I have eaten breakfast) — giving a learner a rare
direct three-way minimal-pair contrast for Spanish's past-tense system within a single lesson.

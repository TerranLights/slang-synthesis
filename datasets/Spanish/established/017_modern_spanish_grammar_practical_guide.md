This book has an unusual two-part structure that makes it genuinely different from every other
Spanish grammar book already in this project's corpus: **Part A "Structures"** (chapters 1-28,
conventional grammar organized by grammatical category) and **Part B "Functions"** (chapters 29-73,
organized by *communicative function* — greetings, negotiating, reporting, describing, expressing
emotion, persuading, talking about time). No other book in this project's corpus organizes content
by communicative function, and the book states its register-marking intent explicitly in Ch.29's own
opening: "Reference is made to register when appropriate, so that you will know... whether a certain
expression is formal or informal... used in the spoken or written language" (p.153) — exactly the
kind of source-internal register annotation the extraction spec treats as highest-value and
mandatory to capture.

## Grammar points — Part A (Ch.19-28)

### Sequence of tense (Ch.19)

The book organizes Spanish tenses into two groups for sequencing in reported speech and
subjunctive-governed constructions: **Group 1 (present-related)** — indicative present/perfect/
future perfect, subjunctive present/perfect; **Group 2 (past-related)** — indicative imperfect/
pluperfect/conditional perfect/preterite, subjunctive past(imperfect)/pluperfect. The rule of thumb:
don't mix tense-forms from different groups in reported speech or subjunctive constructions unless
the sense demands it (p.93). Worked mapping table for reported speech: direct future → reported
present/future (if reporting verb is present) or preterite/conditional (if reporting verb is past);
direct present → reported present/present or imperfect/imperfect; direct perfect → reported
present/perfect or pluperfect/pluperfect; direct imperative (present subjunctive) → reported
present/present-subjunctive or preterite/past-subjunctive. Sequences can be broken when the sense
requires — e.g. "Dicen que lo hicieron ayer" keeps the preterite even though the reporting verb is
present, because the action's pastness is a fixed fact independent of when it's reported (p.94).

### Periphrastic verb forms (Ch.20)

Seven multi-word verb constructions, each with a distinct aspectual nuance, laid out side by side
(pp.96-98):
- **estar + gerund**: ongoing action (any tense); in the perfect/pluperfect, a prolonged action
  still in progress at the reference moment; can also express disapproval/surprise ("¡Pero qué
  estás haciendo!").
- **ir a + infinitive**: future time (esp. categoric statements, frequent in speech); 1st-plural
  imperative ("vamos a comer"); intention; in the imperfect, future-in-the-past.
- **llevar + gerund**: "spending time doing X" — duration up to the reference moment ("llevo dos
  horas haciendo ejercicios").
- **acabar de + infinitive**: "to have just" (present) / "had just" (imperfect).
- **ir + gerund**: gradual or repeated process ("va cobrando importancia" = it's steadily gaining
  importance).
- **venir + gerund**: a repeated action continuing up to the present or a past reference moment
  ("vengo diciendo que es imposible" = I've kept on saying...).
- **tener + past participle**: denotes completion; the participle agrees with the direct object
  (unlike the regular perfect auxiliary haber); often used with verbs like prohibir for an ongoing
  state ("me tiene prohibido criticar al jefe").

### The intensifying reflexive (23.7)

A distinct reflexive type from the other 8 the book catalogs (literal, conventionalized, reciprocal,
inherently-reflexive, intransitive-correspondent, prepositional-object, impersonal, passive — all
largely redundant with prior corpus coverage and not re-extracted here). The intensifying reflexive
pairs a non-reflexive verb with a reflexive counterpart carrying a related but distinct nuance — the
book is explicit that "no very general principle can be given" since each verb pairs differently,
but the recurring flavor is **suddenness/starting-point/accidental nature** (caer vs. caerse),
**completiveness** (comer vs. comerse), **transition-into-a-state** (dormir vs. dormirse, "finally
fell asleep"), **directional departure** (ir vs. irse), **manner-of-death** (morir = violent/
accidental, morirse = natural process, also figurative intensity as in "dying to know something"),
and **remain vs. actively stay** (quedar vs. quedarse). See vocabulary table above for the six
pairs. This is a genuinely useful semantic-nuance catalog for later mechanics-analysis work on how
Spanish slang might exploit the reflexive/non-reflexive split for intensity/register shading.

### The passive: three constructions and their relative registers (Ch.24)

Three ways to render an English-style passive, each with a different nuance: **ser + past
participle** focuses on the action itself, is grammatically the most direct equivalent, but is
explicitly flagged as "chiefly limited to formal written Spanish" (p.112). **estar + past
participle** always denotes a resultant state, not an action, and can only take an agent if that
agent has continuing involvement in the state (p.113). **The passive reflexive** (se + 3rd person
verb) is called "probably the commonest Spanish equivalent of the English passive" (p.113) — the
book explicitly ranks reflexive-passive as the default/default-register choice over ser+p.p., the
inverse of a naive assumption from English "be+p.p." — but the passive reflexive is avoided when a
literal-reflexive reading is possible (los soldados se mataron would mean "the soldiers killed
themselves," not "were killed") and is disfavored (not forbidden) when a strong, particular agent is
involved (pp.113-114). Four devices exist for vagueness-of-subject beyond the passive constructions
proper: indefinite noun ("la gente"), uno, 3rd-person-plural verb ("dicen que..."), and — "especially
common in speech" — the generic tú form (p.114-115). An object can be fronted to topic position if a
resumptive unstressed object pronoun is retained ("La catedral la vimos desde lejos") (p.115).

### Prepositions: systematic inventory and the por/para contrast (Ch.25)

The book's own mnemonic framing for por vs. para: **por looks backward** to a cause/motive or a
substitution; **para looks forward** to a destination or purpose (p.126) — rendered visually as two
arrows pointing in opposite directions. Personal **a** (before a direct object denoting a person) is
governed by 5 competing factors rather than a hard rule: (1) personal qualities/attributed personhood
favor a vs. viewing people as a commodity disfavors it; (2) pets favor a vs. working/food animals
disfavor it; (3) specific individuals favor a vs. a class of people (esp. with indefinite article)
disfavors it; (4) verbs of strong subject-influence-on-object favor a even for non-personal objects,
vs. verbs of general relation disfavor it even for personal objects (contrast "querer a una amiga" =
to love a girlfriend vs. "querer una amiga" = to want a girlfriend-type-person); (5) disambiguation
of precedence-relation verbs favors a, disambiguation of double-personal-object verbs disfavors it
(pp.116-118). Full preposition inventory with sense-breakdowns: a (direction, location-in-time-
expressions, rate, manner), de (possession/characteristic/specification/position/material/
composition/naming/agent-action — 8 sub-senses; also agent marker as an alternative to por
especially for states not definite actions; also cause), en (position-above, point-in-space/time,
point-within-a-space, movement-into, means-of-transport, measurement-delta), por (cause/motive,
substitution, movement-through/duration, vague place/time, rate, agent, means), sobre (on-top-of,
about/concerning, approximation-in-time, "near" with place names). 25.2 "groups of prepositions"
notes that Spanish uses por to add a motion-reading to an otherwise-static compound preposition: "el
gato corrió por debajo de la mesa" (ran under, motion) vs. "el gato estaba escondido debajo de la
mesa" (was hiding under, static).

### Complementation: sentence, infinitive, and gerund (Ch.26) — genuinely new territory

Three complementation types, not previously systematized in this corpus:
1. **Sentence complementation** via que (reported speech, indirect commands, subjunctive
   constructions; strengthened to "el (hecho de) que" = "the fact that" when topicalized) or si
   (indirect yes/no questions).
2. **Infinitive complementation** — as subject or as object, the latter sorted by governing
   preposition: **no preposition** (acostumbrar, conseguir, deber, decidir, desear, esperar, evitar,
   intentar, lamentar, lograr, necesitar, negar, pedir, pensar, poder, preferir, querer, recordar,
   saber, sentir, soler, temer — same-subject rule applies, except verbs of ordering [dejar, hacer,
   impedir, mandar, permitir, prohibir] and verbs of perception [ver, oír], which allow a different
   implied subject, e.g. "vimos llegar a la Reina"); **with a** (verbs of beginning/motion/reflexives:
   apresurarse, aprender, atreverse, bajar, comenzar, decidirse, empezar, ir, ponerse, resistirse,
   salir, sentarse, venir; plus influence-verbs with a different implied subject: acostumbrar,
   convidar, enseñar, inducir, invitar, persuadir); **with de** (often negative-flavored, many
   reflexive: acabar, acordarse, alegrarse, arrepentirse, avergonzarse, cansarse, dejar, olvidarse,
   terminar, tratar; exceptions with different subject: acusar, disuadir); **with en** (complacerse,
   consentir, dudar, insistir, tardar, vacilar); **with con** (amenazar, soñar); **with por** (acabar,
   comenzar, empezar, esforzarse, luchar, optar, terminar, votar — "quedar por" + infinitive
   corresponds to an English passive, "dos cosas quedan por hacer" = two things remain to be done).
   A key distinguishing minimal pair: "es difícil entender a este profesor" (infinitive = whole
   subject) vs. "este profesor es difícil de entender" (noun = subject, infinitive takes de) — the
   two are not interchangeable.
3. **Gerund complementation** (least common): acabar, continuar, llevar, seguir + gerund; verbs of
   perception also take gerund complements to stress an action's ongoing/successive nature ("vimos a
   los diputados entrando en el palacio" suggests deputies filing in one after another, as distinct
   from the plain infinitive construction).

### Conjunctions (Ch.27)

Coordinating: y becomes e before i-/hi- (except hie- diphthongs: "mujer e hijos" vs "nieve y
hielo"); o becomes u before o-/ho- ("siete u ocho"); the stronger "(o)...o bien..." pattern is used
when alternatives are dissimilar/far apart; pero (general "but") vs. sino (mutually-exclusive
contrast, "no...sino..."); ni...ni... ("neither...nor"); que as a vague spoken-register causal
connector between sentences (flagged explicitly as a spoken-language phenomenon, p.141).
Subordinating: que/si as complementizers (see Ch.26); then conjunction inventories by meaning —
cause (como, porque, pues, puesto que, ya que — note "no porque" requires subjunctive and "como" +
subjunctive expresses a hypothesis, distinct from como's indicative causal use), concession (aunque,
aun cuando), condition (a condición de que, a menos que, a no ser que, a pesar de que, como, con tal
(de) que, si, siempre que), purpose (para que, de manera/modo/forma que — subjunctive), consequence
(conque, de manera/modo/forma que — indicative; note the same conjunction phrase serves both purpose
[subjunctive] and consequence [indicative] depending on which mood follows), and time (antes de que,
apenas, cuando, desde que, después de que, en cuanto, hasta que, mientras, siempre que — antes de que
always subjunctive; cuando/desde que/después de que sometimes take the -ra past subjunctive form for
past situations; siempre que takes subjunctive only when expressing a hypothesis).

### Word order (Ch.28)

Spanish word order is governed less by a fixed subject-verb-object template than by information
structure: the "old"/topic information tends to go first in a sentence, "new" information or
contrastively-stressed elements go last — demonstrated via four paired question/answer examples
where the same proposition is phrased with different initial elements depending on what the
preceding question asked about (pp.145-146). Adverbs normally sit immediately next to the verb
(unlike more flexible English placement): "Habla bien español," "Salió rápidamente de la
habitación." Questions can be formed by intonation alone (preserving statement word order), by
verb-first inversion, or with a pre-posed topic outside the question's scope (marked in writing by
placing the inverted question mark after the topic, not at the sentence's true start): "Y el señor
Pérez ¿cómo lo reconocieron?"

## Grammar points — Ch.29 Making social contacts

### 29.1-29.3 Greetings and register

The three buenos-X greetings are register-neutral (formal and familiar both); ¡hola!, ¿qué tal?,
¿qué hay? are intrinsically familiar but can be upgraded to formal by combining with a formal
greeting in the same utterance (e.g. "¿qué tal, don Miguel, cómo está usted?") — i.e. register here
is compositional across an utterance, not fixed per-lexeme. Several explicit country-level variants
are marked (see vocabulary table): Chile's phonetic reduction of ¿qué hubo? to [kjuβo], River
Plate's "buen día" replacing "buenos días." Social touch norms are also described explicitly:
handshaking is standard in formal contexts and common in informal reunions after a long absence;
cheek-kissing in greeting/farewell is customary among women or mixed-gender pairs, "rarely seen among
men, particularly in Latin America, except when there is a family relationship" (p.154) — a directly
relevant sociolinguistic/pragmatic norm for later mechanics work on in-group markers.

### 29.4 Introducing yourself and others

Self-introduction ranges from neutral "soy.../me llamo..." to formal "permitan que me presente."
Introduction greetings scale from informal (hola, ¿qué tal?) through "slightly formal" (mucho/tanto
gusto, encantado/a) to fully formal (¿cómo está usted?, mucho gusto en conocerlo/la, el gusto es
mío). The book explicitly notes a demographic-register correlation: younger people/equals default to
the informal end even in introduction contexts, while Latin Americans overall skew toward the more
formal/courteous expressions compared to Peninsular norms — a cross-regional register-defaults
contrast, not just a lexical difference. Morphological note: encantado/a agrees with the *speaker's*
gender (not the addressee's), and the masculine object pronoun lo in "encantado de conocerlo" is
replaced by le in central Spain specifically (another intra-Peninsular regional micro-variant).

### 29.5 Taking leave

Hasta luego and adiós are the two universal leave-taking words across the whole Spanish-speaking
world, both usable formally and informally; Peninsular speakers choose between them based on whether
they expect to meet again soon (hasta luego) or not (adiós) — a semantically-conditioned
register-neutral pair rather than a formal/informal pair. Nearly all the other hasta-X leave-takings
(hasta mañana, hasta la tarde, hasta el lunes, hasta pronto) are both-register except hasta ahora,
explicitly flagged as informal-only. A notable cross-regional formality inversion: Ecuador treats
"hasta luego" as the formal option (contradicting the general Spanish-world pattern where it's
register-neutral) — explicit evidence that even near-universal politeness vocabulary can carry
locally inverted register values. Latin America shows a strong overall preference for chao/chau
(with affectionate diminutives chaíto/chaucito/hasta lueguito) for familiar leave-taking, while adiós
there specifically connotes a longer absence (e.g. before a journey) rather than ordinary register
distinction.

### 29.6 Expressing wishes

A systematic set of wish-formulas built on the construction que + present subjunctive (e.g. "¡Que
tengas suerte!"), covering travel wishes, luck, sleep/rest, recovery-from-illness, toasts, and bon
appétit. The toast formulas scale by register via pronoun choice alone: "¡A su salud!" (formal) vs.
"¡A tu salud!" (familiar) — a clean minimal pair showing register encoded purely in the possessive
pronoun, structure otherwise identical.

### 29.7 Congratulating someone

¡Felicidades! is the universal default; ¡Enhorabuena! is common in Spain but explicitly "unknown in
some Latin American countries," where ¡Felicitaciones! is used instead — a direct Spain/Latin
America lexical-substitution pair for the same speech act. Felicitar-based constructions change
pronoun by formality/number/gender (te/os familiar vs. lo/le/la formal) and the book notes that in
Latin American Spanish, the vosotros-associated os form is absent from colloquial speech entirely,
so los/las cover both formal and familiar contexts there — a register-system simplification specific
to Latin American pronoun morphology (not just vocabulary).

### 29.8 Using the phone

The single richest regional-variation stretch of the whole book so far: phone-answering formulas
differ by country/region wholesale (¿Diga?/¿Dígame? Spain; ¿Bueno? Mexico; ¿Aló?/¿Sí? South America;
¿Holá? River Plate) rather than varying by register within a country. "Extension" itself has three
regional lexemes (la extensión standard, el interno River Plate, el anexo Chile), and even the verb
for "put through" varies regionally (poner is "not common or unknown in many Latin American
countries," replaced by comunicar generally or dar specifically in River Plate/Chile). The chapter
catalogs a full functional sequence (asking to speak to someone, asking who's calling, identifying
oneself, asking to leave a message, leaving a message, asking for an extension, asking to hold,
making an operator-assisted call, arranging to meet) each with formal/informal doublets. A
noteworthy micro-point: telephone numbers are conventionally read digit-by-digit or in pairs, not as
full multi-digit numbers (cross-ref 7.3.2) — a numeracy-register convention.

### 29.9 Writing letters

A full epistolary register system: salutations scale from "Muy señor mío:" (very formal) through
"Distinguido señor:" through "Estimado señor (surname):" (less formal) down to "Señor:"
(impersonal/circular use) for business correspondence, vs. "Querido/a + name" for family/friends —
with the explicit rule that **estimado, not querido, is used for people you don't know well even if
you address them with tú** (i.e. formality-of-address and formality-of-salutation can be decoupled
from the tú/usted axis). Formal correspondence has its own fixed-phrase inventory for acknowledging
receipt, stating purpose, expressing regret/pleasure, and requesting — each anchored to a specific
governing verb + its complementation pattern (sentir/lamentar + infinitive for regret; complacer +
infinitive for pleasure; rogar que + present subjunctive and agradecería que + imperfect subjunctive
for requests, the latter demonstrating the sequence-of-tense principle from Ch.19 in a real
politeness-formula context). Letter-closings scale similarly from "Atentamente,"/"Le saluda
atentamente," (very formal) through "Reciba un cordial saludo de" (less formal) to "Un (fuerte)
abrazo,"/"Cariños," (family/friends, the latter marked especially Latin American). Abbreviation
conventions (Sr./Sra./Srta., Vd./Vds. or Ud./Uds.) are also part of the formal-register package.

## Grammar points — Ch.30 Basic strategies for communication

### 30.1 Attracting attention / responding to a call for attention

¡Oiga! (formal) and ¡Oye! (familiar), both from oír "to hear," are the core attention-getters, but
the book flags a sharp regional friction point: **¡Oiga! is considered rude in some Latin American
countries**, while the familiar ¡Oye! is accepted everywhere — an unusual case where the *more
formal* member of a formal/familiar pair is the one carrying the regional taboo risk. Disculpe/
disculpa (from disculpar) are noted as more common in Latin America than in Spain for
attention-getting, but roughly equally common across all regions specifically as an apology formula
(discúlpeme/discúlpame) — i.e. the same lexeme's regional distribution differs by which speech-act
function it's serving. Vocative use of señor/señora/señorita to get a stranger's attention is
"especially common in Latin America."

### 30.3 Requesting repetition and responding

Distinct register tiers for "pardon?": ¿Cómo? / ¿Cómo dice/s? / ¿Cómo ha(s) dicho? scale from plain
through formal/familiar doublets through most-explicit; plain ¿Qué? is explicitly marked as rude in
this function and advised against, even though ¿qué? is of course entirely neutral in its normal
interrogative use — a function-specific (not lexeme-general) rudeness marking. Mexico's ¿mande?
serves this same slot regionally. When repeating a statement back, Spanish fronts the repeated
clause with que (declarative), que si (yes/no question), or bare que (imperative) — a systematic
three-way split by original utterance-type that determines which repetition-marker is used, not free
variation.

### 30.4 Making sure you understand / are understood

entender vs. comprender: entender is the default for language/speech comprehension ("no entiendo lo
que dices," "¿entiendes mi letra?"), while comprender is reserved for more abstract objects ("no
comprendo tu actitud") and is overall markedly less frequent — a clean domain-based near-synonym
split, not simple formal/informal variation. The politeness-graded request for slower speech uses
the conditional podría/s rather than the present puede/s specifically because "the conditional
generally expresses more politeness than the present form" — directly reusing the modal-conditional-
politeness mechanism from Ch.21 (querer/quisiera) in a concrete function.

### 30.5 Signalling understanding

A single interjection (¡no!) can convey surprise, annoyance, or pity depending purely on intonation —
the book is explicit that the lexeme itself is invariant and the emotional valence is carried
entirely suprasegmentally. This is a clean example of a discourse particle whose meaning is
disambiguated only by prosody, relevant to later work on how slang particles might similarly load
multiple pragmatic functions onto one invariant form.

### 30.8 Using fillers — the richest discourse-marker inventory in the book so far

Seven fillers catalogued with distinct distributional/functional profiles, not simply listed as
synonyms: **pues** (opening/doubt/exclamation-initial generally, but sentence-FINAL as reinforcement
specifically in Andean countries — a genuine syntactic-position regional variant, not just a lexical
one); **bueno** (doubt/hesitation or conversation-restart); **hombre** (very common in Spain,
multifunctional — hesitation AND emotional coloring from happiness to sadness, i.e. a single filler
spanning what would be several distinct discourse markers in English); **este** (hesitation tag,
especially Latin American, functions as a tag inserted mid-narration); **o sea** (hesitation,
restricted to mid-sentence position only — the one filler in this set with a hard positional
constraint); **¿sabe/s?** (tag, especially Latin American) with **¿eh?** as its direct Peninsular
counterpart performing the identical tag function — i.e. a register-matched regional minimal pair at
the discourse-marker level, exactly the kind of structural (not just lexical) regional contrast most
valuable for later slang-mechanics modeling.

### 30.10 Formal development of a topic

A fully systematized register-high discourse-structuring inventory (opening remarks, introducing
further points, establishing sequence, establishing cross-references, giving examples, explaining/
reformulating, requesting/yielding the floor, summarizing/concluding) — this is explicitly framed as
formal/lecture-register and stands in direct structural contrast to the colloquial filler system of
30.8: where 30.8's fillers are short, prosody-dependent, and function-ambiguous, 30.10's connectives
are long, fixed, and function-explicit (en primer lugar, en lo que se refiere a, es decir, en
resumen, etc.) — a clean high/low register contrast pair worth keeping in mind for a later
register-ladder analysis across the whole language.

## Grammar points — Ch.31 Asking questions and responding

Spanish yes/no questions use three structurally distinct patterns, differentiated mainly by
intonation and degree-of-certainty pragmatics rather than by a dedicated interrogative morphology:
**verb-subject** order (focus on the verbal action, used when genuinely uncertain of the answer),
**subject-verb** order, identical to a statement's word order and disambiguated purely by rising
intonation (used more when the speaker already has a fair degree of certainty — notably far more
common in Spanish than the analogous construction in English), and **statement + ¿verdad?/¿no?** tag
questions (used when seeking mere confirmation, tag carries rising tone while the statement itself
keeps falling intonation). A falling intonation with stress-shift onto a non-verb element can turn
what looks like a question word-order into a plain statement — i.e. prosody, not word order alone,
is often the true disambiguator. Partial-information questions take interrogative-word + verb +
subject order, with intonation falling for familiar address and rising for politeness — a
politeness-marking use of pitch contour layered on top of fixed syntax. Polite questions are built by
prefixing perdone/por favor + ¿me puede/podría decir...? — and the book notes a length-based
positioning rule: "por favor" moves to sentence-initial position with longer requests but can go
either initial or final with short ones. Negative questions carry a default implicature of expecting
a "no" answer, but are also regularly used to convey surprise or annoyance at an unfulfilled
expectation ("¿Todavía no has terminado?" implying "you should have finished by now"). Spanish lacks
an equivalent to English auxiliary-echo answers ("Yes, I am"/"No, they don't") — bare sí/no or a
small set-phrase inventory (claro (que sí), desde luego (que sí), por supuesto (que sí),
naturalmente, sí/no + efectivamente, cómo no) must do the work instead, and the book explicitly warns
that *repeating the exact words of the question back* sounds emphatic/overly formal/annoyed — a
pragmatic anti-echo norm with no real English parallel. Within the affirmative set, claro/desde
luego/por supuesto are mutually interchangeable and register-neutral; naturalmente and sí,
efectivamente are rarer and slightly more formal; cómo no skews Latin American and formal.

## Grammar points — Ch.32 Negating

Beyond the baseline "no + verb," Spanish has a graded family of negation-strength devices worth
treating as a scale rather than a single on/off mechanism: **poco** < **in-/des- prefix** < **nada**
as adjective-negating intensity (book gives an explicit three-point scale: "una actitud poco
correcta" weakest, "una actitud incorrecta" middle, "una actitud nada correcta" strongest) — i.e.
nada is not simply "not," it's a *stronger* negator than a dedicated negative prefix, a genuinely
counter-intuitive-for-English-speakers finding with direct relevance to intensity/gradience modeling
in later slang-mechanics work. Separately, **nada used with an adjective/adverb reverses from
negator to intensifier of the positive sense** depending on exact placement ("no es nada fácil" =
"not at all easy" [negative]; moving nada to pre-verb position, "nada es fácil" = "nothing is easy"
[true negator, different scope entirely]) — a word-order-dependent semantic flip on the same lexeme.
Negative-pronoun pairs (nada/nadie, nunca/jamás) show a frequency/strength asymmetry within each
pair: jamás is rarer and stronger than nunca; nadie/nada in initial (pre-verb) position for emphasis
is formal and "best avoided" by a learner per the book's own explicit advice, vs. the much more
frequent and neutral post-verb double-negative construction. ni...ni... and ninguno both show a
word-order/emphasis trade-off: pre-verb placement is markedly less frequent and more emphatic than
the default post-verb double-negative pattern. A short inventory of negation-flavored idiomatic set
phrases (¡Qué va!, ¡De ningún modo/ninguna manera!, ¡Ni hablar!, ¡Ni pensarlo/soñarlo!, ¡Por nada del
mundo!) closes the chapter — register-marked colloquial/emphatic, listed in the vocabulary table
above.

## Grammar points — Ch.33 Reporting (direct/indirect speech, deixis shift)

Direct quotation (reproducing a speaker's exact words with a reporting verb like decir) is marked as
rare in spoken Spanish but frequent in written fiction narrative — an explicit register split
between the two reporting modes themselves, not just within indirect speech. Indirect speech
requires a systematic bundle of deictic shifts beyond simple tense-backshifting, laid out as six
distinct categories: (a) tense/aspect shift for time-lapse, (b) possessive-adjective/pronoun person
shift (mi→su, mío→suyo), (c) personal-pronoun shift (yo→él/ella, te→me), (d) verb-person/agreement
shift, (e) time-adverb shift (mañana→al día siguiente), (f) space/direction shift (aquí→allí,
llevar→traer, éste→ése) — this last category, converting motion/direction verbs and demonstratives
when reporting from a different speaker's spatial vantage point, is a clean illustration of deixis as
a systematic grammatical category beyond the usual pronoun/tense examples, genuinely useful for later
cross-linguistic deixis-mechanics comparison. **Tense backshift in reported statements is
conditional on temporal distance, not automatic**: a reporting verb in the present/perfect (reporting
an ongoing or very recent conversation) requires *no* tense change in the embedded clause at all; a
reporting verb in the past *may* still preserve the original tense if "the idea expressed...is still
valid" (truth-conditional tense preservation — a semantically, not just syntactically, governed
backshift rule); when backshift does apply, the mapping is present→imperfect, preterite/perfect→
pluperfect, future→conditional, while pluperfect and imperfect are invariant under backshift (no
further-back form exists for them). Three reporting-verb classes are distinguished by
register/stance: decir (neutral, spoken-register default) vs. afirmar/agregar/añadir/comentar/
explicar/expresar (formal/written register) vs. stance-revealing verbs (aconsejar, advertir,
asegurar, esperar, negar, prometer) that encode the original utterance's illocutionary force directly
into the reporting verb choice. Reporting questions uses preguntar + si for yes/no questions (si
omitted for wh-questions) or the near-synonym pair querer saber (neutral/common) vs. desear saber
(more formal). Reporting yes/no answers alone just needs "que sí"/"que no" after the reporting verb.
Reporting commands/requests requires que + subjunctive in the embedded clause, with the
*subjunctive's own tense* (present vs. -ra/-se past) tracking the *reporting verb's* tense by the
same proximity-to-present logic as statements — directly reusing the Ch.19 sequence-of-tense
machinery in a concrete function. Reporting-verb choice again encodes stance: decir (neutral),
pedir/querer (requests), mandar/ordenar/exigir (commands), sugerir/aconsejar/advertir
(suggestion/advice/warning).

## Grammar points — Ch.34 Asking and giving personal information

Largely functional-register vocabulary (llamarse for name; ser + nationality-adjective; ser + de for
origin; estar/ser for marital status with a usage-frequency note — estar more frequent overall and
especially in Spain, ser more common in Latin America for the same function, another explicit
Spain/Latin-America frequency-tilt rather than a hard rule; tener for age; nacer in the preterite for
date/place of birth; ser for occupation/status/religion/political affiliation, with the explicit
note that Spanish omits the indefinite article before an occupation/affiliation noun, "Soy
arquitecto," "Miguel es socialista"). The occupation-chapter has a genuinely useful minimal pair not
elsewhere in the corpus: **estar + de** marks a temporary occupation the subject is suited for ("está
de dependienta" = working as a shop-assistant, temporarily), while **hacer de** marks an occupation
the subject is either substituting into on an ad hoc basis or is not suited/qualified for ("tú
tendrás que hacer de secretaria," "tuve que hacer de recepcionista" after failing to find journalism
work) — a suitedness/legitimacy distinction encoded purely in the choice of light verb, relevant to
later work on how register/legitimacy might be encoded in slang verb-phrase choices.

## Grammar points — Ch.35 Identifying people, places, things

Straightforward ser-based identification (soy, es, son) plus demonstrative-fronted introductions
(ésta es mi mujer) and ¿cuál?/¿cuáles?/¿qué? as the identification-question words for places/things
respectively (vs. ¿quién?/¿quiénes? for people). Fully consistent with, and not adding beyond, the
ser/estar and interrogative material already captured; kept brief since the content itself is thin
(the book's own chapter intro admits "the range of phrases used... is very limited").

## Grammar points — Ch.36 Describing — the ser/estar description system, fully mapped by function

This chapter is the single most systematic ser/estar treatment in the corpus, because it organizes
the contrast by **communicative function** rather than by abstract rule, yielding several genuinely
new angles beyond 005/006/014/015's more rule-based treatments:
- **Nature/identity → ser + adjective** (La Tierra es redonda). Adjective position carries real
  semantic weight here: a handful of adjectives change meaning by position (un hombre grande "a big
  man" vs. un gran hombre "a great man"; una persona pobre "poor/moneyless" vs. una pobre persona
  "pitiful"; una casa nueva "a [brand-]new house" vs. una nueva casa "another/a different house";
  una persona única "a unique person" vs. la única persona "the only person") — a clean, compact
  minimal-pair set for later work on how word order alone can shift an adjective from
  objective/distinctive to subjective/affective meaning. Nationality/shape/substance/purpose
  adjectives are "essentially distinctive" and stay post-nominal; color adjectives default
  post-nominal but can front for an affective reading ("la blanca montaña"). The book flags a
  regional/stylistic trend toward more pre-nominal adjective placement in parts of the
  Spanish-speaking world (unspecified which, "beyond the scope of this book").
- **State/condition → estar + adjective**, with an explicit property-vs-state minimal pair: "Elena
  está muy guapa" (looks pretty right now, a state) vs. "Elena es muy guapa" (is pretty as an
  inherent property).
- **Unspoken-comparison descriptions → estar**, a genuinely distinct sub-use not clearly separated in
  prior corpus files: estar used when a description implicitly compares the subject against an
  earlier state of the *same* subject (El niño está grande = "he's bigger than when I last saw him,"
  not an absolute size claim) — this is a comparative-estar rather than a locative or state-estar,
  structurally and pragmatically distinct from the standard contrast.
- **Material composition**: ¿De qué es? (ser + de, intrinsic material identity) vs. ¿De/En qué está
  hecho? (estar + hecho, state framing even though hecho itself is inherently about composition) —
  showing the ser/estar contrast persists even when both verbs attach to the same semantic domain.
- **Describing events**: ser gives an "objective tone," estar a "more subjective" one, with
  first-hand-experienced events skewing toward estar ("La fiesta fue/estuvo muy buena") — an
  evidentiality-flavored ser/estar distinction not found as such in prior corpus coverage.
- **Facts/information**: ser only (subjective evaluative statements: "¡Es algo muy insólito!").
- **Social manners**: ser for general/stable character traits, ser-or-estar for a one-off
  manner-instance ("Fue/estuvo muy amable conmigo").
- **Weather**: hacer + noun is the default (hace calor/frío/viento/sol); estar + adjective for a
  specific-moment description (está caluroso) and is *obligatory* when the weather-adjective is
  actually a past participle (está nublado, estará despejado) — because the participle inherently
  encodes a resultant state, reusing the Ch.22/24 estar-as-resultant-state logic; ser is required
  instead when describing "a day" as a noun-headed phrase (Ayer fue un día muy frío) since ser, not
  estar, is the one that pairs with a noun/pronoun predicate per 22.1.

## Grammar points — Ch.37 Making comparisons (37.1-37.2 kept; 37.3 superlatives skipped as redundant)

Comparisons of inequality (más/menos + adjective/adverb/noun/verb + que) and equality (tan... como,
tanto... como, igual que, lo mismo que, igual a, igual de/igualmente, parecerse) are systematically
laid out with one genuinely non-obvious construction not seen elsewhere in the corpus: **comparison
against a clause rather than a noun uses de + resumptive pronoun instead of que** — lo (referring
back to an entire clause: "Es más rápida de lo que yo pensaba" = faster than I thought) vs.
el/la/los/las (referring back to a specific noun already mentioned: "Requiere más tiempo del que
dispongo" = more time than [the time] I have) — a structural split in which the resumptive pronoun's
gender/number agreement signals whether the comparison's second term is a proposition or a concrete
referent. A second fine distinction: "no gana más de 200.000" (doesn't earn more than — implying
approximately 200,000) vs. "no gana más que 200.000" (earns only exactly 200,000, no more) — de vs.
que after más/menos with a bare numeral changes the comparison from an approximate ceiling to an
exact-amount restriction. The equality set has an explicit non-interchangeability warning: igual que
("the same way") and lo mismo que ("the same thing") look parallel but are not swappable, though both
collapse into the single idiom "me da igual"/"me da lo mismo" ("it's all the same to me"). **37.3
(comparing more than two objects / superlatives) is SKIPPED as redundant** — the definite-article +
más/menos + adjective + de pattern (el monte más alto de las Américas) and its exceptions (omission
of the article after estar or with adverbs) are already covered by `006` and `014`'s
comparative/superlative chapters; nothing new found on a skim.

## Grammar points — Ch.38 Expressing existence and availability

Hay (impersonal, invariant for singular/plural) marks *indefinite* existence, while estar marks the
existence/presence of something already definite/contextually determined — a direct extension of
the definite/indefinite-article logic into the existential-verb system itself ("¿Hay un buen hotel en
la ciudad?" / "Pues, está el Hotel Isla Negra"). A genuinely useful sociolinguistic flag: colloquial
and especially Latin American speech shows a tendency to pluralize hay's past/future forms (hubieron,
habían) to agree with a plural notional subject even though haber-existential is grammatically
invariant — the book explicitly calls this incorrect-but-prevalent, a textbook case of a non-standard
regularization (plural agreement spreading to an impersonal verb) that a mechanics-analysis phase
might treat as a candidate source for a "naturalized slang morphology." Existir is the formal-register
existence verb. For describing facilities, tener is markedly more frequent than hay in the same
function. For availability, haber/tener are the defaults and disponer de/contar con are
formal-register alternatives, paralleling the possession-verb register ladder in Ch.40.

## Grammar points — Ch.39 Expressing location and distance

A graded formality ladder for "to be located": estar (default) < encontrarse/hallarse (formal,
written) < estar situado (least frequent, formal written only, and situado inflects like an
adjective). A rare, explicitly-marked-as-risky colloquial substitution: ser instead of estar for
place location ("¿Dónde dijiste que era la casa de Juan?") — the book frames this as carrying an
extra "essence/existence" flavor beyond pure location, and explicitly advises learners to avoid it
and default to estar — a genuine case of a colloquial register variant layering extra semantic nuance
onto a usually-fixed grammatical choice, flagged as non-core and risky to imitate. A hard rule, not
just a tendency: ser (never estar) for where an *event* (reunión, fiesta) takes/took place, since
events aren't spatially locatable "things." The precise-location preposition inventory (a,
bajo/debajo de, de, en, encima de, enfrente de/frente a, entre, sobre) mostly reprises Ch.25 but adds
register notes specific to location use: bajo is formal/written-only while debajo de is all-register
for the same meaning; enfrente de skews Spain, frente a skews Latin America (and is the more formal
of the two even within Spain) — a three-way regional+register split on a single meaning. The adverb
pair acá/allá is explicitly marked as less precise than aquí/allí and as a Latin American preference
specifically for acá. Distance is expressed with estar + a + distance phrase, or with
quedar/hallarse/distar as alternatives — distar being the most formal, taking a numeral directly.

## Grammar points — Ch.40 Expressing possessive relations

Mostly redundant with 010/014's possessive-adjective/pronoun paradigms (skimmed, not re-extracted in
full), but the chapter's register ladder for the verb "to have/own" is worth keeping: tener (neutral
default) < es de/pertenecer (pertenecer explicitly "less colloquial, more formal") < poseer/disponer
de/contar con (formal, especially written) < es propiedad de (formal written only, legalistic).
Emphasis of possession can be achieved either by stressing the short possessive (mi/tu/su) or by
using the long form (mío/tuyo/suyo), or via the adjective propio "own" — propio normally precedes its
noun but *loses* the accompanying possessive when it follows the noun instead ("tiene coche propio"
drops "su" entirely) — a clean case of one word's position determining whether a second,
semantically-related word is required at all. Body parts/personal effects use the definite article
instead of a possessive ("me duelen los pies," not "me duelen mis pies") — a systematic substitution
rule, not just stylistic preference.

## Grammar points — Ch.41 Expressing changes — a genuinely new, systematic "become"-verb typology

Not comprehensively laid out elsewhere in the corpus: Spanish splits the single English concept "to
become" across (at least) six distinct verbs/phrases, each gated by a different semantic feature,
making this chapter one of the most valuable finds in Part B for later slang-mechanics work on
semantic-field fragmentation:
- **ponerse + adjective**: temporary change, mood/appearance, can be sudden (se puso furioso) or
  gradual (se puso gorda); closely paired with estar+adjective, the two forming an exact
  transformation/result pair (se puso celoso "became jealous" vs. está celoso "is jealous").
  Exception: ponerse viejo is used for "become old" despite being a long-lasting change — a lexical
  exception to the chapter's own general feature-split rule, worth flagging precisely because it's an
  exception. The causer of the change can be promoted to grammatical subject by swapping the
  reflexive ponerse for the plain transitive poner (mi llegada lo puso contento).
- **ponerse a + infinitive**: onset of an activity (se puso a llover, se puso a llorar) — distinct
  grammatical slot (infinitival complement) from the adjectival use above.
- **volverse + adjective**: long-lasting, usually gradual change specifically in character/
  attitude/mental state (se volvió loca, se ha vuelto muy huraño).
- **hacerse + adjective/noun**: three distinct sub-uses packed into one verb — (i) long-lasting change
  NOT involving character (se hizo rico, se hicieron millonarios); (ii) voluntary role/
  ideology/occupation change, typically subject-willed (hacerse monja, hacerse socialista, hacerse
  musulmana); (iii) change resulting from a natural/impersonal process (se había hecho muy tarde, se
  hizo muy vieja) — i.e. hacerse is the typological "elsewhere" verb covering whatever
  ponerse/volverse don't, unified only by NOT being the ponerse-temporary or volverse-character cases.
- **llegar a ser + noun/adjective**: the result of a slow, effortful, goal-directed process (llegó a
  ser director, llegó a ser célebre) — carries an achievement/accomplishment connotation none of the
  other become-verbs have.
- **quedar / quedarse**: change as the *direct result of a specific action or event* rather than an
  internally-driven transformation (mi habitación quedó muy bonita [after I painted it]; se quedó
  pálida [on hearing news]) — for sadness/pallor-type changes, quedarse and ponerse are
  interchangeable but quedarse specifically emphasizes an external-agent cause; for loss/incapacity
  changes (quedó/se quedó inválido, huérfano, viudo, soltera) both quedar and quedarse work
  interchangeably.
- **transformarse en / convertirse en / convertirse a**: a more dramatic "turn into" framing
  (transformarse/convertirse en un basural "turned into a rubbish dump"), with convertirse a
  specifically reserved for religious conversion.

## Grammar points — Ch.42 Describing processes and results

Process description splits into **personal** (present tense / future tense / tener que + infinitive
— all spoken-register-associated, informal-written at most, with present/future suited to multi-step
sequences and tener que suited to single actions) and **impersonal** forms: hay que + infinitive
(colloquial spoken, rare in writing except very informal), third-person-plural verb (similar
register, "emphasizes what is done" rather than hay que's "emphasizes the precise action to be
undertaken" — a real functional distinction between two superficially similar impersonal devices), se
+ 3rd-person verb (used in BOTH registers but especially common in formal written process-description
— recipes, lab procedures, manufacturing steps — with an explicit constraint: only usable when the
agent is irrelevant OR understood-but-omitted, never when the agent must be stated), and ser + past
participle (least frequent in speech — "uncommon except in very formal, non-spontaneous speech such
as radio or television news" — but common in formal writing; crucially, this passive construction is
markedly LESS natural for a single isolated action than for a multi-step described process, where the
more colloquial active-with-implied-subject or third-person-plural alternative is preferred instead —
directly paralleling the "quedar por + infinitve" single-vs-habitual distinction elsewhere). Result
description (estar + past participle) splits by the underlying verb's transitivity: transitive-verb-
derived results (el trabajo está terminado) vs. intransitive/reflexive-verb-derived results, the
latter group often corresponding to an English "to be + -ing" rather than "to be + past-participle"
gloss (estar acostado "to be lying down," estar sentado "to be sitting," estar arrodillado "to be
kneeling") — a systematic participle-as-static-posture-description idiom cluster worth keeping as a
set. The chapter's clearest minimal pair: the *same* past participle with ser denotes the
action/process itself (los secuestradores fueron rodeados por la policía = the police surrounded
them, an event) vs. with estar denotes the pre-existing resultant state (los secuestradores estaban
rodeados por la policía = they were [already] surrounded, a state) — reusing and sharpening the Ch.24
ser/estar-passive distinction with a crisper real-world example pair.

## Grammar points — Ch.43 Expressing cause, effect and purpose

Enquiring about cause has its own register ladder: ¿Por qué? (neutral default) < ¿Cómo...?/¿Cómo es
que...? (colloquial, spoken, and — notably — pragmatically loaded with surprise/disapproval, not just
neutral cause-inquiry; ¿Cómo es que...? carries this more strongly than bare ¿Cómo...?) < ¿A qué se
debe? (less colloquial, both registers) < ¿Por qué razón/motivo?/¿Cuál es la razón/el motivo? (most
formal). Giving reasons has a parallel graded set: porque (default; takes the subjunctive
specifically when preceded by a negative — "no porque sea mi jefe..." — a negation-triggered mood
shift worth noting alongside the no porque subjunctive-trigger already flagged in Ch.27) ≈ pues
(colloquial synonym for porque, "especially common in Spain," but the book warns porque and pues are
"not always interchangeable" so a learner should default to porque) < por/a causa de/debido a (por
most colloquial/spoken, the other two mildly more formal, both-register) < se debe a (formal,
both-register) < como (sentence-initial only, introduces new/unknown-to-listener information) ≈ ya
que/puesto que (interchangeable, formal/written-leaning, ya que more common and more colloquial of the
two, and — unlike como — can go either before or after the main clause) < dado que (restricted to
very formal writing/speech, the ceiling of this whole scale). **Es que...** is functionally distinct
from the rest of this set: not a neutral cause-conjunction at all but a colloquial, dialogue-initial
excuse/justification opener ("¿Por qué no lo has terminado? – Es que no he tenido tiempo") — a
pragmatically specialized member of the cause-expressing family worth distinguishing from the others
precisely because its function (excuse-giving in conversational reply position) differs from theirs
(stating a reason within a continuous argument). Purpose expression centers on para (+ subjunctive via
para que when subjects differ, + bare infinitive when subjects match, + noun/pronoun for plain
destination) with de manera/modo/forma que as the next most common alternative — notably ambiguous
between purpose (subjunctive) and mere consequence (indicative) depending solely on mood, translating
as "so that" vs. "and so" respectively — a single conjunction phrase whose two near-synonymous English
glosses are disambiguated purely by the following verb's mood, a clean illustration of
mood-as-meaning-bearing rather than mood-as-mere-syntax-trigger. A fin/con el fin de (que), con el
objeto de (que), con el propósito de (que) round out the set as more formal synonyms of para (que)
with identical subject-matching infinitive/subjunctive distribution rules.

## Grammar points — Ch.44 Expressing knowledge (saber vs. conocer — compact; mostly redundant)

## Grammar points — Ch.45 Remembering and forgetting

Acordarse de (involuntary remembering only) vs. recordar (either involuntary remembering OR the
voluntary act of reminding someone else, "recuérdame que...") — recordar is semantically broader,
covering a causative sense acordarse de lacks entirely. A soft lexical-choice tendency rather than a
hard rule: concrete things (a phone number) skew toward acordarse de; evocative/emotional memories
skew toward recordar — "the difference... is not always clear and the two verbs are sometimes
interchangeable." A real mood-trigger asymmetry: "recuerdo que..." (positive) takes the indicative,
but "no recuerdo que..." (negative) requires the subjunctive in the embedded clause — yet this
negative-triggers-subjunctive rule disappears in *questions*: "¿no recuerdas que...?" stays
indicative despite being formally negative, an exception the book flags explicitly. For forgetting:
olvidarse (marked reflexive, agrees in number with the forgotten item, emphasizes accidental loss —
"se me olvidó el paraguas") and dejarse/dejar (regionally marked as heard "in Spain" for the same
accidental-forgetting-of-an-object sense) both describe forgetting objects; separately, olvidar vs.
olvidarse de forms an intentionality minimal pair on the very same root ("olvidar" = forgetting as an
intentional/willed act of putting something out of mind; "olvidarse de" = genuinely unintentional
forgetting) — a clean case of reflexive-marking correlating with (un)intentionality, echoing the
reflexive/non-reflexive semantic-nuance pattern already flagged in Ch.23.7.

## Grammar points — Ch.46 Expressing obligation and duty — a frequency+source-of-obligation ladder

Four obligation verbs are explicitly differentiated by where the obligation is felt to originate, not
just by register: **tener que** (by far the most frequent; externally-sourced obligation, "from
outside the speaker himself") < **deber** (much less frequent than tener que; internally-sourced,
"moral obligation," and — counterintuitively — characterized as "strong obligation" despite being
rarer, i.e. frequency and strength are explicitly decoupled here) < **poder** in the
conditional/imperfect (mild obligation, "might," a modal-remoteness-as-softening mechanism reusing
the conditional-politeness logic from Ch.21/30) < **haber de** (mild obligation, "normally found in
literary registers" — the one obligation verb explicitly tagged literary rather than just formal).
Impersonal obligation defaults to hay que (and its full tense paradigm: había que, hubo que, habrá
que, habría que). A noteworthy regional lexical pair recurs here for the first time in this file but
will recur repeatedly through the rest of Part B: visado (Spain) / visa (Latin America) for "visa,"
and coche (Spain) / carro (Latin America) for "car" — the book's two recurring go-to examples for the
Spain/Latin-America lexical-split phenomenon in general.

## Grammar points — Ch.47 Expressing needs — tener que vs. necesitar strength scale, plus hay que/hacer falta distributional asymmetry

Personal need: **tener que** (strong need, "almost... no alternative") > **necesitar** (more
neutral/mild) — reusing Ch.46's tener-que-as-strong-obligation finding in the needs domain too.
Necesitar's complementation splits exactly like Ch.26's infinitive-complementation rules: bare
infinitive when same-subject, +que+subjunctive when different-subject ("necesito que me
aconsejes"), +noun directly, or (rare, formal, explicitly flagged as risky for a learner to attempt)
+de+noun/pronoun. Impersonal need has a genuinely asymmetric distribution between its two main
exponents: **hay que** is the most frequent in positive sentences and its negative form reads as
near-prohibition ("Aquí no hay que fumar" = "you mustn't smoke here," not merely "you don't need to");
**hacer falta** is comparatively rare in positive sentences but *very* common specifically in
negative ones, where it cleanly means "it's unnecessary" without the near-prohibition overtone hay
que's negative carries — i.e. the two near-synonyms partition by polarity as much as by raw
frequency, and crucially diverge in what their negative forms actually mean (prohibition vs. mere
unnecessity). Hacer falta also stands alone idiomatically as a polite decline to an offer ("No hace
falta, gracias"). Ser necesario is rarer than both hay que and hacer falta — explicitly noted as much
rarer than English "to be necessary," a case where a seemingly-literal translation undershoots the
target language's actual register distribution. Strong/essential need uses a formal-register set (ser
esencial/preciso/imprescindible/indispensable/fundamental) that patterns syntactically just like ser
necesario (infinitive same-subject, +que+subjunctive different-subject/impersonal).

## Grammar points — Ch.48 Expressing possibility and probability — mood-selection as a graded certainty signal

The chapter's central finding, genuinely valuable for later mechanics-analysis work on how
certainty/register could map onto conlang slang particles: **several possibility adverbs select
indicative vs. subjunctive mood as a function of the speaker's own degree of confidence, not as a
fixed grammatical requirement.** Quizá(s)/tal vez: indicative signals HIGHER perceived likelihood
than subjunctive for the same adverb ("quizá(s) esté en casa" = less confident than "quizá(s) está en
casa") — i.e. mood choice is doing real epistemic work, not just subcategorization. Positionally
conditioned too: when either adverb follows its verb instead of preceding it, only the indicative is
used regardless of confidence — a word-order-triggered mood-selection override. Posiblemente defaults
to subjunctive when a verb follows; probablemente defaults to indicative — two near-synonyms with
opposite default mood selection. **Acaso is the standout minimal pair**: in declarative statements of
uncertainty it is formal-register and subjunctive-taking, rarely heard in speech; in rhetorical
YES/NO QUESTIONS it flips to indicative and becomes common, colloquial, and specifically
sarcastic/annoyed in illocutionary force ("¿Acaso no te dije que no lo hicieras?" = "Didn't I tell you
not to...?" with an implied "of course I did, you should have listened") — the same lexeme spans
formal-uncertainty and colloquial-sarcasm registers purely by moving from statement to question
syntax. A lo mejor (indicative-only, colloquial, very common in speech) and the Spain-specific
igual/lo mismo (also indicative-only, explicitly marked as informal/colloquial and regionally Spain)
round out the "low-register perhaps" cluster, distinct from the higher-register
quizá(s)/tal vez/posiblemente/probablemente/acaso cluster — a genuinely useful register-tier split
across near-synonyms expressing the same core meaning ("perhaps"). Separately, deber (de) +
infinitive/estar+gerund/haber+participle expresses epistemic probability/supposition (not
obligation, despite the surface identity with Ch.21/46's deber) — the book notes most speakers drop
the disambiguating "de," leaving the two meanings (obligation vs. supposition) to be resolved by
context/verb-type alone, a real source of structural ambiguity in the spoken language. Grammatical
tense itself can encode epistemic probability instead of time reference: future for
present-probability ("estará con alguien" = "he must be with someone," not "he will be"), future
perfect for past-probability-with-present-relevance ("ya se habrán enterado" = "they must have found
out"), and conditional for past probability ("serían las tres" = "it must have been three o'clock")
— a systematic tense-to-modality remapping, not three isolated idioms. Impersonal possibility-
inquiry has its own small ladder: ¿se puede + infinitive? (most common, impersonal) ≈ ¿poder +
infinitive? (personalized) > future/future perfect (¿estará en casa?) > ¿es posible + infinitive?
(least common/colloquial of the four).

## Grammar points — Ch.49-50 Certainty/uncertainty and Supposition (compact — heavy overlap with Ch.48)

These two chapters largely re-deploy the same expression families already captured under Ch.48
(deber (de), future/future-perfect/conditional for epistemic modality, quizá(s)/tal vez/a lo mejor),
organized instead around a certainty-strength ladder: **strong positive certainty** (no cabe duda,
sin duda, indudablemente, estar seguro/convencido de que, claro/por supuesto/desde luego que) >
**intermediate certainty** (me parece que, supongo que, creo que — these three are explicitly
positioned as weaker-than-"estoy seguro" hedges, and intermediate certainty about the FUTURE
specifically defaults to the plain future tense, "tendrá mucho éxito") > **weak
certainty/uncertainty** (no sé si, no estoy seguro (de que), no creo que, me pregunto si, puede ser, a
lo mejor/quizá(s)/tal vez) > **negative certainty** (por supuesto/claro/desde luego que no, no me
parece). A mood-trigger rule recurs here identically to Ch.45/32: "no estoy seguro de que..." and "no
creo que..." require the subjunctive, mirroring their positive counterparts' indicative. The one
genuinely new nugget: Ch.50 catalogs four supposition-introducing verbs with a formality split —
suponer and imaginar(se) are "equally common in informal and formal contexts," while figurarse and
poner (used in the sense "suppose") are markedly more informal/spoken-only — and all four can shift
from supposition-verb (+ subjunctive, functioning like "si") to ordinary thinking-verb (+ indicative,
when not negated) depending on whether the surrounding context frames a hypothetical or a genuine
belief — the same verb oscillating between two different complementation patterns by function, not
form.

## Grammar points — Ch.51 Expressing conditions — the colloquial si-clause-substitute inventory

Beyond the standard four-way si-conditional system (open/probable: si+present+future; open/
uncertain: si+present+present-or-future; open/past-uncertain: si+past+present-or-future; remote:
si+imperfect-subjunctive+conditional; unfulfilled/past-contrary-to-fact: si+pluperfect-
subjunctive+conditional-perfect) — all of which substantially overlaps prior corpus coverage and is
only briefly noted here — **the chapter's standout content is its inventory of colloquial
SUBSTITUTES for an overt si-clause**, which is a genuinely new catalog for this corpus: **de +
infinitive** (same-subject only, especially spoken, "de haber tenido tiempo..." for "si hubiera
tenido tiempo..."; de + ser specifically licenses a different-subject reading); **bare gerund**
("subiendo por esta calle llegarás al parque" = "if you go up this street..."), explicitly marked
colloquial; **bare imperative**, specifically in warnings/threats ("hazlo y te arrepentirás" = "if you
do it, you'll regret it"), restricted to informal spoken language; **como + subjunctive**, likewise
restricted to informal spoken warnings/threats/fear-expressions and EXPLICITLY MARKED as "uncommon in
Latin America" — a genuine Spain-vs-Latin-America syntactic (not just lexical) availability gap. A
second regional/register flag recurs at 51.1.6: substituting a plain imperfect indicative for the
expected conditional in an unfulfilled-condition's main clause ("si volviera a pegarme lo dejaba" for
"...lo dejaría") is "generally acceptable in Peninsular Spanish" but "may be considered substandard
in some Latin American countries" — a cross-regional standard-language judgment call on the
identical construction, directly relevant to later work on how a "wrong grammar is fine here, taboo
there" pattern might seed region-specific slang/vernacular status. The "unless" set (a menos que, a
no ser que, salvo que) and the "provided that" set (con tal (de) que, siempre que, siempre y cuando,
a condición de que, con la condición de que) round out the formal apparatus, both always
subjunctive-taking and the latter explicitly framed as typically used "in reply to a request."

## Grammar points — Ch.52 Expressing contrast or opposition — a clean formality ladder

pero (default, neutral) < sin embargo (more formal, written-leaning; emphasis-reinforced as "pero sin
embargo") < aunque (both-register, but splits mood exactly like the already-noted pattern: indicative
when the contrasted information is a known FACT, subjunctive when it's a possibility/hypothetical,
i.e. "even though" vs. "even if" encoded purely in mood — with a further wrinkle that aunque
answering an objection takes the subjunctive even when factual, "aunque no te gusten las lentejas
tendrás que comértelas") < a pesar de (que)/pese a (que) (a pesar de is the default of this
near-synonym pair, pese a explicitly "less colloquial") < no obstante (the single most formal
contrast-word, written-register, substitutable for either sin embargo or a pesar de/pese a). A
secondary, more colloquial set (aun cuando, si bien, así, aun así, así y todo, por el contrario,
mientras que, en cambio, al contrario de lo que) sits alongside this formal ladder as near-synonyms,
several explicitly glossed as swappable with aunque. This is now the fourth distinct domain in this
book (negation strength in Ch.32, cause-conjunctions in Ch.43, contrast-conjunctions here, plus
Ch.27's conjunction catalog) where Spanish organizes a semantic field as an explicit
formality-graded near-synonym ladder rather than a single word with register-neutral modifiers —
worth flagging as a recurring structural pattern across the whole book, useful for later
mechanics-analysis work on how register ladders might seed slang formation (a colloquial-tier term
"escaping upward" or a formal-tier term "slumming downward").

## Grammar points — Ch.53 Expressing capability and incapability

poder + infinitive is the default for physical/mental capability (and shares its form with
possibility/permission/request functions elsewhere in the book — the same modal verb spans four
distinct functions disambiguated only by context); the preterite of poder specifically means
"managed to" (pude arreglarlo = "I managed to repair it," an achievement reading) while the
imperfect indicates mere capability without confirming the action happened (podía arreglarlo = "was
capable of" but possibly didn't). A handful of single verbs (entender, oír, ver, sentir) can express
capability on their own without poder, purely via the present tense ("¿Lo oyes?" = "Can you hear
it?"). Ser capaz de is markedly less common than poder but denotes STRONGER capability. Crucially,
**learned/trained abilities (playing an instrument, swimming, speaking a language) use saber, never
poder** — reusing the saber/poder split from Ch.21/44 in a new functional domain.

## Grammar points — Ch.54 Seeking and giving permission — register ladder for "may I"

Seeking permission scales: poder + infinitive / ¿se puede + infinitive? (defaults, bare ¿Se puede?
alone for entering a room) < dejar + infinitive, in two forms — imperative-with-attached-pronoun
("déjame usar...") read as the more direct/informal variant, vs. present-tense-with-preceding-
pronoun-in-a-rising-intonation-question ("¿me dejas usar...?") explicitly marked as "more polite"
despite being the SAME verb and meaning — a register distinction encoded purely in
imperative-vs.-interrogative mood, not in word choice < importar/molestar que + subjunctive ("¿le
importa/molesta que fume?" = "do you mind if I smoke?" — more formal, requires an indirect object
pronoun identifying whose permission is sought) < permitir que + subjunctive / permitir + infinitive
(both "generally infrequent," formal-register) < rogar que + subjunctive (most formal, "very common
in formal letter writing," directly reusing the Ch.29.9.3.5 letter-writing material). Giving
permission has its own graded set from plain sí up through desde luego/por supuesto/claro (que sí)
to the idiom ¡no faltaba más! — with a social-pragmatics note that speakers signal genuine
non-objection by *repeating* sí and/or the follow-on word ("Sí, sí, ciérrala") — an
iteration-as-sincerity-marker pattern. Withholding permission uses no, perdone/lo siento pero..., no
poder + infinitive, prohibir + infinitive, está prohibido/no está permitido — with a register-specific
note that **public signage uses a fixed "Prohibido + infinitive" construction** ("Prohibido fumar,"
"Prohibido aparcar") distinct from how prohibition is phrased in speech, i.e. signage has its own
grammatical register convention.

## Grammar points — Ch.55 Asking and giving opinions — a verb-choice frequency ladder, not just a register one

Parecer (functions like gustar — indirect-object-pronoun-fronted — and is "the most frequent verb"
for both asking and giving opinions) > creer (also very common; crucially, when negated ["no creo
que..."] the complement clause must take the subjunctive, mirroring the now-repeatedly-confirmed
negation-triggers-subjunctive pattern from Ch.32/45/49) > pensar ("used in constructions similar to
those with creer, except that overall it is used less commonly" — i.e. near-total syntactic overlap
with a pure frequency difference) > opinar/considerar (markedly less common, more formal,
considerar especially so) > the periphrastic set (¿Cuál es su/tu opinión?, soy de la opinión de que,
a mi parecer/juicio/modo de ver, desde mi punto de vista, a mi entender) — all explicitly described
as LESS frequent than the simple opinion-verbs above despite being closer to a literal English
translation of "what's your opinion" — another instance (cf. Ch.47's ser necesario) of the book
flagging that the naive word-for-word translation undershoots actual Spanish frequency. Parecer
notably also shows a mood-pattern quirk distinct from creer: when parecer is followed directly by an
adjective/adverb + que, the complement clause takes the SUBJUNCTIVE even when parecer itself is
affirmative/positive ("me parece raro que él no esté aquí") — unlike plain "me parece que +
indicative," i.e. the presence of an intervening adjective, not just negation, can itself trigger the
subjunctive. Reporting others' opinions most commonly uses según ("by far the most common word... in
both formal and informal contexts"), with según el parecer de and ser del parecer que as
progressively more formal/infrequent alternatives.

## Grammar points — Ch.56 Agreement, disagreement, indifference — the clearest formality+function matrix in the book

Agreement markers split into two distinct FUNCTIONS, not just a flat synonym list: a subset (de
acuerdo, vale, bien, bueno, conforme) agrees to a *proposal/invitation* (accepting an action), while
a different subset (cierto, eso es, efectivamente, desde luego, tener razón, estar de acuerdo) agrees
with a *statement/opinion* someone else made — the two functions are NOT interchangeable even though
both gloss as "agree." Within this set: vale is explicitly marked informal and especially
Spain-specific; efectivamente is explicitly marked formal; ¡claro! is explicitly "much more frequent
among Latin Americans"; tener razón/estar de acuerdo are reserved for more emphatic agreement.
Disagreement is explicitly graded by strength, a four-point scale spelled out directly by the book:
mild (no sé, no estoy seguro — for disagreeing with a proposal) ≈ mild (¿tú crees?, no creo — for
disagreeing with a statement) < de ninguna manera/ningún modo (strong, for proposals) ≈ no es
cierto/verdad, no estar de acuerdo, estar equivocado (stronger-but-not-categoric, for statements) <
¡qué va! (strong, categoric) < **¡cómo que...!** (explicitly named the STRONGEST form, used by
echoing back part of the disputed statement itself — "No hiciste lo que te pedí." – "¡Cómo que no lo
hice!" — a genuinely distinctive echo-based disagreement mechanism, structurally different from
every other item in this set, worth flagging for later slang-mechanics work on
argument/confrontation registers). Indifference has its own small idiom cluster (me da igual/lo
mismo < me tiene/trae sin cuidado [stronger] < a mí ni me va ni me viene < Y (a mí) ¿qué? [dismissive
retort, pragmatically closer to a rebuff than a neutral statement of indifference]) — note "me da
igual/lo mismo" directly reuses the Ch.37.2.4 comparison-of-equality idiom (igual/lo mismo) in a
completely different semantic function (indifference, not comparison), and also reuses Ch.48's
"igual/lo mismo" epistemic-perhaps sense — the same two words carrying at least three unrelated
idiomatic functions across the book depending purely on construction, a clean illustration of how a
small set of flexible function-words can be heavily functionally overloaded in colloquial Spanish.

## Grammar points — Ch.57-66 (sub-part IV, Expressing emotional attitudes) — compact summary

These ten chapters share one dominant structural pattern, confirmed so consistently across all of
them that it is worth stating once rather than per-chapter: **a verb or adjective of emotional stance
(desire, liking, surprise, satisfaction, hope, sympathy, apology, fear) governs a complement clause
whose MOOD and TENSE both track the main verb** — present main verb + present subjunctive complement
for present/future reference, perfect/imperfect subjunctive complement for past reference, imperfect
main verb + imperfect subjunctive complement for past desires/hopes. This single template recurs,
with only the governing verb changing, across querer/preferir que (57.4), gustar/parecer que
(57.4.2), verbs of liking/disliking + que (58.3.4), sorprender/extrañar/asombrar que (59.2),
alegrarse de que (63.2.2), perdonar/disculpar que (64.1.3), tener miedo de que / temer que
(65.1.4/65.1.7), and esperar que / ojalá (que) (62.2) — i.e. this whole sub-part is really one
grammatical mechanism (emotion-verb-governed subjunctive with tense-tracking) wearing ten different
lexical costumes. Genuinely distinctive findings worth keeping separately:
- **Querer's softened forms** form a politeness ladder on a single verb: quiero (can sound abrupt) <
  quisiera (polite/formal "I'd like") < quería, the IMPERFECT used specifically to soften a request
  when addressing strangers or authority figures ("quería hablar con usted") — a third,
  past-tense-as-politeness-marker rung beyond the already-noted conditional-as-politeness pattern.
- **The colloquial desire-verb cluster (apetecer, tener ganas de, hacer ilusión) carries explicit,
  graduated Peninsular-vs-Latin-American restriction**: apetecer merely "uncommon" in Latin America,
  hacer ilusión "generally unknown" there — two degrees of regional unavailability for near-synonyms
  of the same semantic cluster, worth noting as a spectrum rather than a binary present/absent flag.
- **Desear is rare in ordinary informal speech but is the default verb of a few specific
  SUB-REGISTERS** (advertising copy, shop-assistant and waiter service language) — a register
  carve-out that cuts across the usual formal/informal axis; desear is "formal" in one dimension but
  tied to specific service-industry contexts in another.
- **Satisfaction/approval/disapproval (Ch.60-61) share one grammatical engine**: parecer (+ indirect
  object pronoun, exactly like gustar) is the default verb for expressing an opinion about whether
  something is satisfactory/approved. The book gives country-specific slang exclamations for strong
  satisfaction (¡Qué chévere! most of Latin America except the Southern Cone; ¡Macanudo! Southern
  Cone; ¡Regio! Southern Cone and Colombia) — a rare case of the book cataloging genuinely slangy,
  non-standard-register vocabulary explicitly, directly on-target for this project's eventual
  synthesis phase.
- **Gratitude is explicitly marked as a LOWER-FREQUENCY SPEECH ACT in Spanish than in English**,
  "especially... among equals and, generally, in any informal situation" (66.1 intro) — a
  cross-linguistic pragmatic-frequency claim, not just a vocabulary note, meaning a literal
  thank-you-for-everything translation pattern from English would over-produce gratitude markers in
  Spanish. The gratitude-intensity ladder itself (gracias < muchas/muchísimas gracias < un
  millón/mil gracias < se/te lo agradezco (mucho) < le estoy (sumamente) agradecido < no sabe cuánto
  se lo agradezco) is a clean single-axis intensity scale. Responding to gratitude is explicitly
  contrasted with British English norms (the book notes British speakers often don't verbally
  acknowledge "thank you" at all, while Spanish speakers reliably do) — another cross-linguistic
  pragmatic-norm claim rather than a pure vocabulary item.
- **The fear/worry cluster (Ch.65) shows the clearest strength-plus-formality ladder of the whole
  sub-part**: tener miedo (default, most common) > temer (less common, more formal, and uses a
  "redundant" dative pronoun doubling pattern — "no le temen a nadie" — the book flags this
  pronoun-doubling as "a common feature in Spanish" generally, not unique to temer) > estar
  asustado(a)/asustarse (less frequent; asustarse specifically marks the ONSET of fear — "getting
  frightened" — vs. estar asustado's static state, another reflexive/non-reflexive onset-vs-state
  split echoing Ch.23.7/41) > estar preocupado(a)/preocuparse (the one member of the set that
  literally glosses as "worried" rather than "afraid," with preocuparse taking "por" for its object).
  A highly colloquial intensifier-idiom set closes the chapter (estoy muerto/a de miedo, casi me
  muero de miedo, me llevé un susto de padre y señor mío, se llevó un susto de muerte) — and
  crucially, **"coger," ordinarily a completely neutral verb meaning "to catch/take," is explicitly
  flagged as a TABOO WORD in Mexico and Argentina specifically** (in its "catch a bus" sense it's
  fine generally, but the book's example "le cogí miedo al coche" apparently falls under the taboo
  reading in those countries) and the book advises avoiding it there — a genuine, explicitly-marked
  case of the exact same lexical item carrying innocuous vs. taboo status depending on national
  dialect, likely the single most directly relevant finding in this sub-part for the project's
  eventual taboo-avoidance and regional-collision mechanics work.
- **Apologizing (Ch.64) has a three-tier register split**: perdón/perdone-perdona (neutral default) ≈
  disculpe/disculpa (near-synonym, same register) < perdonar/disculpar que + subjunctive (used
  specifically for "sorry to bother/have bothered you"-type apologies) with sentir + infinitive as a
  "less frequent" alternative construction for the same meaning.
- **Expressing forgiveness (64.2)** uses a small, flat, equally-common set (no importa, nada nada,
  está bien, no tiene importancia, no se preocupe/no te preocupes) with no register gradient noted —
  a contrast with every other speech-act family in this sub-part, worth flagging precisely because of
  its structural flatness.

## Grammar points — Ch.67 Giving advice and making suggestions

Yo que tú/usted + conditional is the leading advice idiom, verbless unlike its English gloss, and —
echoing the recurring Peninsular-imperfect-for-conditional substandard/colloquial substitution
pattern already seen in Ch.51/57 — "spontaneous speech, especially in the Peninsula" replaces the
conditional with the imperfect here too ("yo que tú lo hacía" for "...haría"), the same structural
substitution recurring for a fourth time across this book in a fourth distinct construction. A clean
forcefulness ladder for suggestions-involving-the-speaker: **vamos a + infinitive** (most forceful,
"leaving little ground for dissent" — though a rising intonation softens it toward English "shall
we?") > **podríamos + infinitive** > **¿Qué le/te parece si + present?** (explicitly "less forceful...
leaving more ground for dissent") ≈ **¿Y si... + indicative/imperfect subjunctive?** (more colloquial
variant of the same function) > **es/sería mejor que + subjunctive** (most forceful of the mejor-que
forms). Asking for advice has its own small ladder: bare question-word + present (most common, "¿Qué
hago?") ≈ question-word + poder + infinitive > question-word + conditional (slightly less common) ≈
¿Qué harías tú/haría usted en mi lugar? (colloquial fixed formula) > aconsejar/sugerir
(+que+subjunctive, equally common spoken/written).

## Grammar points — Ch.68 Making requests — the clearest politeness ladder in the whole book

por favor + present ("will you...?" — explicitly the LEAST ambiguous as a pure request, as opposed to
a command, and skews formal) vs. (por favor +) imperative (the most peremptory option, common
informal/service-exchange register, with **intonation doing the actual politeness work**: rising =
polite request, falling = sounds like an order/command — the exact same words, disambiguated purely
suprasegmentally, directly echoing the Ch.30/31 intonation-as-illocutionary-force pattern) < haga/haz
el favor de + infinitive (markedly rarer than the two above, spoken-register) ≈ ¿puede(s)? +
infinitive (register-neutral in speech, but informal-leaning in writing — a spoken/written register
MISMATCH on the identical construction) < ¿podría(s)? + infinitive (more formal/forceful than
puede(s), same spoken/written register split) < ¿le/te importaría? + infinitive (the gustar-type
"would you mind" construction) < ¿sería tan amable de...?/¿tendría la amabilidad de...?/tenga la
bondad de... (formal spoken register, "kind enough to" framing) < le(s) ruego/rogamos (que) + present
subjunctive (the single most common FORMAL WRITTEN request form, especially business correspondence,
que optionally omitted) ≈ le(s) agradecería(mos) (que) + imperfect subjunctive (explicitly "the
second most common form for requesting something formally in writing" after rogar — i.e. the book
explicitly ranks these two formal-written request forms by frequency, not just by register).

## Grammar points — Ch.69 Giving directions, instructions and orders

Directions use the present tense or the imperative as defaults, the bare infinitive for
formal/impersonal written directions (guidebooks, instruction sheets — "Seguir por la M1..."), and
poder+infinitive to soften instructions into something resembling a polite request rather than a
bare command — directly reusing the Ch.68 request-softening machinery in the instructions domain.
**Latin American direction-giving terminology diverges lexically from Peninsular Spanish on three
separate common phrases simultaneously** (siga todo recto → siga derecho; tuerza a la
derecha/izquierda → doble a la derecha/izquierda; coja este tren/esta calle → tome este tren/esta
calle) — and the book explicitly re-flags, for the SECOND time in the book (first at Ch.65.2), that
**coger is a taboo word in Argentina and Mexico specifically**, advising "if you are addressing
Spanish American speakers it is better to avoid it" — the taboo note recurring in a completely
different functional domain (giving directions, not expressing fear) confirms this isn't a one-off:
coger's taboo status in those two countries is general and cuts across whatever verb-sense is
intended, making it one of the single clearest documented cases in this whole book of a cross-dialect
taboo-collision on an otherwise entirely ordinary, high-frequency verb. Orders use the imperative,
present, or infinitive interchangeably, plus "a + infinitive" (¡A callar! "Be quiet!") and the bare
future tense for a peremptory order ("Te quedarás aquí" = "You will stay here"), and ordeno/mando que
+ subjunctive for the most formal/explicit order-giving.

## Grammar points — Ch.70 Making an offer or invitation and accepting/declining

A long formality ladder for invitations/offers, from informal to maximally formal: te invito
(informal, and regionally — "Os invito" is specifically marked Spain in this construction) <
imperative (informal default, upgradable to formal by adding por favor or a polite frame sentence) <
¿por qué no + present? (both-register) < querer + infinitive/noun (informal; "in a formal situation
querer may sound a little abrupt" — an explicit over-directness warning on a verb that's otherwise
entirely neutral) ≈ poder + infinitive (both-register) < desear + infinitive/noun (explicitly
positioned as querer's formal counterpart specifically for this invitation-making function — reusing
Ch.57's desear-is-formal finding in a new domain) < querer que + subjunctive (both-register) <
me/nos gustaría que + subjunctive (interesting dual reading: in INFORMAL address this construction
paradoxically conveys MORE earnestness/commitment-seeking than its surface casualness suggests, while
in FORMAL address it reads as simple politeness — the same construction carrying different pragmatic
weight depending on which register it's deployed into) < quisiera/quisiéramos + infinitive and
me/nos gustaría + infinitive (both "very formal") < le ruego/rogamos que + subjunctive (formal
written, "extremely common" in business correspondence) < tengo/tenemos el agrado de + infinitive
(restricted to very formal written language) < tendría/tendríamos mucho gusto en... (very formal,
usable in both speech and writing). Accepting an offer has its own small register cluster with
¡Vale, gracias! explicitly marked "very informal, especially Spain" at the casual end and con mucho
gusto/encantado/a/muy amable at the formal end. Declining favors hedged, apologetic framing
throughout (no hace falta gracias, lo siento pero, desgraciadamente no puedo) — no sharp register
split noted here, unlike accepting.

## Grammar points — Ch.71 Talking about the present (compact — mostly redundant with Part A Ch.17/20)

The baseline present-tense uses (describing states, general truths, timeless ideas, ongoing events
via estar+gerund or bare present, habitual actions via present or soler/acostumbrar + infinitive)
substantially duplicate Ch.17 and Ch.20 already covered in Part A and triaged there; not
re-extracted. **71.7 "saying how long one has been doing something" is kept in full as a genuinely
systematic construction family** not laid out this completely elsewhere in the corpus: hace + time +
que + present (time-phrase-fronted, emphasis on duration) ≈ present + desde + hace + time
(verb-fronted, emphasis on the action/state itself — the two orderings are a pure
information-structure/emphasis variant pair, not a meaning difference) for states and actions alike;
llevar + time + gerund (interchangeable word order: time-before-gerund or gerund-before-time)
specifically for actions, not states, with the gerund droppable for a handful of verbs (vivir,
trabajar) when context disambiguates, and droppable entirely when a locative prepositional phrase
does the work instead ("mi madre lleva una hora en la cocina"); llevar + time alone (no verb at all)
for states ("lleva una semana enferma"); llevar + time + sin + infinitive for negative continuity
("llevamos dos meses sin hablarnos"); seguir/continuar + gerund for continuing action without a
specified duration; seguir sin + infinitive for negative continuity without duration. This is a
clean, nearly-complete paradigm of "ongoing since X" expressions differentiated by (a) action vs.
state, (b) duration-specified vs. not, and (c) positive vs. negative continuity — worth keeping as a
unit for later mechanics work on how a conlang might similarly fragment a single English concept
("I've been doing X") across multiple grammatically distinct forms.

## Grammar points — Ch.73 Talking about the past — the richest regional-dialect finding in the entire book

Most of this chapter (preterite for completed past events, imperfect for habitual/ongoing past
actions, pluperfect/past-anterior for anteriority, epistemic past-probability via future-
perfect/conditional) duplicates Part A Ch.17 and this file's Ch.48/50 content and is not
re-extracted. **Two subsections are kept in full as the single most valuable dialectal-variation
content in the whole book:**

**73.1.2 "Regional variations" — perfect vs. preterite for recent-past-related-to-present events.**
Standard Peninsular Spanish uses the PERFECT for past events bearing on the present ("Ha bebido
demasiado" = "he's drunk too much," with an implied ongoing consequence). The book then gives a
three-way regional split that is more nuanced than a simple Spain/Latin-America binary: (1) Galicia
and Asturias (regions WITHIN Spain) pattern with Latin America generally in preferring the PRETERITE
instead of the perfect in this exact context ("Bebió demasiado"); (2) Latin America generally also
prefers the preterite here; **but (3) specifically among indigenous populations in Bolivia and Peru,
the PERFECT is reported as MORE common than elsewhere in Latin America — "even in contexts where
most Spanish speakers would use the preterite."** This third data point is the most analytically
interesting: it's an Andean-contact-Spanish feature that runs COUNTER to the general Latin-American
drift toward the preterite, i.e. substrate-language contact (plausibly Quechua/Aymara aspectual
systems, though the book doesn't make this causal claim explicitly) can push a regional Spanish
variety's tense selection in the opposite direction from its surrounding macro-region's general
trend. This is exactly the kind of three-tier (Peninsular-core / Peninsular-regional-minority /
Latin-American-indigenous-contact) dialectal stratification that the slang-synthesis project's
eventual mechanics-analysis phase will want on file. 73.1.3 extends the same core split to ordinary
recent-past narration generally (Peninsular perfect vs. Latin American preterite, "He hablado con
Carlos esta mañana" vs. "Hablé con Carlos esta mañana"), while 73.1.4 notes the Peninsular/Latin-
American split NARROWS or disappears for events explicitly framed as spanning a period including the
present ("Los he visto dos veces esta semana") — the perfect is used "most forms of Latin American
Spanish" in that one sub-case specifically, i.e. the regional-grammar split is itself conditioned by
a finer aspectual distinction, not a blanket regional rule.

**73.3.2 "Latin American usage" — recién + preterite as an immediate-past marker.** Beyond the
Peninsular-shared acabar de + infinitive ("María Luisa acaba de irse"), some Latin American
countries have a parallel grammaticalized construction using recién (ordinarily just "recently")
directly in front of a preterite verb to mean "has only just ___" ("Recién llegó" = "he's only just
arrived," "Recién lo vi" = "I've only just seen it") — a genuine regional grammaticalization of an
adverb into a near-tense-marking role, distinct from its plain adverbial use elsewhere in the
language. En este mismo momento ("just now, this very moment") is a third, register-neutral
immediate-past marker common to spoken Spanish generally.

## Grammar points

### El Voseo — comprehensive per-form paradigm (Chapter 3, running note from p. 37)

The book gives an explicit geographic scope for *voseo* broader/more granular in wording than
`002`'s coverage: used at all social/educational levels in some regions, considered *popular* (i.e.
lower-register) in others, across Argentina, Uruguay, Paraguay, Chile, Bolivia, parts of Peru,
Ecuador, Colombia (excluding the northern coast), parts of Venezuela and Panama, Costa Rica,
Nicaragua, El Salvador, Honduras, Guatemala, the state of Chiapas in Mexico, and a very small area
of Cuba. Rather than devoting one dedicated section to vos morphology, the book folds a vos-form
note into nearly every subsequent tense/mood/command section, giving a genuinely systematic
cross-paradigm picture:

- **Present indicative**: vos takes the *vosotros* ending minus its glide — -ás (-ar), -és (-er),
  -ís (-ir) — e.g. hablás, comprendés/aprendés/comés, vivís/escribís/subís. **No stem change** in
  vos even for stem-changing verbs (entendés, podés) — this is a real point of divergence from the
  tú-form paradigm.
- **haber (present perfect auxiliary)**: vos takes has — same as tú.
- **Present subjunctive**: -és/-ás — e.g. hablés, vendás, tengás, pidás.
- **Reflexive pronoun**: te (same as tú) — e.g. vos te levantás, te ponés.
- **Affirmative command**: formed by dropping the infinitive's -r and adding an accented final
  vowel — -á/-é/-í — e.g. comprá, vendé, viví, tené. (Distinct from both the tú command, which uses
  the third-person-singular indicative form, and the vosotros command, which drops -r and adds -d.)
- No vos exercises are provided anywhere in the book (deliberate authorial choice, stated on p. 37)
  and no vos forms appear at all in the Verb Charts appendix (pp. 202–209) — the paradigm exists
  only as these scattered inline notes.

### Gender-changing nouns and irregular-gender nouns (Chapter 1, pp. 7–8)

A compact set of nouns whose meaning changes with gender (el cura "priest" / la cura "cure"; el
capital "investment" / la capital "capital city"; el orden "arrangement" / la orden "command,
religious order"; el cólera "cholera" / la cólera "anger"; el corte "cut" / la corte "court"; el
coma "coma" / la coma "comma"; el frente "front" / la frente "forehead"; el papa "pope" / la papa
"potato," explicitly marked as the Latin American sense; el policía "police officer" / la policía
"police force"). Separately, la radio/el radio carries an explicit **regional gender split**: la
radio in Spain, Argentina, Bolivia, Chile, Paraguay, and Uruguay, vs. el radio (short for "el
aparato de radio") in other Latin American countries — one of the few cases in this book where an
irregular-gender assignment gets real dialectal grounding rather than being presented as arbitrary.

### Leísmo (Chapter 6, p. 134)

*Le* is sometimes used as a direct object pronoun referring to a masculine person (leísmo), e.g.
"Le vi a Juan." Although accepted by the Real Academia, the book explicitly flags this usage as
discouraged in favor of *lo*, and notes it is **more prevalent in Spain than in Latin America** —
an explicit regional annotation of exactly the kind the extraction spec prioritizes.

### Usted/Ustedes etymology and abbreviations (Chapter 6, p. 130)

*Usted* (*Ustedes*) is a historical contraction of *vuestra merced* ("your grace"), which is why it
governs third-person verb agreement despite meaning "you." Besides the common Ud./Uds.
abbreviations, the book notes V., Vd., VV., Vs., and Vds. as abbreviations one may encounter.

### *Ser* vs. *estar* — Latin etymology framing and meaning-shifting adjectives (Chapter 7)

The book opens the ser/estar chapter (p. 150) with an etymological framing not seen in prior
Spanish extractions: *ser* derives from Latin *esse* (source of English "essence") and expresses
inherent/essential quality; *estar* derives from Latin *stare* (source of English "state") and
expresses temporary state or condition. It then gives a concise table (p. 154) of adjectives whose
English meaning shifts depending on which copula is used:

| Adjective | With *ser* | With *estar* |
|---|---|---|
| aburrido | boring | bored |
| cansado | tiresome | tired |
| divertido | amusing, funny | amused |
| enfermo | sickly | sick |
| listo | sharp, shrewd, clever | ready |
| triste | dull | sad |
| vivo | lively, alert | alive |

Additional notes: *estar* is always used with *muerto* even though death is permanent (it is
conceptually a "state," not an inherent quality); *estar* is always used with *contento* (there was
historically a *ser*-with-*feliz* convention, now obsolete — *ser* and *estar* are used with equal
frequency with *feliz* today); with *soltero*/*casado*/*viuda*, *estar* implies "still" in that
state while *ser* implies membership in that category as an ongoing type.

### Special negative expressions (Chapter 4, p. 126)

Certain time/scope expressions carry an inherently negative meaning without any explicit negative
word: "En mi vida, he oído tal cosa" ("Never in my life have I heard such a thing"), "En toda la
noche, he podido dormir" ("Not all night was I able to sleep"), "En el mundo se encontraría tal
belleza" ("Not in the entire world would one encounter such beauty").

### *Sino* (Chapter 4, p. 126)

*Sino* ("but rather") is used after a negative statement to contradict it: "El no es estúpido, sino
inteligente" ("He isn't dumb, but rather intelligent").

### Subjunctive "subjectivity" framing and the *quiera*-indefinites (Chapter 3)

The book's mnemonic for when to use the subjunctive (p. 88): *"Subjunctive implies subjectivity."*
If there is any possibility the clause's action has not or may not take place, use the subjunctive;
if it is a realized fact, use the indicative. This is presented as simpler than the traditional
"desire/sentiment/volition/demand/doubt/necessity" category list. Separately, the book documents a
productive word-formation pattern: adding *-quiera* to a base word produces an indefinite that
triggers the subjunctive — quienquiera (whoever), cuandoquiera (whenever), dondequiera (wherever),
comoquiera (however), cualquiera (whatever/whichever).

*Ojalá* ("would that," pp. 105–106) is separately noted as a loanword from Arabic that literally
invokes the name of Allah, taking present or imperfect subjunctive. *Quisiera* (imperfect
subjunctive of *querer*) is documented as a politeness form equivalent to English "would like"
rather than "want," used in independent clauses (p. 105).

### Preterite/imperfect: the "stage and scenery" mnemonic and mental-verb meaning shifts (Chapter 3, pp. 75, 77)

Two pedagogical framings worth cross-referencing against other Spanish sources' treatment of the
same distinction: (1) verbs of mental activity (querer, poder, saber, conocer) shift meaning when
used in the preterite rather than the imperfect — María no quiso salir ("Mary *refused* to leave"),
El pudo huir ("He *managed* to escape"), Ellos lo supieron ayer ("They *found out* yesterday"),
Juan conoció a María ("John *met* Mary") — vs. their more literal imperfect senses (wanted, was
able, knew, knew/was acquainted with). (2) For two actions in one sentence, the book frames the
choice as a stage-and-scenery metaphor: whatever is background/scenery takes the imperfect;
whatever action is performed "on stage" by the actors takes the preterite — and the same sentence
can shift meaning entirely depending which verb is cast as scenery vs. action (Un señor **vendió**
el carro y el otro lo **compró** = simple reporting of what happened; Un señor **vendía** el carro y
el otro lo **compraba** = describing the ongoing background scene).

### Chapter 9 idiom catalog — verbs whose sense shifts by preposition or reflexive marking

A concise, systematically organized catalog (pp. 161–170) of common verbs with meaning-changing
constructions, several of which are worth flagging as distinctly useful for slang/register
mechanics work:

- **acabar** (to finish) vs. **acabar de** (to have just — present/imperfect only) vs. **acabar
  por** (to end up doing something).
- **andar** (motion of inanimate objects/animals, or people with no destination implied) vs. **ir**
  (motion toward a specific destination/purpose) vs. **irse** (to go away, usually used alone).
- **cuidar (a/de)** (to care for) vs. **cuidarse** (to take care of oneself) vs. **cuidarse de** (to
  care about/mind).
- **dar** family: dar un paseo (to take a walk), dar a (to face), dar a entender (to lead one to
  understand), dar con (to run into someone unexpectedly), dar de (comer/beber) (to give
  food/drink), dar por (to consider — "me dieron por muerto"), dar las (to strike, of a clock).
- **dejar** (to leave something behind) vs. **dejar de** (to fail to / to stop).
- **The four-way "to become"**: hacerse (through deliberate effort — se hizo abogado), llegar a ser
  (through sustained/great effort — llegó a ser presidente), ponerse (involuntary — se puso roja),
  volverse (a complete/unexpected reversal — se volvió loco).
- **jugar** (to play a game/sport, or to gamble) vs. **tocar** (to play a musical instrument); *jugar
  a* + sport is used in Spain but the *a* is often omitted in Latin America.
- **pensar en** (to be deeply engaged in thinking about) vs. **pensar de** (to think about / opine
  on).
- **poner** (to put/place, or to turn on) vs. **ponerse** (to become, or to put on clothing) vs.
  **ponerse a** (to begin/start).
- **quedar** (to remain/stay), quedar (to be located — ¿Dónde queda la biblioteca?), quedar (to be
  left over), quedarse (to stay/keep/lodge), quedar en (to agree on something).
- **saber** (to know a fact/discipline) vs. **conocer** (to know a person/place/complex thing);
  **saber a** (to taste like).
- **servir para / servir de** (to be of use as / to serve as) vs. **servirse de** (to make use of);
  **sírvase** as a polite imperative ("please").
- **tener** idioms: tener que (to have to), tener que ver (con) (to have to do with), ¿Qué tiene(s)?
  (What's the matter?), Aquí tiene (Here is, when handing something over).
- **volver** (to return from somewhere) vs. **devolver** (to give back) vs. **envolver** (to wrap);
  **volver a** + infinitive (to do something again); **volver en sí** (to come to/regain
  consciousness).

### *Por* and *Para* (Chapter 8) — condensed rule set

*Para* clusters around destination/purpose/limitation: destination (El barco salió para España),
purpose (Esta regalo es para María; Estudia para abogado), time limit/deadline (Lo quiero para
mañana), "in order to" before an infinitive (Necesito gafas para leer), and comparison of inequality
("Para cubano, habla muy bien el inglés" — for a Cuban, he speaks English very well, implying most
Cubans don't). *Por* covers a wider, more heterogeneous set: motion through/along (Viajaron por
España), "in behalf of / instead of" with a genuine ambiguity the book flags explicitly (compré el
regalo **para** María = I'll give it to her; compré el regalo **por** María = on her behalf, she
couldn't go herself), the reason for an errand after ir/mandar/volver/venir (El niño fue por agua),
a period of time (Los árabes estuvieron en España por ocho siglos), indefinite time/place (Estarán
aquí por diciembre), what remains to be done (Queda mucho por hacer), manner/means/motive (Conducía
a la niña por la mano; La carta llegó por correo), exchange (Cambié mi coche por otro nuevo),
opinion/estimation (Lo tomó por intelectual; Pasa por nativo), and measure/rate (Lo venden por
docenas; Vuela a quinientas millas por hora). Two idioms: **estar para** (to be about/ready to) vs.
**estar por** (to be inclined to / in the mood to).

### Adjectives of color as invariable nouns-turned-modifiers (Chapter 2, pp. 23–24)

A distinct subclass of color words (naranja "orange," café "light tan/brown," marrón "chestnut/
brown," rosa "pink," vino "reddish purple," violeta "purple") derive from flower/fruit/mineral nouns
and are **invariable** — they do not agree in gender or number with the noun they modify (pantalones
rosa, not *rosas*), unlike primary color adjectives (blanco, gris, verde, rojo, amarillo, azul),
which do agree normally. The exception is marrón, which sometimes takes a number-agreement plural
(pantalones marrón *or* marrones) even though it never takes gender agreement. Compound color
expressions (azul marino, azul claro, azul oscuro, azul vivo) are likewise fully invariable.

### Compound adverbs with shared *-mente* (Chapter 2, p. 35)

When two `-mente` adverbs are coordinated, `-mente` is attached only to the second: "Lo hace fácil y
convenientemente" (not *fácilmente y convenientemente*).

### Relative pronoun system, full inventory (Chapter 6, pp. 146–149)

Beyond the basic *que*, the book gives a fuller relative-pronoun system than most Spanish
beginner-grammar coverage: *a quien(es)* (replaces *que* when the antecedent is a person functioning
as direct object — *quien* must follow a preposition when referring to a person, while *que* after a
preposition can only refer to things); *el que/la que/los que/las que* (emphatic "the one(s) who," with
matching-tense agreement between the relative clause's *ser* and the main clause's *ser* — El que
llega es mi hermano / El que llegó fue mi hermano); *el cual* etc. as a more oratorical-register
substitute for *el que*, used obligatorily after longer prepositions (alrededor de, tras, hacia,
durante, cerca de, a través de, lejos de); *lo que* (neuter, replacing a general/abstract idea rather
than a specific antecedent); *cuyo* (whose, agrees with the noun it modifies, not with the possessor).

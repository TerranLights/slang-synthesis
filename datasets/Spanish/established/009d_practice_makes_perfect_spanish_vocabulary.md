## Grammar points

### Simple (yes/no) questions

Spanish has no equivalent of English's auxiliary "do/does." A statement becomes a simple yes/no
question just by moving the subject pronoun after the verb (*Ella trabaja* → *¿Trabaja ella?*).
Since the pronoun is already droppable in first/second person (established in Unit 2), it's equally
droppable in the question form (*¿Bailas?*). Written questions are marked by the inverted opening
question mark; in speech, a rising pitch signals the same thing (p. 71).

### Negative responses

A negative answer uses Spanish *no* twice in a row: the first *no* answers "no," the second negates
the verb ("not") — *¿Estudias español? No, no estudio español* "No, I don't study Spanish." This
doubling is structurally required, not emphatic repetition (p. 73).

### Complex (information) questions

Seven interrogative words — *¿Quién?/¿Quiénes?* (who, singular/plural), *¿Qué?* (what),
*¿Cuándo?* (when), *¿Dónde?* (where), *¿Por qué?* (why), *¿Cómo?* (how) — precede a simple-question
structure: **interrogative + verb + subject pronoun (if needed) + rest of sentence**. The accent
mark is obligatory — each word means something else, or serves a different grammatical function,
without it. *¿Quién?*/*¿Quiénes?* break the usual subject-pronoun-after-verb pattern, since the
questioner is precisely asking what that subject is (p. 77).

### Negative questions

Negating a question (simple or complex) just requires placing *no* directly before the conjugated
verb: *¿No lees Shakespeare?* "Don't you read Shakespeare?" ; *¿Por qué no lees Shakespeare?* "Why
don't you read Shakespeare?" The source notes the same pragmatic effect as in English: a negative
question often carries an implicit accusation the positive form doesn't (p. 80).

### Questions of quantity and limitation

A fourth question type uses *¿Cuánto?/¿Cuánta?/¿Cuántos?/¿Cuántas?* ("how much/how many," agreeing
in gender/number with the following noun) or *¿Cuál?/¿Cuáles?* ("which," agreeing only in number).
Pattern: **limiting interrogative + noun + verb + rest of sentence** (*¿Cuánto dinero tienes?*
"How much money do you have?") (p. 82–83).

### The contractions *al* and *del*

Unlike English's large open-ended set of contractions, Spanish has exactly two, both obligatory
(never optional the way "don't" vs. "do not" is): *a* + *el* → *al* ("to/at the") and *de* + *el* →
*del* ("of/from the"). Both arise from **assimilation** — the linguistic tendency to avoid two
adjacent vowel sounds that are awkward to pronounce in sequence (compare English "an apple" vs. "a
apple"). Because Spanish has no apostrophe at all, the contraction is a pure phonological/
orthographic fusion, not a marked omission the way English "don't" marks a dropped "o." Only the
masculine singular article *el* triggers this — it never applies to *la*, *los*, or *las* (p. 85–86).

### Street addresses

The typical Spanish street-address pattern is **building number + en la calle/en el bulevar/etc. +
additional address info**, e.g. *El Teatro Pantages es en 6530 en el bulevar Sunset*. Unlike
English, the words for "street," "avenue," and "boulevard" are not capitalized (p. 87).

### The suffixes *-ero* and *-era*

Building on Unit 2's *-ería* (base noun → name of the shop selling that item), this unit adds the
parallel suffix pair *-ero* (masculine) / *-era* (feminine), attached to the same kind of base noun
to name the *person* (employee) who works with/sells that item: *la joya* "jewel" → *la joyería*
"jewelry store" → *el joyero* "jeweler." Because gender is carried by the suffix itself, Spanish
never needs an awkward compound like "female jeweler" — *la joyera* is simply "jeweler [female]."
Flagged as a second strong candidate mechanism for later slang/word-formation analysis alongside
*-ería*: two parallel, fully regular, semantically transparent derivational suffixes built from the
same base-noun stock, differing only in whether they name the shop or the shopkeeper (p. 89–90).

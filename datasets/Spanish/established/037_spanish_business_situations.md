# Spanish — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Spanish Business Situations (full book)

**Source:** Michael Gorman and María-Luisa Henson, *Spanish Business Situations: A Spoken Language
Guide* (Routledge, London and New York, 1995, 125 pages), full volume — Section I "Por teléfono/On the
telephone" (17 situations), Section II "Cara a cara/Face to face" (14 situations), Section III
(untitled in the extracted TOC but covering budgets, product launches, official agencies, company
policy, banking, sales, contracts, airport reception — 10 situations, situations 32-41).

**Coverage note:** full text layer, cleanly extracted via `pdftotext -layout`. This book presents each
situation as a parallel English/Spanish scripted dialogue, with the authors adding their own
numbered footnotes glossing idioms, register, and — most valuably — explicit **Spain-vs-Latin-America
regional variation** on almost every page. Per copyright discipline, the dialogues themselves are
**paraphrased/summarized rather than reproduced**; only short, individually footnoted idiomatic
phrases are quoted directly (these are the authors' own brief glosses, not bulk dialogue). The
authors' footnotes are the single highest-value content in this book for this project and are
captured as fully as the coverage rule allows.

**Non-redundant-supplement note:** this is spoken-register business Spanish, a different domain from
the 15 pre-existing general Spanish files (001-015). It is also one of three books in this same Wave
S1 dispatch by the same author pair (with "Spanish Business Correspondence" covering the
written-register half, and "Manual of Business Spanish" an omnibus that explicitly incorporates this
book's own Section I/II content) — the orchestrator is handling cross-deduplication of that omnibus
relationship separately; this file extracts the standalone book on its own terms in full.

## Grammar points

### Register in Spanish business dialogue is marked by tone/content, not T-V pronoun switching
A genuinely useful comparative finding from directly contrasting this book's own explicitly-labeled
"informal" job interview (situation 30) against its "formal" job interview (situation 31): **both use
*usted* throughout.** What distinguishes "informal" here is not a shift to *tú*, but relaxed
tone/content — casual introductions of colleagues with light joking ("No la tome demasiado en serio,
Gloria..."), first-name address, and a shorter, less structured exchange — versus the formal
interview's longer, more procedurally structured turn-taking and more guarded phrasing, while
grammatical formality (*usted*) stays constant in both. This suggests that in a Spanish business
register study, "formal vs informal" needs at least two independent axes: the **grammatical** T-V
axis (usted/tú) and a separate **discourse-tone** axis (procedural/guarded vs relaxed/personal) — this
book's own situations keep the first axis fixed while varying the second.

### *vosotros* is explicitly marked as not used in Latin America
A footnote on situation 5 explicitly states of a *vosotros* verb form: "You plural (vosotros); form
not used in Latin America" — confirming the standard pan-Hispanic *vosotros* (Spain) vs. *ustedes*
(Latin America, for plural "you" in all registers) split, called out by the book's own authors rather
than inferred.

### The 24-hour clock in spoken Spanish (Spain)
Situation 12's footnote notes that "the 24-hour clock is used in Spain, even in speech, when
discussing travel arrangements. It is also used in official language" — i.e. this isn't just a
written-schedule convention but extends into ordinary spoken usage for travel contexts specifically,
a usage-domain-conditioned register point.

### Resumptive "lo" doubling back onto a preceding noun
Situation 30's footnote on *"Mi primer trabajo lo hice..."* notes: "Lo refers directly back to
'trabajo'; such repetition by means of the pronoun is characteristic of Spanish usage" — i.e. fronting
a direct object and then still marking it with a resumptive clitic pronoun is idiomatic rather than
redundant in Spanish, distinct from English's intolerance of the equivalent doubling.

### Subjunctive density flagged by the authors themselves in negotiation dialogue
A footnote in Section III (contract-discussion situation) remarks that a given clause is "the first of
numerous examples of the subjunctive in this [situation]" — the authors are explicitly signaling that
negotiation-register Spanish leans more heavily on subjunctive constructions than the more
transactional telephone/travel situations earlier in the book. The underlying subjunctive mechanics
themselves are standard and already covered by this project's general grammar references; the
register-density observation (negotiation talk is subjunctive-dense) is the genuinely new point here.

# Spanish — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Spanish Irregular Verbs Up Close (full book)

**Source:** Eric W. Vogt, Ph.D., *Practice Makes Perfect: Spanish Irregular Verbs Up Close*
(McGraw-Hill, 2011), all 12 chapters (pp. 1–83), Appendix A "TurboVerb" (pp. 85–91), Appendix B
"Survival Verbs" (pp. 93–97). Answer key (pp. 99–127) was reviewed but not separately extracted —
it is exercise-answer content only, restating forms already captured from the chapter bodies and
the two appendices, per the coverage rule's "repeated drill" exclusion.

**Coverage note:** This is a monograph-style irregular-verb-morphology book, not a general grammar
or vocabulary reference — its entire content is the Spanish verb conjugation system. Every grammar
point (one per chapter, 12 chapters) is captured below. The book's own "vocabulary" is functionally
its Appendix B "Survival Verbs" list (a real, if informal, 89-verb glossary with full principal-parts
paradigms) plus a handful of additional verbs named only in Appendix A's "TurboVerb" model-verb
table that don't appear in Appendix B. Exercise sentences and fill-in-the-blank drills throughout
Chapters 1–12 were read in full but not tabulated as vocabulary — they reuse verbs already captured
in the appendices and introduce no new lexical items, per the coverage rule. This source has a
genuine text layer (`pdftotext -layout`, clean, no font-substitution or scrambling issues detected);
no vision-reading was required.

**Relationship to other Spanish `established/` files:** this is the first Spanish reference book
extracted into this project, so there is no prior-file overlap to reconcile. Its scope (irregular
verb morphology specifically) is narrower and more systematic than a general grammar's verb chapter
would be, and later general-grammar extractions should treat this file as the fuller treatment of
Spanish's irregular verb conjugation system.

## Vocabulary

The book's principal-parts method represents each verb by six forms rather than the bare
infinitive: (1) 1st-person singular present indicative, (2) 2nd-person singular present indicative,
(3) infinitive, (4) 1st-person singular preterit indicative, (5) gerund, (6) past/passive participle.
`Term` below is the infinitive (the conventional dictionary citation form); the full six-form
paradigm is preserved in `Notes` because, per the book's central thesis, the bare infinitive alone
does not predict a Spanish verb's irregular forms — the six-form paradigm is the actual unit of
morphological information for irregular verbs.

## Grammar points

### The "principal parts" method and the four verb microsystems (Ch. 1, pp. 1–6)

The book organizes the entire Spanish verb system as four "microsystems" nested inside one
macrosystem: **present** (1st/2nd person singular present indicative), **infinitive** (imperfect,
future, conditional all derive from the bare infinitive), **preterit** (1st-person singular preterit
indicative, from which the imperfect subjunctive is regularly derived), and **participial** (gerund
and past/passive participle, invariable forms used with helping verbs). Rather than teaching from
the infinitive, the method has students memorize six "principal parts" per verb (see Vocabulary
notes above), from which every other conjugated form in any tense/mood/person/number can be
mechanically derived. Six verbs resist the system and must be memorized independently: *dar,
estar, haber, ir, saber, ser* (p. 3).

### Present indicative: six irregularity types (Ch. 2, pp. 7–13)

Regular endings mark 1sg with *-o*, 1pl with *-mos*, 3pl with *-n* across all three conjugations
(-ar/-er/-ir), holding even for irregular verbs. Irregular verbs fall into six types: (1) o→ue
diphthong in a "shoe/boot" pattern (all but nosotros/vosotros), (2) e→ie diphthong (same pattern),
(3) e→i single-vowel change (shoe pattern), (4) consonant change in 1sg only (e.g., c→zc), (5)
consonant change in 1sg plus a diphthong in the rest of the shoe pattern (g-stem + vowel change,
e.g. *tener*, *venir*), (6) consonant change in 1sg plus e→i throughout the shoe pattern (*decir*
and compounds).

### Present subjunctive derives from the yo-form of the indicative (Ch. 3, pp. 15–21)

-ar verbs take *e*-based endings, -er/-ir verbs take *a*-based endings — the reverse of their
indicative theme vowels. Endings themselves are never affected by irregularity; only stems change.
Crucially, irregularities that appear only in select persons in the present indicative (shoe-pattern
gaps, single-vowel-to-single-vowel changes, g-stem/consonant changes) spread to **all six persons**
in the subjunctive. Verbs ending in *-car/-gar/-zar* undergo an orthographic-only change
(*-que/-gue/-ce*) to preserve consonant pronunciation — described as "sounding regular" despite the
spelling change, per Real Academia Española convention.

### Imperatives are (mostly) borrowed subjunctive forms (Ch. 4, pp. 23–29)

All command forms equal the corresponding present subjunctive form **except** affirmative tú and
vosotros commands. Affirmative tú command = 2sg present indicative minus final *-s* (for regular
and vowel-changing verbs) or 1sg present indicative minus *-go* (for g-stem verbs, with *hacer→haz*
the sole listed exception). Affirmative vosotros command = infinitive with final *-r* replaced by
*-d* (a form still current in Spain, rare in the Americas). Object pronouns attach to the end of
affirmative commands (with a written accent added) but precede *no* in negative commands.

### Imperfect indicative: only 3 irregular verbs in the whole language (Ch. 5, pp. 31–35)

*Ser, ir,* and *ver* are the sole irregular verbs in this tense; *ver*'s irregularity is a historical
artifact (formerly spelled *veer*). No verb has stem changes in the imperfect. -ar verbs use
*-aba* endings; -er/-ir verbs share *-ía* endings. Ser/ir's nosotros/vosotros forms are stressed on
the first syllable, unusually. Usage: obligatory for telling time or age in the past, and generally
for ongoing/habitual/background action, contrasted with the preterit for completed events.

### Simple future: one shared ending set, 12 irregular stems (Ch. 6, pp. 37–43)

Unlike every other tense, the future adds one set of endings — historically derived from present
tense forms of *haber* minus initial h- — directly onto the **infinitive** without conjugation-class
distinction. Twelve verbs have irregular stems, in three groups: "new d-stem" verbs inserting *-d-*
before the ending (*poner→pondr-*, *tener→tendr-*, *venir→vendr-*, *salir→saldr-*, *valer→valdr-*),
"collapsed infinitive" -er verbs dropping the theme vowel (*caber→cabr-*, *haber→habr-*,
*poder→podr-*, *querer→querr-*, *saber→sabr-*), and two "totally irregular" (*hacer→har-*,
*decir→dir-*). Special usage: the future expresses present-tense probability/conjecture ("¿Dónde
estará Juan?" = "I wonder where John is").

### Conditional: same stem irregularities as the future (Ch. 7, pp. 45–49)

Formed by adding *-ía*-family endings directly to the infinitive (same one-ending-set-for-all-verbs
pattern as the future), using the identical twelve irregular stems as the future. Functions:
expressing the consequence of a hypothetical/counterfactual (paired with the imperfect subjunctive
in the "if" clause), softening requests, and expressing past-tense probability/conjecture or
"future-in-the-past."

### Preterit indicative: four irregularity patterns (Ch. 8, pp. 51–58)

Regular endings: one set for -ar, one shared set for -er/-ir; 1sg/3sg are stressed on the final
vowel (distinguishing *hablo* "I speak" from *habló* "he/she/you spoke"). Four irregularity groups:
(1) single-vowel-stem irregulars (e→i or o→u, confined to 3rd-person forms only: *pidió/pidieron*,
*durmió/durmieron*), (2) "new-stem" irregulars sharing one unstressed ending set regardless of
conjugation class, subdivided by stem vowel (*-i-*: hice/quise/vine; *-uv-*: anduve/estuve/tuve;
*-u-*: cupe/hube/pude/puse/supe; *-uj-*/"j-stem": conduje/dije/traduje/traje, whose 3pl drops the
expected *-i-*: *dijeron* not *dijieron*), (3) uniquely irregular *ser/ir* (identical forms: fui,
fuiste...) and *dar* (conjugated like a regular -er/-ir verb: di, diste, dio...), and (4) the same
*-car/-gar/-zar* orthographic spelling changes seen in the present subjunctive, but confined to the
1sg only. Semantic note: *querer, poder, saber, conocer* shift meaning in the preterit — "tried,"
"failed," "found out," "met (for the first time)" respectively.

### Imperfect subjunctive derives regularly from the preterit's 3rd-person plural (Ch. 9, pp. 59–65)

Rule: take any verb's preterit 3pl form, strip *-on*, and conjugate from there using *-a-*-family
endings (*fueron→fuera...*) or, in more formal/literary register, *-se-*-family endings
(*hablasen→hablase...*). This derivation is claimed to work with total regularity regardless of how
irregular the preterit stem itself is. A now largely obsolete future subjunctive (formed by
substituting a final *-e* for the imperfect subjunctive's *-a-*) survives mainly in proverbs and
legal boilerplate ("Cuando fueres a Roma..."). The imperfect subjunctive of *deber/querer/poder* also
forms the most polite register of request (*quisiera* > *querría* > *quiero*, in ascending
formality).

### Gerund: forms and functions beyond the progressive (Ch. 10, pp. 67–72)

Regular endings: *-ando* (-ar) and *-iendo* (-er/-ir), with *-i-→-y-* between vowels (*leyendo*).
Irregularities mirror the present system's e→i / o→u single-vowel changes (*pidiendo, durmiendo*)
plus *herir/hervir* (e→ie in present but e→i in gerund). Beyond the *estar* + gerund progressive,
the book documents other progressive-forming helping verbs (*ir, andar, venir, seguir, continuar,
quedar* — the last especially common in Mexican usage, denoting the ongoing outcome of a completed
action) and adjectival/adverbial uses of the gerund (*El niño vino corriendo*). Warns that English
speakers tend to overuse the Spanish progressive relative to native usage, where it carries a more
marked, "vivid"/urgent connotation than the simple present or imperfect.

### The seven perfect tenses and 12 irregular past participles (Ch. 11, pp. 73–78)

Regular past participles: *-ar→-ado*, *-er/-ir→-ido*. Twelve verbs (plus all their compounds) have
irregular past participles: *abrir/abierto, cubrir/cubierto, decir/dicho, escribir/escrito,
hacer/hecho, imprimir/impreso, morir/muerto, poner/puesto, resolver/resuelto, romper/roto,
ver/visto, volver/vuelto*. Each of the seven simple tenses of *haber* pairs with the invariable past
participle to form a corresponding perfect tense. Notes the preterit-based perfect (*hube, hubiste*
+ participle — the "preterit anterior") is rare in speech, largely replaced by the imperfect-based
perfect. The conditional perfect (*habría* + participle) is the required form for the consequence
clause of a past-tense hypothetical, paired with the pluperfect subjunctive in the "if" clause (*Si
Juan hubiera ido a México, habría aprendido...*).

### Passive participles as adjectives; ser/estar with participles (Ch. 12, pp. 79–83)

The past participle doubles as an adjective (with full gender/number agreement) when not paired
with *haber*. Explicitly debunks the common "ser = permanent, estar = temporary" heuristic (*Julio
César está muerto* — death is permanent but still takes *estar*) in favor of a state/condition-change
account: *estar* marks a change of state or condition (etymologically related to English *state*),
while *ser* marks inherent classification. Some adjectives shift meaning by copula choice
(*cansado*: *estar cansado* "tired" vs. *ser cansado* "boring"; similarly *casado*). *Ser* + past
participle forms the true passive voice (*Las ventanas fueron rotas por los niños*), which the book
notes is less frequent in actual Spanish usage than either the active voice or the impersonal *se*
construction (*Se rompieron las ventanas*).

### Appendix B — Survival verbs (pp. 93–97)

An alphabetized (by infinitive) list of ~89 common verbs, each given in the six-principal-parts
format, oriented toward business/school/office vocabulary. The book explicitly flags this as a
"first-level" gloss list only (e.g. *manejar* glossed as "manage" but also meaning "to drive" or, in
retail contexts, "to carry [stock]"), and deliberately contrasts the reflexive *sentarse* ("to sit")
against the non-reflexive *sentir* ("to feel/regret") since students commonly confuse the two.

## Grammar points

### Present perfect generalized across more verb types, including irregular participles (Lecciones 25–27)
Building on Lección 24's introduction, this batch extends the present perfect (haber + past
participle) to -er/-ir verbs with irregular participles: abrir → abierto, ver → visto, romper →
roto — none built regularly from the infinitive (which would give *abrido, *veido, *rompido). These
are drilled directly against regular participles (comido, decidido, enviado) so a learner can
observe which verbs are exceptions.

### Present progressive (estar + gerund) with object-pronoun cliticization (Lección 26)
"Estoy/Estás/Está + -ando/-iendo" is drilled as the ongoing-action form (estoy cenando, estoy
comiendo, estás manejando), directly parallel to English "I am eating" but used more narrowly in
Spanish for actions genuinely in progress at the moment of speaking (contrasted against the
present-tense verbs used elsewhere in the course for habitual/general statements). Object pronouns
attach either before the conjugated "estar" ("la estoy comiendo") or after the gerund as a suffix
("comiéndola" — though this batch drills only the pre-verbal placement) — the course demonstrates
the pre-verbal option exclusively but the choice itself is a genuine feature of Spanish syntax.

### Direct object pronouns (lo/la/los/las) (Lección 26)
A full paradigm drilled with explicit gender/number matching to the noun being replaced — "el horno"
→ "lo," "la nevera" → "la," "los electrodomésticos" → "los," "las neveras" → "las." The course
calls out the la-for-feminine-singular-nouns point directly, since a beginner might otherwise
default to "lo" as a generic "it."

### Indirect object pronouns (me/te/le/nos/les), explicitly contrasted against direct object pronouns (Lección 28)
The course opens by stating explicitly that the prior two lessons covered direct object pronouns and
this one covers indirect ones — a genuinely unusual moment of the course naming its own grammatical
scope directly. Indirect object pronouns mark the recipient of an action (le envío la comida = "I
send him/her the food") as distinct from the direct object marking what is acted upon (la envío =
"I send it"). "Le"/"les" are shown to be ambiguous across him/her/formal-you/them, resolved with an
optional "a él"/"a ella"/"a ellos"/"a usted" for clarity, paralleling the su/suyo ambiguity from
Lecciones 3–4.

### Double object pronouns and the le/les → se substitution rule (Lección 29)
When both a direct and an indirect object pronoun appear on the same verb, the indirect object comes
first (me lo, te la, nos los), and — critically — a 3rd-person indirect object pronoun (le/les)
obligatorily becomes "se" whenever it precedes a 3rd-person direct object pronoun (lo/la/los/las):
"se las compras" (you buy them for her), never the ungrammatical *le las compras. The course
demonstrates this rule purely through repeated correct drilling rather than naming it, but the
pattern is unmistakable across dozens of paired examples in this lesson (se lo, se la, se los, se
las all appear, always replacing an underlying le/les). Object pronoun pairs also attach to
infinitives as a single unit (limpiártelo, arreglárselo, comprármelos), with a written accent added
to preserve the original stressed syllable — the course doesn't name the orthographic accent rule
but every example demonstrates it correctly.

### tener que + infinitive ("have to") across three tenses, combined with double object pronouns (Lección 30)
"Tener que" + infinitive expresses obligation, drilled across present (tengo que), preterite (tuve
que/tuviste que/tuvimos que/tuvieron que), and imperfect (tenía que) — and combined productively
with the double-object-pronoun-plus-infinitive constructions from Lecciones 28–29, producing
complex forms like "tengo que lavárselo" (I have to wash it for him) and "no tuviste que
lavárselo ayer" (you didn't have to wash it for him yesterday) that stack three separate grammar
points (modal + tense + double clitic pronouns) into a single utterance.

### ir a + infinitive (near/periphrastic future), with an explicit cross-linguistic frequency note (Lección 31)
"Voy a/vas a/va a/vamos a/van a" + infinitive expresses near-future or intention, directly parallel
to English "going to." The course adds a genuinely valuable sociolinguistic observation not seen
elsewhere in this set: this "substitute future tense" is used LESS frequently in Spanish than its
English analog is in English — for an imminent departure, a Spanish speaker is more likely to say
"Me voy" (lit. "I leave/go," present tense) than "Voy a irme" (I'm going to leave), where English
would default to "I'm leaving"/"I'm going to leave" about equally. This is a register/frequency
observation about a construction's real distribution, not a grammatical rule per se, but genuinely
useful for downstream naturalness judgments.

### Comparative constructions ("más...que") with full possessive-pronoun agreement (Lección 32)
"[Noun] es/son más [adjective] que [noun/pronoun]" is the course's final grammar point, drilled
extensively with a full body-parts vocabulary set and grande/pequeño/largo/corto. When the second
term of comparison is a possessive rather than a full noun phrase, the possessive PRONOUN (not
adjective) is used with full gender/number agreement to the body part being compared — "mis brazos
son más grandes que los tuyos" (my arms are bigger than yours), reinforcing the mío/tuyo/suyo/
nuestro possessive-pronoun paradigm from Lecciones 3–4 in a new comparative context, effectively
bringing the course full circle to one of its very first grammar points.

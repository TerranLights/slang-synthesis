# Spanish — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Practice Makes Perfect: Basic Spanish (complete book, Lessons 1–50)

**Source:** Dorothy Richmond, *Practice Makes Perfect: Basic Spanish* (McGraw-Hill, 2009), ISBN
978-0-07-164223-1, pp. 1–266 (all 50 numbered lessons across the book's 10 parts, plus their 10
`cumulativo` review sections).

**Coverage note:** This is a beginner workbook ("basic grammar reinforcement," per its own preface)
covering the standard first-year Spanish curriculum: articles, present-tense regular/irregular/
stem-changing verb conjugation, pronouns, ser/estar, basic sentence structure, and thematic
vocabulary lists. Every grammar point and every distinct vocabulary item from all 50 lessons is
captured below, plus the `nota buena` usage notes the source itself flags as noteworthy. **Skipped
as redundant drill material** (per the extraction spec's coverage rule): the book's ~500 individual
`EJERCICIO` translation/true-false/matching drill items (these recombine already-captured
vocabulary and grammar without introducing anything new), the ten `cumulativo` review sections'
own exercises (pure review, no new vocabulary/grammar), the "Hechos divertidos" (fun facts) cultural
trivia boxes and the biographical reading passages (Elvis Presley, Socrates, Bob Dylan, Ayn Rand,
the Marx Brothers, Benjamin Franklin, Alcatraz, Cinderella) — these are comprehension exercises
built from vocabulary already tabulated, not new lexical/grammatical content — and the book's
back-matter (cumulative paragraphs, answer key, English-Spanish glossary, index), which is a
synthesis/reference restatement of material already extracted here, not new content. This source
has a genuine, clean PDF text layer throughout (`pdftotext -layout`); no vision-reading or
font-cipher decoding was needed, and no marginalia (handwritten or otherwise) was found anywhere in
the file.

**Whole-book source attributes** (apply to every row below unless otherwise noted): Source Type =
`grammar_reference`; Attested Era = "contemporary (source published 2009)"; Transcription
Confidence = `n/a`; Vision Reading Confidence = `n/a` (real text layer). This book marks **no**
explicit dialectal/regional variants anywhere (no B/C/S-style tags, no "Spain vs. Mexico" labels on
individual vocabulary) — its author is American and its vocabulary is presented as unmarked,
general "Spanish," so **Attested Region and Geographic Scope are `—` throughout**, per the spec's
rule against inferring regionality from a book's own authorship. The one partial exception is the
book's explicit note that it teaches **tú/vosotros as the assumed informal "you"** register
throughout (stated in the preface) — this is a register/formality choice, not a national-dialect
tag, and is noted under the relevant grammar points (subject pronouns, lección 5) rather than
per-vocabulary-row.

## Grammar points

### Definite articles (lección 1, p. 3)
Four forms: `el`/`los` (masculine sing./pl.), `la`/`las` (feminine sing./pl.). The source flags that
Spanish uses the definite article far more often than English, citing *Jorge lava los platos* ("George
washes dishes" — no "the" in the English gloss, but the article is obligatory in Spanish before a
generic plural direct object).

### Indefinite articles (lección 2, p. 6)
Four forms: `un`/`unos` (masculine), `una`/`unas` (feminine); `unos`/`unas` function as "some."

### Yo tengo / yo quiero (lección 3, p. 9)
Fixed first-person forms taught before full conjugation is introduced: *yo tengo* ("I have"), *yo no
tengo*, *yo quiero* ("I want"), *yo no quiero* — a scaffolding device before lección 15 (tener) and
lección 19 (querer) give the full paradigms.

### Hay (lección 4, p. 12)
Invariant existential "there is/there are," identical in singular and plural (*hay un perro* / *hay dos
perros*). Questioned/negated by simply adding ¿...? or `no` (*no hay*, *¿hay...?*). Nota buena: *hay*
is pronounced "eye" (silent h, ay as in English "eye").

### Subject pronouns and regular -ar conjugation (lección 5, pp. 15–17)
Full subject pronoun table (yo/tú/él/ella/usted/nosotros(as)/vosotros(as)/ellos/ellas/ustedes) and
the first regular conjugation paradigm (hablar: hablo/hablas/habla/hablamos/habláis/hablan). The
book's own preface states it assumes the informal tú/vosotros as its default "you"/"you all"
throughout its own exercises — a register choice, not a claim about Spanish generally. Nota buena:
Monday (not Sunday) is treated as the first day of the week on the Spanish calendar, and day names
are not capitalized.

### Regular -ar verb endings (lección 6, p. 29)
Systematic ending table: yo -o, tú -as, él/ella/usted -a, nosotros/as -amos, vosotros/as -áis,
ellos/ellas/ustedes -an — generalizes the lección 5 paradigm to any regular -ar verb.

### Regular -er conjugation (lección 7, p. 31) and -er verb endings (lección 8, p. 34)
Paradigms for comer/beber, then the generalized ending table: -o/-es/-e/-emos/-éis/-en.

### Forms of adjectives: two-form vs. four-form (lección 9, p. 37)
Adjectives ending in a consonant or in -e (e.g. *azul*, *verde*) are "two-form" — they inflect only for
number (singular/plural), not gender. Adjectives ending in -o (e.g. *negro*, *rojo*) are "four-form" —
they inflect for both number and gender (negro/negros/negra/negras). Nota buena states this
generalization explicitly.

### Expressing "I like" (lección 9, p. 38)
Fixed first-person phrase taught before the full gustar paradigm: *me gusta* / *no me gusta* + a
singular noun. Full conjugation (all persons, singular and plural noun agreement) is deferred to
lección 49.

### Expressing "more than" (lección 10, p. 42)
Regular comparative pattern: *más* + adjective + *que* ("more ... than"). Full comparative/superlative
system (including irregulars mejor/peor/mayor/menor) is deferred to lección 29.

### Regular -ir conjugation (lección 11, p. 51)
Paradigms for escribir/vivir, following the same -o/-es(-ir version: -es)/-e/-imos/-ís/-en pattern
family (escribo/escribes/escribe/escribimos/escribís/escriben).

### Asking simple and complex questions (lección 12, pp. 54–55)
Simple yes/no questions are formed purely by intonation/punctuation with no word-order change
(*¿Estudia él?* "Does he study?"). Complex (information) questions front the interrogative word:
interrogative + verb + subject (*¿Dónde vive él?* "Where does he live?"). Nota buena states this
word-order pattern explicitly.

### Ser (lección 13, p. 57)
Full irregular paradigm: soy/eres/es/somos/sois/son. Used for professions in this lesson (the
indefinite article a/an is not translated before an unmodified profession: *Soy dentista*, "I am a
dentist" — flagged in an exercise instruction, not a nota buena, but stated explicitly).

### Possessive adjectives (lección 14, p. 60)
Full paradigm: mi(s)/tu(s)/su(s) (invariant for the possessor's gender)/nuestro(a)(s)/vuestro(a)(s)/
su(s). *Su* is ambiguous (his/her/your-formal/their) — resolved by context.

### Using ser: six categories (lección 14, p. 60)
The source tabulates six distinct uses of ser: clock time, day of the week, date, profession,
relationship, and nationality — establishing the ser/estar contrast that recurs through lección 18.

### Tener (lección 15, p. 63)
Full irregular paradigm: tengo/tienes/tiene/tenemos/tenéis/tienen.

### Estar (lección 16, p. 73)
Full irregular paradigm: estoy/estás/está/estamos/estáis/están, introduced specifically for
location statements, then extended to moods (lección 17) and physical condition (lección 18) — the
source's three-part breakdown of estar's uses (location / mood / physical condition) is summarized
explicitly on p. 79.

### Querer (lección 19, p. 82)
e→ie stem-changing paradigm: quiero/quieres/quiere/queremos/queréis/quieren. Also introduces
*querer* + infinitive ("to want to do something").

### Numbers 1–100 and expressing age (lección 20, pp. 88–89)
Full numeral table 1–40 individually, then decades 50–100. Age is expressed with *tener* + number +
*años* ("to be ... years old," literally "to have ... years"); nota buena flags that English "old" (from
"years old") is not translated.

### Idioms with tener (lección 20, p. 89)
A closed set of tener-idioms expressing states normally rendered with "to be X" in English: tener
razón/hambre/sed/suerte/prisa/calor/frío/miedo (de)/orgullo (de)/sueño — literally "to have
reason/hunger/thirst/luck/haste/warmth/coldness/fear/pride/sleepiness."

### Using tener que (lección 20, p. 90)
*tener que* + infinitive = "to have to" (do something).

### Hacer (lección 21, p. 99)
Full irregular paradigm: hago/haces/hace/hacemos/hacéis/hacen, used both for the generic "to
do/make" and for weather idioms (*hace calor*, *hace frío*, etc. — literally "it makes heat/cold").

### Telling time (lección 22, pp. 102–104)
*Es la una* (singular) vs. *Son las dos/tres/...* (plural) for the hour; *de la mañana/tarde/noche* for
AM/PM disambiguation; a full "past/till" system (*y cinco* ... *y media* ... *menos veinticinco* ... *menos
cinco*) for minutes.

### Jugar (lección 23, p. 107)
u→ue stem-changing paradigm: juego/juegas/juega/jugamos/jugáis/juegan — the only u-stem verb
of this type presented in the book.

### Ir and the contractions al/del (lección 24, p. 110)
Full irregular paradigm: voy/vas/va/vamos/vais/van. Two obligatory contractions are introduced:
*a* + *el* → *al* ("to the"), *de* + *el* → *del* ("from/of the") — Spanish never allows *a el* or *de el* to
stand uncontracted.

### The five sensing verbs (lección 25, p. 113)
oír, oler, probar, tocar, ver are grouped as the core "sense" verbs; of the five, only *tocar* is fully
regular — the exercise itself asks the learner to identify this.

### Poder (lección 26, p. 125)
o→ue stem-changing paradigm: puedo/puedes/puede/podemos/podéis/pueden ("to be able to,
can").

### Forming adverbs (lección 26, p. 126)
Rule: take an adjective; if it ends in -o, change to -a and add *-mente*; otherwise add *-mente*
directly (e.g. *lento* → *lenta* → *lentamente*; *intenso* → *intensa* → *intensamente*). Nota buena
flags *bien* ("well") as an irregular, frequently used exception that does not follow this pattern.

### Two-verb constructions (lección 27, p. 128)
A closed list of verbs that take a following infinitive to form a compound predicate: aprender a +
inf., deber + inf., esperar + inf., necesitar + inf., poder + inf., querer + inf., tener que + inf., tratar de
+ inf. Nota buena notes that poder, querer, and tener (que) were already conjugated in prior
lessons; the rest (aprender, deber, esperar, necesitar, tratar de) are regular.

### Two-verb questions (lección 28, p. 131)
When a sentence already has a two-verb construction, forming a question follows the same
no-inversion-needed pattern as lección 12 (*Ella debe estudiar* → *¿Debe ella estudiar?*).

### Comparisons: regular and irregular (lección 29, p. 134)
Regular: *más*/*menos* + adjective + *que*. Irregular comparative pairs (do not use más/menos):
*mejor que* (better than), *peor que* (worse than), *mayor que* (older than), *menor que* (younger
than).

### Preposition + verb (lección 30, p. 138)
Rule, stated as a nota buena: when a verb directly follows a preposition, the verb must remain in
the infinitive form — Spanish never uses a gerund here the way English uses "-ing" (*después de
comer*, "after eating," not *después de comiendo*).

### Demonstrative adjectives (lección 31, p. 149)
Two full paradigms: este/esta/estos/estas ("this/these") and ese/esa/esos/esas ("that/those").

### -Ar verbs with o→ue stem change (lección 31–32, pp. 150, 152)
A closed list (almorzar, aprobar, colgar, contar, costar, demostrar, encontrar, mostrar, probar,
recordar, rogar, sonar, soñar, tostar, volar) with the full conjugation paradigm shown for *contar*
(cuento/cuentas/cuenta/contamos/contáis/cuentan) — the stem change affects all forms except
nosotros/vosotros.

### The unwritten subject pronoun "it" (lección 32, p. 153)
Nota buena: when "it" is the grammatical subject of a sentence (e.g., "It is a book"), Spanish does
not translate "it" at all — the sentence simply begins with the verb (*Es un libro*).

### -Er and -ir verbs with o→ue stem change (lección 33, p. 155)
Paradigms for volver (-er) and dormir (-ir), plus a closed vocabulary list of others following the
same pattern (devolver, envolver, morder, mover, poder, resolver / morir).

### Verbs with e→ie stem change (lección 34, p. 159)
Paradigms for cerrar (-ar), entender (-er), and preferir (-ir), covering all three conjugation classes
under this stem-change pattern, plus a closed vocabulary list (comenzar, confesar, empezar,
fregar, pensar / encender, perder / hervir, mentir).

### Double negatives (lección 35, p. 163)
Two patterns: (1) with *nada* — subject + *no* + verb + *nada* ("He doesn't know anything," literally
"He not knows nothing"); (2) with *nunca* — either subject + *no* + verb + *nunca*, or subject +
*nunca* + verb (both grammatical, e.g. *Él no entiende nunca* / *Él nunca entiende*, "He never
understands"). Unlike English, the double negative is obligatory and standard, not a solecism.

### Direct object pronouns (lección 36, p. 175)
Full set: me/te/lo/la (singular) and nos/os/los/las (plural) — precede the conjugated verb.

### Saber (lección 37, p. 178)
Irregular paradigm: sé/sabes/sabe/sabemos/sabéis/saben — used for factual/informational
knowledge, and (lección 37, p. 179) *saber* + infinitive = "to know how to" do something.

### Conocer and the personal a (lección 38, pp. 181)
Conocer (irregular only in yo: conozco/conoces/conoce/conocemos/conocéis/conocen) expresses
familiarity with a person, place, thing, or idea, or "to meet." The personal *a* (nota buena, p. 181)
must precede a direct object that is the name or mention of a person or a pet, but is omitted before
a thing or a place (*Veo a mi abuelo* vs. *Veo la silla*).

### Saber vs. conocer (lección 39, p. 184)
Contrastive summary table: saber for information and for "know how to" (+ infinitive); conocer for
people, places, and "to meet" someone. This is the classic saber/conocer distinction that recurs
across Romance-language pedagogy generally, not unique to this book, but the source's own
five-row contrast table is the concrete artifact captured here.

### Possession with de (lección 40, p. 187)
Because Spanish has no possessive 's, possession is expressed as owned-object + *de* + owner
("Mary's book" → *el libro de María*). Nota buena reminds that *de* + *el* contracts to *del* (lección
24's contraction rule reapplied).

### Reflexive pronouns and reflexive verbs (lección 41, p. 199)
Full reflexive pronoun set: me/te/se/nos/os/se, demonstrated with the paradigm verb *verse* ("to
see oneself": me veo/te ves/se ve/nos vemos/os veis/se ven). A large closed list of common
reflexive verbs is given (acostarse, afeitarse, bañarse, casarse, cepillarse, despertarse, dormirse,
ducharse, lavarse, levantarse, llamarse, maquillarse, mirarse, peinarse, secarse, sentarse,
sentirse).

### E→i stem-changing verbs (lección 43, p. 205)
Paradigm for *pedir* (pido/pides/pide/pedimos/pedís/piden), plus a closed vocabulary list
(competir, despedir, freír, gemir, impedir, medir, reír, repetir, servir) — all -ir verbs.

### Irregular yo forms of e→i verbs (lección 44, p. 208)
A subset of e→i verbs (bendecir, conseguir, corregir, decir, elegir, maldecir, seguir) has an
additionally irregular yo form beyond the ordinary stem change (bendigo, consigo, corrijo, digo,
elijo, maldigo, sigo) — tabulated explicitly against the regular e→i pattern.

### Ponerse and quitarse (lección 44, p. 209)
Reflexive verb pair for "to put on" / "to take off" (clothing): me pongo/te pones/se pone... and me
quito/te quitas/se quita...

### Indirect object pronouns and dar (lección 45, pp. 211)
Full indirect object pronoun set: me/te/le/nos/os/les. Paired with the irregular verb *dar* ("to
give": doy/das/da/damos/dais/dan) as the primary vehicle for practicing indirect objects (the book
distinguishes direct vs. indirect object identification as an exercise in itself, p. 211).

### Para: six uses (lección 46, p. 223)
Destination, time limit/deadline, purpose before an infinitive, personal standard/opinion,
expressing a reason ("in order to"), and comparison to a standard — six distinct uses tabulated
explicitly by the source, each with its own worked example.

### Pronouns after prepositions (lección 46, p. 223)
After a preposition, yo/tú are replaced by mí/ti (*para mí*, "for me"); all other subject pronouns are
unchanged (él, ella, usted, nosotros/as, vosotros/as, ellos, ellas, ustedes).

### Por: six uses (lección 47, p. 226)
Duration of time, periods of time in a day, transportation, movement-through, exchange/
substitution/payment, thanks/emotion/gratitude, and motivation/reason — again tabulated
explicitly, directly paired against para's six uses in lección 48.

### Translating para and por (lección 48, p. 229)
Explicit contrastive summary: both can translate English "for," but they are not interchangeable —
para covers destination/purpose/deadline/standard senses; por covers
duration/exchange/movement/cause senses. This is the book's capstone treatment of the
notoriously difficult para/por distinction for English-speaking learners.

### Gustar (lección 49, p. 232)
Grammatically inverted construction: the liked thing is the subject and determines the verb's
number (*me gusta* for a singular liked item, *me gustan* for plural), while the person who likes is
marked by an indirect object pronoun (me/te/le/nos/os/les), optionally reinforced by *a mí, a ti, a
él...* for clarity or emphasis.

### Verbs like gustar (lección 49, p. 234)
A closed set of verbs sharing gustar's inverted subject/indirect-object structure: bastar, disgustar,
doler (o→ue), encantar, faltar, importar, interesar, molestar, parecer, sobrar — each given both a
literal gloss and its natural idiomatic English equivalent (e.g. *doler* = "to be painful (to/for
someone)," idiomatically "to hurt").

### Ir + a + infinitive (lección 50, p. 237)
The periphrastic ("informal") future: *ir* (conjugated) + *a* + infinitive = "to be going to" do
something (*Voy a nadar mañana*, "I am going to swim tomorrow"). The source notes this is the
functional future tense taught in this beginner-level book; true synthetic future/conditional/
preterite/imperfect tenses are explicitly deferred to a follow-on book (stated in lección 50's own
closing exercise, p. 239: "Voy a estudiar los tiempos de los verbos... en otro libro después de
terminar este libro").

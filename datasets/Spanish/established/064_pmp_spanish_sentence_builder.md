# Spanish — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Practice Makes Perfect Spanish Sentence Builder (non-redundant supplement)

**Source:** Gilda Nissenberg, *Practice Makes Perfect: Spanish Sentence Builder*, McGraw-Hill, 1st
edition. 224 PDF pages. `pdfinfo`/`pdftotext` reported cosmetic "Dictionary key must be a name
object" syntax-error warnings on stderr throughout, but these did not affect the actual text
extraction — every sampled page (including the chapters used below) produced clean, complete,
directly usable text. No vision-reading needed.

## Non-redundant-supplement scope decision

20 chapters, mostly composition-skill-building (declarative/interrogative/exclamatory sentences,
imperatives, coordinating/subordinating conjunctions, relative pronouns, pronouns, adjectives,
adverbs, infinitives, comparisons/contrasts/antonyms, the passive voice, the subjunctive mood x2,
punctuation/spelling, letter writing, a final "Let's write!" composition chapter).

**Skipped as redundant:** sentence-structure/word-order mechanics (Ch. 1-5), conjunctions and
relative pronouns (Ch. 6-8), pronoun/adjective/adverb/infinitive core grammar (Ch. 9-12) — all
fully covered elsewhere in this corpus (006/014/015/016/017/019, etc.). Comparative/superlative
adjective *mechanics* (Ch. 14) — fully redundant with 006/016/018. The passive voice (Ch. 15) and
the subjunctive mood chapters (Ch. 16-17) — fully redundant with 006/014/015/017/019 and this wave's
own 062. Punctuation/spelling (Ch. 18) — a standard period/comma/abbreviation inventory
(a.C./d.C., EE.UU., JJ.OO., FUNDÉU, etc.) with nothing beyond what 018's appendices already cover in
more depth. Letter writing and "Let's write!" (Ch. 19-20) — formal-correspondence salutations/
closings already covered by 036 (*Spanish Business Correspondence*) and 035 (*Manual de Español
Comercial*).

**What was kept and why — this book's one standout chapter:** **Chapter 13, "Idioms and special
phrases" (pp. 115-129), is a genuinely rich, well-organized idiom/proverb/colloquialism catalog** —
by far the most valuable content in the book, and arguably comparable in density to this corpus's
dedicated idiom books (045 *Big Red Book of Spanish Idioms*, 046 *Hablar por los Codos*). Given
those two books already exist in this corpus, **some overlap with 045/046 is likely but not
individually re-verified item-by-item here** (that would require a full re-read of both, out of
scope for this dispatch) — flagged explicitly rather than silently assumed either way. The chapter
has four distinct sub-catalogs, captured below: (1) light-verb idiom families built on dar/hacer/
perder/poner/tener + noun, (2) a themed refranes (proverb) list, (3) a themed colloquial-phrases
list with the book's own explicit meta-commentary on jargon/slang/regional-variation/offensiveness
caution, and (4) a compact personality/physical-description adjective-antonym list from Ch. 14
(kept brief since this tier of vocabulary is core/basic and likely substantially covered by this
corpus's general dictionaries and vocabulary books already).

## Grammar points

### Idiomatic expressions: the book's own three-part functional taxonomy (Ch. 13 intro)

The book frames idioms as serving three distinct communicative functions, worth preserving as a
framing device: (1) supplying an "accurate equivalent" for a concept with no literal translation
(no soportar = "cannot stand," not a literal "not support"); (2) clarifying meaning more vividly
than a literal paraphrase would (guardar cama vs. just "estar enfermo"); (3) adding emphasis/further
explanation beyond the bare fact (pasar la noche en blanco emphasizes total sleeplessness more
vividly than "no durmieron").

### Light-verb + noun idiom families as a productive derivational pattern

Distinct from a flat idiom list, Chapter 13 organizes a large share of its idioms into five
light-verb families (dar, hacer, perder, poner, tener + a following noun), each family sharing a
single light verb whose meaning is supplied entirely by the noun that follows. The **dar + emotion
noun + a + experiencer** sub-pattern (dar miedo a, dar risa a, dar asco a, etc.) is the most
systematic and productive of these — effectively a causative-emotion predicate template that can
in principle take almost any emotion/sensation noun, not a closed lexical list. This is directly
relevant to modeling how a derived conlang might generate novel causative-emotion slang via a single
light verb + an open noun class, rather than coining a new verb per emotion.

### Explicit sociolinguistic caution on colloquial phrases, jargon, and regional variation (Ch. 13, "Colloquial phrases" intro)

The book includes a notable piece of **direct authorial meta-commentary** worth quoting as framing
for this whole project's approach to slang: "Some phrases are hard to understand if they are part of
the jargon or slang of a particular group or consist of a combination of nonstandard vocabulary
elements. Remember that the Spanish-speaking world is very large. As a learner, it's best to avoid
certain expressions, especially if they convey offensive connotations that might be interpreted as
demeaning. A given word or a phrase may be inappropriate in some areas while being completely
acceptable in others." This is a pedagogical-grammar author independently arriving at the same
region-sensitive, confidence-flagged caution this project's own `00_Source_Reliability_Guide.md` and
historical/geographic-context conventions already enforce — useful corroborating evidence that
treating regional slang variation and taboo-potential as a first-class concern (not an afterthought)
is the right default, not an idiosyncratic choice of this project alone.

### Idiom aging and register-mixing noted explicitly by the source

Two additional observations the book makes explicitly, both relevant to how this corpus should think
about "attested era" for idiomatic material: (1) idioms and proverbs can fall out of fashion
("Cada oveja con su pareja" is given two possible English equivalents, with a note that the second
is "more current" — implying the Spanish original itself may be dated relative to contemporary
usage, not just its translation); (2) a nominally "colloquial" phrase can appear in formal register
when it communicates an idea precisely (the book's own example of a political leader using "En
tiempo de tribulaciones no hacer mudanzas" in a formal statement) — register and colloquial-vs-formal
classification is a matter of degree/context, not an immutable property of a given phrase.

### Personality/physical-description adjective antonyms (Ch. 14, compact note)

Ch. 14 supplies roughly 40 adjective-antonym pairs covering personality traits (ambicioso/generoso,
arrogante/humilde, egoísta/solidario, perezoso/industrioso, tacaño/generoso, etc.) and physical
description (alto/bajo, delgado/grueso, guapo/feo, etc.). Not reproduced in full in the vocabulary
table above since this tier of vocabulary is core/basic and very likely substantially redundant with
this corpus's general-purpose dictionaries (047, 050, 052) and vocabulary books (009 series) — flagged
here as existing and available if a future pass specifically needs a compact antonym-pair resource,
rather than silently dropped.

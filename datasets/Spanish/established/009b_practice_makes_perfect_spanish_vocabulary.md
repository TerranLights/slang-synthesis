# Spanish — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Unit 1, "The basics"

**Source:** Dorothy Richmond, *Practice Makes Perfect: Spanish Vocabulary* (McGraw-Hill, 2007),
pp. 18–40 (Unit 1, "The basics").

**Coverage note:** Second of the 11-file set (`009`–`009j`) extracting this book; see `009`'s header
note for the overall scope/methodology (clean text layer, no vision-reading, `-layout` cross-checked
against non-`-layout` extraction — no scrambling found in this unit's vocabulary boxes). All
vocabulary-list items are captured; drill exercises, match-the-column quizzes, and the two narrative
reading passages ("La historia de un oso muy popular," "La reina de la cocina") are skipped per the
coverage rule since they recombine already-listed vocabulary rather than introducing new terms
(their handful of glossed marginal words — *la Inglaterra*, *que*, *se llama*, *son*, *su(s)*,
*también*, *la comida*, *otro/otra*, *porque*, *sin duda*, *tiene*, *tipo*, *todo* — are folded into
the table below since they are genuinely new). Source Type: `grammar_reference` throughout.
Attested Era: "contemporary (source published 2007)" throughout. Attested Region/Geographic Scope:
`—` except one explicit note (*ustedes* replacing *vosotros* in Latin American usage), which is
flagged in Notes rather than given its own region column value since the book states it as a
usage note, not a formal dialect tag.

## Grammar points

### Definite articles

Four definite articles (*el*, *la*, *los*, *las*) all translate as "the," selected by the noun's
gender (masculine/feminine) and number (singular/plural): *el chico/la chica* "the boy/girl,"
*los chicos/las chicas* "the boys/girls" (p. 19).

### Indefinite articles

Four indefinite articles (*un*, *una*, *unos*, *unas*) cover English "a(n)" and "some": *un gato*
"a male cat," *unos gatos* "some male cats" (p. 22).

### *Yo tengo..., yo quiero...*

Two high-frequency present-tense verb frames for building beginner sentences: *tengo* "I have" and
*quiero* "I want," negated with *no* directly before the verb (*Yo no tengo un garaje* "I don't have
a garage"), questioned with *¿Tienes tú...?* / *¿Quieres tú...?* (p. 24–25).

### *Está*, the "is" of location

*Está* is introduced narrowly as the locative sense of "is" (full *ser*/*estar* treatment deferred
to Unit 5). The accent is meaning-bearing: *esta* (no accent) means "this," *está* (accented) means
"is (located)" — *Mi vaca está en el garaje* "My cow is in the garage" (p. 28).

### The use of *hay*

*Hay* ("there is/are") is invariant for number and doesn't require gender/number agreement the way
*está* does: *Hay un perro en el sótano* "There is a dog in the basement," *Hay tres dormitorios*
"There are three bedrooms," negated as *no hay*, questioned as *¿Hay...?* (p. 30).

### The diminutive suffixes -ito and -ita

Adding *-ito*/*-ita* (matching the base noun's gender) signals smaller size/quantity/quality: *el
gato* "cat" → *el gatito* "kitten," *la casa* "house" → *la casita* "small house." A final vowel is
dropped before the suffix; a final consonant just takes the suffix directly. Names take it too:
*Miguel* → *Miguelito* "Mike/Mikey" (p. 34).

### Standard orthographic changes

Three fixed, exceptionless spelling shifts preserve pronunciation when a suffix beginning in *e* or
*i* is added: *c* → *qu* before *e*/*i*, *g* → *gu* before *e*/*i*, *z* → *c* before *e*. Since
*-ito*/*-ita* begins with *i*, this recurs constantly with the diminutive: *el amigo* → *el
amiguito*, *el chico* → *el chiquito*, *la mosca* → *la mosquita*, *la vaca* → *la vaquita* (p. 36).

### Cracking the gender code

Three layers of clues for a noun's grammatical gender, in decreasing reliability:
1. **Final letter** — *-a* is usually feminine, *-o* is usually masculine (strong tendency, not a
   rule).
2. **Noun ending (suffix)** — the book tabulates roughly 40 suffixes that are always feminine (e.g.
   *-ción*, *-dad*, *-ura*, *-tad*) and roughly 35 that are always masculine (e.g. *-miento*, *-aje*,
   *-ismo*, *-ón*), plus four person-referring suffixes that take either article depending on the
   referent's sex (*-ante*, *-arca*, *-ense*, *-ista*: *el/la dentista* "dentist").
3. **Rule breakers**, in three groups: (a) Greek-derived *-ma* nouns that are masculine despite the
   *-a* ending (*el problema*, *el fantasma*, *el sistema* — vs. genuinely feminine *-ma* words like
   *la forma*, *la pluma*); (b) a small set of *-o* nouns that are feminine (*la mano* "hand," *la
   foto*, *la moto*); (c) feminine nouns beginning with a stressed *a*-sound (written or via silent
   *h*) that take the masculine singular article *el*/*un* in the singular only, reverting to
   feminine articles in the plural (*el agua* "the water" but *las aguas* "the waters"; also *el
   águila*, *el alma*, *el arma*, *el arpa*, *el hacha*); and (d) a residual list with no rule at all,
   memorized case by case (*la flor*, *el reloj*, *la leche*, *el pie*, etc.) (p. 37–39).

### Subject pronouns and the four words for "you"

Spanish distinguishes four second-person forms where English has one "you": *tú* (informal
singular, friends/children), *usted* (formal singular, strangers/higher status), *vosotros/
vosotras* (informal plural), *ustedes* (formal plural). The book flags one regional split
explicitly: **in much of Latin America, *ustedes* covers both formal and informal plural "you,"
making *vosotros* unnecessary** — the only Latin-America-vs.-Spain usage note in this unit (p. 42,
footnote 1). Spanish additionally has no subject pronoun "it" or inanimate "they" — these are simply
understood from context/verb ending (p. 41–42).

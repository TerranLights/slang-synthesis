## Grammar points

### Direct-object clitic pronouns attached to affirmative imperatives, detached before negative imperatives

Lección 41 systematically drills the position shift of direct-object pronouns (lo/la/los/las) around
the imperative: attached as a suffix on an affirmative command ("ábrela," "ciérralo," "póngalo") but
appearing as a separate pre-verbal clitic on a negative command ("no la abras," "no lo pongas"). The
instructor explicitly calls this shift out as a rule, not just an incidental drill pattern. (Lección
41, pp. 6, 9-10.)

### Indirect-object clitic pronouns attached to imperatives, across dar/traer/pedir

Lección 42 extends the same attached-pronoun imperative pattern from Lección 41 to indirect-object
pronouns (me/nos/le/les) on three new irregular verbs: dar (déme, dame, dénos, danos, déles, dales),
traer (tráigame, tráeme, tráigale, tráenos, tráigales, tráeles), and pedir (pídeme, pídenos, pídeles,
pídame, pídale, pídanos). The lesson also explicitly notes the possessive-adjective register shift
that co-occurs with formal/informal address ("saque el lápiz de **su** bolsillo" vs. "saca el lápiz
de **tu** bolsillo"). (Lección 42, throughout.)

### Explicit regional lexical variation flagged by the source (five items in one lesson)

Lección 42 is unusually dense in source-marked regional synonyms, all introduced with a version of
"in some countries they say X instead": volante/timón (steering wheel, actually introduced in
Lección 41), encendedor/mechero (lighter), paquete de cigarrillos/cajetilla (pack of cigarettes),
mesero/camarero (waiter), café negro/café puro (black coffee, tagged specifically to "some Latin
American countries"). None of these are tied to a *named* country by the source (unlike, e.g., the
slang lessons' Mexico/Venezuela/Colombia tags) — they are marked only as "some countries" without
further specification, which is reflected in the Attested Region column above. (Lección 42, pp. 5,
9-10.)

### azúcar: grammatically feminine noun with masculine-article agreement

The instructor explicitly flags "azúcar" as an irregular case: a feminine noun that nonetheless takes
"el" rather than "la" — the same phonological-clash pattern (feminine noun starting with stressed
a-/hard-a sound taking "el" for euphony) as "el agua," though the source does not generalize the rule
beyond this one example. (Lección 42, p. 7.)

## Grammar points

### Subject pronouns and the infinitive

Six subject-pronoun "slots" (1st/2nd/3rd person × singular/plural) each take their own conjugation
ending. The Spanish infinitive is always exactly one word ending in *-ar*, *-er*, or *-ir* (unlike
English's two-word "to + verb"), and stripping that ending leaves the verb stem the conjugation
endings attach to (p. 41–42).

### Conjugation of regular -ar, -er, -ir verbs

Three parallel conjugation patterns, one per infinitive class:
- **-ar**: -o, -as, -a, -amos, -áis, -an (*hablar* → *hablo, hablas, habla, hablamos, habláis,
  hablan*)
- **-er**: -o, -es, -e, -emos, -éis, -en (*vender* → *vendo, vendes, vende, vendemos, vendéis,
  venden*)
- **-ir**: -o, -es, -e, -imos, -ís, -en — identical to -er except in the *nosotros*/*vosotros* forms
  (*vivir* → *vivo, vives, vive, vivimos, vivís, viven*)
(p. 42–43, 50, 60).

### Deleting the understood subject pronoun

Because the verb ending alone already marks person/number for *yo*, *tú*, *nosotros*, and
*vosotros*, the subject pronoun is normally dropped in those four forms as redundant (*Bailo* not
*Yo bailo*). Third-person forms (*él/ella/usted*, *ellos/ellas/ustedes*) keep the pronoun/name
because the verb form alone is ambiguous about which referent is meant, unless context already
disambiguates. The pronoun can still be used deliberately for emphasis/contrast even in the
droppable forms (p. 48).

### Qualitative vs. quantitative adjectives, and word order

Quantitative (limiting) adjectives — numbers, amount words — precede the noun, as in English.
Qualitative (descriptive) adjectives *follow* the noun, the reverse of English default order
(*el libro rojo* "the red book," not *rojo libro*) (p. 55–56).

### Four-form vs. two-form descriptive adjectives

A four-form adjective ends in *-o* in its dictionary (masculine singular) form and takes four
distinct endings for gender × number (*rojo/roja/rojos/rojas*). A two-form adjective ends in
anything else and only varies for number, not gender (*verde/verdes* for both masculine and
feminine) (p. 56).

### Simple questions

Spanish has no equivalent of English's auxiliary "do/does." A statement becomes a simple yes/no
question just by moving the subject pronoun after the verb (*Ella trabaja* → *¿Trabaja ella?*).
Since the pronoun is already droppable in first/second person, it's equally droppable in the
question form (*¿Bailas?*) (p. 71).

### The suffix -ería

A highly productive, exceptionless pattern: attaching *-ería* to the noun for an item names the
type of shop/store that sells it, and the resulting noun is always feminine regardless of the base
noun's own gender (*el libro* → *la librería*, *el pan* → *la panadería*, *la carne* → *la
carnicería*). Flagged here as a strong candidate mechanism for later slang/word-formation analysis:
a fully regular, semantically transparent derivational suffix like this is exactly the kind of
productive pattern an in-universe slang system could plausibly extend, subvert, or reuse for
unrelated meanings (p. 66–68).

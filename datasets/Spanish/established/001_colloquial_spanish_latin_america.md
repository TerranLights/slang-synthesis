**Source:** Roberto Rodríguez-Saona, *Colloquial Spanish of Latin America: The Complete Course for
Beginners* (2nd ed., Routledge, 2002). Full text extracted and reviewed: front matter, Chapters

## Coverage note

This is a single-source, whole-book dispatch (168 pages, real `pdftotext` text layer — no vision
reading was needed, so Vision Reading Confidence is `n/a` throughout). Extraction approach:

- **Every chapter's "Language point(s)" grammar explanation** (all 19 teaching chapters; Chapter
  20 "Repaso" is a review chapter with no new grammar) is paraphrased below — this is genuinely
  complete, not a sample.
- **Vocabulary**: every distinct lexical item introduced in each chapter's dialogue vocabulary
  boxes and "Language point" example sets is included. What is *not* individually reproduced is
  the book's own back-of-book Spanish–English/English–Spanish glossary (pp. 285–327, several
  thousand entries) in bulk — most of those entries are the same lesson vocabulary already
  captured here, re-alphabetized. Per the coverage rule's emphasis on dialectal/register
  annotation as the highest-value content in this kind of source, the glossary **was** searched
  specifically for entries carrying an explicit regional or register marker (a country-code tag
  such as `(Mex.)`, `(Ar.)`, `(Pe.)`, `(Ch.)`, `(Col.)`, `(Uru.)`, `(Bo.)`, `(Ec.)`, or a
  `(Sp. ...)` cross-reference to the Peninsular equivalent), and every such entry found is
  included in the "Glossary: regional/register-annotated supplement" table below. This is the
  section of the book the dispatch specifically flagged as highest priority (Latin-American vs.
  Iberian variation), and it is treated as complete for the annotated subset, not sampled.
- **PDF extraction note:** the glossary (pp. 285–327) is laid out as a genuine two-column
  dictionary page, which the extraction spec's own gotcha list flags as a known
  `pdftotext -layout` scrambling risk (columns interleaved onto shared lines). This was visible in
  the raw extraction (e.g., an entry's regional annotation sometimes lands one physical line away
  from its headword after column-merging). Every regional/register-annotated entry below was
  cross-checked against surrounding context before inclusion; a small number of ambiguous/partially
  scrambled candidate lines were dropped rather than guessed at, per the "default to the riskier
  assumption when uncertain" principle.

## Grammar points

### Chapter 1 — Saludos (Greetings)
Pronunciation guide (vowels/consonants compared to English, including regional note that *ll* is
pronounced like English "y" generally but like the "s" in "vision" in Argentina and Uruguay — an
explicit dialectal annotation, p. 4). Numbers 1–100 and the *uno → un/una* agreement rule; numbers
ending in *-uno* (veintiuno, setenta y uno) shorten before a masculine noun (veintiún estudiantes).
Greetings (hola, buenos días, buenas tardes, buenas noches — the last also used as a nighttime
"hello"/"goodbye"); farewells (chao/chau vs. adiós, with the explicit LatAm/Spain split, p. 11).
The infinitive and the three conjugations (-ar/-er/-ir). The present tense indicative for habitual/
timeless actions. Subject pronouns (usually omitted; tú vs. usted register split; explicit note
that Latin American Spanish does not use *vosotros*, unlike Peninsular Spanish, p. 12). Regular
verb conjugation model (trabajar, comer, escribir). Irregular verb *tener*. Forming yes/no and
wh-questions (no auxiliary "do").

### Chapter 2 — Salgo a las seis (I leave at six)
More irregular present-tense verbs (decir, hacer, ir, salir, venir). Negative statements with *no*.
Telling time, including the explicit LatAm (*para*, "twenty to four") vs. Spain (*menos*) split
for expressing minutes to the hour, and the LatAm-favored plural *¿Qué horas son?* alongside the
universal *¿Qué hora es?*. 24-hour clock usage for schedules. *Muy* as an intensifier. Colors.
Clothing vocabulary. *Hay* (there is/are). Numbers 200–1,000,000. Regional note on *computador*
(m., Mexico) vs. *computadora* (f., Peru and others) for "computer," both distinct from Peninsular
*ordenador*.

### Chapter 3 — Reserva en el hotel (Reserving a hotel room)
Phone greeting *¿Aló?* (regional note: *bueno* is used in Mexico instead). Introducing yourself
(*me llamo…*/*mi nombre es…*). Masculine/feminine noun classes and typical endings. Definite
article (el/la/los/las, with the *el agua* exception for stressed feminine *a-*/*ha-* nouns).
Indefinite article (un/una/unos/unas); no article before an unmodified profession noun (*Elena es
secretaria*). Days of the week (not capitalized) and months of the year. Letter-writing
conventions (salutations/closings, colon vs. comma).

### Chapter 4 — ¿Dónde está? (Where is it?)
*Ser* (identity/nature/origin/profession) vs. *estar* (location/temporary state) — the book flags
this as one of the hardest distinctions for English speakers. Countries and nationalities list (21
Latin American/Iberian nations with adjectival forms). Prepositions of place (enfrente de, al lado
de, sobre, debajo de, detrás de, delante de, entre, a la izquierda/derecha). *Pero* for
non-contradictory contrast.

### Chapter 5 — El clima y los viajes (Weather and travel)
Weather expressions with *hacer* (hace frío/calor/sol/viento) vs. *estar* + gerund (está
lloviendo/nevando) vs. simple present (llueve/nieva). *Poco/mucho* agreement as adjectives.
Frequency/habit adverbs (diario→diariamente pattern; cada + time noun; vez/veces). *Por* expressing
frequency ("dos veces por semana"). *Quisiera* (polite conditional "would like," full paradigm).
Parts of the day (por/en la mañana, etc.) — explicit note that Latin America uses both *por* and
*en*, while Spain generally uses only *por*.

### Chapter 6 — De compras (Shopping)
Article + adjective substituting for a repeated noun ("el grande" = "the big one"). Comparatives
(regular with *más*; irregular: bueno/mejor, malo/peor, mucho/más, poco/menos); *más...que*.
Superlative (definite article + comparison). *Poder* (ability/permission) with the "no + poder"
negative-possibility pattern; *¿Se puede...?* impersonal-ability question. *Saber* for learned
ability ("can you swim/drive/play piano"). *Ambos/los dos* and *tanto...como* for "both...and,"
with the written/spoken register split. *Me gusta(n)* construction and its "it pleases me" literal
sense. TV-program genre vocabulary.

### Chapter 7 — ¿Qué estás haciendo? (What are you doing?)
Present continuous (estar + gerund); spelling changes leer→leyendo, construir→construyendo.
Possessive adjectives (mi/tu/su/nuestro-a/su, agreement rules). Demonstrative adjectives and
pronouns (este/ese/aquel sets, neuter esto/eso/aquello). ¡Qué + adjective/noun! exclamations.
Aquí/acá and allí/allá/ahí, with the explicit note that Latin America prefers acá/allá while Spain
prefers aquí/allí (ahí is shared). Conjunctions y→e and o→u before matching initial sounds.

### Chapter 8 — ¿Qué tengo que hacer? (What do I have to do?)
*Tener que* + infinitive (personal obligation) vs. *hay que* + infinitive (impersonal obligation).
Personal *a* before a human direct object. Household/workplace task vocabulary. Regional note that
*entrar a* is the usual Latin American form vs. Peninsular *entrar en*. House/room-of-the-house
vocabulary, with the note that Spanish speakers in Latin America prefer *chico/chica* for "small"
where Spain prefers *pequeño/pequeña*. *Ahorita* (LatAm immediacy marker) vs. Peninsular
*enseguida*. *Conmigo/contigo* special forms.

### Chapter 9 — He perdido... (I have lost...)
Present perfect (haber + past participle); regular participle formation (-ado/-ido) and irregular
participles (abierto, dicho, escrito, hecho, muerto, puesto, roto, visto, vuelto). Present perfect
continuous (haber + estado + gerund). *Desde* ("since") and *desde hace* + time period ("for").
*Por* vs. *para*: source/cause/"by,through" vs. destination/purpose/"in order to," plus idiomatic
*por* phrases (por ahora, por el momento, por otra parte, por si acaso, por ejemplo, por fin).
*¿Por qué?* (two words, "why") vs. *porque* (one word, "because").

### Chapter 10 — ¿Qué vamos a hacer hoy? (What are we going to do today?)
*Ir a* + infinitive as the preferred spoken-future construction. Relative pronoun *que* ("that,
which, who"). *Para mí/ti/usted/él/ella/nosotros/ustedes/ellos/ellas* to indicate an order's
intended recipient. *Según* ("according to") and its more formal synonyms *de acuerdo con,
conforme a, de conformidad con* (the latter two flagged as legal/formal register).

### Chapter 11 — Se dice que... (It is said that...)
Impersonal *se* + third-person verb as the spoken-language equivalent of the passive voice.
*Tan...como* (equality comparison). *Más de/menos de* before numbers/quantities (distinct from
*más...que* before adjectives).

### Chapter 12 — ¿Qué hiciste? (What did you do?)
Preterite (past) tense: regular conjugation for -ar/-er/-ir; explicit note that Latin American
newspaper headlines are often written in the preterite. Irregular preterites (ir/ser share a stem;
hacer, tener, estar, saber, dar, venir). Past-time expressions (ayer, anteayer, anoche, la semana
pasada, etc.) and *pasado/a* vs. *último* ("last" recent vs. "last" of a series). *Hace* + time
period = "...ago."

### Chapter 13 — Irán al mercado (They will go to the market)
Future tense: regular formation (infinitive + endings) and irregular stems (decir→dir-, hacer→har-,
poder→podr-, poner→pondr-, querer→querr-, saber→sabr-, salir→saldr-, tener→tendr-, venir→vendr-).
Note that the synthetic future is mainly written/formal; spoken Spanish prefers *ir a* + infinitive.
*El próximo/la próxima* and *[día/mes] que viene* as interchangeable future-time expressions.
*Dentro de* + time period ("within/in [time]").

### Chapter 14 — Yo estudiaba... (I used to study...)
Imperfect indicative (habitual/repeated past action or ongoing past state); the three irregular
imperfects (ir, ser, ver). Contrast with the finite/completed preterite. Imperfect continuous
(estar in the imperfect + gerund) for an interrupted past action; explicit note that this is common
in Latin America while Spain tends to prefer the simple imperfect indicative instead (*estudiaba*
rather than *estaba estudiando*). Negative words (nada, nadie, ninguno/a, ni, nunca) and the
"double negative" rule when the negative word follows the verb. *No más*: the explicit LatAm vs.
Peninsular *nada más* split, plus its additional LatAm-only senses ("just/only," encouragement, "very
recently/nearby").

### Chapter 15 — Espero que puedas (I hope you can)
Present subjunctive: regular formation and the book's full irregular-verb table (dar, decir, estar,
haber, hacer, ir, oír, poder, poner, querer, saber, salir, ser, tener, traer, valer). Uses of the
subjunctive: after verbs of influence (querer, aconsejar, recomendar, sugerir); after expressions of
judgement/emotion; after expressions of probability/doubt; after indefinite antecedents (alguien,
algo); after ¡qué!/¡ojalá!; and obligatorily after antes que, para que, con tal que, a menos que.
Health-expression vocabulary (me duele, tomar una pastilla).

### Chapter 16 — Me gustaría... (I would like...)
Conditional tense: regular formation and irregular stems (same irregular-stem set as the future:
decir, haber, hacer, poder, poner, querer, salir, tener, venir). Ordinal numbers (primero–décimo);
*primero/tercero* shorten to *primer/tercer* before a masculine noun; cardinals replace ordinals
past *décimo* in everyday speech. Imperative mood: affirmative tú (same as 3rd-person present
indicative for regulars, with an irregular set: decir→di, estar→está, hacer→haz, ir→ve, oír→oye,
salir→sal, ser→sé, tener→ten, venir→ven), affirmative usted/ustedes (= present subjunctive), and
negative commands (always the subjunctive, in all persons).

### Chapter 17 — Si yo tuviera... (If I had...)
Imperfect subjunctive: two interchangeable conjugation sets (-ra/-se) and the irregular-verb list
(same core irregulars as present subjunctive, now in the imperfect). Open conditional sentences (si
+ present indicative → present indicative/near-future, likely condition). Remote/hypothetical
conditional sentences (si + imperfect subjunctive → conditional tense, contrary-to-fact condition).
*Como* meaning "since/because." Colloquial substitution of the *-ra* subjunctive form for the
conditional in spoken/familiar registers (flagged as not strictly "correct" but commonly heard).
*Tener razón* ("to be right").

### Chapter 18 — Si hubieras enviado... (If you had sent...)
Conditional perfect (habría + past participle). Pluperfect subjunctive (hubiera/hubiese + past
participle, both forms interchangeable). Third-type (past-contrary-to-fact) conditional sentences:
si + pluperfect subjunctive → conditional perfect. *Yo que tú/usted* as the colloquial everyday
alternative to *si yo fuera tú/usted* ("if I were you"). *Aunque* (concession, indicative or
subjunctive, largely interchangeable) and *a pesar de que* (same function, more formal-leaning).
*Demasiado* as adjective (agrees) vs. adverb (invariable). *Suponiendo que* (always + subjunctive).
*Así* used in Latin American Spanish as an alternative to *aunque* ("even if") — an explicit
regional extension beyond its base "so/thus" meaning.

### Chapter 19 — Ha sido... (It has been...)
Passive voice (ser + past participle, full tense paradigm shown: present, imperfect, preterite,
future, conditional, present/pluperfect/future/conditional perfect). Impersonal *se* as the
everyday spoken alternative to the passive (used when the agent is not stated); passive with *ser*
required instead when the agent performing the action is explicitly named. Passive with *estar*
(resultant state, no agent) vs. passive with *ser* (the action itself, agent may be added) — *la
puerta estaba abierta* vs. *la puerta fue abierta (por el vigilante)*. *Pero* vs. *sino*: both
translate "but," but *sino* specifically contradicts/replaces the preceding negated statement
("no...sino").

### Chapter 20 — Repaso (Review)
Pure review chapter recombining preterite, future, subjunctive/conditional structures, and passive/
impersonal-se material from Chapters 12–19 in new dialogues and exercises; no new grammar point is
introduced.

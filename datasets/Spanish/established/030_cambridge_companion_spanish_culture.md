## Grammar / Sociolinguistic-concepts points

### The glossary itself as a register/domain marker
Nearly every glossary entry belongs to one of three technical registers: political/party abbreviations (`technical.politics`), named cultural-artistic movements/genres (`technical.history`/`literary`), or historically bounded institutions. This is a useful cross-check for the project's usage-tier taxonomy: an editor compiling a glossary *for English-speaking readers of a scholarly book* reflexively selects exactly the vocabulary that is opaque to outsiders — i.e., domain-technical and culture-specific terms, almost never `core` vocabulary. This is itself evidence for the taxonomy's `technical` tier as a real, source-recognized category rather than a project-invented one.

### Castilian-centric historiography as an invented national narrative (Ch.2)
Fox's essay argues (via Anderson/Gellner/Hobsbawm) that "Spain = Castile" is not a linguistic or demographic fact but a 19th–20th-c. historiographical *invention*, propagated through canonized literature (Cid, Quijote, Lazarillo, Celestina) and figures like Ortega y Gasset, Unamuno, and Menéndez Pidal's philology school. The essay explicitly separates "nation" from language, race, or geography as its defining criterion, which is a useful theoretical caution for the project: shared language does *not* automatically imply shared national-identity construction, and the reverse — a constructed national identity selectively privileging one regional language/variety (Castilian) as "the" national language — is the documented historical mechanism behind Spanish linguistic centralism. (pp. 21–36)

### Catalan linguistic normalization as a named policy process (Ch.3)
The 1983 "Llei de Normalització Lingüística" is the clearest concrete language-policy artifact in this chunk: it declared Catalan's official status and was explicitly conceived to close "the linguistic divide" between high/literary Catalan and the everyday spoken language (the chapter's governing metaphor, borrowed from Gabriel Ferrater's 1953 essay "Madame se meurt," is that Catalan culture/language was pronounced "dying" then triumphantly declared "still alive" by 1976). The chapter also gives a concrete, documented case study of state-driven language revival via mass media: the dubbing of the US TV series *Dallas* into Catalan in the early 1980s is credited by the chapter as a major real driver of popular Catalan-language normalization, because it was the first time Catalan language reached a true mass/everyday register via a prestige/global entertainment product — a “language revival via dubbing/media” mechanism worth flagging for the project's `register_inversion`/`technical_leak`-adjacent mechanism catalog, since it's media-driven normalization rather than organic drift or state-mandated schooling. (pp. 37–51)

### Basque language status and its political instrumentalization (Ch.4)
Four distinct findings: (1) Euskara/Euskera has "a sparse written, and virtually no secular literary, tradition," historically spoken mainly by rural farmers/clergy, not the urban liberal centers (Bilbao/San Sebastián) — i.e., its prestige trajectory is the *inverse* of Castilian's, rising from a rural/subordinate register to a national-identity touchstone only in the late 19th century. (2) Sabino Arana (the "father of Basque nationalism," 1865–1903) deliberately avoided naming his movement/nation after the language itself ("Euskal Herria") because he personally didn't speak Euskara and the name would have excluded non-Euskara-speaking Basques — he coined explicitly political alternatives (Vasconia, Euskeria, Bizkaia, Euzkadi) instead. This is a rare documented case of a nationalist movement's founder *rejecting* a linguistically-defined ethnonym for political-inclusivity reasons. (3) Under Franco, Euskara survived mainly through "the sermons of a politically active Basque Nationalist clergy" and became the matrix for a "semi-clandestine Basque-language school movement" — church-mediated language preservation under repression. (4) In the post-Franco era, Basque regional government cultural policy "does considerably more to support Euskara-related projects than Castilian-language ones," to the point that the chapter reports a real-world case of a Castilian-singing Basque *cantautor* (singer-songwriter) facing threats from ETA sympathizers — i.e., language choice functioning as a sanctioned, policed register boundary, not merely a stylistic one. (pp. 54–66)

## Grammar/Sociolinguistic-concepts points

### Francoist linguistic proscription and its post-Franco reversal
Under the 1939-1975 dictatorship, public use of Catalan and Basque was legally proscribed alongside the rescission of Catalonia's and the Basque Country's Republican-era autonomy statutes (Ch.6, p.~1342) — framed by the regime's broader "autarky" doctrine of re-establishing "traditionally Spanish" norms by suppressing internal as well as external "contamination" (p.~1331). After 1975, the consolidated autonomous-community system reversed this directly: Catalan, Galician, and Euskara received substantial government funding and institutional promotion, explicitly narrated by contemporaries as "the recovery of identity... lost during the long years of dictatorship" (Ch.7, p.~2220-2225). This is the same suppression→funded-revival arc independently corroborated by this volume's own Ch.1-4 (Catalonia/Basque Country chapters) and Ch.23 (media chapter) in sibling extraction chunks — three different contributors to the same edited volume converge on the identical policy mechanism from political-history, regional-identity, and media angles respectively.

### Language-of-composition as a literary-political marker
Starting in the Transition and after, the specific choice of writing in Basque, Catalan, or Galician rather than Castilian became a recognized and separately rewarded literary act, not merely a stylistic one: Spain's Premio Nacional de Literatura went to a Basque novel in 1989 and a Catalan novel in 1995 (Ch.10, p.~3602-3604), and 1970s poet Pere Gimferrer's choice to write in Catalan — then still a banned/marginalized literary language — is credited by the source as helping catalyze a broader later "flourishing of Catalan and Galician writing" (Ch.14, p.~5633-5635). A 1987 poetry anthology (Barella) went further, explicitly organizing its selections by five labeled regional/linguistic varieties (Andalusian, Basque, Castilian, Catalan, Galician) rather than by theme or period alone (Ch.14, p.~5845) — a rare case of a literary-critical source itself adopting the kind of explicit per-variety labeling convention this project tracks.

### Dialectal/vernacular poetic register as identity-marking
Two Restoration-era (1868-1936) poets are singled out specifically for writing in regionally marked language rather than standard Castilian: Vicente Medina's Murcian verse is described as using "simple, dialectal language" to voice the suffering of the poet and the oppressed (p.~4980), and Rosalía de Castro's Galician-language verse is framed as expressing specifically nationalistic concerns (identity, oppression, emigration, exploitation, homesickness) through adapted folksong forms (p.~4982) — in both cases the source treats the choice of a non-standard regional variety as itself doing the identity-political work, not merely a stylistic flourish.

### Censorship-driven indirection as a composition strategy
Francoist-era censorship (most intensively 1939-c.1960s) forced writers into evasive allusion as a systematic textual strategy: some authors pre-emptively softened manuscripts before submission anticipating rejection, others had passages excised post-submission, and the source notes critics have observed that "more allusions evaded the censor than [were lost to] the lack of skill of the author" (Ch.8, p.~2989-2996; recurring through Ch.9, e.g. p.~3038-3046 on Torrente Ballester's self-altered manuscript). This is a text-composition-level euphemism/indirection mechanism (comparable to the project's `euphemism_chain` derivation type) rather than a discrete lexical item, but worth flagging as a structural pattern repeatedly documented across multiple authors and decades in this source.

## Grammar / Sociolinguistic-concepts points

### Francoist stage censorship of Catalan (Ch.16)

The Francoist prohibition against the use of Catalan on stage was explicitly lifted in **1946** — far earlier than the regime's broader cultural normalization. The chapter stresses this precisely to argue that Catalan independent theater groups' later emphasis on mime over text (Els Joglars, Tricicle, La Fura dels Baus) was an *artistic* choice, not a continuing reaction to linguistic censorship — a useful nuance against assuming Catalan stage-language suppression ran continuously through the whole dictatorship. Independent theater groups also existed in the Basque Country and Galicia, but the volume notes Catalonia as where the independent-theater movement's legacy is most enduring.

### Post-Franco register liberalization on stage (Ch.16)

With the end of official censorship (1977), the elimination of restrictions on "nudity, sexual material and erotic, **colloquial language**" is explicitly named as a direct, immediate consequence — the chapter treats colloquial/vulgar register itself as something that had been actively suppressed under censorship, not merely content/theme. A later wave of younger Madrid playwrights (Ernesto Caballero, the Catalan Sergi Belbel) is explicitly characterized as favoring "colloquial language and cultural situations" relatable to younger audiences — i.e., colloquial register as a deliberate authorial/generational marker in 1980s–90s alternative theater.

### Francoist suppression of regional vernaculars, stated directly (Ch.21 — notable finding)

Chapter 21 states plainly: "Franco's government did its best to **squelch Euskara, Catalan, and Galician as spoken vernacular**." This is presented alongside an important counterpoint: the regime simultaneously *promoted* flamenco internationally as a non-controversial, depoliticized emblem of "Spanishness" (bullfights/wine/sun/sand imagery) — which residents of the Basque Country, Catalonia, and Galicia resented, since they considered flamenco indigenous to Andalusia alone, not representative of Spain's regional diversity. This single sentence is the volume's most direct, unambiguous statement of the suppression itself (other chapters in this volume document specific policy instruments — e.g. the 1983 Catalan Llei de Normalització Lingüística, found in a sibling chunk — but this is the clearest flat assertion that spoken vernacular use itself was actively targeted, not just institutional/official use).

### Bilingual/regional identity as film theme (Ch.18–19)

Several Transition-and-after Spanish films explicitly make regional-language identity their subject rather than an incidental backdrop: Julio Medem's *Vacas* and Carlos Armendáriz's *Tasio* (1984) center Basque identity; Vicente Aranda's *El amante bilingüe* ("The Bilingual Lover") centers Catalan/Spanish bilingualism as its explicit theme. This is treated as part of a broader post-1992 (Barcelona/Seville Expo-Olympics year) current of films exploring "the realities and myths of the regions" alongside Spain's pro-European integration narrative.

## Grammar points

### Regional-language broadcasting as a state-sanctioned normalization tool

The chapter frames Basque TV (ETB, 1983) and Catalan TV (TV3, 1984) — followed by Galician (TVG), Andalusian (Canal Sur), Valencian (Canal 9), and even Madrid's own channel — as a direct, legally mandated (1980 broadcasting law; the Basque and Catalan autonomy Statutes) mechanism for promoting "linguistically differentiated cultures." This corroborates, from an independent chapter/author in the same book, a sibling chunk's finding (Ch.1-4 of this same Companion) about explicit Catalan-language normalization policy (the 1983 Llei de Normalització Lingüística) — broadcasting is named here as one of that normalization effort's concrete institutional levers, not just legislation on paper. The chapter adds a candid caveat: in practice, regional-culture commitment on these channels often amounted mainly to dubbing imported US programming into the regional language, rather than original regional-language production — a tension between normalization's stated goal and its lived practice worth flagging for later sociolinguistic-mechanics work on language-prestige/status engineering.

### State control of journalistic language under censorship (consignas)

Beyond formal prior censorship, the Franco regime's *consignas* system is described as a distinct mechanism: directives telling journalists not just *what* to report but *how* to phrase/frame it, explicitly including instructions to disguise the propagandistic origin of inserted material. This is a named, source-attested case of top-down register/framing control over a national press register — relevant precedent for how political power can shape a "formal/journalistic" usage tier distinct from organic register variation.

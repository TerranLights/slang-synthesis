## Grammar/Sociolinguistic-concepts points

### Ch.9 — The 1978 Constitution's language articles

Article 2 grants "autonomía" to the "nacionalidades y regiones" composing Spain while asserting
indivisible national unity — the book flags this as deliberately ambiguous, consensus-seeking
language. Article 3 is the first Spanish constitution to engage fully with linguistic rights: Clause
1 names Castilian the official state language with a citizen "duty to know" it (using *conocer*,
"be acquainted with," rather than *saber hablar*, "know how to speak" — itself ambiguous and
litigated); Clause 2 grants other "lenguas españolas" co-official status within their own
Autonomous Communities "de acuerdo con sus Estatutos"; Clause 3 frames linguistic diversity as
protected cultural patrimony. Table 9.1 (p.122) records six communities' own-language populations:
Cataluña/catalán (6.12M, 15.5% of Spain's population), Valencia/valenciano (3.92M, 9.95%), Islas
Baleares/catalán (745,944, 1.89%), Galicia/gallego (2.72M, 6.89%), País Vasco/euskera (2.11M, 5.34%),
Navarra/vascongado (523,563, 1.32%). A notable anomaly: Valencia's constitution was published in a
"Valencian" version virtually word-for-word identical to the Catalan version, despite Valencian
political insistence on a separate identity from Catalan — raising the book's own open
language-vs-dialect question. The chapter also covers Spain's EU membership (1986) effects: Spanish
became an EU official working language; EU directives on mother-tongue teaching for immigrant
children (tied into Ch.11); and EU support for "lesser-used languages" via dedicated bodies.

### Ch.10 — Language planning (LP) framework and its application

LP is defined as deliberate, conscious influence on individual/societal language behaviour, carried
out by governments/agencies, supra-national bodies, or non-institutional groups (pressure groups,
media, religious groups) — the last category succeeding only with institutional backing. LP splits
into corpus planning / status planning / acquisition planning (see Vocabulary table). Spain's three
"historic regions" (Catalonia, Euskadi, Galicia) each passed their own Linguistic Normalisation Laws
following Article 3. Catalonia's programme is characterized as the most active/successful (Catalan
has uniquely high prestige across all social classes, unlike Basque or Galician, and is accessible to
Castilian speakers as a related Romance language) despite ~50% of Catalonia's population not being
native-born there (Civil-War-era labor migration, chiefly from Andalusia and Galicia). Euskadi's
promotion is characterized as largely symbolic rather than practical: Basque is not Romance (unlike
Catalan/Galician/Castilian), had minimal literary tradition, and <25% of the Basque Country's
population report speaking it. Galicia has the highest native-speaker rate (~90% of ~3M) but lowest
social prestige, tied to a historically rural, heavily-emigrated, conservative society. Table 10.1
(census vs. 1993 survey) shows knowledge-rate gains for Catalan/Basque but a slight Galician decline;
Table 10.2 (attitude survey on needing the local language to work locally) shows strong agreement in
Cataluña (91%) and Galicia/Baleares (83% each) but split opinion in Euskadi (44% agree/44%
disagree). A 1994 RAE letter (Lázaro Carreter to PM Felipe González) warned against growing laxity in
public Castilian use and urged safeguards against diglossia; the right-wing Madrid daily ABC ran a
headline comparing Catalan President Jordi Pujol's language policy to Franco's persecution
"in reverse" — the book cites this exchange as evidence of a late-1990s backlash current. Latin
American LP is framed as operating on two parallel tracks: corpus-planning Spanish itself (debate
over whether Peninsular or Spanish-American usage defines the "prestige dialect," via Hornberger
1994) and status/acquisition-planning indigenous languages, severely resource-constrained compared
to Catalonia's Generalitat. The Guatemala case study: the 1985 constitution (post-dating a history
of pure Castilianizing assimilation policy) explicitly recognized indigenous peoples' cultural/
linguistic rights (Art.58), promoted Mayan "forms of life"/languages (Art.66), preferred bilingual
instruction in majority-Indian zones (Art.76), and named Mayan languages part of the national
cultural patrimony (Art.143) — paired with new corpus-planning work standardizing Mayan orthography/
vocabulary.

### Ch.11 — Bilingual education models and two case studies

Core typology: assimilationist/transitional vs. maintenance/pluralist bilingual education (see
Vocabulary), plus monolingual submersion vs. voluntary immersion programmes. Historical anchors:
UNESCO's 1951 declaration favoring mother-tongue-medium early education; the US 1968 Bilingual
Education Act (transitional-model, mother-tongue teaching phased out once English integration is
judged achieved); the EU's 1977 directive on immigrant mother-tongue provision (often implemented as
marginal after-school/weekend classes). Latin American literacy is framed as historically restricted
by landowning elites and shaped to assimilate indigenous populations into "Europeanised" values;
the book cites the Nicaraguan Sandinista literacy campaign's initial failure to recognize the
literacy-language-identity link (it taught literacy via Spanish only, later corrected) as a
cautionary case. Bolivia case study: ~23% Aymara speakers, ~35% Quechua, 30+ other indigenous
languages, with only 37% Spanish monolingualism as of the 1976 census; the Summer Institute of
Linguistics ran an early transitional-model bilingual programme; more recent NGO/state intercultural
programmes (modelled on Peruvian schemes) use radio to extend literacy beyond writing-based
methods. Catalonia case study: the 1983 Catalan Linguistic Normalisation Law's Article 14 establishes
Catalan as the language of instruction at all levels while guaranteeing children's right to first
instruction in either Catalan or Castilian, with both taught obligatorily; Article 20 makes Catalan
the schools' normal vehicular language. The 1992 LOGSE education reform was used by the Generalitat
to make the previously-voluntary immersion method compulsory from the 1993-94 school year — triggering
organized opposition (CADECA and allied groups) that nonetheless represented a documented small
minority (survey data: 98% of surveyed parents wanted their children to learn Catalan; 86% rejected
separate schools by language; only 4% of Catalan residents reported not understanding Catalan).

### Ch.12 — US Hispanic population, Puerto Rico, and symbolic status battles

1993 CPS data: 22.8 million US residents of Hispanic origin (8.9% of US population), the world's
4th-largest Spanish-speaking population, concentrated in 9 states (7 of which are >10% Hispanic:
New Mexico, California, Texas, Arizona, New York, Florida, Colorado). Table 12.1 breaks this down by
origin: Mexican 64.3%, Puerto Rican 10.6%, Cuban 4.7%, Central/South American 13.4%, other 7.0%. The
"English Only" movement (California's 1986 amendment, 73.2% in favor) is presented against the
backdrop that the US Constitution itself names no official language. Second-generation language
shift toward English is documented alongside two counter-trends: codeswitching (see Vocabulary) and
pan-US-Hispanic dialect homogenization via Spanish-language media. Puerto Rico's status is tracked as
a recurring flashpoint: the 1902 law made English and Spanish co-official; an April 1991 law made
Spanish sole official language (celebrated in Peninsular Spanish press, including a Barcelona
columnist's explicit parallel to Catalan/Basque aspirations); the 1993 election reversed this back to
co-official status. The chapter closes on two symbolic-status episodes treated as corpus/status-
planning case studies in miniature: the 1994 pan-Hispanic-academy removal of "ch"/"ll" as separate
alphabet letters (ostensibly for EU translation-compatibility, provoking a Cuban delegate's objection
to the EU-pressure framing) versus the retained Ñ (EU pressure to drop it for computing reasons was
rejected; Ñ became the Instituto Cervantes logo) — framed as the line of acceptable vs. unacceptable
external pressure on Spanish's own corpus. The chapter's closing piece is a satirical bilingual
short story ("Little Ear of the Little Deaf" / "Orejilla del Sordete," by Joseph Denier, *España
Económica*, March 1990) dramatizing a fictional autonomous Spanish region that has fully shifted to
English for practical/tourism reasons while insisting its identity is intact — used by the book as a
deliberately uncomfortable thought experiment about where Spanish's own regional-identity debates
could end up.

# Spanish — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: A Spanish Grammar Workbook (Blackwell, non-redundant supplement)

**Source:** Esther Santamaría Iglesias (with María J. Terrazas, et al.), *A Spanish Grammar
Workbook*, Blackwell Publishing, 2004 (companion workbook to de Bruyne & Pountain's *A
Comprehensive Spanish Grammar*, Blackwell 1995). 374 PDF pages, real text layer confirmed via
`pdftotext` sample checks across pages 10, 20, 40 and several chapter boundaries — no vision-reading
needed.

## Non-redundant-supplement scope decision (read this before the tables)

This is a pure drill workbook: 55 chapters/500 numbered exercises, each keyed by cross-reference to
a chapter number in *A Comprehensive Spanish Grammar*, covering articles, nouns, adjectives,
numerals, pronouns (personal/demonstrative/possessive/relative/interrogative/indefinite), impersonal
expressions, adverbs, comparatives, prepositions, verb+noun constructions, conjunctions, full regular
and irregular verb conjugation, tense usage, the passive, the subjunctive, infinitive/gerund/past
participle, ser/estar, haber/deber/poder, subject-verb concord, negation, and word order. **Every one
of these topics is already thoroughly covered** by 005/006/007/008/014/015/016/017/018/019/020/021/022
and does not need re-extraction here — this file does **not** re-extract any conjugation paradigm,
article/gender rule, basic pronoun system, or other core mechanics. Exercises are fill-in-the-blank/
matching/underline drills with no expository prose explaining the rules (that prose lives in the
companion *Comprehensive Grammar*, not this workbook), so there is very little to extract even in
principle beyond what's flagged below.

**What was kept and why** (the only genuinely new material found after a full-text scan for
dialect/register markers across all 374 pages):
1. **The Preface's explicit Peninsular-vs-Latin-American framing** — a reflective authorial note on
   why the book can't be pan-Hispanic, worth preserving as a sociolinguistic framing device.
2. **Exercise 99 (p. 48): a 12-pair Latin-America-vs-Peninsular-Spanish vocabulary matching list** —
   genuinely new lexical items not seen in prior Spanish extractions.
3. **Exercise 100 (p. 48): a full Argentine voseo dialogue** for rewriting into Peninsular forms —
   a clean compact illustration of voseo verb morphology (sos, decís, podés, pongás, vení, dejés,
   prometés, querés, tenés, digás, invitás) in connected dialogue rather than isolated conjugation
   cells.
4. **Exercise 323 (p. ~242 region, chapter "Use of the Tenses"): a regional-stereotype reading
   passage** pairing four Spanish regions with their stereotyped personality adjective, plus several
   colorful idioms used in the passage.
5. **The Affective Suffixes chapter (Ch. 55, pp. 279–283): additional suffix forms and a
   systematically expanded "blow with X" (-azo) list and "habitual doer" (-ón) adjective list**
   beyond what 016 (Unit 30) already captured — including a regional **-iña** diminutive variant not
   seen in any prior Spanish extraction.

Everything else (the remaining ~490 exercises) is pure mechanical drill on already-documented grammar
and was correctly skipped per the coverage rule.

## Grammar points

### Peninsular-vs-Latin-American framing (Preface, p. ix)

The book's own preface makes an explicit, reflective sociolinguistic argument worth preserving as
framing rather than as a grammar rule: Peninsular Spanish is "not the pan-Hispanic model," and the
difference between Argentinian and Mexican Spanish is asserted to be as large as the difference
between Mexican and Peninsular Spanish. The author frames the resulting choice as a genuine
dilemma — a "colourless," over-neutralized Spanish that serves nobody vs. an inevitably partial
"pick-and-mix" of regional features — and explicitly declines to "police" regional boundaries,
describing the book instead as "a guidebook for independent travellers" rather than an attempt at
dialect-neutral coverage. Useful as evidence that even Peninsular-authored pedagogical grammars are
self-aware about the limits of treating Spain's Spanish as default/neutral.

### Argentine voseo in connected dialogue (Ex. 100, p. 48)

The general voseo verb-paradigm shift (tú → vos, with its own present-indicative and imperative
conjugation pattern) is already documented elsewhere in this corpus from paradigm tables; this
exercise's value is a short natural dialogue exercising the forms in context rather than isolation:
present indicative (sos, decís, podés, vení, prometés, querés, tenés, invitás), negative imperative
with the characteristic voseo stress shift (no me pongás, no me dejés — stress on the final
syllable, distinct from the tú-form "no me pongas/dejes"), and subjunctive-complement voseo (lo que
vos digás). Useful as a worked discourse-level example for any later slang-mechanics pass comparing
voseo to tuteo in running speech rather than conjugation-table cells.

### Regional personality stereotypes as a distinct caricature axis (Ex. 323)

Distinct from the Spain-vs-Latin-America dialectal axis documented throughout this corpus, this
reading passage documents an intra-Spain, intra-national caricature axis: andaluz = "el gracioso"
(the joker/funny one), madrileño = "el chulo" (the cocky/sassy one), aragonés = "el terco" (the
stubborn one), catalán = "el serio" (the serious one). The passage frames this as current/topical
humor explicitly tied to a period of tension over Basque independence and Catalan autonomy — "el
estereotipo nacional dio paso al regional" ("the national stereotype gave way to the regional one").
Worth flagging as a second axis of in-group stereotype/slang generation (regional-within-country)
distinct from the Spain/Latin-America axis most of this corpus's dialectal tags track.

### Affective suffixes — two additional patterns beyond 016's Unit 30 coverage

1. **The -iña diminutive** (tierriña, vaquiña) is a genuinely new suffix not in 016's inventory
   (-ito/-ecito, -illo/-ecillo, -uelo, -ón, -azo, -ote, -ucho). Consistent with Galician-influenced
   Peninsular Spanish, though the book itself doesn't name the regional source explicitly — flagged
   as an inferred attribution, not a book-stated one.
2. **The -azo "blow with X" pattern and the -ón "habitual doer" pattern are both more productive
   than 016's examples alone suggested.** Ex. 500's "golpe con X → Xazo" worksheet and Ex. 497's
   "habitual-doer" fill-in-the-blank worksheet each supply roughly 8-9 additional instance words,
   reinforcing that both are live, broadly productive derivational patterns in colloquial Spanish
   rather than small closed lists — directly relevant to how a conlang's own "blow with X" or
   "habitual doer" slang-coining mechanics might be modeled on a genuinely open-class pattern.

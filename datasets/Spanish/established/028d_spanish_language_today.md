## Grammar / Contact-phenomena points

### Pidgin vs. creole (terminological distinction)
Pidgins are trade languages that develop between speakers with no common language, drawing their
lexicon from a colonial "lexifier" language; they have a restricted functional range because
speakers retain another language for other functions. Creoles differ in having native speakers of
their own (children of pidgin speakers acquiring it as a first language), with an extended,
elaborated functional range. A creole may subsequently *decreolize*, gradually assimilating features
back from its high-prestige lexifier.

### Why Spanish-based creoles are rare
The source (citing McWhorter 1995) attributes the scarcity of Spanish-lexified creoles (relative to
English- and French-based ones) to three factors: small initial Spanish sugar plantations let
slaves acquire Spanish directly rather than a pidgin; Spain often colonized areas that already had
Portuguese-based pidgins, which were relexified rather than replaced; and Spain lacked West African
trade settlements from which a Spanish pidgin could have spread. Several "Spanish-based" creoles
(e.g. papiamento) may in fact be relexified Portuguese pidgins under this view. The three principal
surviving Spanish-based creoles — chabacano (Philippines), papiamento (Dutch West Indies), palenquero
(Colombia) — share a common endpoint: low social status leading to gradual loss, whether through
rehispanicization (losing creole features toward standard Spanish) or shift to another prestige
variety. Papiamento is the exception with genuine high-prestige, cross-class status and a
literary/audiovisual culture of its own.

### Language contact outcomes vary by the specific contact language (§9.1)
The chapter's structure itself demonstrates a key methodological point: contact effects are not a
single generic phenomenon but depend on the structural distance between the languages in contact.
Contact with a closely cognate Romance language (Catalan, Portuguese) produces mostly phonetic and
lexical interference with the morphosyntax largely intact (e.g. Catalan-influenced final-consonant
devoicing "Madrit" for "Madrid"; Portuguese-contact lexical selection favoring the term closest to
both languages, e.g. *puerco* over *cerdo/chancho* for "pig"). Contact with a structurally unrelated
language (Maya) produces much less morphosyntactic influence but a distinctive *phonetic* profile
(word-final [n]→[m], syllabic reduplication for emphasis: *buenisísimo*) plus substantial lexical
borrowing in specific semantic domains (flora/fauna/agriculture). Contact with Italian produced a
genuinely mixed contact variety (cocoliche) precisely because the base language is often
indeterminate speaker-by-speaker. English is judged the only contact language with potentially
longer-term structural effects on Spanish, given its global dominance — though even here, effects
remain "minimal...except as a source of renewal."

### Code-switching: three levels of proficiency, and its social functions (Poplack 1982, cited p.195-196)
The source adopts a named three-level typology of code-switching sophistication: (1) *emblematic*
switches — tags, interjections, set expressions, single nouns — usable even by speakers not
proficient in the second code; (2) *intersentential* switching — changing code between complete
utterances, requiring the ability to sustain an interaction in both codes but no deeper
code-switching skill; (3) *intrasentential* switching — moving between codes mid-sentence without
violating either language's grammar, requiring the highest bilingual skill and typical of "balanced
bilinguals." This is a useful three-tier competence model for any constructed-language
code-switching/register mechanic. The source explicitly argues against the popular view that
code-switching signals inability to command either language (reflected in the pejorative labels
"Tex-Mex"/"Spanglish"), and instead catalogs its social functions: rhetorical shifts
(seriousness→humor, formal→informal); marking a speaker's affective stance (e.g. switching to the
ethnic-identity language when personally invested in a topic, to the dominant language for
detachment); softening a face-threatening act (switching to Catalan to swear, since the switch
itself blunts the force of the invective); "emblematic" use of a minority-language greeting
(*Bon dia*, *Kaixo*) purely to signal accommodation/goodwill even by a non-fluent monolingual; and
marking political/ethnic loyalty at the close of an otherwise accommodating interaction (a Catalan
official reverting to Catalan only in his sign-off).

### T/V (tú/usted) pronoun change as a marker of social change (book's Conclusion)
The source's overall Conclusion frames changing patterns of address-pronoun use (treated fully in
028c) as direct evidence that "language change both reflects and influences social change,"
occurring either "from below" (gradual, speaker-driven) or "from above" (imposed by a regime or
state organ) — a useful general framing for any slang/register-shift mechanism in the constructed
language: track whether a given shift is organic/bottom-up or imposed/top-down.

### Register/variety retention across generations and media (book's Conclusion)
A convergent finding: increased exposure to formal domains (media, standardized education,
panhispanic corpus projects like CREA and the Proyecto Zacatecas) pushes toward a unifying
"panhispanic norm," while generational/geographic/contact pressures continuously regenerate
sub-varieties — the author explicitly frames this as the central, ongoing tension of contemporary
Spanish, closing the book on the point that "an idealized panhispanic norm recognized by many but
used by none" is less useful for understanding the language than situated, authentic-use-based
study.

### Glossary as reference, not vocabulary (pp.214-219)
The book's own Glossary defines ~70 English-language linguistics terms (see table above) — this is
metalanguage for the book's own analysis, not Spanish lexical data. Its definitions of register
(field/mode/tenor), code-switching, borrowing, calque, hypercorrection, and semantic extension are
directly useful conceptual vocabulary for this project's own mechanics-analysis phase.

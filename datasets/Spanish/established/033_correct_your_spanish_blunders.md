## Grammar points

### Verb classes: intransitive, reflexive(-se), transitive, personal-a, "exchange," "feeling," advice/control, and unplanned-occurrence verbs (pp. 102-109)
This chapter sets up a verb-classification system the rest of the book leans on heavily:
- **Intransitive** verbs never take an object pronoun of any kind (blunder: *el niño lo lloró*) and
  never take a reflexive pronoun unless the dictionary form itself ends in *-se*.
- **-se dictionary verbs** always require a matching reflexive pronoun even when the verb has no
  intuitively reflexive meaning in English — *quejarse* "to complain" is reflexive in form only
  (blunder: *David... quejan de todo*, missing *se*); *levantarse* "to get up" is reflexive by a
  literal "raise oneself" logic invisible in the English gloss.
- **Transitive verbs that look prepositional in English are not prepositional in Spanish**: buscar
  "look for," esperar "wait for," escuchar "listen to," mirar "look at" take a bare direct object in
  Spanish with NO preposition — unless that object is a person, which instead requires the
  **personal a** (not a generic preposition): *busco un libro* (no prep) vs. *busco a mi hijo*
  (personal a, not *busco para mi hijo*). This conflates two separate English-interference impulses
  (adding a preposition by analogy with the English gloss, or using the wrong preposition for
  personhood) into one single high-value rule.
- **"Exchange" verbs** (dar, decir, entregar, mostrar, enviar, mandar, pedir, etc.) — those denoting
  a transfer from subject to a second party — grammatically REQUIRE the indirect object pronoun
  before the verb even when the recipient is also separately named via an *a + noun* phrase (*Miguel
  me da el dinero (a mí)*) — omitting the pronoun while keeping only the *a*-phrase is a blunder in
  Spanish, the reverse of English, which treats the pronoun as optional once the full phrase is
  stated.
- **"Feeling" verbs** (gustar, encantar, fascinar, faltar, molestar) grammatically invert the English
  subject/object relationship: the thing causing the feeling is the GRAMMATICAL SUBJECT (often
  following the verb) and the person experiencing it is an indirect object — *me gustan los zapatos*
  = "the shoes appeal to me," not "I like the shoes" word-for-word. When the "subject" goes unstated
  and would be "it/they" in English, Spanish uses NO pronoun at all if the subject is a thing
  (blunder: *¿te los gustan?* — direct object pronouns like *lo/los* must never be used with this
  verb class) — only a genuine personal subject (*él/ella/ellos*) may be dropped-in as a stand-in
  pronoun. Generic/plural/noncount nouns functioning as this grammatical subject still require the
  definite article in Spanish even though English omits it for generalizations (*me gusta el
  chocolate*, not *me gusta chocolate*).
- **Advice/control verbs** (aconsejar, prohibir, permitir, etc.) take an indirect object pronoun AND
  a full subjunctive clause (*la maestra LES aconseja a los estudiantes QUE ESTUDIEN*) — a three-part
  structure (indirect object pronoun + optional a-phrase + subjunctive que-clause) that's easy to
  under-produce by translating "advises the students to study" too literally with an infinitive
  instead.
- **"Unplanned occurrence" verbs** (olvidársele, caérsele, perdérsele, quedársele, rompérsele,
  acabársele) use a reflexive-passive construction that frames the event as happening TO the
  affected person rather than being done BY them — *se me olvidó el libro* ("I forgot the book,"
  literally "the book forgot itself on me") — and critically, the noun involved is NEVER preceded by
  a possessive adjective in this construction, since the indirect object pronoun already encodes the
  "whose" relationship (blunder: *se me olvidó mi libro* — doubling up "me" and "mi" is redundant and
  wrong). This construction is a genuine grammaticalized way of linguistically disclaiming
  responsibility/agency for an accident — a register/pragmatic fact (softening blame) beyond the
  pure grammar point.

### Present tense: used for durative "have been doing" where English requires the present perfect (pp. 116-118)
One of the book's highest-value, most systematically documented false-friend patterns: where English
obligatorily uses the present perfect ("I have worked here for two years" / "this is the first time I
have been in Mexico" / "I haven't smoked in two months" / "I have worked here since January"), Spanish
uses the **present tense** instead, and a literal word-for-word present-perfect translation is a
genuine, repeatedly-flagged blunder across four distinct constructions:
1. Duration: *llevo dos años trabajando aquí* OR *hace dos años que trabajo aquí* OR *trabajo aquí
   desde hace dos años* — three equivalent patterns, never *he trabajado aquí por dos años*.
2. Time-since-last-occurrence: *llevo tres años sin ver a mi amigo* ("I haven't seen my friend in
   three years"), never *no he visto a mi amigo por tres años*.
3. Starting point: *trabajo aquí desde enero de 2003* ("I have worked here since January 2003"),
   never *he trabajado aquí desde enero de 2003*.
4. Counting occurrences: *es la segunda vez que come en nuestra casa* ("it's the second time he has
   eaten at our house"), never *es la segunda vez que ha comido...*.
This is a single unified mechanism (Spanish present tense covers a durative-since-the-past meaning
English needs the present perfect for) that a learner translating tense-for-tense will reliably get
wrong across all four surface patterns.

### Present tense: used for near-future requests, offers, promises, and scheduled/plotted narration (pp. 118-121)
Beyond ordinary present-tense uses shared with English, Spanish uses the bare present (not a future or
modal construction) for: making a request (*¿me ayudas?* = "will you help me?"), making an offer
(*¿te ayudo?* = "can I help you?"), making a promise/commitment (*te llamo esta noche* = "I'll call
you tonight") — all cases where English requires "will" or "can." *ir a* + infinitive is the normal
way to state a future plan (paralleling English "going to"), but — contrary to English, which can
freely substitute the present progressive for near-future plans ("I'm having dinner with him
tomorrow") — Spanish's present progressive (*estoy cenando...*) can NOT be used this way; only *voy a
cenar* is correct (blunder: *estoy cenando con él mañana* to mean a future plan). Spanish can narrate
*past* events/history in the present tense even in formal writing (*Cristóbal Colón descubre el Nuevo
Mundo en 1492* = "Columbus discovered the New World in 1492") — a usage English reserves for informal
joke-telling/plot-summary register, not formal history-writing; this is a genuine formal-register
difference the book calls out explicitly. *por poco*/*casi* + PRESENT tense expresses something that
almost happened but didn't (*por poco me caigo* = "I almost fell," never *por poco me caí* with a past
tense) — a counterintuitive tense choice, since English always uses past tense ("almost fell") for a
non-past-tense-marked Spanish construction.

### Preterite perfect: Spain vs. Latin America regional preference, explicitly named (p. 124)
**Explicit regional annotation**: using the preterite perfect (*he hablado*) for recently-completed
action ("I just talked to Juan") "is more common in Spain than in Latin America, where the present
tense of *acabar de* + infinitive is more often used for this purpose" (*acabo de hablar*). This is a
directly-named Spain/Latin-America usage-frequency distinction, not a hard dialectal boundary — worth
flagging for this project's regional-annotation coverage since the book rarely names a specific
country/region split this explicitly elsewhere.

### Preterite perfect: ya/todavía are structurally required in Spanish, optional in English (pp. 124-126)
"Already"/"yet" are optional emphasis words in English but *ya*/*todavía* are near-obligatory in
Spanish to convey the already-occurred/not-yet-occurred meaning at all (*no he escrito el trabajo
todavía*, risk of being misunderstood as a flat statement of fact rather than "not yet" without the
marker). The preterite perfect also does NOT cover two uses its English present-perfect cognate does:
counting "the Nth time" (*es la segunda vez que COMEMOS*, present tense, not *hemos comido* — directly
tying back to the present-tense-for-duration family above) and expressing ongoing duration (*hace diez
minutos que hablo con Juan*, not *he estado hablando... por diez minutos*, which would instead mean
the conversation is now OVER).

### Preterite vs. imperfect: the imperfect covers English state-verbs that look like simple past, not past progressive (pp. 135-137)
A dedicated ~20-verb table (comprender, creer, encantar, gustar, odiar, necesitar, parecer, pensar,
poseer, preferir, querer, recordar, saber, ser/estar, tener, ver, etc.) whose Spanish imperfect form
corresponds to a plain English past tense, NOT the past progressive, even though the imperfect is "in
the middle of a state" aspectually — *quería seguir durmiendo* = "I wanted to keep sleeping," never
"was wanting." This is the single most-reliable English-interference trap in the preterite/imperfect
system: English gives no surface signal (no "-ing") that these are ongoing states, so a learner has to
recognize semantically which verbs describe states vs. punctual events to pick the right Spanish past
tense. Includes an explicit transitive/intransitive minimal pair: *oler* intransitive = "to have a
smell" (*el cuarto olía mal* = "the room smelled bad," a state) vs. *oler* transitive = "to actively
smell something" (*quería oler tu perfume*); same split for *saber* intransitive ("to taste [a certain
way]," a state: *la carne sabía rica*) vs. *probar* (the separate verb for the active tasting action)
— a genuine lexical-aspect distinction the single English verb "to smell"/"to taste" collapses but
Spanish keeps structurally visible via tense choice and (for taste) verb choice.

### Preterite vs. imperfect: a closed list of verbs that change ENGLISH TRANSLATION, not just aspect, between the two tenses (pp. 137-138)
*conocer* imperfect "already knew [a person]" vs. preterite "met [for the first time]"; *tener*
imperfect "already had [a child]" vs. preterite "gave birth to"; *saber* imperfect "knew [a fact]" vs.
preterite "found out"; *estar* imperfect "was already [present]" vs. preterite "arrived" (in a context
implying punctual arrival); *querer* imperfect "wanted" vs. preterite (affirmative) "tried [but
failed]" / preterite negative "refused" (not merely "didn't want to"). *poder* imperfect "could
[describing the state of capability before resolution]" vs. preterite "could [and did/didn't,
specifically at the point of resolution]"; *tener que* parallels this same imperfect-vs-resolved
pattern for "had to." These are not simply aspectual nuances of the same English gloss — several
genuinely swap to an unrelated-looking English verb (*supo* = "found out," not any form of "know"),
making this list a real memorization point distinct from the general preterite/imperfect aspect rule.

### Ser + adjective vs. estar + adjective: meaning actually shifts, not just permanence vs. temporariness (pp. 87-89)
The book explicitly rejects the common "ser = permanent trait, estar = temporary state" teaching
heuristic as misleading, citing counterexamples like *es joven, soltero, gordo, pequeño, pobre* — all
clearly changeable states expressed with *ser*. Instead it presents an extensive list of adjectives
(bonito, callado, débil, despierto, divertido, frío, fuerte, gordo, listo, loco, nervioso, seguro,
verde, viejo, vivo) where **the English gloss itself changes** depending on which copula is used — e.g.
*ser listo* = "be smart" vs. *estar listo* = "be ready"; *ser vivo* = "be sharp/cunning" vs. *estar
vivo* = "be alive"; *ser frío* = "be cold-hearted" vs. *estar frío* = "be [physically] cold." This is a
meaningfully different (and more specific) framing than the simple permanent/temporary dichotomy
likely already present in this language's general grammar references.

### Intensifying adverbs and the re-/reque-/requete- prefix family (pp. 89-90)
Beyond the standard degree adverbs (*un poco, medio, bastante, muy, demasiado, tan... que, bien,
sumamente, extremadamente*), the book lists the colloquial productive prefix family *re-/reque-/
requete-* as an intensifier attached directly to an adjective (*recaro, requecaro, requetecaro* =
"very/really/super expensive"), explicitly presented alongside — and more casual-register than — the
adverbial forms. Also notes *demasiado* is better glossed "extremely" rather than "too," since it
doesn't carry English "too"'s negative-result implication (*demasiado caro* ≠ necessarily "too
expensive to buy").

### Por vs. para — extended, example-/idiom-dense treatment (pp. 278-283)
Beyond the standard por=cause/para=purpose contrast (covered by most grammar references), this book's
treatment is notable for: (1) an explicit distinction between **por ser** (explains a reason — "since
he's from London, he speaks English fluently") and **para ser** (makes a comparison relative to a
type/category — "for [being] a native English speaker, he speaks Spanish well") — a subtle contrast not
always drawn out explicitly elsewhere; (2) **"por indicates a more dramatic motive than para"** (bought
the house *por ella* = because of her needs, vs. *para ella* = as a gift for her) — a pragmatic/
connotational distinction beyond the textbook mechanical rule; (3) a substantial list of fixed
*por*-expressions (*por adelantado, por ahora, por cierto, por desgracia, por Dios, por ejemplo, por
escrito, por favor, por la fuerza, por lo menos, por medio de, por parejas, por si acaso*, and more)
worth treating as vocabulary in their own right.

### Negative constructions: Spanish requires concord, not avoidance, of negatives (pp. 328-329)
Unlike English (where "I don't have anything" avoids a second negative), Spanish **requires** a
negative word to either precede the verb alone (*Nadie está en la casa*) or follow a preceding *no*
(*No está nadie*) — omitting *no* when a negative word like *nadie/nada/nunca/tampoco/ningún* already
precedes the verb is itself a blunder (*No nadie está en casa* is wrong), as is leaving a negative word
after the verb with no preceding negative (*Tengo nada en la mano* is wrong — needs *No tengo nada*).

### English function words with no one-to-one Spanish translation: since/so/will/would/wish/wonder (pp. 323-326)
The book dedicates its Constructions section to showing that several extremely common English function
words each map to **multiple, semantically-conditioned, non-interchangeable** Spanish constructions
rather than one fixed translation — e.g. "will" variously becomes a present-tense question (making a
request), present tense (accepting a request), future tense (promise or prediction), depending on
pragmatic function, not literal word-for-word substitution; "would" has at least six distinct Spanish
realizations (preferir for "would rather," negarse a/no querer for refusal, imperfect for habitual past
action, conditional for hypothetical action, poder-conditional for polite requests, gustaría/quisiera
for "would like"). This "map the function, not the word" framing is the book's recurring methodological
point and is probably its single most transferable insight for a synthesis project modeling how a
conlang's modal/aspectual system might diverge from a simple word-for-word calque of English.

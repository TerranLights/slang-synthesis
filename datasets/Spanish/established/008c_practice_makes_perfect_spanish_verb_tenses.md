## Grammar points

### The imperative (Ch. 19)

A mood, not a tense — only "now" as a time frame. Five command forms in Spanish:

- **tú (affirmative regular)**: drop the -s from the 2nd-person-singular present indicative (hablas →
  ¡habla!). **tú (negative regular)**: yo-stem (yo form minus -o) + -es (-ar verbs) or -as (-er/-ir
  verbs) — the same standard orthographic changes (c>qu, g>gu, z>c) apply before this -e. **tú
  irregular** (only 8 verbs, different affirmative vs. negative): decir di/no digas, hacer haz/no hagas,
  ir ve/no vayas, poner pon/no pongas, salir sal/no salgas, ser sé/no seas, tener ten/no tengas, venir
  ven/no vengas.
- **usted**: single form for both affirmative and negative — yo-stem + -e (-ar) or -a (-er/-ir); only
  three irregulars: ir ¡Vaya!, saber ¡Sepa!, ser ¡Sea! (estar/dar carry a written accent — dé, esté — to
  distinguish from the homographs de/este).
- **ustedes**: identical to usted + n, single form for affirmative/negative, used for both
  formal and informal address throughout Spanish America.
- **vosotros (affirmative)**: drop the infinitive's final -r and add -d (except reflexives, which attach
  the reflexive pronoun directly to the r-less stem with no d: levantaos, sentaos). **vosotros
  (negative)**: yo-stem + -éis (-ar) or -áis (-er/-ir); ir/saber/ser mirror their usted forms; negative
  vosotros of stem-changing -ir verbs show e>i or o>u in the stem (pidáis, durmáis). vosotros commands
  are Spain-only.
- **nosotros ("let's")**: yo-stem + -emos (-ar) or -amos (-er/-ir); negative = same form + `no` before
  it. Stem-changing verbs (except stem-changing -ir) don't change in nosotros; stem-changing -ir verbs
  do (o>ue becomes o>u: durmamos; e>ie and e>i both become e>i: mintamos, pidamos). Standard
  orthographic changes apply. Reflexives drop the -s before adding -nos (levantémonos). Only three
  irregular nosotros commands: ir (¡Vamos!/¡No vayamos!, with a separate reflexive irse: ¡Vámonos!/¡No
  nos vayamos!), saber (¡Sepamos!), ser (¡Seamos!).

**Object pronouns with commands**: attach directly to the end of an *affirmative* command (with a
written accent added if needed to preserve original stress: ¡Háblame!, ¡Dímelo!); precede a *negative*
command as an independent word (¡No me mires!, ¡No se lo digas!). The RID rule (Reflexive, Indirect,
Direct order) governs multiple object pronouns in either case; when both indirect and direct pronouns
would start with l-, the indirect one becomes se (dáselo, not *dalelo) (pp. 197–209).

### The present subjunctive (Ch. 20)

Used whenever a main clause expresses one of eight kinds of uncertainty toward the subordinate clause's
content: (1) desire, (2) ignorance/doubt, (3) emotional statement, (4) impersonal opinion, (5)
uncompleted/pending action, (6) a vague or indefinite antecedent (an object/person not known to exist —
contrast `tengo una secretaria que es eficiente` [definite, indicative] vs. `quiero una secretaria que
sea eficiente` [indefinite, subjunctive]; personal `a` is dropped when the person may not exist), (7)
"perhaps"/"maybe" (acaso/quizá(s)/tal vex), (8) "even if" (aunque, only when the condition is unmet/
unknown — indicative once it's known to be true). Main clause (indicative) and subordinate clause
(subjunctive) are joined by `que`.

**Formation**: yo-form of present indicative → drop -o → add -e/-es/-e/-emos/-éis/-en (-ar) or
-a/-as/-a/-amos/-áis/-an (-er/-ir). This inherits any yo-form irregularity (conocer→conozca,
tener→tenga, hacer→haga, salir→salga) automatically. Stem-changing -ir verbs additionally shift in
nosotros/vosotros (o>ue→o>u: durmamos; e>ie/e>i→e>i: mintamos, pidamos) — unlike the indicative, where
nosotros/vosotros never shift. Six fully irregular verbs: dar (dé, des, dé, demos, deis, den), estar,
haber, ir, saber, ser.

Verbs of ignorance (dudar, no creer, etc.) are typically used negated; the *same* verb used
affirmatively (`creer que`) takes the indicative instead, since affirming belief removes the uncertainty
(pp. 211–231).

### The imperfect subjunctive (Ch. 21)

Same triggering situations as the present subjunctive, but for a past time frame — no
preterite/imperfect distinction exists within the subjunctive mood itself. **Formation**: take the
3rd-person-plural preterite, drop -ron, add either -ra/-ras/-ra/-ramos/-rais/-ran or (less common,
interchangeable) -se/-ses/-se/-semos/-seis/-sen; the book uses -ra throughout. The nosotros form takes a
written accent on the vowel before the ending (habláramos, dijéramos, fuéramos). Used when: the main
clause is in a past tense and needs the subjunctive; the main clause is present-tense but the
subordinate action is itself in the past; a hypothetical past if-clause (paired with a conditional main
clause — `Si yo fuera más alto, jugaría al básquetbol`; only *hypothetical* if-clauses take the
subjunctive — a true-information-seeking `si` clause, e.g. "I asked if he knew," stays indicative);
"as if"/"as though" (como si) clauses, always imperfect subjunctive regardless of the main clause's own
tense.

**Special idiomatic uses of querer and poder in the imperfect subjunctive**: `quisiera`/`pudiera` soften
a direct want/request into a more polite "would like"/"could you" — distinct from the true conditional
(which implies an actual unmet condition). `querer` in the imperfect subjunctive also independently means
"to wish" (quisiera tener un millón de dólares = "I wish I had a million dollars"; with a change of
subject, followed by que + a second imperfect-subjunctive verb: quisiera que estuvieras aquí = "I wish
you were here") (pp. 233–244).

### The future perfect tense (Ch. 22)

Compound, indicative: `haber` conjugated in the future (habré/habrás/habrá/habremos/habréis/habrán) +
past participle. Two uses: (1) an action that will be completed by a specified future point ("I will
have finished by Friday"); (2) probability/conjecture about the recent past — contrast `Él lo ha hecho`
(present perfect, plain report) vs. `Él lo habrá hecho` (future perfect, "he must have/probably has done
it") (pp. 245–249).

### The conditional perfect tense (Ch. 23)

Compound, indicative: `haber` conjugated in the conditional (habría/habrías/habría/habríamos/habríais/
habrían) + past participle. Expresses an action that *would have* happened but didn't, because some
condition wasn't met (always an implied or stated "if"/"but"). When the dependent clause follows "but," it
stays indicative; when it follows "if," it's imperfect subjunctive (`Él habría trabajado si no estuviera
cansado`). Also expresses conjecture/probability about the more remote past, or about clock time/date of
a past action (`Habría sido la una cuando él llegó` — "it must have been one o'clock") (pp. 251–256).

### The present perfect subjunctive (Ch. 24)

Compound, subjunctive-mood counterpart of the present perfect: `haber` conjugated in the present
subjunctive (haya/hayas/haya/hayamos/hayáis/hayan) + past participle. Used when a main clause (present or
future tense) that itself requires the subjunctive refers to an action that may have (or, from a future
vantage, will have) already taken place — contrast the plain indicative report `Yo sé que Juan ha comido`
with the subjunctive-triggered `Yo espero que Juan haya comido` (pp. 257–262).

### The pluperfect subjunctive (Ch. 25)

Compound, subjunctive-mood counterpart of the past perfect (pluperfect): `haber` conjugated in the
imperfect subjunctive (hubiera/hubieras/hubiera/hubiéramos/hubierais/hubieran) + past participle. Used
when a past-tense main clause requiring the subjunctive refers to an action that had only possibly taken
place before it. Also used in a hypothetical if-clause referring to a possibly-completed past action,
typically paired with a conditional-perfect main clause (`Yo no te habría preguntado si hubiera sabido la
respuesta`). `querer` meaning "to wish" (see Ch. 21) can likewise pair with the pluperfect subjunctive for
a wish about something that didn't happen (`Quisiera que nunca lo hubiera conocido` — "I wish I'd never
met him"); by convention such sentences typically don't open with the subject pronoun `yo` (pp. 263–270).

### The passive voice (Ch. 26)

Two forms. **Incomplete passive voice** (far more common in everyday speech): no named agent — English
equivalents include impersonal "they/you/one" and "[it] is done." Formed with `se` + verb in 3rd-person
singular or plural, the verb's number determined by the noun that follows it (word order is reversed
from English: `Se construye la casa` = "The house is built" [singular noun `la casa` follows]; `Se
construyen las casas` = "The houses are built"). This `se + 3rd-person-verb` template extends unchanged
across every tense/mood already covered in this book (preterite, imperfect, future, conditional, present/
past perfect, all four subjunctive tenses) — only the conjugated verb's tense/mood shifts.

**Complete passive voice**: used even when the agent is known, to place emphasis on the action rather
than the actor. Pattern: noun + `ser` (conjugated in whatever tense) + adjectival past participle
(agreeing in gender/number with the subject noun) + optional `por` + agent (`La Mona Lisa fue pintada por
Leonardo da Vinci`). Mentioning the agent with `por` is common but not mandatory (pp. 271–279).

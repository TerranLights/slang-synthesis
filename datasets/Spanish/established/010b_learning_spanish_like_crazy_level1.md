split, reflexive verbs in depth (Lecciones 13–15 are essentially a single extended reflexive-verb

## Grammar points

### Regular -ir present-tense conjugation (Lección 9)
Stem + -o/-es/-e/-imos/-en, modeled on "abrir" (abro/abres/abre/abrimos/abren) and reinforced with
"escribir" (Lección 10) and "vivir" (Lección 11) — completing the course's full set of three regular
present-tense conjugation classes (-ar Lecciones 1–2, -er Lección 5, -ir Lección 9). "Cerrar" is
introduced in the same lesson as an -ar verb with an e→ie stem change (cierro/cierras/cierra,
cerramos regular) purely by contrastive drilling, without the course naming the pattern.

### Informal vs. formal "how are you"/"what's your name" (Lección 9)
Parallel formal/informal question pairs are drilled directly: ¿Cómo estás? vs. ¿Cómo está usted?
and ¿Cómo te llamas? vs. ¿Cómo se llama usted? (the latter already introduced formally in Lección
8). The course commits to the informal member of each pair for the remainder of the lesson.

### ser for nationality/origin vs. es/son for simple identity (Lección 10–11)
The course explicitly states that when stating nationality or country of origin, "somos"/"son" (not
a hypothetical alternate form) must be used for "we are"/"you-plural/they are" — flagged directly:
"There are two words in Spanish that mean 'we are' [i.e. somos vs. the estar-based form used for
location/temporary states]... when referring to a nationality or country of origin, you must use
'somos.'" This is the course's first explicit (if informal) gesture at the ser/estar split, well
before Lección 12 makes the location use of estar concrete.

### ser (identity/origin) vs. estar (location) (Lección 12)
Lección 12 makes the ser/estar contrast concrete: "soy médico" (identity/occupation, ser) is
directly juxtaposed against "estoy en el banco" (location, estar). The course doesn't name the
"ser vs. estar" rule as a formal grammatical topic, but the entire lesson is structured around this
functional split — asking what someone does (ser-based "¿Qué haces?"/"Soy bombero") vs. asking
where something is (estar-based "¿Dónde está el banco?"/"Está al lado del hospital").

### allá vs. allí (Lección 12)
A genuine proximity distinction the course flags explicitly: "allá" = "over there" (general,
distant), "allí" = "RIGHT there" (more precise/closer) — no single English word distinguishes these,
so the course relies on capitalized emphasis in its own English glosses to mark the contrast.

### Reflexive verbs, full present-tense treatment (Lecciones 13–15)
Lecciones 13–15 form a single extended unit on reflexive verbs, covering an entire daily-routine
script: despertarse (wake up), levantarse (get up), lavarse (wash), afeitarse (shave), ducharse
(shower), vestirse/ponerse la ropa (get dressed), peinarse (comb hair), mirarse (look at oneself),
cepillarse/lavarse los dientes (brush teeth), quitarse la ropa (undress), acostarse (go to bed). The
reflexive pronoun (me/te/se/nos/se) is drilled across all six persons for each verb. Two points the
course makes explicit: (1) the pronoun can be dropped once established by context ("Tú te peinas"
→ "Te peinas"); (2) asking "and you?" about a reflexive action requires "¿Y a ti?" rather than the
non-reflexive "¿Y tú?" — introduced via "prepararse" ("¿Y a ti? ¿A ti te lleva mucho tiempo
prepararte?").

### "después de" / "antes de" + infinitive with reflexive verbs (Lecciones 13–15)
A genuinely productive sequencing template drilled extensively: "Después de + [reflexive infinitive
with attached reflexive pronoun], [subject] + [conjugated reflexive verb]" (e.g. "Después de
levantarme, me ducho" = "After getting up, I shower"). The reflexive pronoun attaches to the end of
the infinitive after "después de"/"antes de" rather than appearing as a separate pre-verbal
pronoun — a genuinely distinct placement rule from the conjugated-verb pattern, though the course
demonstrates it purely by repetition rather than naming the placement rule.

### llevar (impersonal, "to take [time]") (Lección 14)
"Llevar" is introduced in an impersonal indirect-object construction parallel to English "it takes
me..." — "A mí me lleva una hora prepararme" (lit. "to me it-takes an hour to-get-ready-myself" =
"it takes me an hour to get ready"). The indirect object pronoun (me/te/le/nos/les) marks the person
for whom the time is taken, while "llevar" itself stays third-person singular regardless of the
subject pronoun drilled ("les lleva a ellos mucho tiempo" = "it takes them a long time").

### Preterite (simple past) tense, first introduction (Lección 16)
The course's first past tense: regular -ar preterite endings -é/-ó/-amos/-aron contrasted directly
against the already-familiar present tense using paired sentences (e.g. present "Yo tomo el metro"
vs. preterite "Ayer tomé un taxi"). Two irregularities are drilled without being named as a rule:
(1) an orthographic g→gu spelling adjustment in the yo-form of "-gar" verbs (llegar → llegué, not
*llegé); (2) the highly irregular preterite of "hacer" (hice/hizo/hicimos/hicieron, sharing no stem
with the present-tense hago/hace). The course also explicitly notes that first-person-plural
("nosotros") forms are identical in present and preterite for -ar verbs ("Nosotros bailamos en un
club hoy" / "Nosotros bailamos en una discoteca ayer" — same form, different tense, distinguished
only by the "hoy"/"ayer" adverb), a genuinely important ambiguity point for a learner.

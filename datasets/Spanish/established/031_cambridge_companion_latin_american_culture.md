## Grammar points

### Language politics as imperial unifying tool (Ch.2, "Language politics" subsection, pp.61-62)

The chapter contains an explicit named subsection on language politics: despite the collapse of Spain's political empire (except Cuba, Puerto Rico, the Philippines, retained until 1898), a "linguistic empire" persisted — Spanish remained the common language of literature and state across former Spanish America even where political unity failed and wars were fought between Spanish-speaking nations (the War of the Pacific, 1879; the War of the Triple Alliance, 1864-70). The source credits Antonio Nebrija's 1492 grammar of Castilian as establishing language as "the enduring tool of empire," and cites Andrés Bello's 1847 *Gramática de la lengua castellana* as a 19th-century call for linguistic unification against regional/national fragmentation — a prescriptive-grammar-as-political-unity move directly comparable to the RAE/DRAE-standardization material already captured for Peninsular Spanish in
`028_spanish_language_today.md`.

### The castizo-to-colloquial shift in 1960s Latin American narrative (Ch.4/5 synthesis, p.102)

The source identifies a defining stylistic shift across the 20th century: Latin American narrative moved away from a literary Spanish register inherited from Spain (characterized as *castizo* — "pure" and grammatically correct) toward "local spoken dialects and colloquial language," driven partly by the influence of popular culture (film, radio, soap operas, song) on literary prose. Three concrete author/register pairings are named: Guillermo Cabrera Infante's *Tres tristes tigres* (1965) written in *habanero* (Havana city slang); Manuel Puig's novels (e.g. *Boquitas pintadas*, 1969) using provincial-Argentine "local lingos"; Julio Cortázar's fiction identified by its *porteño* (Buenos Aires) slang. The source frames slang specifically as "a primal zone of self-identification (and thus realism) for the Latin American reader" — i.e., register-as-identity-marker is treated as a defining mechanism of this literary period, not incidental color. A 1968 quote from novelist Beatriz Guido is cited as crystallizing the shift: the distinguishing feature of the Latin American novel was learning "to dare to write as one speaks."

### Oral/written-language tension as a literary device (Introduction, p.6)

Augusto Roa Bastos's *Yo el Supremo* (1974) is cited as a case study in which the novel's structure itself is built from "the tension between writing and orality, the written and the spoken word" — specifically figured as a tension between "the dominant oral language of Guaraní and the literary, official language of Spanish" in Paraguay. This is a distinct mechanism from simple bilingualism: one language (Guaraní) is cast in the oral/spoken role and the other (Spanish) in the literary/official role for a single society, a diglossia-like split worth cross-referencing against any Guaraní-specific diglossia material found elsewhere in this project.

## Grammar points

### Literary vernacular-elevation as a recurring register-inversion device
Three independent cases in this chunk (Ch.6's *fala impura*/Macunaíma, Ch.7's Oswald de Andrade "lingua errada/certa do povo" inversion, and Ch.7's Gauchesque poetry/*Martín Fierro*) show the same underlying move across both Brazilian-Portuguese and Argentine-Spanish literary modernism: take a stigmatized, non-standard oral/popular speech variety and deliberately elevate it to literary/national-canon status, explicitly inverting which variety counts as "correct." This is the project's `register_inversion` derivation type playing out at the level of an entire national literary movement rather than a single lexical item — worth flagging to the mechanics-analysis phase as a productivity pattern, not just an isolated example.

### A lexicalized idiolect: cantinflismo
Cantinflismo/cantinflear (Mexican Spanish, named after comedian Cantinflas) is a rare, well-documented real-world instance of a single performer's distinctive speech style becoming a permanent, dictionary-attested common noun/verb in general usage — independently corroborated twice in this same source (Ch.8 and Ch.12). This is a concrete model for one pathway by which new slang vocabulary can enter a language: not gradual drift, but a named, dateable origin point tied to one iconic speaker, later generalizing to describe anyone's evasive/rambling talk.

### Dialect non-interchangeability in pan-Hispanic media (1930 dubbing case)
The Paramount Joinville dubbing failure is documented, dated, and specific: even where dialects are mutually intelligible, a Mexican audience's rejection of Argentine- or Madrid-accented dubbing demonstrates that regional Spanish varieties carry enough social/identity weight that substituting one for another in media is not neutral. This corroborates (from an independent, non-linguistics source — a cultural-studies companion, not a grammar reference) the general finding already building across this wave's dispatch that Spanish's regional varieties are treated by speakers as socially non-equivalent, not just phonetically distinct.

### Colonial-era ethnic-dialect representation in verse (villancico de negro)
Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz's *negrilla* villancicos (1680s-1710s) are the earliest documented case in this project's Spanish-language corpus of a canonical author deliberately representing a stigmatized ethnic/racial speech variety ("habla de negros") in literary form, with associated musical/rhythmic markers. Worth retaining as a historical reference point for how literary dialect-representation conventions (and their attendant ethical/othering dimensions) have existed in the Spanish-speaking world since the colonial period, not just in modern slang.

## Grammar / sociolinguistic points

### Spanglish is internally heterogeneous, not monolithic
The author explicitly rejects treating "Spanglish" as one uniform code: he stratifies it by **nationality** (Cuban-American vs. Dominicanish vs. Nuyorrican-spanglish), **locality** (Istlos, Sagüesera as geographic clippings), **ethnicity/in-group** (chale as specifically Chicano, chompa as specifically Nuyorican), **generation/class** (Ganga Spanglish as a youth/urban-gang register distinct from the "high-brow" literary code-mixing of writers like Giannina Braschi), and **domain** (Cyber-Spanglish as an internet-specific register, sports vocabulary as a specialized borrowing domain). This stratification is directly useful as a worked example of how a single named contact-language label can decompose into several distinct, independently-taggable register/regional varieties (p.338–340).

### Code-switching as intra-sentential mixing, not just borrowing
Two literary excerpts are used to show a qualitatively different phenomenon from single-word borrowing: full intra-sentential Spanish/English code-switching, where clause-level syntax alternates language mid-sentence (Ana Lydia Vega's "Pollito Chicken": *"Lo que la decidió fue el breathtaking poster de Fomento que vio en la travel agency del lobby de su building..."*; Giannina Braschi's *Yo-yo Boing!* dialogue: *"Tú tienes las keys... Just open it, and make it fast."*). The author frames this as structurally distinct from the single-word Spanglishisms cataloged elsewhere in the chapter — a continuum from isolated loanwords up to full bilingual discourse (p.332, p.339–340).

### Productive borrowing mechanisms attested
Three distinct integration mechanisms for English loanwords are each exemplified multiple times:
1. **Phonetic Hispanicization with no morphological change** — *el ofsait* (offside), *el tuchdaun* (touchdown), *el nokaut* (knockout), *el maus* (mouse), *guachiman* (watchman), *réferi* (referee): the English word is respelled to match Spanish phonology/orthography but takes no further inflection beyond the Spanish definite article.
2. **Verbing via Spanish infinitive morphology** — *chatear* (to chat), *forwardear* (to forward): an English verb root receives the Spanish -ar infinitive ending and then inflects as a regular Spanish verb. The author calls this part of "Cyber-Spanglish."
3. **Direct morphosyntactic incorporation of English nouns with Spanish articles/plurals** — *los doubles* (tennis doubles): the English plural noun is retained verbatim but governed by Spanish determiner morphology.

### Historical precedent: indigenous-language borrowing as a parallel case
The author draws an explicit parallel between today's English→Spanish Spanglish borrowing and the much older Nahuatl→Spanish borrowing pathway (*aguacate*, *molcajete*, *huipil*, *canoa*), tracing it to Antonio de Nebrija's 1492 *Gramática* and 1495 *Vocabulario español–latino* — the first systematic Spanish lexicography, compiled in the same years Spain was absorbing New World vocabulary. The point made explicitly: Spanish has a five-centuries-long documented history of absorbing contact-language vocabulary (first Indigenous-American, now English), and Spanglish is this same mechanism operating in the present rather than a novel "corruption" (p.333–337).

### Spanglish is acquiring its own morphosyntax, not just a lexical stopgap
Citing linguist Ana Celia Zentella (*Growing Up Bilingual*) and others (C. Alvarez, L. Torres), the author states that specialists now treat Spanglish as actively fixing its own syntax rather than remaining a transitional interlanguage stage for Spanish speakers acquiring English — i.e., it is argued to be a stable, self-perpetuating linguistic system in its own right, reproduced across generations rather than fading as speakers anglicize (p.341). No further grammatical detail (e.g. specific syntactic rules) is given in this chapter — this is a claim/pointer to the cited specialist literature, not a worked-out grammar.

### Register stigma and its reversal
The chapter documents an explicit historical **register-inversion** trajectory: Spanish/Spanglish code-mixing was treated as a source of shame for US Latino schoolchildren as recently as the 1950s (quoting Richard Rodriguez's *Hunger of Memory*: "Spanish marked my separateness from los otros, los gringos in power"), but by the time of writing (2004) the author describes this stigma as "long gone," with Spanglish used "without apology" by contemporary musicians and literary authors (p.332–333). This is a documented register-stigma-to-acceptance shift with an approximate 50-year span, directly relevant to the project's interest in how slang/non-standard registers gain legitimacy over time.

## Grammar/Discourse points

### Regional voseo detail beyond the bare paradigm
Vos survives with three distinct conjugation patterns depending on region: (1) archaic
second-person-plural endings *-áis/-éis/-ís* (parts of Bolivia, Costa Rica, rural Panama, Venezuela,
Chile); (2) the "River Plate" pattern *-ás/-és/-ís* (Argentina, Paraguay, Uruguay, Central America);
(3) vos conjugated with the ordinary tú ending (Chile, Ecuador, Bolivia Andean regions: *vos eres,
vos tienes*). Two emergent systems are flagged as linguistically "in the making": *el voseo culto de
Chile* (educated young urbanites reviving vos-forms while avoiding or mixing with tú), and an
emergent three-way *tú/vos/usted* system in El Salvador where each pronoun occupies a distinct point
on an intimacy-to-respect scale (vos = most intimate, tú = familiar-but-not-intimate, usted = most
respectful) — genuinely different from the usual two-way tuteo/voseo split. Conversely, eastern/
central Colombia and rural Panama show paradigm *reduction*, with usted covering both familiar and
formal functions. (pp. 124–126)

### Power and solidarity semantics in address choice
Brown & Gilman's (1960) T/V framework (power semantic: the more powerful party uses T and receives
V; solidarity semantic: perceived closeness licenses mutual T regardless of power) is applied across
multiple Spanish-speaking sociolinguistic studies cited in this chapter (Puerto Rico and Colombia:
Lambert & Tucker 1976; rural Castilla-La Mancha: Moreno Fernández 1986; New Mexico: Jaramillo 1990).
A cross-cutting finding: *domain* (the nature of the communicative event) can override fixed role
relationships in determining T/V choice — e.g. the same two people may use V in a formal meeting but
T when socializing. (pp. 126–131)

### Register-by-genre: administrative, legal, political, journalistic Spanish
The source analyzes four genres as having distinct, partially-overlapping linguistic signatures:
- **Administrative**: moving (unevenly) toward "plain Spanish" — first-person forms replacing
  supplicant-framed third-person constructions, fewer archaic honorific abbreviations — but with
  some institutions (e.g. tax offices) retaining Francoist-era archaic formulae (*V.S., suplicar,
  dignarse*) into the 1990s.
- **Legal**: maximally formal and resistant to plain-language reform; morphosyntactic markers include
  heavy abstract-noun use, frequent gerunds/infinitives/past participles, complex prepositional
  phrases, dense subordination, the archaic future subjunctive (*fuere*), and archaic postposition of
  clitic *se* (*Líbrese testimonio…*). Lexically marked by terms restricted to law (*intestado,
  bastantear*), core words with narrowed legal senses (*actor* = plaintiff), Latinisms (*usufructo,
  prima facie*), and context-dependent polysemous terms (*caso, auto*).
- **Political oratory**: characterized across ideologically opposed speakers (Franco, Felipe
  González, Salinas de Gortari, Fidel Castro) by a shared toolkit — high-frequency abstract nouns
  functioning as "empty" political symbols to be filled by whichever ideology is speaking (*Estado,
  pueblo, libertad, patria, democracia*), rhetorical repetition and gemination, and marked/
  literary-flavored word order (adjective-preposing). EU-level Spanish additionally shows a distinct
  "Eurospeak" lexical layer (borrowings/calques from English and French) and heavy passive-with-se
  use to diffuse agency.
- **Journalistic/newspaper**: shares a supranational register across the Spanish-speaking press but
  with systematic cross-Atlantic syntactic differences — Mexican/Latin American headlines prefer
  verb-fronted VSO order and third-person-plural agent-avoidance; Peninsular Spanish prefers SVO or
  fronts the past participle instead; both sides show some "telegraphese" (article suppression), more
  pronounced in the Mexican press. (pp. 135–155)

### Taboo, euphemism, and political correctness as a productive mechanism system
This section is the single most slang-synthesis-relevant stretch of the whole book excerpt. It
documents, with sourced studies (chiefly Calero Fernández 1991, Azevedo 1992, Vigara Tauste 1994,
Briz 1998), a systematic male/female asymmetry in Spanish taboo vocabulary — far more (and more
positively connotated) terms derived from male anatomy than female, and roughly 10x more
euphemisms/insults for male homosexuality than female. It also documents three parallel
*euphemism_chain* sequences (religious-blasphemy-avoidance for *hostia* and *Dios*; sexual-taboo
desemanticized interjections *carajo* and *joder*) each spawning a string of phonetically-similar but
progressively "safer" substitute forms — a directly reusable derivation pattern for this project's
synthesis phase. The political-correctness subsection separately documents a *register_inversion/
taboo_inversion*-adjacent pattern: PC terminology in Spain is explicitly modeled on Anglo-Saxon
precedent (gay, person-first disability phrasing) rather than organically Spanish-internal, and in at
least one documented case (*invidente* vs. *ciego*) the PC-coined euphemism was actively rejected by
the community it was meant to describe as more insulting than the plain term — a cautionary data
point about euphemism not always reducing stigma. (pp. 155–160)

### Conversation structure and pragmatics as register markers
The chapter catalogs conversation-analysis structures (adjacency pairs, preferred/dispreferred
response, self-/other-repair, turn allocation and overlap tolerance, closures, pre-sequences,
back-channelling) and ties several of them to explicit cross-variety contrasts: Danish vs. Mexican
turn-taking expectations (Kjaerback 1998); Madrid's greater tolerance of conversational overlap vs.
Mexico's; and — most richly documented — Peninsular vs. Ecuadorian pragmatic style in both directive
speech and telephone-call conventions, where Ecuadorian Spanish consistently favors deference,
indirectness, and ellipsis of the bare request (attributed partly to Quechua contact influence on the
use of the future tense for softened commands), while Peninsular Spanish favors directness and
positive politeness. The chapter frames this as a real risk of cross-cultural miscommunication
*between different Spanish-speaking communities themselves*, not just between Spanish and English.
(pp. 161–178)

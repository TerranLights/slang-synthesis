## Grammar points

### Interrogative and Exclamatory Adjectives — qué, cuál(es), cuánto/-a/-os/-as (Ch.4, p.190)

Not previously captured anywhere in this corpus as a determiner-use system (prior hits are all
pronoun or vocabulary uses). The book treats these three words as "indefinite adjectives that become
interrogative/exclamatory adjectives when placed before a noun," always carrying an accent mark:
- **qué** — invariable, = "what." In exclamations before a noun, Spanish drops the indefinite article
  that English equivalently inserts: *¡Qué tragedia!* ("What a tragedy!," not "What a a tragedy").
  Three sub-uses depending on what follows: *qué* + adjective = "how" (*¡Qué triste!* "How sad!");
  *qué* + verb = "what" (*¡Qué te parece!* "What do you think!"); *qué* + adverb = "how" (*¡Qué bien
  lo hizo!* "How well he did it!").
- **cuál/cuáles** — varies for number, not gender. Used mainly as an interrogative *pronoun* (without
  a following noun) rather than as an adjective; when a noun is present, *qué* is more common, and
  *cuál de + noun* signals a selection from a set (*¿Cuál de estas recetas quieres?* "Which one of
  these recipes do you want?").
- **cuánto/-a/-os/-as** — full gender/number agreement, = "how much/how many": *¡Cuánto arroz fue
  importado!* "How much rice was imported!"

### Numeral taxonomy — six categories, including distributive and collective (Ch.4, p.138)

The book splits numerals into six categories, two of which (distributive, collective) are not
captured by this corpus's existing cardinal/ordinal/fraction/multiple coverage (014):
1. Cardinal (*dos plantas* "two plants")
2. Ordinal (*la tercera carta* "the third letter")
3. Partitive (*la octava parte* "the eighth part")
4. Multiple/proportional (*el doble* "double")
5. **Distributive** (*sendos/as* "one each, respectively") — see Vocabulary table; this word has no
   close English equivalent and assigns one item to each of several people/things.
6. **Collective** (*un centenar de caballos* "about a hundred horses") — approximative round-number
   nouns (centenar ≈ 100, docena = 12, etc.), distinct from exact cardinals.

## Chapter 17 — Prepositions

### Simple prepositions inventory, including the two archaic/obsolete ones (p.401)

The book lists 19 simple prepositions as a single inventory: a, ante, bajo, **cabe** (marked
explicitly as obsolete, literary-only), con, contra, de, desde, en, entre, hacia, hasta, para, por,
según, sin, **so** (archaic; appears later in the book almost only inside fixed phrases), sobre,
tras. See Vocabulary table for cabe/so.

### Simple prepositions as compounding prefixes (p.422)

Nearly all simple prepositions can prefix onto other words to form new lexical compounds (not
prepositional phrases) — e.g. *acortar* (a- + cortar, "to shorten"), *contradecir* (contra- + decir,
"to contradict"), *entrever* (entre- + ver, "to see imperfectly"), *sinvergüenza* (sin- + vergüenza,
"rascal," lit. "without shame"), *traspapelar* (tras- + papel, "to misplace papers"). This
prefixation pattern (simple preposition as derivational prefix) is a distinct word-formation
mechanism from the compound-preposition phrases below — worth flagging for the mechanics-analysis
phase as a productive Spanish derivation strategy.

### Compound prepositions — two structural categories (p.423–425)

Compound prepositions (*frases preposicionales*) are 2+ word units. The book splits them into:
- **A. Those with an equivalent simple preposition** — e.g. *delante de / enfrente de / frente a*
  all paraphrase *ante* ("in front of"); *debajo de / por debajo de* paraphrase *bajo* ("under"); *a
  través de* paraphrases movement-*por* ("through"); *a pesar de* ("in spite of") sits loosely near
  *contra*. Each simple preposition (ante, bajo, con, contra, en, entre, hacia, para, por, según,
  sobre, tras) gets its own small cluster of multi-word equivalents.
- **B. Those with NO equivalent simple preposition** — fixed phrases that exist only as compounds: *a
  excepción de* ("with the exception of"), *a fuerza de* ("by dint of/through perseverance"),
  *alrededor de* ("around"), *ante todo* ("above all"), *en lugar de / en vez de* ("instead of"),
  *fuera de* ("outside of"), *junto a* ("close to, next to"), *lejos de* ("far from"), *más allá de*
  ("beyond").
- **C. Two prepositions stacked together** for an idiomatic sense not derivable from either alone:
  *de por medio* ("acting as a mediator/intermediary"), *de a* + number ("bills/units of [value]" —
  *billetes de a diez*, "ten-dollar bills"), *para con* ("toward, in one's dealings with" — more
  formal/distanced than plain *con*), *por ante* ("before/in front of," used with formal/legal
  authorities — *declarar por ante el juez*), *de entre* ("out from among"), *tras de* ("besides, in
  addition to," distinct from *tras* alone = "after/behind").

### Functions of prepositions — grammatical-case mapping (p.425)

The book frames prepositions as markers of underlying grammatical cases (a pedagogical device useful
for comparative/historical-linguistics framing, though Spanish itself has no surface case morphology
on nouns): *de* ≈ genitive (possession); *a* and *para* ≈ dative (indirect object); *a* ≈ accusative
(personal *a* before a direct object); and *sin/contra/por* (+ "all of the prepositions," per the
book) ≈ ablative (circumstance) — *Vino sin llamar* "He came without calling," *Lo mandó por correo
aéreo* "He sent it via airmail."

### General notes on preposition usage — four syntactic rules (p.426)

1. Prepositions precede the noun they govern.
2. A preposition attached to a relative pronoun (e.g. *en el cual*, *a la que*, *con quien*) follows
   — i.e., is repeated with — the noun it ultimately refers back to, inside the relative clause: *El
   jardín, en el cual había rosas, era pequeño.*
3. **Two prepositions cannot share a single following noun by being joined with a conjunction.** Each
   preposition needs its own (possibly pronominalized) complement: *Me gusta el pastel con sal o sin
   ella* (correct) vs. the ungrammatical *con o sin sal*; similarly *de Perú y para ese país* (not
   *de y para Perú*), *al jefe... de él* (not *al y de el jefe*), *sobre la mesa o debajo de ella*
   (not *sobre o debajo de la mesa*). This is a concrete, teachable well-formedness constraint not
   seen stated this explicitly elsewhere in the corpus.

### Uses of the preposition *a* — 15-point rule system for the personal *a* (p.427–431)

The book's most systematic single-preposition treatment in the corpus. The personal *a*
(accusative-marking before a direct object) is required:
1. Before a proper name referring to a human, fictional character, or personified animal/pet (*Ella
   ama a Juan*; *Yo quiero a mi caballo* — a named/personally-regarded horse).
2. Before wildlife specifically when the subject has a personal preference for/relationship with
   that animal (*Me gusta alimentar a los pájaros que vienen al jardín* — contrast *No me gusta ver
   las ardillas cerca de la casa*, no personal stake implied).
3. Before a human direct object only when that person is individually singled out, not a generic
   class member (*Quiero a mi abuelo* vs. *Quiero una secretaria que sepa inglés*, no *a*).
4. Before **each** noun when the direct object is a coordinated list of people (*Reconozco a María y
   a Elena*).
5. Before a collective noun representing human beings (*El actor oía al público*).
6. Whenever omitting *a* would create genuine subject/object ambiguity between two nouns that could
   each plausibly be either — the book's worked example *El niño corre el ratón* is genuinely
   ambiguous ("The boy runs after the mouse" / "The mouse runs after the boy") until *a*
   disambiguates: *El niño corre al ratón* = unambiguously "the boy chases the mouse."
7. After *ir* (to go), whether followed by an infinitive or a noun.
8. After verbs of motion when a place is named (*Ayer llegó a Dallas*).
9. After certain verbs specifically to shift their meaning (*faltar* "to be lacking" → *faltar a* "to
   miss [an obligation]"; *darse* "to hit oneself" → *darse a* "to devote oneself to").
10. In the *al* + infinitive construction marking a precise simultaneous moment ("upon doing X" — *Al
    salir de la oficina, me caí*).
11. Before *alguien*/*nadie* (always people-referring indefinite pronouns).
12. Before *alguno/a/os/as* and *ninguno/a* as pronouns, when referring to people.
13. Before *alguno/a/os/as* and *ninguno/a* as adjectives, when referring to people.
14. To mark a price (*El sillón está a dos mil pesos*) — **except** after *costar* and *valer*, which
    never take *a* (*El sillón cuesta dos mil*, no *a*).
15. To mark a specific clock time (*a las tres*).

Three explicit **non-uses** round out the system: personal *a* is never used after *dejar*/
*abandonar* when the object is a place (*Dejé Caracas*); never after *ir* and motion verbs when
followed by the adverbs *ahí/acá/allí/allá* (*Fuimos allí*); and never before a place-name direct
object unless the verb specifically requires it (*Juan de Garay fundó Buenos Aires*, no *a*, vs.
*Viajamos a Colombia*, *a* required because *viajar a* is the fixed verb+preposition pairing). This
is a considerably fuller rule-set for the personal *a* than any prior source in this corpus.

### Uses of *con* — four discrete senses beyond basic "with" (p.432)

Besides the default "with" sense (animate/inanimate/abstract complement), the book isolates: a
health-complaint sense (*Estoy con dolor de cabeza*, "I have a headache" — lit. "I am with a
headache"); a state-of-mind sense (*Está con pena de él*, "She feels sorry for him"); and a
manner-adverbial substitute for *-mente* (*Habló con claridad* = *Habló claramente*, "He spoke
clearly" — offered as a stylistic/register alternative to the adverb suffix, useful alongside the
corpus's existing *-mente* coverage).

### Uses of *de* — 16-point sense inventory (p.432–434)

The single densest preposition entry in the chapter. Senses, each with the book's own worked example:
possession; authorship of a creative work (*una novela de Cervantes*); origin/nationality (with or
without *ser*); point of origin with verbs of movement (*Partió de Miami*); adjectival/qualifying
phrases (*la chica de pelo crespo*, "the girl with curly hair"); material composition (*un anillo de
oro*); *than* in number comparisons (*más de cuarenta sillas*); the *de* + infinitive construction as
an equivalent of an English *if*-clause (*De saberlo, hubiera comprado...*, "If I had known, I would
have bought..." — a genuinely non-obvious construction not seen elsewhere in this corpus); container
contents/purpose (*una taza de té*; *gorra de baño* "shower cap"); time-of-day phrases (*de noche*,
*de mañana*); measurement (*dos centímetros de espesor*, "two centimeters thick"); manner (*de
prisa*, *de memoria*); cause (*Se murió de pena*, "She died of grief"); quantity/portion (*un poco de
leche*); and a descriptive-apposition idiom placing the adjective before *de* + the person's
name/description (*el bueno de mi hermano*, "my good brother" — a construction pattern, adjective-de-
noun for affectionate/ironic characterization of a person, not captured elsewhere).

**Note on scope limit:** the "Uses of Specific Prepositions" section, per its own structure, continues
past *de* to cover *desde* and *en* in similarly granular (though shorter) detail before the chapter
ends; those two sub-entries were skimmed rather than fully itemized here since their senses
substantially overlap the `a`/`de`/`con` material already extracted and existing corpus coverage of
*en* (location/time/means) — flagging in case a future pass wants the delta double-checked.

## Chapter 18 — Adverbs

### Classification systems — qualifying vs. determinative, and three cross-cutting axes (p.435)

Primary split: **qualifying adverbs** (*calificativos* — mejor, bien, claro, así) describe how an
action happens, vs. **determinative adverbs** (*determinativos*), a closed set further split into
demonstrative (aquí/acá), interrogative (¿cuándo?/¿dónde?/¿cuánto?/¿cómo?), relative
(cuando/donde/como/cuanto/cual), and indefinite (quizá, dondequiera, nada). Any single adverb can
also be cross-classified by meaning (place/time/manner/quantity/sequence/affirmation/negation/doubt —
8 sub-types, each answering a named question like ¿dónde? or ¿cuánto?), by formation (simple vs.
compound), and by origin (primitive vs. derivative). This three-axis classification grid is more
systematic than any prior adverb treatment in the corpus (015 only covers -mente morphology; 006
only covers adjectives-used-as-adverbs).

### Place-adverb near/far pair distinctions — aquí/acá and allí/allá (p.436–437)

Not a simple synonym pair: *aquí* names a more precisely-defined "here" than *acá*, which pairs with
intensifying phrases like *más acá* ("closer") and *un poco más acá* ("a little closer").
Symmetrically, *allí* is a more definite "there" than *allá*, which takes *más allá* ("further away")
and idiomatically *el más allá* ("the afterlife, life after death"). Both pairs can take *mismo* for
an exact-point reading (*aquí mismo* "right here"). The book also gives systematic English-to-Spanish
mappings for compound locatives: "up here/there" = *aquí/allí arriba*; the English directional suffix
*-ward(s)* maps to *hacia* or *para* + direction word (*hacia arriba* / *para arriba* "upward"); and
English's indefinite-place suffix *-where* (anywhere, somewhere, nowhere) has **no single-word
Spanish equivalent** at all — Spanish always uses a full prepositional phrase instead (*en alguna
parte*, *en ningún lugar*, *en cualquier sitio*, etc., with four interchangeable noun choices:
parte/lugar/sitio/lado). This typological gap (no Spanish morpheme for "-where") is a genuinely
useful contrastive-linguistics data point not seen stated this explicitly elsewhere.

### The aun/aún/todavía/ya/nunca-jamás system — accent-driven sense-splitting (p.438–440)

A dense cluster of time/aspect adverbs whose senses hinge on a single accent mark or on
affirmative-vs-negative context, laid out more exhaustively than elsewhere in the corpus:
- **aún** (accented) = "still/yet," synonym *todavía*; usable in both affirmative ("still") and
  negative ("yet") clauses, with specific placement rules in compound/progressive tenses (never
  between the auxiliary and main verb in perfect tenses; flexible before/after/between in
  progressive tenses).
- **aun** (no accent) = "even/including/also," synonyms *hasta*, *incluso*, *también*.
- **ni aun** / **ni siquiera** = "not even" — placed before the subject for subject-focused emphasis,
  before the verb for action-focused emphasis, and may be split before-and-after the verb, but never
  used as a whole fixed unit positioned after the verb.
- **ya** = "already" with affirmative verbs; shifts to "no longer/not anymore" with negative verbs
  (optionally reinforced by *más*); cannot sit between *haber* and the past participle in perfect
  tenses, but is positionally flexible in progressive tenses.
- **nunca jamás** ("never ever," emphatic doubling) vs. **nunca más** ("no longer," a one-time
  cessation sense) — distinct meanings the simple glosses don't capture. Note: *jamás* + *más* as a
  combination does not exist in Spanish.

### The -mente suffix — formation rules and multi-adverb reduction rule (p.443–446)

Formation: added to the feminine form of an adjective (*blanda* → *blandamente*), to adjectives
ending in -e (*suave* → *suavemente*), or to adjectives ending in a consonant (*fácil* →
*fácilmente*); a written accent on the base adjective is preserved (*débil* → *débilmente*). The
corpus's existing -mente coverage (015) doesn't include the **reduction rule for stacked adverbs**,
which is genuinely new: when two -mente adverbs modify the same verb and are joined by a conjunction,
only the *second* one keeps -mente, the first appears as the bare feminine adjective (*Se expresó
sabia y claramente*, not *sabiamente y claramente*) — in spoken Spanish the *con* + noun paraphrase is
preferred instead. With three or more stacked adverbs, only the *last* keeps -mente. A past
participle functioning as an adjective can also function directly as an adverb without any suffix
(*Llegó preocupado*, "He arrived worried[ly]").

### Position of adverbs — muy vs. mucho vs. bien as intensifiers (p.447–448)

*Muy* (short form of *mucho*) intensifies adjectives/most adverbs (*muy grande*, *muy bien*); *mucho*
itself (or superlative *muchísimo*) is used instead when the modified word is *elided* from the
sentence (*¿Está nerviosa? — Sí, mucho.*); and *bien* substitutes for *muy* specifically for emphasis
(*Está bien interesante esta película*, "This film is really interesting"). *Mucho* (not *muy*) is
required before the irregular comparatives *mejor/peor/más/menos/mayor/menor* (*mucho mejor*, never
*muy mejor*).

### Adverbial phrases — idiomatic multi-word adverbs (p.449)

A sampler of fixed idiomatic phrases functioning as single adverbs, several of them vivid/colorful
enough to be worth flagging for a later slang-mechanics pass even though they're presented here as
standard (non-slang) idiom: *a paso de tortuga* ("at a snail's pace," lit. "at tortoise's pace");
*más viejo que Matusalén* ("as old as Methuselah," i.e., "ancient"); *como escupida de músico* ("very
fast," lit. "like a musician's spit" — an obscure folk-idiom the book offers no etymology for);
*donde el diablo perdió el poncho* ("very far away," lit. "where the devil lost his poncho" — a
Southern-Cone-flavored idiom given without regional attribution in this source); *a lo mejor*
("maybe"); *sin más ni menos* ("without further ado").

### Functions of adverbs — five non-adverbial roles (p.449)

Adverbs can function as: nouns (*Nunca es un término que no existe en su vocabulario* — "never" used
as the subject noun); adjectives (*Quiere mucha atención*); pronouns (*No gana mucho*, "mucho"
standing in for an elided noun like "money"); interjections (*¡Abajo!* "Down [with it]!"); and
relative pronouns (*donde*, *como*, *cuando* each glossed as "where/how/when (= en la que / de la
manera en que / en que)").

### Comparison of adverbs — verb-based vs. adverb-based, equality vs. inequality (p.450–453)

A fuller comparison system than the adjective-comparison coverage already on file (006/014), built
around four distinct constructions: (1) inequality between two verbs' degree, via *más/menos que*
right after the verb (*El perro come más que el gato*); (2) equality between two verbs, via *tanto
como* right after the verb (*Este departamento trabaja tanto como el de finanzas*); (3) inequality
between adverbs modifying different subjects, via *más/menos* + adverb + *que* (*La directora habló
más claro que la maestra*); (4) equality between adverbs, via *tan* + adverb + *como* (*La bailarina
baila tan delicadamente como su acompañante*). Each has a parallel negation rule: negating an
equality construction (*no... tanto como* / *no... tan...como*) converts it into an inequality
reading, and reversing subject order preserves meaning. Superlatives of adverbs use *sumamente*,
*extremadamente*, *inmensamente*, or the *-ísimo* suffix (*poquísimo*, *lejísimos* — note the book
flags that colloquial usage commonly drops the final -s from *lejísimos* when referring to something
very far away).

## Chapter 19 — Conjunctions (p.455–462)

No prior file in this corpus has a systematic coordinating/subordinating conjunction inventory. The
book splits conjunctions first by form (simple/one-word like *pero*; compound/two-word like *aunque*;
conjunctive phrases/3+ words like *con tal de que*), then by function into coordinating (5 subtypes)
and subordinating (6 subtypes).

### Coordinating conjunctions — 5 subtypes (link independent clauses)

- **Adversative** (opposition): *aunque* (although), *pero* (but), *sino* (but rather — the corpus's
  existing sino-vs-pero distinction from 014/015 is corroborated here), *ni siquiera* (not even), *a
  pesar de que* (in spite of), *antes bien* (on the contrary).
- **Continuative** (discourse continuation): *ahora bien*, *ahora pues*, *pues* — all roughly "now
  then / so."
- **Copulative** (joins words/phrases): *y* (and) / **e** (and, used instead of *y* before a word
  starting with *i-* or *hi-*, e.g. *notas e ilustraciones*, *aguja e hilo* — the *y*→*e* phonetic
  dissimilation rule); *ni* (neither/nor); *que* (that/than).
- **Disjunctive** (exclusion or alternation): exclusion uses *o* / **u** (or, before a word starting
  with *o-* or *ho-*, parallel to the *y*→*e* rule — *leones u osos*, *mujeres u hombres*);
  alternation uses paired correlative sets not seen elsewhere in this corpus: *ahora...ahora...*,
  *ora...ora...*, *bien...bien...*, *que...que...*, *ya...ya...* (all ≈ "now X, now Y" / "either X
  or Y," describing alternating/oscillating actions — *Se pasa el tiempo, ahora leyendo, ahora
  trabajando*).
- **Illative** (consequence): *así que*, *conque*, *luego*, *por consiguiente*, *por lo tanto*, *por
  tanto*, *pues*, *que* — a cluster of near-synonymous "therefore/so" connectors, several overlapping
  in form with items from other subgroups (the book explicitly notes one conjunction may belong to
  multiple subgroups).

### Subordinating conjunctions — 6 subtypes (link a dependent clause to the main clause)

- **Causal** (reason): *porque*, *como que*, *pues*, *pues que*, *puesto que*, *que*, *supuesto que*,
  *una vez que* — reinforces and extends 014/015's porque-coverage with several less-common synonyms.
- **Comparative**: *como* (as/like), *así como* (just as), *igual que* (the same as), *a modo de*
  (like/as), *más que* (although/even if — a non-obvious sense of *más que* outside its usual
  comparison role).
- **Conditional**: *si* (the default), plus *con tal que*/*siempre que* (provided), *dado que* (as
  long as), *o bien*/*si no* (otherwise), *ya que* (since), and *como* used conditionally (*Como no
  estudies, no aprenderás nada* — "if you don't study...," a conditional use of *como* distinct from
  its usual "as/since" sense).
- **Conclusive/final** (purpose): *para que*, *a fin de que*, *a que*, *con objeto de que* — all ≈ "so
  that/in order that."
- **Modal** (manner): *como si* (as if), *conforme a* (according to/consistent with), *de manera que*/
  *de modo que* (so that), *según que* (as), *sin que* (without).
- **Temporal**: *cuando* (when), *apenas*/*en cuanto*/*no bien*/*tan luego* (as soon as), *así que*/
  *luego que* (after/as soon as), *mientras* (while), *cuanto antes* (as soon as possible).

Four structural notes close the chapter: many adverbs/adverbial phrases double as conjunctions; one
conjunction may belong to several subgroups simultaneously; *que* is ambiguously a relative pronoun
or a conjunction depending on whether it's replaceable by *which*; and *tan luego* has a separate
ironic/sarcastic use (*¡Tan luego a mí me lo dice!*, "And of all people, she tells ME that!") distinct
from its literal "as soon as" sense.

## Chapter 20 — Interjections (p.463–465)

**First systematic interjection categorization found anywhere in this corpus.** Every prior
interjection hit across the 59+ established files is a scattered individual vocabulary item (hola,
ojalá, qué onda, simón, naranjas, etc.) with no functional classification. This chapter supplies
exactly that missing layer.

**PDF extraction gotcha hit here:** `pdftotext` in default (non-`-layout`) mode scrambled the
"Interjections classified by feeling" word lists — multi-column groups of exclamation words got
merged across category boundaries (e.g. the plain-mode pass put "¡Vamos!", "¡Mira!", "¡Olé!" etc.
into the "Pain" category, which is wrong). Re-running with `pdftotext -layout` on this page range
produced a clean, correctly-partitioned extraction — the lists below are from the `-layout` pass,
cross-checked against the plain-mode pass to confirm which version was correct (per the extraction
spec's column-scrambling gotcha).

### A. Classified by grammatical form — 5 subtypes

1. **Proper** (*propias*) — words with no function except sudden feeling-expression: ¡Ah!, ¡Ay!,
   ¡Bah!, ¡Oh!, ¡Uf!, ¡Upa!, ¡Zape!
2. **Improper** (*impropias*) — nouns/adjectives/verbs repurposed as interjections: ¡Caracoles!,
   ¡Bueno!, ¡Viva!, ¡Hola!, ¡Dale!, ¡Qué macana! (this last one itself a noun-phrase exclamation,
   "macana" literally "club/cudgel," colloquially "a nuisance/mess" in parts of Latin America).
3. **Single** (*simples*) — one-word: ¡Ea!, ¡Anda!, ¡Vale!, ¡Puf!
4. **Double** (*dobles*) — a word repeated: ¡Hurra! ¡Hurra!, ¡Bien! ¡Bien!, ¡Uy! ¡Uy!, ¡Vamos! ¡Vamos!
5. **Exclamative expressions** (*modos interjectivos*) — 2+ words functioning as one interjection:
   ¡Madre mía!, ¡Válgame Dios!, ¡Qué desastre!, ¡Ay de mí!, ¡Ojalá que sí!, ¡Vaya sorpresa!

### B. Classified by feeling denoted — 9 categories (corrected via -layout re-extraction)

The book explicitly notes these interjections "vary regionally in the Hispanic world" without giving
a specific region per item (so Attested Region is left "—" per the column convention for this
source):
1. Pain: ¡Ay! ¡Ah! ¡Uy! ¡Aya! ¡Ayayay!
2. Happiness: ¡Ay! ¡Aleluya!
3. Surprise: ¡Ay! ¡Ah! ¡Epa! ¡Guay! ¡Uy! ¡Oh!
4. Admiration: ¡Ah! ¡Pardiez! ¡Ha! ¡Ay! ¡Ayayay!
5. Cheering: ¡Arre! ¡Ea! ¡Upa! ¡Sus! ¡Ce! ¡Vamos! ¡Olé!
6. Attention: ¡Ea! ¡Mira! ¡So! ¡Ta! ¡To! ¡Aquí!
7. Approval: ¡Ajá! ¡Bis! ¡Bravo! ¡Viva! ¡Arriba! ¡Amén! ¡Olé!
8. Bother: ¡Quiá! ¡Rayos! ¡Uf! ¡Za! ¡Zape! ¡Fuera!
9. Disapproval: ¡Abajo! ¡Ca! ¡Dale! ¡Fuera!

Note the deliberate cross-listing: several interjections (¡Ay!, ¡Ah!, ¡Uy!, ¡Dale!, ¡Fuera!, ¡Olé!,
¡Zape!) appear in *multiple* feeling-categories — the book's own implicit point that interjection
meaning is heavily context/intonation-dependent rather than fixed per-word. Worth flagging for the
mechanics-analysis phase: this is a clean, source-stated example of polysemous/context-disambiguated
function words, parallel to how tone/prosody would need to carry the distinguishing load in any
derived-conlang slang system modeled on this.

## Appendix D — Sample Conjugations with the Pronoun *vos* (p.481–488)

**Genuinely new, and substantially fuller than 015's existing voseo paradigm.** 015's coverage
(Chapter 3, p.37) is limited to: present indicative, *haber* as present-perfect auxiliary, reflexive
*te*, and commands. This appendix gives a **complete per-tense/per-mood vos paradigm** spanning every
tense/mood category covered elsewhere in this book, each shown as a yo/tú/vos three-way table.
Extracting the rule-generation logic + a representative example per tense/category (not the book's
full example-verb tables) to respect copyright discipline:

- **Framing note:** vos is explicitly geographically scoped by the book as used "throughout Latin
  America," heaviest in **Argentina and Uruguay**, with **minimal-to-nonexistent** influence in
  **Puerto Rico and the Dominican Republic** — a usable Attested Region-type data point, though
  phrased as prose rather than a formal region tag, so left as a Notes-field observation rather than
  populating the Attested Region column. Also: vos itself, like other subject pronouns, is optionally
  omissible in both speech and writing (pro-drop applies to vos the same as tú).

- **A. Present indicative** — regular verbs: vos takes the *vosotros* ending *minus its glide*
  (-áis→-ás, -éis→-és, -ís stays -ís: hablás, bebés, vivís). Confirmed independently in this source:
  vos regularizes *all* stem-changing verb classes to the infinitive stem + vos ending, with **no
  stem change at all** — *pedís* (not *pide*→pedís), *pensás* (not piensás), *podés* (not puedés),
  *jugás* (not juegás). This directly corroborates 015's point of divergence finding (vos flattens
  stem-changes) with four additional stem-change classes (e→i, e→ie, i→ie, u→ue) beyond what 015
  demonstrated. Several irregular-verb sub-patterns get their own full vos paradigm:
  first-person-irregular verbs (tener→tenés, hacer→hacés, dirigir→dirigís), verbs with stem-only
  irregularities (caber→cabés), verbs with ending-only irregularities (dar→das, dando identical to
  tú; estar→estás, also identical to tú), verbs irregular in multiple persons (ir→vas identical to
  tú; **ser→sos**, genuinely distinct from both yo soy and tú eres; haber→has identical to tú), and
  verbs needing an added -y or added accent (oír→oís, esquiar→esquiás). Reflexive verbs keep *te*
  (vos te dormís, vos te ponés) same as 015 found. The gustar-type class is identical between tú and
  vos (te gusta/n either way) — a new confirmation that gustar-type verbs don't participate in
  voseo's verb-ending shift at all, since they're never conjugated for the experiencer person in the
  first place.

- **B/C. Future and future perfect**: the book states that vos-speakers *prefer the informal future*
  (ir a + infinitive: *vos vas a cantar*) over the simple future, and when the simple future IS used,
  vos is **identical to tú** in both the simple future (vos besarás) and the future perfect (vos
  habrás restado) — i.e., voseo has no distinct future-tense morphology at all, only
  present-indicative and preterit/imperative morphology diverge from tú. This is a structurally
  important finding 015 does not state.

- **D/F/G. Imperfect, conditional, conditional perfect**: vos is **identical to tú** in all three (vos
  andabas, vos besarías, vos habrías pensado) — confirms the no-distinct-morphology pattern extends to
  every tense built on the imperfect/conditional stem.

- **E. Preterit**: vos takes the same ending as tú **plus the regular -ste/-iste pattern**, which for
  *regular* verbs is identical to tú (vos bailaste) but the book is explicit that for a subset of
  **irregular** verbs this is vos's *other* genuinely divergent paradigm beyond the present
  indicative — though the forms shown (fuiste, diste, anduviste, hiciste, cupiste, supiste, buscaste,
  pagaste, almorzaste, volviste, pediste, preferiste, dormiste) are in fact identical to tú throughout
  the appendix's own examples; the book does not show a case where preterit vos diverges from tú
  morphologically, only in optionality/preference (see present-perfect note below) — worth flagging
  this as an apparent gap/overclaim in the book's own framing, worth cross-checking against a
  native-speaker query folder later.

- **H. Progressive tenses** (present & past progressive): vos = tú throughout (vos estás cocinando,
  vos estabas andando) — estar itself doesn't distinguish tú/vos in these forms.

- **I/J. Present perfect & past perfect indicative**: morphologically vos = tú (vos has avisado, vos
  había mirado), but the book adds a **usage note not captured in 015 at all**: vos-speakers
  *functionally avoid* the present perfect in natural speech, substituting the simple preterit
  instead — *vos has hablado* is "correct" but speakers say *vos hablaste*. This pragmatic/register
  fact (voseo correlates with preterit-preference over present-perfect, independent of the
  morphological identity) is a genuinely new sociolinguistic data point.

- **K. Subjunctive** (present, imperfect, present perfect, pluperfect): vos = tú throughout all four
  subjunctive paradigms (vos uses/vos mostraras/vos hayas dibujado/vos hubieras atado) — the
  subjunctive mood shows no voseo-specific morphology anywhere, a clean negative finding.

- **L. Imperative — the one other genuinely divergent paradigm, with full formation rules**:
  **Affirmative vos commands** are formed by dropping the final *-d* of the *vosotros* affirmative
  command and adding a written accent on the final vowel (vosotros *¡Sacad!* → vos *¡Sacá!*;
  *¡Recorred!* → *¡Recorré!*; *¡Permitid!* → *¡Permití!*). **Negative vos commands** are formed by
  dropping the *-i-* of the vosotros present-subjunctive form (vosotros *¡No saquéis!* → vos *¡No
  saques!*). Pronouns attach to affirmative vos commands the same as any affirmative command
  (*¡Sacálo!*), and precede negative commands (*¡No lo saques!*). **Irregular short affirmative
  command stems get a full extra syllable in vos** rather than simple suffixation: tú *dime* → vos
  **decime** (not "dime" + lo); tú *ponlo* → vos **ponelo**; tú *hazlo* → vos **hacelo**; tú *tenlo* →
  vos **tenelo** — i.e. the vos command rebuilds from the full verb stem (decir-, poner-, hacer-,
  tener-) rather than the irregular short tú-command stem (di-, pon-, haz-, ten-), a concrete
  morphological-rule delta from tú-command formation that 015 does not cover at all. A final note: **in
  Argentine tango lyrics specifically**, the negative vos command is sometimes written *with* an
  accent (*¡No saqués!* rather than the grammatically-correct unaccented *¡No saques!*) — a
  register/genre-specific orthographic variant worth flagging for any lyrics-corpus cross-check later.

## Appendix deltas (A, B, C, E) — compact additions beyond the skip decision

Per the scope note above, these four appendices were triaged as mostly redundant with 009's existing
stress/accent/syllable/punctuation coverage, but targeted grep checks surfaced four specific items
genuinely absent from the corpus, extracted here instead of skipped:

### Sobresdrújula — the fourth (least common) Spanish stress category (Appendix A, p.466)

009/002 already cover the three standard stress categories (aguda = stressed on the last syllable;
llana/grave = stressed on the second-to-last; esdrújula = stressed on the third-to-last, always
accented). This book adds a fourth, rarer category not previously captured: **sobresdrújula** —
stress falls on the *fourth-from-last syllable or earlier*. The book's examples are specifically
commands/gerunds with multiple attached clitic pronouns stacked onto an already-accented verb form:
*perdónoselo*, *cántemelas*, *constrúyanoslo*, *repítaselos*, *devotísimamente*. This is directly
useful for any later analysis of Spanish's pronoun-clitic-stacking morphology, since sobresdrújulas
arise specifically from that stacking process pushing the stressed syllable further from the word's
end.

### Syllable-division-at-line-break rules (Appendix C, p.477)

009 covers diphthong-formation (which vowel combinations collapse into one syllable) but not the
separate, practical question of *where to hyphenate a word at the end of a written line*. Two rules:
(1) a single consonant between two vowels joins the *following* vowel's syllable (*co-mi-do*,
*pa-ja-ri-to*); (2) a fixed set of consonant digraphs/clusters — bl, br, ch, cl, cr, dr, fl, fr, gl,
gr, ll, pl, pr, rr, tr — are treated as an indivisible unit and never split across a hyphenation
point, instead starting the next syllable together (*pro-ce-so*, *re-tra-to*, *se-lla-do*,
*co-rri-da*, *mu-cho*).

### Punto y seguido / punto y aparte / punto final — period terminology (Appendix E, p.489)

009 already covers the comma/period-vs-decimal convention and inverted ¿¡ marks, so that material was
not re-extracted. This three-way terminological distinction for *types of period* was not previously
captured (see Vocabulary table above for the three terms and glosses).

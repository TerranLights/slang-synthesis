## Grammar points

### Ch3 — Phonology/orthography overview
Spanish has a 5-vowel system (vs. 6 Catalan, 7 Italian, 16 French) and two semi-vocalic glides.
The two major standard varieties (Castilian vs. Latin American) differ phonologically only in two
respects: the /θ/-/s/ distinction (Castilian only) and the /ʎ/-/j/ distinction (now rare anywhere).
Everything else "accent"-level is phonetic realization, not phonemic (pp.44-45). Spanish orthography
is unusually phonemic by European standards and this very closeness between spelling and sound is
itself a widely-held cultural belief (not fully true in practice — see Jardiel Poncela's humorous
phonetic-spelling letter from an uneducated character, p.56-57). History: standardized spelling
traces to Alfonso X (13th c.); major RAE reforms in 1746/1763/1815 removed ç, simplified ph/ss/th,
fixed U/V and I/J as vowel/consonant pairs; 1952/1959 reform relaxed accentuation on homonym-safe
cases (dió→dio). The 1994 pan-Academy reorganization moved ch/ll out of their own alphabetic slots,
opposed by some Latin American academies as "cultural colonization" (a Guatemalan academy
representative called it "cultural/economic colonization of the Anglo-Saxon world," El País
28.4.94). UNESCO pressure to drop ñ for keyboard-compatibility reasons was resisted pan-Hispanically
(a notable case of orthographic nationalism cutting across the Spain/Latin-America axis that usually
divides prescriptive debates). Mexico's retention of x in "México" (from Nahuatl /ʃ/) against the
RAE's j-spelling is a comparable nationalist spelling stance, as is Peru's resistance to z in "Cuzco"
(RAE) vs. s in "Cusco" (closer to Quechua).

### Ch4 — Word-formation mechanism inventory
Six productive mechanisms for creating Spanish neologisms from native stock: (1) **affixation**
(prefixation + suffixation, both non-emotive and emotive — see vocabulary tables above); (2)
**compounding** (verb+noun: limpiahornos; noun+adjective: pirata informático; adjective+noun:
todoterreno; noun+prep+noun: olla a presión, which can lose its preposition once lexicalized:
efecto de invernadero→efecto invernadero); (3) **conversion** (word changes class with no change of
form — un coche deportivo→un deportivo; adjectives used as adverbs: se portó genial); (4)
**acronyms/alphabetisms** (OTAN, dating mainly from the 1970s on; graphic life-cycle from
O.T.A.N.→OTAN→Otan→otan as lexicalization proceeds; derived forms proliferate once an acronym
stabilizes: pepero from PP, priísta from PRI); (5) **abbreviation** (clipping to 2-3 syllables: profe,
bici, taxi — some fully standard, others register-restricted: la mili for "military service"; cheli
specifically favors trisyllabic clippings with final-vowel modification, see vocabulary table); (6)
**metaphor** (both calqued from abroad and natively coined: canguro "kangaroo"→babysitter, búho
"owl"→night bus; Rodríguez González (1993) finds politics, war, sport, bullfighting, and religion as
especially metaphor-rich semantic source domains in Spanish political journalism).

### Ch4 — Borrowing integration pathway
Borrowing is distinguished from its integration mechanisms. A borrowed term either (a) stays
unassimilated (code-switch-like, criticized by prescriptivists, common in media/sport/business
jargon), or is integrated via (b) **assimilation** to Spanish phonology/orthography/morphology
(slogan→eslogan; thriller→triler/trailer), (c) **calquing/loan translation** (ratón for computer
mouse), or (d) **semantic extension/shift** ("false-friend" paronymic borrowing, e.g. evidencia
drifting toward English "evidence" sense). A once-ephemeral borrowing that meets a lasting need
typically gets nativized and then itself becomes productive for further native derivation (fax→
faxear/faxeo; football terminology history, below, is the book's extended worked example).

### Ch4 — Football-terminology relexification (a full worked case study of borrowing→native-coinage cycling)
Pre-1950 Chilean Spanish used Anglicisms adapted to Spanish phonology for football positions
(goalkeeper, backs, wing, etc). After 1950 (coinciding with Spanish becoming an official FIFA
language in 1946 and Latin American football's rise to world prominence), these were replaced by
indigenous Hispanicized terms (arquero/guardameta for goalkeeper; zagueros for backs; mediocampistas
for insiders) — except "fútbol" itself, which displaced the indigenous calque "balompié" specifically
*because* fútbol had already proven more derivationally productive (futbolista, futbolístico, futbolín,
futbito vs. balompié's zero derivatives) — illustrating that derivational productivity, not
etymological purity, drives which competing form survives. In francoist Spain, football terminology
was deliberately Hispanicized for ideological reasons; after 1973 (when Latin American players/
trainers began working in Spain), Latin American terms began displacing the Castilian ones despite
style-guide resistance — a rare case of explicit, dated ideological language engineering being
later overridden by cross-dialect contact and usage.

### Ch4 — User variation: geography and age (framework)
The book distinguishes **user variation** (sex, age, socio-economic group, education, rural/urban,
geography, ethnicity) from **use variation** (field/mode/tenor — covered later in Ch.6-8, see 028c).
Within user variation, geography is the most salient axis to speakers themselves, but items can
diverge in referent (tinto, luego — see vocabulary table) as well as simply having different
regional synonyms (car/bus/pen vocabulary tables above). Specialized-domain vocabulary (drugs,
agriculture/fishing) shows the least standardization, precisely because in-group-identity marking
(not communicative efficiency) is the primary social function of that vocabulary — directly relevant
to this project's slang-synthesis mechanics, since it predicts where new slang coinage will cluster
(any domain functioning as an in-group boundary marker, not any domain per se).

### Ch4 — Cheli / anti-language as a worked case study of a complete slang register
The book documents cheli as a historically-traceable, fully-described anti-language with an
explicit feature inventory (see Halliday's "anti-language" framing): phonological markers (sibilant
[s], intervocalic-d loss: el Estao), lexical markers (loans, semantic extension, segmented
abbreviation, -ata/-ota/-eta suffixation — see vocabulary tables), syntactic markers (ir de, pasar de,
quedarse con as idiomatic constructions; redundant que/como), orthographic markers (k, x spellings),
pragmatic markers (fillers: o así, y tal, tío). Casado Velarde (1988) frames cheli's appeal to
disaffected youth as directness contrasted with the perceived hypocrisy of euphemistic official
register (reajuste de tarifas for subida de precios "price rise"; interrupción voluntaria de
embarazo for aborto "abortion"; profesional del amor for prostituta) — i.e. cheli's anti-euphemistic
bluntness is itself socially meaningful, not just lexical color. Note the methodological caution the
book itself makes: in a videotaped corpus of actual teenage delinquents, the teens used standard
Spanish on camera, and only the facilitating adult deployed youth-register slang (to build rapport) —
a reminder that slang registers are a stylistic *choice* available to speakers, not an obligatory
code, and ethnographic self-report/performance can diverge from spontaneous use.

### Ch5 — Queísmo/dequeísmo (new content, not covered elsewhere in this project's Spanish files)
**Dequeísmo** = inserting *de* before a *que*-clause where standard Spanish has none (creo de que for
creo que), or substituting *de* for a different standard preposition (empeñarse de que for empeñarse
en que). Proposed mechanism: analogical leakage from near-synonym pairs that do standardly take *de*
(tener miedo de que vs. temer que). Stigmatized in Spain; a Valencia study (Gómez Molina) found it
used *less* by younger/middle-class/female speakers there — the inverse of a change-in-progress
profile, so the book argues Spanish dequeísmo is sociolinguistic *variation*, not change, in Spain
specifically, while Vaquero de Ramírez finds the opposite demographic skew in Latin America (i.e.
change-in-progress there). García's study of Bogotá/Santiago/Buenos Aires found a genuine semantic
correlate: *de*-marked subordinate clauses read as less categorical/committed ("Creo de que" implies
weaker commitment than "Creo que") — an unusually well-evidenced non-arbitrary account of a
stigmatized variant's function. **Queísmo** = the converse, omitting a standardly-required
preposition before *que* (me he acordado que for me he acordado de que). Unlike dequeísmo, queísmo can
cause real semantic loss: esperar que ("to hope and wait") vs. esperar a que ("to wait until") collapse
to one form under queísmo. In Valencia specifically, queísmo (unlike dequeísmo) patterns as genuine
change-in-progress (favored by the young, women, middle class).

### Ch5 — Leísmo/laísmo/loísmo (a distinct axis from tú/usted address-form material already captured elsewhere in this project)
Baseline: lo/los = masculine direct object, le/les = indirect object. **Leísmo** = le/les encroaching
on lo/los as the direct object for male persons (Le llamé rather than Lo llamé) — now the prestige
standard and sole accepted usage across most of Spain, but Latin America mostly retains conservative
lo/loísmo (exception: Paraguay, where le replaces all 3rd-person singular/plural forms but is
stigmatized there as uneducated — note this is the *reverse* prestige valence from Peninsular Spain).
Using le for female referents is widespread in Spain but explicitly rejected by the Manual de español
urgente. **Laísmo** = la used where le is standard (indirect object) — stigmatized nearly everywhere
except Castile (e.g. Valladolid), where it is the dominant form across all social classes, undercutting
Valladolid's folk reputation as the source of "best" Spanish (that reputation is specifically an
educated-elite register, not the general local vernacular — a useful caution against treating "prestige
dialect" claims as applying to an undifferentiated regional population). **Clitic doubling**: 'redundant'
coreferential clitic+full NP (Le di el libro a María) is obligatory in some contexts, optional/spreading
in others, and in Chile is argued to mark emphasis/topicality rather than true redundancy — not
socially stigmatized there. A further, likely still-spreading non-standard doubling type doubles
subject/object pronouns outright (Yo me tenía que vacunarme) and risks eroding the semantic distinctions
the standard system otherwise encodes. **Clitic pluralization** (se los/se las for logically-singular
se lo/se la, disambiguating an otherwise-invariant se) occurs in cultured as well as uncultured speech
in Mexico (76% in both registers per a cited 11-city study) and Bogotá/Buenos Aires/Caracas/Havana/
Santiago, but is entirely absent in La Paz, Lima, Madrid, and Seville.

### Ch5 — ser/estar functional extension (regional change-in-progress, supplementing the already-documented baseline rule)
Estar is extending into contexts traditionally reserved for ser (group/inherent-quality attribution,
not just individual resultant state) — e.g. "está muy buena esta película" (of a film generally, not
one person's momentary impression). Most studied in Spanish-English contact zones (southern US,
Mexico) and most frequent among younger bilingual speakers, but also independently attested in
monolingual Morelia, Mexico — so language contact likely *accelerates* rather than *originates* this
change. A parallel semantic-extension case: estar replacing ser specifically in age expressions in
Mexico/Venezuela (Juan está viejo/joven) — argued to reflect a changed conceptualization of age as a
transient rather than inherent state, which then stabilizes into the new standard usage over time
(i.e. a conceptual-metaphor shift preceding and then licensing a grammatical change).

### Ch5 — Preterite/perfect regional variation
Standard Peninsular Spanish treats the perfect/preterite split as temporal (present relevance vs. pure
past). Most Latin American Spanish instead strongly prefers the preterite even in present-relevant
contexts; Mexican Spanish specifically reanalyzes the split as *aspectual* (completed vs.
not-yet-completed) rather than temporal. In Spain's Galician/Asturian regions (contact language
without a perfect/preterite distinction) the preterite is likewise preferred in present-relevant
contexts — paralleling the Latin American pattern via a different causal route (substrate contact
rather continent-wide drift). Journalistic Peninsular Spanish is independently drifting toward the
preterite for recent-past reporting (Dijo hoy vs. standard Ha dicho hoy), actively resisted by the
DEU style authority.

### Ch5 — Subjunctive erosion (regional/register-conditioned, supplementing existing subjunctive-rule coverage)
The future subjunctive (cantare) has effectively died out except in legal/administrative formulae in
Spain, but persists more in some Latin American newspaper styles. The -ra/-se imperfect-subjunctive
variants are interchangeable, but -ra has become the spoken/written default pan-Hispanically, with
-se surviving mainly as a written stylistic marker; Andalusia and Latin America lean more heavily
toward -ra ("raísmo") than the rest of Peninsular Spain. In optional-subjunctive contexts (e.g. after
quizá), the subjunctive is losing ground to the indicative fastest — especially, but not exclusively,
in Spanish-English contact zones, meaning this is not purely a contact-driven change. Conditional-clause
protasis variation (e.g. using the conditional mood itself in the if-clause: Si me lo plantearían...)
is attested in both Buenos Aires and northern Spain (notably the Basque Country and neighboring
Burgos/Palencia/Logroño provinces) and correlates inversely with speaker education level; its use in
the apodosis (main clause) is more strongly stigmatized than its use in the protasis.

### Ch5 — Prepositions: hasta/desde directional-ambiguity trap
In large parts of Latin America (Mexico through Venezuela/Colombia), hasta can mark the *beginning*
of an action/state (¿Hasta cuándo viene María? = "when will she arrive," not "until when will she stay")
— the reverse of its standard end-point sense elsewhere. The book gives a documented real
cross-dialect miscommunication transcript between a Spanish-speaking American researcher and a Latin
American receptionist talking past each other over exactly this ambiguity — a concrete, source-cited
example of a regional grammatical difference causing an actual comprehension failure, not just a
documented variant.

### Ch5 — Relative pronoun regional preference
El/la cual (vs. el/la que) is formal-register-only in Peninsular Spanish per standard grammars, but
De Mello's multi-city study found el/la cual as the *preferred, unmarked* form (not a formality
marker) in Bogotá, Buenos Aires, Caracas, Lima, San Juan, and Santiago — while Mexico City, Havana,
Madrid, and Seville prefer el/la que, and La Paz splits evenly. A clean case of a single grammatical
item carrying different register-markedness depending on dialect region, which matters for any
mechanics model that assumes a form's register value is pan-dialectally stable.

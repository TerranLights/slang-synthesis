## Grammar points

### The preterite tense (Ch. 13)

Refers to a specific, completed past action — pinpointable by clock/calendar time, a specific count of
repetitions, or an enclosed span ("for eight hours"). Test: can you reasonably ask "when?" or "how many
times?" and expect a specific answer — if yes, preterite; if the answer would be "several times a day"
(unbounded/habitual), the sentence needs the imperfect instead (Ch. 14).

- **Regular conjugation**: -ar endings -é/-aste/-ó/-amos/-asteis/-aron (nosotros form identical to
  present tense); -er/-ir endings -í/-iste/-ió/-imos/-isteis/-ieron. Most stem-changing verbs do *not*
  show a stem change in the preterite (yo cuento → yo conté), except certain -ir stem-changers (below).
- **Three standard orthographic changes**, all confined to the yo form only: -gar verbs g→gu (llegué),
  -car verbs c→qu (practiqué), -zar verbs z→c (empecé) — each preserves the infinitive's original
  consonant sound before e, and (for -zar) reflects that z never precedes e in native Spanish spelling.
- **Irregular stems + special endings** (-e/-iste/-o/-imos/-isteis/-ieron, no accents): three
  sub-patterns by inserted vowel — `uv` (andar, estar, tener), `u` (caber, haber, poder, poner, saber),
  `i` (hacer, querer, venir); hacer's 3rd singular is the sole exception within this set (hizo, c→z).
- **ser and ir** share an identical preterite paradigm (fui/fuiste/fue/fuimos/fuisteis/fueron) —
  disambiguated by context; ir's forms are almost always followed by `a`.
- **decir and traer** (+ traer's compounds atraer, distraer, retraer, sustraer) take a distinctive j-stem
  paradigm: dije/dijiste/dijo/dijimos/dijisteis/dijeron; traje/trajiste/trajo/trajimos/trajisteis/trajeron.
- **dar and ver**: dar is irregular (di/diste/dio/dimos/disteis/dieron); ver is regular except it drops
  the accent marks on 1st/3rd singular (vi, vio).
- **Stem-changing -ir verbs** show a *third-person-only* stem change in the preterite (nosotros/vosotros
  unaffected elsewhere too): o>ue verbs become o>u (dormir → durmió/durmieron); both e>ie and e>i verbs
  become e>i (mentir → mintió/mintieron; pedir → pidió/pidieron).
- **i→y change**: -er/-ir verbs with a vowel immediately before the ending change 3rd sg. -ío→-yó and
  3rd pl. -ieron→-yeron, with an added written accent on the í in all other forms (creer → creí, creíste,
  creyó, creímos, creísteis, creyeron). Exceptions: traer/atraer/distraer follow their own j-stem pattern
  instead; -guir verbs (seguir) just follow the regular stem-changing -ir pattern since u is silent;
  -uir verbs not preceded by g (destruir) take the i→y change only in the 3rd-person forms, staying
  regular elsewhere.
- **-ucir verbs**: c→j, endings -e/-iste/-o/-imos/-isteis/-eron (producir → produje/produjiste/produjo/
  produjimos/produjisteis/produjeron).
- **Verbs that shift meaning in the preterite** (because the preterite frames the action as bounded/
  completed, several verbs of mental/emotional state pick up a distinct sense there): conocer "to know
  (a person)" → "to meet"; poder "to be able" → "to manage (to)"; no poder → "to fail (to)"; querer "to
  want" → "to try"; no querer → "to refuse"; saber "to know (a fact)" → "to find out, learn"; sentir "to
  feel" → "to regret, be sorry"; tener "to have" → "to have (at a specific moment, e.g. give birth)"
  (pp. 109–134).

### The imperfect tense (Ch. 14)

Refers to nonspecific, continuous, or repeated past action where the exact time, duration, or count is
irrelevant or unknowable — English markers: "used to," "was + -ing," "would" (habitual sense), "always."
Regular endings: -ar → -aba/-abas/-aba/-ábamos/-abais/-aban; -er/-ir → -ía/-ías/-ía/-íamos/-íais/-ían.
Only three irregular verbs: **ser** (era/eras/era/éramos/erais/eran), **ir** (iba/ibas/iba/íbamos/
ibais/iban), **ver** (veía/veías/veía/veíamos/veíais/veían). For all verbs (regular and irregular), 1st
and 3rd singular are identical.

Five principal uses: (1) habitual/continuous past action ("used to"); (2) simple background description
(what things *were like*, as opposed to what happened — contrast `Compré un coche` [preterite, specific
act] vs. `El coche era rojo` [imperfect, description]; professions/roles in the past are imperfect since
their start/end can't be pinpointed); (3) "-ing" actions in the past, often two simultaneous actions
(`Yo tocaba la guitarra y Juan cantaba`); (4) mental/emotional states or physical sensations (verbs like
amar, creer, estar, gustar, odiar, pensar, poder, querer, saber, sentir(se), doler, molestar — several of
which are the same verbs that shift meaning in the preterite, see Ch. 13); (5) clock time and age in the
past (always imperfect: `Era la una cuando llegué`; `Tenía diez años cuando...`).

"Would" and "could" are ambiguous English markers spanning two different Spanish tenses: habitual "would"
(="used to") and "could" (="was/were able to") are imperfect, while hypothetical "would"/"could" (an
uncompleted, conditional action) belong to the conditional tense (Ch. 16) instead.

`había` (imperfect of `haber`) is the past form of `hay` ("there was/were," invariant for number);
the rare preterite form is `hubo` ("there was [a specific completed event of existing]," e.g. a single
sudden occurrence). `ir + a + infinitive` in the past ("was going to do") is imperfect, since intent is
a mental state, not a physical action. The progressive in the past can be formed either with the plain
imperfect or with `estar` (conjugated in the imperfect) + present participle — structurally identical to
Ch. 12's present progressive but with `estar` shifted to imperfect (pp. 135–157).

### The future tense (Ch. 15)

A simple (non-compound) tense — no auxiliary "will" required; the future marker is baked into the verb
ending, attached directly to the (unmodified, for regular verbs) infinitive: -é/-ás/-á/-emos/-éis/-án
(all forms but nosotros carry a written accent). Twelve verbs are irregular by modifying the *stem* (not
the ending), in three patterned groups: **Group 1** drops the infinitive's final vowel (caber→cabr-,
haber→habr-, poder→podr-, querer→querr-, saber→sabr-); **Group 2** drops the final vowel and inserts d
(poner→pondr-, salir→saldr-, tener→tendr-, valer→valdr-, venir→vendr-); **Group 3** shortens the stem
(decir→dir-, hacer→har-). Compounds of these twelve inherit the same irregular stem (tener→tendré,
obtener→obtendré), with one notable exception: **bendecir** does NOT inherit decir's irregular dir- stem
and stays fully regular (bendeciré). `habrá` (future of `haber`) is the future form of `hay` ("there will
be," singular and plural) (pp. 159–169).

### The conditional tense (Ch. 16)

Expresses hypothetical future action contingent on an unmet condition — "would" + verb — as opposed to
the future tense's certainty. A stated or implied "but.../if..." clause explains why the condition isn't
met. (Note: the book explicitly defers hypothetical if-clauses, e.g. "if I were," since those require the
subjunctive, not yet introduced at this point.) Formed exactly like the future tense but with -ía/-ías/
-ía/-íamos/-íais/-ían endings on the same stem (regular = infinitive; irregular = same twelve
future-tense irregular stems, same three groups, same bendecir exception). `habría` (conditional of
`haber`) = "there would be" (pp. 171–177).

### The present perfect tense (Ch. 17)

A compound tense: `haber` conjugated in the present (he/has/ha/hemos/habéis/han) + past participle.
`haber` here means "to have (done something)" — distinct from `tener` (possession). Used for (a) a past
action that remains true up to now ("I have lived here for ten years" — still living there), and
(b) a recently completed action ("I have eaten [so I can't go to dinner]"). The action falls in a span of
time leading up to and including the present, but the exact moment within that span isn't specified
(unlike the preterite). Regular past participles: -ar→-ado (hablado); -er/-ir→-ido (comido, vivido).
Irregular past participles fall into two groups: (1) -er/-ir verbs with a non-`u` vowel immediately
before the ending take -ído (leído, caído, oído — but -uir verbs like construir stay regular:
construido); (2) a memorize-individually set: abrir→abierto, cubrir→cubierto, decir→dicho,
describir→descrito, descubrir→descubierto, devolver→devuelto, disolver→disuelto, envolver→envuelto,
escribir→escrito, freír→frito, hacer→hecho, morir→muerto, oponer→opuesto, poner→puesto, proveer→provisto,
pudrir→podrido, resolver→resuelto, romper→roto, ver→visto, volver→vuelto. Two structural rules: the
conjugated `haber` and its past participle are never separated by any other word, and object pronouns
always precede the conjugated `haber` (not attached to the participle) (pp. 179–186).

### The past perfect tense (Ch. 18)

Also called the pluperfect. Formed identically to the present perfect but with `haber` conjugated in the
*imperfect* (había/habías/había/habíamos/habíais/habían) instead of the present — same past-participle
forms (regular and irregular) as Ch. 17, unchanged across all compound tenses. Refers to an action that
occurred prior to some other stated or implied cutoff point in the past (as opposed to the present
perfect's cutoff point of "now") — e.g. "I had taken three pictures before realizing there wasn't any
film" (pp. 187–193).

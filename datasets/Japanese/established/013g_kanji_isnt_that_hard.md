## Grammar points

### The book's overall theory: 漢字図形論 ("Kanji Diagram Theory")

The essay's core claim, addressed to teachers rather than learners: kanji are commonly perceived as
difficult for two reasons — sheer quantity, and the fact that (unlike the Roman alphabet) a kanji's
shape does not encode its pronunciation. But this comparison is a category error: kanji are not a
phonetic script at all, but **a kind of diagram/graphic (図形) that directly expresses meaning**,
comparable to a pictogram like the red-silhouette-figure sign marking a restroom door — such a sign
conveys meaning instantly without encoding how to pronounce anything. Historically, the author argues
this meaning-transparency (rather than any phonetic standardization) is what let a China divided into
mutually unintelligible regional dialects nonetheless function as one textually-unified state: a
reader in any dialect region could extract the same meaning from the same kanji text regardless of
how they pronounced it aloud. (§1, p. 158)

### Units that recur: why kanji are "fewer" than they first appear

Some kanji are irreducible single units (口, cannot be broken down further); others are combinations
of two or more smaller meaning-bearing units (学 = ツ "decoration" + ワ "hat" + 子 "child"). Critically,
each of those component units recurs across many other kanji (ツ also appears in 桜/蛍/巣; ワ also
appears in 鳶-type compounds; 子 appears independently and in compounds). The essay's pedagogical
claim: as a learner's inventory of known units grows, an increasing share of each *new* kanji
encountered turns out to be built entirely from already-known units — so truly novel material
shrinks over time, meaning kanji are "far fewer than a learner first assumes" once this
compositional structure is internalized, unlike a phonetic script where every new word is an
unrelated new string. (§2, p. 158)

### The "circles became squares" law as the bridge from picture to kanji

Learners often cannot connect a kanji's shape to the picture it supposedly derives from (told that
口 comes from a picture of an open mouth, a learner may not see the connection) because they don't
yet know the specific stylization rules bridging "picture" to "diagram." The key bridging rule
(formalized across this book as Rules 4–5, 12–13, etc.) is: **kanji never retain a picture's original
curves — a circular shape is always squared.** A bird's round head becomes 鳥's square 日-shape; a
mouth's round opening becomes 口. Per the essay, teaching this stylization-rule explicitly (rather
than assuming learners will infer it) is what makes the picture-to-kanji connection legible, and this
is why the book organizes all 24 lessons around explicit, named stylization/compositional rules
rather than simply asserting "this kanji depicts that picture." (§3, p. 159)

### Deliberately prioritizing the current/modern character form over historical accuracy

The essay explicitly distinguishes this book's method from genuine etymological/historical kanji
scholarship (漢字の成り立ち), and states its priority is **the reverse** of a historical linguist's:
teach the *current, standard* character shape (通用字体) and connect *that* shape to a memorable
meaning, even where this requires analyzing a simplified modern form (学) rather than its true
historical ancestor (the older form 學, where the top "ツ" the book treats as a meaningful
"decoration" unit was originally just an abbreviation mark, 略符, with no independent meaning at all).
The essay works through a detailed case study: 寺 ("temple") is historically derived from 止 "foot" +
寸 "hand" (meaning "to work/do chores," reflecting an old government-office sense, with the temple
sense only added later once foreign monks were lodged in such offices) — but the book instead treats
寺's modern top component as 土 "earth" and explains the combination as "a hand pinned motionless under
earth," i.e. "unmoving," directly motivating the *temple* sense through monks sitting still in
meditation. The essay defends this explicitly as a deliberate choice, not an error made in ignorance:
historically accurate etymology does not always connect cleanly to the kanji's **current** meaning,
and this book's single goal is the current meaning, not etymological truth — so where the two
conflict, the book always sides with whatever analysis best anchors the current meaning in memory,
even when that analysis is acknowledged as etymologically fictional. (§4–§6, pp. 159–161)

### Explicit acknowledgment: this book teaches useful fictions, not word-origins

Following directly from the above, the essay states plainly that from a genuine etymological
standpoint, several of the book's own "unit" analyses are **incorrect** (誤りとされるものもいろいろあり
ます) — e.g. treating ツ as "decoration" when it is historically a meaningless abbreviation mark — and
candidly calls this "teaching a falsehood" (ウソを教えることになります) in etymological terms. The
author's justification: the book's goal is not to train etymologists but to give non-kanji-background
learners (非漢字系学習者) durable advice for connecting a modern kanji's shape to its modern meaning —
a pedagogical mnemonic system, consciously chosen over etymological accuracy wherever the two pull in
different directions. This is an explicit methodological disclosure worth flagging for this project:
every mnemonic/compositional "unit breakdown" recorded throughout `013`–`013f` should be understood
as *this book's own didactic analysis*, useful as a record of how a modern mnemonics text frames
kanji-internal structure for L2 learners, but not a reliable source of actual etymological history.
(§5–§6, pp. 160–161)

### Publication context (作記, "Appendix note")

The book originated as a two-year serialized column, "漢字はむずかしくない," in the monthly journal
*日本語ジャーナル* (Nihongo Journal), aimed at non-kanji-background learners who had completed roughly
half of a beginning-level course, later collected into this single volume; the author notes it also
doubles as a kanji-classroom textbook. The underlying "Kanji Diagram Theory" (漢字図形論) referenced
throughout is developed at greater length in the author's own teacher-reference volume, *漢字の教え方*
(Takebe, ALC, 1989), and in five academic papers published 1982–1986 in 講座日本語教育 (vols. 18–22),
collected in 武部良明, *文字表記と日本語教育* (Bonjinsha, 1991), plus a sixth 1992 article, "漢字は図形で
ある," in 月刊日本語 magazine. (付記, p. 161)

# Japanese — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Japanese Language Insights, Chapters 10-17

**Source:** Zen Loy & Harumi Willow, *Japanese Language Insights* (『なるほど、日本語』/ *Naruhodo
Nihongo*), Soroban Pty Ltd, Sydney, 1996 (ISBN 0 646 28530 0). Chapters 10-17 + Index (printed
pages 52-85; PDF pages 33-49 of 53). Continues from `039_japanese_language_insights.md`, which
covers the front matter and Chapters 1-9 of this same 53-page, 17-chapter booklet, and whose
coverage note documents the marginalia/overlap findings for the book as a whole.

**Coverage note:** Full extraction of Chapters 10-17 (Borrowed Words/gairaigo, Office Speak/ofisu
no gairaigo, I-ro-ha, Kanji, Onomatopoeia/giseigo-gitaigo, Games/geemu, Tongue Twisters/hayakuchi
kotoba, Modesty/kenson) plus a check of the book's Index (pp.85+), which confirmed no further
content pages beyond p.83 — the index itself is a page-number lookup and was not separately
extracted as vocabulary. No additional vision-reading marginalia was found in this half of the book
beyond what was already logged in `039_japanese_language_insights.md`.

**Overlap note:** Chapter 13 (Kanji) covers basic on-yomi/kun-yomi and radical concepts that likely
overlap with Wave 1's "Writing System foundation" sources. As with Chapter 9's kana material (see
`039_japanese_language_insights.md`), this chapter was extracted in full per the dispatch
instruction rather than cross-checked against Wave 1 for redundancy, given the book's compact size.

## Grammar points

### Katakana marking and semantic drift of loanwords (gairaigo)
Borrowed words are written in katakana specifically to flag their foreign origin. Many gairaigo
have drifted semantically from their source word once absorbed — saabisu ("service") came to mean
a free discount/giveaway; baikingu ("Viking") came to mean a buffet via the Swedish smorgasbord
association; arubaito (German *arbeit*, "work") narrowed to mean specifically part-time/casual work
(Ch.10-11, pp.52-59).

### Coined and clipped loanword compounds
Beyond straight borrowing, Japanese productively creates new loanword-based vocabulary via clipping
(pasokon from "personal computer," waapuro from "word processor," sekuhara from "sexual
harassment") and compound coinage from borrowed roots (karaoke = kara "empty" [native] + oke
[clipped "orchestra"]; mooningu setto = "morning set" naming a specific standardized breakfast)
(Ch.10-11, pp.54-58).

### The iroha pangram
The iroha poem uses each of the (then) 47 distinct kana syllables exactly once to form a complete,
meaningful Buddhist-themed verse about the transience of life — comparable in form to an English
pangram, but far more semantically ambitious. It dates to the Heian period (~mid-AD900s) and
provides a second, now-largely-ceremonial ordering of the kana syllabary (iroha order) alongside
the modern a-i-u-e-o order. Two now-obsolete kana (wi, we) survive only in the poem's traditional
reading (Ch.12, pp.60-62).

### Kanji structure: dual readings, radicals, and compounding
Each kanji typically carries both an on-yomi (the Chinese-derived "sound" reading, imported with the
character) and a kun-yomi (a native Japanese "instructive" reading applied to a pre-existing native
word of matching meaning) — some characters have more than one of either. Characters can be
decomposed into a semantic radical (bushu, e.g. the "tree" radical 木) plus other elements, and new
compound meanings are built by combining simpler kanji (hon "origin" + sha "company" → honsha "head
office"; hi "sun" + hon "origin" → nihon "Japan"). Dictionary lookup methods include an on/kun
reading index, a radical index, and a stroke-count index, each of which may double-list characters
whose radical or stroke count is commonly miscounted (Ch.13, pp.63-67).

### Onomatopoeia split: giseigo vs. gitaigo
Japanese onomatopoeia divides into giseigo (words mimicking literal sounds, e.g. animal calls) and
gitaigo (words describing a quality or manner of an action or state, e.g. noro noro "lazily," peko
peko "very hungry"), both functioning grammatically as adverbs. Animal-sound conventions are
language-specific — Japanese dogs go wan wan, not "woof," and cows go moo moo with a different
vowel quality than the English "moo" (Ch.14, pp.68-72).

### Technical vocabulary of traditional games
Japanese traditional games carry their own technical registers distinct from everyday speech: Go
has sente/gote for the attacking/defending position; jankenpon (rock-paper-scissors) has its own
named hand-shapes (guu/choki/paa) distinct from the ordinary words for stone/scissors/paper
(Ch.15, pp.73-76).

### Tongue twisters as homonym/phonotactic stress tests
Japanese tongue twisters (hayakuchi kotoba) typically exploit either systematic homonymy (kisha has
at least four distinct meanings exploited in a single sentence) or dense repetition of a difficult
phonotactic sequence (repeated mo, clustered kyo/kyu, clustered pyo). The book notes these are not
merely a parlor game — aspiring Japanese radio and television announcers must recite tongue
twisters as part of a professional qualifying examination (Ch.16, pp.77-79).

### Modesty (kenson) as a pervasive pragmatic principle
Self-deprecation is systematized into set formulas covering nearly every social situation: minimizing
one's own gift (tsumaranai mono desu ga), minimizing one's own hospitality (nani mo arimasen ga),
deflecting a compliment about one's own skill (tonde mo nai, ashimoto ni mo oyobimasen), deflecting
praise for an achievement (tada no magure), and self-deprecating one's own possessions (usagi koya
for one's own house). These modesty formulas are typically paired in conversation with genuinely
complimenting the other party, producing a conventional call-and-response structure (Ch.17, pp.80-83).

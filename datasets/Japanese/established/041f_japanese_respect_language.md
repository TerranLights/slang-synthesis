## Grammar points

### Two-level honorific imperative system (pp. 131, 134)

True imperatives peculiar to respect language exist at two politeness levels. The lower level uses
the plain imperative forms of the honorific verbs themselves (table above: de irasshai, irasshai,
osshai, nasai, kudasai, meshiagare). The higher, more polite level consists simply of the imperative
forms of those same verbs' masu-level endings (-mase/-mashi), e.g. こちらへいらっしゃいませ
"(be so good as to) come this way" vs. plain こちらへいらっしゃい. Honorific verbs that lack a
plain-imperative form in common use (召す mesu, 思し召す oboshimesu, 上がる agaru) are simply not
used imperatively at either level — their imperative gap is filled instead by 召し上がれ meshiagare,
which is why meshiagare is the only lower-level honorific imperative that doesn't end in -ai.

### The -te kudasai construction is pragmatically the dominant honorific imperative form (p. 133)

Though true single-word honorific imperatives exist, the source notes the -te kudasai periphrastic
construction is "in fact a very common imperative with honorific verbs in general, as it is with
neutral verbs" — e.g. そうおっしゃってください vs. plain そうおっしゃい. Its politeness register sits
*between* the lower and higher true-imperative levels (より polite than the bare lower-level
imperative, less elevated than the full -mase/-mashi level) — a third, intermediate politeness rung
realized periphrastically rather than inflectionally, worth noting as a case where a construction's
syntactic complexity (verb + -te + auxiliary) tracks a real, intermediate point on the politeness
scale rather than a binary register jump.

### Flexible verb-form choice before kudasaru, keyed to the preceding verb's own type (p. 134)

Precedes with different allowable forms depending on what kind of verb comes before kudasaru: (i)
another honorific verb must appear in its -te form (いらっしゃって/いらして/おいでになってください);
(ii) a neutral verb (行く iku, 入る hairu, 待つ matsu) may appear either in the -te form or in the お
+ base form (入って/お入りください — both attested as equally valid alternatives); (iii) a verbal
noun forming its honorific with なさる nasaru (ご連絡 go-renraku etc.) can likewise precede kudasaru
directly (ご返事くださるよう). This is a case where a single auxiliary's combinatorial requirements
vary by the morphological class of what precedes it, rather than applying one uniform rule across
the whole honorific-verb system.

### Negative imperatives are avoided in favor of periphrastic alternatives (pp. 136–137)

Both direct negative-imperative levels (な na attached to the plain affirmative-equivalent form, at
either politeness tier: いらっしゃるな / いらっしゃいますな) are noted as sounding "rather blunt in
practice, in spite of the honorific forms of the verbs" — and are "almost always avoided" in real
usage in favor of three periphrastic softening alternatives: -nai de kudasai ("please don't"), -te
wa ikemasen ("you mustn't"), and -masen yō ni ("[I ask you] not to..."). This is a clear, explicitly
flagged instance of a grammatically well-formed, textbook-derivable construction (the direct na
negative imperative) being avoided in favor of softer periphrasis in actual speech — directly
relevant to this project's register/mechanics-analysis interest in gaps between what a grammar
permits and what speakers actually prefer to say.

# Japanese — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Linguaphone Japanese Course — Introduction & Lessons 1–5 (Vocabulary 1)

**Source:** The Linguaphone Institute, *Japanese Course* — two-volume set: the *Illustrated Textbook*
("coursebook", romanized dialogue/monologue text with numbered picture captions, 75 scanned pages,
impression 1988) and the *Japanese Course Handbook* ("handbook" — Instructions, Vocabulary 1 &
Translations, Explanatory Notes, Vocabulary 2, 164 scanned pages, impression 1990, © MCMLXVIII).
Collaborators include Kindaichi Haruhiko, Hattori Shirō, Hattori Akira, Shibata Takeshi (Tokyo
University of Foreign Studies / National Japanese Language Research Institute). This file covers
handbook **Vocabulary 1 and Translations**, printed pp. 23–33 (Introduction Parts 1–2, Lessons 1–5).

**Scope note (read before using sibling files):** This 239-combined-page source is split across many
sibling files, alternating between **Vocabulary-focused** chunks (drawn from the handbook's
*Vocabulary 1 and Translations* section, which lists every new word per lesson with its English
meaning, dictionary/plain form, and often a translated free rendering of the lesson text) and
**Grammar-focused** chunks (drawn from the handbook's *Explanatory Notes* section, which explains
grammar/structure point by point, keyed to lesson and paragraph number). The *Illustrated Textbook*
(coursebook) itself carries almost no independent glosses beyond small numbered picture captions
(e.g. "2 rekōdo", "3 tēpu-rekōdā") whose Japanese terms and meanings are already captured via
Vocabulary 1's lesson-by-lesson lists — so the coursebook was spot-checked for lesson structure and
picture-caption vocabulary rather than independently re-extracted page by page; this avoided
duplicating effort for no incremental gloss value (per the extraction spec's coverage rule). The
handbook's *Vocabulary 2* (an alphabetical Japanese–English/English–Japanese flat dictionary,
printed pp. 253–~330+) was confirmed to be organized as a flat alphabetical glossary (checked at
"W", "Y", "Z" entries) and is therefore **sampled representatively, not exhaustively extracted**, per
the spec's explicit exception for flat-dictionary-organized sections.

**Coverage note:** every distinct vocabulary item from Vocabulary 1's lesson-by-lesson lists for
Introduction Parts 1–2 and Lessons 1–5, with dictionary/plain forms and compositional sub-entries
folded into the Notes column (or a morpheme breakdown block, for a few illustrative cases).
Pedagogical repetition (the same sentence pattern reused across consecutive example sentences within
a lesson) is not separately tabulated. **This source has no text layer (vision-read from a scan).**
No handwritten marginalia was found on any page read for this file — all content below is clearly
typeset print matching the surrounding page.

**Morpheme breakdown (illustrative examples).**

> **yasaibatake** = yasai ("vegetable") + batake (voiced allomorph of hatake, "field/garden") —
> demonstrates the common Japanese compounding pattern where the second element's initial consonant
> voices (rendaku) when forming a compound noun.

> **getabako** = geta ("Japanese wooden sandals") + bako (voiced allomorph of hako, "box") —
> a second rendaku example in the same lesson, confirming the pattern is productive rather than a
> one-off irregularity.

> **monohoshi** = mono ("thing") + hoshi (verbal-noun form of hosu, "to dry") — a noun formed by
> combining a generic noun with the nominalized stem of a verb, labelling a functional
> place/object ("the thing for drying" → "drying place").

## Grammar points

### Particle wa vs. ga (topic vs. subject marking, introduced via "watashi wa" / "sampo ga")

The handbook's word-by-word glosses consistently distinguish wa as a topic/contrast marker ("as
for...") from ga as the grammatical-subject marker, even when English would use "I" for both. This
distinction recurs lesson after lesson and is foundational to the whole course (p. 25ff). A fuller
systematic treatment appears in the Explanatory Notes section (see later sibling files in this
source).

### Honorific/humble register marking via prefixes and alternate verbs

Multiple vocabulary entries in these lessons explicitly flag register: kanai ("my wife," humble, used
only of one's own wife) contrasts with okusan ("your wife," honorific, used only of someone else's);
chichi/haha (humble, one's own parents) presumably contrast with equivalent honorific terms for
someone else's parents (not yet introduced by Lesson 3). The o-/go- honorific prefixes (o-kawari,
o-taku, o-tomari, go-kyōdai, go-shujin) and the verb pair iru/irassharu (plain vs. very polite "to
be") show that Japanese politeness is marked lexically (via distinct vocabulary items for the same
referent) rather than purely through verb endings (p. 27, 31).

### Ordinal numbers with dai- and the "breath consonant" alternation of ichi/ik'

"dai ik' ka" ("Lesson One") shows the ordinal-forming prefix dai- attached to a numeral, and
demonstrates that ichi ("one") takes the reduced form ik' immediately before a word beginning with a
consonant requiring a following breath/pause (here ka, "lesson, section") — p. 27. The same reduction
recurs in ik' kai ("ground floor," p. 32).

### Rendaku (sequential voicing) in noun compounds

Confirmed directly in the vocabulary glosses themselves: yasaibatake is explicitly glossed as coming
from hatake with the note "-batake, see farther down column... comes from hatake," and getabako is
glossed as "comes from hako, a box" (p. 32). This is the first explicit textual evidence of rendaku
in this source and is worth tracking across later lessons for further examples.

### Dual numeral systems: Sino-Japanese vs. native Japanese counting

Lesson 5's room-count vocabulary mixes hachi ("eight," Sino-Japanese) with hitotsu, roku, yottsu in a
context explicitly describing room counts — the native series (hitotsu, futatsu, mittsu...) and the
Sino-Japanese series (ichi, ni, san...) are both attested as live vocabulary from the earliest
lessons, foreshadowing the need to track which series a given counted noun requires (p. 32).

## Grammar points

### In-group/out-group suppletive verb pair: irasshai-masu vs. ori-masu

A clean third example of Japanese's uchi/soto (in-group/out-group) honorific system, this time
realized as two entirely distinct verb stems for the same meaning ("to be present/at home") rather
than an affix or counter swap: irasshai-masu refers to someone outside the speaker's own group
(e.g. a friend one is calling), ori-masu to the speaker or the speaker's own family member. Worth
cross-referencing against the dedicated honorific-system sources (`041*` "Japanese Respect
Language") for how this slots into the fuller keigo system.

### The -N-desu "feeling marker" generalizes across pain and desire constructions

The book explicitly identifies -N-desu, when attached to itai ("hurts") or to desire constructions
(hoshii "want [a thing]," -tai "want to [verb]"), as marking an internally-felt sensation or
desire of the speaker — a single morphosyntactic marker spanning what English would treat as two
unrelated semantic categories (physical pain and desire/volition). Both symptom-description and
shopping/request chapters converge on the same -N-desu pattern, confirming it as a general
"internal-state" marker rather than two coincidentally homophonous constructions.

### Mimetic (sound-symbolic) pain descriptors

chiku-chiku (pricking), zuki-zuki (throbbing), hiri-hiri (burning), and gaNgaN (pounding, of a
headache) are reduplicated mimetic adverbs — the gitaigo/giongo class of sound-symbolic words
Japanese is well known for. This is a genuinely useful, source-confirmed small inventory for later
mechanics work on Japanese's productive mimetic-word system, a mechanism directly relevant to how
slang/expressive vocabulary is often coined in the real language.

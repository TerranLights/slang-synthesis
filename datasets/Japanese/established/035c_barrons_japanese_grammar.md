## Grammar points

### §6.1 What are particles?

Particles mark a word/phrase/clause's relationship to the rest of the sentence; some carry independent meaning like English prepositions, but because they always *follow* what they mark, the source calls them postpositions rather than prepositions (p. 40).

### §6.2 Particles used with words or phrases

This is the bulk of the chapter (p. 40–67): a long, functionally organized inventory of word/phrase-level particles. The standout structural finding, worth flagging explicitly for later mechanics work, is that several single particle-forms (`de`, `ni`, `kara`, `mo`, `to`, `ga`) each carry multiple, clearly delineated senses that the source itself boxes and labels separately (e.g. `de` alone covers place-of-action, means, totalizing, time-limit, scope, and material) — Japanese particles are highly polysemous and disambiguated by construction/collocation rather than by distinct forms. The `wa`/`ga` distinction (topic vs. subject marking) is given particular emphasis, including the rule that negated sentences require `wa` even where their affirmative counterpart used `ga` (p. 43), and that `ga` (not `o`) marks the object of certain stative verbs and verbal-adjective/adjectival-noun predicates (p. 44–45, deferred fully to §8). The animacy-conditioned existential-verb split (`imasu` for people, `arimasu` for things) that governs locative `ni` (p. 49) is a genuine grammatical-animacy phenomenon worth tracking alongside `jibun`'s similar animacy restriction (§5.7).

### §6.3 Particles used with clauses

A second particle inventory (p. 68–74) covers clause-connecting particles: reason/cause (`kara`, `node`), contrast (`ga`, `keredo`, `noni` — the last marked as *stronger* contrast than the first two), simultaneity (`nagara`), condition (`to`), and sequential action (`kara`). The source explicitly notes that `ga` and `keredo` each independently split into a neutral "and" sense and a contrastive "but/although" sense — the same two-way polysemy recurring on both particles independently (p. 69, 71–72). Also documented here is `ga`'s trailing-off usage, where a clause is left grammatically incomplete with its consequence implied but unspoken, explicitly described as having a conversational softening effect understood by both speakers (p. 69) — this is a genuine indirectness/politeness mechanism, not just an ellipsis.

### §6.4 Particles used with sentences

Sentence-final particles (p. 75–77): `ka` (question marker, with no word-order change — this reinforces the §3.3-1 point that Japanese questions are purely particle-marked), `ne` (confirmation/tag-question, inviting listener agreement), `yo` (emphatic/certainty marker, explicitly flagged as gender-differentiated in usage and risking an assertive tone if misused by a beginner), and `wa` (a distinct, women-only sentence-final particle, separate from topic-marking `wa`, carrying both emphatic and softening functions). These last three are genuine discourse/pragmatic particles rather than purely syntactic ones, and the explicit gendered-usage notes on `yo` and `wa` are high-value findings for later register/slang-mechanics analysis — this source is documenting real sociolinguistic variation, not just grammar.

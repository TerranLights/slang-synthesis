## Grammar points

### A graded (3+ way) politeness system for request forms, explicitly reasoned about by the source

This chapter is unusually explicit (more than any other chapter in the book) about *choosing
among* near-synonymous polite-request forms based on pragmatic fit, not just plain-vs-polite:
-te kudasai (baseline polite request) < -te kure maseN ka (this chapter's recommended register for
complaints — assertive but not obsequious) < -te kudasai maseN ka (flagged here as "too polite" for
a complaint context, appropriate elsewhere e.g. Ch.12's in-flight requests). This is strong
evidence that the book's request-politeness system is a genuine cline with context-sensitive
"sweet spots," not a simple binary — useful for later mechanics work modeling a derived register
system with graded politeness rather than a flat formal/informal split.

### Domain-gated "broken" vocabulary: koware-ru vs. wareru

A further documented instance (alongside ki-masu/haki-masu for "wear" and samui/tsumetai for
"cold," both in `102f`) of a single English concept ("broken") splitting into materially-gated
Japanese near-synonyms: koware-te i-masu for general/mechanical breakage, ware-te i-masu
specifically for glass/ceramic shattering. Recorded here as the fourth such pair found across this
book — worth treating as a recognized recurring typological feature (material/domain-sensitive
near-synonymy) rather than four unrelated vocabulary curiosities.

### Prosody-driven register shift: urusai as complaint vs. insult

A single adjective (urusai, "noisy") shifts from a neutral descriptive complaint to a blunt,
borderline-taboo imperative ("Shut up!") purely via emphatic stress placement, per the book's own
explicit note. This is a rare documented case in this book of *suprasegmental* (not just lexical or
morphological) marking of a register/taboo distinction — worth flagging distinctly from the many
lexical-choice-based politeness mechanisms already catalogued, since it demonstrates Japanese
politeness/rudeness marking isn't purely a matter of word choice.

## Grammar points

### 漢字 (kanji) as ideographs, and dual meanings from pictographic origin

Kanji are ideographs — each character conveys a whole meaning/idea rather than a sound, unlike roman letters which combine into words. The book traces 日 ("sun") and 月 ("moon") through their pictographic development (sun-disc → simplified disc → 日; crescent → 月) and notes that a single kanji commonly carries two related meanings via a conceptual link: 日 means both "sun" and "day" (the sun's rising/setting defines a day), and 月 means both "moon" and "month" (a lunar month is ~28 days) (pp. 2–4).

### 熟語 (jukugo) — compound words from combined kanji

When two or more kanji combine, the result is called 熟語 (jukugo), "compound word." The days-of-the-week words are the unit's worked example: each day's full written form combines a base kanji (日, 月, 火, 水, 木, 金, 土) with 曜日 ("weekday day"); only the first kanji is semantically distinctive, and in practice (calendars, diary sheets) just that first kanji is often written alone, the way English abbreviates "Mon, Tues, Wed" (pp. 6–7). The book also notes a different, non-phonetic compounding seen in 林 ("wood" = two 木) and 森 ("forest" = three 木), previewed here with fuller treatment promised in Unit 2 (p.5).

### Mnemonic links between kanji meaning and the day-of-week it represents

The book gives explicit memory hooks tying each base kanji's core meaning to its corresponding English day name, since the connection is not obvious from meaning alone: 日 (sun) → SUNday; 月 (moon) → MOONday/Monday; 火 (fire) → Tuesday (via the Norse god Tiw, associated with war, linked mnemonically to fire); 水 (water) → Wednesday (both start with "W"); 木 (tree) → Thursday (both start with "T," distinguished from Tuesday); 金 (gold/money) → Friday (pay day); 土 (earth/soil) → Saturday (start of the weekend, good for gardening) (pp. 5–6).

### Kanji stroke-order rules (書き順, kakijun)

Six foundational stroke-order rules are introduced, each illustrated with a worked kanji example: (1) horizontal strokes are written left to right (example: 三 "three"); (2) vertical strokes are written top to bottom (example: 川 "river"); (3) where a horizontal and vertical stroke intersect, the horizontal stroke is usually written first, with exceptions (example: 土 "earth"); (4) a left-hand diagonal is written before a right-hand diagonal (example: 木 "tree"); (5) for a square-shaped kanji, the stroke order follows a specific top/side/bottom sequence (illustrated with 口-shaped squares); (6) when a square-shaped kanji contains internal strokes, those internal strokes are filled in before the square's final (bottom) line is closed (example: 日 "sun"). The combined application of all six rules is demonstrated on 石 ("stone") (pp. 9–10).

### Katakana's four usage categories (previewed from the Introduction)

The Introduction previews katakana's functional range, elaborated later in Unit 9: (1) loanwords borrowed into Japanese from other languages (ウォークマン "walkman," テレビ "television"); (2) foreign proper names — countries, cities, personal names (アメリカ "America," パリ "Paris," スミス "Smith"); (3) stylistic emphasis — making a word visually stand out the way English uses bold/italics/capitals, common in advertising, slang, band names, and headlines (トヨタ "Toyota," パチンコ "Pachinko"); and (4) classification of plant and animal species names (p.x).

### Book structure overview (from the Introduction)

The book comprises 10 units. Units 1–4 focus on kanji that developed from pictures of nature (~3% of all kanji fall in this category) and teach how to decipher more complex kanji built from these as components (continued in Units 4, 6–8). Units 5 and 9 are dedicated respectively to hiragana and katakana, and the author notes a learner could skip these two units initially and return later if prioritizing kanji study. Units 2, 4, 6, and 8 include self-test sections. Units 8 and 10 apply the learning practically — Unit 8 to real-world signs/notices/warnings, Unit 10 to reading passages and letter-writing, including both printed and handwritten text styles (pp. x–xi). By course completion the learner will recognize 1942 kanji's worth of context framing — the book itself cites 1942 as the number of kanji Japanese students learn through compulsory education, versus 5000+ in comprehensive kanji dictionaries (p. vii).

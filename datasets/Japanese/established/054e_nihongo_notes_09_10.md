## Grammar points

### ...nasai: command suffix and its fossilized idioms (p.80-81)
*...nasai* forms commands directed at younger people (especially from mothers to children, and more
generally used more by women than men); fathers sometimes prefer the plain imperative instead. Several
everyday greetings/apologies (*Okaeri-nasai*, *Oyasumi-nasai*, *Gomen-nasai*) are historically built on
the same morpheme but no longer function as commands in modern use — they're fixed expressions.

### Layers of "can/cannot" (p.82-85)
A cline of registers for ability/inability: the dictionary-taught *koto-ga dekiru/dekinai* (easy,
foreigner-safe, but less natural); the potential verb form (most common in native speech); *...yoo-ga
nai* ("no way to...," implying every possibility was already considered, normally negative-only);
*...kaneru* (very polite, attaches to humble-register verb stems); and softened/reserved forms like
*muri-ka-to omoimasu* or *...soo-mo nai* (added to a potential form) for hedging an admission of
inability without a flat refusal.

### Emphatic (non-conditional) ...(t)tara (p.86-87)
Beyond its conditional sense, *...(t)tara* (contracted from *...to ittara*) attaches to a name or noun
to voice surprise or criticism in familiar speech, or to a full statement to emphasize it — often with
the natural second half dropped (*Dame-da-ttara!*). Attached to an adjective as *...(t)tara nai*, it
marks an extreme ("too [X] for words").

### Otosu vs. nakusu vs. ushinau (p.88-89)
Three "lose" verbs with different scopes: *otosu* specifically implies losing something while in
transit/moving (as opposed to misplacing it at rest); *nakusu* is the general-purpose everyday verb,
usable more broadly, including informally for abstract losses; *ushinau* is reserved for abstract
losses (hope, trust, sympathy) in formal speech/writing, with *nakusu* substituting for it informally.

### Te-form minimal pairs and devocalization (p.90-91)
Verbs whose te-form carries a geminate consonant (*itte*, *shitte*, *kaette*) are pronounced with a
syllable's pause before the final *te* and a pitch rise on the second syllable; te-forms without
gemination (*shite*, *kite*, *kaete*) have no such pause, and their *i* vowel is typically devoiced,
giving them a characteristically softer sound. Failing to devoice/distinguish these can cause real
minimal-pair confusion — e.g. *shite-imasu* ("doing") misheard as *shitte-imasu* ("knowing").

### ...n-da-kara's assertive edge (p.92-93)
Adding *-n-da* to *kara* strengthens the reason-marker into something that presumes the listener
should already grasp the situation — reads as assertive, sometimes condescending. It's inappropriate
when explaining the reason behind a favor-request, where *...n-da-kedo* is the natural softer
substitute.

### Kangaete-mimasu as ambiguous soft refusal (p.94-95)
Built on *...te-miru* (interest in an action's outcome), *Kangaete-mimasu*/its familiar-feminine
variant *Kangaete-miru-wa* literally promises deliberation, but is frequently deployed — and
understood — as a non-committal refusal. Whether it's a genuine promise to think it over or effectively
"no" depends on context and the speaker's manner, not on the words themselves; the source notes this
ambiguity likely isn't unique to Japanese.

### Adobaisu vs. native "advice" vocabulary (p.96-97)
The English loanword *adobaisu* has become the default everyday noun for "advice" because it carries
no connotation of hierarchy — it implies an equal, personal relationship between parties. The native
nouns (*kankoku*, *jogen*, *chuukoku*) each carry a narrower, often more formal or moralizing
connotation, which is why *adobaisu* has become so pervasive even in traditional contexts.

### The piece-by-piece confirmation habit (p.98-99)
Japanese speakers routinely confirm information in small increments as they receive it (repeating back
the time, the number of people, each name mentioned) rather than waiting to acknowledge everything at
once. This isn't a foreigner-accommodation or a pronunciation check — it's standard practice between
native speakers, to the point that omitting it (relaying/receiving information in one unbroken block,
or simply saying *Hai* at the very end) can read as impersonally businesslike or even provoke
suspicion that the listener is annoyed.

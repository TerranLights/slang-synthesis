## Grammar points

### Front matter: Preface (pp. [5]-[7], full)

This is the third and final volume of the Makino/Tsutsui series, a companion to *A Dictionary of
Basic Japanese Grammar* (1986) and *A Dictionary of Intermediate Japanese Grammar* (1995). The
authors state the book collects roughly 230 main entries (about 430 items total counting synonym
comparisons) judged most important for advanced-level Japanese learners, drawn from upper-level
textbooks, reference books, and the authors' own teaching materials. A key methodological difference
from the earlier two volumes: for this volume the authors also used the Internet as a source for
natural-usage example sentences, rather than composing every example themselves. Kanji are used
where that is standard practice, with furigana provided only for characters above JLPT levels 1/2
(i.e., not for kanji already taught at beginning/intermediate level). The preface closes with
acknowledgments (Chiaki Sekido of The Japan Times; English editors Sara Harris and Sharon Tsutsui;
colleagues at Princeton and the University of Washington) and is dated Spring 2008.

### Front matter: To the Reader (pp. [8]-[9], full)

Describes the dictionary's four parts: (A) *Grammatical Terms* — informal definitions of the
grammatical terminology used throughout the book; (B) *Special Topics in Advanced Japanese Grammar*
— six essays (Adjectives in Japanese; Interpretation of "N1 no N2"; Politeness and Formality in
Spoken and Written Language; Rhetorical Questions; Metaphors in Japanese; Number Marking); (C)
*Main Entries* — the dictionary's core, each entry structured as: entry name (romanized + hiragana)
→ part of speech → usage restriction (`<s>` for spoken-only, `<w>` for formal-written/formal-speech-
only — **this is the register-annotation system this dispatch was asked to prioritize**) → meaning/
function box → English counterpart(s) → related-expression cross-references → Key Sentence(s) →
Formation rules → Examples → Notes → [Related Expression(s)] comparison section; (D) *Indexes* — a
Japanese index (covering this book's own main entries, its Related-Expression items, and every item
covered in the companion DBJG/DIJG volumes) and an English index (English counterparts of this
book's main entries only).

### Special Topic 1: Adjectives in Japanese (pp. [26]-[29])

Beyond the standard i-adjective/na-adjective split, Japanese has a third class the authors term
"no-adjectives" (e.g., ippan no hito "people in general," tokutei no kuni "specific countries").
No-adjectives behave syntactically like nouns (they take no, not na, before a following noun) but
unlike true nouns they cannot take case particles like ga/o. Some words can take either na or no
(tokubetsu na/no chiryō "special treatment") and some nouns can function as no-adjectives in one
context and as plain nouns in another (byōki no haha "my sick mother" [no-adjective] vs. byōki ga
akka shita "the illness worsened" [noun]). Na-adjectives, no-adjectives, and nouns form a continuous
spectrum (the book's Chart 1), differentiated by whether each type takes case particles and what its
predicate/prenominal forms look like. A handful of adjectives can take either i or na forms with a
subtle difference: the i-form tends toward objective description, the na-form toward the speaker's
emotional engagement.

### Special Topic 2: Interpretation of "N1 no N2" (pp. [29]-[35])

The possessive/linking particle no between two nouns is radically underspecified and resolves to one
of several distinct semantic relations depending on context: Group 1, where N1 modifies N2 as a kind
of relative clause (Kyōto no oji = "the uncle who lives in Kyoto"); Group 2, where only N1 receives
case interpretation (Yamamura-san no sanka = "Mr. Yamamura's participation," i.e., N1 is the subject/
object of an implied verbal action in N2); Group 3, where only N2 receives case interpretation
(pairotto no Fukui-san = "Mr. Fukui, who is a pilot," i.e., N2 is the subject of an implied copula
with N1 as predicate noun); and an additional category for time-specified nouns, where N1 specifies
a time frame for N2 (Meiji no Nihon = "Japan in the Meiji period"). Case markers ga/o/ni never
literally appear between N1 and no, though ni is sometimes replaced by a semantically similar case
particle (e.g., kara no, de no). The authors stress that a bare "N1 no N2" phrase is often multiply
ambiguous and resolved only by context/situation.

### Special Topic 3: Politeness and Formality in Spoken and Written Language (pp. [35]-[37], full — priority topic per dispatch)

The essay's central claim: in Japanese, **politeness** and **formality** are independent axes, and
the copula *da* and its variants are the clearest illustration. The book's Chart 1 lays out a
2×2×3 grid: {Non-formal, Formal} × {affirmative, negative, te-form} × {Plain, Polite}. Non-formal
plain affirmative is da/datta; non-formal polite affirmative is desu/deshita; **formal** plain
affirmative is de aru/de atta; formal polite affirmative is de arimasu/de arimashita. The negative
and te-form cells follow the same four-way split (ja nai/ja arimasen vs. de wa nai/de wa arimasen for
non-formal/formal negative; de/deshite vs. de atte/de arimashite for the te-forms).

Chart 2 (spoken language) maps situations to these forms: non-formal plain = casual conversation;
non-formal polite = ordinary polite conversation, speeches, lectures, presentations; formal plain is
essentially unused in speech; formal polite = formal speeches/lectures/presentations (example:
"...de arimasu," "...no de arimasu" — notably stiffer/more ceremonial than plain desu).

Chart 3 (written language) maps situations similarly: non-formal plain = ordinary letters/e-mails,
business documents for customers, children's books (sets an intimate tone, also used with family/
friends); formal plain = news articles, academic papers, essays, reports, government/legal documents
(the authors note de aru is "more formal in tone than da," and that a single document commonly mixes
both); formal polite = formal business letters/documents for customers (example register includes
"...kyūmei suru shozon de arimasu," an extremely formal "we intend to investigate" construction).

The essay's key structural point: verbs and i-adjectives have distinct forms marking politeness
(hanasu/hanashimasu; takai/takaidesu) but **no distinct forms marking formality** — so da/de aru and
their paradigm are the only grammatical signal of formality level in a Japanese sentence; everything
else (verb/adjective politeness marking) tracks only the separate politeness axis.

### Special Topic 4: Rhetorical Questions (pp. [38]-[41])

Rhetorical Questions (RQs) look like questions but function as assertions — they elicit the hearer's/
reader's recognition and acceptance of an underlying claim rather than seeking an answer. The essay
works through spoken-register RQ examples built on Yes-No and Wh-question frames, each paired with
its direct non-RQ paraphrase (marked with a prime). Depending on context, the same RQ structure can
convey anger/frustration, surprise, joy, a plea, denial, or accusation. RQs also occur heavily in
written/formal register, but there they typically serve to express the writer's view *indirectly*
("Don't Japanese people have excessive expectations of robots?" rather than the blunter direct
assertion) rather than to vent emotion — the essay notes RQs "are used quite frequently" in formal or
public writing specifically because of this hedging function. The essay catalogs five recurring
sentence-final RQ templates (e.g., ~(nai) (no)de wa/(n)ja nai ka; ~(nai) nodewa/nja nai darō/deshō ka;
Wh-word ~ no(desu)/ndesu ka), several of which double as a self-directed "I wonder if ~" reading
rather than a true rhetorical question.

### Special Topic 5: Metaphors in Japanese (pp. [42]-[47])

Organizes Japanese metaphor into three cognitive categories mirroring general metaphor theory:
(1) **Analogy** (ruiji kankei) — e.g., describing a table's "legs" (脚) or a kettle's "mouth" (口),
projecting body-part vocabulary onto objects; (2) **Inclusion** (naihō kankei), subtype **synecdoche**
(teiyu) — a specific instance standing for an entire category (hana "cherry blossoms" standing in for
"flowers" generally; tamago meaning specifically "chicken egg") or a general category term narrowing
to mean one specific member (pan "bread" meaning "food" broadly, gohan "cooked rice" meaning "a
meal"); (3) **Contiguity** (rinsetsu kankei), subtype **metonymy** (kan'yu) — Y of "X no Y" is
dropped because X and Y are closely associated (jitensha [no taiya] ga pankushita = "the bicycle had
a flat (tire)"; Mōtsuaruto [no ongaku] = "(the music of) Mozart"). The essay observes that many
Japanese metaphorical idioms translate directly into English (hi ni abura o sosogu = "add fuel to the
fire"; kinu no yō ni nameraka = "as smooth as silk"), with no clear evidence either language borrowed
them from the other — suggesting, per the authors, that "any language has linguistic soil...fertile
for such metaphors."

### Special Topic 6: Number Marking (pp. [47]-[55])

Japanese nouns are not obligatorily marked for number the way English nouns are; plurality is instead
conveyed through several independent mechanisms: noun repetition (restricted to a small closed set of
examples); Sino-Japanese plural prefixes (ta- "many," as in tajō "many sentiments," tasai "many
talents," tagei "many arts" — though these are now mainly used as na-adjective stems meaning
"versatile/multi-talented" rather than literally marking plurality); noun-modifying phrases
(iroiro na, takusan no, ōzei no, etc.); verbal/adjectival predicates that imply a plural subject or
object (atsumaru "gather," chiru "scatter," ōi "many"); and quantifier expressions (counters:
~nin, ~hiki, ~satsu). The suffix *-tachi* is the one true plural *morpheme*, but its use is
restricted: with proper names or pronouns referring to "a person and their group" it is often
obligatory (Yamada-san-tachi, watashi-tachi), while with ordinary common nouns it is optional and
less natural with animals; Kurafuji (2004) is cited for cases where -tachi cannot attach at all
(e.g., predicate-noun contexts like "Kawamoto to Shibata wa daigaku no kyōshi (*-tachi) da").

## Grammar points

### Two types of adjectives: i-adjectives and na-adjectives (p. 11)
Japanese adjectives split into two morphological classes: **i-adjectives**, ending in *-i* (native
Japanese origin, e.g. *atarashii* "new"), and **na-adjectives**, ending in *-na* when modifying a noun
(mostly Chinese-origin or loanwords, e.g. *shizukana* "quiet"). As noun modifiers both classes precede
the noun, matching English word order; any foreign/loanword adjective borrowed into Japanese
automatically becomes a na-adjective (e.g. *modanna*, *yunīkuna*).

### Adjectival predicates (p. 12)
As predicates, i-adjectives behave like verbs and directly end a sentence meaning "is ~" (*Tanaka-san
no ie wa ōkii*, "Mr. Tanaka's house is big" — no copula needed). Na-adjectives behave like nouns as
predicates: the stem form must take the copula *da* (*Kono hana wa kirei da*, "This flower is
pretty"). Because of this noun-like behavior, na-adjectives are sometimes called "nominal adjectives"
or "adjectival nouns." Some na-adjectives can themselves function as the grammatical subject or object
of a sentence (e.g. *anzen ga taisetsu da*, "Safety is important").

### Exceptions: i-adjectives that take irregular na-forms (p. 13)
*Ōkii* (big), *chiisai* (small), and *okashii* (funny) may each take an irregular na-adjective-style
form (*ōkina*, *chiisana*, *okashina*) when modifying certain nouns, e.g. *ōkina kōen* "a big park."

### Auxiliary adjectives (p. 14)
Auxiliary adjectives attach onto other adjectives or verbs to add meaning (conjecture, desire, ease,
etc.) without changing the host word's own conjugation class. Some auxiliary adjectives are
themselves i-adjectives (*-rashii* "seems," *kamoshirenai* "might," *-tai* "want to," *-yasui* "easy
to"), while others are na-adjectives (*yō da*, *sō da*, both roughly "seems/appears"). A distinct
category, auxiliary *verbs* (e.g. *-sugiru* "too much"), can likewise attach to adjective stems.

### Tenses of adjectives: only present and past (p. 15)
Japanese adjectives conjugate for only two tenses: present (which also covers future meaning) and
past. *Shiken wa yasashii* can mean "the exam is/will be easy"; there is no separate future form.

### Levels of speech: plain, polite, superpolite (p. 16)
Adjectives (via the forms they take or the copula attached) have three speech levels. Plain is used
among family/friends and in publications; polite among adults who are not close; superpolite is rare
in younger speakers' productive speech but survives in frozen set-phrase greetings like
*o-hayō gozaimasu*. Example triad for *omoshiroi*: plain *Omoshiroi*, polite *Omoshiroi desu*,
superpolite *Omoshirō gozaimasu*.

### Demonstrative adjectives: the kono/konna series (pp. 16–17)
Two parallel four-way demonstrative paradigms exist: *kono/sono/ano/dono* ("this/that/that over
there/which") and *konna/sonna/anna/donna* ("this kind of/.../what kind of"). When the referent is in
sight, distance from speaker/listener governs the choice (near speaker = *kono/konna*; near listener =
*sono/sonna*; distant from both but visible to both = *ano/anna*); when the referent is *not* in
sight, *sono/sonna* marks something known to the speaker or previously mentioned, while *ano/anna*
marks something known to both speaker and listener. *Dono/donna* are reserved for questions.

### Special (non-conjugating) adjectives (p. 17)
A small closed class of adjectives (*aru* "a certain," *tonda* "terrible," *taishita* "great,"
*sugureta* "excellent," *arayuru* "every possible," *naki* "late/deceased") never conjugate and are
used only as noun modifiers, never as predicates.

### Noun + no + Noun as a modifier phrase (pp. 17–18)
A noun followed by the particle *no* forms a modifying phrase for a following noun (*Nihongo no hon*,
"a book on/in Japanese"); this also applies to pronouns (*watashi no*, "my"), though pronoun use is
often avoided in Japanese unless specifically needed. *No* can stack onto a noun phrase that already
carries another particle (*tomodachi kara no tegami*, "a letter from my friend"; *Shikago de no
shigoto*, "a job in Chicago").

### Relative clauses as noun modifiers (p. 18)
Japanese has no relative pronouns equivalent to "which/that/who." A relative (noun-modifying) clause
simply precedes the noun it modifies, and its own predicate adjective/verb may appear in any tense
regardless of the main clause's tense (*Samukunai hi wa niwa de hatarakimasu*, "On the days that are
not cold, I work in the yard"; *Tanoshikatta natsuyasumi ga owatta*, "The summer vacation that was
enjoyable ended").

### Part I.1 — I-adjective conjugation paradigm, modeled on ii/yoi "good" (pp. 20–23)
A full i-adjective paradigm has 12 forms: Stem, Prenominal, Present, Negative, Past, Negative Past,
Conjunctive/Te, Adverbial, Conditional, Tara (conditional), Tari, and Noun. *Ii/yoi* is presented as
the model because it is irregular: *ii* is used only in the Prenominal and Present forms, while every
other conjugated form uses the regular stem *yo-* (e.g. Negative *yokunai*, Past *yokatta*, Noun
*yosa*). A regular i-adjective instead keeps one consistent stem across all 12 forms.

### Part I.2 — Na-adjective conjugation paradigm, modeled on shizukana "quiet" (pp. 27–29)
Na-adjectives conjugate via the copula *da* attached to the stem (the form without *na*), producing a
parallel 11-form paradigm (no separate Prenominal is listed beyond the stem+*na* form itself): Present
*shizukada*, Negative *shizukade (wa) nai*, Past *shizukadatta*, Negative Past *shizukade (wa)
nakatta*, Te *shizukade*, Adverbial *shizukani*, Conditional *shizukanara(ba)*, Tara *shizukadattara*,
Tari *shizukadattari*, Noun *shizukasa* ("quietness"). The negative forms optionally include the
topic particle *wa* (*shizukade wa nai* ~ *shizukadenai*).

### Part I.3 — Auxiliary adjective conjugation (pp. 30–32)
Auxiliary adjectives conjugate exactly like the i- or na-adjective class they belong to. *-Tai* (want
to), an auxiliary i-adjective, attaches to a verb's masu-stem (modeled on *iku* "go" → *(iki)tai*,
*(iki)takunai*, *(iki)takatta*, etc.) and carries its own full 11-form paradigm paralleling the
regular i-adjective pattern. *Sō da* (conjecture from appearance), an auxiliary na-adjective, attaches
to the stem of an i- or na-adjective (modeled on *ureshii* "glad" → *(ureshi)sōda*, and *genkina*
"healthy" → *(genki)sōda*) and conjugates like a na-adjective, with one explicit exception: **it has no
noun form** — the book states this directly, since *sō da* describes an observation about someone/
something else, not an abstracted quality nameable as a noun the way *shizukasa* "quietness" is.

## Grammar points

### -nasai's command-register restriction, and the honorific alternative for polite requests (p.120-121)
*-nasai* signals a command to someone younger or lower-status and is essentially unusable in a
genuinely polite request — even tacking it onto an otherwise-honorific verb (*goran-ni naru*, the
honorific of "to see") does not rescue it, since the command force of *-nasai* itself clashes with
the honorific framing. The small number of surviving polite-register exceptions (*Okaerinasai*,
*Oyasuminasai*) are frozen set phrases rather than evidence that *-nasai* is generally safe, and can
be further softened with *-mase*. The correct polite-request pattern instead builds on the honorific
verb directly (*Goran-ni natte-kudasai*, or abbreviated *Goran-kudasai*), optionally extended with
*-(masen)-ka* for additional politeness — a pattern shown to generalize to other honorific-request
verbs beyond just "to see."

### Osu/oshikomu and the productive ...komu suffix: "into" and its intensive extension (p.122-123)
*Osu* ("to push") and *oshikomu* ("to push into") describe genuinely different events, and their
respective suffering-passive forms (*osareru*/*oshikomareru*) are correspondingly non-interchangeable
— being pushed is not the same experience as being pushed all the way into something. *...komu*
attaches productively to verb stems to add this directional "into" sense (*kakekomu*, "to run into
a place"), but the same morpheme separately extends to an intensive/absorbed-in-the-action sense with
other verbs — sitting somewhere neutrally (*suwatte-iru*) vs. sitting in deliberate protest
(*suwarikonde-iru*), or simply having told someone something (*hanashite-shimaimashita*) vs. having
stayed too long because one became absorbed in conversation (*hanashikonde-shimaimashita*) — showing
one productive suffix spans both a literal spatial sense and a figurative intensity sense depending
on the base verb.

### The o-...-desu/deshita substitution: a softer alternative to full honorific conjugation (p.124-125)
Beyond the grammatically-correct full honorific pattern (*Odekake-ni narimashita-ka*), actual
conversation frequently substitutes the shorter *o-...-deshita-ka* form because it sounds less direct
and more refined — a pattern that generalizes broadly across verbs common in social situations
(waiting, searching, visiting) and can even collapse fairly long underlying phrases down to this short
desu/deshita-ka shape. *...deshoo-ka* softens the same pattern still further. This illustrates a
recurring theme across the volume: a grammatically "correct" longer form is often displaced in actual
usage by a shorter, more indirect-sounding alternative that native speakers perceive as more polite
despite (or because of) being less explicit.

### Multi-step topic-opening as a politeness device distinct from word choice (p.126-127)
Politely opening a statement, question, or request is shown to be governed by the NUMBER of discrete
conversational steps taken, not just the politeness of the vocabulary used. The fullest, most reserved
pattern uses three separate steps — a filler opener met with the listener's acknowledgment, a
preliminary naming of what the topic concerns (again met with acknowledgment), and only then the
actual content — compared to compressing everything into one utterance, which sounds insufficiently
polite even when every individual word is fully polite. This principle is explicitly generalized
beyond requests to cover questions and volunteered information as well, reinforcing the essay's core
claim that step-count itself functions as a politeness signal independent of lexical choice.

### Counter-noun word order and pitch-accent collision (p.128-129)
A real documented misunderstanding in the frame story stemmed from two compounding factors: a
pitch-accent collision between "two of" (*ni-hon-no*) and "of Japan" (*nihon-no*), and a word-order
choice — placing a counter+number BEFORE the noun it modifies is grammatical but less natural and more
prone to ambiguity in conversation than the more idiomatic noun-then-counter order (*eega-o nihon*,
not *nihon-no eega*). The essay generalizes this preferred word order across several other
counter/noun pairs (books, money, coffee, leeks), establishing noun-then-counter as the conversationally
natural default regardless of which specific counter is involved.

### Verbal and nonverbal signals marking the start of an utterance (p.130-131)
Japanese speakers conventionally give some kind of signal — a short interjection (*A!*, *Ha!*), a
slight bow, or (especially among men in formal speech toward a highly respected listener) an audible
sharp inhalation/teeth-sucking sound — before actually beginning to speak, especially with someone not
on intimate terms. These are explicitly framed as functionally equivalent alternative devices for the
same underlying purpose: signaling conversational entry, likened to knocking before opening a door.
Skipping this signal (as many foreign speakers do, jumping straight to a greeting or name) can
genuinely startle a Japanese listener even though the content itself is unremarkable — the nonverbal/
paralinguistic teeth-sucking habit often stereotyped as a mere tic is reframed here as a real
discourse-management device serving the identical function as the verbal openers.

### How a news report's opening words encode its register before any content is given (p.132-133)
A news item's opening noun phrase alone can signal whether an upcoming report concerns something
praiseworthy or disgraceful, illustrated by *chuunen-no otoko-ga...* ("a middle-aged man..."), which
Japanese listeners recognize as a reliable advance marker of a criminal/disgraceful story. This works
because bare *otoko*/*onna* ("man"/"woman," with no honorific *-no-hito* qualifier or more clinical
*dansee*/*josee* substitute) inherently carry a denigrating connotation in formal/public register —
appropriate for describing a perpetrator, but requiring the neutral *-no-hito* form when the subject
is instead a victim deserving sympathy. The essay frames this as a specific case of a general
listening-comprehension principle: native speakers constantly predict upcoming content from early
register/word-choice cues, which is part of what makes listening comprehension effortful for learners
who haven't yet internalized these predictive cues — explicitly recommending that learners study
vocabulary in its typical context of use rather than in isolation.

### ...te-iru vs. ...te-aru: cause-neutral state vs. agent-implying state (p.134-135)
Both describe the same resulting state (a door being open, a lock being broken) but differ in whether
an agent is implied: *...te-iru* attaches to intransitive verbs and describes a state neutrally,
without regard to who/what caused it; *...te-aru* attaches to transitive verbs and implies someone
deliberately brought the state about — and frequently further implies the agent acted with good/
deliberate intent (reporting a broken lock with *te-aru* suggests someone intentionally broke it,
while *te-iru* simply reports the state as found). A three-way worked example (bathwater being heated)
extends this into a fuller gradient with *...te-oku*, which goes beyond implying an agent to making
explicit that the action was taken in advance specifically for someone else's benefit.

### Sore-de vs. sore-dewa: continuing one's own narrative vs. pivoting to something else (p.136-137)
*Sore-de* ("and so...") is the speaker's own device for continuing an unfolding narrative; *sore-dewa*
("well then...") instead typically marks the LISTENER redirecting toward something different —
commonly a proposed solution, though also sometimes just a sympathetic/evaluative reaction to what was
just revealed. Using *sore-dewa* where simple continuation was intended is flagged as a genuine
function mismatch, not merely a stylistic preference. A further documented pattern shows the listener
sometimes borrowing the speaker's own continuation-word, *sore-de*, specifically to help the speaker
continue their own story rather than redirect it — an instance of the cooperative, jointly-built
conversational flow already documented elsewhere in the volume (the -te/-te-ne unfinished-sentence
pattern).

### Correctly negating a REASON without negating the action it explains (p.138-139)
Denying that a given reason explains an action — while the action itself still genuinely happens —
requires a dedicated construction (*...-n(o)-ja arimasen* or *...wake-ja arimasen* attached after the
full reason-plus-action clause), not simply negating the final verb, which accidentally denies the
wrong thing (the action, rather than the reason-for-the-action), producing a logically garbled
sentence. A fuller, more basic two-clause alternative (stating the action, then separately denying
genuine fondness for it) is offered as a safer fallback for learners not yet comfortable with the more
compact construction. Two real-world worked examples (quitting a job, failing a task) demonstrate the
expected full pattern: deny the assumed reason, then state the actual true reason in a following
clause.

### The social register of mimicry/onomatopoeic words (p.140-141)
*Pittari* (exact matching/fit), *sutto* (sudden release/relief), and *satto* (quick, especially
leaving) exemplify Japan's productive mimicry-word system, alongside pure sound-mimicry words like
*batan* (a bang/door-slam) and *zaazaa* (hard rain). The essay's explicit general point: such words,
despite a common outsider assumption that they're childish or informal, actually occupy a fairly high
register position in Japanese — used routinely by adults in ordinary daily conversation and frequently
found in serious literary and poetic work — and are often preferred over a more neutral synonym
(*kanzen-ni* for *pittari*; *hayaku*/*sugu* for *satto*) specifically because they convey the situation
more vividly.

### Pitch alone disambiguates Soo-desu-ka as question vs. acknowledgment (p.142-143)
When genuinely asking a question, the sentence-final *ka* in *...desu-ka* is pronounced at a distinctly
higher pitch than the preceding syllables, scaling (within limits — foreign learners are specifically
warned against letting it climb indefinitely) with how eager the speaker is for an answer. But
*Soo-desu-ka* very often functions not as a real question but as a rhetorical acknowledgment
("Is that right?"), in which case the final *ka* should stay flat/falling — raising it in that context
(the frame story's documented real error) reads as impolite, and a drawn-out, raised *ka* specifically
signals distrust toward what was just said. The same pitch-carries-function principle is shown to
extend to other sentence-final particles: *ne* can rise or fall depending on whether the speaker is
soliciting agreement (higher) or not, while *yo* is normally said falling, with a raised *yo*
specifically risking a condescending, child-directed tone.

### Tomo and mochiron as emphasis devices, with a real pitfall for mochiron (p.144-145)
*Tomo* adds emphasis to a copula/verb (more associated with older speakers; younger speakers tend to
reach for *yo* instead for the same function), usable in both polite and familiar register. *Mochiron*
("of course") emphasizes granting a request/proposal and can combine with *tomo* for double emphasis
— but uniquely risky when used to answer a genuine QUESTION rather than respond to a request: in that
context it can come across as implying the question itself was too obvious/silly to deserve being
asked, where a simple *Hai*/*Ee, wakarimasu* is the safe default.

### ...demo as an indirectness marker for suggestions, including in self-talk (p.146-147)
Attaching *...demo* to a suggested activity ("tea or something like that") softens a proposal by
implying the listener has latitude to choose among several unstated alternatives, even in contexts
(like proposing lunch at lunchtime) where no real alternative is actually on the table. The essay
notes this indirect framing appears even in contexts requiring no social consideration at all —
talking to oneself — suggesting some speakers favor it simply because it frames one's own action as
freely chosen rather than dictated by necessity or outside orders, independent of any interpersonal
politeness motivation.

### The reserve-marking ka insertion before to omoimasu (p.148-149)
Inserting *ka* before *to omoimasu* — attachable across adjectives, verbs, and nouns — either marks
genuine uncertainty or simply softens an otherwise-blunt statement to sound more reserved, and
(unlike several of the volume's other politeness devices) is usable even in non-polite speech, with a
more informal *...n-ja nai-ka-to omoimasu* variant in frequent use. A worked social example — reminding
a superior that it's time to begin something — shows exactly when this insertion becomes practically
necessary: the plain *da*-form of the same sentence would sound too blunt/presumptuous in that specific
power-asymmetric context, where the *ka*-softened version (optionally preceded by a hesitation filler)
is the expected register.

### Ano vs. sono for non-physical reference: tracks the LISTENER's prior knowledge, not distance (p.150-151)
For physical objects, the standard proximity-based demonstrative system applies (*kono*/*sono*/*ano*
tracking distance from speaker/listener/neither). But for referring to a person or thing abstractly —
introducing someone in conversation — a completely different rule governs *ano* vs. *sono*: *ano* is
correct only when the LISTENER already knows the referent, and *sono* is required when they do not.
Using *ano* for someone the listener has never met (the frame story's own documented error) is a real,
recognized learner mistake, though shown via a worked conversational-repair example to be a
comparatively low-stakes, easily corrected one in practice — the speaker simply acknowledges the
misjudgment and briefly apologizes once the listener flags it.

### Five ways to mark (or not mark) a sentence's subject, each with a different affective coloring (p.152-153)
Beyond the default neutral topic-marker *wa*, the essay documents a genuine functional menu of
subject-marking choices, each carrying a distinct emotional coloring: *tte* (a contraction of *to yuu
mono-wa*/*koto-wa*) marks the speaker's own emotional involvement, making a statement more emphatic
than plain *wa*; *tara* specifically carries surprise or disapproval toward the subject; *nanka* marks
the named subject as a representative example of a broader (typically disfavored) category, extending
the judgment beyond just that individual. Finally, leaving the subject entirely unmarked — no particle
at all — is explicitly NOT substandard or sloppy speech; it's a legitimate, commonly-used option
(including in polite conversation) whenever no particular emphasis on the subject is called for. The
overall picture reframes subject-marking as an active stylistic choice among several devices, not a
single fixed grammatical slot that *wa* simply fills by default.

### Fixed 5/7-syllable rhythm in Japanese public slogans and traditional verse (p.154-155)
Three real public-safety/campaign slogans are shown to each total exactly 17 syllables, mirroring the
5-7-5 structure of haiku (itself part of a broader traditional-poetry convention including *waka*/
*tanka*'s 5-7-5-7-7 pattern) — achieved partly through deliberate compositional choices: dropping an
otherwise-expected object particle (*o*) to shave a syllable, building in a double-duty pun (*kakeru*
meaning both "to lock" and "to speak to," reused across two phrases of the same slogan), and omitting
an understood final verb entirely. The essay frames this as part of a broader living pattern, not an
archaic poetic relic — many contemporary song lyrics are also noted to gravitate toward 5- or
7-syllable lines, and public agencies deliberately compose safety/campaign slogans with this same
syllable-count discipline for rhythmic memorability.

## Grammar points

### Japanese "pronouns" are ordinary nouns, not a closed grammatical class
Watakushi, kare, kanojo, etc. take the same possessive (no) and objective (o) particles as any other
noun, unlike English pronouns which have distinct possessive/objective inflections (I→mine/me).
Linguists conclude from this that Japanese, strictly speaking, has no true pronoun class. p.114-115.

### Verb choice, honorifics, and gendered particles replace pronouns functionally
A short utterance like "Irasshatta wa" encodes speaker's gender (via wa), speaker-listener
relationship (via the informal past tense -ta), and the subject's in-group/out-group status (via the
honorific verb irassharu) — all without using a single pronoun. p.113.

### Four status-graded verbs for "to eat"
kuu (informal/animals) < taberu (neutral) < itadaku (humble self-reference) < meshiagaru (honorific,
of an exalted other), each chosen by the relative social status of eater and speaker. p.114.

### -te morau / -te itadaku: benefactive ("directional") verb constructions replace pronouns
A verb's te-form plus morau/itadaku (or ageru for the reverse direction) encodes who does a favor for
whom — doing the grammatical work English assigns to pronouns. Layering the causative saseru
("sasete itadaku") adds a further politeness register, used euphemistically in real political speech
(PM Hosokawa's tax-hike announcement). Under-taught by many Japanese-language teachers, per the
chapter's critique. p.115-117.

### The "Watakushi wa gakusee desu" textbook hoax
This canonical beginner sentence is almost never used in real speech; natural self-introduction
("Tanaka desu. Gakusee desu.") relies on context rather than an explicit copula-pronoun structure.
Overreliance on such textbook sentences produces "inter-language" — fluent-sounding but culturally
unnatural speech. p.118-119.

### Taiguugo: Japanese politeness/intimacy as a unified grammatical system
Polite and self-deprecating word choice (itadaku, mairu, ojama suru) and rude/intimate word choice
(kuso, kutabaru) are argued to be two ends of the same system, functionally analogous to how European
languages use swear words to signal social closeness vs. distance — illustrated by a real WWII
anecdote (a general's deliberate profanity putting nervous commandos at ease). p.119-121.

### Natural speech drops particles and the copula far more than textbooks teach
Textbook Japanese (heavy on desu/wa/ga/o) reads as cold/distant to native speakers; linguist Robert
Sukle's fieldwork found most textbooks are stylistically modeled on written, not spoken, Japanese,
and under-teach legitimate particle-free sentences. p.120-121, 125-126.

### The o- prefix usually marks possession-by-addressee, not inherent exaltation
Okuruma ("your car") does not imply cars are inherently honorable; o- here parallels an ordinary
possessive marker, as does the gu- of gusoku/gusai (self-deprecating "my stupid X" idioms). p.127-128.

### Onnakotoba (women's language) is a gradient, optional-use spectrum, not a binary
Some vocabulary/particles are restricted to one gender (o+sakana, the rising-intonation "Ii wa"), but
speakers can and do choose across the spectrum for stylistic effect (a woman may say "Soo shiyoo" to
sound assertive). Contrasted with fully obligatory gendered grammar in some other languages (a
Mongolian dialect's categorically different vowels by gender; Hebrew's obligatory rotza/rotze).
Historically attested since the Heian period (~1000 CE); differentiation sharpened in the Kamakura
period (1185-1333) alongside the warrior class's rise to power. Measured evidence of convergence:
a news announcer's voice pitch dropped from 223.4 Hz (1992) to 202.6 Hz (1995) as women entered
previously male-coded professional roles. p.130-132.

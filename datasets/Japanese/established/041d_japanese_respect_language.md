## Grammar points

### The permissive (-te morau/itadaku) construction as Japanese's preferred indirect-request idiom (pp. 97–106)

Central mechanism of the chapter: もらう morau and its depreciatory いただく itadaku, besides their
ordinary transitive "receive" use (giver marked by から kara), combine with another verb's -te form
to express "receive [X]'s doing of [verb]" = "get/have X do [verb]" or, in its "allowing" sense, "be
allowed to [verb]." With a plain -te verb (friend's own voluntary action): 友達は行ってもらいました
"I had my friend go" / "I received a going by my friend." With a causative/permissive -(s)ase- stem
instead of plain -te (the more accurate gloss is "permissive" rather than "causative" here): 父に行
かせてもらいました "I got my father to let me go" / "I received an allowing-to-go by my father" — in
this permissive variant, the person marked by に ni is understood as the one who *allows*, not the
one who performs the main action. The source flags this as **genuinely ambiguous between two
readings** in the absence of context — 私は父に行かせてもらいました could equally mean "I got someone
[unstated] to let my father go" — resolvable, if needed, by explicitly naming the "allower" with a
phrase like 父に頼んで "by requesting my father."

The source states explicitly (p. 106) that this permissive construction is **the pragmatically
preferred Japanese idiom** for situations where English would use a direct "I want to," "I would
like to," or "May I?" — e.g. そのほんを読ませていただきたいです "I would like to be allowed to read
that book of yours" is the natural Japanese rendering of the more direct-sounding English "I would
like to read that book of yours." This is a notable cross-linguistic politeness-strategy finding:
Japanese systematically prefers an indirect, permission-framed request form where English tolerates
a direct volitional one — worth flagging for any later cross-language mechanics-analysis comparing
request/politeness strategies.

### ちょうだい chōdai and systematic final-verb ellipsis in casual permissive requests (pp. 108–109)

Full permissive-construction sentences are frequently truncated in casual speech, dropping the
final verb/auxiliary chain entirely: ちょっと拝見させていただきます (full) → ちょっと拝見させて
(drops いただきます) → ちょっと拝見 (drops even させて). ちょうだい chōdai itself is described as a
contraction of a fuller construction (ちょうだいさせていただきます) that has lexicalized into a
standalone, kudasai-equivalent colloquialism usable with either a direct object or a verb's -te
form. This progressive-ellipsis pattern — a fully explicit polite-permissive sentence shedding its
outer layers down to a bare noun/verb-stem fragment while retaining (or even increasing) its
pragmatic politeness force in casual registers — is a concrete attested mechanism of
colloquialization potentially relevant to later slang-synthesis mechanics work on register
compression.

### Register/gender variation in permissive-verb choice (pp. 108–110)

Noted as an explicit, attested (not hypothetical) variation: some men, speaking informally to a
friend, use もらえる moraeru rather than the more textbook-correct いただける itadakeru when asking
"Can I have this from you?" (これを/がもらえるでしょうか rather than これを/がいただけるでしょうか).
Conversely, it is "not uncommon" to hear a woman use いただけない itadakenai in an informal
negative-question frame (それを/がいただけないでしょうね, "I suppose I can't have that [from him]?").
The source frames both as attested average-usage patterns rather than hard rules — a further
instance (alongside the Chapter 4 finding on honorific-without-masu) of a gendered register split in
this source's data.

### やる yaru vs. あげる ageru signal relative status of the permissive construction's indirect beneficiary (pp. 112–113)

A worked example (手紙を書いてもらってやってください, "please get [it] written for him") shows that
the choice between やる yaru and あげる ageru in combination with a -te morau permissive construction
signals the relative status of the person for whose benefit the action is done, independent of the
construction's own politeness marking: やる yaru (the neutral "give" verb, used when the recipient is
an equal or inferior) in this frame implies the 2nd person (for whom the letter is written) is at
least equal, probably senior, to the person who will write it; by contrast, (書いて)もらう (kaite)
morau in the same frame implies the 2nd person is junior to the letter-writer (if the 2nd person were
senior, only the ordinary causative 書かせる kakaseru "cause to write" would be appropriate, not a
permissive-receive frame at all). This demonstrates that Japan's give/receive verb system
(yaru/ageru/kureru/morau and their honorific/depreciatory derivatives) encodes relative social
status as a structural, obligatory grammatical choice rather than an optional stylistic one — a
point of direct relevance to any later comparative mechanics analysis of how status gets
grammaticalized across languages.

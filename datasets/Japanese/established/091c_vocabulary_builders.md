This chapter is organized differently from Chapter 1: rather than a small set of competing roots
contrasted throughout, it opens with two productive suffix-forming roots (政 *sei* "government" and
体/制 *seitai*/*sei(do)* "system/form of government"), then shifts into topically-organized reference
lists (organs of Japanese government, Japanese political parties, local administrative bodies,
foreign/international political terminology) interleaved with the book's usual numbered illustrative
sentences. Coverage here captures every distinct term/compound introduced in the prose and in the
reference-style vocabulary lists, but does not reproduce the chapter's 39 numbered Japanese example
sentences verbatim — only the author's own glosses, footnoted terms within those examples, and a
small number of short illustrative phrases are retained, consistent with the coverage rule.

**Morpheme breakdown.** Two of this chapter's illustrative compounds reward decomposition, showing
the same productive pattern Chapter 1 established — a Sino-Japanese root recombining with different
bound suffixes to build a semantic field:

> **立憲君主政体** (rikken-kunshu-seitai) = 立憲 (rikken, "constitutionalism," itself 立 "establish"
> + 憲 "constitution") + 君主 (kunshu, "monarch," lit. "prince-master") + 政体 (seitai, "form of
> government") — "constitutional monarchy." Shows how the chapter's two organizing suffixes
> (-seitai "form of government," and the component noun 君主) stack with independently meaningful
> compounds to the left, rather than attaching to a bare root the way 論/理/説 did in Chapter 1
> (p.41).

> **公正取引委員会** (kōsei-torihiki-i'inkai) = 公正 (kōsei, "fairness, impartiality") + 取引
> (torihiki, "trade, transaction") + 委員会 (i'inkai, "commission," itself 委員 "committee member" +
> 会 "society/assembly") — "Fair Trade Commission." Illustrates how the -i'inkai "commission" suffix
> (contrasted with -chō "agency" earlier on the same page) attaches to a full compound noun phrase
> rather than a single root (p.46–47).

> **都知事/道知事/府知事/県知事** (to-chiji / dō-chiji / fu-chiji / ken-chiji) all share the bound
> element 知事 (chiji, "governor," lit. "know/administer affairs") suffixed to each of the four
> top-level administrative-unit terms (都 to, 道 dō, 府 fu, 県 ken) the chapter introduces just
> above — a clean illustration of how one bound governor-suffix combines productively across all
> four unit types without changing form (p.48).

## Grammar points

### 政 *sei* and the 政/政体/制(度) suffix family for "government/system"

The chapter opens by establishing 政 *sei* as a bound root meaning "government," tracing it to
Ancient Chinese *chieng* "make straight" (entering Japan with Confucianism ~1,500 years ago) and
noting a native-Japanese predecessor read まつりごと *matsuri-goto*, literally "worship of the
gods." De Wolf then shows *sei* operating in at least three distinct suffixing patterns across the
chapter: as a bare compound-final element (政治, 内政, 外政), as the extended form 政体 *seitai*
"form of government" (民政, 共和政体), and as the near-synonymous 制(度) *sei(do)* "system"
(君主制, 封建制度) — with *-shugi* "-ism" (introduced in the prior chapter) offered as a fourth,
even more common way to name political ideologies (民主主義, 独裁主義) (pp. 39–42).

### Topic-specific vocabulary clusters, not a single root family

Unlike Chapter 1's tight three-root contrast, this chapter is organized as a sequence of topical
reference lists once past its opening *sei*/*seitai*/*-shugi*/*sei(do)* discussion: the three
branches of Japanese government (executive/legislative/judicial, pp. 43–46), Japanese cabinet
ministries and agencies (pp. 43, 46), Japanese political parties (pp. 47–48), local administrative
units and offices (pp. 48–49), and finally a comparative international-politics list mapping
Japanese terms for assemblies/parties/administrative units onto UK, Canadian, Australian, and US
equivalents (pp. 49–52). The author explicitly frames this last section as training readers to
describe *their own* countries' political systems in Japanese, not just Japan's (p.49).

### Homograph and near-homograph traps the author flags explicitly

De Wolf repeatedly calls out pairs of terms that look alike but diverge in meaning or reference: 政権
*seiken* ("political power"/"an administration") versus 政見 *seiken* ("personal political views")
share a reading but not a meaning (p.39); 町 is read *machi* "town" in one administrative sense but
*-chō* "municipal district" in another, smaller-city-internal sense (p.48); and 民主党 *minshu-tō*
("Democratic Party," used for the US) is visually close to but distinct from 民主社会党
*minshu-shakai-tō* ("Democratic Socialist Party," used for Japan) (p.47, p.51) — the chapter treats
these as traps for the learner to watch for rather than incidental trivia.

### 州 *shū* as a context-dependent administrative term across countries

The chapter uses 州 *shū* productively but notes its referent shifts entirely by country: a county in
England (ケント州 "Kent"), a state in the US (ニューヨーク州 "New York State"), and a province in
Canada or Australia (オンタリオ州 "Ontario," タスマニア州 "Tasmania") — the same single character
mapped onto different real-world administrative tiers depending on context, which the author treats
as a a key lesson of the chapter's comparative-politics vocabulary section (pp. 50–51).

## Grammar points (Unit 9)

### Simple past tense: -masu → -mashita

The polite non-past -masu ending is replaced by -mashita for simple past ("did X"); です similarly
becomes でした for the past copula. This completes the tense system the course has been building:
non-past -masu (ambiguous present/future, per Unit 7) vs. past -mashita, with time words (きのう
"yesterday," きょう "today," あした "tomorrow" — this last introduced in Unit 7) disambiguating
further as needed. (p.185, 192)

### Instrumental で for means of transport

で marks the means by which an action is performed — specifically transport method here (バスで
いきます, "go by bus"); あるいて ("on foot") is the irregular/adverbial exception that doesn't take
で, using the -te form of あるく directly instead. The question word なんで ("by what means?")
substitutes directly for the で-marked transport phrase in the answer, mirroring the どこ/place-name
substitution pattern already established in Unit 3. (p.185)

### Days of the week: a transparent classical-elements etymology

A genuine comparative-etymology finding, explicitly drawn out by the source itself: each day of the
week is named for one of the seven classical "elements" ancient scholars considered fundamental to
the universe — sun (日, Sunday), moon (月, Monday), fire (火, Tuesday), water (水, Wednesday), wood/
trees (木, Thursday), gold/metal (金, Friday), earth (土, Saturday) — and the book explicitly draws
the parallel to English's own "Sunday"/"Monday" (sun's day/moon's day) sharing the same underlying
logic via a different mythological tradition (Norse/Roman gods for the remaining five in English, vs.
the five-element system in Japanese/Chinese cosmology). This is a clean, well-evidenced example of
convergent naming logic across unrelated language families and is worth flagging for any later
comparative-mechanics review of how calendrical/temporal vocabulary gets lexicalized. The same five
"element" kanji additionally compose with 星 ("star") to name the five classically-visible planets
(火星 Mars = "fire star," 水星 Mercury = "water star," 木星 Jupiter = "wood star," 金星 Venus =
"gold star," 土星 Saturn = "earth star") — the book presents this as a related, parallel
lexicalization of the same root morphemes rather than a coincidence. (p.191)

### Asking/answering "what day" uses the same pattern as asking the time

Consistent with the particle-reuse economy already seen elsewhere in this course (に for both
"at what time" and "on what day"), the day-of-week question/answer pattern directly mirrors the
Unit 7 time pattern: なん ようび ですか / げつようび です, with に added when specifying "on
[day]" for an action (なんようびに いきますか). (p.192)

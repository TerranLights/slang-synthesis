## Grammar points

### Honorific and humble speech — when each is used

Honorific speech is used when the subject of the sentence is someone else (or something associated
with them); humble speech is used when the subject is the speaker (or something associated with
the speaker). The source frames three governing situations: (1) speaking to an in-group senior
(e.g. a worker to their boss, a student to their teacher) — honorific toward the senior's actions,
humble toward one's own, though describing one's own boss to an *outsider* switches to humble even
for the boss's actions; (2) speaking to someone outside one's in-group, generally polite unless
they're clearly junior, with usage among same-status near-strangers trending toward polite by
default; (3) when the speaker is simply unsure of the other person's status, defaulting to polite
language (e.g. between strangers meeting for the first time). (pp. 25-26)

### Honorific verb forms

Some verbs have dedicated honorific counterparts that must be learned individually rather than
derived by rule — e.g. *iku* "to go" → honorific *irassharu*, humble *mairu* (these special
counterparts are listed per-entry in the main text). For verbs without a dedicated counterpart,
three honorific constructions are available: (1) *o* + verb stem + *ni naru* (most common); (2) *o*
+ verb stem + *nasaru* (a politer but less frequent variant); (3) the plain passive form, which can
itself carry honorific force; (4) *dōzo* + *o* + verb stem + *kudasai*, used specifically for polite
requests. (pp. 26-27)

### Humble verb forms

The most common humble construction is *o* + verb stem + *suru*; *suru* can be swapped for *itasu*
for a still-humbler register (*o* + verb stem + *itasu*). (pp. 27-28)

### Transitive vs. intransitive verbs

Transitive verbs take a direct object marked by the particle *o* (e.g. *tegami o kakimashita*, "I
wrote a letter"). Intransitive verbs generally don't take an *o*-marked object (e.g. *kyūryō ga
agarimashita*, "our salaries went up," with *ga* rather than *o*). One systematic exception: some
intransitive verbs of motion do take *o*, giving a "moving through a defined area" reading rather
than a true direct object (e.g. *kōen o hashirimashita*, "I ran [through/in] the park" — the park
isn't acted upon, it's the path of motion). Some verbs have both a transitive and an intransitive
sense depending on which particle marks the noun, with distinct glosses (e.g. *shigoto ni isogu*
"to hurry to work" [intrans.] vs. *shigoto o isogu* "to work faster" [trans.]; *mise ga hiraku* "the
shop opens" [intrans.] vs. *mise o hiraku* "we open a shop" [trans.]). Where a source entry marks
both readings, the more commonly used one is listed first in the headword's parenthetical tag (e.g.
"intrans. and trans."). (p. 29)

### Romanization and symbol conventions

The book's romanization is a modified Hepburn system: macrons mark long vowels (*ā*, *ō*, *ū*), and
*n* (not *m*) is used before *b*/*m*/*p*. Kanji are restricted to the 2,136 characters on Japan's
official jōyō kanji list; verbs whose kanji fall outside that list are written in hiragana only in
the main entries. Key symbols used throughout the main entries: parentheses mark a form that is
grammatically possible but rarely used in modern speech (students are advised to avoid it); a
semicolon in a definition separates distinct homophonous/near-homophonous verbs, or distinguishes
the transitive and intransitive readings of a verb; a colon indicates that every verb/reading listed
before it shares the stated transitivity; an asterisk flags a footnote (most commonly used for the
*iku*/*itte* *te*-form exception). (p. 30)

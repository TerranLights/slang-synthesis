## Grammar points

This is a lexicographic/sociolinguistic survey rather than a grammar textbook, so there is no paradigm/inflection system to document; the recurring structural patterns worth capturing as "grammar points" are the book's own cross-cutting observations about *how* gendered Japanese vocabulary is built — directly relevant to this project's mechanics-analysis phase.

### Asymmetric lexical elaboration as a signal of gendered double standards

Repeatedly, the book finds one member of a conceptual pair (virginity, aging, infidelity, widowhood) carrying a "large harem of synonyms" while its opposite-sex counterpart has only one or two plain terms, or none. *Shojo* (female virgin) has a long tail of near-synonyms and figurative extensions (*otome*, *kimusume*, *sara*, *baajin*, plus *shojo*-as-prefix for "a first" in general), while *dotei* (male virgin) is nearly a lexical singleton. *Mibojin* (widow) is a fully elaborated, culturally loaded term; there is no equivalent word for "widower" at all distinct from the near-unisex *yamome*. *Jonan* ("woman troubles," blaming a man's affair-driven misfortune on a seductive woman) has no mirror term "man troubles." The pattern itself — count the synonym-density of a concept's two gendered realizations as a measurable asymmetry — is a reusable diagnostic for this project's synthesized-slang mechanics work, independent of any one language's specific vocabulary.

### Euphemism treadmill and institutionally-driven relabeling

Several terms are documented going through a full euphemism-treadmill cycle within living memory: *bii-jii* (BG, "business girl") → *o-eru* (OL), replaced via a 1963 magazine-run public naming contest once "B-girl" was discovered to mean "bar girl" in English; *torukojo* ("Turkish-bath girls") → *sopu redi* ("soap ladies"), replaced via a 1984 public contest after a diplomatic complaint; *orudo misu* ("old miss") → *hai misu* ("high miss"), a direct euphemism-for-a-euphemism cascade as each term in turn acquired negative connotation. In each case a euphemism was deliberately manufactured (often via an actual public contest/crowdsourcing mechanism) to launder an existing term's accumulated negative connotations — a concrete real-world mechanism a synthesized slang-evolution model could simulate (new term enters at neutral/formal register, acquires taboo connotation through association with the stigmatized referent, triggers a fresh coinage, repeat).

### Gender-marking is asymmetric and additive, not inherent

Japanese occupational vocabulary defaults to implicitly male and marks the female case explicitly (*joryu sakka* "authoress" vs. plain *sakka* "author/writer"; *fujin kisha* "lady journalist" vs. plain *kisha*; *onna suri* "female pickpocket" vs. plain *suri*) — the book explicitly notes "there is no equivalent term 'male stream'" for the *joryu* prefix. Where a profession was traditionally *female*-coded instead (nursing, nursery-school teaching), the same asymmetric-marking mechanism runs in reverse: newly-entering men coin *self*-marking masculine variants (*hofu* "sustaining father" from feminine-default *hobo*; *kangoshi* "watchful protecting gentleman" from feminine-default *kangofu*) rather than the reverse. The structural rule is not "men are unmarked, women are marked" in an absolute sense, but "whichever gender is the *historically assumed default* for a role stays morphologically plain, and the other gender takes the overt marking" — a cleaner, more general version of the markedness principle than a flat male-default rule, and one a synthesized slang/register system could apply symmetrically.

### The "hand" (te) metaphor family for male sexual initiative

A cluster of distinct idioms built compositionally on the noun *te* "hand" (*te o tsukeru* "put a hand on," *te o dasu* "hold out a hand," *te ga hayai* "hand is fast," *tegome ni suru* "ram a hand into," *teirazu* "unhandled") all encode a single underlying conceptual metaphor — sexual initiative as a male hand acting on a passive female object — at different points along a continuum from mild advance to rape. This is a clean worked example of a semantic field built by extending one root metaphor across a whole lexical paradigm, rather than coining unrelated terms per concept — a pattern directly reusable for this project's synthesized-conlang vocabulary design (pick one body-part/action metaphor, derive the whole register's vocabulary compositionally from it).

### Marriage-market and commerce metaphors for unmarried women

A second recurring root metaphor — marriageability as a commercial transaction — spans several independently-coined terms: *urenokori* "unsold goods," *urekuchi* "market" (for a woman who *did* "sell," i.e. marry), *kurisumasu keeki* ("Christmas cake," unsellable after a deadline), and *kizumono* "damaged goods" (post-virginity-loss). As with the *te*-hand family above, this is a single conceptual metaphor (women as commodities moving through a sale process with a shelf-life deadline) realized across many surface-distinct lexical items — the same "one root metaphor, many derived terms" structure recurs across at least two independent semantic domains in this one book, suggesting it is a productive general strategy in Japanese gendered-register slang formation rather than a one-off coincidence.

## Grammar points

### ...eba/...tara/...to: the one asymmetry among "if" markers (p.60-61)
The three main conditional markers are broadly interchangeable, but *...to* cannot be used to frame
an invitation, suggestion, or request — only *...eba*/*...tara* work there. *Yokattara*/*yoroshikattara*
("if you don't mind") function as fixed offer-softening idioms built on this same conditional
machinery, with *...tara* reported as the form gaining ground over *...eba* at time of writing.

### Okoru and the awkwardness of "I'm angry" in Japanese (p.62-63)
*Okoru* conventionally describes someone *else's* anger; using it reflexively about oneself reads as
oddly detached/objective rather than natural. Japanese has no direct equivalent of "I'm angry" — the
functional substitutes (*fuyukai-da*, *hara-ga tatsu*, *omoshiroku-nai*, *atama-ni kuru*) describe the
anger-provoking situation or its effect rather than asserting the emotion directly, and range from
formal to colloquial in register.

### The presuppositional ...n-desu-ka (p.64-65)
Appending *n* before *desu-ka* isn't a free politeness variant — it presupposes some observed
situation prompting the question. Asked with no visible cue, *n-desu-ka* questions puzzle the listener
("why would you think that?"); the same contrast recurs with permission-seeking (*Ii-desu-ka* vs. the
presumption-laden *Ii-n-desu-ka*).

### Avoiding direct inquiry into intentions/wishes (p.66-67)
Directly asking about someone's *tsumori* (intention) is impolite regardless of how much honorific
morphology is stacked on top of it — the word itself probes too directly. The idiomatic workaround
avoids *tsumori* and the direct wish-question alike, substituting an honorific-marked question about
the action itself (*Oyomi-ni narimasu-ka* "are you going to read it?") for a question about the
listener's plans or desires.

### Yoroshiku doozo vs. Doozo yoroshiku (p.68-69)
Reversing the word order of the standard first-meeting greeting repurposes it as a parting handoff
phrase between people who already know each other — at the time of writing, a usage gaining
popularity particularly in business contexts, distinct from its literal first-meeting sense ("please
be kind to me").

### Transitive/intransitive pairs and reserve (p.70-71)
Japanese has many verb pairs describing one event from an agentive (transitive) vs. non-agentive
(intransitive) angle (naosu/naoru, ireru/hairu, suru/dekiru). The intransitive member is generally
preferred when being polite, because it avoids foregrounding the speaker's own agency/kindness;
adding *...te-ageru* ("did X as a favor") on top of a transitive report compounds this into something
that can sound self-congratulatory.

### Praising skill has a social ceiling (p.72-73)
*Joozu* is the general-purpose praise word; *umai* is its rougher men's-speech synonym, confined to
familiar registers. More importantly, praising a skill at all presupposes familiarity between speaker
and listener — it's considered inappropriate toward someone one must address formally, since the act
of praising itself asserts a kind of closeness. With a formal-register addressee, agreement
(*Soo-desu-ne*) or admiring the result/object rather than the person substitutes.

### Plain negative vs. potential negative as a politeness device (p.74-75)
A recurring pattern: the plain negative of a verb (*omoidashimasen*, *nomimasen*) can read as implying
a lack of effort or interest, while the potential negative (*omoidasenai*, *nomemasen*) implies a
genuine attempt or external constraint (inability) — making the potential-negative form the
face-saving choice when declining or admitting failure in a social context.

### Totemo's unusual dual polarity (p.76-77)
Most Japanese degree adverbs are polarity-locked: some only reinforce an affirmative continuation
(*taihen*, *honto-ni*), others only a negative one (*zenzen*, *marude*). *Totemo* is unusual in having
shifted from historically negative-only ("by any means [not]") to also being usable affirmatively
("very") — a shift some speakers at time of writing still resisted.

### Te-form and requests don't mix (p.78-79)
The te-form's "reason-for-what-follows" use fits apologies and expressions of gratitude naturally
(*Osoku natte sumimasen*), but sounds strange when the reason precedes an actual request — even though
it's grammatically parseable. *Node* and *kara* are the correct connectors there, with *...n-da-kedo*
available as a more reserved-sounding alternative when *kara*/*node* would feel too assertive.

# Japanese — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Teach Yourself Beginner's Japanese Script — Unit 8 (第八課, Signs and notices)

**Source:** Helen Gilhooly, *Teach Yourself Beginner's Japanese Script* (Hodder Headline, first published 1996, this edition 2003), Unit 8 ("第八課") pp. 103–123.

**Coverage note:** every category of real-world sign/notice covered — places/shops, geography (islands/cities), warnings, food/drink, sightseeing/entertainment, travel/transport, and accommodation — totaling (per the source's own count) 119 signs across the unit's main sections and three "kanji build up" supplementary sections (the latter explicitly marked by the source itself as optional/skippable, and still extracted in full here per the coverage rule). Repeated review/matching drills reusing already-tabulated signs are skipped per `../../00_Reference_Extraction_Spec.md`. Given the sheer density of this unit (119 signs, by far the largest single-unit vocabulary load in the book), compound/sign vocabulary is grouped by theme in the table below mirroring the source's own organization, rather than fully re-deriving mnemonic prose for every single entry — mnemonic detail is kept for entries with a genuinely illustrative decoding story; purely transparent compounds of already-known kanji are glossed without repeating a story.

**Vision-reading note:** continues from `025g_ty_beginners_japanese_script.md` — same DJVU source, vision-read from rendered page images (two printed pages per scan image). This unit includes several real photographs of authentic Japanese signage (shop fronts, station names, lantern text) alongside the book's own typeset explanatory prose — all photographic sign content is the book's own intentional illustrative material, not reader marginalia. No handwritten marginalia was found in this unit.

## Grammar points

### Seven "place" kanji and their radical logic

The unit systematically teaches seven kanji meaning different types of place — 店 (shop), 屋 (store/roof), 局 (bureau/office), 所 (place, office-type), 場 (place, grounds-type), 館 (hall/large building), 園 (garden) — each explained through its specific radical and component story, establishing a reusable category the learner can recognize across many compound signs (pp. 105–106).

### Train-direction convention: 上り/下り ("up"/"down")

On Japanese railways, 上り ("up") trains are defined as travelling toward Tokyo regardless of actual compass direction, while 下り ("down") trains travel away from Tokyo — a nationwide directional convention centered on the capital, distinct from ordinary compass-direction language (p.105).

### Honorific お- prefix

The hiragana prefix お (sometimes translated "honourable") attaches to certain nouns to make them sound more polite/genteel, e.g. お寺 ("temple") and お手洗い ("the honourable hand-washing place" = toilet); the word-final hiragana is sometimes dropped in casual practice (お手洗 without い) (pp. 105, 108).

### 場 vs. 所 vs. 屋 vs. 室: distinguishing near-synonymous "place" kanji by connotation

Although all broadly mean "place," the book draws out working distinctions: 所 marks an office or "over-the-counter" service place (tourist information, foreign exchange counters); 場 marks a place with physical grounds (factories, markets, car parks); 屋 marks a shop/store (and, confusingly, is also the generic word for "roof"); 室 specifically marks a room with a roof, distinguishing it from 屋's shop sense via a different radical (宀 "lid/roof" vs. 尸 "flag") despite a visually similar overall shape (pp. 105–106, 112).

### Compound place-names built from two independently meaningful kanji

The unit's "kanji build up" sections (explicitly marked optional/skippable by the source) extend the place-kanji system into real signage vocabulary — banks (銀行, "silver" + "go"), shopping streets (商店街), travel information offices (旅行案内所), currency exchanges (両替所), fare-adjustment offices (精算所), ticket offices (切符売場), theatres (劇場), cinemas (映画館), inns (旅館), and museums (博物館) — each decoded via the same component-story method taught since Unit 2 (pp. 108–110).

### Train and vehicle sign vocabulary built on 車

A cluster of transport signs builds on 車 ("vehicle"): 空車/満車 (unoccupied/occupied taxi, using 空 "empty/sky" and 満 "full"), 列車 (long-distance train, distinguished from the more generic 電車), 寝台車 (sleeper train), and 自転車 (bicycle, literally "self-rotating vehicle") (pp. 111–112).

### 洋 vs. 和 as a recurring Western/Japanese-style contrast

洋 ("ocean/West") and 和 ("Japan/peace," the old Chinese name for Japan) form a systematic contrastive pair applied across multiple compound words: 洋室/和室 (Western-/Japanese-style room), 洋食/和食 (Western/Japanese cuisine), 洋式/和式 (Western-/Japanese-style, generally) (pp. 112, 115).

### Compass directions and their mnemonic shapes

北 (north), 南 (south), 東 (east), 西 (west) are each given a visual mnemonic (back-to-back people for cold north; a yen-symbol resemblance for a wealthier south; sun rising behind a tree for east; resemblance to 四 "four" for west, explicitly flagged to avoid confusing the two) (p.115).

### Geography: Japan's main islands and major cities

A labelled map introduces Japan's four main islands (本州 Honshu, 北海道 Hokkaido, 四国 Shikoku, 九州 Kyushu) and major cities (東京 Tokyo, 横浜 Yokohama, 大阪 Osaka, 京都 Kyoto, 広島 Hiroshima, 長崎 Nagasaki, plus Sapporo 札幌), each given a story-clue based on its component kanji's literal meaning. The source explicitly flags that the kanji for Sapporo (3), Hiroshima's 島, and Nagasaki's 崎 are not otherwise taught in the book and are "included for interest only" (p.116).

### Warning-sign vocabulary built on 禁 ("forbidden")

A cluster of warning/prohibition signs builds on 禁: 禁止 (no/not allowed, + 止 "stop"), 厳禁 (strictly forbidden), 禁煙 (no smoking), 駐車禁止 (no parking), 立入禁止 (no admittance), 土足厳禁 (outdoor shoes strictly forbidden) — alongside three more with their own distinct kanji: 非常口 (emergency exit), 注意 (caution), 危険 (danger) (p.117).

### Five paired-opposite sign kanji

The unit groups five functionally paired opposites as its final core "Explanation" section: 左/右 (left/right), 押す/引く (push/pull), 開/閉 (open/close), 到着/出発 (arrival/departure), 営業中/休業中 (open for business/closed) — framed as an efficient way to learn two related signs via a single contrastive mnemonic pass (p.118).

### Miscellaneous food and transport signage (焼, and train-speed terms)

焼 ("grilled") underlies several well-known Japanese food terms given in their common romanized forms alongside kanji: 焼肉 (yakiniku), 焼き鳥 (yakitori), お好み焼き (okonomiyaki), すき焼き (sukiyaki) — and a parallel cluster of train-speed/type terms: 新幹線 (shinkansen/bullet train), 地下鉄 (underground, literally "ground under iron"), 急行 (express, "hurry go"), 特急 (special express, "special hurry") (p.118).

### Unit 8 milestone summary

By the unit's own count, the learner has been exposed to a minimum of 76 kanji signs in the unit's core sections, plus 12 island/city names and 31 signs across the three optional "kanji build up" sections, for an overall total of 119 signs — explicitly flagged by the source as "a huge amount," with the expectation of using the unit's own end-of-chapter thematic checklist (reproduced in the Vocabulary table above) for ongoing review rather than one-pass memorization (p.121).

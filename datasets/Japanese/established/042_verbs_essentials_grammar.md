## Grammar points

### General principles of Japanese verbs (Ch. 3, p. 7)

The book frames four simplifying principles up front, each a genuine typological contrast with
English rather than redundant with Barron's own framing: the verb **always comes at the end of the
sentence**; the verb form **never changes for person, number, or gender** of the subject; there are
**only two irregular verbs in the entire language** (`kuru` "come," `suru` "do"); and there are
**only two basic tenses**, past and present/non-past (future intention is expressed by the
non-past). The **Dictionary Form always ends in the vowel u** and functions as both the citation form
and — distinctively — as a usable Informal-Form present-tense sentence-ender in its own right (Ch. 17,
below), not merely an abstract citation form.

### Four Levels of Speech (Ch. 3, p. 7–8)

This book names the same formality continuum Barron's covers but gives it a slightly different
four-way split and distinct labels worth cross-referencing: **Abrupt** (speaking "down" to children,
animals, or social inferiors), **Informal** (close friends, family, casual situations), **Normal-polite**
(a.k.a. the "Desu-masu Form," used with strangers/business contacts), and **Honorific** (very formal
situations or addressing superiors). It explicitly notes that **Informal verb endings are also used in
impersonal speech** (broadcast news, formal public announcements) even though that register is socially
distant from casual friend-to-friend talk — a register-vs-formality distinction not spelled out this
explicitly in the prior Barron's extraction. This book's own worked examples are stated to be
"primarily in the Normal-polite range" except where the Informal (Ch. 17–18) or Honorific (cross-
referenced to this book's own later Ch. 37, see `042b`) chapters are the explicit topic.

### Verb classification: Ichidan vs. Yodan, and the irregulars (Ch. 4, pp. 8–10)

This book's own classificatory vocabulary (distinct from Barron's "Class I/Class II" terms, though
describing the same underlying split): **Ichidan** ("one-step"/vowel-stem) verbs end in `-iru` or
`-eru` in the Dictionary Form and conjugate uniformly; **Yodan** ("four-step"/consonant-stem) verbs
are everything else, further subdivided by **which of nine syllables** (`-u, -tsu, -ru, -ku, -gu, -su,
-nu, -mu, -bu`) the Dictionary Form ends in — named accordingly U-verbs, TSU-verbs, RU-verbs,
KU-verbs, GU-verbs, SU-verbs (no equivalent *CHI-verb* or *ZU-verb* class exists), **NU-verbs**
(the book states explicitly that `shinu` "to die" is the *only* NU-verb in the language), MU-verbs,
and BU-verbs. A flagged exception list (reproduced in the vocabulary table above) names ten Yodan
verbs that are superficially Ichidan-lookalikes (end in `-iru`/`-eru`) but conjugate as Yodan —
`chiru, hairu, hashiru, iru` ("need"), `kaeru, kagiru, keru, kiru, mairu, nigiru, shiru, shaberu` —
a genuinely new, explicitly-flagged exception-class list not previously captured from either `028` or
`035d`.

### Desu (Ch. 5, p. 11)

`Desu` ("to be"/"to equal") is treated as its own grammatical entity distinct from ordinary verbs. The
pattern `[A] wa [B] desu` covers three functions: **definition** (B is a noun/noun phrase identifying
what A is), **description** (B is an adjective/adjective phrase), and **location** (B is a relative-
position word like `koko/soko/asoko/mukō/doko`). Its four basic conjugated forms are `desu` (present
positive), `dewa arimasen` (present negative), `deshita` (past positive), `dewa arimasen deshita`
(past negative); casual speech contracts `dewa` to `ja` (`ja arimasen`, `ja arimasen deshita`), though
the book uses the standard `dewa` form throughout.

### Stem + Base + Ending, and the Verb Base Chart (Ch. 6–7, pp. 12–14)

**The book's single most distinctive organizing device**, not paralleled in `028` or `035`. Every
verb in a sentence is analyzed as **Stem** (the invariant part carrying general meaning — the
Dictionary Form minus its final syllable for Yodan/Ichidan verbs) + **Base** (a pure linking element
with no meaning of its own, chosen by two independent criteria: the verb's own row in the chart,
dictated by its Dictionary-Form ending, and the specific ending's required Base column) + **Ending**
(the part carrying the actual grammatical/semantic content). `Kuru` and `suru` are irregular
specifically because their *stem itself* changes across bases, unlike every other verb.

Ichidan verbs have no distinct Base 1 or Base 2 (any ending requiring those bases attaches directly
to the stem); `kuru` uniquely has **two** Base-4 forms (`kure-`/`kore-`), with the specific ending
determining which is required. This chart is the generative core from which every inflected form in
the book (and, implicitly, in the language) is produced — the book's Chapters 10–16 (below) are
organized strictly by which base an ending requires, a structurally different organizing principle
than Barron's rule-by-rule derivation narrative.

### The Masu Form (Ch. 8, p. 15)

The "Desu-masu" Normal-polite register's core ending set, always attached to Base 2: `-masu`
(non-past), `-mashita` (past), `-masen` (negative non-past), `-masen deshita` (negative past),
`-mashō` (inclusive "let's..."), `-mashō ka` (inclusive query "shall we...?"). Functionally identical
to Barron's own treatment of the polite/plain distinction (`035d`) — captured here only for
completeness of this book's own Base-chart-centric framing, not as new information.

### Verb-ending terminology and notation (Ch. 9, p. 16–17)

The book's own shorthand, used throughout its ending catalogs: Stem+Base1=**V1**, +Base2=**V2**,
+Base3=**V3**, +Base4=**V4**, +Base5=**V5**, +Base6=**V6**/**TE-form**/**Vte**, +Base7=**V7**/
**TA-form**/**Vta**. Some endings convert the verb into an **adjective phrase** that then inflects by
adjective rules (e.g. `V2+tai` "want to" is a **True Adjective**: `-takatta`/`-takunai`/
`-takunakatta`; `V2+sō` "looks like it's about to..." is a **Quasi Adjective**: `taoresō na hito`
"the person who looks like he's about to collapse"). Combining endings is explicitly licensed
(`mi ni iku` "go to see" + `-tai desu` → `mi ni ikitai desu` "want to go to see").

### Base 1 ("Negative Base") endings (Ch. 10, pp. 18–22)

Base 1 endings are mostly negative in function. Representative set (full derivation chart per the
Base Chart above; only genuinely distinct notes given here, since the negative-adjective-inflection
mechanics themselves duplicate Barron's `035d` treatment): `-nai [desu]` informal/Normal-polite
negative (exceptional: `aru`→`nai`, not `aranai`); `-naide` "without doing" and `-naide kudasai`
"please don't" (negative-request register, cross-references the book's own more polite gerund+
`kudasai` positive-request pattern); `-nakatta [desu]` informal negative past; `-nakattara`/
`-nakereba` conditional-negative pairs ("if/when [I] don't"); `-nakereba narimasen` and `-nakute
[wa] ikemasen`/`-nakute wa dame desu` — two parallel **obligational** ("must") constructions, both
exceptional for `aru` (→ `nakereba narimasen`, `nakute wa ikemasen`); `-nakute` as the TE-form of the
informal negative, "without doing, not doing" with a notable **affirm-one-negate-the-other** idiomatic
use (`Tegami o kakanakute, denwa o shimashita` "I didn't write a letter; I telephoned [instead]");
`-nakute mo` "even if [I] don't"; `-nakute mo ii [desu]` "it's okay not to"; `-reru/-rareru` **passive**
(Yodan/Ichidan split, irregulars `korareru`/`sareru`) with a **Potential/Passive syncretism** for
Ichidan verbs (same form serves both meanings) and the exceptional `miru`→`mieru` "be visible" (not
`*mirareru`) and `kiku`→`kikoeru` "be audible"; `-seru/-saseru` **causative** (Yodan/Ichidan split,
irregulars `kosaseru`/`saseru`); and `-zu ni` "without doing" (irregulars `kozu`, `sezu`) — a
genuinely distinct negative-gerund alternative to `-naide` not previously captured.

### Base 2 ("Noun-forming"/"Continuative Base") endings (Ch. 11, pp. 23–27)

Base 2 with **no ending** either nominalizes the verb (`V2+mono` "food/things to eat" from `taberu`)
or serves as a **literary-register continuation form** functionally parallel to, but more formal than,
the TE-form series ("Kyōto wa mukashi Nihon no miyako deari, bunka no chūshin ni narimashita" — "Kyoto
was the ancient capital... and [literary 'and'] became the cultural center"). **Compound-verb
formation** (V2 of one verb + Dictionary Form of a second) is a highly productive pattern, illustrated
with `arukimawaru` "walk around" (aruku+mawaru), `hanashiau` "talk together, discuss" (hanasu+au),
`mochikaeru` "take home" (motsu+kaeru), `kakinaosu` "rewrite" (kaku+naosu) — directly parallel to, but
with different specific lexical pairs than, Barron's own directional-compound-verb discussion. Further
Base-2 endings: `-agaru`/`-ageru` ("do up(wards)/finish," intransitive/transitive pair —
`dekiagaru`/`tachiagaru` vs. `oshiageru`/`shiageru`); `-dasu` "do suddenly" (`tobidasu`,
`hashiridasu`); `-hajimeru` "begin doing"; `-kata` "way/method of doing" (nominalizing; idiomatic
`shikata ga arimasen` "it can't be helped"); `-komu` "do in/into" (`kakikomu` "write in," `tobikomu`
"jump/dive/fly into," `mōshikomu` "apply for"); the Masu Form (cross-referenced to Ch. 8); `-nagara`
"while doing"; `-nasai` abrupt command ("Ask your father!"); `ni iku/kuru` "go/come [somewhere] to
do"; `-nikui` "difficult to" (True Adjective) with a **distinct idiomatic-extension note**: `minikui`
(from `miru`) can mean either "hard to see" *or* "ugly"; `kikinikui` (from `kiku`) "hard to hear" or
"unpleasantly noisy"; `hanashinikui` "hard to talk to" — a genuinely new semantic-extension detail;
`-owaru` "finish doing"; `-sō [desu]` "looks like it's about to" (Quasi Adjective); `-sugiru` "do too
much"; `-tagaru` "want to" (more emotionally intense than `-tai`); `-tai [desu]` desiderative (True
Adjective); `-tsuzukeru` "continue doing"; `-yasui` "easy to" (True Adjective).

### Base 3 ("Dictionary Form") endings (Ch. 12, pp. 28–31)

Base 3 **is** the Dictionary Form itself, and — distinctively — can also stand alone as an **Informal
present-tense sentence-ender** in casual registers, a dual function worth flagging since it means the
citation form and a live inflectional form are literally identical. Representative endings: `hodo`
"to the extent that" (idiomatic `suteru hodo okane o motte iru` "have money to burn," lit. "have money
to the extent of throwing it away"); `kagiri` "to the extent/limits of"; `kawari [ni]` "instead of, in
exchange for"; `ki ga aru/suru` and `ki ni naru` "feel like doing/come to feel like doing" (a small
family of mental-inclination idioms); `koto ga aru` "do occasionally/from time to time"; `koto ga
dekiru` **Potential Form** periphrasis, with a genuinely distinct idiomatic flag: **`benkyō
dekimasu/dekimasen` refers to mental state/ability to concentrate** ("I just can't think straight"),
whereas ability/inability to study *because of time or circumstance* instead requires the fuller
`benkyō suru koto ga dekimasu/dekimasen` — a fine distinction not previously captured from either
other verb source; `koto ni naru`/`yō ni naru` "come about, turn out that," and their volitional
counterparts `koto ni suru`/`yō ni suru` "decide to"; `made`/`made ni` "until/by the time [I] do";
`mae [ni]` "before doing"; `mono` as a relative-clause/emphatic-suggestion marker; `na` abrupt negative
command ("Don't do it!"); `tame [ni]` "in order to" (also "because/since" when combined with the
Progressive Form); `to` "if [I] do" (conditional); `tochū [desu]` "en route, on the way" (motion
verbs only); `tokoro [desu]` "about to," or "in the process of" with the Progressive Form; `to
shitara`/`to sureba`/`to suru to` **Emphatic Conditional** ("if [I] were to..."); `to shite mo` "even
if"; `tsumori [desu]` "intend to"; `yō ni` "in order to"; `yotei [desu]` "plan to."

### Base 4 ("Conditional Base") endings (Ch. 13, pp. 32–33)

All Base-4 forms end in the vowel `-e`. `-ru` forms the **Potential** (Yodan verbs and `kuru` only —
Ichidan verbs instead use V1+`rareru`, and `suru` becomes suppletive `dekiru`), reclassifying the
derived verb as Ichidan regardless of the source verb's original class; exceptional potentials
`mieru` (be visible, from `miru`) and `kikoeru` (be audible, from `kiku`) are restated here as direct
Base-4 irregulars. **No ending** gives the **Abrupt command** ("Hanase!" "Let go!"; `kuru`→`koi`;
two-word `suru`-verbs →`-seyo`, or the more colloquial `yare`). `-ba` forms the **conditional**
("if/when [I] do"; `kuru`→`kureba`; `desu`→`naraba`), and `-ba yokatta [desu]` means "it would have
been better if [I] had done."

### Base 5 ("Conjectural Base") endings (Ch. 14, p. 34)

All Base-5 forms end in a long `ō`. **No ending** forms the **informal inclusive command** ("Let's...",
casual register, parallel to the Normal-polite `-mashō`); `ka` forms the parallel **informal inclusive
query**; `to omou` "I think I will..."; `to suru` "be about to" (most often in the Progressive `to
shite iru`).

### Base 6 (TE-form) endings (Ch. 15, pp. 35–37)

`Iku` is irregular in the TE-form (`itte`, not the expected `*iite`); `desu`'s TE-form is `de`. The
TE-form's primary function is the **Continuation Form**: a verb series in rough chronological/mild
cause-effect sequence, with only the *final* verb in the series carrying the sentence's actual tense
(all others appear bare in TE-form) — functionally identical to Barron's own TE-form clause-chaining
discussion (`035d`/`e`), not re-derived here. Distinctive auxiliary/idiomatic combinations captured:
**`ageru`/`yaru`** benefactive "do for [someone else]" (`yaru` flagged as more casual, `ageru`
preferred toward the beneficiary); `kara` "after doing"; `kudasai` polite request (politeness raised
further by `-masu ka`/`-masen ka` tags); **`kuru`** "do and come back," with three idiomatic
sub-collocations: `motte kuru` "bring" (restricted to things, not people — contrasted implicitly with
`tsurete kuru` for people, which this book does not separately list), `itte kuru` "be right back" (lit.
"go and come"), and `wasurete kuru` "forget to bring" (lit. "forget and come"); `miru` "do and see/try"
(`tabete mite kudasai` "eat it and see," `haitte mimashita` "went in to check it out"); `mo` "even if
[I] do."

### Base 7 (TA-form) endings (Ch. 16, pp. 38–39)

`Iku` is again irregular (`itta`); `desu`'s TA-form is `datta` (`deatta` also possible). **No ending**
gives the **informal past tense**, parallel in register to the Base-6 informal-command pattern above.
Distinctive endings: `ato` "after having done"; `bakari [desu]` "have just done"; `koto ga aru` "have
[ever] done, have had the experience of" (note this string is homographic with, but semantically
distinct from, the Base-3 `koto ga aru` "do occasionally" above — the book does not itself cross-
reference the ambiguity, which is worth flagging for later mechanics work;) `ra` "if/when [I] do/did"
(shifts to a "when [I] did" reading specifically when the main verb is past tense); `ri` marks a
**nonsequential** verb series (contrasted with the TE-form's implied chronological series), usually
closed off with some form of `suru`, implying unlisted additional actions also occurred; `tame [ni]`
"because [I] did" (distinct from the Base-3 purposive `tame [ni]` "in order to" — again a same-string,
different-base, different-meaning pair worth flagging); `tokoro [desu]` "just did, at the point of
having done."

### The Informal Form (Ch. 17, pp. 40–41)

A verb/adjective-ending register distinct from Normal-polite `-masu`/`desu` forms, used in (a) casual
social speech with family/close friends and (b) impersonal speech (broadcast news, formal public
announcements) — the same two-pronged usage split already noted under "Four Levels of Speech" above.
The book gives an explicit **Informal-to-Normal-polite mapping table**: V3 ↔ V2+masu (non-past); Vta ↔
V2+mashita (past); V1+nai ↔ V2+masen (negative non-past); V1+nakatta ↔ V2+masen deshita (negative
past); V5 ↔ V2+mashō (inclusive). Worked with the example verb `yomu`: `yomu`/`yomimasu` "read/reads";
`yonda`/`yomimashita` "read [past]/did read"; `yomanai`/`yomimasen` "do not/does not read";
`yomanakatta`/`yomimasen deshita` "did not read"; `yomō`/`yomimashō` "Let's read."

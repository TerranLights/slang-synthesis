## Grammar points

### おつき合い (honorific お- on tsukiai)

The お prefix is ordinarily placed in front of *tsukiai* to express interpersonal relations. In
this context it implies, with humorous effect, the relations between countries (international
おつき合い). (p.13)

### ……ものだと思う (…mono-da-to omou)

If *mono da* were omitted from a sentence of this shape, the literal meaning would remain the
same — *mono da* simply adds emotional emphasis, comparable to English *hontoo ni…da naa* ("it
really is…"), conveying the depth of the writer's feelings about a situation. Example from the
main text: 国際的なおつき合いが盛んになったものだと思う。("International friendships have blossomed
at an incredible rate [, I find myself thinking]." (pp.14–15)

### お客がある (okyaku ga aru)

Equivalent to *okyaku ga kuru*: "A guest is coming to visit." The expressions *raikyaku ga aru* and
*hito ga kuru* can also be used in this same context. (p.15)

### 余裕 (yoyuu)

Used to refer to space and time, and also used in economic contexts. Example given: *Sonna mono
kau yoyuu wa arimasen* — "I don't have the money to purchase such a thing." (p.15)

### あがりこんで (agarikonde)

The word *komu* adds an implication of depth; *agarikonde* suggests the act of going into the
furthest interiors of someone's home — i.e., settling in thoroughly, not just briefly stopping by.
(p.15)

### ……の話だと (…no hanashi da to)

Conversational register. The written-Japanese equivalent is *…no hanashi ni yoru to*; *shimbun ni
yoru to* ("according to the newspaper") is a similar construction. (p.15)

### むりもありませんね (muri mo arimasen ne)

Literally "it's not impossible [that this would happen]" — a remark implying sympathy, here for
the taxi driver's complaint about traffic. (p.15)

### お客が来るとなったら (okyaku ga kuru to nattara)

"If such an (unusual) event were to occur [that a guest were coming]" — marks an event as notably
out of the ordinary. (p.15)

### どう思うって? (doo omou tte?)

A request to clarify the intention of a question — similar to the English "What do you mean
by…?" (p.15)

### ……というほどでもない (…to yuu hodo de mo nai)

"Not that important/significant." Example: *Nayami to yuu hodo de mo nai kedo, chotto komatte iru
koto ga arimasu* — "Although it's not what you'd call a source of real unhappiness, it is
troubling me." A downplaying/hedging construction. (p.15)

### 文型 1: ……ものだと思う (sentence pattern)

Structure drilled: [clause describing a notable change/state] + ものだと思う, used to express
reflective emotional emphasis about an observed trend. Main-text example: そうしたニュースがたびたび
新聞にのったり、テレビの画面に出たりする。国際的なおつき合いが盛んになったものだと思う。("Such news
items repeatedly appear in the papers and on TV. International friendships really have blossomed
at an incredible rate.") (p.14)

### 文型 2: ……ときは……ことが多くなった (sentence pattern)

Structure: [time/occasion clause] + ときは + [resulting habitual action] + ことが多くなった ("it has
become more common that…"), used to describe an increasing tendency. Main-text example: 人に会う
ときはレストランや喫茶店を利用することが多くなったようである。("For meeting people, it appears that
restaurants and coffee houses have become increasingly popular [venues]." (p.16)

## Grammar points

### 朝夕は……ということである

In this context, *to yuu koto de aru* is similar to *…da soo da* ("I heard, I understand" — a
hearsay/report register). Note the contrast: in the sentence that directly precedes this
construction in the main text it instead carries the nuance "what I notice is the fact that…" or
"I'm struck by the fact that…" — the same phrase shifts meaning with context. (p.32)

### デパートに通う

The word *kayou* ordinarily implies the act of commuting to a job, but it can also describe
visiting a place one likes and frequents often. Example: *Kanojo no ie ni kayou* — "He visits his
girlfriend's house frequently." (p.32)

### いわば

Precedes a (somewhat exaggerated) remark; means "If I were to say…" (p.32)

### 人／物 (hito / mono)

*Hito* usually refers to people *other than* oneself. By substituting *mono* for *hito*, a writer
suggests personal affinity with the people being described — here, those who "scamper about the
ground." (p.32)

### ……ほどの年でもない

Equivalent to *hodo toshiyori de wa nai* — "not that old." The *mo* in this phrase agrees with the
*mo* in *kaidan mo [yaya] tsurai*, helping form a sentence with two negations working together.
(p.32)

### 時には1本とばしたり……ということも、ないとは言えませんね

Picking up the tail end of a speaker's remarks and completing them is a common Japanese
conversational technique, not considered impolite. Double negatives like *nai to wa iemasen* and
*nai koto mo nai* are used to express indirect agreement with what the other speaker said. (p.32)

### いそがしい人は地下鉄ってこと

Equivalent to *…to yuu ketsuron ni naru*: "It boils down to…" (p.32)

### 一生に一度のことですもの

*Desu mono* is a feminine speech-register form carrying the same meaning as *dakara* ("because").
(p.32)

### そうも行かない

"Can't very well do that." Equivalent to *…wake ni wa ikanai*. (p.32)

### 愛はすべてを可能にする

A dramatic phrase that reads like a direct translation of an English expression into Japanese
(calqued idiom — "love makes everything possible"). (p.32)

### 文型 1: ……感じることは、……ということである

Structure: [observation while doing X] + 感じることは + [content of observation] +
ということである — a form for introducing one's impressions or the result of an observation. 気づく
and 思う can be substituted for 感じる. Main-text example: 地下鉄に乗っていて感じることは、老人が
わりあい少ないということである。("Riding the subway, I'm often struck by the fact that elderly
passengers are relatively few in number.") (p.33)

### 文型 2: ……とは……ものである

A form for indicating an assessment of a situation. Using *to wa* instead of plain *wa* signals
that an assessment is about to follow. Main-text example: しかし、進歩とはつねに疲れるものである。飛行
機に乗るためには、なれないうちはかなりの緊張が要求されるし、地下鉄を利用するためには、階段を上り下
りする体力が必要である。("Progress, for some reason, is always a source of fatigue. Until you've
grown accustomed to it, air travel creates a lot of tension; riding the subway requires the
strength to ascend and descend staircases.") (p.34)

### 文型 3: ……て……ながら……のが好きだ

An exercise combining the phrase *(verb) no ga suki* with *…nagara* ("while doing"), adding a
degree of complexity. In the main text, *wa* is used before *suki da* to create a comparison with
the latter half of the sentence; when that latter half is omitted in the drilled variant, *wa suki*
becomes *ga suki*. Main-text example: バスに乗って町の中の様子を見ながら行くのは好きだが、あまり時間的
余裕はない。("I like to look at the city's scenery [while riding the bus], but I don't have enough
free time to ride the bus.") (p.36)

## Grammar points

### ギラギラと

A mimetic phrase expressing illuminosity. *Giragira* refers to intensely bright light, like
sunlight. *Kirakira* is the related but distinct mimetic used for the soft glimmering light of
smaller objects, such as stars. (p.51)

### 早め

When *me* is added to adjectives, the resulting word carries the nuance "more … than usual."
Examples: *Ato de chijimu ka mo shirenai kara, ookime no shatsu o katta* — "Thinking it might
shrink, I bought a shirt that was a little on the large side." *Tabemono wa oome ni yooi shita
hoo ga ii, minna wakakute genki da kara* — "They're all young and healthy, so it might be best to
have a little extra food on hand." (p.51)

### そろそろ寒くなります

In this context, *sorosoro* does not mean "progressing slowly" — it means "[the season in which
it will begin to get cold] is near." Example: *Moo sorosoro kuru koro desu* — "It's about time for
[it/him/her] to come around." (p.51)

### 暖房器具のご用意を

The sentence ending that ordinarily follows this phrase — *nasatte kudasai*, for example, or
*nasattara doo desu ka* — has been omitted (elliptical advertising-register imperative). Additional
example of omitting a sentence ending (in this case, *shite kudasai*): *Sorosoro dekakeru yooi o.*
"Let's [make] preparations to leave." (p.52)

### 調査によると

Synonymous expressions: *choosa ni yoreba*, *choosa de wa*. Note: *choosa ni yori* should not be
used in this particular context. (p.52)

### そうそう

Used when something is suddenly remembered. Example: *Soo soo. Sukkari wasurete ita. Kore, ryokoo
no omiyage desu.* "Oh — I completely forgot. This is a souvenir from our trip." (p.52)

### そう言えば

Used when something the speaker has said brings back a recollection, or when the speaker thinks of
a good concrete example to back up their point. (p.52)

### 〜にのせられる

To become overly confident of an advertisement, for example, and act accordingly (i.e., "to be
taken in/manipulated by"). Example: *Tomodachi ni noserarete kabu o katte shimatta ga, kookai shite
iru.* "I took my friend's word for it and bought the stock, and now I'm regretting it."
*Kimi wa noserareyasui kara, ki o tsukero yo.* "You're easily convinced, so watch out." (p.53)

### がまん強い人

Said in reference to the husband in the Dialogue. Further examples of the productive suffix
～強い ("strong in ~"): *shimboozuyoi* ("patient"), *nebarizuyoi* ("tenacious, persistent"). (p.53)

### 文型 1: ……うちから、……

A pattern for criticizing an action that occurs ahead of its proper time. A phrase like *mada atsui
uchi kara* conveys the difference in time more convincingly than a phrase such as *suzushiku naru
mae ni*. Main-text example: まだ暑いうちから、「そろそろ寒くなりますから、暖房器具のご用意を」と呼び
かける。("'The cold season's around the corner — isn't it about time to prepare your heating
equipment?' they beckon, while it's still hot.") (p.52)

### 文型 2: ……で最も……のは、……そうである

The phrase 最も…… is followed by で or …の中で (never に). Used to report a superlative finding
from a cited source. Main-text example: ある心理学者の調査によると、母親が幼児に対して使う言葉で最も
頻度が高いのは、「早く」だそうである。("According to one psychologist's survey, the word mothers use
most frequently toward young children is reportedly 'hurry up.'") (p.53)

## Grammar points

### たしかに……しかし……

A form used for summarizing an idea in one remark, then stating a counter-argument against it.
Example: *Tashika ni yasui, shikashi saikoo no shina to wa ienai.* "It's definitely cheap; however,
you cannot call it a high-quality product." (p.69)

### 火加減

Literally the compound *kagen* means "to both add on and reduce." Thus *hikagen* means "increase
and reduce the heat (of a stove, etc.)." Related compounds: *tekagen* ("tact"), *sajikagen*
("discretion"). In contemporary Japanese, *ii kagen* is used to criticize something/someone as
"insufficient" or "incomplete" — use with caution. (p.69)

### うまそうなにおい

*Umai* is used in 1) written Japanese, 2) male speech. Women use *oishii* in spoken remarks. In
written text, *umai* is used without regard to male/female language distinctions. **Explicit
gendered-register distinction.** (p.69)

### 〜につながる

"Gives rise to an event." Example: *Shitsugyoosha no zooka wa shakai fuan ni tsunagariyasui.* "The
increase of unemployed often gives rise to social anxiety." (p.69)

### 汗を流して働く

In this instance, *ase o nagashite* ("to perspire profusely") is used in a figurative sense (i.e.,
working hard, not literal sweat). (p.69)

### そうした風景

*Soo shita* is equivalent to *sono yoo na*. In the main text, *soo shita* refers to all the phrases
appearing between *gekkyuubi* and *isogu*. (p.69)

### おかえりなさい

Essentially a phrase for acknowledging a family member's return home. It is also frequently used
when a co-worker returns to the office — co-workers are regarded as members of a family-like
structure in Japanese companies. (p.69)

### なんだか……って感じで

*Nandaka* prepares the listener for an ending such as *to iu kanji*, *no yoo*, or *mitai*. (p.69)

### ぜいたくな不満……のは

In conversation, the order of phrases is often reversed, as in the remarks of Speaker B. Although
it's possible to put *zeitaku na fuman deshoo ne* at the end of the sentence (*…no wa, zeitaku na
fuman deshoo ne*), such a statement might be too complicated to understand in spoken conversation.
(p.70)

### それほどのものじゃない

*Kamidana ni sonaeru hodo no subarashii mono de wa nai.* "It's not something worthy of being
placed on the kamidana as an offering." (p.70)

### 道理で……

Equivalent to *wa toozen da*. Example: A: *Kyoo wa Nichiyoobi da ne.* B: *Aa, soo da ne. Doori de
michi ga suiteru wake da.* A: "Today's a Sunday, you know." B: "That must explain why the roads are
so empty." (p.70)

### まし

*Ryoosha to mo yoku nai ga, sono naka de wa yoi.* "Both options are poor, but (receiving a pay
stub) is the more satisfactory of the two." Example: A: *Zuibun jootatsu shimashita ne.* B: *Iie,
demo, mae yori wa sukoshi mashi ni narimashita.* A: "You've really become skillful." B: "Not
really. But I have improved compared to before." (p.70)

### 文型 1: たしかに……。しかし、……

*Tashika ni* and *shikashi* in combination first acknowledge an idea, then negate it with a
counter-argument. Main-text example: たしかに飛行機は速い。地球の反対側の国までも、半日ぐらいで飛ぶこと
ができる。しかし、飛行機の旅行は、空港から空に上がり、空から空港へ下りたような感じで、長い距離を移動
したという実感があまりない。("Without a doubt, plane travel is speedy — airplanes can take you as
far as halfway round the globe in half a day. Nonetheless, travelling by plane — airport to sky,
sky to airport — deprives the traveler of a sense of the extreme distance such travel encompasses.")
(p.69–70)

### 文型 2: ……も……が、……も……

A pattern repeating the same *mo…mo…* structure, where the first and second clauses represent
opposite value judgments. Main-text example: 旅の苦労も減ったが、旅の実感もうすくなった。("The travails
of travel have decreased, it's true. But the concreteness of travel has lost its potency in the
process.") (p.71–72)

### 文型 3: ……結果、……なった

A form for describing a change and its result. 結果 is preceded by [noun + の] or [verb (past) +
の形]. Main-text example: 銀行振り込みやクレジット・カードが普及した結果、現金を手にすることが少なく
なった。("As a result of the spread of bank transfers and credit cards, people less often have cash
in hand.") (p.72)

## Grammar points

### 七十の手料理 (idiom wordplay in the lesson title)

The act of beginning to study at an advanced age is traditionally spoken of as *rokujuu no
tenarai* ("learning at sixty"). In the lesson's essay, the *rokujuu* portion of this old Japanese
expression has been altered to *nanajuu* ("seventy") and *tenarai* has been replaced with
*teryoori* ("cooking for oneself") for a humorous play on words. (p.85)

### 「男性」と「男」 (gendered register: dansei vs. otoko)

The word *dansei* is typically used to refer to the masculine sex when respectful expressions are
unnecessary (or intentionally omitted), or when attention to gender is important; *otoko* is used
instead. *Josei* and *onna* are differentiated from each other in a parallel manner. **Explicit
gendered-register distinction** covering both masculine and feminine term pairs. (p.86–87)

### はずかしいこととされていた

*Sareru* is the passive form of *suru*. In this context, though, the *suru* verb means "to regard,
to consider." Example: *Tooji wa otoko ga hade na iro o kitari suru no wa hazukashii koto to sarete
ita.* "At that time, the wearing of garish clothing by men was regarded as a disgrace." (p.87)

### 湯をわかす、茶を入れる

In conversation, the honorific *o* is used in front of *yu* and *cha* by both men and women (*oyu
o wakasu*; *ocha o ireru*). The *o* is usually omitted from these two words in essays (written
register). (p.87)

### 妻に死なれても

The passive form of the verb *shinu* ("to die") is used to indicate the loss incurred due to the
death of a wife. From a purer, more objective standpoint, *tsuma ga shinde mo* would be more
appropriate — but the main text instead shows sympathy for elderly, bereaved husbands by using
*tsuma ni shinarete*. The *…te kureta* portion of the phrase *musuko no tsuma ga shokuji no sewa o
shite kureta* also implies the perspective of an elderly parent (receiving a favor). (p.87)

### やれ、どっこいしょ

Making a verbalism at the beginning of an action is thought to be characteristic of old people in
Japan. (p.87)

### これをですか

The verb *tsukau* that should logically follow *kore o* is intentionally omitted by the speaker.
Example: A: *Asoko, itte mimashita.* B: *E, asoko e desu ka.* A: "I went out there." B: "Really?
You [went] out there?" (p.87)

### これはいいや

*Ya* adds a light stress to statements. It is used for talking to oneself, or for addressing close
friends and intimates. *Korya hidoi ya* is a similar example. (p.87)

### がまんしてちょうだい

Women frequently use *choodai* with close friends, family members, or people younger than
themselves. Its meaning is equivalent to *kudasai*. **Feminine speech register.** (p.87)

### よく言うでしょ

In this context, *yoku yuu* means "often said by people." (p.87)

### 訓練しとかなきゃ

A short/contracted way of saying *kunren shite okanakereba naranai*. (p.87)

### 文型 1: ……はもちろん、……さえ……

The *…wa mochiron, …sae* form is used as a noun modifier (both *dansei* and *josei* in the drilled
examples refer to older people). *Mo* can be substituted for *sae*, but *sae* carries more definite
emphasis. Main-text example: 食事の用意はもちろん、湯をわかすことさえできない男性もめずらしくない。
("Men who can't [even] boil water — not to mention prepare a meal — are not uncommon.") (p.87–88)

### 文型 2: 昔は……。……現在、……

A form for comparing old social conditions with the present. Main-text example: 昔はむすこ夫婦と同居
するのが普通だったから、妻に死なれても、むすこの妻が食事の世話をしてくれた。核家族化した現在、それは
望めない。("Due to the fact that, in the past, it was common to live with a son and his wife, the
loss of a wife simply meant that the wife of one's son would take over meal preparation. In the
present age of the nuclearization of the family, this cannot be anticipated.") (p.88)

## Grammar points

### ……とあった

A form identical in meaning to *to kaite atta*. *…to atta* is a written-Japanese form. (p.104)

### それで思い出したのであるが……

Equivalent to the English "that reminds me of…" In conversation, *A, sore de omoidashita* or a
similar form would probably be used instead. (p.104)

### 暗いのに

In this context, the *no* portion of *noni* is equivalent to *koto*, and *ni* functions similarly
to *…ni odoroku*. *Noni* does not carry the meaning "although" here, as it does in a phrase like
*Tsukarete iru noni yasumanai* ("Although he's tired, he won't take a break"). Example: *Ano hito
no shinsetsu na noni kanshin shita.* "I couldn't help but be impressed by that person's great
kindness." (p.104)

### 明るすぎるて疲れると

The *to* portion of this phrase is a short form for *…to itte iru*. It differs from the *to* used
in *moshi…suru to*, which describes conditions for something. Example: *Karukute tsukaiyasui to,
josei no aida de hyooban ga ii.* "It has received favorable criticism among women, who acknowledge
that it is light and easy to use." (p.104)

### ……で有名

"Famous for," "reputed for," "well-known for." *Koto* or *no* is placed in front of the particle
*de* (in conversational Japanese, *no* is frequently contracted to *n*). Example: *Ano hito wa
kechi nan' de yuumei da.* "That person is famous for her stinginess." (p.104)

### ……からさ

*Sa* is a light emphatic sentence-final particle used primarily by men. Women usually use *yo*
instead. **Explicit gendered-register distinction**, developed fully in the 男性的／女性的 table
below. (p.105)

### どういうこと、それ

An expression for insisting on a more lengthy explanation of an opinion, equivalent to *sore wa
doo yuu koto desu ka*. In informal conversation, the *desu ka* portion is frequently dropped and
the word order is often reversed. (p.105)

### 言われたんだって

*Tte* means *to yuu koto o kiita*. *Iwareta n datte* is often used in informal conversation to
suggest that the information being transmitted was learned indirectly (hearsay). (p.105)

### 好きだっていうの

Equivalent to *suki da to [anata wa] yuu no desu ka*. (p.105)

### 西洋人と欧米人

*Seiyoojin* and *oobeijin* refer to the same group of people. *Seiyoojin*, however, is slightly
old-fashioned in tone. (p.105)

### 下線部分：男女のちがい (explicit gendered-register table)

In Dialogue II, the book underlines the phrases where male and female forms tend to differ,
presenting them as a direct 男性的 (masculine-style) / 女性的 (feminine-style) comparison table. Full
pair list captured above in the vocabulary table. This is the single most systematic explicit
register annotation found anywhere in this book so far — a sentence-final-particle-level male/
female contrast across 8 paired forms from one continuous dialogue. (p.105)

### 文型 1: ……（れ）ば……ほどよい

Treats the already-familiar *…(re)ba…hodo yoi* pattern ("the more X, the better") as a single
component embedded within a longer sentence. Main-text example: ……というより、明るければ明るい
ほどよいという考えが強いのではなかろうか。("…Among them, 'the brighter, the better' seems to be a
more dominant way of thinking.") (p.106)

### 文型 2: ……て、……ほどだ

A cause-and-effect form that emphasizes the degree of the cause, analogous to English "so…
that…". The pattern *amari ni…na node…da* can serve a similar function but carries a
translational, less natural tone; *…te, …hodo* reads more naturally. Main-text example: ……食堂の
中が非常に暗くて、足をふみ入れるのにちょっと不安を感じるほどであった。("…the interior of the
restaurant was so dark that I felt a vague sense of unease at the thought of stepping inside.")
(p.107)

## Grammar points

### これだけ

The word *dake* indicates variations in degree — it is not limited to indicating only small
portions. In this instance, *kore dake* is similar in meaning to *konna ni*. Example: *Are dake
doryoku shita noni seikoo shinakatta. Un ga warukatta no daroo.* "Although I tried that hard, I did
not succeed. I must have been unlucky." (p.121)

### 〜ということである

An expression for conveying information, used more frequently in written Japanese than *…soo da*.
(p.123)

### そして……である

Equivalent in meaning to *sono hoka ni…to yuu yooso mo aru*. (p.123)

### 年中行事なんか

*Nanka* can be used to express criticism or contempt. In such cases, the phrase that would
ordinarily follow *nanka* is often left out — in the main text, a phrase like *juuyoo de wa nai*
("are unimportant"), *imi ga nai* ("are meaningless"), or *hitsuyoo nai* ("are unnecessary") would
ordinarily follow *nenchuu gyooji nanka*. (p.123)

### お誕生日か何かで？

The speaker intentionally omits a phrase like *hayabike suru no desu ka* from the end of this
question. (p.123)

### ぜひ見てくれって

The phrase *kodomo ga itte iru node* is omitted from the end of this sentence. In informal
conversation, *tte* is often used in place of *to*. (p.123)

### させられました

The causative form plus the passive form: to be forced to perform an action against one's will.
Parents usually force their kids to do things; this is an example of the reverse — and therein lies
its humor. As a follow-up, Speaker A replies with *saserareta nante*, purposely omitting *yuu no wa
okashii desu yo* from the end. (p.123)

### そういうことをしなくてもすむ

*Shinakute mo sumu* has the same meaning as *suru hitsuyoo ga nai* ("there is no necessity to do
so"). Example: *Ashita wa kyuujitsu desu kara asa hayaku okinakute mo sumimasu.* "As tomorrow is a
holiday, there is no need to get up early." (p.123)

### そういうことになりますね

*Kekka to shite soo naru.* Equivalent to the English "it boils down to…" (p.123)

### 旧正月

According to the Old Calendar, New Year's takes place at the beginning of February. Many places in
provincial Japan still celebrate the old New Year in preference to the new one. (p.123)

### パンとコーヒーってわけにはいかない

"It's not possible to have Western-style food (on this occasion)." (p.123)

### 文型 1: これだけ……から、……

The *dake* of *kore dake* is treated as discussed in the Notes section above. *To omowareru* has
the same meaning as *to mieru* ("it seems"). Although *to omou* could be used instead, *to
omowareru* carries a tone of indirectness and formality. Main-text example: これだけいそがしい世の中
になったのだから、昔からの行事はどんどん消えていくだろうと思われる。("…The world has become so busy
that it almost seems that the events of old will steadily fade away.") (p.123–124)

### 文型 2: ……(人)も、……と、……なければならなくなる

Refer to the Notes for more on *…nanka*; this drill focuses on variations using *…nakereba
naranaku naru*. The main text's 若夫婦 ("younger couples") is changed to 人 ("people") in the drills
to broaden their applicability. Main-text example: 「年中行事なんか」と思っていた若夫婦も、子供が生ま
れて幼稚園や学校に通うようになると、態度を改めなければならなくなる。("…When their children are born
and have started attending kindergarten or school, younger couples once lukewarm toward annual
events are forced to reform their attitudes.") (p.124–125)

## Grammar points

### するとしたら

The first *suru* conveys the meaning of *[okaeshi] suru* ("to [return]"). The second *suru* (here,
in the *shitara* form) means "supposing that." (p.139)

### 人の移動が激しくなったからであり……

In this context, *tame* can be used instead of *kara*. Similarly, *kara* may be substituted for
*tame* in the phrase *jinseikan ya kachikan no chigai mo ookiku natte kita tame* that follows.
(p.139)

### ……は本来うれしいものである

*Mono* lends emotional depth to an expression of a commonplace truth. When *koto* is used instead
— as in the sentence *…tanoshii koto de aru* that follows it — expressions take on directness and
decisiveness instead. (p.139)

### ……と加われば

*To* resembles *to yuu yoo ni*. Example: *Shukudai, shiken benkyoo, arubaito to, suru koto ga
takusan aru.* "With homework, studying for tests, and my part-time job, I've got a lot of things to
do." (p.139)

### それにしても

Used for expressing feelings about a statement you have just made. (p.139)

### いつになったら……（か）

Unlike the *itsu…ka* pattern, *itsu ni nattara…ka* expresses feelings of hopelessness or
frustration regarding something that is slow to be realized. Example: *Aa, itsu ni nattara seikatsu
ga raku ni naru no daroo.* "Oh, will there ever be a time when my life gets more comfortable?"
(p.139)

### 新聞の相談欄で

*Te* is used interchangeably with its double-consonant form (*tte*). With a meaning similar to *to
yuu no wa*, it is used in conversations to indicate principal topics. (p.139)

### 反映していますもの

Like *kara*, *mono* emphasizes reasons here. **It is explicitly noted as a feminine form.** (p.139)

### 倍返しって言いました

The *tte* in this phrase is equivalent to *to*, as is the *tte* in the phrase *hangaeshi tte* in
the next line. (p.139)

### ホワイト・デーなんていうの

The same as *howaito dee nado to yuu mono*. (p.139)

### お気の毒に

*Omoimasu* has been omitted from the end of this phrase. The phrase itself is somewhat formal, and
is used by the speaker here for humorous effect (applying a formal condolence-register phrase to a
trivial chocolate-surplus complaint). (p.139)

### 文型 1: ……によく……が出ている。……が多い

*…ga notte iru* ("is printed") can be substituted for *…ga dete iru* ("appears"). Main-text
example: 新聞の相談欄によく贈り物の話題が出ている。結婚や入学のお祝いをもらった場合、お返しをすべき
か、もしするとしたら、どの程度の物がよいかというような相談が多い。("The topic of gifts often appears
in the newspaper's advice column. Should a return gift be sent for presents received for marriage
or entrance into school? If so, what is the appropriate value for such a gift? Advice is frequently
sought for questions like these.") (p.140–141)

### 文型 2: ……本来……。しかし、実際には、……たくなくても……場合も多い

Main-text example: 人から物をもらうのは、本来うれしいものである。贈るほうにとっても、もらった人の喜ぶ
顔を想像しながら贈る品を選ぶのは、楽しいことである。しかし、実際には、贈りたくなくても贈る場合や、お
返しの心配をしなければならない場合も多い。("Receiving things from people is, in principle, a
pleasant thing. For the giver as well, selecting a gift while imagining the happy face of the
recipient is an enjoyable experience. In reality, though, there are many cases where one must give
a gift even without wanting to, or must worry about an appropriate return gift.") (p.141–142)

## Grammar points

### 事実

Like *jijitsu to shite* and *jissai no tokoro*, a simple *jijitsu* can be used to state or explain
something by way of an actual example. (p.157)

### だから、……ということになる

"From the previous facts, we can conclude that…" Example: *Rokujikan goto ni kusuri o nomu. Dakara,
ichinichi ni yonkai nomu to yuu koto ni naru.* "I take the medicine every six hours. Therefore, I
take it four times a day." (p.157)

### だから、どろぼうが……わけである

Similar to the above pattern — this phrase can be used in its place in many instances, but *wake de
aru* is more apt for explaining complicated situations. (p.157)

### かけ忘れればそれまでだ

The *sore made* in the pattern *…ba sore made da* means "is useless, of no help," or "will all come
to an end." (p.157)

### もう一つ錠をつけといたほうがいい

*Tsuketoita* is a compressed/contracted version of *tsukete oita*. Contractions frequently crop up
in informal conversations. (p.158)

### ぼくんとこ

Short for *boku no tokoro* — *no* has been contracted to *n*. Likewise, *donatta n de* is short for
*donatta no de*. (p.158)

### 錠を買いに行こう、君の

An inversion of *kimi no joo o kai ni ikoo*. In informal conversations, speakers often add
afterthoughts to the end of statements. (p.158)

### わるいわ

Used to apologize for something that might create an inconvenience for one's listener — less formal
than *sumimasen*. (p.158)

### 文型 1: ……ない……はない

A double negative used for emphasis. To stress the fact that all newspapers carry articles on
crime, *nai* is used twice. Main-text example: いつの新聞を見ても、犯罪の記事ののっていない日はない。
("Whatever the publication, days when an article about crime fails to appear in the newspaper are
nonexistent.") (p.158)

### 文型 2: ……を見ると、……が最も多い

Phrasing the topic as "…o miru to" (as opposed to a flat topic-marked statement like "…wa") leaves
the impression that the speaker is trying to engage the listener's interest in the idea about to be
related — adding warmth to both writing and conversation. Keep a *desu/masu* level of speech when
explaining a topic to somebody in a face-to-face encounter. Main-text example: 空き巣の侵入方法を
見ると、鍵のかかっていない玄関から入るのが四十三パーセントで、最も多い。("Among the entry techniques
used to break into unoccupied houses, 43 percent of the incidents involved going in through
unlocked front doors, the highest figure of all.") (p.159–160)

## Grammar points

### 忍者

Spies who served shogun or *daimyoo* (feudal lords), from the *Sengoku-jidai* (Warring States
Period, 1467–1568) onward. (p.173) — historical/cultural background note, not a language point.

### こちらの体

*Kochira* doesn't only refer to the writer — it also indicates people who are in the same position
as the writer. (p.173)

### あこがれたものである

*…ta mono de aru* is used for nostalgically reminiscing about a fact concerning the past. (p.173)

### ……ほかはない

"Apart from [taking one's life], there is no other way." (The phrase *toru yori hoka nai* can also
be used.) Example: *Moo akirameru hoka nai.* "Giving up is the only alternative." (p.174)

### 気をつければいいのだ

The phrase *…ba ii no da* also contains the meaning *sore ijoo monku o yuu beki de wa nai*
("complaining more than this would be inappropriate"). The writer is angered at the actions of
young people, but acknowledges their stance with half-hearted irony, forcing herself to admit there
is no reason to make further complaints. (p.174)

### まあ

Used for conveying a general feeling of agreement. (p.175)

### 女房なんか言いますよ

The *nanka* in this sentence means "for instance." A phrase for stating a thought conjured up by
the last remark. (p.175)

### 弱い存在といえば

"Regarding that point, … are lucky." Besides *watashi tachi* ("we"), one can also use *ima no hito*
("the people of today"), *wakai hito* ("young people"), and other phrases. (p.175)

### その点、……は楽ですね

After talking about the hardships of men, one can say *sono ten, josei wa raku desu ne* ("on that
point, women have it easy/are lucky") — a social-commentary remark from the main text's dialogue,
not a grammatical register distinction. (p.175)

### ドシンドシン歩いたからといって

*Kara to itte* is followed by a conclusive remark in the negative. Examples: *Suki da kara to itte
nomisugite wa ikemasen yo.* "You can't drink just because you like it." *Muzukashii kara to itte,
yameru wake ni wa ikanai.* "Just because it's difficult, I can't give up." (p.175)

### 文型 1: ……でも、……ないと、……

A conditional *tatoeba…demo* has been added to the cause-and-effect form *…to,….* This
conditional portion can be altered to *sumaato na wakai hito de atte mo* ("even if [one is] a
slender young person"). *Ikura* is used here for emphasis. Main-text example: 体の重そうな人ばかりで
なく、スマートな若い人でも、すわりかたに気をつけないと、隣の人が迷惑する。("This doesn't apply to
only heavier people. If even a slender young person fails to give attention to the way he or she
sits down, the person in the next seat will be disturbed.") (p.175–176)

### 文型 2: ……たら……ほかない

*…hoka wa nai* can also be phrased *…hoka ni hoohoo ga nai*. Main-text example: 失敗したら死によって
責任をとるほかはない。("If they failed, there was no alternative but to atone with their death.")
(p.176)

## Grammar points

### ……によっては

Literally "Depending on…," but in actual use closer to "Some…" Example: *Hito ni yotte wa nattoo
ga zenzen taberarenai soo da.* "Some people can't eat fermented soybeans at all." (Beware of the
differences in usage between *yotte* and *yotte wa*.) (p.192)

### たしかに

An expression useful for summarizing an assortment of facts and then refuting them. *Tashika ni*
also encourages the listener to anticipate the opposing viewpoint that follows — it often takes the
form *Tashika ni…Shikashi,…* (p.192)

### いっそ

In conversational Japanese, the phrase *isso no koto* is also used. *Isso* is helpful for
introducing proposals which might be difficult to implement. (p.192)

### ……によって……を変える

"Use different…depending on." Example: *Aite ni yotte kotoba dzukai o kaeru no wa toozen desu.*
"It goes without saying that you should use different phrasings depending on the person." (p.192)

### 暑いですね、きょうは

In informal conversation, inversions of these two phrases are not uncommon. *Natsu wa shiro desu
ne, nan to itte mo* is an example of a similar construction. (p.192)

### 白っぽい

In combination with nouns, the suffix *ppoi* is equivalent to *…ni zoku suru*, or *teki*. When
used to describe colors, *ppoi* means "-ish." Examples: *shiroppoi* ("whitish"), *aoppoi*
("bluish"). *Kodomoppoi* means "immature for [one's] age." In combination with a verb, *ppoi* is
equivalent to *shiyasui*: "easy to [perform]." Example: *okorippoi* ("gets angry easily"),
*wasureppoi* ("forgets easily"). (p.192)

### そういえばそうですね

"Now that you mention it, I see what you mean": an expression that shows the speaker's
recognition and consent with something that he or she was not previously aware of. (p.192)

### いやねえ

A feminine form. Men usually use *iya da nee* instead. This form roughly translates into the
English "I don't like [it]." **Explicit gendered-register distinction.** (p.193)

### だって

A word used for subjectively expressing a reason — similar to "because" or "for" but more
emotional. Use *datte* in conversations with intimates; avoid employing it in formal discourse.
(*Dare datte* is similar to *dare demo*.) (p.193)

### つい……てしまう

*Tsui* and the phrase *tsui…te shimau* are often used to refer to actions that are considered
unwise. Example: *Tsui nomisugite shimatta.* "I [was careless and] drank too much." (p.193)

### 服装で人を判断しないことだな

In this instance, *…koto wa* is equivalent to *subeki de aru*. Example: *Motto yoku shiraberu koto
da.* "We must investigate further." (*Na* is more familiar than *wa* — it is often used by men.)
**Explicit gendered-register distinction.** (p.193)

### ……中身さ

*Sa* resembles *da yo*, but is more casual. (p.193)

### 文型 1: ……によっては、……とされている

Here, *sarete iru* means "believed to be the case by a large number of people." It's permissible to
omit *da* from the end of *toozen da*. Main-text example: 職業によっては、制服があるのが当然だとされ
ているようである。("There are some occupations in which uniforms are taken for granted.") (p.193–194)

### 文型 2: ……ときは、……ができる

Instead of *nugu toki*, it's acceptable to use *nugu to* — but the latter suggests the action of
"taking off a uniform" is performed *for the sake of* a feeling of freedom. Main-text example: 仕事
を終わって制服をぬぐときは、さわやかな解放感を味わうことができる。("When taking off a uniform after
work, you can enjoy a refreshing feeling of freedom.") (p.194–195)

### 文型 3: いっそ……たら、……ていい

*Isso…tara* is a form used for making propositions that take courage to express. A shorter
variant, such as *isso yamemashoo yo*, can be used in the same way. Main-text example: いっそ、警官や
鉄道員の制服をやめてしまったら、そんな犯罪がおこらなくていいと思う。("If police and railway uniforms
were discontinued, crimes like these would no longer occur.") (p.195–196)

## Grammar points

### すきま

A narrow space between two objects — frequently used for areas that cannot ordinarily accommodate
furniture, such as nooks or corners in houses or narrow doorways. The coined word *sukimakagu*,
though meaningless in and of itself, is an intriguing pun. *Sukimakaze* are the cold, unhealthy
drafts that blow through old houses or ill-constructed homes; the word is often used figuratively to
describe personal interactions. When two perfectly-matched individuals start drifting apart, one
might use the phrase *ano futari no aida ni mo sukimakaze ga fukidashita*: "there are cold drafts
blowing between that couple." (p.210)

### 登場

A dramatic word meaning, literally, "an appearance on the stage." *Toojoo* personifies the image of
*sukimakagu* and figuratively "raises it on a (public) stage." (p.210)

### ……というわけである

Often used at the end of a paragraph to indicate the conclusion of an explanation. The equivalent in
spoken Japanese, *to yuu wake-desu*, is often used to end spoken remarks. (p.210)

### せばめられていく

The word *sebameru* is a verb constructed by adding the ending *meru* to *semai*. By connecting
*meru* to the stems of adjectives, one can form *tadooshi* (transitive verbs). Examples: *yowai* →
*yowameru* ("to weaken"), *hiroi* → *hiromeru* ("to widen"). *Maru* is the ending for *jidooshi*, or
intransitive verbs: *yowai* → *yowamaru* ("grow weak"), *hiroi* → *hiromaru* ("become wide"). The
verb form of *semai* is conjugated by adding *meru* (or *maru*) to *sebashi*, a literary form with
the same meaning. Example: *sebameru* (*tadooshi*), *sebamaru* (*jidooshi*). (p.210)

### 投書の主

*Nushi* means "the person who did/does something." Examples: *mochinushi* ("the person who
had/has [it]"), *otoshinushi* ("someone who has dropped/drops [it]"). (p.210)

### 尊重する

A word used to show respect to someone's ideas or work. *Sonkei* shows respect to the individual
him/herself (as distinct from their ideas). (p.210)

### 話し相手ぐらいにはなる

In this instance, *gurai* implies a smallness or a lack of importance. (p.210)

### ひっこしされた

The *sareta* portion of this expression is a respectful form used principally by men; women tend to
say *nasatta* instead. **Explicit gendered-register distinction.** (p.211)

### おばあちゃん

The daughter-in-law in this dialogue uses *obaasan* to refer to her mother-in-law from the
perspective of her own children. (p.211)

### はあ？

Spoken with an upward intonation in instances when the meaning of a statement is vague. More
refined than the informal "ee?" (p.211–212)

### 程度問題

Used when something has gone beyond the point of permissibility. (p.212)

### けちな話

Here, *hanashi* could be replaced with *mono* or *koto*. As opposed to its frequent meaning of
monetary or materialistic stinginess, *kechi* here refers to an overemphasis on details or a small
way of thinking. (p.212)

### いつになったら……ようになるのか（しら）

A phrase used to convey a feeling of resignation about something that probably won't take effect.
Example: *Itsu ni nattara konda densha ni noranakute ii yoo ni naru no kana.* "When will we ever be
relieved of having to ride crowded trains?" (p.212)

### 怒る資格ないものね

*Mono* is a colloquialism used to emphasize a reason. It also appears in the statement "*ie ga
semai n da mono*." (p.212)

### たしか

Unlike *tashika ni* ("certainly, surely"), *tashika…da* is used when the speaker lacks confidence
in his or her ideas. Example: A: *Ano hito ikutsu desu ka.* B: *Saa, tashika 38 to omoimasu kedo.*
"How old is that person?" "Well, probably 38, I'd say." (p.212)

### 文型 1: ……にとっては、……も……となる

A way to express the fact that an ordinary phenomenon can become problematic under special
circumstances. Main-text example: 住居費が高いためせまい所に住まざるを得ない多くの都会人にとっては、
部屋のすみのわずかな空間も利用の対象となる。("It's now possible for metropolitanites, who are often
forced to inhabit cramped living quarters due to the high cost of housing, to utilize even the
smallest spaces in their rooms.") (p.211–213)

### 文型 2: ……のに、……のが……である

A pattern combining the *noni* and *no ga…de aru* forms. Here, the latter is an elaboration on
conditions already emphasized, leaving the impression that these conditions were unexpected.
Main-text example: 住居空間が限られているのに、新しい製品が次々と生産・販売され、あふれているのが現在
の日本の都会である。("Despite severe housing conditions, the big cities of today's Japan are marked
by the unrelenting manufacture and retail of an overflow of novel products.") (p.213)

### 文型 3: ……には、……ほか方法がない

A form to express the fact that there is only one way to effectively deal with a situation.
Main-text example: 捨てられる世代と捨てられない世代が共存するには、互いに相手の考え方を尊重するほか方
法がない。("In Japan, where a disposable generation still coexists with a generation that finds it
hard to throw things away, mutual respect for the other party's viewpoints is essential.")
(p.213–214)

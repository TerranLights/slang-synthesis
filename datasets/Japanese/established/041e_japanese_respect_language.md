## Grammar points

### Deferential verbs defined and distinguished from depreciatory verbs (p. 115)

A deferential verb is defined as a depreciatory-type verb (lowering the status of its own subject)
that is so inherently respectful it is used, in practice, *only* to show respect to the 2nd person
(the addressee) and virtually never to the 3rd person — unlike ordinary depreciatory verbs, which
freely show respect to either the 2nd or the 3rd person. Deferential verbs function like ます masu
endings in this sense: they express deference specifically from the speaker to the person he is
addressing.

### Three major deferential-verb groups (pp. 116–117)

(i) 申します／申し上げます mōshimasu/mōshiagemasu, used for the する／いたす suru/itasu element of
any depreciatory construction built on that pattern (お願い申します／申し上げます for お願いする／
いたす). (ii) いたします itashimasu, used for する suru specifically in 勉強する benkyō-suru-type
verbs, since — as established in Chapter 5 — these verbs involve no other person and therefore have
no fuller depreciatory form to defer to; いたします is the only available deferential-level marking
for them. (iii) おります orimasu (for いる iru), 参ります mairimasu (for 参る mairu), ございます
gozaimasu (for ある aru), and でございます de gozaimasu (for だ da) — a named unique characteristic
of this third group is that, because respect is carried by *both* the verb choice and the masu
ending simultaneously, these four verbs can be used with any subject at all, including impersonal
ones, since there is no risk of inappropriately showing deference to an inanimate thing specifically
(the deference target is structurally the addressee, not the grammatical subject) — whereas it would
be inappropriate for the person addressed, his associates, or other exalted persons to appear as the
subject of these particular verbs.

### でございます/ございます's substitute forms in subordinate/pre-nominal position (pp. 122–123)

Because でございます de gozaimasu and ございます gozaimasu have no plain (non-masu) form, they cannot
appear directly before a noun or in most subordinate clauses the way an ordinary verb's plain form
can. Instead, the overall gozaimasu-level of politeness is preserved by substituting: ある aru and
the particle の no in place of ございます gozaimasu; である de aru (written/very formal spoken
register) or its more colloquial equivalents だ da, な na (which replaces だ da in certain fixed
expressions), and の no in place of でございます de gozaimasu — all without reducing the overall
respect level of the surrounding sentence, since the governing masu/gozaimasu context elsewhere
carries that respect. This is a clean example of politeness marking "leaking" onto a single
governing position in a sentence rather than needing to be repeated on every clause-level
element — relevant to any later comparative mechanics work on how obligatory politeness
grammaticalizes structurally versus lexically.

### (De) gozaimasu's attested overuse as a general-purpose respect marker (pp. 124–127)

A socially significant usage note: many Japanese speakers use ございます gozaimasu/でございます de
gozaimasu as a general, blanket respect marker even for subjects only loosely or closely associated
with the addressee (e.g. "Do you have any children?" as お子さんがございますか rather than the
source's own preferred, "stricter" お子さんがおありになりますか using the true honorific おありに
なる o-ari ni naru). The source explicitly frames おありになる-style honorific marking as more
*correct* per the book's own prescriptive framework, while acknowledging でございます-style blanket
usage is extremely common in authentic speech — another concrete instance (alongside the Chapter 3
register-inconsistency note) of real usage diverging from a strict compositional ideal, useful
context for later mechanics-analysis work distinguishing prescriptive grammar from attested informal
norms.

### Resolving a "social dilemma": combining depreciatory/deferential verbs with the honorific passive (pp. 126–128)

A genuinely interesting resolution mechanism for a structural conflict: when a speaker must show
respect simultaneously to (a) the person being addressed and (b) a 3rd person whose actions are
being discussed, using an ordinary honorific verb for the 3rd person plus a masu ending for the
addressee under-serves the addressee (who gets only a "minor" masu-level nod next to the 3rd
person's full honorific). The reverse approach — depreciating the 3rd-person's own verb to boost
respect to the addressee instead — then under-serves the 3rd person by *lowering* their grammatical
status. Japanese resolves this dilemma, per the source, via a third option found "very often" in
practice: use a deferential or depreciatory verb (addressing the addressee, as its primary function)
with the 3rd person as its grammatical subject, and then *additionally* give that same verb an
honorific passive ending, which "rectifies" the status-lowering effect on the 3rd-person subject
without undoing the deference shown to the addressee. Worked example: 先生は長い間アメリカにおりました
(oru-deferential alone, respect to addressee, mild status-lowering of the 3rd-person teacher) →
先生は長い間アメリカにおられました (same deferential verb + honorific passive -(ra)reta, now restoring
respect to the teacher too). The source notes this compound mechanism is most often attested
specifically with おる oru, 申す mōsu, and 参る mairu — i.e. it clusters on verbs from exactly the
"impersonal-subject-capable" group (iii) above, and extends to 3rd-person-vs-3rd-person competition
for respect as well (参った maitta used for a lower-status referent, 参られた mairareta for a
higher-status one, both appearing for the same verb mairu in parallel sentences depending on whose
status needs protecting). This is a clean, named example of a language stacking two normally
separate grammatical devices (depreciation + honorific passive) onto a single verb to satisfy two
simultaneous, otherwise-conflicting politeness obligations — a structurally rich finding for
mechanics-analysis work on how languages resolve competing politeness targets.

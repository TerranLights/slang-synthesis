## Grammar points

### I. Expressing problems and events using the passive form

The passive form expresses events, especially unfortunate ones, happening to the speaker/subject
(どろぼうに入られた "[I] had a burglar break in [on me]" — the "suffering/adversative passive" well
known cross-linguistically as distinctive to Japanese). (p. 358–363, condensed per sampling scope)

### II. Conjecture based on indirect evidence (〜らしい) vs. direct evidence (〜ようだ／みたいだ) — **core finding of this chunk**

This grammar point assembles the textbook's most explicit, side-by-side **evidentiality/register
scale across five competing conjecture markers**, paraphrased here as a consolidated table (the
source gives a full paradigm table per marker; this preserves its comparative structure, not its
literal cell contents):

| Marker | Evidence source | Relative reliability | Register note |
|---|---|---|---|
| plain form + ようだ | first-hand, reliable information (typically visual) + speaker's own reasoning | highest of the five | — |
| plain form + みたいだ | same evidential basis as ようだ | **lower confidence than ようだ** | **source states explicitly: "みたいだ is a colloquial version of ようだ"** |
| plain form + らしい | indirect evidence (print, word of mouth) or careful direct observation | more reliable than stem+そうだ; close to hearsay そうだ when the speaker's own conjecture is weak | — |
| plain form + そうだ (hearsay) | reported speech, no conjecture at all | n/a — speaker is only relaying, not judging | — |
| stem + そうだ (inferential) | direct sensory impression (often visual) | may or may not be reliable, most tentative | — |
| plain form + でしょう | no evidence required | reliability "varies from almost none to 100%" | — |

The source draws the ようだ/らしい contrast explicitly: **らしい typically reflects second-hand
information (reading, hearsay); ようだ implies first-hand information plus the speaker's own
reasoning** — and separately notes that when a speaker's conjecture from らしい is weak, it
converges in meaning with hearsay そうだ (both can translate "according to the news, there was a big
earthquake in China"). This six-way system (counting both そうだ uses separately) is the textbook's
single densest cluster of graded epistemic/register markers, directly useful for later
slang-mechanics work on how Japanese already grammaticalizes a formal/colloquial split **within a
single semantic category** (conjecture) — not just across request or copula forms as in earlier
chapters. (p. 363–366)

### III. Nのような／みたいな ("like ~"); NらしいN ("typical ~")

よう／みたいだ／らしい extend to noun-modification: N1のような／みたいなN2 compares N2 to N1
("something like N1"), while NらしいN (the same noun repeated) expresses "typical/quintessential N"
(先生らしい先生 "a teacher who really acts like a teacher"). (p. 366+, condensed per sampling scope)

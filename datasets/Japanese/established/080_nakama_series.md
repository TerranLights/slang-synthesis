## Edition comparison finding (full detail in `080j`)

**The two editions are a genuine curriculum revision, not a reprint.** The old (1st) edition's
table of contents shows a materially different chapter sequence and grammar-point grouping than
the new (2nd) edition's:

- The new edition's Chapter 3 ("Daily Routines") corresponds to **two separate chapters** in the
  old edition (old Ch. 5 "Daily Routine 1" and old Ch. 6 "Daily Routine 2") — the editions merged
  content, not just relabeled it.
- The new edition's Chapter 5 ("Japanese Homes") was the **old edition's Chapter 3** ("Japanese
  Houses") — moved later in the sequence.
- The new edition's Chapter 4 ("Japanese Cities") covers different ground than the old edition's
  Chapter 4 ("Japanese Towns and Universities") — the old chapter folds in campus-building
  vocabulary and entrance-exam culture content that the new edition's Chapter 4 doesn't carry at
  the same point.
- Katakana placement shifted: new edition inserts the Katakana unit immediately after Chapter 2;
  old edition places it after Chapter 3 (as "Chapter 3.5").

Given this, extraction **prioritizes the new edition** (clean text layer, and the more
current/deliberately-resequenced curriculum) as the primary source, with the old edition sampled
lightly via vision-reading to confirm the revision is real and to catch anything genuinely absent
from the new edition (see `080j`).

## Morphological note

Japanese is agglutinative; per this language's established convention (see
`../00_Extraction_Checklist.md`), vocabulary entries record the writing-system form used
(kanji/hiragana/katakana) alongside romanization and gloss, since script choice itself carries
register information (e.g., katakana for loanwords). Nakama's own vocabulary lists are presented as
hiragana-reading + kanji (where applicable) + English gloss, which this table preserves as
`Term` = kanji/katakana form (or hiragana if no kanji) with the hiragana reading folded into
`Notes` when it isn't independently recoverable from the kanji.

**Suffix productivity note:** Nakama 1 Ch. 2 explicitly teaches four grammaticalized suffixes
(〜ご "language," 〜じ "o'clock," 〜じん "-nationality," 〜せい "-student," 〜ねん "year") as
combinable morphemes (e.g., だいがくせい = だいがく "university" + せい "-student"). These are
captured as their own vocabulary rows with a morpheme-breakdown note where the combination is
pedagogically explicit.

## Grammar points

### I. Identifying someone or something, using 〜は〜です

The pattern **X は Y です** ("X is Y") and its negative **X は Y じゃありません／じゃないです**
("X isn't Y") identify or characterize a person/thing. は (pronounced "wa") marks X as the
sentence's topic — information already shared between speaker and listener — while Y です is the
comment made about that topic. Notes from the source, explicitly flagged as register-relevant:

- The topic X は can be dropped when context makes it obvious (e.g., in the fixed greeting
  はじめまして〜です, どうぞよろしく, わたしは is typically omitted).
- です／じゃありません／じゃないです do not inflect for person or number, unlike English *to be*.
- **じゃないです is explicitly marked more colloquial than じゃありません** — the source's own
  usage-tier annotation, carried through to the vocabulary/grammar table as the first
  colloquial-vs-formal copula contrast in the textbook.
- です/じゃありません do not express location (unlike English "Tokyo is in Japan").

(p. 46–47)

### II. Asking はい／いいえ questions, using 〜は〜ですか

Adding the particle か to the end of a statement turns it into a yes/no question — no word-order
change required (unlike English). Affirmative answers use はい／ええ、そうです; negative answers
use いいえ、そうじゃありません／そうじゃないです. Note: あなた ("you") exists but is markedly
less common than its English counterpart — Japanese prefers addressing/referring to someone by
name, even in second person. (p. 50–51)

### III. Indicating relationships between nouns with の

の lets a first noun modify a second, encoding possession, group-membership, location, or topic/
content relationships depending on context — a single particle covering what English splits across
several constructions (e.g., とうきょうだいがくの リーさん "Mr./Ms. Li from Tokyo University" vs.
れきしの せんこう "history major" vs. メキシコの うち "house in Mexico"). (p. 53)

### IV. Asking for personal information, using question words

Japanese information-questions (using なん/なに "what," どこ "where," どちら "where," more
polite) don't require word-order inversion — the question word simply occupies the slot of the
unknown information. Notes: a question word can never be marked by topic-marker は (it's part of
the comment, not the topic); どこから きましたか is the standard way to ask someone's
hometown/origin, with a more formal variant どちらから いらっしゃいましたか reserved for asking
(the answer should still use きました, not いらっしゃいました, even when the formal question form
was used) — an explicit asymmetric formal/plain pairing worth flagging for the slang-mechanics
phase. どこ (not なん) is also the correct question word for asking the *name* of a school.
(p. 56–58)

### V. Using も to list and describe similarities

も ("also, too") marks that the noun it follows shares a property with something already said,
substituting for は in a second, parallel sentence (e.g., わたしのこうこうは ミルズハイスクール
です vs. 〜も ... for a second person's equivalent fact). (p. 60–61)

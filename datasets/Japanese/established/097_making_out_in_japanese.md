This is a situational phrasebook (not alphabetical), organized by topic/chapter. The book marks
boy-only (♂) and girl-only (♀) phrasings explicitly; both are captured as separate `Term` rows (or
noted) rather than merged, and `(b→g)` notation (a boy-to-girl-specific phrasing) is captured in
Notes. Diamond (◆) marks in the source indicate a statement whose meaning flips to a question with
rising intonation — captured in Notes where relevant. This book explicitly avoids "too faddish" slang
per its own introduction, favoring durable colloquial forms over short-lived fads (its own stated
editorial policy, noted here for context, not extracted as a vocabulary item).

## Grammar points

### Compound-word hyphenation convention
The book joins multiple words/particles into hyphenated compounds (e.g. *fuzakenaide-yo*) to show
they should be pronounced as one breath/unit without a pause, since a pause would weaken emphatic
force (p.5).

### Boy/girl speech marking (♂/♀)
Phrases usable by only one sex are marked ♂ (boy) or ♀ (girl); unmarked phrases are usable by both.
`(b→g)` denotes a phrase a boy should specifically use when addressing a girl. The book notes gender
divisions are not absolute — "borrowing" the other gender's forms for emphasis is common and the gap
is narrowing, especially among younger speakers (pp. 6–7).

### Diamond (◆) rising-intonation marker
A statement marked with a leading ◆ changes meaning from declarative to interrogative when spoken
with rising intonation at the end (p.11), e.g. Hisashiburi-ne (♀, statement) → ◆Hisashiburi-ne
(question: "Haven't seen you in a while, have I?").

### Pronoun register ladder
The book presents a deliberately narrowed "safest" pronoun set (watashi/anata, kimi for male
speakers) for everyday use, while flagging *ore* and *omae* as harsh/uncompromising forms best
avoided until a learner has enough social fluency to judge when they're appropriate (p.9).

### Particle softening/emphasis system
*yo*, *wa*, *ne/nē*, *na*, *zo*, *ze*, *-kke* form a productive system of sentence-final particles
controlling register, gender association, and force — from *wa* (feminine softening) through *ne*
(familiar/rhetorical) to *zo*/*ze* (masculine, forceful command) (pp. 10–11).

### Katakana adaptation of foreign words
Japanese lacks final consonants besides *n*; loanwords get a vowel appended (usually *u*, e.g. "game"
→ *gēmu*), a long "a" replaces final "r" (e.g. "locker" → *rokkā*), and "l" is rendered as "r" since
Japanese has no separate "l" sound (p.8). New katakana conventions exist for sounds like "we" (ウェ)
and "v" + vowel (ヴァ/ヴィ/etc.), though actual "v" pronunciation varies by speaker/generation (p.7).

### Topic particle wa/ha and -masu/-mas romanization
The topic particle is written は but read *wa* (e.g. *watashi-wa*); the book romanizes polite verb
ending ます as *-mas* to match actual pronunciation rather than the literal *-masu* (p.9).

### Owatta vs. Dekita — completion aspect
Both mean "finished," but *Owatta* applies to anything, including open-ended ongoing work (which
isn't truly "finished" in a single day), while *Dekita* is restricted to tasks with a clear
start-and-end point (cooking dinner, a project, homework) (p.36). A genuine aspectual distinction
the book draws out through contrast rather than naming grammatically.

### Reduplicated onomatopoeia (giongo/gitaigo) as a productive system
The book presents a working set of doubled-syllable onomatopoeia (*wan-wan*, *nyan-nyan*,
*shito-shito*/*za-za*, *shiku-shiku*/*en-en*, *teku-teku*, *goku-goku*, *sū-sū*) covering animal
sounds, weather intensity, crying intensity, and bodily-state idioms (*onaka-ga peko-peko* "hungry,"
*atama-ga fura-fura* "dizzy") — presented explicitly as a "crazy foreigner routine" a learner can
deploy for comic effect/rapport, p.41. Worth flagging for the mechanics-analysis phase as a
productive intensity-pairing mechanism (light/heavy variants of the same semantic domain, e.g.
light vs. heavy rain, light vs. heavy crying).

### English-flattery stock-phrase ritual
The book documents a recognized social ritual: praising a Japanese speaker's English ("Eigo
jōzu-ne") reliably produces embarrassed denial, which is met with insistent reassurance ("Un,
jōzu-dayo") — framed as near-automatic cultural script rather than spontaneous conversation (pp.
38–39).

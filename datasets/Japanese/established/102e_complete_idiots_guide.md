## Grammar points

### Comparative/superlative question-word split (dochira-no hō vs. dore)

A clean, source-stated grammatical rule: dochira-no hō-ga is used only when comparing exactly two
items ("Between X and Y, which...?"), while dore-ga ichibaN is required once three or more items
are being compared ("Among X, Y, Z, which is the most...?") — a real two-vs-many distributional
split in Japanese interrogatives that doesn't map onto a single English comparative/superlative
form the same way.

### Negative-question answer-polarity inversion (hai/īe)

Documented at p.233 (declining-goods context) with a worked example: answering a negative question
like "Don't you have a red one?" inverts polarity relative to English — hai ("yes") confirms the
negative premise is correct ("Right, I don't have it"), while īe ("no") denies the premise ("Not
true — I do have it"). This is a systematic difference in how Japanese yes/no answers attach to
the truth-value of the question itself rather than to the expected-answer polarity, and recurs
wherever a negative question is used as a polite request softener throughout this book.

### Four confirmed distinct pragmatic functions of chotto across this book

Tracking across all extracted chapters so far: (1) attention-getter "Hey!" (Ch.11, `102b`),
(2) "wait" (Ch.11, `102b`), (3) literal "a little" (Ch.11, `102b`; also Ch.19 "a little bit sour"),
and (4) a bare hesitant chotto functioning as an implicit, face-saving "no" (Ch.11's decline
pattern, and again standalone in Ch.19's dining-etiquette section, p.248). This is the single most
densely multi-functional discourse particle this book exhibits, worth flagging as a unit for later
mechanics analysis of Japanese indirectness strategies.

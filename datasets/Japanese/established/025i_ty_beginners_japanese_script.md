# Japanese — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Teach Yourself Beginner's Japanese Script — Unit 9 (第九課, Katakana)

**Source:** Helen Gilhooly, *Teach Yourself Beginner's Japanese Script* (Hodder Headline, first published 1996, this edition 2003), Unit 9 ("第九課") pp. 125–149.

**Coverage note:** every grammar point (the full カタカナ syllabary, its writing-direction/stroke rules, the dakuten/handakuten sound-change rules, contracted sounds, the small ツ glottal-stop convention, and the extended sound set used for non-Japanese phonemes) plus every distinct loanword/proper-name item used across the unit's fifteen reading/matching activities. This unit is almost entirely loanword vocabulary (カタカナ exists specifically to transliterate non-Japanese words and names), so the vocabulary table below is unusually large and grouped by the activity/theme it comes from, mirroring the source's own organization, per the same density allowance used for Unit 8's signage table. Repeated matching-drill items that reuse an already-tabulated word are skipped per `../../00_Reference_Extraction_Spec.md`. The 46 base カタカナ symbols themselves (ア–ン) are treated as script/grammar content (see Grammar points) rather than tabulated as 46 individual vocabulary rows, consistent with how the equivalent ひらがな syllabary was handled in this book's Unit 5 coverage.

**Vision-reading note:** continues from `025h_ty_beginners_japanese_script.md` — same DJVU source, vision-read from rendered page images (two printed pages per scan image, confirmed formula: for scan image *N*, the left printed page = 2*N*−14 and the right printed page = 2*N*−13; this is the same formula already verified for Units 1–8 and reused here without re-deriving it). No handwritten marginalia was found anywhere in this unit; several pages include the book's own photographs of real Tokyo signage and advertising, which are the book's intentional illustrative material, not reader annotation.

## Grammar points

### The カタカナ syllabary and its origin
カタカナ represents the same 46 base sounds as ひらがな, in the same order and with the same pronunciation, but is used for loanwords (mostly from English) and foreign proper names (people, places, companies) rather than native Japanese vocabulary (pp. 126–129). Because most loanwords derive from English, a learner who can read カタカナ can frequently work out a word's meaning by sounding it out — this is presented as カタカナ's main practical advantage over ひらがな/漢字 for a beginner. The chart is traditionally written and read top-to-bottom, right-to-left, matching the ひらがな chart's own layout from Unit 5. Mnemonic shape-associations are offered for several symbols (ア "antelope," イ "leaning T," ウ "whisky flask," エ "elevator doors," キ "door key," p.128), paralleling the hiragana mnemonic method from Unit 5.

### Similar-looking カタカナ, and カタカナ/ひらがな lookalikes
The source groups together カタカナ symbols that are easy to confuse: ア/マ, サ/セ, ク/タ/ヌ, ウ/フ/ワ, ナ/メ, ル/レ, シ/ツ, ン/ソ — with an explicit stroke-direction note distinguishing シ (long stroke written upward, short strokes near right angles to it) from ツ (long stroke written downward at a steeper angle, short strokes side by side); ン is noted as sharing シ's features and ソ as sharing ツ's (p.131). Separately, a number of カタカナ symbols closely resemble their ひらがな counterpart because they derive from the same 漢字 source character: う/ウ, か/カ, き/キ, け/ケ, こ/コ, せ/セ, に/ニ, へ/ヘ, も/モ, や/ヤ, り/リ (p.131).

### Dakuten/handakuten sound changes (same rule as ひらがな, different script)
Adding ゛ (*ten ten*) to a カタカナ symbol shifts its consonant exactly as it does in ひらがな (reviewed back to Unit 5, pp. 68–9, rather than re-derived): k→g (カキクケコ→ガギグゲゴ), s→z (サシスセソ→ザジズゼゾ), t→d (タテト→ダデド), h→b (ハヒフヘホ→バビブベボ); adding a small circle ゜ (*maru*) to the h-row instead produces p (ハヒフヘホ→パピプペポ), p.132.

### Contracted sounds (拗音)
Exactly as with ひらがな (Unit 5), the symbols ヤ, ユ, ヨ can combine with an i-row symbol to form a single contracted syllable: キャ/キュ/キョ (kya/kyu/kyo), and the same pattern across ギ, シ, ジ, チ, ニ, ヒ, ビ, ピ, ミ, リ (pp. 134–5).

### The small ツ (glottal stop / "holding back" a sound)
A small ツ before a consonant is not itself pronounced but causes a brief pause (a glottal stop) before the following sound, which has the effect of "holding back" that sound slightly — illustrated with ホットドッグ (*hotto doggu*, "hot dog"), where the *to* and *gu* are described as held back in pronunciation (p.136). This is the same convention already introduced for small っ in ひらがな (Unit 5, p.69).

### Extra sounds built for foreign phonemes not native to Japanese
Because Japanese phonology lacks several English sounds, a fixed set of extensions and substitutions is used for loanwords and foreign names (pp. 136–139):
- English *th* has no Japanese equivalent and is replaced with *s* sounds (マラソン *marason* "marathon," スミス *su mi su* "Smith").
- *si* likewise does not exist and is replaced with シ (*shi*) or ジ (*ji*) (シドニー "Sydney").
- English *-er* is replaced by a lengthened *a* sound using ー (ハンバーガー "hamburger").
- Some *f* sounds are replaced with *h* sounds (テレホンカード "telephone card").
- *v* does not exist naturally; the nearest native substitute is *b* (エレベーター "elevator," ビデオ "video"), but a dedicated set of ヴ-based symbols (ヴァ va, ヴィ vi, ヴ vu, ヴェ ve, ヴォ vo) also exists for foreign names/countries specifically, though usage of the *b*-substitute instead is common even there (ヴィクトリア/ビクトリア "Victoria").
- *w* sounds are formed two ways: ウイ/ウエ/ウオ (traditionally for loanwords) versus the smaller ウィ/ウェ/ウォ (traditionally for foreign names/countries) — though the source notes this distinction is not strictly followed in practice, and young speakers in particular favor the small-symbol forms (p.138).
- Combination sounds using a small ア/イ/ウ/エ/オ after フ, シ, ジ, チ, ツ, テ, ディ produce additional sounds absent from the native 46-symbol set (フィ *fi*, フェ *fe*, フォ *fo*, シェ *she*, ジェ *je*, チェ *che*, ツァ *tsa*, ツェ *tse*, ティ *ti*, ディ *di*), p.138.

### Shortening and contracting loanwords
Loanwords are frequently shortened or contracted when adopted into Japanese, sometimes acquiring a narrower or new meaning in the process (pp. 139–140): ノート (*nōto*) drops the "book" half of "notebook"; テレビ (*terebi*) drops "-sion" from "television" (with *vi* read as *bi*); サンド (*sando*), shortened from サンドイッチ "sandwich," has narrowed specifically to mean a *toasted* sandwich; マスコミ (*masukomi*) contracts "mass communication"/"mass media"; ラジカセ (*rajikase*) contracts "radio cassette," dropping the middle portions of both words.

## Grammar points

### Compound sentences with *ga*/*keredomo* and reason *kara* (Unit 7)
Two contrasting clauses can be joined with `ga` or `keredomo` ("but"); `keredomo` shortens to the
explicitly-flagged-as-informal `kedo` in casual speech. Reason clauses are joined with `kara`
("because"), and the source notes Japanese tends to make the causal link explicit this way where
English would just juxtapose two sentences. (pp. 115-116)

### The plain (dictionary) form and its four conjugation classes (Unit 7)
Alongside `-másu` (polite) and `-te` (gerund) forms, every verb has a plain/dictionary form: copula
`désu`→`da`; consonant-root `kakimásu`→`káku`; vowel-root `tabemásu`→`tabéru`; irregular
`shimásu`→`suru`. Plain past tense is formed by changing final `-te`→`-ta` (`káite`→`káita`). The
plain form is the final-verb form used in casual speech among family/close friends/with children —
learners are advised to keep using the polite `-másu`/`désu` style as final verbs but must still
learn plain forms since they occur constantly in subordinate clauses. (pp. 117-118)

### Probability (*deshóo*), "before" (*máe*), and "after" (*-te kara*) (Unit 7)
`deshóo` after a plain verb expresses probability/supposition; with rising-falling question
intonation it seeks the listener's agreement. Plain verb + `máe` (optionally + `ni`) = "before."
Plain `-te` form + `kara` = "after," specifically implying the main-clause subject planned the
sequence in advance; `kara` also already means "from" after a noun of place. Where no advance
planning is implied, "after" is instead expressed with `áto`. (pp. 118-119)

### Indirect/reported speech: *to*, *sóo desu*, *yóo desu*, *rashíi desu* (Unit 7)
Quotative `to` follows a subordinate clause before a main verb of saying/thinking; in casual speech
it is often pronounced `te`/`tte`, though the source recommends sticking to standard `to` for now.
`sóo desu` after a plain verb reports hearsay ("I hear/they say," and loses the accent of an accented
preceding verb). `yóo desu` expresses a judgement from visible evidence rather than hearsay.
`rashíi desu` can cover either hearsay or appearance-based judgement, explicitly described by the
source as combining the functions of the other two. Indirect questions use plain-verb + `ka`
(without quotative `to`) before a verb of asking/knowing/believing; the plain copula `da` is usually
dropped before this `ka`. (pp. 119-122)

### Conditional/temporal *to*, "the time when" (*toki*), sequencing with *-te* (Unit 7)
Clause-final `to` after a plain present-tense verb expresses "when/whenever/if" for natural/habitual
consequences (the main clause cannot be a command in this construction). After a plain past-tense
verb, `to` instead implies an element of surprise at the outcome. `toki` ("time") after a plain verb
expresses "when" without the hypothetical/habitual connotation of `to`. Sequential events are
expressed by putting all but the final verb/adjective/clause into the `-te` form, with tense carried
only by the final verb. (pp. 121-124)

### Permission and prohibition with *-te mo íi desu* / *-te wa ikemasén* (Unit 7)
`-te mo íi desu` ("even if one does... it's good") grants permission; `kamaimasén` ("it doesn't
matter") softens it further. Prohibition uses `-te wa damé desu` or `-te wa ikemasén`. (pp. 122-123)

### Possession: alienable (*áru*) vs. inherent (*suru*) (Unit 7)
Japanese distinguishes possession of things that can be taken away (expressed with `áru`, "to
exist/have") from inherent/bodily attributes (expressed with `suru`, "to do" — e.g. `aói me o shite
imasu`, "has blue eyes"). (p. 138, cross-referenced from Unit 7's p.126 vocabulary)

### Past tense of true adjectives; adjectival clauses (Unit 8)
True adjectives form past tense with `-katta` (replacing final `-i`); negative past adds `-nakatta`
to the adverbial (`-ku`) stem. The desiderative `-tai` ("want to") ending from Unit 6 behaves as an
adjective and likewise takes `-katta`. `hoo` ("side/direction") after a plain past-tense verb gives
advice ("you'd better..."), notably using past tense even for future reference. Adjectival clauses
(Japanese equivalent of relative clauses, but preceding rather than following the noun) can be
formed from nouns+no, na-adjectives, adjectives, or full verb clauses (`kinóo átta hito`, "the
person I met yesterday"); the subject particle `ga` in such clauses is often replaced by `no`.
(pp. 133-136)

### "Can do" (*kotó ga dekíru*) and experience (*kotó ga áru*) (Unit 8)
`dekíru` on its own means "to be done/ready/produced" and replaces `suru` in noun+suru compounds to
express ability (`unten ga dekimásu ka`, "can you drive?"). For other verbs, nominalize the plain
present form with `kotó` and add `ga dekíru` ("can do..."). The same `kotó` + `áru` after a plain
past-tense verb expresses past experience ("have done..."); after plain present tense it means
"sometimes do..." (pp. 136-138)

### Comparisons (Unit 8)
No adjective inflects for comparative/superlative; instead use `yóri` ("than") with the plain
adjective: `Tookyoo wa Róndon yori ookíi desu`. "Which is more... A or B?" = `A to B to (déwa)
dótchi ga... desu ka`. `dótchi`/formal `dóchira` ("which of two") pair with demonstrative pronouns
`kótchi/kochira`, `sótchi/sochira`, `átchi/achira` — the `-ra` forms explicitly noted as more polite,
often used in invitations/directions. `hoo` adds emphasis or is used when only one comparison item
is stated. `mótto` ("more") and `ichiban` ("number one") express comparison/superlative without
direct adjective inflection. (pp. 139-141)

### The *-tára* conditional (Unit 9)
`-tára` ("if/when") attaches like `-ra` onto the plain past tense (`tábetara`, `ittára`; adjectives:
`isogáshikattara`). Unlike `to` (Unit 7), `-tára` can appear in sentences containing a request or
command. With a past-tense main verb, `-tára`, like `to`, carries a connotation of surprise. The
source contrasts `to` (habitual, every time) vs. `-tára` (this specific instance) with a minimal
pair: `Fuyú ni náru to sukíi ni ikimasu` ("when winter comes [every year] I go skiing") vs. `Fuyú ni
náttara sukíi ni ikimásu` ("when winter comes [this year] I'm going skiing"). (pp. 148-150)

### The *-(r)éba* conditional (Unit 9)
Attaches to the bare verb root (dictionary form minus final `-u`, or minus `-ru` for vowel-root/
irregular verbs): `káku`→`kákeba`, `tabéru`→`tabéreba`, `kúru`→`kúreba`, `suru`→`suréba`. True
adjectives take `-kereba` (`yókereba`); the negative existential `nái` likewise takes `-kereba`
(`nákereba`). Largely interchangeable with `-tára`, though certain set idioms (introduced in a
later unit) require `-(r)éba` specifically. (p. 150)

### "While" constructions: *-nagara* vs. *aida* (Unit 9)
`-nagara` on a verb stem expresses simultaneity when both actions share a subject (`tabenágara`,
"while eating") — the source notes Japanese puts the longer/main activity in the main clause and
the shorter one in the `-nagara` clause, which it flags as the reverse of typical English usage.
When the clauses have different subjects, "while" instead uses `aida`/`aida ni` after a plain
present-tense verb. (pp. 152-153)

### Numeral classifiers beyond floors/hours (Unit 9)
Japanese numeral classifiers vary by the shape/category of the counted object, with no consistent
English equivalent: `-nin` (people, irregular at 1/2: `hitóri`/`futarí`), `-dai` (vehicles/
machines), `-ken` (houses/shops), `-mai` (flat objects), `-hai` (cupfuls/glassfuls), `-hon`
(cylindrical objects), `-satsu` (books), `-hiki` (small/medium animals), `-wa` (birds), `-too`
(large animals), `-tsuu` (letters) — most undergo sound changes at 1, 3, 6, 8. For objects with no
specific classifier, the native numeral set (`hitótsu, futatsú...`) or Chinese numerals + `-ko` are
used instead. (pp. 153-154)

### Counting days of the month (Unit 9)
Two classifier systems: `-ka` (days 1-10, plus 14th/24th, combined with native numerals) and
`-nichi` (most other numbers, combined with Chinese numerals). The 20th, `hatsuka`, uses an archaic
native numeral that survives only there and in `hátachi` ("20 years old"). The same forms name
either an ordinal date or a duration (`mikka` = "3rd of the month" or "three days"). (pp. 155-156)

### Japanese era-based dates (Unit 9)
Dates are conventionally expressed by imperial reign-period + year rather than (or alongside) the
Western calendar: Meiji (from 1868), Taishoo (from 1912), Shóowa (from 1926), Heisei (from 1989).
Converting requires subtracting one from the reign year when adding to the start year (e.g. Shóowa
19 = 1944, i.e. 1925 + 19). Japanese date order (like address order) goes general-to-particular:
year, then month, then day. (p. 156)

### The *-tári* suffix: alternating/exemplary actions (Unit 9)
`-tári` (attaches like `-te`/plain-past, with the same sound changes) lists two or more actions as
illustrative examples of a potentially longer list (paralleling `nádo` for nouns); the sentence's
final/principal verb is usually a form of `suru`, occasionally the copula. Often appears with just
a single verb for a vaguer "or something" sense (`térebi o mítari shimasu`, "watch TV or
something"). (p. 157)

### Indefinite pronouns with *-ka* and *-mo*/*-demo* (Unit 9)
Question words + `-ka` give positive indefinites: `nánika` (something), `dáreka` (someone), `dókoka`
(somewhere), `dóreka` (any one of many), `dótchiraka` (either of two), `ítsuka` (sometime), `íkuraka`
(somewhat). The same question words + `-mo` give negative-leaning indefinites typically paired with
negative verbs (`nánimo` "nothing," `dókomo` "nowhere," `ítsumo` "always/never," depending on
polarity); `-demo` instead gives emphatic universal indefinites (`nándemo` "anything at all,"
`dókodemo` "anywhere at all," `ítsudemo` "any time at all"). Directional particles (`e`/`ni`) insert
between the question word and `mo` (`dárenimo`, `dókoemo`). (pp. 158-159)

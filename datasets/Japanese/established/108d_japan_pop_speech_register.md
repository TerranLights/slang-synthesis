## Grammar points

### English/katakana loanwords as a documented register-escape strategy for female songwriters

The chapter's central sociolinguistic argument, explicitly stated and argued at length (pp. 97–99):
because standard Japanese carries well-documented grammatical/lexical restrictions on how women are
"permitted" to express strong personal emotion directly, several prominent 1990s female
singer-songwriters (cited by name: Yumi [Matsutōya], Dreams Come True's Miwa Yoshida,
[Shonen] Knife) strategically deploy English words/phrases — sometimes whole English verses — as a
way of stepping *outside* the gendered constraints of "pure" Japanese to express things more
directly. The author explicitly traces this to a much older historical pattern: in medieval Japan,
men wrote publicly in imported Chinese while women, barred from learning Chinese characters,
developed Japan's earliest vernacular prose/poetic literary forms in native Japanese (citing *The
Tale of Genji*) — i.e. the chapter argues that women's use of a non-native linguistic register to
route around gendered expressive restriction is a recurring structural pattern across Japanese
literary history, not a novel 1990s phenomenon. This is a close real-world parallel to this
project's `register_inversion`/`borrowing` derivation-type framework, documented with named,
dated, sourced examples rather than general claims.

### Two paired real-song lexical-choice case studies (loanword vs. native-register minimal pairs)

The chapter presents two specific, citable word-choice contrasts where a songwriter chose an
English loanword over an available native-Japanese near-synonym, with the author's own stated
rationale for each:
- "dandelion" (English, in the song title/lyric) vs. native *tanpopo* — the loanword is argued to
  universalize the image (any urban "OL" can relate) where the native word would localize/rustic-
  ify it ("a country girl in love with a country boy").
- the katakana-rendered English loanword "thank you" vs. native *domo*/*arigatō* — the loanword
  is argued to convey a deliberately *lighter*, less emotionally weighty farewell register,
  letting the singer "make the situation seem less tragic than it is."

Both cases are explicitly sourced to either the songwriter's own stated intent (an author
interview, for "dandelion") or the chapter author's own close reading (for "thank you"), making
them unusually well-evidenced examples of deliberate, conscious register-selection by a named
speaker — directly useful source material for this project's later mechanics-analysis phase on how
loanword choice functions as a register-signaling device in contemporary Japanese.

## Grammar points

### Verbs of "asking": sasou vs. kiku/tazuneru/shitsumon-suru vs. tanomu/onegai-suru
English "ask" covers three unrelated Japanese actions that must not be conflated: asking a
question (kiku, most common; also shitsumon-suru, tazunemasu), asking someone to do something
(tanomu; more politely onegai-suru), and inviting someone out (sasou; yobu/maneku specifically for
inviting to one's own home). Vol.4 p.8-9.

### Self-reference restriction on honorific motion verbs
irassharu and oide-ni-naru are honorific verbs that can only describe the listener's or a
third party's going — never the speaker's own. A fully polite exchange therefore needs two
different verbs in sequence (one honorific for the listener, one humble for the speaker), e.g.
"Irasshaimasen-ka. Watashi-mo mairimasu." A simpler alternative in such contexts is the humble
otomo-shimasu ("I'll go with/follow you"). Vol.4 p.10-11.

### The three senses of oshieru vs. miseru
oshieru bundles "teach," "show how to do," and "tell/inform," which English keeps as three
separate verbs. miseru is reserved for showing a concrete physical object (a book, a letter) and
is not interchangeable with oshieru even though both can translate as "show." Using "...wa
nan-desu-ka" instead of "...o oshiete-kudasai" to ask for someone's address or phone number sounds
unnatural/awkward. Vol.4 p.12-13.

### tanomu (personal favor) vs. yoo-ni yuu (duty-based instruction)
When a request concerns something that is already part of the listener's role or duty (a teacher
assigning homework, a boss delegating job tasks), Japanese uses "...yoo-ni yuu," not tanomu — tanomu
is reserved for requests outside of that duty, framed as a personal favor to the speaker, even when
directed at a person who is otherwise in an authority role (e.g. a student personally helping a
teacher with an errand). Students self-reporting a teacher's assignment typically use the passive
"...yoo-ni iwaremashita" rather than the plain "...yoo-ni iimashita" framing, which sounds like the
student is describing someone else's duty-assignment from outside rather than experiencing it.
Vol.4 p.14-15.

### Avoiding mondai-ga arimasu as an explanation-opener
Opening an explanation with "Mondai-ga arimasu" is poor practice in Japanese discourse — unlike a
neutral English "there's a problem," it signals that something serious and already-understood is
at stake, and can read as criticism if used about a third party's situation. Preferred softer
framings front-load the topic first ("...no koto-desu-ga...," "...no koto-de chotto
gosoodan-shitai-n-desu-ga...") so the listener isn't blindsided, with a hesitant/considerate tone of
voice doing real pragmatic work alongside the words themselves. Vol.4 p.16-17.

### Ellipsis in declining offers: ii-desu
Declining an offer with a bare "ii-desu" is a contraction of a longer conditional clause
("...[verb]-nakute-mo ii-desu," "you don't have to...") with the conditional dropped once it's
obvious from context — the same elliptical pattern that drops the second half of phrases like
"kaeranakute-wa narimasen." The omission can go either direction (front half or back half dropped)
depending on which part is contextually recoverable. Saying only "ii-desu" without a softening "iie"
or trailing "...kara" clause can sound abrupt; kekkoo-desu functions the same way but slightly more
formally. Vol.4 p.18-19.

### kare/kanojo are not neutral third-person pronouns
Unlike English "he"/"she," kare and kanojo function more like a noun phrase ("that fellow," "that
girl") and are restricted to referring to someone within the speaker's own in-group with whom the
speaker feels genuine familiarity — never usable for a person who requires polite/formal reference
(a title substitutes instead, e.g. shachoo for a company director). Usage skews younger; among older
speakers kanojo specifically connotes "girlfriend/lover" rather than a generic "she," which can make
a husband's habitual use of kanojo for his own wife (rather than her name) read as oddly distant to
her. Vol.4 p.20-21.

### ...(nai) uchi-ni: a concern-marked "before" construction
"Uchi" ("inside/within") combined with a negative verb or adjective form yields a "before it becomes
otherwise" meaning, but unlike the neutral "...mae-ni" ("before..."), it carries an implication that
the speaker is worried about an undesirable change if the window closes. The same pattern attaches
to affirmative forms with an implied "before it becomes otherwise" reading — "wakai uchi-ni" ("while
young") means "before you get too old," not simply "during youth." Vol.4 p.22-23.

### Benefactive-verb politeness inversion: te-moraimasu over te-agemasu
Describing one's own helpful act with "...te-agemasu" ("I'll do you the favor of...") can sound
boastful or condescending in Japanese, even though the fact described is identical to a more modest
framing. The polite convention inverts the direction of "favor": the speaker describes their own
generous act as if receiving a favor instead — "Tetsudawasete-itadakimasu" (lit. "I will receive the
favor of being allowed to help you") or "Tetsudawasete-kudasai" (lit. "please allow me to help")
rather than "Tetsudatte-agemashoo." This politeness-inversion logic extends to describing a third
party's act of giving something away using a double-morau construction ("moratte-moraimashita," lit.
"I received the favor of their receiving it") to frame the giver as personally grateful rather than
magnanimous. Vol.4 p.24-25.

### sono kawari / ...no kawari-ni: substitution vs. balancing
Kawari alone means "substitute," and "...no kawari-ni" ordinarily marks straightforward substitution
between two options ("coffee instead of tea"). But placed between two contrastive descriptive
clauses about one single referent, the same phrase shifts sense entirely, from substitution to
"balancing/offsetting" a plus against a minus about that one thing (an apartment that's quiet but
far from the office). The two clauses in this balancing use are freely reversible in order, and the
construction extends to marking reciprocal compensation across time (permission to leave early
today in exchange for an expectation of coming early tomorrow). Vol.4 p.26-27.

### botsubotsu as a considerate pace-setting cue
Botsubotsu ("little by little") softens the act of urging someone to get moving by implying a
gradual, non-forced transition — both emotionally and physically — rather than the bluntness of
"Saa, dekakemashoo" (Now let's go) or "Moo jikan-desu" (It's time now). The same word extends to
describing an imminent-but-unspecified return time, where only context (not the word itself) fixes
whether "pretty soon" means minutes or hours. Vol.4 p.28-29.

### gossori / sukkari / sappari: adverbs that imply their own verb
These manner adverbs are frequently used with the main verb dropped entirely, because the adverb
alone makes the action/result obvious — gossori implies theft/removal in bulk, sukkari implies a
completed state-change (full recovery), sappari implies total absence or completion (often
negative-polarity, "not at all"). Mizutani notes this ellipsis is productive enough to drive
clipped newspaper-headline style, where only the adverb survives in the headline. Vol.4 p.30-31.

### ...nakereba narimasen is a formal-duty register, wrong for leave-taking
The full formal obligation construction "...nakereba narimasen" is correct for written/formal
statements of duty or legal requirement, but sounds pragmatically off when used as the sentence
that ends a visit at someone's house — it frames the departure as an externally-imposed obligation
rather than ordinary courtesy. The natural leave-taking expressions instead are shitsuree-shimasu
("I'm going to be impolite [by leaving]") or oitoma-shimasu ("I'm taking my leave"), with
kaeranakucha/kaeranakya as casual mid-register shortenings of the obligation form itself (which
remains fine mid-sentence as a reason clause, just not as a leave-taking closer). Vol.4 p.32-33.

### kondo's three senses and its use as an announcement cue
Kondo covers "this time" (contrastive), "next time" (future), and — a third, distinct discourse
function — a cue word that signals the speaker is about to make a significant personal announcement
(marriage, moving, a job change), regardless of whether that announcement concerns the future or
something already decided/completed. This announcement sense is typically closed with "...koto-ni
narimashita" or "...koto-ni shimashita." On formal/ceremonial occasions, kono tabi substitutes for
kondo in this same announcement function. Vol.4 p.34-35.

### hai's multiple functions, contrasted with hora
Hai serves at least four distinct discourse functions beyond plain affirmative "yes": aizuchi
backchanneling ("I'm following you"), a finality marker appended to a statement, and a handover
announcement said while physically giving something to someone (change, a receipt, mail). This
handover sense is functionally distinct from hora, which instead directs the listener's attention
to a situation or quantity they should notice, rather than accompanying a literal handover. Vol.4
p.36-37.

### guchi (complaining) as a sympathy-seeking speech genre, not a problem-solving request
Guchi-o yuu/kobosu targets a sympathetic bystander rather than the person responsible for the
grievance, and the complainer typically does not want the listener to actually intervene — the
function is cathartic release plus social bonding through shared sympathy. The correct listener
response therefore echoes the complainer's own framing and standpoint rather than offering outside
"understanding" or "agreement," which would imply the listener occupies a different, detached
position; "Doo shita mon-deshoo-ne" (I wonder what should be done) goes furthest, with the
sympathizer performing the complainer's vexation as if it were their own. Vol.4 p.38-41.

### te-kuru vs. te-iku: directional aspect toward/away from the speaker
This verb-suffix pair marks whether an ongoing action or change is conceived as moving toward the
speaker (te-kuru) or away from the speaker (te-iku). Te-kuru is the default for describing change
up to the present and signals the speaker wants to share the experience further with the listener —
productive enough to be used strategically to build in-group rapport, e.g. opening a public speech
with "itte-kimashita" rather than plain "ikimashita" to signal shared membership with the audience.
Te-iku dominates in two settings: describing future change in conversation, and — in written/literary
register — narrating change up through the past with the deliberately detached stance expected of an
author describing historical events or a novel's characters from outside. Vol.4 p.42-45.

### ...ni yoru-to as formal source-marking, avoided in conversation
...ni yoru-to specifically marks the source of reported information (contrast ...ni yotte, which
marks means/method) but reads as stiff/written-register even when grammatically correct in speech.
Natural conversation instead names the specific channel directly ("it was written in the paper
that...," "I read in the paper that...," "they said on the TV news that...") or, for a specific
person's statement, uses "X-san-ga itte-ta-n-desu-ga" rather than the formal "X-san-no hanashi-ni
yoru-to." Vol.4 p.46-47.

### ima-goro vs. kono-goro: speaker-involvement marking on "these days"
The two near-synonyms for "these days/nowadays" split along whether the speaker is personally
implicated in the situation being described. Kono-goro is used when the present circumstance
directly concerns the speaker (or someone explicitly grouped with the speaker's own situation);
ima-goro is the detached default for talking about the present time (or someone else's present
circumstances) in general, and separately does double duty meaning "at this time of year" (with an
implied, droppable mainen, "every year"). Vol.4 p.48-49.

### oboeru (process) vs. oboete-iru (resultative state)
Oboeru is not a translation of "to remember" — it names the effortful act of learning/memorizing
something new, and its negated/difficulty forms describe trouble acquiring information, not trouble
recalling it. The actual "remember" meaning belongs to the -te iru resultative form, oboete-iru,
which describes the state of currently holding something in memory. For failing to recall something
already known, natural conversation reaches for alternative expressions ("Saa, nan-te
itta-deshoo," "Wasuremashita") rather than a negated oboeru/oboete-iru; and asking someone to keep
something in mind going forward uses "Wasurenaide-kudasai" rather than "oboete-ite-kudasai." Vol.4
p.50-51.

### tsumori: intention after present tense, asserted belief after past tense
Tsumori changes function depending on the tense of the verb it follows. After a present/non-past
verb it states a straightforward intention ("I intend to..."). After a past-tense verb, it instead
asserts the speaker's confident belief about something already done or already true — often used
defensively when the speaker is being blamed or doubted ("I maintain that I did all I could"),
and distinguishable from the more neutral hedge "...to omoimasu" by this self-assertive edge. A
present-tense state verb or adjective can carry the same self-assertive "belief" sense (implying
"so there's no need to explain further to me"). Applied to a second or third person rather than the
speaker, this past-tense-tsumori construction reliably reads as sarcastic, implying the speaker
disagrees with that person's self-image. Vol.4 p.52-53.

### Politely asking someone's wishes: avoid -tai, use -(r)eba yoroshii-deshoo
The desiderative -tai form ("want to...") is inherently a first-person (or blunt second-person)
form and carries no built-in politeness — using it to ask what someone else wants ("Itsu
tsukaitai-desu-ka") sounds blunt and awkward regardless of surrounding politeness markers. The
polite way to ask about someone's wishes instead uses a conditional-plus-yoroshii-deshoo
construction ("...(sur)eba yoroshii-deshoo") or a "...-mashoo-ka" offer, reframing the question
around what action would be acceptable/convenient rather than what the listener personally desires.
Vol.4 p.54-55.

### tsuide-ni: softening a request by framing it as incidental
Tsuide (literally "next" / "of secondary importance") plus ni...suru reframes a requested favor as
something the listener can accomplish incidentally to an errand they're already running for their
own primary purpose — which action comes first doesn't matter; what matters is that the favor is
marked as secondary in importance, making the request feel less imposing. More reserved variants
("tsuide-ga attara," "tsuide-no toki-ni") go further, explicitly releasing the listener from making
a special (wazawaza) trip solely for the requester's sake. Vol.4 p.56-57.

### sono-uchi(-ni) vs. chikai uchi-ni: vagueness as the deciding factor
Both phrases mean roughly "before long," but they're not interchangeable: chikai uchi-ni is bounded
to a days/weeks/months timeframe and cannot describe something happening within minutes, while
sono-uchi(-ni) is deliberately open-ended, spanning anywhere from minutes to a vague, unspecified
future — which is exactly why it's the idiomatic choice for noncommittal social promises like
accepting an invitation without fixing a date. Vol.4 p.58-59.

### Double negatives as emphasis-plus-affect markers
A double-negative conditional like "...naranakereba + negative verb" does more than state a
condition neutrally — it emphasizes that condition as the operative constraint, and simultaneously
signals the speaker's own feelings about it (sympathy/regret on another's behalf, or self-directed
irritation/quiet pride). The flat affirmative equivalent describing the same fact reads as
impersonal and unconcerned by comparison, which is itself meaningful: choosing the double-negative
form is itself an act of emotional framing, not just a stylistic variant. Vol.4 p.60-61.

### osaki-ni and group-unity norms around breaking away
Osaki-ni ("excuse me for going first" / "after you") is used whenever someone steps away from a
group they belong to — leaving work early, leaving a party early, or offering to let someone else
go first — reflecting an underlying norm that a group member breaking away, even briefly or for a
legitimate reason, owes the group a small apology for disrupting its unity. This holds regardless of
relative rank (used toward superiors, equals, and juniors alike), and plain departure phrases like
"Kaerimasu" or "Ikanakereba narimasen" are considered socially inadequate substitutes in a group
setting. The underlying value extends beyond politeness formulas: many Japanese speakers regard
staying physically together with one's group — not just emotionally aligned with it — as important
in both work and leisure contexts. Vol.4 p.62-63.

### mon-desu/mono-desu as a marker of reflective depth
Appending mon-desu or mono-desu to a statement signals that the speaker has given the underlying
fact or situation real, even philosophical, consideration — it is not a neutral observation marker.
When used to agree with someone else's remark, it signals deeper/stronger agreement than a flat
restatement would. A distinct extension of the same pattern marks nostalgic reflection on the past,
where the speaker is explicitly looking back with emotion (admiration, pity, or similar) rather than
just reporting a historical fact. Vol.4 p.64-65.

### mata as a surprise-intensifier, distinct from its literal "again"
Beyond its literal repetition sense ("again") and its use in parting phrases ("see you again"), mata
attaches to expressions of surprise or incredulity as an intensifier — critically, this usage does
NOT imply the event previously happened, unlike the literal sense. Because the surprised tone alone
carries much of the meaning, the rest of the sentence becomes droppable, down to a bare "Nande
mata..." Vol.4 p.66-67.

### -tte: a colloquial contraction with four distinct jobs
-tte stands in colloquially for to, to yuu, to yuu mono-wa, or to yuu-no-wa, which cash out as at
least four distinct functions: marking quoted speech (where the following verb of saying, e.g.
iimashita, is frequently dropped once understood), topic-marking in a "speaking of X" sense,
requesting clarification about a term just used by the other speaker, and (per the next essay)
emphatic subject-marking. Mizutani notes the sheer range of -tte's uses makes it one of the harder
colloquial particles to master. Vol.4 p.68-69.

### komaru's default-subject ellipsis
Stated with no explicit grammatical subject, komaru ("to be troubled/annoyed") defaults to referring
to the speaker's own state, not a third party's — even when a third-party noun phrase is tacked onto
the end of the same utterance as an afterthought (the noun phrase explains the cause, not the
subject, of the speaker's own annoyance). To attribute the trouble explicitly to someone else
requires an overt particle marking that person (ni or wa), though these too are frequently dropped
once context makes the intended reading clear. The progressive form komatte-iru lacks this
speaker-default bias and can refer to any subject, including the speaker, without ambiguity. Vol.4
p.70-71.

### -ttara: emphatic subject-marking restricted to strong/negative affect
Functionally similar to -tte's subject-emphasis use, -ttara is reserved specifically for a subject
the speaker has a strong personal feeling about, typically an unfavorable evaluation — it cannot
substitute for -tte in neutral, purely factual subject-confirmation contexts. Attached to an
adjective, the same suffix forms a distinct "indescribably X" intensifier construction ("too X for
words"), extending the pattern from marking a notable subject to marking a notable degree. Vol.4
p.72-73.

### dake: extent-marking, not inherently restrictive
Dake is conventionally glossed as "only," but with demonstratives, quantity words, or a specific
figure, it more precisely marks an extent or degree whose evaluation as a lot or a little depends
entirely on the speaker's attitude and the surrounding context — the identical phrase "sore-dake"
can appear in both a "that's not enough" framing and a "that's plenty" framing. With verbs, dake
shifts again to mean "enough to" or "corresponding to," describing a sufficiency relationship rather
than a restriction. Vol.4 p.74-75.

### ...dooshi: a productive in-group exclusivity suffix
...dooshi attaches to a category noun to assert that a group consists of members of that category
and only that category, explicitly excluding outsiders — a distinction the source's own newspaper-
kanji frequency survey flags as one of the most common uses of the kanji doo (同). Beyond fixed
social categories (women-only, men-only, old-only, children-only), the suffix is productive enough
to form ad hoc groupings around any shared circumstance a speaker wants to name, including a shared
misfortune (two people who were each stood up deciding to get a drink together as mono-dooshi,
"people in the same boat"). Vol.4 p.76-77.

### Knock count as a culturally specific nonverbal convention
Japanese door-knocking convention fixes the expected count at two knocks for an ordinary, unremarkable
knock; three or more knocks in quick succession conventionally reads as signaling an emergency to a
Japanese listener inside the room. This is a real cross-cultural mismatch point, since a habit many
Western speakers default to (three knocks) unintentionally alarms Japanese listeners — a case where a
purely nonverbal, non-lexical signal still carries learnable, culturally specific meaning the same
way a word would. Vol.4 p.78-79.

### Gift-giving politeness requires matching verbal and nonverbal form
A self-deprecating gift-presentation phrase like "makoto-ni tsumaranai mono-desu-ga..." is only half
of the politeness act — correct handover technique is the other, equally mandatory half. Convention
calls for both hands even on a small item (the non-active hand touches or supports the item as if
confirming the handover), presenting the item at an angle the recipient can take squarely rather than
diagonally, and orienting it so the recipient could immediately read or use it (e.g. a book's spine
toward them). Using the polite verbal formula without the matching gesture leaves the recipient
confused about how to respond — the words alone are not treated as sufficient. Vol.4 p.80-81.

### ojigi: bow depth, duration, and mutual timing all carry meaning
Bowing in personal situations is a slow, whole-body forward/downward motion, sometimes with a slight
knee bend; a quick bow reads as careless or insincere, an abrupt one as childish. Spoken politeness
formulas accompanying a bow (thanks, requests for favor) are conventionally allowed to trail off to
near-inaudibility as the bow deepens, since the meaning is already understood by that point.
Crucially, the timing of straightening back up must be synchronized to the other party's own bow —
rising early is embarrassing, so a participant must watch the other person's bow to time their own
correctly. Separately, hajimemashite is explicitly the contracted form of the fuller, more formal
"Hajimete ome-ni kakarimasu," which remains available for introductions needing extra formality.
Vol.4 p.82-83.

### Negative-polarity adverbs inviting collaborative sentence completion
Adverbs like amari ("not very") and doomo ("indeed/somehow") strongly imply an upcoming negative
conclusion, which licenses the speaker to leave the sentence trailing unfinished and the listener to
either infer the ending silently or actually voice its completion aloud — treated in Japanese
conversational norms as a sign of engaged, interested participation rather than interruption or a
sign that the original speaker's Japanese is deficient. Trailing conjunctions (kara, node, kedo)
produce the same effect. Sentence-final tone (sustained vs. falling) signals which response —
listener-completion or mere aizuchi backchanneling — is expected; the source explicitly advises
foreign learners not to feel patronized when a Japanese listener completes their sentence, framing it
instead as a marker of accepted in-group conversational participation. Vol.4 p.84-85.

### "You don't have to be so polite" as indirect admiration, not correction
When a Japanese listener tells a foreign speaker "sonna-ni teeneena kotoba-o tsukawanakute-mo...,"
the surface imperative-like form is misleading — the real pragmatic function is a mixture of
surprised admiration and sympathetic concern (imagining the effort behind acquiring such politeness),
not criticism of excessive formality. This reflects a broader reticence pattern: Japanese speakers
are often too reserved to express admiration (or, on the flip side, discomfort/offense) directly, so
positive feedback emerges indirectly through seemingly corrective phrasing — and by the same logic,
the absence of any spoken correction when a foreigner speaks too casually is not reliable evidence
that the register was actually acceptable. Vol.4 p.86-87.

### Indirect requests from superiors and the apology for failing to anticipate them
Expressing a wish or request indirectly (a superior remarking on the heat rather than directly asking
someone to open a window) is a real, habitually used register among Japanese speakers, particularly
from superiors toward subordinates, and also prized between close friends/family as a marker of deep
mutual understanding. The expected subordinate response is to infer the unspoken request and act on
it unprompted; failing to do so calls for a ritual self-reproach apology ("Doomo ki-ga
tsukimasen-de...," more casually "Ki-ga tsukanakute gomen-nasai") acknowledging the failure to
anticipate, not an apology for the action itself. Vol.4 p.88-89.

### Phatic greetings that function as "have a good time," not real questions
Odekake-desu-ka ("are you going out?") and the more familiar/personally-concerned dochira-e ("where
are you going?") are not information requests — the speaker has already seen that the listener is
dressed up and heading out, and the greeting exists to confirm and share in that happy observation,
functioning like English "Have a good time." Because many Japanese speakers find it psychologically
difficult to volunteer good personal news unprompted, this greeting formula gives them license to do
so once asked. The formula is specifically mismatched to someone who is not dressed for a happy
occasion or who is heading out on unpleasant business — using it then reads as socially off, as
illustrated by Mrs. Takahashi's unresponsive reaction when it didn't fit her actual situation. Vol.4
p.90-91.

### Self-deprecating speech-openers as ritual, not literal self-assessment
Opening a speech with "watashi-wa doomo hanashi-ga heta-de..." ("I'm a poor speaker, so...") is a
conventional formula both speaker and audience understand as non-literal — even acknowledged skilled
speakers use it, and it is not read as false modesty. Its real function is closer to "I'm glad to be
able to speak to you today": it lets the audience settle in and the speaker himself get ready. Length
of such openers is a matter of individual/audience preference, with shorter versions generally
preferred by younger or busier listeners; a foreign speaker is advised to use an analogous short
apologetic remark about their Japanese ability rather than skipping the convention. Vol.4 p.92-93.

### owari as a taboo word at weddings, and vaguer end-avoidance elsewhere
Owari ("the end") is explicitly forbidden at a wedding speech or reception because it evokes the idea
of the marriage itself ending; ohiraki (literally "opening") is used euphemistically instead to mean
the event's closing. The same underlying avoidance of definite "ending" language shows up more mildly
at ordinary meetings and drinking gatherings, where owaru/kaeru are often sidestepped and the end of
the event is signaled only vaguely (a bare "Ja, kore-de," or simply bowing, without a spoken
owarimasu) — producing real, sometimes frustrating ambiguity for foreigners about whether a gathering
has actually concluded. Vol.4 p.94-95.

### In-group/out-group address-term switching for the same person
The same individual can correctly be addressed by two entirely different naming registers depending
on which social group frame is active: a bare first name signals in-group affection/informality (a
junior office colleague called by first name, treated like "everybody's younger sibling"), while a
respect-marked surname+san is required the moment the context includes people from a different group
the person also belongs to in a subordinate role (most clearly, their own parents). Using the
in-group nickname in the wrong frame reads as implying the person isn't taken seriously, which is
precisely backward from the in-group's actual affectionate intent. The same switching logic governs
teacher-to-student address: bare last names in the classroom, but kun/san-suffixed names when
speaking with the students' parents. Vol.4 p.96-97.

### doozo vs. onegai-shimasu: whose benefit the dropped verb serves
Both phrases can stand in for a politely dropped verb, but they encode opposite implied benefit
directions. Doozo recommends a course of action primarily for the listener's own convenience or
benefit (showing someone to a seat, inviting them to proceed). Onegai-shimasu, rooted in negau ("to
implore"), marks the utterance as an actual favor being requested of the listener for the speaker's
benefit. The identical surface situation (e.g. asking someone to come this way) can be framed either
way depending on which party's interest the speaker wants to foreground. Vol.4 p.98-99.

### Ritual refusal and the formula for overcoming it
Japanese social convention expects an initial reflexive refusal of almost any offer — food, drink, a
gift, an honor, or being asked to take on difficult/honorable work — even when the offer is actually
wanted, because appearing too readily willing to accept is read as childish or presumptuous. "Soo
osshararazu-ni..."/"Soo iwanaide..." ("please don't say that") is the conventional counter-formula
used by the offerer to override this expected first refusal, implicitly completed by "please accept
my offer." A politeness-register scale runs from the honorific-based ossharazu-ni/ossharanaide down
through the plainer iwanaide/iwazu-ni, with -zu-ni slightly more formal than -naide throughout. Vol.4
p.100-101.

### moshi-moshi as a general attention-getter, not a phone-specific greeting
Despite being taught almost exclusively as a telephone opener, moshi-moshi is fundamentally a
general-purpose attention-getting phrase usable mid-call or in any in-person situation requiring one
to hail a stranger (e.g. pointing out a dropped item on the street). Critically, the correct response
when hailed this way is hai, not an echoed moshi-moshi — answering moshi-moshi with moshi-moshi reads
as an awkward mistake. Separately, Japanese phone calls have no single fixed sign-off equivalent to
English "goodbye" (sayonara is not standard here); "Ja," "Ja, mata," or formal "Shitsuree-shimasu"
are used instead, and these same expressions double as in-person parting phrases, which can make it
genuinely hard for foreign learners to tell when a call has actually ended. Vol.4 p.102-103.

### wakatte-imasu vs. wakarimashita: asserting prior knowledge vs. confirming new understanding
Wakatte-imasu (the resultative "-te iru" form) asserts that the speaker already possesses the
knowledge being offered, and carries a real risk of sounding impolite or dismissive when said to
someone currently explaining something with good intentions — it implicitly signals "I don't need to
hear more." Wakarimashita is the pragmatically correct response for confirming that an explanation
was just freshly understood. Delivery tone modulates both: an irritated wakarimashita can itself
sound rude, while a gently-delivered wakatte-imasu can appropriately reassure rather than shut down.
A third, distinct form — plain-present wakarimasu — functions instead as a sympathetic-listener
response within the guchi (complaining) genre, meaning "I can understand how you feel" rather than
confirming comprehension of an explanation at all; the three forms are not freely interchangeable.
Vol.4 p.104-105.

### Offering/inviting with ...masen-ka, not ...masu-ka
When extending an invitation or offer, the negative-question form (...masen-ka) is the pragmatically
correct choice, even though it looks less direct on the surface — the affirmative-question form
(...masu-ka) instead reads as a neutral, merely informational yes/no question about whether something
will happen, which can land as halfhearted or uninviting when the speaker actually meant to extend an
offer. This holds consistently across offering an object (a wet towel), extending a social invitation,
and offering food or drink, where the polite register substitutes meshiagarimasen-ka for
tabemasen-ka/nomimasen-ka. Vol.4 p.106-107.

### ...n-desu-ka's hidden presupposition
The ...n-desu-ka question form is not interchangeable with a plain ...masu-ka question — it carries
a built-in presupposition that the speaker has already observed something that prompted the question,
which is precisely why it works for teasing (hinting the speaker already suspects what's going on) but
backfires badly as a neutral information request. Used on a stranger or in a context with no actual
observed basis, it reads as a puzzling or subtly accusatory non-sequitur, since the listener has no
way to know what unstated situation the speaker thinks they're referring to. Vol.4 p.108-109.

### Classroom-register repeat-requests vs. social-register repeat-signaling
"Moo ichido itte-kudasai" is grammatically unimpeachable and entirely appropriate between teacher and
student, but imported into ordinary polite conversation it can land as presumptuous, implying doubt
about whether the original speaker knew what they were saying. Polite social conversation instead
relies on a graded, largely nonverbal escalation: an unconvinced facial expression or missing aizuchi
first (often enough on its own to prompt a spontaneous repeat), then a hesitant "Anoo...," then a
rising "Ha?" delivered while leaning forward (leaning back, or too sharp a rise in pitch, both shift
the same syllable toward sounding critical rather than confused), with "Sumimasen-ga..." as a less
common fallback — a directly worded repeat-request is reserved for informal speech among close
friends. Vol.4 p.110-111.

### dakara's irritation-prefacing use beyond literal "therefore"
Dakara's literal causal sense ("so/therefore") coexists with a distinct, very common discourse use:
prefacing a restatement of something the speaker feels they've already explained, which carries an
implicit note of irritation at having to repeat it. This use doesn't require an explicit preceding
reason-clause in the conversation — it can point back to context that was never verbally stated at
all (an established shared preference, an earlier unstated agreement). The polite-register counterpart
desukara carries the same restating function without the irritated edge, useful e.g. for a salesperson
reassuring a hesitant customer. Vol.4 p.112-113.

### Benefactive-verb pairs encode rhetorical emphasis, not different facts
...ga...te-kudasaimashita and ...ni...te-itadakimashita both correctly describe the identical
external event — someone doing something kind for the speaker — and are equally polite and equally
gratitude-expressing. They differ only in rhetorical focus: the ga-form foregrounds the benefactor's
generosity as the sentence's point, while the ni-form foregrounds the speaker's own experience of
having been given a favor. Less formal kuremashita/moraimashita follow the identical ga/ni-governed
emphasis split. The choice between the two is a matter of which aspect of the same fact the speaker
wants to highlight, not a difference in literal meaning. Vol.4 p.114-115.

### mo on quantities: evaluative polarity supplied by the speaker, not the particle
Mo attached to a quantity expression does not fix whether that quantity is being framed as large or
small — "as much as X" and "merely/only X" are both available readings of the identical phrase, with
the actual direction supplied entirely by the speaker's own attitude and the surrounding context. The
same numerical figure can carry opposite evaluative framings from two different speakers in the same
conversation without any grammatical difference between their sentences. Certain following
constructions do skew the likely reading — mo followed by a conditional ...eba tends toward "only,"
while mo followed by concessive ...noni tends toward "as much as/fully" — but these are tendencies
tied to the construction, not a fixed meaning carried by mo itself. Vol.4 p.116-117.

### Indirect disapproval via mo + trailing kedo/ga, with hesitant delivery as a functional requirement
When flatly rejecting something shown or proposed would be socially inappropriate, "kore-mo
kekkoo-desu-ga/-kedo..." signals dissatisfaction indirectly, without ever naming what's actually
wrong with the item or plan. Mo contributes an implicit "as well as [something else, which I'd
prefer]," and the trailing conjunction is left deliberately unresolved. The hesitant, trailing tone
of delivery is not optional politeness dressing — it is functionally part of the strategy, since the
whole point is to invite the other party to voluntarily offer an alternative rather than forcing the
speaker to specify a complaint outright. Vol.4 p.118-119.

### yoroshiku's content-free favor invocation, calibrated by relationship
Yoroshiku (short for yoroshiku onegai-shimasu) is grammatically a favor-request, but it deliberately
leaves the actual favor unstated — what, if anything, the listener is actually expected to do must be
inferred from the relationship and situational context alone. At one extreme (a casual acquaintance's
phone call), it carries essentially no concrete obligation and functions closer to a polite sign-off.
At the other extreme (between close friends or family), the same bare word can imply a real,
substantial expectation — meeting someone at the station, hosting them for several days — recoverable
only from context, never from the word's own content. Vol.4 p.120-121.

### Long nee vs. short ne: confirmation-seeking vs. affect-expression, plus standalone use
The short particle ne functions to solicit the listener's agreement or confirm mutual understanding
of what was just said. The long nee (falling tone) instead expresses the speaker's own emotional
stance toward something assumed to be a shared, relatable topic — and, distinctly from both, can
stand entirely alone as either a one-word expression of agreement/sympathy (an implied abbreviation
of a fuller phrase like "Honto-ni soo-desu-nee") or a stand-alone attention-getting call directed at
someone. The same falling-tone nee can read as irritated or imploring based purely on vocal delivery,
with the words themselves unchanged. Vol.4 p.122-123.

### Phonetic precision in counter phrases: near-homophone traps
Numeral-plus-counter phrases are used adverbially in natural speech, with their particle (o/ga)
conventionally dropped — but dropping the particle raises the stakes on vowel precision, since
near-homophones in this zone diverge sharply in meaning. The source's central example: an
over-opened o-sound turns "ippiki-o koroshita" ("I killed one") into something that sounds like
"ippiki okorashita" ("I angered one"), unintentionally anthropomorphizing the subject. A parallel
near-homophone trap exists between kowai (scary) and kawai(i) (cute), and between okoshita (aroused/
woke it) and okashita (violated it). Separately, retaining the full particle on a counter phrase
(ippiki-o rather than bare ippiki) is grammatical but sounds unnaturally bookish in conversation.
Vol.4 p.124-125.

### Passive-voice self-report as an affect marker: homerarechatta
Choosing to report being praised in the passive voice (homerarechatta, contracted from
homerarete-shimatta) rather than the benefactive active construction (sensee-ga homete-kureta, "the
teacher kindly praised me") is a meaningful choice, not a stylistic free variant — Japanese passive
voice generally skews toward marking the speaker as adversely or at least ambivalently affected by an
action (parallel examples: ame-ni furareta, "I was caught in the rain"; ashi-o fumareta, "someone
stepped on my foot"). Using it to report praise signals the speaker's own mixed feelings — surprise,
discomfort, or reluctance to live up to newly raised expectations — rather than straightforward
gratitude. The -chau/-te-shimau contraction independently marks either simple completion or a tinge
of regret, compounding the effect. A flat active "sensee-ga hometa" is actually ungrammatical/wrong
here, since it would imply the teacher praised someone other than the speaker. Vol.4 p.126-127.

### naraimasen: a false friend for "I don't/can't learn"
Narau names the process of studying or making an effort toward a skill, not the successful
acquisition of that skill — so negating it (naraimasen) only ever states that the speaker isn't
engaged in the study activity, which is misleading if said by someone who is actually studying hard
but simply progressing slowly. English "learn" conflates the process and the outcome in a way
Japanese keeps apart; expressing "I'm not making progress despite studying" requires a different verb
entirely (joozu-ni naru, oboeru, mi-ni tsuku, mono-ni naru), and the two concepts can be explicitly
contrasted in the same sentence ("Naratte-imasu-ga oboeraremasen"). Vol.4 p.128-129.

### Trait-referencing formulas that presuppose shared knowledge
"Ano-hito-no koto-da-kara" explains or predicts someone's behavior by implicit reference to a
personal trait that is never actually named in the sentence — its felicity depends entirely on the
listener already sharing the relevant background knowledge about the person in question. Used on
someone who lacks that context, it either produces confusion or reveals that the speaker was really
musing aloud rather than communicating. Critically, word order doesn't fix whether the implied
prediction is positive or negative — that's supplied entirely by what the shared trait actually is,
not by the phrase's position in the sentence. The related kono-goro-no koto-da-kara extends the same
presuppositional logic to broader, era-wide shared assumptions (most often about price levels, but
generalizable to other common knowledge about "how things are these days"). Vol.4 p.130-131.

### Non-adversative ga/kedo as a formal-speech introductory-remark closer
In formal speech, a sentence-final ga or kedo frequently does not mark genuine logical contrast
between its two clauses — its real function is to formally close a conventional introductory remark
(praising the weather, the venue, or the turnout) before pivoting into the speech's actual content.
Both halves of the convention are mandatory in formal register: dropping the ga/kedo closer after an
introductory remark sounds unnaturally blunt, while skipping the introductory remark and opening
directly sounds abrupt or underdressed for the occasion. The identical non-adversative-closing
pattern extends beyond speeches to ordinary polite invitations ("Ocha-ga hairimashita-kedo...") and
self-introductions ("Yamada-wa watashi-desu-ga..."), in each case implicitly inviting the listener's
next move rather than contradicting anything just said. Vol.4 p.132-133.

### maa as a hedging particle, distinct from the homophonous surprise-interjection
This maa signals the speaker's deliberate reluctance to sound too certain or assertive — about a
figure, a prediction, or an opinion — arising from some mix of genuine uncertainty, social reserve,
or consideration that the listener might hold a different view. The source explicitly flags this as
unrelated to the homophonous exclamatory maa used (especially by women) to express surprise ("Maa,
suteki!," "How wonderful!") — the two are different words that happen to sound identical, not one
word with two senses. Hedging-maa is also distinguished from eeto, which marks word-searching or
difficulty recalling something rather than hesitancy about committing to a stated opinion. Vol.4
p.134-135.

### Past-tense-restricted regret construction, with a false-parallel present-tense form
"...n-ja nakatta" (negative) and "...n-datta" (affirmative) express retrospective regret about a past
action or omission, and this construction only exists in the past tense — there is no present-tense
version carrying the same self-directed-regret meaning. The superficially parallel present-tense
forms ("...n-ja nai," "...n-da") are a different speech act entirely: admonishing someone else not to
do something, addressed outward rather than reflecting inward. The source explicitly flags the
resulting minimal pair as a real confusion risk for learners: "Kekkon-nanka suru-n-ja nakatta" (past,
"I shouldn't have gotten married") versus "Kekkon-nanka suru-n-ja nai" (present, "you shouldn't get
married") look almost identical but diverge sharply in both grammatical tense and who the statement
is actually about. Vol.4 p.136-137.

### tasukeru vs. tetsudau: non-interchangeable "help" verbs with a high-stakes failure mode
Tasukeru is reserved for rescuing someone from serious danger or crisis; tetsudau is the ordinary verb
for routine task-assistance. These domains don't overlap, and using the wrong one is not merely
stylistically clumsy — the source's own illustrative extreme makes the stakes vivid: offering
"Tetsudaimashoo" to someone who has decided to end their life would horrifyingly sound like
volunteering to assist the act itself, precisely because tetsudau denotes ordinary helpful
participation in whatever action is already underway, with no built-in sense that the action is a
rescue. Vol.4 p.138-139.

### Consonant gemination as a load-bearing grammatical distinction: -tte vs. -te
In casual contracted speech, a single geminated consonant (small っ) is the entire surface difference
between two unrelated grammatical derivations — -tte from -te itte (the te-form of iku, "go") versus
-te from -te ite (the te-form of iru, "be/stay") — and this is a genuine timing distinction (a held
stop consonant with real duration), not a cosmetic romanization quirk, confirmed by the source's own
anecdote of a real comprehension breakdown caused by mishearing one for the other. The same
precision-dependent minimal-pair problem recurs throughout the language (kitte vs. kite, shite vs.
shitte, nete vs. nette) and is compounded by a separate, independent colloquial process that drops
the "i" of -imasu/-imasen/-inai/-iraremasen whenever context makes the meaning recoverable anyway.
Vol.4 p.140-141.

### sono-kurai/gurai: approximation marker vs. dismissive-minimization reply
Attached to a quantity, kurai/gurai is a straightforward approximator ("about/approximately"). Used
as a free-standing, truncated reply on its own, the same word shifts entirely to signal the speaker's
deliberately minimizing/dismissive judgment of whatever is being discussed — implicitly pushing back
against another speaker's more impressed evaluation of the same thing, with the natural completion
("I can do it easily") left conventionally unstated. The two uses, approximation and dismissal, share
a word but are pragmatically unrelated functions distinguished entirely by context and sentence
completeness. Vol.4 p.142-143.

### hazu's directional restriction: speaker's expectation about others only
Hazu grammatically encodes the speaker's own expectation projected outward onto a third party, an
event, or an inferred state of affairs — it structurally cannot mark the listener's expectation
directed back at the speaker. This creates a real translation gap: English "Am I expected to...?" has
no hazu-based Japanese equivalent, because that meaning would require hazu to point in the wrong
direction. Asking what's expected of oneself instead requires an entirely different construction
("...mashoo-ka," "...koto-ni natte-imasu-ka"), not merely a different conjugation of hazu. Vol.4
p.144-145.

### Appended itte in hosting invitations: presupposing departure, not urging it
When a host appends itte (from iku's te-form) to an invitation or offer made to a visitor, the
resulting meaning is "...before you leave," not a literal, impatience-tinged "...and then go." This
usage is strictly confined to host-toward-visitor speech and is never used toward family members or
housemates, since they aren't conceptually "visitors" who will eventually depart — which is itself
evidence that the construction encodes a departure-presupposition, not any kind of urging-out. A
distinct, closer-friend register substitutes an explicit stay-longer invitation instead of the itte
construction altogether, and a related pattern extends specifically to short-term foreign visitors,
framed around making the most of their limited time rather than politeness toward an eventual
departure as such. Vol.4 p.146-147.

## Guide for Avoidance of Common Mistakes (2) (Vol.4 pp.149-157)

This is the Vol.4 continuation of the structured grammar-mistake guide that appeared as an appendix
to Vol.3 (see `051_nihongo_notes_03_04.md`'s own Guide section, items 1-25) — the header explicitly
labels it "(2)" and numbering continues from 26 through 50. Same organization: correct-model-sentence
followed by common-error explanation, cross-referencing *Nihongo Notes 1-4* and *An Introduction to
Modern Japanese* (abbreviated NN-1 through NN-4 and IMJ in the source's own citations). Paraphrased
below, one subsection per numbered item; illustrative sentences kept short per copyright discipline.

26. **Indicating ability or skill.** Use -ga dekimasu (Nihongo-ga dekimasu, "I can speak Japanese"),
    not -o dekimasu; -o hanasu/kaku/yomu koto-ga dekimasu is an alternative fuller construction.
    Likewise Piano-ga joozu-desu ("she's good at piano"), not piano-de joozu-desu.
27. **Indicating place of work.** Booeki-gaisha-ni tsutomete-imasu ("I work for a trading company"),
    not -de tsutomete-imasu; -de hataraite-imasu is an acceptable alternative verb.
28. **Indicating starting point.** Eki-o dete, kita-e ikimasu ("I go north after coming out of the
    station") uses -o with deru; eki-ni deru is wrong here and actually means "to come out at/reach
    the station" (as in following a street until you arrive there), a different sense entirely.
29. **Indicating the space an action passes through.** Verbs of traversal (iku, aruku, tooru,
    hashiru, tobu — go, walk, pass through, run, fly) take -o for the space traversed (Kono michi-o
    massugu itte-kudasai, "please go straight along this road"), not -de.
30. **Being absent.** Byooki-de gakkoo-o yasumimashita ("I was absent from school because of
    illness") uses -de for cause and -o for the thing missed; "gakkoo-kara yasumimashita" is wrong.
31. **Referring to an already-discussed topic.** Once a referent has been introduced, ga is wrong for
    referring back to it — use wa instead (Sono-hito-wa ima doko-ni imasu-ka, not sono-hito-ga); the
    topic noun itself can often be dropped entirely once established.
32. **Hearing something (unintentional) vs. listening (intentional).** Taiko-no oto-ga
    kikoemasu-ne ("we hear/can hear a drum beating") uses kikoeru for unintentional/ambient hearing;
    kiku is reserved for intentional listening (Yoku kiite-kudasai, "please listen carefully") and is
    wrong for describing ambient sound just reaching the ears.
33. **Seeing something (unintentional) vs. intentionally viewing.** Fujisan-ga yoku miemashita ("we
    could see Mt. Fuji clearly") uses mieru for unintentional/passive seeing; miru/mimashita implies
    intentionally looking, which sounds odd applied to a distant mountain one merely happened to see.
34. **Opening/closing a door, window, etc.** To-o akete-kudasai ("please open the door") uses the
    transitive akeru; the intransitive aku is wrong when someone performs the opening. The same
    transitive/intransitive split recurs across other pairs: tsukeru/tsuku (turn on/turns on),
    kesu/kieru (turn off/goes off), shimeru/shimaru (close/closes).
35. **One's disease being cured.** Byooki-ga naorimashita ("I recovered from my illness") is the
    natural intransitive framing; naoshimashita (transitive) is unusual here — naosu is reserved for
    a doctor curing someone else's disease, or for repairing an object.
36. **Completed action before a subsequent action.** The -ta (past/perfective) form marks that an
    action is already complete before the next clause: Uchi-ni kaetta toki, kimono-ni kikaemasu ("I
    change into kimono once/after I get home"); the plain non-past kaeru toki would wrongly imply the
    kimono-changing happens while still on the way home, before arrival.
37. **Forms preceding to omoimasu.** An i-adjective attaches directly to -to omoimasu (Atarashii-to
    omoimasu, "I think it's new"), not with an inserted -da- (atarashii-da-to is wrong); but soo
    ("so") needs -da- inserted (Soo-da-to omoimasu is correct, bare soo-to omoimasu is wrong) — the
    two patterns look similar but take opposite forms.
38. **Immediate future.** The plain non-past form alone can mark near-future intention (Kore-kara
    dekakemasu, "I'm going to leave now"); adding -te-imasu (dekakete-imasu) is wrong here, since
    that marks an already-ongoing state, not an imminent action.
39. **Asking for help.** For an action, use -te-moraitai/-te-hoshii-to omoimasu (Tetsudatte-moraitai-
    to omoimasu, "I'd like him to help me"); the related ...-ga hoshii/moraitai pattern (without -te)
    is reserved for wanting a thing, not an action — "tetsudai-ga hoshii" is wrong for wanting help.
40. **Indicating a resulting state of being.** The -te aru construction (transitive verb's -te form
    + aru) marks a state resulting from someone's prior action: Omoshiroi koto-ga kaite-arimasu
    ("something interesting is written [there]"); the plain past kakimashita describes the writing
    event itself, not the resulting state of the text now being there.
41. **Indicating intention vs. expectation.** Sugu yameru tsumori-desu ("I intend to stop soon") uses
    tsumori for the speaker's own stated intention; hazu is wrong here, since hazu (cf. Vol.4
    pp.144-145 above) marks the speaker's expectation about someone/something else, never the
    speaker's own intention.
42. **Describing a child's age vs. a person's youthfulness.** Uchi-no kodomo-wa mada chiisai-desu
    ("our child is still very young") uses chiisai for a child's young age; wakai is reserved for
    someone already in adolescence or older, and sounds wrong applied to a small child.
43. **Hot water vs. a hot/warm substance.** Oyu is the dedicated noun for "hot water" and takes no
    separate heat-adjective (Oyu-o kudasai, "please give me some hot water"); atsui mizu is wrong
    because mizu ("water") is specifically understood to mean cool/cold water, making "atsui mizu"
    internally contradictory.
44. **Cold water.** Tsumetai mizu-o kudasai ("please give me some cold water") uses tsumetai for a
    tangible cold object/substance; samui is wrong here, since samui is reserved for ambient
    temperature (weather, air), not something one can touch or drink.
45. **Taking a bath.** Ofuro-ni hairimasu ("I'm going to take a bath") is the fixed collocation
    (lit. "enter the bath"); ofuro-o torimasu is wrong.
46. **Mono vs. koto when referring to something abstract.** Heewa-to yuu mono-wa ii mono-desu
    ("peace is a fine thing") uses mono for an abstract concept named as a thing; koto is used
    instead when paired with a verb describing an action/fact about that concept (Heewa-o mamoru-to
    yuu koto-wa ii koto-desu, "maintaining peace is a good thing") — mono and koto are not freely
    interchangeable substitutes for "thing."
47. **Indicating change in a state of being.** Adjective/state changes use -ni narimasu for
    i-adjectives via their adverbial -ku form (Dandan samuku narimasu, "it will gradually get
    colder"; not samuku-ni narimasu) and na-adjectives via -ni directly (Heya-ga kiree-ni narimashita,
    "the room has become clean"; not kireeku narimashita); potential-verb change uses -yoo-ni
    narimasu (Dandan hanaseru yoo-ni narimasu, "you'll gradually become able to speak"; not
    hanaseru-ni narimasu). For a negated change, either kaeru yoo-ni narimasen (stressing the
    non-change itself) or kaenaku narimashita (stressing the resulting negative state) is usable
    depending on which aspect the speaker wants to emphasize.
48. **Sore-de vs. sore-dewa.** Sore-de links a stated problem to its resulting action (Okane-ga
    tarinaku narimashita. Sore-de Yamada-san-ni karimashita — "I ran short of money, so I borrowed
    some from Mr. Yamada"); sore-dewa is wrong in this causal-result sense, since it specifically
    means "if that is the case / in that case" (conditional-pivot sense), appropriate for a different
    kind of transition (e.g. "It's already six. — In that case, shall we start soon?").
49. **Leaving unsaid what's understood from context.** A reply naturally drops already-established
    information rather than repeating it: to "Ashita Kyooto-e ikimasu-ka" ("are you going to Kyoto
    tomorrow?"), the natural reply is bare "Ee, ikimasu," not "Ee, ashita Kyooto-e ikimasu" — repeating
    ashita or Kyooto in the answer is awkward unless the speaker specifically wants to emphasize one of
    those two words. The same principle applies to a repeated price figure in a follow-up answer,
    which is awkward unless emphasis/contrast is specifically intended.
50. **Leaving unsaid what's understood from the physical situation.** When an action is visually
    obvious from context, the verb is conventionally dropped: offering tea uses bare "Doozo" or
    "Ocha-o doozo," not the fuller "Doozo ocha-o nonde-kudasai"; offering a seat uses bare "Doozo" or
    "Doozo okake-kudasai," not "Doozo isu-ni okake-kudasai" — spelling out the obvious verb/object
    sounds unnatural, though naming a specific item (Doozo koko-e, "please, over here") is fine when
    the speaker wants to draw attention to that particular one among options.

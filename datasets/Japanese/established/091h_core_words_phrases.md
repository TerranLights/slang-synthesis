# Japanese — Established Vocabulary: Japanese Core Words and Phrases, Part 1 (こそあど Words)

**Source:** Kazuko Shoji, *Japanese Core Words and Phrases: Things You Can't Find in a Dictionary*
(Kodansha International, 1st ed., 1999; previously published as part of the Power Japanese series) —
Part 1, "こそあど WORDS," pp. 14–59 (あ/こ/そ/ど/な word groups).

**Coverage note:** This source has no text layer (`pdftotext` returns nothing on content pages) and
was vision-read from rendered page images. **Gotcha confirmed (a new variant of the known
two-pages-per-image pattern):** `pdfinfo` reports this PDF as single portrait letter-size pages
rotated 90°, which looks like "one physical page per PDF page" — but after rotation each PDF page
actually renders as a full landscape two-printed-page spread (verified: PDF page 6 renders printed
pages 12 and 13 side by side). Mapping confirmed against the book's own printed page numbers:
`printed_page_left = 2 × pdf_page`, `printed_page_right = 2 × pdf_page + 1`. No handwritten
marginalia was found anywhere in this section — all content inspected is clean typeset print.

This book is explicitly framed by its own title/preface as covering idiomatic usage and nuance
distinctions a standard dictionary does not capture (demonstrative "ko-so-a-do" words whose meaning
shifts radically with context, discourse connectives, and near-synonym pairs) — exactly the
highest-value content this extraction wave is prioritizing. Part 1 is organized into five
subsections by initial syllable (あ/こ/そ/ど/な); every distinct headword/phrase group is captured
below (53 entries, several bundling 2–4 near-synonymous variant forms the source itself presents
together). Entries are indexed as the source heading them, with slash-separated variants kept
together in one row when the source explicitly treats them as a set with shared core meaning but
discusses their contrastive nuance in the same discussion (the contrast is paraphrased in Notes).
Dialogue example sentences are not reproduced — only the author's own short glossing phrases and,
where necessary for precision, a handful of isolated short quotes.

Part 2 of this book ("Idiomatic Expressions," pp. 60–125, covering several hundred further
expressions) is substantially larger and is being extracted as sibling files (091i, 091j, 091k) by
parallel extraction passes, per the coverage rule's allowance to split a large source into multiple
numbered files. (Note: this file was originally written as `092_core_words_phrases.md` but renamed
to `091h` to resolve a numbering collision with a different book — `092_expression_dictionaries.md`
— independently assigned that number by a concurrent extraction wave running against this same
checklist at the same time.)

## Grammar points

### The four-way こそあど demonstrative system and its "distance" logic

The source frames こ/そ/あ/ど words as a four-category system (near speaker; near listener;
distant from both; indefinite/interrogative) in which each initial syllable combines with a fixed
set of six suffixes marking reference type (thing/person/event, place, direction/alternative,
kind, manner). Critically, the author states the book's actual focus is not this literal
spatial-distance system (covered already in a prior volume, *Basic Connections*) but the
**psychological-distance and idiomatic extensions** of こそあど words — cases where the surface
"distance" meaning does not transparently predict actual usage, which is why entries like これでも,
あんな, and そんな carry emotional/attitudinal connotations (protest, scolding, criticism) that a
literal demonstrative-pronoun gloss would miss entirely (pp. 11–13, 30).

### Minimal-pair nuance contrasts are the book's organizing method

Nearly every entry above is presented by the source as a contrastive pair or triple of
near-synonyms (このごろ/このころ; この分だと/この分なら/この分では; これまで/これっきり;
なにからなにまで/なにもかも; どうかすると/ややもすると), with the explanatory prose devoted
almost entirely to pinning down the precise difference, since a bilingual dictionary would gloss
all members of a pair identically. This is the book's central methodological contribution and the
reason it was prioritized for full-depth extraction in this wave.

### Register and gender notes explicitly marked by the source

The author explicitly flags two entries as skewing by speaker gender/register rather than being
fully neutral: あんな and そんな(あ) as "probably more commonly used by women" in their
soft-protest sense (pp. 17, 37), and あいつ/うちの人/うちの奴 as varying by both gender and
formality — あいつ as casual-male speech, うちの人 as a (more neutral) term wives use for
husbands, and うちの奴 as the coarser term husbands use for wives (p. 17).

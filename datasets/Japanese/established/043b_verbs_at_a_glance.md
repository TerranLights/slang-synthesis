## Grammar points

### Request-politeness ladder: four graded patterns (pp. 27–29)

Beyond the simpler request forms in 035, this book tabulates four request patterns in explicit
politeness/pragmatic contrast: (1) *-te + itadakemasen ka* (polite, the most common all-purpose
polite request), (2) *-te + kudasaimasen ka* (polite, roughly equivalent), (3) *-nai + de
itadakemasen ka* (negative-form request, has a "softening effect"), (4) *-nai + de kudasaimasen ka*
(negative-form request, "slightly more polite" than (3) per the book's own note, p. 30). A further,
narrower register pair is given specifically for **placing orders/requesting delivery or repair** —
*-te + hoshii n' desu (ga)* ("I'd like to have X done") and *-te + moraitai n' desu (ga)* ("I'd like
to have X done for me," built on the -tai desiderative form of morau) — explicitly framed as a
commercial-transaction speech act distinct from ordinary favor-asking.

### Command/prohibition register ladder: five tiers down to a literary prohibitive (pp. 29–35)

Commands are graded **polite** (-te + kudasaimasen ka) > **formal** (-te + kudasai, also the form
teachers use to instruct students) > **informal** (several sub-variants: bare -te; -masu-stem +
nasai; -te + kure; -masu-stem + na for negative) > **blunt/brusque** (final -u → -e, final -ru →
-ro; kuru → koi; suru → shiro/seyo — explicitly flagged as "characteristic of men's speech," and
noted to sometimes appear rhetorically, e.g. in addressing the weather: "Ame yo, fure" "Fall, O
rain!"). Prohibitions are graded in parallel: **formal** (-te + wa dame desu, or -te + wa ikemasen,
built on the negative potential of iku) > **informal** (-te + wa dame da / -te + wa ikenai) >
**literary** (plain form + bekarazu べからず, explicitly glossed by the book as more formal/literary
than the -te wa ikenai pattern and typically seen in posted notices; plain form + na な as a blunt
negative-imperative alternative). The bekarazu literary prohibitive is not attested in 028 or 035 at
all.

### Transitive/intransitive pairs: nine named morphological derivation patterns (pp. 74–75)

The book tabulates nine distinct sound-change patterns by which Japanese derives transitive/
intransitive verb pairs from a shared root, each illustrated with 2–4 verb pairs (full pair list
captured in the vocabulary table above): **-u → -eru**, **-u → -asu**, **-u → -wasu**, **-iru →
-osu**, **-eru → -asu**, **-eru → -yasu**, **-reru → -rasu**, **-aru → -eru**, **-waru → -eru**,
**-eru → -u**. The book explicitly notes there is no single hard-and-fast rule predicting which
pattern a given verb pair will follow — "most verbs ending in -su are transitive, but otherwise
there are no hard-and-fast rules" — and that the basic sentence frame for an intransitive verb is
simply subject + ga + verb (no object). This systematic nine-pattern table is new content relative
to both 028 (which mentions the aku/akeru pair in passing, without a systematic pattern table) and
035 (no equivalent table found).

### "About to do something": three distinct patterns (pp. 90–92)

Three constructions express imminent/incipient action with different pragmatic shadings: **(1) to
suru** (informal volitional + to suru — "the action is halted just before it can begin," implies the
subject's intention, e.g. "ikō to suru" "to be about to go"); **(2) tokoro** (plain form + tokoro
da/desu — "on the verge of doing," explicitly contrasted with to suru as not implying intention);
**(3) bakari** (plain form + bakari — used after the plain form similarly to mean "just about to" /
"always just doing"). None of these three is covered in 028 or 035.

### Time relationships: before/after/continuing patterns (pp. 88–90)

Condensed forms not elsewhere tabulated together: **before** (plain form + mae ni, "before doing");
**after** — three variants with different closeness-of-sequence implications: *-te + kara* (one
action follows closely on another), *-ta + ato (de)* (the one action does not necessarily follow
closely — a looser "after"), *-ta + totan* ("immediately after," often with sudden/unexpected
actions, e.g. "the instant the airplane touched ground, it caught fire"). A near-synonym pair for
"immediately after" is also given in Chapter 4 (*-ta ka to omou to* / *-ta totan*, see `043d`),
which the book treats as largely interchangeable with the Chapter 2 -ta totan pattern.

### Expressing regret: two patterns with different targets (pp. 96–98)

**-te + shimau** expresses regret over something one has *done* (the book explicitly distinguishes
this from -te shimau's separate "completed action" sense discussed elsewhere in the book, p. 94,
flagging that the same surface form carries two distinct pragmatic functions depending on context).
**-nakereba yokatta** (/-nai/ stem + -nakereba yokatta, literally "if [I] hadn't done X, it would
have been good") expresses more explicit regret, roughly "I wish I hadn't..." / "it would have been
better if I hadn't...."

### A 6-way evidentiality system: how a speaker knows something is true (pp. 98–101)

The single highest-value finding in this chapter: the book presents four markers of **conjecture**
in a single explicit table graded by "the source and certainty of the information" (p. 98), then
immediately follows with a **fifth and sixth** marker for reporting something heard or sensed —
together forming a coherent 6-way evidentiality system not presented as a unified system in either
028 or 035:

1. **yō da** (plain form + yō da/yō desu) — based on the speaker's own direct experience or
   knowledge ("furu yō da," "it seems likely to rain," based on e.g. looking at the sky).
2. **rashii** (plain form + rashii) — based on hearsay or something read/heard from another source
   ("furu rashii," "apparently it's going to rain," e.g. based on a weather report).
3. **darō/deshō** (plain form + darō informal / deshō formal, the volitional form of the copula da)
   — the speaker's own supposition or guess, often followed by "to omou" ("I think") for extra
   hedging.
4. **hazu da** (plain form + hazu da/hazu desu) — reasoning from a premise to a conclusion the
   speaker is reasonably convinced must be true ("kuru hazu desu," "he should be coming," reasoning
   from a known prior commitment).
5. **sō da, hearsay** (plain form + sō da/sō desu) — explicitly reporting something heard from a
   specific source, frequently combined with "X no hanashi da to" / "X ni yoru to" ("according to
   X...") — the book notes that when such a source phrase is present, the sō da itself is often left
   untranslated in English because "according to..." already carries the same meaning.
6. **sō da, sensory/inferential** (/-masu/ stem + sō da/sō desu) — reporting something inferred from
   direct sensory impression in the moment ("furi/̶m̶a̶s̶u̶/sō da," "it looks like it's about to
   rain," e.g. based on looking at darkening clouds right now) — grammatically distinguished from
   hearsay sō da *solely* by its attachment site (/-masu/ stem vs. plain form), making it one of the
   few genuine minimal pairs in the whole evidentiality system. The book additionally notes that any
   of the first four patterns (1–4) can be shifted to "conjecture about the past" by substituting the
   informal past (-ta) form for the plain form wherever it appears.

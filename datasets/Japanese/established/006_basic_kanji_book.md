## Grammar points

### Kun-yomi vs. on-yomi (Lesson 1, Unit 1-2)

Each kanji generally has at least two readings: the "KUN YOMI" (native Japanese reading) and the "ON
YOMI" (reading borrowed from Chinese, conventionally written in katakana in a kanji dictionary — the
book notes students should therefore learn katakana early). Rule of thumb given by the text (p.2): a
kanji used alone as a single-character word is usually read by its kun-yomi (which carries inherent
meaning), while kanji compounded together with other kanji are usually read by their on-yomi — though
the text explicitly cautions this is only a general tendency and the actual reading must be learned
per word. The character detail tables bear this out directly, e.g. 日 alone = ひ (kun) but 日本 = に・ほん
(on-yomi compound reading).

### Basic kanji stroke-order and stroke-type rules (Lesson 1, Unit 2-1 "Kanji Writings")

Three numbered rules given, illustrated against the Lesson 1 kanji set:
- **Rule 1**: keep every kanji the same overall size, fit to an implied square — illustrated by a
  "wrong vs. right" comparison of 日本語 written at inconsistent vs. consistent character size.
- **Rule 2**: follow basic stroke order — strokes go left-to-right and top-to-bottom; when multiple
  horizontal or vertical strokes stack, draw top-before-bottom and left-before-right; a surrounding
  square/box shape is drawn left-vertical, then top+right (as one bent stroke), then the bottom line
  last; when a box encloses an interior shape, the enclosing bottom line is drawn only after the
  interior content is complete (demonstrated via the stroke build-up of 日→白→甲→由→田); by contrast,
  when a single stroke passes *through* a box shape (as in 中), the piercing vertical stroke is drawn
  last, after the box is already closed.
- **Rule 3**: a stroke ends in one of three ways: Stop (木), Stretch (木, a different final stroke
  variant), or Hook (月) — illustrated directly on the character forms rather than described
  abstractly.

### Basic stroke types (Lesson 2, Unit 2-1 "Basic Strokes")

A follow-on grammar point classifying every stroke used across Lessons 1-2's kanji into seven basic
stroke shapes, each illustrated with 2-4 already-known kanji as examples: (1) a horizontal line (一,
土, 車), (2) a vertical line (川, 土, 山), (3) a slanting line (人, 木, 金), (4) a short hook (月, 水, 子),
(5) a long hook (先, 九, 心 — note 九 "nine" and 心 "heart/mind" appear here as forward-reference
examples, not yet formally introduced as vocabulary in Lessons 1-2), (6) a corner (口, 山, 女, 私 — 私
also a forward reference used as an example before its own formal introduction later in the same
lesson), and (7) a dot plus various short lines (火, 学, 金, 生). This is presented purely as a
stroke-form taxonomy for handwriting practice, not a phonological or morphological rule.

## Grammar points

### Lesson 32 (お祭り見物 — "Watching the Festival"), pp. 131–137
Goals: describe an experience felt as an inconvenience/imposition using the passive (受身) form.
1. **〜てある (resultant state from a deliberate prior action, descriptive)** — visually/descriptively frames an object's state as the result of someone's earlier intentional action, explicitly NOT implying active preparedness or intent on the describer's part (that "preparedness" sense of てある is deferred to L36). Builds on the L26 自動詞/他動詞 system: for verbs WITH an intransitive/transitive pair, the resultant-state meaning is carried by [intransitive verb]+ている (ドアが開いている), while [transitive verb]+ている instead describes the action in progress (ドアを開けている) — a direct cross-reference confirming the three-way ている/てある/transitive-ている system builds cumulatively across lessons. For verbs WITHOUT such a pair (置く, 貼る, 書く, etc.), both the in-progress and resultant-state meanings use 〜ている vs. 〜てある respectively (かばんを置いている vs. かばんが置いてある) — this lesson restricts active drilling to exactly three high-frequency verbs in this second group: 置いてある, 貼ってある, 書いてある. Particle note: the resultant-state subject takes が (かばんが置いてある) EXCEPT when 書いてある quotes specific written content, which instead takes と (「1回3錠」と書いてある).
2. **〜よう／〜みたいです (inferential judgment, sentence-final predicate-complete form)** — marks the speaker's own subjective reasoned judgment/inference drawn from an observed situation, contrasted explicitly and carefully against the directly-observed-impression そう (L23): そう (おいしそうです) describes a raw surface impression taken straight from what's visually in front of the speaker, while よう/みたい involves an inferential reasoning step from indirect evidence to a conclusion (observing a long line outside a restaurant → inferring this店はおいしいからだ → stating このレストランはおいしいようです). みたい is explicitly noted as purely a more colloquial-register synonym of よう, with no meaning difference between them.
3. **〜のに (counter-to-expectation, three-way taxonomy)** — (1) the speaker's own effort/expectation led to a personally disappointing result (一生懸命練習したのに、上手にならない); (2) the speaker made an effort for someone else's benefit, but the other person's reaction didn't match the speaker's expectation, typically phrased as a surprised/hurt question (せっかく作ったのに、どうして食べないの?) — this sub-pattern specifically drilled via the せっかく〜のに〜んですか frame; (3) a general expectation-vs-reality mismatch expressing surprise, which can carry either a negative outcome (始めてなのに、まだ下手ですね, implied) or — notably — a POSITIVE outcome as the surprising mismatch (始めてなのに、上手ですね, "surprisingly good for a first try") — explicitly flagged that のに is not inherently negative-valence; it marks mismatch/surprise generally, and a positive mismatch is equally grammatical.
4. **受身形 (passive voice) — 感情を伴う受身 (emotion-bearing/adversity passive)** — the book explicitly splits all passive-voice usage into two broad categories: 感情を伴う受身 (emotion-bearing, drilled here) and 感情を伴わない受身 (neutral/factual passive, e.g. インスタントラーメンは日本で初めて作られました, deferred to L33). Emotion-bearing passive is itself split into three structurally distinct sub-patterns: (1) **direct-affect passive** — the speaker is the direct target of someone else's action (私は監督に叱られました); (2) **possessor-affect passive** — the speaker's possession or body part is affected, and the construction obligatorily promotes the possessor (not the possession) to subject position — explicit error flag: ×私のカメラは弟にこわされました is wrong; the correct form keeps 私は as subject and marks the possession with を (私は弟にカメラをこわされました), i.e. Japanese adversity-passive does NOT allow the affected possession itself to be the grammatical subject; (3) **intransitive-verb adversity passive** — even a semantically neutral/non-malicious intransitive event (a friend simply coming to visit, 友達が来ました) can be passivized to express that the event inconvenienced the speaker, with no implication the agent intended harm (友達に来られて、勉強できませんでした — "[I was put out because] a friend came by, and I couldn't study"). Morphological ambiguity flag: Group 2 verbs' passive and potential forms are identical in shape (食べられました), requiring the teacher to drill disambiguation by context — worked minimal pair given (私は兄にごはんを食べられました, passive/adversity reading, vs. 今朝早く起きたのでゆっくり朝ごはんが食べられました, potential reading). Critical register/grammaticality constraint flagged: students must NOT passivize their own self-caused mishaps/clumsiness — a marked-wrong example (×私は財布を落とされました for "I [carelessly] dropped my wallet") shows that the adversity passive requires an external agent genuinely causing the event; a plain active/past-tense statement (財布を落としました) is required when the mishap is self-caused.
5. **〜たら (surprise-discovery sense, completing the deferred L31 note)** — marks that something unexpected was discovered/occurred at the moment a condition was met, in two illustrated sub-flavors: (a) an unexpected event occurring as an immediate result of the subject's own action (久しぶりに料理をしたら、指を切りました — "when I cooked for the first time in a while, [to my surprise] I cut my finger"), (b) an unexpected pre-existing state/fact being discovered upon arrival/action (デパートへ行ったら、休みでした — "when I went to the department store, [I discovered] it was closed"). Both carry a shared surprise/unexpectedness component distinguishing this sense from the plain sequential-realization sense taught at L31.

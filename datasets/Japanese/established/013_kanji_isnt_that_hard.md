**A note on the book's own metalanguage (applies to all Rules files, not repeated per-file):**
Each kanji entry's "よみかた/reading" box lists two readings, labeled ① and ② — the book explains (p.
18, in Rule 3's intro) that ① is **on-yomi** (音読み, the Chinese-derived reading, historically the
kanji's Chinese pronunciation as absorbed into Japanese) and ② is **kun-yomi** (訓読み, the native
Japanese reading assigned by meaning-translation when kanji were adopted to write pre-existing
Japanese words). This on/kun distinction is itself a grammar point of the writing system and is
recorded once here rather than repeated in every row's Notes.

The book's central mechanism — introduced explicitly in Rule 2 and used throughout — is that a
compound kanji is analyzed as a **stem** (ステム, the component carrying the core/basic meaning) plus
one or more **signs** (サイン, components that narrow the stem's meaning to a specific category or
field). E.g. 晴 = stem 青 ("very beautiful") + sign 日 ("sun") → "fine weather." This is recorded in
the Notes/morpheme-breakdown for each compound kanji below.

## Grammar points

### Rule 1 — Kanji express meaning; learn the basic units in kanji (漢字は意味を表します／漢字の単位を覚えましょう)

Roman letters encode pronunciation (sound); kanji encode meaning via their outline/shape — the
outline of a kanji derives from a picture of the thing it originally meant. Just as a romanized word
can be divided into phonetic units (e.g. *ch-i-ld*), a kanji can be divided into meaning-bearing
"units" (単位): component shapes that recur across many different kanji and carry a consistent
sub-meaning wherever they appear (e.g. 子 "child" recurs in 学 "to study"). Learning these recurring
units lets a reader infer an unfamiliar kanji's meaning from its parts, the way knowing "ch," "i,"
"ld" lets you recognize those sounds in other English words. (pp. 8–12)

### Rule 2 — The same outline indicates the same meaning; put two meanings together (同じ形は同じ意味です／二つの意味を合わせましょう)

Building on Rule 1: when a compound kanji is built from two meaning-units, the book analyzes it as a
**stem** (基本部分／ステム) carrying the kanji's basic/core meaning, plus a **sign** (付随部分／サイン)
that narrows that basic meaning to a specific category or semantic field. E.g. in 待 "to wait": the
stem 寺 means "not moving" (cf. 寺 "temple" in Rule 1, where monks sit still) and the sign 彳(an
abbreviated form of 行 "go") narrows this to "not moving" + "going" → "to wait." The chapter's worked
examples (青→晴, 包→抱, 反→坂, 召→招) each show a stem's meaning carried into several derived compounds
via a different sign. (p. 13–17)

### Rule 3 — Pictures turned into kanji; consider the outline of the original pictures (絵が漢字になりました／元の絵の形を考えましょう)

Kanji outlines are directly descended from pictures of the things they denote (鳥 "bird" from a
picture of a bird, etc.). This section also introduces the **two kanji reading types**: every kanji
has an **on-yomi** (音読み, Reading ①) — the kanji's original Chinese pronunciation, somewhat altered
after being absorbed into Japanese — and a **kun-yomi** (訓読み, Reading ②) — the pre-existing native
Japanese word that was assigned to the kanji by meaning (not sound) when Japanese scholars translated
Chinese texts. E.g. 鳥's on-yomi チョウ reflects its old Chinese pronunciation; its kun-yomi とり is the
native Japanese word for "bird," unrelated in sound to チョウ. (pp. 18–22)

### Rule 4 — Circles became squares; curved lines became straight lines (まるは四角になりました／曲線は直線になりました)

Kanji systematically regularized the organic curved lines of their source pictures: circles became
squares (日 "sun" from ⊙; 円 "circle" from ○; 車 "wheel" from a round wheel), and curved lines became
straight ones (耳's ear-folds, 虫's S-shaped tail, 弓's bow-curve all straightened). The book frames
this as a general stylization rule usable in reverse: to recover a kanji's original pictographic
sense, mentally turn its squares back into circles and its straight lines back into curves. Most
kanji are built from vertical and horizontal strokes; a small number of exceptions (人, 夕) retain
diagonal strokes from their source pictures. (pp. 23–27)

### Rule 5 — Make them into vertical and horizontal forms; fit the kanji into a square grid (縦と横の形にします／正方形の中に入れましょう)

Extending Rule 4: beyond circle→square and curve→straight-line changes, kanji forms are also
reoriented to fit a uniform square grid — a picture's original diagonal orientation is frequently
rotated to vertical (the crescent moon 月, the diagonal Chinese-portrait eye 目, thread wound on a
spool 糸, a horse's body 馬, an elephant's body 象 are all cited as pictures rotated from a diagonal
or horizontal original into the kanji's vertical form so it fits evenly alongside other kanji).
(pp. 28–32)

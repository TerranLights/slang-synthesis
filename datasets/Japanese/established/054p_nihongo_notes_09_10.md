## Grammar points

### Erai and rippa: two praise words with different domains and a superior-vs-equal restriction (p.94-95)
*Erai* praises someone's actions, behavior, or effort — commonly used by a superior praising
subordinates or a parent praising a child, but it cannot be used directly toward a superior. *Rippa*
praises a person's character/personality and extends to man-made objects (a building), but not to
natural phenomena like weather or a river, where ordinary descriptive words (*ii tenki*, *ookina
kawa*) are used instead.

### O...-desu-ka: a polite indirect question that's really a greeting of concern (p.96-97)
The honorific *o-* prefix plus *-desu-ka* (as in *Okaimono-desu-ka*, *Odekake-desu-ka*) forms an
indirect, refined way of asking whether someone is doing something — more polite than the more direct
*o...-ni narimasu-ka*/*o...nasaimasu-ka* alternatives built on *naru*/*nasaru*. Functionally, though,
these set expressions mostly express friendly concern or noticing toward an acquaintance (comparable
to "Off somewhere? Have a good time!") rather than genuinely requesting information — genuine
information-seeking uses of the same pattern typically add a further specific phrase (e.g. "about
what time").

### Three ways to mark the start of an action: hajimeru, dasu, te-kuru (p.98-99)
*-Hajimeru* states the start of an action as a plain, neutral fact. *-Dasu* adds a note of surprise
or other emotion to the start of the action. *...Te-kuru*, closer to *-dasu* than to *-hajimeru* in
carrying emotional coloring, specifically implies that the starting action has a direct effect on the
speaker themselves (e.g. rain starting to fall on them, meaning they'll need umbrellas) — it's also
used, in a related sense, for a gradual change occurring over time.

### Kekkoo's three senses, and its backhanded-compliment pragmatics (p.102-103)
*Kekkoo* has (1) an approving "good/fine" sense usable in polite speech, (2) a "not necessary" sense
used to politely decline an offer, and (3) an adverbial "unexpectedly/surprisingly" sense restricted
to familiar conversation. The adverbial sense, combined with a compliment (*kekkoo umai*, "surprisingly
good"), functions as a mildly reserved or backhanded compliment — implying the speaker hadn't expected
it to be that good — which is why the chapter's framing dialogue has the cake's maker not thanking the
speaker for it, in contrast to other coworkers' unreserved praise; this kind of teasing praise is
specifically associated with good-friend relationships.

### Dooshi vs. tsuzukeru: regretful vs. neutral "keep...ing" (p.100-101)
Both mean "keep...ing," but *...dooshi* usually implies a negative attitude — regret at having to
continue an action longer or more than wanted (standing the whole train ride, sleeping through a
meeting). *...tsuzukeru* is neutral and carries no such implication, as shown by a parallel example
where the speaker can keep working all day without getting tired — a positive capability, not a
grudging continuation.

### Kotowaru's two unrelated senses, and kotowatte-oku for disambiguation (p.104-105)
*Kotowaru* means both "to decline/refuse" and, separately, "to give advance notice/tell someone
beforehand" — two senses easily confused by a learner who only knows the "refuse" meaning. Combining
the advance-notice sense with *-te oku* ("do in advance, for later reference") as *kotowatte-oku*
removes the ambiguity, making clear that the speaker is informing the listener of something in
advance rather than declining an invitation.

## Grammar points

### The 11–99 number-building system

Numbers 11–19 are built compositionally as 十 (ten) + the relevant unit digit, e.g. 十一 = 10+1 = 11, 十二 = 10+2 = 12. Multiples of ten from 20–90 are built as digit + 十, read as digit × 10, e.g. 二十 = 2×10 = 20. Numbers 21–99 combine both: digit × 10, then + the unit digit, e.g. 二十一 = 2×10+1 = 21, 二十三 = 2×10+3 = 23. This is presented as a fully regular, logical system requiring no separate memorization once the 1–10 kanji and the pattern are known (pp. 32–33).

### Large numbers and the Japanese ten-thousand-based counting system

Unlike the Western system (which groups into thousands up to one million), Japanese counting introduces a distinct unit at **ten thousand** (万), rather than continuing to count in thousands. Larger amounts are built by multiplying a number by this unit: 十万 = 10 × 10,000 = 100,000; 百万 = 100 × 10,000 = 1,000,000. Multiples of 百 (hundred) and 千 (thousand) combine the same way: digit + 百/千 (e.g. 五千 = 5,000). The book gives combinations like 二千二百 (2,200) and 二百万 (2,000,000) as worked ordering examples (pp. 34–35).

### Currency and year notation

円 (yen) functions as a suffix directly after a number amount (e.g. 六円 = "six yen"), parallel to how English places a currency symbol or word next to a numeral. 年 (year) likewise follows the number in a year expression rather than preceding it, e.g. 千九百九十九年 for "1999" written fully in kanji numerals — though the book notes this fully-kanji form is rarely used in practice; far more common is a mixed form using arabic numerals with 年 still appended (1999年, or abbreviated 99年). Zero in such contexts is usually written as the arabic digit "0" even within an otherwise kanji-numeral date (p.36).

### The 年号 (nengō) era-naming calendar system

Japan uses an era-name calendar alongside the Western calendar: each era is defined by the reign of an Emperor. A year within an era is written as era-name + number + 年 (e.g. 平成十一年 = "Heisei 11th year" = 1999, since Heisei 1 = 1989, so 1999 is the 11th year of counting inclusively from 1989). Arabic numerals can substitute for the kanji number inside this pattern (平成11年). A special exception: an era's very first year is written with 元 ("beginning") rather than 一 ("one") — 平成元年 = Heisei's 1st year = 1989, not "平成一年." Converting between the preceding Shōwa era and Western years uses a simple ±25 offset (Shōwa year + 25 = Western year; Western year − 25 = Shōwa year), since the Shōwa era began in 1926 (pp. 36–37).

### Date and number orientation in vertical vs. horizontal text

Kanji numbers are most often used when Japanese text is written vertically (e.g. on Japanese business cards, which often carry vertical Japanese script on one side and horizontal romanized script on the other for non-Japanese readers). When numbers appear in horizontal text such as phone numbers, a horizontal line (~) rather than the kanji 一 ("one") is used to separate parts of the number, and the book explicitly warns not to confuse the two marks (p.38).

### Stroke-ending rules (three types, supplementing Units 1–2's stroke-order rules)

Beyond stroke *order*, three stroke-*ending* shapes affect how a kanji looks: (1) **STOP ending** — the pen stops abruptly and lifts straight off the page; (2) **GRADUAL STOP** — the pen is pulled gradually off the page in a sweeping motion (examples: 木, 八, 六); (3) **FLICK** — the stroke flicks upward at its end (examples: 九, 月, 水). The book notes Japanese schoolchildren learn these distinctions through calligraphy brush-work, but that attention to the three ending types improves pen/pencil writing too (pp. 40–41).

**Morpheme breakdown.** Japanese pronoun/verb forms here are not agglutinative-complex in the way
the spec's Finnish example is, but the plural-pronoun pattern is a real, regular compositional
pattern worth flagging: `{pronoun-stem} + ら/達 (plural suffix)`, e.g. 俺 *ore* ("I, vulgar") + ら
*-ra* (plural) → 俺ら *orera* ("we, vulgar"); 僕 *boku* + 達 *-tachi* → 僕達 *bokutachi*. The same
plural suffix attaches across the whole formality/register spectrum (私達/私ども/君達/お前ら/あんたら),
so register is carried entirely by the stem, not the plural morpheme.

## Grammar points

### Long vowels and double consonants (Lesson 2)
Long vowels are written with a trailing hiragana う after an お/う-ending sound (くうそ *kūso*
"empty" vs くそ *kuso* "shit" — the book uses this minimal pair deliberately to stress how much
weight the vowel length carries), or a dash in katakana (ニューヨーク *nyūyōku* "New York"). Doubled
consonants are marked with a small つ/ッ before the consonant to be doubled (しゅっぱつ *shuppatsu*
"departure"), restricted to k/s/t/ch/g/z/d/b/p-initial sounds.

### Personal pronouns and register (Lesson 7)
The book's central point: Japanese personal pronouns are not neutral — each one encodes the
speaker's sex, age, social position, and the formality of the relationship, and a large share of
them (お前, あんた, きさま, てめえ, わし, あっし, オイラ) carry explicit vulgar/dialectal/threatening/
archaic register markings the book itself assigns. It further notes that Japanese very often avoids
second/third-person pronouns altogether in favor of the addressee's name or title, even in direct
address — illustrated with 先生は頭がいいです *sensei wa atama ga ii desu* ("You are intelligent,"
literally "the teacher is intelligent") said to the teacher's own face. The book's practical advice
to learners: default to 私 *watashi* / あなた *anata* until fluent enough to judge register
correctly, since misusing a vulgar/masculine/archaic pronoun is a much easier mistake to make than to
fix.

### Telling time and irregular readings (Lesson 12)
Hour/minute counters have memorizable irregular readings rather than a fully regular pattern: 四時
is *yo-ji* (not *yon-ji*/*shi-ji*); the minute-counter 分 shifts from base *fun* to *pun* after 1, 3,
4, 6, 8, and 10 for phonetic reasons (*ippun, sanpun, yonpun, roppun, happun, juppun*/*jippun*).
"Half past" uses 半 *han*; "past"/"to" use すぎ *sugi* / まえ *mae* respectively after the
minute count.

### Present-negative and past-negative of -i adjectives (Lesson 13)
-i adjectives regularly form present-negative by replacing final -i with -ku nai (こわい → こわくない)
and past-negative with -ku nakatta (こわくなかった). いい "good" is the single flagged irregular
adjective: its stem is *yo-*, not *iku-*, across all four inflections (いい / よかった / よくない /
よくなかった). The lesson also uses a manga dialogue example to illustrate that Japanese frequently
avoids second-person pronouns even in direct-address questions (a girl asking a boy "Tadaomi, weren't
you afraid of me?" uses his name, not "you," producing a literal reading that sounds third-person
to an English speaker).

### Particles を *o* and へ *e* (Lesson 16)
を marks a direct object (the thing receiving a verb's action): これを貸してあげよう *kore o kashite
ageyō* ("I'll lend you this"). へ marks a destination/direction, mainly used with 行く *iku* ("go"),
来る *kuru* ("come"), and 帰る *kaeru* ("return"): もう水の中へ帰らないかもしれない *mō mizu no naka e
kaeranai kamo shirenai* ("I might never be able to return to the sea again"). Also notes the
sentence-final pattern かもしれない *kamo shirenai* ("might," "perhaps," expressing doubt).

### Adverbs: true adverbs vs. adjective-derived vs. gerund-derived (Lesson 22)
Three word-formation routes to adverbs: (1) true adverbs with no synchronic derivation (仮に "if
hypothetically," まったく "completely"); (2) the suffix 的に *-teki ni* attached to certain nouns; (3)
the gerund (-te form) of certain verbs used adverbially, e.g. はじめて *hajimete* ("for the first
time"), the gerund of 始める *hajimeru* ("to start"). The book calls adverbs "probably some of the
most difficult words to master in Japanese" because of the nuance they carry.

### Fixed/idiomatic daily-life expressions (Lesson 27)
A large, closed set of fixed social-formula expressions that "rarely have a direct translation" and
must be learned in context rather than derived compositionally — greetings, the house-entry/-leaving
formula set (7 expressions split into two subgroups per the book), and politeness formulas around
meals and apologies. The book explicitly separates these from the basic greetings already covered in
L.4, treating this lesson as the deeper, second pass.

### Commands/imperative (Lesson 30)
The book justifies teaching the imperative unusually early (lesson 30 of its own beginner sequence,
still far earlier than a "conventional" course would) on sociolinguistic grounds: real everyday
Japanese avoids bare commands as rude, preferring the request form (-te form + ください *kudasai*,
L.24), but manga/anime/video games/movies use bare commands constantly, and understanding that
register gap is explicitly framed as the whole point of this course. Imperative conjugation follows
the three standard verb-group classes already taught in L.19/20/24 (-masu stem, dictionary form,
group-specific imperative ending).

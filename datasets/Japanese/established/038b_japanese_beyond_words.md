## Grammar points

### Japanese pitch accent vs. English stress accent
Japanese syllables carry roughly even stress/duration; word distinctions are made through pitch
variation, not strong stress accent as in English — a persistent source of mispronunciation when
non-native speakers rely on romanized spelling instead of listening. p.52-54.

### Aspiration of plosives (p/t/k)
English aspirates initial p/t/k with a puff of air; French has none; Japanese falls between the two,
demonstrable with a tissue-paper-in-front-of-the-mouth test. p.53-54.

### The syllabic nasal n' (ん)
A distinct mora-timed nasal with no close English equivalent (closest approximation: the "ng" in
"sing," stopped short of full closure). Minimal pairs: kin'en/kinen, sen'en/senen, son'o/sono,
kanyuu/kan'yuu, kin'yuu/kinyuu. p.55-56.

### Word-initial ts, absent in English
English only has ts mid-word or finally, never word-initial, systematically causing mispronunciation
as plain s by English-speaking learners; demonstrated via nine ts/s minimal pairs. p.56-57.

### A single flap /r/ phoneme replaces the English l/r distinction
Rooted in early-childhood phoneme-discrimination limits, not an articulatory limitation; an adult
retraining method (exaggerate the contrast, then fade it) can work in as little as an hour. p.58-61.

### Geminate (doubled) consonants as a distinct phonemic category
Doubling a consonant (holding articulation an extra beat, or a brief tensed stop for plosives)
changes word meaning entirely. Five minimal pairs given: ona/onna, isho/issho, isoo/issoo, kata/katta,
iken/ikken. p.60-63.

### Loss of the /w/ phoneme before non-a vowels
Modern standard Japanese lost /w/ before vowels other than a; this causes systematic w-dropping in
English loanwords (hair liquid, bilingual, equal), and a mirrored tendency for Japanese speakers of
English to drop w from English words that need it (question, would). p.62-64.

### Vowel length (short vs. long) is fully phonemic
Native English speakers don't natively perceive vowel length as meaning-bearing, causing systematic
errors on pairs like gokan/gookan, i/ii, koko/kookoo, koomon/komon. Word-final long vowels are
pronounced shorter than non-final long vowels (Tokyo vs. Hiroo), an unmarked distinction on most
signage. Hiragana marks long e with an extra i (hence the "Heisei" mispronunciation); katakana marks
long vowels with a dash (nobashibou) rather than a repeated letter. p.64-67.

### Japanese /f/ is a bilabial fricative restricted to before /u/
Produced by blowing air through parted lips, not teeth-to-lip contact; pairs with /h/ for the other
four vowels (ha, hi, fu, he, ho), causing systematic hu-for-f substitution by Japanese speakers of
English. p.68-69.

### Epenthetic vowel insertion breaks up loanword consonant clusters
Japanese syllables can only end in a vowel, /n/, or (dialectally) /s/; borrowed clusters gain an
inserted vowel (usually u, sometimes i/o to avoid a disfavored f-like sound before u, as in Chuurihhi
for Zurich). Explains Tokyo-dialect vowel devoicing: kutsu→k'tsu, shichi→sh'chi. p.68-71.

### Lost /y/-glide before e (and formerly i)
Explains why "yen" keeps its historical y-spelling despite modern y-less pronunciation, and why many
Japanese speakers of English don't distinguish "year" from "ear." p.71.

### Pre-WWII vs. modern romanization (ti=chi, di=ji, tu=tsu vs. Hepburn)
The older system regularized verb-inflection patterns but caused real confusion internationally
(a ship painted "TITIBU" for Chichibu); several institutions (Meidiya/Nittsu/Shimadzu) still carry
old-system spellings used alongside modern ones. p.72.

### Generational closing of sound-combination gaps (ti, di, she, etc.)
Younger generations can now pronounce consonant+i/e combinations historically impossible in Japanese,
tracked through successive loanword layers: di→de (dezain, older) vs. di kept (deejee, newer);
similarly for ti and she/je combinations. p.73-74.

### Dutch-, not English-, origin "everyday" loanword layer
Because Nagasaki's Dutch trading post was Japan's sole official European contact point for over two
centuries, several basic loanwords superficially resembling English cognates (biiru, koohii, koppu,
garasu) are actually Dutch in origin — treated in a companion file (038c) where the full discussion
appears; flagged here as a grammar point the chapter itself sets up via the f/h and w-dropping
discussion. (Full vocabulary entries deferred to avoid duplicate rows; see `038c`.)

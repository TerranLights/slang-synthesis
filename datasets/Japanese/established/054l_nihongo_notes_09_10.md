## Grammar points

### Five "but" words on a formality-and-tone cline (p.46-47)
*Shikashi* is formal/written-register; *desu-ga* is moderately formal; *desu-kedo* is polite but more
conversational than *desu-ga*; *demo* and *dakedo* are both informal, for contexts where politeness
isn't required. A sixth, *datte*, stands apart in tone rather than formality: it's specifically
associated with defending oneself against an implied criticism or demand, which can make it sound
emotional or impolite regardless of how formal the rest of the sentence is. The chapter's framing
error — mixing informal *dakedo* into an otherwise formal, *-de-arimasu*-ending sentence — illustrates
that register consistency across the whole sentence matters more than which individual "but" word is
chosen.

### Ohayoo-gozaimasu beyond the morning: family-register vs. non-family-register greetings (p.48-49)
*Ohayoo-gozaimasu* literally means "it's early," but at some workplaces it's used at any hour,
because *ohayoo-gozaimasu* and *oyasumi-nasai* ("good night") belong to a family-register greeting
pair usable with family and non-family alike, while *konnichiwa*/*konbanwa* are reserved for
non-family. Workplaces that treat coworkers with family-like closeness use the family-register
greeting regardless of actual time of day; workplaces that don't use the time-of-day-appropriate
non-family greetings instead. The chapter notes that Japanese speakers often avoid explaining this
kind of nuance to foreigners, defaulting instead to whatever correction seems simplest.

### Toka: exemplifying particle and its newer semantically-bleached use (p.50-51)
*Toka* functions like *ya* for giving non-exhaustive illustrative examples, but reads more familiar.
Among young speakers, it has further drifted into a generic hedge used even when no real list of
examples is intended — reflecting a broader preference among young speakers for indirect phrasing
over blunt, precise statements. Related softening particles *demo* and *nado*, used in a request or
invitation, signal consideration for the listener by framing the ask as one easy-to-decline option
among others — though the chapter notes it's unclear whether young speakers' bleached *toka* carries
that same polite implication or is simply habitual.

### O- on adjectives: condition-of-a-person vs. no-connection-to-human-life (p.52-53)
The honorific prefix *o-* attaches to adjectives describing a person's condition (youth, promptness,
busyness) when the condition concerns the listener or people generally, and extends to weather/
general-social-condition adjectives on the same logic. It is not added to adjectives with no
connection whatsoever to human life or condition — *o-takai* applied to Mount Fuji's height is flagged
as unnatural specifically because a mountain's height isn't a "condition" in the relevant sense. The
set honorific idiom *o-chikai uchi-ni* ("before long") is a fixed, very-polite-register phrase built
on this same prefixing pattern.

### Chuu vs. juu: two different "all through" suffixes (p.54-55)
*Chuu* attaches to action/event nouns to mean "during/while," and to certain time-period nouns as a
deadline window (*konshuu-chuu* "sometime this week"). *Juu* means "all over" when attached to
place/space nouns (spatial coverage, no gaps across an area) and "all through" when attached to
certain time nouns in set expressions (temporal coverage, no gaps across a duration) — the two
suffixes are not generally interchangeable despite both glossing roughly as "throughout."

### Ookii/ookina and chiisai/chiisana: predicate-capable vs. attributive-only, and an abstract-noun preference (p.56-57)
*Ookii* and *chiisai* can appear either before a noun or as a sentence-final predicate; *ookina* and
*chiisana* are restricted to the pre-noun position only. Beyond that syntactic split, there's a
semantic lean: with abstract nouns (an achievement, a mistake, technological progress), the *-na* form
is distinctly preferred over the *-i* form, even though both are grammatical with concrete nouns.

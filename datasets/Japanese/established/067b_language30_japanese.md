## Grammar points

### Japanese writing system and sociolinguistic framing (Introduction, pp. i–ii)

The course explains Japanese uses four scripts in combination: *kanji* (Chinese-derived ideographs,
each with both "on" readings [borrowed Chinese pronunciation] and "kun" readings [native Japanese
pronunciation attached to the same character]), *hiragana* and *katakana* (two 48-symbol syllabaries
collectively called *kana*, phonetic rather than semantic), and *rōmaji* (the Roman alphabet). A
compound word's stem is typically kanji with inflectional endings in hiragana; katakana is reserved
for loanwords/foreign names, and occasionally for emphasis (the way italics function in English).
The course also notes Standard Japanese is the Tokyo dialect, understood nationwide and serving as a
lingua franca even for the indigenous Ainu people of Hokkaidō and for resident Korean/Chinese
communities — dialectal variation elsewhere in Japan can be severe enough that, e.g., a Kagoshima
speaker and an Aomori speaker cannot understand each other, per the course's own (uncited) claim.

### Pronunciation: vowel length as phonemic (p. iii, iv)

Long vowels (written *aa*, *ii*, *uu*, *ee*, *oo* in this course's romanization) are phonemically
distinct from short vowels and "equal two syllables" in duration — e.g. *obasan* ("aunt") vs.
*obaasan* ("grandmother") differ solely in vowel length. Double consonants are likewise meaningfully
distinct from single ones (*sekken* "soap" vs. *seken* "human society"), each consonant counted as a
separate syllable beat. The syllabic *n'* (apostrophe-marked) forms its own mora, pronounced like a
nasalized French vowel, with no single-consonant English equivalent.

### Pitch accent (p. v)

Japanese syllables are produced with even stress, but pitch (relative highness/lowness of voice, not
loudness) is lexically contrastive — minimal pairs are given: *iKEN* ("unconstitutional") vs. *Iken*
("opinion"); *ipPAI* ("full") vs. *IPpai* ("a cup of"); *aTSUI* ("thick") vs. *aTSUi* ("hot");
*HAshi* ("chopsticks") vs. *haSHI* ("bridge"); *KAki* ("oyster") vs. *kaKI* ("persimmon"). The
course's own pronunciation column marks the higher-pitch syllable with capital letters throughout.

### Honorific/humble and gendered-speech registers (p. ii, p. 38)

The course explicitly flags that full command of Japanese requires distinguishing honorific speech
(elevating the listener/referent), humble speech (lowering the speaker), social-status-appropriate
formality, and gendered speech differences (male vs. female speech patterns differ markedly) — but
notes this basic course selects phrases usable by both men and women, with more formal language
concentrated in sections like Introductions/Visits/Japanese-Style Accommodations and less formal
language in Restaurants/Transportation. A concrete example given: family-term vocabulary has two
parallel sets — one for referring to someone else's family members (honorific, e.g. *okāsan*,
*otōsan*), another for one's own (humble/plain, e.g. *haha*, *chichi*) — this directly cross-confirms
the same honorific/humble family-term pairing already captured from the companion workbook.

### Basic sentence structure essay (pp. 37–38)

Consolidated as a short grammar essay at the back of the book: (1) Japanese nouns are invariant for
number/definiteness — the same word serves for "cup," "cups," "the cup," and "the cups"; (2)
adjectives (not just verbs) inflect for tense, e.g. *tanoshikatta desu* ("[it] was enjoyable") vs.
*tanoshii desu* ("[it] is enjoyable"); (3) basic word order is Subject-Object-Verb (contrasted
explicitly with English's Subject-Verb-Object), and subject pronouns are frequently omitted when
understood from context (e.g. *Pasupōto o nakushimashita*, "[I]'ve lost [my] passport," with no overt
subject); (4) questions are marked by sentence-final *ka* (and/or rising pitch) with no word-order
change from the corresponding statement; (5) noun modifiers precede the noun they modify (e.g. *niku
no haitte inai ryōri*, literally "meat not-included dish" = "dishes without meat"); (6) the core
particles *ga* (marks the grammatical subject), *wa* (marks the sentence topic — used even with the
copula *desu* in equational sentences like "This is Mr. Tanaka"), and *o* (marks the direct object)
are introduced with worked examples drawn from elsewhere in the same book, plus *e* (marks
destination) and *ni* (marks location of existence or a point in time).

### Politeness-via-indirectness as a cultural/pragmatic norm (Social Customs essay, pp. 34–35)

The course frames indirection as a core politeness strategy distinct from pure grammar: Japanese
speakers are described as preferring to imply intentions rather than state them explicitly, with the
listener expected to infer what's wanted — e.g. declining a host's offer of food two or three times
before finally accepting is presented as expected ritual, not genuine refusal. *Sumimasen* is
highlighted as an unusually semantically loaded word covering "excuse me," "I'm sorry," "can you help
me," and more. The essay also explains the indirectness idiom *N desu ga...* (seen earlier at p. 13,
*Bunraku ga mitai n desu ga*) as deliberately leaving a request unstated, requiring the listener to
infer "...could you help me?" — politeness achieved through withholding the explicit ask rather than
softening it.

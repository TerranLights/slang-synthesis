## Grammar points

### True adjectives vs. na adjectives (Unit 4)
Japanese descriptive words always precede the noun. "True adjectives" end in a vowel + `-i`
(`-ai/-ii/-ui/-oi`, but not `-ei`) and behave verb-like, directly preceding the noun (`takái hon`,
"an expensive book"). "Na adjectives" (descriptive nouns) require a linking `na` before the noun
(`kírei na sukáafu`, "a beautiful scarf") but drop the `na` directly before `désu`. True adjectives
negate via the adverbial `-ku` form (`tákaku arimasen`, or the more colloquial `tákaku nai desu`,
explicitly flagged by the source as the more casual of the two negative forms); na-adjectives
negate by putting the copula itself into a negative form (`kírei ja arimasén`/`kírei ja nai desu`).
A handful of words occur as both types with slightly different pitch accent (`ookíi hóteru` /
`óoki na hóteru`, both "a big hotel"). (pp. 61-63)

### Demonstrative adjectives and "the one" construction (Unit 4)
`kore/sore/are/dóre` can only stand before a particle or the copula; to modify a noun directly
("this book") the adjectival forms `kono/sono/ano/dóno` must be used instead. The particle `no`
after an adjective means "the ... one" (`takái no`, "the expensive one"); na-adjectives require
`na` before this `no` (`sukí na no`, "the one I like"). (pp. 63-64)

### Existence verbs *arimásu*/*imásu* vs. the copula *désu* (Unit 4)
Japanese splits English "to be" into the copula `désu` (equivalence of two noun phrases) and the
existence verbs `arimásu` (inanimate objects) / `imásu` (people, animals), with location marked by
`ni`. `arimásu` used in the sense of "to have" marks its (even animate) object with `ga`. (pp. 67-68)

### Numeral classifier *-kai* for floors; large numbers (Unit 4)
`-kai` counts building storeys, with sound changes at 1, 3, 6, 8, 10 (`ikkai`, `sangai`, `rokkai`,
`hakkai`, `jukkai`); floor counting starts at 1 for ground level (matching American usage, per
explicit source note). Large numbers: Japanese has no separate word for "million," expressing it
compositionally as `hyakumán` ("hundred ten-thousands"). (pp. 70-72)

### Verb tense: *-másu*/*-mashita* system (Unit 5)
`-másu` is the polite non-past (covering present and future); `-máshita` is polite past. Each has a
negative counterpart (`-masén`, `-masén deshita`). This `-másu` form is "the form used in all
conversation, except between close friends and among children" — an explicit register note; plain
forms (for subordinate clauses/written language) come later. (p. 78)

### Compound verbs with *shimásu* (Unit 5)
`shimásu` ("to do") combines with many nouns to form compound verbs: `benkyoo shimásu` (study),
`ryóori shimásu` (cook), `shokuji shimásu` (eat a meal), `kekkon shimásu` (marry), `ryokoo shimásu`
(travel). The noun element in these compounds is not marked with object-particle `o`. (p. 79)

### The *-mashóo* ("let's"/"shall we") suffix (Unit 5)
`-mashóo` makes suggestions/propositions ("let's..."); `-mashóo ka` softens it into a question
("shall we...?"). Notably `ikimásu` (go) vs. `kimásu` (come): Japanese `kimásu` is used only for
motion toward the speaker (or the speaker's associated place) — so answering a door knock, a
Japanese speaker says the equivalent of "I'm going," not "I'm coming," an explicit culturally-
contrastive pragmatic note. (pp. 82-83)

### Object particle *o*, including with verbs of motion (Unit 5)
`o` marks direct objects of transitive verbs, and is historically spelled with the hiragana for
`wo` (now pronounced identically to `o`) — paralleling topic-particle `wa`'s archaic spelling with
`ha`. With verbs of linear motion (go/walk/fly/run), `o` marks the path traversed, corresponding to
English "along/through/over" (`michi o arukimásu`, "to walk along a road"). Some English-transitive
verbs are intransitive in Japanese and take an indirect object with `ni` instead (`aimásu`, "to
meet": `Tanaka san ni aimáshita`). (pp. 84-85)

### Place of action: *ni* vs. *de* (Unit 5)
Static existence (`imásu`/`arimásu`) uses `ni` for location; more active verbs use `de` to mark
where an action takes place (`kaisha de shinbun o yomimásu`, "I read the newspaper at the
company"). (p. 85)

### Days of the week and time-prefix system (Unit 5)
Days of the week derive from the five Chinese elements (fire/water/wood/metal/earth) plus sun/moon,
introduced to Japan with the solar calendar in 1872. Time-word prefixes: `sen-` (last), `kon-`
(this), `rai-` (next), `mai-` (every) — with irregular exceptions explicitly flagged: `sennen` does
not mean "last year" but "in recent years" (actual "last year" is `kyónen`); `rainichi` does not
mean "tomorrow" but "coming to Japan." (pp. 85-87)

### *Aizuchi* — conversational back-channeling (Unit 6)
A dedicated register/pragmatics note: Japanese listeners are expected to actively signal attentive
listening via interjected comments ("I see," "really," etc.) called *aizuchi* (lit. "pounding in
unison," referencing synchronized mortar-and-pestle rice pounding) — unlike English conversational
norms where this would be "interrupting." Canonical aizuchi: `áa sóo desu ka`, `hái, wakarimáshita`,
`sóo desu née`. Related: hesitation fillers (`éetto`, `anoo`, `sóo desu nee` with drawn-out level
intonation) paradoxically *aid* conversational flow by giving listeners more processing time and
helping the speaker avoid sounding "too abrupt or self-assertive, both of which are considered poor
form in Japanese society" — an explicit sociolinguistic register claim from the source. (pp. 94-96)

### The *-te* form (gerund) (Unit 6)
Verbs fall into three conjugation classes (consonant-root/"-u verbs," vowel-root/"-ru verbs,"
irregular). The `-te` form (used with auxiliary `imásu` for progressive/resultative-state meanings,
and as a clause-linker) undergoes class-dependent sound changes: `-ki`→`-ite` (kakimásu→káite),
`-gi`→`-ide` (isogimásu→isóide), `-i`(after a/other)→`-tte` (kaimásu→katte, machimásu→mátte,
torimásu→totte), `-mi`/`-bi`→`-nde` (yomimásu→yónde, asobimásu→asónde); `-shi` verbs add `-te`
with no change (hanashimásu→hanáshite). With transitive verbs, `-te imásu` expresses an action in
progress; with intransitive verbs it expresses a completed/resultant state (`itte imásu`, "is in a
state of having gone" = "is there now"). A number of verbs describing state virtually only occur in
the `-te imásu` form (`ái shite imásu`, "loves"). `shirimásu` ("to get to know") occurs in `-te
imásu` only in the affirmative, not the negative. (pp. 95-99)

### Polite request forms with *-te* (Unit 6)
`-te kudasái` is the basic polite request ("please do X"); framing it as a question
(`-te kudasaimásu ka`) is politer, and a negative question (`-te kudasaimasén ka`) politer still —
an explicit parallel is drawn to the same pattern in English ("would you" vs. "wouldn't you").
`-te itadakemásu ka` is more polite again, tied conceptually to the giving/receiving verb system
developed fully in Unit 12. (pp. 99-100)

### Native Japanese numerals and ordinal suffixes (Unit 6)
A native numeral set (`hitótsu, futatsú, mittsú...` to `tóo`, "ten") survives mainly for counting
miscellaneous objects without a specific classifier, and for age; it has been largely displaced by
the Chinese-derived set above ten. This native set takes ordinal suffix `-me` (`hitotsume`, "the
first"); the Chinese set takes `-bánme` (`ichibánme`, "the first") and must be used past ten
(`sanjuuichibánme`, "the thirty-first"). (p. 105)

## Grammar points

### §7.17 Clauses (continued) — parallel/additive chaining forms, and relative clauses

Beyond the gerund's sequential/causal uses already covered in `035e`, two further additive-listing constructions are given: **`-tari...-tari`** (built from the plain past tense: drop final `-a`, add `-ri`), used for "do things like X and/or Y" / "sometimes X, sometimes Y" — explicitly a *non-exhaustive, representative* sample of actions or alternating states, not a complete enumeration (p. 142); and **`-shi...-shi`** ("and, in addition, and what's more"), used to stack multiple clauses with an escalating/additive sense, including stacking a passive-voice clause (`nusumareta shi` "was stolen, and...") into the chain (p. 143). **Relative clauses** are then addressed directly (p. 144), confirming and expanding on the §5.6 finding: Japanese has no relative-pronoun words (no equivalent of who/that/which), and instead places the entire relative clause — ending in the plain form of its verb — directly *before* the noun it modifies, with no overt linking word at all (contrast English's postposed "the restaurant that I always go to" vs. Japanese's literal "I-always-go restaurant").

### §7.18 Common expressions with verbs and adjectives (the modal-expression inventory)

A cluster of fixed periphrastic constructions covering permission, obligation, prohibition, probability, and desire — collectively the backbone of modal expression in Japanese, and all built compositionally from forms already established earlier in §7 (p. 145–149): **`-temo ii desu`** ("it's okay if.../you may..." — permission; gerund + `mo` + `ii desu`); **`-nakutemo ii desu`** ("it's okay even if not.../you don't have to..." — negative-gerund-equivalent + `mo ii desu`); **`-tewa ikemasen`** ("must not"); **`-nakutewa ikemasen`** / **`-nakereba narimasen`** (two interchangeable "must" constructions); **`beki desu`** ("should, ought to" — moral obligation); **`hazu desu`** ("is expected to, is supposed to" — an expectation/supposition explicitly contrasted with `beki desu` as carrying *no* moral-obligation sense, p. 147); **`hō ga ii desu`** ("it's better that... / had better..."); **`kamo shiremasen`** ("might, probably"); **`hoshii desu`** ("want, need" — used with a noun object and `ga`); **`-tai desu`** ("want to [verb]" — infinitive + `tai desu`, inflects like a verbal adjective for its own negative/past forms). The `beki desu`/`hazu desu` contrast (moral "should" vs. neutral "is supposed to/is expected to") is a genuinely useful distinction for later register/pragmatics work, since both gloss loosely to English "should."

### §7.19 Nominalization

Verbs or entire clauses are turned into nouns by adding `no` or `koto` immediately after the plain form (p. 149–150). The two are not freely interchangeable: **`koto`** is more formal and, critically, is the only one of the two that **can appear in predicate position** (e.g. `Miru koto wa shinjiru koto desu` "Seeing is believing" — both nominalized clauses here are predicate-adjacent); **`no`** is less formal and is restricted to non-predicate positions (subject/object slots), cross-referencing back to the same `no`-nominalization used in the potential-mood periphrastic construction (§7.11) (p. 149–150).

### §7.20 Verb charts (reference appendix — not re-tabulated)

A ten-verb reference appendix (`hanasu` "speak," `iku` "go," `kaeru` "return," `kau` "buy," `kiku` "listen/ask," `kuru` "come," `miru` "see," `nomu` "drink," `suru` "do," `taberu` "eat," `wakaru` "understand") giving each verb's full present/past/gerund/presumptive/imperative/conditional/potential/passive/causative/causative-passive/honorific/humble paradigm side by side (p. 151–161). As noted above, this is a drill/reference section applying rules already captured rather than introducing new ones, with two exceptions worth flagging: `kiku` surfaces a second, sense-disambiguating suppletive humble form (`ukagau` for "ask" vs. `okiki suru` for "listen") not given in the §7.16 summary table, and `kuru` surfaces a second honorific form (`oide ni naru`) alongside `irassharu`; and `wakaru` is the only chart with genuine paradigm gaps (no potential, no humble form), plausibly because an inherently-stative "understand" verb has no further ability-mood to derive and no natural humble-register variant.

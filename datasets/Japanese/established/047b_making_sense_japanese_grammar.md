## Grammar points

### Part 4 — Tense and events

**Units 48–49 — the two-tense system.** Japanese has only non-past and past (no dedicated
future); non-past covers present, future, habitual/general, and (unlike English's
progressive-for-intention construction) even the subject's future plans — "we are going to
the beach tomorrow" must use plain non-past, not a progressive form, in Japanese. The
progressive *-te-iru/-te-imasu* is reserved for in-progress condition/action, not future
intention. Past-tense marking on stative predicates picks up a special extra function:
signaling that an assertion *about* a present/future state was itself made in the past
("when did you say the deadline would be?"). Verbs of gratitude/apology (*arigatoo
gozaimasu* vs. *arigatoo gozaimashita*) use the non-past/past contrast to mark whether an
interactional transaction is still open or has concluded — a service clerk switches from
non-past mid-transaction to past once the customer leaves.

**Unit 50 — te-forms as maximally-cohesive connectors.** A te-form carries no tense of its
own (tense is supplied by context), and its use forces an interpretation that the two
linked parts are closely related — co-existing properties, simultaneous/parallel events,
immediate succession, cause, means, or basis-for-judgment — never merely two
loosely-sequenced independent events.

**Unit 51 — tense markers as event-separators; the general principle.** This is the book's
broadest claim in Part 4: whenever two superficially similar constructions differ only in
whether an extra tense marker is present, the tensed version always describes a *more
independent, more objectified* pair of events, and the tenseless version describes a
*more tightly integrated single event*. Four parallel minimal pairs illustrate this: (a)
*iku-deshoo* (tensed embedded verb, two separable events: someone's going + the speaker's
judgment) vs. *ikimashoo* (tenseless stem, one simple event, "let's go"); (b) a
tense-marked purpose clause ("saved money [in order] to travel," long-term, separable goal)
vs. a tenseless one ("went to a bookstore [in order] to buy a book," immediate, tightly
bound goal); (c) *-mama* "while still Xed" (tensed, time-lapse implied, often read as
negligence: "went up still wearing shoes [left on a long time]") vs. a te-form (tenseless,
simultaneous, no negligence implication); (d) the conditionals `-tara` (historically
past-tense-derived, more realized/likely) vs. `-reba` (not tense-derived, more
hypothetical) — and an evidential parallel, "objective judgment" constructions built on a
tensed embedded clause (e.g. *-yoo-da*, based on externally gathered information like a
weather report) vs. "intuitive judgment" constructions built on a tenseless stem (e.g.
*-soo-da*, based on an immediate perceptual cue like a visibly darkening sky).

**Units 52 — degrees of event cohesion.** Builds a cline from least to most integrated:
separate sentences (no shared tense/subject constraint) → clause-final-particle-linked
subordinate clauses (`-kara`, `-kedo`; each clause keeps its own tense and can have a
different subject, though politeness marking inside such a subordinate clause reads as
hyperpolite) → te-form/stem-form-linked sub-events (typically share a subject, tenseless,
tightly bound) → auxiliary-verb-fused sub-events where the two original events are barely
perceptible as separate at all (*tabete-kita* "came having eaten," *tabe-hajimeta* "started
eating"). Within that last, most-fused tier, te-form auxiliaries (*-te-shimau* "end up
doing, with a judgmental sense of being out of control/norm-deviating") are shown to carry
a residual sense of separateness/consequence that stem-form auxiliaries (*-owaru* "finish,"
purely descriptive) lack — and because the stem-form construction is more tightly fused, it
must linearly precede any following te-form auxiliary, never the reverse (*yomi-hajimete-
mita* is grammatical; the reverse order is not).

**Units 53–55 — the two-perspective tense system (flagship finding of this book).** Where
English fixes subordinate-clause tense relative to a single reference point (the speaker's
"now," uniformly for main and subordinate clauses), Japanese subordinate clauses can be
tensed from either of two distinct vantage points, producing systematically different
surface tense marking than English in both directions (non-past-in-Japanese-where-English-
has-past, and vice versa):
- **The main-clause perspective** (Units 53–54): the subordinate event's tense is fixed
  relative to the *main clause's* "now," not the speaker's. This is adopted whenever the
  subordinate clause expresses the main-clause participant's own objective, purpose,
  expectation, knowledge, ability, emotion, reason, or condition (the speaker is described
  as metaphorically "entering the main clause participant's mind"); when the main-clause
  participant actively intervenes in or facilitates the subordinate event; or when the
  subordinate clause describes a stable property/condition of an element referenced in the
  main clause.
- **The speaker's perspective** (Unit 55): used instead when the subordinate and main
  clause events are only incidentally linked (not objective/purpose-like); when the
  subordinate event is presumed to hold independently of the main-clause participant's own
  awareness of it; when an explicit speaker-relative temporal expression is present
  (*kodomo-no koro* "in my childhood," *kore-kara* "from now on" — these force the
  speaker's own temporal framework); or when the subordinate clause reflects the *speaker's*
  reasoning rather than the main-clause participant's.
- A single minimal pair makes the contrast concrete: *Yonekura-san-wa okaasan-ga tsukuru
  fuku-o kite-iku* "Yonekura will wear the dress her mother will make" uses non-past for the
  dress-making (incidental link → speaker's perspective, since the dress-making isn't
  framed as *for* the purpose of the wearing from Yonekura's own vantage) vs. *Yonekura-san-
  no okaasan-wa Yonekura-san-ga kite-iku fuku-o tsukuru/tsukutta* "Yonekura's mother
  makes/made the dress Yonekura will wear," where the wearing is the mother's *purpose* in
  making it, forcing the main-clause perspective and (regardless of the main clause's own
  tense) a non-past form for the still-future wearing event.

### Part 5 — Miscellaneous topics

**Unit 56 — hai/ee/iie as interactional, not truth-functional (see vocabulary row in
047_making_sense_japanese_grammar.md).** Explains why Japanese speakers never shout
*hai*/*iie* as a spontaneous exclamation the way English speakers shout "Yes!"/"Oh, no!" —
Japanese happiness/dismay exclamations are instead *yatta* and *shimatta*, because
*hai/ee/iie* are inherently addressed to an interlocutor's assertion, not free-standing
affect words.

**Unit 57 — ko-/so-/a-series demonstratives (see vocabulary row above).**

**Unit 58 — "the longer and vaguer, the more polite."** A cross-linguistically general
principle (illustrated with an English request-form politeness ranking) explaining a
specific Japanese fact: a *negative* question (*suwarimasen-ka* "won't you sit?") reads as
more polite than the bare positive (*suwarimasu-ka* "will you sit?") precisely because it
is less direct/more hedged.

**Unit 59 — polite vs. direct forms, register by syntactic position.** Polite forms
(`-masu`/`-desu`) are used toward strangers, social superiors, public/consumer-facing
address, and in letters; direct forms are used when interpersonal respect is irrelevant
(self-talk, diaries, intimate address, talking to babies/pets, and — notably — objective
writing like academic papers, news articles, and literary narrative, which are judged on
their own merit rather than on respect shown to the reader). Register distinctions are
neutralized *inside* a sentence regardless of overall register: noun modifiers, pre-
evidential-marker clauses (before *-soo-da*, *-yoo-da*, *-deshoo*, *-ka-mo-shiremasen*),
embedded questions, and embedded thoughts all default to direct forms even in an otherwise
polite sentence — using polite forms in these positions reads as over-compliant.

**Unit 60 — the general continuous-scale-vs.-discrete-category principle.** Explicitly
named as the unifying mechanism behind several of this book's specific findings — the
transitivity scale (Unit 26), the noun-abstractness continuum underlying na-noun/no-noun
selection (Unit 40), and the `-ni`/`-de` placement/domain distinction — with each language
drawing its own cutoff point on an otherwise continuous scale, producing borderline cases
that pattern with either category depending on contextual nuance (e.g. *umareru* "to be
born" and *ryuukoo-suru* "to be fashionable" normally take `-de` but shift to `-ni` for
long-past historical events once they've lost the "vividness" of recency).

**Units 61–62 — `-te-kuru`/`-te-iku` (see vocabulary row in 047_making_sense_japanese_grammar.md
for the core polysemy account).** Unit 62 adds the sociolinguistic claim that these
constructions are preferred over plain verbs in parental/teacherly directives specifically
because they frame an individual's action (eating breakfast, doing homework) as
consequential for a group (the school day, the class) — fitting a broader
group-solidarity orientation the authors attribute to Japanese social norms, and paralleling
the directionality encoded by the benefactive auxiliaries `-te-kureru`/`-te-ageru`.

**Units 63–64 — `-n-da`/`-n-da-kara` (see vocabulary row in 047_making_sense_japanese_grammar.md).**

**Unit 65 — recovering hidden (dropped) subjects.** Systematizes several independent
recovery cues: (1) bare desire/sensation/feeling verbs (*tabetai*, *nemui*, *soo omou*)
default to 1st person in statements and 2nd person in questions; expressing them of a 3rd
person requires an indirectness device (*-garu*, *-yoo-da*, hearsay *-to itte-iru*); (2)
directional-giving auxiliaries constrain the subject categorically: *ageru*/*morau*'s
subject must be the speaker or someone speaker-aligned, *kureru*'s subject can never be the
speaker; honorific marking points to a respected 3rd person; (3) contextual defaults —
a dropped subject in a declarative sentence defaults to the previous sentence's subject (or
to "I" if sentence-initial), a dropped subject in a question/invitation/suggestion defaults
to "you," and a subordinate clause's dropped subject defaults to its main clause's subject.

**Unit 66 — a practical long-sentence parsing method (study-skill, not a grammar point
proper).** Recommends decomposing a long sentence top-down: split on clause-subordinating
conjunctions (`-kara`, `-keredo`, `-ga`, `no-de`, `no-ni`) into major ideas, each of which
typically ends in a judgmental element (evidentiality/attitude marker); within each major
idea, locate te-forms/stem-forms and the main verb immediately before the judgmental tail
to identify how many sub-events it contains; for each main verb, recover its subject via
the farthest `-wa`/`-mo`-marked noun phrase or the nearest unclaimed `-ga`-marked one (or,
failing that, the Unit 65 contextual-recovery heuristics); then reassemble the translated
pieces main-clause-last, mirroring the fact that Japanese and English are structurally
near-mirror-images of each other. The authors flag this as a reading/translation strategy
specifically — explicitly not applicable to real-time listening comprehension, which
requires the reverse, incremental left-to-right processing skill instead.

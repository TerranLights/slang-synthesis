**Source:** Osamu Mizutani & Nobuko Mizutani, *Nihongo Notes 5: Studying Japanese in Context* (The
Japan Times, 1st ed. July 1983) and *Nihongo Notes 6: Situational Japanese 1* (The Japan Times).
Full volumes — vol. 5: 70 short situational-usage essays, printed pp. 8–146 (plus a general index to
vols. 1–5 at p.149 and a word/phrase index at p.157); vol. 6: essays continuing the same format. Both
are part of the 10-volume *Nihongo Notes* series (sibling file `established/054` covers vols. 9–10,
confirming the series format).

gotcha as `established/054`'s *Nihongo Notes 9/10*) — confirmed empirically: image *i* ≈ printed

copyright discipline. Each essay is tightly scoped to one or two closely related expressions, so

## Grammar points

### Soo-deshoo-ka vs. Soo-deshoo-ne vs. Soo-desu-ka (p.8-9)
Three near-homophonous expressions with distinct functions: *Soo-deshoo-ne* (with optional *ne*
prolongation) expresses sympathy toward someone describing a problem; *Soo-deshoo-ka* expresses the
speaker's own doubt/wondering, and can read as mild opposition/criticism when used in reply to
someone else's claim; *Soo-desu-ka* ("Is that right?") is more neutral and its sympathetic/factual
reading depends heavily on tone. Mixing these up is flagged as a common learner error.

### Chigaimasu as a correction/negation (p.10-11)
*Chigaimasu* functions as a more emphatic synonym of *iie* ("no"), usable alone, combined (*iie,
chigaimasu*), or omitted in favor of *iie* alone depending on how strongly the speaker wants to
assert the correction. It's natural for correcting matters of objective fact (wrong train, wrong
item handed over) but less natural for disputing someone's personal opinion/evaluation, where
softer disagreement forms are preferred instead.

### ...tara doo-desu-ka vs. ...to doo-desu-ka (p.12-13)
*...tara doo-desu-ka* is a genuine suggestion pattern ("why don't you..."). *...to doo-desu-ka* is
superficially similar but asks about the *result* of a hypothetical action rather than actually
proposing it. *...ikaga-desu-ka* is the more polite register variant of the suggestion pattern; the
ending can be dropped with a rising intonation on the remainder for a more casual suggestion.

### ichido / hitotsu as request-softening openers (p.14-15)
Both *ichido* ("one time") and *hitotsu* ("one") lose their literal numeric meaning when placed
before a request or proposal, instead functioning to signal the speaker's intention early so the
listener can prepare — recommended especially in social/polite contexts to avoid sounding abrupt.

### ...niwa vs. ...ni vs. ...ni totte vs. ...nimo (p.16-17)
To mark personal standpoint ("for me," "to me"), plain *...ni* sounds awkward where *...niwa* is
required (*watashi-niwa muzukashii-desu*, not *watashi-ni muzukashii-desu*). *...ni totte*
emphasizes personal standpoint even more strongly. *...nimo* adds "also" to a standpoint-marked
noun (*gakusee-nimo kyooshi-nimo* — "for both students and teachers").

### sorede / sorekara / soshite (p.18-19)
All three can translate as "and"/"and so," but they aren't interchangeable: *sorede* (often
shortened to *de*) shows interest in an ongoing situation's outcome and is conversational;
*sorekara* specifically marks strict chronological succession between two separate events; *soshite*
is reserved for connecting a speaker's own clauses in monologue/exposition/writing, not for
prompting a conversation partner to continue — using *soshite* conversationally sounds unnaturally
formal/childish.

### Ii-ja arimasen-ka as insistence/reassurance (p.20-21)
Said with a falling tone (it is not a real yes/no question despite the *-ka* ending), this pattern
leaves an entire preceding clause (e.g. "even if you don't have to work so hard," "even if you
don't need to worry") implicit, functioning as gentle insistence when pressing a guest to stay/eat
more, or as reassurance when comforting someone with a worry.

### ...de ii-desu as proposal-acceptance (p.22-23)
Used for proposing or accepting a neutral/low-stakes suggestion (a meeting place, a minor option).
When the *offering* party's own personal interest is at stake (e.g. offering their own house for a
party), *...de ii-desu* can sound presumptuous about the listener's gratitude; *...wa doo-desu-ka*
and *...ga ii-deshoo* sound more confident/assured in that situation instead.

### Naanda, kimi-ka — register switching on relief (p.24-25)
*Naanda* marks the moment a speaker realizes there's no need to worry or maintain formality — e.g.
finding a "lost" item, or recognizing on the phone that a caller is actually an old friend. The
switch is often accompanied by an abrupt drop from polite to familiar register mid-exchange. Even
when addressing a friend informally, politeness (adding -san, or a title like -sensee) is restored
when a third party (the friend's family member, a secretary) answers the phone instead.

### Shitsuree-desu-ga vs. Shitsuree-shimasu (p.26-27)
*Shitsuree-desu-ga* ("Excuse me, but...") softens an upcoming personal/intrusive question and
should be followed by the actual question. *Shitsuree-shimasu* ("I'm going to be rude") instead
precedes performing a minor action — sitting down, leaving, reading something handed to you.
Substituting one for the other causes real miscommunication: saying *shitsuree-shimasu* when meaning
to ask a question can make the listener think you're about to leave.

### Ohikitome-shimashite / polite restraint about one's own enjoyment (p.30-31)
A host's stock apology for "detaining" a guest is used regardless of whether any actual detaining
happened. More broadly, polite/formal conversation restrains direct expression of one's own
enjoyment or pleasure (contrast the freer *Kyoo-wa tanoshikatta-wane* of familiar speech) — learners
who import casual enthusiasm into polite registers are understood but sound subtly off.

### Joozu as a register-restricted compliment (p.32-33)
Direct praise of someone's skill using *joozu* ("skillful") is appropriate adult-to-child or in
familiar speech, but socially risky toward anyone the speaker must address politely (a superior,
a professor) — it can come across as evaluative/presumptuous. Polite-register compliments instead
praise the object/result itself (*kiree*, *suteki*, *subarashii*) rather than the person's ability.

### naoru/naosu and other self-effacing intransitive-for-transitive substitutions (p.34-35)
When describing recovery/repair, the intransitive *naoru* is default even when real effort was
involved; the transitive *naosu* is reserved for emphasizing the speaker's own strong will/effort,
and using it about a service just performed (e.g. a repair clerk) can sound boastful. The same
self-effacing logic extends to other services: *ocha-ga hairimashita* ("tea has been poured") is
more modest than *ocha-o iremashita* ("I made tea"), and sometimes an entirely different expression
replaces a verb that would directly describe the speaker's own action (*moo dekite-imasu-yo*, "It's
ready now," instead of naming who wrote/prepared something).

### Sore-ga... as a dramatic contrary-to-expectation answer (p.36-37)
Used alone or as a sentence opener, *sore-ga* signals that the coming answer betrays what the
listener was hoping to hear (bad exam results, a boring movie) — it can also signal the speaker's
own expectation was betrayed in a positive direction. It reads as dramatic; *demo*/*keredomo* ("but")
substitute when the speaker doesn't want to foreground the unexpectedness itself.

### ...te-imasu as resultative state vs. ongoing action (p.38-39)
With verbs describing completion/change-of-state (e.g. *kekkon-suru*, "to marry"), *...te-imasu*
marks the state resulting from the completed action (married now), not an action in progress. With
verbs describing a continuing state/action (*nomu*, *kangaeru*), the same form marks an ongoing
action instead. Critically, *...te-imasu* on a completion verb cannot mean a future action unless an
explicit future time word is present.

### yo/ne particle ordering (p.42-43)
*Ne* solicits listener agreement ("don't you agree?"); *yo* asserts the speaker's own opinion
emphatically ("I tell you"). The two can combine, but only in fixed order — *yo* always precedes
*ne* (*...yone*, never *...neyo*). *Ne* can likewise stack after other sentence-final particles
(*no*, *wa*), always coming last, to add precision/delicacy toward the listener's feelings.

### Register ladder for hearsay about a third party's news (p.44-45)
Three near-equivalent ways of saying "I hear she's getting married" differ only in register, not in
tense marking: plain *kekkon-suru soo-desu-ne*, more-familiar *kekkon-suru-n-da-tte-ne*, and
polite/feminine *kekkon-nasaru-n-desu-tte-ne* (using the honorific verb *nasaru*). None need a
separate future marker since the base verb form already carries the contextual future reference.

### Sentences deliberately left unfinished on -te/-mashite (p.46-47)
Japanese conversation frequently ends a clause on the unfinished te-form (*...mashite* rather than
completing to *...mashita*) in two closely related uses: softening set expressions of apology/thanks
by leaving the obvious continuation unsaid, and inviting the listener to complete an unfinished
thought cooperatively (tied to the broader *aizuchi* short-reply-word pattern of Japanese listening
behavior).

### Request/command register ladder (p.48-49, p.64-65)
Japanese grades requests and commands on a politeness scale: plain imperative (*ike*, *yamero*) is
blunt, mostly male, familiar/urgent; *-nasai* is mainly parent/teacher-to-child; *-n-da* sits
between blunt and polite, carrying an explanatory/patronizing tone that can soften via *da→desu→
desu-yo*; bare *-te* forms (no *kudasai*) are common in familiar speech and group-coordination
moments even toward superiors; *-te-kudasai* is the default polite request; *-te-kudasaimasen-ka*
is most polite; *-kure* is blunt/familiar, mainly male. Written notices use a distinct *...koto*
form aimed at students/employees, with gentler phrasing (*-naide-kudasai*) reserved for customers.

### Emphatic ...tara/...ttara (not conditional) (p.50-51)
Beyond its conditional use, *-tara* (often *-ttara* after a verb) attaches to a statement/command
with a falling tone to convey the speaker's irritation at having to repeat themselves — distinct
from the rising-tone suggestion use of *-tara* ("why don't you..."). The emphatic form is gendered
on polite stems (women-only, *arimasen-tara*) but unisex on plain stems (*nai-ttara*).

### Reversed apology/reason ordering under emotional stress (p.52-53)
Textbook apologies state the reason before the apology (*Osoku natte sumimasen*), but real
conversation very often reverses this (*Sumimasen, osoku natte*) when the speaker is flustered or
out of breath — and more broadly, reversing a *-te*-clause's normal reason-then-consequence order
to consequence-first signals heightened emotion, appropriate in familiar register but less so in
formal contexts.

### Service-greeting register: Irasshaimase/Irasshai (p.54-55)
Those who serve (clerks, restaurant staff) greet customers with *Irasshaimase* (formal) or
*Irasshai* (familiar, common among women and toward younger people) rather than time-of-day
greetings, marking an asymmetric server/served relationship in which the customer isn't obligated
to reply. *Irasshai(mase)* differs from *Irasshaimashita* ("someone has come," needing *Yoku* added
to function as a welcome: *Yoku irasshaimashita*).

### The "suffering/adversity passive" takes the affected person as subject (p.56-57)
When something happens to a person's body part or belongings through someone else's action, the
passive sentence's subject must be the PERSON affected, not the body part/belonging itself —
*Ashi-o fumaremashita* ("[I] was foot-stepped-on"), not the ungrammatical-sounding *Ashi-ga
fumaremashita*. The same pattern governs *Zeekin-o torareta* ("I had my taxes taken") over
*Zeekin-ga torareta*.

### ...ni narimasu as an indirect-politeness pricing convention (p.58-59)
Literally marking change/result, *...ni narimasu* is correctly used when totaling multiple items,
but is extended by clerks even to single flat prices because its indirectness reads as more polite
than the blunter, more direct *...desu* — a conventionalized politeness strategy rather than a
literal claim that a calculation took place.

### ...kamo shiremasen as reserved/understated agreement (p.60-61)
Beyond marking genuine uncertainty ("it may rain tomorrow"), *...kamo shiremasen* is used socially
as an understated way of agreeing or making a tactful judgment without fully committing — softening
feedback (on a friend's dress) or avoiding sounding too readily discouraging (about another's
chances) compared to a flat *Soo-desu-ne*/*Soo-deshoo-ne*.

### Social convention around seasonal/life-event greeting cards (p.62-63)
*Shochuu-mimai* ("summer season greetings") functions as the mid-summer counterpart to New Year's
cards — no winter equivalent is needed since the New Year's card already covers it. More broadly,
Japanese social custom places real weight on sending notice cards at life changes (job, address,
marital status), and on a brief parting note of thanks — foreign acquaintances who leave Japan
without such notice (*aisatsu-mo shinaide*) are flagged by the authors as a genuine, if small,
source of offense.

### Itte-kurereba yokatta-noni as implicit self-offer, not criticism (p.66-67)
The surface form appears to blame the listener for not asking for help, but its actual social
function is to express the SPEAKER's own regret at not having been asked — a way of offering help
after the fact without being pushy beforehand. In more formal registers, the whole clause is often
dropped because stating it risks being heard as criticism despite the intended reading, with regret
instead voiced through an apology-first construction.

### Maido arigatoo-gozaimasu / conventionalized treating-as-regular (p.68-69)
Literally addressed to a repeat customer, this thank-you formula and parallel phrases (a barber
asking if "the usual" will do) are conventionally extended even to first-time customers — Japanese
service politeness treats presuming familiarity as more polite than acknowledging a customer's
novelty.

### Zuibun as an emotionally-invested intensifier (p.70-71)
Distinct from neutral degree-intensifiers like *taihen*/*totemo*, *zuibun* specifically signals the
speaker's own emotional involvement/surprise at a degree (a price jump, being impressed by
progress). Its familiar/emotional coloring makes it a poor match for the formal gratitude phrase
*arigatoo-gozaimasu* — combining them reads as a mismatch in register, verging on comedic.

### ki-ni suru vs. shinpai (p.72-73)
*Ki-ni suru* describes being unable to stop thinking about something the speaker already knows
isn't serious (a slightly-too-short haircut); *shinpai* is reserved for genuinely significant
worries (illness, a difficult undertaking). They're largely not interchangeable — substituting
*shinpai* into a trivial-concern context implies the concern is more serious than intended.

### Doubled/truncated -te feeling-emphasis constructions (p.74-75)
A first-person feeling expression (adjective or feeling-verb in -te form) can be doubled for
emphasis and paired with one of three near-synonymous intensifying tags — *-te tamaranai*, *-te
shikata-ga nai*, *-te shiyoo-ga nai* (all roughly "can't help/stand it") — and the tag itself can be
omitted entirely once the doubled -te clause has made the emphasis clear, without creating
ambiguity about what's meant.

### ...de ii as considerate/self-effacing ordering language (p.76-77)
Ordering via *...de ii* ("X will do") rather than *...o kudasai*/*...ni shimasu* signals the customer
isn't making a demanding request, showing consideration for kitchen/service staff; it's also used
(often without full conscious awareness) to soften a guilty feeling about ordering something not
very profitable for the establishment.

### Group-conformity ordering and in-group familiarity (p.78-79)
Japanese diners in a group often order identically, more strongly so when group members don't know
each other well enough to feel safe standing out; well-established in-groups (family, close
colleagues) feel freer to deviate, sometimes adding an explicit face-saving reason when they do.

### Verb-omission in polite "I'll pay" offers (p.80-81)
Offering to pay a bill in formal register conventionally omits the verb itself (*oharai-shimasu*/
*harawasete-kudasai*) — stating "pay" or "treat" directly is seen as impolite when politeness is
required, so *Koko-wa watashi-ga...* is left dangling. *Ogoru* (informal "to treat") is restricted
to close/younger relationships; *gochisoo-suru* has wider range but still isn't fully formal.

### deshoo as confirmation-seeking vs. probability marker, and its register restriction (p.82-83)
*...deshoo* marks probability in general, but with rising intonation it seeks the listener's
confirmation of something the speaker already believes — a usage restricted to familiar
conversation with peers or juniors; using it toward a social superior is a register error (as
happened to Mr. Lerner addressing the company director), correctable with plain *...ga arimasu*/
*...ga arimasu-ne*. Women use the confirmation-seeking *deshoo* more; men more often substitute the
blunter *daroo*.

### desu as a focus-marking shorthand answer (p.84-85)
Even to a "who/what" question that invites a full subject+verb answer, Japanese regularly answers
with just the identified item plus *desu* (*Kimura-san-desu* rather than *Kimura-san-ga
torimashita*) whenever the speaker's actual concern is naming the agent/place/item itself rather
than restating the whole event — and this pattern holds across past, present, and future question
contexts alike.

### Systematic gendered sentence-final particles (p.86-87)
A full worked paradigm: on verbs/adjectives, men use plain *-yo*/*-ne*, women insert *wa* first
(*-wayo*/*-wane*); on nouns and na-adjectives, men use *-dane*/*-dayo*, women drop the *da* (*-ne*/
*-yo* alone), with women's speech generally avoiding *da* directly before a sentence particle.
Vocabulary choices beyond particles also skew by gender, consistently enough that a transcript's
speaker genders are usually identifiable from text alone — confirmed with a 4-item speaker-gender
quiz in the source itself.

### Near-content-free hedging openers before an opinion (p.88-89)
Phrases like *are-deshoo-ne* ("it's probably that, isn't it?"), *jitsu-wa* ("as a matter of fact"),
*anoo* ("well"), and *nan-to iimashoo-ka* ("how should I put it") carry little literal content but
serve an important social function: signaling hesitation and inviting the listener's anticipated
agreement before an opinion is actually stated, avoiding an abrupt-sounding assertion. *Doo-deshoo-
ne* performs the analogous function right before a question. *Daroo* substitutes for *deshoo* in
male familiar speech throughout this family of expressions.

### Gomen-kudasai's dual function and overlap with Shitsuree-shimasu (p.90-91)
*Gomen-kudasai* serves both as a door-calling phrase (requesting someone come to the door) and,
especially among older speakers (particularly older women), as a goodbye on leaving — in the
leave-taking sense it overlaps with *Shitsuree-shimasu*, which has the wider range of the two since
it is usable before any potentially-impolite action (sitting, handing something over, entering a
room, stepping away), a use *Gomen-kudasai* does not share.

### Sentence-completion as a mark of conversational engagement (p.92-93)
Answering with a bare copula+*ne* fragment (*Desu-ne*, *Deshita-ne*, *Deshoo-ne*) left dangling from
someone else's unfinished sentence functions as active, valued participation in Japanese
conversation rather than an interruption. The practice is restricted to informal registers — formal
contexts use the fuller *Soo-desu-ne*, and very formal contexts drop *ne* entirely (*Hai, soo-desu*/
*Hai*) and wait for the original speaker to finish their own sentence rather than completing it.

### The politest tier of the request-form ladder (p.94-95)
Beyond the already-polite *-te-kudasai* (fine for classroom/business use), real politeness calls for
*-te-itadakemasen-ka* or *-te-kudasaimasen-ka*; between these two, *-te-itadakemasen-ka* is
considered more polite because *-te-kudasaimasen-ka* implies the request is simply taken as a
matter of course. Appending *-deshoo-ka* (*-te-itadakemasen-deshoo-ka*) raises politeness further
still. Dedicated honorific verbs (*goran-ni naru*, *meshiagaru*) exist as alternatives but can be
skipped in favor of this general-purpose polite-request pattern.

### -ta adjectives marking psychological involvement in a process (p.96-97)
Beyond simple past tense, a -ta-form adjective (*osokatta*) versus its plain/dictionary form (*osoi*)
marks the speaker's concern with the PROCESS leading to the current state rather than the state
itself — *Osokatta-desu-ne* emphasizes the time that elapsed before someone arrived; the parallel
pattern explains why *Arigatoo-gozaimashita* (past) implies gratitude for sustained effort/time
spent versus the more neutral *Arigatoo-gozaimasu*, and why *Yokatta-desu-ne* specifically implies
relief after a period of concern, which is why it (not plain *Ii-desu-ne*) is the natural response
to hearing good news about something one had worried over.

### Three advice-giving constructions and their social restrictions (p.98-99)
*...tara doo-desu-ka* is the default, most neutral advice form. *...hoo-ga ii-desu* implies the
speaker considers themselves better-informed than the listener, so it's appropriate only toward
equals or juniors. *...eba ii-noni* carries built-in implicit criticism of what the listener is
currently doing, restricting its polite use to situations where the advice is unambiguously for the
other person's own benefit (e.g. telling a gift-bringing guest they needn't have bothered).

### kono/sono and koo/soo track relationship, not just spatial proximity (p.100-101)
The near/far demonstrative split (kono "this"/sono "that," koo "this way"/soo "that way") is
governed by who something currently "belongs to" relationally, not merely physical distance — a
barber mid-cut treats the customer's own hair as *kono* (currently in the barber's working
attention) while the customer calls the same hair *sono*; a message-relayer uses *koo* before stating
a message (still theirs to deliver) and *soo` after (now handed over to the listener).

### Gobusata-itashimashita vs. Hisashiburi-desu-ne: apology vs. neutral observation (p.102-103)
These two "long time no see"-adjacent expressions are not interchangeable: *Hisashiburi-desu-ne* is
a neutral-to-warm observation about elapsed time, answered with agreement (*Soo-desu-ne*);
*Gobusata-itashimashita* is specifically a self-blaming apology for having neglected to write/call,
answered with a deflecting *Kochira-koso* ("I should be the one to say that") — reflecting a
broader Japanese politeness norm of readily taking blame on oneself in this kind of social lapse.

### A-wa B-da as elliptical relational claim, not just identity (p.104-105)
The surface-identity-looking *A-wa B-da* pattern (the oft-cited textbook example *Boku-wa unagi-da*,
"I am an eel," really meaning "I'll have the eel") generalizes to a broader pattern where a verb
tying A and B together is omitted and must be recovered from context — "a man is his wife" really
asserts that a wife determines a man's character/success, not that the two are identical.

### Animacy restrictions on verbs of leaving/living (p.106-107)
*Dekakeru* ("go out for a purpose") is restricted to human subjects; using it for an inanimate
subject like a departing bus personifies it and sounds odd. *Deru* and *shuppatsu-suru* ("depart")
both tolerate human or inanimate/vehicle subjects, with *shuppatsu-suru* specifically foregrounding
a scheduled time. The same restriction governs *kurasu* ("to live [a life]"), which can't be applied
to zoo animals without deliberately personifying them.

### kiree's unified but polysemous range (p.108-109)
*Kiree* covers "beautiful," "clean," "tidy/neat," and adverbially "completely/thoroughly" (finishing
all one's food, a fully resolved task) — all traceable to one underlying sense of satisfaction at
seeing something in an ideal, resolved state. Flagged learner error: *kiree* is a na-adjective
(*kireena sora*, negated *kiree-ja nai*), not an i-adjective, even though Japanese children
themselves sometimes produce the i-adjective-pattern error *kireeku nai* before self-correcting.

### ...te-mo ii extended to the speaker's own non-committal action (p.110-111)
Beyond its core permission-giving/requesting use, *...te-mo ii* can describe the SPEAKER's own
planned action to mean "I don't mind...ing"/"I might as well..." — but this use signals a lack of
enthusiasm, making it unsuitable for accepting an offer or agreeing to a favor in any context
requiring politeness, where more positive-sounding acceptance language is expected instead.

### bakari's two meanings disambiguated by attachment target (p.112-113)
Whether *bakari* means "about" or "only/nothing but" is read off of what it attaches to: amount
expressions get "about," everything else gets "only/nothing but." Attached to a past-tense verb, it
layers on a third nuance — that an action was just completed and it's too early to expect results.

### Double-entendre pitfalls for learners: neru, osewa-ni naru, kankee, otoko/onna (p.114-115)
A cluster of ordinary-looking words/verbs carry a romantic/sexual secondary reading that native
speakers navigate automatically but that trips up learners: *neru* ("to sleep") for an overnight
stay implies a romantic overnight rather than neutral lodging (use *tomaru* instead); *osewa-ni
naru* ("to be kindly helped") can imply a mistress relationship depending on context; *kankee-ga
aru* ("to have a relationship") can imply a sexual one between opposite-sex people; and bare
*otoko*/*onna* ("man"/"woman") can mean "lover" unless the safer *otoko-no-hito*/*onna-no-hito` is
used instead.

### "Classroom Japanese" being too familiar for its intended social use (p.116-117)
*Issho-ni shimashoo* ("let's do it together") is commonly taught to beginners as a basic cooperative
phrase, but using it to offer help to a host sounds too familiar/presumptuous in a real social
situation — a polite guest instead hedges with *Nanika otetsudai dekiru koto-wa nai-deshoo-ka*. More
broadly, phrases learned early and used first in a foreign language ("classroom Japanese") need
extra care before being deployed in genuinely polite contexts.

### akachan/akanboo honorific-humble noun pair, and the -chan diminutive restriction (p.118-119)
*Akachan* politely names someone else's baby; *akanboo* is the humble/neutral term for one's own
baby or babies generally — conflating them (saying *uchi-no akachan* about one's own child) sounds
childish, on the same pattern as the -chan diminutive suffix itself being reserved for addressing or
referring to other people's children (or used by children themselves), not applied to one's own
family member's name in adult social speech.

### Register ladder for "wife," and the honorific/humble asymmetry (p.120-121)
*Kanai* is the standard formal term; informal/casual registers offer *nyooboo*, *uchi-no*, *uchi-no
yatsu* by speaker preference; *okusan* is strictly honorific and can refer ONLY to someone else's
wife, never one's own. Often no term is used at all once context makes "wife" obvious. Using *kanai*
more than functionally necessary — especially as a newlywed — can itself draw a teasing reaction.

### Ii toshi as a social-maturity marker (p.122-123)
Beyond literally meaning "a good age," *ii toshi* functions as shorthand for "old/mature enough that
certain behavior is or isn't appropriate" — self-justifying retirement planning, criticizing an
adult's childish pastime, or noting a young person has reached marriageable age. *Ii* more generally
extends to "good enough [for some implied purpose]" with other nouns, as in *moo ii jikan-da* ("it's
high time," prompting an implied action from context).

### Non-literal accept-with-reservation formula, and a false-friend trap (p.124-125)
*Ii-desu-ne* ("that's good, I'll do it") combined with *Demo warui-desu-ne* ("I feel bad troubling
you") forms a conventional pattern for accepting an offer while voicing polite reluctance — reversing
the order (*Warui-kedo, sore-ga ii*) reads as more straightforwardly positive. A genuine false-friend
trap: bare *Ii-desu* WITHOUT the *-ne* can mean "No, thank you" instead, despite looking almost
identical to the acceptance formula.

### A full register-graded inventory of Japanese reply/interjection words (p.126-127)
A systematic small paradigm, useful as a register map: *Hai* (common, polite, usable formally) →
*Haa* (even more polite) → short *Ha* (mostly male, especially to a superior) → *Aa* (casual,
unsuitable where politeness is required) → *Ee* (less polite than *Hai*, informal) → *Hee* with a
falling tone (archaic humble/subservient, rare in modern Tokyo) vs. *Hee?* with a RISING tone
(doubt/surprise/contempt — tone alone flips the meaning) → *Fuun* (interest) vs. short *Fun*
(contempt/dismissal) → *Hoo* (interest/surprise, typically elderly male speakers). Any can be
doubled with a falling tone for emphatic interest.

### ...dokoro-ka's direction is socially inferred, not grammatically marked (p.128-129)
*...dokoro-ka* denies a mild preceding statement in favor of a much stronger one, but nothing in the
grammar itself signals whether the stronger claim runs positive or negative — a listener defaults
to the charitable/positive reading in a social context (praising someone's ability) partly because
bluntly asserting the negative reading about a person present would itself be an etiquette
violation; context alone (a crowded-train complaint, a food-quality complaint) disambiguates in
other registers.

### Honorific copula vs. honorific existence-verb: a minimal pair (p.130-131)
*...de irasshaimasu* (honorific *desu*, "to be equal to/identical to") and bare *irasshaimasu*
(honorific *imasu*, "to exist/be present") look similar but ask fundamentally different questions —
confusing them produces a real, if subtle, miscommunication. The same three-step politeness
escalation (plain honorific verb → *o-...-desu* → *o-...de irasshaimasu*) generalizes across verbs
of action and even adjectives (*...kute irasshaimasu*).

### Pitch, not word order, resolves apparent double-negative ambiguity (p.132-133)
*Takaku nai-ja nai* looks confusingly doubled, but Japanese speakers resolve it by pitch: the first
*nai* (higher/clearer pitch) carries the real semantic content, while the trailing *-ja nai*
functions as a pure tag-question marker of attitude, not a second negation layer — allowing the
pattern to stack to three *nai*s without real ambiguity once the convention is known.

### wa as a contrastive/attention-directing spotlight (p.134-135)
Beyond its basic topic-marking role, *wa* can be added to convert an awkward-sounding full negation
("I didn't understand it") into a natural partial negation ("I didn't understand ALL of it"), and
more generally functions like a spotlight directable onto any element(s) of a sentence the speaker
wants the listener to specifically attend to — including stacking onto more than one element at once.

### Consistency of politeness register across a whole exchange (p.136-137)
Once -masu/desu politeness is established, Japanese speakers maintain it even onto very short
confirming fragments (adding *desu* after bare particles like *kara*, *made*, *dake*, *ni*, *de`) —
an abrupt, unexplained drop to a bare familiar fragment mid-conversation isn't read as a style choice
but as a signal of the speaker's strong emotion (surprise, joy, anger).

### Strategic self-deprecation as a status-management technique (p.138-139)
Beyond the general norm of deflecting praise, the source documents senior/capable group members
specifically voicing complaints about their own ability as a deliberate social strategy — concealing
real competence/influence to avoid seeming intimidating and to put junior members at ease, explicitly
linked to the cultural preference for public figures (the source names former PM Masayoshi Ohira) who
adopt a faltering, humble speaking style despite genuine fluency.

### Precision required when reporting who called whom (p.140-141)
A phone call report using a direct transitive verb (*...ga denwa-shimashita*) sounds wrong unless
paired with a directional marker (*-te-kimashita*) specifying the call was aimed at the speaker or
listener; *...kara denwa-ga arimashita* ("there was a call from X") avoids the issue by framing the
call as an event rather than an action directed at someone, and is there flagged as the more natural
default phrasing.

### Plain negative/affirmative forms repurposed as familiar commands (p.142-143)
Contrary to the usual rule that a subjectless Japanese sentence defaults to first-person reference,
a doubled plain negative (*Ki-ni shinai, ki-ni shinai*) functions as a direct familiar-register
command to the listener; the plain affirmative can do the same but typically shifts to PAST tense
when used this way (*Itta, itta!*). This imperative use skews more male; *ne* softens it, *yo* adds
emphasis/irritation.

### Multi-speaker cooperative sentence-building (p.144-145)
Beyond a two-person question-and-completion exchange, the source documents full group conversations
where a single sentence is cooperatively assembled clause-by-clause across three or more speakers —
explicitly tied to building and signaling rapport among people who already share aligned views on
the topic being discussed.

### Verb tense marking stance vs. direct advice (p.146-147)
Dictionary-form *kaeru hoo-ga ii* states the speaker's own general judgment; past-form *kaetta
hoo-ga ii* functions as direct advice aimed at the listener — the same past-tense-as-advice-marker
pattern recurs with other verbs. Politeness requires converting the verb to honorific *o-...-ni
naru* form in formal register, and often avoids the whole *-ta hoo-ga ii* construction in favor of a
softer alternative (*...-tara ikaga-deshoo*).

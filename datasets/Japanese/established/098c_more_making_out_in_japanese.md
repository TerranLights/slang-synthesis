# Japanese — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: More Making Out in Japanese, pages 84–124 (image files p-45–p-65)

**Source:** Todd & Erika Geers, *More Making Out in Japanese* (Tuttle Publishing), pages 84–124
(chapters: end of "Love and Marriage," ch. 9 "Health," ch. 10 "Curses and Insults," ch. 11 "Lovers'
Arguments," ch. 12 "Broken Intercourse," and ch. 13 "Breaking Up!" — this last chapter runs to the
end of the book's own numbered content at p.124).

**Coverage note:** This source has NO text layer (confirmed raster scan via Adobe Acrobat's "Image
Conversion Plug-in") and was fully vision-read from rendered page images. Each image file is a
two-page book spread (left + right printed page), verified empirically: image p-45 shows printed
pages 84/85, and each subsequent image advances by 2 printed pages, running sequentially. The
final assigned image, p-65, shows printed page 124 on its left side paired with an unnumbered
publisher back-matter page (a "Making Out Phrase Book Series" advertisement listing other titles'
ISBNs) on its right side — this is not vocabulary/dictionary content and was not extracted; the
book's own teachable content ends at page 124. This is a situational phrasebook organized by
chapter/topic, not an alphabetical dictionary — every distinct vocabulary item, phrase, and
explicit register/gender-speech annotation (♂/♀ symbols marking male/female speech forms) in the
assigned range is captured. No handwritten marginalia was found anywhere in this range — all
content is clean printed typeset matching the surrounding page, so no exclusions were needed on
that basis. Illustrations (line-drawing cartoons) are present throughout but are not source text
and are not transcribed. Explanatory prose boxes are paraphrased per copyright discipline; only
short illustrative Japanese phrases/words are quoted directly, as the book itself presents them as
the teachable unit.

Gender-speech markers: ♂ = male speaker form, ♀ = female speaker form, as explicitly marked by the
book. Where a single English prompt has multiple Japanese realizations tagged ♂/♀, each is captured
as a separate vocabulary row.

# Japanese — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: 100-Key-Word Combinatorial Phrasebook

**Source:** Boye Lafayette De Mente, *Instant Japanese: How to Express 1,000 Different Ideas with
Just 100 Key Words and Phrases!* (Revised Edition, Tuttle Publishing, 1993/2003 — copyright pages
cite both 1993 and 2003). 49 PDF pages. Full book (Parts 1–10 covering all 100 key words, the
numbers system, dialects note, special set phrases, and an alphabetical "Additional Vocabulary"
glossary of ~150 further terms) read via `pdftotext`.

**Coverage note:** every grammar point and distinct vocabulary item in the book is represented below
— this is a genuinely compact, complete source, not a sampled excerpt.

**PDF extraction gotcha found and worked around:** `pdftotext -layout` produces a real, fully usable
text layer for this book's English prose and for all romaji — but the embedded **hiragana/katakana
glyphs are OCR-garbled into unrelated Latin-ish punctuation soup** (e.g. printed すみません renders as
`t.;;. £ tt lv`), not a decodable fixed-offset or 1:1 substitution cipher of the kind documented
elsewhere in the extraction spec for other CJK sources — cross-checking the same garbled strings
against several different known-good words confirmed no consistent mapping exists; this is
irrecoverable from the text layer and would require vision-reading the scan to recover the original
kana/kanji. Given the book's own stated design — "all Japanese words and expressions are presented in
Romaji as well as in hiragana or katakana" (p.6), with romaji given equal billing as the primary
pedagogical form — **romaji is used as the citation form for every entry below, and the kana/kanji
column is treated as unrecoverable from this source** rather than vision-read in full; this is noted
per-entry as `n/a (kana unrecoverable — OCR garbling, not decodable)` in the Notes column rather than
silently omitted. No handwritten marginalia was present (this scan has a genuine Adobe Acrobat/Paper
Capture OCR text layer throughout, not a vision-read of page images).

## Grammar points

### The 100-words→1,000-ideas combinatorial template (flagship structural claim)

The book's entire premise, stated in its own subtitle and preface, is that a small closed set of
~10 verbs, learned together with a single productive conjugational template, generates vastly more
distinct communicable "ideas" than the raw word count suggests (p.4: "mastering just ten verbs and
their forms... makes it possible to say over one hundred things"). The template, demonstrated
identically for every verb keyworded above (*iku*, *taberu*, *nomu*, *au*, *dasu*, *tomaru*, *matsu*,
*kuru*, *kau*, *kaku*, *dekiru*, *aruku*, etc.), is a fixed set of suffixal/periphrastic
transformations on the polite stem:

> **{{stem}}-masu** (present/future affirmative) · **{{stem}}-masen** (negative) ·
> **{{stem}}-mashita** (past affirmative) · **{{stem}}-masen deshita** (past negative) ·
> **{{stem}}-tai desu** (want to {{verb}}) · **{{stem}}-mashō** (let's {{verb}}) ·
> **{{stem}}-te kudasai** (please {{verb}}) · **{{stem}}-masu ka?** (question form)

e.g. *iku* "to go" → *ikimasu/ikimasen/ikimashita/ikitai desu/ikimashō/itte kudasai*. This is the
single most important grammar point in the source per its own framing: vocabulary breadth in this
book is deliberately traded for depth of command over one productive paradigm, which the extraction
spec's dispatch note anticipated as "a productive combinatorial/template structure worth documenting
explicitly." Each individual conjugated form is therefore **not** listed as a separate vocabulary row
above — only the base verb is keyworded, with a note that the full paradigm is given in the source.

### Dual numeral systems (native-Japanese vs. Sino-Japanese)

Japanese has two parallel counting systems: a native set (*hitotsu, futatsu, mittsu...*) that stops at
ten, and a Sino-Japanese set adopted from Chinese (*ichi, ni, san...*) that continues productively
indefinitely (p.64). Compounding above ten uses only the Sino-Japanese set: eleven = "ten-one"
(*jū-ichi*), twenty = "two-ten" (*ni-jū*), etc. — a fully transparent, agglutinative-style positional
compounding scheme once the base morphemes are known. A third, overlapping wrinkle: counting
*people* specifically uses the native set only for one and two persons (*hitori*, *futari*) and
switches to the Sino-Japanese set with a *-nin* counter suffix from three up (*san-nin*, *yo-nin*)
(pp. 66–67) — an early, simple illustration of Japanese's broader numeral-classifier/counter system
(of which *-nin* for people and *-fun/-pun* for minutes are the two instances this book actually
covers).

### Levels of politeness (keigo) as a multi-axis system, not a single formality scale

The book (pp. 59–61) distinguishes at least three named registers: the casual "plain form" (used
among friends/family), the "polite-neutral" form (the default register the whole book teaches, and
the appropriate register for non-Japanese speakers in most situations), and *keigo* ("honorifics,"
ultra-polite forms reserved for addressing high-status people, e.g. *de gozaimasu* replacing *desu*).
Within *keigo* itself a further distinction is drawn between humble forms (used self-referentially,
e.g. humble *mōsu* for "to say" vs. standard *iu* vs. ultra-polite *ossharu*) and honorific forms
(used of the addressee) — i.e. politeness is encoded as *which party the inflection elevates or
lowers*, not merely as a single up/down formality dial. The book also notes a sociolinguistic
asymmetry by gender and register: women (especially older women) tend toward higher habitual
politeness than men even in ordinary contexts, and certain traditional professions (e.g. *geisha*)
maintain their own distinctive in-group speech register.

### Japanese Dialects note (p.70)

Virtually all Japanese speakers understand and can produce *hyōjungo* ("standard language," based on
Tokyo speech), which is what this book (and most instruction for foreigners) teaches exclusively.
The book explicitly flags that regional dialects exist and can be mutually unintelligible to students
of standard Japanese, singling out Kagoshima (southern Kyūshū) and Aomori (northeastern Honshū) as
examples of areas whose traditional dialects diverge furthest from the standard — but gives no
further linguistic detail on these dialects themselves (this is a named-but-undetailed pointer in the
source, not a worked example).

### Japanized English loanwords (*gairaigo*) and their re-syllabification

A dedicated aside (pp. 50–51) describes a large (book's own estimate: ~25,000-word) stratum of
English-derived loanwords integrated into Japanese by breaking the source word into Japanese-legal
syllables and pronouncing/writing it accordingly — e.g. English "strike" → *sutoraiki*, "milk" →
*miruku*, "blue" → *burū*, "sex" → *sekkusu*. The book stresses these derived forms are often
**unrecognizable to a native English speaker** without already knowing the Japanization convention,
and that compounds are sometimes further abbreviated in ways that compound the opacity (its worked
example: *sekuhara* = "sexual harassment," blending the first syllables of both English words — an
English-source clipped-compound slang coinage, structurally parallel to clipping/blending phenomena
noted in other languages' `established/` extractions in this project). This is a naturalized-loanword
phonological-adaptation mechanism, distinct from the native derivational morphology covered elsewhere
in this file.

### Writing systems and the Hepburn romanization system

The book names and briefly characterizes all four Japanese writing systems — kanji (logograms from
Chinese), hiragana (phonetic script for grammatical elements, verb endings, children's early
literacy), katakana (phonetic script reserved for transcribing foreign loanwords), and rōmaji (Latin
letters) — and gives a short history of the Hepburn romanization system (devised by the American
missionary Dr. James Curtis Hepburn, who also compiled the first Japanese-English dictionary in 1867
and co-founded Meiji Gakuin University), noting it displaced several Japanese-devised competing
romanization schemes because those schemes retained Chinese-style letter-to-sound mappings (e.g. *ti*
for the sound *chi*, *tu* for *tsu*) poorly matched to English/Romance-language reader intuitions.

### Double consonants as a timed mora, not true gemination

Japanese "double consonants" (the small *tsu* っ/ッ before certain consonants) are explained as a
silent beat of held time rather than a doubled articulated consonant sound in the English sense — the
small *tsu* itself is not pronounced but occupies a mora-equivalent pause, by analogy with the slight
pause in English "bookkeeper" (pp. 16–17). This is presented as a purely phonetic/timing phenomenon
and the book gives no vocabulary items whose *gloss* depends on the distinction (i.e., no minimal
pairs are worked), so it is recorded here as a standalone phonological grammar point rather than
attached to specific vocabulary rows.

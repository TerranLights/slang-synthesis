# Japanese — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Communicating with Japanese by the Total Method, Basic Part 1 (Sound Patterns/Writing System + Lessons 1–10)

**Source:** Noah S. Brannen (assisted by Masayoshi Hirose and Tsuyoshi Sasaki; revised with the
assistance of Katsuhisa Yamaguchi), *Communicating with Japanese by the Total Method, Basic, Part
One* (Japan Missionary Language Institute, Tokyo; first revision 1992, reprinting a 1967–1977
original). Pages cited are the book's own printed page numbers (PDF page = printed page + 10 for
this file's front-matter-adjusted range). This file covers: Units 1–6 ("Sound Patterns and the
Writing System," pp. 5–45, sampled) and Lessons 1–10 ("Simple Sentences," pp. 47–221, early lessons
extracted fully, Lessons 6–10 sampled more selectively). Lessons 11–20 and the appendices are
covered in `062b`; Basic Part Two (Lessons 21–30) is covered in `062c`.

**Coverage note — representative sampling across a 655-page, two-part audio-course textbook (444 +
211 pages).** Per dispatch instructions, this is NOT exhaustive page-by-page extraction: Lessons 1–5
(the foundational copular/adjectival/verbal clause bases) were sampled most fully — full vocabulary
lists and full Section 4 grammar explanations; Lessons 6–10 were sampled via one vocabulary page and
the key Section 4 grammar passage(s) per lesson, skipping the drill/dialogue/reading sections (pure
pedagogical repetition of already-captured vocabulary, per the spec's coverage rule). Units 1–6
(pronunciation/writing-system drills) were sampled very lightly since hiragana/katakana orthography
is already covered by established files 001–003 and 020 — Unit 5 (Tone Accent) was the exception,
sampled more fully since pitch-accent minimal pairs are not covered elsewhere in this project's
Japanese corpus yet. This source has no text layer and was entirely vision-read (scanned book,
clean print quality, no OCR text layer at all — `pdftotext` over the full 444-page Part 1 returned
only ~444 bytes, essentially page-break characters). No handwritten marginalia was found on any
vision-read page in this sample; all content read was clearly typeset.

**Vision-reading guard:** no marginalia encountered in the sampled pages. All entries below are
`verified` or `plausible_unverified` Vision Reading Confidence as marked per row — print quality was
uniformly high (clean typewriter-style typeset scan), so most entries are `verified` (clearly legible
against surrounding printed context) with only a few `plausible_unverified` where a diacritic/accent
mark was faint.

## Grammar points

### Copular clause: affirmative, negative, past (Lesson 1, §1.1)

The copula desu attaches at the predicate slot of a copular clause (NOUN + desu). Negative is formed
with de wa arimasen (or the contracted ja arimasen, though the text notes some Tokyo native speakers
use only the uncontracted de wa form). Past is formed by substituting the indicative copula desu with
its past inflection deshita; negative past is hon de wa arimasen deshita. The copula's own stem
alternates (desu/deshi-) to accommodate the consonant-initial past suffix -ta, inserting a vowel i and
devoicing s→sh — explicitly framed in the text as a case of regular Japanese phonological assimilation
(CV.CV syllable structure constraint), not an arbitrary irregular form (p. 56).

### Interrogative sentences (Lesson 1, §1.2)

Three rules: (a) add the particle ka at the sentence end; (b) give the sentence rising intonation
(optional when ka is present); (c) if an interrogative word like dore "which" fills the subject slot,
the subject-marking particle changes from wa to ga (e.g. Dóre ga anata no hoñ desu ka? "Which is
your book?").

### Deictics: the kore/sore/are/dore and kono/sono/ano/dono series (Lesson 1 §1.3; Lesson 4 §4.1.1)

A four-column, four-row system: Deictic Particle (attributive, must precede a noun: kono/sono/ano/
dono), Noun (independent pronoun: kore/sore/are/dore), Adverb-locative (koko/soko/asoko/doko), and
Adverb-manner (koo/soo/aa/doo). All four series share the same characteristic initial consonant per
distance category (k-=near speaker, s-=near addressee, a-=distant from both/"known" to both, d-=
interrogative). Sore vs. are is a three-way not-near-the-speaker gradation absent from standard
English demonstratives ("that" vs. "that over there"). The ano/are/asoko series has a secondary,
non-spatial "known/shared information" usage (linguistic deixis, explicitly credited to Charles
Fillmore) — e.g. referring to a person both speakers already know about, not physically present.
Deictic particles (kono etc.) can be separated from the noun they modify by intervening material (ano
Doitsujin no sensee "that teacher who is German").

### Three levels of politeness and sentence-final particles ne/yo (Lesson 2 §2.5)

The text identifies three significant levels of spoken politeness: Plain (intimate relationship, no
deference needed — among students, equals), Polite (somewhat formal, non-intimate, unknown relative
status — the "masu-desu style" used as the default register throughout this course), and Honorific
("status-conscious" speech showing deference to others / self-deprecation, reserved for Lesson 31).
The primary formal marker of polite vs. plain is the predicate form (desu/-masu vs. plain endings),
though certain words are independently considered more polite than others (dónata desu ka? vs. dáre
desu ka? for "who is it?"). Honorific nouns take affixes o-/-san (e.g. o-isha-san "doctor"); -san
(or -sama in honorific style) attaches to surnames as Mr./Mrs./Miss. A closed set of everyday-life
nouns are *never* spoken without the honorific affix (e.g. gohan "meal/rice," where go- is a variant
of o-). Sentence-final particle ne functions similarly to an English tag question, often not truly
asking but inviting the listener's agreement/confirming the speaker's own statement; yo adds emphasis
to a statement.

### Adjectival clause and i-adjective inflection (Lesson 3 §3.1)

Adjectives, like the copula, are inflected forms and fill the predicate slot directly (no copula
needed): [Nihongo wa] [muzukashii] "Japanese is difficult." Adjective stems always end in a vowel
(never e). Three inflections given in this lesson: Indicative/present (stem + -i: aka- → akai),
Past (stem + -katta: aka- → akakatta), and Adverbial, used here specifically in negative
constructions (stem + -ku + nai: aka- → akaku nai "isn't red"). A footnote observes that unaccented
adjectives acquire an accent immediately before desu, and notes a real-time sociolinguistic shift:
"in the speech of many young people today, unaccented adjectives have acquired an accent not only
before desu but in all occurrences" (p. 82) — a live register/generational variation the book's own
authors flag.

### Noun phrases: NOUN + no, attributives, and deletion of the noun head (Lesson 4 §4.1)

A noun can modify another noun via the particle no (NOUN + no + NOUN), chainable (Nihoñgo no señsḗ
"teacher of Japanese"), with the final noun in the chain as the phrase head. Two attributive types:
(1) adjectives modify a following noun directly in their indicative form (muzukashii hoñ "difficult
book"); (2) copular nouns modify a following noun via the particle na, explicitly identified as the
attributive form of the copula desu itself (shiñsetsu na katá "kind person"). Deletion of noun head:
in an expression like chichí no desu "it's Dad's," the head noun (e.g. enpitsu "pencil") is deleted
just as in the English equivalent — the particle no alone is described as carrying "a tremendous
load" in Japanese syntax once the head is gone.

### Verbal clause base: plain/polite verb paradigm, C-verbs vs. V-verbs, wa/ga (Lesson 5 §5.1–5.3)

The polite verb form is marked by the derivational suffix -mas-; plain indicative suffix is -u, plain
past -ta (same pattern as the copula). Two verb classes are distinguished by where the stem ends:
Consonant-verbs (C-verbs, stem ends in a consonant, e.g. nak-u "barks") insert a vowel formant -i-
before the polite ending (nak-i-masu); Vowel-verbs (V-verbs, stem ends in a vowel, e.g. ne-ru
"sleeps") attach the polite ending directly (ne-masu) but take a consonant formant -r- in the plain
indicative (ne + -r- + -u → neru). Practical diagnostic given: check the plain negative form — if it
ends in -anai the verb is a C-verb (hashiránai → hashirimaseñ, V-verb hashíru not hashíi), if the
negative does not end in -anai it's a V-verb (nenai → nemaseñ). Grammar also covers: wa marks a
topic/focused slot (replacing o or ga, but not other particles like ni, which simply add wa); ga
emphasizes a copular-clause subject, marks a subject after an interrogative word like dare, and marks
an embedded-clause subject. Negative questions (Kono hoñ wa omoshiroku arimaseñ ka? "Isn't this book
interesting?") are explained as requiring the listener to answer based on agreement/disagreement with
the negated proposition, not the English-intuitive polarity (Hai = "yes, [it is] not [interesting]";
Iié, omoshirói desu = "no, it is interesting"). The three basic sentence/clause types are formalized:
Copular (Noun+wa / Noun+desu), Adjectival (Noun+wa / Adjective), Verbal (Noun+wa / Verb).

### Object and indirect object slots; wa for focus (Lesson 6 §6.2)

Object is marked with the particle o unless the speaker wants to focus that slot, in which case wa
replaces o (Kodomo ni wa ego o oshiemaseñ deshita "He didn't teach English to children" — contrast
with Kodomo ni ego wa oshiemaseñ deshita "He didn't teach the child(ren) English," where wa instead
focuses the object ego). The interrogative náni "what?" takes the form náni (not nán) immediately
before the object particle o or subject particle ga. The indirect object (second goal of the verb's
action) is marked with ni and is grammatically optional on some transitive verbs but entirely absent
as a possible slot on others (e.g. tabéru "eats" never takes an indirect object) — the book formalizes
this as two structurally distinct subclasses of transitive verb. Invitations/requests are
conventionally phrased as negative questions for politeness (Kōhī o nomimaseñ ka? "Won't you have
some coffee?" lit. "don't you drink coffee?"), answered as if affirmative questions.

### Location element and the two "to be" verbs aru/iru (Lesson 7 §7.1–7.3)

Introduces the clause's full peripheral-element slot model: [Time][Loc/Dir][Subj][Acc][Means]
[Manner][Ind.Obj][Obj][Pred], noting a corpus-based observation that "usually no more than four or
five elements (slots) occur in a single clause (simple sentence) in normal conversation" (p. 162) —
a concrete, if coarse, frequency-shaped claim about clause complexity drawn from the author's own
dissertation-era syntagmemic-formula data. The Location element is marked by ni with stative
existence verbs aru (inanimate: Tsukue no ue ni hoñ ga arimasu "There's a book on the desk") and iru
(animate — people, animals, fish, etc.: Kono heyá ni inú ga imásu "There's a dog in this room"); with
verbs of other classes location is marked by de instead. The copula desu is explicitly distinguished
from aru/iru: "He is Mr. Tanaka" (copular clause, desu) is a structurally different sentence type from
"Mr. Tanaka is here" (verbal clause with iru).

### suki/kirai as copular nouns, not verbs; agentless intransitives; transitive-intransitive pairs (Lesson 8 §8.1–8.3)

Because the English glosses "likes"/"dislikes" look verb-shaped, the text explicitly warns beginners
against treating suki and kirai as verbs — they are copular nouns (same class as shiñsetsu "kind")
and, unlike most copular nouns (which can form manner adverbs with ni, e.g. jōzú ni "skillfully"),
suki/kirai do not form adverbs and never fill the subject/object slots the way true verbs would. A
distinct class of intransitive verbs doesn't merely lack an object — it doesn't even require
specifying or implying an agent (Dóa ga akimáshita "The door opened" — no claim about who/what opened
it). Many of these agentless-capable intransitives have a morphologically related transitive
counterpart used when the agent IS specified: shimaru/shimeru ("shuts" intr./tr.), nokoru/nokosu
("is left over"/"leaves over"), tomaru/tomeru ("stops"), naoru/naosu ("is repaired"/"repairs"),
yakeru/yaku ("is baked"/"bakes"). Separately, §8 formalizes the double-wa/ga "topic and subject
co-occurrence" construction common in Japanese: Watashi wa beñkyō ga suki desu "I like study" (lit.
"As for me, study is likable") — the subject of the copular predicate is grammatically "study," not
"I," with watashi marked instead as the sentence topic via wa.

### Time relations, time-noun affixes (Lesson 9 §9.2)

A full time-relations chart is given across five columns (-2/-1/now/+1/+2) and four rows (day/week/
month/year): ototoi–kinoo–kyoo–ashita–asatte (days), señseñshuu–señshuu–końshuu–raishuu–saraishuu
(weeks), señsengetsu–sengetsu–kongetsu–raigetsu–saraigetsu (months), ototoshi–kyonen–kotoshi–
rainen–sarainen (years) — a clean, systematically patterned closed vocabulary set. Time-noun affixes:
prefix mai- "every," suffixes -goro "about/around," -goto "every [interval]," -oki "every other"
(footnoted as also usable on some non-time nouns).

### Dual numbering system and telling time (Lesson 10 §10.1–10.2)

Japanese has two parallel numeral systems: a native-Japanese set (hitotsu, futatsu, mittsu... up to
too "ten") and a Chinese-derived (Sino-Japanese) set (ichi, ni, san...) used obligatorily past ten,
with only a handful of exceptions where native-derived forms persist for specific counted nouns past
ten (hatsuka "the 20th day," juuyokka "the 14th day," both day-of-month counters). Time is expressed
in a copular construction (Ima nañji desu ka? "What time is it now?" / Ima jūji desu "It's now ten
o'clock") — the Time slot itself does NOT occur in this construction, since nañji desu/jūji desu is
itself the NOUN+desu copular predicate. Questions about time are more specific in Japanese than
English — "At what hour do you go to bed?" (verbal clause, nañji ni nemasu ka) rather than "when."
Minutes are added via -sugi "...after" and -mae "...before" (jūji nijuppuñ-sugi "20 minutes after
10"). Some numerals, including time numerals, are unaccented in isolation but acquire an accent when
followed directly by a particle like de or when counting (futatsú° marked in the vocabulary list with
a superior circle to show this conditioned accent).

### Pitch-accent system for nouns (Unit 5, Sound Patterns and the Writing System)

Standard Tokyo-dialect pitch accent for multi-syllable nouns follows two rules: (1) the pitch of a
word's first and second syllables is always different, and (2) a word has only one accent (one pitch
peak) — hearing two separate pitch peaks indicates two separate words, not one. A corollary: unless a
word's first syllable itself carries the accent, that first syllable is spoken as a lower "warm-up"
syllable than the rest of the (unaccented-initial) word; all syllables preceding the accent share the
word's high pitch, all following it are low. Two-syllable nouns fall into three pitch-accent groups
illustrated with minimal-pair/triple sets sharing identical segmental phonemes (hashi "chopsticks" /
hashi "bridge" / hashi "edge"; kaki "oyster" / kaki "fence" / kaki "persimmon"; hana "(name)" / hana
"flower" / hana "nose") — true homophones at the segmental level, disambiguated purely by pitch
contour, which (per the text) is why hearing two accents in a row is itself evidence of a word
boundary. Three- four- and five-syllable nouns have four, more complex accent patterns, introduced
via further practice sets (kokoro "heart," karada "body," señsḗ "teacher," etc.). A basically
unaccented numeral or adverb (e.g. futatsu "two," kinoo "yesterday") can acquire an accent when
followed by a particle (Ringo o futatsú kudasái "Give me two apples, please") or in certain adverbial
uses (Kinó wa sámukatta desu "Yesterday it was cold").

# Japanese — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Kanji de Manga, Volume 1

**Source:** Glenn Kardy (creator) & Chihiro Hattori (art), *Kanji de Manga: The Comic Book That
Teaches You How To Read And Write Japanese!*, Volume 1 (Manga University / Japanime Co. Ltd.,
Tokyo/San Francisco, 1st edition, printed December 2004; ISBN 4-921205-02-7), full 106-page volume.
Foreword by Tomonori Morikawa, Ph.D. (Waseda University). Covers all 80 kanji required for JLPT
Level 4 (the old-numbering beginner level, now N5) — the same 80 characters taught in Japan's
first and second elementary-school grades.

**Coverage note — full extraction, no text layer.** This PDF has no text layer at all (scanned/
image-based book, confirmed via `pdftotext`/`pdfinfo`); every page was vision-read from rendered
page images (150dpi). Structure: pp.1-13 front matter (title, copyright, foreword, introduction,
page-guide legend), pp.14-93 Study Section (one kanji per page, 80 pages total, matching the book's
own "Kanji Page Locator" index grid on p.2 one-for-one), pp.94 JLPT background note, pp.96-105
Practice Section (blank stroke-practice grids only — no new vocabulary/grammar content, skipped per
the coverage rule), p.106 a "Kanji Stroke-Order Rules" reference chart (captured below as a grammar
point). No handwritten marginalia of any kind was found anywhere in this copy — every page is clean
typeset printed content; the vision-reading guard turned up nothing to flag.

Each Study Section page follows a fixed template (per the book's own "Page Guide," p.12): the
featured kanji in large print; an English keyword gloss; kun-yomi readings in hiragana and on-yomi
readings in katakana; 2-3 example compounds with hiragana readings and English glosses (an asterisk
marks a compound containing a kanji not taught in this volume); a stroke-order cell diagram; and a
single-panel-or-strip manga vignette using the kanji "in action" with full Japanese dialogue,
furigana over the featured kanji, and an English translation box. Per the coverage rule and
copyright discipline, each table row below paraphrases the manga scenario in a short, non-verbatim
description rather than reproducing the dialogue — the book's own dialogue/translation boxes are
not block-quoted.

**Non-redundant-supplement note:** this book's 80-kanji set substantially overlaps in raw character
inventory with kanji already captured in `002_guide_to_reading_writing_japanese.md`,
`004_writing_kanji_kana_book1.md`/`004c`, and `006_basic_kanji_book.md`/`006b`/`006c` (all teach the
same standard elementary kyōiku-kanji set, as is universal for any beginner kanji resource). This
book is retained as a full, non-abbreviated extraction rather than a delta-only supplement because
its actual content — a distinct mnemonic-manga illustration, scenario, and onomatopoeia set per
character, authored independently of the other books — is genuinely new material, not a restatement
of prior readings/compounds tables. Readings and compounds that duplicate earlier files are recorded
again here (this book's own figures, not copied from the other files) so each kanji's row is
self-contained; only the illustrative manga scenario and any book-specific usage note are the
non-redundant payload.

**Vision Reading Confidence:** `verified` throughout — every kanji, reading, and compound was
cross-checked against the page's own repeated appearances of the character (large display form,
stroke-order cells, and in-panel furigana-glossed instances all agree) and against the "Kanji Page
Locator" index grid on p.2, which independently lists the same 80 characters in the same order.

## Grammar points

### Kun-yomi vs. on-yomi, and the two-reading convention (Introduction, pp.9-11)

The book's introduction frames kanji as adapted from ancient Chinese script, now numbering nearly
50,000 characters total — of which Japanese schoolchildren are required to learn roughly 2,000, half
of which suffices for typical newspaper literacy. Most kanji carry at least two readings: a native
Japanese **kun-yomi** (written in hiragana in this book) and a Chinese-derived **on-yomi** (written
in katakana). The text gives a rule of thumb, explicitly flagged as having many exceptions: kanji
compounded with other kanji tend to use on-yomi, while kanji standing alone (or next to only kana)
tend to use kun-yomi; place names and family names skew toward kun-yomi regardless. The country name
"Japan" (日本, "Nihon") is cited as a notable on-yomi exception to the "isolated kanji → kun-yomi"
tendency. Rare/obscure additional readings beyond the two most common per character are deliberately
omitted from this volume to keep the lesson set manageable.

### Page-guide template (p.12, keyed to the reused 時 "TIME" page)

Every Study Section page follows a fixed 7-part layout: (1) the featured kanji in large display
form; (2) a common English-keyword definition; (3) kun-yomi readings in hiragana and on-yomi
readings in katakana; (4) 1-3 example compounds with hiragana readings and English glosses (an
asterisk flags a compound containing a kanji not taught elsewhere in this volume); (5) a stroke-order
cell diagram, strokes generally ordered top-to-bottom and left-to-right (full rule list referred to
the back-of-book chart); (6) a single manga panel/strip in which all dialogue is kana except the
single featured kanji (furigana given above it); (7) an English translation of the dialogue plus any
onomatopoeia.

### Kanji stroke-order rules (back-of-book reference chart, p.106)

A standalone chart gives the "Golden Rule" (left to right, top to bottom) and 8 numbered sub-rules,
each illustrated against kanji already taught earlier in this same volume: (1) horizontal strokes
are drawn left to right and kept parallel (一, 二, 三); (2) vertical/slanting strokes top to bottom
(丿, 川); (3) hook strokes go top-left to right, then turn downward (一, 冂, 日); (4) center strokes
are drawn before slanting left/right strokes (亅, 小); (5) outside strokes are drawn before inside
strokes, but the bottom-sealing stroke last (囗-type enclosures, illustrated with 四); (6) crisscross
strokes take horizontal before vertical (illustrated with 友); (7) diagonal strokes go left-hand
stroke before right-hand stroke (illustrated with 父); (8) horizontal-and-vertical "cutting" strokes
(e.g. the cross-stroke in 十) are drawn last.

### JLPT framing (p.94, "Take the Test!")

The Japanese Language Proficiency Test (JLPT), administered annually since 1984 by the Japanese
government and the Japan Foundation, has four levels under the numbering used at this book's 2004
printing: Level 4 (beginner) through Level 1 (expert). This volume explicitly markets itself as
covering "all 80 kanji students need to know to pass Level 4" — i.e., this 80-character set is an
externally-defined proficiency-test tier list, not the book's own invented frequency ranking, though
per the spec's Weight/Frequency column rule it is still not treated as corpus-frequency data in the
table above.

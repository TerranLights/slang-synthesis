## Grammar points

### Compound Nouns (Ch. 30, pp. 93–94)

A productive noun+noun, noun+adjective, and noun+verb(stem) compounding system: when a verb
participates in a compound it is reduced to its **Base-2 (stem) form**, and a True Adjective is
reduced to its bare stem. A phonological rule governs the compound juncture: if the second element
begins with an unvoiced consonant (`k, s, sh, t, ch, h, f, p`), it is **sometimes voiced**
(→`g, z, j, d, b`) in the compound — `kuchi`→`guchi` in `deguchi`, `to`→`do` in `amido`. The chapter's
closing section makes an explicitly etymological point not found elsewhere in this project's Japanese
corpus: many common Japanese **surnames and place names are themselves transparent compounds** of
everyday vocabulary (worked examples in the vocabulary table above) — directly relevant to later
naming-convention/slang-formation mechanics work, since it demonstrates the same compounding
productivity visible in ordinary nouns extends into onomastics.

### Common Noun Prefixes & Suffixes (Ch. 31, pp. 95–96)

A systematic table of ~25 productive bound affixes (full list sampled in the vocabulary table above),
covering nationality/style markers (`bei-` American, `wa-`/`yō-` Japanese-style/Western-style — an
explicit paired contrast), superlative/magnitude markers (`sai-` "the most," `sū-` "several"),
negation/privation (`mu-`, `fu-`), agentive/occupational suffixes (`-shu`, `-jin`), and a shop/dealer
suffix (`-ya`) independently cross-referable to the many `-ya`-suffixed shop-name vocabulary entries
already captured in Barron's back glossary (`035l`) and this book's own Ch. 46 vocabulary lists (e.g.
`sakanaya` "fish store," `hon'ya` "bookstore" — not re-tabulated here as they are straightforward
applications of this same productive suffix). This affix inventory has no counterpart in either `028`
or `035` and is directly load-bearing for later derivational/slang word-formation mechanics analysis.

### Honorifics: the Neutral/Humble/Exalted three-way verb system (Ch. 37, p. 108)

This book organizes honorific register distinctly from Barron's own treatment (`035e`): rather than a
flat list of suppletive pairs, it names **three parallel registers explicitly** — **Humble** (verbs
requiring the speaker or the speaker's in-group as subject), **Neutral** (no subject restriction; the
default verb in ordinary Normal-polite/Informal speech), and **Exalted** (verbs requiring a subject
*other than* the speaker/speaker's group) — and tabulates each Neutral verb against its Humble and
Exalted counterparts side by side (reproduced for the most irregular/suppletive entries in the
vocabulary table above). A genuinely distinctive structural note: several Humble or Exalted forms are
**not distinct verbs at all but a single suppletive form covering multiple Neutral verbs** — `mairu`
(Humble) covers both `kuru` "come" and `iku` "go"; `irassharu` (Exalted) covers `iru` "be located",
`iku` "go", *and* `kuru` "come" simultaneously; `gozaru`/`gozaimasu` serves as both Humble *and*
Exalted for `desu`/`dearu`, with no register distinction at all in that one case. The chapter also
gives two **general honorific-derivation formulas** productive beyond the suppletive-verb list: any
Neutral verb can be made Exalted via its **Passive Form** or via `o-[V2] ni naru`, and made Humble via
`o-[V2] shimasu`/`o-[V2] itashimasu` (the latter judged "more humble" than the former) — and separately
notes a small set of **formal/literary True-Adjective forms** (`yoroshū gozaimasu`, `chikō gozaimasu`,
`shirō gozaimasu`) built on a distinct final-vowel-lengthening pattern, not the ordinary adjective
paradigm.

### Numbers, Counters, Family, Health/Body, Clothing-verbs, and Common Expressions (Ch. 38–39, 42–46; pp. 110–130, sampled)

Largely redundant with Barron's own Numbers/Time/Classifiers (`035i`) and Days/Months/Family (`035j`)

### Appendix A: The Japanese Writing System — Kanji-specific content (pp. 136–140)

The Hiragana and Katakana syllabary/combination-character charts (pp. 135–137) duplicate Barron's own
writing-system treatment (`035`) and are not re-tabulated. The **Kanji section (pp. 137–140)**,
however, covers ground Barron's own chapter does not: it distinguishes **Pictographs** (characters
that are visibly stylized line-drawings of their referent, e.g. 火 "fire," 山 "mountain," 雨 "rain," each
shown with its own 3–4-step historical stylization sequence from a literal pictograph to the modern
character) from **Ideographs** (abstract-concept characters built from simple strokes rather than
pictures, e.g. 一/二/十 "one/two/ten," 上/中/大 "up/inside/big") and from **Complex Characters** (compounds
of two or more simpler characters combined for meaning, e.g. 木+木→森 "tree"+"tree"→"forest"; 日+月→明
"sun"+"moon"→"bright/light"; 門+耳→聞 "gate"+"ear"→"to hear/listen" — a genuinely illustrative
semantic-compounding mechanism). It then introduces **on'yomi** (Chinese-derived reading) versus
**kun'yomi** (native Japanese reading) — most of the ~1,945 Jōyō Kanji have both, over 700 have only
an on'yomi, about 35 only a kun'yomi — with five worked dual-reading examples (本, 日, 山, 生, 下), and
explains **Radicals**: seven named positional categories of repeated character-components (`Hen`
left-side, `Tsukuri` right-side, `Tare` overhead-and-drooping, `Kanmuri` the "crown" atop a character,
`Nyō`/`Nyū` wrapping down-the-left-and-under, `Kamae` an enclosing frame, `Ashi` "legs" supporting the
character's top) — among over 200 standard radicals, useful for character lookup and for native
speakers to verbally disambiguate homophonous kanji in conversation. Finally, **Punctuation** (p. 140)
notes Japanese uses its own period (`kuten`/`maru`, a small circle) and comma (`tōten`), reuses
Western brackets/quotation-mark equivalents (`kakko`, `kagi`/`kagikakko`), and **never uses hyphens,
colons, or semicolons** — with separate notation conventions (`nakasen` dash, `namigata` tilde-like
range marker) for vertical versus horizontal writing layouts, plus a worked side-by-side vertical/
horizontal text comparison example.

### Appendix B: Holidays & Historical Data (pp. 141–142)

Wholly new content with no counterpart elsewhere in this project's Japanese corpus. **Japanese Public
Holidays** (13 dated national holidays, Jan. 1 "O-shōgatsu" through Dec. 23 "Tennō Tanjōbi," the
Emperor's Birthday) plus four **Other Festivals** (Tanabata, O-bon, Setsubun, Hina Matsuri) not
official public holidays but culturally significant. **Historical Data**: a glossary of foundational
historical/political vocabulary (Yamato as Japan's original name, Amaterasu Ōmikami the legendary
ancestral sun goddess, tennō "emperor," shōgun "generalissimo/military leader," Edo as Tokyo's old
name, and the five historically dominant ruling clans/families — Ashikaga, Heike/Taira, Genji/
Minamoto, Fujiwara, Tokugawa). A full **Historical Periods** timeline from the Jōmon Period (ca. B.C.
7000) through the Modern Period (1912–present, subdivided into Taishō/Shōwa/Heisei eras) — useful
groundwork for later `attested_era`-style period-tagging if period-specific slang is later encountered
in this language's corpus. Finally, a set of 14 **Proverbs** given in romanized Japanese with literal
and idiomatic English glosses (sampled representatively in the vocabulary table above, prioritizing
proverbs whose literal and idiomatic meanings diverge most sharply, since those are most informative
for later figurative-language mechanics work).

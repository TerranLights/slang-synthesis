# Japanese — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: *Kanji Power: A Workbook for Mastering Japanese
Characters* (John Millen, Charles E. Tuttle Company, Rutland VT & Tokyo — first edition c. 1993,
190 pp.)

**Source:** *Kanji Power* teaches the first 240 of the Japanese Ministry of Education's 881
"commonly used" kanji (the set taught in the first two years of Japanese elementary school),
organized as **First Grade Characters** (kanji #1-80, pp. 9-64) and **Second Grade Characters**
(kanji #81-240, pp. 65-176), followed by an Answers section (pp. 177-190) for the book's own
quizzes/tests. No text layer (scanned image PDF, "Adobe Acrobat Image Conversion Plug-in" producer)
— vision-read via rendered page images (150dpi).

**Coverage note — representative sampling, not exhaustive (per dispatch instruction).** This is a
flat, dictionary-like progression of 240 individual kanji entries (not a narrative lesson structure
building grammar point-by-point), each following an identical fixed template: character + on-yomi
(katakana) + kun-yomi (hiragana, irregular readings asterisked) + English gloss + a one-line
etymology/mnemonic note + 2-4 numbered example sentences with English translation + 4-5 "common
compounds" (compound word + reading + gloss) + stroke count/stroke order grid. Given 240 such
entries, this file samples representatively across the whole progression rather than extracting all
240 exhaustively: every 3rd PDF page was rendered and read (pages 7, 10, 13 … 175, i.e. roughly
every 4th kanji entry on average, since quizzes/tests/review-tests are interspersed roughly every
6-24 entries and occupy some sampled page slots instead of a kanji box). This yielded 46 kanji
entries captured in full across the book's full range (#3 through #240, spanning both First Grade
and Second Grade sections), plus the complete 240-item "List of Second Grade Characters" index page
(p. 65, reproduced in the Notes below as a checklist of every kanji #81-240 by number — useful as a
pointer layer even though only a sampled subset got full detail rows). Quiz/test/review-test pages
themselves were skipped (drill content, not new vocabulary, per the coverage rule) except to confirm
page-range boundaries.

**Vision-reading guard:** no handwritten marginalia was found on any sampled page — this appears to
be a clean, unmarked copy (unlike some other Wave 1 Japanese sources). All content below is the
book's own printed typeset material.

**Duplicate-check performed:** grepped `established/` for distinctive strings from this book's early
pages (e.g. "Kanji Power describes the first 240 characters", "represents a side view of a person",
"flexed arm") — no match in any existing file. This book's content (individual kanji boxes with
on/kun readings + etymology note + common-compounds list, grade-ordered 1-240) does not overlap with
the already-extracted 006/006b/006c *Basic Kanji Book* (different kanji ordering/pedagogy) or 009
series *Intermediate Kanji Book* (post-240, more advanced kanji), so no redundancy with prior
`established/` content was found.

**Column notes for this file:** Source Type is `grammar_reference` for all rows (a kanji-learning
workbook). Transcription Confidence is `n/a` throughout (no transcript/subtitle content).
Vision Reading Confidence is `verified` for every row below — each kanji character, its readings,
and its compounds were cross-checked against the clearly legible, high-contrast printed boxes (large
display font for the headline character, clean body text for compounds), with no scan-artifact
ambiguity encountered on any sampled page.

## Grammar points

### 1. On-yomi / kun-yomi dual-reading presentation convention

Every entry in this book presents a target kanji with its **on-yomi** (Chinese-derived reading,
written in katakana) first, then its **kun-yomi** (native Japanese reading, written in hiragana),
followed by a general English gloss. Readings the book considers irregular or rare are marked with
an asterisk (e.g. 人 kun-reading ひと is standard, but a compound reading like 大人 "おとな" would be
flagged where it deviates from the regular on/kun pattern). No romanization (rōmaji) is used
anywhere in the text — the author states in the Introduction that this is deliberate, since
dependence on romanized transliteration would undermine the goal of developing reading/writing
fluency. *Furigana* (small hiragana readings printed above compound-word kanji in example sentences)
is used throughout to keep as-yet-untaught kanji accessible within example sentences, per the
Introduction's stated rationale.

### 2. Etymology/mnemonic note as a memorization device

Each kanji box includes a short right-column note on the character's graphic origin or compositional
logic (e.g. 人 "represents a side view of a person"; 力 "originally represented a flexed arm"; 毎
"depicts a mother wearing a headpiece"). These notes are presented as memory aids rather than
rigorous etymology, and several explicitly hedge uncertainty (e.g. for 池: "combines water and
scorpion, which for unclear reasons came to mean pond"; for 万: "derived from a pictograph of a
scorpion... From phonetic borrowing... the character became associated with ten thousand"). This
convention is worth noting for mechanics-analysis purposes: it is a pedagogical device for a
foreign-learner audience, not a claim of certain etymological fact, and should not be treated as an
authoritative etymological source on its own.

### 3. Stroke order and stroke-count pedagogy

Every entry provides the character's total stroke count, a numbered stroke-order diagram (strokes
traced in sequence with directional arrows), and a short calligraphic note on a specific
stroke-formation detail for that character (e.g. "keep aligned," "this stroke longer than one above
it," "don't mistake for [similar-looking character]" — e.g. 人 vs. 入, 右 vs. 石, 毎 vs. 母, 矢 vs.
失). A blank practice grid follows for the learner to copy the character repeatedly. The
Introduction frames handwriting practice as "in part an intellectual feat, but also a manual skill,"
and recommends writing each character "over and over again" until stroke order becomes automatic.

### 4. Graded/leveled character introduction tied to Japanese elementary curriculum

The book's own stated design principle: it covers exactly the 240 kanji corresponding to Japan's
official Ministry-of-Education "first and second grade" elementary-school kanji curriculum (a
subset of the larger 881-character jōyō/commonly-used list), introduced in strict numbered order
(#1-240) with quizzes after every 6 characters, tests after every ~24 characters, and cumulative
review tests after every 80 characters. This is a pedagogically-graded progression rather than a
frequency-ranked one — per the spec's column rules, this justifies the coarse "contemporary (source
published)" Attested Era value for all entries, but does **not** justify treating early-numbered
kanji as higher-frequency than later ones; the ordering reflects elementary-curriculum sequencing,
not an independent frequency count.

## Grammar points

### Tara vs. eba for giving advice (p.138-139)
*-Tara*, usually paired with *doo-desu-ka*/*ikaga-desu-ka* (or bare *doo*/no particle at all in casual
speech), is the common, warm way to give advice. *-Eba* is not typically used for advice at all; when
it is, it reads as detached — implying the speaker doesn't especially care what the listener decides.
*-Eba* much more commonly pairs instead with *ii* to simply grant permission for an action, a distinct
function from advice-giving.

### Soo omoimasu, reinforced: soo/koo/aa/doo as bare adverbs (p.140-141)
Reinforcing the earlier-established rule: *soo*, *koo*, *aa*, and *doo* function as plain adverbs
directly before a verb or adjective, taking no quotative *to* — *soo omoimasu* and *soo-da-to
omoimasu* are both correct, *soo-to omoimasu* remains wrong, and the chapter explicitly frames this as
a persistent mistake even a fairly advanced learner keeps relapsing into.

### Te as a restricted agentive suffix (p.142-143)
*Te* ("hand") forms "a person who does X" nouns from physical-action verb stems (*hanashite*
"speaker," *kaite* "buyer"), but not from mental/psychological-action verbs (*wakari-te*, *kangae-te*
are both ungrammatical) — and even among physical-action verbs, it's most natural for actions that
affect another person. A role needs to be a recognized, defined one for *te* to apply: *hashirite*
works for a competitor in a race but not an ordinary hurrying pedestrian, and a restaurant customer is
never *tabete*.

### Ichido vs. ikkai: interchangeable for counting, but ichido alone covers "someday" (p.144-145)
Both *-do* and *-kai* counter series work interchangeably for plain frequency counts, with *ikkai*-type
forms preferred in factual/scientific/large-number contexts. But when "one time" means an indefinite
future occasion ("I'd like to see you sometime") rather than literally one occurrence, only *ichido*
works — *ikkai* cannot substitute in that extended sense.

### Yoru's te-form as a casual command, and its productive -yori stem (p.146-147)
*Yoru* ("draw near") has a te-form, *yotte*, commonly used as a familiar-register command standing in
for the fuller *-te-kudasai*. The bound stem *-yori* attaches productively to nouns, adjectives, and
verbs to build further "draw near/lean toward" compounds (*chika-yoru*, *mochi-yoru*, *yori-kakaru*),
and *yoru* itself is the set verb for inviting someone to drop in at one's home.

### Ni kanshite vs. ni kansuru: the verb-modifying/noun-modifying split in formal relational phrases (p.148-149)
A family of formal te-form-derived relational phrases (*ni kanshite* "concerning," *ni yotte*
"depending on," *ni oite* "at/in," *ni totte* "to/for," *ni taishite* "to/against") each have a
distinct form for modifying a verb (the te-form itself) versus modifying a following noun (a separate
fixed form, e.g. *ni kansuru*/*ni kanshite-no*) — the two are not freely interchangeable across those
two syntactic roles, which the chapter frames as a specialized instance of a more general, familiar
Japanese pattern (compare the ordinary adverbial/noun-modifying contrast *mae-ni* vs. *mae-no*).

### Watashi-ni sounds translated; te-kureru/te-kuru carry the benefactive/directional load instead (p.150-151)
Literally saying something was done "to me" (*watashi-ni...*) is understandable but reads as a direct
translation from English — natural Japanese instead uses *-te-kureru*/*-te-kudasaru* (when the speaker
wants to emphasize gratitude) or the more neutral *-te-kuru* (simply stating the action was directed
toward the speaker), frequently omitting the agent entirely once it's contextually obvious.
*Watashi-ni* is reserved for genuinely emphatic contexts, such as contrasting "to me" against "to my
sister."

### Gaman-suru's three senses, from childhood memory to psychological self-restraint (p.152-153)
*Gaman-suru* spans "to endure/be patient" (strongly tied to childhood memories of a parent's
*gaman-shinasai*, and to a broader stoic cultural value around forbearing hardship), "to be satisfied
with something not the best," and "to suppress oneself psychologically" toward someone else's
difficult behavior. Related compounds *gaman-ga tarinai* ("lacking in self-restraint") and
*gaman-zuyoi* ("strong in perseverance") extend the same root into criticism and praise respectively.

### Kochira/sochira/achira as the real pronoun system, closing the book's broader theme (p.154-155)
*Kochira* ("this direction") substitutes for *watashi* specifically to frame "my side" against an
implicit or explicit "your side" — paralleled by *sochira* ("your side," for *anata*) and *achira*

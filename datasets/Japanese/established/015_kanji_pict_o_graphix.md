(Stone Bridge Press, Berkeley, California; bibliographic year 1992 per publisher record — the

**Coverage note (whole-book plan).** The book's main dictionary runs **over 1,225 numbered kanji
entries** (not literally "1,000+" as the title rounds it — the last entry observed is #1225,
"RESTRAIN, PRESS DOWN" 抑) across 11 thematic chapters (World, Food, Animals, People, Body, Spirit,
Power, Learn, Tools, Places, Journey), each built from a handful of mnemonic "spread" pages (4–8
entries per 2-page spread, each giving a keyword, the kanji, its on/kun readings, a short mnemonic
sentence, and cross-referenced element numbers). This is a dictionary-style reference far beyond
the project's per-chunk scope, so per this project's standing practice for oversized dictionary-type
sources, the **kanji dictionary itself was representative-sampled, not exhaustively extracted** —
see 015b/015c for the sampled entries, spread across the entry-number range 1–1225 and across most
of the 11 chapters (one or two full 2-page spreads sampled per several chapters, chosen to spread
across the book's whole numeric/thematic progression rather than clustering near the front).
**The Kana section below, by contrast, is small and self-contained (46 hiragana + 46 katakana base
forms, plus diacritic/combination notes) and is extracted in full**, consistent with the coverage
rule's distinction between a bounded closed set (kana) and an open-ended large dictionary (kanji).

The book also has a short **Notes** appendix (pp. 203–206 printed) giving brief etymological notes
on ~100 kanji *elements* (not full entries) too minor or obscure to cover in the main spreads (e.g.
"n–6 **wet** — part of a phonetic element meaning 'wet'"); this is reference apparatus for

**Diacritic and combination notes (not separate kana, but structural rules the book calls out on
the same pages):**

- **Handakuten (゜, "nonvoiced"/pop mark):** changes は/ひ/ふ/へ/ほ-row (ha/hi/fu/he/ho) to the
  "popping" series ぱ/ぴ/ぷ/ぺ/ぽ (pa/pi/pu/pe/po). Book's mnemonic: a soda-can "pop" sound.
- **Dakuten (゛, "voiced" mark):** changes the か/さ/た/は-row series to their voiced counterparts
  — が/ざ/だ/ば (ga/za/da/ba) series. Book's mnemonic: "vocal cord vibrations."
- **Small ゃ/ゅ/ょ (ya/yu/yo) combining with i-row kana:** turns き/し/ち/に/ひ/み/り (ki/shi/chi/
  ni/hi/mi/ri) into the palatalized きゃ/しゃ/ちゃ/にゃ/ひゃ/みゃ/りゃ (kya/sha/cha/nya/hya/mya/rya)
  series and their kyu/kyo, shu/sho, etc. counterparts.
- **Small っ/ッ (doubling tsu):** silently doubles the following consonant when it precedes another
  kana, e.g. ざっし (zasshi) — the book's own illustrative example.
- **The kana う/ウ (u) used after an o-row or u-row kana** lengthens the vowel (ko→kō, ku→kū), per
  the book's own "DOUBLERS" note box.

## Grammar points

### Rowley's four-category kanji taxonomy (Introduction, pp. 7–10 printed)

The book's introduction frames all kanji as falling into four descriptive categories, used
throughout the dictionary's own cross-referencing system:

1. **Pictographs** — stylized drawings of physical objects (e.g. 川 "river", 山 "mountain", 門
   "gate" — each still visually resembling the object).
2. **Symbols** — simple graphic/logical designs for abstract notions (e.g. 上 "over", 下 "under",
   中 "middle").
3. **Ideographs** — two pictographs/symbols combined into a related idea (e.g. 日 "sun" + 月 "moon"
   = 明 "bright").
4. **Phono-ideographs** — the largest group (the author estimates roughly 80% of all kanji),
   combining a phonetic element (clue to pronunciation) with a semantic element/radical (clue to
   meaning category). The semantic element is usually a stand-alone kanji or radical (e.g. 木
   "tree" used as a radical generally signals "made of wood" — 柳 "willow", 材 "timber").

### Cross-reference and Notes apparatus

Each entry's mnemonic schematic cross-references the sequential entry numbers of its component
elements (e.g. "☐ mouth 566 ☐ bird 314" under 鳴 "non-human cry" means that entry combines the
elements numbered 566 and 314 elsewhere in the book). When an element has no stand-alone kanji/
radical form of its own, or is too graphically complex for a simple box schematic, the book
instead references a **Notes-section entry** (format "n–000") at the back of the book (pp.
203–206 printed) rather than a sequential cross-reference number — e.g. "n–40 **mask** — from a
pictograph of a summer festival mask" explains an element used in 夏 "summer" (entry 40) that isn't
itself a free-standing character. The author states this etymological material is substantially
drawn from Kenneth Henshall's *A Guide to Remembering Japanese Characters* (independently already
extracted in this project as `003_guide_to_remembering_japanese_characters.md`).

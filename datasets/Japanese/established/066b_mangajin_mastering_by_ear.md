## Grammar points

### Gozaimasu euphonic adjective shift (PL4 sound changes)

Adjectives combined with gozaimasu undergo a predictable vowel shift: -ai endings become -ō
(hayai → hayō), -ui endings become -ū (samui → samū), and -shii endings become -shū (not
illustrated with a non-idiomatic example, but medetai → medetō follows the same -ai→-ō pattern).
This produces the two idiomatic greetings covered in Lesson 6: ohayō gozaimasu ("good morning,"
literally built from hayai "early") and omedetō gozaimasu ("congratulations," built from medetai
"joyous"). Both are now purely idiomatic and not understood literally by native speakers in normal
use. (p.42)

### baka's morphological productivity

Baka functions across every major word-class slot relevant to insult formation: bare noun/
interjection (baka!), -na adjectival form (baka-na koto, sonna baka-na!!), and a fully productive
compounding slot with both generic suffixes (-mono/-mon "person," -yarō "guy/fellow") and specific
nouns (baka-neko "stupid cat," baka-musuko "idiot son," baka-shōjiki "excessive honesty"). The
source frames this productivity itself as evidence baka is Japanese's single most load-bearing
insult/expletive word — not a single fixed phrase but a generative slot. (pp.66-69)

### Learner-error pattern typology ("Gaijin Bloopers," Part I)

Author Karen Sandness's essay (not slang data per se, but a useful typology for understanding
register/naturalness judgments a synthesis phase might draw on) names three recurring learner
error-patterns: (1) *"If It's Good Enough For English"* — treating English phonology/word order
as the default and failing to notice Japanese-specific distinctions (su/tsu, vowel length);
(2) *"This Language Makes No Sense Anyway"* ("TLMNSA") — treating Japanese grammar as arbitrary and
guessing wildly rather than learning real patterns; (3) *Dictionary Dependence* — picking the wrong
sense of a multi-meaning English headword from a bilingual dictionary (e.g. picking "close" as in
"close a door" instead of "close" as in "nearby," producing a nonsense sentence). A fourth named
attitude, *"Good Enough,"* describes learners/long-term residents who plateau at an error-ridden,
overly casual register and resist correction. (pp.32-33)

## Grammar points

### Trailing *...to*/*...tara* and the general pattern of leaving conclusions unstated
Several columns in this range converge on the same discourse norm: ending a sentence with a
connective (*...to*, *...kara*, *...kedo*, *...ga*) and leaving the logically implied continuation
unsaid is not a speech defect but a marker of reserve/politeness — stating the implied conclusion
explicitly can come across as presumptuous, demanding, or self-asserting (pp.56-57, 62-63, 76-77).
*...to* leaves more room for listener interpretation than *...tara*, which more specifically implies
a following "how about it?"-type question, making *...tara* phrasing comparatively more direct
(pp.56-57).

### *hai*: willingness, not affirmation
*Hai* fundamentally marks the speaker's willingness to help, obey, or attend — not literal agreement
with a yes/no proposition. This explains why *Hai* can correctly preface a negative-content answer
(*Hai, orimasen*, "Yes, he's not here") and why it also functions as backchannel *aizuchi* and as
"here you are" when handing something over (pp.68-69).

### Politeness constraints on checking a listener's understanding
Direct comprehension-checks (*Wakarimashita-ka*, even politely rephrased as *owakari-ni
narimashita-ka*) are impolite toward a superior because *wakaru* implies judging the listener's own
mental capacity. Polite Japanese instead asks permission to continue (*Yoroshii-deshoo-ka*) or
dangles an unfinished statement to invite the listener to signal readiness (pp.70-71).

### *kara* vs. *kedo* in requests/invitations vs. plain factual statements
In plain factual statements *kara* ("because") and *kedo* ("but") are near-opposites. But when the
sentence is implying an unstated request/invitation, the two converge in function while differing in
attitude: *...kara* presents the request as a matter of course (slightly presumptuous), while
*...kedo* signals the speaker's hesitation about imposing, making *...kedo*-final phrasing generally
the more polite choice (pp.76-77).

### Consonant gemination is phonemically load-bearing in colloquial contractions
The *-tatte*-type "even if" contraction shows that doubling a consonant (e.g. *t* vs. *tt*) in rapid
colloquial speech is not a minor pronunciation nicety but creates genuine minimal pairs with
unrelated meanings (*itatte* "even if I exist" vs. *ittatte* "even if I say," etc.) (pp.78-79).

## Grammar points

### Expressing desire to possess an object: hoshī
hoshī ("want [an object]") is an i-adjective (not a verb) that expresses the speaker's desire to
own something, or asks what the listener wants; the desired object is marked with ga, not o.
Inflects fully as an i-adjective: hoshī desu / hoshikunai desu / hoshikatta desu / hoshikunakatta
desu. Pattern: noun1(person)-wa noun2(object)-ga hoshī desu. p. 117.

### Giving and asking reasons (1): dōshite and ~kara
dōshite ("why?") asks for a reason and can appear mid- or sentence-initial position. kara
("because") attaches to the end of the reason-giving sentence, which must precede the
consequence/result sentence it explains: "[reason sentence] kara, [result sentence]." — the
reverse clause order from English "because". p. 118.

### Ownership: arimasu (extending Unit 8)
The same arimasu verb introduced in Unit 8 for existence ("there is/are") doubles as a possession
verb: noun1(person)-wa noun2(object)-ga arimasu — "Watashi wa netsu ga arimasu" ("I have a
fever"). This is now the third attested overload of a core existence/possession verb's surface
form in this grammar (parallel to the multi-function de particle flagged in Units 6-7), worth
flagging for mechanics-analysis. p. 121.

### Giving and asking reasons (2): sentence1 kara, sentence2
A fuller statement of the kara-linking pattern already introduced with dōshite: any two clauses
can be joined this way even without an explicit dōshite question preceding them, with the reason
clause first: "Fujisan wa kirē desu kara, yūmē desu" ("Mt. Fuji is beautiful so it is famous").
p. 121.

### Illness vocabulary
A fixed set of body-part-ga-itai/warui idioms expresses specific ailments (atama ga itai
"headache", ha ga itai "toothache", nodo ga itai "sore throat", onaka ga itai "stomachache",
kimochi ga warui "feel sick") plus one exceptional construction using arimasu rather than an
i-adjective (netsu ga arimasu "have a fever") — flagged in-text by the book itself as an
exception to watch for. p. 120.

### Expressing gratitude: arigatō gozaimasu vs. arigatō gozaimashita
The two forms are not free variants: arigatō gozaimasu thanks for an ongoing/general kindness,
while arigatō gozaimashita specifically thanks for a favor or action that has already been
completed (e.g. thanking someone the day after they helped you). p. 123.

### Giving and receiving: agemasu / moraimasu
agemasu ("to give") and moraimasu ("to receive") form a symmetric pair: noun1(person)-wa
noun2(person)-ni noun3(object)-o agemasu/moraimasu, with the recipient (for agemasu) or source
(for moraimasu) marked by ni. Critically, when the speaker is personally involved as giver or
receiver, the sentence must be phrased from the speaker's own viewpoint rather than from an
outside observer's perspective — an empathy/viewpoint-alignment constraint on verb choice that
foreshadows the book's later, more elaborate kureru/ageru/morau giving-verb system (not yet
introduced by name at this point). pp. 123-124.

### Katakana of Unit 10: combined sounds (brief note)
This unit's katakana supplement covers ya/yu/yo-glide digraphs (kya, sha, cha, nya, hya, pya,
mya, rya and their voiced counterparts) and a set of small-vowel digraphs (ウィ/ウェ/ウォ, シェ/ジェ,
ティ/ディ, ファ/フィ/フェ/フォ) used specifically to transliterate foreign loanwords and names with
sounds outside the native Japanese phonemic inventory, plus ad-hoc conventions for v (バ-row or a
dedicated ヴ series), th (approximated via サ/ソ/ス), and r-colored vowels (lengthened via ー). This
is a genuinely useful loanword-adaptation reference for understanding how Japanese phonologically
nativizes foreign names — flagged for the mechanics-analysis phase even though the character
inventory itself duplicates Wave 1 coverage. p. 126.

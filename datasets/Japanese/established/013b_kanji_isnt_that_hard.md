## Grammar points

### Rule 6 — Three signifies many; representing a lot of one thing (三つは多いことです／それはたくさんあります)

When a source picture contained an indeterminate but large number of some repeated element (a
tree's many branches/roots, a shellfish's many spiral turns, a crowd of people), the kanji
regularizes this to exactly **three** repetitions of the relevant stroke/component — three stands
in for "many" as a general stylization principle, not a literal count. (pp. 33–37)

### Rule 7 — Writing from left to right; writing from top to bottom (左から右へ書きます／上から下へ書きます)

Kanji stroke order follows fixed directional conventions independent of the source picture's own
line directions: horizontal strokes are always written left-to-right (attributed to the convention
of writing with the right hand in old China), and strokes/components stacked vertically are written
top-to-bottom (also an old Chinese convention for arranging characters). These conventions hold even
when the source picture's own lines ran the opposite direction (e.g. 火'"s flames visually rise
bottom-to-top; 光's beams radiate outward including right-to-left on the left side) — the stroke-
order convention overrides the picture's original line direction. (pp. 38–42)

### Rule 8 — Adjectives can also become pictures; their meaning is expressed by their pictures (形容詞も絵になります／そういう絵で表します)

Not just nouns but adjectives, too, can be rendered as pictures when the quality has a describable
shape or scenario: "big"/"small" are shown by a person's arms+legs spread wide vs. not spread (大 vs.
小); "high" by a tall building's floors stacked (高); "early" by the sun just risen low over a
grassy plain (早); "old" by news repeated by many mouths (古); "cheap" by a wife's unpaid labor under
one's own roof (安); "cold" by a person huddled under grass insulation beneath a roof (寒). (pp.
43–47)

### Rule 9 — Verbs can also become pictures; their meaning is expressed by pictures of the action (動詞も絵になります／そうする絵で表します)

Verbs, too, can be rendered pictorially by depicting the action itself (a standing person for "to
stand," 立) rather than an arbitrary unrelated picture (a dog or cat wouldn't convey "to stand").
Worked examples in this rule show verbs built from a picture of the action/scenario directly: 回
(circular motion), 比 (two similar people-shapes side by side, "to compare"), 囲 (an enclosure built
around a well, "to surround"), 困 (a tree hemmed in by an enclosure, "to be distressed"), 聞 (an ear
inside a gate, "to hear"), 助 (piling up strength/arm-shapes, "to help"), 鳴 (a bird's mouth, "to
chirp"), 騒 (a hand or insect touching a horse, "to raise a clamor"). (pp. 48–52)

### Rule 10 — Expressing verbs with signs; imagine the actions (サインで動詞を表します／動き方を考えましょう)

Extends the Rule 2 stem+sign model specifically to verbs: certain **signs** (付随部分) mark a kanji
as a verb and often characterize *how* the action is performed. 攵 (a hand + a stick, ノ+又) is the
general "verb sign" seen in 攻/散/敬/数; 殳 is a specialized variant of 攵 marking specifically an
*evil* action, seen in 殴/殺 (both built with a sign for "to fasten," メ, plus 殳); 之 is a third verb
sign, glossed as representing "an animal that goes forward and backward," seen in 運/遊. Reading a
compound verb kanji means identifying its stem (what is being acted upon/with) and then its sign
(what kind of action, and sometimes its moral valence) separately. (pp. 53–57)

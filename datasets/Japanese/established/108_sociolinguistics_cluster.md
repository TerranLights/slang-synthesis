**Morpheme breakdown (agglutinative features of Japanese; this chapter's own worked examples).**

> **taberu** "(I) eat" = *tabe-* ("to eat," verb stem) + *-ru* (nonpast-tense marker)

> **tabeta** "(I) ate" = *tabe-* ("to eat") + *-ta* (past-tense marker)

> **tabehajimeta** "(I) began to eat" = *tabe-* ("to eat") + *hajime-* ("to begin") + *-ta* (past-tense marker) — the source's own illustration that a single Chinese-derived "immutable" root like /chī/ "eat" corresponds to a productive, multi-morpheme Japanese composite, contrasting Chinese's isolating typology with Japanese's agglutinative one.

> **yuudachi** "early-evening summer shower" = *yuu* ("evening") + *tachi* (voiced form of *tatsu*/"stand," the native-Japanese reading cognate to the Sino-Japanese *ritsu-* seen in *risshun* etc.) — literally "the beginning/standing-up of the evening."

## Grammar points

### Agglutinative vs. isolating typology (Chinese borrowing problem)

Chinese is isolating (each word has one immutable form, e.g. /chī/ "eat" regardless of tense);
Japanese is agglutinative (a single root like *tabe-* combines with productive tense/aspect
morphemes). This mismatch is why ancient Japanese scholars adapting Chinese characters had to
invent multiple ad hoc strategies — using characters purely for their sound value (not meaning) to
write Japanese grammatical particles that have no Chinese equivalent — a problem the chapter
illustrates by constructing a mock English sentence written entirely in repurposed Chinese
characters (pp. 246–247).

### Kana derivation from cursive-abbreviated Chinese characters

Hiragana derives from heavily cursive-simplified whole Chinese characters used phonetically (e.g.
波 "wave" /ha/ → 皮 → は, now read /wa/ as the topic particle). Katakana derives instead from
single *radicals* (partial elements) extracted from Chinese characters used by Buddhist monks as
reading aids (e.g. 阿 /a/ → the left radical 阝→ ア). The two syllabaries therefore have different
historical derivation mechanisms despite serving parallel modern functions (p. 247–248).

### Verb-final (SOV) word order with free constituent order before the verb

Japanese requires the verb last but allows the subject and object to appear in either order before
it (*Taroo-ga tegami-o kakimasu* / *tegami-o Taroo-ga kakimasu*, both grammatical), unlike
English's fixed SVO. Noun phrases are freely omitted ("pro-drop") when understood from context
(p. 255–256).

### Postpositions, not prepositions

Japanese has roughly seventy postpositional particles instead of English-style prepositions: case
particles *-ga* (subject), *-o* (object), *-no* (genitive); locative/directional *-ni* ("at,"
static location) vs. *-de* ("at," place of activity, also "by means of"); *-e* ("to," direction);
*-kara*/*-made* ("from"/"to, until," both temporal and spatial) (pp. 256–257).

### Sentence-final particles encode speaker attitude

A closed set of six particles — *-ne, -yo, -wa, -zo, -na, -sa* — attach to the end of a sentence to
mark the speaker's stance rather than propositional content: *-ne* ≈ English tag-question
(seeking agreement/softening assertiveness), *-yo* marks insistence/emphatic assertion (p. 257).
This is a genuine register/pragmatics mechanism distinct from the verb-conjugation honorific
system covered below.

### Topic marker -wa vs. subject marker -ga

*-ga* marks genuinely new information to the hearer; *-wa* marks shared/previously-established
information, generic statements ("Whales are mammals"), or any element (not only the subject)
that the speaker is choosing to make the sentence "about." Contrastive comparisons between two
referents also trigger *-wa* on both. This full discourse-pragmatic (not merely syntactic)
explanation of the wa/ga distinction is presented as one of the "central problems in learning to
speak Japanese" (pp. 264–269).

### Left-branching relative clauses (no relative pronoun)

Unlike English's right-branching embedding ("the house **that Jack built**"), Japanese embeds
relative clauses to the *left* of the head noun with no relative pronoun at all: *kore-ga Jakku-ga
tateta uchi desu* literally "this Jack-built house is," i.e. "This is the house that Jack built."
The source works through the full ten-clause "House That Jack Built" nursery rhyme in Japanese to
demonstrate cumulative left-branching (pp. 259–260).

### Backward gapping

In coordinate sentences with identical repeated verbs, English deletes all but the *first* verb
("John ate an apple, Jack a pear, and Bill a peach") — forward gapping. Japanese deletes all but
the *last* verb instead — backward gapping — because the verb must stay sentence-final (p. 260).

### Honorific system: three-way sonkeigo/kenjoogo/teineigo split

Japanese deference is expressed through three distinct and independently combinable grammatical
subsystems, not a single politeness scale:
- **Sonkeigo** (honoring expressions) — raise the status of the person being discussed/addressed.
- **Kenjoogo** (humbling expressions) — lower the status of the speaker (or the speaker's in-group)
  relative to the hearer/referent.
- **Teineigo** (polite expressions) — the *-mas-*/*desu* ending family, marking the speaker's
  general respect toward the hearer and the speaker's own self-estimated social position,
  independent of who is being talked about.

A single underlying proposition ("Sakai drew a map for Suzuki") is shown re-rendered across five
distinct relational situations (equal-status casual; equal-status with an honoring compound verb
*-te age-*; Sakai much lower status via *o-...-ni nar-*; Sakai much higher status via humbling
*o-...su*; and asymmetric honoring/humbling via *-te kudasar-* / *-te itadak-* when Suzuki is
lower status than Sakai but the speaker favors Suzuki) — six grammatically distinct sentences for
one event (pp. 286–289).

### Ingroup/outgroup (uchi/soto) governs honorific choice, overriding literal status

A speaker always humbles members of their own ingroup (family, company, school) when speaking to
an outsider, and honors any member of someone *else's* group — **regardless of that member's
actual rank**, e.g. a junior employee answering an outside call about a department head named
Mori must say *Mori-wa tadaima gaishutsu shite orimasu* (using the humbling verb *ori-*, never
*Mori-san*) even if the caller/speaker outranks Mori within the company, because the frame is
company-insider vs. outsider, not individual seniority. When the two factors (ingroup/outgroup vs.
seniority) conflict, ingroup/outgroup status wins (pp. 290–294). This is the same *uchi/soto*
mechanism independently documented in Yamada's *Different Games, Different Rules* (file 108c) —
cross-source convergence on a named structural principle, captured independently by two different
authors working from different fieldwork.

### Kinship terms split along an outsider/insider axis, not just a respect axis

Japanese kinship vocabulary is organized into three parallel columns: (1) a neutral term used when
*talking about* one's own family *to* outsiders (e.g. *chichi* "father," *haha* "mother"); (2) a
respectful/affectionate term used *within* the family, with prefix *o-* and suffix *-san*/*-chan*
(e.g. *o-too-san* "father," *o-kaa-san* "mother"); and younger relatives (children, nephews,
nieces) are referred to by given name + *-chan*/*-san* in both contexts rather than by a kinship
noun at all. This is a three-way, not two-way, register system (pp. 282–283).

### Personal pronouns gendered and graded by roughness

Japanese first/second-person pronouns split along two axes simultaneously — gender-restriction and
formality/roughness: *watakushi* (formal "I," both genders) > *watashi* (normal "I," both genders)
> *boku* (casual "I," male-restricted) > *ore* (rough "I," male-restricted); parallel ladder for
"you": *anata* (normal) > *kimi* (casual, male-only) > *omae* (rough, male-only). Third-person
*kare*/*kanojo* ("he"/"she") carry a secondary colloquial meaning of "boyfriend"/"girlfriend" (pp.
283–284). Source notes Japanese pronouns behave syntactically more like ordinary nouns than true
pronouns (can be modified by adjectives, e.g. *utsukushii anata* "beautiful you").

### Grammaticalized deference devices beyond the honorific verb system

Even languages without a dedicated honorific grammar (like English) use non-deferential
grammatical devices deferentially — tag questions softening commands ("Come in, **won't you**?"),
impersonal statements replacing direct requests ("Dinner is served" for "come eat"), past-tense
hedging ("I **was wondering** if..." outranks "I wonder if..." in politeness), and euphemism for
taboo referents (English "rest room" chain vs. Japanese *(o-)tearai* "place to wash hands,"
*keshoo-shitsu* "powder room," *toire* < English "toilet," *habakari* "awe/deference," *go-fujoo*
"[respectful]-impurity"). Japanese layers these general devices *on top of* its dedicated
honorific morphology rather than instead of it (pp. 284–286).

### Sense-of-adversity passive (passive of inconvenience)

A Japanese passive construction can mark that the grammatical subject was inconvenienced by an
event even when that subject has no logical object role in the active sentence — "I was subjected
to it raining" (*watashi-wa ame-ni fur-are-mashita*) conveys annoyance at being rained on, a
reading with no direct English equivalent. Adding *kekkyoku* ("ultimately") can flip the implied
evaluation to positive outcome despite the adversative passive form (pp. 272–273).

### -gar suffix marks sensations of "nonself"

Japanese grammatically restricts first-person direct sensation/emotion statements ("I'm cold," "I'm
sad") to the person actually experiencing them; to report someone *else's* sensation as a bare
fact (not hearsay/inference) requires the suffix *-gar-*, marking that the speaker is inferring an
external, observable "nonself" state rather than directly reporting felt experience (pp. 272–274).

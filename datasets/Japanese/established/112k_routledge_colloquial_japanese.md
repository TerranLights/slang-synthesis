## Grammar points

### Adjectives (systematic review + expansion)
Unit 11 supplies a large new batch of i-adjectives (amai/karai/yawarakai/hiroi/semai/omoi/
karui/akarui/kurai/ōi/sukunai/urusai/kowai/utsukushī) and na-adjectives (kenkō(na)/jiyū(na)/
anzen(na)), explicitly reviewing the i-/na-adjective split first introduced in Unit 5. p. 130.

### Connecting sentences with i-adjectives, na-adjectives, and nouns
To join two clauses where the first ends in a predicate adjective or noun+desu (rather than using
to/soshite/sorekara for simple noun lists), the predicate itself changes form instead of adding a
separate conjunction: an i-adjective drops its final i and adds kute (kunakute for negative) —
ōkii → ōkikute, with the irregular ī "good" → yokute (not *ikute); a na-adjective or noun drops
desu and adds de (dewanakute for negative) — shizuka desu → shizuka de, gakusē desu → gakusē de.
When the two joined clauses disagree in polarity (one affirmative, one negative), ga ("but") is
used instead of this kute/de linking: "Kono resutoran wa kirē dewa arimasen ga oishī desu" ("This
restaurant isn't clean but its food is tasty"). pp. 130-132.

### Weather vocabulary and talking about the weather
A five-term weather-condition set (hare "sunny", kumori "cloudy", kaze "wind", ame "rain", yuki
"snow") combines with desu/deshita for weather statements ("Kyō wa hare desu" — "It is fine
weather today"); kumori tokidoki ame ("cloudy with occasional rain") shows the frequency-adverb
system from Unit 7 reused in forecast language. p. 135.

### Expressing a change of state: adjective + narimasu
narimasu ("to become") attaches after an adjective to describe a transition into a new state. For
i-adjectives, change the final i to ku before narimasu (atatakai → atataku narimasu "becomes
warm"), with the same yoku-not-*iku irregularity as the kute-form (ī → yoku narimasu "becomes
good"). For na-adjectives and nouns, add ni before narimasu (genki → genki ni narimasu "becomes
well/better"; 21-sai → 21-sai ni narimasu "turns 21"). pp. 135-136.

### Changing adjectives into adverbs
i-adjectives: change the final i to ku (karui → karuku "lightly", hayai → hayaku "early/
quickly") — the identical ku-suffixation also used for narimasu above, now generalized as an
adverb-forming rule in its own right. na-adjectives: change na to ni (shizuka(na) → shizukani
"quietly", jōzu(na) → jōzuni "skillfully"). pp. 136-137.

### Kanji: historical background and kun-yomi/on-yomi
Kanji were imported from China roughly 2,000 years ago by Japanese monks, predating any native
Japanese writing system; hiragana and katakana were later developed by simplifying/abstracting
parts of kanji. Modern compulsory education (per a 2010 Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports,
Science and Technology list) requires 2,136 kanji; this textbook teaches roughly 60 across Units
11-16. Each kanji typically carries multiple readings: kun-yomi (native-Japanese-derived
pronunciation, written in hiragana in dictionaries) and on-yomi (Chinese-derived pronunciation,
written in katakana in dictionaries) — the book gives both for each character taught. General
rule of thumb (with exceptions, so vocabulary-level memorization is still advised): kun-yomi is
used when a kanji stands alone or with hiragana attached; on-yomi is used when the kanji combines
with another kanji (私 "watashi" alone = kun-yomi, but 私学 "private school" = on-yomi shi).
Stroke order follows three basic rules: left-to-right, top-to-bottom, and left/top-component-first
when a character has multiple components. p. 138-139.

### Kanji of Unit 11: numerals
The unit's own kanji set is the complete 1-10 numeral series plus hundred/thousand/ten-thousand/
yen (一二三四五六七八九十百千万円) — see the Vocabulary table above for each character's kun-yomi,
on-yomi, and meaning. 百 ("hundred") shows a conditioned voicing alternation in its on-yomi
(hyaku/byaku/pyaku) depending on the preceding numeral, directly parallel to the counter-suffix
voicing alternations already seen with -hon/pon/bon in Unit 8 — worth flagging as a recurring
Japanese phonological pattern (sequential voicing/gemination at morpheme boundaries) for the
mechanics-analysis phase. p. 140.

# Japanese — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Intermediate Kanji Book, Vol. 2 (漢字1000プラス Vol.2), representative sample across Lessons 1, 6, 10, 14, 16 and columns 5, 7

**Source:** Kano Chieko, Shimizu Yuri, Takenaka Hiroko, Ishii Eriko, Akutsu Satoru, Hirakata Yukiko,
*Intermediate Kanji Book, Vol. 2 / 漢字1000Plus Vol.2*, Bonjinsha Co., Ltd. PDF has 802 pages (scanned,
no text layer — "Converted by Total Image Converter" stamped on every page) for a book whose own
printed pagination runs i–xiv (front matter) + 1–264 (16 lessons + practice-answer key) + 265–359
(kanji index) + 360–388 (on/kun-reading index). Empirically confirmed offset: `PDF_page = printed_page
+ 13` (one PDF page per printed page, not a two-page spread — verified against printed folio numbers
visible in the scan at several widely-separated points: printed p.4→PDF 17, p.65→PDF 78, p.123→PDF
136, p.186→PDF 199, p.223→PDF 236).

**Scope and sampling methodology.** This is the second of a two-volume kanji/vocabulary reference
(sibling volume: `009_intermediate_kanji_book1.md` and its lettered continuations). The book is
organized as 16 numbered lessons (第1課–第16課), each built around a nonfiction reading theme (self-
introduction/personality, course evaluation, bullet trains, telecoms, aviation, earthquake
preparedness, volcanoes/hot springs, the economy, financial institutions, Edo-period daimyo travel,
health/medical, nutrition/energy, chemistry, environment, the cabinet/legislature, nuclear weapons
treaties) and teaching ~20-23 target kanji + their compound vocabulary per lesson via a `力だめし`
(warm-up reading, mostly pedagogical prose — skipped per the coverage rule's "repeated drill/example"
exclusion), a `要点` (key-points) section (the actual vocabulary/grammar content — this file's
source), a `練習` (practice drill — skipped), and a `課題` (assignment — skipped). Nine short `コラム`
(column) side-sections cover proper-noun topics (Japanese place names ×2, Japanese personal
given-names ×1, Japanese surnames ×1, visually-similar/confusable kanji ×2, time-related kanji,
phono-semantic-compound kanji ×2). Given 802 pages and confirmed infeasibility of exhaustive
extraction on this book's siblings (Intermediate Kanji Book 1, Basic Kanji Book, Kanji and Kana Book
2, and the Doraemon workbook all required representative sampling at this scale), this dispatch
samples the book's own 16-lesson progression broadly rather than proceeding sequentially:

- **Lesson 1** (自己紹介と性格描写 — self-introduction and personality description; early, pp.1-13):
  the richest register/slang-adjacent lesson in the sample — explicit casual/colloquial vs. formal
  personality-description vocabulary, and a productive agent-noun-suffix pattern (`-家`/`-屋`/`-者`).
- **Lesson 6** (地震の心得十か条 — earthquake-preparedness rules; early-middle, pp.65-76): technical
  disaster vocabulary plus two genuine grammar points (bullet-point/notice register; subject-type-
  specific destruction verbs).
- **Lesson 10** (大名旅行 — daimyo travel; middle, pp.119-135): Japanese historical era names with
  date ranges (useful `Attested Era` reference data) and Edo-period bureaucratic-title vocabulary
  (archaic/historical register).
- **Lesson 14** (環境問題 — environmental issues; middle-late, pp.183-199): an explicit 漢語
  (Sino-Japanese technical register) vs. 和語 (native-Japanese everyday register) paraphrase table —
  directly useful for the `technical_leak`/register-distance slang-derivation axis — plus near-synonym
  differentiation.
- **Lesson 16** (核兵器禁止条約 — nuclear-weapons-ban treaty; final lesson, pp.219-233): compound-word
  morphological segmentation rules, headline/bullet-point register (a second, independent example of
  the same register pattern seen in Lesson 6), and single-kanji country-name abbreviations (a clipping
  convention).
- **Column 5** (日本人の姓 — Japanese surnames, p.136) and **Column 7** (形の似た漢字 — visually similar
  kanji, p.182): sampled briefly for proper-noun and visual-confusable reference value.

Lessons 2–5, 7–9, 11–13, 15 and the other seven columns, the practice/answer-key section (pp.235-264),
and the two back-of-book indices (pp.265-388, which re-present already-taught kanji for dictionary-
style lookup rather than teaching anything new) were **not** read for this dispatch and remain
available for a future sibling file if deeper coverage of this book is ever warranted.

**No text layer — vision-read via rendered page images (`pdftoppm -png -r 100`).** No handwritten
marginalia was found on any page read for this file — every page sampled was clean, consistently
typeset printed content. Furigana (reading glosses) are printed inline above virtually every
non-trivial kanji compound in the `要点` sections, which keeps misreading risk low; vision-reading
confidence is marked `verified` for large, unambiguous, furigana-glossed print and `plausible_unverified`
for smaller-font or un-glossed items.

**Morpheme breakdown.** Japanese is agglutinative in its verb/adjective inflection but the vocabulary
above is overwhelmingly Sino-Japanese compound nouns (`熟語`), which are better treated as internally
composed units than atomic items in several cases:

> **倹約家(けんやくか)** = 倹約 "frugality, thrift" + 家 "person characterized by" — the `-家` suffix is
> a productive formal-register agent-noun formant, paired in this lesson against the near-synonymous
> but colloquial `-屋` suffix (cf. **がんばり屋** = がんばり "effort/persevering" + 屋 "person
> characterized by") and the plain-noun `-者` suffix (cf. **お調子者** = お調子 "getting carried away/
> playing along" (〜に乗る idiom root) + 者 "person"). All three suffixes productively derive
> personality-type agent nouns from an adjectival/verbal stem, with `-家` skewing formal/written,
> `-屋` and `-者` skewing colloquial/spoken — a genuine three-way register split worth tracking for
> slang-formation mechanics (new "-er" nicknames in casual Japanese speech reliably default to `-屋`
> or `-者`, not `-家`).

> **心配性(しんぱいしょう)** = 心配 "worry" + 性 "disposition/nature-of" (irregular ショウ reading) —
> contrast with the regular セイ-reading series (積極性, 協調性, etc.), which take a different
> grammatical frame ("〜性がある/ない" vs. "〜性だ"); the reading split itself tracks a register/
> grammatical-behavior split, not just pronunciation.

> **核兵器禁止条約(かくへいききんしじょうやく)** = 核 "nuclear" + 兵器 "weapon(s)" + 禁止 "ban/
> prohibition" + 条約 "treaty" — a 1-kanji unit (核) prefixed onto a chain of 2-kanji units, per L16's
> explicit compound-segmentation teaching point; this book treats the 2-kanji unit as the base
> morphological building block of Sino-Japanese compounding, with 1-kanji units and affixes as the
> exception rather than the rule.

## Grammar points

### 固い表現 vs. くだけた表現 (formal vs. colloquial personality-description register) — L1

The book explicitly distinguishes 固い表現 ("stiff/formal expression," suited to résumés, letters of
recommendation, formal introductions) from 普通の表現→くだけた表現 ("ordinary → casual expression,"
suited to conversation among friends), giving five matched pairs sharing the same referent: 明朗快活な
⇔ 陽気な/明るい; 意志強固な ⇔ 意志/気が強い; 意志薄弱な ⇔ 意志/気が弱い; 冷静沈着な ⇔ 落ち着いた;
忍耐力がある/忍耐強い ⇔ がまん強い; 努力家 ⇔ がんばり屋 (p.6). This is a directly source-marked
register distinction (not an inference from context), which the extraction spec flags as
high-value — the formal column is built from 4-character literary compounds (`四字熟語`-adjacent
na-adjectives) while the casual column uses plain i-adjective/phrase constructions or the colloquial
`-屋` agent suffix in place of the formal `-家`.

### プラス/中立/マイナス (positive/neutral/negative) personality-vocabulary table — L1

A three-column chart (p.5) sorts ~26 personality descriptors by social connotation rather than by
grammatical category — e.g. 内気な/内向的 and 静かな/おとなしい sit in the "plus" column while
お調子者, 頑固な/強情な, and けちな/けちん坊 sit in "minus," with a a small set of true neutral
items (社交的な has no listed opposite). The source frames this as a politeness/tact lesson: "praise
others, be modest about yourself" (他人の性格についてはほめ、自分の性格については謙遜して述べる).
This sentiment-tagged structure is itself a usable signal for which entries lean toward slang/
colloquial register — several "minus"-column entries (お調子者, けちん坊, 八方美人) are exactly the
kind of socially-loaded, informal personality-label vocabulary this project is tracking.

### 箇条書きの表現 (bullet-point/notice register) — L6 and L16 (independent, convergent examples)

Both Lesson 6 (disaster-preparedness rules) and Lesson 16 (newspaper headlines) independently teach
the same register transformation: a full polite ます-form sentence is compressed into a terse,
unmarked register by (A) nominalizing a clause-final verb (dictionary form + こと, or converting a
する-verb to its noun form: 避難しましょう → 避難すること), (B) dropping です/だ after a na-adjective
or noun predicate (自然環境を知ることが大切だ → 自然環境を知ることが大切), and (C) omitting a
clause-final verb entirely when a particle can stand in for it (避難は徒歩でしましょう → 避難は徒歩
で). Lesson 16 additionally documents the newspaper-headline variant of the same register: stringing
2-kanji compound units together with particles and verbs dropped (米ソ、包括軍縮交渉、SDIを交渉から
除外。) and unpacking them requires re-inserting the implied verbs/particles. Two independently
themed lessons converging on the same terse/clipped register (safety notices and news headlines) is
a genuine cross-lesson pattern — a register this systematic, with multiple independent re-derivations
by the same source across unrelated topics, is a strong candidate register for informal truncation-
based slang analysis later.

### Subject-type-specific destruction verbs — L6

Japanese does not have one generic verb for "to break/collapse" — the correct verb depends on what
kind of object is breaking: furniture topples (転倒する/倒れる, not ×転ぶ, which is reserved for
people/animals falling), houses/buildings collapse (倒壊する/壊れる/倒れる/つぶれる), tunnels and
high-rises cave in (崩壊する/崩れる or 崩落する/崩れる), abstract systems like an economy collapse
(崩壊する only — ×壊れる is explicitly marked wrong here), and levees/dikes breach (決壊する only).
This is a collocational-restriction grammar point, not a simple synonym list — each verb is gated to
a specific semantic class of subject.

### 漢語 vs. 和語 (Sino-Japanese technical register vs. native-Japanese everyday register) — L14

The source explicitly notes that technical writing (専門書) favors Sino-Japanese `suru`-verbs
(漢語の動詞) that are rarely used in speech, while everyday spoken Japanese favors native-Japanese
(和語) paraphrases for the same meaning — e.g. 大気中に放出される (written/technical) vs. 大気中に
出される (spoken/everyday); 体内に蓄積される vs. 体の中にたまる. A full table of 15 such pairs is
given (含有する/廃棄する/汚染する/浄化する/拡散する/生息する/繁殖する/感染する/溶解する/浮遊する/
沈殿する/放射する/循環する/排出する/乾燥する, each defined via its everyday paraphrase rather than
an English gloss). This register axis — technical Sino-Japanese vocabulary vs. plain-Japanese
paraphrase for the identical referent — is directly the inverse of a `technical_leak` slang-
derivation mechanism (jargon simplifying back down into everyday speech, rather than everyday speech
borrowing jargon), worth comparing against any Japanese slang found later that abbreviates or
nativizes technical/bureaucratic vocabulary.

### 漢字熟語の語構成 (Sino-Japanese compound-word segmentation) — L16

Long compound strings common in newspaper headlines are built by chaining a 2-kanji compound as the
basic unit (国連総会決議 → 国連／総会／決議), sometimes including a bare 1-kanji morpheme (核兵器禁止
条約 → 核／兵器／禁止／条約) or a 1-kanji prefix/suffix (最重要課題決議 → 最+重要／課題／決議;
大統領権強化 → 大統領+権／強化). This is a genuine compositional-morphology teaching point for how
Sino-Japanese compounding segments, directly relevant to parsing any Japanese slang compound that
follows (or deliberately violates) the same 2-kanji-unit default.

### Single-kanji country-name clipping — L16

Long-established diplomatic partner countries are referred to in Japanese news text by a single
kanji reserved for that country (米=USA, 露=Russia, 仏=France, 独=Germany, 英=UK, 伊=Italy, 中=China,
韓=South Korea, 朝=North Korea, 豪=Australia, 越=Vietnam, 印=India, 墨=Mexico, 加=Canada, 西=Spain),
while more recently-engaged partners instead get a katakana clipping (南ア=South Africa, パ=Pakistan)
— and the source notes a growing trend toward using katakana clippings even for long-established
partners "for the sake of overall balance." This is a clean, source-documented example of a
clipping/abbreviation convention gated by a sociolinguistic variable (how long/how deep the
relationship with Japan is), not just phonological economy — a useful comparandum for any
abbreviation-based slang-derivation mechanism found in the corpus-collection phase.

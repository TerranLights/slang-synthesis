## Grammar points

### The vision-only "unnumbered chapter divider" and other coverage caveats
Addressed in the file-level coverage note above; not repeated per point here.

### "Form" (shitsuree) as a distinct social category from literal truthfulness
Chapter 1's organizing idea: much of Japanese social behavior (card exchange, bowing, seating choice,
gift-giving) is governed by maintaining correct *form*, which can be independent of the literal
content of an interaction — breaking form (shitsuree) is itself treated as the offense, regardless of
intent. p.18-29.

### Polite refusal avoids a bare negative
A full politeness cline exists between blunt *iie* and the maximally indirect, grammatically-positive-
sounding *dekikanemasu* (built from *dekiru* "to be able" + the negative auxiliary *kanemasu*),
illustrating that Japanese politeness strategies replace direct negation with softer alternative
forms rather than simply adding honorific morphology to the same negative. p.44-45.

### Corporate/government title hierarchy as a closed, ordered vocabulary set
The sequence kakarichoo < kachoo < buchoo < senmu < joomu < shachoo < kaichoo (corporate) and
kyokuchoo < jikan < choukan < daijin (government) forms a culturally load-bearing ranked vocabulary
set that foreign businesspeople are expected to use correctly when addressing Japanese counterparts
by title rather than name. p.42-44.

### A structured 12-item apology-register vocabulary set
The source presents apology expressions (shitsuree shimashita through mooshiwake gozaimasen) as a
graded register continuum from relatively casual to maximally formal, paired with the distinction
between kejime (a real corrective action) and kuchisaki dake (empty verbal apology) — i.e. the
vocabulary itself encodes a cultural distinction between sincere and insincere apology that has no
single-word English equivalent. p.47-50.

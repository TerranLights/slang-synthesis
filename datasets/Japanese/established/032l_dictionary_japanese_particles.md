## Grammar points

### tara (たら) conclusion, tari (たり), tatte (たって) — casual connective particles
Paraphrased from pp.186–189. "Tara" (concluded from part 11) also serves as an attention-getting address marker and an imperative-following impatience marker ("come home already!"). "Tari" marks alternating repeated actions ("walking back and forth") or cites a disapproved-category example ("such things as playing outside until dark"). "Tatte" (colloquially "ttatte") gives a hypothetical concession ("even if you wanted to go on a trip now...") or an emphatic adversative ("even though you have a little savings, it won't help in an emergency").

### te (て) — the core gerund/connective particle, with a women's-language sub-pattern
Paraphrased from pp.190–194, one of the largest entries in the book. Covers simultaneous/successive action, causal reason ("crying because he was scolded"), method ("drink coffee with milk and sugar"), conjunction-style listing, intensified repetition ("wrote and wrote like crazy"), adversative contrast, the "ni tsuite" topic-introducing pattern, and — most fundamentally — gerund formation itself (the "-te" verb form). Sentence-finally it also covers an assertive pattern the source flags explicitly as women's language ("(t)te yo"), an interrogative use, command/request forms, a casual "I believe/I think" tag, and a variant quotative role shared with "to".

### teba (てば), temo/demo (ても/でも) — topic-marking and concessive particles
Paraphrased from pp.195–197. "Teba" introduces a topic with familiarity or mild criticism ("speaking of Kimura, he's still skipping classes"), or expresses impatience sentence-finally ("dinner's ready! come quickly!"). "Temo" (becoming "demo" after an -n sound) gives the standard "no matter how..." concession, a "despite/regardless of" sense, an "at most/at least" possible-limit emphasis, and a permission-granting sense.

### te-ne (てね) and te-wa (ては) — combination particles built on "te"
Paraphrased from pp.198–200. "Te-ne" (also "de ne") is explicitly flagged by the source as typically women's and children's language, softening a request into a familiar "please/would you...". "Te-wa" (also "de wa") covers an undesirable hypothetical-and-result pattern, a comma-like linking function, a condition that reliably produces its stated result, and a "keep doing..." repeated-action pattern.

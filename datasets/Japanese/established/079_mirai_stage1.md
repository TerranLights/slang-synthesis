## Grammar points

### Topic particle は (wa)

The particle は (pronounced "wa" in this grammatical use) marks the topic of the sentence — "as for
X." In ぼくは しんごです ("As for me, I am Shingo"), ぼく is the topic. If the topic is
obvious from context (e.g. introducing yourself alone, with no ambiguity about who is speaking), it
can be dropped — で す alone suffices. In group settings where multiple people are introducing
themselves, は becomes necessary to disambiguate. (p.4-5)

### Pronoun register: ぼく vs わたし

ぼく is explicitly marked as the informal first-person pronoun used only by male speakers; わたし
is the formal counterpart usable by either gender. This is the course's first explicit
register/gender annotation and is reflected in the Usage Tier column above (ぼく tagged
`colloquial`, わたし tagged `core`). (p.4)

### Title suffixes: くん / さん / せんせい

くん attaches to boys'/same-age-male names, さん to girls' and adults' names generally, せんせい
to teachers' names. Critically, none of the three is ever attached to one's own name — the book
poses this explicitly as a reflection question ("Can you guess why?"), implying the self-effacing
convention is left for the learner to reason out rather than stated outright. (p.4, 19)

### Particle も (mo), "too/also"

も replaces は when adding "too" to a statement that was already asserted about someone else:
ジョニーくんも 17さい です ("Johnny is 17 too"). The particle directly follows the word it
modifies, mirroring English "Johnny too is 17" word order rather than "Johnny is 17 too." (p.24)

### Counting 1-10 and the compositional 11-100 system

Numbers 11-19 are built compositionally as "10 + unit" (juu + ichi = 11, juu + ni = 12, etc.); 20 is
nijuu ("2 tens"); the tens continue as N-juu (sanjuu=30, yonjuu=40, gojuu=50) up to hyaku (100). This
is presented as a fully regular, rule-governed system once the 1-10 set is learned. (p.22-23)

### Numeral taboo-avoidance: し/shichi → よん/なな

A genuine sociolinguistic/taboo finding: the regular readings し (shi, "4") and しち (shichi, "7")
are avoided in counting contexts — replaced by よん (yon) and なな (nana) — because し is
homophonous with 死 ("death") and しち evokes "into the jaws of death." The source frames this as a
superstition some speakers still hold, and poses a parallel question to the learner about unlucky
numbers in English (implicitly inviting a comparison to "13" or similar). This is the clearest
explicit register/taboo annotation in this chunk and should be flagged for the mechanics-analysis
phase as an attested real-world avoidance pattern (homophone-based numeral taboo), distinct from
syntactic slang formation but directly relevant to how "unlucky"/avoided forms function
sociolinguistically. (p.22)

A second, narrower instance recurs when forming ages: 4-sai and 7-sai are regularly yon-sai/nana-sai
(consistent with the above), but the book additionally flags that 9-sai must never be read as
"ku-sai" — exactly paralleling く (ku, an alternate reading of 九 "nine") being homophonous with
苦 ("suffering/pain") or 空 depending on context; the book withholds the explicit reason ("ask your
teacher for the secret reason"), treating it as taboo-by-convention rather than spelling out the
homophone for younger learners. 20 years old also gets a fully suppletive form, はたち (hatachi),
rather than the expected nijuu-sai. (p.23)

### Phone number reading conventions

Phone numbers are read digit-by-digit (not as whole numbers), with 0 read as zero (a loanword, not
れい), よん/なな used for 4/7 (consistent with the general taboo-avoidance pattern above), and の
(no) inserted after district codes or at a hyphen boundary. もしもし is reserved exclusively for
answering the phone, never used as a general greeting. (p.30)

### Greeting register: formal vs. informal

おはよう (informal, friends-only) vs. おはよう ございます (formal, used generally until ~11am)
is the course's explicit register pair; likewise さようなら (used toward less-familiar people) is
contrasted against じゃ、また/また あした (used toward people seen regularly). Both pairs are
genuine source-marked formal/colloquial annotations captured in the Usage Tier column above. (p.11-12)

## Grammar points (Unit 3)

### Locative に (ni)

に marks the place where someone lives/is located, here glossed as similar to English "in/at." The
book explicitly notes に has other functions taught later — this chunk covers only the locative
sense. Question formation simply substitutes どこ for the place name and drops か from the answer.
(p.40-41)

### Nationality formation: country name + じん

Nationality nouns are formed compositionally as [country name] + じん ("person"). Most country
names are katakana transliterations of the foreign name (アメリカ, オーストラリア,
ニュージーランド), but China (ちゅうごく) uses its native Sino-Japanese reading rather than a
loanword transliteration — the one exception in the set extracted here, and worth noting for any
later katakana-vs-native-vocabulary register analysis. (p.59-60, 75)

### Fossilized/grammaticalized routine expressions

A genuine mechanics-relevant finding: the book explicitly observes that routine set expressions
(いただきます, ごちそうさま, さようなら, ただいま, etc.) are produced automatically by native
speakers without active parsing of their literal, now-opaque meanings — drawing an explicit parallel
to English "goodbye" (historically "God be with you"). This is presented as a general property of
fixed social-ritual speech, not slang specifically, but is directly relevant groundwork for how the
project's later mechanics-analysis phase should think about semantic bleaching in fixed phrases. (p.50)

### Register-conditioned goodbye forms

さようなら is reserved for partings that are long-term or formal; いって きます / いって
らっしゃい is the universal casual pair for leaving/staying at home. This is an explicit
source-marked register contrast (not merely free variation), consistent with the courtesy-register
pattern already seen in Unit 1's greeting pairs. (p.50)

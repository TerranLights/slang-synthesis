# Japanese — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: A Dictionary of Intermediate Japanese Grammar (representative sample across full volume)

**Source:** Seiichi Makino and Michio Tsutsui, *A Dictionary of Intermediate Japanese Grammar*
(The Japan Times, 1st ed. June 1995, 19th printing October 2001). Companion/intermediate volume to
*A Dictionary of Basic Japanese Grammar* (1986). Pages covered: front matter (Preface, To the
Reader, List of Abbreviations/Symbols, Grammatical Terms, Special Topics in Intermediate Japanese
Grammar) read in full/near-full; Main Entries (printed pp. 1–612, ~200 alphabetically-ordered
grammar entries) sampled at ~16 points spread evenly across the full A–Y alphabetical range;
Appendixes 3–8 (Compound Particles, Conjunctions, Affixes, More Counters, Cooccurrence, Functional
Expressions and Grammatical Patterns) each sampled at one representative page-spread.

**Coverage note:** This is a 420-PDF-page (approx. 770 printed-page) reference dictionary with no
text layer (`pdftotext` confirmed zero extractable characters at multiple test ranges) — **every
page was vision-read from rendered page images.** Given the scale, this file uses REPRESENTATIVE
SAMPLING per the dispatch instructions, not exhaustive extraction: the book's own front-matter
introduction was read essentially in full and is paraphrased comprehensively below; the ~200 main
dictionary entries and the 6 appendixes were sampled at regular intervals (approx. every 20 PDF
pages / ~40 printed pages for main entries) rather than read entry-by-entry. Sampling was weighted
toward the dispatch's priorities: particles, sentence-final expressions, and any entry explicitly
marked `<s>` (spoken/conversational only) or `<w>` (written/formal only) register.

**Vision-reading guard / marginalia note:** The scanned source is a used copy. On the sampled page
showing the `ni tsuite` entry (printed p. 281), a pencil underline was found beneath one printed
example translation ("an opinion about elementary education") — this is a previous owner's
handwritten annotation, not source content, and has been excluded entirely from this extraction (the
printed text itself was still extracted normally). No other marginalia was identified on the sampled
pages, but given the scale of unsampled pages, more may exist elsewhere in the volume.

**Page-numbering note:** This PDF scans two printed pages per PDF page (confirmed empirically: PDF
page 1 = unnumbered title page, PDF page 5 = printed pp. [10]–[11], PDF page 39 = the "Main Entries"
divider half-title, PDF page 40 = printed p. 1 of Main Entries). All printed page numbers cited below

## Grammar points

### Book structure and apparatus (front matter, pp.[5]–[13])

The dictionary is the intermediate-level companion to the same authors' 1986 *A Dictionary of Basic
Japanese Grammar* (DBJG), covering ~200 grammar items selected by examining textbooks, sentence-
pattern references, and authentic intermediate/advanced-course materials. Unlike DBJG, this volume
gives no romanization for example sentences — Japanese examples are printed with *furigana* (hiragana
reading glosses over kanji) instead. Each main entry follows a fixed template: entry name (romanized
+ hiragana) → part of speech → optional usage-restriction tag `<s>` (spoken Japanese only) or `<w>`
(written Japanese only) → a boxed meaning/function statement paired with English counterpart(s) and
cross-referenced "Related Expression(s)" → Key Sentence(s) (frame tables showing sentence structure,
recurring elements in red in the original) → Formation rules → Examples → Notes → a prose "Related
Expression(s)" comparison section. A cross-reference like `(⇒ aaa (DBJG: 000–00))` always points back
into the companion Basic-volume dictionary.

### Grammatical Terms glossary (pp.[13]ff.)

A reference glossary of metalanguage terms used throughout the dictionary (e.g., Active/Passive
Sentence, Agent, Appositive Clause, Auxiliary Adjective, Auxiliary Verb, Causative Sentence, Compound
Particle, Compound Sentence, Conditional, and many more through the alphabet), each with a brief
informal definition and 1–2 illustrative example sentences. This section is explicitly aimed at
readers unfamiliar with the authors' own terminology from the companion Basic volume; it is
metalinguistic apparatus rather than vocabulary content in its own right, so it was not tabulated
above.

### Special Topics in Intermediate Japanese Grammar (pp.[30]–[55]): overview

This section — read closely as it is the highest-value "register" content in the book's own
introduction — covers four areas:

**1. Discourse Grammar** (inter-sentential reference/cohesion mechanisms, and phenomena of tense and
formality switching across a discourse).

**2. Newspaper Grammar.** Characteristic reductions of written/journalistic Japanese: suru-verb
nominal stems used with `-shi` conjunctive endings instead of full sentence-final conjugation; `da`
routinely dropped after a noun at a clause boundary, producing noun-ending sentences. Illustrated with
real excerpts (cited as drawn from 東京新聞, 日本経済新聞) about national-university tuition increases
and school violence statistics — genuine journalistic register examples of how newspaper Japanese
compresses sentence-final elements relative to conversational or even standard written Japanese.

**3. Conversational Strategies.** The book identifies five named strategies intermediate learners need
for advanced spoken proficiency:
- **(A) *Aizuchi*** ("back-channel" responses) — see vocabulary table.
- **(B) Fillers** — hesitation markers `ano` and `ēto`, the hedging interjection `mā`, and filler
  uses of the particle `ne` (contrasted with its more formal collocate `desu ne`, common in business
  Japanese). The book's own example (8c)/(7) show that *stacking too many fillers in one sentence* is
  itself marked ungrammatical/very awkward (starred, triple-question-marked) — filler use has an
  upper bound of naturalness even in casual speech.
- **(C) Rephrasing** — strategies for substituting a simpler noun-modification paraphrase, a more
  basic verb/adjective, or the frame `~ koto/hito/yōsu o nan to iimasu ka` ("what do you call the
  thing/person/state where...") when a learner cannot recall the target word (e.g., working around
  not remembering *geta*, *genkan*, *nesugosu*, *gaikōteki*).
- **(D) Topic Shift and Topic Recovery** — fixed topic-shift openers (ところで, 話は違いますが, ちょっと
  話がそれますが, 話題が変わりますが, Xと言えば/Xって(言えば)) and topic-recovery openers ranging from
  highly informal (さっきXって言ったけどさ) to very formal/polite (先ほど申し上げたことですが / 先ほどの件
  ですけど) — a clean register ladder for the same discourse function.
- **(E) Avoidance of Decisive Expressions.** Japanese strongly prefers indirect, hedged statements,
  especially for the speaker's own opinions — the book demonstrates that progressively longer
  sentence-final tags (ではないか, ではないだろうか, のではないかと思われる…が, etc.) make an otherwise
  identical core statement progressively less decisive/more humble. Set phrases exist specifically for
  prefacing a disagreement with a superior (そうかなあ [informal]; それでもいいけど; それはそうだけど),
  softening confrontation.

**4. Toward Better Reading Comprehension: Analyzing Sentences Accurately.** Discusses structural
patterns relevant to parsing written Japanese accurately: noun-connecting particles (と, や) and comma-
listing to form compound noun phrases, embedded interrogative clauses functioning as noun equivalents,
pre-verbal adverbials (plain adverbs/adverbial nouns, adverbial forms of adjectives and auxiliary
adjectives, quantifiers, phonomimes/phenomimes/psychomimes such as ワンワン "bowwow" and すらすら
"fluently," and quotation + と).

### Register-marked dictionary entries (main-entry sample)

The sampled main entries confirm the dictionary systematically marks register with the `<s>`
(spoken-only) / `<w>` (written-only) tags described in "To the Reader": `amari` and `o hajime (to
shite)` are both `<w>`; `kekkō` is `<s>`; `mai` and `-yō²` carry no header tag but their own Notes
sections state they are "basically/primarily a written form." Several entries go further and name an
explicit spoken-register replacement for a written-only form — e.g., `o hajime (to shite)`'s Note 3
states conversational Japanese substitutes `dake de (wa) naku ~ (mo)` instead, and `-yō²`'s informal
spoken/written contracted equivalent is `darō` (polite-spoken `deshō`). This written/spoken
substitution-pair pattern recurs often enough in the sampled entries that it looks like a structural
feature of the whole dictionary, not an isolated case.

### Sentence-final particle `sa`: gendered informality (p.360)

The entry for `sa` is the clearest single case of sociolinguistic marking found in the main-entry
sample: Note 1 states it is used "usually by male speakers in very informal speech" for several
distinct pragmatic functions (encouragement, assertive rebuttal of a challenge, a light aside,
irritation at someone's words/behavior), while a male-or-female drawing-attention use is cross-
referenced separately. The book contrasts `sa` directly against `yo`: both are "informal," but `sa`
is described as "far more informal than `yo`," and — mechanically — `yo` can attach to both informal
and formal predicate forms while `sa` can only attach to informal forms (Related Expression I,
example [5]: `~masu yo` is grammatical, `~masu sa` is starred/ungrammatical).

### Appendix 8 (Functional Expressions): the clearest gendered-register table in the volume

Appendix 8 is organized as a functional inventory (requests, permission, suggestions, etc.) and tags
*every* variant form with the same `<s>`/`<w>` apparatus plus additional speaker-gender/formality
codes defined on its own first page: `inf.sit.` (informal situations only), `m.`/`(m.)` (used
by/mostly by male speakers), `f.`/`(f.)` (used by/mostly by female speakers). Section 1 ("Ordering
someone to do something; giving instructions") alone tabulates at least 8 graded command forms from
strongest/bluntest to mildest, with `Vte kure` explicitly `<s>, inf.sit., m.` and `Vte chōdai`
explicitly `<s>, inf.sit., f.` as a directly paired male/female colloquial-command minimal pair — a
genuinely rare piece of explicit, source-marked sociolinguistic gender-register data for this
project's purposes.

### Appendix 6 (More Counters): phonetic mutation types

Rather than vocabulary per se, this appendix documents the sound-change rules Japanese counters
undergo when combined with certain numbers, classified into types: **Type A** (no phonetic change);
**Type A′** (as A, but "4" is read `yo` not `yon`); **Type A″** (as A, but "4/7/9" read `shi`/`shichi`/
`ku`); **Type A‴** (as A, but "3"'s counter-initial consonant shifts `wa→ba`, e.g. 羽 "bird"); **Type
B** (counter-initial `h-` shifts to `p-` after 1, 3, 6, 8, 10); **Type B′** (as B, but "3" shifts to
`p-` not `b-`); **Type C** (counter-initial `k-` gemination/shift pattern after 1, 6, 8, 10); **Type
C′** (as C, but "3" may take either `k-` or `g-`). This is a systematic morphophonological rule set
worth flagging for the mechanics-analysis phase, since any later synthesized-slang counter coinage
would plausibly need to pick a consistent mutation type.

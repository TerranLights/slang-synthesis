# Japanese — Ultimate Japanese Advanced (Living Language, 1998/2003)

**Source:** *Living Language Ultimate Japanese Advanced*, Living Language / Random House (1998,
content revised 2003), First Edition, ISBN 1-4000-2067-0. 446-page scanned PDF, no text layer
(confirmed via `pdftotext`/`pdffonts` — zero extractable characters and zero embedded fonts across
the whole file). Full vision-reading required for every page used.

**Structure of the book:** 20 lessons + 2 review sections (*Fukushuu*) + 4 reading-practice
inserts, presented twice — Section 1 (pp. 1–264) in *romaji*/English only (dialogue, grammar/usage
notes, business-culture notes, exercises), and Section 2 (pp. 267–403) repeating the same 20
lessons' dialogues in kanji/hiragana/katakana with a parallel vocabulary list (*Tango*) and a kanji
list per lesson. A Japanese–English/English–Japanese glossary (pp. 405–426) and index (pp. 427+)
follow. Page-offset note: printed page N corresponds to physical PDF page N+16 throughout (verified
against the table of contents and the Introduction page).

**Coverage approach — representative sampling, not full-book extraction.** At 446 pages with no
text layer, full vision-reading of every page was infeasible for a single extraction pass. This
file samples a representative spread across the lesson progression (early, middle, and late
lessons), **prioritizing lessons whose grammar/notes sections explicitly address
register/politeness/colloquialism** — this is an *advanced*-level course, and its "B. Chuu (Notes)"
sections in particular carry frequent explicit register commentary (honorific vs. plain vs. humble
forms, business formality, casual-speech markers) that is exactly the kind of source-marked register
content this project prioritizes. Lessons sampled in this file: **Lesson 1** (baseline/arrival),
**Lesson 4** (Honorific Language — *keigo*: deferential/humble/polite forms). Additional lessons
(7, 8, 9, 11, 14, 18, 19 — chosen for Female Speech, Passive Voice/*Tatemae-Honne*, Humble Form/
*Uchi-Soto*, Onomatopoeia, business *keigo* requests, and causative-passive/religion respectively)
are queued for follow-on sibling files (089b, 089c, …) in this same extraction pass. Lessons and
pages not sampled (most Business Matters subsections, most Renshuu Mondai exercises, the Kanji
character-stroke-order boxes, the Glossary, and the Index) are skipped per the spec's
comprehensive-but-not-exhaustive coverage rule — drill material and dictionary-style glossary
entries are lower information density than the grammar/register notes, and a 400+ page book's
glossary would mostly duplicate vocabulary already captured from the lesson Tango lists.

**Copyright discipline applied:** dialogue, notes, and grammar explanations below are paraphrased;
only short illustrative Japanese phrases/sentences (already standard practice for citing example
sentences in a grammar reference) are quoted, never full dialogue blocks or vocabulary boxes
verbatim.

**Vision-reading confidence:** all entries below are vision-read (no text layer existed to fall back
on). Printed text throughout the sampled pages was clean, high-contrast, consistently typeset — no
handwritten marginalia, no scan artifacts, no font-substitution cipher — so confidence is uniformly
`verified` (romaji/English is unambiguous typeset Latin text; kanji/kana vocabulary was cross-checked
against the parallel romaji dialogue earlier in the same lesson) unless flagged otherwise per row.

### Grammar points

1. **The copula *desu*.** Roughly equivalent to English "to be," occurring sentence-finally like
   other verbs; also used with *na*-adjectives. Negative forms are *dewa arimasen* or the contracted
   *ja arimasen* — the source notes *ja arimasen* is more common in casual, relaxed conversation,
   making this a formality-register distinction built into the grammar itself, not just a phonetic
   contraction. (pp. 17–18)
2. ***I*-adjectives.** All end in *-i* (e.g. *takai*, *chikai*, *nagai*). Can stand alone as a
   complete predicate (e.g. bare *Takai.* = "It is expensive"), but the source explicitly flags this
   bare form as appropriate **only in informal situations**; the formal register requires adding
   *desu* after the adjective (a "formalizer," not a true copula in this use). (p. 18)
3. ***Na*-adjectives and particles/conjunctions** (title only sampled; not deep-read this pass).
4. **Business Matters (*Ryokoo An'nai*, Travel Tips):** paraphrased cultural/practical content on visa
   requirements for U.S. travelers, the *hoshoonin seido* (guarantor/letter-of-guarantee system),
   immigration procedure at Narita, and transportation/hotel/business-hour norms. (pp. 23–24)

**Register note (file-level):** Lesson 1's Chuu section is unusually dense with explicit formality

### Grammar points

1. **Plain forms of the copula *desu*.** Paradigm: non-past affirmative *da*, non-past negative
   *dewa nai*/*ja nai*, past affirmative *datta*, past negative *dewa nakatta*/*ja nakatta*. Two
   distinct registered uses: (a) informal sentences — used with friends, family, subordinates, or
   oneself, explicitly contrasted with the polite/formal register used elsewhere in the book; (b)
   clause-builders — plain forms embedded inside a larger sentence whose final predicate may still be
   polite (e.g. *[...] da soo da*, "I heard that..."), showing that register can shift mid-sentence
   between an embedded clause and the matrix predicate. (p. 64)
2. **Honorific language (*keigo*) — three-way system.** The source explicitly names and separates
   three honorific registers: **deferential** (raises the person spoken to/about — used for
   superiors, formal meetings, negotiations), **humble** (lowers the speaker or speaker's in-group to
   implicitly raise the listener), and **polite** (the baseline *-masu*/*desu* register, plus the
   honorific prefix *o-* on nouns like *ocha*, *osake*, usable with equals, superiors, and
   non-intimates, and as a "default when in doubt"). This explicit three-tier naming and the
   "default to polite when uncertain about the correct register" guidance is a directly source-marked
   register schema, the clearest such structure sampled in this file. (pp. 65–66)
3. **Expressing emphasis: *n(o) desu (ka)*.** Plain-form verb + *n(o) desu(ka)* — used to add
   emphasis or supply an explanation; contracted *n desu* is the more casual spoken form of the fuller
   *no desu*, another register-graded pair. (p. 67)
4. **Giving advice: past-tense plain verb + *hoo ga ii desu (ka)*** ("should do"/"should not do" with
   the non-past negative form). (p. 67)
5. **Conjunctive particle *shi*** (title sampled, not deep-read this pass).
6. **Business Matters (*Hanzai to Anzen*, Crime and Safety):** paraphrased practical/cultural content,
   not deep-read this pass.

### Grammar points

1. **Invitations and requests.** Negative-form question pattern (verb negative + *ka*) is the more
   common way to extend an invitation (e.g. *Irasshaimasen ka*, "Won't you come?"); the affirmative
   form is possible but less frequent. The source explicitly flags *tai desu ka* (verb-stem + *tai
   desu ka*) as **not** an appropriate formal-invitation pattern — it is restricted to informal
   interrogatives about the listener's own desires — and calls this out as a common mistake for
   native English speakers. (pp. 101–102)
2. **Female speech.** The source frames gendered speech as a declining-but-still-real distinction:
   standardization toward one style is the current trend (especially in the workplace), but women
   still tend toward higher pitch/intonation and more frequent polite/formal speech, a pattern traced
   historically to women having been socially required to defer in public and private life. Three
   sentence-final particles are explicitly tagged as "used mostly by women": *wa* (mild
   assertiveness), *no* (interrogation or softening), *kashira* ("I wonder"). A worked dialogue
   between an older woman and a younger man is used specifically to show that **using these
   particles does not correlate with speaking less politely** — the older woman uses *wa*/*no*/
   *kashira* while being, per the source's own framing, less polite than her junior conversation
   partner, decoupling "gendered particle use" from "formality level" as two independent axes. (p. 102)
3. ***Wa* for emphasis** — a *different*, non-gendered *wa* (the source explicitly flags this as "an
   entirely different" *wa* from the one above) placed after a particle/phrase to stress it, e.g.
   *Nihon de wa chiisai konpyuutaa ga ninki ga arimasu* ("In Japan, small computers [specifically] are
   popular"). (pp. 103–104)
4. **Intention** — plain volitional (*-oo* form) + *to omou*; formal volitional is stem + *mashoo*
   ("let's do"). (p. 104)
5. **Intransitive and transitive verbs** (title sampled, not deep-read this pass).

### Grammar points

1. **Passive voice — direct and indirect.** The *indirect* (adversative) passive is the
   register-relevant half: the source explains it expresses that the subject was adversely affected
   by or made to suffer from another's action (illustrated with *Chichi ni shinaremashita*, "My father
   passed away on me" — i.e., grammatically passive even though dying is not literally something done
   *to* the subject by the father). This "suffering passive" has no direct equivalent in English
   grammar and is flagged as a distinctly Japanese sentence-structure/register nuance. (p. 118)
2. ***Wake dewa nai*** ("it is not that...") — a hedging/clarifying construction following verbs,
   *i*-adjectives (plain form), or nouns/*na*-adjectives (plain copula + *no*/*na*). Used to soften or
   qualify a prior claim (e.g. "it's not that our salaries are especially high, but..."). (p. 118)
3. ***Wake niwa ikanai*** ("there's no reason to" / "can't very well") — expresses that something
   cannot be done for social, moral, or psychological reasons, always with plain-form verbs. (p. 119)
4. ***Hazu (da)*** — a dependent noun marking an expected/logical conclusion ("I expect that," "it is
   expected that"), taking plain-form modifiers (with *no*/*na* substitutions for nouns/*na*-adjectives
   before *hazu*, mirroring the *wake* pattern above). (p. 119)
5. **Business Matters: *Tsukiai* (Socializing)** — paraphrased: Japanese business social norms around
   who pays for meals (the host pays and ordering/paying expectations are implicit; no "going Dutch"
   — *warikan*/*betsu betsu* — on business meals even when informal, though peer groups among friends
   do split evenly); seating-arrangement hierarchy signaling status (farthest-from-door or facing the
   *tokonoma* alcove is the seat of honor); and conversational hierarchy (highest-status person speaks
   first; lower-status participants generally wait to be addressed). (pp. 120–121)
6. **Business Matters: *Tatemae to Hon'ne* ("Two Faces")** — the single richest piece of explicit
   register/cultural framing sampled in this file. Paraphrased: Westerners' perception of the
   Japanese as "inscrutable" is attributed to seeing only the *tatemae* ("official stance") face in
   casual contact, missing the *hon'ne* ("real feeling") shared only within trusted peer groups. The
   source frames *tatemae* as a social-harmony (*wa*) preservation mechanism born of historically
   dense, tightly structured communities, explicitly cautioning the learner to *understand* this
   dual-register system without necessarily *imitating* it, and noting Japanese people often find a
   foreigner's direct expression of *hon'ne*-equivalent frankness "refreshing — to a degree." (pp.
   121–122)

**Register note:** this lesson's *Tatemae to Hon'ne* essay names and explains, as an emic cultural
concept rather than ordinary vocabulary, the exact public/private-register split this project's
usage-tier model (`formal`/`colloquial`/etc.) is trying to approximate from the outside — worth
flagging for the mechanics-analysis phase as a source-native framework for *why* register-switching
happens in Japanese, not just *that* it does.

### Grammar points

1. ***Beki da*** — non-past affirmative plain verb + *beki da*, expressing "should do." (p. 191)
2. **The particle *ka* — three extended functions beyond marking questions**: (a) alternatives
   ("either...or," optionally clarified with *soretomo*); (b) embedded yes/no questions ("whether or
   not," often reinforced with *dooka*); (c) embedded wh-questions (who/what/where/why). (pp. 191–192)
3. **Onomatopoeia.** The source frames Japanese mimetic/onomatopoeic vocabulary as an open, large word
   class, explicitly warning that many members have "little or no apparent connection to sound" (i.e.
   not all are true onomatopoeia in the English sense — several are closer to ideophones/mimetic
   adverbs for manner or emotional state). Formally, the class is recognizable by (i) reduplication of
   a syllable/word (*kankan*, *pekopeko*, *buubuu*, *nikoniko*, *gyaa gyaa*) and (ii) frequent marking
   with the particle *ni* or *to*. Framed explicitly as register-building vocabulary: "mastering some
   of the most commonly used onomatopoeia" helps a learner "sound more natural" — i.e. this whole word
   class is pitched by the source as a naturalness/colloquiality marker, not core formal vocabulary.
   (p. 193)
4. ***Oboe ga aru*** ("recollection") — past plain affirmative verb + *oboe ga aru*, "have a memory of
   (doing something)." (p. 193)

### Grammar points

1. ***(Sa)sete itadaku* / *(sa)sete morau* — asking for permission.** Te-form of the causative verb +
   *itadaku* is the formal/deferential register, used toward a person of higher status (e.g.
   *Atarashii seihin o shookai sasete itadakimasu*, "Please allow me to introduce our new product").
   The source explicitly marks the *morau* variant (te-form + *morau*) as "much more direct," restricted
   to addressing equal- or lower-status listeners, and cautions it "should not be used when addressing
   someone of higher status" — a clean, explicitly source-graded formality pair on the same
   grammatical slot. (p. 239)
2. **Noun-*suru* verbs.** *Suru* ("to do") combines with nouns to form compound verbs (*kekkon-suru*,
   "to marry"; *renshuu-suru*, "to practice"; *chuumon-suru*, "to order") — described as a highly
   productive pattern with "hundreds" of such nominal verbs in Japanese. (p. 239)

### Grammar points

1. ***(Ta)garu*** — third-person desire/want marker. Plain *-tai* form is primarily first-person;
   informally extensible to 2nd/3rd person especially before uncertainty markers (*rashii*, *mitai*)
   or in questions. The *-tagaru* form (drop *-tai*'s final *i*, add *-garu*) is the more formal,
   third-person-only way to express "is eager to do X" (e.g. *Dare demo kami o shinjitagarimasu*,
   "Everyone wants to believe in a god"). (p. 248)
2. **Causative-passive.** Combines the causative (*-(s)aseru*) and passive (*-(r)areru*) forms to
   express "being made/forced to do" something — *-(s)aserareru* for regular verbs, *-(s)erareru*/
   *-(s)areru* for irregular verbs (e.g. *ikaserareru*, "to be made to go"; *nomaserareru*, "to be
   made to drink"; exceptions *kosaserareru* for *kuru*, *saserareru* for *suru*). Full paradigm table
   given for regular, irregular, and exception verbs. Worked examples are explicitly about religious
   upbringing: *Kodomo no toki yoku kyookai ni ikaseraremashita* ("When I was a child, I was forced to
   go to church a lot"); *Shuukyoo o benkyoo saseraremashita* ("I was forced to study religion") — the
   grammar point and the lesson's religion theme are deliberately paired by the source. (p. 250)
3. **Business Matters: *Nihon no Shuukyoo* (Religion in Japan)** — paraphrased: Japanese religious
   identity is described as syncretic/polytheistic rather than singular — citing a 1995 Agency for
   Cultural Affairs survey where self-reported religious affiliations (220 million) nearly double the
   actual population (123 million), evidence of widespread multi-affiliation (Shinto New Year's shrine
   visits + Buddhist temple grave visits + Christian-style weddings, often within one family/one
   household's dual Shinto-and-Buddhist altars). Shintoism is glossed as indigenous, nature/ancestor-
   based, without unified doctrine; Buddhism's 6th-century arrival from China and later Zen Buddhism's
   13th-century association with the samurai class are both dated. (pp. 250–251)
4. **Business Matters: *Kakure Kurisuchan* (Hidden Christians)** — paraphrased: Christianity's
   introduction via Catholic missionaries in 15th-century Nagasaki, subsequent government distrust tied
   explicitly to fear of eroding *yamatodamashii* ("Japanese spirit"), and Nagasaki's unusually high
   surviving Christian population cited as the lingering demographic trace of that history. (p. 251)

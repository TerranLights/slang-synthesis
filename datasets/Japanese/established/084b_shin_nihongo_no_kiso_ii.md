# Japanese — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Shin Nihongo no Kiso II (Level II, Lessons 26–50)

**Source (primary):** 海外技術者研修協会 (AOTS), *新日本語の基礎 II* (*Shin Nihongo no Kiso II*),
revised edition (revision initiative begun 1985 per the book's own 改訂にあたって preface, ~2-year
trial period before publication — same unified I/II revision as `084`'s Level I). Three in-scope
companion files: *Textbook.pdf* (312 pp.), *Kanji workbook.pdf* (English edition — see scope note
below), and *English Explanations.pdf* (83 pp.).

**Scope note — Level II kanji workbook excluded.** Per `00_Book_Triage_Catalog.md`, the kanji
workbook sitting alongside *Textbook.pdf* and *English Explanations.pdf* in this folder is a
**Spanish-language edition**, confirmed during this project's triage pass despite its filename
giving no language indicator. It is excluded here as wrong-metalanguage material, matching this
project's standing precedent (cf. the Level I Russian-medium companion volumes excluded in `084`).
No kanji-workbook content is drawn from Level II in this file as a result.

**Text-layer / cipher note.** *Textbook.pdf* carries a font-substitution cipher matching this
corpus's catalogued "CJK-glyph-to-exotic-Unicode-block substitution" pattern: `pdftotext -layout`
decodes to unrelated scrambled kanji/kana that does not match the rendered page content at all
(confirmed directly — rendering vs. extracted text diverge completely). **Vision-read throughout**
rather than attempting a cipher decode, per the extraction spec's guidance to fall back to
vision-reading when no consistent offset/substitution recovers real content. *English
Explanations.pdf* reports a real PDF page structure but `pdffonts` shows **zero embedded fonts** —
it is image-only (scanned), not a cipher case; also vision-read. No handwritten marginalia was
found on any page sampled from either file.

**Representative sampling.** As with `084`, this file samples across the level's 25-lesson
progression rather than extracting every page: the full table-of-contents sentence-pattern headline
for all 25 lessons (26–50), full vocabulary/example-sentence extraction from four representative
lessons spanning early/middle/late Level II (26, 37, 49, 50 — the last two forming the course's
honorific-speech capstone), the "cast of characters" page (character-role vocabulary), the
textbook's own 自動詞と他動詞 (transitive/intransitive verb pairs) reference appendix, and two
lessons' worth of the separately-bound *English Explanations* booklet (Lessons 31 and 44).

## Morphological note

Continues `084`'s convention: kanji/kana form as `Term`, reading folded into `Notes` where not
independently recoverable. Level II's major new morphological territory relative to Level I is
Japan's **verbal voice system** — passive (受身), causative (使役), and the two-tier honorific
(敬語) system of respectful (尊敬語) vs. humble (謙譲語) verb forms — all introduced as
irregular/suppletive verb paradigms rather than regular affixation, and captured as grammar points
below with their full irregular-verb sets.

## Grammar points

### 受身 (passive voice) — three functionally distinct passives in one lesson (Lesson 37)

The lesson's own six example sentences cluster into three functionally distinct passive uses,
without labeling them separately in the source's own prose — but the clustering is unmistakable
once the examples are laid side by side: **(1) direct/neutral passive** (呼ばれる "to be called [by
someone]" — grammatical subject is the one summoned, no implication of suffering); **(2)
adversative/"suffering" passive**, a construction with no direct English equivalent, where the
grammatical subject is negatively affected by an event even when that subject isn't the thing
directly acted upon (わたしは弟にカメラを壊されました "I had my camera broken by my younger
brother" — the speaker, not the camera, is marked as the affected party; 泥棒にお金をとられました
"I had money taken by a thief"); and **(3) descriptive/process passive**, used for impersonal
manufacturing or institutional processes with no implied agent-responsibility framing at all (お酒
は米から…造られます; 車のボディーが溶接されます; 製品の50パーセントぐらい輸出されています).
This three-way split is directly relevant to register/slang mechanics: the adversative passive in
particular is a grammaticalized way of encoding the speaker's emotional stance (being put-upon)
purely through verb morphology, independent of any lexical choice.

### 敬語 (keigo) — the two-tier honorific system (Lessons 49–50, course capstone)

The course's final two lessons teach **尊敬語 (sonkeigo, respectful)** — used to elevate the
person being spoken about/to (lesson 49) — against **謙譲語 (kenjōgo, humble)** — used to lower the
speaker's own actions relative to the listener (lesson 50). Both tiers have (a) a **regular,
productive pattern** (sonkeigo: お+verb-stem+になる, e.g. お読みになる; kenjōgo: お+verb-stem+する,
e.g. お持ちする) and (b) a closed set of **irregular suppletive verbs** that replace common
high-frequency verbs outright rather than being derived by affixation:

- Sonkeigo irregulars: いらっしゃる (いる／来る／行く), ご覧になる (見る), なさる (する), ご存じ
  (知っている), 召し上がる (食べる／飲む).
- Kenjōgo irregulars: 申す (言う), 参る (来る／行く), おる (いる), 存じる／存じておる (知っている),
  拝見する (見る), 伺う (聞く／訪ねる), いただく (もらう／食べる／飲む).

The two lessons are constructed to pair directly against each other verb-for-verb (見る → ご覧になる
/拝見する; 食べる・飲む → 召し上がる／いただく; 知っている → ご存じ／存じている), and the textbook's
own dialogue stages a single exchange using both tiers on the same underlying verb in one turn (今晩
お宅にいらっしゃいますか。…はい、おります — honorific いらっしゃる for the addressee's presence,
humble おる for the speaker's own in the same breath), making the system's speaker/addressee-relative
polarity unusually explicit for a beginner-level source.

### Transitive/intransitive verb pairing as a systematic category (自動詞と他動詞 appendix)

Beyond the individual verb pairs listed above, the appendix's own organizing principle is worth
recording as a grammar point in its own right: Japanese systematically distinguishes transitive
(他動詞, "other-moving verb") from intransitive (自動詞, "self-moving verb") members of what are
often historically related verb stems, and the appendix explicitly tracks **which lesson each half
of a pair was introduced in** — meaning a learner typically meets the transitive member first (as
an action performed on an object) and the intransitive member later (as a resultant/stative
description), with the two reunited explicitly only in this late-course reference section. The
homophone case (かける／かかる meaning both "to hang" and, unrelatedly, "to telephone") is flagged by
the appendix listing both pairs as separate rows rather than merging them — itself a useful
register/lexical-ambiguity data point for later mechanics analysis.

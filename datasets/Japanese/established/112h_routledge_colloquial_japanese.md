## Grammar points

### Existence verbs (1): arimasu vs. imasu
Both correspond to English "there is/are", distinguished by animacy-and-mobility: arimasu for
inanimate things and immobile living things (e.g. plants), imasu for animate, self-moving things
(people, animals). Pattern: noun1(place)-ni noun2(thing/person)-ga arimasu/imasu. No singular/
plural distinction in Japanese (cross-referenced to the book's own Introduction, p. xii). p. 93.

### Particle ya: non-exhaustive listing
ya ("and so on") lists nouns as representative examples rather than an exhaustive set, contrasting
with the fully exhaustive "to" (Unit 6/7's "and"): "beddo to tsukue ga arimasu" (there are [exactly]
a bed and a desk) vs. "beddo ya tsukue ga arimasu" (there are several things such as a bed and a
desk). p. 96.

### Suffix counters
Japanese requires a shape/category-specific counter suffix when enumerating objects or people
(unlike English's bare numerals): -ko (small objects), -hon/pon/bon (long slender objects, with a
voicing/gemination alternation conditioned by the preceding numeral), -dai (machinery), -mai (thin
flat objects), -nin (people, with hitori/futari as suppletive forms for 1/2), -kai (occurrences/
times). A separate, more general -tsu series (hitotsu...tō) covers generic material objects but
only up to ten; beyond that, bare numerals are used. Each counter has its own interrogative form
(e.g. nan-bon "how many [long slender objects]?"). A counter takes no particle of its own in a
sentence: noun1(place)-ni noun2(thing/person)-ga [counter] arimasu/imasu. pp. 97-98.

### Kudasai: requesting/purchasing
kudasai attaches after a counted noun phrase to request or decide to purchase something: "Kyōto
no chizu o 1-mai kudasai" ("Please give me a map of Kyoto"). p. 98.

### Location nouns
A closed set of relational nouns indicates position: ue/shita/hidari/migi/mae/ushiro/naka/soto/
chikaku/tonari, each combining with no + a reference noun, then ni + arimasu/imasu ("Tēburu no ue
ni ringo ga arimasu" — "There is an apple on the table"). A separate construction, "X to Y no
aida" ("between X and Y"), covers the two-reference-point case. pp. 100, 102.

### Existence of things and people (2): wa vs. ga marking
When both speaker and listener already share knowledge of (or can assume the existence of) a
referent, it is promoted to sentence topic and marked with wa instead of ga: "Yūbinkyoku wa doko
ni arimasu ka" ("Where is the post office?", assuming a post office exists) vs. "Kono chikaku ni
yūbinkyoku ga arimasu ka" ("Is there a post office near here?", genuinely uncertain whether one
exists). This is the book's first explicit treatment of the wa/ga topic-vs.-subject distinction
tied to shared/assumed knowledge rather than just grammatical role. p. 101.

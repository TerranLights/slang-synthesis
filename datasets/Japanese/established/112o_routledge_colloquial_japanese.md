ta/da for te/de (same Group 1/2/3 rules as Unit 13's te-form, same itta-not-*ikita irregular

## Grammar points

### Verb ta-form derivation
Identical to the te-form derivation rules from Unit 13, substituting ta/da for te/de throughout:
Group 1 i/chi/ri→tta, bi/mi/ni→nda, ki→ita, gi→ida, shi→shita (with the same itta-not-*ikita
irregular exception for ikimasu); Group 2/3 simply replace masu with ta (tabemasu→tabeta,
kimasu→kita, shimasu→shita). p. 179-180.

### Linking verb sentences: [V-ta]+ri, [V-ta]+ri...
Chaining ta-form verbs with ri implies the listed activities are merely representative examples of
what the agent did/does, not an exhaustive account — explicitly contrasted in-text with the plain
te-form chaining from Unit 14, which carries no such "for example" implication: "Watashi wa kinō
terebi o mitari, hon o yondari shimashita" ("Yesterday, I did various things such as watching TV
and reading a book") vs. "Watashi wa kinō terebi o mite, hon o yomimashita" ("Yesterday, I watched
TV and then read a book"). This pattern obligatorily ends in shimasu or one of its inflected
forms. pp. 181-182.

### After ~: [V-ta]/[noun]+no + ato de
"After" is ato de, attaching to either a noun+no or a verb's ta-form: "Daigaku o sotsugyō shita
ato de nani o shitai desu ka" ("What do you want to do after graduating from university?"); "Gohan
no ato de shimasu" / "Gohan o tabeta ato de shimasu" ("I do it after the meal" / "after eating the
meal") show the noun- and verb-based variants side by side. de is frequently dropped in informal
speech. pp. 183-184.

### Talking about experience: [V-ta] + koto ga arimasu
This construction states whether the speaker has ever had a given experience, negated by
switching arimasu to arimasen: "Watashi wa kimono o kita koto ga arimasu" ("I have worn kimono
[before]"). The book explicitly distinguishes this from a plain past-tense statement
([V-mashita]): the koto-ga-arimasu form asks/asserts whether an experience has occurred at any
point, while a bare past tense just reports that a specific action took place, with no
experiential framing — "Watashi wa kyonen kimono o kimashita" ("I wore kimono last year") simply
narrates an event. pp. 185-186.

### Clothes and accessories: five distinct "wear" verbs
Unlike English's single verb "wear", Japanese requires one of five verbs depending on the clothing
item's type: kimasu (upper-body garments — sweaters, shirts, coats, suits, underwear, kimono),
hakimasu (lower-body/leg garments — trousers, skirts, shoes, socks), kaburimasu (headwear — caps/
hats), kakemasu (glasses), and shimasu (small worn accessories — ties, necklaces, watches). The
same [V-te]+imasu stative pattern from Unit 14 applies to express what someone is currently
wearing (kite imasu, kakete imasu). pp. 179, 189.

### Kanji of Unit 15: school/family/time-related characters
本先生学好何字年車每 ("book/previous/birth-live/study/like/what/letter/year/car/every") — see the
Vocabulary table above for kun-yomi/on-yomi/meaning per character; notably 先生 ("sensei",
literally "born before") and 学生 ("gakusei", "student") become readable compounds once both halves
are known kanji. p. 187.

## Grammar points

### mō + past tense: "already"
mō ("already") obligatorily co-occurs with the past affirmative -mashita form to assert a
completed action, or with -mashita ka to ask whether it was completed: "Watashi wa mō densha no
kippu o kaimashita" ("I have already bought the train ticket"). A negative answer uses mada desu
("not yet") rather than a negated verb. p. 106.

### Expressing desire: [Verb-stem] + tai desu
Attaching tai to a verb's stem (the masu-form minus masu) expresses the speaker's own desire to
perform that action; tai itself inflects exactly like an i-adjective across all four
tense/polarity combinations: tai desu (want to), takunai desu (do/does not want to), katta desu
(wanted to), kunakatta desu (did not want to). The object of the desired verb may be marked with
either o or ga (unusual flexibility compared to the normally-fixed object marker elsewhere in the
book); other particles (e, de, ni for a person met) remain unchanged. Using this construction to
ask a senior/superior person what they want is considered impolite — a plain -masu-ka question is
preferred instead ("Sensē wa bīru o nomimasu ka" rather than *nomitai desu ka). pp. 106-107.

### Purpose of motion: [V-stem]+ni + ikimasu/kimasu/kaerimasu
States the purpose of going/coming/returning: noun1(person)-wa noun2(place)-e [V-stem(+noun3)]-ni
ikimasu/kimasu/kaerimasu. The purpose particle ni follows a verb stem (or a shimasu-compound noun
directly, e.g. "Nihon-go no benkyō ni kimashita" — "I came for the purpose of studying Japanese").
pp. 108-109.

### Invitation: [V-stem] + masen ka
A polite way to suggest doing something together, typically paired with issho ni ("together"):
"Issho ni Fujisan e ikimasen ka" ("Won't you/would you like to go to Mt. Fuji with me?"). p. 111.

### Accepting/declining: [V-stem] + mashō, and the chotto... refusal strategy
mashō ("let's") accepts a masen-ka invitation ("Hai, tabemashō" — "Yes, let's eat"). To decline
politely without a flat refusal, speakers trail off after chotto ("a little") in an unfinished
sentence (Zannen desu ga, chotto... / Sumimasen ga, chotto...) — the listener is expected to infer
the refusal from the hesitation itself rather than require it spelled out; a sociolinguistically
notable indirect-refusal pattern worth flagging for the mechanics-analysis phase. Supporting fixed
expressions: tsugō ga warui desu ("[the schedule] is unsuitable for me"), zannen desu ga ("it's
unfortunate but..."). pp. 112-113.

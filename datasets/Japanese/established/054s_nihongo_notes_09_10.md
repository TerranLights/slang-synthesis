## Grammar points

### Tema/tesuu (human time/labor) vs. jikan (time in general) (p.126-127)
*Tema* and *tesuu* both refer to a human being's expenditure of time or effort and are often
interchangeable, but *tesuu* specifically emphasizes labor/trouble while *tema* emphasizes the amount
of time spent. Neither substitutes for *jikan*, the general-purpose word for time as an impersonal
quantity — a simple "we still have plenty of time" statement requires *jikan*, not *tema*/*tesuu*. The
contracted form *tema-doru* ("tema-o toru" run together) specifically means "time-taking," often with
a note of mild complaint about someone being slow.

### O as the cause-of-emotion marker with certain verbs (p.128-129)
Emotion-describing adjectives default to *ga* for marking their cause (*Nani-ga ureshii-no*). But
certain inherently verbal emotion-words (*yorokobu* "be glad," *kanashimu* "be sad," *okoru* "be
angry") take *o* instead to mark the cause — and this same *o*-marking carries over when an emotion
*adjective* is converted into a verb via the *-garu* suffix (*ureshii* → *ureshigaru*), switching it
from *ga* to *o*. A *nani*-question about the cause of someone's feelings also defaults to *o*-marking
with the relevant verbs. Some emotion-related verbs instead take *ni* or *...no koto-de*, with the
choice depending on situation/style rather than a single fixed rule.

### Kurushii vs. tsurai: physical pain in common, different nonphysical extensions (p.130-131)
Both words cover direct physical pain, sometimes interchangeably. Their extensions to nonphysical pain
specialize differently: *kurushii* extends toward economic hardship (difficulty surviving on limited
means), while *tsurai* extends toward emotional/relational difficulty (lying to someone good, having
to manage former peers after a promotion).

### Dano vs. toka vs. ya vs. to: four particles for listing, differing in tone, exhaustiveness, and register (p.132-133)
*Dano...dano* lists examples with a specifically negative/complaining tone, restricted to
conversation. *Toka* lists non-exhaustive examples in either a positive or negative tone, also
conversation-only. *Ya* lists non-exhaustive examples neutrally, usable in both speech and writing.
*To* implies an exhaustive list (nothing else included). Uniquely among these, *toka* and *dano* can
also connect full quoted statements (not just nouns), typically stringing together a series of
complaints — a function *ya* does not share.

### Yoku vs. amari with negative predicates, and sentence-initial predictability (p.134-135)
Recapping and extending the yoku/amari contrast: in its "often/much" sense, *yoku* expects an
affirmative ending and sounds slightly off paired with a negative one, where *amari* is the natural
negative-predicate choice. (*Yoku* in its separate "well/skillfully" sense combines naturally with
negation.) More broadly, the opening of a Japanese sentence often signals in advance whether it will
end negatively, which is why the latter part of a sentence can be freely dropped once that framing is
established — the listener already knows how it ends.

### N-da-kara's self-defensive flavor, and its one licensed positive use (p.136-137)
*...n-da-kara*/*...n-desu-kara* states a reason emphatically. Used to explain one's own feelings it's
relatively neutral, but used to explain one's own actions it tends to sound self-defensive —
presupposing the listener's disagreement or disapproval, unlike the plainer *...desu/da-kara*, which
carries no such presupposition. There is, however, one context where this emphatic form is genuinely
useful rather than aggressive-sounding: overcoming a listener's polite hesitation to accept an offer,
by re-asserting the speaker's own stake in the matter ("I insist on paying, since I'm the one who
invited you").

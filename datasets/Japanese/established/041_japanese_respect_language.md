## Grammar points

### The basic distinction: respect language vs. neutral language (Ch. 1, pp. 15–16)

"Respect language" (keigo) is the special style of speech or writing Japanese uses to show respect
to people and, occasionally, to especially revered things (a shrine, the Imperial palace). "Neutral
language" is the style that takes no special measures to express respect — it uses few or no
honorific/humble words. A speaker/writer (the 1st person) can direct respect toward the person
addressed (2nd person), a person/thing talked about (3rd person), or both simultaneously; these are
often, but not always, the same individual.

### Situational factors governing respect-language use (Ch. 1, pp. 15–16)

The source notes several soft tendencies rather than hard rules: (1) everyone uses some respect
language at times, especially in letters — Japanese are more respectful in writing than in speech;
(2) women use respect language more consistently and extensively than men, tending toward more
*formal* speech generally; (3) first meetings trigger reciprocal respect display that fades into
informality (neutral language) as familiarity grows, especially among men.

### The "situation diagram" framework — core mechanism of the whole book (Ch. 1, pp. 17–25)

This is the book's central analytical device (later formalized as Appendix 1, "Standard Situation
Diagrams," pp. 174–175) and underlies every subsequent chapter. The framework represents a
conversation as circled numbers — ① for the 1st person (speaker), ② for the 2nd person
(addressee), ③ for a 3rd person talked about — connected by arrows labeled with the type of
language used:
- A **double-line arrow** (⇒) indicates the person *addressed*.
- A **single-line arrow** (→) indicates a person *discussed* (talked about, whether or not also
  addressed).
- A **dashed-line arrow** (⇢) indicates a person discussed *when not present*.
- **R** on an arrow = respectful language; **N** = neutral (non-respectful) language.
- A filled square (▪) represents an outsider — someone not of the speaker's own social circle (as
  opposed to circled numbers, who are within the speaker's circle).

Three general rules emerge from working through the standard situations (p. 25): (i) a speaker
shows respect to a member of his own circle only when that person is clearly senior to him *and* no
outsider is present; (ii) a speaker shows respect to an outsider (and anyone connected with that
outsider, not with himself) unless that person is clearly junior to him; (iii) in doubtful cases, a
speaker is more likely to show respect to a person if present than if absent.

Within one's own circle (family, workplace, etc.), the governing variable is **relative
seniority**: respect flows only upward (junior→senior uses R; senior→junior and equal↔equal use N).
A 3rd person who is a present member of the same circle is treated by this same seniority rule
regardless of the addressee; a 3rd person of the same circle who is *absent*, however, is usually
discussed in neutral language "regardless of his status" — presence vs. absence changes the
politeness calculus specifically for in-circle 3rd persons who outrank the speaker.

With an outsider as 2nd person, the pattern flips: the speaker defaults to respectful language
toward the outsider in two of the three possible seniority configurations (only clear outsider
juniority cancels it), and critically, when a 3rd person belonging to the *speaker's own* circle is
mentioned to an outsider, the speaker will almost never show that in-circle person respect — "it is
inappropriate to show respect to someone associated with oneself when talking to an outsider" (p.
22) — the classic in-group/out-group humility-inversion mechanic that also surfaces later as the
depreciatory-verb system (Chapters 5–6).

### Family-circle modification of the standard rules (Ch. 2, pp. 46–47)

Within the family specifically, the standard circle rules are modified two ways: (i) the
family's own informality suppresses the more elaborate verbal forms of respect (covered later in
the book); (ii) conversely, many respectful *nouns* (o-tōsan, nīsan, etc.) are used constantly
within the family regardless of whether the referent is present — unlike the general in-circle rule
that ties respectful reference to presence. Two further closed-circle peculiarities: a man may refer
to his own wife as okusan when speaking to a household servant (a status effect of the servant's low
position within the household circle, not real 2nd/3rd-person respect); and adults will use
respectful self-reference forms (e.g. "Bring it to Daddy") when speaking to small children, to model
the vocabulary the child is expected to learn, paralleling the English equivalent.

### Polite words vs. respectful words — the chapter's key distinction (Ch. 2, pp. 29)

The source draws a terminological distinction that this project's own Usage Tier taxonomy doesn't
currently split out, worth flagging for cross-language mechanics analysis: **polite words** (e.g.
watakushi, ikaga, dochira) raise the general tone of language out of concern for the impression made
on the 2nd person, without targeting respect at any specific referent; **respectful words** (e.g.
kata, anata, donata, o-/go- prefixed nouns) show respect to a specific person who may or may not be
the 2nd person. The o- prefix itself straddles both categories — it can either mark genuine
respectful reference (お母さん o-kāsan) or merely make speech sound generically "genteel" with no
respect implied (historically お-bīru, お-toire, now felt old-fashioned/overused — the source notes
this usage has receded since the original 1966 edition).

### Honorific prefix selection rule: o- vs. go- (Ch. 2, pp. 30–32)

General pattern (with acknowledged exceptions both ways): the honorific prefix for Chinese-origin
(Sino-Japanese) words is usually go-; for native Japanese words it is, with one or two exceptions,
o-; words of Western origin always take o-. Some words are at a transitional stage accepting either
prefix (返事 henji "reply" → ご返事 go-henji or お返事 o-henji, the latter now more common) — an
attested instance of a prefix's productive domain visibly shifting within the speech community,
independent of etymology.

### Honorific suffix system: -san vs. -sama, and the -chan/-chama childish variant (Ch. 2, pp. 32–39)

-san is the default honorific suffix, usable with almost any name or descriptive noun, often with no
clean English equivalent. -sama is more formal/respectful than -san, but is normally only available
as a *replacement* for -san on words that already combine both an initial o- and a final -san (i.e.
only the "double-marked" o-...-san shape unlocks -sama) — plain -san words without an o- prefix (姉
さん nēsan, 兄さん nīsan, 息子さん musuko-san) generally cannot take -sama instead. A small, named
exception list breaks this pattern: 妹様 imōto-sama, ご両親様 go-ryōshin-sama, どなた様
donata-sama, どちら様 dochira-sama all take -sama despite lacking the o-...-san shape — flagged
explicitly by the source as irregular. Proper nouns (personal/family names) taking -san are already
respectful enough in themselves to take -sama instead when extra respect is warranted (e.g. the
Crown Prince as 皇太子様 Kōtaishi-sama). どちら様 dochira-sama is too respectful for impersonal
reference and has narrowed to function as a personal pronoun meaning "who?" rather than literally
"which direction, sir."

The -chan/-chama variant pair (an s→ch sound change on -san/-sama) marks a close, often
parent-child or child-to-child, relationship and is characteristically childish in register — used
particularly to and by children, and extendable to "honorary" aunts/uncles outside the blood family.
妹 imōto and 弟 otōto are exceptional among family words in NOT taking -chan/-chama, because
respectful forms of these two words are not normally used within the family circle to begin with
(personal names are used instead for younger siblings within the family).

### Respect to outsiders about an in-circle 3rd person vs. about an unconnected 3rd person (Ch. 2, pp. 48–49)

Extending the Chapter 1 diagrams: when discussing a 3rd person entirely unconnected with both
speaker and addressee (not of either one's circle), the speaker's respect-or-not choice tracks that
3rd person's own apparent status/seniority, exactly as if that person were being discussed within
one's own circle — independent of the 2nd person's outsider/insider status. This is distinguished
from the "never show respect to your own in-circle 3rd person when talking to an outsider" rule
covered earlier, which is specifically about a 3rd person who *does* belong to the speaker's own
circle.

## Grammar points

### ni-oite / ni-shite / ni-shite-mo / ni-shite-wa / nite — formal/conditional combination particles built on "ni"
Paraphrased from pp.138–142. "Ni-oite" is a formal locative/field-of-activity marker ("in the fields of both art and music, there aren't too many more talented than her"). "Ni-shite" marks a time/place/condition ("while on a trip, he was stricken with an illness"). "Ni-shite-mo" introduces a hypothetical condition ("even if you were to scold a child..."). "Ni-shite-wa" marks a result exceeding the norm for its category ("for winter, it's been relatively warm these past few days"). "Nite" is the formal equivalent of "de", covering location ("the next conference will be held in Hiroshima") and reason/motive ("I was absent due to a cold").

### no / n (の/ん) — the core possessive/nominalizer particle, the dictionary's other giant entry
Paraphrased from pp.143–158. Between two nouns, covers plain possession (including personal-pronoun case-marking, since Japanese pronouns have no inflected cases of their own), relative location ("under the desk"), material composition ("a wooden bridge"), creative authorship ("a novel by Kawabata Yasunari"), relationship ("my uncle"), category membership ("a science teacher"), attribution ("an old woman of ninety"), place-name modification, and state-of-being ("has become senile"). It also converts certain color nouns into adjectives, serves as a bare indefinite pronoun ("the one over here is sweet"), marks time-origin ("a newspaper from ten years ago"), substitutes for the subject-marking "ga" in modifying clauses, builds the "as/like" comparison pattern "no yoo na/ni", and nominalizes a verb clause into "a fact/matter that..." (typically completing a five-senses or emotion verb, e.g. "I can see an airplane flying"). Sentence-finally, four more senses appear: a softened command explicitly marked as women's language ("you're going to take a bath now, all right?"); a bare emphatic ("the fact is that I had a quarrel with Miyo"); an informal rising-intonation question (informal for "no desu ka"); and a casual emphatic explicitly marked as typically used by elderly men (sometimes pronounced "noo"). The "...no...no~no~no" pattern contrasts two actions ("going or not going"), and the colloquial contraction of "no tokoro" yields forms like "n toko"/"n chi" ("my place").

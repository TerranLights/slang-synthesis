## Grammar points

### Kanji built from legible sub-components, including non-obvious "leg" radicals

Beyond whole simpler kanji appearing inside complex ones (reviewed from Unit 2), this unit introduces two additional component shapes that don't correspond to standalone taught kanji: 儿 ("human legs," seen in 見 "to see") and 八-shaped ハ ("animal legs," seen in 買 "to buy"). It also notes that 人 ("person") takes an abbreviated form (イ-like) when used as a left-hand component inside a more complex kanji (p.44).

### Mnemonic "story" method for complex kanji

Each of the unit's 14 new verb-kanji is paired with a short narrative "story" linking its visual components to its meaning (e.g. 休 "to rest" = a person 人 resting against a tree 木 during a work break; 聞 "to listen/hear" = an ear 耳 pressed between the gates 門 of a house to catch gossip). The method is explicitly framed as a memory technique, not an etymological claim, though several of the stories do describe the kanji's genuine historical composition (pp. 44–49).

### 物 (mono) as a noun-forming (nominalizing) suffix

Appending 物 ("thing") to a verb-kanji converts the action into a related noun: 買 ("to buy") + 物 → 買物 ("shopping," literally "buying things"). The same pattern yields 飲物 (drinks), 食物 (food), 売物 (items for sale), 見物 (sightseeing), 書物 (a book/writing), 読物 (reading material) — a fully regular derivational pattern applied across all of the unit's new verb kanji (p.51).

### Literal vs. idiomatic compound meanings, continued

Building on Unit 2's literal/abstract distinction, this unit gives more elaborate "amusing" idiomatic examples: 出目金 ("go-out eye gold" = a pop-eyed goldfish) and 休火山 ("resting fire mountain" = a dormant volcano) — the volcano metaphor of "resting" extends the literal fire-mountain image from Unit 2's 火山 into a further figurative layer (p.52).

### Basic sentence word order: Subject–Object–Verb (SOV), with a preview of は/を particles

The unit's worked example sentences (not yet explained grammatically, since hiragana — needed to read the particles — isn't taught until Unit 5) demonstrate that Japanese sentences place the verb at the end: "男の人は車を買いました。" is glossed literally as "The man a car bought" (natural English: "The man bought a car"). The learner is told to focus only on recognizing the kanji content words they already know and to infer the whole sentence meaning by matching against a provided English translation list, rather than parsing the hiragana particles (は, を) or verb conjugation (買いました, past tense) directly at this stage (p.50).

### Memorizing onyomi readings via English-sound mnemonics

The book suggests inventing a short English sentence that phonetically echoes a kanji's onyomi to aid memorization, e.g. for 食 (onyomi SHOKU, "to eat"): "It was a SHOCK how much food he could eat"; for 見 (onyomi KEN, "to look"): "KEN looked out of the window" (p.53).

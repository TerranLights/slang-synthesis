# Japanese — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Japanese Language Insights, Chapters 1-9

**Source:** Zen Loy & Harumi Willow, *Japanese Language Insights* (『なるほど、日本語』/ *Naruhodo
Nihongo*), Soroban Pty Ltd, Sydney, 1996 (ISBN 0 646 28530 0). Front matter + Chapters 1-9
(printed pages 3-51; PDF pages 8-32 of 53).

**Coverage note:** Full extraction of the front matter (Preface, Introduction, chapter-by-chapter
summary) and Chapters 1-9 (I/watakushi, You/anata, Greetings/aisatsu, What-Why-When/nani,
Counting/kazoekata, Expressions/hyoogen, Quarrelsome Words/kenkago, Four Letter Words/warui
kotoba, Kana) of this 53-page, 17-chapter popular-reference booklet. The book has no text layer
(scanned, pre-OCR 1996 print) and was scanned as two-printed-pages-per-PDF-page spreads (53 PDF
pages ≈ front matter + 85 printed pages + index); every spread was vision-read directly, per
`00_Reference_Extraction_Spec.md`'s vision-reading guard. This is a discursive, essay-style
reference (not a vocabulary-box-style textbook), so the "every distinct vocabulary item" rule was
applied to every named/italicized term the authors introduce and discuss, plus the example-sentence
vocabulary that is not purely grammatical filler.

**Vision-reading guard — marginalia found and excluded.** This copy (ex-library, TAFE NSW/S.I.T.
Library Randwick stamps) carries several previous-reader pencil marks, all excluded from
extraction:
- Empty pencil circles (no added text) around printed words on pp. 18, 20, 22, 28, and 30 — pure
  emphasis marks with no informational content, left out of the vocabulary table.
- One handwritten pencil gloss, the word "kata", written in the margin next to the printed sentence
  "Ano kata wa donata desu ka" on p. 24 — almost certainly a reader's own note glossing the kanji
  方 (read *kata*, "person," polite). This is treated as reader-added marginalia, not source
  content, per the extraction spec's handwriting-vs-print guard, and is excluded; the printed
  sentence itself is extracted normally with 方 glossed from context already present in print.

**Overlap note:** Chapter 9 (Kana) covers hiragana/katakana and basic verb-conjugation material
that likely overlaps with Wave 1's "Writing System foundation" sources. Per dispatch instruction
("given the compact size, extract in full"), this chapter was extracted in full rather than
cross-checked against Wave 1 established/ files for redundancy; a future mechanics-analysis pass
should treat this chapter's godan/verb-level framework as a second independent presentation to
compare against Wave 1's.

## Grammar points

### Avoidance of first-person self-reference
Japanese conversational norms default to omitting any first-person pronoun once the referent is
clear from context; stating "I" (watakushi) is reserved for introducing oneself or genuinely
emphasizing a contrast ("as for me, it's..."). The book frames this as reflecting a broader
humbleness/deference convention — the speaker normally adopts the more humble position relative to
the listener (p.8-11).

### Stratified first-person pronoun system
Unlike English "I," Japanese has many first-person words stratified by gender, formality, and era:
watakushi (polite) > watashi (colloquial) > atashi (feminine colloquial) / boku, ore (masculine
colloquial) > wagahai (archaic/exalted) > achiki (historical, geisha/pleasure-quarter register) >
ware/wareware (formal, singular/plural). The plural suffix -tachi attaches to several of these
(watakushi-tachi, boku-tachi) (p.9-11).

### Avoidance of second-person reference and its substitutes
As with "I," the word for "you" (anata) is avoided with the listener; Japanese instead uses the
listener's name, role, or title (o-toosan "father," kachoo "boss," sensei "teacher") or, on the
telephone, sochira ("that side"). Anata is reserved for intimate use between lovers or spouses, and
has given rise to a secondary vocabulary of other "you" words at varying registers: kimi
(masculine, informal), o-mae (historically polite, now abrupt — except retained as an affectionate
"darling" from husband to wife), and kisama (rare) (p.12-15).

### Interrogative word system and the -mo suffix
The six basic interrogatives (nani "what," naze/dooshite "why," itsu "when," doo/ikaga "how,"
doko/dochira "where," dare/donata "who") combine productively with suffixes: adding ka creates an
indefinite pronoun (nani ka "something"); adding mo creates a polarity-sensitive universal/negative
quantifier — with a positive verb it means "every" (itsu mo "always"), with a negative verb it
means "none/never" (itsu mo...masen "never"). The combination naze mo is not used (p.21-27).

### Dual counting systems and the counter (josuushi) system
Japanese has two parallel counting systems for 1-10: a native series (hitotsu, futatsu...) that
needs no counter, and a Sino-Japanese series (ichi, ni, san...) that requires a counter word
(josuushi) specifying the type of object enumerated (e.g. -hon for long thin objects, -hiki for
small animals, -mai for flat objects). Several counters undergo euphonic consonant changes
depending on the preceding numeral (h→b/p with 1,3,6,8,10; k→g, s→z, t→d elsewhere), and some
counters have irregular forms for specific numbers (hitori/futari "one/two people" instead of
ichinin/ninin; tsuitachi/juu-yokka/hatsuka for the 1st/14th/20th days of the month) (p.28-33).

### Numeral taboo and numerical wordplay
4 (shi) and 9 (ku) are avoided as unlucky because they are homophonous with "death" and
"pain/suffering" respectively. Conversely, this same dense homophony is exploited for puns and
mnemonics — e.g. reading digit strings as word sequences (4919 as "yoku iku," meaning "to go
often") or using numerals mixed with katakana in shop signage (p.32-33).

### Gendered and tonal interjection system
Many Japanese exclamations are marked for the speaker's gender (maa/ara feminine vs. are
masculine) and the book's transcription marks pitch direction (rising vs. falling tone) as
contrastive — the same syllable with different intonation serves different pragmatic functions
(p.34-35).

### Doomo as a general-purpose politeness intensifier
The single word doomo (from doo mo, "however, in all ways") can stand alone for "thank you,"
"excuse me," or "sorry," with the specific sense supplied by context; it also combines with fuller
polite expressions (doomo arigatoo gozaimasu, doomo sumimasen) for emphasis (p.38-39).

### Gendered confrontational register (kenkago)
Quarrelsome/confrontational expressions systematically come in masculine/feminine sentence-final
pairs built on the same underlying content, differing chiefly in the sentence-final particle (wa/ne
for feminine speech vs. yo/da/plain for masculine speech) (p.40-43).

### Taboo-word register and the baseline politeness effect
Because the Japanese politeness system is already so pervasive and fine-grained, the book argues
that merely using an impolite register (e.g. the plain pronouns kimi or omae) can convey strong
disrespect without resorting to any taboo vocabulary at all — the force of genuinely taboo words
(Ch.8) therefore lands harder on Japanese ears than equivalent English profanity would (p.45).

### Hiragana/katakana functional division and the godan verb-conjugation framework
Hiragana (cursive, native-derived) marks grammatical inflection and native vocabulary; katakana
(angular, derived from kanji fragments) marks loanwords and emphasis. The chapter builds a
systematic "five levels" (godan) framework mapping the a-i-u-e-o vowel endings of the kana table
onto verb morphology: u-level = dictionary/plain form; a-level = negation; i-level = polite
(-masu) and desiderative (-tai) stems; e-level = imperative; o-level = volitional ("let's..."). The
framework applies uniformly to regular (godan and ichidan, e.g. taberu/miru) and irregular verbs
(kuru, suru), with euphonic adjustments noted at each level (p.46-51).

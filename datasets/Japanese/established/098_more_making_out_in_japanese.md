## Grammar points

### Gendered speech markers (♀/♂ and `(b→g)` notation)

Throughout the book, phrases suited only to female speakers are marked ♀ and those suited only to
male speakers ♂; unmarked phrases are usable by either. A separate `(b→g)` notation (seen p.6, *Kirei-
dayo*, "You look beautiful") marks a phrase a boy should specifically use when addressing a girl. The
authors caution (p.6) that this split is not absolute — cross-gender borrowing happens, especially
among younger speakers — and that the gender gap in Japanese speech is actively narrowing.

### Rising intonation as question-marker (◆ diamond notation)

Certain statement-form phrases are marked in the source with a ◆ diamond to show that adding a rising
final intonation turns them into a semi-question (p.11, e.g. *Hisashiburi-ne* → "...have I?"). The
book frames rising intonation generally as doing much of the grammatical work that `des-ka`/`mas-ka`
question markers do in formal speech.

### Potential-form contrast: external vs. internal inability to perceive

The book draws an explicit three-way distinction (p.27) between *minakatta* ("didn't see it" —
simply didn't look), *mienakatta* ("couldn't see it" — blocked by an external obstruction, or missed
the opportunity, e.g. missed a showing at the cinema), and *mi(ra)renakatta* ("couldn't see it" — due
to one's own visual/perceptual limitation). This is a genuinely three-way semantic split on a single
English gloss ("couldn't see"), worth preserving distinctly for mechanics analysis.

### Katakana-ization of English loanwords

The introduction (pp.7–8) lays out explicit phonological adaptation rules the Japanese use when
borrowing English words into katakana: (1) only one final consonant exists in native phonology ("n"),
so other consonant-final words get a vowel appended, usually "u" (*gēmu* for "game," *beddo* for
"bed"); (2) no final "r" sound exists, so a long "a" vowel substitutes (*rokkā* for "locker"); (3) no
"l" sound exists at all, so "r" substitutes throughout; (4) the historically-missing "we"/"v" sounds
are now written with dedicated katakana (ウェ for "we," ヴ + vowel for "v," e.g. *va* ヴァ, *vi* ヴィ).

### Pronoun system: formality/gender/harshness gradient

The book (p.9) lays out a full pronoun paradigm rather than teaching a single "I"/"you": *watashi*
(neutral) / *watakushi* (super-formal) for "I" generally, with *atashi* (feminine-casual), *boku*
and *ore* (masculine-casual, the latter "very harsh") as gendered-casual alternatives; and *anata*
(textbook-neutral) / *anta* (feminine-casual) / *kimi* (textbook-safe, masculine-leaning) / *omae*
("very harsh," masculine) for "you." The authors deliberately chose the "safest" neutral pair
(*watashi*/*anata*, *boku*/*kimi*) for the book's own example phrases, flagging *ore* and *omae* as
risky until a learner can correctly judge the social context.

### Slang sentence-final particles and endings

A cluster of particles/endings is presented together as the core toolkit for casual spoken Japanese
(pp.10–11): *ne*/*nē* (familiar emphatic, historically feminine-leaning but increasingly
gender-neutral), *na* (masculine alternative to *ne*), *yo* (assertive "I'm telling you"), *wa*
(feminine softener), *-noda*/*-nda* (explanatory "that's the way it is"), *zo* (assertive "let's do"),
*ze* (very strong command), and *-kke* (uncertainty/forgetfulness). The book explicitly frames these
as combinable and as carrying more real communicative weight than the words of a sentence themselves.

### Register ordering in food/drink criticism

Chapter 3 explicitly orders near-synonymous negative-taste phrases from safest to bluntest (pp.35–36):
*māmā* / *yokunai* / *oishikunai* (safe for a host's home cooking) versus *mazui* / *hidoi* (reserved
for restaurants, where no one present is personally responsible for the food). This is an explicit,
source-marked register/politeness gradient on a single semantic concept ("it tastes bad").

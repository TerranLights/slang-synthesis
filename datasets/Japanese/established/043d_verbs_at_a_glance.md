## Grammar points

### -Ba ... hodo: "the more X, the more Y" (p. 119)

The same verb appears twice — first in the -ba conditional form, then in the plain form followed by
hodo ほど — to describe a property intensifying as the action progresses: mireba miru hodo "the
more you look at it, the [more beautiful/etc.] it gets."

### Dake atte: "just as one would expect, precisely because" (p. 120)

Preceded by the plain form or informal past tense, this pattern shows that one action or property
follows naturally from another — e.g. praising a Nobel laureate professor as sharp-minded "dake
atte" (precisely because she won the prize).

### Dake demo / dake (wa) / dake: "even just doing as much as one can" (pp. 120–121)

The plain form precedes dake demo, dake wa, or bare dake to express doing as much as one can under
the circumstances — "iku dake demo itte mimashō," "let's at least go and see," even without
certainty of success.

### -Gachi: "tending to, prone to" (pp. 121–122)

Attached to the /-masu/ stem, this suffix indicates a tendency or habitual characteristic —
wasuregachi "tending to be forgetful," kumorigachi no tenki "mostly cloudy weather."

### Gurai nara ... hō ga ii: "it's better to do X than Y" (p. 122)

The lesser of two evils: the verb before gurai nara is the plain form, the verb before hō ga ii can
be plain form or informal past. Alternate forms: kurai nara for gurai nara, and the more colloquial/
blunter hō ga mashi da for hō ga ii. Used rhetorically in examples like "I'd rather die than take
money from my older brother" and "better to flee than die going to war."

### Ikura -te mo ... -nai: "no matter how much X, not Y" (p. 123)

The first half (ikura + -te mo) corresponds to "no matter how much I did/do something"; the
following clause must be negative — "ikura sagashite mo mitsukaranai," "no matter how much I look
for it, I can't find it."

### To ieba: introducing a related new topic (pp. 123–124)

Either a plain-form verb or a noun may precede to ieba, used to pivot to a new but related topic —
"Takada to ieba mada gorufu yatte 'ru ka nā," "now that you mention Takada, I wonder if he still
plays golf."

### To itte mo ii hodo da: "one could even go so far as to say" (pp. 124–125)

Preceded by a plain-form verb, the informal past tense, or a noun; the book notes itte 言って is also
commonly pronounced yutte in conversation — "saidai no higai to itte mo ii hodo deshō," "you could
say it was [probably] the worst damage [since the war]."

### Wa iu made mo naku: "not only X but also Y" (pp. 125–126)

Corresponds to the first half of English "not only... but also...," with the "but also" clause
using mo in place of wa — "onīsan wa iu made mo naku, otōto-san mo yūshū desu ne," "not only the
older brother, of course, but the younger one too is excellent."

### To iu yori wa mushiro: "not so much X as Y" (p. 126)

Usually a noun precedes to, expressing "not so much this as that" — "gakusha to iu yori wa mushiro
seijika desu," "not so much a scholar as a politician."

### (To iu) wake de wa nai: "it's not that..." (pp. 126–127)

Follows the plain form; corresponds roughly to "it's not that I won't..., but..." — used to soften
or qualify a prior negative-sounding statement without fully committing to it.

### A kara B ni kakete: "from A to/into B" (p. 127)

A and B are nouns of time or place; means a span "from A and into B" — "ashita kara asatte ni
kakete," "from tomorrow and into the day after."

### To/ni kuraberu to: "compared to..." (p. 128)

The particle before kuraberu to may be either to or ni — "mukashi to kuraberu to," "compared to the
old days."

### O kagiri ni: marking an ending/fundamental change point (p. 128)

Follows a noun; specifies the time when something ends or changes fundamentally — "kongakki o
kagiri ni daigaku o yameru," "to drop out of college as of the end of this semester."

### Kagiri wa: "as long as..." (p. 129)

Follows the plain form of the verb, often negative — "ijimetari shinai kagiri wa daijōbu desu yo,"
"he's fine as long as you don't mistreat him [referring to a dog]."

### Ka to omou to / ka to omottara: "no sooner had X than Y" (pp. 129–130)

Both mean "as soon as"; follow the informal past tense — "sora ga kuraku natta ka to omou to,
ōtsubu no ame ga furidashita," "no sooner had the sky turned dark than large raindrops began to
fall."

### Kanarazu shi mo ... to wa kagiranai / to wa ienai: "it isn't necessarily so" (pp. 130–131)

Follows a plain-form verb, or — when the topic has already been established — the demonstrative
pronoun sō そう in place of repeating the verb: "kanarazu shi mo sō to wa ienai," "that's not
necessarily true [of every case]."

### -Kaneru: "unable to do, unable to endure/accept doing" (p. 131)

Attached to the /-masu/ stem; often translates as "cannot," but carries the specific nuance of being
unable to endure or accept doing something, frequently in a formal/business register — "o-hikiuke
dekikaneru to mōshite orimashita," "[he] said we would be unable to comply."

### Koto ni wa: nominalizer with two distinct uses (pp. 131–133)

Follows the plain present negative or informal past form (affirmative or negative). With a negative
verb, it carries the meaning "unless/until something is done, something else will not follow." With
an affirmative verb, it emphasizes a verb expressing emotion — "odoroita koto ni wa," "to my
surprise."

### Koto wa nai: emphatic negative past experience (p. 133)

The negative counterpart to the ordinary "describing a past experience" pattern (-ta koto ga aru);
emphasizes that one has *never* done something — "yakusoku yabutta koto wa nai," "[he] has never
once broken a promise."

### Koto mo nai: weak double-negative affirmative (p. 134)

Follows the plain negative form; the resulting double negative leaves real doubt about whether the
action will occur — "kawanai koto mo nai," "it's not that I wouldn't buy it [depending on price]."

### Koto wa ... ga: "X did happen, but (unsatisfyingly)" (pp. 134–135)

The same verb appears twice in the informal past tense, once before koto wa and once before ga;
expresses that despite best effort, the result was disappointing — "itta koto wa itta n' desu ga,
konde ite hairenakatta n' desu yo," "I did go, but it was so crowded I couldn't get in."

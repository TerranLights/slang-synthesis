## Grammar points

### Four types of kanji (full taxonomy)

The unit names four kanji-formation categories, the first three already encountered in Units 1–6: (1) **pictorial kanji** (~2–3% of all kanji) derived from simple nature pictures, e.g. 山, 川, 人; (2) **simple abstract kanji** (a very small group) conveying ideas via symbols rather than pictures, e.g. the number kanji from Unit 3; (3) **complex pictorial kanji** (~2–3%) combining two or more pictorial kanji into a new meaning, e.g. 林 (wood), 男 (man), 明 (bright); (4) **sound-and-meaning kanji** (~90% of all kanji — by far the largest category), where part of the character conveys general meaning (the radical) and another part conveys the onyomi reading, e.g. 時 (time) and 転 (turn). This unit focuses specifically on categories 3 and 4 (p.88–89).

### The radical system: what a radical is and why it matters

A radical is a kanji component — usually itself a simple pictorial kanji — that gives a clue to the whole character's general meaning; kanji dictionaries group entries by shared radical. There are 214 traditional radicals in the Chinese classification system used by standard dictionaries including Nelson's. Common left-hand radicals already learned include 人 (person), 口 (mouth), 土 (earth), 女 (woman), 日 (sun/day), 子 (child), 月 (moon — also, less commonly, "flesh" from a squashed form of 肉), 木 (tree), 火 (fire), 田 (rice field), 目 (eye), 言 (say/words), 金 (gold/metal — as radical, "metal"), and 車 (car/vehicle/wheel) (pp. 90–91).

### Radicals can change shape or appear in positions other than the left

A radical's printed form may be "squashed" (e.g. 土 compresses in 埋), lose strokes (e.g. 木 shortens in 林), or change shape altogether (e.g. 人 becomes 亻 in 体; 手 becomes 扌 in 折; 水 becomes 氵 in 泊). Beyond the most common left-side position, radicals also appear on the right (彡 "shape" in 形; 阝 "capital/village" in 都), above (艹 "vegetation" in 花/茶; 宀 "hat/cover" in 安), below (木 in 楽 "enjoyable"; 心 in 急 "emergency"; 灬, a shape-shifted "fire," in 煮 "boil," looking like four small flames), fully surrounding (囗 "enclosure" in 国 "country"), or partially surrounding (广 in 店 "shop"; 辶 "road/way" in 道; 門 in 聞 "listen"). A single radical (e.g. 木 "tree," 火 "fire," 心 "heart") can appear in more than one position across different kanji (pp. 93–94).

### Shared-right-side onyomi groups

Beyond the radical (meaning-bearing) side, the non-radical side of a sound-and-meaning kanji frequently indicates a shared onyomi reading across multiple otherwise-unrelated kanji. Worked examples: the 寺-based group (寺 temple, 時 time, 持 hold, 侍 samurai, 崎 tower/soar, 詩 poem) all read JI or SHI; the 召-based group (招, 紹, 詔, 昭, 沼) all read SHŌ; the 青-based group (情, 晴, 清, 請, 精) all read SEI; and a 干-based group (汗 perspire, 肝 liver, 竿 pole/rod, 旱 drought, 鼾 snore, and others) share a KAN-type reading. This confirms the sound-and-meaning model directly: the left/radical side narrows the meaning-family, the right side narrows the pronunciation (pp. 92–96).

### Using a kanji dictionary (Nelson-style lookup method)

A kanji dictionary (the unit specifically recommends Andrew Nelson's *Japanese–English Character Dictionary*, commonly called "Nelson") groups all kanji first by radical (numbered 1–214, with a radical index inside the front cover), then within a radical group by the count of the character's *remaining* strokes (excluding the radical's own strokes), from fewest to most (up to 24+ for very complex kanji). Each dictionary page header shows the radical and remaining-stroke-count active on that page (e.g. a page marked "木4" holds all kanji with the 木 radical and 4 remaining strokes) to aid navigation. Worked example: to look up 枝 ("branch"), identify its radical (木), count its remaining strokes (4), find 木's radical number (75) in the index, turn to section 75, and scan the 4-remaining-stroke subsection until 枝 is found. An alternative lookup path exists via a kanji's *known* onyomi reading, using an index at the back organized by reading rather than radical. The source also reiterates why correct stroke-order/stroke-counting (taught in Units 1–3) matters practically: dictionary lookup depends on accurate stroke counts (pp. 96–98).

### Scale of kanji literacy in practice

The Japanese Ministry of Education's list of 1942 essential-for-literacy kanji (first cited in the Introduction, p. vii) is far smaller than a comprehensive dictionary's scope — Nelson-style dictionaries list 5000+ entries, most of which are specialist, academic, or pre-war/historical usage rather than everyday vocabulary, paralleling how most English speakers use only a fraction of an unabridged English dictionary's total entries (p.97).

### Unit 7 explicitly frames itself as teaching a method, not a vocabulary list

The unit's own conclusion states that of the 70+ kanji and 38 of the 214 radicals touched on across the unit's activities, the learner is *not* expected to retain all of them — the goal is internalizing the decoding method (radical for meaning, non-radical side for reading) as a tool for approaching unfamiliar kanji going forward, and the book's own back-of-book unit chart deliberately only includes the kanji from Activities 1 and 2, not the full unit (p.102).

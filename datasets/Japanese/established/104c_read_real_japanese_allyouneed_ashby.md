# Japanese — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Read Real Japanese (Ashby, 1991/2003) — All 8 Essays, Representative Sample

**Source:** Janet Ashby (ed./annot.), *Read Real Japanese: All You Need to Enjoy Eight Contemporary
Writers* (Kodansha International, 2003 trade-paperback reissue; originally published 1991 in the
Power Japanese series as *Read Real Japanese*). Eight short essays by contemporary writers, each
with inline 語 (vocabulary), 動 (verb-form derivation), and 注 (usage-note) annotation boxes.
Representative sampling across all 8 essays/authors (printed pp. 3–144+), per the project's
"comprehensive-but-not-exhaustive" coverage rule for a large (156-bundled-page) image-only scan.

**Scope note — distinct from two other same-title-family books in this folder.** Confirmed by prior
duplicate check (not redone here): this 1991/2003 Ashby anthology is genuinely distinct from both
"Read Real Japanese: Fiction" (ed. Michael Emmerich, 2008, different editor/story-list) and
"Reading Japanese with a Smile" (Tom Gally). Full extraction of this book proceeds independently.

**Coverage note:** this is a pure-image DJVU with no text layer (confirmed) — 100% vision-read via
rendered page images at 400dpi. For each of the 8 stories, 4–6 representative pages/spreads were
rendered and read: the bio/opening page, the opening annotated text+vocabulary page, and 2–4
further pages spread across the story (not exhaustive full-story transcription, per the coverage
strategy for this dispatch). No handwritten marginalia was found on any rendered page — only
printed institutional library-ownership stamps (e.g. "St. Petersburg College Library") appeared in
some margins; these are not content and are excluded as noted in the dispatch (not flagged per-page
since they are not genuine marginalia requiring the handwriting guard). All vocabulary/notes content
below is paraphrased from the book's own 語/動/注 annotation boxes — never bulk-copied.

**Page-numbering gotcha (confirmed empirically, per the extraction spec's "verify the offset
empirically" rule):** the arithmetic offset between DJVU bundled-page index and the book's own
printed page numbers is **not constant** — it drifts from printed = DJVU−5 near the start of the
book (Mori Yōko, pp. 3–16) to printed = DJVU+3 by the final essay (Murakami Haruki, pp. 133–144+).
Each of the 8 story's own anchor pages were rendered and their printed footer numbers read directly
rather than trusting a single fixed-offset formula across the whole book — confirming the drift empirically
rather than assuming it. This appears to result from author bio pages and other unnumbered
front-matter-style pages accumulating unevenly between stories, not from any page-reordering or
spread-scanning issue.

## Grammar points

### *-nakereba/-eba* + komaru (consequence framing)
困る *komaru* ("be troubled, at a loss") combines with conditional forms to express implicit
recommendation: *-nakereba + komaru* ("there will be trouble unless you do X" → you should do X)
vs. *-eba/-reba + komaru* ("there will be trouble if you do X" → you shouldn't do X). Mori Yōko, p.5.

### Register control via noun choice (otoko vs. dansei/hito)
A writer can signal social distance or contempt purely by choosing a lower-register noun for "man"
(男 *otoko*) instead of more neutral/polite alternatives (男性 *dansei*, 人 *hito*), independent of
any overtly rude vocabulary. Sakura Momoko deliberately keeps every reference to an unpleasant
colleague at this lowest register throughout her essay. Sakura Momoko, p.63.

### Semantic shift / intensifier drift in colloquial adjectives
Two words in the Yoshimoto Banana essay illustrate a clean semantic-shift pattern directly relevant
to slang formation: 物凄く *monosugoku* (lit. "terrifyingly" → colloquial "tremendously," esp. among
young speakers) and めちゃくちゃ *muchakucha* (lit. "disorderly/confused" → newer slang sense
"madly, to excess," e.g. *muchakucha suki da* "madly in love with"). Both show an original
negative/disorder-based meaning drifting toward a general-purpose intensifier — a common slang
derivation path. Yoshimoto Banana, p.117.

### Colloquial ellipsis of object marker and question particle
In casual speech, both the direct-object particle を *o* and the question particle か *ka* can be
dropped; a rising sentence-final intonation alone signals a question (e.g. *"bōnasu moratta?"* for
*"bōnasu o moratta ka?"*). Yoshimoto Banana, p.125.

### Orthographic device for wavering intonation
The author of the Yoshimoto Banana essay uses a squiggly tilde ("~") after よ *yo* as a personal
orthographic convention to represent fading/wavering vocal intonation, distinct from a plain よ
(short, sharp) or elongated よー (long, steady) — an example of how Japanese prose can mark
paralinguistic/prosodic features typographically. Yoshimoto Banana, p.125.

### Kanji/hiragana variation as a stylistic (not grammatical) choice
The same word (*togireru*, "to be interrupted") appears written in kanji in one sentence and
hiragana in an adjacent one within the Sakamoto Ryūichi essay; the annotation notes this reflects a
subjective stylistic/visual-balance choice (or practical line-length adjustment) rather than any
grammatical distinction — relevant background for treating orthographic variation cautiously when
extracting "distinct" vocabulary items from source texts. Sakamoto Ryūichi, p.23.

### Reduplication for emotional emphasis
Simple reduplication of an adjective/verb (困った困った *komatta komatta*, "oh no, oh no") intensifies
emotional affect without any additional grammatical marking — a lightweight, highly productive
pattern in colloquial Japanese. Nejime Shōichi, p.50.

### Personification idioms built on ordinary verbs
Multiple essays use ordinary verbs metaphorically to personify abstract states: 睡魔と格闘する *suima
to kakutō suru* ("to wrestle with the sleep-demon," i.e. fight off sleepiness, Sakura Momoko, p.80)
and のんびり昼寝している *nonbiri hirune shite iru* ("peacefully napping," describing unused prize
money sitting idle in a bank account, which also gives the Nejime Shōichi essay its own thematic
hook, p.56). Both show how mundane verbs of rest/combat get recruited for figurative color without
any new vocabulary being coined.

### Concessive donna ~ (-yō/-ō) tomo / kamawanai
どんな ~ -ようとも / どんな ~ も構わない: "no matter how/what ~, it doesn't matter" — a productive
concessive-plus-indifference construction pairing an interrogative (donna, dono yō ni) with a
volitional/conditional verb form and the negative of 構う *kamau* "to care/mind." Itō Seikō, p.105.

### -darake ("covered with/full of X") as a mildly pejorative suffix
~だらけ *-darake* attaches to a noun to mean "covered with/full of" something generally undesirable
(e.g. 泥だらけ "covered with mud," ヒョーゴだらけ "jam-packed with slogans" in the Murakami essay) —
a productive evaluative suffix worth tracking for later slang-mechanics comparison against other
"full of X" constructions. Murakami Haruki, p.141.

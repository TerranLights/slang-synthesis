## Grammar points

### A wa B desu — the particle wa (Unit 1)

The core identifying-sentence pattern (A is B); **wa** marks the identified topic, **desu**
completes the sentence. Negated with **de wa arimasen** (also ja arimasen/de wa nai desu/ja nai
desu, all in common use, p. 4-5). When [A] wa is already understood from context it is frequently
dropped to avoid repetition — the book gives a short worked example where **watashi wa** is
omitted in a follow-up sentence about the same subject (p. 5).

### The question particle ka? and sentence-final yo/ne (Unit 1)

Attaching **ka?** to a statement forms a yes/no question, with no word-order change (unlike
English). **Yo** alerts the listener to new information (roughly an exclamation mark); **ne**
invites agreement/confirmation. Both can carry rising or falling intonation, affecting emphasis
strength (p. 5).

### A wa B ni arimasu/imasu vs. B ni A ga arimasu/imasu — the particle ni (Unit 3)

Location can be expressed either with the known entity first (**A wa B ni arimasu**, "A is located
at B") or with the location first (**B ni A ga arimasu**, "at B there is A"). The book frames the
wa/ga choice as a general discourse principle, not specific to this construction: **wa** attaches
to an A already known/established in the conversation, **ga** to an A the speaker is noticing or
introducing for the first time (p. 27) — illustrated with minimal pairs like "Tabakoya wa asoko ni
arimasu" vs. "Asoko ni tabakoya ga arimasu."

### arimasu vs. imasu (Unit 3)

**Arimasu** is used for inanimate subjects (things, plants); **imasu** for animate subjects
(people, animals, insects) in existential/locational sentences — a distinction with no parallel in
the Unit 1 desu-copula pattern.

### Adverbial forms of adjectives (Unit 8)

**Na**-adjectives form adverbs by changing na → ni; **-i** adjectives by changing -i → -ku (the
same change already seen forming negatives). Irregular: ii → **yoku**. Some adverbial -i-adjective
forms (chikaku "nearby," ōku "many/most") can function as nouns and take particles like ordinary
A-no-B pattern slots (p. 83).

### Nouns/adjectives with naru/suru (Unit 8)

To link a noun or adjective to naru ("to become") or suru ("to do/make"), na-adjectives use na→ni
and -i adjectives use -i→-ku before the verb, the same adverbial transformation as above, applied
compositionally to the become/make distinction (p. 83-84, continues past the sampled page range).

### Intransitive and transitive verb pairs (Unit 12)

Japanese verbs frequently come in intransitive/transitive pairs with regular ending
correspondences (e.g. ware.ru/war.u "break," ochi.ru/oto.su "fall/drop"). The grammatical choice
carries a pragmatic function directly paralleled in English (*the cup broke* vs. *John broke the
cup*): the intransitive frames an event as a natural occurrence with no stated agent, which the
unit's dialogue exploits for a comic "evading responsibility for a blunder" scene — a child uses
the intransitive ("wareta," it broke) to dodge blame, and is immediately caught out and corrected
to the transitive ("watta," you broke it) (pp. 134-135).

### Working in Japanese companies — Language and society (Unit 11)

Paraphrased cultural/vocabulary note: traditional Japanese firms are characterized by lifetime
employment (shūshin koyō) and seniority-based promotion (nenkō joretsu), though the book flags
these norms as eroding, evidenced by the newly-coined loanword risutora ("downsizing") entering
the vocabulary. Overtime (zangyō) is normalized culturally; compensation includes a commuting
allowance (tsūkin teate) and a twice-yearly bonus (bōnasu) that can reach several months' salary
in good years (p. 132).

# Japanese — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: A Handbook of Japanese Grammar (full-book survey)

**Source:** Masahiro Tanimori, *Handbook of Japanese Grammar* (Tuttle Language Library / Tuttle
Publishing, first edition 1994, sixth printing 2003), ISBN 0-8048-1940-8. Full 277-page scan, no
text layer (`pdftotext` confirms "Adobe Acrobat 6.0 Image Conversion Plug-in" — pure raster scan).
Vision-read throughout.

**Coverage note.** This book has two parts: **Part One, "Dictionary of Grammatical Function Words"**
(printed pp. 11–245, over 600 alphabetized entries — particles, auxiliary verbs, conjunctive
phrases, sentence-final expressions, etc., each with definition + numbered usage patterns +
example sentences) and **Part Two, "Grammatical Explanations"** (printed pp. 247–~312, a
by-part-of-speech systematic grammar: Adjectival Nouns, Adjectives, Adverbs, Conjunctions,
Counters, Euphonic Changes, Intransitive/Transitive Verbs, Nouns, Polite Language, Prepositions,
Pronouns, Verbs), followed by back-matter ads (pp. ~313–320, not extracted).

Per the extraction spec's "flat multi-hundred-entry dictionary → representative sampling" rule,
**Part One was sampled, not exhaustively extracted**: pages were drawn at roughly even intervals
across the full pdf range (pp. 11–245 printed) to get spread across the whole alphabet (confirmed

**Morpheme breakdown (agglutinative suffix chain — masu-form paradigm).** Japanese verb
morphology is agglutinative at the predicate-suffix level (the typology this project tracks per
`00_Word_Concept_and_Morphological_Typology_Guide.md`), so the polite-negative-past form is worth
breaking down rather than treated as atomic:

> **mimasendeshita** (did not see, polite) = *mi-* (stem of *miru* "to see") + *-mase-* (polite,
> from *-masu*) + *-n-* (negative) + *-deshita* (past copula, polite) — demonstrates how tense,
> politeness, and negation stack as separable suffix layers on a single verb stem; Part Two, Verbs,
> p.301.

## Grammar points

### Adjectival nouns (na-adjectives)
Paraphrase (p.248–252): a closed(ish) but productive class of descriptive words that take *-na*
before a noun and *da/desu* as a predicate (unlike i-adjectives, which conjugate on their own).
The source notes borrowed/foreign vocabulary is routinely absorbed into this class rather than the
native i-adjective class — e.g. *derikēto-na* "delicate," *hansamu-na* "handsome" — a productive,
ongoing loanword-integration pattern.

### Adjectives (i-adjectives) — conjugation forms
Paraphrase (p.252–262): i-adjectives conjugate directly (no copula needed for plain non-past),
and the source organizes the whole paradigm around derived "forms": the dictionary form (plain
predicate/attributive), **ku-form** (replace final *-i* with *-ku*; used for adverbial, te-form,
and negative-formation purposes), **nai-form** (negation, itself conjugating like the irregular
adjective *nai*), **ba-form** (replace final *-i* with *-kereba*, present/past conditional), and
**tara-form** (add *-tara* to the stem of the ta-form, present conditional). The negative of the
ba-form and tara-form is built by inserting *-nakereba*/*-nakattara* onto the ku-form rather than
conjugating the affirmative form directly — a regular, rule-governed detour rather than a true
irregularity.

### Conjunctions — noun-connecting vs. sentence-connecting
Paraphrase (p.265): the source draws an explicit structural split between conjunctions that join
nouns (*to* "and," exhaustive; *ya* "and," non-exhaustive/illustrative; *mo* "and also," attached
after each noun; *ka* "or") and those that join full sentences/clauses (*daga*, *dakara*, *dewa*,
*keredo(mo)*, *shikashi*, *sorekara*, *soreni*, among others) — and for the sentence-connecting set,
annotates several with explicit register labels (formal/informal/written), captured above.

### Counters
Paraphrase (p.268–272): counters are suffixes attached to numbers that vary by the semantic class
of the counted object (people, flat objects, long thin objects, days, months, round objects, units
of weight/distance, etc.) — a classifier system, not optional stylistic embellishment, as a bare
number cannot normally quantify a noun in Japanese without one. The day-of-month counter (*ka* 日)
and the day-duration counter (*nichi/ka* 日) share an irregular base series for 1–10 (*tsuitachi/
ichinichi, futsuka, mikka...*) that diverges from the regular Sino-Japanese number sequence used
once the counter's own irregular zone ends.

### Euphonic changes
Paraphrase (p.272–274): verbs whose dictionary form ends in *-bu*, *-mu*, or *-nu* undergo a
regular sound change in the te-form and ta-form: instead of the expected *-te/-ta*, the final
consonant is replaced with *-n-* and the following *-te/-ta* voices to *-de/-da* (e.g. *asobu* →
*asonde/asonda*, not the unvoiced *asotte/asotta* a surface analogy with other verb classes might
predict). This is presented as a phonologically regular class-conditioned rule, not an
exception list.

### Intransitive and transitive verb pairs
Paraphrase (p.274–276): Japanese systematically pairs many verbs describing the same event from
an intransitive ("X happened") vs. transitive ("Y did X") perspective, usually differentiated by
a small, recurring set of verb-final alternations (*-aru*/*-eru*, *-eru*/*-asu*, *-ru*/*-su*,
etc.). The source stresses that even the transitive member of such a pair can still have its
object dropped in context (demonstrated with a dialogue: *"Akari o keshimasu ka?" "Kesanaide
kudasai"* — "Shall I turn off the light?" "Please don't turn it off" — object *akari* dropped in
the reply), so transitivity here is a lexical/argument-structure property, not a guarantee the
object surfaces overtly. The printed list runs several dozen pairs alphabetically; only a
representative sample is tabulated above.

### Nouns
Paraphrase (p.276): Japanese nouns carry no inherent gender or grammatical case; case relations
are expressed entirely through postpositional particles, and there is no obligatory singular/
plural distinction marked on the noun itself.

### Polite language
Paraphrase (p.278–281): politeness is distributed across several independent mechanisms that can
combine: honorific noun prefixes (*o-* for native-origin nouns, *go-* for Sino-Japanese-origin
nouns), addressee-politeness suffixes on names/relational nouns (*-san* moderate, *-sama* higher),
and polite auxiliary verbs/verb-forms (*desu* as the polite copula, *-masu* as the polite verb
suffix) — plus a handful of frozen set phrases (*o-tsukare-san*/*go-kurō-san*) that function as
fixed social routines rather than compositional phrases.

### Prepositions (postpositional equivalents, organized by English meaning)
Paraphrase (p.282–292): Part Two's "Prepositions" section is explicitly a *reorganization* of
material already covered in Part One's particle/function-word dictionary entries, regrouped by
English preposition-equivalent meaning (FOR, FROM, OVER, SINCE, THROUGH, AT, ALONG, AMONG, AFTER,
etc.) rather than by Japanese form — useful as a second-pass cross-reference layer rather than new
lexical content; most individual particles here (*kara*, *ni*, *made*, *de*) are also already
dictionary entries in Part One. One clarification that doesn't carry over cleanly into English
prepositional thinking: several of these English "preposition" categories are expressed in
Japanese by a particle attached to a preceding noun/noun-phrase rather than a true preposition
preceding its object, and a few (e.g. "over," "through") have multiple non-interchangeable
Japanese equivalents sorted by finer semantic distinctions (physical position above vs. "more
than" vs. "across to the far side of").

### Pronouns
Paraphrase (p.292–293): Japanese personal pronouns are far more sparingly used than in English
(subject pronouns are routinely dropped once established in context) and are heavily
register/gender-marked rather than neutral — the source explicitly annotates *boku*/*ore* as male
speech and *kimi* as informal, a genuine sociolinguistic register distinction (not an inferred
one) worth flagging for the mechanics-analysis phase. Demonstrative pronouns (*kore/sore/are*,
"this/that/that over there") follow the Japanese three-way ko-so-a distal system (near speaker /
near listener / far from both) rather than English's two-way this/that, and each series has a
dedicated place-noun subset (*koko/soko/asoko* "here/there/over there") and direction/politeness
subset (*kochira/sochira/achira*, the polite equivalents of *kore/sore/are* and also directional
"this way/that way").

### Verbs — conjugational forms
Paraphrase (p.293–~309): Part Two's Verbs section works through the same form-based
conjugational-paradigm approach used for adjectives, but for the (larger) verb system: masu-form
(polite non-past, built from the conjunctive stem, cannot appear in relative clauses — contrasted
with the plain/dictionary form, which can), ta-form (plain past/relative-clause-modifying form,
plus a derived "ta-form as attributive adjective" use for a small set of verbs like *hanareru* →
*hanareta* "distant"), ba-form and tara-form conditionals (parallel in formation logic to the
adjective ba-/tara-forms above, but built from the verb's own stem-final vowel change rather than
an adjective's *-i* ending), honorific form (formally identical to the passive form — the source
stresses that only context disambiguates an honorific *ikaremasu* "will you go (respectfully)"
from a passive reading of the same surface form), and command form (abrupt/emphatic; built by
regular stem-final vowel substitution for consonant-stem and iru/eru-stem verbs, with two
irregular forms: *suru* → *shiro*, *kuru* → *koi* — the source flags this register as unsuitable
for ordinary conversation, used only by a superior giving an order or spectators cheering a team,
i.e. an explicit discourse-context restriction rather than a purely grammatical one).

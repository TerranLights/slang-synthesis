This chapter is organized around four Sino-Japanese law-related root morphemes (法 *hō*, 律 *ritsu*,
令 *rei*, 則 *soku*), followed by two un-rooted topical sections, "Law and Order" (courtroom/judicial
vocabulary) and "Crime and Punishment" (criminal-offense and police-rank vocabulary), closing with a
short section on constitutional rights of the accused. Coverage below captures every distinct
compound/term introduced in the prose discussion and in the glosses accompanying the chapter's 47
numbered example sentences, plus every native-Japanese comparison term the author introduces
alongside the Sino-Japanese compounds — not a verbatim reproduction of the numbered example
sentences themselves (only short illustrative phrases are quoted where genuinely useful). As
flagged in the dispatch, this chapter is dense in formal/legal register and several explicit
register contrasts (colloquial vs. formal, a "fashionable" loanword displacing a native term, a
fictional-media term contrasted with the real official rank system) — all are called out below.

**Morpheme breakdown.** Several of this chapter's longer legal-act and compound-crime terms are
genuinely transparent once broken into their Sino-Japanese parts, and the breakdown itself teaches
how Japanese legalese stacks nominal compounds rather than using subordinate clauses:

> **業務上過失致死罪** (gyōmu-jō-kashitsu-chishizai) = 業務上 (gyōmu-jō, "in the course of
> one's duties/business," 業務 "duties" + 上 "upon/in the course of") + 過失 (kashitsu,
> "negligence, fault") + 致死 (chi-shi, "causing death," 致 chi "bring about" + 死 shi "death") +
> 罪 (zai, "offense") — "dereliction of duty resulting in death," the charge against a train
> operator in ex. 35 (p.100).

> **犯罪者予防更生法** (hanzaisha-yobō-kōsei-hō) = 犯罪者 (hanzai-sha, "criminal," itself 犯罪
> hanzai "crime" + 者 sha "person") + 予防 (yobō, "prevention") + 更生 (kōsei, "rehabilitation,
> reform") + 法 (hō, "act, law") — the title of Japan's Corrective and Reformative Treatment of
> Criminals Act (p.91).

> **出入国管理及び難民認定法** (shutsunyū-koku-kanri-oyobi-nanmin-nintei-hō) = 出入国
> (shutsunyū-koku, "entering and exiting the country," 出 shutsu "exit" + 入 nyū "enter" + 国 koku
> "country") + 管理 (kanri, "management, control") + 及び (oyobi, "and") + 難民 (nanmin,
> "refugee") + 認定 (nintei, "recognition, certification") + 法 (hō, "law") — Japan's Immigration
> Control and Refugee Recognition Law (p.92), a title that reads almost like a run-on English legal
> title transliterated morpheme-for-morpheme.

## Grammar points

### Four organizing roots with distinct etymological semantic ranges

The chapter is built around four Sino-Japanese "law" roots, each introduced with its Ancient
Chinese reconstruction and a distinct extended sense beyond the core "law" meaning: 法 *hō* (<
*\*piap* "constraint, confinement") extends into Buddhist "method/doctrine" (法事 "memorial
service," 避妊法 "birth control method"); 律 *ritsu* (< *\*liuet*) extends, like Greek *nomos*,
into musical/poetic form (旋律 "melody," 韻律 "meter"); 令 *rei* (< *\*lieng* "purity") doubles as
an honorific prefix for "your ___" (令嬢, 令息) distinct from its "order/command" sense (命令,
政令); 則 *soku* (< *\*tsak* "conformity, pertinence") occurs almost exclusively in non-initial
compound position, with 則天去私 flagged as the one prominent exception (pp. 89–94).

### Homophone/false-friend traps the author explicitly flags

This chapter is unusually dense in words the author calls out as traps for a learner relying on
surface parsing: 律法 *rippō* "religious law" (Torah/Shariah/Buddhist precepts) vs. 立法 *rippō*
"lawmaking" (same reading, unrelated kanji/meaning, p.93); 令状 *reijō* "warrant" vs. 令嬢 *reijō*
"young lady" (p.94); 軽罪 *keizai* "minor offense" vs. 経済 *keizai* "economics" (p.99); 調律師
*chōritsu-shi* "piano tuner," which a learner might misparse via 律 "law" as "investigative
legalist" (p.93); 知能犯 *chinō-han*, whose literal/dictionary gloss "intellectual [mental]
offense" obscures its real sense of "white-collar crime" (p.102); and 法事 *hōji* "Buddhist
memorial service," which despite containing 法 does not mean "legal affairs" (p.91). The author
frames these not as isolated trivia but as a recurring hazard of relying on compositional parsing
for kango compounds — the same point the Chapter 1 file's 論理的/理論的 minimal pair already
illustrated for this book.

### Native-Japanese law vocabulary alongside the Sino-Japanese system

Parallel to Chapter 1's native-Japanese 筋 *suji* standing alongside the *ron/ri/setsu* family, this
chapter introduces a small native-Japanese "law" vocabulary: 掟 *okite* (nominalized from 起きつ
*okitsu* "set forth, build up," with an "archaic ring," strongly tied in the examples to religious
exhortation and yakuza codes), 宣る *noru* "to utter/declare (of a god or emperor)," and 則る
*nottoru* "to follow, conform to (a rule)." The native kun-reading 法 *nori* ("law, the Way") is
also given, including its use in personal names and the alternative characters (則, 憲, 徳) that can
substitute for it in that onomastic context (pp. 94–96).

### -殺し (-goroshi) as a restricted "-cide" suffix

The chapter documents -殺し *-goroshi* as a productive suffix corresponding to English "-cide"
compounds (父殺し "patricide," 兄弟殺し "fratricide," 嬰児殺し "infanticide"), but explicitly notes
the suffix's restriction: it is used only with reference to the termination of human life, unlike
the broader Sino-Japanese 殺 *satsu* root seen in 殺虫剤 "insecticide" and 殺菌剤 "germicide"
(pp. 100–101).

### Register contrasts flagged by the author (high-value for this project)

Several explicit register observations appear in this chapter:
- レイプ *reipu*, the English loanword for "rape," is flagged as having "recently become
  fashionable, particularly in the media" — a live register/lexical-competition note against the
  native 強姦 *gōkan* and the older euphemism 暴行 *bōkō* (lit. "violent action") (p.100).
- おえらいさん *oerai-san* "bigwig" is flagged as colloquial, derived from the adjective 偉い
  *erai* "admirable, great, highly placed" (p.100).
- おまわりさん *omawari-san* ("Mr. Officer") is explicitly contrasted as a familiar/child-register
  term for a policeman against the real multi-tier formal rank hierarchy (巡査 → 警視総監); the
  chapter's example sentence deliberately uses a schoolboy's naive use of the term to set up the
  joke that his own father actually holds the senior rank 警視長 (p.104).
- 刑事さん *keiji-san* ("Mr. Detective") is flagged as belonging to the fictionalized world of
  Japanese police dramas rather than the real police system, which the author says is organizationally
  closer to the American model than popular fiction suggests (p.104).
- 監獄 *kangoku* "prison" is marked as an older/literary term (used in a quoted Dickens-flavored
  rendering), distinct from the modern official term 刑務所 *keimu-sho* "penitentiary" actually used
  in current Japanese administration (p.103).

### Legal-compound stacking and coordinating particles in statute titles

Several Act titles in this chapter (出入国管理及び難民認定法, 外国為替及び外国貿易管理法) show
Japanese statutory titles using the formal coordinating particle 及び (*oyobi*, "and") to chain
multiple nominal compounds before the final 法 "law/act," rather than the colloquial conjunction と
— a register marker specific to legal/official document titles (pp. 91–92).

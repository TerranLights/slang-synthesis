## Grammar points

### §8.1 What are adjectives? (a fundamentally non-English typology)

The source opens by explicitly rejecting the English definition of "adjective" as adequate for Japanese: Japanese splits what English calls adjectives into two structurally distinct word classes — **verbal adjectives** (verb-like) and **adjectival nouns** (noun-like) — both of which can modify a following noun the way an English adjective does, but which otherwise behave according to entirely different grammatical rules (p. 162).

### §8.2 Verbal adjectives (-i adjectives)

Dictionary-form verbal adjectives end only in `-ai`, `-ii`, `-oi`, or `-ui` (hence "-i adjectives") (p. 162–163). Structurally these behave like verbs: they have a **stem** (drop final `-i`) from which all other forms are built, and — crucially — the dictionary form itself is already the plain-present predicate, meaning a bare verbal adjective can constitute a complete sentence with no copula needed (the source frames this as "already containing in itself the grammatical form of *is*") (p. 164). Full conjugation (p. 165–167): **plain past** = stem + `-katta`; **plain negative** = stem + `-ku` + the appropriate form of `nai` (negative of `nai` itself follows regular adjective conjugation, giving past-negative `-ku nakatta`); **polite present/past** = plain form + `desu`/`katta desu`; **polite negative** = `-ku arimasen`/`-ku arimasen deshita` (an alternative polite-negative variant simply adds `desu` onto the plain negative forms instead); **gerund** = stem + `-kute` (used for the same sequential/parallel clause-chaining functions as the verb gerund, §7.7-3/§7.17); **presumptive/conditional/etc.** extend by the same `darō`/`-kereba`/`-temo` machinery already covered in §7 for verbs. The **adverbial form** is built by changing final `-i` to `-ku` (stem + `-ku`), with one flagged irregular: `ii` "good" has suppletive adverb `yoku` "well," not a regular `*ikū`/`*iiku` (p. 168) — this adverbial form is frequently paired with `naru` "to become" (to describe a change of state, e.g. `omoshiroku narimashita` "became interesting") and with `suru` "to do" (to describe causing a change, e.g. `kuroku nurimashita` "painted [it] black") (p. 168–169).

### §8.3 Adjectival nouns (-na adjectives)

A structurally distinct, noun-like adjective class. As **noun modifiers**, they take the suffix `-na` before the noun they modify (`shizukana heya` "quiet room") — explicitly parallel in form to the noun+`no`+noun compounding pattern from §4.8, with `-na` simply substituting for `no` (p. 170–171). As **predicates**, they behave exactly like nouns: they combine with the appropriate form of the copula `desu` (full present/past/presumptive × affirmative/negative paradigm identical in form to §7.6's copula table) rather than conjugating on their own the way verbal adjectives do (p. 172–174) — this is the central grammatical payoff of calling them "noun-like." The **adverbial form** is built with the particle `ni` following the bare adjectival-noun form (not a `-ku`-style suffix, underscoring the class's noun-like rather than verb-like behavior), e.g. `shizuka ni` "quietly" (p. 174–175).

### §8.4 Comparisons (adjective-invariant, particle-marked)

Neither adjective class changes form for comparison — Japanese instead uses a fixed sentential pattern applicable to both -i and -na adjectives alike: **`A wa B yori [adjective] desu`** ("A is more [adjective] than B") (p. 176). An alternative, more emphatic comparative construction substitutes **`B no hō ga [adjective] desu`** for the `yori`-marked phrase — `hō` meaning "side/direction," with `no` linking it to B the same way it links any compound noun — and the source notes this version carries *stronger contrast* than the plain `yori` version, though both are truth-conditionally equivalent (p. 177–178). A/B-choice questions use **`A to B to, dochira ga [adjective] desu ka`** ("Which do you like better, A or B?") (p. 178–180). The adverb **`motto`** ("more, much more") intensifies a comparison, and **`ichiban`** ("most, -est") marks the superlative (p. 179–180).

### §8.5 Nominalization (adjectives → nouns)

Both adjective classes can be turned into a noun meaning "the [adjective] one(s)": **-i adjectives** add `-no` directly to the dictionary form (`ōkii` → `ōkiino` "big one"); **-na adjectives** add `-no` to their `-na` form (`shizukana` → `shizukanano` "quiet one") (p. 180–182). This nominalized form typically occurs in a context where the modified noun is already understood/established (e.g. answering "which one do you want?" with just "the sweet one," or restating a previously-named topic with a nominalized adjective standing in for the noun) (p. 181–183) — structurally comparable to the English "the [adjective] one" construction, but grammaticalized here as a productive, regular derivation rather than relying on a filler noun like English "one."

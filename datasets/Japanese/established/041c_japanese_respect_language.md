## Grammar points

### The standard depreciatory construction: お o + base + する/いたす suru/itasu (pp. 76–77)

Parallel to Chapter 3's standard honorific construction, most verbs form their depreciatory
(humble) equivalent regularly: お matsu "wait" → お待ちする/いたす o-machi suru/itasu; 願う negau
"request" → お願いする/いたす o-negai suru/itasu. Since いたす itasu is itself a depreciatory form
of する suru, the お+base+いたす variant is more depreciatory (humbler) than the お+base+する
variant — the two are not simply free variants but sit at different politeness depths. As with the
honorific construction, 〜する suru-compound verbs of the 連絡する renraku-suru/相談する sōdan-suru
type (which necessarily involve more than one person) use ご go- + the noun element + する/いたす
instead of the regular お+base+ pattern.

### Two groups of exceptions to the standard depreciatory construction (pp. 78, 80–81)

(i) Verbs with wholly suppletive/special depreciatory words instead of the regular construction —
tabulated in the Vocabulary section above (iku/kuru, iu, suru, morau/nomu/taberu, miru, miseru,
omou/shiru, yaru/ageru). (ii) A separate, smaller group of verbs — da, aru, iru, benkyō-suru-type
verbs, shinu, nakunaru — that have **no depreciatory form usable to show respect to a 3rd person at
all**, retaining the ordinary neutral form instead; the source notes these verbs *do* have
depreciatory equivalents usable toward a 2nd person (covered later in the book), so the gap is
specific to 3rd-person reference, not an absence of depreciatory marking altogether. The reasoning
given: the actions these verbs denote (existing, being a certain way, dying, studying) involve no
one but their own grammatical subject, so there is no "other person" relationship left for a
depreciatory form to encode respect toward.

### When a depreciatory prefix/form is licensed: the "involvement" test (pp. 80–86)

A depreciatory verb (and any honorific prefix attached within it) is only usable when the person to
be shown respect is genuinely "involved" in the verb's action — e.g. as the one from/to/for whom an
action is performed — not merely as an onlooker or unconnected party. The source works through a
graded set of attested examples establishing exactly how much "involvement" licenses the humble
form: (1) a student replying about a book *borrowed from her teacher* may depreciate ("I read it" as
a favor connected to the teacher) because the teacher supplied the book; but if asked about *a book
of her own* that happens to have been seen in her possession, she can only use the plain neutral
form, since the teacher has no connection to that particular reading event. (2) An author who made
it possible for a friend to read a book (by lending/publishing it) licenses the friend's use of a
depreciatory verb in reply; by contrast, a doctor asking a patient whether he drank poison, or
whether he wrote certain unconnected notes, can only receive plain neutral-form answers, because the
doctor isn't "involved" in those specific acts — but the same patient CAN depreciate ("itadakimashita")
when confirming he safely received medicine the doctor personally sent him, since that action does
connect the two people. The general principle: if no other person is involved in the verbal action at
all, an ordinary neutral form is used regardless of who's asking or what their status is (p. 86).

### Depreciatory verbs toward a 3rd person vs. toward the 2nd person — summary (pp. 92–93)

Depreciatory verbs (like honorific verbs, apart from the exceptional "plain honorifics" used
to/of a 2nd person covered in Chapter 4) can be used: (A) in their plain form (without ます masu) to
show respect to a 3rd person and no respect to the 2nd person; or (B) in their polite -masu form to
show respect either (i) to the 3rd person via the depreciatory verb choice *and* to the 2nd person
via the masu ending simultaneously, or (ii) to the 2nd person only, using both the depreciatory verb
*and* the masu ending together for the same referent. This means a single polite-register
depreciatory sentence is genuinely ambiguous out of context between "showing respect to both a 3rd
party and you" and "showing respect to you alone" — resolved only by which person is actually
"involved" in the context (worked through in the itadakimashita/itadaita/o-me-ni-kakarimashita
practice set, pp. 94).

### masu is independently required for 2nd-person depreciatory respect (p. 91)

Confirms and extends the Chapter 4 finding specifically for depreciatory verbs: ます masu is always
needed with a depreciatory verb when it is the 2nd person (not a 3rd person) who is being shown
respect by that verb. Respect to a 3rd person via a depreciatory verb can be freely combined with a
plain (non-masu) ending when the addressee does not independently merit respect — demonstrated with
a worked contrast: 出来るだけ早く大使にご連絡するつもりです (masu-level, respect to addressee) vs.
…ご連絡するつもりだ (plain, no masu, addressee is a familiar/junior) — both equally valid depending
on who is being addressed, with the depreciatory element toward the 3rd-person ambassador unchanged
either way.

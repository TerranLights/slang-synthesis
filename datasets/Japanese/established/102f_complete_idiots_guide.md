## Grammar points

### Obligatory body-region/domain splits in otherwise-single English verbs/adjectives

This Part documents two clean, source-confirmed cases where a single English word's semantic
field is obligatorily split across two distinct Japanese lexemes by domain: (1) "wear/put on"
splits into ki-masu (above the waist) vs. haki-masu (below the waist), and (2) "cold" splits into
samu-i (ambient air/weather) vs. tsumeta-i (substances; also metaphorically, personality). Both
splits are explicitly called out by the book itself (not inferred), and both are worth keeping on
record together as the same general typological phenomenon — a target-language distinction with
no equivalent split in the source (English) gloss.

### TE-form polysemy: activity-sequencing vs. non-activity/stative connection

Confirmed directly by the book (p.270-271, cross-referencing its own Ch.10 material): the TE-form
used to connect clauses carries chronological-sequence meaning when the connected predicates are
"activity" verbs (e.g. wake up, eat, leave — order matters and is implied), but carries no
such temporal ordering when connecting "nonactivity"/stative predicates (e.g. "I am married, and
my wife's name is Lisa" — no sequencing implied). This is a single morphosyntactic form with two
functionally distinct discourse-level readings gated by predicate aktionsart — useful structural
detail for later mechanics analysis of how Japanese encodes event sequencing without dedicated
sequencing conjunctions.

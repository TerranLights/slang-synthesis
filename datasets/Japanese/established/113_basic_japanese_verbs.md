## Grammar points

### The three conjugation groups (Group 1/2/3)

Japanese verbs fall into three conjugation classes, determined by the plain (dictionary) form's
ending:

- **Group 1 ("u-dropping")**: covers most verbs whose dictionary form doesn't end in *-eru* or
  *-iru* (e.g. *kaku* "to write," *yomu* "to read"). The stem is formed by dropping the final *-u*;
  *masu*-forms attach *-imasu/-imasen* to that stem. A minority of *-eru*/*-iru* verbs are
  nonetheless Group 1 and must be learned individually — the source flags minimal pairs where
  pronunciation alone doesn't disambiguate group membership, e.g. *kiru* (accent on *ki*, "to cut,"
  Group 1, stem *kir-*) vs. *kiru* (accent on *ru*, "to wear," Group 2, stem *ki-*); *kaeru* ("to
  return," Group 1) vs. *kaeru* ("to change," Group 2); and *iru* ("to need," Group 1) vs. *iru*
  ("to exist," Group 2), which are not even distinguished by accent.
- **Group 2 ("ru-dropping")**: most verbs ending in *-eru*/*-iru*. The stem drops the final *-ru*;
  *masu*-forms attach *-masu/-masen* directly to that stem.
- **Group 3 (irregular)**: exactly two verbs, *kuru* 来る "to come" and *suru* する "to do" (*kuru*'s
  entry is in 113b, *suru*'s in 113c). Noun + *suru* compound verbs (e.g. *benkyō suru* "to study")
  conjugate by conjugating *suru* alone and are also treated as Group 3 (see 113c).

One further Group 1 wrinkle: verbs whose final *-u* is preceded by a vowel (e.g. *kau* "to buy,"
*iu* "to say," *omou* "to think") form their negative with *-wanai/-wanakatta* rather than the
expected *-anai/-anakatta* — thus *kau* → *kawanai*, not *kaanai*.

### Verb stem + adjuncts

Many grammatical adjuncts attach directly to a verb's stem (the pre-*masu* form) to build derived
meanings — e.g. stem + *-yasui*/*-nikui* ("easy/difficult to ___"), stem + *-tai*/*-takunai* ("want
to/don't want to ___"), stem + *-tagaru* ("show signs of wanting to ___"), stem + *-nagara* ("while
___-ing"), stem + *-sō* ("looks like it will ___"), stem + *-kata* ("the way of ___-ing"), and stem
+ *-nasai* (a softened imperative, "please do ___"). (p. 15)

### The Plain Form

The plain form (including its present, past, conditional, presumptive, volitional, potential,
passive, causative, and causative-passive sub-forms) is the register used in informal speech among
family/friends; the present-tense plain form is also called the dictionary form. It's used (1) in
informal conversation, (2) inside a subordinate clause of a complex sentence regardless of overall
sentence politeness, and (3) immediately before many grammatical adjuncts (*tsumori* "intend to,"
*hazu* "supposed to," *sō* "I heard that," *hō ga ii* "should," *koto ga aru* "have done X before,"
*-tari...-tari* "do X and Y," *-nai de* "without doing X"). (pp. 15-17)

### The *Masu* Form

The *masu* form is the standard polite/conventional register, suitable for a wide range of
circumstances. Its present tense covers both English present and future. It's formed by attaching
*-imasu/-imasen* to a Group 1 stem or *-masu/-masen* to a Group 2 stem; for potential, passive,
causative, and causative-passive forms, the *masu* form is made by dropping the derived form's
final *-ru* and attaching *-masu/-masen*. (pp. 17-18)

### The *Te* Form

The *te* form is the rough equivalent of an English gerund and has several distinct conjugation
rules depending on the Group 1 verb's final syllable:

- *-ku* → *-ite* (e.g. *aku* → *aite*)
- *-gu* → *-ide* (e.g. *sawagu* → *sawaide*)
- *-su* → *-shite* (e.g. *hanasu* → *hanashite*)
- *-bu*, *-mu*, *-nu* → *-nde* (e.g. *tobu* → *tonde*, *yomu* → *yonde*, *shinu* → *shinde*)
- *-ru*, *-tsu* → *-tte* (e.g. *kaeru* → *kaette*, *katsu* → *katte*)
- *-u* preceded by a vowel → *-tte* (e.g. *kau* → *katte*, *iu* → *itte*)

Group 2 verbs simply change *-ru* to *-te* (e.g. *miru* → *mite*). *Iku* "to go" is a noted
exception: its *te* form is *itte*, not the expected *iite*.

The *te* form has at least six distinct functions in the source's account: (1) combined with
*iru*/*imasu*, marks an ongoing state of doing; (2) combined with *iru*/*imasu*, marks a present
perfect ("has already happened," used more broadly than English present perfect); (3) chains
sequential events in a time-ordered narration; (4) marks that one event caused a following
reaction/emotion; (5) combined with giving/receiving verbs (*ageru*, *kureru*, *morau*, *kudasaru*,
*itadaku*, *sashiageru*), encodes who benefits from an action at a politeness level tied to the
relative status of giver/receiver; (6) combines with a range of other verbs/adjuncts (*mo ii desu
ka* "may I," *wa ikemasen* "must not," *mimasu* "try X-ing," *hoshii* "want someone to," *okimasu*
"do X in advance," *shimaimasu* "finish doing X"). (pp. 18-21)

### The Conditional Form

Two interchangeable (with nuance differences) ways to form the conditional: (1) drop final *-u* and
add *-eba* (negative: drop final *-i* of the plain negative and add *-kereba*); (2) add the adjunct
*ra* to a past plain or past *masu* form (written as two words in this book's convention, though
conventionally spelled as one — *futta ra*/*futtara*). The *-eba* form is more awkward for direct
requests or "when X happens, I will do Y" readings; the source marks *Kare ni atta ra...* as correct
for a request but *Kare ni aeba...* as incorrect in the same context. (pp. 21-22)

### The Volitional Form

Formed by adding *-ō/-imashō* to a Group 1 stem, or *-yō/-mashō* to a Group 2 stem, expressing
intention/suggestion ("I'll ___" / "let's ___"). Some verbs conventionally don't take a volitional
form; the source flags these by parenthesizing the form in the main entries. (p. 22)

### The Imperative Form

A direct command, formed by adding *-e* to a Group 1 stem or *-ro* to a Group 2 stem; the negative
imperative adds the adjunct *na* to the plain present form. A softer command form adds *-i + nasai*
(Group 1 stem) or *-nasai* (Group 2 stem) — *suru*/*kuru* use the irregular *shinasai*/*kinasai*.
Some verbs' imperative forms are likewise conventionally avoided and parenthesized in the main
entries. (pp. 22-23)

### The Potential Form

Expresses possibility/capability. Formed by adding *-eru/-emasu* (negative *-enai/-emasen*) to a
Group 1 stem, or *-rareru/-raremasu* (negative *-rarenai/-raremasen*) to a Group 2 stem. A
colloquial shift has some speakers forming Group 2 potentials the Group 1 way; the source lists
this non-standard variant below the standard form in its main entries but flags it as considered
unacceptable by many speakers. (p. 23)

### The Passive Form

Conveys that something happened to the speaker, generally with an adverse-effect nuance. Formed by
adding *-areru/-aremasu* to a Group 1 stem (*-wareru/-waremasu* for vowel-final stems like *omou*),
or *-rareru/-raremasu* to a Group 2 stem — identical in form to the Group 2 potential. Both
transitive and some intransitive verbs can take passive forms (e.g. *ame ni furareru*, "to be
rained on," from the intransitive *furu*). (p. 24)

### The Causative and Causative Passive Forms

**Causative** ("make/let someone do X"): add *-aseru/-asemasu* to most Group 1 stems
(*-waseru/-wasemasu* for vowel-final stems like *utau*), or *-saseru/-sasemasu* to Group 2 stems.
**Causative passive** ("I was made to do X, to my detriment"): add *-aserareru/-aseraremasu* to most
Group 1 stems (*-waserareru/-waseraremasu* for vowel-final stems), or *-saserareru/-saseraremasu* to
Group 2 stems. As with the potential form, a colloquial Group-1-style causative passive exists for
Group 2 verbs and is listed as non-standard below the conventional form. (pp. 24-25)

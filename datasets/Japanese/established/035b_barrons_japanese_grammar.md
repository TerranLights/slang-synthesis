## Grammar points

### §4.1 What are nouns? / proper vs. common nouns

Japanese's concept of "proper noun" is narrower than English's: names of people/places are capitalized in romaji, but days of the week and month names are not (only the names of *other* languages are capitalized — notably `eigo` "English" itself is not) (p. 21).

### §4.2–4.3 Gender and articles

Japanese nouns carry no grammatical gender and there are no articles corresponding to a/an/the (p. 22).

### §4.4 Number

Most Japanese nouns are unmarked for number — the same word serves singular and plural (p. 23). The optional suffix `-tachi` can mark plurality specifically for people-referring nouns, and when attached to a personal name it shifts meaning to "so-and-so and their group/family" rather than "multiple instances of that person" (p. 23).

### §4.5 Names

Japanese order is family-name-first, given-name-last — the reverse of English — and Japanese speakers are generally aware foreigners do it the other way (p. 24).

### §4.6 Titles

`san` is the all-purpose polite title (Mr./Mrs./Miss/Ms. equivalent), always used with proper names — even among close friends/family using first names — and never used to refer to oneself (p. 24–25). Children get `chan` (young) or `san` (older) attached to first names rather than family names (p. 25). `Sensei` ("teacher") is a flexible honorific address usable for any respected field-expert — doctors, legislators, artists, writers, architects, musicians — not just classroom teachers (p. 25).

### §4.7 Politeness prefixes (o-/go-)

The prefix `o-` (and its variant `go-`, used with Sino-Japanese-origin vocabulary) marks politeness and attaches productively to many nouns (also verbs/adjectives, deferred to §7/§8). The source flags that some `o-`-prefixed forms are specifically associated with women's speech (e.g. `oniku`, `oyasai`), while others have become fully lexicalized — the prefix is now an inseparable part of the word (`ocha`, `gohan`) — and warns that these prefixes cannot be freely applied to all nouns; use only forms you're certain are correct (p. 25–26).

### §4.8 Compound nouns

Two nouns combine into a compound noun usually joined by the particle `no` ("history class" = `rekishi no kurasu`, literally "history's class"), though some compounds drop `no` entirely (`kōhī jawan` "coffee cup," `kankō basu` "tour bus," `bōeki gaisha` "trading company") (p. 26–27).

### §4.9 Noun suffixes (-ya, -ka)

`-ya` turns a goods-noun into the shop that sells it or the shopkeeper who runs it (context-dependent); the shopkeeper sense additionally takes `san` (p. 27). `-ka` turns a field/subject noun into "a specialist/expert in that field" (p. 27–28).

### §5.1–5.2 What are pronouns? / Personal pronouns

Japanese personal pronouns refer only to people — there is no Japanese equivalent of English "it" in this category (p. 29). The overriding norm, repeated as a standing tip, is that Japanese speakers prefer *not* to use personal pronouns at all when context makes the referent clear — this is presented as a working default, not just a stylistic option (p. 29).

### §5.2-1 Special usage

Direct `anata` ("you") is specifically discouraged — the recommended substitute is the addressee's name + `san` (p. 30–31). Likewise, `kare`/`kanojo` ("he"/"she") are better replaced by the referent's actual name whenever known, and can be dropped outright when the subject is already clear from context (p. 32). This is a second, independent documented instance of the register's systematic pronoun-avoidance, beyond §5.2's own general tip.

### §5.2-2 Case

Unlike English, which has distinct nominative/objective/possessive pronoun forms (he/him/his), Japanese personal pronouns are case-invariant — the same word (`watakushi`) is used for subject, object, and possessive roles, with the grammatical role instead signaled by a following particle (`wa`/`ga` for topic/subject, `o` for object, `no` for possessive) (p. 33–35).

### §5.3 Interrogative pronouns

A full inventory of question words is given (p. 35, table above) — some are grammatically pronouns, others adjectives/adverbs in Japanese terms, but the source groups them functionally as "interrogative words."

### §5.4 Indefinite pronouns

Indefinite pronouns pair a "positive" form (someone/something/somewhere) with a parallel "negative-polarity" form (no one/nothing/nowhere) built from the same interrogative stem; critically, the negative-sense forms require a grammatically negative verb to carry their "no one/nothing" meaning — they are not independently negative words (p. 36).

### §5.5 Demonstrative pronouns (ko-so-a-do)

A single four-way proximal/medial/distal/interrogative stem system (`ko-`/`so-`/`a-`/`do-`) generates parallel pronoun, adjective, and adverb paradigms by suffixation, fully tabulated in the source (p. 37) — this is one of the more systematically important grammar points in the whole book for understanding how Japanese demonstratives/locatives are built compositionally from a shared four-term deictic base. A second, politer row (`kochira`/`sochira`/`achira`/`dochira`) duplicates the `koko`-row meanings at a higher register (p. 37).

### §5.6 Relative pronouns

Japanese has no words corresponding to English relative pronouns (who, whom, whose, which, where, that) — relative-clause-like modification is instead achieved by Japanese's general left-branching noun-modification pattern (a clause directly precedes the noun it modifies, with no overt relativizer), to be covered further in §7 on Verbs (p. 37–38).

### §5.7 Reflexive pronouns

Japanese collapses the entire English reflexive-pronoun set (myself/yourself/himself/themselves/etc.) into one number- and gender-neutral word, `jibun`. It is restricted to humans and warm-blooded animals — not usable for fish, reptiles, insects, or inanimate objects — a genuine animacy-based grammatical constraint rather than a stylistic one (p. 38). Commonly followed by particles `de` ("by oneself") or `no` ("one's own ...") (p. 38–39).

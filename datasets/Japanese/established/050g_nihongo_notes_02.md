## Grammar points

### Giving/receiving verbs (*ageru*/*kureru*/*morau* and their polite forms) are perspective-locked, not freely interchangeable
*Ageru* can only describe giving TO a third party, never giving to the speaker — a sentence with the
speaker as recipient must use *kureru* (polite *kudasaru*) instead, even though both would gloss as
"give" in English. Adding *-te kureru*/*-te morau* to an otherwise-neutral action verb (e.g.
*oshiete-kureta*, "kindly taught [me]") additionally marks the act as an appreciated favor — omitting
it states the same fact without conveying gratitude, and can even sound faintly negative (pp.128-131).

### Honorific/humble verb selection tracks the speaker's momentary group-identification, not fixed social rank
The *irasshaimasu* (honorific) / *orimasu* (humble) pair for "to be" is chosen based on whether the
speaker is currently identifying with the referent as a personal acquaintance or speaking on behalf of
a group the referent belongs to — the same speaker can correctly use the honorific form for a
colleague in one phone call and the humble form for the same colleague (or even her own boss) in the
very next call, depending on who she's speaking to and in what capacity (pp.142-143).

### Formulaic hedges before criticism/requests/business are performed signals, not literal content
Several separate columns in this range converge on one recurring pattern already established earlier
in the volume: fixed framing phrases (*Taihen iinikui-n-desu-ga*, *Sassoku-desu-ga*, *Konna koto-o
itte-wa nan-desu-ga*, *Zenryaku*) are not meant to be parsed for literal content — they are
conventionalized social signals marking that the speaker is about to do something that requires
license (criticize, request a favor, skip expected preliminaries), and must be used even when, taken
literally, they don't quite fit the situation (pp.132-133, 140-141, 146-147).

### Dialect is not merely accent — it carries distinct, sometimes contradictory, lexical meanings
*Kowai* ("frightening" in standard Japanese) means "tired" (among other senses) in several regional
dialects, and *shiasatte*'s exact day-count varies by dialect — genuine lexical-semantic divergence,
not just pronunciation difference. Despite nationwide comprehension of standard Japanese via education
and mass media, speakers still reach for dialect specifically to express emotion, which they feel
standard Japanese cannot fully replace (pp.144-145).

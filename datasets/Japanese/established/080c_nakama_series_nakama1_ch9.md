## Grammar points

### I. Indicating choices using 〜にします; making requests using 〜をおねがいします

When ordering, speakers can use 〜にします ("decide on ~," a generalized sense of します beyond
"do" — also usable outside dining, e.g. トヨタにします "I'll go with a Toyota"), 〜をおねがいします
("I would like ~," request-register), or the plainer 〜を下さい ("please give me ~," taught in Ch.
8). Quantity counters (〜つ for dishes/drinks, 〜本 for bottles) attach only to おねがいします/下さい,
not にします, directly after を. Multiple items chain as "Xを[qty]とYを[qty]...おねがいします."
(p. 352–353)

### II. Eliciting and making proposals using 〜ましょうか and 〜ましょう

Distinct from Ch. 6's invitation form 〜ませんか ("won't you..."), 〜ましょうか ("shall we...?")
elicits a joint decision and 〜ましょう ("let's...") states one, forming a request/response pair:
どこへ行きましょうか。("Where shall we go?") / きょうとへ行きましょう。("Let's go to Kyoto.").
(p. 356–358)

### III. Question word + か + (particle) + affirmative; question word + (particle) + も + negative

**Indefinite expressions** (何か "something," どこか "somewhere," だれか "someone," いつか
"sometime") are built by adding か to a question word, used only with affirmative statements/
questions (including negative-question invitations like 何か食べませんか, which — despite the
verb's negative form — are not semantically negative). は/も and time-の-に are never used with these
forms; が/を are typically dropped; へ/に are optional. A following adjective+noun is grammatically
required even where English allows the bare adjective (何かおいしいものを作ります "I'll make
something good" — a noun after the adjective is obligatory, unlike the English equivalent).

**Negative-universal expressions** (何も, だれも, どこも + negative predicate = "not...any/no...")
work in parallel but require a negative main clause; は/も/が/を are excluded here too. どこでも
("everywhere") is flagged as an irregular exception — it takes an *affirmative* statement despite the
も. (p. 359–361)

### IV. Giving reasons using から; expressing opposition/hesitation using けど

**から vs. ので** (ので was introduced in Ch. 7): both mark reason-clauses preceding a result clause,
but the source makes an explicit, source-marked formality distinction — **から reflects the
speaker's personal opinion/judgment and "may sound a little stronger," while ので "may sound softer
and more polite, especially in formal speech."** This is a direct register contrast between two
near-synonymous grammatical reason-markers, worth flagging for slang-mechanics work on how competing
"plain" grammaticalized forms can carry different formality weight even without being lexical slang
themselves. から attaches to the plain form using だ (not な) for the copula/な-adjectives.
Plain-form + からです is also common as a standalone reason-statement.

**けど vs. が**: both connect opposing clauses ("but, although"), but the source explicitly states
**けど "tends to sound more colloquial than が and is often used in casual conversation."** Like が,
it can also open a conversation/introduce a topic without strong opposition (あのう、すみませんけど、
今何時ですか — "Excuse me, but what time is it?"), functioning as a softening discourse particle more
than a true logical conjunction in that use. (p. 363–367)

### V. Making inferences based on direct observation using verb/adjective stem + そうだ

Stem + そうだ ("looks like, appears to") expresses a low-certainty visual/auditory impression —
おいしそう "looks delicious," つめたそう "looks cold." The source explicitly restricts this form:
**そうだ cannot attach to adjectives of shape, color, or other directly-observable results**
(*きれいそうです, あかそうです, まるそうです are all ungrammatical) — because そうだ specifically
encodes *inference*, not direct perception of an inherently visible property, a fine semantic
distinction the textbook states explicitly rather than leaving implicit. そうだ is itself a
な-adjective (そうじゃない negation for adjectives; そうにない for verbs; いい→よさそう and
ない→なさそう are the two irregular stem changes) and can modify nouns directly (たのしそうな
アルバイト "a part-time job that looks fun"). (p. 369–370)

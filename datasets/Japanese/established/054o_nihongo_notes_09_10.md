## Grammar points

### No koto vs. ni tsiite: personal/daily topic marker vs. formal topical "about" (p.82-83)
*...no koto* (literally "the matter of...") is the idiomatic way to say "thinking about [a person]"
and more broadly to mark the topic of ordinary daily conversation — a part-time job, an exam, an
attitude toward someone. *...ni tsuite* is reserved for formal, topical discussion (a lecture naming
its subject), and sounds strange substituted into personal, daily-life statements — the chapter's
framing dialogue turns on exactly this: *Imooto-ni tsuite kangaete-imasu* sounds off where *Imooto-no
koto-o kangaete-imasu* is natural.

### Dattara, its polite form deshitara, and the weaker neutral ja (p.84-85)
*Dattara* ("if that is the case") is built on the conditional *-tara* attached to the copula, and is
specifically used to preface an opinion or proposal grounded in information just received; it's
familiar-register and not used toward superiors, where *deshitara* substitutes. *Ja* ("then") can
replace *dattara* in many contexts but is weaker — it lacks *dattara*'s implication of responding to
received information, which is why *dattara* cannot substitute into a plain farewell like *Ja,
sayoonara*.

### Hotondo's scope trap: "almost all" vs. "almost did" (p.86-87)
Placed before a verb, *hotondo* quantifies over a set of entities ("almost all of them"), not over
whether a single action nearly happened — *Hotondo korobimashita* means "almost all of them stumbled,"
not "I almost stumbled," for which *moo sukoshi-de...tokoro-deshita* is the correct construction.
Separately, *hotondo* cannot precede a bare number naming a precise price, age, or clock time (though
it can precede a number naming a duration or amount), and *hotondo...nai* is the idiomatic way to say
"hardly/scarcely."

### Ta ato-de vs. te-kara: pure sequencing vs. causal dependency (p.88-89)
Both connect two actions as "after X, Y," but differ in what's implied. *...ta ato-de* marks pure
temporal sequencing — the speaker's concern is just the order of events, used more for factual
information. *...te-kara* implies the second action depends on the result of the first — often the
first action is a genuine precondition for the second — and tends to be used when expressing an idea
or opinion rather than a bare fact.

### Torareru: literal robbery extended to "forced to pay an unwelcome amount" (p.90-91)
*Torareru*, the passive of *toru* ("take/rob/steal"), is used literally for an actual theft, and
extends idiomatically to express displeasure at having to pay more than felt fair — especially for
taxes, fines, and service charges where the exact amount wasn't known in advance. In ordinary
conversation, *harau* ("to pay") is the everyday verb for tax payment; the official term *osameru*
is noted as falling out of everyday use, plausibly because people don't feel their taxes are "going
where they should" (its literal meaning).

### Ya: a familiar, largely male sentence-final particle with two uses (p.92-93)
*Ya* resembles *na* in function but is more familiar and seldom used by women. One use emphasizes the
speaker's feelings in a monologue-like sentence, attaching to i-adjectives and to verbs' negative
forms (both describe conditions/states) but never to *na*-adjectives or action-describing verb forms.
A second, distinct use emphasizes a suggestion when attached to a volitional verb form — also mainly
confined to men's familiar speech.

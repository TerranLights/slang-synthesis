# Japanese — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Introduction to Intermediate Japanese (Wave 3, Audio Courses/Textbooks)

**Source:** Nobuko Mizutani (水谷信子), *総合日本語 初級から中級へ / Introduction to Intermediate Japanese: An
Integrated Course* (ALC/Bonjinsha, 1996 printing used here). Vision-read in full (no text layer; a
scanned PDF, 242 PDF pages). Covers the book's 15 main lessons (レッスン1-15), each built from 本文 (Main
Text), 会話文I/II (Dialogues), 単語のまとめ (Vocabulary List), and 文法ノート (Grammar Notes) sections.

**Coverage note / sampling strategy:** This is a 15-lesson intermediate course with a highly
repetitive internal structure — every lesson follows 本文 → 会話 → 単語のまとめ → 文法ノート → 文型練習 (Structure
Exercises) → ディスコース練習 (Discourse Exercises) → 漢字熟語練習 (Kanji Compound Exercises) → 応用読解練習 (Applied
Reading Exercises). Per the coverage rule, the **単語のまとめ (every vocabulary item) and 文法ノート (every
grammar point)** sections were read and extracted for **every one of the 15 lessons** — this is the
book's high-information-density content and was not sampled down. The 本文/会話文 narrative prose was
skimmed for topic/context only (each lesson's topic is named in its subsection heading below), and
the drill sections (文型練習, ディスコース練習, 漢字熟語練習, 応用読解練習) were skipped entirely as repeated
pattern-practice reusing vocabulary already captured — consistent with the spec's coverage rule.
Lessons 1-3 got a fuller pass including a closer look at illustrative dialogue lines cited in the
Grammar Notes; lessons 4-15 focus on the Vocabulary List + Grammar Notes tables/subsections
(equally complete for those two sections, just without extra dialogue-line color commentary).

**PDF pagination gotcha (new, worth flagging for future sources from this same scan batch):** This
PDF's printed-page-to-PDF-page offset is **not constant** — it starts at `pdf = printed + 1` but
drifts to `pdf = printed + 4` by lesson 2 onward, because a handful of early pages (around printed
pp. 11-12) were evidently scanned twice (duplicate page images found at two different PDF page
indices with the same printed page number). This was caught by checking the printed page number
visible on each rendered page rather than trusting a fixed arithmetic offset — the same discipline
the extraction spec recommends for two-page-per-image spreads, applied here to a different kind of
offset drift (mid-book duplicate pages, not a spread miscount).

**Vision-reading guard:** No handwritten marginalia was found in any page read for this extraction —
all content read was clearly typeset/printed. Some pages show faint bleed-through/ghosting from the
facing page's print (normal scan artifact of thin paper), which was not mistaken for marginalia.

**Vision Reading Confidence:** All entries below are `verified` unless otherwise noted per-row — the
book's own Vocabulary List and Grammar Notes sections are printed in large, clean, high-contrast type
(no font-substitution or OCR-cipher issues; this is a direct image scan, not a corrupted text layer),
and every gloss is directly printed in English alongside the Japanese term (not inferred), so

**Morphological composition:** Japanese is agglutinative; most entries below are simple lexical items

### Grammar points

#### そうである / そうだ (hearsay report), de-aru / de-atta (editorial copula)
In editorials and essays, *de-aru* and *de-atta* are often used in place of *desu* and *deshita*
(e.g. *Shoogatsu-wa zeetaku-o suru toki-de-atta*, "New Year's was a time for luxury"; *deshoo* becomes
*de-aroo* in editorial style). `…soo-desu`/`de-aru` reports what the speaker has heard rather than
directly witnessed. (p. 12)

#### …がきらいな人もある (aru with people/animals as a "some exist" construction)
*Iru* places a person/animal at a specific location; *aru*, used instead for people in sentences like
this, indicates a *characteristic* — closer to English "some" ("there are some people who dislike…").
(p. 12)

#### 海外じゃなくて…… (deliberate ellipsis)
The speaker intentionally leaves off a phrase like *kokunai-desu-ka* ("…or in Japan?") from the end of
a question, letting the implication stand unstated. (p. 13)

#### 〜ほどの金はない (hodo = sufficiency)
*Hodo* indicates amounts and means "is enough [for]." Example: *Shokuji-o suru hodo-no jikan-wa
arimasen* — "We don't have enough time to eat a meal." (p. 13)

#### お酒のんだり (object marker を omission)
In informal conversation, the object-marking particle *o* is frequently omitted. (p. 13)

#### …のんだり、…食べたり (tari…tari suru, exemplifying construction)
The *…tari…tari suru* form describes an event/situation by naming a few of its characteristic
features. (p. 13)

#### あら (feminine surprise interjection)
Similar to *aa*. In informal, feminine Japanese, *ara* expresses the speaker's surprise or
astonishment. (p. 13)

#### わたし、初もうでは好き (topic marker は ellipsis)
In conversations between intimates, the *wa* portion of *watashi-wa* is often dropped. (p. 13)

#### ふうん (mild-surprise plain-speech interjection)
An expression of mild surprise often heard in plain speech. (p. 13)

#### いなかへ行ってくれない? (te-kurenai? informal request)
The informal form *…te-kurenai?* is used for making requests. (p. 13)

#### どうするの (familiar question-ending ellipsis)
In familiar conversation, both men and women omit the *desu-ka* ending (e.g. *Doko-e iku-no?*, "Where
are you going?"). (p. 13)

#### いいわねえ、いなかがあって (clause-order inversion for emphasis)
Equivalent to *Inaka-ga atte kooun-da* ("You're lucky to have a hometown"). Speakers often invert
clause order to add emphasis to their opinion. (p. 13)

#### たいくつだもの (mono as a reason/cause marker)
Like *kara*, *mono* indicates reasons or causes; friends use *da-mono*, and *desu-mono* is the female
form. (p. 13)

### Grammar points

#### 何回も続けると (causal と)
The particle *to* connects a cause with its effect — the same function as in *Ashi-no ura-o
shigeki-suru-to* ("stimulating the soles of the feet, [it]…"). Example: *Goji-ni naru-to kuraku
narimasu* — "When 5:00 rolls around, it gets dark." (p. 29)

#### よい勉強法だったわけである。 (wake-de-aru for conclusions)
*…wake-de-aru* emphasizes conclusions; it's used after a few words of explanation to imply "this is
the way things are." (p. 29)

#### 実験かなにか (ka nanika softening device)
*~ka nanika* is equivalent to "~or something"/"~or other." If a speaker instead said
*Jikken-de wakatta-n-desu-ka*, it would sound brusque/pushy — appending *ka nanika* lightens the
remark. (p. 29)

#### テレビゲームみたいな (mitai-na ≈ no yoo-na)
*Mitai-na* has the same meaning as *no yoo-na* ("like a…") and is the familiar-register equivalent.
(p. 29)

#### クツをぬいで。 (te-form as command)
Here *~de* (the *~te* form) is being used as a command form. (p. 29)

#### ここでですか。 (ellipsis for over-familiarity avoidance)
Short for *koko-de [nugu-no]-desu ka?* — if *desu* were dropped entirely (leaving just *koko-de*), the
statement would be too familiar/inappropriate for addressing a section chief. (p. 29)

#### 竹をふむなんて… (nante as emotional nado-to-yuu-koto-wa)
*~nante* has the same meaning as *~nado-to yuu koto-wa* but is more emotional than *no-wa*; an
implied omitted word (e.g. *iyada*, "is disgusting") is understood at the end. (p. 29)

### Grammar points

#### ふろに入っていたりして (tari-suru for illustrative example)
*Furo-ni haitte-iru + tari-suru*. The *…tari-suru* phrase helps give an example describing a
particular situation. Example: *Ocha-o nondari, okashi-o tabetarishita* — "I sipped tea and ate some
snacks" (two examples describing an enjoyable party, etc.). (p. 43)

#### 出なくてすむ (nakute-sumu ≈ nakute-mo yoi)
*…nakute sumu* ("get by without doing…") is similar to *shinakute-mo yoi* ("not have to do…").
Example: *Kondo-no shiken-wa yasashii-kara, amari benkyoo-shinakute sumu* — "The upcoming test should
be easy, so I think I can get by without studying." *Nakute-mo sumu* (same form with *mo* added) can
also be used in this context. (p. 43)

#### ありがたいこと ("a joyous occurrence")
"Something that makes [us] feel joyful." Example: *Yasumi-ga tsuzuku-no-wa arigatai koto-da* —
"Having several holidays in a row is a blessing." (p. 43)

#### お話しください (polite imperative, more formal than -te kudasai)
More polite than *hanashite-kudasai*. Examples: *oyomi-kudasai* ("please read"); *omochi-kudasai*
("please bring"). (p. 43)

#### 人にも (shortened "hoka-no hito-ni-mo")
A shortened version of *hoka-no hito-ni-mo* ("on other people"). Example: *Jibun-no koto-wa jibun-de
shinasai. Hito-ni tanonde-wa ikemasen* — "Take care of your own matters. Don't ask others to do them
for you." (p. 43)

#### 聞かせたくない (causative + -tai + negative chain)
*Kikaseru + tai + nai*. The causative endings *…seru* and *…saseru* are illustrated: *iku→ikaseru;
yomu→yomaseru; taberu→tabesaseru; deru→desaseru*. (p. 43)

#### ……だな (na as self-directed particle)
*Na* is used by both men and women as a particle for talking to oneself. (p. 43)

#### ……のことなんですけどね (topic-introduction formula)
Used for introducing a topic into the conversation. (p. 43)

#### ……てくださいませんか (more formal than te-kudasai)
More formal than *…te kudasai*. (p. 43)

#### どうなんですか (aizuchi expectation in phone conversations)
In Japanese telephone conversations, speakers pause frequently to wait for their listener's *aizuchi*
(brief acknowledgment words). An absence of *aizuchi* will confuse the speaker — in Dialogue I, the
speaker momentarily forgets he is talking to an answering machine, so he absentmindedly asks "Doo
nan-desu-ka," adding a touch of humor. (p. 43)

#### ないんだった (past tense for a suddenly-recalled forgotten item)
The past tense is often used when a forgotten item (something read or heard) is suddenly recalled.
Examples: *Donata-deshita-ka* — "What was your name again?" (for a name that was learned, then
forgotten); *A, kyoo-wa doyoo-datta* — "Oh — today is Saturday." (p. 43)

#### 旅行中なんだって (familiar hearsay: tte ≈ to itta/to kiita)
A familiar version of *ryokoochuu-na-no-da-soo-desu*; *tte* is a short way of expressing *to itta* or
*to kiita*. (p. 43)

#### 言ったら? (shortened advice form)
A shortened form of *ittara doo (-desu-ka)* — *ittara?* is often used when giving advice in familiar
conversation. (p. 43)

#### 出られない (potential form of 出る, and potential-formation rules)
*Derareru* is the potential form of *deru*. Potential forms are constructed as: (I) *yomu→yomeru;
kaku→kakeru*; (II) *deru→derareru; miru→mirareru*; (III) *kuru→korareru; suru→dekiru*. (p. 43)

### Grammar points

#### できなくなってしまった (shimatta adding a weight of feeling)
Though the phrase's *meaning* is the same as *dekinaku natta*, the *shimatta* at the end expresses a
multitude of feelings about the fact the action has ended — often, as in the Main Text,
disappointment. (p. 58)

#### かぎられる (passive of kagiru for limitation)
The passive form of *kagiru* is often used in this context. Example: *Yosan-ga kagirarete-iru-kara,
sore-wa dekinai* — "Our budget is limited, so we can't do it." (p. 58)

#### たくさん売れるわけである (wake-de-aru/wake-desu for "that's the way it is")
After finishing an explanation of a situation, it's convenient to use *…wake-de-aru* or *…wake-desu*,
similar in nuance to "that's the way it is." (p. 58)

#### 文化の世界にあそぶ (idiomatic ni asobu)
The phrase *~ni asobu* is idiomatic, similar to *~ni kokoro-ga itte-iru* ("…is drawn to [the world of
a culture]"). *De* is not used here. (p. 58)

#### ……と言えよう (volitional-as-"can be said" + ieru-de-aroo)
*Ieyoo* is similar to *ieru-de-aroo*, or *yuu koto-ga dekiru-de-aroo* (lit. "the act of saying [it] can
be done"). A written form whose spoken equivalent is *…to ieru-deshoo*. (p. 59)

#### コーヒーでも飲みませんか (demo as roundabout invitation softener)
*Koohii-o* could be used in place of *Koohii-demo*. When offering an invitation, the speaker uses
*demo* to imply, in a roundabout fashion, that the guest may want to drink something else instead.
(p. 59)

#### お願いするわ (feminine register, polite form onegai-shimasu)
Used in conversations between women who are friends; its polite form is *onegai-shimasu*. Both forms
are for accepting kindnesses from others. Example: A: *Onimotsu o-mochi-mashoo-ka*. B: *Sumimasen.
Onegai-shimasu*. ("A: Shall I carry your bags? B: Thanks, I'd appreciate it.") (p. 59)

#### 1分って (tte for clarifying a listener's statement)
*…tte* is used by the speaker to clarify something her listener has just said. Could be rephrased as
*Ippun-to itta-no-wa naze-desu-ka. Insutanto koohii-o tsukau-kara-desu-ka* ("Why did you say 'one
minute'? Was it because you're planning on using instant coffee?"). (p. 59)

#### だって (personal-viewpoint reason marker, avoid in polite contexts)
*Datte* is used to highlight a strong personal viewpoint while stating the reason for something — for
this reason, avoid it in polite situations. (p. 59)

#### ひまもないし (shi…shi for stating a series of reasons)
*…shi, …shi* is used for stating a series of reasons. Here, a phrase like *sore-de, koohii-o
ireru-no-ni tema-o kakeru koto-wa yameta* ("so I decided to give up taking the time to make coffee")
is implied after *…mezurashikunaku natta-shi*. (p. 59)

### Grammar points

#### …という話である (relayed-information soo-da variant)
Like *…soo-da*, this phrase passes on an item of information that was received earlier — most

*Mitai-na* is more conversational in tone than *yoo-na*; likewise, *mitai-ni* is the conversational
equivalent of *yoo-ni*. (p. 75)

#### 今だって (datte, conversational register of demo)
*Datte* is more conversational in tone than *demo*. (p. 75)

#### 動物園にでもつとめますよ (demo for "for example")
*Demo* here ("for example") is used for stating an example. The verb *tsutomeru* connects with the
particle *~ni*. (p. 75)

#### ここなんか (nanka ≈ nado, persuasive example-giving)
Similar to *nado*, *nanka* is used for stating an example to persuade one's listener. Example:
*Kore-nanka doo-desu-ka. Kirei-desu-yo* — "What about this one? Isn't it attractive?" (p. 75)

#### 地下だもの (mono as familiar justification, more familiar than kara)
*Mono*, a sentence ending used to justify a position, is more familiar than *kara*. (p. 75)

#### 乗らないわけにはいかない (wake-ni-wa ikanai ≈ no alternative)
*Wake-ni-wa ikanai* could be rephrased as *hoka-ni hoohoo-ga nai* (lit. "there are no alternative
ways"). (p. 75)

#### 働くなんて (nante ≈ nado-to-yuu-koto-wa)
Equivalent to *hataraku-nado to-yuu koto-wa* ("the act of working, for example"). (p. 75)

#### それはそうだけど (concessive agreement formula)
A form for showing that one concedes that the speaker's statement is correct. (p. 75)

### Grammar points

#### だから…ことになる (inevitable-result phrase)
A phrase for pointing out an inevitable result, equivalent to "That means…" or "It boils down to…."
Example: *Eki-no chikaku-wa yachin-ga takai, dakara futsuu-no sarariiman-wa eki-kara tooi tokoro-ni
sumu koto-ni naru* — "Rents near the station are high, meaning most salarymen end up living far from
the station." (p. 89)

#### …ようになったら (hypothetical distant-future reference)
Used for referring to the distant future, or to events unlikely to occur for a long time. Example:
*Moshi, kekkon-shite kodomo-o sodateru yoo-ni nattara, ima-no shigoto-wa tsuzukerarenai-ka-mo
shirenai* — "If I were to get married and start raising kids, I might be unable to continue my
current job." (p. 89)

#### …ですって (feminine soo-desu equivalent)
The same as *soo-desu*. Feminine register. (p. 89)

#### …てみますか (interest in the effect of trying, stronger than ukeru)
In the phrase *ukete-miru*, interest in the *effect* of the act of taking is expressed more strongly
than in a simple *ukeru*. Example: A: *Oishii-desu-ka, sore*. B: *Tabete-mite-kudasai* — "A: Does it
taste any good? B: Have a bite and find out." (p. 89)

#### …もとめるのは…だ (no ≈ mono/koto in nominalized predicate)
Here, *no* could be replaced with *mono* or *koto*. Example: *Hitsuyoo-na-no-wa netsui-da* — "What is
needed is enthusiasm." (p. 89)

#### …(し)たいという気持ち (to-yuu for emphasis)
*To-yuu* adds emphasis, making the phrase's meaning ring clear. Example: *Hatarakoo-to yuu kimochi* —
"The feeling of wanting to work." (p. 89)

### Grammar points

#### 開発されている (passive sareru + kanji-compound verbs)
*Sareru* is the passive form of *suru*; it often accompanies kanji compounds. Examples:
*shuppan-sareru* ("to be published"); *happyoo-sareru* ("to be announced"). (p. 103)

#### 〜だけでなく (bakari in place of dake)
*Bakari* can be used in place of *dake*; of the two, *dake* is used more often in conversation.
(p. 103)

#### 〜でなければいけない (naranai/ikenai/dame-da synonymy chain)
*Naranai* can be used in place of *ikenai*; *ikenai* is used more often in conversation. *Dame-da* may
substitute for either word. The phrases *~nakute-wa* and *~nakucha* are often used in place of
*~nakereba*. (p. 103)

#### 〜に対して, 〜に対する (ni-taishite precedes a verb; ni-taisuru precedes a noun)
*~ni taishite* precedes a verb. Example: *Kare-wa gakusei-ni taishite fukai aijoo-o motte-iru* —
"He feels deep affection toward the students." *~ni taisuru* precedes a noun. Example: *Gakusei-ni
taisuru kare-no aijoo-wa fukai* — "The affection that he feels toward the students is deep." (p. 103)

#### 農家の人にとっては (ni-totte followed by evaluative description)
*~ni totte* is followed by a description of pluses and minuses, emotions, commitments, experiences, or
other things relating to someone. Example 1: *Asobu koto-wa, kodomo-ni totte hijoo-ni taisetsu-na
koto-desu* — "Play is extremely important for children." Example 2: *Kore-wa watashi-ni totte
hajimete-no keiken-desu* — "This is a new experience for me." Pay close attention to the difference
between *ni totte* and *ni taishite*. (p. 103)

#### できたんですって (desutte, feminine soo-desu variant)
*Desutte* is a feminine form. With close friends, both men and women may use *datte* instead.
*Soo-desu* is a common form often heard in polite conversation. (p. 103)

#### おじいさん (fictive-kinship address term from a child's viewpoint)
Women typically call the father of their husbands *otoosan*; in families with children, though, all
members often refer to him as *ojiisan*, taking the standpoint of the kids. This dialogue portrays the
lifestyle of a typical farm family in Japan — the grandfather and wife look after the farm; the
husband earns a living elsewhere. (p. 103)

#### 捨てるなんて (nante ≈ nado-to-yuu-koto-wa, implied omission)
*Nante* is equivalent to *nado-to yuu koto-wa* ("[throw away] or do something similar"). A phrase like
*zannen-da* ("is unfortunate") is purposely omitted at the end of the sentence. (p. 103)

### Grammar points

#### 時には……こともある (toki-ni-wa, emphasis via fronting)
Even omitting the *toki-ni-wa* portion of this phrase, it still means "sometimes" — but the meaning
comes across more clearly with *toki-ni-wa* placed at the beginning. (p. 117)

#### カウンセリングをしてきた (kita for a continued-from-the-past action)
The *kita* in *shite-kita* means "to have continued (it) from the past." Example: *Juunenkan kono
daigaku-de oshiete-kita* — "I've been teaching at this university for ten years." (p. 117)

#### ねむれない (potential of nemuru, -u→-eru potential rule)
The negative of *nemureru*, the potential form of *nemuru*. To put verbs like *nemuru* and *iku* into
their potential form, change the final *u* into *eru*. Examples: *iku→ikeru* ("go→can go");
*au→aeru* ("meet→can meet"). (p. 117)

#### おきられない (potential of okiru, -ru→-rareru potential rule)
The negative of *okirareru*, the potential form of *okiru*. To put verbs like *miru* and *okiru* into
their potential form, change the *-masu* form to *-rareru*. Examples: *miru→mirareru* ("see→can
see"); *okiru→okirareru* ("wake up→can wake up"). (p. 117)

#### 必要であろう (de aroo as more literary than daroo)
*De aroo* is more of a written form than *daroo*. (p. 117)

#### 気をつけることですね (verb + koto-desu ≈ -suru-beki-da)
The *verb + koto-desu* form is often used to mean *~suru-beki-da* ("must do~"). Example: *Hito-no
warukuchi-wa iwanai-koto-desu* — "You must not say bad things about people." (p. 117)

### Grammar points

#### こういう ("this type of", neutral register)
"This type of." Though similar to *konna*, this phrase lacks its critical or disparaging tone. Two
variations are *koo-yuu* and *aa-yuu*. (p. 131)

#### 記録される (sareru passive on kanji compounds)
*Sareru* is the passive form of *suru*. Examples: *haitatsu-sareru* ("are delivered");
*kenkyuu-sareru* ("is studied"). (p. 131)

#### 事務所からの指令 (kara-no before a modified noun)
Note that *kara* is followed by *no* when nouns are modified. Examples: *tomodachi-kara-no tegami*
("letter from a friend"); *daigaku-de-no benkyoo* ("study at a university"). (p. 131)

#### 〜ことになってしまう ("end up ~ing", unintended negative result)
"End up ~ing." Used for negative results contrary to one's intentions. Example: *Asa okiru-no-ga
osokute, chooshoku-o tabenai koto-ni natte-shimau* — "I always get out of bed late in the morning,
and end up skipping breakfast." (p. 131)

#### これだけ (dake as "that much/many", not just "only")
In this context, *dake* is equivalent to *kono teido-ni* ("around that amount"); *dake* doesn't merely
mean "only" — it often has a nuance of "that much" or "that many." (p. 131)

#### クロネコ (brand logo used for humorous effect)
The robot doesn't necessarily *have* to be a black cat. A stylized logo of a black cat is the symbol
of one of Japan's most topical home delivery agencies — used here to give the text a humorous effect.
(p. 131)

#### 〜なんて (nante ≈ nado-to-yuu-koto-wa)
The same as *~nado-to yuu koto-wa* ("and things of that nature"). (p. 131)

The ending *(t)te* is an abbreviation for *to-yuu mono-wa*. It indicates the topic of a sentence and
is more conversational in tone than *wa*. The *tte* in *benrina seikatsu-tte* is used the same way.
(p. 131)

#### 便利なもんですね (na mon/mono expressing admiration/awe)
*(na) mon/mono* expresses feelings of admiration and awe. (p. 131)

Equivalent to *to kiita* (lit., "I've heard"); the *tte* here is more conversational in tone than
*…soo-desu*. Women use *desu-tte*; close friends use *datte*. (p. 131)

The same as *yoo*. Conversational. (p. 131)

#### ぼくだって (datte ≈ mo, conversational)
The same as *boku-mo*. Conversational. (p. 131)

Here, *mono* has the same meaning as *kara*. A conversational ending, it is self-justificational in

### Grammar points

#### 問題がないわけではない (wake-de-wa nai, softer than mondai-ga aru)
The phrase *…wake-de-wa nai* ("it isn't that…"; "we don't mean to say that…") is used for making
statements usually followed by further explanations. It's slightly softer in tone than phrases like
*mondai-ga aru* ("might pose a problem"). Example: *Ano-hito-o shinyoo-shinai wake-ja nai-ga,
ki-o tsuketa hoo-ga ii* — "It's not that I don't trust that person. I just think you should be
careful." (p. 144)

#### 金を使いすぎる結果になる (〜sugiru examples, and 〜kekka-ni naru for unintended result)
Other examples of *~sugiru* are *tabesugiru* ("overeat") and *monku-o iisugiru* ("make too many
complaints"). The idea "even if you don't intend to" is implied in the ending *~kekka-ni naru* ("end
up ~ing"). If you like, you can add *shimau* to the end of *tsukaisugiru* — for
*tsukaisugite-shimau*. (p. 144)

#### …というのである (clause-splitting with to yuu to shorten long reported-speech sentences)
The ellipsis […] represents the contents of a letter. When conveying the thoughts of somebody else,
you can also use the phrase *…to kaite-ita* ("was written"). To keep the "…" portion from getting too
long, it's sometimes better to break the sentence into two parts — as in the Main Text — and place
*to yuu* at the end of each part, for a clearer sentence. (p. 144)

#### 理性のある人間 (no ≈ ga in attributive clauses)
Here, *no* is identical to *ga*. *Risei-no aru* could be changed to *Risei-o motte-iru*. (p. 144)

#### …といってもよいであろう (restrained equivalent of "we may as well say...")
Equivalent to the English "we may as well say…," this phrase conveys feelings of restraint. You could
also use *…to ieru-de-aroo* in this context. (p. 144)

#### それはそうですが (concessive acknowledgment with an implied differing viewpoint)
A phrase for acknowledging your speaker's statement while implying, at the same time, that your own
point of view may differ. (p. 145)

#### どうも実感がなくてね (implied suki-ni-narenai ending)
A phrase like *suki-ni narenai* ("and I can't seem to like them") is implied at the end of this
statement. (p. 145)

#### 目の前で金が出ていくわけじゃない (money-vanishing idiom)
This phrase means "money vanishes — but it doesn't vanish before one's eyes." (p. 145)

#### 君のクツを買ったもの (mono as self-justifying reason, stronger than kara)
*Mono* indicates a reason; it has a more self-justifying tone than *kara*. (p. 145)

#### 預金のことかい (〜no koto for "regarding〜"; kai as masculine-intimate question particle)
*~no koto* means "in regards to ~." The *kai* at the end expresses intimacy — close friends often use
*kai*, especially if they are male. Most women would say *yokin-no koto?* instead. (p. 145)

### Grammar points

#### ある町の場合についての投書 (no required before a noun-modifying phrase)
*Aru machi-no baai-ni tsuite* modifies the noun *toosho*, so the particle *no* must be used. Example:
*Kore-wa heiwa-undoo-ni tsuite-no kiji-desu* — "This is an article about the peace movement." (p. 159)

#### 最初はめんどうでも (demo, conversational register of de-atte-mo)
*Mendoo-demo* could be rewritten as *mendoo-de-atte-mo*. *Demo* is more conversational in tone than
*de-atte-mo*. (p. 159)

#### なれてしまいます (te-shimau adding completion nuance)
The ending *~te-shimau* adds a feeling of completion to the sentence. (p. 159)

#### ゴミをあまり出さないように (dasu's range of meanings: "place" vs. "produce")
The verb *dasu* can express many different things. In the phrase *gomi-o wakete dasu*, it expresses
the idea of "place," as in "place the trash in the trash disposal area." In *gomi-o amari
dasanai-yoo* it expresses the idea of "produce" — "don't produce too much garbage." (p. 159)

#### 生ゴミや紙なんか (nanka ≈ nado, listing examples)
*Nanka* has the same meaning as *nado* in this phrase, as well as in *purasuchikku-nanka* and
*ochuugen-ya oseibo-no toki-nanka*. (p. 160)

#### あきカンとかビンとかもね (to-ka listing + mo for "and things of the sort")
Equivalent to *aki-kan-ya bin-nado-mo* (*suiyoobi-ni tatemono-no ura-ni oite-kudasai ne*). (p. 160)

#### 困りませんね (rhetorical surprise prompting an explanation)
The standard reply to *komarimasu-ne* would be something like *soo-desu-ne, komarimasu-ne*. The woman
expresses her surprise with *ee*, hoping to provoke her speaker into explaining the reason for his
strange response. (p. 160)

#### 多いもの (mono as informal-conversation kara)
*Mono*, a sentence ending with the same meaning as *kara*, is used in informal conversation. (p. 160)

#### のこさなきゃ (contracted nokosanakereba)
A contracted form of *nokosanakereba*. (p. 160)

#### じゃ、言いますけど (prefacing a contrary opinion)
A phrase used to preface a contrary opinion. (p. 160)

### Grammar points

#### かにの足のように見える (no-yoo-ni mieru for appearances)
The *…no yoo-ni mieru* form talks about appearances. Example: *Ano-ko-wa sei-ga takakute otona-no
yoo-ni mieru* — "That child is tall, and looks like an adult." The phrase *…no yoo-ni tsukuru* could
be rewritten as *…no yoo-ni mieru yoo-ni tsukuru* ("so that the assembled meat looks like high-grade
meat"). (p. 173)

#### …使ったり…作ったりする (tari…tari suru implying unstated additional actions)
The *…tari…tari-suru* form states common examples of an action, implying that other (unstated)
actions are performed as well. Example: *Nichiyoobi-ni-wa, sooji-o shitari sentaku-o shitari-shimasu*
— "On Sundays, I clean and do the wash." A second example: *Yasui sakana-o takai sakana-no namae-de
uttari-suru*. (p. 173)

#### 食べてくださいって (tte ≈ to, quoted-speech ellipsis)
Here, *tte* functions in the same way as the particle *to*. A phrase like *buchoo-ga iimashita*
("said the department head") is omitted from the end. (p. 173)

#### コピー食品だって聞いたので (tte ≈ to, "I've heard" ellipsis)
This *tte* also functions like *to*. A phrase like *watashi-wa tabemasen* ("so I don't want to eat
it") is omitted from the end. (p. 173)

#### コピーって (abbreviated kopii-to yuu-no-wa)
An abbreviated way of saying *kopii-to yuu-no-wa*. (p. 173)

#### サラダオイルで作るんですって (feminine tte/desutte ≈ datte/da-soo-desu)
Again, this *tte* functions like *to*; *kikimashita* ("I've heard") is omitted from the end.
*Desutte* is a feminine form; its masculine equivalent in a conversation like this would be *datte*,
or for more formal conversations, *da-soo desu*. (p. 173)

#### 部長のおごりですもの (mono ≈ kara, feminine when combined with desu)
*Mono* has the same meaning as *kara*. Note, however, that *desu-mono* is a feminine form. (p. 173)

#### どんな添加物が入っているかわからない (uncertainty-conveying ka clause)
The idea "without doubt, it's full of a variety of preservatives" is connoted. Example: *Sonna koto
shitara, ano-hito donna-ni okoru-ka wakaranai* — "If you do that, that person will definitely get
angry." (p. 173)

### Grammar points

#### ……を……と言う (naming-convention form, with particle insertion caution)
A form for supplying background information about the name of something. Don't forget to insert
particles where they are needed. Example: *Daigaku-no shiken-ni ochi tsugi-no shiken-no tame-no
benkyoo-o shite-iru hito-o roonin-to yuu* — "People who study for [college entrance] exams rather
than go on to college are known as *roonin*." (p. 187)

#### 会社のほうも (hoo-mo reinforcing a contrast, no-hoo omission)
After stating something is advantageous for workers, remarking about the advantages for their
company. Although you're allowed to omit *no-hoo*, note that it reinforces the contrast in your
sentence. Example: A: *Ashita-wa tsugoo-ga warui-n-de, asatte-ga ii-n-desu-ga*. B: *Jitsu-wa
watashi-no hoo-mo soo-na-n-desu* — "A: I'm not open tomorrow. What about day-after-tomorrow? B: As a
matter of fact, that would be better for me, too." (p. 187)

#### いいことばかりではない (bakari ≈ "nothing but")
The *…bakari* form has the same meaning as the English "nothing but…." Example: *Sukoshi-mo
asobanai-de, shigoto-bakari shite-iru* — "You do nothing but work, and never have any fun." (p. 187)

#### ……動物なのであろう (aroo as more speculative than aru; na-no-de-aru as explanatory)
*Aroo* is more indefinite and speculative than *aru*. *~na-no-de-aru* is more explanatory in tone
than *de-aru*. It has the same nuance as *~dakara-de-aroo*. (p. 187)

#### じゃ、これで (abbreviated farewell)
An expression for saying goodbye. The speaker has omitted *shitsurei-shimasu* from the end. (p. 187)

#### 行ってらっしゃい (pseudo-family usage among neighbors)
Although *itte-rasshai* is customarily spoken to family members when they leave the home, it's used
among neighbors too — neighbors sometimes think of themselves as members of a large pseudo-family.
(p. 187)

#### おれ、じゃまか (confined-housing cultural commentary)
In the confined houses of Japan, a husband often gets in the way of housework. (p. 187)

#### なんて? (nanto ≈ nante, to-yuu ellipsis + te/tte shift)
The same as *nanto*. *Yuu-no* is omitted from the end. In conversations, this *to* often becomes *te*
or *tte*. (p. 187)

#### いいじゃないか、うわさなんか (nado-ni ki-ni shinakute-mo ii paraphrase)
The same as *Uwasa-nado, ki-ni shinakute-mo ii* ("You needn't pay any attention to rumors"). (p. 187)

### Grammar points

#### …ばかり (right-after-completion marker)
A form meaning "just after the completion of an action." Example: *Nihon-e kita-bakari-desu* —
"Right after coming to Japan." Note that this form implies that the speaker has not had a chance to
do something. (p. 200)

#### …に対する以上 (taisuru with omitted noun + ijoo as "more than")
A phrase like *kawaigarikata* ("way of bestowing affection") is purposely omitted after *tai-suru*.
Technically, *ijoo* means "the same as or more than," but it can also be used to express a simple
"more than." (p. 200)

#### …だけの (dake as "that much", equivalent to to-onaji)
*Dake* doesn't always mean "a little." Its true meaning is "that much." In this case, *dake* is
equivalent to *…to onaji* ("the same as…"). (p. 201)

#### 感じさせる (kanjisaseru, causative set-phrase)
*Kanjisaseru*, the causative of the verb *kanjiru*, is often used in the form of a set expression.
So, too, is *kangaesaseru*. Example: *Kono hon-wa yomu hito-ni iroirona koto-o kangaesaseru* — "This
book makes its readers think about many things." (p. 201)

#### …ですって (feminine da-soo-desu)
Equivalent to *da-soo-desu*. Feminine. (p. 201)

#### …べき (stressing obligation, sounds overblown in conversation)
A word for stressing a responsibility or obligation. When used in conversation, it sounds a little
overblown. (p. 201)

#### はあ? (formal repeat-request particle)
A word for asking the speaker to repeat a comment. More formal than *e?*. In the dialogue, A is
slightly taken aback when her speaker uses the *…beki* form — she expresses her surprise with *haa*,
hoping to prompt her speaker into repeating his remark. (p. 201)

#### だって (familiar subjective-reason marker, often paired with mono)
A familiar expression for stating a subjective reason. Often, *mono* is placed at the end of sentences
such as this. (p. 201)

#### …ことね (advice-giving ending)
*…koto-desu* is an expression for giving advice. Example: *Muri-o shinai koto-desu-yo* — "You
shouldn't overexert yourself." (p. 201)

### Grammar points

#### ……とか……とか言う (to-ka listing with iroirona-koto-o yuu nuance)
Equivalent to *iroiro-na koto-o yuu* ("says a lot of different things"). Example: *Ano-hito-wa
itsumo, samui-to-ka nemui-to-ka monku-o yuu* — "That person always makes a lot of complaints, like
about being cold, or about being sleepy." (p. 214)

#### 使われる (u→wareru passive-formation rule)
The passive form of the verb *tsukau*. Other examples of verbs for which the *u*-ending is changed to
*wareru* are *kau→kawareru* and *warau→warawareru*. (p. 214)

#### おもしろがっているだけではいけない (dake-de-wa ikenai ≈ hoka-ni-mo shinakereba naranai koto-ga aru)
*Omoshirogaru* can be rephrased as *omoshiroi-to kanjiru* ("feel it interesting") with no change in
meaning. The ending *…dake-de-wa ikenai* is equivalent to *hoka-ni-mo shinakereba naranai koto-ga
aru* ("there are other things we must do as well"). Example: *Asonde-iru-dake-de-wa ikenai.
Benkyoo-mo shinasai* — "Just fooling around isn't enough. Start studying." (p. 214)

#### 注意深くなければ、安全にくらすことはできない (conditional-necessity construction)
Refer to the "Remarks" section of the Structure Exercises: a pattern for expressing an "If…"
condition and its result. Two other forms for indicating conditions are *…tara* and *…to* — but only
*…(e)ba* emphasizes the expectation that the "if" will *positively* come true. (p. 214-215)

#### 事故と言いますと? (echo-question clarification request)
Refer to the "Purpose of the Exercise" section of the Discourse Exercises for this construction's
discourse function — it's used to request clarification by echoing back part of what the other
speaker just said. (p. 214)

#### 相性がねえ (nee as surprised-reflection repetition marker)
A phrase for repeating a remark by the speaker to express surprise or reflection. Example: A:
*Ano-hito, kaisha-o yameta-n-desu-yo*. B: *Soo-desu-ka. Ano-hito-ga-nee…* — A: "Did you know that
he's left the firm?" B: "Really? Him?" (p. 214)

#### 不十分なんですって (feminine da-soo-desu, masculine datte variant)
A feminine form of *fujuubun-na-no-da-soo-desu*. In familiar speech, men use *fujuubun-na-n-datte*.
(p. 215)

#### 使わないことにした (koto-ni suru for commitment to a decision)
A form for showing commitment to a decision. Example: *Ano-hito-to-wa tsukiawanai koto-ni shimashita*
— "I've decided against socializing with him." (p. 215)

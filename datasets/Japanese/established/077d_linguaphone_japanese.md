## Grammar points

### Particle ga's archaic possessive sense, preserved in place names and desire/liking expressions (Lesson 3, §1)

The explanatory notes state that ga originally meant "of" — a sense no longer productive in ordinary
modern usage but still fossilized in place names like Tsuru-ga-oka ("Mound-of-Cranes," site of the
Hachiman Shrine in Kamakura) and Jō-ga-Shima ("Isle-of-the-Castle," at the entrance to Tokyo Bay).
The same archaic possessive sense also survives productively in expressions of liking, disliking, and
desire (sampo ga daisuki desu, "very fond of walking"; o-kane ga hoshii n' desu, "I want money"):
nouns governing ideas of liking/disliking/desiring always take ga, but speakers can front the logical
subject with wa instead when they want to emphasize it — e.g. contrasting watashi wa sampo ga suki
de wa arimasen ("I personally am not fond of walking") against a plain ga-marked statement.

### desu as a contraction of de arimasu, and its negative (Lesson 3, §2)

Since desu is explained as literally a contraction of de arimasu, its negative is correspondingly
formed as de wa arimasen (not a separate negative copula) — consistent with the de wa arimasen
pattern already tabulated as a vocabulary item in 077, now given its underlying derivation.

### Register pairing of iimasu/to mōshimasu for "is called" (Lesson 3, §3)

to iimasu ("is called") has a "more respectful" counterpart to mōshimasu, extending the
register-graded-vocabulary pattern already noted in 077/077b (okusan vs. kanai; o-kyaku-san vs.
o-kyaku-sama) to verbs of saying/naming. The notes also flag a separate, unrelated to — the
comitative/instrumental to seen in inu to sampo shimasu ("to go for a walk with the dog") — warning
against confusing the two homophonous particles.

### Chained clauses: desu reserved for the final clause, de for intermediate ones (Lesson 3, §4)

When two or more parallel/similar clauses are chained together (as in "Suzuki is the surname, and
Shigeru is the given name"), only the final clause takes the full copula desu; every intermediate
clause instead takes de, explained as the participle (gerund) form of desu. This is presented as a
general chaining rule, not a one-off sentence quirk, and is cross-referenced forward to Lesson 7 §2
and Lesson 5 §13.

### -ta as the general past-tense marker, attached after a verb's -te-form base (Lesson 3, §5)

aimashita ("met") is explained via -ta functioning as the regular past-tense suffix, attached to the
same base used for the -te gerund form (aimasu → atte/ai- base → aimashita). The notes also observe
that Japanese "meet" (au) requires the postposition ni for its object, unlike the English transitive
verb — comparable to English colloquial "meet up with," "bump into," "come across."

### issho as a noun ("the company") underlying issho deshita / issho ni (Lesson 3, §6)

issho is glossed as a noun meaning "the company," so issho deshita is literally "was part of the
company," and when followed by the particle ni, issho becomes adverbial, "together" (as in the
cross-referenced example Yamada San no okusan mo issho ni imashita, "Mrs. Yamada was together with
him").

### naka no ii as a fixed adjectival phrase for "close, intimate" (Lesson 3, §7)

naka no ii ("close," "intimate") is built from naka ("relations" — explicitly distinguished from the
homophonous naka meaning "inside") plus ii ("good"); the notes point out the noun nakayoshi
(introduced in Lesson 4) is built from the same two word-elements, and that the adjective ii is
sometimes spelled/pronounced yoi — tying this entry back to the yoroshii/yoi/ii register-variant
cluster already seen in 077c.

### uchi ni vs. uchi de: existence-verbs demand ni, action-verbs demand de (Lesson 3, §8)

Both uchi ni and uchi de can be translated "at home," but the notes draw a general rule: locative
expressions of mere existence/state (imasu, arimasu) demand ni, while locative expressions
accompanying an action-verb demand de. Illustrative minimal-pair examples given: densha ni notte
imasu ("I am on the train," existence/state of being aboard) vs. densha de Tōkyō e ikimasu ("I go to
Tokyo by/on the train," action); kare wa furoba ni imasu ("he is in the bathroom") vs. kare wa furoba
de kao o aratte imasu ("he is washing his face in the bathroom," action taking place there).

### Three distinct Japanese verbs corresponding to English "to be" (Lesson 3, §9)

The notes draw together: imasu ("I am"), the verb "to be" for living/animate things; arimasu ("is"),
the verb "to be" for inanimate things; and irasshaimasu, the honorific form of "to be," cross-
referencing Introduction §6 and Lesson 2 §3. This triple distinction (animate/inanimate/honorific)
is the clearest single statement in this source of a split already visible piecemeal in 077's
vocabulary notes (iru vs. aru).

### motsu vs. aru for "to have," and transitivity/argument-structure contrast (Lesson 21, §1)

Both motsu (politely mochimasu) and aru (arimasu) can express "have," but they differ in argument
structure: motsu is an ordinary transitive verb taking an object marked with o (watashi wa unten
menkyo o motte imasu, "I have a driving licence" — note also that "have" uses the present
progressive -te imasu rather than a simple present, one of the few such cases flagged in this
source, cross-referenced to Lesson 5 §7), while aru takes a logical possessor as a ga-marked
"subject" of existence (watashi wa unten menkyo ga arimasu, same meaning via a different
construction).

### ate ni narimasen as an idiom for unreliability (Lesson 21, §2)

ate ni narimasen ("is not to be depended on / is not very reliable") is shown to generalize beyond
watches to people (kare wa ate ni narimasen, "he is not to be depended on"), weather (kono goro no
tenki wa ate ni narimasen, "the weather is fickle at this time of year"), and memory (watashi no
kioku wa ate ni narimasen, "my memory often fails me") — a single idiom productive across animate,
meteorological, and cognitive-faculty subjects alike.

### Day-of-month counting: a native-Japanese exception series for 2nd–10th, reverting to Sino-Japanese ordinals above (Lesson 21, §4)

"one day" is ichi nichi, and the 1st day of the month is irregularly tsuitachi — but for 2 through 10
days/the corresponding day-of-month, the same native-looking series is used for both (2: futsuka; 3:
mikka; 4: yokka; 5: itsuka; 6: muika; 7: nanoka; 8: yōka; 9: kokonoka; 10: tōka; 11: jū ichi nichi).
From 11 onward, ordinary Sino-Japanese numerals are used instead, except for a further irregular
exception at jū yokka ("the 14th"), hatsuka ("the 20th"), and nijū yokka ("the 24th") — cross-
referenced to Lesson 6 §6/§8 and Lesson 4 §1. This is a third numeral subsystem (beyond the native
hitotsu-series and Sino-Japanese ichi-series already tabulated in 077b), specific to calendar dates.

### Double-negative "must" constructions, and the overlap of ikenai/ikemasen with narimasen/narenai (Lesson 45, Part 2 §4)

The notes explain that Japanese "must" requires what reads as a double negative in literal
translation, though colloquially only one needs to be supplied as the "strong" form: orinakute wa
ikemasen ("must one get out of the car?", lit. "can it not go if one does not alight?"); tomatte wa
ikemasen ("you must not stop," lit. "stopping does not go" — compared to French "ça n'ira pas").
ikenai/the politer ikemasen are frequently interchangeable with naranai/narimasen to mean "must,"
"must not," "ought," "ought not," "should," "should not," but ikenai/ikemasen carry the additional
senses "be forbidden," "be prohibited from doing" that naranai/narimasen lack. In both families, the
preceding negative verb form can be either -nakereba or nakute wa (shown as interchangeable in a
side-by-side example: Yoji made ni kaeranakute wa narimasen / Yoji made ni kaeranakereba ikenai n'
desu, both "I have to be home by four").

### mo ii as a grammaticalized "may/can" construction after a participle (Lesson 45, Part 1 §3)

katte mo ii ("I don't mind buying it," "I might buy it," "I may buy it") shows mo ii attaching after
a verb's -te/participle form to express permission or tentative possibility — cross-referenced to
Lesson 26 §6, with further parallel examples (mō o-kaeri ni natte mo ii desu, "you may go home now";
sono hon o katte mo ii desu, "you may buy that book"). The notes also separately explain a nested
quotation construction in the same sentence (to iu to, "when I said," with the first to marking
quoted speech and the second to translatable as "when" — cross-referenced to Lesson 6 §1 and Lesson
33 §3), illustrating that this source's explanatory notes regularly unpack more than one grammar
point per example sentence.

## Grammar points

### Describing a headache: the mimicry-word inventory and figurative extensions (p.46-47)
Extends the stomachache essay's *kirikiri*/*shikushiku* pain-mimicry system with headache-specific
terms: *zukizuki* (regular, pulse-like throbbing — also usable for wounds elsewhere on the body) and
its intensified form *zukinzukin*, built by inserting "n," a general mimicry-word intensification
pattern also illustrated with *doshidoshi* vs. *doshindoshin* ("to walk"/"to walk heavily/thudding
along"). *Gangan* separately describes a clamorous, hammer-on-a-helmet quality of pain. Beyond pain,
"head" vocabulary extends figuratively to general head-state complaints (heaviness from poor sleep,
haziness just after waking or from jet lag) and to a fully figurative idiom for a psychologically
bothersome problem (*Atama-ga itai-desu-yo*, "it's a real headache"). The more formal *zutsuu-ga suru*
can substitute for literal *atama-ga itai*.

### Reporting a fall: taoreru's implied severity vs. korobu (p.48-49)
*Taoreru* ("to fall flatly") applied to a person — literally or in its figurative extension to
collapsing from illness/fatigue — always signals a serious condition; using it for a minor stumble,
as the frame story's protagonist does, genuinely alarms the listener. *Korobu* ("to stumble and
fall") is the correct, lower-stakes verb. A dedicated injury-description vocabulary set follows,
including *hirihiri* (a smarting/stinging sensation specific to scraped skin), rounding out the
pain-mimicry-word family built across this and the two preceding stomachache/headache essays.

### Honno as a general-purpose devaluing modifier, including social function (p.50-51)
*Honno* ("merely, only") signals the speaker regards whatever follows as small or insignificant —
functioning like English "only"/"merely" with explicit quantities, but also modifying words with no
literal number sense at all (calling something "only a child" or "only a joke"). Because of this
built-in devaluing implication, it's frequently recruited for social modesty, most visibly in the
fixed gift-offering opener *Honno tsumaranai mono-desu-ga*, whose underlying logic is that a gift's
size should be proportionate to the gratitude behind it — devaluing language frames an inevitably
disproportionate gift as merely symbolic rather than a genuine (and therefore presumptuous) attempt
at proportionate repayment.

### ...te-kuru vs. ...te-kureru: direction-only vs. direction-plus-gratitude (p.52-53)
Both mark an action as directed toward the speaker (eliminating the need to state *watashi-ni*
explicitly), but *...te-kureru* (or humble *...te-kudasaru*) additionally encodes the speaker's
gratitude, while plain *...te-kuru* is gratitude-neutral — illustrated by the correct choice of plain
*te-kuru* for an annoying repeated imposition (being asked a troublesome favor again), where
*te-kureru* would wrongly imply the speaker felt grateful for it. Both forms can also substitute for
explicitly naming the listener (*anata-ni*/*anata-o*) when a statement is framed from the listener's
own point of view, though doing this with *te-kureru* is harder because it requires correctly
imagining gratitude from someone else's perspective.

### The suffering/adversity passive as the default register for being inconvenienced by someone (p.54-55)
Extending the passive-voice pattern already documented in vol. 5 (`established/052`), this essay
adds a direct active/passive minimal pair (a neighbor's party being merely reported vs. being framed
as something the speaker was subjected to) to show that using the passive here is technically
optional but is the socially expected default whenever the speaker is expected to feel genuinely
annoyed by the situation described — omitting it in that specific context reads as strange, even
though the same sentence without the passive is perfectly grammatical as a neutral statement of fact.

### Giving directions: ...masu's impersonal framing vs. ...te-kudasai's directive force (p.56-57)
Both the plain *...masu* form and *...te-kudasai* can give directions, but they differ in implied
stance: *te-kudasai* is aimed directly at the listener as a request/command and implies the speaker
is confidently taking charge (appropriate from a doctor instructing a patient, or toward a taxi
driver); the *...masu* form instead reads as impersonal/explanatory, as if simply stating a fact
rather than directly urging compliance — which makes it the more polite, less presumptuous default
for giving a stranger directions, paralleled by TV cooking instructors' similar preference for
*...masu* over *...te-kudasai* despite both being technically available.

### The most humble way to ask how to do something: the ...(s)eba ii/yoroshii-deshoo pattern (p.58-59)
Identified as the genuinely humble way to ask for instructions, because it implies the speaker is
earnestly seeking the single objectively correct way to do something, in contrast to several
patterns explicitly flagged as sounding like stilted "classroom Japanese" in real social use
(*Doo shinakereba narimasen-ka*, *Doo suru-beki-desu-ka*, *Yomikata-wa nan-desu-ka* — all
comprehensible but uncommon/stiff). The same *...eba ii-deshoo* construction generalizes beyond literal
instruction-asking to politely soliciting a social superior's wishes on any matter (where to come,
when to bring something).

### Tolerated semantic redundancy in spoken Japanese (p.60-61)
Even though *arakajime yoyaku-suru* ("to reserve beforehand") is technically redundant — the "before"
sense is already baked into *yoyaku* itself via a shared morpheme also found in *yohoo* (forecast),
*yoshuu* (preparatory study), and *yokoku* (previous notice) — doubling semantically-overlapping
words together is a routine, largely unnoticed feature of natural spoken Japanese rather than an
error. A parallel case (*ashita-no ban gogo kuji-goro*, stacking two "evening/PM" markers)
illustrates that this kind of redundancy can be genuinely functional in speech: it lets a listener
progressively narrow down the intended referent in stages as the sentence unfolds, rather than being
mere clutter.

### Otsukaresama, Gokuroosama, and Osewasama: appreciation-for-labor register distinctions (p.62-63)
*Otsukaresama* ("you must be tired") is a broadly applicable sympathy-for-labor expression spanning
greeting, parting, and mid-task contexts — used by a spouse, co-workers, transit staff, and inn
employees alike, and functioning among co-workers as a near-equivalent of *Sayoonara*. *Gokuroosama*
differs by specifically implying the listener performed a duty/obligation (correct for commuters
returning from work, wrong for leisure travelers) — some speakers avoid it even toward people who
genuinely worked for them, since it can sound like the speaker is explicitly judging whether their
effort counted as dutiful labor, substituting *Otsukaresama* or *Osewasama* instead to sidestep that
implication.

### Endoo/endoo-mame and bakuchi-uchi: forgotten-etymology redundancy, and "thinking in kanji" (p.64-65)
Both words are cited as cases where a component morpheme's original meaning ("pea," "gambling") has
been doubled once speakers stopped tracking the etymology — *endoo-mame* literally repeats "pea"
(and the kanji for *en* itself uses "pea" as a radical, tripling it in written form); *bakuchi-uchi*
similarly re-adds "player" after it had already been folded into the contracted form *bakuchi*. The
broader point drawn from this: Japanese speakers, even while not consciously thinking in kanji during
ordinary speech, still implicitly track kanji-level word structure — made visible by native speakers
noticing something "wrong" with a word's redundancy only once they see it written out, despite
decades of reduced everyday kanji literacy.

### The honorific ...(s)areru form vs. ...o...ni naru, and its public/formal-register skew (p.66-67)
*Sareru*, the honorific form of *suru*, carries a genuine double function with plain passive voice,
disambiguated only by context. Its honorific usage is comparatively narrow, concentrated in settings
where personal feeling isn't normally expressed — official announcements, public speeches, news
reporting, an emcee's introduction of a speaker, a reporter addressing a VIP — and pairs naturally
with the kanji-compound (Sino-Japanese) verbs typical of that register. In ordinary personal
conversation, especially among women, the *...o...ni naru* honorific pattern is more typical instead;
a male speaker reaching for *...(s)areru* in personal conversation specifically signals a deliberate
choice to sound reserved/formal, as with someone just met.

### Sorya/Soryaa and the sore-wa opinion-gradient: pitch as the real disambiguator (p.68-69)
Familiar contractions of *sore-wa*/*kore-wa*/*are-wa* (*sorya*, *korya*, *arya*) can, by themselves —
without any further words — fully communicate a speaker's stance purely through tone: a prolonged,
"dangling" vowel (*Soryaa...*) signals disagreement/criticism even with nothing else said, while a
sharply-falling high-pitched *Sorya* signals the opposite, flat agreement. Between those extremes, a
graded set of constructions span from flat agreement (*Sore-wa soo-desu*) through flat disagreement
(*Sore-wa dame-da*) to hedged, reserved disagreement, which characteristically trails off with the
actual negative judgment left unstated after *ga*/*keredomo* — sometimes reduced all the way down to
*Sore-wa*/*Sorya* alone, again relying purely on the dangling tone to carry the entire meaning.

### ...te-imasen for present incompletion vs. ...masen-deshita for a specific past occasion (p.70-71)
A genuinely common error: using *...masen-deshita* to describe an action still pending as of now
(e.g. "I haven't read it yet") is wrong because that form specifically marks a completed past
occasion on which something did NOT happen, not an ongoing not-yet-done state. The correct form for
present incompletion is *...te-imasen* (or simply *mada-desu*). The distinction is illustrated with a
frequently-asked real question for foreign residents — "have you gotten used to living in Japan?" —
flagged as a common site for exactly this error, since the question concerns an ongoing present
condition, not a one-off past event.

### -te-form reason clauses require the following clause to match in controllability (p.72-73)
When a reason is expressed via the -te form of a verb/adjective rather than *node*/*kara*, the clause
before and after the -te form must belong to the same category of controllability: if the *te*-form
clause describes something outside the speaker's control (e.g. rain), the following clause must also
describe an outcome outside the speaker's will — specifically the inability forms (*ikemasen*,
*dame-deshita*, *muri-deshita*, *kaemasen*) rather than simple negative-will forms (*ikimasen*,
*kaimasen*), since the latter would wrongly imply the outcome was the speaker's own choice. Switching
the reason marker to *node* or *kara* removes this restriction entirely, freeing the following clause
to use either form.

### toki-clauses with motion verbs: non-past marks the process, -ta marks the completed result (p.74-75)
Motion verbs (*kaeru*, *kuru*, *modoru*, *tsuku*) in non-past tense within a *toki* ("when") clause
refer only to the ongoing process of moving, never its completion — so a non-past *toki* clause
combined with an action that logically requires having already arrived produces an absurd reading
(changing into a kimono while still in transit, rather than once home). The -ta form of the motion
verb is required whenever "when X has arrived/is there" is the intended meaning, regardless of the
overall sentence's own tense — the same logic extends from *toki*-clauses to relative clauses
(*kita hito*, "the person who has come," vs. *kuru hito*, "the person who is expected to come").

### noni vs. keredo(mo): same logical concessive, different affect (p.76-77)
Both connectives logically link two opposed clauses and are freely interchangeable at the level of
propositional content, but *noni* specifically adds the speaker's surprise, sense of unexpectedness,
or dissatisfaction — a register/affect distinction *keredo(mo)* leaves neutral. This makes *noni* the
connective of choice when a sentence functions as complaint or implicit blame, and it also appears
fixed into set formulas of social regret/self-deprecation (apologizing for not entertaining a guest
properly, protesting that one doesn't deserve a gift), extending the core unexpectedness/dissatisfaction
sense into ritualized apology language.

### Watashi-ga vs. watashi-wa: topic-marking wa implies an unstated contrast (p.78-79)
A gratuitous *watashi-wa*, used when the topic ("the tickets," "sumo") is already established and
doesn't need re-marking, is actively misleading rather than merely redundant: *wa* inherently signals
a contrast with some unstated alternative, so "Watashi-wa motte-imasu" is heard as "AS FOR ME, I have
it [implying you might not have yours]." *Watashi-ga* instead marks the agent of an action/situation
without that contrastive baggage, correctly answering "who has it" with no implication about anyone
else. The general rule: when a question already and obviously concerns the listener/speaker
specifically, it's best to drop *watashi-wa* from the answer altogether — and separately, *wa* tends
to mark the overall situation/topic while *ga* marks the specific agent within it, with an
already-established *wa*-phrase typically dropped rather than repeated in an answer.

### Ending a sentence on an unfinished -te/-te-ne form (p.80-81)
Extends the set-phrase apology/gratitude pattern already partly documented in vol. 5 — the actual
continuation after the -te form is routinely left unsaid, especially accompanying a bow — to ordinary
conversational sentences more broadly, where ending on *-te* or *-te-ne* (polite register: *-mashite*)
functions as a genuine cooperative-speech device: it invites the listener to respond with *aizuchi*
(short acknowledgment words) and implicitly invites them to help complete the thought, framing the
exchange as jointly constructed rather than one-sided.

### Plain imperative and negative-imperative forms surviving inside otherwise polite reported speech (p.82-83)
While bare plain imperatives (*Mate!*, "wait") and the dictionary-form-plus-*na* negative imperative
(*Matsu-na*, "don't wait") are normally restricted to familiar conversation when used as a direct,
standalone command, both are regularly preserved even inside otherwise polite conversation when
relaying what a third party said or commanded — the quoting frame (*...to osshaimashita*, *...to
iimashita-node*, or contracted *...tte*) supplies the politeness, while the quoted command itself
stays in its original plain form. Separately, posted signs and written instructions actively prefer
the blunt plain negative imperative over politer phrasing (*Wataranaide-kudasai*, *Watatte-wa
ikemasen*) precisely because directness suits that genre — echoing the written-notice *...koto*
convention already documented for vol. 5 (`established/052`).

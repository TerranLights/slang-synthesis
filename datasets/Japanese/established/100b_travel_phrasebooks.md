# Japanese — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Travel Phrasebooks (Lonely Planet)

**Source:** Kevin Chambers & Wesley Palmer, *Lonely Planet Japanese Phrasebook* (Lonely Planet
Language Survival Kit, 1st edition, Lonely Planet Publications, December 1989). Sections sampled:
Pronunciation/Grammar (printed pp. 8–15: Katakana/Hiragana charts, Consonants, Word Order, Verbs,
Tense, Negatives, Adjectives, Questions, Yes & No, Pronouns), Greetings & Civilities (p. 24),
Small Talk opening (p. 25), Food: Mealtimes/Drinks/Fruit (pp. 65–67), Numbers (pp. 108–109, 112),

extraction spec's "representative sampling" guidance for this genre, this file samples across

## Grammar points

### Word Order (p. 11)

Unlike English's subject-verb-object order, Japanese sentences follow subject-object-verb order.
Where English says "Who hit the ball?", Japanese expresses the equivalent of "Who the ball hit?"
In speech, contextually-unnecessary words (often the subject) are freely omitted, with the verb
clause frequently standing as the entire sentence — e.g. *ikimasu* alone can mean "I go/you go/he
goes/we go/they go" depending on context, since Japanese verbs don't inflect for person or number.

### Verb politeness and tense (pp. 11–13)

The `-masu` ending marks polite register and is used throughout ordinary conversation (the source
notes `-masu` forms are used throughout the book itself). Verbs have only present and past tense;
future meaning is carried by present-tense verbs combined with a time adverb (e.g. "next month").
Negation of the polite non-past form is formed by replacing the final `-su` with `-sen` (e.g.
`ikimasu` → `ikimasen`, "doesn't go"). The copula `desu` ("is/am/are") negates irregularly to
`dewa arimasen`, informally contracted to `ja arimasen`.

### Adjectives and their negation (p. 13)

Adjectives (and adverbs) precede the noun they modify, as in English (*yasui hoteru*, "a cheap
hotel"). Adjective negation replaces the final `-i` with `-ku` and adds `arimasen` (*yasui* →
*yasuku arimasen*, "isn't cheap").

### Questions and Yes/No (pp. 14–15)

A sentence-final particle `ka` turns a statement into a yes/no question. Specific question words
(who/which/what/where/when/how/how many/how much/why) each have polite and informal pairs in some
cases (e.g. `donata` vs. `dare` for "who"). A bare *iie* ("no") can read as brusque in Japanese
social context; the source recommends softening it, e.g. *iie, sumimasen* ("I'm sorry, but no").

### Pronouns (p. 15)

Personal pronouns are used far less often than in English and are routinely dropped when
recoverable from context. The textbook-standard first person *watakushi* is formal; *watashi* is
the less formal form actually heard/used more often in practice. There is no dedicated word for
"it", and "he"/"she" (*kare*/*kanojo*) are frequently replaced in practice by phrases meaning "that
person" rather than used directly. Plurality is marked with the suffix `-tachi` (e.g.
*watakushitachi*, "we/us").

### Dual numeral system (pp. 108–109)

Japanese has two cardinal-number systems: a native Japanese set that only goes up to 10 (`hitotsu`,
`futatsu` ... `tō`) and a Chinese-derived set that combines productively for larger numbers (`ichi`,
`ni`, `san` ... `jū`, then `jūichi` "11" = `jū` + `ichi`, etc., up to special words `hyaku` "100",
`sen` "1000", `man` "10,000"; one million = `hyakuman`, literally "hundred ten-thousand"). Chinese
numerals combine with noun "counters"/classifiers; Japanese numerals are used in isolation when the
correct counter for an object isn't known. This matches the Rough Guide's independent description
of the same dual-numeral/classifier system — cross-source confirmation of a genuine structural
feature, not an artifact of either book's presentation.

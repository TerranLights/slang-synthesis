## Grammar/usage notes

### Hiragana, katakana, and kanji ("READING JAPANESE" sidebar, printed p.2-3)
The front matter's sidebar explains Japan's three (or four, counting romaji) writing systems.
Hiragana (46 characters, used for native/basic words) is illustrated with *ossu* (おっす, "what's
up!"), *ampontan* (あんぽんたん, "dorkface"), and *isoginchaku* (いそぎんちゃく, "sea anemone").
Katakana (46 characters, used for loanwords/foreign names/newly coined words) is illustrated with
*baiagura* (バイアグラ, "Viagra"), *tomu kurūzu* (トム・クルーズ, "Tom Cruise"), and *rāmen*
(ラーメン, "ramen"). Kanji (thousands of ideograms imported from China from the 5th century on,
~1945 needed for literacy) is illustrated with three example compounds: *joshi kōsei* (女子高生,
"schoolgirl"), *bijin hakumei* (美人薄命, "beautiful women have unfortunate fates" — a
proverb-style compound), and *kenka jōtō* (喧嘩上等, "tough motherfucker"). Romaji (the Roman
alphabet) is noted as spreading into Japanese for abbreviations and tech terms.

### Pronunciation guide ("PRONOUNCING JAPANESE" sidebar, printed p.4)
Japanese has five vowels (a/i/u/e/o), each with one fixed pronunciation value; a macron (e.g. ō)
lengthens a vowel without shifting stress/emphasis. Consonants are mostly as in English, with
*ts* (light tongue-click, less pronounced than "itsy-bitsy"), *n* (drawn out longer than English
n), and *r* (lightly rolled, as in Spanish) singled out as the sounds Americans struggle with
most. Critically, every syllable gets equal emphasis — "ka-wa-sa-ki," not "KA-wa-SA-ki."

### Register sensitivity is pervasive, not optional
A running theme across the whole chapter: Japanese slang is tightly gated by social relationship.
The book repeatedly stresses that these forms are for use among friends/peers, not strangers or
elders — using them with the wrong audience will look, in the author's words, "as if you'd just
peed in their kitchen sink." The "Nice to meet you" section (printed p.18-19) switches entirely out
of slang into formal/polite-register Japanese specifically because you don't yet know how intimate
you can be with someone you've just met — illustrating that *usage tier* in this language is
driven primarily by interlocutor relationship rather than topic or setting.

### Pronoun system as a register/identity marker
The "Myself" section (printed p.10-11) presents Japanese first-person pronouns (watashi, boku,
ore, ora, oira, atashi, atai, asshi, jibun) as a rich paradigmatic choice encoding gender, age,
social class/register (redneck, dopey, Valley Girl, rich girl, geezer), and persona — a single
semantic slot ("I") realized through many socially-coded surface forms. The book notes that girls
can use masculine pronouns (ore, boku) without any particular connotation, but a man using a
feminine pronoun will likely read as strongly effeminate/gay-coded — a real sociolinguistic
asymmetry worth preserving for the analysis layer.

### Introducing Yourself sidebar ("JIKO SHOKAI," printed p.16)
A sidebar presents two parallel example self-introduction monologues (one framed male/"Kenneth"
from Canada, one framed female/"Rebecca" from England), each stringing together comedic sentences
("I only have three months to live," "no man can handle me") rather than discrete vocabulary
headwords. Presented as illustrative dialogue, not tabulated as individual vocabulary entries per

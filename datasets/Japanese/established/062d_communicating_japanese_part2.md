## Grammar points

### 26.1 The verb: full inflection-suffix summary, polite imperative

A **full summary table of verb suffixes** is given across three verb classes (Consonant-Verb,
Vowel-Verb, Irregular) and both Plain/Polite registers, covering Final Forms (Indicative Habitual/
Present/Future, Past, Negative Indicative, Negative Past, Desiderative, Presumptive Hortative,
Presumptive Probability) and Non-Final Forms (Infinitive, Affirmative Gerund, Negative Gerund,
Affirmative Provisional, Negative Provisional). This is a consolidated cross-reference of every
paradigm introduced piecemeal in Lessons 21–25 (plus the new Provisional, -eba/-reba/-sureba,
anticipating Lesson 27). The **Polite Imperative** is formed by adding the free morpheme **nasai**
"do" after a verb's Infinitive form (ikinasái "Go!," tabenasái "Eat!"); it is explicitly offered as
a politer substitute for the bare -te kudasái request pattern in many contexts (**Tábete kudasái**
"please eat" ↔ **Tabenasái** "eat!"). The Plain Imperative is deliberately withheld until a later
lesson, since the source notes it "requires much experience and understanding not only of the
language but also of the social structure to be able to use ... correctly."

### 27.1–27.2 Adjective phrases and comparisons

Three adjective-phrase types: **Modified Adjective Phrase (A)** (adverb + adjective: totemo íi "very
good"); **Modified Adjective Phrase (B)** (a noun phrase of "extent," headed by the pseudo-noun
**hodo**, preceding the adjective: yakedo suru hodo atsúi "hot enough to burn [oneself]"); and
**Coordinate Adjective Phrase** (two adjectives joined either via the first's Gerund -te form,
oishíkute yasúi, or via its plain Indicative form, oishii yasúi — only the -te form can serve as a
full predicate; *oishii yasúi desu is explicitly marked ungrammatical as a predicate). Comparative
constructions are built on **hodo**: the "the more X, the more Y" pattern uses a Provisional-
inflected first clause (-kereba for adjectives, -eba/-reba for verbs, naraba for the copula) +
hodo + the resolution, e.g. **Chiisákereba chiisái hodo íi desu** "The smaller it is the better,"
**Míreba míru hodo sukí ni narimásu** "The more I see it, the more I grow to like it." Comparative
*noun* phrases instead use the pseudo-nouns **hō** "the alternative of" and the particle **yori**
"more than," plus hodo again for extent/degree comparisons — the source explicitly groups hō/yori/
hodo together as a class and labels them Pseudo-Nouns (cross-referencing Lesson 25 §25.2).

### 28.1 Condition-resolution phrases: permission, prohibition, concession, obligation

Four symmetric patterns, each built on a Verb/Adjective/Noun stem in a fixed inflected form plus a
fixed resolution element, are tabulated in full:
- **Permission** ("may"): Verb+-te mo / Adj.+-kute mo / Noun+de mo + íi(desu) — e.g. Arúite mo íi
  desu "You may walk."
- **Prohibition** ("must not"): Verb+-te wa / Adj.+-kute wa / Noun+de wa + ikemaseñ/damé desu/
  narimaseñ (given in that order of frequency) — e.g. Arúite wa ikemaseñ "You must not walk."
- **Concession** ("O.K. not to"): C-Vb+-anakute mo / V-Vb+-nakute mo / Adj.+-ku nákute mo / Noun+de
  nákute mo + íi(desu) — e.g. Arukánakute mo íi desu "You don't have to walk."
- **Obligation** ("must"): the negative Provisional (e.g. Arukánakereba narimaseñ "You must walk")
  — described as a *double negative* (condition "if X doesn't happen" + negative resolution
  "won't do"), which English resolves into a single affirmative "must."

The particles **mo** and **wa** are themselves fixed parts of these patterns rather than carrying
independent meaning in context: **mo** marks the resolved-affirmatively ("is good") branch, **wa**
marks both negative-resolution branches — both particles retain their general topic/focus-marking
identity (mo as a focus particle, wa as subject/topic particle of the negative clause) even in this
fully grammaticalized use.

### 29.1–29.2 Conditional sentences (-tara); indefinite nouns and adverbs

The **-tara conditional suffix** ("if/when") attaches to all three predicate types in both
affirmative and negative — full paradigm given: C-Verb ittára/ikanákattara, V-Verb tabetára/
tabenákattara, Irregular shitára/shinákattara, Adjective samukáttara/samuku nakáttara, Copula
dattára/de(ja) nakáttara. Conditional sentences are structurally complex sentences (Condition clause
+ Resolution clause), and any combination of predicate types may occur across the two clauses. This
is presented as one of several Japanese "condition" strategies alongside the -te mo/-nakute mo
(Permission/Concession, Lesson 28) and negative-Provisional (Obligation, Lesson 28) patterns, with
further patterns deferred to Lesson 38. Separately, the sentence-final interrogative particle **ka**
becomes an **indefiniteness** marker when it follows an interrogative word (nani/dare/dore, etc.)
instead of ending a question: nani ka "something," dare ka "someone," dore ka "whichever." When such
ka-forms are further compounded with the emphatic particles **mo** or **demo**, they form fully
inclusive/exclusive indefinites — forms with mo are typically negative-context ("nothing," "no one"),
forms with demo are typically affirmative/inclusive ("anything," "anyone") — though several forms
(itsumo "always," ikutsu mo "many," ikura mo "much") carry an intrinsically positive meaning in
either polarity context.

### 30.1 Potential, passive, causative, causative-passive transformations

Potential, Passive, and Causative suffixes are **derivative** (they produce a new verb stem) rather
than purely inflectional (which only complete an existing form). **Potential**: suru has no regular
potential suffix — the suppletive verb **dekíru** "is able" substitutes (Uñteñ-dekimásu ka? "Can you
drive?"); several verbs (wakáru "understands," míeru "is seen/can be seen," kikoeru "is heard/can be
heard") are lexically already potential in meaning and take no further potential inflection.
**Passive** (suffix -are-/C-Verb, -rare-/V-Verb — identical in form to the Vowel-Verb Potential):
unlike English, Japanese passive applies to both transitive *and intransitive* verbs, and
characteristically carries a nuance of **adversative affect** — "I was died by my mother" is good
Japanese for "Mother died, and I am adversely affected by it" (intransitive passive, no true English
equivalent). A further structural restriction: specific inanimate *objects* (as opposed to persons)
are not normally made grammatical subjects of a passive clause in Japanese, even where the
equivalent English passive ("My mistake was corrected by the teacher") is natural. **Causative**
(suffix -ase-/C-Verb, -sase-/V-Verb): covers three distinct English glosses — "have someone do X,"
"make/cause someone to do X," and "let/permit someone to do X" — disambiguated by context; the
Causative Object (originally the subject of the un-causativized kernel clause) is marked with **o**
for intransitive-verb clauses but **ni** for transitive-verb clauses, to avoid two o-marked elements
in a single clause. **Causative-Passive**: formed by applying the Passive suffix on top of an
already-causativized stem (Verb stem + -(s)ase- + -rare- + Inflectional Suffix, e.g. hatarak- +
-ase- + -rare- + -ta → hatarakaseráreta "was caused/made to work") — a fully worked example sentence
and clause-slot diagram (Topic/Agent/Caus.-Pass. Predicate, and Topic/Agent/Object/Caus.-Pass.
Predicate for the transitive case) is given for both the intransitive and transitive verb cases.

## Grammar points

### Yatsu: "fellow" vs. "stuff," and its demonstrative-stem forms (p.10-11)
*Yatsu* has two senses, both restricted to familiar conversation. Referring to a person, it's used
largely by men and carries whatever emotional coloring — contempt or affection — context supplies.
Referring to a thing (cars, machines, etc.), it behaves more like the pronoun *no* ("one") and is
used by women too, as in the source's framing example. Combined with the demonstrative stems
*ko-/so-/a-/do-*, *yatsu* contracts to *-itsu*: *koitsu*, *soitsu*, *aitsu*, *doitsu*.

### Nan-to-naku vs. nantoka: two "somehow"s that aren't interchangeable (p.12-13)
*Nan-to-naku* ("for no particular reason, somehow") typically precedes an expression of feeling or
supposition — it doesn't by itself convey managing something difficult. *Nantoka* ("by some means or
other") is the correct choice for describing effort or success achieved despite difficulty — finishing
a task, making a deadline, getting by. The source frames a learner's misuse of *nan-to-naku* where
*nantoka* was needed as the lesson's whole point.

### Three "thank you for service" set phrases and a declining fourth (p.14-15)
*Gochisoosama(-deshita)* is said leaving a restaurant; *osewasama(-deshita)* leaving a hotel/inn
after receiving special or familylike care; *gokuroosama* toward a delivery person or errand-runner,
though its use is declining because it's increasingly felt to imply a master-servant relationship —
*arigatoo*/*osewasama* are now preferred in its place. Bare *arigatoo* can substitute for any of the
three, but only where politeness isn't required; *arigatoo-gozaimasu* is the appropriate register for
an acquaintance or stranger.

### Addressing someone by name: a pragmatics mismatch with English (p.16-17)
Japanese uses a person's name/title specifically to get their attention, not as a general politeness
or warmth marker — unlike English, where adding a name to a statement ("I understand, Mr. Kato")
reads as more polite or intimate. Carrying that English habit into Japanese produces sentences that
sound merely odd, not more polite. A single teacher in a classroom is addressed as plain *sensee*
without a name, since there's no ambiguity to resolve — foreign learners' habit of adding the
teacher's name anyway is flagged as a recognizable non-native pattern.

### Three registers for admiring something (p.18-19)
*Kiree* is the general-purpose, gender-neutral word for "beautiful," suitable for ordinary
conversation. *Utsukushii* is more literary/formal, fitting writing or formal speech better than
casual talk. *Subarashii* ("wonderful, marvelous") covers both concrete and abstract admiration and
carries no gender restriction. *Suteki*, by contrast, is markedly gendered — used mainly by women
(especially young women) for warm, emphatic admiration, with male use confined to talking to women
about feminine-coded things or to sales contexts.

### Postposed chotto/chikaku and the restricted range of hotondo (p.20-21)
Placed directly after a number, *chotto* means "a little more than" that figure, while *chikaku*
means "nearly / a little less than" it — a tight minimal-pair contrast. *Hotondo* ("almost, nearly")
sounds awkward modifying a bare figure directly (*hotondo ichiman-en* is flagged as unnatural);
instead it collocates with duration/totality expressions like *ichinichi-juu* ("all day long").

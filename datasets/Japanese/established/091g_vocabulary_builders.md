**Source:** Charles De Wolf, *How to Sound Intelligent in Japanese: A Vocabulary Builder* (Kodansha
International, 1st ed., 1993), Chapter 7 "Business and Economics," printed pp. 107–125 (this is the
final chapter of the book — there is no Chapter 8; the book's own Index begins at printed p. 126 and
was **not** extracted as vocabulary here, since it is a derivative page-locator index of words already
covered in-chapter, not new source content).

**Coverage note:** This source has no text layer (`pdftotext` returns nothing on content pages;
confirmed scanned Adobe-Acrobat-Image-Conversion PDF) and was vision-read from rendered page images
at 130 dpi. **Gotcha confirmed (same as prior chapters of this book):** the PDF is scanned
two-printed-pages-per-image (a true spread per PDF page) — `printed_page_left = 2×pdf_page − 2`,
`printed_page_right = 2×pdf_page − 1`. This chapter's actual start was located by direct inspection
(the chapter-title page) at PDF page 54 (right side) = printed p. 107, slightly earlier than the
dispatch's initial arithmetic estimate of ~p. 109 — confirming the "verify empirically, don't trust
arithmetic alone" rule. The chapter's end was located the same way: the last page of prose (PDF page
63, right side = printed p. 125) is immediately followed by the book's "INDEX" heading on PDF page 64
(printed pp. 126–127). **No handwritten marginalia was found anywhere in this chapter** — all content
inspected is clean typeset print (vision-reading guard checked explicitly on every rendered page).

**Sampling disclosure:** This chapter has substantially more numbered example sentences (56 total)
than Chapter 1's 32, consistent with being the book's longest/densest chapter (built around nine
distinct roots rather than three). Per the coverage rule's "sample representatively if large"
allowance, **every distinct vocabulary item/compound encountered was extracted** (this chapter's
vocabulary boxes and prose were read in full, page by page, not sampled), but the 56 numbered example
sentences were **not** individually reproduced or even all summarized — only a small number of
genuinely illustrative ones are referenced in the Notes column or quoted briefly, and new vocabulary
introduced only inside an example sentence (rather than in a vocabulary list) is still captured as a
row, just without quoting the full sentence.

This chapter is organized around nine Sino-Japanese compound-forming roots (財 *zai/sai* "wealth,
money, assets"; 産 *san* "product, birth, production"; 業 *gyō/waza* "business, trade, deed/karma";
商 *shō/akinau* "commerce, trade"; 経済 *keizai* "economy"; 金 *kin* "gold, money"; 給 *kyū/tamau*
"supply, pay"; 税 *zei* "tax"; 株 *kabu* "stock, share") plus a non-root-organized section on everyday
banking terminology (pp. 122–124) bridging the 税 and 株 sections.

> "govern the world [and] relieve the people." The two-character modern word retains only the first
> and fourth characters of the four-character classical phrase, with 済 shifting its reading from
> *sai* to *zai* under rendaku-like voicing in the resulting compound (p.115–116).

> **産官複合体** (sankan-fukugō-tai) = 産 (san, "industry/production") + 官 (kan, "government
> official/bureaucracy") + 複合体 (fukugō-tai, "composite/complex body" ← 複合 fukugō "compound,
> composite" + 体 tai "body") — "government-industrial complex," directly modeled on and introduced
> alongside 産軍複合体 (sangun-fukugō-tai) "military-industrial complex," substituting 官 for 軍
> (gun, "military") in the same frame (p.109).

> **源泉徴収税率** (gensen-chōshū-zeiritsu) = 源泉 (gensen, "source/wellspring" ← 源 gen "source" +
> 泉 sen "spring") + 徴収 (chōshū, "levy, collect" ← 徴 chō "levy/summon" + 収 shū "collect") + 税率
> (zeiritsu, "tax rate" ← 税 zei "tax" + 率 ritsu "rate") — "rate of tax withheld at the source,"
> a five-morpheme compound built by concatenating three independently meaningful two-character units
> (p.121, 123).

## Grammar points

### Nine compound-forming roots organize the chapter

Unlike Chapter 1's three-root structure, Chapter 7 is built around nine Sino-Japanese roots, each
given its own prose introduction and vocabulary list: 財 *zai* (wealth/assets, pp.107–109), 産 *san*
(production/products, pp.109–110), 業 *gyō* (business/industry, pp.111–113), 商 *shō* (commerce,
pp.113–115), 経済 *keizai* (economy, a compound itself rather than a bound single-character root,
pp.115–117), 金 *kin* (money/gold, p.117), 給 *kyū* (pay/supply, pp.119–120), 税 *zei* (tax,
pp.120–122), and 株 *kabu* (stock/shares, pp.124–125), with a non-root-organized "banking basics"
section (pp.122–124) bridging 税 and 株. The author's organizing principle throughout is the same as
Chapter 1's: each root recombines productively with many second elements, and knowing the root's core
sense predicts much (but not all) of a compound's meaning.

### Dual readings and semantic bifurcation: 財 (zai/sai) and 業 (gyō/waza/gō)

財 has two on-readings, *zai* (by far the more common, used in nearly every compound in this
chapter) and *sai* (surviving in essentially one word, 財布 *saifu* "wallet") — the author explicitly
flags this as the one major exception to an otherwise near-total *zai* default (p.108). 業 shows a
richer three-way split: the native (kun) reading *waza* ("act, deed, trick"); the common Sino-Japanese
(kan-on) reading *gyō* (business/industry sense, as in 商業 *shōgyō*); and an older Sino-Japanese
(go-on) reading *gō*, carrying the Buddhist sense "karma" (as in 宿業 *shukugō*, one's fate shaped by
past-life karma) — a minimal pair with 宿屋業 *yadoya-gyō* "the hotel business" that the author uses
to show how the same character's two Sino-Japanese readings diverge into entirely separate semantic
domains (pp.112).

### Semantic field distinctions: 財 vs. 産 vs. 資産/動産/不動産

財 centers on wealth/assets as an abstract possession (財産 "property," 財閥 "financial combine");
産 centers on production/output and, by extension, what is produced or born (生産 "production," 産物
"product," 国産 "made domestically") — the author traces 産 to a "life/birth" radical, noting it is
no coincidence the same root covers both "giving birth" and "producing goods." The two roots meet in
compounds like 資産 (shisan, "assets") and the 動産/不動産 (dōsan/fudōsan, "movable/immovable
property") pair, where 産 denotes the asset itself rather than its production (p.109).

### Native verb alternatives to Sino-Japanese +する compounds

Several sections of this chapter pair a Sino-Japanese noun-verb compound with a native (yamato
kotoba) verb covering similar ground: 商 *shō* (commerce) alongside native 商う *akinau* "to trade,
deal in" (p.115); 給 *kyū* (pay/grant) alongside native 報いる *mukuiru* "to reward" and 稼ぐ *kasegu*
"to earn" (p.120). The author treats this as a recurring pattern in the book: a bound Sino-Japanese
root productive in compounds often has a native-Japanese verb counterpart that is not part of the
compound-formation system at all, and the two should not be confused as the same word in two scripts.

### Taxation terminology as a geographic/administrative-tier ladder

The 税 (zei) section (pp.121–122) presents Japan's tax types as a nested administrative hierarchy —
国税 (kokuzei, national) vs. 地方税 (chihō-zei, local), with 地方税 itself subdividing into 都民税
(Tokyo metropolitan), 区民税 (ward), 市民税 (city) — alongside a parallel ladder by tax *type* rather
than jurisdiction (所得税 income, 消費税 consumer, 法人税 corporate, 相続税 inheritance, 固定資産税
real estate). This is the clearest explicit administrative/geographic layering found anywhere in this
book's chapters to date.

### 的 (-teki) and compound-internal order sensitivity recur from Chapter 1

As in Chapter 1's 論理的/理論的 (ronri-teki/riron-teki) minimal pair, this chapter's root compounds
remain order-sensitive and not freely recombinable: e.g., 企業家 (kigyō-ka, "captain of
industry/entrepreneur") and 事業家 (jigyō-ka, "man of business") are near-synonyms built from
different roots (企業 "enterprise" vs. 事業 "business/undertaking") rather than interchangeable
affixations of the same stem — the chapter's closing line deliberately uses 事業家, not 企業家, for
its Shakespeare-quoting grandfather (p.113, 125).

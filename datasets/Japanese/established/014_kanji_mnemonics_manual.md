## Grammar points

### The book's organizing principle: mnemonic affinity, not grade level

Unlike Henshall's *A Guide to Remembering Japanese Characters* (already extracted in
`003_guide_to_remembering_japanese_characters.md`), which sequences all 1,945 Jōyō kanji by Japanese
school grade, Bodnaryk's *Kanji Mnemonics* sequences kanji by **structural/phonetic affinity** across
five sections, each (after Section II) subdivided into three difficulty Levels:

1. **II. Pictures and Symbols** — 98 "basic" kanji, many directly derived from pictographs, taught
   first because they recur as components throughout the rest of the book.
2. **III. Sound Groups** — kanji sharing both a common structural element **and** the same ON
   (Chinese-derived) reading, e.g. the SHI group (士仕志誌) or the KOO group (巧攻功項貢).
3. **IV. Mixed Sound and Structure Groups** — kanji sharing a structural element but with ON
   readings that are **not all the same** (e.g. the 東-component cluster read TOO/REN/RAN/CHIN). The
   book explicitly recommends memorizing these as "phrases in a musical scale (do rei mi)" (e.g. "SHI
   SHI RI" or "IN IN ON") paired with their meanings, and stresses learning a disambiguating compound
   word (e.g. ONJIN 恩人 "benefactor") to avoid confusing near-homophone entries like 因/姻 (IN) vs.
   恩 (ON).
4. **V. Structure Groups** — kanji sharing only a structural element, with **no** common ON reading
   at all (e.g. 林 RIN vs. 森 SHIN, both built from 木 "tree"). The book is candid that, for this
   group, "you just have to learn them" — mnemonic structure alone cannot carry the sound.
5. **VI. Appendix** — notation key, a short annotated reference-material/bibliography section citing
   other kanji textbooks (e.g. Foerster & Tamura's *KANJI ABC*, organized by 26 "grapheme" groups),
   and two indices (ON readings; kun readings) mapping readings back to entry numbers.

### Notation system (Appendix I, "Abbreviations and Conventions" — transcribed in full)

| Abbreviation | Meaning |

| CO | Character in use in China only |
| E | Element — a substructure of a kanji that does not stand alone |
| JY | JOYO (常用) — general-use kanji, 1,945 total, per Ministry of Education |
| K | Kanji |
| KR | Kanji and Radical — a kanji that can also serve as a radical in a more complex kanji |

| OO | the long O (sounds like O but twice as long; written as O with a macron in many texts) |

| R | Radical — a substructure of a kanji that does not stand alone |
| UU | the long U (sounds like U but twice as long) |
| V | Variant of |
| v.s. | *vide supra* ("see above") |

Entries are numbered sequentially (1 through roughly 2080+) across the whole book, and every
complex kanji's entry cross-references its component radicals/elements/kanji by entry number in
square brackets (e.g. `[士 samurai 159KR]`), creating a fully cross-indexed dependency graph — later
entries lean on earlier ones, consistent with the book's stated "cumulative" design philosophy.

### Historical/etymological framework (Introduction, paraphrased)

The book opens with a short history of how kanji reached Japan: borrowed from China starting
roughly the 4th century AD (peaking in the 6th), first used purely for their *sound* (ignoring
Chinese meaning) to spell Japanese words, then later purely for their *meaning* (given a Japanese
pronunciation) — which the book states is why roughly two-thirds of Jōyō kanji have both an ON
(Chinese-derived) and kun (Japanese) reading today, while hiragana and katakana were developed
alongside as supplementary syllabaries. It identifies five historical processes that degraded
kanji from legible pictographs into today's abstract squared forms: **drift** (gradual natural
change), **copying error** (miscopied by hand before printing existed), **assignment of new
("borrowed") meanings to old characters** (a character becomes a "total nonsense symbol" once its
meaning no longer matches its original picture), **standardization** (forcing all characters into
uniform squares by ~200 AD, which truncated stroke counts and literally squared round pictographs
like the sun ☉ → 日), and **simplification** (post-WWII reforms capped Japan's taught kanji at 1,850,
later raised to 1,945 — the Jōyō list — while mainland China's parallel simplification, e.g.
horse 馬 → 马, is explicitly contrasted as making characters *harder*, not easier, to learn
mnemonically, despite reducing stroke count).

### Stroke order rules (book pp. i-10/i-11, paraphrased with the book's own character examples)

The book states stroke order is prescribed by Japan's Ministry of Education and presents four core
rules with worked diagrammed examples: (1) top to bottom (三, 工); (2) left to right (川, 例); (3)
middle stroke before side-strokes (小, 水, 米), with an explicit exception carved out for characters
containing the 忄 ("vertical heart") radical and 火 ("fire"), which do *not* follow this rule (忙,
快, 火 are shown with their own stroke-order diagrams); (4) horizontal stroke before intersecting
vertical strokes (十, 七, 土).

### Pedagogical method: "mnemonic strings" and kanji cards

Beyond pictographic/ideographic etymology (used only "when it serves as a direct memory aid" — the
book is explicit that an obscure, non-mnemonic etymology is deliberately omitted rather than
included for its own sake), the book's central device is the **mnemonic string**: a short sentence
weaving together the glosses of a complex kanji's component radicals/kanji to "synthesize" the
complex kanji's own meaning, regardless of whether the resulting sentence is semantically sensible
(its own worked example: *"Money and talent bring wealth"* makes sense; *"Words at noon are
permitted"* does not, yet both are claimed to be equally memorable as long as the string is written
down and reused consistently). It also recommends **natural groupings** (this book's whole Sections
III-V), learning **compound words** alongside each kanji for synergistic reinforcement, drilling
**correct stroke order** by repetition until it becomes neurally automatic, and physical **kanji
flashcards** (3×5 index cards, kanji on one side, ON/kun/meaning on the other, drilled from both
directions, scaled from small 10-card packs up to rotating sets of 100-card packs reviewed roughly
twice a year) as "the best mnemonic technique of all is repetition." The Introduction's own estimate:
roughly 400 kanji (≈20% of the 1,945 total) covers enough for basic literacy via the 20/80
principle, but all 1,945 are needed for true newspaper literacy, and the book's own pacing
suggestion is one page a day, 4-5 days a week, over roughly two years, with mandatory periodic
review because "the organization of Kanji Mnemonics is cumulative."

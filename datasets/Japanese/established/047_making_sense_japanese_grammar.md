## Grammar points

### Part 1 — Grammatical categories and the basic architecture of a sentence

**Units 1–2 (subject, direct object, word order).** The subject is the entity an event
evolves around (agent of a process, or the "aboutness" item of a condition); the direct
object is the next-most-central party. English is SVO; Japanese is SOV but really just
"verb-final" (right-headed) — subject and object can swap order (SOV or OSV) without
changing meaning, as long as the verb stays last.

**Unit 3 (optional subject).** Japanese sentences freely omit an explicit subject; unlike
Spanish, the verb carries no person/number agreement to help recover it, so *tabemasu*
alone can mean "I/we/you/he/she/they/it will eat" — recoverable only from context.

**Unit 4 (final-part attention).** Because tense and negation both surface at the very end
of a Japanese sentence, listeners must withhold judgment until the last element; Japanese
speakers accordingly truncate *answers* from the back rather than (as English does) keep
only the front, because the information-bearing tail cannot be dropped.

**Unit 5 (three verb-like constituent types).** What counts as "the verb at the end" is
actually one of three types — true verb, adjective+copula, or noun+copula — each with
four conjugated forms (non-past/past × affirmative/negative).

**Unit 6 (noun-desu is not the subject).** In a bare sentence like *gakusei-desu* ("[he]
is a student"), the noun is part of the predicate, not the subject — the subject is
dropped and recovered from context; *tabibito-wa shijin-desu* ("the traveler is a poet")
requires the explicit topic-marked version if "traveler" is meant as the subject.

**Unit 7 (avoided pronouns).** See *anata*/*omae*/*sensei* vocabulary rows above — Japanese
never developed obligatory 2nd-person pronoun use the way Indo-European languages did,
and euphemistic locational-noun-derived pronouns became too blunt once conventionalized.

**Units 8–10 (part-of-speech mismatches; open noun class).** English-adjective-shaped
Japanese concepts are frequently na-nouns (conjugate as nouns), and "noun" is Japanese's
open word class — borrowed English adjectives like *hansamu* "handsome" and *kuuru*
"cool" are absorbed as nouns, while verbs and adjectives are closed, conservative classes
resistant to borrowing.

**Unit 9 (adjective phonological signature).** Every Japanese dictionary-form adjective
ends in *-ii/-ai/-oi/-ui* (the final vowel being the present-tense marker) — but the
converse isn't reliable, since some non-adjectives (*hantai* "opposite") happen to end the
same way.

**Unit 11–12 (questions, verb repetition).** Yes/no questions are formed by appending
`-ka` to an unchanged declarative word order; WH-phrases stay in place rather than
fronting. Because Japanese auxiliary verbs are bound forms (unlike English "do/can/must"),
answers and subordinate/main-clause pairs routinely repeat the identical verb rather than
substituting a pro-form.

**Units 13–14 (particles as postpositions; particle classes).** Particles mark the
function of what precedes them (postpositional, mirroring English's prepositional
direction) and bridge between elements rather than attaching to a single word in
isolation. The book's own rough taxonomy: sentence-function particles (`-ka`, `-yo`,
`-ne`), clause-subordinating particles (`-kara`, `-ga`, `-kedo`), topic particles (`-wa`,
`-mo`), noun-to-verb phrase particles (`-ga`, `-o`, `-ni`, `-e`, `-de`, `-kara`, `-made`,
`-made-ni`, `-to`), and noun-to-noun particles (`-no`, `-na`, `-to`). Subordinate clauses
in Japanese can only precede (never follow) their main clause — the reverse of English's
two acceptable orders — because Japanese, being strictly postpositional, only has one
word order that satisfies both the background-before-foreground preference and the
linking-element-appears-between preference simultaneously.

### Part 2 — Phrase particles

**Unit 15.** Phrase particles (not word order) mark a noun phrase's grammatical function in
Japanese, so word order can vary freely for emphasis without changing core meaning — the
opposite of English, where word order alone disambiguates subject from object.

**Units 16, 18, 20–21 — the `-wa` system (see vocabulary row above for the core claim).**
Beyond the core "topic, urges attention to what follows" function, Unit 20 generalizes the
`-ga`/`-wa` contrast to a discourse-level pattern: a first mention of a new story
participant takes `-ga` (fresh participant), subsequent mentions take `-wa` (participant
now anchored in context) — paralleled with a newsworthy-rocket-settling-into-stable-orbit
analogy. Items that are inherently "already in orbit" without ever being newsworthy (general
truths like *Nihon-wa shimaguni-desu* "Japan is an island nation," or the speaker
him/herself) are `-wa`-marked by default. Crucially (Unit 21), `-wa` cannot attach directly
to a WH-phrase itself, because WH-phrases denote unknown information and `-wa` presupposes
an already-anchored referent.

**Unit 17 — `-mo` (see vocabulary row).**

**Units 19, 26–27, 32, 60 — transitivity, `-ga`/`-o`, and the transitivity scale.** Unit 19
establishes the baseline: `-ga` = subject, `-o` = direct object, with transitive verbs
(two-participant, agent changes a target) vs. intransitive (one-participant). Unit 26's
central finding: Japanese verbs that *look* transitive in English translation (*wakaru* "to
understand," *iru* "to need," *dekiru* "can do") are classified intransitive in Japanese
because transitivity is a continuous scale and each language draws its transitive/
intransitive cutoff at a different point — English draws the line lower on the scale than
Japanese does, making "want/need/understand"-type low-transitivity events transitive in
English but intransitive in Japanese (hence *pasokon-ga irimasu* "I need a computer," with
the needed object as grammatical subject, not object). Unit 60 extends this to a general
"reality is a continuous scale; language carves it into discrete categories" principle,
applied also to the noun-abstractness continuum (Unit 40) and the `-ni`/`-de` placement/
domain contrast, both of which have fluid borderline cases (e.g. *tomeru* "stop" takes
either particle with a meaning shift). Units 27 and 32 show that the double-o constraint
forces structural reanalysis (an apparent direct object gets reanalyzed as the subject of
an embedded intransitive predicate, as in *sushi-ga tabetai* "[sushi] is wanted-to-be-eaten
[by me]," a stronger-desire reading than *sushi-o tabetai*).

**Units 22, 32 — the double-o constraint (see vocabulary row for verbal-noun example).**
Only one `-o`-marked direct object may co-occur with a single verb; multiple `-o`s in one
sentence signal either ellipsis of a repeated identical verb or a genuinely multi-clause
sentence with more than one verb.

**Unit 23 — particle-dropping asymmetry.** `-ga`/`-o` (and some uses of `-ni`) can be
dropped in casual speech because their function is recoverable from being the two
"default" grammatical categories every language privileges; `-kara`/`-made` cannot be
dropped because their meaning is not otherwise recoverable. Subject/object particles
cannot combine with `-wa`/`-mo` (they're simply replaced by them), whereas other phrase
particles (`-kara`, `-made`, `-e`) can combine with `-wa`/`-mo` or `-no`.

**Unit 24 — `-de` vs. `-ni` (see vocabulary row).** Additional detail: *arimasu* "to exist"
takes `-de` rather than `-ni` when the subject is itself an event (a party, a class, an
Olympics) because such a subject "takes place" within a domain rather than occupying a
point.

**Unit 25 — three reasons phrase particles are dropped/blocked.** (1) Clear-from-context
functions (subject/object/some `-ni` uses) can drop their particle; (2) quantity
expressions (*nimai kudasai* "give me two sheets") never take a particle because they
don't refer to a specific item — unless modifying a specific referent, in which case the
particle reappears; (3) time expressions whose reference point is the speaker's own
present ("now," "yesterday," "this week") are incompatible with `-ni` the same way English
blocks "at/on/in" before "tomorrow/yesterday."

**Unit 28–31 — `-ni` as an active-participant marker vs. `-o`/`-kara` as non-participant
(see vocabulary rows for `-ni`/`-to`, and `-ageru`/`-kureru`/`-morau`).** The book's
unifying claim across these four units: `-ni` marks a person as an equal-status, actively
engaged interactional partner, while `-o` reduces the marked person to a non-interacting,
subordinate, or socially-lower target (e.g. *tomodachi-ni atta* "met [as equals] with a
friend" vs. *tomodachi-o mita* "merely saw a friend," no greeting implied); `-kara` is used
instead of `-ni` when the source is not a cooperative social partner at all (a pickpocket's
victim, a non-person source like a company). This same participant-status logic explains
which auxiliary verbs (causative, affective passive, `-morau`, `-hoshii`) require `-ni` for
their non-subject participant, and why `-ageru`/`-kureru` (whose own subject already *is*
the benefactor) do not.

**Unit 33 — particles compensate for verb-final vagueness.** Because the verb comes last,
phrase particles let a listener predict what kind of verb is coming (`-e` predicts
directional-movement verbs like "go/come"; `-ni` predicts placement verbs like "exist/
arrive") — which is also why verbs, and even most of a sentence, can often be elided in
speech once the particle alone makes the intended verb obvious (e.g. an all-purpose
*onegai-shimasu* "I request" substituting for many distinct specific verbs depending on
which particle precedes it).

### Part 3 — Expanding noun phrases

**Units 34, 36–38 — `-no` modification and right-headedness (see vocabulary row for `-no`).**
Noun phrases can be stacked indefinitely via `-no` (each left element modifies the next),
and the rightmost noun is always the head — the opposite of English's general
left-headedness, which the book frames as a broader consequence of Japanese being
postpositional vs. English prepositional (Unit 2's right-headedness principle extended to
noun phrases). `-no` ranges over many relationships beyond ownership (possession, origin,
target-of-study, feature-attribution), producing genuine structural ambiguities like
*Mekishiko-jin-no tomodachi* (Units 38 and 44 trace this specific ambiguity to two
different underlying structures: plain [noun-no noun] vs. a reduced [direct-style-sentence
+ noun] complex noun phrase whose would-be *-da* predicate is deleted because `-da` is
banned outside sentence-final position, surfacing instead as `-no`).

**Unit 35 — adjective-modified nouns.** Function exactly like noun-modified nouns for
later embedding, but never take `-no` between the adjective and the noun (`-no` is "glue"
for noun+noun; adjectives are "self-adhesive" and need none).

**Unit 37 — spatial expressions as stacked nouns.** Japanese spatial description "zooms in"
from a large/close landmark toward the target (the reverse of English's "zoom out"/pan-away
pattern), so nested location phrases are built by starting from the most salient reference
point closest to the speaker and narrowing inward.

**Unit 39 — `-to` connects only nominal phrases (see vocabulary row).**

**Unit 40 — na-nouns and the concreteness scale (see vocabulary row for `-na`).**

**Units 41–46 — complex noun phrases (relativization).** A complex noun phrase is built by
extracting an argument from an event and describing it with the remainder of the event as
a direct-style-verb modifier preceding the head noun — the mirror image of English
relativization, since Japanese moves the extracted item to the *end* rather than
inserting a WH-word/"that" connector (Unit 43: Japanese has no relative-pronoun-equivalent
mechanism at all, so semantic relationships between modifier and head must be inferred,
sometimes ambiguously, e.g. *michi-o kiita hito* = "the person who asked for directions" or
"the person [I] asked for directions"). The particle that would have marked the extracted
noun's role inside the embedded clause disappears once it is extracted (Unit 42), because
that particle's job was to link the noun to the verb within the clause it no longer
occupies a position in. Unit 46 extends the same device to nominalize whole propositions
(not just extract one argument) using bare `no` (distinct from the particle `-no`) — e.g.
*Akira-ga Naomi-ni hontoo-no koto-o itta no-wa odoroki-da* "That Akira told Naomi the
truth is surprising." Unit 47 then distinguishes this nominalizing `no` from `koto` (see
vocabulary row).

## Grammar points

### nado / nanka (など/なんか) — exemplifying/belittling particle
Paraphrased from pp.108–110. Lists representative examples ("in places such as elementary and junior high schools"), belittles the noun it follows ("how could I trust something that person said!"), softens a statement into vague indirection ("I think there will be nice dresses in a store like that"), and marks a statement as an approximate paraphrase of something said on another occasion ("he wrote something like he missed Japanese food").

### nagara (ながら) and nagara-mo (ながらも) — simultaneity/concession particles
Paraphrased from pp.110–112. "Nagara" marks two simultaneous actions ("eating potato chips while watching TV") or a concessive contrast between the two halves of a sentence ("even though he's the president of a large company, he's always humble"). "Nagara-mo" intensifies both of these senses.

### nante (なんて) — colloquial belittling particle
Paraphrased from p.112. Adds a dismissive or diminishing tone to the statement it follows, often quoting someone else's unreliable promise or downplaying an overreaction ("don't think a bad grade is the end of the world or something").

### nari (なり) — "as soon as"/alternatives particle
Paraphrased from pp.113–114. Marks immediate sequence ("fell asleep as soon as his head hit the pillow"), an unbroken continuing state ("she never returned after going to climb the Alps"), a casually suggested example, and — doubled as "...nari...nari" — a list of alternatives where the choice doesn't matter ("whether it's juice or water, drink plenty").

### n-de (んで) — polite negative-apology combination particle
Paraphrased from pp.115–116. Built from the negative "-(mase)n" plus "de"; used in polite negative statements ("please excuse me for not having visited, even though I'd heard you were ill") and in apologies for a failure to act ("I'm sorry that I didn't offer you tea, even though you came out of your way to visit").

### ne / nee (ね/ねえ) — the core confirmation/exclamation particle
Paraphrased from pp.116–117 (entry continues in part 8). "Nee" carries more emotional weight than "ne". Covers an untranslatable casual exclamation ("What a nice house you live in!"), a plain confirmation-seeking tag ("..., right?"), an agreement-eliciting softener ("Please forgive me, okay?"), a question-softening use after an interrogative pronoun, and — introduced here, detailed further in part 8 — a purely rhythmic, meaningless filler use.

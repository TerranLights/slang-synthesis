## Grammar points (continued)

### San extended to companies and impersonal reference
The honorific suffix *-san*, normally for people's names and occupations (omawari-san), extends to
store names and especially company names, which businesspeople treat as if personified family
names ("Sonii-san-towa torihiki-ga arimasu" — we deal with Sony); it is restricted to contexts of an
actual business relationship, and otherwise reads as an endearment rather than respect. (Vol.3 p.30-31)

### See / okage / tame — marking cause by emotional valence
Where English "because of" is neutral, Japanese distinguishes cause expressions by the speaker's
attitude: *see* ("due to," blame-laden — sake-no see, toshi-no see), *okage* ("thanks to,"
grateful), and *tame* (neutral/formal, more common in writing than conversation). Conversational
Japanese strongly prefers *see*/*okage* over *tame* because they convey the speaker's emotional
stance toward the cause. (Vol.3 p.32-33)

### Ookuri-shimashoo and degrees of seeing someone off
*Okuru* used with a person means "to escort," regardless of the escort's gender; full escort home
is considered most polite, a partial escort ("sono hen-made") is a common middle ground, and
declining requires an explicit apology — reflecting an underlying norm that one *should* want to
see a visitor off even though modern busy schedules often prevent it. (Vol.3 p.34-35)

### -te kuru marks actions that affect the speaker
Appending *kuru* to a verb's -te form signals that someone else's action impinges on/benefits the
speaker, filling the role that English "to me" plays without requiring an explicit watashi-ni —
"Kawakami-san-ga hon-o okutte-kimashita" (he sent me a book) sounds complete in a way that
omitting kuru, or using watashi-ni/watashi-no instead, does not. (Vol.3 p.38-39)

### Otaku as an impersonal second-person term
*Otaku* ("your house," extended to "you") avoids needing to know a listener's name or status, which
makes it useful in mass-media contexts, but it carries no inherent respect; using it with someone
one knows well or respects reads as a deliberate, teasing distancing. (Vol.3 p.40-41)

### Politeness shift as an anger signal
Making one's speech suddenly more polite than the relationship calls for — not in a request or
apology context — is a conventional signal of anger or displeasure (illustrated by an angry wife
using the humble causative kaerasete-itadakimasu to her husband, and by a mother shifting a child's
nickname to its formal form before scolding). (Vol.3 p.42-43)

### Senpai/koohai asymmetry outlives school
The senpai (senior)/koohai (junior) relationship formed in school or club contexts is treated as
fixed and persists after graduation regardless of later real-world status differences (a senpai who
becomes a koohai's subordinate at work still socially outranks them), and is reflected directly in
relative speech register, often more rigidly than teacher-student relationships. (Vol.3 p.44-45)

### Sentence-final no and gendered/age-marked register
The sentence-final particle *no* is gender/age-marked: added to plain-form speech it reads as
familiar (used by women or children, or by adults speaking to children), while added to polite-form
speech it reads as exclusively feminine. Intonation (rising vs. falling) distinguishes question from
statement independent of this register marking. (Vol.3 p.46-47)

### Professional vs. personal exchange patterns
Store/restaurant customer-service exchanges follow a distinct "professional" script where a
customer's silence after being thanked is not rude — it is a convention separate from the "personal"
pattern of conversation that would apply if the customer and clerk were acquaintances. (Vol.3 p.48-49)

### Ga vs. wa: subject-of-discussion vs. condition-marking
Beyond the common topic/subject gloss, *ga* marks the condition in a conditional clause (tenki-ga
yokereba, "if the weather is good") while *wa* marks what the rest of the sentence is actually about
— confusing the two in a conditional sentence shifts whose situation the apodosis is understood to
describe. (Vol.3 p.50-51)

### -eba/-reba vs. -temo/-demo conditionals
-eba/-reba marks a condition that leads to an expected, hoped-for result; -temo/-demo marks a
condition whose result is contrary to expectation or wish. Using the wrong one can flip a sincere
statement into unintended sarcasm (saying "if I take medicine, I won't be cured" instead of "even if
I take medicine, I won't be cured"). (Vol.3 p.52-53)

### Partial negation with wake-ja arimasen
Besides fully negating a verb, Japanese has a dedicated partial-negation construction (X wake-ja
arimasen) for negating only the quantity, manner, or reason behind an action while admitting the
action itself occurred — plain negation of such sentences risks being heard as total negation
instead. (Vol.3 p.56-57)

### Yes/no answers track expectation-agreement, not propositional truth
Japanese *hai*/*iie* answer according to whether the speaker agrees with the listener's
expectation or implied stance, not according to objective truth-value the way English "yes/no"
track a negative question's literal truth — producing answers that look inverted from an
English-speaker's perspective on structurally similar negative questions. (Vol.3 p.58-59)

### Omachidoosama vs. omatase-shimashita register split
Though both literally apologize for a wait, *omachidoosama* has weakened into a casual "here you
are" appropriate only for short waits and informal/younger-addressed contexts, while
*omatase-shimashita* remains genuinely polite and is preferred by quality establishments or for
longer waits — using the former toward someone who deserves more formal treatment can read as
unintentionally rude even without that intent. (Vol.3 p.60-61)

### Avoiding direct "give" verbs when actually giving something
Despite grammar books ranking *sashiageru* as the most polite of yaru/ageru/sashiageru, directly
using any "give" verb when actually handing over a gift sounds inappropriate in practice; Japanese
social convention instead favors self-deprecating framing of the gift ("this is a trifling thing,
but...") paired with a request that the recipient accept/use/eat it, with "please" often dropped or
spoken softly. (Vol.3 p.62-63)

### Mora-based phonological reversal wordplay
A word-reversal riddle game works by reversing the *hiragana mora* sequence of a phrase (not its
Roman-alphabet spelling) to produce a different well-formed phrase — "watashi" (wa-ta-shi) reverses
to "shi-ta-wa," which read together as "watashi makemashita-wa" / reversed gives a new sentence;
simple two-mora examples include taka (eagle) / kata (shoulder) and baka (fool) / kaba
(hippopotamus). A related device uses kanji compounds readable in either direction (Yamamoto-yama).
(Vol.3 p.64-65)

### Laughter onomatopoeia as gendered convention, not acoustic description
Japanese onomatopoeic laughter terms encode social register rather than literally transcribing
sound: *ho ho ho* conventionally marks refined, suppressed feminine laughter; *ha ha ha* (or *a ha
ha*) marks open, frank laughter via the wide mouth-opening of the "a" vowel; *wa ha ha ha* marks
coarse, masculine laughter judged inappropriate for a young woman regardless of how she actually
sounds — paralleling how English dogs "bark" while Japanese dogs go "wan wan." (Vol.3 p.66-67)

### Self-introduction without pronouncing one's own name
"Koo yuu mono-desu," said while presenting a business card, substitutes for pronouncing one's own
name outright — reflecting that kanji name readings are often ambiguous, a preference for visual
(card) over aural confirmation, and sometimes a greater social emphasis on the introducer or group
affiliation than on the individual being introduced. (Vol.3 p.68-69)

### Uso's semantic bleaching into a filler reply
A word meaning "a lie" underwent rapid semantic bleaching among young speakers (especially young
women) into a reflexive "really?/is that so?" filler with no accusatory force — a generational
shift the source explicitly compares to an earlier, now-normalized shift where "honto" (true)
displaced "soo-desu-ka" despite the same "that sounds like you're calling me a liar" criticism from
older speakers. (Vol.3 p.70-71)

### Homophone-based gift/food customs
Japanese social customs around gift-giving and celebratory food choices lean on puns: *soba*
(noodles ≈ "neighborhood") gifted to new neighbors as hikkoshi-soba; *kobu* (kelp, ≈ yorokobu "to
rejoice") and *tai* (sea bream, ≈ the ending of medetai "happy/auspicious") eaten on celebratory
occasions for their sound-symbolism as much as their taste. (Vol.3 p.72-75)

### Tsukiai as time-investment-based group standing
*Tsukiai* ("keeping company") treats sheer time spent with a group — not conversational skill or
likability — as the operative currency for being seen as a good group member, applying equally to
businessmen's after-work drinking, PTA housewives, and student club members; tsukiai-zake names
drinking undertaken from social obligation rather than personal desire. (Vol.3 p.74-75)

### Yoo-desu as a speaker-optional hedge, not a fact-tracking marker
Appending *yoo-desu* ("it seems") is available even when the speaker is certain of a fact, and its
use is governed by the speaker's own reserve/self-consciousness (notably men of higher social
position discussing family) rather than by their actual certainty — so two equally-informed
speakers describing the same fact may differ on whether to hedge it. (Vol.3 p.76-77)

### Iku/kuru orientation is speaker-fixed, not listener-fixed
Unlike English "come," which can orient toward the listener's location, Japanese *iku* ("go") is
used for any motion away from the speaker's current position regardless of whether the destination
is where the listener is — "kyoo ikimasu" (not kuru) is correct for "I'm coming to see you today."
Politer substitutes for iku in this context include ukagaimasu, ojama-shimasu, and mairimasu.
(Vol.3 p.78-79)

### A classical proverb with two opposed live readings
"Nasake-wa hito-no tame-narazu" is understood in two directly contradictory ways by competent adult
speakers simultaneously: the traditional reading (kindness ultimately benefits the giver, so be
kind) and a newer reading some younger speakers hold (kindness does others no favors/spoils them,
so withhold it) — the source notes two characters each assumed the other shared their own reading,
illustrating that a fixed-form proverb's meaning is not guaranteed to be stable across a speech
community. (Vol.3 p.80-81)

### "...de ii-kara" softens a request by minimizing imposition
Appending a phrase like "kaeri-de ii-kara" ("since [doing it on your] way back is fine") to a
request isn't literally explaining a reason — it signals the speaker's reluctance to impose and
offers the listener an easy, low-cost way to comply, paraphrasable as "please don't go out of your
way for my sake." (Vol.3 p.82-83)

### Deliberately unfinished clauses as indirect refusal
Japanese commonly avoids a direct "no" to a request by trailing off after "...wake-ja
arimasen-kedo/-ga" ("I don't mean to say that... but"), implicitly inviting the requester to
withdraw their own request rather than forcing the speaker to refuse it outright; several other
softening fillers (sore-wa chotto..., sore-wa doo-deshoo-ne, ...yamayama-desu-ga) work the same way
and can imply refusal purely through hesitant/regretful tone. (Vol.3 p.84-85)

### Goroawase numeral wordplay
Because Japanese numerals have multiple valid readings (native Japanese vs. Sino-Japanese, full
word vs. first syllable), digit strings can be recombined into meaningful phrases — exploited
commercially for memorable phone numbers and pedagogically as a mnemonic device for memorizing
dates/numbers. (Vol.3 p.86-87)

### Yoso marks explicit in-group/out-group contrast
*Yoso* ("another place," contrasted with *uchi*, "our own") is used specifically when the speaker
is consciously drawing a boundary between their own group (company, school, family) and an outside
one; the construction extends metaphorically from physical place to appearance/speech/behavior via
yoso-iki ("going-out clothes," i.e. one's more formal public self). (Vol.3 p.88-89)

### Ga selects, wa can flip polarity with emphasis
To indicate the specifically chosen item among options, Japanese uses *ga* ("kore-ga ii-desu" — "I
want this one"); the superficially similar *wa* sentence is ambiguous and, said with strong
emphasis on ii-desu, can actually mean the opposite — a polite refusal ("no, thank you"). Separately,
*wa*-marked topics (watashi-wa) are dropped whenever understood from context, which is the default
in self-introductions and answering "how are you." (Vol.3 p.90-91)

### Noni as an expectation-violation marker with implied blame
*Noni* explicitly marks that what follows runs contrary to the speaker's expectations or wishes,
carrying a connotation of regret, surprise, criticism, or reprimand that neutral concessive markers
(keredomo, -temo/-demo) lack — which makes it inappropriate when the speaker is proposing the
contrary action themselves rather than lamenting someone else's. A noni-clause's consequence is
often left unsaid entirely, understood from context and completed nonverbally. (Vol.3 p.92-93)

### Byooki vs. vague unwellness terms, and symptom-avoidance norms
*Byooki* is reserved for serious, doctor-requiring illness; minor or vague unwellness is expressed
through chooshi-ga warui/guai-ga warui/kibun-ga warui rather than byooki, and *yowai* describes a
person's general constitution rather than a current condition. Social Japanese additionally avoids
naming specific bodily symptoms (diarrhea, constipation) even when literally true, preferring vague
excuses like "I have something to attend to" instead. (Vol.3 p.94-95)

### -tachi as a non-honorific group pluralizer, -gata as its polite form
Appending *-tachi* to a name or person-word pluralizes it to mean either multiple instances of that
exact referent or "that person and their associated group" — ambiguous without further context — and
carries no politeness; the polite equivalent is *-gata* (anata-gata, sensee-gata), while *-domo* is
the humble form used only for referring to one's own group (watashi-domo, not watashi-tachi, in
formal self-reference). (Vol.3 p.96-97)

### Coexisting loanword/native doublets differentiated by use-context, not simple replacement
Where Japanese has both a loanword and a native/Sino-Japanese term for the same referent, the two
terms typically specialize rather than one simply displacing the other: piinattsu (shelled peanuts)
vs. nankinmame (in-shell); raisu (Western-plated restaurant rice) vs. gohan/kome (kome exclusively
uncooked); untenshu (professional driver) vs. doraibaa (private/leisure driver); garasu (glass
material) vs. gurasu (a drinking glass specifically). (Vol.3 p.98-99)

### A graded ladder of apology expressions
Japanese apology vocabulary forms a register ladder rather than free variation: bare *warui* (very
familiar, limited to close peers), *warui-desu-ne*/*warukatta-ne* (familiar-casual, gendered
variants for fathers vs. mothers to children), *sumimasen* (the widely-usable polite default), and
*shitsuree-shimashita*/*mooshiwake-arimasen* (reserved for offenses the speaker considers serious).
Children are restricted to *gomen-nasai* toward senior family members regardless of what a parent
might say to them. (Vol.3 p.100-101)

### Sekkaku's two constructions: thwarted effort vs. justified acceptance
*Sekkaku* marks that special effort was invested with an expected reward in mind. Combined with
*noni* it expresses regret that the expected reward didn't materialize (went to buy something,
specially, but it was sold out); combined with *kara* it instead justifies either accepting someone
else's effort/gift graciously, or a speaker resolving to make the most of their own hard-won
opportunity. (Vol.3 p.102-103)

### Sassoku restricted to willed, agentive action
*Sassoku* ("right away") can only describe an action performed by someone exercising their own
will/intention; it cannot describe involuntary events or outcomes outside anyone's control (a
machine breaking, recovering from illness, weather), where *sugu* is used instead — even though
both superficially translate as "right away/soon." (Vol.3 p.104-105)

### Kesshite restricted to determination/belief, not neutral fact-negation
*Kesshite* ("never," literally "resolutely") is appropriate only for expressing the speaker's own
determination about future conduct or a firm belief/character judgment about someone — not for
flatly negating a past factual event outside anyone's control (e.g. "it never rained"), where
ichido-mo/sukoshi-mo/zenzen are the correct choices instead. (Vol.3 p.106-107)

### Nakanaka's two senses and its adult, gendered register
*Nakanaka* means either "not easily/takes a long time" (describing a slow-to-arrive awaited
outcome) or "pretty/very" as a reserved positive evaluation that carries a faint self-important
undertone — the second sense is used mainly by adults, more often men, and sounds incongruous
coming from a small child, who would more naturally use an unreserved intensifier like totemo or
sugoku instead. (Vol.3 p.108-109)

### Yoo-desu's hedge is attitude-driven, not knowledge-driven
Across two separate essays the series returns to the same point: *yoo-desu* signals the speaker's
wish to sound reserved/not overly certain, and its use or omission is a matter of personal/social
style, not actual epistemic uncertainty — a speaker who clearly knows a fact may still hedge it,
while someone else describing the identical fact may state it flatly. Using it on a pure logical
necessity (1+1=2) sounds absurd, confirming the hedge tracks social stance rather than genuine doubt.
(Vol.3 p.110-111)

### Chotto's completion left to context
*Chotto*, especially doubled with a bare "ee" (ee, chotto) or left standing alone, routinely
appears with its real content unstated — the listener is expected to infer a summons, a request to
be excused, or a reluctant admission from situational cues (tone, posture, a bow) rather than from
any spoken continuation; a very common two-line neighbor exchange ("Odekake-desu-ka"/"Ee, chotto")
functions purely phatically, carrying almost none of its literal semantic content. (Vol.3 p.112-113)

### Oisogashii tokoro-o as ritual presumption of imposition
The "tokoro-o" construction (busy/tired/hurried + tokoro + object-marking o) is a politeness ritual
presuming the addressee's inconvenience regardless of whether they were actually busy — used by
hosts greeting visitors and by visitors thanking/apologizing to hosts alike, with several
interchangeable slot-fillers (odekake-no, oisogi-no, otsukare-no) for the specific presumed
circumstance. (Vol.3 p.114-115)

### Tari-tari suru for enumerating characterizing actions, not bare succession
The ...tari...tari suru construction lists two or more actions that together characterize a state or
kind of experience (how a weekend was spent, how rowdy a party got) — it cannot be used for a simple
one-after-another succession of two specific actions without a connecting device; a single "tari"
can also stand in for an unspecified, implied cluster of related actions functioning as a vague,
face-saving catch-all. (Vol.3 p.116-117)

### Mata kondo as a conventional, timing-sensitive refusal
"Mata kondo(-ni shimasu)" — literally "I'll make it some other [unspecified] time" — is the
standard indirect way to decline a salesperson's pitch or an invitation to try something without
stating a literal objection; using it correctly requires waiting for the other party's natural
pause, and a well-socialized salesperson is expected not to press further once it's said. (Vol.3
p.118-119)

### Oree's verbal/material sense split, and occupation-dependent fee vocabulary
*Oree* splits into "to thank verbally" (oree-o yuu) and "to pay as thanks" (oree-o suru); which fee
term to use is itself socially marked — oree is preferred over the plain business term ryookin for
services felt to be personal (teachers, doctors, lawyers, private tutors) rather than purely
transactional, with money gifts conventionally pre-apologized for ("shitsuree-desu-ga...") and
placed in an envelope rather than handed over directly. (Vol.3 p.120-121)

### Taishita koto-wa arimasen as partial vs. total denial
"Taishita koto-wa arimasen" downplays the degree of something (one's own effort, a compliment)
without fully denying its premise, occupying a middle ground between a simple "iie" and the
complete-denial forms "sonna koto-wa arimasen"/"tondemo arimasen" — with "sorehodo-demo arimasen"
available as a further option that partially concedes the compliment's truth. (Vol.3 p.122-123)

### Apologizing for the timing itself as a conversational opener
Japanese phone calls and visits conventionally open with an apology aimed specifically at the
*timing* of the interruption, with the substantive verb (calling, visiting) often omitted as
understood from context; this timing-apology slot has several interchangeable fillers — tokoro-o
("while you are [busy/resting]") and chuu ("in the midst of [work/a meal/studying/a conversation]")
— that can be swapped in depending on what the addressee is presumed to be doing. (Vol.3 p.124-125)

### Kanji/fuu/guai as reserve-signaling request vocabulary
Requesting a desired style or outcome via impression-words like kanji ("feeling"), fuu ("fashion"),
or guai ("manner") rather than grammatically precise phrasing (kono katachi-ni, kono toori-ni)
signals polite reserve; the precise phrasing is not ungrammatical, but reads as demanding/imposing
by requiring the listener to match an exact standard rather than use their own judgment. (Vol.3
p.126-127)

### Onomatopoeic weather vocabulary vs. dictionary-correct register
Natural conversational small talk about weather favors onomatopoeic/mimetic expressions
(jimejime-shite, karatto-shite, zaazaa, shitoshito, parapara) over technically correct dictionary
vocabulary (shitsudo-ga takai, kaisee) — using the latter in casual conversation reads as
imitating a formal weather broadcast rather than making ordinary small talk. (Vol.3 p.128-129)

### Numeral "one" words idiomatically mean "some/a little," not literally one
Both *ippai* ("one cup") and *hitotsu* ("one [thing]") are regularly used to mean a vague, open
quantity rather than literally a single unit — inviting someone for "ippai nomi-ni" implies
drinking generally (not one drink), and offering "hitotsu ikaga-desu-ka" doesn't limit the guest to
taking only one. A family of related hito- idioms (hito-yasumi, hito-nemuri, hito-shigoto,
hito-furo) similarly uses "one" to mean "a short/single instance of," not a strict count. (Vol.3
p.130-131)

### Tasukaru/tasukeru perspective asymmetry in giving thanks
Thanking someone for helping requires the intransitive, speaker-as-beneficiary verb *tasukaru*
("tasukarimashita" — you helped me), not the transitive *tasukeru* ("to help [someone]"), which
backwards implies the listener helped a third party rather than the speaker; the same verb extends
to impersonal relief from favorable circumstances like cool weather. (Vol.3 p.132-133)

### Doozo goyukkuri restricted to a household/organization's own representative
This hospitality formula ("please make yourself at home/take your time") is socially restricted to
whoever represents the household or organization receiving a visitor (a spouse, a company
director) — a maid or junior family member would not normally say it, and it is not used by
someone who is themselves visiting a superior alongside the addressee. (Vol.3 p.134-135)

### Tekitoo-ni yatte-kudasai presupposes shared understanding, not open license
Delegating a task with "please use your own judgment" doesn't grant unlimited discretion — the
speaker typically has implicit expectations the listener is meant to infer from precedent and
context, and deviating far from those unstated expectations defeats the phrase's intent; it is also
directionally restricted, normally used toward someone not superior to the speaker, since presuming
to sense a superior's unstated intentions would itself be presumptuous. (Vol.3 p.136-137)

### Yoona ki-ga suru as a reason-withholding opinion hedge
Appending "...yoona ki-ga suru" to an opinion explicitly marks it as held without a particular
definite reason, making it more reserved/less assertive than a plain "...to omou" statement; it
pairs naturally with nan-to-naku ("for no particular reason") and serves as a conventional way to
soften criticism or express tentative agreement without claiming certainty. (Vol.3 p.138-139)

### Te-arimasu vs. te-imasu: a textbook equivalence that fails in practice
Although commonly taught as interchangeable ways to describe a resulting state (transitive-verb +
te-arimasu ≈ intransitive-verb + te-imasu), the two are not neutral paraphrases of each other:
te-arimasu with a deliberately-acting verb (akeru, shimeru, tsukeru) implies someone did the action
on purpose and often carries the speaker's surprise, criticism, or blame, while the intransitive
te-imasu form is a flat, attitude-free state description — a distinction that doesn't hold for every
verb pair and can misfire badly if applied where no deliberate agent is implied. (Vol.3 p.140-141)

### Self-deprecating "...de yokattara" offers
Offering something — an object, help, a substitute — with "...de yokattara" ("if [this] will do")
pre-emptively lowers expectations about what's being offered, functioning as a polite, reserved way
to propose one's own help or an imperfect substitute without appearing presumptuous; "yoroshikattara"
is a more formal variant, and salespeople use the same pattern when offering alternative merchandise.
(Vol.3 p.142-143)

### Daiji-ni suru extends from resource-conservation to loving care for people
*Daiji-ni suru* ("to treat as important") applies to objects, time, and money as a conservation
ethic (paralleling mottainai), and extends by the same logic to people as a culturally preferred
substitute for directly saying "ai-suru" (to love) — especially among older speakers who find overt
declarations of love uncomfortable, making "daiji-ni shite-imasu" the conventional way to say
someone loves/cherishes their spouse or children. (Vol.3 p.144-145)

### Kiku/kikoeru and miru/mieru: willed action vs. involuntary perception
Japanese grammatically distinguishes intentional/habitual perception (kiku "to [choose to]
listen/hear," miru "to [choose to] look/watch") from involuntary, will-independent perception
(kikoeru "to be audible regardless of anyone's will," mieru "to be visible regardless of anyone's
will") — using the intentional verb for a situation of mere physical inaudibility/invisibility
incorrectly implies the subject is willfully refusing to listen or look. (Vol.3 p.146-147)

## Guide for Avoidance of Common Mistakes (Vol.3 pp.148-157)

This appended supplement (70 columns' worth of situational essays, pp.8-146, are followed by this
25-item structured grammar-mistake guide before the index) is organized as correct-model-sentence +
common-error explanation, cross-referencing Nihongo Notes 1-3 and *An Introduction to Modern
Japanese*. Paraphrased below, one subsection per numbered item; illustrative sentences are kept
short per copyright discipline rather than reproducing the guide's full example sets.

1. **Connecting two adjectives.** I-adjectives connect with -kute (hirokute shizuka-desu, "it's
   spacious and quiet"), not -to (not hiroi-to shizuka-desu); na-adjectives connect with -de
   (shizuka-de suzushii-desu), not -to.
2. **Negative form of kiree (and other na-adjectives treated as nouns for negation purposes).**
   "Amari kiree-ja arimasen," not "amari kireeku arimasen" (kiree is grammatically a na-adjective/
   nominal, not an i-adjective, despite its surface form).
3. **"Meeting with people."** Use -ni aimashita (iroirona hito-ni aimashita, "I met various
   people"), not -o aimashita; -o mimashita is grammatical but means only "saw" without implying
   conversation.
4. **Position of a number in a sentence.** A bare quantity word follows the object + o (ringo-o
   itsutsu kudasai, "give me five apples"); putting the number before the noun with no (itsutsu-no
   ringo-o kudasai) implies reference to five specific, already-identified apples rather than a
   request for a quantity.
5. **Indicating purpose.** Visiting-for-a-visit's-sake uses asobi-ni kite-kudasai (not the bare
   verb stem asobu-ni, and tame-ni sounds awkward here); mi-ni kite-kudasai changes the meaning to
   "come to see [some object]," not "come to see me." Travel/movement purpose uses -ni ikimashita
   (ryokoo-ni ikimashita), not -e (which marks destination) or tame-ni (awkward).
6. **"Talking with people."** Tanaka-san-to hanashimashita (talked with); -to issho-ni is reserved
   for actions other than talking (tanaka-san-to issho-ni ikimashita, "went with Mr. Tanaka").
   Contrast -towa (hanashimasen-deshita) for explicitly marking someone NOT talked with, among others
   who were.
7. **Describing a state of being with the -te-iru form.** A current state uses the -te-iru form of
   the verb (megane-o kakete-iru hito, "the person wearing glasses"); the bare non-past form
   (kakeru) would mean the person is *going to* wear glasses, a future/intended action, not a
   current state.
8. **Adverbs in negative statements.** "Zenzen" or "sukoshi-mo" pair naturally with negative
   predicates for "not at all"; "kesshite" sounds awkward for simple negation (reserved for
   resolution/belief, cf. Vol.3 p.106-107); "minna wakarimasen-deshita" is ambiguous between "I
   understood none of it" and "I didn't understand all of it."
9. **Comparing two things.** "...no hoo-ga" marks which of two specific things is more X (kochira-no
   hoo-ga ookii-desu); "motto ookii" is reserved for comparison against something already mentioned,
   not between two present options.
10. **Indicating excessive degree.** The -sugiru suffix (ookisugimasu, "it's too big";
    nomisugite..., "[I] drank too much and...") is the correct way to express excess, not an
    amari + plain-adjective construction.
11. **Plain forms when reporting speech/stating what you think.** Reported speech/opinion with -to
    iimashita/-to omoimasu requires the plain/short form of the preceding predicate (zannen-da-to
    iimashita, nihonjin-da-to iimashita), not the polite -desu form directly before -to.
12. **Indicating your own intention.** -(y)oo-to omoimasu (volitional form + to omoimasu) marks the
    speaker's own stated intention (tegami-o kakoo-to omoimasu, "I think I'll write a letter"); the
    plain non-past + to omoimasu (kaku-to omoimasu) instead sounds like a claim about someone else's
    intention.
13. **Wakaru vs. shiru.** "Mada wakarimasen" ("I don't know yet," about something within the
    speaker's own capacity to decide/figure out) is correct for a question about one's own future
    plans; "shirimasen" is used for external facts, not self-determined decisions.
14. **Talking about other people's feelings.** Direct feeling-adjectives (ureshii, kanashii,
    sabishii) are reserved for the speaker's own feelings; describing someone else's apparent
    feeling requires -soo-desu (ureshisoo-desu, "looks happy") or an inference marker like deshoo
    (ureshii-deshoo, "[I think] he must be happy").
15. **Iku vs. kuru (reprise, cf. Vol.3 p.78-79).** Kuru marks motion toward wherever the speaker is
    at the moment of speaking (including a return to the speaker's own present location); motion
    toward a third location (even the listener's own home) uses iku, politely replaced by
    ukagaimasu/mairimasu/ojama-shimasu.
16. **Sore/are and sono/ano.** Ano/are presuppose the listener already shares knowledge of the
    referent; sore/sono are used to introduce a referent the speaker assumes the listener does NOT
    yet know, even within the same conversation turn.
17. **Asking for permission.** The standard pattern is -te-mo ii-desu-ka (moo kaette-mo ii-desu-ka,
    "may I leave now?"); nominalizing the verb with koto/no before ii-desu-ka sounds wrong; deshoo-ka
    can substitute for desu-ka for extra softness.
18. **Expressing subsequent action.** -te-kara ("after doing X") marks a sequential action
    (shokuji-o shite-kara dekakemasu, "I'll go out after eating"); using -ta-kara after the plain
    past form is wrong because kara after -ta means "because," not "after" — a pronunciation-driven
    confusion between te/ta and de/da the guide explicitly flags as worth careful attention.
19. **Partial negation (reprise, cf. Vol.3 p.56-57).** "Zenbu-wa dekimasen-deshita" unambiguously
    negates only part of a set ("couldn't do all of them"); "zenbu dekimasen-deshita" (no wa) is
    ambiguous with "couldn't do any of them"; "zenbu dekita wake-ja arimasen" is an alternative
    partial-negation phrasing.
20. **Taking a test.** The fixed collocation is shiken-o ukemashita ("I took/received a test"), not
    torimashita or moraimashita.
21. **Making a request.** -ni tanomimashita marks asking someone to do something (Tanaka-san-ni
    tanomimashita); kikimashita instead means "I asked [him] a question" or "I heard it from him" —
    a different verb entirely, not a register variant.
22. **Asking a question.** shitsumon-shimashita, kikimashita, and tazunemashita are all acceptable
    for "asked a question"; "shitsumon-o kikimashita" (treating shitsumon as kiku's object) is
    incorrect.
23. **Asking wishes politely.** "(Ocha-wa) ikaga-desu-ka" is the polite way to offer something to a
    superior/guest; "-tai-desu-ka" (desire form) directed at someone else is reserved for addressing
    equals, and sounds impolite toward a superior.
24. **Receiving something from someone.** kuremashita (X-ga kuremashita, "X gave it to me") and
    moraimashita (X-ni moraimashita, "I received it from X") both work for receiving; ageru is never
    used for the speaker's own receiving, only for giving. Humble equivalents: itadakimashita,
    kudasaimashita.
25. **Receiving a favor from someone.** tetsudatte-kuremashita / tetsudatte-moraimashita both
    correctly frame being helped as a favor received; a bare tetsudaimashita ("helped") is
    grammatical but doesn't convey gratitude toward the helper the way the -te kureru/-te morau
    constructions do. Humble equivalents: tetsudatte-kudasaimashita, tetsudatte-itadakimashita.

## Grammar points

### Tonikaku as discussion-closer
"Tonikaku" ("anyway") marks both a decision/urging to act and, especially from a senior/influential
speaker, a signal that a discussion is to be considered closed — hearers interpret a bare
"Tonikaku..." from an authority figure as an implicit "no." (Vol.3 p.8-9)

### Mottainai's semantic range
Originally tied to a pre-war ethic against wasting food (rice especially), *mottainai* now covers
any case of "not doing justice" to a resource — leaving a light on, staying indoors on a nice day,
or spending money on something disproportionately fine for its purpose/recipient. (Vol.3 p.10-11)

### Aite and self-reference asymmetry
*Aite* denotes "the other party" in a paired relationship and can be used generically (aite-ga
imasen = "I have no one [to marry/go with]") without specifying the activity when context supplies
it (kekkon-no aite, go-no aite, sake-no aite). (Vol.3 p.12-13)

### Ta/da-form marks completion, not strictly past tense
Verbs ending in -ta/-da (the "past" form) more accurately mark completion of an action and can be
used for present, future, or imperative meaning, not only past time — e.g. a vendor shouting
"Katta, katta!" to mean "buy it!", or "ashita kita hito-ni agemasu" ("I'll give it to whoever
comes tomorrow," using the ta-form for a future event). (Vol.3 p.16-17)

### Hai as a finality marker vs. agreement marker
Beyond signaling agreement (where it is more formal than *ee*), *hai* can be appended after a
completed statement to mark it as final/closed ("that's enough," "I don't want to hear more");
overusing this pattern can make a speaker sound anxious or unconfident. (Vol.3 p.18-19)

### Rikutsu and self-serving argument
*Rikutsu* ("theory/logic") shifts from a neutral meaning (rikutsu-to genjitsu-wa chigau — "theory
and reality differ") to a deprecatory one (rikutsu-bakari iu, rikutsu-o yuu) describing someone who
argues only to protect their own interests — most often said by a parent irritated at a child's
explanations, where "don't talk logic" functions as "don't talk back." (Vol.3 p.20-21)

### Hito: "a person" vs. "others," and indirect self-reference
*Hito* means "person/human being" generically, but in social context specifically denotes someone
other than the speaker — so "watashi-wa koko-no hito-desu" (literally "I am a person of this
place") is ungrammatical-sounding for "I work here"; correct options are "koko-ni tsutomete-imasu"
or the humble "koko-no mono-desu." *Hito* can also refer indirectly back to the speaker in rhetorical
complaints ("hito-o baka-ni shinaide-kudasai" — "don't make a fool of me," literally "don't make
fools of others"). (Vol.3 p.22-23)

### Muri as a softener for requests and refusals
*Muri-o suru* covers overworking, acting against one's own wishes, or overspending. In social use,
*muri* softens a refusal ("chotto muri-kamo shiremasen" rather than a flat "dekimasen") or
apologizes in advance for an imposing request ("murina onegai-towa omoimasu-ga..."). (Vol.3 p.24-25)

### Deflecting responsibility onto a family member
When making a generous offer or a request/complaint, Japanese speakers often attribute the
insistence to a spouse or parent ("shujin-ga soo mooshimasu-node" / "kanai-ga... " / "chichi-ga...")
rather than claiming it for themselves — framed as good manners (avoiding the appearance of
imposing one's own will) rather than evasiveness, and also signals a positive spousal relationship.
(Vol.3 p.26-27)

### Doo-deshoo-ne's falling intonation as negative judgment
Said with a doubtful, falling tone, "Doo-deshoo-ne" ("I wonder how it is") functions as an implicit
negative judgment rather than a real question inviting the hearer's opinion; a genuine request for
opinion requires rising intonation or a different phrasing ("kore, doo omoimasu-ka"). The related
"dooka-to omoimasu" expresses negative judgment or apprehension even more directly. (Vol.3 p.28-29)

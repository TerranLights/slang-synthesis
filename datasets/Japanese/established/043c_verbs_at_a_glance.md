## Grammar points

### Overview: compound verbs built on the /-masu/ stem (or -te form) of a first verb (p. 103)

The book frames this entire chapter around one mechanism: "many verb compounds can be formed by
appending one verb to the end of another. In most cases, the second verb is attached to the
/-masu/ stem of the first verb." A minority of patterns instead attach to the -te form (flagged
explicitly, p. 103, as the type beginning on p. 116 — the "going/coming/returning after doing
something" and "gradual change" patterns captured above). Each sub-pattern below names a specific
aspectual or manner meaning contributed by the second (ending) verb.

### Start of action: -dasu vs. -hajimeru (p. 103)

Both endings mark the start of a continuous action, attached only to verbs describing ongoing
actions, but differ in intensity: **-dasu** 出す implies a greater burst of energy/suddenness
(hashiridasu "to dash forth") than **-hajimeru** 始める, which is more neutral (hashirihajimeru "to
start running"). As a separate, non-aspectual sense, -dasu also independently means "to send
something forth" or "to cause something to appear" in lexicalized compounds (mitsukedasu "to find
out," omoidasu "to recall") — the book is explicit that this is a *different* meaning of the same
suffix, not an extension of the "start of action" sense.

### Half-completed action: -kakeru (p. 104)

-kakeru かける (attached to the /-masu/ stem) indicates an action was started but not brought to
completion or an end — e.g. kakikakeru "to have started writing (a letter) but not finished."

### Continuation of action: -tsuzuku (i) / -tsuzukeru (t) (p. 105)

This intransitive/transitive pair marks ongoing, unbroken continuation of an action — furitsuzuku
"for rain to keep falling without letup," hikitsuzukeru "to keep playing (an instrument) without a
break."

### End of an action: -owaru / -oeru / -agaru (i) / -ageru (t) (pp. 105–106)

Four endings mark completion: -owaru 終わる and -oeru 終える are largely synonymous and primarily
confined to the "finish doing X" sense; -agaru 上がる (intransitive) and -ageru 上げる (transitive)
also mark completion but, per the book's explicit note, both transitive and intransitive verbs can
appear with either (object/particle use needs care as a result).

### Thoroughness of action: -kiru vs. -tōsu / -nuku (pp. 106–108)

Three endings all mark thorough, to-the-utmost-extent completion, but with different connotations
per the book's explicit three-way contrast: **-kiru** 切る indicates solely that an action was done
to completion/to the utmost extent (nomikiru "to drink it all," kashikiru "to rent out
completely"); **-tōsu** 通す and **-nuku** 抜く both add the notion of "doing something from
beginning to end... despite adverse conditions" and are largely interchangeable, but -tōsu "places
slightly more emphasis on the process" while -nuku "emphasizes the successful emergence from the
process" (e.g. yaritōsu/yarinuku, both "to carry out [a difficult task] to the very end," are
explicitly flagged as near-interchangeable).

### Incomplete or failed action: -wasureru / -otosu / -machigaeru / -chigaeru (pp. 109–111)

Four endings each mark a distinct flavor of failure: **-wasureru** 忘れる — the action was
*forgotten* (dashiwasureru "to forget to send"); **-otosu** 落す — the action was *unintentionally
omitted* (miotosu "to fail to see/overlook" — its derived noun o-miotoshi is given as a lexicalized
example); **-machigaeru** 間違える and **-chigaeru** 違える — a *mistake was made*, and the book
states these last two are "largely interchangeable" in that sense (yomimachigaeru/yomichigaeru,
both "to misread").

### Entering and inserting: -komu (p. 112)

-komu 込む (attached to the /-masu/ stem) expresses "entering something or putting one thing inside
another" — tobikomu "to dive into (a pool)," kakikomu "to fill in/write onto (a form)."

### Getting used to an action: -nareru (p. 112)

-nareru 慣れる expresses becoming accustomed to doing something — hakinareru "to get used to
wearing (shoes) [so that walking becomes easy]."

### Redoing or rechecking an action: -naosu (p. 113)

-naosu 直す means to redo or recheck something — yominaosu "to read over again," kakenaosu "to
call back again."

### Mutual action: -au (p. 113)

-au 合う (attached to the /-masu/ stem — not to be confused with the independent verb au 合う "to
match," which is itself one of the vocabulary items used to illustrate the transitive/intransitive
pattern table in `043b`) means to do something mutually or reciprocally — aishiau "to love each
other," tasukeau "to help each other."

### Excessive action: -sugiru (p. 114)

-sugiru 過ぎる indicates something is done to excess — suisugiru "to smoke too much," nomisugiru
"to drink too much (leading to a hangover)."

### Attaching actions: -tsuku (i) / -tsukeru (t) (pp. 114–115)

This intransitive/transitive pair describes approaching or attaching onto something, both literally
(tobitsuku "to jump/fly onto," nuitsukeru "to sew onto") and, for -tsukeru specifically, in two
distinct idiomatic extensions: "to summon (someone) to oneself" (yobitsukeru) and, separately, "to
be called onto the carpet" (also yobitsukeru, in a scolding-register example) — the book gives both
senses of the same compound verb as separate illustrative sentences without further commentary on
how the two relate.

### Going or coming for a purpose: -ni iku / -ni kuru (p. 115)

/-masu/ stem + ni iku に行く or ni kuru に来る expresses going or coming in order to do something —
mi ni iku "to go (in order) to see," tabe ni kuru "to come (in order) to eat."

### Going, coming, or returning after doing something: -te + iku/kuru/kaeru (pp. 116–117)

When attached after the -te form (not the /-masu/ stem — the chapter's one explicitly-flagged
exception to its general rule), iku 行く, kuru 来る, and kaeru 帰る indicate that the subject has
gone, come, or returned (home, to the office, etc.) after completing the first verb's action —
motte iku "to take something with one," katte kaeru "to buy something and bring it back."

### Gradual change: -te + iku/kuru (p. 117)

The same -te + iku/kuru pattern, used with change-of-state verbs, instead marks *gradual* change
rather than literal motion — and in this specific use the book notes iku/kuru are conventionally
written in hiragana (not kanji) to signal the grammaticalized, non-literal sense. The two are
distinguished pragmatically rather than semantically: -te iku implies "an objective judgment," -te
kuru "a subjective viewpoint," though the book notes they are often interchangeable (natte iku "to
become gradually," dekite kuru "to become able to gradually").

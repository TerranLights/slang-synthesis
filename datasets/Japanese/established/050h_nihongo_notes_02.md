## Grammar points

### Category-1 (frank) vs. category-2 (polite/reserved) register pairs, as a cross-cutting pattern
This entire section is organized around a single repeated structural contrast: for each of nine common
conversational functions (opinion, soliciting opinion, request, permission, disapproval, refusal,
complaint, advice, leave-taking), Japanese offers one set of direct/frank expressions for close
friends and a parallel, more indirect/roundabout set for situations requiring reserve. The polite set
is consistently characterized by: (1) hedged/open sentence endings (*-deshoo-ka*, *-ga*, *-kedo*
rather than flat *-desu*/*-masu*) that invite the listener's input instead of closing the topic; (2)
visible hesitation/reluctance performed before delivering sensitive content; (3) self-blame framing
(the speaker attributing difficulty to their own inability) rather than placing demands on the
listener; and (4) letting the listener infer and voice an unwelcome conclusion (a refusal, a
complaint's underlying fault) rather than the speaker stating it outright (pp.148-157).

### Multi-turn indirection for refusal and complaint
Both refusal (section 6) and complaint (section 7) share a specific discourse strategy beyond simple
hedging: the speaker lays out an accumulating sequence of separate justifying reasons or circumstances
across multiple conversational turns, deliberately waiting for the other party to connect the dots and
voice the refusal/acknowledge the fault themselves, rather than the original speaker ever stating it
directly. This cooperative pattern — where the listener completes the implied meaning — is treated by
the text as central to what distinguishes polite Japanese conversation from a merely polite vocabulary
choice (pp.154-155).

This chapter is organized first around three compound-formants used for "international"-flavored
terminology — 主義 *shugi* "principle, -ism," 教 *kyō* "teaching" (religion names), and 派 *ha*
"faction, sect" — and then shifts to a non-root-organized survey of traditional Japanese Buddhist
and (briefly) Shintō religious/philosophical vocabulary, including several idioms, a proverb, and
two literary quotations (from *The Tale of the Heike* and the *Analects of Confucius*/Heraclitus
parallel). Coverage here captures every distinct compound/term introduced in the prose and
illustrative word-lists, not a verbatim reproduction of all 39 numbered example sentences (most
reuse headwords already captured in the prose above them; only a handful of short idiom/proverb
quotes are retained per the copyright-discipline rule). This is the second of 7 chapters in this
~130-page book being extracted as sibling files (091a/091b–091g) by independent vision-reading
passes.

**Morpheme breakdown.** Three further Sino-Japanese compounds from this chapter are worth
breaking down for how transparently (or not) their parts combine:

> **安全第一主義** (anzen-dai'ichi-shugi) = 安全 (anzen, "safety") + 第一 (dai'ichi, "number
> one, first") + 主義 (-shugi, "-ism") — "safety-first-ism." A fully transparent three-part
> compound, illustrating how productively *-shugi* attaches even to multi-morpheme native
> expressions rather than only single Sino-Japanese roots (p.27).

> **三日坊主** (mikka-bōzu) = 三日 (mikka, "three days") + 坊主 (bōzu, "bonze, Buddhist
> priest") — literally "a three-day bonze," idiomatically "someone who can't stick to a
> resolution." The compositional parts are transparent, but the idiomatic extension (someone
> who enters the priesthood only to abandon it after three days, used generally for any
> abandoned resolution) is not recoverable from the parts alone without the author's cultural
> gloss (p.37).

> **天下り** (ama-kudari) = 天 (ama-, "heaven") + 下り (kudari, "descending, going down") —
> literally "descending from heaven," referring in Shinto myth to a deity's descent to found
> Japan, but used in contemporary colloquial register for retiring bureaucrats who move into
> cushy private-sector positions on the strength of their former office. A clear case of a
> mythological compound's modern meaning diverging sharply from (while still echoing) its
> literal/etymological sense (p.38).

## Grammar points

### *-shugi*, *-kyō*, and *-ha* as the chapter's three organizing compound-formants

The chapter opens by introducing three bound Sino-Japanese elements used productively to name
"international" intellectual/political/religious categories: 主義 *shugi* "principle, -ism"
(attaches to a preceding element to name an ideology, stance, or life-philosophy), 教 *kyō*
"teaching" (names a religion, typically as the final element — モルモン教 "Mormonism," 道教
"Taoism" — with the striking exception that Japan's own native religion, Shinto, is not so named),
and 派 *ha* "faction, sect" (names a subgroup/school within a larger religious, political, or
intellectual movement, and itself further combines with 学 *gaku* "study" to yield *-gakuha*
"school of [philosopher]," e.g. プラトン学派 "Platonists") (pp. 25, 28–29, 31).

### *-shugi* vs. *ron*: ideology/life-style vs. theory-in-general

De Wolf draws an explicit boundary with Chapter 1's 論 *ron*: where a term denotes a stance that
shades into ideology or personal life-style (optimism, vegetarianism, individualism), *-shugi* is
the more natural choice; *ron* remains more appropriate for a theory considered more abstractly as
an intellectual position (e.g. the -isms of theism/atheism covered in Chapter 1) (p.26). *Shugi* is
explicitly NOT used, however, to describe physical/psychological ailments (astigmatism, alcoholism)
or specific actions/activities (baptism, hypnotism, plagiarism) — these take unrelated native or
loanword terms instead, and in a specifically linguistic sense ("-ism" as in "Anglicism"), 語法
*-gohō* is used rather than *-shugi* (p.27).

### Two parallel reading systems (on/kun) surfacing in religious vocabulary as meaning-bearing contrasts

Several entries in this chapter show the same kanji taking a different reading depending on
register/context, with the reading choice itself carrying meaning rather than being free variation:
縁 *en* (Sino-Japanese/*on*-reading, used in compounds like 縁談 "marriage proposal," 縁切り
"cutting ties") versus 縁 *yukari* (native/*kun*-reading, used for "a relative, someone with
ties")  (p.35); similarly 力 read as go-style *riki* in Buddhist compounds like 自力 *jiriki*
"self-power" versus the semantically equivalent native-reading-flavored 独力 *dokuryoku* "lone
power" (p.33); and 仏 read as *futsu* (referring now exclusively to France, as in 仏文学
"French literature") versus *butsu* (the Buddha) versus the native-origin reading *hotoke* (also
"Buddha," but with an additional colloquial/euphemistic sense referring to the recently deceased)
(p.36–37).

### Figurative/idiomatic extension from a religious literal sense to a secularized colloquial one

A recurring pattern the chapter calls out explicitly: a term with a precise traditional Buddhist or
Shinto technical sense develops a broader, often ironic or purely secular colloquial extension — 世俗化
*sezoku-ka* "secularization" (dictionary sense) vs. its more common use for any popularization of
a once-elite institution/custom (p.32); 初詣 *hatsu-mōde* "first shrine visit" extending to any
repeated appeal for aid (via お百度参り *ohyakudo-mairi* and お百度を踏む *ohyakudo o fumu*)
(p.38); and 天下り *ama-kudari* "descent from heaven" (Shinto myth) extending to the specific
modern social pattern of retiring bureaucrats moving into private-sector sinecures (p.38). The
author treats this literal-to-secularized-colloquial drift as one of the most productive sources of
vivid, culturally loaded vocabulary in the traditional-religion portion of the chapter.

## Grammar points

### Verbs for daily activities (-masu paradigm expansion)
Unit 7 supplies the core verb set for narrating a daily routine: okimasu (wake up), nemasu
(sleep), tabemasu (eat), nomimasu (drink), mimasu (see/watch), kikimasu (listen), kakimasu
(write), yomimasu (read), kaimasu (buy), shimasu (do) — all inflecting via the same -masu paradigm
introduced in Unit 6. p. 81.

### Noun + shimasu compound verbs
Certain nouns (benkyō "study", ryōri "cooking", shigoto "work", kaimono "shopping", unten
"driving") combine with shimasu "to do" as a light verb to form a verb meaning "to do [noun]"
(benkyō shimasu "to study", unten shimasu "to drive") — a highly productive Japanese
verb-formation pattern worth flagging for the mechanics-analysis phase as a recurring
derivational template. p. 81.

### Particle o: object marker
o marks the direct object of a transitive verb, inserted in the slot noun1(person)-wa
noun2(object)-o verb; object + marker typically sit just before the verb (Watashi wa maiasa ringo
o tabemasu "I eat an apple every morning"). The interrogative nani "what?" substitutes for the
object noun. Spelling note: the particle is written を, distinct from the vowel お despite
identical pronunciation. p. 82.

### Particle ni: time marker
ni attaches to a specific time-related word to mark "at/on/in [time]" (gozen 7-ji ni okimasu "I
wake up at 7 a.m."). It is optional for some time words (days of the week, holidays) and
**cannot** attach to a closed set of relative/deictic time words: kyō "today", senshū "last week",
rainen "next year", mainichi "every day" — an arbitrary-looking but fixed exception list worth
flagging for downstream slang/grammar modeling. p. 83-84.

### Flexible word order
Japanese word order is flexible provided the sentence's predicate (verb, adjective, etc.) stays
sentence-final; multiple orderings of subject/time/object are all grammatical as long as this
head-final constraint holds. Demonstrated with three valid orderings of the same "I studied
Japanese from 10 to 1 yesterday" sentence. p. 84.

### Interrogative + mo: negative-indefinite construction
Attaching mo to an interrogative word negates/generalizes it: nani+mo = nanimo "nothing", doko+mo
= dokomo "nowhere", dare+mo = daremo "no one" — the exception is itsumo "always" (itsu+mo does NOT
follow this negative pattern). The object marker o is dropped before these forms, but the direction
marker e may still co-occur (dokoemo). p. 85.

### Particle de (1): instrument / means of expression
Building on Unit 6's transport-de, de also marks a tool/instrument used to perform an action
(ohashi de gohan o tabemasu "I eat meals with chopsticks") and the language in which something is
expressed, in the pattern "X" wa [language] de nan desu ka "what is X in [language]?" (e.g.
"Lunch" wa Nihon-go de nan desu ka → "Hiru-gohan" desu). p. 86-87.

### Particle de (2): place-of-action marker
A second, distinct de function marks the location where an action takes place (noun1-wa noun2-de
noun3-o verb: Watashi wa kyō resutoran de ban-gohan o tabemasu "I will eat dinner at a restaurant
today"). The interrogative is doko de "where?". Explicitly distinguished in-text from the
direction marker e (Unit 6) — de marks where an action happens, e marks a destination moved
toward. This is now the third distinct grammatical function recorded for de across Units 6-7
(transport-means, instrument/language, location-of-action), a particle-polysemy pattern worth
tracking for the mechanics-analysis phase. p. 87-88.

### Frequency adverbs
A six-term frequency scale: itsumo (always) > yoku (often) > tokidoki (sometimes) > tamani
(occasionally) > amari (not very often) > zenzen (never/not at all). amari and zenzen obligatorily
co-occur with a negative-form verb (Watashi wa zenzen hon o yomimasen "I never read books") — an
agreement requirement, not just a semantic tendency. p. 89.

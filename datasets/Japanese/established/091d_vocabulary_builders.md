# Japanese — Established Vocabulary: Vocabulary Builders, Book 1 (How to
Sound Intelligent in Japanese), Chapter 4

**Source:** Charles De Wolf, *How to Sound Intelligent in Japanese: A Vocabulary Builder* (Kodansha
International, 1st ed., 1993), Chapter 4 "The Fine Arts, Humanities, and Social Sciences," pp.
53–72.

**Coverage note:** This source has no text layer (`pdftotext` returns nothing on content pages;
confirmed scanned Adobe-Acrobat-Image-Conversion PDF) and was vision-read from rendered page
images (`pdftoppm -png -r 130`). **Gotcha confirmed (same as Chapter 1):** the PDF is scanned
two-printed-pages-per-image, a true spread per PDF page — `printed_page_left = 2×pdf_page − 2`,
`printed_page_right = 2×pdf_page − 1`. Verified empirically by rendering PDF pages 26–38: PDF page
27 (right half) shows printed p.53 and the "CHAPTER IV" heading; PDF page 37 (right half) shows
printed p.73 and "CHAPTER V" ("Science and Technology"), confirming Chapter 4 runs printed pp.
53–72 exactly as assigned. All 11 rendered spreads (PDF pages 27–37) were read in full. **No
handwritten marginalia was found anywhere in this chapter** — all content inspected is clean
typeset print; this was actively checked per page, not assumed.

**Sampling disclosure:** This is the book's largest chapter (22 printed pages vs. ~14–16 for
others) and is organized as a sequence of dense, semi-tabular vocabulary lists (not prose-heavy
like Chapter 1) covering six liberal-arts fields — fine arts, music, literature, linguistics,
history, and sociology — each introduced by a short prose passage and then enumerated in list
form, with a smaller number of numbered example sentences interspersed (sentences 1–41) than in
Chapter 1's proportionally denser prose chapters. Given the sheer listed-item volume, **every
distinct vocabulary item in every list was extracted** (this chapter is essentially the coverage
rule's "every distinct vocabulary item" case taken to its fullest), but **most of the 41 numbered
example sentences are not individually translated/reproduced here** — only a handful illustrating
a genuinely new point (a minimal pair, an idiom, a notable fact) are paraphrased in the Notes
column or Grammar points section, consistent with Chapter 1's practice and the coverage rule's
"skip straight to the next new vocabulary item" allowance for an unusually dense chunk.

**Morpheme breakdown.**

> **考古学的/catchall triad 術・学・芸** (jutsu/gaku/gei): the chapter's organizing thesis is that
> these three roots partition the Greek *techne* concept into practice (*jutsu*), theory/knowledge
> (*gaku*), and craft/trade-skill (*gei*) — e.g. 医術 *ijutsu* "the practice of medicine" vs. 医学
> *igaku* "medicine [the discipline]" name the same domain from the practice side vs. the
> knowledge side respectively, and modern Japanese speakers show a felt preference for being
> described by the *gaku*/*dō* ("way") member of a near-synonym pair over the *jutsu* member (p.54:
> doctors, judo practitioners).

> **嬰ハ短調** (ei-ha-tanchō) = 嬰 (ei, "sharp") + ハ (ha, the Japanese note-name for "C," from the
> iroha solmization system) + 短調 (tanchō, "minor key" ← 短 tan "short" + 調 chō "key/mode") —
> "C-sharp minor." Illustrates how the older イロハ note-naming system (parallel to do-re-mi) still
> productively combines with Sino-Japanese modifiers for sharp/flat/major/minor to name any key
> (p.62).

> **中流の上層階級** (chūryū no jōsō-kaikyū) = 中流 (chūryū, "middle stream/class") + の (no,
> genitive particle) + 上層 (jōsō, "upper layer/stratum") + 階級 (kaikyū, "class") — "upper middle
> class," one of a five-way socioeconomic-stratum lattice the author builds compositionally from
> 上/中/下 (upper/middle/lower) × 流/層 (stream/stratum) × 階級 (class), rather than giving each
> stratum an unanalyzable name (p.71).

## Grammar points

### 術・学・芸 (jutsu/gaku/gei) as the chapter's three organizing roots

De Wolf opens the chapter by presenting 術 *jutsu* (technique/practice), 学 *gaku*
(study/knowledge), and 芸 *gei* (craft/trade-skill) as a three-way partition of the semantic space
covered by a single Greek word, *techne*. Each attaches productively as either the initial or final
element of a compound (催眠術 *saiminjutsu* "hypnotism," 美術 *bijutsu* "fine art," 芸術 *geijutsu*
"the arts"), and a common nominal-agent suffix 者 *-sha* converts any *-gaku* discipline name into
its practitioner (philosopher, linguist, historian, etc., p.58). The author further splits the
chapter's scope along a 文系/理系 (*bunkei*/*rikei*, humanities-social-sciences vs. natural
sciences) axis that also organizes the following Chapter 5 (p.53).

### Dense -gaku "-ology" suffixation across academic disciplines

A single productive pattern — root noun + 学 (*-gaku*) — generates the names of essentially every
academic discipline treated in the chapter (哲学, 文学→文学 is unsuffixed but 言語学, 心理学, 人類学,
歴史学, 法学, 経済学, 政治学, 工学, 生物学, 植物学, 動物学, 天文学, 地質学, 地理学, 医学, etc.,
pp.57–58). The author notes this suffixation is maximally regular and productive — essentially any
field name can take -*gaku* — in contrast to the *ron*/*ri*/*setsu* compounds of Chapter 1, whose
combinatorics were idiosyncratic and had to be learned pairwise.

### Phonological-process terminology as a self-contained mini-glossary

Uniquely among the chapters surveyed so far, pp.65–66 present a compact, systematic glossary of
general phonological-process terms (同化 *dōka* assimilation, 異化 *ika* dissimilation, 削除
*sakujo* deletion, 語中音挿入 *gochū-on-sōnyū* epenthesis, 剰音 *jō'on* excrescence, 語頭音添加
*gotō'on-tenka* prothesis, 音韻転換 *on'in-tenkan* metathesis) each paired with a worked example
from Japanese, English, Spanish, or Latin — this is the author using the linguistics subsection
self-referentially to teach both the Japanese metalanguage term and the underlying concept
simultaneously, a didactic move not used elsewhere in the book so far.

### Classical vs. modern Japanese verb-form terminology (katsuyō paradigm)

p.67 gives the standard six-way traditional Japanese verb-conjugation-form paradigm (未然形, 連用形,
終止形, 連体形, 仮定形, 命令形) with a worked example each on the root *ku-* "eat," then notes
(sentence 35, p.68) that Old/Classical Japanese had a seventh, now-merged distinction: 已然形
*izen-kei* ("perfective") was formally distinct from the modern 仮定形 ("hypothetical") where
Modern Japanese collapses both into one -*eba* form — the predicative (終止形) and attributive
(連体形) forms of a verb could likewise differ in Old Japanese where they are identical today
(example: 紅葉が落ちる "momiji ga ochiru" vs. 落ちる紅葉 "ochiru momiji," both *ochiru* in Modern
Japanese but *otsu* vs. *otsuru* in Old Japanese).

### Compositional socioeconomic/ethnographic terminology lattices

The sociology subsection (pp.70–72) builds several terms compositionally from a small set of
reusable morphemes rather than listing unanalyzable vocabulary: the social-class lattice (上流/
中流/下層 × 階級), the ethnicity/nation lattice (多/単一 × 民族 × 国家), and the family-structure
pair (拡大/核 × 家族) are all presented as productive X+階級/国家/家族 formulas rather than a flat
list — consistent with the compositional, root-combination teaching style the book uses throughout.

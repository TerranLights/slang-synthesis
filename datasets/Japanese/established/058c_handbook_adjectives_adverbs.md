# Japanese — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: The Handbook of Japanese Adjectives and Adverbs — Adverbs (Introduction & Usage, Sections 1–9)

**Source:** Taeko Kamiya, *The Handbook of Japanese Adjectives and Adverbs* (Kodansha International,
first edition, 2002), pp. 121–288 (Adverbs: Introduction — Kinds of Adverbs, Word Modifiers,
Onomatopoeic Words, Special Expressions, Adverbs Used as Verbs, Adverbial Position, Other Words Used
as Adverbs; and Usage of Adverbs sections 1–9: Time, Quantity, Degree, Circumstance, Onomatopoeic
Words, Negatives, Different Meanings in Positive/Negative Expressions, Interrogative/Conditional,
Desire/Conjecture/Resemblance).

**Coverage note:** Continues from `058b_handbook_adjectives_adverbs.md`. This is the largest part of
the book (~167 printed pages). Like Part II, it is built from a dense sequence of individually boxed
single-adverb entries (e.g. "1.4 shikiri ni"), each with a short explanation and 1–3 example
sentences, grouped under 9 numbered usage chapters by semantic category (time, quantity, degree,
etc.) — in effect a semantically-organized dictionary of adverbs rather than a flat alphabetical one.
Given the sheer entry count (chapter 1 alone runs 1.1–1.20+; chapter 5, onomatopoeia, runs past 5.42),
this file uses **representative sampling**: every one of the 9 usage chapters plus the introductory
material was sampled at multiple points, capturing the boxed-entry vocabulary actually read rather
than every entry in every chapter. This is consistent with the extraction spec's sampling guidance for
large, densely itemized reference content. Entries not directly sampled (the large majority of chapter
5's onomatopoeic inventory in particular, which likely runs to 60+ items between pp. 214–257) are not
claimed as covered.

**Vision-reading note:** Same two-pages-per-spread DJVU scan as sibling files. No handwritten

## Grammar points

### Kinds of Adverbs: two functional classes (p. 121)
Japanese adverbs are non-conjugating words that modify other words. They split functionally into
(a) adverbs that modify verbs, adjectives, other adverbs, and certain nouns, and (b) adverbs reserved
for "special expressions" that co-occur with negatives, interrogatives, conditionals, and similar
constructions (e.g. kesshite obligatorily pairs with a negative predicate).

### Word Modifiers grouped by semantic concept (p. 122)
Adverbs belonging to class (a) above are further grouped by the concept they express: time (itsumo),
quantity (takusan), degree (totemo), and circumstance (hakkiri, zokuzoku) are the categories the book
uses to organize its entire Usage-of-Adverbs section (chapters 1–4 directly match this taxonomy,
chapter 5 — onomatopoeia — is presented as a special case of the circumstance category).

### Onomatopoeic words: giseigo vs. gitaigo (p. 123)
Japanese onomatopoeia splits into two types: giseigo, which literally imitates natural/mechanical
sounds (zāzā, "sound of pouring rain"), and gitaigo, which describes actions, manners, or states
rather than literal sound (nikoniko, "smiling happily"). Both function grammatically as adverbs in
Japanese. The book notes that English has onomatopoeic words too, but they tend to surface as verbs
("murmur," "scuttle," "screech") rather than adverbs — a typological asymmetry between the two
languages' onomatopoeia systems.

### Special Expressions: adverbs keyed to negative/interrogative/conditional/desire/conjecture/resemblance context (p. 124)
A second major functional class of adverb exists purely to co-occur with a specific sentence type:
negative (sappari), interrogative (naze), conditional (moshi), desire (zehi), conjecture (tabun +
darō), and resemblance (marude + yō ni). This taxonomy directly previews Usage-of-Adverbs chapters
6–9.

### Adverbs used as verbs via + suru (p. 125)
Adverbs expressing manner of action or human emotion — especially gitaigo-type ones — commonly combine
with the verb suru to form a full verb (iraira suru, "to feel irritated"). This is a highly productive
pattern seen repeatedly across the gitaigo entries sampled in chapter 5 (hotto suru, gakkari suru,
mutto suru, etc.).

### Adverbial position: free but constrained by complexity (p. 125)
An adverb's position in a sentence is relatively free as long as it precedes the word it modifies — a
simple sentence can place the adverb sentence-initially or immediately pre-verbal with no change in
meaning (mō sugu example). However, in a sentence of any real complexity, the book advises placing the
adverb close to the word it actually modifies, illustrated with a case where kesshite misplaced before
the wrong clause produces an ungrammatical/incorrect reading, while placing it immediately before the
negative verb it governs is correct.

### Other words used as adverbs: adjective adverbial forms, time/number nouns, and verb te-forms (p. 126)
Beyond dedicated adverbs, three other word classes can function adverbially without any derivational
marking: (1) adjectives in their adverbial form (already covered in Part II.6); (2) certain nouns that
express time or number, used directly before a verb with no particle (kesa hayaku okita; ringo o
mittsu kudasai; gakusei ga gonin kita); and (3) verbs in the te form (sofa ni nete hon o yomimasu,
"lying on a sofa, I read a book").

### Usage of Adverbs 1 — Time (pp. 128–155, sampled)
A long catalog of individually boxed time adverbs distinguished by subtle differences in frequency,
formality, and connotation that a learner could easily conflate: tabitabi (casual-conversation
register) vs. shibashiba (its more neutral/interchangeable counterpart); tokidoki (intermittent);
yagate (more formal than sono uchi ni, and able to imply causation, shifting to "at long last" with a
past-tense final verb) vs. mamonaku (shortly, or "not long after"); shikiri ni (continuous action,
constancy, or eagerness depending on context).

### Usage of Adverbs 2 — Quantity (pp. 156–172, sampled)
minna (colloquial) vs. mina (written) both mean "all" and are drawn from the same root; subete
("all," more formal/written, usually interchangeable with mina) and takusan ("many/much/excessive")
round out the chapter's core quantity vocabulary, alongside a cluster of approximation adverbs (hobo,
yaku, taitei, zatto, daitai, ōyoso) whose fine shades of "approximately/mostly/usually" the Practice
exercises drill directly against each other.

### Usage of Adverbs 3 — Degree (pp. 173–184, sampled)
taihen and zuibun both mean "very/extremely," but zuibun is explicitly marked as primarily casual-
conversational register, while taihen carries no such restriction. The book explicitly notes this
chapter overlaps with the Quantity chapter, since many degree adverbs also describe amount.

### Usage of Adverbs 4 — Circumstance (pp. 185–213, sampled)
Adverbs describing manner of action, human emotion, states, or situations; the book notes that some
circumstance adverbs optionally take particle to (yukkuri (to)) or ni (tsugitsugi (ni)), and that many
combine with suru to form verbs — directly linking this chapter back to the Introduction's "Adverbs
Used as Verbs" section. kitchiri and pittari ("exactly/perfectly") are flagged as only partially
interchangeable — correct for one example context (a calculation being exactly right) but not another
(a jacket fitting perfectly) — a fine distributional distinction the book draws out explicitly rather
than treating the two as free variants.

### Usage of Adverbs 5 — Onomatopoeic Words (pp. 214–257, sampled)
The chapter catalogs both giseigo and gitaigo items (running at least to entry 5.42 in the sampled
pages) covering registers of eating manner (gatsugatsu "ravenously" vs. morimori "heartily" vs.
pekopeko "starving" — three distinct manners around the single concept of hunger/eating), speech
manner (hakihaki "briskly" vs. perapera "fluently" vs. berabera/pechakucha, both glossed toward
"chattering on" with a mildly negative/colloquial connotation implied by the English glosses given,
vs. hisohiso "in whispers"), and emotional/physical states (ikiiki "full of life," nobinobi "at ease,"
dokidoki "heart pounding"). burabura is noted as carrying three distinct senses (dangling,
aimless-leisurely-movement, and idling one's time away) unified by a common thread of
purposelessness/looseness.

### Usage of Adverbs 6 — Adverbs Used with Negatives (pp. 258–266, sampled)
sukoshi mo and chittomo both mean "not at all" in the form [adverb] ... nai, but chittomo is explicitly
flagged as more colloquial than sukoshi mo; sappari (also "not at all") is likewise more colloquial
than sukoshi mo and additionally implies the situation described is unfavorable — a three-way register/
connotation distinction among near-synonyms the book draws out point by point rather than listing them
as interchangeable.

### Usage of Adverbs 7 — Adverbs with Different Meanings in Positive and Negative Expressions (pp. 267–273, sampled)
This chapter's organizing idea: a single adverb can carry two distinct, non-overlapping meanings
depending on whether the sentence it appears in is affirmative or negative. mada means "still"
(continuation) in a positive sentence but "not yet" (incompletion) in a negative one; mō means
"already" (completion) in a positive sentence but "(not) anymore" (cessation) in a negative one. This
is presented as a systematic pattern, not a coincidence specific to mada/mō — issai, betsu ni,
hotondo, and nakanaka are drilled in the chapter's practice set on the same principle.

### Usage of Adverbs 8 — Interrogative Adverbs and Adverbs Used with Conditionals (pp. 274–279, sampled)
dō ("how") inquires about a state, a manner of doing something, or is used to elicit an opinion or
make an offer; ikaga is its polite-register counterpart, used specifically in polite speech for the
same three functions (inquiring after someone's well-being, asking how something is, or politely
offering something).

### Usage of Adverbs 9 — Adverbs Expressing Desire, Conjecture, or Resemblance (pp. 280–288, sampled)
dōzo ("please," offering something or politely requesting an action) and dōka ("please," used to ask a
favor in a more emphatic register than dōzo) form a near-minimal register pair; zehi expresses a
person's own strong desire to do something, distinct from dōzo/dōka's function of making a request of
someone else.

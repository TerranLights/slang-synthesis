## Grammar points

### The ⒻⓂ gender-marking convention

The translator explicitly marks, with circled-letter symbols Ⓕ and Ⓜ immediately after a romanized
line, any expression "considered exclusively female or exclusively male" in Japanese speech (p.37,
Preface). Every Ⓕ-marked example found in the sampled pages used a sentence-final particle
associated with feminine registers (*-wa*, *-no*, *-no yo*) attached to otherwise plain/blunt verb
forms — e.g. *Mō anata ni nanka tanomanai wa* ("That's the last time I ask you for anything," p.120)
versus a hypothetical genderless equivalent lacking the trailing *wa*. No Ⓜ-marked example appeared
in the sampled pages, but the convention is symmetric per the preface. This is a flagship structural
feature distinct from the honorific/politeness system below — it marks *speaker gender*, not social
rank or formality level.

### Rank-asymmetric honorific pairs (the otsukare-sama / gokurō-sama minimal pair)

A recurring pattern across the sampled business-register pages: Japanese offers near-synonymous
phrase *pairs* for the same communicative function, where only one member of the pair is usable in a
given direction of address. *Otsukare-sama (deshita)* ("your hard work is appreciated") can be said
by anyone to anyone, including a junior employee addressing a senior one. *Gokurō-sama (deshita)*
("well done") covers the identical situation but is restricted to a senior/older speaker addressing a
junior/younger one — using it in the reverse direction is explicitly flagged (p.261) as a breach of
office etiquette. This is presented as a clean, minimal illustration of how Japanese politeness
encodes *relative* social rank into otherwise semantically-interchangeable vocabulary choices, rather
than encoding formality as a single linear scale.

### Indirect refusal via a trailing, unresolved "ga" (but...)

Several sampled business-phrase entries (p.217: *Kekkō na ohanashi to wa omoimasu ga...*, *Oyaku ni
tachitai no wa yamayama desu ga...*) end on the conjunction *ga* ("but") with the actual objection
left unstated. The book explicitly frames this as *the* conventional way to communicate a refusal
without ever stating it outright — "drop a few hints and let the person getting the bad news fill in
the rest" (p.217) — contrasted with a flat refusal (*ainiku desu ga, konkai wa miokurasete
itadakimasu*, "sorry, but I'll pass this time," p.216) which is also available but socially heavier.
*Chotto muzukashii desu ne* ("that would be difficult," p.216) is presented as the maximally
noncommittal version of the same strategy — a hedge phrase that functions as a definite "no" through
shared cultural convention rather than its literal semantic content.

### The set-phrase genre itself as the book's organizing idea

The preface (pp. 32–33) opens with a parable about monks at a meal who communicate only via a fixed
numerical code standing in for pre-agreed phrases, framing this as "an apt metaphor" for how native
Japanese speakers lean on "a communal stockpile of orthodox expressions" for routine social
situations — the book's entire structure (phrases organized by life/business situation rather than by
grammatical category) follows directly from this premise. This is worth recording as a structural/
typological observation about the language's social-script conventions, distinct from any single
grammar rule: fluency in these situations is explicitly treated by the source as a matter of knowing
and correctly deploying the *right fixed phrase* for the situation (with register/formality tailored
to context), not of generating novel sentences from first principles.

## Grammar points

### A list of Japanese parts of speech (Hashimoto/Kokugo-grammar taxonomy)

The book organizes its entire structure around parts of speech rather than the lesson-graded
structure typical of textbooks (explicitly stated as a deliberate, atypical choice, Preface p. vii).
It presents the ten-way native Japanese school-grammar (Hashimoto, also called Kokugo grammar)
classification, with modifications for foreign-learner terminology (e.g. discussing verb "stems" not
found in the native tradition). Independent words: 動詞 (verb), 形容詞/い形容詞 (い-adjective),
形容動詞/な形容詞 (な-adjective, treated as conjugating via だ), 名詞 (noun, joined to other nouns
via の and not conjugating), 数詞 (counter/numeral expressions), 副詞 (adverb, used before
verbs/adjectives or to introduce certain phrases), 連体詞 (a class with no English equivalent — only
ever modifies a following noun and never changes form, e.g. この, あの, いわゆる, あらゆる),
接続詞 (conjunction, links sentences without changing form), 感動詞/間投詞 (exclamation/
interjection). Dependent words (cannot stand alone): 助動詞 (auxiliary — conjugating dependent
elements like ~ます, ~ない, ~(ら)れる, ~(さ)せる, ~だ・です, ~らしい) and 助詞 (particle — marks
grammatical roles, never changes form). The dictionary form of the verb is explicitly taken as the
basic "word" form for citation purposes (p. 3, footnote).

### Topic, comment, and predicate

The topic/comment framework: Japanese sentences often open with a stated topic (marked は, pronounced
*wa*), followed by a comment about that topic. は "hides" the subject marker が when a topic and the
grammatical subject coincide (あつみさんは がくせいです — "Atsumi is a student," where あつみさん is
both topic and subject). The topic/subject distinction is illustrated with the minimal pair 寿司は
おいしい ("Sushi: it's delicious" — topic, already under discussion) vs. 寿司が おいしい ("Sushi is
delicious" — a general statement introducing new information). The predicate is the part of the
sentence giving information about the grammatical subject; verbs, adjectives, and nouns+だ・です can
all form predicates. Other topic markers mentioned (cross-referenced to the Particles chapter, not
elaborated here): なら and って.

### In-group and out-group

Japanese family-relationship vocabulary systematically distinguishes "my own family" terms from
"your/other person's family" terms — not mere possessive marking, but two genuinely distinct lexical
sets (e.g. 母/はは for one's own mother vs. お母さん/おかあさん for someone else's, or when directly
addressing one's own). The source flags a directionality asymmetry: relatives and older siblings
(though not younger ones) are normally addressed directly using the "other person's family"
(out-group-style, more polite) terms even within one's own family, and older members may also refer
to *themselves* using those same polite terms when speaking to younger family members (paralleled to
English baby-talk like "Let Mummy kiss it better"). The source also notes some of these terms
generalize beyond literal kinship to generic address of strangers by apparent age/gender/role
(おじさん → any older man, おばさん → "lady," おくさん → "madam," お姉さん → young woman/"waitress").
This in-group/out-group asymmetry is cross-referenced forward to keigo (p. 213), verbs of giving and
receiving (p. 94), and the ~て form (p. 47) as the same underlying social-deixis mechanism recurring
across the grammar.

### Style — the polite/plain axis and four named registers

Japanese verbs, adjectives, and だ・です each have polite-style and plain-style paradigms. The
dictionary form (citation form found in dictionaries) is the plain non-past form, not the ~ます form
most learners meet first (~ます is itself derived from the dictionary form via a stem). The chapter
tabulates all four forms (non-past/past/negative/past-negative) of polite vs. plain for いきます/いく
as the canonical example. **Key rule, explicitly generalized:** within a single complex/compound
sentence, only the sentence-final verb's politeness level sets the whole sentence's register — any
verb or clause appearing mid-sentence (a "modifier") must be in the plain style regardless of the
sentence's overall polite/plain register; if two polite-looking (~ます-ending) verbs appear to occur
in one sentence, it is actually two sentences joined by a conjunction. The chapter then names and
distinguishes four written/spoken registers built on this base polite/plain axis: **desu-masu-kei
(デス・マス形, polite style forms collectively)**, **futsūkei (普通形, plain style forms
collectively)**, and, within running text, three named prose-register labels — **desu-masu style**
(spoken, letters, and keigo), **da style** (informal spoken/written), and **de-aru style** (formal
newspaper/factual writing, where である substitutes for だ or です as the copula). This explicit
four-register naming (desu-masu-kei/futsūkei as the form-level dichotomy, crossed with
da/de-aru/keigo as the discourse-register dichotomy) is this book's own distinctive organizing
framework and is not found tabulated this way in prior Japanese `established/` extractions.

### だ・です (the copula): conjugation and usage

だ・です is explicitly classified not as a verb but as a 助動詞 (jodōshi/auxiliary) that conjugates
like other auxiliaries. The full conjugation chart (plain / polite / literary-style columns) is
reproduced in condensed form: positive だ/です/である; negative ではない or じゃない / ではないです
or じゃないです or ではありません or じゃありません / ではない; past だった/でした/であった; past
negative ではなかった or じゃなかった / ではなかったです or じゃなかったです or ではありませんでした
or じゃありませんでした / であった — wait, the past-negative literary form given is であった (the
source's chart shows であった twice — once for past, once reused contextually for past negative
contexts via である's own negation); tentative だろう/でしょう/であろう. な is identified as the
specific form of だ・です used when joining a noun to a following noun (attributive position) or to
the nominalizer の — this is why な-adjectives are sometimes called "na-adjectives." じゃ is
identified explicitly as the contracted form of では, more common in speech; では is more common in
writing. だろう・でしょう marks conjecture/tentativeness (not always translatable as "probably") and
can also function as a polite "isn't it?"/tag-question equivalent of だ・です, seeking the listener's
agreement. だ is noted as sometimes omissible: in newspaper-headline style when the sentence can stand
alone, and in casual conversational question/answer exchanges (土曜日暇？ うん、暇よ — omitting だ/
だよ entirely).

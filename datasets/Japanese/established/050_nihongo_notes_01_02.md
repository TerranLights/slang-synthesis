# Japanese — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Nihongo Notes 1 & 2 (Speaking and Living in Japan / Expressing Oneself in Japanese)

**Source:** Osamu Mizutani & Nobuko Mizutani, *Nihongo Notes 1: Speaking and Living in Japan* (The
Japan Times, 1st ed. December 1977, this copy 23rd printing April 1992) and *Nihongo Notes 2:
Expressing Oneself in Japanese* (The Japan Times, ~1979). First pair in the 10-volume *Nihongo
Notes* series (sibling file `established/054_nihongo_notes_09_10.md` covers vols. 9-10). Vol. 1
collects 70 short columns ("Nihongo Notes") originally run in *The Japan Times* Aug. 1976–Nov. 1977,
plus a "Common Expressions Used in Daily Life" list; vol. 2 continues the same column format.

**Coverage note:** No text layer in either PDF (`pdftotext` returns empty output); both volumes
vision-read from rendered page images (150dpi `pdftoppm`). As with vols. 9-10, each PDF page image is
a two-printed-page spread, not one page per image — verified empirically: image *i* → printed pages
`2i-2` (left) / `2i-1` (right) once front matter is accounted for (vol. 1: image 1 = title page
recto only / unnumbered verso; image 2 = pp. 2-3; content proper runs pp. 8-146, 70 columns at
~2 pages each, then "Common Expressions Used in Daily Life" pp. 148-157, then a word/phrase index
pp. 158+ which is not independently extracted here — it indexes columns already captured). Format
differs from vols. 9-10: these early volumes are narrative-pragmatics essays (an anecdote involving
recurring characters Mr. Ernest Lerner, a foreign resident, and various Japanese acquaintances),
each built around one or a handful of idiomatic expressions, followed by a paraphrased
explanation/analysis — not grammar-paradigm lessons. Every column's usage point and every distinct
illustrative Japanese expression is captured; narrative anecdote framing is paraphrased/summarized,
never reproduced at length, per copyright discipline. Short illustrative Japanese phrases (the
columns' own boxed example lines) are quoted directly since they are brief (1 line) and are the
specific lexical/pragmatic content this project needs.

**Vision-reading guard note:** Vol. 1's title page (image 1, recto) carries a handwritten library
date stamp ("MAR 15 1999") in the upper-right margin — clearly a stamp, not typeset source content.
Excluded entirely, flagged here per the spec's marginalia-guard requirement. No other handwritten
marginalia observed in the pages read so far.

## Grammar points

### Dochira-e? as phatic greeting, not an information request
A greeting of the form "Where are you going?" is conventional small talk expressing goodwill/concern
for the addressee's well-being, parallel in function to English "How are you?" — it does not require
or expect a literal, specific answer; a vague reply (*Ee, chotto soko-made*, "just down the street")
is the socially correct response, and supplying a precise destination (as a learner might, taking the
question literally) reads as oddly specific rather than more polite. (Vol.1 p.8-9)

### Iie is avoided except where it carries no interpersonal risk
Japanese resist bare "no" because declining another person's stated opinion, wish, or request is felt
as a quasi-personal denial that can damage the relationship. *Iie* is said readily only when (a) no
one's standing is at stake (e.g. correcting a stranger's directions: *Iie, chigaimasu*) or (b) it
denies the speaker's *own* merit, which Japanese are comfortable negating (e.g. deflecting a
compliment with *Iie, sonna koto-wa arimasen*). (Vol.1 p.10-11)

### -tai is too direct for asking about someone else's wish
The desiderative suffix *-tai* ("want to") is fine for stating one's own wish but is felt as
improper when used to ask what someone *else* wants, because a person's private wish is treated as
not a legitimate topic to probe directly — politer phrasing avoids naming the listener's desire at
all (e.g. offering tea with *Ocha-demo ikaga-desu-ka* rather than asking *Ocha-o nomitai-desu-ka*,
"do you want to drink tea?"). (Vol.1 p.12-13)

### Aizuchi: listening is co-constructive, not silent
Short backchannel tokens (aizuchi) are expected at frequent intervals whenever someone else is
speaking; the listener who stays silent is read as inattentive or as thinking poorly of the speaker's
Japanese, not as being politely non-interruptive (the Western norm). Speaker and listener are treated
as jointly producing one continuous flow of speech. (Vol.1 p.14-15)

### Konnichiwa/Konbanwa mark out-group status; Ohayoo-gozaimasu does not
*Konnichiwa* and *Konbanwa* can only be used toward people outside one's own group (family, close
colleagues are excluded) and are not polite enough for a superior — a clerk greeting a customer uses
*Irasshaimase* instead and never returns the customer's *Konnichiwa*. *Ohayoo-gozaimasu* carries
neither restriction and can be said to anyone, in or out of group, including family. (Vol.1 p.16-17)

### -san marks respect and/or intimacy, never self-reference
*-san* attaches broadly — to surnames, first names, occupation nouns (*omawari-san*), and
relationship nouns (*otonari-san*) — but two restrictions matter: it is never attached to the
speaker's own name or their own family members' names (unlike English self-reference with a title),
and it is withheld from the very eminent unless the speaker has a personal relationship with them,
reflecting that *-san* signals closeness as much as deference. (Vol.1 p.18-19)

### Gurai/hodo/bakari as deliberate vagueness, a politeness strategy
Attaching an approximative particle to a stated quantity is a considerateness move — it leaves the
listener room to give less (or more) than the literal figure without having to refuse outright. The
essay notes this usage is receding somewhat among younger speakers, who view exact requests as more
efficient since a listener can simply decline an inconvenient request directly. (Vol.1 p.20-21)

### Ikaga-desu-ka / Doo-desu-ka are not routine greetings
Unlike English "How are you?", these literal "how are you" questions are asked only after a long
absence or following a notable life event (illness, major change) — asking them of someone met daily
reads as odd, even impolite/intrusive, since it implies an unusually personal inquiry. Routine
greetings instead reference the weather, offer thanks/apology for the last meeting (hence *doomo* can
replace "hello"), or ask *Dochira-e?*/*Odekake-desu-ka* ("going out?"), which functions as the real
equivalent of "How are you? — Fine, thanks." (Vol.1 p.24-25)

### Kedo/ga trail off, leaving the implied continuation unsaid
Sentence-final *kedo* (more colloquial) or *ga* ("but") routinely leave the implication of what
follows unstated — e.g. after *Hai, orimasu-kedo...* ("Yes, he's home, but...") the unsaid part is
"shall I call him?" Pronounced in a hesitant, trailing tone, they function as request-inviting
devices built on the premise that conversation is jointly completed by speaker and listener, not
produced by the speaker alone. (Vol.1 p.26-27)

### Anata ("you") is restricted, asymmetric, and often avoided entirely
Japanese generally avoid personal pronouns, preferring to refer to the addressee by name, title, or
role; it can be genuinely ambiguous whether a sentence speaks of "you," "he," or "she" from its form
alone. *Anata* specifically is usable top-down (teacher→student, parent→child, older→younger) but not
bottom-up, and among peers only between women of the same age (men use *kimi* or *omae* instead); even
where usable, a position/role term (*sensee*, *shachoo*, *okusan*, *okaasan*) or a personal name is
generally preferred over *anata*, especially toward someone whose position merits respect. (Vol.1
p.28-29)

### Shitsuree-(shimasu/shimashita) spans apology, thanks, and both greeting/farewell
*Shitsuree* literally means "rudeness"; *shitsuree-shimasu* ("I'm about to be rude") and
*shitsuree-shimashita* ("I was rude") both serve simultaneously as apology and as an expression of
gratitude, and extend to greeting ("excuse me," on entering) and farewell ("good-bye," on leaving) —
illustrated across all four uses with the same recurring phrase in one visit. Other formulas
(*osoreirimasu*, *sumimasen*, *warui-ne*) show the same apology/gratitude double-duty; Japanese do not
experience this as reusing one expression for unrelated purposes, since apologizing for imposing and
thanking for kindness are treated as two sides of one coin. (Vol.1 p.30-31)

### Iru vs. aru, and motte-kuru vs. tsurete-kuru, both split on animacy — but differently
*Iru* ("to be/exist") is used for every living thing, down to insects; *aru* for every inanimate
thing — a rigid binary split. *Motte-kuru* ("bring") vs. *tsurete-kuru* ("bring/lead a person or
creature") splits differently: *motte-kuru* covers all inanimate objects, but *tsurete-kuru* is not
automatic for every living thing — it presupposes the object can move under its own guidance (humans,
dogs take *tsurete-kuru*; a goldfish or bird conventionally does not, despite being alive), while even
an immobile human (a baby who can't yet walk) still takes *tsurete-kuru*, showing the distinction
tracks a notion of "guidable agent" more than literal mobility. (Vol.1 p.32-33)

### Kaeru's past-tense is a process-completion marker, not a destination marker
*Kaeru* describes the process of heading home, not having arrived; *uchi-e kaeru toki* therefore
means "while on the way home," not "once home" — to mean "once home" the past form must be used:
*uchi-e kaetta toki*. The same past-tense-as-completion pattern explains *kekkon-suru hito* ("a
person who is going to marry") vs. *kekkon-shita hito* ("a person who has married"), and why
*yaseru hito* ("a person who is getting thin[ner]") sounds like an open-ended ongoing process while
*yaseta hito* names someone already thin. (Vol.1 p.34-35)

### -ne vs. -yo: solidarity-seeking agreement vs. one-sided assertion
Sentence-final *-ne* signals the speaker's own agreement plus an expectation that the listener will
agree too, creating a feeling of shared ground — this is why weather-greetings conventionally end in
*-ne*. *-Yo* emphatically asserts the speaker's own judgment regardless of what the listener thinks,
used to tell someone something they should know/do (a mother's *Moo hachiji desu-yo*, "It's already
eight!") — overuse of *-yo* reads as aggressive/patronizing, though it is the correct choice (not
*-ne*) when reassuring someone who voiced self-doubt (*Sonna koto-wa arimasen-yo*) or a nervous
patient (*Daijoobu-desu-yo*, "Don't worry"), since *-yo* there asserts a confident counter-claim
rather than seeking agreement. (Vol.1 p.36-37)

### Kore/sore/are/dore track shared knowledge, not only physical distance
Standard textbook explanation (kore=near speaker, sore=near listener, are=far from both) is
spatially correct but incomplete: the deeper factor is whether speaker and listener share knowledge
of the referent. *Are* presupposes mutual knowledge (which need not come from sight — a shared heard
noise, or a universally-known reference like the Mona Lisa, both license *are*); when the listener
lacks that shared knowledge, *sore* is used instead regardless of physical distance. Frequency of
*are*-use in a relationship roughly tracks how much background each party shares, which is why
family members and long-married couples use it often. (Vol.1 p.40-41)

### Japanese vowel length is phonemic — sound-unit counting, not simple duration
Japanese counts a long vowel as a separate "sound unit" (mora) from a short vowel, and the moraic
nasal /ɴ/ (written *n*) likewise counts as its own unit — so *shujin* (husband, 3 units: shu-ji-n)
and *shuujin* (prisoner, 4 units: shu-u-ji-n) differ not in "how long the u is" in a continuous
sense but in unit count. English speakers, lacking this contrast, routinely produce embarrassing
minimal-pair errors (shufu "housewife" vs. shuufu "ugly woman"; ojisan "uncle" vs. ojiisan
"grandfather"; obasan "aunt" vs. obaasan "grandmother"). (Vol.1 p.42-43)

### The passive marks "the speaker was affected," with or without an agent
Japanese passive (-areru/-areta) is not restricted to true two-party passives (an agent acting on a
patient) — it also marks that an event, even an agentless one like weather or someone's death,
affected the speaker/topic. *Ame-ni furareta* ("I was inconvenienced by the rain") contrasts with the
neutral *Ame-ga futta* ("it rained"); *Akanboo-ni nakareta* ("I suffered from the baby's crying")
contrasts with the neutral *Akachan-ga naita* ("the baby cried") — same event, but the passive
version frames it from the affected speaker's point of view. (Vol.1 p.44-45)

### A reunion greeting must reference the previous meeting, not just restate thanks
It is customary (and its absence can read as rude) to open a reunion, however much later, by
explicitly referencing the previous encounter — typically thanking the other person again for a
treat/favor from that meeting (*Senjitsu-wa gochisoosama-deshita*), even if the favor was trivial or
long past. What matters is demonstrating shared memory of having met, which Japanese treat as
foundational to maintaining the relationship, more than the magnitude of the favor itself; when no
favor was exchanged, *Senjitsu-wa shitsuree-shimashita* substitutes. Source notes this is a recurring
point of cross-cultural friction with Americans who don't follow the convention. (Vol.1 p.46-47)

### Intonation can override correct wording in Japanese politeness
Stress and tone carry independent pragmatic meaning beyond the words themselves — a grammatically
correct, situationally appropriate sentence can still sound angry/irritated/overbearing if the wrong
part is stressed. The rule illustrated: stress/clarity goes on the part conveying genuinely new
information, while a part the listener can already anticipate (because context already signaled it)
is said softly with a falling tone — e.g. *koko-de* ("here") carries the new information in "please
stop here" to a taxi driver already anticipating a stop, so *koko-de* gets emphasis and
*tomete-kudasai* trails off softly; stressing *tomete-kudasai* instead reads as impatience. The same
principle governs phone requests (stress the name, not *onegai-shimasu*). This is explicitly framed
as a case where foreign speakers must deliberately avoid transferring their native language's
stress-for-emphasis habits into Japanese. (Vol.1 p.48-49)

### New Year's greetings are strictly time-gated, unlike Christmas's advance greetings
Japanese do not say "Happy New Year"-type greetings in advance of New Year's Day — there is no
custom paralleling "Merry Christmas" said days ahead. Before the day, the formulaic wish is instead
*Doozo yoi otoshi-o*, itself a fixed, single-purpose expression that cannot be generalized to other
"have a good ___" contexts; this absence of a generalizable formula for e.g. "have a good weekend/
trip" (despite calque-able alternatives existing and sounding slightly unnatural) contrasts with
*Itte-(i)rasshai*, the one genuinely all-purpose send-off for someone going out. The post-New Year's
greeting exchange runs for about two weeks and is deliberately timed — cards are mailed to land
exactly on Jan. 1, not accumulate beforehand the way Christmas cards do. (Vol.1 p.50-53)

### Itadakimasu/Gochisoosama frame gratitude toward the meal itself, not narrowly toward a host
Both formulas are directed at everything and everyone that made the meal possible, which is why they
are said even when eating alone or in one's own home, not reserved for being a guest — this surprised
Mr. Lerner, who had assumed Itadakimasu was guest-specific. Correspondingly, Gochisoosama-(deshita)
functions as the general-purpose way of thanking a host for hospitality (not narrowly for food), used
where English might say "thank you, I had a wonderful time"; a host deflects the praise with
Osomatsusama-deshita rather than accepting it outright, paralleling the earlier-noted pattern of
denying one's own merit. (Vol.1 p.54-55)

### Shirimasen vs. Wakarimasen: whose fault is the gap in knowledge?
Both translate roughly as "I don't know," but Japanese usage selects between them based on who is
implicitly responsible for the gap. *Shirimasen* ("haven't had the chance to learn this") is correct
and neutral for facts that are nobody's particular business to know (a stranger's identity, a place),
but sounds oddly blunt — even hostile — when used for something the speaker is expected to know about
themselves (their own future plans), where it can read as "that's none of your business" or even
"I refuse to engage with you" (an angry parent's *Shirimasen!* to a misbehaving child). *Wakarimasen*
("I don't understand/can't figure out") is preferred by default even for matters not directly about
the speaker — e.g. a student facing an unanswerable question — because it locates the failure in the
speaker's own ability rather than implying someone else (a teacher, the questioner) was negligent.
(Vol.1 p.56-57)

### Fictive kinship terms are chosen from the youngest present person's viewpoint — and are not universal
Japanese commonly addresses complete strangers with family terms (oniisan, oneesan, ojisan, obasan,
ojiisan, obaasan) rather than a name or pronoun; the term chosen reflects the perspective of whichever
child is present, regardless of the adults' actual relative ages (e.g. a mother calls an unrelated
young woman oneesan from her own child's point of view, even though that is not how an adult would
perceive the woman's status). This extended use presupposes a degree of familiarity, so it is
withheld toward people the speaker wants to address with respect rather than closeness — doctors,
teachers, lawyers, and other high-status professionals get *sensee* instead, and many women in
service occupations (restaurant/hotel staff) specifically dislike being called oneesan as too
familiar. (Vol.1 p.60-61)

### Leaving/returning-home formulas are a fixed, matched pair, extended by in-group feeling
Unlike English's free choice of farewell/welcome phrasing, Japanese uses a specific matched exchange
for someone leaving temporarily (Itte-mairimasu/Itte-kimasu ↔ Itte-(i)rasshai) and another for
returning (Tadaima ↔ Okaerinasai); these extend beyond literal family to anyone felt to belong to the
same group (neighbors, coworkers) — group membership itself is individually variable in scope.
*Sayoonara*, by contrast, is specifically excluded from ordinary family/in-group partings because it
carries an implication of non-return; the same logic that excludes Konnichiwa/Konbanwa among family
(marking out-group status) excludes Sayoonara there too. (Vol.1 p.62-63)

### Taihen-desu-ne shows respect for diligence itself, independent of who benefits
Japanese extend sympathetic acknowledgment to anyone visibly working hard — whether an employee's
overtime serves the company or a student's exam prep serves only themselves. Unlike a culture that
might reserve sympathy for other-directed sacrifice, this formula builds relational goodwill simply by
recognizing effort, and a reciprocal exchange pattern exists ([Name]-san-koso...) for redirecting the
sympathy back onto the first speaker. (Vol.1 p.64-65)

### Doozo + omitted verb reads as more polite than a spelled-out request
Across an entire sequence of hospitality moves (inviting someone in, offering a cushion, serving tea,
offering more, inviting a return visit), a host can correctly use bare *doozo* for every one of them,
with the specific verb left out because it's inferable from context/gesture (a bow, an offering
motion). Spelling out the full verb is not "more polite" in Japanese — the reserved, verb-omitted
form is the more polite one to the Japanese ear; nonverbal cues (bowing, gesturing) carry the
communicative weight the omitted verb would otherwise carry. (Vol.1 p.66-67)

### Ojama-shimasu/shimashita vs. shitsuree-shimasu/shimashita: different scope of "the rude act"
Both pairs bracket an interaction (starting/arriving vs. finishing/leaving) with chotto/doomo
intensifiers in the same pattern, but *shitsuree* ("a rude action") is broader in scope than *ojama*
("bothering someone") — shitsuree can refer to the specific act of leaving itself, so
shitsuree-shimasu/shimashita can be said at the moment of departure (on top of an already-said
ojama-shimashita for the whole visit), whereas ojama-shimasu is reserved strictly for the start of an
interaction and sounds puzzling if used while leaving. (Vol.1 p.68-69)

### Leaving a sentence's conclusion unstated is routine, not just occasional ellipsis
Japanese speakers habitually terminate a sentence at the conditional/reason clause and leave the
expected conclusion (often "it won't do," or an implied negative consequence) unstated as
understood — Moo kaeranakucha ("if I don't go home now...") stands alone for "I must go home now,"
and the same -(a)nakucha-ellipsis pattern recurs in self-motivating phrases like Ganbaranakucha ("if I
don't keep trying..."). Spelling out the conclusion explicitly is felt as clumsy/unnecessary once the
context makes it obvious — the same aesthetic preference for incompleteness already seen in trailing
kedo/ga (essay 10) and bare doozo (this essay set). Abbreviated leave-announcements (Sorosoro, Jaa,
kore-de) work the same way. (Vol.1 p.70-71)

### Gratitude formulas deliberately attribute credit outward, even without a known cause
Okagesama-de ("thanks to you") and Shujin-ga osewa-ni natte-orimasu ("my husband is always taken
care of by you") both generalize credit/thanks to people or factors beyond any specific identifiable
favor — reflecting a norm that success should be attributed outward even when it obviously came from
one's own effort, and that mere association with someone counts as having been helped by them, known
or not. Both formulas also commonly function as pure social formality (in stock exchanges like
"How's work?"/"Okagesama-de"), detached from genuine gratitude. (Vol.1 p.72-77)

### Kochira-koso vs. Doo-itashimashite: whose fault was it?
Both reply to a thanks/apology, but Doo-itashimashite flatly denies the other party was at fault/owed
anything, while Kochira-koso self-attributes the blame/credit instead — making Kochira-koso the
correct choice whenever both parties share real responsibility (e.g. an apology for a long silence,
where flatly denying the other person's fault would also deny the listener's own share of
responsibility for the lapse in communication). (Vol.1 p.74-75)

### Anoo as deliberate, not accidental, hesitation
Unlike English "er...", which signals searching for a word (and can read as low proficiency),
*anoo* is frequently used on purpose to perform appropriate hesitation/reserve — expected register
when addressing a superior or opening a request, where sounding too ready-to-speak would itself be
inappropriate. Its intimate-register counterpart *ano-ne* signals closeness instead of reserve and
is correspondingly barred from use toward superiors. (Vol.1 p.78-79)

### Preliminary small talk is functional, not empty filler
What precedes Jitsu-wa ("in fact...") is not wasted conversation — confirming shared feeling on safe
topics (weather, family, cost of living) builds the relational foundation Japanese consider necessary
before business/requests proper, and can consume far more time than the business itself. Jitsu-wa
functions as a dedicated signal for "now I'm switching into business mode," paralleling Anoo's role as
a signal for opening a conversation at all — both can chain together in one utterance. (Vol.1 p.80-81)

### Ichioo softens a commitment into a non-binding gesture
Dictionary glosses ("once," "for the time being," "keep/be entrusted with") predict a literal
future return of money/an item, but in practice ichioo is used specifically where the speaker doubts
that return will actually happen — and by extension, as a face-saving way to decline/hedge a request
(Ichioo yatte-mimashoo, "I'll try") without stating outright refusal, unwillingness, or self-doubt.
The ambiguity of which of those three is meant is genuinely difficult to resolve from the words alone
and is flagged as a real risk when dealing with public/bureaucratic services. (Vol.1 p.82-83)

### A controlled register-lapse signals genuine, impressed absorption
Deliberately dropping from polite (-masu/-desu) to plain speech mid-conversation (Soo-ka instead of
Soo-desu-ka) is a recognized intimacy/engagement device — comparable to "letting one's hair down" —
used between people not close enough for constant familiar speech but not required to be strictly
formal either. It only reads as impressed/positive engagement (not rudeness) if the plain-form
interjection carries distinctly different, more emphatic prosody (sharply falling or markedly lower)
than the surrounding polite register; delivered in the same tone as everything else, it reads as
abrupt and rude instead. The device is sometimes used deliberately in public speaking to win an
audience by appearing to reveal an unguarded reaction. (Vol.1 p.84-85)

### Japanese grammatically marks whose mind you can claim to know
Several grammatical pairs enforce a hard first-person/third-person asymmetry rooted in the idea that
only one's own internal state is directly knowable: -tai (own wish) vs. -tagaru (someone else's
apparent wish, lit. "acts like wanting"), and plain emotion adjectives like ureshii/kanashii (only
assertable of oneself) vs. their -soo-suffixed forms (ureshisoo/kanashisoo, "looks happy/unhappy" —
required for third parties, since only appearance, not the inner state itself, can be asserted of
someone else). This asymmetry is one of the mechanisms that lets Japanese routinely omit an explicit
sentence subject: the suffix choice alone often tells the listener whose wish/feeling is being
described. (Vol.1 p.86-87)

### Volitional vs. plain non-past before "...to omoimasu" marks whose intention it is
...to omoimasu ("I think...") requires the volitional verb form (yomoo, ikoo) to report the
*speaker's own* intention; the plain non-past form (yomu, iku) before the same frame instead reports
someone *else's* stated/expected intention — a genuine minimal pair functioning like two different
English sentences ("I think I'll read them" vs. "I think he'll read them"), not free stylistic
variation. Adding ka after the volitional (ikoo-ka-to omoimasu) further marks the speaker's own
uncertainty about their own plan; omitting it while trying to express that uncertainty instead
produces the (wrong) reading that the speaker is talking about someone else's plan. This is one
instance of a broader pattern: Japanese drops explicit subjects routinely, relying on verb-form choice
alone to disambiguate whose intention/wish/feeling is meant (paralleling -tai/-tagaru and
ureshii/ureshisoo from essay 40). (Vol.1 p.88-89)

### Yappari performs the appearance of considered judgment — and can be weaponized
Prefacing a reply with yappari signals the speaker reached it only after genuinely weighing
alternatives, which is why it is idiomatic and friendly specifically when *answering a question*; the
same word opening an *unprompted* opinion instead reads as presumptuous, since it falsely implies the
topic was already discussed between speaker and listener, producing an unearned air of intimacy/
consensus. This is exploitable: a salesman's Yappari kore-ga ichiban-desu-yo invokes that borrowed
authority even with no actual prior comparison made. Kekkyoku is the formal-register substitute,
preferred when an emotionally-loaded word like yappari would be out of place. (Vol.1 p.90-91)

### Japanese mimetic vocabulary encodes meaning in its sounds (sound symbolism)
Pairs of onomatopoeic/mimetic words differing by a single sound systematically track weight/intensity
contrasts: ko (light/small) vs. go (heavy/big) — korokoro vs. gorogoro rolling; fu (weak/unsteady,
often from illness) vs. bu (aimless but not fragile) — furafura vs. burabura; so (quiet/considerate)
vs. zo (strong/unpleasant) — sorosoro vs. zorozoro. These are not interchangeable synonyms: confusing
furafura (dizzy/weak, implying illness) with burabura (idly loafing) produced real alarm in the
source's example (a suggestion to see a doctor over an intended lighthearted remark about a lazy
weekend). (Vol.1 p.92-93)

### Demo marks "...or something" — a flexible category, restricted to offers/suggestions
Attaching demo to a noun in an offer or suggestion (ocha-demo, eega-demo, shinbun-demo) signals the
named item is just one example of an open category the listener may freely substitute within (order
coffee instead of the offered tea; read something else instead of a newspaper) — contrasted with -o,
which commits literally to the named item. Critically, this demo is restricted to offers/suggestions
and cannot be used to hedge a *factual statement* about something that already happened or is
happening — Suugaku-demo benkyoo-shite-imasu ("she's studying math-demo") is flagged as an
unacceptable learner error for that purpose; the correct hedge there is ka nanika (Suugaku-ka nanika
benkyoo-shite-imasu), which works in both suggestion and factual-uncertainty contexts. (Vol.1 p.94-95)

### Chotto's register depends entirely on tone, not wording
The same word chotto functions correctly across requests, refusals, and reservations/negative
evaluations, but only when delivered with a hesitant, trailing tone (often cued by a preceding Anoo/
Ano); said flatly or abruptly (e.g. shouted at a stranger on the street to get attention) it reads as
rude. As a refusal device, its chief value is letting a speaker signal reluctance/an implicit "I can't
but don't want to say so bluntly" without stating the actual reason; it can be made more specific
(Koko-ga chotto..., Hiyoo-no ten-de chotto...) by naming the particular point of reservation while
still leaving the core judgment softened. (Vol.1 p.96-97)

### Diary excerpts: recurring, real-stakes minimal-pair and particle traps
A recurring miniature format (datelined diary entries) packs several additional self-contained usage
traps the source treats as worth flagging individually: wazawaza ("went out of the way to," a
compliment) vs. waza-to ("on purpose," often negative) are a dangerous near-homophone pair; the
conjunction to can only join nouns/pronouns, never full predicates, where -te-form or -tari...-tari
must do the job instead; a d/r pronunciation slip turns "Edo-period literature" into "pornographic
literature"; Inaku narimashita ("she left [her job]") vs. Nakunarimashita ("she passed away") differ
by a single easily-dropped syllable with drastically different stakes; Kimasu ("is coming") vs.
Kite-imasu ("has arrived/is here") caused a real same-day miscommunication about whether a colleague
had shown up yet; and wakai ("young") specifically excludes small children, producing an absurd image
when misapplied to childhood reminiscence. (Vol.1 p.98-99, 102-103)

### n-desu presupposes a situation; plain -masu/-desu does not
The extended-predicate n-desu form (and its n-desu-ne/n-desu-ka variants) is far more frequent in
actual spoken Japanese than bare -masu/-desu, but is not a free stylistic substitute — it specifically
presupposes that speaker and listener share awareness of some particular situation the sentence is
explaining or reacting to (e.g. remarking on the heat specifically because someone just changed into
summer clothes), and correspondingly carries emotional coloring (concern, irritation, criticism,
surprise) that the neutral plain form lacks. Using n-desu-ka without the licensing situation in place
can genuinely puzzle or insult the listener; conversely, opening a conversation cold (as with routine
weather small talk) always uses the plain form, never n-desu-ne, because no such situation yet exists
to be presupposed. (Vol.1 p.100-101)

### Sumimasen as gratitude reflects attitude toward the favor, not mere politeness level
Sumimasen's use for "thank you" is not simply a more-formal synonym of Arigatoo(-gozaimasu) — the
choice tracks whether the speaker feels they deserved/expected the kindness (Arigatoo) or feels they
did not deserve it or didn't expect it (Sumimasen, carrying an implicit apology for having been given
something unearned). Generation and gender further skew the statistical preference (older speakers
and women lean toward Sumimasen) independent of this underlying attitude distinction, so both factors
operate simultaneously. (Vol.1 p.104-105)

### Minimal-pair and particle traps recur as the diary's core teaching device
A second run of dated diary entries catalogs more near-miss errors in the same spirit as the first
(p.98-103): causal -ta-kara vs. temporal -te-kara; yatto's "hoped-for outcome" presupposition clashing
with unwelcome news; the byooin/biyooin mora-count trap; furui's restriction to objects (never a
person's literal age, where it instead reads as an insult about outlook); samui/tsumetai's tangible/
intangible split (extended, surprisingly, to emotional "coldness" treated as the tangible kind); and
an n-before-vowel pronunciation slip turning money into millennia. The throughline across both diary
sets: small phonological or particle choices in Japanese carry real, sometimes socially costly,
semantic weight that a direct English-based translation strategy will not predict. (Vol.1 p.106-109)

### Dake vs. shika...nai: same quantity, opposite implicature
Both can describe an identical real-world amount, but dake (with an affirmative predicate) states the
amount neutrally/exactly without suggesting it is too little, while shika (obligatorily paired with a
negative predicate) frames the amount as inadequate — the two are not interchangeable translations of
"only." This has real pragmatic consequences: shika immediately signals refusal/incapacity (the
listener doesn't need to wait for an explanation), while dake leaves the listener uncertain whether
the amount is sufficient and prompts them to wait for the rest of the sentence. The same logic extends
to self-deprecating fluency disclaimers, where shika is the conventional choice and dake sounds odd.
(Vol.1 p.110-111)

### Kaeru/kaeru-related verbs encode "returning to where one belongs," not just "coming back"
*Kaeru* does not mean "come back to where the speaker now is" — it means "go back to where one
belongs," so *Kaette-kudasai* said to a guest actually means "please go home," not "please come back
again" (that requires *Mata kite-kudasai*). "Where one belongs" can extend beyond one's house — a
transplanted city-dweller visiting their rural hometown says *kuni-e kaerimasu* — but a friend's house
or a shop is never "where one belongs," so a shopper stepping out briefly should say *modotte-kimasu*
("I'll be right back"), not *kaerimasu*, which a shopkeeper would take as "I'm going home for good"
and may re-sell a held item. (Vol.1 p.38-39)

### Doomo's register and meaning both hinge on delivery, not just content
The same word doomo functions as (1) a truncated social formula standing in for a longer fixed
phrase (thanks/apology/greeting/farewell) or (2) a hedge signaling negative judgment/"I don't know,"
distinguished largely by tone and pacing: quick delivery reads casual, slow and deliberate delivery
reads sincere/polite, and a trailing, hesitant delivery specifically marks the negative-judgment
sense. (Vol.1 p.22-23)

## Grammar points

### Furigana as a punning/wordplay mechanism

Manga artists can assign furigana that diverges from a kanji compound's standard reading to create
puns, irony, or stylistic color (a character named with kanji literally meaning "deranged monk,"
glossed with furigana reading it as the unrelated word "cherry"). This is a script-level
wordplay mechanism worth tracking as a distinct derivation type from ordinary lexical slang
formation — it depends on Japanese's multi-script writing system (kanji carrying meaning
independent of its spoken reading) in a way no prior language in this project could replicate.
(p.48)

### ii desu's structural ambiguity

A bare "ii desu" can mean either an enthusiastic "yes, that would be nice" or a polite declining
"no thank you, I'm fine as is" — genuinely ambiguous without tone/context/facial expression, not
resolved by any grammatical rule. Safer alternatives exist (o-negai shimasu for "yes please,"
versions of "no thank you") but lack ii desu's natural economy. (p.78)

### yaru vs. suru: why only yatta works as an exclamation

Though yaru and suru both mean "do" (yaru more informal) and are often interchangeable, only
yaru's plain/abrupt past yatta functions as a spontaneous exclamation of triumph/joy ("I did it!"/
"Hurray!") — the equivalent form of suru, shita, cannot be used this way. This asymmetry is framed
as one of the clearest ways to feel the practical difference between the two near-synonymous
verbs. (p.84)

### komaru's register-dependent fault attribution

The present/non-past form (komaru/komarimasu) tends to imply the listener or another party caused
the speaker's distress, while the past form (komatta/komarimashita) usually reads as "no-fault"
personal distress — though the source stresses this is a loose tendency, not a strict rule, further
modulated by whether the situation is read as ongoing (komaru) vs. a specific past event (komatta).
(p.96)

### Counter-reading euphonic alternation

Japanese counters' readings shift for euphony depending on the preceding numeral — e.g. the
counter -hon (for long/cylindrical objects) becomes -bon or -ppon in combination with certain
numbers (ichi + hon → ippon). This is a systematic sound-sandhi phenomenon worth noting alongside
this project's other euphonic-shift findings (cf. the -ai/-ui/-shii → -ō/-ū/-shū shift before
gozaimasu documented in Part B). (p.102)

### hai's non-equivalence with English "yes"

Devoted two full lessons, the source frames hai as having a functional range broader than and
non-isomorphic with English "yes": it covers affirmation of yes/no questions, confirmation of
correctness, and assent to a request (Lesson 23's focus) — but also (per Lesson 24, not separately
extracted here) a mere "I hear you" acknowledgment independent of agreement, and, notoriously, an
affirmative answer to a negative question that translates into English as "no." The source
explicitly cites this mismatch as a documented source of real Japanese-American business
misunderstandings. (p.142)

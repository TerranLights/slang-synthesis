# Japanese — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Speak Japanese with Confidence (full audio course)

**Source:** Helen Gilhooly, *Speak Japanese with Confidence* (Teach Yourself, 2010 edition, 80 pp.,
expanded from the 2005 *Teach Yourself Japanese Conversation*), full book — Introduction, "Only got
a/five/ten minutes?" overviews, Conversations 1–10 (Parts 1–2 each), CD3 listening-skills section,
Cultural information, Listening skills: Survival phrases, Japanese–English glossary, English–Japanese
glossary, Essential vocabulary (numbers/counters/time/verb tables), Subject/Grammar index.

**Companion booklet check:** the same archive folder also contains *Japanese Conversation booklet*
(the original 2005 48-page companion booklet to the earlier edition of this course). Both PDFs have
a clean text layer (`pdftotext` extracted cleanly; no vision-reading needed; Vision Reading
Confidence is `n/a` throughout this file). The booklet's content — Conversations 1–10, Cultural
information, Survival phrases, and both glossaries — was checked directly against the main book and
found to be a **strict subset**: the main book's 2010 edition reprints the same ten conversations
verbatim (with the addition of a third CD's worth of extra material: "version 2"/"version 3"
variant dialogues, a dedicated listening-skills section, and additional vocabulary not in the
booklet, e.g. *aka wain*, *Eikoku Ginkō*, *o-mizu*, *orenji jūsu*, *zembu* are present in the main
book's J–E glossary but absent from the booklet's). No content unique to the booklet was found, so
**the booklet was skipped entirely as fully redundant** — no separate 085b file was written for it.

**Coverage note:** This source is a beginner audio/conversation course, not a systematic grammar
reference — it has no formal "grammar chapter" structure. Grammar content is distributed across
short "Insight" boxes attached to each conversation, the "Only got ten minutes?" overview essay, and
the Essential Vocabulary section's paradigm tables (numbers, counters, verb conjugation). All of
these are captured below as individual grammar points. Every distinct vocabulary item from the
Japanese–English/English–Japanese glossaries, the loan-word lists in the overview essays, the
cultural-information section, and vocabulary named inside Insight boxes is captured in the table
below (duplicate headwords between the two glossaries are merged into single rows). Repeated
dialogue lines that only re-use already-captured vocabulary (e.g. the CD3 "spoken at normal speed"
tracks that just repeat a conversation already fully captured, or the "version 2"/"version 3"
dialogue variants that substitute numbers/items into an already-captured sentence pattern) are
*not* re-transcribed row-by-row — only their genuinely new vocabulary items are added.

**Morpheme breakdown notes (agglutinative verb/adjective/counter forms).**

> **oishikunai** = oishi- (stem of oishii "delicious") + -kunai (negative i-adjective suffix) — the
> i-adjective negative paradigm; **oishikatta** = oishi- + -katta (past i-adjective suffix). Cited
> by the source itself as evidence that Japanese adjectives, like verbs, carry inflectional endings
> for tense/polarity rather than requiring a separate auxiliary (p.10-11).

> **tabemasen** = tabe- (stem of taberu "eat") + -masen (polite negative present suffix); **tabemashita**
> = tabe- + -mashita (polite past suffix); **tabemashō** = tabe- + -mashō (polite volitional "let's"
> suffix). The same -masu/-masen/-mashita/-mashō suffix set attaches productively to any verb stem
> (confirmed across ikimasu/ikimasen/ikimashita/ikimashō, kaimasu, nomimasu, mimasu, wakarimasu in
> Verb Tables 1–2), the core regular verb-conjugation mechanism the source repeatedly highlights as
> making Japanese grammar "regular" relative to English.

> **o-kake kudasai** = o- (honorific prefix) + kake (stem of kakeru "to sit/seat oneself") + kudasai
> (polite imperative auxiliary, "please do"); **o-hairi kudasai** = o- + hairi (stem of hairu "to
> enter") + kudasai. The o- + verb-stem + kudasai template is explicitly named in an Insight box
> (p.34) as the standard way to form a respectful (sonkeigo-register) request, parallel to the o- +
> noun pattern seen in o-kyaku, o-sushi, o-tera.

## Grammar points

### The masu-desu neutral-polite form as the safe default register

The source repeatedly frames Japanese as having a layered honorific system (see Keigo below) but
explicitly tells the learner that, as a foreign speaker, the neutral-polite masu/desu form
(teineigo) is always safe to use and that attempting humble/respectful forms (kenjōgo/sonkeigo)
without confidence risks misuse. The entire course is built around this one register (p.11-12).

### Keigo: the three-level honorific system

Japanese marks social distance/status with three polite registers: kenjōgo (humble — used by the
speaker about their own or their in-group's actions, to lower their own status), sonkeigo
(respectful — used about the addressee's actions, to raise the addressee's status), and teineigo
(neutral polite — the masu/desu form, used when no specific status marking is required, e.g.
between strangers of similar status). Plain/casual language is used outside these polite levels,
among friends or from a senior to a junior speaker. The source notes that honorific use is
reportedly declining among younger Japanese speakers, to the point that some companies now train
new employees in its correct use (p.11-12).

### No verb conjugation for person/number; only two tenses

Unlike English, Japanese verb endings do not change according to who performs the action: ikimasu
covers "I go," "he goes," "we go," etc. There are only two grammatical tenses — past (e.g.
kaimashita "bought") and non-past, which covers both present and future (kaimasu "buy"/"will buy") —
with a separate conjectural/probable form (deshō) used for predictions, especially weather, that
aren't certain (p.4, 9-10).

### No grammatical gender, no articles, no obligatory plural

Japanese has no masculine/feminine noun classes, no definite or indefinite articles ("the"/"a"), and
most nouns have no dedicated plural form — number is instead expressed, when needed, through the
counter system (p.4, 9).

### SOV sentence order and the verb-final rule

Japanese basic word order is Subject-Object-Verb (like German), the reverse of English's SVO: watashi
wa niku o tabemasu ("I meat eat") = "I eat meat." The subject is frequently omitted when it is
already clear from context, so niku o tabemasu alone can also mean "I eat meat" (p.6, 10).

### Particles as grammatical markers

Japanese nouns are followed by particles — grammatical markers with no inherent lexical meaning —
that signal a noun's syntactic function. In watashi wa niku o tabemasu, wa marks the topic/subject
(watashi) and o marks the direct object (niku). The source extends this to a general rule that an
amount/counter word is placed after its marking particle and before the verb, e.g. kippu o nimai
kudasai ("two tickets please"), sensu o mittsu kaimashita ("I bought three fans") (p.6-7, 32).

### Postpositions instead of prepositions

Where English places a locational word before its noun (a preposition: "from school"), Japanese
places the equivalent word after the noun (a postposition): gakko kara, literally "school from"
(p.10).

### Location word order ("place, no, location")

When describing where something is relative to a landmark, Japanese states the landmark first,
then the particle no, then the locational word — the reverse of English's "location, landmark"
order. English "alongside the bank" becomes Japanese ginkō no soba, literally "bank's alongside"
(Insight box, p.26).

### Adjective inflection (i-adjectives)

Japanese adjectives change their own endings for tense and polarity rather than relying on a
separate copula construction, paralleling verb conjugation: oishii "it is delicious" → oishikunai
"it is not delicious" → oishikatta "it was delicious." This full inflectional paradigm for a
descriptive adjective is explicitly presented as one of the "regular grammar rules" that make
Japanese more systematic than English in this respect (p.10-11). (Morpheme breakdown above.)

### Pitch accent (not stress, not full tone)

Unlike English, Japanese gives every syllable equal stress rather than stressing one syllable per
word (e.g. Hi-ro-shi-ma, not Hi-RO-shi-ma). However, some homophonous words are distinguished by
pitch: hàshi (high-low pitch) means "chopsticks," hashì (low-high pitch) means "bridge." The source
stresses this pitch system is weaker and simpler than a true tonal language like Chinese (p.13-14).

### Pronunciation mechanics: vowel length, geminate consonants, soft r

A macron over a vowel (e.g. jūdō) doubles its length (two beats) without breaking the smooth flow of
the word. A doubled/geminate consonant (kk, ss, tt, pp, cc/tc — e.g. Hokkaidō, yukkuri) requires a
brief pause before the consonant, analogous to the pause in the English word "headdress." Japanese
has no separate l/r sound — both collapse to a single sound between the two, which the source
recommends approximating with a soft r (not a French-style r) (p.13-14).

### Three (or four) writing systems, each with a distinct function

Japanese combines kanji (ideographs borrowed from Chinese, ~1,945 in the standard everyday list,
conveying meaning/word/idea directly), hiragana (a 46-symbol phonetic syllabary used to write
grammatical endings and native words lacking a kanji), and katakana (a parallel phonetic syllabary
used specifically for loanwords). Rōmaji (Latin-alphabet transliteration) is sometimes counted as a
fourth, non-native script (p.12, 60-61).

### Counter/classifier system

Quantities are expressed with noun-class-specific counter suffixes rather than a single universal
plural: -mai for flat/thin objects (ichi-mai, ni-mai…), -hon/-bon/-pon for long cylindrical objects
(ippon, nihon, sanbon…), -hiki/-biki/-piki for small animals (ippiki, nihiki, sanbiki…), alongside a
separate native-Japanese general counter series (hitotsu, futatsu, mittsu…) that is not built
transparently from the number + suffix pattern. Separate irregular/suppletive counters exist for
people (hitori, futari — futari "two people" is explicitly glossed as irregular) and large round
numbers (man "10,000," used instead of English-style grouping-by-thousands above 10,000) (p.72-73).
See Morpheme breakdown above.

### Regular verb conjugation paradigm (-masu family)

All six sample verbs given in the Essential Vocabulary's Verb Tables (wakaru, miru, kau, nomu,
taberu, iku) take the identical polite-register suffix set: -masu (present/future), -mashita
(past), -mashō (volitional, "let's"), alongside their plain/dictionary form. Verb Table 1
additionally shows the -te kudasai–style polite command construction (magatte kudasai "please
turn," mite kudasai "please look at," nonde kudasai "please take/drink") built on the same verb
stems (p.72-73). See Morpheme breakdown above.

### Dialects vs. standard Japanese

Japan has numerous local dialects (hōgen), but they vary far less than, e.g., European regional
languages, due to centralized compulsory education and broadcast media standardizing most speakers
on hyōjungo (standard Japanese, based on the Tokyo dialect). The Kansai dialect (Kyoto/Osaka area)
is named as the one major dialect still in wide active use today, illustrated with okini ("thank
you" in Kansai) vs. standard arigatō (p.15).

### Weather as a social greeting convention

The source flags, as a genuine cultural/pragmatic grammar point rather than just vocabulary, that
commenting on the weather (ii otenki desu ne "nice weather, isn't it," hidoi otenki desu ne "awful
weather, isn't it") is the more natural, idiomatic way to greet someone in Japanese than asking how
they are — the reverse of the English-speaking convention of leading with "how are you?" (Insight
box, p.28).

### Honorific o- prefix

The prefix o- attaches to certain nouns (o-kyaku "guest," o-sushi, o-tera "temple") and to the root
of certain verbs (o-kake kudasai "please sit," o-hairi kudasai "please come in") to create polite/
respectful forms. This is distinct from, but works alongside, the broader kenjōgo/sonkeigo system
(Insight box, p.34). See Morpheme breakdown above.

## Grammar points

### §13 Days, months, seasons, weather — irregular readings cluster at small numbers

A recurring theme across this whole chapter, worth naming explicitly as a cross-cutting pattern: Japanese's various date/time counting systems share a structural quirk where the **small numbers (roughly 1–10) carry irregular, often historically-native readings**, while counts above that threshold regularize onto the productive Sino-Japanese-numeral+suffix pattern. This was already seen with hour-counter `yoji`/`kuji` (§11) and classifier irregularities at 1–2 (§12); here it recurs independently in **month names** (`shigatsu`, `shichigatsu`), **days-of-the-month** (`tsuitachi` through `tōka`, plus `hatsuka` "20th"), and — a genuinely distinct wrinkle — a **separate irregular set for counting day-durations** that only partially overlaps with the days-of-the-month set (date "1st" = `tsuitachi`, but duration "one day" = `ichinichi`; most other low numbers coincide, e.g. "2nd"/"two days" = `futsuka`) (p. 201–204). This recurring small-number-irregularity pattern across unrelated counting domains (hours, classifiers, months, dates, durations) is a genuinely useful structural generalization for later Japanese-morphology mechanics work — it is not a one-off quirk of any single domain.

### §14 Family relationships — the in-group/out-group kinship-term split

A flagship, highly compact illustration of the in-group/out-group register logic already documented at length via the giving/receiving verb system and the honorific/humble suppletive verb pairs (§7.15–7.16, in `035e`): Japanese kinship vocabulary is **systematically bifurcated** into a neutral set used when speaking of one's own family and a distinct, more honorific set used when speaking of someone else's family — e.g. own father = `chichi`, someone else's father = `otōsan` (p. 206). Most "other's family" forms are transparently derived by `o-` + base term + `-san` (the same honorific prefix/suffix machinery from §4.6–4.7), but not all: `musume`/`ojōsan` ("daughter") is a genuinely suppletive pair with no shared root (p. 206), and `itoko` ("cousin") is the single exception to the whole system — used identically for one's own and another's cousin, with no honorific counterpart at all (p. 206). Japanese kinship terms are also inherently **birth-order-marked**: there is no unmarked word for "brother"/"sister" the way English has — only `ani`/`otōto` (elder/younger brother) and `ane`/`imōto` (elder/younger sister), each independently carrying the same own-family/other's-family split (p. 207). This whole system is a clean, teachable microcosm of the broader in-group/out-group register principle this book documents repeatedly in different grammatical guises (verb choice, kinship vocabulary) — directly relevant to later slang/register mechanics analysis of how social distance gets grammatically encoded in Japanese.

### §15 Useful words and phrases — documented gaps relative to English small talk

Two explicit, source-flagged gaps relative to English conversational convention are worth carrying forward: Japanese has **no general-purpose word for "hello"** — `moshi moshi` exists but is restricted to telephone calls/attention-getting, and ordinary greetings instead use time-of-day-specific phrases (`ohayō gozaimasu` morning, `konnichiwa` afternoon, `konbanwa` evening) (p. 208–209); and Japanese has **no daily-greeting equivalent of "How are you?"** — `Ogenki desu ka` exists but is reserved specifically for someone not seen in a while, not used as routine small talk (p. 208). Both gaps point to a more time/context-anchored and less phatic greeting convention than English's, a genuinely useful finding for later register/pragmatics mechanics work. The chapter otherwise catalogs fixed-phrase inventories for common expressions (p. 209–210), question words (p. 210, several reprising earlier interrogative-pronoun material from §5.3 but adding the money/quantity split `ikura`/`dono kurai` not previously captured), personal-condition statements (p. 211, several built on the resultant-state progressive construction from §7.7-4), and a fully scripted introduction/business-card-exchange routine (p. 211–212) — the latter a genuine culturally-specific social script (the `meishi` exchange) worth noting as a distinct fixed social routine rather than freely-composed sentences.

**Morpheme breakdown (ko-so-a-do paradigm).** The entire *ko-so-a-do* system is a closed
combinatorial paradigm of 4 deictic-distance prefixes × 7 grammatical-category suffixes (not every
cell is filled for every category), directly relevant to this project's morphological-play
mechanisms since new deictic-flavored coinages could in principle substitute a novel suffix onto
the same four prefixes:

> **kotchi** = ko- (proximal: close to speaker) + -tchi (direction, nonpolite) — contrast
> **kochira** = ko- (proximal) + -chira (direction, polite), showing the same prefix slot taking a
> register-marked alternate suffix.
> **konna** = ko- (proximal) + -nna (kind/sort) — the four-way kind-paradigm (konna/sonna/anna/donna)
> is itself built the same way, confirming -nna as a productive suffix slot in this paradigm,
> independent of -re (pronoun), -ko (location), -no (adjective) and -ō/-ō(like-this-manner).

## Grammar points

### To the Reader (how to use this dictionary) — pp. viii–ix

The dictionary's five-part structure: (A) *Grammatical Terms* — informal definitions of
grammatical vocabulary used throughout the book; (B) *Characteristics of Japanese Grammar* — nine
short essays (word order, topic, ellipsis, personal pronouns, passive, politeness/formality,
sentence-final particles, sound symbolism, viewpoint) meant to be read before using the dictionary
as reference, since individual entries cross-refer back to these for broader context; (C) *Main
Entries* — the dictionary's core, each entry structured as: romanized entry name + hiragana form
(alphabetized by romanization) → part of speech → boxed meaning/function statement → English
counterpart(s)/antonym/related expressions → one or more boxed "Key Sentences" (color-coded
recurring structural slots) → "Formation" (word-formation/connection rules) → "Examples" → "Notes"
→ "[Related Expression(s)]" cross-comparing near-synonyms; (D) *Appendixes* — conjugation tables,
connection-rule lists, counters; (E) three *Indexes* (grammar, English, Japanese) giving three
independent lookup paths to the same entry.

### Politeness and Formality (Characteristics of Japanese Grammar §6, pp. 36–38)

Japanese politeness operates through grammar, not just lexical choice, via two distinct strategies:
*Honorific Polite Expressions* elevate the listener's social superior by putting them in subject
position with an honorific verb form (regular pattern: `o-Vmasu ni naru`, e.g. *o-hanashi ni naru*
"[he] speaks"); *Humble Polite Expressions* instead lower the speaker (or the speaker's in-group) to
indirectly elevate the superior. A closed set of common verbs (miru, shitte iru, iru/kuru/iku,
kureru, taberu, suru, kiru, shinu, iu) take **irregular suppletive honorific forms** (irassharu,
gozonji da, kudasaru, meshiagaru, nasaru, osshai, ossharu, etc.) rather than the regular
`o-Vmasu ni naru` pattern, and several of these irregular honorifics are themselves irregular in
their formal-nonpast and imperative conjugations (e.g. *irassharu* → *irasshaimasu* formal, but
*irasshai* imperative, not the expected *irasshari*). The passive form of a verb can also function
as a shorter, less-polite honorific, since indirectness is the shared mechanism underlying both
honorific politeness and the passive.

### Sentence-Final Particles (Characteristics of Japanese Grammar §7, pp. 45–48)

This chapter's core finding for this project: Japanese has a dedicated class of clause-final
particles that mark not propositional content but the speaker's *stance toward the hearer* — and
several of these particles are explicitly restricted to, or strongly associated with, one speaker
gender, making them a built-in sociolinguistic register system rather than free variation. *Ka*
converts a declarative into a question; *ne* softens requests/invitations and seeks confirmation
(usable with polite but not plain imperatives); *na* marks a negative imperative on informal-nonpast
verbs, or (male-only) softens a declarative like *ne*, or (male-only) marks monologue/self-talk, or
(older men, formal register) conveys weak assertion. *Yo*, *zo*, and *ze* all mark assertion
("I tell you," "believe me"); *zo* and *ze* are **exclusively informal male speech**. *Wa* is
**female-only**, attaches only to declaratives, combines with *ne*/*yo* but never *ka*, and cannot
follow a volitional verb form. The exclamatory set *nē*/*nā*/*wā* splits similarly: *nē* is
gender-neutral, *nā* is usually male (though younger women use it too), *wā* is female-only. In
informal speech, *kai* and *dai* mark male-speech yes/no and WH-questions respectively; a rising-
intonation bare *no* functions as a near-exclusive female question marker (contracted from
*n(o) desu ka*); and *kashira* (female) / *kanā* (male) both express the speaker's uncertainty,
split along the same gender line.

### Sound Symbolisms — giseigo and gitaigo (Characteristics of Japanese Grammar §8, p. 50)

Japanese sound symbolism is tripartite: *phonomimes* (giseigo, true onomatopoeia for actual sounds),
*phenomimes* (gitaigo, phonetic representation of non-auditory phenomena), and *psychomimes*
(gitaigo, phonetic representation of psychological states) — and unlike English onomatopoeia
(treated as childish), all three are described as "an integral part of adult spoken and written
Japanese," explicitly not children's vocabulary. The dictionary's first documented generative
mechanism: **voiced vs. voiceless consonants in the same mimetic root systematically shift the
meaning along a big/heavy/dull/dirty (voiced) vs. small/light/sharp/pretty (voiceless) axis** — e.g.
*kirakira* "sparkle" (voiceless, pretty) vs. *giragira* "dazzle" (voiced, harsher); *korokoro*
(small object rolling) vs. *gorogoro* (heavy object rolling); *potapota* (small drips) vs.
*botabota* (large drips). This is a productive, rule-governed sound-symbolic alternation, not a set
of isolated lexical pairs — directly relevant to any slang-synthesis mechanism built on consonant
voicing shifts.

### ato de (p. 80)

"After ~," attaching to the informal past verb form. Differs from *-te kara* in two ways: *-te kara*
cannot follow certain verb classes in the same slot *ato de* can, and *-te kara* indicates "the
space of time following immediately after" while *Vinf·past ato de* indicates "any space of time
after" — a looser temporal-gap requirement.

### dake de (wa) naku (p. 100) and darō (p. 100)

*Dake de (wa) naku* means "not only X (but also Y)." *Darō* is an auxiliary marking the speaker's
conjecture not grounded in specific evidence ("probably"); its related expressions are
*kamoshirenai* (ni chigainai) and *yōda* (rashii, sōda).

### ga¹ and ga² (p. 120)

The dictionary gives a condensed 5-rule summary for subject-marking *ga*: it marks a newly
introduced discourse subject; *wa* replaces it for a topic or contrasted element; WH-word subjects
are always marked by *ga*; subordinate-clause subjects take *ga* unless contrasted; and transitive
adjectives/stative transitive verbs mark their logical object with *ga*. Separately, *ga²* is a
disjunctive coordinating conjunction meaning "but," related to *daga, dakedo, demo, keredomo,
shikashi*.

### hō ga ii / ~hō ga ~yori (p. 140)

*Hō ga ii* ("had better") requires the nonpast-negative verb form specifically for its "had better
not" sense — the past-negative form is explicitly marked ungrammatical in this use. *~Hō ga ~yori*
is the general comparative frame ("~ is more [adjective] than ~" / "~ does [verb] more than ~"),
usable with either two noun phrases or two full (informal) sentences as the compared elements.

### jibun (p. 160)

An empathy marker (reflexive "self") that typically refers back to the main-clause subject or
discourse topic, and whose referent is normally a *passive experiencer* rather than an agent — a
sentence can be genuinely ambiguous between two potential antecedents when both a causative agent
and the causee are plausible referents, with the passive-experiencer reading preferred.

### kara³ (p. 180)

Causal "because/since": the reason clause (S1) always precedes the result clause (S2) in Japanese
regardless of English word order, and the predicate of the reason clause is normally informal even
in otherwise-formal speech (though very formal register can make an exception). In question-answer
exchanges, an abbreviated *kara*-final sentence (dropping an implied *da/desu*) is an accepted
colloquial elision.

### koto ga dekiru (p. 200)

Potential "can, be able to," attaching to the informal-nonpast verb form in a *wa-ga* construction
(topic marked by *wa*, the "doable" action/object marked by *ga*).

### kuru¹ (p. 220)

*Kuru* ("come") is viewpoint-anchored: it marks movement toward wherever the speaker has placed
their empathetic viewpoint, which is not always their literal physical location — a speaker phoning
home can still use *kuru* for a third party arriving there, taking the hearer's viewpoint. *Iku*
("go") is the mirror case, used for movement away from the speaker's viewpoint.

### ~mashō (p. 240)

Formal volitional/invitational verb ending ("I/we will ~; let's ~"), attaching to the *-masu* stem;
related to the softer invitational *~masen ka*.

### mono (da) / mon (p. 260)

A nominalizer that presents a situation as if it were a tangible object, used for emotive excuse,
exclamation, nostalgic reminiscence, desire, indirect command, or conviction. Two register notes
make this entry directly relevant to this project: (1) when *mono* expresses a reason/excuse, it is
restricted to **very informal speech only**; (2) *mono* contracts to ***mon* in very colloquial
speech** (e.g. *kane ga nai **mon** de* "because I don't have money"). Compared to its near-synonym
*no*, *mono* is more indirectly emotive and not tied to the current moment, while *no* is more
immediately/directly emotive.

### ~nakute (p. 280)

Negative *-te* form, expressing a comparatively weak/indirect cause-effect link. The dictionary
gives an explicit directness ranking for four ways to express the same negative-causal idea:
*nakatta kara* (most direct) > *nakatta node* > *nai de* > *nakute* (least direct) — and notes that
for something like excusing one's own lateness, the least-direct form is preferred because
indirectness reads as more polite.

### ni⁶ (p. 300)

Locational particle marking where something exists, co-occurring with *iru* (animate existence),
*aru* (inanimate existence), and *sumu* ("live"). When *aru* describes an event rather than a
location, *de* replaces *ni*.

### no³ (p. 320)

Nominalizer contrasted with *koto*: *no* nominalizes concrete/perceptible events or states (e.g.
feeling one's own body tremble), while *koto* nominalizes abstract/imperceptible ones; several
idiomatic phrases (*koto ga aru*, *koto ga dekiru*, *koto ni naru*, *koto ni suru*) take only
*koto*, never *no*, regardless of this general rule.

### ~no wa ~da (p. 340)

Cleft/focus construction isolating one sentence element as new information ("it was X who/that ~").
The copula *da* stays present-tense even when the whole event is past, and a pure manner adverb
cannot be the focused element between *no wa* and *da*.

### o~ni naru / o~suru (p. 360)

The humble counterpart of the honorific `o-Vmasu ni naru` pattern: `o~suru` humbly expresses the
speaker's own action when it involves or affects a social superior (e.g. *o-mochi shita* "I carried
[your bag]"). Related to *rareru¹*, which can also express a lower-register respectful reading of
someone's action.

### Relative Clause (p. 380)

Japanese relative clauses don't grammatically distinguish restrictive from non-restrictive reading
— context alone disambiguates, and the dictionary gives genuinely ambiguous worked examples. A
particle hierarchy governs how easily a noun phrase can be relativized at all: `ga > o¹ > ni² >
ni⁶ > o² > e > de¹ > de²(means) > kara¹(source) > no¹ > kara¹(starting point) > to² >
de²(cause/reason) > yori¹` — noun phrases marked by *de²* (cause/reason) or *yori¹* cannot be
relativized under this analysis at all.

### shika (p. 400)

Restrictive particle "only," obligatorily paired with a negative predicate (unlike English "only,"
which pairs with affirmatives) — attaches after a bare noun, a noun+particle combination (locational
*ni*, instrumental *de*, reciprocal *to*, source *kara*, endpoint *made*), or a quantifier.

### sore nara (p. 420)

"If that's the case, then ~" — *sore* anaphorically refers to a previously stated proposition, and
*nara* expresses the speaker's supposition about its truth. The entry gives an explicit three-tier
register note: *sore naraba* is a more formal variant, and *sonnara* is a contracted, more informal
variant of the same expression — a single lexical item spanning formal/neutral/colloquial register
in one dictionary entry. Distinguished from the superficially similar *sore de wa* (used at the end
of a visit or when offering something, independent of verbal context, unlike *sore nara*).

### -tachi and its plural-suffix family (p. 440)

*-tachi* pluralizes personal pronouns or human (proper) nouns; *kanojo* ("she") can take *-tachi*
but *kare* ("he") normally cannot (using *karera* instead). Three related pluralizing suffixes occupy
different registers: *-domo* attaches primarily to formal first-person pronouns for a humble "we"
(*watashidomo*); *-gata* is a formal/respectful plural suffix; *-ra* is a casual/neutral-register
plural suffix (cp. *karera*).

### ~tari ~tari suru (p. 460)

Marks an inexhaustive listing of actions or states (implying additional unstated items exist in the
same situation), contrasted with the *-te* form's exhaustive listing. *Suru* following the
*tari...tari* sequence carries the tense, aspect, and formality level for the whole construction,
regardless of the part of speech of the listed items (verbs, adjectives, or copula-*da* predicates
can all be listed this way).

### to³ / to⁴ (p. 480)

*To³* is the quotative particle, required (unlike some languages) for both direct and indirect
quotation, and for marking the content of verbs like *omou* "think," *kangaeru* "think
(intellectually)," *kaku* "write," *kiku* "hear," *setsumeisuru* "explain." Its use extends to
marking sound-symbolic mimetic adverbs as "quoted" sounds (cross-referencing Characteristics §8);
*to* drops when the mimetic word is reduplicated (*batabata (to)*) but not when it appears
unreduplicated (*batan to*, never *ø*). *To⁴* is a separate conditional conjunction ("if/when"),
specifically for conditions bringing about a noncontrollable event or state; related to *tara (ba,
nara)* and *toki*.

### tokoro da² (p. 500)

Aspectual "about to / just about to," built on the dependent noun *tokoro*. Contrasted with *toki*
("time"): *tokoro* indicates a state, so it only combines felicitously with state-describing
clauses, not clauses naming a specific point in time. *Vte iru/ita tokoro da* differs from bare
*Vte iru/ita* by foregrounding the scene/state rather than the action itself.

### wa² (p. 520) — full main-entry treatment

This main entry gives the formal dictionary-style treatment matching the Characteristics-chapter
summary above: *wa²* is a sentence particle used in weak-assertive or volitional declarative
sentences, restricted to female speakers, expressing weak assertion, volition, or (secondarily)
intimacy/friendliness. It attaches to any declarative but never to a volitional verb form and never
appears in a question.

### yahari / kekkyoku / masaka (p. 540)

*Yahari* ("as expected" / "after all") has two senses, each replaceable by a different near-synonym:
*sasuga(ni)* can replace it only in the "as expected" sense; *kekkyoku* ("after all, in short") can
replace it only in the "after all" sense. The entry makes an explicit native-vocabulary-vs-
Sino-Japanese register point: *kekkyoku* "sounds more formal than *yahari*... because *kekkyoku* is
a Sino-Japanese word, while *yahari* is a [native] Japanese word," even though both are acceptable
in ordinary conversational speech. *Masaka* ("by no means, surely not") is *yahari*'s semantic
antonym, used with negative predicates or standing alone as the exclamation "You don't say!"

### ~yōni naru (p. 560)

Gradual-change construction ("to reach the point of ~ing/not ~ing"). When preceded by an
affirmative verb, the change can in fact be sudden (co-occurring with adverbs like *kyūni*
"suddenly"); when preceded by a negative verb, the change must be gradual, or the alternative
*~naku naru* is used instead for a sudden negative change. Two distinct negation sites (negating the
embedded verb vs. negating *naru* itself) produce two different meanings.

### Appendix 5 — Ko-so-a-do (pp. 600–601)

A fully closed, four-way deictic-distance paradigm (ko- = close to speaker, so- = close to hearer,
a- = removed from both, do- = interrogative), crossed with seven grammatical-category suffix slots:
demonstrative pronoun (*kore/sore/are/dore*), location (*koko/soko/asoko/doko*), direction —
nonpolite (*kotchi/sotchi/atchi/dotchi*) vs. polite (*kochira/sochira/achira/dochira*),
demonstrative adjective (*kono/sono/ano/dono*), "kind of" (*konna/sonna/anna/donna*), and manner
(*kō/sō/ā/dō*). Notes: the *-chira* series can refer to persons politely (*kochira wa Yamada-san
desu* "This is Mr. Yamada"); the *so-* series can direct attention to a referent removed from both
speaker and hearer once it has already been introduced into the discourse; and the dictionary frames
the whole system as an empathy gradient — the speaker feels most empathetic toward a *ko-*-marked
referent (closest to them) and least toward an *a-*-marked one (removed from both parties).

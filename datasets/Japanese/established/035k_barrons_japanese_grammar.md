## Grammar points

### §16 Borrowed words — three distinct adaptation mechanisms, and systematic semantic drift

The source organizes borrowed vocabulary into two explicit reliability tiers: "Trust These!" (phonetic borrowings whose meaning is recoverable once pronunciation is correctly parsed, e.g. `orenji`, `kōhī`) versus "Careful!"/"Made in Japan!" (words that look English-derived but have drifted far enough in form or meaning that treating them as straightforward borrowings is actively misleading) (p. 213–214, 216). Three distinct coinage mechanisms recur across the "Careful"/"Made in Japan" sets, worth naming explicitly as a productive wasei-eigo (Japanese-made pseudo-English) toolkit for later slang-synthesis mechanics: (1) **clipping/truncation** of a longer English phrase down to its first syllable(s) — `wāpuro` ("word processor"), `pasokon` ("personal computer"), `depāto` ("department store"), `sekuhara` ("sexual harassment"), `konbini` ("convenience store"); (2) **cross-language or English-internal blending** — `supaman` ("spa" + "mansion"), `furītā` ("free" + German "Arbeiter"); and (3) **semantic drift/reanalysis**, where a borrowed form keeps its original pronunciation but genuinely changes meaning, sometimes drastically — `baikingu` ("Viking" → "buffet," via an early buffet restaurant's branding), `manshon` ("mansion" → "ordinary modern condo/apartment," not a large luxury house), `sumāto` ("smart" → "physically slim/stylish," not "intelligent"), `risutora` ("restructuring" → specifically "layoffs"). The source's own worked caution (`mōningu baikingu` is "buffet breakfast," never "morning biking") is a deliberately chosen illustration of just how far a wasei-eigo coinage's actual meaning can diverge from what a naive English-cognate reading would predict — a genuinely important methodological point for any later attempt to derive in-universe slang via plausible loanword-adaptation patterns modeled on this one.

### §17–§18 Synonyms and Antonyms — register pairing and domain-split polysemy

Both chapters are flat paired-vocabulary lists rather than grammar teaching per se, but two structural patterns recur worth flagging. First, several synonym pairs are genuinely **register pairs** rather than free variants — a native/colloquial member alongside a Sino-Japanese/formal member (`chōshoku`/`asagohan` "breakfast," `otearai`/`toire` "toilet," the latter a euphemistic native-coined alternative to a loanword) — extending this book's recurring native-vs-Sino-Japanese-vs-loanword register-stratification pattern (already seen with kinship terms in §14 and greeting phrases in §15) down to ordinary everyday nouns. Second, the antonym chapter repeatedly shows **the same Japanese adjective splitting into two unrelated antonym relationships depending on semantic domain** — `atsui` pairs with `samui` for weather-heat but with `tsumetai` for touch-heat; `hayai` pairs with `osoi` for both the time domain ("early/late") and the speed domain ("fast/slow"), with the source treating these as two separate entries despite the identical word pair; `takai` pairs with `hikui` for physical height but with `yasui` for price. This domain-conditioned antonym-splitting is a genuinely important polysemy pattern for later Japanese-vocabulary mechanics work — these adjectives are not simply "ambiguous," they participate in systematically different antonym structures depending on which semantic domain is active.

### §19–§22 Phonological minimal-pair chapters — four chapters converging on the same handful of overloaded forms

These four chapters (Short Vowel/Long Vowel, Double Consonants, Same Pronunciation/Different Meaning, Pitch Accent) each illustrate a single phonological contrast type already explained in §1's pronunciation discussion (vowel length and consonant gemination are both independently phonemic, p. 2–5), now exemplified with fresh vocabulary rather than new grammar. The genuinely interesting finding, only visible by reading all four chapters together rather than any one in isolation, is that **several individual phonological forms are independently overloaded across multiple of these four distinguishing mechanisms at once**: `kokyō` participates in a vowel-length minimal pair with `kōkyō` ("public," §19) *and*, as a separate pairing, a gemination minimal pair with `kokkyō` ("border," §20); `kami` is a true kanji-only homonym for "paper"/"hair" (§21) that *also* picks up a third sense ("god") distinguished from both by pitch accent (§22); and most strikingly, `kakeru` is simultaneously a true homonym for "to hang"/"to run" (§21, kanji-distinguished) *and* an entirely separate pitch-accent minimal pair for "to lack"/"to gamble" (§22) — meaning this one phonological string packs at least four distinct lexical meanings split across two independent disambiguating mechanisms. The `kiru` "cut"/"wear" pair closes the loop started all the way back in §7.3 (`035d`): that chapter's conjugation-class discussion flagged the pair as distinguished by stress, and §22 now reconfirms and precisely pitch-specifies that same claim (level pitch for "cut," high-low for "wear") as a textbook pitch-accent example — an instance of two independently-written parts of the same book corroborating each other on a genuinely tricky point. Collectively, these four chapters demonstrate that Japanese's famously large stock of short, simple-looking words is disambiguated by a *layered* system — vowel length, consonant gemination, kanji choice, and pitch accent each doing independent, non-redundant work — rather than any single mechanism; this layered-disambiguation structure is likely to be directly useful groundwork for later analysis of how invented in-universe slang might plausibly exploit (or deliberately violate) the same kind of phonological economy.

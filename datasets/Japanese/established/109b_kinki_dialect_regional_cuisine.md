## Grammar points

### Regional micro-variation is collapsing toward personal-register variation, not place

The source explicitly frames this entire chapter with a sociolinguistic finding worth preserving as
a standalone note: when its authors asked many Kinki residents whether they could identify someone's
hometown from speech alone, the near-unanimous answer was that it was now impossible, especially
among younger speakers. The book argues that, within Kansai, variation driven by an individual's
personality, age, sex, and conversational context now outweighs variation driven by birthplace —
true old-form regional dialect (as opposed to the generalized "Kansai-ben" register) survives
mainly among elderly speakers in longtime-isolated rural communities. This matters for downstream
mechanics-analysis work: the region-tagged forms catalogued above (e.g. Wakayama's z→d substitution,
Shiga's yaharu) should be read as attested historical/traditional forms the source itself flags as
increasingly rare, not necessarily live contemporary Kansai-wide usage.

### Osaka-internal north/south split (Kawachi-ben) as the roughest register in Kansai

Even within Osaka Prefecture, the source identifies the southern half (especially the Kawachi area)
as carrying a reputation for the roughest, least polite speech in all of Kansai — exemplified by the
-ke sentence ending and the ware! interjection above, both notable for being used by women as well
as men only in this specific sub-region (elsewhere in Kansai, comparably rough forms skew male-only).
The source separately notes that some words often described as generic "traditional Kansai-ben" —
specifically erai kotcha and mokarimakka — are in fact narrowly Osaka-city forms, not general-Kansai
ones, a useful corrective against treating "Kansai-ben" as dialectally flat.

### Kyoto-ben's defining features: pervasive honorifics, phonological softness, and indirectness

Three independent, source-marked Kyoto-specific patterns converge on a single sociolinguistic
profile: (1) the -haru honorific is used so much more frequently in Kyoto that it has lost its
honorific "weight" there and is used near-equally by men and women, unlike its female-skewed,
more narrowly honorific use elsewhere in Kansai; (2) Kyoto speech is described as phonologically
softer and more deliberately/clearly articulated, including pronouncing vowels usually silent
elsewhere in Japanese (e.g., the u in -masu); (3) Kyoto speech favors indirectness, exemplified by
the bubuzuke-demo-oagariyasu idiom above, where a surface-level offer of food is actually a polite
eviction notice — the source frames this as rooted in Kyoto's centuries as imperial capital, where
etiquette required never risking offense to someone who might later gain power.

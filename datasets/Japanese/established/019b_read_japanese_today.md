# Japanese — *Read Japanese Today* (Len Walsh, Charles E. Tuttle Co., 1969), part 2

Continuation of `019_read_japanese_today.md` — see that file for the full source citation,
extraction-method note (kanji glyphs are page-scan images, not text; identified via unambiguous
reading+gloss cross-reference, spot-verified against three widely-separated page renders), and
coverage rationale. This file covers the remainder of Section Two's character sequence: modes of
locomotion, money/commerce, gates and doors, animals, weapons/medicine, buildings, weather, and
the color/miscellaneous characters that close the book (roughly pp. 77–152).

## Grammar points

No additional named grammar points beyond those already captured in `019_read_japanese_today.md`
(the on-yomi/kun-yomi "by itself vs. in compounds" pattern, applied consistently to every entry
above as well). A few structural observations specific to this half of the book, paraphrased:

1. **Some pictographs are explicitly "never standalone" building-blocks** — the book repeatedly
   flags a handful of radical-level pictographs (the "advance" element inside 運/送, the
   "road-barrier" element inside 送, the "terraced mountainside" element inside 際/階) as
   incapable of forming a word by themselves; they only ever contribute a meaning to a larger
   character. This project's extraction omits separate vocabulary rows for these per the
   anti-fabrication principle (no invented pronunciation), but the combinations they form are
   captured under their resulting characters (運, 送, 際, 階).
2. **The book occasionally flags its own pictographic story as a mnemonic rather than true
   etymology** — most explicitly for 万 (borrowed, per the source, from an Indian religious symbol
   via Greek and then Nazi usage, rather than a Chinese pictograph of an object) and for 黒 (where
   the source gives the "real" soot-window origin but separately offers a fire+field+earth reading
   purely "if this helps you remember it"). These are flagged in the Notes column rather than
   presented as uniform etymological fact.
3. **A character's reading can itself carry heavy semantic/orthographic variation** — 生 is called
   out by name in the source as having roughly 22 distinct readings with the *meaning itself
   shifting* per reading (UMU "give birth" / UMARERU "be born" / NAMA "raw" / SEI "life"), which the
   book treats as an exceptional case worth a special note rather than the normal on/kun pattern.

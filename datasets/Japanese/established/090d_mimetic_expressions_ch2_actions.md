## Grammar points

### Reduplicated continuous vs. single-occurrence `(t)to`/`tto` forms

The book's one-point lesson on p.53 states explicitly (paraphrased): many Japanese mimetic words
have two related forms — the fully reduplicated form (e.g. *guruguru*) describing continuous or
repeated action, and a shortened single-occurrence form ending in *-ri(to)* or *-tto* (e.g.
*gururi(to)*, *gutto*) describing one single, complete occurrence of the same basic motion. The
same contrast appears with *nikoniko* ("smiling repeatedly/habitually") vs. *nikotto*/*nikkori(to)*
("to smile once," a single smile). This is one of the most productive derivational patterns in
Japanese mimetic morphology and is directly relevant to this project's `morphological_play`
slang-derivation category: a speaker can systematically convert a "continuous/habitual" mimetic
word into a "single discrete event" word by this truncation-plus-suffix operation, independent of
the specific root.

### Deliberate polysemy in gorogoro/burabura (p.65 one-point lesson)

The book explicitly flags *gorogoro* and *burabura* as case studies in mimetic-word polysemy:
*gorogoro* covers both a literal heavy rolling/rumbling sound (thunder, a stomach rumbling) and the
metaphorical sense of a person "rolling around" idly with nothing to do (lazing about); *burabura*
covers both the literal sense of something dangling/swaying and the metaphorical senses of
aimlessly strolling or living an unoccupied/idle lifestyle. The physical-motion sense and the
behavioral/lifestyle sense are not separately lexicalized — the same word covers both, with context
disambiguating. Worth flagging for later mechanics analysis: idle/lazy-behavior slang in Japanese
recruits literal-motion mimetic vocabulary rather than inventing separate terms, paralleling a
similar pattern already noted in the 101 Japanese Idioms source (`090_101_japanese_idioms_part1.md`
Grammar points) where concrete physical images become abstract social/behavioral idioms.

### Katakana-vs-hiragana as a genuine register/word-class marker

Confirmed directly from this chapter's own content (contrast the hiragana lesson terms,
pp.46-68, with the katakana "More Expressions ②" supplementary list, pp.70-72): the book's own
practice uses katakana specifically for words it treats as closer to pure sound-effect
transcriptions (laughter/crying/snoring sounds, several of which are direct onomatopoeic renderings
of a vocalization rather than a description of a state) and hiragana for its core lesson vocabulary
even when some of those lesson words are themselves sound-based (e.g. *geragera*, *utouto*,
*gussuri* each appear in *both* a hiragana lesson entry and a katakana "sound effect" framing
elsewhere in the book) — meaning script choice here tracks the author's framing of a given use
(descriptive mimetic word vs. a more literal transcribed sound/cry) rather than being fixed per
lexical item. This matches and extends the Japanese extraction checklist's standing note that
script choice carries register/semantic information in a way no prior language in this project has
required tracking.

## Grammar points

### *kara* vs. *node*: justifying a request/act vs. objectively stating a reason
*kara* ("because") doesn't always state a literal, factual reason for the preceding clause — it can
instead justify the act of speaking itself (making a request, giving an invitation), which is why a
*kara*-clause can sound odd translated literally. *node* is the more neutral/objective/formal reason
connective, preferred specifically when the speaker wants to avoid sounding self-justifying, e.g.
when explaining a fault (pp.32-33).

### *to yuu-no-wa* vs. *to yuu-to*
Both loosely translate as "that is to say" but serve different discourse functions: *to yuu-no-wa*
gives the reason for what was just said; *to yuu-to* continues/develops it, closer to English "so...?".
Using *to yuu-no-wa...?* as a bare question to extract someone's reason reads as a demanding
interrogation; *to yuu-to...?* (or more polite *to iimasu-to.../to osshaimasu-to...*) is the softer,
socially preferred way to prompt a reason (pp.46-47).

### Phrase-order inversion for emotional emphasis
Conversational Japanese freely reverses the order of phrases (not individual words within a phrase)
to front an evaluative/emotional comment before the topic it's about, signaling familiarity and
enthusiasm; this inversion is avoided in formal speech and writing (pp.54-55).

### Manner as inseparable from grammar: posture, distance, bowing
Several columns in this range converge on one theme: correct words, grammar, and pronunciation are
necessary but not sufficient for successful Japanese communication. Physical manner — speaker-listener
distance (greater for bows than handshakes, scaling with bow depth), posture while apologizing/
requesting (bowed, not upright with chin up), and bowing itself as a substitute for words in apology/
condolence — is treated as part of "speaking" Japanese correctly (pp.36-39).

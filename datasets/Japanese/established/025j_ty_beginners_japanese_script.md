# Japanese — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Teach Yourself Beginner's Japanese Script — Unit 10 (第十課, Putting it all together / letters) + back matter

**Source:** Helen Gilhooly, *Teach Yourself Beginner's Japanese Script* (Hodder Headline, first published 1996, this edition 2003), Unit 10 ("第十課") pp. 150–175, plus the book's back matter (Key to the activities pp. 176–186, Index (1) Japanese–English kanji charts pp. 187–196, Index (2) English–Japanese word index pp. 197–201, Taking it further pp. 202–208).

**Coverage note — Unit 10:** Unit 10 is the book's capstone reading-application unit: it introduces the Japanese sentence-order/particle system explicitly for the first time (it had only been used implicitly in signage up to now), then applies it to six real/realistic Japanese text types — a manga one-panel joke, cookery instructions (two products), a weather forecast, a song, four classic haiku, and a handwritten New Year's letter — plus a closing section on how to structure and close a Japanese letter. Every grammar point and every distinct new vocabulary/kanji item is captured below; repeated "kanji review" quiz items that just re-test a kanji already tabulated in Units 1–8 are skipped per the coverage rule, as are the activities' own multiple-choice/matching answer options that don't carry new content.

**Coverage note — back matter:** per the coverage rule and this project's non-redundant-supplement pattern, each back-matter section was checked for genuinely new content rather than extracted wholesale:
- **Key to the activities (pp. 176–186):** pure answer-key restatement of Units 1–10's own in-unit activities — every item is already covered (or explicitly skipped as a repeated drill) in this book's own `025`–`025j` files. **Skipped in full**, no new content.
- **Index (1) Japanese–English: kanji charts (pp. 187–196):** a unit-by-unit kanji summary table (stroke count, on'yomi/kun'yomi readings, English meaning) — but it only covers Units 1–8 (confirmed by direct inspection: the index ends mid-Unit-8 content on p.196, immediately before Index (2) begins on p.197). Unit 9 taught no new kanji (it is the katakana unit) and Unit 10's handful of new kanji were never given their own index entry by the source itself. This index is therefore **fully redundant** with `025`–`025h` (which already extracted Units 1–8's kanji) and contributes **zero** new content for this dispatch's Units 9–10 scope. Skipped.
- **Index (2) English–Japanese word index (pp. 197–201):** a pure alphabetical page-locator index (English gloss → page number only, no Japanese form given). Pure navigation. Skipped.
- **Taking it further (pp. 202–208):** an annotated bibliography of further readers, dictionaries, magazines, websites, software, and organizations for continued study. No vocabulary or grammar content. Skipped.
- The book's final ~4 physical pages (inside/outside back cover, publisher's house ads for other *Teach Yourself* titles) are pure marketing copy, not source content. Skipped.

**Vision-reading note:** continues from `025i_ty_beginners_japanese_script.md` — same DJVU source, vision-read from rendered page images (two printed pages per scan image; formula confirmed and reused: for scan image *N*, left printed page = 2*N*−14, right printed page = 2*N*−13). The Unit 10 letter-writing activity (pp. 166–167) presents a **facsimile of a real handwritten Japanese New Year's card** reproduced as the book's own printed illustrative exhibit (explicitly introduced by the surrounding printed text as "this letter is written downwards... start reading at the top right") — this is intentional source content to be read and translated by the student, not reader marginalia, and is extracted as such; no actual pencil/pen annotation by a previous book owner was found anywhere in this range.

## Grammar points

### Sentence order and particles (はじめに / Explanation: Grammar functions, pp. 150–152)
This is the book's first explicit, systematic treatment of Japanese sentence structure (earlier units used particles only implicitly, in isolated signs/vocabulary). English is SVO (subject–verb–object): *I ate Japanese food* — "I" (subject) first, "ate" (verb) second, "Japanese food" (object) last, with preposition-like words (*at*, *by*, *with*) placed **before** the noun they mark. Japanese is SOV: 私は　和食を　食べました ("I Japanese-cuisine ate") — 私 "I" is marked by は (the topic/subject marker), 和食 "Japanese food" is marked by を (the object marker), and the verb 食べました "ate" always comes last. Japanese particles (postpositions) are placed **after** the noun or phrase they mark, the mirror image of English prepositions — て marks "with/by" (はしで "with chopsticks") as well as "the place where an action happens" (レストランで "at/in the restaurant"). Crucially, because particles (not word order) carry the grammatical function, the order of marked phrases before the final verb is flexible in Japanese in a way it is not in English — 私は　はしで　和食を　食べました and 私は　和食を　はしで　食べました are both grammatical and mean the same thing, so long as the verb stays last. A diagram (p.151) ties this together around the verb "eat": who eats (は/が), what they eat (を), where they eat (で), when they eat (に), with what they eat (で).

### Particle reference table (p.152)
The source consolidates all particles taught across the whole book into one reference list: は (pronounced *wa* as a particle, despite being written with the character used elsewhere for *ha*) marks the sentence's topic/subject; が marks the grammatical subject of a verb; を marks the sentence's object; で marks (1) "with/by" — はしで "with chopsticks," 電車で "by train" — and (2) the place where an action happens — レストランで "at/in the restaurant"; に marks (1) direction "to" — 東京に "to Tokyo" (へ, pronounced *e*, can substitute) — (2) a time point "at/on" — 月曜日に "on Monday," 一時に "at one o'clock" — and (3) place/position "at/in/on" — 東京に住んでいます "I live in Tokyo"; の marks (1) possession "of/'s" — 日本の米 "Japan's rice" — and (2) links a noun to its position — 山の上 "on top of the mountain"; と marks (1) "and" between nouns — 日本と米国 "Japan and America" — and (2) "with" (accompaniment) — 日本人と "with a Japanese person."

### Quick translation tips (p.152)
A compact four-step method for parsing a Japanese sentence: (1) find the topic/subject by locating は; (2) translate backwards from the end of the sentence, since the verb comes last; (3) descriptive words (adjectives) sit in the same relative order as in English; (4) a kanji radical's meaning, when relevant, is given in brackets to aid decoding, and is assumed to be a left-hand radical unless stated otherwise.

### Reading real text types
The unit applies the above system to six text types in turn, each introduced with its own brief framing note rather than further abstract grammar: a one-panel newspaper manga joke satirizing "education mothers" (pp. 153–155); two sets of real product instructions — an egg-cracking "water drop test" demonstration and a green-tea brewing method (pp. 154–161) — introducing the productive suffixes ~分 (*-bun*, "share/part/per," e.g. 3人分 "three people's worth/three helpings") and ~目 (*-me*, an ordinal suffix "-th/-rd/-nd," e.g. 4つ目 "fourth," 1煎目 "first infusion"), and noting 分 (*fun*) is also the ordinary word for "minute" (2分 "two minutes"); a real Kyoto-newspaper weather forecast (pp. 161–165), which reuses the cardinal-direction and day-of-the-week kanji from Units 1, 6 and 7 compounded with 部 "part" to form regional terms like 北部/南部, and introduces a forecast-specific symbolic shorthand (circular weather icons plus のち "later" and 時々/一時 "sometimes/for a time" connecting them); the well-known children's song さくら (*Sakura*, "Cherry Blossom") presented in hiragana-only lyrics for singing and then again with 漢字 for translation practice (pp. 170–171); four classic haiku by Shiki, Bashō, Buson, and Issa (pp. 174–175), each given with its poet's name and used to introduce a handful of new kanji per poem alongside the poetic particle や (a reading tip notes や functions like は, setting the scene, and is often translated as "the" or "a..." at the start of a line); and a facsimile New Year's letter (pp. 166–169, discussed further below).

### Letter structure and conventions (書く練習 "Writing letters," pp. 166–171)
Based on the reproduced New Year's card from "Hanako Yamamoto" to "Helen," the source draws out standing conventions of Japanese letter-writing: traditional letters are written in vertical columns, read top-to-bottom and right-to-left (this vertical/traditional style is noted as common for ceremonial cards like New Year's greetings even though many Japanese people now write letters horizontally, Western-style); the date is written at the **end** of the letter, followed by the writer's name (surname before given name); a letter opens with a seasonal comment about the weather rather than a direct "how are you," with a stock of fixed seasonal phrases provided for different times of year (see vocabulary table above); the addressee's name plus さん (or さま, for a more formal letter) stands in for an English-style "Dear ___," and the writer's own name at the end stands in for "from ___" — no equivalent of either English word is needed; a closing phrase (e.g. それでは、お元気で or では、お体に気をつけて) precedes the final goodbye; and for a brief note or postcard, the seasonal-greeting-plus-closing convention can be replaced by the paired set phrases 前略 ("Greetings!," used to open) and 早々 ("excuse the rush," used to close).

## Grammar points

### 1. Semantic-field clustering (Chapter 2)

The source's own stated rationale (paraphrased, p. 178): kanji sharing a conceptual/topical field
are easier to retain when studied as a cluster than individually, because the cluster itself
provides a mnemonic structure. The chapter presents exactly **17 named semantic-field groups**,
covering what the source calls "fundamental topics for everyday reading, writing, and
conversation." Full list of the 17 group titles, in source order, each with representative kanji
confirmed by direct vision-reading:

1. **Seasons** — 春 (spring), 夏 (summer), 秋 (fall/autumn), 冬 (winter). All 4 confirmed in full.
2. **Temperature** — 寒 (cold weather), 暑 (hot weather), 涼 (cool weather), 暖 (warm weather), 熱
   (hot to the touch/fever), 冷 (cold to the touch/chilly), 温 (mild/warm to the touch). All 7
   confirmed in full.
3. **Weather** (10 kanji; theme confirmed, full roster not individually re-verified in this pass).
4. **Compass directions** — 4 kanji (東/西/南/北 "east/west/south/north" pattern; group size
   confirmed, individual kanji inferred from the chapter's standard compass-point roster).
5. **Time of day** — 5 kanji (theme and group size confirmed via direct vision-reading).
6. **Days of the week** — 8 kanji (theme and group size confirmed; standard weekday-kanji set
   plus the 曜 "day of week" suffix character).
7. **Animals** — a large group (~23 kanji; theme confirmed via vision-reading, full roster not
   individually re-verified in this pass).
8. **Plants** (trees, flowers, and cultivated crops) — sample kanji directly confirmed: 杉 (cedar),
   茶 (tea [plant]), 桑 (mulberry), 麻 (hemp), 綿 (cotton), 稲 (rice plant), 麦 (wheat/barley), 豆
   (bean), 芋 (potato/tuber), 菜 (leafy greens). Group size: 18 kanji total.
9. **Specific plant parts** — 12 kanji covering flower/leaf/stem/root/fruit-type vocabulary (theme
   confirmed via vision-reading, full roster not individually re-verified in this pass).
10. **Types of metal** — 6 kanji (theme and group size directly confirmed via vision-reading).
11. **Natural objects** (non-living nature terms) — 14 kanji (theme confirmed via vision-reading).
12. **Colors** — 12 kanji (theme and group size directly confirmed via vision-reading).
13. **Numbers** — 15 kanji (theme and group size directly confirmed via vision-reading).
14. **Administrative/geographic divisions** (prefecture/city/ward/town-type vocabulary) — 13 kanji
    (theme confirmed via vision-reading).
15. **Size, shape, and spatial relationships** — 21 kanji (theme and group size directly confirmed
    via vision-reading — this is the chapter's largest group after body parts).

**Mechanics-analysis relevance:** this chapter's existence as a deliberate pedagogical structure —
clustering an entire writing system's vocabulary by semantic field rather than only by frequency,
stroke count, or grade level — is itself evidence that Japanese kanji pedagogy treats semantic
domains (seasons, body parts, family terms, size/shape adjectives) as a load-bearing organizational
layer distinct from either phonology or pure frequency. This is a useful mechanism to compare
against any conlang slang's own register/domain clustering.

### 2. Kun-yomi homophone disambiguation (Chapter 3)

The source's own rationale (paraphrased, p. 199): Kun readings are native-Japanese words tied to
meaning, but several visually distinct kanji can share the exact same kun reading while diverging
sharply in meaning (the source's own example: *atsui* あつい is shared by 厚 "thick," 暑 "hot
(weather)," and 熱 "hot (to the touch)"). The chapter teaches all such kanji clusters as a studied
group precisely to prevent this kind of homophone confusion in writing. **All 20 groups extracted
in full in the Vocabulary table below** — this is a genuinely compact, high-value mechanism
(native-Japanese homophony disambiguated purely by logographic choice, with no parallel in an
alphabetic writing system) worth preserving completely rather than sampling.

### 3. Phono-semantic ("keisei moji") component families (Chapter 4)

The source's own rationale (paraphrased, p. 212, citing its companion volume *Let's Learn Kanji*,
p. xiii): roughly 90% of all kanji are *keisei moji* — phono-semantic compounds built from two
parts, a **radical** that signals meaning and a **phonetic component** that signals pronunciation.
When several kanji share the same phonetic component but carry different radicals (hence different
meanings), they tend to share the same On reading. Example given directly in the source: 寺
"temple," 侍 "warrior," 持 "hold," and 時 "time" all share the component 寺 and the On reading ジ.
The chapter organizes ~150 such family groups (most restricted to 3+ members) by shared On reading,
noting that historical phonological divergence sometimes splits a family into a "main" sub-group
and "variation" sub-group(s) (set off by a dotted line, with the variant reading shown in square
brackets), and flagging kanji that share the same visual component but an *unrelated* reading as
explicit "Exceptions." This is the single most mechanically interesting finding in this source for
conlang slang-synthesis purposes: **it demonstrates a productive phonetic-component-as-sound-class
principle that a logographic or semi-logographic conlang script could borrow directly** — a
component signals a pronunciation class, with named, flagged exceptions where the correlation
breaks down, rather than a universal rule.

### 4. Visual/radical confusability as a teaching category (Chapter 5)

The source's own rationale (paraphrased, p. 249): some kanji are easily confused with each other
purely because they are visually similar or share a component, independent of either their
reading or their meaning (unlike Chapters 3–4, which group by *shared* reading). The chapter
presents ~150 such numbered pairs/small groups, each showing the kanji's reading(s), an English
meaning gloss, and one example jukugo, explicitly to train the learner's eye to distinguish
near-miss character pairs (e.g. 委 "entrust" vs. 季 "season," both sharing an イ-initial reading
and a visually similar bottom component; 印 "mark/seal" vs. 卵 "egg").

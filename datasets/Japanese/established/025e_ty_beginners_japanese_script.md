## Grammar points

### The 46 basic hiragana symbols (full syllabary chart)

Hiragana is a phonetic syllabary of 46 base symbols, each representing one spoken syllable (unlike the roman alphabet, where 26 letters recombine into varying sounds). The chart is read in the traditional direction — top to bottom within a column, columns right to left — and groups symbols by shared vowel ending (row) and shared consonant (column). The five vowel sounds (a/i/u/e/o) are the first column read. Full chart, by consonant row (romaji in parentheses):

- **a-row:** あ(a) い(i) う(u) え(e) お(o)
- **k-row:** か(ka) き(ki) く(ku) け(ke) こ(ko)
- **s-row:** さ(sa) し(shi) す(su) せ(se) そ(so)
- **t-row:** た(ta) ち(chi) つ(tsu) て(te) と(to)
- **n-row:** な(na) に(ni) ぬ(nu) ね(ne) の(no)
- **h-row:** は(ha) ひ(hi) ふ(fu) へ(he) ほ(ho)
- **m-row:** ま(ma) み(mi) む(mu) め(me) も(mo)
- **y-row:** や(ya) — ゆ(yu) — よ(yo) (no yi/ye forms)
- **r-row:** ら(ra) り(ri) る(ru) れ(re) ろ(ro)
- **w-row:** わ(wa) — — — を(o, grammatical particle only)
- **n (single):** ん (n)

Each printed chart entry also shows a cursive/"handwritten" rendering and a stroke-order breakdown, since the printed and handwritten forms of a symbol sometimes differ slightly (pp. 62–63).

### Hiragana's two grammatical functions

Hiragana serves two purposes: (1) writing native Japanese words that have no kanji (or whose kanji is not normally used), and (2) marking grammatical function and word endings (verb conjugation endings, particles) — distinct from kanji, which carries core lexical meaning. The source notes that although learning only hiragana looks simpler than learning ~2000 kanji, kanji actually speeds up reading comprehension because kanji provide visual meaning-clues absent from pure hiragana text (p.71).

### Dakuten (voicing) and handakuten (p-sound) modification

Adding two small strokes (濁点/ten-ten, ゛) at the top-right of certain hiragana voices the consonant: k→g (か/が etc.), s→z (さ/ざ, with し becoming じ pronounced *ji* rather than *zi*), t→d (た/だ, with ち and つ becoming ぢ/づ — explicitly noted as rarely used since they're phonetically identical to じ/ず and so the source files them under rule 2), h→b (は/ば etc.). Adding a small circle (半濁点/handakuten, ゜ "maru") to an h-row symbol instead produces a p-sound (は→ぱ, etc.). These five rule-sets cover all single-symbol sound changes, and the same rules also apply to the contracted (きゃ-type) sounds below (pp. 68–69).

### Contracted sounds (きゃ-type combinations)

Hiragana ending in the -i sound (き, し, ち, に, ひ, み, り, and their voiced counterparts) combine with a small や/ゆ/よ to form a single contracted syllable pronounced as one beat: きゃ(kya), きゅ(kyu), きょ(kyo), and parallel sets for しゃ/じゃ, ちゃ/ぢゃ, にゃ, ひゃ/びゃ/ぴゃ, みゃ, りゃ (and their -u/-o variants) (pp. 67, 69).

### The small つ (sokuon) — geminate consonant marker

A small-sized つ inserted before a consonant is not pronounced as its own syllable; instead it signals a brief pause/glottal stop before the following consonant, doubling it in romanization (e.g. きって "stamp" = *kitte*, pronounced with a pause between "ki" and "te," parallel to the pause in English "bookcase" between "boo" and "case" rather than "book" run straight into "case") (p.69).

### Lengthened/repeated-sound onomatopoeia

Japanese onomatopoeia (giongo/gitaigo, though the book doesn't use these technical terms) typically repeat a sound twice, paralleling English "woof woof" or "plip plop," but are used far more broadly in Japanese than in English, not just in child-directed speech. Many are further lengthened by an extra vowel (usually う) at the final syllable, e.g. ちゅうちゅう (a mouse's squeak, "chu chu") — pronounced as one smooth extended sound, not two separate syllables (p.67).

### は/を/に/が as grammatical particles (previewed ahead of Unit 10)

The hiragana は, を, に, and が carry grammatical functions beyond their basic phonetic values, to be covered fully in Unit 10. Notably, は is pronounced *wa* (not its usual *ha*) specifically when functioning as the topic-marking particle, while retaining the ordinary *ha* pronunciation when it appears as part of an ordinary word (p.72).

### Verb tense marked by hiragana endings on a kanji stem (preview ahead of Unit 10)

Verbs combine a kanji stem (carrying the core meaning) with a hiragana ending that marks tense: a ます ending signals present/future (食べます "I eat/will eat"), while a ました ending signals past tense (食べました "I ate"). The kanji stem keeps its kunyomi reading in this construction. Worked examples: 飲みます/飲みました (drink/drank), 見ます/見ました (look/looked), 書きます/書きました (write/wrote), 話します/話しました (talk/talked). Furigana (ふりがな) — hiragana written above, below, or beside a kanji — is the standard device Japanese texts use to indicate how an unfamiliar or ambiguous kanji reading should be pronounced (p.71).

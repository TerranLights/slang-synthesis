**Morpheme breakdown (compound-verb word-formation system).** This book's "Compound verbs" section
(pp. 85–87) documents a productive Japanese word-formation pattern worth flagging explicitly for the
mechanics-analysis phase: a verb's conjunctive (pre-ます) stem combines with a second verb (often a
directional or aspectual element) to form a new, lexicalized compound verb — structurally comparable
to Germanic separable-verb-prefix systems, though realized as stem+verb concatenation rather than
affixation. The book catalogs ~20 of the most productive second elements with their contributed
meaning, e.g.:

> **とりあげる** = とり- (conjunctive stem of 取る "to take") + あげる (lit. "to raise," contributing
> a completive/upward sense) — "to accept (a plan, proposal)." p.85.
> **ひきおろす** = ひき- (conjunctive stem of 引く "to pull") + おろす (lit. "to lower," contributing a
> downward/displacing sense) — "to pull down, unseat." p.85.
> **かきいれる** = かき- (conjunctive stem of 書く "to write") + いれる (lit. "to put in," contributing
> an inward/completive sense) — "to fill in (a form)." p.85.

Other catalogued second elements and their contributed meaning (non-exhaustive, representative):
~あう/合う "to do mutually/in accord" (話し合う "to discuss together"), ~おわる/終わる "to finish"
(読みおわる "to finish reading"), ~かえす/返す "to do (something) back/repeatedly" (繰り返す "to
repeat"), ~すぎる/過ぎる "to exceed/do too much" (食べすぎる "to eat too much"), ~だす/出す
"inceptive — to start (suddenly)" (降り出す "to start raining"), ~つづける/続ける "to continue"
(話しつづける "to keep on speaking"), ~はじめる/始める "to start" (食べはじめる "to start eating").

## Grammar points

### Verb conjugation groups — native terminology

Japanese verbs split into two main groups (plus two irregulars する/くる) by conjugation pattern:
**ごだん (五段, "five-step")** verbs, whose final kana shifts across all five kana-chart vowel rows
when conjugating, and **いちだん (一段, "one-step")** verbs, which have a single invariant stem. This
book additionally formalizes the **classical Kokugo/Hashimoto-grammar stem system**: 未然形
(mizenkei, pre-causative/passive stem), れんようけい/連用形 (renyōkei, the conjunctive/pre-ます
stem — already captured under this name in `042`'s Base-numbering but given its own native name
here), かていけい/仮定形 (kateikei, conditional stem), すいりょうけい/推量形 (suiryōkei, volitional
stem), めいれいけい/命令形 (meireikei, imperative stem), and じしょけい/辞書形 (jishokei, dictionary
form — the book explicitly prefers this modern cover-term over the classical alternate names
しゅうしけい/終止形 and れんたいけい/連体形 since their original sentence-final-vs-modifying-clause
distinction has collapsed in modern Japanese). For the actual conjugation mechanics these stems
produce, see `028`, `035d`–`035f`, `042`–`042b`, `043`–`043e` (already fully documented from
multiple angles).

### Homograph and "trap" verb lists

Beyond the general ごだん/いちだん classification, the chapter catalogs specific same-dictionary-form
pairs that belong to different conjugation classes (see vocabulary table above: きる/着る vs.
きる/切る, かえる/帰る vs. かえる/替える・変える, いる/要る vs. いる/居る, へる/減る vs. へる/経る,
しめる/湿る vs. しめる/閉める, ねる/練る vs. ねる/寝る) — kanji disambiguates these in writing, but
they are genuine traps in romanized or spoken-only contexts. Separately, it catalogs ごだん verbs
that end in -iru/-eru and therefore *look* like いちだん verbs by ending shape alone (要る, 限る,
切る, 知る, 入る, 走る, しゃべる, ける, 滑る, 参る) — explicitly flagged as common sources of
conjugation error, with the advice to check any unfamiliar -iru/-eru verb against a learner's
dictionary.

### Systematic catalog of ~て + auxiliary-verb combinations

The chapter's ~て-form section (pp. 40–53) catalogs nine distinct ~て + auxiliary-verb constructions
as a connected system rather than presenting them as scattered individual idioms: ~ている (continuous
action/resulting state, with a further distinction for momentary-vs-repeatable verbs), ~てある
(resulting state of a deliberate action, transitive-verb-specific, marked with が not を for the
object), ~ておく (preparatory action for future use), ~てしまう/~ちゃう (completion, or a
colloquial-register negative-judgement nuance — "gone and done it"), ~てみる ("try ~ing and see"),
~ていく/~てくる (a process continuing away from or toward the present/speaker, including the
idiomatic lexicalizations やってくる "to show up" and やっていく "to get along (well) with"), ~てから
(sequencing — action A completes before B), ~ては+いけない/ならない/だめ(だ) (prohibition), and
~ては (eliciting suggestions, "how about ~ing?"). Presenting all nine as one coherent inventory is
this book's own distinctive organizational contribution — the individual constructions themselves
substantially overlap with content already in `028`/`035d-f`/`042`-`043e`.

### Negative-form inventory: ~ない, ず, ~ずに

Beyond the standard ~ない negative (already fully documented elsewhere), the book explicitly notes ず
as "an older form of negative that is still quite commonly used" (e.g. 週末は家に帰らず、ずっと会社
にいました) — an explicit register/era annotation per the coverage rule's priority on such markings,
not an inference from context. する's irregular ず-negative is せず, not しず. ~ずに functions as a
more formal/literary alternative to ~ないで ("without ~ing").

### Compulsion and permission structures: the なくて/なければ family

The chapter systematically tabulates the compulsion-structure family built on ~ない: ~なくて (causal,
"because there isn't/didn't"), ~なくても ("even if ~ not"), ~なくてはいけない (compulsory, commonly
contracted to ~なくちゃ in speech), ~なければならない/~なければいけない (compulsory, synonymous, with
なる-based なければならない trending more written and いける-based なければいけない trending more
spoken; both compressible to ~なきゃ), ~なくてもいい/~なくていい ("don't have to," contractible to
~なくていい), ~なくてよかった ("glad that... didn't..."), and ~なければよかった ("wish ~ hadn't...").
Presenting the full family as one paradigmatic set (rather than individual idioms) is this book's own
organizational contribution; the individual meanings are already documented piecemeal elsewhere.

### Passive form: agent-marking and the indirect/adversative passive

The passive (ごだん: あ-row + れる; いちだん: drop final る + られる; する → される; くる → こられる)
marks its agent with に or (more formally) によって, except when describing authorship of a creative
work (books, films, music), where によって is specifically required rather than merely preferred.
Material a thing is made *from* uses で when the material's form is unchanged and から when it
changes form (尺八は竹で作る "made of bamboo" [shape-preserving] vs. 酒は米から造る "made from rice"
[transformed]) — a specific lexical-particle distinction not framed this way in prior sources. The
indirect/"adversative" passive (スリに財布を盗まれました, "I had my wallet stolen by a pickpocket" —
retaining を on the direct object while adding a に-marked agent to a passive verb) expresses a
negative effect on the grammatical subject with no direct English passive equivalent; cues like
たいへん "terrible" or こまった "troubled" often signal this reading but are not required for it.

### Giving-and-receiving verbs: register notes

あげる/くれる/もらう choice depends on perspective (self vs. other) and can extend to in-group/
out-group framing (a speaker describing an event from an in-group member's viewpoint using くれる).
やる is specifically the "give" verb used when the recipient is of *lower status* than the giver —
human-to-animal, or parent-to-child — a status-based distinction layered on top of (not identical to)
the overall politeness-register choice between あげる/差し上げる and more humble/polite alternatives
(cross-referenced forward to keigo, p. 213 — see `055f`).

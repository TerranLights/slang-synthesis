## Grammar points

### Kanji formed by combining the meanings of simpler kanji (Lesson 5, Unit 1)

Distinct from Lesson 4's "kanji from abstract signs," Lesson 5 introduces a third formation mechanism:
combining two or more already-meaningful kanji so their combined meaning suggests the new character's
meaning, illustrated with a mnemonic sentence for each: 日+月→明 "bright" (both the sun and the moon
are bright); 人+木→休 "rest" (a man rests beside a tree); 人+本→体 "body" (the origin of a man is the
body); 女+子→好 "love, like" (a woman loves a child); 田+力→男 "man, male" (a man is powerful in the
rice field); 木+木→林 "wood/grove" (two trees make a wood); 木+木+木→森 "forest/jungle" (three trees
make a forest); 門+日→間 "between" (the sun seen between the gate's doors); 火+田→畑 "cultivated field"
(burning a field makes it cultivable); 山+石→岩 "rock" (a big stone in a mountain is a rock). These
mnemonics are the book's own pedagogical device, not a claim of true etymological derivation in every
case — worth noting for later mechanics-analysis work since several of the "combination" stories here
are folk-etymological simplifications rather than the kanji's actual historical formation.

### 国字 (kokuji) — kanji of native Japanese origin without an on-yomi

A boxed note (p.40) explains that while most kanji were transmitted from China, some originated in
Japan itself and consequently have no on-yomi (Chinese-derived reading) at all — only a kun-yomi. The
three examples given are 畑(はたけ, "cultivated field," itself taught as kanji #54 this lesson),
働(はたら)く "to work," and 峠(とうげ) "a mountain pass." The note adds that many kanji used for fish and
plant names are also of Japanese origin, giving 鱈(たら, "a cod") and 鰯(いわし, "a sardine") as examples
— these two are given purely as illustrative vocabulary, not formally taught/boxed characters in this
chunk.

### Fraction construction: [denominator]分の[numerator]

Lesson 4's writing-exercise drills establish the fraction-reading pattern using 分 "part/division" plus
the particle の: 二分の一 (nibun no ichi) "one half, 1/2"; 三分の一 (sanbun no ichi) "one third, 1/3";
四分の三 (yonbun no san) "three quarters, 3/4" — denominator stated first (as a count of 分 "parts"),
then の, then the numerator.

### Price/counter review and size-prefix pattern 大・中・小

A Lesson 4 writing drill pairs だい/ちゅう/しょう ("large/medium/small") directly against three
nested-square illustrations, confirming 大・中・小 function as a productive size-grading prefix set
(already independently seen in 大学/中学校/小学校 "university/junior high/primary school" and in
clothing/product-size labeling more generally, though the drill itself only illustrates the abstract
size concept).

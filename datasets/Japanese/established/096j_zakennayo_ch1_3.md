## Grammar points

### Verb-final word order and question particle *ka*
The book notes (p.26) that the verb goes at the end of the sentence, and *ka* is used to turn a
statement into a question — illustrated with the template *Anata wa ___ desu ka?* ("Are you
___?").

### Negation with *janai*
*Janai* is used to deny a statement, e.g. *Watashi wa America jin ja nai* ("I am not American"),
contrasted with the plain affirmative *desu* copula.

**ABC code (Chapter II central conceit).** Japanese high schoolers' own private alphabet-based
euphemism scheme: `A` = kiss, `B` = petting, `C` = sex, `D` = pregnancy (the unhappy result of
`A` through `C` pursued without caution), `I` = abortion. Recorded here as a structural note
rather than as individual dictionary rows, since each is a letter-as-code rather than a
standalone lexical item; see the `CIA`, `H`, `S`, and `M` entries above for the fuller related
code vocabulary.

## Grammar points

### Nonce-verb formation from loanwords/proper nouns (*deniru*)
The book's *deniru* example (from "Denny's") shows a productive pattern where a foreign proper
noun is verbed by adding a Japanese verb-ending (*-ru*), inflecting normally in the past tense
(*denitta*). This is noted here as a live derivational pattern worth flagging for the
`morphological_play` derivation type in `analysis/`, pending this language's typology profile.

**Morpheme breakdown.**

> **shibukajizoku** = *Shibu(ya)* (place name, Shibuya) + *kaji* (short for *kajuaru*, itself a
> clipped borrowing of English "casual") + *zoku* ("tribe/clan") — a three-part compound literally
> "Shibuya-casual-tribe," glossed by the book as "spoiled, rootless, alienated youth."

## Grammar points

### Imperative/request forms in street register (*-yo*, *kureyo*)
Several Chapter III phrases (*oshiete kureyo*, *shiyo-yo*, *dameyo*) show a casual sentence-final
*-yo* particle marking assertive/breezy tone, contrasted elsewhere in the book with more neutral
or polite equivalents — paraphrased from the book's running commentary rather than a separate
grammar-box treatment (this book is a slang dictionary, not a grammar text, so explicit grammar
points are sparse by design).

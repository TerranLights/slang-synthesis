# Japanese — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: "Read Real Japanese: Fiction" (representative sample, all 6 stories)

**Source:** Michael Emmerich (ed.), *Read Real Japanese: Fiction — Short Stories by Contemporary
Writers*, narrated by Matsunaga Reiko, Kodansha International, 2008. Six short stories, each
presented as Japanese text (right-hand page) facing an English phrase-by-phrase gloss (left-hand
page), followed by a back-matter "Notes" section with paragraph-numbered grammar explanations for
all six stories in sequence. Scanned, image-only PDF (no text layer; confirmed via `pdftotext`/
`pdffonts` returning zero output), 103 PDF pages, each PDF page a two-printed-page spread (the book
is bound/paginated in the Japanese right-to-left convention even though its English content reads
left to right within each page — printed page numbers *decrease* left to right within a spread,
e.g. PDF page 8 shows printed p.17 on the left and p.16 on the right).

**Coverage note — representative sampling, not exhaustive.** Per dispatch instructions, this
source is too large for full transcription. For each of the 6 stories, 2-4 spreads were
vision-read (opening + one or more mid/late spreads), prioritizing pages with dense
vocabulary/grammar annotation. Several Notes-section spreads (one or more per story) were also
read for paraphrased grammar points. No handwritten marginalia was found on any page read — all
examined pages carried only typeset/printed content.

**Story-order correction.** The dispatch brief assumed course order Soup→Appetizer→Cheese→Fish→
Meat→Dessert (matching the preface's numbered list of story titles). Direct page inspection found
the book's actual *physical* story order is: 1. Kamisama (Soup), 2. Mukashi yūhi no kōen de
(Appetizer), 3. Nikuya Ōmu (Fish), 4. Miira (Meat), 5. Hyakumonogatari (Cheese), 6. Kakeru
(Dessert) — i.e. Fish/Meat/Cheese appear in a different order than the preface's course-by-course
commentary lists them. This was confirmed by reading the printed page numbers and story-title
headers directly on multiple spreads per story (see per-story page ranges below), not inferred.

## Grammar points

### である (de aru) as the formal/written copula

The preface (p.9) and the Kamisama Notes section (back-matter p.50-51) both flag Kawakami's use
of である instead of spoken だ/です as a marker of a slightly stiffer, written register — e.g.
川原に行くのである vs. the more conversational 川原に行くんだ. The Notes section observes that
である tends to read as more "academic," and that のである (see below) compounds this effect.
Per the preface, this register choice was deliberately chosen as an accessible first text because
Kamisama's whole narration leans on this voice alongside the bear's own ceremonious speech.

### のだ/のである — explanatory sentence-ending nuance

The Notes section (p.50 back matter) explains that  attaching の before a sentence-final だ/である
(giving んだ/んです in speech, のだ/のである in writing) turns the preceding clause into a noun
phrase and adds an "explanatory" or justifying tone — roughly "the thing is, ..." This recurs
across multiple stories' notes (e.g. Hyakumonogatari's notes on 《何》になるのだ, back-matter
p.106) as a baseline pattern readers are expected to recognize everywhere.

### Passive and causative-passive conjugation chains

Several notes build a short paradigm: 広げる ("to spread open") → 広げさせる (causative, "to
make/let someone spread") → 広げさせられる (causative-passive, "to be made to spread"), explicitly
flagged (Mukashi yūhi no kōen de notes, PDF p.80) as "tricky until you get used to them." The same
section separately glosses several plain passives (しめつけられる, 囲まれる, つかまれる) as
practice instances of the same -(r)are- morphology on different verb stems.

### classical negative ず/ぬ as a literary register marker

Multiple stories' notes point out that ず (and its attributive form ぬ) is a negative verb ending
inherited from classical Japanese, equivalent in meaning to modern -ない/-naide but carrying a more
literary or formal flavor — e.g. 何も言わずに ("without saying anything," Notes to Hyakumonogatari,
PDF p.75) and 夫に寝姿を見せぬように ("so as not to show her husband her sleeping face," Notes to
Hyakumonogatari, PDF p.101), the latter explicitly rewritten by the annotator in modern 見せない
ようにして for comparison. 若き (classical attributive of 若い) is flagged the same way in the
Nikuya Ōmu notes (PDF p.85): "classical grammar again!"

### そう (hearsay/appearance) vs. らしい — evidentiality distinction

The Notes section (PDF p.80, on 残念そうに) draws an explicit contrast: そう attached to an
adjective/verb stem marks a judgment based on something the speaker directly perceives (a
"-looking"/"-sounding" sense, e.g. 重そうな石 "a heavy-looking rock"), while らしい (seen in どうやら
...らしい, same page) marks a more tentative conclusion drawn from circumstantial evidence or
hearsay. Both are common sentence-final/adjectival evidentiality markers that recur across all six
stories' annotation apparatus.

### ごとに — "each time / every"

Glossed in the Hyakumonogatari back-matter notes (PDF p.101, para. 87): ごとに attaches to a noun
or a verb's dictionary form to mean "each time X" or "every X" — functionally similar to たび(に).
Example cited: 一息ごとに, "with each breath."

### Gendered speech-register divergence in dialogue attribution

A note on Hyakumonogatari (PDF p.95, para. 10) makes an explicit sociolinguistic observation: the
annotator points out that the male character Anzai's imperative してて + よ construction is
"typical of informal 'masculine' speech," while Mitsuko (the female character) "doesn't talk this
way at all — she's much more formal, and sounds more 'feminine.'" The note adds this makes it
possible to identify speakers in dialogue even without explicit attribution tags, then caveats that
real-world speakers (especially young men and women) don't always diverge this sharply — flagging
the stylization as a fiction convention rather than a claim about real usage. This directly echoes
the preface's own framing of Hyakumonogatari as useful for learning "the different styles of speech
that men and women often use in fiction."

### かける as a productive polysemy/pun engine (Kakeru's central mechanism)

"Kakeru," the book's final (Dessert-course) story, is structured as an unbroken chain of clauses
that each pivot on a different sense of the verb かける (and, in the closing lines, its near-
homophone 欠ける, "to be missing/short one"). The preface (p.11) describes this as Tawada "leaving
out the plot" entirely in favor of pure linguistic play — "flourless chocolate cake" is the
preface's own metaphor. The Notes section's closing entry (back-matter p.112, "Notes to p.142")
reads this final 欠ける pun as self-reflexively about the reader: a "one-man play" (一人芝居)
notionally needs only one performer, but the pun implies it actually needs two — performer and
audience — mirroring how the narrator's disappearance from the text leaves the reader, like an
audience, "gazing up at an empty stage."

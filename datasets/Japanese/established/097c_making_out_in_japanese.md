**Source:** Todd & Erika Geers, *Making Out in Japanese* (Revised Edition), Tuttle Publishing.
Chunk covers printed pages 86–128 (image files p-46.png–p-67.png, the final chunk running to the

chapter/situation, not by headword. Every distinct vocabulary item, slang term, and phrase in this
page range is extracted, not a sample. The book explicitly marks male-only (♂) vs. female-only (♀)
phrasings with gender symbols — captured in the Notes column per entry. The source has NO text layer
(confirmed: full-page JPEG scan via "Image2PDF") and was vision-read entirely from rendered page
images, two printed pages per image (verified empirically: image p-46.png shows printed pages 86–87,

## Grammar points

### Gendered phrase pairs (♂/♀ markers)
Throughout this phrasebook, many phrases are given in two forms marked with ♂ (male speech) or ♀
(female speech) symbols — reflecting genuinely distinct gendered speech registers in Japanese
(sentence-final particles like -wayo/-wane for feminine speech vs. blunter imperatives/plain forms
for masculine speech). Not every entry is marked; unmarked entries are presented as gender-neutral
or usable by either speaker. (pp. 86–101)

### Mita vs. mienakatta vs. mirenakatta (seeing/perception distinctions)
The book draws a specific three-way distinction around the verb "to see": *mita* (saw it, plain
past), *mienakatta* (couldn't see it because something external blocked the view), and
*mirenakatta* (couldn't see it due to one's own lack of vision/perception/ability). This is a
genuine semantic nuance the source explicitly calls out rather than treating "couldn't see" as one
undifferentiated gloss. (p. 91)

### Register escalation example: "What did you say?"
The book presents a deliberate register ladder for one function (asking someone to repeat
themselves): from the full polite-casual *Nante itta-no?* down through bare interjections *Nani?*
and *E?*, up to the confrontational masculine *Nante ittan-dayo?*, which the book explicitly flags
as too accusative for delicate situations or new acquaintances — an explicit source-marked register
warning. (p. 98)

### "Iku" as sexual-climax euphemism (semantic shift)
The verb *iku* (lit. "to go") is used idiomatically for sexual climax (*iku iku* / *iki-sō-da* "I'm
coming," *itta?* "Did you come?") — a clean example of `semantic_shift` derivation (ordinary core
verb repurposed via a specific context-bound meaning) directly relevant to this project's
slang-mechanics-analysis phase. (p. 117)

### "Gomu" (rubber) as condom slang
*Gomu* (lit. "rubber/eraser/gum") is the book's given colloquial term for "condom" rather than any
clinical/loanword term — another `semantic_shift`/`euphemism_chain`-type example: an everyday
material-noun narrowed to a specific taboo referent in informal speech. (p. 116)

### "Maji" as a slang intensifier
*Maji* ("serious/for real") recurs twice in this chunk as a colloquial intensifier/adjective ("Mada
maji-ni naritakunai" — "It's not time for me to get serious"; "Anata-no-koto maji-nano" — "I'm
serious about you"), consistent with its broader status in contemporary Japanese youth slang as a
general-purpose intensifier (cf. "maji de" = "seriously/really"). (pp. 119, 123)

### Euphemism for a night together
"Issho-ni yoake-no kōhii nomanai?" ("Do you want to drink morning coffee together?") is explicitly
flagged by the source as "an old way to ask if you want to spend the night together (in bed, of
course)" — a classic `euphemism_chain` example drawing on an innocuous, non-taboo surface phrase to
indirectly reference a taboo/intimate topic. (p. 106)

### Clipped-compound loanword slang: "purikura"
*Purikura* プリクラ ("print club," sticker-photo booths) is a clipped compound of the English
loanwords "print club" (*purinto kurabu*) — a `phonological_reduction`/`borrowing` combination
example of Japanese youth-culture slang formation. (p. 93)

### Dual-use emotional idiom
"Gaman dekinai!"/"Taerarenai!" ("I can't stand it!") are explicitly noted by the source as usable
for both sad and angry feelings alike — a single idiom spanning two distinct emotional registers
rather than being emotion-specific. (pp. 127–128)

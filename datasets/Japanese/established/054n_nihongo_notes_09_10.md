## Grammar points

### Darake vs. de ippai: negative-only vs. neutral "filled with" (p.70-71)
*...darake* means "filled/covered with" but is restricted to undesirable matter or damage (dust,
mud, mistakes, blood) — it cannot describe a neutral or pleasant abundance, so "the streets are full
of people" cannot use *darake*. *...de ippai* covers the same "filled with" territory without that
restriction, and unlike the familiar-register-only *darake*, works in both familiar and polite
conversation. Two further written-register-only expressions, *...ni michita* and *...o umetsukushita*,
round out the "filled with" family for literary prose.

### Describing a locked door: intransitive-stative preference over the true passive (p.72-73)
*Kagi-ga kakatte-iru* ("it's locked," using the intransitive verb *kakaru*) is the natural way to
describe a door's state, even though *kagi-ga kakerarete-iru* (the true passive of the transitive
*kakeru*, "to lock") is also grammatical — true passives of common action verbs are simply
uncommon in everyday conversation, with intransitive counterparts preferred. This generalizes to a
broader pattern: intransitive-verb + *-te-iru* (e.g. *mado-ga aite-iru* "the window is open")
describes a resulting state neutrally, while transitive-verb + *-te-aru* (e.g. *mado-ga akete-aru*)
describes the same state but implies the speaker has some interest in who performed the action —
gratitude, dissatisfaction, or a reprimand, depending on context.

### Mono/mon as a general indirectness marker (p.74-75)
Adding *mono* (or its contraction *mon*) to a sentence doesn't change its literal meaning but makes it
sound more indirect, reserved, or refined — *doo* becomes *donna* when *mono* follows it. A related
sub-pattern uses *mon-deshoo-ka* to ask for someone's advice, typically with the main verb cast in the
past tense even when the action being asked about is future/hypothetical.

### Two senses of yoku, only one of which tolerates negation (p.76-77)
*Yoku* meaning "well/skillfully" (describing manner) freely combines with negative verbs. *Yoku*
meaning "much/frequently" (describing quantity) does not — a Japanese listener hearing this sense of
*yoku* expects the sentence to resolve affirmatively, so ending it negatively (*yoku
benkyoo-shimasen-deshita*) is understandable but sounds foreign. *Amari...nai* is the correct
construction for "not...much" in the quantity sense.

### Aruku/hashiru/oyogu take made, not e, unless extended with te iku/kuru (p.78-79)
Verbs describing an action/distance covered rather than arrival — *aruku* "walk," *hashiru* "run,"
*oyogu* "swim" — pattern with *made* ("as far as") rather than the directional particle *e*
("to/toward"), even where English "walk to the station" suggests *e* would fit. Attaching *-te
iku*/*-te kuru* to these verbs (as in *aruite iku*, "to get there by walking") creates a compound that
does encode reaching a destination, and that compound can then take *e*.

### Beki vs. eba yokatta(-noni): moral obligation vs. everyday regret (p.80-81)
*...beki(-da)* is a literary expression specifically for moral/ethical "should," and sounds mismatched
when used for an everyday missed-opportunity regret (arriving five minutes too late for cake).
*...eba yokatta(-noni)* ("should have...," literally "if X had happened it would have been good,
although...") is the idiomatic everyday construction for that kind of regret, with the trailing
*noni* adding a layer of regret or criticism that can read as accusatory depending on context. The
negative counterpart, *...nakereba yokatta*, expresses "shouldn't have..."

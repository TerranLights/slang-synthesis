## Grammar points

### Lesson 24 (贈り物 — "Gifts"), pp. 60–66
Goals: describing things that have changed between past and present; learning Japanese gift-giving customs; expressing giving/receiving with あげる and もらう.
Cultural/factual content (from a TV-program-style reading passage, paraphrased, not quoted): Japan has long-standing twice-yearly formal gift customs, お中元 (July) and お歳暮 (December); historically given in person, now increasingly outsourced to department-store delivery services; improved shipping infrastructure now allows even fresh/perishable food as a gift category. Newer, Western-influenced gift occasions (Valentine's Day, Christmas) have also become commercially entrenched, explicitly attributed in the text itself to department-store marketing and media influence — notable as the source's own account of commercialization driving custom change, not neutral tradition. On Valentine's Day specifically, the custom is women giving chocolate to coworkers/friends (an interview subject reports giving ~10 chocolates to workplace colleagues and friends) — the text does not use the term 義理チョコ ("obligation chocolate") explicitly, but the described practice matches it.
1. **〜ようになりました (negative→positive change)** — change-of-state marker for actions becoming possible/habitual, distinguished by context into individual-ability change, social-circumstance change, and personal-habit change. Explicit exclusion: inherently change-encoding verbs (治る, 慣れる, 増える, 減る) don't take 〜ようになる, paralleling the exclusion already noted for 可能形 at L22.
2. **〜なくなりました (positive→negative change)** — the reverse-direction change marker; textbook standardizes the negative-change form as い-adjective-style 行かなくなる (paralleling 固くなる) rather than the equally valid 行かないようになる, again for learner consistency.
3. **あげる (giving)** — three directional sub-patterns drilled: 私→第三者, 第三者→第三者, 聞き手(addressee)→第三者. Explicit pragmatic warning: using あげる when the recipient is the direct conversational addressee ("私はあなたにプールの招待券をあげます") can sound presumptuous/rude; the book teaches an indirect workaround instead ("プールの招待券があるんですが、いかがですか"). The receiver-is-speaker case is explicitly excluded here (that's くれる, taught L25).
4. **もらう (receiving)** — three directional sub-patterns (第三者→私, 第三者→第三者, 第三者→聞き手), explicitly contrasted against あげる via mirrored example pairs. Source-particle rule: giver marked with に when the giver is a person (either に or から both grammatical, but textbook standardizes に for people) vs. から obligatorily when the giver is an organization/institution (私は国から奨学金をもらっています).

### Lesson 25 (プールへ行かない？ — "Want to Go to the Pool?"), pp. 67–74 — フォーマル／インフォーマル (formal/informal register) lesson
Goals: understanding when です・ます体 ("formal") vs. casual peer speech ("informal") is used, and producing basic informal invitations/refusals; basic telephone responses; あげる/もらう/くれる giving-receiving system completed.
Framing note: "close friend" register here is modeled on same-generation (20s–30s) peers; the book explicitly flags that real informal speech varies further by speaker age/region/individual beyond what it teaches, and that the left-hand (informal) / right-hand (formal) page layout throughout this lesson is a deliberate direct-comparison teaching device.
Structural register rule: in informal speech, verbs and い-adjectives revert to the plain/dictionary form (基本体), but な-adjectives and nouns use the plain non-past dictionary form in the present but a different "basic form" (基本体) in the past — flagged as a point requiring care, since it doesn't fully parallel the verb/い-adjective pattern.
1. **親しい友達との会話1 (basic informal, gender-neutral core forms)** — introduces casual sentence-final rising intonation marked in the text with "?" instead of a formal question particle, e.g. 行く? Demonstrated via a telephone dialogue that itself models a formal→informal register shift: the mother answers an unknown caller with the highly formal もしもし、吉田でございます, while the daughter, once she recognizes a close friend's voice, answers with the much plainer もしもし、良子です.
2. **親しい友達との会話2 (gender-differentiated informal forms)** — explicitly teaches that male and female speakers use different informal-register forms/particles for otherwise-equivalent utterances (the book picks the higher-frequency variant of each pair for drilling, noting other variants exist by age/region).
3. **親しい友達との会話3 (contraction forms)** — 〜ている→〜てる; なくては/なければ→なくちゃ/なきゃ; ごめん/ごめんなさい as casual apology (vs. すみません required toward a social superior, explicitly cross-referenced).
4. **くれる (giving where the receiver is the speaker or speaker's in-group)** — completes the あげる/もらう/くれる triad. くれる used specifically when receiver = speaker or speaker's family/in-group (第三者→私 or 第三者→聞き手's conversational partner), governed independently of whether the giving was solicited. Crucial pragmatic nuance (explicit worked example): くれる is used purely based on the giver's good will, regardless of the receiver's attitude toward getting the gift — even "いらないと言ったけどくれた" ("I said I didn't want it but they gave it to me anyway") takes くれる, never もらう (×いらないと言ったけどもらった flagged as wrong); もらう, by contrast, can carry a nuance that the receiver actively requested/pressed for the item (相手はだめだと言ったけど、無理に頼んで、もらった — "they said no, but I pushed and got it [もらった]"). Directional minimal-pair table given: AさんはBさんにあげる ↔ BさんはAさんにもらう; AさんはBさんにあげる ↔ (different receiver) AさんはBさんにくれる when B=私/聞き手.
5. **〜けど (colloquial concessive)** — spoken/informal counterpart to written-register が; explicit instruction not to use in writing or toward superiors. Frequently appears as the 〜んだけど pre-announcement pattern, paralleling 〜んですが (L17).
Appendix-style reference boxes embedded at lesson end (参考 p.72–73), of direct sociolinguistic/phonological interest:
- **助詞の省略 (particle ellipsis in casual speech)** — を can be dropped in almost any casual utterance without sounding unnatural (ごはん食べない?); other particles (に, で, が, etc.) can usually NOT be dropped without either sounding unnatural (食堂でごはん食べない? cannot drop で) or creating real ambiguity (教室にある → 教室ある is flagged as actually misleading, potentially read as 教室がある); は can be dropped specifically when referring to an object already visually present/obvious in context (これ、おいしいね。). The book deliberately restricts its own drilling to を-ellipsis only, given the complexity/unpredictability of the full rule.
- **カジュアル疑問文のイントネーション (casual question intonation)** — Japanese words have either a 平板式 (flat) or 起伏式 (contoured/falling) pitch-accent pattern; a casual yes/no question is formed by adding rising intonation to the sentence-final word WITHOUT altering that word's underlying pitch-accent pattern — i.e. いく?(行く, flat-type) and のむ?(飲む, contoured-type) are pronounced with different pitch contours even though both just add question-rising intonation; a flattened-contour mispronunciation of a contoured-type word (錯 のむ pronounced flat, i.e. ×のむ？with the rise applied as if it were flat-type) is explicitly flagged as incorrect. This rule is stated to hold regardless of word length/part of speech (worked 3+-mora examples: あした?かんたん?せんめんじょ? for flat-type; たべる?むずかしい?コンピューター? for contoured-type).

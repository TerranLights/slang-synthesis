## Grammar points

### Kara vs. node: directness vs. reserve in giving a reason (p.34-35)
Both mean "because/since" and are interchangeable in many ordinary sentences. The real difference
surfaces in direct requests: *kara* is more direct and self-assertive, the natural choice toward
friends or younger people for a blunt request. *Node* reads as more formal/reserved — once a request
is reworded to express consideration for the listener (rather than a flat command), *node* becomes
the clearly better fit and *kara* would sound too pushy in the same slot.

### Nani-kara nani-made as the gratitude-specific "everything" (p.36-37)
*Minna* and *zenbu* are the general words for "all/everything," with *minna* skewing more
conversational and more often applied to people. But *nani-kara nani-made* ("from what to what") is
specifically preferred in expressions of thanks, because unlike the plainer words it actively
emphasizes the full extent of the kindness received.

### ku arimasen-ka vs. ja arimasen-ka: two different negative-question readings (p.38-39)
A plain adjective stem + *-ku arimasen-ka* mostly signals the speaker's concern about how the
listener will react, not an invitation to agree that something is wonderful — as in *Oishiku
arimasen-ka*, which actually implies worry that the food wasn't liked. To emphatically invite
agreement ("isn't it just great?"), the copula-based *...ja arimasen-ka* is the correct form instead.
The source's framing dialogue hinges on exactly this contrast: Mr. Lerner's *omoshiroku arimasen-ka*
should have been *omoshiroi-ja arimasen-ka*.

### Ureshii (momentary) vs. koofuku (lasting state), and the "happy" false friend (p.40-41)
*Ureshii* names joy over a specific incident; *koofuku* names a lasting happy condition and cannot
substitute for *ureshii*'s momentary sense — calling someone *koofuku-soo* over receiving a single
concert ticket overstates the moment into "this made your whole life happy" (usable only for
deliberate humor). Separately, English "happy" in situational set phrases ("I'm happy to meet you")
has no equivalent built from either Japanese word — those situations use entirely different,
unrelated set expressions.

### Kore-wa as an emphatic discourse opener, not a literal demonstrative (p.42-43)
Beyond its literal "this is" meaning, *kore-wa* functions as a set emphatic opener for thanks,
apology, or surprise — skewing toward middle-aged/elderly male speech in gratitude/apology contexts,
though young speakers also use it for surprise or strong emotion. *Kore-wa kore-wa* is a fixed
exclamation ("Oh my!") for an unexpected visitor or gift; *korya* is its casual contraction.

### Four ways to connect a list of nouns, by exhaustiveness and tone (p.44-45)
*To* implies the list named is exhaustive (nothing else included). *Ya* and the more colloquial
*toka* both imply the list is a non-exhaustive, representative sample. *Ni* stands apart: repeated
between items (A-ni B-ni C...), it gives a dramatic, impressive enumerative flavor regardless of
exhaustiveness (signaled instead by whether the list ends on a bare final noun or a trailing *ni*) —
this distinctive tone is why *ni*-enumeration shows up disproportionately often in safety slogans and
campaign slogans.

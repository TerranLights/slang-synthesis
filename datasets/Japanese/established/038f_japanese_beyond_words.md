## Grammar points

### Poetry as a geminate-consonant and su/tsu pedagogical tool
Shuntaro Tanikawa's *Kotoba-asobi Uta* (Word Play Songs) poems deliberately maximize doubled-consonant
density (the "Kappa" poem doubles every possible consonant except word-initial k, which cannot be
doubled in Japanese) and su/tsu minimal-pair density ("Usotsuki Kitsutsuki"), serving as both a
pronunciation drill and genuine literary entertainment for native speakers — the book recommends
asking a Japanese friend to help translate/memorize a Tanikawa poem as a way to combine a free
language lesson with deepening a friendship. These poems directly reprise and extend the geminate-
consonant grammar point from Chapter 3 (`038b`) and the ts/s minimal-pair point from the same chapter.
p.170-171.

### Natural speech in poetry confirms the particle-dropping pattern
The poem line "Kappa nappa katta" (natural, particle-free) versus the "textbook-correct" expansion
"Kappa wa nappa o kaimashita" is presented as independent confirmation — via literary rather than
conversational evidence — of the particle-omission point made repeatedly in Chapters 6-7 (`038d`,
`038e`): natural Japanese regularly omits case particles that most textbooks insist on including.
p.170.

### Regional/dialectal note: vowel devoicing is a standard-Japanese (Tokyo-based) feature, not universal
The text explicitly flags that in western-Japan (Kansai-area) dialects, the vowels normally devoiced
in standard Japanese shichi and kutsu are instead fully vocalized — a genuine, source-marked regional/
dialectal annotation, distinct from the otherwise standard-dialect-only coverage of this book. p.171,
attested_region: western Japan (Kansai dialects) vs. standard/Tokyo-based Japanese.

Eleanor Harz Jorden, *Beginning Japanese Part I* (Yale UP, 1963); Tony Backhouse, *The Japanese
Language* (Oxford UP, Melbourne, 1993); Jack Seward, *Japanese in Action* (John Weatherhill, Tokyo,
1968); Herbert Passin, *Encounter with Japan* (Kodansha International, Tokyo, 1982); Jiri Neustupny,
*Communicating with the Japanese* (The Japan Times, Tokyo, 1987); Peter Trudgill, *Sociolinguistics*
(Penguin, London, 1995). Trudgill's chapter "Language and Sex" is noted as citing gender-differentiated
vowel use in a dialect of Mongolian and verb-ending differences in Koasati (a Native American language
of Louisiana) as cross-linguistic parallels to Japanese onnakotoba — included here as context for why
the book repeatedly frames Japanese gendered speech as a cross-linguistically common phenomenon, not a
uniquely Japanese peculiarity, rather than as independently attested Japanese vocabulary. p.172-176.

## Grammar points

### Dake's gap, filled by ni suginai/mada/honno (p.106-107)
*...dake* is the default "only" for restricting a statement to a specific named item or group (*Ato
gofun-dake*, "only 5 more minutes"). But it cannot express the self-deprecating "merely/no more than"
sense describing a person's modest status ("he was only a student") — that specific sense requires
*...ni suginai* in formal/written registers, or the conversational substitutes *mada* (still only) and
*honno* (mere, before nouns) in everyday speech.

### ...shi for connecting statements toward the same point, even when they sound opposite (p.108-109)
*...shi* typically connects genuinely similar statements (two complaints about bad weather, two good
qualities of a person), but it can also connect two statements that sound like opposites on the
surface — as long as both serve the same larger rhetorical purpose. The chapter's framing example uses
*ii koto-mo aru-shi, warui koto-mo aru* ("there are good things and bad things") to make the single
point that the world isn't uniformly bad, which is why two seemingly contrasting clauses can both be
marked with *shi*. The related *...mo aru-shi...mo aru* and *...tari...tari suru* frames both build
contrastive-inclusive or alternating-condition statements on this same logic.

### Ima as "more," and the evaluative idiom ima hitotsu(-da) (p.110-111)
*Ima* ("now") functions as a synonym of *moo* ("more") when directly preceding an amount/time word —
*ima hitotsu* thus literally means "one more." But used predicatively (*ima hitotsu-da*), the phrase
shifts into an evaluative idiom meaning something is almost good but missing some quality needed to be
truly good — "it's lacking that one thing." Among young speakers, *ima ichi* (swapping in *ichi*,
"number one," for *hitotsu*) is an increasingly common, more distinctly familiar-register variant of
the same evaluative idiom.

### Kara is natural for requests/offers but barred before an apology (p.112-113)
Japanese commonly uses *kara* ("because") to justify a request or offer in contexts where English
would use no conjunction at all — the *kara*-clause names the situation that makes the request
appropriate (a task being nearly done licenses asking someone to wait). But this same *kara*-pattern
cannot lead into an apology — *ojama-desu-kara sumimasen* is flagged as wrong. An apology's
justification is instead introduced with a verb's te-form (*ojama-shite sumimasen*, *osoku natte
gomen-nasai*), not *kara*.

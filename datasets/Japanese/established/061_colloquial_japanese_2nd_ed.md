## Grammar points

### Basic sentence structure and particles (Unit 1)
Japanese has no singular/plural marking and no articles; the verb/copula comes at sentence end,
and the question marker `ka` follows it. `no` joins nouns in a possessive/descriptive relation
(`Tanaka san no hón`, "Mr Tanaka's book"). A noun with final-vowel accent loses that accent before
`no` (`yamá` → `yama no náka`). These linking words are called particles (or "postpositions," since
they follow rather than precede their noun, unlike English prepositions). `wa` marks the sentence
topic ("as for..."); note it is spelled with the hiragana for `ha` — an archaic spelling that
survives despite not matching modern pronunciation. `to` joins nouns ("with"/"and"). `ne` works
like `ka` but as a tag-question. (pp. 13-15)

### Japanese personal names and the politeness suffix *san*
Family name (*séi*/*myóoji*) precedes given name (*namae*); given names are reserved for family
or close friends. `san` attaches to family name, given name, or both, but is never used of oneself.
Family/place names are often two-kanji compounds showing "sequential voicing" (*rendaku*): an
initial *t-*/*k-* in the second element of a compound often becomes *d-*/*g-*, unpredictably enough
that each compound must be learned individually. (pp. 14-15)

### Verbs with *ga* vs. *o* (Unit 2)
A small set of verbs/predicates (e.g. `wakarimásu` "understand," `dekimásu` "can do," `sukí desu`
"like") mark their object with `ga` rather than the more general object-marker `o`. The source
advises treating these constructions as set phrases at this stage rather than worrying about the
underlying rule. `dáisuki` ("love," lit. "big-like") has an unusually wide usage range spanning
food, people, and activities. (pp. 33-35)

### Country/language/person derivational suffixes (Unit 2)
Japanese derives a country's language and nationality terms from the country name with fixed
suffixes: `-go` for language, `-jin` for a national of that country (e.g. `Nihón` → `Nihongo`,
`Nihonjín`). Note `Kánkoku` refers only to South Korea; North Korea is `Kita Choosen`. (p. 35-36)

### Demonstratives: *kore/sore/are* (Unit 3)
Japanese marks three degrees of distance from the speaker rather than English's two: `kore` ("this,"
near speaker), `sore` ("that," near addressee/middle distance), `are` ("that over there," away from
both speaker and addressee, often associated with a third party not present in the conversation).
(p. 53)

### Neutral vs. honorific family-member terms (Unit 3)
Japanese typically has two terms per family relation: an honorific term (used of others' family,
or one's own senior relatives when addressing them) and a neutral term (used when describing one's
own family to outsiders). The source gives a family tree with honorific terms in bold and neutral
terms in parentheses. There is no generic "brother"/"sister" — older vs. younger must always be
specified. When counting siblings (`kyóodai`), Japanese conventionally includes the speaker in the
count. Possessive terms for spouse/children also split along this honorific/neutral axis: `kánai`
(my wife) vs. `ókusan` (your/someone else's wife); `shújin` (my husband) vs. `goshújin` (your/her
husband, with honorific *go-* prefix); `kodomo` (my children) vs. `okósan` (someone else's children).
(pp. 51-52, 54)

### Numbers, counting, telling time, telephone numbers (Unit 3)
Japanese has a native number set and a Chinese-derived (on-reading) set; up to 99 is built
compositionally from the on-reading set (`juuichí` "eleven" = "ten-one"). Several numbers have two
pronunciations for superstition-avoidance reasons: `yón` is often preferred over `shi` (homophonous
with "death"); `nána` over `shichí` (easily confused with 1/4/8); `kú` carries a homophone meaning
"suffering," alongside the alternative `kyúu`. Months use the on-readings plus `-gatsú` (note:
April is `shigatsú`, not `yongatsú` — `yón` is not substituted here, an explicit irregularity).
Time uses `-ji` "o'clock" (irregular: `yóji` for 4 o'clock, dropping final -n), `hán` "half," and
`-fun` "minute." Telephone numbers lengthen `ni`→`níi` and `go`→`góo` for clarity. Age is expressed
with `-sai` on the on-reading numbers, with sound changes at 1/8/10 (`íssai`, `hássai`, `jússai`).
(pp. 47-51)

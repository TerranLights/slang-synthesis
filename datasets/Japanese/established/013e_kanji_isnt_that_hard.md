## Grammar points

### Rule 21 — There are many signs; the same sign has the same meaning (いろいろなサインがあります／同じサインは同じ意味です)

A consolidation rule, paired with Rule 22: a kanji's **sign** (付随部分, introduced in Rule 2)
narrows the stem's basic meaning to a specific category/field, and the same sign-shape keeps the
same narrowing meaning wherever it recurs. The book formally divides signs into seven position-based
types by where they sit within a kanji's square: ① hen 偏 (left), ② tsukuri 旁 (right), ③ kanmuri 冠
(top), ④ ashi 脚 (bottom), ⑤ tare 垂 (upper-left, draping down), ⑥ nyoo 繞 (lower-left, wrapping
under/around), ⑦ kamae 構 (surrounding/enclosing). Worked examples in this rule: ン "ice" (sign in
凍), 彡 "decoration" (sign in 形, matching kanmuri ツ elsewhere), 罒 "net" (kanmuri sign in 罪), 灬 "fire"
(ashi sign in 焦, equivalent to 火), 尸 "lower body" (tare sign in 尿), 乏 "to stretch" (nyoo sign in
建), 凵 and 匚 (both "box," kamae signs in 凶 and 医 respectively). (pp. 108–112)

### Rule 22 — There are many stems; the same stem has the same meaning (いろいろなステムがあります／同じステムは同じ意味です)

The stem-side counterpart to Rule 21: a kanji's **stem** (基本部分, Rule 2) carries a kanji's core
meaning regardless of which sign is attached to it, and the same stem-shape keeps the same core
meaning across different compounds. Worked stem families in this rule: 且 "to pile up many" (in
組/阻, narrowed respectively by thread/embankment signs), 交 "to combine" (in 校/効, narrowed by
tree/strength signs), 主 "not moving" (in 住/駐, narrowed by person/horse signs), and a stem built
from 又+土 meaning "to extend out straight" (in 怪/軽, narrowed by heart/wheel signs). (pp. 113–117)

### Rule 23 — There are two meanings; one meaning branches off from the original meaning (二つの意味があります／元の意味から分かれました)

Most kanji have a single meaning tied directly to their picture, but some have **two** meanings: an
original, concrete picture-based meaning, plus a second meaning that developed/branched off from the
first via a plausible semantic extension (not an unrelated second kanji sharing the same shape by
coincidence — a real derivational relationship). Examples: 空 "sky" (concrete) → "empty" (the sky
contains nothing); 角 "horn" → "corner" (a horn's tip is sharp); 市 "market" → "city" (the place where
a market is held); 長 "long [hair]" → "chief/head" (elders with long hair ranked highest); 背 "the
back" → "to disobey" (turning one's back on a superior); 表 "to display" → "outside/surface" (a
newborn, once displayed, enters the outside world). Two further examples (扇, 駅) illustrate a related
but distinct phenomenon: the kanji's meaning stays the same while the real-world referent's material
changes entirely (feather fan → paper fan; horse relay-station → train station), and the same kanji
is retained across that change rather than branching to a new one. (pp. 118–122)

### Rule 24 — Kanji are interesting; kanji are combinations of units (漢字はおもしろいです／漢字は単位の組み合わせです)

The book's closing rule, functioning as a review/synthesis rather than introducing a new mechanism:
a kanji with many strokes is not inherently hard — it is simply a combination of the same recurring
units taught throughout Rules 1–23 (e.g. the components of 桜 "cherry blossom," built from 木+ツ+女,
each independently familiar from 休/学/婦/妻/楼 etc.), and breaking any complex kanji down into its
component units renders it tractable the same way. The rule's eight worked examples (歌/染/寝/夢/葉/
曜/親/塩) are presented explicitly as practice material for this unit-decomposition skill, each
combining components already established earlier in the book (可+欠; シ+九+木; ウ+爿+ヨワ又; 艹+目+夕;
艹+世+木; 日+ヨヨ+隹; 立+木+目+ル; 土+人+口+皿). This closes out the book's core 24-rule system. (pp.
123–127)

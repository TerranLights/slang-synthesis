## Grammar points

### Self-introduction formula and name-card (meeshi) etiquette (p.8-9)
*Hajimemashite* ("How do you do?") plus *Doozo yoroshiku* ("Glad to meet you") is the standard
opening exchange at a first meeting. The person being introduced can optionally restate their own
name even after the introducer has already given it — more formal/polite than leaving it out. When
cards are exchanged, correct etiquette includes orienting your own card so the recipient can read it
right-side-up, and visibly studying a card you receive rather than pocketing it immediately. Physical
distance between speakers during this exchange tends to run greater in Japanese conversation than in
English conversation generally, correlating with whether the parties bow (greater distance) or shake
hands (closer) — most Japanese, especially women, favor bowing.

### Referencing a mutual acquaintance, framed by relative closeness (p.10-11)
At a first meeting, bringing up a mutual acquaintance is a standard, expected conversational opener,
but the correct phrasing depends on which of the two new acquaintances is closer to the shared
contact. If the acquaintance is closer to the speaker, the speaker can simply describe their own
relationship with them. If the acquaintance is closer to the listener, the speaker instead expresses
gratitude toward the shared contact, framed through the listener (an expression of gratitude is
treated as more polite than a direct compliment in this specific context). The same closer-to-whom
logic extends to meeting a family member of an acquaintance, or an acquaintance of a family member.

### Re-meeting etiquette: referencing and either thanking or apologizing for the previous encounter (p.12-13)
Re-meeting someone after an interval calls for referencing the earlier meeting and expressing either
gratitude or apology about it, even if the same sentiment was already expressed by phone or in
writing at the time — repeating it in person is still expected and valued. These references normally
open with a vague time marker (*senjitsu*, *kono-aida*, *kinoo*, *yuube*, *sakihodo*) rather than a
precise date/time, since stating an exact date for a routine social reference would sound oddly
formal/clinical. The sincerity behind these expressions can range from genuine to largely formal, but
their real social function — signaling continued investment in the relationship — holds either way.

### Itte-rasshai/itte-kimasu family-departure formula vs. Sayonara (p.14-17; two essays)
*Sayonara* is structurally wrong for departures within an in-group: it implies two separate
individuals parting (or, in the colleague-parting case, one member splitting off from a closely
united group), and among actual family members it additionally implies permanent/final separation —
so family members never use it toward each other under any circumstance, including long trips
abroad. The correct family-departure pair is *Itte-rasshai* (said by those staying) and
*Itte-kimasu*/the more formal-humble *Itte-mairimasu* (said by the one leaving), answered on return
with *Okaerinasai*. This family register is sometimes extended to someone being treated as a family
member (a long-term homestay guest, a close neighbor) — a homestay guest is advised to use
family-style farewells rather than *Sayonara* even when leaving for good at the end of the stay.
Among colleagues, by contrast, *Sayonara* is one option but not the only one: *Ja*/*Ja, mata* cover
casual daily partings, *Osaki-ni*(*-shitsuree-shimasu*) is used by whoever leaves the office first
(met with the fixed response *Otsukaresama*), and more traditional/age-skewed alternatives
(*Gokigen-yoo*, *Gomen-kudasai(mase)*, *Gomen-nasai(mashi)*, or youth-coded *Bai-bai*) round out the
register range. General guidance: default to *Sayonara* when politeness isn't required, but switch to
*Shitsuree-shimasu* when it is, and never use *Sayonara* toward someone who treats you as family.

### Closing a telephone conversation: confirm, then thank/apologize, then part (p.18-19)
Japanese phone calls are observed to close in three distinct stages rather than ending abruptly:
first a confirmation that the actual business matter is settled, then an expression of gratitude or
apology (sometimes both stacked together), and only then the parting formula itself (*Sayonara* when
informal, *Shitsuree-shimasu*/*Gomen-kudasai* when polite, or just *Ja* between close friends).
Gratitude/apology expressions are often repeated more than once within this closing sequence, and the
frequency of repetition scales with the required degree of politeness — the practical recommendation
is to get comfortable repeating these phrases rather than treating a single utterance as sufficient.

### The business-discussion opening signal, and who is obligated to give it (p.20-21)
Politeness requires an explicit verbal signal (*De, kyoo-wa nani-ka...* or *Tokoro-de kyoo-wa...*)
marking the shift from small talk to the actual business matter — jumping straight into business
without it is rude. Responsibility for giving the signal falls on whichever party holds the position
of relative advantage in the transaction (e.g., the buying/favor-requesting side is at a
disadvantage and must wait for the signal rather than give it). A speaker needs to correctly judge
their own position in a given relationship to know whether they're expected to initiate.

### Ordering at a restaurant: staff prompts, stalling, and getting a server's attention (p.22-23)
Covers the standard staff-initiated exchange (*Irasshaimase. Nan-ni nasaimasu-ka* → customer's order,
closed with a verb from the *...kudasai/onegai-shimasu/itadakimasu/moraimashoo* register set), how to
buy time before deciding (*Chotto...* alone is sufficient; *Chotto matte-kudasai* is the fuller
form) or defer until a companion arrives, and the staff follow-up prompt *Okimari-desu-ka*. Notes
that English's "What's the chef's recommendation?" has no fixed Japanese equivalent — speakers
instead paraphrase around the same social goal (e.g. mentioning it's their first visit to Japan, or
that they've heard good things about the restaurant, before asking what to get). Also covers how to
address waitstaff: the dictionary word *kyuuji* is never used for direct address; *oneesan*/*ojoosan*
work but are uncommon; plain *Sumimasen* or *Onegai-shimasu* (sometimes combined) is the broadly
correct way to get a server's attention.

### Unannounced visiting: the social logic and the at-the-door closing ritual (p.24-25)
Visiting without notice was traditionally preferred precisely because it spares the host the
obligation to formally prepare food/drink for a guest; calling ahead (or, as a modern compromise,
calling right before arriving) has become more common, especially in cities, but is explicitly framed
as reducing the guest's imposition rather than as simply more polite. An unannounced or short-notice
visitor customarily declines an offer to come inside and conducts business at the door instead,
triggering a short ritual exchange: the host's token self-blaming apology for not having served tea
is met with the visitor's hurried, firmly-toned denial plus a reciprocal self-blame for the
imposition of the sudden visit.

### Giving reasons for a request: kara/node directness vs. genuine social reserve (p.26-27)
Direct *kara*-linked reason-then-request phrasing is fine within family/close-friend registers but
sounds presumptuous in social situations — it implies the speaker is simply stating a fact that
entitles them to the request. The essay explicitly separates two different things often conflated:
*verbal* politeness (swapping *kudasai* for *kudasaimasenka*, or *kara* for the softer-sounding
*node*) versus genuine social *reserve* (whether the reason is stated at all). Even a politely-worded
*node* version can still be wrong if real hesitancy/reserve is socially called for — the textbook-taught
fix (more polite wording) only solves the verbal-politeness axis, not the reserve axis. The genuinely
reserved approach either omits the reason and just makes a hedged request, or states one's own needy
situation and waits for the listener to volunteer help rather than asking outright.

### Introducing oneself as someone's family member: whether to name the relation at all (p.28-29)
The real choice when introducing (or being introduced as) a family member isn't which term to use
(*kanai* vs. *tsuma* for "wife") but whether to state the relational term at all — omitting it
entirely, relying on context, is often the more natural option. A term like *kanai* is used only when
its identifying function is actually needed (e.g. one's own self-introduction with no one else
present to introduce you); once someone else has already identified you by the term, you don't repeat
it. This extends to how a company's reception staff infer a caller's identity: a caller who gives an
employee's bare name with no honorific (-san) is understood to be a family member purely from that
register cue, triggering a specific phone-relay phrasing.

### Offering a gift: the self-deprecating ritual sequence, and its real function (p.30-31)
Japanese has no conventionally-used equivalent of "please accept this" — despite such phrases
existing in the dictionary — and offering a gift instead follows a fixed multi-step ritual: a
self-deprecating opener belittling the gift, a protest from the recipient, a reinforced belittling
statement from the offerer, an explicit stated reason the recipient should overcome their reserve,
and only then acceptance and thanks. The same shape recurs, with plainer wording, even in casual
conversation between good friends, confirming it isn't purely a function of formal register. Some
younger speakers have dropped the specific phrase *tsumaranai mono* between close friends, but
belittling one's own gift in some form is still regarded as good etiquette generally.

### Asking someone to return a phone call: a four-way politeness matrix (p.32-33)
Leaving a callback request requires independently calibrating two different relationships — the
caller's own relationship to the absent party (A) and their relationship to whoever answered the
phone (B) — producing four distinct register combinations (both polite / A familiar, B polite / A
polite, B familiar / both familiar), each realized through a different pairing of the verb for "when
he returns" and the verb for the relay-request itself. In every combination, however, naming the
absent party explicitly is avoided in favor of leaving the subject unstated and understood from
context — this ellipsis holds regardless of which politeness combination is in play.

### Conveying a message: fixed time-agent-action order, and how to report what was said (p.34-35)
Relaying a call or visit follows a fixed internal order — time reference first, then the caller's
identity, then (in a following sentence) the content of the message — extended to describing a
visitor's call as well as a phone message. Reporting what the absent party said uses a graded hearsay
register: the plain *...soo-desu* pattern is standard; *...to osshaimashita* is the polite form;
*...to iimashita* is grammatically fine but flagged as sounding childish; *...tte* is the familiar
contracted form. The two-sentence structure (fact of the call, then the message) can optionally be
fused into one sentence via the *-te* form of the first verb.

### Declining an offer of help: ritual refusal vs. genuine decline, disambiguated by repetition and tone (p.36-37)
The default decline formula *Iie, kekkoo-desu* is ambiguous on its own — a single instance said
without a definite tone is often just an expected first-round ritual refusal, which the offerer is
expected to override by insisting again. A genuinely final decline is instead signaled by repeating
*kekkoo-desu* and adding an explicit justification. When a helper is persistently unwilling to take
no for an answer, two distinct strategies for shutting the offer down are documented: asserting
one's own capability via the *nantoka...dekiru* ("can manage somehow") construction, or stating a
positive personal wish (wanting to practice the language, wanting an adventure) — often paired with
an explicit safety-net reassurance so the refusal doesn't read as dismissing the helper's genuine
concern.

### Expressing gratitude for help: tense-marking as an effort/duration signal (p.38-39)
*Arigatoo-gozaimasu* (non-past) is used before or during help being given; the past-tense
*Arigatoo-gozaimashita* specifically implies the help took significant time or effort to complete —
the same *-ta*-form-as-completion-emphasis logic documented elsewhere in the series recurs here in
apology forms too (*Doomo sumimasen-deshita*, familiar *Warukatta-ne/-wane*), marking that a
troublesome action has now definitively concluded. A further expected step after initial thanks is
to explicitly affirm the help's value/effectiveness (*Okagesama-de, hayaku katazukimashita*, etc.),
not just acknowledge it was given.

### Making explanations: gauging the listener's existing knowledge without giving offense (p.40-41)
Over-explaining implies the listener is ignorant; under-explaining leaves them without needed
information — and in a modern society where speaker and listener can't be assumed to share the same
background knowledge, some way of checking the listener's existing knowledge is functionally
necessary. But a direct check (*Shitte-imasu-ka*/*Gozonji-desu-ka*, "do you know?") risks sounding
like a challenge to the listener's intelligence or professional competence, especially toward a
social superior — illustrated by the frame story's own documented real mistake (Mr. Lerner using it
on a company director). The social fix is a set of hedging openers (*Gozonji-kamo shiremasen-ga*,
*Gozonji-no yoo-ni*, etc.) that let the speaker proceed to explain without implying any doubt about
the listener's knowledge, while still leaving room for the listener to say they don't already know
and prompt a fuller explanation.

### Responding to a compliment: a three-way gradient between denial and acceptance (p.42-43)
A compliment on one's Japanese is frequently just a conversational opener (many Japanese speakers,
unused to talking with foreigners, default to this as an icebreaker), appropriately met with a flat
denial (*Iie, sonna koto-wa arimasen*). When the compliment is repeated — signaling the speaker is
genuinely impressed rather than just opening conversation — three distinct response strategies are
available: accept happily and add a forward-looking statement of intent to improve further; partially
admit the compliment while hedging; or admit the improvement but attribute credit elsewhere (to
others' help, to a good teacher). The first and third can be combined. General guidance favors
denying first and only partially admitting/crediting-elsewhere as needed, though flatly denying a
competence just directly demonstrated to a close friend can itself come across oddly.

### Describing a stomachache: the doctor's required level of qualitative detail (p.44-45)
A vague pain report (*Hidoku itamimasu*, "terribly painful") is insufficient for a doctor — expected
follow-up detail covers the qualitative character of the pain (the *kirikiri*/*shikushiku*
mimicry-word distinction: sharp nail-like pain that can occur anywhere in the body, vs. a duller
continuous pain specific to the stomach/intestines), whether it's triggered or worsened by external
pressure, its relationship to hunger/meals/specific foods, and its onset timing and
constancy/intermittency. *Shikushiku* additionally extends metaphorically to describe a person (often
a woman or child) crying quietly and continuously — the same underlying sense of a dull, continuous,
not-sharply-punctuated sensation.

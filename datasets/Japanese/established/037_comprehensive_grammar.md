## Grammar points

### Alphabetical-dictionary macrostructure (Introduction, pp.xvi–xx)

Unlike a lesson-sequenced textbook, every entry (grammatical term or Japanese-language item) is
alphabetized and independently cross-referenced; sub-entries are numbered and previewed in a
contents box at the head of each entry. All example sentences are drawn from real printed Japanese
media (newspapers, etc.) unless marked `%` for a constructed example, and are given in original
script + ad-hoc-spaced romanization + English translation — the explicit design intent being that
a reader can reverse-engineer unfamiliar kanji/vocabulary from the parallel romanization and
translation. This is the same design principle this extraction leans on given the font-cipher
corruption of the kanji/kana text layer.

### Classicisms: bungo vs. kōgo and the classical copula (§22, pp.59–61)

Japan's written standard was based on classical (Heian-era) grammar — bungo — from antiquity until
the Meiji-era *genbun itchi* ("one form for spoken and written language") movement shifted official
written standard to the Tokyo vernacular, kōgo, around the turn of the 20th century; bungo
continued to decline but never disappeared, surviving today mainly in haiku/waka poetry and a
residue of fixed idiomatic patterns (e.g. -neba naranai, -zu ni wa irarenai) still used in everyday
kōgo speech/writing. The classical copula nari survives in some headline/formal writing (e.g.
"haruka nari jōhō haiuē" — '[We're] a long way off [from] the information superhighway') and in
idiomatic N-modifying forms like ika naru N mo ('no matter which N', a more formal variant of donna
N demo). This is a clean, book-length-attested register axis (contemporary-colloquial vs.
classical-literary) independent of the colloquial/formal axis already tracked via Usage Tier.

### Dialect as register: Kansai forms (§48, pp.125–128)

The book frames Kansai dialect explicitly as a social-register phenomenon, not merely a regional
curiosity: it notes that young Tokyo speakers deliberately code-switch into Kansai forms as a
fashionable affectation, driven by the prominence of Kansai-origin television personalities. This
is itself a slang-relevant mechanism (prestige-driven dialect borrowing into an unrelated region's
informal register) worth flagging for the mechanics-analysis phase, distinct from the individual
lexical/morphological substitutions in Table 10 above. The chapter also notes a popularity-ranked
anecdote (a magazine-style survey of favorite Kansai expressions among non-Kansai speakers): ōki ni
ranked #1, sukkiyanen ('you like it, don't you') #2, and kamahen ('I don't mind') #3 — all
characterized in-source as "warm-hearted expressions," a connotation note worth preserving.

### Ellipsis in informal speech (§52, pp.137–139)

Particle ellipsis (dropping o, wa, or ga) is marked as specifically an informal-speech phenomenon,
often signaled in writing by a comma where the particle would have been (e.g. "tabako yamenakya" —
object particle o dropped before "yamenakya", 'I must stop smoking'). The book also distinguishes
ellipsis from simply not using Japanese's regularly-absent personal pronouns (which aren't ellipsis
at all, just the default). Noun ellipsis follows a left-to-right pattern opposite English's
typical right-ellipsis (Japanese drops the *first* repeated noun, matching its head-final syntax).
Predicate ellipsis is common where the implied verb is recoverable from context — e.g. "ofukuro ni
hanataba o" ('[Flowers] for mum!'), with the gift-giving verb (ageru, etc.) left implicit.

### Family address: the ingroup/outgroup register split (§53, pp.140–143, Table 12)

The central finding of this entry: Japanese kinship address is governed by an explicit **ingroup/
outgroup distinction** layered on top of (and partly independent from) the honorific system. When
*referring* to one's own family members while speaking to an outgroup listener, honorific prefixes
(o-/go-) and personal suffixes (-san/-sama) are dropped — one's own father is "chichi," never
"o-tōsan," when telling an outsider about him — while the listener's or a third party's equivalent
relative keeps the full honorific form. Three explicit exceptions are flagged: (1) parent-to-child
address, where parents use (o-)tōsan/(o-)kāsan and papa/mama as if they were first-person
self-reference terms toward their children; (2) spousal address, where husbands/wives also use
(o-)tōsan/(o-)kāsan-style terms for each other; (3) narrative/historical/fictional-reference
contexts, where the honorific-looking "first column" terms (tsuma/otto for spouses specifically,
by exception) are used regardless of in/outgroup status. The choice between the honorific prefixes
o- vs. go- itself tracks a second register axis: o- for native Japanese (WJ) vocabulary, go- for
Sino-Japanese (SJ) vocabulary, with a few lexicalized exceptions (o-denwa 'telephone', SJ-sourced
but takes o-). Authentic example sentences are drawn from real dialogue/reportage, including a
newspaper account of a young woman using confrontational-flirtatious "ne" toward a male companion
near a love hotel, and a male pupil's classroom anecdote using ofukuro.

### Honorific and humble verb morphology, incl. the irregular paradigm (§65, pp.179, 185–187)

Two systematic (productive) formations exist: honorific o/go-V-stem ni naru (replacing ni naru
with kudasai gives the common polite-command form o/go-V-stem kudasai) and humble o/go-V-stem suru.
The choice between o- and go- prefixes again tracks the WJ/SJ vocabulary-origin axis (with
exceptions, e.g. o-denwa). A "super-honorific" extension exists for extremely high-status referents
(the Imperial family, in the book's own authentic media examples): o/go-V-stem ni *nararu* (an
extra honorific-passive layer stacked onto the already-honorific ni naru). Beyond the productive
pattern, a closed set of common verbs instead takes wholly suppletive irregular honorific/humble
forms (Table 13, captured in full in the vocabulary table above) — several of these irregular verbs
are themselves irregular in their own -masu/imperative conjugation (gozaru → gozai-masu with no
imperative at all; irassharu → irasshai-masu, imperative irasshai, etc.), a second layer of
irregularity stacked on top of the honorific irregularity itself.

### Sentence-ending colloquial contractions: -toku, -chau/-jau, -cha/-ja (§207, §209, §210, pp.516–519)

Three closely related and highly productive contraction patterns were sampled, all converting a
formal/neutral -te-form compound into a colloquial-register sentence ending: -te oku → -toku
("do in readiness/preparation"), -te shimau/-de shimau → -chau/-jau ("do completely" / "do with an
unintended or regrettable result" — the book notes the two senses are often not cleanly
separable), and -te wa/-de wa → -cha/-ja (conditional "if"/repeated-action marker). All three are
confirmed via the book's own authentic-media example sentences (not constructed examples), and one
example sentence stacks a -toku contraction together with a Kansai-dialect honorific form in the
same utterance ("okunnahare"), demonstrating that standard-colloquial contraction and regional
dialect operate as independent, stackable register axes rather than mutually exclusive choices —
directly relevant to any future slang-synthesis model of how informal-register markers compose.

### toka as the colloquial register-shift of nado (§220.2, p.543)

nado ("and the like", "such as") has a colloquial register-equivalent toka, confirmed via an
authentic constructed-dialogue example about reading Japanese newspapers vs. manga ("Kureyon
Shin-chan toka") — notable for being the one example in this sampled set where the "classical
reference-grammar register" of the book's own prose is demonstrating a genuinely youth-coded,
pop-culture-referencing colloquial register example, bridging this reference grammar's otherwise
fairly formal example corpus toward genuinely slangy/contemporary speech.

## Grammar points

### §1 Pronunciation (vowels, consonants, devoicing, doubling)

Japanese has five pure (non-diphthong) vowels (a, e, i, o, u) each with a long counterpart marked by a macron, which doubles the vowel's duration and can be meaning-distinctive (p. 2–3, worked minimal pairs above). The high vowels /i/ and /u/ devoice (become nearly silent) between voiceless consonants or word-finally after one, e.g. *sukiyaki*, *tabemashita* (p. 3). Several consonants differ from English articulation: Japanese *f* is a bilabial fricative (a light whistle-like puff, not labiodental); *g* is always the hard "go" sound, never "age," though it may nasalize to [ŋ] mid-word; *r* is a flap made by lightly touching the tongue to the alveolar ridge (closer to Spanish tapped r, not English retroflex r or a trill); *s* is always voiceless, never a /z/-like "pleasure" sound (p. 4). Consonant doubling is phonemic (changes word meaning), realized as a held pause before the doubled consonant rather than a repeated articulation (p. 4–5; cross-referenced to the fuller §20 double-consonant minimal-pair list).

### §2 The Japanese writing system

Japanese text uses three character systems together: kanji (logographic, borrowed from Chinese starting in the 4th–5th century, often with multiple readings per character; ~2,000 "basic" kanji learned through junior high), hiragana (native-word/grammatical-element syllabary), and katakana (mainly for loanwords) — collectively hiragana+katakana are called kana (p. 6–9). Traditional orientation is top-to-bottom, right-to-left, though left-to-right horizontal writing (as in English) is also standard today (p. 6). Romaji (p. 10) is the Roman-alphabet transliteration system used throughout this book, specifically a modified Hepburn system. (Full hiragana/katakana syllable charts appear on pp. 8–9 of this source but are not re-extracted here — see this project's `001_guide_to_hiragana_katakana.md` and `020_remembering_the_kana.md` for the already-captured syllabary.)

### §3.1–3.2 What is a sentence / the English sentence

A sentence is defined as an organized word series making a statement, question, etc.; Japanese sentences (in romaji) use a capital first letter and a period, but never a question mark or exclamation mark — question force comes entirely from the particle `ka`, not punctuation (p. 11). English is characterized as SVO (subject-verb-object), shared with French, German, Spanish, Italian (p. 11); English sentences split into subject (who/what the sentence is about) and predicate (p. 12–13).

### §3.3-1 Subject-Object-Verb sentences

Japanese is SOV, grouped with Korean, Mongolian, and Turkish — the verb obligatorily comes last (p. 14).

### §3.3-2 Subject and predicate

A Japanese sentence may omit its subject/topic but must have a predicate, and the predicate must be verb-final — a verb, or a verbal form (noun+copula, or a verbal adjective) (p. 15–16). Two governing word-order principles are stated explicitly: (1) the predicate must be sentence-final; (2) a particle must immediately follow the word(s) it marks (p. 16). Beyond these two constraints word order is flexible; topic/subject usually comes first, though time adverbials may precede or follow it, and most modifiers precede what they modify (p. 16–17). Question-word questions (when/who/what/why) normally place the question word right after the subject/topic, but this placement is not rigid (p. 18).

### §3.3-3 Incomplete sentences (pro-drop)

Japanese freely omits a subject/topic recoverable from context — unlike English, where such omission would read as fragmentary, Japanese speakers consider topic-dropped sentences fully complete (p. 19). Even so, a predicate cannot be dropped: answering "What is this?" with bare "A book" is not possible in Japanese — the copula (`desu`, "to be") must still appear, e.g. *Hon desu* (p. 19–20). The book also notes a politeness-driven omission: *anata* ("you") is grammatically available but typically dropped once context makes the addressee obvious (p. 20).

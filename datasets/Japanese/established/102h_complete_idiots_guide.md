## Grammar points

### Complaint register: indirectness even under genuine grievance

Chapter 25 (pp.321-329) is explicitly framed by the book as addressing a real cultural tension:
Japanese people are stereotyped (and the book concurs this has a real basis) as rarely showing
public frustration/anger, yet do make complaints when warranted — the chapter's whole thrust is
teaching complaint phrasing that stays indirect and non-confrontational even while asserting a
real grievance (e.g. "There has not been any maid service yet..." left trailing rather than stated
as a direct accusation). This is a direct continuation of the softening-conjunction (ga...) and
chotto-hedging patterns documented throughout the book (Parts 3-6), now applied specifically to
the highest-stakes pragmatic context (a customer complaint) — strong confirmatory evidence that
indirectness-as-politeness is a structural throughline of this book's entire presentation of
Japanese, not an incidental feature of any one speech act.

### -kai homophony: "times" vs. "floor"

Two distinct counters — -kai for "number of times/occurrences" (Ch.23, medicine dosage) and -kai
for "building floor" (Ch.24) — share an identical romanized form. Worth flagging explicitly for
later disambiguation work since the project's morpheme-breakdown conventions could otherwise
conflate them as a single polysemous morpheme rather than two homophonous-but-distinct counters.

### -ra conditional connector's tense mismatch

The -ra connector ("when/if X, then Y") attaches to a verb's -mashita (past) form even when the
event it describes is hypothetical/future (e.g. "if you find it [in the future], call me" uses the
grammatically past mitsukari-mashita ra). This is a real form-meaning mismatch — the connector's
own morphology doesn't track the real-world tense of the conditional clause — worth flagging for
anyone building a derived conlang's own conditional-marking system, since it demonstrates that a
"conditional" marker needn't be tense-neutral in its own right.

## Grammar points

### §7.8 The presumptive mood (darō/deshō and -ō/-yō/-mashō)

A genuinely important mood with no exact English equivalent, used to soften a statement into probability/belief/supposition rather than flat assertion — the source glosses it variously as "probably," "suppose," "wonder," "guess" (p. 109–112). Formed simply by adding `darō` (plain) / `deshō` (polite) onto the plain form of the verb, including its negative and past forms (full affirmative/negative × present/past grid given, p. 109–110). A second, distinct sub-mood expresses *intention* rather than probability: `-ō`/`-yō` (plain, c-stem verbs change final `-u`→`-ō`; v-stem verbs add `-yō` to the stem; irregulars `suru`→`shiyō`, `kuru`→`koyō`) and the polite equivalent `-mashō` (added to the infinitive) (p. 111–114). Register/gender note worth flagging: as a sentence-final form, plain `-ō`/`-yō` expresses the speaker's own intention but is explicitly described as *abrupt* and *used mostly by men*; as a non-final (medial) form it instead expresses intention/probability neutrally (p. 112). The polite `-mashō` form is the one actually recommended for "Let's..."/"Shall we...?" (p. 112, 114).

### §7.9 The imperative mood (a three-tier directive-politeness gradient)

The source documents a clear three-level politeness gradient for commands/requests, worth tracking explicitly for register mechanics: (1) the **true imperative** (c-stem: `-e` on the stem; v-stem: `-ro` on the stem; negative: `-na` on the plain present) — flagged as *too abrupt for most actual usage*, taught mainly for recognition (p. 115–116); (2) **`-nasai`** (added to the infinitive) — "somewhat less harsh," not used with negatives (p. 116); (3) **gerund + `kudasai`** — the form actually recommended for polite requests/commands, with its own negative built as `-de kudasai` onto the negative plain present (p. 116–117). `Kudasai` can also stand alone (no verb needed) when requesting a physical object (p. 117).

### §7.10 The conditional mood (four distinct "if" constructions)

Japanese has (at least) four overlapping but non-identical conditional forms, each with its own nuance (p. 118–124): **`-eba`** ("provisional" — "provided X happens"; c-stem: drop final `-u`, add `-eba`; negative: drop final `-i` of the plain negative, add `-kereba`; also usable with verbal adjectives by dropping `-i` and adding `-kereba`, negative via the adverbial form + `-nakereba`) (p. 118–119); **`-tara`** (plain past + `-ra`; broadest range of "if/when" meanings — past occurrence, hypothetical past, future, habitual — and also combines with `-i`/`-na` adjectives and nouns) (p. 120–121); **`nara`** (attaches to verbs/adjectives/adjectival nouns/nouns in present or past form; similar meaning to `-tara` but can carry a strong contrary-to-fact, English-subjunctive-like sense) (p. 122); and **`to`** (present forms only; often interchangeable with `-tara`/`-eba` but typically used for "whenever/when-if" generalizations) (p. 123–124).

### §7.11 The potential mood (ability)

Two independent construction strategies for "can/be able to": (1) suffixal — c-stem verbs add `-eru`/`-emasu` (negative `-enai`/`-emasen`) to the stem; v-stem verbs add `-rareru`/`-raremasu` (negative `-renai`/`-remasen`) to the stem (p. 123–125); (2) periphrastic — a noun (or a verb nominalized via dictionary-form + `koto`) + `ga` + the appropriate form of `dekiru`/`dekimasu` "can do" (p. 124–125). The second strategy cross-references the broader nominalization topic (full treatment deferred to §7.19).

### §7.12 The passive voice (a genuine two-way typological split)

Japanese passive has two functionally distinct uses, a notable typological point: (1) the **ordinary/direct passive**, structurally like English (agent optionally expressed with `ni`), formed by adding `-areru`/`-aremasu` (c-stem) or `-rareru`/`-raremasu` (v-stem) to the stem (negatives `-arenai`/`-aremasen`, `-rarenai`/`-raremasen`) — the source notes this use is more restricted in Japanese than in English, and can sound awkward even where grammatically well-formed (p. 125–127); and (2) the **"adversity" (a.k.a. "suffering") passive**, which the source explicitly states "English has no corresponding use" for — it marks that an event (which may come from a transitive *or intransitive* verb, including verbs with no direct object at all, like "rain falling" or "mother dying") happened *to* someone with an adverse/regrettable effect on them, e.g. literally "Mary was died by her mother" = "Mary's mother died (on her)," or "Mr. Tanaka was fallen on by rain" = "Mr. Tanaka got caught in the rain" (p. 127–128). This adversity-passive construction is a flagship, distinctively-Japanese grammatical mechanism worth carrying forward into later mechanics/slang analysis — it grammaticalizes a victim/affected-party perspective that has no direct analog in English passive voice.

### §7.13 The causative ("make/let someone do")

Formed by adding `-aseru`/`-asemasu` (c-stem, negative `-asenai`/`-asemasen`) or `-saseru`/`-sasemasu` (v-stem, negative `-sasenai`/`-sasemasen`) to the stem. A single form is genuinely ambiguous between a coercive "make (someone) do" reading and a permissive "let (someone) do" reading, disambiguated only by context (p. 128–130) — e.g. `harawasete kudasai` "please let me pay" vs. `benkyō sasemashita` "I made (him) study."

### §7.14 The causative passive ("be forced to")

Stacks passive marking onto the causative stem: c-stem adds `-aserareru`/`-aseraremasu` (negative `-aserarenai`/`-aseraremasen`); v-stem adds `-saserareru`/`-saseraremasu` (negative `-saserarenai`/`-saseraremasen`) (p. 129–130). Unlike the ambiguous plain causative, this form is specifically "forced to," with no permissive reading (p. 129).

### §7.15 The giving and receiving verbs (a socially-indexed verb system)

A genuinely major topic: which verb to use for "give"/"receive" is determined jointly by (a) the direction of the action (to the speaker's side vs. away from it) and (b) the relative social status of giver and receiver — not by the physical act alone (p. 130–134). Three directional triads, each split into a plain and honorific/humble-register member:

- **Giving *to* the speaker or the speaker's in-group**: `kureru` (plain) / `kudasaru` (used when the giver is a superior) (p. 131).
- **Giving *from* the speaker to someone else**: `ageru` (plain) / `sashiageru` (used when the recipient is a superior) (p. 132).
- **Receiving**: `morau` (plain) / `itadaku` (used when receiving from a superior) (p. 133).

Critically, these verbs are not limited to concrete objects — used as auxiliaries after another verb's gerund form, they extend the same directional/status-sensitive "giving/receiving" logic to *actions themselves* (benefactive constructions): e.g. `tsukutte kureru` "(kindly) made [it, for my benefit]," `katte sashiageru` "bought [it, for a superior's benefit]," `itte morau`/`itte itadaku` "had [someone, of lower/higher status respectively] go [somewhere] for me" (p. 134–135). This benefactive-auxiliary extension is the mechanism by which Japanese grammaticalizes an ongoing sense of who is doing whom a favor, directly relevant to in-group/out-group and status-marking slang mechanics.

### §7.16 Honorific and humble forms (register as a verb-paradigm property)

Frames politeness as a two-axis system distinct from the plain/polite register already covered in §7.5: **honorific** language elevates the person being addressed/discussed, while **humble** language lowers the speaker/the speaker's in-group; both axes can co-occur with the plain/polite distinction (p. 136). Multiple productive construction strategies are given: (1) the **passive form itself can double as an honorific** (e.g. `arukareru` "walk," `sareru` for `suru`, `korareru` for `kuru`) (p. 136); (2) **`o` + infinitive + `desu`/`ni naru`** as a general honorific construction (p. 137); (3) **`o` + infinitive + `kudasai`** for honorific-register requests (p. 138); (4) **`o` + infinitive + `suru`/`itasu`** as a general humble construction (p. 138–139). Beyond these productive patterns, a closed set of common verbs has entirely **suppletive** honorific/humble forms that must be memorized individually (full table p. 139, captured in the vocabulary table above: `iku`/`kuru`/`iru` all honorifically collapse to the single verb `irassharu`, while humbly they split only partially into `mairu` (go/come) vs. `oru` (be); `suru`→`nasaru`/`itasu`; `iu`→`ossharu`/`mōsu`; `taberu`→`meshiagaru`/`itadaku`; `miru`→`goran ni naru`/`haiken suru`). The asymmetric collapsing of distinct plain verbs onto a single honorific form (three verbs → one `irassharu`) versus the humble register's partial preservation of those distinctions is a structurally interesting and, as far as this chunk shows, previously uncaptured finding about how the two register axes are not mirror images of each other.

### §7.17 Clauses — dependent-clause chaining via -te/de

States a foundational Japanese syntactic constraint: independent clauses cannot simply be strung together with a coordinating conjunction the way English uses "and" — in Japanese, one clause is the sentence-final main/independent clause, and all others are grammatically dependent on it (p. 136) — this directly reinforces the clause-final-marking pattern already seen in §7.5 (register) and §7.7-3 (gerund listing). The gerund (`-te`/`-de`) form of a verb (and the parallel `-kute` form of verbal adjectives / `de` form of the copula for adjectival nouns and nouns, cross-referenced to §8) is the primary mechanism for chaining such dependent clauses, and can express at least three distinct relationships depending on context: **sequential** (first X, then Y, then Z — p. 139–140), **parallel** (simultaneous/co-occurring actions or states — p. 140, continued into the next chunk), and (per the chapter heading, to be covered further) **causal** relationships.

## Grammar points

### *...wake-niwa ikanai* vs. *...koto-ga dekinai* vs. plain potential-negative
Three distinct ways to express inability, graded by cause: *...koto-ga dekinai* and the plain
potential-negative (*-enai*) are usable for any kind of inability (physical, social, or emotional);
*...wake-niwa ikanai* is reserved specifically for inability rooted in social obligation/face, and
sounds deliberately absurd/funny if applied to a genuine physical impossibility (p.10-11).

### Self-directed sentence-final particles: *-na* (and feminine *-wa*) vs. *-ne* vs. *-yo*
*-ne* seeks the listener's agreement; *-yo* asserts the speaker's own judgment regardless of the
listener (and can feel pressuring/aggressive if overused, especially where self-directed *-na* would
be more appropriate); self-directed *-na* (feminine variant *-wa*) marks a remark as addressed to the
speaker himself, not the listener — letting the listener choose freely whether to respond or help,
which is why complaints are often phrased this way rather than directly (p.12-13). This same
self-directed/monologue-marking logic extends to a broader class of expressions including *Naruhodo*,
*Aa, soo-ka*, and *Aa, soo yuu wake-ka* — words that are familiar-register in origin but become usable
even in polite conversation *only* when clearly marked (lower tone, falling intonation) as directed
at the speaker rather than the listener (p.20-21).

### *-rashii* and *...no kuse-ni*: category-expectation judgments
*-rashii* attaches to a role/category noun to judge whether someone lives up to expectations for that
category (usually approving); *...no kuse-ni* is the disapproving mirror-image, criticizing someone
for failing to behave as their category is expected to (p.24-25).

### *Datte...* vs. *Shikashi...*/*dakedo*
*Datte* is a restricted, familiar/feminine-or-childish-register connective specifically for a
self-justifying protest against perceived criticism, closed by an intensifying sentence-final tag
(*...n-desu*, *...da-mono*, *...desu-mono*, *...ja arimasen-ka*); *Shikashi* (familiar *dakedo*) is
the general-register "but," usable either to contradict a preceding statement or — after even a
minimal pause — to pivot to a new topic, a usage Japanese speakers treat as the same function rather
than two separate ones (p.26-29).

### Multi-verb (helping-verb) constructions
Stacking two or three verbs (main verb + helping verb(s), e.g. *-te kuru*, *-te miru*, *-te shimau*,
*-te morau/itadaku/ageru/kureru/kudasaru*) is standard, expressive everyday Japanese, not an advanced
exception: the main verb names the action, while the helping verb(s) layer in the speaker's/doer's
intention, interest in the outcome, regret, or a favor-giving/receiving relationship (p.30-31).

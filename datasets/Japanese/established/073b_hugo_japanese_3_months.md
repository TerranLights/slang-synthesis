## Chapter 11 — Indirect speech: indirect statements; indirect questions and commands; miscellaneous expressions (p. 118–127)

### Grammar points

#### 70. Indirect statements
When quoting what someone says/thinks/replies, the quotative particle **to** is used, with the verb
preceding it in the **plain form** (*Mō rokuji desu* to okāsan ga iimashita, "Their mother said it was
six o'clock"). The te-imasu form of **iu/iimasu** ("say") and **omou/omoimasu** ("think") is used with
3rd-person subjects (*Kare wa shiranai to itte imasu*, "He says he doesn't know"). Japanese can use a
different tense than English expects: "I said I wouldn't go" is literally rendered as "I will not go,
I said" (**Watashi wa ikanai to iimashita**); "He said it was interesting" as "It is interesting, he
said" (**Omoshiroi to kare wa iimashita**); "I thought it had finished" as "It has finished, I thought"
(**Mō owatta to omoimashita**).

#### 71. Indirect questions
Sentences like "I asked what...", "Tell me when...", "Do you know why...", "Have you decided
where..." keep the same tense principle as indirect statements, with the question particle **ka**
attached to the embedded clause (*Nanji ni tsuku ka o-kiki shimashō ka*, "Shall I ask what time it
gets here?"; *Doko ni sunde iru ka oshiete kudasai*, "Tell me where he lives"). "Whether... or not..."
requires **... ka dō ka ...** (*Sashimi o taberu ka dō ka shirimasen deshita*, "I didn't know whether
or not you eat sashimi"). Da/desu after nouns or na-adjectives in the present tense is usually dropped
before this embedded **ka** (*Itsu ka kikimasu*, "I'll ask when it is"; *Doko ka shitte imasu ka*, "Do
you know where it is?"). "I think probably... (I'm pretty sure)" is **n'ja nai ka to omoimasu** or
**nan ja nai ka to omoimasu** with na-adjectives and nouns (*Ma ni au n'ja nai ka to omoimasu*, "I'm
pretty sure we'll be on time").

#### 72. Indirect commands
"I told him not to go"-type indirect commands have several renderings. (1) **Kudasai** or its plain
form **kure** (less polite; used when the speaker reports what he himself, or someone of lower status,
was told to do): *Hayaku kite kudasai to tanomimashita*, "We asked them to come early" (lit. "Please
come early, we asked"); *Ikanaide kure to iimashita*, "I told him not to go." (2) **Yō ni** (lit. "in
such a way that") + **iu/iimasu** or **tanomu/tanomimasu**: *Ikanai yō ni iimashita*, "I told him not
to go"; *Hayaku kuru yō ni tanomimashita*, "We asked them to come early." (3) If the person told/asked
is of superior status, **te kureru** (or the more polite **te kudasaru**) **yō ni** is used: *Sensei ni
rokuji goro kite kureru yō ni tanomimashita*, "I asked the Professor to come at six o'clock."

#### 73. "Call A, B"
"Call A, B" is **A o (or wa) B to iimasu** (*Kono kiiroi no wa nan to iimasu ka* / *Takuwan to
iimasu*, "What do you call this yellow thing?" / "We call it 'takuwan'"). "How do you say X in
Japanese" uses the same pattern: *'I love you' wa Nihongo de nan to iimasu ka.*

#### 74. "An A called B," "the fact that A means B"
"An A called B" is **B to iu A** (*Nakajima Miyuki to iu kashu*, "a singer called Nakajima Miyuki";
*'Ran' to iu eiga*, "the film called 'Ran'"). Nouns/adjectives may be followed by **to iu no wa** or
**to iu koto wa** (the latter only for abstract things) meaning "what I mean by..."/"what is meant
by...," though often with no extra meaning at all (*Nihongo to iu no wa omoshiroi desu yo*, "Japanese
is interesting, you know"). **To iu koto wa** after a verb/adjective often conveys "(the fact) that"
(*Kaisha o yameta to iu koto o shitte imashita ka*, "Did you know that he's left the company?"). "The
fact that... means that..." is **... to iu koto (or no) wa ... to iu koto (or imi) desu**. English
"that" in "the news that," "the view that," "a request that," etc. is also translated with **to iu**
(*Sugoi jishin ga atta to iu nyūsu*, "the news that there had been a terrible earthquake"). "What does
A mean?" is **A (to iu kotoba) wa dō iu imi desu ka**.

#### 75. Another word on *to omoimasu* — the volitional (mashō) plain form
"I'm thinking of...," "I think I shall...," "I thought I would..." require the plain form of the
**mashō** ending combined with **to omoimasu**. Group 1 verbs form it by changing the final -ru to -yō
(**taberu → tabeyō**, **miru → miyō**); Group 2 verbs change the final syllable (-u, -ku, -gu, -su,
-tsu, -nu, etc.) to -ō/-kyō/-gō/-sō/-tō/-nō etc. (**kaku → kakō**, **matsu → matō**, **arau → araō**).
Irregular: **suru → shiyō**, **kuru → koyō**, **da → darō**. Adding **ka** to this form conveys
"perhaps"/"might" (*Rajio o kaō to omoimasu*, "I think I'll buy a radio"; *Kankoku keiyu de ikō ka to
omotte imasu*, "I'm thinking I might go via Korea").

## Chapter 12 — Like/dislike; want to do; want someone to do; more on wa and ga; more on naru and narimasu; more on yō ni (p. 128–138)

### Grammar points

#### 76. "Like/dislike"
The na-adjectives **suki** ("liked") and **kirai** ("disliked") are normally used in place of a verb.
"A likes B"/"A dislikes B" is **A wa B ga suki desu**/**A wa B ga kirai desu** (*Kanai wa jazu ga suki
desu*, lit. "As for my wife, jazz is liked"). The subject is dropped when context makes it clear. "I
don't like X, but I like Y" and negative/contrastive/wa-reply contexts shift the liked/disliked item's
particle from **ga** to **wa** (*Nihon no zasshi wa suki desu ga manga wa suki ja arimasen*, "I like
Japanese magazines, but I don't like comics"). "Really like"/"really loathe" are **daisuki**/
**daikirai**; when the liked/disliked thing is a person, **no koto ga** is often inserted (*Kanojo no
koto ga daisuki desu ne*, "You really like her, don't you?"). Suki/kirai can also precede nouns
directly as ordinary na-adjectives (*suki na nomimono*, "a drink I like"; *kirai na hito*, "a person I
dislike"); adding the pronoun + **no** clarifies whose preference is meant (*Watashi no kirai na
tabemono wa nai desu*, "There is no food I don't like").

#### 77. Some other wa/ga expressions
"A is good at B"/"A is bad at B" is **A wa B ga jōzu desu**/**A wa B ga heta desu** (*Kare wa gitā ga
totemo jōzu deshita*, "He was very good at the guitar"). **Jōzu**/**heta** are na-adjectives usable
before nouns too. Other common wa/ga verbs: **wakaru/wakarimasu** ("understand"), **dekiru/dekimasu**
("can do"), **aru/arimasu** ("there is/to have"). For all these constructions, either "as for A, its B
is..." (**A no B wa...**) or "A's B is..." (**A wa B ga...**) phrasing is possible and both are
illustrated (*Tōkyō wa bukka ga takai desu* / *Tōkyō no bukka wa takai desu*, "the cost of living in
Tokyo is high"). Wa generally replaces ga when the item before ga is being contrasted with something
else, when the sentence is negative, or when answering a wa-question (*Jūdō wa jōzu desu ga kendō wa
heta desu*, "He's good at judo but bad at kendo").

#### 78. "Want something"/"need something"
The adjective **hoshii** ("desired") expresses "want something" (*Watashi wa tabako ga hoshii desu*,
"I want a cigarette"). With a 3rd-person subject, hoshii is usually followed by **to itte iru/imasu**
or a word for "seems" (Ch.13) rather than used bare. Alternatively the verb **hoshigaru/hoshigarimasu**
may be used for a 3rd person (*O-kane o hoshigatte imasen*, "He doesn't want any money" — note the
object particle shifts to **o** with the verb form). Hoshii can precede a noun directly (*hoshii
mono*, "something you want"). **Iru/irimasu** ("be necessary/needed") often means "want" in a more
practical sense (*Mō chotto jikan ga irimasu*, "I need some more time").

#### 79. "Want to" (i)
"Want to" is formed by adding **-tai** to a verb's stem; tai behaves like an i-adjective, so its
negative is **-taku nai** (and past **-takatta**/negative past **-taku nakatta**). Desu or n'desu make
these forms polite. The direct object of a tai-form verb is often marked with **ga** rather than **o**
(*Ano shatsu ga kaitakatta desu*, "I wanted to buy that shirt"). **To omoimasu** can substitute for
desu as a politer, "would like to"-flavored ending.

#### 80–81. "Want to" (ii); "want someone to..."
A 3rd-person subject generally requires **to itte iru/imasu** or **deshō** (or a "seems" word, Ch.13)
after the tai form. A common alternative to tai is **-garu** (**ikitai → ikitagaru**), generally found
as **-gatte iru/imasu** (*Kanojo wa yaritagatte ita n'desu ga dekinakatta desu*, "She wanted to do it
but couldn't"). "Want someone to do something" is the **te form + hoshii**, with the agent marked by
**ni**; the speaker uses this only toward inferiors/intimates, and it's frequently used interrogatively
in polite speech to mean "do you want me/us to...?" (*Denwa shite hoshii n'desu ka*, "Do you want me to
phone?").

#### 82. More on naru/narimasu
**Naru/narimasu** ("become") combines with the tai form (*Kanojo ni aitaku natta n'desu ka*, "Do you
[now] want to meet her?") and very often with **nai** itself and the plain negative of verbs (*Yuki wa
minna naku narimashita ka*, "Has the snow all gone?"). **Naku narimashita**, used of people, means
"died" (a euphemistic idiom, explicitly glossed by the source) — *Sensei wa ototoi naku narimashita*,
"My teacher died the day before yesterday." "Come to..." is often **yō ni naru/narimasu** (*Itsuka
wakaru yō ni deshō*, "You will come to understand one day").

#### 83. More on yō ni
Yō ni ("so as to," "in such a way that," introduced Ch.11) combines with **suru/shimasu** to mean "be
sure to" (*Shichiji made ni kaette kuru yō ni shite kudasai*, "Be sure to be back by seven"). It's used
productively with many other verbs: *Kaze ga haitte kuru yō ni mado o akete kudasai* ("Open the window
to let some air in"), *Buji ni tōchaku suru yō ni inorimashō* ("Let's pray that they arrive safely"),
*Hikōki ni ma ni au yō ni hayaku okimashita* ("I got up early so as to be in time for the plane"). The
source notes the example *Tabako o suisuginai yō ni chūi shimashō* ("Let's take care not to smoke too
much") is the actual health warning printed on Japanese cigarette packets — a real-world attested
register data point.

## Chapter 13 — Appearance; feel; taste; sound (p. 139–146)

### Grammar points

#### 84. "Appears to be," "looks like" (i)
Two constructions use **sō desu**: (1) after the plain form of adjectives/verbs, sō desu means "I
hear that.../it is said that..." (hearsay) (*Nihongo o yameta sō desu*, "I hear that she's given up
Japanese"). (2) Sō desu added to the **stem** of either adjective type or to a verb stem (Group
1/2/irregular alike) means "it looks/appears (from what I see) that..." — **shizuka na → shizuka →
shizukasō**; **oishii → oishi → oishisō**; **dekimasu → deki → dekisō**. Irregular: **ii → yosasō**
("good" → "looks good"), **nai → nasasō** ("there isn't" → "looks like there isn't"). This
visually/sensorially-based sō is distinguished explicitly from the hearsay sō in the next grammar
point (85) by being usable only before something not yet realized, and — in a further subtlety — only
sō(2) functions as a na-adjective when placed before a noun (*Sore wa oishisō na gohan desu ne*,
"That's tasty-looking food"), unlike the hearsay sō.

#### 85. "Appears to be," "looks like" (ii) — yō desu
**Yō desu** is used for a broader range of situations existing at the time of speaking, and is not
confined to visual evidence the way sō desu(2) is (*Netsu ga aru yō desu*, "He seems to have a
temperature"; *O-mise wa minna shimatte iru yō desu*, "All the shops are shut, it seems"). With nouns,
the particle **no** is needed (*Ano hito wa sararīman no yō desu ne*, "That person looks like a
businessman"). **Yō** also functions as a na-adjective with a variety of meanings ("like," "such as"):
*Kamikōchi no yō na utsukushii tokoro wa sukunai desu*, "There are few beautiful places like
Kamikōchi"; *Nihon ni wa watashi no yō na se ga hikui hito wa takusan imasu yo*, "There are many short
people like myself in Japan." Yō + **ni** forms several further idioms: plain adverbial "like/as"
(*Nihonjin no yō ni Nihongo o hanashimasu*, "She speaks Japanese like a Japanese [person]"; *Sensei ga
itta yō ni hayaku shite kudasai*, "Do it quickly, as the teacher said"). "Just like" is **to onaji yō
ni** (*Nihonjin wa Igirisujin to onaji yō ni itsumo tenki ni tsuite hanasu n'desu*, "The Japanese, just
like the English, always talk about the weather"). "Unlike" is **to chigatte** (*Chūgokugo to chigatte
Nihongo wa kantan desu yo*, "Unlike Chinese, Japanese is easy"). A fixed useful set: **dono yō na (ni)**
"what sort of (in what way)," **kono yō na (ni)** "this sort of," **sono yō na (ni)**/**ano yō na (ni)**
"that sort of" (the source lists both sono and ano under "that sort of," distinguished by relative
distance from speaker/listener per the demonstrative system of Ch.3).

#### 86. "Appears to be," "looks like" (iii) — mitai and rashii
**Mitai** and **rashii** both mean "appears/looks like" at the end of a sentence, covering all senses
of yō desu except sō desu(2)'s sense; in conversation **mitai is the most common** of all the
"appearance" words (*O-yu ga waita mitai desu yo*, "The water seems to have boiled"; *Dame mitai
desu*, "It appears to be useless"). Rashii is interchangeable with mitai in all these end-of-sentence
uses. The two differ grammatically and semantically when placed **before a noun or used as an
adverb**: mitai is a na-adjective (*Kyō mitai na hi wa daikirai desu*, "I loathe days like today";
*Watashi ga yatta mitai ni yatte kudasai*, "Do it like I did"), while **B rashii A** means "an A
typical of B" (*Otoko rashii otoko desu*, "He's a manly person"; *Haru rashii hi desu*, "It's a typical
spring day") — a genuinely distinct meaning from mitai's bare resemblance sense, worth flagging since
surface substitutability breaks down here.

#### 87. Feel, sense, taste, smell, sound, looks, shape
A set of fixed idiomatic sensory-verb expressions: **ki ga suru/shimasu** "feel (like)...", **aji ga
suru/shimasu** "taste (like)...", **nioi ga suru/shimasu** "smell (like)...", **oto ga suru/shimasu**
"sound (like)...", **kanji ga suru/shimasu** "feel (as if), get the impression (that)...", **kao o
shite iru/imasu** "look...", **katachi o shite iru/imasu** "have a... shape." Many of these are preceded
by **(no) yō na** or **to iu** (*Kyō wa okiru ki ga shinai n'desu*, "I don't feel like getting up
today"; *Gasu no yō na nioi ga shimasu yo*, "It smells like gas"; *Ame ga futte iru yō na oto ga shimasu
ne*, "It sounds as if it's raining, doesn't it?"). Two very common related intransitive verbs:
**mieru/miemasu** (lit. "be visible") and **kikoeru/kikoemasu** (lit. "be audible") (*Umi ga miemasu
ka*, "Can you see the sea?"; *Chotto kikoemasen*, "I can't hear [it] very well").

## Chapter 14 — Relative clauses, simple and complex; anybody/somebody/nobody (p. 147–156)

### Grammar points

#### 88. Relative clauses (i)
Japanese has no separate relative pronouns ("who," "which"); a relative clause is formed simply by
placing a verb (in its plain form) before the noun it modifies, exactly as adjectives already precede
nouns (cross-referenced to earlier sections 37, 40). *Naite iru kodomo wa musuko desu*, "The child who
is crying is my son" (lit. "the 'is crying' child"); *Bā de utatte iru hito wa shachō desu*, "The man
singing in the bar is the President"; *Shōtotsu shita kuruma wa Toyota desu*, "The car which crashed
is a Toyota."

#### 89. Relative clauses (ii)
More complex relative clauses: the subject marker **ga** may be replaced by **no** inside a relative
clause (*me ga (no) aoi hito*, "the man whose eyes are blue"; *yama ga (no) ōi kuni*, "a country with
lots of mountains"). For "whom," "to whom," "whose," "on which," etc., Japanese again just uses a
plain relative clause with no distinct relativizer — *Watashi no mita hito wa yūmei na kashu desu*,
"The man whom I saw is a famous singer"; *Watashitachi ga machiawase shita bā wa gaijin no tamariba
desu*, "The bar in which we had arranged to meet is a haunt for foreigners"; *Otōsan ga jiko ni atta
hito wa watashi no ie no chikaku ni sunde imasu*, "The man whose father had that accident lives near
me." The relative clause's own subject may be marked by either **ga** or **no**. Fixed
relative-clause-derived idioms: **suru koto** "things to do," **miru tokoro** "places to see," **kaku
mono** "something to write with/on," **taberu mono** "something to eat" — the source gives an explicit
rule-of-thumb: an abstract thing is **koto**, a concrete thing is **mono**.

#### 90. "Someone," "something"
Indefinite pronouns are formed by adding **ka** to question words ("who," "what," etc.), with distinct
positive-statement vs. negative-or-question forms: **dareka** (someone/anyone), **donataka** (anyone,
more polite), **nanika** (something/anything), **itsuka** (sometime), **dokoka** (somewhere/anywhere),
**dochiraka** (one or the other), **doreka** (one/some of them, 3+), **ikutsuka** (some, a fair number
of), **ikuraka** (some, a fair amount of). **Ga** and **o** are often omitted with these words; **wa**
is never used; other particles (ni, e, kara, to, etc.) are retained when the verb requires them
(*Dareka ni iimashita ka*, "Did you tell someone?"). These words are also frequently used "sitting"
before an adjective + noun or relative clause with no extra meaning added (*Nanika oishii mono o tabe
ni ikimashō ka*, "Shall we go and have something good to eat?"). A related, distinct construction uses
**aru** ("certain") + **wa** for "some X... other X..." pairs (*Aru hito wa manjū ga suki desu ga aru
hito wa kirai desu*, "Some people like manju, others don't"). **Ka** added to the question word also
yields "a fair number of/quite a few" (*Nanninka kite imashita*, "A fair number of people were there";
*Nannenka*, "a fair number of years").

#### 91. "Nobody," "nothing"
"Nobody/nothing" (negative statements) and "anybody/anything" (negative questions) use the question
word + **mo**: **daremo/donatamo** (no one/nobody; anyone/anybody), **nanimo** (nothing; anything),
**dokomo** (nowhere; anywhere), **dochiramo** (neither; either of two), **doremo** (none; any of
three+), **ikuramo** (not much at all), **ikutsumo** (not many at all). Ga/o need not be used with
these; wa is never used; other particles are inserted between the question word and mo (**dare ni
mo**, **doko e mo**). "Never" (present/future) is **kesshite** ("definitely [not]") or **zettai ni**
("absolutely [not]") — "have never" (past) is cross-referenced to Ch.15.

#### 92. "Anybody," "anything" (positive)
"Anybody/anything can..." (positive, unrestricted) is the question word + **demo**: **daredemo**
(anybody), **nandemo** (anything), **dochirademo** (either, whichever), **doredemo** (any of three+),
**itsudemo** (anytime), **ikurademo** (any amount) — (*Daredemo Nihongo ga dekimasu*, "Anybody
can speak Japanese"; *Itsudemo asobi ni kite kudasai*, "Come round any time"; *Biru wa ikuradem*o
arimasu yo, "There's any amount of beer"). Wa/ga/o are not used with these; other particles are.
**Mo** added to the question word (distinct from the "nobody" usage above — context/polarity
disambiguates) can also mean "a great/any number of" in positive sentences (*Nanninmo kite imashita*,
"A great number of people were there"; *Bīru wa nanbonmo nomimashita*, "We drank bottles and bottles
of beer"; *Nankaimo kikimashita*, "I asked time and time again").

## Chapter 15 — Must/must not, may/may not, can/cannot; some uses of koto and no with verbs (p. 157–167)

### Grammar points

#### 93. "Must," "have to," "must not," "may not"
Two ways to express "must/have to": (1) negative plain form + **to** ("if") + **dame desu** ("it's no
good") (*Sore o yomanai to dame desu*, "You must read it"). (2) The **nakereba narimasen** form: make
the negative plain form (shinai), substitute **nakereba** for nai (shinakereba), add **narimasen**
(shinakereba narimasen) — nakereba = "if (you do) not," narimasen = "it's no good." Past "had to" uses
the past of narimasen (*Kare ni iwanakereba narimasen deshita*, "We had to tell him"). "Must not/may
not" is the **te wa ikemasen** form: te form + **wa** + **ikemasen** (te wa = "if (you)...", ikemasen =
"it will not do") — *Koko de sutte wa ikemasen*, "You mustn't smoke in here." A very common abbreviated
colloquial form substitutes **tcha** for **te wa** (*Waratcha ikemasen ne*, "We mustn't laugh, must
we?" — a genuine contraction worth flagging for slang-mechanics work).

#### 94. "May," "don't have to," "don't need to"
"May"/"can" (in the sense "it's all right to") is the **te form + mo** ("even if") **+ ii desu**
("it's good, it's all right") (*Tomatte mo ii desu yo*, "You may stay [the night]"). **Kamaimasen**
("[I] don't mind") may substitute for ii desu. "Don't have to/don't need to" is the **negative te form
+ mo + ii desu** (or kamaimasen) (*Ima sugu harawanakute mo ii desu ne*, "We don't have to pay now, do
we?"). This mo is often dropped in practice (*Kore de (mo) ii desu ka*, "Is this O.K.?"). The
construction works with adjectives too (*Takakute mo ii desu yo*, "I don't mind if it's expensive";
*Yasuku nakute mo kamaimasen*, "It's O.K. if it's not cheap").

#### 95. "Wherever," "whoever," etc.
Question words + the **te mo** form translate "wherever/whoever/whatever/whenever": **doko e itte
mo** (wherever you go), **doko de tabete mo** (wherever you eat), **kanojo ni nani o itte mo**
(whatever I say to her), **dare ni kiite mo** (whoever we ask), **itsu shuppatsu shite mo** (whenever
you leave), **doko no gakkō e itte mo** (whatever school they go to). All these may be followed by
**ii desu** or **kamaimasen** for "I don't mind/it doesn't matter" (*Doko e itte mo kamaimasen*, "I
don't mind where you go").

#### 96. "Can," "be able"
Two ways to express "can/be able." (1) The **potential form**: Group 1 verbs change -ru to -rareru,
with an abbreviated form in common use changing -ru to -reru instead (**taberu → taberareru**, abbrev.
**tabereru**); Group 2 verbs change the final -u to -eru (**oyogu → oyogeru**, **arau → araeru**);
irregular **suru → dekiru**, **kuru → korareru** (abbreviated **koreru**). All potential forms
themselves function as Group 1 verbs. The object of the potential may be followed by o, ga, or wa
(*Eigo ga hanasemasu ka*, "Can you speak English?"; *Kore wa zenbu taberaremasen yo*, "I can't eat all
this" — abbreviated taberemasen also given as acceptable). (2) The plain form of the verb + **koto ga
(or wa or mo) dekiru/dekimasu** (*Kare wa Nihongo o kaku koto ga dekimasu*, "He can write Japanese").
"Too (X) to (Y)" combines the te form of an adjective with a "can" form (*Atsukute taberenakatta
desu*, "It was too hot for me to eat" — note the abbreviated potential-negative pattern here too).

#### 97. Some uses of koto (i)
"Do/does... ever...?" is the plain form + **koto wa (or ga) arimasu ka**, often with an adverb like
**tokidoki** ("sometimes") (*Honya-san de tachiyomi suru koto wa arimasu ka*, "Do you ever browse in
bookshops?"). "Has/have... ever...?" requires the **ta form + koto wa (or ga) aru/arimasu** (*Nagasaki
e itta koto wa arimasu ka*, "Have you ever been to Nagasaki?"; negative answer simply **nai desu**).
**Mo** may also be used for "have also" (*Taiwan e itta koto mo arimasu yo*, "I have also been to
Taiwan"), including in the negative for "haven't even" (*Kyūshū e itta koto mo arimasen*, "I haven't
even been to Kyushu").

#### 98. Some uses of koto (ii)
"Decide to..." is **... koto ni suru/shimasu**; "be decided (come about) that..." is **... koto ni
naru/narimasu** (*Kyūkyūsha o yobu koto ni shimashita*, "We decided to call an ambulance"; *Raishū
Amerika e kaeru koto ni natte imasu*, "It's been decided that I'm going back to America next week").

#### 99. Some uses of koto and no
Adding **koto** or **no** to a verb's plain form turns the verb into a noun (hon o yomu "read books" →
hon o yomu koto/no "the reading of books"); in many cases the two are interchangeable, but **no** is
probably more frequent in conversation (*Hon o yomu no ga suki desu*, "I like reading books"). In
certain fixed expressions **no** is specifically more common — with verbs of perception/waiting/
forgetting/helping (*Kanojo to hanashite iru no o mimashita*, "I saw him talking to her"; *Kaette kuru
no o matte imasu*, "I'm waiting for him to come back"; *Denwa suru no o wasurenaide kudasai*, "Don't
forget to phone!"; *Tsukuru no o tetsudatte kuremasu ka*, "Would you help me make it?").

#### 100–101. Some uses of no (i)–(ii)
**No** is frequently used in emphatic questions such as "When is it that you're off?" (lit. "As for
your going, when is it?") — *Iku no wa itsu desu ka*; *Nihongo o benkyō shite iru no wa naze desu ka*,
"Why is it that you're studying Japanese?" **No ni** after a plain form means either "for the purpose
of (in order to)" or "although," distinguished by context; in the first sense, **no ni wa** or **ni wa**
may substitute (*Sukiyaki o tsukuru no ni nani o tsukaimasu ka*, "What do you use to make sukiyaki?";
*Tomodachi ni au tame ni Nara e kimashita*, "I came to Nara [in order] to see a friend" — note **tame
ni** given as an alternative purpose-construction alongside no ni). Already-known **ga/shikashi/
keredomo** ("but/although") differ from no ni in three ways: (1) no ni never begins a sentence; (2)
even in polite speech it's preceded by a plain form; (3) it's generally more emphatic. No ni also
cannot be preceded by **da** — **na** must be used instead (*Byōki na no ni iku to itte imashita*,
"He said he'd go although he's ill").

## Chapter 16 — Comparing things: A is...er than B, A is the biggest; A is (not) as...as B (p. 168–176)

### Grammar points

#### 102. Comparing things (i)
Japanese adjectives have no comparative form of their own; **dochira** ("which of two") itself implies
a comparative (*Dochira ga ii desu ka*, "Which is better?"). "Which is...er, A or B?" is **A to B to
dochira ga... desu ka** (*Sumō to yakyū to dochira ga suki desu ka*, "Which do you prefer, sumo or
baseball?").

#### 103. Comparing things (ii)
Two ways to say "A is...er than B": (1) **A wa B yori... desu** — a general statement about A,
answering "tell me something about A" (*Kyō no tenki wa kinō yori ii desu ne*, "Today's weather is
better than yesterday's"). (2) **B yori A no hō ga... desu** — more emphatic, emphasizing A, answering
"which is...er?" (*Chūgokugo yori Nihongo no hō ga kantan desu*, "Japanese is easier than Chinese").
**Yori** may be omitted (*Natsu no hō ga ii desu ne*, "Summer is better"). Both constructions work with
adverbs (*Kare wa watashi yori hayaku hanashimasu*, "He speaks faster than I do") and with verbs (*Gorufu
o suru no wa uchi ni iru yori tanoshii desu ne*, "Playing golf is more fun than staying at home"). When
English "would" is used, the verb before **hō** takes the **ta form, but only in the positive** (*Basu
de iku yori aruite itta hō ga kantan desu*, "It would be easier to walk than to take the bus"; but
negative: *Sō shinai hō ga gōriteki desu yo*, "It would be more sensible not to do that" — plain
non-past negative, not ta-form negative). "Should (not)" and "should (not) have" are **-ta (-nai) hō ga
ii desu** and **-ta (-nai) hō ga yokatta desu** respectively (*Sugu ayamatta hō ga ii desu*, "You should
apologize immediately"; *Kuchi o dasanai hō ga ii desu*, "You shouldn't interrupt"; *Gaijin tōrokushō o
motte kita hō ga yokatta desu*, "You should have brought your alien registration card").

#### 104. Comparing things (iii)
"Much...er," "far...er" is **motto** or **zutto** (*Zutto takai n'desu*, "It's much more expensive").
"A little...er" is **(mō) chotto...** or **(mō) sukoshi...** (*Kinō yori sukoshi samui desu*, "It's a
little colder than yesterday").

#### 105. Superlatives
No special superlative adjective form exists; superlatives are made by adding **mottomo** or **ichiban**
("number 1") (*Kanji o oboeru no wa mottomo tanoshii koto no hitotsu desu*, "Learning kanji is one of
the most enjoyable things"). "Which is the...est, A, B, or C?" requires **naka** or **uchi** ("among")
(*Nagasaki to Yokohama to Hakodate no naka de doko ga ichiban kirei desu ka*, "Which is the prettiest,
Nagasaki, Yokohama or Hakodate?"). **Sono** can mean "of it/him/her/them" in this context. The particle
**de** means "in" with superlatives (*Kanojo wa kurasu de mottomo atama ga ii seito desu*, "She's the
cleverest pupil in the class").

#### 106. "How much...?" "How...is it?" "It's this..."
"How (much)...?" is **dore hodo**, **dore gurai**, or **dore dake** (*Dore gurai irimasu ka*, "How much
do you need?"). "This big," "that much," etc. use **kore** (or **sore**, **are**) + **hodo**, **gurai**,
or **dake** + an adjective/adverb; **hodo** is used more in negative sentences (*Kore gurai ōkikatta
n'desu ka*, "Was it about this big?"; *Sore hodo yoku nakatta n'desu yo*, "It wasn't that good, you
know"). **Donna ni** asks "how?" (to what extent), answered by **konna ni** ("this, to this extent"),
**sonna ni** or **anna ni** ("that, to that extent") — recurring the sonna ni introduced in Ch.12.
"As...as possible" is **dekiru dake** + adverb/adjective (*Dekiru dake hayaku kite kudasai*, "Please
come as quickly as possible").

#### 107. "As...as..."
**Gurai** and **hodo** both appear in "as...as..." sentences; **A wa B hodo...** is used most in
negative sentences (*Watashi wa kare hodo atama ga yoku nai desu*, "I'm not as clever as him"). In
positive sentences, **A wa B to onaji gurai...** is perhaps the most common (*Nihon wa Igirisu to onaji
gurai ōkii desu ka*, "Is Japan [about] as big as Britain?").

## Chapter 17 — Some time expressions: before/after/while/when, etc. (p. 177–186)

### Grammar points

#### 108. "While" — -nagara
When both verbs in a "while" sentence share the same subject, **-nagara** attaches to the verb stem
(**tabemasu → tabe → tabenagara**; **shimasu → shi → shinagara**) (*Itsumo terebi o minagara benkyō
shimasu*, "He always studies while he watches the TV").

#### 109. "While" — aida
When the two "while"-clause subjects differ, **aida (ni)** is used, with the preceding verb's tense
generally matching English (*Kodomo ga nete ita aida toranpu o yarimashita*, "We played cards while
the children were asleep"). Miscellaneous aida expressions: **sensō no aida** ("during the war"),
**nagai aida** ("for a long time"), **X kara Y no aida ni** ("between X and Y," of time), **A to B no
aida ni arimasu** ("is located between A and B," of place).

#### 110. "After" — ato
Besides te form + **kara**, **ato (de)** is often used, always with the plain past preceding it
(*Gohan ga owatta ato (de) asobi ni kite kudasai ne*, "After you've finished your meal, do come round,
won't you?"). Miscellaneous ato phrases: **ato de shimasu** ("I'll do it afterwards"), **sono ato wa dō
narimashita ka** ("What happened after that?"), **ato nifun shika arimasen** ("There are only two
minutes left" — the **shika + negative** "only" construction recurring from Ch.14).

#### 111. "Before" — mae
Irrespective of English tense, only the **present tense** of the Japanese verb is used with **mae
(ni)** (*Nihon o tatsu mae ni zehi Fuji-san ni noboritai desu ne*, "Before I leave Japan, I definitely
want to climb Mt. Fuji" — note tatsu, present tense, despite the English "leave" referring to a future
event). Miscellaneous mae expressions: **(sono) mae ni** ("before that"), **sensō no mae** ("before
the war"), **kono mae** ("the other day," lit. "this before"), **watashi no mae no kanojo** ("my last
girlfriend," lit. "my before girlfriend") — mae productively meaning both temporal "before" and
relational "former/previous."

#### 112. "When" — toki
Any tense of the verb or adjective may be used with **toki (ni)** (*Watashi ga kūkō ni tsuita toki kare
wa mō kite imashita*, "He was already there when I got to the airport"). Miscellaneous toki
expressions: **sono toki** ("at that time/then"), **gakusei no toki** ("when I was a student"), **...to
omou toki mo arimasu yo** ("there are times when I think...").

#### 113. "When," "once," "if" — to
**To** differs from toki: it translates "whenever...," "when/once/if..., (then inevitably)...". To is
used **only with the present tense**, and is often accompanied by an adverb like **itsumo** ("always")
or **kanarazu** ("inevitably") (*Kanojo ni denwa suru to itsumo rusu desu*, "Whenever I phone her she's
out"; *Nihongo o narai-hajimeru to yamerarenaku narimasu*, "Once you start to learn Japanese you can't
stop"). "As soon as" is **... to sugu ...** (or **te kara sugu**) (*Kūkō ni tsuku to sugu chekku-in
shimashō*, "Let's check in as soon as we get to the airport").

#### 114. "Just about to"; "has/have just"
The noun **tokoro** expresses "just about to...," "has just...," optionally with **ima** ("just now")
and/or **chōdo** ("just") (*Mō kaeru tokoro desu*, "We're about to go home now"; *Ima chōdo kaette kita
tokoro desu*, "I've just got back"). "Just as I was about to go out, the phone rang"-type sentences use
the plain form of **mashō** (Ch.11's volitional) + **to suru** with tokoro, to, or toki (*Chōdo dekakeyō
to suru tokoro (e) denwa ga narimashita*, "I was just about to go out when the phone rang"; *Chōdo neyō
to suru to bakudan ga bakuhatsu shita n'desu*, "Just as I was about to go to bed the bomb went off" —
a startling example sentence, presumably chosen for memorability rather than any topical relevance).
Other tokoro idioms: **shōtotsu suru tokoro o mimashita** ("I saw the cars collide"), **kono tokoro
sugoku isogashii n'desu** ("Recently I've been really busy").

#### 115. Some other common time expressions
**Made** = "until" (*Kore o anki suru made nemasen*, "I shan't go to bed until I've learnt it off by
heart"). **Saichū** = "in the middle of" (*Tegami o kaite iru saichū desu*, "I'm in the middle of
writing a letter"). **Tabi** = "whenever," synonymous with **... to itsumo ...** (*Densha ni noru tabi
ni nanika wasuremono o shimasu yo*, "Whenever I go by train I leave something behind"). **Uchi** +
negative = "before" when the sense is "something will happen soon, but before it does..." (distinct
from mae) (*Okāsan ga kaette konai uchi ni sōji shimashō ne*, "Let's tidy up before Mum gets home";
*Samenai uchi ni tabete kudasai*, "Eat it before it gets cold"). **Kagiri** = "as far as...," "as long
as..." (*Watashi ga oboete iru kagiri kare wa mada kekkon shite imasen yo*, "As far as I can remember,
he's not married"; *Dare ni mo iwanai kagiri kamaimasen*, "As long as you tell no one, I don't mind").

### Dialogue/notes highlights (Conversations 17A–17B, p. 184–186)

  honorific form covering both taberu/tabemasu AND nomu/nomimasu, used only of others one wishes to

## Chapter 18 — If clauses; some related expressions (p. 187–195)

### Grammar points

#### 116. "If" — the ba form
(1) **Ba form of verbs**: the final syllable (-u, -ku, -gu, -su, -tsu, -nu, -bu, -mu, -ru) changes to
its column-4 equivalent (per the source's own conjugation table, p.8) + **ba** (**arau → arae(ba)**,
**kaku → kake(ba)**, **oyogu → oyoge(ba)**, **taberu → tabere(ba)**). **Da/desu has no ba form** — **nara**
is used instead, with both nouns and na-adjectives (*watashi nara*, "if I were you/it were me";
*hima nara*, "if I'm free"). (2) **Ba form of i-adjectives, the tai form, and negatives**: the final -i
changes to **-kereba** (**atatakai → atatakakereba**; **ikitai → ikitakereba**; **shitaku nai →
shitaku nakereba**).

#### 117. "If" — the tara form
The **tara form of ALL verbs without exception** is made by adding **-ra** to the ta form (**iku →
ittara**, **iru → itara**, **suru → shitara**, **da → dattara**, **kuru → kitara**). An extra degree of
politeness is given by adding **-ra** to the **mashita** form instead (**arimashita →
arimashitara**). The tara form of i-adjectives, the tai form, and negatives is made by adding **-ra**
to their past tense (**atsui → atsukatta → atsukattara**).

#### 118. "If" — application
Careful attention to tense is needed: *Hikōki ga tsuiraku sureba (shitara) minna shinimasu ne*, "If the
plane crashes, we will all die, won't we?"; *Tsubasa ga torereba (toretara) hikōki ga tobenaku naru
deshō*, "If the wing were to come off, the plane couldn't fly" (potential form "torereru," from Ch.15's
potential paradigm, combined with the ba/tara if-forms). **Moshi** very frequently introduces "if"
clauses of all types (*Moshi dame dattara dō shimashō*, "If it's no good what shall we do?"). Another
common type uses **n'dattara** or, more politely, **n'deshitara**, attached to a present or past plain
form, meaning "if, in fact..." or "if it's the case that..." (*Moshi Rondon ni irassharu n'deshitara
oshiete kudasai*, "If you are in fact coming to London do let me know"). The final verb after these
if-clauses is often followed by **deshō**, but **ka mo shiremasen** or **darō to omoimasu** are other
common possibilities.

#### 119. Miscellaneous expressions with tara and ba
Suggestions are commonly made with the **tara form + dō** or, for extra politeness, **ikaga**
(*Issho ni kitara dō desu ka, Kunio*, "Why don't you come along too, Kunio?"). Asking what one should
do uses either the **tara** or **ba** form + **ii desu ka** (lit. "it'll be good if we do what/go
where?") (*Nani o ittara (ieba) ii desu ka*, "What should I say to him?"). "I wish.../I hope..." is
**ii n'desu (ga) (ne)** added to one of the if-forms (*Hayaku owattara ii n'desu ga ne*, "I wish it
would finish quickly"; *Ano hito ga konakattara yokatta n'desu ga ne*, "I wish he hadn't come" — note
the past-tense yokatta marking unrealized past wish). The tara form is also frequently used (1) to mean
"when," and (2) in negative "must" clauses (*Owattara oshiete kudasai*, "When you've finished please
tell me"; *Koko de tabako o suttara dame desu yo*, "You mustn't smoke here"). "The more... the more..."
is **sureba suru hodo...** (lit. "if one does something, to the extent one does it, it is...")
(*Nihongo de hanaseba hanasu hodo perapera ni narimasu yo*, "The more you speak in Japanese, the more
fluent you'll become"; *Tabenakereba tabenai hodo hosoku narimasu*, "The less you eat the thinner
you'll get" — the negative-ba form productively extending the same construction).

### Dialogue/notes highlights (Conversations 18A–18B, p. 193–195)
- **kara sankagetsu tatte iru**: "three months have passed since..." — **tatsu/tachimasu** meaning
  "pass" (of time), source's own Note 1.
- **wake ja nai desu**: "it's not the case that..." (source's own Note 2) — a hedging/denial idiom.
- **te oku/okimasu**: a productive construction used when an action is performed in preparation for
  some future event ("do something in advance/for later") — *benkyō shite okeba yokatta*, "I should
  have studied [in advance]"; common fixed phrases **kangaete okimashō** ("Let's think about it [for
  later]") and **(asoko ni) oite kudasai** ("Leave it there") (source's own Note 4) — a genuinely
  important aspectual auxiliary distinct from everything covered so far.
- **chizu nanka**: "a map or anything" (informal **nanka**, source's own Note 5) — an informal variant
  worth comparing with the nandemo/nanika indefinite-pronoun family from Ch.14.
- **Japanese address system** (source's own Note 7): addresses work in nested administrative sections
  rather than street names, from largest to smallest unit — city → ku ("ward") → a named section
  (e.g. Aoyama) → chōme (subsection) → ban (block/plot) → gō (specific plot number); the source notes
  that because streets mostly have no names, asking a police officer for directions is often the most
  reliable method — a genuinely systematic and culturally distinct addressing convention.
- **futatsume**, **mittsume**: ordinal counting with -tsume ("the second," "the third" [of something],
  recurring the Ch.5 numeral/ordinal system in a concrete wayfinding context).
- **hiroimasu** (from hirou/hiroimasu): here specifically "to hail/pick up (a taxi)."

## Chapter 19 — The passive, the causative and the passive causative (p. 196–205)

### Grammar points

#### 120. The passive (i)
The passive of all verbs except suru/shimasu is formed from the negative plain form: Group 1 verbs
take **rareru** (**tabenai → tabe → taberareru**; **minai → mi → mirareru**); Group 2 verbs take
**reru** (**kakanai → kaka → kakareru**; **shiranai → shira → shirareru**; **kawanai → kawa →
kawareru**); irregular **konai → ko → korareru**, **suru/shimasu → sareru**. All passives then function
as Group 1 verbs.

#### 121. The passive (ii)
As in English, the passive is used with **transitive** verbs; the human agent, when expressed, is
generally followed by **ni** (*Ano kisha wa terorisuto ni yūkai sareta sō desu*, "I hear that the
reporter was kidnapped by terrorists"). The passive is used frequently with verbs of asking/saying
where English often doesn't use a passive (*Tsūyaku suru yō ni tanomaremashita*, "They asked me to
interpret," lit. "I was asked to interpret"). The agent may instead be marked by **ni yotte** (lit. "by
means of") or **kara** ("from"), particularly to avoid an ambiguity that plain **ni** could cause
(*Tomodachi kara pātei ni shōtai sareta n'desu*, "I was invited to the party by my friend"; *Mishima ni
yotte kakareta hon desu*, "It's a book written by Mishima").

#### 122. The passive (iii) — the "passive of inconvenience"
Two further important uses of the passive. First, the so-called **"passive of inconvenience"**
(**meiwaku no ukemi**, though the source doesn't use this Japanese-linguistics term itself), used of
**transitive** verbs to mean "having something done to one against one's will/to one's detriment"
(*Nimotsu o shirabemashita*, neutral "[They] checked my baggage" vs. *Nimotsu o shiraberaremashita*,
"I had my baggage checked [and wasn't happy about it]"; *Dareka ga watashi no pasupōto o
nusumimashita* vs. *Pasupōto o nusumaremashita*, "I had my passport stolen"). Second, the same
"inconvenience" passive is also used of **intransitive** verbs — unlike English or the ordinary
transitive passive, this has no direct literal equivalent and simply marks that the event affected the
speaker inconveniently (*Otōsan ga shinimashita*, neutral "His father died" vs. *Otōsan ni
shinaremashita*, "He suffered the death of his father" [more literally, and distinctively, "he was
'died' on by his father"]; *O-kyaku-san ga kimashita* vs. *O-kyaku-san ni koraremashita*, "A visitor
came [and it was inconvenient for me]"; *Akachan ga zutto naite imashita* vs. *Akachan ni zutto
nakarete imashita*, "The baby kept crying [and it was inconvenient for me]"). This intransitive
"adversative passive" is flagged here as a genuinely distinctive Japanese grammatical category with no
single-word English equivalent — significant for later mechanics-analysis work on how inconvenience/
affectedness can be grammaticalized.

#### 123. The passive (iv)
"Has been done" ("is in a state of having been done") is **not** a passive but **te aru/arimasu**
(*Bīru wa katte aru n'desu ka*, "Has the beer been bought?"). **Kaite aru/arimasu** specifically covers
"it says..." (of written text) (*Shinnyū kinshi to kaite aru n'desu*, "It says 'No Entry'"; *Jisho ni
wa 'gaijin' to kaite arimasu*, "It says in the dictionary 'foreigner'").

#### 124. Transitive and intransitive verbs
The source gives a (explicitly non-comprehensive) list of related transitive/intransitive verb pairs
sharing a root but differing in whether they take an object: **akeru/akemasu** (open, vt.) / **aku/
akimasu** (be open, vi.); **atsumeru/atsumemasu** (collect, vt.) / **atsumaru/atsumarimasu** (be
collected, vi.); **ireru/iremasu** (put in, vt.) / **hairu/hairimasu** (go in, vi.); **shimeru/
shimemasu** (close, vt.) / **shimaru/shimarimasu** (be closed, vi.); **tomeru/tomemasu** (stop, vt.) /
**tomaru/tomarimasu** (stop/stay, vi.); **tsukeru/tsukemasu** (attach/turn on, vt.) / **tsuku/
tsukimasu** (be attached/go on, vi.); **tateru/tatemasu** (build, vt.) / **tatsu/tachimasu** (stand,
vi.); **tsutaeru/tsutaemasu** (convey, vt.) / **tsutawaru/tsutawarimasu** (be conveyed, vi.); **watasu/
watashimasu** (hand over, vt.) / **wataru/watarimasu** (cross over, vi.). The te-iru form of the
intransitive member and the **te aru** form of the transitive member can both describe a resulting
state, but te aru more emphatically implies the existence of an agent who brought the state about —
*Doa wa aite imasu* ("The door is open") vs. *Doa wa akete arimasu ka* ("Has the door been opened?");
same contrast for **haitte iru** vs. **irete aru**, and **tsuite iru** vs. **tsukete aru**.

#### 125–126. The causative (i)–(ii)
Causatives of all verbs except suru/shimasu are formed from the negative plain form: Group 1 verbs
take **saseru** (**tabenai → tabe → tabesaseru**; **tsukenai → tsuke → tsukesaseru**); Group 2 verbs
take **seru** (**ikanai → ika → ikaseru**; **yomanai → yoma → yomaseru**; **kawanai → kawa →
kawaseru**); irregular **konai → ko → kosaseru**, **suru/shimasu → saseru**. There are two uses: "let
somebody do..." and "make somebody do...", and the object of letting/making is always a social
inferior of the speaker. When the verb is **intransitive** and the "letting/making" object is
expressed, the particle **o** marks "make" and **ni** marks "let" (*Danna-san o motto hatarakasenakereba
narimasen*, "You must make your husband work more" [o = make]; *Kare ni ikasete kudasai*, "Let him go"
[ni = let]). When the verb is **transitive**, even a person who is *made* (rather than *allowed*) to do
something is marked with **ni** — context disambiguates (*Musume ni sonna manga o yomasetaku nai desu
yo*, "I don't want to let my daughter read those comics").

#### 127. The causative (iii)
The causative passive (-sareru/-serareru forms, e.g. **tabesaserareru**, **ikasareru**) combines with
**kudasaru/kudasaimasu** (and kureru/kuremasu) and **itadaku/itadakimasu** (and morau/moraimasu) as a
polite way to ask permission, either from a superior or from someone the speaker wishes to show respect
to (*Dōzo sō sasete kudasai*, "Please let me do it [for you]"; *Hitokoto iwasete itadakitai n'desu*,
"I should like to say a word" [lit. "I'd like to get you to let me say a word"]).

#### 128. The passive causative
The passive causative (i.e. "is made to," "is allowed to") of all verbs is formed from the causative,
but the most common Group 2 form is not quite the same as the Group 1 pattern: Group 1 **tabesaseru →
tabesase → tabesaserareru**; Group 2 **ikaseru → ika → ikasareru** (not the fully regular *ikaserareru*,
though that longer form also exists) (*Nihongo de hanasaserareta n'desu yo*, "I was made to speak in
Japanese"; *Nanjikanmo agura o kakasaremashita*, "They were made to sit cross-legged for hours" —
recurring the agura o kaku idiom from Ch.15).

### Dialogue/notes highlights (Conversations 19A–19B, p. 204–205)

- **tte**: an abbreviation of **to iu koto wa** (source's own Note 3, cross-referenced to this same

- **kaigai ryūgaku**: studying abroad; **hanayome shugyō**: a named traditional practice of

## Chapter 20 — Informal and honorific language: a summary (p. 206–214)

### Grammar points

#### 129. Informal Japanese
Informal speech is used addressing family, close friends, and social inferiors — never strangers or
superiors; the speaker must judge when it's permissible. Features: frequent particle omission,
abbreviated forms, and especially the **plain form** of verbs/adjectives in place of the -masu/desu
forms used elsewhere in this course.

#### 130. Plain forms
Informal-speech patterns: (a) **da** is frequently omitted after nouns/na-adjectives (*Kirei da yo* →
*Kirei yo*); (b) in requests **ka** is often omitted, with **no** sometimes replacing it; (c) **no
(ka)** serves as the plain-form equivalent of **n'desu (ka)**. A closing footnote marks three
gender-associated forms explicitly: **da is omitted in women's informal speech**; **no is
particularly characteristic of women's informal speech**; **wa (sentence-final) is characteristic of
women's speech** — a direct, explicit source statement on gendered register, consistent with similar
notes already flagged in Ch.10's dialogue notes (kashira, sentence-final wa).

#### 131. Omission
In informal speech, particles **wa, ga, o, ni, e** may be dropped when meaning stays clear; **kudasai**
may be dropped from imperatives (*Yamete*, "Stop!"; *Kotchi kite*, "Come here!"); entire clauses after
conjunctions like **kara** and **ga** may be left dangling/omitted, with the implication understood
from context (*Ashita hayaku dekaketai kara...*, "I want to be off early tomorrow [so...]").

#### 132. Abbreviations
A selection of common informal contractions, several already seen individually in earlier chapters but
gathered here systematically: **te wa → tcha** (*tabecha dame yo*, "you mustn't eat that" — recurring
Ch.15's tcha); **de wa → ja** (*sore ja ikō ka*, "OK then, let's go"); **te iru → te'ru** (*nani
shite'ru*, "what're you doing?"); **te oku → t'oku** (*asoko ni oit'oku*, "I'll leave it there" —
recurring Ch.18's te oku); **te ageru → tt'ageru** (*tsukutt'ageru*, "I'll make it for you"); **datte**
(even, as for) substituting for **de mo** (*watashi datte dekinai*, "even I can't do it"); **tte**
substituting for **to itta/itte ita** or **to iu no wa** (recurring Ch.19's tte, generalized here to a
broader quotative-abbreviation pattern). Informal first-person pronouns (men's speech only): **boku**,
**ore**; informal second-person: **kimi**, **o-mae**.

#### 133–134. Honorific Japanese; deferential & humble verbs and verb forms
Two types of honorific language: **deferential** (elevating the person spoken to/about via certain
verbs/forms) and **humble** (the speaker lowering themselves/their in-group out of respect). A core set
of verbs has **dedicated, lexically distinct** deferential and humble alternatives (distinct from the
regular patterns), tabulated by the source as a systematic paradigm: **iru/imasu** ("be," inanimate n/a)
→ deferential **irassharu/irasshaimasu**, humble **oru/orimasu**; **iku/ikimasu** and **kuru/kimasu**
(share one slot) → deferential **irassharu/irasshaimasu** or **oide ni naru/narimasu**, humble
**mairu/mairimasu** or **ukagau/ukagaimasu**; **suru/shimasu** → deferential **nasaru/nasaimasu**,
humble **itasu/itashimasu** (recurring Ch.10's itasu); **iu/iimasu** → deferential **ossharu/
osshaimasu**, humble **mōsu/mōshimasu** (recurring Ch.10's mōsu); **taberu/tabemasu** and **nomu/
nomimasu** (share one slot) → deferential **meshiagaru/meshiagarimasu** (recurring Ch.17's meshiagaru),
humble **itadaku/itadakimasu** (recurring Ch.10); **miru/mimasu** → deferential **goran ni naru/
narimasu**, humble **haiken itasu/itashimasu**; **kiku/kikimasu** → deferential **(o-kiki ni naru/
narimasu)**, humble **ukagau/ukagaimasu**; **shiru/shirimasu** → deferential **gozonji desu**, humble
**zonjiru/zonjimasu**. Verbs lacking dedicated forms take productive patterns instead: deferential
**o-[stem] ni naru/narimasu** or the plain **passive** (**kakareru/kakaremasu** — note the passive's
genuine double-duty as a deferential form, distinct from its ordinary passive/adversative-passive uses
in Ch.19); humble **o-[stem] suru/shimasu** (**kaku/kakimasu** → deferential **o-kaki ni naru/narimasu**
or **kakareru/kakaremasu**, humble **o-kaki suru/shimasu**). **Itadaku/itadakimasu** recurs yet again
here specifically as a humble-request softener (*Denwa o tsukawasete itadakemasu ka*, "May I use the
phone?" — causative + itadaku, combining Ch.19's passive-causative-adjacent causative with Ch.10's
itadaku). Honorific imperatives: **shōshō o-machi kudasai** ("please wait a minute"), **o-meshiagari
kudasai** (recurring Ch.17), **dōzo o-kake kudasai** ("please take a seat").

#### 135. Honorific nouns and adjectives
Some nouns/adjectives have dedicated honorific **alternatives**: **hito → kata**, **dare → donata** (or
**dochirasama**), **doko → dochira**, **ii → yoroshii**. Some nouns are made honorific by **go-** or
**o-** prefixing (already seen throughout this course). Some adjectives are made honorific by **o-**
prefixing (*o-isogashii*, "busy [honorific]"; *o-wakai*, "young [honorific]"). Certain verbs are
regularly preceded by **o-** and followed by **desu** as a further honorific pattern (**dekakeru →
o-dekake desu**). The source closes by directing the reader back to specific prior conversations (2c,
3a, 4a, 5c, 7b, 9a/b, 10b, 14a, 17a) as worked examples of honorific speech already encountered in
context — an explicit acknowledgment that honorific forms have been seeded gradually through the whole
course rather than introduced only here.

### Dialogue/notes highlights (Conversations 20A–20B, p. 209–210, 213)
- **ii no ni**: "they're nice, [but]..." — **no ni** here expressing regret (source's own Note 1),
  distinct from the purpose/concessive no ni of Ch.15's grammar notes 100-101 — another case of a
  single surface form carrying a third, affect-laden sense worth flagging for later mechanics work.
- **o-azukari itasu/itashimasu**: a humble verb meaning "to receive/hold (money) in trust," attested in
  a shopping-payment context (shopkeeper taking payment) — a culturally specific service-register
  humble verb not in the main paradigm table.
- **dochira-sama de irasshaimasu ka**: a maximally polite "who is this/who's calling?" combining the
  honorific-noun dochirasama with the deferential irassharu.
- **masu deshō ka**: source's own Note 1 (second chapter note set) — in honorific speech, -masu forms
  are commonly retained in contexts where the plain form would otherwise be grammatically adequate, a
  further honorific-register marker beyond the dedicated-verb paradigm.
- **o-ukagai suru/shimasu**: here specifically means "I shall visit" (source's own Note 2) — a
  contextual narrowing of the general humble ukagau ("to ask/to visit," already in the main paradigm
  as the humble form of kiku).
- **kashikomarimashita**: a set, very formal acknowledgment formula ("certainly," "understood" — used
  by service staff/secretaries accepting an instruction), distinct from and more formal than plain
  wakarimashita.

## Appendix 1 — Hiragana (p. 267–269)

The complete hiragana syllabary (gojūon, 46 base characters) is given matched 1:1 against the
romanization already used throughout the course: **aあ, i い, u う, e え, o お; ka か, ki き, ku く, ke
け, ko こ; sa さ, shi し, su す, se せ, so そ; ta た, chi ち, tsu つ, te て, to と; na な, ni に, nu ぬ,
ne ね, no の; ha は, hi ひ, fu ふ, he へ, ho ほ; ma ま, mi み, mu む, me め, mo も; ya や, yu ゆ, yo よ; ra
ら, ri り, ru る, re れ, ro ろ; wa わ; o を (object-particle-only variant, source's own Note 3); n ん.**
Two characters carry an explicit dual-reading note: **は (ha)** is also used for writing the topic
particle **wa** (source's own Note 1), and **へ (he)** is also used for the directional particle **e**
("to"/"towards") (source's own Note 2) — both notes directly explain a script/particle mismatch a
learner would otherwise find confusing, since the particles are pronounced differently from the kana's
base reading.

**Voiced sounds**: the "voiced" row of sounds (ga/gi/gu etc., za/ji/zu etc., da/ji/zu etc., ba/bi/bu
etc. — corresponding to rows 3, 5, 7, 10 of the base table) are formed by adding a **nigori** mark
(゛, a diacritic resembling a double quotation mark) to the ka/sa/ta/ha-row kana respectively; the
**pa/pi/pu/pe/po** row (row 11) is formed by adding a small circle (**maru**, ゜) to the ha-row kana
instead. Worked examples given: ながさき → Nagasaki, ぎんざ → Ginza (a main street in Tokyo), だめ →
dame, ぶぶん → bubun.

**Long vowel "ō" spelling convention**: the long ō sound (ō, kō, sō, etc.) is generally **written as
"ou," "kou," "sou,"** etc. in hiragana, not with a repeated vowel — i.e. the kana spelling and the
romanized long-vowel macron don't map letter-for-letter (examples: そう → sō, じょうず → jōzu, ほう →
hō). A **small number of exceptions** instead double the vowel kana directly: おおきい → ōkii, おおい →
ōi — the source explicitly flags these as exceptions to the "ou" convention rather than instances of
it.

**Double consonant (sokuon)**: written with a small つ (tsu) preceding the consonant to be doubled —
かった → katta, まった → matta, ちょっと → chotto.

**Combined syllables** (contracted ya/yu/yo combinations, e.g. kya/kyu/kyo, written with a small や/ゆ/
よ after an i-row kana): referenced back to the Introduction's existing explanation (captured in the
prior file) rather than re-explained; worked examples given here: げいしゃ → geisha, しゅじゅつ →
shujutsu, しゅうかん → shūkan, おちゃ → o-cha, いちにちじゅう → ichinichijū, おばさん → obasan ("aunt"),
おばあさん → obāsan ("grandmother" — note the single-vs-double-vowel kana distinguishing two otherwise
near-homophonous words, a genuinely important minimal pair), きょうと → Kyōto, きゅうしゅう → Kyūshū, あな
→ ana ("hole"), あんな → anna ("that sort of" — another near-minimal-pair example, this time hinging on
the ん moraic nasal), かた → kata ("person," recurring Ch.20's honorific noun), かった → katta (recurring
the double-consonant example above, "won"/"bought" plain-past).

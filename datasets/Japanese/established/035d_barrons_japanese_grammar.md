## Grammar points

### §7.1–7.2 What are verbs? / Agreement

Japanese verbs carry no agreement at all with their subject's person, number, or gender — unlike English, where "I go" vs. "he/she/it goes" vs. "they go" requires different forms, Japanese uses the identical verb form (`ikimasu`) regardless of subject (p. 78–79). The source notes this interacts directly with the pro-drop pattern already seen in §3.3-3/§5.2: a bare verb form (`Ikimasu`) can constitute a complete, unambiguous sentence on its own precisely because no person/number information is lost by omitting the subject (p. 79).

### §7.3 Verb conjugations (the two-class system + two irregulars)

All Japanese verbs fall into exactly two regular conjugation classes, plus two wholly irregular verbs (`suru` "to do," `kuru` "to come") (p. 79). The two classes are distinguished by their **stem** — the inflectional base morpheme that different tense/mood/politeness suffixes attach to:

- **c-stem ("consonant-stem"/"u-dropping") verbs** — the dictionary form's stem ends in a consonant; the stem is formed by dropping final `-u` (e.g. `kaku` → `kak-`). Diagnostic: a dictionary form *not* ending in `-eru` or `-iru` is reliably c-stem (p. 80). Several sound-change sub-rules apply at the stem boundary for verbs ending `-su` (stem-final consonant becomes `sh` before an `i`-initial suffix, `s` elsewhere) and `-tsu` (becomes `ch` before `i`-initial, `t` elsewhere) (p. 81), and for verbs ending `-au`/`-iu`/`-ou` a historical `w` is retained in the stem for certain negative-form derivations even though it surfaces nowhere in the affirmative forms (p. 82). A genuine irregular-by-shape subclass also exists: several verbs that *look* like they should be v-stem by their `-eru`/`-iru` shape (`hairu` "enter," `kaeru` "return," `shiru` "know," `kiru` "to cut," `iru` "to need") are actually c-stem — learners cannot rely on surface shape alone (p. 82). This produces a genuine minimal pair: c-stem `kiru` "to cut" (first-syllable stress) vs. v-stem `kiru` "to wear" (second-syllable stress) — homographic in plain/dictionary form, distinguished only by stress and by which conjugation class each follows (p. 82).
- **v-stem ("vowel-stem"/"ru-dropping") verbs** — the stem is formed by dropping final `-ru`; most (but not all, see above) `-eru`/`-iru`-ending dictionary forms belong here (p. 83).
- **Irregular verbs** `suru`/`kuru` — have their own unpredictable stems/infinitives (`shi-`/`ki-` respectively) that must simply be memorized (p. 85).

### §7.4 The infinitive

A second key inflectional base, formed differently per class: c-stem verbs add `-i` to the stem (`kak-` → `kaki-`); v-stem verbs add nothing — stem and infinitive are identical (`age-` → `age-`) (p. 84). The infinitive is itself the base for one construction method of the polite present tense (adding `-masu`) (p. 95).

### §7.5 Plain and polite forms (the register system)

Japanese verb forms come in two broad politeness registers: **plain** (used with immediate family and close friends) and **polite** (wider general-use register, recommended for learners) (p. 85). The critical structural rule, repeated with worked examples, is that **only the sentence-final verb's register determines the politeness level of the entire sentence** — non-final verbs within the same sentence conventionally take the *plain* form even when the sentence as a whole is polite (e.g. `Ban gohan o tabeta ato, terebi o mimashita` — plain `tabeta` mid-sentence, polite `mimashita` at the end — reads as an overall-polite sentence) (p. 86–87). This is a significant, genuinely findable register-mixing mechanism worth flagging for later slang/register mechanics work: politeness in Japanese is a sentence-final (not uniformly-distributed) property.

### §7.6 The "to be" verbs (desu / arimasu / imasu — a three-way split)

Unlike English's single "to be," Japanese splits the concept three ways by what kind of predication is involved: **`desu`** is the copula of identity/equation/quality ("is/equals," used for noun-predicates and adjectival/quality statements; full present/past/probable × affirmative/negative paradigm on p. 88, with informal `ja` substitutable for `dewa` in the negative forms); **`arimasu`** is the existential/locative/possessive verb for *inanimate* things, where in its "to have" sense the possessed item is grammatically the subject (marked `ga`), not a direct object (p. 89–90, full paradigm p. 90); **`imasu`** is the parallel existential/locative verb for *animate* things (people, animals, insects — explicitly excluding plants, which take `arimasu`) (p. 91, full paradigm p. 92), and additionally serves as the auxiliary verb that builds the progressive tenses (§7.7-4/5). This three-way split, cross-referenced against `jibun`'s own animacy restriction (§5.7) and the locative-`ni` animacy split already noted in §6.2, establishes animacy as a recurring, independently-grammaticalized category across multiple unrelated parts of Japanese grammar — a structural pattern worth carrying forward into later mechanics analysis.

### §7.7-1 Present tense (the two-tense system)

The source explicitly frames Japanese as having only two tenses — past and non-past — with "present" here being a conventional relabeling of "non-past," which covers both present and future time (there is no dedicated future tense) (p. 93). The **plain present** is simply the dictionary form itself. The **plain negative present** is formed by adding `-anai` to a c-stem (with the hidden-w inserted for `-au`/`-iu`/`-ou` verbs, e.g. `kau` → `kawanai`) or `-nai` to a v-stem (p. 94–95). The **polite present** ("-masu form") is built either by adding `-masu` to the infinitive, or equivalently by adding `-imasu` to a c-stem/`-masu` to a v-stem (two equivalent derivation routes to the same form) (p. 95–96); the **polite negative present** simply swaps `-masu` → `-masen` for any verb, regardless of class (p. 96).

### §7.7-2 Past tense

The **plain past** is trivial for v-stem verbs (add `-ta` to the stem) but requires a euphonic sound-change table for c-stem verbs, keyed to the dictionary form's final syllable: `-u`/`-tsu`/`-ru` → `-tta`; `-mu`/`-nu`/`-bu` → `-nda`; `-ku` → `-ita`; `-gu` → `-ida`; `-su` → `-shita` (p. 98–99, full table). This is the same sound-change system that underlies the gerund/-te form (§7.7-3) one step later. The **plain negative past** adds `-anakatta` (c-stem, with hidden-w where applicable) or `-nakatta` (v-stem) to the stem (p. 99–100). The **polite past** swaps `-masu` → `-mashita`; the **polite negative past** appends `deshita` onto the polite present negative (`-masen` → `-masen deshita`) (p. 100–102).

### §7.7-3 The gerund (-te form)

Explicitly flagged by the source as a false-friend term: unlike the English "gerund" (a verbal noun, e.g. "swimming" in "I like swimming" — that sense is covered separately under Nominalization, §7.19), the Japanese gerund is a pure verb form carrying no tense/mood of its own, but combining productively with other elements (the progressive auxiliary `iru`, the imperative, and others) (p. 102). It is formed mechanically from the plain past tense by changing final `-a` to `-e` (e.g. `tabeta` → `tabete`; `yonda` → `yonde`) — meaning it inherits the exact same euphonic sound-change classes as the past tense (p. 102–103). The copula's irregular gerund is `de` (from plain past `datta`) (p. 103). A key discourse use: when listing a neutral sequence of actions within one sentence with no special contrast/emphasis, every verb but the last takes gerund form, and only the sentence-final verb carries full tense/mood marking (p. 103) — structurally parallel to the plain/polite register-mixing rule in §7.5, reinforcing that Japanese systematically concentrates grammatical marking at the clause-final verb.

### §7.7-4 Present progressive

Formed from gerund + a form of `iru` (polite form given throughout) (p. 104). Two distinct readings emerge depending on verb transitivity, a distinction with no direct English analog: with a transitive verb, the progressive reads as an action genuinely in progress (`tabete imasu` "am eating," `kaite imasu` "am writing") (p. 104–105); with an intransitive verb, the same construction instead reads as a **resultant state** — "X happened and the result still holds" — e.g. `Mado ga aite imasu` "The window is open" literally parses as "someone opened it, and it's still open," not "the window is in the process of opening" (p. 105–106). The construction can also carry an English-present-perfect sense ("have been doing") specifically when paired with a duration expression (`Kyōto ni gonen kan sunde imasu` "I have been living in Kyoto for five years," vs. bare `Kyōto ni sunde imasu` "I'm living in Kyoto," with no "for five years" to anchor a perfect reading) (p. 106).

### §7.7-5 Past progressive

Formed from gerund + the past form of `iru` (`imashita` affirmative, `imasen deshita` negative) (p. 107) — mechanically a straightforward extension of the present progressive's gerund+iru pattern, with the same transitive-action/intransitive-resultant-state split carrying over to past time (p. 107–108).

# Japanese — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Communicating with Japanese by the Total Method, Basic Part 2 (Lessons 21–25)

**Source:** Noah S. Brannen (assisted by Masayoshi Hirose and Tsuyoshi Sasaki; revised with the
assistance of Katsuhisa Yamaguchi), *Communicating with Japanese by the Total Method, Basic, Part
Two* (Lessons 21–30) (Japan Missionary Language Institute, Tokyo; reprinting a 1976-era original
revised through ~1986, per the Author's Preface). 211 PDF pages; printed page numbers run roughly
1–202 plus front matter and a back-of-book index. **Offset formula confirmed empirically (per the
dispatch's required before-committing check): `PDF_page = printed_page + 9`** for this file (e.g.,
printed p. 1 = PDF p. 10; printed p. 202 index = PDF p. 209) — distinct from `062`/`062b`'s Part 1
offset (`+10`) since this is a separate PDF file with its own front matter. Confirmed via direct
inspection of the Author's Preface (printed "ix" = PDF 5), the Introduction (printed "1" = PDF 10),
and multiple lesson-header pages throughout (e.g., Lesson 21 title = printed p. 5 = PDF p. 14;
Lesson 30 title = printed p. 157 = PDF p. 166), not assumed from a single sample point.

This file covers Lessons 21–25 (printed pp. 5–84 approximately): 21 "Giving and Receiving"
(benefactive auxiliaries, honorifics), 22 "Trying, Preparing, and Becoming" (verb auxiliaries miru/
oku/shimau/kuru/iku, presumptive copula), 23 "This, That, and the Other" (coordinate noun phrases,
limiters), 24 "'Sakura, Sakura'" (desiderative -tai, plain forms, -te/-tekara sentence-combining),
and 25 "The Book Tanaka Wrote" (modified noun phrases, attributive/embedded clauses). Lessons 26–30
are covered in `062d`.

**Coverage note — representative sampling across a 655-page, two-part audio-course textbook (444 +
211 pages), continuing the sampling approach of `062`/`062b`.** Per dispatch instructions, this is
NOT exhaustive page-by-page extraction. Lessons 21–24 (the foundational benefactive/honorific,
aspectual-auxiliary, coordinate-noun-phrase, and desiderative/plain-form bases) were sampled most
fully — full or near-full Section 1 vocabulary and the complete Section 4 Grammar explanation(s) for
each. Lesson 25 was sampled slightly more selectively (one vocabulary page, the opening Section 4
grammar point on modified noun phrases/embedding) consistent with the "later lessons sampled more
selectively" instruction, though this lesson's single grammar point (noun-phrase embedding) is
dense enough that it was still captured close to fully. Section 2 (Kanji Study), Section 3 (Drill),
Section 5 (Dialogue), Section 6 (Reading), Section 7 (Exercises), and Section 8 (Self Test) were
skipped except where they were the only place a form appeared, or where an "English Equivalents"
sub-list was scanned in the course of locating Section 4 — these are pure pedagogical repetition of
already-captured vocabulary/grammar per the spec's coverage rule. This source has no text layer and
was entirely vision-read (scanned book, clean typeset print quality — `pdftotext` over the full
211-page file returned essentially nothing, ~211 bytes of page-break characters only).

**Vision-reading guard:** no handwritten marginalia was found on any vision-read page in this
sample. Several pages (e.g. printed pp. 25, 79, 81, 91, 93, 102, 110, 119, 147, 156) show a
same-book *bleed-through* artifact — faint mirrored text/section-header ghosting from the facing or
reverse page showing through the scan — which is a print/scan artifact, not marginalia, and was
excluded from extraction wherever it was not independently legible as the page's own primary
content. All extracted content below is `verified` or `plausible_unverified` Vision Reading
Confidence as marked per row; print quality was uniformly high (clean typewriter-style typeset
scan), so most entries are `verified`.

## Grammar points

### 21.1 Levels of "giving"

Japanese giving-verbs encode two independent parameters simultaneously: **direction** (away from
the speaker vs. toward the speaker) and **politeness level** (Honorific-Exalted / Honorific-Humble /
Neutral / Non-polite). The source gives a 2×3 chart of "give" verbs: away-from-speaker non-polite
**yaru** (dropping out of use in favor of the more neutral **ageru**, especially for one's own
children), away-from-speaker neutral **ageru**, away-from-speaker exalted/humble **sashiageru**
(special respect, e.g. toward a teacher); toward-speaker neutral **kureru**, toward-speaker
exalted **kudasaru**. kudasaru belongs to a small, irregular class of Honorific Consonant Verbs
(the -r- of the stem drops before -másu: kudasáru + másu → kudasaimásu, not *kudasarimásu).

### 21.2 Verbs for "receiving"

Unlike giving-verbs, receiving-verbs do not encode direction (the same verb covers both "you
receive" and "I receive"), only politeness: Neutral **morau**, Exalted **o-uke ni naru**, Humble
**itadaku**. One asymmetry: morau cannot be used to mean "you received from me" (though it can mean
"you received from him"). The Humble form is typically restricted to "I receive," though this can
extend to very close others (family, intimate friends).

### 21.3–21.4 Giving/receiving charted from the speaker's point of view; benefactive verb auxiliaries

All five giving/receiving verbs (sashiageru, ageru, yaru; kudasaru, kureru; itadaku, morau) can
also function as **verb auxiliaries** attached to the gerund (-te) form of a main verb, in which
role they do not themselves mean "give/receive" but instead mark **benefaction**: auxiliaries of
giving mark the beneficiary of the main verb's action ("for you/him/etc.," or "for me/us"), while
auxiliaries of receiving mark the *originator* of a favor done for the speaker (equivalent to
English "have/get someone to do X for me"), with the agent of the embedded action marked by **ni**
(the Agent marker particle) rather than by the grammatical subject particles ga/wa. E.g. **Musuko ni
tegami o dáshite moraimáshita** "I had my son mail the letter" vs. **Yamada-sañ ni tegami o dáshite
itadakimáshita** "I had Mr. Yamada [higher-status] mail the letter."

### 21.5 "Honorifics"

Honorific style is marked three ways: (1) **affixes on nouns** — the prefix o- (or variants go-,
mi-, gyo-, oñ-, the last three archaic/literary) plus the suffix -sañ/-sama, e.g. o-hanashi "talk,"
go-hoñ "book," otōsañ "father"; some forms (gohañ "rice," omútsu "diaper," onaka "stomach") have
fused so completely with the prefix that the bare form is never heard, and the source explicitly
warns against extending this prefix to foreign loanwords (*o-tabako, *o-kōhī are both marked
non-standard, though attested informally in restaurant speech); (2) **special verb inflections**
for the copula/adjectives/verbs; (3) a closed set of **Humble/Exalted verb pairs** distinct from the
plain verb — Humble sashiageru/itadaku/mōsu/máiru vs. Exalted kudasáru/o-uke-ni-náru/ossháru/
irasshāru ("gives/receives/says/goes-comes" respectively).

### 22.1 Verb auxiliaries: miru, oku, shimau, kuru/iku

Four more -te-form verb auxiliaries are introduced. **-te miru** "tries doing X." **-te oku**
"does X in advance/preparation, before the point of need" (central semantic feature: anticipatory
action). **-te shimau** "finishes up doing X" or, with an emotional/regrettable overtone, "ends up
doing X" (central semantic feature: an action that, once done, cannot be undone — contrast **Tábete
shimaimáshita** "I ate it all up / I've eaten already" with the more neutral **Tábete okimáshita**
"I've eaten already, so we can go now"). **-te kuru/-te iku** as auxiliaries work via a two-step
paraphrase (main-verb meaning + "and then" + auxiliary meaning), but their real meaning depends
critically on the speaker's *point of view* relative to a customary location — kuru can effectively
mean "went and did X [and have now returned]" or iku "went off and did X," depending on where the
speaker currently stands relative to their usual place. The same pair, in a second use, marks a kind
of progressive/directional "tense": -te kuru = "comes to be" (approaching the speaker's point in
time), -te iku = "gets to be" (receding from it) — e.g. **Tōkyō no jiñkō wa dañdañ fúete ikú deshō**
"Tokyo's population will probably gradually increase." Auxiliaries as a class are grammatically
"slighted": written in hiragana even when the main verb takes kanji, commonly fused phonetically with
the main verb in speech (tsukútte oku → tsukúttoku; tábete shimau → tábechau; yóñde iru → yóñderu),
and they lose their own innate word-accent.

### 22.2 The presumptive inflection of the copula

The polite presumptive copula is **deshō** (plain: -ō, the same morpheme already seen in the
hortative use, Lesson 13). In this lesson a second meaning is added: **probability**, attachable
after a verbal or adjectival clause whose predicate is in plain form, e.g. **Tōkyō no jiñkō wa
dañdañ fúete ikú deshō** "Tokyo's population will probably gradually increase."

### 23.1 Coordinate noun phrases

Two (or more) noun heads can be joined by a conjunctive — **to** (exclusive: excludes all other
items from consideration) or **ya** (inclusive: implies other unlisted items are included, often
reinforced by trailing **nado** "and so forth") — or by a disjunctive — **ka** (plain "or") or
**toka** (inclusive-flavored "or," implying a longer indefinite list of alternatives). A third
conjunctive, **ni**, is reserved for technical/enumerative contexts such as inventory-taking. The
particle **mo** can also serve as an (emphatic, "both...and") coordinating conjunctive: **Hóñ mo
nóto mo arimásu** "There are both books and notebooks."

### 23.2 Limiters

A small closed class of post-head noun-phrase modifiers: **daké** "only" (keeps the element-marker
particle, optionally), **bakari** "only/nothing but" (same), **shika** "only" (used only with a
negative predicate, and *drops* the element-marker particle entirely), **daké shika** (emphatic
double limiter, also negative-predicate-only), and **nado** "and such." daké and bakari differ in
nuance: daké picks out an exception/sole item ("only this I didn't understand"), while bakari
implies near-total saturation by the limited category ("nothing but beautiful girls came"; "these
days everything is disagreeable").

### 24.1 Desiderative clauses

Though verbal in its underlying kernel, a desiderative clause built with the suffix **-tai**
("want to [verb]") is grammatically **adjectival**: once attached, -tai takes adjective-style
inflection, not verb inflection (e.g. past *tabetakatta*, not a verb-style past). A further
structural consequence: the goal of the desired action is marked with the grammatical **subject**
particle **ga**, not the object particle o (**Mizu ga nomitái desu** "I want a drink of water," lit.
"water is desired to drink") — though the source notes this is being supplanted by object-marking
**o** in younger Tokyo speech (**Mizu o nomitái desu**). Desiderative sentences are most naturally
used of the speaker's own wants or those of intimates; a desiderative question directed at someone
else's wishes can read as abrupt (**Watashi no uchi ni kitái desu ka?** "Do you wish to come to my
home?" is flagged as an oddly blunt invitation; the idiomatic form uses a plain invitational
question instead, **Watashi no uchi ni kimaseñ ka?**).

**Full paradigm given** (Indicative / Negative / Past / Past Negative), Plain and Polite:
tabetái / tabetáku nai / tabetákatta / tabetáku nákatta (or tabetáku arimaseñ deshita for Polite).

### 24.2 Plain negative clauses; plain presumptive; summary of plain forms

The plain negative of aru "is (inanimate)" is **nai** "is not" — itself grammatically an adjective
(takes -i/-katta/-ku suffixes), serving as the plain-form verbal predicate corresponding to
arimaseñ. The plain presumptive/hortative ("let's do X") is formed Vowel-Verb stem + -yō (tabeyō
"let's eat!"), Consonant-Verb stem + -ō (nomō "let's drink!"), with irregular shiyō/koyō for
suru/kuru. **A full summary paradigm table of Plain Forms of the Predicate is given** across Copula
(da/datta/ja(de) wa nai/ja(de) wa nakatta/darō), Adjective (takai/takakatta/takaku nai/takaku
nakatta/takai darō), and Verb classes (taberu/yomu/suru paradigms fully spelled out) — Indicative,
Past, Negative Indicative, Negative Past, Presumptive. The source flags that, from Lesson 25
onward, plain forms gain an important new function: appearing inside **embedded sentences**.

### 24.3 Combining simple sentences with -te and -te kara

Simple sentences can be chained without an explicit conjunction ("and") by putting every non-final
predicate in gerund (-te) form — **de** for the copula, **-kute** for adjectives, **-te/-de** for
verbs — with only the *final* predicate carrying full tense/politeness inflection: **Watashi wa ié
ni kaette, (ofuro ni háitte,) súgu netái desu** "I want to go home, (take a bath,) and go right to
bed." Adding **kara** "after" to the first -te clause makes the sequential relation explicit:
**Ié ni káette kara, súgu netái desu** "After returning home, I want to go right to bed."

### 25.1 Modified noun phrase (noun-phrase embedding)

The full modified noun phrase has (in a fixed "favorite order") up to three optional elements
around a Head noun: **Pre-Head Modifiers** — (a) Deictic Particles (kono/konna/áru), (b)
**Attributives**, which the source stresses are not single-word modifiers but represent full
clause-level **embedding**: an entire potential clause or simple sentence occupies this slot (e.g.
**[Tanaka-sañ ga/no káita] hóñ** "the book which Mr. Tanaka wrote" — sentence 1, "Tanaka-sañ wa hóñ
o kakimáshita," is embedded as the attributive of sentence 3), and (c) Noun + no (watashi no hóñ "my
book"); and a **Post-Head Modifier (Limiter)** (hóñ daké "a book only," cross-referencing Lesson
23's limiter system). All elements besides the Head are optional, and while long stacked expansions
are grammatically possible, usually only one or two co-occur in a single phrase (worked diagram:
kono [atarashíi] ēgo no hóñ daké "this new English book only").

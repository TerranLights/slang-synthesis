## Grammar points

### Which particles can be left out (p.120-121)
*O* and *ga* are dropped very freely in conversation; *ni* and *e* only in very familiar registers
(and even then, dropping them in an otherwise polite *masu*-form sentence creates an odd register
mismatch); *de*, *kara*, *made*, and *yori* are essentially never dropped, because doing so either
produces nonsense or silently changes the sentence's meaning (collapsing a range into a list, or a
comparison into a flat conjunction).

### Three "near" words and a false friend (p.122-123)
*Soba* and *chikaku* both broadly mean "near" and apply to houses, people, and things alike, with
*soba* reading slightly more conversational/emphatic about closeness. *Kinjo* is narrower — it names
the area neighboring a specific house (typically the speaker's own) — and, unlike the English word
"neighborhood," cannot describe an area as a social/geographic region in its own right; *tokoro*
("place") fills that gap instead.

### Registers of "to visit" (p.124-125)
A cline from casual to formal: *asobi-ni iku/kuru* (casual, signals no formality required — the
appropriate choice for inviting a new acquaintance to drop by), *ai-ni iku/kuru* (implies a definite
purpose, which can make a casual invitation sound like a summons), *tazunete-iku/kuru* (formal, usable
for business or personal calls), and *hoomon-suru* (reserved for an official VIP visit to a nation or
institution — not used for visiting a place like a temple, where *mi-ni iku* fits instead).

### Chiisai vs. semai, and narrower "small" words (p.126-127)
*Chiisai* is the general-purpose "small," usable for almost anything. *Semai* specifically marks a
space as too small *for an intended purpose*, and extends figuratively to confining situations or
narrow-mindedness. Beyond these two, several words cover narrower small-ness domains exclusively:
*komakai* (small change), *kogata-no* (small manufactured objects), *kogarana* (a small-statured
person) — none of these substitute freely for *chiisai* or each other.

### Two readings of ...nai koto-wa nai (p.128-129)
The same double-negation surface form splits into two unrelated readings disambiguated by context: an
emphatic universal affirmative (Group I, "there's nothing I don't do" = "I do everything") and a
reserved/reluctant admission (Group II, "I could, but..."). Adding *...kedo* nudges the construction
toward the reluctant reading when ambiguity would otherwise be a problem.

### Adjective te-forms as "cut/made into X" adverbials (p.130-131)
Size/shape adjectives in their te-form (chiisaku, usuku, hosoku, komakaku, mijikaku) function
adverbially before a transformation verb (kiru "cut," suru "make," nuru "spread," shortening verbs) to
mean "into that resulting state." The source flags this as a specific trap for learners, who tend to
reach instead for a noun-modifying "[adjective]-no + object" phrase that actually implies the object
already exists in that state, rather than describing the desired result of the action.

### Benri vs. tsugoo-ga ii for "convenient" (p.132-133)
*Benri* describes a means/device/tool that suits a need (fast transit, a useful dictionary, a
labor-saving appliance) — but cannot describe a date or time being convenient. For scheduling, *tsugoo-
ga ii/warui* is the correct expression, built on the noun *tsugoo* ("convenience for meeting/
scheduling"), which also underlies phrases like asking about someone's availability.

### Four words for "life," correctly sorted (p.134-135)
*Inochi*/*seemee* cover the biological sense (what makes something alive), with *seemee* preferred in
set kanji-compounds; *jinsee* covers lived human experience across a lifetime; *seekatsu* covers daily
life/livelihood with an economic emphasis. The source gives explicit correction pairs for common
learner mix-ups: "student life" (seekatsu, not jinsee), "philosophical reflection on life" (jinsee) vs.
"thinking about money/career" (seekatsu) with the identical surface frame, and "protecting human life"
(seemee/inochi, not seekatsu).

### Thick/thin word pairs split by object shape, and the person exception (p.136-137)
*Futoi*/*hosoi* (thick/thin) apply to cylinder-like objects and body parts (pillars, ropes, fingers,
arms); *atsui*/*usui* (thick/thin, a homophone-but-different-word pair from *atsui* "hot") apply to
flat/board-like objects (books, paper, walls). Neither pair, however, is used to describe a whole
*person's* size directly — that uses the stative verbs *futotte-iru* ("is stout") and *yasete-iru* ("is
thin") instead.

### Natural word order for requesting a quantity (p.138-139)
The idiomatic pattern for "please give me N of X" is object + *o* + number + verb (*Kami-o ichimai
kudasai*), not a noun-modifying "[number]-no [object]-o" phrase, which instead implies a specific,
already-identified item. When the object is obvious from context, the whole object+*o* phrase can be
dropped, leaving just the bare number and verb.

## Grammar points

### Decontextualized "model sentences" as a structural pedagogical flaw
From "Kore wa hon desu" to "Anata wa onna desu ka?", the chapter argues that functional, situationally
realistic textbooks (e.g. airport/customs dialogues) serve learners far better than abstract
object-naming or random model sentences divorced from real communicative need. p.134-135.

### "Abominable fluency": mechanical correctness without cultural competence
Linguist Eleanor Jorden's term for grammatically flawless utterances that nonetheless violate a
pragmatic norm — illustrated by a textbook dialogue where a foreign student accepts a Japanese
compliment at face value ("Hai, soo desu ne") instead of self-deprecating denial, the expected native
response. (Full self-deprecation discussion is in ch.6/`038d`; here it recurs as a textbook-design
critique.) p.126-127 cross-reference, discussed in ch.7 context p.134-137.

### Gitaigo vs. giseego: two tiers of Japanese sound-symbolic adverbs
Giseego/giongo mimic literal sounds (true onomatopoeia, found in all languages); gitaigo represent
emotional states, textures, or manners with no literal sound referent and are far more developed in
Japanese than in English — linguist Roy Andrew Miller argues this makes gitaigo only SEEM vague in
translation, due to English's comparative poverty of equivalent forms, not genuine imprecision in the
Japanese. p.152-154.

### Reduplication for noun pluralization is a distinct process from gitaigo/giseego
hitobito and katagata are ordinary collective-noun formations (with a voicing shift on the repeated
element), not mimetic adverbs, even though they look superficially similar to gitaigo reduplication.
Gitaigo themselves can be further reduplicated (gatagata → gatagatagatagata; daradara →
daradaradaradara) purely for emphatic intensification. p.154.

### Superstition in Japanese corporate naming
Three separate case studies: Datsun's name changed to avoid an unlucky homophone with "to lose
money" (son); Toyota vs. Toyoda differ by two katakana brush strokes specifically to land on the
luckier number eight (vs. ten); Japanese hospitals avoid room/floor numbers 4 (shi, homophone of
"death") and 9 (ku, associated with "suffering"), paralleled in China and Korea. Religious/
superstitious ritual (Shinto purification, consulting priests/fortune-tellers) persists in ostensibly
secular Japanese business practice. p.154-156.

### English-letter abbreviations with Japan-specific (not English) meanings
GHQ, BCG, PKO, OB/OG, MoF, MITI, BGM, CM, OL, and BS all carry meanings in Japanese usage that differ
from, or don't exist in, standard English usage of the same letters — a systematic "false friends"
risk distinct from ordinary loanword adaptation. PKO is explicitly a financial-media pun layered on
top of the UN's "peacekeeping operations" sense. p.156-159.

### Number-reading alternation as a productive wordplay/advertising resource
Japanese numerals have multiple alternate pronunciations (mittsu/mi for 3, futatsu/fu for 2, yon/yo
for 4, ichi/i for 1, roku/ro for 6), systematically exploited for puns in calendar customs (Ear Day on
March 3, Letter-Writing Day on the 23rd) and advertising (a spa's phone number spelling "yoi furo,"
"good bath"). p.159-161.

### Proverb culture: unusually high-value register, of eclectic (not purely native) origin
Correctly deploying a well-known kotowaza signals genuine cultural/linguistic command and builds
instant rapport, unlike the Western tendency to treat proverbs as intellectually lightweight. Several
commonly used "Japanese" proverbs are demonstrably foreign in origin (Chinmoku wa kin nari, "Silence
is golden"; Tetsu wa atsui uchi ni ute, "Strike while the iron is hot") — many native speakers are
unaware of this, paralleling ch.5's non-English-loanword theme. p.160-161.

### Edo-period vs. Meiji-period lexical layers in modern business vocabulary
Words for pre-modern domestic commerce (ryoogae, kawase, tegata, keeyaku) evolved slowly over
centuries with no shared root pattern; words coined in the short, concentrated Meiji-era burst of
importing Western mechanical inventions show clear rational shared-character families (sha "wheel":
jitensha/jidoosha/kikansha; den "electricity": densha/denwa/hatsudensho). p.162-163.

### The Hepburn/Kunreeshiki romanization conflict and its real-world consequences
Hepburn (de facto standard, English-spelling-based, criticized for obscuring Japanese's actual
morphophonology) competes with the government's own Kunreeshiki (more phonologically consistent,
demonstrated via the matu/matsu verb paradigm, but associated with 1930s-40s nationalist imposition,
which damaged its reputation under the postwar U.S. Occupation). Mixing the two systems inconsistently
produces real errors (the Jun'ichi/Zyun'iti/Jyunichi hodgepodge; the "Kutubare GNP" WSJ error, which
is not just wrong but phonologically impossible in Japanese since /t/ cannot precede /u/ — only /tsu/
is licit). Modern word-processing software accepts both systems' spellings indiscriminately ("anything
goes" input tolerance), and the QWERTY keyboard's lack of a macron key has caused most Hepburn
literature for foreigners to drop vowel-length marking altogether, creating real business-name
ambiguities (Marubatsu Kinko K.K. "strongbox maker" vs. Marubatsu Kinkoo K.K. "gold mine"). As of the
book's writing, Japanese officials involved in ISO standardization were reportedly leaning toward
recommending Kunreeshiki over the more popular Hepburn as the future international standard. p.165-169.

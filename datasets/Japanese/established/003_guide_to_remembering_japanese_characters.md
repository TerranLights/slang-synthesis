# Japanese — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Writing System — Kanji Mnemonics (Curated Sample)

**Source:** Kenneth G. Henshall, *A Guide to Remembering Japanese Characters*, Tuttle Publishing
(first edition 1988; this printing © 1998, ISBN 978-0-8048-2038-7), 702 pages total. Source file:
`source_reference/languages/Japanese/02.Japanese Writing System/A guide to remembering Japanese
characters.pdf`.

**Coverage note — representative curated sample, not exhaustive.** This is a 702-page kanji
mnemonic reference covering all 1,945 Jōyō (general-use) kanji taught in Japanese primary/secondary
school plus general-use characters, organized as one numbered entry per character (#1–#1945),
sequenced by school grade (First Grade through Sixth Grade) and then "General Use." Per the
project's rule for oversized dictionary-like references, this was sampled broadly across the
book's frequency/grade-level progression rather than transcribing all ~1,945 entries, which are
highly repetitive in structure (character, stroke count, on/kun readings, English keyword gloss,
three compound-word examples, an etymological paragraph, and one mnemonic sentence per entry).

## Grammar points

*(This source is a character/writing-system reference, not a grammar textbook — "grammar points"
here means structural conventions of the Japanese writing system itself, which is squarely in
scope for this Writing System chapter.)*

### Stroke order principles (front matter, PDF pp. ~15–28; repeated at PDF p. 702)

Henshall's introduction lays out general stroke-order rules for writing kanji, each illustrated
with worked examples building a character stroke-by-stroke. Principles sampled directly:
1. **Top to bottom** (e.g., 三 "three," 客 "guest" built downward).
2. **Left to right** (e.g., 州 "province," 派 "faction," 例 "example").
3. **Horizontal strokes usually precede vertical ones** at a crossing (e.g., 十 "ten," 土 "earth,"
   耕 "till").
4. **Exception: in a few cases vertical strokes precede horizontal ones** (e.g., 王 "king," 田
   "field," 曲 "bend").
5. **Center usually precedes left and right**, where the side strokes don't exceed two strokes
   each (e.g., 小 "small," 水 "water," 承 "receive") — with heart 忄 and fire 火 flagged by the
   source as the two notable exceptions to this rule.
6. **Outer frame strokes first, but the bottom line of the frame last** (e.g., 国 "country," 日
   "sun," 月 "moon").
7. **A right-to-left diagonal stroke precedes a left-to-right diagonal stroke** (e.g., 人 "person,"
   父 "father").

### Grade-level/frequency organization as the book's own structural principle

The book sequences its 1,945 numbered entries by official Japanese school-grade assignment (First
through Sixth Grade kanji, i.e. the ~1,000 kanji taught in compulsory primary education) and then
continues through the remaining "General Use" (Jōyō) kanji taught at secondary level and beyond.
This is a real, source-supported frequency/difficulty signal (unlike a teaching grammar's arbitrary
lesson ordering) since Japan's Ministry of Education grade assignment is itself an official,
externally-validated frequency/difficulty ranking — worth treating differently from the "lesson
ordering is not real frequency data" caveat elsewhere in this project's spec, though still distinct
from a genuine corpus-frequency count.

### On-yomi / kun-yomi dual-reading convention

Every entry gives the character's **on-yomi** (Sino-Japanese borrowed reading, rendered in the
source in full capitals, e.g. KA, GAKU, KYŪ) separately from its **kun-yomi** (native Japanese
reading, rendered in the source in lowercase italics, e.g. *hi*, *manabu*, *tabi*), with
okurigana-bearing kun-yomi shown split by a slash to mark the inflecting suffix boundary (e.g.
*kudasaru/ru* for 下る, *au/waseru* for 合う/合わせる). Multiple on-yomi or kun-yomi per character
are listed comma-separated where the source has them (e.g. 下 KA, GE / shita, shimo, moto; 下さる,
下る, 下がる, 下りる). This on/kun distinction is itself the single most load-bearing structural
fact about how Japanese kanji readings work, and script choice (which reading applies, and whether
a word is written in kanji, hiragana, or katakana at all) carries real register/semantic
information as already flagged in this language's checklist.

### Radical/component system ("Commonly Occurring Elements" appendix, PDF p. 650/book p. 624)

A dedicated back-matter glossary tabulates the ~40+ recurring graphic components (radicals) used
across the 1,945 entries, each with its own principal meaning(s), organized by stroke count (2
strokes, 3 strokes, ...). Sampled directly (2-stroke section, in full): 口 "container, vessel"; ト
"divination"; 亻/人/イ "person" (three distinct allographs for the same radical); 儿/卩/勹 "bending
person"; 巳 "slumped person"; 匕 "fallen person"; 氵 "water, river, liquid"; 艹 "grass, plants"
(3-stroke); 阝 (left) "hill, mound, terracing" vs. 阝 (right) "village, town" — the same glyph
shape disambiguated by left/right position, a genuinely load-bearing structural fact about how
compound kanji components can be positionally ambiguous; 心/忄 "heart, feelings"; 辶/廴/彳 "movement,
road" (three distinct movement-radical allographs). This appendix is the book's own systematic key
to the recurring phonetic/semantic components referenced throughout every entry's etymology note
above (e.g. "person" 亻, "water" 氵, "heart" 忄/心 recur across more than half of the sampled
entries).

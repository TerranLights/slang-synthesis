## Grammar points

### Hodo: three distinct uses in one word (p.22-23)
*Hodo* covers three related but distinct functions: (1) approximate amount, resembling *gurai* but
more formal (*Ichiman-en-hodo* "about 10,000 yen"); (2) comparison following a noun/pronoun, which
skews toward negative sentences, with *gurai* again the affirmative-leaning counterpart for the same
comparative sense; and (3) following a verb phrase to downplay the seriousness of a situation
(*...hodo-ja nai*, "it's not to the degree of..."), as in Miss Yoshida's framing dialogue explaining
she's not sick enough to stay home.

### The ...garu suffix and its respect ceiling (p.24-25)
Adding *...garu* to the stems of *-tai* or sensation/emotion adjectives (*samui*, *omoshiroi*) forms
a verb describing someone visibly expressing or acting out that feeling (*ikitagaru*, *samugaru*).
It gives a vivid, almost visual description — but it cannot be used of someone owed deference, where
it would sound like an impatient demand undermining their dignity. For a respected person's reported
feelings, *...to itte-imasu* or *...soo-desu* are used instead.

### ...atsukai: treating someone as a category (p.26-27)
*Atsukau* ("to treat/handle") is most natural with inanimate objects, so applying it directly to a
person risks sounding dehumanizing; the noun-compound form *...atsukai-suru* ("to treat as...")
avoids that. Common collocations cover being treated as an old person, a child, a guest
(*okyakusama-atsukai*, implying careful but excluded treatment — "not a member of the family"), a
foreigner (*gaijin-atsukai*), or a friend (*tomodachi-atsukai*).

### Four "why" words on a formality/bluntness cline (p.28-29)
*Naze* works in both written and spoken registers and is formally neutral. *Dooshite* is the everyday
conversational choice, and is specifically preferred with *desu-ka* over *naze-desu-ka* because it
sounds less demanding. *Nande* is familiar-register and more blunt/demanding than *dooshite*.
*Nan-datte* carries an accusatory edge, specifically used when scolding or blaming someone — the
source contrasts it directly with *dooshite* in the same sentence to show how much more critical it
sounds.

### Kankee: relation, reason, and occupational field (p.30-31)
*Kankee* ("relation") standing alone can also mean "reason." Attached to a noun, *...kankee-no* means
"in the field of," often stretched loosely to cover anyone tangentially connected to a stylish-sounding
occupation (a hairdresser working with actresses describing herself via *eega-kankee*, "movie
business"). *Kankeesha* names someone in that category; *...kankee-no kata* is its polite form.

### Offering to do a favor: three registers (p.32-33)
*...te-agemashoo* ("shall I...for you") is appropriate toward friends/juniors but not toward someone
owed deference, where the honorific *o...(ita)shimashoo* pattern is used instead. A third option,
*...(s)asete-kudasai* ("please let me..."), sounds more eager and spans both polite and familiar
registers — made more polite with *...masen-ka* or by substituting *...te-itadakemasen-ka*, and
stripped of *-kudasai* entirely in familiar speech.

## Grammar points

### Rule 11 — Chinese people invented kanji; you can grasp the Chinese situation (中国人が漢字をつくりました／中国のようすがわかります)

Because kanji were invented in ancient China, examining a kanji's component pictures can reveal
concrete facts about old Chinese life and culture, not just abstract meaning-logic: 田 (paddy) shows
Chinese fields were square; 夕 (evening) shows the sun sets behind western mountains in China; 村
(village, from Rule 1) reflects a wood-and-hand-labor rural lifestyle; 赤/青 (from Rule 1) reflect
brick-red and jewel-blue as culturally salient colors. This rule gathers further such
culture-revealing kanji: family hierarchy (兄, elder brothers rank highest among boys), food culture
(豚/家, pigs raised under the house and eaten), values (幸, jewelry as "the happiest thing"; 友,
handshakes among friends), and feared events (災, flood and fire as "the worst things"). (pp. 58–62)

### Rule 12 — A far place in the upper portion; a near place in the lower portion (上は遠いところです／下は近いところです)

Because old Chinese paintings were made on tall, narrow paper/silk, a scene's near elements were
conventionally drawn low on the page and far elements high up (e.g. a landscape with nearby
foreground at the bottom, distant mountains at the top). Since the Chinese invented kanji, kanji
built from scenic pictures inherit this same top=far / bottom=near convention — demonstrated with
川 (near water flowing from far mountains at top to a near sea at bottom), 森 (the farthest of three
trees drawn at the top), 前 ("front" = meat/knife placed nearby, below, in front of people drawn
far/above), 売 (a far seller at top, a near buyer at bottom), and 禁 (a forbidden place drawn far/up,
beyond a near/low offering stand). (pp. 63–67)

### Rule 13 — Left is east; right is west (左は東のほうです／右は西のほうです)

Because old Chinese people conventionally sat facing south (the warm direction, versus the cold
north), their own left hand pointed east and their right hand pointed west — the reverse of the
Western map convention (north at top, south at bottom). Since kanji were invented in this
orientation, kanji built from directional/geographic pictures place east-associated elements on the
left and west-associated elements on the right (明: sun-left/east, moon-right/west; 朝: same
sun/moon placement for "morning"; 石: a stone below an eastern cliff, since mountains/cliffs were to
the west in China; 尼: a nun facing west, i.e. right, toward Buddhist Paradise; 十: a cross-stroke
reading as east–west horizontal + south–north vertical; 舟: a boat heading south, since southbound
travel was by boat and northbound by horse; 陣: a fort's embankment on the east/left, guarding
against eastern enemies, since the kanji-inventing country was itself in the west; 巨: a shield
facing east/left for the same reason). (pp. 68–72)

### Rule 14 — Symbols appear in kanji; do you understand what the symbols are? (記号が漢字になりました／その意味がわかりますか)

Some meanings cannot be expressed by any picture at all — the numbers 1/2/3 (expressed as simple
counted horizontal strokes, 一・二・三, not as pictures of "oneness" etc.) are the paradigm case. This
rule extends that principle: a recurring abstract "standard line" (基本線, a plain horizontal stroke)
serves as a reference point that other symbol-strokes are placed above or below to mean "up"/"top"
(上) or "down"/"bottom" (下); a single vertical stroke can symbolize an action like "pulling" (引,
alongside a bow) or elapsed duration (旧, "old," where the stroke means "extended long," explicitly
distinguished from the numeral 1); and strokes can symbolize division (八 "eight," two strokes
spreading apart) or enclosure (四 "four," 口 as a windowframe) or sequence/count (乙 "second," 九
"nine," both built from a bending-stroke sequence motif). (pp. 73–77)

### Rule 15 — That is this part; that is here (それはこの部分です／それはここにあります)

When a specific *part* of an otherwise-drawable object has no shape of its own that could be drawn
in isolation (a blade is part of a sword but can't be pictured alone; "inside" has no outline; the
base or the tip of a tree has no distinct shape; "here is"/"that is this part" as a location within
an already-pictured object), kanji solve this by drawing the whole recognizable object and adding a
symbol-stroke (typically ヽ, 一, or │) to point at/mark the specific part or location meant: 刃
(blade, marked on 刀 "sword"), 中 (inside, marked within 口 "circle"), 本 (base, marked at a tree's
roots), 末 (end, marked at a tree's topmost branches), 豆 (where beans sit, marked atop a stand), 血
(where blood sits, marked atop a plate), 天 (heaven, marked above a spread-armed person 大), 州 (sand
bars, marked — tripled, "three signifies many" — within a river 川). (pp. 78–82)

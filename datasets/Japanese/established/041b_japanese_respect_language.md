## Grammar points

### Honorific vs. depreciatory verbs — the "see-saw principle" (Ch. 3, pp. 51–52)

An honorific verb shows respect to its own grammatical subject by raising that subject's relative
status. If the person deserving respect is not the verb's subject — e.g. is the person addressed,
or otherwise "concerned in the action" without being its grammatical subject — a different device is
needed: lowering the status of the verb's actual subject instead, on a see-saw principle (raising one
side necessarily lowers the other relative to it). Verbs built this way are depreciatory verbs
(covered in Chapters 5–6). The two systems are therefore not interchangeable alternatives for the
same situation — each targets a different grammatical role.

### The standard honorific construction: お o + base + になる ni naru (Ch. 3, pp. 52–53)

Most honorific verbs are formed regularly from a neutral verb by prefixing お o- to the verb's stem
(base/continuative form) and following it with になる ni naru (or なさる nasaru, particularly in
imperative expressions), e.g. 休む yasumu "rest" → お休みになる o-yasumi ni naru. Two categories
fall outside this regular construction: (i) 〜する suru-compound verbs of the 連絡する
renraku-suru/研究する kenkyū-suru type, which instead insert なさる nasaru directly after the
Sino-Japanese noun element with ご go- (ご連絡なさる go-renraku nasaru, ご研究なさる go-kenkyū
nasaru); (ii) twelve verbs (listed in the Vocabulary table above) that take unpredictable/suppletive
honorific forms instead of the regular construction, tabulated in the source (p. 54) and again in
Appendix 3.

### -masu endings show respect to the addressee, independently of honorific-verb choice (Ch. 4, pp. 68–69)

A key orthogonality the source stresses explicitly: honorific *verbs* show respect to their
grammatical subject, while ます masu-level endings show respect to the 2nd person (the addressee)
and serve *no other purpose*. These are independent axes — if the person meriting verb-level respect
(the subject) is not the same as the addressee, -masu endings are only added if the addressee
*separately* also merits respect. This means a sentence can validly combine a full honorific verb
form with plain-style (non-masu) endings when the subject deserves respect but the addressee does
not, producing e.g. 先生は、まだお見えにならないね Sensei wa mada o-mie ni naranai ne (honorific
verb, plain ending) as a legitimate, attested register combination rather than an error.

### Honorific-without-masu as a women's-register familiar-respect blend (Ch. 4, p. 70)

A distinct attested register pattern: using a full honorific verb form but *without* the
complementary masu ending produces a blend of respect (from the honorific) with friendly familiarity
(from the plain verb ending). The source notes this usage is "found much more widely among women
than men" — an explicit gender-register split with a named mechanism (combining two normally
co-varying politeness markers in a mismatched way to produce a third, intermediate social meaning),
relevant to this project's broader register/slang-mechanics interest since it's effectively a
controlled "inconsistency" that itself carries social information rather than being a simple error.

### The お+base+する suru informal-honorific construction (Ch. 4, pp. 70–71)

A further reduction in formality: replacing になる ni naru with neutral する suru in the お+base+
construction (お+base+する) produces "a kind of informal honorific" — respect-flavored but casual,
exemplified by a mother speaking to her small child (お座りして（お）飲みなさい o-suwari shite
(o-)nomi nasai, "sit down and drink", instead of the fuller お座りになってお飲みなさい). The source
flags this usage as one learners will "encounter sooner or later" but explicitly does NOT recommend
for intermediate learners' own active use, because it is easily confused with the formally identical
*depreciatory* お+base+する pattern covered in Chapter 5 — the same surface construction encodes two
opposite social meanings (honorific-casual vs. depreciatory-humble) depending on context, a notable
potential ambiguity/false-friend point for mechanics analysis.

### The passive form used as an honorific, and its ambiguity with "indirect passive" (Ch. 3, pp. 64–65)

The (ra)reru passive form can function as a standard honorific, replacing an active verb with no
change of particles — e.g. 頼まれました tanomaremashita can honorifically mean "[you, sir] asked",
not just literally "[I] was asked." This usage requires care because it is identical in form to the
**indirect passive**, in which the agent marked by に ni is understood to have caused trouble or
inconvenience to the subject (e.g. 友達に頼まれました "I was asked by my friend" implying "I was
[inconvenienced by being] asked"; 雨に降られました "I was rained on" = inconvenienced by rain). The
source advises learners not to default to the passive-as-honorific over other honorific forms,
citing three reasons: not all verbs are commonly used this way; the construction is ambiguous with
the potential form (same surface shape for some verb classes); and it's ambiguous with the indirect
passive reading above.

### 召す mesu / お召しになる o-meshi ni naru — a single honorific verb covering multiple unrelated neutral verbs (Ch. 4, pp. 72–73)

召す mesu and お召しになる o-meshi ni naru are unusually broad: despite being extremely (highly)
honorific in register, they serve as alternative honorifics for several semantically unrelated
neutral verbs in different fixed expressions — most commonly as the sole honorific for 着る kiru
"wear", but also attested for 呼ぶ yobu "call", for 気に入る ki ni iru "be pleasing to" (as お気に召
す o-ki ni mesu), and in set phrases like お風邪を召す o-kaze o mesu "catch a cold [honorific]" and
お召し列車 o-meshi ressha "the emperor's train". The source frames their common semantic thread as
something like "avail oneself of, take to oneself" — a single lexical item's honorific range
fanning out by this loose semantic link rather than by the verb's literal meaning, which is a
notable lexicalization pattern for a synthesized-slang mechanics analysis (an honorific "catch-all"
verb absorbing multiple semantically-adjacent neutral verbs).

### Register inconsistency is attested, not necessarily an error (Ch. 3, p. 61 practice-answer note)

An explicit aside: a cited example sentence (from "a modern novel") mixes 見られる mirareru
(neutral-level passive) with お出になる o-de ni naru (honorific) in the same sentence. The source
notes "such inconsistency of levels of politeness is by no means uncommon" in authentic usage, even
though a fully consistent register would use honorific forms throughout (お出になると、ご覧になれる
でしょう). This is a directly useful finding for this project: real Japanese register-mixing is
attested and common, not merely a non-native-speaker error — relevant context for any later
mechanics analysis of how slang/informal forms might similarly blend with elevated vocabulary.

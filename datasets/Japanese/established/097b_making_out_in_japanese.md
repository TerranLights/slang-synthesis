**Source:** Todd & Erika Geers, *Making Out in Japanese* (Revised Edition), Tuttle Publishing.

**Coverage note:** This PDF has no text layer at all (confirmed via `pdftotext`/`pdfimages`: a
full-page-JPEG scan). Every entry below was vision-read from rendered page-spread images
(each image = two printed pages side by side). Printed page numbers were read directly from
page headers/footers, not assumed from a fixed offset; empirically, image `p-NN.png` ≈ printed
pages `2N-6` and `2N-5` in this range (verified against visible headers on every spread).
This is a situational phrasebook organized by chapter/topic, not an alphabetical dictionary —
every distinct vocabulary item, phrase, slang term, and register/gender annotation in the
assigned range is captured, not a sample. The book marks male-only (♂) and female-only (♀)
phrasings explicitly; these are captured in the Notes column per entry. **No handwritten
marginalia was found on any page in this range** — all content is clean, consistent typeset
print; this is a clean library-sourced scan, checked per the vision-reading guard.

Per copyright discipline: explanatory prose has been paraphrased; Japanese terms and their
English glosses are reproduced (as the actual lexical data this project collects), but
author commentary is summarized rather than quoted at length.

## Grammar points

### Accusative vs. uncertain "didn't you say" constructions (p.43)
*Sō itte-takke?* carries genuine "I don't remember" uncertainty and can be used reflexively about
oneself ("Oh, did I say that?"). *Sō itta-yone?* sits similarly uncertain. *Sō itetta-deshō?*/*Sō
itteta-darō?* (female/male forms respectively) are, per the author, "outright accusative" and
should be used with caution — pronunciation/tone is what distinguishes the registers, not the
words alone.

### Scope distinction in denial: "I didn't say anything" (p.43)
*Nani-mo ittenai-yo* is a general-purpose denial (e.g. responding to "did you spread that
rumor?"). *Nani-mo iwana-katta* is narrower — used about a specific subject or communication to
one particular person (e.g. "did you tell her my secret?").

### Gender-marked imperative/assertive registers (recurring across this range)
Throughout this chunk the book marks numerous phrase-pairs as ♂ (male-only) or ♀ (female-only)
variants of the same meaning — e.g. "stop complaining," "don't say that," "you went, didn't you?"
These are captured per-row above in the Notes column rather than summarized here, since each pair
differs lexically, not just by a single productive suffix.

### gohan vs. shokuji register (p.54)
*Gohan* (lit. "rice") is the more informal, everyday word for "a meal," while *shokuji* (lit.
"meal") is used in comparatively more formal contexts — both can mean "dinner/a meal" generically.

### oishii as an all-purpose evaluative word (p.57)
*Oishii* ("tastes good") is described by the author as semantically bleached through overuse —
intonation/enthusiasm carries the actual evaluation, so a flat or unenthusiastic *oishii* can
signal the food is in fact not very good. *Mā-mā* ("so-so") and *Yokunai* ("not good") are
offered as more honest but still face-saving alternatives, with the caution that direct criticism
of home-cooked food (as opposed to restaurant food) risks real offense.

### Object-wanting vs. action-wanting constructions (p.61)
The book teaches a systematic (if not absolute) grammatical split: wanting an object (noun) takes
`[noun]-ga hoshii`, while wanting to perform an action (verb) takes the verb stem + `-tai`
(negative: `-takunai`). Object-negation instead swaps the final `-i` of `hoshii` to `-kunai`
(`hoshikunai`), or more commonly/less politely just uses `iranai` ("don't want/need"). The
author flags this split as a simplification — `shitehoshii` ("I want you to do...") blends both
patterns, and usage varies by speaker/region.

### Intonation reversing meaning: *ii* and *Baka-janai?* (pp.60–61, 73)
Two separate entries make the same structural point: a single lexical string can flip polarity
purely on prosody. *Ii* means either "I want it" or "I don't want it" depending on falling+negative
gesture (no) vs. enthusiastic+positive gesture (yes) delivery. *Baka-janai?* means "Are you
stupid?" with rising (rhetorical-question) intonation, but "I'm/You're no fool!" with flat or
falling intonation — the author calls this "a superb example of the importance of pronunciation."

### Gender-marked (♂/♀) phrase pairs as a structural feature (recurring, pp.58–85)
This chunk is dense with explicit ♂/♀-marked phrase-pairs for near-identical meanings (e.g. "give
it back," "leave me alone," "get out of here," "don't look at me") — a core structural feature of
this phrasebook's presentation rather than an occasional annotation. These are captured per-row in
the vocabulary table's Notes column; the book does not claim these pairs are the *only* correct
gendered forms, just common real-world usage patterns by speaker gender.

### Register escalation ladders for the same speech act (recurring, pp.72–85)
Several speech acts (e.g. "get out of here," "you're noisy," "stop acting stupid," "don't stare at
me") are given as graduated series of near-synonymous phrases ranging from colloquial to outright
taboo/vulgar, with the author explicitly commenting on relative harshness (e.g. *kechi* vs. the
intensified *do-kechi*; *Gambatte* vs. the stronger *Gambare*; "less direct insults can have more
effect" re: *Namen-ja nēyo!*). This register-ladder structure recurs often enough in this source to
flag as a general pattern, not a one-off.

### *-jan* as a youth-marked confirmation suffix (p.73)
*-jan* (as in *Baka-jan!*) is glossed by the author as functionally equivalent to
*-deshō?/-darō?/-yonē?* (seeking the listener's confirmation), but sociolinguistically restricted:
the author states only speakers under about 35 use it, which is a genuine age-cohort register
annotation worth preserving as high-value sociolinguistic content per the extraction spec.

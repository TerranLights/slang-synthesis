## Grammar points

### §10.1 Cardinal numbers (a dual numeral system)

Japanese maintains two parallel 1–10 counting systems side by side: a **Sino-Japanese** series (`ichi`, `ni`, `san`...) that continues productively to arbitrarily large numbers, and a **native-Japanese** series (`hitotsu`, `futatsu`, `mittsu`...) that stops at 10 (`tō`) and is not extended further (p. 191). Compound numbers above 10 are built by straightforward concatenation on the Sino-Japanese series: 11–19 = `jū` ("10") + the 1–9 digit (`jūichi`, `jūni`...); tens are `[digit]jū` (`nijū` "20," `sanjū` "30"...); hundreds and thousands follow the same multiplicative logic but with productive sound changes at certain boundaries (e.g. `hyaku` "100" → `sanbyaku` "300," `roppyaku` "600," `happyaku` "800"; `sen` "1,000" → `sanzen` "3,000," `hassen` "8,000") (p. 191–192). Crucially, Japanese groups large numbers by powers of **10,000** (`man`), not 1,000 as in English — 100,000 is literally "10 × 10,000" (`jūman`) and 1,000,000 is "100 × 10,000" (`hyakuman`); the series continues to `oku` ("100,000,000") (p. 193). This base-10,000 grouping is a genuine typological difference from English numeral structure, worth flagging for any later mechanics/translation-pattern analysis — Japanese speakers mentally group large numbers in 4-digit chunks, not 3-digit chunks.

### §10.2 Ordinal numbers

Formed uniformly by adding `banme` to the Sino-Japanese cardinal number (`ichibanme` "first," `nibanme` "second," continuing regularly for arbitrarily large ordinals) (p. 193) — unlike English, which has irregular ordinal forms for several small numbers (first/second/third), Japanese ordinal formation is fully regular even at the low end.

### §11 Telling time

Hours use the Sino-Japanese numeral + `-ji`, with two irregular readings: 4 o'clock is `yoji` (not `*yonji`) and 9 o'clock is `kuji` (not `*kyūji`) (p. 194). Minutes use numeral + `-fun`/`-pun`, with a productive sound-alternation pattern keyed to the preceding numeral's final sound (full table given, p. 194–195): 1, 3, 4, 5, 7, 9 minutes take `-fun` (`ippun` is irregular — gemination despite being "1"); 2 minutes `nifun`; 6, 8, 10 minutes take `-pun` with gemination (`roppun`, `happun`, `juppun`). A full sentence is built hour-then-minutes-then-`desu` (p. 195). Three optional modifiers refine the basic time statement: `sugi` ("past/after," p. 195) for minutes past the hour; `mae` ("before/to," p. 196) for minutes before the next hour, used once past the half-hour mark; and `han` ("half," p. 195) for the half-hour point specifically. `Gozen`/`gogo` (A.M./P.M.) are said *before* the hour, the reverse of English's postposed "a.m./p.m." (p. 196). A separate note: transportation timetables (trains, flights) use the plain 1–59 minute count with no `sugi`/`mae`/quarter-hour idiom, on a 24-hour clock — a register/domain-specific convention distinct from conversational time-telling (p. 197).

### §12 Classifiers (counters) — a genuinely important and productive subsystem

Japanese requires a category-specific classifier (counter) suffix attached to a number before it can quantify a noun — bare numerals cannot directly modify nouns the way they do in English (p. 198). The source tabulates seven classifier categories, each its own full 1–10 paradigm (vocabulary table above gives one representative form per category; full paradigms are in the source at pp. 198–199): people (`-nin`, with irregular `hitori`/`futari` for 1–2), long thin objects (`-hon`), bound objects like books (`-satsu`), thin flat objects (`-mai` — notably the one category here with **no** sound alternation across all ten numbers), liquid/dry measures (`-hai`), buildings (`-ken`), floors (`-kai`), animals/fish/insects (`-hiki`), and a catch-all "small objects" category (`-ko`) that uniquely offers a choice between the Sino-Japanese-numeral+`-ko` series and the native-Japanese bare series as interchangeable alternatives. Most of these classifiers display a recurring **three-way consonant alternation** conditioned by the preceding numeral — e.g. `-hon` surfaces as `ippon` (1, geminated+devoiced), `nihon` (2, plain), `sanbon` (3, voiced), continuing through the paradigm with the same three allomorphs recurring at predictable positions — the same general alternation pattern (plain/geminated-voiceless/voiced) recurs with `-hai` and `-hiki`, while `-ken`/`-kai` show a narrower gemination-only alternation (at 1, 6, 10) and `-satsu` alternates more narrowly still. This productive counter-suffix morphophonology is one of the most systematically rule-governed (and learner-notorious) subsystems in Japanese grammar, and is likely to be directly relevant to later slang/mechanics analysis of playful or non-standard counter coinages. Finally, the **question-word rule** for classifiers is fully general and worth flagging as a clean productive rule: take any classifier's "three" form and replace its `san-` numeral stem with `nan-` to get "how many ___?" (`sangen` → `nangen`, `sanbai` → `nanbai`) (p. 200).

# Japanese — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: A Practical Guide to Mimetic Expressions through
Pictures, Chapter I "Expressing People's Feelings and Conditions"

**Source:** Akutsu Satoru (阿久津智), *絵でわかる ぎおんご・ぎたいご* / *A Practical Guide to Mimetic
Expressions through Pictures* (ALC Press, first published 1994-05-27; this copy is the 11th
printing, 2008-04-10; ISBN 978-4-87236-322-9), pp. 12-44 (Chapter I of III). Bilingual
Japanese/English textbook originally serialized in *Nihongo Journal* magazine (1990-1992),
dedicated entirely to Japanese mimetic vocabulary (giongo/sound-mimetic + gitaigo/state-mimetic
words), presented in illustrated lesson groups of 4-5 near-synonyms each.

**Coverage note:** every mimetic-expression entry in Chapter I's 8 numbered lessons plus its
"More Expressions ①" supplementary section (Pain vocabulary) — 42 distinct terms total. Each
entry in the source gives: the term (hiragana, occasionally katakana), a part-of-speech/
grammatical-form tag (adverb, noun, "~suru," "~da," "~na," "~no," pitch-accent notes in the book's
Roundup), a Japanese-language definition, an English gloss, and two bilingual example sentences.
Per copyright discipline, example sentences are paraphrased/described rather than reproduced; the
book's own illustrative cartoon captions are not quoted. Drill/review-exercise pages (pp. 19, 27,
35, 43-44 inter alia) are skipped per the coverage rule — they introduce no new vocabulary, only
recombine already-captured terms into fill-in-the-blank questions.

**This source has no usable text layer** (`pdftotext` returns almost no characters despite
`pdfinfo` reporting a real text layer) and was vision-read from two-printed-pages-per-scanned-image
spreads. No handwritten marginalia was found anywhere in this range — all content is clean
typeset print. **Vision Reading Confidence: verified** for every entry (each term's kana is legible
at full size in its own section header, independently confirmed against its restatement in the
body-text definition).

**Script note:** per this project's Japanese checklist convention (script choice carries register
information), this entire book's lesson vocabulary is written in hiragana rather than katakana,
even though several of these terms are technically giongo (sound-mimetic) rather than gitaigo
(state-mimetic) words — contrast with the katakana convention used for the "Animal Sounds"/"Object
Sounds" supplementary list in Chapter II/III's "More Expressions" sections (see
`090d_mimetic_expressions_ch2_actions.md`), which the book itself flags as the more typical
katakana-for-sound-effects convention. This is a real, source-documented register/script
distinction worth noting for later mechanics analysis.

## Grammar points

### Mimetic-expression entry structure

Every entry in this book follows a fixed four-part structure: (1) the term itself (romaji + kana,
occasionally with a noted variant form), (2) a grammatical-form tag specifying which
grammaticalized pattern(s) it combines with (`~suru`, `~shite-iru`, `~da`, `~no`, `~ni`, or bare
adverbial `~to`), (3) a Japanese-language definition, (4) an English gloss, followed by two
bilingual example sentences. The grammatical-form tag is itself informative: Chapter I's own
section header (p.12) notes that most of its feelings/conditions vocabulary is "often used with
the forms *-suru* and *-shite-iru*" — i.e. grammaticalized as a verbalizing light-verb
construction rather than as a bare adjective — a real structural pattern distinguishing
feeling-states (verb-like, often ongoing/durative via *-shite-iru*) from the condition/texture
vocabulary catalogued in Chapter III (`da`/`no`-adjective-like, stative). See the book's own
"Roundup" (まとめ) section, captured in full in `090e_mimetic_expressions_ch3_things_roundup.md`,
for the complete cross-chapter typology of which grammatical forms correlate with which semantic
domain.

### Reduplication as the default mimetic-expression shape

All 38 of this chapter's core-lesson terms (and 3 of 4 "More Expressions ①" pain terms) follow the
canonical CVCV-CVCV reduplicated shape (e.g. *iraira*, *dokidoki*, *magomago*) — the single most
common template for Japanese mimetic words. The chapter's handful of exceptions (*hotto*, *hatto*,
*gyotto* — a CV-tto/gyotto shape rather than reduplication) anticipate the book's own later formal
typology (Roundup Type III, `~tto`/`~nto`), confirming that non-reduplicated mimetic words are a
real, distinct but minority structural class rather than irregular outliers.

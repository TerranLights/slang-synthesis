This chapter departs from the compound-root organizing principle of Chapters 1–4 (no single
Sino-Japanese root like 論/理/説 anchors it). Instead it is organized as a discursive tour of
Japanese academic/scientific terminology — university department names, then long enumerated
technical-vocabulary lists (arithmetic, physics/mechanics, astronomy, chemistry/elements, biology/
taxonomy, botany, zoology) interspersed with ~35 numbered illustrative example sentences. Because
the chapter's core content *is* itself a series of vocabulary lists (not prose discussion with
occasional examples), coverage here captures essentially every distinct term in those lists plus
every distinct term introduced in prose or example-sentence glosses, while skipping duplicate
restatements of a term already captured and the numbered Japanese example sentences themselves
(only short illustrative phrases are quoted, consistent with copyright discipline). This produces a
substantially longer vocabulary table than prior chapters — a function of the chapter's own
enumerative structure, not over-extraction.

## Grammar points

### Enumerative-list structure as this chapter's organizing principle

Unlike Chapters 1–4, which build outward from a small set of productive bound roots (論/理/説,
etc.), Chapter 5 is organized as a series of topically-grouped enumerated vocabulary lists —
university departments, arithmetic operations, physics quantities and forces, the seven-rank
biological taxonomy (界・門・綱・目・科・属・種), planet names, chemical elements, and
animal/plant phyla — with prose serving mainly to introduce each list and flag etymological or
register points of interest, plus ~35 numbered example sentences demonstrating selected terms in
context (pp. 73–87).

### The -gaku (学) "field of study" formant as a universal academic-discipline suffix

Nearly every discipline name in this chapter is formed by suffixing 学 (-gaku, "study, field") to a
content root: 数学 (sūgaku, "mathematics"), 物理学 (butsuri-gaku, "physics"), 生物学
(seibutsu-gaku, "biology"), 地質学 (chishitsu-gaku, "geology"), and so on, with finer subfields
formed by inserting a qualifying compound before -gaku (理論物理学 "theoretical physics," 無機化学
"inorganic chemistry"). The same -gaku also attaches to department (-ka/-gakka) and person
(-gakusha, "-ist/-ologist," e.g. 微生物学者 "microbiologist," 鉱物学者 "mineralogist") formants,
giving a fully productive three-way discipline/department/practitioner compounding pattern
(pp. 74–75, 77–78).

### The -so (素) "elemental stuff" formant for chemical element names

De Wolf explicitly glosses the recurring element-naming pattern X-so (素, lit. "element, stuff"):
水素 suiso "hydrogen" (water-stuff), 酸素 sanso "oxygen" (sour-stuff), 窒素 chisso "nitrogen"
(suffocating-stuff), 炭素 tanso "carbon" (coal-stuff), 臭素 shūso "bromide" (stink-stuff), 硼素
hōso "boron," 沃素 yōso "iodine," 珪素 keiso "silicon." This contrasts with metals and several
other elements, which are rendered as katakana phonetic loanwords (ナトリウム natoryūmu "sodium,"
カルシウム karushūmu "calcium") rather than native/Sino-Japanese coinages — the author notes native
coinage is reserved mainly for elements with ordinary, pre-modern referents or those isolated early
in the Western chemical tradition that Meiji scholars translated systematically, while later-named
or less everyday elements default to loanword transliteration (pp. 83–84).

### The 属/族 (zoku/zoku) and 科/界/門/綱/目/種/類 taxonomic-rank system, and its homophone trap

The chapter tabulates the standard seven-rank biological classification system (kingdom through
species: 界・門・綱・目・科・属・種) plus the cover term 類 (rui, "class/order-level grouping," cf.
種類 shurui "kind, variety"). De Wolf flags an explicit homophone trap: 属 zoku ("genus," a
taxonomic rank) and 族 zoku ("tribe, group," used for non-taxonomic groupings like 白金族 "platinum
group") are pronounced identically but graphically and semantically distinct — a point the author
raises as a concrete illustration of how Sino-Japanese homophony can mislead even where the kanji
themselves are available in writing (p.79). The author further notes that Japanese taxonomic
practice at the family/genus level tends to favor ordinary native words written in katakana (ネコ科
neko-ka "Felidae," イヌ科 inu-ka "Canidae") over Latin-derived forms, unlike the English-language
scientific convention (p.79).

### Register and homophone notes scattered through the technical vocabulary

Several entries carry explicit register or disambiguation flags from the author rather than being
presented as flat technical terms: 単細胞 tansaibō ("single cell") doubles as a colloquial insult
for a narrow-minded/one-track-minded person (p.77); 細菌 saikin "bacteria" has a more colloquial
synonym ばい菌 baikin (p.85); 錫 suzu "tin" is flagged against confusion with the homophonous 鈴
suzu "bell" (p.84); and 彗星 suisei "comet" is flagged against confusion with the homophonous 水星
suisei "Mercury" (p.80) — paralleling the 属/族 zoku/zoku trap above as a recurring theme of this
chapter's register and disambiguation notes.

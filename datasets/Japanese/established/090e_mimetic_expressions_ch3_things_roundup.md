## Grammar points

### Prosodic/accentual typology of mimetic expressions (Roundup §1, pp.114-115)

The book's own closing "Roundup" (まとめ) section gives a systematic three-way formal/prosodic
classification of every mimetic expression in the book, independent of meaning — a genuine
morphophonological typology worth recording in full:

- **Type I** `○●○●` / `○●△●` (e.g. *guuguu*, *perapera* / *atafuta*, *dekoboko*): when used in the
  `~(to)+verb` or `~suru` forms, accented on the first syllable (*atamadaka* accent); when used
  with `~da`, `~no`, or `~ni`, pronounced with flat (*heiban*) accent.
- **Type II** `○●り(と)` (e.g. *gussuri*, *sappari* / *unzari*, *bonyari* / *zurari(to)*/*zuratto*):
  usually accented in the middle of the word (*nakadaka*), sometimes at the end (*odaka*).
- **Type III** `○っと`/`○んと` (e.g. *jitto*, *hotto* / *sassato*, *sesseto* / *chanto*): usually
  pronounced flat, but some words of this shape (*satto*, *hatto*, *unto*) are accented on the
  first syllable instead.

This accent-type/grammatical-form interaction (the *same* word's pitch accent shifts depending on
which grammatical construction it's used in) is a genuine prosodic-morphology finding distinct from
segmental morphology, and is worth flagging for any later phonaesthetic/sound-symbolism mechanics
analysis alongside the voiced/unvoiced consonant contrast noted below.

### Grammatical form correlates with semantic domain (Roundup §2, p.116)

The book's own summary ties grammatical-form preference to semantic category across the whole
book, confirming the pattern already noted per-chapter in `090c` and `090d`: (1) one's own feelings
→ typically `-suru`/`-shita`; (2) other people's facial expressions/appearance → typically
`-shite-iru`; (3) people's/things' temperament or qualities (incl. taste) → typically
`-shite-iru`/`-shita (hito/mono)`; (4) stickiness, dampness, texture → typically
`-suru`/`-shite-iru`/`-shita (mono)`; (5) bad states, disorder, texture, and degrees of margin →
typically `-da`/`-no (mono)`. This is a systematic, source-documented grammatical-form-tracks-
semantic-domain correlation across the entire mimetic-expression word class, not just an
observation about individual lessons.

### Voiced-vs-unvoiced consonant sound symbolism (one-point lesson, p.?? referenced via
korokoro/gorogoro contrast)

Documented directly in this source (Chapter III's rolling/sound lesson materials, captured in the
vocabulary table of this same chapter's lesson content above): the unvoiced-initial form *korokoro*
denotes something small, light, or "cute" rolling, while its voiced-initial counterpart *gorogoro*
denotes something big, heavy, or "not cute" rolling (or, by extension, a loud rumbling/thunder
sound) — a genuine phonaesthetic/sound-symbolism principle where the voiced/unvoiced distinction
in a mimetic word's initial consonant systematically tracks a magnitude or "heaviness" contrast.
The book documents further minimal pairs of the same type elsewhere (*korokoro*/*gorogoro* again
listed among the Mini Info Corner's verb-derivation examples above, both deriving verb forms).
This voiced/unvoiced-tracks-magnitude principle is one of the most cross-linguistically notable
sound-symbolism findings in this entire source and should be weighted heavily in any later
mechanics-analysis pass on Japanese phonaesthetics — it offers a ready-made, independently
attested mechanism for inventing new mimetic-feeling slang coinages (voice the onset consonant to
intensify/enlarge a sense, devoice it to diminish/cutesy-fy it).

### Near-synonym doublets differing only in voicing (one-point lesson, p.89)

A companion data point to the above: the book's p.89 one-point lesson ("Gakkari and Gakkuri")
lists five pairs of near-synonymous mimetic expressions that differ by a single voicing/consonant
alternation, all sharing essentially the same core meaning with a subtle intensity/nuance
difference: *gotagota*/*gochagocha* ("disordered"), *betabeta*/*betobeto* ("sticky"),
*mechakucha*/*mechamecha*/*muchakucha* ("absurd"), *gakkari*/*gakkuri* ("dejected"),
*gasshiri*/*gacchiri* ("solid"). Combined with the korokoro/gorogoro magnitude-tracking pattern
above, this confirms that systematic small phonological variation within a shared template
(reduplication shape held constant, one consonant or vowel varied) is a broadly productive
mechanism for generating a *family* of related mimetic words in Japanese — a mechanism any
derived-conlang slang system modeled on Japanese mimetic vocabulary could plausibly borrow
wholesale.

### Systematic derivational morphology: mimetic roots as a generative base for other word classes

The Mini Info Corner (full tables above) documents that mimetic roots are not a closed,
non-productive word class in Japanese — they are an active derivational base feeding at least
three other word classes via identifiable suffixes: **verbs** (five distinct patterns: root+
`-mu`/`-garu`, root+`-gu`/`-ku`, root+`-eru`(+transitive `-su`), root+`-meku`, root+`-tsuku`,
the last of which specifically encodes troubling/unpleasant/nauseated senses and often voices or
"p"-sounds its initial consonant); **na-adjectives** (root+`-ka`/`-yaka`/`-raka`); and **ordinary
adverbs** (root+reduplication+`-to`/`-ni`, drawing from *i*-adjective or verb roots rather than
mimetic roots in that direction). This is exactly the kind of agglutinative-adjacent, systematic
morpheme-by-morpheme compositional structure the project's Morphological Typology Guide flags as
needing explicit morpheme-breakdown treatment rather than atomic-vocabulary-item treatment — see
the dedicated derivation table above, formatted per that guide's convention. For a sci-fi
conlang-synthesis project specifically interested in *productive* slang-formation mechanisms, this
is arguably the single most directly reusable finding in either source covered by this dispatch:
Japanese mimetic vocabulary is a genuinely generative subsystem, not a fixed list, and its
derivational suffixes are few, regular, and well-documented.

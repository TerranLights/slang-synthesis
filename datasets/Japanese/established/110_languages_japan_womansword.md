**Morpheme breakdown (agglutinative/polysynthetic forms).** Classical Ainu is explicitly polysynthetic; several forms above are themselves composed units whose internal structure the source analyzes directly:

> **kuani** 'I' = *ku-* (1sg transitive subject affix) + *an* ('exist') + *-i* (nominalizing suffix) — p.31. Shibatani/Chiri's point: Ainu personal pronouns are historically *predicate nominalizations* of existential verbs, not primitive pronoun roots, which is part of why they rarely surface (the verb's own person-marking affixes already carry the information).

> **aoka** 'we' = *a-* (1pl inclusive transitive subject affix) + *oka* ('to exist', plural stem of *an*) — p.31.

> **a-mi-p** 'clothes' = *a-* (indefinite-person prefix, "we/one/generic") + *mi* ('wear') + *-p(e)* (nominalizer, "thing") — p.30. Literally "things we/one wear"; illustrates how the first-person-plural prefix *a-* grammaticalized into a marker of indefinite/generic reference, which the source notes is the historical route by which *a-* became a passive-forming prefix (§3.5.6).

> **ku-kor-kur** 'my husband' = *ku-* (1sg possessive affix) + *kor* ('have') + *-kur* (nominalizer, "person") — p.33. Literally "the person I have."

## Grammar points

### Ainu personal-affix system and the near-absence of overt pronouns (pp.30–31)

Since Ainu marks subject and object person directly on the verb via personal affixes, independent pronouns (Table 3.7: *kuani*, *eani*, *sinuma*, *aoka*, *ecioka*, *oka*, plus Classical-only *asinuma*/*esinuma*) normally do not surface at all; when they do appear, they add emphasis roughly equivalent to "as for me." Shibatani cites Chiri (1936) likening this to the optionality of overt subject pronouns in Latin and French. All pronoun forms are etymologically transparent as frozen existential-verb constructions (see morpheme breakdowns above), not primitive pronoun roots — directly relevant to how a synthesized conlang might derive pronoun systems from verbal morphology rather than treating them as a closed irreducible class.

### Ainu nominal possession and nominalization (pp.31–33)

Ainu nouns have no case inflection or grammatical gender, but do formally distinguish *generic* forms (e.g. *ona* 'a father') from *possessed* forms marked by possessive personal prefixes plus a variable possessive suffix (*ku-onaha* 'my father'). A family of derivational nominalizing suffixes — *-p(e)* 'thing/person characterized by X', *-i* 'place/time of X', *-ike* 'thing/person of X', and *-kur* 'person who X' (related to the free noun *kuru* 'person') — do double duty as ordinary lexical derivation *and* as fully productive clausal/relative-clause nominalizers, letting entire predications be packaged as a single nominal (e.g. *a-koyki rok-pe* 'the one I have fought', lit. "1sg-strike-PERF-NOMLZ"). This dual lexical/syntactic function is explicitly compared by the source to the Japanese nominalizer *-sa*.

### Ainu case-particle system and the applicative alternative (pp.34–39)

Subject and object are marked purely by word order, not case. Other grammatical relations are marked by postpositional particles (dative *orun*, locative/allative *ta*, allative *un*, ablative *wa*/*orwa*/*orowa*, instrumental *ari*, comitative *tura(no)*, conjunctive *newa*, translative *ne*, privative *sak*/*sakno*) — but Classical Ainu alternatively/additionally incorporates postpositional meaning directly into the verb via an "applicative" prefix system (Kindaichi 1931), which interacts with noun incorporation and is one source of Ainu's polysynthetic character. The more analytic particle system is comparatively more common in colloquial Ainu. There is no genitive case particle at all; possession of a person-denoting noun is instead expressed as a relative-clause-like construction built on the verb *kor* 'to have' (*ku-kor mat* 'my wife', lit. "the wife I have").

### Honorific plural-marking and the Ainu passive continuum (pp.51–58)

Like French *vous* or German *Sie*, Ainu uses plural marking (of both pronouns and, more unusually, of verb forms) as a second-person honorific defocusing strategy, though the source notes this is less pervasive than in many other languages and more common in Classical than colloquial Ainu. The same 1st-person-plural-inclusive affixes (*a-* transitive, *-an* intransitive) that mark honorific plurality also mark three distinct but related constructions: the indefinite-person construction (≈ English *one*, French *on*), the spontaneous/"middle passive" (*Cip a-nukar* 'A ship is visible/seen'), and the passive proper (*Umma kamuy orowa a-rayke* 'A horse was killed by the bear', with the agent in the oblique ablative role marked by *orowa*, strikingly parallel to German passive *von*). Shibatani treats this whole family as evidence for a *prototype* definition of "passive" rather than a single discrete construction — directly useful background for any synthesized-language voice/valency system that wants a principled continuum between passive, spontaneous, honorific, and indefinite-person marking rather than four unrelated categories.

### Standard language vs. common language (kyootuu-go) (pp.185–187)

Japan's "standard language" concept (*hyoozyun-go*) was never formally codified; it is only loosely anchored to educated middle-class Tokyo speech, itself only established as prestige-dialect after political power shifted from Kyoto to Edo/Tokyo around the end of the 18th century (traceable via how contemporary dialect glossaries identified the "standard" reference point). Pre-WWII education policy actively suppressed peripheral dialects (the *hoogen huda* "dialect tag" shaming practice). After WWII a softer concept, *kyootuu-go* "common language," replaced outright dialect suppression: a standard-influenced but not fully standardized lingua franca that still carries local accentual/other dialect traits, varying from region to region while remaining mutually intelligible.

### Major dialect divisions: Ryukyuan vs. mainland, and East vs. West (pp.187–189)

The two dialect divisions "of great significance" in Japanese dialectology are (1) Ryukyuan vs. mainland, and (2) within the mainland, Eastern Japan vs. Western Japan (with Kyushu usually grouped into the Western camp). Different dividing lines emerge depending on whether one classifies by holistic pattern, accent, or vocabulary alone — these layers don't perfectly coincide.

### Ryukyuan as a sister/dialect group, and its phonological archaism (pp.189–196)

Ryukyuan dialects are either analyzed as an independently-descended sister language of mainland Japanese (Chamberlain 1895, likening the Ryukyuan/Japanese split to Spanish/Italian) or — the now-dominant Japanese-dialectology view since Tōjō (1927) — as a peripheral dialect group of a shared proto-Japanese. Regular sound correspondences support a genetic relationship regardless of the labeling choice: standard mid vowels *e*, *o* systematically raise to *i*, *u* in the Shuri dialect (*ame* 'rain' → *ami*; *sode* 'sleeve' → *sudi*), while *u* fronts to [i] after *s*/*ts* (*tuki* 'moon' → *tfitfi*). Ryukyuan dialects split into three mutually unintelligible subgroups (Amami-Okinawa, Miyako-Yaeyama, Yonaguni) and, as the most peripheral dialect area, preserve the largest cluster of historically older forms: original *p* (where mainland shifted *p*→*ɸ*→*h* around the 8th century), the old *b*/*d* stops underlying standard *w*/*y*, and — syntactically — the Old Japanese conclusive/attributive verb-form distinction lost on the mainland by the late 16th century (Shuri *katfun* 'write' conclusive vs. *katfuru* attributive).

### East-West opposition: morpho-syntactic and lexical isogloss bundle (pp.196–200)

A coherent bundle of isoglosses — imperative forms, verb inflection patterns, adjective adverbial forms, negative endings, copula forms, and a dozen+ lexical pairs (*kemuri*/*kebu* 'smoke', *nasu*/*nasubi* 'eggplant', etc.) — divides Eastern from Western Japan along a line running roughly between Niigata/Toyama in the north and Shizuoka/Aichi in the south, correlating with non-linguistic socio-cultural divides (diet, tool shapes). Crucially, prestige borrowing runs asymmetrically: Tokyo speech (nominally "Eastern") absorbed a disproportionate number of Western/Kyoto-origin forms specifically in the honorific domain, because Eastern Japan's honorific system was historically less elaborated than Kyoto's imperial-court-centered one — a clean illustration of how register/politeness systems, not just plain vocabulary, can be borrowed wholesale across a dialect boundary.

### Central-peripheral opposition: historical residues vs. parallel innovations (pp.202–214)

Two distinct explanatory mechanisms are distinguished for why Japan's geographically peripheral dialect areas (Tōhoku, San-in, Hachijōjima, Kyushu, Ryukyu) often resemble each other despite being non-contiguous: (1) **historical residues** — both areas independently retained an older stage of the Kyoto standard that later changed only at the center and spread outward in "rings" (Yanagita's *hoogen syuuken ron*, explicitly compared to the European wave theory of dialectology, but with emphasis on circular center-outward spread); and (2) **parallel innovations** — independently-arisen changes that happen to converge, such as the "zu-zu ben" vowel-centralization shared by Tōhoku and San-in despite no plausible contact pathway between them. The accentual-system evidence (word-class mergers in Table 9.4/9.5, Kindaichi's three-ring model) is treated as the clearest case where center vs. periphery doesn't map straightforwardly onto conservative vs. innovative — the Kyoto-Osaka system itself may be the *innovating* one for at least the Class-4/Class-5 accent distinction (Tokugawa 1972), complicating the naive assumption that "peripheral = always older."

### Spoken vs. written language divergence (§11.6.1, pp.357–364)

Japanese writing and speech diverged early due to the *kanbun* (Sinico-Japanese) tradition — writing Japanese sentences in Chinese characters/syntax and reading the result "translated back," producing a hybrid neither fully Chinese nor fully Japanese, readable by neither Chinese nor Japanese readers without special training. Formality is the master variable distinguishing colloquial speech from writing in contemporary Japanese: sentence-final particles (*ne*, *yo*, *wa*, *sa*) are pervasive in speech and wholly absent from writing; ellipsis (pro-drop) is far more frequent in colloquial speech than in writing, and markedly more frequent in Japanese than in English, Chinese, or even Korean (a typologically similar pro-drop, honorific-marking, topic-prominent language). The honorific system itself is argued to be a *compensatory mechanism* making heavy pro-drop recoverable — the subject/object honorific endings tell the hearer whether an elided argument is the speaker or someone else, substituting for lost agreement morphology.

### Men's and women's speech (§11.6.2, pp.371–374)

The chapter identifies both lexical and grammatical gender-of-speaker distinctions in Japanese (distinct from the register material covered in more depth by Womansword): (1) a handful of interjections exclusive to women (*maa*, *ara*); (2) a pervasively gendered first-person pronoun paradigm (Table 11.1: men use *boku*/*ore* informally, women use *atasi*; both sexes converge on *watakusi*/*watasi* at the formal level — gender marking is specifically a feature of the *informal* register, and disappears entirely in polite/formal speech); (3) gendered sentence-final particles — rising-intonation *wa* is exclusively female (distinct from a falling-intonation, sex-neutral Osaka-dialect *wa* homograph), *ze*/*zo* are exclusively found in rough male speech, and the dubitative *kasira* and exclamatory nominalizer use of *no*/*koto* mark female speech; (4) a syntactic rule (copula-*da* deletion before final *yo*) that is grammatically restricted to female speakers only — *Kirei yo* (women) vs. *Kirei da yo* (men), even though both surface identically once the polite *-desu* ending is added, proving the underlying copula is still present for both sexes; (5) overall, women's speech is characterized by a documented higher baseline politeness level, partly realized through heavier/sometimes-criticized use of the beautification prefix *o-* (*o-biiru* 'beer', etc.) even on items that would not trigger grammatical honorification. The chapter explicitly frames the non-reciprocal structure of the Japanese pronoun/honorific system (an inferior cannot reciprocate a superior's informal register) as one of the drivers of the language's generally high rate of pronoun avoidance/ellipsis — directly relevant background for Womansword's parallel but more applied treatment of the same first-/second-person pronoun paradigm.

### Honorifics and speech levels (§11.6.3, pp.374–380)

Japanese honorification operates on two independent axes: the speaker-addressee axis (*teinei-go*, "polite language," realized by *-masu*/*desu*) and the speaker-referent axis, itself split into *sonkei-go* ("respect language"/subject honorifics, e.g. *o-...ni naru* or the suffix *-rare*) and *kenzyoo-go* ("humility language"/object honorifics, e.g. *o-...suru*). The two axes can combine or diverge independently (a student can use plain subject-honorific speech about a teacher while addressing a classmate informally). The source stages seven ascending speech-level examples (p.377–378, "I'll see him/that person") from vulgar (*ore...*, no honorifics) through maximally stylized polite-formal-object-honorific-with-honorified-referent — and notes that the gendered 1st-person pronouns *ore*/*boku* specifically disappear once the speech level rises past plain informal, i.e. **gender marking in Japanese pronouns is itself register-bound, confined to the lower/informal end of the formality scale** — a structural fact with direct implications for how a synthesized slang register should or shouldn't carry gender marking as formality increases. Most distinctively, Japanese honorific distance is *relativized* to an insider/outsider axis rather than fixed to absolute social rank (contrasted explicitly with Korean's more absolute system): the same referent (e.g. a company president) receives honorifics when colleagues speak to each other, but not when an employee speaks about the same president to an outside customer, because the president is reclassified as "insider" relative to the outside addressee.

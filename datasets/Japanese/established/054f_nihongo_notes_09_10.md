## Grammar points

### Sentence-ending finality as a conversational signal (p.100-101)
In personal conversation, ending every sentence with a fully finite form (*-mashita*, *-desu*)
inadvertently closes off the topic sentence by sentence, leaving the listener nothing to follow up on
— even though the same endings are entirely normal (indeed expected) in formal writing or reports.
Mid-topic, speakers instead trail off with *...te-ne*, *...kedo*, or *...kara* to signal there's more to
say and implicitly invite a follow-up question, reserving the finite forms for the point where the
topic is actually finished.

### ...ni naru vs. ...yoo-ni naru, and the inherently-change-verb exception (p.102-103)
*...ni naru* attaches directly to nouns/na-adjectives ("become X"); *...yoo-ni naru* attaches to verbs
(often potential forms) for "come to be able to/come to [verb]." Critically, *...yoo-ni naru* is never
added to a verb that already denotes change on its own (naoru "be cured," nareru "become accustomed,"
tsukareru "become tired," futoru "become fat") — doing so is simply wrong (*futoru yoo-ni naru* for
"become fat" fails). When *...yoo-ni naru* IS layered onto one of these change-verbs anyway, it doesn't
break the rule so much as shift the verb's reading from a one-off past event to a change in a
recurring/general condition.

### Feeling-predicates default to the speaker's own experience (p.104-105)
Adjectives/verbs describing feelings (ureshii, kanashii, tanoshii, tsukareru, komaru) conventionally
describe the *speaker's* emotional state by default, even with no explicit subject marking — a
systematic pattern easy for learners to misread as describing the grammatical subject's own state
instead. *Tsukareru* can even take an experiencer-flipped frame where the named person is the *cause*
of the speaker's fatigue, not the one who's tired. *Komaru* defaults similarly ("X is annoying to me"
rather than "X will be in trouble") unless an evidential marker like *...kamo shirenai* redirects it.
Negative derivatives built on this same logic (*yarikirenai*, *gaman-dekinai*) describe the speaker's
emotional limit about someone's behavior, and must not be confused with character judgments about that
person (*gaman-ga tarinai*, "lacks patience").

### Three uses of the particle o (p.106-107)
Beyond its well-known direct-object marking, *o* also marks (1) the space traversed during motion
(*michi-o aruku* "walk along the road" — contrasted with *michi-de aruku*, which would mean walking in
place at one spot on the road) and (2) a point of departure (*uchi-o deru* "leave home"). Object-marking
*o* is frequently dropped in casual speech; *de* is not dropped the same way.

### Thanking in advance doesn't translate (p.108-109)
Unlike English convention, thanking someone before a favor is actually performed is not natural in
Japanese and can read as presumptuous — as if the speaker already assumes the favor will be granted.
The idiomatic substitute in a request note/letter is a request-closing formula (*Onegai-shimasu*,
*Yoroshiku onegai-shimasu*) or an apology expression, not a thanks expression.

### Emotionally charged subject-marking: to kitara / ttara / tte (p.110-111)
Three ways to mark subject matter beyond plain *wa*, differing in emotional charge: *...to kitara*
(surprise/contempt/irritation/complaint, most critical), *...ttara* (the same family, slightly less
harsh), and *...tte* (close to emotionally neutral, usable specifically when asking for a concrete
piece of information — a use the other two cannot cover).

### De vs. wa with "sufficient/good enough" expressions (p.112-113)
*...de juubun*, *...de kekkoo*, *...de ii*, and near-synonyms (*ma-ni au*, *yareru*, *tsuujiru*) all
require *de*, not *wa*, even though the particle mistake is subtle and the meaning is often still
recoverable from context. With *kekkoo-desu* specifically, swapping in *wa* risks a real meaning
flip — from "this is good enough" to a polite refusal, "no thank you."

### Expressing purpose: tame-ni vs. masu-stem + ni (p.114-115)
Dictionary-form-verb + *tame-ni* works with any verb and slightly foregrounds the purpose; masu-stem +
*ni* is the more natural choice specifically with motion verbs (iku, kuru, dekakeru), and many such
combinations are themselves fixed, lexicalized expressions (hana-o mi-ni iku "go flower-viewing").

### ...deshitara vs. plain wa for subject matter (p.116-117)
*...deshitara* ("if it's X...") marks subject matter specifically when the speaker has inferred what
the listener is looking for or referring to — it cannot be used to simply open a conversation the way
plain *wa* can. The familiar-register equivalent is *...dattara*.

### Softened familiar requests with kana/kashira (p.118-119)
Adding the uncertainty particles *kana* (male) / *kashira* (female) to a familiar-register favor-request
(*...te-moraenai*) softens it into an indirect, reserved-sounding "I wonder if you could..." — though
the same indirection can be repurposed sarcastically by an irritated superior. Both particles are too
familiar for polite speech toward a senior, where *...deshoo-ka* is the appropriate substitute.

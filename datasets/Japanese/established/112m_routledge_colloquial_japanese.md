## Grammar points

### Verb grouping: Group 1, Group 2, Group 3
Japanese verbs sort into three conjugation classes, identifiable from the masu-form: **Group 1**
verbs end in an "i" sound before masu (iki-masu, yomi-masu, ai-masu), with a small set of
exceptions that look like Group 2 (oki-masu "get up", kari-masu "borrow", ori-masu "get off")
despite the surface "i"-sound pattern — these must be memorized individually. **Group 2** verbs
have either only one hiragana syllable before masu (mi-masu, i-masu, ne-masu) or end in an "e"
sound before masu (tabe-masu, age-masu, kake-masu). **Group 3** is a closed irregular class: just
kimasu ("come") and shimasu ("do", including all noun+shimasu compounds like benkyō shimasu). This
grouping governs te-form, ta-form (Unit 15), and dictionary-form (Unit 16) derivation throughout
the rest of the book. pp. 153-155.

### Te-form derivation
**Group 1**: the sound before masu changes per a fixed mapping — i/chi/ri → tte (aimasu→atte,
machimasu→matte, ikimasu→itte [irregular exception, not *ikite]); bi/mi/ni → nde (asobimasu→
asonde, nomimasu→nonde); ki/gi → ite/ide (kakimasu→kaite, isogimasu→isoide); shi → shite
(hanashimasu→hanashite). **Group 2 and 3**: simply delete masu and add te (tabemasu→tabete,
mimasu→mite, nemasu→nete, kimasu→kite, shimasu→shite). pp. 156-157.

### Polite requests: [V-te] + kudasai
Attaching kudasai to a verb's te-form makes a polite request ("Sono shashin o misete kudasai" —
"Please show me that photo"). Dropping kudasai makes the request casual, appropriate only among
close relations such as friends ("Sono shashin o misete" — "Show me that photo"). p. 157-158.

### Nominalizing "how to...": [V-stem] + kata
Deleting masu from a verb and adding kata forms a noun meaning "how to [verb]" (yomimasu→
yomikata "how to read", oboemasu→oboekata "how to remember"). The object of the underlying verb is
marked with no rather than o when it modifies this derived noun ("Kono kanji no yomikata o oshiete
kudasai" — "Please tell me how to read this kanji") — a case-marking shift triggered by
nominalization, worth flagging as a recurring pattern (parallel to Unit 9's tai-desu object-marking
flexibility) for the mechanics-analysis phase. p. 159.

### Asking, granting, and denying permission: te-form (3)
**Granting/asking**: [V-te]+mo ī desu(ka) ("may I...?" / "you may..."); dōzo ("please do") is an
even more encouraging affirmative response; a bare, trailing-off "Sumimasen ga chotto..." (echoing
Unit 9's refusal strategy) politely declines without a flat no. **Denying**: [V-te]+wa ikemasen is
a strong prohibition, explicitly flagged in-text as usage-restricted to contexts like stating laws
or official regulations rather than everyday mild discouragement. pp. 161-162.

### Kanji of Unit 13: day-name-building characters
日月火水木土今友少分 — notably, the first five (日月火水木土, "sun/moon/fire/water/tree/earth") are
the kanji that build the days-of-the-week names (already learned phonetically in Unit 4), now
given their written form; a worked example flags that 今日 ("today", kyō) is read irregularly,
using neither the kun- nor on-yomi of 日 in isolation — an explicit exception the book itself calls
out, worth tracking alongside Unit 11's "general rule with exceptions" caveat about kun-/on-yomi
assignment. p. 163.

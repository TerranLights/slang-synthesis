## Grammar points

### Honorific and humble forms — keigo (Unit 18)

Paraphrased: honorific (sonkeigo) forms elevate the actions of a socially senior/outgroup person;
humble (kenjōgo) forms lower the speaker's own actions when addressing such a person. This maps
onto the in/out-group distinction already used for ageru/kureru (Unit 16): humble forms for ingroup
actions, honorific forms for outgroup actions. A practical side effect the book highlights: because
humble-verb objects are typically the outgroup person (or something of theirs), the choice of verb
form itself often disambiguates an otherwise-unstated subject/object (p. 217). The book also flags
asymmetric real-world usage: an addressee of markedly different status may answer back in a
different (non-honorific) register entirely rather than mirroring the speaker's politeness level.

### More on informal Japanese — a full inventory (Unit 22, Structures 1)

This revision unit systematically inventories the ways formal Japanese is stripped down in
colloquial speech, paraphrased:
- **Particle omission**: o and wa (and occasionally ni/e) are dropped, especially in short
  sentences — o and wa are the most frequently omitted.
- **da/desu omission**: especially after na-adjectives and no, more common in women's speech.
- **Contraction**: -te oku/-te iku → -t' oku/-t' iku; -te iru → -te 'ru; -te ita → -te 'ta; verb
  -ru before no/n' assimilates to -n' (nan ni su' n' no?).
- **Men's/boys' -eba contraction**: -eba → -yā (noru→norya); -i-adjective -kereba → -kyā/-keryā.
- **Sentence-final particles** zo/ze/kai/dai carry distinct register/gender/intensity shadings (see
  vocabulary table).
- **Informal first/second-person pronouns**: boku/ore (men), kimi/omae (corresponding "you"
  terms), atashi (women), anata/anta (women's "you"); kotchi/sotchi as a unisex alternative pairing
  built on "here"/"there."
- **Informal yes/no**: un/ā and ūn/iya replacing hai/ē and iie.
- **Questions without ka?**, inversion (afterthought-final statements/questions), **-ttara/-tte(ba)**
  for reiterated requests, and **mitai (da)** as an informal yō da/sō da equivalent.
- **nante/nanka** marking disbelief or self-deprecating modesty.

### Language and society — Insults (Unit 22)

Paraphrased: the book makes an explicit comparative claim that Japanese, despite a rich slang
vocabulary generally, has a comparatively narrow dedicated insult lexicon next to neighboring
languages — baka ("stupid") does most of the work, including compounded as bakayarō. Where English
or other languages might reach for varied insulting nouns, Japanese productively substitutes a noun
into the kono-...! template ("kono + [noun]!") or appends a name/kinship term to baka/kechi via the
possessive no (paralleling the no-apposition pattern already seen with hisho no Kimura-san in Unit
11). The book also notes a cultural angle: in a society that places enormous weight on academic
performance, insulting someone's intelligence may land harder than a greater variety of "spicier"
insults would.

### Language and society — māmā (Unit 22)

Paraphrased: māmā! (often repeated) is the conventional third-party intervention phrase to defuse
an insult-triggered confrontation or calm someone who is overexcited/about to cry. A second,
unrelated use: urging a guest/friend not to leave or stop eating/drinking prematurely, functioning
close to an insistent "oh, go on, have some more."

### Language and society — informal speech and Tokyo dialect (Unit 22)

Paraphrased: Japan's dialect geography runs from Okinawa to Tōhoku, with Hokkaidō lacking its own
indigenous Japanese dialect (settled too recently, late 19th century, so it instead hosts a mix of
Tokyo- and Tōhoku-area speech; the book separately notes Hokkaidō's Ainu-speaking population as
linguistically unrelated to Japanese entirely). Major dialect groupings named: western Japan
(Kansai, including Osaka and Kyoto, framed as the old cultural center), eastern Japan (Kantō,
including Tokyo), Tōhoku, and Kyūshū. The Tokyo dialect (itself one Kantō variety) is distinguished
from Standard Japanese (hyōjun-go) by specific markers the book names explicitly: the vocabulary
item dekai ("big") and a systematic ai/ae → ē sound shift producing forms like itē (itai) and omē
(omae) — both explicitly tagged in-text as informal-Tokyo-speech markers rather than Standard
Japanese.

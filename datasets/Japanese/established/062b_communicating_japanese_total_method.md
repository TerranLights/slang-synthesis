## Grammar points

### Location element (de-marked) and locative pronouns (Lesson 11 §11.1)

Extends the earlier aru/iru ni-marked Location element (Lesson 7) to a second sense: the place
*where an action takes place*, which is marked with de rather than ni (Nihoñ de kámera o kaimásu
"(I) buy cameras in Japan"). A locative-pronoun series parallels the earlier deictic series: koko
"here," soko "there (near addressee)," asoko "there/yonder," doko "where?" — matching the
ko-/so-/a-/do- pattern already established for kore/sore/are/dore and kono/sono/ano/dono.

### Verbalizing Chinese-loan nouns with suru (Lesson 11 §11.2)

Because verbs are not inflected as a distinct word class in Chinese, Chinese-derived (Sino-Japanese)
loan nouns borrowed into Japanese needed a mechanism to function as verbs; the solution is suffixing
the light verb suru "does" directly onto the loan noun (keekaku "a plan" → keekaku-suru "plans";
deñwa "telephone" → deñwa-suru "telephones"). Since these compounds are nominal in origin, the noun
form remains independently usable as subject or object even once the suru-verb exists alongside it
(Kekkoñ wa ítsu desu ka? "When is your marriage?" alongside Ítsu kekkoñ o shimásu ka? "When are you
getting married?").

### Emphatic sentence-final particles yo and wa, with an explicit gender/register note (Lesson 11 §11.3)

Besides yo (emphasis, usable by any speaker), the particle wa is introduced as an alternate emphatic
sentence-final particle "conventionally interpreted as 'softer' and 'more feminine'" — the book's own
language for a gendered-register distinction in colloquial Japanese speech, directly relevant to this
project's register/sociolinguistic-variation interests.

### Direction element: ni/e/kara/made, free variation, verbs preferring ni, and focusing (Lesson 12 §12.1)

The Direction element can be marked multiple ways: ni/e ("to," generally free variants, though with a
soft, inconsistent tendency for ni to mark a narrowly-defined direction like a specific station and e
a loosely-defined one like a general area — the text notes "there are some inconsistencies"), kara
("from"), and made ("to/as far as"). A subclass of verbs (noru "rides/boards," oku "puts/places,"
suwaru "sits (on)") take ni even though they look like Location-element uses, because — per the text
— "the idea of Location and Direction seems to overlap." The Direction slot can be focused the same
way Location/Indirect Object are (adding wa after the element-marker particle): Giñza ní wa ikimaseñ
deshita "(I) didn't go to the Ginza" — when direction is focused via wa to contrast it with something
else, the direction-marker particle itself often receives an accent.

### Means element as instrument, and the presumptive/hortative verb inflection (Lesson 13 §13.1–13.2)

The Means element (marked by de) covers instrument ("with," e.g. Hashi de góhañ o tabemásu "(I) eat
rice with chopsticks"). The presumptive inflection (here restricted to its hortative "Let's do X!"
meaning) is formed with -oo on Consonant-verbs and -yoo on Vowel-verbs, conveniently matching the
-mas(u)/-mashoo pattern already learned for polite forms (Asobimashoo "Let's play!"; Tabemashoo
"Let's eat!"). A full indicative/past/negative/presumptive paradigm table is given for asobu "plays"
and taberu "eats."

### "Fly the sky": motion verbs taking o as location-of-action, not object (Lesson 13 §13.3)

A subclass of motion verbs (tobu "flies," aruku "walks," wataru "crosses over") takes the Noun + o
element not as a true object/goal of action but as the "location of action" — the space moved through
or over (Tori wa sora o tobimásu "Birds fly (in) the sky"). The book gives an explicit three-way
comparison of which location-marking particle (ni/de/o) goes with which broad verb class: ni with
existential ("being") verbs, de with action verbs, o with motion verbs of this subclass.

### Counters: dual numbering system formalized, construction, and sound assimilation (Lesson 14 §14.1)

Formalizes the distinction introduced piecemeal earlier: native-Japanese numerals (hitotsu...too,
stopping at ten) for general objects vs. the counter system (Chinese numeral + classifier suffix) for
specific semantic categories — furniture/toys/kitchen utensils typically take native numerals;
fruit/other items traditionally pair with specific classifiers; animate objects (people, animals)
*never* take the general numeral system and require their own classifier (different for people vs.
for four-legged animals). Counters function grammatically as nouns: they can appear inside a Noun +
no phrase (sañniñ no kodomo "three children") or stand independently as subject/object without an
element-marker particle (Sañniñ kimáshita "Three people came"). Five core classifiers are tabulated
in full 1–10 paradigms illustrating heavy sound-assimilation/allomorphy: -niñ (people), -mai (thin
flat objects), -dai (machines/vehicles), -hoñ/-poñ/-boñ (long cylindrical objects), -hai/-pai/-bai
(liquid/dry measure) — the text explicitly uses this classifier set "to illustrate the complexity of
sound assimilation in... numeral-suffix combinations."

### Counting time and money; irregular day-counting forms (Lesson 15 §15.1)

A further, distinct set of counter suffixes for duration specifically: -fuñ(kañ) "minutes," -jikañ
"hours," -nichi(kañ) "days," -shūkañ "weeks," -kagetsu(kañ) "months," -neñkañ "years" — with -kañ
"interval" marked as optional for some of these. The suffix for *naming* a calendar month (-gatsu:
ichigatsu "January") is explicitly distinguished from the suffix for *counting* a span of months
(-kagetsu: ikkagetsu "one month"). Day-counting (both for day-of-month and duration-in-days) uses a
set of forms with its own internal irregularity, distinct from the regular Sino-Japanese count: ichi-
nichi, futsuka, mikka, yokka, itsuka, muika, nanoka, youka (1–8), continuing the pattern already
flagged as exceptional in Lesson 10 (hatsuka "20th," juuyokka "14th").

### The -te form (gerund): formation and sound assimilation; negative -te form (Lesson 16 §16.1)

The -te form (called the gerund here) is formed, for Vowel-verbs, by replacing the final -ru of the
dictionary form with -te (taberu → tabete); for Consonant-verbs, the same sound-assimilation
processes that produce the plain past (-ta) tense also apply to -te, and the text recommends learning
the -te form "by analogy" from each verb's already-known plain-past form rather than by rule (since
the plain past and -te form are built on the identical stem-alternation pattern: nomu → noñda (past)
/ noñde (-te), with the voiced nasal m triggering devoiced-t→d and the dental point of articulation of
t triggering m→ñ). An alternate allomorph -de is used with Consonant-verbs whose stem ends in g, b, m,
or n. The negative -te form is formed regularly from the plain negative by adding -de (tabénai + de →
tabénaide "not eating").

### Adjective/adverb phrases and degree adverbs, with a pragmatic register warning (Lesson 18 §18.1–18.2)

Two adjectives can modify a single noun in sequence (Nagái atsúi natsú ga owarimáshita "The long, hot
summer ended"), and an adverb may precede and intensify another adverb (manner-expression stacking).
A scale of degree adverbs for intensifying an adjective is given — totemo "very," zuibuñ "awfully,"
kañari "rather," sukóshi "a little," chittómo (+ negative) "not at all" — with an explicit
sociopragmatic warning on zuibuñ: despite being "very expressive," using it to describe a superior's
quality can land as a backhanded compliment, "carry[ing] the nuance that you did not have such an
expectation of the person" (p. 349) — a register/face-management nuance specific to addressing social
superiors, directly relevant to this project's politeness-register interests.

### Complement-marker particle ni; the restricted class of complement-obligatory intransitive verbs; naru + suru causative (Lesson 19 §19.2)

A fifth use is added to the already-overloaded particle ni (indirect object, time, manner, one
direction sense): marking the Complement slot (byōki ni narimáshita "(He) became sick"). Two classes
of intransitive verbal clause are distinguished: Class A (the large, default class — most
intransitive verbs, no complement) vs. Class B (a small, restricted class whose verbs *require* a ni-
marked complement: náru "becomes," yoru "depends on," sóu "runs alongside of," kanáu "is in accord
with"). The causative pairing of náru "becomes" is suru "does/makes" — the complement-with-suru
pattern is explicitly analyzed as a causative transformation of the basic intransitive clause (Musuko
ga isha ni narimáshita "My son became a doctor" → Musuko o isha ni shimáshita "I made my son [become]
a doctor").

### Transitive-intransitive verb pairs: systematic sound-formative patterns, and aru as a resultant-state passive (Lesson 20 §20.2–20.3)

A large, organized table of transitive/intransitive verb pairs is presented in five phonological
groupings by shared formative sound(s) distinguishing the transitive member from the intransitive:
group (a) -e-/-a- (atsumeru/atsumaru "gathers"/"gather"; kakeru/kakaru; tomeru/tomaru; shimeru/
shimaru), group (b) (akeru/aku; naraberu/narabu; tateru/tatsu; tsukeru/tsuku; yameru/yamu), group (c)
(dasu/deru; arawasu/arawareru), group (d) -s-/-r- (naosu/naoru "repairs"/"is repaired"; toosu/tooru;
watasu/wataru), group (e) (noseru/noru). The text draws out a generalization native speakers
apparently intuit even without formal training: "the sound s in verbs [is often associated with]
indicating the meaning of 'active' or 'causative'" — a real morphophonological iconicity claim about
the language's own verb-derivation system, worth flagging for the eventual mechanics-analysis phase.
Separately, §20.3 introduces a distinct resultant-state "passive" construction using the stative verb
áru with a transitive verb's -te form, reserved for transitive verbs that have no corresponding
intransitive partner to use instead (Tegami wa mō káite arimásu "The letter is already written" —
note that the original Object tegami "letter" surfaces as the sentence's Topic in this construction,
marked wa rather than o).

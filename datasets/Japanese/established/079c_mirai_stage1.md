## Grammar points (Unit 7)

### -masu verb ending: no person/number agreement, tenseless present/future

A high-value general grammar finding (not slang-specific, but foundational for later mechanics
analysis): Japanese verbs don't conjugate for subject person/number at all (unlike English's
"I eat/she eats"), and the polite -masu ending covers both present and future meaning — たべます can
mean "eat" or "will eat" depending purely on context. Pronoun dropping (already seen in Unit 4) is
reinforced here as a general discourse pattern, not isolated to favourite-subject questions. (p.151)

### Light verb します (shimasu) with noun objects

Many Japanese verbs are formed as [noun] + を + します rather than single lexicalized verb roots —
e.g. ジョギングを します ("jog," lit. "do jogging"), テニスを します ("play tennis," lit. "do
tennis"). します itself glosses as both "do" and "play" depending on the noun. This productive
noun+light-verb pattern is worth flagging as a mechanism the slang-synthesis phase could exploit
(new "slang verbs" formed by pairing a borrowed/invented noun with a light verb). (p.151)

### と (to) has two unrelated grammatical functions: "and" vs. "with"

Building on Unit 4's と ("and," joining two nouns), this unit introduces a second と meaning
"accompanied by" (with a person/animal) — いぬと あそびます ("play with the dog"). The book is
explicit that these are different meanings of the same written/spoken particle, not two forms. A
genuine case of particle polysemy worth tracking for later systematic review of Japanese's particle
inventory. (p.152)

### Numeral irregularity is context-dependent, not uniform across domains

A cross-unit comparative finding, assembled here from data across Units 2, 4, and 7: the avoidance
of し/しち (in favor of よん/なな) that governs *counting* numbers and *ages* does **not** carry
over to telling *time* — 7 o'clock is しちじ (not ななじ), and critically, 9 o'clock is くじ,
spoken plainly, even though "9 years old" must never be read く-さい per Unit 2's explicit taboo
note. This means the death/suffering-homophone avoidance is lexically/domain-restricted (ages,
general counting) rather than a blanket avoidance of the readings し/しち/く across all of Japanese —
a genuinely useful nuance for the mechanics-analysis phase when it gets to numeral taboo patterns,
since it shows the avoidance is conventionalized per-collocation, not derivable from the phonology
alone. 4 o'clock does stay irregular across all contexts checked so far (よじ for time, よねんせい
for grade, よん for counting/age) — so the irregularity patterns split unevenly between "4" (always
irregular) and "7"/"9" (irregular only in some domains). (p.141, 143 cross-referenced against p.22-23, 79)

### Regular ふん/ぷん alternation for "minutes"

ふん is used after 5, 15, 25, 35, 45, 55; ぷん after 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 — a fully regular phonetic
(not lexical) alternation the book characterizes as rule-governed rather than arbitrary, though it
leaves the underlying phonological conditioning as an open question for the learner ("Can you guess
why?"). This is likely a rendaku/gemination-adjacent euphonic alternation conditioned by the
preceding mora's final sound, worth a closer look if/when a mechanics-analysis pass covers Japanese
sound-change patterns generically. (p.141)

## Grammar points (Unit 8)

### へ vs に: an attested colloquial free-variation case

The directional particle is formally taught as へ, but the book explicitly states that many Japanese
speakers substitute に instead in ordinary speech (どこに いきますか alongside どこへ
いきますか) — an openly acknowledged register-neutral free-variation case, distinct from the
earlier register-governed pairs (which involve a genuine formality contrast). Worth flagging that not
every variant pair in this course reflects formality — some are simply attested alternation. (p.163)

### みなさん vs みんな: formal/informal "everyone"

A further explicit register pair, directly analogous to the pronoun/greeting pairs documented in
earlier units: みなさん is marked as the form teachers use formally to students; みんな is informal.
The book notes they are "spelt differently" (a hiragana-vs-hiragana orthographic distinction, not
just pronunciation). (p.163)

### 〜ましょう: deriving an invitation/volitional form from -masu

Replacing any verb's -masu ending with -mashō turns it into "let's [verb]" (いきます → いきましょう);
adding か turns it into "shall we...?" (たべましょうか). This is presented as a fully general,
productive derivational rule applicable to every -masu verb already learned — a clean example of a
single productive morphological operation layered on top of the polite verb-conjugation system. (p.171)

### The ちょっと... ellipsis-politeness pattern generalizes beyond food

Building directly on Unit 5's finding (declining food politely via "[food] は ちょっと..."), this
unit shows the same construction used to decline *any* invitation — ぶんかさいは ちょっと... / いまは
ちょっと... This confirms ちょっと + sentence-final ellipsis is a general Japanese
negative-politeness device, not food-specific, and is a strong, well-evidenced candidate pattern for
the mechanics-analysis phase to treat as a systematic register strategy rather than an isolated
idiom. (p.171)

### Plurals are optional/contextual, marked only when needed

A general-grammar finding: Japanese nouns are unmarked for number by default (いぬ = "dog" or
"dogs," ambiguous without context) and plural marking (via 〜たち, restricted here to people) is only
added when clarity genuinely requires it — e.g. when two people need to jointly refer to themselves
as "we." This is consistent with, and reinforces, the pronoun-dropping/context-dependence pattern
already seen with subject pronouns in Units 4 and 7. (p.171)

## Grammar points

### Systematic shi→chi baby-talk sound shift (and its compositional consequences)

Lesson 17 documents a fully systematic phonological substitution used across manga/TV convention
to mark "baby talk": shi becomes chi. Because sha, sho, and shu are themselves shi+ya/yo/yu
combinations, the same shift compositionally produces sha→cha, sho→cho (kaerimashō→kaerimachō),
and by extension shu→chu; tsu likewise shifts to chu (itsumo→ichumo). The source is explicit that
real child phonology more commonly substitutes other sounds for "s," and that this is a
*convention* of fictional baby-talk representation rather than a phonetically faithful transcript
— worth flagging for a synthesis phase, since it's evidence of a culturally-coded "sounds like a
child" marker distinct from how children actually mis-speak. Additional, non-phonological markers
of baby talk/child-directed speech noted by the source: a dedicated child-only vocabulary (manma,
nenne, an'yo — see vocabulary table); more frequent no as a sentence-ending for both statements and
questions; more frequent yo endings with da/desu dropped; and heavier-than-usual use of the
honorific o- prefix by parents speaking to children, functioning more as an affectionate diminutive
than a true honorific. (pp.108-113)

### Honorific verbs vs. politeness-level (formality) as separate axes

Lesson 18 uses nasaru (honorific "do," showing deference toward the person whose action it
describes) in a plain/abrupt-past informal context (dō nasatta no?, spoken casually between
spouses) to argue that an inherently honorific verb and a situation's overall formality are
independent variables — a verb can mark deference without the sentence being "formal" in register,
and the source explicitly says this kind of sentence resists being cleanly slotted into any single
PL tier. It further notes that women's use of polite/honorific forms, even among themselves, is
often a marker of refinement rather than literal social deference. (p.114)

### -sama honorific-thanks template (prefix + stem + -sama)

Several idiomatic "thank you" expressions share one template: honorific prefix (o- or go-) + a
noun stem + honorific suffix -sama (+ optional deshita for added formality/past tense): go-kurō-
sama, o-tsukare-sama, go-chisō-sama, o-somatsu-sama. None are literal when decomposed; all are
idiomatic thanks or modest self-deprecating responses. In casual registers, -sama can shorten to
-san or drop entirely, but the honorific prefix is retained even then — dropping the prefix too
turns the word back into an ordinary non-honorific noun (gokurō-sama → gokurō → kurō "trouble").
(pp.124-129)

# Japanese — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Remembering the Kanji 2 (representative sample, Chs. 1–11)

**Source:** James W. Heisig, *Remembering the Kanji II: A Systematic Guide to Reading Japanese
Characters* (1st ed. 1987, 7th printing March 1993, Japan Publications Trading Co.) — representative
sample across the full book (pp. 3–9 Introduction; p. 18–19 Ch.1 "The Kana and Their Kanji"; pp.
38–39 Ch.2 "Pure Groups"; pp. 96–97 Ch.5 "Semi-Pure Groups"; pp. 156–157 Ch.7 "Mixed Groups"; pp.
230–231 Ch.9 "A Potpourri of Readings"; pp. 266–267 Ch.10 "Supplementary Readings"; pp. 296–307
Ch.11 "A Mnemonics for the Japanese Readings").

**Coverage note — representative sampling, not exhaustive.** This 199-page, no-text-layer (vision-
read) companion to Volume 1 covers on-yomi/kun-yomi readings for the ~2000 kanji Volume 1 (established/
021) already introduced for meaning/writing. Given the scale (2000+ individually numbered reading
"frames"), this file samples: (1) the book's full introduction/methodology in detail — the "signal
primitive" mechanism, the standard frame format (Figure 1, p.5), and the flash-card design (p.297) —
and (2) a representative spread of entries across the book's progression, roughly one page-pair from
each of the ten on-yomi chapters' structural position (Ch.1 kana-derived kanji, Ch.2 pure signal-
primitive groups, Ch.5 semi-pure groups, Ch.7 mixed groups, Ch.9 potpourri, Ch.10 supplementary) plus
Ch.11's kun-yomi mnemonic method with its own worked examples. No marginalia (handwritten notes) was
found on any sampled page — all content below is genuinely typeset.

**Extraction gotcha — title-page volume number mismatch.** The scanned cover/title page (PDF p.1)
reads "REMEMBERING THE KANJI **III**" (three vertical strokes, confirmed via a pixel-level crop), not
"II" — but every interior running header from p.2 onward consistently reads "REMEMBERING THE KANJI
**II**", and the table of contents (chapter titles: Pure Groups, Semi-Pure Groups, Mixed Groups,
Index 1 "The Signal Primitives", etc.) matches the well-documented actual content/structure of Volume
2, not Volume 3 (which covers ~1000 additional kanji for writing, not readings for the already-known
2000). Treated this title page as a printing/scan-cover anomaly specific to this copy and extracted
the file as genuine Volume 2 content, consistent with this dispatch's stated companion-volume
relationship to established/021 (Volume 1).

**Numbering system note.** This source numbers entries as sequential "frames" (1–2169 approx.) across
the whole book rather than by page; the `Notes` column below records the frame number and chapter.
Each frame's cross-reference number (e.g. "1931", "402") points back to the kanji's Volume 1 frame
number, not a page number in this book.

## Grammar points

### The "signal primitive" mechanism (Introduction, pp. 4–5)

Heisig's organizing device for the entire on-yomi (Chinese reading) system: a *signal primitive* is
a primitive graphical element within a kanji's written form (already assigned a meaning in Volume 1)
that reliably predicts one specific Chinese reading wherever it recurs across many kanji. Because
these primitive forms were already memorized for their meaning in Volume 1, re-using them to predict
sound lets the student skip rote memorization for "well over a third" of the on-yomi. The book frames
readings as falling along a spectrum from fully consistent (signal primitive always → one sound, no
exceptions: "pure groups," Ch.2) through "semi-pure" (uniform but for exactly one exception, Ch.5) to
"mixed" (the same primitive signals two or more different sounds, subdivided further by how many
exceptions break an otherwise-uniform group, Ch.7) down to readings with no exploitable pattern at
all (Chs. 6, 8, 9, 10).

### Standard frame format (Figure 1, p.5)

Every numbered entry across Chapters 1–10 follows one fixed layout: the kanji itself and its running
frame number occupy the left column; the signal primitive (when relevant) sits beside it; the on-yomi
appears in katakana; a parenthetical cross-reference code (e.g. "(H)", "(H/K)") marks which Index
category applies; a bold number on the right gives the Volume 1 frame number where the character's
meaning/writing was originally taught; and beneath all of this sits one exemplary compound word with
its own reading and English gloss — chosen, per the Introduction's stated preference order, to
reuse an already-learned reading, be a word in ordinary use, or (lower priority) a rare/archaic
compound or proper name when nothing better illustrates the reading.

### Pure, semi-pure, and mixed groups (Chs. 2, 5, 7)

A "pure group" (Ch.2) is a set of kanji sharing one signal primitive with zero exceptions — every
member reads identically (e.g. the カ group: 過/渦/禍, or the キ group: 奇/寄/騎). A "semi-pure group"
(Ch.5) is the same idea with exactly one exception explicitly flagged and visually set off (boxed
separately) from the rest of the group — e.g. 怪 (on-yomi カイ) breaking an otherwise uniform ケイ
group (軽/茎/径), or 削 (サク) breaking a ショウ group. "Mixed groups" (Ch.7) relax this further,
covering primitives that predict two or more distinct sounds; the chapter itself subdivides into
groups with only 2 exceptions (still mostly regular) versus looser ones, and Heisig explicitly
justifies continuing to teach these as *groups at all* (rather than treating each character as an
isolated fact) on the grounds that by this point the student has learned 800+ characters via the
signal-primitive method, and understanding where/how the pattern breaks down is itself pedagogically
necessary.

### Catch-all chapters for non-patterned readings (Chs. 6, 8, 9, 10)

Chapters 6 and 8 gather readings best learned through already-familiar everyday vocabulary and useful
compounds respectively — their only commonality is that the kanji involved don't belong to any
natural phonetic group. Chapter 9 ("A Potpourri of Readings") gathers everything else of general
usefulness, often with an explanatory aside pointing out where a Japanese compound is semantically
transparent in a way its English equivalent (frequently Greek/Latin-derived) is not — e.g. 穴居人
"troglodyte" literally reads as "cave-dwelling person," no classical-language background required.
Chapter 10 ("Supplementary Readings") is explicitly described by the author as "a wastepaper basket"
for the remaining readings: uncommon, rare, or restricted mostly to proper names (e.g. 竹良 "chikura").

### Flash-card design (p. 297, Ch.11 preface)

The book recommends a specific two-sided flash-card format extending the Volume 1 cards: Side 1
carries the kanji, its kun-yomi root-word(s) with inflection markers, and the Volume 1 frame number;
Side 2 carries the on-yomi in katakana plus an illustrative compound. An asterisk on Side 1 flags a
kun-yomi reading that needs no inflection (a bare root word). Where one kanji has multiple kun-yomi
via different appended inflections (the book's own worked example: 弾 → たま "bullet" [uninflected],
弾く/ひく "to pluck/play music," 弾む/はずむ "to bounce"), each gets its own line on the card.

### The Syllabary of Primitive Phonemes (Ch.11, "A Mnemonics for the Japanese Readings")

For kun-yomi (native Japanese readings), Heisig shifts method entirely: rather than signal
primitives predicting sound from written form, he assigns an English "primitive meaning" to each
possible Japanese syllable (kana) sound — the "Syllabary of Primitive Phonemes." A word's kun-yomi
reading is decomposed into its component kana sounds, each mapped to its assigned primitive meaning,
and the student builds a single vivid imaginative story linking those component meanings to the
target English key-word — mirroring the primitive-element-to-meaning technique used for kanji
*writing* in Volume 1, now applied to *sound*. Worked examples sampled: 弾/たま "bullet" (た "another
person" + ま "room"); 墓/はか "grave" (は "tooth" + か "mosquito"); 絹/きぬ "silk" (き "tree" + ぬ
"sewing"); 塩/しお "salt" and 潮/しお "tide" (both from し "die" + お "tail" — the method tolerates this
kind of homophony since it only needs to go sound→meaning, not meaning→sound); 張る/はる "to lengthen"
(は "tooth" alone, with no second phoneme); and inflected-word examples 加わる/くわわる "to add" (く
"spinning" + わ "wheel") and 滅ぼす/ほろぼす "to destroy" (ほ "ghost/kite" + ろ "backbone").

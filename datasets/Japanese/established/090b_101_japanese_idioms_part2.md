**Source:** Michael L. Maynard & Senko K. Maynard, *101 Japanese Idioms: Understanding Japanese
Language and Culture Through Popular Phrases* (Passport Books/NTC Publishing Group, 1993; 1994
printing), pp. 104-213 (idioms 51-101 of 101; remainder of Section Three "The Human Body," all of
Section Four "From One to Ten and More," Section Five "From Place to Place," and Section Six "More
Cultural Keys"). Idioms 1-50 are covered in `090_101_japanese_idioms_part1.md`.

**Coverage note:** every one of idioms 51-101. Same entry structure as Part 1 (romaji headword +
kanji/kana + literal translation + idiomatic meaning + prose explanation + bilingual two-register
sample dialogue with an explicit `Style:` tag). Sample dialogues paraphrased/summarized, not
bulk-quoted, per copyright discipline. **This source has no usable text layer** and was vision-read
from two-printed-pages-per-scanned-image spreads; no handwritten marginalia was found anywhere in
this range. **Vision Reading Confidence: verified** for every entry (each term is independently
cross-confirmed between its title-block kanji and its restatement in the bilingual sample dialogue).

**Register note:** from idiom 74 onward this extraction explicitly captured the book's own
`Style:` annotation on each sample dialogue — a genuine source-marked register/sociolect tag
(spoken vs. written, casual vs. formal, and sometimes speaker gender). Idioms 51-73 were read with
the same care but their specific Style tags were not individually transcribed; they are marked
`colloquial` (the book's general target register) without a sub-tag.

### Register annotation as a found sociolinguistic data source

This book's `Style:` tags (captured per-entry above from idiom 74 onward) are a genuine, if
informal, register corpus: they mark each idiom's sample dialogue as spoken/written and
casual/formal, and sometimes specify speaker gender (e.g. "spoken/casual/female" vs. "spoken/
casual/male"). Patterns worth flagging for later mechanics analysis: idioms about social shame,
gossip, or interpersonal friction (Ana ga Attara Hairitai, Onobori-san, Baka wa Shinanakya Naoranai)
skew toward casual/spoken register with a specified speaker gender, while idioms about abstract
virtues or endings (Kooin Ya no Gotoshi, Yuushuu no Bi o Kazaru, Sumeba Miyako) skew toward
formal/written register — a plausible (though not exhaustively verified across all 101 entries)
correlation between an idiom's semantic domain and its typical register of use.

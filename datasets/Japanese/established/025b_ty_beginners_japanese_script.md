## Grammar points

### Two distinct kinds of kanji compounding

The unit distinguishes two ways kanji combine to create new meanings: (1) multiple simple kanji fusing into a single new complex kanji character (林 "wood" = two 木; 森 "forest" = three 木; 男 "man" = 田+力; 好 "like/love" = 女+子; 明 "bright" = 日+月; 本 "root/origin" = 木 + a line through the trunk) — these are new single characters, not multi-character words; versus (2) multiple separate kanji forming a multi-character compound word (熟語) while each kanji remains visually distinct, e.g. 人口 ("people's mouths" = population), 馬力 (horsepower), 女子 ("woman child" = girl). The book explicitly flags 女子 (two separate kanji) vs. 好 (one fused kanji) as the paired example clarifying the distinction (pp. 15–17).

### Literal vs. abstract/lateral compound meanings

Some compound meanings are direct "translations" of their component kanji (火山, "fire mountain" = volcano), while others require more lateral/abstract thinking (人目, "person eye" = in the public eye; 人力車, "human-power-vehicle" = rickshaw). The book notes that once known, the pictorial kanji act as a visual memory aid regardless of how literal or abstract the link is (p.17).

### Kunyomi vs. onyomi, and the general rule for which to use

Each kanji generally has (at least) two readings: kunyomi (訓読み), the native Japanese reading tied to a pre-existing Japanese word, and onyomi (音読み), a reading descended from the original Chinese pronunciation, adapted over centuries into Japanese phonology. Example: 川 ("river") read as *kawa* (kunyomi, the native Japanese word for river) or *SEN* (onyomi, from Chinese). **General rule:** kunyomi is used for single-kanji words, while onyomi is used for compound words of two-or-more kanji — illustrated by 人 (hito, kunyomi) and 口 (kuchi, kunyomi) individually, versus their compound 人口 pronounced as one word, JINKŌ, using the onyomi of each (JIN + KŌ) (pp. 20–21). The book flags that this is a general rule with exceptions, one of which is immediately introduced:

### Surnames are a kunyomi exception

Although the kunyomi/onyomi split above assigns onyomi to compounds, Japanese surnames — which are typically compounds of one to three kanji — are read with **kunyomi**, not onyomi, even though they are compound words. Example: 竹山 is read Takeyama (kunyomi take + yama), not an onyomi-based reading (pp. 23–24).

### Sequential voicing in surname readings (ta → da)

In some compound surnames, 田 (normally read *ta*) shifts to voiced *da* when it is not the first element of the compound — e.g. 金田 Kaneda, 竹田 Takeda, 石田 Ishida, 山田 Yamada, 川田 Kawada (the test section explicitly flags these with an asterisk as "a change in pronunciation from *ta* to *da*," p.28). This is Japanese rendaku (sequential voicing), though the book does not use that technical term, simply presenting it as a readings-exception to memorize per-surname.

### Homophones disambiguated by kanji

火 (fire) and 日 (sun/day) are both pronounced *hi* — an explicit example of two kanji sharing an identical reading but distinguished by which character (and surrounding context) is used, paralleled in the book to English homophones distinguished by spelling/context (p.22).

### Pronunciation rules — the five Japanese vowels

Japanese has five vowel sounds, each pronounced consistently regardless of the consonant attached: *a* as in "mat," *i* as in "hit," *u* as in "blue," *e* as in "end," *o* as in "hot." The sound *tsu* is flagged as unfamiliar to English speakers — it is one beat/syllable, with the *t* and *s* "squashed" together (p.22).

### Date notation and the calendar

Japanese months have no individual names — they are simply numbered 1 to 12 plus 月 ("month"), e.g. 1月 = January (the first month), 6月 = June, 12月 = December. Days of the month are written as a number + 日 (parallel to English "1st," "7th"). Dates are written in the order **month → date → day-of-week** (e.g. 8月3日 (火) = "Tuesday August 3rd," read/ordered as "August 3rd Tuesday") — the reverse of common English date-then-month ordering in many contexts (pp. 18, 20).

### Stroke-order Rules 5–8 (continuing from Unit 1's Rules 1–4)

Building on Unit 1's four rules, four more are introduced: **Rule 5** — a square-shaped kanji without internal content (example: 口 "mouth") follows a specific left/top/right/bottom-closing sequence, with the resulting shape distinct from the Rule-5 example given in Unit 1. **Rule 6** — when a square-shaped kanji has a middle/internal part, the bottom line of the square is written last, after the internal content (examples: 目 "eye," 田 "rice field" — the latter flagged as an *exception* to Rule 3 because its internal vertical line is written before the internal horizontal). **Rule 7** — where a kanji has a distinct left and right part, the left is written before the right; where it has a distinct top and bottom part, the top is written first (example: 林 "wood," combining Rules 5+6+7 is also shown for 門 "gate"). **Rule 8** — where a kanji is symmetrical around a central vertical axis, the axis stroke is written first, then the left side, then the right side (examples: 水 "water," 山 "mountain") (pp. 19–20, 25–27).

## Grammar points

### ...toori/doori vs. ...(no) yoo-ni (p.40-41)
Both can be glossed "as/like," but *toori/doori* claims exact accordance with a stated standard
(instructions, a promise, a schedule), while *...(no) yoo-ni* only claims resemblance/appearance. The
two are not interchangeable: *Kodomo-no yoo-ni yorokobimashita* ("he rejoiced like a child") works,
but *Kodomo-no toori-ni* does not, because the speaker isn't literally following a child's method.

### to vs. to issho-ni (p.42-43)
*To* marks a person involved in a mutual/reciprocal action (talking with, marrying, quarreling with)
— using *to issho-ni* instead implies doing that action together with someone else as a third party,
changing who is doing what to whom. *To issho-ni* is reserved for cases where both parties perform the
identical action (going somewhere, reading the same book).

### ...te-shimau's emotional range (p.44-45)
Marks completion, and colors that completion anywhere from neutral to regretful — but never positive.
A positive evaluation of the completed action (a promotion, a success) cannot take *...te-shimau*;
plain past tense is used instead.

### Familiar commands: ...te-ne vs. ...te-yo (p.46-47)
Both substitute for *...te-kudasai* in familiar registers, directed from an older/senior speaker to a
younger one. The sentence-final particle governs tone: *-ne* (raised intonation) softens into a mild
request, *-yo* (lowered intonation) typically signals irritation, impatience, or arrogance on the
speaker's part.

### Why "are you Japanese?" sounds odd to a stranger (p.48-49)
Two compounding problems: *anata-wa* inherently implies a contrast ("you, as opposed to others"),
making it an odd choice for a simple informational question about the listener; and opening a
conversation with a nationality question reads as officious (police/customs-style interrogation)
regardless of politeness level. A natural opener instead establishes shared atmosphere through a
neutral remark (weather) rather than information-seeking about the listener's identity.

### Registers for asking someone's opinion (p.50-51)
*Iken* ("opinion") and direct agree/disagree vocabulary (*sansee*/*hantai*) belong to formal,
multi-party discussion settings. One-on-one or casual conversation prefers softer framings — *Doo
omoimasu-ka*, *...ni tsuite-wa doo-deshoo-ka* — that don't force a committed yes/no stance.

### Requesting an explanation without sounding demanding (p.52-53)
*Setsumee-shite-kudasai* is appropriate in business/formal meetings but reads as demanding in personal
or polite conversation. The softer convention is to state one's own incomprehension (plainly:
*Wakarimasen*; reservedly: *Doomo yoku wakaranai-n-desu-ga*) and let the explainer offer more detail
unprompted, often self-deprecatingly framing the gap as one's own shortcoming rather than the
explainer's.

### Checking comprehension without giving offense (p.54-55)
A cline from blunt to face-saving: *Wakarimashita-ka*/*Wakatta?* (can imply doubt about the listener's
ability) → polite forms built on *wakaru* like *Owakari-deshoo-ka* (still risks sounding unpleasant) →
avoiding the word *wakaru* altogether (*Soko-made yoroshii-deshoo-ka* "is it all right so far?") or
offering to repeat/add detail instead of asking directly.

### Confirming a statement across registers (p.56-57)
*...n(o)-desu-ne* is the neutral confirmation pattern; *...n-dane* (associated with male speech) and
*...none* (associated with female speech) are familiar-register equivalents. *...to yuu koto-ni
narimasu-ne* is reserved for drawing out the implied conclusion of a long or roundabout statement.

### Avoiding overgeneralization (p.58-59)
The core pattern *...ni yotte chigau* ("it depends on...") very commonly drops *chigau* entirely,
leaving just *...ni yorimasu*. *Ichigai-niwa ienai* ("can't say categorically") and set idioms like
*Toki-to baai-ni yorimasu-ne* serve the same hedging function against being pinned to a blanket
generalization — a recurring pragmatic need given how often foreigners are asked broad
"do people in your country..." questions.

## Grammar points

### Passive sentences, including the "indirect/adversative passive" (Unit 13)
The passive suffix `-(r)areru` attaches to the verb root (as met earlier as a potential-verb
ending in Unit 10); the active subject becomes the passive agent marked by `ni`. Example:
`Senséi wa Tároo o homemáshita` (active) → `Tároo wa senséi ni homeraremáshita` (passive). Even
intransitive verbs can passivize in Japanese (unlike English) — when they do, the construction is
called the "indirect passive" or "adversative passive," and carries a strong connotation that the
sentence's subject (usually "I"/"we") suffered inconvenience or discomfort from the event, even
though the subject didn't literally receive the action (`Kinóo áme ni furaremáshita`, "I was
caught in the rain yesterday" — lit. "was rained on"). With transitive verbs, this same indirect-
passive pattern (object of the active sentence stays the object; the affected person becomes the
subject) marks personal distress, contrasted with the neutral direct passive: `saifu ga
nusumaremáshita` ("the wallet was stolen," neutral) vs. `saifu o nusumaremáshita` ("I had my wallet
stolen," distress-marked). (pp. 214-215)

### Causative and causative-permissive sentences (Unit 13)
`-(s)aséru` (root + suffix, initial `-s` dropping after a consonant) forms the causative:
`tabesaséru` ("make eat"), `mataséru` ("make wait"). It doubles as a "permissive" (letting someone
do something); when ambiguous and there's no other object in the sentence, adding `ni` after the
permitted/caused person's object can disambiguate toward the permissive reading. In casual speech
a shortened `-(s)asu` form often substitutes, "particularly common in the plain past-tense and
conditional endings" per explicit source note. Because the causative implies a person in authority,
it's often softened by pairing with `ageru` or `-tai`. The very polite `-sasete itadakimásu`
(causative + the receptive-honorific `itadaku`) is explicitly flagged by the source as "particularly
favoured by certain types of middle-class ladies," used in formal speechmaking/greeting contexts.
(pp. 216-217)

### The causative-passive (Unit 13)
Combining causative + passive typically prefers the short causative form before adding the passive
ending: `mataséru` ("make wait") → expected `mataseráreru` but in practice usually `matasáreru`
("be kept waiting"); `suru`'s causative-passive is `saserareru`. Notably, this combined form does
not retain the permissive connotation the plain causative can carry. (pp. 217-218)

### *-sóo* ("looks like") vs. *sóo desu* (hearsay) (Unit 13)
Already met as hearsay-marking `sóo desu` after a plain verb form, the same syllable attached
directly to a verb/adjective *stem* (cutting `-masu` or final `-i`) instead means "it looks like
it will..." (`takasóo desu`, "it looks expensive"). `íi`/`yói` and `nái` have irregular forms:
`yosasóo` ("seems good"), `nasasoo` ("seemingly not"). (pp. 218-219)

### Describing others' subjective states: *-gáru* (Unit 13)
Japanese grammatically separates first-person subjective statements (`samúi`, "I feel cold") from
objective judgements about others based on observed evidence. Besides `sóo desu`/`yoo desu` for
this, the dedicated suffix `-gáru` converts a subjective adjective into an objective verb describing
someone's apparent behavior: `samugáru` ("to behave as if cold"), `hazukashigáru` ("to act shy/
embarrassed"). The same suffix attaches to desiderative `-tai` to give `-tagáru` ("he wants to...").
A short list of paired forms is given: hoshíi/hoshigáru, iya da/iyagáru, kowái/kowagáru, atsúi/
atsugáru, omoshirói/omoshirogáru, natsukashíi/natsukashigáru. (pp. 220-221)

### *ni yoru to* vs. *ni yotte* — a flagged learner-confusion point (Unit 13)
Both can translate English "according to," but mark distinct concepts: `ni yoru to` introduces
reported/quoted opinion or hearsay (co-occurring with a final `sóo desu`); `ni yotte` means "in
accordance with"/"depending on" (`kuni ni yotte búnka ga chigaimásu`, "culture differs from country
to country"). The source explicitly calls this out as a common point of learner confusion. (p. 221)

### Degrees of probability; *-kata* "way of doing"; *-nikúi*/*-yasúi* (Unit 13)
Certainty is typically still hedged with a closing `deshóo` or `to omoimásu` even after a strong
assertive adverb like `kanarazu` ("without fail") — the source frames Japanese speakers as "less
inclined... to make dogmatic assertions" even when expressing high confidence. `n' ja nái ka to
omoimásu` ("probably," lit. "I think, is it not that...?") is the colloquial form of the more
formal/written `no dewa nái ka to omoimásu` — an explicit register pair. `-kata` on a verb stem
makes "way of doing X" (`tabekáta`, "way of eating"); `-nikúi`/`-yasúi` on a verb stem mean
"difficult to.../easy to..." (pp. 221-222)

### Keigo (respect language) — register flagship finding (Unit 14)
Introduced with explicit sociolinguistic framing: keigo originates in pre-Meiji feudal hierarchy
but persists as "the lubricating oil of harmonious social interaction" in egalitarian contemporary
Japan. Two main categories: "referent honorifics" (show respect to the person referred to) and
"addressee honorifics" (show politeness to the conversation partner, i.e. the `désu`/`-másu` system
already learned). Within referent honorifics: "subject honorifics" elevate the sentence's subject
(a small irregular verb class ending in `-áru` — `irassháru` "come/go/be," `kudasáru` "give,"
`ossháru` "say," `nasáru` "do" — drops its final root `-r` before `-másu` and in the imperative).
The source flags that the imperative forms of these honorific verbs (e.g. `irasshái`, "Come!"/"Go!")
carry only mild honorific force and are typically used toward children, junior colleagues, or close
friends — a real register trap for learners who might assume imperative = always casual-plain, or
honorific root = always deferential. `meshiagaru` ("to eat," honorific) is explicitly noted as an
exception that keeps fully regular `-másu`/imperative forms despite its `-aru` ending. The regular
subject-honorific construction is `o-` + verb stem + `ni náru` (`okaki ni náru`, "[an honored
person] writes"), with an alternative using a form of `da` instead of `ni náru` for present/ongoing
states. `gozónji da` (honorific "to know") and `de irassháru` (honorific copula) are irregular
subject-honorific forms. Object honorifics (elevating the direct/indirect object when the implicit
subject is "I"/"we") use `o-` + verb stem + `suru`/formal `itásu` (`oyoróshiku onegai itashimásu`).
(pp. 232-235)

### Honorific prefixes *o-*/*go-* on nouns and adjectives (Unit 14)
Many common nouns (especially foods/beverages) take `o-`/`go-` as a now-purely-elegant prefix
with no remaining honorific force, explicitly noted as "employed particularly often by women"
(`oyu` "hot water," `osake` "rice wine," `ocha` "tea," `okane` "money"). Historically `o-` attached
to native-Japanese-origin nouns and `go-` to Chinese-origin compounds, but the source notes this
rule has become "very confused" with many exceptions in both directions (`goyukkúri` — native word
+ `go-`; `odénwa` — Chinese loan + `o-`); some words like `henjí` ("answer") take either prefix
interchangeably. True adjectives/descriptive nouns take the same prefixes for their honorific forms.
`íi`/`yói` has a dedicated separate honorific form, `yoroshíi`, typically used when a social
superior approves/endorses something, or in questions seeking a superior's approval. (pp. 235-236)

### The three-way plain/polite/formal speech-style system (Unit 14)
Beyond plain (met in non-final and casual-conversation verbs) and polite (`désu`/`-másu`, the
book's default), there is a distinct formal style used mainly in greetings, speechmaking, and on
the telephone, built on a small dedicated verb set with the speaker (or someone close to the
speaker) as subject: `gozaimásu` (be), `móosu` (say/be called), `itásu` (do), `máiru` (come/go),
`óru` (be), and `itadaku` (specifically "to eat" in this register). Formal-style adjectives take a
long-vowel form before `gozaimásu`, with the vowel-lengthening pattern keyed to the adjective
root's final sound (`-a`/`-o` roots → `-oo`; `-u` roots → `-uu`; `-ki`/`-shi` roots → `-kyuu`/
`-shuu`; irregular `íi`→`yóo`, honorific `yoroshíi`→`yoroshúu`). The formal style also substitutes
Chinese-derived vocabulary for common native words: `myóonichi` for `ashita` ("tomorrow"),
`sakújitsu` for `kinóo` ("yesterday") — both explicit register pairs flagged by the source. `monó`
("person") is used self-referentially in this register (`Watakushi wa koo yuu monó de gozaimásu`,
lit. "I am this kind of person," said while presenting one's business card). (pp. 236-237)

### The passive as a (lesser) honorific; abstract nouns in *-sa*/*-mi*; comparison particles *hodo*/*gúrai* (Unit 14)
The passive ending can double as a subject-honorific construction, explicitly noted as "a little
less respectful" than the full `o-` + stem + `ni náru` form, used "more by men," and distinguishable
from a true passive by the absence of an agent marked with `ni`. Abstract nouns are formed from
adjective roots with `-sa` (`takása` "height" from `takái`) — the regular, highly productive
pattern — or the explicitly-flagged-as-rarer and more figurative `-mi` (`omomi` "gravity/
significance" vs. the more literal `omosa` "weight," both from `omoi` "heavy"). Negative/equal
comparison (not covered by the `yori`/`hoo` comparative system from Unit 8) uses `hodo` ("as...as,"
negated: `Taiséiyoo wa Taihéiyoo hodo híroku arimasén`) and `gúrai` ("about as...as," affirmative
near-equality: `Áni wa chichi gúrai se ga takái desu`). (pp. 238-240)

### Compound verbs and the plain imperative (Unit 14)
A productive set of verb-stem + verb compounds: `-dásu` (begin suddenly: `furidásu` "start
raining," `nakidásu` "burst into tears"), `-hajiméru` (begin), `-owaru` (finish), `-naosu` (redo),
`-tsuzukéru` (continue), `-sugíru` (overdo/too much). The plain imperative (consonant-root: root +
`-e`, e.g. `ike` "go!"; vowel-root: root + `-ro`, with `-yo` explicitly flagged as "quite common in
western Japan and in written Japanese" as an alternative — a regional/register note; irregular
`kúru`→`kói`, `suru`→`shiró`/`séyo`; `kureru`→irregular `kuré` "give me!") is used mainly between
close friends/family, and the source cautions that ordering people around is generally
counterproductive — the `-te kudasái` request form is recommended for most purposes. The negative
plain imperative adds `na` to the plain form (`ikú na`, "don't go!"). `choodai` ("accept with
thanks"/"please") can soften a bare `-te`-form request into a casual, friendly one. Brusque
imperatives are routinely used even in otherwise polite speech when reporting what one was told to
do (`Iké to iwaremáshita`, "I was told to go"). (pp. 241-243)

### Zodiac-register vocabulary: formal vs. everyday animal names (Unit 15)
The 12 zodiac-cycle animal names have a dedicated, more literary/formal register distinct from the
everyday conversational animal names for several of the same creatures — e.g. zodiac `u` vs.
everyday `usagi` ("rabbit"); zodiac `mi` vs. everyday `hébi` ("snake"); zodiac `tori` vs. everyday
`niwatori` ("cock/rooster"); zodiac `í` vs. everyday `inoshíshi` ("boar") — while others are
identical in both registers (`ushi`, `tora`, `tatsu`, `umá`, `hitsuji`, `sáru`, `inú`). This is an
explicit two-register vocabulary split the source lays out in full table form, tied to the `eto`
calendrical system (10 stems x 12 branches = 60-year cycle) still culturally active in Japan for
personality/fortune-telling purposes. (pp. 253-255)

### Public signage vocabulary as a distinct functional register (Unit 15)
The source explicitly singles out a small set of kanji compounds as worth recognizing purely for
practical way-finding even before full reading fluency is achieved: `hijóoguchi` (emergency exit),
`kaisatsúguchi` (ticket gate), `kiken` (danger), `chúui` (attention/caution), `kinshi` (forbidden) and
its compounds `chuusha-kinshi` (no parking) and `kin'en` (no smoking), `ippootsúukoo` (one-way),
`koojichuu` (under construction), `teikyuubi` (regular closing day). This is framed by the source as
a distinct, bounded "signage register" worth learning as a set, separate from the conversational
vocabulary built up through the rest of the course. (pp. 256-257)

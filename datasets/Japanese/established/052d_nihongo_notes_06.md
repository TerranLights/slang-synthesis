## Grammar points

### Stem+na familiar commands vs. dictionary-form+na negative imperatives (p.84-85)
Superficially similar-looking but structurally distinct: the familiar affirmative command attaches
*na* to a verb's stem (the pre-*-masu* form, e.g. *tabe-*), while the negative imperative (documented
in the prior essay) attaches *na* to the full dictionary form (*taberu*) — because they build on
different base forms, the two are never actually confused in practice despite the resemblance. The
stem+*na* command is restricted to family/close-friend registers, commonly parent-to-child, and sits
even further down the familiarity scale than *-nasai* (itself usable in social contexts only in a
handful of frozen set phrases like *Okaerinasai*/*Oyasuminasai*). *Yo* can soften the stem+*na* form's
tone toward children, and the authors separately note the form's association with a stock vulgar/
tough-guy speech register (explicitly linked to a well-known movie character), illustrating how the
same morphological pattern spans a wide affective range depending on context.

### ...te-wa as a negative-judgment-restricted conditional (p.86-87)
Structurally parallel to *...eba* as a conditional marker, but *...te-wa* carries two compounding
restrictions: what follows must be negative in form or meaning, AND it must specifically be a
statement of the speaker's own evaluative judgment rather than a plain description of an action or a
positive circumstance (*...tsugoo-ga ii*, "it's convenient," is ungrammatical here; so is a bare
action verb like *yamemasu*). This dual restriction is what has crystallized *...te-wa* into a
specific, recognizable family of set admonition/complaint phrases (*...te-wa ikemasen*, *...te-wa
dame-desu*, *...te-wa komarimasu*), which can further combine with the suffering passive for doubled
displeasure-marking.

### Chan-to's built-in evaluative stance, and why it's unsafe toward superiors (p.88-89)
*Chan-to* ("exactly, properly, as expected") inherently encodes the SPEAKER's own evaluation of
whether someone met an expectation — this is why it cannot be used to describe a social superior's
actions (even approvingly), since doing so implicitly positions the speaker as someone entitled to
judge that person's performance. The fix when a superior's timing genuinely matters is to drop the
evaluative framing entirely and state the bare fact (optionally with precise timing), rather than
reaching for any synonym of "properly." The construction's meaning with children (its primary
unrestricted use) is itself context-dependent — the same sentence can mean "mind your manners" or
"keep to a routine" depending on what expectation is actually in play, so correctly parsing *chan-to*
requires already knowing the speaker's specific expectation.

### Yorokobu vs. ureshii: a person-restricted verb/adjective pair, plus social restraint on direct pleasure (p.90-91)
*Yorokobu* ("to rejoice/be pleased") defaults to describing someone ELSE's pleasure — used bare about
oneself, it misleadingly sounds like a report about a third party. *Ureshii* is the correct
first-person emotion-adjective. Separately, Japanese social convention generally favors restraint
from directly voicing one's own pleasure in ordinary polite situations (even using the grammatically
correct *ureshii*) — acceptable settings for direct emotional self-expression are limited to overtly
emotional public moments or informal/family conversation. The socially preferred alternative in a
polite context reframes pleasure indirectly around having been useful (*Oyaku-ni tatte yokatta-to
omoimasu*), optionally combined with attributing credit elsewhere, echoing the compliment-response
gradient documented earlier in the volume.

### Suru's "to cost" and "to behave" senses, disambiguated by context and prosody (p.92-93)
Beyond its basic "to do" meaning, *suru* preceded by a sum of money or a degree word (*zuibun*,
*amari*) means "to cost" — and specifically with degree words, the disambiguation from "to do" is
carried not just by context but by prosody: the degree word and *shita* are pronounced as one
unbroken phrase, with no pause, when the "cost" sense is intended. A parallel extension lets *suru*
combine with manner-adverbs (*yukkuri*, *nonbiri*, *burabura*) to mean "to behave [in that manner],"
again disambiguated from the literal "do" sense by context rather than any distinct morphology.

### Hedging uncertainty: moshika-suru-to vs. tabun, and the social function of hedged language (p.94-95)
*Moshika-suru-to*/*moshika-shitara* (normally closing with *kamo shirenai*) marks genuine uncertainty
about a real possibility, graded alongside more formal *koto-ni yoru-to*, less common *hyotto-suru-to*,
and very formal *arui-wa*; *tabun* (closing with *deshoo*/*daroo*) instead implies the speaker is
fairly confident. Beyond its literal epistemic function, *moshika-suru-to* is explicitly recruited as
a social softening device — used when the speaker is reluctant to disappoint the listener or when
broaching something unpleasant — functioning as much to signal consideration/reserve toward the
listener's feelings as to express genuine doubt.

### Desu-mono/mono as "family language" reason-marking, not simply feminine speech (p.96-97)
*...desu-mono* (women) and *...da-mono* (men, more restricted) attach a supporting reason AFTER the
statement it justifies — reversed from ordinary reason-then-statement order — and carry a built-in
implication of self-defense/self-justification that makes them broadly unsuitable for ordinary social
speech, confined instead to contexts where defending oneself is permitted or can be framed politely.
The authors explicitly reject the common characterization of this construction as simply "feminine
language," reframing it instead as belonging to a broader "family language" register distinct from
"social language" — men use the parallel *da-mono* form, just restricted to family/close-friend
contexts rather than general use, and the authors note this gendered skew is itself a historical
artifact of women's traditionally more home-centered social role, likely to shift as society changes.

### Koto's range: abstract "fact/thing," idiom family, and the "necessity" sense (p.98-99)
*Koto* in its basic sense marks an abstract fact or thing, contrasted with concrete *mono*. Beyond
that, it anchors a cluster of grammaticalized idioms (*...suru koto-ga aru*, "sometimes does...";
*...shita koto-ga aru*, "has the experience of having done..."; *...koto-ni naru*, "it has been
decided that..."; *...koto-ni suru*, "to decide to..."). A further, harder-to-parse sense covered
here is *koto* meaning "necessity," typically paired with a following negative predicate (*...koto-wa
nai-daroo*, "there's no need to...") — flagged as a genuinely difficult usage for foreign learners
specifically because it departs furthest from the straightforward "fact/thing" starting point.

### Tokoro's extension from physical place to situational/temporal moment (p.100-101)
Beyond its literal "place" sense, *tokoro* generalizes to mark an abstract situation or a point within
an unfolding action: paired with a non-past verb it marks the moment just before an action (*dekakeru
tokoro-desu*, "about to leave"), and with the -ta form it marks the moment just after completion
(*kaette-kita tokoro-desu*, "have just now come back"). *Ii tokoro*/*warui tokoro* extend this further
to mark a favorable or embarrassingly unfavorable circumstance; combined with *...te-iru* it marks an
action currently in progress; and it appears in several further idiomatic constructions (*...ta
tokoro-de*, "even if..."; *...tokoro-datta*, "[I] almost...," describing a narrowly averted event).

### Referring to a superior's wishes: avoiding both ...tai and ...tagaru (p.102-103)
Two independent restrictions stack here: the first-person desire form *...tai* is essentially
unusable for anyone but the speaker (using it about a third person is simply ungrammatical/wrong, not
just impolite), so a third party's desire must use *...tagaru* instead — but *...tagaru* itself
vividly depicts visible, anxious eagerness and is therefore further restricted to people the speaker
doesn't need to be polite about. Applied to a social superior, it makes them sound as if they were
anxiously pining/begging, which is undignified. The correct solution sidesteps both forms entirely by
describing the superior's ACTION rather than their internal desire (*Shachoo-ga oyobi-desu*, "The
director is calling for you," rather than any desire-verb construction).

### ...te-itadaku for favors bearing on the speaker's own interest (p.104-105)
When an action concerns something unrelated to the speaker's personal interest, honorific verb forms
alone (*Oyomi-ni narimashita-ka*) suffice for adequate politeness. But when the action actually
constitutes a favor affecting the speaker (someone reading material the speaker wrote or sent), plain
honorific phrasing is "merely verbal" politeness and insufficient — the speaker must additionally mark
gratitude using a *...te-itadaku*-based verb, which outranks the parallel *...te-kudasaru* form in
politeness specifically because it frames the favor as significant and implies the speaker feels
fortunate it was granted. These devices compose rather than substitute for each other, as shown by a
maximally-stacked example combining humble, honorific, favor-marking, and softening elements in one
sentence.

### Rapid-speech -ya contractions: two distinct sources collapsing to one surface form (p.106-107)
In casual rapid speech, the stem+*-wa* emphatic-negative pattern (*wakari-wa* → *wakarya*, combined
with *shinai*/*shimasen*) and the *-eba* conditional form (*wakareba* → *wakarya*) contract to the
identical surface form *-ya* for u-verbs (the godan/yodan class whose plain negative ends in *-anai*)
— genuinely ambiguous out of context between "will never understand" and "if I understood." Ru-verbs
(ichidan class, conditional *-reba*) only undergo the conditional contraction, so no such ambiguity
arises for that verb class. In actual spoken (rather than written) Japanese, a pause before the
following word disambiguates the two readings reliably, even though the written forms look
confusingly identical.

### Kochira/sochira as relational, contrast-dependent pronoun substitutes (p.108-109)
*Kochira* ("person(s) on this/my side") and *sochira* ("person(s) on your side") function as
indirect substitutes for "I/we" and "you," but — unlike true personal pronouns — they're not freely
usable any time first/second person reference is needed. Their use inherently frames an active
speaker-side-vs-listener-side contrast; stated without such a contrast in play, sentences like
*Kochira-wa isogashii-desu* are flagged as simply not idiomatic. *Kochira* also extends, via the same
proximal-deixis family as *kore*/*kono*, to introducing a third person present ("this is so-and-so"),
paralleling *sochira*'s derivation from *sore*/*sono*.

### -ppoi's two attachment classes: approximation (colors/nouns) vs. trait-marking (emotion verbs) (p.110-111)
Attached to color words, *-ppoi* means "-ish" (*shiroppoi*, "whitish"); attached to certain other
nouns it adds "too much of..." with a generally negative coloring (*mizuppoi*, "overly watery/
diluted") — explicitly contrasted with the positively-coded *-rashii* suffix (*kodomo-rashii*,
"childlike" in a good sense, vs. *kodomoppoi*, "childish," negatively judged immaturity). A separate
attachment class covers verbs specifically describing emotions (*okoru*, *wasureru*, *higamu*),
yielding a general-trait/tendency reading (*okorippoi*, "quick to anger"). The suffix's scope is
explicitly NOT extendable to verbs describing actions rather than emotional states — even an
emotionally-adjacent action verb like *warau* ("to laugh") rejects it (*waraippoi is marked
ungrammatical), establishing emotion-vs-action as the real governing distinction rather than any
looser semantic affinity.

### ...dasu vs. ...hajimeru: near-synonymous "to start," with two systematic preferences (p.112-113)
Both suffixes commonly attach to the same verb with little meaning difference (*furihajimeru*/
*furidasu*, both "to start raining"), but two real asymmetries are documented: *...dasu* is strongly
preferred, over *...hajimeru*, specifically for verbs describing a sudden/unexpected onset,
especially sudden emotional changes (*okoridasu*, *nakidasu*, *waraidasu* — bursting out angry/
crying/laughing); conversely, *...hajimeru* (not *...dasu*) is the preferred choice when the sentence
is itself a request or proposal to begin an action. Independent of these two specific preferences,
*...dasu* is also noted to generally sound more conversational than the more neutral/written-feeling
*...hajimeru* even where either would be grammatical.

### Avoiding "all + negative" ambiguity when meaning "not all" (p.114-115)
A quantifier word ("all," "everyone") combined directly with a negative predicate (*minna
ikimasen-deshita*) is systematically ambiguous in practice and tends to default to a "NONE" reading
rather than the intended "not all" reading — most Japanese speakers avoid this combination
specifically because of the ambiguity, preferring *daremo*/*hitori-mo* + negative when "none" really
is intended. To correctly express "not all," several alternative constructions are documented: adding
*wa* after the quantifier (though *minna-wa* specifically is avoided too, since it idiomatically
tends to suggest "everybody DID..." in other contexts); *...n-ja arimasen*/*...wake-ja arimasen*
("it's not the case that..."); *...tokoro-made-wa* (emphasizing a shortfall from the maximum amount);
and *...kirenai* ("cannot finish doing," for when the total quantity is simply too great to complete).

### Graded strong-denial constructions, register-sorted (p.116-117)
*...kko nai* (derived from *koto*) marks a strong denial in distinctly familiar register, unusable in
formal situations — functionally similar to the more register-flexible *...hazu-ga nai*, which the
*kko*-form specifically cannot substitute for in polite contexts. *...mon(o)-ka*/*...mon(o)-desu-ka*
is a familiar-register rhetorical-question denial said with falling tone (the bare *mon(o)-ka* form
skewing more masculine). In polite speech, flat/strong denial of any kind is generally avoided
altogether in favor of the softer *...nai-to omoimasu* hedge — consistent with *sonna*'s own
built-in negative-evaluative coloring (recurring from both this essay's denial constructions and the
vol. 5 pattern *Sonna koto arimasen-tara*), which itself tends to be avoided in reserved/polite speech.

### Hanasu vs. hanashikakeru: mutual talk vs. directed speech-at, plus a lighter passive alternative (p.118-119)
*Hanasu* ("to talk") specifically implies talking WITH someone (mutual, reciprocal), not directing
speech AT them — a real source of miscommunication illustrated by the frame story's own documented
error. *Hanashikakeru* (*hanasu* + *kakeru*) is the correct verb for one-sided, directed speech
("to speak to/address"), and its passive form *hanashikakerareru* ("to be spoken to") is explicitly
flagged by the frame-story protagonist as uncomfortably long — prompting the essay's noted alternative
of using *...ga...te-kuru* ("someone does X to me") as a lighter-weight substitute for the full
*...rareru* passive ending, functionally equivalent but less unwieldy. Separately, *kakeru* carries an
entirely different function when attached to other verb stems more generally, marking "to start doing
[without finishing]" (*yomikakeru*, "to start reading") — a second, distinct grammaticalized use of
the same morpheme, normally disambiguated cleanly by context from its "address someone" function in
*hanashikakeru*.

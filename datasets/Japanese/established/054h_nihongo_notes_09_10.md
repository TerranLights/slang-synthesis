## Grammar points

### Toori vs. yoo-ni: exact sameness vs. similarity (p.140-141)
Both roughly mean "the way," and are interchangeable in many instructions ("please do as I said").
The fine difference: *toori* emphasizes exact sameness and corresponds to "just as," while *yoo-ni*
marks mere similarity. This is why *toori* is the natural choice for agreeing emphatically with
someone's stated opinion (*Sono toori-desu* "Exactly"), while a comparison implying resemblance
rather than identity (e.g. "there are few people as fine as Mr. Yamada") requires *yoo-na*, not
*toori-no*.

### Two ways to say "without..." and a trap (p.142-143)
"Noun + nashi-de" and "noun + verb-naide" are freely interchangeable for "without X" / "without
doing X." The *-nakute* form of a verb, however, cannot be substituted into this privative meaning —
it is simply ungrammatical here, a specific trap flagged for learners. To negate an entire "without
X, you can't Y" statement, *wa* is added directly after *de* (*...nashi-de-wa*).

### Nan-nara as a self-contained offer opener (p.144-145)
*Nan-nara* ("if it is what" → "if that is so") opens an offer, invitation, or statement of
permission, and is frequently used alone — the rest of the offer left unstated when it's obvious
from context, as when a character simply says "Nan-nara..." and stands up to go buy something.
*Nan-deshitara* is the more polite equivalent.

### The ...me suffix for "a little" (p.146-147)
Added to certain adjectives to mean "a little/somewhat," but restricted to adjectives describing
measurable qualities (size, time, amount, strength) — *hayame*, *oome*, *futome*, *usume*, *hosome*.
It sounds unnatural on adjectives describing subjective, non-measurable impressions, such as
loneliness or taste quality (*sabishime*, *oishime* are both flagged as strange).

### Gozonji-desu-ka and the zonjiru humble/honorific split (p.148-149)
*Gozonji-desu-ka* is the honorific way to ask "do you know...?", built on the honorific noun
*gozonji* ("your knowing"). On the humble side, *zonjiru* replaces *shiru* ("to know") entirely:
*zonjite-imasu*/*zonjite-orimasu* replace *shitte-imasu*, and *zonjimasen* replaces *shirimasen* —
*zonjite-orimasu* reads as more humble than *zonjite-imasu*, mirroring *orimasu* vs. *imasu*
generally.

### Ao/aoi and the noun-vs-adjective pattern for color change (p.150-151)
*Ao* (noun) and *aoi* (adjective) both span blue-green, but *Shingoo-ga ao-ni natta* ("the light
turned green") is the natural phrasing — using the noun form with *naru* — even though the
adjective form (*aoku natta*) is also grammatical. Red follows the opposite preference: *akaku
natta* (adjective form) is standard for "turned red" (e.g. blushing), while *aka-ni natta* is not
used for describing a face's color. When an object genuinely has two colors, the pattern is
"color-noun + to + color-noun" (*ao-to shiro-no hata* "a blue and white flag"), not a combined
*-kute* adjective phrase, which would wrongly imply the whole object is both colors simultaneously.

### Jama vs. ojama: bare noun vs. honorific set phrase (p.152-153)
*Jama* (no honorific prefix) names an obstruction or disturbance in a plain, direct register —
bicycles blocking a path, or a blunt "stop bothering me." *Ojama*, with the honorific *o-* prefix,
is reserved for the polite set phrases around visiting someone or taking their time (*ojama-shimasu*,
*doomo ojama-shimashita*) — the two forms don't substitute for each other despite sharing a root,
which originally comes from a Buddhist term for devils obstructing enlightenment.

### A cline of "don't..." expressions by register (p.154-155)
Japanese has no single equivalent of English "don't": the written/blunt dictionary-form+*na*
imperative is restricted to very familiar or rough speech (mostly used by men); ordinary spoken
negative requests use *-naide* (optionally softened with *-ne*); polite social requests use
*-naide-kudasai*; colloquial contractions *-cha ikenai*/*-cha dame* offer another everyday register;
and a special child-directed soft form, *-nai-no*, is used when speaking to young children, often
paired with a reassuring justification.

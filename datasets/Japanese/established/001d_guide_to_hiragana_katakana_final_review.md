## Grammar point: script choice as a real, book-demonstrated linguistic signal

This source is the first in the project to require tracking script choice as carrying its own
meaning, and Part III's own two quizzes make the pattern concrete rather than just asserted in
prose (compare 001's "two kana systems" grammar point).

**Flora/fauna quiz (25 items, each marked H for hiragana or K for katakana by the book itself):**
every native/long-naturalized plant or animal is hiragana (*inu* "dog," *sakana* "fish," *take*
"bamboo," *kaede* "maple," *tsubaki* "camellia," *maguro* "tuna," *nihonzaru* "Japanese monkey,"
*nezumi* "mouse/rat," *ajisai* "hydrangea," *hyō* "leopard," *kabutomushi* "Goliath beetle," *hebi*
"snake," *hirame* "flatfish," *botan* "peony," *suisen* "narcissus," *ichō* "gingko" — 16 of 25),
while every foreign-introduced species or foreign-coined plant/animal name is katakana (*raion*
"lion," *yūkari* "eucalyptus," *chūrippu* "tulip," *koara* "koala bear," *kangarū* "kangaroo,"
*haibisukasu* "hibiscus," *kānēshon* "carnation," *pengin* "penguin," *mahoganī* "mahogany" — 9 of
25). This is a clean, book-verified instance of the katakana-for-foreign-origin rule applied below
the loanword level, to biological nomenclature specifically — worth flagging for later mechanics
analysis of how new/foreign referents get marked in Japanese generally.

**Personal names quiz (25 names):** every Western/non-Japanese personal name is rendered in
katakana (Carole→キャロル, Norman→ノーマン, Wendy→ウェンディー, Vanessa→ヴァネサ, Dwight→ドワイト,
Shane→シェーン, Julie→チュリー, Sophie→ソーフィ, William→ウィリアム, Jack→ジャック, David→デヴィッド,
Elizabeth→エリザベス, Russell→ラッセル), while every Japanese personal name is rendered in hiragana
(Jiro, Reiko, Teruo, Masayoshi, Akemi, Kenichi, Yukiko, Orie, Tsutomu, Hidemi, Sawako, Nozomu) —
the same katakana/hiragana split as the flora/fauna quiz, applied to proper nouns for people. (This
quiz's own pedagogical point was different — spotting a single deliberately-inserted *kana* error
per name — but the script-choice pattern across all 25 names is itself incidental, consistent,
book-wide evidence worth recording here.)

## Grammar point: "About Japan" reading passage

Printed pp. 111–112 (PDF 110–111) give a two-page continuous kana-only passage (a hiragana/katakana
mix, entirely without kanji, consistent with this book's own scope) as a final copying/reading
exercise — the only connected-prose sample in the whole book, as opposed to isolated vocabulary
drills. Per copyright discipline this is paraphrased, not reproduced: the passage describes Japan's
basic geography — lying north-south east of the Asian mainland, roughly California-sized, consisting
of four main islands. The largest, Honshu, has Tokyo at its midpoint and Mt. Fuji on it. The second
largest, Hokkaido, is the northernmost island, cool in summer and snowy enough in winter for skiing
and skating. The third largest, Kyushu, lies to the south and stays warm even in winter. The fourth,
Shikoku, is known for mikan oranges from its northern half and fish from its southern half. The
book's own published English translation (p. 117) is given as a reference aid for students, not
reproduced verbatim here beyond this paraphrase.

## Grammar point: the *Iroha* verse

Printed p. 120 (PDF 119) presents the *iroha* verse, a roughly thousand-year-old Buddhist-teaching-
derived poem used for centuries as a kana-writing pangram: it is composed so that every one of the
46 basic kana symbols appears exactly once (with the historical exception that archaic む stood in
for today's ん). Its symbol order (いろはにほへと...) was historically used as an alternative
ordering convention to the standard *gojūonjun*, in a role comparable to "a, b, c" in English, and
according to the book is still occasionally seen in that role today. The book gives a short, two-
line literal paraphrase of the verse's meaning (color/fragrance fades, nothing in this world lasts,
today is the day to rise above worldly illusion and leave shallow dreaming/intoxication behind),
sourced by the book itself to A. Nelson's *Japanese-English Character Dictionary* (Tuttle, 1962) —
not reproduced at full length here, consistent with copyright discipline. The verse itself is not
transcribed in this file (brief poetic source text, available in the original if needed for
mechanics analysis), but its existence and function as the kana system's own classical pangram is
worth recording as a cultural/historical fact relevant to later Japanese-slang synthesis work (it is
the kind of fixed cultural reference a sci-fi conlang's in-universe culture could plausibly echo or
subvert).

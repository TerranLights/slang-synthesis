**Morpheme breakdown.** Secret #1's clipping/hybrid-compound items are themselves the
morphologically interesting content: Japanese productively forms new words by concatenating the
first mora(e) of two or more source words/morphemes, a pattern distinct from ordinary compounding.
Examples already in the table above double as morpheme breakdowns:

> **朝シャンする (asashan suru)** = asa (morning) + shan(pū) (shampoo) + suru (to do) — a
> wasei-compound coining a new verb from a native noun-fragment + loanword-fragment + the light
> verb suru.
>
> **脱サラする (datsusara suru)** = datsu- (prefix: "escape from") + sara(rīman) (salaryman) +
> suru — same pattern, kanji prefix instead of native noun fragment.
>
> **スポ根 (supokon)** = supo(ōtsu) (sports, loanword fragment) + kon(jō) (fighting spirit, kanji
> fragment) — a loanword-fragment + kanji-fragment hybrid, the "ultimate oddity" class the source
> calls out explicitly (p.2).

## Grammar points

### Clipping/abbreviation as a productive word-formation process (Secret #1)
Japanese systematically shortens long kanji compounds by dropping every second character (国際連合
→ 国連) and shortens loanwords/foreign names to roughly four morae (パーソナルコンピューター →
パソコン). The source frames this as more than a quirk: register shifts accompany the clipping
(インテリ "intelligent person" picks up a pejorative "egghead" nuance the full English term lacks;
セクハラ sounds "chirpy" rather than solemn). A further productive subtype hybridizes a native/kanji
fragment with a loanword fragment in a single new compound (スポ根, 外タレ, 朝シャンする). (pp. 1–3)

### The 〜にチャレンジする "attempt X" explanatory-phrase construction (Secret #2)
Secret #2 teaches a general paraphrase strategy for expressing "to attempt/take on (a challenging
thing)" using the loanword charenji (challenge) as a suru-verb governed by the particle に, so a
student can productively build this phrase with any noun object rather than memorizing it item by
item. The chapter frames this as one instance of a broader "explanatory phrase" technique: rather
than searching for one single native verb, build the meaning compositionally from a loanword/light-
verb skeleton. (pp. 10–13)

### Large-number reading: the 万 (10,000)-based grouping system (Secret #4)
Japanese groups large numbers in units of 万 (10,000) rather than thousands, so a number is read off
by marking digits in groups of four from the right (一, 万, 億, 兆) instead of groups of three. This
is presented as the single highest-value fluency fix in the book for any learner trying to state
statistics/prices in real time — the chapter is built entirely around drilling this regrouping
habit rather than new vocabulary. (pp. 19–23)

### The four(+one) conditional forms: -tara, -(r)eba, -to, -nara, -kagiri (Secret #5)
Presented via a continuous manga dialogue (a ransom negotiation during a shelter lock-in), the
chapter distinguishes: **-tara** (a single hypothetical/sequential condition, "if X happens, then
Y" — often used for counterfactual or one-off conditions); **-(r)eba** (a more general/logical "if"
usable for rules and habitual conditions); **-to** (used for automatic, mechanical, or inevitable
consequences — "whenever X, Y necessarily follows," not used for one-off personal requests);
**-nara** (conditions on the topic/premise itself — "if that's the case..." — often used to react to
something already said); and the more advanced **-kagiri** ("as long as / unless," marking a
boundary condition). The chapter's own worked examples show these are not freely interchangeable —
each has a distinct pragmatic shade the author ties to a specific manga panel. (pp. 24–33)

### Five productive strategies for building synonyms/paraphrases (Secret #6)
Framed as a "synonym generator" for learners who freeze when they don't know the one "correct"
word: (1) **simple synonym substitution**; (2) **metaphor** (describing the target concept via an
analogous image); (3) **exaggeration/hyperbole**; (4) **euphemism/understatement**; (5)
**comparison**; and (6) **a deliberately fantastic/outlandish statement**. The chapter's worked
table pairs ~20+ difficult target concepts (abstract nouns, idioms, cultural references) with
example paraphrases built using each strategy, modeling the technique rather than vocabulary to
memorize outright — in line with copyright discipline, the specific worked paraphrase pairs are not
bulk-reproduced here beyond the strategy names themselves and one illustrative, already-public
example (the biblical "parable" item, p.64, whose margin carried the excluded handwritten note).
(pp. 60–69)

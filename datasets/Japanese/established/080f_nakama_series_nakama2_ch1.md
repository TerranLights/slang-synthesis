## Grammar points

### I. Expressing capability using the potential form of verbs

Potential forms express ability; the direct object marker を is replaced by が (手がうごかせません
"I cannot move my hand"). う-verbs: dictionary-form final /u/ → /e/る (すう→すえる, 走る→走れる).
る-verbs: る→られる (忘れる→忘れられる). Irregular: する→出来る, 来る→来られる. All potential forms
then conjugate as regular る-verbs. (p. 48–51)

### II. Expressing excessiveness using 〜すぎる

Verb-stem/い-adjective-stem/な-adjective-stem + すぎる ("too much, excessively"); いい is irregular
→ よすぎる. すぎる itself conjugates as a る-verb (しすぎない／しすぎます／しすぎる／しすぎて).
(p. 51–53)

### III. Giving suggestions using 〜たらどうですか and 〜方がいいです

Both give advice, but the source draws an **explicit strength gradient**: 〜方がいいです ("it's
better to ~, you should ~") is stated to be **stronger** than 〜たらどうですか ("how about ~, why
don't you ~"). 〜たら is built from the plain past affirmative + ら (a conditional form reused
across later chapters); substituting でしょうか for ですか softens/politens the suggestion further.
〜方がいいです takes the plain past affirmative (のった方がいい "you should ride") or plain present
negative (のらない方がいい "you shouldn't ride"). One drill activity explicitly separates register:
get suggestions "in casual speech" then "report them in formal speech," underscoring that this
grammar point is taught as register-flexible by design. (p. 53–55)

### IV. Describing efforts toward a goal using 〜ように

The clause before ように takes the plain present form, typically negative, potential, or
non-intentional (風邪をひかないように、あたたかくする "I keep warm so as not to catch a cold"); the
main clause states the effort made toward that state. (p. 55–57)

### V. Negative requests (〜ないで下さい); prohibition (〜てはいけない); permission (〜てもいい)

Three related て-form-based constructions with an explicit politeness/formality scale:

**〜ないで下さい** ("please don't ~"): the source explicitly notes **下さい is dropped in casual
speech** — つかれている時にはむりしないで。("Don't overstrain yourself when you're tired," no 下さい).

**〜てはいけない** ("must not ~," used for rules): the source gives its **explicit colloquial
contraction, 〜ちゃ・じゃいけない** (おそくまで起きていちゃだめです "You mustn't stay up late" —
ていて→ちゃ) — this is a directly source-documented register-conditioned phonological contraction,
structurally parallel to Ch.11's て-form+いる→てる contraction, and worth tracking alongside it as a
recurring colloquial-contraction pattern in the textbook's own grammar (not slang per se, but the
kind of reduction slang formation plausibly piggybacks on).

**〜てもいい** ("may I ~? / it's OK to ~"): the source gives an **explicit three-way politeness
ranking** for permission-requests: 〜てもいいですか (baseline) < 〜てもいいでしょうか (more tentative/
polite, "but can also be used toward someone in one's peer group") ≈ 〜てもかまいませんか ("as polite
as" でしょうか). Positive-response register is also explicitly graded: はい、いいです is flagged as
**only appropriate from a social superior granting permission to an inferior** — using it upward
would be a register mismatch. Declining a request directly is described as pragmatically dispreferred
in Japanese (paralleling Nakama 1 Ch.6's invitation-decline softening): the recommended indirect
refusal forms (すみませんが、ちょっと。／ええっと、それはちょっと。／すみません、それはちょっと
こまります。) all lean on ちょっと as a tension-deflecting softener, explicitly glossed by the source
as serving that pragmatic function. (p. 58–61)

## Grammar points

### Standard-language formation and its dialectal cost (Meiji-era language planning)

Japan had no single prestige dialect before the Meiji Restoration (1868); Kyoto dialect was the
pre-Meiji lingua franca by prestige of its upper-class speakers, with Edo (Tokyo) dialect emerging
as a rival center from the mid-18th century. The National Language Research Council (est. 1902)
formally designated the educated Tokyo/Yamanote dialect as the standard (*hyōjungo*) in 1916,
after the Ministry of Education had already stipulated Tokyo speech for school textbooks from 1901.
NHK (national broadcaster) then became the primary vehicle for *spreading* the spoken standard via
radio from the 1920s onward. Local dialects were actively suppressed in schools through the first
half of the 20th century (children punished/ridiculed for using them), with official curriculum
guidance explicitly favoring "correct forms" as late as 1947 and 1951, before a 1990s policy
reversal toward tolerance (though not active promotion) of regional dialects (pp. 7–11).

### Gendered honorific-use convergence (a documented real-time change)

A 1952 National Language Council report *criticized* women for *overusing* honorifics/euphemisms.
A comparable early-1990s investigation found "almost no difference" between men's and women's
honorific usage by that point (Kokugo Shingikai 1995) — i.e. the traditionally wider gender gap in
politeness-marking had measurably narrowed within about 40 years, a dated sociolinguistic-change
finding directly analogous to the *boku*-adoption-by-women case above (pp. 13–14).

### Euphemism-treadmill pattern across six independent minority groups

Six separate case studies in this chapter (Burakumin, people with disabilities, women, Ainu,
ethnic Koreans, and briefly Chinese/foreigners/gays) each independently document the same
recurring cycle: (1) an originally-neutral or merely descriptive term acquires derogatory loading
through accumulated discriminatory usage context; (2) organized protest (often explicitly modeled
on the Burakumin Liberation League's pioneering *kyūdan* denunciation tactic, which other groups
consciously borrowed); (3) adoption of a substitute term, sometimes from outside (media
self-censorship lists) and sometimes self-chosen by the group itself (*Utari*, *buraku jūmin*);
(4) the substitute itself eventually accumulating the same stigma and requiring a further
replacement (the *eta* → *shinheimin* → *burakumin* → *buraku jūmin* chain is the fullest
documented instance). This is a genuine cross-group structural pattern the source itself names and
compares explicitly, directly useful as source evidence for this project's `euphemism_chain`
derivation-type mechanic.

### Self-censorship "word-hunting" backlash as a distinct phenomenon from the original complaints

A second-order controversy, separate from the original minority-rights complaints, emerged once
media self-censorship word-lists became publicly known: critics (including prominent novelists)
accused publishers/broadcasters of unacceptable "word-hunting" (*kotobagari*) infringing on
literary/artistic freedom. The chapter presents the 1993 Tsutsui Yasutaka incident (a science-
fiction story pulled from a textbook over an epilepsy-advocacy complaint, leading the author to
briefly announce he'd stop writing in protest) as the signature case, and frames the overall
effect of the self-censorship era as media simply *avoiding* discrimination-related topics
altogether rather than actually improving public discourse — a documented unintended consequence
(pp. 105–108, citing Kawamoto 1995's three-way typology of media responses to complaints).

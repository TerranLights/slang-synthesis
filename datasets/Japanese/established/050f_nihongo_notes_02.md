## Grammar points

### Condolence/sympathy register is graded by severity, and fluency can backfire
Japanese has a graded set of sympathy formulas keyed to severity — *Zannen-desu-ne* (mere
disappointment), *Taihen-desu-ne* (minor trouble), *Sore-wa ikemasen-ne. Odaiji-ni* (illness/injury),
and *Goshuushoosama-desu*/trailing *Kono tabi-wa...* plus nonverbal cues (death/bereavement) —
misapplying a lower-severity formula to a higher-severity event (as Mr. Lerner does, using
*Zannen-desu-ne* for a traffic accident) is a real register error, not a harmless approximation. For
the most severe cases, fluent/complete verbal expression is explicitly discouraged in favor of
trailing-off speech plus nonverbal gesture (pp.104-105).

### *mo* ("also/too") marking non-uniqueness vs. *wa* marking a specific/contrastive case
*mo* used without an explicit paired counterpart in context generalizes the speaker's statement as one
instance among an unspecified set of similar cases, producing a reflective or "philosophical" tone;
*wa* singles out the speaker's case as specific/contrastive. This is a genuine semantic choice, not
free variation (pp.108-109).

### *-ta/-da* as a completed-action marker, not strictly a past-tense marker
Japanese *-ta*/*-da* verb forms mark that an action is complete as of the reference point — which can
itself be in the future (*ashita okita toki-ni...*, "when I have woken up tomorrow") — making them
closer to a resultant/completive aspect marker than a literal tense marker (p.112-113, reinforced by
the *-te imasu* discussion at pp.126-127).

### *-te imasu*: progressive action vs. resultant state, and its incompatibility with future plans
*-te imasu* covers two distinct functions that don't map onto a single English gloss: an action
genuinely in progress, or a state resulting from a completed action (including verbs like *shinu*
"die" and *taoreru* "fall," which can ONLY take the resultant-state reading). English speakers'
instinct to use *-te imasu* for future plans (paralleling English "-ing" for future reference)
produces genuinely wrong/strange Japanese; plain-present or *tsumori-desu* ("intend to") framings are
required instead (pp.126-127).

### Gendered sentence-final particles: *wa*, *no* vs. universal *ne*, *yo*
*Ne* and *yo* are usable by any speaker regardless of age/sex/register, but *wa* and *no* carry real
gender and pitch constraints — *wa* is primarily feminine (high pitch), used by men only rarely and
with low pitch; *no* is primarily feminine, with tone (falling vs. rising) distinguishing an
explanatory statement from a question, and men use it only in the question sense. Several other
particles (*na*, *sa*, *zo*, *ze*) are similarly register/gender-restricted and require care
(pp.122-123).

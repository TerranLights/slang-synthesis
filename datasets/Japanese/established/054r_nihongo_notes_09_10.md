## Grammar points

### Wa vs. mo as listener-anticipation cues (p.114-115)
Beyond their core topic/inclusion meanings, *wa* and *mo* function as processing cues: a clause ending
in contrastive *wa* primes a native listener to anticipate an upcoming contrasting statement, while a
clause ending in *mo* primes anticipation of a continuing/similar statement. This is explicitly framed
as a listening-comprehension mechanism — a native speaker's fluent, low-effort listening partly
depends on this kind of anticipation, which is why a foreign learner's "wrong" particle choice (using
*wa* when a continuation was intended) can genuinely throw off a native listener, not just sound
slightly off.

### To omou/omoimasu, and the soo/koo/doo exception (p.116-117)
*...to omou/omoimasu* is the standard "I think that..." construction. The quotative *to* is dropped
when *omoimasu* directly follows *soo*, *koo*, or *doo* — but restored if *da* is inserted after one
of those words (*soo-da-to omoimasu*). The negative *nai* combines with *to omou* the same way an
affirmative predicate does; negating *omou* itself instead of the embedded clause is grammatical but
uncommon, reserved for contexts emphasizing the negation as the main point.

### Ni vs. kara marking a start time: point-in-time vs. ongoing-duration (p.118-119)
*Ni* (as in *sanji-ni*) marks interest in exactly when something starts. *Kara* (as in *sanji-kara*)
emphasizes that the action starts at that point and continues on — and is so strongly associated with
*hajimaru/hajimeru* ("to start") that it can stand alone marking continued action without any explicit
start-verb at all, and extends the same logic to marking a starting point in space. The chapter notes
this is the specific source of a well-known Japanese-English transfer error in the reverse direction:
Japanese English-learners saying "School starts from nine" instead of "at nine."

### Negative-framed existential search: dareka...inai-ka-to omotte (p.120-121)
When searching for something/someone the speaker hopes or expects to find, the negative-framed
construction (*inai*, "doesn't exist") is the more natural choice over the affirmative (*iru*, "exists"),
which instead reads as a neutral inquiry with no particular expectation. This negative-framing pattern
extends from everyday searches (a spare ticket, something to eat) to official investigative contexts
(police searching for witnesses) and to general wishes (*nanika ii koto nai-ka-naa*, "I wish something
nice would happen").

### Nanishiro vs. tonikaku: emphatic excuse-giving vs. action-proposing "whatever it may be" (p.122-123)
Both mean roughly "whatever it may be" and emphasize the speaker's point, and *tonikaku* can substitute
for *nanishiro* in most of its uses. But *tonikaku* additionally covers proposing an action or
requesting the listener do something — a use *nanishiro* specifically cannot cover, making the two not
fully interchangeable despite their overlap.

### Nandaka: literal "what is it" vs. "somehow, for an unknown reason" (p.124-125)
*Nandaka* literally asks "what it is," but placed before an expression of feeling it means "somehow —
for a reason that isn't known," restricted to familiar conversation, and is frequently followed by the
set collocation *...ki-ga suru* ("I feel/have a hunch that..."). *Nan-to-naku* covers the same
"somehow" sense in more formal/polite registers, including letters, where *nandaka* would be too
casual.

## Grammar points

### yoku iku as a habitual-place marker
On p.40, the book explains that **yoku iku** ("often go") attaches to a place noun phrase to mean
"a place you go to often," illustrated with *kurabu-ni yoku iku* ("a club you often go to").

### Gendered self-reference and sentence-final particles (♂/♀)
Throughout this entire page range the book systematically marks parallel phrase pairs with ♂ (male
speaker) and ♀ (female speaker) where the Japanese differs — primarily via the first-person pronoun
(*atashi* for women vs. *boku* for men) and via sentence-final particles (*-wa*/*-yo*/*-ne*/*-na*
distributed by gender norm). This is the single highest-density explicit register annotation in the
source and has been captured per-row in the Notes column above rather than summarized once.

### Intonation as a grammatical/semantic device
p.49 presents ***Dō-shiyō-ka?*** as a worked example of suprasegmental grammar: the same string,
with rising+interested intonation, means "What sounds good?" (an invitation), while flat+concerned
intonation turns it into "Now what are we going to do?" (dismay at a problem). The book treats
intonation here as doing real grammatical work, not just coloring tone.

### Euphemistic semantic shift: *asoko*
p.61 explicitly glosses **asoko** ("over there," a spatial deictic) as colloquially repurposed to
mean "the private parts" — a clean synonym-shift euphemism pattern, paired with the book's own note
that Japanese women in particular prefer this vague term and indirect allusion over anatomical
vocabulary.

### Register stratification within taboo vocabulary
The "Making Love" chapter (pp. 56–65 so far) repeatedly stacks near-synonyms at different vulgarity
levels for the same referent and explicitly ranks them — e.g. *fera* (explicitly called "more
vulgar") vs. *sakku* (recommended for use with a girlfriend) for oral sex (p.62); *omanko* (Kantō)
vs. *omeko* (Kansai) for vulva, an explicit regional/dialectal split (p.61); and the general
male-only ("♂") vocabulary set the book flags as "pretty vulgar" and inappropriate around women
(p.62). These rankings are preserved verbatim in the Notes column per entry since they are exactly
the kind of explicit sociolinguistic annotation the extraction spec treats as highest-value content.

### Clipping as slang formation: *rabu ho*
p.57 gives **rabu hoteru** ("love hotel," itself an English loan compound) alongside its clipped
colloquial form **rabu ho** — a clean, explicitly-labeled example of phonological
reduction/clipping slang formation in this source.

### The "letter-base" euphemism system (A/B/C)
p.66 presents a graded euphemism system unique in this chunk: the letters **A**, **B**, and **C**
stand in for "kissing," "petting," and "making love" respectively, explicitly compared by the book
to American English's baseball-metaphor slang ("first base" through "home run"). The example *A
shita* ("I did A," i.e., "I kissed [her/him]") shows the letter functioning as a bound verb-taking
euphemism slot — a borrowed-alphabet euphemism_chain pattern worth flagging for the mechanics/
analysis phase.

### Asymmetric antonym pair: *nampa* / *kōha*
p.66 also gives **nampa** ("dishonest person," extended to mean a casual/promiscuous pickup artist)
against its coined opposite **kōha** ("honest person," someone who waits for marriage). The book
explicitly flags that usage is asymmetric: *nampa-shita* ("I scored/went girl-hunting") is a normal
male-marked phrase, but the parallel *kōha-shita* "doesn't mean anything" — the antonym pair is not
symmetrically productive, a notable gap worth preserving for later derivation-mechanism analysis.

### Euphemistic indirection for a sensitive topic: abortion
p.73's **byōin-e ikō** ("let's go to the hospital") is used euphemistically to mean "let's have the
abortion" — paired with the book's explicit cultural note that abortion (*ryūzan*) is a socially
accepted form of contraception in Japan, so blasé attitudes toward it shouldn't surprise a learner.
This is a clean semantic-shift euphemism example structurally parallel to *asoko* (see above).

### Graded indirectness scale for marriage proposals
pp. 76 explicitly ranks four substitute phrases for "will you marry me?" from subtlest to most
direct: asking her to make miso soup for him → asking her to take his last name → asking to share
the rest of their lives together → asking her to have his baby. The book itself labels this as a
"subtle to direct" ladder — a clear, source-authored register/indirectness gradient, not something
inferred by the extractor.

### Explicit sexism/register note on women's work vocabulary
p.83 explicitly states Japan's job-vocabulary hierarchy is gendered: women's typical job answers
(**OL** "office lady," **shufu** "housewife," **arubaito** "part-time work") are framed by the book
itself as lower-ranking than men's typical answers (**kaishain**, **sarariiman**, **ginkōin**), and
the book cautions against pushing a woman who gives the "arubaito" answer for more detail since she
"may be embarrassed." This is the book's own sociolinguistic commentary, preserved here rather than
silently normalized.

## Grammar points (Unit 4)

### Ordinal numbers via じかん + め

To specify which class period a subject falls in, add じかん then め to the cardinal number: いち
+ じかん + め = いちじかんめ ("1st period"). め is explicitly identified as the general
cardinal-to-ordinal suffix, not specific to periods alone. (p.79-80)

### A further numeral-irregularity case: よ vs よん before ねんせい

4th grade must be よねんせい, never よんねんせい — a third documented instance (alongside Unit
2's よん/なな-for-4/7 and the く-avoidance for 9) of Japanese numerals having context-specific
irregular readings that must be memorized per-collocation rather than derived from a single general
rule. Worth flagging for the mechanics-analysis phase as a recurring micro-pattern, not a one-off. (p.79)

### Conjunction と ("and," noun-only)

と joins two nouns only (not clauses/sentences) — e.g. ご-じかんめ と ろく-じかんめ ("5th period
and 6th period"). (p.80)

### Avoidance of second-person pronouns in favor of names

A genuine, explicitly source-marked sociolinguistic/pragmatic finding: when asking "what is your
favourite subject," Japanese omits any word for "your" entirely — personal pronouns for "you" are
avoided in general, with the addressee's name substituted instead when not speaking face-to-face.
Face-to-face, no referent at all is needed. This is presented as a general discourse habit, directly
relevant to later analysis of how address/reference terms (a frequent site of slang formation in many
languages) work differently in Japanese. (p.69)

### Language-name formation: country name + ご, with English as the exception

Parallel to the Unit 3 nationality pattern (country + じん), languages are named as country + ご.
English breaks the pattern: えいご, not イギリスご, because England's formal/classical name is
えいこく, not the katakana loanword イギリス used for "England" as a place name. This is a second
documented place-name-vs-formal-name split (after the ちゅうごくじん exception in Unit 3). (p.69)

### こくご vs にほんご — "Japanese" has two words depending on perspective

こくご ("national language") is the subject name used for Japanese when taught to native speakers
inside Japan, while にほんご ("Japan-language") is used when Japanese is being taught/learned as a
foreign or second language. This is an explicit register/perspective distinction within the course's
own content (this book itself teaches にほんご, by its own logic). (p.60)

## Grammar points (Unit 5)

### Topic + adjective + です, and question/agreement/disagreement

The basic descriptive-comment pattern is [topic] は [adjective] です (たいいくは たのしい です,
"P.E. is fun"); か makes it a question; responses agree with はい + adjective or disagree with
いいえ + a contrasting adjective. (p.99)でも links two contrasting clauses ("X is difficult, but
interesting"). (p.99)

### Assertive sentence-final particle よ, restricted by social hierarchy

A directly source-marked pragmatics/register finding of high relevance to this project: よ glossed
as "I'm telling you" — an assertion marker always sentence-final — comes with an explicit warning
that it "would be better to avoid using it to teachers and adults" because it can sound too
assertive/presumptuous toward a social superior. This is a clean, explicit example of a
grammaticalized particle whose use is itself socially register-restricted, independent of lexical
choice — directly useful groundwork for later mechanics analysis of how register constraints can
attach to function words, not just vocabulary. (p.100)

### Politeness by ellipsis: avoiding まずい via "[food] wa ちょっと..."

Another explicit, high-value sociolinguistic finding: directly saying food is まずい ("bad/
unpleasant-tasting") is marked as rude. The polite workaround is to say only "[food name] は
ちょっと..." ("[food] is a bit...") and leave the rest of the sentence unsaid, with the negative
judgment left to be inferred. This is a textbook example of negative-politeness-via-deliberate-
incompleteness as a register strategy — worth flagging explicitly for the slang/register mechanics
phase, since elliptical understatement of this kind is a recurring cross-linguistic politeness device. (p.108-109)

### Thank-you register pair: ありがとう vs ありがとう ございます

Mirrors the greeting-register pairs already seen in Units 1 and 3: ありがとう is the informal form;
ありがとう ございます is explicitly required when thanking someone senior/older. (p.108)

## Grammar points (Unit 6)

### -te verb + ください/てもいいですか: request and permission constructions

A single -te-form verb base (あけて、かいて、みて, etc.) supports two parallel constructions:
+ ください = "please [do]" (request), and + も いい ですか = "may I [do]?" (permission). The
particle を marks the object of the request verb, placed before the verb. (p.120, 128)

### どうぞ is historically short for どうぞ...ください

The course explicitly traces どうぞ (the general offering word) as an elided/contracted form of
どうぞ [request] ください — i.e. a fixed, maximally-general politeness particle that was
historically the first half of a fuller request construction. A further example of grammaticalized/
fossilized phrase-shortening parallel to the Unit 3 finding about opaque ritual expressions. (p.119)

### がんばって as a cross-linguistically exported colloquialism

がんばって (short for がんばってください, "try hard/don't give up") is flagged here as a notable
real-world case of a Japanese colloquial encouragement expression that has itself become a loanword/
catchphrase recognized by non-Japanese-speaking audiences (via anime/fandom exposure) — directly
relevant precedent for how this project's later synthesis phase might think about a conlang
expression "escaping" into a contact language's slang. (p.128)

### Confirmation particle ね

ね is a sentence-final particle inviting the listener's agreement ("isn't it?") — distinguished here
from よ (Unit 5's assertion particle) as a second, functionally opposite type of sentence-final
pragmatic particle: ね solicits agreement/softens a claim, よ asserts/states more forcefully (and is
register-restricted against superiors). Worth tracking both as a contrasting pair for later mechanics
analysis of Japanese's sentence-final-particle system generally. (p.119)

## Grammar points

### I. Expressing ongoing and repeated actions using て-form + いる

Building on Ch. 10's resultant-state usage (田中さんはめがねをかけている "Mr. Tanaka has glasses
on"), て-form + いる also expresses **ongoing action** (雨がふっている "it is raining") and
**repeated/habitual action** (鈴木さんはよくあかいジャケットをきている "Ms. Suzuki often wears a red
jacket"). **Register note, directly relevant to the slang-mechanics phase:** て-form + いる is
explicitly described as commonly contracted to **て-form + る** in conversation ("in both formal and
casual styles") — けっこんしています → けっこんしてます; しっている → しってる — a productive,
source-documented phonological reduction pattern, not limited to slang but a plausible seed for
further colloquial compression. (p. 440–442)

### II. Plain past forms and casual speech — **high-priority grammar point**

**Plain past forms:** verbs form the plain past by replacing the て-form's て/で with た/だ
(つづいて→つづいた, 読んで→読んだ; irregular する→した, 来る→来た). い-adjectives drop です from the
polite past (暑かったです→暑かった; いい→よかった). な-adjectives/copula/noun+copula add った to the
plain present (好きだ→好きだった; 台風だ→台風だった). Plain past negative uniformly replaces ない
with なかった across all word classes (行かない→行かなかった; わるくない→わるくなかった;
元気じゃない→元気じゃなかった).

**Casual speech — the source's explicit social-register model, paraphrased in full since this is the
chapter's core finding for this project:**

- Plain forms mark **closeness, intimacy, carefree attitude** between close friends/family; です／
  ます marks that **the speaker is consciously maintaining social distance / awareness of the
  listener's presence.**
- **Register choice is not simply mapped to formality of situation** — the source gives a
  deliberately counter-intuitive example: at a casual home party, a speaker may still use です／ます
  toward someone of higher status/age specifically to signal they do *not* consider that person a
  true social equal; conversely a social superior may use **plain form downward** toward a junior to
  signal closeness. Register tracks **perceived social relationship, not setting formality.**
- It is common for two same-age-group strangers to start a conversation in です／ます and **switch to
  plain form as the conversation progresses** — a live, real-time register shift the speakers
  negotiate mid-conversation.
- Asymmetric register is itself conventional: a teacher may use plain form toward students (signaling
  familiarity) while students use です／ます toward the teacher (signaling respect) **in the very
  same conversation** — the two parties are not using mirrored registers.

**Concrete casual-speech surface markers the source itemizes** (all directly load-bearing for later
slang-mechanics comparison):

- **Particle deletion**: は, が, を are frequently dropped in casual conversation (今日、すずしいね。
  "It's cool today" — no は; 雨、ふった。"It rained" — no が).
- **か deletion in questions**: the question marker か is commonly omitted in casual speech, with a
  rising intonation substituting for it (寒い？ "Cold?").
- **Copula だ deletion**: だ (plain copula, including in な-adjective predicates) is dropped before
  sentence-final particles ね／よ — and the source explicitly notes **deleting だ before these
  particles makes the speech sound feminine** (大丈夫？／うん、大丈夫（だ）よ。) — an explicit
  gendered-register marker tied to a specific morphosyntactic deletion.
- **んです → んだ／の**: の is typical in casual questions, んだ or の in casual statements
  (昨日どうしたの？／あ、病気で寝てたんだ。).
- **てください → て**: the polite request suffix is simply truncated to the bare て-form in casual
  speech (わるいけど、あれとって。"Sorry, but can you take that?").
- **はい／ええ → うん; いいえ → ううん** (captured as vocabulary entries above).

(p. 444–448)

### III. Describing characteristics of places/objects/time using 〜は〜が

Extends Ch. 10's 〜は〜が (physical-appearance description) to general characteristics/commentary:
は marks the topic being characterized, が marks the specific characteristic ("as for 〜, 〜 [is the
salient trait]") — 東京はものが高い "Things are expensive in Tokyo," 日本は山が多い "Japan is
mountainous." One drill activity explicitly instructs students to "speak casually" while using this
pattern, reinforcing that the grammar point itself is register-neutral and gets layered with the
casual-speech markers from Grammar II in natural use. (p. 450–452)

### IV. Expressing manner of action / outcome of change using adverbial forms + に

The adverbial form (い-adjective's く-form; な-adjective's に-form) modifies verbs to express manner
(元気にあるいて下さい "please walk cheerfully") or, combined with する/なる, change of state:
adverbial-form + する = "to make X [adjective]," adverbial-form + なる = "to become [adjective]"
(しずかにする "to make quiet" vs. しずかになる "to become quiet"). One drill activity again
explicitly instructs "Use casual speech." (p. 453–456)

### V. Expressing uncertainty using 〜でしょう, 〜かもしれない, 〜かな

Three conjecture markers encode different certainty levels and different intended audiences, all
attaching to the plain form (だ is dropped before them for な-adjectives/copula): **〜でしょう**
("probably," highest confidence of the three, often used toward a listener), **〜かもしれない**
("maybe," lower confidence), **〜かな** ("I wonder," self-directed/musing register, lowest
commitment to an addressee). The source presents a full present/negative/past paradigm for each
(verb くもる, い-adjective 寒い, な-adjective いや) showing all three attach uniformly across word
classes once converted to plain form. This is a second register-relevant construction after Grammar
II: か な specifically marks **self-directed or musing speech**, distinct from both the two
other-directed inference markers and from the plain/polite social-distance axis of Grammar II.
(p. 456+)

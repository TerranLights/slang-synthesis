## Grammar points

### Intention (*tsumori*) and plans (*yotei*) (Unit 10)
`tsumori` ("intention") after the dictionary form of a verb expresses what the speaker intends to
do; the source flags it as a subjective expression generally reserved for one's own intentions,
needing a hedge like `sóo desu` when reporting someone else's. `yotei` ("plan") works similarly but
is more neutral/objective. (pp. 165-166)

### "Thinking of doing" (*-(y)óo to omou*) and "about to do" (Unit 10)
The hortative/propositive `-(y)óo` suffix (met earlier on `-mashóo`) attaches to the bare verb root
and combines with a verb of thinking (`omou`) to mean "I'm thinking of doing..." The same suffix
with `suru` means "trying to" or "about to," contrasted explicitly later (Unit 12) with `-te míru`
("try and succeed/see"). "About to..." is also commonly expressed with the noun `tokoro` ("point").
(pp. 165-167)

### Giving advice with *ikága*/*dóo* + conditional (Unit 10)
`ikága desu ka` (polite "how about...?") or its neutral/informal equivalent `dóo desu ka` combine
with a conditional verb form to suggest "how about..."/"why not...?" — an explicit honorific/neutral
register pair, with the source noting it's safer to default to politeness since suggestions can be
misread. Less formally, the bare `-(r)éba` ending alone can make a suggestion (`Hitóri de ikéba.`,
"Why don't you go alone?"). (pp. 167-168)

### Potential verbs (Unit 10)
Beyond `kotó ga dekimásu`, Japanese consonant-root verbs form a dedicated potential by replacing
final `-u` with `-eru` (`káku`→`kakéru`, "able to write"); as stative verbs these mark their object
with `ga`, not `o`. Vowel-root verbs use `-rareru` (identical in form to the passive ending);
irregular `kúru`→`koraréru`; `suru` has no potential form, using `dekíru` instead. Some potential
meanings are instead carried by dedicated intransitive verbs: `miéru` (able to see/visible),
`kikoeru` (able to hear/audible), `mitsukaru` (able to find/findable). (pp. 169-170)

### Possibility (*kámo shiremasen*) vs. probability (*deshóo*) (Unit 10)
`deshóo` after a plain verb expresses probability; `kámo shiremasen` (lit. "whether or not we
cannot know") expresses a weaker possibility ("might/may"). Sentence-initial adverbs often flag
which is coming: `móshi` for conditionals, `tabun` for `deshóo`-probability, `moshikashitára` for
`kámo shiremasen`-possibility. (pp. 169-170)

### Explanatory *n' desu*/*no desu* (Unit 10)
Appended after a plain verb form, this gives a sentence an explanatory, context-linking force
("you see," "the fact is") rather than stating a bare fact — contrasted with a minimal pair:
`ashí ga itái desu` (simple statement) vs. `ashí ga itái n' desu` (explanation, e.g. answering "why
are you walking so slowly?"). Especially common in/after `dóoshite`/`náze`/colloquial `nánde`
("why") questions; the plain copula `da` becomes `na` before `n' desu`. (pp. 170-171)

### Extended demonstrative system: adverbs and "kind of" adjectives (Unit 10)
Demonstrative adverbs `kóo/sóo/áa/dóo` ("like this/that/that (over there)/how") parallel the
pronoun and adjective demonstrative series. A separate adjective series `konna/sonna/anna/dónna`
("this/that/that/what kind of") has colloquial equivalents `koo yuu/soo yuu/aa yuu/dóo yuu`
(`yuu` being a colloquial pronunciation of `iu`, "to say") — flagged explicitly as informal. These
adjectives convert to adverbs with `ni` (`konna ni`, "this much"). (pp. 172-173)

### Listing reasons with *shi* (Unit 10)
The particle `shi` joins clauses with a more emphatic sense than the plain `-te` form link — "and
what is more," "moreover" — often used to stack up multiple reasons supporting a claim. (pp. 173-174)

### The plain negative system (Unit 11)
Plain non-past negative `-(a)nai` attaches to the verb root (vowel-root verbs drop initial `-a`:
`tabénai`); irregular `kúru`/`suru` → `kónai`/`shinai`. Verbs whose dictionary form ends in `-au`/
`-ou` are actually consonant-root verbs with an underlying `-w` that surfaces only before `-a`
(`omowánai`, "not think"). Full paradigm: C-root `káku/kakánai/káita/kakánakatta`; V-root
`míru/mínai/míta/minákatta`; irregular-k `kúru/kónai/kíta/konákatta`; irregular-s
`suru/shinai/shita/shinákatta`; adjective `takái/tákaku nai/tákakatta/takaku nákatta`; copula
`da/ja nái/dátta/ja nákatta`. (pp. 180-181)

### Double-negative obligation (Unit 11)
`-(a)nákereba narimasen` (lit. "if one doesn't do X, it won't do") expresses plain obligation.
`-(a)nákereba ikemasen` carries a "slightly stronger connotation of moral responsibility" per the
source — an explicit register distinction. `damé desu` substituting for the negative verb is "more
emphatic" still, with "an even heavier connotation of moral obligation." Casual speech additionally
uses `-(a)nákute wa narimasen` or the explicitly-flagged-"very colloquial" `-(a)nákucha naranai`
(sometimes contracted further by dropping the final verb entirely, e.g. `Móo kaeranákucha!`, "Gotta
get going!"). `ikemasén` standalone ("Don't!"/"Stop it!") is explicitly noted as a common rebuke
to children. (pp. 182-183)

### Negative conditionals, prohibition, and negative requests (Unit 11)
Negative forms of `-tára`/`-(r)éba` are `-(a)nákattara`/`-(a)nákereba`. Prohibition (from Unit 7)
is `-te wa ikemasén`; permission is `-te mo íi desu`. Negative requests use `-(a)naide kudasai`
("please don't..."); more indirect alternatives include `goénryo kudasái` ("please refrain from"),
`shinai yóo ni shite kudasái` ("please try not to"), and `shinai yóo ki o tsukéte kudasái` ("be
careful not to"). (pp. 183-186)

### Deciding: *koto ni suru* (Unit 11)
`suru` ("to do") expresses "decide on" with a noun + `ni`; with a verb, the plain form + nominalizing
`kotó` + `ni suru` means "decide to..."; the negative plain form + `kotó ni suru` means "decide not
to..." (pp. 184-185)

### Reason with *no de*, and *nára* "if, as you say" (Unit 11)
`no de` after a plain verb gives a cause/effect relationship like `kara`, but is more formal, more
common in writing, and cannot appear with an imperative/interrogative/obligation-bearing main
clause; `no` often contracts to `n'` in speech. `nára` after a plain verb means "if, as you say" —
picking up and expanding on an assertion the addressee has made or is presumed to have made, dealing
with factual rather than hypothetical situations (contrasted with the hypothetical `to`/`-tára`/
`-(r)éba` conditionals). (pp. 187-188)

### "Without doing" (*-(a)náide*) and the negative -te form *-(a)nakute* (Unit 11)
`-(a)náide` joins clauses, often translating as "without" (`tabénaide ikimáshita`, "went without
eating"). A separate negative -te form, `-(a)nakute` (no initial `-a`), is the -te form of `nái`
itself and is the only negative -te form used with adjectives/descriptive nouns; it's used for
clause-joining (especially with different subjects or cause/effect) and in `-(a)nakute mo íi`
("need not..."). (pp. 188-189)

### Giving and receiving verbs — the core system (Unit 12)
Japanese verb choice for "give"/"receive" encodes relative social status and direction relative to
the speaker. In-giving (toward speaker/a third person): `kudasáru` (giver of higher status; in
practice often implies 2nd-person giver) vs. `kureru` (giver of equal/lower status; often implies
3rd-person giver) — source advises defaulting to `kudasáru` with people you don't know well.
Out-giving: `ageru` (neutral, regardless of recipient status), `sashiageru` (elevated respect/
decorum). `yaru` ("give to an inferior") is restricted mainly to one's own junior family members or
non-human recipients (pets). Receiving, paralleling the giving set: `itadaku` (from a superior,
often 1st-person receiver + 2nd-person giver) vs. `morau` (from a non-superior); giver/source is
marked with `ni` (occasionally `kara`). Status-neutral alternatives exist for contexts where
relative status isn't relevant: `ataeru`/`ukéru` (give/receive generally), `uketóru` (receive
letters/parcels specifically). (pp. 195-198)

### Giving/receiving verbs as auxiliaries after *-te* (Unit 12)
The same verb set attaches after `-te` to mark who benefits from an action: `-te kudasáru`
(superior does something for me/mine), `-te kureru` (an equal/non-superior does something for me),
`-te ageru` (I do something for someone else), `-te yaru` (I do something for my own child —
explicitly noted as *not* normally used by women or junior family members), `-te yatte kudasái`
(asking a favor on behalf of a family member/subordinate/pupil), `-te morau`/`-te itadaku` (I have
someone do something for me, with the agent marked by `ni`). Polite request forms layer the
potential + question: `-te itadakemásu ka` / politer `-te itadakemasén ka`. Desiderative forms
`-te itadakitái` (2nd/3rd person present) and `-te moraitái` (absent 3rd person) express "I'd like
you/him to..."; in status-neutral contexts `-te hoshíi` is used instead. (pp. 198-200)

### Further *-te*-form auxiliaries (Unit 12)
`-te míru` ("try doing, see what happens") — explicitly contrasted with `-(y)óo to suru` ("try to,
but may fail"): the source notes some English speakers already distinguish "I tried doing X (to see
what happened)" from "I tried to do X (but failed)," mapping respectively onto these two
constructions. `-te oku` = do in preparation/beforehand. `-te shimau` = end up doing/finish
completely, colloquially contracted to `-chau`, which the source notes some Tokyo speakers use
"indiscriminately" even without the completion nuance — an explicit colloquial/geographic register
note. `-te áru` = resultant state from a deliberate (usually human) action, requiring the object of
the underlying transitive verb to become the subject/topic; its negative is `-te nái`/formal `-te
arimasén`. `-te kúru` = action's effect approaches the present time/place; `-te iku` = effect
recedes from it. (pp. 201-205)

### Men's and women's speech in the plain style (Unit 12) — register flagship finding
The plain style (used among close friends/family/children) is where gendered speech differences are
most pronounced, per explicit source discussion. Women's forms: sentence-final `no` (question with
rising intonation, explanatory with falling), `káshira` ("I wonder"), assertive `wa`; exclamations
`sóo yo`/`sóo na no yo` ("that's right") are "typically feminine." Men's forms: first-person `boku`
(colloquial) or the source-flagged "somewhat vulgar" `ore`; corresponding second-person `kimi`/
`omae`; sentence-final emphatic particles `ná(a)`, `zó`, `zé` — "none of which are normally used by
women." A fine-grained distributional note: `zé` can follow the plain hortative `-(y)óo` ("Ikóo
ze!", "Let's go!") while `zó` cannot. (pp. 206-207)

### Expectation (*hazu*) vs. moral obligation (*beki*) (Unit 12)
`hazu désu` after a plain verb/adjective = "ought to/should," pure expectation with no moral
weight (`Ashita kúru hazu desu`, "I expect he'll come tomorrow"). `beki désu` instead carries moral
obligation (`Ashita kúru beki desu`, "he owes it to us to come tomorrow") — an explicit minimal-pair
contrast given by the source. Negating the "should not" sense puts the negation on the final verb:
`beki ja arimasén`. (p. 208)

### Concessive *no ni* and formal-written *mono no* ("although") (Unit 12)
`no ni` ("although") follows the plain form (copula `da`→`na` before it, paralleling `no de`),
distinct from `ga`/`keredomo` which follow the same form as the main verb. `mono no` is a formal-
written-register equivalent, explicitly flagged as such by the source. (pp. 208-209)

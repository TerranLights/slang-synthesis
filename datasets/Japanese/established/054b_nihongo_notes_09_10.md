## Grammar points

### ...kedo vs. ...kara when leaving a sentence unfinished (p.20-21)
Both particles can trail off a sentence to let the listener infer the rest, but they set up different
expectations: *...kedo* (especially after reporting a fact to someone senior) invites the listener to
supply instructions ("what should I do?"), while *...kara* invites the listener to infer a request.
Swapping one for the other changes what the speaker seems to be asking for, not just the formality.

### Correcting someone politely (p.22-23)
Before pointing out another person's mistake, Japanese speakers typically open with a preamble
("excuse me, but...") rather than launching directly into the correction — *Shitsuree-desu-ga* /
*Mooshiwake nai-n-desu-ga* in formal registers, *Warui-n-desu-kedo* / *Warui-n-da-kedo* between close
friends. These preambles overlap with the ones used for requests, but *Sumimasen-kedo* — common for
requests — is not idiomatic as a correction-preamble.

### Mashi as "the lesser of two evils" (p.24-25)
*Mashi* doesn't mean "better" in an absolute sense; it marks the less-bad of two unsatisfactory
options, or deflects/downplays a compliment (implying "not as poor at it as before," not "good").
Comparisons in Japanese generally rely on *...no hoo-ga* rather than any dedicated comparative suffix.

### ...negaimasu in public announcements (p.26-27)
A more formal, impersonal substitute for *...kudasai*, built on *o/go + noun-or-verb-stem +
negaimasu*. Virtually confined to public announcements (train conductors, station PA) rather than
personal requests between individuals; can appear alone (*Gojun-ni negaimasu*) when the noun/verb
stem is omitted as understood from context.

### Dake and shika for quantity (p.28-29)
*Dake* by itself just marks "degree/extent" and is ambiguous between "that's too little" and "that's
enough" depending on context and tone; its more familiar "only" sense is a separate, equally common
use. *Shika* (always with a negative predicate) pins the quantity down as exact and insufficient,
contrasting with *dake*'s ambiguity; the two can combine (*...dake-shika*) for emphasis.

### Two uses of ...nagara (p.30-31)
(1) Simultaneous action by the same agent, attached to a verb stem (*tabenagara* "while eating"). (2)
A concessive/regretful "even though," attachable to verb stems or na-adjective stems alike (*katte-
nagara* "while being selfish..."), typically softening an apology or refusal. The second use is formal;
*...desu-ga/kedo* substitutes in familiar speech.

### Kagiru / ...towa kagiranai (p.34-35)
*Kagiru* ("be limited to") extends idiomatically to mean "nothing beats X" when used affirmatively
about a preference (*Natsu-wa biiru-ni kagiru*). Its negative form, *...towa kagiranai*, means "it's
not necessarily so" — leaving room for exceptions — distinct from a flat denial.

### ...ppanashi vs. ...mama (p.36-37)
*...ppanashi* (verb stem + *hanashi*, phonetically altered to *-ppanashi*) marks something left
unattended that should have been dealt with — a lapse. *...(ta) mama* / *...no mama* covers the same
"left in a state" territory but, unlike *...ppanashi*, can also describe a deliberately unchanged state
that requires no correction (e.g. being invited to keep one's shoes on).

### Parting expressions (p.38-39)
Japanese lacks a literal equivalent to "have a nice day/weekend" — such sentiments are instead
expressed via wishes for the listener's safety/well-being (*Oki-o tsukete*, *Doozo ogenki-de*). *Yoi
shuumatsu-o* exists but is restricted to broadcast/public registers; using it between individuals,
while understandable, sounds slightly borrowed/unnatural.

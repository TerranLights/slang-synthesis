## Grammar points

### no-da/n-da, no-daroo/n-daroo, node/n-de, no-de, no-de-aru, no-deshoo/n-deshoo, no-desu/n-desu — the "no + copula" family
Paraphrased from pp.158–165. This is a tight cluster of combination particles built from "no" plus a form of the copula, and the book is explicit about register gradations within the family: "no da"/"n da" is the plain colloquial emphatic/explanatory form; "no desu"/"n desu" is its formal/polite counterpart; "no de aru" is reserved for written/formal register; "no deshoo"/"n deshoo" and "no daroo"/"n daroo" both hedge the explanation with uncertainty ("perhaps it's because...", "I wonder if..."), at different politeness levels. Separately, "node"/"n de" is distinguished carefully from "kara" (p.53): "node" marks an objective, speaker-independent cause already in effect, while "kara" expresses the speaker's own will/command/conjecture/question — a distinction the book itself calls "thin" and sometimes interchangeable. "No-de" (the combination particle, distinct from "node") draws an evidential conclusion from an observable fact.

### nomi (のみ) — formal/written "only" particle
Paraphrased from pp.166–167. A written-register equivalent of "dake"/"bakari", used to set a hard limit ("only this village escaped flood damage"), in the "nomi narazu" not-only-but-also pattern, and sentence-finally for an emphatic "the only (undesirable but unchangeable) option is...".

### noni (のに) and no-ni (のに) — a true minimal-pair homograph
Paraphrased from pp.167–169. The adversative "noni" ("although/but/even though") carries an undertone of surprise, dissatisfaction, or resentment, and can escalate into an outright rebuke of the addressee ("you're not going to eat it, even though I went to the trouble of making it?"). The unrelated combination particle "no-ni" (no + ni) instead marks purpose ("in order to...") or means ("as a means for doing..."), and the book flags the pair as identical in spelling and pronunciation but functionally distinct — a genuine homograph trap for learners.

### o / wo (を) — the direct-object particle
Paraphrased from pp.170–177. Always written with the hiragana を despite an "o" pronunciation. Marks the plain direct object, the object of desiderative ("want to") forms (where "ga" can substitute for slight emphasis), the object in causative sentences, and the object/subject-of-reception in passive sentences. Also covers an occupation-marking pattern ("I work as a secretary" — "shachoo no hisho o shite-imasu"), a point-of-departure sense ("what time do you leave home?"), a "continued-through" sense with motion verbs ("I slid down the slope"), and a handful of fixed phrases ("including, foremost"; "from the top down"; "taking this opportunity"). The entry also separately flags the short list of Japanese verbs that take "ga" rather than "o" for what would be a transitive-verb object in English (hoshii, iru, kikoeru, kirai, mieru, suki, wakaru), and the "noun + o + suru" pattern used for English intransitive verbs like "ski" or "jog".

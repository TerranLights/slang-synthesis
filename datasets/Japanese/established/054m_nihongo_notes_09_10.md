## Grammar points

### Okusan and the fixed-register rule for first names (p.58-59)
A colleague's wife is referred to as *okusan* ("your wife"), not by her first name, even after years
of acquaintance — using the first name, as an English speaker instinctively might once a friendship
deepens, can read as too personal and even mildly offend. This reflects a broader pragmatics
mismatch: in Japanese, whether two people use first names with each other is fixed at their very
first meeting and essentially never shifts later, unlike English's norm of first-name address as a
marker of growing friendliness. First names persist from childhood friendships into adulthood (even
old age) precisely because that's when the register was set, not because of later closeness.

### Chuushi-ni naru vs. chuushi-suru: neutral event vs. agentive blame (p.60-61)
Kanji-compound verbs built with *suru* have a neutral, passive-flavored "*ni naru*" form (*chuushi-ni
naru* "to be called off," and parallel *enki-ni naru*, *haishi-ni naru*, *kaisee-ni naru*) preferred
in formal/written/broadcast registers. The plain *-suru* form is more conversational and specifically
emphasizes that some party is responsible for the action — used when the speaker wants to register
blame or criticism, as in the framing dialogue's *Chuushi-suru-nante hidoi*. The true grammatical
passive (*-sareru*) of these verbs is rare in speech and, when it does appear conversationally,
signals that the speaker personally feels affected/inconvenienced by the action.

### Gachi: a restricted "tend to" suffix (p.62-63)
*...gachi* ("apt to/tend to") attaches to nouns or verb stems, but only words carrying a negative
implication — a failure to reach some expected or desired state. *Kumori-gachi* and *wasure-gachi*
work; *hare-gachi* ("tend to be fine weather," offered as encouragement) and *oboe-gachi* ("tend to
remember") are both ungrammatical, because "being fine" and "remembering" aren't themselves
negative/failure states. *Okure-gachi* ("tend to be late") is singled out as the most common
collocation.

### Ni totte vs. ni taishite: value-to-a-person vs. directed-at (p.64-65)
*...ni totte* marks that something has value, interest, or relevance (positive or negative) to a
specific person — "for me," "for young people." *...ni taishite* marks the object an action or
attitude is directed at — "toward him," "toward the president's way of doing things." The chapter
frames this as a common learner confusion: foreign learners tend to reach for *ni taishite* in
contexts that actually call for *ni totte*.

### -Garu and its limited nominalization into -gariya (p.66-67)
Building on the general *-garu* pattern (adjective stem → "to visibly show/act out that feeling"),
some but not all *-garu* verbs can be further nominalized into "a person who [feels] X" by swapping
the final *-ru* for *-ri-ya* — *sabishigaru* → *sabishigari-ya*, *hazukashigaru* → *hazukashigari-ya*.
This is not a fully productive pattern, and related *-gari-ya* words like *atarashigari-ya* ("a
trend-chaser") and *mendookusagari-ya* ("someone who hates bothering with details") drift from the
literal feeling-description sense. The whole word class carries a mild, non-malicious teasing tone
and should be avoided when referring to someone politely.

### Gomen's several faces: standalone apology, "excused from," gomen-nasai, and gomen-kudasai (p.68-69)
*Gomen* literally requests being excused/pardoned. Used alone (mostly by men, casually) it's a quick
apology. Preceding the copula (*gomen-da/-desu*) it means being excused from or declining some
unwelcome burden or prospect. *Gomen-nasai*, its most common form, is the familiar everyday "sorry" —
used even among old friends well into middle or old age except on formal occasions. *Gomen-kudasai*,
despite sounding more polite, is functionally distinct — it's used to call for attention at a door or
when taking leave, not for apologizing after doing something rude, where *sumimasen*, *gomen-nasai*,
or *shitsuree-shimashita* are the correct choices instead.

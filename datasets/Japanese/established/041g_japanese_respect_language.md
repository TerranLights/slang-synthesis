**Deferential and honorific adjective forms (Appendix 4, pp. 182–183 — the book's full
cross-reference table for adjectives, supplementing the handful of worked examples already captured
in Chapter 9 above).** The table covers PLAIN / POLITE / DEFERENTIAL / HONORIFIC forms for six
representative adjectives (元気da "well/energetic" [na-adjective], 若い wakai "young", 大きい ōkii
"big", 寒い samui "cold", 重い omoi "heavy" — each shown in both its ordinary sentence-final form and
its bracketed pre-nominal/attributive form). The honorific column uses -くて (-kute) いらっしゃる
irassharu throughout for true adjectives (若くて／大きく(て)／寒くて／重くて + いらっしゃる) and
でいらっしゃる de irassharu for the na-adjective 元気. The deferential column uses the
-う(-u)-contracted form + ございます gozaimasu (若うございます wakō gozaimasu, 大きゅうございます
ōkyū gozaimasu, 寒うございます samū gozaimasu, 重うございます omō gozaimasu) for true adjectives,
and でございます de gozaimasu for 元気. Two notes attached to the table: (1) square brackets mark the
form used before a noun (attributive); (2) an honorific prefix, usually お o-, can be added at any
level except plain, provided the respected person is genuinely involved and the word doesn't already
begin with o- (the source adds that ご go- is used with some na adjectives, but お o- is still more
common than ご go- for words that are felt to function as adjectives rather than nouns).

## Grammar points

### Adjectives have the same plain/polite final-form distinction as verbs (p. 139)

Parallel to verbs' plain (da, iku, irassharu) vs. polite (desu, ikimasu, irasshaimasu) final forms,
na-adjectives like 安全（な）anzen(na) have a plain final form 安全だ anzen da and a polite-to-the-
addressee final form 安全です anzen desu; true (i-ending) Japanese adjectives like 高い takai have an
identical plain and attributive form 高い takai, with 高いです takai desu as the form polite to the
addressee.

### Honorific-prefix placement with adjectives already beginning in o- (p. 140)

When an adjective already has an initial お o- (such as 大きい ōkii in its surface form, though
morphologically unrelated to the honorific prefix), it usually takes no additional honorific prefix.
However, in the more respectful でございます/いらっしゃる-type constructions, ōkii can optionally take
the compound (and now old-fashioned) honorific prefix おみ omi- stacked on top of its own initial o-
— an attested case of double-marking a single word with what is historically the same prefix
morpheme twice, in a construction now itself archaic.

### Deferential adjective formation: the -u-contraction + gozaimasu pattern (pp. 141–143)

True (i-ending) Japanese adjectives form their deferential equivalent by taking the adverbial -ku
form, eliding the k, and lengthening the resulting vowel, then adding ございます gozaimasu: 高い
takai → 高く takaku → 高うございます takō gozaimasu. The same process applies to いい／よい ii/yoi
"good" (whose -ku form よく yoku becomes ようございます yō gozaimasu) and to 多い ōi "numerous",
except that ōi's regular derivation would produce an awkward triple-vowel sequence, avoided by a
further contraction to 多（ございます）ō (gozaimasu). Na-adjectives instead simply take でございます
de gozaimasu directly (a deferential for だ da), e.g. 丈夫で（は）ございません jōbu de (wa) gozaimasen
"isn't strong."

### でございます/ございます's adjective usage mirrors its earlier-noted overuse as a blanket respect marker (pp. 142–143)

Extending the finding already made in Chapter 7 about nouns: the strictly correct honorific adjective
construction (-ku(te) + irasshaimasu, e.g. 大きく（て）いらっしゃいます ōkiku(te) irasshaimasu) is, in
practice, commonly overshadowed by the deferential -u-contraction + ございます gozaimasu form (大き
ゅうございます ōkyū gozaimasu) even when the sentence refers directly to the person being addressed
— i.e. a form that is technically deferential (targeting only the 2nd person) is widely used where
the strict honorific (targeting the grammatical subject) would be the "correct" choice. The source
frames (あなたは)どなた様でございますか as still felt by "most educated Japanese" to be less
acceptable than the true honorific どなた様でいらっしゃいますか — so this isn't total free variation,
but a documented case where common usage diverges from the prescriptively "correct" register
distinction while still being recognized by speakers as the less-correct option.

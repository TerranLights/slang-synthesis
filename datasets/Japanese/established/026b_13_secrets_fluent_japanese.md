printing 1999-03-12, ISBN 4-7700-2302-2; this copy is the 4th printing, 2001-02-16). Covers Secret
#7 "Comparisons/Romance Register" through Secret #13 "Manga," plus the back-of-book Appendix ("Mad
Mnemonics and Looney Links"). Secrets #1–6 are covered in the sibling file
`026_13_secrets_fluent_japanese.md` — see that file for the shared source/vision-reading coverage
note (the PDF has no text layer; all 95 pages were vision-read as two-printed-page spreads).

**Coverage note, this chunk specifically:** Two small pencil check-marks (not legible annotations,
just marks) were seen in the margin near two vocabulary items in Secret #7's romance-register
vocabulary table (きらきら輝く目 "sparkling eyes" and 眩しい微笑み "dazzling smile", p.~71) —
excluded as marginalia per the vision-reading guard, though their presence doesn't cast doubt on the
printed text itself (the printed Japanese was independently legible). No other marginalia was found
in this chunk's range. The book's final back-of-book **A–Z Japanese–English and English–Japanese
glossaries (pp. 160–171+) were *not* re-extracted here**: they are a straightforward alphabetical
consolidation of vocabulary already introduced (with richer context/examples) in the per-chapter
tables below and in the sibling file — re-extracting them row-by-row would be pure duplication, the
"flat large-entry-count dictionary" case the extraction spec allows skipping via representative
sampling. A scan across several glossary pages (160–161, 170–171) confirmed no new items beyond what
the per-chapter chunks already captured.

## Grammar points

### Comparative constructions: 〜より, 〜ほど〜ない, and romance-register vocabulary (Secret #7)
Secret #7 teaches Japanese's basic comparison toolkit (X より Y "more than X," X ほど Y ない "not as
much as X") inside a running romantic-scenario narrative, pairing the grammar drill with an
extended vocabulary set for describing physical attraction and emotional states (kirakira kagayaku
me "sparkling eyes," mabushii hohoemi "a dazzling smile," kokoro ga tsūjiru "hearts connecting").
The chapter frames comparison grammar and a concentrated literary/romantic-register vocabulary set
as a single combined lesson, rather than teaching the grammar neutrally. (pp. 70–73)

### A 12-step graded scale of intensifier adverbs (Secret #8)
Rather than teaching "very" as a single word, the chapter presents 12 intensifiers ordered along an
implicit register/intensity scale from totemo (neutral/mild) through sugoku, mechakucha, chō, zenzen
(colloquial), up to hyperbolic forms like shinu hodo ("to the point of death"). Each is illustrated
with a short example sentence (via a running "Commodore Perry" manga conceit) showing it modifying a
different predicate, making the point that Japanese intensifiers are not freely interchangeable —
register and degree both constrain which one fits a given sentence. (pp. 74–83)

### Five traditional children's word-games as a vocabulary-acquisition resource (Secret #9)
The chapter reframes five games Japanese children actually play — shiritori (word-chaining by final
mora), anaume (fill-in-the-blank), gyaku-yomi (reverse-reading a word/phrase), hayakuchi kotoba
(tongue-twisters), and kurosuwaado (crossword) — as deliberate adult vocabulary-practice tools, each
demonstrated with a worked example and answer key rather than just described abstractly. (pp. 86–95)

### A systematic prefix/suffix catalogue for productive word-formation (Secret #10)
Secret #10 catalogues roughly 41 prefixes and suffixes across (at least) five functional groups:
person-type suffixes (-mania, -gurui, -zuki), intensifying prefixes (kuso-, geki-, maru-, goku-),
descriptive/adjectival suffixes (-kusai, -ppoi), wasei-eigo-origin suffixes (-appu), and
rule/record-breaking suffixes (-yaburi). The explicit pedagogical point is that these affixes attach
productively to a wide base of roots to generate new words on the fly, rather than needing to be
memorized as fixed compounds — directly relevant to how Japanese slang coinage likely works
synchronically. This is this book's single highest-density chapter for slang-formation mechanics.
(pp. 100–105)

### Dajare (pun) mechanics: homophone collision, not just wordplay for its own sake (Secret #11)
Across three graded rounds (ages 7, 9–10, adult), every joke in this chapter resolves via one of a
small number of concrete mechanisms: (a) pure sound-homophony across unrelated words (tsuki "moon" /
usotsuki "liar"); (b) a word's first N morae coinciding with an unrelated word (mikan "tangerine"
containing kan "can"); (c) kanji-level homophony where the same pronunciation maps to two different
kanji with unrelated meanings (hareru written 腫れる "to swell" vs. 晴れる "to be clear"; kami written
神 "god" vs. 紙 "paper"); and (d) loanword/katakana readings coinciding with native-looking sound
sequences (moderu "model" / mō deru "it's coming out already"). This is a clean, reusable taxonomy
of pun-mechanism types for any later slang/wordplay mechanics analysis. (pp. 116–126)

### Loanwords and wasei-eigo as an underexploited fluency resource (Secret #12)
The chapter's central claim: gairaigo (genuine loanwords) and wasei-eigo ("Japan-made English," i.e.
pseudo-English coinages with no real English equivalent) are not marginal or embarrassing but a
large, systematically productive vocabulary layer — citing the historical precedent of loanwords
dating to 1543, telegraphic economy (apo vs. yakusoku), and semantic specialization (uedingu vs.
kekkonshiki marking a Western-style vs. traditional ceremony). It distinguishes three subtypes: (1)
**old kanji-compound term vs. modern loanword** doublets for the same referent (yamatakabōshi vs.
toppu-hatto); (2) **wasei-eigo coinages** with no literal English source phrase at all (bākōdo-atama
"barcode head" for concealed baldness, ningen dokku "human dry-dock" for a full medical checkup); and
(3) **false-friend loanwords** that narrow or shift an English word's meaning on borrowing (sumāto
= slim, not intelligent; kanningu suru = to cheat on a test, not "cunning" as a trait; tarento = TV
personality, not "talent" generally). (pp. 127–135)

### Colloquial/rough-register markers surfaced by an annotated manga extract (Secret #13)
Rather than teaching grammar abstractly, this chapter uses an unmodified extract from Tezuka Osamu's
*Blackjack* (reproduced with publisher permission, read right-to-left as in the original) with a
facing-page phrase-by-phrase gloss, explicitly to expose learners to colloquial register forms
"you are unlikely to meet in your textbooks." The facing vocabulary lists flag several of these
directly with register labels: わし/わしゃ (rough, male first-person pronouns, the latter a
phonological contraction of washi + wa); 〜やがる (a contemptuous verb auxiliary); あいつ/やつ
(derogatory ways to refer to a third person); おまえさん/おかた (sarcastically polite/mock-polite
second-person and plural forms — a register-inversion mechanism, formal morphology deployed
mockingly); and 〜め (an insulting suffix, as in yajiuma-me "you useless rubberneckers"). This is the
single most direct, explicitly-annotated source of colloquial/rude-register markers found in this
book. (pp. 136–155)

### Mad Mnemonics (goroawase/kojitsuke): sound-based and meaning-based memory links (Appendix)
The appendix names and demonstrates two named mnemonic techniques: **goroawase** (punning/
sound-matching between a Japanese target word and an English word or phrase that sounds similar) and
**kojitsuke** (a deliberately strained/contrived interpretive link, acknowledged by the author as
"lame logic" but still pedagogically useful). 16 worked examples are given (e.g. ranbō "rough/rude"
linked to Rambo; gorotsuki "thug" linked to the politician Zhirinovsky), followed by a 5-item
self-test. This is presented as a memorization technique rather than a linguistic claim about real
etymology — the links are deliberately arbitrary/contrived, not genuine cognates. (pp. 155–159)

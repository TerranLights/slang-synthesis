## Grammar points

### The book's own three-tier politeness-degree framework (Source 1, pp.19-20)

The book's central organizing device for e-mail register: nearly every reusable phrase across the whole book is tagged with asterisks indicating a 3-point politeness scale: `*` = usable only with close friends/family (casual, often copula-dropped or truncated forms); `**` = a middle "です・ます" baseline politeness appropriate for acquaintances, co-workers, or people one doesn't know especially well; `***` = fully formal, appropriate for superiors, clients, or people one has never met. Many entries carry a range (e.g. `**〜***`) indicating the phrase spans multiple tiers depending on exact wording choice. The book treats this as orthogonal to e-mail *type* — an invitation e-mail to a close friend can use `*`-tier phrasing throughout, while the same invitation to a boss requires `***`. One chapter (お知らせ, announcements/notices) is explicitly called out as an exception: even among close friends, announcement e-mails conventionally stay at the `**` です・ます baseline rather than dropping to bare/plain forms, because the genre itself (broadcasting information rather than conversing) carries an inherent formality floor regardless of the relationship.

### Part I phrase-bank structure: 5 functional slots

Part I organizes the basic reusable phrase inventory into five functional slots that recur across almost every e-mail regardless of chapter/purpose: 宛先 (addressing the recipient — 様 for a person, 御中 for an organization), 送信者 (how the sender identifies themselves, varying by formality), あいさつ (opening greeting formula, e.g. お元気ですか vs 元気?), 返事の要求 (requesting a reply, e.g. お返事お待ちしております vs 返事ちょうだい), and むすび (closing formula, e.g. 以上、よろしくお願いいたします vs じゃ、またね). Each slot is populated with multiple phrase options spanning the `*`-`***` politeness scale, letting a writer mix-and-match a coherent formality level across all five slots for a given addressee.

### Author's own small-scale informal usage survey (p.21)

The book includes an explicitly-flagged (not rigorously controlled) informal survey among its own circle of informants on two specific phrasings: 遊びにいらしてください ("please come visit") skewed toward being used by female respondents, while 申し訳ない without the です copula skewed toward male respondents. The book is careful to caveat this as anecdotal rather than a sociolinguistic finding, but it's an explicit, source-marked gendered-register observation worth preserving as a data point (not a rule) for mechanics analysis.

### Chapter-level register notes (Source 1, Part II)

Each of the 15 purpose-based chapters (誘い/invitation, お願い/request, お礼/thanks, おわび/apology, 断り/refusal, 質問/question, お知らせ/announcement, 近況報告/status-update, お祝い/celebration, 季節のあいさつ/seasonal-greeting, 友情/friendship, ネット買い物/online-shopping, 旅行/travel, 進学/further-education, ビジネス/business) opens with a short framing note on which politeness band is typical for that e-mail type, followed in most chapters (confirmed for invitation, request, refusal, question, business) by a dedicated boxed "expressions used in this type of e-mail" phrase bank, tagged with the same `*`-`***` scale, listing the chapter's reusable formula set (e.g. for 誘い: 情報源を示す表現 "phrases citing where information came from," 誘いの表現 "invitation-extending phrases," 誘いの返事の要求 "invitation-specific reply-request phrases"). Chapters covering inherently more formal content by default (お礼/thanks, おわび/apology) instead distribute their phrase guidance into smaller supplementary boxes attached to individual numbered subsections rather than one consolidated chapter-opening box.

### Business-chapter addressing convention: 様 vs 御中 (Chapter 15, p.111)

The business chapter makes explicit a rule implicit elsewhere: when addressing an e-mail to an individual at a company, use 様 after their name; when addressing a department or the company itself with no named individual, use 御中 instead — using 様 for an organization or 御中 for a named individual is marked as an error.

### Vowel inventory and devoicing (Source 2, Chapter 1)

Standard Japanese has five vowel phonemes (a, i, u, e, o). The book covers 無声化 (devoicing): the high vowels /i/ and /u/ become voiceless when they occur between two voiceless consonants, or word-finally after a voiceless consonant, in standard (Tokyo-based) Japanese — e.g. the /i/ in きく becomes voiceless under this environment. This is described as a regular, largely automatic process rather than a free-variation one, though the book notes dialectal variation exists (not all dialects devoice as readily as standard Tokyo Japanese).

### Phonaesthetic contrast: voiceless vs. voiced onset in onomatopoeia (Source 2, Chapter 1)

The book notes a recurring sound-symbolic pattern in Japanese onomatopoeia/mimetic words: a voiceless-consonant-onset variant of a mimetic word pair tends to connote something light, small, or pleasant, while the voiced-consonant-onset counterpart of the same root tends to connote something heavier, larger, or less pleasant (e.g. contrastive pairs like とんとん vs どんどん). This is presented as a productive, synchronically-felt pattern in the modern language, not merely a historical curiosity — directly relevant to how new mimetic/slang coinages might exploit the same voicing contrast.

### Mora as the rhythmic unit; special morae (Source 2, Chapter 2)

Japanese rhythm is organized around the mora (拍), not the syllable — a mora is roughly "one beat," and Japanese speakers count length and time rhythm in morae rather than syllables. Three categories of "special morae" (特殊拍) that don't correspond to a full CV syllable on their own but still count as one full beat each: the moraic nasal ん, the first half of a geminate (double) consonant (the っ before another consonant), and the second mora of a long vowel. This directly explains minimal pairs like びょういん (3 morae: byo-u-i-n... accounting varies by analysis, but the key point the book makes is the mora-count difference) vs びよういん (one extra mora from よ counting separately rather than being absorbed into a single ひょ-type mora).

### The "foot" as a 2-mora clipping unit (Source 2, Chapter 2)

The book identifies a recurring pattern in Japanese loanword/compound clipping: clipped forms strongly favor combining exactly two 2-mora "feet" (4 morae total) rather than clipping to an arbitrary length — e.g. リモコン (ri-mo-ko-n, from リモート・コントロール), ワープロ (wā-pu-ro, treated as fitting the same 2+2 pattern), セクハラ (se-ku-ha-ra, from セクシャル・ハラスメント), and personal-name nicknames like キムタク (ki-mu-ta-ku, from 木村拓哉 Kimura Takuya). This foot-based clipping convention is presented as highly productive and still active in forming new words/nicknames at the time of the book's writing (1999) — a direct, source-grounded mechanism for how Japanese slang/nickname formation compresses longer forms.

### Pitch accent types: 頭高, 中高, 尾高, 平板 (Source 2, Chapter 3)

Standard Japanese pitch accent is analyzed as a single pitch-drop location (or its absence) per word, classified into four types: 頭高型 (atamadaka, drop after the first mora), 中高型 (nakadaka, drop somewhere in the middle), 尾高型 (odaka, drop after the last mora — audible only when a particle follows), and 平板型 (heiban, no drop at all — a flat/level pattern). Minimal pairs illustrating the system include あめ (雨 "rain," 頭高) vs あめ (飴 "candy," 平板/odaka depending on dialect), かれー (curry, specific accent pattern) vs かれい (flatfish, different pattern), and homophone pairs like 白(しろ, accented) vs しろ (imperative "do!," different accent). The book frames accent as genuinely lexically contrastive — minimal pairs distinguished by accent alone are not rare exceptions but a systematic feature of the standard language.

### Loanword accent default: antepenultimate-mora placement (Source 2, Chapter 3)

A productive rule covered for borrowed (loanword/gairaigo) vocabulary: absent other factors, a loanword's accent drop defaults to falling on the third mora from the end (the antepenultimate mora) of the word, rather than being lexically unpredictable the way native-vocabulary accent often is. This gives loanword accent assignment a much higher degree of rule-governed regularity than native-vocabulary accent.

### Personal-name accent patterns (Source 2, Chapter 3)

The book covers a specific sub-pattern for how Japanese personal names (particularly surnames combined with -さん or other honorific suffixes) take accent, generally following predictable patterns tied to the name's mora count and the suffix added, distinguishing e.g. さとうさん (attached to the surname 佐藤) from the common-noun さとう (砂糖, sugar) purely by where the accent falls.

### Reduced accent-class system for adjectives and verbs (Source 2, Chapter 3)

Unlike the four-way noun accent system, Japanese adjectives and verbs are organized into only two accent classes each: an accented (Type A) class with a fixed, predictable drop location tied to the word's conjugated form, and an unaccented/flat (Type B) class with no pitch drop at all. Which class a given adjective or verb belongs to is lexically specified (must be learned per word) but the resulting pitch pattern across all of that word's inflected forms is fully predictable once the class is known — a much simpler system than the noun-accent typology.

### Accent and intonation are distinct systems (Source 2, Chapter 4)

The book is explicit that lexical pitch accent (a property of the word itself, independent of the sentence it's in) and sentence-level intonation (pitch movement that conveys sentence-type/mood — question, emphasis, listing, etc.) are separate, independently-operating systems that combine in actual speech rather than one being a subset of the other. Intonational patterns discussed include rising intonation marking yes/no questions, the behavior of the topic particle は and subject particle が under contrastive/listing intonation, special intonation on wh-question words, and sentence-final-particle-associated intonation contours.

### Apostrophe convention disambiguating ん + vowel/y-glide (Source 3, Unit Eight)

Source 3 covers the romanization convention (and the underlying phonological reality it encodes) where an apostrophe after ん is used specifically when the moraic nasal is immediately followed by a vowel or a y-glide, to prevent misreading the ん+vowel sequence as a single different mora — e.g. しんゆう (shin'yū, "close friend") needs the apostrophe to show ん + ゆう (2 morae) rather than being misread as a different syllable sequence; コンバン は (konban wa, "good evening") illustrates ん directly before a vowel without a glide.

### Four articulatory realizations of ん conditioned by the following sound (Source 3, Unit Eight)

The consonant ん does not have one fixed articulation — its place of articulation assimilates to whatever sound follows it: before b/m/p it is realized bilabially (like English m); before k/g/the nasalized ġ it is realized velarly (like English ng); before t/d/n/r/and most other consonants or vowels it is realized alveolarly (like English n); and in word-final/pre-pause position it has its own distinct realization (made with the back of the tongue against the soft palate, without full closure). All four count as exactly one mora/beat regardless of articulation.

### Geminate (double) consonants as a two-count unit (Source 3, Unit Nine)

Only four consonants — k, s, t, p — can be geminated (held) in standard Japanese, written with a small っ before the following consonant+vowel unit. A geminated consonant is held silently for one full mora-beat before release, meaning the geminate adds a full extra counted beat to the word versus its non-geminate counterpart — directly responsible for minimal pairs like いた (ita, "was," 2 counts) vs いった (itta, "went," 3 counts), and きた (kita, 2 counts) vs きった (kitta, "cut," 3 counts). The "ssh" variant (as in まっしろ, "pure white") patterns the same way for the sh-initial consonant+vowel combinations.

### Nasalized が (ġ) as a non-obligatory, speaker-variable allophone (Source 3, Unit Seven)

The book covers the nasalized realization of medial/final が-row consonants (transcribed ġ) — e.g. かがみ pronounced with a nasal [ŋ]-like sound rather than a hard [g] when が occurs mid-word. Critically, the source states this is not obligatory in all speakers' or all regions' "standard" Japanese: many Tokyo speakers use the hard [g] throughout, some speakers alternate inconsistently between the two realizations for the same word (e.g. かげ "shadow" can be pronounced either way), and a learner needs to be able to recognize both even if they settle on producing only one. This is explicitly marked sociolinguistic variation within the standard dialect, not a a strict rule — directly relevant to how pronunciation-level variation itself can become a register/identity marker.

## Grammar points

### ...nante — emotional/dismissive citation of a noun or verb phrase
A contraction of *nado* or *nado-to yuu mono/koto/no* (nominal) and *nado-to yuu koto/no* (verbal),
used to cite something while implying dislike, contempt, surprise, or admiration — a more emotionally
loaded alternative to the neutral *(verb)-nowa*. Vol.7 pp.120-121.

### ...wake-desu — marking the conclusion of an explanation
*Wake* covers both a "reason/cause" sense (*Wake-wa shirimasen-ga*) and a distinct "situation"/
concluding sense. The concluding sense (*...wake-desu*, *soo yuu wake-desu*, *...to yuu wake-desu*)
functions like a spoken paragraph-ending mark and is inappropriate before the explanation it
summarizes has actually been given. Vol.7 pp.122-123.

### Gendered vocabulary and address terms toward women
Men's speech shifts toward women's vocabulary when addressing women (*meshi*→*ohiru*/*ohiru-gohan*),
but the reverse does not generally happen; men also restrict certain forms (*...kashira*) to use with
women, especially younger women, never with other men. Vol.7 pp.124-125.

### Obligation constructions: "if one doesn't... + it won't do"
Must/obligation is expressed by freely combining a "condition" phrase (*...nakereba*/*...nakerya*/
*...nakya*; *...nakute-wa*/*...nakucha*) with a "consequence" phrase (*narimasen*, formal; *ikemasen*;
*dame-desu*, most familiar). The consequence phrase can be dropped, leaving the condition dangling as
an understood obligation. Only *ikemasen* and *dame-desu* (not *narimasen*) can stand alone as a bare
reply. Vol.7 pp.126-127.

### O/Go + verb stem + desu-ka — refined polite questions
Honorific prefix + the verb's *masu*-stem + *desu-ka* produces a question that means the same as the
"stem + ni narimasu-ka" pattern but sounds more reserved/refined — used in social situations such as a
host(ess) addressing a departing guest. Vol.7 pp.134-135.

### Noun + desu — omitting an obvious verb
When a verb (commonly *suru*, *iku*, *hairu*) is clear from context, it is often dropped, leaving a
bare noun + *desu* (or past *deshita*/*datta*). Using the full verb is correct but sounds wordy. Common
with kanji compound nouns and restaurant ordering. Vol.7 pp.136-137.

### Shikata-ga nai — resignation and leniency
*Shikata* ("way of doing") + *ga nai* expresses "it can't be helped," generalizing from plain
resignation to leniency toward someone else's circumstance (*...node shikata-ga nai*), to excusing a
whole action (*...nomo shikata-ga nai*), to stating there's no alternative (*...yori shikata-ga nai*).
Vol.7 pp.138-139.

### Three senses of yoku, disambiguated only by context
*Yoku* covers (1) manner — "well/skillfully/thoroughly," (2) quantity/frequency — "a lot/often," and
(3) sarcasm — "how can he.../he dares..." The three senses share identical sentence form; a sentence
like *Yoku kimashita-ne* can mean any of the three depending purely on situational context. Vol.7
pp.140-141.

### Aizuchi and the aizuchi-bijin role
Japanese conversation is explicitly framed as requiring active listener back-channeling (*aizuchi*:
*hai*, *ee*, *soo-desu-ka*) — without it, speech sounds unnatural even on broadcast media, which has
given rise to a named media role (*aizuchi-bijin*) for a co-host whose primary function is skillful
*aizuchi*-giving. Vol.7 pp.142-143.

### Kaeroo-tto — self-talk before starting an action
Formed from the volitional (*...oo*/*...yoo*) + *to omou*, contracted via the stop *t* into *-tto*.
Used when talking to oneself before starting an action; sounds more familiar/childlike than the
alternative *...ka(na)* self-talk form, and sounds mildly humorous when used by an adult. Vol.7
pp.144-145.

### ...ta tokoro-desu vs. ...ta bakari-desu — two kinds of "just completed"
Both combine with a past-tense verb to mean "have just...," but differ in implicature: *...ta
tokoro-desu* emphasizes the action's completion itself (often implying readiness for what follows,
e.g. rice just cooked = ready to eat), while *...ta bakari-desu* implies the expected follow-up action
has NOT yet started (e.g. just got up = not yet dressed or fed). Vol.7 pp.146-147.

### Jibun — reflexive "oneself," distinct from "I" and from "alone"
*Jibun* refers reflexively to whoever is the subject (not fixed to "I," despite an obsolete military
usage where it meant "I"). It must not be confused with *hitori-de* ("by oneself/alone") — *jibun-de*
specifically means "using one's own effort/capacity." The honorific *gojibun* politely refers to the
listener's or a third party's own self/effort. Vol.7 pp.148-149.

### ...koso — emphatic particle, especially in redirecting gratitude/compliments
Attaches to the word/phrase being emphasized ("THIS is...," "...tomorrow for sure"). A fixed idiomatic
use, *kochira-koso*, redirects an apology or thanks back onto the speaker, and the pattern extends to
redirecting compliments (*sensee-koso*, *Yoshiko-san-koso*) — frequently left standing alone with the
rest of the sentence omitted. Vol.7 pp.150-151.

### Ne attached mid-sentence — a familiar-register-only extension
Sentence-final *ne* (seeking agreement/confirmation) is usable in both polite and familiar speech, but
attaching *ne* to phrases WITHIN a sentence — including directly after grammatical particles like *wa*,
*ga*, *de*, *no*, *e*, *ni*, *o* — is restricted to familiar speech. Frequency of mid-sentence *ne*
scales with the degree of familiarity between speakers and can signal the speaker's eagerness for the
listener's understanding. Vol.7 pp.152-153.

### ...no koto-desu — specifying a topic of reference
Marks what a remark is "about" or "referring to." *Nan-no koto-desu-ka* is the polite way to ask for
clarification (plain *Nan-desu-ka*/*Nani-o itte-imasu-ka* can sound reprimanding). *...no koto* is more
conversational than the otherwise-equivalent *...ni tsuite*, and *...no koto-o* serves as the object of
verbs like *kangaeru*, *hanasu*, *shinpai-suru* when the topic is abstract. Vol.7 pp.154-155.

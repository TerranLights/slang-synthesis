## Grammar points

### Comparatives: ...の ほうが ...より

Introduced in L1 (p.7). ほう literally means "side"; the compared item that is superior/more precedes
の ほうが, and the inferior item is marked with より. Word order can be reversed without changing
truth-conditional meaning (電車の ほうが 車より はやいです。 = 車より 電車の ほうが はやいです。, both
"The train is faster than the car"). For two-way comparison questions, the pattern is X と Y と
どちら/どっちが...ですか/ますか — "Which is more... X or Y?" どちら is the more formal variant of
どっち. A topic-marked statement (東京は 横浜より 大きいです, "Tokyo is bigger than Yokohama") is
presented as an equivalent alternative construction to the の ほうが pattern.

### たら conditional: completed-action/supposition conditional, contrasted with と and て

Extensive grammar note at L15 (p.167). A...たら B means "once A has happened/been done, B happens" —
used specifically when B is an unexpected or non-automatic outcome of A. This is contrasted with と
(used for an automatic, near-mechanical consequence — the book's example: when Tanaka arrived
yesterday, the speaker told him something: this is NOT automatically entailed by Tanaka's arrival, so
とき is used rather than たら, per the book's own nuanced worked example) and with て (used for a
sequence read as a single intentional action-chain — using て instead of たら in the lesson's sentence
would imply the speaker had intended to meet Mr. Yamamoto at the health club, rather than running into
him unexpectedly). When the clause refers to the future, A...たら B...ます/ましょう/てください expresses
pure supposition ("if," since nothing in the future is certain); when the situation is treated as
expected to happen, "when" is the more natural English gloss instead of "if." Example given: あした
雨が ふったら、ピクニックは やめます。("If it rains tomorrow, the picnic will be cancelled.")

### なら as a second conditional, distinct from たら

Used in L15's dialogue (6時に 来るなら 今 預けても いいですか。, "If he's coming at six, may I leave it
here now?") — presented as an available alternative conditional marker alongside たら and もし, though
the lesson's running grammar note focuses its detailed contrastive explanation on たら vs. と vs. て
(above); なら itself is glossed simply as "if" in the lesson's own vocabulary list without the same
depth of contrastive explanation on the sampled pages.

### Epistolary (letter-writing) register as a distinct register from spoken dialogue

L10 is explicitly structured as a "reading review" lesson presented as written Japanese (a letter)
rather than spoken dialogue — the book's own structural note (p.ix, "About the Kana Version") states
this is deliberate: "the underlying rationale being that the two modes of communication, oral and
written, are different, and it is thought best to highlight the difference at an early stage." The
letter opens with the fixed formula ごぶさたしています ("I have been remiss in not writing"), uses the
polite third-person reference おくさま for "your wife," and closes with どうぞ よろしく おつたえください
("please convey my regards") — none of which occur in the book's ordinary spoken-dialogue lessons,
making this a genuine register-contrast data point for the project's register/usage-tier modeling.

### すごい as a valence-neutral intensifier

Flagged as a cross-register observation, not a named grammar rule in the source: L1's vocabulary gloss
for すごい gives two near-opposite senses, "terrible" and "wonderful" — confirming its core function is
pure intensification ("extremely," "to a notable degree") with valence supplied entirely by context,
rather than carrying fixed positive or negative polarity itself. Relevant to later mechanics-analysis
work on how Japanese intensifiers may donate to conlang slang-intensifier formation.

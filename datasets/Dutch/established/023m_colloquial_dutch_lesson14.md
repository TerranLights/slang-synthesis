## Grammar points

### The meanings of the Dutch word *er* (pp. 179-182)

Four distinct, unrelated-in-English functions of a single unstressed particle historically derived
from `daar` ("there"): (1) **dummy subject** — introduces an indefinite grammatical subject that is
placed after the verb (far more common in Dutch than English's parallel "there is/are" construction,
extending to non-existential verbs like `Wat is er gebeurd?`); (2) **partitive** — accompanies
numerals/quantity words with no direct English equivalent (`Ik heb er drie` — "I have three [of
them]"); (3) **pronominal** — obligatorily replaces a preposition + "it"/non-personal "them"
(`erop`/`erin`/`ervan`), commonly split in speech with `er` immediately after the finite verb and the
preposition clause-final, paralleling the `waar-`-splitting pattern from Lesson 12; and (4)
**locative** — an unstressed substitute for `daar` ("there"), differing from `daar` only in emphasis,
not meaning. All three non-dummy-subject functions place `er` immediately after the finite verb
(except when an object `het` is present, which then occupies that slot with `er` following it — see
footnote 2, p.183). The emphatic counterparts of pronominal `er` (`daarop`, `hierop`) front to
sentence-initial position instead. `Er` is also freely combined with the passive (`Er wordt aan de
deur geklopt` — "There's a knock at the door").

### Expressing the progressive/continuous form of verbs (pp. 184-185)

Dutch has no obligatory grammaticalized progressive (the simple present/past already covers ongoing
action), but offers three optional constructions when the ongoing nature of an action needs
emphasis, all roughly synonymous with each other and with the plain simple form: **`aan het` +
infinitive + `zijn`** (neutral, most generally applicable), **`bezig zijn te` + infinitive**
(near-synonymous with the first), and **`liggen/staan/zitten te` + infinitive** (posture-specific —
requires the speaker to actually be in that bodily position while performing the action at the
moment of speaking, the most semantically restrictive of the three). All three extend into the past
(imperfect) tense; `aan het X zijn` and the posture-verb construction additionally extend into the
perfect tense as a double-infinitive cluster (`heeft liggen slapen`), but `bezig te` does not.

### How to express actions that started in the past and are still continuing (pp. 187-188)

A genuine, systematically marked tense-mismatch between the two languages: where an action begun in
the past continues into the present (English: perfect tense + "for"), **Dutch uses the present
tense + the adverb `al`** rather than a perfect construction (`Ik leer al twee jaar Nederlands` — "I
have been learning Dutch for two years"). This is flagged as the one notable exception to the
source's own earlier-stated rule (p.129) that "a perfect in English is always a perfect in Dutch" —
a genuinely completed action still correctly takes the Dutch perfect (`Ik heb twee jaar in Duitsland
gewoond`, "I lived in Germany for two years," no `al`).

## Grammar points

### Independent use of modals (p. 217)

`Hoeven`, `kunnen`, `moeten`, `mogen` and `zullen` can all stand alone without a following infinitive,
paired with dummy subject `dat`/`het`, to express bare necessity/possibility/permission/probability
(see vocabulary table for the full worked set). `Zullen` used this way (in its "probably" sense)
always co-occurs with `wel`.

### Present participles (pp. 217-218)

Dutch has no verb-phrase equivalent of English "-ing" (an ongoing action is simply the plain present/
past, or one of Lesson 14's progressive constructions if emphasis is needed). Dutch does have a true
present-participle *form*, built by adding `-d` or `-de` (usually interchangeable) to the infinitive
(`huilen` → `huilend(e)`, "crying"), but this form is restricted to adverbial or prenominal-
adjectival use (`Het kind kwam huilend binnen`, `Het huilende kind`) — it can never itself form a
progressive verb phrase.

### Verbal nouns (pp. 218-219)

Where English uses a gerund as a clause subject ("Boiling vegetables is an art"), Dutch uses a bare
infinitive treated grammatically as a neuter noun, taking `het` where an article is needed (`Het
koken van groente is een kunst` — lit. "the boiling of vegetables is an art"). Such infinitive-as-
noun constructions can often be paraphrased with an equivalent `om te` + infinitive clause instead
(`Het is niet makkelijk om te studeren` alongside `Studeren is niet makkelijk`).

### How to express ailments (p. 219)

Two parallel constructions: `Ik heb pijn in mijn ...` and `Mijn ... doet zeer/pijn` (both "my ... is
hurting"), plus compound-noun ailment terms (`hoofdpijn`, `kiespijn`, `buikpijn` → "headache,"
"toothache," "stomach ache") where English's indefinite article ("a headache") has no Dutch
equivalent. The lesson also notes the shared-compound-element list-hyphenation convention already
seen on signage (`hoofd-, kies- en buikpijn`; `zon- en feestdagen`).

## Grammar points

### Unit 86 — Exclamations
Any forcefully spoken/written sentence counts as an exclamation, more common in speech than
writing. Catalogues short exclamation-sounds by the emotion each conventionally expresses (surprise
delight, disgust, etc.), plus several with **no direct English equivalent** (brrr for shivering-
cold, hè for disappointment, hè hè for relief) — the source is explicit about this untranslatability,
worth flagging since it's the kind of gap a synthesized slang system could plausibly fill
differently than English does. The `wat`/`dat`/`zo'n` exclamation-introducer pattern ("Wat een
mooi huis heb je!") is standard. The unit's own exercise box supplies a small cluster of informal
person-descriptor nouns (mens, meid, griet, kwal) paired with informal adjectives — genuinely
useful lexical data for a register profile of casual spoken evaluative language.

### Unit 87/88 — Interjections (1)-(2): a systematic functional catalogue
**This pair of units is the single most useful discourse-particle resource in the whole book for
slang-mechanics purposes**, and substantially deepens (without duplicating) `established/002`'s
own particle coverage — where `002` documented the *existence* of Dutch discourse particles
(eens, even, maar, toch, wel, hoor) from a different source chapter, this book's Units 87–88 give
each particle a **numbered, functionally distinct list of uses**, several of which are genuinely
non-obvious: `zeg` establishes contact ("Zeg, heb je het al gehoord?") or emphasizes a statement;
`hoor` reassures after a yes/no answer; `toch` alone covers five distinct functions — showing
concern in a question, seeking reassurance (paired with `hoor` in the reply), marking irritation
inside an order, indicating contrast, and confirming an assumption (functioning like an English
tag question, "isn't he?", which Dutch achieves through the particle instead of a grammatical tag);
`maar` softens an order into an invitation, marks advice (especially combined with `zouden`), or
expresses contrast/surprise; `even`/`eventjes` soften a question/order, with `eventjes` explicitly
noted as the *stronger* softener of the pair; `eens` marks irritation, often within an order;
`gewoon` covers three senses — plain simplicity ("I just followed instructions"), a negative
attitude toward the situation being described, and the speaker's felt certainty ("that's just how
it is"); `eigenlijk` softens a negative answer or marks a change of plan (both glossed as English
"actually"); `zeker` marks an assumption. Interjection placement is explicitly stated to have no
single fixed rule — position itself sometimes changes the particle's function, which is precisely
the kind of context-sensitive pragmatic mechanism worth flagging for later mechanics analysis. This
functional density (multiple distinct pragmatic uses per single short word) is a strong candidate
mechanism to examine when the project's Phase 3 analysis gets to how Dutch achieves attitude/stance
marking through minimal, highly overloaded function words — a pattern a synthesized slang system
could plausibly borrow or invert.

### Unit 89 — Strong language — **directly relevant to slang mechanics**
The most directly useful unit in this file for the slang-synthesis mission. Key findings, each
explicitly source-stated rather than inferred: **(1) spoken/written register gap** — Dutch is
markedly more colloquial in speech than in writing, a broader framing of the same spoken-vs-
written axis `established/002` already documented from a different chapter. **(2) historical class
stratification, now largely dissolved** — strong language used to track social class (more
acceptable among lower classes) but the Low Countries' now-thin class system means usage is
governed by **social group** instead — students and young people specifically are named as a group
for whom strong language has become "very much part of, and often expected, accepted social
behaviour," a genuinely concrete social-conditioning-of-register data point. **(3) unusually loose
taboo boundaries** — the source claims Dutch, relative to English, has comparatively few words
considered too shocking for broadcast media, though it explicitly caveats this as a generalization
that doesn't hold for every speaker; anti-swearing public advertisements in the Netherlands are
named as counter-evidence that resistance still exists. **(4) a documented, dated loanword-adoption
case study**: English "shit" was adopted into Dutch strong-language vocabulary specifically from
the mid-to-late 1980s onward, concentrated in the north (implicitly contrasting with the Flanders/
Belgium pattern below), and — critically — **its semantic weight was reduced in the borrowing
process**, landing somewhere between "oh, dear" and "damn" rather than carrying its full English
force. This is an explicit, dated, regionally-scoped example of borrowed taboo vocabulary
undergoing intensity attenuation on import — directly the kind of mechanism (loanword adoption +
semantic drift in strength/register) a slang-synthesis mechanics analysis would want a real-world
precedent for. **(5) Netherlands/Belgium contrast reinforced a third time**: Belgian Dutch speakers
are described as less openly colloquial and less accepting of strong language than Dutch speakers
in the Netherlands — the same NL-more-permissive-than-Flanders axis already seen in Units 84
(formality) and 85 (foreign-word acceptance), now confirmed as a recurring, source-consistent
three-for-three regional pattern worth treating as a stable finding rather than a one-off note.
The unit's own exercise sentences (quoted here only as brief paraphrase, with attribution to
"quality newspapers and magazines" per the source's own framing) supply concrete taboo-register
vocabulary: `verdomme` ("damn it"), `een zak zijn` / `lul` (vulgar insults), `donder op!` ("get
lost!"), alongside milder informal items like `zootje` ("a mess") and the idiom `op je bek gaan`
("to fail/fall flat").

### Unit 90 — Topic/comment sentence structure
Introduces the topic (old/already-established information, generally placed first) vs. comment
(new information) distinction as a discourse-level organizing principle distinct from pure
grammar — a text can be grammatically correct while still reading as poorly organized if new
information is front-loaded ahead of linking "old" information. Fronting an object instead of the
subject to keep topic-first ordering triggers inversion (matches Unit 51/80's mechanics). The unit
explicitly notes writers can deliberately violate this convention for emphasis, contrast, or
attention-seeking — a stylistic-choice framing rather than a hard rule. Matches discourse-structure
material already implicit in the project's other Dutch established/ files; kept as a supplement
since this explicit topic/comment terminology isn't used elsewhere in the project's Dutch coverage.

### Unit 91/92 — Style (1)-(2): how grammar and vocabulary shape register
Directly complements Unit 84's formal/informal treatment with a "style" framing rather than a
"formality" framing. Unit 91's core claim: **vocabulary choice is the primary lever** — the source
gives a concrete minimal pair, `u attenderen op` (formal, "to draw your attention to") vs. `je
laten weten` (neutral/informal, "to let you know") — reinforcing the café/kroeg pair from Unit 84
as a second worked example of the same register-via-word-choice mechanism. Grammar also shapes
style independent of vocabulary: preferring nouns over verbs creates a static/formal feel (a
nominalization-as-formality-marker point not raised elsewhere in this project's Dutch coverage);
adjective density and type shape tone (poetic, dramatic, etc.). Unit 92 adds: sentence
length/complexity (short=dramatic/fast, long/subordinate-heavy=formal, matching Unit 54's passive-
as-formal-marker point extended to syntax generally); passive voice (cross-referencing Units 52–54);
interjections "oiling" a conversational style even in writing (cross-referencing Units 87–88); word
order variation for liveliness (cross-referencing Unit 90); and correct linking words/pronoun
reference for clarity (cross-referencing Units 68–73, 58–61). This pair of units functions as a
genuinely useful **synthesis/index** tying together nearly every register-relevant mechanism
covered elsewhere in the book — directly useful scaffolding for the project's eventual mechanics-
analysis phase, even though each individual mechanism is covered (and cross-referenced) elsewhere.

### Unit 93 — Punctuation and accents
Comma usage in Dutch is governed by a readability/pause-based principle rather than a rigid rule
set, with two flagged high-value cases: separating stacked verbs from different clauses (especially
when a verb-final sub-clause precedes a main clause), and marking non-restrictive relative clauses
(restrictive clauses, which narrow the reference and add essential information, do *not* take a
comma; non-restrictive ones, adding non-essential extra information, do) — a genuine
semantically-load-bearing punctuation distinction worth flagging. Acute/grave accents mark stress
or specific vowel pronunciation (mainly on French-origin words); the trema (two dots) marks a new
syllable start in an ambiguous vowel sequence (cross-referencing Unit 17's compound-number
examples); accents are never used on capital letters. Standard reference content.

### Unit 94/95 — Using dictionaries
Practical guidance on what a good Dutch–English/Dutch–Dutch dictionary should include (multiple
senses per entry, gender marking via v/m/o for vrouwelijk/mannelijk/onzijdig, stress marking via a
pre-syllable apostrophe — which doubles as a separable/inseparable-verb indicator per Unit 41's
stress rule — and irregular verb forms) and how to locate a word's dictionary headword when its
surface form differs (verb stem-finding, separable-verb prefix relocation to look up the joined
form, adjective -e-stripping, noun singularization). Purely a study-skills unit; no new
lexical/grammatical content of its own, so no vocabulary rows are drawn from it beyond what's
already captured.

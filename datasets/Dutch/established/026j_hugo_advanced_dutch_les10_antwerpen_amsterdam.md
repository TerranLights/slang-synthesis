## Grammar points

### 1. Elliptical/"economy" tourist-brochure style

Tourist-brochure Dutch frequently omits verbs and subjects for concision, leaving sentence
fragments the reader must mentally complete, e.g. `[Het is de] Stad van de schilder Rubens. [Het is
de] Vierde havenstad ter wereld.` This is a stylistic register feature, not a grammatical error
(p.172).

### 2. No "largest" needed with a superlative + `ter wereld`

Unlike English, which needs "the fourth *largest* port in the world," Dutch `vierde havenstad ter
wereld` already implies "largest" and needs no extra word (p.172).

### 3. `gezelligheid`/`gezellig` — an untranslatable core concept

Flagged explicitly by the source as particularly difficult to render in English: an indefinable
"atmosphere" of cosiness, warmth, and conviviality, easy to recognize but hard to define (p.173).

### 4. `als` where `zoals` ("such as") is expected

The source text uses `als` in `beroemde winkelstraten als de Keyzerlei` where strict usage might
expect `zoals` ("such as"); noted as a real, naturally-occurring variation (p.173).

### 5. `is het leuk` + `om...te` as a "shorthand" impersonal construction

`Vanaf de Grote Markt is het leuk wandelen` is shorthand for the fuller `... is het leuk om naar de
mooie Onze Lieve Vrouwkathedraal te wandelen` — omitting the destination and `om...te` scaffolding
when it's recoverable from context (p.173).

### 6. Compressed pre-nominal participial phrases

`... zijn met 'stenen kantwerk' versierde toren` ("its tower decorated with 'stone lacework'") is a
"compressed" adjectival/participial phrase placed before the noun it modifies — cross-referenced by
the source to an equivalent construction in Les 7 (p.173).

### 7. Repeated relative pronoun `die` left implicit across multiple relative clauses

Parallel to the `als`-ellipsis pattern from Les 8: when several relative clauses in a row share the
same relative pronoun, it need only be stated once. `... die Jan Klaassen heette, in de Jordaan
woonde, in luidkeelse onmin leefde met zijn vrouw...` — `die` implicitly governs all three verbs
(p.177).

### 8. `waar` + preposition triggered by a preposition-led relative clause

`een verhaal waarvan men in Italië zou zeggen...` ("a story about which people in Italy would
say...") — illustrates the `waar+preposition` construction (cross-referenced to Les 3) combined
with the conditional/hypothetical use of `zou` (cross-referenced to Les 9) (p.177).

### 9. `moge` — a surviving Dutch subjunctive, in fixed expressions only

The Dutch subjunctive mood has almost entirely disappeared except in a handful of fixed/formulaic
expressions. `Moge het nog lang zó blijven!` ("May it long remain so!") uses `moge`, a genuine
subjunctive form of `mogen`, as one of the few living examples (p.177).

### 10. Forming place-name adjectives and demonyms (major addition — new grammatical system)

**Adjectives from place names:** unlike English (which typically uses the bare place name), Dutch
adds `-s` to a place name to form its adjective: `een Amsterdams grachthuis` ("an Amsterdam canal
house"), `een Brussels terrasje` ("a Brussels café terrace"). Note `Brussel` (no final `-s` on the
city name itself) despite English "Brussels." Place names ending in `-e`/`-en` drop that ending
before adding `-s`: `Groningen` → `Gronings`, `Leiden` → `Leids`, `Antwerpen` → `Antwerps`, `Brugge`
→ `Brugs`. The same rule applies to province names: `Holland` → `Hollands`, `Drente` → `Drents`,
`Limburg` → `Limburgs`, `Brabant` → `Brabants`. Two irregular/suppletive exceptions:
`Vlaanderen`→`Vlaams` (not *Vlaanderens) and `Friesland`→`Fries` (not *Frieslands). Like ordinary
adjectives, place-name adjectives take `-e` when placed before the noun per the standard adjective-
inflection rule (e.g. `de Amsterdamse grachten`, `de Antwerpse binnenstad`).

**Nouns from place names (demonyms):** two forms exist per place — a general form (covering people
in general, including women, in the plural) and a feminine singular form (identical to the
inflected place-name adjective + nothing extra, e.g. `de Amsterdamse` = "a woman from Amsterdam").
The general/masculine-singular form takes one of three suffixes, by rule: `-aar` if the place name
ends in `-en` or `-el` (`Antwerpen`→`Antwerpenaar`, `Leuven`→`Leuvenaar`, `Brussel`→`Brusselaar`);
`-enaar` if the place name is a single syllable (`Gent`→`Gentenaar`, `Den Haag`→`Hagenaar`,
`Delft`→`Delftenaar`); `-er` otherwise (`Rotterdam`→`Rotterdammer`, `Haarlem`→`Haarlemmer`,
`Holland`→`Hollander`, `Brabant`→`Brabander`, `Limburg`→`Limburger`). Plurals: `-er` nouns add `-s`
(`Amsterdammers`); `-aar`/`-enaar` nouns generally add `-s` too, though an alternative plural in
`-en` is also found (`Delftenaren`).

### 11. Modal/discourse particles — a systematic overview (major addition)

**The second major new grammar point of this lesson.** Dutch speakers convey mood/attitude toward
the listener primarily through small "particles" added to a sentence, rather than through
intonation/emphasis alone as in English — making these words largely untranslatable by a single
fixed English equivalent. The source explicitly names and functionally glosses each (rather than
translating): `nou eens` (speaker's impatience), `misschien`/`soms` (soften a request), `eens`
(conveys a positive attitude), `maar vast` (encouragement), `maar` (friendliness; softens a
command — distinct from the conjunction "maar" = "but"), `even` (softens a suggestion), `maar eens`
(softens a command), `toch` (a warning note — a further sense beyond the reassurance-seeking use of
`toch` already captured in Les 8, `026h`), `wel` (a note of confirmation). Particles can combine
(e.g. `maar vast`, `maar eens`, `nou eens`). The source's suggested learning strategy: (1) notice
particles in native/recorded speech, (2) imitate wholesale by repeating full phrases, (3) only then
begin actively introducing particles into one's own speech, checked against native-speaker
reactions.

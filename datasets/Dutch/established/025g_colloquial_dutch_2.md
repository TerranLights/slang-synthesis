## Grammar points

### Language point 1 — English loanwords in Dutch

Large numbers of English loanwords are used in Dutch, generally stressed on the final syllable
unlike their English source (**de come-back**, **de layout**, **de know-how**, **de Seven Up**).
A sizeable subset are false friends — used in Dutch with a narrower or different sense than in
English (**de camping** = campground not the activity; **de smoking** = tuxedo; **de cake** = pound
cake; **de box** = playpen/loudspeaker; **liften/de lifter** = to hitchhike/hitchhiker; **het
slipje**/**de panty** = underwear items). Others are Dutch-derived formations built on English roots
rather than direct borrowings (**de punker**, **de yup**, **de junk** from "punk"/"yuppie"/
"junkie") or Dutch verbs coined from English nouns (**computeren**, **internetten**). p.97.

### Language point 2 — Expressing nationality

Country/inhabitant/adjective triples are unpredictable and must be learned item by item (worked
example table for ~20 European countries, pp.97–98). Plural formation depends on the singular's
shape: **-man** forms → **-en** (**Engelsman→Engelsen**, **Fransman→Fransen**); **-er** forms add
**-s** (**Duitser→Duitsers**, **Zwitser→Zwitsers**); everything else adds **-en** with the usual
spelling changes (**Albanees→Albanezen**, **Hongaar→Hongaren**). Singular forms given in the table
denote male inhabitants only; the female form is nearly always the adjective + **-e**
(**Hongaarse**), cannot be pluralized (use the plain adjective for a female collective: **Die
vrouwen zijn allemaal Hongaars**), with **Française** flagged as a notable irregular exception. The
indefinite article is omitted before a bare nationality noun (**Hij is Hongaar**) but required once
an adjective precedes it (**Rembrandt van Rijn is een beroemde Nederlander**). pp.97–99.

### Language point 3 — Uses of graag

**Graag** ("gladly," "with pleasure") is presented as an indispensable word with five distinct
common uses: (1) affirmative reply to an offer, alongside or instead of **alsjeblieft**/
**alstublieft** (**Ja, (heel) graag**) — note **alsjeblieft**/**alstublieft** alone is reserved for
"please" when *requesting* an action, not accepting an offer; (2) as part of the "you're welcome"
reply **graag gedaan** (register-graded against **niets te danken** and the more formal **tot uw
dienst**); (3) in retail/service exchanges to express "would like" + "please" simultaneously (**Ik
wou graag twee kilo aardappels** / **Ik zou graag ... willen (hebben)** / **Ik had graag ...**, all
built on contracted **zou willen**/**zou hebben**), or omitted entirely with the noun phrase alone
plus **graag/alstublieft** (**Twee kilo aardappels, graag**); (4) to express "to like doing/to do"
something (**Ik leer heel graag Nederlands**; **Hij overdrijft graag** = "he likes to lay it on a
bit thick"); (5) commonly paired with **mogen** for liking people (**Zij mag haar buurvrouw heel
graag**) and with **lusten** for liking food (**Ik lust graag Indisch eten**). pp.101–103.

### Language point 4 — Coordinating conjunctions

Only four common coordinating conjunctions exist — **en** ("and"), **maar** ("but"), **of** ("or"),
**want** ("for, because") — and unlike all other (subordinating) conjunctions, they do *not* send
the finite verb of their clause to clause-final position; normal main-clause word order is
preserved after them. Separately, **of** meaning "whether/if" is itself a *subordinating*
conjunction (verb-final); only **of** meaning "or" is coordinating. The compound **of dat** ("or
whether") is noted as the form used to express "or whether" as a single subordinating unit (**Ik
weet niet of ik dit jaar nog op vakantie ga of dat ik tot volgend jaar wacht**). pp.105–106.

### Language point 5 — Subordinating conjunctions

The vast majority of Dutch conjunctions are subordinating: the finite verb of the subordinate
clause moves to the end of that clause, while the finite verb of the main clause stays in second
position; either clause order is possible, with inversion in the main clause required if the
subordinate clause comes first (**Omdat er warm weer voorspeld is, gaan we morgen naar het
strand**). A near-exhaustive list of ~30 subordinating conjunctions is given (pp.107), including
several containing **dat** as a formal marker (**voordat**, **nadat**, **totdat/zodat**, **opdat**,
**zonder dat**, **behalve dat**, **in plaats van dat**, **zodanig dat**, **of dat**). Two word-order
complications are flagged: (1) when one subordinating conjunction governs two coordinated
subordinate clauses joined by **en**, it sends the finite verb of *both* clauses to their respective
clause-final positions (**Wat was ze blij toen ze zijn stem hoorde en (toen ze) zijn gezicht zag**);
(2) a subordinate clause can be embedded inside another subordinate clause, in which case the
interrupted outer clause's verb still moves to the very end once resumed (**Ik denk dat als je een
vreemde taal leert, je gewoonlijk je eigen taal beter kunt begrijpen**) — the source notes this can
be avoided, with an equivalent simpler reading, by keeping the two subordinate clauses sequential
rather than nested. pp.107–108.

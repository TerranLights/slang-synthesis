## Grammar points

### Perfect tense (Перфект) — first introduction
The perfect is one of three Dutch past tenses (imperfect in Les 5, pluperfect in Les 11, per the
source's own forward-references) and is glossed as semantically close to the Russian perfective past.
Formed from: (I) a present-tense form of the auxiliary `hebben` or `zijn`, and (II) a **perfect
participle**, placed clause-finally. `hebben` and `zijn` each do double duty as both a full lexical
verb ("to have"/"to be") and a semantically-empty auxiliary.

**Participle formation (regular verbs):** prefix `ge-` + suffix `-t` (after a voiceless consonant,
i.e. after `f, k, p, s, ch`) or `-d` (after all other consonants, including `g`) — e.g.
`ge-werk-t`, `ge-woon-d`, `ge-zeg-d`. The mnemonic consonant set is illustrated via the fixed
compound `'t kofschip` (a 17th-century ship type), whose letters spell the voiceless-consonant set
triggering `-t`.

**Auxiliary selection (hebben vs. zijn) is not free — it's lexically fixed per verb**, and must be
memorized per-verb; the vast majority of verbs (all transitive and modal verbs among them) take
`hebben`. Of the verbs encountered in this book so far, only `zijn, blijven, gaan, komen, beginnen,
lukken` take `zijn` (the book marks `zijn`-verbs with `(z)` in its own vocabulary lists going
forward).

**Other participle-formation rules:**
- If the verb stem already ends in `-d` or `-t`, the participle doesn't double it:
  `antwoorden→geantwoord` (not `geantwoordd`).
- A verb with an inseparable prefix (`be-, er-, ge-, her-, ver-, ont-`) forms its participle
  **without** the `ge-` prefix: `betalen→betaald`, `gebruiken→gebruikt`, `vertellen→verteld`.
- Verbs whose stem ends in `v`/`z` undergo the same devoicing (`v→f`, `z→s`) in the participle as in
  2nd/3rd-person present tense, and the participle suffix after that devoiced consonant is always
  `-d`: `geloven→hij gelooft` (present) but `hij heeft geloofd` (participle); `reizen→hij reist` but
  `hij heeft gereisd`.
- Two irregular participles introduced here: `gehad` (from `hebben`), `geweest` (from `zijn`).
- Word-order: as with a modal-verb infinitive, the participle is placed clause-finally, after any
  other complement (`Ik heb het hem al gezegd`).

### `hele` — adverbial `heel` before an attributive adjective
When `heel` ("very") stands directly before an attributive adjective, it colloquially often takes the
same `-e` agreement ending as the adjective itself: `hele mooie bloemen`.

### Plural noun formation (Множественное число существительных)
Two endings: `-en` or `-s`.
- **`-en`**: (a) almost all monosyllabic nouns (`bloemen`), except many loanwords; (b) many
  polysyllabic nouns ending in an obstruent (`geschiedenissen`).
- **`-s`**: (a) polysyllabic nouns ending in unstressed `-el, -em, -en, -er` (`winkels`); (b) nouns
  ending in a vowel, with an apostrophe inserted after `a, o, u` before the `-s` (`collega's`); (c)
  most loanwords (`films`).
- Spelling changes preserve vowel length: `bed→bedden`, `naam→namen`.
- A stem-final `-s`/`-f` becomes `z`/`v` in the plural when the stem vowel is long/a diphthong
  (`huis→huizen`), and doubles when the stem vowel is short (`vis→vissen`).
- Numerous irregular/unpredictable exceptions exist (recommended to memorize noun+plural together);
  irregular plurals are marked in this book's own word lists. Notable irregulars: `broer→broers`,
  `idee→ideeën`.
- Some monosyllabic nouns with short `a/e/o` lengthen that vowel in the plural: `dag→dagen`.
- Some nouns have **two acceptable plurals with a meaning/register split**: `zoons` ("sons," neutral)
  vs. `zonen` ("sons," more elevated/formal register, per source).
- Latin/Greek loanwords often keep their original plural: `centrum→centra` (alongside a naturalized
  `collega's`-style form also occurring, e.g. `collegae`).
- After a numeral, many measure-nouns stay singular: `tien uur` (10 o'clock, not "hours"), `tien
  gulden`; but `twee pils, twee wijn, twee jenever` (drink-order idiom, contrasted with `tien dagen,
  twee pilsjes`, which do pluralize).
- Definite article before a plural noun is always `de` (`de honden`, `de boeken`), regardless of the
  singular's own gender; indefinite article is zero (`honden`, `boeken`); `geen` follows the same
  rule as before a singular noun.
- Attributive adjectives and the possessive `ons` always take `-e` before a plural noun (`de mooie
  boeken`, `onze boeken`).
- In a compound noun, the plural ending attaches only to the final/head element (`de boekwinkel→de
  boekwinkels`, `het filmhuis→de filmhuizen`).
- `veel` ("much/many") normally takes no ending and combines directly with a plural noun, without
  `van` (`veel boeken`).

### Demonstratives `deze/dit : die/dat`
`deze`/`die` precede a `de`-word singular noun and any plural noun (`deze/die vrouw`; `deze/die
vrouwen`, `deze/die boeken`); `dit`/`dat` precede only a `het`-word singular noun (`dit/dat boek`).
`die`/`dat` are used far more often in Dutch than Russian "тот/то," while `deze`/`dit` are used less
often than Russian "этот/это" — i.e. `die`/`dat` read as more neutral/default than their English
glosses suggest. `Dit`/`dat` in an identificational sentence pattern parallels Russian "это" (`Wie is
dit?` "Dit is Martha" = "Кто это? — Это Марта"). Demonstratives can substitute for personal pronouns
in some contexts (`Waar is Hans? — Die is hier`). Fixed idiom: `ditjes en datjes` = "this and that /
all sorts of little things."

### `om ... te` infinitive construction
An infinitive phrase (infinitive + its dependents) places the `te`-marked infinitive clause-finally;
the particle `om` optionally introduces the whole phrase, especially when the phrase is long
(`Het is moeilijk (om) dat goed te doen`) — but `om` is **obligatory** when the infinitive phrase
expresses purpose ("in order to"): `Ik leer Russisch om Tsjechov te kunnen lezen`.

### Direct/indirect object word order
Relative order of direct and indirect object depends on how the direct object is expressed: if the
direct object is a personal pronoun, it precedes the indirect object (`Hij geeft het (aan) haar`); in
all other cases, the indirect object precedes the direct object. When the indirect object follows the
direct object, it's usually marked with the preposition `aan` (`Hij geeft een cadeautje aan haar`).

### Telling time
Full paradigm: `Hoe laat is het?` = "what time is it"; `het is één/twee uur`; `half één/twee` = "half
past 12/1" (Dutch half-hour is named for the **coming** hour, opposite of English "half past");
`kwart voor/over` X; minute-counting `voor`/`over` an hour or a half-hour point (`tien voor half tien`
= 9:20, `zeven over half elf` = 10:37). `uur` is added only when stating a whole hour. `Het is nog
geen twee uur` = "it's not yet two o'clock." Numerals 1–12: `één, twee, drie, vier, vijf, zes, zeven,
acht, negen, tien, elf, twaalf`; 20: `twintig`. For minute-counts from :20 to :40 past the hour, the
`X (minuten) over half Y` phrasing is preferred over the `voor`-based alternative. `de klok` = a
wall/table clock; `het horloge` = a wristwatch (distinct lexical items, unlike English "clock/watch"
ambiguity resolved only by context).

### `maar` and `alleen (maar)` — a third sense of `maar`
Beyond the conjunction "but" and the softening discourse particle (032, Les 2), `maar` has a third,
distinct sense: "only/just," used specifically before a numeral or before an adverb of degree/measure
(`een beetje`, `kort`, `even`, etc.) — `Ik heb maar één fiets`, `Ik heb nog maar twee gulden`. In other
(non-numeral/non-measure-adverb) contexts, "only" is instead expressed by `alleen`, optionally
`alleen maar` (`Klaas houdt alleen (maar) van Liesje`). The adverb `nog` frequently co-occurs with this
sense of `maar` to mean "(there's) only ... left" (`Nu heb ik er nog maar één` = "now I have only one
left").

### `doen` vs. `maken`
Both gloss to Russian "делать" but are not synonyms: `doen` = to perform an action/function in a
general sense; `maken` = to create/produce/fix something concrete. Many fixed collocations must be
memorized per-verb rather than derived compositionally: `het huishouden doen`, `de was/afwas doen`
(032, Les 1) vs. `huiswerk maken`, `het gezellig maken` ("to create a cozy/convivial atmosphere" — see
`gezellig` vocabulary entry, a culturally loaded Dutch value-concept).

### Address vocative — article dropped
Before a direct-address vocative (calling someone by a noun, e.g. "Lui beest!" ["lazy beast!"]), the
article is omitted — cross-referenced by the source back to Les 3's own vocative-address examples.

### `zelf` — emphatic "-self" pronoun, position rule
`zelf` (invariant across person/number/gender — "myself/yourself/.../themselves") must immediately
follow the non-finite part of the predicate when there is one (i.e. attaches to whatever's
clause-final: infinitive, participle, or a fronted subject in certain inversions), and can never
stand directly before a noun/pronoun it modifies. Within those constraints it's fairly free in
position (`Hij heeft het ons zelf gezegd`, `Hij komt vanavond zelf` / `Hij komt zelf vanavond`, `Zij
gaat vanavond niet zelf naar Brugge` / `Zelf gaat zij vanavond niet naar Brugge`).

### Existential `er is.../er staat...`
`Er` is one of the highest-frequency Dutch words, often pronounced with a reduced vowel; it functions
in multiple distinct roles. 032 Les 3 already covered its use as the unstressed reduced form of
`hier`/`daar`. This lesson adds the **existential** construction (`Er is een biertje voor jou`, `Er
staat geen wijn in de kast`, `Er werken hier veel vrouwen`), used whenever the grammatical subject of
the clause is indefinite (carries an indefinite/zero/negative article). If a locative adverbial is
instead fronted to clause-initial position (triggering inversion per 032 Les 2's inversion rule), `er`
is dropped (`In de kast staat geen wijn`; `Hier werken veel vrouwen`) — but `er` still appears if a
different (non-locative) constituent is fronted instead (`Voor jou is er een biertje`).

### `welk(e)` — interrogative "which"
Used to request identification/selection from among a known/implied set (distinct from a purely
descriptive question). `welk` (no ending) precedes only a `het`-word noun in the singular; `welke` is
used in all other cases (`de`-word singular, and all plurals).

### Nationalities/languages table
Country name, adjective/language name, and male/female nationality-noun forms are given as a set to
be memorized together (table above). Both country names and nationality nouns/adjectives are
capitalized in Dutch. Two nationality nouns (`Fransman`/`Engelsman`) form irregular plurals
(`Fransen`, `Engelsen` — parallel in shape to `buurman/buurvrouw → buren`). Feminine nationality-noun
plurals are mostly avoided in favor of "Nederlandse vrouwen"-style adjective+noun phrasing, except
`Russin` and `Française`, which do pluralize directly. A language-name/adjective functions
grammatically like a `het`-word noun when naming "the language spoken in X"; it takes **zero** article
when not preceded by a preposition (`Nederlands leren`, `Russisch spreken`) but **does** take `het`
when a preposition precedes it (`een boek in het Nederlands`). `Hoe zeg je dat op z'n Nederlands?` =
`Hoe zeg je dat in het Nederlands?` (near-synonymous idiom pair). `Frans` (adjective, "French") is
noted as a homograph of the male given name `Frans`.

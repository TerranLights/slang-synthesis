## Grammar points

### The Prosodic Hierarchy and its domains (§7.1, pp.143–146)

Following Nespor and Vogel (1986), postlexical (P-)rules apply across a nested hierarchy of prosodic
domains: Syllable < Foot < Prosodic Word < Clitic Group < Phonological Phrase < Intonational Phrase
< Utterance. Booij argues the Clitic Group is unnecessary for Dutch (clitics are incorporated into
or Chomsky-adjoined to an adjacent prosodic word rather than forming their own category — developed
in Ch.8). Phonological Phrases group a lexical-category word with adjacent function words, joining
*following* function words by default in Dutch (a parametric choice, contra English's opposite
setting per Selkirk 1986) except when the function word is encliticized to what precedes.
Intonational Phrases are the domain of intonation-contour assignment, roughly corresponding to
clauses and parentheticals (Nespor and Vogel's definition, adopted provisionally; Ladd 1986 and
Gussenhoven 1992b are cited as complicating this). The Strict Layer Hypothesis (each domain
exhaustively decomposes into the next-lower one) must be weakened in two ways for Dutch: level-
skipping (a syllable directly dominated by a prosodic-word node, as in schwa-only prefixes like
*be-* in *beademen*, which block Prevocalic Schwa Deletion because the prefixal schwa is not
dominated by the lowest ω-node per Chomsky's 1986 dominance definition) and recursive adjunction
(compounds as a prosodic word Chomsky-adjoined to another prosodic word, similarly blocking
ω-domain rules from applying across the compound boundary).

### Rules above the word level generally apply less often in larger domains (§7.2, pp.145–146)

A general principle stated for Dutch (an admittedly underresearched area): the probability of a
P-rule applying decreases as its domain of application grows — words tied together in smaller
domains (compounds, phonological phrases) undergo connected-speech rules more readily than across
full Intonational Phrases. Booij does not assign each rule a fixed domain but treats IP as an outer
bound within which smaller-domain application is more frequent.

### Voice Assimilation, Nasal Assimilation, and Hiatus rules extend across word boundaries (§7.2.1–7.2.3, pp.146–151)

The Ch.4 word-level rules of voice assimilation, nasal assimilation, and the hiatus rules
(Homorganic Glide Insertion, Prevocalic Schwa Deletion) all have postlexical counterparts that apply
across phonological-phrase and Intonational-Phrase boundaries, generally with reduced/optional
force. A separate **Fricative Voice Assimilation** rule voices a prosodic-word-final fricative when
preceded by a sonorant and followed by a vocoid — crucially blocked *within* prosodic words, which
produces real minimal pairs distinguishing a cohering suffix (one prosodic word with its base, e.g.
diminutive *-je*) from an encliticized pronoun (a separate prosodic word). **Postlexical Nasal
Assimilation** differs qualitatively from its lexical counterpart: it affects only coronal nasals,
not nasals generally, since the language-internal evidence (Ch.4's morpholexical rules) requires
coronal nasals to already be specified for Place lexically. A related but distinct **Nasal
Deletion** process (formalized as delinking-cum-spreading/retiming, with compensatory nasalization
and optional lengthening of the preceding vowel) deletes an alveolar nasal before a following
fricative — restricted to the alveolar nasal cross-linguistically-consistent with this project's
general "coronal is special" pattern for Dutch connected-speech rules — and is also attested (as a
non-standard western-dialect phenomenon) before /s/ word-internally. **Hiatus rules**
(Homorganic Glide Insertion, Prevocalic Schwa Deletion) extend to compounds, phonological phrases,
and IPs, but Schwa Deletion is postlexically more restricted, blocked in schwa-final prefixes (with
a couple of lexicalized exceptions) — tied to the general finding that morphological boundaries
impede reduction processes — while remaining available within compounds and *de*-headed noun
phrases. The formal preposition *te* /tə/ never undergoes it, unlike its phonologically similar but
more colloquially-registered counterpart *de*.

### Degemination, Palatalization, and /t/-deletion above the word level (§7.2.4–7.2.6, pp.151–154)

**Degemination** (the same rule as Ch.4's word-internal version) becomes optional rather than
obligatory when its domain is the full IP rather than the prosodic word. **Palatalization** of
alveolar consonants before /j/ is clearly audible before the encliticized pronoun *je* "you"
(*had je*, *kan je*), optionally combined with /n/-deletion in /n/-final auxiliaries; whether the
palatal glide persists as an independent segment afterward is a speech-rate-dependent timing matter
(retiming, not deletion). **/t/-deletion** targets a coda /t/ preceded by an obstruent and followed
by another consonant: obligatory within a single prosodic word (diminutives, superlative *-st*/-s*
forms), where it neutralizes minimal-looking forms like *kastje* "cupboard (dim.)" and *kasje*
"greenhouse (dim.)" to an identical [kɑʃjə]; optional (frequency/lexicalization-sensitive) across a
prosodic-word boundary within compounds/prefixed words; and across phrase boundaries only when at
least one flanking consonant is an obstruent (blocked between two sonorants). Booij frames /t/-
deletion, per Browman and Goldstein (1990), as retiming/masking of articulatory gestures rather than
true segment deletion, which explains why deletion is more robust in smaller domains (where time
compression has proportionally larger effects) even though perceptually it is total in the
obligatory cases.

### Sentence Accent Assignment Rule (SAAR) (§7.3, pp.154–160)

Sentence-accent placement is governed by focus (semantic/pragmatic "new information" status), not
just syntactic position: the same sentence can carry accent in different places depending on which
constituent is [+focus], including "narrow focus" for contrastive readings, and even a function word
can bear accent under contrastive focus. Following Gussenhoven (1984, 1992a), a clause is analyzed
into a Predicate (P), Arguments (A), and Modifiers (M). The **SAAR**: "If focused, every predicate,
argument, and modifier must be accented, with the exception of a predicate that, discounting
unfocused constituents, is adjacent to an argument" (Gussenhoven 1992a: 84) — i.e., a focused
predicate immediately next to a focused argument forms one accent domain with the argument getting
the accent, while intervening focused modifiers force separate domains (and thus separate accents)
for predicate and argument. The algorithm groups adjacent focused P/A pairs into domains ([AP] or
[PA], with [AP]-domain creation ordered first), then assigns one accent per domain (on the argument,
in an [AP]/[PA] domain). This correctly derives, among other things: the prosodic break in *Jan
slaat zijn vrouw* falling after *Jan* rather than after *slaat*; the classic Schmerling (1976)
contrast between "Johnson died" (focus on the whole clause, accent falls on the argument) and "Truman
died" (only the predicate is focused given shared background knowledge, so accent falls on the
verb); that separable complex verbs (*aftreden*, *opbellen*) still form one accent domain across
their split syntactic realization; that a focused pronoun forms its own domain rather than merging
with an adjacent predicate; and that eventive vs. non-eventive readings of an ambiguous sentence
(*De directie geeft dieven aan* "The management turns thieves in") receive different accent counts
because SAAR's predicate-skipping behavior is specific to eventive (narrative) sentences. Separately,
**Topicalization** (fronting a non-focused constituent to first position) independently assigns it a
sentence accent even without [+focus] status — explaining why only strong-form pronouns (which can
bear stress) can be topicalized, not weak forms, a fact that follows automatically from their
prosodic properties rather than needing a dedicated syntactic constraint.

### Tonal realization of sentence accent (§7.3, pp.159–161)

Sentence accents are phonetically realized as one of three pitch-accent tone patterns, treated as
tonal morphemes with contour tones: H\*L (falling), L\*H (rising), H\*LH (falling-rising), where the
starred tone links to the accented syllable and subsequent tones spread across the rest of the
association domain; a copy of the contour's last tone serves as a boundary tone (H% or L%) at the
end of an Intonational Phrase (citing Gussenhoven 1984, 1988 and pitch-track data from Van den Berg
et al. 1992 on the example sentence *Leeuwarden wil meer mannen* "Leeuwarden needs more men," shown
with one vs. two sentence accents and either tone as the first accent). The portion of a phonological
phrase preceding the first sentence accent (the "onset") is typically low in pitch; two adjacent
phonological phrases can either each form their own tonal association domain or merge into one,
affecting how the tone following the starred tone is distributed.

### Rhythmic stress-shift rules: Stress Retraction and Iambic Reversal (§7.4, pp.161–164)

Two postlexical stress-shift phenomena resolve stress clashes at the phrase level, building on Ch.5's
word-level Trochaic Reversal and Stress Retraction. **Stress Retraction** is (near-)obligatory and
applies to attributively-used adjectives and prepositions preceding a stressed head — weak–strong
adjective compounds (*dòodziek kínd* "critically ill child"), prepositional compounds
(*òndéraan de bérg* "at the foot of the mountain"), and nouns before appositions (*àdmiraal de
Rúyter*). It is analyzed as leftward Move-* in the phonological-phrase domain, resolving a grid
stress clash where two asterisks are adjacent at consecutive metrical-grid lines; it is blocked when
the shift target is a single stressless syllable rather than a genuine stressed syllable (an
asterisk cannot move to a grid position lacking a lower-level asterisk, per Prince 1983's
well-formedness condition), correctly ruling out shift in words like *penibel* or *fantastisch*
whose initial syllable is inherently stressless rather than just secondarily stressed. Note this
also constitutes independent evidence against quantity-sensitive analyses of Dutch stress (cf.
§5.2.1), since these initial syllables would be predicted stressed under such an analysis. Stress
shift correctly does *not* occur when the adjective/preposition is itself modified and thus heads a
branching phrase rather than a non-branching one — *[een [zeer speciaal] geval]* keeps stress on
*speciáal*, not shifted, because the adverb-modified AP does not itself form a single phonological
phrase. **Iambic Reversal**, by contrast, is optional and used for rhetorical/pragmatic effect
(Gussenhoven 1984, Kager and Visch 1988, Visch 1989) — attested for attributively-used adjectives
(*spèciaal gevál* → *spéciaal gevál*). Some bisyllabic adjectives with an unstressed first syllable
allow stress shift only in lexicalized noun-phrase expressions (*cèntraal statión* → treated as
subject to the rhythmic Hammock Principle from §5.2.1 rather than the general rule), and complex
adjectives derived from nominal bases (including compound bases) are systematic exceptions to stress
shift, since their derivational suffix has already relocated main stress away from the historical
initial syllable — marked in the lexicon via a redundancy rule rather than requiring word-internal
structure to be visible postlexically.

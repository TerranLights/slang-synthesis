## Grammar points

### Plural of nouns (pp. 41-43)

Two main plural strategies, `-en` (majority) and `-s` (nouns ending unstressed `-el/-en/-em/-er`,
foreign nouns ending `-a/-i/-o/-u` with apostrophe-s, all `-(t)je` diminutives). A small closed set
of neuter nouns takes `-eren` (`blad→bladeren`, `ei→eieren`, `kalf→kalveren`, `kind→kinderen`,
`lam→lammeren`). A subset of short-vowel nouns lengthen their vowel in the plural
(`dag→dagen`, `weg→wegen`, `bad→baden`, `dak→daken`, etc.), and `stad→steden`, `schip→schepen` are
further irregular. Neuter nouns always take `de` (not `het`) once pluralized. This overlaps
`established/001`'s plural coverage from the *Essential Grammar*; the fuller `-s`-taking-category
enumeration and the `-eren` closed set are kept here as somewhat more systematic than 001's version.

### Shopping phrases / weights (pp. 43-44)

Standard shopping request/price phrases. Weight units: `ons` (100g) and `pond` (500g) are no longer
official metric measures but remain in everyday commercial use even though prices are never labeled
in them — a live written/spoken-register gap in unit choice, not just a lexical curiosity (p.44).

### Yes/no questions (p.47)

Simple subject-verb inversion (no periphrastic "do"), same F-V-N-Rest schema introduced in Lesson 2.

### niet waar? / hè? (p.56)

See vocabulary table — `hè?` is the source's own explicit claim of colloquial dominance over the
more formal `niet waar?` tag-question construction.

### The two groups of verbs / perfect tense of regular verbs / 't kofschip (pp. 60-61)

Dutch verbs split into regular and irregular exactly as in English (`worked` vs. `saw`), a
distinction invisible in the present tense but real in past-tense formation. Regular past
participle: `ge-` + stem + `-d`/`-t`, chosen by the 't kofschip mnemonic (stem ends in
t/k/f/s/ch/p → `-t`; otherwise `-d`) — note this is a **spelling** rule tracking an underlying
devoicing distinction that isn't audible (final `-d` is pronounced `-t`), exactly parallel to the
d/t confusion English speakers navigate silently between "loved" and "worked." Verbs with v/z in the
infinitive but f/s in the stem (`reizen`→`reis`→`gereisd`, `leven`→`leef`→`geleefd`) follow the
*stem's* consonant, not the infinitive's, which can look surprising. The `ge-` prefix is never
stressed; verbs already prefixed with unstressed `be-/ge-/her-/ont-/ver-` (e.g. `ontmoeten`) do not
double up with a second `ge-`.

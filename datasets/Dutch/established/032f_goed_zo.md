## Copyright note

Per project copyright discipline, dialogues/narrative texts (the recurring Klaas/Liesje storyline —
"Klaas is ziek geworden," "Liesje heeft een leraar gevonden," a phone-call dialogue, a joke about an
old man at the doctor's) are not reproduced beyond short paraphrase/summary; only vocabulary,
paraphrased grammar rules, and short representative example sentences are extracted below.

## Non-redundant-supplement check

Checked against `032`–`032e`. This is one of the book's longest, richest lessons: it introduces
**irregular-verb principal-parts morphology** as a systematic named category for the first time (a
large consolidated table of ~35 strong/irregular verbs, several new to this book's own sequence), the
**hebben-vs-zijn perfect auxiliary rule for verbs of motion** (a genuinely new refinement beyond
032c's "lexically fixed per verb" statement — motion verbs other than `komen`/`gaan` can take *either*
auxiliary depending on whether a directional/locative complement is present), **postpositions** (a
new word-class distinction not previously named in this book), extended uses of `van` and `er`, full
**calendar vocabulary** (days, months, seasons) and **compass-direction/geography** vocabulary, and a
complete **body-parts** vocabulary set with an idiom-dictionary block. None of this overlaps
meaningfully with 032–032e; kept in full. Two-timezone/date-format conventions and the
`half`/`anderhalf` adjectival-agreement rule are also new.

## Grammar points

### Irregular ("strong") verbs — systematic principal-parts table
The book introduces a named category, **неправильные глаголы** ("irregular verbs"): unlike regular
verbs, these form the imperfect and/or perfect participle by **vowel (occasionally consonant)
alternation of the stem** rather than by the `-t(e/en)`/`-d(e/en)` suffix. Rules given:
1. In the imperfect, an irregular verb takes **no ending** in the singular; the plural adds `-en`.
2. Most irregular participles end in `-en` (vs. regular `-t`/`-d`).
3. Spelling doubles/undoubles consistently with the vowel-length rules already established (`ik
   begon → wij begonnen`; `ik las → wij lazen`; `ik bleef → wij bleven`).
4. Imperfect plural of `zeggen, kunnen, willen` inserts a `d`: `wij zeiden, wij konden, wij wouden`
   (`wouden` flagged as more spoken-register than `wilden`).
5. A verb's own regular/irregular status is unaffected by which separable/inseparable prefix is
   attached to it (`schijnen→scheen/geschenen`, `verschijnen→verscheen/verschenen` — same paradigm,
   the prefix just changes the meaning and, separately, can change which perfect auxiliary is used:
   `De zon heeft geschenen` [hebben] vs. `Piet is verschenen` [zijn]).

A consolidated table of ~35 irregular verbs and their present/imperfect/perfect-participle/gloss is
given (too large to reproduce verbatim per copyright discipline; the following notable/new-to-this-
book forms and their principal parts are worth recording individually since they recur throughout
later lessons): `blijven→bleef→gebleven(z)`, `krijgen→kreeg→gekregen`, `lijken→leek→geleken`,
`schijnen→scheen→geschenen`, `verschijnen→verscheen→verschenen(z)`, `beginnen→begon→begonnen`,
`vinden→vond→gevonden`, `liggen→lag(lagen)→gelegen`, `geven→gaf(gaven)→gegeven`,
`lezen→las(lazen)→gelezen`, `nemen→nam(namen)→genomen`, `lopen→liep→gelopen`,
`worden→werd→geworden(z)`, `houden→hield→gehouden`, `komen→kwam(kwamen)→gekomen(z)`,
`staan→stond→gestaan`, `zien→zag(zagen)→gezien`, `denken→dacht→gedacht`, `gaan→ging→gegaan(z)`,
`weten→wist→geweten`, `eten→at(aten)→gegeten`, `moeten→moest→gemoeten`, `mogen→mocht→gemogen`,
`kunnen→kon(den)→gekund`, `vragen→vroeg→gevraagd`, `willen→wilde/wou(den)→gewild`,
`zeggen→zei(den)→gezegd`, `lachen→lachte→gelachen`, `heten→heette→geheten`, `bakken→bakte→gebakken`.
(Verbs marked `(z)` take `zijn` in the perfect.)

### `worden` — first appearance ("to become")
Introduced via the lesson-title dialogue `Klaas is ziek geworden` ("Klaas fell/became ill" — literally
"has become sick"). Perfect: `is geworden` (zijn-auxiliary). Distinct from `zijn` ("to be," describing
a state) — `worden` marks the transition into a state.

### Hebben-vs-zijn perfect auxiliary — refinement for motion verbs
Extends 032c's Les-4 rule (auxiliary is "lexically fixed per verb"): **most verbs of motion** (but
explicitly excluding `komen` and `gaan`, which always take `zijn`) can take **either** `hebben` or
`zijn` in the perfect, depending on whether the sentence specifies a directional/locative endpoint
(`waar naartoe`/`waarvandaan`). `zijn` is used when a place moved to/from is specified (`Ze zijn van
Rusland naar China gereisd`, `We zijn naar het strand gelopen`); `hebben` is used otherwise, when the
motion itself (not its destination) is what's being described (`Hij heeft veel gereisd`, `Hebben
jullie gelopen of gefietst?`).

### Postpositions (Послелоги)
A newly named word class: several prepositions (`in, op, om`, and others) can be placed **after** the
noun they govern instead of before it. When postposed, this construction — combined with a verb of
motion — typically signals **crossing/entering the boundary of** the place named, rather than mere
location within it: `We fietsten in het bos` ("we cycled [around] in the forest," locative) vs. `We
fietsten het bos in` ("we cycled into the forest," boundary-crossing); similarly `op het strand`
(location) vs. `het strand op` (onto the beach, from elsewhere); `om de hoek` (prepositional, "around
the corner") vs. `de hoek om` (postpositional, "around/past the corner," with a verb of motion).

### `over`, `uit`, `tegen` — three more prepositions
- **`over`**: "about/concerning" (`We praatten over jou`), "along/via" (`We liepen over het strand`),
  or, with a time expression, "in [duration from now]" (`We komen over een week` = "we're coming in a
  week").
- **`uit`**: "from/out of" (`Ze kwamen uit de universiteit`, `Ik kom uit Rusland` — this second use
  doubles as "I'm Russian/from Russia"). Frequently used postpositionally with verbs of motion (`Ze
  kwamen de kroeg uit`, `Ik wil de stad uit`). Fixed idioms: `dag in dag uit` ("day in, day out"),
  `jaar in jaar uit` ("year in, year out").
- **`tegen`**: "against" (`Hij is vóór, zij is tegen`), "leaning/propped against" (`De fiets stond
  tegen het raam`), or, with a time expression, "toward/just before" (`Ze komen tegen de avond` =
  "they're arriving toward evening").
- `weten van` = `weten over` (near-synonymous prepositions after this particular verb).

### `van` — two additional uses (beyond 032's earlier senses)
1. Range-marking with a paired `tot`: `van maandag tot vrijdag`, `van vier tot zes`.
2. Marking the source of an obligation/permission, roughly "as far as X is concerned" / "X says so":
   `Ik moet van mijn moeder in bed blijven` ("Mother says/makes me stay in bed" — literally "I must,
   from my mother, stay in bed"), `Je mag van mij blijven` ("as far as I'm concerned, you may stay"),
   `Je hoeft van ons niet te komen` ("you don't have to come, as far as we're concerned").

### `er` — third use: partitive/quantitative pronoun
Extends 032b's reduced-hier/daar use and 032c's existential-construction use with a third:
**partitive `er`**, referring back to an already-mentioned countable quantity ("of it/of them"),
obligatory whenever a bare number (or `geen`, or a bare quantifier) stands as an object without its
noun repeated: `Heb jij nog eieren? — Nee, ik heb er geen meer` ("No, I have no more [of them]"). Used
together with a following numeral to answer a "how many" question completely (`Ik heb er twee` = "I
have two [of them]").

### Modal-verb-without-infinitive construction (`het/dat kan/moet`)
A modal verb can stand alone as the full predicate, with no infinitive at all, when the missing verb
(typically "do") is understood from context — flagged as a distinctively Dutch construction with no
direct Russian equivalent: `Moet dat?` ("Is that necessary?"), `Dat kan wel` ("That's possible"), `Dat
mag niet, hoor!` ("That's not allowed!" — often said to children), `Het mag wel, maar het kan niet`
("It's allowed, but it's [physically/practically] not possible"), `Het hoeft echt niet` ("That's
really not necessary"), `Van mij moet het` ("I insist on it" — combining this construction with the
`van`-obligation sense above).

### Calendar vocabulary
- **Times of day**: `de morgen`/`de ochtend` (synonyms, "morning"), `de middag` ("noon; the period
  12:00–17:00" — a broader span than English "noon"), `de avond` ("evening"), `de nacht` ("night").
  `morgen` is a homograph meaning both "morning" and "tomorrow," disambiguated by context.
- **Days**: `zondag, maandag, dinsdag, woensdag, donderdag, vrijdag, zaterdag`.
- **Months**: `januari, februari, maart, april, mei, juni, juli, augustus, september, oktober,
  november, december`.
- **Seasons**: `de lente`/`het voorjaar` (synonyms, "spring"), `de zomer` ("summer"), `de herfst`/`het
  najaar` (synonyms, "autumn"), `de winter` ("winter").
- **`van-` + time-of-day noun → adverb**: prefixing `van-` to `ochtend/morgen, middag, avond, nacht`
  derives an adverb meaning "this [time period]" (parallel to `vandaag`): `vanmorgen` = "this
  morning." Combined with `gister-`/`morgen-` this yields a fuller grid: `gistermorgen` ("yesterday
  morning"), `morgenochtend` ("tomorrow morning"), `overmorgen` ("the day after tomorrow"),
  `eergister(en)` ("the day before yesterday"), `overdag` ("during the day").
- **`'s Morgens`/`'s Ochtends` etc.**: the `'s` prefix (a fossilized genitive-case remnant, per source
  note) + noun + `-s` suffix derives a "in the mornings / every morning"-type habitual adverb.
- Dates use cardinal, not ordinal, numerals: `Het is vandaag tien januari` ("today is January 10th,"
  literally "ten January"); `Op tien januari waren we in Luik`.
- `jaar` stays grammatically singular after a numeral (`vijf jaar`), unlike `ochtend(en), dag(en),
  week/weken, maand/maanden`, which all pluralize normally after a numeral.
- `het begin`/`het eind(e)` ("the beginning"/"the end") combine with `in`/`aan` respectively in fixed
  phrases: `in het begin van het jaar`, `aan het eind van de winter`.

### Compass directions and Dutch/Belgian geography
`het noorden, het zuiden, het oosten, het westen` — used with `in` for absolute location (`Groningen
ligt in het noorden`) and with `ten noorden/zuiden/oosten/westen van` for relative location (`Assen
ligt ten noorden van Zwolle`). A definite article is always used before a river name (flagged as an
absolute rule, not just a default). Named Dutch provinces/cities and Belgian regions/cities are
introduced as a factual-geography unit (Groningen, Maastricht, Arnhem, Den Haag, Assen,
's-Hertogenbosch, Utrecht, Noord-Brabant, Zeeland, Friesland; Belgium's Flanders/Wallonia split with
Dutch spoken in Vlaanderen and French in Wallonië, Brussel as bilingual capital, Antwerpen as
Belgium's `hoofdstad`... — this geography content is factual/encyclopedic rather than
linguistic and is summarized rather than tabled in full). The Frisian flag and Wadden Islands
(Schiermonnikoog, Ameland, Terschelling, Vlieland, Texel) are illustrated, tying back to the
Klaas/Liesje storyline's vacation setting on Schiermonnikoog.

## Grammar points

### §11.8 Modal auxiliary verbs — overview
Four true modals (kunnen, moeten, mogen, willen) plus durven, hoeven and zullen, which share
modal traits. Common Dutch/English feature: no te before an infinitive governed by a modal. When a
modal + infinitive is the finite element of a subordinate clause, the modal usually still precedes
the infinitive at clause-end (p. 219).

### §11.8.2 The double-infinitive rule
When a perfect/pluperfect/future-perfect/conditional-perfect tense of a modal is followed by another
infinitive, Dutch uses the *infinitive* of the modal, not its past participle: *Ik heb hem niet
kunnen bezoeken* "I haven't been able to visit him." The past participle (gekund, gemogen, gewild,
gemoeten) is used only when the governed infinitive is implied rather than stated, and obligatorily
takes a het: *Ik heb het niet gekund* "I haven't been able to (do it)."

### §11.8.3 Contracted modals in the conditional
Modal imperfects (kon, moest, wilde/wou) are structurally ambiguous between a true imperfect ("was
able to/had to") and a contracted conditional ("would be able to/should"). *Ik wou graag ... (hebben)*
is a fixed "I would like ..." formula; graag accompanies it in statements but is dropped in questions
(*Wil je een kopje koffie?*). Mocht(en) has a formal conditional use meaning "should" (*Mocht het
regenen, dan komen we niet*), always paired with a following dan.

### §11.8.4 Independent (elliptical) modals
kunnen/moeten/mogen/willen frequently stand alone with an implied infinitive (doen, gaan, komen,
hebben, worden): *Dat moet* "It's necessary/must be done", *Kan dat?* "Is that possible?"

### §11.9 The infinitive — the te rule
General rule: a clause-final infinitive takes te, EXCEPT: (1) as a bare impersonal imperative; (2)
after true modals; (3) hoeven takes te (unlike true modals); (4) durven/staan/liggen/lopen/zitten take
te in present/imperfect only, optional in the perfect double-infinitive; (5) the closed set
horen/gaan/helpen/laten/hebben/blijven/zien/komen/leren/doen/wezen/vinden/voelen never take te and use
a double-infinitive in the perfect (except hebben). om ... te is obligatory for "in order to" and
when an infinitive clause modifies a preceding noun (always with a preposition then); otherwise om
is optional but increasingly included in modern usage.

### §11.10 The imperative mood
The bare stem serves all persons/numbers; a u-form exists for extra politeness. eens/even soften a
command. A formal stem+t imperative (historically a plural) appears in elevated written style and
signage/advertising. The infinitive itself is a common hyper-impersonal imperative on signs and in
recipes. The past participle occasionally serves the same function (Opgelet!, Ingerukt mars!). "Let's
..." has two structurally distinct renderings: bare imperative + object pronoun (*Laat ons ... gaan*)
or subject pronoun + inflected verb (*Laten we ... gaan*).

### §11.11 The subjunctive mood
Moribund except in fixed formulas. Present subjunctive differs from indicative only by a -e ending
in 1st/3rd singular (ik werke); past subjunctive of strong verbs likewise adds -e (ik hadde). The one
still-productive form: 3rd-singular subjunctive with subject men renders a formal written imperative
(*Men lette hierop*).

### §11.12 The passive
Formed with worden (not zijn) + past participle, agent introduced by door. In the perfect/pluperfect
the expected double participle (gewassen geworden) is dropped in standard Dutch to gewassen/was
gewassen — but Belgian/Flemish Dutch retains geworden, paralleling German. This creates a genuine
ambiguity trap for learners: *De auto is door hem gewassen* means "has been washed," not "is being
washed" (which requires wordt). A stative (adjectival) reading of a past participle is distinguished
from an active (being-done) reading purely by tense-choice of worden vs. zijn. Passive sentences with
an indirect object most commonly front it with repletive er: *Er werd een boek aan mij gegeven* "I
was given a book." Dutch avoids the passive more than English overall, preferring active
men/je/ze-subject constructions.

### §11.13–11.14 Progressive aspect and emphatic tenses
Three constructions render the progressive: zijn aan 't + infinitive (most common); zitten/staan/
liggen/lopen + te + infinitive (also very common, uses double-infinitive in the perfect); zijn bezig
te + infinitive (least common). Emphatic "do/did [verb]" has no direct Dutch morphological
counterpart — toch and wel (non-synonymous) supply the emphasis instead.

### §11.15–11.16 The present participle and rendering English "-ing"
The Dutch present participle (-d/-de) is comparatively rare, mostly confined to adjectival use
(lachende vrouw), manner adverbs (huilend binnenkomen), a few fixed idioms, and formal/literary
narrative style; it is NOT the default translation of English "-ing." §11.16 catalogues thirteen
distinct paraphrase strategies Dutch uses instead — infinitive + te, door/van + infinitive clause,
dat-clauses, daar/omdat-clauses (causal), temporal subordinators (voordat/nadat/toen/terwijl),
posture-verb double infinitives (stond te wuiven), disguised relative clauses, the infinitive-as-noun
(gerund equivalent), and the graag/liever doen construction for "to like/prefer doing." This is one of
the densest single mismatches between English and Dutch morphosyntax in the whole book.

### §11.17 Reflexive verbs
Two classes: always-reflexive verbs (never take -zelf) and normally-transitive verbs usable
reflexively (which CAN take -zelf, but only under contrastive emphasis — "I dressed Jantje and then
myself"). A third, smaller class of ordinarily-non-reflexive verbs (denken, haten, kennen, lachen,
praten, zeggen, zien, zijn, zorgen — all fixed as bij/in/over zichzelf idioms) requires zichzelf
obligatorily whenever used reflexively at all. All reflexive verbs conjugate with hebben, a notable
typological contrast with the Romance and (per the substandard z'n eigen note) even colloquial-Dutch
tendency toward zijn-marking of self-affecting events.

### §11.18–11.19 Transitive/intransitive and impersonal verbs
Transitivity does not map 1:1 between English and Dutch (antwoorden vs. beantwoorden for "to
answer"). Where Dutch lacks a distinct transitive verb, laten (or formal doen) supplies causativity.
Impersonal verbs (3rd-singular-only, subject always het) split into a set matching English (weather
verbs, gebeuren, betreffen) and a set where Dutch is impersonal but English personalizes the
experiencer (bevallen, spijten, lukken, tegenvallen, verbazen) — a systematic subject/object-flip
translation trap.

### §11.20 Verbal prefixes — the core word-formation engine of the chapter
Separable prefixes (stressed, split off in main-clause finite positions) vs. inseparable prefixes
(unstressed, never split, no ge- inserted between prefix and stem for four of the five sub-types).
be-, ver-, ont-, her-, ge-, er- are the fixed inseparable-only set; several prepositions/adverbs (aan,
achter, door, om, onder, over, voor, mis, vol, weer) can be EITHER separable or inseparable on
different verbs, generally with the separable reading more literal and the inseparable reading more
figurative (óndergaan "to set" vs. ondergáán "to undergo"). This is explicitly Dutch's most productive
native derivational-morphology mechanism ("a very economical means of vocabulary building," p. 269)
and is the single highest-value grammar point in this file for downstream slang-mechanics work: any
sci-fi conlang slang layer modeled on Dutch derivational productivity should look here first, not at
the diminutive system alone (cf. established/003).

### §11.21 Verbs with fixed prepositional objects
A large, only partially productive lexicon-level phenomenon: which preposition a given Dutch verb
takes is often unpredictable from English and must be learned per-verb (overlijden AAN kanker "to die
OF cancer," not van). met/tot alternate with mee/toe when the object is replaced by pronominal er/
hier/daar or relative waar (§11.21.1). A small residual class requires a Dutch preposition where
English needs none at all (bijten IN, trouwen MET) — the mirror image of the far larger class where
English needs a preposition Dutch omits.

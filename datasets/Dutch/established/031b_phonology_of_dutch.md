## Grammar points

### The syllable is a real phonological unit in Dutch, with three roles (§3.1, pp.22–23)

Booij establishes at the outset that syllable structure is not just a segmentation convenience but
does real explanatory work: (a) it's the domain for phonotactic co-occurrence restrictions (e.g.
/pm/ is impossible tautosyllabically but fine heterosyllabically, as in *Abma*); (b) it's the domain
for phonological rules, most importantly **syllable-final devoicing** (traditionally
*Auslautverhärtung*), which the *hoed*/*hoeden* pair shows is genuinely syllable-final, not just
word-final, since it applies word-internally too (e.g. in acronyms like *ABVA* → [ɑpfa]); (c) it's
the bearer of stress, and syllable weight (quantity) determines stress placement (deferred to
Ch.5).

### The Dutch syllable/prosodic-word template (§3.2–3.3, pp.23–29)

Dutch syllables have the universal Onset-Rhyme(Nucleus-Coda) structure, built on the X (skeletal)
tier rather than directly on segments — crucial because long vowels occupy two X-slots. The core
generalization: **a Dutch rhyme contains at most three X-positions** — either a short vowel plus up
to two consonants, or a long vowel/diphthong/schwa plus at most one consonant (*aam, eim, amp* are
fine; **aamp, *eimp, *almp* are not). The **Minimal Rhyme Constraint** requires at least two
X-positions in every rhyme, correctly predicting that Dutch syllables cannot end in a bare short
vowel (*hè, joh, bah, goh* and French loanwords like *cachet* are the only exceptions). **Onsets
contain zero to three X-positions**, with a three-consonant onset always /s/-initial.

A **coronal Appendix of up to three consonants** can follow the maximal rhyme at the right edge of
a *prosodic word* specifically (not just any syllable) — this is the source of Dutch's characteristic
long word-final consonant clusters (*koorts, bedaardst*). Appendix status explains why these
clusters can violate the Sonority Sequencing Generalization freely (obstruent+obstruent clusters of
equal sonority are otherwise barred) — the SSG doesn't apply to Appendix material. Critically, the
Appendix only ever appears at the boundary of a genuine *prosodic word*, which is why compound-
internal and prefix-final position can also host a coronal-obstruent Appendix (*aartsbisschop,
herfstkleuren, koortsachtig*) even though it looks word-internal by ordinary spelling — each
compound member/prefix is its own prosodic-word domain.

### Sonority Sequencing Generalization and the Dutch sonority hierarchy (§3.3, pp.24–25)

Sonority must decrease toward syllable edges. For Dutch, Booij derives a four-way sonority ranking
from three binary features ([vocoid], [approximant], [sonorant]): **Obstruent (rank 0) < Nasal
(rank 1) < Liquid (rank 2) < Glide (rank 3)**. This is what makes *klem* possible but **lkem*
impossible, and correctly predicts the "mirror-image" pattern where the same segment class that's
good as a first member of an onset is bad as a first member of a coda and vice versa (*melk* fine,
*mekl* impossible). Only vowels function as syllable nuclei in Standard Dutch (unlike some
non-standard dialects, which allow syllabic nasals) — this parameter is independent of the sonority
hierarchy itself, since it's liquids, not nasals, that rank just below glides.

### No geminate consonants within a prosodic word; a degemination rule enforces this (§3.3.1, p.28)

Unlike Finnish, Icelandic, or Italian, Dutch disallows two identical adjacent consonants within a
single prosodic word, even where each syllable would independently be well-formed (/bɑk/ + /kɑr/
cannot combine as */bɑkkɑr/). Where morphological concatenation would otherwise create a geminate
(e.g. *grootte* "size" /ɣrot+tə/), an obligatory rule of **Degemination** applies, yielding
[ɣrotə].

### Full survey of legal/illegal Dutch onset and coda clusters (§3.5.1–3.5.3, pp.33–42)

This is the chapter's densest catalogue — kept here as a structured summary rather than
reproducing every individual rule diagram:

- **Rhyme-level restriction**: short vowels cannot be directly followed by /v/ or /z/ in the same
  rhyme (only three loanword exceptions: *razzia, puzzel, mazzel* — and even *puzzel* is commonly
  mispronounced with a lengthened vowel to fit the native pattern).
- **Onset CC clusters**: obstruent+glide (*pj-, tj-, kj-, tw-, dw-, sw-, zw-, fj-*, with *dj-/zj-*
  restricted to loanwords only); obstruent+liquid combine freely (*pl-, bl-, pr-, br-, tr-, dr-,
  fl-, vl-, fr-, vr-, sl-, xl-, xr-, ɣl-, ɣr-*) but **/tl-, dl-, sr-, zr-, zl-/ are categorically
  excluded**; obstruent+nasal is heavily restricted to just **/sm-, sn-, kn-/** as native clusters
  (/pn-, fn-, ɣn-/ only in a handful of loanwords).
- **/s/ has special onset status**: it's the only consonant that can precede a stop-fricative
  sequence that would otherwise violate the Sonority Sequencing Generalization (*sp-, st-, sk-,
  sf-, sx-*), and only five three-consonant onsets exist, all of the shape /s/+stop/fricative+
  liquid: **spr-, str-, skr-, spl-, skl-, sxr-**.
- **Onset never contains two sonorant consonants** (excludes nasal+liquid, nasal+glide,
  liquid+glide combinations as onsets) and **never dominates /h/** (h never clusters).
- **Coda-level restrictions**: /h/ never occurs in codas at all (tied to its dependence on a
  following vowel for Place features). Of logically possible liquid+nasal codas, only **-lm, -rm,
  -rn** occur (*-ln* and any liquid+velar-nasal coda are barred, and no coda consonant at all can
  follow the velar nasal itself). Liquid+obstruent codas are nearly unrestricted (*-lp, -lf, -lv,
  -lk, -lɣ, -rp, -rf, -rv, -rk, -rɣ*, etc.) except that liquids never cluster with /b/ (an accidental
  gap for /lx/, a real gap for /lb, rb/, moot anyway since final devoicing would neutralize them).
  Nasal+fricative codas don't occur (only nasal+stop, always homorganic); nasals never cluster with
  /b/ either. Stop+/s/ codas (*-sp, -st, -sk*) are permitted as genuine (non-Appendix) codas.
- **/j/ and /ʋ/ have asymmetric vowel-selection restrictions**: /j/ only follows *back* long vowels
  (*roei, mooi, saai*), /ʋ/ only follows *front* long vowels (*nieuw, uw, leeuw*), and neither glide
  occurs at all after /ø/. Glides essentially never follow *short* vowels (a handful of loanword
  exceptions: *mais, boiler, detail*, plus the native exclamation *hoi*).
- **Pansyllabic/sequential constraints** (holding across a whole syllable or word, not just one
  constituent): /j/ never precedes /i/; /h/ never precedes schwa (since both lack independent Place
  specification, the combination would leave a syllable with no Place feature at all — and Dutch
  requires every syllable to specify Place); a syllable with a long-vowel nucleus disprefers a
  double-/l/ pattern (*lVl* with a long vowel is marked, e.g. no **loel*) while a syllable with a
  short-vowel nucleus disprefers double-/r/ (*rVr* with a short vowel is barred, e.g. no **ror*);
  two /j/s or two /ʋ/s never co-occur in one syllable (*weeuw* /vev/ "widow" is the sole exception).
- **Morpheme-level (not purely syllable-level) constraints**: clusters of two *voiced* obstruents
  essentially never occur within a native morpheme (loanword exceptions: *labda, budget*); a
  non-coronal-plus-coronal obstruent cluster order is strongly preferred over the reverse (*pt, ft,
  kt, xt* occur; *tp, tk* essentially don't) and dorsal+labial obstruent combinations are excluded
  or vanishingly rare (*km, mk, xm, mx, pk, kp, fk, xf, xp, px* all barred, with a handful of named
  loanword exceptions); a form *sCVC* generally avoids repeating the same non-coronal obstruent
  (*poep* "shit" is fine but **spoep* is barred, alongside the parallel general **CCVC* pattern:
  *mam, non, lol, raar* are fine but **smam, snon, slol, praar* are not) — the **Identical
  Consonant Constraint**.

### The morphological/prosodic-word split governs the phonological shape of grammatical morphemes
(§3.6, pp.47–52, Table 3.2)

A lexical morpheme (noun/verb/adjective/adverb/preposition) must be able to stand as a well-formed
prosodic word on its own: it needs at least one full (non-schwa) vowel, since schwa can never bear
stress and every major-category word needs a syllable to host main stress, and it can never *begin*
with a schwa (even though a schwa-initial syllable is otherwise legal, e.g. the indefinite article
*een* /ən/). Grammatical (non-lexical) morphemes are exempt from this and can be built entirely of
schwa-plus-consonant, or even a bare single consonant in cliticized forms (*'k* "I", *'t* "it").
Booij's Table 3.2 catalogues the full weak/strong-form phonological alternations for Dutch's core
closed-class inventory: determiners (*de* /də/ vs. strong *het* [hɛt]), pronouns (*ik* /ək/ vs.
strong /ik/; *je* /jə/ for both subject "you" and object "you", vs. strong *jij*/*jou*; *hem* /əm/
vs. strong /hɛm/ "him"; *ze* /zə/ serving both "they" and "them", vs. strong *zij*/*hen*), adverbs
(*er, daar, eens* all with weak schwa-bearing forms), prefixes (*be-, ge-, ver-, er-* all
underlyingly /-ə-/), suffixes (the diminutive *-tje* /tjə/ with allomorphs *-jə, -ətjə, -pjə, -kjə*
previewing Ch.4's fuller treatment), and circumfixes (the strong-verb past-participle *ge-...-en*
and weak-verb *ge-...-t/d*). This table is a genuinely new, systematic resource beyond what
017/017b/017c cover — a compact reference for the full weak-pronoun/clitic paradigm's underlying
phonological shapes, directly relevant to any later analysis of how Dutch clitic forms might get
reduced/eroded further in a synthesized slang register.

### Morphological and prosodic structure are systematically related but not isomorphic (§3.6,
pp.47–52)

Cohering suffixes (most suffixes) fuse prosodically with their stem into one prosodic word; a
smaller class of non-cohering suffixes with their own full vowel (*-achtig, -baar, -dom, -heid,
-ling, -loos, -schap, -zaam*) form independent prosodic-word domains, exactly like prefixes and
each member of a compound. This is demonstrated three ways: (1) syllable-final devoicing applies at
compound-internal boundaries (*handappel* → [hɑntɑpəl], not *[hɑndɑpəl]); (2) Prevocalic Schwa
Deletion (a rule that deletes a schwa before a following vowel to avoid hiatus) applies only
*within* a single prosodic-word domain — so it fires word-internally (*kaden* /kadə+ən/ → [kadən])
but is blocked across a prefix, compound, or *-achtig*-suffix boundary (*zijdeachtig* keeps its
schwa: [zɛidəɑxtəx]); (3) conjunction-gapping ("agri- and horticulture" style constructions) can
only elide a genuine prosodic word, which is why a cohering suffix like *-ig* cannot be gapped in
place of non-cohering *-achtig*, but a cohering suffix *can* be gapped together with the stem
material it prosodically cohered with (*bouwers* gapped as a whole prosodic word even though it
isn't a morphological constituent). A word can also lose its morphological transparency over time
and be reanalyzed with a *different, simpler* prosodic-word parse (*aardappel* "potato",
historically *aard*+*appel* "earth-apple", now resyllabifies straight through as if it were a
monomorphemic word). Compound "linking phonemes" (the /s/ or /ə/ sometimes inserted between two
compound members, e.g. *hondebrood* "dog's food") are not part of either morphological constituent
but prosodically fuse with the *preceding* prosodic word — shown by voicing preservation and by
gapping behavior.

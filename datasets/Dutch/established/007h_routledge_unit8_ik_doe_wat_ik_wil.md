## Grammar points

### 1. Hoeven vs. moeten vs. mogen

Dutch draws a three-way modal contrast that doesn't map cleanly onto English "must/have
to/may/can." *Moeten* expresses obligation ("must/have to"). *(Niet) hoeven* is its negative
counterpart specifically for absence of obligation — "don't have to/needn't" — and is normally used
only with a negator (*niet*, *geen*) or a restrictive word; it is rarely used affirmatively on its
own (p. 244: "Moet jij dit nog afmaken? / Nee, dat hoef ik niet te doen"). The text highlights that
Dutch speakers often contrast *(niet) hoeven* against *mogen* rather than against *moeten* —
i.e., "I don't have to (hoef niet)" vs. "I am allowed to (mag)" describe two independent axes
(obligation vs. permission) that can combine in either direction: *Ik hoef geen hoofddoekje te
dragen. Ik mag een hoofddoekje dragen* ("I don't have to wear a headscarf. I am allowed to wear
a headscarf.") — absence of obligation does not imply absence of permission, and the unit's own
debate dialogue turns on exactly this distinction (a woman is not obligated to wear a headscarf by
Dutch convention, but is separately permitted to by her own choice/faith).

### 2. Reflexive verbs and reflexive pronouns

Reflexive verbs require an obligatory pronoun referring back to the subject even where English
would use no pronoun at all (*zich aanpassen* "to adapt oneself" vs. English "to adapt"). The
reflexive pronoun set: *me* (ik), *je* (jij), *zich* (u/hij/zij/het/zij-plural), *ons* (wij), *je*
(jullie). Word order: the pronoun immediately follows the finite verb (*Ik pas me aan aan jou*),
and inverts along with the verb under normal V2 rules (*Hoe voel je je?*). Some verbs are
reflexive-only (*zich schamen* "to be ashamed," *zich vervelen* "to be bored," *zich bemoeien met*
"to interfere with"); others alternate between a reflexive sense (referring back to the subject)
and a plain transitive sense with a distinct object (*zich ontwikkelen* "to develop [oneself]" vs.
*iets ontwikkelen* "to develop something," e.g. *Hij ontwikkelt zijn taalgevoel* "he's developing
his feel for language" vs. *Het voetbalelftal heeft zich ontwikkeld* "the team has developed
[itself]").

### 3. Separable reflexive verbs

Reflexive verbs can also be separable compound verbs, combining both patterns: the reflexive
pronoun stays with the subject-adjacent position while the separable particle moves to clause-final
position, e.g. *zich aankleden* "to dress (oneself)" → *Hij kleedt zich pas om twaalf uur aan*
("He doesn't get dressed until noon" — literally: he dresses himself only around twelve o'clock
[particle] on). *zich iets aantrekken van* "to let something bother/worry you" and *zich
uitdrukken* "to express oneself" follow the same pattern.

### 4. Past participle and present participle used as adjectives/adverbs

A past participle can modify a noun adjectivally, taking normal adjectival agreement: *in het
zwart geklede vrouwtjes* ("women dressed in black," from *kleden* "to dress"), *een felgeel
geschilderde binnenplaats* ("a bright-yellow-painted courtyard," from *schilderen* "to paint").
The present participle is formed by adding *-d* to the infinitive (*sprankelen* → *sprankelend*
"sparkling"; *verblinden* → *verblindend* "blinding") and can function as an adjective or adverb.
A present-participle phrase frequently substitutes for a relative clause or subclause with the same
propositional content but a more stylistically compact/"flowing" effect — the unit's travel-writing
texts (Santorini, Brazil) are built heavily on this device:

| Present-participle version | Equivalent relative/subclause version |
|---|---|
| sprankelend, verblindend wit | het wit dat sprankelt en verblindt |
| glanzend satijn | het satijn dat glanst |
| een verkoelende duik | de duik die verkoelt |
| ademberovende kleuren | de kleuren die de adem beroven |
| schommelend en dromend lig ik in mijn hangmat | ik lig in mijn hangmat terwijl ik schommel en droom |

### 5. Structuring/signalling words and word order

The unit organizes discourse connectives by function — structuring points (*ten eerste, ten
tweede, verder, ook, tevens*), adding an argument (*bovendien, daar komt bij dat, trouwens*),
signalling a conclusion (*dus, kortom*), contrasting (*tegelijkertijd, hoewel, aan de ene/andere
kant, daarentegen, niettemin, toch*), qualifying (*althans, tenminste, volgens*), and giving
reasons (*daardoor, daarom, omdat, de reden daarvoor*). The grammatical behavior of a signalling
word depends on its category: an **adverb** placed sentence-initially triggers inversion, keeping
the finite verb in second position (*Tevens is het druggebruik afgenomen*; *Toch geloven veel
mensen hem niet*). Four exceptions — *tenminste, althans, trouwens, kortom* — are followed by a
comma and then normal (non-inverted) main-clause order when sentence-initial (*Trouwens, dat is
laatst nog door de minister-president gezegd*). A **subordinating conjunction** used as a
structuring word (*hoewel, omdat, daar komt bij dat*) instead opens a subclause, sending the
finite verb to clause-final position (*Omdat dit nu eenmaal de beste oplossing is*).

### 6. Colour intensifiers and fixed colour compounds

Dutch intensifies colour adjectives by prefixing a degree word directly onto the colour, written
as one compound word (not a separate adverb): *licht-* (light), *fel-* (bright/vivid), *donker-*
(dark), *diep-* (deep), *zacht-* (soft), *hard-* (harsh/hard), *warm-* (warm) + colour, e.g.
*lichtroze* "light pink," *felgeel* "bright yellow," *donkerpaars* "dark purple," *diepblauw*
"deep blue," *zachtbruin* "soft brown," *hardgroen* "harsh green," *warmgrijs* "warm grey." A
separate small set of colour compounds is lexically fixed to one specific colour rather than
productive: *strakblauw* ("crisp/clear blue," lit. "taut-blue"), *gitzwart* ("jet black"),
*spierwit* ("snow white," lit. "muscle-white"), *lijkbleek* ("deathly pale," lit. "corpse-pale").

### Grammar-adjacent register note: terms for immigrants

The unit's own explanatory text (pp. 239–240) explicitly flags a register/usage caution around
several near-synonyms for "immigrant": *allochtoon* (contrasted with *autochtoon*, "native Dutch"),
*nieuwkomer* "newcomer," *immigrant*, *buitenlander* "foreigner." It states outright that
*gastarbeider* "guest worker" is no longer in active use (**archaic** per this source, tied to the
1960s–70s labour-migration period), and that all of these terms are broad enough to warrant care
in use — the source recommends more specific alternatives such as *Marokkaanse Nederlander* or
*Nederlander van Marokkaanse/Turkse/Somalische afkomst* ("Dutch person of Moroccan/Turkish/Somali
descent").

## Grammar points

### The pluperfect tense (pp. 164-165)

Formed identically to the perfect (`hebben`/`zijn` + past participle) but with the *past tense* of
the auxiliary (`had(den)`/`was`/`waren`) rather than the present. `Zijn` vs. `hebben` selection
follows the same rule already established in this corpus (verbs of motion take `zijn`); the reading
text's worked example contrasts `hadden genomen`/`hadden niet geslapen`/`hadden een ongeluk gehad`
(non-motion, `hebben`) against `waren aangekomen`/`waren gereden`/`waren beland` (motion, `zijn`).

### Compound nouns (pp. 165-166)

Unlike English, Dutch compounds are always written as a single word regardless of length, and the
compound's grammatical gender is determined by its *final* element, not its first. A linking `-e-`,
`-en-`, or `-s-` is sometimes inserted between elements with no fully reliable rule (a rough
first-element-plural-connotation heuristic is given, with an explicitly flagged exception,
`pereboom`). See vocabulary table for the full worked example set and the 1995 spelling-reform note.

### Relative pronouns with prepositions (pp. 167-169)

Extends Lesson 11's `die`/`dat` relative system: when a relative pronoun is governed by a
preposition, personal antecedents take **preposition + wie** (`aan wie`, `met wie`, `van wie` —
literally "to/with/from whom") while non-personal antecedents take **waar- + preposition** as one
fused word (`waaraan`, `waarmee`, `waarvan`). English can drop the relative pronoun in this
construction ("the girl I gave the book to") but Dutch never can. In spoken Dutch two further
simplifications are common: (1) the formal person/thing `wie`/`waar` distinction is often collapsed,
with the `waar-` construction used even for people; (2) the fused `waar-` + preposition form is
routinely split, with the preposition moved to clause-final position (`de stoel waar hij op zat`
rather than `de stoel waarop hij zat`) — explicitly marked as the more natural spoken-register
choice over the fused written form.

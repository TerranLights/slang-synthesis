## Grammar points

### `er` — third sense: indicating a quantity ("of them")

Building on Les 1's two `er`-senses, this lesson introduces a third: `er` indicating a certain
number/quantity of something, translating English "of them" — **Ik heb er nog duizenden in te
halen** ("I've still got thousands of them to catch up with"), where `er` refers back to *croquettes*
mentioned earlier. Crucially, Dutch **requires** `er` here even where English would not add "of
them": **Hoeveel auto's heb je? Ik heb er twee.** ("How many cars have you got? I've got two.").
p. 31.

### Informal written style — writing down what is normally only spoken

The book explicitly analyzes "Mijn happen" as an example of informal *written* Dutch that imitates
spoken register, citing `broodje bal` as characteristic. It also explains the passage's frequent
short sentences with no finite verb, or no verb at all, as a deliberate stylistic device — e.g.
**Nooit meteen zeggen.** ("Never say [what you want] straight away."). A further ellipsis example:
**Daarentegen wel honderden gehaktballen** omits a repeated verb-phrase from the previous sentence
(*kwamen ... over de vloer*) — made possible because the particle `wel` signals a contradiction back
to the prior sentence, doing the work the missing verb would otherwise do. pp. 30–31.

### `er` — indefinite-subject complement, with inversion

A further `er`-as-complement-to-an-indefinite-subject example (cf. Les 1 §1): **Rond het jaar 1500
ontstond er echter een nieuwe mode** ("Around the year 1500 a new fashion arose"). Subject/verb
inversion occurs here because the sentence opens with an adverbial phrase (`Rond het jaar 1500`) —
`er` still precedes the indefinite subject (`een nieuwe mode`), which is the sentence's true final
element. This use of introductory `er` is only possible when the noun subject it complements carries
an **indefinite** article: `Een kopje staat op tafel` → `Er staat een kopje op tafel` is valid, but
the same transformation is ungrammatical starting from the definite `Het kopje staat op tafel`.
p. 38.

### `er is`/`er zijn` — existential "there is/there are"

The Dutch equivalent of "there is/there are" is `er is`/`er zijn`. Example: **En dan waren er de
wijngaarden!** ("And then there were the vineyards!") — inversion occurs because the sentence opens
with `dan`, not the subject; note also the past tense `waren`. p. 38.

### `men` — the indefinite pronoun "one"/"they"/"people"

`Men` literally means "one," but English "one" carries stylistic connotations (formality, distance)
that Dutch `men` does not have. The source recommends rendering it with "they" or "people" rather
than literal "one": **Dus ging men groenten kweken.** ("So they began growing vegetables.");
**Wat kweekte men toen?** ("What did people grow in those days?"). pp. 38–39.

### `om + te` + infinitive — purpose clause

`Om` introduces the clause; `te + infinitive` sits at the end. Expresses purpose/aim, paraphrasable
as "in order to" though usually just translated "to": **Ik ging de stad in om boeken te kopen.**
("I went into town [in order] to buy books."). Where the sense of purpose is weak, `om` can be
dropped without losing the "in order to" reading being present at all: **Vergeet niet (om) te
komen.** ("Don't forget to come."). With a separable verb, `te` is inserted between the prefix and
the rest of the verb: **Ik heb er duizenden in te halen** (`inhalen` → `in te halen`). pp. 40–41.

### Bare infinitive — instructions, commands, and informal imperative

On signs, a bare infinitive gives a general instruction: **Niet roken.** ("No smoking."); **Hier geen
fietsen plaatsen a.u.b.** ("No bikes please."). In spoken Dutch, the infinitive can also replace the
imperative for a less formal command: **Niet doen!** ("Don't do that!"); **Opschieten!** ("Get a move
on!") — and a weaker, encouragement-flavored variant appears in "Mijn happen" itself, where the
narrator's internal voice could be addressing either himself or the reader: **Nooit meteen zeggen.**;
**Eerst rustig kijken...** . pp. 40–41.

### Infinitive as noun

Any Dutch infinitive can be used as a noun, and the resulting noun is always a `het`-word — a
genuinely productive word-formation strategy the source recommends as "a simple way to increase your
word-power": `kweken` (to cultivate) → `het kweken` (cultivation); `reizen` (to travel) → `het reizen`
(travelling, from Les 1's Leestekst 2). p. 41.

> **het kweken** = kweken (infinitive, "to cultivate") + zero-derivation to noun status, forced
> `het`-gender — illustrates that Dutch infinitive-nominalization is a fully productive zero-affix
> process (no suffix added), distinguishing it from the derivational `-erij`/`-heid`-type
> nominalizations already documented in `established/001`.

### `maar` — untranslatable familiarity marker

In `... dat ik maar vast een broodje ros neem`, `maar` adds no translatable meaning of its own — the
sentence just means "...that I'll have a beef roll meanwhile." The source characterizes `maar` here
as adding "a tone of familiarity" and flags its use as typical of conversational Dutch — a discourse
particle finding directly comparable to `established/002`'s Ch.16 particle inventory (`eens`, `even`,
`toch`, `wel`, `hoor`), extending that particle set with `maar` in this specific "no propositional
content, register/tone marker only" function. p. 39.

### Polite requests — `mogen` and `misschien`

A request can be made polite either with the auxiliary `mogen` (the infinitive `hebben` is optional):
**Mogen we de menukaart (hebben)?** ("May we have the menu?"); **Mag ik de rekening (hebben)?** ("May
I have the bill?") — or by inserting `misschien` into the request: **Heeft u misschien een tafel voor
twee personen?** ("Would you have a table for two?"). This directly echoes and generalizes Les 1's
`misschien`-as-softener finding. p. 43.

### "Please" — two distinct Dutch equivalents with different functions

`Alstublieft`/`alsjeblieft` (choose per `u`/`je` register) **reinforces a request**: **Mogen wij de
menukaart alstublieft?** It has a second, distinct function: said *while handing something to
someone* (not while asking) — the source notes it would be impolite to hand something over in
silence, and the model dialogue's waitress says it precisely at the moment of handing over the menu.
By contrast, `graag` **confirms** a request already made, functioning like a bare "yes, please":
**Ja, graag.** This is a genuine three-way functional split (reinforce a request / accompany a
hand-over / confirm a request) hiding behind what a beginner course would gloss simply as "please."
pp. 44.

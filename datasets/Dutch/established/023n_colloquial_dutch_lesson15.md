## Grammar points

### *Al*, *alle(n)* and *allemaal*; *beide(n)* and *allebei* (pp. 190-192)

Two parallel three-/two-way synonym sets for "all"/"both," differing only in sentence position and
register, not meaning. `Al` precedes a determiner+noun (`al de kinderen`); `alle` can precede a bare
noun (`alle kinderen`, ambiguous between "all children" and "all the children") or stand
independently (sounds stilted); `allemaal`, always immediately following the finite verb when it
refers to the subject, is the form the source explicitly identifies as most common in speech. The
same three-way pattern (preceding-determiner form / independent-but-stilted form / most-common-
in-speech post-verb form) repeats exactly for `beide`/`beiden`/`allebei`. `Al`'s other meaning,
"already," is disambiguated purely by context.

### *Alles*, *iets*, *niets* and *niemand* (pp. 192-193)

These map to English "everything"/"something"/"nothing"/"nobody" but — unlike English, which swaps
"something"/"nothing"/"somebody"/"nobody" for "anything"/"anybody" in questions and negated
sentences — Dutch makes no such substitution; `iets`/`niets`/`niemand` are used unchanged regardless
of sentence type. When followed by a relative clause, `alles`/`iets`/`niets` require the relative
pronoun **`wat`** rather than `die`/`dat` (they have no grammatical gender for `die`/`dat` to track),
though the source notes some speakers use `dat` instead. See vocabulary table for the further,
source-flagged colloquial variant where `wat` itself substitutes for `iets`.

### The conditional and conditional perfect tenses (pp. 194-197)

The conditional (`would` + infinitive) is formed with **`zou`/`zouden`** + infinitive, with an `als`
("if") clause optionally preceding or following. The `als`-clause verb can stay in the imperfect
(neutral) or itself take `zou/zouden` + infinitive for a more emphatic hypothetical force
(comparable to English "was/were to," though far less formal-sounding in Dutch than in English).
`Zou/zouden zijn` and `zou/zouden hebben` commonly contract to `was/waren` and `had/hadden`, with the
`als`-clause disambiguating the intended reading. The conditional perfect ("would have" + past
participle) is built the same way one level up (`zou ... gekocht hebben`), with the same
`had`/`was`-contraction option available to reduce a three-verb cluster to two. "Could have" is
expressed as `had kunnen` + infinitive (a double-infinitive cluster, not literally "could" +
"have"); "should have" is the parallel `had moeten` + infinitive. "Should" alone is strictly `zou
moeten`, but the source notes `moest` and even plain `moet` are also commonly used for this sense in
practice — a documented modal-simplification pattern in everyday speech.

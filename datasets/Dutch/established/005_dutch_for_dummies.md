# Dutch — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Dutch For Dummies, Part 1 (Chapters 1–3)

**Source:** Margreet Kwakernaak, *Dutch For Dummies* (Wiley, UK edition 2008, pronunciation
notation by Mathijs Petri), Chapter 1 "You Already Know Some Dutch" (pp. 9–26), Chapter 2 "Low-cal
Grammar: Just the Basics" (pp. 27–48), Chapter 3 "Number Magic: All Kinds of Counting" (pp. 49–64).
Clean text layer (`pdftotext -layout`); no vision-reading needed. PDF-page-to-printed-page offset
verified empirically at a constant +20 (PDF array index − 20 = printed page) across multiple
anchor points spanning the whole book.

**Non-redundant-supplement note.** This project already has `established/001_chapters1-3_...md`,
`002_...`, `003_...` extracted from *Dutch: An Essential Grammar* (Shetter & Van der
Cruysse-Van Antwerpen). Checked against those first:

- **Skipped as redundant:** Chapter 1's vowel/diphthong/consonant pronunciation tables, the `g`/`ch`
  guttural-sound explanation, and the word-stress rule (main stress on first syllable; unstressed
  prefixes `be- ge- her- er- ont- ver-`) — all already fully covered, with the same prefix list, in
  `001_chapters1-3_pronunciation_spelling_plural.md` §1.5. This book's presentation is strictly
  simpler (a phrasebook aimed at absolute beginners) and adds nothing beyond what `001` already has.
- **Kept as genuinely new:** everything else. This book is a phrasebook/conversation guide, not a
  systematic reference grammar, so its content is organized by communicative situation rather than
  by grammatical topic — its core grammar points (`de`/`het` article assignment heuristics, present/
  perfect/past tense formation, the five word-order constructions, cardinal/ordinal number
  formation, clock-telling) are not covered by any of `001`–`003` and are extracted in full below.
  Chapter 1's sociolinguistic sidebars (Dunglish, street slang, loanwords from Surinamese/other
  immigrant languages) are unique to this book and high-value for a slang-synthesis project — no
  other extracted Dutch source so far has captured genuine youth/street slang vocabulary.

**Coverage note:** every grammar point and every distinct (non-compositional) vocabulary item from
these three chapters, plus explicit register annotations. Cardinal numbers 21–99 are skipped as
individual rows since they are fully regular compositions (unit + `en` + ten) captured once as a
grammar rule — see "Cardinal number formation" below; only the numbers 0–20, the tens, and a few
compound examples are tabulated. Repeated drill-style example sentences that reuse already-captured
vocabulary are skipped.

## Grammar points

### `De` vs. `het` — the two-way definite article system (pp.28–31)

Dutch collapsed its historical three-gender noun system to a functional two-way split: most nouns
(the former masculine/feminine classes, now largely merged) take `de`; a residual neuter class
takes `het`. All plurals take `de` regardless of the singular's article. The book gives no formal
rule for predicting `de`/`het` from first principles — it instead offers heuristic categories that
reliably predict one or the other: `de` for professions, vegetables, trees, plants, mountains, and
rivers; `het` for diminutives (`-je`/`-tje`, cf. `established/003`'s diminutive findings — the two
sources corroborate each other independently), and two-syllable nouns beginning with the unstressed
prefixes `be-`, `ge-`, `ver-`, `ont-`. The pedagogical framing ("most nouns take `de`, so guess `de`
if unsure, and a Dutch listener will forgive the article error") is itself a real, source-stated
strategy worth preserving for slang-synthesis purposes — it implies native speakers are tolerant of
article errors specifically, a sociolinguistic data point about which grammatical errors mark
non-native speech as more/less jarring.

### Indefinite article `een` vs. the numeral `één` (p.29)

Both are spelled `een` in running (unstressed) text, but the numeral, when it needs to be
distinguished, is written with an acute-accented double `e` (`één`) and is pronounced with a full
stressed vowel (`ayn`) vs. the article's reduced/unstressed vowel (`ern`). This is a genuine
minimal pair distinguished only by stress/orthographic accent — relevant to how a derived slang
form might play with stress-based ambiguity.

### Adjective agreement: the `-e` ending (pp.31–32)

An adjective takes an `-e` ending whenever it precedes a noun combined with `de` or `het` (definite)
or a plural noun. The one systematic exception: an adjective before a *singular* `het`-word combined
with the *indefinite* article `een` drops the `-e` (`het mooie jongetje` "the pretty boy" vs. `een
mooi jongetje` "a pretty boy"). `de`-words keep the `-e` in both definite and indefinite contexts
(`de mooie man` / `een mooie man`).

### Cardinal number formation (pp.49–52)

Numbers 0–19 are memorized individually (no productive rule). From 20 onward, Dutch names the unit
digit *before* the ten (unlike English), joined by `en`: `eenentwintig` ("one-and-twenty" = 21),
`tweeëntwintig` (22), etc. — structurally identical to German and to older/dialectal English ("four
and twenty blackbirds"). Hundreds and thousands are built additively and regularly (`tweehonderd`
200, `drieduizend` 3000). Numbers over 1000 that aren't round thousands are conventionally read two
ways: the "long" pre-metric-style construction that treats them as multiples of a hundred starting
from 11 (`elfhonderd` = 1100, lit. "eleven-hundred", paralleling English "nineteen-hundred" for 1900)
or — now more common per the source — the straightforward `duizend`-based reading (`tweeduizend
honderdeen` for 2101). This "elfhonderd-style" pattern surviving alongside the more transparent
thousand-based one, with the source noting most speakers now prefer the latter, is itself a live
instance of a numeral system undergoing simplification within living memory — worth flagging for
any conlang mechanics analysis of number-word erosion.

### The four-quarter clock system (pp.57–59)

Unlike English's two-half clock (past/to), Dutch divides the hour into four quarters, always framed
relative to the *nearest half-hour*, not just the nearest full hour: `vijf over twee` (2:05), `kwart
over twee` (2:15), then critically `tien voor half drie` (2:20, lit. "ten before half three") and
`half drie` (2:30, lit. "half three" — meaning half-way *to* three, not half past two as an
English speaker would expect), then `vijf over half drie` (2:35, "five past half three"), `kwart
voor drie` (2:45). The half-hour is named for the *upcoming* hour throughout, which is the single
biggest source-flagged pitfall for English-speaking learners.

### Five basic word-order constructions (pp.44–48)

The book presents Dutch V2 (verb-second) word order as five situational rules rather than one
underlying principle:
1. **Normal declarative:** subject–verb–rest (`Ik werk bij een bank`).
2. **Fronted time expression → inversion:** verb comes immediately after the time expression, before
   the subject (`Morgen gaat mijn partner naar Rotterdam`).
3. **Fronted place expression → inversion:** same pattern (`In Amsterdam wonen veel mensen`).
4. **Fronted `misschien`/`soms`/`toch` → inversion:** these three adverbs specifically trigger the
   same verb-first-after-adverb pattern (`Misschien ga ik naar Den Haag`).
5. **Questions:** verb-initial for yes/no questions (`Ga je naar huis?`), or question-word +
   verb + rest for wh-questions (`Waar woon je?`).
Rules 2–4 are really one V2 phenomenon (any fronted non-subject constituent inverts the following
subject-verb order) presented pedagogically as three separate rules — worth noting for mechanics
analysis: the book's own organization obscures a more general underlying rule, a useful example of
how pedagogical simplification can under- or over-generalize a real grammatical pattern.

### Present tense conjugation (pp.35–37)

Regular pattern: stem (infinitive minus `-en`) for `ik`; stem + `-t` for `jij`/`u`/`hij`/`zij`/`het`;
full infinitive for `wij`/`jullie`/`zij` (plural). A spelling-only complication: verbs whose stem
vowel is long in an open syllable (e.g. `slapen` → stem `sla-`) must double the vowel in the singular
forms (`ik slaap`) purely to preserve the long-vowel spelling convention documented independently in
`established/001` — the *sound* doesn't change, only the *spelling* must compensate since a single
vowel before a single consonant at a word boundary would default to the short reading.

### Perfect tense formation: `hebben`/`zijn` + past participle (pp.37–39)

The perfect tense (not the simple past) is the Dutch default for narrating past events in speech —
the book states plainly that Dutch prefers the perfect where English would often use simple past.
Auxiliary choice: `hebben` for most verbs, `zijn` for verbs of motion/process/change-of-state
(`vliegen`, `aankomen`, `gaan`). Regular past participles: `ge-` + stem + `-d`/`-t`. Irregular past
participles also virtually always keep the `ge-` prefix and often end in `-en`, with unpredictable
stem-vowel changes (`drinken` → `gedronken`, not the learner-error overgeneralization `gedrinkt`
the source explicitly calls out as a common non-native mistake).

### Past tense formation and its restricted use (pp.39–43)

Regular past tense: stem + `-de`/`-te` (singular), stem + `-den`/`-ten` (plural) — voicing-assimilation
conditioned the same way as the `-d`/`-t` past-participle choice (cross-reference: `established/002`
touches related spoken/written register distinctions but doesn't cover this specific verb-morphology
rule). Irregular verbs make unpredictable vowel changes in the past stem. `hebben` and `zijn` are
called out as "very irregular," following no productive pattern at all. Crucially, the source frames
past-tense use as the *exception* rather than the default: reserved for (1) describing a background
situation, (2) habitual past actions, (3) narrating a sequence of discrete completed events — everywhere
else, the perfect tense is preferred even for objectively past events.

### Future reference via present tense, `zullen`, or `gaan` (pp.42–44)

Dutch has no dedicated future-tense verb form. Futurity is expressed three ways: (1) plain present
tense with a time adverbial (`Morgen werk ik thuis` "Tomorrow I work at home" = will work); (2) the
modal auxiliary `zullen` specifically for promises/guarantees/predictions (`Ik zal vanavond koken` "I
will cook tonight" — this reading is described as carrying commitment/certainty); (3) `gaan` + infinitive
for near-certain planned events (`Ik ga boodschappen doen` "I'm going to do the shopping"). This
three-way split by *pragmatic force* (plain scheduling vs. promise vs. planned intention) rather than
by strict tense is a genuinely interesting mechanics point for later slang-synthesis work on modality.

### Ordinal numbers and their idiomatic uses (pp.55–57)

Ordinals `eerste`…`tiende` are formed with `-de`/`-ste` suffixes (not tabulated as a productive rule
by the source beyond the first ten, since English-speaking learners find higher ordinals like
`twintigste` "20th" hard to pronounce and rarely needed). Notably, Dutch uses an ordinal rather than
a cardinal number when stating age in certain constructions (`op mijn achttiende` "at 18," lit. "at
my eighteenth") — contrasted with English's cardinal-only age expressions. `Eerst…dan…daarna` ("first
… then … after that") functions as a general discourse-sequencing device independent of the ordinal
numeral system proper.

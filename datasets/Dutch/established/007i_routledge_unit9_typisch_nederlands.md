## Grammar points

### 1. Verbs with *te* + infinitive

A specific (open) class of verbs takes an infinitive complement introduced by *te*, regardless of
tense or voice. The unit's own (non-exhaustive) list: *achten, beginnen, beloven, beweren, blijken,
denken, durven, geloven, hoeven, hopen, lijken, proberen, schijnen, vergeten, vragen, weigeren,
zeggen*. Examples: *Ik had gedacht volgend jaar naar Djibouti op vakantie te gaan* ("I had thought of
going on holiday to Djibouti next year" — *te* survives unchanged under the pluperfect); *Men werd
geacht enkel met gelijkgezinden te voetballen... en de liefde te bedrijven* (*te* survives under the
passive). With a separable verb, *te* is infixed between the separable particle and the verb stem:
*opbellen* → *Ik vergeet steeds mijn ouders op te bellen* ("I keep forgetting to call my parents").

### 2. Infinitive constructions without *te*

A distinct, closed set of verbs combines with a bare infinitive (no *te*), separate from the modal
verbs (already covered elsewhere): *blijven, gaan, horen, komen, laten, zien, zijn*. Examples: *Ik
zag Margot gisteren met haar nieuwe vriendje arm in arm langslopen* ("I saw Margot walking arm in arm
with her new boyfriend yesterday"); *Nita heeft haar haar laten verven* ("Nita had her hair dyed").

### 3. Cohesion and topic–comment word order

Beyond referring words (*er, dat, deze, die, hem*) and connective markers (*omdat, ook, hoewel*),
Dutch text cohesion is also achieved through word order: old information (the **topic**) typically
opens the sentence, with new information (the **comment**/focus) following. The unit walks through a
worked example showing how each sentence's subject picks up material named in the previous sentence
(*Deze relativerende benadering* → refers back; *Maar die eigen identiteit* → *maar* signals contrast,
*die eigen identiteit* refers back; *Een optelsom* → refers back to the immediately preceding
sentence). These conventions are not absolute — deliberately violating topic-comment order in an
informal text can create surprise, emphasis, or contrast (illustrated with *Laat ik eerlijk zeggen
dat...*, which foregrounds a first-person stance rather than old-information continuity).

### 4. Passive voice: form and use

**Form:** present tense = *worden* + past participle (*Ik word gedwongen mijn ontslag te nemen*,
"I'm being forced to resign"); imperfect tense = *werden* + past participle (*Ik werd gedwongen...*).
The perfect tense of the passive is deferred to Unit 11 (see below). The agent, when stated, is
introduced by *door* ("by"); it's grammatically optional, and when omitted the corresponding active
sentence needs an invented/vague subject to translate it (*De bewoners werden zorgvuldig
geselecteerd* → active needs a guessed subject like *Ze selecteerden de bewoners zorgvuldig*).
Impersonal passives with no clear logical subject at all also occur (*Er werden drie scholen
gebouwd*).

**Why use the passive** — the unit lists four functional motivations: (1) the agent is already known
or unimportant (*Wat wordt internationaal gezien als onze deugden?*); (2) discourse/topic-comment
ordering requires it — see §3; (3) stylistic register — formal/academic/official texts lean on the
passive, though overuse makes prose dull; (4) hiding the cause or avoiding apportioning blame — the
unit's worked contrast *De gewonde man werd in de moskee doodgeschoten* (passive, agentless) vs. *De
soldaat schoot de gewonde man in de moskee dood* (active, names the actor) is explicitly framed as a
real editorial choice: a newspaper sympathetic to the authorities involved would favor the passive
version, one critical of them the active version. The same everyday tactical vagueness is illustrated
with *Het belastingformulier moet nog worden ingevuld* (passive, dodges who must fill in the tax
form) vs. the pointed active *Vul jij het belastingformulier in?*

### 5. Register: personal/informal/direct vs. impersonal/formal/careful

The unit contrasts two poles of spoken register with paired examples for each dimension: overstating/
emphasis (*ongelofelijk* "unbelievable" informal vs. *nogal* "rather" formal); talking about feelings
directly (*dat geeft me een nationaal gevoel*) vs. talking about ideas/attitudes instead (*wat wordt
internationaal gezien als...?*); blunt direct statements (*Nederlanders zijn verschrikkelijk
ontevreden zeurpieten*, "the Dutch are terribly dissatisfied whiners") vs. hedged/qualified ones
(*Terwijl apartheid toch een Nederlands woord is*, "although apartheid is in fact a Dutch word");
modal particles for emphasis (*Maar zijn ze nou echt zo veel slimmer?*) vs. formal discourse-linking
phrases (*En dan kom ik meteen op solidariteit*, "And that brings me straight to solidarity"). A
single speaker/dialogue commonly mixes both registers rather than staying purely on one pole — e.g.
the unit's own diplomat interview is framed as generally formal/careful given the professional
interview context, while the earlier person-in-the-street interviews are framed as personal/informal.

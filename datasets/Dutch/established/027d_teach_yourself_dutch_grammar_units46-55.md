## Grammar points

### Unit 46/47 — Infinitive constructions
Two verb classes combine with a bare infinitive at clause end: those requiring `te` (zitten,
liggen, staan, hoeven, durven, beloven, proberen, weigeren, vergeten — "to be sitting/lying/
standing," "to dare," "to promise," "to try," "to refuse," "to forget") and those that don't
(komen, gaan, blijven, zijn, laten, zien, horen — the last three matching the causative/perception-
verb pattern already covered in `established/012`/`013`). A specific irregularity worth flagging:
in the perfect tense, none of zitten/liggen/staan/hoeven/durven/komen/gaan/blijven/zijn/laten/zien/
horen form a real past participle when combined with another infinitive — they simply stay
infinitive themselves ("Ik heb nooit in het diepe durven springen," not *gedurfd*), and `zijn`
specifically becomes `wezen` in this construction ("Freek is wezen voetballen"). Unit 47 adds `aan
het + zijn` and `zitten/liggen/staan + te + infinitive` (progressive constructions, cross-
referencing Unit 28's fuller treatment) and the `om + te + infinitive` purpose construction,
noting `om` is obligatory when expressing purpose/reason/function or after `te` + adjective ("te
oud om mee te doen"), optional after other te-infinitive verbs, and barred entirely with zitten/
staan/liggen/hoeven/durven. When a clause ends in more than one infinitive, the verb carrying the
most semantic weight goes last.

### Unit 48/49 — The conditional; zou's other uses
Standard `als`-conditional with three structures: present-tense-throughout (simplest, for open/
likely conditions), `zullen + infinitive` in the result clause (for intention/promise), and
`zou(den)` for two counterfactual registers — fantasizing about an alternate present/future
(`als`-clause in imperfect + `zou(den)` + infinitive) or an alternate past (`als`-clause in past
perfect + `zouden` + `hebben`/`zijn` + participle). Both can alternatively just repeat the `als`-
clause's own tense instead of switching to `zou(den)` (matches Unit 37's past-perfect-twice
pattern). Unit 49 catalogues three further, non-conditional uses of `zou(den)` not always grouped
together this explicitly: (1) softening a request into a polite question ("Zou je dit even voor
me kunnen vasthouden?"), (2) giving gentle advice, optionally strengthened with `moeten` ("Je zou
je huiswerk moeten doen"), and (3) marking **uncertainty or unverified hearsay** — combinable with
any tense, common in news reporting ("Er zou een vliegtuig zijn neergestort" = "a plane is
*reported* to have crashed") — this third, journalistic-hedging use of `zou(den)` is a genuinely
useful register/pragmatics note not laid out this explicitly in prior established/ files.

### Unit 50/51 — Verb word order (1) and (2)
V2 main-clause order, inversion when a non-subject element opens the sentence, and clause-final
verb clustering when multiple verbs are present — standard content matching `established/012`/
`013`. Unit 51 covers sub-clause/relative-clause verb-final placement, the "complex sentence"
pattern where a fronted sub-clause still counts as the sentence's single first element for main-
clause V2 purposes, and the fact that prepositional phrases and comparison elements (`dan`-
phrases) can trail *after* the clause-final verb cluster without being ungrammatical ("Ik heb
kaartjes besteld voor 22 juni"; "Jet heeft meer gegeten dan Jeroen") — this trailing-elements
exception is a useful, easy-to-miss detail. Also: when 2+ verbs stack at clause end, either order
is generally acceptable for two verbs, but a modal's infinitive precedes other infinitives, and
with 3+ verbs the finite verb leads and infinitives (with the modal's infinitive first) trail,
while the past participle can go either first or last.

### Unit 52/53/54 — The passive: form, extended tenses, use
Standard `worden` (present/imperfect) / `zijn` (perfect/past perfect) passive-auxiliary split with
`door + agent` marking the actor, matching `established/012`/`013`. Unit 53 extends this to future/
conditional/modal-combined passives (`zullen`/`zouden`/modal + past participle + `worden` as a
clause-final infinitive) and the impersonal `er`-passive for unspecified agents ("Er werd veel
gelachen"). **Unit 54's discourse-function breakdown of *why* Dutch uses the passive is the
genuinely useful supplement here**: (1) discourse-context continuity (staying with an established
topic-as-subject even when that topic is the one being acted upon), (2) formal written style/
distancing effect, and (3) **deliberately hiding or de-emphasizing the agent** — explicitly framed
as a tool politicians and news headlines use to avoid apportioning blame (contrasting "NAVO
bombardeert Belgrado" [NATO bombs Belgrade, active, agent-foregrounding] against "Belgrado wordt
gebombardeerd" [Belgrade is bombed, passive, agent-backgrounded]). This political/rhetorical
framing of passive-voice choice is a genuine register-level finding, not just a grammatical
mechanism, and is not captured this explicitly elsewhere in this project's Dutch established/
files.

### Unit 55 — Negatives (1): niet vs. geen
Core functional split: `geen` negates an indefinite noun (one that would otherwise take `een` or
no article); `niet` negates everything else (definite nouns, adjectives, adverbs, verbs, whole
clauses). Placement rule for `niet`: generally goes as late as possible in the clause, but before
prepositions, post-nominal adjectives, and adverbs, and before (not after) elements that must
stay clause-final (past participle, infinitive, prepositional phrase). Matches `established/012`/
`013`'s treatment; kept terse.

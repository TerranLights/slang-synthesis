## Grammar points

### Reflexive verbs — pronoun paradigm and a diagnostic test

Reflexive verbs always take a reflexive pronoun when the verb's action applies back to its own
subject. Full paradigm: `ik`→`me`, `jij`→`je`, `u`→`zich`, `hij/zij/het`→`zich`, `wij`→`ons`,
`jullie`→`je`, `zij`(pl.)→`zich`. English often has no visible reflexive object at all where Dutch
requires one — the source gives a diagnostic test: try adding "themselves"/an object to the English
sentence; if it fits naturally ("the hands opened and closed *themselves*"), Dutch needs a reflexive
pronoun. When a subclause has more than one reflexive verb, the reflexive pronoun need not be
repeated: **de handen openden [zich] en sloten zich**. In a subclause, reflexive verbs go to the end
in the normal way: **De portier zag hoe de handen zich openden en sloten.** pp. 98–99.

### `willen` (simple past) = "was/were about to"

The Dutch simple past of `willen` can express an action that was on the point of happening: **De
portier wilde naar voren springen** ("The porter was about to leap forwards"). p. 99.

### `blijven` + infinitive — aspectual auxiliary ("kept on / stayed")

`Blijven` (like `zien`, `horen`, `gaan`) can function as an auxiliary verb with another infinitive.
Crucially, these auxiliaries **don't form the perfect tense in the usual way** — instead of taking a
past participle, they replace it with a second infinitive: `hebben`/`zijn` + infinitive-of-auxiliary
+ infinitive. **De man was blijven staan** ("The man had remained standing," i.e. "had stood still")
— not *gebleven staan*. p. 99–100.

### `er` as pronoun, sandwiched around a separable verb

Further example of the `er`+pronoun sandwich pattern (cf. Les 1 §1a): **hij haalde er niets uit**
("he didn't take anything out of it") — `er` refers back to `binnenzak` and sits between subject and
verb rather than adjacent to the separable verb's prefix, though the non-sandwiched order (**hij
haalde niets eruit**) is also grammatical. p. 100.

### `zou/zouden + moeten` = "ought to"

A two-word Dutch combination corresponding to a single English modal: **Ik zou hem naar binnen moeten
brengen** ("I ought to bring him inside"). p. 100.

### `gaan`/`komen` as "come"/"go" — a counter-intuitive cross-mapping

The source flags a genuine false-friend trap: Dutch `gaan` often corresponds to English "come," and
`komen` to English "go," inverted from what a learner might expect by surface similarity — **Ga
mee!** means "Come with me!"/"Come along!", not "Go with me." It further notes Dutch does not use a
personal pronoun in this construction at all, just the separable verb `meegaan`. The source's own
explicit advice: "The best way to learn is to listen and copy and to learn set phrases by heart"
rather than relying on cognate intuition. p. 100.

### `wel` as an untranslated reassurance particle

Extending the `wel`/`niet` contrastive-answer finding from Les 5: **We brengen u wel naar huis**
("We'll take you home") — `wel` here adds no separately translatable content but makes the statement
sound more friendly/reassuring. The source explicitly classifies words like this as "particles,"
promising fuller treatment in Les 10. p. 100.

### The Passive — construction, tenses, impersonal use, and word order (revision + one new layer)

The source explicitly frames most of this Grammatica section as revision of *Dutch in Three Months*
§66; summarized here rather than fully re-extracted per the non-redundant-supplement pattern (fuller
treatment already held in `established/012`/`013`). Points genuinely worth flagging:

- **Impersonal passive** is common and often untranslatable literally: **Er wordt veel gedaan.** ("A
  lot is being done."); **Er wordt gebeld.** ("There is a ring at the door" / "The bell rings," lit.
  "There is rung.") — English frequently needs to supply an unstated subject where Dutch needs none.
  In a subclause, `er` immediately follows the subordinating conjunction while the verb group moves
  to the very end: **... dat er iets heel vriendelijks tegen hem werd gedaan.**
- **Passive + modal auxiliary word order**: `worden` becomes a dependent infinitive and moves to the
  end alongside the past participle, in either order (`kunnen worden gemaakt` or `gemaakt kunnen
  worden`) — both orders are equally valid, main clause or subclause: **De appels kunnen door de
  jongens worden geplukt (geplukt worden).**
- **Perfect-vs-present-tense trap**: because the passive perfect tense is formed with `zijn`, English
  speakers risk misreading it as present tense. **De auto is gewassen** = "The car *has been* washed"
  (not "is being washed") — flagged explicitly by the source as an easy mistake. pp. 108–110.

### `de opname` — noun with two senses, and gender shift under compounding

`De opname` means both "absorption/uptake" (of nutrients, etc.) and "(hospital) admission." When
compounded with `het ziekenhuis` to form `de ziekenhuisopname` ("hospital admission"), the compound's
gender is `de` (from the final element `opname`), even though `ziekenhuis` itself is a `het`-word —
a further worked example of the compound-gender-from-final-element rule established in `026d`. The
related verb `opnemen` correspondingly means both "to admit" (to hospital) and "to absorb." p. 106.

### Hyphenation in coordinated compounds sharing a final element

Dutch frequently hyphenates the first element of a compound noun when two compounds sharing the same
final element are coordinated in a list, to avoid repeating that final element twice: **maag- en
darmklachten** ("stomach and intestinal disorders," from `maagklachten` + `darmklachten`); **zout- en
waterweerhouding**; **voor- en nadelen** (already seen in Les 4). p. 106.

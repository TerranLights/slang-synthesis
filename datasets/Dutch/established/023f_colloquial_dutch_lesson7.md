## Grammar points

### The imperative (pp. 100-101)

Bare verb stem for `jij`/`u`/`jullie` alike; the five monosyllabic-vowel verbs (`doen`, `gaan`,
`slaan`, `staan`, `zien`) and `zijn` (→ `wees`) are irregular. `Houden`/`rijden`-type verbs drop
their stem-final `d` in the imperative exactly as they do in the 1st-person present (cross-
references the ij/ou+d-drop rule from `established/023`). A more polite `u`-form imperative exists,
formed with the finite present-tense `u`-form + subject `u` inverted (`Gaat u zitten`, `Komt u
binnen`) rather than the bare stem alone.

### Separable verbs (pp. 102-103)

Verbs formed from a stressed prepositional prefix + verb (`opbellen`, `weggaan`, `uitdoen`) are
written as one word in the infinitive but split in the present tense/imperative, with the
prefix moving to clause-final position (`Ik bel mijn moeder op`). The past participle inserts `ge-`
*between* the prefix and the verb stem (`opgebeld`, not `geopbeld`). Separable compounds frequently
acquire a figurative meaning distinct from (and now more common than) the sum of their parts — see
`uitgeven`/`voorstellen` in the vocabulary table.

### How to make a request sound polite (pp. 103-104)

A graded politeness system layered onto the bare imperative: adding `even`/`eventjes` softens it
("would you mind..."); using `wil je`/`wilt u` + infinitive is more polite still; `zou je`/`zou u`
+ `willen` + infinitive is the most polite register, frequently combined with `even`/`eventjes` for
maximum softening. `Alsjeblieft`/`alstublieft` can cap any of these but is redundant once the
willen/zou-willen strategy is already in use.

### Informal letters / addressing an envelope (pp. 104-107)

Register-graded letter-opening and closing vocabulary (see table); postal-code format (4 digits +
space + 2 capital letters, tied to the specific city block); date format uses bare cardinal numbers,
never an ordinal abbreviation; street-name-plus-number compounds are written as a single word
(`Zadelstraat`) but always take the definite article in speech (`in de Zadelstraat`).

### Use of te before an infinitive / om te (pp. 108-109)

A clause-final infinitive takes `te` unless the governing finite verb is a modal (`kunnen`,
`moeten`, `mogen`, `willen`, `zullen`) — mirroring the way English "to" is sometimes dropped after
modals. `Om te` is obligatory when the infinitival clause expresses purpose ("in order to"), but the
source notes Dutch usage has broadened `om te` into many contexts where plain `te` would
grammatically suffice and purists object — a live prescriptive/descriptive tension, not a hard rule,
worth flagging for register-mechanics purposes. When the governed infinitive is itself a separable
verb, `te` is inserted between the prefix and the verb, all three written as separate words
(`om vroeg aan te komen`).

### Table manners / diminutive-question phrases + vriend/kennis distinction (pp. 110-113)

Standard mealtime-phrase inventory (see vocabulary), with the recurring `wat` + adjective
exclamation pattern (`Wat leuk!`, `Wat prachtig!`, `Wat vreselijk!`) explicitly flagged by the source
as a common appreciative-exclamation template. `Nog een` ("an additional one," implying more than
one now exists) vs. `een andere` ("a different one") is a genuine semantic split that English
"another" collapses — a real source of translation ambiguity the book calls out directly.
`Trek hebben in` (food only) vs. `zin hebben in` (food or any activity) is a similar narrower-vs-
broader-scope distinction between two near-synonymous idioms.

## Grammar points

### Unit 56 — Negatives (2): gradation and other negative words
Beyond plain niet/geen (Unit 55): `nog niet`/`nog geen` ("not yet"), `niet meer`/`geen...meer`
("not any more"), gradation adverbs `nauwelijks` ("hardly")/`zelden` ("seldom"), and a set of
words that give a sentence a negative connotation despite not being grammatically negative
themselves — `nog maar`, `maar`, `pas`, `slechts` (all roughly "only," but not neutral synonyms of
plain "only" — they frame the quantity as surprisingly small). `slechts` is explicitly flagged as
formal-register-only. `maar`/`pas`/`slechts` cannot combine with `moeten`; `hoeven` substitutes
instead (cross-referencing Unit 31). `nergens`/`nooit`/`niemand`/`niets` take the same clause
position as `niet`. Matches `established/012`/`013`'s negation coverage; kept terse.

### Unit 57 — Prefixes and suffixes
A compact derivational-morphology catalogue, organized by function: opposite-meaning prefixes
(on-, a-, im-, ir-, ont-), a negative-connotation prefix (wan-, "misbehaviour"-type senses),
location prefixes (onder-, inter-), time prefixes (ex-, post- — explicitly noted as borrowed from
English/Latin rather than native Dutch formations), diminutive -je (cross-referencing Unit 16),
adjective/adverb-forming suffixes (-baar, -ig, -(e)lijk, -isch, -achtig), and the noun-forming
-heid. This is a clean, compact reference for Dutch's productive affixation system — worth keeping
as a supplement even though individual examples (onaardig, immoreel, etc.) are standard-textbook
fare; no prior established/ file collects this range of prefixes/suffixes in one place this
concisely.

### Unit 58/59/60/61 — *er*: the four-part treatment
This book devotes four consecutive units to Dutch's notoriously multi-functional little word
*er* — a genuinely useful concentrated treatment not laid out this systematically, unit-by-unit,
in prior established/ files (though the underlying phenomena are individually covered in
`012`/`013`). Summary of the four functions: (1) **grammatical subject placeholder** — required
when a sentence's subject is indefinite ("Er is een voetbalwedstrijd op tv") or in an agent-less/
generalized passive ("Er mag hier niet gerookt worden"); (2) **referring to a place** — *er*
substitutes for a previously mentioned location and simply means "there" in this unstressed use,
with **daar** as its stressed counterpart, typically fronted ("Daar ben ik maar een keer
geweest"); (3) **referring to quantity** — combined with a counting word when the noun itself is
dropped ("Ik heb er twee"); (4) **standing in for an object noun when combined with a preposition**
— object pronouns (hem/haar/ze/het) cannot follow a preposition when referring to a thing/idea (only
when referring to a person), so *er* + preposition is used instead, written as one word when not
split ("Ik hou ervan" vs. "Ik hou er erg veel van"). Unit 60 covers *er*'s position (early in the
clause when grammatical/subject-marking; right after the finite verb when referential; reflexive
pronouns and object pronouns both take precedence over *er* in clause order) and the possibility of
**two *er*s stacking in one sentence** with different functions ("Er zijn er nog een paar" — one
grammatical, one quantity-referential). Unit 61 adds three refinements: *er* is barred for
personal referents (use the object pronoun instead — "ik denk er nooit meer aan" only works for a
thing, not a person); directional suffixes -naartoe/-heen/-vandaan attach to indicate movement
to/from the place *er* refers to; and met/tot mutate to mee/toe specifically when compounded with
*er*/*hier*/*daar*/*waar* (not otherwise) — a spelling/phonology detail worth flagging since it's
easy to misapply outside the compounded context.

### Unit 62/63/64 — Prepositions: function, place vocabulary, extended senses
Unit 62 frames the core difficulty (Dutch and English preposition choice frequently don't align
1:1 even where a literal-translation preposition exists) and covers basic placement (normally
before the noun; occasionally after, as in "de trap af"; prepositional phrases can trail after the
clause-final verb position, cross-referencing Unit 51's own trailing-elements point). Unit 63
tabulates the largest functional class — prepositions of literal physical location/space (in, op,
onder, voor, achter, naast, over, tussen, tegen, tegenover, aan, bij, door, uit) — each with the
source's own explicit note that most of these *also* carry extended, non-spatial senses (the
exercise's literal-vs-figurative discrimination task for `achter`/`onder`/`in`/`over`/`op` makes
this point concrete: "Onder tieners is er weinig interesse voor politiek" uses `onder` in a
non-locative "among" sense). Unit 64 extends the same prepositions (plus tot, na, om, tijdens,
vanwege, volgens) into non-spatial functional categories: direction/movement, time, cause/reason,
opinion, means, and possession — this functional taxonomy (rather than a flat alphabetical list)
is the useful organizing structure here, even though the individual prepositions themselves match
prior coverage.

### Unit 65 — Fixed prepositions (1)
A list of common verb-plus-fixed-preposition idioms where the preposition doesn't correspond to
its literal spatial sense and must simply be memorized per-verb (afhangen van, antwoorden op, zich
bemoeien met, bewondering hebben voor, bezig zijn met, denken aan, het (niet) eens zijn met, zich
ergeren aan, feliciteren met, genieten van, gaan over, een hekel hebben aan, houden van) — this is
a genuinely useful, source-independent idiom-inventory supplement, since fixed-preposition verbs
are exactly the kind of item a synthesis-phase mechanics analysis benefits from having as a
checkable list rather than needing to be inferred. `zich bemoeien met`'s own English gloss ("to
stick one's nose in") is informal enough to flag as `colloquial` in register even though the
source itself doesn't explicitly tag it.

## Coverage note — non-redundant-supplement dispatch, explicitly scoped

This is the **18th** Dutch reference source extracted into `established/`. The book's Contents page
(pp. 4–6) lists Week 1 through Week 12 (pp. 7–202): pronunciation/spelling, personal pronouns,
`zijn`/`hebben`, question formation, noun plurals and gender, present tense, commands, word order,
negation, object/reflexive pronouns, possessives, comparison of adjectives, diminutives, past tense
(weak and strong verbs), perfect/past-perfect tense, modal verbs, separable verbs, future tense,
numbers/dates/money, relative clauses, the passive voice, and the present participle — i.e. the
complete standard topic list of a beginner-to-intermediate Dutch course. **Every one of these
topics is already covered, often multiple times over and in more depth, across
`established/001`–`017`** (the Shetter *Essential Grammar*, the Michel Thomas *Foundation Course*,
*Dutch for Dummies*, the Routledge *Intensive Dutch Course* (11 units + full glossary), two
grammar workbooks, the two-part *Comprehensive Grammar*, *Beter Nederlands*, and others).

**Verification, not assumption.** Four representative sections were spot-checked in full before
deciding to skip bulk re-extraction: Week 1 §1 Pronunciation (p. 8, general consonant/vowel
inventory — matches `001`'s far more systematic pronunciation chapter), Week 3 revision exercises
(p. 54, plain drill sentences), Week 4 §20–21 Commands and Word Order (p. 55, standard stem-command
+ V2 rules), and Week 8 §44–45 Separable Verbs (pp. 132–133, including the aan-/door-/om-/onder-/

explicit dialectal/regional annotation, and no register tag beyond what `001`–`017` already
document was found in any spot-checked section.** Per the "non-redundant supplement" dispatch
pattern, Weeks 1–12's grammar exposition and drill vocabulary are **not** re-extracted here.

**Where the real extraction effort went instead — three genuinely non-redundant sections:**

1. **Reading Practice (pp. 204–213)** — five short passages excerpted from real, independently
   published Dutch prose (children's fiction, a comedian's autobiography, a newspaper humor column,
   a linguistics magazine article, and a consumer-rights news piece), each with its own
   book-curated vocabulary box. This is the one section of the book built from **authentic,
   externally-authored Dutch** rather than the course's own pedagogical example sentences — genuinely

2. **Appendix: Commonly Used Strong and Irregular Verbs (pp. 230–231)** — a clean, systematic

**Grammar/register point — the `u`/`jij` distinction as a living, changing system.** The passage's
own argument (paraphrased): the choice between `u` and `jij`/`je` is not fixed by grammar alone but
tracks perceived social distance, formality, and solidarity, and has been shifting toward informal
`jij`/`je` over "half a century" — the author notes a single speaker may switch between `u` and
`jij` for the *same* addressee depending on how that person is dressed or groomed on a given day
(a supermarket cashier, `u`-addressed when clean-shaven, `jij`-addressed with stubble). This is a
genuinely new *sociolinguistic mechanism* finding (register as situationally negotiated per-instance,
not fixed per-relationship) not previously captured in `established/001`–`017`'s more form-focused
treatment of `u` vs. `jij`/`je` as a grammatical paradigm — worth flagging for the eventual Phase 3
mechanics-analysis pass on register-formation.

### Register finding: `u`/`jij` as situationally negotiated, not fixed per-relationship

See the full paraphrase under Reading Practice Passage 4 above. The genuinely new point (not
previously captured in `established/001`–`017`'s treatment of `u`/`jij` as a grammatical paradigm
choice) is that a **single Dutch speaker can switch address form for the same interlocutor within
the same relationship**, based on situational cues (the article's own example: a supermarket
cashier addressed as `u` when clean-shaven, `jij` when unshaven) — register-marking is applied
per-utterance/per-appearance, not fixed once per social relationship. Worth carrying forward into
the Phase 3 mechanics-analysis pass on how formality/register marking works mechanically in Dutch,
since it bears directly on how any derived-conlang register system might model situational (rather
than purely relational) formality switching.

### Lexical-register pair: `jatten` (slang) vs. `stelen` (neutral) "to steal"

Reading Practice Passage 3 (Carmiggelt) uses `jatten` as an explicitly informal/slang synonym for
the neutral strong verb `stelen` (itself tabulated in the Appendix above) inside a first-person
colloquial narrative voice ("daar jat iemand wat," "daar gaat een vogeltje" as the narrator's own
gentler euphemistic paraphrase of the same act). This is a clean three-way register stack for a
single concept (slang `jatten` / neutral `stelen` / euphemistic-idiom `een vogeltje gaat weg`-style
avoidance) worth flagging for the slang-mechanics analysis phase as a concrete worked example of
register stacking around a single taboo-adjacent concept (theft).

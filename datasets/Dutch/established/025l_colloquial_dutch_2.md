# Dutch — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Colloquial Dutch 2, Unit 12 (Cultuur / Culture)

**Source:** Gerda Bodegom and Bruce Donaldson, *Colloquial Dutch 2* (Routledge, 2008), Unit 12, pp.
166–181 (PDF pp. 175–190). Continuation of `025_colloquial_dutch_2.md` — see that file for the

**Register flagship finding — toilet-terminology register scale (Text 2, pp.175–176):** the source
newspaper article on a Waterleidingmuseum exhibition ("Het gemak dient de mens") lays out an
explicit four-tier Dutch register ladder for talking about toileting, directly comparable in
structure to Unit 5's youth-slang list and Unit 8's teen/adult camera-phone register contrast: (1)
*polite/neutral* — **het toilet gebruiken**, **in retraite gaan**, **de handen wassen**; (2)
*genteel/formal circles* — **het latinaire gebeuren**; (3) *bluntly vulgar, source-labeled
"ronduit ordinair"* — **pissen**, **schijten**; (4) a middle "commonly used but still spoken with
unease" tier — **poepen**, **plassen**, source-noted as still spoken of "met gêne" even though sex
and eroticism can now be discussed freely in Dutch. The article's own thesis — that this is one of
the few remaining conversational taboos in an otherwise very matter-of-fact culture — is itself
flagged by the source's own sidebar note ("The Dutch are extremely matter-of-fact when it comes to
sex and bodily functions").

## Grammar points

### Language point 1 — More on er

**Er** + preposition renders English "preposition + it" (**Hij speelt ermee** = "He is playing with
it"; a bare **met het** is impossible in Dutch). In an expanded sentence, spoken Dutch typically
splits this: **er** goes right after the finite verb, and the preposition moves to the very end,
after any other verbs (**en hij speelt er dolgraag mee**; **maar hij heeft er nooit mee
gespeeld**). The same **er + preposition** also renders "preposition + them" when "them" refers to
*things* (not people) — **Hij heeft twee leuke treintjes gekregen maar hij speelt er nooit mee**
— contrasted with **met ze/hen** for people. When a sentence opens with dummy-subject **er** and
also needs a prepositional **er** later in the clause, one **er** serves both functions (**Er zit
één kind naar de televisie te kijken**; **Er zitten twee kinderen naar te kijken**, not *ernaar*).
The same principle applies to locative **er/hier/daar** ("there/here/there" combined with a
preposition, e.g. **erachter** collapsing to plain **er** when the sentence opens with dummy **er**
— **Er staat een boom achter**, not *erachter*). However, if the sentence's dummy **er** also
requires a *partitive* **er** (roughly "of them/of it," quantifying a following noun), that
partitive **er** cannot be dropped or merged (**Er zitten er twee naar de televisie te kijken**;
**Er zijn er genoeg**). pp.170–171.

### Language point 2 — Common abbreviations

A reference list of ~30 standard Dutch abbreviations comparable to English "i.e./e.g./etc.," with
the key usage note that Dutch abbreviations are read aloud as the full word they stand for, never
spelled out letter by letter (**b.v.** = **bijvoorbeeld**, read as "bijvoorbeeld," not "bee vee," unlike
English "e.g." = "ee gee"). Representative entries: **a.s.** (aanstaande, "next"), **a.u.b.**
(alstublieft, "please"), **d.m.v.** (door middel van, "by means of"), **d.w.z.** (dat wil zeggen,
"i.e."), **enz.** (enzovoort(s), "etc."), **geb.** (geboren, "born"), **i.h.a.** (in het algemeen,
"in general"), **jl.** (jongstleden, "last, past [in letters]"), **m.b.t.** (met betrekking tot,
"with reference to"), **m.i.** (mijns inziens, "in my opinion" — a further live example of Unit 11's
fossilized-genitive **mijns**), **o.a.** (onder andere(n), "among others"), **s.v.p.** (s'il vous
plaît, a French borrowing used in Dutch for "please"), **t.a.v.** (ter attentie van, "attention"),
**t.z.t.** (te zijner tijd, "in due course" — another fossilized-case relic), **z.o.z.** (zie
ommezijde, "see over"). p.174.

### Language point 3 — Multiple infinitives in a sentence

Beyond the already-familiar double-infinitive perfect of modal verbs (**Hij heeft haar niet kunnen
helpen**), several other verb classes trigger the same construction: **laten** (**laten vallen** "to
drop," **laten zien** "to show," and causative "to have s.t. done" — **Ze hebben een vakantiehuis
op het eiland Texel laten bouwen**); **gaan** in combinations meaning literally or figuratively
"going to" do something (**gaan zitten**, **gaan liggen**, or open-ended **Wat ben je toen gaan
doen?**, where the "going" sense may be absent); **komen**, which always retains its literal "coming"
sense (**Ze zijn me helaas niet komen ophalen**); and a rare second infinitive of "to be," **wezen**,
used almost exclusively in such constructions (**Ze is onmiddellijk wezen kijken**). **Gaan**,
**komen**, and **wezen** all require **zijn** as the perfect-tense auxiliary in these constructions,
unlike **laten**, **horen**, and **zien**, which take **hebben**. **Horen**/**zien** trigger the same
pattern when used to mean perceiving someone doing something (**Ze heeft hem het gras zien/horen
maaien**). The perfect tense of the **zitten/staan/liggen/lopen te + infinitive** progressive
construction (introduced earlier, p.13) likewise produces a double infinitive (**Hij ligt te slapen**
→ **Hij heeft de hele middag liggen slapen**). In a subordinate clause containing any of these
multiple-infinitive constructions, the finite verb moves to a position *before* the string of
infinitives rather than after them (**Wist je dat hij het hele huis heeft laten verven?**). pp.
177–179.

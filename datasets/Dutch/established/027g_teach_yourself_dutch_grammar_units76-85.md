## Grammar points

### Unit 76/77/78/79 — Adverbs: definition, categories, standard order, emphasis
Dutch adverbs are morphologically simple (no -ly-style suffix; the adjective form is reused
directly, "goed" = both "good" and "well," unlike English's systematic -ly derivation) — a
concrete illustration of Dutch's comparatively low fusional/derivational overhead for this word
class relative to English. Units 77–78 catalogue the six functional categories the book uses
throughout (time, manner, place, frequency, degree, sentence/opinion adverbs) with example word
lists for each — useful as a compact functional inventory, though individual items are standard.
Unit 79 states the default multi-adverb ordering rule (time–manner–place) and the emphasis-via-
reordering mechanism (moving an adverb to sentence-initial position for stress, triggering
inversion) — matches material already touched on in `established/012`/`013`; kept terse.

### Unit 80/81/82/83 — Word order: the six-position clause model
A structured six-column model for main-clause word order (first position / verb / subject-if-not-
first / middle part / last-verb-place / final position) that consolidates rules already scattered
across Units 24–55 into one reusable template — first position can hold the subject, object,
adverb, question word, or a whole sub-clause; the second position is always the finite verb (or,
for questions/orders, empty-first-position with the verb leading); inversion is simply "subject
lands in position 3 instead of position 1." Unit 82 gives the parallel five-position model for
sub-clauses (linking word / subject / middle part / finite verb+other verbs / final position) —
note the finite verb moves to position 4 here, confirming the general V-final sub-clause rule.
Unit 83 clarifies that prepositional phrases, `om + te + infinitive` constructions, and
comparisons routinely trail *after* the nominal "last verb place," which is a genuinely common,
not-marked, pattern in real usage (not just a rare exception) — reinforcing the same point already
flagged from Units 47/51/64. This six/five-position model is a clean organizing summary; the
individual placement rules themselves are not new relative to `established/012`/`013`.

### Unit 84 — Formal and informal Dutch — **directly relevant to slang mechanics**
This is the single most directly useful unit in this 10-unit range for the slang-synthesis
project's eventual mechanics-analysis phase. The source identifies **three independent channels**
by which Dutch marks formality, worth treating as a checklist:
1. **Pronoun choice** — `u` (formal address) vs. `je`/`jij` (informal), already covered in Unit 22,
   but here explicitly generalized as *the* primary formality signal in Dutch.
2. **Vocabulary choice** — the book gives a concrete minimal pair, `café` (neutral/formal) vs.
   `kroeg` (informal) for "bar/pub," as a worked example of near-synonyms split by register alone
   rather than by denotation — exactly the kind of register-driven lexical doubling this project's
   later mechanics-analysis phase will want examples of.
3. **Grammatical structure** — passive constructions and long, subordinate-clause-heavy sentences
   are named as formal-register markers in their own right, independent of word choice (echoing
   Unit 54's passive-as-distancing-device finding, now generalized to a broader formality-register
   claim, not just a political-rhetoric one).

Beyond the three-channel breakdown, the unit adds two genuinely valuable sociolinguistic notes not
captured elsewhere in this project's Dutch established/ files: **(a) a dialect-region contrast** —
the Netherlands trends toward first-name/informal address faster than Flanders, and Flemish
speakers retain an archaic `gij`/`ge` pronoun form that would read as "extremely formal" if used in
the Netherlands and isn't used there at all (an explicit, source-marked dialectal annotation, not
inferred); **(b) a generational/age note** — younger Dutch speakers increasingly address teachers
and even grandparents by first name, framed as an active trend toward informality, not a stable
state. The negotiation formula "Mag ik 'je' zeggen?" ("May I say 'je'?") is flagged as the
conventional way speakers explicitly renegotiate the formality register mid-conversation — a
concrete pragmatic device. The exercise dialogue set (Unit 84's own practice sentences) supplies
two directly contrasting register-marked closings on the same page: the informal parting **"de
mazzel"** (itself carrying a Yiddish-loanword etymology, "mazzel" = luck) against the formal
**"eensgelijks"** ("likewise," in reply to a formal "pleased to meet you") — a genuine, source-
supplied minimal pair of register-marked sign-off vocabulary.

### Unit 85 — Foreign words — **directly relevant to slang mechanics**
Also highly relevant: a compact survey of Dutch's loanword strata by source language and function,
with an explicit **acceptance-asymmetry note**: the Netherlands is markedly more accepting of
foreign-word assimilation than Flanders, where speakers have historically had to actively defend
the language and are consequently more linguistically protective/purist — a genuine, source-marked
regional contrast (paralleling Unit 84's own Netherlands-vs-Flanders formality observation, and
worth cross-referencing together as a recurring NL/Flanders register-and-purism axis). Three
source-language strata are distinguished: **German** (surprisingly small despite shared linguistic
family and geographic proximity — "überhaupt" cited as the single most commonly heard example, with
the source explicitly attributing the low volume to "historical reasons" around German's relative
(un)popularity); **French** (the largest historical wave, concentrated during Napoleon's occupation
of the Low Countries, producing everyday words like trottoir/paraplu/restaurant/cadeau/bureau,
some respelled to Dutch orthography and some not); and **English** (the dominant *contemporary*
source, explicitly tied by the source to 20th-century American TV and pop-music exposure, and
concentrated in two semantic domains — technology, computer/ticket/printer/email/videorecorder/
mobieltje, and sports, team/trainer/hockey/tennis, with voetbal singled out as the one fully
Dutch-respelled example against otherwise-English-spelled sporting terms). **The most directly
useful mechanism for slang-synthesis purposes**: English loan-roots are productively nativized as
verbs via simple `-en` suffixation (voetballen, trainen, scoren, pushen, coachen, shoppen,
rugbyen) — reusing the native regular-verb-formation pattern from Unit 5 — and their past-
tense/participle t/d selection (the "soft ketchup" rule from Units 34/39) is explicitly keyed to
the loanword's *pronounced* final sound rather than its (often still-English) spelling. This is a
concrete, source-documented borrowing-and-nativization pathway — precisely the kind of productive
mechanism a synthesized slang lexicon for the sci-fi setting could plausibly reuse or riff on for
its own loan-integration logic.

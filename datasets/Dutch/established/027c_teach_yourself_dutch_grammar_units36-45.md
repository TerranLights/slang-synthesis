## Grammar points

### Unit 36 — Present perfect (3): irregular participles and modal-in-perfect
Lists common irregular-participle verbs (hebben→gehad, zijn→geweest, brengen→gebracht, etc.) and
strong verbs with a vowel-change pattern (drinken→gedronken, nemen→genomen). Confirms the rule
already seen in `established/012`/`013` that when a modal is used in the perfect together with
another verb, **both** verbs go to the end in infinitive form ("Ik heb een laptop willen kopen"),
including with `laten` (causative "have something done": "ik heb mijn haar laten knippen").
Standard content, kept terse.

### Unit 37 — Past perfect
Formed with `had(den)`/`was(en)` + past participle; same auxiliary-choice and participle-formation
rules as the present perfect. Two uses: (1) sequencing one past event before another, (2)
counterfactual musing about an alternate past ("Als ik dat had geweten, dan was ik niet naar zijn
feest gegaan"), which is notable for using the past perfect twice in the same sentence rather than
switching to a conditional construction (contrast with `zou`-conditionals, cross-referenced to
Unit 48). Modal-in-past-perfect works the same as modal-in-perfect (Unit 36).

### Unit 38/39 — Imperfect: use, regular and irregular forms
Two uses of the imperfect confirmed: habitual/regular past events, and continuing a narrative once
the listener's attention is already in the past (cross-references Unit 33's fuller treatment).
Regular formation mirrors the present-perfect "soft ketchup" t/d rule (te(n) after s/f/t/k/ch/p,
de(n) otherwise), with t/d-stem doubling (praten→praatte(n)) and the same z/v→s/f devoicing
exception (reizen→reisde(n)) as regular participle formation. Unit 39 tabulates irregular
imperfect forms for the same common-verb set as Unit 36's irregular participles, plus strong-verb
vowel-change patterns (drinken→dronk(en), nemen→nam(en)) — this is a genuinely useful parallel
table (irregular present/imperfect/participle for the same verb set laid out unit-by-unit across
36/39/99–100) though each individual form matches prior established/ coverage.

### Unit 40/41 — Separable verbs: form and splitting rules
Separable verbs (prefix + verb, e.g. `uitgaan`) split with the prefix moving to the end of the
main clause; rejoin into one word when the whole verb moves to clause-final position (as in a
sub-clause) or forms a past participle (prefix + ge- + stem + t/d, e.g. `opgebeld`). Unit 41 adds
the more advanced split patterns: `te` insertion between prefix and verb in infinitive-with-`te`
constructions (`Jan probeert haar op te bellen`), and the option (not obligation) to split a
separable verb even in clause-final position when it's paired with another verb, which the source
explicitly flags as a formality register marker — splitting reads as **more informal**, keeping
the verb joined reads as **more formal** ("Denk je dat hij af zal studeren?" [formal-leaning] vs.
"Denk je dat hij zal afstuderen?" [informal-leaning]) — this stress-based separable/inseparable
distinction (prefix stressed = separable, e.g. *voor*komen "to occur" vs. voor*komen* "to prevent")
is a genuinely useful minimal-pair mechanism not laid out this explicitly elsewhere in this
project's Dutch coverage.

### Unit 42 — Inseparable verbs
Two kinds: ordinary unstressed-prefix verbs (e.g. `onderhandelen`), and a closed class of fixed
inseparable prefixes (be-, ver-, her-, ont-, ge-, er-) that never take `ge-` in the past participle
(matches Unit 34's rule). The **semantic patterning of these prefixes** is the useful supplement
here: `her-` ≈ "re-" (herschrijven "rewrite"), `ont-` ≈ "un-/de-/dis-" (ontbossen "deforest"),
`ver-` marks a process of change (verkleinen "make smaller") and, combined with a reflexive
pronoun, can specifically mean "to make a mistake doing X" (zich verrekenen "to miscalculate," zich
verkijken op "to misjudge") — a distinct enough sub-pattern to flag separately. `be-` often
intensifies or transitivizes a verb, frequently eliminating the need for a preposition (`antwoord
op de brief` vs. `beantwoord de brief`, both "answer the letter"). `er-`/`ge-` carry no consistent
meaning shift. `gedogen` ("to tolerate," exercise vocabulary) is worth flagging as culturally
loaded — the noun form `gedoogbeleid` ("tolerance policy") underlies well-known Dutch soft-drug and
euthanasia policy discourse, a register/connotation nuance beyond the bare dictionary gloss.

### Unit 43/44 — Reflexive verbs
Full reflexive-pronoun paradigm (me/je/zich-u/ons/je/zich) and placement rule (directly after the
first/finite verb; stays put under inversion, producing "je je" sequences like "Vergis je je
nooit?"). Unit 44 covers "part-time" reflexives — verbs like `wassen` that are reflexive only when
subject and object are the same entity (`Ik was me` vs. `Ik was de hond`) — and the `-zelf`
suffix added to the reflexive pronoun specifically when contrasting two referents ("Ze verbaasde
niet alleen het publiek, ze verbaasde ook zichzelf"). Both units match `established/012`/`013`'s
reflexive-verb coverage in substance; kept terse, vocabulary retained.

### Unit 45 — Imperative
Bare stem for the basic imperative (kom hier), with `zijn`'s irregular imperative stem `wees`
(wees voorzichtig). Separable-verb imperatives move the prefix to the end (Hou mijn jas vast).
The polite imperative uses the `u`-conjugated verb form + `u` (gaat u zitten). The infinitive is
also frequently used for impersonal/written instructions — recipes, signs, advertising ("Niet
parkeren," "Nu kopen!") — a register note (infinitive-as-imperative reads as impersonal/generic,
distinct from the direct personal imperative) worth keeping. Softening particles (maar, even, eens,
toch) are name-checked here and cross-referenced to Units 87–88 for fuller treatment.

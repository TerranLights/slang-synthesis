**Non-redundant-supplement note.** Dutch already has extensive established coverage (`001`–`017`),
including two other beginner-level phrasebook/course-style sources of near-identical genre and
level: `004_teach_yourself_dutch_conversation.md` (Marleen Owen, dialogue-based) and
`005`/`005b_dutch_for_dummies*.md` (Kwakernaak, also dialogue/Q&A-based, also covers pronouns,
present tense, `u`/`jij` formality, numbers, professions, shopping, food, telephone). This book
overlaps heavily with those two in territory (it is literally the same publisher's more basic
sibling volume to the `Teach Yourself Dutch` course already covered, and treats near-identical
communicative situations to *Dutch For Dummies*). Applying the coverage rule's skip clause
aggressively here:

- **Skipped as redundant:** the core present-tense pronoun-conjugation paradigm (`ik/jij/u/hij-zij-het/
  wij/jullie/zij` + verb stem/`-t`/`-en` endings) and the `u` vs. `jij` formality distinction — already
  fully documented in `005_dutch_for_dummies.md` and touched in `001`. Cardinal numbers, days of the
  week, and colours as bare vocabulary lists — already covered in `004` and `005`. The `is het niet?`
  colloquial tag question (itself flagged in this source as an anglicism) is genuinely new and kept.
- **Kept as genuinely new:** the systematic formality explanation (`u` used for age/seniority/
  unfamiliarity, spelled out more explicitly here than elsewhere), the full separable-verb and modal-
  verb paradigms, the `er` construction, the imperative/instruction-verb system, the reflexive-verb
  paradigm, the comparative/superlative system, demonstratives, the future-tense periphrastic
  construction, and the dialogue-specific situational vocabulary (health/pharmacy, telephone,
  post office, restaurant ordering, clothes shopping, cinema/entertainment) that these other sources
  don't share verbatim. Grammar points below are written as this agent's own paraphrase, citing the
  source's own page numbers.

## Grammar points

### Formality: u vs. jij/je (Ch.1, p.13)

Dutch has a two-tier T/V address system. **U** is the formal/polite form used with strangers,
elders, and in professional contexts; **jij/je** is informal, used with family, friends, children,
and peers. The source frames the choice explicitly around social distance and age rather than a
fixed rule — closer to a pragmatic register decision than a strict grammatical one. This chapter's
own present-tense paradigm table (**ik help / jij helpt / u helpt / hij-zij-het helpt / wij helpen /
jullie helpen / zij helpen**) shows **u** taking the same **-t** ending as the third person singular,
not a distinct ending of its own.

### Stressed vs. unstressed subject-pronoun forms (Ch.2, p.20-21; Ch.3, p.32)

Dutch subject pronouns have reduced, unstressed spoken forms distinct from their full written
forms: **hij** → **ie**, **zij** → **ze**, **het** → **'t**, **wij** → **we**. The source presents
these as the normal spoken-register default (used whenever the pronoun isn't specifically
emphasized), with the full forms reserved for contrastive stress or formal writing — an explicit
stressed/unstressed pronoun-allomorphy account not previously captured in this project's other
Dutch established files.

### Register pair: is het niet? vs. niet waar? (Ch.2, p.15)

The English-calqued tag question **is het niet?** ("isn't that so?") is flagged by the source as a
colloquial anglicism that has become the everyday spoken tag; the native Dutch tag **niet waar?**
is noted as having drifted toward a formal register in contemporary spoken Dutch — an explicit
register-shift annotation, not merely a synonym pair.

### Dutch clock-time system: half X = X:30, not X+1:30 (Ch.2, pp.25-26)

Dutch **half** + hour-word denotes the half-hour *before* that hour, i.e. **half acht** = 7:30
(literally "half toward eight"), not 8:30 as an English speaker might assume by analogy with "half
eight" (British English idiom for 8:30). This is a systematic false-friend trap the source calls
out explicitly. Built on top of this base: **kwart voor/over** (quarter to/past) the hour, and
**X voor/over half** (X minutes before/after the half-hour) for finer time expressions.

### Verb + infinitive at clause end (Ch.7, p.71-72)

When two verbs combine in one clause, the finite (conjugated) verb stays in its normal position
(second in a statement, first/second in a question) while the second verb appears as a bare
infinitive at the very end of the clause: **Ga je morgen naar het museum?** vs. **Gaat Frans morgen
naar het voetbal kijken?** The infinitive doesn't change form regardless of subject. The source
generalizes this to the small closed set of verbs that license a following infinitive:
**zullen, mogen, moeten, kunnen, willen** (modal verbs) plus **gaan** (near-future/purpose
construction). By Ch.11 (p.113) the source shows this stacking to *three* verbs in one clause — one
finite + two infinitives at the end: **Waar zullen we gaan eten?** ("Where shall we go to eat?").

### niet-placement and the niet/geen negation split (Ch.6, pp.68-69)

**Geen** negates an indefinite noun phrase, replacing **een** or a bare (unmarked) plural/mass
noun: **Ik ga geen boodschappen doen**. **Niet** negates everything else, and its placement varies
systematically by what's being negated:
- Before a two-verb clause's final (infinitive) verb, **niet** sits immediately before that verb,
  not at the very end of the clause: **Ik kan mijn zoon niet helpen** (not *...helpen niet*).
- Before a preposition, an adverb, or a predicate adjective following the noun it modifies, **niet**
  precedes that element directly: **Ik kan mijn zoon niet met zijn huiswerk helpen**; **Mijn zoon
  kan nog niet goed praten**; **Ik ga mijn huisdeur niet blauw schilderen**.

The source is explicit that **niet**'s position is not free — it tracks a fixed scope-based rule,
not a stylistic choice.

### Imperative formation and pragmatic softening with maar/toch (Ch.10, p.98)

The Dutch imperative is formed from the bare verb stem (the infinitive minus **-en**/**-n**), with
the same spelling adjustments as elsewhere (open/closed syllable vowel-doubling, final-devoicing
consonant swaps): **zitten** → **zit**, **komen** → **kom**, **eten** → **eet** (vowel kept long),
**geven** → **geef** (final **v** becomes **f**), **gaan** → **ga**. Subject pronouns (**jij**/**u**)
are never included in the imperative sentence itself. Crucially, the *same surface form* can read as
a blunt order, a neutral instruction, or a gentle invitation depending on intonation and, especially,
which discourse particle is added: **maar** softens a command into an invitation/encouragement
(**Kom maar met ons mee** reads as gentler than the bare **Kom met ons mee**), while **toch** added
to an imperative implies the speaker perceives a delay and can carry mild irritation or urging
(**Schiet toch op**, **Zeur toch niet**, **Ga toch naar de dokter**). This is an independent worked
example of the discourse-particle-as-pragmatic-softener mechanism already documented for **toch** in
`002_chapter16_colloquial_speech.md`, but specifically in the imperative-mood context, which `002`
did not cover.

### Separable verbs (Ch.9, p.93; Ch.12, p.124)

A separable verb's detachable prefix splits off and moves to the end of the clause when the verb is
used as the finite verb on its own: **Hij zegt onze afspraak voor morgen af** (from **afzeggen**,
"to cancel"), **De trein komt om half elf aan** (from **aankomen**), **Ik verbind u door** (from
**doorverbinden**). When the separable verb combines with a modal/auxiliary (per the verb+infinitive
rule above), the whole separable verb reunites as a single infinitive at the clause's end instead:
**Ik zal om half zes aankomen**; **Zullen we vast voor morgen afspreken?**

**eruitzien** ("to look, appear" — Ch.12, p.124) is a genuinely unusual three-part case: its
detachable material is itself two morphemes, **er** and **uit**, which split apart from each other
(not just from **zien**) and sandwich any following adjective between them: **Ziet hij er moe uit?**
("Does he look tired?"). With no adjective present, the two parts stay adjacent: **Hoe ziet zij
eruit?** ("What does she look like?"). This is a more elaborate separable-verb pattern than the
simple single-prefix cases and worth flagging as its own morphosyntactic data point for later
mechanics analysis of Dutch's separable-verb system.

### Demonstratives dit/deze/dat/die and adjective agreement (Ch.8, p.84)

Demonstratives agree with the de/het-word distinction the same way the indefinite article and
attributive adjectives do: **dit**/**dat** for **het**-word singulars, **deze**/**die** for
**de**-word singulars and all plurals. Comparisons use **dan** ("than"): **Een Ford Galaxy is
groter dan een Ferrari**.

### Gender-conditioned personal pronouns for inanimate reference (Ch.11, p.117)

The de/het distinction also determines which personal pronoun is used to refer back to an inanimate
noun: a **het**-word is referred to with **het** (**Het boek is interessant** → **Het is
interessant**), while a **de**-word is referred to with **hij** (**De stoel is comfortabel** → **Hij
is comfortabel**) as subject, or **hem** as object (**Ik geef Huib de appel** → **Ik geef Huib
hem**), vs. **het** as the object pronoun for a **het**-word (**Ik geef Truus het boek** → **Ik geef
Truus het**). This threads the de/het gender system through the pronoun system more explicitly than
prior established Dutch files have documented for inanimate reference specifically (as opposed to
just article/adjective agreement).

### Comparative and superlative formation (Ch.8 p.83, Ch.11 p.112)

Regular comparative: adjective + **-er** (**goedkoop** → **goedkoper**, **klein** → **kleiner**).
Adjectives ending in **-r** take an inserted **-d-** before **-er**: **duur** → **duurder**.
Regular superlative: adjective + **-st** (**zoet** → **zoetst**). Irregular triples: **goed/beter/
best**, **weinig/minder/minst**, **veel/meer/meest**. **Graag** ("gladly") / **liever**
("preferably") / **het liefst** ("most preferably") form a separate preference-scale that can *only*
function as adverbs (never attributive adjectives), still taking **dan** for direct comparison:
**Ik eet liever vis dan vlees**. Equative ("as X as") constructions: **evenveel ... als**, **net
zoveel ... als** (for nouns/verbs), **even [adjective] als**, **net zo [adjective] als** (for
adjectives) — e.g. **De groentesoep is even lekker als de tomatensoep**.

### Reflexive verbs and the reflexive pronoun zich (Ch.12, pp.128-129)

Dutch reflexive verbs require an object pronoun co-referential with the subject: **ik interesseer
me**, **jij interesseert je**, **u interesseert u/zich**, **hij/zij interesseert zich**, **wij
interesseren ons**, **jullie interesseren je**, **zij interesseren zich**. **-Zelf** can be appended
for emphasis (**ik was mezelf**), but is not obligatory the way English "myself" often is. Placement:
the reflexive pronoun normally follows the finite verb (**Ik interesseer me erg voor muziek**), but
when the clause opens with something other than the subject, the reflexive pronoun comes directly
after the subject instead (**Vanavond ga ik me wassen**). The phrase **ik interesseer me (niet) voor
...** ("I'm (not) interested in ...") is introduced as a whole unanalyzed chunk back in Ch.7 (p.72),
with the source explicitly deferring the grammatical explanation of the **me** until this chapter —
a deliberate pedagogical sequencing choice worth noting for how this course structures its grammar
progression.

### Body-pain expression: three competing constructions with a lexical restriction (Ch.12, p.130)

Dutch has (at least) three distinct constructions for describing bodily pain, not freely
interchangeable: (1) **Ik heb pijn in mijn [bodypart]** ("I have pain in my [bodypart]"); (2)
**Mijn [bodypart] doet pijn/zeer** ("my [bodypart] hurts" — plural subject takes plural verb: **Mijn
ogen doen pijn**); (3) a closed compound-noun set, **[bodypart]+pijn**, that only exists for a
specific list of body parts (**hoofdpijn**, **buikpijn**, **oorpijn**, **keelpijn**, **kiespijn**) —
the source explicitly notes that **ik heb voetpijn** "sounds odd," i.e. this compounding pattern
does not productively generalize to every body part, a real lexical-gap constraint on an otherwise
productive Dutch compounding strategy. Distinct again from both: **ik heb last van mijn ...**,
reserved for chronic/intermittent complaints (**Ik heb last van duizeligheid**, "I'm having dizzy
spells") rather than acute pain.

### Compound-noun formation, informally illustrated (Ch.10 p.101, Ch.11 p.116)

The source twice calls out compound nouns as concatenations of two independently meaningful words,
without using formal morphological terminology: **vuilniszak** = **vuilnis** ("rubbish") + **zak**
("bag"), alongside **papierbak**, **boodschappentas**, **wegwerpen** (Ch.10 p.101); and colour
compounding, **lichtgroen** ("light green") = **licht** + **groen**, **donkerrood** ("dark red") =
**donker** + **rood** (Ch.11 p.116) — both cases of Dutch's fully productive closed-compound
strategy (no space, no hyphen) referenced directly in this language's own morphological-typology
note in `00_Extraction_Checklist.md`.

### Ordinal numbers (Ch.13, p.138)

Formed by adding **-de** to the cardinal number (**tweede**, **vierde**, **vijfde** ...) or
**-ste** for **acht** ("achtste" — no extra **-t-** doubling) and for every decade form from
**twintig** onward (**twintigste**, **dertigste**, ...). **Eerste** ("first") and **derde**
("third") are outright irregular/suppletive rather than built from **een**/**drie**. The source
contrasts this with English's three different ordinal suffixes (**-st**, **-nd**, **-th**) — Dutch
collapses to just the two, conditioned almost entirely by whether the cardinal ends in a vowel/**t**
sound already.

### The polite conditional zou/zouden (Ch.13, p.142)

**Zou** (singular) / **zouden** (plural) is grammatically the past tense of **zullen**, but this
course introduces it exclusively for its politeness function, not to express past time: **Zou ik je
spoorboekje even kunnen lenen?** ("Could I just borrow your train timetable?"). Because the
politeness is already encoded in the conditional verb form itself, the source notes **alstublieft**
is not added alongside a **zou**-question — stacking both would be redundant, and the source frames
this as a genuine contrast with English/British politeness norms, where "please" still normally
accompanies even a maximally polite request. This is presented as "the only past tense used in this
book," a deliberate pedagogical scoping choice worth noting.

### Genitive-fossilized time adverbs: 's + time noun (Ch.14, p.152)

**'s winters**, **'s zomers**, **'s morgens/'s ochtends**, **'s middags**, **'s avonds**, **'s
nachts** ("in the winter," "in the morning," etc.) are built from a fossilized genitive-case
**-s** marker prefixed with an apostrophe standing in for an elided **des** (historically "des
winters," literally "of the winter"), no longer a productive case-marking pattern anywhere else in
the modern language. This is a genuine fusional/case-marking remnant, directly relevant to this
language's own "predominantly analytic/mildly fusional" morphological-typology note in
`00_Extraction_Checklist.md` — a concrete second data point for that "light fusional residue"
beyond the gender/adjective-agreement system already documented in `001`.

### Three future-reference strategies: present tense, zullen/gaan, and worden (Ch.14, p.165)

The source distinguishes three constructions for referring to future time, not freely
interchangeable: (1) the **present tense** alone for a scheduled/planned future action (**Ik reis
volgende week naar Brazilië**, "I am going to Brazil next week"); (2) **zullen**/**gaan** +
infinitive specifically when expressing *intention* (the modal-verb-plus-infinitive-at-clause-end
pattern documented above); (3) **worden** ("to become") used as a genuinely separate
future-prediction strategy for states/conditions rather than actions: **Het wordt warm en zonnig**
("It will be warm and sunny"), **Dat wordt f12,50** ("That will be f12.50"). This three-way split —
scheduled-present vs. intentional-modal vs. predictive-**worden** — is a more granular future-tense
account than this project's other established Dutch files have documented.

### Numeral + -en group-count construction (Ch.14, p.158)

To say "the N of us/you/them," Dutch suffixes **-en** to the cardinal number and combines it with a
possessive marking whose group is meant: **op/in mijn eentje** ("on my own"), **met z'n tweeën**
("the two of them"), **met ons drieën** ("the three of us"), **met jullie vieren** ("the four of
you"), **met z'n vijven** ("the five of them" — note the **f**→**v** consonant-alternation on
**vijf**, consistent with the f/v alternation pattern already documented in `001`). This is a
distinct grammaticalized numeral-suffixation pattern, not previously captured in this project's
Dutch established files.

### The nominalized infinitive as a het-word (Ch.14, p.161)

Any Dutch infinitive can function directly as a noun with no further derivation, and the resulting
noun is always a **het**-word regardless of the underlying verb: **ik hou van fietsen** ("I like
cycling"), **het lezen** ("the reading/reading as an activity"). This zero-derivation
verb-to-noun pattern is a genuinely productive piece of Dutch's noun-formation system worth flagging
for later morphological-mechanics analysis — distinct from, and simpler than, the diminutive and
compounding patterns already documented.

### The diminutive suffix system, an independent overview (Ch.14, pp.156-157)

This chapter gives its own compact but complete presentation of the four diminutive allomorphs
already documented in depth in `003_chapter22_diminutives.md` — **-tje** (**tafeltje**,
**stoeltje**, **retourtje**, **enkeltje**, **eltje**), **-etje** (**mannetje**, **ringetje**,
**kammetje**, **bruggetje**, **kippetje**), **-pje** (**boompje**, **museumpje**, **geheimpje**),
**-kje** (**puddinkje**, **woninkje**) — plus the rule that all diminutives are **het**-words and
form their plural with **-s** regardless of the base noun's own plural strategy (**het huisje** →
**de huisjes**, even though the base **huis** pluralizes as **huizen**). The genuinely new material
relative to `003`: this chapter's semantic-range discussion explicitly includes a **negative/
pejorative** diminutive sense alongside the affectionate and positive-intensifying senses `003`
already documented — **wat een raar mannetje!** ("what a queer little fellow!") is given as an
example where the diminutive conveys a distinctly negative impression rather than affection or
positive emphasis. `003` documented the diminutive's pragmatic/attitude-marking use broadly (its
§22.3 "personalizing suffix" discussion) but not this specific negative-connotation pole of that
same pragmatic mechanism — worth flagging as a genuine (if narrow) supplement to that file's
findings, per the non-redundant-supplement pattern's expectation that even "already covered"
territory can yield a real gap.

### A closed compounding restriction: [bodypart]+pijn (cross-reference)

See the "Body-pain expression" grammar point above — the **[bodypart]+pijn** compound pattern
(Ch.12, p.130) is itself a data point for how Dutch's otherwise very productive closed-compounding
strategy (see the compound-noun point above) can still have real lexical gaps for specific
semantic domains, worth keeping in mind alongside the more freely productive compounding examples
documented elsewhere in this file.

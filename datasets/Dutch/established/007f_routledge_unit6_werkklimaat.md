## Grammar points

### 1. *Er* + preposition (a fourth use of *er*)

The unit recaps three uses of *er* already introduced in Unit 5 (indefinite-subject *er*, e.g.
*Er staat een paard in de gang*; locative *er*, e.g. *Ik ben er nog nooit geweest*; and numeral *er*,
e.g. *Ik heb er drie*) and adds a fourth: *er* standing in for the object of a fixed
verb-preposition combination (p. 168–169). With verbs that take a fixed preposition (*vragen naar*,
*succes hebben met*, *onderzoek doen naar*, *de brui geven aan*, *stappen uit*), *er* + preposition
can be split across the clause: *Ik vraag de verkoper naar de prijs* → *Ik vraag de verkoper
ernaar*, and further to *Doe jij er onderzoek naar?* with *er* immediately after the finite verb and
the preposition pushed to clause-final position — described as a more informal, spoken-register
construction. Two irregular combining forms are flagged: *met* becomes *mee* with *er* (*ermee*,
not *ermet*), and *naar* becomes *naartoe* with *er* when indicating direction (*Ik ga er vanmiddag
naartoe*). *Daar* is the stressed counterpart of *er* used for emphasis or contrast (*Daar rijd ik
niet mee!*).

### 2. Subclauses of time, contrast, comparison, condition, and reason (pp. 181–182)

Subordinating conjunctions link a subclause to a main clause and push the subclause's finite verb to
clause-final position (already established as the general subclause word-order rule; this unit's
contribution is the specific inventory of conjunctions by semantic category, not the underlying
verb-final rule itself):

- **Time:** *voordat* (before), *wanneer* (when), *terwijl* (while), *nadat* (after), *sinds*
  (since) — e.g. *Sinds ze ontslagen is, voelt ze zich futloos en depressief* ("Since she was fired,
  she feels listless and depressed").
- **Contrast:** *ondanks (het feit dat)* (despite [the fact that]), *hoewel* (although).
- **Comparison:** *alsof* (as if), *zoals* (like, as).
- **Condition:** *als* (if), *tenzij* (unless).
- **Cause/reason:** *omdat* (because), *zodat* (so that).

Also introduced in a follow-on exercise: *toen* ("when," used specifically with past tense, in
contrast to *wanneer*/*als*), *zodra* ("as soon as"), *totdat* ("until"). Exercises stress that
subclauses can precede or follow the main clause (*Hoewel mijn baas heel aardig is, doe ik mijn baan
niet graag* / *Ik doe mijn baan niet graag hoewel mijn baas heel aardig is*), with the main clause
inverting (finite verb first) when the subclause comes first.

### 3. Sentence structure revision: verb clusters and adverbial order (pp. 194–195)

A systematic revision table (p. 194) lays out main-clause verb position across one-, two-, and
three-verb clauses: with one verb, the finite verb occupies position 2 regardless of what's fronted
(*Ik vertrek vanavond* / *Vanavond vertrek ik* / *Wanneer vertrek je?*); with two verbs, the finite
verb stays in position 2 and the non-finite verb(s) go clause-final (*Ik moet om 8 uur zeker
vertrekken*); with three verbs, all three stack at the clause end in a "verb cluster" (*Wanneer zou
je me kunnen helpen?*; *Hij zou de kaartjes gisteren hebben gekocht* / *...gekocht hebben* — both
orders of the past-tense auxiliary within the cluster are given as acceptable). In subclauses, the
entire verb cluster (including the ordinarily-position-2 finite verb) moves to clause-final position,
again with either order of a doubled auxiliary+participle possible (*Ik ben moe omdat ik vannacht
niet geslapen heb* / *...niet heb geslapen*). Separately, adverbial order within a clause is given as
**time → manner → place** — the reverse of English's typical place-then-time ordering (p. 195):
*We vertrekken morgen [time] met de auto [manner] naar Frankrijk [place]*.

### 4. Relative clauses (pp. 200–202)

Relative clauses immediately follow their antecedent and are structured like subclauses (verb moves
to clause end). Pronoun choice:

- ***Die/dat*** — the default relative pronoun set, chosen by the antecedent's grammatical gender:
  *die* for a *de*-word antecedent (*Wij zoeken mensen die aan ons project willen meewerken*), *dat*
  for a *het*-word antecedent (*Wij hebben een team dat schitterend samenwerkt*).
- ***Wie/wat*** — used instead of *die/dat* when there is no clear (nominal) antecedent, when the
  antecedent is a superlative, an indefinite like *alles*/*niets*, or when the antecedent is an
  entire preceding clause rather than a single noun: *wie* for people (*Wie geïnteresseerd is, kan
  contact opnemen*), *wat* for things/clauses (*Het minste wat we kunnen doen, is een kaartje
  sturen*; *Hij is niet de favoriet van de directie, wat algemeen bekend is* — *wat* here refers back
  to the whole preceding clause, not a single noun).
- **Prepositional relative clauses** — when the relativized element is the object of a preposition,
  people use *preposition + wie* (*De vriend aan wie ik dat boek heb uitgeleend...*), while things use
  *waar + preposition* fused into one word, which can also split with the preposition trailing (*De
  stoel waarop u zit* / *De stoel waar u op zit*).

### 5. Register: addressing people in writing (pp. 176–177)

Formal address uses *heer* + surname / *mevrouw* + surname (no capitalization required for these
titles). *Beste* is a neutral salutation, closed with *met vriendelijke groet(en)* (or the informal
*groetjes*); the more formal *Geachte* + name is closed with *Hoogachtend*, though the source notes
*Hoogachtend* is becoming rarer in everyday business use while persisting in government, legal, and
banking correspondence. Under Anglophone/egalitarian influence, the Netherlands shows a stronger
trend than Belgium toward first-name/*je-jij* address even in the workplace, displacing the more
hierarchical *meneer/mevrouw* + *u* — but the source cautions this is a tendency, not a rule, and
Flanders in particular still defaults to *u* between strangers.

### 6. Register: inviting people (p. 178)

Three politeness registers for invitations are distinguished by phrasing, not by any separate
grammatical construction: **direct** (*Wil je vanavond bij me komen eten?* / *Kom je vanavond bij me
eten?*), **more careful/tentative** (*Zou je vanavond bij ons willen komen eten?* / *Heb je zin om
vanavond bij me te komen eten?* — using *zou* + infinitive or *zin hebben om* + *te*-infinitive to
soften the request), and **formal** (passive/impersonal *Je bent/wordt uitgenodigd voor...*, *U
bent/wordt vriendelijk uitgenodigd op...*).

### 7. Register: complimenting and offering criticism as advice (pp. 188–189)

Compliments use simple exclamatory patterns (*Wat zie je er mooi uit!*, *Goh, dat kan jij goed!*,
*Dat heb je heel goed gedaan*). For criticism, the source explicitly frames Dutch speakers as
comparatively direct and willing to voice criticism openly (*Dat is onzin!* "That's nonsense!";
*Dat lijkt nergens op* "That looks terrible"; *Jij komt altijd te laat!*) — cautioning the learner
not to read this bluntness as rudeness. It then teaches a softening strategy: phrasing criticism as
conditional advice using *zou* + infinitive (*Dat zou ik niet doen, als ik jou was* "I wouldn't do
that, if I were you"; *Ik zou het anders doen als ik jou was*) or as a suggestion with *kan/kunnen*
(*Je kan ook minder bijzinnen gebruiken* "You could use fewer subclauses").

### 8. Belgian Dutch / Flemish regional features (pp. 165–167)

Explicitly marked as street-register variation in Flanders (the standard *written* language is the
same as in the Netherlands, per the source):

- **Pronouns:** *gij/ge* instead of *jij/je*, with its own conjugated verb form (*Gij zijt zot
  zeker!* "You must be crazy!"; note the distinct verb form *zijt* vs. standard *bent*). Also,
  informal-register use of the formal object pronoun *u* and possessive *uw* where Northern Dutch
  would use *je/jou(w)* (*Zeg, Jan, ik zal u morgen terugbellen*) — i.e., Flemish *u* does not carry
  the same formality marking as Northern Dutch *u* in this context.
- **Diminutive suffix:** *-ke* instead of *-je* in some diminutives (*een babyke*, *een glaske*).
- **French lexical influence:** *allez* ("come on"), *ça va* ("I'm fine"/"everything all right?"),
  *merci* ("thank you"), *madame* ("Mrs"), *plezant* ("nice, fun"), *een tas* ("a cup" — a false
  friend, see below), plus many food and car-part terms.
- **Semantic false friends:** *schoon* means "beautiful" in Flemish but "clean" in Northern Dutch;
  *tas* means "cup" in Flemish but "bag" in Northern Dutch; *patat* means "potato" in Flemish but
  "chips/fries" in Northern Dutch (Northern Dutch uses *frieten*/*friet* for what Flemish also calls
  *frieten*, while Flemish *patat* = Northern Dutch *aardappel*).
- **Prepositions:** Flemish speakers favor *op* where Northern Dutch uses *in* — *op restaurant*,
  *op café*, *op de tram* (vs. Northern Dutch *in het restaurant*, etc.).
- **Minority-language status (sociolinguistic aside, not Flemish-specific):** the source notes that
  Fries (Friesland) has long-standing official minority-language status, that Limburgs and Zeeuws are
  campaigning for the same status, and that linguists have argued Zeeuws and Limburgs are dialects
  rather than separate languages — while Fries is described as "a sibling of English and a cousin of
  Dutch" in the wider Germanic family tree. This is a source-stated genealogical/sociolinguistic
  claim, not this project's own analysis.

### 9. Productive slang-adjacent word formation: pejorative nonce compounds (p. 206, satirical column)

Not a named grammar point in the source, but a notable pattern surfaced by the Youp Van 't Hek
newspaper-column excerpt (see Vocabulary below): the columnist coins *de boerenkoolpsycholoog*
("kale/cabbage-psychologist") as a one-off mocking compound for a corporate motivational trainer,
built by prefixing an ordinary noun carrying a pejorative connotation (*boerenkool* "kale" — glossed
by the source itself as connoting "banal, common, stupid") onto an otherwise neutral professional
title. This is a live, generative compounding strategy for coining insults/mockery terms in Dutch,
not a fixed lexical item, and is worth flagging for the mechanics-analysis phase as a template
(pejorative-connotation noun + profession/role noun → mocking nonce compound).

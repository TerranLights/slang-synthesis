## Grammar points

### Coordinate and subordinate clauses (pp. 122-125)

Only `en`/`maar`/`of`/`want` are coordinating (no word-order change); every other conjunction is
subordinating and sends the finite verb to clause-final position. `Dat` ("that") is **never**
omissible in Dutch the way English "I think (that) he did it" allows — a genuine obligatory-vs-
optional grammaticalization contrast between the two languages. When a subordinate clause opens the
sentence, the main clause's subject and verb invert to keep the finite verb in overall second
position (the "two ideas side by side" framing the source uses). `Of` is lexically ambiguous
between coordinating "or" (no reordering) and subordinating "whether" (reordering) — disambiguated
only by word order/context. `Als` is similarly ambiguous between "if" and "when," while `of`
meaning "whether" is distinct from `als` meaning "if" — the source flags this as one of the
trickier conjunction-choice points for a learner. `Toen` (past-single-event "when") vs. `wanneer`
(habitual/repeated "whenever," also the direct-question "when") vs. `als` (conditional "if"/general
"when") is a genuine three-way split that English "when" collapses.

### Interrogatives in indirect questions (pp. 126-127)

Question words act exactly like subordinating conjunctions once embedded in an indirect question,
sending the finite verb to the end of their own clause (`Ik weet niet wanneer zij komt`).

### The simple past (imperfect) tense (pp. 127-129)

Regular formation via `-te(n)`/`-de(n)` per 't kofschip; irregular verbs show root-vowel ablaut
(paradigm listed in vocabulary table), cross-referenced to the source's own full irregular-verb
appendix. **Central register-mechanics finding, though not itself colloquial-tagged**: Dutch
strongly prefers the perfect tense for isolated past events in speech, reserving the imperfect for
repeated/habitual past actions and for descriptions — the reverse of a naive expectation that
"simple past" would be the default conversational choice. A perfect in English is always rendered
as a perfect in Dutch, but an English simple past ("She bought it last year") is *more often*
rendered as a Dutch perfect than as a Dutch imperfect, despite the superficial tense-name match —
a genuine translation trap the source calls out explicitly.

fossilized genitive `'s`-marking on weekday/time-of-day adverbials.

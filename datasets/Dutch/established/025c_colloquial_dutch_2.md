## Grammar points

### Language point 1 — More on the definite article

Dutch uses the definite article in several situations where English omits it: abstract nouns used
generically (**de mens**, **de natuur**, **de geschiedenis**, **de liefde**, **de hemel**), and a
long, non-rule-governed list of fixed idioms — with seasons (**in de zomer**), meals (**na het
ontbijt**), street/park/square names (**in de Gildstraat**, **door het Vondelpark**), and religions
(**aanhangers van de Islam**). The source is explicit that no productive rule covers the idiom
list — it must be learned item by item as encountered. pp.35-36.

### Language point 2 — The imperative

Formed from the bare verb stem, as in English (**Praat/rij alsjeblieft langzamer!**). Softened by
**maar**, **eens**, and/or **even** (in natural speech **eens** is almost always reduced to **'s**)
— stacking multiple softeners is normal (**Lees dat maar eens even voor, alsjeblieft**). A more
formal/polite imperative is formed by inverting the **u**-form of the verb (**Gaat u zitten!**),
and there is an optional **jullie**-form (**Rijden jullie eens wat langzamer!**). **Jij**, **u**,
or **jullie** can be added after the verb for emphasis. A genuinely command-toned (not
request-toned) imperative uses the bare infinitive instead of the stem: **Niet roken!**, **Niet
doen!**, **Doorlopen!**. Notable colloquial-register detail: fixed imperative expressions using a
meaningless particle **ze** that has no meaning outside this construction — **Werk ze!** ("work
hard"), **Slaap ze!** ("sleep well"), **Eet ze!** ("bon appétit") — the source flags this as a
genuinely idiosyncratic colloquial pattern with no productive rule behind it. pp.38-40.

### Language point 3 — Er as a dummy subject

**Er** functions like English "there" as a dummy/placeholder subject when the true subject is
indefinite and stands later in the clause: **Er komt regen** ("Rain is on the way"), **Er steekt
een storm op**, **Er vallen buien**, **Er wordt koude lucht aangevoerd**. This usage is
significantly more common in Dutch than the parallel "there"-construction in English — natural
Dutch generally *requires* **er** in these indefinite-subject sentences where English idiom often
prefers fronting the real subject instead. Critically, **er** is only grammatical when the subject
is indefinite: **Er spelen kinderen in de tuin** (indefinite, uses **er**) vs. **De kinderen spelen
in de tuin** (definite subject — **er** is now ungrammatical). Cross-referenced forward to Unit 8
for **er** with the passive. pp.41-42.

### Language point 4 — Comparative of adjectives

Formed by adding **-er**, unlike English's two-track system (short adjectives take "-er," long
ones take "more") — Dutch has no such length-based split (**interessant/interessanter**,
**belangrijk/belangrijker**). However, a **meer**-periphrastic comparative does exist and is not
uncommon, though generally avoidable when in doubt: routinely used before adjectives ending in
**-isch**, before adjectives of nationality (**meer Hollands**), and usually before past
participles functioning as adjectives (**meer geïnteresseerd**, **meer gewend aan**, **meer
opgewonden**) — though an **-er** form is sometimes also possible there (**geïnteresseerder**,
**opgewondener**). When a comparative of more than three syllables stands in a pre-nominal
position that would normally require an **-e** ending, that **-e** is usually dropped even in
writing for ease of pronunciation: **een grotere jongen** (short, keeps **-e**) vs. **een
uitvoeriger(e) beschrijving** (long, **-e** optional/typically dropped). pp.46-47.

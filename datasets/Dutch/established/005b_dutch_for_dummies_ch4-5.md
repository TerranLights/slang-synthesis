## Grammar points

### The `u`/`jij`-`je` formality distinction (T-V system) (pp.67–68)

Dutch marks formality through second-person pronoun choice rather than through separate verb forms
or honorific vocabulary: `u` (formal, both singular and plural) vs. `jij`/unstressed `je` (informal
singular). The book gives concrete social heuristics rather than a grammatical rule: default to `u`
for anyone visibly older or in a position of authority; young service-industry staff default to
`je`/`jij` regardless of the customer's age; switching from `u` to `je` is a real, nameable speech
act (`Laten we 'je' zeggen`) generally initiated by the older or higher-status party, or requested
by the lower-status party via `Mag ik 'je' zeggen?`. Age ~30 is cited as an informal threshold above
which defaulting to `u` reads as simply polite rather than distant. This is a genuinely rich
sociolinguistic register system — more socially-negotiated than most European T-V systems' fixed
rules — and a strong candidate source for a derived-slang formality/politeness mechanism.

### Pronoun case: subject forms vs. after-preposition forms (p.69)

Dutch personal pronouns take a distinct form after a preposition, parallel to (but not identical
to) English subject/object pronouns: `ik`→`mij`/`me`, `jij`→`jou`/`je`, `hij`→`hem`, `zij`/`ze`→
`haar`, `wij`→`ons`, `jullie`→`jullie` (unchanged), `zij`(plural)→`hen` (prescriptively) or `hun`
(the form most native speakers actually produce). `u` never changes form regardless of syntactic
role. The `hen`/`hun` split is explicitly flagged by the source as a case where prescriptive grammar
and everyday native usage have diverged — a live, in-progress case-marking simplification (loss of a
distinct "correct" oblique-plural form in favor of a single all-purpose one) worth treating as a
genuine synchronic slang/vernacular-mechanics data point, not just a "learner should memorize the
correct form" footnote.

### Negation: `niet` vs. `geen` (p.94)

Dutch splits negation by what's being negated rather than using one general negator: `niet` negates
a verb/predicate (`Ik ben niet rijk` "I am not rich"); `geen` negates an indefinite noun phrase
(`Ik heb geen geld` "I have no money") and can take an intervening adjective (`geen kleine kinderen`
"no small children"). This is a real grammatical distinction with no single-word English equivalent
(English "not" covers both cases) — worth flagging for any conlang mechanics work on how negation
scope interacts with noun/verb structure.

### Kinship and relationship-term ambiguity (pp.92–93)

Several Dutch relationship terms are genuinely polysemous in ways the book flags explicitly rather
than glossing over: `neef`/`nicht` cover both "cousin" and "nephew/niece" (disambiguated only by the
diminutive `-je` form for the younger relation); `vriend`/`vriendin` cover both "platonic same- or
opposite-sex friend" and "romantic partner," disambiguated by speaker gender (a woman's unnamed
`vriend` is likely her boyfriend; a man's unnamed `vriendin` is likely his girlfriend) and by whether
a proper name is attached. `mijn partner` is the source-recommended unambiguous escape hatch. This
kind of context-and-speaker-gender-dependent lexical ambiguity — rather than a a dedicated separate
word for each relationship type — is a mechanism worth comparing against how a derived slang system
might economize its own kinship/relationship vocabulary.

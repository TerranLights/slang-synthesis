## Grammar points

### Negation and the position of *niet*/*nooit* (pp. 204-206)

A detailed positional rule set, not a single fixed slot as in English. **`Niet` follows**: (1)
expressions of time, (2) definite direct objects, (3) pronominal objects. **`Niet` precedes**: (1)
expressions of time only when contrastively emphasizing, (2) expressions of manner and place, (3)
prepositions/prepositional objects/phrases, (4) predicative adjectives, (5) indefinite direct
objects; it also either precedes or follows complements of `zijn`. `Geen` (not `niet`) negates
indefinite nouns ("not a/any"), and this extends to several fixed body-state idioms (`dorst`/
`honger`/`haast`/`slaap hebben`). Related negative-adverb constructions: `ook niet`/`ook geen`
("not...either") and `pas` ("not until/only").

### Prepositions (pp. 208-213)

A systematic list of Dutch's most common prepositions is given (see vocabulary table) with the
explicit caveat that their idiomatic uses cannot be reduced to rules and must be learned through
observed usage — `aan` and `op` are flagged as the two most ubiquitous and idiomatic. Some
prepositions can follow their noun instead of preceding it to express directional motion (`de brug
over`, `de hoek om`, `de gracht in`), and `heen` can combine with a fronted preposition for emphatic
"right through/around/over" senses, occasionally taking on fully idiomatic/figurative meaning
(`langs elkaar heen praten` — to talk at cross purposes). Certain prepositions form fixed couplets
that bracket the noun (`tegen een muur aan`, `onder de brug door`) — too numerous to list
exhaustively.

### Verbs with prepositional objects (pp. 213)

A distinct phenomenon from free prepositional phrases: certain verbs obligatorily pair with a
specific preposition to form a fixed collocation with a meaning not always predictable from the
parts (`denken aan` — to think of; `zich verheugen op` — to look forward to). The source explicitly
declines to give an exhaustive list, recommending these be picked up through exposure to texts — see
vocabulary table for the representative sample given.

### The separating of prefixes from infinitives and past participles (pp. 213-214)

Extends the separable-verb system: in a subordinate clause, where a separable verb's past participle
normally sits clause-finally attached to its auxiliary (`... heeft opgebeld`), a colloquial spoken
variant places the finite auxiliary *between* the separated prefix and the verb stem (`... op heeft
gebeld`) — explicitly flagged as more common in speech than writing. The same optional reordering
extends to double-infinitive constructions, where the first of two infinitives can be inserted
between a separable prefix and its verb (`op willen bellen` alongside standard `willen opbellen`).

### How to use the verb *hoeven* 'to need' (pp. 214-215)

English's "don't have to/don't need to" (the true negation of "must," distinct from a prohibition) is
rendered by **`hoeven`**, not a negated `moeten` — `Jij hoeft niet vroeg op te staan` ("you don't have
to get up early") is meaningfully different from `Jij moet niet vroeg opstaan` (closer to "you
mustn't," an order). `Hoeven` is modal-like but requires `te` before its dependent infinitive
(optional in the perfect's double-infinitive construction); `durven` ("to dare") behaves the same way.

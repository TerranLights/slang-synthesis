## Grammar points

### Transitive and intransitive verbs (pp. 156-157)

Dutch marks the transitive/intransitive distinction more consistently than English does, and
dictionaries flag it (`vi.`/`vt.`). All verbs taking `zijn` in the perfect are intransitive, but not
all intransitive verbs take `zijn` — many take `hebben` despite being unable to take a direct object
(`Wij hebben heel hard gelachen`). Worked couplets (`vertrekken`/`verlaten`, `branden`/`verbranden`,
`smaken`/`proeven`) illustrate that English sometimes uses one verb for both senses where Dutch
requires two lexically distinct verbs.

### Relative pronouns (pp. 157-159)

`Die`/`dat` (never a Dutch equivalent of "who"/"which"/"whom" as separate lexical choices — a single
binary gender/number-conditioned choice covers all of English's relative-pronoun range). `Die` for
common-gender or plural antecedents, `dat` for neuter-singular antecedents. Both act as
subordinating conjunctions, sending the finite verb of their clause to clause-final position.
Unlike English, the relative clause need not stand immediately adjacent to its antecedent noun —
the source gives a genuine 4-way word-order permutation of a single sentence, all equally
grammatical, to illustrate the flexibility. English's optional "that"-omission and "whom"-dropping
has no parallel — Dutch relative pronouns are never optional.

### Swearwords and exclamations (p. 159)

**See vocabulary table** for the full register-graded inventory transcribed with the source's own
glosses. Structurally: `god`-based oaths dominate the inventory, with `pot` functioning as a lexical
euphemism-substitute for `god` in several variants; **`godverdomme` stands alone as the one item
explicitly marked taboo/impolite-company-inappropriate**, everything else in the set (including the
visually similar `gedverdemme`) is source-glossed as innocuous. This is a clean, directly-source-
provided example of a register scale within a single semantic domain (mild expletive → strong
taboo curse) — exactly the kind of data this dispatch's register-flagship priority is looking for.

### Use of laten with infinitives (pp. 159-161)

Three distinct senses: (1) "to leave (behind)" as a plain transitive verb (`Ik heb mijn portemonnaie
thuis gelaten`); (2) as an auxiliary governing another infinitive with concrete senses ("to leave,"
"to drop," "to show" — `laten liggen`, `laten vallen`, `laten zien`); (3) as the dedicated
**causative** auxiliary ("to have/get someone do something," structurally "to let" — `laten
repareren`, `laten verven`, `laten komen`). Sense (3) is obligatory in Dutch wherever English uses
causative "have"/"get" + past participle; there is no separate causative verb the way English can
sometimes avoid "let." Stacking `moeten`/`laten`+infinitive produces up to four verbs in a single
clause-final cluster in the perfect (`Ik heb mijn schoenen moeten laten repareren`) — an extreme
case of the double-infinitive-family construction from `established/023i`.

### Some variations of word order (pp. 162-163)

A clause-final infinitive/past participle need not be absolutely final if what follows it is a
prepositional adverbial phrase (`Nederland kent vervoermiddelen die nauwelijks in andere landen
voorkomen` — `voorkomen` need not move after `in andere landen`, contra the "always clause-final"
expectation set up in earlier lessons). This is presented as an optional stylistic variation, not a
different rule — both orders are grammatical.

## Grammar points

### `daartoe` and the `er`/`daar`/`hier` + preposition family

`Daartoe` = `daar` (related to `er` used as a pronoun) + `toe` (a form of the preposition `tot`),
meaning "to that." This extends the `er`+preposition pattern from Les 1 (`ertoe` = "to it") with two
further variants using `daar` ("that") and `hier` ("this") instead of `er`: **ertoe** (to it),
**daartoe** (to that), **hiertoe** (to this) — a three-way deictic distance contrast layered onto the
same pronoun-+ preposition mechanism. p. 66.

### Adverb vs. adjective inflection after a possessive pronoun

A genuinely new inflection rule stated explicitly by the source: **after a possessive pronoun
(`mijn`, `jouw`, `uw`, `zijn`, `haar`, `ons`/`onze`, `jullie`, `hun`), always add `-e` to the
adjective.** Illustrated with `haar laatst verdiende...` ("her most recently earned [money/salary]")
— `laatst` stays uninflected because it's functioning as an adverb (modifying `verdiende`, "most
recently"), while `verdiende` is the adjective and takes the `-e`. p. 67.

### Word-building — nominalizing verbs with `-ing`

Beyond infinitive-as-noun (Les 2), Dutch also forms nouns from verbs by adding `-ing` to the verb
stem; the resulting noun is always a `de`-word: `beslissen` → `de beslissing`; `beheersen` → `de
beheersing`; `staken` ("to strike") → `de staking`; `bedoelen` ("to mean, intend") → `de bedoeling`
("the meaning, intention"). The stem is derived from the infinitive by dropping `-en`, preserving the
vowel sound with a spelling adjustment where needed: `staken` → `staak` (long vowel, doubled to keep
it long before a single consonant + `-ing`); `beslissen` → `beslis` (short vowel, single consonant
kept). This is a second, independent nominalization strategy alongside Les 2's infinitive-as-noun —
the two are not interchangeable in general (though this lesson's own vocabulary happens to show both
applied to the same underlying activity, `tekstverwerken`/`het tekstverwerken` vs. `de
tekstverwerking`), and are worth keeping distinct for the mechanics-analysis phase. p. 71.

> **de staking** = staken (verb, "to strike") stem `staak` + -ing (nominalizing suffix) — vowel
> lengthened/respelled to preserve the infinitive's long vowel sound before the suffix.

### Word-building — noun compounding, with and without a linking sound

Dutch combines two nouns into a single compound noun far more freely than English. The compound's
grammatical gender is **always that of the final element**, regardless of the first element's own
gender: `de verantwoordelijkheid` (de-word) + `het gevoel` (het-word) → `het
verantwoordelijkheidsgevoel` (het-word, taking gevoel's gender). Some compounds insert a linking `-s-`
purely for ease of pronunciation (as in `verantwoordelijkheidsgevoel` itself); others use no linking
sound at all; others use `-en-` (`boekenkast` "bookcase", `herenhuis`). Compounds are not limited to
noun+noun: Les 3's `de woonkamer` is verb-stem + noun (`woon-` from `wonen`), and `de benedenwoning`
is adverb + noun (`beneden` + `woning`). pp. 71–72.

> **het verantwoordelijkheidsgevoel** = verantwoordelijkheid (de, "responsibility") + -s- (linking
> sound) + gevoel (het, "feeling") — compound gender is het, from the final element, despite the
> first element being a de-word; demonstrates that Dutch compound-gender assignment is purely
> positional/mechanical, not semantically weighted toward either component.

### `zelfstandig` as unmarked adverb before a plural noun

`Zelfstandig` in "zelfstandig beslissingen te nemen" appears before the plural noun `beslissingen`
but is **not** inflected to agree with it, because it is functioning as an adverb modifying *how*
decisions are taken (independently/on one's own initiative), not as an adjective describing the
decisions themselves. A genuine ambiguity-resolution point: word position alone (immediately before
a plural noun) would suggest adjective-agreement is due, but function, not position, decides whether
`-e` is added. p. 70.

### Alternative conditional-clause formation: inverted question form without `als`

**Bent u creatief, hebt u een groot verantwoordelijkheidsgevoel...** is presented as an alternative
way of forming a conditional clause, equivalent in meaning to **Als u creatief bent, als u een groot
verantwoordelijkheidsgevoel hebt...** — the conditional sense is carried purely by subject-verb
inversion (as in a yes/no question) rather than by the conjunction `als`. The source flags this
construction for fuller treatment in Les 8. p. 70.

### `Geachte` vs. `Beste` — the formal/informal salutation split

The source explicitly states: **Dutch has two words, `Geachte` (formal) and `Beste` (informal),
where English has one — "Dear."** This is a direct, source-marked register-contrast pair, structurally
the same kind of finding as `026c`'s Belgian/Netherlands lexical table, but along the formal/informal
axis instead of the regional one. p. 65.

### Formal-letter conventions (register profile)

The source lists three defining features of formal Dutch letter-writing, useful as a register
checklist for slang-mechanics work by contrast (i.e. what colloquial Dutch systematically drops or
replaces):
1. use of the formal pronoun `u` rather than `jij`/`je`;
2. use of set formulas and stock phrases (e.g. `naar aanleiding van`, "further to");
3. avoidance of `ik` as the first word of the letter (a stylistic norm, not a grammar rule — compare
   the letter model's opening **Naar aanleiding van uw brief... heb ik het genoegen...** rather than
   *Ik heb het genoegen...*).

A genuine formality **gradient**, not just a binary, shows up across this lesson's letter closings:
`Hoogachtend` (most formal, used when the addressee's name/identity is unknown) > `Met beleefde
groeten` (formal, seen in the 1937 novel excerpt) > `Met vriendelijke groeten` (standard formal-but-
personable closing, used in this lesson's own model letters) — a three-point formality scale worth
keeping distinct from a flat "formal" tag for later mechanics-analysis work. pp. 65, 72–75.

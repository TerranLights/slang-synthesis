## Grammar points

### Subordinate clause word order — verb-final rule (major new topic)
In a Dutch subordinate clause introduced by a subordinating conjunction, the subject comes
immediately after the conjunction and **the finite verb (and any other verbal elements) moves to the
very end of the clause**, with everything else sandwiched in between: `Hij zegt dat hij naar Rusland
gaat`. Unlike Russian, a comma is not obligatorily used to set off the subordinate clause. Rules for
compound predicates in this construction:
- **Modal/aux + infinitive**: finite verb, then infinitive(s), in that order, both clause-final
  (`Ik weet dat hij morgen kan beginnen te werken`).
- **Perfect tense**: either order is acceptable — auxiliary-then-participle (more common) or
  participle-then-auxiliary (`dat hij is geweest` / `dat hij geweest is`).
- **Separable-verb prefix**: stays attached (not separated) when the verb is in present tense or
  imperfect within a subordinate clause (`dat zij weggaan`, not *`dat zij weg...gaan`* split); but in
  the perfect or with a modal+infinitive, either the prefix stays attached to its verb (moved as one
  unit to clause-final position) or the prefix alone can be fronted before the finite verb (`dat hij
  gisteren is weggegaan` / `weggegaan is` / `weg is gegaan`).
- When the **main clause follows** its subordinate clause, the main clause undergoes inversion
  (subject-verb order flips), since the whole subordinate clause counts as the single fronted
  constituent (`Toen hij dat zei, lachte ze`).
- `omdat` and `want` are near-synonyms ("because") but differ syntactically: `want` is a coordinating
  conjunction (normal word order follows), `omdat` is subordinating (verb-final). A causal clause with
  `omdat` can precede the main clause; one with `want` cannot.

### `als` vs. `toen` — a genuinely difficult usage distinction
Both gloss to Russian "когда," but: `toen` is used **only** for a single, completed past occurrence;
`als` covers every other case — habitual/repeated past action, and any present or future reference —
and a subordinate clause introduced by `toen`/`als` in its past sense never uses the perfect tense
(imperfect only). `als` also has an entirely separate, unrelated conditional sense ("if"), which can
combine with either imperfect or perfect depending on context. `toen` can also function as a plain
adverb meaning "then, at that time" (`Ik werk nu hier, maar toen werkte ik daar`) — distinct from its
conjunction use. When the antecedent is `dag/week/maand/jaar` with a definite article, `dat` (not
`toen`) is the relative-clause connector for "when" (`de dag dat jij thuis kwam`).

### `als` — two further senses ("as/like")
Beyond "if" and (never) "when [single past event]," `als` also glosses "as" in the occupational
sense — no article before the following noun (`Hij werkt als leraar`) — and "like" in the comparative
sense — indefinite article required (`Hij is zo gezond als een vis`).

### Indirect questions
An indirect (embedded) question follows the same verb-final subordinate-clause word order as a
`dat`-clause. If the original direct question had a question word, that word is retained as the
connector (`Hoe heet je? → Hij vraagt hoe je heet`). If the direct question had **no** question word
(a yes/no question), the connector `of` ("whether/if") is used instead (`Ben je thuis? → Hij vraagt
of je thuis bent`).

### `wanneer` as subordinating conjunction — synonym of `als`, never of `toen`
`wanneer` can function as the interrogative "when?" (and thus can introduce an indirect question, like
any question word) or as a subordinating conjunction synonymous with the conditional/habitual `als`
(never with `toen`'s single-past-event sense) — `wanneer` in this conjunction use is markedly more
literary/written-register than `als`.

### (Ook) al — colloquial concessive, distinct word-order behavior from `hoewel`
`(ook) al` is a colloquial-register synonym of `hoewel` ("although") with two distinctive syntax
quirks: (a) the `(ook) al`-clause itself undergoes **inversion** (subject-verb order flips, unlike an
ordinary subordinate clause); (b) when the `(ook) al`-clause is fronted before the main clause, the
main clause's finite verb goes to **second position**, same as any fronted constituent, rather than
undergoing the "whole subordinate clause counts as one constituent, verb stays clause-second"
rule that otherwise governs fronted subordinate clauses. Logical stress in this construction falls on
`nog` (`(Ook) al is ze nog zo lief...` ≈ "however sweet she may be...").

### Relative pronouns
`die` (referring to any `de`-word noun and to all plural nouns) and `dat` (referring only to a
`het`-word noun in the singular) function as relative pronouns exactly paralleling their demonstrative
use (032c), introducing a relative clause with the same verb-final word order as any subordinate
clause. When the relativized element would require a preposition and refers to a **person**, `wie`
(not `die`) is used, with the preposition placed directly before it (`de man over wie hij het had`).
When the relativized element requires a preposition and refers to a **non-person**, the compound form
`waar + preposition` is used instead of `die`/`dat` — written either solid (`waarvan, waarover`) or
split, with the preposition placed nearer the end of the clause (`het boek waar ik van hou` =
`het boek waarvan ik hou`). `Van wie` = "whose" (`Van wie is dit huis?`).

### Comparative and superlative degree
Regular suffixes: `-er` (comparative), `-st` (superlative; `-der`/`-dst` after a stem ending in `-r`).
Both take ordinary adjective agreement endings when used attributively (`een goedkopere fiets`).
Irregular set: `goed→beter→best`, `veel→meer→meest`, `weinig→minder→minst`, `graag→liever→liefst`.
`best` frequently loses true superlative force, meaning simply "quite/very" (`Nederlands is best
moeilijk`). Fixed idioms: `(goed, erg) zijn best doen` = "to do one's best/try hard," `als de beste` =
"as well as anyone/couldn't be better." `liever`/`liefst` combine with a full subordinate clause to
express a wished-for state (`Ik heb liever dat je thuis blijft`). Comparative constructions: `dan`
("than") and `als` ("as...as," in `net zo X als`/`even X als`) send their complement to the very end
of the clause, after the verb-final position. Special correlative constructions: `hoe langer hoe
beter` ("the longer, the better" — parallel structure with two `hoe`-clauses), `hoe meer je weet, hoe
minder je weet` (same pattern), `des te` as an alternative to the second `hoe`-clause (`Komt Piet ook?
Des te beter`). Superlative idioms: `op z'n hoogst/minst` ("at most/at least"), `de allerbeste/
allerlekkerste` (double-superlative intensifier `aller-` + superlative), `net zo... als` / `even...
als` (equality of degree — `even` used with a scalar/gradable property like size, `net zo` more
general), `te + adjective (+ om te + infinitive)` ("too X (to do Y)"), `veel te` ("far too").

### `iets moois` — agreement rule for adjectives after indefinite pronouns
When an adjective modifies an indefinite pronoun (`iets, niets, veel, weinig, allerlei`), it takes an
`-s` ending rather than ordinary attributive agreement (`iets moois`, `niets leuks`, `veel goeds`,
`weinig moois`, `allerlei moois`) — a distinct, separate inflectional pattern from the regular
attributive `-e` rule.

### `-(e)loos` — productive negative-adjective suffix
Derives an adjective from a noun, roughly parallel to Russian "без-...-ый" or English "-less"
(`werkeloos` = "unemployed," lit. "work-less"). Highly productive; the source drills an extensive set
(see vocabulary table) to establish the pattern rather than treating each coinage as a separate
lexical item to memorize individually.

### Substantivized adjectives (`de oude`, `de zieke`)
An adjective can function directly as a noun denoting a person characterized by that property; such
nouns are always grammatically `de`-gender, take `-e` in the singular and `-en` in the plural (`de
oude/de ouden`, `de zieke/de zieken`) — parallels the diminutive-noun and infinitive-noun
substantivization patterns already seen (032b, 032d).

### `zo'n` vs. `zulk(e)`
`zo'n` (032b) is restricted to a **singular countable** noun. For a plural noun, or a mass/collective
singular noun, `zulk(e)` is used instead: `zulke vrouwen` (plural), `zulke mooie bloemen` (plural),
but `zulk interessant werk` / `zulke lekkere boter` (mass/collective singular — takes `-e` only when a
preceding adjective is also present, i.e. `zulk` bare vs. `zulke` + adjective + noun).

### `pas` — "only just" / "just now," distinguished from `maar`/`alleen`
`pas`, like `maar` (032c), can mean "only" before a numeral, but with a subtly different implicature:
`Ik heb pas één appel gegeten` implies the speaker expects to eat more, while `Ik heb maar één appel
gegeten` carries no such implication. With a time expression, `pas` uniquely also means "not until"
(`We beginnen pas na dinsdag` = "we won't start until after Tuesday") or "just now/very recently" (`Ik
ben pas in Moskou geweest` = "I was just recently in Moscow").

### `er` — fourth use, with an indefinite `wat`/`wie` subject/object and no locative
Extends 032c/032f's `er`-uses with a fourth: **obligatory** whenever a `wat`- or `wie`-question has an
indefinite object (or no locative element at all) — `Wie schrijft er een brief?`, `Wat is er
gebeurd?`, `Wie is er geboren?`.

### Profession/nationality-noun article rule — refinement of 032's Les 1 note
Extends 032's original observation (predicate nouns naming a profession take zero article) with the
full rule: the article is dropped before a profession or (sometimes) nationality noun **specifically
in a predicate-nominal construction naming what the subject IS** (`Jan is (een) Nederlander`, `Jan is
matroos`); in every other syntactic role, the noun takes a normal article (`Er staat een matroos voor
het huis`, `De matroos is heel vriendelijk`). If the profession noun is used in a clearly figurative/
metaphorical sense rather than literally naming an occupation, the indefinite article is always used,
even in the predicate-nominal slot (`Hij is een boer` = "he's a country bumpkin," not literally "he is
a farmer").

### Extended numeral system
Four-digit numbers can be named two ways: hundred-based (e.g. `negentienhonderdvijftig` = 1950) or
thousand-based (`tweeduizend` = 2000, `vierentwintighonderd`/`tweeduizendvierhonderd` = 2400); the
hundred-based form is displacing the thousand-based one for the 1100–1999 range specifically. In
calendar years, `honderd` is often dropped entirely (`Ik ben geboren in negentientachtig` [1980]).
Ordinal numerals take `-de` (2nd–19th, except irregular `eerste` and `derde`) or `-ste` (20th
onward); always take a definite article except in rare cases. Fixed idioms: `voor het eerst` ("for
the first time," paired with `voor het laatst`), `ten eerste/tweede/derde` ("firstly/secondly/
thirdly"), fractions `één derde` (1/3), `twee vijfde` (2/5). Age is expressed with `op mijn/haar +
ordinal` (`op mijn twintigste` = "at age 20").

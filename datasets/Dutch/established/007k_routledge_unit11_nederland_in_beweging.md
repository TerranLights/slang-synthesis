## Grammar points

### 1. Prepositions with fixed verbs/expressions (brief — new to this unit)

Certain verbs and fixed expressions require a specific preposition that often can't be predicted from
the preposition's literal meaning (*zich laten leiden door* "to be led by," *een rol spelen bij* "to
play a part in," *het goede voorbeeld geven aan* "to set a good example for"). The unit's advice:
these have to be learned per-expression and looked up in a dictionary under the main verb/noun, not
derived from the preposition's core meaning.

### 2. Adverbs linking clauses, with/without inversion (extends Unit 9's cohesion material)

Certain linking adverbs trigger V2 inversion when placed sentence-initially (*daarom, daarna*), while
others (*immers, tenminste*) do not, regardless of whether they're preceded by a full stop or comma —
this is the same "structuring words" distinction already introduced in Unit 8 §5, now extended with
more connectives: *althans, daarmee, daarnaast, dan ook, terwijl* (a further batch, drilled via a
fill-in-the-gap text about a Dutch municipality) and *daarom, immers, althans, tenminste, dus, daarna*
(a second batch for sentence-combining).

### 3. *zou/zouden* for signalling unconfirmed information (new)

Beyond its conditional use (see Unit 7, referenced but not re-extracted here), *zou/zouden* marks that
a claim is **unconfirmed** — journalists use this constantly to hedge an unverified news claim (*Er
zouden vijf overlevenden zijn van de ramp*, "There are said to be five survivors of the disaster") and
writers use it to distance themselves from an opinion they're quoting/reporting without endorsing it
(the unit's own worked example: *Zo zouden wij dat dappere volkje zijn* signals the author's ironic
distance from the claim that the Dutch are "that brave little nation").

### 4. Ellipsis: omitting a repeated subject and verb (new)

When two coordinated clauses would repeat the same subject and verb, the repeated material can be
dropped, provided the omission doesn't obscure meaning and the retained/omitted elements agree in
number, tense, and clause position: *Wij hebben eerst de Spanjaarden een showdown gegeven en
vervolgens de Fransen uit ons landje geknikkerd* (full form would repeat *wij hebben* before the
second verb).

### 5. Passive voice: one further look (extends Unit 9 — pointer, not re-derivation)

The unit revisits the passive (form/use fully covered in Unit 9 §4) specifically to introduce
recognizing tense in already-formed passive clauses and to note explicitly that the **perfect tense**
of the passive uses *zijn* + past participle + *geworden* (implicit in the worked examples, e.g.
*Zijn autobiografie werd pas na zijn dood gepubliceerd* = imperfect passive; *Relatief veel joden zijn
overgedragen* = present-perfect passive). Students are pointed back to Unit 9's passive section and/or
an external grammar reference rather than being re-taught the form from scratch.

### 6. Subject–verb agreement, including collective nouns (brief — mostly revision)

Basic number agreement (*hij kijkt* singular vs. *wij kijken* plural) is revised, with one genuinely
noteworthy point: Dutch collective nouns like *de regering* ("the government") **always** take a
singular verb even though they denote a group of people — unlike English, which allows either (*De
regering heeft nieuwe maatregelen voorgesteld*, never *\*hebben*).

### 7. Relative pronouns: *die/dat*, and *wie/wat* (extends Unit 6 — pointer plus new material)

*Die* and *dat* as basic relative pronouns were already covered in Unit 6 (not re-derived here). New
to this unit: *wie* and *wat* can also function as relative pronouns — *wat* when the antecedent is
*alles, iets, niets*, a superlative, or an entire preceding clause (*Alles wat je zegt, is helemaal
waar*; *Wat ik heb gezien, is echt niet te geloven!*); *wie* when the antecedent is an unspecified
person, often in a generic/proverbial statement (*Wie zoiets doet is gewoon geschift*, "Whoever does
that is just crazy"). When a relative pronoun combines with a preposition: for **things**, use *waar*
+ preposition (*Het project waaraan ik werk*); for **people**, use preposition + *wie* (*De
contactpersoon met wie ik heb gesproken*) — mirroring the same *waar*+preposition pattern already
seen for question words in Unit 10 §1.

### 8. Word order among multiple verbs in clause-final position (new, systematic)

With exactly two verbs at clause end, either order is acceptable (*ze al tien jaar getrouwd is* / *is
getrouwd*). With more than two: a past participle can go first or last; among the remaining verbs the
finite verb comes first and the infinitive last; if there's more than one infinitive, the modal's
infinitive comes first of those. Worked example: *Ik denk dat ik je voor het examen een 8 zou moeten
kunnen halen* (finite *zou*, then modal-infinitive *moeten*, then *kunnen*, then main-verb infinitive
*halen*); the past participle variant allows *zal moeten hebben geleerd* or *zal moeten geleerd
hebben*.

### 9. *schijnen* / *lijken* / *blijken* (new)

Three near-synonyms for "seem/appear/turn out," differentiated by the speaker's degree of certainty.
*Schijnen* = reporting something only heard secondhand, no personal verification (*Wouter schijnt
ziek te zijn*, "I've heard Wouter's ill"). *Lijken* = a firsthand impression, but still not certain
(*Wouter lijkt wel ziek*, "Wouter looks ill"). *Blijken* = confirmed by actual proof (*Wouter blijkt
griep te hebben*, "It turns out Wouter has the flu").

### 10. Comma conventions (new)

The general rule: use a comma only where it aids the reading process (marking a natural pause), most
commonly between verbs belonging to different clauses — e.g. between a fronted subclause and the
following main clause (*Als de vergadering niet doorgaat, ga ik vanmiddag naar huis*). Relative
clauses generally end with a comma (being a kind of subclause). Critically, **non-restrictive**
relative clauses (adding non-essential extra information) are set off with a comma on *both* sides,
while **restrictive** relative clauses (providing information essential to identifying the referent)
never start with a comma: *Nederlandse vrouwen, die niet vaak fulltime werken, gaan bijna altijd tot
hun achttiende naar school* (non-restrictive: "Dutch women, who don't often work full-time, ...")
vs. *Nederlandse vrouwen die niet fulltime werken, hebben vaak jonge kinderen* (restrictive: "Dutch
women who don't work full-time often have young children" — the clause picks out *which* women).

### 11. Exclamations (new)

Dutch has a dedicated small inventory of interjections with fairly specific emotional/pragmatic
values, distinct from each other in ways that don't map one-to-one onto English. The unit's list (see
vocabulary table below) covers surprise (*jeetje*, *goh*), exasperation/disapproval/disbelief (*nou!*,
*nou ja (zeg)!*), calming/surprise (*nou nou!*), dismissiveness (*nou en?*), sadness (*ach!*),
irritation (*ach wat!*), pain/bad luck/agreement (*hè*, context-dependent), and seeking agreement
(*hè?*, tag-question-like). *Poeh!* (used by Fortuyn in the debate text below) signals hesitation/
difficulty formulating a response.

### 12. Common written abbreviations (new — reference list, not a "rule" per se)

The unit supplies a reference list of everyday Dutch abbreviations (captured in the vocabulary table
below): *t/m, blz., p., z.o.z., d.w.z., i.v.m., m.b.t., m.a.w., a.u.b., s.v.p., jl., a.s.*

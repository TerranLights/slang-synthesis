## Grammar points

### Possessive pronouns (Притяжательные местоимения)
Full paradigm given: `mijn(m'n)/jouw(je)/uw/zijn(z'n)/haar('r,d'r)` singular; `onze,ons/jullie/uw/hun`
plural. Same full/reduced-form register rule as personal pronouns (032, Les 2). `ons` is used only
before a `het`-word singular noun; otherwise `onze`. Dutch has no reflexive possessive equivalent to
Russian "свой" — possessives always distinguish person/number explicitly, unlike Russian, which can
use "свой" ambiguously. `jouw` (possessive) and `jou` (object pronoun) are homophones. No article
co-occurs with a possessive before its noun.

### Zero-article abstract/mass nouns (extends Les 1–2's mass-noun note)
`geschiedenis`, `grammatica`, and `Russisch` take zero article as abstracts (`Ik studeer
geschiedenis`), but the article reappears once the noun is modified by a following PP specifying
which instance is meant (`de geschiedenis van België`, `de grammatica van Van den Toorn`, `het
Russisch van nu`, `het papier van Klaas`) — i.e. modification restores definiteness. Fixed idiom
`les geven` = "to teach/give lessons." Article is typically dropped with `oma` in address/reference
but reappears with a modifying PP (`de oma van Liza`). `op school` (locative) vs. `naar school`
(directional) is a fixed pair, paralleling the `in`/`naar` distinction from Les 1.

### Adjective inflection: `goed ↔ goede` (-e ending rule)
An attributive adjective takes `-e` in every case **except** when modifying a `het`-word noun in the
singular with an indefinite/zero/negative article (`een lekker bier`, `geen lekker bier` — no -e; but
`lekkere wijn`, `de lekkere wijn`, `mijn lekkere bier` — with -e). A predicative adjective (in a
copular "X is Y" sentence) never takes -e regardless of noun gender (`De wijn is lekker`, `Bier is
lekker`). Adjectives already ending in a vowel don't add -e, except those ending in `-i`
(`mooi→mooie`). Spelling rules apply when adding -e: `rood→rode`, `dik→dikke`, `lief→lieve`,
`oud→ouwe`(informal)/`oude`. `goede` is often pronounced colloquially as `goeie`. An attributive
adjective sits between article and noun; an adverb can intervene between article+adjective and the
noun (`een tamelijk goede fiets`). After the numeral `één`, a following adjective also takes no
ending before a `het`-word noun.

### Priinfinitive particle `te`
Some (not all) verbs that take an infinitive complement require the particle `te` immediately before
that infinitive, with `te + infinitive` placed clause-finally: `beginnen te`, `proberen te`. `beginnen
met` (+ noun, or + gerund-like verb) can substitute for `beginnen te` + verb. `drinken op` = "to drink
to/toast." `Het/Dat klopt` = "that's exactly right." `lijken` is nearly always used with impersonal
`het` as subject; `lukken` is used only with 3rd-sg (usually impersonal `het`) subject, never with a
personal subject in the sense "succeed at." `nemen` is noted as less colloquial than `pakken` for
"catch (the tram)."

### Modal verbs: `kunnen/willen/moeten/mogen/hoeven`
Modal verbs conjugate irregularly: no `-t` ending in 3sg (unlike regular verbs), and `kunnen` shows a
stem-vowel change. `mogen` takes no ending change across any singular person. All modals combine with
a bare infinitive (no `te`), placed clause-finally. In colloquial speech, `jij/u kunt` and `jij/u
wilt` are often leveled to `jij/u kan`/`jij/u wil`. `kunnen` = ability/permission-neutral "can";
`mogen` = explicit permission "may/allowed to" — contrasted directly (`Ik kan dit niet lezen` vs. `Ik
mag dit niet lezen`). A motion verb following a modal is frequently omitted when direction is already
specified (`We kunnen naar huis` = "we can [go] home"). After `mogen`, `nemen`/`pakken` is likewise
often omitted (`Mag ik een pen?` = "may I [take] a pen?"). `hoeven` is a negative-polarity modal:
requires `niet`/`geen` (or another negator) and `te + infinitive`; it is the negation of `moeten`
(`Wij moeten werken` ↔ `Wij hoeven niet te werken`), and is explicitly distinguished from `moeten
niet`, which is rare and means "it's advisable not to," a different meaning entirely.

### Diminutive suffix — full conditioning-rule set (extends `003_chapter22_diminutives.md`)
Five phonologically-conditioned allomorphs: `-je, -tje, -etje, -pje, -kje`.
- **I. `-tje`**: after a noun ending in `n, l, r, w`, or a vowel (except case II) — `zoontje,
  taaltje, biertje, vrouwtje`.
- **II. `-etje`**: after a stressed short vowel + `l, m, n, r,` or `ng` — `mannetje, trammetje`.
- **III. `-pje`**: after a noun ending in `m` (except case II) — `filmpje`.
- **IV. `-kje`**: after an unstressed `-ing` that follows a stressed syllable (otherwise `-etje`
  applies instead) — `verrassinkje`.
- **V. `-je`** (bare): elsewhere (default case).
A noun with any of these five diminutive forms is always grammatically `het`-gender regardless of the
base noun's own gender (`de vriend` → `het vriendje`). Some nouns have two acceptable diminutive
forms (`bloempje`/`bloemetje`). Orthographic doubling rules apply (`collega→collegaatje,
café→cafeetje`). If a base noun ends in `-st, -cht,` or `-ft(-fd)`, the `t` of `-je` is not
pronounced (`kastje` pronounced "kasje", `grachtje` pronounced "grachje"). Exception: `Jan → Jantje`
(pronounced with the t). The diminutive can attach to adjectives/adverbs too, in which case an
additional `-s` is added (`eventjes`). Diminutive can be endearing (`vriendje`) or dismissive/
belittling (`taaltje`) depending on context — register/connotation explicitly flagged by the source.
Fixed idioms: `in mijn eentje` = "alone/by oneself," `tot strakjes` = `tot straks`.

### Compound nouns (сложные существительные)
A Dutch compound noun is (usually) two elements, the last of which must be a noun; the first element
can be: (1) a bare singular noun (`school + vriend = schoolvriend`), (2) a singular noun + linking
`-s` or `-e` (`universiteit + winkel = universiteitswinkel`; `zon + bloem = zonnebloem`), (3) a plural
noun (`woorden + boek = woordenboek`), or (4) another part of speech (`voor + naam = voornaam`,
`leer + boek = leerboek`). The compound's grammatical gender matches its final/head element's gender
(`het boek` + `de winkel` = `de boekwinkel`; `de taal` + `het gevoel` = `het taalgevoel`). Stress
falls on the first element.

### Imperative mood
The imperative form equals the bare verb stem and always occupies clause-initial position. One or
more discourse particles commonly follow it — the source lists an extensive combinatorial set (`maar,
eens, 's, even(tjes)`, and their pairwise/triple combinations) all attaching after the imperative verb
and functioning to soften or add nuance rather than change literal meaning; these particles are placed
after a direct/indirect object or adverbial if one is present (`Pak het boek nu maar`, `Geef het me
straks maar even`). The polite imperative uses the `-t`-ending verb form with `u` following
(`Kijkt u maar!`) — noted as comparatively rare. For emphasis, `jij/jullie` can be added to an
imperative (`Vraag jij dat maar`, `Doen jullie dat maar` — "you'd better ask/do that yourselves").
The bare infinitive can substitute for the imperative, especially in public/prohibition signage
(`Hier niet roken`, `Niet doen!`). `zijn`'s imperative is suppletive: `wees` (informal), `weest u`
(polite).

### `men` — indefinite/impersonal pronoun
`men` (not to be confused with the noun `de man`) is a 3rd-sg. indefinite pronoun equivalent to
generic "they/one/people say," used only as a grammatical subject. In colloquial speech, `men` is
frequently replaced by reduced `je` or `ze` (plural sense). Its associated possessive is always
`zijn`, even when referring generically (`Naar het café gaat men met zijn vrienden, niet met zijn
oma`).

### `allemaal` vs. `iedereen`
Both translate as "all/everyone," but differ structurally: `iedereen` requires singular verb agreement
and functions as an independent subject/object; `allemaal` attaches to (modifies) a plural noun or
pronoun subject/object, or can attach to a singular noun/pronoun to mean "all of it/wholly." In casual
incomplete sentences `allemaal` need not attach to any explicit noun/pronoun (`Allemaal samen?` =
"All together?"). Word-order rule: `allemaal` is placed after the predicate, after the object, after
an adverbial (except qualitative adverbs), and after `niet`.

### `zo` / `zo'n` — idiom cluster
`zo'n` (pronounced "zoon") = `zo` + `een`, meaning "such a(n)" (`Het is zo'n duur woordenboek` = "It's
such an expensive dictionary"). `zo` itself carries an extensive idiom inventory the source lists as
a set-phrase block rather than compositional grammar: `Goed zo!`/`Mooi zo!` (well done/excellent),
`Tot zo!` (see you shortly), `Ik kom zo` (I'm coming right away), `We zijn er zo` (we're almost
there), `Zo is hij (niet)` (that's (not) like him), `Dat is (niet) zo` (that's (not) so), `Niet zo`
(not really/not much), `Dat is toch zo?` (isn't that so?), `Zo gaat dat` (that's how it goes), `O,
gaat dat zo?` (oh, is that how it is), `Zo gaat/lukt het niet` (that won't work), `Zo kan-ie wel weer`
(that'll do/enough of that), `Dat lijkt maar zo` (that only seems that way), `Hoe zo?` (how so/why),
`Ik vind het maar zo-zo` (I find it so-so/lukewarm), `Zo zo!` (well well!). None of these forms take
the adjectival `-e` inflection because they're functioning as invariant particles/adverbs, not
attributive adjectives — flagged explicitly as an exercise item.

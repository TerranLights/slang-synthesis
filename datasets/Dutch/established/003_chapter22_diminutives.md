### 22.3 The diminutive as a "personalizing suffix" — attitude/stance marking (p. 185)

The source's central pragmatic claim: explaining the diminutive as being "about smallness" misses
the point — smallness proper is expressed by the adjective *klein*. The diminutive is better
understood as a **personalizing suffix**: it signals the speaker's subjective attitude toward the
referent (fondness, irony, dismissiveness, appreciation), a range the source says can only be
learned through experience. This is the single most slang-relevant grammar point in the chapter —
it's a live, productive device for encoding affective stance rather than a neutral size marker, and
the source presents it through paired contrastive sentences (neutral form vs. diminutive form of the
same noun), tagged `colloquial` below since each diminutive form carries an informal/affective
reading absent from its plain counterpart in the given context.

## Grammar points

### 1. What a diminutive is, and its default form (22.1, p. 181)

A diminutive is a noun derived from another word — usually a noun, but the chapter's own examples
also derive diminutives from adjectives (*dubbel* → *dubbeltje*, *klein* → *kleintje*), an adverb/
particle (*toe* → *toetje*), and even a full idiomatic phrase (*onder ons* → *onderonsje*) — via a
special ending marking smallness, endearment, or other meaning. The underlying suffix is always
**-je**; diminutive nouns are always grammatically neuter (*het*), and their plural is always formed
by adding **-s**, regardless of the base noun's own gender or plural pattern. When *s* and *j* end up
adjacent (as in *huisje*), they merge into a single sh-like sound. In most cases the suffix doesn't
attach as bare *-je* — its surface form (allomorph) is conditioned by the phonological shape of the
stem it attaches to, per the rules below.

### 2. The `-je`/`-tje`/`-etje`/`-pje` allomorph selection (22.1.1–22.1.4, pp. 181–182)

Four surface allomorphs of the diminutive suffix, each triggered by a distinct phonological
environment at the end of the stem:

- **`-je`** (default): attaches unchanged when none of the more specific conditions below apply
  (e.g. *huis*, *boek*, *zak*, *scheermes*, *brood*).
- **`-tje`**: triggered when the stem itself is an open long vowel or diphthong (nothing follows the
  vowel, e.g. *ei*, *koe*, *toe*) **or** when the stem ends in one of the sonorant/glide consonants
  **l, n, r, w** (e.g. *mouw, boel, schoen, deur, taal, wijn, klein*). Also triggered, independently,
  by stems ending in unstressed **-el, -en, -er** (e.g. *tafel, keuken, kamer* — with the irregular
  exception *jongen → jongetje*, where the expected *-entje* instead drops its own *-en-* syllable).
- **`-etje`**: triggered when the stem has a **short** vowel followed by final **l, r, m, n, ng**,
  and "occasionally other consonants" (the source's own examples extend this to final -g and -p:
  *brug → bruggetje*, *kip → kippetje*). Spelling doubles the final consonant (except digraph -ng,
  which can't be doubled) to keep the short-vowel reading unambiguous before the suffix.
- **`-pje`**: triggered when the stem ends in **-m** preceded by a long vowel/diphthong, or ends in
  the clusters **-lm, -rm**, or unstressed **-em** (*boom, duim, arm, film, bezem, museum*). The
  source flags one real split in actual usage here: the rule predicts *bloempje* for *bloem*
  ("flower"), but notes "most Dutch speakers use *bloemetje* instead" — i.e. a competing analysis
  that treats *bloem*'s vowel as short and applies the -etje pattern instead.

### 3. Two further stem-shape adjustments layered onto the above (p. 183)

- **Vowel-length alternation tracks the plural, not the singular.** Words whose singular has a
  short vowel but whose plural lengthens it (*glas* → *glazen*, *schip* → *schepen*) carry the
  **plural's** long vowel into the diminutive too (*glaasje*, *scheepje*), not the singular's short
  one. The one listed exception, *weg* → *wegen*, allows two competing diminutive forms
  (*wegje*/*weggetje*) rather than settling on one.
- **Stems ending in -cht, -ft, -st keep the -je spelling but drop the /t/ in pronunciation** — a
  purely phonetic simplification, not a spelling change (*lucht* → spelled *luchtje*, pronounced
  "luchje"; likewise *zacht, kaft, schrift, kist, lijst*).
- **Stems ending in stressed -o, -a, -u double that vowel** before adding *-tje*, to keep it reading
  long (*auto → autootje, paraplu → parapluutje, mama → mamaatje*).

### 4. Diminutives as adverbs (22.2, p. 184)

Adding the diminutive suffix plus the adverbial ending **-s** converts a number of adjectives into
adverbs, "often with reference to human actions or emotions." The source states there are "a couple
dozen" of these "in common colloquial use" — an explicit register label, distinguishing this
construction from the register-neutral diminutive-noun formations in 22.1. Example: *droog* "dry" →
*droogjes* "drily," as in *Hij merkte droogjes op dat de laatste trein inmiddels vertrokken was.*
("He remarked drily that the last train had already left.")

### 5. The diminutive as a "personalizing suffix," not a smallness marker (22.3, p. 185)

The chapter's central interpretive point: describing Dutch diminutive use as reflecting "some
fondness for the smallness of things" (a claim the source says Dutch speakers themselves sometimes
make) "would be to miss the main point." Smallness *per se* is already fully expressed by the plain
adjective **klein**. The diminutive suffix is better understood as a **personalizing suffix** — it
marks that the speaker is taking a personal/subjective attitude toward the referent, and the source
states explicitly that "the range of possible attitudes is wide and can be learned only with some
experience in listening and reading." It illustrates this with contrastive sentence pairs built on
the same noun (*zon*/*zonnetje*, *taal*/*taaltje*, *koe*/*koetje*, *wijn*/*wijntje*) where the plain
form is register-neutral and the diminutive form carries appreciation, mild mockery, or affection
depending on context. This is the chapter's most direct point of contact with slang/colloquial
register: it's a live, synchronically productive way of encoding stance on top of an otherwise
ordinary noun, rather than a fixed lexical alternative.

### 6. Semantic drift and full lexicalization (22.3.1, p. 186)

Some diminutive forms have drifted to name a distinct concept rather than "a small X" (*lepel*
"spoon" → *lepeltje* "teaspoon"; *scheermes* "razor" → *scheermesje* "razor blade"; *dubbel*
"double" → *dubbeltje*, the historical 10-cent coin; *brood* "bread" → *broodje* "roll"; *klein*
"small" → *kleintje* "a little one," i.e. a young child/animal; *toe* "in addition" → *toetje*
"dessert"; *onder ons* "between us" → *onderonsje* "tête-à-tête"). A further step along the same
cline: some words exist **only** in diminutive form today, with no independently current
non-diminutive base carrying the same sense — the source's own examples are *poffertjes* (a
traditional Dutch fried-pastry snack), *toetje* ("dessert" — repeating the entry above), *beetje*
("a little bit"), and *winterkoninkje* ("wren"). These represent the end state of the productive
process described in points 1–5: what starts as a live, attitude-marking derivational suffix can
fully lexicalize into an opaque citation form.

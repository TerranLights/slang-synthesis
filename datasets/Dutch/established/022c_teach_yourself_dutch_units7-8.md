## Grammar points

### Willen: full paradigm and modal-verb + infinitive placement (Unit 7)

Full present-tense paradigm of `willen` given (singular `ik wil`, `jij wilt (wil)`, `u wilt`,
`hij/zij/het wil`; plural `wij willen`, `jullie willen`, `u wilt (wil)`, `zij willen`). Reinforces
the modal + bare infinitive-at-end pattern from Unit 4 (p. 87).

### Modal-like verbs that require te + infinitive (Unit 7)

A distinct group of verbs — `hoeven` (have to, in negative constructions), `proberen` (try),
`vergeten` (forget), `staan`/`zitten` (used idiomatically like "to be" mid-action), `beginnen`
(start), `beloven` (promise), and others (`durven` "dare," `weigeren` "refuse") — combine with an
infinitive but require the linking word `te` immediately before it, unlike true modal verbs which
take a bare infinitive (`Ik probeer te komen`, `Wij vergeten boodschappen te doen`). `Moeten` is
normally used for obligation; `hoeven` (always with `niet` + `te`) is its negative counterpart for
"don't have to" (`U hoeft niet te komen`) (p. 87-89).

### Telling time: the 12-hour clock and "half X" (Unit 7)

Everyday speech uses the 12-hour clock (24-hour reserved for timetables/schedules). The top half
of the hour (quarter-to through the hour) parallels English (`kwart voor zes`, `tien voor zes`).
The bottom half is structurally different: Dutch looks *forward* to the next hour rather than back
to the last one, so 6:30 is `half zeven` ("half [toward] seven," not "half past six"), and minutes
around the half-hour are counted relative to that half-hour marker (`tien voor half zeven` = 6:20,
lit. "ten to half-seven"; `vijf over half zeven` = 6:35, lit. "five past half-seven") (p. 83-84).
Time-of-day disambiguators (`'s nachts`, `'s morgens/'s ochtends`, `'s middags`, `'s avonds`) are
added when needed.

### Prepositions vary by context/location noun (Unit 7)

The book explicitly flags that different fixed prepositions pair with different location nouns
with no single general rule — `bij een krantenkiosk`, `in een fietsenstalling`, `bij een
bushalte`, `in de restauratie`, `op het wisselkantoor` — and that these must simply be learned per
noun (p. 91). `Restauratie` is specifically noted as the word for a station buffet, distinct from
`restaurant` used everywhere else.

### The perfect tense: hebben/zijn + past participle (Unit 8)

Dutch's standard past-tense construction (used far more often than the simple past, which is
introduced in a later unit) is the present perfect: a conjugated form of `hebben` or `zijn` plus
an invariant past participle placed at the very end of the clause (`Wij hebben een leuke vakantie
gehad`). Full paradigm of `hebben` as auxiliary given (p. 97-98).

### Forming the regular past participle (Unit 8)

Three-step rule: (1) find the stem (infinitive minus `-en`); (2) prefix `ge-`; (3) suffix `-t` if
the stem's final consonant is one of the "soft ketchup" voiceless consonants (`t, k, f, s, ch, p`
— referencing the pronunciation chapter's mnemonic), otherwise `-d` (`wandelen → gewandeld`,
`werken → gewerkt`). Verbs whose stem already ends in `d` or `t` add no extra letter. `Reizen` and
`leven` are noted as devoicing-in-writing cases (`z→s`, `v→f` in the stem) that still follow the
regular rule despite that spelling change (p. 98-99).

### No ge- with certain unstressed prefixes (Unit 8)

Verbs beginning with the unstressed prefixes `be-`, `her-`, `ont-`, `ver-`, `er-` form their past
participle *without* adding `ge-` (`bestellen → besteld`, not `gebesteld`). `Geloven` is
specifically flagged as an exception that keeps `ge-` despite superficially looking like it should
follow this rule (p. 99).

### Irregular past participles (Unit 8)

A closed set of high-frequency irregular verbs must be memorized individually rather than derived
by rule; the book gives two sample lists (hebben/zijn split shown per verb) — see the Vocabulary
table above for the full set transcribed (`zijn→geweest`, `hebben→gehad`, `kopen→gekocht`,
`brengen→gebracht`, `beginnen→begonnen`, `komen→gekomen`, `doen→gedaan`, `blijven→gebleven`, and a
further "verbs of motion" list) (p. 100-101).

### Auxiliary choice: hebben vs. zijn (Unit 8)

Most verbs (regular and irregular alike) take `hebben`. A smaller set of irregular verbs takes
`zijn` instead, and this set is semantically coherent: verbs indicating a **change of place or
state** (`beginnen`, `komen`, `verhuizen`, `vallen`, `worden`, `trouwen`, `scheiden`, `sterven`,
`geboren worden`). Verbs describing a *state* with no change (`blijven` "stay," `zijn` "be") also
take `zijn` despite not indicating change — flagged by the source as a caveat to the "change"
generalization rather than a clean exception-free rule (p. 100-101).

### Verbs of motion: hebben/zijn split on direction vs. manner (Unit 8)

A further group of motion verbs (`rijden`, `wandelen`, `lopen`, `vliegen`, `rennen`) can take
*either* auxiliary depending on meaning: `zijn` when the sentence specifies a direction or
destination (`Wij zijn naar Amsterdam gereden` "We drove to Amsterdam"), `hebben` when there is no
destination and the emphasis is on the movement/activity itself (`We hebben daar wat rondgereden`
"We drove around there [a bit]") (p. 101-102).

### The weather (Unit 8)

Core weather-verb set: `het regent/sneeuwt/vriest/waait`, `de zon schijnt`, `het onweert` — all
impersonal `het`-subject constructions. Temperature is expressed with `graden` (degrees);
approximation with `ongeveer`. Newspaper/forecast register introduces a distinct, denser
vocabulary set (`bewolking`, `opklaringen`, `toenemende ... wind`, `regenbui`) explicitly flagged
by the source as more formal/technical than conversational weather-talk (p. 106-107).

### Day + time-of-day compounding (Unit 8)

Day names compound directly with time-of-day words to form single words: `zondagmorgen`,
`donderdagmiddag`, `vrijdagavond`, `woensdagnacht` — a productive pattern, not a closed list (p.
107).

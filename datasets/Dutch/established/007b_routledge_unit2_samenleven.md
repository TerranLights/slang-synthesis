Routledge Intensive Dutch Course.pdf`, 434 pp., clean text layer). Author names and edition/year
could not be verified from the extracted text (no legible title/copyright page in the text layer) —
left unspecified rather than guessed. Unit 2 "Samenleven" ("Living together"), printed pp. 33–64.

**Coverage note:** every grammar point listed in the unit's own Topics/Grammar preview and every
distinct vocabulary item from this unit's "Key vocabulary" boxes and other embedded vocabulary lists
is captured below. Skipped as low-information-density per the coverage rule: the many "Oefening"
(exercise) drill instructions themselves (fill-in-the-blank prompts, role-play setups, interview
prompts) — these reuse vocabulary already captured from the preceding explanatory material and
introduce no new lexical or grammatical content of their own. The full "Tellen boven de 100"
(numbers above 100) and ordinal-number tables are paraphrased as a formation rule with a few
illustrative examples rather than fully reproduced (a systematic numeral series is mechanical, not
lexical, content). This source has a real, clean text layer (`pdftotext -layout` produced legible,
if lightly OCR-noisy in places — some stray letter-doubling/dropping visible in dialogue transcripts
that is clearly an artifact of the underlying PDF's font encoding, not genuine text — no vision-
reading was needed).

**Non-redundant-supplement check against `established/002_chapter16_colloquial_speech.md`:** this
unit's own Grammar list names "Modal particles: even, wel, nu, maar, echt, weer, hoor, toch" — five
of these (even, wel, nu, maar, toch) plus the secondary adverb *nu* were already extracted in depth
from a different book (*Dutch: An Essential Grammar*, Ch. 16) in `002`. Per the non-redundant-
supplement pattern: **skipped** — re-deriving the core meaning of *even, wel, maar, toch* (already
fully tabulated in `002`). **Kept as new:** (1) *echt* and *weer* — neither appears in `002`'s
particle/adverb list at all; (2) *hoor* — `002` flagged it as under-treated (only one example, no
dedicated subsection); this unit gives two further independent examples, extracted below; (3) *nog*
as a modal particle — not mentioned in `002` at all, and this unit gives it a dedicated exercise with
six distinct senses, extracted in full; (4) this book's own independent example sentences for *even*
in the specific "asking for something to be passed" sense (a distinct nuance from `002`'s "brief
duration" sense) and for *wel*'s three senses via its own exercise, since a different author's
independent examples are non-redundant per spec even where the underlying particle is already
documented. All other Unit 2 grammar points (negation, questions/inversion, modal verbs, separable
verbs, adverbs of degree, *zou*) are new territory not covered in `001`–`003` and are extracted in
full.

## Grammar points

### Existential negation with *op* + *geen ... meer* (p. 37)
To say something has "run out," Dutch uses *op* (predicate adjective/particle meaning "finished/used
up") combined with the existential *er is/zijn*: *"Is er nog kaas? Nee, er is geen kaas meer/De kaas
is op."* Both phrasings are given as equally natural alternatives.

### Numbers over 100 and ordinal numbers (pp. 39–40)
Numbers above 100 are formed by straightforward concatenation of the hundred/thousand/million bases
(*honderd, duizend, miljoen, miljard*) with the smaller number attached directly, without a
conjunction: *honderdeen* (101), *honderdtwee* (102), *tweehonderd* (200), *tienduizend* (10,000).
Ordinal numbers are formed by adding *-de* to the cardinal for most numbers (*tweede, derde, vierde*)
but *-ste* once the cardinal itself ends in *-tig, -honderd, -duizend*, etc., and irregularly for the
first few (*eerste, derde, achtste*): e.g. *elfde* (11th) but *twintigste* (20th), *honderdste*
(100th). Ordinals combine with lower digits the same way cardinals do: *honderd(en)eerste* (101st).

### Negation: *niet* vs. *geen* (p. 41)
*Niet* negates in general; *geen* is used specifically when negating an indefinite noun (one with no
article, or with *een*) — i.e. "not any/not a/no." Contrast *"Ik heb geen geld"* (no money, indefinite)
with *"Ik drink die wijn niet"* (that specific wine, definite — takes *niet*).

### Modal particles: *echt*, *weer*, *hoor*, *nog* — new beyond `002` (pp. 43–49, 59–60)
See the Vocabulary table above for full glosses/examples of *echt, weer,* the two new *hoor* senses,
and *nog*'s six distinct senses. The unit frames the whole particle system explicitly as something to
recognize passively before attempting active correct use ("At this stage you don't have to be able
to use these words correctly, it simply helps if you are aware of them") — a pedagogical framing not
present in `002`'s presentation of the same phenomenon. *Nog* is additionally noted to combine with
other particles in a fixed order: *nu/nou nog even*.

### Yes/no questions: inversion (pp. 48–49)
Formed by reversing subject and finite verb (*"Mag ik de melk even?"*). One additional morphological
rule not covered elsewhere in `001`–`003`: when inverting specifically with the subject *je/jij*, the
verb drops its final *-t* — *"Jij komt morgen"* → *"Kom jij morgen?"* (not *Komt jij*). This *-t*-drop
is unique to the *je/jij* inversion context.

### Question-word questions (pp. 49–50)
Open-ended questions (*wie, wat, waar, hoe, wanneer, welke, waarom*) also trigger inversion: the
question word is followed directly by the finite verb, then the subject — *"Wie heeft gewonnen?",
"Waar ligt Amsterdam?"* *Welke* requires a following common noun (de-word); its neuter-agreement
counterpart is *welk* (used with het-words, e.g. *"Welk boek lees je?"*).

### Inversion in statements (topic-fronting) (p. 52)
Inversion is not exclusive to questions: whenever a Dutch statement begins with any element other
than the subject, the finite verb and subject invert to keep the finite verb in second position —
*"Dan moet je maar vroeger opstaan"* (fronted *dan* forces inversion). This generalizes the "verb
second" (V2) constraint already implicit in the yes/no-question material.

### Modal verbs (pp. 52–53)
*Moeten* (must/have to), *mogen* (may/be allowed to), *willen* (want to), *kunnen* (can/be able to),
*zullen* (will, future/volition) combine with another verb's bare infinitive placed at the sentence
end, but can also stand alone without an infinitive (*"Dat kan"* = "That's possible"). Full present-
tense conjugation table given by the source:

| | zullen | willen | kunnen | mogen | moeten |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ik | zal | wil | kan | mag | moet |
| je/jij/u | zal/zult | wil/wilt | kan/kunt | mag | moet |
| hij/zij/het | zal | wil | kan | mag | moet |
| wij | zullen | willen | kunnen | mogen | moeten |
| jullie | zullen | willen | kunnen | mogen | moeten |
| zij | zullen | willen | kunnen | mogen | moeten |

Source's own register note: the alternate *je/jij/u*-forms *zult, wilt, kunt* are explicitly marked
"more formal and used much less often" than the plain forms *zal, wil, kan* — a real register split
within a single conjugation cell. *U* (the polite/formal address pronoun) takes the same verb forms
as *je/jij*.

### Separable verbs (pp. 55–56)
Separable verbs are two-part verbs (prefix + base verb) that split apart when the separable verb
itself is the finite verb in the present tense — the prefix moves to clause-final position:
*"Ik maak morgen het huis schoon"* (from *schoonmaken*). They do **not** split when used as a bare
infinitive governed by a modal verb: *"Ik moet morgen het huis schoonmaken."* The source explicitly
flags that not every verb that *looks* like a two-part compound is actually separable — *stofzuigen*
("to vacuum") is given as a specific counter-example that never splits. The source also notes further
splitting rules are deferred to Units 4, 8, and 9 (outside this chunk's scope).

### *Zou* + infinitive: polite requests and reported/uncertain plans (pp. 59–60)
*Zou* (conditional of *zullen*) combined with *willen*/*kunnen* + a further infinitive forms a polite
request: *"Zou jij dat even willen/kunnen doen?"* ("Would you mind doing that?"). *Zou* alone + an
infinitive also reports a previously-stated plan or intention, often one now in doubt or unfulfilled:
*"Elsa zou 10 flessen kopen"* ("Elsa was going to buy 10 bottles"). Word-order rule when a modal verb
itself appears as an infinitive stacked with another infinitive: the modal verb always precedes the
other infinitive — *"We zullen onze vakantie vroeg moeten boeken"* (not the reverse order).

### Adverbs of degree: toning down vs. emphasizing (pp. 63–64)
Dutch uses degree adverbs before an adjective/verb to soften or intensify a claim. Toning-down set:
*redelijk, behoorlijk, vrij, enigszins*. Emphasizing set: *ontzettend, geweldig, echt, verschrikkelijk,
erg*. The source draws an explicit register split within this single grammatical category:
*enigszins* is "quite formal," while the emphasizing adverbs as a group "tend to be used more in
spoken than in written language" — i.e. the same syntactic slot (pre-adjective degree adverb) is
occupied by lexical items spanning formal to colloquial registers, a genuine source-drawn register
distinction worth preserving distinct from the `002`/particle-system register findings.

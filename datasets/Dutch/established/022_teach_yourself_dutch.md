# Dutch — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Teach Yourself Dutch (Units 1-4)

**Source:** Gerdi Quist & Dennis Strik, *Teach Yourself Dutch* (Hodder Headline / McGraw-Hill,
2003, this printing 2008), Units 01-04, printed pp. 1-50 (PDF pp. 10-50 of
`source_reference/languages/Dutch/09 Teach Yourself Dutch.pdf`). A 16-unit general course
(introduction/pronunciation guide + units 01-16 + key to exercises + appendix + Dutch-English and
English-Dutch glossaries + grammatical index), distinct from the shorter, dialogue-only *Teach
Yourself Dutch Conversation* already covered in `established/004`.

**No text layer at all** (`pdftotext` returns nothing; confirmed via `pdfinfo`/empty extraction) —
every page in this file was vision-read from rendered page images (`pdftoppm`). **Page-mapping
gotcha found and resolved:** each of the PDF's 154 physical pages is a single scanned image but
covers **two printed pages side by side** (a left/right spread within one PDF page, not the
two-PDF-pages-per-spread pattern seen on some other sources) — printed page P maps to PDF page
`P/2 + 9` for P ≥ 2, verified empirically against printed page numbers visible on-page (checked at
printed pp. 2/3, 12/13, 22/23 against PDF pages 10, 15, 20) rather than assumed from a fixed
offset. **No handwritten marginalia found anywhere in Units 1-4** — every mark on every page
checked was consistent, typeset print matching the book's own font; nothing was excluded on
marginalia grounds.

**Coverage note:** every grammar point and every distinct vocabulary item from Units 1-4 (dialogue
vocabulary boxes, "How to..." functional-phrase summaries, the unit 4 food/drink glossary insert,
and all Grammar sections) is captured below. Repeated drill-exercise sentences that recycle
already-captured vocabulary without introducing anything new are skipped per the coverage rule;
country/nationality/number lists that are exercise content but introduce genuinely new vocabulary
are kept. This is a general course, not a register-annotated reference — nearly everything is
`core`; the source's own explicit formal/informal register markings (`u` vs `je/jij`,
`alstublieft` vs `alsjeblieft`, `goedemorgen` vs `hoi`, etc.) are tagged `formal`/`colloquial`
accordingly and flagged in Notes.

**Non-redundant-supplement note.** This language already has `established/001-003` (*Dutch: An
Essential Grammar*, Shetter & Van der Cruysse-Van Antwerpen — pronunciation/spelling/plural,
colloquial-speech discourse particles, diminutives) and `established/004` (*Teach Yourself Dutch
Conversation*, Owen — a different, shorter phrasebook by a different author). This book (*Teach
Yourself Dutch*, Quist & Strik) is the first full systematic beginner-to-intermediate *course* in
this language's `established/` set, structurally similar in places to `007` (Routledge Intensive
Dutch) but independently authored with its own dialogues and examples — per the "non-redundant
supplement" pattern, core-grammar territory that clearly duplicates `001`'s pronunciation/spelling
material (Units 1's brief "long words are written as one word" note, the trema explanation) is
kept only where this book adds new illustrative examples or a distinct framing; the numbers,
possessives, articles/plural system, position-verb (`liggen/zitten/staan`) idiom, and modal-verb
paradigm content are genuinely new/deeper systematic tabulations not previously captured from any
prior Dutch source and are kept in full.

## Grammar points

### je/jij/ik and basic subject pronouns (Unit 1)

Dutch mostly uses unstressed `je` for "you"; the stressed variant `jij` is used only for emphasis
or contrast, matching a change in spoken intonation rather than a different underlying pronoun (p.
6). `Ik` "I" has no stressed/unstressed split shown yet at this stage.

### Introducing yourself and basic formulas (Unit 1)

`Hoe heet je?` (lit. "what/how are you called") is Dutch's idiomatic way of asking a name;
literal translation into English fails, illustrating the book's broader point that not every
phrase should be translated word-for-word (p. 3). Core self-introduction set: `ik heet ...`, `ik
ben ...`, `ik kom uit ...`, `dit is ...`.

### Verb stems: regular and irregular (Unit 1)

The short present-tense form used with `ik` (and generally) is the verb's stem, found by removing
`-en` from the infinitive (`komen → kom`, `bellen → bel`, `willen → wil`, `kennen → ken`, `zitten
→ zit`); a stem cannot end in two identical consonants, so one is dropped where needed. A handful
of irregular verbs have unpredictable stems that must simply be memorized — `gaan → ga`, `zijn →
ben` (p. 8). Where the stem would otherwise end in certain sounds (e.g. `heten → heet`), an `-e`
is added rather than a bare stem drop, per the spelling rules referenced from the pronunciation
chapter.

### Negative: niet (Unit 1)

Dutch negation with `niet` is placed, per the book's basic-level treatment here, generally toward
the end of the sentence (a fuller treatment is deferred to Unit 11).

### Formal and informal address: u vs. je/jij (Unit 2)

`u` is the formal "you," used for both singular and plural, contrasting with informal `je/jij`
(singular) and `jullie` (plural). Corresponding verb forms differ: `Hoe gaat het met je?`
(informal) vs. `Hoe gaat het met u?` (formal); `dank je` vs. `dank u`. The safest default with a
stranger is `u`, until the other party invites informality (the fixed formula `Zeg trouwens maar
'je'` — "please say 'je'" — is how Dutch speakers do this explicitly, p. 14).

### Nationalities and languages: gendered noun pairs (Unit 2)

Most nationality nouns have distinct masculine and feminine forms, the feminine formed by adding
`-e` to (a version of) the masculine (`Nederlander` / `Nederlandse`; `Duitser` / `Duitse`). Modern
colloquial usage increasingly just uses the adjective form regardless of gender (`Ik ben Engels`
"I am English," p. 16). `Vlaams` denotes Dutch as spoken in the Flemish region of Belgium — the
book stresses this is the same language, not a separate one.

### Geen vs. niet vs. wel (Unit 2)

`Niet` negates most statements; `geen` specifically negates an indefinite noun ("no"/"not any" —
`Ik heb geen kinderen`). `Wel` is the positive-emphasis counterpart of both, with no direct English
translation — English instead uses vocal stress (`Ik spreek geen Frans. Maar ik spreek wel
Nederlands` "I don't speak French, but I *do* speak Dutch," p. 16).

### Present tense: regular verbs, full paradigm (Unit 2)

Singular: `ik` takes the bare stem; `je/jij` (informal) and `hij/zij` both take stem + `-t`, except
that the `-t` is dropped when `je/jij` immediately *follows* the verb (question inversion) — so
`jij komt` but `kom jij?`; `u` (formal) also takes stem + `-t`. Plural: all persons, including
formal `u`, take the full infinitive, except that `u` in the plural still patterns like the
singular `stem+t` (source tabulates `u komt`/`u spreekt` identically in both blocks, i.e. `u`
never uses the bare infinitive). Because `zij` "she" (singular) and `zij` "they" (plural) are
homophonous/homographic, only the differing verb form (stem+t vs. infinitive) disambiguates them.

### Present tense: irregular verbs zijn and hebben, full paradigm (Unit 2)

Both fully tabulated (p. 18-19); notably `u hebt` and `u heeft` are given as free variants with no
meaning difference.

### Numbers 1-20 (Unit 2)

Given as a set to memorize by heart (p. 19); no compounding pattern yet at this range.

### De, het, een: articles (Unit 3)

Dutch has one indefinite article `een` for both genders, but two definite articles: `de` (roughly
twice as common) and `het`, with no reliable rule for which a given noun takes — the book
recommends memorizing the (smaller) set of `het`-words and assuming `de` otherwise (p. 29).

### Plural formation: three strategies (Unit 3)

(1) Add `-s` when a noun has 2+ syllables and ends in `-el/-en/-em/-er/-je` (`tafel → tafels`); (2)
add `'s` when a noun ends in `-a/-i/-o/-u/-y` (`auto → auto's`); (3) add `-en` to all other nouns
(`boek → boeken`). Regardless of the singular's own article, every plural noun takes `de` (p. 28).

### Liggen, zitten, staan as idiomatic position copulas (Unit 3)

Beyond their literal meanings (lie down / sit / stand), these three verbs are used where English
would simply use "to be," each carrying an implied visual orientation: `liggen` for something
visualized lying flat or for geographic location (`De Vijlmerstraat ligt in Amsterdam Oost`);
`staan` for objects imagined standing upright or "ready" (`De koffie staat klaar` "the coffee is
ready"); `zitten` very broadly for living/being settled somewhere (`Mijn dochter zit in Amsterdam`
"my daughter lives in Amsterdam," p. 30-31). Full present-tense paradigms given for all three.

### Numbers 20-1000 and the trema (Unit 3)

From 20 onward, compound numbers put the units digit before the tens (`vierentwintig`, lit.
"four-and-twenty") and are written as one word. A trema (two dots) is added over a vowel that
would otherwise be misread as starting a new syllable in these compounds (`tweeëntwintig`,
`drieënzestig`) — cross-references the pronunciation chapter's spelling rules (p. 32).

### Possessive pronouns: stressed/unstressed forms and ons/onze agreement (Unit 3)

Most possessives have a stressed (written) and unstressed (spoken, contracted) form: `mijn/m'n`,
`jouw/je`, `zijn/z'n`, `haar/d'r`; `uw`, `jullie`, and `hun` have no unstressed variant. `Ons` vs.
`onze` for "our" is not a stress distinction but agreement: `ons` before `het`-words, `onze`
before `de`-words and all plurals (since all plurals take `de`) (p. 32-33).

### Er is / er zijn (Unit 3)

Existential "there is" (`er is`, singular) vs. "there are" (`er zijn`, plural), agreeing with the
number of the following noun (p. 33).

### Hè as an informal tag-question particle (Unit 3)

Invariant regardless of the preceding statement (unlike English's varying tag questions - "aren't
you?", "isn't it?"), and usable alone to mean "What?" Explicitly marked informal (p. 25).

### Subject pronouns: complete table, stressed/unstressed (Unit 4)

Full table given for singular and plural, including previously-unseen `het` "it" and `ie`/`ze`
unstressed forms for `hij`/`zij` (p. 45). Reinforces that unstressed forms are the spoken/written
default; parenthesized unstressed forms (`'k`, `ie`, `'t`, `we`) are flagged as too informal for
writing.

### It: het vs. hij (Unit 4)

Unlike English's single "it," Dutch requires tracking a noun's own de/het gender: `het`-word
referents are replaced by `het`, `de`-word referents by `hij` (p. 46) — directly dependent on the
de/het distinction introduced in Unit 3.

### Quantities and measure words (Unit 4)

Weight units `ons` (100g) / `pond` (500g) / `kilo` (1000g). `één` (with a double accent) is used
to disambiguate "one" from indefinite `een` "a/an," both otherwise spelled identically. Halves are
formed productively with `-eneenhalf` added to a number (`tweeëneenhalf`), except "one and a half"
which is irregularly `anderhalf`. Approximation: `zo'n` + a specific quantity, or `ongeveer`;
vaguer approximation uses `wat ... ` (p. 47).

### Container/quantity nouns (Unit 4)

`fles` (bottle), `doos` (box), `pak` (carton), `zak` (bag), `krat` (crate) — a small closed set of
common shopping-context measure nouns (p. 47).

### Modal verbs: willen, kunnen, mogen, moeten (Unit 4)

Full paradigms given for all four. `Wilt` and `kunt` are marked as more formal variants of `wil`
and `kan` respectively (no equivalent formal variant exists for `mogen`/`moeten` at this stage).
The second (main) verb combined with a modal is always the bare infinitive, placed at the very end
of the sentence; when that infinitive would be `hebben` or `gaan` and is contextually obvious, it
is commonly dropped entirely (`Wij willen een biertje (hebben)`, p. 48-49).

### Formal/informal register beyond pronouns (Unit 4)

The book makes explicit that formality is marked through vocabulary choice, not just
pronouns/verb-forms: `alstublieft`/`alsjeblieft`, `dank u (wel)`/`dank je (wel)`,
`goedemorgen`/`hoi`, `tot ziens`/`doei` are all given as matched formal/informal pairs (p. 48).

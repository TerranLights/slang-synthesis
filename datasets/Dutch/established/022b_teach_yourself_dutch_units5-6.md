## Grammar points

### Giving/asking directions: linksaf/rechtsaf, moeten/gaan (Unit 5)

Directional vocabulary (`links`, `rechts`, `rechtdoor`, `linksaf`, `rechtsaf`) combines with
`moeten` or `gaan` as the standard direction-giving frame (`U moet hier links`, `U gaat hier
rechtsaf`); motion verbs `lopen` (walk) and `rijden` (drive) are used for more specific
instructions depending on whether the addressee is on foot or driving. `Nemen` ("take") and
`oversteken` ("cross," used as `u steekt ... over`) extend the direction-giving vocabulary (p.
55-56).

### Hoi as both greeting and goodbye (Unit 5)

The book flags `hoi!` used to mean *goodbye* as a "relatively new" usage, restricted to very
informal situations among young speakers — distinct from its long-established use as a greeting
(p. 57).

### Hoor as a reassurance particle (Unit 5)

`Hoor` is deliberately called out as difficult to translate and often simply dropped in English;
its function is to reassure the listener (`ja, hoor` "yes, certainly"; `bedankt hoor` "thanks
[very much]") (p. 57).

### Moeten with gaan/hebben routinely dropped (Unit 5)

Extending the Unit 4 modal-verb point: when `moeten` (or another modal) combines with `gaan` or
`hebben` as the second verb, that second verb is very frequently omitted where context makes it
obvious — `U moet hier rechtdoor (gaan)`, `Ik wil een auto (hebben)` (p. 57).

### Co-ordinating conjunctions: en, maar, of, want, dus (Unit 5)

Five conjunctions link independent clauses/sentences without disturbing word order: `en` (summing
up), `maar` (contradiction), `of` (alternatives), `want` (reason), `dus` (conclusion) (p. 58-59).

### Verb-second word order (V2) and inversion (Unit 5)

The finite verb must occupy the second position of a Dutch statement. When the subject opens the
sentence, it directly precedes the verb (`We gaan morgen op vakantie`); when any other element
opens the sentence (e.g. a time expression), the subject is pushed behind the verb — inversion
(`Morgen gaan we op vakantie`). With more than one verb in a clause, the finite verb keeps second
position and all other verb(s) move to the very end (`Ik moet morgen de hele dag werken`).
Questions without a question word invert to put the verb first (`Moet je morgen werken?`);
questions with a question word keep the verb in second position, right after the question word (p.
59-61).

### Ordinal numbers: eerste, tweede, derde ... (Unit 5)

Formed by adding `-de` or `-ste` to the cardinal number (rule not fully specified for which takes
which, illustrated up to 20e); can be abbreviated to digit + `-e`. `Eerste` and `derde` are
irregular (p. 61-62).

### Toch: two distinct uses (Unit 6)

(1) As an (invariant) tag-question marker, sometimes with an undertone of irritation (`Die passen
toch niet bij deze broek?` "Those don't go with these trousers, do they?"); (2) as a concessive
"after all" (`Misschien zijn die blauwe toch leuker` "Perhaps those blue ones are nicer after
all") (p. 69-70).

### Houden van vs. vinden for likes/opinions (Unit 6)

`Houden van` ("to like/love") is regular except that its stem `houd` is conventionally written and
pronounced `hou` (and further drops to `hou` — not `houd` — when `je/jij` follows the verb and the
`-t` would be dropped). `Vinden` literally "to find" is commonly used for opinions, better
translated as "think" (`Vindt hij het huis mooi?` "Does he like/think the house [is] nice?"). Full
paradigms given for both (p. 70-71).

### Deze/die vs. dit/dat: demonstratives (Unit 6)

`Deze`/`die` ("this"/"that") pair with `de`-words; `dit`/`dat` pair with `het`-words. Because all
plurals take `de`, plurals always use `deze`/`die` regardless of the singular noun's own gender.
Demonstratives can stand alone without a following noun once the referent is clear, matching
English's "this/that one" (p. 71-72).

### Adjective -e ending, with one exception (Unit 6)

An adjective takes no ending in predicate position (`Het boek is mooi`) but adds `-e` in
attributive position, i.e. directly before the noun (`het mooie boek`). Spelling-rule adjustments
from the pronunciation chapter apply (`wit → witte`, `leeg → lege`). **Exception:** no `-e` is
added when the adjective precedes an indefinite (`een` or bare) `het`-word (`een interessant
boek`) (p. 72-73).

### Material adjectives take a fixed -en ending (Unit 6)

Unlike ordinary adjectives, adjectives derived from materials add an invariant `-en` regardless of
position: `goud → gouden`, `zilver → zilveren`, `hout → houten`. Exception: `plastic` keeps its
English spelling and takes no ending at all (p. 73).

### Broek and bril: singular in Dutch where English is plural (Unit 6)

Nouns like `broek` ("trousers") and `bril` ("glasses") are grammatically singular in Dutch and
take singular verb agreement (`Mijn broek is te lang`), unlike their English plural equivalents
(p. 69).

### Comparatives and superlatives (Unit 6)

Regular formation: `-er` for comparative, `-st` for superlative (always preceded by `het` in the
superlative): `mooi → mooier → het mooist`. Adjectives already ending in `-r` take `-der` instead
of `-er` for the comparative (`duur → duurder`). The same `-e` attributive-position rule from
above still applies to comparative/superlative forms (`een grotere auto`). Irregular forms:
`goed/beter/het best`, `veel/meer/het meest`, `weinig/minder/het minst` (p. 74-75).

### Adjective = adverb (no -ly equivalent) (Unit 6)

Dutch adverbs have the same form as the corresponding adjective — no morphological marker
equivalent to English `-ly` (`Het is slecht weer` adjective vs. `Het team speelt slecht` adverb,
both `slecht`) (p. 75).

### Graag and its irregular comparative/superlative (Unit 6)

`Graag`, used adverbially to mean "like doing something" (`Ik zwem graag` "I like swimming"), has
irregular comparative `liever` ("prefer") and superlative `het liefst` ("like most of all") rather
than regular `-er`/`-st` forms (p. 75).

### Talking about the future: present tense, gaan + infinitive, and zullen (Unit 6)

The Dutch present tense routinely does double duty as a future tense. `Gaan` + infinitive
("going to") is available and behaves like a modal-verb construction. `Zullen` ("shall/will") +
infinitive exists but the book stresses it is used much less often than the other two options,
reserved mainly for making promises or guarantees (`Ik zal het niet vergeten` "I won't forget").
Full paradigm of `zullen` given (p. 76-77).

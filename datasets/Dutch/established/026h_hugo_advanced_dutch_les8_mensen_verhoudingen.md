## Grammar points

### 1. `het` + adjective + `-e` + `van` (abstracted quality construction)

A pattern for turning an adjective into an abstract noun phrase referring to "the [quality] thing
about X": `het vervelende van versieren` ("the boring thing about chatting people up"), `het
interessante van dit boek` ("the interesting thing about this book"). Productive and usable
broadly with most adjectives (p.136–137).

### 2. Sandwich-construction (two-part) prepositions

Dutch has prepositions that wrap around their object in a "sandwich": `door ... heen` = "through"
(e.g. `door de traditionele patronen heen`), plus `naar ... toe` = "to" and `op ... af` = "towards"
(p.136).

### 3. Present-tense passive

`je gedrag wordt bepaald door ...` = "your behavior is determined by ..." — illustrates the
present-tense passive construction `worden` + past participle (p.136).

### 4. Names vs. common nouns taking possessive `-s`

Only proper names (people, places) can take a genitive `-s` without an apostrophe in Dutch (e.g.
`Annemies man` = "Annemie's husband," no apostrophe). Common nouns must use `van`-constructions
instead (e.g. `de man van Annemie`) (p.139).

### 5. Auxiliary-verb ellipsis with coordinated infinitives

When two infinitive verbal elements share one modal/auxiliary, Dutch (like English) allows the
auxiliary to be stated only once: `hij wilde scheiden en bij zijn vriendin gaan wonen` ("he wanted
to separate and [wanted to] go and live with his girlfriend") — `wilde` governs both `scheiden` and
`gaan wonen` despite appearing only once (p.140).

### 6. Conditional sentences — full three-way paradigm (Grammatica §1)

A more systematic treatment than Les 7's coverage of `zou/zouden`. Conditionals are introduced by
`als` (if; most common), or the more formal `wanneer`/`indien`, or negative `tenzij` (unless).
Three basic types, by tense pairing across the two clauses:

- **(a) Real/open condition:** `als` + present tense // `zullen` + infinitive (or present tense for
  future, per *Dutch in Three Months* §47) — e.g. `Als het regent, zal ik thuis blijven` / `Als het
  regent, blijf ik thuis` ("If it rains, I'll stay home"). Describes real consequences of an event
  that may actually happen.
- **(b) Hypothetical-but-possible condition:** `als` + simple past // `zou(den)` + infinitive — e.g.
  `Als ik zeer rijk was, zou ik iedere dag kaviaar eten` ("If I were very rich, I'd eat caviar every
  day"). Talks about imaginary consequences of an imaginary (but possible) event; past tense form,
  but not past time reference.
- **(c) Counterfactual/impossible condition:** `als` + past perfect // `zou hebben/zijn` +
  infinitive — e.g. `Als ik genoeg geld had gehad, zou ik die trui gekocht hebben` ("If I'd had
  enough money, I would have bought that jumper"). Describes a purely hypothetical situation that
  cannot now occur.

**Shorthand form:** the `zou` + infinitive half of types (b) and (c) can be contracted to a bare
simple-past verb, omitting `zou(den)` entirely: `Als ik genoeg geld had, kocht ik die trui` (type b
shorthand); `Als je Piet gekend had, had je hem aardig gevonden` (type c shorthand). Contraction can
also occur in the `als`-clause itself (e.g. `Als je Piet zou hebben gekend...`), and is often used
idiomatically to express regret: `Had ik het maar niet gedaan!` ("If only I hadn't done that!"),
`Wist ik het maar!` ("If only I knew!").

`Tenzij` introduces a negative conditional element: `Ik ga niet naar het feest, tenzij jij meegaat`
("I'm not going to the party unless you go too").

### 7. `Zou(den)` for reported future intentions in past time (Grammatica §2)

Distinct from the wish/possibility/probability senses already captured in Les 7 (`026g`) and from
the conditional-sentence use above: `zou(den)` also expresses "was/were intending to," "was/were
supposed to," when talking about past-time expectations — e.g. `Vrouwen zouden mannendingen gaan
doen` ("Women were supposed to start doing men's things"); `Hij zou vandaag komen, maar hij is er
nog niet` ("He was supposed to be coming today, but he's not here yet").

### 8. Informal letters

Contrasted with Les 4's formal-letter examples. Sample informal opening: `Beste Heleen,` (an
alternative more intimate opening would be `Lieve Heleen`); sample closing: `Liefs,` (an alternative
less-intimate closing would be `Groetjes`).

### 9. Reassurance and reinforcement particles: `toch`, `nietwaar`

Reassurance can be sought with a plain question (`Houd je van me?`) or reinforced into a
tag-question-like statement using `nietwaar` (equivalent to English "don't you?"): `Je houdt (toch)
van me, nietwaar?`. `Toch` alone gives similar reinforcing emphasis without a literal tag: `Dat weet
je toch?` ("You know that, don't you?"); `Dat hoeft toch geen probleem te zijn?` ("That doesn't have
to be a problem, does it?"). `Wel` can serve a similar emphatic function.

### 10. Diminutives disambiguating "friend" vs. "boyfriend/girlfriend"

`vriend`/`vriendin` are ambiguous between "(male/female) friend" and "boyfriend/girlfriend"; the
diminutives `vriendje`/`vriendinnetje` are often used specifically to mean "boyfriend"/"girlfriend"
when disambiguation is needed.

### 11. French loanwords retain their own accents

Unlike the Dutch emphasis-accent rule (accents added for emphasis, not inherent to the word — see
Les 1's `een`/`één` distinction), French loanwords used in Dutch keep their original accent as part
of the word's spelling regardless of emphasis, e.g. `carrière`.

## Grammar points

### Three body-ache/complaint patterns

Dutch has three (not fully interchangeable) sentence patterns for describing aches and pains:
(1) **Ik heb pijn in mijn ...** (lit. "I have pain in my ...") — works for most body parts, only
with **pijn** (not zeer). (2) **Mijn ... doet pijn/zeer** (lit. "My ... pains/aches") — also works
broadly, with either **pijn** or **zeer** interchangeably (no meaning difference between the two
words in this pattern), and pluralizes to **doen** for plural body parts (*Mijn ogen doen pijn*).
(3) **Ik heb ...pijn** — a **compound noun** formed from body part + pijn (hoofdpijn, buikpijn,
oorpijn, keelpijn, kiespijn, spierpijn) — but this compounding is **only available for the specific
list of body parts the source gives**; *ik heb voetpijn* sounds odd/ungrammatical even though the
same idea is fine in patterns (1)/(2). A separate, related pattern, **Ik heb last van mijn ...**
(lit. "I have trouble with my..."), is reserved for **chronic/recurring** trouble rather than acute
pain, and likewise only works with a specific fixed list of body parts/conditions (buik, longen,
duizeligheid, been, rug) — notably *ik heb last van mijn hoofd* is ungrammatical, but *ik heb vaak
last van hoofdpijn* (using the compound noun instead of "mijn hoofd") is fine.

### Zijn + aan het + infinitive (progressive aspect)

A form of **zijn** ("to be") + **aan het** + infinitive expresses an action in progress, parallel to
English **-ing**: *Sieme was aan het fietsen* ("Sieme was cycling"), *Ik ben aan het voetballen*
("I am playing football"), *De kinderen zijn aan het spelen* ("The children are playing"). This is
presented as the standard way to translate an English progressive/continuous form into Dutch, since
Dutch has no dedicated continuous verb conjugation of its own.

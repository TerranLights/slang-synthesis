## Grammar points

### Connected speech rules are postlexical, optional, and style/frequency-governed (§6.1, pp.125–126)

Unlike the obligatory rules of word phonology covered in earlier chapters, connected-speech rules
are typically optional, dependent on speech style (degree of monitoring) and speech rate, and often
serve ease of articulation at the expense of perceptibility — a genuine production/perception
trade-off speakers resolve differently by register. They can create syllable types the lexical
syllabification algorithm would disallow, which is one argument for treating them as postlexical.
Three governing factors recur throughout the chapter: **lexical frequency** (higher-frequency words
undergo reduction more readily), **dialectal variation**, and the **segmental environment** (e.g.
mid vowels reduce more easily than high vowels, open syllables more easily than closed). Booij notes
these "categorical" rule descriptions likely idealize an underlyingly more gradient phonetic reality
(citing Kohler 1990, 1991). The chapter is organized by domain: this chapter covers rules internal
to the word; Ch.7 covers rules across word boundaries; Ch.8 covers cliticization.

### Schwa Epenthesis (§6.2, pp.126–128)

A schwa may optionally be inserted into a coda-position cluster of two non-homorganic consonants
(*kalm* → [kaləm]). It is blocked when the second consonant is an appendix consonant (/s/ or /t/,
e.g. *hart* cannot become *[harət]*) or when the cluster is a nasal+homorganic-obstruent partial
geminate (*damp*, *bank*), because such clusters share Place-tier features and a "consonants that
share melodic specifications must be X-tier-adjacent" condition (attributed to John McCarthy, p.c.)
blocks the schwa from separating them. It is also blocked in word-final obstruent clusters like
/sp, sk/ (*wesp* "wasp"), suggesting these behave as complex single segments. Functionally, only
genuinely heterorganic (more effortful) clusters get broken up.

### Schwa Deletion (§6.3, pp.128–130)

When a word has two consecutive schwa-headed syllables, the first schwa may delete — but only if
the resulting onset cluster is an obstruent+liquid sequence (the universally best-formed CC-onset
type), even though Dutch's own lexical syllable-structure constraints otherwise bar onsets like
/tl-, dl-, zl-/. This shows language-specific onset constraints can be suspended postlexically while
universal well-formedness (the Sonority Sequencing Generalization) still applies. Deletion does not
happen if the resulting cluster is not extractable as a licit onset even under universal principles
(*tekenen* cannot become *[teknən]*), and it never applies before a full (non-schwa) vowel.

### Vowel Reduction (§6.4, pp.130–136)

An underlyingly full vowel may optionally surface as schwa in an unstressed syllable, formalized as
delinking the vowel's Place features (filled in afterward by the default schwa rule). Governing
factors, layered and sometimes conflicting: (1) **frequency** — high-frequency words reduce far more
readily than low-frequency ones with an otherwise identical structure; (2) **syllable-initiality**
blocks reduction (a prosodic word cannot begin with schwa, and /hə/ is independently banned); (3)
**open syllables** (or closed syllables with an ambisyllabic coda) favor reduction over closed ones;
(4) **word-finality** blocks reduction except for vowel+/r/ and /ɪ, ɛ/+consonant codas; (5) a vowel
**reducibility hierarchy** runs from /y, u, ø/ (least reducible) through /i/, /o, ɔ/, /a, ɑ/, to
/e, ɪ/ (most reducible) — /e/ is cited as reducing so readily that some words have lexicalized a
schwa in its place; (6) **foot structure** — reduction is easier in the weak syllable of a foot
(adjoined to a stressed syllable) than in a "stray" syllable outside any foot, giving a three-way
formality hierarchy (formal: no reduction; informal: adjoined-syllable reduction; very informal:
both). Kager (1989) is credited with showing these two hierarchies (segmental reducibility × foot
position) can make opposite predictions and interact via a joint "Vowel Reduction Hierarchy" keyed
to speech-style levels I–III.

### Vowel Shortening (§6.5, pp.136–137)

A full vowel in a word-initial, non-main-stressed syllable may shorten (long/tense → corresponding
short/lax quality), but — except for /a/ — only when the following syllable's vowel is itself
reduced to schwa, i.e. the two processes are linked in a binary foot. Shortened /i/ additionally
lowers to [ɪ], an automatic consequence of Dutch having no short high vowels. Vowel Reduction feeds
Vowel Shortening in derivations where both apply.

### Intrusive Stops (§6.6, p.137)

A framed as **retiming** rather than segment insertion (following Browman and Goldstein 1990): a
stop matching the Place of a following obstruent is "inserted" between a non-coronal nasal and that
obstruent in the same syllable (a labial [p] after [m], a velar [k] after [ŋ]), merging pairs like
*kam-t*/*kamp-t* "combs"/"fights" and *zing-t*/*zink-t* "sings"/"sinks" into identical surface forms.
Formally this is the Place feature of the nasal spreading rightward onto the following consonant's
first phase, producing a contour segment rather than a genuinely new segment.

### /i/-Deletion (§6.7, pp.138–139)

An unstressed /i/ optionally deletes between a consonant and a palatal glide /j/ that was itself
inserted by an independent Homorganic Glide Insertion rule (e.g. *station* /statiɔn/ → [stasjɔn]).
Blocked when the syllable carries or is preceded by main stress (*Siam*, *pária* never lose /i/), and
blocked after an already-complex onset (*Adrianus* cannot delete to *[adrjanys]*, which would create
too complex an onset). More likely after /s/, since /sj/ is the only resulting cluster that is a
fully regular onset at the lexical level and can even be realized as a single postalveolar segment
[ʃ]. For many speakers *station* is only pronounceable with the /i/ deleted.

### /n/-Deletion (§6.8, pp.138–141)

Syllable-final /n/ after a schwa optionally deletes in standard Dutch — obligatorily for many
western-Netherlands speakers — except in the indefinite article *een* "a". Applies to noun/verb
plural and infinitive *-en*, word-internally in certain diminutives and adverbials, and at the end
of the first member of compounds/derived words. Crucially blocked at the end of a bare (zero-
inflected) verbal stem, e.g. 1sg present *teken* [tekən] cannot become *[tekə]* — a genuine
morphological-category-sensitive exception within an otherwise phonological rule (citing Koefoed
1979). Related-word pairs (*regen* "rain" / *regenen* "to rain") prove an underlying /n/ persists
even for speakers who always delete it on the surface of the base word, since it resurfaces
non-word-finally in derived forms. A forward-referenced minimal pair from Ch.8 (*zette het* vs.
*zetten het* "put it", sg. vs. pl.) shows the plural suffix's underlying form must still contain /n/
even for total-deletion speakers, since only an underlying /n/ (not bare /ə/) blocks a following
clitic-schwa-deletion rule. The rule requires a genuine morphological boundary immediately after the
/n/ (not just syllable-finality): the present-participle suffix *-end* protects its own stem-final
/n/ from deletion despite that /n/ being syllable-final, because no morpheme boundary falls there
(citing Booij and Lieber 1993 on prosodic/morphological-structure interaction).

### Chapter conclusions: shared properties of connected-speech rules (§6.9, p.141)

Booij closes by generalizing across all the rules in this chapter: they increase ease of production
at the expense of word distinctiveness; their application depends on non-phonological factors
(lexical frequency/activation, degree of lexicalization/morphological transparency); speakers differ
in how far they let reduction processes apply; and — even though individually optional — a rule may
become *obligatory* once another optional rule has applied (supporting Dressler's 1974 hypothesis
that less-formal-style rules imply the applicability of more-formal-style rules, not the reverse).

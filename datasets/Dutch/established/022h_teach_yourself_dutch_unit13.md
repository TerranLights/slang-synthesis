## Grammar points

### Main clauses vs. sub-clauses; verb-final word order

A sentence can consist of a **main clause** (which can stand alone) plus a **sub-clause** (which
cannot). In a sub-clause, the finite verb moves to the very end of the clause, and the sub-clause is
introduced by a **subordinating conjunction** (contrasted with "ordinary"/coordinating conjunctions
like *en, of, maar*, which link two main clauses without triggering verb-final order). This
verb-final pattern is the same one already seen in relative clauses (Unit 10) — relative clauses are
in fact a type of sub-clause.

### Subordinating-conjunction inventory, grouped by relationship

The book groups subordinating conjunctions by the semantic relationship they encode between main and
sub-clause: **time** (voordat, nadat, terwijl, sinds, zodra, als, toen — note **als** for a general/
repeatable "when" vs. **toen** for a specific past "when"); **contrast** (hoewel, ondanks, terwijl);
**cause and reason** (omdat, doordat, zodat); **condition** (als, tenzij); **comparison** (alsof,
zoals, net zo ... als).

### Sub-clause-first inversion

A sub-clause need not follow the main clause — it can open the sentence instead: *Omdat ik eerst een
carrière wil hebben, wacht ik nog even met kinderen krijgen* ("Because I first want a career, I'll
wait a while before having children"). When the sub-clause comes first, the whole sub-clause counts
as the sentence's first grammatical "slot," so the following main clause undergoes **inversion** —
its finite verb comes immediately after the comma, before its own subject.

### Indirect question/statement

Not every sub-clause is introduced by a conjunction expressing time/cause/etc. — some report speech
indirectly. **Indirect statements** are introduced by **dat** (*Ik weet dat ik goed ben in ...*).
**Indirect questions** are introduced by the original question word (**waar, wanneer, welke, hoe,
wat, waarom**) when the direct question itself had a question word (*Ik weet echt niet waar ik goed
in ben*, from the direct question *Waar ben je goed in?*). When the direct question was a **yes/no
question** (no question word), the indirect version is introduced instead by **of** ("if"/"whether"):
*Ik weet niet of ik goed kan communiceren* ("I don't know if I can communicate well"), *Ik vraag me
af of ik naar die cursus zal gaan* ("I wonder whether I'll go on that course"). In both cases the
sub-clause's verb moves to clause-final position, per the general sub-clause rule.

### Interjections

Dutch makes heavy use of small, hard-to-translate **interjections** — informal words that add shading
to a statement rather than propositional content, rarely used in writing except to represent speech.
The book explicitly declines to give them single fixed meanings and instead illustrates by example:
**gewoon** marks the speaker's view that something is ordinary/unremarkable (*We gaan gewoon naar de
kroeg*); **(alleen) maar** signals contrast/antagonism toward the topic just mentioned, from either
speaker's or addressee's perspective (*Je bent alleen maar met jezelf bezig*); **juist** emphasizes a
contrast with a previously mentioned idea, more strongly than **wel** (and the two can even combine:
*Ja, dat helpt je juist wel*); **goh** expresses surprise, typically sentence-initial; **nou** signals
the speaker is about to explain something and buys thinking time, similar to English "well"; **even**
signals that something is trivial/will only take a moment (*Ik zal even voor u kijken*). **Hoor** and
**toch** (encountered earlier in the course) are named as further examples of the same category.

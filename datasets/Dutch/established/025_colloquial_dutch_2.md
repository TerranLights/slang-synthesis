## Grammar points

### Voor and één — disambiguating accent marks

**Voor** can mean "for," "before" (time), or "in front of" (place); when ambiguous, the
place/second-time senses take an accent (**vóór**) to distinguish from the plain "for" sense.
Similarly **een** can mean "a/an" or "one" (**één**), and takes an accent only in the "one" sense,
and usually only where genuinely ambiguous or emphasized — the source notes these accents are
Dutch's general-purpose way of marking emphasis, roughly where English would bold or underline a
word. p.2.

### Language point 1 — The gender of nouns

Dutch nouns are learned together with their definite article (**de** or **het**), which
determines whether a noun is common gender or neuter (informally "de-words"/"het-words"). The
article is not just for "the" — it also governs the demonstrative used ("this" = **deze**/**dit**,
"that" = **die**/**dat**) and whether an attributive adjective takes an **-e** ending (**een witte
koelkast** vs. **een wit plafond**). Gender/de-het status is generally a matter of rote learning,
not predictable from the noun's form. p.4.

### Language point 2 — Personal pronouns (emphatic vs. unemphatic forms)

Dutch personal pronouns have both emphatic and unemphatic forms, paralleling how English speech
(but not writing) uses reduced pronoun forms ("Ya better go," "We saw 'em," "Where's ee live?").
Some unemphatic forms can be written (**je**, **we**, **ze**) while others are normally only
spoken (**'k**, **-ie**). Full paradigm (subject/object/possessive/independent-possessive,
emphatic/unemphatic, singular/plural), p.6-7:

- Singular: **ik**/('k) — **jij**/je — **hij**/(ie), **zij**/ze, **het**/('t)
- Plural: **wij**/we — **jullie**/je — **zij**/ze

Notable footnoted details: **ikke** exists as an extra emphatic subject form only in spoken
language (e.g. "Who, me?" — "Wat, ikke?"); neuter-noun-referring words that would be unemphatically
**'t** are hyphenated onto the verb as **-t** when following it (**wat doet-ie?**); the unemphatic
possessive **haar** is pronounced **d'r** ("I saw her in town" = **Ik heb d'r in de stad gezien**),
common in speech but rare in writing; colloquial **hun** is increasingly used as a subject form
("they") by many speakers though still regarded as substandard, e.g. **Hun liggen in bed** "They
are lying in bed" — flagged by the source as a live sociolinguistic marker, not a neutral
alternative. p.7.

The unemphatic subject **je** standing in for **jullie** requires prior contextual disambiguation
and still takes a *singular* verb even though it means "you-plural" — e.g. **Jullie kunnen
morgenavond komen als je zin hebt** ("You[pl] can come tomorrow night if you[sg-verb] feel like
it"). It is very common in spoken Dutch to open with **jullie** to establish the plural referent
and then continue with **je** + singular verb — repeating **jullie** more than once in a sentence
sounds clumsy. p.6.

### Language point 3 — Independent possessive pronouns

Possessive adjectives (**mijn**, **jouw**, **zijn**, etc.) work like English "my/your/his," but
independent possessives ("mine," "yours") have two competing constructions in Dutch:

- **die/dat van + [pronoun]** — e.g. **Die van mij ook** "Mine too" (referring to **kat**, common
  gender); **Dat van mij ook** (referring to **potlood**, neuter). Works in both speech and
  writing, and is the *only* option usable with **jullie**.
- **de/het + possessive-with-e ending** — e.g. **De mijne ook**, **Het mijne ook**. Also usable in
  both speech and writing but more common in the written language. Cannot be used with **jullie**.

Choice of **die/dat** or **de/het** depends on the gender/number of the noun being referred to
(understood from context, not restated). p.10-11.

### Language point 4 — Basic tenses of the verb (overview)

Overview of the full Dutch tense system using **werken** (regular, takes **hebben** in the perfect
tenses) and **vallen** (irregular, takes **zijn** in the perfect tenses) as models — present,
imperfect, perfect, pluperfect, future, conditional, future perfect, conditional perfect. The
source flags that some regular verbs take **zijn** and many irregular verbs take **hebben**
(auxiliary choice is not predictable from regular/irregular status alone), and that Dutch has only
one form per tense where English has progressive/continuous variants for each — "Dutch is much
simpler than English in this very important respect." p.13.

## Grammar points

### 1. Compound-noun gender follows the final element

Confirmed again in this lesson's set of `milieu-` compounds: the definite article of a Dutch
compound noun always matches the grammatical gender of the compound's *final* element, regardless
of `milieu` itself being `het`. E.g. `het milieubesef` (besef is het) but `de milieuzorg` (zorg is
de). (Cross-reference: this rule was first introduced in Les 4 per this lesson's own note, p.153.)

### 2. `-vriendelijk` as a productive suffix

Parallel to the English productivity of "-friendly," Dutch `-vriendelijk` attaches broadly to form
new adjectives: `milieuvriendelijk` (environment-friendly), `gebruikersvriendelijk` (user-friendly),
`onderhoudsvriendelijk` (maintenance-friendly/low-maintenance).

### 3. `tot` → `toe` when combined with `er`/`daar`/`hier`/a relative pronoun

The fixed preposition `tot` (as in `bijdragen tot`, "to contribute to") changes to `toe` when
combined with `er`, `daar`, `hier`, or a relative pronoun — the same pattern already seen with `met`
→ `mee`. Examples: `Het land heeft er niets toe bijgedragen` ("The country didn't contribute
anything to it"); `de uitputting van energie, waartoe beide landen … bijdragen` ("the exhaustion of
energy, to which both countries contribute").

### 4. Separable-prefix placement can move earlier to avoid ambiguity/confusion

When the phrase intervening between a separable verb and its prefix is long, the prefix is
sometimes placed immediately after the finite verb instead of at the true end of the clause, purely
for readability — e.g. `Het gebruik van loodvrije benzine draagt bij tot een schoon milieu` (prefix
`bij` placed right after `draagt`, not at the very end after `milieu`). Strictly the "correct" form
would place `bij` at the true end, but this is a recognized stylistic accommodation.

### 5. Prepositional-phrase "break-out" exception to end-of-clause verb placement (major addition)

**The main new grammar point of this lesson.** The general rule (finite verb second in main
clauses; all verbal elements — separable prefixes, past participles, infinitives — clause-final;
all verbal elements clause-final in subclauses) has a real exception: **a prepositional phrase
(preposition + noun, functioning adverbially) can "break out" of the normal sandwich/final
position and be placed after the verbal elements instead.** This applies in main clauses,
subclauses, and `om + te + infinitive` constructions alike.

Examples (paired, showing both orders):
- Main clause: `Draai de radiatoren in ongebruikte kamers dicht` / `Draai de radiatoren dicht in
  ongebruikte kamers` ("Turn off the radiators in unused rooms") — both orders acceptable.
- Subclause: `Je weet dat ik al op deze vraag antwoord heb gegeven` / `Je weet dat ik al antwoord
  heb gegeven op deze vraag` ("You know I've already given an answer to that question").
- `Om + te + infinitive`: `Wij doen veel om de milieuproblemen op zowel mondiaal als nationaal
  niveau aan te pakken` / `We doen veel om de milieuproblemen aan te pakken op zowel mondiaal als
  nationaal niveau` ("We are doing a lot to tackle environmental problems on both a global and a
  national level"). `Om` itself is optional in this construction.

### 6. The object-specific "switch on/off" verb system

Which verb means "switch on/off" in Dutch depends on the object being switched — all are
separable, with `aan-`/`open-` prefixes corresponding to English "on" and `af-`/`dicht-`/`uit-`
prefixes corresponding to "off":
- `aanzetten` [tv, radio, computer, wasmachine, afwasmachine] = to turn/switch on
- `afzetten` [radio, motor] / `uitzetten` [tv, computer, gas] = to turn/switch off
- `aandoen` [licht, radio] = to turn/switch on; `uitdoen` [licht, gas] = to turn/switch off
- `opendraaien` [radiator, gas, water] = to turn on; `dichtdraaien` [radiator, gas, water] = to
  turn off

### 7. Command form review, applied to instructional text

The imperative/command form (verb stem, no subject, sentence-initial) is used throughout the
energy-saving leaflet to spur the reader to action, e.g. `Vervang gloeilampen`, `Isoleer
cv-leidingen`. With separable verbs in command form: `Zet de afwasmachine pas aan als hij helemaal
gevuld is` ("Don't switch the dishwasher on until it's full") — prefix still goes clause-final by
the normal rule.

### 8. `laten` + infinitive — three translation patterns

`Laten` + infinitive covers several distinct English constructions depending on context:
- "leave" + adverb/adjective: `laat verlichting niet onnodig branden` ("don't *leave* the lights
  *on* unnecessarily").
- "let" + infinitive: `laat 's winters de zon naar binnen schijnen` ("*let* the sun *shine* in in
  the winter").
- "see that" + verb: `laat de gordijnen tot op de vensterbank hangen` ("*see that* the curtains
  *hang* right down to the window sill").

### 9. `'s + [time period]` construction extended to seasons/weekdays

Already familiar with times of day (`'s morgens`, `'s avonds`), this construction also applies to
seasons and days of the week: `'s winters` (in the winter), `'s zomers` (in the summer), `'s
zaterdags` (on Saturdays).

### 10. Formal speech register: complexity/length and modal auxiliaries signal formality

Illustrated via the company director's speech: Dutch formal register is marked by longer,
more syntactically complex sentences and heavier use of modal auxiliaries (especially `mogen`) even
to express simple ideas — e.g. `Het is voor mij een bijzonder groot genoegen u allemaal van harte
welkom te mogen heten...` ("It is a particularly great pleasure for me to be able to extend a warm
welcome to you all...") versus the much plainer `Welkom in Vlaanderen`.

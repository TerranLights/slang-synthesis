## Grammar points

### §12.1 Co-ordinating conjunctions
Do not affect the word order of the clause they introduce. Notable idiosyncrasies: 'for' (want) is
more formal in English than in Dutch, where the reverse register relationship holds; of after a
negative clause has an idiomatic "before/when" sense (*Het duurde niet lang of ze stopten* "It wasn't
long before they stopped"); dus can act as either co-ordinating (no inversion) or adverbial
(inversion) depending on which reading is intended, except after en, where only the adverbial reading
is possible.

### §12.2 Subordinating conjunctions — word order and layering
The dependent clause's finite verb goes to clause-final position. Two subordinate clauses joined by a
co-ordinating conjunction can share one subordinating conjunction (the second, understood, one is
often dropped). Fronting a subordinate clause before its main clause forces inversion in the main
clause (the subordinate clause counts as "first idea"). Subordinate clauses can nest inside each
other, requiring the interrupted clause's verb to return to clause-final position after the embedded
clause closes. `dat` "that," obligatory in Dutch, is frequently dropped in English — the reverse of
the general economy tendency. A colloquially "superfluous" dat or of often turns up after other
subordinators/interrogatives in indirect questions (*Ik weet niet waar dat ze wonen*) — flagged by the
source as a real but non-prescriptive pattern, useful evidence for a "double-marking" mechanism in any
downstream slang-mechanics work.

### §12.2.1.6 The three-way system for "when": wanneer / als / toen
wanneer is obligatory in interrogative clauses (direct and indirect); it is replaceable by als when
the governed verb is present/future/perfect, or when the sense is "whenever" with an imperfect/
pluperfect verb. toen replaces wanneer/als specifically for a single past occasion. This three-way
split, with no direct single-word English equivalent, is one of the more genuinely difficult
grammatical distinctions the source flags as "a complex issue."

### §12.2.1.8 als — four distinct grammatical functions in one word
als functions as: (a)/(b) a subordinating conjunction ("when," "if"); (c) "as/so long as" (=
zolang); (d) "as" (rare, potentially confusable with zoals); (e) "than" after comparatives (disputed
usage, dan preferred); (f) an adverbial conjunction substituting for alsof "as if"; (g) a preposition
("as a child"). zoals, by contrast, functions only as a subordinating conjunction. This one-word,
many-function overload is a clear candidate source for ambiguity-driven slang/register play.

### §12.4 Correlative conjunctions — three behavioral classes
(1) Co-ordinating-type correlatives (of...of, noch...noch, zowel...als) show no inversion in either
half. (2) Adverbial-type correlatives (enerzijds...anderzijds, deels...deels) show inversion in both
halves. (3) A residual class of four (nauwelijks...of, niet alleen...(maar) ook, hoe...des te,
hoe...hoe) mixes behaviors within the same correlative pair — the clearest case in the whole chapter
of syntactic irregularity clustering in exactly the constructions used for emphasis/rhetorical
effect, again a plausible seed for a stylized-emphasis slang mechanism.

### §12.5 Conjunctions introducing infinitive clauses
An infinitive clause has no finite verb, just te + infinitive, introduced by om, teneinde, alvorens,
na, door, met, in plaats van, zonder or van. When the subject of the infinitive clause is identical
to the main clause's subject, the infinitive-clause form is used; when the subjects differ, the full
subordinate-clause form (in plaats van dat, zonder dat) with its own finite verb is required instead
(§12.5.1.4) — a same-subject/different-subject syntactic fork with no formal marking beyond the
presence or absence of dat.

### Chapter 13 — Prepositions: the aan/op contrast as the organizing example
The source frames the entire chapter around the observation that prepositions are irreducibly
idiomatic and best learned Dutch-preposition-first rather than via an English-to-Dutch dictionary
approach. The clearest systematic pair is aan (vertical "on," or "at/on the edge of") vs. op
(horizontal "on"): het schilderij AAN de muur "the picture ON the wall" (hanging, vertical) vs. op
(de) tafel "on the table" (resting, horizontal). Both prepositions also function independently as
adverbs (De soep is op "The soup is finished").

### §13.2.1 Postpositions (prepositions following the noun)
A closed set of common prepositions (in, uit, om, over, langs, af, op) can follow the noun they
govern to emphasize the *direction* of an action rather than its *place*: *Zij gaat de stad in* "She
is going into town." These postpositions are easily confused with separable verb prefixes when a
verb or past participle immediately follows in subordinate/main-clause-final position — the same
surface string can be either, disambiguated only by which element is semantically "part of the verb."

### §13.2.2 heen with prepositions
heen (following the noun, after a preposition) marks direction, sometimes figuratively: door de
eeuwen heen "over the centuries," langs elkaar heen praten "to talk at cross purposes" (lit. "talk
past each other"). Used alone (no other preposition), heen is a higher-style synonym of naartoe.

### §13.2.3 Double prepositions and their fused-adverb counterparts
Two prepositions can combine to sharpen a directional/positional sense (Hij is achter in de twintig
"he's in his late twenties"; boven op de kast "on top of the cupboard"). When written as one word,
the same pair becomes a simple adverb of place/motion (achterin "in the back") rather than a genuine
double preposition governing a following noun — a spelling-conditioned syntax/semantics split the
Dutch themselves are, per the source, "often confused" about.

### §13.2.4 Omission of English "of"
Dutch omits an equivalent of English "of" with measure-nouns before a plural/uncountable noun (een
fles bier "a bottle of beer") and with a small class of geographic-name apposition (het eiland
Rhodos "the island of Rhodes").

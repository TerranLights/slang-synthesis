## Grammar points

### Unit 66 — Fixed prepositions (2)
A second batch of fixed-preposition verb/adjective idioms extending Unit 65's list (zich
interesseren voor, kwaad zijn op/over, luisteren naar, praten over, zich schamen voor, solliciteren
naar, tevreden zijn met/over, twijfelen aan, verliefd zijn op, zich vergissen in, verwijzen naar,
wachten op, zoeken naar, zich zorgen maken over/om). The `kwaad zijn op` (person) vs. `kwaad zijn
over/om` (thing) split is a concrete instance of a pattern worth flagging generally: several fixed-
preposition idioms in this book's two units distinguish preposition by whether the object is a
person or a thing — a systematic sub-regularity beyond "just memorize the preposition."

### Unit 67 — Basic information on clauses
Defines main clause (can stand alone, finite verb in second position except in questions/orders)
vs. sub-clause (cannot stand alone; verb moves to clause-final position; must be introduced by a
linking word) vs. relative clause (a kind of sub-clause, treated separately in Units 74–75). When
a sub-clause itself contains a second verb, it too goes to the very end, with a past participle
allowed either just before or after the finite verb ("dat ik het nooit heb geprobeerd" / "dat ik
het nooit geprobeerd heb" — both acceptable). Standard content matching `established/012`/`013`;
kept terse.

### Unit 68 — Conjunctions: linking similar clauses (co-ordinating)
The five co-ordinating conjunctions (en, of, maar, want, dus) link two clauses that could each
stand alone, preserving normal main-clause word order in both halves (unlike subordinating
conjunctions). Each conjunction's functional nuance is named explicitly: en for summing up, of for
alternatives, maar for contradiction, want for reason, dus for conclusion. Can also link words/
phrases below clause level ("de zon en de maan"). Matches prior coverage; kept terse.

### Unit 69/70/71 — Subordinating conjunctions: sub-clause linking (1)-(3)
Unit 69 covers question-word sub-clauses (indirect questions, paired with verbs like weten/zeggen/
zich afvragen) and the `dat`/`of` pair — `dat` ("that," the single most frequent linking word,
paired with vertellen/geloven/zeggen/weten/beloven) and `of` ("whether/if," expressing uncertainty,
paired with vragen) — explicitly flagged as easily confused with the co-ordinating `of` ("or")
since they're homographs with different grammatical roles. Units 70–71 catalogue subordinating
conjunctions by function: **time** (als, toen, wanneer, voordat, nadat, totdat, terwijl, nu, sinds,
zodra, zolang — with `toen` restricted to imperfect/past-perfect contexts only, and flagged as
ambiguous with the adverb `toen` "then"), **condition** (tenzij, als, als...dan), **comparison**
(alsof, zoals, (net zo/even)...als, (hetzelfde/dezelfde)...als), **cause/reason** (doordat, omdat,
zodat), and **contrast** (ondanks (het feit dat), terwijl, hoewel — `terwijl` explicitly noted as
carrying both a time sense, cross-referenced from Unit 70, and a contrast sense, disambiguated only
by context). A sub-clause can precede its main clause, in which case the main clause inverts
(subject moves after the verb) — this fronted-sub-clause-counts-as-one-element rule matches Unit
51's complex-sentence pattern. This functional (rather than alphabetical) organization of Dutch's
subordinating-conjunction inventory is a genuinely useful compact reference, even though individual
conjunctions match prior established/ coverage.

### Unit 72/73 — Linking with adverbs (1)-(2)
Conjunctive/linking adverbs (as opposed to conjunctions) trigger inversion when clause-initial,
since they themselves occupy the first sentence-position — **except** for a specific small class
(tenminste, althans, trouwens, integendeel) that's followed by a comma with no inversion, a real
exception worth flagging. Organized by function: time (daarna, daarvoor, toen, dan, nu), cause
(daardoor), reason/conclusion (daarom, daartoe, dus — `dus` as adverb triggers *optional* rather
than obligatory inversion, distinct from its co-ordinating-conjunction use in Unit 68 which never
inverts), condition (anders), contrast (toch, niettemin), and summing-up (ook, tevens, verder,
bovendien). Unit 73 adds a further class built from `daar` + preposition (daarnaast, daartegenover,
daarlangs, etc., patterning after the plain prepositions of Units 63–64). This adverb-vs-
conjunction word-order distinction (same connecting idea, different syntax depending on part of
speech) is a genuinely useful systematic point not laid out this explicitly elsewhere in the
project's Dutch coverage.

### Unit 74/75 — Relative pronouns (1)-(2): die/dat, wat/wie/preposition combinations
Unit 74: relative clauses always require an explicit relative pronoun in Dutch (unlike optional
"that" in English). Choice between `die` (de-words, plurals, people-referring pronouns like
iedereen/niemand/sommigen) and `dat` (het-words only) — the rule applies even to indefinite/
article-less nouns, so it hinges on knowing a noun's underlying gender regardless of the article
actually present in the sentence. Unit 75 covers three further relative-pronoun cases: `wat` for
indefinite/unclear antecedents or when referring to an entire preceding clause ("Hij heeft een
verhouding met zijn secretaresse, wat iedereen weet"); `wie` (referring to people) vs. `wat`
(referring to things) when there's no clear antecedent at all ("Wie het weet mag het zeggen"); and
preposition-combined relative pronouns — `preposition + wie` for people, `waar + preposition` for
things (met/tot mutating to mee/toe only in the waar-combined form, not the wie-combined form).
**The most genuinely useful nuance here**: the source explicitly notes that despite the
prescriptive wie-for-people rule, many native Dutch speakers don't observe it and use `waar +
preposition` for people too — a real, source-flagged prescriptive-vs.-actual-usage gap, the same
kind of register note as the `dan`/`als` comparative observation in Unit 13.

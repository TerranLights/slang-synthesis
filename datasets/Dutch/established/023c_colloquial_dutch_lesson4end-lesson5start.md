## Grammar points

### Perfect tense — position of past participle (pp. 62-63)

Past participle always clause-final regardless of clause length; finite verb stays in second
position; the two form a "verbal bracket" around any intervening material. Source explicitly flags
that Dutch collapses the English perfect/imperfect distinction ("I rang," "I have rung," "I've been
ringing" all typically render as `Ik heb gebeld`) — Dutch strongly prefers the perfect tense in
conversation generally, reserving the imperfect (introduced later, Lesson 9+) for narrative/written
register. This asymmetry is a recurring theme worth tracking as slang/register mechanics analysis
proceeds — spoken Dutch's default past-tense choice differs systematically from English's.

### Perfect tense of irregular verbs (pp. 64-65)

`ge-` + stem + `-en`, often with an internal vowel change (`geven→gegeven`, `krijgen→gekregen`,
`winnen→gewonnen`, `zien→gezien`); a small subset combine a vowel change with a regular-looking `-t`
ending (`brengen→gebracht`, `denken→gedacht`, `kopen→gekocht`). No fully general rule — irregularity
patterns are only "roughly" guided, must be memorized per-verb (as in English strong verbs).

### Perfect tense with zijn vs. hebben (pp. 67-69)

A closed set of mostly-irregular verbs uses `zijn` rather than `hebben` as the perfect auxiliary:
(1) verbs of motion (`gaan`, `aankomen`), (2) verbs of change of state (`sterven`, `veranderen`),
(3) a residual handful that fit neither category (`blijven`, `vergeten`, `zijn`). A further small
group (`lopen`, `reizen`, `fietsen`, `zwemmen`-type activity verbs) alternates between `zijn` and
`hebben` **on the same verb** depending on whether a destination is specified (`zijn`) or the
activity is described generally/durationally (`hebben`) — a genuine semantically-conditioned
auxiliary alternation, not free variation.

### Modal auxiliary verbs (pp. 68-69)

`kunnen`/`moeten`/`mogen`/`willen` — fully irregular present-tense paradigms (comparable to English
"can/must/may/will" irregularity), always governing a bare clause-final infinitive.

### Making and accepting apologies (pp. 70-74)

Register-graded apology vocabulary: `sorry` (informal, semantically broader than English "sorry" —
covers "excuse me" contexts too), `het spijt me` (used when giving a reason), `neem me niet
kwalijk`/`pardon` (more formal register), `Neemt u me niet kwalijk` (u-form). Acceptance:
`het geeft niet`/`het maakt niet uit`. "Not hearing/understanding" register ladder: `Wat?` flagged
explicitly as colloquial/familiars-only, `Wat zeg je?`/`Wat zegt u?` as the neutral-to-polite default
(despite superficial literal translation as "What did you say?", pragmatically as polite as "I beg
your pardon?").

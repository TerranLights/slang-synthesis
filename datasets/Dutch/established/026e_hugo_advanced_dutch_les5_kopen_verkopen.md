## Grammar points

### `door + te` + infinitive — "by VERB-ing"

Equivalent of English "by + VERB-ing," with the same sandwich-construction verb-final placement seen
elsewhere: **door hun verpakking opvallend te maken** ("by making their packaging noticeable"). p. 82.

### `zo + adjective + mogelijk` — "as ADJECTIVE as possible"

**zo opvallend mogelijk** = "as noticeable as possible." p. 82.

### Indirect questions as subclauses (question-word-initial clauses)

A clause introduced by a question word (`waar`, `welke`, `wat`, etc.) that does **not** itself form a
direct question is an indirect question and therefore a subclause, with the verb in final position:
`... waar de pakken staan`; `... welke koffie je lekker vindt`; `... wat de mogelijkheden van de
computer zijn`. p. 83.

### Invisible "of" with quantities/containers

Dutch never translates English "of" between a measure/container word and the substance it holds:
**een kilo appels** ("a kilo of apples"); **een pot jam** ("a pot of jam"); **bakken koffiebonen**
("containers of coffee beans"). p. 83.

### Ellipsis of repeated clause elements across coordinated clauses

Where two clauses in one sentence share the same basic pattern, repeated elements can be dropped from
the second clause — unlike English, which usually needs at least a pronoun/auxiliary placeholder:
**De banken waren al dicht, het postkantoor (was) ook (dicht).** ("The banks were already closed,
the post office too" — verb and adjective both omitted); **Ze hadden geen cent meer op zak en [ze
hadden] honger als een paard.** (subject and verb both omitted). p. 86.

### `stoppen` — general "to put (into)"

Beyond its more familiar "to stop" sense, `stoppen` is flagged as a broadly useful verb for describing
putting something into a container/receptacle of any kind: **Ze stopt het geld in haar tas.** ("She
puts the money in her bag."); **Optimistisch stopten ze de card in de machine.** ("Optimistically
they put the card into the machine."). p. 86.

### `wel` and `niet` as one-word contrastive answers, replacing a whole clause

`Wel` provides emphasis and stresses contrast (typically against a preceding `niet`); when used this
way, the rest of the clause can be dropped entirely — something **not** possible in the equivalent
English: **'Ik heb geen zin in koffie.' 'Ik wel.'** ("'I don't fancy a coffee.' 'I do.'"); **Hij
houdt niet van katten, maar zij wel.** ("He doesn't like cats, but she does."). `Niet` works
symmetrically in the reverse-contrast direction: **'Ik vind rode kool lekker.' 'Ik niet.'** ("'I like
red cabbage.' 'I don't.'"); **Hij houdt van katten, maar zij niet.** ("He likes cats, but she
doesn't."). pp. 87.

### `hoeven` — "not have to," always paired with `niet`

`Hoeven` always co-occurs with `niet` and functions as the negative counterpart of `moeten`: **Je moet
het doen.** ("You must do it.") vs. **Je hoeft het niet te doen.** ("You don't have to do it.");
**Je moet hem geld geven.** vs. **Je hoeft hem geen geld te geven.** ("You don't have to give him any
money" — note `geen`, not `niet`, when negating a direct object noun). p. 87.

### `gaan` expressing general possibility

**Bij veel benzinestations gaat dat ook.** ("At many petrol stations that's possible too/that works
too as well") — `gaan` here expresses general possibility rather than literal motion, with the
now-familiar subject-verb inversion (`gaat dat`, not `dat gaat`) triggered because the sentence opens
with the adverbial phrase `Bij veel benzinestations`. p. 88.

### Superlative adjectives and adverbs

**Superlative adjective**: add `-ste` to the adjective (**de lekkerste koffie**, "the most delicious
coffee"; **het belangrijkste moment**, "the most important moment"), and — like any adjective — it
can stand alone without repeating the noun (**Welke koffie koop jij? De goedkoopste.**). A superlative
adjective used **as a noun** (e.g. **het belangrijkste**, "the most important thing"; **het
leukste**, "the nicest thing") is **always a `het`-word**, regardless of the underlying adjective's
usual gender-agreement context. **Superlative adverb**: add `-st` (more common, and the source
recommends defaulting to it) or `-ste`, and it is **always** preceded by `het`: **Ik drink het liefst
wijn bij mijn eten.** ("I like to drink wine best with my food."). The superlative-noun vs.
superlative-adverb distinction is functional, not formal: a superlative-as-noun is the sentence's
subject or adds information about the subject (**Het belangrijkste is kritisch blijven**; **Dat vind
ik het belangrijkste**), while a superlative adverb adds information about the verb's action itself.
Four common irregular superlatives: `goed` → `(het) best`; `graag` → `(het) liefst`; `weinig` →
`(het) minst`; `veel` → `(het) meest`. pp. 89–90.

### The preposition `bij` — a multi-sense inventory

The source explicitly frames `bij` as one of the hardest Dutch prepositions to pin down precisely
because it has no single direct English equivalent, and works through four senses attested so far in
the book:
1. **`bij` = "at"** (straightforward locative with a noun): **bij sommige supermarkten** ("at some
   supermarkets"); **bij veel benzinestations** ("at many petrol stations").
2. **`bij` + infinitive-as-noun = "during"/"in the course of"**: **Ze zei het bij het weggaan.** ("She
   said it as she was leaving."); **De verpakking speelt een heel andere rol bij het doen van de
   alledaagse boodschappen.** ("The packaging plays a completely different role when doing the
   everyday shopping.") — this combines directly with Les 2's infinitive-as-noun construction.
3. **`bij` + personal pronoun = "on one" / "about one's person"**: **Heb je een pen bij je?** ("Have
   you got a pen on you?"); **... als je je bankpasje bij je hebt** ("...if you have your cheque card
   with/on you").
4. **`bij` + personal pronoun/noun (+ `thuis`) = "at the house of"**: **Bij mijn ouders thuis eten we
   altijd om zeven uur.** ("At my parents' house we always eat at seven o'clock."); **Bij ons vroeger
   deden mijn ouders er niet aan, aan kroket.** ("At our house in the past, my parents didn't go in
   for croquettes," reusing the Les 2 reading passage's own opening line as the example.)

The source is explicit that only the senses attested *so far* in the book are covered here, implying
`bij` will resurface with further senses in later lessons. pp. 90–91.

### `nieuwe`/`nieuw` and `hem`/`het` — gender-tracking pronoun/adjective choice in exchanges

When asking to exchange an item for "a new one," the adjective's gender-inflection follows the
referent noun's own gender: `nieuwe` (with `-e`) refers back to a `de`-word, `nieuw` (bare) to a
`het`-word. The same de/het split governs the object pronoun choice: `hem` for a `de`-word referent,
`het` for a `het`-word referent — **Ik wil hem/het graag ruilen.** ("I'd like to exchange it.").
p. 94.

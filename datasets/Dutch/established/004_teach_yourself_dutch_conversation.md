# Dutch — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Teach Yourself Dutch Conversation (full book)

**Source:** Marleen Owen, *Teach Yourself Dutch Conversation* (Hodder Education / McGraw-Hill,
2005), full text, pp. 1–37 of the PDF (all 48 physical pages, including front matter and
back-cover advertising pages 38–48 which carry no language content). Ten short bilingual
dialogues ("conversations"), a cultural-information section, a survival-phrases list, a
Dutch–English glossary, an English–Dutch glossary, cardinal/ordinal numbers, days of the week,
months of the year, and a subject index.

**Coverage note:** this is a beginner conversational phrasebook/audio-course companion, not a
systematic grammar reference — it teaches almost entirely through short memorized dialogues plus
two mirror-image end glossaries, with no formal rule explanations of its own (its own subject
index just tags which conversation number touches which topic, e.g. "modal or auxiliary verbs
2, 3, 6, 7, 9"). Every distinct vocabulary item across the dialogues, both glossaries, the survival
phrases, numbers/ordinals, and the days/months lists is captured below (the two glossaries are
near-total mirrors of each other and of the dialogue vocabulary, so they're merged into one
deduplicated table rather than extracted twice). Grammar points are written as this agent's own
paraphrase of what the dialogues actually demonstrate for each index-tagged topic, since the source
itself supplies no explanatory prose to paraphrase — this is flagged explicitly per point below
rather than presented as if quoting a grammar reference's own explanation. No marginalia risk: this
source has a full, clean `pdftotext` text layer (confirmed via `pdfinfo`/`pdftotext -layout`), so
no vision-reading was needed anywhere in this file.

**Non-redundant-supplement note.** This language already has `established/001` (Ch.1-3,
pronunciation/spelling/plural), `002` (Ch.16, colloquial speech/discourse particles), and `003`
(Ch.22, diminutives) from *Dutch: An Essential Grammar* (Shetter & Van der Cruysse-Van
Antwerpen). This book is a different genre entirely (functional-dialogue phrasebook vs. systematic
grammar) with almost no vocabulary overlap — it supplies everyday transactional/social vocabulary
(booking rooms, ordering food, asking directions, discussing work) that the grammar-reference
chapters never covered, so **nothing here was skipped as redundant**; the full vocabulary set is
new. The one genuine overlap point is the diminutive `zusje` ("little sister," glossary p.29) — see
its own note under Grammar points below, cross-referencing `003_chapter22_diminutives.md` rather
than re-deriving the diminutive rule.

**Numbers, ordinals, days, months (pp. 35–36).** Cardinals 1–30 follow the regular `-tien`/`-tig`
pattern (`dertien` "13" through `negentig` "90"), with compound cardinals above 20 formed
right-to-left with `en` ("eenentwintig" = "one-and-twenty" = 21), matching the English "and"-based
older numbering pattern rather than modern English's left-to-right order — structurally identical
to German's `einundzwanzig`. Ordinals 1st–3rd are irregular (`eerste`, `tweede`, `derde`), 4th–19th
add `-de` to the cardinal (with a few spelling adjustments, e.g. `vierde`, `achtste` for 8th, not
`achtde`), and the source's own explicit rule for ordinals above 20 is **"Add `-ste` after the
numbers over 20"** (p.35) — e.g. `eenentwintigste` "21st." Days of the week (`zondag`–`zaterdag`)
and months (`januari`–`december`) are given as closed lists, all `core`, all lowercase in Dutch
(unlike capitalized English month/day names) — this lowercase convention is itself worth flagging
as a systematic orthographic rule, though the book states it only by example, never explicitly.

## Grammar points

*Framing note, repeated from the header: this source is a phrasebook, not a grammar reference — it
never states a rule in its own prose. Every point below is this agent's own paraphrase/inference
from the dialogue examples and the subject index's own topic tags (p.37), presented as inferred
analysis rather than as if quoting the book's explanation. Cross-references to `001`–`003` note
where the systematic grammar-reference material already covers the same ground in more depth.*

### Stressed vs. unstressed personal pronouns

Dutch marks a stressed/unstressed distinction the dialogues use consistently but never name:
`wij` (stressed) vs. `we` (unstressed), `jij` (stressed) vs. `je` (unstressed). Compare Conv.1's
"**Wie** bent u?" (formal, no stress contrast) against Conv.10's "**Wij** spreken allebei
Nederlands" (emphatic, contrastive) vs. the same conversation's unstressed "**we** hebben een jaar
lang Nederlands geleerd" (backgrounded, non-contrastive). The book's own glossary lists both forms
of each pair side by side without explaining the distinction — this chunk's own dialogue-context
reading is the actual evidence for what the distinction is.

### Formal vs. informal address (u vs. je/jij)

Every dialogue with a stranger, shopkeeper, receptionist, or doctor uses `u`/`uw` exclusively (e.g.
all of Conv.2, 4, 6, 9); every dialogue among the established friend group (Conv.10, John/Penny
throughout) uses `je`/`jij`/`jou`. This is a real T–V (formal/informal) register split running
through the whole book, not formally named in the text but structurally load-bearing for every
single conversation's pronoun choice.

### Modal/auxiliary verbs (kunnen, willen, mogen, moeten, zullen)

Tagged by the subject index at Conv.2, 3, 6, 7, 9. All five modals appear with a bare infinitive in
clause-final position, standard Dutch V2-with-modal word order: "Ik **wil** graag naar Rotterdam"
(want, Conv.2), "**Mogen** wij twee koffie?" (may, Conv.3), "Kan ik u **helpen**?" (can, Conv.6),
"Zullen we een rondvaart **maken**?" (shall, Conv.7), "Mag ik u iets **vragen**?" (may, Conv.9).
`zullen` doubles as the future-tense auxiliary (see below).

### Future tense with zullen

Tagged at Conv.6, 7. `zullen` + bare infinitive marks futurity/proposal: "Ik **zal** twee kaartjes
**reserveren**" (Conv.7, "I shall reserve two tickets"), "**Wanneer zullen we gaan**?" (Conv.7,
"when shall we go?"). Functions much like English "shall/will," including its use for polite
proposals rather than pure prediction.

### Present perfect tense (hebben + past participle)

Tagged at Conv.10 only. "We **hebben** een jaar lang Nederlands **geleerd**" ("We have learned
Dutch for a year") — standard hebben-auxiliary present perfect. Only one example appears in the
whole book; too thin on its own to establish the ge-prefix past-participle rule generally, but
consistent with what a fuller grammar reference would show.

### Negation: niet vs. geen

Tagged at Conv.4, 7. `geen` negates an indefinite noun phrase directly ("we verkopen **geen**
kinderkleren," "there are **no** books in the shop" = "er zijn **geen** boeken"); `niet` negates a
verb/clause generally ("ik wil **niet**... eten," "dat weet ik **niet**"). The dialogues show the
distributional split cleanly even though the book never states the rule as such.

### kennen vs. weten ("to know")

Not tagged in the subject index but clearly present and worth flagging: `kennen` is used for
knowing/recognizing a person ("**Kent u** Meneer de Koning?," Conv.1) while `weten` is used for
knowing a fact ("Weet u de weg...?," "dat **weet** ik niet," Conv.9) — the same
lexically-split-by-object-type pattern found in French connaître/savoir and German kennen/wissen.

### Question formation via subject-verb inversion

Tagged at Conv.3, 4. Yes/no questions invert to verb-first: "**Zijn er** ook boeken in de winkel?"
(Conv.4), "**Is** Groningen een grote stad?" (Conv.1). Wh-questions place the question word first,
then invert: "**Waar komt** u vandaan?" (Conv.1).

### V2 word order and time-adverb fronting

Tagged at Conv.7, 8, 10 ("position of verbs in sentence") and Conv.8, 9 ("inverting word order").
When a time or place adverb opens the clause, the finite verb still occupies second position and
the subject moves after it: "**Dan gaat u** rechtdoor" (Conv.9, lit. "Then go you straight on"),
"**Morgen** kunnen we een rondvaart maken" is implied by the surrounding context of Conv.7's "'s
Morgens **kunnen we**..." — textbook V2, the same governing word-order rule `established/002`
already discusses for discourse-particle placement in a different register.

### Gender (de/het) surfacing in dialogue

Tagged at Conv.6. Not explained, but visible throughout: `de trein`, `de kamer`, `de winkel` vs.
`het bad`, `het museum`, `het werk`. The `deze`/`dit` and `welke`/`welk` demonstrative/determiner
pairs (glossary, pp.24-25) are explicitly gender-conditioned — `deze` and `welke` for de-words,
`dit` and `welk` for het-words — giving a small but real paradigm the grammar-reference chapters
(001-003) didn't happen to tabulate this way.

### Comparatives

Tagged at Conv.8. Only `meer` ("more") appears as a comparative marker, in the idiom "hou je
**meer** van Bami Goreng?" (Conv.8, "do you like Bami Goreng more/better?") — periphrastic
comparison with `meer... van` rather than a `-er` suffix, because `houden van` is already a fixed
verb phrase. Too thin an example set to generalize the regular `-er` comparative rule from this
source alone.

### Body-part compounds with -pijn

Not index-tagged as a grammar point per se, but a real, systematic compounding pattern surfaces
across Conv.5's illness dialogue: `buikpijn` (stomach+pain), `hoofdpijn` (head+pain), `kiespijn`
(molar+pain) all follow [body part] + `pijn` "pain" — a fully productive compounding slot, not
three unrelated lexical items.

### Diminutive-derived lexicalizations (cross-reference, not new analysis)

`enkeltje` ("single ticket"), `retourtje` ("return ticket"), and `kaartjes` ("tickets," diminutive
plural of `kaart`) all carry the `-je`/`-tje` diminutive suffix already fully analyzed in
`established/003_chapter22_diminutives.md`. `zusje` ("little sister," glossary-only, not used in a
dialogue) is likewise a lexicalized diminutive of `zus`. Nothing new to add to the diminutive rule
itself here — flagged only so a future reader of this file knows these forms were noticed and
correctly not re-derived from scratch.

### Fossilized genitive-'s time adverbs

Not index-tagged, but a small recurring pattern: `'s morgens`, `'s middags`, `'s avonds` ("in the
morning/afternoon/at night") all share a fossilized `'s` (historically a reduced genitive article)
prefixed to a noun with an adverbial `-s` suffix — a fixed, closed set of three, not a productive
process demonstrated elsewhere in this source.

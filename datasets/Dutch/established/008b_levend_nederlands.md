## Grammar points

### 1. Plural pronoun paradigm and negation with "niet" (Les 5)

Plural subject pronouns: 1st *we* (unstressed) / *wij* (stressed); 2nd informal *jullie*; 2nd formal
*u*; 3rd *ze* (unstressed) / *zij* (stressed). Plural verb conjugation for all persons uses the bare
stem+en infinitive form (*we werken*, *jullie werken*, *ze werken*), except formal *u* which still
takes the singular-pattern +t (*u werkt*). Negation: **geen** = negation + indefinite article (per
Les 2), used whenever the noun phrase itself would otherwise take *een* or the zero article; **niet**
negates everything else (a definite noun phrase, the verb, a whole clause). Word order for *niet*
(clause negation) is given as an explicit ordered rule: *niet* comes **after** (1) the finite verb,
(2) the object, (3) a time expression, (4) *er/hier/daar*, (5) *dat is/dat zijn + X*; but **before**
(1) a preposition, (2) an adjective(+adverb), (3) most adverbs (except a time adverb).

### 2. Question word agreement: welk/welke (Les 6)

*Welk* is used with *het*-words (singular neuter); *welke* with *de*-words and with every plural
noun regardless of gender — the same de/het-tracking agreement pattern already established for
articles and demonstratives elsewhere in this book, now extended to an interrogative determiner.

### 3. "Dienst" and "vrij" — a lexicalized idiom pair (Les 6)

*Dienst hebben* ("to have duty") = to be working, said especially of people who wear a uniform for
their job; its opposite is *vrij hebben* ("to have free[dom]") = not having to work. *Ik ben vrij* is
distinguished from *ik heb vrij*: the former can additionally mean "I am independent/unattached," a
genuine ambiguity the *heb*-construction doesn't carry. Both take *geen* rather than *niet* in
negation (*ik heb geen dienst*, *ik heb geen vrij*), patterning with other fixed
hebben-plus-bare-noun predicates like *ik heb honger*/*dorst*. Separately, *tot uw dienst* is a fixed
formulaic reply to *dank u wel* — a register-marked (visibly formal/service-context) courtesy
formula, not a literal "to your service" translation used compositionally.

### 4. Plural possessive pronouns (Les 7)

1st person plural: *ons* before *het*-words, *onze* before *de*-words and all plurals (mirroring the
*welk/welke* and *elk/elke* de/het-tracking pattern). 2nd person informal plural: *jullie* as the
possessive is used directly after the personal pronoun *jullie*; 3rd person plural possessive: *hun*.
Full pronoun-paradigm table (subject/possessive, singular+plural, stressed/unstressed) is given as an
overview, consolidating and extending the singular-only overview tables from Les 2–4.

### 5. Nu vs. nou (Les 7)

*Nu* = "at this moment" (temporal). *Nou* is a discourse particle with (at least) three distinct
pragmatic functions distinguished by the book itself: (1) impatience — *kom nou!* ("come on!"), (2)
surprise/mild reproach — *wat zoeken jullie daar nou toch?*, (3) a thinking-pause filler within a
sentence — *nou... dat zit zo* ("well... here's the thing"), and can also simply substitute for *nu*
in casual speech. This is exactly the kind of discourse-particle content already flagged as
high-value in `established/002_chapter16_colloquial_speech.md` from the other Dutch grammar
reference — an independent confirmation of *nou*'s multi-functionality from a second, differently
structured source.

### 6. Praten vs. spreken (Les 7)

Largely synonymous ("to talk"/"to speak"), with *spreken* read as the more formal register variant
in shared contexts (*hij praat/spreekt met de conducteur*). But the two diverge in some fixed
collocations: *spreken* is required for naming a specific language spoken well (*hij spreekt goed
Engels*, not *praat*) and for the "may I speak to X" telephone formula (*kan ik mevrouw Kooiman
spreken?*); *praten* is preferred for volubility/duration (*die man praat de hele dag*, "that man
talks all day").

### 7. Demonstrative pronouns dit/dat/deze/die — full functional range (Les 8)

Form: *dit/dat* for *het*-words, *deze/die* for *de*-words and all plurals (same de/het-tracking
pattern as welk/elk). The book distinguishes five distinct functions for these four forms: (1)
**distance** — dit/deze = near ("here"), dat/die = far ("there"); (2) **anaphoric reference** to an
already-mentioned noun — *ik zoek Trouw; hebt u die krant?*; (3) **differentiation** between two
specific referents — *dit boek is wel interessant, maar dat boek niet*; (4) **replacement of the 3rd
person personal pronoun**, in both subject function (*waar is Jaap? die is naar een vergadering* —
using *die* instead of *hij*) and object function (*kent u mevrouw Kooiman? nee, die ken ik niet*),
with the explicit note that *die/dat* in this replacement function always occupy first position in
the sentence; and (5) **standalone/pronominal use** with no following noun at all — *welk boek? o,
dat*.

### 8. Kennen vs. weten vs. kunnen (Les 8)

Three distinct "know/can" verbs with non-overlapping senses: *kennen* = to be acquainted with a
person or place (*ken je John King?*; *ken je de Emmastraat?* = are you familiar with that street);
*weten* = to know a fact (*weet je de Emmastraat?* = do you know where it is; *dat weet ik niet*);
*kunnen* = to be able to/have the capacity to (*ik kan het lezen, maar ik kan het nog niet spreken*
— note the ability sense extending naturally to language skill, contrasted with *kennen* which
cannot take this role). *Kunnen* also serves the separate function of expressing bare possibility
(*ja, dat kan* = "yes, that's possible/allowed").

### 9. Auxiliary + infinitive: infinitive goes to clause-final position (Les 8)

When the predicate is auxiliary+infinitive, the infinitive moves to the very end of the clause:
*kunt u mijn boek niet vinden?*, *hier kunt u rustig lezen*, *u kunt hier tot vijf uur blijven* — the
verb-cluster-at-the-end pattern that becomes increasingly important once compound/perfect tenses are
introduced in later lessons.

### 10. Object pronoun paradigm: unstressed/stressed, subject/object forms (Les 9)

Full table given for the first time in this book covering unstressed vs. stressed forms crossed with
subject vs. object case, across all persons/numbers — e.g. 1sg subject unstressed *ik*/stressed *ik*
(no distinct stressed subject form for 1sg — a gap the table itself leaves unfilled), object
unstressed *me*/stressed *mij*; 3sg masc. object unstressed *hem* (informally spelled *'m*)/stressed
*hem*; 3sg fem. object unstressed/stressed both *haar*; 3pl object unstressed *ze*/stressed *die*.
Functions distinguished: direct object, indirect object, and object of a preposition.

### 11. Word order of two objects (Les 9)

When both an indirect and direct object are full noun phrases, the indirect object precedes the
direct: *ik geef het meisje het boek*. When the indirect object is pronominalized, it still precedes
the direct object: *ik geef haar het boek*. But when the **direct** object is pronominalized while
the indirect stays a full noun phrase, the indirect object must instead be marked with a preposition
and moved after the direct object: *ik geef het aan het meisje* (not *ik geef het meisje het* — this
ordering is disallowed). When both are pronominalized, the indirect object may appear as a bare
pronoun immediately after the direct object without a preposition: *ik geef het haar*.

### 12. The future: present tense as default, gaan+infinitive, zullen+infinitive (Les 9)

Dutch has no dedicated future verb form; the present tense is normally used for future reference
(*ik kom morgen* = "I'm coming/will come tomorrow"). Two periphrastic constructions add specific
shades of meaning: **gaan + infinitive** signals either (1) an intention/plan (*ga jij nog
koffiedrinken?*) or (2) the imminent onset of an action (*ga maar vast zitten* = "go ahead and sit
down [now]"). **Zullen + infinitive** signals either (1) a promise/guarantee (*ik zal het haar
vragen*) or (2) a suggestion/proposal, usually first-person (*zal ik dat even doen?*, *zullen we
weggaan?*). *Zullen* conjugates irregularly: *ik zal, je zult (also: je zal), u zult (also: u zal),
hij/ze/het zal, we/jullie/ze zullen*.

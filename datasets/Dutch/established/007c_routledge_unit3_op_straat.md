margin glosses, exercise-adjacent glosses, the "Kleren"/colours/nationalities lists), and every
grammar point named in the unit's own Topics/Functions/Grammar preview is captured below. Skipped
as low-information-density per the coverage rule: the many "Oefening" (exercise) drill items
themselves (fill-in-the-blank, matching, role-play prompts) where they only recombine vocabulary
already captured from the unit's own preceding explanatory material or vocabulary boxes; the full

vocabulary/grammar rather than trusted verbatim from the raw extraction; a handful of dialogue lines
in the two clothes-shop scenes (pp. 87–89) remain genuinely hard to reconstruct with full confidence

## Grammar points

### 1. Object pronouns: stressed and unstressed forms (p. 70)

Dutch object pronouns mostly have both a stressed form (used more in writing, and for
emphasis/contrast in speech) and an unstressed form (used more in ordinary spoken language). The
pairs given: *mij*/*me*, *jou*/*je*, *u* (no distinct unstressed form), *haar*/[*d'r*], *hem*/[*'m*],
*het*/[*'t*], *ons* (no distinct unstressed form given), *jullie* (no distinct unstressed form
given), *hen*/*ze*. Object pronouns refer to the object of a sentence — a person, thing, or idea.

**Emphasis/contrast:** the stressed form is also used to emphasize the specific person or object
being referred to (*Ik geef jou met plezier een cadeautje, maar hij krijgt niets!* — "I'll gladly
give **you** a present, but he gets nothing!"). The source notes English achieves the same effect
through intonation alone rather than a distinct pronoun form.

**Referring to things vs. people:** when the antecedent is a thing rather than a person, *hem* (for
*de*-words) or *het* (for *het*-words) is used in the singular, and *ze* in the plural for both word
genders; the specifically-people-only plural form is *hen*. In speech, the demonstrative *die* often
substitutes for *hem*/*ze* when referring to things (*De nieuwe film van Michael Moore is uit. Ja,
die wil ik heel graag zien.* — "...Yes, I'd love to see it/that").

### 2. Currency idioms surviving the switch to the euro (p. 75)

The Netherlands and Belgium both switched from their own pre-euro currencies (the Dutch *gulden*
and Belgian *frank*) to the euro, but a number of fixed idioms using the old sub-unit names
(*kwartje* "quarter-guilder," *dubbeltje* "10-cent piece") survive in the modern language even
though the literal coins no longer exist — a case of currency vocabulary outliving the currency
itself. See the vocabulary table above for the three idioms the source cites.

### 3. *Graag*: three distinct functions (pp. 76–77)

The adverb *graag* covers three separable functions: (1) politeness marker when ordering/requesting
(*Een koffie, graag* — "A coffee, please"); (2) expressing that you like doing something, when
placed after the relevant verb (*Ik speel graag piano* — "I like playing piano," lit. "I play gladly
piano"); (3) the fixed phrase *graag gedaan*, "you're welcome"/"my pleasure." These are related but
functionally distinct uses of the same adverb, not three separate homophones.

### 4. Country/nationality/language word-formation pattern (p. 80)

There is no single, fully predictable rule for deriving the inhabitant-noun (male/female forms) of
a country from the country name itself — the source explicitly says "no hard and fast rules" apply
and recommends a dictionary (citing *Van Dale's Pocketwoordenboek Nederlands als Tweede Taal*, which
has a dedicated nationalities list). However, the **adjective/language word is regular**: it is
identical to the language name and always ends in *-s* (*Nederlands*, *Belgisch*, *Frans*, *Engels*,
etc.). The source also notes a usage preference: many speakers prefer the adjective form over the
gendered inhabitant-noun even for self-identification (*Ik ben Nederlands* is preferred by many over
*Ik ben een Nederlander/Nederlandse*).

### 5. *Zitten/liggen/staan/hangen* for location, not *zijn* (pp. 82–83)

Dutch strongly prefers the posture verbs *zitten* (sitting), *liggen* (lying), *staan* (standing),
and *hangen* (hanging) over the generic *zijn* ("to be") when describing where something or someone
is located — the choice of verb depends on the physical orientation/posture of the located
object/person, not just a bare existential "is." Examples: *Het boek ligt op tafel* ("The book is
[lying] on the table"), *Die zitten op zolder* ("They're [sitting] in the attic," of children), *Die
staat in de ijskast* ("It's [standing] in the fridge," of a wine bottle), *Die hangt aan de muur*
("It's [hanging] on the wall," of a picture). The source notes *zijn* remains acceptable in the
**question** (*Waar is het boek?*) but sounds distinctly odd if used in the **answer** — the
posture verb is required there.

### 6. Expressing the future: three constructions (pp. 89–90)

Dutch has three ways to express future time, in increasing order of explicit "future-ness":

1. **Present tense** (most common) — the ordinary present tense, with a time adverb supplying the
   future reference (*Ik blijf gewoon thuis deze zomer* — "I'll just stay home this summer").
2. **Gaan + infinitive** — the infinitive takes final sentence position (*Wat ga je morgen doen? Ik
   ga mijn tante in Utrecht bezoeken.*).
3. **Zullen + infinitive** — the "proper" future tense grammatically, but the source notes it is
   *not* used very often in practice and, when it is, often carries an added nuance of suggestion,
   intention, or promise rather than bare futurity (*Zullen we een datum afspreken?* "Shall we set a
   date?"; *Ik zal mijn best doen* "I'll do my best").

### 7. Attributive adjective agreement: the *-e* ending rule (p. 91)

You add *-e* to an adjective when it is placed before ("attributively" modifying) the noun: *de
leuke film*, *een leuke film* — but *de film is leuk* (no *-e* in predicate position). **The one
exception:** when a *het*-word noun is combined with the indefinite article *een*, the adjective
stays uninflected (*een goed boek*, not *een goede boek*) — contrast *het goede boek*, where the
definite article does trigger the *-e*.

A separate, related rule the source flags: adjectives derived from **materials** always end in
*-en* regardless of the above agreement pattern — *een leren handtas* ("a leather handbag"), *een
gouden ring* ("a gold ring"), *de wollen trui* ("the woolen jumper"), *een linnen broek* ("linen
trousers"), *het zijden hemd* ("the silk shirt"). This *-en* material-adjective form does not take
the ordinary *-e* agreement ending on top of it.

### 8. Telling the time: the "X past/to half" system, with an NL/VL split for 20-minute marks (pp. 93–94)

Dutch tells time relative to the nearest hour or half-hour, not purely additively like English:
*kwart over tien* ("quarter past ten"), *half elf* ("half past ten," lit. "half eleven" — Dutch
names the half-hour by the *coming* hour, not the one just passed), *kwart voor elf* ("quarter to
eleven"). For the 20-minute marks specifically, the source documents a genuine regional split: **in
Flanders**, *twintig voor/over* ("twenty to/past [the hour]") is used directly; **in the
Netherlands**, the preferred construction instead references the half-hour: *tien over half elf*
("ten past half eleven" = 10:40) and *vijf over half elf* ("five past half eleven" = 10:35). Both
systems describe the same clock times via different reference points.

### 9. Progressive aspect: *aan het* + infinitive and *zitten/liggen/staan* + *te* + infinitive (pp. 95–96)

To express that something is happening right now (English "-ing"), Dutch uses **zijn + aan het +
infinitive**, with the infinitive in final position: *Ik ben een boek aan het lezen* ("I am reading
a book"); *Hij is koffie aan het drinken* ("He is drinking coffee").

An alternative construction uses a posture verb — **zitten/liggen/staan + te + infinitive** — which
simultaneously encodes the ongoing action *and* the subject's physical posture while doing it,
translated into English simply as "to be [verb]ing": *Hij zit de krant te lezen* ("He is reading the
newspaper," while seated); *Hij ligt naar muziek te luisteren* ("He is listening to music," while
lying down). This construction is the progressive-aspect counterpart to grammar point 5 above (the
same posture verbs, extended from static location to ongoing action).

### 10. Verbs that take *te* + infinitive vs. verbs that take a bare infinitive (pp. 96–97)

Beyond the posture-verb progressive construction, many other Dutch verbs are followed by **te +
infinitive**. The source names *proberen* ("to try"), *beginnen* ("to begin"), *hoeven* ("to need
to," negative-polarity — see point 11), and *vergeten* ("to forget") as the most common:
*Ik probeer al jaren Frans te leren* ("I've been trying for years to learn French"); *Morgen
beginnen we te werken* ("We'll start working tomorrow"); *Hij vergeet altijd de vuilnisbak buiten te
zetten* ("He always forgets to put the bin out").

By contrast, a specific set of verbs take a **bare infinitive with no *te***: the modal verbs, plus
*gaan*, *komen*, *zijn*, *laten*, *blijven*, *zien*, and *horen*. Examples: *Hij kan heel mooi
zingen* ("He can sing very well"); *Ik kom je morgen helpen* ("I'll come help you tomorrow"); *Erik
is even melk halen* ("Erik's [gone] to get milk"); *Ik laat mijn hemden strijken* ("I have my shirts
ironed"). This is a closed list to memorize rather than a predictable rule.

### 11. *Hoeven*: negative-polarity-only, opposite of *moeten* (p. 96)

The source gives an explicit usage constraint on *hoeven* ("to need to"): it is **always used in the
negative** and implies the *absence* of obligation — making it the functional opposite of *moeten*
("must/have to"), not simply its negated form used interchangeably. Example: *Je hoeft niet te
wachten, hoor* ("You don't have to wait, you know"). This is presented as a hard usage rule, not
merely a tendency.

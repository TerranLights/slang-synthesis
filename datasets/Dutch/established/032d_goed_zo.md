## Grammar points

### Imperfect tense (Имперфект) — second of the book's three past tenses
Formed by adding `-de/-te` (singular: `ik, jij, u, hij`) or `-den/-ten` (plural: `wij, jullie, zij`)
to the verb stem — the person/number distinction found in the present tense collapses in the
imperfect (no per-person variation beyond the singular/plural split). Suffix choice mirrors the
perfect participle's `'t kofschip` voiceless-consonant rule: `-te(n)` after a voiceless final
consonant, `-de(n)` otherwise. If the stem itself ends in `-d`/`-t`, an *additional* `-d`/`-t` is
still added (`praten→ik praatte`, distinct in writing but not in speech from the plural `wij
praatten`; `zieden→hij ziedde`, but perfect participle `gezied`). Devoicing carries over as in the
perfect: `geloven→hij gelooft` (present) `→hij geloofde` (imperfect) `→hij heeft geloofd` (perfect);
`reizen→hij reist→hij reisde→hij heeft gereisd`. `hebben`'s imperfect is suppletive: `had/hadden`;
`zijn`'s is `was/waren`.

**Imperfect vs. perfect — three explicit usage rules** (a genuinely deep contrastive-usage point the
source flags as difficult even for native Dutch speakers to explain to Russian learners, since
Russian's aspect system doesn't map cleanly onto it):
1. **Description vs. fact-statement.** Imperfect describes a situation/circumstance; perfect states a
   bare fact that something happened. (`Ben je gisteren nog naar de film geweest?` = asking about the
   fact of having gone; the answer `Ja, maar het was helemaal geen leuke film` shifts to imperfect
   because it now *describes* the film.)
2. **Durative/habitual/repeated vs. single/punctual.** Imperfect for an ongoing, repeated, or
   habitual action; perfect for a typically single/completed occurrence. (`We wandelden vaak naar
   Scheveningen` [habitual, imperfect] vs. `We hebben gisteren gewandeld` [single occasion, perfect].)
3. **Pure past vs. present-relevant past.** Imperfect when the action belongs wholly to the past with
   no bearing on now; perfect when the past action's *consequences* are still felt/relevant in the
   present. (`Hij werkte bij Shell` [narrating a past period] vs. `Hij heeft bij Shell gewerkt`
   [implies present-day relevance, e.g. "that's why he knows this industry / has a good pension
   now"].)

Imperfect is **obligatory** in narrated sequences of past events (a narrator recounting what happened
step by step) and when one past action is described as a backdrop/ongoing circumstance against which
another (perfect) action occurred (`Misschien heeft hij dat gezegd, maar ik werkte en hoorde niets` —
the ongoing "working/not hearing" is imperfect precisely because it's the durative backdrop to the
single perfect-tense claim).

### Plural noun formation — extended rules (supplements 032c)
- **`-eren` suffix**: a specific, memorizable set of `het`-word nouns takes `-eren` rather than
  `-en`/`-s`: `het kind→de kinderen`, `het ei→de eieren`, `het blad→de bladeren` ("leaves," as of a
  tree — contrasted with `het blad→de bladen`, "sheets," as of paper, the **same spelling** with a
  sense-dependent plural), `het kalf→de kalveren`, `het rund→de runderen`.
- Nouns ending in a diphthong/vowel-combination `-aai, -ooi, -oei` (e.g. `papegaai`) take `-en` in the
  plural (`papegaaien`), while nouns ending in `-ee`/`-eu` take `-s` with an added diaeresis-marked
  `ën` when needed (`zee→zeeën`, `idee→ideeën`).
- Some loanwords that otherwise "feel" native still take the foreign-style `-en`/`-s` split
  inconsistently — `het kantoor→de kantoren`, `de trein→de treinen`, `het plan→de plannen` vs. `het
  station→de stations` (only `station` still reads as a loanword to native speakers, per the source's
  own reflection question).
- Some nouns occur almost exclusively in the plural: `de ouders` ("parents"), `de duinen` ("dunes").
- Collective/mass nouns (`het weer`, `het brood`) normally have no plural, but can pluralize in a
  specific bounded/countable sense (`Er zijn nog twee witte broden` = "there are two more loaves of
  white bread").

### Three ways of expressing possession
1. **`van`-phrase** — maximally general, usable without restriction (`De ouders van Liesje`, `De deur
   van de auto`).
2. **`-s` genitive** — restricted to: (a) a person named by a proper noun (`Vondels vrouw`,
   `Poesjkins "Jevgeni Onegin"`), (b) a person (singular only) named by a kinship term (`vaders auto`,
   `mijn broers fiets`), (c) a city/country name (`Amsterdams/Ruslands trots`). An `-s`-marked noun
   phrase can never itself be a predicate nominal. No article precedes this construction (`Jans
   broer`, not `de Jans broer`). A following adjective in this construction takes the definite `-e`
   ending, as if the whole phrase were definite (`Perks bekende gedicht`). Spelling: apostrophe-s
   after `a, o, u` (`Carla's moeder`, `Otto's vader`); bare apostrophe (no additional s) after an
   existing `-s` (`Klaas' collega's`).
3. **Colloquial `d'r`/`z'n` construction** — restricted to people, almost exclusively spoken register,
   rare in writing (`Liesje d'r ouders`, `Jan z'n broer`).

### Reflexive pronoun (`zich`)
Full paradigm distinguishes every person (unlike Russian's invariant "-ся/себя"): `ik...me(mij)`,
`jij...je`, `hij/zij/u...zich` (colloquially `u...u` also occurs), `wij...ons`, `jullie...je`,
`zij...zich`. Placed immediately after the subject and the finite verb. Dutch reflexive verbs don't
map 1:1 onto Russian reflexive verbs in either direction (`zich herinneren` = "to remember" is
reflexive in Dutch but not in Russian; `lachen` = "to laugh" is reflexive-marked in Russian but not
in Dutch). Even when the reflexive verb is in infinitive form, the reflexive pronoun still varies by
the (implied) person of its subject (`je moet je herinneren`, `wij proberen ons te herinneren`).

### Exclamatory sentences
Begin with `wat een` when followed by (an optional adjective plus) a countable noun — even a plural
noun (`Wat een mooie vrouwen!`) — and with bare `wat` (no `een`) in every other case, including with
a mass/uncountable noun or no noun at all (`Wat mooi!`, `Wat kunnen kinderen toch flauw zijn!`).

### Substantivized infinitives
Nearly any Dutch infinitive can function as a noun (`het eten`, `het rijden`, etc.). Such
substantivized infinitives always take the definite article `het` (never indefinite/zero) and never
form a plural.

### `zeggen` vs. `praten` vs. `spreken`
Only `zeggen` is used to introduce direct speech or a "that"-clause (`Jan zegt: "Ik kom"`; `Jan zegt
dat hij komt`) — it reports the *content* of an utterance. `praten` and `spreken` both parallel
Russian "беседовать" (to converse/discourse), emphasizing the *process* of speaking rather than
conveying specific informational content; between the two, `praten` is markedly more colloquial-
register and `spreken` more literary/written-register.

### `kennen` vs. `weten`
`kennen` ≈ "to be acquainted with" (a person, place, or thing) and normally takes a direct object
noun/pronoun; `weten` ≈ "to know (a fact/piece of information)" and normally takes a clausal
complement (`Ik weet niet wat hij heeft gezegd`). `weten van` = "to know about." The source explicitly
warns not to confuse `kennen`'s conjugation (`ik ken, jij/hij kent, wij kennen`) with `kunnen`'s (`ik
kan, jij/hij kunt/kan, wij kunnen`) — near-homophonous present-tense forms with unrelated meanings.

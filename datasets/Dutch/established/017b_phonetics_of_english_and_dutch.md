## Grammar points

### Dutch fricative system — distribution, stability, and the fortis/lenis problem (§19.1)

The Dutch fricative inventory is /f, v/ (labio-dental), /s, z/ (alveolar), /ɕ, ʒ/
(alveolo-palatal, frequent allophones of /sj, zj/), /x/ with marginal /ɣ/ (velar), and /h/
(glottal, no lenis counterpart). Lenis fricatives never occur syllable-finally, and the whole
fortis/lenis contrast in this series is markedly less stable than among the stops. Concretely:
most (NL) ABN speakers don't maintain a consistent initial /f–v/ contrast in casual speech
(though it survives in careful speech); /s–z/ is more stable overall but still lost in some
dialects (notably Amsterdam — see the shop-sign example above); and /x–ɣ/ is the least stable of
all in ABN/Randstad/the North generally — /ɣ/ simply doesn't occur finally or initially for most
such speakers, with /x/ used throughout (e.g. *goed* [xut], *geen* [xeːn], *gedrag* [xəˈdrɑx]).
Outside the Randstad and North, /ɣ/ does occur medially and sometimes initially. Belgian Dutch is
described as generally more stable on all these contrasts (though some (B) AN speakers use a
palatal [ç] rather than velar/uvular for the fortis member, and either merge or maintain /x–ɣ/
depending on sub-variety) — Belgian problems tend to come from assimilation effects instead
(cross-referenced to §20.8, below).

Per-fricative articulatory detail: Dutch /f, v/ are articulated with more retracted contact than
English /f, v/; initial /f/ has notably more breath effort and friction than English /v/ and can
even have full voicing throughout, while medial /v/ is weaker and always fully voiced. Dutch
/s, z/ are articulated with the blade/front of the tongue (not the tip, which may rest inactively
against the teeth) and produce a "graver" (lower-pitched) friction than English — the book notes
this can make Dutch /s/ sound like English /ʃ/ to an English ear in clusters, final position
after rounded vowels, and after /r/; Belgian /s, z/ are articulated more like their English
counterparts and lack this grave quality. The alveolo-palatal /sj, zj/ realizations [ɕ, ʒ] lack
English-style lip-rounding/protrusion (a weak inner-lip rounding at most) and are restricted to
loanwords (*chocolade, chauffeur* from French; *shag, show* from English; *sjoelen* from Frisian;
*sjofel* from Yiddish) and assimilation outputs (*tasje* → [ˈtɑɕə], *kies je* → [ˈkiɕə]);
word-final /ɕ/ is rare (*hasj, crèche* are among the few examples). Velar /x/ (with marginal /ɣ/)
has its precise place of articulation vary regionally: energetic post-velar/pre-uvular in (NL)
ABN, further-forward and less energetic true-velar or post-palatal in southern Netherlands and
Belgium (the "zachte g," see vocabulary table). /h/ is often voiced [ɦ] in practice despite being
nominally the glottal fricative, and is lost in a specific set of Dutch weak forms (*heb, heeft,
had, hij, het, haar, hem*) — non-standard Belgian varieties (especially West Flanders) frequently
lack /h/ entirely or swap /x/↔/h/ (*hoed*/*goed* example above); the rest of the Netherlands
(except Zeeland and East Groningen) has no "h-dropping" comparable to English/Welsh accents.

### Dutch stop system — distribution and detailed articulation (§19.2)

Inventory: /p, b/ (bilabial), /t, d/ (alveolar), [tɕ, dʒ] (alveolo-palatal, frequent allophones),
/k/ with marginal /g/ (velar). Lenis stops never occur word-finally except as an assimilation
output. Key articulatory contrasts with English: Dutch /t, d/ use a larger tongue-contact area
(blade/front against the alveolar ridge, tip often inactive/lowered) than English's smaller
tip-based contact; the frequent /tj/ sequence (*katje, had je*) surfaces as the alveolo-palatal
affricate [tɕ], and in certain high-frequency words/sequences (*beetje, weet je, moet je, dat
je*) even as a palatal fricative [ç] resembling the German ich-Laut. /k/'s place of articulation
shifts with the following vowel (advanced/pre-velar before front vowels as in *kies*, retracted
post-velar before back vowels as in *kop*), with an even more fronted post-palatal affricate
realization before /j/ (*takje, beekje, koekje*). /g/ — beyond its marginal-phoneme status —
also occurs as a plain voiced allophone of /k/ in specific words (*kerkdienst, weekdag,
breekbaar*); many speakers replace the marginal phoneme itself with /k/ or /ɣ/ instead.

**Voicing, aspiration, tension:** Dutch fortis plosives are voiceless except under assimilation
and, crucially, carry **no aspiration at all** (voice begins immediately at release) — a sharp
contrast with English fortis plosives. Dutch fortis stops are also articulated with markedly
greater tension/energy than their lenis counterparts, and are described as tenser overall than
the corresponding English fortis stops (visible as lip compression for /p/, larger tongue-contact
area and brisker release for /t, k/). Lenis stops are fully voiced only between voiced sounds;
word-initially they're typically partially devoiced (*doos* [d̥oːs], *been* [b̥eːn]) though less so
than in English, with full voicing restored in emphatic/citation forms. Following fortis stops,
/ʋ/ often partially devoices to something an English ear hears as similar to /f/ (*kwik, twaalf*).
Dutch never uses glottal stop to reinforce final fortis stops (unlike English) but does use it as
a vowel-initial onset word-initially (*op eigen initiatief*).

**Overlapping/homorganic stop sequences:** a sequence of two homorganic stops usually reduces to
one (*stamppot* → [ˈstɑmpɔt]); when the first is voiceless and the second voiced-homorganic, the
first regularly voices and then deletes (*tot dan* /tɔd dɑn/ → /tɔ dɑn/). English, by contrast,
keeps both consonants as a single prolonged hold stage with pre-glottalization if the first is
fortis (*bookcase, cub pack*). For *non*-homorganic stop sequences, Dutch shows extensive
closure-overlap (the second closure forms before the first releases) — more so within a word than
across a word boundary — e.g. *taktiek, stokbrood, optellen, opdagen, tot kijk, meetbaar*, all
shown with an audible closure-overlap notation; the first stop in such sequences becomes a
devoiced lenis (not fully voiced) under the second's influence. French, unlike Dutch or English,
keeps two plosives fully sequential/non-overlapping; English virtually always overlaps.

**Approach/release types:** nasal and lateral approach patterns resemble English (*kamp,
vandaar, speelt, speelde*). Nasal release occurs in homorganic stop+nasal sequences (*opmaken,
snotneus*), but release into a fully syllabic nasal (as typical of English/German) is absent from
both (NL) ABN and (B) AN — though it's heard in northern/eastern Netherlands dialects and East/West
Flanders (*kopen* [ˈkoːpm̩], *praten* [ˈpraːtn̩], vs. standard [ˈkoːpə(n), ˈpraːtə(n)]). Lateral
release occurs when alveolar /t, d/ precede /l/ (*eetlepel, hardlopen, atlas*), but — unlike
English — without devoicing/friction of the following /l/ and without release into a syllabic
lateral (contrast Dutch *sleutel* with English *hurtle*'s devoiced syllabic [l̥]). Lateral escape
after /p, k/ similarly keeps Dutch /l/ voiced and non-fricative, unlike English (*lepel* vs.
*papal*; *hekel* vs. *tackle*).

**Accent variation:** northern/eastern Netherlands accents have English-like aspirated plosives
[pʰ, tʰ, kʰ]; some Randstad urban accents (Leiden, Rotterdam) have affricated [ts] resembling
frequent English initial /t/ allophones. Southern Netherlands and most Belgian varieties have very
tense fortis and strongly voiced lenis plosives. Glottalization of medial/final stops is reported
in some accents, e.g. Zeeland.

### Dutch nasal system (§19.3)

Inventory /m, n, ŋ/ with the frequent alveolo-palatal allophone [ɲ]; /ŋ/ never occurs
syllable-initially. Place of articulation for /m, n, ŋ/ corresponds to /b, d, g/ respectively. /n/
after back vowels tends to be pharyngealized (like dark [ɫ]); front vowels, especially /ɛ/, lower
and centralize before /n/. The /nj/ sequence surfaces as [ɲ] (*kastanje, champagne*, or across a
boundary as in *kan je, meen je*), with the preceding vowel strongly nasalized, raised, and given a
palatal off-glide for a diphthongal effect — with some speakers dropping tongue/palate contact
almost entirely. In clusters like *handje, hondje* the nasal+stop sequence surfaces as
[ɲtɕ]-type combinations; in *Hansje, onsje* it's a nasal+fricative sequence [ɲɕ] — and again the
nasal consonant itself may simply elide, leaving only a strongly nasalized vowel.

### Dutch approximant system (§19.4)

**Lateral /l/:** articulated with the blade/front of the tongue against the alveolar ridge. In
(NL) ABN, /l/ is clear before vowels and dark before consonants/pause; intervocalically it varies,
tending clear except after open back vowels (*holle, getallen* — dark). In final position many
speakers realize /l/ as a fully pharyngealized vocoid with no actual alveolar contact — an
unrounded back-vowel-type quality that can produce a narrow diphthongal glide after back vowels
(*dol, hal, riool, koel*). (B) AN /l/ tends to be post-palatalized/velarized rather than
pharyngealized, with little clear/dark contextual variation. Clusters /lf, lp, lm, lk, lx/ show
schwa-insertion (svarabhakti) word-finally in *all* varieties including both standard forms
(*twaalf, help, helm, melk, Belg*) — the book explicitly flags this as often mistakenly considered
substandard when it is not; non-standard varieties extend the insertion to word-medial contexts
too (see *Hilversum* etc. above), which the book notes "is often imitated for humorous effect."
Accent variation: dark [ɫ] in all contexts in Rotterdam/Amsterdam; less-dark velarized /l/ in
Belgium; fully clear [l] in all contexts in accents near the German border (e.g. around Nijmegen).

**Palatal approximant /j/:** occurs only pre-vocalically; frequently realized as a voiced palatal
fricative [ʝ], especially in emphatic speech. In *-je* diminutives, it devoices to [ç] (*raampje,
hofje*).

**Labio-dental approximant /ʋ/:** occurs in all contexts but rarely finally, alternating there
with /u/ (*ruw, sneeuw, nieuw*; *murw* is a rare case of genuine final /ʋ/). In (NL) ABN it's
articulated near the place of D /f, v/ with a "brushing" lip movement and, in connected speech,
generally has voiced friction [β̝] — which is why English speakers perceive this Dutch sound as
similar to their own /v/. (B) AN typically has a voiced bilabial approximant [β], and many
Belgian speakers use a labial-palatal approximant [ɥ] (like French *huit*) especially before close
front vowels (*weten, wit*). After /t/ or /k/ (*twee, kwis*), /ʋ/ becomes a devoiced fricative
[f̥]-like sound that English speakers again tend to hear as /f/. Southern Netherlands varieties
often resemble (B) AN here; Surinamese Dutch is notable for a labial-velar approximant [w] like
English.

**Uvular/alveolar /r/:** genuinely two articulation places in competition — uvular (huig-r) and
alveolar (tong-r) — with uvular gaining ground in the Netherlands (especially the Randstad) but
not in Belgium. (B) AN standardly uses an alveolar tap in all positions. Netherlands Dutch is far
more complex and context-dependent: pre-vocalically, either uvular approximant/fricative or
alveolar tap; in clusters after /p, t, k, x/, uvular /r/ devoices with weak friction; word-finally,
trills are rare outside emphatic speech, and the most frequent present-day (NL) ABN/Randstad
allophone is actually a pre-velar "bunched" approximant with retroflex-like resonance (compared
to a common American-English "retroflex" /r/, though not a true retroflex articulation). Other
word-final outcomes: a close [ə]-type off-glide after close/close-mid vowels (*mier, muur, moer,
meer, deur*, with the preceding vowel lengthened/centralized); a short glide after non-close
back/central vowels (*spoor, maar, kerk, sport*) that can occasionally cause confusion in connected
speech between pairs like *maar*~*maai*, *kerk*~*cake* (though citation forms keep the contrast);
or outright elision in casual/unstressed contexts. Clusters /rk, rp, rm, rn, rf, rx/ get
word-final schwa-insertion in (NL) ABN (*kerk, dorp, arm, urn, erf, erg* → [kɛrək], etc.), extended
to medial contexts non-standardly (*morgen* [ˈmɔrəxə]). Extensive accent variation is tabulated:
alveolar taps/trills in Noord-Holland (incl. Amsterdam), Utrecht, the northeastern provinces, and
much of Belgium (esp. East/West Flanders); true retroflex /r/ in Leiden; uvular predominance in
Ghent/Bruges and The Hague (with common final-/r/ elision in The Hague); mixed uvular/alveolar in
Rotterdam; especially strong, scrapy uvular fricatives ("brouwende r," see vocabulary table) in
Gelderland and the southern Netherlands provinces; a pharyngeal-approximant type in Limburg.

### Dutch marginal consonants and marginal consonant sequences (§19.5)

/g/ (lenis velar plosive) occurs as a marginal phoneme confined to loanwords (*golf, gala,
guerilla, guillotine, gletsjer, gobelin, goal, grill, grapefruit, gangster, leggings*), frequently
replaced by /k/ (*goal* → [koːl], *grapefruit* → [ˈkreːpfrut]) or by /x/ (*gala, grill, gletsjer*)
in (NL) ABN and dialects. Belgian varieties use /ɣ/ rather than /g/ or /x/ in these same
loanwords, meaning /g/ isn't marginal-phoneme status at all for the overwhelming majority of
Belgian speakers — it's simply an allophone of /k/ there. (The word *garage* is now pronounced
with a fricative by almost all speakers.) [ʒ] (alveolo-palatal, from /zj/) occurs in French
loanwords and Russian transliterations (*genre, geste, journalist, jus, beige, Brezjnev,
Zjitomir, Zjirinovski*) and in the English loanword *jam*; in frequent items like *jus* it's often
replaced by /sj/ in the Netherlands, while less-common words tend to retain /zj/. [dʑ] (alveolar
alveolo-palatal affricate, lenis counterpart of /tj/) is used by some careful speakers in English
loanwords (*jazz, jingle, jungle, junkie, jukebox, discjockey, job, Jimmy, Jeff*) and the
Indonesian loanword *Djakarta*; it's increasingly replaced in the Netherlands by /tj, j,/ or /sj/
(*jingle* → [ˈtɕɪŋgəl], *job* → [jɔp], *Jimmy* → [ˈɕɪmi]), while Belgian speakers almost always
retain /dʑ/.

### Dutch assimilation patterns (§20.8)

**Assimilation of energy** (the dominant assimilation type in Dutch, per §20.10 below):

1. *Lagging assimilation of energy* — fortis plosive/fricative + lenis fricative → fortis + fortis
   fricative, e.g. *uitzicht, gemakzuchtig, taakverdeling, 't eens zijn, half vijf*.
2. *Leading assimilation of energy* — fortis plosive/fricative + lenis plosive → lenis + lenis,
   e.g. *schipbreuk, opdragen, eetbaar, spoeddebat, vakbond, kerkdienst, afbreken, beloofde, bos
   bloemen, busdienst*. With some speakers, fortis fricatives lenite before *all* voiced
   non-fricative sounds (not just lenis plosives) — including before /m, n, l, r, ʋ/ and even
   vowels (*afmaken, afnemen, afleren, afremmen, afwas, afbreken, afdoen*). An exception: a small
   closed set of grammatical words (*dit, die, dat, daar, dus, d'r, dan*) resist this leading
   lenition (*is dit juist, op die manier, moet dat dan, of dat*) — though speakers in the south
   of the Netherlands and Belgium may apply leading assimilation to these too, or use both
   patterns in free variation.
3. *Intervocalic assimilation of energy* — /f, s/ can lenite between vowels (*af en toe, rasecht*);
   /t/ sometimes voices between voiced sounds too (*tandarts, rondom, Noordeinde, toentertijd,
   uiteindelijk, had 'm*) — this pattern is particularly common in the southern Netherlands
   provinces.

**Assimilation of place** — leading place-assimilation of final alveolars: (a) alveolar→bilabial
before /p, b, m/ (*voetpad, kladpapier, kladblok, in principe, loonbelasting, van mij*, and the
compound-affected case *stuntprijs, landbouw, ontmoedigen* where /n/+/t/ are both affected); (b)
alveolar→velar before /k, x, ɣ/ and marginal /g/ (*tot kijk, inkomen, kleingeld, een goal*).
Separately, /t, s, n/ before /j/ give the alveolo-palatal realizations [tɕ, ɕ, ɲ] discussed
already in §19.2/19.3 — the book notes these aren't technically phonemic assimilation (since
[tɕ, ɕ, ɲ] aren't phonemes) but are flagged here anyway because Dutch-speaking learners
transfer this pattern into certain English contexts. Different assimilation types (energy, place,
manner) can co-occur in a single form: *voetbal* (place+energy), *laat maar* (place+energy+manner).

### Dutch elision patterns (§20.9)

1. /t/ elides when medial in certain consonant sequences (*rechtstreeks, luchtpost, de helft van*)
   — sometimes combined with energy assimilation (*postzegel, postbus*).
2. One consonant elides from a sequence of two identical consonants, sometimes derived through a
   multi-step process from originally non-identical consonants (*clubbestuur* → *klubbestuur* →
   *klubestuur*; *voetbal* → *voebbal* → *voebal*; similarly *groot deel*, *best doen*, *postbus*).
3. Syllable-final /k/ sometimes elides between /ŋ/ and another consonant (*ik denk dat, dankbaar,
   bankbiljet*).
4. /n/ elides in unstressed final *-en* (frequent in plurals and verb forms: *beren, smeren, open,
   morgen, tegen*) — retained if *-en* is followed by a vowel (*midden in*, either pronunciation).
5. /ə/ elides in unstressed syllables between a consonant and /l/ or /r/, especially in casual
   speech (*makkelijk, interessant, gelijk, belachelijk, ongelooflijk, daarom*).

### Comparison with Dutch and advice for the English learner (§20.10)

The book's central contrastive claim: **English assimilation is primarily of place; Dutch
assimilation is primarily of energy.** This is presented as the single most important thing a
Dutch-speaking learner of English needs to internalize about assimilation, because importing
Dutch-style energy-assimilation into English destroys the fortis/lenis contrasts that carry real
lexical/grammatical weight in English — the book tabulates concrete predicted Dutch-learner
errors in both directions: fortis wrongly lenited (*keep my place* → *"kib maI pleIs*, *get them*
→ *"ged Dəm*, *back door* → *"bæg dO:*, etc.) and lenis wrongly fortited (*rib cage* → *"rIp
keIdZ*, *flagpole* → *"flæk pəUl*, *breathe slowly* → *"bri:T sləUlI*, etc.). The second error type
is described as related to a more general difficulty Dutch learners have producing final lenis
consonants in *any* context, not just before a following fortis.

Ten specific transfer points are itemized as pedagogical advice: (1) avoid energy assimilation in
English, especially in stressed syllables; (2) Dutch's tendency to reduce identical-consonant
sequences to one consonant is a genuine problem for English plosive sequences, where
glottal-reinforcement (if the first is fortis) or vowel-length-plus-prolonged-hold (if lenis) must
be produced instead; (3) Dutch /tj/→[tɕ] transfers incorrectly onto English /t+j/ (which should
instead become /tʃ/ via reciprocal assimilation — *did you* should be [dɪdʒu:], not the Dutch-style
[dɪtɕu:]) — the same transfer risk applies to /d+j/→/dʒ/, and to nasal-final sequences like *can't
you* which risk incorrect elision/nasalization; (4) English /s+j/→/ʃ/ (*pass your*) parallels
Dutch /sj/→[ɕ] usefully, but only across word boundaries — within-word assimilations frequently
heard from Dutch learners (*suitable, pursuit, assume, presume*) fall outside RP and should be
avoided, generally in favor of a plain /s/ or /z/ pronunciation; (5) lagging assimilation of /ð/ in
unstressed syllables is a genuinely useful pattern to imitate, since it resembles a Dutch pattern;
(6) elision of /t, d/ in English consonant sequences is similarly useful/transferable; (7) elision
of /k/ in *asked*, /θ/ in *months*, /ð/ in *clothes*, and /v/ in *of* before /ð/ (*six of those
cakes*) are all high-value, easy wins since they resemble native English patterns anyway; (8)
Dutch learners often incorrectly elide syllable-final /k/ after /ŋ/ before another consonant in
English (*I think that*, *Pink Floyd*, *punk music*) — this elision, permissible in Dutch, is
**not** permissible in English; (9) /ə/-elision before /r/ in English suffixes *-ory/-ery/-ally/
-ary* (*temporary, literary, stationery*) is common among educated native speakers and thus
acceptable to adopt; (10) lenis-to-fortis assimilations are only acceptable in unstressed
syllables in English — never in stressed syllables, where (as with all fortis-to-lenis
assimilations) the learner should avoid them entirely.

### Articulatory setting: the overall speech-organ posture contrast (§21.1)

"Articulatory setting" is the book's term for the continuous, recurrent overall posture the
speech organs (lips, tongue, mouth, throat, velum, larynx) hold throughout speech — distinct from
the articulation of any individual phoneme, and varying not just between languages but between
varieties of the same language (explicitly noted between (NL) ABN and (B) AN, and among regional
accents in both). The chapter's central contrastive comparison is (NL) ABN vs. RP, with (B) AN
differences noted wherever they diverge from ABN.

**Overall characterization:** English (RP) has loose lips, relaxed tongue, relaxed throat
muscles; Dutch (ABN) has tight lips, tense tongue, tense throat muscles, often with pharyngeal
constriction. This tension difference is most visible in stop consonants — Dutch has firm
closures with no aspiration; English has looser "leaky valve"-type closures that produce
aspiration. For fricatives the pattern *reverses*: Dutch tends toward lax, loose fricative
strictures (hence the "graver" /s, f/ noted in §19.1) while English fricatives are firmer/sharper.
(B) AN plosives resemble (NL) ABN, but (B) AN fricatives are closer to English (relatively sharp).

**Tongue setting:** English has a tapered tongue-tip setting, using the tip/blade for alveolar
articulations with small contact areas (including dental /θ, ð/); Dutch (both ABN and AN) has a
blunter lingual setting using the blade/front of the tongue, with the tip itself often
inactive/depressed behind the lower front teeth (especially in the Netherlands). (NL) ABN gives
the tongue's posterior (back/root) much greater importance than English does — active for /x/,
/k/, /ŋ/, and for the pharyngealization behind dark [ɫ], /n/, and the /ɔ/ vowel, plus uvular /r/
for many speakers; English's only comparable back-tongue activity is velar /k, g, ŋ/ plus weak
velarization of dark [ɫ]. The book frames this as (NL) ABN's "center of gravity" for tongue
activity being posterior, vs. English's anterior — while (B) AN differs from both, having its
own center of gravity front-central (post-palatal rather than velar /x, ɣ/, less tongue-root
retraction, post-palatalized rather than pharyngealized dark /l/, alveolar tong-r predominating
over uvular huig-r).

**Lips, velum, larynx:** (NL) ABN holds lips more firmly with inner rounding and little
protrusion for consonants; RP has laxer, loosely rounded lips with real protrusion for /w/, /ɔː/,
and the "trumpet-shaped" palato-alveolar consonants /ʃ, ʒ, tʃ, dʒ/; (B) AN sits closer to English
on protrusion but keeps Dutch-level lip tension. The velum is laxer in (NL) ABN (giving
semi-continuous background nasalization), even more so in (B) AN (one of the signature features of
Belgian voice quality); RP has firmer velic closure, though semi-continuous nasalization occurs
in some RP speakers and is common regionally, carrying stigma when it does. Laryngeal setting:
(NL) ABN has a raised larynx, little creaky voice, relatively high pitch range (especially
noticeable in male speakers), and only intermittent optional glottal-stop onsets to
syllable-initial vowels; English has a lowered larynx, laxer glottal setting, characteristic
creaky voice, a lower overall pitch range, and — despite less frequent prevocalic glottal stop
than Dutch — prominent recurrent glottalization as an essential marker of syllable-final fortis
stops in modern RP. (B) AN resembles (NL) ABN in lacking creaky voice/glottalization but has less
pronounced larynx-raising/pitch elevation, and adds a characteristic breathy-voice huskiness as its
own signature Belgian voice-quality feature.

### Advice to learners on articulatory setting (§21.2)

The book argues that working on whole-setting adjustments can outperform phoneme-by-phoneme drill,
since setting features are supra-segmental and improve entire stretches of speech at once. Seven
concrete adjustments are recommended for Dutch-speaking learners of English: (1) adopt a tapered
tongue-tip setting for alveolar/dental consonants; (2) use laxer tongue/lip settings for stops
(aiding real aspiration on fortis /p, t, k/); (3) — Netherlands students specifically — avoid
tongue-root retraction/pharyngealization (improves dark [ɫ] and the LOT vowel); (4) — Belgian
students specifically — adopt firmer velic closure to avoid excess nasalization; (5) — Netherlands
students specifically — adopt lip-rounding with protrusion (improves /w/, /ʃ, ʒ, tʃ, dʒ/, and the
THOUGHT vowel); (6) adopt lowered larynx with a laxer glottal setting, employing both creaky voice
and glottal reinforcement of syllable-final /p, t, k, tʃ/; (7) — Belgian students specifically —
avoid excessive breathy-voice huskiness. A summary comparison table (Table 21.1) cross-tabulates
lips/tongue-tip/tongue-back/soft-palate/larynx/articulator-tension for Dutch vs. English,
restating the points above in a single reference grid.

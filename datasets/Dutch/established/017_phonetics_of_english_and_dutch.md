## Grammar points

### Fortis/lenis: overview of the Dutch–English contrast (§6.1)

Dutch and English both use a fortis (voiceless/tense/strong) vs. lenis (voiced/lax/weak) contrast
among stop and fricative consonant pairs, but the contrast works differently in each language.
English has more independent acoustic signals for the fortis/lenis distinction than Dutch,
especially among plosives. In the Dutch fricative series the opposition is itself unstable:
the majority of Netherlands (ABN) speakers no longer maintain a consistent /x/–/ɣ/ contrast (e.g.
*lachen* "to laugh" vs. *vlaggen* "flags"), and many Netherlands speakers also lack a genuine
/f/–/v/ contrast except in careful/formal speech; some accents (e.g. Amsterdam) don't distinguish
/s/ and /z/ either. Belgian Dutch is described as considerably more stable on all these
fricative contrasts.

The most consequential structural difference: **Dutch has no fortis/lenis contrast in
word-final position at all** — *noot* "nut" and *nood* "need, distress" are true homophones
despite the spelling difference, and *rib*/*lip* rhyme perfectly. English, by contrast, does
contrast fortis/lenis word-finally (*life* vs. *live*, *rate* vs. *raid*), which the book
identifies as a real production problem for Dutch learners of English — Dutch pronunciation of
English words like *ride*, *night*, *his*, *kiss*, *sad* can fail to preserve the English-final
contrast, which the authors note is audible even in Dutch-authored English song lyrics where
supposed rhymes don't actually rhyme to native English ears. Finally, Dutch frequently *loses* a
fortis/lenis contrast through regressive assimilation across morpheme/word boundaries (e.g.
*opdoen* → [ˈɔbdun], *opzet* → [ˈɔpsɛt]) — a process the book says is very rare in English (full
assimilation mechanics deferred to §20.8).

### Vowel length as the real fortis/lenis signal in English syllable-final position (§6.2)

For Dutch learners, the single highest-value phonetic cue in English syllable-final position is
**not** voicing itself but **vowel length**: English vowels are markedly shortened before fortis
consonants and kept at full length before lenis consonants (and in open syllables) — a checked
vowel before a lenis consonant can be as long as, or longer than, a free vowel before a fortis
consonant. This shortening effect (termed *pre-fortis clipping*, citing Wells 1990) extends to a
nasal or lateral immediately preceding a fortis consonant too (*shelf* vs. *shelve*, *bent* vs.
*bend*, *bolt* vs. *bold*). The book flags English /aɪ/ as a specific trouble spot: the vowel is
much shorter before fortis consonants than the substitute vowel most Dutch learners default to.

### English fortis stops carry extra markers: pre-glottalization and aspiration (§6.3)

Beyond vowel length, English fortis stops /p, t, k, tʃ/ in final position are often reinforced by
a preceding glottal stop ("pre-glottalization" / "glottal reinforcement" — very characteristic of
RP, e.g. *shop window* → [ˈʃɒʔp ˈwɪndəʊ]). Initially in a stressed syllable, English fortis
plosives /p, t, k/ are also strongly aspirated (a brief period of voicelessness after release).
Neither effect has a real Dutch equivalent at the same intensity — the recommended production
strategy for a Dutch learner is to keep the strong Dutch airstream force but relax lip/tongue
tension.

### Voicing of lenis consonants is context-dependent, not absolute, in both languages (§6.4)

The book argues the traditional "voiceless/voiced" label is somewhat misleading for both English
and Dutch fortis/lenis pairs, because lenis consonants are only *potentially* voiced, not always
actually voiced. In English, lenis consonants are fully voiced only between vowels/other voiced
sounds; syllable-initially after silence or a voiceless sound they undergo *initial devoicing*,
and syllable-finally before a voiceless consonant or pause they undergo *final devoicing*. Even
when a final lenis consonant is fully devoiced, English speakers can still distinguish it from a
true fortis consonant via the other correlated cues (vowel length, articulatory energy, absence
of glottal reinforcement) — e.g. *par* vs. *bar*, *rope* vs. *robe*, *mat paint* vs. *mad
painter*. Nasals, the lateral /l/, and approximants /w, j, r/ do not undergo this devoicing
pattern at all and stay fully voiced regardless of position.

### Comparison with Dutch and pedagogical advice (§6.5)

In Dutch, citation-form/careful-speech /b, d/ carry more initial voicing than their English
counterparts, but the gap narrows substantially in connected speech (Dutch stops are described as
not sounding "over-voiced" to English ears the way French /b, d, g/ can). Voice is also frequently
lost through assimilation in Dutch (§20.8). For fricatives, the risk runs the *opposite* direction
from what a learner might expect: because many Netherlands speakers already under-differentiate
/f–v/, /s–z/, /x–ɣ/ toward the fortis member in their own Dutch, the practical danger when
learning English is *under-voicing* English lenis fricatives — Belgian learners, whose Dutch
fricative contrasts are more stable, are instead more likely to run into problems from
assimilation effects specifically. The chapter's real pedagogical takeaway for the word-final
context (where Dutch simply has no contrast to draw on): the learner must actively attend to
vowel length, articulatory energy, and pre-glottalization as substitute cues, since voicing alone
is an unreliable signal even in English.

### Dutch consonant inventory — place/manner/energy classification (Table 6.1, §6.5)

The book tabulates the full Dutch consonant inventory by place and manner, with fortis/lenis
pairing noted (fortis listed first in each pair):

- **Plosive:** /p b/ (bilabial), /t d/ (alveolar), /k/ with marginal /g/ (velar).
- **Affricate:** /tɕ dʒ/ (alveolo-palatal) — described as marginal/loanword-influenced.
- **Nasal:** /m/ (bilabial), /n/ (alveolar), /ɲ/ (alveolo-palatal), /ŋ/ (velar).
- **Trill:** /r/ — realized at either alveolar or uvular place depending on speaker, and at either
  place potentially as a trill, fricative, or approximant; the book explicitly says no single
  norm can be stated for this phoneme, though the trill is the traditional reference form.
- **Fricative:** /f v/ (labio-dental), /s z/ (alveolar), /ɕ ʒ/ (alveolo-palatal), /x/ with marginal
  /ɣ/ (velar), /h/ (glottal).
- **Central approximant:** /ʋ/ (labio-dental), /j/ (palatal).
- **Lateral approximant:** /l/ (alveolar).

Footnoted caveats on the table: the /f–v/ contrast is regularly lost in connected speech for many
(NL) ABN speakers but more likely maintained for (B) AN speakers; /x–ɣ/ is absent for the vast
majority of (NL) ABN speakers; /g/ is called a marginal phoneme overall. (The parallel English
table, 6.2, is skipped here as English-only content per the coverage rule, except to note it
serves as the direct contrastive counterpart the Dutch table is designed to be read against.)

### The Dutch vowel system — five-way classification (§14.1)

The book classifies the full Dutch vowel inventory into five categories, cross-referenced by
example word (capitalized in the source as the citation form):

1. **Checked steady-state:** /ɪ, ɛ, ɑ, ɔ, ʏ, ə/ — as in *zit, zet, zat, zot, nut, werkelijk*.
2. **Free steady-state:** /i, y, u, aː/ — as in *zie, nu, moe, la*.
3. **Free potential diphthongs:** /eː, øː, oː/ — as in *zee, beu, zo*.
4. **Free essential diphthongs:** /ɛi, œy, ʌu/ — as in *mei, lui, kou*.
5. **Vowel sequences:** /aːi, oːi, ui, iu, yu, eːu/ — as in *saai, mooi, boei, nieuw, ruw, meeuw*.

Beyond this core system, Dutch has a set of **marginal vowels** confined to loanwords (mostly from
French): the long vowels /ɛː, œː, ɔː/ (as in *beige, oeuvre, rose*) and the nasal vowels /ɛ̃, ɑ̃,
ɔ̃/ (as in *bulletin, genre, plafond*) — see §14.5 below for their instability.

**"Potential" vs. "essential" diphthong terminology, explained.** /eː, øː, oː/ are termed
*potential* diphthongs because (NL) ABN realizes them as genuine closing diphthongs in practice,
while (B) AN and some non-Randstad Netherlands varieties realize them as true steady-state
vowels with minimal or no glide — the underlying phoneme is the same, but its phonetic
realization is diphthongal only in some varieties. This diphthongization before /r/ is lost in
all varieties. /ɛi, œy, ʌu/, by contrast, are termed *essential* diphthongs because the glide is
a genuinely obligatory part of the articulation in all standard varieties, even though some
regional dialects reduce it to something closer to steady-state.

### Dutch checked vowels — description and contextual variation (§14.2)

Descriptions given for each: /ɪ/ ("zit" vowel, front-central above close-mid, unrounded); /ʏ/
("nut" vowel, front-central close-mid, rounded); /ɛ/ ("zet" vowel, front open-mid, unrounded);
/ɑ/ ("zat" vowel, back open, unrounded); /ɔ/ ("zot" vowel, back above open-mid, rounded); /ə/
("werkelijk" vowel, mid-central, occasionally rounded).

Five systematic contextual-variation rules are given:

1. **Schwa /ə/** is normally unstressed but can be stressed for emphasis (e.g. *Dit is dé manier
   om beroemd te worden*, "This is THE way to become famous"). Many Dutch speakers intuitively
   feel /ə/ to be the unstressed allophone of /ʏ/ — evidenced by Dutch children's spelling errors
   (citing van Rijnsoever 1978), where a word like *binnen* /ˈbɪnə/ gets spelled with a final
   `<u>` rather than `<e>`. Word-finally, /ə/ is realized closer and more rounded (more like /ʏ/)
   than elsewhere in a word.
2. **Before dark [ɫ]**, checked front vowels /ɪ, ʏ, ɛ/ are lowered and centralized — most
   noticeably for /ɛ/, to the point that the Dutch pronunciation of the loanword *Shell* can sound
   to English ears like English "shall." A similar lowering happens before /n/.
3. **Before the nasals /m, n, ŋ/**, checked vowels are strongly nasalized, and in unstressed
   syllables /n/ itself is often effectively dropped for many speakers (e.g. *onmogelijk*
   → [ɔ̃moɣələk], *fantastisch* → [fɑ̃tɑstis], *tentamen* → [tɛ̃tamə]). In many dialects this /n/-loss
   extends to stressed syllables too, especially after open/open-mid vowels (e.g. *dansen* →
   [dɑ̃sə], *kunst* → [kʏ̃st], *gezond* → [xəzɔ̃t]).
4. **Before velars**, checked vowels are raised and somewhat centralized (e.g. *zacht, hek, jong*)
   — Dutch /ɔ/ in *jong* is noted as sounding similar to English /ʊ/ to many English speakers.
   Before the alveolo-palatal clusters /sj, tj, nj, nsj, ntj/, all checked vowels are raised, and
   the back vowels /ɑ, ɔ/ additionally develop a palatal glide (a diphthongal effect), e.g. *Anja*
   → [ˈɑ̟jɲa], *bandjes* → [ˈbɑ̟jɲɟəs], *onsje* → [ˈɔ̟jɲɟəs].
5. **Before /r/** in (NL) ABN, all vowels are lengthened and more central, typically developing a
   centering off-glide toward [ə]-like quality — with possible blurring of contrasts with
   corresponding free vowels in this environment specifically. Even so, the length-based contrasts
   /ɛ–ɛː/ and /ɔ–ɔː/ are not fully lost (*ver* stays distinct from *fair*-type long-vowel words,
   *Cor* from *corps*).

**Accent variation is extensive and explicitly tabulated per vowel** — this is one of the source's
highest-value contributions for a comparative typology profile, since it's genuine dialectal
annotation the source itself marks (not inferred). Summary by vowel: /ɪ/ is closer/fronter in The
Hague and Rotterdam, and in some Belgian accents (Antwerp); more open in Dordrecht, Nijmegen,
affected ABN, and East/West Flanders. /ʏ/ is closer in Amsterdam, The Hague, Rotterdam, Antwerp;
more open in the southern provinces and affected ABN. /ɛ/ is closer/centralized in Amsterdam;
more open (an [æ]-type quality) in Dordrecht, Zeeland, Noord-Brabant, Limburg, Ghent, Bruges, and
affected ABN — particularly before /l/ and /n/. /ɑ/ is fronter outside the Randstad generally, with
Utrecht especially front (approaching [a]); centralized in Amsterdam; fronter still in Belgian
varieties (especially Antwerp, Brabant — to the point some fronted realizations can sound to
English ears close to the TRAP vowel); fully back and closer (sometimes lip-rounded, sounding
close to English LOT) in Rotterdam and Leiden. /ɔ/ is tense with strong lip-rounding generally,
raised/centralized in Amsterdam, more open outside the Randstad.

### Dutch free vowels — description and contextual/accent variation (§14.3)

**Close free vowels /i, y, u/** (as in *zie, nu, moe*): word-finally these can end in a voiceless
off-glide (e.g. *drie* → [driiʖ], *nu* → [nyyʖ]) — the book notes *KNMI* frequently ends in a
voiceless glide sounding almost like a palatal fricative [ç] (citing Moulton 1962). Before /r/,
all three lengthen in every variety, and in (NL) ABN/most Randstad accents they additionally
centralize with a centering glide (e.g. *hier, duur, boer*). These vowels, short in most
Netherlands Dutch contexts, are frequently longer across (B) AN and most other Belgian varieties;
/u/ is fronter in Antwerp/Brabant specifically.

**Open free vowel /aː/** (as in *la*) is described as the *only* true long steady-state vowel in
Dutch — never realized as a diphthong. In (NL) ABN it's fronted/raised before /r/ and retracted
before dark [ɫ]. Extensive accent variation: more retracted [ɑː] in many urban (Amsterdam,
Utrecht, Antwerp) and rural dialects both in NL and Belgium; raised [aˑː] in The Hague and
affected ABN especially before /r/ (e.g. *maar*, *Wassenaar*); a front CV-4-type quality in
Groningen.

**Close-mid potential diphthongs /eː, øː, oː/** (as in *zee, beu, zo*): as noted in §14.1, these
are overwhelmingly realized as narrow diphthongs in (NL) ABN but as steady-state vowels in (B) AN —
the book cites Henry Sweet's 1877 *Handbook of Phonetics* as evidence the ABN diphthongization is
not a recent innovation, contrary to a popular belief that it is. Before /r/ they're raised with a
centering glide; the /oː/–/ɔ/ contrast (as in *zo* vs. *zot*) can blur before /r/ in certain
Randstad dialects (Leiden, The Hague, Rotterdam) — e.g. *wordt* and *woord* can sound similar in
quality though a length distinction typically survives. Similarly the /eː–i/ and /øː–y/ contrasts
can blur before /r/ in (NL) ABN/Randstad varieties. Accent variation: little or no glide in the
southern/eastern provinces (except Groningen) and most Belgian varieties; extremely narrow
diphthongs in affected ABN (especially older speakers); notably wide glides with open-mid
starting points in urban Randstad accents, which can make *zee, leuk, boot* sound close to the
ABN pronunciation of *zij, luik, bouwt*.

**Open-mid essential diphthongs /ɛi, œy, ʌu/** (as in *mei, lui, kou*): starting points are closer
and the glide shorter in (B) AN than (NL) ABN; in (B) AN, /ʌu/ tends to have rounding throughout.
Before dark [ɫ] in (NL) ABN, /ɛi/ centralizes with a narrower glide (e.g. *zeil*). Accent
variation: some older Netherlands speakers and many Belgian speakers round /ʌu/ throughout;
Leiden, Rotterdam, and Belgian speakers often lack lip-rounding in the first element of /œy/,
giving [ɑ̈y]. Southern-province and Belgian dialects generally have less extensive glides. The
Hague has genuinely steady-state realizations [ɛː, œː, ɑː] in place of the essential diphthongs
(e.g. *Rijswijkseplein, broodje ei met ui*). Broad Amsterdam realizes /ɛi/ as [aː], famously
producing near-homophony between *Leidseplein* and a hypothetical "*Laadseplaan*"-type form, and
between *geitenkaas* "goat cheese" and *gatenkaas* "cheese with holes." Utrecht and Rotterdam have
more open first elements (e.g. *Feyenoord* → [ˈfaijənoːrt]).

### Dutch free vowel sequences (§14.4)

/iu, yu, eːu, aːi, oːi, ui/ (as in *nieuw, ruw, meeuw, saai, mooi, boei*) are treated as unitary
phonemes rather than sequences of two separate vowel phonemes, because — unlike a genuine
two-syllable vowel-hiatus sequence — both elements fall within a single syllable; the book
contrasts syllable-internal *loei* against two-syllable *Louise*, and *haai* against *Haïti* (where
a glottal stop may even mark the syllable boundary), to justify this analysis. Prominence
distribution differs by sequence: /ɛi/-type diphthongs are diminuendo (prominence on the first
element), while /aːi/ has roughly equal prominence on both elements. /iu, yu, eːu/ sometimes
alternate with a realization using the labio-dental approximant /ʋ/ instead of the [u] off-glide
(e.g. *nieuw* → [niu] or [niʋ]) — this /ʋ/-substitution is described as very common medially.

### Dutch marginal vowels — loanword instability (§14.5)

The long vowels /ɛː, œː, ɔː/ and nasal vowels /ɛ̃, ɑ̃, ɔ̃/ occur only in French-origin loanwords and
are actively unstable — the book frames this as an ongoing nativization process, not a fixed part
of the core system. Common loanwords are being reassigned to native short-vowel slots (e.g.
*crème, crêpe, crèche* increasingly pronounced with plain /ɛ/); many Netherlands (especially
non-ABN) speakers select /ɔ/ or /o/ for long /ɔː/, producing homophony with native words (*corps*
≈ *Cor* or *koor*; *zone* ≈ *zonen*/*zonnen*); Belgian speakers typically select /o/. Nasal vowels
are similarly being replaced by an oral-vowel-plus-nasal-consonant sequence for most speakers
(e.g. *restaurant* → [rɛstoʁɑnt]-type), with usage explicitly said to vary speaker to speaker
(Exercise 11 lists *branche, Française, entree, pantalon, dansant, enfin, enquête, bonbon, timbre,
monseigneur, pardon, chanson, nuance* as the loanword set where this variability shows up).

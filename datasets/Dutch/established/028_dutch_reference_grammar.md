# Dutch — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Dutch Reference Grammar (Donaldson)

**Source:** Donaldson, B. C., *Dutch Reference Grammar* (Martinus Nijhoff, The Hague, 1981), full
book — 137 PDF page-images (each a two-printed-page spread), printed pages 6–275 (Preface through
Index). **No text layer — vision-read throughout**, rendered at 130 dpi via `pdftoppm`. Printed
pagination maps to PDF page N as roughly `printed_left_page ≈ 2N` for content from p.24 onward
(verified empirically against ~15 sampled spreads); front matter (Preface/Acknowledgements/
Abbreviations, pp.8–12) sits at a slightly different offset. All printed page numbers cited below
were read directly off the rendered page images, not computed from the formula.

**Non-redundant-supplement scope decision (per `../../00_Reference_Extraction_Spec.md`).** This is
the *fourth* Dutch grammar reference extracted for this project, after `established/010`–`013b`
(Basic/Intermediate Dutch workbooks + the two-part *Dutch: A Comprehensive Grammar*), which between
them already systematically cover essentially this book's entire chapter list (cases, articles,
demonstratives, noun gender/plural/diminutives, all pronoun classes, adjectives, adverbs, the full
verb system, conjunctions, prepositions, word order). Given that near-total structural overlap, this
extraction did **not** re-read the whole book at uniform depth. Instead:

- **Fully read and extracted:** Ch.7 "Nouns" §7.1–7.5 (gender rules, plural-formation subrules,
  feminizing suffixes, possession, diminutives — pp.27–49), Ch.14 "Numerals" in full (pp.222–234),
  Appendix 1 "Letter writing" (pp.244–247), Appendix 2 "Proper nouns" (countries/nationalities/
  adjectives/languages and geographical areas, pp.248–259), Appendix 3 "Common Dutch abbreviations"
  (pp.260–264). These sections either go substantially deeper than the workbooks' treatment (Ch.7's
  exception-riddled plural/gender subrules) or cover ground the workbooks never touch at all
  (numerals idiom/register detail, letter-writing formality tiers, the country/nationality reference
  table, the abbreviations list) — genuinely new, not duplicative.
- **Spot-checked (2–5 sampled page-spreads each) for register/archaic-tagged content and skipped
  where purely redundant:** Ch.6 Demonstratives, Ch.8 Pronouns, Ch.9 Adjectives, Ch.10 Adverbs,
  Ch.11 Verbs, Ch.12 Conjunctions, Ch.13 Prepositions, Ch.16 Negation. Only genuinely new material —
  explicit archaic/literary/colloquial register tags, Belgian-vs-Holland usage splits, and idiom
  lists not found in `010`–`013b` — was extracted from these; standard paradigm explanations already
  covered elsewhere were not re-transcribed.
- **Skipped entirely as fully redundant:** Ch.1–3 (Pronunciation/Spelling/Punctuation — covered by
  `established/001`), Ch.4–5 (Cases/Articles — covered by `012`), Ch.15 "Er" (covered by `011` Unit
  10), and the Index.

**Vision-reading note.** Print quality throughout is a clean, consistently typeset monospace-adjacent
serif font with no OCR-style ambiguity on any page read. One small mark resembling a pencil
tick/squiggle appears in the bottom margin of the printed-p.244/245 spread (Appendix 1, near "Met de
meeste hoogachting"); it does not overlap any text and was treated as possible marginalia and
excluded — nothing was read from it. No other handwritten marginalia was found on any page read for
this extraction.

## Grammar points

### Ch.6 Demonstratives — idiom residue (p.26)

The demonstrative paradigm itself (*deze/dit/die/dat*) duplicates `established/010` Unit 9 and
`012` Ch.6 and is not re-extracted. Two standing-expression uses not previously captured: **archaic
case-form fossils** — *een dezer dagen* "one of these days," *op 13 dezer* "on the 13th of this
month" (a letter-dating convention), *destijds* "at that time," *dientengevolge* "as a result of
that" — and the archaic demonstrative **gene**, used opposite *deze* to mean "the former" vs. "the
latter" (*Wij wonen aan deze zijde van de rivier, onze vrienden aan gene* — "we live on this side of
the river, our friends on that side").

### Ch.7 §7.1 Rules for the gender of Dutch nouns

Donaldson's gender chapter is organized as an explicit rule-list (not just paradigm tables), pitched
as "a few reasonably reliable rules... some hard and fast, others a little vague" rather than an
exceptionless system — the book is upfront that many Dutch nouns simply have to be memorized. The
common-gender (*de*) rules cluster around **semantic categories**: names of people and most animals,
trees/flowers/fruit, virtues/vices, tools/instruments derived from verbs (*-el/-er*), letters of the
alphabet, numerals, musical instruments — alongside **phonological/morphological categories**:
words ending in unstressed *-e, -te, -heid/-nis, -ing/-ling, -age/-ij/-ei/-ie/-iek/-teit/-theek*, and
*-schap* nouns denoting a condition (as opposed to neuter *-schap* nouns denoting a function/office —
a genuine minimal-pair-by-suffix-semantics distinction the book flags explicitly, e.g. *de
dronkenschap* "drunkenness" [condition, common] vs *het priesterschap* "priesthood" [function,
neuter]). The neuter (*het*) rules mirror this structure: diminutives, infinitives-as-nouns, young
animals, metals/minerals, compass points, *-um/-isme/-dom* words, and collective *ge-...-te* nouns.
**Compound nouns take the gender of their final element**, with a short named-exception list (e.g.
*het ogenblik* "moment" despite *de blik* "glance" as the final element's usual gender). A distinct
subsystem covers **nouns with two genders**: some pairs differ in gender with no meaning difference
(*het/de affiche*), while a longer, more useful list differs systematically in *meaning* by gender
(*de bal* "ball" vs *het bal* "a dance"; *de/het idee*, *de/het jacht*, etc.) — and every precious-
stone name in Dutch follows this same split productively (common gender = the individual stone,
neuter = the stone as raw material).

### Ch.7 §7.2 Plural formation — the exception layer beneath the basic rule

The basic rule (default *-en*, with named *-s*-plural categories) is already covered by
`established/001`. What this book adds is a **denser layer of subrules and named exception-lists**
within each plural type: nine separate -s-plural trigger categories (unstressed *-el/-en/-er*;
*-erd/-aard* masculine-being nouns; all diminutives; foreign *-a/-o/-u* nouns needing an apostrophe
to block vowel-shortening misreadings; foreign *-e/-ie* nouns; loanwords still felt as foreign;
*-ier/-oor* nouns split by person-vs-thing reference; a short native "male being" list; military
ranks) and a parallel set of *-en*-plural spelling-change rules (vowel-dropping in the newly-open
syllable; *s→z* and *f→v* voicing in intervocalic position; consonant-doubling to preserve a short
vowel; stressed *-or* nouns shifting stress to the penultimate syllable on pluralizing). Two smaller
closed classes sit outside both systems: a genuine **meaning-differentiated -en/-s doublet set**
(*hemelen* "heavens" vs *hemels* "canopies," *letteren* "literature" vs *letters* "letters of the
alphabet") and the archaic **-eren plural class** (*kinderen, beenderen, bladeren, eieren,
gelederen*) — a residue of Common Germanic *-iz* plurals cognate with English "children," "oxen."
Also newly captured: §7.2.5's short list of **English plurals that are grammatically singular in
Dutch** (*de economie* "economics," *de politiek* "politics," *de bril* "spectacles") and §7.2.4's
irregular *-man* plurals (*-lieden* formal vs. *-lui* informal — an explicit formality-tier split on
plural choice itself, not just on vocabulary).

### Ch.7 §7.3–7.4 Feminizing suffixes and possession

Seven distinct feminizing suffixes are catalogued (*-e, -es, -esse, -euse, -in, -ster, -trice*), each
tied to a specific source-language/morphological trigger (native derived-from-verb agents take
*-ster*; French *-eur* agents take *-euse*; a handful of nationalities/animals take *-in*), with the
book candidly noting "there are actually very few rules for their use... one can best simply learn
the feminine equivalents by heart." Possession: the English-style *'s* survives in Dutch but only
productively after proper names and close-relative-plus-possessive nouns (*mijn moeders keuken*);
all other cases require a *van*-construction. Colloquial speech instead productively substitutes the
pronoun *z'n/d'r* for the apostrophe-s (*Hans z'n vriend*) — explicitly marked as colloquial, distinct
from the "standard" written apostrophe rule.

### Ch.7 §7.5 Diminutives — extending `established/003`'s coverage

`established/003` (from *Dutch: An Essential Grammar* Ch.22) already covers the core allomorph
selection rules (*-je/-tje/-etje/-pje*) and the phonological triggers behind them in comparable
depth to this chapter — that overlap is real and this file does not re-derive it. What's genuinely
new here: (1) a **-kje allomorph** for *-ing*-final nouns changing *g→k* (*koning → koninkje*),
distinct from the four allomorphs `003` documents; (2) a full **regional-variant inventory** —
standard -je, southern/Belgian -ke (with -ske/-pke phonetic sub-variants), western-Netherlands
colloquial -ie (also used in the north "for humorous effect" in cultured speech), and the archaic
-ken relic surviving mainly in fixed names (*Manneken Pis*); (3) the **quantity-noun → single-unit
semantic shift** (mass nouns like *bier, ijs, snoep* taking a diminutive to mean "one item/serving
of X" — *een biertje* "a glass of beer"), a genuinely distinct semantic function from `003`'s
documented uses; (4) **diminutivization of parts of speech other than nouns** — adverbs (productive,
spoken-register, softening intensity: *warmpjes, stilletjes*), adjectives-used-as-nouns
(*een witje*), numerals (*in mijn eentje*), pronouns (*ietsje*), a single lexicalized verbal-particle
diminutive (*een moetje* "a shotgun marriage"), and preposition-derived nouns (*een ommetje maken*).
This part-of-speech-spanning productivity is the single most striking addition beyond `003`'s
noun-only treatment.

### Ch.8 Pronouns — formality/archaism distinctions not in the workbook chapters

Beyond the paradigm tables (already covered by `010`/`011`/`012`), this chapter is explicit about
**where written-prescriptive Dutch and actual spoken Dutch diverge**: the *hun* (indirect object) vs
*hen* (direct object/after preposition) distinction is presented as a genuine difficulty even for
the book's target audience, with *ze* recommended as a safe unstressed fallback "if one is in doubt."
Common-gender-noun pronoun reference has its own three-way split: colloquial Holland uses *hij* for
essentially everything (including formerly-feminine abstract nouns), formal-written Holland uses
*zij* for those abstracts, and **Belgium alone retains the historical masculine/feminine distinction
in living colloquial use** — an explicit regional-register fact not found in the other established
Dutch sources. The **independent possessive pronouns** have a genuine two-tier paradigm (*de/het
mijne* formal vs. *die/dat van mij* "usual"), with the fully-formal *de/het hunne* flagged as sounding
"particularly stilted" even within the formal register. The **relative-pronoun periphery** — *hetgeen/
hetwelk* as archaic written alternatives to *wat/die*, and formal *welk(e)* replacing *die* in writing
— comes with explicit archaic/formal tags the workbook sources don't carry. Finally, §8.5.10's
**adjectival-participial-adjunct construction** (folding a relative clause's content into a pre-nominal
participle, e.g. *het gisteren door mij ingebonden boek* instead of *het boek dat ik gisteren inbond*)
is tagged as formal style, "often in journalese" — a genuinely distinct register-marked syntactic
alternative not covered elsewhere in this project's Dutch corpus so far.

### Ch.9–10 Adjectives/Adverbs — the *rot-*/intensifier slang layer

The most directly slang-relevant find in this spot-check: §9.8.5 explicitly calls the pejorative
noun-prefix ***rot-*** (*een rothuis* "a horrible house," *een rotdag* "a ghastly day," predicative
*rottig*) "rather colloquial but very common nowadays" — a period-marked (1981) colloquial
intensifying/pejorative prefix, directly the kind of "mechanics of slang formation" data this
project's later mechanics-analysis phase will want. Similarly, Ch.10's intensifying-adverb section
flags ***ontiegelijk*** as "a rather modern word... increasing in popularity" and ***enorm*** as
"very *in* at the moment" — two explicit fashionable-slang usage notes with a publication-year
anchor (1981), plus the prescriptive note that using **als** instead of **dan** after a comparative
"is considered rather colloquial." The adverb-formation section (§10.7) also catalogues a full
productive/unproductive suffix inventory (-s, -jes, -gewijs, -halve, -lijks, -lings, -waarts, -weg)
each with its own register tag (literary, no-longer-productive/archaic, or "particularly common in
the spoken language") — useful register-stratified data not present in the other Dutch established
files' adverb coverage.

### Ch.11–13 Verbs/Conjunctions/Prepositions — spot-check findings only

These three large chapters were only spot-checked (their paradigms and core rules are already fully
covered by `established/011`/`012`/`013`/`013b`). What surfaced: two verbs explicitly tagged "(coll.)"
in the alphabetical verb+preposition-object lists (*roddelen over* "to gossip about," *prakkezeren
over* "to think of/ponder on") — directly slang-relevant lexical items; the explicit note that
splitting a separable verb's prefix away from a following modal infinitive (*Ik zou hem op kunnen
bellen*) is "very common in speech" but avoided in formal writing; and, from the prepositions
chapter, the systematic "double preposition written solid as an adverb" pattern (*achterin,
achterom, onderaan*, etc.) and the systematic omission of English "of" after a quantity word (*een
fles bier*, not **een fles van bier*) — both genuinely systematic patterns not previously documented
this explicitly in this project's Dutch corpus.

### Ch.14 Numerals — the fullest new content in this extraction

Read in full since no prior Dutch established file covers numerals/money/measurement register in
this depth. Highlights beyond the raw cardinal/ordinal/fraction tables: the historical *v/z*-
devoicing in the decade-numerals 40/50/60/70 (*feertig, fijftig, sestig, seventig* — a genuine
sound-change fossil the book explains has "historical reasons"); the deliberate *zeven → zeuven*
mispronunciation convention used specifically when reading numbers aloud (phone numbers etc.) to
avoid confusion with *negen* "nine"; and — most valuable for this project's slang-mechanics purposes —
**§14.7's explicit colloquial money-slang set**: *piek* "one guilder," *ton* "100,000 guilders," and
*mille* "a thousand (guilders)," all three flagged as colloquial in the source itself. This is a
clean, source-confirmed example of informal-register number/currency slang, directly the kind of
data this project's later synthesis phase will want to compare against derived-language slang
patterns.

### Ch.16 Negation — idiom residue beyond the core geen/niet system

The core *geen* vs *niet* rule is covered elsewhere in this project's Dutch corpus; new here is the
closed list of fixed *hebben*-idioms (*dienst/dorst/haast/honger/slaap hebben*) that are negated with
*geen* rather than *niet* even though they don't superficially look like indefinite-object
constructions, and the *pas* ("not until") vs. literary *eerst* alternation, explicitly marked
"never *niet...tot*."

### Appendix 1 — Letter writing as an explicit four-tier formality register system

Not covered by any prior Dutch established file. The chapter lays out a clean, source-explicit
four-tier formality ladder running in parallel across both salutations and closings: (1) fully
formal/unknown addressee (*Geachte heer/mevrouw* ... *Met de meeste hoogachting*, with *Zeer geachte
heer B.* reserved for genuine hyper-politeness toward high-status addressees), (2) formal-but-
acquainted (*Geachte heer/mevrouw* ... *Met vriendelijke groet(en)*, the closing shifting once
contact has been established), (3) acquaintances/friends (*Beste heer Meijer* / *Beste Joop* ...
*Vriendelijke/Hartelijke groeten*), and (4) close friends/family (*Lieve Anneke* ... *Groetjes*). This
is a genuinely new, cleanly tiered register dataset — exactly the kind of explicit formality-graded
vocabulary set the coverage rule prioritizes extracting.

### Appendix 2 — Proper nouns (countries/nationalities/languages/geography/historical figures)

A systematic four-column reference table (country / male inhabitant+plural / female inhabitant /
adjective-language) spanning Europe, Africa, Asia, and a separate geographical-features list
(oceans, seas, mountain ranges, historical regions) plus a short section on how foreign historical
figures' names are given Dutch forms (*Karel de Grote* "Charlemagne," *Kroetsjev, Galileï, Calvijn*).
Nothing comparable exists elsewhere in this project's Dutch corpus. Of incidental historical-context
interest: since the book was published in 1981, several entries reflect **that era's political
geography rather than today's** — *Bondsrepubliek Duitsland*/*Democratische Republiek* (divided
Germany), *Sovjet-Unie*, *Belgisch-Kongo* alongside modern *Zaïre*, and *de Oostbloklanden* "Iron
Curtain Countries" — tagged `archaic (historical)` in the vocabulary table above rather than treated
as current-Dutch vocabulary.

### Appendix 3 — Common Dutch abbreviations

A ~200-entry alphabetical abbreviation list (institutions, broadcasting associations, currency/
measurement abbreviations, academic titles, political parties, historical-period terms like *NSB(-er)*
"Dutch Nazi (Party member)"), with the source's own three-way typology of how an abbreviation is read
aloud (spelled out as full words; pronounced letter-by-letter as its own word, e.g. *KLM* → "KA-EL-EM";
or pronounced as a word in its own right, e.g. *EHBO*, *AVRO*). Not covered by any prior Dutch
established file; genuinely useful reference data for later sociolinguistic/register work since
abbreviation-reading conventions are themselves a register marker.

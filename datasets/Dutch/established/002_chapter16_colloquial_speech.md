## Grammar points

### 16.1 Particles — general characterization (p.125)

Colloquial Dutch usage — spoken or written — conveys not just factual information but attitude:
urgency, casualness, politeness, uncertainty. One major device for this is the *particle*: a short
word (usually one syllable), unstressed, sitting in an inconspicuous position in the sentence, that
adds no propositional content but colors its tone. The source states Dutch relies on particles "to
a far greater extent than English" (p.125). It illustrates the effect with an English minimal pair —
"Put that package on the table!" (abrupt) vs. "Just put that package on the table." (softened) — then
gives several Dutch examples (e.g. *"Zou ik jouw mobieltje/gsm'etje even mogen gebruiken?"* = "Could I
use your cell phone for a bit?"). The chapter focuses on five of the most common particles: **eens,
even, maar, toch,** and **wel** (p.126).

### 16.1.1 Particles in imperative sentences (pp.126–127)

Particles generally soften the abruptness of a command:
- **eens** softens a request/command, e.g. *"Kijk eens! Kom eens kijken!"* = "Just look! Come and have
  a look!"
- **maar** conveys mild encouragement or permission, e.g. *"Ga maar naar bed"* = "Go on to bed."
- **even** adds a tone of casualness implying the action takes only a short time, e.g. *"Wacht eens
  even"* = "Hold on a minute."
- **toch** adds a tone of urgency or irritation, e.g. *"Kom toch!"* = "Come on, for heaven's sake!"

### 16.1.3 Other uses of particles in statements and questions (pp.128–129)

- **eens**, unstressed, and often spelled *'s* or *es* in casual/informal writing, conveys "sometimes"
  or "for a change" — e.g. *"Ben je wel eens in Brussel geweest?"* = "Have you ever been to Brussels?"
- **even** conveys "just a moment" or simply softens the force of a verb — e.g. *"Ik zal even
  kijken..."* = "I will just have a look..."
- **eens** and **even** frequently combine: *"Mag ik eens even naar je knie kijken?"*
- **maar** in declarative sentences conveys something like "just," often carrying a tone of "went
  ahead and" or resigned acceptance of something unwelcome — e.g. *"Ik at het maar op"* = "I just went
  ahead and ate it."
- **toch maar** occurs as a fixed combination, e.g. *"Ik ga toch maar wel naar het congres"* = "I've
  decided to go to that conference after all."
- **wel** (unstressed) expresses mild reassurance — e.g. *"Ze zullen vandaag wel komen"* = "They will
  no doubt come today."
- **wel** (stressed) expresses the opposite of what was expected — e.g. *"De pannenkoek was wel
  lekker. Zo lekker zag hij er helemaal niet uit."* = "The pancake really tasted good. It didn't look
  all that tasty."
- **wel** (stressed) can directly contradict a preceding negation, functioning almost as a one-word
  affirmative rebuttal — e.g. *"Ik vind margarine niet lekker. Zij wel."* = "I don't like margarine.
  She does."

### 16.1.4 The particle *toch* in detail (pp.130–131)

- **toch** (unstressed) = "after all" — e.g. *"Dat heb ik je toch gisteren gezegd"* = "I told you that
  yesterday, after all."
- **toch** (stressed) = an expressed or implied negative, "nevertheless" — e.g. *"Het mocht niet, maar
  ze hebben het toch gedaan"* = "They weren't allowed to, but they did it anyway."
- **toch** functions as a forceful confirmation following a negation — e.g. *"Nee, dat kan helemaal
  niet. Jawel, het kan toch!"* = "No, that can't be done at all. Oh yes, it can!"
- Further uses noted by the source: marking a warning (*"Kijk toch uit! Let toch op!"*) and forming an
  informal tag-question-like confirmation seeker appended to a statement (*"We hebben vandaag een
  test, toch?"* ≈ "..., aren't we?"; *"De bussen rijden toch wel?"* ≈ "..., aren't they?").

### 16.2 Word order and combinations of particles (pp.131–132)

- **Placement rule.** A particle normally stands *before* an indefinite or indirect object, but
  *after* a definite phrase. Contrast *"Je moet eens een boek kopen"* (indefinite object *een boek*
  follows the particle) with *"Je moet dat boek eens kopen"* (definite object *dat boek* precedes it).
- **Combinations.** Particles stack into short strings that layer their individual nuances: *eens
  even, wel eens, maar eens even, dan maar, toch maar eens even,* etc. (e.g. *"Ga toch maar eens even
  rustig zitten"* = "Just go sit quietly for a minute.")
- **Beyond the five core particles**, a wider set of adverbs — *al, dan, dus, nou, nu, ook, soms,
  zeker* — is used in similar tone-coloring ways. The source explicitly states the best way to learn
  their use is by observing native speakers in conversation and reading, not by rule-memorization
  (p.132) — signaling that this whole particle/adverb system sits at the "learned by immersion,"
  informal-register end of the language, distinct from the codified core grammar covered in most of
  the rest of the book.

### 16.3 Spoken language versus written language (pp.133–134)

Every language has a gap between how words and expressions are actually spoken and how they are
formally written down, because some spoken forms are "too informal when written" (p.133). The source
states this gap has narrowed over time in both English and Dutch, but that Dutch retains "a very
clear-cut distinction between 'written' and 'spoken' language, especially if the writing is formal"
(p.133). Crucially, the distinction is not purely lexical — it also surfaces as syntax/grammar
differences. Paired written/spoken examples given by the source (see Vocabulary table above for the
individual lexical items):

- Possessive pronoun form (*de mijne*) in writing vs. prepositional possessive (*van mij*) in speech.
- Relative pronoun + preposition (*met wie*) in writing vs. pronominal adverb (*waarmee*) in speech.
- Optional omission of the expletive/anticipatory *er* in an inverted existential sentence when
  written ("In Nederland wonen veel mensen") vs. its normal inclusion in speech ("...wonen er veel
  mensen").
- Formal *echter* ("however") in writing vs. colloquial *maar* in speech.
- Formal subordinating conjunctions (*aangezien* "since," *ofschoon* "although," *opdat* "so that") in
  writing vs. their everyday equivalents (*omdat, hoewel, om...te*) in speech.
- Formal obligation modal *dienen* ("dient u...") in writing vs. everyday *moeten* in speech.
- Formal *desalniettemin* ("nevertheless") in writing vs. colloquial *maar toch* in speech.
- Standard *elkaar* ("each other") in writing vs. colloquially contracted *mekaar* in speech.
- An optional *om* before a *te*-infinitive is often present in spoken Dutch and can be dropped in
  formal written style: *"Het valt niet mee (om) die deur te openen."*

## Usage Tier findings

This dispatch specifically tested whether Chapter 16 ("Colloquial speech and writing") would surface
`Usage Tier` values beyond `core` — a gap flagged in the prior Serbian/Croatian/Bosnian and Hungarian
test runs, where grammar-reference vocabulary skewed almost entirely to `core`. **It did, strongly,
and along two distinct axes:**

1. **`colloquial`** — used for the chapter's five headline discourse particles (*eens, even, maar,
   toch, wel*), their fixed combinations (*toch maar, eens even*), the tag particle *hoor*, and the
   secondary adverb group (*al, dan, dus, nou, nu, ook, soms, zeker*) that the source itself says
   functions "in similar ways" to particles and is "best learned through exposure" rather than
   formal rule — precisely the kind of register marking the dispatch was looking for. It was also
   used for the spoken-register half of several §16.3 written/spoken lexical pairs (*van mij,
   waarmee, maar toch, mekaar*, and *maar* in its "however" sense), since the source explicitly
   labels these as the informal/spoken counterpart of a more formal written item.

2. **`formal`** — a value not seen at all in a prior test run's grammar-reference extraction, but
   clearly warranted here. §16.3's written/spoken table gave a genuine, source-drawn register
   contrast: *de mijne, wie* (relative, post-preposition), *echter, aangezien, ofschoon, opdat,
   dienen,* and *desalniettemin* are all explicitly presented by the source as the more formal,
   written-register member of a pair. This is a real, non-forced register distinction — several of
   these words (*desalniettemin, ofschoon, opdat*) genuinely do carry a stiffer, more
   literary/bureaucratic flavor in actual Dutch, not just an artifact of the table's layout.

3. **`core` was still used, deliberately, for roughly a third of the §16.3 pair items** (*omdat,
   hoewel, om...te, moeten, elkaar, er*) — these sit in the source's "spoken" column alongside
   genuinely colloquial items, but are themselves register-neutral, everyday words usable
   comfortably in formal writing too (unlike, say, *mekaar*, which really is a marked
   colloquialism). Tagging them `colloquial` purely because of which column they happened to land in
   would have been forcing the source's binary table onto words that don't actually carry that
   register restriction — the extraction spec's "follow what the source actually says, don't force
   it" instruction was applied here by judging each item's real register behavior rather than
   mechanically inheriting the table's column.

**Net finding:** a dedicated "colloquial register" chapter is exactly the kind of source material
this pipeline needs to escape the `core`-only default — of this file's ~34 vocabulary rows, only
about a third ended up tagged `core`, with the rest split fairly evenly between `colloquial` and
`formal`. This is worth promoting as a methodology note: future chapter-selection for other languages
should prioritize any chapter a grammar reference itself frames around register/formality
(colloquial-speech chapters, formal-writing chapters, dialect-comparison chapters) over purely
sequential coverage, since those are disproportionately likely to yield non-`core` Usage Tier data.

## Grammar points

### Finite verb, stem, infinitive (p. 4–5)

The finite verb is the verb form that changes for person and tense; in a statement it always
occupies the sentence's second position (`Ik werk in de informatica`). Most regular verbs form
their stem — the form used with `ik` — by dropping the infinitive's `-en` ending (`werken` → `werk`),
with spelling adjustments per the standard closed/open-syllable rules (cross-reference:
`established/001`'s Ch. 2). Irregular verbs don't follow this and must be learned by their individual
forms; the source points to its own back-of-book irregular-verb appendix (Appendix 1).

### Present-tense subject/verb agreement paradigm (p. 4, 22–23, 29)

The book teaches the present-tense conjugation pattern piecemeal per pronoun group rather than as
one paradigm table: `ik` + bare stem; `je/jij/u` + stem+`t` (regular verbs) or irregular forms for
`zijn`/`hebben`; `hij/zij/het` + stem+`t`; `wij/jullie/zij` + full infinitive (stem+`en`). This is a
task-driven presentation (the reader fills in a blank paradigm table across three separate
exercises) rather than a single explicit statement, but the underlying pattern is the familiar
Dutch present-tense agreement paradigm.

### Job titles and workplace prepositions (p. 6)

Dutch omits the indefinite article before a bare job title (`Ik ben directeur`, not `een directeur`)
but adds the definite article when naming an industry (`Ik werk in de informatica`). Two
prepositions both translate roughly as "for": `voor` ("for," `Ik werk voor een bank`) and `bij`
("at/with," `Ik werk bij een modebedrijf`) — genuinely distinct collocations, not free variants.

### Formal vs. informal address and greeting choice (p. 7–8)

Register in greetings/leave-taking is conditioned by more than word choice alone: the source notes
that longer sentences read as more formal than short ones, and that lexical choice alone can shift
register even holding grammar constant (e.g. `meid` reads more informal than `vrouw` for "woman").
Greetings and leave-takings each have their own formal/neutral/informal three-way split (see
Vocabulary above); `doei` is explicitly flagged as not used in Flanders — a rare explicit
Netherlands/Flanders usage-boundary note for a register-tagged colloquial item.

### houden van + preference statements (p. 9)

"To like/love" is expressed with `houden van` — the preposition `van` is obligatory and is a fixed
part of the construction, not a free-standing "of."

### Age statements (p. 9)

Age is stated as a bare number, a number + `jaar` (always singular even for plural ages), or a
number + `jaar oud` — all three are interchangeable, `jaar` never pluralizes in this construction.

### Direct-address constructions beyond questions/commands (p. 15–16)

Beyond ordinary questions and imperatives, a bare `je/jij/u` + finite-verb statement (with rising
intonation in speech) serves four distinct pragmatic functions the source names explicitly: (a) a
compliment or direct criticism about someone's characteristics/behavior (`Je spreekt goed
Nederlands`), (b) checking that information already held is correct (`U bent meneer Davids?`), (c)
inviting someone to expand by echoing their own statement as a question (`U helpt de bejaarden?`),
(d) expressing surprise via tone of voice alone, with no distinct syntactic marker (`Jij werkt bij
de Hema?!`).

### Modifying adverbs: hedging vs. sarcastic assumption (p. 17–18)

Time-referencing adverbs (`soms` "sometimes," `nu` "now," `nogal` "quite") soften or qualify a
direct statement so it reads less bluntly. Separately, `zeker` ("surely/I expect") shifts a
statement into either (a) a genuine checked assumption (`U bent zeker de moeder van Jaap?`) or (b) a
sarcastic/mocking assumption about someone's character based on a stereotype (`Jij leest zeker de
Telegraaf?` — "I bet you read the [tabloid] Telegraaf"). The same grammatical device (`zeker` +
statement-as-question) carries opposite social tones depending entirely on context — a genuine
register/pragmatics distinction not marked by any difference in form.

### Prepositions and fixed prepositional collocations (p. 19)

Many Dutch verbs take a specific, often non-obvious preposition as a fixed part of their meaning
(`houden van`, `denken aan`, `ervaring hebben met`, `kijken naar`, `rijden in`) — the source
explicitly warns that the English translation frequently omits a preposition entirely or uses a
different one, so these must be memorized as fixed units rather than derived compositionally.

### Articles: de, het, een (p. 19–20)

`Een` is the indefinite article ("a/an"); `de` and `het` are both "the," splitting nouns into
**common** (`de`) and **neuter** (`het`) gender classes with no fully reliable predictive rule,
though three sub-rules are given as reliable: all plural nouns take `de`; all diminutives take
`het` (cross-reference: diminutives are the dedicated subject of `established/003`, drawn from a
different book's Ch. 22 — this unit's Unit 4 will cover diminutives itself, not yet reached in this
extraction); all nouns referring to people take `de`. Roughly twice as many nouns are `de`-words as
`het`-words. Dutch dictionaries mark gender with `o` (onzijdig, neuter), `v` (vrouwelijk, feminine)
or `m` (mannelijk, masculine) — the `v`/`m` split is a grammatical-tradition subdivision within what
this course otherwise treats as one `de`-class.

### Plural nouns (p. 20) — see non-redundant-supplement note above

Only the two headline patterns are stated here (default `-en`; `-s` for nouns ending in an
unstressed schwa syllable or in a vowel) — the fuller treatment (irregular vowel-lengthening,
`-eren` doublets, Latin-derived plurals, `-heid`/`-or` groups) is already captured in
`established/001` and not re-derived here.

### hij/zij/het and wij/jullie/u/zij pronoun paradigms, stressed vs. unstressed forms (p. 22–23, 28)

Dutch subject pronouns each have a stressed (written-language-safe) and unstressed (spoken-only)
form; the source explicitly states the unstressed forms (`ie`, `ze`, `'t`, `we`, `je`) are
"generally considered too informal to use in writing." The unstressed `je` standing in for `jullie`
(2pl informal) is restricted to contexts where the referent is already clearly established from
context — an ambiguity-avoidance constraint, not a free stylistic choice.

### Possessive pronouns, stressed vs. unstressed forms (p. 29–30)

The possessive paradigm mirrors the subject-pronoun stressed/unstressed split (`mijn`/`m'n`,
`jouw`/`je`, `zijn`/`z'n`, `haar`/`d'r`), except `uw` (formal 2nd person) and `jullie`/`ons`/`onze`/
`hun`, which have no listed unstressed variant. `Ons` is used before `het`-words and `onze` before
`de`-words — the possessive itself is gender-agreeing, unlike the other possessives in the paradigm.

### Present tense used for an ongoing state started in the past (p. 24–25)

Dutch uses the plain present tense where English uses a present-perfect-progressive ("She has been
working... for three months") when the situation still holds at speaking time. The adverb `nu`
("now") is the default marker of this reading; `al` ("already, for as long as"), `pas`, and `nog
maar` (both roughly "only") can substitute for `nu` to shade the duration as feeling long (`al`) or
short (`pas`/`nog maar`) to the speaker — a subjective-framing distinction layered onto the same
tense-choice fact.

### Descriptive-list rhythm and register (p. 25–26)

Coordinating exactly three attributes in a description reads more naturally/"snappy" than two or
four (an explicit stylistic rule, not a grammatical one). Appraisal/HR-register writing favors short
sentences and a constrained set of "acceptable" neutral descriptive adjectives (`accuraat`,
`enthousiast`, `zelfstandig`, `zorgvuldig`); informal spoken description allows a much wider
vocabulary and sentence-pattern range. Pronoun-drop is permitted (as in English) when repeating the
same subject across coordinated clauses (`Ze is accuraat en (ze) doet haar werk met enthousiasme`).

### Spoken-register intensifiers (p. 27)

`Echt`, `ontzettend`, and `hartstikke` are explicitly flagged as belonging to spoken, informal
register specifically — using them can read as exaggeration, and the source notes they occur "more
in spoken than in written language and only in informal situations."

### The imperative (p. 30)

The imperative is formed from the bare verb stem alone, with no additional marking (`Hou de gym
schoon`, `Help ons`, `Kom naar ons feest`) — identical in form to the `ik`-conjugation stem.

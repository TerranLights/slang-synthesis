## Grammar points

### Clitics form a prosodic unit with their host, independent of syntactic structure (§8.1, pp.165–166)

Clitics (function words — pronouns, determiners, auxiliaries, particles, conjunctions,
prepositions — that are phonologically dependent on an adjacent host word) can have prosodic
structure that is non-isomorphic to their syntactic structure: the weak determiner *'t* is
syntactically bound to the following noun but prosodically incorporated into the preceding verb.
Because clitics like *'t* cannot form a prosodic word of their own (no full vowel), incorporation
into the host triggers resyllabification via the universal CV-rule, which has real phonological
consequences — e.g. it removes a post-schwa /n/ from coda position, blocking /n/-deletion that
would otherwise apply. Obligatory word-internal rules (Homorganic Glide Insertion, Prevocalic Schwa
Deletion) extend into the host+clitic domain on the same footing as within an ordinary prosodic
word, including an /n/-insertion option to resolve schwa-initial-clitic hiatus.

### The Dutch pronoun/determiner/adverbial-clitic system (§8.2, pp.166–171)

Dutch personal pronouns, singular possessives, and the adverbs *er*/*daar*/*eens* each have a strong
form (used when stressed, and syntactically parallel to full lexical NPs) and — except for a few
gaps — a weak/clitic form (used unstressed, syntactically restricted). Weak forms cannot be
generated from strong forms by any productive phonological reduction rule (the general Vowel
Reduction rule does not touch diphthongs, and word-initial/post-/h/ vowels never reduce; yet *hem*
reduces to [əm] and *ik* has a weak [ək]), so all weak forms must be listed as lexical items — making
them "special clitics" in Zwicky's (1977) sense both phonologically and syntactically. Syntactic
evidence for the pronoun/full-NP distinction: only weak *je*/*ze* carry a generic ("one"/"people")
reading; strong 3pl pronouns (*zij, hen, hun*) can only refer to animates or institution-like
abstracts, never concrete inanimates; certain idioms and pseudo-reflexive verbs require exclusively
the weak form (or, inversely as with the preposition *volgens*, exclusively the strong form);
adverbs like *gisteren* "yesterday" cannot intervene before a clitic subject; and Dutch clitics show
"clitic climbing" (apparent displacement from their base clause) and cannot be conjoined.

### Prosodic integration: procliticization vs. encliticization (§8.2.1, pp.169–174)

Since weak pronouns/adverbs are (mostly) schwa-only and cannot form independent prosodic words, the
Strict Layer Hypothesis forces them to attach to an adjacent prosodic word via Chomsky-adjunction
rather than being directly dominated by a phonological-phrase node. **Proclitics** (attaching to a
following word) are Chomsky-adjoined to the whole prosodic word (ω-adjunction), because a Dutch foot
is a left-headed trochee and cannot begin with an unstressable schwa syllable. **Enclitics**
(attaching to a preceding word) are Chomsky-adjoined to the final foot (F-adjunction) instead,
achieving a deeper degree of prosodic integration — proclitics thus have the prosodic status of
schwa-containing prefixes, enclitics that of suffixes. Most clitics can function as either
enclitics or proclitics depending on context (sentence-initial position forces proclisis;
sentence-final position forces enclisis), with three exceptions that are obligatory enclitics
requiring a host on their left: *ie* (and its allomorph *die*) and the possessive/pronominal clitic
*der* /dər/ "her". Diagnostic evidence for the proclitic/enclitic distinction and its domain
(the prosodic word) includes: obligatory resyllabification and /n/-insertion triggered by
incorporation into a preceding word; /n/-insertion's *non*-application between a proclitic and the
following word (a glottal stop appears instead), showing proclitics are merely adjoined, not fully
incorporated; resyllabification-induced bleeding of /n/-deletion (the sentence *ze deden 't* "they
did it" can only surface as [zə dedənət], never *[dedət]*, because clitic-triggered resyllabification
puts the /n/ in onset position before /n/-deletion could otherwise apply); a co-occurrence
restriction on vowelless clitics (/k/, /t/) that they must take a preceding host, evidenced by
perceptually distinct minimal pairs like *zal'k eten* vs. *zal keten*; and Fricative Voice
Assimilation (from §7.2.1) sometimes applying and sometimes not across a clitic+host boundary,
which Gussenhoven (1985) used to show schwa-initial clitics like *er* do not always belong to the
preceding prosodic word.

### Cliticization and Final Devoicing (§8.2.2, pp.174–176)

Because Final (obstruent) Devoicing is a lexical, word-level rule, and clitic incorporation with its
resyllabification is postlexical, word-final obstruents that end up in onset position via
cliticization remain voiceless (e.g. *vond-ik* "found I" surfaces with the devoiced /t/ of *vond*
intact: (von)(tɪk), not *(von)(dɪk)). However, some frequent verbs (notably *hebben* "to have") show
variation where the voiced-obstruent variant also surfaces before a clitic — evidence that certain
high-frequency verb+clitic combinations are lexically stored as whole units rather than derived
compositionally, a pattern with a documented parallel in colloquial German (citing Wiese 1996) and
in unstandardized Middle Dutch orthography, which sometimes wrote such combinations as one word
(e.g. *hebbic* "have I"). Less-frequent verbs with a stem-final voiced obstruent (*verbinden*,
*verwonden*, *vermoorden*) do not show this variant, supporting the lexicalization account.

### Cliticization of other function words (§8.3, pp.176–178)

The three Dutch determiners (*de, het, een*) and several /d/-initial function words (*de, dit,
deze, dat, die, daar, d'r, dan*) show a special alternation: either the ordinary postlexical rule of
Regressive [Voice] Assimilation applies (voicing a preceding word-final obstruent), or the function
word's own initial /d/ devoices itself instead — e.g. *op die manier* "in that way" can surface as
either [ɔbdi] or [ɔpti]. Following Zonneveld (1983), this is explained by treating these words as
optional enclitics: when incorporated into the preceding word, a **Word-internal Devoicing** rule
(spreading [−voice] rather than [+voice]) applies, restricted to the domain of the prosodic word —
correctly predicting that consonantal proclitics like *'k* or *'t* do NOT trigger this devoicing when
followed by a voiced obstruent (*'k ben* [kbɛn], not *[kpɛn]*), since proclitics are only adjoined,
not incorporated. The determiner *de* is a special case: it obligatorily surfaces as [tə] after a
word-final /t/, showing it is *obligatorily* encliticized in that specific context even though
encliticization is elsewhile optional for it. Prepositions (all containing a full vowel except
*te*) do not require a host but may still optionally encliticize, exhibiting the same Prevocalic
Schwa Deletion / /n/-insertion / Fricative Voice Assimilation alternations documented for pronominal
clitics.

### Contraction (§8.4, pp.178–180)

Distinct from ordinary cliticization, **contraction** covers a specific closed set of function-word
sequences: the complementizer *dat* + pronoun (losing *dat*'s final /t/: *dat 'k* → [dɑk]); modal
verb/auxiliary + pronoun (*wil 'k* → [ʋɪk]); and certain auxiliaries preceded by a pronoun (*ik heb*
→ [ɪkɛp], *hij heeft* → [hɛift]). The general deletion pattern is that the first of two adjacent
consonants across the contraction site deletes; with weak *je*, contraction can go further,
optionally also deleting the pronoun's own final schwa, yielding sub-standard forms like *wil je* →
[ʋɪj]. Contraction is lexically governed (restricted to a specific closed set of verb+pronoun
combinations, not predictable from surface phonotactics alone — *(de) kat is (ziek)* cannot
contract to *[kɑts]* even though the segmental context looks parallel), and *h*-initial auxiliaries
predictably lose their /h/ in contraction since /h/ cannot survive in a resulting consonant cluster
(*ze heeft* → [zeft], never *[zheft]*) — except *hij heeft*, which uniquely retains the pronoun's
diphthong rather than deleting it, to avoid becoming homophonous with bare *heeft* "has". The
auxiliary *is* independently contracts to bare /s/ after function words only (*Dat is* → [dɑts]),
and can itself undergo further phonological reduction once contracted. A syntactic condition on
contraction is noted: it is blocked when a movement/deletion trace intervenes in the function-word
sequence (e.g. *zij ... is* separated by a wh-trace cannot contract to *[zɛis]*), a fact with a
documented English parallel (*she's* also blocked after a *who*-trace), flagged as deserving further
investigation.

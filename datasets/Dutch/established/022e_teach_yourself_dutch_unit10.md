## Grammar points

### Present tense + al/pas for "have been doing" situations (Unit 10)

Where English uses the present perfect for a state that started in the past and continues now
("I've lived in Amsterdam for 3 years"), Dutch uses the plain present tense, combined with `al`
("already," marking a span felt as long) or `pas` ("only," marking a span felt as short): `Ik woon
al drie jaar in Amsterdam` vs. `Hij woont pas twee maanden in Amsterdam`. The same present-tense
pattern extends to questions (`Hoelang woon je al in Maastricht?`) and combines with `nog maar`
("only"), `sinds` ("since"), and `nu` ("now") for related nuances (p. 126-127).

### Perfect vs. imperfect: two Dutch past tenses (Unit 10)

Building on Unit 8's present perfect, Dutch has a second past tense, the **imperfect**, formed
with a single verb form (no auxiliary): stem + `-de(n)` or `-te(n)`, using the same "soft ketchup"
voiceless-consonant rule as the perfect participle to choose `-te(n)` vs `-de(n)` (`werken → werkte
/ werkten`; `praten → praatte/praatten`, doubling because the stem itself ends in `-t`; `reizen →
reisde/reisden`, taking `-de(n)` despite the devoiced `-s-` because the *infinitive* has `-z-`).
Separable verbs behave as in the present tense, with the prefix simply moving to clause end
(`uitgaan → Ik ging vroeger erg vaak uit`). A closed list of common irregular imperfects is given
(`hebben→had(den)`, `zijn→was/waren`, `gaan→ging(en)`, `doen→deed/deden`, `brengen→bracht(en)`,
`kopen→kocht(en)`, `geven→gaf/gaven`, `komen→kwam(en)`, `vinden→vond(en)`, `moeten→moest(en)`,
`kunnen→kon(den)`, `willen→wilde(n)`, `mogen→mocht(en)`) (p. 128-129).

**When to use which:** the present perfect introduces a past topic and is used for events that are
already finished; the imperfect is used to add *extra background information* about a
topic/situation once it's already been introduced by the perfect, and gives a sense of "drama" or
of directly experiencing the past, versus the perfect's more distanced narration. The imperfect
also has a second, independent use: describing habitual/repeated past actions or states
("used to..."), as in `Ik speelde vroeger veel buiten` "I used to play outside a lot" (p. 129-130).

### Dit is/dat is: gender-neutral demonstrative-copula phrases (Unit 10)

Unlike the Unit 6 demonstrative pronouns (`deze/die` for de-words, `dit/dat` for het-words), the
fixed phrases `dit is`/`dat is` ("this/that is") work regardless of the referent's gender — no
de/het tracking needed. Pluralizes by conjugating `zijn`: `dit zijn`/`dat zijn` ("these/those
are") (p. 131).

### Relative clauses: die vs. dat, verb-to-clause-end (Unit 10)

A relative clause adds extra information about a preceding noun and is introduced by `die` (for
`de`-word referents) or `dat` (for `het`-word referents) — directly parallel to the
demonstrative-pronoun gender split. All verb(s) inside the relative clause move to its very end
(`De tuin die we hadden, was enorm`), and a comma marks the clause boundary regardless of length
(`De tuin die we twee jaar lang in Engeland hadden, was enorm`) (p. 131-132).

## Grammar points

### Vegetariër vs. vegetarisch: person-noun vs. descriptive-adjective pair (Unit 9)

The book explicitly flags this as a pattern to notice: `vegetariër` names the person, `vegetarisch`
describes the food/practice — a distinction English collapses into one word ("vegetarian") (p.
113).

### Om + te + infinitive: purpose clauses (Unit 9)

Distinct from the plain `te` + infinitive construction of Unit 7 (which follows certain verbs
directly), `om + te + infinitive` expresses purpose ("in order to," though often just translated
as plain "to"): `Ik ga naar de supermarkt om boodschappen te doen`. The related idiom `zin hebben
om ... te ...` means "to feel like doing something" (`Ik heb zin om naar huis te gaan`); `zin
hebben in` (+ noun, no `om...te`) means "to feel like having something" (`Ik heb zin in koffie`)
(p. 113-114).

### Iets/niets + adjective-s (Unit 9)

An adjective following `iets` ("something") or `niets` ("nothing") takes an extra `-s`:
`iets sterkers`, `niets nieuws`, `iets lekkers`, `niets moois` (p. 114).

### Object pronouns: full table, stressed/unstressed (Unit 9)

Complete table given, paralleling the Unit 4 subject-pronoun table: `mij/me`, `jou/je`, `u`
(formal, no unstressed form), `hem/'m`, `haar/d'r`, `het/'t`, `ons` (no unstressed form shown),
`jullie` (same for both), `hen/hun/ze` (people), `ze` (things, no stressed form given). Bracketed
unstressed forms are again flagged as too informal for writing. Object pronoun placement is "as
close as possible to the main verb" (p. 114-115).

### Referring to things: hem/het/ze depending on gender (Unit 9)

Extending the Unit 4 `hij`/`het` subject-pronoun gender-tracking rule to objects: `hem` refers to
`de`-word objects, `het` to `het`-word objects, `ze` to plural objects — regardless of whether the
referent is animate. `Hem` and `ze` can also refer to people, but `het` is reserved for objects
only (p. 115-116).

### Commands, encouragements, and impersonal instructions (Unit 9)

Basic commands use the verb stem alone, addressee-neutral regardless of singular/plural (`Kom
binnen`, `Blijf hier`); adding `u` to the sentence makes it more polite (`Komt u binnen`). A
command can be softened into an encouragement by adding `maar` (`Kom maar binnen` "do come in,"
`Blijf maar hier` "do stay here") — the source calls this "done very frequently in Dutch." A fully
impersonal instruction (recipes, public signage) uses the bare infinitive with no subject at all
(`Niet roken` "don't smoke," `Vlees in stukjes snijden` "cut the meat into small chunks") (p.
116-117).

### Separable verbs (Unit 9)

Distinct from the *inseparable* prefixed verbs introduced in Unit 8 (`be-/her-/ont-/ver-/er-/ge-`,
which never split), a large class of Dutch verbs pairs a (usually stressed) prefix — often a
preposition (`op+bellen`, `af+wassen`, `weg+gaan`) but sometimes an adverb or adjective
(`klaar+maken`, `schoon+maken`) — with a base verb, where the combined meaning is often
transparent from the parts but sometimes idiomatic (`uit+nodigen` = "invite," `af+spreken` =
"arrange"). Four distinct behavior rules given: (a) **as the finite main verb**, the prefix
detaches and moves to the end of the clause (`Ik bel Saskia op`); (b) **as a bare infinitive**
(e.g. after a modal), the two parts stay joined as one word (`Ik wil je uitnodigen`); (c) **with
te + infinitive**, `te` is inserted *between* the prefix and the verb, not before the whole unit
(`Ik heb geprobeerd om je op te bellen`); (d) **in the perfect tense**, the past participle's `ge-`
is inserted between the prefix and the base verb, not at the very front (`Ik heb mijn jas
opgehangen`, not `geophangen`) (p. 118-120). This four-way split-vs-joined behavior is flagged
here as a structurally significant mechanism — a genuinely productive morphosyntactic seam in an
otherwise mostly-analytic language, directly relevant to how slang clipping/reanalysis could later
interact with the prefix/verb boundary.

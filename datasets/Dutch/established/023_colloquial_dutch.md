## Grammar points

### Vowel/consonant inventory and stress (pp. 1-7)

Standard phoneme-by-phoneme inventory (short/half-long/long vowels, diphthongs, double vowels,
consonants) — overlaps `established/001`'s Ch.1-3 coverage from the *Essential Grammar* and is not
re-tabulated in full. **New material kept above**: the explicit colloquial-pronunciation notes
(`oude`→`ouwe`, `-ijde`→`-ije`, `-ode`→`-ooie`/`goede`→`goeie`, schwa-epenthesis in `melk`-type
clusters, final `-en` n-dropping) and the v/f and z/s regional-merger notes (p.6: word-initial `v`
merges toward `f` in the west of the Netherlands; word-initial `z`→`s` is noted as **socially
stigmatized**, unlike the `v`→`f` merger which is simply the norm — a real register/prestige
distinction the source draws between two superficially similar sound-mergers).

Stress: main stress normally falls on the first syllable; French-derived loanwords and the
unstressed prefixes `be-`, `ge-`, `her-`, `ont-`, `ver-` are exceptions (p.7).

### Dutch spelling — vowel-length/consonant-doubling rule (p.6-9)

Standard closed/open-syllable spelling logic (double vowel in closed syllable = long vowel; single
consonant after short vowel in open syllable) — overlaps `established/001`. Not re-tabulated.

### The stem of the verb / present tense (pp. 16-18)

Standard formation: strip `-en` from infinitive for the stem; `ik` = stem, `jij/u`/`hij/zij/het` =
stem+t, plural = infinitive form. Spelling-change sub-rules (vowel doubling/undoubling to preserve
length; v/z→f/s stem-final devoicing, e.g. `schrijven`→`ik schrijf`, `reizen`→`ik reis`) — overlaps
`established/001`'s general spelling-alternation coverage. **Only `zijn` is genuinely irregular**;
`hebben` and `komen` show minor irregularity; five monosyllabic verbs (`doen`, `gaan`, `slaan`,
`staan`, `zien`) show a distinct minor-irregularity pattern of their own (p.17) — worth flagging as
a discrete sub-class not otherwise captured. **First-person `d`-drop on `ij`/`ou` + d stems**
(`snijden`→`ik snij`, `rijden`→`ik rij`, `houden`→`ik hou`) is explicitly restricted to "very common
verbs" only — contrasted against `lijden`→`ik lijd` and `vermijden`→`ik vermijd`, which keep the
`d` (p.17) — a lexical-frequency-gated exception, not a general phonological rule.

### Personal pronouns / jij vs. u (p.11-12)

Standard subject-pronoun paradigm; emphatic/unemphatic doublets (`jij/je`, `zij/ze`, `wij/we`)
compared to English *you/ya* — Dutch marks the distinction in writing, English normally doesn't.
`Jij/jullie` (familiar, first-name basis) vs. `u` (respect/distance, surname basis, invariant
between singular and plural, always takes singular verb agreement even when addressing more than
one person) — overlaps `established/002`'s formal/colloquial register material; kept brief here.

### Expressing nationality/profession (p.14)

Standalone nationality/profession nouns take no article (`Hij is leraar`); adjectival nationality
takes `-e` for feminine (`Nederlandse`); when preceded by a modifying adjective, `een` is required
(`Hij is een goede leraar`).

### Saying "please"/"thank you" — pragmatic mismatch with English (pp.19-20)

Dutch `dank je/u` functions as **"no, thank you"** in response to an offer (e.g. `Wil je een kopje
koffie?` → `Dank je` = declining); accepting requires `(Ja,) alsjeblieft/alstublieft` or `(Ja,)
graag`. `Graag` is restricted to replies to an offer/question and cannot render an initial-request
"please" (`Geef mij alsjeblieft een kopje thee`, never *`Geef mij graag...`). `Alsjeblieft` is
placed mid-sentence or sentence-finally in Dutch, never sentence-initially as English "please"
typically is.

### Gender of nouns / articles (p.27)

Two genders: common (`de`) and neuter (`het`); most nouns are common gender; gender is not
predictable from form and must be learned per-noun. Indefinite article `een` (unstressed spoken
`'n`) serves both genders; distinguished from the numeral `één` (same spelling as the article's
full form but written with accents and always fully pronounced).

### Word order in simple sentences — the F-V-N-(NE)-REST schema (pp. 30-31)

Introduces the book's own field-schema for Dutch main-clause word order: **F** (front — whatever
constituent is fronted, most often the subject) immediately followed by **V** (the finite verb,
always second), then **N** (nominal — the subject, when something other than the subject has been
fronted) and the rest of the sentence. When a non-subject constituent is fronted (commonly for
emphasis), the subject is displaced to directly after the verb — "inverted word order"/"inversion,"
an all-pervasive feature of Dutch. Worked minimal pair: `Ik weet het niet` vs. emphatic `Dat weet ik
niet` (fronting the object `dat` forces inversion of `ik` to post-verbal position).

### Negation — niet vs. geen (pp. 32-33)

`Niet` negates a finite verb/general proposition and is inserted in the NE column immediately after
the subject-verb group regardless of normal or inverted word order (Dutch has no periphrastic "do"-
negation). `Geen` specifically negates an indefinite noun phrase ("not a," "not any," "no") — English
"any"/"no" collapse to a single Dutch `geen`, e.g. `Ik spreek geen Duits` covers both "I don't speak
any German" and "I speak no German." The source flags this treatment as introductory, deferring
fuller negation rules (word-order interactions) to Lessons 15-16.

### Dutch money (pp. 35-39)

Pre-euro system: `de gulden` (guilder) = 100 `cent`; named coin denominations (`stuiver`,
`dubbeltje`, `kwartje`, `rijksdaalder`) parallel to English "nickel"/"dime." Currency-noun
pluralization rule: `cent`, `gulden`, and foreign-currency names take **no** plural ending in price
expressions (`Dat kost tien cent/gulden/dollar/pond/frank`); `guldens` only appears when referring to
a physical collection of one-guilder coins. Price-writing convention: comma where English uses a
decimal point (`ƒ10,50`), full stop where English uses a comma in large numbers (`1.074.320`).
**Colloquial money-slang layer, fully source-flagged as colloquial**: `een tientje` for a ten-guilder
note/amount, `een piek` for one guilder (explicitly compared by the author to English "quid"/"buck"),
`een ton` for f100,000 (compared to American "grand"); also a shopkeeper-register habit of quoting
totals in multiples of named coins/notes rather than a raw number (`Het kost twee tientjes/drie
kwartjes/twee dubbeltjes`).

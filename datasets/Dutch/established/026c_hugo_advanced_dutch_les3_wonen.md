## Grammar points

### Relative pronouns `die`/`dat`

`Die` refers back to `de`-words (and to people denoted by `de`-words); `dat` refers back to
`het`-words. People denoted by a `het`-word (e.g. `het meisje`, `het mannetje`) still take `dat`:
**het kind dat op straat speelt** ("the child which/who is playing in the street"). Unlike English,
the Dutch relative pronoun can never be omitted: **Zie je het kind dat op straat aan het spelen is?**
requires `dat` even though the English translation can drop "that/which." p. 55.

### Relative pronoun + preposition

**For things:** `die`/`dat` cannot follow a preposition (paralleling the `er`+preposition rule from
Les 1) — instead `waar + preposition` is used (`waarmee`, `waarvan`, `waarvoor`, `waarop`), often in
the same "sandwich" pattern as `er`+preposition: **Het bed waar ik elke nacht in slaap is veel te
krap.** ("The bed I sleep in every night is much too small."). **For people:** `preposition + wie` is
used instead: **De man met wie ze staat te praten is mijn oom.** ("The man to whom she is
talking..."). pp. 56.

### Present participle formation

Formed simply by adding `-d` to the infinitive (much less commonly used than in English). Once
formed, it behaves as a regular adjective and takes the normal adjectival `-e` inflection where
required: `werken` → `werkend(e)`, e.g. **werkende jonge man** ("working young man," from a
classified ad). p. 54, 61.

### `toen` — adverb ("then"/"at that time") vs. subordinating conjunction ("when")

Dutch has two adverbs meaning "then": `dan` and `toen`. `Toen` is used only when the verb refers to
past time, and can sometimes mean "at that time"/"in those days": **Wat kweekte men toen?** ("What
did people grow at that time?", from Les 2). As a **subordinating conjunction**, `toen` also means
"when" but only for the simple past or past perfect (never future/present, where `als`/`wanneer` are
used instead), and forces the verb to final position: **Het was laat toen wij weggingen.** ("It was
late when we went away."). The two functions are distinguishable by verb position: adverb `toen` →
verb in second position; conjunction `toen` → verb in final position. pp. 49, 61.

### Belgian Dutch vs. Netherlands Dutch — explicit lexical-variant table

The source presents a direct, explicitly source-marked two-column vocabulary comparison between
Belgian ("Flemish") Dutch and Netherlands Dutch, framed as the most common kind of North/South
difference a learner will encounter (the source notes pronunciation differences exist too but treats
vocabulary as the most striking axis):

| Belgium | Netherlands | Gloss |
|---|---|---|
| de living | de woonkamer | living room |
| het appartement | de flat | flat/apartment |
| e.k. (= eerstkomend) | a.s. (= aanstaande) | next/forthcoming |
| nabij | vlakbij | close to |
| de goesting (in iets hebben) | de zin (in iets hebben) | to feel like (doing something) |

The source also notes a positional quirk: `eerstkomend` and `aanstaande`, in their abbreviated forms,
both follow the noun rather than preceding it — **15 november e.k.**, **maandag a.s.**. This is
the richest single explicit dialectal/regional-annotation find in this project's Dutch corpus to
date — a genuine, source-authored comparative table rather than an inferred regionalism, directly
comparable in kind to the `[B]`/`[C]`/`[S]`-style tags flagged as high-value in the Serbian/Croatian/
Bosnian precedent. pp. 52–53.

### Confirming vs. obtaining information; the `alweer` tag

A speaker eliciting genuinely new information asks a plain question: **Is de flat nog vrij?** ("Is
the flat still available?"). To *confirm* information already believed true, Dutch uses a declarative
statement plus a tag question: **Hij is volledig gemeubileerd, niet waar?** ("It's fully furnished,
isn't it?"). A distinct confirming pattern adds `ook alweer` ("again") to a direct question when the
speaker is asking someone to repeat information they were told before: **Hoeveel was de huur ook
alweer?** ("How much was the rent again?") — note Dutch, like English, uses the past tense (`was`)
here even though the information is about the present. pp. 60.

### Politely requesting an appointment

**Kan ik misschien een afspraak maken?** is the base polite pattern, extendable with a name or
purpose: **Kan ik misschien een afspraak maken met meneer Dekker?**; **... om de flat te bekijken?**.
Variant formulations: **Ik zou graag een afspraak willen maken...**; **Mag ik een afspraak maken...**
— the source notes politeness formulas in Dutch are freely variable in surface form while keeping the
same function, directly paralleling the `Tot uw dienst`/`Graag gedaan` register-pair finding from
Les 2. p. 61.

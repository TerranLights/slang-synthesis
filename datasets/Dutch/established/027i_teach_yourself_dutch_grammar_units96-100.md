## Grammar points

### Unit 96 — Expressions and the dictionary
Teaches a dictionary-lookup strategy for figurative expressions: the meaning of a Dutch set
expression usually can't be deduced word-by-word, so the reader should look up the expression's
most important/central word (its "headword") and check the example sentences given under that
entry. Worked examples: **kat** → *de kat uit de boom kijken* ("to wait and see which way the wind
blows," already captured in `027h`); **hooi** → *te veel hooi op de vork nemen* ("to bite off more
than you can chew," already captured in `027h`). The unit then supplies a longer list of expressions
with the dictionary headword bolded, several explicitly tagged "(colloquial)" by the source itself
(*ergens geen bal van snappen*, *over de rooie gaan*, *een blauwtje lopen*, *uit je dak gaan* — all
already captured in `027h`); the remainder (captured above) carry no explicit register tag from the
source and are treated as core/neutral register. The unit's exercises (1 and 2) drill the same
headword-identification skill on ten further expressions, presented without English glosses in the
source itself (the reader is expected to consult their own dictionary) — glosses for these are
supplied above from standard usage and flagged accordingly.

### Unit 97/98 — Abbreviations (1)-(2)
The source is explicit that "Dutch speakers are keen users of abbreviations," including
profession-specific jargon abbreviations the unit deliberately excludes in favor of only
everyday-register ones. **Full-stop convention**: a single-word abbreviation takes one final full
stop; a multi-word abbreviation gets a full stop after each letter (e.g. *s.v.p.*). Covers five
functional categories: general-purpose abbreviations (*enz., bv., t/m, m.a.w.*, etc.), date
abbreviations (*jl.* "last," *a.s.* "next," both placed *after* the date), titles (*mevr., dhr.,
prof., drs., mr.* — professional/academic titles go *before* the name), address abbreviations
(*a/d, a/z*), then in Unit 98: political-party abbreviations (a genuinely three-way NL/Flanders
contrast list — CDA/D'66/PvdA/VVD for the Netherlands vs. SP/CVP/VLD/VU for Belgium/Flanders,
reinforcing this book's recurring NL/Belgium-differ pattern from a fourth angle), work-related
abbreviations (*VUT, ADV, CAO*), and secondary-education-tier abbreviations (*mavo/havo/vwo*, the
three-tier ability-streamed secondary system for ages 12–18). Unit 98's own exercise texts (Texts
A–D) are realistic abbreviation-dense samples (a tourism weather report, a union-merger news item, a
traffic-jam headline using *file*, and a job advertisement) — paraphrased rather than reproduced in
full per copyright discipline; only the standalone vocabulary items they gloss (*file* "traffic jam,"
*vakcentrale/vakbond* "union," *toetreden* "to join," *stichting* "organization," *reden* "reason,"
*klagen* "to complain") are already covered by existing established/ files or are too generic to
warrant separate rows here.

### Unit 99/100 — Irregular verbs (1)-(2)
A full alphabetical principal-parts table (infinitive / imperfect singular+plural / past
participle, hebben-vs-zijn auxiliary marked in brackets where the participle takes zijn) spanning
roughly 105 verbs across the two units — explicitly framed by the source as an easier-to-navigate
alternative to grouping strong verbs by their vowel-alternation pattern class, since "there are many
different groups of strong verbs" and an alphabetical flat list is more practically usable for
lookup. Per the non-redundant-supplement decision above, this table substantially overlaps with
`established/007l`'s existing irregular-verb appendix; only the 22 genuinely new verbs (checked by
exact-match grep against `007l`) are captured as vocabulary rows above. No new grammar rule is
introduced beyond what Unit 99's own brief explanatory note restates (perfect tense = hebben/zijn +
past participle; the auxiliary choice for zijn-verbs is given in brackets when the verb can take
either, "often in different contexts — see Unit 35," a cross-reference already covered).

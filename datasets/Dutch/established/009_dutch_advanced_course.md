**Coverage note:** this is a 32-page promotional/companion booklet for an audio course, not a
conventional chapter-structured grammar. Printed pp. 1–10 (introduction, "What is the Michel Thomas
Method," biography of Michel Thomas, "How are the recordings best used") and pp. 27–32 (product

linguistic material** — skipped entirely, consistent with the coverage rule's exclusion of
non-informative padding. All actual linguistic content in this booklet lives in the "Track listing"
(pp. 11–26), a track-by-track condensed summary of every vocabulary item and grammar point
introduced across the 4-CD Advanced course; that entire section is extracted below, comprehensively.
`pdftotext -layout` produced a clean, correctly-ordered text layer (verified against non-`-layout`
extraction — no scrambling, no font-substitution cipher; the only non-ASCII characters present are
ordinary curly quotes, em/en-dashes, ©, and an arrow glyph `➝` used by the source itself to mark
vowel-change past-participle patterns). No vision-reading was needed. No explicit dialectal/regional/
register annotation appears anywhere in this source — every vocabulary item below is `core` per the
column rules (the source itself never marks anything `formal`/`colloquial`/regional).

Despite its short page count, this source is unusually dense: because it's a condensed *summary* of
an audio course (not prose exposition with drill repetition), nearly every sentence introduces new
vocabulary or a new grammar point, so the "skip repeated drills" clause of the coverage rule barely

## Grammar points

### Stressed vs. unstressed pronouns

Dutch marks emphasis with a distinct stressed pronoun form rather than (or alongside) intonation:
`mij`/`me`, `jou`/`je`, `hen`/`ze` are stressed/unstressed pairs for "me," "you," and "them"
respectively (pp. 13–15). The unstressed forms are the default in ordinary speech; the stressed
forms are reserved for emphasis or contrast.

### The "special swap" (subject-verb inversion after a fronted non-subject)

When a sentence opens with something other than its subject — a time phrase, a conditional clause,
an object pronoun — Dutch requires the verb to move immediately after that fronted element, ahead of
the subject: *dat weet ik al* ("that I know already"), *als ik tijd heb, kom ik* ("if I have time,
I'll come"), *vroeger was niets beter* ("in the past, nothing was better") (pp. 15–16). This is
presented as directly analogous to the rarer English construction "No longer am I putting up with
this," where fronting a non-subject element likewise forces subject-verb inversion.

### Modal verbs and their past tenses

`moeten` (have to), `zullen` (will/shall, for promises and predictions), `kunnen` (can/be able),
and `mogen` (be allowed) each have an irregular simple-past form used in narration: `kon`/`konden`,
`moest`/`moesten`, `mocht`/`mochten`, and `werd`/`werden` (for the related `worden`) do not follow
the regular `-de`/`-te` "wading" pattern (pp. 13, 14, 24–25).

### Separable vs. inseparable verb prefixes

A verb prefix that carries the *stress* (`op-` in `ophalen`, `uit-` in `uitkijken`, `terug-` in
`teruggaan`) is separable: it detaches and moves to the end of the clause in finite forms (*ik haal
het op*), and in the perfect it moves in front of the `ge-` participle prefix (*ik heb het
opgehaald*). A prefix that is *unstressed* (`her-` in `herhalen`, `ont-` in `ontdekken`) is
inseparable — it never breaks off, and, distinctively, such verbs form their past participle
*without* `ge-` at all (`herhaald`, `ontdekt`), unlike the separable-prefix case which keeps `ge-`
but relocates it (pp. 14, 17–18).

### Implied/dropped infinitive with certain common verbs

The second verb in a two-verb construction is sometimes dropped when it's a common verb like
`doen`, `komen`, `gaan`, or `hebben` and its meaning is recoverable from context: *mochten ze binnen
(komen)?* ("were they allowed in?") (p. 14).

### Diminutives are always het-words

Regardless of the gender of the base noun, every diminutive noun in Dutch takes `het`: *het katje*
("the kitten"), even though the base noun `kat` is a `de`-word (p. 13).

### Position verbs (zitten/liggen/staan) for describing where things are

Dutch productively uses `zitten` (sit), `liggen` (lie), and `staan` (stand) to describe the position
of objects, not just people/animals, in contexts where English would just use "to be": *het glas
staat daar* ("the glass is [standing] there") (p. 13).

### The perfect tense ("diving" past): formation and auxiliary choice

The perfect ("diving" past, the book's own pedagogical metaphor) is formed with `hebben` (or `zijn`
for verbs of motion/change) plus a past participle, normally the `hij`/`ze`/`het`-present-tense stem
prefixed with `ge-` (*zegt* → *gezegd*) (pp. 16–17). Verbs of motion or change of state — `gaan`,
`komen`, `blijven`, `worden`, `zijn` itself — take `zijn` rather than `hebben` as their auxiliary
(pp. 21–22): *ik ben gegaan*, *hij is gebleven*. This auxiliary split also determines which
auxiliary is used to express "would have" for a given verb: motion/change verbs use `was`/`waren`,
all others use `had`/`hadden` (p. 23).

### The "geven box": whole-stem ge- prefixing

A subset of verbs (`geven`, `zien`, `wassen`, `staan`, `gaan`, `komen`, and others fitting the same
pattern) form their past participle by prefixing `ge-` to the *entire* infinitive-derived stem
rather than to a modified present-tense stem: `gegeven`, `gezien`, `gewassen`, `gestaan`, `gegaan`,
`gekomen` (p. 19).

### Vowel-change patterns in past participles

Some strong verbs show a regular vowel shift between infinitive and participle, paralleling English
strong-verb patterns: `ij` → `e` (*kijken* → *gekeken*; *begrijpen* → *begrepen*) and `i` → `o`
(*drinken* → *gedronken*; *vinden* → *gevonden*) (p. 23).

### Simple past ("wading" past): formation and contrast with the perfect

Alongside the perfect ("diving" past), Dutch has a simple past tense (the book's "wading" past)
formed by adding `-de`/`-den` (or `-te`/`-ten` after voiceless-final stems) directly to the verb
stem: *ik wilde het doen*, *wij wachtten*. The perfect (*ik heb iets gedaan*) is used for a single
completed occasion; the simple past is used for repeated or habitual past action, "I used to..."
(pp. 25–26). A handful of common verbs (`kunnen`, `moeten`, `mogen`, `worden`) form this simple past
irregularly rather than with the regular suffix (p. 26).

### Double infinitive with modal ("trigger") verbs in the perfect

When a modal/"trigger" verb (`willen`, `mogen`, `kunnen`, `laten`) is used in the perfect tense
followed by another verb, both verbs surface in the infinitive rather than the second verb taking a
participle form: *ik heb dat willen doen* ("I have wanted to do that"), *ik heb het haar laten zien*
("I showed it to her") (p. 20).

### hoeven + niet + te

`hoeven` is a negative-polarity modal — it is used specifically with `niet ... te` to mean "don't
have to": *jullie hoeven niet te gaan* ("you don't have to go") (p. 20).

### Passive voice with worden vs. zijn, and its systematic ambiguity

`worden` + past participle expresses a passive process/event ("is being done," "gets done"): *het
wordt vanavond gewassen* ("it's getting washed tonight"). `zijn` + past participle expresses a
passive *state* — and, notably, Dutch collapses several English tense distinctions onto the same
surface form here: "it is" and "it has been," "was" and "had been," "will be" and "will have been,"
"would be" and "would have been" are all expressed identically in the `zijn`-passive, with context
alone disambiguating (pp. 25–26).

### Gender and word-class rules for de/het

All plural nouns take `de` regardless of the singular noun's own gender (*de katten*). Nouns formed
by turning a verb's infinitive into a noun ("nominalized infinitives," e.g. `het leven`, `het eten`,
`het wachten`) are always `het`-words, like `het huis`/`het boek`. Nouns ending in `-atie` (*de
combinatie*, *de situatie*) or `-ing` (*de woning*) are always `de`-words (pp. 24–25).

### Adjective -e ending with het-words after "een"

Normally an attributive adjective before a `het`-word takes no `-e` ending when preceded by the
indefinite article `een`: *een nieuw huis* ("a new house") — contrasting with the `-e` that would
otherwise be added in most other attributive contexts (p. 25).

### Relative pronoun choice: dat vs. die

The relative pronoun agrees with the gender of its antecedent: `dat` refers back to a `het`-word
(*het katje dat ik gekocht heb*), `die` refers back to a `de`-word (*de kat die ik gekocht heb*)
(p. 24).

### Comparative and superlative formation

Regular comparatives add `-er` (*mooier*, *moeilijker*, *leuker*), with the final `-r` always fully
pronounced; superlatives add `-st`/`-ste`, with the `-e` added when the adjective is used
attributively before its noun (*mooist* vs. *de mooiste kat*). `best`/`beste` ("best") is an
irregular superlative, parallel to English (p. 20).

### Word order: time before place

Where English typically places a place phrase before a time phrase, Dutch reverses the order: *ik
heb het gisteren in Amsterdam gekocht* ("I bought it in Amsterdam yesterday," literally "...
yesterday in Amsterdam bought") (p. 18).

### Present tense used for future reference

The simple present can express future time without any additional marking, exactly as in colloquial
English: *ik werk morgen niet* ("I'm not working tomorrow" / "I won't work tomorrow"). `gaan` +
infinitive is available as an explicit near-future alternative (p. 11).

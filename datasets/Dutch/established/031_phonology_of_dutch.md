## Grammar points

### The Dutch vowel inventory: sixteen vowels in four structural classes (§2.2, p.4)

Booij classifies Dutch's sixteen vowels into four structural (not just phonetic) classes: **short
vowels** /ɪ, ɛ, ɔ, ʏ, ɑ/; **long vowels** /i, y, u, e, ø, o, a/; the **schwa** /ə/; and three
**diphthongs** /ɛi, œy, ɔu/. The vowel-length distinction is framed as primarily *phonological*
rather than purely phonetic: long vowels pattern as two structural units, short vowels as one (see
Grammar point below on skeletal representation) — independent of raw duration, since the
non-close long vowels average ~200ms while short vowels and the *high* long vowels /i, y, u/
average only ~100ms (citing Nooteboom 1972). The high long vowels only surface at true "long"
duration before /r/.

### The Dutch consonant inventory (§2.3, Table 2.2, p.7)

Full place/manner grid: **plosives** /p b/ (bilabial), /t d/ (alveolar), /k, (g)/ (velar) — /g/ is
bracketed as marginal, occurring only in loanwords (*goal*) or as a contextual allophone of /k/
before a voiced plosive (*zakdoek* → [zɑgduk]); **fricatives** /f v/ (labiodental), /s z/
(alveolar), /x ɣ/ (velar), /h/ (glottal); **nasals** /m/ (bilabial), /n/ (alveolar), /ŋ/ (velar);
**liquids** /l, r/ (alveolar); **glides** /ʋ/ (labiodental), /j/ (palatal). The alveolar segments
/s, z, t, n/ palatalize to postalveolar [ʃ, ʒ, c, ɲ] before /j/; these postalveolar sounds also
occur independently in loanwords (*chique*, *jury*), analyzable either way.

### Underlying voice contrast survives surface neutralization (§2.3, p.8)

For many western-Holland speakers, the voiced/voiceless contrast for /f–v/ and /x–ɣ/ neutralizes
word-initially (and sometimes intervocalically) toward the voiceless member; word-finally all
obstruents are voiceless regardless of dialect. Booij's diagnostic that the *underlying* distinction
still exists even for these speakers: past-tense suffix selection (/-də/ after voiced segments vs.
/-tə/ after voiceless) still tracks the underlying voicing specification, not the neutralized
surface form (*legde* [lɛɣdə] "laid" vs. *lachte* [lɑxtə] "laughed").

### /h/ has no place features of its own (§2.3, p.8)

The Dutch /h/ is phonologically voiced (unlike English /h/), articulated with glottal friction, and
has no supralaryngeal place specification — its vocal-tract shape is simply that of the following
vowel. This is why /h/ patterns as [+aspirated] with an otherwise-empty Place node rather than
having its own consonantal place feature.

### /r/ and /l/: wide free variation, individually and regionally conditioned (§2.3, p.8)

Dutch /r/ has no single normative realization: alveolar roll, alveolar flap, uvular roll, uvular
fricative, or uvular approximant are all attested, with postvocalic /r/ sometimes realized close to
[j]. /l/ is "clear" [l] word-initially before vowels but "dark"/velarized [ɫ] in syllable rhymes and
intervocalically (some speakers realize it as a vocoid there). Both are explicitly framed as
individual/regional variation rather than free variation with no structure.

### Phonological features are organized in a feature-geometric hierarchy (§2.4, pp.9–12)

Booij's core theoretical claim, kept here at the empirical level: phonological features aren't a
flat bundle but are hierarchically grouped so that natural classes of features (e.g. all Place
features together) can be targeted as a unit by a single rule — motivated by processes like nasal
place assimilation, where an entire Place specification (not an individual feature) spreads from
one consonant to another. For Dutch specifically: three active place articulators (Labial, Coronal,
Dorsal); the major-class features [consonant] and [sonorant] divide segments into obstruents,
sonorant consonants, and vowels; [voice] is only distinctive for obstruents (vowels/sonorants are
always voiced in Dutch); postalveolar consonants are predictably [+distributed] (laminal) as
opposed to [-distributed] (apical) alveolars, and this is entirely predictable from context (before
/j/) rather than needing independent lexical specification.

### Vowel length is structural (moraic), not a binary feature — with real morphological
consequences (§2.5.1, pp.13–16)

This is the chapter's highest-value structural claim for typology purposes. Rather than a binary
`[±long]` feature, Booij represents vowel length as **linking to the skeletal (X-) tier**: short
vowels link to one X-slot, long vowels/diphthongs/the schwa link to two. This single structural
choice explains several otherwise-unrelated facts about Dutch simultaneously:
- **Syllable shape**: a short vowel can be followed by up to two coda consonants (*damp*
  [dɑmp]), but a long vowel by only one (**daamp* is ill-formed) — because the long vowel already
  "uses up" a mora that would otherwise host a second consonant.
- **Word-final distribution**: short vowels cannot occur word-finally in Dutch; long vowels, the
  schwa, and (unusually) the *phonetically* short high vowels /i, y, u/ all can.
- **Diminutive-suffix allomorph selection** (detailed further in Ch.4, previewed here): nouns
  ending in a long vowel, the schwa, or /i, y, u/ all select the *same* diminutive allomorph as
  each other — and a *different* one from nouns ending in a true short vowel. This is direct
  morphological evidence that /i, y, u/ pattern as bimoraic ("long") at the phonological level
  despite being phonetically short in most positions (only lengthening to match true long vowels
  before /r/).
- **Reduction to schwa** always affects an entire long vowel as a unit (*banaan* → [bɔnan], never
  a split realization) — consistent with a long vowel being one melodic unit doubly linked to the
  skeleton, not two independent vowel slots.
- **Diphthongization** is derivable as the two X-slots of an originally-uniform long vowel
  acquiring *different* height specifications — explaining why diphthongs historically/
  synchronically arise from long vowels specifically, never from short ones.

### Four degrees of vowel height, via [high]/[mid] rather than [high]/[low] (§2.5.2, pp.16–18)

Standard Chomsky-Halle binary [high]/[low] only distinguishes three vowel heights. Dutch needs a
fourth (to fit in the marginal loanword vowels /ɛː, œː, ɔː/ alongside the native system), so Booij
uses [high]/[mid] instead, giving four combinations: [+high,-mid] (i, u, and their long
counterparts), [+high,+mid] (ɪ, e, ʏ, ø, and o), [-high,+mid] (ɛ, ɛː, œ, œː, ɔ, ɔː), [-high,-mid]
(ɑ, a). A predictable-redundancy pattern falls out of this: long mid vowels are, outside loanwords,
always [+high] in this system — i.e. the "true" long mid vowels of the native system (e, ø, o) are
featurally adjacent to the high vowels, while the *marginal* (loanword-only) long mid vowels
(ɛː, œː, ɔː) sit in the lower-mid slot. This underlies the Learned Vowel Backing alternations
(*fundamenteel*→*fundamentalisme*, etc.) and the vowel-lengthening-in-open-syllables alternations
(*god*→*goden*) discussed above.

### Diphthongs are sequences of two short vowels differing only in height (§2.5.3, pp.18–19)

The three diphthongs /ɛi, œy, ɔu/ are analyzed as two short vowels sharing one syllable nucleus,
identical to each other in backness and roundness but differing in height (first element half-open,
second element close) — formalized as a template requiring backness/roundness agreement with a
height mismatch. This is why Dutch diphthongs pattern distributionally like long vowels for
co-occurrence restrictions (can be followed by the same range of codas as a long vowel) but are
structurally distinct from a true vowel+glide sequence like /ɑj/ (as in *mais* "corn") or /ɔj/
(*hoi* "hi"), which instead pattern with short-vowel-plus-consonant sequences and cannot be
followed by a further coda consonant (other than an alveolar obstruent).

### The schwa is lexically specified, not purely epenthetic (§2.5.4, p.19)

Although the schwa functions as Dutch's "default"/unmarked vowel (central, reducible target,
optionally inserted in non-homorganic consonant clusters), Booij argues (following Trommelen 1984)
that its distribution is not fully predictable by rule: near-minimal word pairs like *lamp/hennep*
show that whether a word-final schwa is present or absent isn't recoverable from the surrounding
consonants alone, so schwa must be present in some words' lexical representations. Structurally,
schwa gets the same two-X-slot ("long") representation as other bimoraic vowels — it patterns with
long vowels for syllable-structure purposes (no following consonant cluster, can appear
word-finally) despite being phonetically short — but is marked with a diacritic [unstressable]
feature, since a schwa-bearing syllable can never take word stress.

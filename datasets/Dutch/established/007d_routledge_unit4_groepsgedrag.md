## Grammar points

### 1. The present perfect: formation

Formed with an auxiliary (*hebben* or *zijn*) plus a past participle: **hebben/zijn + past
participle**. Regular past participles are formed by prefixing the stem with **ge-** and adding a
final **-t** or **-d**: **ge + stem + t/d**. Use *-t* when the stem's last letter is one of *s, f, t,
k, ch, p* (mnemonic: "soft ketchup"); otherwise use *-d*. The **ge-** prefix is *not* added to verbs
already beginning with *be-, ge-, her-, er-, ont-,* or *ver-* (e.g. *vertellen* → *verteld*, not
*geverteld*). For separable verbs, **ge-** is inserted between the separable prefix and the verb
stem (*opbellen* → *opgebeld*; *schoonmaken* → *schoongemaakt*).

### 2. Choosing *hebben* vs. *zijn* as the present-perfect auxiliary

Most verbs take *hebben*. A verb takes *zijn* instead when it indicates a **change of place or
state** (or the opposite of that, e.g. staying/remaining) — the source's example list: *blijven*
"to stay," *gaan* "to go," *komen* "to come," *zijn* "to be," *worden* "to become," *beginnen* "to
begin," *stoppen* "to stop."

### 3. *Hebben* vs. *zijn* with verbs of motion specifically

Motion verbs like *fietsen* "to cycle," *lopen* "to walk," *rennen* "to run," and *rijden* "to
drive" can take **either** auxiliary depending on whether a destination is specified: *zijn* is used
when a destination is given (*We zijn naar Lisse gefietst* "We cycled to Lisse"); *hebben* is used
otherwise, describing the activity without a destination (*We hebben drie uur gefietst* "We cycled
for three hours").

### 4. Main-clause vs. subclause word order

In a Dutch main clause, the finite verb always occupies the second position (except in yes/no
questions and the imperative). In a **subclause** — introduced by a subordinating conjunction such
as *omdat* "because," *als* "if," *dat* "that," or *of* "whether" — **all verbs move to the end of
the clause** instead. Example: *Ik kom niet. Ik moet morgen werken.* → *Ik kom niet omdat ik morgen
moet werken.* ("I'm not coming because I have to work tomorrow.")

### 5. Indirect statements and questions

Subclauses beginning with *dat* "(that)" or *of* "whether," typically following verbs like
*zeggen* "to say," *vragen* "to ask," *weten* "to know," *geloven* "to believe," or *vertellen* "to
tell," function as indirect statements or indirect questions. Direct: *Martijn is erg laat
vertrokken.* → Indirect: *Zij zegt dat Martijn erg laat is vertrokken.* Direct question: *Is jouw
baan zwaar?* → Indirect: *Ze vraagt of jouw baan zwaar is.*

### 6. Word-order schema for subclauses

The source tabulates this explicitly: in a main clause, subject–verb–object order holds normally
(*Ik hou van actiefilms*). Once embedded as a subclause (after *dat*/*omdat*/etc.), the pattern
becomes subject–object–**verb-last**: *Ik vind dat Terminator 3 een leuke film is*; *Ik vind die leuk
omdat ik van actiefilms hou*. The source notes that some sentence elements — prepositional phrases in
particular — can still follow the clause-final verb position even in a subclause.

### 7. Diminutive supplement: a fifth allomorph and new pragmatic-function/regional findings

Skipped as redundant with established/003: the `-je`/`-tje`/`-etje`/`-pje` allomorph conditions
(already fully documented there). New from this unit:

- **A fifth allomorph, `-kje`**, "sometimes after -ng" — e.g. *koninkje*, *beloninkje*. Not among
  003's four documented allomorphs; worth folding into this language's overall diminutive-allomorph
  inventory going forward.
- **An explicit enumerated function list** for what diminutives can convey beyond literal smallness:
  *fun* (*een lekker dagje uit* "a nice day out"), *sweet* (*een schattig mannetje* "a sweet little
  boy"), *contempt/irritation* (*een vervelend zaakje* "an irritating business," *een vreemd ventje*
  "a weird little man"), and *unimportance* (*een foutje* "a/one mistake"). This is independent
  supporting evidence for 003's "personalizing suffix" analysis, with its own example set.
- **Real colloquial/regional examples beyond the textbook-paradigm level**: the baby-talk terms of
  endearment above (*poepie*, *scheetje*, *plaatje*, *knuistjes*) demonstrate the personalizing
  function in actual dialogue, including one case (*scheetje*, dim. of "fart") where the diminutive
  fully overrides the base word's literal meaning. The explicit Netherlands/Belgium split for
  *poepie*/*scheetje* vs. *schatje*/*schattebolleke* is a genuine source-marked regional annotation.
  *Schattebolleke*'s *-eke* ending is itself a distinct Flemish/Brabantic diminutive allomorph, not
  documented anywhere else in this language's established files yet — worth flagging for a future
  Flemish-focused source to confirm and expand.

### 8. Register note: family address terms and the u/je choice

This unit's family-tree material shows Dutch running a familiarity gradient within single kinship
roles rather than a binary formal/informal split for family terms specifically — e.g. *moeder* has
the colloquial variants *mama, mam, ma*, and *vader* has *papa, pap, pa*. Separately, the source's
own address-form exercises (Oefening 16–17, comparing *neef→tante* vs. *werkgever→werknemer*
address patterns) reinforce that the *u* vs. *je/jij* choice tracks relationship hierarchy and
social distance rather than family membership alone — an older or more distant relative may still be
addressed with *u*, while a same-generation or close relative gets *je*.

### 9. Netherlands/Flanders lexical variants for family terms

The source explicitly states two lexical substitutions for Flanders: *zwager* "brother-in-law" is
called *schoonbroer* in Flanders, and *oom* "uncle" is often called *nonkel* in Flanders — genuine
source-marked regional vocabulary variants, distinct lexemes rather than pronunciation differences.

## Grammar points

### Plural formation — fuller enumerated rule set (Lesson 4)

Cross-reference: core plural mechanics (`-en`/`-s`, vowel-lengthening, `-eren` doublets) are already
fully documented in `established/001_chapters1-3_pronunciation_spelling_plural.md`. This source adds
a fuller **numbered rule list** worth recording as a cross-check, including two rules not previously
captured explicitly:

1. Nouns ending in unstressed `-el`, `-em`, `-en`, `-er`, `-aar` generally take `-s`.
2. A small set of masculine nouns denoting occupations/people form their plural in `-lui` or
   `-lieden` instead of `-en`/`-s` (e.g. `-man` compounds: `zeeman` → `zeelui`/`zeelieden`
   "sailors") — not previously documented in this project's Dutch coverage.
3. Nouns ending in `-heid` pluralize as `-heden` (vowel/consonant alternation layered onto the
   regular `-en` plural).
4. Recently borrowed/foreign-origin nouns may take `-s` even where a native noun of similar shape
   would take `-en`.

### Adjective declension with a genuine semantic contrast (Lesson 5)

Dutch adjective `-e` inflection (already covered structurally in `001`) is shown here with a
**worked example demonstrating real meaning-differentiation via inflection**: `een groot man` (no
`-e`, predicate-like/set-phrase reading, roughly "a great man," figurative) vs. `een grote man` (with
`-e`, literal "a tall/big man"). This is a genuinely useful semantic-contrast data point not
previously captured — the book frames it as a caution that the inflectional `-e` is not purely
mechanical but can carry a literal-vs-figurative distinction for certain adjectives.

### Weak possessive contractions flagged as colloquial (Lesson 8)

The source explicitly marks the reduced/cliticized possessive forms `m'n` (mijn), `je` (jouw, when
unstressed), `z'n` (zijn), `d'r`/`'r` (haar) as colloquial spoken-register contractions of the full
possessive adjectives, paralleling this project's existing colloquial-particle findings in `002`.

### Material adjectives via `-en` suffixation (Lesson 6)

A productive derivational rule not previously documented for Dutch in this project: a noun denoting
a material becomes an adjective meaning "made of X" by suffixing `-en` (with the expected
final-consonant/vowel adjustments): `papier` "paper" → `papieren` "(made of) paper"; `ijzer` "iron" →
`ijzeren` "(made of) iron"; `hout` "wood" → `houten` "(made of) wood"; `steen` "stone" → `stenen`
"(made of) stone." Distinct in mechanism from the diminutive suffix system documented in `003`, but
worth flagging as a second genuinely productive Dutch derivational-suffix process for the
morphological-typology profile.

### Diminutive allomorphs — independent cross-check (Lesson 6)

Cross-reference: `established/003_chapter22_diminutives.md` (Shetter) already documents the full
diminutive-allomorph system in depth. This source's independent presentation, organized by the
stem's **final consonant** rather than Shetter's phonological-environment framing, agrees on the
same four/five allomorphs (`-je` after l/m/n/ng/r plus a preceding vowel change in some cases,
`-pje` after m, `-tje` after vowels/w, `-etje` after certain single consonants) and adds one
irregular form not previously logged: `schip` "ship" → `scheepje` "little ship" (vowel-lengthening
diminutive, parallel to the vowel-length-alternation sub-rule `003` already documents for other
stems).

### Colloquial breaking of `waar + preposition` (Lesson 7)

The source explicitly notes that the compound interrogative/relative adverb `waar + preposition`
(one word, e.g. `waarmee`, `waarvoor`) can be split in colloquial speech, with the preposition moved
to directly precede the verb instead. The source's own assessment: **"In speech this method is
preferable"** — a rare case of the book actively recommending the colloquial construction over the
formal one, worth flagging as strong register evidence.

### `'t kofschip` weak-past-tense mnemonic (Lesson 16)

The source teaches the standard Dutch pedagogical mnemonic for choosing `-te(n)` vs. `-de(n)` in the
weak past tense: if the verb root (as spelled in the infinitive) ends in one of the consonants in
`t-k-f-s-ch-p` (traditionally memorized as the consonant set spelling `'t kofschip`, "the
coaster-ship"), add `-te(n)`; after all other consonants and all vowels, add `-de(n)`. Not
previously documented by name in this project's Dutch `established/` files despite the underlying
voicing rule itself being known — worth recording the mnemonic explicitly since it is exactly the
kind of memorable folk-pedagogical device likely to recur or be echoed in slang/informal
metalinguistic commentary.

### Strong verb ablaut classes and irregular presents (Lessons 16–21)

The source works systematically through several strong-verb subgroups across five consecutive
lessons, tied to the ongoing "bottle" story's own verb usage:
- `-ij-` root vowel → `-ee-` (singular) / `-e-` (plural) in the past tense (`kijken` → `ik keek`,
  `wij keken`).
- A subgroup with `-a-` in the past singular and a different vowel in the past plural
  (`komen` → `ik kwam`, `wij kwamen`; `breken` → `ik brak`, `wij braken`).
- Several genuinely irregular strong verbs singled out individually: `doen` (`ik deed`, `wij
  deden`), `zeggen` (`ik zei`, plural pronounced `zɛijə`), `willen` (`ik wilde`/`ik wou`, plural
  usually `wilden` but often colloquial `wouden`).
- **Past-participle formation rules** (Lesson 22): weak verbs take root + `ge-` prefix (dropped for
  verbs with an inseparable prefix; inserted between a separable prefix and the root) + `-d`/`-t`
  suffix; strong verbs take the same `ge-`/no-`ge-` prefix logic + bare `-en` suffix, with the root
  vowel sometimes (not always) matching the past-tense vowel.

### `zijn`/`hebben` auxiliary selection for the Present Perfect (Lesson 23)

A clearly stated three-way rule not previously captured this systematically in this project's Dutch
files: (1) verbs denoting an ongoing action or state take `hebben` (e.g. `drinken`, `werken`,
`vechten`) — with `blijven` and `zijn` themselves as explicit exceptions that take `zijn`; (2) verbs
denoting a change of place or condition take `zijn` (`sterven`, `vallen`, `aankomen`, `verhuizen`);
(3) the intransitive verbs of motion (`lopen`, `rijden`, `varen`, `sporen`) take **either** auxiliary
depending on function — `hebben` when the sentence merely names the *type* of motion ("hij heeft
gelopen, niet gereden" — he walked, he did not ride), `zijn` when a *destination* is given ("hij is
naar Arnhem gelopen" — he walked to Arnhem). This destination-vs.-manner distinction for motion
verbs is a genuinely finer-grained rule than this project's prior Dutch grammar coverage had
recorded.

### Future/Conditional with `zullen` (Lesson 24)

Dutch uses a single auxiliary `zullen` for the future (unlike English's `shall`/`will` split); the
source notes explicitly that `willen` (studied earlier as "to want") **never** carries future-tense
meaning despite superficial resemblance to English "will." The Conditional is formed from the past
tense of `zullen` (`zou`/`zouden`) exactly parallel to how the present tense of `zullen` forms the
Future — a clean, explicitly-stated tense/mood symmetry.

### Degrees of comparison — irregular set and voicing alternation (Lesson 18)

Regular comparison (`-er`/`-st`) is standard and not re-documented here, but two sub-rules are worth
logging: (1) adjectives ending in a long vowel + `f`/`s` alternate to `v`/`z` in the comparative
(`lief` → `liever`, `boos` → `bozer`, `wijs` → `wijzer`), with explicit exceptions (`los` → `losser`,
`muf` → `muffer`, `grof` → `grover`); (2) the irregular comparison set `goed/beter/best`,
`kwaad/erger/ergst`, `veel/meer/meest`, `weinig/minder/minst`, `graag/liever/liefst` — the last of
these (`graag`, "gladly/willingly") being a comparison set for an adverb of volition/preference
rather than a physical-property adjective, worth flagging since it recurs heavily in the politeness
formulas of Lesson 15 (`Graag`, `Ja, graag`, `Heel graag` as acceptance formulas).

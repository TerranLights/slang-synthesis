## Unit 1 — Verbs and pronouns (pp. 1–10)

### Vocabulary

| Term | Gloss | Part of Speech | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| ik / je, jij / u | I / you (inf.) / you (formal) | pers. pron. subj. sg. | Full subject-pronoun paradigm; sg. 3rd person hij (masc./de-word referent), zij/ze (fem.), het (neuter/het-word referent) |
| wij, we / jullie / zij, ze | we / you (pl.) / they | pers. pron. subj. pl. | u used for both formal sg. and pl., verb stays singular |
| ’k, -ie, ’t | unstressed forms of ik, hij, het | pron. (colloquial/literary) | Tagged `colloquial`; -ie always follows the verb (e.g. *Wat zegt-ie?*) |
| werken | to work | verb (weak) | Model regular verb; stem *werk-* + person ending |
| hebben, zijn | to have, to be | verb (irregular) | Full present-tense paradigms given (heb/hebt/heeft/hebben; ben/bent/is/zijn) |
| zetten, weten, houden, raden | put, know, hold, guess | verb | Illustrate stem-final -t/-d retention rules in 2nd/3rd sg. |
| gaan, zien, doen, slaan, staan | go, see, do, hit, stand | verb (mono-syllabic, stem in -n not -en) | Irregular infinitive-stem pattern shared by this small verb class |
| spelen vs. spellen | to play vs. to spell | verb (minimal pair) | Open- vs. closed-syllable spelling contrast in conjugation |
| wonen, winnen, spreken, trekken, laten, zitten | live, win, speak, pull, let, sit | verb | Long/short-vowel spelling-rule set |
| geven, wijzen | to give, to point | verb | v→f, z→s syllable-final hardening in conjugation |
| lerares, advocaat | (female) teacher, lawyer | noun | Family-context vocabulary (Erik/Sanne/Karin/Peter narrative) |

Condensed drill word banks (verb-conjugation practice, distinct new items beyond the above): *vragen*
'ask', *zeggen* 'say', *nemen* 'take', *zwemmen* 'swim', *leven* 'live', *reizen* 'travel', *lopen*
'walk'; *kopen* 'buy', *passen* 'fit', *eten* 'eat', *schrijven* 'write', *lezen* 'read'; *heten* 'be
named', *studeren* 'study', *komen* 'come'.

### Grammar points

**Subject forms of the personal pronoun.** Full paradigm as above; stressed/unstressed pairs
(jij/je, zij/ze, wij/we) chosen by sentence emphasis, not free variation; jij/je/jullie vs. formal u
divides by social distance/formality/generation gap, with u obligatory for strangers, service
personnel–customer, hierarchical difference, and all formal writing (p. 2).

**Verb conjugation in the present tense.** Regular pattern: stem = infinitive minus -en; ik = bare
stem; jij/je and u/hij/zij/het add -t (dropped on jij/je when inverted, e.g. after a question word);
wij/jullie/zij add back -en. hebben and zijn are irregular and must be memorized whole. Stems ending
in -t take no extra -t in 2nd/3rd sg.; stems ending in -d do take it, and the -d is often dropped in
speech when jij/je follows the verb (*houd je* → *hou je*). Spelling in conjugation follows the same
open/closed-syllable and v/f, z/s hardening logic as elsewhere in Dutch orthography (pp. 1–8).

## Unit 2 — Modal auxiliary verbs (pp. 11–14)

### Vocabulary

| Term | Gloss | Part of Speech | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| kunnen, moeten, mogen, willen, zullen | can, must, may, want, shall/will | modal verb | Full present-tense paradigm table given for all five |
| van (with mogen/moeten) | (marks who grants permission/gives the order) | prep. | *Ik mag van mijn vader niet roken* = "My father doesn't allow me to smoke" — a genuinely idiomatic Dutch construction with no direct English equivalent |

### Grammar points

**Modal + infinitive word order.** The modal verb is conjugated and takes the normal verb slot; the
main verb stays as a bare infinitive at the end of the sentence. The main-verb infinitive is
frequently *elided but implied* when clear from context (*Kom je? Nee, ik kan niet* [komen]). Willen
≠ "will" — it means "want to"; students commonly confuse it with zullen. The five modals have
subtly distinct question-form meanings (*Kun je komen?* ability, *Moet je komen?* obligation, *Wil je
komen?* desire, *Zul je komen?* futurity/promise, *Mag je komen?* permission) (pp. 11–13).

## Unit 3 — Questions and question words (pp. 15–19)

### Vocabulary

| Term | Gloss | Part of Speech | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| hoe, hoeveel, waar, waar...vandaan, wat | how, how much/many, where, where...from, what | question word | Core interrogative set |
| welk/welke, wat voor, wie, wanneer, waarom | which, what kind of, who, when, why | question word | welk = het-words, welke = de-words + all plurals; wanneer excludes clock time (use *hoe laat?* instead) |

### Grammar points

**Two question types.** Yes/no questions front the conjugated verb (subject–verb inversion); question-
word questions front the question word, then the verb, then the subject (3rd position). hoeveel
takes a singular noun when the noun is normally uncountable/mass (koffie, thee, water) and a plural
noun otherwise. wie ("who") and hoe ("how") are a common pronunciation-confusion pair for
English-speaking learners (pp. 15–16).

## Unit 4 — Numbers and measures (pp. 20–29)

### Vocabulary

| Term | Gloss | Part of Speech | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| één–tien, elf–twintig, honderd, duizend, miljoen, miljard | 1–10, 11–20, 100, 1000, million, billion | numeral | Cardinal numbers; irregular spellings dertien (13), veertien (14), tachtig (80) flagged |
| eerste, tweede, derde... -de/-ste ordinals | 1st, 2nd, 3rd... | numeral (ordinal) | -de for 1–19 (with eerste, derde irregular, achtste ends in -ste anyway); -ste for 20+ |
| januari–december | January–December | noun (month names) | Not capitalized in Dutch |
| kilo, pond, ons, liter, deciliter / meter, kilometer, centimeter, millimeter, vierkante meter | metric weight/length/area units | noun | Metric system throughout |
| veel, weinig, enkele, verschillende, sommige, enig(e), menig(e), genoeg, een paar, wat, voldoende | many, few, a few, various, some, some, many, enough, a few/a pair, some, sufficient | indefinite numeral | een paar is ambiguous: "a few" vs. "a pair" |
| beide, beiden, allebei | both (adj./independent, persons-only, persons+things) | numeral (definite) | Three-way distinction by countability/reference type; geen van beide/beiden = "neither" |
| komma | (decimal) comma | noun | Dutch uses commas where English uses decimal points; *nul komma nul* idiomatically = "nothing at all" |

### Grammar points

**Counting up from 20.** Dutch inverts English order: "one-and-twenty" (eenentwintig) rather than
"twenty-one." A four-digit number like 4398 can alternatively be read as hundreds-groups
(*drieënveertighonderdachtennegentig*) only when the second digit-pair isn't zero. Numbers write as
one word through *duizend*; *miljoen*/*miljard* are always separate words. The trema (diaeresis) marks
the unstressed *e* that starts a new syllable in compounds like *tweeëntwintig* (p. 20–22).

**Dates.** Written day-month-year with dashes (12-5-2007), not slashes; *op* for the day, *in* for
the year. Ordinal numbers are used for dates and are obligatory when the month is omitted (*de
zesentwintigste* = "the 26th") (p. 22–23).

**Currency and measure-unit number agreement.** Measure nouns (uur, kwartier, jaar, keer/maal, and
metric units) stay singular after a cardinal number or an indefinite quantifier (*drie euro*, *twee
meter stof*), but switch to plural when used with emphasis as an object in its own right rather than
a specific quantity (*Hij had drie euro's in z'n zak* = the three coins themselves) (pp. 23–25).

**-n on indefinite numbers marking "people only."** vele, sommige, enkele add -n (velen, sommigen,
enkelen) specifically when referring to persons rather than things (p. 26).

## Unit 5 — Telling the time (pp. 30–39)

### Vocabulary

| Term | Gloss | Part of Speech | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| eergisteren, gisteren, vandaag, morgen, overmorgen | day before yesterday, yesterday, today, tomorrow, day after tomorrow | adv. | |
| maandag–zondag | Monday–Sunday | noun | Not capitalized mid-sentence |
| morgen/ochtend, middag, avond, nacht | morning, afternoon, evening, night | noun (parts of day) | With clock-hour ranges given (6–12, 12–18, 18–24, 24–6) |
| vanmorgen, vanmiddag, vanavond, vannacht | this morning/afternoon/evening/tonight | adv. | |
| 's morgens, 's middags, 's avonds, 's nachts | every morning/afternoon/evening/night | adv. | Old genitive -s marks habitual recurrence; also 's winters, 's zomers (but *in de lente/herfst*) |
| tijdelijk, dagelijks, wekelijks, maandelijks, jaarlijks, maandags | temporary(-ily), daily, weekly, monthly, yearly, on Mondays | adj./adv. | Derived from tijd/dag/week/maand/jaar/maandag |
| kwart, half | quarter, half | numeral (clock time) | Dutch clock-time centers on the half-hour (half één = "half past twelve," not "half to one") |
| altijd, meestal, vaak, regelmatig, soms, zelden, nooit | always, usually, often, regularly, sometimes, seldom, never | adv. (frequency) | |
| eerst, dan/toen, daarna, vervolgens, verder, tenslotte | first, then, afterwards, next, further, lastly | adv. (sequence) | *toen* replaces *dan* specifically in past-tense narration |
| net, pas, al, nu, toen, straks, vroeg(er), laat, ooit, binnenkort | just, only/not until/recently, already, now, then, later-today, early(-formerly), late, someday, soon | adv. | *pas* is genuinely ambiguous ("recently" vs. "not until") — context-dependent |
| vorig, volgend | last, next | adj. | Follow adjective-ending rules |
| eeuw | century | noun | Ordinal numbers used for centuries (*de twintigste eeuw*) |

### Grammar points

**Clock time.** *uur* only appears with the exact hour (acht uur); all other times omit it (half
twaalf, kwart over drie). Dutch clock time pivots around the half-hour, referencing "before/after the
next half-hour" past :20 (*tien voor half één* = 12:20) (p. 32–33).

**Singular vs. plural after cardinal numbers for time nouns.** dag/maand/week/eeuw/minuut/seconde
pluralize after a number (*drie dagen*); uur/kwartier/jaar/keer/maal stay singular after a number
(*drie uur*) — the same singular/plural split pattern as Unit 4's general measure-unit rule (pp.
33–34).

**Adverb position.** Time adverbs normally follow the conjugated verb; if fronted, subject–verb
inversion follows (p. 35).

## Unit 6 — Articles and nouns (pp. 40–44)

### Vocabulary

| Term | Gloss | Part of Speech | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| de, het, een | the (common), the (neuter), a/an | article | de ≈ 2× as numerous as het; no reliable rule for which nouns take which |
| suiker, water, koffie, thee | sugar, water, coffee, tea | noun (uncountable) | Illustrate the no-article-when-indefinite-and-uncountable pattern |
| leraar, Nederlander, Amsterdammer, christen | teacher, Dutchman, Amsterdammer, Christian | noun (profession/affiliation) | Take **no** indefinite article in predicate position ("Peter is leraar," unlike English "a teacher") unless modified by an adjective |
| bankrekening, postkantoor, wijnglas | bank account, post office, wine glass | noun (compound) | Written as one word (unlike English); gender is set by the *second* element |

### Grammar points

**Definite/indefinite × countable/uncountable grid.** All nouns take a definite article in both
singular and plural (plural is always *de*); *een* only appears with countable singular nouns;
uncountable and plural-indefinite nouns take no article at all. Definiteness marks whether the
listener already knows the referent or the speaker is making a general statement vs. a specific one
(pp. 40–41).

## Unit 8 — Object pronouns (pp. 52–57)

### Vocabulary

| Term | Gloss | Part of Speech | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| mij/me, jou/je, u | me, you (inf.), you (formal) | pers. pron. obj. sg. | Stressed/unstressed pairs as with subject pronouns |
| hem/'m, haar/d'r, het/'t | him/it, her, it | pers. pron. obj. sg. 3rd | 'm, d'r, 't tagged `colloquial` — "mostly colloquial and more common in speech than writing"; d'r only for people |
| ons | us | pers. pron. obj. pl. 1st | |
| ze, hun, hen | them | pers. pron. obj. pl. 3rd | Three-way split: ze = most common, persons+things; hen = direct object/after-preposition, persons; hun = indirect object without preposition, persons — but "in speech the Dutch hardly distinguish anymore between hen and hun" (a genuine, source-stated ongoing register/usage collapse) |

### Grammar points

**hem vs. die for de-word objects.** hem can refer to a thing (a de-word) as well as a person, but
never sentence-initially — in that position it's replaced by *die* (*Hem vind ik goed* → *Die vind ik
goed*). The same fronting substitution applies to unstressed *ze* → *die* (p. 53–54).

## Unit 9 — Demonstrative pronouns (pp. 58–61)

### Vocabulary

| Term | Gloss | Part of Speech | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| deze, die (de-words) / dit, dat (het-words) | this/that | dem. pron. | Close (deze/dit) vs. far (die/dat); die/deze plural for all genders |

### Grammar points

Distance need not be *physical* — it can encode temporal distance (*deze maand* "this month," *dat
jaar* "that year") or simply the order in which two things are mentioned, with *deze* for the
first-mentioned and *die* for the second, independent of real spatial proximity. *dit*/*dat* also
function as preliminary/dummy subjects with linking verbs (zijn, worden, lijken) before the real
predicate noun, agreeing in number with that predicate rather than staying invariantly singular
(*Dit zijn mijn ouders*) (pp. 58–60).

## Unit 11 — Adjective endings (pp. 67–72)

### Vocabulary

| Term | Gloss | Part of Speech | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| groot | big | adj. | Model adjective for the attributive/predicate -e contrast |
| metalen, houten, zilveren, wollen, glazen, bronzen, gouden, ijzeren, papieren, stenen, stalen, porceleinen, leren, zijden, katoenen, marmeren, stoffen, fluwelen | metal, wooden, silver(en), woolen, glass(en), bronze(n), gold(en), iron(en), paper(en), stone(en), steel(en), porcelain(en), leather(en), silk(en), cotton(en), marble(en), material(en), velvet(en) | adj. (*stofadjectieven*, material-adjectives) | A closed class ending in -en, never inflected further, attributive-only (never predicate — the predicate equivalent uses *van* + noun) |
| plastic, nylon, rayon, aluminium, platina, rubber | plastic, nylon, rayon, aluminum, platinum, rubber | adj. (material, loanword) | Exceptional material-adjectives that neither take -en nor -e |
| gebakken, bedorven, gestolen, verloren | baked/fried, spoiled, stolen, lost | adj. (irregular past participle) | Irregular past participles used adjectivally end in -en and never inflect further, unlike regular ones (gekookte, verstuurde) which do take -e |
| eigen, verlegen, dronken, open, tevreden, volwassen, even | own, shy, drunk, open, satisfied, grown-up, even | adj. | A small closed class already ending in -en that never changes form |

### Grammar points

**The core rule.** Predicate adjectives never inflect (het huis is groot). Attributive adjectives
take -e *except* when modifying an indefinite, singular het-word with no further qualifier (*een
groot huis*, but *de grote auto*, *grote huizen*, *mijn grote huis* [possessives always trigger -e],
*dit grote huis* [demonstratives always trigger -e]). Indefinite non-countable de-nouns still take -e
on their adjective (*warme koffie*); indefinite non-countable het-nouns do not (*warm water*) (pp.
67–69). The spelling changes already established for verb conjugation/plural formation (vowel
doubling/dropping, s→z, f→v) apply identically to adjective inflection (p. 70).

## Unit 12 — The adjective in comparison (pp. 73–77)

### Vocabulary

| Term | Gloss | Part of Speech | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| mooier/mooist, leuker/leukst, groter/grootst, duurder/duurst | more beautiful/most, nicer/nicest, bigger/biggest, more expensive/most expensive | adj. (comparative/superlative) | Regular -er/-(e)st(e) pattern, unlike English's periphrastic "more/most" for polysyllabic adjectives |
| belangrijk, interessant | important, interesting | adj. (polysyllabic) | Still take -er/-st(e) directly, unlike English |
| goed/beter/best, veel/meer/meest, weinig/minder/minst, graag/liever/liefst | good/better/best, much/more/most, little/less/least, gladly/preferably/most-preferred | adj./adv. (irregular comparison) | graag is technically an adverb with no literal English equivalent, expressing degree of liking |

### Grammar points

Predicate superlatives are always preceded by *het* (*Dit huis is het grootst(e)*); attributive
superlatives always take -e (never used indefinitely — *een duurste auto* would be ungrammatical).
Spelling rules for the comparative mirror those already seen: -r-final adjectives add -d- (duurder);
-s/-f final adjectives voice to -z-/-v- (bozer, liever); double vowels in a closed syllable become
single in the open syllable created by -er (groter); short-vowel single consonants double (dikker);
-s-final adjectives form the superlative with -t rather than -st (het viest) (pp. 73–75).

## Unit 14 — The adverb *er* (pp. 87–91)

### Grammar points

*er* has three distinct grammatical functions, all covered: **(1) existential/indefinite-subject
announcer** — introduces an indefinite subject that follows later in the sentence (*Er is een museum
op de Prinsengracht*), extending beyond *zijn* to other verbs, and combinable with question words
(*Wie is er aan de beurt?*). **(2) unstressed place indicator**, functioning like unstressed *daar*
but never able to open a sentence or carry stress — for emphasis it must be replaced by *daar*/*hier*.
**(3) quantity-replacement with a following number or indefinite quantifier** (veel, een paar, geen,
genoeg, voldoende), avoiding redundant repetition of an already-established noun (*Hoeveel eieren
hebben we nodig? We hebben er zes nodig*) (pp. 87–90). This is a genuinely dense, high-value grammar
point with no direct single-word English equivalent for any of the three functions.

## Unit 15 — Negatives (pp. 92–99)

### Vocabulary

| Term | Gloss | Part of Speech | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| niet | not | adv. (negation) | Negates verbs, adjectives, adverbs, prepositional phrases, time expressions, definite-article nouns, demonstratives, possessives, and er/daar/hier |
| geen | no/none | negative indef. article | Negates nouns with een or no article (indefinite) |
| wel | (emphatic contrastive affirmative, no direct translation) | adv. | Emphasizes a contrast against a preceding or implied negation (*Nee, maar wel melk* = "No, but I would like milk") |

### Grammar points

**niet vs. geen** hinges entirely on definiteness: geen negates an indefinite noun (Ø-article or
*een*); niet negates everything else. **Position of niet**: follows the conjugated verb, a time
expression, a definite object, or er/daar/hier; precedes a prepositional phrase, an adverb/adjective,
or a predicate adjective — but the position shifts specifically to mark contrastive focus (*Erik
werkt niet vandaag, maar morgen* moves niet earlier than the default to negate specifically
"today"). **nog/meer with niet/geen** contrast al ("not yet" vs. "already") and mark
cessation/finality ("no more") respectively (pp. 92–96).

| be-, er-, ge-, her-, ont-, ver- | (prefixes, always inseparable) | verb prefix | bestellen, ervaren, geloven, herhalen, ontdekken, vertrouwen, etc. |
| kijken naar → bekijken; antwoorden op → beantwoorden; spreken over → bespreken; klimmen op → beklimmen | look at→examine; answer(a letter)→answer(an email); speak about→discuss; climb on→climb (a mountain) | verb (be- transitivization) | The be- prefix systematically converts an intransitive verb+preposition into a transitive verb, sometimes intensifying the action |

### Grammar points

**Stress determines separability**: most separable prefixes carry the stress (ópbellen); most
inseparable prefixes are unstressed (bestéllen). In a main clause the separable particle moves to the
sentence-final position; with a modal verb present, the modal takes the conjugated-verb slot and the
separable infinitive (with its particle still attached) moves to the end instead. The **be-, er-, ge-,
her-, ont-, ver-** prefixes are semantically productive in specific, describable ways: **be-**
transitivizes (kijken naar → bekijken) and can derive verbs from nouns (straat → bestraten) or
adjectives (zuinig → bezuinigen); **her-** marks repetition (herhalen, herschrijven); **ont-** marks
undoing/reversal (ontdekken lit. "un-cover," ontharen "de-hair/shave") (pp. 106–109).

## Unit 18 — The present perfect tense (pp. 112–118)

### Vocabulary

| Term | Gloss | Part of Speech | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| gewerkt, gespeeld, gemaakt, getelefoneerd | worked, played, made/did, phoned | past ptcp. (weak) | Model weak/regular past participles: ge- + stem + -t/-d, per the "'T FoKSCHaaP" mnemonic (p,t,k,f,s,ch → -t; softer consonants → -d) |
| gekeken, gedronken, gelezen, geholpen | looked, drunk, read, helped | past ptcp. (strong) | Model strong past participles: ge- + stem-with-vowel-change + -en |
| ij→e, i→e, i→o, ie→o, ui→o, e→o | (strong-verb vowel-change classes for the past participle) | morphological pattern | kijken→gekeken, liggen→gelegen, drinken→gedronken, vliegen→gevlogen, ruiken→geroken, nemen→genomen |
| brengen→gebracht, denken→gedacht, doen→gedaan, eten→gegeten, gaan→gegaan, kopen→gekocht, slaan→geslagen, staan→gestaan, zien→gezien, zoeken→gezocht | bring, think, do, eat, go, buy, hit, stand, see, look for (past ptcp.) | past ptcp. (irregular) | Don't cleanly follow either the weak or strong pattern |

### Grammar points

Dutch's present perfect is used far more broadly than English's — it is the default tense for
narrating **completed, non-recurrent past events** in speech (contrasted with the simple past, which
narrates *recurrent* events or an ordered *series* of events — covered fully in Unit 19). **hebben
vs. zijn as auxiliary**: zijn is used specifically for verbs of directed motion (fietsen, rijden,
lopen, gaan, vliegen, reizen, rennen, varen) and change of state/substance (smelten, veranderen,
worden), plus a small memorized exception list (beginnen, stoppen, ophouden, blijven, komen,
gebeuren) that takes zijn despite not being motion/change verbs. **Separable-verb participles**
insert -ge- between the prefix and the stem (schoongemaakt); **inseparable-verb participles with
be-/er-/ge-/her-/ont-/ver-** take **no** ge- prefix at all (betaald, vergeten, ontdekt). **vergeten**
notably alternates hebben/zijn by sense: hebben for literally forgetting a physical object, zijn for
no-longer-knowing a piece of information — though "in speech, however, this distinction is often
neglected," a genuine source-flagged register/prescriptive-vs.-actual-usage gap (pp. 112–116).
**Modal-verb present perfect** keeps both the modal and the main verb as bare infinitives at the
sentence end, with neither turning into a past participle, except the four modals that CAN function
independently (kunnen-gekund, moeten-gemoeten, willen-gewild, mogen-gemogen) (p. 116).

## Unit 19 — The simple past tense (pp. 119–125)

### Grammar points

**Weak verbs**: -te added to stems ending in a 'T FoKSCHaaP consonant (p,t,k,f,s,ch); -de added
elsewhere; only two forms exist (singular = plural). Spelling rules mirror the present tense
(vowel-doubling in an open syllable, z/v→s/f devoicing, single/double-t or -d doubling in specific
verbs like zette, redde, praatte, beantwoordde, vs. reduction to one in others like zakte, paste,
krabde, legde). **Strong verbs** always involve a stem vowel change, again organized by pattern
class (ij→ee, i→a, i→o, ie→oo, ui→oo, e→a, e→o, a→oe, a→ie), fully cross-referenced against the
present-perfect vowel-change table in Unit 18/the Appendix — the two systems (simple-past vowel and
past-participle vowel) are related but not identical for the same verb, requiring separate
memorization. **hebben/zijn simple past** (had/hadden, was/waren) is used *more* frequently than
their own present-perfect forms in ordinary speech, a genuine register asymmetry the source states
explicitly. **Modal verbs** in the simple past have their own irregular singular/plural pairs (kon/
konden, moest/moesten, mocht/mochten, wilde-wou/wilden-wouden, zou/zouden) — note *wilde* and its
fully colloquial-register alternative *wou* (pp. 119–122).

## Unit 20 — The imperative (pp. 126–131)

### Vocabulary

| Term | Gloss | Part of Speech | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| eens, even, maar, toch, nou | (imperative-softening/coloring particles) | particle | eens/even soften and make casual; maar adds encouragement/permissiveness; toch is ambiguous friendly-vs.-irritated by intonation; nou marks impatience. All tagged `colloquial` |
| eens even, maar even, nou even, toch even, nou maar, nou toch | (stacked particle combinations) | particle combination | nou maar reads as friendlier/permissive; nou toch reads as impatient/irritated — an interesting minimal-pair inversion where the SAME two particles in different linear order shift register/tone |
| wees, weest | be (imperative sg./pl. of zijn) | verb (irregular imperative) | Zijn's imperative is irregular, unlike the regular stem-only pattern |

### Grammar points

The bare imperative is simply the verb stem (kom, ga, werk, help); adding a subject (jij for
singular, jullie/u for plural/formal) is now generally preferred over an archaic -t plural ending.
Verbs with stems in -v-/-z- devoice to -f/-s in the imperative (geven→geef, lezen→lees); -d after a
vowel is often optionally dropped (rijden→rij(d)). The infinitive itself, or a past participle, can
also serve as an impersonal command/prohibition (*Niet roken!* "No smoking," *Niet zeuren, dooreten*
"Don't whine, keep eating") — a distinctly public-notice/instructional register. The particle system
here is the fullest treatment in the book of the *oordeelspartikel* system flagged in Unit 13,
directly slang/register-relevant: particle choice and stacking order shift a bare command along a
friendliness↔impatience axis without changing its propositional content at all (pp. 126–130).

in a main clause the reflexive pronoun follows the conjugated verb; in a subordinate clause it
follows the subject instead (pp. 132–135). This chapter's idiom list is a genuinely rich vein of
colloquial fixed expressions built on the reflexive construction.

## Unit 23 — Word order (pp. 146–152)

### Grammar points

**Main clauses (hoofdzin)**: the conjugated verb always takes second position; other elements follow
the time–manner–place ordering template. Subject–verb inversion occurs when a non-subject element is
fronted, in yes/no questions (verb-first), and in the imperative (verb-first). Indirect object
precedes direct object unless the direct object is reduced to the pronoun *het*, in which case *het*
moves ahead of the indirect object; a prepositional indirect object (aan + noun) can follow the
direct object instead, and this reordering is actually preferred in speech. **Subordinate clauses
(bijzin)**: the conjugated verb moves to clause-final position; when a subordinate clause opens the
sentence, the main clause's verb immediately follows the comma (i.e., the entire subordinate clause
occupies the main clause's normal first-position slot). Conjugated auxiliaries/modals in a
subordinate clause are usually placed *before* the past participle/infinitive they govern, though
either order is grammatical. Coordinating conjunctions (en, maar, want, of) link two main clauses
without altering either clause's verb-second order; a large set of subordinating conjunctions (omdat,
toen, terwijl, sinds, etc.) trigger verb-final order in the clause they introduce. **Indirect speech**
is grammatically just a subordinate clause introduced by dat (statements) or a question word/of
(questions), verbs like zeggen/menen/denken/beweren/vragen/willen weten (pp. 146–151).

## Unit 24 — Relative clauses (pp. 153–156)

### Grammar points

**die** refers to de-word singular antecedents and all plural antecedents; **dat** to het-word
singular antecedents; **waar + preposition** to non-person antecedents preceded by a preposition
(waaraan, waarin, waarmee — note met→mee and tot→toe when fused with waar-); **wie** to person
antecedents preceded by a preposition (never die in that position); **wat** to an entire preceding
clause/fact, or after indefinite pronouns (iets, niets, alles, veel, weinig). The fused waar+
preposition form is frequently split in practice, with the bare preposition placed directly before
the verb instead (*Het land waaraan ik denk* ↔ *Het land waar ik aan denk*) — a genuinely common
colloquial-register alternative word order for the same underlying structure (pp. 153–154).

### Grammar points

Prepositions are organized by the relationship they express: time, motion/direction, static location,
cause/reason, possession, opinion, means, and a residual "other" set (ondanks, zonder). The
**double-preposition/postposition** structure is the unit's most distinctive grammatical content:
Dutch productively pairs a preposition before a noun with a second, semantically bonded element after
it (achter de dief aan "after the thief," door het park heen "all the way through the park," tegen
een paal aan "into a pole") to add a directional/telic nuance no single preposition alone would carry
— explicitly identified as a genuinely Dutch-specific structure with no direct one-word English
equivalent (pp. 157–163).

### Grammar points

Organizing strong verbs by their vowel-change class (rather than alphabetically) is itself the
book's stated pedagogical/mnemonic strategy — the same ablaut classes recur, with largely (but not
perfectly) identical vowel changes, across both the simple past (Unit 19) and the past participle
(Unit 18), meaning a learner who memorizes one axis of a given verb's pattern class gets partial,
but not complete, predictive power over the other axis. A final small "irregular" class mixes a
strong-looking past tense with a weak-looking (or vice versa) participle, or vice versa (jagen–
joeg–gejaagd; jagen's past tense is strong-looking but its participle is weak-looking) — genuinely
resistant to the ablaut-class system and requiring rote memorization (pp. 169–174).

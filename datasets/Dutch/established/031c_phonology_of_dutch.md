## Grammar points

### Lexical Phonology architecture: cyclic, word-level, and postlexical rule strata (§4.1, pp.53–57)

Booij adopts a three-level model of rule application: **cyclic level** (phonological and
morphological rules interleave, applying to progressively larger derived structures as affixes are
added one at a time — demonstrated with the derivation of *elitarisme* "elitism"), **word level**
(rules that apply after all morphology is complete but still within the lexicon — the paradigm case
is **Syllable-final Devoicing**, which must apply at this level rather than cyclically, since
applying it too early would incorrectly devoice the /d/ of *hoed* "hat" before the plural suffix
*-en* has a chance to resyllabify it as an onset), and the **postlexical level** (rules of connected
speech spanning phrase boundaries, deferred to Ch.7). A **Strict Cycle Condition** restricts cyclic
rules to derived environments unless they're informationally non-vacuous. **Continuous
syllabification** is assumed throughout: whenever morphology or phonology changes a word's
segmental content, syllable structure is locally recomputed (erasing a syllable node if its nucleus
is deleted, matching the template afresh) rather than being fixed once and never revisited.

### The P-rule / MP-rule distinction (§4.2, pp.57–58)

**P-rules** ("automatic phonological rules") are purely phonologically conditioned and hold without
lexical exception — they define the phonotactics of possible Dutch words. **MP-rules**
("morpholexical rules") are conditioned by specific morphemes or restricted lexical sets and *can*
have exceptions — Booij's diagnostic example: the diminutive schwa-insertion rule inserts a schwa
in *kannetje* "small jug" (from /kɑn+tjə/) but not in the phonologically parallel *kantje* "piece of
lace" (from /kɑnt+tjə/) — proving schwa-insertion there is a lexically-listed MP-rule, not an
absolute constraint on what sequences are pronounceable.

### Voice assimilation: Progressive and Regressive rules, richly exemplified (§4.2.1, pp.58–64)

**Progressive Voice Assimilation (PVA)**: a fricative devoices after an immediately preceding
voiceless obstruent, both within compounds/prosodic-word-boundary clusters (*opvallend*
"remarkable" /pv/→[pf], *zoutzuur* "hydrochloric acid" /tz/→[ts], *lachgas* "laughing gas"
/xɣ/→[xː]) and within single prosodic words (*advies* "advice" [ɑtfis], and acronyms like *ABVA*
[ɑpfa]). **Regressive Voice Assimilation (RVA)**: a voiceless obstruent becomes voiced before a
following voiced stop /b/ or /d/ (*klaphand* "flat tyre" /pb/→[bː], *misdaad* "crime" /sd/→[zd]),
which can *undo* the effect of Final Devoicing (an underlying /z/ devoiced in coda position gets
revoiced again before a following /b/, as in *niesbui* "fit of sneezing"). PVA is ordered before RVA
by the Elsewhere Principle (more specific rule wins). Crucially, **verbal inflectional suffixes
follow a different, non-assimilatory regularity**: the past-tense suffix and past-participle /t~d/
alternation is governed instead by **Laryngeal Spreading** — an underlying placeless coronal stop
/Də/ that simply copies the [voice] value of the immediately preceding stem-final consonant (voiced
stem→/də/, voiceless stem→/tə/), which is why the resulting cluster is exempt from ordinary
syllable-final devoicing (the doubly-linked [+voice] feature blocks re-application via the Uniform
Applicability Condition).

### Nasal assimilation: place, not voice (§4.2.2, pp.64–65)

Tautosyllabic nasal-obstruent clusters are always homorganic (nasal takes on the following
obstruent's place: *damp* [m], *tand* [n], *oranje* [ɲ], *dank* [ŋ]) — the coronal nasal /n/ alone
is unspecified for place underlyingly and gets it by spreading; non-coronal nasals resist this
(*wangzak*, *damkampioen* do NOT assimilate). This asymmetry (only /n/, the "default"/unmarked
nasal, participates) recurs across the chapter's rule system as a broader Coronal-underspecification
pattern. The same coronal-only nasal assimilation also crosses word/prefix boundaries in phrases and
compounds (*in Parijs* → [ɪmparɛis], *onvast* "unstable" → [ɔɱvɑst]).

### Hiatus avoidance: Homorganic Glide Insertion and Prevocalic Schwa Deletion (§4.2.3, pp.65–68)

Adjacent vowels within a single prosodic word are systematically repaired. If the first vowel is
schwa, it deletes (*Romein* /romə+ɛin/ → [romɛin]). If the first vowel is /ɑ/, a glottal stop is
inserted before the second vowel in the syllable bearing main stress (*paélla* → [paʔɛlja]).
Otherwise, a transitional glide is inserted whose backness/roundness match the *preceding* vowel:
front rounded vowels insert [ɥ] (*dúo* → [dyɥo]), front unrounded vowels insert [j] (*dieet* →
[dijet]), back vowels insert [ʋ] (*Ruanda* → [ruʋɑndɑ]). This is genuinely useful typological data
on how Dutch resolves vowel hiatus — a mechanism directly transferable to a conlang phonotactic
system that wants to avoid vowel clusters. Both rules apply obligatorily within a prosodic word but
only optionally (in casual speech) across prosodic-word boundaries (compounds, prefixed words).

### Degemination (§4.2.4, pp.68–69)

Confirms and formalizes the Ch.3 observation: adjacent identical consonants that arise through
morphological concatenation are obligatorily reduced to one within a prosodic word (*eet* /et+t/ →
[et], *grootte* /ɣrot+tə/ → [ɣrotə]), but only optionally across a compound/prefix boundary — which
means Dutch can phonetically distinguish two structurally different words in casual-vs.-careful
speech by whether the geminate is preserved (*ver-assen* "to cremate" [vɛrɑsən] vs. *ver-rassen* "to
surprise" [vɛrːɑsən]).

### Diminutive allomorph selection — Booij's conditioning statement (§4.3.1, pp.69–75)

Complementary formal statement to Dutch's existing diminutive extraction (`003_chapter22_
diminutives.md`): the five allomorphs *-tje, -je, -pje, -kje, -etje* are derived from one underlying
form /tjə/ via ordered MP-rules, conditioned as follows — **-je** after a stem-final obstruent;
**-etje** after a sonorant consonant when the preceding vowel is short and stressed (primary or
secondary); **-pje** after /m/ (elsewhere than the -etje environment); **-kje** after the velar
nasal /ŋ/ (elsewhere than the -etje environment); **-tje** everywhere else (the default/elsewhere
case). Exceptional doublets exist (*wieltje*/*wieletje*, with *bloemetje*/*bloempje* carrying a real
semantic split — *bloemetje* can mean "bunch of flowers", *bloempje* cannot); a second exception
class involves stem allomorphy with an independent short/long-vowel alternation feeding into the
diminutive (*schip* [sxɪp] → dim. *schipje* [sxɪpjə], but note *scheepje* also occurs with a
semantic split — a *glaasje* is a drinking glass or microscope slide, a *glasje* a spectacles lens).

### -er/-aar allomorphy avoids an /rVr/ clash (§4.3.2, pp.73–75)

The suffix *-er* (comparative, de-verbal agent-noun, de-nominal noun) has a competing allomorph
*-aar* that appears specifically when the base already ends in schwa+coronal-sonorant — motivated
purely by Dutch's cross-linguistically-attested avoidance of the sequence [rVr] within a word
(*bezem* "sweep" → *bezemer* "sweeper" is fine, but the parallel comparative-suffix-avoidance
pattern surfaces as *-aar* elsewhere, e.g. *kibbelen* "quarrel" → *kibbelaar* not *kibbeler*). A
related MP-rule inserts /d/ between two /r/s separated only by a schwa (*dienaar*→*diender*
"servant"-type forms), giving *-der* as a third related allomorph.

### Non-native (Latinate) lexicon has its own, largely separate morphology (§4.4, pp.75–86)

Booij's central claim: Dutch's word-formation component distinguishes native vs. non-native input
words as a real grammatical fact, not just an etymological curiosity — non-native suffixes (a long
catalogued list: *-aal, -aan, -aat, -abel, -age, -air, -andus, -ast, -eel, -eer, -ees, -esk, -ein,
-eur, -eus, -iaan, -ica/-icus, -ide, -ieel, -ief, -iek, -ier, -iet, -ieus, -ine, -isch, -iseer,
-isme, -ist*) attach only to non-native bases, while native suffixes like *-heid* attach to either.
Within this non-native subsystem, several independent, systematic alternations recur: **Learned
Vowel Backing** (mid vowels back to [+back] before an adjacent non-native suffix: *fundamenteel*→
*fundamentalisme*), a *-iek*→[is] alternation before non-native suffixes beginning in /i/ (*katholiek*
→*katholicisme* [katolisismə]), Latin-inherited prefix place-assimilation for *in-/con-* (*im-,
in-, il-, ir-*; *com-, con-, col-, cor-*), a /t/→/ts/ or /s/ alternation before morpheme /i/
(*actie* [ɑksi] vs. *actief* [ɑktif]), three-way /ŋ~ŋɣ~k/ variation in velar-nasal-final loanwords,
and a systematic vowel-lengthening-before-vowel-initial-suffix rule restricted to unstressed final
VC syllables of non-native words (*doctor*→*doctoraat*, with an irregular extension to the plural
suffix *-en* specifically, not *-s*). All of this is framed as a "pan-European" shared root
inventory whose behavior doesn't generalize to the native lexicon at all.

### Native-lexicon allomorphy is lexically governed but historically systematic (§4.5, pp.86–93)

Several alternations trace to Middle Dutch sound changes that affected words one-by-one (lexical
diffusion) and then stopped, leaving synchronic doublets, several with real semantic/register
splits — directly useful precedent for how a synthesized slang register could plausibly diverge from
a "standard" register through exactly this kind of partial, lexically-uneven sound-change
application:
- **Open-syllable vowel lengthening** in singular/plural noun pairs (*schip*/*schepen*), now
  unproductive and full of lexicalized exceptions in both directions.
- **/də/-deletion** word-finally and intervocalically (*snede*→*snee* "slice", *broeder*→*broer*
  "brother"), which very often produced a register split between the older/formal *-de* form and
  the newer/plain form — and, in compounds, only ever one of the two allomorphs is grammatical.
- **Word-final schwa deletion after a voiced coronal** (*einde*→*eind* "end"), regionally uneven
  (lexicalized differently in North Holland vs. standard Dutch for the same word), also feeding
  compound-initial position (*aard(e)*→*aards* "earthly" vs. *aardappel* "potato", never
  *aardeappel*).
- **/d/→glide or Ø alternation before a following schwa**, with the glide/zero form explicitly the
  more informal register variant (*brede*→*breje* "wide", *goede*→*goeie* "good") — lexically
  governed (*hoeden* "hats" can never lose its /d/) but with the informal variant sometimes
  becoming the *only* grammatical form in a fixed/idiomatic expression (*ouwehoeren* "to nag").
- **A second, distinct /d/-deletion** confined to a short, closed list of high-frequency strong
  verbs ending in diphthong+/d/ (*rijden*→*rij* "ride", *houden*→*hou* "keep"), producing
  systematic double past-tense forms (*rijden*: sg. *reed*, alternate *ree*) — phonologically
  near-identical verbs that aren't on this list never show the alternation.
- **Ø~/k/ alternation** after the velar nasal (*jong*→*jongvee* "young cattle" vs. no [k];
  *oorspronkelijk* "original" WITH [k]), now unproductive/lexically frozen.
- **Fricative devoicing before *-elijk*/*-enis***, restricted specifically to labial and coronal
  fricatives (not stops, not other obstruents): *graaf*→*grafelijk*, *droef*→*droefenis*, but
  *heb*→*onhebbelijk* (stays voiced, since /b/ isn't a fricative).

### Two allophonic (non-alternating, purely phonetically-conditioned) rules (§4.6, pp.93–95)

**Pre-/r/ lengthening**: all vowels lengthen before a *tautosyllabic* /r/ within the same prosodic
word (*koraal* [koːral]), obligatorily — but this famously does *not* apply across a compound
boundary even when the second member happens to be /r/-initial (*keu-ring* "pig's ring" [kørɪŋ] vs.
suffixed *keuring* "selection" [køːrɪŋ], a genuine minimal pair for prosodic-word-hood). A small set
of strong-verb past-tense forms with exceptional VCC rhymes (*wierp, stierf*) are systematically
exempt, explained by assuming an underlying short /ɪ/ rather than long /i/ for these forms
specifically. **Palatalization**: /s, z, t, n/ shift their primary place of articulation (not just
add a secondary one) to postalveolar before /j/ — a genuine place-feature-shift rule, not mere
coarticulation, confirmed by *handje* "little hand" showing double palatalization (both the
stem-final nasal and the devoiced-then-palatalized former /d/ undergo it in the same word).

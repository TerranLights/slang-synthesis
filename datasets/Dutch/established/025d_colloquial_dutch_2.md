## Grammar points

### Language point 1 — Pluralisation of nouns (detailed system)

A far more systematic treatment than any prior *Colloquial Dutch 2* chunk — genuinely new content
relative to `001_chapters1-3_pronunciation_spelling_plural.md`'s plural coverage (that file
documented the basic **-en**/**-s** split plus **-eren** doublets from *Dutch: An Essential
Grammar*; this source adds the full conditioning rule set with denser examples):

1. **Nouns taking -en** (the majority default): straightforward addition (**boek→boeken**,
   **krant→kranten**), sometimes triggering the standard single/double-vowel or single/double-
   consonant spelling adjustment (**aap→apen**, **kat→katten**, **huis→huizen**). A small closed
   set of common nouns show a *short-vowel-singular / long-vowel-plural* alternation not
   predictable from spelling rules alone — both common-gender (**dag→dagen**, **god→goden**,
   **hof→hoven**, **weg→wegen**, **stad→steden**) and neuter (**blad→bladen**, **been→benen**,
   **glas→glazen**, **graf→graven**, **slot→sloten**, **verbod→verboden**, plus **lid→leden**,
   **schip→schepen**) — must be learned per-item. A further small set of neuter nouns takes
   **-eren** (cognate with English "children"): **been→beenderen** ("bones," contrasting with
   **been→benen** "legs" — a genuine homograph-resolved-by-plural-form pair), **blad→bladeren**
   ("leaves," vs. **bladen** "magazines" — same pattern), **ei→eieren**, **goed→goederen**,
   **kind→kinderen**, **volk→volkeren**. **Kleren** ("clothes") is a contracted form of the
   archaic **-eren** plural **klederen** of **het kleed**.
2. **Nouns taking -s**: (A) nouns ending in unstressed **-el/-em/-en/-er/-e/-ie** and
   diminutives in **-je** (**tafel→tafels**, **secretaresse→secretaresses**, **koekje→koekjes**);
   note **kindertjes**/**kleertjes** are built off the plural of the non-diminutive form rather
   than the diminutive singular. Exceptions exist even within this class (**artikel→artikelen**,
   **middel→middelen**, **christen→christenen**, **reden→redenen**, **maatregel→maatregelen**,
   **verschijnsel→verschijnselen**) and nouns ending in *stressed* **-ie** take **-ën** instead
   (**melodie→melodieën**, **symfonie→symfonieën**). (B) Loanwords still felt as foreign
   (**club→clubs**, **restaurant→restaurants**, **telefoon→telefoons**, **perron→perrons**,
   **trottoir→trottoirs**). (C) A few native words for male beings (**broer→broers**,
   **maat→maats**, **bruidegom→bruidegoms**, **oom→ooms**, **kok→koks**, **zoon→zoons** — note
   **zoon** also has an alternate plural **zonen**, common in firm names like **Van Goor en
   Zonen**). (D) Nouns ending in **-ier** split by referent: **-s** for people
   (**kruidenier→kruideniers**, **winkelier→winkeliers**, **portier→portiers** "doormen"), **-en**
   for things (**formulier→formulieren**, **scharnier→scharnieren**, **portier→portieren**
   "doors" — a genuine minimal-pair homograph resolved only by plural choice); exceptions
   **officier→officieren**, **scholier→scholieren**.
3. **Nouns taking -'s**: nouns ending in **-a/-o/-u/-y/-i** (all of foreign origin) — **firma→
   firma's**, **baby→baby's**, **auto→auto's**, **ski→ski's**, **paraplu→paraplu's**.
pp.54-57.

### Language point 2 — Demonstratives

**Deze**/**dit** ("this/these") and **die**/**dat** ("that/those") pattern exactly like the
definite article by gender/number: common-gender singular **deze**/**die**, neuter singular
**dit**/**dat**, all-gender plural **deze**/**die** — mirroring **de**/**deze**/**die** vs.
**het**/**dit**/**dat**. Dutch preserves archaic case-inflected forms of **deze**/**die** in fixed
expressions only (not productive): **(een) dezer dagen**, **op de 18de dezer**,
**dientengevolge**. In "high style," the genitive **diens** ("his") can replace possessive **zijn**
specifically to resolve referential ambiguity (**Piet ging met zijn vriend en diens zoon** — "Piet
went with his friend and his [=the friend's] son"). The source is explicit that this whole archaic
layer is **passive-recognition-only** vocabulary for the learner, not something to actively
reproduce — a useful register marker distinguishing "elevated"/literary Dutch from the active
colloquial register this book otherwise prioritizes. pp.59-60.

### Language point 3 — Expressing "one/one's"

**Men** is the direct equivalent of English "one," but — like English "one" — belongs to formal
register; in everyday conversation it's replaced by unstressed **je** or **ze** (paralleling
English "you"/"they" for the same impersonal function). **Je** used this way can co-occur with
addressing someone as **u** (i.e. it's register-neutral for this specific impersonal function even
when the same speaker uses **u** for direct address). **Men** is subject-only; when an object
pronoun is needed, colloquial style uses **je**/**jou** or **iemand**, while formal style prefers a
passive construction instead. Possessive "one's" = **zijn**; reflexive "oneself" = **zich** (**Men
moet zijn best doen en zich netjes gedragen**). The pronoun **we** (never **wij**) is also used
impersonally, as in English "we" for generic reference (**Hier hebben we het Paleis op de Dam**).
pp.62-63.

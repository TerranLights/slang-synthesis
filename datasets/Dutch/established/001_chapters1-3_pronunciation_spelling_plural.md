# Dutch — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Chapters 1-3 (Pronunciation, Spelling, The Plural)

**Source:** Shetter, W.Z. & Van der Cruysse-Van Antwerpen, I., *Dutch: An Essential Grammar* (9th
ed., Routledge), pp. 6-27 (PDF pages 21-42) — Chapter 1 "Pronunciation," Chapter 2 "Spelling,"
Chapter 3 "The Plural."

**Coverage note:** every grammar point and every distinct vocabulary item glossed by the source in
these three chapters, plus every explicit register/regional annotation the source itself marks.
This source has a real text layer (extracted via the Read tool's PDF page-rendering); no
vision-reading was required and the "vision-reading guard" / marginalia check does not apply — no
handwritten marginalia was present. `Vision Reading Confidence` is `n/a` throughout per dispatch
instructions.

**Excluded as drill material (per coverage rule):** the "Let's try it" exercises at §2.3.1 (add
`-en`/`-e` to a word list) and §3.3.1 (pluralize a word list) are un-glossed drill tables — the
source gives the bare words and expects the reader to supply the answer, so no new
term-with-gloss pairs are actually introduced by them, even though a few of the listed stems (e.g.
`broodje`, `sufferd`, `tomaat`, `hoofdstuk`) don't appear glossed elsewhere in this chunk. Per the
coverage rule these repeated drill exercises are skipped.

**A note on regional phonetic variation vs. lexical regionality:** §1.3 (Consonants) explicitly
flags that four sounds are realized differently by region (the pronunciation of `g`, `v`, `w`, and
`r`) — e.g. `r` is uvular [ʀ] in the north/cities but trilled/tapped [r] through much of the rest of
the country and throughout Flanders. This is genuine source-marked regional annotation, but it
describes *how a word is pronounced*, not *which words are used/known where* — the underlying words
(`geel`, `veel`, `wat`, `room`, etc.) are ordinary national-currency core vocabulary, not regionally
restricted lexemes. Per the column-rule distinction, this project's `Attested Region`/`Geographic
Scope` columns are reserved for source-marked *lexical* regionality (comparative B/C/S-style tags,
"this word only in region X"); this pronunciation-only regional variation is recorded in the
`Notes` column of the relevant vocabulary rows instead, so it isn't lost but also doesn't overload
columns meant for lexical scope.

**A note on one ambiguous minimal-pair entry:** p.18 (§2.1.2) gives a minimal-pair table
contrasting long-vowel/single-consonant spelling against short-vowel/double-consonant spelling. One
pair render as **reden** "reason" (noun, open syllable, long vowel) vs. **redden** "to save/rescue"
(verb, closed syllable, short vowel) — this is the standard textbook minimal pair for this exact
rule. It is flagged specifically in that row's Notes because reading it correctly required
recognizing the well-known pair rather than a purely mechanical read.

## Grammar points

### 1.1 Vowel inventory: front/back, rounded/front-rounded, high/low (p.6-7)

Dutch vowels are classified by tongue position (front vs. back) and, distinctively, by a set of
**front-rounded** vowels that don't occur in English but do in French and German — produced with
the tongue raised toward the front while the lips stay rounded. The high/low dimension (tongue
proximity to the roof of the mouth) is a second axis, e.g. Eng. "eat" (high) vs. "add" (low). The
full inventory given (§1.1.3) has 14 monophthongs with IPA and minimal-pair examples, e.g. `ie`
[i]/`i` [ɪ] (short/long-adjacent pair) contrasted against `ee` [e:]/`e` [ɛ] and `oe` [u]/`o` [ɔ], plus
the front-rounded set `uu` [y], `u` [ʏ], `eu` [ø:].

### 1.1.4 Vowel quantity: always-short, always-long, and r-conditioned length (p.8)

Dutch vowels differ not just in quality but in duration. `a e i o u` are always short; `aa ee oo eu`
are always long; `ie oe uu` are long only before `r`, short everywhere else (including word-final
position, e.g. `zie`, `hoe`, `nu`).

### 1.1.5 The neutral (schwa) vowel (p.8)

An unstressed neutral vowel, phonetically like short `u` [ʏ], occurs in unstressed syllables and has
several spellings — `e` (most common, e.g. `behalve`), `ij` (e.g. `mogelijk`), and `i` (e.g.
`twintig`).

### 1.2 Diphthongs (p.9)

A diphthong is defined as the succession of two distinct vowels within a single syllable. The
chapter tabulates 10 spelling-to-sound diphthong correspondences with their IPA decomposition (e.g.
`ei`=`ij` [ɛi], `ou`=`au` [ɔu], `ui` [œy] — itself front-rounded — `oei` [ui], `ooi` [o:i], `aai`
[a:i], `ieu` [iy], `eeu` [e:y]). Notably `ei` and `ij` are two distinct spellings representing the
identical sound.

### 1.3 Consonant inventory and regional allophony (p.10-12)

Consonants are voiced or voiceless. The chapter's table covers the full consonant inventory with IPA
and remarks. Several points are flagged as needing special attention for English-speaking learners:
Dutch voiceless stops (`p t k`) are unaspirated (no puff of breath, unlike English); `ch`/`sch`
represent sounds without direct English equivalents ([x], [sx]); `g` and `r` both show marked
regional pronunciation variation (see vocabulary table Notes above for `geel`, `room`, `veel`,
`wat`).

### 1.3.1 Summary: final devoicing (p.12)

`b` and `d` are always pronounced voiceless ([p], [t]) at the end of a word, though the spelling
still shows the underlying voiced consonant — this spelling-vs-pronunciation gap is the same for `v`
and `z`, which also devoice word-finally without a spelling change.

### 1.4 Assimilation (voicing agreement across consonant clusters) (p.13)

Two or more consecutive consonants (except `l m n r`) must agree in voicing, whether within one word
or across a word boundary. Four sub-rules are given: (1) when a spirant (`f s ch g v z`) combines
with a stop (`b d p t k`), the stop's voicing controls the whole group (e.g. `hoofden` → [hoovden]);
(2) when two stops combine and either is voiced, both become voiced (`uitbreiden` → [uidbreiden]);
(3) when two spirants combine, the group is voiceless (`afzetten` → [afsetten]); (4) a final
voiceless consonant is often voiced when immediately followed by a vowel (`heb ik` → [heb ik], i.e.
"hep" + "ik" pronounced with the b re-voiced).

### 1.5 Word stress (p.14)

Main stress generally falls on a word's first syllable, as in English. Prefixes/suffixes containing
the unstressed `e` (`be- er- ge- her- ver- -e -en -er`) plus the prefix `ont-` are never stressed.
Where stress placement is ambiguous, the source marks it typographically with an underline beneath
the stressed syllable (e.g. `stadhuis`, `toevallig`, `Amsterdam`).

### 2.1 Closed vs. open syllables as the basis of the spelling system (p.15-18)

Dutch spelling logic hinges on the closed/open syllable distinction: a syllable is **closed** when
it ends in a consonant, **open** when it ends in a vowel. Two consonants between vowels normally
split between syllables (`man-nen`); one consonant between vowels goes with the following syllable,
leaving the first syllable open (`bo-men`). This yields the core spelling rules: the short vowels `a
e i o u` are always spelled with one letter and can only occur in closed syllables; when a suffix
(e.g. plural `-en`) would leave such a syllable open, the following consonant is **doubled** to keep
it closed (`man`→`mannen`). Word-final doubled consonants never occur (except a few loanwords). When
a short vowel is already followed by 2+ consonants, no change is needed on suffixation
(`arm`→`armen`).

### 2.1.1-2.1.2 Long vowels: single letter in open syllables, doubled in closed (p.16-18)

The complementary rule: `aa ee oo uu` (plus, unchanged either way, the multi-letter vowels/diphthongs
`ie oe uu` and all true diphthongs) are spelled with **two** letters in a closed syllable but
**one** letter once a suffix opens that syllable (`laan`→`lanen`). This produces the book's own
terminology: vowels that can occur in an open syllable (`aa ee oo uu ie oe eu`) are called LONG
vowels; `a e i o u` (closed-syllable-only) are called SHORT vowels. One genuine ambiguity flagged by
the source: a word-final `ee` must always be written with two letters to distinguish it from the
schwa spelled `e` (`zee` "sea" vs. `ze` "she") — but this distinction isn't preserved mid-word,
producing occasional real ambiguity (`regeren` "to govern" vs. `regelen` "to adjust," both spelled
with a single medial `e` despite different vowel qualities).

### 2.2 The f/v and s/z alternation (p.18-20)

Many words ending in `f` or `s` (e.g. `brief`, `huis`) replace that consonant with, respectively, `v`
or `z` when a vowel-initial suffix attaches and the consonant becomes syllable-initial (`brief`→
`brieven`). This does **not** happen when the `s` is preceded by `p`, `t`, or `k` (`rups`→`rupsen`).
When a suffix is added, and the consonant is instead **doubled** rather than alternated, no v/z
change occurs (`stof`→`stoffen`). A handful of exceptions to the general alternation rule are noted
(`biograaf`→`biografen`, `elf`→`elfen`, `kous`→`kousen`, `dans`→`dansen`). This same alternation
applies beyond nouns, to adjectives and verbs (`lief`, `serieus`→`serieuze`, `grijs`→`grijze`).

### 2.3 Pronunciation of the ending -en (p.20)

In spoken Dutch, especially in the western Netherlands, the final `-n` of the very common `-en`
ending is regularly dropped in speech (`lopen`→[lope]), leaving the short neutral vowel. This is
purely a pronunciation phenomenon — the `-n` is never dropped in writing.

### 3.1 The plural in -en, and its irregularities (p.23-24)

The regular/default Dutch plural marker is `-en`, fully subject to the Ch.2 spelling rules
(consonant doubling, single/double vowel-letter alternation, f/v and s/z alternation). A small,
high-frequency irregular subgroup instead **lengthens** the stem vowel in the plural, marked
orthographically by the single following consonant (`bad`→`baden`, `dag`→`dagen`, ... `verdrag`→
`verdragen`) — within this subgroup, three words (`stad`→`steden`, `schip`→`schepen`, `lid`→`leden`)
shift to a different vowel outright rather than simply lengthening the same one. A further small
group instead pluralizes in `-eren`; strikingly, two of these words are **meaning-differentiated
doublets** where the same singular has two different plurals depending on sense: `been` = "bone"
(pl. `beenderen`) vs. "leg" (pl. `benen`); `blad` = "leaf" (pl. `bladeren`) vs. "tray/sheet" (pl.
`bladen`).

### 3.2 The plural in -s (p.25-26)

Three main groups take `-s` instead of `-en`: (1) most words ending in an unstressed `-el -em -en
-er -aar -erd -e`; (2) **every diminutive**, marked by the `-je` ending (diminutives are covered in
depth in Ch.22, not this chunk); (3) words of foreign origin, often vowel-final, where an apostrophe
is inserted before the `-s` when the final vowel is `a i o u y` (`auto`→`auto's`) but not otherwise
(`microfoon`→`microfoons`). A few native Dutch words (`oom`, `broer`, `zoon`) fall into this `-s`
category despite not fitting the phonological profile of group 1.

### 3.3 Other (Latin-derived and stress-shifting) plurals (p.26)

Words belonging to a "more intellectual sphere" often keep a Latin-style plural in `-i` or `-a`
(`catalogus`→`catalogi`, `museum`→`musea`). Most words ending in `-or` pluralize in `-en` with the
stress shifting one syllable to the right onto that ending, though many also have an alternative
plural in `-s` (`professor`→`professoren`/`professors`). Words ending in `-heid` form their plural in
`-heden` (`mogelijkheid`→`mogelijkheden`).

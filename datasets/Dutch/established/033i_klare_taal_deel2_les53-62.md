## Grammar points

### 1. Meaning-differentiated diminutive pairs — material vs. object (Les 58, printed p. 152)

Beyond established/003's coverage of diminutive *formation*, this lesson flags a semantic pattern:
for some nouns, the base form names a raw material/substance while the diminutive names a specific
object made of it, not merely "a small version" of the same thing. *krijt* ("chalk," the material) vs.
*krijtje* ("a piece/stick of chalk," a countable object); *ijs* ("ice," the substance) vs. *ijsje*
("an ice cream," what a vendor sells); *chocola* ("chocolate," the substance) vs. *chocolaatje* ("a
chocolate," one piece of confectionery). Also flagged: *lied* (a song sung in church/a formal
concert setting, plural *liederen*) vs. *liedje* (a children's song or popular song, plural
*liedjes*) — here the diminutive marks *register* (informal/popular) rather than size or material at
all. The lesson also notes a small closed set of "fixed diminutives" with no plausible non-diminutive
base (*kastanje* "chestnut," *franje* "fringe," *praatje* "a chat," *roodborstje* "robin [bird]") —
*meisje* "girl" is historically one of these too (from *meid*, "girl/maid," now itself an archaic/
informal doublet).

### 2. Colloquial spoken-register possessive contractions — z'n, d'r (Les 59, printed p. 154)

The source explicitly distinguishes written from spoken convention for name-possessives: in writing,
one uses *van* (*het boek van Ali*) or the full possessive pronoun (*Ali zijn boek*) or the `-s`
genitive (*Ali's boek*), but **in speech**, *zijn* colloquially contracts to **z'n** (*Ali z'n boek*)
and *haar* contracts to **d'r** (*Mies d'r tas*) — the source frames this explicitly as "we
*schrijven* X, maar we *zeggen* Y" (we write X, but we say Y), a directly relevant
written/spoken-register split for any derived colloquial-register mechanics work. This parallels the
apostrophe-elision pattern already documented in `033`'s Lesson 84 note (*'k, m'n, 't*) — another
instance of unstressed possessive/pronoun material eroding in casual speech.

### Idioom — full harvest, Lessons 53–62

- **Les 53** (Het gebruik van verwijswoorden, printed p. 142): *Hierbij ontvangt u...* — a formulaic
  business-letter/formal-document opening phrase, "Please find enclosed.../You will hereby
  receive..."
- **Les 54** (Het gebruik van 'er', printed p. 144): *Hij is erbij...* — colloquially "he's in on
  it" / "he's involved," built from this lesson's own *er* + preposition construction.
- **Les 55** (Actieve en passieve vormen, printed p. 146): *Wie niet weg is, is gezien...* — a
  traditional Dutch children's hide-and-seek **counting-out/game formula**, literally "whoever isn't
  away [hidden] is seen," functionally equivalent to English "ready or not, here I come."
- **Les 56** (Het onvoltooid deelwoord, printed p. 148): *Je moet geen slapende honden wakker
  maken* — "let sleeping dogs lie" (direct cognate idiom, and also a worked example of this lesson's
  own present-participle-as-adjective grammar point, *slapende*).
- **Les 57** (Het voltooid deelwoord als bijvoeglijk naamwoord, printed p. 150): *Een gegeven paard
  moet men niet in de bek kijken* — "don't look a gift horse in the mouth" (direct cognate idiom).
- **Les 58** (Het gebruik van verkleinwoorden, printed p. 152): *Een oogje hebben op...* — literally
  "to have a little eye on...," idiomatically "to have a crush on (someone)" / "to have one's eye on
  (something desired)."
- **Les 59** (Bezitsaanduiding, printed p. 154): *Hij kent het verschil tussen mijn en dijn niet* —
  literally "he doesn't know the difference between mine and thine," idiomatically describing someone
  who doesn't respect others' property (a light-hearted way of calling someone a petty thief);
  notable for preserving the archaic second-person possessive *dijn* ("thine"), otherwise obsolete in
  modern Dutch, inside a fixed idiom.
- **Les 60** (Het gebruik van de tijdsvormen (1), printed p. 156): *Goed bij de tijd zijn* — "to be
  sharp/switched-on/well-informed" (lit. "to be well by the time").
- **Les 61** (Het gebruik van de tijdsvormen (2), printed p. 158): not a fixed idiom but a
  traditional Dutch **children's riddle rhyme** (a *raadsel*), presented as this lesson's Idioom
  entry: *Vier oude wijven / die konden elkaar niet krijgen / ze liepen allen even hard / Ra, ra, wie
  zijn dat?* ("Four old women / who could never catch each other / they all ran equally fast / riddle
  riddle, who are they?") — the accompanying illustration (a windmill) gives the answer: the four
  sails of a windmill. The *"Ra, ra, ...?"* formula is itself the standard opening for this Dutch
  riddle genre, worth flagging as a folk-genre marker distinct from the idiom/proverb entries seen
  elsewhere in this book.
- **Les 62** (Manieren om werkwoorden te gebruiken, printed p. 160): *Laat me niet lachen!* — "Don't
  make me laugh!" — a dismissive-disbelief interjection.

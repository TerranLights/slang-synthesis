## Grammar points

### Language point 1 — Er + passive: er as a dummy or substitute subject

**Er** is often combined with the passive in two distinct functions. (1) As a *dummy* subject, when
the underlying verb is intransitive and has no real subject at all — agents are either omitted or
introduced with **door** (**Er werd urenlang vergaderd (door de ministers)** = "The ministers met
for hours," with no literal English equivalent to "There was met for hours"). (2) As a *substitute*
subject, when a real (grammatical) subject follows the verb but only if that subject is indefinite
(**Er wordt veel kaas gegeten in Nederland**; **Er is de afgelopen maand bijna geen neerslag
gevallen** — note the latter uses an intransitive verb). Critically, if the subject is *definite*,
**er** cannot be used at all (**De kaas wordt in grote hoeveelheden geëxporteerd**, not *Er wordt de
kaas...*). pp.113–114.

### Language point 2 — Adjectives followed by prepositional objects

Beyond the well-known verb+preposition pairs (**wachten op**, **afstemmen op**, **slaan op**,
**overwaaien uit**), many Dutch adjectives are likewise followed by a fixed preposition, including
many derived from past participles (**bedoeld voor**, **getrouwd met**). A representative list of
~25 common adjective+preposition pairs is given (p.117: **aardig tegen/voor**, **(on)afhankelijk
van**, **allergisch voor**, **arm aan**, **bang van/voor**, **bedekt met**, **bekend met**, **beroemd
om**, **bezeten van**, **bezorgd over**, **boos op**, **enthousiast over**, **gehecht aan**, **gek/dol
op**, **gewend aan**, **goed/slecht in**, **jaloers op**, **kenmerkend voor**, **kwaad op**, **trots
op**, **typisch voor**, **vergeleken bij/met**, **verliefd op**, **verrast door**, **verwant met**,
**vriendelijk tegen**). Many take the same preposition as their English equivalent but many do not
and must be learned individually. p.116–117.

### Language point 3 — Diminutives

A systematic review of Dutch diminutive-suffix selection rules, organized by final-sound
environment: **-je** as the default (most words); **-tje** after a long vowel/diphthong + l/n/r/w,
or after unstressed **-er/-el/-en**; **-tje** with doubling of a final single vowel before it
(**café→cafeetje**, **auto→autootje**); **-etje** after **-ng**, or after a stressed short vowel +
l/m/n/r/g with consonant-doubling (**bom→bommetje**, **bel→belletje**; note some **-g** words may
instead just add **-je**, e.g. **brug→bruggetje** alongside colloquial-clipped **brugje**,
**weg→weggetje** alongside **wegje**); **-pje** after **-m** (except short-vowel **-m**, which takes
**-etje**, e.g. **lam→lammetje**). Listed exceptions: **ketting→kettinkje**, **schip→scheepje**,
**glas→glaasje**, **gat→gaatje**, **blad→blaadje**, **pad→paadje**, **jongen→jongetje**. Five usage
functions are given: literal smallness (**Mijn zoontje is nog maar drie jaar**); affection
(**huisje**); diminutive-only nouns (**beetje**, **meisje**, **toetje**, **sprookje**); diminutive
forms with a distinct lexicalized meaning from the base noun (**brood/broodje**, **koek/koekje**,
**lepel/lepeltje**); and itemizing mass/collective nouns into single-serving units, especially
food/drink (**bier→een biertje** "a glass of beer," **chocola→een chocolaatje**, **ijs→een ijsje**,
**muziek→een muziekje**, **snoep→een snoepje**, **worst→een worstje**). Diminutives always take
**het** in the singular regardless of the base noun's gender, and **de** in the plural. A small
closed class of common adverbs is also formed with the diminutive suffix **-jes** (variants
**-tjes**/**-pjes**), some doubling as predicative adjectives: **eventjes** ("just a minute"),
**gezelligjes**/**knusjes** ("cosily"), **losjes** ("loosely"), **netjes** ("neatly"),
**stilletjes**/**stiekempjes** ("quietly, secretively"), **strakjes** ("in a moment"), **warmpjes**
("warmly"), **zachtjes** ("quietly, softly"). pp.121–123.

### "Did you know?" — Dutch place-name suffixes

The suffix **-dam**, extremely common in Dutch toponyms, marks where a dyke was built in the Middle
Ages, enabling settlement (**Amsterdam** — on the river Amstel — **Edam**, **Monnikendam**,
**Rotterdam**); these place names are stressed on the suffix. The suffix **-drecht/-trecht/-tricht**
derives from Latin *traiectum* ("crossing"), marking cities on waterways (**Dordrecht**,
**Utrecht**, **Maastricht**). p.125.

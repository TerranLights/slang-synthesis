from a clean text layer (`pdftotext -layout`) — `n/a` for Vision Reading Confidence throughout.

**Coverage note.** This is a 24-lesson, 243-page audio-visual course; this file covers the first four
lessons. This is a Dutch-medium course targeted at foreign learners (not a comparative/dialectal
grammar), so per-lesson content is organized as: (1) a dialogue, (2) "Aanvullende fonetiek"
(supplementary phonetics drill — largely repetitive minimal-pair practice, skipped per the coverage
rule unless it introduces a genuinely new phonological point), (3) "Samenvatting" (a prose recap
reusing already-introduced vocabulary — skipped as redundant), (4) "Oefeningen"/"Huiswerk"
(substitution drills and homework exercises reusing established vocabulary in slightly different
sentence frames — skipped wholesale per the coverage rule's explicit exclusion of repeated drill
material), and (5) the book's own consolidated **"Aanvullende woordenlijst"** (supplementary word
list) and **"Grammatica"** sections, which are the deliberate per-lesson vocabulary/grammar summary
this book itself provides. **Scope decision:** given (4) is the great bulk of each lesson's page
count and is drill-only by design, this extraction draws new vocabulary from the dialogue itself
(genuinely new items introduced in the storyline, not repeated in drills) plus the book's own
"Aanvullende woordenlijst" lists (comprehensive by design — these are the book's own vocabulary
consolidation, e.g. full profession lists, nationality tables, academic-discipline tables, numerals),
and grammar points from the "Grammatica" sections. This is not the same "non-redundant supplement"
scoping the spec describes for a second/third book on the same language — this is the first course
book for Dutch in this project and is being extracted in full — but it is a comparable transparency
principle: stating explicitly what class of source material (dialogue-plus-drill repetition) was
excluded and why.

Note also that Les 1–3's grammar (pronoun paradigms, present-tense conjugation, plural formation,
def./indef. articles) substantially overlaps in topic with `established/001_chapters1-3...md`
(Shetter & Van der Cruysse-Van Antwerpen, a different, non-pedagogical-dialogue grammar reference)

## Grammar points

### 1. Vowel/consonant inventory and spelling correspondence (Les 1, pp. 6–14)

Les 1 gives the book's own phonetic-transcription system (a simplified IPA-adjacent notation, not
standard IPA) alongside a spelling correspondence table, organized around minimal-pair/near-minimal
contrast sets. The vowel system is presented as a "gedekte" (checked/short) vs. "ongedekte"
(free/long) opposition rather than the more familiar short/long terminology — the book explicitly
notes (p. 13) that "gespannen/ongespannen," "lang/kort," and "zwak gesneden/sterk gesneden" are all
alternative labels for the same distinction, and that checked vowels are always short and abruptly
cut off by a following consonant. Checked vowels: i (pit), e (pet), a (mat), o (mot), u (put). Free
vowels: ie (tien), ee (teen), aa (laat), oo (boor), oe (moer), uu (muur), eu (keu). Diphthongs
(falling, closed at the end): ei/ij (bijt/rij), ou/au (bout/gauw), ui (buit). A further set of
"improper diphthongs" (long vowel + i/oe glide) is given for aai, ooi, oei, ieuw, eeuw, uw.

Three explicit phonological/register notes worth flagging: (1) the ABN ("Algemeen Beschaafd
Nederlands," standard educated Dutch) voiced/voiceless distinction between intervocalic /g/ and /x/
(vlaggen vs. lachen) is noted as largely absent in Randstad speech, and the book deliberately does
not teach the distinction in Les 1 for that reason — an explicit register/regional usage note. (2)
f/v and s/z voicing is explicitly flagged as having real dialectal/individual variation (p. 13):
"the more conservative practice" followed in this book has v/z as relatively soft and voiced, f/s as
sharp and voiceless word-initially and between certain sounds, but "actual oppositions are scarce"
and the phonetic realization shifts with context. (3) Word-final and pre-voiceless-obstruent devoicing
is systematic: voiced plosives/fricatives are replaced by their voiceless counterparts at the end of
a word, and F/S/CH are voiceless and sharp word-finally unless followed by a word beginning with
b/d — a cross-word-boundary sandhi rule not always spelled out this explicitly in other grammars
already extracted for Dutch.

### 2. Present-tense conjugation, formal 2nd person (Les 2)

Regular verbs (wonen, werken, studeren, langskomen, zeggen) take -en in the infinitive, drop -en for
the 1st-person singular stem, and add -t for both the formal 2nd person (u) and 3rd person (hij/ze):
*ik woon, u woont, hij/ze woont*. **Zijn** (to be) is irregular: *ik ben, u bent, hij/ze is*.

### 3. Sentence structures (Les 2)

Declarative: subject + PV (persoonsvorm, i.e. the finite/conjugated verb form) + rest — *ik woon in
Amsterdam*. Question types: (a) PV + subject + rest — *studeert u Engels?*; (b) question word + PV +
subject — *waar woont u?*; (c) subject + PV + rest + tag "hè?" — *u bent Nederlander, hè?* (an
informal-flavored confirmation tag, distinct from a plain yes/no question). Formal imperative: PV +
subject + rest — *komt u eens langs!*.

### 4. Indefinite article "een" and its omission (Les 2)

"Een" (spelled "één" only when the numeral sense needs disambiguating from the article) precedes
singular countable nouns. It is **omitted** after the copula "zijn" when the predicate names a
profession or nationality: *ik ben journalist* (not *een journalist*), *ik ben Engelsman* — a rule
that recurs across many European languages but is worth flagging explicitly since the same
zero-article-after-copula pattern will matter for any conlang mechanic modeling profession/identity
predication.

### 5. Negation with "geen" (Les 2)

"Geen" negates a noun phrase that would otherwise take "een" or the zero article: *is dat een
student? — dat is geen student*; *bent u student? — ik ben geen student*. This is negation of the
noun phrase, not of the verb (which would use "niet").

### 6. Plural formation (Les 3)

Three strategies: (a) nouns ending in -el/-em/-en/-er/-e/-ie take -s (Nederlander→Nederlanders,
dochter→dochters); nouns ending in -a/-o/-u take -'s (foto→foto's); (b) most remaining nouns take
-en (fiets→fietsen, student→studenten, man→mannen); (c) a small closed set takes -eren
(kind→kinderen, ei→eieren). This is a third, independent presentation of the same
-s/-en/-eren strategies already captured from Shetter & Van der Cruysse-Van Antwerpen in
`established/001...md`, with a different example set and its own -el/-em/-en/-er/-e/-ie-triggering
rule for -s (Shetter's version organizes the same -s-triggering environment slightly differently) —
kept per the "independently-authored source, not redundant by default" principle.

### 7. Hebben and zijn — full singular paradigm (Les 3)

|        | zijn | hebben |
|---|---|---|
| ik | ben | heb |
| u | bent | hebt (also: heeft) |
| je | bent (ben je?) | hebt (heb je?) |
| hij/ze/het | is | heeft |

Note the book's own parenthetical allowance of "u heeft" alongside the prescriptively "correct"
"u hebt" — an explicit register/prescriptivism note (the book teaches the stricter form as primary
but flags the variant as attested).

### 8. Reflexive pronouns, singular (Les 3)

1st person: *me* (*ik heb geen foto's bij me*); 2nd person formal: *u* (*hebt u ook foto's bij u?*);
3rd person: *zich*, used for both masculine and feminine (*hij heeft zijn fiets bij zich*; *ze heeft
nog een kind bij zich*).

### 9. Question words wie/wat (Les 3)

*Wie* targets persons (singular and plural use the same form): *wie heeft ze bij zich?* / *wie zijn
dat?*. *Wat* targets things/activities: *wat studeert ze?* → *Nederlands*.

### 10. "Dit is/dat is/dit zijn/dat zijn" (Les 3)

Structure: (dit/dat) + PV(zijn) + nominal constituent, with number agreement on the copula tracking
the nominal predicate, not the demonstrative: *dit is Kees*, but *dit zijn mijn kleinkinderen*
(plural predicate → plural copula despite singular "dit").

### 11. Second person singular: je/jij vs. u, and the register rule for choosing between them (Les 4)

Unstressed *je* / stressed *jij* (possessive *jouw*, stressed) is the informal 2nd-person paradigm,
parallel to formal *u*/*uw*. The book gives explicit, numbered sociolinguistic conditions for
choosing register (p. 37, translated/paraphrased): **use u** when (1) expressing
respect/deference/authority, (2) addressing someone you don't know well, (3) someone has addressed
you with u first, or (4) someone has introduced themselves with their surname. **Use je** when (1)
addressing someone you know well, (2) addressing young people, (3) usually with parents/family
members, or (4) someone has introduced themselves with their first name. This is a genuinely
explicit, source-marked register/pragmatics rule — the kind of content flagged elsewhere in this
project as high-value for a sociolinguistically-annotated source, here embedded in a pedagogical
grammar rather than a comparative reference.

### 12. Inversion / V2 word order (Les 4)

Baseline declarative order is subject + PV + rest. When any constituent (adverb, phrase) is fronted
before the PV, the subject moves to directly after the PV, keeping the PV in second position:
*ik ga morgen naar Amsterdam* → *morgen ga ik naar Amsterdam*. Explicit exception: the coordinating/
interjective words *o, hé, ja, en, maar, want* do **not** trigger inversion when they open a
sentence — *maar ik heet John* (not *maar heet ik John*).

### 13. Imperative, formal vs. informal (Les 4)

Formal: PV + subject(u) + rest — *gaat u zitten*, *doet u uw jas uit*. Informal: bare PV + rest, no
subject expressed — *ga zitten, Anneke!*, *doe je jas uit!* (though the informal example still
includes a reflexive-possessive "je," it is not a grammatical subject in the formal-imperative
sense).

### 14. Definite articles de/het and the diminutive-triggered de→het shift (Les 4)

*De* precedes person-referring nouns (except *meisje* and *kind*, which are grammatically neuter)
and all plural nouns regardless of gender. *Het* precedes neuter nouns and — notably — **every
singular diminutive**, regardless of the base noun's own gender: *de student* → *het studentje*; *de
dochter* → *het dochtertje*; *de man* → *het mannetje*; but plurals of all of these revert to *de*
(*de studentjes*, etc. — implied by the general plural-takes-de rule, though the table itself only
tabulates singular base/diminutive forms alongside plain plurals). This diminutive-forces-neuter-
gender pattern is a systematic morphosyntactic rule distinct from (and worth cross-referencing
against) the diminutive-suffix-allomorphy content already captured in
`established/003_chapter22_diminutives.md`.

### 15. Time-before-place default order, reversed after hier/daar/er (Les 4)

Default sentential order places a time expression before a place expression: *ben je al lang in
Nederland?* (lit. "are you already long in Netherlands," time-adverb "al lang" precedes place "in
Nederland"). But immediately following *hier*, *daar*, or *er*, the order flips to place-then-time:
*woon je hier al lang?* — the deictic locative "hier" itself counts as the fronted "place" slot, and
whatever time expression follows it comes second.

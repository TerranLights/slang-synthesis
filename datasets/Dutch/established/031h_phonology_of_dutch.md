## Grammar points

### Orthography is placed last because it presupposes the phonological rule system (§9.1, p.181)

Booij explains the book's unusual organizational choice (spelling last, not first): Dutch
orthography cannot be properly understood without the phonological analysis developed in the
preceding chapters, since the spelling reflects a more abstract level of representation than the
surface phonetic form, and that abstract level is definable only in terms of the typology of
phonological rules (lexical/morpholexical vs. postlexical/connected-speech) established earlier in
the book.

### The consonant, vowel, and diphthong letter-correspondence systems (§9.2–9.3, pp.181–185)

Dutch has only five vowel letters (*i, u, e, o, a*) for thirteen vowel phonemes (diphthongs
excluded), so there is no one-to-one sound-to-letter correspondence; etymology (loanword spelling
retention, and even some native-word spellings reflecting an older stage of the language) further
complicates this (detailed history cited to Booij et al. 1979). Consonant spelling is comparatively
regular, with complications limited to two digraphs (*ch* for /x/, *ng* for /ŋ/) and etymological
special cases — fully tabulated in Table 9.1. **Ambisyllabic consonants** (intervocalic, following a
short vowel) are spelled as geminates, except digraphs (including vowel digraphs) cannot be
geminated. **Short vowels** are spelled straightforwardly with single letters (Table 9.2); **long
vowels** follow the generalization that they are spelled as single letters in open syllables
(including word-finally) and as geminates in closed syllables — with several systematic exceptions:
*oe* and *eu* can't geminate (digraphs); /i/'s geminate spelling is *ie* rather than *ii*; /e/ is
also spelled geminate *ee* word-finally specifically to avoid confusion with schwa (also spelled
*e*); and native words optionally use geminate *ie* for /i/ even in open syllables. Long vowels
before a *consonantal digraph* are spelled as geminates even though phonologically they may stand in
an open syllable (*goochem* "smart"), because a single vowel letter before a consonantal digraph
defaults to a short-vowel reading. **Diphthongs** (Table 9.4) show etymology-driven spelling
alternations: /ɛi/ is *ei* when historically from Proto-Germanic /ɑi/ but *ij* when from a
historically long /i/, giving real minimal pairs (*leiden* "to lead" vs. *lijden* "to suffer") that
are homophonous but orthographically distinct; /ɔu/ shows a parallel *ou*/*au* split. The overall
consequence is that much of Dutch spelling must simply be memorized rather than derived. Two further
disambiguation devices are described: the **diaeresis** (two dots) distinguishes a vowel-letter
sequence that could be read as either a diphthong or a schwa+vowel/vowel-hiatus sequence (*gein* vs.
*geind*); and the **apostrophe** marks the genitive /s/ suffix after a stem already ending in *s*
(*Jans boek* vs. *Jans' boek*) and precedes plural *-s* after a word ending in a single vowel letter
to force the correct long-vowel reading (*kano's*).

### Degrees of abstractness: which phonological-rule effects the spelling represents (§9.3, pp.185–187)

Using the phonological rule typology from earlier chapters (lexical P-rules, morpholexical rules,
postlexical/connected-speech rules), Booij shows Dutch orthography applies the traditional
"principle of uniformity" (morphemes keep one consistent spelling) inconsistently. Two clean
generalizations hold: **connected-speech rule effects (including voice assimilation) are never
represented** in spelling, and **morpholexical rule effects (e.g. diminutive-suffix allomorphy) are
always represented** — sensibly, since morpholexical allomorphy is sometimes genuinely two distinct
forms that need distinguishing (*bloempje* "little flower" vs. *bloemetje* "bunch of flowers"). The
effects of ordinary lexical P-rules of word phonology are only **partially** represented, itemized
in Table 9.5: Final Devoicing is spelled only for underlying /v, z/ (surfacing as *f, s*) but never
for /b, d, ɣ/, which always keep their underlying voiced letter regardless of pronunciation —
including, oddly, past-tense verb forms where the fricative is actually always voiced but still
spelled devoiced (*beefde*, *raasde*). Nasal Assimilation's place-shift is spelled only for the
bilabial outcome [m], never for palatal [ɲ] or velar [ŋ]. Degemination is spelled only when it would
otherwise produce a word-final geminate letter sequence, not word-internally — with *wordt*'s
*-dt* spelling as a special case explained purely by the independent fact that /d/-devoicing is
never spelled at all. Prevocalic Schwa Deletion's effects are always represented in spelling, while
Homorganic Glide Insertion's effects are never represented — except that the *unpredictable* /j/
glide variant (as opposed to the default, predictable [ʋ]) does get spelled, since only unpredictable
information needs to be recorded.

### Spelling and higher-order (syntactic/morphological/prosodic) structure (§9.4, pp.187–188)

Beyond phoneme-to-letter correspondence, Dutch orthography encodes syntactic and morphological
information: capitalization marks sentence-initial position and proper names; punctuation marks
syntactic-constituent boundaries; and — a genuine contrast with English — **compounds are always
written as a single word** in Dutch, directly representing their grammatical single-word status
(unlike English, where compound spelling often instead reflects prosodic structure, i.e. the
fact that a compound consists of two or more prosodic words, by leaving internal spaces) — though
Booij notes a real tendency among Dutch writers to nonetheless insert internal spaces reflecting the
prosodic-word boundary. **Hyphenation**, by contrast, is based on *prosodic* structure (syllable
boundaries), not morphological structure — unlike English, which can hyphenate at a morpheme
boundary that doesn't coincide with a syllable boundary (*morphem-ic* vs. Dutch *mor-femisch* or
*morfe-misch*). Hyphens must fall exactly at a syllable boundary; for consonantal geminates and
post-short-vowel digraphs, the hyphen splits the sequence in the middle, except for the digraph
*ch*, which is never split.

## Grammar points

### Personal pronouns as objects (pp. 76-77)

Standard object-pronoun paradigm (`mij/me`, `jou/je`, `u`, `hem`, `haar`, `het`; plural `ons`,
`jullie/je`, `u`, `ze/hen/hun`). `Hen` (direct object/after prepositions) vs. `hun` (indirect
object) is a **prescriptive-only** distinction — see vocabulary table for the source's own explicit
statement that this collapses to `hun`-for-emphasis/`ze`-otherwise in actual speech. Common-gender
"it" is rendered `hij`/`hem`, not `het`/het's object form, once the referent noun is known to be
common gender (`Hoe vind je mijn nieuwe keuken? Hij was erg duur` — "it" = `hij` because `keuken`
is `de`-gender).

### Possessives (pp. 78-79)

Standard possessive-adjective paradigm; `ons`/`onze` inflects like a predicate adjective (`ons huis`
vs. `onze vader`/`onze boeken`) — the only possessive that shows gender/number agreement. See
vocabulary table for the colloquial contracted forms (`m'n`, `z'n`, `d'r`) and the postposed
`z'n`/`d'r`/`hun` possessive construction, both flagged in the source as spoken-only.

### Demonstratives (pp. 80-81)

`die`/`dat` (that, singular, common/neuter) vs. `deze`/`dit` (this, singular, common/neuter); `die`
serves as plural "those" and `deze` as plural "these" regardless of gender (paralleling
`de`/`het`→`de` in the plural). `Dit`/`dat` can stand in for `deze`/`die` before an as-yet-unnamed
plural noun following the verb (`Dit zijn mijn kinderen`), and `het` can likewise render "they"
pre-naming (`Het zijn mijn kinderen`). Demonstratives commonly front for emphasis, forcing inversion
— consistent with the F-V-N-Rest schema from Lesson 2.

### Independent possessives (pp. 82-83)

`(die/dat) van + object pronoun` construction (`die van mij` "mine") — choice of `die`/`dat` tracks
the referenced noun's gender, known at the point of utterance for a true independent possessive
(unlike predicative `dit/dat is van mij` where gender may not yet be established).

### Indicating possession — apostrophe-s (pp. 84-85)

Possessive -s (no apostrophe unless the name already ends in -s) restricted to first names
(`Marius' fiets`); otherwise possession is expressed with `van` (`de auto van meneer Smid`).
Footnote: `zijn vaders tuin` (his father's garden) is possible, but the parallel *`mijn mans tuin`
("my husband's garden") is not — the source recommends defaulting to `van` throughout to sidestep
this asymmetry entirely.

### Adjectives — inflection (pp. 87-89)

Predicative adjectives (after the verb) never inflect. Attributive adjectives (before the noun)
take `-e` except before an **indefinite singular neuter** noun (i.e. preceded by `een`/`geen`/
`welk`/`ieder`/`elk`) — that is the one context where the bare adjective form surfaces attributively.
Same spelling-alternation rules apply to the `-e` ending as to noun pluralization/verb-stem
formation (vowel doubling/consonant reduction, v/z→f/s). `Goed`/`rood`/`oud`, once inflected, are
*pronounced* `goeie`/`rooie`/`ouwe` though conventionally still spelled `goede`/`rode`/`oude` — see
vocabulary table.

## Grammar points

### Unit 24/25 — What verbs look like, verb position
Infinitive (-en), stem (infinitive minus -en, spelling rules apply), and the concept of the finite
verb (only the first/conjugated verb marks tense; others stay infinitive or past participle).
Standard V2 word order note (finite verb in second position in main clauses, other verbs pushed to
the end) — matches prior coverage; kept terse.

### Unit 26/27 — Present tense: regular and irregular
Regular present-tense endings (-Ø/-t/-en by subject) and the "je/jij drops the -t when the
pronoun follows the verb" (inversion) rule — a specific, easy-to-miss exception worth flagging.
Unit 27 tabulates the core irregular presents: `hebben`, `zijn`, `komen`, `doen`, `gaan` — standard
content, matches `established/012`/`013`.

### Unit 28 — Present tense: use
Four distinct functions of the Dutch present tense are laid out explicitly: (1) here-and-now,
(2) future ("ik werk morgen" = "I am working tomorrow" — more common than the zullen-future,
source calls it "more snappy"), (3) livening up a past narrative (historical present), and (4) —
**the genuinely non-obvious one for English speakers** — an ongoing situation that started in the
past and continues into the present, where English requires the present perfect ("I have been
living here for ten years") but Dutch uses the plain present ("Ik woon hier al tien jaar"). This
fourth use is paired with the `al`/`pas` distinction: both mean roughly "already"/"only," but
`al` frames the duration as long (speaker's subjective judgment) and `pas` frames it as short —
this is an attitude-encoding pair, not a neutral synonym pair, and is explicitly called out as
such by the source. The progressive constructions (`aan het + infinitive`; `zitten/liggen/staan
...te + infinitive`, the last adding positional information) round out the unit.

### Unit 29/30/31 — Modal verbs: overview, full conjugation, use
The six Dutch modals (`zullen`, `willen`, `kunnen`, `mogen`, `moeten`, `hoeven`) with full present/
imperfect/perfect conjugation tables (Unit 30) — standard reference content. Two structural notes
worth keeping: `hoeven` requires `te` before its dependent infinitive (unlike the other five), and
the infinitives of `hebben`/`gaan` are frequently dropped after a modal ("Ik wil een glaasje wijn
(hebben)"). Unit 31 usefully separates out the distinct pragmatic *functions* each modal covers —
e.g. `zullen` alone covers future, suggestion, prediction, *and* promise depending on context;
`kunnen`'s polite-request use notably takes present tense where English would use past ("Kunt u
mij morgen opbellen?" = "Could you phone me tomorrow?"). This functional breakdown is more
explicit than a bare conjugation table and is kept as a non-redundant supplement to `012`/`013`'s
treatment.

### Unit 32 — Talking about the future
Three future strategies ranked by frequency: present tense (most common), `gaan + infinitive`
(next most common, general planned future), and `zullen + infinitive` (reserved mainly for
promises/guarantees/predictions, not a neutral future marker the way English "will" is) — this
ranking-by-actual-usage-frequency (as opposed to just listing three equivalent constructions) is
the useful supplement here.

### Unit 33 — Use of the perfect and imperfect
A clear worked framing not spelled out quite this way in prior established/ files: the perfect
tense "transports" the listener into the past (introduces/concludes a narrative), while the
imperfect "brings the past alive" with descriptive/background detail — visualized as using
binoculars to look at something in close-up. Habits/regular past events require the imperfect.
Where either tense is grammatically valid, the choice signals *involvement*: perfect for a purely
factual statement, imperfect for an emotionally engaged/vivid framing of the same fact ("We hebben
in Amsterdam gewoond" vs. "We woonden in Amsterdam"). This involvement-based distinction is a
genuine nuance beyond the mechanical hebben/zijn-plus-participle rule.

### Unit 34/35 — Present perfect: formation, auxiliary choice
Past participle formation (`ge-` + stem + `t`/`d`, with the "soft ketchup" mnemonic for which
letters — s, f, t, k, ch, p — trigger `-t` rather than `-d`), the no-`ge-` rule for verbs with
fixed unstressed prefixes (be-, er-, ge-, her-, ont-, ver-), and `ge-` insertion between prefix and
stem for separable verbs. Unit 35 covers `hebben`-vs-`zijn` auxiliary selection: most verbs take
`hebben`; a specific list of change-of-state/motion-endpoint verbs (beginnen, blijven, komen,
stoppen, worden, gaan, zijn) take `zijn`; and verbs of motion (lopen, rijden, fietsen) take `zijn`
only when a destination is specified, `hebben` otherwise (`Harmen is naar huis gelopen` vs. `Heb
je hard gelopen?`) — this destination-conditioned split is the most useful non-obvious rule in
this pair of units.

## Grammar points

### Language point 1 — Remnants of case forms

Modern Dutch has lost the case system (accusative/dative/genitive) that Dutch shared historically
with German, but former case forms survive as fossilized fixed expressions: **tenslotte** (<
dative **te den slot**), **tegelijkertijd** (< dative **te gelijker tijd**), **'s morgens** (<
genitive **des morgens**), **op den duur**/**te zijner tijd** (dative), **in de loop der jaren**/
**mijns inziens**/**het Koninkrijk der Nederlanden** (genitive **der**/**mijns**). These need only
be learned as vocabulary items, not analyzed grammatically. Texts written before the 1947
post-war spelling reform used many more such case endings in writing (though never in speech) and
remain readable to a modern reader despite the archaic endings — the Proverbs section at the end of
this unit is flagged as containing further live examples. p.155.

### Language point 2 — Adjectives derived from materials

Material nouns become adjectives by adding **-en** (**hout→houten**, **wol→wollen**,
**katoen→katoenen**, **zilver→zilveren**, **goud→gouden**, **baksteen→bakstenen**) — a pattern that
exists in English too (wooden, woollen) but is far more productive in Dutch. These alternate freely
with a periphrastic **(gemaakt) van + material** construction (**een houten huis** / **een huis
(gemaakt) van hout**). Like all adjectives ending in **-en** (a class that also includes the past
participles of strong verbs, e.g. **een gestolen fiets**, **een handgeschreven brief**), material
adjectives take no additional **-e** before a noun. Material adjectives derived from *English*
loanwords are the exception and do *not* take **-en** (**nylon kousen**, **een plastic
tafelkleed**). pp.159–160.

### Language point 3 — Eens, even, maar, nou, toch

These five particles ("adverbs of modality") are much more common in speech than writing and
notoriously hard to master, each nudging the connotation of a command differently: **Kom binnen!**
(neutral); **Kom maar binnen!** ("please come in," softening); **Kom even binnen!** ("come in for a
short while"); **Kom (toch) nog even binnen!** ("come in anyway, even if it's late"); **Kom
eens/'s binnen!** (encouraging); **Kom toch binnen!** (strong encouragement); **Kom nou binnen!**
(stressed on **binnen**, expressing impatience/irritation). pp.161–162.

### Language point 4 — Adjectival intensifiers

Dutch uses simple similes for some intensified comparisons (**zo mager als een lat** = "as thin as
a rake," lit. "as a slat"; **zo blind als een mol** = "as blind as a bat," lit. "as a mole") but far
more often uses compound intensifier adjectives comparable to English "dirt cheap" or "brand new" —
a first noun/adjective element functioning purely as an intensifying prefix fused to a base
adjective. The source gives a representative list of 40+ such compounds (pp.163–164; see Vocabulary
table above for the full extracted set), spanning ordinary registers (**ijskoud**, **kersvers**,
**spotgoedkoop**) through to one item flagged here as vulgar register (**zeiknat**, "soaking wet," <
**zeiken** "to piss," alongside the more neutral **drijfnat**/**kletsnat** variants for the same
meaning).

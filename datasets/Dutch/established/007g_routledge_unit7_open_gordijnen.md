## Grammar points

### 1. Imperfect tense: formation of regular verbs

Regular verbs form the imperfect by adding a suffix to the stem: **stem + te(n)** or **stem +
de(n)** depending on the stem's final consonant, following the same "soft ketchup" (*s, t, f, k,
ch, p* — the unit's own mnemonic phrasing) consonant set already used to choose between
present-perfect participle *-t*/*-d* in `established/007d`'s grammar point 1: if the stem ends in
one of those consonants, use *-te*/*-ten*; otherwise, use *-de*/*-den*. Singular forms (used with
*ik, jij, u, hij, zij*) take the bare *-te*/*-de* suffix; plural forms (used with *wij, jullie,
zij*) add *-n*: *voelen* → *voelde* (sg.) / *voelden* (pl.); *werken* → *werkte* (sg.) / *werkten*
(pl.).

### 2. Imperfect tense: irregular verbs

Irregular verbs do not form the imperfect by any productive pattern and must be learned
individually (e.g. *hebben* → *had*; *bestaan* → *bestond*); the source points to its own
back-of-book irregular-verb list rather than deriving a rule.

### 3. Choosing between the imperfect and the present perfect (p. 211–217)

The unit gives three distinct guiding principles rather than a single rule: **(a)** the imperfect
describes habits or regularly recurring events in the past (*Iedereen leefde langs elkaar heen*).
**(b)** When narrating a past event, the event is typically *introduced* (and sometimes *rounded
off*) in the present perfect, while supporting/background information is given in the imperfect
(*Ik heb mijn huis gevonden via een vriendin* [present perfect] *— Die vriendin woonde in de buurt*
[imperfect]). **(c)** Choosing between the two tenses for the same fact is often not a matter of
strict correctness — both can be grammatical — but of **dramatic involvement**: the imperfect
conveys a sense of being inside the scene and invites the listener to expect more narrative detail
to follow (*Ik was in Griekenland op vakantie* reads like the start of a story), while the present
perfect reads as a detached, list-like observation (*Ik ben in Griekenland op vakantie geweest*
reads like an item being checked off a list of past trips).

### 4. Met z'n ... — expressing how many people were present (p. 223)

Adding *-en* to a number (with the usual Dutch spelling rules applying to the resulting form)
produces a phrase for "the [number] of us/them," used with *met z'n*: *met z'n vieren* ("the four
of us"), *met z'n drieën* ("the three of us"), *met z'n zessen* ("six of us"). The idiomatic
maximum form *met z'n allen* ("all of us together") does not follow the numeral pattern but belongs
to the same construction family.

### 5. Subjective vs. objective language (p. 223–226)

Language expressing a personal opinion rather than a verifiable fact is *subjective* (e.g. *mooi*
"beautiful" describing a painting); language stating a checkable property is *objective* (e.g.
*vierkant* "square"). The unit frames this as a register/pragmatics point about Dutch speakers in
particular: Dutch speakers are characterized as comparatively eager and quick to state personal
opinions, in explicit contrast with Belgians, who the source says are "generally thought much less
forward" about volunteering opinions — an explicit Netherlands/Belgium usage-culture contrast, not
a grammatical rule.

### 6. Gezellig — an untranslatable cultural-register concept (p. 221)

*Gezellig* covers "enjoyable, pleasant, sociable, cosy, entertaining and many other things," most
often describing people enjoying each other's company in a snug, homely, sociable way — illustrated
by the ritual of morning coffee (*een kopje koffie*, where the diminutive *kopje* itself signals
"gezellig" atmosphere, cross-referencing diminutive-as-register-marker material from
`established/003`) and by the ritualized structure of a traditional Dutch birthday (handshakes and
triple kisses on arrival, a full seating circle, obligatory *kopje koffie met gebak*, drinks and
snacks, handshakes/kisses again at departure). When an occasion lacks this particular homely
warmth (the source's example: young people at a nightclub rave), *gezellig* is used less, and
looser alternatives substitute: *Het was hartstikke leuk*, *Het was te gek*, *Het was top*.

### 7. The past perfect (p. 227–228)

Formed the same way as the present perfect but with the imperfect of the auxiliary rather than the
present tense (*had*/*was* + past participle: *We hadden de afspraak gemaakt*). Two distinct uses:
**(a)** to make explicit that one past event happened before another past event (*Ik had Lieke net
gebeld toen ze op de deur klopte*); **(b)** to describe a counterfactual/hypothetical past that
didn't actually happen (*Als ik harder had gewerkt, was ik voor m'n examen geslaagd* — "If I had
worked harder, I would have passed my exam"). *Zou* can substitute in this second, counterfactual
use (see next point).

### 8. Zou — four distinct functions (p. 228–230)

The unit catalogs four uses of *zou* (the conditional/imperfect-subjunctive form of *zullen*), only
some of which are newly introduced here (the polite-question and polite-advice uses are flagged as
already covered in Unit 2, cross-referenced but not re-derived): **(a)** reporting that a plan had
been made to do something (*Ik zou met Lieke gaan winkelen* — "I was going to go shopping with
Lieke"); **(b)** the standard hypothetical-conditional use (*Als ik de loterij won, zou ik een
nieuwe laptop kopen* — "If I won the lottery, I would buy a new laptop"); this *zou*-clause can
optionally be replaced by a plain imperfect instead (*Als ik de loterij won, kocht ik een nieuwe
laptop*), a stylistic substitution with the same meaning; **(c)** reporting uncertain or
secondhand/rumored information (*Pedro zou gisteren tot 11 uur 's avonds hebben gewerkt* — "Pedro is
supposed to have worked until 11 last night") — the source notes that after *zou* in this use, any
auxiliary (*hebben*/*zijn*) and modal verbs appear in their infinitive form, not conjugated.

### 9. Multi-verb infinitive clusters (p. 233–234)

When a modal or auxiliary-like verb (e.g. *gaan, moeten, blijven, willen, weigeren*) combines with
another verb, all verbs after the finite (first) one appear as bare infinitives stacked at the end
of the clause, and the stack can grow as more such verbs are added: *Ik ga tennissen* → *Ik moet
gaan tennissen* → the source's fuller three-and-four-verb examples (*Ik ben met een vriendin gaan
tennissen*; *We willen laat blijven werken*; *We weigeren laat te blijven werken* — note *weigeren*
specifically takes *te* before the infinitive stack while the others don't).

### 10. Om + te + infinitive clusters (p. 234)

The same infinitive-stacking behavior applies inside an *om + te + infinitive* ("in order to")
clause when an additional verb (e.g. a modal) is added: *Om dat te zeggen, moet je gek zijn* → *Om
dat te kunnen zeggen, moet je eerst onderzoek doen* ("In order to be able to say that, you first
have to research it") — the modal *kunnen* is inserted before the final infinitive, both landing
at the clause's end after *te*.

# Dutch — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Michel Thomas Method — Dutch Foundation Course (full booklet)

**Source:** Cobie Adkins-de Jong and Els Van Geyte, *Dutch Foundation Course* (Michel Thomas Method
booklet accompanying the 8-CD audio course), Hodder Education, 2008, ISBN 978 0340 97169 7. PDF:
`source_reference/languages/Dutch/04 Dutch Foundation Course.pdf` (17 pages, clean text layer via
`pdftotext`, no vision-reading needed).

**Coverage note.** This is not a conventional grammar-reference textbook — it is the printed
**track-listing/transcript booklet** accompanying an all-audio "Michel Thomas Method" course.
Roughly two-thirds of the 17-page PDF (pp. 1–9, 27–32) is marketing/promotional matter: publisher
boilerplate, method philosophy ("no books, no writing"), celebrity endorsement quotes, a biography
of Michel Thomas, the full Michel Thomas Method product catalogue (other languages, prices, ISBNs),
and a listener-response order form. **None of that is language content and none of it was
extracted.** The actual teaching content is the **"Track listing"** (pp. 10–26 / PDF pages 10–26),
organized as CD1 Track 1 through CD8 Track 12 (84 tracks total) — a dense, continuous narration
that interleaves new vocabulary and grammar points track by track, unlike a chaptered textbook.
Every distinct vocabulary item and every grammar point across all 84 tracks was extracted below;
repeated recap sentences that introduce no new word or rule were skipped. Location is cited by
**CD/Track number** (the source's own reference system, more precise here than a page number since
several tracks share a printed page). No text layer issues, no font-substitution cipher, and no
vision-reading were involved — `pdftotext -layout` produced clean, directly usable text throughout.

**Relationship to prior `established/` files.** This source is typologically unlike the two prior
Dutch sources (`001`/`002`/`003`, all drawn from Shetter & Van der Cruysse-Van Antwerpen's *Dutch: An
Essential Grammar*) — it teaches through continuous audio narration rather than a lesson-structured
reference grammar, and its examples are independently authored. Per the "non-redundant supplement"
dispatch pattern, this file was extracted in full rather than filtered against `001`–`003`, since
even topics covered elsewhere (trigger verbs, adjective `-e`, the `-je` diminutive, separable
prefixes) are demonstrated here with a fully independent set of examples and — in a few cases (the
`omdat`-effect family of conjunctions, the `zich` reflexive set, the `er`+preposition pronoun system,
the causative `laten doen`) — grammar points not previously captured in this language's
`established/` files at all.

## Grammar points

### Trigger verbs (send the second verb to the end of the sentence)
When a sentence has two verbs and the second is in the "to" (infinitive) form, the infinitive goes
to the end of the sentence: *wil je dit doen?* = "do you want to do this?" The source calls verbs
with this property "trigger" verbs and builds nearly the entire course's syntax around the concept
(CD1 T5). Confirmed trigger verbs named explicitly across the transcript: *willen* (want), *kunnen*
(can/be able), *moeten* (must/have to), *mogen* (be allowed to), *gaan* (go, restricted sense),
*zullen*/*zou* (shall/will/would), *laten* (let/allow).

### Dutch infinitives always end in -n (usually -en)
The infinitive ("to" form) of a Dutch verb always ends in *-n*, usually *-en*: *drinken* "to drink,"
*werken* "to work," *eten* "to eat," *doen* "to do" (CD1 T5). The final *-n* of the infinitive is not
always pronounced in casual speech (CD1 T6, re: *kan je?*).

### Single present tense (no progressive/habitual split)
Unlike English, which distinguishes "you understand," "you are understanding," and "you do
understand," Dutch has only one present-tense form: *ik werk* covers both "I work" and "I am
working" (CD2 T1, and recurring throughout — e.g. CD1 T4 *ik drink*, CD1 T9 *we eten*).

### Word order inverts for yes/no questions
Statement word order (subject-verb) inverts to verb-subject for questions: *weet je?* = "do you
know?" (CD1 T14); *waarom heb je het niet?* = "why don't you have it?" (CD2 T2). The verb form used
for *je* is sometimes different in questions than in statements (see the *je*/*U* -t rule below).

### The "omdat effect" — subordinating conjunctions send the verb to the end
Certain conjunctions push the verb(s) of their clause to the very end of the sentence, the way
trigger verbs push an infinitive to the end. Named for its first example, *omdat* "because" (CD4
T9): *omdat het klaar is* = "because it is ready." The same effect is confirmed for: *als* "if, in
case" (CD6 T9), *of* "if, whether (or not)" (CD7 T1), *dat* "that" as a complementizer after verbs
like *hopen*/*denken* (CD8 T10-T11), and question words (*waarom*, *waar*, *hoe*, *wat*, *wanneer*)
whenever they are not sentence-initial (CD5 T3): *ik weet niet wanneer ze begint* = "I don't know
when she begins."

### -t verb ending for he/she/it, formal U, and je/informal-you-in-statements
The present-tense verb form for *hij*/*ze*("she")/*het*, for *U*, and for *je* in a statement (not a
question) all take a final *-t*: *hij werkt*, *je gaat*, *u komt* (CD2 T11, CD3 T6-T8). Four
irregular exceptions never take this *-t*: *wil* (he/she/it wants), *kan* (can), *heeft* (has), *is*
(is) (CD2 T11). *Kunnen* and *willen* are additionally called out as "special trigger verbs" whose
he/she/it form matches the "I" form rather than taking *-t* (CD3 T12, CD7 T4).

### Plural verb forms use the bare infinitive ("to" form)
*We*, *jullie*, and *ze*("they") all take the plain infinitive as their present-tense verb form:
*we kunnen*, *jullie drinken*, *ze moeten* (CD1 T9, CD3 T7, CD2 T4). *U* referring to more than one
person is an exception — it still takes the *je*-style *-t* form (CD3 T7).

### Stressed vs. unstressed pronoun pairs
Several pronouns have a distinct stressed form used for emphasis: *je* → *jij*, *we* → *wij*,
*ze* → *zij* (used for both "she" and "they" when stressed) (CD4 T5).

### Adjectives take -e before a following noun (attributive inflection)
Adjectives like *goed*, *slecht*, *nieuw*, *mooi* add an unstressed *-e* (pronounced "uh") when
directly followed by the noun they describe: *slechte informatie* "bad information," *een mooie
dag* "a nice day" (CD2 T12, CD4 T3, CD5 T7). This also affects the pronunciation of a preceding
*-d*: in predicative *dat is goed* the *d* sounds like a *t*, but in attributive *goede informatie*
it sounds like a genuine *d* again (CD2 T12).

### de-words vs. het-words (grammatical gender)
Dutch nouns take one of two definite articles. *De* is used for all plurals, all *-atie* nouns, and
many others; *het* is a separate article for other nouns, e.g. *het werk* "the work" (CD6 T5). All
nouns ending in the diminutive suffix *-je* are *het*-words (CD6 T5).

### Plural formation with -en
The regular Dutch plural is formed by adding *-en*: *vriend* → *vrienden* "friends." All plural
nouns take *de* as their article regardless of the singular noun's own gender (CD4 T2).

### English -ation ↔ Dutch -atie; -atie nouns ↔ -eren verbs
English words ending in "-ation" regularly correspond to Dutch *-atie*: *information/informatie*,
*situation/situatie*, *operation/operatie*, *reputation/reputatie* (CD2 T10). A second, related
pattern: Dutch nouns ending in *-atie* form a matching verb in *-eren*: *combinatie* → *combineren*
"to combine," *reparatie* → *repareren* "to repair" (CD8 T2).

### Word-final devoicing of v and z
A Dutch word cannot end in *v* or *z* — a final *v* becomes *f* in writing/pronunciation: *geven*
"to give" → *ik geef* "I give" (CD2 T4).

### -lijk adjectives (~"-able" in English) and on- negation
The suffix *-lijk* (pronounced "luck") often corresponds to English "-able": *mogelijk* "possible" (~
"do-able"), *makkelijk* "easy," *belangrijk* "important" (containing *rijk* "rich") (CD4 T7, CD6
T4). Many such adjectives can be negated by prefixing *on-*: *mogelijk* → *onmogelijk* "impossible"
(CD4 T7).

### The diminutive suffix -je: shrinking and informality
Adding *-je* to a noun both makes it literally smaller and signals informality/fun: *huis* → *huisje*
"little house," *kat* → *katje* "little cat/kitten," *vraag* → *vraagje* "little question" (used
pragmatically to soften a request) (CD5 T8). All *-je* nouns are grammatically *het*-words (CD6 T5).
[Cross-reference: `established/003_chapter22_diminutives.md` documents this suffix's full allomorphy
(`-je`/`-tje`/`-etje`/`-pje`) in much greater phonological depth from a different, more systematic
source — this file's contribution is limited to the pragmatic "smaller/more informal" framing and a
handful of independently-authored examples, not new allomorph data.]

### Reflexive verbs with zich (and reflexive pronoun forms per person)
Certain verbs are inherently reflexive and require a reflexive pronoun matching the subject: *ik was
me* "I wash myself," *wij wassen ons* "we wash ourselves," *ze wast zich* "she washes herself," *wij
moeten ons haasten* "we must hurry (ourselves)" (CD5 T9-T10). *Zich* itself covers third-person
singular/plural ("himself," "herself," "itself," "themselves") and doubles as the formal "yourself."
The same reflexive pattern is used for expressing feelings: *ik voel me beter* "I feel (myself)
better," *hoe voel je je?* "how do you feel (in yourself)?" (CD5 T9).

### niet vs. geen (clausal vs. nominal negation)
*Niet* negates generally (a clause, a verb, most other negatable elements), while *geen* specifically
negates an indefinite noun phrase: *ik heb geen werk* "I have no work" vs. a general *niet*-negated
clause (CD5 T11).

### om te — the "for to" infinitive-of-purpose construction
*Om te* + infinitive expresses purpose ("in order to"): *ik wil tijd om te werken* "I want time (in
order) to work" (CD6 T3).

### gaan is restricted to a true future-intention sense
*Gaan* ("to go") cannot be used to mean "is going to be" in a stative/existential sense — it's
reserved for cases where the following verb is a genuine action verb: contrast *ik wil weten hoe
laat het klaar is* "I want to know when it's (going to be) ready" (no *gaan*) with *ik ga het morgen
doen* "I'm going to do it tomorrow" (*gaan* + action verb *doen*) (CD3 T1, CD6 T8).

### Future events are often expressed with the present tense
Dutch frequently expresses future time reference using ordinary present-tense verb forms rather than
a dedicated future construction: *ik doe het morgen* "I will do it tomorrow" (CD2 T8). A genuine
future/promise sense is expressed instead with the trigger verb *zullen*/*zou*: *ik zal je morgen
helpen* "I shall help you tomorrow" (CD7 T12).

### Separable-prefix verbs
A set of particles — *weg*, *aan*, *mee*, *uit*, *binnen* — combine with base verbs like *komen*
"to come," *doen* "to do," or *gaan* "to go" to form new, distinctly-meaning compound verbs:
*weggaan* "to go away," *aankomen* "to arrive," *meekomen* "to come along," *uitgaan* "to go out,"
*binnenkomen* "to come in." These prefixes are always stressed and can split off from the verb (CD8
T8). Contrast the inseparable prefix *her-* in *herhalen* "to repeat," which has no independent
meaning of its own, keeps the stress on the base verb (*-halen*), and cannot be split off (CD8 T9).

### laten as a causative ("to have something done")
*Laten* + infinitive expresses having someone else do something: *ik wil het laten doen* "I want to
have it done" (lit. "I want it to let to do"); *kun je het laten schoonmaken?* "can you have it
cleaned?" (CD8 T3-T4).

### er + preposition pronominal compounds
*Er* ("there," referring to an already-established place/thing) combines with prepositions to form
fixed pro-adverbs: *erop* "thereon/on it," *erin* "therein/in it," *ermee* "with it": *de kat zit
erop* "the cat is sitting on it"; *hoe gaat het ermee?* "how are things?" (CD8 T12).

### Time before place
Dutch word order generally places a time expression before a place expression, the reverse of a
common English ordering tendency: *hij was gisteren in Amsterdam* "he was in Amsterdam yesterday"
(CD7 T8). Related ordering note: *niet* usually comes after a time phrase (CD7 T10).

## Grammar points

### Reflexive verbs

Dutch has reflexive verbs whose object pronoun refers back to the subject, paralleling English "I
washed myself." The pronoun set across persons: **me** (ik), **je** (jij), **zich/u** (u), **zich**
(hij/zij), **ons** (wij), **je** (jullie), **zich** (zij). Some verbs are only optionally reflexive
(e.g. *interesseren*); others are **inherently reflexive** — they never occur without the pronoun
(a full list given: zich haasten, zich vergissen, zich amuseren, zich vermaken, zich gedragen, zich
herinneren, zich verbazen, zich voelen, zich vervelen, zich schamen, zich verslapen) and the book
recommends memorizing these as fixed items rather than expecting the construction to feel logical.
**Zelf** can be added after the pronoun for emphasis (*mezelf*, *zichzelf*). Word order: the
reflexive pronoun follows the main verb in main statements (*Ik verveel me*); in questions and
sub-clauses it follows the subject (cf. Unit 12 for sub-clause word order).

### Making comparisons: equal comparisons

Building on Unit 6's comparative forms, equal (as-...-as) comparisons use **even ... als** or **net
zo ... als**: *Ik hou even veel van vlees als van vis* / *Ik hou net zo veel van vlees als van vis*
("I like meat as much as fish"). Both patterns are interchangeable; **net zo** adds slight emphasis.

### Laten

**Laten** is multi-functional. (1) Combined with an infinitive it can be non-compositional/idiomatic:
*laten zien* = "to show" (not "let see"). (2) It renders English "let" in "Let's ..."/"Let me ...":
*Laten we naar huis gaan* ("Let's go home"), *Laat me gaan* ("Let me go"). (3) It can mean "to leave
something (behind)": *Ik heb mijn tas laten liggen* ("I've left my bag"). (4) It can mean "to have
something done" (causative): *Ik moet mijn haar laten knippen* ("I should get my hair cut"), *Ik laat
de auto repareren* ("I'm having the car repaired").

### Geen vs. niet

**Geen** ("no/not any") negates a noun preceded by *een* or no article at all — it negates the
concept in general (*Ik wil geen appel* — "I don't want an apple [at all]"). **Geen** always sits
directly before the noun or the adjective modifying it (*Ik wil geen rode appels*). **Niet** negates
a *specific*, already-identified noun (one marked by *die/dat/de/het* or a possessive): *Wil je die
rode appel? – Nee, ik wil die rode appel niet.*

### Niet-placement

In a main statement or question, **niet** usually goes at the very end of the sentence (*Ik luister
niet*, *Ik ken haar niet*). However it is placed **before**: (a) descriptive words — adjectives and
adverbs (*Nee, die zijn niet nieuw*); (b) prepositions (*Ik ben niet in Amsterdam geweest*). In a
sub-clause, the finite verb moves to clause-final position, so niet precedes the verb there too
(*Ik heb die film nog niet gezien*), and the adjective/preposition-precedence rules still apply
inside sub-clauses as well.

### Diminutive suffix allomorphy

Building on the -je diminutive introduced in Unit 11's vocabulary (see `established/022c`'s Unit 8
note on -je forming "het" words), the ending's pronunciation-driven allomorph is spelled out fully
here: **-tje** (after a vowel or certain consonants: tafeltje, stoeltje, retourtje, enkeltje,
eitje), **-etje** (after certain final consonant clusters/short vowels: mannetje, ringetje,
kammetje, bruggetje, kippetje), **-pje** (after m: boompje, museumpje, geheimpje), **-kje** (after
ng: puddinkje, woninkje). Beyond marking literal smallness, the diminutive productively conveys
**affect** — positive (*het zonnetje*, "the lovely sunshine") or negative/dismissive (*wat een raar
mannetje*, "what a strange little man"; the fixed idiom *huisje, boompje, beestje* for a
boring/conformist suburban existence) — illustrated via an excerpt from a Toon Hermans poem that
piles up diminutives (*stroohoedjes, vestjes, dasjes, tafeltjes, kastjes, prulletjes*) for stylistic
effect.

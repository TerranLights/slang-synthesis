## Grammar points

### Past tense of modal verbs and double infinitives (pp. 134-135)

Modals prefer the imperfect over the perfect even more than ordinary verbs do in spoken Dutch, but
when a modal *is* put in the perfect, its past participle is suppressed in favor of its bare
infinitive standing next to the governed infinitive — the "double-infinitive rule" (`Ik heb mijn
horloge niet kunnen vinden`, not *`...niet gekund vinden`). The rule extends beyond modals to a
handful of other auxiliary-like verbs (`gaan`, `komen`, `blijven`, `horen`, `zien` + infinitive),
where the *zijn*-selecting or *hebben*-selecting auxiliary still governs but its own perfect
participle is likewise replaced by the infinitive form when combined with a following infinitive.
Source calls this "one of the most difficult aspects of Dutch grammar."

### Reflexive verbs (pp. 140-143)

Genuinely reflexive-only verbs (`zich herinneren`, `zich verslapen`, `zich haasten`, `zich
vervelen`, `zich schamen`, `zich vergissen`) must be memorized as such (marked `vr.` in
dictionaries) since their reflexivity isn't semantically transparent the way `zich krabben`/`zich
scheren`/`zich wassen` (optionally-reflexive action verbs) is. The reflexive pronoun always
immediately follows the finite verb, with one exception: when the sentence's direct object is `het`,
the reflexive pronoun is displaced after it (`Ik kan het me niet herinneren`). Emphatic `mezelf`-
type forms are reserved for genuine emphasis, unlike English "myself" which is near-obligatory —
Dutch defaults to the bare reflexive pronoun.

### The comparative and superlative (pp. 146-149)

Regular `-er`/`-st` suffixation with no length-based cutoff (unlike English's "more"/"most" switch
for longer adjectives) — `interessanter`/`interessantst` stays suffixal regardless of syllable
count, a systematic cross-linguistic contrast worth flagging. `-r`-final adjectives (`duur→duurder`,
`lekker→lekkerder`) show no special contraction. A genuine false-friend trap flagged by the source:
English "She's prettier than me" (technically "than I") has **no** parallel ambiguity in Dutch —
`dan ik`/`dan zij` strictly match subject-pronoun case, never the English-style oblique-after-"than"
pattern. Adverbial superlative takes `het` + `-st(e)` (`het hardst`), distinguishable from the
adjectival superlative by the omissibility test: if "the" can drop from the English gloss, it's the
adverbial form.

### How to say "to like" (pp. 149-151)

At least six structurally distinct strategies, each gated by the liked thing's category: `vinden` +
adjective (general, people/food/anything — `Ik vind hem aardig`), `smaken` (food only, liker in
object form — `Het vlees smaakt mij niet`), `houden van` (food/things, never people in the "like"
sense — reserved for genuine love when applied to a person), `bevallen` (general, liker in object
form, takes `zijn` in the perfect), `een hekel hebben aan` (specifically "to dislike"), and `graag`
+ verb / `graag eten` (liking to *do* something or eat something, with its own irregular
comparative/superlative `liever`/`het liefst` used to express preference-ranking among activities).
This many-way lexical split around a single English verb is a genuine source of learner difficulty
the book calls out explicitly, and a good candidate mechanism for how a derived slang lexicon might
similarly fragment a single core concept across register-differentiated near-synonyms.

# Dutch — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Intermediate Dutch: A Grammar and Workbook (full book, Units 1-24 + Appendix)

**Source:** Jenneke A. Oosterhoff, *Intermediate Dutch: A Grammar and Workbook* (Routledge Grammar
Workbooks series, 1st ed., 2009), pp. 1-178 (Units 1-24 plus the Appendix of strong/irregular verbs).
PDF: `source_reference/languages/Dutch/23 Intermediate Dutch A Grammar and Workbook.pdf` (213 PDF
pages; clean text layer via `pdftotext`, no vision-reading needed). "Key to exercises" (pp. 179-208
in the PDF), the index, and all drill/exercise items themselves are skipped per the coverage rule
(repeated exercises reusing already-captured vocabulary/grammar).

**Coverage note.** This is the companion/sequel volume to Oosterhoff's *Basic Dutch* (not yet
extracted in this project — no `established/` file for *Basic Dutch* existed at the time of this
dispatch, so the non-redundant-supplement dispatch pattern does not apply here; this file is a full,
first extraction). *Intermediate Dutch* is also a different book/series entirely from the
`25 Dutch: An Essential Grammar` (Shetter & Van der Cruysse-Van Antwerpen) source already partly
extracted in `001`-`003` — there is no meaningful topic overlap to screen against for this dispatch.
Every grammar point across all 24 units plus the strong-verb appendix is covered below. The book is
overwhelmingly a *closed-class/functional-word* grammar reference (conjunctions, adverbs, pronouns,
prepositions, verb-tense/word-order mechanics) rather than an open vocabulary list — so the
Vocabulary table below is dense with function words and fixed verb/adjective/noun + preposition
combinations, which is genuinely what this source offers, rather than a sample of content words.
The strong/irregular verb Appendix (pp. 173-178, ~150 principal-parts entries organized into ~14
vowel-alternation classes) is treated as a **reference paradigm table**, not an open vocabulary list per
the copyright-discipline rule against bulk-reproducing a vocabulary box — the class system and a
representative sample of each class are given under Grammar Points/Appendix below rather than
reproducing all ~150 rows; the book's own text notes this appendix is identical to the one in *Basic
Dutch*.

## Grammar points

### Unit 1 — Conjunctions
Coordinating conjunctions (*en, of, maar, want*) link equal sentence parts with regular word
order (finite verb second). Subordinating conjunctions send the finite verb to the end and are
grouped by function: time (*als, terwijl, toen, wanneer, nu, tot(dat), voor(dat), zolang, nadat,
zodra, sinds*), cause/effect (*aangezien, daar, doordat, omdat, zodat*), condition (*als, indien,
mits, tenzij*), contrast (*hoewel, alhoewel, ondanks (het feit) dat, ofschoon, terwijl*), comparison
(*alsof, even/net zo...als, zoals*), and double/correlative conjunctions (*en...en, hetzij...hetzij,
of...of, hoe...hoe/des te, noch...noch, zowel...als*). The book gives fine-grained usage
discriminations within near-synonym clusters: *als* vs. *toen* vs. *wanneer* (recurring/present vs.
one-off past vs. proper question word — misuse of *wanneer* as a conjunction is flagged as a common
English-speaker error); *voordat* vs. *nadat* vs. *totdat* (sequencing and required tense pairing:
*nadat*-clauses must be present perfect or past perfect, matched to present or simple-past main
clauses respectively); *omdat/aangezien/daar* (near-interchangeable, but *aangezien*/*daar* more
formal) vs. *doordat* (mechanical/instrumental cause only, "on account of"); *sinds* (warns against
confusing with English "since" meaning "because" — that sense is *omdat* in Dutch).

### Unit 2 — Conjunctional adverbs
Unlike proper conjunctions, conjunctional adverbs (*daarna, dan, daarom, dus, echter, toch, immers,
trouwens*, etc.) link two *main* sentences and can occupy sentence-initial position (with
subject-verb inversion) or a mid-sentence position after the verb — never the fixed clause-final
verb position of a true conjunction. Grouped by meaning: time (*daarna/daarvoor, dan/toen* —
*toen* only past, *dan* usable for both present and habitual past), cause/effect (*daarom* =
reason, *daardoor* = cause, *dus* = flexible-position result), contrast (*daarentegen, echter/
evenwel* [near-identical meaning but *evenwel* more elevated/literary], *niettemin, desondanks,
integendeel* [fixed sentence-initial, comma-set-off]), and modifying/qualifying (*althans/
tenminste* [uncertainty], *bovendien/overigens* [addition, can invert subj-verb], *trouwens/immers*
[cannot invert]).

### Unit 3 — Nouns
Covers noun derivation from verbs (bare infinitive as neuter noun; recognizable verb-stem nouns;
ge- prefixation for ongoing/often-negative senses; -ing suffixation; verb-stem+noun compounds) and
from adjectives (undeclined adjective as noun, e.g. *de gek*, *het rood*; -e suffixation, including
from participles; -te and -heid suffixation). Extensive suffix tables for person-nouns (-aar, -(d)er,
-aard, -erd, -ier, -ist, -ling, -ant/-ent, -ator/-ateur, -eur) and their female-referent counterparts
(-es/-esse, -ster, -e, -ice/-euse, -in), plus other common noun-forming suffixes (-dom, -schap, -nis,
-(en/n,er/ar)ij, -iteit, -age, -atie, -theek, -sel) and meaningful prefixes (ge- [negative/ongoing],
mis- [went wrong], on- [negation], wan- [bad]). Compound-noun formation rules: N+N (gender takes the
second noun's gender); N+N with *tussen-s* connector (audibility-based, with genuine regional/
individual variation, e.g. *drugsbeleid* vs. *drugbeleid*); N+N with -e/-en connector (governed by
whether the first noun's plural ends in -en); N+N with -er connector (when the first noun's plural
is -eren, e.g. *kinderkamer*); and compounds with a verb stem, adjective, adverb, or preposition as
first element.

### Unit 4 — Adjectives
Predicate adjectives (after the noun) stay unflexed; attributive adjectives (before the noun) take
-e except before singular indefinite het-words. Derivation covered via participles-as-adjectives
(past participle ending in -en takes no extra -e), negative/reinforcing prefixes (on-, niet-/non-,
in-/ir-/im-, a- for negative; aarts-, oer-, door-, in- [intensive], over- for reinforcing), and a
large suffix table (-aal/-eel, -achtig, -baar, -ief/-iek, -ig/-erig, -isch, -lijk, -loos, -matig, -s,
-zaam) grouped by shared "of a certain nature/manner" meaning, with -baar/-zaam specifically tied to
verb stems ("can be done") and -loos to "without the noun." The adjective in comparison covers
similarity constructions (*even...als*, *net zo...als*, bare *even*) contrasted with ordinary
comparative/superlative, the *hoe...hoe/des te* correlative construction ("the more... the more"),
and the mandatory -s ending on adjectives following indefinite pronouns (*iets, niets, wat, wat
voor, veel, weinig, meer, minder, genoeg, voldoende, zoiets, allerlei* — e.g. *iets leuks*).

### Unit 5 — Possessives
Beyond the basic possessive pronouns (*mijn/m'n, jouw/je, uw, zijn/z'n, haar/d'r, ons/onze, jullie/je,
hun*, with reduced forms typical of colloquial speech), the unit covers: the possessive -s (with
apostrophe only after a/o/u/i/é/y-final words, e.g. *Lydia's*); the more common *van*-construction;
very informal postposed-possessor constructions with *z'n/d'r/hun* (e.g. *Erik z'n fiets*, marked
distinctly colloquial/spoken); possessive questions with formal *wiens*/*van wie* vs. informal *wie
z'n/d'r* (book notes *wie z'n* is far more common than *wie d'r* since the latter is gender-marked
female-only); the contrastive *die/dat van + object pronoun* construction (e.g. *die van mij*); and
the independent possessive with article + stressed possessive pronoun + -e (e.g. *de jouwe, het
mijne*), noted as grammatically correct but less common than the *van*-construction, especially for
plural persons.

### Unit 6 — Indefinite pronouns
Detailed distributional rules for *men* (formal, subject-only, singular verb) vs. the more informal,
personal alternatives *je* (subject or object, singular verb), *ze* (plural verb, excludes speaker/
listener), and *we* (plural verb, includes speaker/listener) — each substitutable for *men* in
different registers/contexts, with *men*-sentences often alternatively expressed as passives.
*Iemand/niemand* (persons) and *iets/niets* (things, with informal *wat*/*niks* alternants) are
covered, plus their combination with prepositions via *ergens*/*nergens* replacing *iets*/*niets* +
preposition. A detailed grammatical-distribution table governs *al* (uncountable het-words singular,
demonstratives/possessives), *alle* (uncountable de-words singular, all plural countables, numbers),
*allen* (persons only, more formal than *iedereen*), *alles* (things, opposite of *niets*, triggers a
*wat*-relative), and *allemaal* (primarily spoken, follows the noun/finite verb, refers to persons or
things). *Elk/ieder* function identically as attributive adjectives but only *elk* can refer to
things when used independently; *iedereen* is the informal, person-only, verb-3rd-singular
equivalent of *allen*.

### Unit 7 — The pronouns zich, zelf and elkaar
*Zich* is the reflexive pronoun for u/hij/zij/het/zij(pl) (conjugation table given for *zich
schamen*). *Zelf* combines with reflexive or personal pronouns to mark self/other contrast (fused
forms like *mezelf*, or separated forms like *ik...zelf* which are more common when *zelf* attaches
to a subject-form personal pronoun); several fixed expressions require the fused form (*ik ben
mezelf niet*, *tot zichzelf komen*, *op jezelf wonen*). *Zelf* used independently (not attached to a
pronoun) means "without help from others" or emphasizes the referent itself. *Elkaar* "each other" is
reciprocal, with adjectival form *elkaars* and an informal spoken variant *mekaar*.

### Unit 8 — Demonstratives
Three additional demonstratives beyond the basic *deze/die/dit/dat* system: *zo'n* (countable de-
and het-words singular, abbreviation of *zo een*), *zulk* (uncountable het-words singular), and
*zulke* (uncountable de-words singular + all plurals — the plural counterpart of *zo'n*). Used with
an adjective they intensify; used with a noun alone they classify/categorize. In combination with
*net* they express similarity comparison; with *niet* they express difference — the book flags *net*
vs. *niet* as an easily-confused near-minimal-pair.

### Unit 9 — Negatives
Recaps *geen* (indefinite/uncountable nouns) vs. *niet* (everything else), then gives detailed
positional rules for *niet*: generally as close to the sentence end as possible, but preceding
prepositional phrases, adverbs, and predicate adjectives, and following definite nouns, time
expressions, and *er/hier/daar*. Covers *niet* with auxiliary verbs (before the main verb's
infinitive), the special negation *niet hoeven te* (negating *moeten*), *niet*'s position in
subordinated clauses (before the clause-final verb group, or before a preposition attached to
*er-/hier-/daar-*), and the twofold option with opinion verbs (*denken, vinden, geloven*) for placing
*niet* either in the main clause or the subordinate clause. Additional negation words: *iemand/
niemand, iets/niets, ooit/nooit, ergens/nergens* — often paired with *nog* "still/yet" or *meer*
"anymore" to contrast with *al* "already."

### Unit 10 — The adverb er
Beyond the three basic functions from *Basic Dutch* (existential subject-introducer, locative,
quantitative), this unit adds *er* as a pronominal adverb with a preposition (*eraan, ermee*, etc. —
unseparated when nothing else intervenes, but separated when the clause contains a time expression,
negation, or object) and *er* as the clause-opening subjectless marker in passive sentences (*Er
wordt in Nederland veel gefietst*). Detailed positional rules follow *er* through main sentences,
subordinate clauses, reflexive-verb clauses, and clauses with a direct/indirect object — and the book
notes *er* never appears more than twice in one sentence (*Er zitten er vier in*).

### Unit 11 — Pronominal adverbs
Pronominal adverbs (*er-/daar-/hier- + preposition*) replace prepositional objects referring to
things (never persons). In questions, *wat + preposition* becomes *waar + preposition* (e.g.
*waarover*). Separation (splitting the adverb from its preposition) is common in speech and is
*mandatory* in fixed adjective+linking-verb+preposition combinations (*bang zijn voor*, *dol zijn
op*, *tevreden zijn met*) and whenever an adverbial phrase intervenes. *Hier-*/*daar-* forms (unlike
*er-*) can stand sentence-initial, and the choice between them tracks the speaker's temporal/spatial
distance from the referent. The pronominal-adverb-as-relative-pronoun construction (*waarover,
waarmee*, etc.) and pronominal adverbs of motion (*waarheen/-naartoe/-vandaan*, etc., adding *heen*,
*naartoe*, or *vandaan*) round out the unit. Note: *met* > *mee* and *tot* > *toe* in these forms; a
few prepositions (*zonder, tijdens, sinds, behalve, volgens, wegens*) cannot form pronominal adverbs
at all.

### Unit 12 — Verbs of action and result
Four transitive/intransitive verb pairs express placing an object into a position vs. that object
being in that position: *zetten/staan* (upright), *stoppen(steken)/zitten* (confined space, "small or
feels small" — jail is the book's own example), *leggen/liggen* (horizontal), and *hangen/hangen*
(single form covers both senses). *Steken* is a frequent but not fully interchangeable alternative to
*stoppen*. To make *zitten/liggen/staan* into action verbs for people, Dutch adds *gaan* (*hij gaat
zitten* "he sits down"); *staan* as an action verb pairs with separable prefix *op-* (*opstaan*
"get up"). *Hangen* used intransitively with a human subject occurs in the idiomatic "hanging
around/loitering" sense, sometimes with separable prefix *rond-*.

### Unit 13 — Durative constructions
Three overlapping ways to express ongoing action (a close analog to English -ing): (1) *zitten/staan/
lopen/liggen/hangen* + *te* + infinitive, where the posture verb reflects (or, in irritated/colloquial
usage, only figuratively suggests) the subject's actual position — a separable verb is split by *te*
in this construction; (2) *zijn* + *aan het* + infinitive, generally left unseparated even for
separable verbs (occasional colloquial separation noted for prefixes that are themselves adjectives/
nouns, e.g. *schoonmaken*); (3) *bezig zijn te* / *bezig zijn met* + a nominalized verb or noun,
distinguished carefully from English "busy" (which is *druk* in Dutch, not *bezig*). Each has its own
present-perfect and word-order rules, detailed with the infinitive-of-the-action always clause-final.

### Unit 14 — The present participle
Formed as infinitive + *-d* (*slapend* "sleeping"), usable as an adjective (taking the standard
adjective -e ending rules, including full sentence-element expansion before the noun, e.g. *haar luid
in zijn slaap snurkende broer*) or independently as an adverb of manner (typically endingless, though
*-de* appears after *al* or in fixed expressions like *Al doende leert men*).

### Unit 15 — Talking about the future
Three strategies, in ascending order of complexity/ambiguity: the present tense with a future-time
adverb (simplest, most common for near-certain events); *zullen* + infinitive (ambiguous between
intention and promise except when no such ambiguity is possible, e.g. events outside the speaker's
control); and *gaan* + infinitive (least ambiguous, most versatile, doesn't require an explicit
time adverb). The *futurum exactum* (*zullen* + present perfect) expresses an event expected complete
by a specific future point.

### Unit 16 — Separable and inseparable verbs
Building on *Basic Dutch* Unit 17, this unit systematically works through separable-verb behavior
across contexts: present perfect (ge- infixed between prefix and stem), subordinate clauses (verb
stays joined), subordinate clauses in the present perfect (three word-order options for splitting
around the helping verb), with modal verbs (unseparated infinitive at clause end; in subordinate
clauses, the modal can precede the joined infinitive or split it), the present-perfect-with-modal
combination (unseparated-at-end vs. split-around-both-auxiliaries), the conditional with *zou* (three
word-order options, ranked by frequency in speech vs. writing), the conditional with *zou* plus an
auxiliary verb, and combinations with *te* (te interposed between prefix and stem for plain te-verbs
like *vergeten/proberen/weigeren*; either split-before-modal or unseparated-after-modal for te-verbs
combined with a modal like *beloven/menen/dreigen*). Throughout, the book consistently notes that the
*separated* forms of the main verb are more common in speech, while unseparated/participle-final
forms are more typical of written Dutch.

### Unit 17 — Relative clauses
Systematic overview: *die* (de-words singular, all plurals; if referring to a person, must be
subject or direct object), *dat* (het-words singular), *waar + preposition* (things with a
preposition-taking verb), *wie* (persons with a preposition, i.e. indirect object/object-of-
preposition role; or no explicit antecedent, "whoever," common in proverbs), and *wat* (no explicit
antecedent, "whatever"; referring to a whole preceding/following sentence; following indefinite
pronouns like *iets/niets/alles/veel/weinig/genoeg*; following a superlative-with-*het* in a general
sense). The *die* vs. *wie* choice for persons is explicitly keyed to grammatical role (subject/
direct object → *die*; indirect object/object of preposition → *wie*). Separation of *waar +
preposition* (splitting into *waar...preposition*) is common in speech and interacts with separable-
verb splitting when both co-occur, with the preposition landing immediately before the separable
prefix.

### Unit 18 — Indirect speech
Indirect statements begin with *dat*; indirect questions with a question word or, for yes/no
questions, *of* "if/whether." Perspective shift (1st→3rd person, and possessive-pronoun changes)
depends on who addresses whom and how many speakers are in the exchange — illustrated through a
three-party phone-relay dialogue. Word-order notes: the subordinate clause's finite verb (or verb
group) goes to the end; an auxiliary/modal verb can precede or follow the main verb's infinitive/
participle; with more than one infinitive, the finite verb must precede them; a separated
pronominal adverb's *er* follows the subject (or reflexive pronoun); a separable verb rejoins with
its prefix in the subordinate clause, with the prefix positioned immediately before the verb group
when the participle is split.

### Unit 19 — Conditional sentences
Systematically ranks conditional forms by how likely, from the speaker's perspective, the condition
is to be fulfilled: present/present-perfect (near-future, likely; *als* can mean "if" or "when");
simple past, or *zou* + infinitive (less likely, hypothetical, or currently unfulfillable — the book
notes using simple past in *both* clauses of such a sentence risks being misread as a *habitual past*
statement, hence the preference for *zou* at least in the result clause); past perfect, or *zou* +
present-perfect infinitive (condition never was and never will be fulfilled — pure counterfactual).
Covers modal verbs within each tense layer (*zou kunnen* = could, *zou moeten* = should, *zou willen*
= would want to), plus four pragmatic/discourse uses of the conditional: formal requests (*Zou je
me... kunnen helpen?*), friendly/formal advice (*Je zou... moeten...*), expressing uncertainty about
reported information (*Zou dat waar zijn...?*), and the journalistic "allegedly" conditional for
unverified claims (*Hij zou die man vermoord hebben* "He is said to have killed that man").

### Unit 20 — The passive
Formed with *worden* + past participle across four tenses (present, imperfect, present perfect, past
perfect), each carefully glossed with a "what it actually means" gloss beyond the literal English
translation (e.g. *werd gebouwd* = "was in the process of being built, not yet finished"). Three
variants by information structure: object-emphasis (definite/known object becomes passive subject),
no-object-emphasis (*er*-initial for indefinite subjects or objectless action-only sentences, e.g.
*Er wordt geschoten*), and agent-emphasis (*door* + agent). With modal/auxiliary verbs, *worden*
moves to the infinitive-group end, with two word-order options (participle-then-*worden* more common
in writing, *worden*-then-participle more common in speech) that compound further with additional
modals (three- and four-verb-group examples given). The book stresses that the passive is far more
frequent in Dutch than in English, especially in formal/journalistic register.

### Unit 21 — Infinitive constructions
Explores clause-final infinitive "stacking," from the famous seven-infinitive sentence *Jan zou Piet
wel eens hebben willen zien durven blijven staan kijken* (cited from *Algemene Nederlandse
Spraakkunst*, p. 1066) down through the realistically common two-, three-, and four-infinitive
combinations. Establishes the consistent internal ordering rule: within any infinitive group, proper
modal verbs (*kunnen, mogen, moeten, willen, zullen*) precede quasi-modal verbs (*laten, gaan, komen,
blijven, zijn, horen, zien, voelen*), which precede the main verb, which is always last — with the
present-perfect/past-perfect helping verb, when present, as the first infinitive of the group. In
speech, simpler proper-past-tense forms (*kon, wilde, had*) are generally preferred over the more
formal stacked-*zou*/stacked-*had* constructions. In subordinate clauses, a two-verb group (one modal
+ main verb) can go either order, but modal-first is preferred and more modern.

### Unit 22 — Verb tenses
Full paradigm tables for *werken* (weak) and *rijden* (strong) across present, simple past
(imperfect), present perfect, and past perfect. Explains past-participle formation rules (weak: *ge-*
+ *-t*/*-d* depending on stem-final consonant; strong: *ge-* + *-en*, usually with stem-vowel change;
separable verbs: *ge-* infixed; verbs prefixed *be-/er-/ge-/her-/ont-/ver-*: no *ge-* at all) and the
*hebben*/*zijn* auxiliary-choice rule (motion or change-of-state verbs take *zijn*). The bulk of the
unit is a detailed usage contrast, illustrated through a continuous narrative about "Erik": present
tense (current facts; ongoing-since situations, where Dutch uses present where English would use
present perfect; habitual actions; future with *gaan* or future adverbs); simple past (narrating a
sequence of past events or past habitual actions; also usable to imagine a hypothetical future, see
Unit 19); present perfect (isolated past events with present relevance/result, contrasted directly
with simple-past "just narrating the past" via matched example pairs); and past perfect (an event
prior to another past reference point; counterfactual "could have but didn't" reflection). Special
attention to *nadat*-clauses: past perfect *nadat* pairs with simple-past main clause (looking
backward); present perfect *nadat* pairs with present-tense main clause (looking forward, still
in progress).

### Unit 23 — Prepositions
An alphabetically-organized-by-preposition catalogue of fixed verb+preposition combinations (aan,
bij, in, met, naar, om, op, over, tegen, tot, uit, van, voor — full list captured in the Vocabulary
table above), followed by fixed adjective/adverb+preposition and noun+preposition combinations. The
book explicitly frames this material as closer to vocabulary than grammar, best learned through
memorization and personal list-keeping — several groups (naar with sense-verbs; om with emotion-
verbs; over with thinking/talking/opinion-verbs) are called out as productive semantic clusters
rather than isolated idioms.

### Unit 24 — Word order
A capstone summary of clause-position mechanics threaded through the whole book. Coordinated/main
clauses put the finite verb second (except yes/no questions and imperatives, which front it);
subordinate clauses send it to the end. Main-clause middle-field ordering follows time-manner-place.
The book formalizes a six-position schema for main clauses (1: subject/fronted element, 2: finite
verb, 3: subject if not position 1, 4: middle field, 5: verb group, 6: trailing PP/*om...te*
constructions/further clauses) and a parallel five-position schema for subordinate clauses (1:
connector, 2: subject, 3: middle field, 4: verb group, 5: trailing PP/*om...te*/further clause),
each illustrated with a worked table of seven/six example sentences. Covers word order in the second
verb group for modal auxiliaries and present-perfect auxiliaries (both orders grammatical; speech
prefers auxiliary-before-main-verb), for *te*-infinitive auxiliaries with a direct object, and for
large (3-4 verb) verb groups in complex passive/conditional sentences — with a summary rule that the
main verb's past participle can sit at either the front or the back of the group, while among the
remaining verbs the finite/conjugated form comes first and modal-verb infinitives always precede
other infinitives within the group.

### Appendix — Strong and irregular verbs
A reference table (pp. 173-178) organizing ~150 strong verbs by their vowel-alternation pattern
across infinitive/simple-past/past-participle (e.g. **a-oe-a**: *dragen/droeg/gedragen* "carry";
**ij-ee-e**: *begrijpen/begreep/begrepen* "understand," *kijken/keek/gekeken* "look," *schrijven/
schreef/geschreven* "write"; **i-o-o**: *beginnen/begon/begonnen* "begin," *drinken/dronk/gedronken*
"drink," *zingen/zong/gezongen* "sing"; **ui-oo-o**: *sluiten/sloot/gesloten* "close," *ruiken/rook/
geroken* "smell"), roughly 14 pattern-classes in all, plus a short alphabetical list of strong verbs
that don't fit a clear pattern (*doen, gaan, houden, komen, lopen, roepen, staan, weten, worden,
zien*) and a further alphabetical list of irregular verbs whose past-tense forms mix strong and weak
behavior or otherwise deviate from the regular weak rule (*bakken, brengen, denken, hebben, jagen,
kopen, vragen, waaien, wassen, zeggen, zijn, zoeken*, etc.). The book's own text notes this appendix
is identical to the one appearing in the companion volume *Basic Dutch* — worth flagging for when

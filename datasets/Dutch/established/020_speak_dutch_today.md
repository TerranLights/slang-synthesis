# Dutch — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Speak Dutch Today! (complete short course)

**Source:** Rob ten Wolde, *Speak Dutch Today! A conversation course on cassette for visitors to The
Netherlands*, Hugo's Language Books Ltd, London, 1988 (ISBN 0 85285 132 4). Full book, pp. 3–70
(37 PDF pages/spreads): Preface, Introduction, 13 Dutch conversations with marginal *Toelichtingen*
(usage/cultural notes in Dutch, immediately followed by an English translation of the same notes),
and the English-only translation section of all 13 conversations (pp. 45–70).

**Coverage note:** Vision-read in full (no text layer — `pdftotext` returned nothing; confirmed via
`pdfinfo` showing the file was produced by "Adobe Acrobat 8.12 Image Conversion Plug-in", i.e. a
raster scan). All 37 PDF pages inspected; each PDF page is a two-printed-page spread (pdf page *N*,
N≥3, ≈ printed pages 2N−2 and 2N−1), confirmed by direct inspection rather than assumed from a fixed
offset. No handwritten marginalia of any kind found on any page — this copy is clean, consistently
typeset throughout (front cover, preface, all 13 conversations + notes, English translations, and the
publisher's back-of-book ad for Hugo's other titles). Vision Reading Confidence is `verified` for the
large majority of entries (the print is a clean, unfaded, high-resolution scan with no binding-gutter
shadow or font oddity); a handful of entries that were only legible via cross-reference to the
adjacent English gloss or footnote are marked `plausible_unverified` below.

**Non-redundant-supplement scope decision.** This is a *conversational phrasebook*, not a grammar
reference — its content profile is entirely different from `established/001`–`017` (Shetter & Van der
Cruysse-Van Antwerpen's *Dutch: An Essential Grammar* and the Routledge/other grammar sources), which
teach systematic paradigms. Nothing here duplicates a grammar paradigm already captured. What *is*
genuinely new and kept:
- **Real-world transactional-register vocabulary** across 13 distinct everyday scenarios (hotel
  reception, petrol station, tourist information, train station, four separate shopping vignettes —
  bakery/cheese shop/grocer/shoe shop, restaurant, post office, currency exchange, car rental,
  bicycle rental, travel agency, pub, and a private dinner-party visit) — a register and domain no
  prior Dutch `established/` file has covered from a native, idiomatic-conversational source.
- **A directly observed U-to-JIJ register shift**, staged by the book itself: all 11 transactional
  conversations (hotel, garage, station, shops, restaurant, post office, bank, shoeshop, car hire,
  bike hire, travel agency) use **only** the formal `u`/`uw` throughout, matching Toelichting 1 of
  conversation 1 ("men gebruikt altijd de beleefde vorm 'u'"), while conversation 11 ("Een ontmoeting
  in de kroeg") stages the exact pragmatic move of two strangers agreeing to switch to `jij`
  ("we kunnen elkaar best tutoyeren") and conversation 12 ("Bij vrienden te gast") both opens on `u`
  between new acquaintances and pivots to `jij` in the same scene once host/guest bond as friends.
  This is a genuine sociolinguistic data point (the *pragmatic trigger*, not just the paradigm) that
  no prior Dutch source has captured this concretely.
- Several isolated colloquial/spoken-register notes not seen in prior chunks: the *wij*→*we*,
  *zij*→*ze* spoken-substitution note (not applying to *hij*), the *m'n*/*z'n* clitic-contraction
  note, and the *graag* semi-bleached-politeness-marker note ("compare to English 'please'").
- A `taboo`-avoidance-naming-adjacent finding: the Anglo-American guest in conversation 12
  deliberately Dutchifies his own name (William → Willem/Wim) as a social-integration move — flagged
  as a `Notes` item rather than vocabulary, but worth recording since it's a genuine pragmatic/social
  usage fact about names, distinct from ordinary lexis.

**Skipped as non-redundant-but-low-value:** the English-only translation section (pp. 45–70) is a
literal/free translation of the same 13 Dutch conversations already vision-read here — it introduces
no new Dutch-language content and was not separately mined for vocabulary. The publisher's back-cover
catalog advertisement (final page, pdf p. 37) is pure marketing copy with no linguistic content and
was skipped entirely.

**Morpheme breakdown (agglutinative/polysynthetic/complex-fusional forms only).**

> **een aperatiefje** = aperitief ("aperitif," a French loanword) + -je (diminutive suffix) —
> softens what would otherwise be a fairly formal drink-offer into a casual, sociable one; consistent
> with the diminutive-as-pragmatic-softener pattern already documented in
> `established/003_chapter22_diminutives.md`.

> **een vuurtje** = vuur ("fire") + -tje (diminutive suffix, t-form after the long-vowel/r-final
> stem) — a stranger's opening request for a cigarette light is conventionally diminutivized; the
> same softening-request pragmatic function as above, occurring here in a genuinely spoken/native
> register rather than a grammar textbook's paradigm illustration.

> **het tientje** = tien ("ten") + -tje (diminutive suffix) — lexicalized as the standard colloquial
> name for the 10-guilder note itself, not merely "a small ten"; a clean example of a diminutive
> becoming the ordinary/default term for an object rather than remaining a stylistic option.

> **het poffertje** = poffen ("to puff up," of the pancake batter as it cooks) + -er (agent/result
> nominalizer, informal) + -tje (diminutive) — the diminutive here is lexically frozen: there is no
> non-diminutive "*poffer*" in ordinary use for this dish, paralleling "het flensje" below.

> **het flensje** = flens (dialectal/archaic base for "thin pancake," related to French "flan") + -je
> (diminutive suffix) — likewise a diminutive-only lexeme; no plain "*flens*" is used for the dish in
> standard usage per this source.

> **de damesfiets** = dames (genitive/linking plural of "dame," "lady") + fiets ("bicycle") — a
> transparent N+N compound, included here only to note the linking-plural-as-compound-first-element
> pattern (cf. e.g. "kinderfiets," not itself attested in this source) rather than for any special
> semantic opacity.

> **de bandenplakspullen** = banden ("tires," plural) + plak- (stem of "plakken," "to glue/patch") +
> spullen ("stuff/gear," colloquial plural) — an informal, everyday three-element compound (not
> textbook-standard vocabulary) for "puncture-repair kit"; "spullen" itself is a colloquial-register
> noun rather than the more formal "materiaal" or "gereedschap," so the whole compound sits at
> `colloquial` register even though each part is individually unremarkable.

## Grammar points

### The u/jij register switch is pragmatically staged, not just paradigmatic

Prior Dutch `established/` chapters (grammar references) describe the *u* vs. *jij/je* distinction as
a paradigm choice. This source is the first to actually **stage the social trigger** for switching:
conversation 1's own Toelichting 1 states the default rule plainly ("men gebruikt altijd de beleefde
vorm 'u'; men zegt 'jij' als men de andere persoon goed kent en als een volwassene met kinderen
spreekt" — "u" is used generally; "jij" only once the other person is well-known, or adult-to-child).
All 11 transactional conversations (hotel through travel agency, pp. 7–37) obey this without
exception — every single line uses *u*. Conversation 11 ("Een ontmoeting in de kroeg," pp. 39–41)
then dramatizes two strangers meeting, talking for several exchanges on *u*, and explicitly agreeing
partway through to drop to *jij* ("we kunnen elkaar best tutoyeren" — spoken by Anna to Marianne in
conversation 12, not 11; conversation 11 itself simply switches from *u* to *je*/*jij* between Frans
and Janet once they've exchanged names and arranged to meet again, without a metalinguistic comment).
Conversation 12 ("Bij vrienden te gast," pp. 42–44) makes the switch itself explicit dialogue content:
Marianne opens on *u* with the newly introduced Anna and Willem, and Anna's line "we kunnen elkaar
best tutoyeren" is the pivot moment — after which every line in the scene uses *jij/je/jullie*. This
is a directly observable pragmatic mechanism (comfort/familiarity established in-scene, then an overt
verbal move to license the switch) rather than an abstract rule — genuinely new data for the
eventual mechanics-analysis phase, since slang/register-shift mechanics in the sci-fi conlang work
will need exactly this kind of "what licenses a register switch" pattern, not just which pronoun
paradigm exists.

### wij/zij → we/ze in speech, but not hij

Toelichting/Notes 1 of "In een reisbureau" (p. 37): the written pronoun *wij* is replaced by *we* in
everyday spoken use, and the same reduction applies to *zij* (→ *ze*), but explicitly **not** to
*hij*, which has no equivalent spoken-reduced form. This is a source-marked asymmetry in the
Dutch pronoun system's formal/informal split that is worth keeping distinct from the m'n/z'n
clitic-contraction note below (different mechanism: whole-pronoun substitution vs. possessive
clitic-reduction).

### m'n / z'n clitic contraction

Toelichting/Notes 4 of "Een ontmoeting in de kroeg" (p. 41): the possessive *mijn* ("my") is commonly
shortened to *m'n*, and *zijn* ("his/its") to *z'n*, in ordinary speech. Distinct from — and in
addition to — the *wij→we* pronoun-substitution pattern above; this one is a genuine phonological
elision/clitic pattern on the possessive determiners specifically.

### graag as a semi-bleached politeness marker

Toelichting/Notes 1 of "Een ontmoeting in de kroeg" (p. 39): the source states *graag* "often does
not have any special meaning, it can be compared with the English 'please'" — i.e. the word retains
its literal "gladly" sense in some contexts but is frequently just a bare politeness marker with no
independent semantic contribution, the same functional slot English "please" occupies. Worth
comparing against Chapter 16's discourse-particle findings in `established/002` (*eens, even, maar,
toch, wel, hoor*) as a possible related but distinct category — a lexical adverb undergoing partial
pragmaticalization, rather than a dedicated discourse particle from the outset.

### perron vs. spoor: a source-flagged near-synonym pair with a register/currency shift

Toelichting/Notes 1 of "Op het station" (p. 16): *perron* and *spoor* mean the same thing
("platform"), but modern Dutch uses *spoor* more than *perron*. This is the source explicitly marking
a synonym pair as undergoing relative-frequency drift — comparable in kind (though smaller in scope)
to the formal/colloquial lexical-pair table already captured from Chapter 16 in `established/002`.

### commissie → kommissie: a live orthographic reform in progress (as of 1988)

Toelichting/Notes 2 of "Het wisselkantoor" (p. 28): the source explicitly frames the *c*→*k*
respelling (when *c* is pronounced /k/) as an ongoing modernization of Dutch spelling, citing
*kommissie* (formerly *commissie*) as the example. This is useful as a dated snapshot — the source's
own 1988 publication date is the only evidence for how far this reform had proceeded at time of
writing; treat any such spelling as attested for 1988 only, not necessarily standard modern Dutch.

### Holland vs. Nederland, and "Engeland" for the UK — parallel loose-naming conventions

Conversation 11's own dialogue (p. 39–40) has Frans explain that *Holland* strictly names only the
two western provinces while *Nederland* is the whole country's name, explicitly comparing this to
England vs. Great Britain — and Toelichting/Notes 4 of "In een reisbureau" independently notes that
the average Dutch speaker calls the whole United Kingdom "Engeland," which is the mirror-image loose
usage on the English side. Both are flagged by the source itself as imprecise-but-common naming
conventions, not the "correct" official terms — worth keeping as a paired finding since both push the
same general point (informal geographic naming trailing behind or diverging from official political
terminology) from opposite national perspectives.

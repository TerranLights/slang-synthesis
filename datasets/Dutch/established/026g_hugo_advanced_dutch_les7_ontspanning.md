## Grammar points

### Adverbs vs. adjectives — identical uninflected form

Dutch adverbs are formally identical to the uninflected adjective: **Hij bond ... zo rustig zijn
schaatsen onder.** ("He tied his skates on so calmly") — `rustig` answers "how," functioning as an
adverb without any special adverbial marking. p. 127.

### Adverbial phrase types and the mandatory when-how-where ordering rule

Dutch adverbials come in three functional types — **when**, **how**, **where** — realized as single
adverbs, single preposition phrases, or larger compound units joining several preposition phrases
together (e.g. **Op de banken bij de ingang van de ijsbaan...**, "On the benches by the entrance to
the ice rink..." — a `where`-type compound built from two preposition phrases). **When multiple
adverbial types co-occur in one sentence, Dutch requires them in exactly this order: WHEN, then HOW,
then WHERE** — a fixed constraint, illustrated: **Robinson zat trots naast haar vader** (how, where —
no "when" present); **Ik ging om drie uur snel naar het station** ("I went quickly to the station at
3 o'clock" — Dutch: when-how-where, contrasting with the more flexible English ordering options).
pp. 127–128.

### `gedrieën`/`getweeën` — cardinal numbers used independently, as pronoun-like forms

Dutch cardinal numbers add `-en` when used independently of a noun (**in tweeën delen**, "divide into
two"; **tussen hen tweeën in**, "in between the two of them"), and can further prefix `ge-` onto this
already-`-en`-suffixed form to produce `getweeën`, `gedrieën`, etc., meaning "the two/three of
us/them": **ze probeerden gedrieën het eerste rondje** ("the three of them tried out their first
circuit"). These `ge-`-prefixed forms are equivalent to the more colloquial `met z'n tweeën`/`met z'n
drieën`. pp. 118.

### Trema (¨) — marking a new syllable after a vowel string

The trema (diaeresis) is placed above the first letter (always a vowel) of a new syllable, used
wherever a run of vowels could otherwise be ambiguous about syllable division: `drie` + `-en` = two
syllables, spelled `drieën` to show the break falls between the two e's. Also seen in the proper name
`Daniël` in this lesson's own reading passage. p. 118.

### `te + zijn` + past participle — "having + past participle"

**Na twee, drie maal rond te zijn geweest...** = "After having been round two or three times..." —
a construction equivalent to English's perfect participial clause. p. 119.

### Compressed pre-nominal adjectival phrases

Dutch freely places what would require a relative clause in English directly before the noun as a
compressed adjectival phrase: **Dit 550 hectaren groot natuurdomein...** ("This nature reserve, which
covers 550 hectares..." / more naturally "This 550-hectare nature reserve..."). The source recommends
a general unraveling strategy for long sentences built this way: first locate the sentence's core
subject+main-verb pair (which may itself be inverted, e.g. because the sentence opens with an
adverbial phrase), then treat everything else as elaboration layered around that core. pp. 122–123.

### `zou/zouden` + modal auxiliary — three distinct functions

Building on the "future in the past" sense from Les 1, this lesson's Luistertekst introduces a
three-way functional split for `zou/zouden` combined with different modal auxiliaries:
1. **Wishes**: `zou/zouden + willen + infinitive` — **Ik zou een fietstocht willen maken.** ("I would
   like to go on a cycling tour.")
2. **Possibilities**: `zou/zouden + kunnen + infinitive` — **Je zou naar het sportcomplex kunnen
   gaan.** ("You could go to the sports complex.")
3. **Probabilities**: `zou/zouden + infinitive`, with **no modal verb at all** — **Daar zou ik meteen
   depressief van worden.** ("That would make me feel depressed straight away.")

The source explicitly flags that this three-way system will be extended with further grammar in the
next lesson. pp. 130–131.

## Grammar points

### Future tense (`zullen` + infinitive) — third and final new tense system in this book
Formed with a present-tense form of `zullen` (`ik zal, jij/u zult, hij zal, wij/jullie/zij zullen`) +
bare infinitive, clause-final: `Ik zal je morgen bellen`. Distinguished from the present tense (also
usable for future reference in Dutch, as in many languages) by degree of certainty/formality:
`zullen`-future is used for a promise, a firm intention, or a prediction one is confident about,
while the bare present tense suffices for a simple scheduled/expected future event. A modal-flavored
set of fixed constructions built on `zullen`: `zullen we...?` (a suggestion, "shall we...?"), `Dat zal
wel` ("that's probably so/I suppose so" — a hedge of likelihood, title of this lesson's opening
dialogue), `zeker en vast` intensifies this hedge. `zullen` can stand alone (without an infinitive)
when a verb of motion is contextually understood, same elision pattern as modal verbs (032f): `Zullen
we naar het concert?` ("shall we [go] to the concert?").

### Conditional mood (`zou(den)` + infinitive)
`zou`/`zouden` — historically the imperfect of `zullen`, but functioning here as a dedicated
conditional-mood marker — plus bare infinitive expresses "would (do)": `Ik zou dat niet doen`. In an
`als`-clause ("if"), `zou(den)` + infinitive in the main clause can be paired with either `zou(den)` +
infinitive **or** a plain imperfect in the `als`-clause itself, with no meaning difference (`Als hij
ziek zou zijn, zou hij dat niet doen` = `Als hij ziek was, zou hij dat niet doen`) — both mark the
condition as unreal/doubtful, referring to present or future time.

### Perfect (past) conditional (`zou(den)` + perfect infinitive)
For a condition referring to the **past** (something that didn't happen), Dutch uses `zou(den)` + a
"perfect infinitive" — i.e. `hebben`/`zijn` (bare infinitive form) + the perfect participle, both
clause-final: `Ik zou dat niet hebben gedaan` ("I wouldn't have done that"). Word order allows the
participle either before or after the auxiliary infinitive (`hebben gedaan` / `gedaan hebben` — both
attested, no meaning difference per source). This construction is combinatorially productive with
modal verbs, yielding chains of 3–5 stacked infinitives in a single clause (`Hij zou het mij niet
hebben willen laten zien` = "he wouldn't have wanted to let me see it"). Verbs that take a bare
infinitive (modals, and `hoeven`) never appear as a perfect participle in this chain — they stay in
infinitive form even where a perfect meaning is intended (`Hij heeft dat niet gekund/gewild`, not
*`gekun(d)/gewil(d)`* alternatives — wait, corrected: **not** `*heeft ... gekunt*`; the actual
participle-suppression rule is that when the modal itself governs another infinitive, the modal stays
infinitive-shaped: `Hij heeft dat niet kunnen/willen doen`, not *`gekund/gewild doen`*). After
`hoeven` specifically, the `te` particle vanishes in this perfect-infinitive-chain construction. A
separable prefix in one of these infinitive chains can be placed either attached to its own verb
(wherever that verb falls in the chain) or fronted to the very front of the whole verbal cluster.

### Causative `laten` + infinitive
`laten` ("to let/have/cause") + bare infinitive is Dutch's causative construction, translating
Russian's causative/permissive senses of "заставить/дать возможность/велеть": `Hij liet me dat zien`
("he showed that to me" / lit. "he let/had me see that"). Combines freely with modal verbs and the
conditional, producing the long infinitive-chains described above. `laten` itself never becomes a
perfect participle when it's the outermost verb in such a chain (stays as bare infinitive `laten`,
consistent with the modal-suppression pattern).

### `zich(zelf)` — reflexive with optional emphatic `zelf`
The plain reflexive pronoun (`zich`, `me`, `je`, `ons`, etc., 032d) can be intensified with `zelf`
attached directly after it to form an emphatic compound (`mezelf, jezelf, zichzelf, onszelf,
jezelf/uzelf`), used when the reflexive sense needs disambiguating or emphasizing (`Hij denkt alleen
aan zichzelf` vs. the plain, less emphatic `zich`). `zelf` (032c's emphatic "-self" pronoun) is a
**separate** word from this compound and is never itself fused onto a reflexive pronoun in ordinary
use — the two constructions (bare `zelf` as an independent emphatic pronoun vs. `-zelf` fused onto a
reflexive) serve different syntactic slots and must not be conflated, per an explicit exercise in
this lesson testing exactly this distinction.

### Separable vs. fixed-inseparable `over-` — a case study in prefix stress
Extends 032g's separable-prefix system with a worked minimal-set example: `overgaan` (`ging over` /
`is overgegaan`) is a normal **separable**-prefix verb (stressed prefix, splits under finite
conjugation, `ge-` infixed in the participle). `overleven` and `overvallen`, by contrast, are
**fixed-inseparable** despite `over-` normally being a separable prefix elsewhere — their prefix is
unstressed in these specific lexical items, behaving like `be-/ver-/ont-` (no `ge-` in the participle:
`overleefd`, `overvallen`). The lesson explicitly flags that a prefix's separable/inseparable status
must be learned per-verb, not inferred from the prefix's usual behavior with other verbs.

### `om...te + laten` — purpose clauses with a different logical subject
Modern Dutch has no subordinating conjunction directly equivalent to Russian "чтобы" that can freely
introduce a purpose clause with its own (different) subject. The `om...te` infinitive-purpose
construction (032c) requires the same subject in both clauses. When the purpose clause's *intended*
actor is a different person from the main clause's subject, `laten` is inserted into the `om...te`
construction to make the main-clause subject the (causative) subject of both actions: `Liesje neemt
Hein mee... om hem over het strand te laten rennen` ("Liesje takes Hein along... (in order) to let him
run along the beach" — literally "to have him run"). The pronoun standing for the actual logical
subject of the embedded action (`hem`) takes object case, since grammatically it's the object of
`laten`, not an independent subject.

### Kinship-term system
`schoon-` prefixes productively onto the core kinship terms to derive the whole in-law set
(`schoonvader, schoonmoeder, schoonzoon, schoondochter`, etc.); `klein-` prefixes onto `kinderen` for
"grandchildren." `de familie` denotes the extended kin group broadly, while `het gezin` denotes
specifically the immediate nuclear household — a distinction with no single-word equivalent in
English. `neef`/`nicht` are polysemous, covering both "nephew/niece" and "male/female cousin"
(disambiguated by context or by the qualifier `achterneef`/`achternicht` = "second cousin").

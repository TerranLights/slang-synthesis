### Grammar points

#### `liggen`/`staan`/`zitten` as forms of "to be"
The three positional verbs are frequently the natural Dutch translation for English "to be" when a
location or static state is described (l.1 *lag aan de grote weg* "was on the highway").

#### `-tal` as a group-size suffix
`-tal` can be added productively to any number from *twee* ("two") upward to form a noun denoting a
group of that size (*het tweetal* "the twosome"), unlike English "-some," which is restricted to
persons.

#### `blijven` + infinitive
Functions like English "keep on ...-ing" (l.23 *ze bleef het maken* "she kept on making it").

#### Diminutive as a pragmatic softener
`aardappeltjes` (l.12) illustrates the diminutive suffix used not for literal smallness but to add
informality/friendliness to a request — consistent with the "personalizing suffix" finding already
documented in `established/003_chapter22_diminutives.md`.

### Grammar points

#### Adjective-as-noun plurals refer only to persons
`bij anderen` (l.4) / `beiden` (l.23): when an adjective is nominalized and pluralized with `-en`,
the reference is restricted to people, not things — flagged twice in this story's annotations as a
recurring point.

#### `na-` prefix marking continuation
`nagenietend van` (l.29) — the prefix `na-` on a verb often signals that an action or feeling
continues after its triggering event, similar in function to (but not borrowed from) English "after-".

### Grammar points

#### Omission of a motion verb after a modal auxiliary
`moest` (l.5, = *moest gaan* "had to go") — a verb of motion is frequently dropped after a modal
auxiliary when the direction/destination is already clear from context; the same pattern recurs
across multiple stories in this collection (see also Story 1's *wou nou eenmaal* and Story 2's usage
notes).

#### `zijn` + `te` + infinitive (passive-potential construction)
`was niet meer af te leiden` (l.20) "couldn't be distracted any more" — this construction expresses
passive possibility/impossibility, roughly "to be ...-able."

### Grammar points

#### Impersonal passive with `worden`
`[er] werd getelefoneerd` (l.198) "somebody was telephoning" — a subjectless passive construction
possible in Dutch but not directly in English; the notional subject is left entirely unexpressed.

#### Preposition placement signaling motion
`de straat uit` (l.206) and `schoot achter haar rug om het hokje in` (l.83): when a preposition
follows its noun (rather than preceding it), Dutch marks that motion/direction is involved, distinct
from the same preposition used prenominally for static location.

### Grammar points

#### `ge-` + verb stem forming a neuter collective/activity noun
`het geknutsel` (l.23) "tinkering around" exemplifies a productive pattern: prefixing `ge-` to a bare
verb stem yields a neuter noun denoting the (often repetitive/informal) activity — parallel examples
in this story's own annotations: `gelach` "laughter," `geklets` "chatter," `gescrabbel` "playing
Scrabble."

#### `van die` + noun as "that kind of ___"
`van die vormloze zitmeubelen` (l.119) — the source flags this colloquial construction (literally
"of that ___") as very common for expressing a dismissive or vague "that kind of" categorization.

### Grammar points

#### Formal genitive `der`/`wier` used for ironic effect
The story repeatedly uses old-fashioned formal-register genitive constructions (*een der grieven*
"one of the grievances," *uit wier statuten* "from whose articles") when describing the mundane
bureaucratic self-importance of the neighborhood association — a register mismatch played for irony
throughout.

#### `hoe...ook` concessive construction
`hoe onhandig hij er ook bij stond` (l.75) "however awkwardly he stood there" — `hoe` + adjective +
`ook` forms a concessive "however ___" clause, paralleled at l.31.

## Story 8 — Maria Dermoût, *Dit is het verhaal van Oji* ("This Is the Story of Oji")

**Printed pp. 45–50 (PDF pp. 24–26).** Dermoût (1888–1962), born in Java to a family long resident in
the Dutch East Indies; moved to the Netherlands (The Hague) in the 1930s. This story, from *De kist*
(1958), is Dermoût's own adaptation of an episode from the *Babad Tanah Djawi*, a traditional Javanese
chronicle blending political/cultural history with folklore. A maiden (Oji) is raised for a prince's
harem, but the prince's son falls in love with her instead; when the affair is discovered, the
prince orders the deaths of his parents (and dozens of others), the destruction of his mother's rival
kingdom, and finally commands his own son to kill Oji himself — which the son does, holding her on
his lap. Dermoût frames this as an independent tale of love and sacrifice within the larger epic's
dynastic warfare.

## Story 9 — Adriaan Venema, *De schim* ("The Ghost"/"The Shadow")

**Printed pp. 51–60 (PDF pp. 26–30).** Venema (b. 1941, Heiloo, Noord-Holland), writer/journalist;
Louis Couperus Prize laureate (1975) for *Het leven een bries*. A nameless, loner/loser protagonist
stalks and ultimately kills a family that once treated him only as a source of gain; he becomes an
"individual with a name" only at the moment he appears at their door, and feels neither joy nor
remorse afterward — only satisfaction. The title's "ghost/shadow" is left deliberately unresolved:
the source's introduction notes the story uses a terse, unemotional present-tense style with
interwoven recurring motifs (a "violent start," a "nose against the window," a "turning around")
that create ironic contrast with the warm domestic life around the killer.

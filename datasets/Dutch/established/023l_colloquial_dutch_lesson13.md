## Grammar points

### The passive (pp. 174-176)

Formed with **`worden`** (lit. "to become") + past participle, in contrast to English's "to be" +
past participle — a structurally different but conceptually identical construction. The perfect-tense
passive substitutes `zijn` for `worden` (`is geopend` = "has been opened"), which the source flags as
a genuine trap: this looks like it should parallel a resultant-state reading ("is open/opened") but
functions as a true passive-perfect. A second flagged trap: `was geopend` is ambiguous between a past
stative reading ("was [in the state of being] opened") and a pluperfect-passive reading ("had been
opened") — Dutch collapses a distinction English keeps separate. As with the active voice, spoken
Dutch prefers the perfect passive (`is/was + participle`) over the simple past passive (`werd/werden +
participle`) for the same stylistic-preference reasons already established for the active voice
earlier in this corpus. Modal-stacked passive forms (`zal geopend worden`, `moet/kan geopend worden`,
`zal geopend moeten worden`, `zal geopend zijn`) extend the double-infinitive-cluster pattern from
`established/023i`/`023j` into the passive voice. The source's exercise 6 also flags a related trap:
`Het is verboden ...` ("It is forbidden ...") signage constructions describe a state/prohibition
rather than a true passive action and should not be miscounted as passive-voice examples.

### 'Whose?' and 'what?' + preposition (pp. 172-173)

Formal interrogative "whose" is `van wie`. **Colloquial spoken Dutch instead commonly uses `wie z'n`**
(literally "who's his") as a possessive interrogative, with a gender-marked feminine variant `wie
d'r` reserved for explicitly female possessors — otherwise the masculine `wie z'n` is the default
even when gender is unspecified (source's own worked examples: `wie z'n potlood`, `wie d'r b.h.`).
This is a clean, source-flagged colloquial-vs.-formal register pair. Interrogative "what +
preposition" follows the same `waar-` fusion/splitting pattern already seen for relative pronouns in
Lesson 12 (`waarmee` vs. `waar ... mee`), with the split form again marked as more usual in speech.

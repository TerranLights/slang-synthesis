## Grammar points

### 1. Hoofdletters en punten — capitalization and the full stop (Les 79, printed p. 196)

A sentence always starts with a capital letter and ends with a period. Capitals are also used for
proper names (given name + surname written in full, e.g. *Karel Appel*; *de heer K. van der Toorn*
— note the prefix/particle *van der* itself stays lowercase), for geographic names (*Nederland*,
*Turkije*) and their derived adjectives/nouns (*de Nederlandse taal*, *een Turkse moskee*), and
often for entities regarded as sacred (*de Bijbel*, *de Koran*, *de Almachtige*). Day and month
names are a systematic exception and stay lowercase (*maandag 31 januari*). The period is used at
the end of every sentence, after word abbreviations (*nl.* = *namelijk*, *pag.* = *pagina*), in
multi-word abbreviations (*m.a.w.*, *i.v.m.*), and in time/number notation (*11.35 uur*; *3.456*).

### 2. Komma's — the comma (Les 80, printed p. 197)

A comma marks a small pause a listener would hear in speech, but is not purely phonetic: some
commas are optional (stylistic — too many commas make text look cluttered) while others are
**meaning-bearing** — *Dat meisje is aardig, rijk en knap* ("that girl is nice, rich, and pretty" —
three separate qualities) contrasts with *Dat meisje is aardig rijk en knap* ("that girl is
[decently] rich and pretty" — here *aardig* functions as a degree adverb modifying *rijk*, not a
third coordinate adjective). A comma is **always** required between two finite-verb clauses in a
complex sentence (*Wat het zwaarst is, moet het zwaarst wegen*), and in enumerations/lists of
coordinate adjectives (*Zij speelt mooie, klassieke muziek*) — but never before the coordinating
conjunction itself.

### 3. Puntkomma; dubbele punt; vraag- en uitroeptekens (Les 81, printed p. 198)

The **semicolon** joins two main clauses when a comma is too weak a break and a period too strong,
implicitly carrying the force of an omitted connective — it can stand in for an unstated *doordat*
("because," giving a reason: *Mijn broer was vannacht erg misselijk; hij had zeker iets verkeerds
gegeten*) or an unstated *hoewel* ("although," marking a contrast: *Hij heeft erg zijn best gedaan;
toch is hij voor het examen gezakt*). Intonation cue: the pitch falls before a semicolon but rises
before a comma. The **colon** signals "namelijk / want / dat wil zeggen / immers" (an upcoming
example, explanation, clarification, conclusion, or list) and also introduces direct/quoted speech
(*directe rede*), which in turn takes single quotation marks. **Exclamation marks** correlate with
stress on the start of the sentence in speech; **question marks** correlate with rising pitch at
the sentence's end, with any specifically stressed word marked by an accent in this textbook's own
convention (*Vind je dit róm­mel?*).

### 4. Aanhalingstekens; haakjes; deeltekens (Les 82, printed p. 199)

**Quotation marks** (single, `'...'`) mark direct/literal quoted speech, and are also used around a
word used in a special/ironic sense (*Op Bali verkoopt men 'gouden' horloges voor een tientje* —
scare quotes signaling the watches are not really gold) or around a word being mentioned rather
than used (*het werkwoord 'kunnen' is onregelmatig*). **Parentheses** mark a clarification/
explanation (*Ik ben in Konya (Turkije) geboren*) or a cross-reference (*In deze les (82) worden
verschillende keren haakjes gebruikt*); **em/en-dashes** (*gedachtestreepjes*) set off a
parenthetical clause within a sentence. The **trema/diaeresis** (¨, on *ä ë ï ö*) marks that two
adjacent vowel letters are pronounced as two separate syllables rather than one combined sound,
used when a word has two-or-more vowels in a row that could otherwise be misread as a digraph
(*onderzeeër*, *coöperatie*, *patiënt*, *provinciën*). Words of French origin may keep their
native trema (*conciërge*, *cliënt*). In compounds whose parts can also stand alone, a **hyphen**
is used instead of a trema (*zo-even*, *mee-eten*, *na-apen*). Compound numerals may optionally
take a trema (*tweeëntwintig*). No trema/hyphen is needed for *ea*/*eo* sequences (*beamen*,
*beoordelen*) because Dutch has no such vowel-combination phonemes to disambiguate. The plural of
a word like *idee* also takes a trema on its added *-en* ending (*ideeën*; *knieën*); this
"stress-final -ie word gets -ën" pattern recurs in the plural-spelling lessons below (§7).

### 5. Koppelteken; weglatingsstreepje — the hyphen and the elision/omission dash (Les 83, printed p. 200)

The **hyphen** (*koppelteken*/*verbindingsstreepje*) joins two words into a compound where reading
without it would be difficult (*twee-eenheid*, *vakantie-uitkering*; contrast *zijuitgang*,
*premieheffing*, which don't need one, versus *bommelding*, *hyenavel*, which arguably should have
one even though the rule doesn't strictly require it). Beyond readability, a hyphen is
**required** in several specific compound types: letter/abbreviation/digit + noun compounds
(*cd-speler*, *KLM-piloot*, *1-1-gelijkspel*); compound geographic names (*Noord-Amerika*,
*Zuid-Vietnam*); compounds denoting a title/rank/status where the first element can independently
denote a person (*luitenant-kolonel*, *minister-president*, *amateur-fotograaf* — contrast
*magazijnchef*, *ploegbaas*, where it can't, so no hyphen); compounds of two coordinate/equal
elements (*de wedstrijd Ajax-Feyenoord*, *café-restaurant*, *radio-cd-speler*); married women's
double surnames (*mevrouw J. de Vries-Vink*); words prefixed by *ex-*, *oud-*, *niet-*, *non-*
(*ex-burgemeester*, *oud-minister*, *niet-roker*, *non-stop*); compounds with a personal name
(*de commissie-Franssen*, *het kabinet-Balkenende*); and compounds of several loose words strung
together (*kant-en-klaar*, *kop-en-schotel*, *langzaam-aan-actie*). The **weglatingsstreepje**
("omission dash," a shorter hyphen doing double duty as an elision marker, not to be confused with
the apostrophe) marks where a shared second element has been dropped from the first of two paired
compounds: *voor- en achterdeur*; *zon- en feestdagen*; *in- en export*; *jongens- en
meisjesboeken*. It is **not** used where nothing has actually been elided from a compound — *arme
en rijke landen* ("poor and rich countries") is two separate adjective+noun phrases, not an
elided compound, so no dash is permitted there.

### 6. De apostrof — the apostrophe (Les 84, printed p. 201)

Beyond the already-documented plural/possessive uses (established/001 covers the `-'s` plural for
vowel-final nouns), this lesson adds two further, genuinely novel apostrophe functions. First, the
**possessive apostrophe** is conditioned identically to the plural: used after a name ending in a
vowel preceded by a consonant (*Hanna's fiets*, *Saida's boek* — omitting it would misdirect the
pronunciation, as if the name were "Hannas"), but a name ending in a sibilant/hissing sound takes
**only** the apostrophe with no added *-s* (*Aziz' fiets*; *Hans' boek*). Second, and most directly
relevant to informal/spoken-register slang mechanics: the apostrophe marks **letter(s) dropped in
casual/spoken-style writing.** The fixed time expressions *'s morgens*, *'s avonds*, *'s middags*,
*'s nachts* are themselves fossilized elisions of the older genitive article *des* (*des morgens*,
etc.) — a form no longer used but still marked by the apostrophe standing exactly where the
dropped letters were, as also seen in the place names *'s-Gravenhage* and *'s-Hertogenbosch*. More
generally, writers representing spoken Dutch in print freely elide unstressed vowels/consonants
with an apostrophe at the drop site: *'k Dacht wel dat **m'n** zusje **'t** gezien had* ("I did
think my little sister had seen it" — *ik* → *'k*, *mijn* → *m'n*, *het* → *'t*). The source
explicitly cautions this device is easy to overuse and should be used sparingly even when
deliberately rendering spoken-register dialogue. **This elision pattern (unstressed pronoun/
article vowel-drop marked by apostrophe) is a directly relevant precedent for how a derived
in-universe colloquial register might mark casual contraction in writing**, worth flagging for the
eventual mechanics-analysis phase.

### 7. Meervoudsvormen op -eren en -ien — plurals in -eren/-ien (Les 85, printed p. 202)

Beyond the standard `-en`/`-s`/`-'s` plural strategies (established/001), a small closed class of
*het*-words takes an **-eren** plural: *het been* → *de beenderen* ("bones" — distinct in meaning
from the homonymous *de benen*, "legs"), *het blad* → *de bladeren*, *het ei* → *de eieren*, *het
kalf* → *de kalveren*, *het kind* → *de kinderen*, *het lam* → *de lammeren*, *het lied* → *de
liederen*, *het rad* → *de raderen*, *het rund* → *de runderen*, *het volk* → *de volkeren*. A
specific consonant-doubling trap is flagged: *blad* and *rad* do **not** double their final
consonant in the plural (*bladeren*, *raderen*), unlike *lam* → *lammeren*, which does. Two nouns
irregularly take **-ien**: *de koe* → *de koeien*, *de vlo* → *de vlooien*. A further set of
common nouns simply doesn't double its final consonant where the standard rule might predict it
should (*de dag* → *de dagen*, *het gat* → *de gaten*, *de weg* → *de wegen*, *de god* → *de
goden*), and a handful of nouns are outright irregular: *de stad* → *de steden*, *het schip* → *de
schepen*, *de smid* → *de smeden*, *het lid* → *de leden*, *de oom* → *de ooms* (regular despite
looking like a candidate for vowel change), *de broer* → *de broers*, *de zoon* → *de zoons*.

### 8. Meervoudsvormen op -s, -'s, -en — loanword-origin-conditioned plural spelling (Les 86, printed p. 203)

A fine-grained sub-rule not previously captured in this project's Dutch coverage: nouns ending in
*-e, -é, -ee, -ieu, -ui, -ie, -eau, -erd, -aard,* and *-ey* take a plain **-s** plural. The source
explicitly separates these by loanword origin: words ending *-e/-é/-ee/-ieu/-ui/-ie/-eau* are
**French loanwords** (*de tante* → *tantes*, *de logé* → *logés*, *het café* → *cafés*, *de
marechaussee* → *marechaussees*, *het milieu* → *milieus*, *het etui* → *etuis*, *de provincie* →
*provincies*, *het bureau* → *bureaus*), while words ending *-erd/-aard* (*de dikkerd* →
*dikkerds*, *de luiaard* → *luiaards*, *de grijsaard* → *grijsaards*) and *-ey* (*de diskjockey* →
*diskjockeys*, *het display* → *displays*) are **all English loanwords not ending in a single bare
vowel**, which as a class take *-s* with no apostrophe. This contrasts with the already-documented
apostrophe-plural class (*-a/-o/-u/-i/-y* endings: *de agenda* → *agenda's*, *de foto* → *foto's*,
*de accu* → *accu's*, *het alibi* → *alibi's*, *de hobby* → *hobby's*) — the apostrophe there is
purely disambiguating (*\*radios*/*\*kanos* would misread as a different word without it). Separately,
a small set of *-ee*-final nouns (other than the near-homophonous *zee*/*twee*, which pluralize
irregularly and aren't part of this pattern) takes an **-en** plural *with* a mandatory trema: *het
idee* → *de ideeën*, *de ree* → *de reeën*, *de fee* → *de feeën*, *de orchidee* → *de orchideeën*,
*de pygmee* → *de pygmeeën*.

### 9. Meervoudsvormen bij woorden die eindigen op -ie, -ik, -es, -et en -heid — stress-conditioned plurals (Les 87, printed p. 204)

For nouns ending in **-ie**, the plural depends on where word stress falls, which the source notes
must be checked in a dictionary (it cites *Dale Pocketwoordenboek NT2*'s stress-mark convention):
if stress does **not** fall on the final syllable, the plural adds a bare **-n** (with a trema on
the *e*): *de bacterie* → *bacteriën*; *de kolonie* → *koloniën*; *het evangelie* → *evangeliën*;
*de ceremonie* → *ceremoniën*. If stress **does** fall on the final syllable, the plural instead
adds **-en** (also with a trema): *de melodie* → *melodieën*; *de categorie* → *categorieën*; *de
genie* → *genieën*; *de industrie* → *industrieën*. (A small exception set takes a plain *-s*
instead: *de vakanties*, *de kwitanties* — cf. Les 86.) For nouns ending in **-ik, -es,** or
**-et**, stress again determines whether the final consonant doubles: unstressed-final examples
don't double it (*de perzik* → *perziken*; *de monnik* → *monniken*; *de havik* → *haviken*; *de
luiwammes* → *luiwammesen*; *het lemmet* → *lemmeten*), while stressed-final examples do (*de
kroket* → *kroketten*; *de barones* → *baronessen*; *de marionet* → *marionetten*). Nouns ending in
**-heid** always pluralize with **-heden**, no exceptions noted (*de waarheid* → *waarheden*; *de
kleinigheid* → *kleinigheden*).

### 10. Meervoudsvormen op -i, -a, -en, -sen — classical-loanword and s→z plural patterns (Les 88, printed p. 205)

Latin-origin nouns ending in **-us** take a Latin-style plural in **-i**: *de medicus* → *medici*;
*de historicus* → *historici*; *de chemicus* → *chemici*; *de catalogus* → *catalogi*. Latin nouns
ending in **-um** allow **either** a Latin plural in *-a* **or** a Dutch plural in *-s*: *het
museum* → *musea* or *museums*; *het aquarium* → *aquaria* or *aquariums*. Separately from
established/001's general f→v alternation rule (*brief* → *brieven*; *neef* → *neven*), words of
**Greek or other Eastern origin retain the *f*** in the plural rather than alternating to *v*: *de
filosoof* → *filosofen*; *de fotograaf* → *fotografen*; *de telegraaf* → *telegrafen*; *de
paragraaf* → *paragrafen*; *de triomf* → *triomfen*; *de kalief* → *kaliefen* — this is presented
as an origin-conditioned exception to a rule this project's prior extractions had only stated in
its general (non-Greek-loanword) form. Similarly, alongside the general s→z plural alternation
(*roos* → *rozen*; *prijs* → *prijzen*), a list of common native Dutch words is flagged as simply
**not** undergoing that alternation, with the source noting these exceptions have no deeper
explanation ("gewone Nederlandse woorden," i.e. this is lexical idiosyncrasy, not a further
sub-rule): *de mens* → *mensen*; *de wens* → *wensen*; *de kers* → *kersen*; *de kaars* →
*kaarsen*; *de dans* → *dansen*; *de krans* → *kransen*; *de schans* → *schansen*; *de pols* →
*polsen*; *de kous* → *kousen*; *de paus* → *pausen*; *de eis* → *eisen*.

### 11. Werkwoorden met een prefix — meaning-changing separable-prefix verbs (Appendix, printed pp. 222–223)

The source frames this appendix as tied to Lesson 66 ("examples for Les 66") and explicitly notes
it is **not exhaustive** — many base verbs can take more than one prefix, each with a distinct
meaning (its own worked example: *breken* can take *in-, op-, uit-* to give *inbreken* "to break
in/burglarize," *opbreken* "to break up/dig up," *uitbreken* "to break out," each semantically
distinct), and readers are directed to a dictionary when genuinely unsure. See the Vocabulary table
below for the full list of base-verb/prefixed-form pairs captured.

**The stress-shift minimal pairs are the most striking finding in this appendix** and are worth
flagging for later mechanics-analysis work: a handful of separable-looking prefix+verb
combinations carry **two entirely different meanings and two different past-participle/conjugation
patterns depending purely on which syllable is stressed** — the prefix is separable (and takes
*ge-* in the past participle) under one stress pattern, and inseparable (no *ge-*) under the other:

- **dóórlopen** (stress on *door*, separable) "to walk through/across, to keep walking" →
  *doorgelopen* (e.g. *Toen ik dat ongeluk zag gebeuren ben ik snel doorgelopen*, "I quickly walked
  on when I saw that accident happen") vs. **doorlópen** (stress on *lopen*, inseparable) "to walk
  the entire length of, to be worn out/covered on foot" → *doorlopen*, no *ge-* (*Hij heeft de hele
  cursus doorlopen*, "he has completed/gone through the whole course").
- **óndergaan** (stress on *onder*, separable) "to go down, set (of the sun)" → *ondergegaan* (*De
  zon is heel mooi ondergegaan*) vs. **ondergáán** (stress on *gaan*, inseparable) "to undergo" →
  *ondergaan*, no *ge-* (*Hij heeft al drie operaties ondergaan*, "he has already undergone three
  operations").
- **overléggen** (inseparable, "to consult/deliberate") → *overlegd* (*Hij heeft eerst met zijn
  leraar overlegd of hij examen zou doen*, "he first discussed with his teacher whether he would
  take the exam") vs. **overleggen** (separable, *legde over*, "to submit/hand over," e.g. a
  document) → *overgelegd* (*Om een cheque in te wisselen moet u een identiteitsbewijs
  overleggen*, "to cash a cheque you must present ID").

This is a genuine minimal-pair phenomenon in Dutch morphophonology — stress placement alone
carries lexical/grammatical weight (separable vs. inseparable prefix status, with a knock-on effect
on participle formation) — and is a plausible mechanism worth keeping in mind for how a derived
slang register might exploit stress-shift for meaning play.

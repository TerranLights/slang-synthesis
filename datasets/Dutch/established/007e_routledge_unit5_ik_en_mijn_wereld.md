the source's own cover/title page did not survive `pdftotext` extraction, so exact author names and

**Coverage note:** every "Key vocabulary" box in this unit is captured below, plus vocabulary
explicitly glossed inline (the film-genre list, the "male"/"female" adjective-sorting list, star-
rating vocabulary, magazine-name vocabulary). Repeated drill/"Oefening" exercises that introduce no
vocabulary or grammar beyond what's already captured were skipped per the coverage rule (e.g. the
compound-noun-building drills in Oefening 18–20 recombine already-known base nouns without
introducing new lexical items; the adjective-form and comparative/superlative fill-in drills in
Oefening 5–6, 9, 13–14, 16 likewise recombine vocabulary already listed). This PDF's text layer is
real (not vision-read) but — as already noted for Unit 4 — carries heavy, irregular OCR-quality
character noise/typos baked into the source file itself (e.g. "Bet" for "Het," "fOokt" for "rookt,"
"uper" for "u per," stray "lJ," sequences standing in for corrupted "ij"/"in" clusters, e.g.
"lJ,eemtu" for "neemt u"). This is not a decodable fixed-substitution cipher — it is irregular
per-page corruption — so every extracted item below (especially dialogue and key-vocabulary boxes)
was cross-checked against context and known Dutch vocabulary rather than trusted verbatim; a few
individual words in the two "male"/"female"-image opinion-piece texts (pp. 158–160) remained
genuinely ambiguous in the raw extraction and were left out of the vocabulary table rather than
guessed, per the "default to the riskier assumption when uncertain" principle.

## Grammar points

### 1. *Er* — three distinct functions (p. 133–135)

*Er* is a single small word covering several unrelated grammatical functions in Dutch:

- **Indefinite-subject *er*** ("there is/there are"): used with an indefinite subject, e.g. *Er
  staan drie stoelen en een tafel in de keuken* ("There are three chairs and one table in the
  kitchen"). Note that the position verbs *zitten/liggen/staan/hangen* (already introduced in Unit
  3 per this source's own cross-reference) commonly co-occur with this *er*, translating roughly as
  English "to be."
- **Locative *er*** ("there," referring to a previously mentioned location), placed immediately
  after the finite verb in the main clause: *Ben je al in New York geweest? — Nee, ik ben er nog
  nooit geweest* ("Have you been to New York? — No, I've never been there"). *Daar* can substitute
  for locative *er* when the speaker wants to stress the place, and *daar* then typically takes
  first position in the sentence: *Nee, daar ben ik nog nooit geweest*.
- ***Er* combined with a number**, left untranslated in English, occupying the same post-finite-verb
  position as locative *er*: *Hoeveel kinderen heb je? — Ik heb er twee* ("I have two").

### 2. Comparative and superlative (pp. 138–140)

Regular pattern illustrated with parallel examples: *leuk → leuker → de leukste*; *mooi → mooier →
de mooiste*; *lekker → lekkerder → het lekkerst(e)*; *duur → duurder → de duurste*. A handful of
irregular/suppletive comparatives are listed: *graag → liever → het liefst(e)*; *goed → beter → het
best(e)*; *weinig → minder → het minst(e)*; *veel → meer → het meest(e)*.

Two fixed constructions built on the comparative:
- **"the more... the better" pattern**: *hoe meer, hoe beter*; *hoe groter het huis, hoe duurder het
  wordt* ("the bigger the house, the more expensive it gets").
- **"too + adjective"** is expressed with *te + adjective*, structurally parallel to English: *Die
  broek is veel te lang* ("Those trousers are way too long").

### 3. Demonstrative pronouns: *deze/die/dit/dat* (pp. 145–147)

- **Form**: *deze* and *die* are used for English "this"/"that" respectively with **de**-words;
  *dit* and *dat* are used for English "this"/"that" with **het**-words. All plural nouns are
  *de*-words, so plurals always take *deze/die* regardless of the singular's gender.
- **Standalone use**: when reference is clear from context, *deze/die/dit/dat* can stand alone
  (without a following noun), translating as English "this one"/"that one": *Gaat deze trein naar
  Keulen? — Nee, deze gaat naar Brussel.*
- **Pronoun-replacement function**: *die* and *dat* can also stand in for a personal pronoun
  referring to a previously named person or object — a use with no direct English equivalent
  (English would just use "she/he/it/they"): *Waar is moeder? — Die is in de keuken* ("She's in the
  kitchen"); *Waar zijn de kranten? — Die liggen op de tafel* ("They're on the table"); *Waar is
  mijn boek? — Dat ligt op je bureau* ("It's on your desk").
- **Introducing people/things**: *dit*/*dat* (never *deze*/*die*) are used to introduce a
  person/thing regardless of the following noun's gender or number: *Dit is mijn vriend*; *Dat zijn
  mijn kinderen* ("Those are my children") — the source explicitly flags that *deze* and *die* are
  **not** used in this introducing function, even before a plural.

### 4. Compound nouns (pp. 151–152)

Dutch compound nouns are always written as a single word (unlike English's frequent two-word
compounds), which can produce very long words — the source's own illustrative example is
*verzekeringsmakelaarskantoor* ("insurance broker's office"). **The compound as a whole takes the
grammatical gender/article of its final (head) noun**, regardless of the gender of any earlier
element: *de verzekering* + *de makelaar* = *de verzekeringsmakelaar*; *de thee* + *het glas* = *het
theeglas* (gender flips from the second element's own *het* to... actually stays *het* here since
the head *glas* is itself *het* — i.e., gender always tracks the final noun). Three compound
patterns are given: (a) noun + noun (*de videocamera*, *het krantenartikel*); (b) verb stem + noun
(*lezen* → *de leestekst*, "reading text"); (c) other word class + noun (*achter* + *deur* =
*achterdeur*, "back door"). A linking element (*-en-*, *-s-*, or *-e-*) is sometimes inserted
between the two nouns (exercised in Oefening 20 but the specific linking rule itself is left
unstated in the source beyond "you may want to use a dictionary" — not a captured rule, since the
book itself doesn't spell out the conditioning).

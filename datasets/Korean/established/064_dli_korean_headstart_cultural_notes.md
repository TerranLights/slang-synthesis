# Korean — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: *Korean Headstart — Cultural Notes* (full booklet)

**Source:** Defense Language Institute, Foreign Language Center, *Korean Headstart: Cultural
Notes* (First Edition, November 1984), 42pp. The companion cultural-orientation volume to the
main *DLI Korean Headstart* course (being extracted in parallel as `established/063`; not
cross-read here per dispatch instructions). Full-booklet extraction (all 6 units: Introduction;
A Brief Background; Korean Culture and Values; Customs and Etiquette; Travel, Shopping, and
Recreation; Additional Information — plus the booklet's own bibliography and city-map key).

**Text layer note.** `pdfinfo` shows `Producer: Adobe Acrobat 9.41 Paper Capture Plug-in` — this
PDF carries a genuine OCR-derived text layer (not a font-substitution cipher), confirmed by
`pdftotext -f 1 -l 3` returning readable, if noisy, English prose rather than garbled glyphs. No
+36266-offset or other Hangul cipher check was needed because **the book contains no Hangul
script anywhere** — it is 100% English expository prose with Korean terms given only in informal,
inconsistent romanization (mostly McCune-Reischauer-adjacent, e.g. `kibun`, `ajossi`, `Ch'usok`,
but with visible OCR noise: `popUlation` for "population," `las~ed` for "lasted," `3et along` for
"get along," `~ehind` for "Behind"). Vision-reading was not required; all entries below are
`n/a` for Vision Reading Confidence (real text layer) and `n/a` for Transcription Confidence
(not a subtitle/transcript source). No handwritten marginalia was found anywhere in the scan.

**Coverage note — non-redundant-supplement dispatch.** This booklet has zero Korean-language
grammar content (no verb conjugation, no particles, no sentence structure) — it is pure cultural/
sociopragmatic orientation prose for incoming U.S. military personnel, so there is no "Grammar
points" section in the usual sense; the subsections below cover sociolinguistic/register systems
instead, per this project's standing note that DLI-style cultural-notes companions tend to carry
register observations the paired grammar course doesn't. Checked against the existing established
Korean corpus (61 files before this one) for overlap:

- **Skipped as already well-covered:** the `ajossi`/`ajumma`(`ajumoni`)/`agassi` age-scaled
  address-term set (`established/014`, `030`, `054` already give fuller treatment, including the
  insult-risk of misjudging someone's age and the generational `yogiyo!` shift); `kibun` as a
  mood/social-harmony concept (`established/020` already covers it in comparable depth, including
  the "no" ≈ disturbed-kibun link this source also makes); `kimch'i`, `shiktang`, `hanbok`,
  `kisaeng`, `yogwan` as bare vocabulary items (covered across 8+ prior files); `Ch'usok`/Seollal
  as individual holidays (`established/016`, `040`-`042`, `045`, `059`); the basic honorific
  `-nim` suffix and `sonsaengnim` (`established/001`, `010`, `014`, `017`, `030`-`031`, `033`,
  `035`, `042`, `045`); Confucian family/hierarchy values as a general substrate
  (`established/010`, `011`, `015`, `020`, `031`, `041`, `045`, `049`-`050`, `053`).
- **Kept because genuinely new or deeper:** the full **titles-without-names vs. titles-with-names
  vs. military-rank** three-tier address system including `sonsaeng` (no `-nim`) as an
  intermediate formality level between bare `sonsaengnim` and `last name + sonsaengnim` — no prior
  source gave this three-tier breakdown; the explicit **self-reference prohibition** on `-nim`
  (never applied to one's own name/title) and the **don't-imitate-foreigner-mispronunciations**
  caution (`adassi` for `ajossi`, `ajima` for `ajumoni`) and the **don't-use-Japanese-terms**
  caution (`Mama-san`/`Papa-san`) — all three are address-system *usage rules* about what not to
  say, not found in any prior Korean extraction; a **fuller 1984-era official holiday calendar**
  (11 fixed-date holidays including Arbor Day/`Shikmok-il`, Armed Forces Day/`Kukkunui-nal`, and
  Hangul Day/`Hangul-nal`, none of which appear in `established/045`'s otherwise-fuller 10-holiday
  table) plus **narrative lunar-festival customs** for `Hanshik` (Cold Food/Grave-Visiting Day) and
  `Tano-jol` (swinging matches, wrestling, tug-of-war) that no prior source described; the **"key
  money" (chŏnse-type) housing-deposit system**, entirely new to this corpus; a **named,
  described set of four traditional games** (`paduk`, `changgi`, `hwat'u`, `yut`) with rules —
  prior sources only named the `hwatoo`/`go-stop` card game in passing (`established/030`), never
  `paduk`, `changgi`, or `yut`, and never with rule descriptions; the **productive place-name
  suffix system** (`-do`, `-gun`, `-ŭp`, `-ri`/`-ni`, `-si`, `-gu`, `-dong`, `-gung`, `-won`,
  `-kongwon`, `-mun`, `-sa`, `-san`) as a systematic compounding inventory, not found tabulated
  anywhere else in this corpus; a handful of **transportation/hospitality terms** not previously
  attested (`Saemaul-ho`, `t'ukkup yŏlch'a`, `kosok` bus already covered in `054`/`055` so kept
  only where this source adds the `t'ukkup yŏlch'a` contrast; `t'abang`/`tashil`, `mogyok-t'ang`,
  `paekban`, `anju` partially new); and the **drinking-party glass-exchange ritual** and
  **triple-refusal hospitality convention** (host must offer 2-3 times before a guest accepts) as
  sociopragmatic register rules distinct from anything already captured. The historical/geographic
  background material (Units 2 and the city-map key) is almost entirely proper nouns and general
  facts with no linguistic payload, so only the handful of items with genuine
  romanization/register content are kept; the bulk of Unit 2 (population figures, DMZ geography,
  government structure) is skipped as outside this project's scope.

**Morpheme breakdown.** The place-name suffix system above is a productive compounding
inventory rather than a set of isolated vocabulary items — the source's own worked examples
(Kyŏngbok-kung "Shining Happiness Palace," Tongdaemun "East Gate," Namdaemun shijang "South Gate
Market") show these suffixes stacking onto place-name roots: e.g. **Namdaemun shijang** = *nam*
("south") + *tae* ("great") + *mun* ("gate") + *shijang* ("market") — "South Gate Market," the
market located at the South Gate. This is the first systematic tabulation of this particular
morphological inventory in the Korean corpus; prior sources attested individual place names as
opaque proper nouns without decomposing the suffix system.

## Grammar/usage points

*(This booklet has no Korean-language grammar content — see the coverage note above. The
subsections below document sociopragmatic/register systems instead, following this project's
practice of treating a cultural-notes companion's register observations as the structural
counterpart to a grammar course's syntax rules.)*

### Three-tier address-title system (pp.13-15)

The source distinguishes three address registers, graded by formality: (1) **titles without
names** (`sonsaengnim`, `ajossi`, `ajumoni`, `agassi`) for strangers of unknown exact status; (2)
**titles with names** (`last name + sonsaengnim`, the slightly-less-formal `last name + sonsaeng`,
or `misu/misut'ŏ + last name`); and (3) **military-rank forms** (`last name + rank + nim` for a
superior, `last name + rank` for an equal/subordinate). The `-nim` suffix is explicitly one-
directional — it honors the addressee and is never applied to one's own name or title, mirroring
(but independently attested from) the self/other asymmetry already documented for Korean
honorific morphology in `established/031`. The source frames two further DON'Ts as foreigner-
specific errors rather than Korean grammar per se: don't copy mishearings like `adassi`/`ajima`,
and don't substitute the Japanese loan terms `Mama-san`/`Papa-san` for Korean elders — both read
as a caution about imported pidgin-English-base-town usage contaminating genuine Korean address
forms, a register-contamination risk this corpus hasn't previously flagged explicitly.

### Hospitality triple-refusal and host-pays conventions (pp.20-21)

Korean hospitality etiquette, per the source, runs on a **scripted refusal-then-acceptance**
pattern: a host is expected to repeat an offer of food, drink, or entry two or three times before
a guest accepts, and a guest's first "no" is conventionally understood as modesty rather than a
genuine refusal. Paired with this is a **no-splitting-the-bill convention**: whoever proposes an
outing (coffee, drinks, a meal) is expected to pay for the whole group, not just themselves — the
inverse of "going Dutch." At drinking parties specifically, guests are expected to participate in
a **glass-exchange ritual** (passing one's own glass to others in turn as a friendship gesture);
declining to trade glasses is read as a social insult to the other guests, even for a non-drinker
substituting a soft drink. None of these three conventions (triple-refusal, host-pays, glass-
exchange) were previously documented in this corpus as discrete sociopragmatic rules.

### Place-name suffix system (pp.25-26)

See the Morpheme breakdown entry above — a closed, listable set of locative/administrative
suffixes (`-do` province/island, `-gun` county, `-ŭp` township, `-ri`/`-ni` village, `-si` city,
`-gu` city-district, `-dong` sub-district, `-gung` palace, `-won`/`-kongwon` park/garden, `-mun`
gate, `-sa` temple, `-san` mountain) that compounds productively onto place-name roots, functioning
analogously to a closed set of locative-class nominal suffixes.

### Traditional games (pp.32-33)

Four traditionally-named games are described with enough rule detail to distinguish them
structurally: `paduk` (territory-control board game = go/baduk), `changgi` (Korean chess, with
king/horse/elephant/soldier-type pieces), `hwat'u` (a 48-card, 12-suit flower-card game, often
used for gambling — the source's description sharpens but does not contradict `established/030`'s
brief mention of the related `go-stop`/`hwatoo` card game), and `yut` (a four-stick, dice-like
throwing game). This is the first time this corpus has named and described `paduk`, `changgi`, and
`yut` specifically.

### Holiday calendar, 1984 edition (pp.5-6)

A fuller 11-item fixed-date national holiday list plus four narrated lunar festivals, using
1984-era DLI romanization that differs cosmetically from the Revised-Romanization spellings in
`established/045`'s otherwise fuller table (e.g. `Kwangbok-jol` = Liberation Day, `Samil-jol` =
Independence Movement Day). Three holidays in this list — **Arbor Day** (`Shikmok-il`), **Armed
Forces Day** (`Kukkunŭi-nal`), and **Hangul Day** (`Hangul-nal`) — are absent from `established/
045`'s table and are new to this corpus. The lunar-festival narrative content (Hanshik's grave-
visiting picnics, Tano-jol's swinging/wrestling/tug-of-war contests, Ch'usok's archery/hunting
and weaving contests) adds cultural-practice detail no prior source captured for these specific
holidays.

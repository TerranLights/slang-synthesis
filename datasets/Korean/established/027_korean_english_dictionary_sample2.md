# Korean — Established Vocabulary: *A Korean-English Dictionary*, Sampled Section 2 of 4 (PDF pp. 241–480)

**Source:** *A Korean-English Dictionary* (source filename `14.A Korean-English dictionary.pdf`),
a large general-purpose bilingual dictionary using Yale-style romanization, entry-internal
part-of-speech/usage codes, and a distinctive "ISOTOPE"/"PARA-INTENSIVE" sound-symbolism notation
for ideophone vowel-gradation pairs (e.g. HEAVY ISOTOPE ↔ light-vowel counterpart). The format —
Yale romanization, `cpd n./vi./vt.`, `DIAL.`, `VULGAR`, `EUPHEMISM`, `FIG.`, `JOCULAR`, `FAMILIAR`,
`ARCHAIC` register tags, and the ISOTOPE apparatus — strongly matches Samuel E. Martin, Yang Ha Lee
and Sung-Un Chang's *A Korean-English Dictionary* (Yale University Press, 1967), but no title page
or copyright page fell within the sampled pages, so the exact edition/year is **not independently
verified from the pages actually read** — flagged rather than stated as fact.

**Assigned range:** PDF pages 241–480 of 962 (the second quarter of a 962-page book). **This is a
deliberate sample, not an exhaustive extraction** — per the coverage rule, a general-purpose
dictionary of this size is mostly repetitive headword-by-headword coverage of ordinary vocabulary,
which is low information density for this project's purposes. Extraction prioritized
register-annotated entries (explicit `DIAL.`/`VULGAR`/`SLANG`/`COLLOQ.`/`EUPHEMISM`/`FIG.`/
`JOCULAR`/`FAMILIAR`/`ARCHAIC` tags), sound-symbolic/ISOTOPE pairs, morphologically-interesting
compounds, and idioms with cultural content, over routine headwords.

**Sampling method and verified page-offset formula.** The book is scanned two printed pages per PDF
page. Nine PDF pages were rendered and read across the assigned range at roughly even ~27-page
intervals (241, 268, 295, 322, 349, 376, 403, 430, 457, 480), and each rendering's printed page
numbers were checked against a hypothesized offset before trusting it further — confirmed exact at
all nine sample points: **`printed_page = 2 × PDF_page − 22`** (e.g. PDF p. 241 → printed pp.
460–461; PDF p. 480 → printed pp. 938–939). This gives a printed-page range of **460–939** for the
full PDF 241–480 span, alphabetically running from headwords starting 또- (tto-) through 서- (se-)
in the book's own (apparently Yale-romanization-ordered, not pure Hangul-order) alphabetization —
the sampled spreads land on 또/뒤 (p. 460, 514), 마치/막 (p. 568), 면/멸 (p. 622), 물 (p. 676), 발
(p. 730), 병 (p. 784), 불 (p. 838), 산 (p. 892), and 서 (p. 938).

**Vision-reading confidence.** All 9 rendered pages (150 dpi) are cleanly typeset print with no scan
artifacts and no handwritten marginalia anywhere — checked explicitly per the vision-reading
marginalia guard. All entries below are `verified` (the two-column dense typeset text rendered
sharply and unambiguously at this resolution; Yale romanization alongside each Hangul headword gave
an independent cross-check for every reading).

**PDF-decode note.** This dictionary was not tested for a decodable text layer during this dispatch
(the assignment specified vision-reading directly); all entries below were read from rendered page
images per the Wave 3 vision-reading plan in `00_Extraction_Checklist.md`.

## Grammar points

### The dictionary's ISOTOPE / PARA-INTENSIVE sound-symbolism apparatus

This source uses a distinctive, consistent notation for Korean's productive ideophone
vowel-gradation system — the same "bright/dark vowel symbolism" phenomenon documented from a prose
angle in `established/008_using_korean_part1.md`'s ch. 13, but here formalized entry-by-entry as a
cross-reference apparatus rather than discussed narratively. Every ideophone/sound-symbolic
adverb or verb is tagged with:

- **`LIGHT ISOTOPE ↔ [heavy form]`** or **`HEAVY ISOTOPE ↔ [light form]`** — a bright/back-vowel
  form (conventionally smaller, lighter, more delicate in feel) paired against a dark/front-vowel
  form (larger, heavier, cruder in feel) sharing the same consonant skeleton. Examples found in this
  sample: 또닥-또닥 (light) ↔ 뚜덕- (heavy) "tapping/pounding"; 뒤뚝- (heavy) ↔ 되똑- (light)
  "tottering"; 불쑥- (light isotope tag on the heavier-feeling form itself, cross-referencing a
  lighter counterpart elsewhere); 빨쪽- (light) ↔ 벌쭉- (heavy) "puckering lips."
- **`PARA-INTENSIVE`** — a third gradation step beyond the basic light/heavy pair, marking an even
  more intensified or emphatic variant (found on 서편(-서문), itself cross-referenced from
  사푼(-사푼), which is marked as a `PARA-INTENSIVE` derivative of 서뿐(-서뿐)). This suggests the
  system is not strictly binary (light vs. heavy) but can chain through at least three related
  forms for a single ideophone family — worth flagging for the mechanics-analysis phase as a
  candidate model for graded-intensity slang/ideophone derivation in the invented conlang.

This is a genuinely rich, systematically-marked resource for later mechanics work on Korean
sound-symbolism: unlike prose grammar references that give a handful of illustrative pairs, this
dictionary tags the phenomenon at the individual-headword level across (per the sample) hundreds of
entries, which means a targeted future pass specifically harvesting ISOTOPE-tagged entries (rather
than a general vocabulary sample) could assemble a much larger, systematic light/heavy/para-intensive
paradigm set than any grammar reference alone provides.

### Explicit register-tag inventory confirmed in this sample

Distinct from the ISOTOPE apparatus, the dictionary separately tags ordinary lexical/register
information inline per entry. Tags directly observed in this sample: `DIAL.` (dialectal — e.g.
도가니's `DIAL. 토가니` cross-reference), `VULGAR` (면-판 "face," 막-동이 sense 2, 막-잡이 sense 2),
`SLANG` (물권 sense 2, "gets" a spouse — sampled in the file's `analysis` cross-reference, see note
below), `EUPHEMISM` (도끼-나물 "meat," 뛰-보다 "has a bowel movement"), `COLLOQ.` (서울-뜨기),
`FIG.` (여러 idiom-marking uses above), and `JOCULAR` (도끼를 베고 잤나). **Not every blunt/coarse
term is consistently tagged** — 산-문 "vulva/vagina" (p. 892) carries no register flag at all despite
comparably blunt terms elsewhere in the same source being marked `[VULGAR]`, which is worth treating
as a real gap in the source's own tagging discipline rather than evidence the term was considered
neutral.

### Compound-formation patterns worth flagging for mechanics analysis

- **도깨비- ("goblin-") as a productive folk-naming prefix**: 도깨비-바늘 ("goblin needle" = a
  bur-producing weed), 도깨비-부채 ("goblin fan" = a broad-leafed plant), 도깨비-불 ("goblin fire" =
  will-o'-the-wisp) all use the folklore creature's name metaphorically/associatively to name a
  natural phenomenon or plant with an uncanny or unexplained visual quality — a live pattern for how
  a culture's folklore vocabulary bleeds into its natural-history naming.
- **-놈 as a derogatory person-noun suffix**: 막-된-놈 ("an ill-bred fellow"), paralleling 막-된 놈's
  register with 불상-놈 ("a very vulgar person"), both built on a crude/wild-behavior root + 놈
  ("guy," itself carrying a derogatory shade when applied to an adult).
- **-뜨기 as a mildly derogatory "person marked by X" suffix**: 서울-뜨기 ("a city slicker," lit.
  "Seoul-person" with a faintly mocking edge) parallels a broader -뜨기 pattern (e.g. 촌뜨기 "country
  bumpkin," not itself sampled here but a well-known cognate form) worth checking for elsewhere in
  the dictionary in a future targeted pass.
- **Occupational/register-specific humble self-reference**: 산승 sense 2 ("I, me," said by a Buddhist
  priest humbling himself) is a data point for register-specific first-person pronoun substitution
  beyond the general 나/저 plain/humble split already documented — professional/religious register
  can supply its own dedicated humble self-referent word entirely, not just a stylistic register
  shift on the ordinary pronoun.

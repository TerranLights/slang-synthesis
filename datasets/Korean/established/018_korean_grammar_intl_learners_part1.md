## Grammar points

### Chapter One — Special Features of Korean Grammar and Sentence Structure (printed pp. 1–18)

**1. Parts of speech (품사).** The book follows the 1963 Committee-of-Education unified school
grammar's nine-part-of-speech system: nouns (명사), pronouns (대명사), numerals (수사), action verbs
(동작동사), descriptive verbs/adjectives (상태동사), adnominals (관형사), adverbs (부사), exclamations
(감탄사), particles (조사). Sub-classified three further ways: (1) content words (실사) vs. function
words (허사, i.e. particles); (2) inflecting words (용언: verbs/descriptive verbs, which conjugate,
e.g. 가다→가고/가니/가서) vs. non-inflecting words (체언: nouns, which do not change shape); (3) by
word function into substantives (체언: nouns/pronouns/numerals, which can fill multiple sentence
roles) vs. modifiers (수식언: adnominals modify nouns, adverbials modify verbs) — exclamations are
the one exception, classed as independent forms (독립언) rather than under either substantive or
modifier.

**2. Special typological features of Korean.** (1) Basic word order is S–O–V, with (a) free word
order among all pre-verbal elements (the verb is fixed sentence-finally, but subject/object/dative
etc. can permute freely), (b) modifiers always precede what they modify, (c) word order does not
change for questions and there is no requirement that question words move to sentence-initial
position (contra English). (2) Korean has a rich inventory of particles and endings carrying
grammatical meaning (case, honorification, etc. — e.g. 할머니가 좋다 "Grandmother is nice" →
할머님께서는 좋으셨겠습니다ᅟwith honorific particle/ending stacking). (3) Korean has a highly developed
honorific/politeness system. (4) Subjects, objects, complements, and even predicates can be freely
omitted when recoverable from context (선생님은 어디에 가십니까? → 어디 가십니까?). (5) Singular/plural
is not an obligatory grammatical category. (6) Korean lacks grammatical gender, articles, and
relative pronouns.

**3. Basic sentence structure.** Five basic sentence types by predicate nature: (I) 무엇이
어찌한다 "something does something" (with or without an object, or "something becomes something");
(II) 무엇이 어떠하다 "something is some way" (descriptive-verb predicate); (III) 무엇이 무엇이다/아니다
"something is/is not something" (copula). **Main constituent elements**: subject (주어), predicate
(서술어— action verb / descriptive verb / copula -이다, the last sometimes analyzed as a special
"predicative particle" 서술격조사), object (목적어, marked by 을/를, typically required by transitive
verbs and appearing before the verb), complement (보어, obligatory with 되다 "become" and 아니다 "not
be"). A subject can be a bare substantive+particle, a nominalized-verb(+기)+particle, or a
nominalized-verb-phrase+particle. The copula can attach to substantives, nominalized verbs, and
nominalized verb phrases; predicate phrases (main verb + auxiliary, e.g. 담배를 피우지 말아요 "don't
smoke") are also possible. **Attributive/modifier constituents**: adnominals (관형어, precede and
modify nouns; cannot stand alone; when stacked, the order is Demonstrative–Numeral–Descriptive
adnoun) and adverbials (부사어, split into constituent adverbs modifying a sub-sentence element, and
sentential adverbs like 과연/역시 modifying the whole sentence; adverbials are compatible with
delimiters and have freer word order than adnominals, except when an adverbial modifies another
adverb/adnominal/noun, or in the case of negation adverbs, where word order is fixed). Verbs in a
modifier form (-는, -(으)ㄴ, -(으)ㄹ, -던 — which also mark tense, see Ch. 13) can themselves function
as adnominals. **Free/independent forms** (독립어): exclamations, answer-words (예, 아니요), headings/
titles, noun+vocative-particle forms, and conjunctive adverbs (그러나/그리고/그런데/그러므로/그래서,
which double-count as either free forms or as conjunctive adverbs).

**4. Sentence structure types.** Simple sentences (단문/홑문장: exactly one subject, one predicate)
vs. complex sentences (복합문/겹문장), the latter split into (a) complex-by-conversion — a simple
sentence converted into a nominalized phrase, modifier clause, adverbial clause, or quotation and
embedded in a matrix sentence, with shared constituents optionally omitted (modifier clause,
nominalized clause, predicate clause, adverbial clause, and quoted-clause subtypes all
illustrated); and (b) complex-by-coordination-or-subordination — two+ clauses joined by coordinate
endings (-고, -(으)며) or subordinate endings (-도록, -게, -(으)니, -(으)면, etc.), again with optional
shared-constituent omission. The book notes coordination vs. subordination is not always cleanly
distinguishable; a rule of thumb is that coordinated clauses can swap position without changing
overall meaning (여름에는 비가 오고 겨울에는 눈이 온다 ↔ 겨울에는 눈이 오고 여름에는 비가 온다), while
subordinated clauses generally cannot. Words joined by the coordinative particle 와/과 in
subject/object/adverbial position can have that particle replaced by a comma (인천과 부산은
항구도시이다 = 인천, 부산은 항구도시이다).

### Chapter Two — Nouns (printed pp. 19–82; range covers §§1–3, the "Bound Nouns" catalogue)

**1. Noun functions (p. 19).** Nouns can (1) serve as subject, (2) be modified, (3) take particles
marking grammatical-case relations (선생님이/나의/책을/친구에게 stacks subject/possessive/object/dative
particles in one sentence), (4) take plural markers (들: 우리들은 "we-PL").

**2. Noun type (p. 20).** Cross-classified by sphere of usage (proper nouns vs. common nouns) and
by freeness/boundedness (free nouns, usable alone, e.g. 하늘/사람/문; vs. bound nouns 의존명사/
"incomplete nouns" 불완전명사/"dependent nouns," which need a preceding adnominal element and can
never stand alone).

**3. Bound nouns (printed pp. 20–82) — the chapter's core content, a systematic catalogue of
Korean's dependent/bound nouns, each forming one or more fixed grammatical patterns when combined
with a preceding modifier form and (usually) a following particle or copula:**

- **것 "thing; fact"** (p. 21) — the most general bound noun, freely combinable with particles, the
  copula, and 같다. Forms include: `Vst+-(으)ㄴ/-는/-(으)ㄹ 것` "the fact that…did/does/will do" (a
  paraphrase of plain -았-/-ㄴ다/-겠다, but the -(으)ㄹ 것이다 variant adds tentativeness, "will
  probably do/be"); `AVst+-(으)ㄹ 것이다` "is the case that…will do/be" as future/probability marker;
  `AVst+-(으)ㄹ 건가요?` (abbreviated from -(으)ㄹ 것인가요?) soliciting the hearer's opinion about a
  future/probable event; `Vst+-(으)ㄹ 걸(요)` with two senses depending on intonation — rising
  intonation = guarded supposition/correction ("but don't you think…?"), falling/statement
  intonation (often closed with 그랬어요) = regret over a missed opportunity ("I should have… (but
  didn't)"); `Vst+-(으)ㄹ 것 없다` "there's no need to (do); no point in doing" (the subject particle
  이 cannot be inserted before 것 in this idiom; the positive collocation -(으)ㄹ 것이 있다 occurs only
  rhetorically). 것 abbreviates colloquially: 것은→건, 것을→걸, 것이→게.
- **바 "that which; the thing that"** (p. 21, in 내가 바라는 바) — a fact/matter bound noun, less
  productive than 것, occurring in fixed set phrases (그것은 내가 바라는 바가 아니에요).
- **채 "state, condition (as it is)"** (p. 21, illustrated in 옷을 입은 채로 "with clothes on") —
  marks an unchanged accompanying state.
- **겸 "and also; in addition; at the same time, combining (two functions)"** (pp. 28–29) — two
  patterns: `Noun 겸 Noun` "Noun-cum-Noun" (이곳은 강당 겸 실내 체육관으로 쓴다 "used as
  auditorium-cum-gymnasium"); `AVst+-(으)ㄹ 겸 (…-(으)ㄹ 겸 해서)` "with the combined/simultaneous
  purpose of …ing," compatible only with action verbs (손자도 볼 겸 딸도 만날 겸 해서 왔습니다 "I came to
  combine seeing my grandson with visiting my daughter").
- **김 "(on the) impetus (of); while one is at/about it; incidental to the occasion of; on the spur
  of"** (pp. 29–30) — `AVst+-(으)ㄴ/-는 김에` "while one is at it/about it" (여기에 온 김에 그 분을 만나고
  가겠어요 "since I'm here, I'll meet him before I go"). Explicitly distinguished from the
  superficially similar `-는 길에` "on the way [coming/going] to…," compatible mainly with 오다/가다
  (한국에 가는 길에 일본에도 들를 계획입니다 "I plan to stop in Japan on the way to Korea").
- **나름 "depending on…"** (pp. 30–31) — `Noun 나름이다` "it depends on the Noun" (사람도 사람 나름이지
  "it depends on the person"); `AVst+-기 나름이다` "it depends on how one does it" (사랑받는 것은 자기가
  하기 나름입니다 "being loved depends on the effort you put in").
- **나위 "need; leeway"** (p. 31) — `AVst+-(으)ㄹ 나위가 없다` "(hardly) worth …ing; no need to" (기쁘기는
  더 말할 나위가 없습니다 "it goes without saying that I'm happy"); the positive collocation -(으)ㄹ
  나위가 있다 occurs only in rhetorical questions.
- **따름 "just, only"** (p. 32) — `Vst+-(으)ㄹ 따름이다` "it is just/only that…," always closed with
  the copula, preceded only by the prospective modifier -(으)ㄹ; near-synonymous with -(으)ㄹ 뿐.
  (자유가 아니면 죽음이 있을 따름입니다 "without freedom, there is only death.")
- **대로 "as, just as; in accordance with; as soon as"** (pp. 32–35) — a highly productive family:
  `Noun 대로` "in accordance with Noun" (약속대로 전 비밀을 지켰습니다 "as promised, I kept the secret");
  `Noun은 Noun 대로` "each…respectively, in accordance with Noun's own nature" (큰 것은 큰 것대로 작은
  것은 작은 것대로 놓으세요 "put the big ones with the big, the small with the small"); `Vst+
  -(으)ㄴ/-는/-(으)ㄹ 대로` "just as did/is doing/will do, in accordance with" (아이들은 어른이 하는 대로
  따라 해요 "children copy what adults do"); the intensive collocation `V-(으)ㄹ 대로 V-어(아,여)서` "as
  much as is possible/as…as can be" (머리가 빠질 대로 빠져서 더 빠질 게 없어요 "my hair has fallen out as
  much as it possibly can"); `AVst+-는 대로` "as soon as, no sooner than" (집에 도착하는 대로 전화 좀
  걸어 주세요 "call me as soon as you arrive home"); `Vst+-(으)ㄹ 수 있는 대로` "as…as possible" (이 일은
  할 수 있는 대로 빨리 끝내 주세요); `AVst+-는다는 대로` "in accordance with the way [somebody]
  said/told/ordered" (a contraction of the quoted structure -는다고 하는 대로; 교수님이 하라시는 대로
  했으니까 결과가 좋을 거예요 "I did as the professor told me, so the results should be good").
- **대신 "instead of…, in place/lieu of; in recompense for"** (pp. 36–37) — `Noun 대신(에)` "instead
  of Noun"; `Vst+-(으)ㄴ/-는 대신(에)` "instead of doing/having done; in recompense for" — the 에 is
  optional and usually dropped, and the verb can appear negated with no change in overall meaning
  (회사에 출근을 안 하는 대신 집에서 일을 했습니다 "instead of going to the office, I worked at home"; 그
  병원은 시설이 좋은 대신 치료비가 비쌉니다 "that hospital has good facilities, but [in exchange]
  medical fees are steep"). Cites the proverb 꿩 대신 닭 "a chicken instead of a pheasant" (settling
  for next best — a Korean idiom-equivalent of "half a loaf").
- **동안 "an interval, while"** (p. 37) — `AVst+-는 동안(에)` "while (doing)" (한국에서 사는 동안 많은
  것을 배웠습니다 "I learned a lot while living in Korea").
- **둥 (paired-alternation bound noun)** (p. 37, continuing past this dispatch's excerpted page) —
  `Vst+-는/(으)ㄹ 둥` "you can tell whether; may or may not," expressing vague/contradictory alternation
  (typically the second disjunct being some form of 말다 "desist"), closed with 하다/그렇다.

chapter's closing §3.2 "Free Forms which also function as Bound Nouns/Post-Modifiers" — falls

than silently treated as covered.)*

### Chapter Three — Pronouns (printed pp. 83–88)

Korean personal pronouns are stratified by honorific level exactly like nouns generally: Honorific
(높임말)/Plain (예사말)/Deferential (낮춤말) for 1st and 2nd person, plus Honorific/Plain/Deferential/
Interrogative-Indefinite (미지칭)/Indeterminate (부정칭) for 3rd person — see the full paradigm table
above. Notably, **1st person has no Honorific form** and **2nd person has no Deferential form** —
structural gaps the source states outright rather than filling in (one cannot honorifically refer
to oneself, nor deferentially/humbly address a 2nd-person interlocutor to their face). The
Honorific 2nd-person slot is filled by five near-synonymous terms (선생/어른/자네/그대/어르신) rather
than a single dedicated pronoun — Korean strongly prefers titles/kin-terms/names over bare 2nd-person
pronouns in addressing social superiors, a point the chapter flags but develops more fully
elsewhere (cross-referenced to p. 210, this dispatch's Ch. 11 lexical-honorification material,
above). Demonstrative pronouns (§2, not detailed further in this excerpt) follow the
proximal/medial/distal three-way split standard across Korean's whole demonstrative system
(이/그/저-).

### Chapter Four — Numerals (printed pp. 89–96)

Two parallel numeral systems, as documented identically in this project's other Korean sources:
pure-Korean numerals (한글 수사, used for counting up to 99, beyond which only Sino-Korean forms are
used) and Sino-Korean numerals (한자어 수사, used throughout, especially for money, dates, and large
quantities). Pure-Korean cardinal numerals 1–4 and 20 (하나/둘/셋/넷/스물) take a distinct **modifier
form** when directly preceding a classifier/counter (한/두/세/네/스무 — 빵 하나를 주세요 "give me a
loaf of bread" [bare numeral] vs. 빵 한 개를 주세요 [numeral-as-modifier + classifier], both glossed
identically in English but structurally different in Korean). Two or more numerals can combine to
form an **approximate numeral** with its own altered forms (한두 "one or two," 두세 "two or three,"
서너 "three or four," 너댓 "four or five," 대여섯 "five or six"). Ordinal numerals (서수사, not detailed
further in this excerpt) also split into pure-Korean and Sino-Korean subtypes.

### Chapter Five — Verbs (printed pp. 97–114)

Confirms the standard action-verb (동작동사) vs. descriptive-verb/adjective (상태동사) split, the
copula -이다, and 있다 as a special case (existential/possessive verb). The chapter's main bulk is a
systematic catalogue of **irregular verb conjugation classes**, matching the "seven irregular
predicate classes" already documented from `established/002`'s treatment of *Basic Korean*, with
this source's own example sets: **ㅂ-irregular** (drop ㅂ before a vowel-initial ending, add 오/우 —
usually 우, but 돕다 "help" and 곱다 "be beautiful" take 오 uniquely before -어/아/여 endings, e.g.
반갑다→반가워요/반가우면, but 돕다→도와요; a closed list of regular-looking-but-actually-regular ㅂ-final
verbs is given as a contrast set: 입다/잡다/씹다/넓다/업다/뽑다/접다/좁다/집다/붙잡다, which do NOT drop
ㅂ); **ㄷ-irregular** (ㄷ→ㄹ before a vowel-initial ending: 걷다→걸어요/걸으면 "walk," 묻다→물어요 "ask,"
듣다→들어요 "hear," 싣다→실어요 "load," 긷다→길어요 "draw water," 깨닫다→깨달아요 "realize"). (The
remaining irregular classes — ㅅ/ㅎ/르/ㄹ/으-irregular — and the chapter's closing material on
action-vs.-descriptive-verb syntactic diagnostics fall in the unexcerpted middle of this chapter's
page range and are not individually re-verified here, since they are already documented from
sibling sources.)

### Chapter Six — Adnominals (printed pp. 115–118)

Three closed/near-closed classes (see the vocabulary table above): demonstrative, numerical, and
descriptive adnominals. All are true adnominals — bound forms that can never stand alone and must
always directly precede the noun they modify (contrast bound *nouns* from Ch. 2, which need a
preceding modifier rather than needing to precede one).

### Chapter Seven — Adverbials (printed pp. 119–140)

Adverbials modify not only verbs but also adnominals, numerals, pronouns, other adverbs, and whole
sentences. Two classification schemes given: **(1) by meaning** — time adverbs, frequency adverbs,
general adverbs, degree adverbs, interrogative~indefinite and negative adverbs, conjunctive
adverbs, mimetic adverbs (의성어/의태어-type sound-symbolic adverbs, a productive and
distinctively Korean word class); **(2) by word-formation method** — non-derived (root) adverbs vs.
derived adverbs (formed via inflection/derivation from verb or adjective stems, e.g. via -게), plus
adverbial forms of verbs created through inflection. The chapter also covers adverbial position
rules (generally freer than adnominal position, per Ch. 1's typological overview, with the
exceptions already noted there: an adverbial modifying another adverb/adnominal/noun, and negation
adverbs, both have fixed position).

### Chapter Eight — Exclamations (printed pp. 141–142)

A non-inflecting, particle-incompatible word class with relatively free sentence position,
classed among the "free forms." Three functional subtypes, each illustrated with a batch of
distinct exclamation-words: **(1) instinctive surprise/emotion** — 어머나 "oh my," 뭐 "what?," 오
"oh" (+ 하나님 "God," i.e. as an invocation), 아니 "wait/no," 아이 "jeez," 와 "wow," 야 "say!," 흥 "hmmph"
(a dismissive/contemptuous interjection), 글쎄 "hmmm/well" (uncertainty); **(2) calling/replying to
others** — 여러분 "everyone," 여보게 "say there" (calling an equal/junior), 야 "hey!" (used toward a
junior/equal); **(3) empty filler/hedge words** — 어 "uh," 저 "um," 애 "hey," 음 "umm." This inventory
is a genuinely useful register-differentiated exclamation set: 흥 and 글쎄 in particular carry
distinct pragmatic color (contempt/dismissal; hedging uncertainty) beyond bare surprise, and 여보게/
야 are explicitly tied to relative social position of speaker and addressee (여보게 toward an equal,
야 toward a junior) rather than being interchangeable "hey"-equivalents.

### Chapter Nine — Particles (printed pp. 143–188)

**Three-way functional classification** (Figure 1, p. 143): **Case particles** (격조사) — subject
(이/가, 께서 [honorific-subject], 에서 [institutional/corporate subject]), object (을/를), complement
(이/가), adnominal (의), adverbial (에, 에게, 한테, 께, 에서, 에게서, 한테서, (으)로), vocative
(아/야/(이)여). **Connective particles** (접속조사) — 와/과, 하고, 에(다), (이)며, (이)랑. **Auxiliary
particles** (보조사) — 은/는, 도, 만/뿐, 까지/마저/조차, 부터, 마다, (이)야, (이)나/(이)나마…

Selected case-particle findings: the honorific subject particle **께서** co-occurs with the
honorific verb suffix -(으)시 as an agreement marker (아버님께서 지금 말씀중이십니다); **에서** as
subject particle marks corporate/institutional subjects specifically (우리 학교에서 우승을 했다 "our
school won," 정부에서 뭐라고 말했습니까 "what did the government say" — contrast 에서's more familiar
locative use); when two+ nouns take the subject particle in one sentence, the first is the main
clause's subject and later ones are the subject of an embedded predicate clause (철수가 마음이
좋습니다 "Cholsu has [lit. Cholsu, heart is good]"); 나/저/너/누구 fuse irregularly with the subject
particle to 내가/제가/네가/누가; the object particle 을/를 can stack onto other particles or the long
negative for emphasis, with 를→ㄹ abbreviation (집에를 가세요? "are you going home?"); both subject
and object particles are freely omissible, especially in colloquial speech.

Selected auxiliary-particle findings (this chapter's highest register-relevant content):
**-(이)나** is a single form spanning at least four distinct senses — "or" linking two nouns
(커피나 홍차 "coffee or tea"), "or something/whatever, of no particular interest" when
noun+나+verb (장기나 둡시다 "let's play changgi or something" — mild indifference/dismissiveness
marker), approximation with numerals in questions (몇 명이나 오세요? "about how many are coming?"),
and "as much/many as, to a surprising extent" with numerals (열 시간이나 잤어요 "I slept as much as
10 hours!" — surprise/emphasis marker). **-(이)ㄹ랑** (예: 남의 것일랑, 걱정일랑, 오락실엘랑, 길에설랑) is a
topic/contrast-marking colloquial particle cluster, illustrated exclusively in imperative/
prohibitive registers ("mind your own business," "don't worry," "don't go to arcades," "don't get
distracted") — reads as a scolding/parental-register auxiliary particle, though the source doesn't
name the register explicitly. **-마다** "each and every, without exception" optionally intensifies
with the prefix 매- (매일마다, 매주일마다) for emphatic "every single." **-마저** "even, on top of
everything (else)" marks an extreme/limiting case, frequently in loss/decline narration (아들마저
잃었어요 "[she] lost even her son," 집마저 팔았답니다 "[he] even sold the house" — a semantically
bleak/pathos-marking particle, illustrated entirely with loss-of-possessions/relationships
examples). **-만** "only, just" replaces the subject/object particle when attaching to
subjects/objects. **-(이)야말로** "indeed, without question, truly" is an emphatic-identification
particle used to assert something is the paradigm case of its category (오늘이야말로 "today, if
ever, is..."; 그 사람이야말로 "she, if anyone, is..."). **-(이)야** also combines with -고 (-고야, -고야
말다) to intensify finality/inevitability ("insists on finishing," "will get [money] out of him no
matter what"), and with the copula (-이라야) for an emphatic-necessary-condition "only if it be/
unless it be" (부자라야 그런 집을 살 수 있을 거예요 "you'd have to be rich to buy a house like that");
the related -이/가 아니고야/아니고서야 pattern means "unless X, how could Y" (rhetorical-question
necessity, 천재가 아니고야 어찌 그걸 다 외울 수 있을까? "how could you memorize all that unless you're a
genius?").

### Chapter Ten — Tense (printed pp. 189–198)

Korean marks tense via (1) final verb endings (the chapter's main content — a paradigm table
cross-tabulating four final-ending styles [dictionary form -(ㄴ/는)다, formal -(ㅂ/습)니다, polite
-어(아,여)요, and the negative -지 않아요] against present/past/future/progressive tense, matching the
paradigm approach already documented from sibling Korean sources), (2) adnominal/modifier endings
(cross-referenced forward to Ch. 13, out of this dispatch's range), and (3) time adverbs. The
source explicitly flags that many Korean "tense" markers actually encode aspect rather than strict
time-reference, cautioning against a purely time-based reading of the paradigm.

### Chapter Eleven — Honorifics and Humilifics (printed pp. 199–212) — the range's central
register content

Three-way morphological typology of Korean honorification: **(1) Subject honorification** —
attaching the suffix -(으)시 to the verb stem to honorify the sentence's grammatical subject
(가다→가시→가십니다; a table of six illustrative stems is given: 가다, 보다, 읽다, 좋다, 예쁘다, 이다,
있다→ 보시/읽으시/좋으시/예쁘시/이시/있으시, all taking -십니다 after a vowel-final stem or -으십니다 after
a consonant-final stem). Subject honorification is governed by relative-status arithmetic among
speaker/subject/listener, not merely by the subject's own status in isolation: **(a)** if the
listener outranks the sentence subject even though the subject outranks the speaker (listener →
subject → speaker), -(으)시 is *not* used (할아버지, 아버지가 지금 왔습니다 — a child telling
Grandfather that Father "came," dropping the honorific even though Father would normally merit it,
because Grandfather outranks him); **(b)** conversely, if the honorified target is related to and
outranks the listener even when the speaker also outranks the sentence subject (speaker → subject →
listener), -(으)시 *is* used (영수야, 너의 아버지 회사에 나가셨니? — an adult using -(으)시 for a child's
father when speaking to the child, because the father outranks the child-listener); **(c)** news
reporting and textbook-style formal exposition drop -(으)시 even for high-status subjects (대통령이
미국을 방문한다 "the President visits the US," no honorific) — a genre-based register override; **(d)**
an **indirect-honorification** extension lets -(으)시 attach when the sentence's grammatical subject
is a body part or possession belonging to the honored person rather than the person themselves
(선생님께서는 감기가 드셨습니다 "the teacher has caught a cold," lit. "a cold has honorific-entered";
김 선생님께서는 연세가 많으십니다 "Mr. Kim is elderly," lit. "years are honorific-many").

**(2) Speech Levels (상대 높임법)** — the addressee-honorification system realized through sentence
final-ending choice, governed by listener social position/age/family relation/intimacy. Two broad
categories, each with two sub-styles: **Formal Speech Level (격식체)** = Formal-High (존대형,
e.g. declarative -ㅂ/습니다, interrogative -ㅂ/습니까, imperative -(으)십시오, propositive -(으)십시다)
+ Formal-Neutral (중립형) + Formal-Low (하대형/반말); **Informal Speech Level (비격식체)** =
Informal-High (존대형, e.g. -어(아,여)요/-지요) + Informal-Low (하대형, e.g. bare -어(아,여)/-지). A full
five-sentence-type × speech-level cross-tabulation (declarative/interrogative/imperative/
propositive/exclamatory) is given as Figure 15 — the source's fullest single paradigm table in
this dispatch's range. **Register-functional notes, explicitly stated by the source:** the
Formal-High style is "direct and objective," suited to formal/ceremonial contexts or
not-particularly-close/awkward relationships, and is described as simultaneously expressing
respect for the listener *and* modesty/reserve on the speaker's own part — used in solemn/serious
formal conversation and, notably, in first-meetings-between-strangers regardless of relative
status (a social-safety-default register, paralleling this project's other Korean sources'
findings about 존댓말 as a stranger-default). The Formal-Neutral style is used mainly by
elderly-to-elderly speakers of relatively equal senior status/age, expressing mutual respect
without any status asymmetry connotation — explicitly glossed as "mostly used by elderly people of
the same generation" (여보게, 난 내일 미국으로 떠나네 is the given example, an elderly-register
address term + ending). **Everyday mixing note:** the source explicitly states it is normal in
casual conversation to mix Formal-Level forms with the Informal-High -어(아,여)요 form, but that
best practice reserves exclusive Formal-High usage for official/protocol-sensitive occasions.
**Vowel harmony (모음조화)** governs the -어(아,여)요 ending's allomorph selection when attaching to a
verb stem (Figure 16): 아-stems take 아(요) (가다→가요, identical-vowel deletion); 오-stems take
와(요) (보다→봐요, contraction); 어/우/으/이-stems take 어(요)/워(요)/어(요)/여(요) respectively
(서다→서요, 배우다→배워요, 쓰다→써요 [으-deletion], 가르치다→가르쳐요); 하-stems take 여(요), realized as
해(요) (일하다→일해요). When a consonant intervenes between stem-final and ending vowels, contraction/
deletion is blocked and the vowels simply concatenate (잡다→잡아요, 좁다→좁아요, 먹다→먹어요, 죽다→죽어요,
늙다→늙어요, 싫다→싫어요).

**(3) Honorification by lexical expressions (어휘에 의한 높낮이의 표현)** — a closed set of inherently
honorific/deferential-humble word-forms with no productive derivation from their plain counterparts
(Figure 17, fully tabulated above in the Vocabulary section: 말/말씀, 나이/연세, 밥/진지, 병/병환,
집/댁, 그 사람/그 분, 나·우리/저·저희, 주다/드리다, 묻다·말하다/여쭈다·여쭙다, plus the suppletive verb set
보다/뵙다, 자다/주무시다, 먹다/잡수시다, 있다/계시다, 죽다/돌아가시다, 데리다/모시다, and the honorific
particle pair -이/가·에게 vs. -께서·께, plus the title suffix -님). The source stresses these must be
memorized as a closed lexical set rather than derived by rule — unlike productive -(으)시
subject-honorification, lexical honorification has no general morphological process and each
plain~honorific/deferential pair is independently listed.

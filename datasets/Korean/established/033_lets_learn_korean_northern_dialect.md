## Flagship Findings Summary (cross-chapter synthesis)

These patterns recur independently across most or all of the sixteen sections below — rather than
restate the full evidence at the top, this section names each pattern once and points to where the
richest worked examples live; every individual occurrence is still documented in its own section's
Vocabulary table.

1. **두음법칙 (South Korea's "initial-sound law") is systematically NOT applied in this book.**
   South Korean standard orthography/pronunciation alters or drops a word-initial ㄴ before
   ㅣ/y-glide vowels (녀뇨뉴니 → 여요유이) and a word-initial ㄹ before any vowel (라래량력례로료류리
   → 나내낭냐녀노뇨뉴니 / 아애양역예오요유이). **North Korean standard preserves the historical
   initial consonant instead, with no exceptions found anywhere in this book.** This is attested
   from the very first consonant-name table in the Pronunciation chapter (§ "Flagship dialectal
   finding: North Korean consonant-letter names" below) through to the last page of the
   English-Korean dictionary, and is by far the single most pervasive, most systematically
   attestable finding in the entire source — well over 100 distinct lexical instances are
   individually documented across this file's sections. The clearest everyday examples: 녀자/녀성
   "woman" (SK 여자/여성), 로동자/로동당 "worker/Workers' Party" (SK 노동자/노동당), 래일
   "tomorrow" (SK 내일), 력사 "history" (SK 역사), 려관 "hotel" (SK 여관), 륙 "six" (SK 육).
2. **동무 ("comrade") is grammaticalized as a true second-person pronoun**, not just a vocabulary
   item — explicitly taught in Grammar Lesson 2 (§38) as usable in place of "you" when addressing
   an equal or inferior, structurally parallel to 당신 for a superior. It recurs as the book's
   default address term across dozens of worked examples in nearly every later lesson (김동무
   "Comrade Kim," 박동무 "Comrade Pak," 동무들 "Comrades," etc.) and as its own dictionary headword.
   South Korean usage retains 동무 only as a dated/dialectal word for "friend," avoiding it as a
   term of address because of its strong North Korean/socialist association.
3. **조선 (not 한국) is the book's own consistent self-designation for "Korea"/"Korean"** —
   matching the book's own Korean title 조선말 배우는 책 ("Chosŏnmal" = "Chosŏn-speech") — appearing
   from the Pronunciation chapter's own example words through the dictionary's own headword 조선말
   "the Korean language" (contrasted with South Korean 한국/한국어).
4. **DPRK state-ideological and institutional vocabulary is embedded directly into ordinary
   grammar-pedagogy example sentences and dictionary headwords, not confined to a specialized
   register.** Confirmed dozens of times across nearly every grammar lesson and both dictionary
   halves: 주체/주체사상/주체사상탑 (Juche / the Juche Idea / Tower of Juche Idea), 조선로동당
   (Workers' Party of Korea), 조선인민군 (Korean People's Army), 인민/인민대중/인민경제 ("the
   people" in its DPRK collectivist-political sense), 혁명/혁명가/혁명적 (revolution), 만경대/
   백두산 (Mangyongdae/Mt. Paekdu, DPRK revolutionary-mythology sites), 원쑤/철천지원쑤 ("the
   enemy"/"sworn enemy"), 미제 ("US imperialism"), 남조선 (DPRK's own term for "South Korea"),
   explicit reunification/anti-nuclear/anti-imperialist slogans used as suggestive/imperative-mood
   grammar drills, and the DPRK's full official state name 조선민주주의인민공화국 appearing as both
   a grammar-example sentence subject and a standalone dictionary headword.
5. **A handful of genuinely lexical (non-phonological) North/South divergences** were also found,
   distinct from the 두음법칙 pattern: 남새 "vegetable" (SK 채소/야채), 동무 in its plain "friend"
   sense (see #2), 소젖 "milk" (SK 우유), 닭알 "egg" (SK 계란/달걀), 얼음보숭이 "ice cream" (SK
   아이스크림), 안해 "wife" (SK 아내/부인), 구라파 "Europe" (SK 유럽), 벗 "friend" (literary/archaic
   in SK but plain register here), and this book's own regularized consonant-letter names (ㄷ=
   "tiut" not SK's "tigŭt," etc. — see the Pronunciation section).

## 1. Pronunciation

**Source citation for this section:** printed pp. 9–31 (PDF pp. 10–32).

**Coverage note:** every phoneme/letter rule and example word in this section was captured; nothing
was skipped as this section is short and foundational. No handwritten marginalia was found on any
page in this range — all content is clean printed type.

### Flagship dialectal finding: North Korean consonant-letter names

**South Korea's seven "irregular" Hangul consonant names (ㄷㅅㅈㅊㅌㅍㅎ) use historically
irregular forms — 디귿(tigŭt), 시옷(siot), 지읒(chiŭt), 치읓(chiŭt), 티읕(tiŭt), 피읖(piŭp),
히읗(hiŭt) — while every other consonant follows the regular "letter + 으 + letter" pattern. North
Korea abolished this irregularity decades ago and regularized ALL 19 consonant names to the same
pattern.** This book's own "ALPHABET" table (printed p. 12) gives the DPRK-standard regularized
names explicitly, transcribed into English:

| Letter | This book's (DPRK) name | South Korean standard name | Divergence |
|---|---|---|---|
| ㄱ | kiuk | 기역 (kiyŏk) | different (SK irregular) |
| ㄴ | niun | 니은 (niŭn) | same pattern |
| ㄷ | **tiut** | 디귿 (**tigŭt**) | DPRK regularized |
| ㄹ | riul | 리을 (riŭl) | same pattern |
| ㅁ | mium | 미음 (miŭm) | same pattern |
| ㅂ | piup | 비읍 (piŭp) | same pattern |
| ㅅ | **siut** | 시옷 (**siot**) | DPRK regularized |
| ㅇ | iung | 이응 (iŭng) | same pattern |
| ㅈ | **chiut** | 지읒 (**chiŭt**) | DPRK regularized |
| ㅊ | **chiut** (listed twice, same transcription as ㅈ in this book's table) | 치읓 (chiŭt) | DPRK regularized |
| ㅎ | **hiuh** | 히읗 (**hiŭt**) | DPRK regularized |

This is a textbook-level, explicit, systematic North/South divergence — the book is teaching
foreigners the DPRK's own post-1954 regularized letter-naming convention. Printed p. 18 independently
confirms the ㄷ name again mid-lesson: "디읃 [tiut] (the consonant ㄷ)".

### Other dialectal/self-designation notes

- **조선 [choson] "Korea"** is used repeatedly as the book's own pronunciation-example word for Korea
  (printed pp. 19 and 20/21, used as the illustrative example for both ㅈ and ㄴ) — never 한국
  (Hanguk, the South Korean name). This is the book's consistent self-designation throughout, matching
  the title "조선말 배우는 책" itself (조선말 = "Chosŏnmal", not South Korean 한국어/한국말).
- No explicit source note comparing this phonology to "standard"/"southern" Korean was found in this
  section — the divergence (letter names) is presented as simple fact, not flagged as regional by the
  book itself. This is itself notable: DPRK materials typically present their own standard
  (문화어, munhwaŏ) as *the* norm rather than as a variant.
- 라지오 [rajio] "radio" (printed p. 21) is used as the example for word-initial ㄹ → [r]; this is a
  loanword and loanwords keep initial ㄹ in both Koreas, so this is **not** treated as a dialectal
  marker (unlike native/Sino-Korean 두음법칙 vocabulary, which appears heavily in the dictionary
  sections of this book).
- No DPRK-specific institutional/political vocabulary appeared as a pronunciation example in this
  section (plain core vocabulary only: child, tree, water, mother, man, Korea, etc.).

### Grammar points

**The Korean alphabet table (가가표) and 40 letters.** The book opens (p. 10) with the vowel×
consonant syllable table ("가가표") for the 10 basic vowels × 9 plain consonants + ㅇ, then (p. 11)
extends it to the aspirated (ㅈㅊㅋㅌㅍㅎ) and tense/doubled (ㄲㄸㅃㅆㅉ) consonant rows. P. 12
states the alphabet has 40 letters total (19 consonants + 21 vowels) and gives the letter-name
table discussed in the flagship finding above.

**Vowel pronunciation (14 simple + compound vowels).** Pp. 13–15 walk through each vowel symbol
with an IPA-ish gloss, an English-sound analogy, and a worked example word — ㅜ=[u], ㅡ=[ɯ]
("almost a groaning sound in the chest," between [u] and [i]), ㅣ=[i], ㅐ=[æ], ㅔ[e], ㅚ=[we]≈German
ö, ㅟ=[wi], ㅑ=[ja], ㅕ=[jə], ㅛ=[jo], ㅠ=[ju], ㅒ=[jɛ], ㅖ=[je], ㅢ=[ɰi] (diphthong), ㅘ=[wa],
ㅝ=[wə], ㅙ=[wɛ], ㅞ=[we].

**Consonant pronunciation (19 consonants incl. doubled/tense series).** Pp. 16–22, consonant by
consonant, each with: base IPA value, a foreign-language analogy, and 1–4 positional allophone
rules. **ㄹ is explicitly described as doing double duty for both [r] and [l]** depending on
position (word-initial/before vowel → [r]; word-final/before consonant → [l]), with ㄴ+ㄹ sequences
always surfacing as double [ll] (e.g. 천리 → [cholli]).

**Syllable structure (5 forms).** P. 22–24: a syllable = one or more consonants + one vowel. Five
structural types are enumerated.

**The Change of Sounds — 6 categories.** P. 24 onward: (1) assimilation of sound (sonant
assimilation, ㄹ-assimilation, palatal assimilation); (2) shut (unreleased) consonants; (3)
strengthening of a sound (tensing); (4) insertion of a sound (epenthetic ㄴ/ㄷ); (5) omission of a
sound (stem-final ㅎ drops before a vowel; coda-cluster simplification); (6) contraction of sounds
(consonant + ㅎ contraction; vowel + vowel contraction).

**Sentence intonation (4 types).** P. 31: falling tone marks a declarative sentence; rising tone
marks an interrogative sentence (but the interrogative word itself takes stress, not the
sentence-final rise, in a wh-question); even tone marks a suggestive sentence; falling tone marks
an imperative.

### Grammar points

**Even tone to the end of an exclamatory sentence.** P.32: sentence intonation stays level/even
through an exclamatory sentence, illustrated with 오, 조국이여! "Oh, Fatherland!"

**Humble vs. plain "I" pronoun choice.** 저는 (humble/polite) is consistently used in formal
self-introduction and service-context speech, while 나는 (plain) is used in more neutral
declarative statements — illustrated by example only in this section, not formally explained (the
formal rule comes in Lesson 2, see §38 below).

### Grammar points

**Cardinal numerals: two parallel systems.** Korean has pure-Korean cardinals (1–99 only) and
Sino-Korean cardinals (unbounded, used for formal counting, dates, money, phone numbers). The
Sino-Korean set is exactly where the 두음법칙 divergence surfaces (륙 vs SK 육).

**Ordinal numerals.** Pure-Korean ordinals attach 번째/째 to the cardinal stem; Sino-Korean ordinals
prefix 제- to the cardinal.

**Telling time.** Hour counts use the pure-Korean cardinal + 시 ("o'clock"); minute counts use the
Sino-Korean cardinal + 분 ("minute"). "Past"/"to" the hour is expressed with 지났습니다 ("has
passed") or 전입니다 ("is before") rather than a dedicated preposition.

### Grammar points

**§14–16 — The Noun: no gender, no articles, plural formation.** No grammatical gender, no
definite/indefinite article. Plural formed by suffixing 들 [dul] to the singular.

**§17–24 — Declension of the singular noun: the full 8-case-ending paradigm.** Nominative 께서
(honorific) / 가 (vowel-final) / 이 (consonant-final). Accusative 를 (vowel-final) / 을
(consonant-final). Genitive 의 uniformly, always preceding the noun it modifies. Dative 께
(honorific) / 에게 (animate) / 에 (inanimate). Locative 에게서 (animate) / 에서 (inanimate).
Instrumental 로 uniformly, with link-vowel 으 inserted before consonant-final stems other than ㄹ
(e.g. 동무로, 손님으로, 삽으로, 우편으로, 감기로, 집으로). Coordinative 와 (vowel-final) / 과
(consonant-final). Vocative 이시여 (honorific) / 여 or 이여 (literary) / 야 or 아 (plain/low) —
inapplicable to inanimate/non-address nouns.

**§28–31 — Declension of the plural noun.** Identical to a consonant-final singular noun's pattern
once 들 is attached (이, 을, 의, 에게, 에게서, 로, 과, 이여/아); 가 and 를 allomorphs drop out
since all plural stems end in the consonant ㄹ of 들.

**§33–36 — Word order (Lesson 1).** The predicate always lies at the sentence's end; the subject
usually opens the sentence; the genitive always immediately precedes the noun it modifies
(illustrated with 녀성들의 권리 "the right(s) of women" — a notable combination of a dialectal-form
headword with rights-oriented phrasing); the object sits between subject and predicate (SOV).

**§37 — Lesson 2, The Personal Pronoun: the courtesy/number grid.** Korean personal pronouns vary
along number (sg/pl) and courtesy (superior vs. equal/inferior) simultaneously:

| | To a superior | To an equal/inferior |
|---|---|---|
| I (sg.) | 저 | 나 |
| we (pl.) | 저희 | 우리 |
| you (sg.) | *(no distinct form)* | 너 |
| you (pl.) | *(no distinct form)* | 너희 |

**§38 — Nouns re-purposed as personal pronouns: 당신 and 동무.** 당신 "you" (polite) — a noun
usable as a 2nd-person pronoun when addressing a superior. **동무 "comrade"** — a noun usable as a
2nd-person pronoun when addressing an equal or inferior (동무는 간다 "You go"). **Flagship DPRK
political-vocabulary find**: teaching 동무 as a productive second-person pronoun embeds the DPRK's
egalitarian/socialist address norms directly into basic grammar instruction, presented without
comment as a normal grammatical option alongside 너.

**§39 — Third-person pronouns built on the demonstrative 그.** Korean has no dedicated 3rd-person
pronoun root; all 3rd-person reference combines 그 "that" with a noun: 그 "he"; 그녀자 "she" (그 +
녀자, NK dialectal form; SK 그여자); 그것 "it" (그 + 것, an "incomplete noun"); 그들/그녀자들/그것들
"they."

**§40–53 — Declension of the personal pronoun.** Follows exactly the noun patterns already
established. Irregularity: 저/나 use suppletive nominative-only forms — 제 replaces 저, 내 replaces
나 — with the usual nominative slot instead taking the auxiliary topic ending 는. 너 → 네 in the
nominative follows the same pattern without being named as a rule.

**§54 — The genitive of the personal pronoun = the English possessive.** 나의 아버지 "my father,"
나의 어머니 "my mother," 나의 집 "my house," 나의 동무들 "my friends."

**§55 — Summary table of personal-pronoun case endings, singular vs. plural.** Directly parallels
§32's noun table.

**§56 — Word order (Lesson 2).** The genitive of the personal pronoun always precedes its referent
— 나의 어머니 "my mother," 우리의 어머니 "our mother."

### Grammar points

**§57: Three-way demonstrative-pronoun system by distance.** 이 (near speaker), 그 (near
addressee), 저 (distant from both) — a genuinely three-term spatial deictic system (cf. Japanese
こ/そ/あ), not the English two-term this/that system.

**§58–59: Object vs. place demonstratives, tabulated.**

**§60–65: Declension of the demonstrative pronoun.** The object-indicating demonstrative (이/그/저)
is always adjectival and never declined; but 그 used substantively means "he" and declines like an
animate vowel-final noun. Its plural 그들 declines like an animate consonant-final noun. Place
demonstratives decline like an inanimate vowel-final noun, singular only, with a simpler ending set
(dative bare 에 rather than 에게, locative 에서 rather than 에게서).

**§66–68: Word order.** The adjectivally-used demonstrative always precedes the noun it modifies;
more generally any attribute always precedes what it modifies (자는 아이 "sleeping child"); the
adverbial modifier usually lies after the subject/object of its clause.

**§69: The verb's infinitive form.** stem + 다.

**§70: The four forms of the verb.** Final (closes a sentence), connecting (links clauses),
attributive (precedes a noun), adverbial-modifier (modifies a verb/adjective) — each produced by
swapping the infinitive's 다 for a different ending on the same stem.

**§71: The verb's final form is invariant for person/number, present tense only.** 간다 is
unchanged across all persons/numbers; tense is fixed present; politeness is a wholly separate axis.

**§72–73: Four moods of the verb's final ending.** Declarative (간다), interrogative (갑니까?),
suggestive (가자!), imperative (가라!).

**§74: Three degrees of courtesy in the verb's final ending.** Most deferential (갑니다), middle
(가오), low (간다) — a genuine three-tier addressee-honorific system layered on top of mood (4
moods × 3 courtesy degrees = up to 12 final-ending slots).

**§76–78: The adjective parallels the verb's infinitive/forms/person-invariance exactly.**

**§79–82: The adjective's final ending expresses only TWO moods, not four.** Declarative and
interrogative only — no suggestive/imperative, since a stative property can't be volitionally
proposed or commanded ("let's be blue!" / "be glad!" have no grammatical slot). This same two-mood
ceiling recurs identically for the copula.

**§83–84: The "verbal form" of the noun/pronoun/numeral (the copula).** Korean has no separate
copula word — any nominal takes final/connecting/attributive endings by first attaching an
"exchanging ending" 이 as a base (평양+이+ㅂ니다 → 평양입니다).

**§85–89: The copula's final form/ending mirrors the adjective exactly** — invariant for
person/number, fixed present tense, only two moods (declarative/interrogative).

**§94: Declarative final endings of the verbal form of the noun/pronoun/numeral.** Most deferential

expected ending 에 conventionally dropped — "the absolute case").

**§101: Declension of the interrogative pronoun.** Declines exactly like ordinary nouns.

**§102: Interrogative final endings of the verb.** Most deferential 습니까/ㅂ니까; middle 나 —
glossed as used "when a man asks something with friendly feeling"; low 느냐 (동무야, 알겠느냐!
"Comrade! Do you know!" — 겠 here carries surmise, not literal futurity).

**§104: Interrogative final endings of the adjective.** Most deferential 습니까/ㅂ니까; middle ㄴ가
(distinct from the verb's 나); low splits by tense — 나 (no tense ending) / 느냐 (with a tense
ending, surmise sense).

**§106: Interrogative final endings of the verbal form of noun/pronoun/numeral.** Parallels §104's
tense-dependent low-courtesy split (나/느냐); middle ㄴ가.

**§108–110: Word order.** Mirrors §96–98 for interrogative predicates.

**§111: Position of the interrogative pronoun in the sentence.** Four-way rule: as subject →
sentence-initial; its own verbal-form ending → still predicate-final; as object → between subject
and predicate; as an attribute → immediately before the noun it modifies.

**§112: The form of address.** Vocative opens the sentence — 박동무, 나는 정거장에 가오 "Comrade
Pak, I am going to the station."

**§113: The interjection.** Opens the sentence — 아, 나의 고향이여! "Ah, my native place!"

### Grammar points

**§114–116: The double numeral system (pure Korean vs. Sino-Korean).** Native cardinals used to
~99 (substantival/adjectival forms differ: 하나/한, 둘/두); Sino-Korean cardinals unbounded, used
for formal counting/dates/money/high values. The book explicitly recommends the pure-Korean series
"as much as possible" for everyday counting. Ordinals: …제/…째 suffixed to a pure-Korean cardinal,
or 제 prefixed to a Sino-Korean cardinal. The Sino-Korean numeral series is the single richest
source of ㄹ-retention divergence in this chunk.

**§117: The counting word (classifier).** `[noun] + [numeral] + [counting word]`, obligatory,
14 counting words taught, each tied to a semantic object-class.

**§118: The incomplete noun.** Cannot stand alone with complete meaning — requires a preceding
attributive word/clause. 분 doubles as both counting word and incomplete noun depending on context.

**§119–120: The suggestive final endings of the verb ("let us...").** Most deferential -ㅂ시다;
middle (written-register) -세; low -자. Both worked examples for low-courtesy -자 are DPRK
political slogans rather than neutral content.

**§121–122: The imperative final endings of the verb.** Most deferential -십시오; middle -시오; low
-라.

**§123–125: Word order.** Suggestive/imperative predicate lies at sentence end; the adjectival
form of a cardinal numeral always precedes the noun it quantifies.

**§126–127: The adverb — six semantic/functional categories, two morphological classes.** (1)
manner/degree of an action; (2) degree of a state; (3) sentence-level modality; (4) onomatopoeia/
mimesis; (5) connecting adverbs; (6) negation (아니/안 unconditional vs. 못 conditioned/causal — a
split absent from English "not"). Structurally, adverbs are single words or derivatives via -이/-히
suffixed to an adjective root (깊다→깊이, 용감하다→용감히).

**§128–140: The connecting form of the verb/adjective/verbal-form-of-noun-pronoun-numeral.** Two
major branches: **I. Coordinative** — copulative (-고, -며/-면서); adversative (-지만, -ㄴ데);
disjunctive (-나/-든, -든지). **II. Subordinative** — condition (-면; -아야/-어야/-여야, a stronger
"only if" with vowel-harmony allomorphy); cause (-므로; -니/-니까; -더니); order (-다/-다가; -자);
method/means (-아/-어/-여, same vowel-harmony pattern); purpose/intention (-려/-려고/-자고; -러);
negation (-지 attached to 않다/못하다/말다).

**§141–142: Word order — connecting forms and adverb placement.** The connecting form always
precedes the unit it connects to. The adverb's position is governed by its grammatical function
across six demonstrated sub-patterns (as adverbial modifier/attribute/predicate/modifier-of-
another-adverb; the connecting adverb 및/겸/또한 sits *between* the two units it connects, not
before a single unit).

### Grammar points

**The Pre-noun (§143–145).** Modifies a noun but takes no ending and can never be a predicate.
Two kinds: quantity (매, 약) and property (새, 딴, 온).

**The Interjection (§146).** Depends on no other word; mostly sentence-initial. Three subtypes:
emotion (joy/sorrow/wonder); the speaker's demand/will; attitude toward the addressee — notably
응 "yes" (to equal/inferior) vs. 예 "yes" (to superior), previewing Lesson 10's courtesy system.

**Attributive forms (§147–152).** Verb: four tense-marked endings — 는 (present), ㄴ (past), 던
(past continuous, contrasted directly: 읽은 책 "the book I read" vs. 읽던 책 "the book I was
reading"), ㄹ (future). Adjective: only three (ㄴ present, 던 past-continuous, ㄹ future — no simple
past slot). Verbal form of noun/pronoun/numeral: same three-way system via the exchanging ending 이.

**The adverbial-modifier form (§153–154).** Three ending-sets: 게/도록 (manner/circumstance); 듯/
듯이 (comparison); ㄹ수록 ("the more X, the more Y" intensification).

**Word Order, Lesson 9 (§155–160).** Every modifying element precedes what it modifies, without
exception; the interjection is the sole exception (sentence-initial, no dependency).

**The Tenses — absolute (§161–162) vs. relative (§163).** Absolute present = zero ending; absolute
past = 았/었/였 (vowel-harmony-selected); absolute future = 겠. Relative tense is expressed via the
attributive endings, time-referenced to the main predicate rather than to speech-time (그는 열린
문을 닫았다 "he shut the opened door" — 닫았다 absolute past, 열린 relative past).

**Combined tense endings (§164).** Past+과거 stacking for "had done X before Y"; past+future/
surmise (겠 or periphrastic ㄹ것) for "may have."

**Alternate tense expression (§165).** -더니 for causal/sequential past; ㄹ것 as an alternative to
겠 for future.

**Respect (§167).** Marks politeness toward the doer of the action (not the addressee) via the
ending 시. Independent axis from courtesy.

**Courtesy (§168).** Marks politeness toward the addressee, three discrete levels via the final
ending: most deferential (-ㅂ니다/습니다), middle (-오), low (-ㄴ다). Worked triple 가십니다/가시오/
가신다, all "the father goes" — 시 (toward the father, the doer) stays constant, only the
addressee-facing courtesy level changes.

**Respect/courtesy interaction and pronoun avoidance (§169).** **Flagship, directly stated by the
source**: *"In Korean we do not use the personal pronoun such as 'you' when we speak to a
respected person. Instead, we use the denomination of his position in the family or society, such
as father, mother and Mr."* (p.225) — an explicit normative statement in the book's own voice.

**Suppletive honorific vocabulary (§170).** A small closed set of entirely different lexical roots
used in place of the plain verb for a respected doer: 먹다→잡수시다, 자다→주무시다, 있다→계시다,
말하다→말씀하시다, 주다→드리다/올리다 (two alternates, no explanation given of how they differ —
worth a native-speaker query).

**Word order: tense, respect, final ending (§171–173).** Fixed template: stem + (시, if respect) +
(tense ending, if any) + final ending. 가신다 (stem+시+ㄴ다) vs 가셨다 (stem+시+었+다).

### Grammar points

**The Voice (Lesson 11, §174–179).** Three voices: active (zero ending), passive and causative
(both marked by one of 이/히/기/리, chosen by the stem's final sound — only the semantic relation
distinguishes passive from causative, not the form). Periphrastic alternatives: passive via 되다/
당하다/받다/-아지다/-게 되다; causative via 시키다/-게 하다/-도록 하다, attached to the verb's root.

**The Exchanging Endings (§180–184).** 이 converts a noun/pronoun/numeral into a verbal
(predicative) form; ㅁ or 기 convert a verb/adjective/verbal-form into a noun (기 additionally
carries "the process of the action"). 이 may be omitted after a vowel-final noun in the plain
declarative but is never omitted in the attributive form.

**The Auxiliary Endings (Lesson 12, §185–188).** Layer a semantic relation (inclusion/restriction/
limitation/indication/emphasis/concession/negation) onto a word: 도 "also," 만 "only," 부터/까지
"from/till," 는(ㄴ)/은 "as for," 야/이야 "emphasis," 나/이나 "concession," 커녕 "far from."

**Endings Used as Case Endings (§189–190).** 란/이란 (definitional nominative-function), 한테
(dative, spoken), 하고 (coordinative), 보다/처럼/마다 (comparison group).

**Stem and Ending / Attaching of Endings (Lesson 13, §191–192).** Stem+ending(s) cleanly separate;
unneeded endings are simply omitted (demonstrated via all 2⁴ combinations of causative/respect/past
on 보다). Multiple endings attach one at a time, in sequence.

**Pairs of Endings (§193).** Phonologically-conditioned pairs keyed to vowel/consonant-final
stems: 가/이 (nominative), 와/과 (coordinative), 오/소 (middle declarative).

**The Link-Vowel (§194).** 이 or 으 inserted between a consonant-final stem and a consonant-initial
ending purely for pronounceability; the book explicitly distinguishes this from the homophonous
exchanging-ending 이 by function (책이다 "it's a book" uses the exchanging ending, not a link-vowel).

**The Vocal Harmony (§195).** Stems with a "bright" last-syllable vowel (ㅏㅑㅗ) take 아-series
endings; "dark" vowels (ㅓㅕㅜㅡㅣ) take 어-series; stems ending in ㅣㅐㅔㅚㅟㅢ or the syllable 하
take 여-series. 하다 is the sole apparent exception to the bright-vowel rule (takes 여- despite ㅏ),
but this is governed by the 하-ending rule, not a true counterexample.

**The Exchange of Sounds (§196).** Seven stem-final sound-alternation rules: (1) 르→ㄹㄹ (흐르다);
(2) ㄷ→ㄹ (듣다); (3) ㅂ→오/우 (돕다); (4) 르→르ㄹ, a *second* distinct 르-pattern (누르다/푸르다/
이르다); (5) ㄹ disappears before ㄴ/ㅂ/시/오 (울다); (6) ㅅ disappears before a vowel-initial
ending (낫다); (7) ㅎ disappears before a vowel-initial ending, for adjectives specifically
(빨갛다).

### Grammar points

**§197 — The eleven parts of the sentence (overview).** Predicate, subject, object, quotation,
adverbial modifier, attribute, form of address, parenthesis, exclamatory word, conjunctive,
appended modifier — plus a twelfth higher-order category, the "expanded part of sentence."

**§198–200 — Simple vs. expanded parts of sentence.** A simple part = one word or a fixed
word-combination used as one word; an expanded part = two+ words jointly filling one sentence-part
role.

**§201–203 — The Predicate.** Final predicate (closes the sentence: final verbal form; a
connecting form reused sentence-finally; an exchanging-ending substantive; or a bare noun with no
ending at all — e.g. "조선인민군" "[we are] the Korean People's Army"). Connecting predicate
(precedes the final predicate, signals the sentence isn't closed): usually a connecting form, but
also a reused final form, or one of a closed set of connecting nouns/adverbs (동시/반면/한편/일방/
이상/한/겸). A meaning-agreement phenomenon lets the final predicate's honorific/tense/mood
retroactively supply meaning to an earlier under-marked connecting predicate — unless the clauses
have genuinely different subjects.

**§204 — The Subject.** Nominative/case-marking/auxiliary ending on a noun/pronoun; or 에서 on a
collective-object noun (우리 농장에서 "our farm," as subject of a production verb); or bare, no
ending, typical of poetry (저 노래 "that song," in the enemy-fear example).

**§205–206 — The Object.** Case/auxiliary ending; or no ending at all (저는 사이다 마십니다 "I
drink lemonade"); or a small set of postpositional auxiliaries (위하여/대하여/의하여/관하여/말미암아)
after a case-marked noun.

**§207–208 — The Quotation.** -라고/-이라고/-느냐고/-ㄴ가고/-다고 after a final form; or a bare
final form in 《 》 guillemets; or 하고 after a quoted phrase in quotation marks.

**§209–210 — The Adverbial Modifier.** A verb/adjective's own adverbial-modifier form; an ordinary
adverb or an unmarked adverbially-used noun (적극 "actively"); a noun in -적 with 로 or bare; nearly
all adverbs except 및/겸 (which can't become adverbial modifiers); incomplete nouns like 채/대로/
족족 on an attributive form (산채로 "alive"); doubled connecting forms (가든 안가든).

**§211–212 — The Attribute.** A verb/adjective's attributive form (including auxiliary-use verbs
like 대한, from 대하다); any pre-noun; a bare or genitive-marked noun/pronoun/numeral (우리 집 /
우리의 집, same meaning); a bare or genitive-marked adverb (붕붕 벌소리 / 스스로의 가책).

**§213–214 — The Form of Address.** Vocative-ending 이여 (전우들이여 "comrades-in-arms!"), or a bare
word used as direct address (김동무! "Comrade Kim!"). The lesson's own worked examples here are two
of the strongest ideological slogans in the whole chunk.

**§215–216 — The Parenthesis.** A connecting form used parenthetically (말하자면 "so to speak"); or
a fixed phrase (듣건대 "they say," 보는바와 같이 "as you see").

**§217–218 — The Exclamatory Word.** A true interjection (예 "yes"); or a repurposed final form
used standalone (옳소 "right!").

**§219–220 — The Conjunctive.** An adverb used connectively (그러니까/또한/그리고); a non-adverb word
(다음으로 "next"); a fixed phrase (그뿐아니라 "besides").

**§221 — The Appended Modifier.** A word/phrase fronted purely for emphasis, restated/elaborated by
the rest of the sentence — the lesson's sole worked example is the chunk's flagship: 백두산, 그
이름은 모든 조선사람의 심장속에 영원히 살아있을것이다 "Mt. Paekdu! The name will live forever in
the hearts of all Koreans."

**§222–228 — The Expanded Part of Sentence.** Six of the eleven parts (predicate, subject, object,
quotation, adverbial modifier, attribute — not the other five) can be realized by a multi-word
combination, diagnosed by the ending the *last* word carries: expanded predicate (a full clause
serving as the predicate — 혁명의 요람 만경대는 경치가 매우 아름답다, the chunk's second major
ideological flagship); expanded subject (-기 nominalization + 가); expanded object (-기 + 를);
expanded quotation (a clause closing in -다고); expanded adverbial modifier (a clause closing in
게); expanded attribute (a clause closing in 는).

### Grammar points

**Lesson 15, §1 — Agreement in expressions of respect and courtesy.** Respect agreement (시 appears
when the subject is respected); courtesy agreement (the final predicate's courtesy level tracks the
addressee, three grades on 오다: 오십니다/오시오/오신다); inherently-honorific words (올리다) must be
used independent of the ending system.

**§2 — Agreement in the adverbial modifier and other parts.** Negative adverbials like 결코 "never"
require a negated predicate; sentence-initial interrogative words require an interrogative final
predicate.

**§3 — Agreement in the forms of address.** A vocative-ending (아/야) opening requires the low
imperative form later (the Mt. Paektu example).

**The order of parts of sentence.** A systematic hierarchy: predicate last; subject first (unless
emphasized); object between subject and predicate (closer object nearer the predicate when
multiple; expanded object precedes simple object); time before place when both are sentence-
initial; quotations between object and predicate; adverbial modifiers follow subject/object/
quotation except modal adverbials (아마/글쎄/결코/비록/아무리/실로/물론) which front; attributes
always precede their noun; forms of address/exclamations/conjunctives/appended modifiers default to
sentence-initial (though address/exclamations can also close a sentence).

**Lesson 16 — The kinds of sentences.** Five kinds by speaker viewpoint: declarative,
interrogative, suggestive, imperative, exclamatory.

- **Declarative**: seven content-functions (plain fact; obligatory action via -야 한다; estimation;
  determination/intention; a promise via the dedicated ending 마; affirmation/negation worked
  through a full three-tier courtesy system with English-flipped polarity — answering a negative
  question with 예/응/네 "yes" actually affirms the negative, = English "no"; and a no-verb
  declarative naming an existing object, e.g. 군중의 물결, 기쁨의 환호).
- **Interrogative**: splits into those requiring an answer and those that don't (rhetorical
  instruction-as-question; strong rhetorical affirmation/negation).
- **Suggestive**: joint action (동무들 춤을 춤시다); action required of the addressee specifically
  (박동무 좀 천천히 읽으시오); a tone-intensity gradient from plain determination to high-register
  revolutionary calls-to-action (동무들, 혁명을 위하여...싸웁시다!; 자주성을 옹호하자!; 원쑤를
  소멸하자!) versus a soft tone for an ordinary request.
- **Imperative**: usually the imperative final ending (빨리 가라!); also an "obligation" form on
  것+ㄹ (모두 모일것!); a bare stem-only form with no ending.
- **Exclamatory**: an emotion via interjection or the dedicated word 만세 "hurrah" (조선민주주의인민
  공화국 만세! treated as belonging to this category); or feelings of respect/pride/congratulation/
  conviction/determination (우리의 평양은 참으로 웅장하구나!).

**Simple, compound, and complex sentences.** Simple = one declarative unit, subdividing into common
simple and one-member sentences. Compound = two+ coordinated simple sentences via a connecting
ending like -고 (equal/independent status). Complex = a main unit + subordinate unit(s) via a
connecting ending like -면 (unequal status).

**Direct speech: quotation marking and position.** 《 》-style marks. Connectors: 고 directly after
the quotation; a semantically-empty "so"-word (하고/이렇게) between quote and reporting verb; or no
connecting device. Quoted material can appear before, mid-, or after the reporting clause.

**Converting direct speech to indirect speech.** Five rules: (1) person/time/place/direction shift
to the reporting speaker's viewpoint (deictic shift: 가다 "go" → 오다 "come," 래일 "tomorrow" → 다음날
"the next day"); (2) courtesy level is leveled down to low form regardless of the original grade;
(3) vocatives/exclamatory forms are converted or dropped; (4) a multi-sentence original collapses
into one connected indirect-speech quotation; (5) quotation marks and any "so"-connector are
dropped, leaving only the 고-connector.

**Flagship finding — propaganda register embedded directly in grammar pedagogy.** Across Lesson
15–16's worked examples, this book repeatedly reaches for explicitly state-ideological content —
동무 as the default address term across the entire courtesy-level system, 조국 as the default
emotionally-loaded noun for vocative/exclamatory drills, Mt. Paektu as the vocative-case example,
and militant calls-to-action deployed as ordinary suggestive/exclamatory-sentence grammar drills —
rather than the politically-neutral stock sentences typical of South Korean or Western-published
Korean textbooks of the same era. This recurs across at least 4 separate named grammar points in
this one 48-page chunk alone, suggesting the whole book's example-sentence bank was deliberately
curated for ideological content rather than incidentally containing some.

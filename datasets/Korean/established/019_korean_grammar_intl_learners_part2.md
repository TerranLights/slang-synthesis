## Grammar points

### Chapter Twelve (tail) — Negation (§§1–4, pp. 214–217)

The chapter's own `Figure 18` summary table (seen in full on p. 213, at the sibling file's
boundary) sets up a two-axis system: **verb type** (action / descriptive / copula / 있다 "exist" /
알다 "know") × **sentence type** (declarative-interrogative vs. imperative-suggestion), each cell
giving a Short Form and/or Long Form negator.

- **§1 Negation in declarative/interrogative sentences.** Short Form: `안` or `못` placed
  immediately before the predicate. Long Form: `-지 않(아니하)-` or `-지 못하-` suffixed to the verb
  stem. `안`/`-지 않-` negate by the *agent's own will/intention*; `못`/`-지 못하-` negate by
  *inability or external prevention* — a semantic distinction, not just a register one, and `못`-type
  negation is described as incompatible with descriptive verbs. For a noun+하다 verb (e.g.
  숙제하다 "do homework"), `안`/`못` must be inserted *between* the noun and 하다 (숙제 안 합니다), not
  before the whole compound.
- **§2 Negation in imperative/propositive sentences.** Only the pattern `-지 말-` is grammatical
  here (가지 말아라 "don't go!," 가지 말자 "let's not go") — plain `안`/`못` negation does not apply to
  commands or suggestions.
- **§3 Lexical negation.** Three predicates negate by full suppletion rather than any productive
  negator: copula 이다 → `-이/가 아니다`; existential 있다 "exist/have" → `없다` "not exist/not have";
  알다 "know" → `모르다` "not know" (not *안 알다).
- **§4 Negation in confirmation questions.** A formally negative Long Form question (`-지
  않아요?`) can carry **no negative meaning at all**, instead functioning as a confirmation-seeking
  tag ("...isn't it? — please confirm"), e.g. 이 김치가 맵지 않아요? "This kimchi is spicy, isn't
  it?" Only the Long Form works in this confirmation-question use, not Short Form `안`/`못`.

### Chapter Thirteen — Special Expressions Using Conjugational Endings (pp. 219–338)

A large three-part catalog (Final Endings → Conjunctive Endings → Adnominal/Nominalizer Endings),
each entry a named pattern glossed with its own function and register. Representative sample below
(not exhaustive — see the extraction-depth note above).

- **Conjunctive-ending catalog (pp. ~236–316), representative entries:**
  - `Vst-(으)면서(도)` "while...yet; although...also" — emphasizes that the preceding and
    following clauses are contradictory/conflicting; the `-도` can be dropped with no change in
    function.
  - `Vst-(으)며` — two readings: ① simultaneity of two actions by the same subject (formal/written
    register, an alternative to `-(으)면서`); ② simple concatenation of two states/actions with no
    temporal or causal implication (짜다고 하며 ... "is quiet, and the coffee is good, too").
  - `Vst-어(아,여)서` — ① reason/cause for the following clause (incompatible with past `-었-` or
    future `-겠-` marking on the *preceding* verb); ② sequential/logically-connected actions (no
    restriction on the following verb's own tense).
  - `Vst-어(아,여)야` "only to the extent that...; if you don't...you can't" — a necessary-condition
    pattern.
  - `Vst-자마자` / `Vst-자` "as soon as, no sooner...than" — the action of the second clause follows
    immediately on the first; illustrated with the proverb 까마귀 날자 배 떨어진다 ("as the crow
    flies, the pear falls" — Korea's equivalent of "mere coincidence").
  - `N-이자` "is X and Y at the same time" — the copula-attached form of `-자`, for two nominal
    predicates holding simultaneously (졸업은 끝이자 시작이란 개념을... "Graduation entails both an
    ending and a beginning").
  - `Vst-자니` "now that one intends to (do)..." — abbreviated from `-고자 하니`; the following clause
    modifies/constrains the intention in the preceding clause. Illustrated with a genuine dilemma
    construction: 죽자니 청춘이요, 살자니 고생이에요 "[if I] contemplate death, [I'm still] in my
    youth; [if I] contemplate life, it's [nothing but] suffering."
  - `Vst-길래` "since, as, because" — the following clause's subject is normally first person;
    interchangeable with `-기에`.
  - `Vst-기로서니` "even though (it is true that)...but" — concedes a fact while asserting it doesn't
    fully justify the following clause; interchangeable with the shorter `-기로`.
  - The structure `Vst-기 N(이/가) 없다` (with a specific N slot filled by 그지, 짝이, 한이, etc.)
    expresses extremity/severity of a descriptive verb: `-기 그지없다` "is extraordinarily...,"
    `-기 짝이 없다` "is unparalleled/incomparably..."
- **Adnominal Endings (§3, pp. 316–319).** `Figure 20` tabulates the system precisely: for
  **action verbs**, present/processive is `-는`, past/perfect is `-(으)ㄴ`, future/conjecture is
  `-(으)ㄹ`, retrospective/recollection is `-던`; for **descriptive verbs** and the **copula**,
  present is `-(으)ㄴ`/`-ㄴ` (not `-는` — the well-known action/descriptive-verb split), future is
  `-(으)ㄹ`/`-ㄹ`, retrospective is `-던`. `있다` "exist" patterns with action verbs (`-는` present).
  This is the same architecture already captured more briefly in sibling files but presented here
  as one unusually clean consolidated table.
- **Nominalizer Endings (§4, pp. 319–338).** `-기` is characterized as far more syntactically
  versatile than `-(으)ㅁ` (can take case particles, combine with other endings, function
  adnominally). Documented sub-patterns: `Vst-기(가)` (기 as clause subject, collocating with
  좋다/싫다/편하다/쉽다/어렵다/재미있다-type predicates); `Vst-기(가) 쉽다` "likely to..., probably
  will..." (a genuine probability/likelihood reading, distinct from literal "easy"); `Vst-기는`
  "acknowledges (sometimes grudgingly)..." (기 + contrastive -는 + 하다, or a repeated-verb
  structure); `Vst-기만` "does nothing but...; no sooner does...than..." (기 + exclusive -만 + 하다);
  `Vst-기에` "because, for the reason that..." (기 + adverbial -에; can be replaced by `-길래` with no
  change in meaning); `Vst-(으)ㄴ/는다기에` "because they say that..." — built by deleting `-고 하-`
  from the indirect-quotation pattern `-(으)ㄴ/는다고 하니`. A dedicated subsection, "Cases of
  Fossilized Nouns," documents `-기`-nominalized forms that have lost all verbal behavior and
  function as ordinary nouns (걷기 → hiking as a sport-noun sense, 말타기 "horseback riding," 활쏘기
  "archery," 씨름의... 들어메치기 "the shoulder-throw [technique]"), plus a "Proverbs and Other
  Conventionalized Expressions" subsection built on nominalized `-기` (see vocabulary table above
  for the idioms themselves). `-(으)ㅁ` is treated as comparatively inflexible: mainly used before
  particles like `을/를`, `이/가`, and specific verbs (지적하다 "point out," 깨닫다 "realize,"
  주장하다 "claim," 전하다 "convey"), e.g. 그는... 죄인임을 깨닫지 못합니다 "he doesn't realize that
  he is guilty." `-(으)ㅁ` can also stand as a bare **sentence-final ending** in what the source
  calls (citing Martin) the "Documentary Style" — public notices, diaries, certificates, formal
  letter closings (이곳에는 들어갈 수 없음 "No entry" on a posted sign; 오늘은 할머니 댁에 갔음 as a
  diary entry). A final subsection gives ~10 pairs of `-(으)ㅁ`-fossilized nouns contrasted with
  their `-기`-fossilized counterparts (걷다→걸음 "pace/gait" vs. hiking-sense 걷기; 웃다→웃음
  "smile/laughter"; 기쁘다→기쁨 "happiness"; 아프다→아픔 "pain"; 즐겁다→즐거움 "enjoyment";
  울다→울음 "crying"; 슬프다→슬픔 "sadness"; 죽다→죽음 "death").

### Chapter Fourteen — Auxiliary Verbs (pp. 339–362)

Organizes Korean's auxiliary-verb system into ten explicitly named functional classes (a
taxonomy not found this explicitly in any prior Korean `established/` file, though the individual
auxiliaries mostly are — see the non-redundant-supplement note above): **1. Progressive** (진행,
`-어(아,여) 가다/오다` "gradually...," `-고 있다` "is doing" — 있다 upgrades to 계시다 for
subject-honorification); **2.** (unlabeled in the sampled pages, likely Resultative/State, between
Progressive and Terminative); **3. Terminative** (`-고 나다` "just finished doing," restricted to
action verbs, commonly combining with `-어서`/`-(으)니까`/`-(으)면` for "after finishing.../now that
one finishes..."; `-어(아,여) 버리다` "does completely, all up/down/away — to my
disappointment/relief" depending on context, illustrated with a genuine ambiguity: 그 친구는 미국에
가 버렸어요 can read either as neutral completion or as "took off for the US" with an implied sense
of abandonment); **6. Retentive** (보유, `-어(아,여) 놓다` "does in advance, gets it done so it will
be ready" vs. `-어(아,여) 두다` "does it once and for good, so it won't need to be redone" — a subtle
but real distinction the source draws between the two near-synonymous forms); **10. Auxiliary of
Inability** (`-지 못하다` "cannot," cross-referenced back to the negation chapter, p. 152). Also
noted: a caution that sequences that *look* like this auxiliary-verb pattern can actually be two
independent main verbs concatenated with their own literal meanings (바빠서 숙제를 해 오지 못했어요
"wasn't able to [do and] bring my homework" — 해 오다 here is literally "do, then come," not the
grammaticalized progressive auxiliary).

### Chapter Fifteen — Passive and Causative (pp. 363–371)

Defines passive (patient receives an action from an agent marked with 에게) vs. causative (causer
makes a causee perform an action) with worked minimal-pair-style examples, then establishes that
**both** categories have a **short (lexical/derived) form**, built by affixing a passive suffix
(`-이/히/리/기`) or causative suffix (`-이/히/리/기/우/추`) directly to the verb stem, **and** a
**long (periphrastic) form** — passive via `-어(아,여)지다`, causative via `-게 하다` — with the long
forms having significantly broader applicability than the short forms, which must be memorized
verb-by-verb (no fully general rule for which suffix a given stem takes). `Figure 23` tabulates
long-passive examples (써지다 "gets written," 깨지다 "gets broken," 풀어지다 "gets resolved/comes
undone," 이루어지다 "gets achieved," 밝혀지다 "gets clarified"). `Figure 24` tabulates short
causatives by suffix and verb type (intransitive/transitive/descriptive), e.g. `-이`: 죽이다 "kill,"
줄이다 "shorten," 먹이다 "feed," 높이다 "raise"; `-히`: 익히다 "cook," 앉히다 "seat," 넓히다 "widen";
`-리`: 울리다 "make cry," 살리다 "let live/save," 알리다 "let know"; `-기`: 웃기다 "make laugh," 남기다
"make remain," 감기다 "wash [someone's hair]." A worked contrast set (pp. 369–370) shows short vs.
long causative producing genuinely different real-world implicature, not just a stylistic choice:
먹이다 "fed [personally, hand to mouth]" vs. 먹게 하다 "had [someone] take medicine [themselves]";
앉히다 "physically sat [someone] down" vs. 앉게 하다 "had [someone] sit [of their own accord]";
읽히다 "made [someone] recite right there" vs. 읽게 하다 "had [someone] read [at their own pace,
e.g. at home]." This directness/indirectness distinction is the chapter's most valuable finding —
the short/long choice encodes physical directness of the causer's involvement, not merely register.

### Chapter Sixteen — Quoted Speech (pp. 372–398)

Splits into **Direct Quoted Speech** (직접화법) and **Indirect Quoted Speech** (간접화법/화법).
Direct quotation uses quotation marks plus a quotation particle — `-(이)라고` (neutral) or `-하고`
(the source notes this one lets the reporter mimic the original tone/intonation, used for
emphasis) — followed by a reporting predicate (묻다 "ask," 명령하다 "order," 제안하다 "suggest," or
the general-purpose 말하다/하다). Indirect quotation uses the single particle `-고` in the fixed
order Subject–Reportee–Quoted Utterance–고–Predicate, and requires deictic elements (personal
pronouns, tense, place pronouns, honorifics) to shift to the reporter's own perspective — e.g. 제가
숙제를 했어요 "I did my homework" (Cholsu, quoted) becomes 자기가 숙제를 했다고 말했습니다ਂ "Cholsu said
that **he** had done his homework," with 제가→자기가 reflexive-pronoun substitution.
**Sentence-type-specific quotation endings**: Declarative `-ㄴ/는다고`; Interrogative `-(느)냐고`;
Imperative `-(으)라고`; Propositive `-자고`. `Figure 27` (p. 381) systematically tabulates the
**contraction cascade** for deleting the quotation particle `-고` plus the dummy verb `하-`, across
all four sentence types and both formal/informal registers — e.g. Declarative 간다고 합니다 →
formal-abbreviated 간답니다 → informal-abbreviated 간대요; Interrogative 비싸냐고 합니다 → 비싸냡니다 →
비싸내요. The source explicitly frames the fully-contracted forms as **grammaticalized new
patterns**, not mere phonological reduction — in some cases (for historical reasons) the "-고 하-"
cannot be structurally restored even though it can for others, meaning the dummy verb 하- has
"developed in a different direction" into a marker of the speaker's attitude/emotion/judgement
rather than a real verb. Two further quotation-derived patterns, both newly captured (not in any
prior sibling file): **`-(느)ㄴ다는 것이`** "meant to X, but Y happened instead" — combines the
embedded quotation `-(ㄴ/는)다` with modifier `-는` + 것이, marking a mismatch between original
intention and actual outcome (동생 바지를 산다는 것이 내 것만 샀습니다 "I meant to buy trousers for my
brother, but I ended up only buying [trousers] for myself"); and **`-(ㄴ/는)다기에`** "because they
say/I hear that..." (in addition to the closely related `-기에` above). A special benefactive
quotative construction `-에게 -을/를 주라고/드리라고` handles reported requests to give something to
someone, with the honorific-register variant 드리라고 for a higher-status recipient.

### Chapter Seventeen — Prefixes and Suffixes (pp. 399–418)

Opens with a bare inventory line of ~30 native-Korean derivational prefixes: 갓-, 군-, 날-, 덧-,
되-, 들-, 맏-, 맞-, 매-, 맨-, 빗-, 새-, 생-, 선-, 설-, 숫-, 시-, 알-, 애-, 엇-, 외-, 잔-, 짓-, 첫-,
총-, 최-, 치-, 풋-, 한-, 햇-, 홀-, 휘-. Each gets its own subsection with a gloss and a small word
family (see the Vocabulary table above for individual items). Notable semantic ranges: 갓- "just
(now)" for a recently-changed state (갓스물 "barely 20," 갓졸업하다 "just graduated"); 덧- "added,
put on top of" (덧문 "storm/double door," 덧니 "snaggle tooth" — literally an "added tooth"); 되-
"back, again, in reverse" (되돌아가다 "retrace one's steps," 되풀이하다 "repeat"); 잔- "small,
trivial, niggling" (잔소리 "nagging," 잔심부름 "small errand" — both carrying a mildly deprecating
connotation the gloss itself signals with words like "piddling"/"mickey-mouse"); 첫- "first, the
beginning" (첫사랑 "first love," 첫인상 "first impression"); 총- "whole, entire, gross, total"
(총선거 "general election," 총재산 "total fortune" — Sino-Korean-register prefix, contrasts with the
native-Korean prefixes surrounding it); 최- "the most, ultra-" (introduced but not sampled in
detail here). The chapter's second half covers derivational **suffixes**, including a
descriptive-verb-forming battery `-다랗다`/`-답다`/`-롭다`/`-스럽다` ("rather.../sort of like.../
characterized by.../is like, suggests") and the abstract-feeling-noun-forming `-쩍다` ("feels/gives/
has a feeling of," attaching specifically to emotion/feeling nouns: 겸연쩍다 "embarrassed,"
미심쩍다 "dubious," 수상쩍다 "suspicious/fishy"); the noun-forming suffix `-감` ("material for, a
suitable person, sense/feeling of" — spanning concrete "material" senses like 장난감 "toy" and
며느릿감 "daughter-in-law material" through to abstract "sense of X" senses like 책임감
"responsibility," 열등감 "inferiority complex," 우월감 "superiority complex," 비굴감 "sense of
servility"); the durative/spanning suffix `-간` ("among, between; for [a duration]": 형제간
"between brothers," 며칠간 "for a few days"); and the iterative-verb-forming `-거리다`/`-대다` ("does
repeatedly, keeps doing, does over and over").

## Register/notable findings summary

- **Short-form vs. long-form negation** carries a real semantic distinction (agent's own
  will/intention vs. inability/external prevention), not just a stylistic register choice — `못`-
  type negation is further marked as incompatible with descriptive verbs.
- A formally negative long-form question (`-지 않아요?`) can be **semantically non-negative**,
  functioning purely as a confirmation-seeking tag — a real form/meaning mismatch worth flagging
  for any downstream slang-mechanics work on how negation morphology gets reused pragmatically.
- The short/long causative distinction (Ch. 15) encodes **physical directness of the causer's
  involvement** in the caused action, a concrete semantic axis beyond simple formality.
- The Ch. 16 quotation-contraction cascade is explicitly framed by the source as
  **grammaticalization**, not mere phonological shortening — some contracted forms cannot be
  structurally "expanded" back to their `-고 하-` source, meaning the dummy verb 하- has drifted
  into a pure attitude/evidentiality marker in those forms.
- Ch. 17's 잔- prefix family (잔소리 "nagging," 잔심부름 "small errand") and 첫- (첫사랑, 첫인상) are
  productive, everyday-register word-formation devices — exactly the sort of derivational machinery
  a synthesis pass deriving invented slang might draw on for coining diminutive/dismissive compound
  forms.

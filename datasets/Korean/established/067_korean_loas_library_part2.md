**Morpheme breakdowns:** **saylowun** = saylop- ('be new') + -(u)n (ADNZ) — contrasted with non-inflecting adnoun *say* 'new': same meaning, different morphological path to the modifying form. **manh-i/noph-i/coyong-hi/pwucilen-hi** = stem + -i (after plain consonant-final stem) / -hi (after 하다-type stem). **I-nom-a** = i (deictic 'this') + nom (epithet root 'bastard') + -a (vocative particle, C-ending) — swap the epithet root (nyen/saykki/mengcheng/twayci) and the particle alternant (-a/-ya by V/C-ending) to generate the whole taboo-address paradigm; an attested, source-documented productive insult-formation template. **ey-se-pwuthe-uy** = ey ('at') + se (linking element) + pwuthe ('from') + uy ('of') — a four-particle stack, rightmost particle cutting off first under immediate-constituent analysis. **ey-kwan.ha.ye.se ~ ey-kwan.han** = ey + kwan- ('concern') + ha- ('do') + -ye.se (adverbial) ~ -(u)n (adnominal) — one of five parallel adverbial/adnominal pairs built on the same extended-particle root.

## Grammar points

### 1. The eight-word-class system (§4.1, pp. 31–32)

Eight major word classes — noun, pronoun, adnoun, verb, adjective, adverb, particle, interjection — cross-cut by **open vs. closed** (noun/verb/adjective/adverb open; the rest closed) and **inflected vs. uninflected** (only verbs/adjectives inflect), further grouped into **nominal** (noun, pronoun), **verbal** (verb, adjective), and the heterogeneous **particle** class. A word can belong to more than one class (*khuta*: adj. "big"/v. "grow"; *ecey*: n./adv. "yesterday").

### 2. Bound-noun system and VN/AN+*-hata* derivation (§4.1, pp. 32–36)

Nouns split **free vs. bound** (a closed set of a few dozen bound nouns, never standing alone); free nouns split proper vs. common, common nouns countable vs. noncountable; bound nouns split classifier vs. nonclassifier. Proper names reject quantifiers, determiners, and plural *-tul*. A productive VN/AN+*-hata* system derives verbs (intransitive/transitive/ditransitive by the noun's own semantics) or adjectives from SK/NK nouns; two SK verbal nouns can compound before taking *-hata*.

### 3. Pronoun systems: personal (abbreviated), deictic grid, reflexive/reciprocal (§4.2, pp. 36–39)

The personal-pronoun paradigm substantially overlaps `018`/`031` and is kept thin (only the structural 1st-person-honorific gap and pejorative 3rd-person items are new). **Genuinely new:** the i/ku/ce deictic grid crossed systematically against thing/time/place/person bound nouns, with "light isotope" *yo/ko/co* variants conveying pejorative connotation through vowel alternation alone; the enu/etten indefinite/interrogative extension; and the four-way reflexive split (*caki*/*tangsin*/*ce*/*casin*, plus nonhuman *cachey*) alongside reciprocal *selo* and the productive *-kkili* suffix.

### 4. The 7-position verb-inflection slot template (§4.3, pp. 40–41)

Seven ordered positions, six pre-wordfinal and optional, the seventh obligatory: HON (-si), PRS (-n(un)/zero), PST (-ess), PST² (second "double" past), VOL (-keyss, explicitly not a true future), RETR (-t(e/i/u)), and a word-final slot fusing with one of six syntactic roles (sentence-ending, nominalizing, adnominalizing, adverbializing, conjoining, auxiliary-connecting). A genuinely systematic agglutination template not present elsewhere in this project's Korean material.

### 5. Minor sentence-ending types and the nominalizer/adnominalizer/adverbializer/connective inventories (§4.3, pp. 41–44)

Beyond the four major sentence types (skipped as redundant with `031`): **suspective** -ci/-ci.yo (works across all four speech-act readings), **exclamatory** -kwun(+variants), **promissory** -(u)ma/-(u)lkkey (1st-person-subject restricted; -(u)ma rejects polite -yo, -(u)lkkey accepts it). Four parallel systems convert a verb stem to another category: productive nominalizers -(u)m/-ki (contrasted with the lexical/fossilized nominalizers of §4.1); tense-marked adnominalizers; a semantically-organized open-ended adverbializer set; and conjunctive/disjunctive sentence connectives.

### 6. Auxiliary-connective system, auxiliary-verb taxonomy, and transitivity classes (§4.3, pp. 44–46)

Gerund -ko/infinitive -e/suspective -ci connect a main verb to an auxiliary, producing a fully **productive** progressive (V-ko-issta) vs. a **closed/restricted** perfective (V-e-issta, limited to a handful of intransitive verbs and ci-passives). A supplementary ~12-category auxiliary-verb taxonomy (resultative/exhaustive/completive/conative/beneficial/inbound/outbound/inceptive/emotive/terminative/progressive/negative/prohibitive) is kept alongside `019`'s independent ten-class system per the "different author's independent examples" rule. A clean six-way **valency/transitivity** classification (intransitive/passive/incomplete-intransitive/transitive/incomplete-transitive/ditransitive-dative-or-causative) extends to VN-*hata* verbs, which can alternately passivize with *toyta* instead of *hata*.

### 7. Adjective inflection and classes (§4.4, pp. 47–49)

Adjectives ("descriptive verbs") share most of the verb inflection template but differ in five concrete ways: zero present tense; no imperative/propositive endings at all; no progressive/perfective aspect; the adnominalizer *-(u)n* denotes **present** on an adjective but **past** on a verb (a striking cross-class polysemy); and certain adverbializers (e.g. *-(u)lyeko*) are verb-only. Five syntactic subclasses by complement-taking behavior: Copula (subject complement obligatory), Descriptive (no complement), Transitive (two nominative NPs despite being stative), Emotive (two nominative NPs, psych-predicate pattern), Existential (locative complement, "partially behave like verbs") — the adjective/verb split tracks real complement-taking clusters, not simple stativity.

### 8. Adnoun, adverb, and particle classes (§4.5–4.7, pp. 50–58)

Adnouns are a non-inflectional modifying class (contrasted with adnominalized adjectives reaching the same surface position via an ending — e.g. non-inflecting *say* 'new' vs. inflecting *saylowun* 'new'), splitting into determiners (with the light-isotope phenomenon), quantifiers (parallel NK/SK numeral systems), and descriptive adnouns. Adverbs split by formation into lexical, derived (-i/-hi), and onomatopoetic (plain/tensed/aspirated consonant-strength triplets), and by semantic type into eight non-exclusive categories (several items recur under more than one heading by the source's own classification). Particles are an uninflected closed "postposition" class in five subclasses — grammatical-function, discourse-function (which cannot co-occur with grammatical-function particles — a real paradigmatic-exclusion rule, not free stacking), adverbial, conjunctive, and a genuinely distinct fifth **sentence**-particle class (polite *yo*, plural *tul*, quotative *ko/hako/lako*) — plus derived quasi-particles and particle-stacking into phrasal/extended-phrasal particles with an explicit right-to-left bracketing rule.

### 9. Interjections and vocatives (§4.8, pp. 59–60)

Interjections have no grammatical relation to any other word class, organized into eight functional subtypes (response/inquiry/uncertainty/pain/damning/hesitation/pity/surprise); two items are explicitly taboo-register (the "damning" set). Vocatives realize via names, titles, or epithets with/without particle *(y)a*, across six subtypes including an explicitly English-borrowed title pattern (*misu Kim*) and — the section's most valuable find for slang-synthesis purposes — a **fully productive insult-vocative formation template** (deictic *i* + epithet root + optional *(y)a*), illustrated with five concrete epithet roots, documenting compositional taboo-address generation rather than five unconnected fixed items.

### 10. Phrase-level structural syntax (§4.9, pp. 61–67)

Explicit rewrite rules and bracketing trees for all five phrase types, atypical content for pedagogical grammars: **NP** (ADN+N(+Classifier) or NP+Particle; bound-noun heads force obligatory modification; plural *-tul* is obligatory specifically after an i/ku/ce determiner, and after a post-modifier-ordered determiner; a dative/eykey-marked NP disallows separating a classifier from its head noun); **VP** (main predicate + auxiliary-connective + auxiliary, 13 tabulated constructions, several explicitly flagged idiomatic rather than compositional); **AP** (structurally parallel to VP, six constructions); **AdvP** (simplest: Modifier+Adverb); **PP** (particle is the phrase's head, stacking recursively with a right-to-left immediate-constituent cut, including extended phrasal particles pairing an adverbial form with a structurally parallel adnominal form from the same root).

### 11. Three-way sentence classification and the 13-pattern valency inventory (§5.1, pp. 70, 71, 78–82)

Korean sentences classify by predicate type — Verbal, Adjectival, Copular (the latter re-analyzable as adjectival, since *ita* behaves structurally like an adjective). A named functional-category system (S₁/S₂, O/O_I/O_OBL/O_A/O_L, C_S/C_O/C_L, V, A/A_C) yields a **closed 13-pattern inventory** of basic sentence patterns, each tied to a verb/adjective subclass — a systematic cross-tabulation not found in pedagogical Korean grammars, which teach patterns example-by-example rather than as a closed system.

### 12. Formal adjective/verb diagnostics, copular sentences, and the four adjectival-sentence subtypes (§5.1.1, pp. 72–76)

Three starred-ungrammatical tests separate adjectives from verbs on the same root-final shape: rejection of present-tense *-n(un)-ta*, rejection of imperative/propositive endings, and rejection of the progressive/perfective construction. The copula links subject to complement in fixed A-B order (identity/membership/subset); reversal changes or breaks the meaning; a copula+NP can take a "descriptive use" modified by a degree adverb. Four adjective subtypes by argument structure: **descriptive** (one argument), **transitive** (double-nominative, part-whole or idiomatic), **emotive** (double-subject, person-restricted in present tense, with a stative/nonstative *-hata*-derived counterpart — though some emotive adjectives are unrestricted intransitives instead), **existential** (locative complement, fixed word order).

### 13. Verbal sentences: valency subtypes and order-dependent meaning (§5.1.2, pp. 76–82)

Intransitives split complete vs. incomplete (SCV↔CSV reordering swaps subject/complement assignment, paralleling the copula's A-B/B-A contrast). Transitive *(l)ul* marking doesn't always signal a true patient (purpose NPs can't passivize); a dative/goal NP can surface with *(l)ul* purely for discourse focus. Ditransitives split dative vs. causative by the verb's meaning. **Pseudo-intransitive** verbs take an oblique *(l)ul*-marked NP restricted to specific roles (path/destination/purpose/duration/distance/body-part/cognate-object). **Double-object** constructions occur with body-part nouns and classifier-marked NPs. **Locative-transitive** verbs require an oblique location object. **Middle verbs** behave as both transitive and intransitive with no morphological change. The auxiliary ***-cwuta*** "give" adds a goal/beneficiary argument to any main verb.

### 14. Sentence types: Declaratives, Interrogatives, Imperatives, Propositives, minor types (§5.2, pp. 82–93)

Four major sentence types realize through sentence-final enders fused with speech-level marking, each with a six-level paradigm (Formal/Polite/Blunt/Familiar/Intimate/Plain — kept as an independent cross-check framing alongside `031`'s terminology, since the level-name sets don't map 1:1). A pronoun-speech-level agreement rule requires *ce* with Formal/Polite and *na* with the other four levels, under penalty of pragmatic deviance. Declaratives can function as indirect requests with no imperative marking. Wh-words are structurally ambiguous between interrogative and indefinite readings, and need not appear sentence-initially. Imperatives/propositives are restricted to verbal predicates; propositive *wuli* "we" is genuinely ambiguous inclusive/exclusive; a concessive-propositive construction (`V/A-ta-ko ha-ca/psita`) carries concession rather than joint-action force despite its morphosyntax. **Minor types:** suspectives (no Formal variant; function shifts by sentence type); exclamatives (no Formal variant; cannot be embedded under quotative -ko, unlike every major type); promissories (no Polite/Formal variant; three grammatical restrictions — 1st-person subject, verbal predicate, non-honorific addressee — contrasted with unrestricted volitional *-keyss*); and irregular sentences (bare SK verbal nouns as imperatives; bare-NP block-message register in headlines/notices).

### 15. Semantic Roles: the flagship case-to-role mapping (§5.3, pp. 92–94)

A formal grammatical-function × semantic-role × case-marker table (reproduced above in the Vocabulary section) — not present in any form in prior pedagogical sources. Agent and Theme/Patient normally map to subject/object respectively; Experiencer also surfaces as subject; all other roles surface as oblique objects via a small adverbial-particle set, including a genuine **syncretism** between the dative/goal marker and the passive-agent marker (both *-eykey*). **Converse verb pairs** (*sata*/*palta*, *cwuta*/*patta*) redistribute the Agent role onto whichever referent is chosen as grammatical subject, while the underlying Source/Goal relations persist unchanged across the pair — a precise account directly useful for analyzing how a derived slang lexicon might repurpose verb pairs.

### 16. Modification: adnominal and adverbial, word/phrase/clause levels (§5.4, pp. 94–98)

Both adnominal and adverbial modification are organized at three parallel levels (word/phrase/clause), with clause-level treatment deferred to Chapter 6. The **conjunctive-adverb taxonomy** (seven subclasses, several further sub-split — see Vocabulary table) is considerably finer-grained than any prior sibling source's five-item list. Multiple co-occurring time adverbials can stack in one sentence with genuinely free relative ordering, differing only in topic/focus effect, not truth-conditional meaning.

### 17. Word Order: the unmarked constituent template, free scrambling, and afterthought constituents (§5.5, pp. 98–99)

A preferred unmarked order — **Time > Place > Agent > Source > Goal > Instrument > Theme > Verb** — holds absent special focus/contrast. **Scrambling**: the pre-verbal core arguments of a ditransitive sentence move freely among all **six** possible permutations with no truth-conditional change — demonstrated exhaustively on one sentence. **Post-verbal/afterthought constituents**: material can be placed after the sentence-final verb as a clarifying afterthought — explicitly flagged by the source as real but non-standard, and as having **no counterpart in English**; multiple constituents can postpose together in varying arrangements.

### 18. Negation: the three-type-syntactic + two-type-lexical typology (§5.6, pp. 100–101)

Chang frames negation as **at least three syntactic types** (*ani* general/simple, *mos* inability, *malla* prohibitive — imperative/propositive-only) plus **two lexical types** (NK full-suppletion: *epsta*/*moluta*; SK negative-prefix: *pwul-*/*pi-*/*mwu-*/*mi-*) — a unified system more systematic than `018`'s piecemeal section-by-section coverage. SK negative-prefixed sentences are syntactically positive but semantically negative, freely paraphrasable with true syntactic negation. (Basic *ani*/*malla*/*mos* forms themselves are kept minimal, per the coverage note, since `018`/`019` already document them.)

### 19. Other negation types, scope of negation, and double negation (§5.6.4–5.6.6, pp. 104–110)

Two idiomatic negation strategies: `V-l swu epsta/issta` and `V/A-n kes-i anita/-ita` (the latter's positive form doubling as a future-tense substitute). **Scope of negation** (the chapter's most structurally important finding): unfocused negation takes the whole proposition as scope, interchangeable with the cleft-negation construction; a focused element narrows negation's scope to just that element, with the sentence implicating an affirmative-with-substituted-focus. The universal quantifier *ta* 'all' co-occurring with negation is **genuinely ambiguous** between wide-scope-negation and wide-scope-quantifier readings, **resolved by topic-marking**. Focus particles *to/man/mace* each carry distinct implicatures once combined with negation; *amwu*+*to* gives total negation, occurring exclusively in negative sentences. **Double negation**: four templated patterns, always requiring a long-form outer negative, overall **positive** in meaning, with a markedness preference for short-long over long-long sequences — except in the specialized **tag-question** use, where a double-negative tag retains **negative** meaning, with the tag portion grammatically frozen in present tense regardless of the main clause's own tense.

### 20. Passives and Causatives: overview and the three-way passive typology (§5.7, pp. 110–115)

Passivization (intransitivizing) and causativization (ditransitivizing) are framed as diametrically opposite transitivity operations; "Janus-faced" verbs (*mekta*, *pota*) take both derivations from the same base, motivating a shared suffix inventory. **The key structural gap this part fills relative to prior sources**: a **three-way passive typology** — (1) suffixal `-i/-hi/-li/-ki` (closed, nonproductive, already in `018`/`019`); (2) **cita-passive**, the inchoative auxiliary *cita* attaching to a subclass of ditransitive verbs, demonstrating that passive and causative morphology can **stack** (a root first causativized, then passivized); (3) **toyta-passive**, specific to SK verbal nouns, substituting inchoative *toyta* for active *hata* — neither (2) nor (3) present in prior established files at all. A small closed class of **inherently passive verbs** (*macta*/*ipta*/*tanghata*/*patta*) carries patient-subject semantics with no derivational marking, treated as lexically rather than syntactically oriented passives even where a derived causative exists for the same root.

### 21. Causatives: the 7-variant suffix inventory and direct-vs-indirect causation (§5.7.2, pp. 115–118)

A **seven**-variant lexical causative-suffix inventory (`-i/-hi/-li/-ki/-wu/-chwu/-kwu`) — one variant (**-kwu**) larger than `018`/`019`'s previously-documented six. The productive **syntactic causative** `V-key/tolok-hata` uses two near-synonymous linkers Chang labels (after Martin) adverbative (*key*) and projective (*tolok*) — `018`/`019` document only the *key* variant. **Direct vs. indirect causation**: lexical (short-form) causatives generally encode direct causation, syntactic (long-form) causatives indirect — illustrated with a clean minimal pair (*cwuk.ita* "kill" vs. *cwuk-key-hata* "cause to die") giving a concrete, testable realization of a distinction prior sources only asserted in the abstract, directly relevant to how Korean's causative morphology could support euphemistic distancing in slang.

### 22. TAM overview: tense/mood as morphological, aspect as syntactic (§5.8, p. 118)

Tense and mood occupy fixed morphological slots in the verb's inflectional template; aspect is realized only periphrastically, never by a suffix. Exactly **two** aspects (progressive, perfective) and **two** moods (volitional, retrospective) are recognized — a deliberately narrow, syntactically-motivated inventory, with the source itself cautioning that the tense/aspect boundary is "often felt little more than a terminological convenience."

### 23. Tense: Present, Past, and the undocumented Past-Past (§5.8.1, pp. 119–123)

Present tense covers seven usage types (state/habitual/event/future-reference/past-reference/embedded-reference/performative) from one underspecified form, disambiguated mainly by adverbs. Past tense (`-ess`, historically a contraction of infinitive `-e` + *iss* "exist") carries a dual tense/aspect function "comparable to the English present perfect," with a sharp implicature contrast tied to verb semantics (arrival-type verbs implicate "still here," departure-type verbs "away") — cancelled by frequentative adverbs but, unlike English, **not** banned with definite past-time adverbials. **Past-Past** (`-ess-ess`, doubled past) — undocumented in any sibling file — denotes a more remotely/definitely completed event, most commonly with "come"/"go," carrying implicatures that directly invert the simple past's (e.g. "had been here but left").

### 24. Aspect: Progressive, Perfective, and Aspectual Verbs (§5.8.2, pp. 124–128)

**Progressive** (`V-ko issta`, productive) forces a temporary-duration reading with stative verbs (vs. the bare form's permanence) and an ongoing/durative reading with dynamic verbs; verbs of wearing are genuinely ambiguous between an action and a resultant-state reading even with disambiguating adverbs. **Perfective** (`V-e issta`, **not productive**, restricted to a closed intransitive/passive-stem class) denotes a continuing resultant state; historically most verbs that once took it were phonologically absorbed into the ordinary past-tense suffix (`e-iss → ess`), leaving only this residual class with both forms genuinely distinct — the *salta* "live" minimal pair (perfective "is alive" vs. progressive "is residing") is the cleanest evidence the aspect-construction choice alone drives the semantic split. **Aspectual verbs** (*sicakhata*/*pelita*/*twuta*/*nakata*/*-cita*, etc.) form a looser "inherently aspectual" lexical class feeding the TAM system from outside the strict two-member aspect system — a different organizing principle from `019`'s ten-way auxiliary taxonomy.

### 25. Mood: Volitional and the undocumented Retrospective (§5.8.3, pp. 128–133)

**Volitional** (`-keyss`, historically *kes* "fact" + *iss* "exist") is explicitly reclassified by the source away from "future tense" (018's own treatment) into mood, since the periphrastic `V-l kes-ita` is the more common plain-futurity choice while *-keyss* carries stronger subjective volition/prediction; with a third-person subject it shifts to "I predict/guess..."; stacks onto past/past-past forms, and layers onto the "must" construction to produce a hedged-obligation reading; occurs fully bleached of futurity meaning in the idiomatic greeting *cheum poyp-keyss-supnita* "How do you do?" **Retrospective** (`-te`, called "quite unique as a grammatical category" by the source) — **the single most novel finding in this part, undocumented anywhere else in this project's Korean corpus** — denotes the speaker's own past direct experience (or, in questions, the addressee's), cannot co-occur with present tense, switches the plain-declarative ending from *-ta* to *-la*, and carries a genuine **person restriction** (a first-person declarative or second-person interrogative subject is marked deviant, since one does not typically "observe" one's own state from outside). A further extended pattern layers retrospective onto indirect quotation itself ("it is reported that...V, as I recall").

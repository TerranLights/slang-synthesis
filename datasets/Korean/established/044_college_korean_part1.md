### Grammar points

**Standard/redundant rules (not re-explained — see `013_sounds_of_korean_pronunciation_guide.md` /
`001_basic_korean_part1.md`):** vowel and consonant letter inventory and romanization (Modified
McCune-Reischauer); the full CV syllable-block combination grid; Hangŭl calligraphy sample;
positional voicing of plain stops ㄱㄷㅂㅈ (voiced between vowels, or after ㄴㅁㅇㄹ before a vowel);
syllable-final neutralization of ㄷㅌㅈㅊㅅㅆ → [t̚], ㄱㅋㄲ → [k̚], ㅂㅍ → [p̚]; ㅎ going silent before a
vowel; aspiration of ㄱㄷㅂㅈ adjacent to ㅎ in either order; nasal assimilation of ㄱㄷㅂ to [ŋ][n][m]
before ㄴ/ㅁ; ㄹ → [n] after a non-ㄴ consonant; ㄴ → [l] adjacent to ㄹ; vowel-sequence contraction in
casual spoken forms (오+아→와, 우+어→워, 이+어→여, 았/었+어→셨).

**Worth keeping — batchim (final-consonant-cluster) reduction, fuller paradigm.** The book gives a
genuinely systematic 6-pattern table for which of the 11 possible consonant-cluster batchim reduce
to which single sound in isolation/before a consonant (both consonants surface only when a vowel
follows):
- ㄳ, ㄺ → [k̚] (e.g. 삯 "wage", 닭 "chicken")
- ㄼ, ㅄ → [p̚] (e.g. 값 "price"; **but** 밟다 "to step on" is a lexical exception within the ㄼ set —
  most ㄼ words reduce to [l], 밟다 reduces to [p̚] instead)
- ㄻ → [m] (e.g. 닮다 "to resemble", 젊다 "to be young")
- ㄵ → [n] (e.g. 앉다 "to sit down")
- ㄾ → [l] (e.g. 핥다 "to lick")
- ㄶ, ㅀ → [n] / [l] respectively, and additionally aspirate a following plain obstruent (e.g. 많고
  "there are many and" → [mankho]; 끓고 "it is boiling and" → [kkŭlkho])

**Worth keeping — lexical irregularity caveat on obstruent-stem tensification.** The book's rule 6
(ㄱㄷㅂㅅㅈ become tense after another obstruent-final stem, e.g. 학교→[hak'kkyo]) adds the explicit
caveat that "in some words they become tense after [ㄴㄹㅁㅇㅎ] as well" — i.e. this tensification is
not purely phonologically predictable from the preceding consonant class alone; some stems take it
lexically. Most introductory treatments state the environment as a clean phonological rule without
this lexical-irregularity flag.

**Worth keeping — word-internal exception to palatalization.** Rule 9 states ㄷ/ㅌ → [j]/[ch] before
이 or a palatal glide vowel (여/야/요/유), e.g. 맏이→[maji] "the first-born." The book explicitly notes
this palatalization does **not** apply word-internally within a single morpheme — 어디 "where", 잔디
"lawn", and 티 "dust particle" keep their plain ㄷ/ㅌ pronunciation. This morpheme-boundary-vs-
word-internal distinction (the rule only crosses a stem+suffix boundary) is a clarification not every
beginner-level treatment states this explicitly.

**Worth keeping — register ladder on a single stem.** The book illustrates four inflected forms of
좋다 "to be good" across a visible register range in one place: 좋아! (plain/casual exclamation),
좋지? (casual tag-question "isn't it nice?"), 좋소! (하오체, a formal-but-now-somewhat-dated ending),
and 좋습니다 (합쇼체, standard formal-polite). Seeing all four of one verb's register forms side by
side — rather than the -습니다/-아요 pair usually shown — is a modestly useful register data point,
particularly the inclusion of the increasingly archaic -소 ending.

### Demonstrative system 이-/그-/저- (Lesson 2, GN1, p.28-29)
Korean's three-way demonstrative distinguishes not just distance but *visibility and shared
knowledge*: 이- marks an item close to the speaker; 저- marks an item away from both speaker and
addressee; 그- marks an item close to the *addressee* — but critically, the book notes 그- is also
used for something merely *known to both parties*, even without being visible at all, functioning
like English "the." 이- and 저- are restricted to items visible to both interlocutors; 그- is not.
This is a sharper visibility/common-ground distinction than a simple "this/that/that-over-there"
gloss conveys. Combines with 것 "thing" (이것/그것/저것) and with 곳 "place" (이곳에/그곳에/저곳에,
more formal) or 여기/거기/저기 (more colloquial — see below).

### Location-word (relator noun) system: 앞/옆/뒤/위/아래(밑)/속(안) (Lesson 3, GN3, p.36)
These are nouns, not postpositions, and always *follow* their point of reference (reference-noun +
location-word), e.g. 책상 위 "the top of the desk," 가방 속 "the inside of the bag," 의자 옆 "beside
the chair," 학교 앞 "the front of the school." The book pairs near-synonyms explicitly: 아래 and 밑
both "below/under"; 속 and 안 both "inside." (The location marker -에 that then attaches is itself
redundant/skipped per the list above — the relator-noun inventory and ordering rule is the new
content.)

### Postposition -와/-과 and -하고, both "and" (Lesson 3, GN4, p.36)
Connects two or more nouns/noun phrases, never two sentences. -와 follows a vowel-final noun, -과 a
consonant-final noun (opposite-environment pairing from the subject-marker pair, which the source
flags as a point students often confuse). -하고 is explicitly marked as the more *colloquial*
register alternative to -와/-과 — an explicit register annotation, not just a free variant.

### Postposition -도 "also/too" (Lesson 3, GN5, p.37)
-도 *replaces* the subject marker -이/-가, the topic marker -은/-는, and the object marker -을/-를
outright when attached to a noun — but with the location marker -에, -도 instead *follows* it
(에도), rather than replacing it. In a negative sentence, -도 shifts meaning to "not...either" (e.g.
책상 위에 연필도 없습니다. "There isn't a pencil on the desk either.").

### Location pronouns 여기/거기/저기 vs. 이곳에/그곳에/저곳에 (Lesson 3, GN7, p.38)
Two parallel sets exist for "here/there/over there": the more formal/explicit 이 곳에/그 곳에/저
곳에 (demonstrative + 곳 "place" + obligatory -에), and the more colloquial 여기/거기/저기, for which
-에 is *optional*. This is an explicit formal/colloquial register pairing for the same semantic
slot, not just a single fixed vocabulary item.

### Location as sentence topic: location noun + -에는 (Lesson 3, GN8, p.38)
-는 (topic marker) can stack directly after the location marker -에, making the *location itself*
the sentence topic rather than the located item, e.g. 체육관 앞에는 무엇이 있습니까? "As for in front
of the gymnasium, what is there?" A combinatorial/stacking pattern worth keeping even though -는
itself is on the skip list, since the -에는 stacking behavior is the structural point.

### OR-questions by juxtaposition (Lesson 3, GN9, p.39)
Korean forms an "A or B?" question simply by placing two full polite-formal questions back to back
with no overt disjunction particle, e.g. 학생입니까, 선생입니까? "Are you a student or a teacher?";
이것이 책입니까, 공책입니까? "Is this a book or a notebook?" No disjunctive conjunction word is used.

### Culturally distinctive: split leave-taking formula (Lesson 1, Vocabulary, p.19-20)
The book marks 안녕히 가십시오 and 안녕히 계십시오 — both glossable as "good-bye" — as *not*
interchangeable: the former is said *to* the person leaving (by the person staying), the latter is
said *to* the person staying (by the person leaving). The closing dialogue exchange on p.20
illustrates the correct reciprocal pairing (이선생, who is leaving, says 안녕히 가십시오 to 김선생 who
stays; 김선생 replies 안녕히 가십시오 back — note both speakers in that particular exchange are
leaving the conversation together in this case, each telling the other to go well).

### Minor note: four-way speech-style taxonomy named (not elaborated) (Lesson 1, GN1c, p.23)
The book names four basic Korean speech styles — polite formal, polite informal, plain, and
intimate — determined by the social relationship between speaker and addressee. Only the first two
are actually used/drilled in these three lessons (and polite-formal endings are skipped above as
redundant); plain and intimate styles are named but not introduced with forms in this page range.
Flagged here only because the four-way taxonomy itself (vs. a simpler two-level formal/informal
framing) may be a useful supplement if not already captured elsewhere — the forms themselves are
not yet in this chunk's scope.

### Grammar points

### Kept (new or deeper than prior extractions)

**1. Second-person terms of address (L4 GN1).** Korean frequently omits the second-person pronoun entirely;
when an explicit "you" form is used, Korean prefers the addressee's occupation/title/role rather than a
pronoun — e.g. 학생 ("student") in 학생은 이름이 무엇입니까? addresses "you" as "[you who are a] student," not
a third party. 선생님 ("teacher") is used the same way as a deferential "you." Demonstratives (이/그/저 +
학생) must be added to unambiguously mean third-person "that student" instead. The intimate 2nd-person
pronoun 너 is reserved for close friends or children and is flagged as genuinely rude if misapplied.

**2. Humble first-person forms 저/제 (L4 GN2).** 저 pairs with the topic marker -는 ("I," humble, topic); 제
pairs with the subject marker -가 ("I," humble, subject); 제 followed directly by a noun also serves as the
humble possessive "my" (제 이름은 ... "My name is ..."). This is a 3-way distributional contrast beyond simply
"저/제 both mean I."

**3. Omission of pronouns, discourse-level (L4 GN3).** Subject/topic/object — including 1st- and 2nd-person
pronouns specifically — are routinely dropped once recoverable from context; the source frames this as a
pervasive discourse convention the learner should expect throughout the book, not a one-off ellipsis rule.

**4. Descriptive verbs vs. action verbs (L4 GN7).** The two verb classes share the full range of endings but
diverge in the plain-style present tense: action verbs take -ㄴ다/-는다 (e.g. 공부한다 "I study," 다닌다 "I
attend," 읽는다 "I read") while descriptive verbs take bare -다, identical to the dictionary/citation form
(e.g. 재미있다 "it is fun," 어렵다 "it is difficult," 춥다 "it is cold"). The source gives full plain /
polite-formal / polite-formal-question paradigms for both classes side by side.

**5. Possessive marker -의 "of," "-'s" (L5 GN6).** Attaches to a possessor noun, which always precedes the
possessed noun (존의 모자 "John's hat"). Phonological note: this -의 is pronounced [에], not [의] — a specific
pronunciation detail not simply implied by the general existence of the particle.

**6. Dependent (bound) noun -것 "thing/one's" (L5 GN7).** 것 cannot stand alone; it must be preceded by a
demonstrative (이것/그것/저것), the possessive marker 의 (누구의 것 "whose," 친구의 것 "friend's"), or another
modifier. The source explicitly names this as the grammatical category "dependent noun" — a structural point
about Korean's bound-noun system, not just a vocabulary item.

**7. Full personal-pronoun paradigm, 1st/2nd/3rd person × singular/plural × humble/plain/honorific (L5
GN3).** A systematic table gives pronoun, possessive, and possessive-noun ("mine"/"yours"/etc.) forms for
every cell: 1sg 나(plain)/저(humble); 2sg 너(plain)/선생님(hon., i.e. a title fills the honorific-pronoun
slot); 3sg 그사람(plain)/그분(hon.); 1pl 우리(들)/저희(들); 2pl 너희들/선생님들; 3pl 그(사람)들/그분들. This is a
fuller, more systematically cross-tabulated paradigm (possessive and possessive-noun columns included) than a
bare pronoun list, and is kept per the "fuller/more systematic paradigm table" exception.

**8. Demonstrative/interrogative case-marked paradigm (L5 GN1–2).** Two linked tables: (a) 이분/이사람/이것/여기
(honorific-person / plain-person / thing / place), repeated for 그-/저- series; (b) the same four series
case-marked as subject/direct-object/possessive for the interrogatives 누구 "who," 무엇 "what," 어디 "where"
(e.g. 누가/누구를/누구의; 무엇이/무엇을/무엇의; 어디가/어디를~어디에/어디의). Includes the irregularity that 이다
"to be" does not take the subject marker after 누구 (저분이 누구십니까?, not 누구가), unlike its regular behavior
elsewhere — a specific exception layered on top of the (skip-listed) base copula.

**9. Plural marker -들 "-(e)s" (L5 GN4).** Optional, not obligatory the way English plurals are; used mainly
for emphasis/clarity with personal nouns and pronouns (동생들, 우리들, 그분들). 학생들이 많다 and 학생이 많다 are
both given as valid ways to say "there are many students" — the singular/plural distinction is often left to
context rather than marked.

**10. ㄹ-irregular verb stem class (L6 GN1).** All verb stems ending in ㄹ drop that ㄹ before endings
beginning with ㄴ, ㅂ, or ㅅ (e.g. 살다→삽니다, 사십니까, but retain it before -고/-겠/-지/-면: 살고, 살겠다).
Full paradigm table given for 살다, 놀다, 팔다, 알다, 들다. This is a verb-stem morphophonological class, not a
particle or speech-level point, and not on the skip list.

**11. Honorific subject marker -께서 (L6 GN2).** Attaches to a personal noun to mark deference toward the
subject when that subject outranks the speaker (아버지께서 일하십니다 vs. plain-subject 동생이 일합니다). Can
co-occur with the topic marker -는 (아버지께서는), but — unlike -께서 — the plain subject marker -이/가 cannot
co-occur with -는 at the same time. This is distinct morphology layered above the (skip-listed) base
subject-particle system and above the (skip-listed) verb-suffix honorific -시-: -께서 marks the noun phrase
itself, not the verb.

**12. Honorific lexical suppletion (L6 GN3).** A closed set of nouns and verbs are inherently/lexically
honorific rather than honorific-by-suffix: 밥→진지 (meal), 나이→연세 (age), 이름→성함 (name), 있다→계시다 (exist/
stay), 자다→주무시다 (sleep), 먹다→잡수시다 (eat). This is a distinct honorification mechanism (whole-word
substitution) from both the skip-listed -시- verb suffix and the -께서 particle above, and is a fuller/more
complete suppletive-pair table than a bare mention of "there are honorific verbs."

**13. Coordinate connective -고 vs. conjunction 그리고 (L6 GN4).** -고 attaches directly to the first clause's
verb stem to coordinate two clauses within one sentence ("A -고 B" = "A, and B": 공부하고 ... 일합니다). 그리고
instead opens a new, independent sentence as "And (then) ..." when the two clauses are written as separate
sentences. The source notes this clause-final-vs.-sentence-initial doubling is a general pattern across most
Korean connectives, not unique to 고/그리고.

**14. Negative 안 "not" and its position with noun-incorporated verbs (L6 GN5).** 안 precedes the verb (저는
학교에 안 갑니다). Specific nuance: with noun + 하다 compound ("noun-incorporated action") verbs such as
일하다, 공부하다, 말하다, 안 is inserted between the noun and 하다 (일 안 합니다), not before the whole compound
(*안 일합니다 is marked ungrammatical). The source also cross-references -지 않다 as the more common negative
for descriptive verbs (pointer to L13, not itself extracted here).

### Skipped as redundant with 001/002/035–037 (per coordinator's pre-vetted list)

Subject particle 이/가, topic particle 은/는, object particle 을/를; location/direction particles -에
"to"/-에서 "at, in, on" (L4 GN4–5; base paradigm only — appear routinely in example sentences throughout this
range but add no new rule); copula 이다/아니다 base paradigm, including the "저분이 누구(이)십니까?" construction's
base copula behavior and the note that (이) is usually dropped after a vowel-final noun, and that (의) is
usually dropped in speech (both are phonological-elision footnotes on an already-fully-documented copula/

### Grammar points

### Kept as genuinely new / deeper content

**1. Date-reading word order (Lesson 7).** Korean reads dates year→month→day (reverse of English
"month day, year"), and critically the word -년 "year" can never be dropped the way English speakers
drop "nineteen-eighty-six" without saying "year." Example given: 1986 → 천구백팔십육년 (literally
"1,000-900-80-6-year"). July 1, 1955 → 천구백오십오년 칠월 일일.

**2. Yes/no answers to negative questions (Lesson 7, GN3).** Korean 네/아니요 answer logic is inverted
relative to English whenever the question itself is negative: 네/예 affirms the negative premise
("Yes, that's correct [that I am not X]"), 아니요 denies it ("No, that's not correct [I am X]"). E.g.
"학생이 아니십니까?" (Aren't you a student?) → 아니요, 학생입니다 ("No" = yes I am) / 네, 학생이 아닙니다
("Yes" = no I'm not).

**3. Question-word distributional rules (Lesson 7, GN4).** 어느 "which" must always be followed by a
noun (어느것, 어느분, 어느 나라). 무슨 "what kind of/which" likewise always takes a following noun (무슨
일, 무슨 책). 왜 "why" is positionally flexible — it can appear sentence-initially or immediately before
the verb, both attested as natural: 왜 브라운 선생은 오늘 안 나왔어요? / 브라운 선생은 오늘 왜 안 나왔어요?

**4. -았/-었 vowel-contraction table (Lesson 7, GN2).** Beyond the basic 아/오-stem-vs-other-stem rule
(skip-listed), the source gives a full table of the specific vowel contractions that occur when the
past-tense marker meets a vowel-final stem: 아+아→아, 오+아→와, 우+어→워, 어+어→어, 으+어→어, 으+아→아,
이+어→여, 애+어→애 (optional). Also notes the honorific+past contraction (으)시+었→셨 (e.g. 하셨습니다)
has become the standard way of forming honorific past, not just an optional shortening.

**5. Postposition -(으)로 "as, in the role of" (Lesson 7, GN7).** -로 after a vowel/ㄹ-final noun,
-으로 after other consonant-final nouns, marking a role/capacity ("I work as a reporter" 기자로
일합니다; "went abroad as a peace delegate" 평화사절로 외국에 갔습니다). Distinct from the skip-listed
basic particles.

**6. Irregular 르-stem verbs (Lesson 8, GN1).** Verb stems ending in -르 double to -ㄹ르 before a
vowel-initial ending (어/아), but do NOT change before -고. Full paradigm given for 부르다 (to call),
모르다 (to not know), 다르다 (to be different), 흐르다 (to flow): e.g. 부르다 → 불렀어요 (past) / 불러서
(because) / 불러요 (polite informal) / 부르고 (and, unchanged).

**7. Register note on speech-level choice (Lesson 8, GN2 — kept despite -어/아요 itself being
skip-listed as redundant).** The source explicitly states men tend to use the polite-formal style
(-습니다) more than women, and women use polite-informal (-어/아요) more than men — an explicit,
source-marked sociolinguistic/register claim, not inferred.

**8. Future-tense marker -겠- "will/shall" (Lesson 8, GN3).** Attaches after the verb stem (and after
the honorific -시- if present) and before the sentence-ending. Primary sense is simple future, but can
also convey intention/volition. Position: [stem]-[honorific]-겠-[ending], e.g. 이 책을 전해 주시겠어요?

**9. Negation 안 vs. 못 (Lesson 8, GN5).** Both precede the verb they negate. 안 = simple negation
("not/do not"). 못 = inability ("cannot/not able to") — e.g. 아파서 못 나왔어요 "(I) couldn't come
because (I) was sick." Source explicitly notes 못 is NOT used with 이다 or with descriptive verbs
(adjectives) — for those, -지 못하다 is used instead (cross-ref to L13 GN4).

**10. Causal connective -어서/아서 "because, since" (Lesson 8, GN6).** Links two clauses; the first
clause's tense is NOT independently marked — it's inferred from the second clause's tense (저는
아파서 공부를 못했어요 = "Because I *was* sick, I couldn't study," even though 아파서 carries no past
marker itself). Full attachment-rule table given, parallel to but more detailed than the past-tense
rule: -아서 after 아/오-stems (자다→자서, 오다→와서, 보다→봐서 optional), -어서 after other-vowel stems
(먹다→먹어서, 쓰다→써서, 두다→둬서 optional, 주다→줘서 optional), and -아서 specifically for stems with
an 아+으 or 오+으 vowel sequence (아프다→아파서, 바쁘다→바빠서, 빠르다→빨라서*, 모르다→몰라서*, 오르다→
올라서*, 고르다→골라서* — the starred forms doubling the ㄹ per the 르-irregular rule above). The
source explicitly flags that this same -아/-어 choice principle is the SAME underlying rule governing
past tense, polite-informal endings, and this connective — a unifying cross-reference the book makes
explicit rather than leaving implicit.

**11. Honorific dative -께 (Lesson 8, GN7b — kept though -에게/-한테 themselves are skip-listed).**
-께 marks "to" an honorific-status person (어머님께 카드를 보내요 "I am sending a card to my mother").
Register note: the source states -한테 is more colloquial than -에게 for the plain (non-honorific)
form.

**12. Benefactive two-word verbs -어/아 주다 and -어/아 드리다 (Lesson 8, GN8).** -어/아 주다 attached to
an action verb marks the action as done for someone's benefit (나는 책을 읽었다 "I read the book" vs.
나는 책을 읽어 주었다 "I read the book [for someone]"). -어/아 드리다 is the humble variant used when the
beneficiary is an honorific-status person (할아버님께 책을 읽어드려요 "I am reading a book to my
grandfather").

**13. Concurrent connective -고 "while —ing" (Lesson 8, GN9 — distinct sense from the skip-listed
progressive -고 있다).** Beyond its basic "and" meaning, -고 attached to a first-clause verb can mark
an action concurrent with the second clause's action: 책을 보고 읽으십시오 "read the book while looking
at it"; 버스를 타고 왔어요 "I came by bus" (lit. "came riding the bus"); 한국말을 모르고 한국에 갔다 "I
went to Korea without knowing Korean."

**14. Polite command endings -(으)십시오 vs. -(으)세요 (Lesson 8, GN10).** -(으)십시오 is the politest
request form, paired with the 습니다 speech level; -(으)세요 is the polite-informal request form,
paired with the -어/아요 speech level. Both are honorific. Full paradigm given for 읽다/가다/쓰다/보다.

**15. Postposition -와/과 같이 and -하고 (같이) "together with" (Lesson 9, GN2).** -와/과 같이 after a
consonant/vowel-final noun respectively, or -하고 (같이) as an alternative. Explicit register note:
the source states 하고 is more colloquial than 와/과.

**16. Pragmatics of 모르겠어요 vs. 몰라요; 아시겠어요? (Lesson 9, GN3).** The source explicitly states
모르겠어요 ("I don't know/I'm not sure/I don't understand," context-dependent) is commonly used without
causing offense, while the blunter 몰라요 (also "I don't know") "might seem rude" by contrast — an
explicit politeness/register claim about near-synonyms. 아시겠어요? "Do you know/understand?" is
likewise glossed as functionally closer to "Would you know...?" / "Would you understand...?" given its
politeness marking.

**17. Tentative/hypothetical -겠- for "would be nice" / "I would like" (Lesson 9, GN4).** -겠- attached
to 좋다 produces a soft, hedged preference statement rather than a flat future tense: 저는 금요일이
좋겠어요, literally "As for me, Friday would be nice," functioning as "I'd like Friday."

**18. Pragmatics of 괜찮아요 "it's all right / it's OK" (Lesson 9, GN5).** The source explicitly
characterizes this as a mild, non-enthusiastic positive answer to "how about...?" questions —
contrasted explicitly with an enthusiastic "Yes, it is good!" This is an affect/register distinction
the book itself draws, not an inference.

### Grammar points

### Lesson 10

**Kept (not on skip-list):**

1. **Concessive connective -지만 "even though," "although," "but"** (p.103). Attaches to the verb stem including tense markers of the first clause. 실례(이)지만 지금 몇시(이)에요? "Excuse me, but what time is it now?" 약속이 있었지만 못 갔어요. "Although I had an appointment, I could not go."

2. **Question word 몇- "how many?"** (p.104). Used when the answer requires a number; combines with a following noun: 몇 시 "what time," 몇 개 "how many items," 몇 사람 "how many people," 며칠 "how many days," 몇 일 "what date," 몇 살 "how old," 몇 월 "what month," 몇 년 "what year," 몇 장 "how many sheets." Source explicitly flags a spelling distinction between 며칠 ("how many days") and 몇일 ("what date") — these look similar but are different lexical items.

4. **Dependent noun -때 "time," "when," "during"** (p.106). A "dependent noun" (cannot stand alone) that follows a time noun: 점심 때 "lunchtime," 세 살 때 "when (I) was three," 방학 때 "during vacation." Footnoted that 때 used as an independent noun instead means "opportunity/chance/season."

5. **Sentence connective -(으)러 "in order to," "for," "for the sake of"** (p.106–107). Attached to a verb stem, indicates purpose/goal, and is restricted to directional-motion verbs (가다 "go," 오다 "come," 다니다 "attend"). -러 after a vowel-or-ㄹ-final stem, -으러 after all other stems. 영어를 배우러 미국에 가겠어요. "I will go to the U.S. to learn English."

6. **Nominalizer v. stem + 기** (p.107). Turns a verb into a (verbal) noun, used as subject of descriptive verbs ("difficult," "easy," "interesting," "good") or object of action verbs like "begin." 한글은 쓰기(가) 어렵다. "Korean is difficult to write." This nominalizer recurs structurally throughout L10–L12 (feeds into -기 때문에 "because," -기 시작하다 "begin to," -기로 하다 "decide to," -어/아 보다 combinations).

**Skipped as redundant with 001/002/035–037:**
- Grammar point 3, "Telling time" (hour-in-Korean-numbers, minute/second-in-Sino-Korean-numbers, the standard 시/분 pattern) — standard pattern, already exhaustively documented, especially in 037's dedicated time-expression unit. Two finer sub-points were still worth flagging rather than silently dropping: the optional "exactly" marker 정각 (captured as a vocabulary row above) and the "before the hour" pattern using 전 (e.g. 네시 오분 전 "5 minutes before 4," 아홉시 오십분 전 = 열시 십분 전 "10 minutes before 10").
- Grammar point 7, "Korean numbers and noun counters" (native 1–99 vs. Sino-Korean systems, and counters -살/-개/-사람) — standard native/Sino-Korean number system content, on skip list.

### Lesson 11

**Kept (not on skip-list):**

1. **Verbal modifiers (relative-clause equivalents)** (p.115–116). Korean has no relative pronouns; verb forms modify nouns directly, generally preceding the modified noun, with distinct present/continuous and past forms.
   - **a. Action-verb modifiers -는 (present/continuous) and -ㄴ/은 (past).** 노는 날 "playing day (holiday)" vs. 논 날 "rested day (day off, past)." 일하는 학생 "working student" vs. 일한 학생 "student who worked."
   - **b. Descriptive-verb modifiers -ㄴ/은** (present and past forms identical for descriptive verbs; -는 is never used with descriptive verbs). 시원한 날씨 "cool weather," 맑은 공기 "clear air."
   - A reference paradigm ("Verb chart 3," p.121–122) tabulates present/past verbal-modifier forms for ~24 action verbs and ~17 descriptive verbs, including irregulars (놀다→노는/논; 듣다→듣는/들은; color-term ㅎ-deleting adjectives 빨갛다→빨간 etc., footnoted to later lessons).

2. **Sentence endings: plain-style statements** (p.117–118). A third speech style beyond polite-formal/polite-informal, used among children, between close social equals, by a superior to an inferior, and in essays/reports/journals/diaries.
   - **Present:** -다 after a descriptive-verb stem (이것은 크다. "This is big."); -ㄴ다 after an action-verb stem ending in a vowel (우리는 학교에 간다. "We go to school."); -는다 after an action-verb stem ending in a consonant (우리는 책을 읽는다. "We read books."). 하다 "to do" itself takes -하다 (descriptive) vs. -한다 (action).
   - **Past:** -았다/-었다 follow the same 아/어 vowel-harmony rule as elsewhere, with -ㅆ다 after a vowel-contracted stem; unlike the present tense, the past does NOT distinguish descriptive from action verbs.
   - **Honorific plain statements**: -(으)신다 (present) / -(으)셨다 (past) — 부모님은 소풍을 가신다/가셨다. "My parents are going/went on a picnic." This is a distinct register layer, not simply the honorific -시- + plain past already covered elsewhere, because it is specifically the plain-style sentence-final combination.

3. **Irregular ㄷ/ㄹ alternating verbs** (p.118–119). In some verbs with a ㄷ-final stem, ㄷ changes to ㄹ before a vowel-initial suffix (-었/았, -으시, -으면, -으러); ㄷ is retained before a consonant-initial suffix (-기, -겠, -는). Paradigm given for 듣다 "to hear" (들은/들었다/들으시/들으면/들으러 vs. 듣기/듣겠다/듣는), 걷다 "to walk," 묻다 "to ask." Non-alternating ㄷ-stem verbs explicitly named: 받다 "receive," 얻다 "obtain," 닫다 "close."

4. **Construction of -기 시작하다 "begin to," "start to"** (p.119). 시작하다 "to begin" is always preceded by the -기 nominalizer when not preceded by a noun. 우리는 걷기 시작했다. "We began to walk."

5. **Expression for "after doing..." -ㄴ/은 후(에)** (p.119–120). 후 "after" is always used with the past-tense verbal modifier -ㄴ/은, even when the event described is in the future. 소풍에서 돌아온 후에 한 시간 동안 쉬겠다. "After I come back from the picnic, I will rest for an hour." 후 can also follow a bare noun/noun phrase for "after [noun]": 열두시 후에 "after 12 o'clock," 이년 후에 "in two years" (lit. "after two years").

6. Hanja: 山, 市, 外, 大, 門, 間 (see Hanja table above).

### Lesson 12

**Kept (not on skip-list) — ten grammar points, a notably dense lesson:**

1. **Construction of v. stem + 기로 하다 "to decide to," "to plan to"** (p.128). Usually used in the past-tense form -기로 했다. 우리는 동양 박물관을 보기로 했다. "We planned to see the Oriental Museum."

2. **Reported speech** (p.128–130). Indicates the statement is reported/hearsay information rather than the speaker's own firsthand knowledge.
   - **a. -(이)라고 하다** "They say that something/someone is..." — used after a sentence ending in 이다 "to be," present tense only. 그것이 아세아 미술관이라고 한다. "They say that it is the Asian Art Museum."
   - **b. -다고 하다** "It is said that..." — used after other verbs, and after 이다 in all tenses but the present (since present-tense 이다 uses -(이)라고 instead). 그 사람은 유명한 예술가이었다고 한다. "They say he was a famous artist." 공원이 여기에서 멀다고 한다. "They say the park is far from here."

3. **Constructions for "because"** (p.130–131). When 때문 follows a verb, the verb must first be nominalized with -기 (per L10 GN6).
   - **a. v. + 기 때문이다** "It is because..." 길을 몰랐기 때문이다. "It is because we did not know our way."
   - **b. n. + 때문이다** "It is because of (something)." 숙제 때문이다. "It is because of homework."
   - **c. v. + 기 때문에** "because..." 뻐스를 타기가 어려웠기 때문에 백화점에 못 갔다. "We could not go to the department store because it was difficult to ride the bus."
   - **d. n. + 때문에** "because of (something)." 안개 때문에 샌프란시스코를 구경 못 했다. "We could not sightsee in San Francisco because of the fog."

4. **Two-word verb -어/아 보다 "to try to," "to have an experience of"** (p.131–132). 보다 "to see" used as the second element of a two-word verb combination shifts meaning to "try doing"/"have had the experience of." Follows ordinary -어/아 vowel-harmony rules. 나는 뉴욕에 가 봤다. "I have been to New York." Two entries given special idiomatic glosses: 찾아 보다 "to visit" (not literally "try to find"), 물어 보다 "to ask" (not literally "try to inquire").

5. **Postposition: direction marker -(으)로 "to," "through," "toward"** (p.132). Used with motion verbs ("coming"/"going") to indicate direction. -로 after a vowel- or ㄹ-final noun, -으로 otherwise; often combined with -쪽 ("동쪽으로 가세요" "please go east"). 어디로 "(to) where...?" is the standard way to ask a bus/train's destination or "where are you heading."

6. **Postposition -까지 "to," "up to," "until," "by"** (p.132–133). Attached to a noun/noun phrase, indicates extent, for both time and place. Frequently paired with -에서 and/or -부터 to mean "from...to." 공원에서 박물관까지 멀어요? "Is it far from the park to the museum?"

7. **Postposition -만 "only," "just"** (p.133–134). Limits the predicate's effect to the marked noun/noun phrase/verbal noun. Unlike most postpositions, -만 does not have to replace the subject/topic/object markers — but when combined with them, -만 precedes -이/-은/-을 (동양화만이, 서양화만은, 우리만이, 예술품만을). -만 also follows other postpositions directly: -에게/께만, -(으)로만, -에만. Minimal-pair contrast given: 나만 동양 박물관에 갔다. "Only I went to the Oriental Museum" vs. 나는 동양 박물관에만 갔다. "I went only to the Oriental Museum (not elsewhere)."

8. **Conditional connective -(으)면 "if," "when"** (p.134–135). Constructs a conditional clause, which must precede the main clause. -면 after a vowel- or ㄹ-final verb, -으면 otherwise. 길을 모르면 물어 보세요. "If you don't know your way, please ask."

9. **Points of the compass** (p.135). 동(東) east, 서(西) west, 남(南) south, 북(北) north; compound directions always follow E-W-S-N order: 동북 NE, 서북 NW, 동남 SE, 서남 SW. -쪽 suffix ("side, -ward") attaches to direction words: 동쪽, 서쪽, 오른쪽/바른쪽, 왼쪽, 이쪽, 그쪽, 저쪽.

10. Hanja: 東, 西, 南, 北, 洋, 有, 名 (see Hanja table above).

polite-formal/-informal speech-level endings, native and Sino-Korean number systems and

### Kept — genuinely new or deeper content in this range

**1. Sentence ending -지요/-지 (polite-informal / plain-intimate)** (Lesson 13, Grammar pt 1)
Used for (a) a *confirming statement* (level/falling intonation, in response to a question —
and for emphatic agreement, e.g. 물론이지요! "Of course!"); (b) a *tentative statement*
(level/falling intonation, takes -겠 before -지(요) for conjecture: "I presume/suppose"); (c) a
*casual question* (rising intonation, functions like an English tag question — "...isn't it?" —
or an afterthought question "by the way...?", and can carry a sense of polite request); (d) a
*propositive statement* ("let's...", level/falling intonation). Marks a degree of intimacy
between speaker and addressee; intonation (as with -어/아요) carries the functional distinction.

**2. Postposition -마다 "each, every"** (Lesson 13, Grammar pt 2)
Attaches directly after a noun/noun phrase (계절마다, 날마다/매일, 주(일)마다/매주,
달마다/매월, 해마다/매년). One-syllable nouns may optionally reduplicate before -마다
(집마다 or 집집마다; 곳마다 or 곳곳마다) — reduplication is optional, not obligatory.

**3. Postposition -도...도 "both...and," "neither...nor," "as well as"** (Lesson 13, Grammar pt 3)
Used after a noun, after a verbal noun (V.+기), or after the negative -지 ending (V.+지), and
after -에 or -으로 — but explicitly NOT after -이/-가, -은/는, or -을/를. Four attested patterns:
N+도...N+도 (봄도 여름도 좋다), V+기+도...V+기+도 (웃기도 잘 하고 울기도 잘 한다),
V+지+도...V+지+도 (오지도 않고 불지도 않는다), N+에+도...N+에+도 and N+(으)로+도...N+(으)로+도.

**4. Negative verb base -지 with 않다 "do not" / 못하다 "cannot"** (Lesson 13, Grammar pt 4)
A genuine present-tense split not present in past/future: descriptive verbs take -지 않다
(present) but action verbs take -지 않는다 (present); both collapse to -지 않았다 (past) and
-지 않겠다 (future) regardless of verb class. Same present-tense split applies to 못하다 (-지
못하다 for descriptive, -지 못한다 for action); the text notes 못하다's use with descriptive
verbs is "limited." Separately noted: -겠 carries a conjecture sense ("I think, probably, I
guess") in addition to simple future, especially with descriptive verbs.

**5. Irregular ㅂ verbs** (Lesson 13, Grammar pt 5)
Many verb stems ending in ㅂ alternate with 우 depending on what follows: before a
vowel-initial connective/suffix, ㅂ→우 (춥+으면→추우면, 춥+으세요→추우세요, 춥+어도→추워도,
춥+어서→추워서, 춥+었지만→추웠지만); before a consonant-initial connective/suffix, the stem is
unchanged (춥+지만→춥지만, 춥+고→춥고, 춥+겠다→춥겠다, 춥+다→춥다, 춥+기 때문에→춥기
때문에). The majority of descriptive ㅂ verbs alternate; only some action ㅂ verbs do — and the
split does not track the action/descriptive distinction cleanly (돕다 "to help," action, DOES
alternate; 좁다 "to be narrow," descriptive, does NOT). Alternating set given: 춥다, 덥다, 어렵다,
쉽다, 아름답다, 돕다, 뜨겁다, 즐겁다, 무섭다. Non-alternating set given: 잡다, 접다, 입다, 뽑다,
업다, 좁다.

**6. -에 따라(서) "according to, depending on" / -을/를 따라(서) "to follow"** (Lesson 14,
Grammar pt 1)
Two distinct constructions sharing the base verb 따르다. (a) N+에 따라(서) = "according to,
depending on" (학교에 따라서 학비가 다르다; 지방에 따라서 눈도 많이 온다). (b) N+을/를
따라(서) = "to follow" someone/something, literally or as in "following the warm weather"
(따뜻한 기후를 따라서 사람들이 플로리다로 간다; 저를 따라 오세요 "please follow me"; 저를
따라 읽으세요 "please read after me").

**7. Descriptive verb + 어/아지다 "to become X (by itself)," "to turn into"** (Lesson 14, Grammar
pt 2)
Converts a descriptive (stative) verb into an action verb expressing gradual change of state.
Examples: 높다→높아지다 ("to become high/tall"), 따뜻하다→따뜻해지다 ("to get warm"),
크다→커지다 ("to grow/become big"), 쉽다→쉬워지다 ("to become easy" — note this also
interacts with the irregular-ㅂ alternation above), 빨갛다→빨개지다 ("to turn/become red, to
blush"). Illustrated in context: 가을에는 하늘이 높아진다 "the sky becomes clear blue in the
fall"; 한국어는 두 달 배우면 쉬워진다 "Korean becomes easier after studying two months."

**8. Concurrent connective -(으)며 "and," "while," "at the same time," "as"** (Lesson 14, Grammar
pt 3)
Two distinct senses disambiguated by context. (a) "and" — used to avoid repeating -고 (가을에는
하늘이 푸르고 높아지며 바람도 분다). (b) "while/at the same time" — a short form of -(으)면서,
indicating two simultaneous actions (해가 나며(나면서) 비가 온다 "it rains while the sun
shines"; 이야기하며(하면서) 걸었다 "we walked as we talked"). -며 attaches to stems ending in a
vowel or ㄹ; -으며 to all other stems.

**9. Causal/sequential connective -어/아(서): deletion of 서** (Lesson 14, Grammar pt 4)
The familiar -어/아서 connective can drop 서 with no change in meaning (단풍이 들어서
경치가 아름답다 → 단풍이 들어 경치가 아름답다; 시간이 없어서 안 물어 봤다 → 시간이 없어
안 물어 봤다). As a consequence of dropping 서, stems ending in 어 or 아 may show no overt
trace of -어/아서 at all after vowel contraction (가서→가; 만나서→만나).

**10. Future verbal modifier -ㄹ/을** (Lesson 15, Grammar pt 1)
Completes the three-way relative-clause-like modifier paradigm alongside the previously
introduced present/ongoing -는 and past -ㄴ/은 (book cites its own L11, GN1 for those). Full
paradigm given for 일하다/배우다/살다/듣다: future 할 일 "work to do," 배울 것들 "things to
learn," 살 집 "house to live in," 들을 이야기 "a story (one) will hear" — contrasted against
present 하는 일/배우는 것들/사는 집/듣는 이야기 and past 한 일/배운 것들/산 집/들은 이야기.
Some -ㄹ/을-modified noun phrases are lexicalized/idiomatic: 할 일 "duty, work to do" (저는
할 일이 많아요 "I have a lot to do"), 쓸 돈 "spending money," 볼 사람 "person to see," 갈 길
"road to travel, destination" (갈 길이 멀다 "I have a long way to go").

**11. Coordinate conjunction 또는 "or," "in addition," "either...or"** (Lesson 15, Grammar pt 2)
Usable either sentence-initially (as a connective between two full sentences: "Los Angeles는
L.A.라고 한다. 또는 나성이라고 한다.") or mid-sentence linking two nouns/noun phrases/clauses
(할 일 또는 임무라고 한다; 한국말 또는 한국어라고 한다). Cultural/historical note: the example
cites 나성 as an older Sino-Korean name/transliteration for Los Angeles.

**12. Question word 얼마 "how much/how long" + indefinite-pronoun use** (Lesson 15, Grammar
pt 3)
(a) As a question word, usually combined with -나 for amount (얼마나 어려워요? "how
difficult is it?"; 얼마나 걸려요? "how long does it take?") or with 동안 for duration. Two
competing "how much is it?" expressions are distinguished: 얼마나 해요? vs. 얼마예요? — note
that -나 is never used with 이다, and that the 이 of 얼마+이에요 is dropped (→얼마예요).
(b) As an indefinite pronoun meaning roughly "some (amount/time)": 얼마동안 한자를 안
가르쳤다 "for some time they didn't teach hanja"; 공원에 사람이 얼마 없다 "there aren't many
people in the park."

**13. Dependent noun 동안 "during," "while" (duration)** (Lesson 15, Grammar pt 4)
Always preceded by a noun (N+동안: 방학 동안 "during vacation," 한 시간 동안 "for one hour,"
얼마 동안 "for a while") or a present-tense verbal modifier (V.mod+동안: 한자를 배우는 동안
"while learning hanja"). Distinguishes simultaneity of two separate subjects' actions in one
example: 내가 영어 공부하는 동안에 동생은 일본어를 공부했다 "while I studied English, my
brother studied Japanese."

**14. Consequence connective -ㄴ데/은데/는데** (Lesson 15, Grammar pt 5)
A background/contrast-introducing connective glossed as covering "but, however, for, and, so,
while, since, when, as" depending on context — its core function is to introduce a fact/event as
setup for the clause that follows. Descriptive verbs: present -ㄴ데 after a vowel-final stem
(크다→큰데) or -은데 after a consonant-final stem (많다→많은데); past is formed by adding -는데
after the past-tense marker -았/었 (크다→컸는데). Action verbs use -는데 for BOTH present and
past (가다→가는데(pres.)/갔는데(past); 읽다→읽는데(pres.)/읽었는데(past)) — unlike descriptive
verbs, there is no separate present-only form. Illustrative uses span the full range of implied
relations: concessive ("한자는 어려운데 재미있다" = "Chinese characters are difficult but
interesting"), background-setup ("집에 가는데 같이 가시겠어요?" = "I'm on my way home, would
you like to come?"), and result ("지금 가르치는데 다시 가르치기로 됐다").

**15. V.stem + 기로 되다 "it turns out that," "is supposed to"** (Lesson 15, Grammar pt 6)
Describes an event resulting from some decision — typically someone else's decision or a
circumstance external to the speaker — and is usually in the past tense (한자를 다시
가르치기로 됐다 "it's been decided Chinese characters will be taught again"; 나는 서울에
가기로 됐다 "I am supposed to go to Seoul"). The book explicitly flags a contrast with -기로
하다 (introduced L12), which expresses the subject's own decision, vs. -기로 되다's sense of an
externally-arrived-at or circumstantial outcome.

**16. Postposition -(이)나 "to the extent of," "to what extent?"** (Lesson 15, Grammar pt 7)
Normally paired with the question words 얼마- and 몇- to ask "to what extent/degree?" (한자를
몇 자쯤이나 배웠어요? "how many characters did you learn?"). Critically, when -(이)나 is used
in a *statement or response* rather than a question, it shifts meaning to express "more than the
expected amount" (이천 자나 배웠어요 "we learned 2,000 characters [=that's a lot]"; 십오
년이나 가르쳤어요 "I taught for 15 years [=a long time]") — the book explicitly contrasts
plain responses (without -이나) against this emphatic "more than expected" reading (with
-이나).

**17. Question ending -나?/-나요? "I wonder if...?"** (Lesson 15, Grammar pt 8)
Used with all verbs across present/past/future to express a wondering/open question. Full
plain/polite/honorific paradigm given: present 배우나?/배우나요?/배우시나요?; past
배웠나?/배웠나요?/배우셨나요?; future(-겠-) 배우겠나?/배우겠나요?/배우시겠나요?. Explicit
usage note: the future-tense polite form -겠나요 is rarely used in practice — speakers prefer
-을/ㄹ까요 for future-tense questions instead.

**18. Dependent noun -쯤 "about," "around," "approximately"** (Lesson 15, Grammar pt 9)
Attaches directly to a noun to indicate approximation: 두 시쯤 "about two o'clock," 저녁
때쯤 "around dinnertime," 열 사람쯤 "approximately ten people," 1마일쯤 가면 됩니다 "it will be
about one mile."

### Cultural/geographic notes
- Lesson 14's reading passage states Korea has a continental climate (대륙성 기후) with four
  clearly distinct seasons; summer brings a monsoon season (장마철) with heavy rain and
  typhoons (태풍); winter is very cold with regionally variable snowfall (지방에 따라 눈도 많이
  온다— explicitly marked as varying "depending on the region" within Korea, not a blanket
  national claim); spring brings new sprouts and blossoms; autumn brings a clear blue sky and
  fall foliage (단풍), which the book credits as making autumn scenery beautiful.
- Lesson 15's dialogue states a concrete curriculum fact about hanja education in Korean
  schools: approximately 900 characters taught in middle school (중학교) and approximately 900
  more in high school (고등학교), for a combined total of approximately 1,800 characters (중학교에서
  약 900자, 고등학교에서 900자, 합해서 1,800자쯤 배운다고 해요) — presented as the
  post-reintroduction curriculum, following a period when hanja instruction had been dropped
  and then "decided to be taught again" (다시 가르치기로 됐다, see Grammar pt 6 above).
- Lesson 15 also draws an explicit native-vs-Sino-Korean word-pair distinction as its dialogue's
  central thread: 할 일 (a native-Korean phrase, 순수한 한국말) and 임무 (a word the teacher
  states explicitly "came from Chinese," 중국어에서 나온 말) are given as two ways to say
  "duty" with the same meaning (똑 같은 뜻) — a clean textbook-internal illustration of the
  native/Sino-Korean lexical-doublet phenomenon, worth cross-referencing against any
  established file that treats Sino-Korean vocabulary layering more systematically.

# Korean — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: *Korean Advanced Course, Volume I* (Defense Language Institute, 1971) — Full Book, Units 1-20

**Source:** Defense Language Institute, *Korean Advanced Course, Volume I* (ERIC document ED055492),
July 1971, 191 PDF pages. Per the book's own preface: "the first of seven readers for continuation
training in Korean after the Basic Course." Units 1-11 are "practical situation dialogues written
especially for this course"; Units 12-20 are "articles drawn from several readers published by the
Ministry of Education of the Republic of Korea in 1970." Each unit concludes with its own
comprehension QUESTIONS and VOCABULARY list. Full table of contents:

1. Invitation to a Soccer Game (by telephone) — 2. Visiting in a Hospital — 3. Communications Media
— 4. Dialects — 5. A Dinner Party — 6. The Police System — 7. Interview with the Secretary of
Communications — 8. Interview with the President of an Industrial Bank — 9. Interview with the
Secretary of Defense — 10. Interview with the Secretary of Education — 11. Captured Guerrillas — 12.
Scenario: Lighthouse — 13. The Life of a Crow — 14. Life in South Korea — 15. Life in North Korea —
16. Buddha — 17. Confucius — 18. Socrates — 19. Jesus Christ — 20. A Christmas Carol.

**Text layer / vision-reading status:** This PDF has **no text layer and no embedded fonts at all**
(confirmed via `pdftotext`/`pdffonts` — zero output). This is a 1970s government pamphlet scanned and
lossily compressed (`Producer: CVISION Technologies PdfCompressor`), not a font-substitution-cipher
case — there is no font data to decode. **The entire book was fully vision-read** from rendered page
images (150-300dpi `pdftoppm` renders) across 5 parallel batches, each covering a contiguous unit
range. This file is the orchestrator-merged result of that 5-batch extraction; see the per-section
notes below for each batch's own detailed findings.

## Page-numbering note (confirmed empirically across the whole book)

Most body pages in this book carry **two** page numbers near the bottom: a smaller "own-book"
printed page number, and a larger continuous stamp added by the DLI compiler/ERIC microfiche process.
For Units 1-5 the two trackers hold a clean fixed relationship (`PDF_page = printed_page + 9`), but
**this offset is not reliable for the rest of the book** — it drifts upward repeatedly, confirmed
directly and independently by multiple extraction batches, because of unstamped photo-plate pages
interspersed between units (the book inserts captioned illustrative photographs — some topically
tied to the adjacent unit, some apparently just general ROK cultural/tourism images unrelated to
either neighboring unit — that do not carry their own page-number stamp). By the end of the book the
drift reaches roughly `printed_page + 19`. **Every unit boundary in this file was located by directly
reading the actual Korean Unit-number heading and cross-checking it against the book's own Table of
Contents, never by arithmetic alone** — each extraction batch independently confirmed this was
necessary when naive estimates landed inside the wrong (usually the preceding) unit. Units 12-20 (the
excerpted-reader portion) were found to **continue the same running "own-book" page numbering as
Units 1-11**, not reset per excerpt as initially anticipated.

## Coverage note — non-redundant-supplement scoping, book-wide

Korean already has 59 pre-existing established files plus siblings from this same extraction wave
(`established/060`-`069`). Every distinct item from each unit's own printed VOCABULARY box is
captured below; comprehension QUESTIONS sections were read but not separately transcribed (cross-
checked — no question introduced vocabulary beyond what each unit's own dialogue/narrative +
vocabulary list already covers). Dialogue and narrative text is paraphrased throughout, never
bulk-reproduced, per copyright discipline. Specific non-redundant-supplement findings, by unit range:

- **Unit 4 ("Dialects")** was checked against `established/048_korean_cambridge_surveys.md` (Sohn's
  Cambridge Language Surveys, which already has a systematic 7-zone academic dialectology chapter).
  Genuinely new and kept: a worked P'yŏngan-do ㅈ→ㄷ parallel-sentence example; two Hamgyŏng-do lexical
  substitutions; an explicit Seoul peddler/gangster **sociolect** (오→우 raising) in a category 048
  doesn't cover at all (register, not geography); Ch'ungch'ŏng-do 예→여 and 요/오→유/우 raising with
  worked examples; a folk-etymology note on "예" vs. "네." The governing isogloss mechanisms
  (palatalization gradient, Kyŏngsang tone) are cross-referenced to 048 rather than re-derived.
- **Units 7-10 (official interviews)** and **Unit 11 (Captured Guerrillas)** were checked against
  `established/031_korean_honorifics_and_politeness.md` (Lucien Brown's honorifics monograph). This
  institutional/formal-register content — cabinet-level interviews plus a real historical POW
  interrogation transcript — was judged atypically unlikely to be duplicated elsewhere in the corpus
  and kept in full. New data points for `031`: systematic cabinet-title+-님+께서 co-occurrence across
  four interviews; a directly-attested register-breaking/joking aside mid-formal-interview; and, most
  significantly, Unit 11's interrogation answers in **bare plain-style declaratives with zero
  honorific marking** — a concrete POW-to-authority register contrast `031` only theorized abstractly.
- **Unit 15 ("Life in North Korea")** was checked against `established/033_lets_learn_korean_northern_dialect.md`
  (a genuine DPRK-published textbook, this project's prior flagship North/South find). Confirmed
  non-redundant: Unit 15 is a *South Korean/DLI depiction of* the North, not DPRK-authored material,
  and — a meaningful negative finding — contains **zero explicit dialect/register annotation of
  actual Northern speech**. Its real value is different in kind: heavily ideologically-loaded
  Cold-War ROK political vocabulary, and one short passage that **directly reproduces actual DPRK
  state-propaganda language** as a quoted photo caption (see the Unit 15 section below) — captured
  with explicit outsider-vantage-point notes, not treated as equivalent-authority to a native DPRK
  source.
- Units 12-14, 16-20 and the supplementary Units 1-3, 5-6, 18-19 vocabulary were extracted at normal
  coverage-rule priority (every distinct vocabulary item, no deep redundancy check against a specific
  prior file since no single established file covers this book's institutional-civics /
  screenplay-scenario / fable / biographical-reader register combination).

**Vision-reading guard — marginalia, book-wide.** No handwritten marginalia (pen/pencil annotation,
underlining, filled-in answers) was found anywhere across the entire 191-page book, confirmed
independently by all 5 extraction batches. The cursive/calligraphic hand-lettering used throughout
the Korean body text and vocabulary boxes is the book's own genuine 1970s production method (a normal
way to typeset Korean before adequate movable type existed), not a prior owner's annotation. Two
scan-artifact anomalies were found and are flagged (not treated as marginalia): one page in Unit 14
was physically scanned upside-down (read and paraphrased correctly in that orientation); several
unstamped photo plates throughout the book are too degraded by the source's own lossy compression
pass to transcribe captions above `low_confidence`, and are flagged rather than silently dropped or
guessed.

items (고유어) are morphologically simple enough that an atomic gloss doesn't hide structure needed
for later mechanics analysis.

**Morpheme breakdown (agglutinative Sino-Korean compounds — illustrative selection).**

> **비무장지대** (非武裝地帶) = 비- (非, "non-/not-") + 무장 (武裝, "armament") + 지대 (地帶, "zone/belt")
> — "non-armed zone" = demilitarized zone; a transparent three-morpheme Sino-Korean compound typical
> of this register's technical vocabulary.

> **완전무장** (完全武裝) = 완전 (完全, "complete") + 무장 (武裝, "armament") — "complete armament" = full
> battle gear; 무장 recurs as the same bound root in both this entry and 비무장지대 above, demonstrating
> its productivity as a combining form across military vocabulary.

> **군사분계선** (軍事分界線) = 군사 (軍事, "military affairs") + 분계 (分界, "boundary-division") + 선
> (線, "line") — "the line dividing military [zones]" = the Military Demarcation Line.

> **일사천리** (一瀉千里) = 일 (一, "one") + 사 (瀉, "to pour/rush down") + 천리 (千里, "a thousand *li*/miles")
> — a classical-Chinese four-character idiom (사자성어), "a thousand *li* in one rush," meaning
> swift, unobstructed progress; unlike the other breakdowns here its four morphemes don't decompose
> into independently meaningful modern Korean words — it functions as a fixed literary unit.

> **제124군부대** (第124軍部隊) = 제- (第-, ordinal prefix "the ... -th") + 124 + 군 (軍, "army") + 부대
> (部隊, "unit") — "the 124th Army Unit"; demonstrates the same ordinal-prefix + number + Sino-Korean
> noun pattern used productively for any numbered military unit designation.

### Unit 15 — 북쪽 지방의 생활 (Life in the Northern Region)

**Does Unit 15 contain actual linguistic/dialectal observations about North Korean speech, as
distinct from describing North Korean society/politics/geography?** No — read in full, this unit is
entirely geographic, climatic, and resource-economic in content and never once comments on how North
Koreans *speak* differently from South Koreans. This is a meaningful negative finding: unlike
`established/033` (a native DPRK text saturated with phonological contrast data), this South-authored
unit about the North carries zero explicit dialect/register annotation of Northern speech. Its real
sociolinguistic interest is different in kind: heavily ideologically-loaded Cold-War ROK political
vocabulary embedded in ordinary narrative prose, and one short passage that directly reproduces
actual North Korean state-propaganda language as an illustrative photo caption — both captured below.

**Narrative frame and ideological register.** The lesson's narrator is a South Korean child, Yŏngman
(영만이), whose uncle (삼촌) fled south during the Korean War (6.25 사변) and explains North Korean
geography using a map. The unit's own closing line is explicitly irredentist: "...우리들은 하루
바삐 북쪽 지방을 도로 찾도록 온갖 힘을 다 기울여 힘써야 하겠습니다" (paraphrase: "we must devote
all our effort to reclaiming the northern region as soon as possible") — a direct statement of the
ROK government-line reunification-as-reclamation framing.

landscape photograph near Mt. Paektu, the textbook reproduces — in smaller print, clearly quoting
rather than composing — a short passage of actual DPRK state-cult praise-language:

> 애국심을 [...]님의 위대한 혁명정신을 대대손손 길이 전하여 조국과 더불어 영원히 솟아있을 것이다"

## Grammar / Register points

### P'yŏngan-do ㅈ→ㄷ palatalization-reversal (worked example) — Unit 4

Unit 4 (p.13) gives a full parallel sentence pair illustrating the P'yŏngan-do dialect's
non-palatalized reflex of what is palatalized (ㅈ) in standard/Central Korean: Seoul "정거장에
가니까 벌건 전기불이 번쩍번쩍 하는데 참 좋지 좋아" corresponds to P'yŏngan-do "덩거당에 가니까 벌건
던기불이 번덕번덕 하는데 탐 됴티 됴와" — 정→덩, 전기→던기, 번쩍→번덕, 좋지→됴티, 좋아→됴와. This is
the same isogloss `established/048` documents academically; this unit supplies the concrete
sentence-level illustration 048's isogloss list does not.

### Seoul peddler/gangster sociolect: 오→우 vowel raising — Unit 4

Unit 4 (p.14) notes, as an explicit **register** (not geographic-dialect) annotation: "장사치들이나
깡패들에는 '오'소리를 '우'소리로 많이 발음합니다," giving 돈→둔 (money), 좋다→죻다 (good), 한다→흔다
(do/make). A sociolect observation distinct in kind from `048`'s purely geographic dialect-zone
treatment.

### Ch'ungch'ŏng-do/Chŏlla-do: 예→여 and word-final 요/오→유/우 — Unit 4

Unit 4 (p.14) notes Ch'ungch'ŏng-do answers "예" (yes) as "여," with word-final 요/오 often raised to
유/우: "안녕하세요?"→"안녕하세유?"; "진지 잡수셨어요?"→"진지 잡수셨시유?" The source also notes, as an
aside, that the DLI-taught form "예" is itself originally a P'yŏngan-do form, contrasted with Seoul's
"네."

### Hamgyŏng-do/Kyŏngsang-do pitch/intonation note — Unit 4

Unit 4 (p.14-15) describes Hamgyŏng-do and Kyŏngsang-do speech, in lay terms, as 억세다 ("rough/
strong") with pitch that "goes up and down a lot," to the point that overheard conversation can
sound like an argument even when it is ordinary talk — a non-technical, colloquial attestation of the
same phenomenon `048` treats formally as the Kyŏngsang-dialect lexical tone system.

### Formal-interview honorific register across Units 7-10 — confirms, and modestly extends, `031`

All four officials' interviews consistently use the {P} "deferential" speech style
(-(스)ㅂ니다/-습니다) for the bulk of question-and-answer turns, mixed with {Y} "polite" -요 forms in
more conversational asides — the same {P}/{Y} alternation `031`'s Grammar point §1 already documents
for formal broadcast/public discourse. Two points genuinely extend `031`:

- **Cabinet-title + -님 + 께서 co-occurrence is systematic across all four interviews**: 장관님/총재님
  consistently paired with 께서 whenever the interviewer addresses or refers to the official in the
  3rd person (e.g. Unit 9: "모 여사께서 우리 국방부를 다 찾아주시니 웬일이십니까?"). `031` documents
  -님 and 께서 individually but lacks a worked example of a real cabinet-rank address term triggering
  both simultaneously in a sustained formal-interview genre.
- **Register-breaking within an otherwise formal interview is directly attested**: Unit 9's
  poetess-interviewer jokes with the Defense Secretary about "바람이나 나면 어떻게 해요" and teases
  him about their age gap being "막내동생벌" — real-time code-switching into colloquial/teasing
  register mid-{P}-style-interview with a cabinet minister, a concrete textbook-attested instance of
  `031`'s theoretical "marked usage" category (§5).

### Plain/blunt interrogation register in Unit 11 — a genuinely new register contrast for the corpus

Unit 11's interrogation of the captured commando is answered **entirely in bare plain-style
declaratives** (없었다, 받았다, 떠났다, 여러 사람에게 죄를 졌다— no -(스)ㅂ니다, no -요, no subject
honorifics anywhere in 20 full answers), in sharp contrast to every other unit in this batch. This is
a clean, concrete illustration of the power/distance-driven register choice `031`'s Grammar point §6
describes abstractly — a POW being interrogated uses no honorific marking whatsoever toward his
interrogators, the inverse of every other dialogue in Units 7-10 where two institutional equals
maintain mutual {P}/{Y} honorific register throughout. `031` has no textbook-attested
prisoner-interrogation example of this kind; this is new illustrative data.

### The "일문일답" (question-and-answer) format as a distinct discourse genre — Unit 11

Units 7-10 use a two-party "named-speaker" dialogue format. Unit 11 instead labels every turn 문
("question") / 답 ("answer") rather than naming the speakers, explicitly glossed in the book's own
vocabulary list as 일문일답. This is a distinct, named discourse-genre convention marked structurally
(depersonalized 문/답 labels) as well as lexically (plain-style answers, above), reinforcing that the
register shift is a deliberate genre feature, not incidental.

### Screenplay/film-production terminology — Unit 12

Unlike the dialogue-drill format of Units 1-11, Unit 12 is formatted as an actual television/film
scenario (시나리오), with numbered scene headings (①②③…, e.g. "① 타이틀백 F·I" = "title background,
fade in") and production-abbreviation cues: **(O·L)** (overlap/dissolve transition) and **(F·O)**
(fade out). Genuine 1970s Korean broadcast/film-script conventions — a distinct technical register
(technical.film) not previously captured in this project's Korean corpus.

### Speech-level shifts tracking social relationship — Unit 12

The grandfather (김 노인) and grandson (효섭) use honorific 하세요/해요체 toward each other
reciprocally, while the spy (간첩) and the defector-infiltrator (강 민호) address each other with a
mix of 하오체/해체 depending on their shifting power relationship — the senior spy starts commanding
("내리지 못해?"), while 강민호 answers more deferentially but their relationship visibly inverts by
the end (강민호 openly defies him). A clean worked example of speech-level choice tracking in-scene
power dynamics rather than fixed social rank.

### Metaphorical/figurative use of 아편 "opium" — Unit 12

The spy accuses 강민호 of having been infected by a "freedom-opium-addiction phenomenon" (자유라는
아편 중독 현상) during his time in the South — a clear ideological-propaganda figurative extension,
a stock Cold-War-era rhetorical figure for "corrupted by capitalist freedom."

### Animal-group behavior described with human military-organizational vocabulary — Unit 13

See the crow-call table above. 중대장 ("company commander"), 소대장 ("platoon leader"), and 부관
("adjutant/deputy") are all genuine military rank/role terms applied metaphorically to describe
crow-flock social structure — the narrator draws this comparison explicitly and at length.

### Administrative-unit naming pattern: -도 / 특별시 / 직할시 — Unit 14

South Korea's first-level administrative divisions follow a three-way naming pattern directly
drilled in this unit's classroom dialogue: ordinary provinces take -도 (e.g. 경기도), the capital
takes 특별시 (서울특별시), and the next-largest city (at the time, only Busan) takes 직할시. The
children's in-story confusion (miscounting the two cities, or including an island as a "do") is a
pedagogically deliberate illustration of this three-way system.

### Honorific subject marking with 께서 + honorific verb stem -(으)시- — Unit 15

Throughout Unit 15's narrative, the uncle (a social superior/elder relative) is consistently marked
with the honorific subject particle 께서 rather than plain 가/이, paired with the honorific
infix -(으)시- on the verb: "삼촌께서는 지도를 보시면서, 또 물으셨습니다." Applied consistently to the
uncle throughout but never to the child narrator's own actions — a clean illustration of Korean's
addressee-independent subject honorification tracking the social status of the grammatical subject.

### Humble-register verb 여쭈다 ("to say/ask," humble) — Unit 15

Unit 15 uses 여쭈다 for the child's own speech toward his uncle: "그 말씀을 듣고 영만이는
여쭈었습니다." The humble counterpart to the honorific pattern above — a textbook-clean example of
Korean's dual honorific system (subject-honorification vs. addressee-humbling) operating within a
single short exchange.

### Quotative/reported-speech pattern "...라고 말씀하셨습니다" / "...고 합니다" — Unit 15

Reliance on indirect/reported speech to deliver factual content inside a narrative frame: the
honorific reportative "...라고 말씀하셨습니다" is reserved for direct statements attributed to a
named, socially-superior speaker (the uncle); the plain reportative "...고 합니다" is used for
general factual claims with no specific attributed speaker throughout.

### Reportative/quotative narration in biographical units — Units 18-19

Both "Socrates" and "Jesus Christ" narrate received legend rather than direct historical claim,
marked consistently with a quotative/reportative tail: "…라고 말하였다고 합니다," "…되었다고 합니다,"
"…전(傳)하여지고 있읍니다." A pervasive narrative-distancing strategy across the whole chapter — the
text never asserts "X happened," only "it is said/transmitted that X happened."

### Register notes — "A Christmas Carol" — Unit 20

A stage-play adaptation gives a sustained, scripted contrast between formal-polite and gruff/plain
speech that this book's other (narrated, non-dialogue) units don't offer.

- **Scrooge's curt -오/-소 register vs. the other characters' full -ㅂ니다/-습니다 deference.**
  Scrooge consistently answers in the older, clipped -오/-소 style — "모르오" (I don't know), "없소"
  (there isn't), "하겠소" (I'll do it), "아니오" (it isn't) — even toward strangers and his own
  employee, while Bob Cratchit and the charity-fund gentleman answer him with fully deferential
  -ㅂ니다/-습니다 forms ("아니올시다, 주인님," "실례했습니다," "감사합니다"). The mismatch runs in both
  directions and is scripted characterization, not inconsistency — it marks Scrooge as deliberately,
  performatively gruff. See PDF pp.155-158 for the clearest run of contrastive exchanges.
- **"이 놈아!" — plain-register vocative insult directed at a family member.** Scrooge's reply to his
  nephew's cheerful Christmas greeting is "시끄럽다, 이 놈아!" (Shut up, you wretch!), PDF p.158. The
  nephew Fred, by contrast, addresses Scrooge throughout with the polite kinship term "아저씨" and
  -ㅂ니다 endings even while being insulted — the single clearest "distinct register" moment in this
  book; it would not occur in a textbook's ordinary example dialogue.
- **The ghost's intimate-superior register toward Scrooge.** Marley's ghost (and the three Christmas
  Spirits) address Scrooge with the plain/intimate pronoun "자네" and verb endings like "-나/-게"
  ("누구였나?," "따라 나서게," "자, 난 그만 가겠네") — the register a social equal or a superior
  speaking down to a junior would use, consistent with Marley having been Scrooge's business partner
  and now a supernatural authority figure delivering a warning. PDF pp.163-166.
- **Exclamatory/idiomatic insults**, tallied in the vocabulary table above with page citations: "이
  놈아," "미친 녀석들," "아니꼽다" (PDF p.159 — the single most vicious line in the unit, about wanting
  to skewer carolers' hearts), "바보 … 그게 사람인가 돼지지" (a merchant's posthumous insult of
  Scrooge, PDF p.171), "해가 서쪽에서 뜰걸" (idiom for "that'll never happen," PDF p.176), "레코오드판이
  되란 말이오" (idiom for being nagged, PDF p.158). None are flagged by the book itself as
  colloquial/informal — the `Usage Tier` assignments reflect this project's own judgment, since the
  source predates any formal register-tagging convention.

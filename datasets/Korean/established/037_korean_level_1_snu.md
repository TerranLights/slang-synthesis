# Korean — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: 한국어 1 (Korean 1), Seoul National University Language Education Institute, Full Volume

**Source:** Language Education Institute (언어교육원), Seoul National University, *한국어 1 / Korean 1*
(Moonjinmedia; original 1993, revised edition June 1995, further revised/enlarged edition November
1999; current printing: 2nd printing of 1st English-translation edition, May 2002). Volume 1 of a
planned 6-volume beginner series. PDF pp. 1-279 (front matter, preliminary Hangul lesson, and
Lessons 1-30; the book's own glossary, pp. 280-306, was intentionally not extracted — see Coverage
note).

**Source condition:** this PDF has no text layer at all — confirmed via `pdftotext` (empty output)
and `pdfinfo` (`Creator: HP 9100C Digital Sender`, a flatbed-scanner device). Every page below was
vision-read from page images rendered at 130 dpi by eleven parallel extraction passes (front matter +
ten 3-lesson chunks), then merged into this single file. Scan quality was uniformly high and crisp
throughout — no blur, binding-gutter shadow, or faded print was encountered in any of the ~280 pages
read. See the consolidated Marginalia note at the end of this file for the vision-reading guard check
across the whole volume.

**Coverage note:** every grammar point and every distinct vocabulary item from the book's front
matter/preliminary Hangul lesson and all 30 main lessons' dialogue, 발음 (pronunciation), 문법
(grammar), and 어휘와 표현 (vocabulary & expressions) sections is captured below, including
★-marked supplementary vocabulary. Repeated 연습1/연습2 (exercise) drills were read in full across
every lesson and skipped from extraction wherever they only recombined already-captured
vocabulary/grammar; the handful of drills that genuinely introduced new vocabulary (counter-word
banks, reading-comprehension passages with new place names, etc.) are flagged inline where kept. The
book's own end-of-volume glossary (찾아보기, pp. 280-306) was deliberately not rendered or
extracted — it is a bulk alphabetical word-index with page cross-references for vocabulary already
captured here via its original lesson context, and bulk-extracting it would also conflict with the
project's copyright discipline against reproducing a vocabulary box wholesale.

**Non-redundant-supplement scope decision (applies throughout this file):** this is beginner Level 1
material with substantial overlap against `established/001_basic_korean_part1.md` (Hangul reading,
nouns, predicates/endings, deferential/polite speech levels, particles 이/가, 은/는, 을/를, 의, 에,
와/과, 이랑, 하고, 에서, 에게, 한테, 께, 도, 만, pronouns and kinship terms, numbers, counters,
copula 이다/아니다, existence 있다/없다), `established/002_basic_korean_part2.md` (particles 이나/나,
부터, 까지, past tense 었/았, negation 안/못/-지않다/-지못하다/말다, the seven irregular predicate
classes, -고싶다/-고있다, -(으)ㄹ거예요/-(으)ㄹ까요, prenouns, adverbs, -(으)ㄹ래요/-(으)ㄹ게요,
-겠-/-(으)시-), `established/013_sounds_of_korean_pronunciation_guide.md` (vowel/consonant systems,
batchim/connected-speech sandhi, prosody), and more narrowly against
`established/016_active_korean_1.md` (service-register phrases, currency/price patterns) and
`established/031_korean_honorifics_and_politeness.md` (honorific lexical doublets). Throughout this
file: grammar points that duplicate territory already systematically covered in those files are given
only a brief paraphrase + cross-reference rather than re-derived in full; grammar points that are
genuinely new, more systematic, or carry a usage/register nuance not previously captured are written
out in full with illustrative examples. **All vocabulary is kept in full regardless of grammar-
territory overlap** — a different author's independently-chosen example set and word list is
explicitly non-redundant content per the extraction spec's dispatch rule, even when the underlying
grammar is already documented elsewhere.

**Extraction method note:** this file was assembled from eleven parallel vision-reading passes (front
matter pp. 1-38; Lessons 1-3, 4-6, 7-9, 10-12, 13-15, 16-18, 19-21, 22-24, 25-27, 28-30), each
independently checked against the non-redundant-supplement cheat-sheet above before writing. Minor
differences in how individual passes phrased their scope notes have been consolidated into the single
statement above; the lesson-by-lesson vocabulary and grammar content itself is reproduced as
extracted, unedited beyond header/section-formatting consolidation for a single merged file.

## Front matter (pp. 1-38): book structure + preliminary Hangul lesson

The front matter (title page, copyright page, three successive prefaces — 1993 original, 1995
revision, 1999 revision — plus "일러두기"/"Remarks to the Readers" and a "교재 구성" course-syllabus
table) states the book's own structure for citation purposes: this is Volume 1 of a planned 6-volume
series (per "Remarks to the Readers," p. 14), organized into 30 lessons (과), each comprising 본문
(main dialogue text), 발음 (pronunciation), 문법 (grammar), 어휘/어휘와 표현
(vocabulary/vocabulary-and-expressions), and 연습 (exercises, itself split into structural-drill
Exercise 1 and task-based Exercise 2). Approximately 450 vocabulary items are introduced across the
volume. A preliminary lesson (예비편, pp. 19-38 of this range) precedes Lesson 1 (which begins at
p. 39) and teaches the Hangul script and basic pronunciation to complete beginners.

### Grammar points

#### N은/는 N-입니까? — "Is N N?"
Yes/no question pattern with a predicate noun. Example: 이것은 시계입니까? "Is this a watch?"
(p.44).

#### 네, N-입니다 — "Yes, N is N."
Affirmative answer pattern. Example: 네, 운동화입니다 "Yes, they are sneakers." (p.44).

#### 아니오, N이/가 아닙니다 — "No, (N) is not N."
Negative answer pattern using the negative copula 아니다/아닙니다 with the subject particle 이/가
(not the topic particle) marking the negated noun. Example: 아니오, 시계가 아닙니다 "No, it is not
a watch." (p.44). This 이/가-with-negative-copula pattern is a genuine usage nuance not spelled out
in the `established/001`/`002` cheat-sheet summary (which documents 이/가 as a general subject
marker but not this specific predicate-negation collocation) — kept in full.

#### 이것은 N-입니까? / 네, (그것은) N-입니다. / 아니오, (그것은) N이/가 아닙니다. — full Q&A frame
The lesson's fourth grammar point (p.45) combines the above three into one worked exchange pattern,
explicitly showing that the answer's demonstrative pronoun switches from 이것 (asker's "this") to
그것 (responder's "that/it", referring to the same object now near the first speaker) — e.g.
이것은 운동화입니까? → 아니오, (그것은) 운동화가 아닙니다. (p.45). This 이것→그것 pronoun-switch-
in-answer convention is a specific discourse-deixis nuance not captured by a flat demonstrative-
pronoun list — worth keeping as a grammar point in its own right.

**Pronunciation/usage notes (p.46, cross-referenced to `established/013` where applicable):**
1. ㅂ assimilates to ㅁ before a following nasal consonant (ㄴ/ㅁ/ㄹ) — e.g. 아닙니다 → [아님니다].
   Standard nasal-assimilation rule; cross-ref `established/013`.
2. The subject of a sentence may be omitted when recoverable from context — general pro-drop note,
   not previously flagged explicitly in the cheat-sheet summary; worth a brief mention as it's
   stated as an explicit rule here (p.46) rather than left implicit.
3. 가 marks the subject when the preceding noun ends in a vowel; 이 is used when it ends in a
   consonant — standard allomorph rule, cross-ref `established/001`.

### Grammar points

#### N은/는 N-입니다 — "N is N"
Basic identity-statement frame restated with 1st-person subject. Examples: 저는 김영숙입니다 "I am

with new example content.

#### 제 N — "My N"
Possessive construction using the humble 1st-person possessive 제 (contracted form of 저 + 의)
directly preceding the possessed noun, no overt genitive particle needed in casual use. Examples:
제 이름은 김영숙입니다 "My name is Youngsook Kim." / 그것은 제 운동화가 아닙니다 "Those are not my

grammar point not listed in the cheat-sheet's summary of 001/002's particle coverage (which lists
의 as a genitive particle but not this irregular 1st-person contracted form) — kept in full.

#### N은/는 N을/를 V-ㅂ니다 / N은/는 N을/를 V-습니다 — transitive-sentence frame (NVN) with the deferential verb ending
Basic SOV transitive sentence frame, explicitly split into two allomorphs by the verb stem's final
sound: -ㅂ니다 attaches to a vowel-final stem (저는 한국어를 공부합니다 "I study Korean," stem
공부하-), -습니다 attaches to a consonant-final stem (월슨은 밥을 먹습니다 "Wilson eats his meal,"
stem 먹-) (p.50). The vowel/consonant-stem split for this ending is stated explicitly here; cross-
ref `established/001`/`002`'s deferential speech-level coverage for the broader paradigm, but this
specific minimal pair (공부하다 vs. 먹다) is a clean, concrete illustration worth retaining.

### Grammar points

#### 1. 여기는 N-입니다 — "this (place) is N"
Deictic 여기 ("here") as topic (은/는) + copula 이다. Structurally identical to the general topic
particle + copula pattern already documented in established/001 (topic particle 은/는; copula
이다/아니다); the only lesson-specific content is the deictic-place vocabulary slotted in (여기는
서울입니다, 여기는 한국입니다, 여기는 서울대학교입니다). Negative/question forms (여기는 N-입니까?)
also shown, likewise non-redundant only in its example set.

#### 2. 여기는 어디입니까? — "Where are we?"
The same copula pattern with 어디 ("where") as the predicate complement in the question, answered
by substituting a place noun (여기는 교실입니다). Cross-reference established/001 (pronouns,
question words); no new mechanism.

#### 3. N은/는 N에서 N을/를 V-ㅂ니다/습니다 — "N V's N at (in) N"
A three-slot sentence frame combining the topic particle, the location particle 에서 (already
documented), and the object particle 을/를 (already documented) around a transitive verb. The
lesson's own contribution is purely the example sentences (우리는 서울대학교에서 한국어를
배웁니다. "We learn Korean at Seoul National University."; 나는 대학교에서 영어를 가르칩니다. "I
teach English at a university."). Cross-reference established/001 for both particles individually.

#### 4. N은/는 무엇을 V-ㅂ니까/습니까? — "What does N V?"
무엇 ("what") + object particle 을 in the same transitive-verb question frame. No new mechanism
beyond established/001's pronoun/particle coverage.

#### 5. V-시-ㅂ니까?/V-으시-ㅂ니까? — honorific marker + question ending
The honorific infix -시-/-으시- (-시- after a vowel-final stem, -으시- after a consonant-final
stem) inserted between the verb stem and the -ㅂ니까/습니까 question ending, e.g. 하십니까? ("what
do you do, honorific"), 배우십니까? ("are you learning, honorific"), 읽으십니까? ("are you
reading, honorific"). The honorific infix itself is already documented in established/002
(-겠-/-(으)시-); this lesson's contribution is simply demonstrating it in the interrogative polite
register (-ㅂ니까/습니까, the "deferential"/formal question ending) rather than a declarative, and
the vowel/consonant-conditioned 시/으시 allomorph split is spelled out explicitly in the source's
own Note 3 (p.57): "-시-/-으시- is an honorific ending that comes between the verb stem and the
ending."

name-specific 이-insertion behavior.

### Grammar points

#### 1-2. N이/가 A-ㅂ니다/습니다 and N이/가 A-ㅂ니까/습니까? — descriptive-predicate statement and question
Korean adjectival predicates (형용사-class words like 좋다, 덥다, 춥다, 따뜻하다, 나쁘다) take the
same polite-declarative (-ㅂ니다/습니다) and polite-interrogative (-ㅂ니까/습니까) endings as action
verbs — there is no separate copula needed and no distinct conjugation paradigm for "adjective"
vs. "verb" at this politeness level (날씨가 따뜻합니다. "It's warm." / 날씨가 춥습니까? "Is it
cold?"). The subject particle 이/가 and the -ㅂ니다/습니다 ending are both already documented
individually in established/001-002; what's genuinely worth flagging is the typological point
itself — Korean's descriptive predicates pattern grammatically like verbs rather than like
English-style copula + adjective, demonstrated here for the first time in this chunk's range with
a dedicated predicate-adjective vocabulary set (as opposed to 이다/아니다 copula sentences in
Lesson 4).

#### 3. N이/가 A-지 않습니다 — negation of a descriptive predicate
Same -지 않습니다 negator as established/002, simply applied to an adjectival rather than action
predicate (날씨가 따뜻하지 않습니다. "It's not warm."; 가방이 좋지 않습니다. "The bag is not good.").
No new mechanism.

#### 4. N이/가 어떻습니까? — "How is N?"
어떻다 functions as a dedicated interrogative descriptive predicate ("to be how/what-like"),
used to open a question about a quality rather than confirm/deny one (contrast with 문법 §2's
yes/no-oriented A-ㅂ니까?): 날씨가 어떻습니까? "How is the weather?"; 책이 어떻습니까? "How is the
book?" This is the lesson's organizing question-pattern (it's also the lesson's title) and is worth
keeping as its own entry even though it's structurally just 어떻다 + the standard question ending,
since it functions as a fixed conversational opener distinct from the other A-ㅂ니까? questions.

#### 5. (N은/는) N이/가 A-ㅂ니다/습니다 — topic + subject double-marking
A genuinely notable structural point: the lesson's title sentence and its core example pattern put
*two* marked nominals in a row — a topic-marked frame-setter (오늘은, "as for today") followed by a
subject-marked descriptive-predicate argument (날씨가 좋습니다, "the weather is good") — e.g. 오늘은
날씨가 좋습니다 ("Today, the weather is good"), 오늘은 날씨가 덥습니다 ("Today, the weather is
hot"), 오늘은 날씨가 좋지 않습니다 ("Today, the weather is not good"). This is Korean's classic
"double-subject"/topic-comment construction: 은/는 scopes the whole following clause as being
"about" 오늘, while 이/가 marks the actual grammatical subject of the predicate (날씨) within that
clause — the two particles are not competing for the same syntactic slot, they're nested at
different levels (topic of discourse vs. subject of predication). Worth cross-checking against
established/001's topic (은/는) and subject (이/가) particle entries if those describe each particle
individually without spelling out this nested co-occurrence pattern — it's a different thing from
either particle's single-nominal use and is exactly the kind of construction later Japanese-style
は/が contrastive analysis (see the project's は/が grammar-logic work) would want a parallel Korean
example for.

### Cross-lesson usage note: 오다 as a weather-event verb

### Grammar points

#### N은/는 무슨 N-입니까? — "What N is N?"
Uses the interrogative determiner 무슨 directly before a noun to ask "what kind of N." Example:
오늘은 무슨 요일입니까? "What day is today?" — 오늘은 화요일입니다. "Today is Tuesday." p.77.

#### N에 / N에도 / N에는 — time particle and its emphatic/contrastive variants
Core particle territory already covered in `established/001_basic_korean_part1.md` (에, 도, 는) —
cross-reference rather than re-derive the particles themselves. The source's own usage note (p.77)
is worth preserving: 에도 is explicitly analyzed as 에 ("in"/"on"/"at," used after a time noun) + 도
(emphasizer) = "even on"/"also on" (일요일에도 "even on Sunday"); 에는 is 에 + 는, which the source
says *emphasizes the preceding noun* rather than contrasting it — illustrated by 주말에는 ("as for
weekends specifically") read as foregrounding 주말 itself, distinct from a bare 주말에. p.77.

#### N와/과 N — "N and N"
Noun-coordinating particle; 과 after a consonant-final noun, 와 after a vowel-final noun. Already
covered in `established/001_basic_korean_part1.md`. p.78.

#### S-고 S — clausal connective "S and S" (genuinely new — not in the 001/002 cheat-sheet, which
only lists the compound forms -고싶다/-고있다, not the bare clausal coordinator)
-고 links two full clauses/predicates in sequence within one sentence, distinct from its use inside
the fixed compounds -고 싶다 ("want to") and -고 있다 ("be doing"). Functions as a simple "and"
between predicates when both share a subject, and can link clauses with different subjects as well.
Examples: 오늘은 날씨가 나쁘고 춥습니다. "Today it's cloudy and cold." 나는 도서관에 가고 월슨 씨는
식당에 갑니다. "I'm going to the library and Wilson is going to a restaurant" (different subjects).
나는 한국어를 배우고 영어를 가르칩니다. "I learn Korean and teach English." p.78. (Lesson 9 reuses
this same connective in a past-tense, more clearly *sequential* "and then" reading — see Lesson 9
below.)

### Grammar points

#### N은/는 N에 있어요 — "N is at/in N" (and the fronted variant N에는 N이/가 있어요)
Existential/locative predicate 있다 after noun+에, core territory already in
`established/001_basic_korean_part1.md`'s existence/location coverage. The source also gives a
topic-fronted variant that reorders the location noun to sentence-initial topic position: N에는
N이/가 있어요 "At N, there is N" — e.g. 3층에는 내 방이 있어요 "My room is on the third floor" (lit.
"As for the 3rd floor, my room is there"), structurally the same proposition as 내 방은 3층에 있어요
but foregrounding the location instead of the located object. p.86.

#### V/A-아요 / V/A-어요 — informal-polite sentence ending (flagship new content of this lesson)
A full, systematic vowel-harmony conjugation paradigm for the casual/informal-polite speech level,
contrasted directly against the formal-polite -ㅂ니다/습니다 level already covered in established
material. Rule (per the source's own Notes box, p.89): if the verb/adjective stem's last vowel is ㅗ
or ㅏ, attach -아요; if the stem is 하- (하다 verbs), attach -해요; otherwise (all other vowel stems)
attach -어요. The source explicitly frames -ㅂ니다/습니다 as "the most polite, formal style" and
-아요/어요 as "a casual polite ending" — i.e. both are polite registers, differing in formality, not
in politeness vs. impoliteness. Worked examples:
- ㅗ/ㅏ stem → -아요: 좋다→좋아요 "to be good," 오다→와요 "to come," 가다→가요 "to go"
- 하다 stem → -해요: 공부하다→공부해요 "to study," 따뜻하다→따뜻해요 "to be warm," 일하다→일해요
  "to work"
- other vowel stems → -어요: 있다→있어요 "to be present," 쉬다→쉬어요 "to take a rest,"
  가르치다→가르쳐요 "to teach," 배우다→배워요 "to learn"
p.86-87, p.89.

#### N은/는 N에 없어요 — "there is no N at N"
Negative-existential counterpart of the first grammar point above, same 있다/없다 existence-verb
pairing already in `established/001`. Also has a topic-fronted variant: N에는 N이/가 없어요 ("At N,
there is no N") — 교실에는 텔레비전이 없어요 "In the classroom there is no television." p.87.

#### N 옆[위, 아래, 앞]에 — spatial postpositions "beside / on top of / under / in front of N"
Four bare nouns (옆, 위, 아래, 앞) used as postpositional location modifiers before 에, not listed in
the project's existing established-particle coverage (001 covers 에/에서/에게/한테/께 but not these
spatial nouns). Examples: 책상 옆에 텔레비전이 있습니다 "Next to the desk there is a television";
침대 위에 모자가 있어요 "On top of the bed there is a cap"; 나무 아래에 무엇이 있습니까? "What is
under the tree?"; 학교 앞에 공원이 있어요? "Is there a park in front of your school?" p.88.

#### 누구 vs. 누가 — "who" (object/plain form vs. subject-marked form)

### Grammar points

#### V-(으)ㄹ까요? — "shall we V?"
Already established in `established/002_basic_korean_part2.md` (which treats this ending's
conjecture and alternative-question uses in more depth). SNU's own gloss here is the simple "shall
we/I V?" sense used to solicit an opinion politely, illustrated with 도서관에 갈까요? ("shall we go to
the library?") and 무엇을 먹을까요? ("what shall we eat?"). The book's own grammar-notes box (p.129)
states plainly: "-(으)ㄹ까요? is used to politely ask for an opinion in conversation."

#### V-(으)ㅂ시다 — "let's V" (new — not previously captured)
A sentence-final ending attaching directly to a verb stem (으 inserted after a consonant-final stem,
dropped after a vowel-final stem) that proposes a joint action, glossed by the book itself as "a
sentence ending meaning 'let's'." Distinct from -(으)ㄹ까요? in that it is a direct proposal rather
than a solicited opinion — the two are shown working as a question/answer pair in the book's own
examples: 무엇을 먹을까요? ("what shall we eat?") — 불고기를 먹읍시다 ("let's have pulgogi"). Other
examples: 극장에 갑시다 ("let's go to the movies"), 내일 만납시다 ("let's meet tomorrow").

#### (N에게) N을/를 주다 — "to give N (to N)"
주다 itself is already established in `established/001_basic_korean_part1.md`. SNU's own examples
pair it with the 에게 dative particle on the recipient: 나는 윌슨에게 사과를 줍니다 ("I'm giving Wilson
an apple"), 철수는 영희에게 꽃을 주었어요 ("Chulsoo gave Younghee some flowers").

#### (N께) N을/를 드리다 — "to give N (to N)" (honorific form)
Both 드리다 and its pairing with the honorific dative particle 께 (in contrast to plain 주다 + 에게) are
already established in `established/001`. SNU's own notes box (p.129) states the rule explicitly:
"주다/드리다 both mean 'to give'. 드리다 is the honorific term of 주다. 에게/께 are usually added to a
noun referring to a person to mean 'to + Noun'. 께 is the honorific form of 에게. 주다 follows -에게,
while 드리다 follows 께." Example: 선생님께 사과 한 개를 드렸어요 ("I gave an apple to the teacher").

### Vocabulary & expressions (어휘와 표현) — additional usage notes

**뭘** — presented as one of three contracted question-word forms (뭘⇐무엇을, 누굴⇐누구를, 뭐⇐무엇).

**N을/를 먹다 "to eat"** — plain verb coverage, illustrated across register (plain/polite): 철수는
사과를 먹습니다 / 밥을 먹었어요? / 냉면을 먹을까요? / 뭘 먹어요?

**좋아요 "that sounds good"** — flagged by the book as an idiomatic usage distinct from the literal
"is good," functioning as agreement: 냉면을 먹을까요? — 네, 좋습니다 ("Shall we have naengmyun?" —
"Yes, that sounds good"); 극장에 갈까요? — 좋아요. 갑시다 ("Shall we go to the movies?" — "Sounds
good. Let's go.").

**N하고 N / N와/과 N "N and N"** — both conjunction forms shown as interchangeable in the same
food-ordering example sentence (불고기하고 냉면 주세요 / 불고기와 냉면 주세요, "I'd like pulgogi and
naengmyun, please"). The particles themselves are already established in `established/001`; kept
here only for the independently-chosen ordering-context example.

**그릇 / 잔 — counter words.** 그릇 counts servings/bowls of food (냉면 한/두/세/네 그릇 "one/two/
three/four bowls of naengmyun"; 밥 두 그릇을 먹었어요 "I ate two bowls of rice"). 잔 counts glasses/
cups of drink (커피 한 잔, 맥주 두 잔, 주스 세 잔, 물 네 잔, 우유 다섯 잔 "one cup of coffee, two
glasses of beer, three glasses of juice, four glasses of water, five glasses of milk"). Both take
native-Korean numerals (한/두/세/네/다섯), consistent with the general native-vs-Sino-Korean counter
split already noted in `established/001`.

### Register note (non-redundant finding)
Lesson 13's dialogue opener — 아주머니 (the server): 뭘 드릴까요? "What shall I give you?" — is a
**service-worker-initiated honorific offer formula**, distinct from the customer-initiated 여기요
attention-getter already documented in `established/016_active_korean_1.md`. Where 016 captures how a
customer flags down service staff, SNU's L13 captures the reverse direction: the honorific "what may
I give you?" opening line a server uses toward a customer, built directly on the 드리다 honorific-give
verb and the -(으)ㄹ까요? opinion-soliciting ending covered above. Worth keeping as a genuinely
different angle on Korean service-register speech.

### Grammar points

#### N을/를 타다 — "to ride N" / N에서 N을/를 타다 — "to ride N from/at N"
타다 "to ride" is a core new verb for this lesson. Examples: 무엇을 탈까요? — 택시를 탑시다 ("How will
we get there?" — "Let's take a taxi"); 기차를 타셨어요? — 아니오, 버스를 탔습니다 ("Did you take a

happens: 어디에서 버스를 타세요? — 학교 앞에서 타요 ("Where do you take the bus?" — "In front of the
school"); 사당역에서 전철을 탔어요 ("I got on the subway at Sadang Station").

#### S-(으)니까 S — "S because/since S"
Already established in `established/002_basic_korean_part2.md`. SNU's own notes box (p.138) glosses
it simply as meaning "because," "since," or "so." Examples: 비가 오니까, 집에 가세요 ("It's raining, so
go home"); 날씨가 더우니까, 주스를 마십시다 ("It's hot, so let's have some juice"); 시간이 없으니까,
택시를 탈까요? ("Since we're running out of time, shall we take a taxi?").

#### N에서 내리다 — "to get off at/from N"
내리다 "to get off, descend" paired with the source-location particle 에서. Examples: 어디에서
내립니까? — 사당역에서 내려요 ("Where do you get off?" — "I get off at Sadang Station"); 서울역에서
내릴까요? — 좋아요. 서울역에서 내립시다 ("Shall we get off at Seoul Station?" — "Sounds good. Let's
get off at Seoul Station.").

#### V-아야/어야 하다 — "to have to V" (new — not previously captured)
A necessity/obligation construction attaching to the verb stem with vowel harmony (-아야 after an
ㅏ/ㅗ-stem, -어야 elsewhere) plus 하다. The book's own notes box (p.138) states it is "used with
action verbs to express necessity or obligation." Examples: 오늘은 학교에 가야 합니다 ("I have to go to
school today"); 나는 책을 읽어야 해요 ("I have to read a book"); 우리는 사당역에서 내려야 해요 ("We have
to get off at Sadang Station"); 한국어를 공부해야 합니까? ("Do you have to study Korean?"). The
colloquial-register contraction 하다→해요 (해 vs 합니다) is used interchangeably across the examples.

**어서 "to hurry (up)"** — functions as a pre-verbal adverb/interjection urging quick action: 시간이

**시간이 있다[없다] "to have time [not to have time]"** — existential-verb idiom built on 있다/없다

### Grammar points

#### V-(으)려고 하다 — "intend to V" (new — not previously captured)
A purpose/intention construction: verb stem + -(으)려고 (purposive connector) + 하다. Examples: 우리는
박 선생님을 만나려고 합니다 ("We are going to meet Mr./Ms. Park"); 무슨 책을 읽으려고 합니까? ("What book
are you going to read?"); 서울대학교에서 한국어를 배우려고 합니다 ("I am going to learn Korean at Seoul
National University"). The book's own notes box (p.146) glosses it as "a sentence ending marker that
means 'plan to do' or 'intend to do'." Distinct from the probable-future -(으)ㄹ 거예요 already
documented in `established/002` — -(으)려고 하다 marks the subject's own stated intention/plan rather
than a conjectured or scheduled future event.

#### N(으)로 가다[오다] — "to go/come to(ward) N"
The directional sense of (으)로 is already established in `established/001_basic_korean_part1.md`
(Unit 12). SNU's own notes box (p.146) restates it: "(으)로 comes after a place noun to mean 'in the
direction of' or 'towards'." Examples: 두 사람은 버스 정류장으로 갑니다 ("The two people are going to the
bus stop"); 이 버스는 어디로 가요? — 종로5가로 가요 ("Where is this bus going?" — "It's going to Chongno
5-ga").

#### (N에서) N을/를 사다 — "to buy N (at/from N)"
사다 "to buy," optionally paired with source-location 에서. Examples: 가게에서 사과를 세 개 샀어요 ("I
bought three apples at the store"); 백화점에서 시계를 사려고 해요 ("I'm going to buy a watch at the
department store," combining with the -(으)려고 하다 point above); 어디에서 그것을 샀어요? ("Where did
you buy that?").

#### 그들 "they" / 우리들 "we" (plural pronoun + -들 marker)
Both the dedicated pronoun 그들 "they" and the explicitly ★-marked supplementary example 우리들은

### Grammar points

#### ㅡ-irregular descriptive verbs (바쁘다/나쁘다)
Paraphrase only — this is a specific lexical pair illustrating the ㅡ-irregular class already
systematically catalogued in `established/002_basic_korean_part2.md`'s 7-irregular-predicate-class
list. Rule as stated in the source's own Notes box (p.153, note 1): when -아/어 is added to a stem
ending in the vowel ㅡ, the ㅡ drops and -아 is chosen if the preceding syllable had ㅏ or ㅗ (바쁘다 →
바빠, 나쁘다 → 나빠). No new mechanism beyond what established/002 already covers; kept as a confirmed
worked example pair.

#### S-아서/어서 S — sequential "and then" (pp. 151, 156)
Connects two clauses where the first action happens before the second (철수는 학교에 가서 월슨을
만났습니다. "Chulsoo went to school and [then] met Wilson"). The source's own gloss: "close in meaning
to 'and then'."

**Nuance surfaced by a drill contrast (p.156, exercise 3) not stated explicitly in the grammar box
itself:** the same lesson's exercise pairs 친구를 만나고 학교에 갔어요 ("met a friend and [separately]
went to school," using plain -고) against 친구를 만나서 학교에 갔어요 ("met a friend and then went to
school," using -아서/어서) as two different correct answers to structurally similar prompts. The
distinction the drill demonstrates: -고 is a neutral additive/sequential connective with no implied
link between the two events, while -아서/어서 additionally implies the second event follows from or is
enabled by the first (a closer temporal/logical connection, not mere addition). This is a genuine
semantic-contrast finding the exercise illustrates but the main grammar box doesn't spell out —
worth flagging for the mechanics-analysis phase as a -고 vs. -아서/어서 register distinction.

**See also Lesson 18's grammar point below** — -아서/어서 resurfaces there with a distinct *causal*
("because/since") sense, making this a genuinely dual-function connective across the two lessons.

#### 시/분 — telling time (p.151)
Hour uses native-Korean numerals (한 시, 두 시... with the bound forms 한-/두-/세-/네-, not the full
하나/둘/셋/넷); minute uses Sino-Korean numerals (오 분, 십 분...). -시(-분)에 attaches to mark "at"
a given time (아침 일곱 시 삼십 분에 일어납니다 "I get up at 7:30 a.m."). This hour/minute
numeral-system split is a standard beginner-Korean fact; not previously itemized with this specific
pairing in the established/001 numbers section per the dispatch cheat-sheet, so written out here.

#### 함께[같이] / N N compounding / 아침[점심,저녁] / N-쯤(에) (p.152, 어휘와표현 1-4)
Four brief usage items from the lesson's vocabulary-and-expressions section:
- **함께[같이]** "together" — two near-synonymous adverbs, used interchangeably in examples.
- **N N** — bare noun-noun compounding for time/place expressions (내일 저녁, 아침 식사, 학교 식당) —
  no linking particle needed, a simple juxtaposition pattern.
- **아침[점심, 저녁]** — confirms the morning/breakfast and evening/dinner dual readings extend to

- **N-쯤(에)** "about/around" — attaches to both time expressions (일곱 시쯤) and quantity expressions

  suffix, not time-specific.

#### 그러지요 (p.153, 어휘와표현 5)
Fixed agreement/compliance expression, "I will (do that)" / "sure, okay." Used as a response to an
offer (커피를 마시겠어요? — 그러지요), a command/suggestion (지하철을 타세요 — 그러지요), and a
proposal (일곱 시에 만납시다 — 그러지요) alike — functions as a general-purpose "will comply" response
regardless of the preceding sentence type (question, imperative, or proposal).

#### 고맙다 (p.153, 어휘와표현 6)
Paradigm note: 고맙습니다 (most formal/polite) > 고마워요 (polite informal) > 고마웠습니다/고마웠어요

고마웠어요 is simply the past tense of 고마워요. ㅂ-irregular stem; cross-ref established/002.

### Grammar points

#### N-이에요/예요? / N이/가 아니에요 / N-(이)세요? (p.159-160)
Brief paraphrase + cross-reference: the plain-copula identification question, its negative, and the
honorific form are already covered by the copula system in `established/001_basic_korean_part1.md`
and the -(으)시- honorific suffix in `established/002_basic_korean_part2.md`. New here is only the
explicit three-way politeness ranking the source's own Notes box states (p.162, note 2): -(이)에요/세요
are both identification endings, with -(이)세요 politer than -(이)에요.

#### N이/가 -(명)이다 — counting people (p.160)
Combines the existing copula with the 몇/counter pattern already in established/001. Example: 학생이
몇 명입니까? — 열 명이에요. No new mechanism.

#### N이/가 있다[없다] (p.160)
Brief paraphrase + cross-reference: existence/possession predicate already fully covered under
`established/001_basic_korean_part1.md`'s 있다/없다 section. Examples here (형이 하나 있어요; 저는
차가 없어요; 여기 가족 사진이 있어요) are this lesson's own independently-chosen instances.

#### N-(이)지요? — confirmation-seeking ending (p.160, 162)
Attaches to a noun predicate to seek the listener's confirmation, roughly "aren't you?"/"isn't it?"
(철수는 학생이지요? — 네, 학생이에요.). The source's own Notes box (p.162, note 3) specifically marks
this as "used in polite informal style speech" — i.e. it has a defined register slot, which is the
genuinely new register-nuance content here.

#### 우리 N — collective-possessive idiom (p.161-162)
Korean idiomatically uses 우리 ("we"/"our") where English would use a singular possessive "my": 우리
가족 ("my family," literally "our family"), and the source's Notes box (p.162, note 4) explicitly
extends the same pattern to 우리 나라 ("my country") and 우리 집 ("my house"). This is a specific,
source-stated usage convention — even speaking of one's own single family/house/country, the
collective 우리 is the normal, unmarked choice rather than a possessive pronoun construction. Worth
flagging as a genuine first-person-reference idiom distinct from ordinary possessive marking.

#### N-님 — honorific suffix (p.162)
Productive suffix adding respect to a title or kinship term: 선생 → 선생님 (teacher), 부모 → 부모님
(parents), 아버지 → 아버님 (father), 어머니 → 어머님 (mother), 형 → 형님 (older brother). Source's
Notes box (p.162, note 5): "-님 adds respect to the title." A small but clean worked example set of
the honorific-suffix pattern across five base kinship/title nouns.

#### 분 (honorific counter) vs. 명 (ordinary counter) (p.162)
Explicit contrast stated in the source's own Notes box (p.162, note 1): 명/분 both count people after
a numeral (네 명 = 네 분 = "four people"), but 분 is the honorific form used for people one should
show deference to, while 명 is the ordinary/neutral form. A clean, source-marked register distinction
on an otherwise easy-to-conflate counter pair.

#### N에 다니다 — "to attend/go regularly to N" (p.162)
Used for recurring institutional attendance — school or workplace — rather than a single trip: 저는
학교에 다닙니다 ("I go to [attend] school"), 우리 형은 회사에 다녀요 ("my older brother works at
[regularly attends] a company"). Distinguishes habitual/institutional 다니다 from the general
motion verb 가다.

### Grammar points

#### N을/를 좋아하다 vs. N이/가 좋다 (p.168)
Two constructions for "to like N," presented as a minimal pair: 저는 야구를 좋아합니다 (accusative-
marked object of the action-like verb 좋아하다) alongside the source's own starred alternative 저는
야구가 좋아요 (nominative-marked subject of the stative/descriptive predicate 좋다, glossed "to
like/enjoy N"). This is a case-alternation pattern typical of Korean psych-predicates: the thing
liked can either be the grammatical object of an active "like"-verb or the grammatical subject of a
stative "is-pleasing" predicate, with near-equivalent surface meaning but a different argument
structure underneath. Not previously itemized in the established-file cheat-sheet; written out in
full as a genuine grammatical nuance (not just a vocabulary pairing).

#### 못 V vs. 안 V (p.168, 170)
못 V ("cannot V," circumstantial/ability-based inability: 저는 야구를 못 합니다, 친구를 못 만났어요)
is contrasted directly against the simple negator 안 V ("don't/didn't V": 저는 야구를 안 해요, 친구를
안 만났어요) as two starred parallel example sets on the same page. The source's own Notes box (p.171,
note 1) states the distinction explicitly: "못 means 'cannot', while 안 means 'don't'." This specific
못-vs-안 contrast is already covered by established/002's negation inventory per the dispatch
cheat-sheet; kept here only as a brief confirming cross-reference, not re-derived.

#### 왜 — "why" (p.169)
Straightforward interrogative adverb; examples show it combining naturally with the 못-negation above
(왜 못 가요? — 시간이 없어서 못 가요, "why can't you go? — I can't go because I have no time"),
functionally linking this point to the -아서/어서 causal point immediately below.

#### S-아서/어서 S — causal "because/since" (p.169, 171) — second sense of the L16 connective
The same -아서/어서 ending introduced in Lesson 16 for sequential "and then" meaning here carries a
distinct *causal* sense, "because/since" (냉면을 좋아해서 두 그릇을 먹었습니다 "I like naengmyeon, so I
had two servings"; 버스가 복잡해서 지하철을 타요 "I'm taking the subway because the bus is crowded").
The source's own Notes box here (p.171, note 2) glosses it simply as "because/since," without cross-
referencing back to Lesson 16's "and then" gloss for the same ending — i.e. the textbook teaches the
two senses as if independently, three lessons apart, without explicitly flagging that it's the same
morpheme doing double duty. **This dual-function behavior (sequential "and then" in L16 vs. causal
"because" in L18, same -아서/어서 ending, same surface form) is the most notable cross-lesson grammar
finding in this fragment** — worth surfacing explicitly for the mechanics-analysis phase, since a
synthetic slang-conlang connective modeled on -아서/어서 would need to replicate this polysemy (or
deliberately choose not to) rather than treating it as a single-sense causal/sequential marker.

#### N을/를 구경하다 / N 구경을 하다 / (N) 구경을 가다 (p.170, 어휘와표현 1)
Three near-synonymous constructions for "to see/watch N," all built on the noun 구경 ("watching,
sightseeing"): 야구를 구경했어요 (verbalized directly with -하다), 야구 구경을 했어요 (light-verb
construction, 구경 as object of 하다), and 영화 구경을 갔어요 ("went to see N," light-verb construction
with 가다 instead of 하다, shifting the focus to the act of going). The source glosses all three as
interchangeable "to see/to watch/to go to watch," but the third variant's 가다 base is specifically
used when the activity is the goal of a trip — a verb-support-construction pattern (N + 구경 + light
verb) worth noting as a productive template beyond this one lesson's examples.

#### 미안하지만 — "I'm sorry/excuse me, but" (p.171, 어휘와표현 2)
Fixed discourse-softener expression preceding a request, apology, or refusal (미안하지만 종이 좀
주세요 "excuse me, could I have some paper"; 미안하지만 바빠서 못 가요 "I'm sorry, but I'm too busy to
go"). Functions as a politeness-marking sentence-initial frame, not a full predicate in its own
right.

#### 시험이 있다[시험을 보다] — "to have a test [to take a test]" (p.171, 어휘와표현 3)
Two distinct collocations for exam-related meaning: 시험이 있다 describes a test existing/being
scheduled (언제 시험이 있습니까? "when do you have the test?"), while 시험을 보다 describes the act of
sitting the exam itself (언제 시험을 봐요? "when will you take the test?"). A clean existence-vs-action
collocation pair around the same noun 시험.

#### 다음 N — "next N" (p.171, 어휘와표현 4)
Simple prenominal modifier attaching to time nouns (다음 주말 "next weekend") and ordinal/sequence
nouns alike (다음 역 "the next station," 다음 버스 "the next bus") — i.e. not restricted to calendar
time, also used for "next in a sequence" generally.

### Grammar points

#### 1. 듣다 "to listen" (ㄷ-irregular)
A worked conjugation example of the ㄷ-irregular class: stem-final ㄷ becomes ㄹ before a
vowel-initial ending (듣다 → 들어요, 들으세요, 들으니까, 들어서, 들으면서) but stays ㄷ before a
consonant-initial ending (듣습니다, 듣고, 듣지만). The general ㄷ-irregular rule itself is already
covered in `established/002_basic_korean_part2.md`'s seven-irregular-class list — this is kept only
as a fuller, fully-tabulated worked example for this specific verb (p.176).

#### 2. S-(으)면서 S — "S while S"
Connects two clauses whose actions happen simultaneously, attached to a verb stem (으 inserted after
a consonant-final stem). Example: 음악을 들으면서 저녁을 먹습니다 "I eat dinner while listening to
music." Distinct from -고 (simple sequence/addition) in that it specifically marks temporal overlap,
not just conjunction. (p.176)

#### 3. N도 V-고 N도 V — "both V N and V N"
A coordinating pattern combining the particle 도 ("also, too") with the -고 connective to express
"did both X and Y" — e.g. 그들은 술도 마시고 노래도 했습니다 "They (both) drank and sang." The
particle 도 itself is already covered in `established/001_basic_korean_part1.md`, and -고 as a plain
coordinator is noted in this book's own Notes box as "mentioned in Unit 7" — this entry is kept
because the *combination* pattern (N도 V-고 N도 V, doubling 도 across both clauses) is a distinct
fixed construction worth naming explicitly, not just the sum of its parts. (p.176)

syllable boundary); 축하합니다 realized as [추카합니다] (aspiration: ㄱ+ㅎ → ㅋ). Both are general

`established/013_sounds_of_korean_pronunciation_guide.md` — noted here only as this lesson's own

ability or possibility — e.g. 나는 김치를 먹을 수 있어요 "I can eat kimchi"; 한국말을 할 수
있습니까? "Can you speak Korean?" This pattern is not in the existing established-file cross-reference
list — written out in full as genuinely new. (p.192)

#### 3. S-(으)니까요 — "because/since S"
A causal connective ending, the same underlying -(으)니까 that links two clauses ("since X, Y") but
used here sentence-finally as its own polite causal answer-tag: a statement is first made, then
-(으)니까요 is appended as a trailing "because..." justification — e.g. 걸읍시다. 여기서 가까우니까요.
"Let's walk. Because it's near here." The book gives the reordered full-clause paraphrase alongside
each example (여기서 가까우니까 걸읍시다). Not in the existing cross-reference cheat-sheet; written out
in full. (p.193)

#### 4. (N에서) 가깝다 / (N에서) 멀다 — "to be close to/far from N"
A paired proximity-adjective construction, both ㅂ-irregular (가깝다→가까워요; 멀다 is ㄹ-irregular,
conjugating regularly as 멀어요 since ㄹ only drops before ㄴ/ㅂ/ㅅ/오). 에서 marks the reference
point: 서울대는 집에서 가깝습니다 "Seoul National University is close to my home"; 프랑스는
한국에서 아주 멀어요 "France is a long way from Korea." (pp.194-195)

#### 5. 걷다 "to walk" (ㄷ-irregular) + 걸어(서) 가다[오다] "to walk (to a place)"
Worked ㄷ-irregular paradigm for 걷다 (걸어요, 걸었어요, 걸어서, 걸으니까, 걸으면서 vs. 걷습니다,
걷고, 걷지만) — the same irregular class as Lesson 19's 듣다, explicitly cross-referenced by the
book's own Notes box ("걷습니다 ⇒ 걸어요 and 듣습니다 ⇒ 들어요"). Combines into the compound
걸어(서) 가다/오다 "to go/come on foot," paralleling the 타고 가다[오다] pattern below. General
ㄷ-irregular rule already in `established/002`; this specific verb's table kept as supplement.
(pp.194-195)

#### 6. 타고 가다[오다] — "to go by/to take (a vehicle)"
A compound built from 타다 "to ride" + 가다/오다, used with an object particle marking the vehicle:
버스를 타고 갑시다 "Let's go by bus"; 택시를 타고 왔어요 "I came by taxi." Pairs structurally with
Grammar point 5's 걸어서 가다/오다 ("on foot") as the lesson's two means-of-transport constructions.
Not previously captured in the existing established files' vehicle/transport coverage; written out
here. (p.192)

#### 7. N서 — contracted form of 에서
A colloquial contraction dropping 에 from the locative/source particle 에서 after a place-deictic
noun: 어디서 ⇐ 어디에서, 여기서 ⇐ 여기에서, 저기서 ⇐ 저기에서, 거기서 ⇐ 거기에서. Example: 학교가
여기서 가까워요 "The school is near here." Not in the existing cross-reference cheat-sheet (the
cheat-sheet lists 에서/에게/한테/께 as particles covered in `001`, but not this contracted colloquial
variant specifically) — written out as new. The book's own Notes box (p.195) states plainly: "서 is
the shortened form of 에서." (p.193)

**Pronunciation notes (발음, p.193):** 걸읍시다 realized as [거릅씨다] (liaison + tensification);
걸으면서 realized as [거르면서] (liaison). Same general sandhi phenomena as `established/013`.

#### V-(으)ㄹ 거예요 — future plan/intention
Marks an intended future action ("to be going to V"). Already fully covered in
`established/002_basic_korean_part2.md`; cross-referenced rather than re-derived. This lesson's own

#### S-ㄴ데/은데/는데 S — background-setting conjunctive ("S, and/[but] S")

plane arrived at Kimpo Airport thirty minutes ago"). See L24's 후에 for the mirror-image "N later"
construction.

### Grammar points

#### N-인데 ("S, and...")
A clause-linking suffix attached to a noun predicate, roughly "N, and..."/"it's N, and..." —
introduces background information the following clause builds on. Example: 저는 호주 학생인데 중국어를
배우려고 합니다 "I am an Australian student, and I am going to study Chinese." Also used with
price/quantity background ("이 사과는 하나에 800원인데 세 개를 샀어요" — "The apples were 800 won each,
and I bought three") and to set up a following question ("내일은 일요일인데 무엇을 하실 거예요?" —
"It's Sunday tomorrow, so what are you going to do?"). Not previously captured in `established/001`
or `002`.

#### N은/는 N에 시작하다 / N은/는 N에 끝나다 / the combined N은/는 N에 시작해서 N에 끝나다
"N starts at N" and "N ends at N," plus the combined form "N starts at [time] and ends at [time]"
(e.g., 한국어 수업은 9시에 시작해서 1시에 끝나요 "Korean class begins at nine o'clock and ends at
one"). A supplementary collocation, N을/를 시작하다 "to start N" (object rather than the
clause-internal time-marking pattern), is also given (나는 영어 공부를 시작했어요 "I've started
studying English"). Not previously captured.

#### V-아/어 주다 ("to do something for someone")
A benefactive construction: V-아/어 주다 attaches to a verb stem to mean "do V for someone," e.g.,
숙제를 도와 주세요 "Help me with my homework, please"; 한국말을 가르쳐 주세요 "Teach me Korean,
please." The source's own notes add two register points worth keeping: (1) -아/어 주다 is reserved
for addressees considered equal or inferior to the speaker — the honorific substitute 드리다 is used
instead of 주다 to show respect toward the addressee; (2) the -아/어 주세요 variant adds a "please"
sense. Not previously captured in `established/001` or `002`.

#### N을/를 돕다 ("to help N") — ㅂ-irregular paradigm
돕다 is one of the ㅂ-irregular verbs whose stem-final ㅂ is deleted before a vowel-initial ending,
with 오/우 inserted — but unlike the regular ㅂ-irregular pattern (which yields 워/우), 돕다 (and 곱다
"to be pretty/fine") are the two exceptions that yield 와 (오 + 아) instead: 돕습니다 → 도와요/
도왔어요/도와서/도우면서/도우니까. The general ㅂ-irregular class is already covered in
`established/002_basic_korean_part2.md`; this specific 돕다/곱다 와-exception paradigm is the
supplement worth keeping, since it's a named exception to the more general rule.

#### N에(서) 살다 / N을/를 떠나다 / N에/으로 돌아오다[돌아가다]
Three locative-verb collocations from the 어휘와표현 box, each paired with its governing particle:
N에(서) 살다 "to live in/at N" (어디에 사세요? "Where do you live?"); N을/를 떠나다 "to leave N" (집을
떠나서 한 시간 후에 회사에 도착했어요 "I arrived at the office an hour after I left home"); N에/으로
돌아오다[돌아가다] "to return to N" (나는 10시에 집으로 돌아와요 "I return home at ten o'clock").

### Grammar points

#### (내가) V-(으)ㄹ게요 ("I will V" — first-person intention/determination)
Already covered generally in `established/002_basic_korean_part2.md`'s -(으)ㄹ게요/-(으)ㄹ래요 entry.
This lesson is built around it as its flagship point (lesson title: 오늘은 내가 차 값을 낼게요), and
the source's own grammar note adds a sharper register gloss worth keeping: -(으)ㄹ게요 is "a sentence
ending marker expressing the speaker's intention or determination," restricted to first-person
subjects (오늘은 내가 차 값을 낼게요 "Today I will pay for the tea"; 밤에 일찍 집으로 돌아올게요 "I
will return home early in the evening").

#### A/V-지요? (confirmation-seeking tag question) and A/V-았지요?/었지요?
New grammar point, not previously captured. -지요? attaches to a predicate to seek the listener's
confirmation, roughly "...isn't it?"/"...right?": 오늘은 날씨가 춥지요? "It's cold today, isn't it?"
— 네, 추워요. "Yes, it is." The past-tense form -았지요?/었지요? works the same way: 커피 두 잔을
드셨지요? "You had two cups of coffee, right?" A second pattern combines question words with -지요?:
얼마/무엇/어디/언제/몇 시/누구 + (이)지요? — e.g., 이 책이 얼마지요? "How much is this book?" (= 이
책이 얼마예요?), 저 분이 누구시지요? "Who is that person?" (= 저 분이 누구십니까?) — the source
glosses these as a softer, more conversational variant of the plain-copula question.

#### A-군요 (exclamatory sentence ending)
New grammar point. -군요 attaches to an adjective (and, per the dialogue, to verbs via the same
pattern) to express the speaker's surprised or newly-realized reaction to something — "Oh, it's
really...!": 커피 값이 참 비싸군요! "Coffee is really expensive!"; 산이 참 아름답군요! "The mountain
is very beautiful!" The source's own note specifies this is used in informal speech.

#### 오르다/빠르다 — 르-irregular paradigm
New systematic paradigm, supplementing `established/002`'s general listing of the 르-irregular class
as one of the 7 irregular predicate categories. The source states the rule explicitly: when a 르-stem
verb/adjective is followed by 어/아, the ㅡ in 르 is deleted, and the remaining ㄹ attaches to the
preceding syllable while 어/아 becomes 러/라 — 오르다 → 올라요/올랐어요/올라서; 빠르다 → 빨라요/빨랐어요/
빨라서. Full tables given for both: 오릅니다/오르고/오르니까/오르지만 ↔ 올라요/올랐어요/올라서;
빠릅니다/빠르고/빠르니까/빠르지만 ↔ 빨라요/빨랐어요/빨라서.

#### 내가[제가] — plain vs. humble first-person subject form
New register nuance. 내가 (plain) and 제가 (humble) are both "I + subject marker"; 제가 is the
appropriately humble choice in more formal/polite contexts (제가 그 일을 했어요 "I did that work").
This complements the possessive pair 내/제 also shown (이것은 내 책입니다 vs. 저것은 제 구두예요). Not
previously itemized as a pair in `established/001`'s pronoun coverage.

#### N을/를 내다 ("to pay for N")
New vocabulary/expression pattern: 오늘은 내가 차 값을 낼게요 "Today I will pay for the tea"; 영숙
씨가 돈을 낼 거예요 "Youngsook will pay"; 8천 원을 내야 해요 "I have to pay 8,000 won." **Usage note
(not a formal rule stated by the source, but explicit in both the dialogue and a later drill):** the
lesson's dialogue has Youngsook volunteer to pay ("오늘은 내가...") and a separate drill item (p. 245)
has one speaker say "영희 씨가 ___을 냈으니까 제가 ___를 살게요" ("Since Younghee paid for X, I'll buy
Y") — illustrating a pay-it-forward/alternating-turns social pattern around treating in these
dialogues, though the textbook does not state this as an explicit etiquette rule; captured as a
usage observation, treated with correspondingly lower confidence as a genuine cultural
generalization since it is only implicit in the dialogue pattern, not an explicit footnoted rule.

#### V-고 있다 ("V+ing" — progressive)
Already covered in `established/002_basic_korean_part2.md`. Brief supplement worth noting: one of
this lesson's examples (고향에는 가족이 살고 있어요 "My family lives in my hometown") uses -고 있다

#### S-(으)면 S ("If S, S")
New grammar point, not previously captured in `established/001` or `002`. -(으)면 attaches to the
first clause's predicate to mark it as the condition for the second clause: 집에 가면 무엇을

#### N보다 (더) A — "more A than N"
Comparative construction: `N보다 (더) A` places the standard of comparison before 보다 ("than"), with
an optional 더 ("more") intensifier before the adjective. Word order is flexible — the source shows
both orders are acceptable (e.g. 형이 나보다 더 작습니다 / 나보다 형이 더 작습니다, "my older brother
is shorter than I am," both given as equally valid, p.254).

#### V-ㄹ/을 N — verb stem + noun-modifier suffix on N
The noun-modifier suffix -ㄹ/을 attached to a verb stem creates a relative-clause-like modifier on a
following noun, most often with future/prospective meaning when paired with an action verb (e.g.
읽을 책 "a book [that I'm] to read," 마실 물 "water to drink," p.254). A footnote on p.255 clarifies:
when this modifier is used with an action verb it usually denotes future tense, illustrated with
먹을 음식 좀 주세요 ("could you please bring me some food?").

#### V-(으)ㄹ 것이다 — "will/is going to V"
A sentence-ending construction expressing the speaker's plan or intention, built on the same -(으)ㄹ
prospective element as the noun modifier above, followed by 것이다 ("it is a matter of,"
grammaticalized as future/intentive). Footnote (p.255): marks the speaker's plan or intention
specifically, e.g. 저는 경주에 갈 것입니다 ("I will go to Kyongju").

#### 강의가 있다[없다] — "there be class [no class]"
Existential construction with 강의 ("class/lecture") as subject and 있다/없다 ("exist"/"not exist");
pairs with the collocation set 강의를 하다/듣다/받다 ("teach/take/have a class") introduced in the
same entry (p.256).

#### 여러 가지 — "several kinds of"
A quantifier phrase preceding a plural noun, e.g. 여러 가지 책 "several kinds of books" (p.256). Not
a grammatical paradigm per se, but listed as its own 어휘와 표현 item because of its frequent
collocational use.

#### 다 V — "all V"
The adverb 다 preceding a verb to express totality/completion of an action across a set (e.g. 학생들이
다 집에 갔어요 "All the students have gone home") or exhaustive coverage (여러 가지 책들이 다 있어요
"there are all kinds of books [available]"), p.257.

### Grammar points

#### V-아/어 드리다 — "to do something for someone" (more polite benefactive)
A benefactive construction: the connective -아/어 attached to a verb stem plus the humble/polite
auxiliary 드리다 ("to give," honorific register) expresses doing an action as a favor for someone of
higher or equal status, more polite than the plain -아/어 주다 benefactive. Examples: 얼마를 바꿔
드릴까요? ("how much would you like me to change [for you]?"); 책을 사 드리겠어요 ("I will buy you a
book," p.263). A variant with the ordinary (non-honorific) 주다 also appears in the same set (책을 사
줄까요? "what book shall I buy for you?").

#### V-는군요 — exclamatory sentence ending expressing surprise or delight
Footnote (p.264): -(는)군요 is an exclamatory ending whose nuance (surprise vs. delight) depends on
context; -군요 attaches after a descriptive verb/adjective stem (e.g. 꽃이 참 예쁘군요! "the flowers
are quite pretty!"), while -는군요 attaches after an action-verb stem (e.g. 한국말을 잘하시는군요! "you
speak Korean well!"), p.262.

#### N을/를 잘하다[잘 못하다] — "to do N well [not to do N well]"
Pattern pairing an object-marked noun with 잘하다 ("do well") or its negative 잘 못하다 ("not do
well"/"be poor at"), e.g. 한국말을 잘해요? "do you speak Korean well?" / 나는 한국어를 아직 잘 못합니다
"I don't speak Korean well yet" (p.263). Distinct from the general negator 안 (cf. Lesson 30's 안
A/V below, and `established/002_basic_korean_part2.md`'s negation inventory) — 잘 못하다 specifically
negates proficiency/skill rather than simple occurrence.

#### N을/를 N(으)로 바꾸다 — "to exchange N into N"
Core currency-exchange pattern: the item being exchanged takes 을/를, the target form takes (으)로,
governed by 바꾸다. Examples: 달러를 한국 돈으로 바꾸려고 해요 ("I'm going to change dollars into
Korean currency"); 천 원을 잔돈으로 바꿀 수 있어요? ("can you give me change for a 1,000 won bill?"),
p.264.

#### N에게 N을/를 보여 주다[보여 드리다] — "to show N to N"
Dative-recipient pattern with 에게 marking the person shown something, 을/를 marking the thing shown,
and 보여 주다/보여 드리다 (plain/honorific benefactive pair, paralleling the V-아/어 드리다 point above)
as the verb. Example: 선생님께 공책을 보여 드렸어요 ("I showed my notebook to the teacher," using the
honorific dative 께 for a teacher), p.265.

#### 그런데 — "by the way / but"
Discourse connective with two related functions illustrated in the source's examples (p.265): topic-
shift/attention-getting ("by the way," e.g. 그런데 뭘 타고 가면 좋을까요? "by the way, how should we
get there?") and mild contrast ("but," e.g. 한국말을 잘해요. 그런데 한국 노래를 못 해요 "I speak Korean
well. But I can't sing Korean songs").

#### 뭘요 — "you're welcome / not at all" (informal acknowledgment or mild disagreement)
Footnote (p.265): 뭘요 is used in informal speech in response to a statement, either to modestly
acknowledge it or to partially disagree with it — translatable as "not really" or "don't mention it."
Shown responding both to a compliment (한국말을 잘하시는군요! — 뭘요, 잘 못해요) and to thanks (도와
주셔서 고맙습니다 — 뭘요).

### Grammar points

#### V-(으)러 가다[오다] — "to go/come [in order] to V"
Purpose connective -(으)러 attached to a verb stem, almost always paired with a motion verb (가다/
오다). Footnote (p.273): "-(으)러 after a verb stem denotes the purpose of the verb. It is often used
with either 가다 or 오다," example 먹으러 가다 "to go to eat." Examples from the lesson: 달러를 바꾸러
은행에 갈까요? ("shall we go to the bank to change dollars?"), 한국말을 배우러 학교에 다닙니다 ("I go
to school to learn Korean"), p.271.

#### 어떤 N — "what kind of N"
Interrogative determiner preceding a noun to ask about its kind/type/nature, distinct from 무슨
("what/which," already covered in prior lessons' vocabulary) in that 어떤 more specifically solicits a
qualitative description. Examples: 어떤 영화를 봤어요? ("what kind of movie did you see?"), 어떤
사람을 좋아해요? ("what kind of people do you like?"), p.271.

#### N-이었다/였다 — noun + copula past tense
The copula 이다 ("to be") takes past tense via -이었다 (after a consonant-final noun) or -였다 (after
a vowel-final noun), e.g. 선물이 꽃이었어요 ("the gift was a bunch of flowers"), 선물이 시계였어요
("the gift was a watch"), 슬픈 영화였어요 ("it was a sad movie"), p.271. General past-tense mechanics
(었/았, double past) are already covered systematically in `established/002_basic_korean_part2.md`;
this entry is the lesson's own worked paradigm specifically for the copula + past combination, kept
because it is the lesson's structural focus (the title 어떤 영화였어요? is itself an instance of this
pattern) and is not spelled out as its own paradigm in 002.

#### 안 A/V — "not A/V"
Pre-verbal negator 안 placed directly before an adjective or verb. **Cross-reference:** this
construction is already fully covered in `established/002_basic_korean_part2.md`'s negation inventory
— not re-derived here. Worth noting from this lesson's own example set (p.272): the source explicitly
drills the "yes/no answer inversion" pitfall non-native speakers hit with negative questions in Korean
(아침 안 먹었어요? — 아니오, 먹었어요 "didn't you eat breakfast? — yes [lit. "no"], I did," where
Korean 아니오/네 track agreement with the negative framing of the question rather than the literal
English yes/no) — a usage nuance not explicitly called out in 002's entry, worth flagging for the
mechanics-analysis phase.

## Text layer and OCR note

`pdftotext` (no `-layout`) produces a **genuine, directly usable text layer** — this is OCR output
(ABBYY FineReader) over a real scanned book, not a font-substitution cipher. Overall quality is good
for continuous prose (dialogues, grammar explanations, example sentences) but the OCR has three
recurring failure modes, all confirmed and worked around:

1. **Stray corrupted glyphs as line-noise.** Isolated jamo/CJK-lookalike characters (`ᅳ`, `ᄀ`, `ᄎ`,
   `ᄉ`, `ᄂ`, `^`, `0`, `@`) appear scattered through the text, especially around section markers and
   numbered-list bullets. These carry no semantic content and were silently filtered out; they never
   looked like genuine source words once cross-checked against the parallel Chinese gloss.
2. **Drill-exercise tables (fill-in-the-blank grids, multi-column answer sheets) are heavily
   scrambled** — cell order is lost and text comes out as disconnected fragments. This is squarely
   the spec's "repeated drill exercises" category and was skipped per the coverage rule; it was never
   a source of new vocabulary or grammar.
3. **Two-column vocabulary boxes extract as two separate same-order blocks** (all terms, then
   separately all POS-tag/gloss pairs in the same relative order) rather than interleaving
   incorrectly — cross-checked at several points by matching block position 1:1 against the parallel
   Chinese gloss, and the order held every time. This is a different (benign) failure mode than the
   spec's "two-column glossary interleaving" warning; flagged here because it is easy to mistake for
   a problem when it first appears mid-lesson.
4. The book's own cumulative back-matter glossary (`찾아보기`, pp. ~331+ internally) genuinely **is**
   the two-column-interleaving failure described in the spec (terms, page numbers, POS tags, and
   Chinese glosses each come out as separate disordered blocks with no reliable alignment) — this
   back-matter index was not extracted (see Scope below), so the interleaving was not worth decoding.

No handwritten marginalia of any kind was found anywhere in the range (this is a clean institutional
scan, not a used/annotated copy).

## Non-redundant-supplement scope decision

This is an **intermediate-advanced** course (34 lessons of connected-discourse grammar: reported
speech in all its moods, the full causative/passive suffix pairing, concessive and purpose
connectives, aspectual auxiliaries, idiomatic sentence-final endings) sitting well above the
beginner material already covered by `established/001`–`002` (Byon's *Basic Korean*), `016` (*Active
Korean 1*), and the early units of `003`/`005` (Byon's *Intermediate*/*Continuing Korean*). Checked
against those files' documented scope before extracting: **no wholesale redundant chunk was found** —
every one of this book's 34 lessons introduces either a distinct sentence pattern, a distinct
lexical set, or (in two cases) a deeper/more systematic version of a pattern already named elsewhere.
Specific overlap calls, made explicit rather than silently skipped:

- **Lesson 8's passive `-이/히/리/기` suffix table and Lesson 17's causative `-이/히/리/기/우` suffix
  table** use the *same four/five morphemes* `established/003` already documents as **causative**
  markers. Kept in full anyway: Lesson 17's causative table is far more exhaustive (22+ verb pairs
  plus a dedicated diary-narrative fill-in exercise) than `003`'s, and Lesson 8's passive-voice
  pairing with the identical morphemes is a genuinely different grammatical function never
  previously captured for Korean in this project — the two lessons are kept side by side here
  specifically so a learner/analyst can see the causative/passive syncretism directly.
- **Lesson 10's `-아/어 두다`** (preparatory/resultative aspect) overlaps the auxiliary-verb battery
  `established/003` already lists (`놓다/두다` among others) — kept only briefly, as a single
  grammar-point entry, not re-derived at length.
- Ordinary core particles, tense marking, and the basic honorific/plain speech-level split are
  **not** re-extracted anywhere in this file — they are assumed prerequisite knowledge the book itself
  never re-teaches.

**Grammar points.**

- **-(으)ㄴ/는/(으)ㄹ 줄 몰랐다[알았다]** — "didn't know/thought that [fact/manner]" — after a
  verb/adjective stem, expresses the speaker didn't realize, or mistakenly believed, something. *지하철이
  이렇게 빠르고 편리한 줄 몰랐어요* "I didn't know the subway was this fast and convenient."
- **N인 줄 몰랐다[알았다]** — the same construction with a copula predicate: *오늘이 철수 씨 생일인 줄
  몰랐어요* "I didn't know today was Cheolsu's birthday."
- **을/를 빼다** — "to leave out/subtract X," used both for physically omitting an ingredient
  (*고추장을 빼고 주세요*) and for arithmetic subtraction.
- **사정이 있다** — idiom, "to have [unspecified personal] circumstances" — a polite catch-all excuse
  for an unstated reason.

**Grammar points.** This lesson is a dense cluster covering the full reported-speech paradigm:

  respectively.

### Lesson 34 — 시와 노래 (Poetry and Songs — literary capstone lesson)

A culminating literature-appreciation lesson, structurally different from every prior lesson (no
dialogue, no comprehension-check table): four reproduced Korean poems/songs with their own small
vocabulary sets, plus one poem written by a non-Korean graduate of the institute itself. Selective
paraphrase only — full lyrics/poem text not reproduced per copyright discipline; each work is named
and its theme described rather than quoted at length.

1. **엄마야 누나야 (강변살자)** — Kim So-wol (김소월), a folk-song-style poem about living together
   by the riverside, with glittering sand and reed leaves "singing" outside the back door.
2. **옹달샘** — Yun Seok-jung (윤석중), a children's song about a small mountain spring that a rabbit
   (at dawn) and a roe deer (by moonlight) each come to drink from and then run off.
3. **고향의 봄** ("Spring of My Hometown") — Lee Won-su (이원수, lyrics)/Hong Nan-pa (홍난파, music), a
   classic, widely-known Korean song about a flower-filled home valley (peach, apricot, azalea
   blossoms) the speaker is nostalgic for.
4. **꽃** ("Flower") — Kim Chun-su (김춘수), a well-known modern Korean poem on naming and existence:
   a thing is "merely a gesture" until its name is called, at which point it becomes something to the
   namer — a reciprocal desire to be each other's unforgettable "glance."
5. **하늘** ("Sky") — Thomas de France, explicitly credited in-text as a graduate of the Seoul
   National University Language Education Institute (i.e. a non-native-Korean-speaker's own creative
   composition, included as a model of what a learner can produce), imagining the sky's colors,
   clouds, sun, and stars as deliberately "made" by someone who liked blue and felt lonely.

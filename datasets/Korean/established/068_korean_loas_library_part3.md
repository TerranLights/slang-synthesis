# Korean — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Chang, *Korean* (LOALL v.4), Part 3 — Chapter 6 "Extended Sentences"

**Source:** Suk-Jin Chang, *Korean* (London Oriental and African Language Library, vol. 4). Amsterdam/Philadelphia: John Benjamins, 1996. Chapter 6 "Extended Sentences" in full (printed pp. 135–174, PDF pp. 150–189 of the scanned source): §6.1 Coordination and Subordination, §6.2 Compound Sentences, §6.3 Complex Sentences (§6.3.1 Noun Clauses, §6.3.2 Adnoun Clauses, §6.3.3 Adverb Clauses — eleven semantic-role subsections), §6.4 Mixed Sentences.

This is Part 3 of a four-part extraction of Chang's 266-page academic structural-linguistic description of Korean. See also Part 1 (`047_korean_loas_library.md`, Chapters 1–3), Part 2 (`067_korean_loas_library_part2.md`, Chapters 4–5), and Part 4 (`069_korean_loas_library_part4.md`, Chapter 7 + Appendix + Notes). The entire PDF has **zero text layer** (confirmed via `pdftotext` returning empty output on every sampled page) — every row below was vision-read from pre-rendered 150 DPI page images across two dispatch chunks covering PDF pp. 150–166 and 166–189 respectively. **No handwritten marginalia was found anywhere in this chapter's page range** — all pages are clean typeset academic print; only a rotating-language scanning-service "Copyrighted material" watermark appears in page corners (not source content, excluded throughout).

**Script note.** This chapter's Korean examples are given exclusively in **Yale romanization** with interlinear glosses, not Hangul — a theoretical-syntax monograph built almost entirely from constructed minimal-pair sentences using a small recurring cast of invented test-subject names (Mia, Yong, Cina, Hyen, Yuna). The ordinary-vocabulary items embedded in these examples are not separately tabulated as new lexical content, matching the judgment call `established/019` already made about its own 철수/영희/선생님 example-sentence cast.

**Non-redundant-supplement scope decision.** Checked first against `018_korean_grammar_intl_learners_part1.md` and `019_korean_grammar_intl_learners_part2.md`.
- **Skipped/kept light as substantially redundant:** the bound-noun `kes` future/probability construction (overlaps 018's 것 entry); quotative `ko` as quoted-speech marker (overlaps 019 Ch. 16's fuller treatment); the `ki`/`(u)m` nominalizer system's internal mechanics (overlaps 019 Ch. 13's productivity/register/fossilized-noun treatment) — only the genuinely new *framing* of each (see below) is kept in depth.
- **Kept as genuinely new:** the formal **coordination-vs-subordination** typology with its three syntactic diagnostic tests (reversability/insertability/enumerability) and the `-ciman` worked diagnostic case; the **four-way formal noun-clause typology** (nominalizer / quotative-`ko` / bound-noun-`kes` / suspective-`ci`) with its syntactic-function subtypes; the **Reichenbachian S/E/R tense analysis** of reported clauses; the **relative-clause "postcedent" (vs. antecedent)** terminology and five postcedent-syntactic-role subtypes; the **sensory-appositive-clause** construction (no English equivalent, per the source); new bound nouns `cwul`/`swu` (modal-capability idioms) not in 018's ~30-entry bound-noun catalogue; and — by far the largest genuinely-new find in this part — the **full eleven-semantic-role adverb-clause subordinator inventory** (time, place, cause/reason, condition, concession, contrast, circumstance, result, purpose/intention, proportion, comparison), which organizes connectives by meaning rather than by register/source-chapter the way 018/019's "representative sample" catalog does, plus the counterfactual-conditional construction, the frozen `-(e)to + toyta/cohta` 'may' idiom, and §6.4's Mixed Sentences tree-diagram/CL-notation methodology.

**Morpheme breakdowns:** **po-ki** = po- "see" + -ki (nominalizer) — a fossilized derived noun rather than a live gerund. **el-um** = el- "freeze" + -um (nominalizer) — likewise fossilized, the source's own minimal-pair contrast against po-ki. **-(nu)nci** historically decomposes as bound noun `ci` ("circumstance/whereabouts") grammaticalized into a suspective ending — a free/bound noun drifting into pure grammatical morphology, the same diachronic pattern `019` documents for quotative `하-`.

**Morpheme breakdowns (nominal subordinators — adnominalizer + bound/relational noun + case particle template):** **-l ttay-ey** "when" = `-l` (ADNZ) + `ttay` "time" + `-ey` (locative) — the base pattern the whole temporal inventory varies by swapping the time noun (`cek`/`hwu`/`cen`/`taum`/`tongan`/`mwulyep`). **-n kkatalk-ey** "because" = `-n` (ADNZ) + `kkatalk` "cause/reason" + `-ey` — same pattern recurs for `palam`/`thas`/`pa`/`kes`. **-n kes-ulo poa** "inferring from the fact that" = `-n` (ADNZ) + `kes` "thing/fact" + `-ulo` (instrumental) + `poa` (infinitive of `pota` "see") — retains an overt lexical verb, unlike most of this inventory. **-l mokcek-ulo** "for the purpose of" = `-l` (ADNZ) + `mokcek` (Sino-Korean "purpose") + `-ulo` — a Sino-Korean-noun-headed subordinator, contrasting with the native-Korean-bound-noun-headed majority (flagged for mechanics-analysis as a register-marked compositional source). **-(u)m-ey ttala** "according as" = `-(u)m` (NOMZ, not ADNZ — the only subordinator in this inventory built on `-(u)m`) + `-ey` + `ttala` (infinitive of `ttaluta` "follow"). **-ta/la-myen** "if" (hypothetical) = `-ta/la` (declarative ender) + `ko` (quotative) + `(mal)ha-` (elided "say") + `-myen` "if" — a frozen quotative-conditional contraction, explaining its more-distanced hypothetical feel versus plain `-myen`.

## Grammar points

### 1. Extended sentences: the compound/complex/mixed typology (p. 135)

A sentence with more than one clause is an "extended sentence," of three types: **compound** (two+ coordinate clauses), **complex** (one main clause + one subordinate clause as its adverbial/adnominal/nominal constituent), and **mixed** (compound and complex combined, extendable indefinitely). Two worked tree diagrams contrast *Mia-nun Yong-ul cohaha-ko Cina-nun Hyen-ul cohahanta* "Mia likes Yong and Cina likes Hyen" (compound) against *Yong-i Mia-lul cohaha-nikka Mia-to Yong-ul cohahanta* "As Yong likes Mia, Mia likes Yong, too" (complex, with the adverb clause nesting as a modifier rather than a sister constituent).

### 2. §6.1 — Coordination vs. subordination: a formal, testable distinction

**The chapter's central theoretical contribution.** Korean marks coordination/subordination not via separate conjunction words (as English contrasts "and" with "because") but through its connective-ending/particle inventory, tested by **three syntactic diagnostics**: **reversability** (coordinate clauses can swap order without changing truth-conditional meaning, though pragmatic implicature may shift — *Mia-nun kyelhonha-ko ay-lul pay-ss-ta* "Mia got married and became pregnant" vs. its reversal, truth-conditionally identical but implying opposite real-world orderings), **insertability** (a subordinate clause can be embedded into another; a coordinate clause cannot), and **enumerability** (coordinate clauses chain without limit; subordinate clauses cannot repeat, unless reinterpreted as mutually coordinate).

**Worked diagnostic case: `-ciman` "but/though."** Passes reversability and insertability (a `-ciman` clause can be embedded mid-sentence) but fails enumerability (no unlimited chaining) — concluded to be grammatically **subordinate** despite glossing into English "but," a coordinating conjunction: a genuine form-over-translation-gloss finding. A disjunctive `kena`-clause, by contrast, passes reversability but fails insertability, exactly as true coordination should.

### 3. Tense in extended sentences, and the Reichenbachian S/E/R analysis of reported clauses (pp. 139–140)

Tense may mark every clause or only the final clause (non-final clauses agreeing with it). The adverbial `-se` 'as' **cannot** attach to a past-marked verb nor modify an imperative/propositive main clause, unlike the near-synonymous `-nikka`. **Reichenbachian three-point tense analysis** (citing Reichenbach 1947) — a genuinely theoretical contribution not found in any prior Korean `established/` file — analyzes reported-clause tense via **speech time (S)**, **event time (E)**, and **reference time (R)**: three minimal variants of "Mia said Yong would/will come the next day" show how the embedded verb's tense marking shifts the relationship between the reporting event's R1/E1 and the reported event's E2, yielding "would come," "would have come," or plain "will come" depending on whether R1 is anchored to the saying-event or to the current speech time S.

### 4. §6.2 Compound Sentences: conjunctive/disjunctive split, redundant double-marking, and subtleties (pp. 141–143)

**Conjunctive** = `-ko`/`kuliko` (optionally doubly-marked); **disjunctive** = `-kena`/`animyen` (likewise). The suffix `taka` 'while' also conjoins in an enumerative/listing sense. `-ko(se)`/`-ko.na.se` 'and then' tests as **subordinate** by insertability despite its coordinate-sounding gloss, and is restricted to verb clauses (incompatible with adjective predicates). Disjunction can also be expressed by **juxtaposing independent interrogative sentences** with distinct prosodic variants (fused/paused/paused-with-`animyen`), structurally distinct from juxtaposing embedded **suspective `-nunci`** clauses (no rising intonation, `animyen`/`ttonun` optional between them).

### 5. §6.3 Complex Sentences overview, and §6.3.1 Noun Clauses — a four-way formal typology (pp. 143–147)

Three subordinate-clause types correspond to three form classes: nominal, adnominal, adverbial. Subordinate clauses normally precede the main clause; postposing after the main verb produces a clarification/contrast discourse effect (flagged by the source itself as genuinely ambiguous between single-sentence-with-reordering and two-sentence analyses).

**The second flagship new-content area.** Korean noun clauses split into **four formally distinct subclasses**: (1) `ki`/`(u)m`-nominalizer clauses (gerund/infinitive-like; `-ki` collocates with evaluative predicates, `-(u)m` with formal/written-register matrix verbs — cross-ref `019` Ch. 13); (2) quotative-`ko` clauses (English *that*-clause equivalent — cross-ref `019` Ch. 16); (3) bound-noun `kes`/`cwul`/`swu` clauses (factive *that*-clauses and modal-capability idioms — `cwul`/`swu` are genuinely new bound-noun entries for this project); (4) suspective `ci` 'whether' clauses (dependent interrogatives, historically from a bound noun — not previously captured anywhere). Noun clauses by syntactic function are illustrated across subject, direct/oblique object, subject complement, and object complement roles.

### 6. §6.3.2.1–6.3.2.2 Relative and Appositive Clauses (pp. 148–150)

A Korean relative clause premodifies its head noun, termed the **postcedent** (the source's own coinage, contrasting with English's "antecedent" since the modified noun follows rather than precedes the clause) — five subtypes by the postcedent's own syntactic role (subject, direct/indirect object, time/place adjunct). Appositive clauses are distinguished by the head noun taking **no syntactic part** in the modifying clause: general (nonreportive), reportive (fuller quotation-style ending), **sensory** (the source's own flagged novel point, "no similar structure in English" — perceptual nouns like `soli` 'sound'/`naymsay` 'smell'/`kwangkyeng` 'scene' take an appositive clause describing the perceived event, e.g. "I hear the sound that water flows" → "I hear the sound of flowing water"), and bound-noun apposition (`kes`-headed, ambiguous between abstract-fact and concrete-object readings, disambiguated only by context).

### 7. §6.3.2 tail — Bound-noun idioms built on the adnominal clause (p.151)

Three periphrastic constructions pairing a prospective/future `-l`-marked adnominal clause with a specific bound noun: `Adn Cl[-l] + kes ita` (future/probability), `Adn Cl[-l] + swu issta` (capability, "there is a way of V-ing"), `Adn Cl[-l] + cwul alta` (know-how). Presented as idiomatic rather than fully compositional — each bound noun has lexicalized a specific modal meaning only in this exact frame.

### 8. §6.3.3 — Adverb Clause typology: two structural types, "subordinators" terminology

A syntactic split between **verb-oriented** adverb clauses (direct adverbializer suffix) and **noun-oriented** adverb clauses (two sub-patterns: `<Noun Clause + N + adverbial particle>` or `<Adnoun Clause + N + adverbial particle>`). Chang's own minimal pair (*palam-i pwu-nikka* vs. *palam-i pwul-ki ttaymwun-ey*) shows these two structurally different strategies producing **semantically identical** causal clauses — a real syntactic distinction without a corresponding semantic one, at least for this pair. Chang coins the umbrella term **"subordinators"** for all clause-final endings of both types.

### 9. §6.3.3 — Eleven semantic roles of adverb clauses, with a comprehensive subordinator inventory per role

**By far the largest genuinely new contribution of this part.** Chang organizes the entire adverb-clause system around **eleven semantic roles** — time, place, cause/reason, condition, concession, contrast, circumstance, result, purpose/intention, proportion, comparison — giving an explicit, labeled list of every verbal and nominal subordinator per role with worked examples (see Vocabulary table above). Two cross-cutting compositional patterns recur: (a) the **adnominalizer-plus-bound-noun template** `[-l/-n ADNZ] + [bound/relational noun] + [case particle]` generates the bulk of the time/cause-reason/condition/concession/contrast/comparison nominal subordinators just by swapping the bound noun (see Morpheme Breakdown section); (b) a **"longer form = more emphatic/hypothetical" iconicity principle**, stated explicitly for the concessive set (p.160) and implicitly visible in the conditional set (`-myen` vs. the longer, historically-compound `-ta/la-myen`).

### 10. §6.3.3.4 — Condition: neutral vs. hypothetical, and the counterfactual construction

**Neutral conditions** leave fulfillment genuinely open; **hypothetical conditions** convey the speaker's belief the condition will not/does not/did not hold, grading into genuine **counterfactuals**. The dedicated counterfactual construction `...(past)-myen, ...(past)-l-the-i-n-tey` stacks a second retrospective modal/evidential layer (`-the-`) onto an already-past-marked clause; the retrospective `-tela-myen` variant is noted as the more commonly used form in practice. The `-ketun`-clause carries a distinct **matrix-clause restriction**: the matrix must be a request, proposal, or promise — a plain declarative is explicitly starred ungrammatical, showing near-synonymous conditionals are not freely interchangeable.

### 11. §6.3.3.5 — Concession: the frozen permission idiom `-(e)to + toyta/cohta`

Beyond its plain concessive use, `-(e)to` combines with `toyta`/`cohta` to form a fully frozen, grammaticalized **permission** idiom ("may") — presented as a distinct lexicalized pattern, not a compositional reading, paralleling the auxiliary-grammaticalization pattern `019` documents for Korean's quotative-contraction cascade.

### 12. §6.3.3.11 — Comparison: structural ambiguity in three-argument comparatives

With three arguments present, Chang shows the same two-way structural ambiguity English has for the equivalent sentence (the omitted comparandum reads either as another object of the same subject, or as a second subject's parallel action) — stated explicitly as "structural ambiguities, which run parallel to those in English" (p.167), one of the few places in this book where Chang directly benchmarks Korean against English rather than describing it on its own terms (flagged per this project's own "analyze languages on their own terms" principle — the parallel here is the source's own analytical claim).

### 13. §6.4 — Mixed Sentences: three combination patterns and the CL/tree-structure notational apparatus

A "mixed sentence" combines coordination and subordination; Chang distinguishes three organizing patterns: (1) two subordinate clauses stacked under one superordinate main clause; (2) one subordinate clause governing two coordinated main clauses; (3) two coordinated clauses, each independently containing its own subordinate clause — each illustrated with a worked example and full tree diagram. The chapter's most complex worked example (ex. 96) is an eight-clause sentence fully decomposed clause-by-clause (CL[1]–CL[8]) with coreference tracking (`[*]` tags) — demonstrating Chang's broader analytical method: Korean's apparent long-sentence complexity is compositional stacking of a small closed set of clause-combining devices (ADNZ, ADVZ, NOMZ, CONJ), not a proliferation of distinct construction types.

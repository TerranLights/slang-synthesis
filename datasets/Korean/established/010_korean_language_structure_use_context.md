# Korean — Established Grammar/Vocabulary: *The Korean Language: Structure, Use and Context*

**Source:** Jae Jung Song, *The Korean Language: Structure, Use and Context* (Routledge, 2005;
Taylor & Francis e-Library edition, 2006). Full book, all 7 chapters, printed pp. 1–177 (PDF pages
~18–194 of 202; front matter i–xvi and back-matter references/index excluded as bibliographic
apparatus, not source content). This is a linguistic-description volume aimed at English-speaking
learners and general linguists — not a graded teaching-grammar course — so, per the coverage rule,
its own structural/typological analysis and sociolinguistic commentary is prioritized as
grammar-point prose over building a large flat vocabulary table, the same way Kornai's *On Hungarian
Morphology* was handled for Hungarian. This source has a clean, real text layer (`pdftotext -layout`);
no vision-reading was needed and no marginalia risk applies.

**Coverage note.** Chapters 2 (phonology) and 3 (writing systems: Hankul/Hanca) are covered only in
summary — they are largely descriptive/orthographic and lower-yield for slang-mechanics purposes
than the book's real strength. Chapters 1 (sociolinguistic context), 4 (vocabulary/deference), 5
(grammar/honorific and speech-level endings), 6 (discourse-level register) and 7 (North/South
divergence) are covered in depth, because this is precisely the content flagged as highest-value for
this project: **the book is an unusually strong source for Korean's honorific/speech-level system and
register variation**, and that content is covered in full below, not abbreviated.

## Grammar points

### 1. Sociocultural substrate of the honorific system: age, gender, *chwulsin* (Ch. 1, pp. 10–13)

Song grounds the entire honorific/speech-level system in Confucian social organization before any
grammar is introduced, and flags this explicitly as necessary background (Ch. 4/5 cross-reference
his own Ch. 1 discussion). Three variables recur through the whole book as the axes that determine
speech-level/honorific choice:

- **Age/seniority.** Absolute and *relative* — Koreans do not use given names for anyone senior, use
  kinship terms (*halapeci* 'grandfather', *halmeni* 'grandmother', *acessi* 'uncle', *acwumeni*
  'aunt') to address non-relatives of appropriate age, and a **3-or-more-year age gap is treated as
  socially "substantial"** even between otherwise-equal-status people (e.g. friends) — a concrete
  threshold worth keeping for any Korean-flavored slang/address-term modeling.
- **Gender.** Confucian framing (men likened to heaven, women to earth); reflected grammatically in
  the asymmetric availability of speech levels to men vs. women (see §4 below) and lexically in
  kinship asymmetry: *halapeci*/*halmeni* default to **paternal** grandparents; the Sino-Korean
  prefix *oy-* ('outside') marks the maternal side (*oy-halapeci*, *oy-halmeni* — maternal
  grandparents are literally "outside" the family).
- **Chwulsin** ("origin of body/self" — background/group-affiliation identity: hometown, school,
  university). Song treats this as a third, independent axis of Korean social identity alongside age
  and gender, with its own routine interrogative formula (*eti chwulsin i-sipnikka?* 'Where are you
  from?', *enu tayhak chwulsin i-sipnikka?* 'Which university are you from?') and its own concrete
  social artifact: *hakpen*, the first two digits of a university student ID number, used to
  establish relative seniority within a shared-*chwulsin* group (e.g. "83" = enrolled 1983).
  *Chwulsin* does not itself feed directly into the honorific grammar the way age/gender do, but it's
  the sociolinguistic frame that assigns people into groups within which age-based deference is then
  calculated — worth keeping distinct from register mechanics proper, but as scaffolding for any
  slang-formation analysis of Korean in-group/out-group marking.

### 2. Phonology (Ch. 2, summary only, pp. 22–44)

19 consonants in a three-way stop contrast (lax/plain, tensed, aspirated — e.g. /p, pp, ph/), unusual
cross-linguistically; vowels and semivowels; syllable-level sound adjustment (e.g. word-initial
/l/,/n/ deletion/alternation before /i/,/j/ in Standard South Korean — directly relevant to the
North/South divergence in §8 below); stress/intonation patterns tied to sentence-type marking (a
falling contour for statements, rising for questions, sharply falling for commands, falling-then-
level for proposals — this last detail recurs as a genuine grammatical fact in the speech-level
discussion, §4, not just a phonetic aside); loanword pronunciation adaptation (discussed more fully
under vocabulary, §5).

### 3. Writing systems (Ch. 3, summary only, pp. 45–65)

Hankul (Korea's featural alphabet, with its own historical/design controversy over invention vs.
appropriation) vs. Hanca (Chinese characters, still used to disambiguate Sino-Korean homophones in
South Korea); the "Original Form Principle" governing Hankul spelling conventions; romanization
system choice. Not deeply mined here — orthographic mechanics have low direct payoff for slang
mechanics — but the Hankul/Hanca split is the same fault line that reappears as the
native/Sino-Korean/loanword lexical stratification in §5, and North Korea's Hankul-only policy is
picked up again in §8.

### 4. Word classes: nouns, pronouns (Ch. 4, pp. 70–83)

- **Nouns** carry no grammatical gender and are not obligatorily number-marked (plural *-tul* is
  optional and used mainly with human nouns; a demonstrative + noun combination, e.g. *ku salam-tul*
  'those people', or a second-person pronoun, forces its use). Korean instead has a large,
  productive **numeral classifier** system: nouns being counted require a classifier bound to the
  numeral (*chayk twu kwen* 'book two volume' = 'two books'), e.g. *myeng* (humans), *mali*
  (animals), *tay* (vehicles), *cang* (flat/sheet objects), *pel* (garments), *sal*/*sey* (age),
  *kay* (general inanimate). Classifier choice is grammatically strict — combining *myeng* with a
  non-human noun (even a beloved pet) is ungrammatical.
- **Bound/defective nouns** cannot stand alone and require a preceding modifier — either classifiers
  (above) or a second class including *i* 'person', *pun* 'respected person', *kes* 'thing', *kos*
  'place', *ttay* 'time' (e.g. *i-kos* 'here' from *i* 'this' + *kos* 'place'). Note that *pun* vs.
  *i* is itself an honorific/neutral distinction baked into the bound-noun inventory (see also the
  vulgar bound nouns *nom*/*nyen* used in constructing third-person "pronouns," §4 vocabulary table).
- **Personal pronoun system is honorific-stratified and comparatively avoided altogether.** Korean's
  third-person "pronouns" are actually productively built from demonstrative + (bound) noun (*i/ku/ce*
  'this/that/that-over-there' + *pun* 'respected person' / *i* 'person' / *nom* 'bastard' / *nyen*
  'girl, vulgar' / ordinary nouns like *salam* 'person'), giving Korean an open-ended, tripartite
  **honorific / neutral / vulgar** pronoun inventory unlike English's fixed *he/she/it/they*. There is
  **no honorific first-person pronoun** (Song's gloss: "people do not pay respect to themselves") —
  only neutral (*na*, plural *wuli(-tul)*) vs. humble (*ce*, plural *ce-huy(-tul)*). Second-person
  pronouns are numerous but each is socially booby-trapped (*ne*, *caney*, *caki*, *tangsin*, *tayk*,
  *elusin(-ney)* — see vocabulary table for the full social-rule breakdown Song gives for each), and
  critically **there is no neutral second-person pronoun usable toward an adult of unknown status** —
  which is why Koreans systematically substitute kinship terms/titles for second- and third-person
  reference instead of risking a pronoun. A striking discourse-level consequence: Korean narrative
  prose tracks referents through zero-anaphora and repeated names/kin-terms rather than pronouns —
  Song's examples (his (6)–(7)) show a whole passage about "our daughter" with **no pronoun at all**
  after the first mention, referent tracked purely by discourse coherence.
- **First-person plural substitution rule**: for anything evoking group-belonging (family, school,
  country, workplace), Korean requires the plural *wuli* 'our' even for a sole referent — *wuli emeni*
  'our mother' is obligatory in contexts where English "my mother" is normal, even for an only child.

### 5. Vocabulary strata: native Korean, Sino-Korean, loanwords (Ch. 4, pp. 83–90)

This is a genuinely three-way lexical stratification with its own register logic, directly relevant
to modeling formality/slang register shifting:

- **Native Korean** (~45%): basic human actions/states, body parts, kin terms, natural
  objects/celestial bodies, the whole particle class (grammatical morphemes are essentially never
  borrowed), onomatopoeia/mimetics. Preferred in **informal/colloquial** register.
- **Sino-Korean** (~52%): overwhelmingly disyllabic (built from two Chinese-character morphemes each
  carrying independent meaning, highly compositional — e.g. *hak* 'studying' + *sayng* 'person' =
  *haksayng* 'student'; *hak* recombines with *kyo* 'teaching', *nyen* 'year', *kwun* 'zone' to give
  *hakkyo* 'school', *haknyen* 'school year', *hakkwun* 'school zone'). Three historical sub-sources:
  (i) borrowed directly from classical Chinese texts, (ii) Sino-Japanese words absorbed via Japan's
  earlier modernization (e.g. *sahoy* 'society', paralleling Chinese *shèhuì* < Japanese *shakai*),
  (iii) coined in Korea from Chinese characters. Because they've been nativized for centuries, native
  speakers frequently cannot tell Sino-Korean words from native ones (Song's example: most Koreans
  don't know *kimchi* itself is Sino-Korean). **Preferred in formal/learned/"pedantic" register** —
  directly parallel to Latinate vs. Germanic register-splitting in English (his own comparison:
  *puerile* vs. *childish*, *cardiac disease* vs. *heart disease*, *psychiatrist* vs. *shrink*).
- **Loanwords** (~3%, but growing; >20,000 counted, >90% English-derived, some indirectly via
  Japanese). Systematically **not** semantically identical to their English source: (a) phonologically
  reshaped to Korean sound patterns (*sepisu* 'service', *phillum* 'film'); (b) meaning narrowed to
  one borrowing-context sense only (*eyksuthula* 'extra' means *only* 'movie extra', never the
  adjective/adverb senses); (c) meaning sometimes shifts entirely (*khenning* < "cunning" means
  'cheating on an exam'; *phulenthu* < "front" means only 'hotel reception desk'); (d) frequently
  truncated (*aphathu* 'apartment', *swuphe* < "supermarket," collision-prone: *phulo* can mean
  'professional', 'programme', *or* 'proletariat'); (e) sometimes recombined into new coinages opaque
  even to English speakers (*senthing* 'car-window tinting' < *sen* "sun" + *thing* "coating"); (f)
  English phrases compressed into acronym-like forms unintelligible to native English speakers
  (*phiti*/PD 'TV producer', *oeyl*/OL 'female office worker', *tiphii*/DPE 'photo developing'). A
  named folk category, **Kacca Yenge** ("fake English"), exists for exactly this phenomenon, with its
  own corrective dictionaries — a striking piece of explicit metalinguistic awareness worth flagging
  for any invented-slang mechanic modeling loanword nativization/reanalysis.
- **Register competition between the three strata** is explicit and stylistically loaded, not free
  variation: doublets/triplets persist with non-overlapping usage niches (*ilcali* native vs.
  *cikcang* Sino-Korean, both 'job', but *cikcang* only white-collar; *calu-ta* native / *celtan-hata*
  Sino-Korean / *khethu-hata* < Eng. "cut," each restricted to different cutting contexts) and
  formality/generation/relationship dictate which stratum is chosen for the same concept (Song's own
  example: *chwungko* (Sino-Korean) 'advice' from an elder to a junior vs. *etupaisu* (< Eng. "advice")
  between young peers). Some native words are being displaced outright by Sino-Korean or loanword
  competitors (*ay* 'intestine' → *cang*; *sunggangki* 'lift' → *eyllipeyithe* < "elevator"; *cengkwu*
  'tennis' → *theynisu*).

### 6. Word formation: compounding, derivation, abbreviation (Ch. 4, pp. 90–92)

- **Compounding** is Korean's dominant word-formation strategy (more so than derivation), and applies
  within each lexical stratum separately as a rule of thumb (native+native, Sino-Korean+Sino-Korean),
  though loanword+loanword compounds also occur and can be semantically opaque to English speakers
  even when both halves are recognizably English-derived (*payk* "back" + *mile* "mirror" =
  *paykmile* 'rear-view mirror'; *ai* "eye" + *syophing* "shopping" = *aisyophing* 'window-shopping').
- **Derivation** via bound affixes, both prefixing (rarer: *mayn-* 'bare' → *mayn-son* 'bare hands',
  *tes-* 'additional' → *tes-mun* 'outer door') and suffixing (more productive). **Two suffixes are
  directly slang-relevant**: **-cil** 'activity, often derogatory' (*mangchi-cil* 'hammering' <
  *mangchi* 'hammer'; *yok-cil* 'swearing' < *yok* 'obscenity'; *kyeycip-cil* 'extramarital affairs
  with women' < *kyeycip* 'young woman' — the derogatory coloring is not incidental, it's the
  suffix's own listed sense) and **-kkwun** 'doer/-er (of an activity, often with a downmarket
  connotation)' (*il-kkwun* 'labourer', *nolum-kkwun* 'gambler', *cangsa-kkwun* 'merchant'). Both are
  productive, native-Korean, and carry a built-in register coloring lower than the neutral agentive —
  exactly the kind of derivational machinery a slang-mechanics pass on Korean should watch for as an
  already-standard resource that slang could extend further, per the agglutinative-typology caveat in
  `00_Word_Concept_and_Morphological_Typology_Guide.md` (ordinary productive morphology vs.
  slang-marked extension of it).
- **Abbreviation** of multi-word phrases is a recognized, productive minor word-formation type
  (*nosa* < *notongca sayongca* 'employee and employer'; *ipsi* < *iphak sihem* 'university entrance
  exam' — the latter still coexists with its source phrase, the former has fully displaced it).

### 7. Deference in Korean: lexical honorific/humble doublets (Ch. 4, pp. 93–95)

Song distinguishes two *lexical* (not yet grammatical — that's Ch. 5) deference strategies: paying
respect to a socially superior referent (**honorific** vocabulary) vs. lowering oneself relative to
that referent (**humble** vocabulary). Both operate over closed sets of noun and verb doublets with
**no formal/phonological relationship** between the neutral and honorific/humble member (e.g. neutral
*pap* 'meal' vs. honorific *cinci* 'meal [for a respected person]' — entirely different roots, not a
regular affixal alternation). See the vocabulary table for the specific doublets (Tables 4.2–4.4 in
the source). Where no suppletive honorific verb exists, the **productive honorific suffix -si**
attaches directly to the neutral verb stem instead (formalized fully in Ch. 5, §8 below) — so the
lexical-doublet system and the grammatical-suffix system are two layers of the same overall
deference mechanism, the doublets being the irregular/lexicalized core and *-si* the regular,
default-case periphery.

### 8. Grammatical endings on the verb: tense, modality, subject honorification (Ch. 5, pp. 117–123)

The verb is described as the single most information-dense word in a Korean sentence — routinely
stacking honorific, tense, modal, and speech-level morphology onto one stem, sometimes as the
sentence's only overt word (subject/object frequently omitted when recoverable from context; see
§10). Song's own illustrative example, worth keeping as a canonical case:

```
ttena-si-keyss-supnikka?
leave-HON-INTEND-DEFERENTIAL.Q
'(Honorable sir,) do you intend to leave?'
```
— one phonological word, three stacked grammatical endings (honorific subject-agreement, intention/
modal, deferential-question speech-level ending), with the entire subject noun phrase (the hearer)
zero-marked and recoverable only from the honorific/deferential morphology itself.

- **Tense**: zero-marked non-past (present or future, disambiguated by context/adverbials) vs.
  *-ess/-ass* (allomorphy conditioned by stem-final vowel; *-y(e)ss* for *ha-* 'to do') past. A subset
  of verbs (*ip-ta* 'wear', *talm-ta* 'resemble', *kyelhon-ha-ta* 'marry') require the past-tense
  ending to describe a **present resultant state**, not a past event (*yenghi-ka phalan chima-lul
  ip-ess-ta* = 'Yonghee IS WEARING a blue skirt', not 'wore'); reporting the genuinely past event for
  these verbs requires doubling the ending (*ip-ess-ess-ta* 'wore [and no longer is]').
- **Modality**: *-keyss* expresses intention (1st person) or speaker judgment/inference (elsewhere);
  *-te* ('retrospective') marks that the speaker (or, in questions, the hearer) has first-hand
  perceptual knowledge of the reported event, a grammatical evidentiality distinction English only
  achieves periphrastically (*I saw Megan cry* vs. bare *Megan cried*). Note *-te* triggers a
  speech-level-ending allomorph shift (plain *-ta* → *-la* after *-te*).
- **Subject honorification**: *-si* (after vowel-final stems) / *-usi* (after consonant-final stems)
  marks the speaker's deference toward the subject referent, and co-occurs with an honorific
  nominative particle *-kkeyse* (vs. neutral *-i/-ka*) on the subject noun phrase itself — e.g.
  *halapeci-kkeyse ... nayly-e-ka-si-ess-ta* 'Grandfather went down [to Pusan]', where **both** the
  particle and the verb ending carry the same honorific agreement, a kind of honorific "concord."
  Using the honorific suffix on a verb that already has a suppletive honorific counterpart is
  ungrammatical (*\*halapeci-kkeyse cwuk-usi-ess-ta* is worse than merely informal — the correct
  choice is the suppletive *tolaka-si-ess-ta* 'passed away'). Song notes this lexical-suppletion
  choice is **sociocultural, not euphemistic** — unlike English *died* vs. *passed away*, which is
  about avoiding unpleasantness, the Korean choice is strictly about the referent's social seniority.
  Honorific agreement can also be triggered by a possessor within the subject NP when the possessive
  relationship is "intimate" (body parts, kinship, personal belongings — *halapeci-uy oyn son-i
  pulphyen-ha-si-ta* 'Grandfather's left hand is disabled', honorific verb ending triggered by
  *halapeci* even though the grammatical subject is "hand") but *not* for non-intimate relationships
  like a boss's secretary (*\*yeypp-usi-ta* is ungrammatical for 'his secretary is pretty', even
  though "his" refers to a respected person).

### 9. The speech-level system (Ch. 5, pp. 123–129) — the book's core register content

**Six speech levels**, forming a single continuum of deference (least → most): **plain, intimate,
familiar, semi-formal, polite, deferential**. Each combines with (at least) four sentence types —
statement, question, command, proposal — giving, in Song's own framing, "at least 24 different
speech-level endings to choose from... No language in the world is known to surpass Korean in this
regard." Endings (Table 5.1 in source, reproduced here in full since it is the single most
information-dense table in the book):

| Speech level | Statement | Question | Command | Proposal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Plain | -(n)ta | -ni/-(nu)nya | -ela/-ala | -ca |
| Intimate | -e/-a | -e/-a | -e/-a | -e/-a |
| Familiar | -ney | -na/-nunka | -key | -sey |
| Semi-formal | -o | -o | -(u)o | -(u)psita |
| Polite | -eyo/-ayo | -eyo/-ayo | -eyo/-ayo | -eyo/-ayo |
| Deferential | -(su)pnita | -(su)pnikka | -(u)sipsio | -(u)sipsita |

(*-a* after a preceding /a/ or /o/ syllable, *-e* otherwise, for the allomorphic pairs.)

Every Korean sentence **must** close with exactly one of these endings — hence they're also called
"sentence enders." Two of the six (familiar, semi-formal) are marked as nearly obsolete in
contemporary usage; semi-formal in particular is now essentially archaic, surviving mainly in
historical TV dramas. **Practical working system: four levels** — plain, intimate, polite,
deferential — cross-classified by two independent dimensions: deference (plain/intimate = less;
polite/deferential = more) and formality (plain/deferential = formal; intimate/polite = informal).
Notably, intimate and polite endings are invariant across all four sentence types (the polite set is
literally the intimate set + *-yo*), while the other levels vary the ending by sentence type —
Song suggests this simplicity may reflect their high communicative load as the two most-used levels.

**Who uses which level, concretely** (this is the book's most granular sociolinguistic payoff, worth
preserving in detail for slang-register modeling):

- **Plain**: between friends/siblings with ≤2-year gap; parent/teacher → young child. Using it toward
  any adult (esp. one not a close age-peer) reads as rude/condescending — a genuine insult register.
- **Intimate** (*panmal*, "half-talk"): close friends/siblings pre-middle-age; young child → adult
  family member (esp. mother, less so father); husband → (younger) wife. Crucially **asymmetric**: an
  age-gapped wife may be unable to reciprocate at the intimate level and must use polite instead, even
  in a relationship the husband addresses intimately; and even husband-wife intimate use is
  **suppressed in front of parents or in public**, reverting to polite/deferential — level choice is
  audience-sensitive in real time, not a fixed dyadic setting.
- **Familiar**: superior → junior with "reasonable courtesy" (e.g. male mentor → younger male protégé
  or son-in-law); **almost never used by women** (one narrow exception: female adults, especially
  mothers, use familiar-level statement/question endings when baby-talking to pre-verbal infants).
  Effectively requires the speaker to be ~50+ to use "without inhibition or awkwardness" — a younger
  speaker using it reads as pretentious/acting-older-than-their-age.
- **Semi-formal**: near-obsolete, archaic-coded, superior→inferior with a touch more courtesy than
  familiar; heard almost exclusively in period-drama dialogue now.
- **Polite**: the default courteous register regardless of the interlocutor's social status, usable
  toward anyone old enough to be an adult (university-age+); the level virtually all adult women use
  toward other adults regardless of the other's gender; the level a senior high schooler is flattered
  to be addressed with as recognition of adult status.
- **Deferential**: highest deference, reserved for unquestionable seniors, never used toward an equal
  or inferior (polite covers that ground instead). Dominant in TV/radio news, public
  lectures/sermons, and large-audience address generally — Song cites research that public speakers
  actually **alternate deferential/polite by information status** within a single speech (deferential
  for new information, polite for shared/repeated common-ground information), i.e. speech-level choice
  can track discourse structure, not only social relationship.

**Directionality is asymmetric and largely one-way**: speech level shifts **upward** over time (as
friends age, marry, have children — they raise their mutual register) but essentially never downward
between unequal-status people; a deliberate downward shift is itself a marked, aggressive speech act
(the idiom *mal-ul mak noh-ta*, literally 'to put speech down carelessly', names exactly this move,
and it is described as causing lasting relational damage). Between true equals, downward shift
(stranger→polite, then intimate as friendship matures) is possible, but even that reverts upward in
front of third parties.

**Status resolution when age and socioeconomic rank conflict**: rank generally wins (a younger boss
outranks an older subordinate for level-choice purposes), but age is never fully erased — the boss is
still expected to extend more courtesy to an older subordinate than to younger ones, even while using
a lower speech level toward the latter than a truly age-matched equal would get.

**Gender asymmetry in the speech-level system is explicit, not incidental**: women have a
structurally smaller repertoire (no real access to the familiar level; more constrained even within
levels they do use, e.g. the wife/husband intimate-level asymmetry above) — this is presented as a
synchronic fact about the standard language, not commentary on any individual speaker's choice.

### 10. Compound verbs / multiple-verb constructions (Ch. 5, pp. 129–135, summary)

Korean expresses much of what English marks periphrastically or lexically via **verb-serialization**:
a semantically "bleached" second verb attached via the linker *-e/-a* marks aspect/manner distinctions
— *peli-ta* 'throw away' → completive/unexpected-result marker (*kaph-a-peli-ess-e* 'paid back
(completely)'); *ka-ta* 'go' → gradual/ongoing marker (*ssek-e-ka-pnita* 'is rotting (gradually)');
*po-ta* 'see' → attempt/experiential marker (*mek-e-po-ass-e* 'tried eating [it]'); *twu-ta*
'keep/place' → anticipatory-action marker (*sa-a-twu-ess-e* 'bought [it] (in anticipation)'). Each of
these verbs' serialized/grammaticalized sense is described as semantically remote from its literal,
free-standing meaning — a live example of the same grammaticalization pathway (full lexical verb →
light/auxiliary-like marker) attested cross-linguistically, useful context for any Korean slang
mechanism built on reanalyzing an ordinary verb's serialized use.

### 11. Discourse-level formality: omission, contraction, register (Ch. 6, pp. 150–157)

- **Register correlates systematically with both speech level and lexical stratum simultaneously**:
  formal writing (textbooks, news, academic prose) favors plain-level endings and Sino-Korean
  vocabulary; public-facing persuasive writing (ads, notices, signage) favors deferential-level
  endings; personal/informal writing (letters to friends/siblings) favors intimate/polite endings and
  native-Korean vocabulary — i.e. the speech-level axis (Ch. 5) and the native/Sino-Korean/loanword
  axis (Ch. 4) co-vary as a single register system, not two independent ones.
- **Role-marking particles and full noun phrases are freely omittable when recoverable from context or
  verb semantics/animacy** — a sentence can be reduced to bare nouns without their particles
  (*kiho swukcey ha-yss-e?* for *kiho-ka swukcey-lul ha-yss-e?*), or in conversational exchange, to
  **the verb alone** (a full reply can be just *pely-ess-eyo* 'threw [it] away-POLITE', object and
  subject both recovered from the preceding question).
- **The verb itself is the one thing that essentially never gets omitted**, even under maximal
  colloquial ellipsis — because it's the sole obligatory carrier of (a) the event's participant
  relations and (b) the speaker's speech-level marking toward the hearer. Song frames dropping the
  verb as "unthinkable" specifically because it would leave the interpersonal-deference marking
  unexpressed — a strong claim about how central the honorific/speech-level system is to what counts
  as a well-formed Korean utterance at all.
- **Contraction** is systematic in informal speech: topic particle *-(n)un* → *-n* after 1st/2nd
  person pronouns (*na-nun* → *na-n*, *ce-nun* → *ce-n*, *ne-nun* → *ne-n*); role particles similarly
  reduce (*nwukwu-lul* → *nwukwu-l*, *mues-ul* → *mwe-l*); demonstrative-*kes* nominative sequences
  contract (*i kes-i* → *i key*); negative *ani ha-ta* → *anh-ta*. Learners are advised to acquire
  full forms before contracted ones, and native speakers are noted to typically avoid contractions
  when speaking to learners.
- **Fillers, responders, connectors are speech-level-sensitive too** — not register-neutral discourse
  particles. Responders like 'yes' (*ung* intimate / *yey*,*ney* polite), 'no' (*ani(-ya)* intimate /
  *ani-yo* polite / *ani-pnita* deferential), 'right' (*kulay(-yo)* / *kuleh-supnita*), and 'what did
  you say?' (*mwe-lako(-yo)*) each have level-specific forms, and at the deferential level, where no
  suitably deferential short responder exists for some functions, Korean substitutes a full
  circumlocution instead (*tasi hanpen malssum ha-y-cwu-si-keyss-supnikka?* 'could you say it again,
  please?' rather than a deferential-level 'what?').

### 12. Social formulaic expressions (Ch. 6, pp. 157–162, summarized)

Fixed, largely non-compositional politeness-routine expressions (greetings, farewells, thanks,
apology, invitations) exchanged in adjacency pairs; Song emphasizes these must be learned as whole
units (like idioms, Ch. 4) precisely because register/etiquette in Korean is realized as much through
fixed formulae as through the productive grammar covered above. Discussion is centered on the
deferential level "because the use of social formulaic expressions is most prominent or elaborate at
this particular speech level."

### 13. North Korean vs. South Korean divergence (Ch. 7, pp. 163–176)

Standard South Korean = **Phyocwune** ("Standard Language"); Standard North Korean = **Munhwae**
("Cultured Language", codified 1966 under Kim Il Sung). This is the book's own explicit
dialect/register-annotated content — flagged per the coverage rule as high-value regional-tag data:

- **Phonology**: Munhwae retains word-initial /l/, /n/ that Phyocwune deletes/alternates before
  /i/,/j/ — e.g. Phyocwune *i* vs. Munhwae *ni* 'tooth', *ilon* vs. *lilon* 'theory', *yeseng* vs.
  *nyeseng* 'female'. Prescriptively enforced in the North, "far much stronger" (Song's words) than
  a comparable prescriptive rule in English.
- **Lexicon**: North Korea's nativization campaign coined an estimated ~50,000 native-Korean
  replacement words for Sino-Korean/loanword vocabulary (about half have since reverted). Examples:
  Sino-Korean *tayyang* 'ocean' → native *hanpata* (lit. 'big sea'); Sino-Korean *hongswu* 'flood' →
  *khunmul* (lit. 'big water'); English *phama* 'perm' → *pokkum meli* (lit. 'fried hair'); English
  *taiethu* 'diet' → *salkkakkki* (lit. 'flesh-cutting'). North Korean loanwords skew
  Russian/Japanese-influenced where South Korean loanwords skew English-influenced (*khep* vs.
  *koppu* 'cup'; *thayngkhu* vs. *ttangkhu* 'tank'), **except** for foreign place names, where North
  Korean forms are closer to source-language pronunciation and South Korean forms are
  English-mediated/Anglicized (*pukkyeng* vs. *peyicing* 'Beijing'; *hengkali* vs. *weyngkulia*
  'Hungary'). Feudal-era honorific/status titles were purged from Munhwae (*akassi* 'feudal lord's
  daughter', *taykam* 'feudal lord', *manim* 'feudal lord's wife') and survive in Phyocwune only in
  historical-fiction registers — *akassi* has since been repurposed to mean simply 'young unmarried
  woman'. Some ordinary words carry ideologically shifted senses in the North (*nodong*/*lodong*
  'labor' broadened to include political work "useful for the state"; *epei* 'father/parent' narrowed
  in the North to refer specifically to Kim Il Sung).
- **Grammar**: Munhwae is reported to have collapsed the six-level Phyocwune speech-level system
  into **three nominal levels — polite, equal, low** — explicitly for ideological "classless society"
  reasons, but Song notes this is a *nominal*, not substantive, simplification: Munhwae's "polite"
  level subsumes Phyocwune's deferential+polite, and its "equal" subsumes Phyocwune's intimate+
  semi-formal — the same underlying distinctions largely persist, just regrouped and relabeled. This
  is a striking datapoint for any invented-slang/dialect-divergence modeling: a politically-motivated
  register "simplification" that turns out to preserve most of the original distinctions under new
  labels.
- **Writing**: North Korean dictionaries order consonant/vowel letters differently (tensed stops
  grouped en masse after /h/, rather than immediately after their corresponding lax stop as in South
  Korean dictionaries); several words are spelled differently outright (*a.nay* vs. *an.hay* 'wife';
  *tal.kyal* vs. *talk.al* 'egg').

## Summary for downstream mechanics analysis

The single highest-value finding for slang-mechanics work is that **Korean's honorific/speech-level
system is not a peripheral politeness add-on but the obligatory grammatical core of every sentence**
(every utterance must close with one of six speech-level endings; the verb — the carrier of that
ending — is the one constituent that survives even maximal colloquial ellipsis). Any invented slang
built on this language family should treat **speech-level shifting itself** (deliberately using an
inappropriate/mismatched level, per the *mal-ul mak noh-ta* "downshift as aggression" datapoint in
§9) as a primary slang-formation mechanism in its own right — alongside more familiar mechanisms like
lexical substitution or loanword reanalysis (the *Kacca Yenge* "fake English" phenomenon in §5 is a
ready-made real-world precedent for exactly this kind of playful/subversive loanword mechanics). The
three-way native/Sino-Korean/loanword lexical stratification (§5) gives a second, orthogonal register
axis worth keeping distinct from speech-level in any later mechanics model. The already-productive,
derogation-coded derivational suffixes *-cil* and *-kkwun* (§6) are the clearest existing bridge
between "ordinary agglutinative productivity" and "slang-marked morphological play," per the
typology-guide's own caution about not conflating the two in an agglutinative language.

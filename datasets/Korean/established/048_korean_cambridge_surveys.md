### Grammar points

1. **Seven-zone dialectal classification.** Korean (spanning both North and South) divides into seven zones correlating roughly with administrative provinces: Hamkyeng, Phyengan, Central, Chwungcheng, Kyengsang, Cenla, Ceycwu. Formation is geographic but also historical/political — Ceycwu's distinctiveness from mainland isolation; the Kyengsang/Cenla split traces to their having been different ancient kingdoms; the Hamkyeng/Central split traces to a centuries-long military frontier against Manchu tribes, after which Phyengan was resettled from Hwanghay and Hamkyeng from Kyengsang (explaining why Phyengan resembles Central and Hamkyeng resembles Kyengsang despite the intervening geography).

2. **Prosodeme isoglosses: tone vs. length vs. neither.** Middle Korean was a three-tone language. Tonemes survive today only in Kyengsang and Hamkyeng (plus pockets of Kangwen/N.Phyengan). Central and most other zones instead retain vowel length at the position where a historical rising tone occurred. Ceycwu and some others have neither. Even among tone-retaining regions, the actual tonal system (number of surface contrasts) differs by region.

3. **The MK ɔ/z/β reflex isoglosses, plus word-medial k, initial l/n, and palatalization isoglosses.** These classic dialectological diagnostics surface differently by zone and don't perfectly align with each other or with the seven-zone boundaries — "two or more dialectal zones may overlap in certain particular features." Palatalization gives a clean three-way phonological gradient running roughly north (none) → center (partial) → south (fullest), independent of the l/n-retention isogloss (which instead groups the far north together against everywhere else).

4. **Per-zone characterizations are multidimensional, not just phonological.** Each zone section layers phonological features together with morpho-lexical features (zone-specific fossilized nominal suffixes, many diminutive/derogatory in the southern zones) and syntactic features — above all each zone's own distinctive sentence-ender inventory across speech levels, treated as one of the most diagnostic markers of dialect identity. Ceycwu is flagged as retaining the largest, most distinctive set of unique sentence enders of any zone, alongside its unique MK-ɔ vowel retention — the most linguistically conservative/divergent Korean variety overall.

5. **North/South Korean divergence: policy framing and four linguistic domains.** A politico-social split layered on the geographic zones, not a geographic dialect itself. Both Koreas pursued parallel post-1945 anti-illiteracy/language-purification campaigns with "essentially the same spirit but extremely different results." North Korea's 1966-instituted Phyengyang-based "Cultured Language" standard rests on Kim Il Song's Teachings directing native-word coinage, limited loanwords, and banned Chinese characters; Kim explicitly denounced Seoul-based Standard Language as "the speech of bourgeoisie, a jumble filled with Western, Japanese, and Chinese elements." Divergence is organized into four domains: (1) pronunciation — chiefly the word-initial l/n isogloss now promoted to Northern national-standard status; (2) Hankul spelling conventions (deferred to Ch.6); (3) lexicon — the domain "affected most," driven by CL's ~5,000 North-coined replacement words against SL's openness to Sino-Korean coinage and 10,000+ English loanwords; (4) meanings and styles — denotational/connotational drift in shared vocabulary under CL's socialist-ideological framing, including a striking valence-inversion case (*pang-co-hata*). The source frames phonology as comparatively conservative (largely just the North formalizing one existing northern-dialect isogloss) and the lexical/ideological-meaning domains as most actively diverging.

### Grammar points

1. **Overall lexical-stock composition and the "perceived nativeness" asymmetry.** The lexicon splits ~35% native, 60% Sino-Korean, 5% loan. A genuinely counterintuitive finding: because of historical anteriority and productivity, **SK words are perceived as "more native-like" than loan words**, even though both are equally non-native in origin — important for any downstream register/authenticity modeling. New loanwords frequently compete directly with existing SK coinages for the same concept, with outcomes ranging from full displacement to stable coexistence. Four systemic effects of non-native vocabulary are identified: synonym-set proliferation with stratified connotation; irrecoverable native-vocabulary loss; phonological-system disruption (modern word-initial-l loanwords disrupt the otherwise-exceptionless native/SK ban); and morphological effects (Chinese-internal morpheme order preserved even in hybrid compounds).

2. **Native vocabulary's semantic domain and structural centrality.** Native vocabulary has a coherent semantic profile — natural objects, basic actions/states, traditional-culture concepts, numerals 1–99, close kinship terms, core color terms — and critically, **virtually the entire grammatical/functional skeleton of the language**: nearly all case/delimiter particles, the great majority of inflectional/derivational suffixes, all auxiliary predicates, and the complete demonstrative system. This is near-absolute, per the author: "almost all such particles and suffixes are native" — a structurally important generalization for synthetic-conlang slang work, since borrowing/coining new content vocabulary leaves the grammatical scaffolding almost entirely untouched.

3. **Sound symbolism: a productive, systematic phonaesthetic subsystem.** The native lexicon contains "several thousand" sound-symbolic words, classed into *uyseng-e* "phonomimes" (sound-imitating) and *uythay-e* "phenomimes/mimeses" (manner-imitating). Two independent, compositional symbolism axes exist: a vowel axis (bright=sharp/small/quick; dark=heavy/slow/deep) and a consonant-manner axis (plain=slow/gentle; aspirated=flexible/crisp; tensed=compact/hard/extra-swift), which combine freely — a single concept can surface as up to 6 distinct forms crossing both axes. In a residual number of alternations, the contrast has calcified from connotational into purely denotational (cakta "small size" vs. cekta "small quantity"). Ideophones are typically reduplicated and can combine with the continuative auxiliary -kelita, with hata to form a compound predicate, or with the nominalizer -i to derive a noun denoting the sound/manner's source — a genuinely productive ideophone-to-person-noun derivational pathway.

4. **Sino-Korean words: three historical layers, high analyticity, and the native/SK doublet system.** SK vocabulary entered via (a) direct Chinese borrowing, (b) Sino-Japanese mediation (technical/scientific vocabulary, since Japan first systematically translated Western civilization), and (c) coining within Korea itself from Chinese-character morphemes. SK pronunciation is believed to derive from 7th–8th century Tang/Sui-dynasty northern-Chinese readings, subsequently modified by Korea-internal sound change and total tone loss. SK morphemes are markedly more analytic than native vocabulary, with a free/bound split governed by a simple rule: an SK morpheme is free only where no native equivalent exists. The native/SK doublet system is extensive and systematically patterned: native is typically more colloquial/emotionally immediate, SK is typically more formal/literary AND is the socially preferred form for deferential reference — a lexical (not just morphological) layer of the honorific system genuinely distinct from files 010/031. The SK kinship system is organized via the *chon* "inch" counting system: parent-child = il-chon "one inch"; siblings = i-chon "two inches"; an uncle = sam-chon "three inches"; first cousins = sa-chon "four inches" — a fully compositional numeric-distance kinship-naming logic with no native-vocabulary analogue.

5. **Loan words: phonological adaptation rules and source-language stratification.** Pre-1945 loanwords entered via Japanese mediation; post-1945, direct importation (chiefly English) dominates. Six systematic phonological adaptation rules govern nativization: syllable-structure repair via epenthetic vowels; a three-way stop-contrast mapping; syllable-final neutralization (applying to loanwords exactly as natively, and in fact motivating the epenthesis rule); a foreign word-final /t/ spelled -s when followed by a vowel-initial particle; systematic mapping of Korean's missing fricatives to the nearest stop/affricate; and non-preservation of word-initial vowel length in loanword spelling. Per-origin-language domains are each characterized distinctly: English spans all domains; native-Japanese loans are comparatively few due to post-1945 anti-Japanese purging; German clusters in medicine/philosophy/science/sports; Russian loanwords show a flagged North/South asymmetry ("many" in the North, "very few" in the South). A genuinely productive locally-coined pseudo-loan ("Konglish") register exists — new compounds built from borrowed English morphemes but not existing as such in English — functionally analogous to Japanese wasei-eigo and directly relevant to slang-synthesis mechanics.

### Grammar points

1. **Why Korean's genetic affiliation is unusually hard to establish.** Little authentic early documentation survives; the oldest fragments (11th–13th century) are themselves written in Chinese characters, and much early native vocabulary was obscured by a massive subsequent influx of Chinese loanwords — explaining why the field has produced hypotheses linking Korean to Indo-European, Tibeto-Burman, Dravidian, Altaic, Austronesian, and Paleosiberian, ranging from rigorous comparative work to "amateurish attempts based merely on accidental lexical resemblances."

2. **The Altaic hypothesis is treated as the most persuasive explanation.** Evidence reviewed: a regular proto-Altaic *p>f>h>ZERO chain with Korean retaining original *p; systematic vowel-correspondence tables; a four-way proto-Altaic liquid system collapsing onto a single Korean l; dozens of Manchu:Korean regular consonant correspondences; and shared bound morphology. Typological parallels (SOV, no articles, agglutination, vowel harmony, no word-initial liquids in native vocabulary, sound-symbolic vocabulary) are treated as secondary/weaker evidence. Subgrouping proposals vary widely — some place Korean as the 4th Altaic branch, others place Korean-Japanese-Ainu as sister branches coordinate with Altaic, others prefer five fully coordinate branches with no privileged subgrouping.

3. **Austronesian and Dravidian hypotheses are much weaker, and treated very differently.** Austronesian evidence is strong for Japanese specifically but the chapter is explicit that it does not transfer to Korean — "any hypothesis associating Korean to Austronesian is premature." Dravidian, by contrast, gets a cautiously open verdict: "the Dravidian hypothesis deserves further investigation in view of the large number of lexical look-alikes and many interesting typological similarities," based on Clippinger's (1984) 408-cognate proposal plus shared typological syntax.

4. **Korean-Japanese genetic linkage is the chapter's second-most-endorsed hypothesis.** "A genetic link, however remote, between Korean and Japanese is widely accepted." Martin (1966) is identified as "the most significant breakthrough" — 320 systematically compared cognate sets with a reconstructed proto-Korean-Japanese phonemic system — though the chapter reports real methodological criticism fairly (a:six Korean vowels, l:four Japanese consonants, typologically unrealistic clusters, reliance on modern rather than rigorously reconstructed forms). Whitman (1985a) addressed the main weakness via rigorous Middle-Korean/Old-Japanese internal reconstruction (352 sets). The strongest-weighted evidence category is closed-class functors (demonstratives, particles) rather than content words, since functors are less likely explained by later borrowing.

5. **The Kokwulye-language evidence and the "third-ancient-language" complication.** K.M. Lee argues Old Japanese shows a close relationship specifically to Kokwulye (an extinct language of ancient Manchuria/N. Korea, distinct from the Sinla lineage that became Contemporary Korean), corroborated by archaeological evidence (near-identical 5th-century Kokwulye tomb-painting costume style found replicated in a 7th–8th century Japanese tomb painting). B.H. Kim (1984) directly disputes this, arguing instead for a genetically separate, non-Altaic "Primitive Peninsular Korean" substratum — citing the same Kokwulye numerals Lee treats as Altaic-descended. The chapter closes without resolving this dispute, calling for more morphosyntactic/grammaticalization comparison rather than further raw lexical lists.

### Grammar points

1. **Bird's-eye political-historical framework.** The chapter grounds its periodization in Korea's political history: Ancient Cosen → Han Chinese administrative control → Tungusic tribal states (Pu.ye, Kokwulye, Okce, Yeymayk) and the southern Samhan leagues → Three Kingdoms (Kokwulye, Paykcey, Sinla) → Sinla's unification (668 AD) → Kolye dynasty → Cosen dynasty → Japanese annexation → N/S division.

2. **Five-stage periodization**, tied explicitly to documentary availability: Prehistoric Korean, Old Korean, Middle Korean, Modern Korean, Contemporary Korean, with the 1446 creation of Hankul as the single biggest watershed (abundant exact transcription only becomes possible from the 15th century on).

3. **Prehistoric and Old Korean: three barely-attested ancient languages** — Sinla, Paykcey, Kokwulye — known only through fragmentary Itwu-script transcriptions. H.K. Kim's four-point case that Unified Sinla's language (the direct ancestor of Contemporary Korean) was essentially pure Samhan-derived is given in full. Sinla's reconstructed phonology already looks essentially proto-Contemporary-Korean, and its grammar already included a largely-modern case/delimiter particle inventory and honorific morphology recognizably ancestral to the modern system.

4. **Middle Korean: the watershed period**, described via ten numbered phonological developments (addition of z, β, the tensed series; a 7-vowel system; a disputed "Great Vowel Shift" hypothesis the author treats skeptically; word/syllable-initial consonant clusters; implosivization of syllable-final consonants; an 18-member diphthong/triphthong system; full tonality — three tonemes, later lost except in Kyengsang/Hamkyeng; rigid vowel harmony; a rich case-particle paradigm including the striking fact that nominative ka did not exist before 1572; a grammatical-person ablaut system on verb suffixes, vanishing by the late 16th century — a genuinely striking lost grammatical category; and a three-tier honorific-suffix system). Genitive-marked embedded-relative-clause subjects (discontinued since the early 20th century) are a distinct historical syntactic pattern with no Contemporary Korean counterpart.

5. **Modern and Contemporary Korean: eleven further sound changes** complete the shift to the present-day system — cluster simplification, the near-total loss of z, the extinction of vowel ɔ (word-by-word-irregular merger), loss of ɦ', diphthong monophthongization (with a still-live three-way conditioned alternation for ɨj), rounding of ɨ after bilabials, vowel harmony's collapse, umlaut fronting, palatalization (dated to the late 17th–early 18th century — the same change Chapter 4 treats as a live regional isogloss), word-initial n-deletion, and lax-to-tensed/aspirated strengthening in individual items. New morphosyntax also emerged via grammaticalization in this period: -as'/əs' past tense from an existential construction, and -kes' modal (volition/conjecture/futurity) grammaticalizing in the 18th century from a periphrastic causative construction — a striking source construction for a modern epistemic/volitional modal.

# Korean — Established Vocabulary: *A Korean-English Dictionary* (Martin, Lee & Chang), Sample 1 of N

**Source:** Samuel E. Martin, Yang Ha Lee, Sung-Un Chang, *A Korean-English Dictionary* (Yale
University Press, New Haven and London; copyright 1967, second printing 1975). A large general
scholarly dictionary of the "core of the language" (per the book's own Introduction, §3), dictionary
body runs printed pp. 1–1902 across 962 PDF pages (2 printed pages per PDF-page spread, confirmed by
direct inspection). **This dispatch covers PDF pages 1–240 of 962 (the assigned first quarter).**

**Coverage note — deliberately non-exhaustive sampling, not a full extraction.** This is a large
general-purpose dictionary with many thousands of entries in the assigned range alone; per the
dispatch instruction, entries were **sampled at even intervals**, not transcribed exhaustively.
Eleven two-page spreads were rendered and read (PDF pages 10, 15, 20, 45, 60, 90, 120, 150, 180, 210,
240 — 22 printed pages total), landing on printed pages **8–9, 18–19, 38–39, 68–69, 98–99, 158–159,
218–219, 278–279, 338–339, 398–399, and 458–459**, which alphabetically span the headwords **가 (ka)
through 도 (to)** — the front portion of the ㄱ–ㄷ consonant range, not the full assigned quarter's
alphabetical span (the actual end of PDF page 240 reaches into ㄷ; later spreads within pp. 1–240 that
fall between these sampled points, and the tail end past printed p. 459, were not inspected). A
follow-up dispatch sampling further spreads within pp. 1–240 (or a `_sample2` file) could deepen this
if useful, but was not attempted here per the "8-10 sampled spreads" scope given. Within each sampled
spread, entries were selected for the table below by **prioritizing every entry that carries the
dictionary's own explicit register/usage-tier annotation** (`[DIAL.]`, `[VULGAR]`, `[OBSCENE]`,
`[SLANG]`, `[ABUSIVE]`, `[DEROGATORY]`, `[AFFECTIONATE]`, `[JOCULAR]`, `[LIT.]`, `[ARCHAIC]`, "light
isotope"/"heavy isotope" sound-symbolic cross-references, etc.) plus a handful of entries illustrating
interesting derivational/compounding morphology or genuinely vivid idiom. **Ordinary untagged core
entries on the sampled pages were skipped by the hundreds** — this table is not a page transcription.

**No PDF text layer; vision-read throughout.** `pdftotext` on this file returns nothing (confirmed on
a test range) — a pure image scan (Adobe Acrobat 7.08 Image Conversion Plug-in, 2008), consistent with
the "undecodable / no text layer" category already seen on other Korean sources in this project. All
entries below were read directly from 150dpi page renders. Print is sharp, clean, unwatermarked
typeset text throughout every sampled page — **no handwritten marginalia of any kind was found** on
any of the 11 spreads inspected (nor on the title/copyright-page spread used to confirm the citation).
Vision Reading Confidence is marked `plausible_unverified` for the great majority of rows (clearly
legible print, not independently cross-checked against a second source) and `verified` only where a
form was corroborated by an explicit cross-reference or isotope-pair entry elsewhere on the same
sampled page.

**Register-tag taxonomy mapping (this dictionary's own abbreviation system, from its own
Abbreviations key, PDF pp. 15–17 = printed pp. xvi–xvii, also directly inspected):** `colloq.`
colloquial, `dial.` dialect (= non-Seoul form, unspecified further by this dictionary's own
convention — it does not name which regional dialect), `vulg.` vulgar, `lit.` literary, `fam.`
familiar style (a grammatical verb-ending register, not applied below since it marks inflected forms
rather than distinct lexemes), `joc.` jocular, and bracketed all-caps editorial tags `[VULGAR]`,
`[OBSCENE]`, `[SLANG]`, `[ABUSIVE]`, `[DEROGATORY]`, `[AFFECTIONATE]`, `[ARCHAIC]` used ad hoc on
individual sense-lines. Mapped onto this project's `00_Usage_Tier_Taxonomy.md` as: `[DIAL.]` →
`regional`; `[VULGAR]`/`[OBSCENE]`/`[ABUSIVE]` → `taboo`; `[SLANG]`/`[DEROGATORY]`/`[JOCULAR]` →
`slang` (with `.derogatory`/`.jocular` subcategory where the distinction is worth keeping);
`[AFFECTIONATE]` → `colloquial.affectionate`; `[LIT.]`/`[ARCHAIC]` → `literary`/`archaic` respectively.

**Flag — genuine taboo/slang-register content found clustered in this range.** Per this project's
Phase 1 (reference) vs. Phase 3 (corpus) content-type boundary: everything below is the *dictionary's
own* lexicographic register annotation on headwords, not raw slang collected "in the wild" (subtitle/
social-media/forum text) — this is legitimately Phase 1 `established/` content, not something that
belongs in `language_corpus/Korean/`. That said, it's worth flagging explicitly for whoever plans
later corpus/mechanics work: the printed pp. 338–339 spread (녠장, 년, 녀석, 노닥다리, 노다지) and the
printed pp. 158–159 spread (골-머리, 꼴리다, 꼴-사납다, 껄렁껄렁-하다) are unusually dense clusters of
`[VULGAR]`/`[OBSCENE]`/`[SLANG]`/`[DEROGATORY]`-tagged headwords relative to the mostly-neutral
Sino-Korean vocabulary surrounding them — a good candidate area to revisit if a targeted "mine the
dictionary for register-tagged headwords" pass is ever run across the full 962 pages, since a sparser
even-interval sample like this one will necessarily miss most such clusters.

## Grammar points

### The dictionary's own register-tag system as a usable taxonomy source

Unlike most of this project's grammar-reference extractions, this dictionary marks register directly
on individual senses via a bracketed-tag convention (`[VULGAR]`, `[OBSCENE]`, `[SLANG]`, `[ABUSIVE]`,
`[DEROGATORY]`, `[AFFECTIONATE]`, `[JOCULAR]`, `[LIT.]`, `[ARCHAIC]`, `[DIAL.]`) documented in its own
front-matter Abbreviations key (printed pp. xvi–xvii). Several entries sampled here (년, 녀석) carry
*different* tags on different senses of the *same* headword — the register isn't a property of the
word in isolation but of the specific sense/usage, which matters for any later mechanics-analysis work
trying to model how a single lexical item can shift register by context (insult vs. endearment,
literal vs. figurative). This is a stronger and more systematic register-marking convention than most
of the grammar textbooks already extracted for Korean, which mark register only sporadically in prose
asides.

### The light/heavy phonaesthetic isotope system, cross-referenced lexicographically

Consistent with the vowel-symbolism system already documented from *Using Korean* (`established/008`,
ch. 13) and *The Sounds of Korean* (`established/013`), this dictionary systematically cross-references
"light isotope" and "heavy isotope" pairs of ideophones/descriptive adjectives directly in entries
(e.g. 까드름 ↔ 거드름 "strutting," 단작-스럽다 ↔ 딘적- "stingy," 꼴막 ↔ 꿀먹 "partly filled"). Bright/back
(light) vowels mark a smaller, lighter, more delicate variant of a sound-symbolic root; dark/front
(heavy) vowels mark the larger, heavier, or more intense counterpart. Because the dictionary indexes
these as explicit cross-references rather than burying them in prose, this source is a strong candidate
for systematically mining the *entire* isotope-pair inventory in a future dedicated pass, rather than
the incidental few pairs caught by this even-interval sample.

### A Sino-Korean semantic cluster: the 姦/奸 "treachery/deceit" root

The printed pp. 38–39 spread (alphabetically 간독 through 간웅) is almost entirely occupied by
Sino-Korean compounds built on the 姦/奸 root (etymologically "illicit, treacherous, adulterous"):
간독 "insidiousness," 간사 "cunning, deceit," 간악 "wickedness," 간위 "trickery," 간음 "adultery,"
간웅 "a major traitor." This is a clean illustration of how a single bound Sino-Korean morpheme
generates an entire register-neutral formal-vocabulary cluster around one core negative-moral-judgment
concept — useful comparanda for later Korean mechanics-analysis work on how the native-Korean/
Sino-Korean/loanword three-tier stratification (already documented in `established/008`, ch. 9) plays
out concretely within one semantic field.

### Figurative food/refuse-term pejoration as a productive pattern

Three separate entries sampled here (개똥 "dog dung," 개떡 "bran cake," and — via a very different
loanword route — 뗌뿌라 "tempura") show the same shift: a literal low-value/refuse food term acquires a
fully lexicalized figurative sense meaning "worthless, fake, nonsense, of no value." This recurs across
three unrelated etymological sources (native compound, native compound, foreign loanword) within this
same sampled range, suggesting a genuinely productive Korean pejoration pattern (concrete low-value
object → abstract "worthless/fake" predicate) rather than an isolated coincidence — worth flagging for
the eventual mechanics-analysis phase as a candidate slang-formation mechanism.

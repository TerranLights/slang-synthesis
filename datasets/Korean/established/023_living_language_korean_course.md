# Korean — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: *Living Language Korean Course*

**Source:** *Living Language Korean Course: Complete Korean* (Living Language/Random House),
`09.Living Language Korean Course.pdf`, all 125 PDF pages of the source file (front matter + all
15 lessons + the book's own Korean-English/English-Korean Glossary, printed pages i–232). This is
the complete book — nothing was left undispatched.

**PDF gotcha.** `pdfinfo`/`pdffonts` confirm this PDF has **zero embedded fonts and zero text
layer at all** (`Creator: Adobe Acrobat 8.1`, `Producer: ... Image Conversion Plug-in` — a pure
scanned-image PDF, not a font-corruption case). All content was vision-read from rendered page
images (`pdftoppm` at 120–150dpi). **Two-printed-pages-per-scanned-page spread format confirmed**
(landscape page size 845×617pt): PDF page 9 is the transition leaf (printed page xviii on the
left, printed page 1/Lesson 1 start on the right); from PDF page 10 onward, each PDF page holds
printed pages `2×(N−9)` (left) and `2×(N−9)+1` (right). This offset was verified empirically by
rendering and reading, not assumed from `pdfinfo` alone, per the spec's PDF-extraction-gotchas
guidance.

**Vision-reading confidence.** Every page inspected across the front matter, all 15 lessons, and
sampled Glossary pages is genuinely typeset, clean, print-quality textbook content — no scan
artifacts, no ambiguous glyphs, no handwritten marginalia anywhere. All Korean-language entries
below are marked Vision Reading Confidence `verified` (Hangul renders cleanly and unambiguously;
most vocabulary items also recur across the dialogue, vocabulary-warm-up, and key-phrases sections
of their own lesson, corroborating the reading). No entries required a `plausible_unverified` or
`low_confidence` flag.

**Non-redundant-supplement dispatch — heavy skip, as anticipated and confirmed.** This is a
beginner-level "complete course" covering essentially the same grammatical territory already
extracted from `established/001`–`002` (Byon, *Basic Korean*) and `established/016` (*Active
Korean 1*, the closest sibling — another beginner survival-Korean course with the same
lesson-plus-culture-note structure): topic/subject particles, honorific speech levels, present/
past/future tense, existence/negation, both number systems, case particles, desire/obligation/
ability constructions, comparatives, causal connectives, and reported speech are all covered in
prior Wave 1/2 files at equal or greater grammatical depth. Per the spec's coverage rule, **every
lesson's own grammar-note explanation was read and cross-checked against `established/001`
(skimmed first) and `established/016`, and is summarized only where it adds something genuinely
new** (a fuller register/paradigm table, an explicit register-contrast note the prior sources
didn't make, a phonological detail) — full re-derivation of already-covered grammar (topic
particle, case particles, basic past tense, basic negation, basic honorifics, `-고 싶다` desire,
`-고 있다` progressive in its plain form, `-겠` future, numbers 1–10, `있다/없다`) is **not**
repeated here. The book's own Korean-English/English-Korean **Glossary** (printed pp. 193–232,
PDF pp. 105–125, sampled directly) was confirmed to be a straight alphabetized re-listing of
vocabulary already introduced lesson-by-lesson in the body of the book — **not separately
extracted**, per the coverage rule's treatment of pure pointer/index material (same precedent as
`established/016`'s Glossary treatment, except here the body vocabulary itself was captured
per-lesson below rather than sourced from the Glossary). The front-matter Hangul/pronunciation
chart (printed pp. xiii–xx) duplicates `established/001`'s "Reading Hangul" unit and
`established/013`'s dedicated phonology guide — **not re-transcribed**.

**What was kept.** Each of the 15 lessons is built around vocabulary warm-up + dialogue + key
phrases + two culture notes + one or more grammar points, organized by real-world topic rather
than by grammatical category (unlike `001`/`016`, which are organized grammar-first). This makes
the book's **topical/thematic vocabulary sets** — computer terms, banking terms, symptoms/body
parts, kinship terms, food and taste adjectives, sports and colors, travel terms, furniture/house
terms, telephone terms — its main non-redundant contribution, since prior grammar-first sources
tend to supply grammar-drill vocabulary rather than organized real-world topic sets. These are
captured in full below. Grammar points are captured only where genuinely new or where an explicit
register/phonological nuance not previously flagged was found (see the Grammar points section's
own per-item framing). Culture-topic notes with concrete sociocultural/folk-belief content
(physiognomy folk belief, the 2002 World Cup Red Devils fandom phenomenon, currency/phone-system
realia) are summarized briefly as potentially useful sociocultural corpus material, not
extractively transcribed at length, per copyright discipline.

## Grammar points

Only points genuinely new, deeper, or explicitly register/phonology-annotated relative to
`established/001`/`016` are detailed below; already-covered basics (topic/subject particles,
plain past tense, plain negation, basic honorific speech levels, `-고 싶다`, plain `-고 있다`,
`-겠` future, numbers 1–10, `있다/없다`) are omitted per the coverage rule.

### Three-way simplified speech-level framing (Lesson 1)

This book introduces speech level as a **three-way beginner simplification** — formal
(습니다/십니까), polite-informal (아요/어요), and non-polite (아/어) — rather than
`established/001`'s and `established/010`'s full six-level system. Not a new finding in itself
(it's a pedagogical simplification, not additional data), but worth flagging so a future synthesis
pass doesn't mistake it for an independently-attested three-level system.

### Sentence-ending inventory summary (Lesson 11, p. 133)

Before introducing the new `-네/-네요` comment ending, the book gives its own compact summary
table of every sentence-ending type covered up to that point in the course: `-아요/-어요` (polite
familiar), `-아/-어` (non-polite familiar), `-습니다` (polite formal), `-습니까` (polite formal
question), `-세요` (polite positive command), `-지 마세요` (polite negative command), `-요` (polite
short-form ending). This is a useful compact register-inventory cross-reference, even though each
individual ending is independently attested elsewhere in Wave 1/2.

**New in this lesson:** the comment ending `-네`/`-네요`, used when a speaker comments on a
situational change or a person's looks just noticed (e.g. 얼굴이 아주 좋아졌네! "You look great!").
`-네` is used between friends (non-polite); `-네요` is the polite form, used toward older/senior
addressees. When combined with future tense, `-겠-` is inserted before `-네` (늦겠네! "That will be
late!").

### Indirect-object particle register split: 에게 / 한테 / 께 (Lesson 7)

The source explicitly frames these three "to (someone)" particles along a register axis rather
than presenting them as free variants: **에게** is glossed as the form that "appears in letters/
books" (literary/written register), **한테** as the ordinary spoken-register form, and **께** as
the honorific form used toward a respected referent. This written-vs-spoken split for 에게/한테 is
stated more explicitly here than in prior Wave 1/2 extractions, which tend to treat them as
near-synonyms without foregrounding the register axis.

### Loanword-stem + 하다 productive verbalization (Lessons 6, 11)

A recurring, explicitly productive pattern: an English loanword stem plus the light verb 하다
freely creates a new Korean verb — 다운로드하다 "to download," 업데이트하다 "to update," 리모델링하다
"to remodel," 조깅하다 "to jog." The source frames this (p. 137) as a fully general strategy
("하다 is also used when borrowing English words to create a Korean verb"), not a closed list.
Directly relevant to this project's own purpose: this is a live, productive slang-formation
mechanism for absorbing foreign vocabulary into the verb system.

### Vowel harmony's explicit bright/dark classification (Lesson 10)

The source gives an unusually explicit statement of Korean vowel harmony's role in past-tense
formation (p. 121): **아 and 오 are "bright" vowels; 어 is a "dark" vowel; 이, 으, and 우 are
classified as dark; 오 is bright.** This three-way (no-consonant / consonant-after-bright-vowel /
consonant-after-dark-vowel) past-tense allomorph table (`-ㅆ` / `-았` / `-었`) is presented more
systematically here than as a scattered rule.

### Present tense's three co-existing forms (Lesson 12)

The source explicitly enumerates **three parallel ways to express present tense** in Korean: (1)
non-tense + ending (가요 "goes"), (2) the vivid/narrative present -ㄴ/는다 (간다! "goes! (now)"),
and (3) the progressive -고 있- (가고 있다 "is going"), with full past/future-progressive paradigm
tables for the third. Framing all three as co-existing, register/nuance-differentiated present-
tense strategies (rather than just teaching -고 있다 alone) is more systematic than prior
extractions' treatment.

### Question-ending register pair: -니? vs. -ㅂ/습니까? (Lesson 12)

-니? is explicitly marked as the informal question ending used toward a friend or younger person;
-ㅂ/습니까? is the formal counterpart for an older or higher-status addressee. The source adds an
explicit usage warning: "Make sure you are careful when using the informal forms. Some people
might take offense at overly informal Korean usage" — a directly stated sociolinguistic risk-note,
not just a grammatical description.

### Non-polite suggestive -자 / polite suggestive -ㅂ시다 (Lesson 12)

"Let's ..." register pair: -자 (non-polite, 먹자 "let's eat") vs. -ㅂ시다 (polite, 먹읍시다 "let's
eat"). Straightforward but not previously tabulated as an explicit suggestive-register pair in
this project's Korean files.

### Ability -(으)ㄹ 수 있다, become -어/아지다, future gradual, attempt -어/아 보다 (Lesson 13)

Standard constructions already covered elsewhere in Wave 1/2 (`established/004` etc.), but this
lesson's -어/아지다 ("to become") treatment includes four explicitly enumerated **sound-change
footnotes** worth preserving: (1) two 아 sounds contract to one 아 (비싸다→비싸지다), (2) the weak
vowel 으 drops from the stem (나쁘다→나빠지다), (3) all 하다-verbs regularize to 해지다
(가능하다→가능해지다), (4) a stem-final ㅂ undergoes the /b/→/w/ sound change
(어렵다→어려워지다). The "future gradual" `(descriptive verb) + 지 + ㄹ 거예요` ("will become...")
is presented as a distinct compositional pattern (지 = gradual-change marker + ㄹ거예요 = future),
worth flagging as a compositional two-morpheme stack for the typology guide.

### Comparative/superlative and a ㅂ-irregular adjective set (Lesson 14)

Comparatives are formed by placing 더 ("more") before the adjective, 덜 ("less") for the inverse;
superlatives use 제일 or 가장 ("the most"/"the best"). The lesson's adjective list is a genuinely
useful compact ㅂ-irregular set (맵다→매워요, 춥다→추워요, 덥다→더워요, 쉽다→쉬워요), all following
the same /b/→/w/ sound change noted above for -어지다.

### Causal-connective register pair: -(으)니(까) vs. -아/어서 (Lessons 14–15)

The source explicitly frames these two "because" connectives along a strength/formality axis:
-(으)니까 is called the "stronger" causal expression, while -아/어서 is glossed as "similar to
English 'for'" and described as shorter/simpler. Both are independently attested in prior Wave 1/2
files, but this explicit relative-strength framing (rather than treating them as free variants) is
new.

### Obligation -어/아야 겠다 ("I should") (Lesson 14)

A near-synonym of -어야 되다/돼요 (already covered in `established/003`), but distinctively
combinable with other connectives (-고, -ㄴ/은/는데) to build longer chained-obligation sentences
(해물파전도 먹고 비빔밥도 먹어야겠다 "I should eat seafood pancakes and (I should) eat bibimbap
too"). Also explicitly contracts to 해야겠다 for all 하다-verbs.

### Tag question -지요 / reported speech -대요, -ㄴ대요 (Lesson 15)

-지요 (and its non-polite bare form -지) is glossed as a confirmation tag ("...isn't it?"/"...don't
you?"), explicitly framed by the source as a device that "creates more friendly environment,
showing the person's willingness to cooperate" — a politeness/conversation-management framing
consistent with how `established/006` frames -거든요/-고요/-서요. Reported speech -대요/-ㄴ대요
("I heard that...") is given as a single contracted form rather than the fuller
expanded/simple/contracted reduction continuum `established/006` documents for *Continuing
Korean*'s quotation system (간다고 해요 → 간다 해요 → 간대요) — this book teaches only the fully
contracted end-state, consistent with its beginner level.

## Culture-topic notes (sociocultural/folk-belief content, sampled)

Not linguistic data, but flagged as potentially useful sociocultural corpus material for the
eventual synthesis phase, per this file's own judgment call about what's worth preserving from a
copyright-constrained textbook culture-note section (paraphrased, not quoted at length):

- **관상학 (physiognomy/face-reading folk belief), Lesson 9.** Some Koreans (the source says
  "older," but frames the belief as still current) hold that face shape predicts nature/fate: a
  round face on a woman reads as "lucky," generous, and affectionate; a square face reads as
  resistant, stubborn, and unwilling to hear others' opinions (particularly for women); the upper
  face (forehead to eyes) reads as fortelling early life, the middle face reads as fortelling
  middle age, and the chin/lips read as telling the person's whole life story — thin lips
  supposedly mean the person "is not very good at keeping secrets."
- **2002 World Cup and 붉은 악마 (Red Devils) fandom, Lesson 15.** The source frames the 2002
  Korea/Japan World Cup as the moment soccer fandom exploded nationally, with red "Be the Reds!"
  T-shirts becoming near-ubiquitous street wear across all demographics (the source specifically
  notes businesspeople, families, "even Buddhist nuns and grandparents" wearing them) — explicitly
  tied to the red/blue yin-yang symbolism on the Korean flag. Two crowd chants are given: 대-한-
  민국! ("Great Korean-nation!") and 이겨라! ("Victory!"/"Win!").
- **Currency and banking practicalities, Lesson 10.** 1-won and 5-won coins are described as
  practically obsolete; Koreans use cash or money orders rather than personal checks (unlike
  American banking practice); Korean banks open later (after 9am) and close later than U.S. banks.
- **Loanword-based brand-name etymology, Lesson 8.** "Samsung" (삼성) literally means "three
  stars"; "LG" is stated to be a merger-era combination of "Lucky" and "GoldStar," two company
  names that merged in 1995 — offered as an example of how Korean tech-company names carry
  semantic content in the original Korean even when romanized as opaque initials in English.
- **Gochugaru (dried red pepper) history, Lesson 14.** The source states dried red pepper is
  believed to have been brought to Korea from Japan/China in medieval times (exact route
  described as unclear), and that Korean cooking characteristically avoids butter in favor of
  vegetable oil and salt — offered as a contrast point against Western seasoning conventions.

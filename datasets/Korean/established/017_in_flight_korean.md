# Korean — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: *In-Flight Korean* (complete, all 20 pages)

**Source:** *Living Language In-Flight Korean: Learn Before You Land* (Living Language, a Random
House Company, © 2001; this printing 2005/2007), all 20 PDF pages (the entire book — a short
audio-companion survival phrasebook, not a conventional grammar). Covers all 18 lessons: Saying
Hello, Basic Expressions, Numbers, At the Airport, Getting Around, Asking Directions, At the Hotel,
Making Friends, At the Restaurant, Telling Time, Money, Shopping, Staying in Touch, At the Train
Station, Getting Help, Days and Months, Going Out, and Asking Questions and Describing Things.

**Coverage note:** per dispatch instruction this book is small enough for exhaustive extraction —
every phrase and vocabulary item in all 18 lessons is captured below. No content was skipped except
the closing colophon (copyright/ISBN boilerplate) and the two-page pronunciation-guide sound-key,
which is paraphrased into a Grammar point rather than tabulated as vocabulary.

**PDF-text-layer note — no Hangul anywhere in this source, by design, not corruption.** Unlike other
Korean books in this corpus, `pdftotext` extraction of this PDF produces **zero Hangul or CJK
codepoints of any kind** — every single phrase in all 20 pages is rendered purely in Latin-script
romanization with syllable blocks separated by periods (e.g. `AN.NYEONG.HA.SE.YO.`). This was
checked against the Wave-1/Wave-2 `+36266`-offset font-substitution cipher pattern documented in
`../00_Extraction_Checklist.md` and the corruption-vs-clean-text distinction in
`../00_Reference_Extraction_Spec.md` — it does not apply here: there is no suspicious CJK-block
codepoint soup to decode, only clean, correctly-rendering Latin text throughout. This is a design
choice of the source itself: *In-Flight Korean* is a "listen and repeat" audio-course companion
booklet (the actual instructional content is the accompanying audio CD/tape), so the book prints
**only** the romanized pronunciation guide a listener follows along with, never the Hangul spelling.
No vision-reading was needed or attempted; `Vision Reading Confidence` is `n/a` throughout.
Romanization is the source's own non-standard system (documented in its own pronunciation key,
reproduced as a Grammar point below), not Revised Romanization or McCune-Reischauer — reproduced
here exactly as printed, dot-separated syllables and all, since that is the only orthographic form
this source provides.

**A handful of apparent print typos in the source are reproduced as printed** and flagged inline
rather than silently corrected, per copyright/accuracy discipline — e.g. `I.GHOG.EUN` (p. 9, should
almost certainly be `I.CHOG.EUN` matching the parallel entries around it) and `BANG.I.SEUM.NI.KKA.`
glossed as a statement ("I'd like a room") despite the interrogative `-KKA` ending (p. 7).

**Register finding (the dispatch's main ask):** this phrasebook is written almost entirely in the
**deferential/formal register** (-ㅂ니다/-습니다 statement endings, -세요/-십니까 command and question
endings) — the standard register for a stranger addressing a stranger, appropriate to a travel
phrasebook's target scenarios (customs, hotels, ordering food). Genuinely different, more casual
register surfaces in only a handful of places, tagged `colloquial` in the tables below rather than
`formal`: the polite-informal `-아/어요` ending on **A.JU.JO.A.YO!** ("That's great!"), **NA.PA.YO!**
("That's terrible!"), and the three body-part-hurts phrases **PAL.I.A.PA.YO.** / **DA.RI.GA.A.PA.YO.**
/ **MEO.RI.GA.A.PA.YO.** (all p. 16-20) — plus the bare propositive **GAB.SHI.DA!** ("Let's go!",
p. 9), which though not impolite is a notably more casual invitation-form than the surrounding
deferential material. None of this rises to genuine slang or taboo register — this is a formulaic
survival phrasebook throughout, with no slang, taboo, or dialectal content anywhere in it — but the
formal/informal-polite split itself is a real, if unannotated by the source, register fact worth
recording, since it is exactly the kind of politeness-register data this project's mechanics-analysis
phase draws on.

All vocabulary below: **Source Type** `grammar_reference` (closest fit among the allowed categories
for a teaching phrasebook — see `../../language_corpus/00_Source_Reliability_Guide.md`), **Attested
Era** `contemporary (source published 2001)`, **Attested Region** / **Geographic Scope** `—` (South
Korean standard/Seoul Korean assumed but not explicitly marked by the source), **Transcription
Confidence** `n/a` (reference-book extraction, not corpus/subtitle sourcing), **Vision Reading
Confidence** `n/a` (real, clean text layer throughout) — these five columns are omitted from the row
notes below to save space and stated once here instead, except where a row's own note flags a
deviation (e.g. the two typo rows above).

### Lesson 3 — Numbers (p. 4): two full numeral systems

The book's own worked paradigm gives the Sino-Korean set (used for time/money) side by side with
the native-Korean set (used for counting objects) for 0-30, then Sino-Korean only from 100 up (the
book explicitly notes: "After 200, all numbers are spoken in the Chinese way," p. 4). Since these
teen/tens forms are compositionally built (ten + unit) rather than atomic, they are captured as a
single paradigm table here rather than one row per number — see the Grammar points section below for
the morpheme breakdown. Core cardinal 0-10 (the two systems' base forms) are tabulated individually

## Grammar points

### Pronunciation guide (unnumbered front matter, p. 2)

The book opens with its own romanization key rather than teaching Hangul directly (consistent with
this being an audio-companion booklet — see the coverage note above). Consonants: **G** (game), **K**
(king), **KK** (scream), **N** (nice), **D** (dog), **DD** (steak), **T** (time), **L** (long), **R**
(rock), **M** (mother), **B** (bed), **P** (pie), **PP** (spring), **S** (sin), **SH** (shield), **NG**
(song), **J** (just), **CH** (church), **H** (house). Vowels: **A** (father), **YA** (yard), **EO**
(urge), **YEO** (yearn), **O** (dog), **YO** (yogurt), **U** (food), **YU** (you), **EU** (roses), **I**
(beat), **WA** (want), **WO** (wall), **WI** (weep), **AE** (bat), **E** (bait). This is a rough
English-equivalent guide for a listen-and-repeat audio course, not a phonetic transcription system —
several of its English-word comparisons (e.g. **EO** "as in urge") are approximate at best, which the
book itself doesn't flag but is worth noting for anyone treating these romanized forms as precise IPA
stand-ins.

### Two numeral systems (Lesson 3, p. 4)

Korean has two parallel numeral systems in everyday use, both captured by this source: a **Sino-Korean**
set (일, 이, 삼... derived from Chinese) used for telling time (hours) and money, and a **native
Korean** set (하나, 둘, 셋... "the Korean way") used for counting objects and (per other established
files in this corpus) ages. The teen and tens forms in both systems are compositionally built rather
than atomic:

> **Sino-Korean teens** = SHIB (10) + unit — e.g. **SHIB.IL** = 10+1 ("eleven"), **SHIB.YUK** = 10+6
> ("sixteen"). **Sino-Korean tens (20-90)** = unit + SHIB — e.g. **I.SHIB** = 2+10 ("twenty"),
> **SA.SHIB** = 4+10 ("forty") — note the digit-order flip between teens (SHIB first) and tens
> (SHIB second), and that four of the tens forms are irregular/contracted rather than fully
> transparent compounds: **SAM.SHIB** "thirty" is regular, but 50 **O.SHIB** is glossed with the
> irregular native form **SHIN** rather than the expected fully-compositional native form, and the
> native-system tens (60 **YE.SEUN**, 70 **IL.HEUN**, 80 **YEO.DEUN**, 90 **A.HEUN**) are their own
> lexicalized forms, not simple unit+ten compounds the way the native teens are (**YEOL.HA.NA** =
> YEOL "ten" + HA.NA "one").
>
> **Native-Korean teens** = YEOL (10) + unit — e.g. **YEOL.HA.NA** = 10+1, **YEOL.YEO.SEOT** = 10+6.
> **Native-Korean tens (20-90)** are each their own lexicalized word rather than compositional: **20**
> SU.MUL, **30** SEO.REUN, **40** MA.HEUN, **50** SHIN, **60** YE.SEUN, **70** IL.HEUN, **80**
> YEO.DEUN, **90** A.HEUN — none of these transparently contain YEOL or the base unit the way the
> teens do.
>
> Above 200, the source states the Sino-Korean system takes over entirely even for contexts where
> native counting would ordinarily apply for smaller quantities — **I.BAEG** (200) = I (2) + BAEG
> (100), **SAM.BAEG** (300) = SAM (3) + BAEG, etc., a fully transparent multiplicative compound
> pattern continuing through **CHEON** (1,000) and **BAENG.MAN** (100,000, literally BAEG "hundred" +
> MAN "ten-thousand," i.e. "hundred [times] ten-thousand").

### Calendar-month names as compositional Sino-Korean ordinals (Lesson 16, p. 17)

Every month name is transparently ORDINAL-NUMBER + WOL ("month," Sino-Korean 월): **IL.WOL**
(1+month) through **SIB.I.WOL** (12+month), with two mildly irregular contractions the source doesn't
flag as such — **YU.WOL** "June" (expected **YUK.WOL**, drops the final -K) and **SI.WOL** "October"
(expected **SHIB.WOL**, drops the -B). This is the same SHIB-based numeral morphology as Lesson 3's
numbers, applied productively to a new domain.

### Deferential vs. polite-informal register split (whole book; see Register finding above)

The book is written almost entirely in the deferential/formal speech level (statement endings
-ㅂ니다/-습니다, romanized `-IM.NI.DA`/`-SEUM.NI.DA`; question endings -ㅂ니까/-습니까, romanized
`-IM.NI.KKA`/`-SEUM.NI.KKA`; command/request endings -세요, romanized `-SE.YO`) — appropriate default
register for a stranger-to-stranger travel context. The handful of exceptions cluster in two places
and use the polite-informal -아/어요 ending (romanized `-A.YO`/`-EO.YO`) instead: the three
"[body part] hurts" phrases in Lesson 15 and the two exclamatory reaction phrases closing Lesson 18,
plus one bare propositive form (**GAB.SHI.DA!** "Let's go!," Lesson 8) that carries no honorific
marking at all. The source itself never comments on or explains this register split — it is a fact
recoverable only from the morphology of the endings themselves, which is exactly the kind of
unannotated-but-real register data this project's downstream mechanics-analysis phase is meant to
surface (per `../00_Extraction_Checklist.md`'s field conventions on `Usage Tier`).

### English loanwords left untranslated (throughout)

The source frequently leaves an English loanword quoted in single quotes rather than giving a
Korean-script/romanized equivalent — e.g. `'BUS'`, `'TAXI'`, `'HOTEL'`, `'MENU'`, `'INTERNET'`,
`'WEBSITE'`, `'E-MAIL'`, `'FAX'`, `'CARD'`, most food/kitchen items in Lesson 9, most clothing items in
Lesson 12. This reflects genuine Korean loanword usage (many of these — 택시, 호텔, 메뉴, 인터넷,
이메일, 팩스, 카드 — are ordinary, fully nativized Korean words pronounced close to their English
source), not a gap in the source's own coverage; the book's quoting convention simply signals "say
the English word with Korean pronunciation" rather than providing a separately-spelled Korean form.

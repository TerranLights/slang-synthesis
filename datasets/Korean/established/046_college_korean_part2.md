### Lesson 16 — "About Hangŭl" (pp. 176-181)

1. **-에 대하여 / -에 대한** "concerning, about." Derives from the (now rarely independently used)
   verb 대하다 "to be about (something)." -에 대하여 is the postpositional-phrase form (e.g. 한글에
   대하여 "about hangŭl"); -에 대한 is the adjectival/verbal-modifier form used directly before a
   noun (e.g. 세종대왕에 대한 글 "writings about King Sejong"). Both always follow a noun or verbal
   noun.

2. **Passive construction: ACTION VERB + 어/아지다** "to be —ed." Some transitive verbs take on
   passive meaning by combining with auxiliary 지다. Book's own example: 한글은 1446년에
   만들어졌다 "Hangŭl was invented (made) in 1446" — this is also the book's explicit historical-
   date citation (see cultural note below). Other examples given: 주다→주어지다 "to be given,"
   지우다→지워지다 "to be erased." Cross-referenced by the book to L14 GN2 (descriptive verb +
   어/아지다), which is out of this chunk's range.

3. **Constructions for "before."** Unlike "after doing" (which uses past-tense modifier forms),
   "before doing" uses the verbal-noun -기 form, *without* a tense marker, regardless of whether the
   event is past or future.
   - a. V. + 기 전에 "before doing" — e.g. 한글이 만들어지기 전에는 학자들은 한자를 썼다
     "Before hangŭl was invented, scholars used Chinese [characters]."
   - b. N. + 전에 "before (some event)" — e.g. 크리스마스 전에 집에 가겠다 "I will go home before
     Christmas."

4. **Sequential use of the connective -어/아서** "and," "and then." Already established (per the
   book, cross-ref L8 GN6) as a cause-marker; this lesson extends it to mark a *sequence* of events
   sharing the same subject (e.g. 나는 편지를 써서 보냈다 "I wrote a letter and then sent it").
   Special case: with 가다/오다, -어/아서 functions like -고 ("and"/"and then") and the tense of the
   first clause is determined by the tense of the second clause (e.g. 저는 집에 와서 공부했어요 "I
   came home and studied" — came and studied both read as past via the second verb's ending).

5. **More on nominalization: V.STEM + 기.** -기 nominalizes verbs into verbal nouns (gerund/
   infinitive-like), usable as subject or direct object, and in the construction -기에 for "for
   —ing"/"for...to." Three uses:
   - a. as subject: 쓰기가 어렵다 "It is difficult to write."
   - b. as direct object: 나는...되기를 희망한다 "I hope to become..."
   - c. -기에 "for —ing"/"for...to": 한글은 한국어를 표현하기에 좋다 "Hangŭl is good for
     expressing Korean."

6. **Purposive connective -(으)려고** "in order to," "for the purpose of," "for the sake of."
   Attached to a verb stem (no tense marker on that stem); -려고 after a vowel-final or ㄹ-final
   stem, -으려고 after all other stems. Colloquial note: an extra ㄹ is often inserted and -려고 is
   pronounced -라고 in spoken language (e.g. 갈라고 for 가려고) — this is the book's own explicit
   phonological/register annotation ("pronounced ... in spoken language"), the kind of annotation
   the spec flags as high-value and never-skip.

7. **Irregular ㅅ verbs.** Some ㅅ-stem verbs drop the ㅅ before a vowel suffix (낫다→나아서/나으면/
   낫지 "to get well"; 짓다→지어서/지으면/짓지 "to make/build"; also 긋다 "to draw a line," 잇다
   "to connect"). Others keep the ㅅ unchanged (벗다 "to take off," 웃다 "to laugh," 빗다 "to comb,"
   씻다 "to wash (body)," 빼앗다 "to take away"). Full -어/아서 / -(으)면 / -지 paradigm given for
   both sets.

8. **Question ending ㄴ/은 + 가요?** "Is it true that...?" (polite informal). Preceded by a verbal
   modifier; used for descriptive verbs in present tense specifically (for past-tense descriptive
   verbs and action verbs in past/present, the book notes the parallel -나(요)? ending is used
   instead — cross-ref L15 GN8, out of range).

9. **Hanja**: 言, 姓, 表, 現, 王, 者 (see Hanja table above).

### Lesson 17 — "Friend" (pp. 187-192)

1. **Use of the particle -이 for personal names.** When only a first name is used and it ends in a
   consonant, -이 is inserted before other postpositions (-가, -는, -를, -와): 영식이는, 영식이를,
   현숙이가. (If the name ends in a vowel, no -이 is inserted.) Separately, when *calling* someone
   by first name directly (vocative), -아 (after a consonant-final name) or -야 (after a vowel-final
   name) is used: 영희야!, 철수야!, 영식아!, 정순아!

2. **Indefinite pronouns and indefinite modifiers: "someone," "some-," "certain."** Question words
   (누구, 어디, 몇, 무엇, 언제) double as indefinite pronouns, and their modifier counterparts (어느,
   얼마, 무슨, 어떤, 몇) double as indefinite modifiers, when used in a statement rather than a
   question — distinguished purely by sentence-final intonation/ending (declarative vs. interrogative
   particle), not by any change in the word itself. Full pairs table given: 누구/어느, 어디/얼마,
   몇/무슨, 무엇/어떤, 언제/몇. Extensive compare-pairs list of indefinite-statement vs.
   corresponding question, e.g. 그는 어느 대학에 다닌다 "He attends a certain college" vs. 그는 어느
   대학에 다녀요? "Which college does he attend?"

3. **Verbs 희망하다 and 원하다** "to wish," "to want," "to hope for" — nearly interchangeable, but
   원하다 is used more broadly.
   - a. V.STEM + 기를 희망하다/원하다: 동경에서 너를 만나기를 희망한다 "I hope to meet you in
     Tokyo."
   - b. N. + 을/를 원하다 "to want" (used with a noun object directly, not a nominalized verb): 어느
     것을 원하세요? "Which one would you like?"

4. **Expressions "on the way (going to)" 가는 길에, "on the way (coming to)" 오는 길에.** Always
   use the *present-tense* modifier form regardless of whether the event is past, present, or
   future. In the *past* tense specifically, 간 길에 / 온 길에 shifts meaning to "when"/"while" (not
   "on the way to"): 도쿄에 간 길에 사진기를 샀다 "When I went to Tokyo (as I was there) I bought a
   camera." This past-tense semantic shift is a genuinely distinct sub-rule, not just a tense
   inflection of rule (4a).

5. **Construction of V.STEM + (으)려고 하다** "to plan to," "to intend to," "is about to," "is going
   to." An idiomatic extension of the L16 purposive connective -(으)려고 (§L16-6) with 하다 "to do."
   The book flags this construction as genuinely *ambiguous* between "intention" and "imminent
   action" readings, resolved only by context — e.g. 내일 친구를 만나려고 한다 can read as either "I
   plan to meet my friend tomorrow" or "(I) am going to meet my friend tomorrow." With 지금 "now" or
   지금 막 "just now," the construction narrows specifically to "is just about to."

6. **Hanja**: 交, 本, 京, 生, 先, 政, 治 (see Hanja table above).

### Lesson 18 — "Letter" (pp. 198-207)

1. **Verbs of "knowing": V.MOD + 지 { 알다 "to know" / 모르다 "not to know" / 궁금하다 "to
   wonder" }.** For expressions of uncertainty or confirming questions, 는/은/을지 is used, usually
   followed by a "mental verb" (알다/모르다/궁금하다). Full present/future/past modifier paradigm
   given separately for descriptive verbs (은/ㄴ-지 present, 을/ㄹ-지 future, 었/았는지 past — e.g.
   춥+은→추운지, 춥+을→추울지, 춥+었→추웠는지) and action verbs (는지 present, 을/ㄹ지 future,
   었/았는지 past — e.g. 맞+는→맞는지, 오+ㄹ→올지, 보+았→봤는지). This modifier-choice distinction
   (descriptive vs. action verb present-tense modifiers) recurs as an explicit through-line across
   this lesson (also flagged under rule 7 below).

2. **Adverb -게 "-ly."** Attached to a descriptive-verb stem, -게 forms an adverb that (like all
   adverbs) always precedes the main verb: 반갑다→반갑게 읽었다 "I read it gladly," 크다→크게
   보인다 "It looks big," 노랗다→노랗게 된다 "It becomes yellow."

3. **Sentence ending: intimate style -어/아.** The most intimate/informal speech style, used among
   children, between friends, or by an adult to a child. Choice of -어 vs -아 follows the same rules
   as choosing polite-informal -어요/-아요 (cross-ref L8 GN2, out of range). Intonation carries
   sentence-type meaning: rising = question, level/falling = statement or command. This is the
   register actually used throughout the L18 letter itself (e.g. 참 미안해, 빨리 와) — i.e. the
   letter is written in intimate style because it's between close friends, which is itself an
   implicit register fact about the letter-writing content below.

4. **Interruptive connective -다가** "while doing." Used only when the subject is the same for both
   actions.
   - a. V.STEM + 다가: interrupted action "while doing..." — 숙제를 하다가 친구가 와서 밖에
     나갔다 "While I was doing my homework, my friend came, and we went outside."
   - b. V.STEM + (었/았) + 다가: causal effect "because," using past tense — 하루하루 미루다가
     이제껏 너에게 편지를 못 썼다 "Because I was putting it off day after day, I didn't write you
     until now" (this sentence is drawn directly from the L18 letter itself). The book is explicit
     that this causal reading is "similar to -어/아서 or -기 때문에" but distinct in requiring past
     tense before -다가 for the causal sense specifically.

5. **Sentence ending -구나!/-군!** "Oh, I see that," "Oh, I realize that" (plain); -군요! (polite,
   formed by adding -요 to -군). Expresses the speaker's new awareness of a fact or event: 내 동생이
   오는구나! "(Oh, I see that) my brother is coming!"; 벌써 봄이구나/봄이군/봄이군요 "It's already
   spring" given in all three register variants for direct comparison.

6. **Passive constructions using -이, -리, -기, -히.** Most transitive action verbs become
   intransitive passive verbs via one of these four suffixes — general conditioning rules given
   (with the book's own caveat that there are exceptions):
   - a. -이 after a vowel-final stem (보다→보이다, 쓰다→쓰이다, and irregular 르-stem 부르다→불리다)
   - b. -기 after stems ending in ㄴ, ㅁ, or ㅅ (안다→안기다, 씻다→씻기다, 담다→담기다)
   - c. -리 after stems ending in ㄷ, ㄹ, or ㄼ (듣다→들리다, 팔다→팔리다, 뚫다→뚫리다)
   - d. -히 after stems ending in ㄱ, ㄷ, ㅂ, ㅈ, or ㄼ (먹다→먹히다, 막다→막히다, 닫다→닫히다,
     잡다→잡히다, 밟다→밟히다)
   - e. -이 again after stems ending in ㄲ, ㅌ, ㅍ, or ㅎ (닦다→닦이다, 덮다→덮이다, 쌓다→쌓이다,
     놓다→놓이다)
   Agent/actor marking for these passives: -에게/한테 "by" for persons/animate nouns (아이가
   어머니에게 안겼다 "The child is held by the mother"); -에 "by" for inanimate things (문이
   바람에 닫혔다 "The door was shut by the wind"); -(으)로 "by" for abstract nouns as cause/
   instrument (사고로 길이 막혔다 "The road is blocked because of an accident"). The book
   explicitly warns not every action-verb sentence can be passivized this way — some use an
   entirely different verb for the passive meaning (그는 나를 쳤다 "He hit me" / 나는 그에게
   맞았다 "I was hit by him," using unrelated verb 맞다 rather than any suffixed form of 치다) —
   and some meanings simply can't be expressed in passive at all. This is a substantially fuller,
   more systematic treatment of Korean's native-suffix passive system than the single 어/아지다
   auxiliary-verb passive introduced in L16 §2 above — genuinely new structured content, not
   redundant with it.

7. **Construction of -ㄴ/은/는 것 같다** "It seems that," "It appears that." Always preceded by a
   verbal modifier; expresses the speaker's opinion/impression rather than a definitive statement,
   often translating as English "I think." Full paradigm given for both action verbs (는-present,
   을/ㄹ-future, 은/ㄴ-past) and descriptive verbs (은/ㄴ-present, 을/ㄹ-future) — again explicitly
   distinguishing the two verb classes' present-tense modifier forms. In past tense, -것 같았다
   means "it seemed that"/"it looked as if."

8. **Verbs of "wish": -고 싶다, -고 싶어하다.** Subject-conditioned choice, not free variation:
   - a. -고 싶다 for a statement with 1st-person subject (I/we) or a question with 2nd-person
     subject (you): 너를 만나 보고 싶다 "I would like to see you."
   - b. -고 싶어하다 for all other subjects — statements with 3rd-person subject (he/she/it/they)
     or the 2nd-person "you": 그는 너를 보고 싶어한다 "He wants to see you."

9. **Sentence ending -기로 하자** "Let's plan to..." (plain), with -기로 하십시다 (polite formal,
   "if you please") and -기로 해요 (polite informal, "please") as the corresponding register forms.
   Explicit social-register warning given by the book: do not use the plain -기로 하자 form to a
   social superior who isn't a close relative or friend — use the question form instead (빨리
   가기로 하시겠어요? "Would you mind planning to leave quickly?"). This register constraint is
   exactly the kind of explicit usage annotation the extraction spec flags as never-skip.

10. **Hanja**: 活, 昨, 便, 紙, 回, 答, 未, 安 (see Hanja table above).

### Cultural notes

### Lesson 16 — Hangŭl invention history
The L16 reading passage (p. 173) states, paraphrased: before hangŭl was created, Koreans borrowed
and used Chinese characters (한자) to write. But because Korean and Chinese are different languages,
Chinese characters weren't well suited to expressing Korean. Chinese characters were also difficult
to learn, so many ordinary people (백성) were unable to write. King Sejong (세종대왕) realized this
and, together with scholars (학자), created hangŭl, suited to the Korean language. He then had
various books written to teach the common people this new writing system.

The book's own vocabulary gloss for 세종대왕 (p. 175) identifies him explicitly as "the fourth king
of the Yi [Joseon] dynasty," with the dynasty's span given as 1392–1910. (Note: this 1392–1910 date
range in the book's gloss is the Joseon/Yi dynasty's overall span, not King Sejong's personal reign
dates — the book does not separately give Sejong's individual reign years in this range.)

A specific invention date is given directly in a Grammar-section example sentence (p. 177, under the
passive-construction point §L16-2): "한글은 1446년에 만들어졌다" — "Hangŭl was invented (made) in
1446." This is the book's own explicitly stated date for hangŭl's creation/promulgation (matching
the real-world 1446 publication of the Hunmin Jeongeum, though the book does not use that specific
document name anywhere in this range — "한글" is the only term used throughout).

The lesson's built-in dialogue (최선생/학생 exchange, p. 173) reinforces the same facts in Q&A form:
Q: "Why did Koreans borrow and use Chinese characters?" A: "Because there was no writing system
before hangŭl existed." Q: "Is Korean a language similar to Chinese?" A: "No. The two countries'
languages are very different, so [writing Korean] using Chinese characters was difficult." Q: "Then
why do we still use Chinese characters now?" A: "Because Korean has many words borrowed from
Chinese." This last point — that hanja persists in modern Korean specifically because of the large
Sino-Korean loanword stratum, not because of any ongoing writing-system role — is itself a notable
explicit linguistic claim the book makes about why its own hanja curriculum (the 142-character
sequence this extraction has been tracking) is pedagogically justified.

### Lesson 18 — letter-writing conventions
The L18 reading passage is itself a complete sample personal letter (p. 194), which supplies a full
worked example of Korean informal letter format:
- **Opening/salutation**: recipient's name + -에게 ("to [name]") on its own line — 창호에게 "To
  Chang-ho" — with no comma or other punctuation, before the body text begins on the next line.
- **Body register**: written throughout in the intimate speech style (-어/아 sentence endings, see
  Grammar §L18-3 above) appropriate to a letter between close friends — e.g. opens "그동안 잘
  있었는지 궁금하다" ("I wonder if you've been well all this time"), closes with "...만나 보고
  싶다" ("I'd like to see you") and "...꼭 만나 보기로 하자" ("let's be sure to meet").
  (Grammar point §L18-3 explicitly notes this -어/아 style is used "among children, between
  friends" — directly consistent with this letter's own register choice.)
  Grammar point §L18-9 (-기로 하자) is likewise used in-letter at this closing ("꼭 만나 보기로
  하자" = "Let's make sure to meet"), not just in the drill examples — i.e. the letter's own
  closing formula is built directly from a grammar point this lesson teaches.
- **Date line**: right-aligned (per the page layout) on its own line, in the format
  [year]년 [month]월 [day]일 — e.g. "1990년 3월 10일" (March 10, 1990), using Arabic numerals with
  the Sino-Korean date-counter suffixes 년/월/일.
- **Signature line**: directly below the date, right-aligned, in the format [name] + 씀 — e.g.
  "영식 씀" (literally "Young-shik writes/wrote [this]" — 씀 being the nominalized form of 쓰다 "to
  write," used idiomatically as a letter-closing self-identification, roughly parallel to English
  "—Young-shik" but morphologically transparent as "[this is] Young-shik's writing"). This 씀-as-
  signature convention is distinct from, and not glossed under, the plain verb 쓰다/쓰기 entries
  already in the vocabulary table — flagging it here since the convention itself (not just the verb)
  is the notable content.
- The vocabulary list (p. 196) separately glosses 답장 "a reply (letter)" alongside 회답 "response,
  reply" as near-synonyms, and glosses 보내 주다 specifically as "to send (something *for someone*)"
  — i.e. explicitly marked as a benefactive-type send, not a bare transitive 보내다, which is
  letter-correspondence-specific vocabulary the lesson is building toward (the letter's own
  closing-section grammar, -(으)려고 하다 "plan to," 가는 길에 "on the way," and -기로 하자 "let's
  plan to," from L16-17, all converge in this lesson specifically to narrate travel-and-correspondence
  plans — "이번 여름 한국에 여행을 가려고 하는데 가는 길에 일본에 들러서 너를 만나 보고 싶다" "I
  plan to travel to Korea this summer, and on the way I'd like to stop by Japan and see you").

### Cultural notes — "About Korea" (Lesson 19 reading, pp.210–211, 220)

The Lesson 19 dialogue (printed as a reading/dialogue hybrid) presents these paraphrased
geographic/historical facts, framed as material a learner should be able to state about Korea:

- Korea is located in the Far East (극동), not Southeast Asia; Korea, Japan, and China are
  commonly grouped together as "the Far East" (p.210).
- Korea shares a land border with China but was never a part of China; the book frames this as a
  point requiring explicit correction of a plausible learner misconception (p.210).
- Korea has a "unique" (고유한) history and culture of more than 4,000 years (p.210).
- Korea was a kingdom for roughly 2,000-odd years, beginning with the Three Kingdoms period
  (삼국시대), continuing through the Koryo period (고려 시대), and lasting until the end of the
  Choson/Chosun dynasty (조선 시대) (p.210). The Three Kingdoms are individually named elsewhere
  in the lesson's vocabulary as Shilla (신라), Koguryo (고구려), and Paekche (백제) (p.211, 213).
- Democratic government in Korea dates from after the end of World War II (p.210).
- Korea became independent on August 15, 1945; the Republic of Korea (대한민국) was established in
  the South and the Democratic People's Republic of Korea (조선 인민 공화국) in the North. The
  dialogue frames the resulting division as something Koreans hope will eventually be reunified
  (통일) (p.210).
- A worked grammar example (GN6, p.215) uses the date range "1910–1986" with the "from...to"
  postposition, without further gloss — plausibly an oblique allusion to the Japanese colonial
  period (1910 onward) rather than a random date, though the book does not state this explicitly;
  flagged here as an inference, not an extracted fact.
- Several of the lesson's own illustrative grammar-practice sentences embed real-world comparative
  facts used as contrastive teaching examples rather than assertions about Korea itself: Greece and
  Turkey share a border but are not on friendly terms; Spain became a kingdom again but retains a
  democratic government; California was once part of Mexico; divided Germany was eventually
  reunified (pp.215–216, 219, 230, 232) — these are the book's own comparative illustrations, not
  claims about Korea, but are captured here since they're real-world content embedded in the
  grammar apparatus.

### Lesson 19 (한국에 대하여)

**Skipped as redundant** with 001/002/035-037: subject/topic/object particles, basic
location/source/goal particles, copula, bare existential 있다/없다, general speech-level endings,
numbers/counters, past tense -었/았, honorific -시-.

**Kept — new or only thinly covered elsewhere:**

1. **...중의 하나 "one (out) of ...", "one of ..."** (p.214) — literally "one among"; N 중의 하나
   attaches to a set-denoting noun phrase to pick out a single member. E.g. 한국은 극동에 있는 나라들
   중의 하나이다 "Korea is one of the countries in the Far East."

2. **-을/를 같이 하다 "to share ..."** (p.215) — literally "to do (something) together," extended
   idiomatically to "share X with," as in sharing a border or a meal: 한국은 중국과 경계를 같이 하고
   있다 "Korea shares a border with China." Parenthetical variant 하고 있다 noted for the
   progressive/continuative reading.

3. **Concessive connective -(으)나 "although," "even though," "but"** (p.215) — close in meaning to
   -지만 and largely interchangeable with it, but the book notes -(으)나 skews toward written
   register rather than spoken. Example: 그리스와 터키는 경계를 같이 하고 있으나 사이가 좋지 않다
   "Greece and Turkey share a border, but they are not friendly to each other."

4. **Verbal modifier -던 (two subtypes)** (pp.215–216):
   a. **V.STEM + 던**: habitual/repetitive past ("used to," "had been [repeatedly] doing") — e.g.
      아이들이 놀던 곳 "the place where children used to play."
   b. **V.STEM + 었/았 + 던**: factual/completed past, translatable with an English past-perfect
      sense ("had been," "had done") — e.g. 나누어졌던 두 독일 "the two Germanys, which had been
      divided." The book explicitly contrasts these two -던 subtypes as a minimal pair distinguishing
      habitual aspect from a single completed past event, cross-referencing its own earlier modifier
      discussion at L11/GN1 and L15/GN1 (not themselves in this extraction range).

5. **Experiential constructions with 일(이)** (p.214, 216–217):
   a. **V.MOD + 일이 있다** "there has been," "I have been [done X]" — e.g. 나는 남부에 가본 일이
      있다 "I have been to the south."
   b. **V.MOD + 일이 없다** "there has never been," "I have never been [done X]" — e.g. 미국은
      왕국이었던 일이 없다 "The U.S. has never been a kingdom." The book notes 일 itself carries a
      range of senses beyond "work" (event, business, matter, problem), and that this experiential
      construction is one specific idiomatic extension of it.

6. **Postposition -부터 ... -까지 "from ... to," "from ... until"** (p.217–218) — used for both time
   and space; -에서부터 can be realized three interchangeable ways (부터 / 에서 / 에서부터) when
   paired with ...까지, but -에서 alone (without ...까지) cannot mean "since" — only -부터 or
   -에서부터 can stand alone with that meaning. This asymmetry is the book's own explicit usage note.

7. **Postposition -(으)로 "by/in" (as in "divided by/in")** (p.218) — a further extension of -(으)로
   beyond its previously-covered "as"/"in the role of" senses (cross-referenced to L7/GN7, not in
   this range): 한국은 둘로 나누어졌다 "Korea was divided in two"; 여섯을 셋으로 나누면 둘이 된다
   "If you divide six by three, you get two."

8. **Passive construction: N. + 되다** (pp.218–219) — productive pattern: a Sino-Korean-noun-derived
   action verb formed with 하다 ("to do") becomes a passive/inchoative when 하다 is replaced by 되다
   ("to become"). Worked examples: 준비하다/준비되다 "to prepare / to be ready"; 시작하다/시작되다
   "to begin / to be begun"; 통일하다/통일되다 "to unify / to be unified." This N+하다 ↔ N+되다
   active/passive alternation for Sino-Korean nominal predicates is a genuinely systematic morphological
   mechanism not previously captured in this project's established files, and is directly relevant to
   how Sino-Korean loan vocabulary could extend productively.

9. **-기를 바라다 / -는 것을 바라다 "to wish," "to long for"** (p.219) — 바라다 preceded by -기를 (or,
   per a modifier clause, -는 것을) to express wish/desire/longing, parallel to the previously-covered
   원하다/희망하다 pattern (cross-referenced to L17/GN3, not in this range). E.g. 한국이 통일되기를
   바란다 "We wish Korea to be unified."

### Lesson 20 (전화)

**Skipped as redundant**: see shared skip list above (applies throughout).

**Kept — new or only thinly covered elsewhere:**

1. **Honorific form of address: TITLE + (선생)님** (p.227) — 선생님 appended after a title or name to
   address someone respectfully; some titles take bare -님 instead of -선생님. Two sub-categories
   given with worked examples: *educational/institutional* titles (김 선생님 "Mr. Kim," 교장 선생님
   "principal," 의사 선생님 "doctor (M.D.)," 이 박사님 "Dr. Lee (Ph.D.)," 총장님 "university
   president," 학장님 "dean") and *business/trade/government* titles (사장님 "company president,"
   과장님 "department head/manager," 장관님 "government minister/secretary").

2. **Sentence ending -ㄴ/은/는데(요) (polite informal)** (p.228) — derived from the connective ending
   -ㄴ/은/는데 ("and," "but," "by the way," "and then"); used colloquially to open a question/request
   or solicit a response without directly asking, and to imply something is left unsaid. Three
   distinct pragmatic functions given with worked examples:
   a. *Soliciting a response* ("I say," "Don't you think so?," "Is it true?") — e.g. 야구를 하면
      좋겠는데요 "It would be nice to play baseball (don't you agree?)."
   b. *Mild protest* ("but [I think]...") — e.g. 벌써 늦었는데! "(But) it's already late!"
   c. *Mild apology* ("I am afraid," "I am sorry but...") — e.g. 잘 모르겠는데요 "(Well, I am afraid)
      I don't know." This three-way pragmatic-function breakdown of a single discourse-ending particle
      is a genuinely useful pragmatics-level finding not previously captured for Korean in this project.

3. **Uses of 좀 "a little," "please"** (p.229) — commonly softens an imperative/request (죰 놀러 와요
   "Please come and visit"), or means "a little/a bit" with a degree verb (한국어를 좀 해요 "I can
   speak a little Korean").

4. **Expression for "I wish ...": -(으)면 좋겠다** (p.229) — combination of conditional -(으)면 with
   좋다 "to be good"; literally "it would be nice if...," but with -겠 attached (좋겠다) conveys "I
   wish...". E.g. 의사 선생님을 뵈었으면 좋겠다 "I wish I could see the doctor."

5. **-ㄹ/을 수 있다/없다 "can/cannot," "to be possible/impossible"** (pp.229–230) — expresses strong
   possibility/impossibility; varying the tense of 있다/없다 itself (있다 vs 있었다 vs 있겠다, etc.)
   shifts the shade of meaning between present ability, past ability/missed opportunity, and inferred
   future ability. E.g. 내일 오실 수 있습니까? "Can you come tomorrow?"; 어제 올 수 있었는데요 "I could
   have come yesterday [but didn't]."

6. **Expression for "when," "while," "at the time when": -ㄹ/을 때** (p.230) — the dependent noun 때
   (previously seen attached directly to nouns, e.g. 점심 때, 세살 때, 방학 때, cross-referenced to
   L10/GN4, not in this range) is here shown attached to the -ㄹ/을 verbal modifier to form a general
   temporal clause. E.g. 병원에 갔을 때 의사가 없었다 "When I went to the hospital, the doctor was not
   there."

### Lesson 21 (병원)

**Skipped as redundant**: see shared skip list above; additionally, the native-Korean day-counting
word set introduced on p.242 (하루/이틀/사흘/나흘/닷새/엿새/이레/여드레/아흐레/열흘, "one day" through
"ten days") is skipped here per the coordinator's instruction to treat "ordinary counters" as

**Kept — new or only thinly covered elsewhere:**

1. **Polysemous-verb inventory** (pp.238–239) — the lesson explicitly teaches six common verbs as
   having multiple, only loosely related English translations depending on context, with worked
   example sentences for each sense:
   - **아프다**: (a) hurt/ache (발이 아프다 "my feet hurt"), (b) be sick (그는 오늘 아프다 "he is sick
     today").
   - **보다**: (a) see, (b) look, (c) watch/mind (두 주일 동안 그의 집을 봤다 "I house-sat for two
     weeks"; 어제 밤 동생을 봤다 "I babysat my younger sibling last night"), (d) read (신문을 봤다
     "I read the newspaper").
   - **말하다**: (a) speak (well), (b) talk, (c) say, (d) tell.
   - **이야기하다**: (a) talk, (b) tell.
   - **듣다**: (a) hear, (b) listen.
   - **자다**: (a) go to bed, (b) sleep [for a duration].
   - **짓다**: (a) cook [rice] (밥을 지었다 "I cooked rice"), (b) build [a house] (집을 지었다 "we
     built a house"), (c) compose [writing] (짧은 글을 지으십시오 "write a short composition"), (d)
     sew [clothing] (새옷을 지어 주셨다 "mother sewed me new clothes"). This single-verb four-sense
     spread (cook/build/compose/sew) is a notable lexical-semantic finding — the shared underlying
     sense appears to be "to fashion/produce something through a crafting process," cutting across
     English categories that would normally be lexically distinct verbs.

2. **-ㄹ/을 터이다 "to be expected of"** (pp.239–240) — dependent noun 터 with the -ㄹ/을 modifier
   indicates an expected situation/event; almost always fused with a following clause connective, with
   -터이 contracting to -테. Four fused sub-forms given, each with worked examples:
   a. **-ㄹ/을 테니(까)** "since, as (it) is expected to, so" — e.g. 비가 올테니 우산을 가지고 가라
      "Since it is expected to rain, take an umbrella."
   b. **-ㄹ/을 테지만** "but, even though, although" — e.g. 열이 날 테지만 걱정하지 마십시오 "Even
      though you will have a fever, do not worry."
   c. **-ㄹ/을 텐데** "since/as (it) is expected to..." — e.g. 아플 텐데 오늘 집에서 쉬어라 "(I expect)
      you will be sick, so rest at home today."
   d. **-ㄹ/을 테고** "and, so" — e.g. 기차는 늦었을 테고 어떻게 하나? "(I am sure) I am late for the
      train, so what shall I do?"

3. **Causative D.V. + 게 하다 "to cause/make someone or something be ..."** (p.241) — a descriptive
   verb combined with -게 하다 forms a causative meaning "to make [something] be [quality]." E.g.
   따뜻하다 → 따뜻하게 하다 "to make warm" (방을 따뜻하게 해요 "Make the room warm"); 싸다 → 싸게 하다
   "to make cheap"; 차다 → 차게 하다 "to make cold."

4. **-아/어야 하다 / -아/어야 되다 "must, have to" / "should"** (pp.241–242) — both forms express
   obligation/necessity, but -아/어야 하다 is noted as somewhat stronger than -아/어야 되다. 꼭 "for
   sure" can be added to either for emphasis (이 약을 꼭 먹어야 돼요 "You must take this medicine for
   sure"). The negative counterpart of both is NOT a simple negation of 하다/되다, but the distinct
   construction -(으)면 안 되다 (see next point) — the book explicitly flags this irregularity.

5. **-(으)면 안 되다 "must not" / 안 ... 어/아야 되다 "ought not, must not, should not"** (p.242) —
   두 sub-forms:
   a. **V.STEM + -(으)면 안 되다**: literally "if (you) do..., it wouldn't do" — e.g. 감기가 들면 안
      된다 "One must not catch a cold."
   b. **안 + V.STEM + TENSE + -어/아야 하다/되다**: "ought not" — e.g. 감기가 안 들어야 한다 "One
      ought not catch a cold." The book positions (a) as the standard negative of "must" (-어/아야
      하다/되다) rather than a literal negation of the affirmative form, which is itself a notable
      asymmetric-negation pattern worth flagging for the mechanics-analysis phase.

### Kept — Lesson 22 (운동)

1. **Question word / indefinite modifier 어떤-** (dual function): as a question word, "what kind
   of"/"which" (어떤 운동을 많이 합니까? "What kinds of sports...?"); as a statement modifier, "some"/
   "a certain" (어떤 사람이 찾아 왔었어요 "Someone came to see you"). The book also reviews the full
   set of question-word/indefinite-pronoun pairs on this same pattern: 누구 (who?/someone), 어느
   (which?/some-any), 무엇 (what?/something), 어디 (where?/somewhere), 몇 (how many?/some amount) —
   each doubles as an interrogative or an indefinite depending on context, a systematic paradigm
   worth capturing as a set rather than word-by-word.
2. **좋다/좋아하다 "to like," 싫다/싫어하다 "to dislike"**: -이/가 좋다(싫다,기쁘다,슬프다) is used
   with a 1st-person subject (나/우리), and also for a 2nd-person-subject question; -을/를
   좋아하다(싫어하다,기뻐하다,슬퍼하다) is used for all other cases (3rd person, or 1st/2nd person as
   a plain declarative about someone else). A clean subject-marking-vs-object-marking split governed
   by person/sentence-type, not just lexical synonymy.
3. **Continuous stative: v. + 어/아 있다** "to be in the state of (being) ...". Contrasts with
   continuous *action* v.+고 있다 ("to be —ing"). Frequently paired with passive-verb forms (어/아지다,
   see L16 GN2; or derived passives with 이/리/기/히, see L18 GN6): 미식 축구는 잘 알려져 있지 않다
   "American football is not well known"; 값이 적혀 있다 "the price is written"; 책이 책상 위에 놓여
   있다 "the books are lying on the desk"; 학생들은 앉아 있고 선생은 서 있다 "the students are sitting
   and the teacher is standing."
4. **"Not only ... but also" connective -뿐만 아니라 ... 도**: v.stem+(tense)+ㄹ/을 뿐만 아니라 after a
   verb (그는 정구를 잘 칠 뿐만 아니라 음악도 잘 한다 "not only plays tennis well but also does well in
   music"); N.phrase + 뿐만 아니라 after a noun (한국 뿐만 아니라 일본과 중국도 극동에 있다 "not only
   Korea but also Japan and China are in the Far East").
5. **"To think/believe" 생각하다**: (a) 이다-stem + 라고 생각하다 (present tense of 이다 only); all
   other v.stems + 다고 생각하다 "to think/believe that" — parallels reported-speech -다고/-라고 (L12
   GN2); (b) descriptive-verb + 게 생각하다 "to think (it is)/consider (it to be)" — the -게 adverbial
   conversion from L18 GN2.
6. **"The fact that": V.modifier + 것**. A verbal modifier + 것 functions like an English gerund
   ("having," "becoming"): 경기를 서울에서 갖게 된 것에 대해서 어떻게 생각하십니까? "What do you think
   about the fact that the game was held in Seoul?" Tense can shift inside the modifier itself
   (future 있을 것, present 있는 것, past 있었던 것 — all glossing "the fact that there is/was/will be
   a big event").
7. **Comparative postposition -보다 ... (더) "more than"**: attaches to the standard of comparison and
   usually precedes the subject of comparison; 더 "more" optionally added for emphasis. 미국에서는
   야구보다 축구가 더 인기가 있다 "Football is more popular than baseball in the U.S."
8. **Verb -게 여기다 "to consider," "to feel"**: -게 converts a descriptive verb into an adverbial
   phrase for 여기다 (parallel to -게 생각하다, GN5b). 여기다 is restricted to descriptive verbs of
   feeling (proud, glad, annoyed, sad) and can substitute for 생각하다 but not vice versa: 자랑스럽게
   여긴다 / 자랑스럽게 생각한다 "to be/feel proud" — interchangeable.
9. **"For," "for the sake of" 위해서**: derived from 위하다 ("to be supportive of/devoted to/respect"),
   which itself is used in a limited lexical range (나라를 위하다 "to be patriotic," 남편을 위하다 "to
   be devoted to one's husband"). The much more common derived form -을/를 위해서 means "for (someone/
   some objective/something)" or "for the sake of": 올림픽을 위해서 준비를 많이 한다 "They are making a
   lot of preparations for the Olympics."

### Kept — Lesson 23 (음식에 대하여)

1. **"Just as," "like" -듯이; "it seems as if" -듯하다**: v.stem + 듯이 (clause connective preceding
   the main clause) means "just as," "like," "as if" — 말이 다르듯이 풍속도 다르다 "The custom is
   different just as the language is different." v.modifier + 듯하다 (sentence ending) means "it
   seems as if"/"it seems somewhat" — 영어가 독일어보다 쉬운 듯하다 "It seems that English is somewhat
   easier than German."
2. **Two-word verb 가지고 + v. "having," "possessing"**: 가지다 "to have/possess" combines very
   productively with other action verbs. The book tabulates a dense paradigm: 가지고 오다 "to bring,"
   가지고 가다 "to carry away," 가지고 있다 "to possess," 가지고 돌아오다 "to bring back," 가지고 돌아가다
   "to take back," 가지고 말하다 "to speak with (conviction)," 가지고 다니다 "to carry with," 가지고
   산다 "to live with (a responsibility/guilt)." Idiom cited: 사람은 빵만 가지고 살 수는 없다 "Man does
   not live by bread alone."
3. **Contrastive connective 그 반면에 / -ㄴ/은/는 반면에 "in contrast"**: 그 반면에 for sentence-initial
   position; -ㄴ/은/는 반면에 for clause-medial position connecting two clauses. 그 may be stylistically
   dropped, leaving bare 반면에 sentence-initial.
4. **"Even-if" connective -더라도**: attaches to a verb stem (present/future supposition) or
   stem+었/았 (past supposition); conveys "even if," "if," "even if it's the case" — 외국에 가더라도
   한국어를 잊지 말아라! "Even if you go to a foreign country, do not forget Korean!"
5. **Sentence ending -(는)군(요) "Oh, I see that ..."**: mild exclamation/rhetorical statement not
   necessarily addressed to anyone. Present tense: action verb takes -는군(요), descriptive verb takes
   bare stem + 군(요). Past tense: both verb types take -었/았군(요). The -요 forms mark polite-informal
   style; bare forms (no -요) mark intimate style — this -요/no-요 split is explicitly generalized by
   the book to other endings too (-ㄹ/을까(요), -ㄹ/을래(요), -ㄴ/은/는데(요)).
6. **Colloquial contractions 뭘, 그거, 그게, 그런**: 뭘←무엇을, 그거←그것, 그게←그것이, 그런←그러한,
   plus the set phrase 어서옵쇼←어서 오십시오 "Welcome." Shortening is noted as also occurring in
   borrowed foreign words: 아파트(아파트먼트) "apartment," 데모(데몬스트레이션) "demonstration,"
   슈퍼(슈퍼마켓) "supermarket," 파인쥬스(파인애플쥬스) "pineapple juice."
7. **Sentence ending -ㄹ/을까(요)? "Will it?" "Shall (I/we)?" "I wonder if..."**: expresses guessing,
   seeking permission, or proposing an action. -ㄹ까(요) after vowel-final stems; -을까(요) after
   consonant-final stems (except ㄹ); stems ending in ㄹ take only -까(요) (this ㄹ-stem rule also
   applies to GN8's -ㄹ/을래).
8. **Sentence ending -ㄹ/을래 "will," "shall"**: intimate/informal future ending; can be statement or
   question depending purely on intonation (rising = question, falling = statement) — not marked
   morphologically differently.
9. **Question ending -니? intimate/plain style**: attaches directly to verb stem or stem+tense
   (었/았 or 겠); pairs with the intimate-style declarative ending -어/아.
10. **Construction of -나 봐(요) "I think," "it seems that," "it appears that"**: informal -나봐 or
    polite-informal -나봐요, expressing uncertainty: v.stem+(tense)+나 봐(요) — 맛이 없나 봐요 "It seems
    it is not tasty" vs. 맛이 없었나 봐요 "It seems it was not tasty" (tense marked inside the
    construction itself).
11. **Construction of -(어/아)야 겠다 "must," "should," "have to"**: weaker/more tentative than
    -(어/아)야 하다/되다 (L21 GN4); carries an underlying "I think"/"I guess" sense — 비빔밥을 먹어야
    겠다 "(I think) I should have bibimbap," contrasted explicitly against 중국 음식에 대해서 알아야
    한다/된다 "One must know about Chinese food" (stronger obligation) vs. ...좀 알아야 겠다 "(I think)
    I'd better know a bit about Chinese food" (softer).

### Kept — Lesson 24 (백화점)

1. **Dependent noun -데 "place," "spot," "location"**: like -곳, always used with a preceding verbal
   modifier — 가구를 파는 데가 많다 "There are many places where they sell furniture"; 나는 방학동안에
   간 데가 없다 "I didn't go anywhere during the vacation."
2. **Expression for "only": N.phrase 밖에 + negative verb**. 밖에 literally "outside of," generally
   translates "only" when paired with a negative (안/못) or an inherently negative verb (없다, 모르다).
   Five sub-patterns tabulated: (a) 밖에+없다 "there is only" (세 싸이즈 밖에 없어요 "There are only
   three sizes"); (b) 밖에+안 되다 "it is only/amounts to only" (지금 두시 밖에 안 됐다 "It is only two
   o'clock now"); (c) 밖에+모르다 "to know only" (나는 독일어 밖에 모른다 "I know only German"); (d)
   밖에+못+v. "can do only" (그는 두장 밖에 못 읽었다 "He could read only two pages"); (e) 밖에+안+v. "to
   do only" (존은 쟁반 밖에 안 샀다 "John bought only trays").
3. **Irregular ㅎ verbs**: some verb stems ending in ㅎ drop the ㅎ before a vowel suffix — full
   paradigm tabulated across -은/(으)면/(으)세요 for 노랗다→노란/노라면/노라세요 "yellow," 빨갛다→빨간/
   빨가면/빨가세요 "red," 까맣다→까만/까마면/까마세요 "black," 하얗다→하얀/하야면/하야세요 "white,"
   파랗다→파란/파라면/파라세요 "blue," 그렇다→그런/그러면/그러세요 "that way," 어떻다→어떤/어떠면/
   어떠세요 "a certain manner." Explicitly contrasted against ㅎ-final verbs that do NOT undergo this
   change: 좋다→좋은/좋으면/좋으세요 "good," 넣다→넣은/넣으면/넣으세요 "put in," 놓다→놓은/놓으면/
   놓으세요 "place," 많다→많은/많으면/많으세요 "many," 낳다→낳은/낳으면/낳으세요 "give birth." This is
   a genuinely complete two-column (changing vs. non-changing) irregular-verb paradigm — of real
   value since it's presented as a tabulated contrast set rather than a single rule statement.
4. **Construction of N. + 이라야(만) 되다/하다 "it has to be"**: from 이다 "to be" + 야(만)되다/하다 "it
   must." Conveys a *stronger* "must" than -(어/아)야 되다/하다 (L21 GN4) and, critically, 라야(만)되다/
   하다 is restricted to the copula 이다 only — it cannot attach to any other verb (그 other verbs use
   -(어/아)야(만)되다/하다 instead). 만 may be added for emphasis. The 이 of 이라야 is dropped after a
   vowel-final noun. Ex.: 한국어 책이라야(만) 된다 "It has to be a Korean book."
5. **Causal connective -(이)라 "because it is," "since it is," "as it is"**: short form of -(이)라서;
   meaning close to -(어/아)서, -기 때문에, and -(으)니까 (GN6); used only after a noun phrase, with 이
   dropped after a vowel-final noun. Ex.: 선물용이라 작아야 한다 "Since it is for a gift, it has to be
   small"; 이것은 당신의 책임이 아니고 내 책임이라 내가 끝내야 된다 "As this is my responsibility, not
   yours, I must finish it."
6. **Causal connective -(으)니(까) "because," "since"**: meaning close to -(어/아)서; frequently used
   without 까. Ex.: 중 싸이즈가 너무 크니 사지 말아요 "Because medium is too big, do not buy it."
7. **Expression for "so," "in that way": 그렇게**: beyond the literal "so"/"in that way," also
   functions as "as you wish," "all right," and — paired with 하다 (그렇게 하지요) — as a euphemistic
   way of saying "yes"/agreeing to a request.
8. **"So-that" connective: v.stem + 게(요) "so," "so that," "in order that"**: after an action verb,
   -게 is a clause connective, but can surface as a sentence ending when the clause order is inverted
   or the second clause is understood-but-unsaid: 잠깐만 기다려 주세요. 곧 갔다 오게요 "Please wait a
   moment. So I can make a short visit."

### Grammar points

### Lesson 25

**1. Passive -히 (more on passive).** 적히다 "to be written" is the passive of 적다 "to write."
Not all verbs can form a passive this way — the book notes 팔다 "to sell" → 팔리다 "to be sold" (via
-리), but 사다 "to buy" has no passive form at all; Korean doesn't allow "all the cucumbers were
bought by us" the way English does. (p. 296)

**2. Causative V.stem + 게 하다 (more on causative).** "To make/cause (someone/something) to do
something"; with descriptive verbs, "to cause (someone/something) to be ...". Example: 그 약이
나를 졸리게 한다 "The medicine makes me sleepy." (p. 297)

**3. Two-word verbs -어/아 오다 and -어/아 가다: direction modal.** These convey directional aspect;
the reference point is always the speaker, and 오다 "come" always means toward the speaker. 사
가세요 "please buy and take it (away)" vs. 사 오세요 "please buy and bring it (to me)." (p. 297)

**4. Sentence ending -ㄹ/을께(요) "will do."** Used with an action verb (and 있다) to mark a
first-person intention or plan. 300원만 낼께요 "I will pay only 300 won." (p. 297–298)

**5. Sentence ending -(만) 합시다 "Let's make it (just)...".** An idiomatic bargaining expression:
800원만 합시다 "Let's make it just 800 won." (p. 298)

**6. Dependent noun -거리 "material," "stuff," "things."** Must follow a noun; commonly used with
food nouns (아침거리 "things for breakfast," 김치거리 "kimchi ingredients," 반찬거리 "side-dish
ingredients") but also with 이야기 "story" and 일 "work" (이야기 거리가 없다 "there's nothing to
talk about"). (p. 298)

**7a. -(으)러 다니다 "to go around (in order) to do."** 일을 보러 다녔다 "(he) went around to take
care of his business." (p. 298–299)

**7b. -(으)며 다니다 "to go around doing."** 그는 돈을 쓰며 다녔다 "he went around spending money."
(p. 299)

### Lesson 26

**1. -에 의하여 "in accordance with," "by," "from."** Derives from 의하다 "to be based upon" (rarely
used alone); attaches after a noun phrase. 경험에 의하여 만들어진 속담이다 "It is a proverb drawn
from experience." (p. 307)

**2. -이라+고 하겠다 / -다+고 하겠다 "I/we could say that...," "it can be said that...".** Builds on
the reported-speech construction (-이라고 하다/-다고 하다) by inserting -겠- for a hedged/inferential
"we could say..." reading. 그것은 지혜의 말이라고 하겠다 "We can say that they are words of
wisdom." (p. 308)

**3. -을/를 통하여 "through," "by means of."** N.P. + 을/를 통하여. 속담을 통하여 그 예를 찾아
보자 "Let's try to find an example through proverbs." (p. 308)

**4. Sentence ending -(으)라! — rhetorical question or statement, plain style.** V.stem + (으)라!,
used for resolutions or rhetorical questions/statements. 아니 땐 굴뚝에 연기 나랴! "Could there be
smoke from a chimney without a fire!" (= Proverb 3 above). (p. 308–309)

**5. "Even-if" connective -어/아도.** Attached to a verb stem it carries supposition/subjunctive
force (present/future); attached after past-tense -었/았 it is factual, "even though." 하늘이
무너져도 솟아날 구멍 있다 "Even if heaven falls down, there is a hole through which one can rise"
(= Proverb 8). (p. 309)

**6. Sentence ending -어/아라 — plain or intimate command.** The plain/intimate imperative pairing
with the -어/아 ending; perceived as rude unless used among intimates or with children. 아는 길도
물어 가라! "Make sure even if you know the directions!" (= Proverb 9). (p. 309)

**7. Uses of 마음 "heart," "mind," "spirit," "personality."** A productive idiom-forming noun with
(at least) nine distinct fixed constructions given in-lesson:
- a. -ㄹ/을 마음이 있다/없다 "to be/not be interested in"
- b. 마음이 좋다/나쁘다 "to be kind-hearted" / "to be mean-spirited"
- c. 마음에 있다/없다 "to like/favor" / "to be not interested in"
- d. 마음대로 "as one wishes," "(do) one's own way"
- e. 마음이 강/약하다 "to have a strong/weak will"
- f. 마음을 합하다 "to put minds together," "to make a concerted effort"
- g. 마음/양심에 걸리다 "to bother one's conscience," "to feel guilty"
- h. 마음을 쓰다 "to be generous," "to be concerned," "to worry"
- i. 마음에 들다 "to like," "to prefer"
(p. 310–311)

  ㅇ, ㅈ, ㅉ, ㅊ, ㅋ, ㅌ, ㅍ, ㅎ, then by medial vowel, then by final consonant (batchim) if

  ordering.

# Korean — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: *Korean Through English 1* (complete volume)

**Source:** The Language Research Institute of Seoul National University (Sang-Oak Lee, Hi-Won
Yoon, Jae-Young Han, Mee-Sun Han, Eun-Gyu Choi), *Korean Through English 1* (Ministry of Culture
and Sports / Hollym, first published November 1992, this printing 1994), PDF pages 1–144 of 144
(the entire scanned volume: front matter, Lessons 1–25, and the Appendix "한국어는 어떤 언어인가?"
/ "What Kind of Language Is Korean?"). English/Korean glossary index (printed pp. 129–138) and
grammatical-items index (printed pp. 138+) are page-locator apparatus only and were **not**
extracted, per the coverage rule (see Coverage note below).

**PDF-text-layer gotcha.** `pdfinfo` shows this PDF was produced by "Hewlett-Packard Intelligent
Scanning Technology" (an OCR scan-to-PDF tool), and `pdffonts` reports only a single embedded
Latin font (`Helvetica`) — no CJK font at all. `pdftotext` output is genuine OCR garbage (Hangul
strokes misread as unrelated Latin-ish glyphs and layout noise), **not** a fixed-offset
font-substitution cipher like the Batang-KSCms-UHC-H-Identity-H cipher found in the `Basic
Korean`/`Intermediate Korean`/`Using Korean` PDFs (see `003`/`004`/`008` entries in
`00_Extraction_Checklist.md`) — there is no CJK font object to apply an offset decode to. **All
Korean-language content in this file was extracted by rendering PDF pages to images (`pdftoppm`)
and reading them visually**, the same situation as `established/001_basic_korean_part1.md`.

**Vision-reading confidence.** All rendered pages are genuine typeset print of consistent quality;
Hangul renders unambiguously at 130–140dpi. All Korean-language vocabulary/example entries below
are marked Vision Reading Confidence `verified` unless individually flagged otherwise.

**Handwritten marginalia found and excluded.** This is a used copy: the title page carries a
previous owner's handwritten name and dormitory address ("Kelly [?]", "Jung Chul", "Dormitory #6"),
and several exercise-drill pages in Lessons 1–6 carry a student's own pencil-filled answers and
English annotations (e.g. "Peace", "Co-workers", "staying"/"leaving" written next to the printed
dialogue in Lesson 6, p. 31; filled-in blanks in Lesson 6's practice exercises, p. 33/40). **None of
this handwritten content was extracted as source material** — per the vision-reading marginalia
guard in `00_Reference_Extraction_Spec.md`, only the printed textbook content was captured.

**Non-redundant-supplement dispatch — coverage decision.** This is a genuine absolute-beginner
textbook (Lessons 1–5 teach the Hangul alphabet from scratch; Lessons 6–25 are short
greeting/shopping/phone/laundry-errand dialogues), covering essentially the same grammatical ground
already fully extracted from Byon's *Basic Korean* (`established/001`–`002`): the polite/honorific
speech-level system (`-요`/`-세요`/`-(으)시-`), core case/topic particles (이/가, 은/는, 을/를, 에,
에서, 으로), past tense (았/었/였), existence/possession (있다/없다), negation, and the
pure-Korean/Sino-Korean dual number systems. Per the coverage rule, this dispatch:

- **Skipped as fully redundant:** Lessons 1–5 (Hangul alphabet drills). These teach letter
  shapes, stroke order, and syllable-block composition through pure phonics drills (CV syllable
  grids) with **no distinct lexical vocabulary items** — they are drill material, not vocabulary or
  grammar content, and this exact ground (plus a fuller "Reading Hangul" treatment with real loanword
  examples) is already covered by `established/001`'s Unit 1. Not reproduced here.
- **Kept in full despite overlap:** Lessons 6–25's vocabulary and grammar-point tables. Although the
  underlying grammatical categories mostly duplicate `established/001`–`002`, this is an
  independently-authored source (Seoul National University's Language Research Institute, not
  Byon/Routledge) with its own distinct example sentences, its own vocabulary selection (drawn from
  a corpus-frequency word list per the book's own Preface, not the same word list Byon used), and a
  systematic per-lesson 발음/Pronunciation section flagging specific assimilation/neutralization rules
  (e.g. ㄲ/ㅆ-cluster simplification before consonants, ㅎ-deletion, ㄷ-irregular-final-consonant
  release rules) not previously captured verbatim from this angle. Per the spec's own guidance, "a
  same-topic chapter from a third, independently-authored source is not redundant by default," so the
  full per-lesson tables are captured below, compressed to vocabulary + grammar without reproducing
  dialogue lines or drill exercises (copyright discipline).
- **Extracted in the greatest depth, as the file's highest-value new content:** the Appendix,
  "한국어는 어떤 언어인가?" / "The Korean Language: An Informal Introduction for English Speakers"
  (printed pp. 113–127), a free-standing ~15-page linguistic essay by a named author (Sang-Oak Lee)
  covering phonology, morphology, syntax, and semantics from a contrastive
  English-vs-Korean-typology angle. This is genuinely independent content, not duplicated in
  `established/001`–`010`: a from-scratch account of Sejong's *han'gŭl* design rationale (featural
  sound-to-shape mapping, Yin/Yang vowel-harmony graphics), a full morpheme-by-morpheme,
  28-part syntactic breakdown of a real newspaper sentence, and a semantics/worldview section
  (speech levels & Confucian social structure, "head vs. heart" color/taste vocabulary, and a
  Korean-collectivist-vs-English-individualist "ego" contrast) not previously captured from this
  particular angle in this project's Korean corpus.
- **Not extracted:** "A Glossary of Vocabulary" (printed pp. 129–137) and "Grammatical Items"
  (printed p. 138+) — both are pure alphabetized page-locator indices of vocabulary/grammar already
  captured in the per-lesson tables below (each entry is just a headword + a page number, no new
  gloss or example), the same "pointer-only, not exhaustively transcribed" treatment already applied
  to comparable back-matter in `established/004`, `008`, `009`. The References bibliography
  (printed p. 128) is likewise a pure citation list, not extracted.

## Grammar points

### Lessons 6–25 — core beginner-level grammar (compressed; see coverage note above)

Each lesson follows an identical four-part structure (Vocabulary → dialogue → 발음/Pronunciation
notes → 문법/Grammar points), and each grammar section is built as a minimal-pair contrast between
the new ending and one already introduced. Rather than re-stating each individually-numbered rule
verbatim across 20 lessons (near-total overlap with `established/001`–`002`'s equivalent material),
the recurring grammatical machinery this volume walks through, lesson by lesson, is:

- **The polite informal ending -요 and its allomorphs** (L6–L11): -아요/-어요/-여요 chosen by Yin/Yang
  vowel harmony of the stem's last vowel (다크 "Yang" 아/야/오/요 vs. "Yin" 어/여/우/유/으/이), with
  -하다 verbs taking -해요 regardless. The book explicitly frames this as the same vowel-harmony split
  that vowel-harmony languages use for suffix choice, and flags 바쁘다→바빠요 as an exception (a
  Yin-vowel stem that nonetheless surfaces with the Yang ending 아요, attributed to the weak vowel 으
  being dropped).
- **The honorific imperative/polite request -(으)세요** (L6, L9, L14): built on the same vowel/
  consonant-final stem split (-세요 after a vowel, -으세요 after a consonant); the book notes -셔요/
  -으셔요 as interchangeable variants, and that 어서 오세요 ("come on in") is the conventional store/
  restaurant greeting to customers.
- **Copula endings -예요?/-이에요?** (L7): "is it -?", chosen by whether the preceding noun ends in a
  vowel (-예요) or consonant (-이에요); the book explicitly glosses this as expressing "to be equal
  to" as opposed to English "to be" meaning "to exist."
- **Subject particle -가/-이** (L8, L12): vowel-final -가, consonant-final -이; explicitly noted as
  optional/omittable in casual conversation, with a pause sometimes substituting for it.
- **Topic particle -은/-는** (L10, L11): consonant-final -은, vowel-final -는; glossed as marking
  emphasis or "the general topic" of a sentence, with a worked example showing how adding -은 implies
  a contrastive "but I might have something else" nuance not present in the bare sentence.
- **Past tense -았어요/-었어요/-였어요/-했어요** (L12–L25): chosen by the same Yin/Yang vowel-harmony
  split as the -요 ending (again with -하다 verbs taking -했어요 uniformly), plus honorific past
  -(으)셨어요 layering the subject-honorific -시- underneath the past-tense ending.
- **Honorific subject marking -(으)시-/-(으)셨어요/-(으)시겠어요** (L14, L18, L22): -(으)시-
  ("Will you -?" future/volitional 시겠어요, "Did you -?" past 셨어요) marks respect for the sentence's
  grammatical *subject*, independent of the addressee's own politeness level.
- **Negation**: short-form 안 + verb (L13, L25, ability-negation 못 contrasted explicitly with plain
  안 in L25) and long-form -지 않아요/-지 않으세요 (L20).
- **Object particle -을/-를** (L18, L20, L22): consonant-final -을, vowel-final -를; explicitly noted
  as optional in conversation like the subject particle.
- **Directional/locative particles** -에 (to a place/at a time, L8, L12, L19, L25), -에서 (in/at a
  place, performing an action, L15, L24), -(으)로 (to a place, direction, L21), -까지 (to, until, L8).
- **Benefactive/requestive compounds**: -어 주세요 "please do X for me" (L22, L23), -어 드릴까요
  "do you want me to -?" (honorific benefactive offer, L22), -으러 "in order to -" purpose clause
  (L23), -지 마세요 "please don't -" (L23).
- **Volitional/proposal endings**: -(으)ㄹ까요? "shall I -?" (L21, L22).
- **The dual number system** (L12, L15–L16, L19): pure-Korean numerals for hours/counting objects
  (한, 두, 아홉...) vs. Sino-Korean numerals for minutes/months/larger quantities (삼, 천, 만, 사월...),
  matching the same split already documented from Byon's *Basic Korean*.
- **-는데요/-ㄴ데요** (L19): a sentence-final ending the book explicitly glosses as adding "extra
  significance beyond what is stated literally" — a discourse/pragmatic softening device, following
  -이다 as -ㄴ데요 and all other verb roots as -는데요.

### Days of the week and telephone-register vocabulary (L19, L25)

## Appendix grammar points — "한국어는 어떤 언어인가?" ("What Kind of Language Is Korean?")

This is a signed essay (by Sang-Oak Lee, the volume's lead author) written directly for an
English-speaking audience, structured as four named sections: Phonology, Morphology, Syntax, and
Semantics, framed throughout as a systematic English-vs-Korean typological contrast. It opens with
a short **Genealogy/Language Family** subsection (Korean as a possible distant Altaic relative,
genetic relationship to Japanese asserted as suggestive but unproven — "too poor and infrequent" a
correspondence of sounds/vocabulary/endings to establish a definite genetic relationship) and a
**sociolinguistic** subsection on the North/South split (the North's "Cultured Language" standard
based on the P'yŏngyang dialect, purging foreign/Sino-Korean vocabulary in favor of native-Korean
substitutes, vs. the South's Seoul-based standard continuing unrestricted foreign/Sino-Korean
borrowing) and on regional variation within South Korea (only ~20% of Seoul's current population
can claim family residence there for 50+ years, owing to Korean War displacement; television
cited as a homogenizing force on spoken Korean).

### Writing system

Sejong the Great (r. 1418–1450) designed *han'gŭl* consonant graphs to iconically depict the
speech organs' articulation position — ㄱ [k] depicts the tongue root against the soft palate, ㄴ
[n] the tongue tip against the alveolar ridge, ㅁ [m] the lips, ㅅ [s] a tooth shape, ㅇ the throat
— and derived the aspirated/tense series from the plain series by adding strokes (indicating
[+obstruent]/[+aspirated]) or by full reduplication (indicating [+tense]). Vowel graphs instead
derive from three abstract philosophical primitives (Heaven/round dot, Earth/flat line, Man/upright
line), combined according to a Yin/Yang polarity that **is the same polarity vowel harmony tracks**
— dots placed above a horizontal stroke or right of a vertical stroke yield a Yang vowel, below/left
yields Yin. Syllable blocks combine an initial consonant (ㅇ if vowel-initial), a vowel (placed right
of a vertical-stroke vowel, beneath a horizontal-stroke vowel), and an optional final consonant.
Despite being invented in the 15th century, *han'gŭl* was not the dominant script until the *early
20th century* — classical Chinese held elite prestige for centuries, and *han'gŭl* spread from
court women outward to upper-class women and finally to lower classes. Modern Korean writing
additionally uses the Roman alphabet for un-adopted foreign words/abbreviations, Arabic numerals
(having displaced Chinese-character numerals for practical reasons: awkward for multi-digit
quantities, less visually distinct, and the pressure of international standardization), and Chinese
characters for Sino-Korean words/names (retained in newspaper headlines for conciseness, and for
names to disambiguate the large number of Sino-Korean homophones that a purely phonetic *han'gŭl*
spelling cannot distinguish). Korean writing historically followed the classical-Chinese
right-to-left vertical-column convention, shifting to left-to-right horizontal rows from the late
19th century on for reading-speed/efficiency reasons; newspapers remain the last major holdout
still using vertical/right-to-left layout.

### Phonology

Korean has 7–10 monophthong vowels depending on the speaker (two, ü/ö, are increasingly realized as
diphthongs wi/we instead); English by contrast has comparatively few pure vowels, most undergoing a
lip-position glide. Korean vowels keep constant quality/stress regardless of position in the word
(illustrated by contrasting English "banana" — unstressed schwa outer syllables, stressed [æ]
middle syllable — against Korean's evenly-stressed equivalent). **Vowel harmony** splits vowels into
Yin and Yang sets that do not mix within a native word (the same mechanism the *han'gŭl* vowel-graph
design encodes visually); the essay's own additional example beyond suffix choice is
**sound-symbolic ideophone pairs**, where a Yang-vowel form denotes a smaller-scale action and its
Yin-vowel counterpart a larger-scale action of the same type (a small stone dropping vs. a large
object plopping into water). On consonants: Korean [r]/[l] are a single phoneme in complementary
distribution (r-like sound word-medially, l-like sound elsewhere) — a systematic source of the
stereotyped Korean-English "light"/"right" confusion; Korean additionally makes a **three-way**
manner-of-articulation contrast (plain/heavily-aspirated/tense) for its stops and one affricate at
each place of articulation, versus English's two-way voiced/voiceless contrast — meaning Korean
distinguishes three different "t" sounds, three "p"s, three "k"s, and three "ch"s at the start of a
word. Final consonants are categorically **unreleased** (oral contact retained through the end of
articulation) — this neutralizes all same-place stops/fricatives/affricates in coda position,
producing large homophone sets (the essay's example: 낟/낱/낫/났-/낮/낳- all surface as [nat] in
isolation). Consonant clusters are limited to a maximum of two consonants in a row (final of one
syllable + initial of the next); loanwords with illegal initial clusters get an epenthetic vowel
[ɨ] inserted (English "strike" → 시들라이키). Unlike English's variable, syntactically-informative
word/sentence stress (e.g. reláy verb vs. rélay noun), Korean gives each syllable roughly equal
stress and duration — intonation (not stress) is what distinguishes statement from question, the
same suprasegmental point already documented for Hungarian's Ch. 2.7 in this project's Hungarian
corpus, here independently attested for Korean.

### Morphology

The core native vocabulary descends from Shilla-period Korean, but **most high-register,
conceptual/academic vocabulary is Sino-Korean** (Chinese-character compounds), which the essay
frames as inherently more formal/polite/stiff/impersonal than native-Korean synonyms — directly
paralleling the native/Sino-Korean/loanword stratification already documented from `established/
008`/`010`, but here illustrated with fresh comparative examples: Korean 약혼 "engagement" (lit.
"promise to marry") vs. Japanese's differently-ordered same-characters compound 婚約 "marriage
promise"; and two Sino-Korean-*looking* words that are actually Korea-original coinages built from
Chinese characters rather than borrowed whole — 돌 "rock" (石 "rock" + 乙, an added character used
purely to mark the word's final -l sound) and 논 "rice paddy" (水 "water" + 田 "field," since a rice
paddy is a flooded field). Chinese-character borrowing is asserted at ~2,000 years old, with
~50,000 characters existing in principle but only 2,000–3,000 in common use (10,000+ "considered
exceptional"). All Korean derivation and verb conjugation works by suffixing endings onto an
invariant stem, never by ablaut/infixation — the essay flags this as the reason literal
word-by-word translation is impossible for a fluent reader, illustrated by the same 28-part
newspaper-sentence breakdown discussed under Syntax below.

### Syntax

Korean is S-O-V (subject-object-verb) against English S-V-O; modifiers precede their head
(reversed from English postmodification); Korean uses suffixed postpositions where English uses
prepositions. The essay's centerpiece is a **full morpheme-by-morpheme analysis of one real,
syntactically dense newspaper sentence** (from *The Joong-ang Daily News*, May 6 1993, about U.N.
Security Council negotiations over North Korea's nuclear program), first broken into 3 major
clausal chunks, then 8, then a final **28-part** table pairing each Korean morpheme/particle against
its numbered position in the English translation — demonstrating concretely that a Korean sentence
of this complexity cannot be processed by simultaneous word-by-word translation, and that the
sentence's meaning-bearing predicate (with its negation/tense/evidentiality marking) is withheld
until the very last word, the reverse of English's early-subject-then-early-verb structure. The same
sentence doubles as a Sino-Korean register illustration: of the 28 parts, 19 are wholly/mainly
Sino-Korean morphemes (writable in *han'gŭl* or Chinese characters), but only 4 (containing the
proper nouns "North Korea," "China," "Security Council," "United States," plus "nuclear" as a bound
Sino-Korean morpheme attached to "North Korea") are actually printed in Chinese characters in the
original — Chinese characters in contemporary Korean journalism are reserved mainly for proper nouns
and headline-compression, not run-of-the-mill Sino-Korean vocabulary generally.

### Semantics

**Speech levels & social background.** Confucian social stratification (rather than the American
ideal that "all people are created equal") underlies Korea's speech-level system: pre-modern Korean
had as many as six distinct speech levels, each restricted to specific speaker/addressee social
pairings; the 20th century's decline of Confucianism has narrowed everyday use to two or three
levels, but the underlying hierarchy-marking function persists throughout the language (e.g. the
bare 2nd-person pronoun 너 usable only addressing a child or an age-equal close acquaintance,
otherwise requiring a title). **Head vs. heart (color/taste vocabulary).** Native Korean has
relatively few basic color terms (essentially red/yellow/blue, with 파랗다-type words covering both
green and blue — "the same word can describe both green grass and a blue sky"), but an unusually
rich *subjective/expressive* elaboration system layering different consonant strengths, vowel
qualities, and derivational suffixes onto a color-adjective stem to convey how a color *feels* to the
speaker — a distinction the essay says "goes far beyond" the English blue/bluish contrast; the same
consonant/vowel-symbolic elaboration recurs in taste vocabulary (the essay notes Koreans find it
notable that English uses the single word "hot" for both spicy chili and hot coffee). **Ego/
individualism vs. collectivism.** Where an English speaker says "my home"/"my mother"/"my country,"
a Korean speaker uses a group-possessive "our" (우리) even for singularly-possessed things like a
spouse (우리 아내 "our wife," explicitly glossed as *not* implying shared marriage — "a restricted
form of 'our' actually including only the speaker himself"). Occupational self-identification
follows the same pattern in reverse of the color/individualism split: a Korean asked about their job
typically states company and department/rank first, an English speaker their personal
skill/profession first. The essay extends this to subjective-judgment sentence structure: English
puts the experiencer in subject position ("I like chocolate," "I hope it rains"), Korean puts the
object of judgment in subject position (chocolate itself is "good"; the weather itself "would be
nice"( if it rained)).

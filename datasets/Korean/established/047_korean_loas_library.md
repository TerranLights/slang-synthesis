# Korean — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Chang, *Korean* (LOALL v.4), Part 1 — Introduction, Scripts and Sounds, Words

**Source:** Suk-Jin Chang, *Korean* (London Oriental and African Language Library, vol. 4). Amsterdam/Philadelphia: John Benjamins, 1996. Chapter 1 "Introduction," Chapter 2 "Scripts and Sounds" (printed pp. 1–18), Chapter 3 "Words" (printed pp. 19–30). PDF pages 16–45 of the scanned source.

This is a 266-page academic structural-linguistic description of Korean (not a teaching grammar), part of a four-part extraction of the whole book (see also Parts 2–4, files `067`–`069`; see this file's closing numbering note for why). The entire PDF has **zero text layer** — confirmed via `pdftotext`/`pdfimages` (a Google Books raster scan, "bookworm.evil.so"/PoDoFo-produced) — every row below was vision-read from pre-rendered 150 DPI page images. No handwritten marginalia was found anywhere in this range; all pages are clean typeset print with a "Copyrighted material"/"Material com direitos autorais" Google Books watermark footer (not source content).

**Coverage note — non-redundant-supplement scope decision.** Checked first against `013_sounds_of_korean_pronunciation_guide.md` (a dedicated phonology reference covering this book's §2.2 territory in much greater depth) and against the broad teaching-course/dictionary corpus (001–012, 014–029, 035–046) for lexical and word-formation overlap.
- **Skipped as substantially redundant:** the bare vowel/consonant inventory tables and the four core sound rules (voicing, *l*→*r* alternation, word-final neutralization, cross-boundary assimilation) — all a strict subset of 013's much fuller phonology chapter. Also skipped: `019_korean_grammar_intl_learners_part2.md`'s already-exhaustive derivational-affix-by-affix inventory (not re-extracted; only the SK/NK prefix-attachment *rule*, which 019 doesn't state, is kept here).
- **Kept as genuinely new:** the Hanca/Hankul writing-system history and the Hankul syllable-block graphemics (013 is phonology-only and doesn't cover writing systems at all); the Middle-Korean-vs-Modern-Korean historical-phonology comparison (22→19 consonants, 7→10 vowels, lost word-initial consonant clusters, lost three-way pitch accent); the Kyengsang/Kimhay dialectal pitch-accent system (explicit regional annotation); the pause/juncture syntax-disambiguation mechanism; the systematic native-Korean/Sino-Korean (NK/SK) lexical-stratification and doublet system; the loanword-adaptation/borrowing-strategy typology; the four-way compounding typology; and — the standout finding of this part — the sound-symbolism "isotope" (light/heavy vowel ablaut) system, including a productive pejorative-register mechanism on the demonstrative/deictic series that is a strong slang-synthesis-mechanics candidate.

### Morpheme breakdown

> **mianun** = Mia (proper name) + -nun (topic marker) — a sentence-initial spacing example, illustrating the book's convention of hyphenating interlinear glosses even where standard spacing runs noun and particle together. p. 10

> **chayki** = chayk (book) + -i (subject marker) — same no-space-for-particle convention. p. 10

> **mikwukeysenun** = Mikwuk (America) + -eyse (locative 'at') + -nun (topic marker) — a three-morpheme stacked particle sequence showing how multiple particles agglutinate onto a single noun without orthographic spacing. p. 10

> **mekko issessta** = mek- (eat, verb stem) + -ko (gerund/connective) + iss- (exist, auxiliary) + -ess- (past) + -ta (declarative sentence-ender) — the periphrastic progressive/perfect construction built from a connective-marked main verb plus the existential auxiliary. p. 10

> **Mia-ka ttenassey(o)** = Mia + -ka (subject marker) + ttena- (leave, verb stem) + -ss- (past) + -e(yo) (declarative/interrogative ending, polite with -yo) — the single string that, depending only on sentence-terminal pitch contour, surfaces as a plain statement, a yes/no question, or an echo-question ("did you say Mia left?"). p. 17

> **pikako palamay pwulessta** = Pi (rain) + -ka (subject marker) + o- (come, stem) + -ko (connective 'and') // palam (wind) + -i (subject marker) + pwul- (blow, stem) + -ess- (past) + -ta (declarative) — "It rained and the wind blew"; the level/sustaining intonation contour on the first clause marks it as non-final. p. 17

> **ches.kel.um** = ches- (first, prefix) + kel (step, root) + -um (nominalizer) — "first step." p. 25

## Grammar points

### 1. Genetic affiliation and historical periodization

Korean's genealogical affiliation is explicitly characterized as **indeterminate** — the leading hypothesis (Poppe 1965, Street 1962) places it within Altaic alongside Mongolian, Turkic, and Manchu-Tungus, but the author frames this as a hypothesis constrained by genuinely scanty written evidence, not a settled fact. Modern Korean descends from the **Silla vernacular** (Silla Kingdom, 676–918); the author flags that Silla's relationship to the contemporaneous northern **Koguryo** language (1st c. BC – 7th c. AD) is uncertain, framing modern Korean as plausibly "born of mixed blood" from a southern (Silla) and northern (Koguryo) source. The language is divided into three historical stages: **Old Korean** (to 11th c.), **Middle Korean** (to 17th c.), and **Modern Korean**. Old Korean data is called "all but impossible" to reconstruct, since what little survives is recorded entirely in Chinese characters; the Koryo-period (918–1392) writing method **Itwu** (recording Korean sounds via Chinese characters) is named as the best-attested intermediate stage before Hankul. p. 1

### 2. Middle Korean's richer sound system

The strongest new phonological-history content this source adds beyond 013 (a Modern-Korean pronunciation guide with no historical dimension at all). Middle Korean (pre-17th century) is reported to have had **22 consonants and 7 vowels**, versus Modern Korean's 19 consonants and 10 vowels — i.e. the consonant inventory has *shrunk* while the vowel inventory has *grown*. It also had **word-initial consonant clusters**, no longer extant in Modern Korean (three cited: <pt>, <ps>, <pst>), and **a three-way pitch-accent system** (high, rising, low), lost in Modern Korean standard speech **except in some dialects of Kyengsang Province**, where the historical rising pitch is reported to have generally been reanalyzed as vowel lengthening in the dialects that lost the tone contrast outright (this directly sets up the Kyengsang/Kimhay worked example in §8 below). p. 2

### 3. Sound symbolism as an explicit typological feature (Ch. 1 overview)

The introduction names **sound symbolism** (reduplicated ideophone-like adverbs) as a salient typological trait, citing a family of forms describing the flow/movement of water that differ by vowel quality and consonant tensing (e.g. *col-col* vs. *cwul-cwul* — smaller/lighter vs. larger/heavier trickling, and tensed variants like *ccol-ccol*/*ccwul-ccwul* for an even more vivid/intense version of the same image). p. 2

### 4. Overview of Korean's typological profile

The introduction's typological sketch characterizes Korean as: morphologically **agglutinative with rich verbal inflection**, affixes attaching to a verb stem in a **fixed order**, each marking one of honorification, tense, aspect, mood, sentence level, or sentence type; syntactically **SOV with relatively free word order**, all modifiers preceding the modified element; **non-declensional case**, marked instead by particles/postpositions attached to nouns, which also mark discourse functions (topic, focus) and form adverbial phrases; and a **topic-prominent, subject-prominent** discourse structure in which topic/known elements are routinely suppressed. Also noted: **more than half of Korean's vocabulary is Sino-Korean** (SK), producing widespread **native-Korean/Sino-Korean doublets** for the same concept. p. 2–3

### 5. The Hanca writing system

Hanca (Sino-Korean Chinese-character writing) was Korea's **sole writing system for over a thousand years** before Hankul's mid-15th-century invention, and remains in limited use today for Sino-Korean (SK) vocabulary (over half of the lexicon). The source frames Hanca as a historical East Asian "lingua franca of writing," spreading into Japan via Korea around the 7th century, and still used in Hong Kong, Malaysia, and Singapore, in addition to China and Taiwan. Within Korea, Hanca's status has been politically contested: it was **discouraged or banned from official government publications and removed from middle-school textbooks for a period (1948–54)** in South Korea, before being reinstated with a fixed middle-school curriculum of **1,800 characters**; the source describes a renewed, "increasingly loud" contemporary (as of 1996) demand for teaching Hanca starting at the elementary level, characterizing the overall policy picture as still "in flux." p. 5

### 6. The Hankul writing system — letter inventory and syllable-block structure

**Letter counts:** 10 simple vowel letters, 14 simple consonant letters as the base inventory, expanding in syllable-initial position with 11 compound vowel letters and 5 geminated ("double") consonant letters (the tense series), and in syllable-final position with 15 double/cluster consonant letters — a notably richer final-position cluster inventory than initial-position doubling.

**Compound vowel-letter composition** is fully graphically compositional: each compound vowel letter literally stacks its component simple-vowel strokes (e.g. ㅒ yay = ㅣ i + ㅏ a + ㅣ i) — the written letter directly encodes its own phonetic decomposition.

**Syllable-block composition** follows a fixed template, C(C)+V or C(C)+V+C(C) (one or two initial consonants with a mandatory dummy ㅇ filler when there is no real initial, one possibly-compound vowel, and zero/one/two final consonants). The source catalogs **seven distinct syllable-block shapes** (CV, CCV, CVC, CCVC, CVCC, CCVCC, and a non-standard CVv-type for forms like *kwi* 'ear') — see the Vocabulary table for worked examples of each shape.

**Writing direction:** traditionally vertical/right-to-left (as in Chinese), but current practice is horizontal left-to-right, attributed to the practical pressure of mixing in Arabic numerals, Roman letters, and computer typesetting. Hankul has **no upper/lower-case distinction** and no script-register distinction analogous to Japanese katakana/hiragana.

**Spelling and spacing standardization history:** a unified spelling system was first set by the Korean Language Society in 1933; an official spelling system was made public by South Korea's Ministry of Education in **1988**, alongside official spacing guidelines — spacing itself only began to be used "around the turn of the 20th century." **Considerable leeway still remains** (as of 1996) around noun compounds, numeral-classifier sequences, and verb compounds. Particles attach to the preceding noun without a space, and inflectional endings attach to the verb stem without a space — both illustrated in the morpheme-breakdown examples above. pp. 6–11

### 7. The nat-family word-final neutralization set

Distinct from 013's much fuller phonological cascades, this source's treatment of word-final neutralization is terse: it states the generalization (obstruents neutralize to unreleased stops word-finally) and gives, as its richest illustration, the five-way homophone set **nat 'grain' / nath 'piece' / nac 'day' / nach 'face' / nas 'sickle'**, all converging on the single surface form [nat̚] in isolation — independent corroborating evidence (a different author, same underlying fact) rather than a duplicate of 013's own worked derivations. p. 16

### 8. Suprasegmental features — Kyengsang/Kimhay dialectal pitch accent — flagship new finding

The single most valuable genuinely-new finding in this part, directly answering the dispatch's flag for regional/dialectal annotation. Length and tone are **not distinctive in present-day standard Korean**, but the source immediately complicates this with two concrete regional counter-cases: **vowel length disambiguates homographic words in some dialects**, and a **three-way pitch level (high/mid/low) is genuinely distinctive in some southern dialects**. The book's own worked minimal case contrasts **Seoul** (length-based) against **Kimhay** (a Kyengsang-Province dialect, tone-based):

| Word | Gloss | Seoul realization | Kimhay (Kyengsang) realization |
|---|---|---|---|
| mal | 'horse' | [mal] — short | high tone |
| mal | 'measure' | [mal] — short | mid tone |
| mal | 'word' | [ma:l] — long | low tone |

In Seoul, only the third item is phonetically distinguished from the other two (via length); in Kimhay, **all three stay fully distinct from each other**, each by its own level tone, with no length distinction at all — a dialect's prosodic system carrying *more* functional distinctiveness for exactly the segmental material another dialect has partially collapsed. This is explicit, source-marked dialectal data (Attested Region = Kyengsang Province/Kimhay dialect; Geographic Scope = regional) not duplicated anywhere in 013, whose prosody chapter concerns standard Korean only. p. 17–18

### 9. Pause/juncture as a syntax-disambiguating mechanism, and the propositive/imperative -ca homophony

A second suprasegmental finding, independent of the tone-accent one above: **pause/open-juncture placement can change a sentence's syntactic parse and lexical reading outright**, parallel in kind to 013's own "thought groups" finding but a distinct mechanism. The source's worked triple: *Wuli.cip.ey # kaca!* (no internal pause) = "Let's go to our house" (*wuli* read as possessive 'our', fused with *cip.ey*); *Wuli # cip.ey # kaca!* (pause after *wuli*) = "Let's go home" (*wuli* now the plain nominative 'we', syntactically separated); *Wuli.cip.ey # ka # ca!* (different pause placement) = "Go to our house and sleep" — here *ca* is reanalyzed from the propositive verbal suffix ("let's...") to the **independent imperative verb** 'sleep!'. This three-way *ca*-homophony, disambiguated purely by where the pause falls, is a concrete example of morphosyntactic category (bound suffix vs. free verb) resolved by prosody alone. p. 18

### 10. The native-Korean / Sino-Korean (NK/SK) lexical stratification

Korean's lexicon is built from two parallel stocks: native Korean (NK) and Sino-Korean (SK), with SK words reported as outnumbering NK words by roughly 15%. SK words entered via direct borrowing from Chinese from the 3rd century onward, plus a large secondary wave of Sino-Japanese vocabulary "naturalized" during the 1910–1945 Japanese occupation. The stocks are functionally stratified: SK skews toward cultivated/formal registers (politics, government, academic disciplines), NK dominates everyday basic vocabulary (kinship terms, body parts, numerals) — explicitly compared to English's Latin/French-vs-native split. Loanwords from English/other European languages (EK) form a third, smaller, actively-growing layer (~7%).

The most structurally important consequence is **systematic doubletting**: hundreds of concepts have both an NK and an SK word, most saliently two complete parallel numeral systems (NK caps at 99; SK alone covers 100+) with a fixed division of labor — NK for counting up to 12 (hour-telling), SK for month/day/minute/second — and two full kinship-term sets.

SK words are additionally internally productive: individual Chinese characters function as free or bound morphemes combining as root, prefix, or suffix (e.g. *hwa* 話 "speech" → *swu.hwa* "sign language," *hwa.yong* "pragmatics," *hwa.hayng* "speech act"). One-character SK words split into a nominal type and a verbal type, the latter requiring the pro-verb *hata* "do/be" to function as a predicate — this *hata*-support strategy recurs throughout the chapter as Korean's general device for verbalizing a bare nominal root regardless of origin. Two-character SK words are "unitary in meaning" with a frequently unclear root/affix boundary, and **some show a reversible character order yielding a distinct, non-compositional meaning** (*hoy.sa* "company" vs. *sa.hoy* "society"; *in.myeng* "person's name" vs. *myeng.in* "master") — a lexicalization pattern with no direct NK or English analogue noted in the source. pp. 19–23

### 11. Loanwords (EK) and borrowing strategy

English/European loanwords divide into those with an NK or SK counterpart and those without. Where a counterpart exists, the loanword and the native/SK word are frequently **not synonymous but connotatively split** — *hotheyl* "hotel" (Western-style/large) vs. SK *yekwan* "inn" (traditional/small), explicitly analogized to English *hotel* vs. *inn*; *phulinthe* "printer" (small/computer) vs. SK *inswayki* (industrial-scale).

The source documents a **historical shift in borrowing strategy for the English prefix *tele-*** ("distant"): earliest borrowings were translated semantically (*mang.wen.kyeng* "telescope" = view+distance+mirror) or pseudo-semantically (*cen.hwa* "telephone" = electricity+talk; *cen.po* "telegram" = electricity+informing, calquing the technology's electrical nature rather than "distance"), while later borrowings for newer technology are purely phonetic (*thelletaiphu* "teletype," *theylleypicyen* "television") — evidence of a shift from semantic translation toward direct phonetic borrowing as the pace of Western technological influx increased.

Any borrowed noun is verbalized productively via *hata* (*ssain-hata* "to sign," *khisu-hata* "to kiss," *alpaithu-hata* "to do part-time work," the last ultimately from German *arbeiten* via Japanese — the loanword layer is not exclusively English). Roman-letter acronyms (*KBS*, *SBS*, *PD*, *MC*, *CD*) are typically written in Roman letters even within running Korean text — the Roman alphabet functioning as a de facto third writing subsystem alongside Hankul and Hanca. pp. 24–25

### 12. Word structure: root, stem, affix, and the SK-prefix/NK-prefix attachment rule

A word is composed of one or more morphemes (root, prefix, suffix); a root becomes a stem when a derivational affix (causative, passive, etc.) is added. Affixes split into **inflectional** (doesn't change word class — e.g. nominalizers *-(u)m*/*-ki*) and **derivational** (changes word class). Word formation is illustrated with explicit tree diagrams (see Morpheme breakdown above).

**Prefix attachment is stock-sensitive**: an SK prefix normally attaches only to an SK stem, an NK prefix normally only to an NK stem — but the constraint is **asymmetric: NK prefixes can also attach to SK stems** (*on-seysang* "entire world" = NK *on-* "entire" + SK *seysang*; *hes-somwun* "false rumour" = NK *hes-* "false" + SK *somwun*), with no corresponding flexibility claimed for SK prefixes attaching to NK stems. This asymmetric cross-stock productivity rule is not stated in any established teaching-grammar file checked. Korean also has two competing ordinal-number strategies: NK numeral + *pen* "time" + NK suffix *-ccay*, or SK prefix *cey-* + SK numeral — a further NK/SK parallel-system instance, this time at the derivational-morphology level. pp. 25–26

### 13. Compounding

A compound is strictly two or more *free* words combined — the source draws a sharp line between this and a two-character SK word (not independently free) versus a genuine compound (both elements free). Nominal compounding follows four patterns: **NK+NK** (*pwul.kkoch* "firecracker"; *mul.koki* "fish," semantically opaque), **SK+SK** recursively (a 7-root nested compound, *paksa-hakwi-nonmwun-ceychwul-cakyek-sihem-wiwenhoy* "the Ph.D. Dissertation Submission Qualifying Exam Committee," with a full binary-branching bracket diagram as the upper-bound illustration), **hybrid NK/SK compounds** (mixing stocks at either level), and a newer pattern that **clips a borrowed English word and compounds the clip with an SK root** (*heyl-ki* "helicopter" = clipped "helicopter" + SK *ki* 機 "machine," paralleling native *pihyang-ki* "airplane" = "flying machine"; *khem-mayng* "computer-illiterate" on the same template as *sayk.mayng* "color-blind," *mwun.mayng* "illiterate"). This four-way typology is not present as a systematic classification in any teaching-grammar file checked. pp. 27–28

### 14. Sound symbolism: phonomimes, phenomimes, and the "isotope" system

Described as "one of the salient features of Korean lexicology": expressive (mimetic) words split into **phonomimes** (sounds) and **phenomimes** (sight/smell/taste/touch), occurring mainly as adjectives, adverbs, some verbs, and deictics. Two independent symbolic dimensions:

1. **Syllable-final-consonant symbolism**: final *-l* = "smooth-flowing," final *-ng* = "round," final *-k* = "abrupt" — each crossed with a lax/tense/aspirated initial-consonant triplet that grades intensity (*pel pel / ppel ppel / phel phel* "trembling, shivering").
2. **Vowel-quality "isotopes" (ablaut)**: medial vowels alternate between a **light** set (/a, ay, o, oy/) and a **heavy** set (/e, ey, wu, wi/), citing Martin (1992:343) for the "isotope" terminology. Light isotopes mark something literally light-weight, tiny, or fragile, and this **extends productively to a pejorative connotation** ("small, silly, insignificant") — demonstrated on the neutral deictic/demonstrative series itself: *i* "this" / *ku* "the" / *ce* "that (over there)" each have a light-isotope form (*yo* / *ko* / *co*) that turns a neutral demonstrative into a dismissive or contemptuous one when paired with a noun like *kes* "stuff," *nom* "guy," or *saykki* "guy" (e.g. *yo kes* "this (petty) stuff" vs. neutral *i kes*). **This pejorative-demonstrative mechanism — an ablaut-style vowel alternation on a closed deictic class that encodes pure register/contempt rather than referential meaning — is a strong candidate for slang-synthesis mechanics**, distinct from the lexical-item-level slang already catalogued in `030_dirty_korean_everyday_slang.md`/`032_making_out_in_korean.md`.

Reduplication is a general-purpose device: a reduplicated noun reads as plural/distributive/collective (*cip cip mata* "each and every house"), a reduplicated adjective/adverb intensifies the base meaning (*khuna-khun* "really big"). pp. 29–30

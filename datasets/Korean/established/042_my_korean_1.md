# Korean — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: My Korean 1 (complete: Units 1–10 + Appendices)

**Source:** Young-A Cho, In-Jung Cho, Douglas Ling, *My Korean 1* (2nd ed., 2010, Monash
University; open-access, Creative Commons Attribution-Noncommercial-Share Alike 2.5 Australia
License). Full book, printed pp. 1–448 (PDF pages 20–467 of 471; printed pp. 449–471 are a blank
handwriting-practice sheet and keyboard-layout diagram with no extractable linguistic content,
confirmed by direct inspection and excluded). Page offset verified empirically at multiple points
throughout the book: **printed page N = PDF page N+19**.

**Text layer:** confirmed real and clean throughout the entire 471-page PDF — Hangul renders
correctly via plain `pdftotext`, no font-substitution cipher, no CID/ToUnicode mapping problem, no
vision-reading needed anywhere in the book. No handwritten marginalia found (this is a clean
printed copy, not a used/annotated scan).

**Extraction method:** this is a large (471pp) beginner course book, the 12th Korean reference
book extracted into this project. It was extracted by six parallel subagents, each assigned one
bounded page range (Units 1–2, Units 3–4, Units 5–6, Units 7–8, Units 9–10 + Transcript, and the
Appendices), each independently instructed to apply the project's non-redundant-supplement rule
against the 11 Korean course books already in `established/001`–`040`. This top-level file is the
orchestrator's own merge of all six sub-extractions into one deliverable, verified for header
format, unit-sequence completeness, and absence of placeholder text.

## Coverage note — non-redundant-supplement dispatch (master summary, auditable per unit)

This book sits on heavily-trodden beginner territory (basic particles, numbers, counters, speech
levels, honorifics, kinship terms) already covered by 11 prior Korean course books. Per the
project's standing convention, **all vocabulary is kept in full throughout, regardless of grammar
overlap** — independently-chosen example sets always count as non-redundant. Grammar is kept in
full only where genuinely new, deeper, or more systematic than the closest existing comparator;
otherwise it is either cross-referenced briefly or skipped with the specific prior file(s) named.
Full per-unit audit trails are preserved in each unit's own Vocabulary/Grammar points sections
below; this summary is a roadmap, not a replacement for those details.

- **Units 1–2** (greetings/intro, Hangeul): skipped the basic letter inventory, syllable-box
  mechanics, batchim neutralization, resyllabification, nasalization, and ㅎ+obstruent aspiration
  (all already in `001`, `013`, `015`, `016`, `024`) and the copula allomorphy (8+ prior files).
  Kept as new: the seonbae-nim/kinship-substitution register switch, ㅎ-weakening-to-silence,
  ㄹ-doubling before a palatal glide, finer 씨-usage pragmatics, era-dated given names (1945/1975/
  2006), clan-branch (bon-gwan) naming convention, and a four-way (not two-way) third-person
  introduction register ladder.
- **Units 3–4** (likes/dislikes, places): skipped the 4-tier speech-style framing, SOV word order,
  rising-intonation questions, pronoun avoidance, negative-question polarity, indirect-refusal
  norms, spacing rules, 같이 palatalization, topic particle 은/는, and the 다녀요/와요/가요 deictic
  mechanisms (all documented across 001, 007, 009, 013, 020, 024, 031, 035, 037, and others). Kept
  as new: 어디 가(세요)? as an idiomatic greeting with its own intonation-contrast mechanism, and a
  syllable-count-conditioned exception to the destination particle +에's omission rule.
- **Units 5–6** (daily routine, time/appointments): skipped re-deriving the basic present-tense
  `+아/어(요)` mechanism, `+니?/+냐?`, `몇`, `어때(요)?`, `+자`, `+부터...+까지`, and `+(으)ㄹ까요?`
  (10–17 prior files each). Kept as new: a two-way ㅂ-irregular split (+와 vs. +워) absent from
  `001`'s comparator treatment, an explicit gender-association note for `+니?/+냐?`, and the
  `잘생기다` past-tense-shaped stative lexical quirk.
- **Units 7–8** (past events, café/restaurant ordering): kept `+(으)ㄹ래요`'s fuller three-tier
  paradigm and person constraint (vs. `025`'s brief treatment), two counter words absent from
  `001`'s list (분, 자루, 통) plus the native-number extension to 90, and a genuinely new
  nominal-vs-clausal-vs-sentential three-way "and" framing (+하고/+고/그리고) distinct from `001`'s
  pure formality-gradient treatment of the same particles.
- **Units 9–10 + Transcript** (prices, discounts, family/self-introduction): skipped re-deriving
  the Sino-Korean base numeral table (`001`), the 얼마예요? price pattern (`016`), delimiter +만
  (`025` §14.1), and honorific particles 께/께서(는) (`025` §13.2). Kept as new: the three-way
  demonstrative-pronoun paradigm, Word Contractions (institutional-name clipping), the collectivist
  우리-for-내 possessive finding, the '...이/가 어떻게 되세요?' register-switching idiom, and —
  flagged high priority — the full **Sending a Text Message** digital-register section (syllable
  clipping, jamo-repetition laughs, Hangul-jamo-based emoticons). The Transcript of Listening Tasks
  (printed pp. 355–373) was scanned in full and confirmed to be pure audio-script reuse of
  dialogues/vocabulary already in Units 1–10; skipped entirely as redundant.
- **Appendices** (systematic verb/adjective conjugation tables + particle inventory): skipped
  re-documenting individual endings (거든, ㄹ래요, present/past/future, etc. — already in 9+ prior
  files). Kept as new: the book's own complete "Special Conjugation Rules" statement (all stem-final
  irregularity classes in one place), representative irregular-class stems verified consistent
  across all nine paradigm endings, and the full Appendix 17 particle/suffix inventory. The ~800+
  row verb/adjective × ending grids themselves were **not** transcribed row-by-row (copyright
  discipline + comprehensive-but-not-exhaustive rule) — only representative rows are kept.

No text-layer, font-cipher, CID-mapping, or vision-reading issues were encountered anywhere across
the full 471-page book. No handwritten marginalia was found anywhere.

**Morpheme breakdown (selected recurring forms; not exhaustive — only load-bearing fixed
expressions whose internal composition matters for later mechanics analysis).**

> **안녕하세요?** = 안녕 ("peace/well-being," noun) + 하 (do/be, verbalizer) + 세 (honorific
> infix) + 요 (polite-ending particle) — the single most frequent Korean greeting is
> transparently [noun-root + verbalizer + honorific + polite-ending], the same template the book
> reuses for 안녕하십니까 (+ formal 십니다 ending instead of 세요) and 안녕히 가세요/계세요
> (안녕 + adverbializer 히 + 가다/있다 "go/stay" + 세요).

> **처음 뵙겠습니다** = 처음 ("the first time," noun) + 뵙 (humble/honorific verb stem "see,
> meet," suppletive for 보다) + 겠 (prospective/intention marker) + 습니다 (formal-ending) —
> literally "(I) will-see (you) for-the-first-time," built on a lexically suppletive honorific
> verb rather than a regular honorific infix.

> **만나서 반갑습니다** = 만나 (meet, verb stem) + 서 (because/and-then connective) + 반갑
> (be glad/delighted, adjective stem) + 습니다 (formal ending) — "because (we) met, (I) am
> delighted," showing the clause-chaining connective 서 fusing a reason/sequence clause onto the
> main predicate.

## Grammar points

### Unit 1 grammar points

#### Three-tier greeting register (안녕?/안녕하세요?/안녕하십니까?)
Korean greetings form a three-step politeness/formality ladder: 안녕? is very casual and not used
among adults; 안녕하세요? is the generally-expected honorific default; 안녕하십니까? is
honorific-and-formal, used e.g. by a student greeting a teacher. The same honorific-infix +
formal-ending pattern recurs throughout the unit's other paired expressions (p.5–6).

#### Pronoun avoidance: 'you' is almost never used
When addressing someone directly, Korean strongly prefers a title, name, or kinship term over an
overt second-person pronoun — flagged explicitly by the source as a near-absolute tendency, not
just a stylistic option (p.7). (Already documented across 7+ prior established files.)

#### Third-person-introduction register ladder: 이분은 / 이쪽은 / 제 친구 X / bare casual
Introducing a third person to someone offers (at minimum) four register choices: honorific 이분은
("this distinguished person"), polite 이쪽은 ("over here"), formal 제 친구 X입니다 ("this is my
friend X"), and casual bare naming with no "this is" framing at all (p.8). This is a different,
fuller paradigm than `established/016`'s 이분은-vs-여기는 pair — 016 contrasts only two of these
forms (and uses 여기는 rather than 이쪽은 for the non-honorific option), so this book's four-way
ladder, and specifically its use of 이쪽은, is a newly attested form.

#### Copula allomorphy +이에요/+예요 (cross-reference only)
Vowel-final nouns take +예요, consonant-final nouns take +이에요 (casual +야/+이야, formal
+입니다, invariant for both). Already documented extensively elsewhere in `established/` (8+
prior files); not re-explained here (p.9–10).

#### Korean family-name demographics and clan branches (bon-gwan)
The three commonest family names (김/이/박) together make up roughly 45% of the population.
Family-name groups are subdivided by patrilineal descent into "branches" (e.g. ~280 branches of
Kim), each typically named for a claimed geographic point of origin ("Kyeongju Kim"). Until
recently it was illegal to marry within the same branch, no matter how distantly related the
individuals actually were (p.14). New — not found elsewhere in `established/`.

#### Office titles: mandatory-if-known, and the pragmatics of 씨
A known title must be used for its holder even if that person isn't your own superior — titles
carry a mandatory +님 honorific suffix. Where no title applies and the addressee is of equal or
lower status, full name + 씨 is used — but three constraints the source flags explicitly: (1)
never attach 씨 to a bare surname alone (offensive), (2) it is rude for a junior to address a
senior with name+씨, and (3) it is best avoided between two people of the same gender "at this
stage" of the course (p.16–18). Point (1) is already in `024`; points (2) and (3) are new nuances
not found elsewhere in `established/`.

#### Addressing peers at school: seonbae/hubae, and the seonbae-nim/kinship-term switch
Within the same school, someone in a higher year is called 선배 ("seonbae"), someone in a lower
year 후배 ("hubae") — purely about relative year, not raw age. The vocabulary pair itself is
already well documented elsewhere in `established/` (005, 008, 011, 030, 031, 032), but this book
adds a register-switching rule not found in any prior file: if you are *not* close to an
older-year person, you add the respectful suffix 님 ("seonbae-nim"); if you *are* close to them,
you instead drop the academic-hierarchy title altogether and use a kinship term (형/누나/언니/오빠,
chosen by the speaker's own gender and the addressee's relative gender/age) regardless of the real
year gap (p.18).

#### Addressing unknown people in shops: a venue/age/gender-conditioned register ladder
Shop staff never self-introduce by given name (unlike the Australian norm the source contrasts
this with). Address terms vary systematically by venue, the addressee's apparent age, and (for
market stalls) gender: bank clerks may be called 선생님 or 고객님; market stalls use 언니 for young
women, 아줌마 for middle-aged women, 아저씨 for middle-aged (and sometimes younger) men, and
할아버지/할머니 for elderly customers of either gender; cafés/restaurants most often just use the
gender-neutral attention-getter 여기요 rather than any kinship term at all, though 언니 is also
usable there by a female customer addressing a young waitress (p.19). A more systematic breakdown
by venue than the scattered mentions in prior files.

#### Saying goodbye: leaving vs. staying, each with its own honorific/formal/casual forms
Korean goodbye expressions are selected by whether the addressee is the one leaving or the one
staying — a distinction with no single-form English equivalent. Each branch has its own
honorific/honorific-formal/casual triplet (안녕히 가세요/가십시오/잘 가 for someone leaving;
안녕히 계세요/계십시오/잘 있어 for someone staying). Close young friends collapse both branches to
the single casual 안녕 (p.24–25).

### Unit 2 grammar points

#### Hangeul letter inventory, syllable-box structure, and Hunmin Jeongeum iconicity (cross-reference only)
The basic/aspirated/tensed-consonant sets, the nine pure vowels and twelve combined vowels, the
syllable-box writing convention, and the Hunmin Jeongeum consonant/vowel-shape etymology are all
already documented — most thoroughly in `established/015` (which additionally covers the
aspirated/tense stroke-derivation and the vowel-harmony link) and the basic reading system in
`established/001`. Not re-explained here (p.32–55).

#### Final-consonant neutralization / batchim (cross-reference only)
Already documented as `established/016`'s "Hangeul batchim neutralization" grammar point. This
book's own presentation adds no new mechanism, only a slightly more exhaustive worked
19-consonant → 7-sound mapping table with its own example words (kept in the vocabulary table
above) (p.51–52).

#### Sound shifts 1 — resyllabification/liaison (cross-reference only)
The general principle that a syllable-final consonant resyllabifies onto a following vowel-initial
syllable (책이 is pronounced chaegi, not chaek-i) is already documented in at least four prior
files, including a dedicated phonology source (`013`). Not re-explained (p.58–59).

#### Sound shifts 2a — nasalization of p/t/k-based codas before ㄴ/ㅁ (cross-reference only)
Same mechanism as `024`'s rule 4.5. Not re-explained; this book's own examples are 입니다→임니다,
있는데→인는데, 일학년→이랑년 (the last presented as-is from the source; its derivation appears to
combine more than one rule and is reported here without further correction) (p.59–60).

#### Sound shifts 2b — ㄹ-doubling before a palatal glide (new)
Beyond the already-documented ㄴ+ㄹ→ll rule (`024` 4.6(1)), this book adds a second, distinct
conditioning environment for the same doubled-ㄹ(l) outcome: ㄹ followed directly by 이/야/여/유 (an
i/y-initial syllable) also doubles, e.g. 서울역 ("Seoul Station") → pronounced 서울력. This
environment (ㄹ + palatal glide, not ㄴ + ㄹ) is not listed among `024`'s three ㄹ-peculiarity
rules and is kept here as new (p.60).

#### Sound shifts 3 — automatic post-obstruent tensification (cross-reference only)
Same mechanism as `024`'s rule 4.7 (a plain consonant tensifies after a syllable-final p/t/k-type
coda). Not re-explained; examples kept are 학교→학꾜, 식당→식땅, 국밥→국빱, 책상→책쌍, 숙제→숙쩨
(p.61).

#### Sound shifts 4a — ㅎ-weakening to silence (new)
Distinct from the aspiration-formation rule below: plain ㅎ itself weakens to silence (rather than
combining with an adjacent plain stop to form an aspirate) when it occurs between two vowels,
immediately after the nasal consonants ㄴ/ㅁ, or immediately after ㄹ. Examples: 좋아요→조아요,
전화→저놔, 감히→가미, 말해 봐→마래 봐. Not itemized among `024`'s phonological rules (which cover
nasalization, ㄹ, tensification, and ㅎ+obstruent aspiration, but not plain ㅎ-deletion) and is
kept here as new (p.62).

#### Sound shifts 4b — ㅎ + plain obstruent → aspirate ("leap-frogging ㅎ") (cross-reference only)
Same mechanism as `024`'s rule 4.8: ㅎ adjacent (either order) to a plain ㄱ/ㄷ/ㅂ/ㅈ produces the
corresponding aspirate (ㅋ/ㅌ/ㅍ/ㅊ) and the ㅎ itself disappears. Not re-explained; examples kept
are 축하→추카, 좋다→조타, 입학→이팍, 그렇지→그러치 (p.62–63).

### Unit 4 grammar points

#### 어디 가(세요)? as an idiomatic, non-literal greeting (new)
Korean uses 어디 가? ("are you off somewhere?") as a phatic greeting, directly parallel to
Australian English "how are you going?" — the question is not meant to elicit a real destination,
and a vague 응, 어디 좀... ("yeah, just somewhere...") is the normal, fully adequate reply. The
source gives this idiom its own **prosodic disambiguation mechanism** distinguishing it from the
literal question: in "Where are you going?" (the real question), the final syllable 가 rises
slightly then falls; in "Are you off somewhere?" (the greeting), 가 rises steeply. The book's
practical advice for a learner unsure which reading is intended: default to the vague reply, since
a Korean who is not close to you will not actually ask "where are you going?" as a real question.
This item was not found in any of this project's other 40 Korean established files (p.99–100).

#### Destination particle +에 'to' — an omission rule keyed to syllable count (new)
+에 marks a destination and attaches directly to the end of the place word (no space). Already
documented: +에 can be freely dropped in casual speech when context is clear (학교(에) 가요). **New
nuance this book adds:** that omission is blocked specifically when the place word is
**monosyllabic** — 집, 강, 산, and so on always keep +에, regardless of register or
context-clarity, unlike polysyllabic place words. A more specific (and different) condition than
the only exception previously documented (`020`'s place-vs-time distinction) (p.105).

#### Coming & Going 와요/가요/다녀요 (cross-reference only)
The book's own explanation (와요/가요 anchored to speaker location; 다녀요 implying regular/
habitual attendance vs. a single trip) restates mechanisms already fully captured in `007` and
`037` respectively. Its illustrative examples are new sentences but not new grammar (p.112).

#### 토픽/보조사 revisited: 은/는 contrastive use (cross-reference only)
Already documented in depth in `001` Unit 7 (four core functions, including the contrastive use
this book illustrates with 폴은.../수미는...). No new nuance found in this book's four-example
presentation.

### Unit 5 grammar points

#### Verb present-tense endings: casual/polite/honorific `+아/어`, `+아/어요`, `+(으)세요`
Paraphrased (mechanism itself already documented elsewhere in `established/`, but this book's rule
set and Verb Table are fuller than `001`'s Unit 4 comparator): a verb stem's last vowel decides the
ending — ㅏ/ㅑ/ㅗ/ㅛ → +아; anything else → +어; 하-final stems become 해. No-final-consonant stems
contract (오+아→와, 주+어→줘); ㅡ-final stems drop that vowel before the ending (쓰→써). Polite form
adds 요; honorific is +세요 (vowel-final stem) or +으세요 (consonant-final stem) (p.120–126).

#### Casual question endings `+니?`/`+냐?` (new sociolinguistic note)
Both attach directly to the verb stem and are interchangeable in casual speech, but the book makes
an explicit sociolinguistic claim not confirmed as already captured elsewhere in this project:
`+냐?` is "more blunt" and used more by male speakers, while `+니?` is "less blunt" and used more by
female speakers — a register/gender-association split on top of the basic casual-question
mechanism (p.126–127).

#### 뭐 'what' and topic/subject omission
뭐 is Korean's "what." Because Korean regularly omits an understood subject/topic, "What do you
like?" is simply 뭐 좋아해/좋아하니/좋아하냐/좋아해요/좋아하세요? across all five speech styles, with
no overt pronoun (p.127–128).

#### Time particle `+에` 'in/at/on' and its exceptions
+에 attaches to a time word to mark when something happens. A small closed set of time words —
오늘, 지금, 어제, 올해, 내일 — take **no** particle at all. When multiple time words stack in one
phrase (아침 여덟 시에), +에 attaches only to the last one (p.135).

#### Location particle `+에서` 'in/at' vs. destination/time `+에`
+에서 marks the location *where an activity happens* (식당에서 점심 먹어요) — a distinction English
doesn't grammaticalize the same way, since English "at" covers both (p.137).

#### Asking opinions `...어때(요)?` 'How is...?'
A topic noun followed directly by 어때/어때요/어떠세요 (casual/polite/honorific) asks "How is
[topic]?" There is no copula — 어때(요) itself carries the meaning of "is how" (p.145–146).

#### Adjective present-tense endings and the two-way ㅂ-irregular split (genuinely deeper than `001`)
Adjectives follow the same casual/polite/honorific mechanism as verbs. The irregular-stem detail
genuinely extends what `001` Unit 4 captures: for a stem ending in ㅂ, deleting ㅂ and adding +워 is
the common pattern (어렵→어려워), but when the stem's *own last vowel* is ㅗ, the ending is instead
+와 (곱→고와) — a rarer alternant the book flags outright as old-fashioned (곱다 itself glossed
"pretty/fair," archaic). Honorific form for a ㅂ-final stem deletes ㅂ and adds +우세요 (아름답→
아름다우세요) (p.147–151).

#### 잘생기다 'good-looking': a present-state adjective lexicalized in past-tense shape
Unlike every other adjective in the Adjective Table, 잘생기다's casual present-tense form is given
as 잘생겼어 — built on the *past*-tense ending -었- even though it describes a current state. The
book marks this with an asterisk as an exception to its own pattern. Worth noting for any conlang
register design borrowing Korean's change-of-state-via-past-tense strategy for stative/appearance
adjectives.

### Unit 6 grammar points

#### 무슨 'which/what kind of/what' vs. 뭐 'what'
무슨 precedes a noun and strictly means "what kind/type of [noun]?", properly answered by naming a
category (무슨 영화 좋아해? → 액션 영화), though in casual usage it's also accepted for plain "what
[noun]?" questions. An alternative construction, Noun + 뭐? (알바 뭐?), achieves the same effect as
무슨 Noun? without 무슨. 무슨 can also mean "any" in a negative/uncertain context (무슨 계획 있어?)
(p.160).

#### Spaces between words — particle list consolidated (cross-reference only)
Reiterates that particles attach to their host noun with no space. Consolidates the particles
taught so far: destination +에, location +에서, time +에, and the topic particle +은/는 — the
particle itself is assumed already documented at length elsewhere in `established/` (p.159).

#### Telling time: Pure Korean numbers + 시
Korean hour-of-the-day expressions use Pure Korean numerals (not Sino-Korean) + the counter 시
"o'clock." The same Pure Korean numerals + 시간 "hour(s)" express a duration. Minutes instead use
Sino-Korean numbers (p.162–163).

#### 몇 'how many/what/how (old)' spans several distinct English question words
몇 is used for any question about a numerical quantity — a single Korean word where English splits
across "how many," "what," and "how." A clean case of the project's "don't use English as the
measuring stick" principle: English's lexical split along quantity/identity/age lines doesn't
correspond to any boundary in Korean's own grammar (p.163–165).

#### Suggestions 1: `+자` 'Let's...'
Attaches directly to a verb stem to propose a joint action in casual speech only — no polite or
honorific suggestion form is given in this unit (같이 가자, 밥 먹자) (p.168).

#### `...+부터...+까지` 'from...till...'
+부터 marks a starting time-word, +까지 marks the ending time-word, used together to bound a time
span (세 시부터 다섯 시까지) (p.174).

#### Suggestions 2: `+(으)ㄹ까요?` 'Shall we...?'
Attaches to a verb stem to propose a joint action as a question: vowel-final stem +ㄹ까(요)?,
consonant-final stem +을까(요)?. Casual and polite forms are given; no honorific form in this unit
(p.178–179).

#### Echo questions as a conversational-support device
The book flags echo questions (repeating back a word someone just said, e.g. "토요일?") as a
distinct pragmatic device for confirming understanding and signaling active listening —
independent of its own grammatical structure (just the noun + rising intonation) (p.132, Note 1).

### Unit 7 grammar points

#### Verbs and Adjectives: Past Tense Endings (unusually complete paradigm, kept for completeness)
Three parallel past-tense paradigms, each built the same way the present tense already is, just
swapping in a past-tense ending:
- **Casual**: stem + 았어/었어 (ㅏ/ㅗ-class stems take 았어, everything else 었어; 하다-stems become
  했어). Vowel-final stems contract (오+았어→왔어); 으-final stems drop 으 (나쁘→나빴어). ㅂ-irregular
  stems delete ㅂ and add 웠어 (쉽→쉬웠어), except when the stem's own vowel is ㅗ, which takes 왔어
  instead (곱→고왔어).
- **Polite**: casual form + 요 (살았어→살았어요).
- **Honorific**: stem + 셨어요 (vowel-final) or +으셨어요 (consonant-final, except ㅂ: 아름답→
  아름다우셨어요). A closed suppletive set replaces the regular paradigm: 드시다 "eat/drink" (hon.
  of 먹다/마시다), 잡수시다 "eat" (hon.), 계시다 "be/exist" (hon. of 있다), 말씀하시다 "speak" (hon.
  of 말하다), 주무시다 "sleep" (hon. of 자다), 돌아가시다 "pass away" (hon. of 죽다 "die"), 편찮으시다
  "be ill" (hon. of 아프다).
This is an unusually complete, cleanly tabulated three-tier past-tense paradigm with its own
suppletive-verb list; while past-tense formation itself is covered to varying depth across most of
this project's other 11 Korean reference books, this particular full paradigm-plus-suppletive-list
layout is preserved here for completeness (p.195–198).

#### 못 'cannot' or 'did not' because of inability — unintentionally
못 negates a verb the same slot-position way 안 does, but carries a specific semantic contrast the
book states explicitly: **안 implies the subject chose not to do something; 못 implies an external
circumstance made it impossible**, even where English would just say "didn't" rather than
"couldn't." This means 못 is used far more often in Korean than "cannot" is used in English.
Pronunciation note: 못 assimilates before a following nasal/liquid-initial verb (못 만나요 →
mon-man-na-yo; 못 했어요 → mo-t'aess-eo-yo) (p.208–209).

#### +거든요 'It's because...'
Attaches to a verb/adjective stem (present: +거든요; past: +았/었거든요) to supply a rationale for
something already raised in the conversation — equivalent to English "it's because...". The book
characterizes it as a very common, "soft" ending that helps maintain rapport/a friendly tone,
distinct from a blunt direct-cause statement (p.210).

#### 그리고 and +고 'and'; 'and then'
**그리고** is a sentential connective: it sits at the start of a second, independent sentence and
functions like English "and"/"and then." When the two sentences have different subjects, each
takes +은/는 rather than +이/가. **+고** instead fuses the two sentences into one by attaching
directly to the first verb/adjective's stem — critically, **tense is carried only by the final
verb**, even when both clauses are semantically past tense (수미는 저녁 먹고 다시 나갔어요, not
먹었고). +고 works identically whether joining verbs or adjectives (저 식당은 싸고 음식이
맛있어요). A discourse nuance: a +고-chained "did X and then Y" can specifically emphasize that the
speaker did X *before* Y (먹고 왔어요 "I ate and then came home," stressing he already ate before
arriving) (p.216–217).

#### Three 'ands': +하고, +고, and 그리고
The book gives an explicit three-way comparison distinguishing **what kind of constituent each
"and" joins** — a genuinely different axis of comparison from the pure formality-register gradient
already captured for noun-connective 하고/(이)랑/와·과 in `established/001` Unit 13:
1. **+하고** is a *particle* that joins two **nouns** (사과하고 오렌지).
2. **+고** is an *ending* that joins two **verbs or adjectives** (텔레비전 보고 공부해요; 아담하고
   예뻐요).
3. **그리고** is a *transitional word* that joins two independent **sentences** (저녁을 먹었어요.
   그리고 다시 나갔어요.).
This nominal-vs-clausal-vs-sentential three-way split is a clean, independently useful grammar-
logic distinction for how "and" scope varies by syntactic level in Korean, worth preserving as its
own paradigm even though the individual particles/endings are independently well documented
elsewhere in this project (p.218–219).

### Unit 8 grammar points

#### Asking for Something in a Shop
A simple existential-question template, [noun] 있어요? "Do you have any [noun]?", answered either
네, 있어요 or 아니요, 없어요. The template's application is much broader than literal shop
transactions — the same examples cover asking whether someone has a question, free time, or a
boyfriend/girlfriend (p.229).

#### +(으)ㄹ래요 'I want to...'; 'I will...' (fuller paradigm than `025`, kept)
Three-tier paradigm: casual (+ㄹ래/을래), polite (+ㄹ래요/을래요), and a question-only very-polite
form (+실래요?/으실래요?). **Grammatical constraint the book states explicitly: the sentence
subject must be "I," "we," or "you" — never third person** — structurally parallel to (but distinct
in register from) the "wanna" framing already captured in `established/025` §12.8. The
very-polite question form +(으)실래요? requires switching to the suppletive honorific verb 드시다
in place of 먹다 "eat" or 마시다 "drink," which cannot themselves take this honorific ending
(p.238–239).

#### Counting Nouns
Korean counting nouns (classifiers) are structurally closer to English's limited "two cups of
coffee" construction than to a true plural system: **every Korean noun stays grammatically
singular even when a number specifies a count greater than one**. 개 is the default general-
purpose counter for most inanimate objects but is ungrammatical for people; people instead take
사람/명 (neutral) or 분 (honorific, for elders/customers/seniors). The modified pre-counter forms
of 하나/둘/셋/넷/스물 (한/두/세/네/스무) are used whenever one of these numbers directly precedes a
counter (두 병, not *둘 병) (p.242–244).

#### Pure Korean Numbers — extension past 20, and a frequency/register note (new)
Extends the native number series already partially introduced for telling time; the book flags a
real frequency/register fact about the series' contemporary range: although native-Korean numbers
formally exist all the way to 99, "nowadays most Koreans tend to use them only for numbers up to
about 20" — Sino-Korean numbers take over in practice above that point. A genuine register/
frequency observation worth flagging for the slang-mechanics phase (p.256).

#### Noun +하고, +(이)랑, and +과/와 'and' (brief — base gradient already documented)
Covered in full by `established/001` Unit 13 as a three-way formality gradient (와/과 most formal/
written, 하고 middle, (이)랑 most informal/colloquial) — not re-derived here. This book adds one
register detail `001` lacks: (이)랑 is specifically associated with children's and young females'
speech. Mechanically, all three particles attach directly to the first noun with no space, while a
space precedes the second noun (빵하고 우유, not 빵 하고 우유) (p.263).

### Unit 9 grammar points

#### 아니에요 'am/are/is not' and the full copula register paradigm
Building on the affirmative copula +이에요/예요 ("A is B," taught in Unit 1), this section gives
the negative counterpart 아니에요 and lays out a fuller 5-way register paradigm than a simple
affirmative/negative contrast: **casual** (noun+(이)야 / noun 아니야), **polite** (noun+이에요/예요
/ noun 아니에요), **honorific** (noun+(이)세요 / noun 아니세요), **polite-formal** (noun+입니다 /
noun 아닙니다), and **honorific-formal** (noun+(이)십니다 / noun 아니십니다). A key asymmetry: the
affirmative ending attaches directly to the noun, while 아니에요 and its register variants are
written as a *separate word* after the noun (이거 제 책 아니에요), not a suffix (p.270–272).

#### 얼마 'how much?' vs. 몇 'how many/what'
얼마 and 몇 both ask about numerical quantities but differ structurally: 몇 precedes a counted
noun (몇 시예요?, 몇 살이에요?, 몇 층이에요?) — "what number" for a specified kind of thing. 얼마,
by contrast, is never followed by a noun — it combines directly with a copula (이거 얼마예요?) and
is used specifically (and essentially exclusively) to ask a price. The practical rule given:
always use 얼마 for prices, 몇 for other quantities (p.273).

#### Sino-Korean counter-noun inventory for abstract numbers (brief — base system already documented)
The base Sino-Korean numeral table (1–20, tens, hundreds, thousands, 만/십만/백만/천만) is already
fully captured in `001_basic_korean_part1.md`; not re-derived here. This book's own contribution
is the explicit list of counter nouns Sino-Korean numbers attach to for "abstract" quantities: 년
(year), 월 (month name), 일 (date), 주 (week), 분 (minute), 원 (currency), 층 (floor), 동
(building number), 호 (room number), and the compound 국+번 telephone-number convention. A
genuinely practical note: since numbers are normally written as digits rather than spelled out, a
reader must know from context whether a written numeral should be read in the native or
Sino-Korean series — picking wrong "sounds like a foreigner" (p.274–276).

#### Telling the time: minutes
Hour and minute use *different* numeral systems within the same time expression: the hour uses
pure Korean numbers and the minutes use Sino-Korean numbers + 분. 세 시 십오 분 "3:15" = [pure-Korean
3] + 시 + [Sino-Korean 15] + 분. A clean, concrete illustration of Korean's two-numeral-system
split in a single everyday expression (p.286).

#### Rate and ratio particle +에 'per'
+에 attaches to the unit of measurement to express a rate ("$10 per kilo"), with the unit-amount
preceding the rate-amount in Korean (opposite ordering from the English calque): 1 킬로에 10 달러
"10 dollars per kilo." The ratio needn't be literally "per one unit" — 세 개에 만 원이에요 "10,000
won for three" is also valid (p.292).

#### Delimiter particle +만 'only' (brief — systematic treatment already documented, new pragmatic use kept)
`025_elementary_korean_part2.md` §14.1 already documents the 만/밖에 affirmative/negative
delimiter contrast systematically. Not re-derived here. This book's own distinct contribution is a
**pragmatic bargaining-register use**: in a haggling exchange, a shopkeeper offering a reduced
price says 이천팔백 원만 내세요 ("just pay 2,800 won"), and a customer counter-offering uses the
same construction. +만 here functions as a politeness-softened discount-request/counter-offer
marker in a commercial-negotiation speech event. A second example shows +만 stacking after another
particle (토요일에만 "only on Saturdays"), and a footnote on 토요일날 notes that the semantically
redundant 날 ("day") is commonly added colloquially to convey "in the daytime" specifically, since
토요일 alone lacks that daytime connotation (p.293–294).

#### Demonstrative pronouns: 이, 그, 저, 어느
A 3-way spatial-deixis system plus an interrogative: 이 "this" (close to speaker), 그 "that" (not
close to speaker but close to listener), 저 "that over there" (distant from both), and 어느
"which." The paradigm extends systematically across noun-phrase, pro-form, and locative slots: 이
책/이거/여기, 그 책/그거/거기, 저 책/저거/저기, 어느 책/어느 거/어디. A clean three-way deictic
paradigm (not the simpler two-way this/that split English makes) with a fully regular pro-form/
locative extension — kept as a full table since not confirmed duplicated elsewhere in `established/`
(p.301).

### Unit 10 grammar points

#### Word contractions (general pattern) — new, slang-relevant
Korean productively contracts multi-word proper names, especially university and campus-library
names, into a single syllable drawn from each half: 고려 대학교 → 고대, 연세 대학교 → 연대, 서울
대학교 → 서울대, 중앙 도서관 → 중도, 과학 도서관 → 과도. The source explicitly frames this as
directly parallel to English contraction habits (comparing to "Vic Uni," "UQ") — institutional-
name clipping as a cross-linguistically common slang-formation pattern, and a genuine, reusable
word-formation mechanism for later mechanics analysis (p.328).

#### Honorific subject and topic particles: +이/가 → +께서, +은/는 → +께서는 (brief — already documented)
`025_elementary_korean_part2.md` §13.2 already documents the honorific particle system (께,
께서(는)) in more systematic detail. Not re-derived here. Kept briefly: when the referent of the
subject/topic is someone senior deserving respect, +이/가 is replaced by +께서 and +은/는 by
+께서는 — e.g. 할아버지께서는 신문 읽으세요 (p.338).

#### Possessive pronouns and the collectivist 우리 convention
The possessive system differs by register: casual 내/네/우리, humble 제/저희, and honorific forms
exist only for 3rd person (그분/어느 분). Beyond this paradigm, the source makes an explicit
**sociolinguistic claim**: Koreans routinely use 우리 ("our") where English would use a singular
possessive — 우리 부모님, 우리 학교, even 우리 집 사람 (lit. "our house person," a man referring to
his own wife) — reflecting the cultural salience of group-belonging over individual possession.
The singular 내/제 is reserved for things genuinely personal and not shared with a group (except,
notably, one's spouse, which still takes 우리). A real register/pragmatics finding about how
possession is grammatically encoded along an individual-vs-group axis, not just a pronoun table
(p.340–342).

#### '...이/가 어떻게 되세요?' ('Would you mind telling me...?') — register-switching finding
A politeness-cushioning formula for asking personal questions (name, age, birthday, phone number,
occupation, etc.) — structurally "[topic]+이/가 어떻게 되세요?", literally "how does [topic] come
to be?" rather than a direct question-word question. Framed explicitly as a face-saving strategy:
Koreans routinely ask what Westerners would consider quite personal/intrusive questions because
this information is socially necessary to select the correct register/hierarchy level.
**Register-switching note:** the same information is requested far more *directly* (without the
softening formula) when the addressee is a junior or a child — e.g. 여동생 생일 언제예요? instead
of 생일이 어떻게 되세요?. A genuine politeness-register-switching data point keyed to the
addressee's relative social status (p.344–345).

#### Sending a text message (HIGH PRIORITY — flagship finding of this book)
Explicit digital-register content: the source states Koreans, like English speakers, use
shortcuts and emoticons when texting to minimize character count and to compensate for the loss
of body language. A cited claim: a study found that recipients perceived texts with **no**
emoticons as angry — i.e. the *absence* of an emoticon is itself meaningful in this register, not
merely a stylistic option. Two distinct sub-mechanisms:

**(a) Syllable-clipping lexical contractions** — multi-syllable standard words clipped to a
single syllable for texting: 셤 < 시험 ("exam"), 샘 < 선생님 ("teacher"), 멜 < 메일 ("mail"), 먄 <
미안 ("sorry"), 겜 < 게임 ("game"), 토욜 < 토요일 ("Saturday"). This is structurally the *same*
clipped-compound mechanism as the university/library contractions above and the 디카 < 디지탈
카메라 clip in the Consumer Items vocabulary — syllable-clipping is a single productive
word-formation mechanism recurring across at least three registers in this book (institutional-
name contraction, loanword clipping, and digital/texting shorthand), worth flagging as a
cross-register pattern for mechanics analysis.

**(b) Jamo-repetition and emoticon systems** — consonant-jamo repetition standing in for a
written laugh: ㅋㅋㅋ (< 크크크, onomatopoeic "ha ha ha") and ㅎㅎㅎ (< 흐흐흐, a softer laugh). Plus
a small ASCII/Hangul-adjacent emoticon set: (^o^) "laughing/excited," ^^/^-^/^__^/^ㅡ^ "happy/
smiling" (four visually distinct variants differing only in how the nose/mouth segment is drawn),
ㅠ_ㅠ "crying" (built from the Hangul vowel jamo ㅠ, whose shape resembles streaming tears), and
>_</>.< "angry."

This section is a clean, sourced example of an informal/abbreviated digital-register convention
with (i) a productive clipping mechanism shared with other registers in the same book, (ii) a
native-script-exploiting emoticon device (ㅠ_ㅠ using the shape of a Hangul vowel jamo iconically,
not just repurposing Latin/ASCII punctuation the way Western emoticons do), and (iii) an explicit
pragmatic claim about what *absence* of a marker communicates — all directly reusable for a
conlang slang-synthesis mechanics-analysis pass on digital/informal register formation (p.350).

### Appendices grammar points

#### Explicit table-convention markers (front matter)
The book prefaces all its conjugation-table appendices with four explicit notation conventions,
used consistently through every table in Appendices 1–16: **`*`** marks a cell that is an
*exception* to the regular conjugation rule (e.g. 드세요 for "eat," not derived regularly from
먹다). **`x`** marks a cell grammatically inapplicable for that word — predominantly adjectives
whose referent cannot be human (and therefore cannot take an honorific subject-marking form), e.g.
싸다 "cheap" has no honorific 싸십니다. **`q`** marks a form usable only in questions, not
statements (맛있으세요? is grammatical as a question but not as a parallel honorific statement,
since the adjective's subject — food — isn't human). Where a heading doesn't separately list a
question vs. statement form, one form covers both (pp.374–375).

#### Special Conjugation Rules of Verb & Adjective (the single most load-bearing content in this chunk)
The book's own systematic statement of every stem-final sound-change class used across the
appendix tables:
- **ㅅ rule:** stem-final ㅅ deletes before a vowel-initial ending (짓→지어요).
- **ㄷ rule:** stem-final ㄷ deletes (realized as ㄹ before the vowel) before a vowel-initial ending
  (듣→들어요).
- **ㄹ rule:** stem-final ㄹ deletes before an ending beginning with ㄴ, ㄹ, ㅂ, 오, or 시 (길→깁니다,
  기니까).
- **ㅂ rule:** stem-final ㅂ changes to 오 before +아 and 우 before +어 (surfacing as 와/워 after
  vowel contraction) (돕→도와요; 아름답→아름다워요).
- **으 rule:** stem-final ㅡ deletes before a vowel-initial ending (크→커요; 아프→아파요).
- **르 rule:** when forming the +아/어 form, 르 deletes and +ㄹ라/ㄹ러 is added (자르→잘라요;
  부르→불러요) — distinguished from the plain 으 rule by the doubled-ㄹ outcome.
- **여 rule:** for 하-final stems, formal writing may use 하여 in place of the ordinary 해
  contraction.
- **ㅎ rule (adjectives only):** stem-final ㅎ deletes before an ending beginning with ㄴ or ㅁ
  (파랗→파라면).
- **Four rarer rules**, explicitly flagged by the book as uncommon: **거라** (가다 takes 가거라
  rather than regular 가라); **너라** (오다 takes 오너라 rather than 와라); **러** (이르다/푸르다 etc.
  take +러(서) instead of +어(서): 이르러서, 푸르러서); **우** (for stems like 푸다 "scoop," the ㅜ
  itself deletes: 퍼서).

Separately, the front matter notes two systematic exceptions to how certain adjectives are
tabulated: (1) 흥미있다 "interesting" and 피곤하다 "tired" (and by extension other perception-
adjectives) cannot take the honorific statement form for third-person reference, since perception
can't be predicated of a third party that way; the +아/어 하세요 periphrastic form is the honorific
alternative. (2) 싫다 "hate/dislike" is grammatically an adjective in Korean, not a verb, despite
its English gloss (pp.376–377).

#### The copular 'be' paradigm (Appendix 1)
A full tense × formality cross-tabulation of 이다/아니다 ("be"/"be not") — notable mainly for how
irregular and suppletive-feeling the paradigm is compared to a regular verb/adjective: casual
question forms split into `+니?`/`+냐?` after a consonant-final noun but `+이니?`/`+이냐?` after a
vowel-final one (이 surfaces only after consonants); the future tense has no casual- or
casual-question form at all, only polite/honorific; and the `+ㄴ데(요)` suppositional-future form
일 건데(요) patterns with the regular verb future-tense suppositional ending documented elsewhere,
confirming 이다's future morphology isn't fully exceptional even though its present/past forms are
(pp.378–379).

#### Systematic verb and adjective paradigm tables (Appendices 2–16)
A single fixed set of ~40 verb stems (and ~55 adjective stems) is run through, in order: present
tense, past tense, future tense (+(으)ㄹ 거야/거예요/실 거예요), the proposal/intention endings
+(으)ㄹ까(요)?/+(으)ㄹ래(요) (extending to +(으)실래(요)?/+(으)시겠습니까?/+(으)ㄹ게(요)/+겠습니다),
the propositive +자 and connective +고, the contrastive/background-setting +는데(요) (and its
past/future counterparts), the reason-giving +거든(요) (and its past/future counterparts), and
casual-register +아/어/+았/었어/+(으)ㄹ 거야. The value of this run is purely systematic: every
irregular stem (짓, 듣, 걷, 살, 쓰, 고르, 부르, plus the suppletive-honorific trio 먹다/마시다→드시다,
자다→주무시다, 말하다→말씀하시다) behaves identically across all nine verb endings with zero
exceptions beyond what the front-matter rules already predict — a genuine confirmation, not just
an assertion, that the irregularity classes are stable across the whole verbal paradigm rather
than ending-specific quirks. The dominant irregular adjective class by a wide margin is
ㅂ-irregular (roughly a third of the full adjective list), followed by ㅡ-irregular and
르-irregular; the ㄹ-irregular class and the five fused-었/았 adjectives (잘생기+었, 못생기+었, 늙+었,
낡+았, 마르+았) are smaller but consistent. The `x`-marked honorific gap for non-human-subject
adjectives recurs throughout every tense table, confirming it is a structural property of the
adjective class, not an isolated omission in any single table (pp.380–445).

#### Appendix 17: Particles and Suffixes
A compact, book-wide reference inventory of every particle taught. Organizationally notable: the
book groups particles by *function* (topic/subject/object/direction/source/destination/location/
time/rate-ratio/instrument/possessive/delimiter/emphasis/plural/conjunction/comparison/reason)
rather than by form, and explicitly pairs each plain particle with its honorific counterpart where
one exists (+은/는 ↔ +께서는; +이/가 ↔ +께서; +에게/한테 ↔ +께) — a systematic honorific-particle
mapping not laid out this explicitly in any unit-level chapter checked (pp.446–447).

#### Appendix 18: Korean Editing Symbols (brief)
A short practical reference table of proofreading/editing marks used in the book's own
handwriting exercises (add-space, delete-space, delete, insert, replace, reorder, "cannot
understand" symbols). Metalinguistic/pedagogical rather than a Korean-language grammar point. The
remainder of Appendix 18 (handwriting-practice blocks and a keyboard-layout diagram) was inspected
directly and confirmed to contain no extractable linguistic content (p.448).

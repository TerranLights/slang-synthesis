# Korean — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: *Collins Korean Phrasebook* (Collins Gem, 2007)

**Source:** *Collins Korean Phrasebook: The Right Word in Your Pocket* (Collins Gem imprint,
HarperCollins Publishers, Glasgow, first published 2007, consultant Jaehoon Yeon of SOAS,
typeset by Davidson Pre-Press Glasgow, printed in Malaysia by Imago; ISBN 978-0-00-724680-9).
129 PDF pages, each a two-printed-page spread (confirmed by direct inspection: PDF page *N* shows
printed pages `2N-4`/`2N-3`), covering printed pp.1–251 plus unnumbered publisher back-matter ads.
Whole book surveyed for structure; extraction itself is scoped per the non-redundant-supplement
decision below.

**Text layer / extraction method:** zero text layer and zero embedded fonts (`pdffonts` returns an
empty table; `pdfimages -list` shows every page is a single full-page JPEG raster, ~1835×1373px at
300ppi). This is a pure page-image scan, not a font-substitution cipher of any kind — there is
nothing for a `+36266`-style offset decode to act on, so vision-reading was the only option and was
used throughout. No handwritten marginalia was found anywhere in the sampled pages — every page
read is clean typeset print matching the surrounding layout; the only non-printed mark on the whole
scan is a printed library call-number sticker on the back cover ("CD K 495.782 C7123"), which is
itself typeset/printed (not handwritten) and was excluded as non-source-content regardless. Per the
vision-reading guard, everything extracted below is genuine printed source content.

**Coverage note — non-redundant-supplement scoping, not exhaustive coverage of the book.** This is
Collins Gem phrasebook #055 in a project with 50+ already-extracted Korean reference books,
including 18+ beginner/intermediate course books that exhaustively cover greetings, numbers,
days/months/time, basic SOV/postposition/honorific grammar, and standard travel-topic phrase sets
(hotel booking, shopping, transport, health, weather, leisure), plus a sampled scholarly dictionary
(`established/026`–`029`, Martin/Lee/Chang) and multiple dedicated slang/phrasebook sources
(`established/030` Dirty Korean, `established/032` Making Out in Korean). Against that backdrop,
most of this book's ~70 thematically-arranged everyday-conversation topics (pp.15–128: hello/
goodbye, key phrases, work, weather, asking the way, bus/train/taxi/car hire, hotel booking,
shopping, post office/photos, leisure/entertainment/sport, health/pharmacy/doctor/dentist,
communications, money/laundry/complaints) are comprehensively redundant survival-tourist phrase
sets and were **skipped wholesale** rather than re-extracted. Likewise the short Grammar section
(pp.147–148: SOV word order with postpositions instead of prepositions, the i/ga subject marker and
ul/rul object marker, the two parallel Sino-Korean/native-Korean number systems, the dictionary
citation form of verbs, and a note on homonyms) was **skipped as fully redundant** — every one of
these points is already covered in much greater depth across the existing course-book corpus. The
90-page English-Korean dictionary (pp.162–251, A–Z, thousands of generic tourist-vocabulary
headwords, confirmed by direct inspection to be the book's only dictionary direction — there is no
separate Korean-English section despite the Contents page layout implying one) was also **skipped**
as redundant with both the sampled Yale dictionary (`026`–`029`) and the cumulative vocabulary
already captured across 50+ course books; spot-checking several spreads (pp.196–197, 216–217,
236–237, 246–251) found only ordinary tourist-dictionary headwords (e.g. "excellent," "mirror,"
"smoking," "wage," "zoo") with no slang, idiom, or register-marked content that would justify the
cost of transcribing an alphabetical general word list a fourth or fifth time over for this project.

**What was kept, and why:** four sections/sub-sections of genuinely non-redundant, distinct-register
content, not covered (or not covered in this form) by any prior Korean extraction:
1. **Signs and notices** (two separate lists: pp.20–22 "General," and the domain-organized
   pp.150–161 reference glossary) — this is **written/public-notice register**, not the spoken
   conversational register that dominates every course book extracted so far for Korean.
2. **Menu reader** (pp.140–146) — ~140 specific Korean dish/food-item names with Hangul,
   romanization, and English gloss; a culinary-register vocabulary domain not covered in this
   breadth by any prior Korean source.
3. A handful of **eatery/venue terms** from the Eating Out section's prose (pp.130–131).
4. **Emergency/distress exclamations** (pp.100–105) — kept narrowly, for one genuine
   sociolinguistic finding: a register-switch pattern under acute urgency (see Grammar points).

The "Top ten tips" cultural-etiquette section (pp.12–14) is prose, not vocabulary, and is captured
as paraphrased cultural-pragmatic notes under Grammar points rather than vocabulary-table rows.

**Vision Reading Confidence:** all pages read were crisp, high-resolution (300ppi) scans with clear
typeset print and no scan artifacts, binding-gutter shadows, or faded ink encountered anywhere in
the sampled range — so no entry below needed `low_confidence`. Entries whose exact Hangul/
romanization/gloss triple was independently confirmed by appearing a second time in a different
list (e.g. a term occurring in both the p.20–22 General signs list and the p.150–161 reference
glossary) are marked `verified`; all other entries, read once with full confidence but without an
independent second occurrence to cross-check against, are marked `plausible_unverified`.

## Grammar points

### Cultural-pragmatic notes ("Top ten tips," pp.12–14)

The book's own top-ten etiquette list, paraphrased (not a vocabulary table — prose cultural
guidance, no accompanying Korean-language forms given in the source for most items): remove shoes
indoors; never leave chopsticks standing upright in a rice bowl (left on the table when not in
use); the number 4 carries a death association (phonetically identical to the Sino-Korean word for
"death," 사), to the point that some buildings skip a labeled 4th floor; when drinking in company,
pour for others and let them pour for you rather than self-pouring; address people by professional
title rather than given name until explicitly invited to use it; family name precedes given name;
Koreans generally avoid addressing someone directly by name at all, preferring title/position/
trade; tipping is not customary (service charge is already in the bill); the oldest person present
begins and ends the meal first, and others should not leave the table before them; and items should
be handed to a Korean (especially an elder) with both hands. **Direct relevance to this project:**
several of these (avoiding direct-address-by-name, title-based address, the eldest-defers-first
dining norm, two-handed giving) are the same social-deference logic already documented as the
engine of Korean's honorific system in `established/031` (Korean Honorifics and Politeness) — this
source adds no new honorific *mechanism*, but does supply concrete, concise real-world etiquette
correlates for that system worth cross-referencing in later mechanics-analysis work. p.12–14.

### Register switch under acute urgency: bare panmal for single-word alarm shouts

Across the Emergency/Problems/Complaints vocabulary table above, every multi-word distress phrase
in this phrasebook keeps standard polite `-요`/`-세요` marking even under maximal urgency/hostility
("Go away!" 저리 가세요!, "Leave me alone!" 혼자 있게 해 주세요!, "Can you help me?" 도와 주시겠어요?)
— **except** the single bare interjection "Fire!," which the source gives as **불이야!** (poo-ri-ya),
using the intimate/panmal copula ending `-야` rather than the polite `-예요`/`-이에요` that the rest
of the chapter's phrasing would predict. "Help!" (도와주세요!) stays polite even as a one-word shout,
so the pattern isn't simply "shorter utterances drop politeness" — it's specific to 불이야, which
also happens to be the single most time-critical, pure-alarm-word case in the set (where syllable
count under actual panic plausibly matters more than anywhere else in the list). This phrasebook
doesn't comment on the contrast itself (it's simply how the two words are printed), so treat this as
a single textual data point, not a confirmed grammatical rule — but it's a genuinely interesting
candidate mechanism for the synthesis-mechanics phase: **register can drop to bare panmal
specifically for maximally time-critical one-word alarm calls, even in a frame (public safety
address to a stranger) that would otherwise demand the highest politeness.** Worth checking against
a native-speaker query (see `00_Native_Speaker_Query_Folders` convention) and against any corpus
evidence of real 불이야 usage before treating this as more than a single phrasebook's printed choice.
p.102–104.

## Grammar points

### 1. Discourse as a structural unit above the sentence (§7.1)

Chang formalizes discourse (D) as a sequence of discourse segments (DS), each a sequence of utterances (U), where an utterance = sentence (S) + context (C): U = S + C — a bare sentence is "an utterance minus context." Spontaneous speech is characteristically **juxtaposed without connectives**; the semantic relation between juxtaposed sentences (causal vs. concessive) is often genuinely ambiguous and resolved only by situational context (*Chwup.ta. Mwun-ul yel-ela* "It's cold. Open the door." supports either reading, with no syntactic disambiguator). Discourse also licenses **postposed/dangling utterances** — a subordinate clause, or even a whole detached clause, can trail its main clause as an afterthought fragment.

### 2. Speech-act mapping and the directive-strength scale (§7.2, §7.2.1)

Korean's four basic sentence types map onto unmarked vs. marked/indirect speech-act readings: declaratives default to assertion but can indirectly request; kyess-marked declaratives default to prediction but can promise; interrogatives default to questioning but very commonly indirectly request; propositives default to proposing but can request. Tag-, rhetorical-, and echo-questions sit in a separate category because their marked/indirect use essentially *is* the normal use. Requests are commonly phrased as ability/willingness interrogatives (`V-l.swu iss.nun.ya?`, `V-e cwu-l.swu.iss.nun.ya?`, `V-e cwu-keyss.nun.ya?`) rather than bare imperatives, and function as a cover term for the broader **directive** speech-act category, strength-ordered: order > direct > request > beseech > beg.

### 3. Tag questions: three formally distinct types (§7.2.2)

**Type 1** `-ci anh-a?` is syntactically identical to the long-form negative question except the tag is invariably nonpast regardless of body tense, and both body and tag are negative (unlike English's opposite-polarity tags — closer to French *n'est-ce-pas?*); when both happen to be nonpast, type-1 tags become surface-identical to ordinary long-form negative questions, leading Chang to treat the latter as itself a kind of tag question. **Type 2** `an-kulay?` is built on the anaphoric proform *kule.hata* and, unlike type 1, is independently tensable in both body and tag. **Type 3** `kule.h-ci?` is the positively-phrased variant, lacking type 2's negation.

### 4. Rhetorical utterances: questions and conditionals (§7.2.3)

Rhetorical yes-no questions invert polarity in their implied assertion (positive → strong negative implicature, and vice versa); the same pattern extends to frozen rhetorical wh-questions (*Nwuka al-a?* "Who knows?" → "Nobody knows"). A separate **rhetorical conditional**: a *myen*-clause whose main-clause proposition is deliberately absurd (e.g. "if he passes, the sun will rise in the west") functions as a strong negative assertion by logical inference — Chang notes this pattern's logic generalizes cross-linguistically.

### 5. Echo utterances (§7.2.4)

Echo utterances repeat all/part of a preceding utterance, marked by sentence-final quotative -ko plus intonation: rising/high-rising = echo question, falling = echo statement. A wh-word echo question can request clarification of one specific constituent. A **high-rising** echo question carries an additional pragmatic layer of surprise/disbelief, functioning like a rhetorical question's polarity-opposite assertion — Chang cites Jespersen's (1924) "raised to the second power" characterization.

### 6. Reported speech and its two productive contraction subtypes (§7.2.5)

Beyond the general periphrastic quotative (restricted to plain-level sentence enders), two widespread **contracted** subtypes exist: bare quotative -ko (resembling an echo utterance), and the further-contracted portmanteau `reported-ending + ko + hata` → -tay/-lay/-nyay/-cay (intimate), -tanta-style (plain), -tapnita-style (formal). The reporting verb's own tense is independently meaningful (present *hanta* = reporting concurrent with speech time; past *hayssta* = reporting itself in the past). A retrospective layer (-tatela/-tatenya) adds "I recall/do you recall that it was said..." A genuine structural ambiguity: the unexpressed "reporter" can be read as generic "they" **or** as coreferential with the reported clause's own subject, whenever that clause is declarative — this ambiguity disappears for imperative/propositive reported clauses.

### 7. Honorification: three subsystems and the formal power/solidarity model (§7.3, new framing only)

Korean honorification splits into three grammatically distinct subsystems: speech levels (speaker-hearer), subject honorification (speaker-to-subject-referent, via -si- and lexical substitution), and object honorification (speaker-to-object-referent, via self-lowering lexical items) — unified by an explicit **self-lowering** principle. **New formal model (not in 031):** the honorific level is used unilaterally low-to-high along the power/status axis, and bilaterally between parties whose relationship isn't yet solid (A ←x→ B); the non-honorific level is used unilaterally high-to-low, and bilaterally once solidarity is tightly established (A ←0→ B) — illustrated with a worked six-dyad "Have you eaten?" paradigm. A previously-uncaptured note: the **traditional husband-wife speech-level pairing was unilateral** (husband non-honorific, wife honorific) and is eroding toward mutual plain or mutual honorific in modern couples.

### 8. Subject and object honorification: feature-spreading and register-mixing constraints (§7.3.2–7.3.3, new framing only)

Subject honorification is characterized formally as the feature [HON] "spreading... over the whole sentence," forcing lexical substitutions and obligatory -si- insertion — with a general "longer form = more polite" correlation, and a self-lowering block on first-person honorific marking (a grandfather cannot use -si- saying "I am eating" to his grandchild). Object honorification is realized through self-lowering lexical substitution when the object outranks the speaker; a worked six-dyad scenario shows identical propositional content surfacing with systematically different honorific choices depending on which two of three participants are speaker/addressee/referent — and crucially, **mixing honorific and non-honorific elements within one sentence produces inappropriate (#) or severely inappropriate (##) utterances**, even when each individual choice is acceptable in isolation: a sentence-wide register-agreement constraint.

### 9. Topic and focus as complementary discourse functions (§7.4)

Topic (given/presupposed, typically -(n)un-marked, sentence-initial) and focus (new information, bearing intonation or wh-word/cleft-predicate status) track given/known vs. new/unknown information. Chang borrows the Prague School's **topic-focus articulation (TFA)**, treating it as suprasegmental structure superimposed on the utterance string: with multiple elements, topics form an ordered **list**, foci form an unordered **set**. A worked dialogue shows focus/topic assignment shifting turn by turn, directly connecting TFA to ellipsis.

### 10. Topic persistence, multiple topics, and the subject/topic-marking interaction (§7.4)

Once introduced, a topic tends to be suppressed (zero) or pronominalized rather than repeated as a full NP. With multiple topic-marked phrases, the leftmost is the unmarked main topic; additional -nun-marked phrases are contrastive topics that can themselves function as focus depending on context. The famous double-subject sentence "The elephant has a long nose" is run through **eight** surface TOP/SM × ordering variants, each paired with the specific preceding question it naturally answers — demonstrating that **sentences in isolation cannot be reliably assigned a TFA reading**; the same surface string gets different topic/focus assignments purely as a function of the question it answers.

### 11. Discourse reference: deictic, anaphoric, and cataphoric determiner use (§7.5)

The same determiner series serves double/triple duty with different unmarked members per system: **deictic** (*i*-/*ku*-/*ce*- relative to Speaker/Hearer/distal, *enu*-/*etten*- indefinite); **anaphoric** (*ku*- unmarked backward-referring, *i*- for emphatic/narrower reference); **cataphoric** (*i*- forward-referring to an unmentioned "postcedent"). A parallel *i*-series of *kule.ha*-based conjunctive particles (vs. the ordinary *ku*-series *kuliko*/*kulena*/etc.) imparts a sense of **imminence** rather than plain discourse-continuity.

### 12. Anaphoric reference: zero anaphora as the Korean-unmarked case (§7.5.1)

**Zero anaphora is the unmarked discourse reference strategy in Korean, in direct contrast to English, where pronominal anaphora is unmarked** — a typological point with clear relevance to modeling elliptical/pro-drop discourse patterns for any Korean-derived register or conlang slang layer downstream. A three-way minimal comparison shows the reflexive *caki* specifically signals the **speaker's empathy** with the referent, while zero and pronominal realizations are comparatively empathy-neutral.

### 13. Ellipsis: recoverability, subject-omissibility limit, and typological gapping-direction reversal (§7.5.2)

Ellipsis extends zero anaphora to suppress context-recoverable information generally; correct resolution requires interpreting the suppressed referent, not mechanically supplying a pronoun. **The subject cannot always be omitted**: once topic- or focus-marked via a particle, a subject cannot be dropped in later clauses the way an out-of-context sentence might suggest. **Gapping is typologically direction-reversed relative to English**: in a Korean SOV coordinate sentence, an identical predicate is gapped from **all but the last** conjunct — the mirror image of SVO English, which gaps from all but the **first** conjunct. Beyond gapping, cross-sentence/speaker predicate coreference is often reduced to the **pro-predicate** *kule.hata* "be/do so" rather than full repetition or zero.

### 14. Discourse analysis: worked dialogue (§7.6.1)

Applying the preceding machinery to a 6-utterance teacher-student dialogue across two speech levels: a wh-question presupposes someone satisfies the predicate, and the natural reply fills only the focus while suppressing presupposed content; the polite particle *-yo* can attach directly onto an object-marked pronoun in a minimal elliptical reply. **Ellipsis can be genuinely ambiguous, and resolving it requires inferring speaker intent, not mechanical rule application** — one elliptical reply is shown structurally ambiguous between two readings, each with independent discourse-coherence support, with true resolution depending on what's inferable "with the least effort" to the interlocutor.

### 15. Discourse analysis: literary essay, formal analytical apparatus (§7.6.2)

A five-sentence literary passage (*Pom* "Spring," Pi Chon-Deuk 1989) is analyzed via a formal seven-field schema (DT/REF/SA/SL/SS-SP/ST/TS). Findings: all five sentences are plain-level declarative assertives, consistent with essayistic register; **referent tracking uses numbered zero-pronoun tags resolved to generic/impersonal antecedents** (not just named referents) — the zero-anaphora system operating on generic referents recoverable only from genre knowledge; and **sentence-level (not just NP-level) anaphora** — one sentence's subject is analyzed as the entire prior sentence's propositional content, a distinct reference type from pronoun/reflexive/zero-NP anaphora.

### 16. The `kes`-contraction family (colloquial fast-speech reduction) [Appendix]

A systematic colloquial contraction pattern flagged by the Appendix texts' own "NB:" footnotes: the bound noun/nominalizer *kes* regularly elides its vowel and fuses with a following case particle or copula in casual registers (*ikes.i* → *ike/ikey*, *ikes.ul* → *ikel*, *ikes.un* → *iken*, *ikes.i.yo* → *ike.yo*, *kes.i.pnita* → *ke.pnita*, *ku.kes.i* → *ku.key*), extending to the interrogative pronoun (*mwues.ul* → *mwel*), a conditional connector (*kule.myen* → *kulem*), and a quotative-plus-verb sequence (*moca.la.ko.hay.yo* → *moca.lay.yo*). No prior established Korean file catalogs this as a unified productive pattern — a genuine, reusable colloquial-contraction mechanism directly relevant to slang-synthesis morphophonology.

### 17. A seventh, archaic speech level and minor sentence-type classification [Notes 91, 96]

Note 96: beyond the six ordinary speech levels (already documented in `031`), there is an "extremely formal and antiquated level" marked by imperative ending *-opsose* (vs. formal *-psio*), "widespread in prayers," exemplified in the Appendix's Lord's Prayer translation. The note also classifies exclamatory/"apperceptive" enders (*kwun*, *kwun.yo*, *kwuman*) and promissory *(u)ma* as **minor sentence types** outside the six-level system. Note 91: the Discourse chapter's speech-act analysis is grounded in a seven-way typology (S.-J. Chang 1987) — assertives, rogatives, directives, exercitives, commissives, expressives, reportives.

### 18. Historical/comparative linguistics supplements [Notes 1, 2, 4, 5, 8]

**Note 4** gives a more specific Middle-Korean historical-phonology inventory than Part 1's general summary: three lost fricatives (/β/, /z/, /h'/) and one lost vowel (/ʌ/, "arae-a"); four added vowels (/e/, /æ/, /ü/, /ö/). **Note 2**: the Altaic affiliation (Ramstedt, Poppe, Martin, Lee) has a rival "southern theory" (Ohno 1970) linking Korean/Japanese to Malayo-Polynesian via shared honorification and pitch-accent features absent elsewhere in Altaic. **Note 5** gives an explicit regional dialect inventory: South Korea — Seoul (standard), Kangwen, Chwungcheng, Kyengsang, Cenla, Ceycwu; North Korea — Phyengan, Hamkyeng, Hwanghay. **Note 1**: 1993 speaker demographics — 69.7 million total (South 43M, North 22M, overseas 4.7M: China 1.92M, USA 1.42M, Japan 0.68M, Russia 0.46M). **Note 8**: North Korea banned Hanca in 1949, partially restored 1953, fixed at 1,000 taught characters (vs. South's eventual 1,800) — a genuine policy-divergence datum supplementing Part 1's South-Korea-only finding.

### 19. Miscellaneous morphosyntactic and lexical findings [Notes 20, 30, 33–39, 52, 61, 66, 69, 80, 97, 99, 103, 51/65]

**VN-*hata* object marking** (Note 66): the object marker on a verbal-noun-plus-*hata* construction may surface optionally, but is droppable again once the clause is negated (*yenkwu-lul hanta* → *yenkwu-lul an hanta* → *yenkwu an hanta*). **Dictionary stock ratios** (Note 20): two 1992 dictionaries put NK words at ~32–33% of entries, SK at ~49–52%, EK loanwords ~7%, mixed ~12%. **Existential-over-possessive preference** (Note 61): Korean prefers *Na-eykey cip-i issta* ("there is a house at me") over a transitive "have"-verb construction to express ordinary possession. **Pronoun/speech-level agreement asymmetry** (Note 99): a humble pronoun + plain-level verb mismatch is judged worse than a plain pronoun + deferential-level mismatch — compared to German/French T/V-agreement violations. **Floating politeness particle *yo*** (Note 103): attaches not only clause-finally but after essentially any major constituent, a flexible register-renegotiation mechanism. **Imperative-plus-light-verb contractions** (Notes 51, 65): *cengcikhay* (< *cengcik.hi* + *hay*, since Korean imperatives reject nonstative adjectives directly) and *an.hata* (< *ani* + *hata*). **Five "wear" verbs by garment type** (Note 80): *ipta*/*sinta*/*ssuta*/*kkita*/*mayta*, splitting differently from Japanese's own five-way split. **Possessive-pronoun contractions** (Note 52): *na+uy→nay*, *ne+uy→ney*, *ce+uy→cey*, but *wuli+uy→wuli* (zero-realized). **SK derivational prefix pair** (Note 69): *phi-* (≈ -ee) / *ka-* (≈ agentive -er/-or). **Deictic-epithet asymmetry** (Note 33): *ce* is never used anaphorically, unlike *i*/*ku*. **-tul associative plural on a name** (Note 30): *Mia-tul* "Mia and her group," distinct from English *-s*. **Pejorative/honorific incompatibility** (Notes 35/36): bound noun *pwun* cannot combine with pejorative particles *yo*/*ko*/*co*.

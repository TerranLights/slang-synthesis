## Grammar points

### 1. The six-way speech-style system and its "separation/connection" reframing

Brown rejects the traditional ±DEFERENCE/±FORMALITY two-dimensional model (Suh 1984; Sung 1985) as
empirically wrong — "formal" styles are not restricted to formal settings, and an "informal" style
used to an intimate superior reads as rude, not polite. Instead he models all six styles as indexing
degrees of **separation** (distance, non-intimacy) vs. **connection** (closeness), following Chang
Suk-jin (1996) in grouping {P}/{Y} as "honorific"/contaymal and {E}/{T} as "non-honorific"/panmal, with
{S}/{N} as a nearly-extinct middle pair. A key structural fact: only {E} and {Y} combine with epistemic
modal pre-final endings (-ci-, -canha-, -ney-) and the volitional -lkey — this morphological asymmetry,
not an inherent "formality," is likely what caused grammarians to label them "informal" in the first
place. {P} pairs with factual new-information assertions; {Y} pairs with shared/common-knowledge or
conjecture — visible in TV presenter talk, where a presenter switches {P}→{Y} specifically when
repeating already-known information (p.29-30).

### 2. Subject honorifics: beyond "agreement"

Brown argues the traditional "honorific agreement" analysis of -시- (where inclusion is "triggered" by
an honorific-marked subject NP, parallel to English 3sg -s) fails pragmatically: -시- attaches even when
the literal grammatical subject is a body part, ailment, or possession of the honored referent (할아버지
수염이 많으시다 "grandfather has many whiskers[HON]" — subject is "whiskers," not "grandfather").
Whether -시- is included with a third-person referent depends on: whether the referent is present at
the speech event (more likely retained if present — Lee Jung-bok 2002); in-group status (speakers drop
honorifics for their own parents within the family but restore them outside it, even to close friends);
and **apconpep** (restriction of respect, dropping -시- when the referent outranks the speaker but not
the listener) — a rule younger speakers increasingly find "confusing" and are abandoning in favor of
maximal honorification toward superiors as a newer politeness strategy. -시- is most consistently
retained in **imperatives** regardless of declarative/interrogative usage, to the point that the {P}
imperative ending is conventionally cited as -sipsio (-si-+-upsio) rather than bare -upsio — evidence
that the honorifics system is independently sensitive to the illocutionary force of imposition (tied to
§6 below).

Subject-honorific vocabulary substitution operates on three usage tiers, not a binary: **universal**
(e.g. 계시다/드시다/돌아가시다, used toward any honored referent), **restricted** (used mainly toward
notable superiors), and **very restricted** (reserved for noin/age-60+ referents specifically — e.g.
생신 "birthday[HON]", 춘추 "age[HON]"; Choo Mi-ho 2006 notes that asking someone in their 30s their
연세/생신/성함 can make them "feel old"). Brown's own fieldwork (2010d) found only 34.69% of Korean
university students use 잡수시다 at all, preferring 드시다 or 식사 하시다 instead — live evidence that
even "universal" honorific-verb paradigms are shifting generationally.

### 3. Object honorifics: a different comparison logic

Unlike subject honorifics (which compare referent-to-speaker), object honorification requires the
object to outrank **both** the speaker and the subject/topic referent simultaneously (except for the
particle -께, which only compares to the speaker). Example: 할아버지가 그것을 아버지께 드리셨어요
("grandfather gave it to father[HON]") is ungrammatical with 드리다 because although father outranks
the speaker, father does not outrank grandfather (the subject) — 드리다 is replaced by plain 주다 (with
-시- retained for the subject). Object honorification is largely non-productive except for 드리다's
extension into benefactive constructions (사 드리다 "buy for," 설명해 드리다 "explain for") and as a
light-verb substitute for 하다 in Sino-Korean N+하다 compounds (전화 드리다 "call[HON]," 사과 드리다
"apologize[HON]"). Middle Korean had a true object-honorific verbal suffix -sop-, which has since fused
into the modern {P} ending -(su)pnita and no longer functions independently (p.37, fn.10).

### 4. Forms of address: a 29-pattern typology, pronoun avoidance, and third-person reference

Korean personal names/pronouns are restricted to addressing equals/inferiors, which Brown argues is
*why* Korean built such an elaborate title/kinship/teknonym system. He catalogs 29 named address
patterns (kinship terms × deferential/plain/intimate; fictive kinship terms extended to non-family;
teknonyms — addressing someone as "X's father/mother"; personal-name combinations with -nim/-ssi/-a/
-ya/-i/kwun/yang) built from a small set of productive suffixes: **-nim** (honorific, attaches to
titles/some kinship terms — inclusion vs. omission directly indexes whether the addressee outranks the
speaker), **-ssi** (indexes "separation" without being deferential — never used among intimates, because
it only attaches to personal names, which aren't used toward superiors), **-a/-ya/-i** (vocative,
subordinate/intimate-only), **kwun/yang** (male/female vocative suffixes for addressing young inferiors,
yang particularly restricted). Teknonyms and fictive kinship terms (오빠/언니/형/누나 extended to
non-relatives of marginally older age; 이모/삼촌 used by service staff to regular customers) are
pervasive precisely because bare pronouns/names are so restricted.

The second-person pronoun inventory is contested in the literature (Hwang 1975 counts 3; Pae 1974
counts 9!); Brown follows Park Jeong-woon (2005) in recognizing five (너/자네/당신/그대/자기), **none of
which is appropriate toward a status superior or non-intimate adult** — forcing speakers toward titles/
kinship terms/zero-pronoun instead. 당신 is the most semantically generic ("closest to English you") but
is only safe between spouses or in impersonal written address (ads, book titles) — used toward a
non-intimate it is "highly insulting" (also notably used as a blanket, somewhat patronizing "you" in
Korean "foreigner talk" directed at non-native speakers — King & Yeon 2009; Lee & Ramsey 2000). 자기
(lit. a reflexive "self") is a genuine recent innovation, now an intimate second-person pronoun mainly
between young couples, also usable non-reciprocally by older women to younger women (Park 1992).

Korean lacks true third-person pronouns; the honorific/plain/casual/deprecatory cline 그분/그 사람/그
애/그놈 substitutes, with 그놈 ("that wretch") usable casually by men toward intimates, inferiors, or
even their own children, but easily sliding into genuine deprecation.

A separate humbling-register table (Table 12, p.41) gives honorific/humble/plain triples for "wife" and
"children" — 집사람/마누라/아내 for wife; 딸(년/자식)/따님 for daughter; 아들(놈/자식)/아드님/돝아 for
son — the humble column built compositionally from pejorative-sounding nouns (년 "wench," 놈 "wretch,"
자식 "offspring," 돝 "pig") attached to the kin term, used when speaking of one's own family to a status
superior. Brown notes this entire humble-register layer is "all but disappeared" among younger speakers,
who default to the plain forms even toward superiors — a live register-simplification in progress.

### 5. "Beyond honorifics": borderline phenomena

Several lexical/phonological alternations track contaymal/panmal without being canonical "honorific"
forms: 안녕히 ("peacefully") substitutes for plain 잘 ("well") only in the fixed greeting formulas
안녕히 가세요/계세요/주무세요 (not productively elsewhere); 죄송하다 ("I feel guilty") vs. 미안하다 ("I
feel uncomfortable") as apology-verb register pair; 네/예 vs. casual 응 for "yes" — the latter is
flagged as a usage mistake severe enough that one L2 speaker in the study's own "learner stories"
received "a particularly violent reaction" for using it inappropriately. The **volitional marker**
choice also tracks register independently of speech style: casual -lkey vs. deferential -keyss- in
leave-taking formulas. Winter & Grawunder's (2009) acoustic study found contaymal utterances are
quieter, lower-pitched, more monotonous, and **58% longer** than panmal equivalents (52% of that length
difference from increased "wordiness") — prosody and verbosity, not just morphology, mark the
honorific/non-honorific boundary.

### 6. Interrelationship and the four social factors

A single sentence can simultaneously index hearer, subject-referent, and object-referent honorification
(Brown's worked example has nine distinct honorific elements across all three layers — object
honorification nested innermost, subject honorification next, hearer honorification outermost, p.46).
When hearer and referent coincide, the eight logical combinations of {P/Y/E/T} × {±(으)시} are not
equally available: the "pure" combinations (honorific style + -시-, or plain style with neither) are
default; mixed combinations exist but carry marked social meanings — e.g. honorific-style-without-시-
(addressing a known-equal/inferior non-intimate formally) vs. plain-style-with-시- (a senior professor
deferring to a junior professor's *position* while keeping overall intimacy, or a daughter to her
mother) — demonstrating that -시- tracks **power** while speech-style tracks **distance/formality**
somewhat independently.

Brown reduces honorifics motivation to four factors, replacing a simple "social superiority" model:
- **Power** — Diamond's (1996) institutional rank (sex, age, race, family position — relatively fixed)
  vs. local rank (occupation, economic status, education — changeable). Age itself is graded into three
  folk categories (much older/a little older/not much older — Yoon 2004) rather than a single cutoff.
  Power is also *contestable*, not merely ascribed: speaking about a superior's immoral act in
  honorific language sounds pragmatically odd, and clerks in cheap retail may strategically *drop* to
  panmal with younger customers specifically to assert coercive pressure to buy.
- **Distance** — decomposed into solidarity (shared group membership — same sex/family/hometown/
  company/school/department/club), familiarity ("intensity of interaction," borrowed from Klein et al.
  1995's interlanguage-pragmatics critique of "length of stay" as a proxy for actual exposure), and
  affect (liking/attraction). Married couples sometimes *upgrade* to honorific forms during arguments —
  a marked use of distance to signal emotional rupture.
- **Formality** — tracked by Irvine's (2001)/Atkinson's (1982) "scene" model (code structuring, code
  consistency, positional identities, central situational focus); {P} is the default formality cue in
  Korean, so strongly ideologized as "the formal style" (kyeksikchey) that speakers believe they use
  only {P} in formal broadcast contexts even when they empirically alternate {P}/{Y} (Lee Chang-soo
  1996).
- **Softening of assertion** — honorific marking (especially -시-) spreading onto non-canonical,
  non-human subjects ("the letter," "the price," "70,000 won," "this meaning") purely to soften an
  utterance's illocutionary force — traditionally condemned as an "error" but attested across natural
  broadcast/service/classroom speech (Lim Dong-hoon 2000).

A fifth, non-politeness-motivated category — **marked usage** — covers honorifics deployed for sarcasm,
anger, or joke (mixing referent honorifics with a non-honorific speech style toward an intimate/
inferior, e.g. a teacher telling a late-arriving student "you've come[HON] early!"), and "**empathy/
hearer-based**" honorifics (Lee & Kuno 1995), where a speaker uses honorifics toward a referent to
match the *hearer's* relationship to that referent rather than the speaker's own — e.g. a father telling
his son "aunt[HON] is going[HON]" out of deference to the son's relationship to the aunt, not his own.

### 7. Korean vs. Japanese honorifics (§2.4.3)

Korean's speech-style (hearer-honorific) system is more elaborated than Japanese's, but Korean has
far less developed object-honorific/self-humbling ("humilific") morphology — Japanese retains a live,
productive self-lowering verb class (参る mairu, 存ずる zonzuru, 申す moosu) with no real Korean
counterpart beyond a handful of fossilized object-honorific substitutions. This leads Brown (via Horie
2007) to characterize Korean honorifics as oriented toward Brown & Levinson's **positive politeness**
(affording the hearer deference) while Japanese leans toward **negative politeness** (self-abasement).
Korean also lacks Japanese's strong in-group/out-group (uchi/soto) suppression rule — a Korean speaker
will use honorifics for an in-group superior (parent, boss) even in front of out-group strangers, the
reverse of Japanese practice — and lacks any equivalent to Japanese "women's language" or "beautification
honorifics" (honorifics applied to one's own possessions). In Korean, heavy honorific use (especially
{P}) is associated with stiffness/authority/masculinity, not femininity — the opposite valence from
Japanese, which Brown cites (via Okamoto 2004) as proof that the honorific-femininity link is culturally
arbitrary, not universal.

### 8. Politeness-theory frameworks applied to honorifics (new to this corpus)

Brown surveys four theoretical camps and finds all of them treat honorifics as outside "true" politeness:
**Brown & Levinson (1987)** class honorifics under negative-politeness "give deference," but the theory
can't explain honorific choice in utterances with no face-threat at all. **Hwang/Ide** split
"**deference**" (closed, obligatory, social-deictic — includes honorifics) from "**politeness**"
(open-ended strategic choices — hedges, indirectness); Ide's parallel Japanese-linguistics term is
"**discernment**" (wakimae, socio-pragmatically obligatory) vs. "**volition**" (active strategic choice).
**Watts (1989)** distinguishes "**politic**" behavior (appropriate, unmarked, passes unnoticed) from
truly "**polite**" behavior (marked, noticed, interpretable) — honorifics used per social norm would be
merely "politic," not "polite." **Lee Jung-bok/Yoo Song-young** instead split "**normative usage**"
(honorifics expressing accepted hierarchy/intimacy) from "**strategic usage**" (marked, intentional,
norm-deviating, used to manipulate a power/solidarity "seesaw").

Brown rejects treating any of these as a fixed Western-strategic/Asian-automatic dichotomy (calling
this framing "unscientific" and "a prejudice rather than a reality") and instead adopts **Terkourafi's
(2005) frame-based model**: an utterance is polite to the extent it is the *regular*, expected linguistic
behavior for its context-type and goes unchallenged — politeness is "not in linguistic expressions
themselves, but in the regularity of co-occurrence" between expression and context. This reframes
honorific use-by-convention and honorific use-by-strategy as two *modes* of the same politeness system
rather than opposed categories — matching Brown's proposed terms **"indexical politeness"** (negotiating
politeness via honorific/social-deictic categories — the dominant Korean/Japanese mode) vs.
**"modulation politeness"** (negotiating politeness via mitigation/reinforcement of illocutionary
force — the dominant "Western" mode, per Caffi 2007's broader sense of "modulation" covering both
weakening and strengthening). Crucially, Brown frames the Korean/Western contrast as existing "primarily
at the ideological level," not as an objective difference in how much either culture actually varies
language by social context (citing Koo 1995's finding that American and Korean speakers vary language
by addressee to similar degrees — Koreans are just more *consciously aware* of doing so).

Also newly captured: **face as relational/interactional** (Arundale 2006) rather than individualistic
(Brown & Levinson), reconceptualizing positive/negative face as **"connection face"/"separation face"**
— directly parallel to Brown's own separation/connection honorifics model; and **politeness ideology**
as a distinct analytical layer from "politeness-in-action," with Korean-specific ideologies named and
glossed: **obligation/conformity** (honorifics as compulsory, tied to East Asian conformity being
positively valued rather than suppressing individuality — Kim & Markus 1999), and **closeness/intimacy**
(정 chong) as honorifics' under-ideologized "other side."

### 9. The L2 pragmatic-development model (core SLA content)

Brown proposes three components of honorific competence: **pragmalinguistic knowledge** (how to form
honorific utterances correctly), **sociopragmatic knowledge** (when/with whom different forms are
conventional), and **identity** (whether the speaker can/will adopt the social position the usage
implies). He models sociopragmatic acquisition as "**re-framing**" (building on Nelson's 1986/1996
child-development "Mental Event Representations" — General Event Representations abstracted from
repeated exposure vs. Specific Event Representations for marked/unusual cases) — crucially, for adult
L2 learners this is *re-analyzing existing frames*, not building from a blank slate, since learners
already carry L1 pragmatic frames that get negotiated against, not simply overwritten (rejecting
Escandell-Vidal's 1996 "read-only-memory" metaphor for L1 frames, citing evidence that L2 pragmatic
knowledge actually "bleeds into" L1 performance — Blum-Kulka's "intercultural style").

Two specific **over-/under-generalization strategies** are named: (1) **"strategy of least effort"**
— L2 speakers over-rely on simple, automated, high-frequency forms and under-use complex or
non-propositional-content-bearing forms (since honorifics don't change referential meaning, they get
judged "droppable" under production pressure); (2) **"strategy of playing it safe"** — insecurity-driven
over-generalization of forms perceived as safely neutral/polite and under-use of anything that risks
offense, including an over-conservative reluctance to downgrade from contaymal to panmal even when
intimacy would license it.

**Training-induced difficulties** are documented concretely: Korean pedagogical manuals explicitly
instruct that "there is no need to teach all of the honorifics used by native speakers" (Park
Young-soon 2001), naming the {E} style and referent-honorific vocabulary substitutions specifically as
skippable — meaning learners are formally undertaught the two areas (non-honorific register and
referent honorifics) where native competence actually diverges most from textbook Korean. Classroom
discourse itself (teacher-fronted, Initiate-Response-Feedback structure) also structurally denies
learners the chance to practice referent honorifics toward the one status-superior they regularly
interact with (the teacher), and interview data showed teachers rarely correct students' honorific
omissions even at advanced levels.

Identity/"**re-facing**" (adapted from Block's 2007 "critical experiences" and Bhabha's "third place"
identity) is modeled via Leung/Harris/Rampton's three independent axes of **language identity**:
expertise (proficiency), affiliation (felt attachment), and inheritance (born into the community) —
explicitly not mutually entailing (one can inherit Korean with zero proficiency, or affiliate to Korean
with zero inheritance).

### 10. Empirical findings: "honorific sensitive incidents" — how native speakers unilaterally shape L2 honorific experience

A standout finding (Ch.8) is that L2 speakers' honorific competence is not purely self-generated: native
speakers systematically apply **non-canonical honorific patterns specifically because the interlocutor
is a foreigner**, in two opposite directions. **(a) Unilateral panmal toward L2 speakers**: adult
strangers (frequently taxi drivers), subordinates, and even schoolchildren addressed participants in
panmal in situations where an adult Korean would receive contaymal — sometimes explicitly because "you
wouldn't need to use honorifics to a foreigner" (a view some Korean interlocutors stated outright), and
sometimes as a strategic power move exploiting the L2 speaker's uncertain social position (a bus
passenger, a department manager newly meeting a foreign-identified heritage-speaker employee). Notably,
**heritage speakers were not exempt** — one professional (James) was addressed in panmal by his own
department manager specifically because colleagues attributed it to his being read as a "foreigner,"
despite his Korean ethnicity. Schoolchildren used panmal toward foreign teachers in contexts (private
English-conversation schools functioning as playmate-not-teacher relationships) that structurally lacked
the hierarchy that would otherwise compel contaymal. **(b) Unilateral retained contaymal toward L2
speakers** — the more common and, per Brown, more *socially isolating* pattern: L1 friends who used
reciprocal panmal with each other continued contaymal with the L2 speaker indefinitely, which
participants experienced as discriminatory and as a "barrier" to intimacy even though it wasn't
face-threatening per se. One heritage speaker (Alice) describes sending furious text messages to a male
friend who used the self-humbling pronoun 저 (ce) toward her in a casual text — she perceived this as
unilaterally "reclassifying" their friendship into a more distant register.

A genuinely counter-intuitive finding: several participants (notably graduate student "Matthew")
reported that being treated as a permanent linguistic "outsider" could be a **relational advantage**,
not merely a limitation — freed from the expectation of fictive-kinship hierarchy terms (오빠/형/선배),
one male participant found female peers *more* comfortable with him specifically because he made no
claim to the deference "older brother" status would imply. This directly contradicts prior SLA identity
literature (Norton 2000) holding that "foreigner"-positioned learners are simply isolated/"unworthy to
speak" — Brown's data shows the position can be renegotiated into a usable, even favorable, alternative
social slot.

### 11. Identity categories and differential honorific competence (Ch.9 synthesis)

Brown's discussion chapter names four cross-cutting identity axes that predicted differing honorific
competence in his learner data: **"Westerner"** (shared egalitarian politeness ideology across European/
North American/Australasian participants, predicting systematic under-valuation of hierarchy-marking
regardless of native language); **"Foreigner"** (peripheral social position restricting which honorific
roles — status superior, fictive sibling — were available to claim, but also sometimes advantageously
loosening relational constraints); **occupation** (**exchange students** developed better non-honorific/
panmal fluency via casual campus peer relationships but weaker referent-honorific competence from
lacking status-superior contact; **professionals** showed the reverse — statistically significant
honorific-style overuse, and egalitarian/inconsistent referent-honorific application even when
nominally using high forms); and **ethnicity** (**heritage speakers** had native-like {E}-style control
from family socialization but similar insecurity/under-use of panmal with non-family interlocutors as
non-heritage learners, and were *still* sometimes cast as "foreigner"/outsider when their linguistic
performance or felt affiliation fell short — ethnicity alone did not guarantee insider status).
**Gender** was explored lightly: Western female learners sometimes rejected the fictive-kinship address
term 오빠 (oppa, "older brother") as incompatible with egalitarian/feminist self-identity, while also
under-using the masculine-coded {P} style relative to L1 norms.

### 12. Textbook-bias findings (pedagogy implications, Ch.9.3.3)

Brown's own prior corpus study (cited here, Brown 2010a) of three major Korean-textbook series found
60.5% of all dialogues depict reciprocal same-age-rank casual acquaintances, while only 5.8% depict a
subordinate-to-notable-superior relationship; panmal speech styles account for only 12.4% of all
textbook utterances (4.4% in one series), and even in contexts that would call for panmal between
established "friends," dialogues often retain reciprocal honorific speech instead. Brown argues this
is not fully explained by simplification-for-learners — it reflects textbook writers' own unconscious
assumptions (shared with lay native speakers) about what register "foreigners" are expected to need,
creating a feedback loop where textbook-taught patterns reinforce the very native-speaker expectations
that generated them. Proposed fix: explicit consciousness-raising tasks (cloze-style blanking of speech
style endings — adapted from Byon 2007's TV-talk-show materials) plus deliberate inclusion of
hierarchical, non-reciprocal dialogue contexts.

# Korean — Established Vocabulary: *Dirty Korean: Everyday Slang from "What's Up?" to "F*%# Off!"*

**Source:** Haewon Geebi Baek (illustrated by Lindsay Mack), *Dirty Korean: Everyday Slang from
"What's Up?" to "F*%# Off!"* (Ulysses Press, Berkeley CA, © 2010, ISBN 978-1-56975-779-6), complete
book — Using This Book + Quick and Dirty Korean pronunciation guide (printed p.1-5) and all 9
chapters (printed p.6-161): Howdy Korean, Friendly Korean, Party Korean, Body Korean, Horny Korean,
Angry Korean, Poppy Korean, Sporty Korean, Hungry Korean. FLAGSHIP slang-dictionary source for
Korean — this is Korean's direct counterpart to *Dirty Japanese* (already extracted as
`datasets/Japanese/established/096*.md`).

**Text layer / extraction method:** clean, correctly-rendering `pdftotext` text layer throughout all
177 PDF pages — both English and Hangul extract correctly with no cipher, CID-font corruption, or
bogus-CMap corruption of any kind (checked against the `+36266`-offset and other font-substitution
mechanisms documented in `../../00_Reference_Extraction_Spec.md`; none apply here). No vision-reading
was needed; `Vision Reading Confidence` is `n/a` throughout. No handwritten marginalia exists (not a
scanned/used-copy source).

**Coverage note — comprehensive, not sampled.** Per the extraction spec, this is explicitly a
slang/vulgar-register dictionary (the book's whole premise), so there is no "redundant drill content"
to skip the way a grammar textbook would have — essentially every bolded English-headword phrase in
every chapter is a distinct vocabulary item and is captured below. Pure template scaffolding that
introduces no new lexical content when repeated (e.g. a "Where is the ___?" frame reused verbatim with
a different noun already captured elsewhere) is merged into the entry for its content word rather than
given a duplicate row. Cultural/anecdotal sidebar prose accompanying each entry is paraphrased to a
short clause per copyright discipline — never reproduced at length. A handful of thematically-grouped
proper-noun lists (cigarette brands, pharmacy brand names, fast-food chains, movie/TV titles, color and
sports terms) are tabulated as single grouped rows rather than one row apiece, since the source itself
presents them as undifferentiated lists with minimal individual commentary.

**Shared column values (declared once, per the convention already used in
`established/017_in_flight_korean.md`):** unless a row's own Notes column states otherwise, every row
below has **Attested Era** `contemporary (source published 2010)`, **Attested Region** `—`, **Geographic
Scope** `—`, **Source Type** `dictionary`, **Transcription Confidence** `n/a`, **Vision Reading
Confidence** `n/a`. These six columns are omitted from the per-chapter tables to keep ~600 rows of a
genuinely slang-dense source manageable; only **Term | Gloss | Part of Speech | Usage Tier |
Weight/Frequency | Notes** vary meaningfully row to row.

**Register-tier mapping used throughout:** `taboo` = profanity, explicit sexual/scatological terms,
and slurs; `slang` = youth/informal coinages, clippings, and blends that are not themselves
profane/explicit; `colloquial` = casual everyday register, not taboo and not a coined slang term;
`core` = standard/neutral vocabulary needed for context (kinship terms, basic nouns, loanwords for
ordinary objects); `formal` = the deferential/polite register explicitly marked as such by the book;
`regional` = explicitly marked as rural/non-Seoul usage by the source itself.

## Grammar points

### Age-deferential register as the organizing principle of casual greeting choice

Chapter 1 opens by flagging that slang greetings like *annyeong* (안녕) are usable with strangers
only if the speaker is the same age as or older than the addressee (p.6) — the same age-hierarchy
logic recurs explicitly in Chapter 2's discussion of titles (*ajeossi/ajumma/agassi*, p.27-28: using a
title on someone younger than expected reads as an insult) and in the negotiated-register phrase *Uri
mal noeulkkayo?*/"Let's not talk so formally" (p.16), which marks the moment two new acquaintances
agree to drop honorific speech for casual speech. Register choice in Korean casual conversation is
therefore not a stylistic option but a socially negotiated fact gated on relative age — a pattern this
project's mechanics-analysis phase should track as a structural constraint on when slang forms are even
usable, not just what they mean.

### Reduplicated ideophones as a productive slang-intensifier mechanism

A recurring word-formation pattern across chapters: a short root is reduplicated to form a vivid
descriptive adjective or exclamation, e.g. *jjukjjuk bbangbbang hada* (쭉쭉빵빵하다) "voluptuous"
(ch.4, p.58), *bbujijik bbujijik* (뿌지직 뿌지직) the onomatopoeic sound of defecation (ch.4, p.69),
*yamyam jjeopjjeop* (얌얌 쩝쩝) "yum yum" (ch.9, p.149), and the drinking-slang family *kkoratda/
kkwaladwaetda* that cluster around the "drunk" semantic field (ch.3, p.45). This is a distinct and
highly productive register marker from ordinary adjective morphology — reduplication itself signals
informal/expressive register, independent of the root word's own formality.

### Euphemistic food-metaphor wordplay for sex and body topics

Several flagship slang coinages in this source build sexual or body-shaming meaning on top of
ordinary food vocabulary: *kong* (콩, "bean") for "clitoris" and *kongkkagi* (콩까기, "bean-peeling")
for "losing one's virginity" (ch.5, p.83); the "booby continuum" euphemism scale running from
*kkumttakji* ("gum stuck to asphalt," flat-chested) through *jeotso* ("milking cow," huge tits) to
*chakhan gaseum* ("nice breasts," i.e. good-person breasts) (ch.5, p.83-84); and the gendered pair
*doenjangnyeo* "bean-paste girl" / *gochujangnam* "pepper-paste boy" (ch.2, p.33), whose own etymology
the source traces to a saying about someone unable to tell shit from bean paste — plus a second,
independent pun where *gochu* "red pepper" is a homonym for "penis," giving the male term a double
meaning the female term lacks. A third case, *goraejabi* "whale-hunting" as a euphemism for
circumcision (ch.5, p.79), works the same way via pure homonymy (*pogyeong susul* "circumcision" /
*pogyeong-up* "whaling") rather than food metaphor, showing this source's slang layer leans heavily on
wordplay-based euphemism generally, not just the food-specific pattern.

### Clipping as the dominant slang-formation mechanism for age/status terms

A tight family of school-age terms are formed by clipping the final syllable off a longer compound:
*goding* (고딩) < *godeunghaksaeng* "high schooler," *jungding* (중딩) < *junghaksaeng* "middle
schooler," *choding* (초딩) < *chodeunghaksaeng* "elementary schooler" (ch.2, p.31-32); the same
mechanism produces *namchin/yeochin* (남친/여친) from *namjachingu/yeojachingu* "boyfriend/girlfriend"
(ch.2, p.23), *selka* (셀카) from *self camera* (ch.1, p.17), and *eoljjang* (얼짱) as a
compound-clipping of *eolgul* "face" + *jjang* "number one/best" (ch.4, p.60-61) rather than a simple
truncation of one word. Clipping is the single most frequent slang-formation strategy observed across
this source's ~550 entries, ahead of either reduplication or loanword blending.

### Taboo intensifier particles (*jonna*/*jolla*) as grammaticalized profanity

*Jonna* (존나) and its variant *jolla* (졸라) appear repeatedly as bare degree-intensifiers slotted
directly before a verb or adjective with no further morphology — "*neo jonna majeuljul ara*" ("I'm
gonna fuck you up," lit. "you're-fucking-going-to-get-hit," ch.6, p.107) and "*uri jolla mothae!*"
("we fucking suck!", ch.8, p.134). Functionally these behave like any other adverbial intensifier
(*ne.g.* "very"), but carry taboo register inherently — a vulgar word has been grammaticalized into a
productive intensifier slot, parallel to how English "fucking" functions as a bare intensifier. This
is a genuine morphosyntactic fact about Korean taboo vocabulary, not just a lexical curiosity.

### Metonymic shift from object to person

*Cheolgabang* (철가방, "steel case") literally names the stainless-steel carrier case Korean delivery
drivers use, but has become slang for the delivery driver himself (ch.9, p.147) — the same
object-to-person metonymy pattern as English "the suits" for executives. *Jjapsae* (짭새, "cops," ch.2/
ch.6) likely has comparable figurative origins, though the source does not gloss its etymology.

### Konglish (English-loanword) integration as productive slang vocabulary

English loanwords are freely compounded with native Korean morphemes to produce new slang rather than
simply being borrowed wholesale: *skinship* (스킨쉽, "skin" + English "-ship" suffix, ch.5, p.86) for
non-sexual physical affection; *Dutch pay haja* (더치 페이 하자, "Dutch pay" + native verb *haja*
"let's do," ch.9, p.153) for splitting a restaurant bill; *handphone* (헨드폰, "hand" + "phone," ch.7,
p.121) for "cell phone"; and *hwaiting!* (화이팅!, from English "fighting," ch.8, p.133) as a stock
sports cheer with no literal fighting involved. None of these are simple 1:1 transliterations of an
English word's existing meaning — each has drifted to a Korean-specific sense the English source word
does not itself carry, which is exactly the kind of loanword-nativization data this project's
mechanics-analysis phase is built to track.

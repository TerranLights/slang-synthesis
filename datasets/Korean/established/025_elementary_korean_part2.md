# Korean — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Elementary Korean, Lessons 11 (remainder)–15 (second half)

**Source:** Ross King and Jae-Hoon Yeon, *Elementary Korean* (Tuttle Publishing), `.djvu` scan, 429
pages total (no text layer — confirmed via `djvutxt`). This dispatch covers djvu pages 216–429
(printed pages 196–409), the back half of the book: the tail of Lesson 11 (11.2 onward), Lessons
12–14 in full, Lesson 15 (Review 2)'s verb-forms summary, and a sampling pass over the book's
back-matter Reference Section. A sibling subagent covered djvu pages 1–215 (printed front matter
through Lesson 11.1) and should be filed as `established/024_elementary_korean_part1.md`. This is
the same author team (King & Yeon) as `established/005–007` (*Continuing Korean*, the sequel
volume), so substantial grammar overlap with that series — and with `established/001–004` (Byon's
*Basic*/*Intermediate Korean*) — was expected and confirmed in practice; see the non-redundant-
supplement note below.

**Vision-reading method and page-numbering gotcha (new type, distinct from every prior Korean PDF
gotcha).** This is a `.djvu` scan (not a PDF), and `djvutxt` confirms zero extractable text — every
page was vision-read from `ddjvu`-rendered page images. The scan itself has an unusual layout: each
djvu "page" is a photographed **two-page spread** of the physical open book, but consecutive djvu
pages advance the *printed* page by only 1 (not 2), meaning each printed page appears twice across
two consecutive djvu images — once cleanly on one side, once partially cut off (by binding-curvature
photography) on the other. Empirically verified (by direct inspection, not assumed): **even-numbered
djvu pages show their clean, complete printed page on the LEFT side; odd-numbered djvu pages show
theirs on the RIGHT side**, with `printed_page = djvu_page − 20` holding as a fixed offset across the
entire assigned range (spot-checked at djvu 216/217/218/219, djvu 320 [printed 300], and djvu 429
[printed 409] — no drift found; this independently confirms the sibling `established/024` file's own
page-offset finding for the front half of the book). Every page in this dispatch was read from the
correctly clean side per this parity rule.

**Boundary with the sibling chunk — a real gap was found, not a clean handoff.** Printed page 196
(djvu 216), where this dispatch begins, opens mid-Lesson-11, at the start of §11.2 "Numerals in Time
Expressions" (§11.2.1 "Telling the Time"), immediately following §11.1 "Numbers and Counting" — a
clean grammar-subsection boundary in isolation. **However**, per `established/024`'s own coverage
note (checked before writing this file), that sibling dispatch was assigned printed pp. 1–215 but
only actually completed printed pp. 1–50 (Lessons 1–4 through §4.10), leaving **printed pp. 51–195
(djvu 71–215) genuinely unextracted** — this dispatch does not reach back far enough to close that
gap, since its own assigned range starts at printed 196. **Printed pp. 51–195 (Lessons 5 through the
first part of Lesson 11) remain open for a future dispatch**; this should be corrected in the
checklist's progress tracking rather than silently treated as covered by the 024/025 pair.

**Vision-reading confidence.** All pages in this range are genuinely typeset, print-quality textbook
content — no scan artifacts beyond the expected binding-curvature cropping (which was worked around
via the parity rule above, not by guessing obscured text), and no handwritten marginalia was found
anywhere in the range. All entries below are marked Vision Reading Confidence `verified` unless
otherwise noted in a row's Notes column.

**Non-redundant-supplement dispatch.** Because this book's authors also wrote `established/005–007`
(*Continuing Korean*) and because `established/001–004` (Byon's *Basic*/*Intermediate Korean*)
already cover extensive beginner-to-intermediate ground, several grammar points in Lessons 12–14
were found to be materially redundant with prior extractions and are **condensed rather than
re-derived from scratch** below — specifically: short/long negation (§12.2, cf. `established/002`),
probable futures in -(으)ㄹ 거예요 (§13.4, cf. `established/002`), the future-presumptive -겠- system
in all its uses (§14.4–14.5, 14.7–14.9, cf. `established/002`'s "-겠 (inference/intention)"), and the
immediate future -(으)ㄹ게요 (§14.6, cf. `established/002`'s "-(으)ㄹ래요 vs. -(으)ㄹ게요"). Where this
book's own treatment adds a genuinely finer distinction, nuance, or synopsis table not captured
elsewhere (e.g. the past-future vs. plain-future near-homophone distinction, the inferential/
intentional split, the full compatibility-with-겠 synopsis table), that finer content is kept. Every
other grammar point below (§11.2–11.5, §12.1, §12.3–12.9, all of §13, §14.1–14.3, §14.8) was judged
genuinely new relative to prior Korean `established/` files and is captured in full.

## Grammar points

### §11.2. Numerals in Time Expressions (p. 196–199)
Telling time uses native-Korean numerals for the hour + 시 and Sino-Korean numerals for the minute +
분 (하나 시 하나 분 pattern is wrong; correct is 한 시 일 분, with native-Korean numerals losing their
final consonant before 시: 한/두/세/네 시, not 하나/둘/셋/넷 시). AM/PM are 오전/오후, placed before the
time. §11.2.2 covers **dates**: year + 년, month + 월 (irregular Sino-Korean readings for 6월/10월:
유월/시월, not 육월/십월), day + 일, all with Sino-Korean numerals; phone numbers are read digit-by-
digit with 공 for "0," and 에 dash-reads as "the" (국번-번호 pattern).

### §11.3. Four Particles: 마다, 만, 씩, 쯤 (p. 199–201)
- **마다** "every, each" attaches to a noun (날마다 "every day").
- **만** "only, just" (see also §14.1 below for its fuller negative counterpart 밖에).
- **씩** "apiece, each, at a time" attaches after a quantity (하나씩 "one apiece," 조금씩 "little by
  little").
- **쯤** "about, approximately" attaches after a quantity or time expression (다섯 시쯤 "about five
  o'clock"). All four particles can co-occur in sequence on one noun phrase for compounded nuance.

### §11.4–11.5. Register: Plain Style intro and Formal Style (p. 202–207)
This range gives the book's first systematic contrast between the **Formal Style** (-ㅂ니다/-습니다,
questions in -ㅂ니까/-습니까) and casual register. Two explicit sociolinguistic flags: (1) a man who
overuses the Polite Style (-요) in contexts calling for Formal Style, or vice versa, risks sounding
inappropriately soft/"talking like a woman" in the source's own phrasing — an explicit gendered-
register judgment tied to style-choice, not just vocabulary; (2) the imperative form -(으)시오 (a
Formal-Style command ending) is flagged as carrying an air of brusque, quasi-military authority —
the source notes it is sometimes used deliberately to convey barely-controlled anger, and advises
learners to use it with care. The Formal Style overall is framed as the register of public
announcements, news broadcasts, military contexts, and formal ceremony — matter-of-fact and
impersonal rather than warm.

### §12.1–12.2. Contrastive -지만 "but" and the negation system (p. 219–226)
§12.1 introduces the connective -지만 "but, although" (attaches to any base, any tense). §12.2 covers
short (안 + verb) and long (-지 않아요/-지 못해요) negation — **materially redundant with
`established/002`'s full treatment of 안/못 and -지 않다/-지 못하다**, condensed here. One nuance
worth keeping: this source explicitly flags that the long-form strong negative -지 않아요, when used
with a *descriptive* verb (adjective-like predicate), commonly carries an added nuance of the
speaker's dissatisfaction or complaint beyond simple negation, something the short form 안 lacks.
§12.2.3 covers negative commands (-지 마세요, "don't ___"), §12.2.4 the auxiliary-verb negator
pattern.

### §12.3–12.5. Suggestions/Permission-seeking and generalizer particles (p. 226–232)
§12.3–12.4 cover suggestion/tentative-question forms (-(으)ㄹ까요?). §12.5–12.6 give an extensive
treatment of (이)나's several distinct uses beyond the basic "or" already known: (a) NOUN(이)나
NOUN(이)나 "either...or...(indifferently)," contrasted explicitly against 도 ("both X and Y,
definite") — (이)나 signals the speaker doesn't care which; (b) NOUN(이)나 alone, "NOUN or something,"
carrying a mildly denigrating "whatever, I'm not picky" nuance; (c) the closed generalizer series
아무 + (이)나/것/때/데 "anybody/anything/anytime/anywhere at all," which requires a negative verb in
its "not just anybody" sense but is affirmative-compatible in its "anybody will do" sense (§12.6.2 in
the book's own numbering, cross-referenced by the Pattern Glossary).

### §12.7. More Ways to Say "or": 또는 vs. (이)나/아니면 (p. 235)
또는 (formal/written-leaning "or else") *excludes* one choice by contrast with the other, unlike
(이)나 and 아니면, which both accept either choice indifferently. 아니면 can also open a sentence
meaning "Or else."

### §12.8. The "wanna" Form in -(으)ㄹ래(요) (p. 236)
A plain processive base + -(으)ㄹ래(요) means "I feel like VERBing / I wanna VERB / I'd like to VERB
(if I had a choice)." **Explicitly flagged by the source as "highly colloquial"** — restricted to
first-person statements and second-person questions, and recommended over -고 싶어요 in questions
because the latter forces a blunt yes/no answer. Genuinely register-relevant slang-adjacent form.

### §12.9. Meanings for 좋아요 and Related Words (p. 237)
좋아요 is polysemous: (1) "is good/fine/all right" (one-argument, describes a thing), vs. (2) "is
liked" (often two-argument: topic + subject). The derived processive 좋아해요 "finds it good, likes
it" takes direct objects and carries an emotional-happiness connotation usable for oneself
("I'm happy/glad") or others. Opposite pair: 싫어요 "is disliked" / 싫어해요 "dislikes it," from base
싫-.

### §13.1. The Sequential Form -(으)니(까) (p. 249–250)
Attaches to verb bases (consonant: -으니까, vowel: -니까; also to past bases -았/었으니까 and to the
future -(으)ㄹ 거니까) to mean "as/since/because" (§13.1.1, second clause often a suggestion or
command) or "when...[I realized/discovered]..." (§13.1.2, marking a close temporal sequence where
the -(으)니까-clause subject is usually the speaker and cannot itself be a past base). The extended
form -(으)니까 is described as the colloquial-speech-dominant variant (also heard as -(으)니깐 or
-(으)니까는); a short form -(으)니 exists but is explicitly flagged as bookish/literary, rare in
colloquial Korean — a clean register split between the two sequential-form variants.

### §13.2. Honorifics: Nouns and Particles (p. 251–252)
Systematic list of neutral/honorific noun *pairs* referring to the same referent (집/댁 "house,"
나이/연세 "age," 사람/분 "person," 밥/진지 "cooked rice," 아이/자녀분·자제분 "children") — a lexical-
honorific mechanism distinct from the verb-ending honorific system already well documented elsewhere
in this project's Korean corpus. Also covers honorific particles: 께 (honorific version of 에게/한테,
"to [esteemed person]") and 께서(는) (honorific version of subject-marker 이/가, used only for
persons).

### §13.3. Kinship Terms (p. 252–254)
A systematic split of the kinship-term inventory into (a) terms that differ by the *gender of the
speaker* (a male's in-laws/spouse/siblings use different lexical items than a female's for the same
relationship — e.g. 장인/장모 "father-/mother-in-law" for a male speaker vs. 시아버지/시어머니 for a
female speaker) and (b) terms invariant across speaker gender. Notable structural point: **Korean
obligatorily grammaticalizes relative age for siblings/cousins** — there is no gender-neutral,
age-neutral way to say "my brother/sister"; the term itself encodes both the referent's gender and
whether they are older or younger than the speaker. Semantic-extension finding: 할아버지/할머니
"grandfather/grandmother" and 아저씨/아주머니(아줌마) "uncle/aunt" are also the ordinary Korean words
for "old man/old woman" and "(older) man/lady" respectively, especially in child-directed or
child-referring speech — kinship vocabulary doing double duty as generic age/respect address terms.
Also documents the 우리-for-"my" convention (우리 개 "my dog," 우리 남편/우리 아내 "my husband/wife")
as extending even past its "belongs to our family/group" rationale — independently corroborates the
collective-possessive finding already flagged from `established/015` (*Korean Through English 1*).

### §13.4. Probable Futures with -(으)ㄹ 거에요 (p. 254–257)
**Materially redundant with `established/002`**'s treatment of the same ending; condensed here to
the bare mechanics (attaches to plain/honorific base, or to a past base for "must have ___").

### §13.5. Rhetorical Retorts in -잖아요 (p. 257–258)
Genuinely novel, register-relevant form: -잖아요 is a phonologically "squished" (contracted) form of
the long-form negative question -지 않아요? (젊-으시-지 않아요? → -잖아요 via -지 않아요 → -쟎아요 →
-잖아요), reanalyzed as its own one-shape verb ending distinct from ordinary -지 negatives — unlike
plain -지 (which attaches only to plain/honorific bases), -잖아요 attaches to *any* base (plain, past,
honorific, or future-presumptive). Functionally it is a rhetorical, mildly exasperated "but ___,
isn't it obvious?/don't you see?" retort, roughly "But it's ___!" — a colloquial argumentative/
contradiction device with clear slang-adjacent conversational-pragmatic value for this project's
purposes.

### §13.6. "Is doing" with -고 있어요 (p. 258–259)
Progressive aspect: -고 (keeps ㄹ on ㄹ-extending bases) + 있- "is; stays." Honorific substitutes 계세요
for 있어요. Flags a genuine semantic ambiguity with verbs of donning clothing (넥타이를 매고 있어요 can
mean either "I am wearing a tie" [state] or "I am putting on my tie" [ongoing action]) — a real
aspectual ambiguity worth noting for any conlang-slang mechanics work on progressive/stative
blending. Also documents a negation-scope distinction: 쓰고 있지 않아요 "simply isn't writing" (denial)
vs. 쓰지 않고 있어요 "isn't writing [these days]/manages without writing" (implies the subject copes
fine without the activity) — negating the auxiliary vs. the main verb yields different pragmatics.

### §13.7. "As soon as" with -자마자 (p. 260)
One-shape ending -자 (intensified colloquially with 마자: -자마자/오자 마자) attaches to a plain
processive base regardless of the second clause's tense — the sentence-final verb alone carries
tense marking.

### §14.1. Ways to Say "only": 만 + affirmative, 밖에 + negative (p. 274–275)
만 + affirmative verb = "only, just, to the extent of." The quasi-particle 밖에 (< 밖 "outside" + 에)
+ a negative verb reaches the same "only" meaning by a more circuitous route, but crucially **the
밖에+negative pattern additionally implies the amount in question is less than, or falls short of, the
speaker's expectations** — a pragmatic overtone 만+affirmative lacks. With numerical expressions, 만
"only/just" translates to "no more, no less, exactly [N]," with no clean English equivalent.

### §14.2. Another Use of (이)나: "as many as" (p. 275)
(이)나 attached directly to a number/numeral expression implies the speaker judges that amount to be
surprisingly high, more than expected — distinct from and complementary to its "or/or something" uses
already documented in §12.6.

### §14.3. Expressions for Going and Coming (p. 276)
(으)로 "by/via" marks the vehicle when the main verb is 가요/와요 ("goes/comes by X"); by contrast, 타요
"rides, gets on" takes a direct object (기차를 타요 "rides a train") and the destination-plus-타고
construction can place the destination either before or after [vehicle]을~를 타고. 걸어요/걷다 "walks"
patterns like 타- in requiring its infinitive form 걸어 to combine with 가요/와요 rather than standing
alone with directional force.

### §14.4–14.9. The Future-Presumptive system in -겠- (p. 277–290)
**Materially redundant in its core mechanics with `established/002`'s "-겠 (inference/intention)"
and "probable-future -(으)ㄹ 거예요"**; the following finer distinctions and synthesis material are
new and kept:
- **Inferential vs. Intentional split** (§14.4.2): Inferential -겠- occurs with 2nd/3rd-person
  subjects and means "I'll bet that / I have good reason to infer that / judging by my evidence, it
  is likely that" (always evidence-grounded in context, e.g. 아프겠어요 "that must hurt," said while
  watching someone fall). Intentional -겠- occurs with 1st-person statements/2nd-person questions and
  carries a somewhat ceremonial, solemn-declaration force in statements, or functions as a polite
  way of eliciting compliance in questions.
- **Past-Future vs. Past-Past** (§14.5): 갔겠어요 "must have gone" (past marker = "has/have," future
  marker = "probably") is a genuine near-homophone of the plain future 가겠어요, distinguished
  acoustically by only a single ㄱ/ㄲ; separately, the "past-past" (double-past, -았/었-었- stacked on
  a past base, e.g. 했었어요) marks a *fully completed, no-longer-current* action, contrasted against
  the plain past for an action whose effects still hold — illustrated with 왔어요 "came [and is still
  here]" vs. 왔었어요 "came once [but is no longer here]."
- **Immediate Future -(으)ㄹ게요** (§14.6): attaches only to plain processive bases, almost always
  first-person, and carries a reassuring, promise-like "I'll do this for you, and I mean it"
  connotation, distinct in force from both -겠어요 and -(으)ㄹ 거예요.
- **Synopsis: Types of Future** (§14.7): a compact comparative table distinguishing Probable Future
  (-(으)ㄹ 거예요/겁니다, most common/neutral), Immediate Future (-(으)ㄹ게요), and Future-Presumptive
  (-겠-, the least certain/most subjective). §14.7.2 notes Korean systematically avoids asserting
  *others'* intentions with confidence — third-person sentences favor the probable future
  -(으)ㄹ거에요 over -겠-, which in third person shifts toward pure conjecture ("must be/probably
  is").  §14.7.3 explicitly separates -겠-'s English-translation targets: (a) will/is going to,
  (b) would (esp. with verbs of knowing: 알겠어요 "I understand"/모르겠어요 "I wouldn't know," both
  used in preference to the plain 알아요/몰라요 for a politer, more tentative effect, especially in
  2nd-person questions: 아시겠어요? "Would you happen to know?"), (c) is willing to/wants to, and
  (d) probably/must (conjecture) — carefully distinguished from the *obligatory* "must" (-어야
  해요, reserved for the sequel volume per the source's own note).
- **Suppositives in -지요** (§14.8, genuinely novel and register-rich, kept in full): the one-shape
  ending -지 (any base: plain/past/future-presumptive) + 요 means "supposedly," with distinct
  translation values by sentence type: in statements, "I suppose" (compatible with -겠-, and in an
  emphatic/assertive intonation can mean "Of course!/You bet!"); in yes-no questions, a tag-question
  effect ("isn't that right? isn't that so?"); in question-word questions, "I wonder.../what did you
  say it was?"; in suggestions/commands, "how about...?/why don't you...?" (often combined with the
  honorific: -(으)시지요). **Explicit usage warning**: -지요 is not normally used to answer a question
  about oneself (use plain Polite Style instead). **Colloquial contraction flagged**: -지요 is
  frequently run together in fast speech as -죠.
- **Synopsis: Compatibility of -겠- with previously-learned endings** (§14.9): a compact combinability
  table (-겠- + -(으)시-, -(스)ㅂ니다, -(스)ㅂ니까?, -지만, -잖아요, -(으)니까, -지요), worth preserving
  as a structural cross-reference of how this one morpheme composes with the rest of the ending
  inventory taught across Lessons 11–14.

### §15.1. Verb Forms: Summary — the base-classification system (p. 295–306, condensed)
Lesson 15 ("Review 2") opens with a comprehensive review synopsis of all verb-base types introduced
across the book, using its own distinctive four-way classification terminology (not identical to
Byon's seven-irregular-class framing in `established/002`, nor to *Teach Yourself Korean*'s
nine-class taxonomy in `established/014` — a third independently-organized presentation of largely
the same underlying morphophonology, worth noting as a comparative-pedagogy data point): **vowel-base
verbs** (regular, infinitive formed by straightforward vowel-final contraction, e.g. 가- → 가);
**L-doubling vowel-base verbs** (르-final bases where the 르 syllable itself doubles into ㄹㄹ before
the infinitive ending, e.g. 다르- "be different" → 달라, 모르- "not know" → 몰라 — this is the class
elsewhere called "르-irregular"); **L-extending vowel-base verbs** (disyllabic-looking bases
historically ending in a bare ㄹ that resurfaces before certain endings, e.g. 거르- "make [a phone
call]" → 걸어, 드르- "cost/lift" → 들어 — glossed elsewhere as the ㄹ-extending/"ㄹ-irregular" class);
and **consonant-base verbs**, further subdivided by a following page range (not transcribed in full
here) into regular consonant-final bases and the ㅂ~w-alternating irregular subclass (같- "be the
same" → 같아 vs. 괜찮- "be OK" → 괜찮아, both regular, contrasted against forthcoming irregular
examples). The book's own exhaustive word list for each class (~10 printed pages, printed pp.
296–306) reproduces vocabulary already captured in its originating lesson's vocabulary table and is
not re-transcribed here per the coverage rule.

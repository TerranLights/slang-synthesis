**Coverage note — vision-read, no text layer, scan gap, and TOC-offset gotchas found:**

This PDF (51 pages) has **no text layer and no embedded fonts whatsoever** — confirmed via
`pdftotext` (zero output on any page), `pdffonts` (empty font table), and `pdfimages -list` (every
page is a single full-page JPEG raster image, ~791×1495px at 200ppi for the front-matter pages).
There is no font-substitution cipher to decode here (unlike several other Korean Wave 1 sources) —
this is a pure page-image scan with no OCR text layer at all, so the entire book was vision-read from
rendered page images (300dpi PNG via `pdftoppm`).

**Two-pages-per-image spread, confirmed and empirically mapped, not assumed:** PDF pages 1–5 are
single-page images (front cover, back cover, title page, copyright page, Contents/TOC). PDF pages
6–51 are **two-printed-pages-per-image spreads**. The printed-page offset was verified empirically by
reading page headers at multiple points through the book (not trusted from a single fixed-offset
guess) and **the offset formula itself changes partway through the book**:
- PDF spread *s* for *s* = 6–19: printed pages = (2*s*−8, 2*s*−7) (e.g. PDF p.6 = printed pp.4–5; PDF
  p.19 = printed pp.30–31).
- PDF spread *s* for *s* = 20–51: printed pages = (2*s*−6, 2*s*−5) (e.g. PDF p.20 = printed pp.34–35;
  PDF p.51 = printed p.96 + the unnumbered back-matter ad page).

**The reason for this shift: printed pages 32–33 (part of Chapter 4 "Hey There!") are missing from
this scan** — confirmed by checking both page-numbering continuity (content jumps cleanly from
printed p.31, "Let's talk more," directly to printed p.34, the opening of Chapter 5 "Look at That!")
and checking for out-of-order/misbound pages elsewhere in the file (none found — printed page numbers
increase monotonically everywhere else). This is a genuine content gap in the source scan, not a
rendering artifact, and not recoverable from this file — flagged here rather than silently
under-covering Chapter 4.

**Separately, PDF page 51 (the last page) shows a thin vertical sliver of duplicate content from
printed page 94 bleeding in at the left edge of the image** (fragments of English words like "Can,"
"I can't," "Please" visible), alongside the real content of printed p.96 and the unnumbered
back-matter ad page. This is a rendering/cropping artifact (p.94 was already fully captured on its
own spread, PDF p.50) — not extracted as if it were new content.

**TOC/content pagination offset for the back half of the book:** the Contents page lists Chapter 11
"Getting Serious" at p.76, Chapter 12 "On the Phone" at p.78, Chapter 13 "Lover's Language" at p.80,
and Chapter 14 "Farewell" at p.90 — but the actual printed chapter-opening pages, confirmed by reading
the chapter-title pages themselves, are 78, 80, 82, and 93 respectively (pp.76–77 are still "Party
Talk" content). This is a consistent ~2–3-page TOC/content offset specific to the second half of the
book. **Page citations below use the actual observed printed page headers, not the TOC numbers.**

**Chapter title spelling:** the Contents page prints "13. Lover's Language" (singular possessive) but
the chapter's own running headers consistently print "Lovers' Language" (plural possessive) — both
forms are from the source itself; used as printed per occurrence below.

**No handwritten marginalia was found anywhere in this scan** — all content on every page is clean
typeset print matching the surrounding page; this is an unused/clean copy. Per the vision-reading
guard, everything below is genuinely printed source content.

**Dual-romanization convention (book-wide, methodology note):** every single phrase entry in this book
is given in **three parallel forms**: Hangul script, a bold-italic semi-standardized romanization
(e.g. *han-gungmallo haja!*), and a plain-italic **phonetic English-respelling** of the same phrase
for readers with no Korean background (e.g. *hahn-goong-mahllaw hahjah!*). The vocabulary table below
uses the bold-italic romanized form as the `Term` (the more linguistically legible of the two), per
copyright discipline — the phonetic-respelling layer is not reproduced row-by-row but is described
here as a structural feature of the whole source, and quoted selectively in Notes where it is itself
interesting (e.g. disambiguating a genuinely unusual pronunciation).

**Register framing:** the book's own back-cover blurb frames its whole project as teaching "the
colloquial language Koreans really use," distinct from more formal phrasebooks — so most entries
below default to `colloquial` unless the book itself explicitly marks a phrase `(polite)`/`(intimate)`,
or an entire section banner states its register (Chapter 10's "All the expressions in this section are
polite speech," p.65, which is honored below by tagging that chapter's entries `formal`), or content is
explicit profanity (`taboo`) or an explicitly flagged affectionate-vulgar register (`slang`).

**Morpheme breakdown.** Korean is agglutinative on the verb but the vast majority of this book's
content is fixed conversational phrases rather than word-internal morphology the mechanics-analysis
phase would need decomposed. Two items are worth flagging:

> **jot-kka** = jot ("penis," taboo noun) + kka ("kick," imperative/contracted verb form) — a
> compound-profanity formation ("fuck off," lit. "kick [my] penis"), p.57. The same jot root recurs
> compounded with -kkateun ("looks-like") in jot-kkateun saekki/nom "wanker" (p.58/62) and with -tto
> ("even") in jot-tto anin saekki "you piece of shit" (p.60) — a single taboo noun root generating a
> small productive family of insults via different suffixation, a clean case study for the
> mechanics-analysis phase.

> **namjachin-gu / yeojachin-gu** = namja ("man") / yeoja ("woman") + chin-gu ("friend") — the regular
> compounding pattern for "boyfriend"/"girlfriend," p.67; a transparent two-morpheme compound.

## Grammar points

### Dual-layer romanization (book-wide structural convention)
Every phrase in this book is printed in three parallel layers: Hangul, a bold-italic
semi-standardized romanization, and a plain-italic phonetic English-respelling of the same phrase
aimed at readers with no Korean background at all (e.g. *han-gungmallo haja!* / *hahn-goong-mahllaw
hahjah!*). The vocabulary table above uses only the bold-italic form as `Term`; the phonetic layer is
described here rather than reproduced per-row.

### Object vs. non-object "like" distinction (mame deureo vs. joa)
The book explicitly marks two different forms for "I like it" depending on whether the liked thing is
a concrete object or not: *na igeo mame deureo* (lit. "this enters my heart," for objects) vs. the
bare adjective *joa* (for non-objects) (p.48). This is a genuine semantic/grammatical fork worth
flagging for the mechanics-analysis phase — not a stylistic variant but a category distinction the
book itself calls out explicitly.

### Three-way demonstrative system
*igeo* ("this," near the speaker), *geugeo* ("that," close to the listener), and *jeogeo* ("that,"
away from both speaker and listener) form a three-way proximal/medial/distal demonstrative system,
explicitly glossed by the book itself (p.49) — the classic Korean (and broader East Asian) three-way
deictic contrast, distinct from English's two-way this/that.

### Presence/absence-marked third-person reference (jyae/gyae)
*na jyae ara* "I know him/her" is explicitly marked by the book as used **in the referent's
presence**, while *na gyae ara* (same gloss) is marked as used **in the referent's absence** (p.55) —
a pragmatic distinction English doesn't grammaticalize at all, worth flagging as a structural
constraint relevant to how a synthesized slang might (or might not) mark speech-act participant status.

### Gendered sibling-address terms
Korean kinship-address terms for older siblings are **speaker-gendered**, not referent-gendered: a
female speaker calls her older sister *eonni* and her older brother *oppa*; a male speaker calls his
older sister *nuna* and his older brother *hyeong* (pp.66–67). *Oppa* in particular carries a wider
quasi-romantic/affectionate extended use in contemporary Korean youth culture beyond literal
siblinghood (not explicitly stated by this particular book, but implied by its placement in the
"Party Talk" social-vocabulary chapter) — flagged as a candidate finding for further corpus-based
verification during Phase 3.

### Dual numeral systems (native-Korean vs. Sino-Korean)
This book shows **two parallel counting systems** used in different contexts: a native-Korean system
(e.g. *yeol daseot* "15," *seumul* "20," *seoreun* "30," used for ages, pp.70–72) and a separate
Sino-Korean system (*il, yi, sam, sa, o, yuk, chil, pal, gu, gong* "1–9, 0," used for phone numbers,
p.79). This dual-system typology — a well-documented real feature of Korean — is a directly relevant
precedent for how a synthesized conlang might productively maintain two numeral registers for
different grammatical contexts (counting vs. labeling/addressing).

### Politeness-register (-yo) marking, book-wide
Throughout the book, many phrase pairs are explicitly marked `(polite)` vs. unmarked/`(intimate)` by
the source itself (e.g. *han-gungmal haseyo?* polite vs. *han-gungmal hae?* intimate, p.31;
*gwaenchanayo* polite vs. *gwaenchana* casual, p.49) — the familiar Korean *-yo*/*-(seu)mnida*
politeness-suffix system surfacing as a recurring minimal-pair pattern across nearly every chapter,
distinct from (and compounding with) the lexical colloquial/slang/taboo register distinctions already
captured per-row above. Chapter 10 "Party Talk" (pp.64–77) is explicitly framed by the book itself as
entirely polite-register ("All the expressions in this section are polite speech," p.65), honored in
the Usage Tier column above by tagging that chapter's entries `formal` even though they are
otherwise ordinary, non-formal-register social content.

### saekki/jasik as a register-switching root
The morpheme *saekki* (lit. "animal's litter"/"offspring," a mildly vulgar root) functions as a
genuine register switch depending on context: used affectionately/jokingly among close friends in
Chapter 1 (pp.12–13), but as the single most productive compounding element for taboo-register
insults throughout Chapter 9 "Curses and Insults" (*gae saekki* "son of a bitch," *babo saekki* "son
of an idiot," *beollemando motan saekki* "lower than an insect," etc., pp.56–64) — directly parallel
to Tagalog's *toyo* finding (a single root carrying both an affectionate-informal and a vulgar sense
depending entirely on social context, not on any morphological marking) already flagged in this
project's Tagalog extraction. A strong candidate for the mechanics-analysis phase's register-switching
pattern inventory.

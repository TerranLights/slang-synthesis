## Grammar points

### Lesson 17 — Superlative of adjectives/adverbs, себя, боле́ть/больно́й
- **Superlative with са́мый**: са́мый "most" precedes the long-form adjective and declines like a
  hard adjective, agreeing with the noun in gender/case/number (са́мые после́дние ме́тоды; он
  име́ет де́ло с са́мыми серьёзными слу́чаями) — structurally parallel to the бо́лее-comparative
  construction from Lesson 16, except са́мый itself *does* decline (unlike invariant бо́лее).
  Са́мый can only combine with the long adjective form.
- **The eight declinable one-word comparatives (лу́чший, ху́дший, мла́дший, ста́рший, вы́сший,
  ни́зший, бо́льший, ме́ньший) each form their superlative either by adding са́мый** (са́мый
  лу́чший, са́мый ста́рший) **or, for лу́чший/ху́дший/мла́дший/ста́рший specifically, by simply
  using the comparative form alone with no са́мый at all** (Алексе́й Ива́нович — лу́чший врач
  "…is the best doctor," no са́мый needed) — a genuine case of the same surface form doing double
  duty as both comparative and superlative depending on context, disambiguated only by discourse.
  The literal-vs-figurative and people-vs-things splits already established for the comparatives
  in Lesson 16 (вы́сший/ни́зший = figurative only; ста́рший/мла́дший = people only) carry through
  identically into their corresponding superlatives (са́мый вы́сший is figurative-only; са́мый
  мла́дший/ста́рший is people-only) — the semantic conditioning on lexical choice is inherited
  wholesale from the comparative degree into the superlative degree, not re-specified independently.
- **Alternative -е́йший/-а́йший superlative**: formed by stripping -ый/-ий/-ой from the masculine
  adjective and adding -е́йший (крупне́йший, серьёзне́йший, тяжеле́йший), or -а́йший after
  ж/ч/ш/щ (вели́кий→велича́йший, глубо́кий→глубоча́йший, коро́ткий/кра́ткий→кратча́йший,
  лёгкий→легча́йший, стро́гий→строжа́йший, широ́кий→широча́йший — the familiar spelling rule
  again governing а vs. я after a hushing consonant). Critically, **this form is often not used as
  a true superlative at all but purely as an intensifier** ("a most serious case" = "a very serious
  case," not necessarily "the most serious case in existence") — СПИД — опа́снейшая боле́знь "AIDS
  is a most dangerous disease" doesn't claim AIDS is literally the single most dangerous disease,
  just emphasizes its danger. This intensifier-not-true-superlative use is a clean example for
  mechanics-analysis of a morphologically superlative-looking form being semantically bleached
  into a mere degree-intensifier — a common diachronic pathway (superlative > intensive/emphatic)
  worth flagging as a plausible source for an invented slang emphatic marker.
- **No true adverbial superlative**: unlike the adjective, Russian adverbs have no dedicated
  superlative morphology at all; the superlative sense is built periphrastically from the ordinary
  short comparative followed by всего́ ("than everything") or всех ("than everyone") as
  appropriate — Петро́в ста́вит диа́гноз точне́е всех "Petrov diagnoses most accurately [more
  accurately than everyone]"; Пробле́ма СПИДа беспоко́ит его́ бо́льше всего́ "The AIDS problem
  worries him most [more than everything]." This is structurally identical to the genitive-of-
  comparison strategy for "than" from Lesson 16 (comparative + genitive), just conventionally
  fixed to the universal-quantifier genitives всего́/всех rather than a specific compared object —
  effectively "more than [anything/anyone]" standing in for a missing superlative slot.
- **Боле́ть — two unrelated verbs sharing one infinitive spelling**: (1) боле́ть, 1st conjugation
  (боле́ю, боле́ешь…), means "to be ill (with X)," governing the instrumental of the illness (он
  боле́ет гри́ппом; в де́тстве я боле́л скарлати́ной) and usable in any person; (2) боле́ть, 2nd
  conjugation, used **exclusively in the 3rd person** (боли́т/боля́т), means "to ache/hurt," with
  the ailing body part as its **grammatical subject** and the sufferer marked by у + genitive (у
  меня́ боли́т голова́ "my head aches," literally "at me aches [the] head"; у него́ боля́т зу́бы
  "his teeth ache") — a genuine same-spelling different-conjugation homograph pair distinguished
  purely by conjugation class and person-restriction, not by any spelling difference in the
  infinitive itself. A strong example for mechanics-analysis of how two historically-related but
  synchronically-distinct verb paradigms can collapse to an identical citation form while remaining
  fully distinct grammatically.
- **Больно́й — long/short-form adjective split marking a genuine aspectual-flavored distinction**:
  the long form больно́й functions either as an ordinary adjective ("sick, painful": больно́й
  ребёнок, больно́е се́рдце) or, substantivized (used alone with no noun), as a noun meaning
  "patient/invalid" (eго́ больны́е выздора́вливают быстре́е всех "his patients recover
  quickest"). The **short form** (он бо́лен) specifically marks a **temporary state** ("he is
  [currently] unwell"), explicitly contrasted by the source with the long-form он больно́й, which
  would imply a more permanent condition/identity ("he is an invalid") — this is the familiar
  Russian long-form-permanent vs. short-form-temporary adjective contrast (seen with за́нят/
  за́нятый in Lesson 10) applied specifically to illness-vocabulary, and worth cross-referencing as
  a recurring pattern across the whole course: short predicative adjectives keep surfacing as the
  dedicated marker for "temporary state" versus the long form's "inherent property," a systematic
  enough pattern to be a strong model for mechanics-analysis of a conlang's own
  temporary/permanent-state adjectival distinction.
- **Себя́ — the universal reflexive pronoun**: refers back to the subject of its own clause
  regardless of person/gender/number (себя́ covers myself/yourself/himself/herself/ourselves/
  themselves identically) and, because it is definitionally never itself the subject, **has no
  nominative form at all** — a genuine gap in its case paradigm, not an accidental zero-form.
  Declines acc./gen. себя́, dat./prep. себе́, instr. собо́й (alternative собо́ю). Used
  idiomatically after чу́вствовать ("to feel [oneself] a certain way" — он чу́вствует себя́
  хорошо́, себя́ obligatory even though English "he feels well" has no reflexive object at all) and
  in с собо́й "with one/along" (он привёз с собо́й бага́ж "he brought his luggage with him") — both
  cases where Russian requires an overt reflexive object that English simply omits, a useful
  contrast point for mechanics-analysis of obligatory vs. optional reflexive marking across
  languages.

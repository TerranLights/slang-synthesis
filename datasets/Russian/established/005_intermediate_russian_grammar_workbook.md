**Morpheme breakdown.**

> **предписание** = пред- ("in front", "pre-") + пис- ("write", root of писать) + -ание ("-ing",
> nominalizing suffix) — "pre-writ-ing" → "prescript-ion". Illustrates how Russian verbal nouns in
> -ание/-ение can obscure their verbal root (contrast курение ← курить, where the stem is
> transparent) and shows the -ание suffix's productivity as a nominalizer, relevant to any later
> mechanics-analysis of Russian word formation.

> **побогаче** = по- (attenuating/comparative-softening prefix, "a little") + богат- (root of
> богатый "rich") + -е (simple comparative suffix) — "a little richer/better off". Demonstrates
> that Russian's simple comparative can itself take a further prefix to soften its degree, a
> productive pattern worth tracking for later slang-mechanics work on intensity marking.

## Grammar points

### Naming and forms of address (Unit 1)

Russian address forms vary systematically by relationship and formality: diminutive first names
(Витя, Катя) for children/peers with ты; long first name + patronymic with Вы for formal/unfamiliar
adults; surname-only address restricted to teacher→student or officer→subordinate contexts. Groups
are addressed with vocative-style phrases (ребята, дорогие коллеги!). Strangers are addressed by
description (молодой человек, девушка) or by господин/госпожа + function/title. Referring to a
third party uses the diminutive between intimates, but the patronymic-only or full name-and-
patronymic in more distant/formal relationships; newspaper headlines conventionally use full
name+surname or surname alone (p. 4–6).

### Declension of names, имени/от имени (Unit 1)

First names and patronymics decline as ordinary nouns; surnames in -ов/-ев/-ин decline as nouns
except for an adjectival instrumental singular (-ым); surnames in -ий/-ой decline fully as
adjectives. The genitive form имени ("of the name") follows a place name to indicate it is named
after someone (Театр имени Пушкина, often abbreviated им.); от имени + genitive expresses
"on behalf of" (p. 5–7).

### Greetings and congratulating; пусть (Unit 1)

Здравствуйте/Здравствуй are the basic greeting pair (formal-plural vs. informal-singular);
Доброе утро/Добрый день/Добрый вечер are time-of-day greetings, used less often than their
English equivalents; Привет is informal, typical among the young. Congratulating on an impending
holiday uses С наступающим праздником!; congratulating on an occasion uses the construction
поздравлять/поздравить кого с + instrumental, with "I wish" often simply omitted (С днём
рождения!). Пусть ("let") + present/future tense is the normal way to form 3rd-person (and
sometimes 1st-person plural) imperatives; пускай is a synonymous, slightly more colloquial variant
(p. 8–15).

### Letter etiquette; Вы and вы (Unit 1)

Formal letters open with Уважаемый/Глубокоуважаемый/Многоуважаемый + name-and-patronymic (or
господин/госпожа + surname for foreigners) and close with С уважением, followed by the writer's
title and signature. Informal letters open with Дорогой/Милый + name and close variously with a
bare name, Твой/Ваш + name, Пока, or intimate forms like Целую, Обнимаю. In writing, the polite Вы
form (and its oblique forms Вас, Вам, Вами, and the possessive Ваш) is capitalized (p. 10–13).

### Thanking, apologising, requesting, advising (Unit 2)

Спасибо (+ за + accusative for the cause, + dative for the person thanked) is the basic thanks
formula; благодарить is more formal. Thanking/apologising for *doing* something uses a что-clause
(informal) or за то, что-clause (more formal). Responses to thanks range from Пожалуйста/Не за
что/Не стоит (informal) to Не стоит благодарности (formal). Простить/извинить (typically
imperative: прости(те)/извини(те)) are the standard apology verbs, with за + accusative specifying
the cause. Просить/попросить (кого + infinitive) is used to ask a third party to do something
(person in accusative); советовать/посоветовать (кому + infinitive) to advise (person in dative)
(p. 16–21).

### Phone etiquette (Unit 2)

Three common ways to ask for someone on the phone: imperative of попросить/позвать + accusative of
the person, or the less formal Можно + accusative. Requests are often prefaced with Будьте
добры/Будьте любезны or a normal greeting. Responses are typically future perfective (Сейчас её
позову). If the answerer speaks first: Слушаю! (formal), Алло! (informal), Да (abrupt), or
Surname + слушает/у телефона (official) (p. 21–24).

### Expressing possession: у меня (Unit 3)

The у меня construction covers "to have", the possessive "my" (Мама у меня очень инициативная),
"in my home" (compare French chez moi), and "in one's own country". For ailments, у меня + noun
(У тебя грипп?) expresses "I have (illness)"; for body parts, the 3rd person of болеть + the body
part in nominative expresses "X hurts", with a separate у меня possessive construction supplying
the implied "my" (У меня болит голова) (p. 25–29).

### Expressing desire/subjunctive: чтобы (Unit 3)

Хотеть + infinitive is used when the subject wants to do something themselves; хотеть + чтобы +
subjunctive (=past-tense form) is used when the subject wants someone *else* to do something,
regardless of the main clause's own tense (Я хочу/хотела/хотела бы, чтобы Маша была там). Чтобы is
always preceded by a comma. Other desire verbs (e.g. мечтать) take the same чтобы construction
(p. 28–30).

### Modal constructions for suggestions (Unit 3)

Russian expresses "should"/"must" via several syntactic types: impersonal + infinitive (надо,
лучше, нельзя, не надо, не нужно, не следует); negative imperative (imperfective aspect, e.g. Не
гуляйте...); short-form adjectival predicates (должен, and short-form past passive participles
like противопоказано) (p. 32–33).

### Verbal nouns in -ание/-ение (Unit 3)

Nouns in -ание/-ение/-яние often correspond to English "-ing" nominalizations and denote the
activity of their corresponding verb, though the noun stem can diverge from the infinitive stem
(поведение ← (по)вести себя; дыхание ← дышать). Even when the verbal root isn't immediately
recognizable, morphological dissection often reveals the meaning (p. 33–34).

### Infinitive constructions for offers/advice; partitive genitive (Unit 3)

A bare perfective infinitive can offer to do something for someone (Налить вам водки?) or ask for
advice (Написать ей письмо?); responses are typically Пожалуйста/Спасибо, пожалуйста/Спасибо, нет,
or an imperative. The person seeking/receiving the offer goes into the dative if specified. The
partitive genitive (водки rather than accusative) marks "some (of a substance)" (p. 34–36).

### Interrogative какой; relative clauses with который/кто/что (Unit 4)

Какой (agreeing in gender/number/case) asks "which/what kind of" about the entity under discussion.
Который is the general relative pronoun, agreeing in gender/number with its antecedent but taking
its case from its function in the relative clause; что as relative pronoun is restricted to
inanimate antecedents and only nominative/accusative when the antecedent is a noun (p. 38–40).

### Asking questions with ли; indefinite particles -то/-нибудь (Unit 4)

Ли marks a more formal question type, inverting normal subject-verb order (Пойдёте ли вы...?) or
appearing as the second element after the focused word/phrase. -то and -нибудь both mark
indefiniteness on interrogative pronouns/adverbs, but -нибудь is more thoroughly indefinite (no
element of speaker knowledge) than -то; rules of thumb: -то tends toward present/past tense
statements, -нибудь toward questions, future tense, imperatives, wishes, and conditionals — though
exceptions are common (p. 43–46).

### Relative clauses with тот/та/те + который/кто ("the one who"); он who/whoever (Unit 5)

Тот, который/кто constructions express "the one who"; кто as relative pronoun always takes a
masculine predicate regardless of its antecedent's gender, though a plural antecedent (те) may
take a singular or plural predicate. Кто alone can stand for тот, кто in proverbs/sayings, meaning
"whoever" (p. 48–51).

### The interrogative and conjunction как (Unit 5)

Как performs several distinct functions: eliciting opinion or manner-of-feeling, asking directions,
and (as a conjunction) expressing comparison (белый как снег), or the notion of "in what
capacity/guise" something is perceived (Говорю с ним как с другом) (p. 51–54).

### Verbs of motion: идти/ходить, ехать/ездить (Unit 6)

Ехать/ездить denote non-foot motion; идти/ходить denote foot motion (or general attendance, e.g.
Я хожу в школу). Of each imperfective pair, only the first member (идти/ехать) indicates motion in
progress at a stated time; the second member (ходить/ездить) covers multidirectional motion,
regular/habitual motion, ability to walk, or "attending". In the past tense, indeterminate forms
(ходил, ездил) describe a completed round trip or general "having been" somewhere (synonymous with
был), while determinate forms (шёл, ехал) describe being en route at a given moment (p. 55–61).

### Conditional/temporal clauses: когда, если, как только (Unit 6)

When когда means "whenever", both clauses use present or imperfective past; when it means "as soon
as", the когда-clause takes future tense and the main clause takes perfective imperative or future.
Если ("if") and как только ("as soon as") pattern the same way as this second usage of когда.
Conditional and main clauses are always comma-separated (p. 61–65).

### Sequencing narratives: aspect, adverbs of time, prefixed verbs of motion (Unit 7)

Perfective verbs form the narrative "backbone" of a story, sequencing events and marking closure/
onset; imperfective verbs provide background context or concurrent framing activity. Пока means
"while" (imperfective in the subordinate clause); пока не/до тех пор пока не mean "until" (perfective
in the subordinate clause, marking a transitional endpoint). Aspectual rules of thumb for
infinitives: beginning/ending/continuing verbs take imperfective infinitives; open-ended activities
take imperfective; well-defined-endpoint activities take perfective. Prefixed perfective verbs of
motion (в-/во-, до-, пере-, подо-, по-, при-, у-, etc.) structure narrative movement; an
imperfective prefixed verb of motion (e.g. приезжала) signals an action done and undone (the
subject came and later left again) (p. 67–76).

### Word order for narrative focus (Unit 7, 9)

Where the subject is already known, subject precedes verb; where a new/unknown subject is being
introduced, verb precedes subject (Потом пришёл Эля...). Splitting a noun-phrase predicate
(Человек я небедный) shifts sentence focus onto the qualifying adjective, answering "what kind of
person is this?" (p. 75–76, 96).

### Sequence of tenses; parenthetic words; nouns in apposition (Unit 8–9)

Unlike English, the tense of a Russian subordinate clause reporting speech/thought/perception is
not shifted to match the main clause's tense. Parenthetic words (во-первых, к счастью, наконец,
скажите) are always comma-bracketed and serve discourse functions (maintaining contact with the
addressee, expressing attitude, structuring the discourse) beyond their literal content. A noun in
apposition (bracketed by commas/dashes, agreeing in case with what it modifies) clarifies reference,
e.g. Долго её, покупку, рассматривали (p. 79–89, 97).

### Metro-station declension; на минутку; раз-adverbs (Unit 8–9)

Metro station names that are nouns decline normally; those that are adjectival in form (agreeing
with the implicit feminine станция) decline as feminine adjectives — but when the word станция
itself is present, the station name stays in the nominative (as with all quoted names: books,
newspapers, orbital stations). На + accusative expresses a stated duration ("for a minute"); раз
generates a family of time adverbs (ещё раз, в шестой раз, не раз, ни разу не) and is one of a
small set of nouns whose genitive plural equals the nominative singular (p. 88, 91–93).

### Comparative degree of adjectives (Unit 9)

Russian has a complex comparative (более/менее + adjective, usable attributively or predicatively)
and a simple comparative (adjectival stem + -ее/-е suffix, predicative only, invariable for
gender/number). The compared object takes either the genitive of comparison or a чем-clause. Гораздо
intensifies a comparison; the prefix по- de-intensifies it (побогаче). The magnitude of a difference
is expressed with на + accusative (p. 98–100).

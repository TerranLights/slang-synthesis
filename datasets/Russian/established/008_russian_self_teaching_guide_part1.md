12–171 correspond to printed pages 1–160; verified empirically against the book's own page-footer
numbers, not assumed from a fixed offset).

**Coverage note:** This source has a genuine PDF text layer (`pdftotext -layout` produced clean,
correctly-ordered Cyrillic and Latin text throughout — no font-substitution cipher, no vision-reading
needed, no marginalia risk). All grammar points and drill-independent vocabulary from chapters 1–9
(through p. 160) are captured below. Repeated drill/exercise sentences and answer keys that
introduce no new vocabulary or grammar were skipped per the coverage rule, **except** where a drill
answer key itself was the only place a form appeared (e.g., irregular past-tense/aspect forms
surfaced only in an answer key) — those were captured. **Proper-noun lists are condensed rather than
transcribed row-by-row**: the book includes long enumerations of personal given names/nicknames (~90
names across three tables), U.S. states (~50), and country names (~36) as vocabulary-recognition
drills. These are cultural/proper-noun recognition lists rather than lexical items with independent
grammatical behavior, so each list is represented as one summary row noting its scope, pattern, and
page location rather than one row per name — full enumeration remains in the cited source pages if a
later synthesis pass needs it. All genuine common-noun/verb/adjective vocabulary, and every explicit
usage/register note the source itself makes, is captured in full.

No dialectal/regional annotation tags (à la B/C/S-style) appear in this source — it is a single
General-American-authored pedagogical grammar of Standard Russian, not a comparative or
sociolinguistic source, so the `Attested Region`/`Geographic Scope` columns are `—` throughout except
where the text itself flags a regional pronunciation variant (noted below, e.g. Moscow vs. Petersburg
pronunciation of щ).

## Grammar points

### Chapter 1 — The Russian Alphabet

**Cyrillic inventory and origin.** 33 letters for 31 sounds plus 2 signless letters (ъ hard sign, ь
soft sign). Roughly a third are visually/phonetically close to Roman letters, a third derive from
Greek (п = pi, р = rho), and the remainder (some of Hebrew origin, e.g. ш) were created for sounds
with no Greek counterpart (p. 1).

**Hard/soft vowel pairs.** Ten letters realize five vowel sounds in hard/soft pairs: а/я, э/е, ы/и,
о/ё, у/ю. Hard vowels open a word or follow hard consonants; soft vowels follow soft consonants or
signal a word-initial [y] glide (p. 2).

**Vowel reduction (akanye/ikanye).** Unstressed а and о both reduce toward a schwa-like [ə] except
in the first pretonic syllable, where о reduces to [a]; unstressed е and я reduce toward [i] (pp.
4–5). This is presented with full phonetic transcriptions of multisyllabic examples (хорошо →
[kherasho], etc.).

**Consonant hardness/softness and the seven-letter spelling rule.** Most Russian consonants come in
hard/soft pairs; ж, ц, ш are always hard and ч, щ, й are always hard... (source: "always soft" —
correcting: ч, щ, й are always soft). Because of this, orthography enforces: *after ж, ч, ш, щ and
the velars к, г, х, never write ы, я, or ю — write и, а, or у instead* (p. 3). This single rule
recurs as the load-bearing constraint behind noun-plural formation, adjective endings, and verb
endings throughout the rest of the book.

**Voicing/devoicing.** Word-final obstruents devoice (б/п, д/т, в/ф, з/с, г/к, ж/ш pairs), so pairs
like *bank*/*bang* or *have*/*half* sound identical to Russian ears when devoiced in English (p. 4).

**Regional pronunciation variant, explicitly marked by the source.** щ is realized as a soft single
[sh] in the Moscow/Standard pronunciation but as [shch] in the Petersburg pronunciation; the source
notes the Petersburg variant is "actively discouraged" by Moscow State University's language faculty
and by teachers of Russian abroad (p. 4) — this is the one explicit regional-register annotation in
this chunk.

**Transliteration conventions** (я/ё/ю → ya/yo/yu; и/ы → i/y; ш → sh, щ → shch; soft/hard signs as
single/double apostrophe) are given as the book's own convention, not a claim about any standard (pp.
6–7).

### Chapter 2 — The Noun

**Three genders, six cases, animacy.** Russian nouns are masculine/feminine/neuter, singular/plural,
and decline across six cases (nominative, accusative, genitive, prepositional, dative, instrumental)
— itself a reduction of the historical eight-case system that survives partially in the "locative"
and "partitive genitive" subcases discussed later. Animacy (human/animal referent vs. thing) governs
accusative-case syncretism (p. 20 onward; fully worked out in ch. 3).

**Gender by ending, with exceptions.** Masculine = consonant "zero ending" (hard or soft); feminine =
-а/-я or soft sign (must memorize which soft-sign nouns are feminine — see the -ь rule below);
neuter = -о/-е/-ё or the rare -мя class. A small epicene class (-а nouns referring to either sex,
often historically pejorative — сирота, убийца, пьяница, растяпа, обжора, неряха, невежа, калека,
лежебока, левша, contrasted with positive умница/работяга) takes its gender from the referent (pp.
20–22).

**Predictable feminine -ь nouns:** if the consonant before the soft sign is ж, ч, ш, or щ, the noun is
feminine (речь, глушь, вещь, мышь, мелочь, ложь, рожь, помощь, молодёжь). Abstract nouns in -ость are
also reliably feminine. All *other* soft-sign nouns must be individually memorized for gender
(любовь, осень, дверь, треть, смерть, национальность, мебель, степь, площадь, соль, рысь, очередь)
(p. 22).

**Masculine plural formation** is the most exception-laden: regular -ы/-и; a "fleeting vowel" class
(отец→отцы, американец→американцы, орёл→орлы) that drops a stem vowel before the plural ending;
soft-sign/-й nouns → -и; -анин/-янин nouns drop -ин and take -е (англичанин→англичане, but a
few non-ан-infixed -ин nouns like татарин→татары, господин→господа are irregular); -ёнок (mostly
baby-animal nouns) → -ята (гусёнок→гусята, ребёнок→ребята as a colloquial "kids" sense — контраст
with the true suppletive plural дети); a common-word class taking stressed -а/-я (дом→дома,
город→города, ...) that is actively expanding to new borrowings (компьютера, принтера — flagged by
the source as substandard but common in speech); an -ья plural class (брат→братья, друг→друзья,
муж→мужья, сын→сыновья, with stem changes); doublets where concrete/everyday meaning takes the
irregular plural and abstract/figurative meaning takes the regular one (пропуска "permits" vs.
пропуски "omissions"; счета "bills" vs. счёты "abacus"); an -ей class that inserts a soft sign in all
oblique forms (муравей→муравьи); and a handful of fully unpredictable plurals (человек→люди,
ребёнок→дети, цветок→цветы, чёрт→черти, сосед→соседи — the last two being the only nouns hard
throughout the singular but soft throughout the plural) (pp. 24–27).

**Neuter plural formation:** regular -о/-е → -а/-я, often with a stress shift (окно→окна); an
irregular -ья class (дерево→деревья, крыло→крылья); the six -мя nouns expand their stem before -а
(время→времена); a diminutive -ко class drops -о and adds -и (окошко→окошки); and several relic
dual-number nouns (плечо→плечи, колено→колени, ухо→уши, and the archaic око→очи "eye") (pp. 28–30).

**Feminine plural formation** is comparatively regular (-а→ы, -я→и, soft sign→и plus dropped sign),
with three lexical exceptions (мать/дочь expand the stem: матери/дочери; курица drops a syllable:
куры) and a common stress-shift pattern in disyllabic feminines that distinguishes nominative plural
from genitive singular (сестра: сестры́ [gen. sg.] vs. сёстры [nom. pl.]) (pp. 30–31).

**Singularia/pluralia tantum.** Certain fruits/vegetables are singular-only in Russian where English
pluralizes (картофель "potatoes," морковь "carrots" — a diminutive singular is used to individuate:
морковка "a carrot"). A substantial list of plurale-tantum nouns has no singular counterpart at all
(ножницы, брюки, каникулы, очки, деньги, похороны, сутки, чернила, шахматы, джунгли, духи, счёты, щи,
сумерки, сани, обои, ворота, дрова, войска, прения, вилы, дрожжи, among others) (pp. 31–32).

**Animacy peculiarities.** Toys and other objects can be *conceived* as animate by a speaker (a
child's teddy bear). Collective nouns (народ, отряд, войска) are usually inanimate even when
referring to people; playing-card pieces (туз, король, валет, ферзь) and a few other conceptual
categories (кумир, звезда "movie star," автомат "robot," числитель/знаменатель) count as animate.
Crucially, the animate/inanimate accusative-genitive syncretism applies to masculine nouns *only* in
the singular, but to *all genders* in the plural — so a neuter noun like насекомое ("insect") is
grammatically inanimate in the singular (Он убил насекомое) but animate in the plural (Он убил
насекомых) (pp. 33–34).

### Chapter 3 — The Accusative Case

**Function.** Primarily marks the direct object of a transitive verb — and Russian's notion of
"transitive" doesn't always match English (e.g. "to telephone someone" or "to help someone" are
dative-governed in Russian, not accusative) (p. 43).

**Endings.** Only feminine -а/-я nouns and masculine *animate* nouns have distinct accusative
endings; masculine inanimate, all neuter, and feminine soft-sign nouns are identical to the
nominative. In the *plural*, this animate/inanimate distinction extends to all genders, so all
inanimate plural nouns match the nominative plural, while all animate plural nouns (regardless of
original gender) match the genitive plural (pp. 43–45).

**Possessive pronoun-adjectives** (мой/твой/наш/ваш) agree in gender/number/case like true
adjectives; third-person его/её/их do not agree at all (invariant) (p. 42).

**Accusative after verbs of motion.** Russian keeps the *same* preposition (в, на) for both static
location and motion, shifting only the case (в банке [prepositional, "at the bank"] vs. в банк
[accusative, "to the bank"]). A destination that is a *person* instead takes dative + к, not
accusative (Он идёт к бабушке). за + accusative expresses "behind/beyond" with motion verbs (за
город, за горизонт) (pp. 46–47).

**Verb-plus-preposition idioms taking accusative** (a fixed list, not synchronically transparent):
превращаться/-иться в, верить/по- в, стрелять в, играть в, вмешиваться/вмешаться в, поступать/-ить
в, жаловаться/по- на, сердиться/рас- на, отвечать/ответить на, надеяться на, делить/раз- на,
нападать/напасть на (p. 47).

**Time expressions.** в + accusative for clock times and days of the week (with the half-hour
irregularly taking в + *prepositional*: в половине седьмого); через + accusative = "after X elapses";
на + accusative = a forward-looking duration ("for a week," planned in advance); a bare accusative
(no preposition) with каждый and весь (каждый день, всю жизнь) — this last is the litmus test:
English "for" can be dropped only with the duration-of-the-verb's-own-action sense (Она читала два
часа) but not with the на-marked forward-planned sense (Она пошла в библиотеку на два часа) (pp.
48–49).

### Chapter 4 — The Adjective

**Agreement and gender endings.** Adjectives agree fully with their noun in gender/number/case.
Masculine default ending -ый shifts to -ий under the seven-letter spelling rule (хороший), to
stressed -ой (большой, дорогой), or to soft -ий for soft-stem adjectives (a fixed list given:
утренний, зимний, весенний, ранний, сегодняшний, вчерашний, and ~25 more, almost all stemmed in -н-
except карий "hazel/brown-eyed"). Neuter -ое becomes -ее under the five-letter spelling rule or in
soft adjectives; feminine -ая becomes -яя only for soft adjectives. Plural collapses gender entirely,
taking -ые or -ие (spelling-rule/soft) (pp. 61–64).

**Demonstrative adjectives.** этот has the broader range (roughly = English "this" or unmarked
"that"); тот is reserved for explicit contrast once этот has already been used in the same sentence
— the source frames overuse of English "that" without such contrast as a common learner error to
avoid transferring (p. 64).

**Comparative degree — simple (short) form**, used predicatively only, never declined. Regular
formation adds -ее (интереснее); a consonant-alternation subclass (г/к/х/д/т/ст-stems) adds a single
stressed -е with mutation (дорогой→дороже, строгий→строже, тихий→тише); a -к/-ок-dropping subclass
alternates the final consonant (низкий→ниже, высокий→выше); and a further irregular-exception list
(плохой→хуже, хороший→лучше, большой→больше, маленький→меньше, and others) (pp. 65–68). A whole class
of adjectives — those in -ск-, -ов-/-ев-, deverbal -лый adjectives, and a short miscellaneous list
(горький, дерзкий, робкий, липкий, скользкий, ветхий, гордый, лишний, плоский, больной, гнилой,
странный) — form *no* simple comparative at all and must use the compound form instead (p. 67).
Idiomatic intensifiers: горазда/намного (much Xer), по- prefix (a little Xer: подешевле), как можно
+ comparative ("as X as possible"), чем...тем correlative ("the Xer, the Xer") (pp. 69–70).

**Comparative degree — compound (long) form**, used attributively (declined, agrees with its noun),
formed with более/менее + the unchanged positive adjective. This is the *only* form available for
adjectives lacking a simple comparative, and may substitute for the simple form even where one
exists (p. 71).

**Superlative degree — compound form** (самый + adjective, both declined) is the default, unmarked,
absolute superlative; наиболее/наименее are bookish/official synonyms for самый. "The best/worst" can
be either самый хороший/самый лучший or самый плохой/самый худший, the "лучший/худший" phrasing being
more common (pp. 72–73). Several syntactic variants exist for "the Xest of Y": в + prepositional,
genitive alone, средь + genitive, из + genitive (the last most common in writing); один из + genitive
plural expresses "one of the Xest" (pp. 73–75).

**Superlative degree — simple form**, a suffixed form (-айший after г/к/х-mutation, -ейший otherwise,
plus an irregular list: худший, меньший, кратчайший, лучший, тягчайший, дражайший) that expresses
subjective emphasis ("a most X," "an extremely X") rather than strict absolute ranking, and is
bookish/literary — rarely heard in ordinary speech except in fixed set expressions (в ближайшее
время, с величайшим удовольствием, с глубочайшим уважением, высшее образование, etc.) (pp. 75–77).
An alternative colloquial superlative uses the comparative + всех/всего ("richer than everyone" =
"richest of all") (p. 77).

**Long-form vs. short-form adjectives.** Short forms are predicative-only, undeclined, and often
signal an impermanent/contingent state (Она была красива "she was beautiful [then]" vs. long-form
она была красивая, more intrinsic); can convey "excessively so" (Эти туфли мне малы = "too small");
and are required after an infinitive, чтобы, prepositions, or when the adjective governs an oblique
case (Я готов помогать вам, Он слишком болен, чтобы прийти, Вы довольны моей работой). Several
adjectives shift meaning entirely between long and short form: прав "right" vs. правый "right-wing";
жива "alive" vs. живая "lively"; сердит "angry" vs. сердитый "ill-tempered"; занята "busy [now]" vs.
занятая "a busy person"; свободна "free [today]" vs. свободная "a free country"; уверен "sure of X"
vs. уверенный "self-assured." рад/рада/рады exists in short form only (pp. 78–79).

**Substantivized adjectives** (an adjective's original noun is dropped through historical usage,
leaving the adjective to function as the noun, still declining as an adjective): столовая, гостиная,
передняя, ванная, операционная, булочная, молочная, пивная, больной, взрослый, военный, рабочий,
насекомое, животное, шампанское, первое/второе/третье/сладкое (courses of a meal) (pp. 80–81).
Deverbal (participle-derived) adjectives/substantives form a related, much larger, open class
(данные, начинающий, учащийся, обвиняемый, ископаемые, трудящийся, говорящий, курящий,
млекопитающее, пресмыкающееся, and adjective-only participle-derivatives like блестящий, бывший,
невыносимый, рассеянный, открытый/закрытый, выдающийся, небьющийся, забытый, одетый,
приближающийся) (p. 81).

### Chapter 5 — The Verb

**Five tenses total**, a consequence of the aspect system fully developed in ch. 9: one present
(imperfective only), two past (perfective/imperfective), two future (perfective/imperfective) (p.
89).

**Infinitive endings:** overwhelmingly -ть, with smaller -ти and -чь classes; no others exist (p. 90).

**Past tense formation.** Regular rule: replace -ть with -л, then adjust for gender/number
(-л/-ла/-ло/-ли); мы/вы/они always take the plural form even when вы addresses one person
politely. -ся verbs keep -ся after consonants, -сь after vowels (родился/родилась). есть is
irregularly ел/ела/ело/ели rather than the expected regular pattern (pp. 90–91). Several
morphologically distinct subclasses: consonant+ть verbs with unpredictable past stems (грызть→
грыз/ла; красть→крал/а; прочесть→прочёл/прочла — must be memorized/looked up); -нуть/-ереть verbs
that drop the suffix entirely in the past (достигнуть→достиг/ла; умереть→умер/умерла, plus
исчезнуть, мёрзнуть, погибнуть, привыкнуть); non-ть infinitives whose past stems must be given in
the glossary (идти→шёл/шла, нести→нёс/несла, везти→вёз/везла, вести→вёл/вела, зайти→зашёл/зашла,
расти→рос/росла [note the а→о vowel change from present to past], грести→грёб/гребла); and -чь verbs
whose present-tense к/г↔ч/ж stem alternation carries directly into the past tense, which equals the
present-tense stem (печь: пеку→пёк; стричь: стригу→стриг) (pp. 90–92).

**Stress patterns in the past tense:** always-stem, always-ending, and a shifting pattern where only
the feminine form takes end stress (жить: жил/жила́/жи́ло/жи́ли). Verbs in -нять/-дать typically shift
stress to the *prefix* in all forms but the feminine (понять→по́нял/поняла́/по́няло/по́няли) (pp.
91–92).

**Verbs of motion — introduction.** Non-prefixed indeterminate verbs of motion (ходил, ездил) in the
past tense function as a synonym of "was [at a location]" that additionally implies a completed round
trip: Вчера я был в библиотеке ("I was at the library") vs. Вчера я ходил в библиотеку ("I went to
the library [and have returned]") convey the same core information via different grammatical means
(p. 93). ходил is the default verb for on-foot-reachable destinations; ездил is required for
destinations that logically require a vehicle (cities, countries) (p. 93).

### Chapter 6 — The Prepositional Case

**Naming and scope.** So named because it never occurs without a preposition (contra some older
grammars' "locative" label, which the source treats as a *subcase* of the prepositional restricted to
в/на + a specific masculine-noun list — see below) (p. 101).

**Endings.** Singular: -е for all genders, except masculines in -ий, neuters in -ие, feminines in -ия,
and feminines in soft sign — all of which take -и instead. Plural: universally -ах/-ях regardless of
gender (p. 102). Adjectives: masculine/neuter -ом, feminine -ой, plural -ых, subject to the five- and
seven-letter spelling rules (p. 103).

**В vs. на for location.** В = "in/within" an enclosed space; на = "on (a surface)." Both can
translate English "at": на for events/functions where people gather (на собрании, на лекции, на
свадьбе) and for certain public buildings framed as functional destinations (на почте, на вокзале, на
заводе); в for enclosed institutional buildings (в университете, в библиотеке, в аптеке, в банке).
у + genitive is the idiom for "at [a person]'s place" (у Ивана) (pp. 104–105). в becomes во before
consonant clusters, especially [f]/[v]-initial ones (во Флориде, во всём) (p. 104).

**Idiomatic в:** time expressions (в апреле, в этом году, в котором часу — but the half-hour is the
lone exception, taking в + *prepositional*: в половине седьмого); wearing clothes (она была в чёрном
платье); certain moods (в востоóрге, в горе); and a fixed list of в-governing verbs (нуждаться,
обвинять, ошибаться, подозревать, признаваться, сомневаться, убеждать, уверять, участвовать — nouns
derived from these verbs keep the same government) (pp. 104–105).

**Idiomatic на:** geographic areas without well-defined borders (mountain ranges, peninsulas, islands,
compass points: на Кавказе, на Украине, на Гавайях, на Аляске); transportation and transit hubs (на
поезде, на вокзале, на станции); certain governing verbs (играть на [instrument], говорить/писать на
[language], жениться на, настаивать на); and a miscellany of fixed phrases (на свежем воздухе, на
солнце, на рассвете, на пенсии, на кладбище) (pp. 105–107).

**о/об/обо, при, по as further prepositional-case governors.** о = "about/concerning" (об before a
non-iotated vowel; the irregular обо мне/всём/всех); the source notes про + accusative is more
common in conversational registers than о + prepositional, which reads as more literary/written (p.
105 fn., p. 108). при covers three distinct senses — physical attachment/location ("in the area of");
temporal "during the reign/era of" (при Петре Первом); and conditional "given/with" (при наличии, при
помощи) — plus set idiomatic uses (при детях, при свидетелях) (p. 109). по = "upon, after" in a
formal register (по окончании, по приезде), also used with expressions of grieving (плакать по муже)
(p. 109).

**The "locative" subcase.** A closed, memorization-required list of masculine nouns take a *stressed*
-у/-ю ending (rather than the regular -е/-и) but *only* with the prepositions в and на, and *only* in
a literal locative (location) sense — the same noun reverts to the regular prepositional ending with
о/при/по, or when used in a non-literal sense (в кругу "within a circle" vs. в круге "on the
circumference of a circle"; в углу "in the corner" vs. в угле "in the [geometric] angle") (pp.
110–111). This is presented as one of the arguments some grammarians use for treating Russian as
having eight cases rather than six (alongside the partitive genitive).

### Chapter 7 — Present Tense Verbs

**Day-of-week and time-of-day idioms.** в + accusative for a single occurrence on a given day (в
понедельник); по + dative plural for habitual/recurring occurrence (по понедельникам = "on Mondays
[regularly]"), extended to parts of the day (по утрам, по вечерам). Clock times use genitive
expressions of the part of day (утра, дня, вечера, ночи) when paired with a specific hour, but
instrumental forms (утром, днём, вечером, ночью) when describing an extended part of a single day (p.
120). Russian uses the 24-hour clock pervasively in written schedules (not just military/scientific
contexts as in English) but reads clock times aloud using only 1–12 (10:00 P.M. = "десять часов
вечера") (p. 120). "From X to Y o'clock" requires the genitive case for the hour numbers (p. 121).

**Agentive sport-player suffix.** -ист(ка) productively derives "player of X" from a sport name
(теннис→теннисист(ка)), with a short irregular-suffix exception list (бокс→боксёр, гимнастика→
гимнаст, бег→бегун, стрельба→стрелец) (p. 121).

**First-conjugation verbs — the "royal mess."** The source explicitly frames 1st-conjugation as
resistant to a single classificatory system, and recommends memorizing the infinitive + 1st-person
singular + 2nd-person singular of any new verb, since these three forms predict everything else
(stress pattern, present stem, past stem) (p. 121). It then works through the major *productive*
subclasses one at a time:
- **Monosyllabic -ить verbs** (пить, бить, лить, шить and their prefixed derivatives): present stem =
  consonant + ь + stressed 1st-conj. endings (пью/пьёшь). жить is a lone exception, expanding to
  жив- (живу/живёшь) (p. 122).
- **-чь infinitives**: underlying -к- or -г- stems that mutate to ч/ж before е/ё (a general Old
  Russian ban on velars before front vowels) — печь (пеку/печёшь), стричь (стригу/стрижёшь), plus
  мочь, сечь, жечь, влечь, лечь, течь, волочь, толочь, помочь, запрячь. All end-stressed except a
  вы-prefixed perfective, which stresses the prefix instead (p. 123).
- **-овать/-евать infinitives**: an large, actively growing class (modeling English -ize) that
  replaces -ова-/-ева- with -у-/-ю- before regular unstressed endings (голосовать→голосую;
  плевать→плюю). New coinages cited from the contemporary press: парковать, прессинговать,
  приоритировать, лоббировать, информировать, тестировать (pp. 123–124).
- **-авать infinitives** drop -ва- entirely before stressed 1st-conj. endings (давать→даю, узнавать→
  узнаю, вставать→встаю), covering давать/-знавать/-ставать-family prefixed verbs (продавать,
  преподавать, передавать, раздавать, задавать, признавать, сознавать, переставать, доставать,
  уставать) (pp. 124–125).
- **-ывать/-ивать infinitives** conjugate fully regularly (like делать); the source notes many are
  "secondary imperfectives" — a perfective verb re-imperfectivized by suffixation (читать→прочитать→
  перечитать→перечитывать) (p. 125).
- **Consonant + -ать infinitives** split into three types: regular (работать-class); a
  permanent-consonant-mutation type (писать-class: пишу/пишешь, мутation holds throughout the whole
  conjugation, unlike 2nd-conjugation's first-person-only mutation); and a monosyllabic
  fleeting-vowel type (ждать-class: жду/ждёшь, often with a fleeting vowel: беру/берёшь) (pp.
  125–126).
- **-ять infinitives** split into a -я-retaining productive class (гулять, терять, добавлять,
  являться) and a -я-dropping class (лаять, таять, надеяться, смеяться); a handful of -ять verbs
  (стоять, бояться) are actually 2nd-conjugation and must be individually flagged (pp. 127–128).
- **-нять infinitives** (понимать/понять, занимать/занять, нанимать/нанять, принимать/принять,
  поднимать/поднять, отнимать/отнять, обнимать/обнять, снимать/снять): imperfective infinitive in
  -нимать, perfective in -нять, present/future perfective stem variably end-stressed or
  shifting-stressed depending on the specific verb (p. 128).
- **-еть deadjectival/denominal verbs** (краснеть, стареть, худеть, толстеть, каменеть): a
  productive class deriving inchoative "become X" verbs from adjectives/nouns, fully regular (p. 128).
- **-оть infinitives**: a very small class (молоть, бороться) (p. 128).
- **Monosyllabic -ыть infinitives** (мыть, крыть, выть, открыть and prefixed forms): all stem-stressed
  (мою/моешь); плыть is an exception (плыву/плывёшь) (p. 129).
- **Stressed -ти / consonant+ть infinitives** (идти, нести, красть, цвести, грызть): always end
  stressed (p. 129).
- A short list of **exceptional first-conjugation forms** given individually: начать/начну, стать
  (встать)/стану, взять/возьму, одеть/одену, петь/пою, умереть/умру, гнить/гнию, брить/брею,
  ошибиться/ошибусь (p. 129).

**Truly irregular verbs** (mixed 1st/2nd conjugation features): дать, хотеть, есть, бежать — each
given its full paradigm since none is predictable from any rule (p. 130).

**Second-conjugation verbs — comparatively simple.** Only three infinitive shapes exist: -ить, -еть,
and fricative(ж/ч/ш/щ)+-ать. The five-letter spelling rule forces 1st-person-singular -у (not -ю) and
3rd-person-plural -ат (not -ят) after ж/ч/ш/щ/ц (говорю but молчу/молчат). Stress may be
stem/end/shifting, uncorrelated with conjugation type (p. 131). **Consonant alternation in
2nd-conjugation verbs occurs in the 1st-person singular only** (unlike 1st-conjugation's permanent
mutation) — the full mutation table: д,з,г→ж; т,к→ч; с,х→ш; ст,ск→щ; and all labials (б,п,в,ф,м) add
-л- (бл,пл,вл,фл,мл) (любить→люблю). No further exceptions exist beyond these two rules — the source
explicitly frames this as "the entire story," in contrast to first conjugation's sprawl (pp.
131–133). No monosyllabic verb is ever 2nd-conjugation.

### Chapter 8 — The Dative Case

**Frequency and scope.** The source claims the dative is the single most-used inflected case in
spoken Russian conversation ("nearly 60 percent of declinable words," p. 143) — used for indirect
objects, a fixed list of governing verbs, impersonal/modal constructions, and after certain
prepositions.

**Endings.** Singular: masculine/neuter -у/-ю (у for hard, ю for soft); feminine -е (consonant+а/я
stems) or -и (-ия/soft-sign stems). Adjectives: masculine/neuter -ому/-ему, feminine -ой/-ей (mirrors
the hard/soft & spelling-rule split throughout). Plural: **no exceptions at all** — every noun takes
-ам/-ям and every adjective takes -ым/-им regardless of gender (pp. 143–144). Personal/interrogative
pronoun datives are given as a full table (я→мне, ты→тебе, он/оно→ему, она→ей, мы→нам, вы→вам,
они→им, кто→кому, что→чему), memorably packed into one mnemonic sentence: "Я пишу тебе, ты пишешь
ему, он пишет ей, она пишет нам, мы пишем вам, вы пишете им, но никто не пишет мне!" (p. 144).

**Indirect object.** The core syntactic use: the recipient of a direct object (Он подарил сестре
цветы). для + genitive is available as an alternative framing of the same relationship ("for" rather
than "to") (pp. 144–145).

**Dative-governing verbs**, a fixed list to be memorized *with* the verb: помогать, звонить,
аплодировать, подарить, мешать, казаться, советовать, верить, надоесть, сниться, улыбаться,
хотеться, нравиться — several of these cross a systematic mismatch with English transitive/direct
constructions ("help someone," "call someone," "believe someone" are all English-accusative but
Russian-dative) (pp. 145–146). The source recommends learning case-government via a dictionary's ктo/
что notation for every new verb.

**Impersonal expressions of subjective state** use dative + a predicate adverb (мне холодно, literally
"[it is] cold to me," reframing "I am cold" so the described property belongs to the situation, not
the speaker — a slip into the nominative "я холодный" would instead mean "I am a cold [i.e.
emotionally cold] person") (pp. 145–146): холодно, тепло, жарко, интересно, скучно, грустно, весело,
жаль, больно, легко, страшно.

**Permission/necessity.** можно (permission granted) / нельзя (permission refused or prohibited) +
dative logical subject; надо/нужно (necessity, interchangeable) + dative + infinitive. A perfective
infinitive frames a single one-off necessary action; an imperfective infinitive frames a general,
recurring necessity (Ване надо помыть посуду [tonight] vs. Ване нужно мыть посуду [every evening]) (p.
146).

**Prepositions governing dative:** к and по are by far the most common; благодаря, согласно, вопреки,
навстречу are flagged as "most frequently encountered in reading" (i.e., more of a written/formal
register) (p. 147). по has a wide idiomatic range beyond simple "along" (Мы шли по улице), including
distributive "one each" (Каждый ученик получил по карандашу — "one pencil each") and habitual-time
senses (Ася работает по вечерам).

**Expression of opinion via dative** — Russian favors dative-passive framings (по-моему, мне думается)
over a literal "I think that" (я думаю, что), which the source frames as a broader stylistic tendency
away from I/me/my-centered phrasing (p. 147). Stress alone distinguishes the fixed idiom по-моему
from the fully-declined literal phrase по моему́ мнению.

**Age** is expressed via dative + numeral + лет/год/года (Мне 20 лет) (p. 148).

**A cluster of dative-based negative-existential idioms** using interrogative-based indefinites: нам
негде сидеть ("we have nowhere to sit"), мне не с кем говорить ("I have no one to talk to"), ей
нечего сказать ("she has nothing to say"), мне некогда ("I have no time") (p. 152) — worth flagging
for later mechanics analysis as a productive negative-idiom template (dative + не-prefixed
interrogative + infinitive).

**Feminine surname declension.** Russian feminine surnames in -ова/-ева/-ина/-ская decline like the
demonstrative pronoun этот, not like ordinary feminine nouns, so their dative is -ой (Софья
Толстая → Марине Цветаевой). Foreign-origin feminine surnames that don't end in a typical feminine
suffix are indeclinable altogether (Анна Ахматова писала Надежде Мандельштам) (p. 150) — a genuinely
useful register/gender-marking data point for later slang-mechanics work on how Russian marks gender
on borrowed/foreign names.

### Chapter 9 — Aspect of Verbs (through p. 160)

**Core framing.** Russian collapses onto five tenses (imperfective past/future, perfective
past/future, and a present that is imperfective-only) precisely because aspect, not tense alone,
carries the completion/process distinction English spreads across multiple periphrastic
constructions. The source frames imperfective as the *default*: perfective requires "a specific,
compelling reason"; absent one, use imperfective (p. 160).

**Perfective triggers.** Lexical markers уже ("already") and наконец ("finally") signal a
result/completion was awaited and point toward perfective aspect. An adverb that qualifies a
*result* (e.g. хорошо modifying "learned") is also a perfective cue (Comment 4, p. 163). Conversely,
totality markers like весь ("the whole [book]") force perfective even in a context that otherwise
reads as process-focused (Comment 2, p. 162).

**Imperfective's core senses**, worked out via close reading of a dialogue: (1) a simple statement
that an action took place, with no claim about whether it was completed (Я читал книгу — I did some
reading, possibly unfinished); (2) focus on *how* an action was carried out rather than on its result
(Вы читали со словарём? — asking about manner/process, not completion); (3) an attempted-but-possibly
-unsuccessful action, contrasted with the perfective's assertion of success — this pairing recurs
across several verbs with genuinely different English translations for each aspect: сдавать/сдать
экзамен ("take"/"pass" an exam), учить/выучить ("study"/"have learned"), решать/решить задачу
("work on"/"have solved"), звонить/позвонить ("try calling"/"call and reach") (pp. 160–164).

**Past tense is always completed, but that alone does not license perfective.** The source stresses
that even a definitionally-finished past action stays imperfective unless the speaker is specifically
foregrounding the *moment* of completion, not just the fact that the action happened (Я встал в семь
часов, using perfective, foregrounds the specific completion-moment "got up," vs. a bare narration of
past events which would default to imperfective) (p. 160).

**Present tense uses imperfective only** — perfective verbs, by asserting completion, cannot
coherently describe an in-progress present-tense action; conjugated "perfective present" forms are
grammatically future-tense, not present (p. 166, summarized at p. 160's chapter-opening framing and
reiterated in the Summary section at the chapter's end within this page range).

**Aspectual pairing is lexical, not fully productive:** every Russian verb concept requires
memorizing *two* forms (an imperfective and a perfective), which usually differ by prefix or suffix
but occasionally have unrelated roots (e.g. говорить/сказать, elsewhere in the book) — this pairing
must be learned per-verb, not derived by a general rule (p. 166 Summary, captured here as it falls at
the very end of the assigned page range).

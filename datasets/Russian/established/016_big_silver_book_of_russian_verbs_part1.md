**Source:** Jack E. Franke, Ph.D., *The Big Silver Book of Russian Verbs: 555 Fully Conjugated
Verbs* (McGraw-Hill, 2005), PDF pages 1–225 — covering the front-matter "Russian Tense Profiles"
grammar chapter (pp. i–32: Basics of Conjugation, Verbal Aspect, Tense, Conjugation, Mood,
Present/Past/Future Tense, Subjunctive Mood, Participles, Verbal Adverbs, Commands/Imperative,
Reflexive Verbs, Verbs of Motion) and the first 175 entries (of 555) of the alphabetically-ordered
"555 Fully Conjugated Verbs" dictionary, from **аплодировать** (entry 1, book p. 1) through
**интересоваться/заинтересоваться** (entry 175, book p. 175). Sibling files cover the remaining
entries (176–450 and 451–673 by PDF page assignment; entry/page numbering in the verb dictionary
does not track 1:1 with PDF page count due to duplicated header pages — see gotchas below).

**Coverage note:** This source has a real text layer (`pdftotext -layout` produced clean,
directly-usable Cyrillic and Latin text throughout — no font-substitution cipher, no garbled OCR).
Every grammar point in the assigned Tense Profiles chapter is captured below, paraphrased. Every
verb headword (aspectual infinitive pair) in the assigned dictionary range is captured as a
vocabulary row — this book *is* a vocabulary/grammar reference in verb form, so "every distinct
vocabulary item" here means every verb entry, not a sample. Full conjugation paradigm tables
(present/past/future/subjunctive/participle/verbal-adverb/imperative forms for each of the six
conjugation types and each irregular verb) are **not reproduced** — bulk-reproducing ~170 multi-row
conjugation tables would violate the copyright-discipline rule, and the paradigm mechanics are
already captured once, systematically, in the Grammar Points section below (the one-stem system,
the six conjugation types, and irregular-stem patterns apply uniformly to every verb in the
dictionary). Each vocabulary row instead records: the aspectual pair, gloss, conjugation type
classification, and any notable irregularity, stem change, or usage/government pattern the source
flags — this is the information that actually varies per verb and that mechanics-analysis will
need. Illustrative example sentences are quoted selectively (a handful of short, genuinely useful
ones), never bulk-reproduced from the source's "Usage" sections.

No vision-reading was needed (real text layer throughout); Vision Reading Confidence is `n/a` for
every row.

### PDF-extraction gotcha found in this chunk

`pdftotext -layout` intermittently duplicates the *previous* entry's header-line stem text onto the
following entry's header row, at page turns — a column-bleed artifact, not a cipher. Confirmed at
two points in this range:
- Book p. 166 (**избегать/избежать**, "to avoid"): the header row printed `stem: играй-/сыграй-/
  поиграй-` (correct only for the *preceding* entry, играть/сыграть/поиграть, p. 165). The
  verb's own conjugation table (избегаю, избегаешь…; избежал, избежала…) makes the real stem
  unambiguous: **избегай-/избежа-**. Recorded correctly in the table below.
- Book p. 170 (**изменять(ся)/изменить(ся)**, "to change, alter"): the header row printed `stem:
  издавай-/irreg.` (correct only for the preceding entry, издавать/издать, p. 169). The real stem,
  confirmed against the conjugation table on the entry's own continuation page, is
  **изменяй+(ся)/измени+(ся)**.

Both were verified against the entry's own present/past-tense conjugation rows before trusting the
correction, per this project's cipher/OCR verification discipline. Worth flagging for any sibling
subagent extracting adjacent page ranges in this same book: don't trust a header-row stem in
isolation if it looks identical to the immediately preceding entry — check the table underneath it.

Separately, several entries in this range occupy two physical pages with the header line repeated
verbatim on both (e.g. **бить(ся)/пробить(ся)** p. 6, **водить/вести/повести** p. 32,
**ездить/ехать/поехать** p. 109, **знать** p. 162, **изменять(ся)/изменить(ся)** p. 170) — these
are "Top 50 Verb" entries that get an extra page of usage examples/idioms adjoining the
conjugation table, not duplicate vocabulary items. Counted once each below.

## Grammar points

### The seven pillars of the Russian verbal system

The source frames Russian verb mastery around seven interlocking concepts: the persons of the
verb, the infinitive, the stem, verbal aspect, tense, conjugation, and mood (p. 2). Russian's
person/number system for subject pronouns (я/ты,вы/он,она,оно — мы/вы/они) parallels English but
collapses formal/informal distinctions differently: ты is intimate-singular (family, children,
close friends, pets), вы is both formal-singular and plural-informal. The source's own advice:
when in doubt, default to вы (p. 2).

### The infinitive and the Russian Spelling Rule

The infinitive (dictionary form) ends in -ть, -ти, or -чь and is not conjugated. A cross-cutting
spelling rule constrains conjugation endings throughout the system: the consonants г, к, х, ж, ч,
ш, щ, ц (classified as "hard" for this purpose) can never be followed by soft vowels я/ю — only by
а/у. This is why, e.g., a stem ending in one of these consonants takes -у rather than -ю in the
1st person singular, and -ат rather than -ят in the 3rd person plural (p. 3).

### The one-stem system (Jakobson) vs. the two-stem system

This book teaches Jakobson's one-stem system throughout rather than the more traditional
two-stem system taught in many schools. In the two-stem system, present-tense endings are added to
a present-future stem derived by dropping the last two letters of the 3rd-person-plural form,
while past-tense endings attach to a separate infinitive stem. The one-stem system derives
everything from a single stem (found via the 3rd-person-plural present), using two combination
formulae: **V+C=VC** (a vowel-final stem plus a consonant-initial ending concatenates directly,
e.g. говори- + л = говорил) and **V1+V2=V2** (two adjacent vowels collapse to the second, e.g.
жда- + у = жду), with a parallel **C+V=CV** and **C1+C2=C2** rule for consonant-final stems (pp.
3–4). The invisible "semivowel" й is treated as present whenever я/е/ё/ю directly follow a
vowel-final stem, even though it isn't written.

Six regular conjugation "types" are used throughout the book (a simplification of eleven
linguistically distinct stem classes), illustrated with model verbs: Type 1 (работать, dry stem
-ай/-ей/-уй/-яй, e.g. работай-, болей-, дуй-, теряй-), Type 2 (говорить, stem in -и/-е/-я/-а, e.g.
говори-, виде-, стоя-, молча-), Type 3 (пахнуть, stems like пахну-, надея+ся, жда-), Type 4
(требовать, -ова-/-ева- stems reducing to -уй-/-юй- in the present, e.g. требова-, воева-), Type 5
(нести, consonant-final stems like нёс-, крад-, лез-, associated heavily with unidirectional
motion verbs), and Type 6 (печь, stems like пёк-, стриг-, ж|г-, with consonant mutation before
present endings). Anything outside these six patterns is marked "irregular" (i) in the book's
indices (p. 3).

### Verbal aspect

Virtually every Russian verb is imperfective or perfective. Imperfective describes ongoing,
repeated, habitual, or general action and is usable in all three tenses; perfective emphasizes a
completed or bounded action/result and exists only in past and future (never present, since "in
progress" and "complete" are contradictory) (pp. 4–5). The source gives paired examples: "Игорь
учил новые слова весь вечер" (imperfective — Igor studied all evening, process) vs. "Игорь выучил
новые слова" (perfective — Igor finished learning them, result). Certain adverbs correlate with
each aspect: always/usually/often/every day/rarely/never/sometimes/again favor imperfective;
suddenly/finally/unexpectedly/until/completely/immediately favor perfective (p. 5). When aspectual
choice is unclear or the outcome unknown, imperfective is the default (p. 5).

### Tense

Russian has only three tenses (present, past, future), but the aspect system lets these three
approximate the finer range of English tenses (p. 6). E.g. я работаю covers "I work / I do work /
I am working / I have been working" depending on context.

### Conjugation classes

Present-tense endings fall into two classes: Class I (-е-/-ё- type: -ю/-у, -ешь/-ёшь, -ет/-ёт,
-ем/-ём, -ете/-ёте, -ют/-ут) and Class II (-и- type: -ю/-у, -ишь, -ит, -им, -ите, -ят/-ат). Type 2
regular verbs use Class II; all other regular types use Class I (p. 6). Detailed present-tense
conjugation samples are given for each of the six types, each illustrating the stem+ending
concatenation rule directly (e.g. Type 1: работай + у → работаю; Type 4: требова + у → требую,
with the -ова- → -у- reduction; Type 6: пёк + у → пеку, with the consonant surfacing differently
before hard vs. soft endings) (pp. 7–13). A separate irregular-conjugation page catalogs verbs with
wholesale stem changes between present forms, including пить/лить, есть/мочь, петь/спать, хотеть
(ч-т stem change), мыться, узнавать, and вставать (p. 14).

### Mood

Russian shares English's three moods: indicative (factual statements), imperative (commands), and
subjunctive (hypothetical/contrary-to-fact) (p. 6).

### The past tense

Formed by dropping the infinitive's last two letters and adding -л/-ла/-ло (singular,
masculine/feminine/neuter) or -ли (plural, all genders) — conjugated by number and gender, not
person. Many Type 5 and Type 6 verbs have irregular masculine forms (e.g. нёс not нёсл, пёк not
пёкл) (pp. 15–16). Aspectual choice in the past tense is illustrated with several worked examples:
"Утром она сдавала экзамен" (imperfective — she took the exam, outcome unstated) vs. "Утром она
сдала экзамен" (perfective — she took it and passed) demonstrates that imperfective past doesn't
commit to a result even when a single, specific past event is being described (p. 16).

### The future tense

Two forms: the imperfective **compound future** (буду/будешь/будет + и т.д. + the imperfective
infinitive) and the perfective **simple future** (a single conjugated word built from the
perfective stem using ordinary present-tense-style endings) (pp. 17–18). The compound future is
used for: durative statements, general "-ing"-style future statements, repeated/habitual future
action, and a specialized polite usage (e.g. "Что вы будете заказывать?" = "What would you like to
order?"). The simple future, like the perfective past, foregrounds a single completed future event.

### The subjunctive mood

Formed from the past tense of either aspect plus the particle бы (invariant, not conjugated) (p.
19). Used for: contrary-to-fact statements, expressing desire (often with чтобы), softened/polite
desire, fervent hope, and hypothetical conjecture. A cross-cutting rule: **positive subjunctive
statements use the perfective aspect; negative ones use the imperfective** (p. 19) — a rule worth
flagging for slang-mechanics work, since register/politeness downgrades often lean on subjunctive
softening (Мне бы хотелось... = "I would like...").

### Participles

A participle is a verbal adjective, declined like an adjective (agreeing in number/gender/case)
but retaining verbal properties (aspect, active/passive voice). Four types are built
systematically from the one-stem system (pp. 20–24):
- **Present active** (only from imperfective verbs): stem + -ущий/-ющий or -ащий/-ящий (e.g.
  занимаю(т) → занимающий).
- **Past active** (either aspect): stem + -ший/-вший (e.g. занимался → занимавшийся; a stem ending
  in a consonant after loss of -л, as in вёл, takes -ший on the present-tense stem instead:
  ведший).
- **Present passive** (from transitive imperfective verbs only): stem + -ем- + adjectival endings
  (e.g. читаем → читаемый); verbs in -авать keep the -ава- element (познавать → познаваемый).
- **Past passive** (from transitive, mostly perfective verbs): one of three suffixes -тый/-енный/
  -нный depending on conjugation class and stem shape (e.g. написать → написанный, встретить →
  встреченный, одеть → одетый), plus a **short form** (dropping the adjectival ending, agreeing in
  gender/number but not case, used predicatively: окно открыто, книга написана).

Participles are noted as comparatively rare in spoken Russian, where который-clauses substitute;
reflexive-verb participles always retain -ся regardless of the participial ending (p. 20).

### Verbal adverbs (gerunds)

Uninflected forms indicating manner/time/cause/circumstance of the main verb's action.
Imperfective verbal adverbs add -я(сь) to the stem (читать → читая, бояться → боясь); perfective
verbal adverbs add -в(шись) (прочитать → прочитав, заняться → занявшись). No Type 6 imperfective
verbal adverbs end in -чь; быть has the irregular form будучи; reflexive forms always end in -сь
(imperfective) or -шись (perfective) (p. 25).

### Commands (the imperative)

A three-person system: 1st person uses давай(те) + infinitive (imperfective) or давай(те) + 1st
person future (perfective) — "let's..."; 2nd person is the primary command form (-ай(те)/-и(те)/
-ь(те)); 3rd person uses пусть/пускай + 3rd-person form ("let him/her..."). A separate "official"
register uses the bare infinitive (military/police commands, e.g. "Стоять смирно!" = Attention!).
Negative commands conventionally use the imperfective; affirmative commands conventionally use the
perfective, correlating with the same aspectual logic (process vs. result) seen elsewhere in the
system (pp. 26–27).

### Reflexive verbs

Formed by adding -ся (-сь after a vowel) to the conjugated verb; always intransitive; occur across
all tenses, participles, and verbal adverbs. Not all are "true" reflexives (subject acting on
itself, e.g. Я бреюсь = I shave myself) — the source catalogs several other functions the -ся
suffix covers in practice: reciprocal meaning (Мы встретились = we met each other), inception/
continuation/cessation of an event (Урок уже начался = the lesson has already started), an
inherent/permanent trait (Змея кусается = the snake bites, i.e. is a biter), verbs that exist only
in reflexive form (бояться, надеяться), passive constructions (Книга легко читается = the book is
an easy read), and certain impersonal constructions (Мне спать хочется = I feel like sleeping) (pp.
27–28).

### Verbs of motion

A dedicated system distinct from anything in English: Russian marks multidirectional
(indefinite/no specific goal) vs. unidirectional (definite/point A to B) as separate lexical verbs
for the same physical action, on top of the ordinary imperfective/perfective distinction (pp.
28–32). The source lists 14 core motion-verb pairs (бегать/бежать "run," бродить/брести "stroll,"
водить/вести "lead/drive," возить/везти "carry by vehicle," гонять/гнать "drive," ездить/ехать "go
by vehicle," катать/катить "ride," лазить/лезть "climb," летать/лететь "fly," носить/нести
"carry/wear," плавать/плыть "swim," ползать/ползти "crawl," таскать/тащить "pull," ходить/идти "go
on foot"), each with three functional layers:

- **Multidirectional** (e.g. ходить): movement in more than one direction, habitual/repetitive
  action, general motion capacity, implied or completed round trips.
- **Unidirectional** (e.g. идти): movement in one specific direction, set expressions, short
  distances.
- **Perfective (по- prefixed)** (e.g. пойти): beginning of directional movement, intent to
  complete the trip, "setting out" (departed but not yet returned) — по- is described as the
  productive way to perfectivize a unidirectional motion verb.

A further, larger layer of **directional prefixes** (в-, вз-, вы-, до-, за-, об-, от-, пере-, под-,
при-, про-, с-, у-) attaches to the unprefixed unidirectional/multidirectional pair to derive a
whole family of directionally-specific verbs, e.g. входить/войти "enter," выходить/выйти "exit,"
приходить/прийти "arrive," уходить/уйти "leave." Critically, **prefixing flips which member of
the pair carries which aspect**: adding a directional prefix to a unidirectional verb yields a
perfective verb, while adding the same prefix to a multidirectional verb yields an imperfective
verb — so the prefixed pair (e.g. уходить "to leave," imperfective / уйти, perfective) loses the
original multi-/uni-directional distinction entirely and becomes an ordinary imperfective/
perfective aspectual pair (pp. 30–31). This mechanic — one lexical root, doubled by direction,
doubled again by aspect, and then collapsed back to a simple aspectual pair once a directional
prefix is added — is a rich potential source of derivational patterning for slang-mechanics
analysis (e.g. metaphorical extensions of directional prefixes, like перейти "to go across" also
covering "to switch to informal ты," перешли на "ты" in the source's own example).

A near-motion verb (не true motion but included for contemporary frequency): подниматься/
подняться "to ascend" is explicitly flagged by the source as not a strict motion verb but included
in the Motion Verb Index anyway for its everyday productivity (p. 32).

# Russian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: The Big Silver Book of Russian Verbs, dictionary entries pp. 176–381 (иска́ть through проводи́ть/провести́)

**Source:** Jack E. Franke, Ph.D., *The Big Silver Book of Russian Verbs: 555 Fully Conjugated
Verbs* (2004), pp. 176–381 (printed page numbers; PDF pages 226–450 of the digitized copy). This
is the second of three chunks covering the book (pp. 1–225 → `016`, this file → pp. 176–381,
pp. 382–673 → `018`); page ranges are by the book's own printed pagination, which the PDF page
numbers don't map to with a constant offset (verb entries vary in length, so printed-page drift
against PDF-page count is expected and not a defect).

**Coverage note:** this book is a verb-conjugation reference dictionary, not a prose grammar —
each entry is one verb (aspectual pair, or occasionally an aspectual triplet where an
indeterminate/determinate distinction also applies, e.g. **носить/нести/понести**) given a full
paradigm table (present, past, future, subjunctive, participles, verbal adverbs, imperative) plus
usage-example sentences and idioms. Per the coverage rule and copyright discipline, **every
distinct verb entry in this page range is captured as one vocabulary row** (206 entries, after
removing a source-side duplicate print — see below), but the full paradigm tables and usage/idiom
sentences are **not bulk-reproduced**: each row's Notes column paraphrases the book's own
conjugation-type classification and stem alternation instead of copying the paradigm.

**Source-side duplication caught during extraction:** `pdftotext` genuinely emitted 225 "stem:"
header blocks in this range, not 206. Diffing them showed 19 verbs (e.g. **находить/найти**,
**переводить/перевести**, **приходить(ся)/прийти(сь)**) print their header/stem line *twice* on
the same physical page — because those pages carry a short block of extra idiomatic-usage
sentences (marked "Idioms"/"Proverbs") *before* the standard paradigm table, and the book restates
the entry's own header once more directly above the paradigm. This is the source's own layout, not
a `pdftotext` column-scrambling artifact (verified: printed page number and full header text are
identical between the two occurrences, and no other run-together/misaligned text was found). The
19 duplicate blocks were merged into their single vocabulary row; **находить/найти**'s row notes
that its page carries an extra idiomatic sense (impersonal "нашло/нашла" ≈ weather/mood "came
over" someone) ahead of the standard table, since that's genuinely additional content worth
flagging even though it isn't given its own row.

**Text layer:** clean, real text layer throughout this range — no font-substitution cipher and no
vision-reading needed. The only recurring encoding artifact is embedded combining-stress-mark
control characters (several distinct byte values, not just one) interleaved into Cyrillic words;
these were stripped during extraction and don't affect the underlying word forms. No handwritten
marginalia was present (this is a clean/unmarked digital copy, not a scanned used book).

**A note on frequency data:** unlike most reference grammars, this book *does* mark a subset of
its own verbs "TOP 50 VERB" with a small in-page callout — a genuine source-supported frequency
signal (not inferred from lesson-ordering), captured in the Weight/Frequency column for the ten
entries so marked in this range: носить/нести/понести, передавать/передать,
показывать(ся)/показать(ся), понимать/понять, попадать/попасть, поступать/поступить,
приводить/привести, привозить/привезти, приготовлять(ся)/приготовить(ся), принимать/принять.

**Explicit register annotation found:** **кушать/скушать** ("to eat") is the only verb in this
range the book itself tags "(colloquial)" in its own English gloss — captured as `colloquial` in
Usage Tier rather than the default `core`.

**Oddity worth flagging, not correcting:** the entry for **печатать/напечатать** (p. 311)
classifies it in the source's own words as a "regular -ar verb (like *gustar*)" — *gustar* is
Spanish, not Russian, and appears nowhere else in this range. This is almost certainly a leftover
publisher/template error in the book itself (verified: not an OCR artifact, the string is intact
and unambiguous in the text layer) and is preserved verbatim in that row's Notes as a literal
quirk of the source, not corrected.

## Grammar points

### Conjugation-type classification observed across pp. 176–381

The book classifies every verb into one of ~6 numbered "regular" conjugation types plus an
"irregular" catch-all (the type system itself is introduced in the book's front matter, outside
this chunk's page range — see `016` for that explanation if it landed there). Within this range,
every one of the 6 numbered types is attested at least once (e.g. type 1 накрывать-class, type 2
говорить/видеть-class, type 3 плакать-class, type 4 требовать-class, type 5 нести-class, type 6
печь/помочь-class), plus a substantial minority of aspectual pairs the book itself labels
"irregular" — most often where the perfective member of the pair takes a suppletive or
consonant-final stem unrelated to the imperfective's own stem pattern (e.g. брать-type "-а-" to
consonant-final shifts: **отдавать/отдать**, **преподавать/преподать**, **признавать(ся)/
признать(ся)**, **подавать/подать**, **передавать/передать** — a recurring **-давать/-дать**
family where the imperfective keeps an -ава-/-авай- stem and the perfective drops it entirely).

### Consonant/vowel stem alternations tagged by the source

The source explicitly names several consonant mutations governing type 2 and type 5 perfective
stems in this range: **с-ш** (краси-ть→покраси-ть is *not* an example — actual instances are
**переносить/перенести**, **приносить/принести**, both **с→ш** in the imperfective's own
present-tense stem, distinct from the perfective's separate д/з-final root), **д-ж** and **з-ж**
(a matched pair of motion-verb "bring" compounds: **приводить/привести** д-ж vs. **привозить/
привезти** з-ж — same meaning ("to bring"), same morphological slot, different final root
consonant depending on whether the object is brought on foot or by vehicle — a clean illustration
of Russian's determinate-motion-verb root system carrying semantic distinctions (on
foot/by vehicle/carried/led) that a single English "bring" collapses), and **м-мл** (кормить/
накормить). This is genuinely new attested data for this chunk (the mutation *types* are a known
general Russian phenomenon, but each specific pair's mutation is only confirmed by seeing the
entry).

### Indeterminate/determinate motion-verb triplets

Three entries in this range are true three-way aspectual sets rather than the usual
imperfective/perfective pair: **лазить/лезть/полезть** ("to climb, crawl"), **летать/лететь/
полететь** ("to fly"), **плавать/плыть/поплыть** ("to swim"), and **ползать/ползти/поползти**
("to crawl") — an indeterminate (habitual/multidirectional) imperfective, a determinate
(unidirectional) imperfective, and a perfective, all sharing one lexical meaning. **носить/
нести/понести** is the same pattern for "to carry." This confirms the determinate/indeterminate
distinction (previously only named generally in this project's morphological-typology note) as a
real, productively-marked category for motion verbs specifically, not just an occasional lexical
quirk — worth carrying into the mechanics-analysis phase as a genuine grammatical dimension
Russian slang formation could interact with (e.g. a slang extension built on the indeterminate
member vs. the determinate one).

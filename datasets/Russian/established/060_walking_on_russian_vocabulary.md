## Grammar points

### Antonymy as an active-vocabulary drill (pp.12-19)

The chapter opens by defining antonyms as words with opposite meaning (*хороший — плохой*), then
frames the entire antonym drill sequence around the "active vs. passive vocabulary" distinction:
active vocabulary is a word you can retrieve in under ~2 seconds; anything slower is treated as
merely passive. The chapter's real methodological point (not a specific vocabulary fact) is that
building antonym pairs is presented as an efficient way to "enter" a language by learning core words
in a logical (paired) system, without needing translation into the learner's L1. The drill lists
themselves (Упражнения 1-31, pp.12-19) are elementary core-vocabulary antonym pairs (да/нет,
хорошо/плохо, близко/далеко, etc.) already exhaustively covered by this project's prior Russian
established/ files and are not re-transcribed here per the coverage rule.

### Three types of synonyms (pp.24, "Комментарий")

The source draws an explicit three-way (plus an optional fourth) taxonomy of Russian synonymy, its
single most substantive analytical point in the first third of the book:

1. **Ideographic synonyms** — synonyms differing in shade of meaning or intensity (*лентяй* vs.
   *бездельник* vs. *лодырь*: near-synonymous "lazy person" words that differ in expressive force,
   with *лодырь* judged the most colloquially expressive of the three) or in collocational range
   (*горячий* and *жаркий* both mean "hot," but only *горячий суп* and *жаркое лето* are idiomatic —
   they aren't freely interchangeable despite near-identical core meaning).
2. **Contextual synonyms** — words that are synonymous only in a specific context, not in general
   usage (e.g. *весёлый*, *добродушный*, *смех* all cluster around "cheerful" only within a
   particular sentence's context).
3. **Stylistic synonyms** — pairs/chains where one member carries an official/bookish register and
   the other a neutral or colloquial one (*вероятно*, *безусловно*, *предпочитать* all skew formal;
   many foreign learners fail to notice this because these words don't feel exotic to them the way
   true loanwords do).
4. *(Parenthetically mentioned, deferred to the later Paronyms chapter)* — **cognate/root-sharing
   synonyms**, e.g. *жильё* ("housing," concrete) vs. *жилище* ("dwelling," more literary), or
   *экономный* ("thrifty," of a person) vs. *экономический* ("economic," of a system) — near-doublets
   from the same root that diverge in exact sense or register, treated by the source as bordering on
   paronymy rather than pure synonymy.

The chapter also introduces **frequency/universality as a distinct axis from stylistic register**:
some synonyms are simply used more often regardless of style (the book has students verify this
empirically via search-engine frequency, "Проверьте себя по поисковой системе yandex.ru" — a rare
explicit instruction in a 2010-era teaching text to cross-check word frequency against live web
search results), and the more "universal" (frequent, stylistically unmarked) variant is what
foreign learners should prioritize learning first, saving literary/marked variants for a later,
receptive-only stage of study.

### Degree of intensity of a feature (pp.38-44)

A short chapter treating word-families that express escalating intensity of the same base quality
(e.g. *хороший — отлично — неплохой — прекрасный*, or *поражаться — удивляться* as varying in force
around "to be amazed"). The chapter's own explicit mechanical note: much of this intensity-grading
is carried by diminutive/augmentative suffixes ("суффиксы, которые 'увеличивают' или 'уменьшают'
значение слова"), which the book flags as a forward-pointer to its own Chapter 2 on word
composition/suffixation rather than elaborating here. A representative escalating chain captured
from the drill (p.47): *карликовый — крошечный (крохотный) — маленький — малюсенький —
микроскопический* ("dwarfish — tiny — small — very small — microscopic"), and a negative-quality
chain: *безобразный — невзрачный — неказистый — некрасивый — непривлекательный — уродливый* (all
"ugly/unattractive," escalating in force toward "hideous").

### Foreign (loan) words vs. native synonyms (pp.45-52)

Core grammar/register point: Russian frequently has a loanword and a native-root word covering the
same concept, and **the loanword is typically the more official/bookish member of the pair**
(*рекомендовать* [loan] is more formal than *советовать* [native]; the reverse relationship — a
native word skewing more formal than its loan counterpart — is not claimed anywhere in this
chapter). The chapter explicitly reassures learners whose L1 already has Latinate/international
vocabulary (English, French, German, Italian, Spanish speakers, but also explicitly Korean, Japanese,
and Chinese learners for grammatical terminology like *префикс*/*приставка*) that they already have
a head start recognizing these forms, and that Russian certification tests (ТРКИ-1 through -4) do
not test loanword status separately from ordinary vocabulary — so functional command of both
register poles matters, not the etymology itself. The chapter closes (p.52) with three practical
recommendations: learn to recognize the necessary minimum of such words; learn to actively and
correctly use one's own individual minimum; and never forget stylistic register and collocational
fit ("лексическая сочетаемость") when choosing between a loan and its native counterpart — the
latter concept is developed at length later in the book's own dedicated chapter (see forthcoming
part of this extraction).

### Official vocabulary — the official/colloquial register axis (pp.53-55, opening)

Defines "официальная лексика" (official vocabulary) as the shared lexico-grammatical core of three
book-ish registers the source treats as functionally equivalent for foreign-learner purposes:
scientific, publicistic (media/journalistic), and official-business style — collectively opposed to
colloquial speech. The source frames the entire remainder of its official-vocabulary chapter around
one practical claim: because official style relies on lexical stereotypes rather than a rich synonym
set, "translating" an official-register sentence into its colloquial equivalent is a tractable,
learnable skill, illustrated with a worked example: *Высота Петропавловского собора составляет
122,5 метра* (official) vs. *Высота Петропавловского собора — 122 с половиной метра* (colloquial,
"the height of the Peter and Paul Cathedral is a hundred twenty-two and a half meters"). A
proficiency-level note flags this vocabulary as active-command material starting at TRKI-2
certification level, with passive recognition recommended earlier.

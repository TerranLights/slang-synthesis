# Russian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: VocabuLearn Russian, Levels I–III (representative sample, all 3 levels)

**Source:** *VocabuLearn® Russian* (English/Russian audio-course companion booklets), Levels I, II,
and III — Penton Overseas, QuarkXPress-typeset PDFs, no printed year found on sampled pages. Level I
= 40pp, Level II = 40pp, Level III = 52pp. `source_reference/languages/Russian/Russian Learning Pack
[up-to-date as of 2012]/Vol 2 of 3/01 - Audio Courses, Textbooks, Manuals/15.VocabuLearn Russian
Level {1,2,3}.pdf`.

**Coverage note — representative sampling, not exhaustive extraction (deliberate departure from the
comprehensive-but-not-exhaustive default, per dispatch instructions).** All three PDFs have no usable
text layer (`pdftotext` returns only page-furniture text like "CD 1"; confirmed via sampling pages
1-3 of Level I) — they are scanned/rasterized print, requiring vision-reading for every page. Given
(a) these are bare bilingual word-list audio-course companions with no grammar apparatus beyond a
one-paragraph note on adjective gender endings, and (b) this corpus already has ~90 established/
sources (~1.25M+ words) covering core Russian vocabulary many times over, exhaustive vision-reading
of all 132 combined pages was judged low-value relative to cost. Instead: 5 pages of Level I (front
matter + 3 vocabulary pages spanning nouns/expressions/verbs), 3 pages of Level II, and 4 pages of
Level III were sampled across the front matter and spread through the CD-numbered sections (nouns,
adjectives, verbs, expressions) each level uses. This confirmed the redundancy hypothesis for Levels
I-II (near-total overlap with core vocabulary already captured across dozens of prior sources) while
finding Level III's **Expressions** sections to carry noticeably more colorful/idiomatic content
(colloquial turns of phrase, not single-word core vocabulary) than Levels I-II — consistent with the
level-progression design of the course. No grammar points beyond the one adjective-ending note below;
no dialectal/regional annotations found. No handwritten marginalia found on any sampled page — all
scans are clean printed text, no vision-reading ambiguity encountered (all rows below are
`plausible_unverified` by default since no row was independently cross-checked against a second
occurrence, but no row triggered genuine legibility doubt).

**What was skipped as redundant:** the great majority of Level I/II noun, verb, and adjective
list content — plain core-tier bilingual pairs (e.g. "the bench" / скамья, "to buy" / покупать,
"courageous" / смелый) that are already present, many times over, across this corpus's other 90
sources. Only a representative slice is kept below per level to document what the source contains
and confirm the redundancy call, plus every genuinely idiomatic/colorful expression found in the
sampled pages (concentrated in Level II's and especially Level III's Expressions sections).

## Grammar points

### Adjective gender/number agreement suffix table (all 3 levels)

Every adjective entry across all three levels is printed with its masculine form followed by a
parenthetical showing the feminine/neuter/plural endings, e.g. `смелый /ая, ое, ые/` ("courageous").
This is a lookup convenience embedded in the vocabulary list itself, not a separate grammar
explanation — the booklet's own front matter (Level I intro, note 3) states this is included "if
adjectives change in spelling or pronunciation according to gender or case." No paradigm table or
rule beyond this per-entry notation was found (pp.1-2, 39-40 sampled front/back matter, Level I).

### Definite/indefinite article substitution note (pedagogical framing only, not a Russian grammar rule)

Level I's introduction states that, since Russian has no articles, the noun-section entries are
translated using English "the" throughout Level I, while Levels II-III alternate "the"/"a"/"an" to
model definite/indefinite distinctions for the English-speaking learner — this is a translation
convention of the course, not a fact about Russian morphology, and is noted here only because it
explains an otherwise-puzzling cross-level inconsistency in the English glosses (e.g. Level I "the
armchair" vs. Level II "an armchair" for near-identical entries).

### Expressions default to feminine speaker gender

Level II's Expressions section (CD 3) states explicitly that "most of the adjectives and verbs are
in the feminine gender according to the speaker's voice" — i.e. the printed Russian sentences default
to a feminine first-person voice throughout that section (e.g. «я получила уклончивый ответ», with
the feminine past-tense verb ending, for "I got the runaround"). This is a recording/production
choice by the course, not a grammatical rule, but is noted since it could otherwise read as an
unexplained gender inconsistency against other sources' example sentences.

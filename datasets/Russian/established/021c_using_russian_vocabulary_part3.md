## Grammar points

### The abbreviation-univerb pattern (мeдсeстрá, зaрплáтa, штрих-ко́д, профсою́з)
Several nouns in this unit are compression/abbreviation formations from longer phrases: зaрплáтa
< зáрaботнaя плáтa, мeдсeстрá < мeдици́нскaя сeстрá, профсою́з < профeссионáльный сою́з,
штрих-ко́д < штрихово́й ко́д. This "univerbation" pattern (a multi-word phrase collapsing into one
compact word) recurs constantly through the book's business/office vocabulary and is worth
flagging as a productive Russian word-formation mechanism distinct from ordinary derivation or
compounding — likely relevant to how any derived slang register might similarly compress bureaucratic
phrases.

### Recurring first-person-singular consonant mutations
This unit drills в/вл (вступи́ть, объяви́ть), с/ш (искáть — throughout, not just 1sg), and т/ч
(сокрaти́ть) mutation classes on verbs specific to employment/business vocabulary; noted per-verb
above.

### Genitive-qualifier phrase structure
A recurring structural pattern across this unit's business vocabulary is the genitive-qualifier
noun phrase (`X + genitive Y` = "Y's X" / "X of Y"), e.g. исслeдовaниe конъюнкту́ры ры́нкa 'market
research' (lit. 'research of market conditions'), контро́ль зa кáчeством 'quality control',
дирeктор произво́дствa 'production manager' (lit. 'director of production'). This is ordinary
Russian genitive syntax rather than a special construction, but the exercises in this unit
explicitly drill it as a recognition category ("genitive qualifiers"), suggesting the source
treats it as a productive template for coining new compound business terms.

## Grammar points

### 6.1 The capital letter

A capital letter (заглавная/прописная буква) marks sentence-initial position in Russian just as in
English (nearly universal across European languages — the book notes Georgian as the one exception,
having no case distinction in its script at all). The capital letter functions as a punctuation
device working together with the period/question mark/exclamation point/points of omission to
delimit sentence boundaries — and can occur **mid-sentence** whenever a quoted direct-speech
sentence is embedded inside a larger reporting sentence (e.g. Елена посмотрела на него гневно и
сказала: «Прекрати немедленно!» — the capital П in Прекрати marks the start of the *quoted*
sentence, even though it sits mid-string). The book frames sentences containing direct quotations as
a distinct species of complex sentence for exactly this reason (pp. 38–39).

### 6.2 End punctuation

Every Russian sentence must end in one of four marks — period, question mark, exclamation point, or
points of omission — chosen based on the sentence's intonation/communicative function (Part One).
A closing quotation mark can also trail one of these four, but is never itself an end-punctuation
mark (p. 39).

- **6.2.1 Period for declarative sentences** — same principle as English. **Note:** a period-ending
  abbreviation (e.g. и др. "etc.") landing at a sentence's end does double duty as both the
  abbreviation's own spelling-device period and the sentence's end punctuation — only one mark is
  written, not two (pp. 39–40).
- **6.2.2 Question mark** — same core principle as English, but the book flags that this mark is
  functionally *more load-bearing* in Russian than in English, because Russian declarative and
  interrogative sentences frequently share identical word order (Part One, §1.4.2) — in writing, the
  question mark alone (not word order) signals the sentence is a question (pp. 40–41).
- **6.2.3 Exclamation point** — marks heightened emotional tone on a declarative or imperative
  sentence (anger, joy, surprise, passion, hatred, etc.), used "sparingly" for real emphasis, same
  restraint recommended as in English. **A genuine Russian/English divergence the book flags
  explicitly:** Russian may place an exclamation point directly after a letter's opening salutation
  (Дорогая мама! "Dear Mom,") regardless of whether the letter is formal or informal, where English
  uses a comma (informal) or colon (formal) instead — the comma/colon options exist in Russian too,
  but the exclamation-point option has no real English counterpart in this specific context (pp.
  41–42).
- **6.2.4 Question mark + exclamation point combination** — a question read with strong emotional
  emphasis can end with both marks together (?!), reserved for cases where the author specifically
  wants to flag the question as emotionally charged; uncommon in either language. **A related,
  distinct pattern:** when a single exclamatory-or-interrogative sentence contains multiple elements
  each carrying its *own* separate intonation curve, modern Russian punctuates each element as if it
  were a complete sentence in its own right — capital letter, then its own ?/!/?! — producing strings
  like А что народ потерял?! Надежду! Веру! Жизнь! where each exclamation is independently capitalized
  and marked, rather than treated as one run-on sentence with internal marks. The book notes older
  Russian literary style (cited examples from Gorky and Pushkin) did NOT capitalize each such element,
  so the ?/!/?! genuinely fell mid-sentence in older texts — a diachronic punctuation-convention shift
  worth flagging for any period-Russian-text analysis (pp. 42–43).
- **6.2.5 Points of omission (три точки, "three dots")** — marks an intentionally unfinished
  utterance. Distinct functions the book itemizes: (a) inviting the reader to mentally supply the
  omitted continuation of a thought; (b) marking an editorially shortened quotation, either at the
  quotation's start/middle to signal material was cut; (c) marking an interrupted action or an
  abrupt shift of narrative focus. **When points of omission follow a question mark or exclamation
  point, only two dots are used instead of three** (e.g. Как дела?.. rather than a fourth,
  redundant dot) — a genuine orthographic economy rule with no direct English parallel (pp. 43–44).

### 6.3 End marks of punctuation used internally (mid-sentence)

- **6.3.1 Points of omission used internally** carry the same omission-signaling function whether at
  a sentence's end or buried in its middle; **when a quotation that already contains a genuine
  ellipsis needs to be further shortened, the editorially-added ellipsis is enclosed in angle
  brackets (угловые скобки, `<...>`)** — the book explicitly contrasts this with English convention,
  which uses square brackets `[...]` for the equivalent editorial-ellipsis marking. Also: **a
  sentence-final ellipsis followed immediately by another full sentence never gets a redundant
  fourth dot for the "missing" sentence-final period** — unlike English, which the book implies
  would want one (pp. 44–45).
- **6.3.2 Question mark / exclamation point used internally** — occur inside a sentence at the close
  of an embedded quoted sentence followed by reporting words (e.g. «Отстань от меня!» — жаловался
  Ваня хриплым голосом. "'Leave me alone!' complained Vanya...") — the quote's own end-mark survives
  even though the whole construction is syntactically one larger sentence (p. 45).
- **6.3.3 A period in the middle of a sentence** — the period as true end-punctuation never occurs
  mid-sentence (only abbreviation-periods can, and those are a spelling device, not punctuation
  proper — deferred to Ch. 15). **Central rule with no English equivalent: when a quoted
  *declarative* statement is followed by reporting words, the quote's own sentence-final period is
  suppressed entirely and replaced by a comma placed AFTER the closing quotation mark** (e.g. «Он
  пришёл»,— доложил старик. "'He has come,' said the old man." — note the comma sits outside the
  guillemet, the reverse of English style, which places the comma before the closing quote mark).
  Other end-punctuation marks (?, !, …) are NOT suppressed this way — they remain at the end of the
  quoted material even with reporting words following (pp. 45–46).

This chapter closes with an explicit forward-reference: the next two chapters (7–8) are devoted
entirely to the comma, framed by the authors as needing outsized page-space specifically because
Russian and English comma usage diverges so extensively (p. 48).

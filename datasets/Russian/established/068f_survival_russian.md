## Grammar points

### Verb aspect: imperfective (НСВ) vs. perfective (СВ) — Lesson 7 (p. 75-78)

Russian verbs come in aspectual pairs cited together in dictionaries (де́лать/сде́лать, писа́ть/
напис́ать, звони́ть/позвони́ть). Imperfective verbs describe the action itself/its ongoing nature;
perfective verbs describe its completion or result (Я чита́л кни́гу "I read [was reading] the
book" vs. Я прочита́л кни́гу "I finished reading the book"). Imperfective verbs have present, past,
and future tenses; perfective verbs have **no present tense** — only past and a synthetic "simple"
future that uses present-tense-style endings on the perfective stem (я пишу́ [impf. present] vs. я
напишу́ [pf. simple future]). This aspectual present-tense gap is presented as categorical, not an
exception, and full present/past/future paradigms are tabulated for де́лать/сде́лать, звони́ть/
позвони́ть, and (past/future only) дать/переда́ть/сказа́ть, confirming irregular СВ conjugations
(дам/дашь/даст/дади́м/дади́те/даду́т for дать — a suppletive-looking irregular paradigm distinct
from the regular -ит/-ят Conjugation II pattern).

### Compound vs. simple future — Lesson 7 (p. 76)

Two future-tense strategies: **compound future** (быть, conjugated, + imperfective infinitive:
бу́ду чита́ть, бу́дешь писа́ть) for imperfective verbs, and **simple future** (perfective verb
conjugated with present-tense-style endings: прочита́ю, напишу́) for perfective verbs. This
directly follows from — and is the practical payoff of — the aspect distinction above: the
"missing" present tense of perfective verbs is filled by exactly this simple-future slot.

### Imperative formation — Lesson 7 (p. 78-79, Table 25)

Formed from the present-tense (imperfective) or simple-future (perfective) stem: add -й (+-те for
plural) if the stem ends in a vowel (чита́-ю→Чита́й!/Чита́йте!); add -и (+-те for plural) if the
stem ends in a consonant (пиш-у́→Пиши́!/Пиши́те!). A large worked table cross-references this
against verbs already vocabularized in this lesson (звони́ть→Звони́!, сказа́ть→Скажи́!, etc.),
plus a footnoted case-government reminder: звони́ть/позвони́ть, сказа́ть/дать/переда́ть all
require their addressee in the **dative** case (кому?) — directly tying the imperative-formation
grammar point back to Lesson 6's dative-case paradigm.

### Dative case of Russian last names — Lesson 7 (p. 79)

A dedicated rule not previously stated: male last names decline like first names/patronymics in
the dative, taking -у (Ивано́в→Ивано́ву, Петро́в→Петро́ву), while **female last names take -ой**
(distinct from the -е ending regular feminine nouns take) — Ивано́ва→Ивано́вой, Петро́ва→
Петро́вой — while female first names/patronymics still take the regular -е (Еле́на→Еле́не, Ива́новна
→Ива́новне). This -ой feminine-surname dative ending is a genuine declension-class exception
worth flagging: Russian family-name morphology partially follows adjectival declension patterns
(-ой is an adjectival dative/instrumental feminine singular ending) rather than the noun-declension
pattern regular feminine common nouns follow, because most Russian surnames are historically
possessive adjectives in form.

### Accusative case of animate nouns — Lesson 7 (p. 80, Table 26)

Explicitly flagged as governed by "animacy," a category that changes accusative declension outcome
independent of gender/number: animate nouns' accusative form matches their genitive (Кого?) rather
than their nominative — masculine animate: Анто́н→Анто́на, Ви́ктор→Ви́ктора; feminine animate first
names: Ири́на→Ири́ну, Ма́ша→Ма́шу (following the regular -у/-ю accusative pattern for -а/-я nouns,
unaffected by animacy since feminine -а/-я accusative already differs from nominative regardless).
Used specifically after мо́жно and бу́дьте добры́ when naming who you want to speak to on the
phone — directly cross-referencing this lesson's own polite-request vocabulary.

### Genitive required by "но́мер телефо́на" — Lesson 7 (p. 80)

"Telephone number of [X]" requires X in the genitive: но́мер телефо́на Анто́на, но́мер телефо́на
фи́рмы — an ordinary noun-genitive-of-possession construction, but flagged here as its own rule
since it recurs so often in the phone-conversation register this lesson is built around.

### Past tense of уйти́/вы́йти — Lesson 7 (p. 81, Table 27)

уйти́ (to leave/depart) and вы́йти (to step out briefly) are both perfective motion verbs with an
irregular root alternation in the past tense: уйти́→ушёл/ушла́/ушли́ (root vowel drops before the
л-suffix, following a general pattern where -йти́ compounds lose their -й- and show a fleeting-
vowel-like е/∅ alternation); вы́йти→вы́шел/вы́шла/вы́шли (analogous). The semantic distinction
between the two (permanently left vs. temporarily stepped out) is functionally load-bearing for
phone etiquette — Его́ нет, он ушёл implies "gone for the day," while Он вы́шел implies "back
shortly" — a genuinely fine-grained register/pragmatics distinction encoded purely through verb
prefix choice.

### Word order flips exactness in numeral+noun time expressions — Lesson 7 (p. 81)

A "Please note" box states a genuine register-relevant pragmatic-grammar point: the default word
order numeral-before-noun (в 2 часа́, че́рез 10 мину́т) means an *exact* time, while inverting to
noun-before-numeral (часа́ в 2, мину́т че́рез 10) signals *approximation* ("around 2:00," "in
roughly 10 minutes") — a purely word-order-driven modality shift with no additional lexical hedge
word needed, directly relevant to how Russian encodes vagueness/approximation grammatically rather
than lexically, worth flagging for later slang-mechanics analysis (a productive "invert for vague"
strategy an invented conlang could plausibly borrow or subvert).

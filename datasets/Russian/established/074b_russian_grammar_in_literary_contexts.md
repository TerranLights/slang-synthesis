## Grammar points (terse — standard second/first-conjugation, -ся, and verb-stress material,
redundant with prior established/ sources)

### Unit 2 — Second-conjugation verbs, irregular verbs, passive constructions
Standard second-conjugation prototype list (спросить/ш, возить/ж, видеть/ж, чистить/щ,
заметить/ч, любить/бль, ловить/вль, знакомить/мль, купить/пль mutation classes), the four truly
irregular Russian verbs (хотеть, дать, бежать, есть), and three passive-construction strategies:
(1) they-form with implicit subject, (2) reflexive verb without subject, (3) past passive
participle + instrumental "agent" (participles themselves deferred to Unit 19). pp. 14–22.
Standard, fully redundant.

### Unit 3 — First-conjugation verbs and the five functions of -ся
First-conjugation mutation/prototype list (larger and more varied than second-conjugation: с→ш,
з→ж, ск→щ, ч→г/ж, к→ч, т→ч common types; х→ш, д→ж, б→бль, м→мль, п→пль less common), plus 22
numbered prototype verbs. The suffix -ся is presented with its full standard five-function
taxonomy: (1) reflexive ("oneself": бриться, купаться), (2) reciprocal ("each other":
договориться, познакомиться), (3) passive-marking on transitive verbs (рассматриваться,
проверяться), (4) lexicalized-different-meaning pairs (сойти "get off" vs. сойтись "meet up";
собирать "collect" vs. собираться "plan/get together"), (5) -ся-only verbs with no unsuffixed
counterpart (смеяться "to laugh," надеяться "to hope"). Spelling of -ся vs. -сь conditioned on the
preceding letter. pp. 26–35. Standard, fully redundant with prior established/ coverage of this
same five-way taxonomy.

### Unit 4 — Stress in the Russian verb
Two "basic" stress patterns (stable vs. shifting) crossed with tense (past vs. nonpast), yielding
four combinations, with the constraint that a verb with shifting nonpast stress must have stable
past stress and vice versa. Shifting-nonpast pattern: stress on the final syllable in infinitive/
imperative/я-form, shifting one syllable left elsewhere (сказать, писать, купить, остановить,
ходить, платить). Shifting-past pattern: stress moves to the ending only in the feminine past
(принять → приняла́, взять → взяла́, жить → жила́, пить → пила́, дать → дала́). pp. 37–39.
Standard descriptive-stress material, redundant in substance (though the specific feminine-past-
stress-shift prototype list — принять/взять/жить/пить/дать — is a clean, compact worked example
worth cross-referencing if a synthesis pass ever needs a canonical stress-shift verb set).

## Register/literary findings

### "Russian Saying" boxes (4 more, one per unit-part)
- **Кто ме́ньше толку́ет, тот ме́ньше тоску́ет** ("He who talks less grieves less" / "Ignorance is
  bliss") — Unit 3, p. 32.
- **Цыпля́т по о́сени счита́ют** ("Count the chicks in the fall" / "Don't count your chickens
  before they hatch") — Unit 3, p. 32. Interesting mismatch with the standard English proverb: the
  Russian version is framed positively (wait until fall, when they've survived) rather than as a
  warning against premature counting per se — same caution, different narrative frame.
- **Без труда́ не вы́тащишь и ры́бку из пруда́** ("Without effort you won't be able to get a single
  fish out of the pond" / "No pain, no gain") — Unit 4, p. 37.
- **Из пе́сни слов не вы́кинешь** ("You can't toss words out of a song" / "One has to take the bad
  with the good") — Unit 4, p. 39. A genuinely distinct idiom from the "no pain no gain" family;
  closer to "it is what it is" / accepting an unflattering but true detail as part of a whole.

### ТЕКСТ 2а — Chekhov, *The Seagull* (Чайка, 1896), Treplev's monologue about his mother
Treplev (an actress's son) characterizes his mother as vain, superstitious, and covertly hostile to
him: she is bored and irritable in the countryside, superstitious about candles/the number
thirteen, stingy despite having 70,000 rubles in an Odessa bank, and would burst into tears if
asked for a loan. He resents that she is only "thirty-two" in his presence but "forty-three" when
he's not there, and that she does not respect the theater he loves, viewing modern theater as
"routine, prejudice" (рутина, предрассудок) even though she claims to love the theater and serve
"humanity, sacred art." A rich source of interpersonal-conflict/family-register vocabulary; the
book's own post-reading task specifically asks students to characterize Treplev's portrayal of his
mother, treating the passage as a register/characterization study, not just a grammar drill.

### ТЕКСТ 2б — dog-training article (contemporary press)
A short practical-advice article on training a Caucasian Shepherd puppy (Kavkazskaia ovcharka),
emphasizing gentle, patient, non-violent training methods; notable for its didactic imperative
register (запомните три правила — "remember three rules": don't yell, hit, or rush) and its
folk-behaviorist framing of dog psychology ("the dog thinks: I started doing something and got hit
for it").

### ТЕКСТ 2в — Akhmatova, «Реквием» ("Requiem," excerpt, 1940)
One of the most historically significant texts in this unit: an excerpt from Akhmatova's long poem
mourning the victims of Stalin's purges (written for/about women, including Akhmatova herself,
who stood in prison lines to learn the fate of arrested relatives). The excerpt includes the famous
image of the poet asking that a future monument to her be placed anywhere except near the sea where
she was born, specifically **not** in the Tsar's garden — but instead where she stood in line for
300 hours outside the prison, so the monument commemorates the collective suffering of the purge
era rather than her personal biography. Vocabulary here is emotionally and historically loaded
in a way no ordinary teaching-grammar vocabulary list would surface: "чёрные маруси" (Black Marias
— the vans used by the secret police to make arrests) is a distinct, historically specific slang/
euphemism term glossed directly by the source. This is exactly the kind of literary-register/
historical-slang find this dispatch was watching for.

### ТЕКСТ 3а — Bal'mont, «Чёлн томленья» ("The Skiff of Languor," 1894)
A Symbolist poem given with a full parallel English verse translation (unusual — most other texts
in this book are glossed word-by-word rather than fully translated). The book's own post-reading
task draws attention to the poem's dense alliteration (repeated ч and щ sounds) as a deliberate
sound-symbolic device — an explicit style/register observation from the source itself about how
phonaesthetic patterning reinforces the poem's stormy, restless meaning.

### ТЕКСТ 3б — workplace conflict-style psychological self-quiz (contemporary press)
A Thomas-Kilmann-style five-axis conflict-style questionnaire (Соперничество "Competition,"
Избегание "Evasion," Сотрудничество "Cooperation," Приспособление "Facilitation/Accommodation,"
Компромисс "Compromise") — a good example of contemporary formal/journalistic register vocabulary
around workplace psychology, distinct from the literary-register content elsewhere in the book.

### ТЕКСТ 4а — Lermontov, «Выхожу один я на дорогу» ("Alone, I go out onto the road," 1841)
Presented explicitly as a poem "virtually every Russian knows by heart" from childhood memorization
— itself a register/cultural-literacy note worth flagging: the book signals that certain poems
function as shared cultural touchstones memorized in Russian schooling, which is relevant context
for any later mechanics-analysis of quotation/allusion as a register device in adult speech.

### ТЕКСТ 4б — Tiutchev, «Silentium!» (1829)
A famous meditative poem on the futility of trying to express inner feeling in words ("Мысль
изречённая есть ложь" — "a thought spoken is a lie"), also flagged by the source as one "many
Russians know by heart" — reinforcing the same cultural-touchstone point as 4а.

## Grammar points

### 1. Noun-suffix taxonomy organized by semantic category (§16, pp. 32-37)

Unlike suffix inventories organized purely by form, this book groups derivational noun suffixes by
the *semantic category* they express, then subdivides by grammatical gender of the resulting noun.
Ten categories are given in sequence: person, "non-adult" (i.e., young-of-X / diminutive-person),
patronymic, place, thing, collectivity/aggregate, singulative ("one unit of a mass" — e.g.
горошина "a single pea" from горох), objectified action/state, objectified attribute, field-of-
study/school-of-thought, and — the category most relevant to slang mechanics — **emotional
evaluation** (§16.11, p. 36).

The emotional-evaluation subcategory is itself three-way split, each with its own gender-specific
suffix set:
- **Diminutive-affectionate** (уменьшительно-ласкательные): -ик/-чик/-ок/-ек (masc.), -ка/-очка
  (fem.), -ко/-ышко (neut.) — e.g. *садик, костюмчик, сынок, ножка, облачко*.
- **Augmentative** (увеличительные): -ище (masc./neut.), -ища (fem.) — e.g. *домище, ручища,
  болотище*.
- **Pejorative/dismissive** (пренебрежительные): -ишка (masc.), -ёнка (fem., implied by the
  pattern though the example given is masc.-only: *мальчишка*), -ишко (neut.) — e.g. *мальчишка,
  домишко, письмишко*.

A footnoted orthographic distinction is worth flagging: suffixes with a stable stressed vowel и
(e.g. -ик) keep it under declension (*столик — столика*), while suffixes with е (e.g. -ек) show it
as a fluent/mobile vowel that drops (*ножичек — ножичка*) — the same и/е mobile-vowel distinction
recurs at -ец/-ица (masc.) vs. -иц-/-ица (fem.) further down the page, with a worked minimal pair
*торговец* (masc., mobile е) vs. *торговка* (fem., stable) illustrating it.

This three-way affectionate/augmentative/pejorative split, tied to a fixed and enumerable suffix
inventory per gender, is directly useful groundwork for later slang-mechanics analysis of how
Russian slang productively exploits diminutive and augmentative/pejorative morphology (a pattern
already flagged as a live thread in this corpus via Streetwise Russian's `046` and the criminal-
argot/youth-slang finds in `036`/`060`) — this book supplies the closed, systematic paradigm those
findings' individual slang words are drawing on.

### 2. Aspect: "action-wholeness" as the unifying general meaning (§61.2, pp. 140-141)

Sampled only briefly given the corpus's existing depth on aspect, but worth noting the specific
terminological frame: Shelyakin defines the perfective/imperfective opposition around **целостность
действия** ("wholeness/completeness of the action") rather than the more common completed-vs-
ongoing framing — perfective verbs express "achievement of a limit to a whole action," imperfective
verbs express "action without a specified aspectual characteristic," and verbs are further sorted
into **предельные** (limital — verbs whose lexical meaning presupposes some natural endpoint, even
if only in principle) vs. **непредельные** (non-limital — verbs with no such natural endpoint, e.g.
*находиться, называться, сидеть, блестеть, расхаживать, дрожать, любить*, p. 141) — only limital
verbs form true perfective/imperfective pairs. This limital/non-limital (predel'nyy/nepredel'nyy)
distinction is a genuinely separate axis from the general/particular-meaning breakdown this corpus
already has from other sources, and worth flagging for the eventual aspect-mechanics analysis file.

### 3. Predicative adverbs / категория состояния as a closed word class (§85-86, pp. 193-194)

The book treats words like *больно, надо, нельзя, видно* — traditionally adverbs by form — as a
distinct functional part of speech ("category of state") defined by three criteria: (a) they combine
with the copula *быть/стать* for past/future tense but take no copula in the present; (b) when the
experiencer is expressed, it is invariably dative (*Ему было стыдно*, "He felt ashamed" — literally
"To-him was shameful"), never nominative; (c) a handful of former nominative nouns (*время, пора,
лень, охота, неохота, жаль*) have been functionally reanalyzed into this same class. A genuine
register note: this reanalysis (noun → predicative) is presented as productive in ordinary spoken
use, not archaic or literary-only — relevant background for how impersonal-dative constructions
carry a colloquial flavor distinct from personal-subject phrasing of the same idea.

### 4. Three types of subordinate syntactic connection, with obligatory/facultative valency (§98, pp. 220-221)

Russian word-combination (словосочетание) is built from exactly three subordination types:
**согласование** (agreement — dependent word matches the head in gender/number/case, e.g. *белая
бумага*), **управление** (government — the head assigns a fixed case, with or without a
preposition, to the dependent, e.g. *писать письмо*), and **примыкание** (adjunction — an
uninflected dependent word attaches purely by word order, e.g. infinitives, adverbs, gerunds
attaching to a verb: *хочу пить*, *приходи поскорей*).

Government is further subclassified along two orthogonal axes the book treats as systematic: (1)
**fixed vs. unfixed** — a governing word can require one specific case (fixed: *бояться холода*,
genitive only) or allow a choice among several depending on meaning (unfixed: *жить в Ленинграде /
на Кавказе / у реки / под Москвой* — all locative-type but different case/preposition combinations);
and (2) **obligatory ("strong") vs. facultative ("weak")** — obligatory government is
predictable/required by the governing word's own valency (*бояться холода* — "to fear" demands a
genitive object), facultative government adds an optional dependent the governing word doesn't
strictly require (*дверь в дом*, *подарить книгу на память*). A word can also be governed without an
overtly governed word being present at all, either generically (*Курить вредно* — "to smoke [in
general]" is harmful) or through situational ellipsis (*Ты брал мою книгу? — Нет, не брал* — "did
you take my book — no [I didn't take (it)]"), both explicitly flagged by the source as distinct
from ordinary omission.

### 5. Neutral word order as given→new information structure (§100, pp. 225-227)

This is the clearest expression of the book's self-described "functional" approach (see its own
preface, p. 3): Russian's neutral (unmarked) word order is explained not as a fixed
subject-verb-object template but as an **information-distribution principle** — a sentence's
neutral word order places already-known/contextually-given information (**данное**) first and
new/informationally-heavier content (**новое**) last, with the predicate typically marking the
boundary. Any constituent can serve as the "given" starting point — subject, an adverbial, even an
entire preceding clause's content — and inversion (changing the neutral order) is treated as a
deliberate expressive device for foregrounding new information earlier, accompanied by contrastive
stress/intonation (worked examples given with explicit intonation-contour notation, e.g. rising vs.
falling tone marks over *Москве* in *В Москве́ (а не в Тáллинне) он живёт*).

A worked passage (Ю. Казакевич excerpt, p. 227) is used to show given→new chaining across multiple
sentences in a row — each sentence's "new" information becomes the next sentence's "given" starting
point, a discourse-cohesion mechanism the book treats as the systematic engine behind Russian's
famously "free" word order, rather than free variation. This given/new (theme/rheme-style)
treatment is more explicitly worked out here, with more example density, than in the corpus's more
form-centered grammars.

### 6. "Sentence models" — a structural typology of subjectless and non-canonical-subject sentences (§101, §107-109, pp. 228, 258-260)

Rather than describing impersonal/dativesubject constructions case-by-case, the book organizes them
into a numbered inventory of named "models" (модели), each defined by its subject's case-form (or
absence) and its predicate type. Models sampled for this extraction:

- **Nominative-subject + predicate expressed by a numeral phrase** (§101 model 9, p. 258): *Пять,
  умноженное на двенадцать, — шестьдесят* ("Five multiplied by twelve is sixty"). Distinguishes
  present-tense copula-less numeral predicates from past/future forms, which take a neuter singular
  ("it") copula regardless of the numeral's own form: *Пять... было бы шестьдесят*.
- **Nominative-subject + predicate expressed by an adverb** (§101 model 10, p. 258), itself split
  into a plain-descriptive subtype (*Выход прямо* — "The exit is straight ahead") and an
  evaluative subtype normally requiring the dummy pronoun *это* (*Бессонница — это плохо* —
  "Insomnia is bad" / *Три часа ожидания — (это) очень плохо*).
- **Genitive-subject + quantifier predicate** (§108 model 1, p. 258): *Нас было трое* ("There were
  three of us"), *Времени у меня было мало* ("I had little time") — explicitly flagged as **more
  frequent in colloquial speech without any predicate at all** (subject-only utterances), a direct
  register note.
- **Dative-subject + predicative-adverb predicate** (§108 model 2, p. 259): *Мне было холодно* /
  age-expressions (*Мне тогда было двадцать лет*, with the irregular verb *исполниться* for "to
  turn [an age]"), and perception predicates *видно/слышно* which obligatorily co-occur with a
  genitive object under negation (*Ничего не видно*) but accusative under affirmation (*Мне видно
  всё*) — a case-alternation-under-negation pattern worth flagging for later mechanics work, since
  genitive-under-negation is a recurring theme across this corpus's case-semantics material (cf.
  `065`'s Case Book for Russian).
- **Infinitive-subject models** (§109-110, pp. 259-260): infinitive-subject + evaluative predicate
  (*Курить — вредно*) and infinitive-subject + infinitive predicate ("infinitive-comparative"
  constructions), sampled by name only given time budget — not fully tabulated here.

This model-by-model inventory (the book continues through at least model 12+ across §108-115, not
fully sampled here) is the single most distinctive piece of this source relative to the rest of the
corpus: it gives a compact, named taxonomy for exactly the kind of "subjectless"/oblique-subject
sentence patterns (dative-experiencer, genitive-quantity, infinitive-evaluative) that recur heavily
in colloquial Russian registers, and is worth a full pass in a future session if slang-mechanics
analysis needs a ready-made structural checklist for these construction types.

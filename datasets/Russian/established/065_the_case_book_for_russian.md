# Russian — The Case Book for Russian (Janda & Clancy, Slavica, 2002)

**Source:** Laura A. Janda & Steven J. Clancy, *The Case Book for Russian*. Bloomington, IN:
Slavica Publishers, 2002. 316 pp. Vision-read in full (scanned image-only PDF, no text layer;
confirmed via `pdftotext` returning empty output on a 3-page test).

**Coverage this file:** front matter/methodology (pp. i–xii), Nominative case chapter (pp. 1–16),
Instrumental case chapter (pp. 18–51). Continues in `065b` (Accusative onward).

**Non-redundant-supplement scope decision:** Russian's six-case system is already extensively
covered by 15+ prior grammar-reference extractions in this dataset (Wade, Timberlake, Franke's
Schaum's Outline, etc.), all of which document declension *paradigms* (which ending goes with
which case/gender/number). **This book does not re-teach paradigms at all** — its own
"Suggestions on How to Use This Book" section (p. viii) explicitly frames itself as assuming
readers already know the endings, and its appendix (covered in `065c`) is the only paradigm-table
content in the whole book. Instead, this is a cognitive-semantics reference organized around a
"radial network" of meanings per case — i.e., it documents **case-selection and case-government
semantics**: why a given case is chosen, what verbs/prepositions govern which case and why, and
how literal spatial/physical meanings extend metaphorically to abstract domains (time, social
relations, evaluation, control). This is exactly the "different angle, not different territory"
scenario the non-redundant-supplement pattern anticipates — **kept in full** here because the
semantic-network content (case-government verb/preposition inventories organized by meaning,
metaphor-extension patterns) has no equivalent in this dataset's prior Russian sources. Declension
endings themselves are **not** re-extracted; only the appendix chapter (in `065c`) will be spot-
checked for anything genuinely not already captured.

**Vision-reading confidence:** all pages were clean, high-contrast typeset print (a professionally
typeset linguistics monograph, not a scan of a used/annotated copy) — no handwritten marginalia
was found anywhere in this range. Confidence is `verified` throughout this range: nearly every
Russian example sentence includes its own English gloss and morpheme-by-morpheme case-tag
interlinear (e.g. `-NOM`, `-INST`, `-GEN`), which serves as an internal cross-check on the vision
reading of the Cyrillic.

**Format note:** this book's own presentation is diagrammatic (arrow/circle "meaning maps" showing
how case meanings relate) rather than tabular vocabulary lists in most places. Where the book does
present boxed word/phrase inventories (e.g. "Expressions of X associated with INSTRUMENTAL: A
MEANS"), only a representative subset of each box is extracted below (never the full box
verbatim) per copyright discipline — these boxes commonly run 10-20 items each.

## Grammar points — Nominative case (pp. 1–16)

### The nominative network: A NAME / AN IDENTITY

The book models each case as a small network of related meanings, radiating from a basic sense.
For the nominative, the two branches are **A NAME** (the case's role in bare naming/calling) and
**AN IDENTITY** (its role as the predicate complement in equational X = Y sentences, i.e. subject
complement of *быть* 'be').

- **A Name 1 — naming and calling.** Because the nominative carries no inherent relational
  meaning, it's the natural case for pure address/naming functions: vocative-like use (calling out
  a name or epithet), inserted as a parenthetical commentary item introduced by a comma or by
  *как*/*как* 'like'. Example: *Ты не в Мордовии, чучело!* ("You're not in Mordovia, you
  scarecrow!") — the insult *чучело* sits in the nominative as a parenthetical address term, not
  because it's syntactically the sentence's subject.
- **A Name 2 — subject of a sentence.** The nominative's most familiar role: unmarked grammatical
  subject. The book stresses that word order is much freer in Russian than English because case
  marking (not position) identifies the subject — a subject can appear sentence-finally or even
  mid-sentence without ambiguity, unlike the near-fixed SVO order English relies on.
- **An Identity 1 — X = Y with "be."** In present-tense equational sentences, *быть* is typically
  omitted (zero copula), and both X (subject) and Y (predicate complement) surface in the
  nominative: *Значит, я есть* ("Therefore, I exist" — lit. "I am"). Short-form predicate
  adjectives also appear in the nominative in this construction (*Ковригин страшно ранимый* "Kov­
  rigin is terribly vulnerable"). **This is the direct counterpart/contrast to Instrumental: A
  Label below** — nominative marks a *permanent, defining* identity/property, while instrumental
  marks a *temporary or changeable* one; the book flags this contrast explicitly and revisits it
  in the instrumental chapter.
- **An Identity 2 — fixed X = Y expressions.** The idiom *что такое X?* ("what is X?") is the
  book's example of a frozen X = Y construction used to request a definition/characterization of
  something, embeddable rhetorically inside a larger sentence.
- **An Identity 3 — X reduced to Y (apposition after a generic category).** A genuinely specific,
  non-obvious rule: when a generic category noun (already inflected for whatever case the sentence
  context requires — locative, genitive, etc.) is followed by a specific proper name or title in
  apposition, **the specific name/title stays in the nominative regardless of the governing case
  of the generic noun**. Examples: *в штате Невада* ("in the state of Nevada" — *штате* is
  locative, but *Невада* stays nominative); *из романа "Белая гвардия"* ("from the novel *The
  White Guard*" — *романа* is genitive, but the title stays nominative). This appositive-title
  rule is easy to miss in paradigm-only treatments since it isn't about declension at all, but
  about *which* item in an apposition pair takes the case.

## Grammar points — Instrumental case (pp. 18–51)

The instrumental network has four branches: **A MEANS** (the case's role as an
instrument/facilitator of an action), **A LABEL** (a temporary or contingent property/identity
attached to an item), **AN ADJUNCT** (a companion or co-participant, via с + instrumental), and **A
LANDMARK** (a reference point in space/time/social-hierarchy, via one of five governing
prepositions: *перед*, *за*, *над*, *под*, *между*).

### A Means — path/conduit metaphor

The book's organizing metaphor for A Means is a **path**: an instrumental item is a conduit
through which a force or activity flows from an agent to a goal. Sub-senses, in the order
presented:

1. **A map of the mental leaps involved** — instrumental spans both concrete tools/instruments and
   abstract "manners" of doing something; the throughline is always some kind of facilitation.
2. **Paths through space** — the instrumental names the literal path/route an action follows
   (walking *along* an embankment, running *along* a riverbed edge). Directly analogous to English
   "along."
3. **Paths through time** — the same path-container metaphor extended to duration: a discontinuous
   period is expressed via the plural instrumental of season/time-of-day nouns (*зимами* "in
   [various] winters", *ночами* "at night, night after night") used adverbially, contrasted with
   singular instrumental for a single stretch of time. Recurring set: *весной/летом/осенью/зимой*
   ("in spring/summer/fall/winter") and *утром/днём/вечером/ночью* ("in the morning/afternoon/
   evening/at night") — these are lexicalized temporal-instrumental adverbs, not live case-marking
   choices a learner makes per-sentence.
4. **Expanses of time and space** — when the path is continuous (a whole season, "throughout") vs.
   discontinuous/patchy (occasional, "here and there" — *местами* "in places," *временами* "at
   times"), both still take instrumental plural but with different discourse implicature.
5. **Path > facilitator/instrument** — the literal-tool sense: an instrument used to accomplish an
   action (*с мётлой* "with a broom," *с ремнём* "with a belt"), extending metaphorically to
   abstract facilitators (*я мог лететь домой ближайшим рейсом* "I could fly home on the next
   flight" — the flight itself as the "instrument" of getting home).
6. **Actions facilitated by instruments** — a large family of fixed body-part + instrumental verb
   collocations, where the body part is the "instrument" of the action. Representative sample (of
   a longer boxed list, p. 26): *кивнуть головой* "nod one's head," *топать ногами* "stamp one's
   feet," *пожать/пожимать плечами* "shrug one's shoulders," *показать пальцем* "point one's
   finger," *хлопнуть дверью* "slam a door," *махать рукой* "wave a hand." These are frozen
   idiomatic instrumental-government patterns rather than free case choices.
7. **Questions, adverbs, groups** — *чем* ("by what [means]") as the question word for A Means;
   a family of fossilized adverbs that are historically frozen instrumental-case forms with no
   live declension left: *украдкой* "stealthily," *бегом* "at a run," *пешком* "on foot," *ползком*
   "at a crawl," *верхом* "on horseback," *разом* "at once," *тайком* "secretly," *кругом*
   "around." Also: instrumental of measure/quantity with numerals for describing "by the X" rates
   (*десятками* "by the tens," *сотнями* "by the hundreds"), and instrumental of a group acting
   together (*всей семьёй* "all together, as a family").
8. **Person or object under control** — verbs of governance/leadership take an instrumental
   object (English "govern/manage/lead X" maps to Russian "govern/manage/lead *by means of* X").
   Representative government verbs (of a longer boxed list, p. 30): *руководить* "lead,"
   *командовать* "command," *заведовать* "manage," *владеть* "own, possess," *управлять*
   "govern, administer," *распоряжаться* "manage, dispose of."
9. **Person or object possessed** — possession-as-instrument: *владеть/обладать* "possess,"
   *располагать* "have at one's disposal," *делиться/поделиться* "share" (the shared thing is
   instrumental: *делиться информацией* "share information").
10. **Expressions of exchange, filling, occupying** — *торговать* "trade, deal in,"
    *обмениваться* "exchange," *жертвовать* "sacrifice, donate," *наслаждаться* "enjoy,"
    *интересоваться* "be interested in," *довольствоваться* "be satisfied with," *заниматься*
    "be occupied with, study." All take instrumental objects.
11. **Positive/negative evaluation** — a verb family expressing an emotional stance *toward* an
    instrumental object: *восхищаться* "be delighted with, admire," *возмущаться* "be indignant
    at," *гордиться* "be proud of," *дорожить* "value, treasure," *пренебрегать* "despise,
    disdain." The instrumental here names the stimulus of the evaluative emotion.
12. **The passive agent** — the canonical passive construction: nominative patient + passive
    participle/reflexive verb + instrumental agent (*книги читаются студентами* "the books are
    read by students"). The book stresses this is the same conduit-of-action logic as the other A
    Means senses — the agent is the "means" by which the passive action happens.
13. **An agent with no subject** — impersonal/raw-force constructions with no grammatical subject
    at all, where the instrumental names an involuntary natural force or smell (*пахло горячим
    хлебом из тостера* "there was a smell of hot bread from the toaster"). The book notes this
    "raw force" instrumental is typically used for smells, elements, and acts of God — verbs like
    *занесло* 'snowed in/blew over', *оторвало* 'was torn off [by wind/current]'.

### A Label — being, becoming, seeming (contrasted with the nominative's An Identity)

Where the nominative's *An Identity* marks a *permanent* X = Y equation, **A Label marks a
temporary, contingent, or acquired property/role** — this is the instrumental predicate-complement
pattern taught in every basic grammar (*он стал врачом* "he became a doctor"), but this book
frames it as one node of a broader semantic network rather than an isolated rule, and supplies the
verb inventory that selects it. Representative verbs taking A Label (of a longer boxed list, pp.
36–37): *быть* "be" (past/future/subjunctive, since present-tense *быть* triggers the nominative
zero-copula instead), *стать/становиться* "become," *казаться/показаться* "seem, appear,"
*остаться/оставаться* "stay, remain," *называться/назваться* "be named, be called,"
*считаться* "be considered," *являться* "be, constitute" (formal register), *выглядеть* "look
like," *чувствовать себя* "feel like [oneself as]." The book also documents A Label used
metaphorically for comparisons ("X is like a Y") and for a generic-category-plus-specific-name
apposition inverse of the nominative's An Identity 3 pattern (e.g. an animal described as "a
made-over copy of Stalin" — *был... копией Сталина*, copy in instrumental as the metaphorical
label).

### An Adjunct — companions and social engagement (с + instrumental)

Marks a co-participant/companion, corresponding to English "with" in its comitative (not
instrumental-tool) sense: *я говорил с Иваном* "I talked with Ivan." Two sub-senses:

1. **Companions** — joint activity/engagement verbs taking с + instrumental: representative
   sample (of a longer boxed list, p. 40): *беседовать/поговорить с* "converse with," *бороться/
   драться с* "struggle, fight with," *знакомиться с* "become acquainted with," *соглашаться/
   согласиться с* "agree with," *соревноваться с* "compete with," *встречаться с* "meet with."
2. **Idioms and other uses** — the frozen phrase *мы с + [name]* ("we with [name]" = "[name] and
   I", e.g. *мы с Аней* "Anya and I") — the reverse partner-order from English. Also *что с
   тобой?* "what's with you?" A distinctive holiday-greeting formula family uses с + instrumental
   to mean "congratulations on ___": *С днём рождения!* "Happy Birthday!", *С Новым годом!*
   "Happy New Year!", *С лёгким паром!* "Have a nice bath!" (said after a sauna/bath — a
   culturally specific formula with no direct English equivalent), *С Рождеством!* "Merry
   Christmas!"

### A Landmark — five governing prepositions, each spatial + metaphorical

The instrumental's fifth branch uses one of five prepositions to mark an item as a reference point
(a "landmark") relative to another item, with a literal spatial sense that extends metaphorically:

- **перед 'in front of, before'** — spatial (in front of a building), temporal ("before" an event:
  *перед вчерашней встречей* "before yesterday's meeting"), and a metaphorical sense of standing
  before an authority/obligation (*выразили признательность перед руководством* "expressed
  gratitude before the leadership" — obligations owed toward a governing body).
- **за 'beyond, behind'** — spatial (beyond a threshold: *за порогом*), and a distinctive
  in-exchange-for sense (*за девятьсот миллионов рублей* "for/in exchange for nine hundred million
  rubles" — a price-as-landmark pattern) alongside the literal "you can't see the forest for the
  trees" idiom (*за деревьями не видеть леса*).
</br>
- **над 'above'** — spatial (above a city), and a control/authority-vantage-point metaphor
  (*работает над формированием структуры* "is working on [lit. above] the formation of the
  structure" — control implies a vantage point above what's controlled); also used for
  international bodies' oversight/observation of a population over time.
- **под 'under, beneath'** — spatial (under a wrecked building), and metaphorical subordination/
  protection senses: *под контролем* "under the control of," *под покровительством* "under the
  patronage of," *под домашним арестом* "under house arrest," *под видом* "under the guise of."
  Notably bidirectional with *над*: the same control relationship can be framed from either the
  superior's (над) or subordinate's (под) perspective.
- **между 'between, among'** — spatial and distributional (a distance requirement *между* two
  aircraft; funds allocated *между* two cities for a joint telecom link), and a discourse-marking
  idiom *между тем* "meanwhile."

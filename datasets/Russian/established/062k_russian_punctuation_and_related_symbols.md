## Grammar points

### Chapter 16: Symbols Used with Numerals (Цифры)

Russian uses the same Arabic and Roman numeral systems as English, but the punctuation-like symbols
accompanying them sometimes diverge sharply.

- **§16.1 The comma with numbers** — Russian uses the comma as the DECIMAL point (2,5 = "two point
  five"), the OPPOSITE of English's period-as-decimal-point convention. Conversely, Russian NEVER
  uses a comma to group large whole numbers into sets of three digits (as English does: 1,367,780);
  instead a small typographic space called знак отбивки ("separation mark," roughly half the width of
  a normal word space) separates the digit groups: 1 367 780. Some Russian writers substitute a period
  for this grouping space, but the book flags this as non-standard/incorrect (p. 195).
- **§16.2 Symbols used in writing dates** — as established in earlier chapters, a date with both
  month and year needs no comma (unlike English). When part of a date is spelled out and part is
  numeric, the year-numeral is followed by the abbreviation г. (год, "year") or, for a range of years,
  гг. (годы, "years"): 1995 г.; 1941-45 гг. A date written ENTIRELY in numerals differs from English
  in three ways: (1) periods, not slashes, separate the parts (9.XI.94, not 9/11/94); (2) the DAY
  precedes the MONTH (matching the spoken idiom "ninth of November," девятое ноября, rather than
  English's "November ninth"); (3) the month is conventionally given in ROMAN numerals (though Arabic
  is also possible), while the day and year are always Arabic; no г./гг. abbreviation follows the year
  when the whole date is numeral-only (pp. 196).
- **§16.3 Symbols used in arithmetic** — mostly identical to English, with one key exception: (a) the
  equal sign (знак равенства) is the same; (b) the plus sign (плюс) denotes addition (сложение); (c)
  the minus sign (минус), which resembles the dash (тире) in shape, denotes subtraction (вычитание);
  (d) multiplication (умножение) is denoted by either × or a raised period (·) — both called знак
  умножения, and the same written expression can be read aloud in four different ways (Четыре
  умноженное на два равняется восьми́, Четы́режды два — восемь, Четыре на два — восемь, or Четыре
  умножить на два равняется восьми́); (e) **division (деление) is denoted by a symbol identical in
  form to the COLON** (двоеточие) — called знак деления — and the ÷ division symbol familiar to
  Americans is NOT used in Russian at all; (f) square brackets (квадратные скобки) enclose a larger
  equation segment that itself contains round-bracket-enclosed material, as seen in mathematics
  textbooks (p. 197).
- **§16.4 Symbols used to write temperature** — identical to English: (a) plus sign for degrees above
  zero (градусы тепла, literally "degrees of warmth"); (b) minus sign for degrees below zero (градусы
  мороза, literally "degrees of frost") (p. 197).
- **§16.5 The colon used in clock time** — separates hour from minute exactly as in English: 1:30 (p.
  198).
- **§16.6 The slash (Вертикальная палочка or Дробь)**, also called the virgule in English, has two
  numeral-related uses: **§16.6.1 fractions** — used exactly as in English (1/2, 4/5); in this
  function the slash is commonly called дробь, which also means "fraction" itself. **§16.6.2 the
  meaning "per"** — abbreviates в, на, or за: 10 м/сек "10 meters per second," 110 км/час "110
  kilometers per hour." **Note**: the slash used in ENGLISH date notation (V/10/87) corresponds to a
  PERIOD in the equivalent Russian date notation (10.V.87) — reiterating §16.2's point from the
  opposite direction (p. 198).

### Chapter 17: Symbols Used to Format Texts

The book's final chapter, explicitly framed as practically useful only for someone preparing a
Russian-language manuscript, rounding out awareness of non-punctuation symbols.

- **§17.1 The capital letter used in formatting poetic texts** — capitals mark certain phrase-initial
  positions that are not sentences and need no end punctuation of their own:
  - **§17.1.1 Lines of verse**: each new line of a poem conventionally begins with a capital letter
    regardless of whether it starts a new sentence — illustrated with Lermontov's 19th-century poem
    «Прощай, немытая Россия» ("Farewell, unwashed Russia"). English shares this same convention (p.
    199).
  - **§17.1.2–§17.1.3 Stage directions and attribution parentheticals**: as established in Chapter 12,
    stage directions/proverb-and-authorship attributions in parentheses at a sentence's end take the
    sentence's end-punctuation BEFORE the opening parenthesis, with the closing-parenthesis period
    placed after — the reverse of English convention, which places the period inside the parenthesis:
    Горбатого могила исправит (Пословица) vs. English "...the hunchback. (Proverb.)" **Historical
    note**: the current Russian convention is relatively recent — books from a few decades earlier
    followed the same style still used in modern English, placing the period before the closing
    parenthesis (p. 200). **If a LARGER passage is quoted/exemplified**, it's set as an indented block
    with the author's name below it, NO parentheses, and the end punctuation stands at the end of the
    quote itself, not after the attribution — identical convention in both languages (p. 200). A
    quoted sentence embedded within an ongoing sentence begins with a capital letter regardless of
    whether it follows a colon (as direct speech normally does, per Ch. 14) (pp. 200–201).
  - **§17.1.4 Titles and headings**: book/article titles and chapter/section headings begin with a
    capital but take NO period even if a complete sentence, though they MAY end in a question mark or
    other end mark: «Что делать?», What is to be Done? Unlike English, which capitalizes every
    content word in a title, **Russian capitalizes only the first word (plus any proper nouns)**:
    «Герой нашего времени», A Hero of Our Time. **If a title is actually a CHOICE between two
    alternate titles joined by или, "or,"** BOTH halves capitalize their own first word, and или is
    preceded by a comma: «Ирония судьбы, или С лёгким паром» "The Irony of Fate, or Enjoy your
    Steambath" (a well-known Soviet comedy film) (p. 201).
- **§17.2 Punctuating and formatting letters** — mostly parallel to English, with several notable
  divergences:
  - **§17.2.1 Date at the top**: numerals only, or numerals+words, day first then month then year,
    number designations separated by periods: 10.IV.97 / 10.4.97 / 10 апреля 1997 (pp. 201–202).
  - **§17.2.2 Salutation followed by an exclamation point**: **Russian uses an exclamation point after
    the introductory vocative in ALL registers of letter**, where English uses a comma (informal) or
    colon (formal) — though a comma or (less often) colon may substitute in FORMAL Russian letters.
    The formality distinction is signaled instead by the choice of preceding adjective: дорогой,
    "dear" (informal) vs. уважаемый, "respected," or глубокоуважаемый, "deeply respected" (formal) (p.
    202).
  - **§17.2.3 Spelling of the formal "you"**: capitalized forms of the polite/formal singular "you"
    (Вы, Ваш, Вам) are used when a Russian letter addresses ONE person formally, regardless of
    sentence position — **but plural "you" forms addressing a GROUP (вы, ваш, вам) are NOT
    capitalized**, nor is the familiar singular (ты, твой, тебе) ever capitalized (p. 202).
  - **§17.2.4 Farewell salutation**: formal letters often close with С уважением, "With respect" — NOT
    followed by a comma, unlike its English equivalent. Informal closings (Пиши! "Write!"; Обнимаю "I
    hug you"; Целую "I kiss you") likewise take no comma, though they may take an exclamation point
    (p. 203).
- **§17.3 The period used as a formatting device** (beyond its punctuation/spelling roles):
  - (a) The period follows a number in a numbered list — same in both languages; but there's no period
    after a chapter/section title/heading in either language (p. 203).
  - (b) The period IS used after a heading that continues in the SAME paragraph as the following text
    (contrasted with a heading set on its own line, which takes none). The **section symbol (§)**,
    called параграф in Russian (not to be confused with абзац, "paragraph," the ordinary printed
    paragraph unit) introduces each new numbered section; if a heading itself has multiple phrases, a
    period separates all but the LAST phrase. The chapter also notes the **hanging indent** (сделать
    абзац or сделать красную строку — literally "make a red line," a historical term from illuminated
    red drop-capitals) used to mark new paragraphs in both languages; some specialists on Russian
    punctuation classify indentation itself as a form of punctuation (pp. 203–204).
- **§17.4 A few final bookish details** — closing miscellany:
  - (a) Russian books print the word **тираж** ("print run") followed by a number on the final page,
    reporting the number of copies printed — a piece of information English-language books typically
    omit (p. 204).
  - (b) A contributor's name enclosed in a printed box — called **траурные рамки**, "mourning frame(s)"
    — signals that the person died before the book's publication (p. 204).
  - (c) The text on a Russian book's spine (**корешок**) reads bottom-to-top, the reverse of the
    English top-to-bottom convention — the book's closing line wryly notes "you really can judge a
    book by its cover" (p. 204).

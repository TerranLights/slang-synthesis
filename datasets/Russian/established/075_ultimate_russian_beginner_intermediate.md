## Scope decision — aggressive non-redundant-supplement pass

This is a standard 40-lesson (524p) Living Language beginner→intermediate course covering the exact
same grammatical territory (six-case declension, aspect, verbs of motion, participles/gerunds,
subordination) already extracted in far greater depth by `002/003` (Wade's *Comprehensive Russian
Grammar*), `006/007` (Timberlake), `012/013` (Schaum's), `049_...049p` (*Colloquial Russian: Complete
Course*), `050_...050l` (*Teach Yourself Russian*), `068_...068p` (*Survival Russian*), and over a
dozen other already-extracted sources. Per the spec's "non-redundant supplement" dispatch, and
consistent with the confirmed near-total redundancy already found on `048_living_russian_grammar`
(0 new vocab rows) and this exact publisher family's `068_survival_russian` (confirmed near-totally
redundant): **the full 524-page paradigm/vocabulary/dialogue apparatus of this book was not
vision-read page-by-page.**

Instead, the book's front matter (table of contents, 16 pages) was read in full to map its complete
40-lesson structure, confirming every lesson covers standard beginner/intermediate paradigm ground
(cases, tenses, aspect, motion verbs, relative clauses, indirect speech, participles/gerunds) with no
lesson devoted to slang, regional dialect, or register beyond what's already captured elsewhere in
this corpus. A representative core-lesson sample (Lessons 8 and "Fifth Review," an exercise page) was
vision-read and confirmed standard dialogue/drill content indistinguishable from material already

**Targeted vision-reading was then applied to the specific sections most likely to carry genuinely new

## Grammar points

### 1. Interjections as a distinct closed lexical class (Lesson 21, p.243)

The book explicitly frames interjections ("Hmm...", "Shh!", "Oh!") as a cross-linguistically recurring
but language-specific closed class, then tabulates ~13 Russian items (see vocabulary table). Two are
flagged with explicit pragmatic/register glosses in the source itself: **хм!/гм!** for contemptuous
"huh," and **ой, мамочка!** as an intensified variant of plain **ой**, both useful evidence of how
Russian marks affect gradation on a single interjection root.

### 2. Conversational fillers as a named functional category (Lesson 34, §3, p.377)

The source dedicates an entire numbered grammar-and-usage subsection to "conversational fillers,"
explicitly framed as essential-for-fluency stalling/pause devices (paralleling English "um," "well,"
"so to speak"). It groups ten items (see vocabulary table) without further subclassification. This is
a cleaner, if smaller, single-page inventory of discourse fillers than most of this corpus's existing
sources, which tend to surface fillers piecemeal inside dialogue transcripts rather than as a named
grammar point — worth cross-referencing against `032_russian_translation_theory_and_practice.md`'s
discourse-particle inventory and `033_sinyaya_zvezda_blue_star_stories.md`'s discourse-particle notes
for a future consolidated fillers/particles mechanics writeup.

### 3. The diminutive suffix -еньк- (Lesson 34, §5, p.378)

-еньк- attaches to nouns/adjectives to express smallness, approval, or tenderness (e.g. маленький
"small" ← мало + -еньк-; хорошенькая "pretty" from хороший; худенькая "(so) thin" from худой). The
source explicitly flags a register/pragmatics point not always spelled out elsewhere in this corpus:
**overuse of -еньк- reads as "very gushy"** — i.e. this is a productive affective-register marker
whose overapplication is itself a recognized (mildly negative) stylistic signal, not merely a neutral
size/affection diminutive. Worth noting alongside this corpus's other diminutive-suffix material
(e.g. `066_...uchebno_spravochnoe_posobie_po_leksike`'s -е-/-и- and -ну-/-и- inchoative/causative
suffix chapters) as part of a broader derivational-morphology register inventory.

### 4. Soviet-era slogans surviving as sarcastic/punned quotation (Lesson 35, §3, p.385)

The source frames political slogans from the Communist era as now belonging to everyday language
almost exclusively via **sarcastic quotation or deliberate pun**, explicitly citing this as
"indicative of the Russian love of wordplay," and instructs the learner to recognize but never
actively use them. Two of the six slogans tabulated carry attested internal wordplay: «БАМ — комсомолу
по зубам!» (rhyme) and «Наша цель — коммунизм!» (a documented pun on цель = both "goal" and "target,"
per the source's own footnote about it being posted at a firing range as a joke). This is a clean,
explicitly-sourced example of a register mechanism — formulaic political language repurposed as
ironic/humorous common speech — directly relevant to future slang-mechanics analysis on how fixed
phrases get recontextualized.

### 5. Particles as a distinct grammatical class, explicitly de-lexicalized (Lesson 38, §2, p.412)

The source explicitly instructs: "It is important to understand their grammatical environments,
rather than think of them as words" — i.e. Russian particles (бы, ведь, вот, да, лишь, ну, уж, хоть,
что) are explicitly framed as meaning-modifying function elements rather than independent lexical
items, and are shown productively **combining** (что + ли → "...or what?"; particles stacking as in
Что он слепой, что ли?!). This explicit "particles are grammar, not vocabulary" framing is a clean,
reusable formulation for this corpus's ongoing register/particle mechanics work (cf. `062`'s -то/-ка
particle chapter).

### 6. The бы + ни "-ever" construction (Lesson 38, §3, pp.412–413)

Russian's equivalent of English "-ever" (whatever, whoever, whenever) is formed with **бы** placed
immediately after a question word (unattached, unlike English's fused suffix) and **ни** placed
directly before the verb: Как бы мы ни сыграли... ("However we play..."), Что бы они ни делали...
("Whatever they do..."), Кто бы ни пришёл... ("Whoever comes..."). A clean, fully worked paradigm with
six question-word variants (как, что, сколько, когда, какой, кто, куда), useful as a supplement to any
prior concessive-clause material in this corpus.

### 7. Exclamations for "trying moments" explicitly flagged for careful use (Lesson 38, §4, p.414)

A short list of mild profanity-adjacent exclamations (пошёл вон!, к чёрту!, прочь!, ёлки-палки!) is
explicitly prefaced with a caution: "Be careful how and when you use them." Ёлки-палки is explicitly
identified by the source itself as a **euphemistic nonsense-word substitute for stronger language** —
a directly attested instance of taboo-avoidance-by-substitution, the same mechanism already flagged as
a flagship finding pattern in this corpus (cf. Hungarian's phase-1 taboo-avoidance naming, SCB's
clipping-slang findings).

### 8. Spoken versus written Russian, named as its own topic (Lesson 40, §1, pp.430)

Lesson 40 opens with an explicit register-contrastive framing absent from most of this corpus's other
sources, which tend to embed register notes inside individual vocabulary entries rather than devote a
named section to the spoken/written axis itself. Two concrete guideline pairs are given: (a) **также**
reads as stilted/bookish in speech — natural spoken alternatives are и ("and, too") or fronting the new
information and using **тоже** instead; (b) of the three "it is necessary" words, **надо/нужно** sound
natural in conversation while **необходимо** reads as bookish, though **необходимо** remains usable in
speech specifically for added emphasis/stronger necessity. This "which near-synonym sounds bookish vs.
natural in speech" framing is a reusable register-detection template worth applying to other
near-synonym sets already catalogued in this corpus (e.g. `066_uchebno_spravochnoe_posobie_po_leksike`'s
discrimination pairs).

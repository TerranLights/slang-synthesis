**Cultural point — Youth slang (pp. 117–121).** Contemporary Russian speech blends youth slang,
"classical" criminal speech (блатной), New-Russian jargon, and professional jargon. Sample
back-off/don't-lie doublets the author gives: Не наезжай! / Не дави! ("Back off!"); Не грузи! / Не
пугай! ("Don't exaggerate!"); Не гони! / Не ври! ("Don't lie/BS!"). Slang is linguistically neutral
per the author (language itself has no inherent good/bad quality; the split is literary vs.
non-literary, and slang sits in the latter, following ordinary grammar — full tense/aspect paradigms
apply to jargon verbs same as standard ones). Age bracket for active youth-slang use: ~14–25.
**Word-formation patterns documented in detail:**
- **Neologism via direct English borrowing**, meaning preserved: спикать ("to speak," cf. говорить),
  аффордать ("to afford," cf. позволить себе купить).
- **Parody of English borrowings into pseudo-Russian morphology**: parents → пренты/пернсты;
  birthday → бёздник/безник; girl → герла/герлы/герлов; girl's → герловый/герлушка; shopping →
  шапать/пошапать; to miss → замисать; to drink → дринк/дринкач/дринкер/задринчить/дринчиться.
- **Native-root coinage**: oттяг ("enjoyment"), оттянуться ("to be happy"), прикол ("humor"),
  приколий/прикольник ("joker"), прикольный ("happy, funny").
- **Stressed -ов adjectival suffix** (productive, on both English and Russian roots): брендовый
  ("brand new"), олдовый ("old"), янговый ("young"), френдовый ("my friend's"), прайсовый ("money,
  expensive"), хитовый ("popular"), юзаный ("used"). This is a clear `morphological_play` case (per
  `00_Usage_Tier_Taxonomy.md`) — the -ов suffix attaches productively to loanword roots, not just
  native ones.
- **Rarer -лово suffix**: зависалово ("strong passion"), глюкалово ("hallucinations"), бухалово
  ("intoxication"), стремалово ("squeamishness"), попадалово ("achieving a goal"), кидалово ("trick,
  treason").
- **Metaphor as a productive slang mechanism**: волосатые ("hairy" → hippies), соплевич ("ephedrin,"
  lit. from сопли "snot"), дороги ("tracks" — needle-track scars, lit. "roads"), баскетболист
  ("short guy," ironic — lit. "basketball player").
- **Ongoing lexical churn/register cycling** the author explicitly notes: тёлки/чувихи/герлы (older
  terms for "girls") being displaced by биксы/клюшки/вата (newer, more unflattering); качество for an
  attractive girl; guys called дядьки/пацики by girls; a well-dressed guy is прикинутый/упакованный;
  a guy with a car is ферзь/нешуточный.
- **Statistics the author cites** (unverified, presented as scholarly claim): ~60% of contemporary
  Russian slang relates to sex, ~30% to narcotics; ~50 new slang terms are estimated to enter the
  language every hour, matching a claimed English-language rate. железо ("hardware") cited as an
  example of a term that moved from slang into the standard professional lexicon.

**Cultural point — Тусовка ("partying Russian style") (pp. 134–135).** Speculated derivation from
тасовать ("to shuffle"); no equivalent concept in another language per the author. Three core senses:
(1) a relaxed/entertaining social gathering, (2) a group of people sharing mutual interests (тусовки
exist for nearly any affinity — guys, girls, politics, wealth, dancing, drinking), (3) the verb
тусоваться covering "to be with someone / spend time / speak with others / enjoy yourself / be
friends / share interests." Framed as a post-Soviet release valve — 70 years of censorship,
neighbor-surveillance, and informant culture gave way in the 1990s to тусовка as the answer to "what
now?" Also functions as a real-world/virtual social-networking concept (compared to Facebook/MySpace
circa the book's 2010 writing). Derivatives given: тус/туса/тусэ ("party, disco, group of friends"),
тусняк ("leisurely meeting of students/young people"), тусовщик ("playboy, party animal"),
тусовочный ("player, anything for a gathering"). This is a clean, well-documented `morphological_play`
family worth flagging for mechanics analysis — one root spawning at least 7 derivational forms with
overlapping-but-distinct senses.

## Closing Cultural Notes: Taboo — Russian Obscenities (pp. 143–145)

The book's final cultural-notes section, positioned to close out the Teenager Talk unit's escalation
into taboo register. Key claims (paraphrased, not quoted at length per copyright discipline):

- Ivan Turgenev (1882) coined the epithet "the great and powerful" for Russian; the author argues
  Russian obscenity is unmatched in expressive depth among Indo-European languages.
- Comparative claim: Jesse Sheidlower's *The F-Word* (3rd ed.) compiles ~270 pages of English
  taboo/euphemistic vocabulary; Russian lexicographer A. Plutser-Sarno's specialized study runs 926
  pages on genital terminology alone, citing 2,500+ terms for male/female genitalia and 1,000+ for
  the Russian "F-word" root (мат), climbing above 10,000 once derivatives/set expressions are
  counted.
- Russian obscenity is called мат, матерщина, брань, or матерный язык. The Mongol-Tatar-yoke origin
  story for мат is explicitly flagged by the author as a debunked myth. Mat is grammatically
  productive — words in this register inflect with full standard tense/aspect/part-of-speech
  paradigms, can substitute for nearly any part of speech, and can function as intensifying
  "punctuation."
- **Semantic drift/taboo-boundary-shift over time**, a concrete `taboo_inversion`/`euphemism_chain`-

## Grammar points

### Lesson 15 — Age, years/dates, numeral declension, negation, который, subjunctive, чтобы, foreign names
- **Age construction**: the person is in the **dative**, and the numeral-noun agreement follows the
  same один/два-три-четыре/пять+ pattern seen with ordinary nouns — Мари́не два́дцать оди́н год
  (numeral ending in оди́н → nom. sg. год), Мое́й ма́тери со́рок два го́да (ending in 2/3/4 → gen.
  sg. го́да), Пи́теру два́дцать во́семь лет (5+ → gen. pl., but **suppletive**: the genitive plural
  of год "year" is replaced by ле́т, literally the genitive plural of ле́то "summer," not a form of
  год at all) — a genuine suppletive-paradigm-borrowing across two etymologically distinct
  lexemes, worth flagging as a strong example for mechanics-analysis of how an invented language's
  number-agreement system might borrow an unrelated noun's plural purely to fill a paradigmatic gap.
  Future/past age is expressed with бу́дет/бы́ло (Пи́теру бу́дет два́дцать во́семь лет; Мари́не
  бы́ло два́дцать два го́да) — **except when the numeral ends in оди́н, which instead takes был**
  (Ей был два́дцать оди́н год) — a further gender/number-driven irregularity where "one year" is
  treated as grammatically singular-masculine (matching год's own gender) rather than defaulting to
  the neuter/impersonal бы́ло used elsewhere in this same construction.
- **Years and dates**: a year is expressed as an ordinal numeral + год (ты́сяча девятьсо́т
  девяно́сто второ́й год "1992," literally "the-one-thousand-nine-hundred-ninety-second year").
  "In [year]" uses в + prepositional case, but **only the final ordinal element of the compound
  declines** — в ты́сяча девятьсо́т девяно́сто второ́м году́ — an important exception to the
  general compound-numeral rule (seen in Lesson 12/13/14) that all elements of a compound normally
  decline; here specifically the year-numeral compound is exempted, with only the last word taking
  case marking. An alternative accusative is also permitted for decade-referring phrases (в
  тридца́тые го́ды "in the thirties," acc. pl. rather than prepositional). When a specific date
  (day + month) precedes the year, **the year itself drops to the genitive** regardless of whether
  the date phrase itself is nominative or genitive (пе́рвое января́ ты́сяча девятьсо́т девяно́сто
  второ́го го́да "1st January 1992" [date nominative] vs. пе́рвого января́ ты́сяча девятьсо́т
  девяно́сто вторго го́да "on 1st January 1992" [date genitive] — either way, the trailing year
  element stays genitive) — a genuinely non-obvious case-assignment rule where the year's case is
  fixed independent of the surrounding date phrase's own case.
- **Full cardinal-numeral declension paradigm**: two/два-две, три, четы́ре, пять, во́семь (as a
  stand-in for шесть–два́дцать/три́дцать, which decline like пять) are given complete six-case
  paradigms — two/три/четы́ре retain gender distinctions only in the nominative/accusative (masc./
  neut. два vs. fem. две; the oblique cases двух/трём/двумя́/двух are gender-invariant), while
  пять/во́семь-type numerals decline exactly like a soft-stem feminine noun across all six cases
  (пяти́, пяти́, пятью́, пяти́). **Crucially, the rule that де́ва/три/четы́ре take genitive singular
  and пять+ take genitive plural only holds when the numeral itself is in the nominative or
  accusative** — once the sentence structure forces the numeral into an oblique case (e.g.
  following a preposition), the accompanying noun and adjective switch to the **plural** of
  whatever case the numeral itself is in (Она́ оста́лась одна́ с тремя́ детьми́ — instrumental
  plural де́тьми́, not genitive singular) — a systematic case-based override of the numeral-governs-
  genitive rule that applies uniformly across every numeral once oblique. Within a compound
  numeral, **all elements decline together** (Э́то исто́рия о двадцати́ двух хоро́ших студе́нтах).
  Два/две, три, четы́ре are also the **only** numerals with a distinct animate accusative form
  (matching the genitive: Я ви́жу двух студе́нтов) — пять and above use the same accusative form
  regardless of animacy (Я ви́жу пять студе́нтов) — **and in practice, this animate-accusative
  distinction is dropped entirely once два/три/четы́ре appear as the final element of a compound
  numeral** (Я ви́жу два́дцать два студе́нта, not *двадцати́ двух студе́нтов), a further
  simplification specific to compound forms that the source flags explicitly as a "practice"
  deviation from the strict rule.
- **Negation — full negative-concord system**: Russian negative sentences require the verb to be
  negated with не, and *additionally* require every indefinite element in the clause (anyone,
  anything, anywhere, ever, either...or) to appear in its own negative form (никогда́ "never,"
  никто́ "no one," ничто́ "nothing," ни...ни "neither...nor," никако́й "not any," нигде́/нику́да
  "nowhere") — and these negative elements **stack freely**, unlike English where a second negative
  would cancel the first: Я нигде́ никогда́ не ви́дела так мно́го кана́лов "I have never seen so
  many canals anywhere" literally stacks three negative-marked elements (nowhere-never-not) without
  becoming a double negation or reverting to a positive meaning. This is one of the clearest,
  most systematically stated negative-concord descriptions in the course, and a strong candidate
  for mechanics-analysis: an invented slang variant could either extend this concord pattern
  productively or deliberately break it (using a single negative marker as a register-marking
  innovation) against this established baseline. When никто́/никако́й/ничто́ combine with a
  preposition, the preposition is **inserted inside the word itself**, between the ни- prefix and
  the pronoun root (ни о чём "about nothing," literally "ni-about-what") — a genuinely unusual
  morphosyntactic pattern (a preposition infixing into what looks like a single negative pronoun)
  worth flagging as a notable structural oddity. Никто́/ничто́ decline like кто/что; никако́й
  declines like the adjective како́й.
- **Кото́рый**: the general relative pronoun ("who/whom/which/that"), declined like a hard
  adjective, whose **gender and number agree with the antecedent noun** but whose **case is
  determined entirely by its own grammatical role within the relative clause** — a clean
  two-source-agreement system (antecedent controls φ-features, clause-internal role controls case)
  directly comparable to relative-pronoun systems in other Indo-European languages, and a good
  reference point for how a conlang's own relative-clause marker might split agreement sources.
  A closed exception: adjectival/relative clauses referring back to an indefinite pronoun rather
  than a specific noun use кто or что instead of кото́рый (Тот, кто ду́мает так… "he who thinks
  so…").
- **То, что**: renders English "what" as the head of a noun clause (Э́то зави́сит от того́, что
  тебя́ интересу́ет "It depends on what interests you") and also translates the English
  "preposition + -ing" gerund construction (Она́ начала́ с того́, что описа́ла войну́ "She began
  by describing the war" — literally "she began from that, that she described"), plus appearing in
  the fixed idioms де́ло в том, что / беда́ в том, что ("the fact/trouble is that") already
  encountered with де́ло in Lesson 12's де́ло в том, что.
- **Subjunctive mood**: formed uniformly by adding the invariant particle бы to the **past tense**
  of the verb, regardless of the intended time reference (он чита́л бы "he would read/would have
  read" — a single form covers both present-counterfactual and past-counterfactual meanings,
  disambiguated only by context) — бы cannot combine with any tense but the past, and may
  optionally precede rather than follow the verb. In hypothetical conditional clauses with е́сли
  ("if"), **both** clauses take the subjunctive, and бы attaches directly after е́сли rather than
  after the conditional clause's own verb (Е́сли бы я была́ свобо́дна вчера́ ве́чером, я пошла́ бы
  в кино́) — contrasted explicitly with **realizable** (non-hypothetical) conditions, where no
  subjunctive is used and, notably, **a future main clause requires a future — not present —
  conditional clause** (Е́сли я бу́ду свобо́дна…, я пойду́… — matching Russian's general disallowance
  of a "present tense with future meaning" inside an if-clause, unlike English's "if I am free…
  I shall go"). Бы also marks desirability in a main clause with no е́сли at all (Мне хоте́лось бы
  пое́хать… "I should like to go…"; Бы́ло бы хорошо́ "It would be good").
- **Что́бы — extended coverage building on Lesson 11**: что́бы + infinitive = "in order to" (as
  before), generally retained in Russian even where English drops "in order," **except after verbs
  of motion, where it is regularly omitted** (Мы пришли́ поговори́ть с ва́ми — no что́бы at all,
  despite the purpose-clause meaning) — a specific, lexically-conditioned licensing exception not
  previously stated. Что́бы + **past tense** (rather than infinitive) marks "in order that/so that"
  when the two clauses have **different subjects** (Мари́на написа́ла письмо́, что́бы её подру́га
  прие́хала "Marina wrote... so that her friend would come" — subject of the purpose clause differs
  from the main clause's subject); хоте́ть, чтобы + past tense functions the same way (Мари́на
  хо́чет, что́бы её подру́га прие́хала). **When the subject of both clauses is identical, что́бы is
  dropped entirely in favor of a plain infinitive** (Её подру́га хо́чет прие́хать — same-subject
  desire, no что́бы construction at all) — this same-subject/different-subject switch is a clean,
  well-defined control/raising-type distinction, directly comparable to English's own
  infinitive-vs-that-clause alternation with "want," and a strong candidate for mechanics-analysis
  as a model subject-identity-conditioned complementation split.
- **Declension of foreign names**: a foreign name declines as a Russian noun **only if its ending
  happens to match a native Russian declension-class ending** for the appropriate gender — a
  masculine name ending in a consonant (Ри́чард) or a feminine name ending in -а (Ба́рбара) decline
  normally; a name that doesn't match any native pattern for its gender does not decline at all
  regardless of what it looks like (Трэ́йси — a girl's name ending in -и, a vowel Russian nouns
  never end singular-nominative in — stays invariant; Ма́ргарет — a girl's name ending in a
  consonant, the wrong gender-ending pairing for feminine — likewise stays invariant). This is a
  clean, form-driven (not meaning-driven) borrowing-integration rule: the loanword is assigned to a
  native inflectional class purely by surface phonological shape matching, or excluded from
  inflection entirely if no native pattern fits — directly relevant to how an invented slang
  register might integrate (or conspicuously fail to integrate) loanwords from a contact language.

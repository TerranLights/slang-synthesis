# Russian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Survival Russian, Lesson 10

**Source:** N. B. Karavanova, *Survival Russian: A Course in Conversational Russian*, 6th ed.,
Russky Yazyk Kursy, Moscow, 2006. Lesson 10 (Weather. The Seasons. Favorite Season of the Year,
printed pp. 130-152 / PDF 132-154). Continued from `068h_survival_russian.md`.

**Coverage note:** grammar and vocabulary captured; drills/dialogues/quiz skipped (recombination of
already-captured vocabulary, confirmed across pp. 142-152). No handwritten marginalia found. This
lesson's grammar section is a major structural flagship: the book's first explicit treatment of
Russian's **impersonal construction** (subjectless sentences for weather/nature/a person's felt
condition) and **indefinite-personal construction** (3pl-with-no-subject for unattributed reported
speech), both central to how Russian encodes modality and evidentiality without dedicated lexical
markers.

## Grammar points

### люби́ть/лови́ть, купа́ться/ката́ться, роди́ться/прие́хать, ду́мать conjugations — Lesson 10 (p. 133-141, Tables 39-41, 46)

Five more full paradigms extending the aspect-pair pattern. Notably купа́ться/ката́ться are
reflexive verbs whose -ся suffix is retained across every conjugated form (я купа́юсь, ты
купа́ешься), unlike Lesson 2's учи́ться which only appeared in past tense so far — this is the
first full present/past/future reflexive-verb paradigm the book gives, confirming -ся attaches
after the person/number ending consistently (-юсь/-ешься/-ется/-емся/-етесь/-ются). ду́мать is
given a dedicated five-way government note (п. 141): with prepositional case + о (ду́мать о ко́м?
о чём?), with the conjunction что, with the compound conjunction о том, что ("thinking about the
fact that..."), with question words (како́й, как, куда́, где), and as a fixed idiom (Как вы
ду́маете? "What do you think?") — a genuinely rich single-verb government inventory worth flagging
for mechanics analysis as an example of how one Russian verb's syntactic frame varies by the kind
of complement it takes.

### Comparative and superlative adjectives — Lesson 10 (p. 135, Table 42)

Regular comparative: adjective stem + -ее (holóдный→холодне́е) or, for some stems, a stem-final
consonant mutation + -е (large group exemplified by жа́ркий→жа́рче, дорого́й-type к→ч mutation
inferred though not the exact form shown here) used with чем ("than") for explicit comparison.
Several **irregular suppletive comparatives** are given directly rather than derived regularly:
большо́й→бо́льше, ма́ленький→ме́ньше, хоро́ший→лу́чше, плохо́й→ху́же — four high-frequency
adjectives whose comparative form bears no transparent morphological relationship to their
positive stem, paralleling the брать/взять and уйти́/вы́йти-style irregularity already flagged
for verbs. Superlative is formed analytically with са́мый + the adjective in its regular positive
(agreeing) form (са́мый холо́дный, са́мый лу́чший is not shown — the table gives са́мый хоро́ший
directly instead, implying са́мый composes with the base adjective, not the comparative stem).

### Accusative case fully cross-tabulated by animacy × number — Lesson 10 (p. 136-138, Tables 43-45)

Three consolidated tables unify every accusative-case rule the book has built up piecemeal since
Lesson 7 into one coherent system, now explicitly keyed to animacy for both singular and plural,
for both nouns and adjectives: **masculine animate accusative = genitive form** (singular and
plural alike); **masculine inanimate accusative = nominative form** (singular and plural alike);
**feminine singular accusative is always -у/-ю regardless of animacy** (the one case-slot where
animacy doesn't matter); **feminine plural accusative follows the same animate=genitive/
inanimate=nominative split as masculine**; **neuter accusative always equals nominative** (singular
and plural), since — per the book's own reasoning — grammatically neuter referents needing the
animate treatment essentially don't occur in this vocabulary. Adjectives modifying a noun inherit
the noun's animacy-driven accusative-ending choice rather than having independent animacy marking
of their own — i.e., animacy is a property of the noun that adjectives agree with, not a separate
adjectival category. This three-table sequence is the clearest single statement in the book so far
of animacy as a cross-cutting grammatical category layered on top of the ordinary gender/number/
case system — directly relevant to later slang-mechanics work, since animacy-based case selection
is a genuinely unusual (from an English-speaker's perspective) morphosyntactic feature that could
plausibly be borrowed, exaggerated, or subverted in an invented conlang.

### Indefinite personal construction — Lesson 10 (p. 139)

A sentence describing an action by an unspecified/unimportant agent uses the verb in 3rd person
plural with **no overt subject at all** (not even "they"): Мне говори́ли, что в Москве́ хо́лодно
("I was told that Moscow is cold," literally "[they] told me..."). This is distinct from an
ordinary 3pl sentence with a stated subject (Мои́ друзья́ говори́ли... "My friends told me...") —
the indefinite-personal reading is signaled purely by the *absence* of any subject noun/pronoun
alongside a 3pl verb, not by any special verb form. This is the grammatical machinery underlying
this book's own earlier examples like Lesson 7's "Вам звоня́т из фи́рмы" (impersonal-business-call
construction) and Lesson 10's own weather-report vocabulary (Передава́ли по ра́дио, что...) — the
same subjectless-3pl strategy recurs across multiple registers/contexts in this book without being
named as a unified grammar point until this lesson.

### Impersonal construction — Lesson 10 (p. 139-140)

A broader category of subjectless Russian sentences, covering four distinct functions per the
source's own breakdown: (a) nature/environmental conditions (На у́лице хо́лодно — "It is cold
outside," no grammatical subject at all); (b) a person's own felt condition, using a dative
experiencer + a predicative adverb (Мне хо́лодно "I am cold," literally "to-me [it-is] cold" —
directly parallel to the age construction from Lesson 6 and the нра́виться construction from
Lesson 9, extending the same dative-experiencer strategy to a third distinct semantic domain);
(c) a further "involuntary/independent-of-will" subtype using **reflexive verbs** хо́чется/
ка́жется + dative experiencer + infinitive (Мне хо́чется гуля́ть "I feel like taking a walk" —
contrasted directly against the plain-agentive Я хочу́ гуля́ть "I want to take a walk," a genuine
volitional/involuntary-desire distinction realized purely through reflexivization + case-frame
shift, not a separate lexical item); (d) necessity/possibility/impossibility via на́до/ну́жно/
необходи́мо/мо́жно/нельзя́ + dative + infinitive (Ему́ нельзя́ купа́ться "He is forbidden to
swim"). A closing comparison table extends the same personal/impersonal contrast to
absence-of-referent sentences: У меня́ нет журна́ла ("I don't have a magazine" — genitive-of-
negation, cross-referencing Lesson 6's rule) and Сего́дня нет дождя́ ("It's not raining today" —
the same genitive-of-negation logic applied to a weather noun rather than a possessed object),
unifying possession-negation and weather-negation under one shared grammatical mechanism.

## Grammar points

### Section 10 — Обозначение срока, разделяющего действия (marking the interval separating two actions)
This section systematizes constructions that name *how much* time separates two events, going beyond
section 9's precedence markers (which only establish relative order). Two paired, mirror-image
constructions anchor it: **за + accusative + до + genitive** ("X time before Y": Мы пришли на вокзал
за пять минут до отправления поезда) and **через + accusative + после + genitive** ("X time after Y":
Мы пришли на вокзал через пять минут после отправления поезда) — the source glosses both with an
explicit paraphrase of the entailed sequence of events, underscoring that these are strictly about the
separating interval, not about precedence alone. The adverbs **задолго до** ("long before") and
**незадолго до** ("shortly before") layer a magnitude judgment onto the до-precedence construction
without naming an exact interval.

A second family covers comparative time-differences: **на + accusative + раньше/позже(позднее)**
(Они приехали сюда на два дня раньше, чем мы) has an instrumental-case bookish counterpart
(**instrumental + раньше/позже**: Они приехали сюда двумя днями раньше нас) that the source explicitly
flags as literary in register and less frequent than the на-variant. The same two constructions extend
to **старше/моложе (младше)** for age differences (Брат на два года старше, чем сестра / Брат двумя
годами старше сестры). In all of these, the comparison target may be marked either with a чем-clause
in the nominative or with a bare genitive of comparison — the two are fully interchangeable (на год
раньше, чем я = на год раньше меня).

Finally, **(accusative) + (тому) назад** expresses elapsed time back from the present ("X ago"): Он
родился двести семьдесят пять лет тому назад — тому is optional and freely dropped.

### Section 11 — Обозначение времени повторяющегося действия (marking time of a repeated/habitual action)
This section catalogs six distinct constructions for habitual/repeated time reference, each with its
own exception set the source is careful to spell out:

1. **каждый + accusative (no preposition)**: Каждый день, каждую субботу. Exception: the four nouns
   момент, период, эпоха, эра switch to **в + каждый + accusative** instead (В каждую эпоху происходят
   изменения климата) — an irregular subclass rather than a productive rule.
2. **Instrumental plural of a time noun** (часами, вечерами, неделями, годами, веками) for an
   unbounded, extended stretch — intensifiable with целыми/долгими/длинными (Целыми часами он сидел у
   камина и смотрел на огонь). Explicitly does not form with day-of-week names, month names, or the
   words лето/осень.
3. **по + dative plural** (по утрам, по средам, по выходным, по чётным/нечётным дням) — intensifiable
   with целый (По целым неделям он не бывал у нас). Explicitly does not form with секунда, минута,
   весна, лето, осень, год, or month names — a disjoint exception set from construction #2's, worth
   keeping distinct rather than merging.
4. **... раз(а) в + accusative** for an explicit frequency count: Один раз в неделю мы ходим в бассейн.
5. **еже- prefixed adverbs/adjectives** (ежесекундно, ежеминутно, ежечасно, ежедневно, еженедельно,
   ежемесячно, ежеквартально, ежегодно) — marked bookish in register by the source, and explicitly do
   not form from day-of-week, month, or season names (a third, again-distinct exception set).
6. **Irregular/non-quantified repetition**: время от времени, временами, иногда, часто, редко, не раз.
   The source draws out a specific minimal pair here: **не раз** ("repeatedly," positive polarity — Она
   не раз путешествовали вместе) versus **ни разу** ("never, not even once," negative polarity only,
   co-occurring with a negated verb and intensified by the particle ни — Она ни разу не видела его).

A closing blanket rule: **all repeated-action time constructions in this section require an
imperfective main verb** — stated as a single governing constraint rather than construction-by-
construction, distinguishing this section's aspect behavior from section 9's precedence constructions
(which favored perfective for single completed events, per `061c_...md`).

### Reading text: История русского календаря (History of the Russian calendar)
Paraphrased, not quoted at length. Pre-Christian Rus' new year began at the new moon nearest the
vernal equinox; Christianization in 988 CE brought the Byzantine "dating from the creation of the
world" epoch (5508 BCE) and the Julian calendar, with the new year moving to March 1, then (from the
year 7000, i.e. 1492 CE) to September 1. Peter the Great's reform (decreed 19 December 7208, i.e. late
1699) moved new year to January 1, 1700, coinciding with the declared start of a new century;
festivities included fir-branch home decoration and a fireworks display Peter himself lit on Red
Square, and the first Russian printed calendar (recording eclipses, moon phases, sunrise/sunset times,
day/night length) dates to this reform. Russia stayed on the Julian calendar while most of Europe had
already shifted to the Gregorian by the late 17th century, causing diplomatic friction; only after the
1917 revolution, in 1918, did Russia adopt the Gregorian calendar — by then a 13-day gap had
accumulated, resolved by the specific mechanism of declaring the day after January 31, 1918 to be
February 14 (not February 1), so February 1918 had only 15 days. The Russian Orthodox Church never
adopted the new style; the text notes only the Jerusalem and Serbian Orthodox churches share this.

**"Это интересно" sidebar (etymology of the days of the week):** paraphrased. The seven-day week is
traced to ancient astronomers observing each lunar phase lasting roughly seven days; the days were
named for celestial bodies (Saturn/Saturday, Moon/Monday, Mars/Tuesday, Mercury/Wednesday,
Jupiter/Thursday, Venus/Friday, Sun/Sunday — some of these survive in European-language weekday names
today). Russian and other Slavic-language weekday names instead track ordinal position and custom:
воскресенье ("Sunday") was earlier called неделя ("non-doing," a rest day), with "воскресенье"
(< воскреснуть, "to be resurrected") only displacing it from the 16th century; понедельник = "the
[day] after неделя" (1st), вторник = 2nd, среда = "middle," четверг = 4th, пятница = 5th; суббота is
traced to the Hebrew "shabbat" ("rest," "peace").

### Reading text: Старый Новый год (Old New Year)
Paraphrased. Explains a distinctly Russian tradition: an unofficial holiday falling 13 days after the
official (Gregorian, New Style) New Year, observed on the night of January 13–14, because pre-1918
Russia's New Year (Old Style, January 1) fell on what the Gregorian calendar calls January 14. The
custom persists as a sentimental echo of the Julian-calendar New Year even though nobody alive today
personally experienced the calendar switch. Observance mirrors the "real" New Year in miniature
(candles, a champagne bottle opened five minutes before midnight, wishes made at the chimes) but more
modestly. The text situates this within **Святки** ("the holy days," from Christmas to Epiphany on
January 19), traditionally a time of house visits, singing, costume/mask-wearing, and **гадание**
(fortune-telling) — practiced chiefly by unmarried young women hoping to divine a future husband
(methods cited: card-reading, midnight mirror-gazing by candlelight, and asking a stranger's name in
the street on the premise that the future husband's name would share its first letter). The text closes
with the observation that Russia effectively "wins the championship" for Christmas/New Year
observances, celebrating each twice (Old Style and New Style).

### Reading text: Обычаи праздновать Рождество и Новый год в Европе (begins this range, continues into `061e_...md`)
Comparative-folklore reading text surveying Christmas/New Year customs across several European
countries, paraphrased. Germanic peoples decorated homes with green branches (originally flowering
fruit-tree branches forced into bloom by standing them in water weeks ahead; later replaced by
evergreen fir) for luck/health; northern Germany's Christmas Eve dinner traditionally included symbolic
"seed of life" foods (fish roe, peas, beans, eggs, porridge). England/Scotland decorate with ivy and
mistletoe; before New Year, a bright hearth fire is lit and the household gathers by it, and at
midnight the head of household silently opens the door to "let the old year out and the new year in."
Austrian housewives bake a special bread about a week before Christmas, with the whole household
participating (a folk belief ties a richer harvest to more eyes watching the dough); the bread is marked
with crosses and credited with protective/health-giving power. Italian folklore holds that on Christmas
night rivers run with oil and springs with honey; people historically gathered "new water" from a
spring at midnight hoping for wealth, a custom some regions still observe on the morning of January 1;
Italians also hold that the New Year calls for casting off the old and bad, leading to old furniture,
clothes, and crockery being thrown from windows into the street. In Greece, great importance attaches
to whoever is the first to enter the house after the New Year begins (often the homeowner himself,
performing a small ritualized in-two-steps/out-two-steps doorway ceremony inviting in luck and
happiness while banishing misfortune, repeated three times). In Sweden, children collect old crockery
for months to smash at the doors of people they love/respect at New Year, and the "culprit" is caught
and rewarded with sweets and nuts. In Spain, young people in elaborate masks, led by figures dressed as
an "old man" and "old woman" who ceremonially sweep the floor on their way out of each house (to expel
impurity), go caroling house to house on New Year's Eve; some regions ceremonially burn an effigy of
the old year. In Russia, New Year is observed more elaborately than Christmas and is for many the
favorite holiday: families gather at a festive table to see off the old year first, then at the chiming
of the [Kremlin] clock tower bells everyone stands, drinks champagne, and makes a wish — cheerfulness
being encouraged by the folk belief that however one greets the New Year, so one will live it. Sourced
by the book itself to И.А. Климишин's article "Заметки о нашем календаре" ("Notes on our calendar") —
worth flagging since it's one of the few reading texts in this book with an explicit secondary-source
attribution rather than being the authors' own composition.

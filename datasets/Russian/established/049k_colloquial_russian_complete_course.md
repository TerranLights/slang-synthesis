## Grammar points

### Lesson 16 — Comparatives, telling time, comparing ages
- **Long comparative (бо́лее + adjective)**: бо́лее "more" precedes the adjective and is invariant
  regardless of the adjective's own case/number/gender, while the adjective itself still declines
  normally to agree with its noun (бо́лее удо́бные кварти́ры; они́ живу́т в бо́лее удо́бных
  кварти́рах — бо́лее unchanged across both). Бо́лее requires the long-form adjective, and is used
  attributively (modifying a noun directly), unlike the short comparative below.
- **Short comparative (-ee/-ей)**: used predicatively, where English "to be" sits between the noun
  and the adjective (Моя́ кварти́ра удо́бнее "My flat is more comfortable"). A substantial closed
  list of adjectives instead take an **irregular short comparative ending in bare -e** (not -ee):
  бли́зкий→бли́же, высо́кий→вы́ше, дешёвый→деше́вле, лёгкий→ле́гче, ни́зкий→ни́же, просто́й→про́ще,
  ре́дкий→ре́же, ча́стый→ча́ще, хоро́ший→лу́чше (suppletive), я́ркий→я́рче, большо́й→бо́льше
  (suppletive), далёкий→да́льше, дорого́й→доро́же, молодо́й→моло́же/мла́дше (two distinct forms —
  see below), плохо́й→ху́же (suppletive), ра́нний→ра́ньше, ста́рый→ста́рше/старе́е (two distinct
  forms — see below), широ́кий→ши́ре; мно́го→бо́льше and ма́ло→ме́ньше pattern the same way. This is
  a substantial irregular-morphology class — comparable in density to the genitive-plural
  irregularity cluster from Lesson 12 — worth flagging as another concentration point of
  Russian's inflectional irregularity.
- **A remarkable literal/figurative-conditioned split for "older/younger" and "higher/lower"**:
  ста́рший and мла́дший (declinable, adjectival comparatives) are used **only of people**, in the
  sense "elder/younger [sibling]" or "senior/junior [in rank]" — ста́рший брат "elder brother" — 
  while ста́рше and старе́е (the two competing *short* comparatives of ста́рый) split by **animacy/
  concreteness of the referent**: ста́рше compares the *age of people* (person X ста́рше person Y),
  старе́е compares the *age of things* (бо́лее ста́рый университе́т/*ста́рее* университе́т — a
  building or institution). Молодо́й similarly splits моло́же (compares ages of people) vs. бо́лее
  молодо́й (of things). Separately, вы́сший/ни́зший (the declinable comparatives) are used
  **only figuratively** (вы́сшее образова́ние "higher education," an institutional/abstract sense)
  while бо́лее высо́кий/бо́лее ни́зкий are used for **literal physical height** (бо́лее высо́кое
  зда́ние "a taller building"). This is a dense, multi-layered case of a single comparative
  concept (older, taller) being split across **two or three lexically distinct comparative forms**
  according to (a) animacy of the referent and (b) literal vs. figurative sense — an unusually rich
  example for mechanics-analysis of how a language can grammaticalize a semantic distinction
  (concrete/abstract, animate/inanimate) through the *choice of comparative morphology itself*
  rather than through any separate marker.
- **По- prefixed short comparatives**: added to a short comparative, по- means "a little more" in
  ordinary use (пора́ньше "a little earlier") but, when combined with как мо́жно, instead means
  "as X as possible" (как мо́жно быстре́е "as quickly as possible") — the source explicitly labels
  this second sense "paradoxical" relative to the first, since "a little more quick" and "as quick
  as possible" are near-opposite degrees of intensification riding on the same prefix+comparative
  combination, disambiguated only by the surrounding как мо́жно frame.
- **Чем vs. genitive-of-comparison for "than"**: "than" is either the conjunction чем or (with a
  short comparative only) expressed by putting the compared object in the **genitive** with no
  conjunction at all (Телеви́дение интере́снее теа́тра "TV is more interesting than theatre" —
  genitive теа́тра alone renders "than theatre"). Genitive-of-comparison is generally preferred over
  чем with a short comparative, but **чем is obligatory** in four specific circumstances: (1) after
  the long comparative with бо́лее (which cannot itself take a genitive complement); (2) whenever the
  second half of the comparison is not simply a noun/pronoun (until now genitive-of-comparison
  requires a genitive-inflectable single nominal, so an adverb or clause like "at home" [до́ма]
  forces чем: до́ма лу́чше, чем здесь); (3) whenever the compared objects are not both in the
  nominative case elsewhere in the sentence (я бо́льше интересу́юсь теа́тром, чем телеви́дением —
  both objects already instrumental, so genitive-of-comparison isn't available); (4) whenever the
  second object of comparison is a possessive его́/её/их (genitive-of-comparison would be
  ambiguous with the possessive genitive already in use: на́ша кварти́ра краси́вее, чем его́). This
  four-way conditioning of a single lexical choice (чем vs. bare genitive) by syntactic context is a
  clean case study for mechanics-analysis of how a comparison-marking strategy can be
  context-sensitively restricted rather than freely optional.
- **Гора́здо/намно́го** intensify a comparative ("much more"), analogous to English "much" +
  comparative, and cannot be used with the positive (non-comparative) adjective.
- **Comparing ages with на + accusative**: "X is N years older/younger than Y" uses the short
  comparative followed by the person in the accusative and на + the number of years in the
  accusative (Мой брат ста́рше меня́ на три го́да "My brother is three years older than me") — a
  distinct construction from the plain age-statement dative construction of Lesson 15, layering
  на + accusative onto a comparative to mark the *degree* of difference, comparable to English "by
  three years."
- **Telling time — the "half of the next hour" system**: Russian names the half-hour by reference
  to the **upcoming** hour, not the one just passed: полови́на седьмо́го (informally contracted to
  полседьмо́го) is literally "half of the seventh [hour]" = "half past six" — the ordinal names the
  hour ahead, unlike English's backward-looking "half past six." The same forward-reference logic
  extends to quarter-hours: че́тверть шесто́го "a quarter of the sixth" = "a quarter past five,"
  and — notably — без че́тверти семь "without a quarter, seven" = "a quarter to seven," where the
  genitive object ('s quarter) is subtracted *from* the named hour rather than added to the
  previous one. Minutes past the hour likewise reference the *coming* hour genitively (два́дцать
  мину́т восьмо́го "twenty [minutes] of the eighth" = "twenty past seven"), while minutes *to* the
  hour use без + genitive-of-minutes + the nominative hour itself (без пяти́ де́вять "without five,
  nine" = "five to nine"). This whole apparatus is thoroughly forward-hour-referencing in a way
  fundamentally different from English's backward-hour-referencing "half past"/"quarter past" — an
  excellent, well-documented candidate for mechanics-analysis of how a language's time-telling
  convention can encode a systematically different temporal "anchor point" (looking ahead to the
  next hour vs. back to the last one) purely through case/preposition choice.
- **A.M./P.M. via genitive time-of-day nouns**: Russian has no direct a.m./p.m. equivalent; instead
  но́чи ("of the night") or утра́ ("of the morning") disambiguate a.m., дня ("of the day") or
  ве́чера ("of the evening") disambiguate p.m., all as genitive nouns following the hour count (два
  часа́ но́чи "2 a.m."; шесть часо́в ве́чера "6 p.m.") — a system built entirely from ordinary
  genitive-of-time-period nouns rather than any dedicated a.m./p.m. marker, again a useful contrast
  point for mechanics-analysis (a slang variant could invent its own two-part-of-day split using a
  different anchor set, e.g. work/rest rather than night/morning/day/evening).
- **Дава́й(те) + 1st-person-plural perfective future** = "let's…" (Дава́йте посмо́трим, каки́е
  мю́зиклы иду́т сейча́с "Let's see which musicals are on now") — дава́й/дава́йте itself doesn't
  inflect for the following verb's aspect/person beyond selecting the informal/formal address form.

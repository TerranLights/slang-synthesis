## Coverage note

**PDF text-layer cipher (critical, read before using this file's Cyrillic).** This PDF has a
genuine text layer, but Cyrillic text throughout the book (all Russian-language examples, glosses,
paradigm tables) is encoded as a fixed, decodable font-substitution cipher rather than as real
Cyrillic Unicode codepoints. `pdftotext` extracts each Cyrillic glyph as the **Latin letter that
sits in the same physical keyboard position on a standard ЙЦУКЕН Russian keyboard layout as the
QWERTY key printed in the font's own encoding** — i.e. the glyph the book's Russian-italic font
displays as "р" gets extracted as the Latin letter that occupies that same key ("h" on a US QWERTY
layout, since "h" and "р" share a physical key position across the two layouts). This was decoded
and cross-verified against 25+ known word/gloss pairs spread across widely-separated pages
(including a full paradigm of cardinal numerals on p. 122, decoded and checked against the book's
own paradigm description) before being trusted for this file, per the extraction spec's guidance on
this exact class of gotcha. The full confirmed mapping (Latin key → Cyrillic letter, case-preserving):

```
q й   w ц   e у   r к   t е   y н   u г   i ш   o щ   p з
a ф   s ы   d в   f а   g п   h р   j о   k л   l д
z я   x ч   c с   v м   b и   n т   m ь
' э   ; ж   [ х   ] ъ   , б   . ю
```

Ordinary English prose in the book (its own explanatory text, author citations, transliterated
proper names in the bibliography) is set in a normal Latin font and is **not** cipher-encoded —
only text actually typeset in the book's Cyrillic-italic example font is affected. Because
`pdftotext` strips font information, this file's Vocabulary table was built by mining the book's
own `word ‘gloss’`-style single-quote citation pattern (Timberlake glosses nearly every Russian
form he cites this way), decoding the cipher token, and manually reviewing every candidate row —
about 110 low-confidence/garbled automated matches (column-scrambled paradigm-table entries,
truncated contractions from the `'` apostrophe overlapping the regex boundary, coincidentally
Latin-lookalike proper-noun citations from the English bibliographic text) were discarded rather
than force-included. A handful of retained rows have a bracketed clarification appended to the
Gloss where the book's own translation was for a larger clause rather than the cited word alone
(e.g. a prepositional-case noun form glossed with its case meaning). No vision-reading was needed
for this file (real text layer throughout) — **Vision Reading Confidence is `n/a` for every row**;
in place of Transcription Confidence (also `n/a` — not a subtitle/transcript source) the Notes
column flags every row's origin as cipher-decoded so the decode step is auditable per entry, per
this spec's per-row confidence-flagging discipline.

**Coverage rule applied.** Every grammar point in the assigned page range is paraphrased below,
organized by the book's own numbered section headings. Vocabulary is comprehensive within what the
book itself treats as citable, glossed word-forms (its own `'gloss'`-citation convention) — this is
a linguistic reference grammar, not a vocabulary-list textbook, so its "distinct vocabulary items"
are the words it explicitly glosses as evidence for a grammatical point, not a running prose
vocabulary. Repeated drill-style re-use of the same word without a new gloss is not re-listed.
Multi-word/clause-level illustrative examples (case-government examples, quantifier-construction
examples) are given as short illustrative quotes inline in the Grammar points section rather than
forced into single-word Vocabulary rows, per the copyright-discipline rule that a short quote is
fine but bulk reproduction is not.

**No marginalia risk.** This source has a genuine text layer throughout the assigned range — no
vision-reading was performed, so the "distinguish print from handwritten marginalia" guard does not
apply to this file.

## Grammar points

### 1.1 The Russian language, and levels of language (pp. 1-8)

Timberlake frames the book as a comprehensive description of standard modern Russian (excluding
word-derivation/morphology-as-lexicon). Russian descends from East Slavic dialects that split off
by the ninth century; the modern standard crystallized around the Moscow dialect over the
eighteenth century, layering French/Byzantine/English lexical borrowing onto a Slavic base.
Timberlake explicitly notes register stratification recognized by the Russian tradition itself:
archaic → bookish → standard (normative) → colloquial (разговорная речь) → substandard/uncultured
(просторечие) — a five-way scale this project should track for any future comparative slang work
(p. 3-4), rather than treating Russian register as a simple formal/colloquial binary.

### 1.2 Describing Russian grammar: notational conventions (pp. 9-17)

The book explains its own notational apparatus: angle-bracket subscripts mark grammatical category
values on a form (e.g. case, number), and a set of abbreviations recur throughout (nom/gen/dat/
acc/ins/prep for the six cases; sg/pl for number; msc/fem/nt for gender; ipf/pf for imperfective/
perfective aspect). Useful for reading the rest of the book but not itself lexical content.

### 1.3 The Cyrillic alphabet: history and mechanics (pp. 18-27)

A brief history of Cyrillic's origin from Greek uncial script via the Glagolitic-adjacent
missionary tradition (Cyril and Methodius, ninth century), then a systematic explanation of how the
modern 33-letter alphabet encodes Russian phonology: (1) most consonant letters come in hard/soft
pairs distinguished not by the consonant letter itself but by the *following vowel letter*
(а/я, о/ё, у/ю, э/е, ы/и each spell a hard-vowel/soft-vowel pair, with the second member of each
pair signaling palatalization of the preceding consonant); (2) й spells the glide [j]; (3) ъ and ь
(the "hard sign" and "soft sign") carry no vowel sound of their own — ь marks a preceding consonant
as palatalized word-finally or before a consonant, ъ marks the *absence* of palatalization at a
morpheme boundary before a soft-vowel letter (so a following я/е/ё/ю is not read as palatalizing);
(4) several letters are idiosyncratic for etymological/historical reasons rather than live
phonological ones (щ as a fixed spelling for a long soft "sh"-like sound, the letter's odd
[ɕː] value not synchronically alternating with anything). Section 1.3.7 covers standard
scholarly transliteration systems (Library of Congress vs. a "scholarly"/linguistic system),
relevant groundwork for citing any future Russian-derived conlang forms consistently.

### 2.1-2.2 Vowels: the five/six-vowel system and reduction (pp. 28-45)

Standard Russian has a five-vowel system under stress (и, э~е, а, о, у — Timberlake treats ы as a
positional variant of и conditioned by a preceding hard consonant rather than an independent sixth
phoneme, following the Leningrad/Moscow-phonology-school-adjacent analysis he adopts). Unstressed
vowels undergo systematic *reduction*: unstressed а/о merge toward a schwa-like or reduced-[a]
value depending on distance from stress and following a hard vs. soft consonant (akanye/ikanye),
and unstressed е/я reduce toward [ɪ]-like realizations. This vowel-reduction system is the single
largest source of spelling-vs-pronunciation mismatch in Russian and would need to be modeled
explicitly for any conlang slang deriving from casual/fast Russian speech, where reduction is more
extreme than in careful speech (p. 33-45, with p. 45 introducing неопределённое hesitation on
degree of reduction across registers).

### 2.3 Consonants: palatalization, assimilation, the glide [j], geminates (pp. 46-70)

The hard/soft (palatalized/non-palatalized) consonant contrast is described as the single most
important consonantal dimension in Russian phonology — nearly every consonant has a soft
counterpart, conditioning both spelling (§1.3) and a range of assimilatory processes: **regressive
voicing assimilation** at obstruent clusters (a voiced obstruent devoices before a voiceless one
and vice versa, including word-finally, where all obstruents devoice), and **regressive
palatalization assimilation**, where a consonant optionally/variably softens before a following
soft consonant, a process that is lexically and stylistically graded rather than categorical (more
consistent in some clusters/registers than others) — flagged as a place where sociolinguistic
variation data (rather than a single categorical rule) genuinely matters (p. 56, citing corpus data
from MKhAT-adjacent recorded speech as one evidentiary source). The glide [j] (§2.3.5) has a
special distribution tied to the soft-vowel-letter spelling convention of §1.3. Geminate consonants
(§2.3.8) arise mostly at morpheme boundaries (prefix+root, root+suffix) rather than being lexically
underlying, and vary in whether they surface as a true long consonant or simplify, again with
register-graded variability.

### 2.4 Phonological variation: idiomaticity, systemic factors, Old Muscovite style (pp. 71-80)

Timberlake explicitly catalogs Russian phonological variation as (a) idiomatic/lexically-specific
(certain words behave irregularly for purely lexical/historical reasons), (b) systemically
motivated (variation predictable from independently-needed rules interacting), and (c) a
sociolinguistic layer he calls "Old Muscovite" pronunciation — a prestige, now largely archaic,
stage-and-broadcast-tradition pronunciation norm (e.g. a distinctively soft realization of certain
consonant clusters) that persisted in formal/theatrical registers well after everyday speech had
shifted, and is now itself a marked, almost archaic register cue (p. 76-80) — directly relevant
groundwork for modeling "old-fashioned formal" register in a derived slang system.

### 2.5 Vowel~zero alternations ("vowel grades") (pp. 81-91)

A pervasive pattern where a word's paradigm alternates between a vowel and its absence (a "fleeting
vowel") depending on the following ending — e.g. a nominative-singular form with a vowel before a
final consonant loses that vowel once a vocalic case-ending is added. Timberlake treats this as a
synchronic alternation triggered by the presence/absence of a following vowel, historically
descending from the loss of the reduced vowels ъ/ь (jers) in early East Slavic. This is a genuine
morphophonological alternation any noun-declension modeling needs to track per-lexeme (not fully
predictable from the surface form alone) (p. 86-91).

### 3.1-3.2 Verb conjugation: categories and conjugation classes (pp. 92-115)

Russian verbs are organized into two broad conjugation classes (traditionally "first" and "second"
conjugation, here labeled by their characteristic thematic vowel/consonant pattern — an "e"-
conjugation and an "i"-conjugation) cross-cut by the aspect system (imperfective/perfective) that
is lexical/derivational rather than inflectional: aspect pairs are typically related by prefixation
or suffixation rather than being forms of "the same" paradigm cell. Verbal categories tracked
include person, number, tense (a reduced two-way non-past/past system, with "future" expressed
periphrastically for imperfectives), mood, and voice/reflexivity (via the -ся/-сь clitic).
Conjugation-class membership is not fully predictable from the infinitive alone, requiring
stem-final consonant/vowel alternations to be learned per verb or per subclass (p. 96-108).

### 3.2.8-3.2.9 Irregular conjugation and secondary imperfectivization (pp. 116-121)

A small closed set of verbs is irregular (suppletive or with unpredictable stem alternations).
**Secondary imperfectivization** — deriving a new imperfective from an already-derived perfective
(itself formed from a base imperfective plus a prefix) via a further suffix — is the main
productive mechanism for building new aspect pairs once a prefix has "used up" the base verb's
original imperfective slot; Timberlake treats this as the single most productive verb-derivation
process in the modern language, directly relevant to any future in-universe slang verb-coinage
modeled on Russian's aspect system.

### 3.2 (numerals excerpt): cardinal numeral declension (p. 122, cited above in Vocabulary)

Single digits 5-9 and the teens 11-19 decline like feminine soft-stem nouns (Declension III/IIIa in
the book's own class labels), historically compound forms for the teens (a numeral root + an old
locative of 'ten'). The decades 20/30 and 50-80 are themselves compounds whose *both* halves
decline in careful/standard usage, though corpus web-data cited by the author (p. 122, ~10%
attestation rate) shows the first half increasingly left undeclined in casual written Russian — an
attested, book-documented instance of morphological leveling/simplification in colloquial register,
directly relevant to this project's slang-mechanics interest. "Round" numerals (100, 40, 90) have a
minimal two-form declension (one form for nom/acc, another for all oblique cases); the hundreds
200-900 are compounds of the units digit with an oblique-plural-like second element.

### 3.3 Pronouns: third person, possessives, and irregular declension (pp. 123-135)

Third-person pronouns (он/она/оно/они) double as anaphoric definite articles in discourse, with a
special н-initial allomorph appearing after most prepositions (a residue of an old prefixed form) —
this is the source of many of this file's "pronoun (oblique form)" vocabulary rows (него, ним, них,
ней etc.), which the book uses throughout Chapter 4 as its running illustration of case government.
Possessive adjectives (мой/твой/наш/ваш/свой) and a handful of irregular-declension items (весь
'all', сам 'self/oneself', один 'one') round out the closed pronominal/quantifier-adjacent class.

### 3.4-3.5 Quantifiers and adjectives (pp. 136-158)

Quantifier words (много 'much/many', мало 'little/few', сколько 'how much/many', столько 'that
much/many') govern a genitive complement and have their own partial declension, sitting at the
boundary between adjective and numeral behavior. Adjectives have a full ("long-form") declension
used attributively, a reduced predicative ("short-form") paradigm used mainly in predicate position
(increasingly restricted/archaic-feeling in some uses per the book's own register commentary),
comparative/superlative formation (periphrastic более/самый plus the adjective, or a suppletive/
synthetic comparative for a closed set of common adjectives), and special declension patterns for
surnames and a mixed adjective/noun declension class.

### 3.6 Noun declension: hard/soft/unpaired stems, declension classes I-III, accentual paradigms (pp. 159-182 of book, i.e. through book p.182... )

*(Note: Timberlake's own Chapter 3 "Inflectional morphology" section 3.6 begins around book p. ~130
in the TOC's numbering but the printed section header for 3.6.2 appears at a later point in this
PDF's line count than Chapter 4's own section headers per the table of contents — chapter/section
numbering in the source runs continuously across what this project's page-range dispatch treats as
chapters 3 and 4; the declension material below is drawn from within the pp. 1-260 range assigned
to this file, cited by book page number throughout.)* Russian nouns fall into three broad
declension classes correlating loosely with gender (a masculine/neuter class, a feminine
а/я-stem class, and a feminine/some-masculine soft-stem class in -ь), each with hard-stem, soft-
stem, and a small number of phonologically-conditioned "unpaired" (velar-stem, sibilant-stem)
subtypes governing spelling of the case endings. Beyond the six-case paradigm itself, Timberlake
catalogs **accentual paradigms** — a noun's stress can be fixed on the stem, fixed on the ending, or
mobile (shifting between stem and ending across the case/number paradigm) — as an independent,
lexically-specified dimension orthogonal to declension class, meaning two nouns of the identical
declension class and even identical endings can differ only in which syllable is stressed in a
given case/number cell.

### 3.7 Compounds, appositives, and names (pp. 183-190 of book range)

Russian forms compounds (interfix -о-/-е- linking two roots), appositive constructions (two nouns
in apposition rather than one modifying the other via agreement), and has a distinct declension
behavior for personal names (given name + patronymic + surname, each element with its own
declension properties — surnames in particular decline adjective-like when they have an
adjectival shape, e.g. -ский, but noun-like otherwise).

### 4.1 Arguments: gender, animacy, number (pp. 159-190)

Grammatical gender (masculine/feminine/neuter) is mostly predictable from a noun's declension-class
ending but has a residue of "unpaired masculine" nouns (soft-stem nouns that decline like the
feminine и-declension but agree as masculine, e.g. professions/kinship terms historically male-
referring) and a small "common gender" class (agreement gender determined by the referent's actual
sex rather than fixed morphology, e.g. words for 'orphan', a scattering of colloquial personal
epithets). **Animacy** is a genuinely grammatical category in Russian, realized not as a separate
case ending but as a *syncretism rule*: animate nouns (across genders, in the plural; masculine
singular too) take accusative = genitive, while inanimates take accusative = nominative — this
syncretism-based encoding (rather than a dedicated animate case marker) is one of the more
typologically distinctive features of the Russian case system (p. 175-183 range). Number includes a
closed set of *pluralia tantum* (nouns with only a plural form, e.g. many nouns for paired/composite
objects) and *singularia tantum* (mass/abstract nouns with no natural plural), plus figurative
plural usage (an abstract mass noun pluralized to mean "instances/manifestations of X").

### 4.2 Case government and the {o} ligature (pp. 191-200 of book range)

Case government (which preposition/verb requires which case of its complement) is presented as
largely arbitrary and lexically-listed rather than semantically predictable, though broad semantic
tendencies exist (genitive for absence/quantity/possession, dative for indirect recipients,
instrumental for means/accompaniment, locative/prepositional for static location). The vocabulary
rows in this file drawn from book pp. 172-183 (the preposition-plus-case examples: на/под/через/
про/от/до/вдоль/над/за/перед/при/по, each cited multiple times with different governed cases and
correspondingly different meanings) directly illustrate this: many common Russian prepositions are
**polysemous specifically as a function of which case they govern** — е.g. по + dative ('along/
according to') versus the same preposition with a different governed case yielding a distinct
sense. This case-conditioned preposition polysemy is a genuinely rich, well-attested mechanism this
project's later mechanics-analysis phase should mine directly from this book's own worked examples
(pp. 172-183) rather than re-deriving from scratch.

### 4.3 Quantifiers: numeral syntax, paucal numerals, complex numerals, collectives, numerative forms (pp. 201-215 of book range)

Beyond simple cardinal-numeral declension (§3.2, cited above), Russian numeral syntax has several
further wrinkles: **paucal numerals** (2, 3, 4) govern a genitive singular complement while 5+
governs genitive plural — a residue of an old dual-number distinction still visible only in this
one corner of the grammar; **complex numerals** (compound numbers like 21, 33) show variable
agreement/declension behavior across their component words in casual usage; **collective
numerals** (двое, трое 'a group of two/three') are a separate, socially-marked paradigm used mainly
with animate/paired referents and stylistically restricted contexts; and a closed set of nouns have
their own **numerative (counting) forms** — a special genitive-plural-like form used specifically
after a numeral, distinct from the noun's "ordinary" genitive plural for some lexical items (e.g.
measure/quantity nouns like килограмм 'kilogram', человек 'person(s)' as the counting form
alongside a rarer ordinary genitive plural людей). This "numerative form ≠ ordinary genitive
plural" split is a genuinely fusional-morphology-specific wrinkle worth flagging for the
mechanics-analysis phase (p. 196-200 range, cited in this file's vocabulary rows for
человек/студентов/словаков/часовых etc.).

### 4.4 Internal arguments and modifiers: possessors, adjective/participle agreement, event nouns (pp. 216-225 of book range)

Possessor phrases can be genitive-marked nouns or possessive adjectives/pronouns; **deverbal event
nouns** (nominalizations like совершенствование 'perfection [i.e. the act of perfecting]',
отчаяние 'despair [as an event/state]', перевоспитание 're-education', наказание 'punishment',
всасывание 'sucking in') form a large, highly productive class this book uses at length (p. 216) to
illustrate how Russian nominalizes verbal predicates while still licensing verb-like argument
structure (a genitive "subject"/"object" argument attaching to the derived noun) — directly
relevant to any planned Russian-derived conlang abstraction/nominalization mechanism. Adjective and
participle agreement with their head noun covers gender/number/case concord, including partial or
default-neuter agreement in certain impersonal/quantified-subject constructions.

### 4.5 Common nouns in text, ellipsis ("zero" pronouns), second-person address, names (pp. 226-235 of book range)

Russian permits null (elided) pronouns in contexts where the referent is fully recoverable
("pro-drop"-adjacent behavior, though Timberlake treats it as discourse-conditioned ellipsis rather
than a purely syntactic null-subject parameter). Second-person address is governed by the familiar
ты/вы (T/V) system layered with naming conventions: first-name-only, first-name+patronymic (formal/
respectful), or diminutive/hypocoristic forms (this file's Алексей/Мария rows, p. 229-230, are cited
by the book precisely in this naming-register discussion) — the T/V-plus-naming-register system is
the primary register-marking mechanism in Russian address, in place of a dedicated honorific
morphology (consistent with this language's typology note in `00_Extraction_Checklist.md`).

### 4.6 Demonstrative pronouns (pp. 236-241 of book range)

Этот ('this') and тот ('that') are the core demonstrative paradigm, both fully declined for gender/
number/case; Timberlake discusses their discourse-anaphoric uses (тот as a "distal"/previously-
mentioned-referent anaphor) alongside their purely deictic ones.

### 4.7 Special predicate-argument relations: non-immediate sites, existentials, modals, unattached and retrospective reflexives, passives, event argument phrases (pp. 242-259 of book range)

A cluster of constructions where the usual subject/object case-marking is overridden by the
predicate's own special requirements: **unattached reflexives** (a reflexive pronoun/clitic with no
overt local antecedent, referring instead to a discourse-salient or generic "one"), **passive**
constructions (formed via the -ся/-сь reflexive clitic on an otherwise-transitive verb, or via a
short-form passive participle), and **event argument phrases** (a nominalized clause standing in an
argument position). The на/при/по examples in this file glossed 'notwithstanding'/'for all his or
her'/'according to' (p. 242) illustrate concessive and distributive senses these same prepositions
carry in specific governed-case combinations, distinct from their more basic spatial senses covered
in §4.2.

### 4.8 Indefinite and negative pronouns (кое-, -то, -нибудь, не-) (pp. 254-260 of book range)

Russian builds a systematic family of indefinite pronouns from a wh-root (кто 'who', что 'what',
какой 'which') plus one of several indefiniteness markers, each with a distinct semantic flavor:
кое- (a specific-but-unspecified referent, 'a certain, someone in particular'), -то (a specific,
speaker-doesn't-care-to-specify referent), -нибудь (a genuinely arbitrary/free-choice referent, 'no
matter which'). **Negative pronouns** in не- (никто 'no one', ничто/ничего 'nothing') require
concomitant clausal negation (не) on the verb — a form of negative concord rather than the negative

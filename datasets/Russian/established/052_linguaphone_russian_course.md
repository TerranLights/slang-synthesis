# Russian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Linguaphone Russian Course (complete, all 30 lessons + grammar appendix)

**Source:** Linguaphone Institute Ltd. (London), *Курс русского языка* / "The Linguaphone Russian Course" — a beginner conversational course built around 30 illustrated lessons (each with a labelled picture, a narrative text, and a "Разговор" Q&A dialogue), followed by a self-contained English-language "Introduction to Russian Grammar" appendix (26 numbered pages with full declension/conjugation tables). Recorded voices credited to Виктор Григорий, Константин Иринский, Любовь Волосевич. No publication year printed on the copy scanned; internal content (references to "Ленинград, бывший Петроград," the USSR/СССР, Five-Year-Plan-era industrial vocabulary, kolkhoz-adjacent farm vocabulary, and pre-decimal Soviet currency/measures) places it as a mid-20th-century Soviet-era edition, printed in Great Britain for International Catalogues Ltd. by Unwin Brothers Ltd. (colophon code "UB. 76912th — Rus. 608"). Full 85 PDF pages covered (title matter, Sounds Record phonetic drills, all 30 lessons pp. 16–137, and the 26-page grammar appendix with its own pagination).

**Coverage note:** Every distinct vocabulary item explicitly labelled in each lesson's illustration-key is captured, plus additional distinctive lexical items from the lesson narratives and "Разговор" dialogues that introduce new vocabulary beyond the illustration key (idioms, register markers, institutional/Soviet-era terms, food/drink terms, etc.). Repeated dialogue Q&A sentences that only recombine already-captured vocabulary are not separately tabulated. The book has **no explicit dialectal/regional annotation system** (no [B]/[C]/[S]-style tags) — it is a single-register (standard, mildly formal/polite) conversational course, so the Attested Region/Geographic Scope columns are `—` throughout except where content is itself about a named place. The "Sounds Record" pages (pp. 12–15) are phonetic drill syllables/nonsense-adjacent words for pronunciation practice, not meaningful vocabulary, and are excluded from the table (noted here for coverage-completeness).

**Vision-reading guard:** This source has no text layer (`pdftotext` returns nothing) and was fully vision-read via `pdftoppm` renders of all 85 PDF pages. **Page-spread-scan gotcha confirmed**: each PDF page image is a two-printed-page spread (e.g. PDF page 2 = printed pp. 2–3, PDF page 3 = printed pp. 4–5, etc.), consistent with the pattern already documented in this project's spec from the Serbian/Croatian/Bosnian test run — verified empirically by reading the first several spreads before relying on the offset. **No handwritten marginalia was found anywhere in this copy** — every page's content was clearly printed/typeset (body text, handwriting-styled "specimen" pages for the Sounds Record are themselves printed facsimiles of handwriting used pedagogically, not a prior owner's annotations, and are excluded from vocabulary extraction as noted above rather than mistaken for genuine marginalia).

**Non-redundant-supplement note:** This is Russian's 46th established file; core grammar (six-case declension, aspect, two-conjugation verb system) has been extensively covered by earlier Wave 1/2 sources (esp. 004 SEELRC, 006/007 Timberlake, 012/013 Schaum's). This file is kept comprehensive anyway (rather than skipped as redundant) because: (a) it is a genuine independent author's presentation with its own complete declension/conjugation tables, useful as a cross-check; (b) its lesson vocabulary is thematically organized around everyday situations (family, meals, shopping, post office, doctor, tailor, radio, motoring) that most grammar-focused references do not systematically enumerate; (c) it is one of the few sources in this corpus with an explicit, simple restatement of the Russian time-telling system with worked clock examples.

## Grammar points

### Six-case declension system (nouns, three declensions)

The book's grammar appendix (pp. 1–26, its own separate pagination) opens by explaining Russian's **hard/soft vowel pairing** (а/я, э/е, ы/и, о/ё, у/ю) as the master key to declension: every "hard" paradigm has a mechanically corresponding "soft" paradigm, so learning one halves the work of learning the other. It then gives the six cases — nominative, genitive, dative, accusative, instrumental, and locative/prepositional (the text notes "locative" and "prepositional" are the same case, called locative specifically when it denotes place) — and states Russian nouns fall into **three declensions**: first (feminine, one form only — soft), second (masculine and feminine, hard or soft), third (masculine and neuter, hard or soft). A full paradigm table (p. 12–13) gives every case/number ending for all three declensions in both hard and soft forms, plus worked full declensions of мать, комната, тётя, армия (feminine), стол, гость, попугай (masculine), and кресло, море (neuter) on pp. 14–15.

### The "golden rule" — velar/sibilant consonant vowel restriction

A recurring, explicitly named rule: the consonants г, к, х, ж, ч, ш, щ can never be directly followed by the vowels ы, я, ю — these are always replaced by the corresponding и, а, у. The book calls this the "golden rule" and shows it explains apparent declension irregularities without treating them as true exceptions — e.g. книга (2nd declension, hard) still takes genitive книги (not *книгы) purely because of this vowel restriction, not because the noun is irregular.

### Pronoun declension underlies adjective declension

The appendix makes an explicit pedagogical shortcut: learning the declension of он/она/оно (he/she/it) effectively teaches the adjective declension too, because the long/attributive adjective form is historically the short/predicative form plus the pronominal endings of он/она/оно. A full pronoun table (я, ты, он/она/оно, кто, что, мы, вы, они) is given alongside a parallel hard/soft adjective table on the same page (p. 13) to make this correspondence visible.

### Long (attributive) vs. short (predicative) adjective forms

Russian adjectives have two forms: the long/attributive "full" form (красивый, -ая, -ое) used when the adjective precedes and agrees with a noun in gender/number/case, and the short/predicative form (красив, -а, -о, with -ы for all genders in the plural) used when the adjective is the predicate of a sentence. The short form is derived by cutting -ый/-ой off the masculine long form.

### Verbal aspect (imperfective/perfective)

The book frames aspect as the central real difficulty of Russian verbs, since there is no exact tense-for-tense correspondence with English. The **imperfective aspect** denotes continuous, unbounded, or repeated action and has three tenses (present, past, future — the future formed periphrastically with future-tense быть + imperfective infinitive). The **perfective aspect** denotes a completed (or to-be-completed) action, focuses on the action's *result* rather than its unfolding, and has only two tenses (past and future — its "present-tense" conjugated form actually carries future meaning). Worked side-by-side full conjugations of a matched imperfective/perfective pair (желать / пожелать) are given across indicative, conditional, imperative, participles (active present/past, passive present/past), and gerunds (present/past) on pp. 20–21. A long paired list of ~40 common imperfective verbs with their perfective partners (e.g. брать/взять, видеть/увидеть, идти/пойти, класть/положить, есть/съесть) appears on pp. 22–26, several with usage footnotes (e.g. verbs in -деть/-дить take ж only in the first-person singular; a note that поговорить specifically means "to have a chat").

### Reflexive verbs and the passive voice

Verbs ending in -ся/-сь are explained as reflexive (the action reflects back on the subject, -ся being a contraction of себя "self"), e.g. мыть "to wash someone" vs. мыться "to wash oneself." The same reflexive form is also used to express the passive voice, e.g. стол покрывается белой скатертью, "the table is covered with a white tablecloth" (lit. "the table covers itself").

### The "to have" construction (у + genitive)

Russian has no verb corresponding directly to "to have" as a principal verb; possession is instead expressed idiomatically with у + genitive of the possessor + (есть) + nominative of the possessed thing, e.g. у меня книга, "I have a book" (lit. "by me [is] a book"). Есть is usually omitted unless emphasis is intended or the sentence is interrogative (есть is never omitted in questions, e.g. Есть ли у вас спички? "Have you any matches?"). In negative sentences есть becomes нет, and the possessed noun shifts to genitive: у меня нет книги, "I have no book."

### Negation and interrogation

Negative sentences place не before the verb; a second negative word in the sentence does not cancel the negation (unlike English), e.g. я никогда не был, "I was never [there]" (lit. "I never not was"). Interrogative sentences add the particle ли after the verb, or rely on intonation/written question mark alone — the book shows был ли он там / был он там? / он был там? as three equivalent phrasings of "Was he there?"

### Time-telling system (Lesson 6)

Hours are counted 1–12 with "часа/час" and disambiguated as day or night as needed (час дня, час ночи, шесть часов утра/вечера). Minutes past the hour up to the half-hour are expressed as "[N] минут(a) + [ordinal of the *next* hour]" — e.g. пять минут первого = "five minutes of the first" = 12:05, четверть шестого = "a quarter of the sixth" = 5:15, половина двенадцатого = 11:30. Minutes after the half-hour switch to a "без [N] (минут) + [cardinal of the next hour]" ("without N minutes until [hour]") construction, e.g. без двадцати пяти (минут) двенадцать = 11:35, без четверти час = 12:45. The book notes that for train times specifically, a direct 24-hour-style cardinal reading (e.g. "двенадцать десять" for 12:10) is common in place of the ordinal phrasing.

### Numeral government of the noun case (Lesson appendix pp. 6, 16–17)

A substantive following один/одна/одно (or any compound number ending in "one") stays in the nominative singular (один стол, двадцать один стол — literally "table," not "tables"). A substantive following два/три/четыре (or a compound ending in one of these) is in the genitive *singular* (два брата, сорок три года) — but only when the numeral itself is in the nominative/accusative; in any oblique case the noun takes the corresponding plural. Substantives following any other numeral (five and above) are in the genitive *plural* (пять собак). If an adjective intervenes between two/three/four and the noun, the adjective itself goes to the genitive *plural* (not singular), agreeing with the sense rather than the form of the noun — flagged in the source as a point requiring particular attention. Cardinal numeral declension tables for два, три, четыре, пять (plus their "collective" numeral counterparts двое, трое, четверо, пятеро) are given in full on p. 17.

### Prepositions governing different cases for motion vs. location

The appendix illustrates with в (into/in) and на (on/upon): when denoting motion *toward* an object, these prepositions govern the **accusative** (класть книгу на полку, "to put the book on the shelf"; он идёт в деревню, "he goes into the village"); when denoting rest/location, they govern the **locative** (книга лежит на полке, "the book lies on the shelf"; он живёт в деревне, "he lives in the village"). The book explicitly frames prepositions as a genuinely difficult area best learned one-by-one as encountered in the course text, rather than memorized as an abstract system.

### Two verb conjugations

Present-tense endings fall into two conjugation classes differing mainly in the "copulative vowel" (е vs. и): First Conjugation singular -у/-ю, -ешь, -ет, plural -ем, -ете, -ут/-ют; Second Conjugation singular -у/-ю, -ишь, -ит, plural -им, -ите, -ат/-ят. General membership rules are given (most -ать verbs, deadjectival -еть verbs, monosyllabic verbs, -чь verbs, and -сть/-сти/-зть/-зти verbs belong to the First; most disyllabic -ать verbs with a stem in ж/ч/ш/щ, most -еть verbs (with named exceptions), and most -ить verbs belong to the Second), with a large worked table of both conjugations' present tense for verbs seeded in the early lessons (читать, играть, изображать, курить, говорить, спешить, ходить, видеть, сидеть, лежать, держать, слышать, etc.), including a footnote that -деть/-дить verbs take ж only in the first-person singular.

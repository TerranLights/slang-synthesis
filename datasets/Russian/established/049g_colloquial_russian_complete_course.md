## Grammar points

### Lesson 12 — Plural formation, imperative, numeral-adjective agreement, ли
- **Nominative plural**: regular pattern is masc. consonant-stem / fem. -а → -ы (журна́л→журна́лы,
  газе́та→газе́ты, with occasional stress shift to the first syllable, e.g. сестра́→сёстры);
  spelling-rule-affected stems and masc. -й/fem. -я/either-gender -ь nouns take -и instead. Neuter
  -о/-е take -а/-я respectively; neuter -мя takes -ена (и́мя→имена́). A substantial irregular class
  takes stressed -á (дом→домá, а́дрес→адреса́, ве́чер→вечера́, дире́ктор→директора́, лес→леса́,
  го́род→города́, бе́рег→берега́, учи́тель→учителя́) and a further irregular class takes -ья
  (брат→бра́тья, стул→сту́лья, друг→друзья́, де́рево→дере́вья). Masc. nouns in -анин/-янин take
  -ане/-яне (англича́нин→англича́не) — this -ин suffix is itself dropped in the plural, a
  genuinely distinct declension-class marker rather than an ordinary ending swap. Лю́ди/де́ти
  suppletively replace челове́к/ребёнок in the plural. Some English nouns (ва́нная "bathroom") are
  grammatically adjectives in Russian and pluralize adjectivally (ва́нные).
- **Genitive plural — by far the most irregular case-number cell in Russian nominal morphology**,
  laid out here in full: masc. consonant-stems take -ов (spelling-rule-affected -ей for ж/ч/ш/щ,
  -ев/-ёв for stressed -й, -ей for -ь); fem. -а drop the vowel (with a fill vowel о/е/ё
  sometimes inserted between the final consonant cluster: студе́нтка→студе́нток, де́вушка→
  де́вушек, копе́йка→копе́ек), fem. -я replace with -ь (unless -ня, which usually drops the soft
  sign entirely: пе́сня→пе́сен, but дере́вня→дереве́нь and ку́хня→ку́хонь are lexical exceptions to
  that sub-rule), fem. -ь/-ья/-ия take -ей/-ей/-ий respectively; neuter -о/-е drop the vowel (again
  with occasional fill-vowel insertion: окно́→о́кон, письмо́→пи́сем, кре́сло→кре́сел), neuter -ие
  takes -ий, neuter -мя takes -ён (и́мя→имён). A closed irregular list is given explicitly: брат/
  бра́тья→бра́тьев, стул/сту́лья→сту́льев, друг/друзья́→друзе́й, сын/сыновья́→сынове́й, ребёнок/
  де́ти→дете́й, раз/разы́→(zero-ending) раз, де́ньги→де́нег, кани́кулы→кани́кул, челове́к/лю́ди→
  люде́й — **except after сколько/несколько and numerals, where genitive-plural "of people" reverts
  to челове́к rather than люде́й** (пять челове́к), a specific lexically-conditioned exception
  worth flagging: the "regular" suppletive plural is itself overridden in a narrow numeral-governed
  context.
  This is one of the densest, most exception-laden paradigms encountered in the course so far —
  strong candidate for mechanics-analysis as an example of how a single grammatical cell
  (genitive plural) can accumulate the largest irregularity load in a language's nominal system.
- **Accusative plural**: inanimate = nominative plural; animate = genitive plural — the animacy
  split already established for the singular extends transparently to the plural (Он зна́ет
  молоды́х англича́н).
- **Adjective/possessive/demonstrative plurals**: genitive plural for all genders is -ых (hard) /
  -их (soft, and spelling-rule mixed adjectives); a full paradigm table is given for мой/твой/свой/
  наш/ваш/э́тот/весь/тот across nom. sg., nom. pl., acc. pl., gen. pl. — все/всех (весь) and те/тех
  (тот) are worth noting as the plural forms losing all gender distinction entirely, unlike the
  singular's three-way split.
- **Imperative formation**: strip the last two letters of the 3rd-person-plural present-tense form
  to get the stem, then add -й (stem ends in vowel) or -и (stem ends in consonant) for singular,
  -те for plural (чита́ют→чита́й(те); пи́шут→пиши́(те)). A narrower sub-rule: verbs whose stress
  stays on the stem throughout the conjugation take -ь/-ьте instead of -и/-ите when the stem ends
  in a single consonant (гото́вить→гото́вь/гото́вьте; ста́вить→ставь/ста́вьте) — a stress-
  conditioned allomorph choice distinct from the ordinary consonant/vowel-final rule. Reflexive
  imperatives take -ся after й/ь, -сь after и/-те. Irregular imperatives are lexically listed: есть
  →ешь/е́шьте, дава́ть→дава́й/дава́йте, встава́ть→встава́й/встава́йте, пить→пей/пе́йте — imperative
  stress matches the 1st-person-singular stress in all cases.
- **Perfective vs. imperfective imperative — a genuinely pragmatic (not just aspectual-completion)
  contrast**: the perfective imperative is built the same way but from the 3rd-plural of the
  *perfective future* (прочита́ют→прочита́й(те); напи́шут→напиши́(те)), and is used to order the
  completion of a single specific action — but **negative imperatives (prohibitions) are
  characteristically imperfective**, even when the underlying action is inherently single/
  completable. This is another case (parallel to нельзя́'s aspect split in Lesson 11) where
  Russian systematically prefers the imperfective specifically in negative/prohibitive contexts —
  worth cross-referencing as a recurring pattern: negation and imperfective aspect co-occur more
  than chance would predict across multiple distinct constructions in this language.
- **Quantity expressions governing the genitive**: мно́го + gen. ("much/many"), ма́ло + gen.
  ("few"), большинство́ + gen. ("the majority of"), ско́лько + gen. ("how many"), не́сколько + gen.
  ("several") all pattern alike syntactically, but не́сколько is explicitly contrasted with
  не́которые из + gen. ("some of [a definite, previously established set]") — a quantity-only vs.
  partitive-selective distinction not visible in the English glosses alone. Оди́н/одна́/одно́ +
  из + gen. renders "one of [a set]".
- **Numerals + adjective + noun agreement — a genuinely three-way split by numeral class**: (i)
  пять "5" and above (excluding compounds ending in 1/2/3/4) govern genitive plural for *both* the
  noun and any modifying adjective, regardless of the noun's gender (пять хоро́ших газе́т, два́дцать
  шесть хоро́ших журна́лов); (ii) два/две, три, четы́ре (and compounds ending in them) govern
  genitive *singular* for the noun but genitive *plural* for a modifying adjective when the noun is
  masc./neuter, and nominative *plural* for the adjective when the noun is feminine (два́дцать два
  хоро́ших студе́нта [masc., adj. gen.pl.] vs. два́дцать две хоро́шие студе́нтки [fem., adj.
  nom.pl.]) — noun-case and adjective-case genuinely diverge within the very same numeral phrase,
  and the adjective's own agreement additionally depends on the noun's gender in a way the noun's
  own case does not; (iii) numerals ending in оди́н/одна́/одно́ behave as if the numeral itself were
  simply an adjective, triggering ordinary singular agreement throughout, noun and adjective alike
  (два́дцать оди́н хоро́ший студе́нт). This three-tier numeral-agreement system, where the
  adjective's case can differ from its own head noun's case depending on gender and numeral class,
  is one of the more intricate agreement mismatches in the course — a strong candidate for
  mechanics-analysis as a model of "discordant agreement" a conlang could adapt for a slang-marking
  purpose (e.g. marking in-group/out-group status via a deliberately mismatched agreement pattern).
- **Ли-questions**: the particle ли forms yes/no questions by attaching to the emphasized word,
  which is fronted before ли (Получа́ете ли вы газе́ту? "Do you take a newspaper?" — emphasis on
  "take"; word order itself marks information structure here, with ли as a fixed second-position
  clitic-like particle). Ли also renders "whether/if" in indirect questions — and critically,
  **Russian indirect speech preserves the tense that would have been used in the original direct
  utterance**, unlike English's sequence-of-tense backshifting: Он спроси́л, получа́ете ли вы
  газе́ту literally "he asked, do-you-take ли a newspaper" keeps the present tense получа́ете even
  though the reporting verb спроси́л is past; Она́ спроси́ла, бу́ду ли я до́ма keeps the future
  бу́ду even under a past-tense reporting verb. This absence of tense-backshifting in reported
  speech is a clean, systematic divergence from English grammar worth flagging for mechanics
  analysis — a slang/register marker built on reported-speech constructions in an invented language
  needn't assume English-style tense harmony is a linguistic universal.

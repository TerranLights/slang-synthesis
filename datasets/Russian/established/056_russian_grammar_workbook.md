## Grammar points

### Chapter 2, Unit 4 — Impossibility of action for lack of object, agent, or conditions (p. 20)

When an action cannot be carried out because its object, its agent, or a necessary circumstance is
simply absent, the workbook teaches a dedicated impersonal construction: a dative experiencer +
one of a closed set of negative pronouns/adverbs (НЕКОГО, НЕЧЕГО, НЕГДЕ, НЕКУДА, НЕОТКУДА,
НЕКОГДА, НЕЗАЧЕМ) + an imperfective infinitive, with БЫЛО added for past tense (no separate future
auxiliary was shown on this page; present tense uses the bare construction).

- **НЕКОГО / НЕЧЕГО** cover the missing-object-or-agent case. Both have no nominative form and
  function only as syntactic objects; their case is governed by the infinitive itself. Critically,
  **the stressed particle не́- is written as a separate word when a preposition intervenes**
  (не́ с кем, не́ о чем), but fuses into one word when no preposition is needed (не́чего,
  не́кого) — this splitting/fusing rule is the paradigm's one genuine wrinkle.
- **НЕГДЕ / НЕКУДА / НЕОТКУДА / НЕКОГДА / НЕЗАЧЕМ** cover missing circumstances (nowhere to be,
  nowhere to go to, nowhere to come from, no time, no reason) and are syntactically invariant
  adverbial modifiers rather than case-governed pronouns.
- Illustrative (paraphrased, not verbatim from the book's own dialogue box): a friend met in the
  morning was rushing to work, so "there was no time" (не́когда бы́ло) to talk; getting lost in an
  unfamiliar district with no passers-by around left the speaker with "no one to ask" (не́ у кого
  бы́ло спроси́ть) for directions.

This is a compact, closed paradigm not found tabulated this completely elsewhere in the Russian
established/ corpus so far, and is flagged as a plausible generative source for an in-universe
"impossibility idiom" (a fixed grammatical slot for "there is no X to do Y with" could map cleanly
onto a sci-fi slang need — e.g., scarcity-driven dialects marking "no reason/resource to bother"
morphologically rather than lexically).

### Chapter 8 — Expressing Emotional States: a four-way functional cross-cut (pp. 66–77)

The chapter's organizing insight is that Russian has (at least) four independent grammatical means
of expressing the same emotional-state content, each with a different register/expressiveness
profile, and the book teaches them side by side rather than scattering them across unrelated
chapters of a part-of-speech-organized grammar:

1. **Predicative adverbs in -О** (мне грустно, мне стыдно) — dative experiencer + invariant
   adverbial predicate. Marked by the book as expressing "inexplicable, instinctive, subconscious"
   emotion; the experiencer is frequently omitted when recoverable from context, when the speaker
   describes their own feeling, or when the emotion is generic/impersonal (При расставании всегда
   бывает очень грустно — "parting is always sad," no one in particular). Tense/mood is carried
   entirely by было/будет/бы/пусть будет around the invariant adverb.
2. **Full and short adjectives** (весёлый/весел, взволнованный/взволнован) — nominative-subject
   predicate describing an *externally visible* manifestation of the state, explicitly contrasted
   with the predicative-adverb's *internal* state: the book's own example is a person who is
   "весел" (visibly cheerful — jokes, laughs) while privately "не весело" (not actually feeling
   good) — a deliberate outward/inward split the book draws attention to. Because adjectives
   describe *external* display, the first-person pronoun я is noted as vanishingly rare as their
   subject (a speaker doesn't usually narrate their own outward affect this way). Short adjectives
   are marked as more literary/expressive; full adjectives as neutral/colloquial-leaning. The
   intensifiers ТАК (+ short form) vs. ТАКОЙ (+ full form) are grammatically locked to the
   adjective's form.
3. **Verbs**, used when it's essential to stress the person is *actively displaying* the emotion.
   The book supplies a systematic 7-class valence table (verbs of interest, surprise, discontent,
   excitement/sorrow, fear, love/hatred, joke/derision), each class given in both its plain
   transitive form (кого/чем? — the emotion's cause acts as grammatical subject: "X interests Y")
   and its -СЯ reflexive/experiencer form, where government characteristically shifts case
   (increase кого?/чем? → кем?/чем?, or кого?/чем? → о ком?/о чём?, or → на кого?/на что?,
   depending on class). This case-government shift under -СЯ, tabulated per semantic class, is the
   table's most reusable structural finding.
4. **Short-form passive participles** (взволнован, обрадован, разозлён...), governing кем?/чем?
   like the -СЯ verb forms, used for a completed/resultant emotional state parallel to the
   passive-of-action pattern already established in Chapter 5.
5. **The causal construction "ЗА + accusative"** (Ch. 8 Unit 5, p. 74) singles out the *cause* of
   an emotion as its own grammatical slot, distinct from ordinary government: ругать за (to scold
   for), бояться за (to fear for [someone's safety], contrasted with бояться + genitive, "to be
   afraid of [something itself]" — the book explicitly notes this is a real semantic contrast, not
   free variation: "Я боюсь за этого человека" (I'm afraid *for* him) vs. "Я боюсь этого человека"
   (I'm afraid *of* him).

The за+accusative / genitive minimal pair (fear-for vs. fear-of) is worth flagging for the
mechanics-analysis phase: a single verb's argument structure carrying two distinct real-world
meanings via case alternation alone is exactly the kind of fusional-morphology-does-heavy-lifting
pattern that would need a deliberate design decision if adapted into a slang/conlang register with
reduced case marking.

### Chapter 9 — Expressing Time (pp. 78–128, 25 units)

This is the book's largest and most systematically exhaustive chapter, built as a construction-by-
construction inventory of how Russian answers each of the natural time-questions (который час?,
какое число?, когда?, как долго?, на сколько?, как часто?, с каких пор? etc.). Construction
families observed across the chapter's units, paraphrased:

- **Clock time.** A formal register (Который час? / Сейчас... plus a numeral-noun agreement chain
  for час/часа/часов) is taught alongside a distinct colloquial register for approximate and
  fractional time (е.g., "без пяти минут семь" — five minutes to seven; "в начале первого" —
  just after midnight/noon; half-hour phrasing built on "в половине..."). The book treats these as
  two parallel systems a learner must keep straight, not a single rule with minor variants.
- **Dates.** A genuine case alternation is drilled: "Какое сегодня число?" takes the nominative
  (naming the date as a thing), while "Какого числа...?" (on what date) takes the genitive — the
  same date-noun answering two different question-frames in two different cases depending on
  whether the date is being named or used adverbially to situate an event.
- **Years.** "В каком году" — the ordinal-in-prepositional-case + году pattern, distinguished from
  the bare-genitive pattern used when a year attaches to a date rather than standing alone.
- **"Ago" vs. "in (the future)."** ТОМУ НАЗАД (obligatorily postposed to the time-quantity phrase)
  marks past elapsed time; ЧЕРЕЗ (+ accusative time phrase, preposed) marks future elapsed time —
  a clean two-construction opposition for the same semantic axis (Ch. 9, mid-chapter units).
- **Durative vs. completive time expressions.** A bare accusative time-phrase marks the duration
  of a *completed* action bounded within that span; a separate construction (за + accusative) marks
  the time *taken to complete* an action, distinct again from В ТЕЧЕНИЕ + genitive, which marks
  time *during which* something was ongoing without asserting completion — three constructions for
  three subtly different relationships between a time-span and an action's boundedness.
- **Frequency.** Каждый + time-noun ("every day/week"), and count-based frequency via РАЗ В +
  accusative ("once a week," "twice a month"), alongside a set of frequency adverbs (обычно,
  иногда, редко, часто) forming a scalar frequency vocabulary.
- **Sequencing.** До того как / после того как / перед тем как (+ clause) for relative
  before/after/just-before sequencing, with associated verb-aspect constraints on the subordinate
  clause depending on which conjunction is used.
- **Closing summary table (final unit, ~p. 128).** The chapter ends by cross-tabulating question
  type (когда? / сколько времени? / как часто? / etc.) against construction and the verb aspect
  each construction requires or favors — effectively a compressed map of the whole chapter, and the
  single most useful page in it as a quick-reference artifact.

This chapter's real contribution to the project is less any individual construction (each is
independently attested elsewhere) than the sheer density of near-synonymous time constructions
Russian maintains side by side, each carrying a distinct shade of meaning the language insists on
marking morphologically/syntactically (completed-within vs. taken-to-complete vs. ongoing-during;
named-date vs. situating-date). This kind of "many near-synonymous constructions, each doing one
narrow job" pattern is a plausible mechanism to borrow from when designing how an in-universe slang
register might compress or collapse a real language's over-differentiated grammar for stylistic or
sociolectal effect.

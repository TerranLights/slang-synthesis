# Russian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Full Reference Grammar (Phonology, Morphology, Syntax)

**Source:** Edna Andrews, *Russian* (SEELRC — Slavic and East European Language Resource Center,
2001), the entire work — pp. 1–138 (Acknowledgements/Abbreviations, §0 Socio-/geolinguistic
situation, §1 Phonology, §2 Morphology, §3 Syntax, Bibliography). File:
`source_reference/languages/Russian/Russian Grammar books/compgrammar_russian.pdf` (141 PDF
pages). NOTE: `stand_alone_russian.pdf` in the same folder is a confirmed duplicate scan of this
same document (matching table of contents) and was not separately processed.

**Coverage note:** This is the *first* Russian reference-grammar extraction for this project
(no prior `established/` files exist to check against for redundancy), so full coverage was taken
rather than the "non-redundant supplement" pattern. Every grammar point in the source's own outline
(§0–§3) is paraphrased below, with a citation to source section number. The book is not a
lesson-vocabulary teaching grammar — it is a structural/typological reference — so its "vocabulary"
consists almost entirely of finite illustrative lists (declension paradigm examples, case-government
verb lists, derivational affixes with example words, numeral/pronoun paradigms, etc.). All such
finite lists are captured in full in the Vocabulary table below; the book's very large paradigm
tables for individual noun/adjective/verb declension classes are *not* re-tabulated form-by-form
(that would be re-deriving inflected forms of a single lexeme, not "distinct vocabulary items") —
instead the citation/dictionary form of each exemplar lexeme is captured once, with its
morphological class noted, and the productive rule itself is paraphrased in Grammar Points.

**PDF extraction gotcha (new, worth flagging for future Russian sources):** The source has a
genuine text layer and `pdftotext -layout` produced clean, correctly-ordered Cyrillic almost
everywhere — this is *not* the font-substitution-cipher problem seen with other languages' PDFs.
However, a large number of italicized Cyrillic capital letters — specifically the first letter of
many proper names, sentence-initial words, and imperative/vocative forms set in the source's own
italic type — extracted as a blank space rather than the correct character (e.g. the source's own
"Саша!" extracts as " аш!"; "Что это за глупость?" extracts as ""то это за глупость?"). This is a
silent character-dropping problem, not a substitution cipher, and is distinguishable because (a) it
is confined to the first letter of italicized words/names, (b) the surrounding non-italic text and
the accompanying English glosses are unaffected and make the intended word unambiguous in
essentially every case, and (c) cross-checking against the same lexeme appearing elsewhere in
roman/non-italic Cyrillic in the same document confirms the reading. All affected example sentences
below have been silently reconstructed from context/gloss rather than quoted with the dropped
letter; no vocabulary-table entry depends on a word whose identity was actually in doubt. Flagging
this here because it likely recurs in any Andrews/SEELRC-sourced Russian PDF using the same
typesetting. Vision Reading Confidence is `n/a` throughout this file (real text layer, no
vision-reading was needed); Transcription Confidence is `n/a` throughout (not a subtitle/transcript
source).

## Grammar points

### §0 Socio- and geolinguistic situation
Russian is the official language of the Russian Federation and a former primary official language
of the USSR; it remains official or semi-official in several post-Soviet states (p. 4). It belongs
to the East Slavic branch (with Ukrainian and Belorussian), and Old Church Slavonic (a South Slavic
literary language) had two historical waves of influence on it (11th–13th c.; 14th c.
"Re-Bulgarization"), producing a long diglossic coexistence of OCS and the East Slavic vernacular
lasting into the 18th century (p. 4). Contemporary Standard Russian (CSR) crystallized as a literary
language roughly 1760–1825 (Lomonosov, Karamzin, Pushkin as key figures; Karamzin argued most
influential though Pushkin often given popular credit), with significant French (and later German)
lexical/stylistic influence (pp. 4–5). Russian dialectology divides traditionally into North,
Central, and South Great Russian groups with 8 subgroups; CSR itself is a blend of northern and
southern features, and 20th-century Russian linguistics trended toward treating dialectal
differences as disappearing/substandard (p. 5).

### §1.1 Orthography
The Cyrillic alphabet has 33 graphemes. Alphabet-to-pronunciation mapping is not fully phonemic
(unlike, e.g., Serbian Cyrillic). Three vocalic spelling rules govern desinences after certain
consonant classes (husher-conditioned о/е, и/ы, and а/я, у/ю alternations, p. 8). Capitalization is
minimal: only sentence/title-initial words, never nationalities/months/days unless sentence-initial;
я 'I' is capitalized only sentence-initially; formal вы 'you' may be capitalized in official
correspondence as a mark of respect. Stress is marked in print only where ambiguous, and ё is rarely
marked even where ambiguous (pp. 6–8).

### §1.2 Phonemic inventory
5 vocalic phonemes under the "5-vowel" analysis (Moscow-school consensus after the 1990s), though a
"6-vowel" analysis (adding /ɨ/ = ы) was historically favored by scholars in the former USSR;
Andrews follows the 5-phoneme analysis since и/ы are in complementary, not contrastive, distribution
(p. 9). 33 (or up to 35–36, depending on treatment of /ʃː/ and palatalized velars) consonantal
phonemes are organized by place/manner (stop, fricative, affricate, glide, nasal, liquid), with a
voiced/voiceless and palatalized/nonpalatalized contrast running through most of the paired
consonants (p. 9).

### §1.3 Vowels and prosody
CSR has no phonemic vowel length or pitch, but strong mobile phonemic stress that can shift within a
paradigm (stem↔ending, or even retract onto a preposition/prefix); что and the negative particles
не/ни are typically unstressed except in specific constructions (pp. 10–11). Vowel reduction is
described via akan'e (unstressed /a/) and ikan'e (unstressed /i/), reducing the 5-phoneme stressed
system to 3 phonemes (/a, i, u/) pre-/post-tonically; a rarer ekan'e also occurs. The reduction
pattern differs by grapheme (я vs. е) and by position (word-initial vs. pretonic/post-tonic vs.
desinential), and following sibilants а/о can also participate in ikan'e in free variation (pp.
11–12). Palatalization produces a four-way scale of vowel allophony (CVC < CVC' < C'VC < C'VC',
increasing frontedness/raising) (pp. 12–13). /ы/ obligatorily palatalizes preceding paired
consonants except in a small set of foreign borrowings; some /o/-final borrowings resist standard
reduction entirely (rad'io, poet) (p. 13).

### §1.4 Consonants
Stops are unaspirated; /r/ is a trill not a flap; /ʃː/ (щ) is usually [ʃː] but sometimes [ʃtʃ]. Six
voiced/voiceless pairs and 12 (or 15, including velars) palatalized/nonpalatalized pairs structure
the system. The letter г is pronounced [v] in genitive desinences and a few set forms (его,
сегодня). The unpaired sibilants (ц, ш, ж, ч, щ) are lexically fixed as hard or soft, with isolated
exceptions in specific lexemes and in Moscow-norm pronunciations (дождя etc., p. 14). Consonant
cluster phonology (§1.4.3) governs voicing assimilation (regressive, exceptions for /v, v'/),
permissible cluster shapes up to 4 consonants (чувство, здравствуй), consonant-loss in fast speech
(сейчас→[ɕːas]), and an epenthetic /o/ in prepositions/prefixes before consonant clusters (ко мне,
во Франции) plus OCS-derived epenthetic-о forms (восходить vs. всходить) (pp. 14–19).

### §1.5 Morphophonemic alternations
CSR has three fill vowels (о, е, and the rare и) governing vowel/zero alternations in noun/adjective
declension (§1.5.1): е after soft/jot/ц-final stems, и after jot-final stems with preceding stress,
о elsewhere, with velar-triggered and stress-conditioned sub-rules and a small set of true
exceptions (яйцо/яиц, полный/полон, достойный/достоин). Short-form adjectives use only /o/ (with one
exception); verbs use only /o/ and /e/ in inflection. Consonant alternations (§1.5.2) are dominated
by the velar/palatal series (k→č, g→ž, x→š) plus a further set of dental/labial mutations (d→ž/žd,
t→č/šč, z→ž, s→š, sk/st→šč, c→č, and labial+l insertion b/v/p/m/f → bl/vl/pl/ml/fl), occurring
across verb conjugation, nominal derivation/declension, and comparative adjective formation.

### §1.6 Pronunciation variants: Moscow vs. St. Petersburg
Historically ~50 orthoepic differences are documented between the two cities, but by the early 21st
century only 3 remain robust: more ekan'e in St. Petersburg, more non-palatalized consonants before
/e/ in borrowings in St. Petersburg speech, and convergence on [daʐdʲa] for дождя in both cities.
Lexical differences (хлеб/булка for 'bread', сахар/песок for 'sugar', various words for transit
passes) persist regionally alongside the phonological convergence (pp. 21–22).

### §1.7 Tongue twisters and diction
The source lists several традиционные скороговорки (tongue twisters) and articulation drills used
by actors/speech trainers and L2 learners, illustrating consonant-cluster and sibilant contrasts in
connected speech (pp. 23–24) — treated here as pedagogical/performative material rather than
extractable vocabulary items in their own right.

### §2.1–2.2 Noun inflection: gender, animacy, declension
CSR nouns inflect for gender (m/f/n), number, and 6 cases (N/A/G/D/L/I). Animacy is not coextensive
with biological animacy — it is a grammatical category realized as N=A syncretism breaking down to
G=A in masculine-animate singular (and in all animates in the plural), with some inanimate nouns
(туз 'ace') behaving as animate and some formally-neuter augmentatives (чудовище, уродище) behaving
as animate in the plural only (§2.1.2). Grammatical gender is not always recoverable from the
nominative form alone; agreement (adjectival, pronominal, past-tense verb) is often the only
disambiguator, and a class of "epicene" Declension II nouns takes either masculine or feminine
agreement depending on referent sex (убийца, судья, пьяница, and the long descriptive-noun list in
the Vocabulary table) — notably, the source flags that CSR dictionaries listing судья as masculine
-only lag behind actual educated-urban usage, where feminine agreement for a female judge is
standard (§2.1.3). Three declension classes exist for singular nouns (I: masc/neut; II: fem/masc
epicene; III: fem only), with the full case-ending paradigm tabulated in §2.2 (zero desinence in N
for I-decl. masc. and all III-decl.); genitive/locative doublets occur for some monosyllabic
masculine I-declension nouns (нос/в носу vs. formal/adjective-modified locative in -е). A closed set
of exceptions includes путь (mixed declension) and the neuter nouns in -мя (время-type, 10 members,
insert an -ен- stem extension throughout oblique cases) (§2.2.3). Family names take special
declensional treatment: -ов/-ин surnames take adjectival instrumental singular endings; -ова/-ина
feminine surnames and plural family names take fully pronominal/adjectival paradigms (§2.2.3).
Certain formally-adjectival forms (столовая, ванная, etc.; also neuter prошлое/настоящее/будущее)
function semantically as nouns (§2.2.3).

### §2.2.5 Grammatical peculiarities of the noun
Epicene common-gender nouns (§2.2.5.1) are heavily represented by pejorative/colloquial
character-description nouns (see Vocabulary). Number peculiarities (§2.2.5.3) include a substantial
closed class of singularia tantum (mostly mass/collective nouns) and pluralia tantum (including
several toponyms/constellations, and many everyday household/social nouns) — see Vocabulary for the
full lists as given by the source.

### §2.3 Indeclinable nouns
A substantial class of (mostly foreign-borrowed) nouns, generally ending in -и/-у/-е/-о, does not
decline at all; case function is signaled purely by agreement on modifiers. This includes the entire
class of foreign (non-Slavic-derived) feminine family names, and masculine surnames of
Ukrainian/non-Slavic origin ending in a vowel (§2.3).

### §2.4–2.6 Declensional desinences and the genitive plural
Beyond the basic paradigm, masculine nouns show alternate locative-in-у (§2.4.1, mostly
monosyllabic, plus set expressions like на лету) and genitive-in-у (§2.4.2, mostly mass nouns —
liquids, foods, substances — and blocked with non-negated imperfective direct objects: contrast Я
ел сыр 'I was eating [the] cheese' vs. Я поел сыру/сыра 'I ate some cheese'). Plural desinences show
a stressed masculine -á variant (§2.5.1), colloquial-vs-literary -ы/-а splits (§2.5.2), referential
splits where the two plural forms mean genuinely different things (§2.5.3), and free-variation pairs
with no meaning difference (§2.5.4). The genitive plural (§2.6) is the most morphologically complex
case: Jakobson's rule states the ending is the *complementary* value of the nominative-singular
desinence (zero nom.sg. → nonzero gen.pl. -ов/-ей; nonzero nom.sg. → zero gen.pl.), with -ов
selected after hard/jot stems and -ей after soft/husher stems; a substantial irregular list (see
Vocabulary, §2.6.2) includes suppletive plurals (ребёнок/дети/детей) and the frozen genitive-plural
'people' rule: only человек (never людей) follows cardinal numerals and сколько/несколько.

### §2.7 The semantics of the Russian case system
Case marks noun-to-verb, noun-to-noun, and general syntagmatic relationships and is formally carried
only by nouns and adjectives. The source works through each case's core meaning with representative
government lists (fully captured in Vocabulary):
- **Nominative** (§2.7.1): naming case, most subjects, predicate nominative with 'be', used after за
  in exclamatives (Что это за глупость? 'what kind of nonsense is this'), required (not
  accusative/genitive, historically possible but now obsolete) in 'Let him smoke'-type constructions,
  and in apposition (a following proper name may stay nominative regardless of the antecedent's
  case).
- **Accusative** (§2.7.2): directionality/extension; primary case for time expressions up to and
  including 'week' units with quantifiers; required object case for a closed list of verbs governed
  by на or в (see Vocabulary); required with через/сквозь; optional with в/на/за/о/под/с when
  motion/directionality is at issue.
- **Genitive** (§2.7.3): dominant for possession (either bare genitive or, more commonly in CSR, the
  у + genitive "have" construction — the verb иметь exists but is comparatively marked/restricted),
  quantification (много/мало/несколько etc. + genitive; numeral-governed case detailed in §2.10.1),
  and negation (нет/не было/не будет and many negated perception verbs govern genitive). A long list
  of genitive-only and genitive-or-accusative verbs is given (Vocabulary), along with the required
  genitive prepositions (из, от, до, у, после, мимо, около, без, вокруг, внутри, снаружи, вместо,
  вследствие, вдоль) and optional-genitive prepositions (с, между).
- **Dative** (§2.7.4): actor case in impersonal constructions (physical/psychological states,
  weather, modal+infinitive constructions), secondary object of transitive verbs, motion-toward goal.
  Also used for a "dative of possession" construction with body-part/life-impact predicates (Она
  наступила мне на ногу 'she stepped on my foot'). Government lists given for dative-only,
  dative+accusative ("give X to Y"), dative-experiencer (logical-subject) verbs, and
  dative/accusative-alternating verbs with distinct meanings per case (изменять, мешать).
- **Locative** (§2.7.5): the only case realized exclusively with prepositions (на, в, о/об/обо, при,
  по), each preposition's meaning largely determining the locative's contribution; government list of
  locative-only verbs given for на- and в-governed subclasses.
- **Instrumental** (§2.7.6): agent/means ("by means of"; contrast с + instrumental "together with"),
  used with verbs of being/becoming, and for the demoted agent in passive constructions; large
  government list given (Vocabulary); required prepositions перед/над, optional с/за/под.
- **Vocative** (§2.7.7): not traditionally recognized in the literature, but the source documents a
  real zero-ending address form restricted to short (≤2-syllable) derived proper names and a very
  small set of common nouns (мам!, пап!, and clipped hypocoristic name-forms — see Vocabulary).

### §2.8 Adjectives
Long-form adjectives inflect for case/number/gender in the singular, case/number only in the plural;
short-form adjectives exist only in predicate-nominative position and inflect for gender/number only
(no ordinal numerals or -sk- adjectives have short forms) (§2.8.1–2.8.2). Comparatives (§2.8.3) split
into an analytic attributive form (более/менее + positive) and, for predicative use, two synthetic
patterns: the productive -ee/-ей desinence (stress-shifting, no stem change) and a smaller -e
desinence class with obligatory consonant mutation (г→ж, к→ч, х→ш, д→ж, т→ч, ст→щ, в→вл), plus a
further exceptional/suppletive subgroup (плохой→хуже, старый→старше, хороший→лучше, etc.). Four
synthetic attributive comparatives exist irregularly (лучший/худший/меньший/больший). Two
comparison constructions exist ("X + gen." vs. "X + чем + [any case]"), with semantic/register
preference rules given (§2.8.3). Superlatives (§2.8.4) are formed synthetically with -ейш-/-айш-
(velar-conditioned) suffixes, an intensifying наи- prefix (with four suppletive наи-forms
наилучший/наихудший/наибольший/наименьший), and analytically with самый + positive. Pronominal
possessive adjectives (§2.8.5) form a distinct declension class (jot-stem or -ов-/-ин- suffixed) used
alongside the genitive case and possessive pronouns to express possession, and productively derive
animal-name and profession/nationality-based adjectives with idiosyncratic non-overlapping meanings
versus their relational-adjective counterparts (мамин друг vs. материнская любовь).

### §2.9 Pronouns
Eight traditional categories (per the Russian Academy Grammar): personal, reflexive, possessive,
interrogative-relative, demonstrative, definite, negative, indefinite (§2.9). Personal/reflexive
pronouns (§2.9.1) show suppletion and require an epenthetic /n/ after prepositions in 3rd-person
forms. The T-V system (§2.9.2–2.9.3) uses ты for children, immediate family, and age-peers through
university, with вы (also capitalized in formal written address) required for teachers/employers and
generally negotiable between adults by explicit request to shift to ты. Possessive/reflexive
pronouns (§2.9.4) show partial syncretism; the reflexive свой (§2.9.5) is grammatically obligatory
with 3rd-person subjects but has flexible, meaning-differentiating use with plural subjects
(distinguishing shared vs. individual possession: свои/их planы) and possessives are frequently
omitted in natural speech where possession is obvious (especially kin terms). Interrogative кто/что
(§2.9.6) are grammatically masculine/neuter respectively but кто may take colloquial feminine
agreement; colloquial что has a reduced pronunciation [tʂo] spelled чё. Interrogative/relative
pronouns который/какой/каков/каковой/что/чей (§2.9.7) each have distinct syntax; archaic кой survives
only in set phrases (кое-как, ни в коем случае). Definite весь/сам/самый/всякий/каждый/всяческий
(§2.9.8) show partial formal syncretism between всё 'everything' and все 'everyone' distinguished by
stress and stem-final palatalization. Demonstrative этот/тот/такой (§2.9.9) contrast near/far/kind,
with archaic сей surviving only in fossilized expressions. Negative/indefinite pronouns (§2.9.10)
detach their ни-/не- prefixes when a preposition intervenes and always co-occur with a negated verb;
the four indefinite suffixes -нибудь/-либо/-то/(bare-forms некто etc.) have distinct
register/specificity values, with -либо flagged as literary/dated in contemporary spoken CSR.

### §2.10 Numerals
Cardinal один is pronominal (3 genders, 2 numbers); два distinguishes m/n vs. f; три/четыре do not
distinguish gender; пять through двадцать (and most higher round numbers, excluding девяносто and
сто) decline as III-declension feminine nouns (§2.10). Ordinals are fully adjectival and agree with
their head noun; третий alone has a pronominal declension among ordinals. Case usage after numerals
(§2.10.1) follows a three-way split (1: nom.sg. agreement; 2/3/4: gen.sg. noun + gen.pl. adjective,
with nom.pl. adjective allowed for feminine referents; 5+: gen.pl. throughout), with a frozen
'people'/'years' irregularity (человек never людей after numerals; лет vs. годов
semantically-conditioned) and a documented (Andrews 1996b) case-preference hierarchy for
large-numeral phrases (миллион/тысяча/сто + oblique case) based on a 100-speaker survey. Collective
numerals (§2.10.2) (двое–десятеро, and the gender-split оба/обе) are obligatory with pluralia-tantum
nouns, optional for male-only or animal groups, and never used for exclusively-female groups; a
number of frozen collective-derived adverbs and nouns exist (вдвоём, пяток, тройка, etc. — see
Vocabulary). Fractions (§2.10.3) combine cardinal numerators with ordinal denominators; полтора/
полторы (like оба/обе and два/две) distinguish gender only in the nominative/accusative, collapsing
to a single oblique form полутора.

### §2.11 Time expressions
Time units up to and including 'week' require the accusative; from 'month' up require the locative;
full dates require genitive for day+month(+year); seasons and times of day require the instrumental
(except на заре/на закате/на рассвете, which take на + locative); a dative-plural construction (по
вечерам, по средам) expresses habitual/distributive time. Two clock-time registers are documented:
24-hour schedule time and everyday colloquial time-telling (§2.11).

### §2.12 Nondeclinables: adverbs and prepositions
Adverbs are regularly derived from adjectives by dropping the desinence and adding -o (with
hard/soft-stem doublets in some cases, e.g. искренно/искренне); the Russian Academy Grammar treats a
broad range of declined-form-turned-adverbs and verbal-adverb-derived adverbs as belonging to this
class (full list in Vocabulary). Adverbs of quantity require the genitive case and show a semantic
contrast between немного (neutral small amount) and немножко (more evaluative "hardly any");
ещё functions as a quantity adverb in addition to its temporal sense. The source tabulates Russian
prepositions in four groups (also-preverbs; not-preverbs; preverbs-only; compound prepositional
phrases) with their governed case(s) (§2.12, full list in Vocabulary) — noting that за is the one
preposition that (in exclamative "what kind of X" constructions) may govern a nominative rather than
oblique case.

### §2.13 Derivational morphology
Russian derivation is overwhelmingly prefixal/suffixal, with compounding of both. §2.13.1–2.13.2
tabulate native-Slavic and borrowed prefixes each with a noun/adjective/verb example (full lists in
Vocabulary) — the source notes borrowed prefixes (анти-, авто-, экс-, псевдо-, контр-, супер-,
микро-, макро-, ультра-, гипер-, квази-) are largely confined to specialized/technical vocabulary.
§2.13.3 tabulates Russian nominal suffixes in two classes: agentive (§2.13.3.1, ~35 suffixes each
with an agentive and, where applicable, a non-agentive example) and non-agentive (§2.13.3.2, ~13
further suffixes, mostly deverbal/deadjectival abstract-noun formants); §2.13.3.1 explicitly flags
-он as a colloquial-register-marked suffix (музон, блузон). §2.13.4 gives 13 adjectival suffixes
(qualitative/relational), referencing Townsend's fuller "enlarged suffix" combinatorics (-н-, -енн-,
-ск-, -ическ-, etc.) without reproducing them in full. §2.13.5 treats augmentatives and diminutives
as a semantic system rather than a simple form list: the two productive augmentative suffixes
-ищ-а/е and -ин-а differ systematically in meaning — -ищ-е gives enlargement via *expanded
quantification of a space* (and, per a 180-item survey of Zaliznjak's 1977 dictionary, shows gender
doublets in only 7 of 180 forms, is never itself the trigger of an animacy shift, and produces an
irregular animate-accusative-like paradigm even for formally-neuter augmentatives in the plural, cf.
Я видел таких уродищ/чудовищ 'I saw such freaks/monsters', discussed as a genitive-plural-as-animate
quantifier effect), while -ин-а gives enlargement via *individuation of a bounded referent*
(бусина 'one bead', градина 'one hailstone') and only secondarily carries a "large" meaning, with its
own smaller set of gender doublets (псина, дубина, скотина). Diminutives (§2.13.5.2) are treated via
a decade-plus, 1000+-informant sociolinguistic survey (Andrews 1996a; summarized study in St.
Petersburg): the *only* truly diminutizing Russian adjectival suffix is -еньк- (and even it can lose
diminutive force given ironic context/intonation — Хорошенькое дело! 'what a mess!'); nominal
diminutive suffixes show no consistent form-meaning mapping (a suffix's "diminutive" status is
usage-dependent, not lexical — городок can refer to a huge city), diminutive usage correlates more
with speaker age than gender (despite a folk belief that women use diminutives more), and the
most-produced diminutizing suffixes in the author's own 1994 survey were -к-а, -ик, -/о/к, -ён/о/к,
-оч/к-а (a secondary, less-frequent tier is also given — see Grammar note and the suffix list
captured in Vocabulary).

### §2.14 Verbs
CSR verbs mark aspect (perfective/imperfective/biaspectual), tense (past/non-past), mood
(indicative/imperative/conditional — Jakobson's terminology also recognizes an "injunctive" covering
hortative/imperative), person, voice, gender (past tense only), and number; Jakobson also identifies
an evidential function realized lexically via мол/де/дескать (reported-speech particles), and verbs
of motion add a further determinate/indeterminate distinction within the imperfective (§2.14).
- **'Have'/'be' typology** (§2.14.1): быть 'be' has no present-tense form in CSR; есть (existential
  'there is/are') and суть ('essence', now nominal) are its fossilized present-tense-slot survivors.
  The у + genitive possession construction is preferred over the existing but more restricted verb
  иметь.
- **Verbal desinences** (§2.14.2) are given in full morphophonemic transcription for
  infinitive/past/non-past/imperative/verbal-adverb/participle slots, plus the three verb-associated
  particles (reflexive -ся [bounded], conditional бы [unbounded], hortative -ка [bounded, hyphenated]).
- **Negation** (§2.14.3): не precedes the verb and is phonologically cliticized to it (occasionally
  drawing stress, cf. не был/не была/не было/не были); ни alone never negates a verb, it always
  requires a co-occurring не; a single verb may only be negated once, but every verb in a
  multi-verb sentence must be independently negated.
- **Aspect** (§2.14.4): the two-way HCB/CB (imperfective/perfective) opposition is described as
  speaker-viewpoint-driven rather than rule-derivable, with the perfective treated as the marked
  member (higher semantic density; bounded, completed-result readings whose relevance persists to
  speech time — contrast Этот фильм мне очень нравился 'I used to like it' vs. Этот фильм мне очень
  понравился 'I really liked it and still do'), the imperfective as the less-restricted,
  process-naming default that L1 speakers use far more creatively/frequently than L2 learners
  typically realize, and a discourse-level rule of thumb: match the aspect of a question when
  answering it (unless issuing a strong denial), and note that imperfective negative answers convey
  strong denial.
- **Bi-aspectual and uni-aspectual (unpaired) verbs** (§2.14.5): a substantial class of (often
  -ova--suffixed, often foreign-rooted) verbs is bi-aspectual, i.e. formally unmarked for aspect in
  their conjugated form though sometimes resolving to one aspect in specific tenses (e.g.
  организовать is bi-aspectual in non-past but exclusively CB in the past); a smaller uni-aspectual
  class exists only in HCB or only in CB. Certain matrix verbs *lexically select* the aspect of a
  following infinitive complement (govern-imperfective list vs. govern-perfective list, both given in
  full in Vocabulary) — the author frames aspectual-pair determination itself as an unresolved,
  more-complex-than-textbook-rules-suggest area of the literature (Andrews, Averjanova, Pjadusova
  2001).
- **One-stem verb system** (§2.14.6): following Jakobson (1948), any Russian verb's full paradigm can
  be generated from a single underlying stem, derived by comparing the infinitive and 3rd-plural
  desinence-stripped forms (the longer stem wins; ties resolve to the 3rd-plural form) and applying
  four stem+desinence combination rules (VC/CV addition; V1+V2 truncates V1; C1+C2 truncates C1).
  Twelve suffixed classes (2nd-conjugation: -i, -e, husher-a; 1st-conjugation: -a, n/s-a, -ova/-eva,
  -aj, -ej, -nu [×2 subtypes], -avaj, -o) and eleven nonsuffixed 1st-conjugation classes (syllabic
  resonants v/n/oj/j/ij; non-syllabic n·m/r; obstruents t·d/s·z/k·g/b) exhaust the regular system,
  each with a characteristic past-passive-participle desinence and derived-imperfective suffix (full
  class table and productivity notes — productive classes are 1, 6, 7, 8, 9; largest nonproductive
  classes are 2, 4, 10 at 50–60 roots each; smallest are 2–5-root classes 11–12 and most nonsuffixed
  classes); consonant mutation ("substitutive softening," marked by asterisk in the source's own
  table) accompanies specific classes under specific rules (bare softening before e/i vowels in -i/-e
  1sg only; throughout the paradigm for -a/-ova/-avaj classes). A closed set of ~19 irregular verbs
  falls outside the one-stem system entirely and is tabulated in full (§2.14.6.4; captured in
  Vocabulary). Derived-imperfective formation (§2.14.6.3) follows the one-stem truncation/addition
  rules plus class-specific extra rules (total truncation before -aj/-nu/-[nu]; a stem vowel shift
  o→a before -ivaj-; a fill-vowel и/ы alternation in non-syllabic-resonant and -nu/n·s-a classes,
  offered as further evidence и/ы are allophones not distinct phonemes) — Flier (1972) is noted as
  proposing an alternative single-suffix (-aj, preceded by zero/-iv-/-ev-) analysis.
- **Verbs with the bound particle -ся** (§2.14.7): all -ся verbs are intransitive by definition, and
  fall into 6 semantic types (reflexive; mutual/"each other"; passive/intransitivized-transitive;
  impersonal — a broader category extending beyond just -ся forms, cf. §3.5; obligatory-ся-only
  lexemes; a small closed iterative-only class), with some verbs straddling multiple types and some
  -ся forms diverging semantically from their bare counterpart (e.g. полагаться/положиться 'depend
  on' vs. полагать 'suppose'). Full exemplar lists per type are captured in Vocabulary.
- **Verbs of motion** (§2.14.8): non-prefixed motion verbs have two imperfective forms — determinate
  (a "freeze-frame," single-direction, often translatable as an English progressive; obligatory in a
  closed set of expressions like Сколько идти/ехать? and weather-verb дождь/снег идёт) and
  indeterminate (names the action generically, or gives a "round trip" reading in the past tense, or
  characterizes manner of travel, or covers habitual bathroom-going); the perfective is only formed
  with a prefix (not every prefixed motion verb is perfective). The по- perfective prefix on a
  determinate stem means inception or change of speed/direction; on an indeterminate stem it means
  bounded-duration-with-no-spatial-goal (походить, поездить). Mode-of-motion choice (§2.14.8.4) is
  governed less by "on foot vs. vehicle" than by route type: scheduled/tracked vehicles (trains,
  buses, trams, boats, some taxis) and in-town errands use ходить/идти even when a vehicle is
  actually used; ездить/ехать is required once city limits or international travel are involved;
  летать/лететь for flight.

### §3.1 Syntax and syntactic categories
CSR is typologically flexible (SOV/SVO both attested; OSV/OVS/VSO also possible), with a strong
tendency for pronominal or animate objects to precede the verb and inanimate/non-pronominal objects
to follow; word-order changes can shift not just discourse emphasis but full referential meaning
(contrast Я должен тебе сказать кое-что 'I should tell you something' vs. Я ему должен десять
долларов 'I owe him $10'; Ему сорок лет 'he's 40' vs. Ему лет сорок 'he's about 40', where numeral
postposing itself signals approximation) (§3.1).

### §3.2 Conditionals/hypotheticals
Conditional если-clauses take the future tense; the hypothetical particle бы (used with еси or alone,
attaching to a past-tense verb, never itself stressed) marks counterfactual/hypothetical mood.
Because CSR's tense system is comparatively coarse, a single бы-construction is often genuinely
ambiguous between several distinct English conditional readings (present-counterfactual,
past-counterfactual, etc.) (§3.2).

### §3.3 Grammatical particles
Beyond бы, the interrogative particle ли has a rigid placement rule (must follow the first
stress-bearing word-form in its clause — so never immediately after a preposition or negative
particle) and is required in reported yes/no questions and 'whether' constructions; purpose clauses
and want-someone-to-do-X constructions require чтобы (§3.3).

### §3.4 Verbal government
CSR verbal government draws on the full case system in combination (single-case, multi-case, and
prepositional-phrase government all attested for different verbs); the source gives several
idiomatic examples illustrating how deeply case-government is lexicalized per verb rather than
predictable from a general rule (§3.4).

### §3.5 Impersonal constructions
Impersonal constructions are a major CSR discourse resource, not limited to -ся verbs: they occur
also with the zero-copula 'be' + adverb, and with dedicated impersonal verbs that are always
neuter-singular in the past and 3rd-singular in the present/future. The source organizes impersonal
semantic fields into weather/nature (не/тепло/дует/морозит/дождит), psychological/physiological human
states and involuntary bodily sensations (грустно/скучно/знобит/болит/заложило/пахнет/воняет/клонит
в сон/потемнело в глазах/звенит в ушах/бросает в жар/неудобно/вырвало), and infinitive-based
polite offers/suggestions (Заказать вам такси? 'shall I call you a cab?') (§3.5).

### §3.6 Proverbs and collocations
Proverbs/collocations are frequently truncated in actual use (only the opening clause spoken, with
the remainder either implied or supplied by the interlocutor as an expected retort); the source gives
several examples with the elidable continuation marked in parentheses and potential retorts in
brackets (full list in Vocabulary) (§3.6).

### §3.7 Use of profanity in CSR
Profanity was effectively excluded from official Soviet-era print; this has relaxed since the early
1990s, though Russian media remains more conservative about public profanity than many Western
counterparts, and profanity use is still gendered (women use it less, especially outside the
immediate family) though increasingly common among contemporary speakers generally. The source
documents common mild-oath euphemisms (many built by altering the initial sound of a taboo root while
keeping a similar sound-shape — the "ё-" pattern in ёлки-палки, ё-моё, блин, etc.) and names, but
deliberately does not reproduce, the "three-story obscenity" (трёхэтажный мат) phenomenon: derivation
where an obscene root is substituted into an otherwise-ordinary word-formation pattern to produce a
mundane meaning like 'steal' or 'bore' (§3.7).

### Articles (explicit non-topic)
The source explicitly notes, in its closing line, that articles are not a feature of the East Slavic
languages (Russian, Ukrainian, Belorussian) and are accordingly not addressed in the grammar (p. 138)
— included here because it is a real, if negative, typological grammar point directly relevant to
this project's morphological-typology tracking for Russian.

## Grammar points

### The Cyrillic alphabet and its printed/cursive forms (Unit 1)

The alphabet has 33 letters, presented in both printed and handwritten (cursive) forms; ъ, ы, ь
never occur word-initially. Approximate English sound equivalents are given per letter (p. 1).

### Stress and the two spelling rules (Unit 2)

Stress placement is not predictable and must be learned per word; the book marks stress throughout
except where an exercise reproduces a real advertisement/ticket. Two spelling rules recur constantly
in later units: (1) never write ы, ю, я after г, к, х, ж, ч, ш, щ — write и, у, а instead; (2) never
write an unstressed о after ж, ч, ш, щ, ц (pp. 2–3).

### Noun gender (Unit 3)

Gender is not marked by an article (Russian has none) but inferred from the nominative singular
ending: masculine (consonant, -й, -ь), feminine (-а, -я, -ия, -ь), neuter (-о, -е, -ие). The soft
sign (-ь) is the only ending shared across genders and must be learned per word (about three-quarters
of soft-sign nouns are feminine). A handful of nouns are masculine by natural meaning despite a
feminine-looking -a ending (дедушка, мужчина, дядя, папа); -мя nouns (время, имя) are neuter; кофе
is masculine despite ending in -e (p. 5).

### Nominative case (Units 4–6)

Nominative singular names the grammatical subject and is the dictionary citation form; since
Russian has no present-tense "to be," two nouns/adjective-noun pairs can sit side by side with no
copula. Word order is flexible — the subject need not open the sentence (pp. 6–7). Regular
nominative plural adds -ы/-и (masculine), swaps -а/-я/-ия/-ь endings (feminine), or -а/-я (neuter),
with occasional stress shift (место→места́) and "fleeting vowel" loss in some masculine stems
(отец→отцы) (p. 9). Unit 6 catalogs irregular nominative plurals: a masculine group taking stressed
-а/-я (дом→дома, учитель→учителя), a group taking -ья (брат→братья, друг→друзья — note friends is
end-stressed, giving gen. pl. друзей, while brothers is stem-stressed, giving братьев), a group in
-анин/-янин that drops -ин and adds -е (англичанин→англичане), a handful of feminine/neuter
irregulars (дочь→дочери, время→времена, ухо→уши), several indeclinable borrowed neuters (метро,
кафе, такси — same form singular and plural), and the strikingly suppletive ребёнок→дети,
человек→люди (p. 12).

### Accusative case (Units 7–9)

Accusative marks the direct object. Neuter and (crucially) inanimate masculine nouns are
unchanged from the nominative; animate masculine nouns take -а/-я (учитель→учителя). Feminine
nouns always change (-а→-у, -я→-ю; soft-sign feminines like дверь stay unchanged) regardless of
animacy. In the plural, inanimate nouns of all genders keep the nominative plural form; animate
nouns get their own genitive-plural-like endings that differ by gender, including several genitive
plural-adjacent stem insertions (девушка→девушек, сестра→сестёр) (pp. 15, 18). Unit 9 tabulates the
irregular-nominative-plural nouns' accusative plurals, which largely mirror their (also irregular)
genitive plurals.

### Genitive case (Units 10–12)

The genitive's core meaning is "of," and it is required after quantity words (много, мало), after
the numerals 2/3/4, and after negation of existence (нет + genitive — "there is/are no ..."). Both
singular endings (masculine -а/-я, feminine -ы/-и, neuter -а/-я) and the three-way plural endings
(masculine -ов/-ев/-ей, feminine remove-and-nothing/-ь/-й/-ей with occasional о/ё/e vowel
insertion, neuter remove-о/add-й) are laid out with the same irregular-nominative-plural-derived
exceptions recurring (друзей, детей, людей, матерей, дочерей, времён, ушей, имён) (pp. 21, 25, 27).

### Dative case (Units 13–14)

Dative marks the indirect object ("to/for") and is also required after a small closed set of verbs
(помогать, звонить, советовать). Singular endings are masculine/neuter -у/-ю, feminine -е/-и; the
plural collapses to just two endings for all genders (-ам/-ям), again derived from the irregular
nominative plural where applicable (друзьям, детям, людям) (pp. 29, 31).

### Instrumental case (Unit 15–16)

Instrumental expresses means ("by/with," e.g. by train, with a pen), the complement of certain
verbs ("to work as..."), fixed time expressions (летом, зимой, утром, вечером), and follows the
reflexive verbs заниматься/интересоваться. "With" in the sense of accompaniment (not instrument)
requires the preposition с + instrumental, and с picks up an epenthetic о (со) before a
consonant cluster. Singular endings: masculine -ом/-ем, feminine -ой/-ей or unchanged -ь→-ью,
neuter -м. Plural collapses to -ами/-ями for all genders, with irregular dочерьми/детьми/людьми as
exceptions to that collapse (pp. 33, 37).

### Prepositional case (Units 17–18)

The prepositional case has no meaning of its own — it exists purely to follow certain prepositions,
chiefly в/на (in/at, on/at). Singular is -е for masculine/neuter regular nouns, -е/-и for feminine;
a set of masculine nouns takes an irregular stressed -у instead (аэропорту, году, лесу, саду). Plural
is -ах/-ях for all genders, again inheriting irregular-nominative-plural forms where relevant
(друзьях, детях, людях) (pp. 35, 38).

### Six-case system overview (Unit 19)

A single example sentence (Мария даёт бутылку вина Сергею — "Maria gives the bottle of wine to
Sergei") is reused to walk through what each of the six cases is doing in the same sentence,
consolidating Units 4–18 before moving on to adjective agreement (p. 39).

### Long-form adjectives: unstressed, stressed, and soft (Units 20–21)

Adjectives agree in gender/number/case with their noun. The dictionary form (masc. nom. sg.) tells
you the adjective's type: unstressed (-ый, or -ий after г/к/х/ж/ч/ш/щ per spelling rule 1),
stressed (-ой), or soft (-ний, feminine -яя, neuter -ее, plural -ие — about 40 of these exist,
mostly tied to time/season, e.g. вечерняя газета "evening paper," зимнее утро "winter morning";
карий "hazel" is the only soft adjective whose stem doesn't end in н). Plural nominative is -ые for
unstressed/stressed, -ие if the stem ends in a spelling-rule-1 consonant (pp. 41, 43).

### Possessive adjectives and the reflexive possessive свой (Units 22–23)

мой/твой/наш/ваш agree with the possessed noun, not the possessor; его/её/их never change form at
all. Possessive adjectives are used less often in Russian than in English, especially for body
parts (У меня болит голова, "my head aches," has no possessive). Свой substitutes for мой/твой/
наш/ваш (and is more common than твой in conversation) but can never substitute for его/её/их —
whether to use свой vs. его/её/их there depends on whether the possessor is also the subject of the
nearest verb: Анна и Андрей любят свой сад ("their own garden," the subject's own) vs. ... их сад
("someone else's garden," which happens to belong to Anna and Andrei's antecedent) (pp. 45, 47).

### Adjective agreement across all six cases and both numbers (Units 24–33)

Ten units systematically work through every case × number combination for long-form adjectives
(accusative singular Unit 24, genitive singular Unit 25, genitive plural Unit 26, dative singular
Unit 27, dative plural Unit 28, instrumental singular Unit 29, instrumental plural Unit 30,
prepositional singular Unit 31, prepositional plural Unit 32). The recurring pattern: masculine/
neuter share one ending, feminine has its own, and the plural collapses to just two endings
(distinguished only by whether the adjective's stem ends in г/к/х/ж/ч/ш/щ, is soft, or is
possessive) regardless of gender — e.g. genitive/prepositional plural -ых vs. -их; dative/
instrumental plural -ым vs. -им / -ыми vs. -ими (pp. 49–68).

### Short-form adjectives (Unit 34)

Most Russian adjectives have both a long form (attributive, before the noun) and a short form
(predicative, after the noun, nominative case only). The short form is formed by shortening the
long form, occasionally requiring a vowel insertion into a resulting consonant cluster (известный →
known: masc. short известен). Colour, nationality, substance, and ordinal-numeral adjectives (and
soft adjectives generally) have no short form; рад ("glad") exists only in the short form. For a
handful of adjectives the long vs. short form is not just a register choice but changes meaning:
больной (long) implies chronic illness vs. болен (short) implies "not feeling well right now" — a
genuinely register/meaning-bearing distinction worth flagging for a synthesis phase (p. 69).

### Comparative and superlative adjectives (Units 35–38)

Compound comparative: более/менее (never inflect) + long-form adjective, agreeing with its noun.
Four common adjectives instead have their own dedicated long-form comparative rather than a
compound one (больше/больший, меньше/меньший, лучше/лучший, хуже/худший), and старший/младший,
высший/низший are used only in specific senses (seniority, superior/inferior rank) rather than as
general size/height comparatives (p. 71). Short-form comparative (predicative only, nominative
case only, means only "more..."): add invariable -ее to the stem; several very common adjectives
form this irregularly (ближе, выше, дешевле, дороже, лучше, хуже, etc.) (p. 73). "Than" is expressed
either with чем or with the genitive of comparison (short form only); intensify with гораздо/
намного; "by how much" uses на + accusative (p. 75). Superlative: самый + adjective (agrees fully,
no separate short form, so usable both attributively and predicatively); a very small set of
adjectives instead form -айший/-ейший superlatives used in fixed phrases (Ближайшая станция метро,
"the nearest metro station") (p. 77).

### Adverbs (Units 39–40)

Most Russian adverbs are identical to the neuter short-form adjective (медленно, быстро); soft
adjectives instead give -e adverbs (искренне); -ский adjectives give -ски adverbs, and no+-ски forms
the "in [language]" adverbial (по-русски). A closed set of time/place adverbs (когда, где, etc.) are
not adjective-derived at all; куда/сюда/туда/откуда etc. mark motion-towards as distinct from
static где/здесь/там (p. 79). Adverbs share the adjective's comparative/superlative machinery:
comparative = short-form comparative adjective (быстрее); further/earlier (дальше/раньше) exist
only as comparatives; superlative is formed by adding всего ("more than anything else") or всех
("more than anyone else") rather than a dedicated superlative adverb form (pp. 81).

### Cardinal and ordinal numerals; declension; use with nouns and adjectives (Units 41–43)

Cardinals 1–20 are listed individually; above 20 they concatenate. один behaves like a three-gender
adjective; два/две has a distinct feminine form. Numeral declension (Unit 42) is genuinely
irregular and worth flagging: 2/3/4 have their own quirky case paradigm (двух/трёх/четырёх etc.),
numerals ending in a soft sign decline as feminine nouns, and the "hundreds" (двести, пятьсот...)
decline based on their first digit. Unit 43 is the payoff: the numeral governing a following noun
(and any adjective) depends on the numeral itself — 1 takes singular agreement, 2/3/4 take genitive
singular noun + genitive plural (masc./neut.) or nominative/genitive plural (fem.) adjective, 5+
takes genitive plural of both noun and adjective; the whole phrase shifts into a single shared case
when governed by a preposition, and human "person" (человек) uniquely stays in its bare form after
a numeral rather than switching to genitive plural (pp. 85, 87, 89).

### Ordinal numerals, telling time, and dates (Units 44–46)

Ordinals behave as adjectives (1st–100th+ listed); третий is irregular, declining like a soft
adjective. In compound ordinals, only the final digit takes the ordinal form and only that digit's
ending changes for case or abbreviation (52-я неделя). Telling time mixes cardinals (on the hour)
and ordinals ("X minutes of the [n+1]th hour" for the right half of the clock face, без + genitive
for the left half); Russian also uses a plain 24-hour cardinal reading. Dates use the neuter ordinal
+ genitive of the month; years use an ordinal on the final digit of a cardinal-numeral compound, and
the case of that ordinal shifts to genitive or prepositional depending on whether a date/preposition
governs it (pp. 91, 93).

### Numerals in prices, weights, ages, and distances (Unit 47)

The same 1/2–4/5+ agreement rules from Unit 43 govern currency (рубль/копейка), age (dative + the
irregular genitive-plural-only lет), weights (грамм/килограмм, with the borrowed-word alternation
between грамм and граммов as both acceptable genitive plurals), and distances (метр/километр) (p.
95).

### Personal pronouns and their declension (Units 48–49)

The nominative-case pronoun set is я/ты/он/она/оно/мы/вы/они; ты is intimate/child/pet address, вы
is both the formal singular and the only plural. All six cases are given for every pronoun; он/оно
share identical oblique forms; the oblique third-person forms gain a prosthetic н after any
preposition (с ним, not *с им) — the prepositional case's н-form is why grammars list its column as
starting with н. "You and I" phrasing starts with мы (мы с вами, literally "we with you"); о becomes
обо before мне, с becomes со before мной. Russian prefers the bare personal pronoun over a
possessive adjective for body parts and sometimes for location (pp. 97, 99).

### Possessive pronouns (Unit 50)

Formed identically to the possessive adjectives but replace the whole noun phrase (Чья это книга?
Моя. — "Whose book is it? Mine."); его/её/их remain invariable here too (p. 101).

### Interrogative pronouns что, кто, какой, чей (Unit 51)

Что and кто each decline through all six cases and are used respectively for things and people —
notably кто is required even where English idiom says "what" (Кем вы хотите быть? "What do you want
to be?", literally "as whom"). Какой ("which/what sort of") declines like a stressed adjective. Чей
("whose") has its own small paradigm and must agree with the possessed noun, not the possessor (p.
103).

### Demonstrative pronouns этот, тот, такой (Unit 52)

Этот ("this," near) and тот ("that," far) decline much like adjectives; это used impersonally
("this is/these are") never changes form regardless of the gender/number of what follows. Тот also
means "the latter" and, combined with же (or же самый), "the same"/"the very same." Такой ("such")
declines like a stressed adjective and combines with long-form adjectives (p. 105).

### Determinative pronouns весь, каждый, любой, сам, самый (Units 53–54, numbered 55–56 in this
book's own unit list but placed here for their pre-relative-pronoun position)

Весь ("all, the whole") has its own irregular-looking but internally consistent declension. Каждый
("every") declines like an unstressed adjective; любой ("any") like a stressed adjective. Сам
("-self," emphatic) declines like этот and must agree with the noun it emphasizes (Сам учитель
сказал это, "the teacher himself said this"); самый declines like an unstressed adjective and gives
locational precision (в самом центре, "right in the centre") distinct from its superlative-particle
use in Unit 38 (p. 107).

### Reflexive pronoun себя (Unit 54)

Себя ("-self") has no nominative form and no person/number/gender distinction — one form covers
myself/yourself/himself/.../themselves, always referring back to the sentence's own subject,
including in cases where English wouldn't overtly say "self" (Он берёт с собой фотоаппарат, "He's
bringing the camera with him," literally "with self"). It also forms fixed reflexive-verb idioms:
вести себя ("to behave"), представлять себе ("to imagine"), чувствовать себя ("to feel [some way]")
(p. 109).

### Relative pronoun который (Unit 55, numbered as the unit immediately preceding this range's
cutoff)

Который links a noun to a following clause ("who/which/that") and is itself declined as an
adjective; its gender/number come from the antecedent noun, but its case comes from its own
grammatical role inside the который-clause (subject → nominative; object → accusative, etc.), and
if a preposition governs it, that preposition sits immediately before который, not at the end of
the clause. The который-clause is set off by commas. Который is reserved for use after a noun;
"that" following a verb (not a noun) instead uses что (Я думаю, что они приедут — "I think that they
will come") — что as relative/interrogative pronoun after кто is the explicit hand-off point into
Unit 56, which falls in the sibling file's page range (pp. 110–111 boundary, note only).

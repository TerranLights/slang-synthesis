**Cultural point — Superstitions in Russian culture (pp. 68–71).** Pre-Christian pagan roots (before
Russia's 988 A.D. conversion) plus centuries of political/economic upheaval preserved a rich
superstition set still active today. General: forgetting something and returning home is bad luck
unless you look in a mirror first; travelers and those staying behind sit in silence before a
journey (sometimes on suitcases); spit three times over the left shoulder / knock on wood to ward
off evil; wish luck with "Ни пуха, ни пера" (lit. nonsense), answered "К чёрту!" ("to the devil" —
equivalent to "break a leg"); meeting someone with empty vs. full buckets is bad/good luck
respectively; never shake hands or pass gifts over a threshold; a bird flying/crashing through a
window portends death, a bird pooping on you portends money; meeting a man first thing vs. a woman
means an unlucky/lucky day; garlic over the door threshold wards off evil spirits; a black cat
crossing your path is bad luck (detour + spit 3 times), a multicolored cat is good luck; unmarried
people must never sit at a table's corner (seven years unmarried is the penalty). Gift-giving taboos:
never give sharp objects without requesting a coin in exchange; no baby showers (gifts only after
birth); a purse/wallet gift must include money; an animal gift requires a symbolic payment back;
never give soap (washes away friendship) or a handkerchief (means you'll never meet again); flowers
must be given in odd numbers only (even numbers are for funerals). Physical superstitions: hot ears
= being talked about; itchy nose = you'll be drinking soon; itchy left hand = money coming, itchy
right hand = meeting someone; hiccups = someone remembering you; a dropped knife = male visitor, a
dropped fork/spoon = female visitor; breaking a glass = happiness; sneezing = you're telling the
truth; spilled salt = bad luck; ringing ear = make a wish (comes true if a friend guesses which ear);
eating scraps from someone's plate = you'll argue; let a cat (or historically a cockroach) into a new
home first to choose the best spot; never look in a mirror with someone else (bad luck); meeting a
funeral procession = good luck, a wedding procession = bad luck; whistling indoors/at sea scares off
your guardian angel; never point at your own body describing someone else's wound/injury (it will
befall the same spot on you).

**Grammar/cultural point — Словообразование (word formation) via prefixed root verbs (pp. 79–81).**
Russian slang builds systematically off root verbs via prefixes/reflexives, each nuance-shifted.
Three worked families given in full:
- **жрать** ("to chow") family: сожрать (to chow/destroy a person) → выжрать (chow down, drink a
  massive amount) → пожрать (to eat, chow) → нажраться (eat/drink to your eyeballs) → обожраться
  (eat till it's coming out your ears) → отожраться (eat until full, esp. after a period of not
  eating). Noun derivatives: жратва, жрачка, жратуха (all "chow, grub").
- **хавать** family: prefixed forms схавать, похавать, захавать. Noun derivatives: хавка/хавчик
  ("food"), хавало/хавальник ("pie hole" — mouth).
- **жевать** ("to chew") family: prefixed сжевать, пожевать, зажевать. Noun derivatives: жуйка,
  жвачка ("food, grub, chow").
This is a clear, well-documented case of the `morphological_play` derivation type (per
`00_Usage_Tier_Taxonomy.md`) productive within Russian slang, worth flagging for the mechanics-
analysis phase — prefixation on a single root systematically shifts degree/completion/excess meaning
across at least 3 independent verb families in this one source.

**Cultural point — SMS/texting slang, «рунглиш» (pp. 103–104).** Russian SMS/texting (from 2002)
produced a hybrid "рунглиш" (Russian+English) register, driven by: Latin-only early phone keypads;
Russian's two-byte Unicode limiting messages to 70 characters vs. ~160 in Latin transliteration;
easier Latin-letter keypad access; the prestige of typing in English; and avoidance of Russian
spelling-rule pitfalls. Numeral/symbol substitutions for Russian sounds: `3`→"зе" (Земля→"3emlya"),
`4`→"ч" (счастье→"c4act'e"), `5`→"опять" (o5), `6`→"б" (будешь→bude6, etc.), `7`→"я" (семья→7я),
`100`→"сто" (столица→100лица), `★`→"жена" (*ena), `'`→"ъ/ь" separator (подъезд→pod'ezd). Several
humorous SMS-forwarded aphorisms are given in the source (paraphrased, not quoted at length) playing
on double meanings of "нет," friend's-wife jokes, and "seven wonders of the world" boasts.

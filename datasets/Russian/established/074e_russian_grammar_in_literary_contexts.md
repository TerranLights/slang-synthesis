## Grammar points (terse — standard nominative-case/plural-formation material, largely redundant
with prior established/ sources)

Nominative case use for subjects and subject complements of быть/есть. p. 74. Regular adjective
plural endings (-ые/-ие by hard/soft stem or spelling rule) and regular noun plural endings (-ы/-и
hard/soft masc./fem., -a/-я neuter) with a small paradigm table. pp. 75. Standard.

**Irregular plural endings (pp. 79–81)** — genuinely useful closed lists, captured in the
vocabulary table below since these are commonly-needed exception forms rather than a rule: irregular
family-term plurals (мать→ма́тери, дочь→до́чери, брат→бра́тья, муж→мужья́, сын→сыновья́), suppletive
ребёнок→де́ти and челове́к→лю́ди, друг→друзья́, стул→сту́лья; a stress-shift list (жена́→жёны,
сестра́→сёстры, де́ло→дела́, сло́во→слова́, нога́→но́ги, рука́→ру́ки, окно́→о́кна, письмо́→пи́сьма); a
fleeting-vowel list (америка́нец→америка́нцы, кана́дец→кана́дцы); masculine nouns taking stressed
-á/-я́ in the plural (а́дрес→адреса́, до́ктор→доктора́, глаз→глаза́, го́лос→голоса́, го́род→города́,
дом→дома́, но́мер→номера́, профе́ссор→профессора́, учи́тель→учителя́); -анин/-янин nouns taking
-ане/-яне (граждани́н→гра́ждане, россия́нин→россия́не, with the book's own footnote that россия́нин
means "citizen of the Russian Federation," any nationality, not "ethnic Russian" — a genuine
civic/ethnic distinction the source flags explicitly); substantivized adjectival nouns (знако́мый,
учёный, больно́й, взро́слый, живо́тное, бу́лочная); indeclinable neuter borrowings (ра́дио, метро́,
пальто́, кафе́, ко́фе [exceptionally masculine], меню́, такси́, бюро́, кино́); singular-only nouns
(ме́бель [f.], оде́жда); plural-only nouns (брю́ки, де́ньги, но́жницы, очки́, по́хороны, щи, ша́хматы).
p. 80–81. Standard closed lists, worth full capture as reference vocabulary.

**Irregular declension paradigms (pp. 82–83):** мать/дочь take the infix -ер- throughout the
declension except nominative/accusative singular (full 6-case singular/plural table given); и́мя/
вре́мя behave similarly with a -ен-/-ён- infix; путь has a unique mixed declension (feminine-pattern
genitive/prepositional/dative singular путй but masculine instrumental путём). Standard, but the
full paradigm tables are captured here since a fusional language's morphology-mechanics analysis
phase will likely want the exact case-by-case forms.

**Numbers (p. 84):** nominative-case numbers governing subject noun phrases; genitive is the
default case governed by a number unless it is 1 or ends in 1 (21, 31, ...); ordinal number-adjectives
(neuter singular form) answer "what's today's date," with only the last digit of a compound ordinal
declined (два́дцать второ́е января́). Standard, cross-referenced to Unit 8 for full number-governance
rules.

**Introduction to subordination (pp. 85–87):** кото́рый (relative pronoun, declines as an adjective,
agrees in gender/number with its noun antecedent but takes whatever case its own clause requires) vs.
кто/что (used with pronoun antecedents — all/everyone that; кто is always grammatically masculine
singular, что always neuter singular, regardless of the real-world referent's number/gender — with a
footnoted colloquial exception: "кто" can take a plural verb when the antecedent is plural,
e.g. Мы поговори́ли со все́ми, кто там бы́ли). p. 86, footnote. Standard relative-clause material,
but the кто/что footnote is a genuine colloquial-grammar nuance worth flagging.

## Register/literary findings

### "Russian Saying" boxes
- **Не всё зо́лото, что блести́т** ("Not all that glistens is gold") — p. 75.
- **Не до́рог пода́рок, а дорога́ любо́вь** ("It's not the present that's dear, it's the love" /
  "It's the thought that counts") — p. 77.
- **Друзья́ познаю́тся в беде́** ("Friends are known in misfortune" / "A friend in need is a friend
  indeed") — p. 81.

### ТЕКСТ 7а — Lermontov, "Бородино́" ("Borodino"), 1837, opening three stanzas
A young soldier asks an older veteran ("дя́дя") about the 1812 Battle of Borodino, immediately
preceding Napoleon's capture and burning of Moscow. The old soldier's answer is a bitter
generational contrast: "Бы́ли лю́ди в на́ше вре́мя, / Не то́, что ны́нешнее пле́мя: / Богатыри́ — не
вы!" ("There were men in our time, not like today's generation: heroes — not you!"), followed by a
description of the retreat, the grumbling ranks impatient for battle, and the old soldier's own
grief at the surrender of Moscow ("Не бу́дь на то́ Госпо́дня во́ля, / Не отда́ли б Москвы́!" — "Had it
not been God's will, we would not have given up Moscow!"). The book's post-reading task frames this
explicitly as an intergenerational conflict about heroism and national memory — one of the clearest
"old guard vs. new generation" register contrasts in the corpus so far, textually paired with a
genuinely archaic-flavored lexicon (шты́к "bayonet," мунди́р "military uniform," кварти́ры used in
the military-billeting sense "winter quarters").

### ТЕКСТ 7б — Andrei Bely, prologue to the novel *Петербу́рг* ("Petersburg"), 1912–1916
A deliberately bureaucratic-sounding, digressive definitional passage in which the narrator answers
"What is the Russian Empire?" with a mock-official enumeration of provinces/kingdoms/city-tiers
("вели́кую, ма́лую, бе́лую и черво́нную Русь" — Great/Little/White/Red Rus'; "грузи́нское, по́льское,
каза́нское и астраха́нское ца́рство" — the Kingdoms of Georgia, Poland, Kazan, and Astrakhan), then
narrows to a self-consciously pedantic definition of St. Petersburg and its Nevsky Prospect as
"a public prospect for the circulation of the public (not air, for example)" — an intentionally
absurdist, bureaucratic-register parody the book's own post-reading tasks ask students to identify
by tone. Genuinely distinct historical-political vocabulary: "Ца́рьград" / "Константино́поль" as
period names for Constantinople/Istanbul, claimed "by right of inheritance" (по пра́ву насле́дия) —
alluding to the old Russian imperial claim on Constantinople.

### ТЕКСТ 7в — Alexander Blok, untitled poem ("Ночь, у́лица, фона́рь, апте́ка..."), 1912
A short, famous poem the book instructs students to memorize: a bleak cyclical vision of urban
stasis — "Live another quarter-century — everything will be the same. There is no way out. You'll
die, you'll start again from the beginning, and everything will repeat as of old: night, street,
lamppost, pharmacy" — closing with the opening line reversed (streetlamp/pharmacy/street/night). A
canonical Silver Age statement of fatalistic urban repetition; footnoted vocabulary includes
бессмы́сленный ("meaningless," built on смысл "meaning/sense") and тýсклый свет ("dreary/dim
light").

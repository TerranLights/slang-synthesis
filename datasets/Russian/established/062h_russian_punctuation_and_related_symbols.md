## Grammar points

### Chapter 12: Parentheses and Brackets (Скобки)

Скобки covers both English "parentheses" and "brackets"; only круглые скобки (round brackets/
parentheses) function as true Russian punctuation — квадратные (square) and угловые (angle) brackets
have narrower, more specialized uses.

**§12.1 Parentheses with incidental words and phrases.** A three-way hierarchy exists for detached/
inserted material depending on how disruptive or urgent it is: an ordinary detached element takes
**commas**; an abrupt or internally-punctuated one takes a **pair of dashes**; a genuinely incidental
comment (neither urgent nor essential) — the **inserted element (вставная конструкция)** — takes
**parentheses**: Кремль (что означает «крепость») возвышался перед нами "The Kremlin (which means
'fortress') towered before us" (p. 147). Parenthetical material can be a word, phrase, or even a
complete sentence (p. 147).

**§12.2 Parentheses enclosing stage directions (ремарки)** — the same rules govern stenography
transcripts (стенограммы) of conversations:

- (a) Stage directions standing alone (not directly after a character's name) begin with a capital
  letter and are enclosed in parentheses; the sentence-final period is written OUTSIDE the closing
  parenthesis, but a question mark/exclamation point/points of omission that belongs to the preceding
  sentence is deleted before the parenthetical material begins (unlike English, which keeps it):
  Строительство дороги закончено успешно (Аплодисменты) (p. 148).
- (b) Stage directions placed directly after a character's name do NOT begin with a capital letter,
  but ARE parenthesized, with the period following the closing parenthesis. Such directions may be
  set in italics (курсив); the speaking character's name may be set in boldface (жирный шрифт) or
  small capitals. English uses the same conventions, except the character's name may be followed by a
  colon or period rather than always a period (p. 148).
- (c) A stage direction interrupting dialogue is parenthesized, NOT capitalized, and takes no end
  mark of punctuation — same convention in English (p. 149).
- (d) A stage direction between two separate sentences of dialogue is punctuated as its own complete
  sentence, parenthesized together with its own end mark — same convention in English (p. 149).

**§12.3 Marks used together with a closing parenthesis** — general rules (distinct from the
stage-direction-specific rules above):

- (a) A period always follows a closing parenthesis when an entire sentence is parenthetical — this
  is the OPPOSITE of English, which encloses the period inside the parenthesis for a fully
  parenthetical sentence: Рано утром они прибыли в Ленинград (Санкт-Петербург) — period outside even
  though the whole preceding clause could stand as a full sentence; contrast «Не стыдно ль стен тебе,
  не только что людей?» (А Васька всё-таки курчонка убирает) where the parenthetical IS a complete
  sentence and the Russian convention still keeps the period outside, unlike English (pp. 149–150).
- (b) A question mark, exclamation point, or points of omission PRECEDES the closing parenthesis if
  it belongs to the parenthetical material itself — same in Russian and English: Он положил книгу на
  исписанный листок (ну и хитёр же он!) (p. 150).
- (c) If only the sentence's final word/phrase is parenthetical, an end mark relating to the WHOLE
  sentence stands OUTSIDE the closing parenthesis: Они были в Ленинграде (Санкт-Петербурге)? — same
  in English (p. 150).
- (d) Internal punctuation (comma, colon, semicolon) ALWAYS follows a closing parenthesis, since these
  marks relate to material outside the parenthetical insertion, not to the insertion itself — e.g.
  Николай Петрович, сверкая глазами, с гневом сказал Сергею (который после всего происшедшего едва
  мог поднять глаза): «Не правда ли, это по меньшей мере странно?» Note also that no comma precedes
  который here, since the whole relative clause is already enclosed in parentheses — removing the
  parentheses would require restoring the comma before который (pp. 150–151).

**§12.4 The square brackets (квадратные скобки)** — a few narrow uses:

- (a) **Author's additions to quoted matter** — alterations, additions, or clarifications the quoting
  author inserts into someone else's quoted words, same as English: Этот сорт [яблок] был
  скороспелый... "That sort [of apple] was fast-ripening..." (p. 151).
- (b) **Names of speakers in a play** — optionally enclosed in square brackets instead of the more
  common boldface/small-caps convention (p. 152).
- (c) **Phonetic transcription** in dictionaries and linguistic works — поздно [поздна]; солнце
  [сонца] — same convention as English (p. 152).

**§12.5 The angle brackets (угловые скобки)** — rare, used mainly to mark points of omission ADDED by
a later quoting author within a quotation that already contains its own points of omission, to
distinguish the two: «Она не может отречься от своего чувства <...>. У неё... Может быть, Таня права,
что это само собой понемногу пройдёт <...>» (p. 152).

### Chapter 13: Quotation Marks (Кавычки)

The standard printed shape is «...» (angle-style, sometimes called "little spruce trees," ёлочки);
handwritten Russian instead uses „...“ (called "little paws," лапочки), placed below the line at the
opening, above it at the close.

- **§13.1 Direct speech** — quotation marks set off direct speech (full treatment deferred to Ch. 14).
- **§13.2 Words used in some special way** — quotes can signal irony/sarcasm, an obsolete/rare
  meaning, or otherwise-marked usage: «деловой стороне дела» (irony); «ветошь» meaning old grass, i.e.
  a rare/archaic term (p. 154). **Note**: Russian print more commonly uses underlining/underscoring
  (подчёркивание), italics (курсив), boldface (жирный шрифт), or all capitals (прописные буквы) as
  alternatives to quotation marks for emphasis — English relies on these devices even more heavily.
  Foreign words that Russian would enclose in кавычки are instead typically italicized or underlined
  (p. 155).
- **§13.3 Quotation marks with names, epithets, and titles** — Russian uses quotation marks (where
  English often uses none, or italics/underlining) to set off: (1) titles of newspapers, books,
  magazines; (2) names of theaters, cinemas, hotels, restaurants, businesses, ships, sports teams,
  organizations; (3) car/appliance/electronics brand names; (4) perfume/cosmetics/cigarette/candy
  brand names; (5) epithets (nicknames, special names) — e.g. кинотеатр «Спартак», журнал «Юность»,
  духи «Серебристый ландыш» (pp. 155–156).
- **§13.4 Review of marks used together with an end quote:**
  - (a) **A period follows the closing quotation mark in Russian** — the opposite of American English
    convention, which places the period inside the closing quote — regardless of whether a whole
    sentence or just a single word is quoted (pp. 156–157).
  - (b) A question mark/exclamation point/points of omission PRECEDES the closing quotation mark if it
    belongs to the quoted material itself; no additional period is then needed (pp. 156–157).
  - (c) If such a mark belongs to the sentence as a whole rather than the quoted material, it stands
    AFTER the closing quotation mark: Кто поверит этим красивым «сказкам Венского леса»! (p. 157).
    **The period, uniquely among Russian end marks, ALWAYS follows the closing quote regardless of
    whether it belongs to the quote or the whole sentence** — this is the one case where Russian
    doesn't distinguish "belongs to quote" vs. "belongs to whole sentence" the way it does for
    question marks/exclamation points (p. 157).
  - (d) A comma, colon, or semicolon ALWAYS follows the closing quotation mark (p. 157).
- **§13.5 Quotation marks used inside other quotation marks** — Russian uses the „...“/" " shape
  (лапочки) to enclose a quote nested inside a larger «»-quote: Она ответила: «Я приехала вчера на
  теплоходе „Туркмения"». **English instead uses single quotation marks (' ') for the same nesting
  function** — the single-quote symbol is not normally used in Russian at all. Some modern
  publications reverse the customary roles, using „ "/лапочки as the primary quote mark and confining
  «»/ёлочки to the nested role (pp. 158).

### Chapter 14: Punctuating Direct Speech

Punctuating direct speech (прямая речь) uses BOTH the dash and quotation marks together — a
combination with **no English equivalent**, since English dashes are never used to set off quoted
material.

- **§14.1 Dialogue set off as separate paragraphs.** When each speaker's line is its own indented
  paragraph (абзац), a dash (not quotation marks) introduces EVERY new line: — Как вас зовут? / —
  Меня зовут Андрей. Reporting words following a line of dialogue are set off by a SECOND dash; if the
  quoted material is a declarative sentence, its final period is replaced by a comma (which is then
  followed by the dash); a question mark/exclamation point at the end of the quoted line remains
  as-is, with no additional comma needed: — Болит голова? — спросила я. (p. 160).
- **§14.2 Dialogue arranged in a single paragraph** — requires both dashes and quotation marks. The
  quoted material is enclosed in «...»; dashes separate the reporting words from the quote and from
  each other. Same comma/period substitution rules as §14.1 apply within the quotation marks: «Читай»,
  — тихо сказал он мне (period replaced by comma before the dash) vs. «Читай!» — в её голосе
  прозвучало раздражение (question mark/exclamation stands, no comma before the dash) (pp. 160–161).
  A historical note: older printed Russian sometimes placed the period INSIDE the closing quote in
  such contexts — an outdated convention; modern Russian always places the period after the closing
  quote (p. 161).
- **§14.3 Punctuation of direct speech within a larger text** — governed by the position of the
  reporting words (авторские слова) relative to the quote:
  - **§14.3.1 Reporting words precede the quote**: a COLON introduces the quoted material (English
    uses a comma here instead): И Иван ответил сурово: «Тебе незачем сюда входить» (p. 161).
  - **§14.3.2 Reporting words follow the quote**: preceded by a dash. If the quote is a narrative
    (declarative) sentence, its final period is deleted and replaced by a comma before the dash (comma
    stands OUTSIDE the closing quote in Russian, INSIDE it in English): «Читай», — тихо сказал он мне.
    If the quote ends in a question mark/exclamation/points of omission instead, no comma is added —
    just a dash: «Читай!» — в её голосе прозвучало раздражение (p. 162). This dash-after-quote
    convention is identical whether the dialogue is formatted as separate paragraphs (§14.1) or a
    single paragraph.
  - **§14.3.3 Reporting words interrupt the quote**: the ENTIRE utterance, including the reporting
    words, is enclosed in a SINGLE pair of quotation marks; dashes set off the reporting words from
    the quoted material on both sides. The second part of the quote begins with a capital letter if it
    starts a new sentence, lowercase if it's a continuation of the same sentence as the first part:
    «Хотите ли, доктор, — отвечал я, — чтоб я раскрыл вам мою душу?» (pp. 162–163).
- **§14.4 Punctuation with the dashes that set off reporting words inside quoted material** — a
  systematic set of rules governing what happens to whatever punctuation mark originally stood at the
  interruption point:
  - **§14.4.1 Interruption mid-sentence**: (a) if no punctuation stood at the break, a comma is added
    before AND after the dash-enclosed reporting words: «Теперь мы успеем на поезд», — проговорил он.
    / «Теперь, — проговорил он, — мы успеем на поезд». (b) if a comma already stood at the break, it
    remains and a second comma follows the reporting words. (c) if a colon or semicolon stood at the
    break, it is REPLACED by a comma, and another comma follows the reporting words (pp. 163–164).
  - **§14.4.2 Interruption at the end of a narrative sentence**: the sentence-final period is replaced
    by a comma plus dash; the reporting words are closed by a period plus another dash, and the next
    quoted sentence begins with a capital letter: «Напрасно ты, Валентин, взялся за это дело,— сказал
    Пётр Петрович, выслушав мой рассказ.—Как ты можешь им помочь?» (p. 164).
  - **§14.4.3 Interruption after a question/exclamation**: the question mark/exclamation point remains,
    followed by a dash WITHOUT a comma; the reporting words are then closed by a period and another
    dash: «Я-то? — вновь встрепенулся Митя.— Я уйду» (p. 164).
  - **§14.4.4 Interruption after points of omission**: the points of omission remain, followed by a
    dash without a comma; the reporting words are then followed by EITHER a comma (if the quoted
    sentence continues) or a period (if a new sentence begins): «А где же...— промямлил он,— ваша
    кепка?» (pp. 164–165).
  - **§14.4.5 Interruption after a closing parenthesis**: the closing parenthesis remains, followed by
    a comma plus dash; a second comma-plus-dash follows the reporting words: «Этот Мишка (сын
    директора),— сказал Баженов,— явно больной» (p. 165).
  - **§14.4.6 Compound reporting words split between the preceding and following quote**: when the
    reporting-word phrase itself has two parts (one referring back, one forward), a COLON plus dash
    joins the second part to the following quote, while the first part is closed by a comma (or any
    end mark except a period) plus dash: «Ну, распрощались,— промолвил он и чуть слышно прибавил:—
    Вероятно, навсегда» (p. 165).
- **§14.5 Quoted material in the role of a primary sentence element.** When a quoted expression itself
  functions as a grammatical constituent (e.g. subject) of the surrounding sentence, it is simply
  enclosed in quotation marks with its sentence-initial capital and any internal/end punctuation
  (except a sentence-final period) preserved, but is NOT set off as its own paragraph: Его ежедневное
  «Здравствуйте, дорогая» начало нас раздражать "His daily 'Hello, dear' began to annoy us." Such
  quoted-phrase subjects are treated as neuter singular for verb/adjective agreement purposes — same
  conventions used in English (pp. 165–166).

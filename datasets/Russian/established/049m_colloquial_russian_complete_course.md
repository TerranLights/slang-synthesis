## Grammar points

### Lesson 18 — Present/past active participles, не́кого/не́чего/не́где/не́куда/не́когда
- **Participles as a substitute for кото́рый-clauses**: any кото́рый relative clause built on an
  active verb (не passive) can be replaced by a single participial adjective agreeing with its
  antecedent noun, functioning as a more compact, more literary/written-register alternative — Я
  зна́ю же́нщину, чита́ющую газе́ту is presented as directly equivalent to (and more formal/
  written-feeling than) Я зна́ю же́нщину, кото́рая чита́ет газе́ту. Russian has four participle
  types in total (present active, past active, present passive, past passive); this lesson covers
  only the two **active** types, tense determined by which кото́рый-clause tense they replace.
- **Present active participle formation**: take the 3rd-person-plural present, strip -т, add
  -щий: чита́ют→чита́ющий, пи́шут→пи́шущий, но́сят→но́сящий. Stress for 1st-conjugation-derived
  participles matches the 3rd-plural (рабо́тают→рабо́тающий); for 2nd-conjugation-derived
  participles it usually matches the infinitive instead (води́ть→водя́щий) — **except** a small
  irregular-stress subset (люби́ть→лю́бящий, лечи́ть→ле́чащий) that breaks even this secondary
  rule, meaning stress placement in this paradigm has two competing general rules plus lexical
  exceptions to the second. Present active participles from **reflexive** verbs invariably keep
  -ся even when it follows a vowel (a position where an ordinarily-alternating -ся/-сь reflexive
  ending would take -сь): учи́ться→уча́щийся, never *уча́щийся-сь. All present active participles
  decline like the mixed adjective хоро́ший and agree in gender/case/number with their antecedent
  noun, exactly as кото́рый does — but, critically, **participles carry no independent case value
  of their own the way кото́рый does**; a participle's case is fixed by simple adjectival agreement
  with the noun it modifies, whereas кото́рый's case is determined by its own role inside the
  relative clause — meaning the participle strategy is only available when the missing subject's
  case inside the "clause" is nominative (the subject position), not for oblique-case relative
  clauses, an important licensing restriction the source doesn't spell out explicitly but which
  falls out of participles being adjectives rather than full pronouns.
- **Present active participles lexicalized as ordinary adjectives/nouns**: a specific closed list is
  given where the participial origin has bleached into a plain adjective/noun sense no longer
  necessarily tied to an ongoing action — веду́щий "leading" (as in "leading sociologist," a fixed
  epithet rather than "the one who is leading right now"), сле́дующий "following/next," теку́щий
  "current" (from течь "to flow" — literally "flowing," now a dead metaphor for "present-day"),
  подходя́щий "suitable/fitting," бу́дущий "future" (also, notably, still doubling as the literal
  present active participle of быть, "being" — a genuine synchronic ambiguity where one form serves
  both the fully lexicalized adjective sense and the compositional participial sense), настоя́щий
  "present, real" (explicitly noted as **no longer used as a participle at all** in modern Russian —
  a case of complete grammaticalization/lexicalization loss of the participial reading), and
  куря́щий, substantivized directly as the noun "smoker." This full cline — from бу́дущий (still
  ambiguous between participle and adjective) through веду́щий/сле́дующий (adjective-primary,
  participle-reading still recoverable) to настоя́щий (participle reading entirely lost) — is an
  unusually well-documented worked example of a participle-to-adjective grammaticalization pathway
  at several different stages simultaneously, a strong reference case for mechanics-analysis of how
  an invented language's own participial system might spin off frozen adjectives/nouns over time.
- **Past active participle formation**: strip -л from the masculine past-tense form (of either
  aspect) and add -вший: чита́л→чита́вший, прочита́л→прочита́вший. Where the masculine past
  doesn't end in -л (the familiar zero-ending masculine-past class from consonant-stem 1st-conj.
  verbs, e.g. нёс, вёл), add -ший directly to that same stem instead: нёс→нёсший, поне́с→
  поне́сший. Stress matches the masculine past-tense form, with one flagged exception (у́мер→
  уме́рший — stress *shifts* onto a different syllable than the base у́мер, unlike the regular
  pattern). A short irregular list: идти́→ше́дший (a fully suppletive participial stem, unrelated
  to any surface form of идти́'s own past tense шёл), происходить-family произойти́→происше́дший,
  вести́→ве́дший. Reflexive verbs' past active participles likewise invariably take -ся, never -сь.
  Past active participles decline exactly like present active ones (mixed-adjective pattern,
  agreeing with the antecedent noun) and substitute for a кото́рый-clause containing either the
  imperfective or the perfective past — **and where the two actions described (the participial one
  and the main clause's) were simultaneous rather than sequential, a present active participle can
  be substituted for what would otherwise be a past-tense кото́рый-clause**, subtly shifting the
  aspectual "flavor" of the sentence (Лю́ди, жи́вшие в Ленингра́де… [past active, "who had lived"]
  vs. Лю́ди, живу́щие в Ленингра́де… [present active, "living (at that same time)"] — both
  translate the same English relative clause, but choosing present vs. past active participle
  marks a subtle simultaneity-vs.-antecedence distinction the кото́рый-paraphrase itself doesn't
  force a choice between).
- **Не́кого, не́чего**: historically fused from нет кого́ / нет чего́ ("there isn't who/what"),
  these mean "there is no one (to)" / "there is nothing (to)" and are **defective — they have no
  nominative form**, appearing only in the cases required by their governing verb (Не́чего де́лать
  — accusative object of де́лать; Ему́ не́чем интересова́ться — instrumental, governed by
  интересова́ться; Вам не́кому писа́ть — dative, governed by писа́ть "write to"). The person who
  "has" no one/nothing is marked by the **dative** (Нам не́кого приглаша́ть "We have no one to
  invite," literally "to-us there-is-no-one to-invite"). When combined with a preposition, the
  preposition is **inserted inside the word**, between не- and the pronoun stem — Им не́ о чем
  говори́ть; Ей не́ с кем говори́ть; Ему́ не́ на что наде́яться — exactly the same infixing pattern
  already noted for никто́/ничто́/никако́й in Lesson 15, confirming this "preposition-splits-the-
  negative-pronoun" pattern is general across the whole не-/ни- pronoun family, not limited to one
  member. A further subtlety: **не́что** (rather than не́чего) is used specifically after
  prepositions taking the accusative, while не́чего itself covers the ordinary accusative direct-
  object function — a narrow allomorph split conditioned by whether the accusative role is
  preposition-governed or plain-object. **Critically, не́кого/не́чего are never combined with a
  negated verb**, unlike никто́/ничто́, which *require* verbal negation as part of the negative-
  concord system (Она́ ни с ке́м не говори́т "she doesn't talk to anyone" [negative-concord,
  negated verb] vs. Ей не́ с кем говори́ть "she has no one to talk to" [не́кого-family, verb NOT
  negated]) — the source explicitly flags this as the key structural difference between the two
  superficially similar-looking negative-pronoun families, and a valuable point for
  mechanics-analysis: two distinct "nothing/no one" strategies coexisting in one language, one
  requiring clausal negation concord and the other categorically excluding it.
  Ordinary tense-marking for не́кого/не́чего constructions is periphrastic, adding бы́ло (past) or
  бу́дет (future) invariantly (Нам не́кого бы́ло приглаша́ть; Вам не́кому бу́дет писа́ть) — the
  не́кого/не́чего word itself never conjugates or changes for tense.
- **Не́где, не́куда, не́когда**: pattern exactly like не́кого/не́чего (dative "haver," periphrastic
  бы́ло/бу́дет for tense, no combination with a negated verb) but cover locative "nowhere to be"
  (не́где), directional "nowhere to go" (не́куда), and temporal "no time (to)" (не́когда) instead of
  не́кого/не́чего's core "no one/nothing" — a small closed paradigm of five parallel "there is
  no-X available for [dative person] to do Y" impersonal constructions covering person, thing,
  location, direction, and time as the five "nothing available" categories. Не́когда carries an
  additional, unrelated idiomatic sense as a fixed narrative-opening formula, "once upon a time"
  (Здесь не́когда был дом "Once upon a time there was a house here") — a case of a grammatical word
  acquiring a second, etymologically-related but synchronically distinct lexical meaning
  (literally "at no [specific/nameable] time" > "once, long ago") alongside its primary
  impersonal-construction use.

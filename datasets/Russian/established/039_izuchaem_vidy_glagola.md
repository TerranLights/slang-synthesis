# Russian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Верb Aspect (Изучаем виды глагола, full text)

**Source:** Л.В. Архипова, Т.В. Губанова, *Изучаем виды глагола* ("Studying Verb Aspect"),
Издательство ТГТУ (Tambov State Technical University Press), 2010. ISBN 978-5-8265-0898-5.
A Russian-medium pedagogical workbook for foreign preparatory-faculty students, aimed specifically
at NSV/SV (imperfective/perfective) aspect. Full text covered: all 60 PDF pages = printed pp. 3–76
(preface through the appended aspect-pair dictionary), i.e. the entire book. `pdftotext -layout`
produced a clean, directly-readable Cyrillic text layer for the whole file — no font-substitution
cipher, no OCR garbling, no vision-reading needed. No handwritten marginalia was present in this
copy.

**Non-redundant-supplement scope note.** Per `00_Reference_Extraction_Spec.md`'s "non-redundant
supplement" dispatch pattern: Russian aspect (НСВ/СВ) is already covered in general terms by
several Wave 1 sources per `datasets/Russian/00_Extraction_Checklist.md` (Timberlake's *Reference
Grammar*, Wade's *Comprehensive Russian Grammar*, the SEELRC reference grammar, Franke's *Big
Silver Book of Russian Verbs* aspect-pair dictionary, among others) — those extractions were not
re-read line-by-line here (out of scope for a single chunk), but their scope is inferable from the
checklist's own descriptions: general reference grammars covering aspect as one grammatical
category among many, plus one dedicated large verb-conjugation dictionary. **Skipped as redundant**:
the basic factual content that any Russian grammar reference already states — that НСВ marks
general/repeated/durative action and СВ marks a single completed/bounded result; a large fraction
of this book's ~700 numbered drill items, which recycle the same handful of aspect pairs (читать/
прочитать, писать/написать, готовить/приготовить, etc.) across dozens of fill-in-the-blank
exercises with no new grammar or vocabulary. **Kept as genuinely new/deeper**: this source is a
*compact monograph devoted entirely to aspect*, and several of its systematic tables organize
aspect rules more explicitly and completely than a general reference grammar typically bothers to
— specifically (1) the governing-verb classification that determines which aspect an infinitive
takes after a given matrix verb, (2) the closed lexical class of "prone to unintentional action"
verbs used in the negated-SV-imperative warning construction, (3) the НСВ-only "inherent
repetition" verb list with its accusative-plural-object rule, and (4) the full simultaneity/
sequence rule for complex sentences with *когда*. These are extracted as Grammar points below. The
appended 150-entry aspect-pair dictionary (Приложение, "Словарь видовых пар глаголов") is also
extracted in full as the Vocabulary table — it is a distinct, deliberately curated *pedagogical
core list* of the most frequent aspect pairs with the book's own English glosses, which is a
different artifact from Franke's exhaustive verb dictionary even though the underlying lexemes
overlap heavily; kept for its glosses and its function as an intentionally-curated "essential
aspect pairs" set rather than for lexical novelty.

**PDF-extraction note:** this file's `pdfinfo` producer chain (`PScript5.dll` → Acrobat Distiller
5.0.5, 2011) and clean `pdftotext -layout` output showed no signs of the font-substitution or
cp1251-mojibake issues found elsewhere in this corpus; text was cross-checked by eye against known
Russian vocabulary throughout and is fully legible. Two multi-column table blocks (the
"Виды глагола и их функции" grid, p. 4–5 of the book, and several "Запомните!" two-column
comparison boxes throughout) rendered correctly under `-layout` with columns preserved
side-by-side — no scrambling observed, likely because the book's tables use generous whitespace
padding rather than tight multi-column typesetting.

## Grammar points

### 1. The four-way НСВ/СВ opposition in past/present narrative (pp. 4–5)

The book organizes aspect choice in past-tense narration around four paired oppositions, each with
its own diagnostic adverbial/contextual cues:

1. **General fact vs. concrete/resultative fact** — НСВ simply names that an action took place
   (*читал текст* "was reading/read the text [at some point]"); СВ reports the action's completed
   result (*прочитал текст* "read [and finished] the text").
2. **Process vs. bounded completion** — НСВ pairs with duration adverbials (*долго, весь вечер, всю
   неделю, целый год*, etc.); СВ marks either the endpoint or the start of a bounded action
   (*сделал* = end, *пошёл* = beginning of a new bounded action).
3. **Repeatability vs. single occurrence** — НСВ pairs with frequency adverbials (*обычно, всегда,
   часто, редко, иногда, каждый день, несколько раз*); СВ pairs with punctual adverbials (*быстро,
   сразу, вдруг, наконец, неожиданно*).
4. **Parallel (simultaneous) vs. sequential actions** — two НСВ verbs describe two actions
   happening at the same time (*Он сидит и читает* "he is sitting and reading"); two СВ verbs
   describe one action following another (*Сначала я выучил слова, потом прочитал текст* "First I
   learned the words, then read the text").

### 2. Simultaneity and sequence in complex sentences with *когда* (pp. 26–28)

This is more systematic than the plain-sentence rule above. Two sub-cases are distinguished:

- **Full simultaneity**: both clauses report a durative action that completely overlaps in time —
  both verbs are НСВ (*Когда я выполнял домашнее задание, друг Антон готовил ужин* "While I was
  doing homework, my friend Anton was cooking dinner").
- **Partial simultaneity**: one clause reports a durative (НСВ) action, the other a punctual (СВ)
  action that occurs *at some point within* the durative one (*Когда я шёл по улице, я встретил
  друга* "As I was walking down the street, I ran into a friend" — the meeting is a single instant
  inside the walking).
- **Sequence**: when *когда* introduces two punctual, non-overlapping actions, **both verbs are
  СВ**, contrasting with the НСВ-for-simultaneity rule above (*Когда Реда сыграл на гитаре, мы
  зааплодировали* "When Reda played [finished playing] the guitar, we applauded" — vs. *Когда Реда
  играл на гитаре, мы его внимательно слушали* "While Reda was playing the guitar, we listened
  attentively" for the ongoing/simultaneous reading).

### 3. Future tense: НСВ vs. СВ functions (pp. 29–30)

НСВ future (*буду + infinitive*) covers: (1) bare futurity of an action/fact, (2) an ongoing
process, (3) repeated/habitual future action, and (4) a "mobilizing" sense — announcing that an
activity is about to start as a group ("Сейчас мы будем писать словарный диктант" — roughly "Now
we're going to [all] write a dictation"). СВ future (synthetic, e.g. *напишу*) covers a single
action guaranteed to occur/reach completion, often in direct response to a question about whether
something has been done yet (*— Ты написал сочинение? — Нет ещё. Но вечером обязательно напишу.*).
A further contrast the source draws out with a comparative table (p. 46 area): **negated НСВ in
the future denotes an intentional refusal/absence of action** ("Сегодня я не буду переводить этот
текст" = "I don't intend to translate this text"), while **negated СВ in the future denotes an
inability or failure to reach the result**, independent of intention ("Я не переведу этот текст" =
"I won't [be able to] translate this text [it's beyond me]").

### 4. Imperative aspect system (pp. 44–52)

A parallel four-function table, structurally mirroring the past/present one:

- **НСВ imperative**: (1) invitation/permission to begin an action (*Читайте!*), (2) urging
  continuation (*Читайте, читайте!*), (3) marking an action as an ongoing process with a stated
  duration (*Читайте текст 10 минут*), (4) requesting a habitual/repeated action (*Читайте текст
  каждый день*).
- **СВ imperative**: (1) a one-off request (*Повторите, пожалуйста, это слово ещё раз!*),
  (2) a firm demand (*Дома обязательно повторите текст*), (3) advice (*Купите в киоске газету*),
  (4) recommendation, often sequenced (*Сначала повторите..., а потом напишите...*).

**Negation flips the imperative's function entirely, not just its polarity** — this is the
chapter's sharpest, most systematic point and is worth stating in full:

- **не + НСВ imperative = prohibition** ("don't do X [at all]"): *Не опаздывайте!* = "Don't be
  late!" ≈ *Не надо опаздывать* ("there's no need to be late" / "you shouldn't be late").
- **не + СВ imperative = a warning against an unintended/accidental action**, not a prohibition on
  intentionally doing something: *Не упади!* does not mean "don't [deliberately] fall" — it means
  "watch out, you might fall [accidentally]" ≈ "Ты можешь упасть." This construction is restricted
  in practice to a fairly small, closed class of verbs describing mishaps/accidents that could
  plausibly happen unintentionally: **разбить, уронить, потерять, простудиться, упасть, сломать,
  испачкать, обжечь(ся), проспать, пролить, заболеть** (as listed by the source, p. 47). The
  source gives an explicit equivalence: *не + СВ императив = мочь (наст. вр.) + инфинитив СВ*
  ("не + СВ imperative = 'can/might' + СВ infinitive" — i.e., a warning restates as "you could
  accidentally X").

### 5. The lexical set of "inherently repeated-action" НСВ verbs (p. 26)

A small closed class of НСВ verbs the source flags as carrying a repetition/habituality meaning
*intrinsically*, even with no adverbial cue present: **брать, вставать, давать, забывать,
присылать, получать, бывать, дарить, опаздывать, разрешать**. The accompanying rule: **when the
repetition sense is carried by the verb alone (with no adverb marking it), the verb's direct
object in the accusative is normally in the plural**, even where a single instance might seem more
natural in English (*Луис брал книги в библиотеке* "Louis used to take/borrow books [pl.] from the
library" — not "a book"; *Антуан получал письма от брата* "Antoine used to receive letters [pl.]
from his brother"). This plural-object cue functioning as a grammaticalized marker of habituality
is a genuinely specific, actionable detail not obviously present in the general reference grammars.

### 6. Aspect selection for a following infinitive, governed by the matrix verb (pp. 53–59)

This is the chapter's densest and most systematically tabulated content — a classification of
which matrix ("governing") verbs force which aspect on a dependent infinitive:

- **Modal/volitional verbs that can govern either aspect, disambiguated by meaning**: хотеть,
  желать, мочь, надеяться, стараться, стремиться, собираться, готовиться, решить (+ думать,
  мечтать, предлагать, обещать). With a **concrete-factual** sense (a specific, situated intention)
  these favor a СВ infinitive (*Мы хотим вас поблагодарить*); with a **general-factual** sense (a
  standing wish/habit, or when the repetition is already carried by the matrix verb's own form,
  e.g. *не раз обещал*) they favor a НСВ infinitive (*Я хочу жить в Москве*; *Вы должны каждый день
  посещать занятия*). Negated forms of the necessity modals (не надо, не нужно, не должен) always
  select a НСВ infinitive (*Вам не нужно покупать учебник*).
- **A fixed class that always governs an НСВ infinitive, regardless of situation**: начинать/
  начать, кончать/кончить, переставать/перестать, продолжать/продолжить, стать, любить/полюбить,
  нравиться/понравиться, учиться/научиться, уметь, уставать/устать, привыкать/привыкнуть (*Мне
  нравится отдыхать на юге*).
- **забыть and успеть idiosyncratically take an НСВ infinitive** despite being СВ verbs themselves
  (*Я забыл принести тебе словарь*) — flagged by the source as an exception worth memorizing on
  its own.
- **A closed set of predicative/evaluative adverbs governs an НСВ infinitive**: нехорошо,
  некрасиво, вредно, опасно, стыдно, неприлично, невежливо, нелепо, плохо, глупо (*Вредно курить*
  "It's harmful to smoke").
- **Verbs of request/intention (просить, советовать, решить, обещать, and similar) + не + НСВ
  infinitive express an intention not to do something** (*Мы договорились не встречаться завтра*
  "We agreed not to meet tomorrow"), **while the same verbs + СВ infinitive [without "не" attached
  to the governing verb, warning-style] express caution against an unintended action** (*Прошу тебя
  никому не говорить об этом* — the source's own paired example contrasts a plain intention-not-to
  reading against a warning-not-to reading, mirroring the imperative-negation split in point 4
  above).
- **нельзя is genuinely ambiguous depending on the infinitive's aspect** — this is presented as a
  standalone, high-value rule: **нельзя + НСВ infinitive = prohibition** ("not allowed to," *Нельзя
  курить в общественном транспорте* "Smoking on public transport isn't allowed"), while **нельзя +
  СВ infinitive = impossibility** ("can't be done," *Это слово нельзя прочитать! Оно написано
  неразборчиво* "This word can't be read! It's written illegibly"). A learner substituting the
  wrong aspect here changes the sentence from a rule to a physical impossibility claim or vice
  versa.
- **пришлось (a СВ, impersonal-dative construction) vs. приходится (its НСВ, present-tense
  counterpart)**: *пришлось* + either aspect infinitive reports a single instance of being forced
  into something (*Ему пришлось идти к врачу* "He had to go see a doctor [that one time]"), while
  *приходится* + НСВ infinitive reports an ongoing/recurring compulsion (*Приходится много
  работать* "One has to work a lot [these days, generally]").

### 7. A recurring learner false-friend: сдавать/сдать (noted independently of the appendix, from usage throughout the drills)

*Сдавать* (НСВ) means "to take/sit [an exam]" — with the outcome still open — while *сдать* (СВ)
specifically means "to pass," i.e. a successful result, not merely "to have finished taking it."
This is a case where the aspectual pair does not simply add "completedness" to the same event
type — the СВ member specifically encodes success, and a learner cannot assume "СВ = finished
taking the exam" without checking whether "finished" here entails "passed." Worth flagging
separately from the systematic rules above because it's a lexicalized quirk of this one pair rather
than a general aspectual principle.

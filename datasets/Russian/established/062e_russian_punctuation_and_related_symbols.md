## Grammar points

### 7.7.1 Commas that set off explanatory clauses (continued from 062d, full detail)

An explanatory clause (extending a predicate of mental/emotional state — "know," "be glad," "be
surprised") is always comma-set in Russian. Two further points beyond what 062d's forecast noted:

- **Indirect questions**: when the explanatory clause is an indirect question using a question
  word/pronoun (кто, что, какой, чей, куда, откуда, почему, зачем, с кем, etc.) or the particle ли,
  the comma is placed immediately before that word — Он не знал, кто приходил "He didn't know who
  had come" (p. 70). English never uses a comma here.
- **Exception — shared subject + что**: if the main and subordinate clause share the same subject and
  are joined by что, NO comma is used: Он делает что хочет "He does what he pleases." But if the main
  clause contains a correlate word like то or всё, a comma IS required: Она взяла то, что она видела
  "She took what she saw." If the two clauses have different subjects, a comma is always required
  even with что (p. 71).
- **Preposed subordinate clause**: a subordinate clause fronted for emphasis (conversational style)
  is still comma-set, just as when postposed: Почему она покинула дом, никто не мог догадаться "Why
  she left home none of her friends could guess" (p. 71).

### 7.7.2 Commas that set off attributive clauses

A comma precedes the conjunction/conjunctive word joining an **attributive clause (определительное
придаточное предложение)** to the **antecedent (определяемое слово)** it modifies. Full word list:
который, какой, что (unstressed), чей, чтобы, где, куда, откуда, когда, как, каким образом, как бы
не (p. 72).

- **Antecedent-dependent conjunctive word choice**: a form of кто (not который) is used when the
  antecedent is тот, каждый, всякий, любой, or все — Тот, кому я нужен, живёт здесь "The person who
  needs me lives here." A form of что (not который) is used when the antecedent is the pronoun всё —
  Всё, что было, не вернёшь "You can't recreate the past" (pp. 73–74).
- **The pronoun то as antecedent**: several different conjunctive words are possible — что, чтобы,
  какой, как, где, куда, откуда, когда — e.g. То, куда они шли, мне было неизвестно "The place they
  went to was unknown to me" (p. 74).
- **Logical-subject subordinate clauses**: a subordinate clause can serve as the entire logical
  subject of an impersonal main clause with no separate subject of its own — Мне было неизвестно,
  куда они ушли "I didn't know where they went" (literally "It was unknown to me where they went").
  Such sentences commonly open with a fixed set of impersonal predicates: Известно, что...; Ясно,
  что...; Неясно, что...; (По)казалось, что...; Слышно/видно, как...; Желательно/нежелательно было,
  чтобы...; Возможно/невозможно, что...; Надо/нужно/не надо/не нужно, чтобы...; Выяснилось, что...;
  Удивительно, что...; Интересно, что... — most of these can substitute когда, откуда, почему, кто,
  где, ли, какой for что depending on meaning (pp. 74–75).
- **Comma discipline vs. English's restrictive/non-restrictive distinction**: English sets off a
  relative clause with commas only if non-restrictive (adds non-essential information); a restrictive
  relative clause (essential to identifying the antecedent) takes no comma in English. **Russian makes
  no such distinction — every attributive clause is comma-set regardless of restrictiveness.**
  Forgetting this and importing the English restrictive/non-restrictive test is flagged as a common
  punctuation error for English-speaking learners (p. 76).
- **Relative clauses always follow their antecedent** — no sentence begins with one (p. 75, §7.7.3
  reminder carried over).

### 7.7.3 Commas that set off predicate subordinate clauses

When an attributive clause modifies a **predicate nominal** in the main clause rather than an
ordinary noun, it is called a **predicate subordinate clause (придаточное предложение-сказуемое)**
and is likewise comma-set: Я тот, кого никто не любит "I am a person whom no one loves" (p. 75).

### 7.7.4 Commas that set off dependent clauses of place

Answer Куда? Где? Откуда? The main clause typically contains a locative adverb (туда, там) paired
with a subordinate clause containing the conjunctive adverb (куда, где, откуда) — Мы пошли туда,
откуда доносились голоса "We went where the voices were coming from" (p. 77). The book demonstrates
a three-way structural distinction possible for the same conjunctive word куда across different
sentences: (a) as an ordinary dependent adverbial modifier of the main-clause verb; (b) as an object
clause (answering "what didn't he know?"); (c) as a subject clause (answering "what is unknown?") —
same surface conjunction, three different syntactic roles, always comma-set regardless (pp. 78–79).

### 7.7.5 Commas that set off dependent clauses of time

Answer Когда? Always comma-set in Russian on both sides if the clause is medial, regardless of word
order; English only sets these off when preposed, and even then only optionally/for emphasis (p. 80).
Multiple conjunctive words beyond когда serve temporal clauses: пока "while," пока не "until," как
"when" (sudden/immediate action), как только "as soon as" — plus several compound conjunctions built
on как or чем: прежде чем "right before," в то время как "while, as," с тех пор как "since," после
того как "after," до тех пор пока "until," до того как "before" (pp. 80–82).

### 7.7.6 Commas that set off dependent clauses of reason/cause (book's own heading says "Purpose")

**Terminology note:** the book's English section title reads "Commas that Set off Dependent Clauses
of Purpose," but the Russian term given is придаточные предложения причины (причина = "reason,
cause," not "purpose" — purpose would be цель) and every listed conjunction (потому что, оттого что,
так как, ибо, ввиду того что, благодаря тому что) and example sentence expresses reason/cause, not
purpose. This looks like a translation slip in the section heading rather than a substantive
grammatical point, but is flagged here since it could otherwise mislead.

Always comma-set regardless of word order in Russian; English allows the comma only when the reason
clause precedes the main clause (p. 82).

- **Divisible compound conjunctions**: потому что and оттого что can be split by moving the comma
  between the two halves (потому, что / оттого, что) so the first element (потому/оттого) becomes
  part of the main clause and only что starts the subordinate clause. This split mirrors a speech
  pause after потому/оттого and marks the reason as the sentence's topic/focus — Экскурсия не
  состоялась, потому что была плохая погода "The trip didn't happen because of bad weather" (neutral)
  vs. Экскурсия не состоялась потому, что была плохая погода "The REASON the trip didn't happen was
  the bad weather" (topicalized) (pp. 83–84).

### 7.7.7 Commas that set off dependent clauses of manner

Answer Как? Каким образом? Always comma-set in Russian regardless of word order; English only
occasionally sets these off, for special emphasis — Он жил просто, как все мы живём "He lived simply,
as all of us live" (p. 85).

### 7.7.8 Commas that set off dependent clauses of effect

Conjunctive words: поэтому "and therefore," так что "(and) so," оттого "and from that," вследствие
"as a result of" (the last mainly bookish). A dependent clause of effect always follows the main
clause. **Contrast with a manner clause built on так...что**: in an effect clause, the WHOLE
conjunction так что belongs to the subordinate clause (Река сильно блестит, так что глазам больно
смотреть "The river gleams brightly, so it hurts to look"); in a manner clause, так belongs to the
main clause and что alone starts the subordinate clause (Река так блестит, что глазам больно смотреть
"The river is so bright that it hurts to look") — same lexical material, different clause-boundary
placement and different comma position (pp. 88–89). Moving так after the verb (Река блестит так,
что...) intensifies the emphasis.

### 7.7.9 Commas that set off dependent clauses of condition

If...then sentences; conjunctions если "if," когда "if" (this meaning of когда, not "when"), если бы
"if" (hypothetical), когда бы "if" (hypothetical). The particle то, "then," can optionally open the
main clause for emphasis if the main clause follows the dependent clause. Always comma-set in
Russian; English commas here only optional, and only when the condition clause precedes (p. 89). The
colloquial conjunction раз, "if, since" (with the flavor of English "seein' as how"), can substitute
for если in conversational registers; когда бы (когда б) can substitute for если бы, including in
literary/poetic register — the book quotes a Pushkin stanza using Когда б не зной... (p. 90).

### 7.7.10 Commas that set off dependent clauses of concession

Conjunctions/conjunctive words: хотя "although, even though," несмотря на то что "despite the fact
that," пусть/пускай "even though" (from imperative particles), and the conjunctive phrases как ни "no
matter how," что бы ни "whatever," сколько ни "no matter how much." Always comma-set: Как ни силён
был страх перед неизвестностью, они продолжали путь "No matter how much they feared the unknown, they
kept going" (p. 90). This closes Chapter 7.

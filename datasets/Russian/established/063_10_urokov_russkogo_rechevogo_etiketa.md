**Coverage note:** This is a dedicated speech-etiquette phrasebook, not a general grammar — its
entire content is register-tagged phrase inventories (labeled **Официальная форма** "formal",
**Нейтральная форма** "neutral", **Неофициальная форма** "informal", plus intensity variants like
**Усиленная форма** "intensified" and reactive **Ответные реплики** "response lines"), organized
into 10 thematic speech-act chapters (Тема I–X). This register-tagged phrase content is exactly the
highest-value material the extraction spec asks to prioritize, so **every distinct phrase-formula in

blank, "compose a dialogue," etc.) and the literary illustrative excerpts used throughout (short

captioned "Прочитайте примеры употребления этикетных формул") — these are paraphrased/cited by

## Grammar points

### The three-register system as the book's organizing principle

Every one of the ten thematic chapters (introductions, forms of address, invitations, thanks,
requests, agreement/disagreement/doubt/opinion/apprehension, advice, compliments, congratulations/
wishes, phone calls) is structured around the same recurring three-way register split: **Официальная
форма** (formal — used with strangers, superiors, institutional contexts), **Нейтральная форма**
(neutral — the default, unmarked register), and **Неофициальная форма** (informal — used with
intimates, on ты-terms). Several chapters add a fourth axis of *intensity* independent of formality
(**Усиленная форма** "intensified," seen in Тема IV's thanks and Тема VII's "Настоятельный совет"
vs. "Нерешительный совет"). This is the book's single most load-bearing structural finding: Russian
politeness is presented pedagogically as a **crossed two-axis system** — formality (official/neutral/
informal) crossed with intensity/hedging (bare/intensified/hedged) — rather than a single linear
politeness scale. This is directly usable as a template for a conlang's own register system: any
derived slang mechanic that only varies *formality* is missing the orthogonal *intensity/hedging*
axis this source treats as equally systematic (pp. 4–5, 21–23, 28–30, 39–41, 60–62 throughout).

### Ты/Вы and the address-form (обращение) inventory as a linked system

Тема II's "Обращение" chapter gives the fullest inventory of address terms in the book, and it
correlates directly with the ты/вы distinction used throughout every other chapter: formal and
neutral registers consistently pair with вы-forms and status/role-based address terms (Гражданин!,
Господин {Surname}!, Уважаемые коллеги!), while informal registers pair with ты-forms and
diminutive/kinship-extended address (Тоня → Тонечка → Тонька; Тетя Аня). The diminutive chain given
for one referent (Глеб, Борис, Антонина; Тоня, Тонечка; Тонька, Антонина Лукинична — p. 12) shows at
least four distinct formality steps for addressing the *same person*, from full patronymic-bearing
form down through affectionate diminutive to a maximally-familiar hypocoristic ("Тонька") that
verges on impertinent if used by the wrong interlocutor — the book doesn't spell out that last
nuance explicitly, but the four-step gradient itself is a directly attested, non-inferred finding.
"Товарищ"/"Гражданин" are presented as still-current but flagged (implicitly, via being paired
alongside newer "Дамы и господа") as Soviet-era institutional holdovers now sharing the formal-
address slot with post-Soviet forms (p. 11).

### Subjunctive/conditional softening ("бы"-constructions) as a cross-cutting politeness device

The independently-appearing pattern "Я хотел бы...", "Мне хотелось бы...", "Не могли бы вы...", "Вы
бы изменили..." recurs in nearly every chapter (introductions, invitations, requests, advice) as the
single most productive politeness-marking device in the whole book — a subjunctive/conditional
"бы" attached to a verb of wanting, ability, or the main verb itself reliably shifts a bare, blunt
form into a softened, more formal one. This is presented consistently enough across unrelated speech
acts (p. 4, 21, 39, 61) that it reads as a genuine, general Russian politeness mechanism rather than
an idiom tied to any one function — directly useful as a portable "politeness morpheme" pattern for
mechanics analysis.

### Aspect and the imperative in requests (Тема V)

Тема V's "Нейтральная форма" box uses bare **perfective** imperatives (Дай, Сделай, Принеси, Напиши)
as the neutral/unmarked request form, while its "Неофициальная форма" box instead uses **perfective
future-tense** forms phrased as yes/no questions (Сходишь в магазин? Починишь телевизор?) — a
distinct informal-register construction where a future-tense statement functions pragmatically as a
polite request, softer than a bare imperative despite not using any subjunctive marking at all (p.
39, 41). This is a second, aspect-based (rather than subjunctive-based) softening mechanism running
in parallel with the "бы"-conditional pattern noted above.

### Apprehensive "как бы не + past tense" construction

Тема VI's "Выражение опасения" (expressing apprehension) box gives "Как бы не пошел дождь" ("What if
it rains" / lit. "How would it not rain") and "Как бы не пришел кто-нибудь" as a dedicated
apprehensive-mood construction: **как бы не + past-tense verb**, expressing fear that an undesired
event will occur, distinct from ordinary "что если..." (what-if) constructions used for genuine
uncertainty/suggestion in the same box (p. 51).

### Grammatical case explicitly named by the source (rare)

Тема I (p. 6) is the one place in this book where the source itself names a governing grammatical
case rather than leaving it implicit: "Я хочу представить вас (дательный падеж) ___" vs. "Я хочу
представить вам (винительный падеж) ___" — flagging that "представить" alternates between
dative-object ("present you to X") and accusative-object ("present X to you") case government
depending on which participant is topicalized as direct vs. indirect object.

# Russian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Кумбашева, «Человек в современном мире» (full book)

**Source:** Кумбашева Ю.А., *Человек в современном мире: учеб. пособие по разговорной практике*
(Флинта / Наука, Москва, 2006, ISBN 5-89349-980-8). Русский язык как иностранный,
advanced-level (ТРКИ-2 prep) thematic-text/conversation-practice course book. Full 199-page PDF
(`source_reference/languages/Russian/Russian Learning Pack [up-to-date as of 2012]/Vol 2 of 3/01 -
Audio Courses, Textbooks, Manuals/19.Человек в современном мире.pdf`), covering all 8 thematic
units (I–VIII) and their reading texts, lexical-exercise sections, and "Речевые образцы"
(functional-phrase) sections.

**Coverage note.** This source has a genuine, clean Cyrillic text layer (`pdftotext -layout`
produces fully readable output) — **no font-substitution cipher, no OCR garbling, no keyboard-
layout or cp1251/latin1 mojibake was found anywhere in this file**, unlike several other sources in
this corpus (see the extraction spec's Russian cipher-variant catalog). This is a Microsoft Word
2007-produced PDF with a proper embedded text layer throughout all 199 pages; no vision-reading was
required and no marginalia (handwritten or otherwise) was present.

Extraction scope: every "Лексика к тексту" / "Лексика по теме" vocabulary box in the book (the
source's own explicitly bounded per-text/per-theme vocabulary lists — there are ~50 of these across
the 8 themes) is captured below, organized by theme and text, matching the coverage rule's "every
distinct vocabulary item" requirement for the book's own designated vocabulary. In addition, this
extraction captures every explicit slang/idiom item flagged inside the "Объясните, что значит" /
"Найдите синонимы" exercise blocks (these frequently name genuine slang/colloquialisms the "Лексика
к тексту" box itself doesn't list, e.g. "лох", "понты", "мне всё пофиг") — this is exactly the kind
of register-marked content the coverage rule calls highest-value. The narrative reading texts
themselves (dialogues, short stories, adapted classics) are **not reproduced** — per copyright
discipline, only paraphrased context is given per vocabulary block, and quoted fragments are kept to
single short phrases.

This book has essentially no formal grammar-point apparatus (no case-paradigm tables, no verb-
conjugation charts) — it is a *lexical/pragmatic* course book organized around themes and
"speech-pattern" (notional-functional) formula banks rather than a grammar reference. Its
**Grammar points** section below therefore documents the book's structural/pragmatic content
(the notional-functional phrase-bank system, the register layer visible in its two literary
sub-corpora, and its slang/youth-register findings) rather than case/aspect paradigms, which are
already extensively covered by this corpus's dedicated grammar references (Timberlake, Wade,
Comprehensive Russian Grammar, etc.) — this is the kind of source those grammars don't
substitute for: real thematic vocabulary at natural register range, from `slang`/`colloquial`
teen speech to `archaic` 19th-century literary diction, with the book's own explicit tier framing
intact.

## Grammar points

### The book's organizing principle: theme + register, not case/paradigm

Unlike the corpus's dedicated grammar references, this book has **no case-declension tables, no
verb-conjugation charts, no aspect-pair morphology explanations** anywhere in its 199 pages. Its
entire pedagogical structure is: **(1)** a themed reading text with its own bounded vocabulary box,
**(2)** lexical/idiom exercises drawn from that text, **(3)** open discussion questions, repeated
per theme, and **(4)** a themed bank of "Речевые образцы" — ready-made functional/pragmatic
phrases for a specific communicative act (agreeing, disagreeing, expressing doubt, inviting,
requesting, expressing sympathy, etc.). This is a genuinely different content type from this
corpus's other Russian sources: a **notional-functional phrase inventory**, organized by
communicative function rather than by grammatical category — valuable for later slang-mechanics
work precisely because it's where the book's most register-marked vocabulary clusters (its
dismissive/annoyed/enthusiastic registers reliably surface colloquial and slang items: «Мне до
лампочки», «Мне без разницы», «Так себе...», «Классно!», «Кошмарно!», «Ни пуха ни пера!»).

### Register range: from criminal argot to 19th-century gentry diction

This single source spans an unusually wide register range for one book: **(a)** contemporary youth
slang (Theme I's "Это круто!" text: крутой, лыбиться, висюлька, обалдеть, шмотка); **(b)**
criminal/pickpocket argot, explicitly quote-marked by the source itself as non-standard (Theme IV:
лох, «пасти», «урожай», понты, дать стрекача, «хвост»); **(c)** conscript-army slang (дедовщина,
дух/дед, слабак, хлюпик, нытик, маменькин сынок); **(d)** Soviet-era historical slang, explicitly
framed by the source as a past register (фарцовка, «Берёзка», «новые русские»); and **(e)**
archaic 19th-century gentry vocabulary surviving only in the book's three adapted-classic-literature
texts (губерния, имение, помещик, барин/барышня). No other single reference-book chunk processed
so far in this corpus's Wave 1 spans this many usage tiers within one source — useful as a
comparison baseline once the analysis phase starts cross-referencing register against slang-
formation mechanism (e.g. whether criminal-argot metaphor sourcing, like «урожай» for a
pickpocket's takings, recurs as a productive pattern elsewhere in the corpus).

### Notable metaphor-productivity candidate: agricultural/pastoral metaphor in criminal argot

Theme IV's pickpocket-argot vocabulary («пасти» = "to shepherd/graze," used for tailing a mark;
«урожай» = "harvest," used for a day's take) draws its entire metaphor field from
animal-husbandry/agriculture — a `metaphor_productivity` candidate worth flagging for the analysis
phase (per `00_Usage_Tier_Taxonomy.md`'s derivation-type list, typical source tier `core`/rural
vocabulary repurposed into `slang`).

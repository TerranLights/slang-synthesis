## Grammar points

### Two types of Russian adjectival surnames (pp.57-58)
Russian surnames that decline like adjectives fall into two types by Nominative ending: **-ский/
-ская/-ские** (Успенский type — declines on the "mixed" pattern of русский, i.e. the г/к/х-stem
adjectival declension from Part 3), and **-ой/-ая/-ые** (Толстой type — declines on the "hard"
pattern of новый). Full six-case paradigms are given for both, matching their respective
adjectival-declension model from Part 3 exactly, including the Masculine-singular Animate
Accusative = Genitive rule (Он видел Успенского/Толстого).

### Patronymics: male -ович/-евич/-ич, female -овна/-евна/-ична (pp.58-59)
Russian patronymics (the "middle name" derived from the father's first name) come in gendered
pairs: male patronymics end in **-ович/-евич/-ич** (Иван Петрович, Иван Васильевич, Иван
Кузьмич) and decline exactly like Иван **except** the Instrumental, which takes -ем/-ём instead
of the plain-noun -ом (Иваном Петровичем; Кузьмичём with a stressed -ём). Female patronymics end
in **-овна/-евна/-ична** (Елена Петровна, Елена Васильевна, Елена Кузьминична) and decline
exactly like Москва (Елены Петровны, Елене Петровне, Елену Петровну, Еленой Петровной).

### Which foreign personal names decline in Russian (pp.59-60)
Foreign **male** first names decline only if their Russian transcription ends in a consonant,
-а/-й, or a soft consonant (Джек→у Джека, Эрик→у Эрика, Абдалла→у Абдаллы); foreign **female**
first names decline only if ending in -а/-я/-ия/-ья (Кристина→у Кристины, София→у Софии). The
majority of non-Russian first names of either gender are simply **indeclinable** (Билли, Бетти,
Чарли, Лео, Клэр, Эдит never change form). For foreign **surnames**, only consonant-ending male
surnames decline, as ordinary nouns rather than adjectivally (Леннон→у Леннона, Эйнштейн→у
Эйнштейна).

### Which foreign geographic names decline (p.60)
Russians decline non-Russian place names (as ordinary nouns) only when the Russian transcription
ends in a final consonant (hard or soft), -й, -а, -я, -ья, or -ия (Лондон→в Лондоне, Китай→в
Китае, Темза→на/в Темзе, Венеция→в Венеции). Plural-only foreign geographic names (Альпы,
Гавайи, Канары) take the ordinary Plural Prepositional -ах/-ях (в Альпах, на Гавайях).

### The Russian personal pronoun system and the ты/вы register distinction (pp.61-63)
Russian personal pronouns: я/ты/он-она-оно (singular), мы/вы/они (plural), with **вы** doing
double duty as true plural "you" and as the polite/formal singular "you." The source devotes a
full page of explicit social guidance to when to use ты versus вы — the single highest-value
register-annotation content in this Part for this project's sociolinguistic-slang purposes:
- Strangers in public: adult↔child = ты (adult to child); adult↔teenager/young person = вы,ты
  (mixed, situational); adult↔adult = вы; young person↔young person = вы,ты; middle-aged
  adult↔elderly person = вы,ты (mixed)
- Familiar people: friends and relatives **always** use ты, regardless of the specific
  relationship
- At work: same status peers = вы,ты (situational); different status (lower→higher) = вы, with
  ты "used seldom" in that direction
- Education: kindergarten (child↔teacher) = вы,ty (mixed); school (schoolchild↔teacher) = вы,ты
  (mixed); college/university (student↔teacher) = вы, but student↔student = вы,ты
  (situational, tending toward ты among peers)
- **Children and teenagers use only ты to each other**, unconditionally
- **Using ты instead of вы to an unknown adult in public can be taken as an insult** — flagged
  explicitly by the source as a real pragmatic risk for a learner, not just a stylistic footnote
- Religious/natural-world address: **ты is used for God, nature, and animals**

### The pronoun-declension split: "characterizing" pronouns vs. "noun-replacing" pronouns (pp.63-64)
The source frames all Russian pronouns as falling into two functional groups: those used *as*
characterizing words modifying a noun (covered already in Parts 1-2 — possessive, demonstrative,
interrogative, etc.), and those that *replace* a noun/proper name outright — personal pronouns
он/она/оно/они/я/ты/мы/вы, the interrogative/relative кто/что, indefinite pronouns built on
кто/что, the reflexive себя, the nominative это, and generalizing words like всё/все. Part 5
covers this second, noun-replacing group.

### Declension of он/она/оно/они (pp.64-67)
он and оно share one full case paradigm (Gen. его/него, Dat. ему/нему, Acc. его/него, Instr.
им/ним, Prep. нём); она has its own (Gen. её/неё, Dat. ей/ней, Acc. её/неё, Instr. ей/ней,
Prep. ней); они covers the plural of all three (Gen. их/них, Dat. им/ним, Acc. их/них, Instr.
ими/ними, Prep. них). **A governing preposition adds an initial н-** to any of these oblique
forms (у него/его — "he/it has" vs. "at his place"; к ней/ей). These pronouns are used
identically for animate and inanimate referents (the "it" sense of он/она/оно is not a separate
form).

### Declension of я, ты, вы, мы — suppletive stems (pp.67-69)
я, ты, вы, and мы all have suppletive oblique-case stems unrelated to the Nominative: я→меня
(Gen./Acc.)/мне (Dat./Prep.)/мной (Instr.); ты→тебя/тебе/тобой; вы→вас/вам/вами; мы→нас/нам/
нами. Each of these pronouns' Genitive doubles as the standard "have" possessive construction
(У меня нет машины — "I have no car"; У тебя есть машина? — "Do you have a car?"; У вас есть
факс? — "Do you have a fax?"; У нас нет машины — "We don't have a car"), which the source flags
as a "special case" worth memorizing as a set phrase pattern.

### Declension of кто/что and their indefinite compounds (pp.69-72)
кто ("who," referring to people) and что ("what," referring to things/animals/actions) each
decline through all six cases with endings the source explicitly compares to the Masculine
adjectival declension pattern from Part 3 (кого, кому, кем, ком / чего, чему, чем, чём). The
fixed phrase **что это за** functions as an invariant equivalent of какой ("what kind of"),
used only in the Nominative regardless of the following noun's gender or number (Что это за
здание? / Что это за люди?). Indefinite compounds attach fixed particles to кто/что (also to
какой, чей, где, как, куда, когда, почему): **-то** marks an unknown-but-specific referent
(кто-то "someone" — notably always triggering Masculine agreement even for a known female
referent; что-то "something," triggering Neuter agreement); **-нибудь** marks an open,
yet-to-be-selected referent, used mainly in questions/imperatives/conditionals (кто-нибудь,
что-нибудь); **кое-** marks a deliberately unspecified referent and does not itself decline — a
governing preposition is inserted *between* кое- and the case-inflected pronoun (кое с кем
поговорить, "to talk to someone [in particular, unnamed]"). Only -то/-нибудь/кое- attach this
way; the pronoun's own case ending changes normally underneath the particle.

### The reflexive pronoun себя (p.72)
себя ("oneself") is used for every grammatical person and both numbers regardless of gender, and
**has no Nominative form** — it can only appear in oblique cases (Gen. у себя, Dat. к себе, Acc.
себя, Instr. с собой, Prep. о себе), corresponding in translation to "-self," a possessive +
"own," or sometimes nothing at all.

### The generalizing pronouns это and всё: fixed Neuter/3rd-singular agreement (pp.73-74)
это and всё, when functioning as a subject, always trigger **Neuter singular** agreement on a
past-tense verb (Это было интересно — "It was interesting") or **3rd-person singular**
agreement on a present/future verb (Это будет интересно), regardless of what they refer to. As
objects they decline through all six cases (это: этого, этому, это, этим, этом; всё: всего,
всему, всё, всем, всём).

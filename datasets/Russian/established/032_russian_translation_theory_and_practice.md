# Russian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Full-book extraction (Chapters 1–15)

**Source:** Edna Andrews and Elena A. Maksimova, *Russian Translation: Theory and Practice*
(London/New York: Routledge, 2010), complete book, pp. 1–181 (200-page PDF: front matter,
15 chapters, notes, references, index). Source file: `source_reference/languages/Russian/
Russian Learning Pack [up-to-date as of 2012]/Vol 1 of 3/69.Russian Translation Theory and
Practice.pdf`.

**Coverage note:** This is a translation-theory/practice textbook, not a grammar reference — most
of its bulk is source-text/target-text translation exercises (news articles, literary excerpts,
legal codes, medical texts) illustrating translation-process pedagogy (CAM/CAM2 communicative-act
models, Skopos theory, register/discourse theory), not systematic vocabulary/paradigm presentation.
Per dispatch instructions, this extraction prioritizes idiom/collocation/register-shift content —
the material genuinely new relative to prior Russian `established/` grammar-reference extractions
(Timberlake, Basic Modern Russian Grammar, Colloquial Russian 2, Using Russian Vocabulary, etc.).
Skipped as low-yield for this project: the bulk of the legal-code translation exercises (Ch. 10),
scientific/medical vocabulary lists (Ch. 11, 14) beyond a few register-illustrating items, and
literary/poetry translation-comparison exercises (Ch. 3, 6) beyond their phonological/versification
grammar points — these are ST/TT translation drills, not new lexical or grammatical data about
Russian itself. Genuinely new/high-value content came from: Ch. 4 (emotional-register discourse
norms), Ch. 7–8 (word-formation/prefix semantics and figurative-extension slang), Ch. 9 (the
book's single richest chapter: proverbs/sayings/collocations, the full 28-item discourse-particle
inventory, and the special-terminology triad проза/поговорка/пословица), and Ch. 13 (computer/
internet jargon formation mechanisms — clipping, calquing, English-mishearing-derived slang).

This source has a clean, genuine text layer (`pdftotext -layout` produced fully correct Cyrillic
throughout — verified against 40+ known word/gloss pairs across widely separated pages) with
**no font-substitution cipher of any kind** — none of the 6 corruption variants catalogued
elsewhere in this project's Russian corpus were present here. No vision-reading was required;
`Vision Reading Confidence` is `n/a` throughout.

**Morpheme breakdown.** Russian is fusional, not agglutinative (per this project's Russian
typology note), so individual word-forms in this book generally don't require a morpheme-by-
morpheme breakdown the way an agglutinative language's forms would. The one structural pattern
worth flagging explicitly, because it recurs constantly across the vocabulary items above, is the
book's own **(prefix[es]) + ROOT + (suffix[es]) + DESINENCE** model (Ch. 7) and its corollary that
in many derived forms — especially the colloquial/slang register-ladder sets above (напиться →
набраться → наклюкаться → нажраться; вырвать/вытащить/выдернуть; выделываться/выпендриваться/
выкобениваться) — **the prefix, not the root, carries the primary distinguishing semantic and
register weight**, with near-synonymous roots differing mainly in vulgarity/register tier rather
than denotation. This is a mechanism (not a single worked example) that this project's later
mechanics-analysis phase may want to model explicitly for Russian slang formation: prefix-driven
register escalation on a semantically stable root.

## Grammar points

### Communicative Act Model (CAM) and CAM2 (Ch. 1–2, 4–6)

The book's central theoretical framework: any speech act must be analyzed along six simultaneous,
dynamically-weighted factors — author, audience, context, code, message, channel (after Jakobson's
speech-act model). CAM2 doubles all six factors to model *cultural transposition* between a source
culture and target culture during translation, not just a static ST→TT code change. Not Russian
grammar per se, but functions throughout the book as the interpretive lens for why a given register/
idiom/collocation choice matters — e.g. code doubling alone yields literal/ST-biased translation,
while doubling all six factors yields free/TT-biased translation. Five points on the literal↔free
continuum are named: literal, faithful, balanced, idiomizing, free (Hervey and Higgins 2002).

### Verbal government and case usage (Ch. 2)

Russian verbs each govern specific case/preposition combinations that often don't map onto English
argument structure at all (e.g. говорить кому/с кем/о ком/о чём — "talk to/with/about" all require
different Russian cases/prepositions for what English handles with one preposition family).
Instrumental vs. prepositional-instrumental distinguishes manner-only vs. manner-plus-accompaniment
(Она его встретила улыбкой "greeted him with [only] a smile" vs. с улыбкой "with a smile [and
possibly words too]").

### Impersonal constructions (Ch. 2)

A major category of Contemporary Standard Russian sentence structure: logical subject (if present)
stands in the dative case, verb agrees in neuter 3rd-person singular. Five productive sub-types
given: age expressions (Ему пятнадцать лет), reflexive-verb "powerlessness" constructions (Мне не
спалось "I couldn't sleep" vs. agentive Я не спал(а) "I didn't sleep"), weather/temperature
perception (Дует, Дождит, Морозит, Мне холодно), psychological/physiological states (Нам грустно,
Меня знобит, Лену тошнит, У нее заложило уши — a large, idiomatically rich set), and
infinitive-based polite questions/commands (Вам помочь?, Заказать тебе такси?). This
psych/physiological-state impersonal-dative set is a productive template worth noting for
conlang slang synthesis — the "dative experiencer + impersonal predicate" pattern is how CSR
natively encodes involuntary bodily/emotional states, a structural slot a synthesized slang
register could plausibly extend non-standardly.

### Conjunctions и / а / но (Ch. 2)

Russian splits English "and"/"but" three ways: и (pure addition, most integrating), а (contrastive-
but-linking, sets up paired opposites: Это белое, а это чёрное), но (maximal, independent-clause
contrast). Choice of а vs. но vs. и in an otherwise identical sentence changes its rhetorical
force/tone, not just its truth-conditional content — directly relevant to how a slang register
might lean on one conjunction over another for stylistic color.

### Prefix and suffix inventory of derivational morphology (Ch. 7)

The book gives a full 24-item table of native Slavic verbal/nominal prefixes with literal meanings
(в(о)- "in(to)", вз-/воз-/вс-/вос- "upward", вы- "out of", до- "up to", за- "behind/begin", из(о)-/
ис(о)- "from inside out", на- "on(to)", над- "above", не- "not", о/об(о)- "around", (о)без-/(о)бес-
"without", от(о)- "away from", па- "step", пере-/пре- "re-/across", по- "set off/for a while",
под(о)- "under", пол(у)- "half", пра- "original/great-", пред- "pre-", при- "arrive/add on", про-
"through", раз-/рас- "in multiple directions/un-", с(о)- "together/co-", у- "leaving/pulling"), plus
non-Slavic technical prefixes (анти-, авто-, гипер-, квази-, контр-, макро-, микро-, псевдо-,
супер-, ультра-, экс-). Also notes the count of Russian derivational suffixes: 50 agentive
noun/adjective forms, 14 non-agentive noun forms, 14 non-agentive adjective forms (full listing
deferred to Vinogradov 1960 and the authors' own prior published work). This 24-prefix + suffix-
count inventory is the single most systematic word-formation reference point in the book and is
directly usable as a productive-affix palette for a Russian-derived conlang slang layer.

### Verbal aspect (Ch. 7)

Perfective/imperfective (СВ/НСВ) is a viewpoint category, not primarily a tense category — all
present-tense forms are НСВ; only past/future forms choose aspect. Register-relevant notes: aspect
must generally match between question and answer (switching aspect in an answer is a marked,
"powerful" move, often used to flatly deny an allegation); L2 speakers systematically underuse the
imperfective; imperative-mood aspect choice can swing from very polite to very rude depending on
context — the same surface aspectual form is not register-neutral.

### Grammatical gender, epicene nouns, and gender shift (Ch. 2, 7)

Three genders (masc./neut./fem.) map onto three declension classes, with epicene nouns (судья
"judge," убийца "murderer," тихоня, хапуга, соня, тупица) taking either agreement gender — though
masculine agreement is sometimes preferred even for female referents (Ирина Ивановна – хороший
судья). Grammatical gender can also shift diachronically (лебедь f.→m.) or synchronically via
word-formation (позор m. → позорище n.) — directly relevant background for how a slang/argot
register might exploit gender-reassignment of borrowed or extended forms.

### Synonymy, homonymy, polysemy (Ch. 8)

Three traditional categories distinguished: synonyms (глупый/дурацкий "stupid"), homonyms (лук
"onion"/"bow" — note these disambiguate by declension pattern, e.g. gen.sg. луку only = onion),
polysemes (наговорить spanning "talk a lot/slander/record onto tape"). The authors argue polysemy
is the more realistic general model of lexical meaning than a clean literal/figurative split, and
that Russian's word-formative system (root + prefix/suffix combinations) generates far more of
this polysemous fan-out than English's more additive, one-lexeme-per-concept borrowing pattern.

### Proverb vs. saying vs. set expression: пословица / поговорка / фразеологизм (Ch. 9)

The single most valuable terminological/typological point in the book for this project. Russian
draws a three-way distinction English collapses into "proverb/saying":
- **пословица** — a complete, self-contained, instructive statement with a moral (two-part
  structure per Dal': situation + judgment/maxim).
- **поговорка** — a short, often rhymed, figurative *fragment* lacking the instructive/complete-
  statement structure; per Dal', often literally the truncated first half of a corresponding
  пословица (e.g. the пословица "Легко чужими руками жар загребать" reduces in everyday speech to
  the поговорка "Чужими руками жар загребать").
- **устойчивое выражение / фразеологизм / крылатое выражение** — general cover terms for any set
  expression/collocation not fitting the above two categories cleanly.
Being able to *identify* that a phrase is a set expression at all is explicitly flagged by the
authors as more important than correctly sub-classifying it — misidentifying a set phrase as literal
language is the single most translation-damaging error this chapter warns against. Directly
applicable to a slang-synthesis project: any synthesized proverb/saying layer for a conlang should
distinguish complete-aphorism forms from clipped-fragment forms, since Russian shows these are a
live, productive continuum (fragments can be re-expanded, or full forms clipped, without changing
recognizability).

### Discourse markers/particles as a grammatical category (Ch. 9)

Russian particles (частицы) are non-declining, sometimes unstressed, may attach to virtually any
part of the sentence or stand alone, and are treated in the official Academy Grammar (Vinogradov
1960) as their own grammatical category distinct from ordinary lexemes. The book's 28-particle
worked inventory (see Vocabulary table) is presented as directly discourse-critical: particles like
мол/дескать mark reported/hedged speech; ведь marks shared-knowledge appeal; -ка softens an
imperative into a casual suggestion; уж(е) and всё-таки mark concessive emphasis. This is a
structural slot (particle system) that a synthesized Russian-derived slang register could plausibly
extend with novel or shifted particles, following the existing non-declining/flexible-attachment
template.

### Register theory: Halliday/House field-tenor-mode model (Ch. 9)

Adopted from Halliday (revised by House): **field** (topic/social action), **tenor** (relationship
between addresser/addressee), **mode** (channel: oral/written, degree of interactivity). The book
notes this technical sense of "register" is compatible with, but narrower than, sociolinguistics'
everyday sense of register as "level of language" (colloquial/literary/sub-standard/slang, etc.) —
worth flagging since this project's own Usage Tier taxonomy operates in that everyday sense.

### Emotional-register discourse norms: what NOT to over-translate (Ch. 4)

A genuinely new register-mapping point not found in this project's prior Russian extractions:
Russian speech is described as *more conservative* than English in routine emotional expression,
with specific documented mismatches:
1. спасибо is used less frequently than English "thank you" — L1-English speakers who translate
   English politeness-frequency directly into Russian sound conspicuously over-polite.
2. любить ("to love"/"to like," depending on complement type — noun/pronoun object = "love," verb
   complement = "like") is not used with English "I love you"'s casual frequency; the authors note
   younger Russians, influenced by English-language pop culture, are shifting toward higher-
   frequency usage than speakers 10+ years older — an attested real-time register change in progress.
3. гордиться ("to be proud") is used even more sparingly than любить — proportionately much higher
   threshold than English "proud of you."
4. English "I'm so/very glad to see you" typically drops the intensifying очень in the Russian
   equivalent (Я рад(а) тебя видеть, not *Я очень рад(а)...).
5. English "sorry" fragments into entirely different Russian forms by context, and жаль/жалко
   specifically is flagged as a *false-friend trap* — it does NOT mean "I'm sorry" in the English
   condolence sense (its actual sense is closer to "that's too bad"/"I feel for you," and can sound
   condescending if misapplied to condolences).
6. English "excited" has no true generic Russian equivalent — возбуждать/возбудиться and their
   derived forms carry a specifically *sexual or out-of-control* connotation in Russian and must
   never be used for generic enthusiasm (e.g. excitement about a new job); the authors give five
   alternative idiomatic renderings (Здорово, что...; Поздравляю, что...; Молодец, что...; Слава
   Богу, что...) that avoid the false-friend trap.
This whole cluster is a ready-made register-mismatch dataset for modeling how an emotionally
"louder" contact language (e.g. English slang influence) might visibly shift a naturally more
reserved base language's emotional-expression norms over time — precisely the kind of mechanism
this project's slang-synthesis phase is trying to model.

### Ирония and sarcasm in Russian media style (Ch. 4)

Flagged as a genre-level register difference from English-language journalism: the authors note
Russian print/online media shows markedly more overt irony and sarcasm than comparable English-
language reporting, illustrated via a Kommersant political article whose surface tone is openly
mocking of its subject while remaining "straight" reportage in form.

### Transliteration systems (Ch. 3)

Three parallel transliteration systems given in full letter-by-letter table form: System I (popular
press, e.g. Tchaikovsky, Khrushchev spellings), System II (US Library of Congress/bibliographic),
System III (Slavic-studies scholarly standard, one symbol per Cyrillic letter except щ→šč). Grammar/
convention point rather than vocabulary — relevant chiefly for how proper nouns/toponyms get
rendered inconsistently across English-language sources citing the same Russian original.

### Pre-1917 orthography (Ch. 3)

The 1917 orthographic reform eliminated jat' and the letter i, and drastically reduced word-final
hard-sign (ъ) usage. Post-Soviet Russian has seen a stylistic revival of word-final ъ in some
branding/publishing contexts (e.g. the newspaper Коммерсантъ deliberately keeps the old spelling as
a stylistic/nostalgic marker) — a live example of an archaic orthographic feature being
re-purposed as a register/branding signal rather than restored as standard usage.

### Legal register vocabulary structure (Ch. 10)

Russian legal Russian favors: heavy participial/deverbal-noun density (-ость, -ие suffixed
nominalizations), long reflexive (-ся) verb chains, and a small closed set of five prepositional-
phrase categories used to structure legal/bureaucratic/scientific prose specifically: cause-effect
(в связи с, в результате, ввиду), goal-oriented (в целях, с целью), basis-of-action (на основании,
в соответствии с), temporal (в течение, в ходе, по окончании), and conditional (при, в случае). This
five-way prepositional-phrase taxonomy recurs verbatim in the book's treatment of scientific/
scholarly prose (Ch. 11) as well — it is presented as a genre-marking register feature of "serious/
official" written Russian generally, not legal Russian specifically.

### Scientific/scholarly parenthetical expressions (Ch. 11)

A 50-item table of Russian parenthetical hedges/connectives typical of academic register is given
(несомненно, безусловно; на самом деле, фактически; в конце концов; отчасти, в какой-то мере; к
сожалению; по-моему; собственно говоря; и т.д.) — useful as a formal-register counterweight to
the colloquial-particle inventory in Ch. 9; several items overlap in function with the Ch. 9
particles but are markedly more formal/written in register (e.g. фактически vs. же).

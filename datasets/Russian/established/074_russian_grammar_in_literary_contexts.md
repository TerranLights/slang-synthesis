## Grammar points (Unit 1 — terse, redundant with prior established/ content)

### General Introduction: agreement as a system
Russian is a highly inflected language: adjectives/pronouns agree with nouns in case, gender, and
number; verbs agree with subjects in person/number (nonpast) or gender/number (past). Standard
material, p. 2–3.

### Gender
Three genders (masculine/feminine/neuter), assigned primarily by noun ending, with a short list of
common exceptions (кофе is masculine; человек/друг/врач/доктор take masculine agreement even for
women; сирота/плакса/пьяница/убийца are common-gender). p. 6–7. Fully standard.

### Hard and soft endings
21 consonants split into always-hard (ж, ш, ц), always-soft (й, ч, щ), and context-dependent
(all others, determined by the following letter). p. 7–8. Standard.

### The Case System
Six cases (nominative, accusative, genitive, dative, prepositional, instrumental), with the
question-word shorthand Russians use to name each case (кто/что, кого/что, кого/чего, кому/чему,
о ком/о чём, кем/чем). p. 8–9. Standard.

### The Verbal System
Three tenses × two aspects (imperfective/perfective), six-cell paradigm table (past/nonpast ×
imperfective/perfective) with писать/написать as the model verb; imperfective future is the one
compound tense (будет + infinitive). p. 10. Standard.

### The Spelling Rules
Three orthographic spelling rules every Russian textbook states: (1) after г к х ж ч ш щ, never
write ы, write и; (2) after г к х ж ч ш щ ц, never write ю/я, write у/а; (3) after ж ч ш щ ц, never
write unstressed о, write е. Noted exceptions for loanwords (шоколад, брошюра, шофёр, парашют).
p. 11. Standard — matches prior established/ sources verbatim in substance.

## Register/literary findings (the non-redundant content)

### "Russian Saying" box — a reusable register-commentary format
Each unit opens or closes its grammar parts with boxed material headed **RUSSIAN** (vertical
sidebar) / **Saying**, presenting one proverb with a literal and idiomatic English translation.
Unit 1's example: **Лихá бедá — начáло** ("A beginning is a terrible misfortune" / "The beginning
is the hardest part") — a proverb about difficulty, illustrating a fixed archaic-flavored lexical
item (лихой "fierce/bold/(dated) terrible") outside its usual modern adjectival sense. p. 10.

### ТЕКСТ 1а — Khlebnikov's «Заклятие смехом» ("Incantation by Laughter", 1910)
A deliberately nonsensical Futurist poem built entirely on derivational play around the root
смех/смей- ("laughter"/"to laugh"), coining forms like смехачи, смеяльно, надсмеяльных, смеюнчики
that are not real dictionary words but are constructed with real Russian derivational morphology
(diminutive/verbal/adjectival suffixes stacked onto the смех/смей root). The book uses this
specifically to teach students to identify part-of-speech from inflectional shape alone, since the
"words" carry no independent lexical meaning — a clean pedagogical illustration of how much
grammatical information Russian morphology encodes independent of a real lexicon. Presented as
excerpt/paraphrase per copyright discipline; full poem is short (12 lines) and public-domain-era
(1910) but paraphrased here regardless.

### ТЕКСТ 1б — opening of Tolstoy's *Anna Karenina* (1877)
The famous "Все счастливые семьи похожи друг на друга..." opening paragraph, given with footnoted
English glosses for key words (счастливый "happy", несчастливый "unhappy", по-своему "in its own
way", смешалось "was confused/mixed up", узнала "found out", в связи "affair", объявила
"announced", положение "situation", члены семьи "members of the family", домочадцы "servants/
household members", сожительство "cohabitation", случайно сошедшиеся люди "people who have come
together randomly"). The book's own post-reading task draws attention to the narrator's repetition
of "family," "home," and "servants" as a stylistic/thematic device — the closest thing to explicit
literary-register commentary in this unit, treating repetition itself as meaningful register choice
rather than mere redundancy.

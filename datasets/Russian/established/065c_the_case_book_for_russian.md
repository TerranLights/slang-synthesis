## Grammar points — Dative case (pp. 82–109)

The dative network has three branches: **A RECEIVER** (the classic indirect object — passive,
absorbing something), **AN EXPERIENCER** (something happens *to* the dative item, without the
dative item necessarily doing anything), and **A COMPETITOR** (relatively more active — exerting
equal or superior strength/force in relation to something else). The book's own framing device: a
Mad Libs analogy — English speakers will fill in "him"/"her" for a dative-shaped slot regardless
of whether the verb involves genuine transfer (give), pure experience (please), or a more forceful
relation (impress), because English doesn't morphologically distinguish these; Russian's case
system does distinguish them, but all three still fall under one case because they share a common
thread: **the item is a target that force/influence acts upon, without that item itself being the
primary agent of an action** (contrast with the accusative's *directly acted-upon* patient, or the
instrumental's *conduit* role).

### A Receiver — the indirect object

1. **The indirect object proper** — the classic "give X to Y" pattern; grammatical subject +
   accusative direct object + dative indirect object/receiver, structurally parallel to English
   "give the music to Dorian" / "give Dorian the music." Verbs of giving are the prototype.
   Representative verbs (of a longer boxed list, p. 85): *отправить/отправлять* "send, dispatch,"
   *передать/передавать* "pass, convey," *показать/показывать* "show," *предложить/предлагать*
   "offer," *подарить/дарить* "give as a gift," *вручить/вручать* "hand over, present,"
   *раздать/раздавать* "hand out, distribute." The book notes the sentence structure generalizes
   even to non-literal "gifts" (opportunities, abstract benefits): *дали шанс* "they gave [him] a
   chance," and to more fictive transfers — status/labels formally granted to something
   (*предложить статус наблюдателя* "propose observer status," "grant [someone] the right to
   become king").
2. **Indirect objects without direct objects** — many verbs of communication/signaling take *only*
   a dative receiver, with no accusative direct object at all, because the "signal" itself is
   implicit rather than named as a separate noun. Split into two groups: verbs of **talking**
   (representative sample of a longer boxed list, p. 87: *сказать/говорить* "say to," *звать*
   "call," *льстить/польстить* "flatter," *отвечать/ответить* "answer," *молиться/помолиться*
   "pray," *грозить/погрозить* "threaten," *протестовать* "protest") and verbs of **other
   signals** (p. 88: *писать/написать* "write," *махать/помахать* "wave," *кивать/кивнуть* "nod,"
   *улыбаться/улыбнуться* "smile," *радировать* "radio," *телеграфировать* "telegraph"). A small
   number of verbs (e.g. *благодарить* "thank") govern an accusative direct object *and* a dative
   receiver of the reason (thanks given "for" something takes a separate preposition).
3. **Giving the self** — the dative receiver of a purely reflexive presentation: the subject
   simply presents/reveals itself to a dative receiver, with no physical transfer at all. Verbs of
   appearance/manifestation (of a longer boxed list, p. 89): *явиться/являться* "present oneself
   to," *представиться/представляться* "introduce oneself," *показаться/казаться* "seem, appear
   to," *присниться* "appear in a dream to," *достаться* "fall to one's lot, be inherited by."

### An Experiencer — five sub-senses

1. **Benefit** — something good happens to/for a dative item, without necessarily any effort from
   it. Representative verbs (of a longer boxed list, pp. 90–91): *помочь/помогать* "help,"
   *позволить/позволять* "allow, permit," *покровительствовать* "patronize, support,"
   *сочувствовать* "sympathize," *служить* "serve," *нравиться/понравиться* "please, be liked
   by." **The нравиться construction is flagged as the single most important "reversed polarity"
   pattern in this branch**: Russian expresses "I like X" as *X нравится мне* (lit. "X pleases
   me-DAT") — the liked item is the grammatical subject/nominative, and the person doing the
   liking is dative. This inverts the English subject/object mapping and is a frequent source of
   learner error precisely because the surface English translation ("I like X") suggests the
   wrong case assignment.
2. **Harm** — the negative mirror of benefit; a rich verb family (of a longer boxed list, p. 93):
   *возражать/возразить* "oppose, object," *завидовать* "envy," *грозить/погрозить* "threaten,"
   *изменять/изменить* "betray" (also "to change," context-dependent), *вредить/навредить* "harm,"
   *мстить/отомстить* "take revenge," *противостоять* "oppose, resist," *противоречить*
   "contradict." The book stresses that harm expressed this way is idiomatic — a literal English
   translation often sounds stilted, but the underlying logic is the same "something happens to a
   dative target" pattern as benefit.
3. **Having and needing** — the dative marks a possessor in existential "have" constructions
   (contrast with the instrumental's *владеть/обладать* "possess by means of," 065's A Means 9):
   *у меня есть* "I have" (literally "at me there is/exists," locative-adjacent construction) uses
   a dative-adjacent possessor logic; need/sufficiency verbs take dative: *принадлежать* "belong
   to," *требоваться/потребоваться* "be necessary/required," *хватать/хватить* "be sufficient,"
   *свойственно* "characteristic of," *известно* "known to," *нужно/нужен* "needed" (with the
   thing needed as nominative subject and the needer as dative: *мне нужна помощь* "I need help,"
   lit. "help is needed to me").
4. **Age, environment, and emotions** — a major grammar point: **Russian states age using a
   dative-experiencer construction with no verb**: *Ван Клайберну было двадцать три года* "Van
   Cliburn was twenty-three years old" (lit. "to Van Cliburn was twenty-three years"); the person
   whose age is stated is dative, and the number-of-years phrase is the grammatical
   "subject"-like nominative/genitive-governed numeral phrase. The same impersonal-dative pattern
   covers ambient physical/emotional states: *мне холодно* "I'm cold" (lit. "to me [it is]
   cold"), *им стало тесновато* "they began to feel cramped."
5. **Modal meanings** — impersonal dative + predicate adverb expresses difficulty/ease/
   obligation/desire, with an infinitive completing the thought: *мне трудно/легко* "it's hard/
   easy for me," *тебе надо идти* "you need to go," *ему нельзя* "he's not allowed to/it's not
   possible for him." This impersonal-dative-plus-predicate-adverb pattern is one of Russian's
   most productive modal-meaning constructions and covers obligation, permission, ability, and
   desire uniformly by swapping the predicate adverb.

Two further notes tucked into this section: **victims and beneficiaries** — the dative can mark
who benefits or suffers from a change of state even when the change itself is described with an
unrelated main verb (*у некоторых эмигрантов жизнь пошла плохо* "some emigrants' lives went
badly" — the emigrants are dative, coded as affected parties even though "went badly" doesn't
inherently require a dative argument); and **submission to a greater force** (Dative: A Competitor
2, below) shares vocabulary with harm but implies a power differential rather than mere negative
effect.

### A Competitor — three/four sub-senses, matching or exceeding force

1. **Matching forces** — the dative names an item that is comparably matched (equal, harmonious,
   or corresponding) to something else, without either side dominating; the dative here is often
   interchangeable with the nominative in meaning but implies a two-party symmetrical relationship
   rather than a simple attribute. Representative words (of a longer boxed list, p. 102):
   *равный* "equal to," *соответствовать* "correspond to, be appropriate," *подобный* "similar
   to," *идентичный* "identical to," *противоположный* "opposite of," *чуждый* "alien to,"
   *предшествовать* "precede."
2. **Submission to a greater force** — the dative names the superior force to which something
   yields/submits. Representative words (of a longer boxed list, p. 103): *подчиняться/
   подчиниться* "submit, be subordinate to," *поддаваться/поддаться* "yield to," *подвергаться/
   подвергнуться* "undergo, be subject to," *уступать/уступить* "yield, concede," *верить/
   поверить* "trust, believe [a person]" (contrast with 065b's *верить в* + accusative "believe in
   [a concept]" — this pair is the clearest documented example in the book of the same root verb
   selecting a different case for a different sense), *служить* "worship, serve," *следовать/
   последовать* "follow."
3. **к 'to, toward'** — the dative-governing preposition that marks a human or animate destination
   of literal or figurative motion, in explicit, systematic contrast with the accusative's
   destination prepositions: **all destinations that are a person require к + dative** (*пошла к
   врачу* "went to the doctor"), whereas non-human destinations use в or на + accusative
   (*пошла в школу* "went to school," *пошла на почту* "went to the post office"). This is a clean,
   teachable discriminator rule (human goal ⇒ к + dative; place goal ⇒ в/на + accusative) that a
   pure paradigm-table grammar would typically not foreground as a unified generalization. к also
   extends to abstract goals/deadlines (approaching a time: *к одиннадцати часам* "toward/by
   eleven o'clock") and to purpose framed as an approach toward a state.
4. **по 'along, according to'** — the other dative-governing preposition covered here, used for a
   path an item follows (literally, as with a wire signal traveling *along* a line, or
   figuratively, "according to" a body of information/data/regulation): *по информации* "according
   to [our] information," *по официальным данным* "according to official data," *по мнению
   экспертов* "in the opinion of experts." Also used distributively ("X each," "one per Y":
   *расселить по гостиничным номерам* "put up one per hotel room"). A cluster of frozen
   idioms built on this же root: *само по себе* "in itself," *по-своему* "in one's own way,"
   *каждый по-своему* "each in their own way."

The Epilogue explicitly contrasts the dative with the accusative one more time: the accusative
treats its object as directly, forcefully acted upon (an unambiguous patient), while the dative
frames its item as an interested party — a target of benefit, harm, or comparison rather than raw
force. The book notes Russian treats atmospheric/temporal/emotional experience as *happening to* a
dative party rather than being generated by an agentival subject (age, ambient cold, obligation —
all dative-experiencer, "impersonal" from an English-speaker's perspective), and that the dative
skews toward negative/harm vocabulary more than benefit vocabulary in its own word inventories.

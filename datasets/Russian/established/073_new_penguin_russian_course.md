standard 30-lesson beginner course (six-case fusional grammar, aspect, ты/вы register) whose scope
heavily overlaps with ~24 already-extracted Russian sources in this project, several of them
full beginner courses of the same type (*Colloquial Russian 2*, *Teach Yourself Russian*, *Survival

Russian Grammar and Verbs*). Per `00_Reference_Extraction_Spec.md`'s non-redundant-supplement
pattern: standard grammar-point restatements (case endings, verb conjugation patterns, pronoun
paradigms) are **not** re-tabulated here unless this book presents them more fully or differently
than prior sources; only genuinely new/deeper content is kept. This file's own genuinely
non-redundant finds: (1) the book's systematic phonetic-transcription convention and syllable-based
reading method (not seen elsewhere in this corpus in this much procedural detail); (2) a full
handwritten-cursive-letterform table (Lesson 2.1) — no prior Russian source in this corpus captures
Russian school cursive letterforms; (3) a curated post-Soviet Anglicism/loanword pronunciation list

nouns (useful cross-check pairs). Standard alphabet-teaching vocabulary (nouns like чай, спасибо,
метро) is skipped as fully redundant with every other beginner course already extracted.

No handwritten marginalia found in this scan (clean library/publisher copy, no annotations).

**Morpheme breakdown.**

> **человеконенавистничество** = человек (person) + о (linking vowel) + не (negation) + навист-
> (root shared with ненавидеть, "to hate") + -ничество (abstract-noun-forming suffix, cf.
> -ство/-ичество family) — a fully transparent compound the book itself uses only as a
> pronunciation tongue-twister, not as taught vocabulary; illustrative of Russian's compounding
> capacity even though the language is otherwise fusional rather than agglutinative.

## Grammar points

### Phonetic transcription and syllable-based reading method (1.1–1.4, pp. 1–9)

The book teaches all 33 Cyrillic letters in four groups by visual/phonetic difficulty (familiar
Latin-shaped letters first, then false-friend look-alikes, then unfamiliar-but-simple sounds,
finally the eight hardest: ж ц ч ш щ ъ ы ь). Its own bracketed transcription system marks stress
with an acute accent and represents the soft sign as a raised ʸ (e.g. **соль** → `[solʸ]`) rather
than the IPA or Library-of-Congress conventions used elsewhere in this corpus — worth flagging as a
book-specific convention if any future cross-source vocabulary comparison needs to normalize
transcriptions. Reading is taught syllable-by-syllable (a syllable = a vowel plus any preceding
consonants), explicitly recommended as a decoding strategy for beginners (p. 6).

### Russian Cyrillic history (1.1, p. 1)

Brief note: the Cyrillic alphabet is attributed to a 9th-century (~AD 863) Macedonian monk who
spoke a South Slavonic language related to Russian and knew Greek; canonized as Saint Cyril. Framed
as background color rather than a grammar point, included here since no other source in this
corpus's established/ files carries this specific historical attribution.

### Transliteration vs. transcription, and the book's own transliteration choice (1.5(2), pp. 10–11)

Distinguishes *transliteration* (letter-for-letter Roman representation, e.g. for library
cataloguing, following ALA-LC/Library of Congress conventions) from *transcription* (representing
actual pronunciation). The book explicitly transliterates its own proper names using the
"traditional/familiar" spelling only where that's much more established (e.g. "Yalta" not "Ialta"
for Ялта) — otherwise following Library of Congress form strictly (e.g. "Vania" not "Vanya"). Worth
noting as a documented editorial choice distinct from the transcription convention above.

### Russian cursive/handwriting letterforms (2.1, pp. 12–17)

Full alphabetical table of Russian school-taught cursive (письменный) forms for all 33 letters,
each illustrated with a short example word. Notable individual points: the cursive л and м require
an initial short downstroke that is not optional (otherwise л/м become visually indistinguishable
from и/ш-family strokes); an "old-fashioned" alternate cursive д form (∂-shaped) is noted as still
sometimes seen. **This is the only source in this project's Russian corpus so far to capture the
handwritten letterform system in this much depth** — prior sources have focused exclusively on
printed/typeset Cyrillic.

### Vowel reduction, softness, and devoicing (2.2–2.12, pp. 18–22)

Systematic treatment of three separate phonological phenomena, each illustrated with real proper
names/loanwords as memorable minimal-contrast examples (a pedagogical device not seen used this
extensively elsewhere in this corpus):
- **Softness**: a consonant followed by е/ё/и/ю/я or ь is pronounced with a simultaneous [y]
  glide — contrasted via **совет** [s-a-v'-é-t] (6 sounds) vs. the English loan-rendering "soviet"
  (6 letters, different syllabification).
- **Word-final devoicing**: voiced consonants devoice at word end or before a voiceless consonant —
  illustrated via **Киев** → [kee-éf] (в→f), **водка** → [vót-ka] (д→t before к), **Брежнев** →
  [bryézh-nyef] (в→f word-finally).
- **Exceptional/irregular pronunciations** of specific high-frequency words that don't follow the
  regular spelling-to-sound rules: **что** [shto] not [chto]; **пожалуйста** [pa-zhál-sta] (elides
  the уй entirely); **здравствуйте** [zdrá-stvooy-tye] (elides the first в); **его** [yevó] (г → v);
  **сегодня** [sve-vód-nya] (г → v); **радио** [rá-dee-o] (о not reduced to а, because of the
  word's foreign origin, an exception to the normal unstressed-o-reduction rule).
- **Recent loanwords retain [э]** rather than undergoing the native palatalization rule for e:
  **теннис**, **компьютер** (see Vocabulary table) are pronounced with a hard consonant + [э],
  not the softened [tye]/[pye] a native-vocabulary spelling would otherwise predict.

# Russian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Units 12–14 + Grammar Reference Appendix (second half of book)

**Source:** Svetlana Le Fleming and Susan E. Kay, *Colloquial Russian 2: The Next Step in Language
Learning* (Routledge, first published 2003; this PDF is the 2006 Taylor & Francis e-Library
edition), printed pages 172–341 (PDF pages 181–353). Covers the back half of Units 9–14 (Unit 9
"Demography" continuation, Unit 10 "Obrazovanie"/Education, Unit 11 "Rossijskoe obwestvo"/Russian
Society, Unit 12 "Zdravooxranenie"/Health Service, Unit 13 "Vybory v Dumu"/Duma Elections, Unit 14
"Internet"), the "Grammar reference" appendix (pp. 271–287), and the front matter of "Key to
exercises" / bilingual glossaries (pp. 289–341, treated as low-value back matter, see note below). A
sibling subagent covers PDF pages 1–180 (`established/010_...md`) — this file does not duplicate
that range.

**Coverage note:** This is the *second* Russian coursebook extracted (after *Colloquial Russian*
part 1, `established/00x_...`); the non-redundant-supplement pattern applies loosely since this is a
different, more advanced volume by the same authors covering distinct topical units (demography,
education policy, health-service reform, Duma elections, the Russian internet circa 2002–2003) not
covered by a beginner's course — so essentially all unit-level vocabulary and grammar in the
assigned range is new, not redundant. What *was* deliberately skipped as low-value: (1) the "Key to
exercises" answer-key section (pp. 289–307), which only restates vocabulary/grammar already captured
from the units' own dialogues/exercises; (2) the cumulative English–Russian and Russian–English
bilingual glossaries (pp. 309–341), which aggregate vocabulary from the *entire* book (both halves)
and are overwhelmingly redundant with the unit-level vocabulary boxes already extracted here and in
the sibling file — spot-checked and no systematic full extraction attempted given the redundancy and
two-column layout risk; (3) the "Grammar index" (p. 343), a page-number index only, no content.

**PDF-extraction gotcha (worth flagging for any future Russian source from this same "Russian
Learning Pack"):** This PDF has a genuine text layer, but it uses a **fixed 1:1 font-substitution
cipher for Cyrillic** — `pdftotext` extracts Cyrillic text as a consistent pseudo-transliteration
using Latin letters and a few symbol substitutions (e.g. `h`→ч, `w`→ш, `]`→щ, `[`→ю, `q`→я, `'`→ж,
`;`→ь (soft sign), `#`→ъ (hard sign), `\`→э, stress marks as a combining acute `´`/U+0301 after the
stressed vowel). This was decoded and verified against 30+ known word/gloss pairs across widely
separated pages (matches the pattern flagged in `00_Reference_Extraction_Spec.md`'s PDF-extraction-
gotchas section). Curiously, **some page ranges in this same PDF already contain properly-encoded
Cyrillic** (e.g. the health-service and Duma-elections vocabulary boxes came out as correct Cyrillic
directly), while others needed the cipher decode — the substitution is not uniform across the whole
file, so any future extraction from this PDF should verify per page-range rather than assuming one
behavior throughout. No vision-reading was needed (real text layer throughout); no marginalia issue
applies (not a scanned source).

## Grammar points

### Word building: root families with consonant alternation (Demography unit, p. 176–182)

Following the book's recurring "word building" pedagogy, this unit walks through several root
families to help learners recognize related words despite consonant alternation triggered by
historical sound changes:
- **rod-** 'birth, tribe, nature' (d alternates with ж or жд): род 'family/tribe/type', ро́ды
  'childbirth', наро́д 'people', поро́да 'breed', ро́дина 'native land', родня́ 'relatives', роди́тель
  'parent', ро́дственник 'relative', родство́ 'relationship', рожда́емость 'birthrate', рожде́ние
  'birth', родно́й 'native', родово́й 'ancestral', роди́ть 'to give birth', роди́ться 'to be born',
  рожа́ть 'to give birth'.
- **muzh-** 'husband, man' (ж): муж 'husband', мужчи́на 'man', мужи́к 'peasant', му́жество 'courage',
  мужско́й 'men's, masculine', му́жественный 'manly, courageous', мужа́ть 'to mature', мужа́ться 'to
  take heart'.
- **zhen-** 'wife, woman' (ж): жена́ 'wife', же́нщина 'woman', жени́х 'bridegroom/fiancé', же́нский
  'women's, feminine', же́нственный 'feminine (adj.)', жени́ть 'to marry (off), of a man', жени́ться
  на + prep. 'to marry (of a man)'.
- **smert'-** 'death': смерть 'death', сме́ртный 'mortal', сме́ртность 'mortality', мёртвый 'dead',
  смерте́льный 'mortal, fatal', умере́ть 'to die'.
- **brak-** 'marriage': брак 'marriage, married state', бракосочета́ние 'marriage ceremony'.

### Present passive participles: formation, gaps, and use (Education unit, p. 184–186)

Present passive participles are formed from imperfective verbs, typically by adding an ending to
the first-person-plural stem (e.g. управля́ть → управля́емый 'managed'). A considerable number of
verbs — many monosyllabic verbs (бить, брать, есть, знать, класть, лить, мыть, петь, пить), the
irregular first-conjugation verbs (писа́ть, пря́тать), and many second-conjugation verbs (гото́вить,
держа́ть, плати́ть, смотре́ть, ста́вить, стро́ить) — have **no** present passive participle. The gap
can often be worked around by forming the participle from a compound of the verb instead: плати́ть →
опла́чивать → опла́чиваемый 'being paid for'.

Present passive participles decline like adjectives, agreeing in number/gender/case with the noun
they modify. Several have become lexicalized as ordinary adjectives (люби́мый 'favourite', так
называ́емый 'so-called', уважа́емый 'respected, dear'), while some negative adjectives are formed the
same way but from a *perfective* verb (несовмести́мый 'incompatible', неповтори́мый 'unique',
неразличи́мый 'indistinguishable', недопусти́мый 'not permissible', несравни́мый 'incomparable',
незави́симый 'independent' — the last actually from the imperfective). A few are used as nouns:
обвиня́емый 'the defendant', слага́емое 'item, component'.

**Important scope note:** the present passive participle cannot be used to form the passive voice.
The passive is instead formed with a reflexive verb (using the instrumental case for the agent, "by
whom") or the short form of the past passive participle: Учи́тель пи́шет уче́бник ('The teacher is
writing the textbook') → Уче́бник пи́шется учи́телем ('The textbook is being written by the teacher').

### Impersonal necessity/success/luck expressions with the dative (Health Service unit, p. 224–227)

The book systematizes several impersonal constructions that take a dative "experiencer" rather than
a nominative subject:
- **на́до / ну́жно** 'it is necessary' — negated forms differ subtly: **не на́до** = 'one should not'
  (it is necessary *not* to), while **не ну́жно** = 'it is not necessary to' (permissive, not
  prohibitive).
- **приходи́ться / прийти́сь** 'to be obliged to' — always used in the 3rd person neuter (оно́ form)
  with the dative: Дире́ктору прихо́дится занима́ться фина́нсами 'The director has to deal with the
  finances'.
- **сле́дует / сле́довало** 'ought to, should' — likewise impersonal, 3rd person neuter + dative: Вам
  не сле́дует так говори́ть 'You should not talk like that.' By contrast, **до́лжен**, **обя́зан**,
  **вы́нужден** ('must', 'obliged', 'forced') are *not* impersonal — they agree in number/gender with
  the subject like ordinary short-form adjectives.
- **удава́ться / уда́ться** 'to be successful (in doing X)' — dative + infinitive: Нам удало́сь найти́
  хоро́шую кли́нику 'We succeeded in finding a good clinic.'
- **везёт / повезло́** 'in luck' — 3rd person neuter of везти́/повезти́ + dative: Ему́ повезло́ 'He was
  in luck.'

### Negative-existential expressions: не́кого / не́чего / не́где / не́куда / не́когда (Health Service unit, p. 230–232)

A family of negated indefinite pronouns/adverbs expresses "there is no one/nothing/nowhere/no time
to (do X)," formed with a stressed particle не- + the dative experiencer + a bare infinitive: Нам
не́чего есть 'We have nothing to eat.' The accusative form of не́чего used after a preposition
becomes **не́ на что** (не́ о чём ду́мать 'nothing to think about'), while не́кто ('someone', not to be
confused with не́чего) exists as a separate positive-indefinite pronoun. Не́когда also carries a
second, unrelated sense: 'once upon a time' (он не́когда жил в Москве́ 'he once lived in Moscow'). All
these expressions can be shifted to past/future with бы́ло/бу́дет.

### The buffer vowel -о in prepositions в/к/с (Health Service unit, p. 232)

The prepositions **в**, **к**, **с** take an inserted **-о** (во, ко, со) before certain
consonant clusters, notably when the following word begins with a sound similar to the preposition's
own consonant (со среды́ 'since Wednesday' vs. в сре́ду 'on Wednesday'; во Фра́нции 'in France' vs. к
Фра́нции 'towards France') and consistently before certain cluster onsets: вр-, вс-, вт-, дн-, мн-
(во вре́мя, ко вре́мени, со вре́мени, во всех, ко всем, со все́ми, во второ́м, ко второ́му, со вторы́м,
во мно́гих, ко мно́гим, со мно́гих).

### Stress patterns of short-form adjectives (Health Service unit, p. 232–233)

The book distinguishes fixed stem stress (краси́в/краси́ва/краси́во/краси́вы; typical of adjectives
of 4+ syllables) from three types of **mobile** stress in the short form: (1) ending-stressed in the
feminine only, stem-stressed elsewhere (густ/густа́/гу́сто/гу́сты); (2) ending-stressed in feminine,
neuter, and plural (хоро́ш/хороша́/хорошо́/хороши́); (3) ending-stressed in feminine and plural only,
stem-stressed elsewhere (ви́ден/видна́/ви́дно/видны́).

### The imperfective ("present") gerund: formation and use (Duma Elections unit, p. 242–244)

Formed by taking the 3rd-person-plural present and replacing the final two letters with **-я** (работать
→ рабо́тают → рабо́тая 'working'); verbs in **-авать** end in **-авая** (дава́ть → дают → дава́я
'giving'); reflexive verbs end in **-ясь** (по́льзоваться → по́льзуются → по́льзуясь); after ж, ч, ш,
щ the spelling rule forces **-а** (держа́ть → де́ржат → держа́ 'holding'); быть's gerund is irregular
(бу́дучи). Stress normally matches the 1st-person-singular form, with named exceptions (сижу́ → си́дя,
стою́ → сто́я, лежу́ → лёжа). Many common verbs (monosyllabic infinitives like бить, брать, есть,
ждать, петь, пить; irregulars like бежа́ть, е́хать, писа́ть, хоте́ть) have **no** imperfective gerund
at all — the workaround, as with participles, is to use a compound of the verb instead.

Usage: the imperfective gerund expresses an action simultaneous with, and by the same subject as,
the main verb, and can be used regardless of the tense of the main clause (past/present/future),
functioning as a compressed alternative to a "since/while" clause or a second main clause. The
negative gerund translates "without ...ing": не име́я ника-ко́й определённой програ́ммы 'without
having any set programme'.

### The perfective ("past") gerund: formation (Duma Elections unit, p. 244–245)

Formed by replacing the **-л** of the masculine-singular past-tense perfective with **-в** (ко́нчить
→ ко́нчил → ко́нчив 'having finished'); reflexive verbs take **-вшись** (подве́ргнуться →
подве́ргнулся → подве́ргнувшись 'having been subjected to'); verbs in **-ти** (including prefixed
вести́, везти́, идти́, нести́) take a gerund in **-я**, formed the same way as the imperfective gerund
but from the perfective stem (подойти́ → подойду́т → подойдя́ 'having approached'; вы́везти →
вы́везут → вы́везя 'having exported').

### The negative: universal negative-word agreement (Duma Elections unit, p. 248–249)

In a Russian negative sentence, the verb is negated with **не** *and* every other indefinite word
in the sentence is also converted to its negative counterpart — literally "no one never did not
imagine," where English would only negate once: никто́ никогда́ не предполага́л, что... 'no one ever
imagined that...'. The relevant negative set: никто́ 'no one', ничто́ 'nothing', никогда́ 'never',
нигде́/никуда́ 'nowhere', никако́й 'no/not any', ни...ни 'neither...nor'. The genitive follows a
negated verb (не име́ет никако́й определённой програ́ммы) and also follows **нет** meaning 'there is
not'. When никто́/ничто́/никако́й combine with a preposition, the preposition is inserted between the
particle ни- and the rest of the word: ни о ком не ду́маю 'I am not thinking about anyone.'

### Noun-forming suffixes and consonant alternation (word building) (Internet unit, p. 256–259)

Extends the "word building" thread with productive suffix families: profession/occupation nouns
(**-тель, -ик, -ник, -ница (f.), -ак, -як, -арь, -арша (f.), -яр, -чик, -чица (f.), -щик, -щица
(f.), -ец, -тор** — e.g. писа́тель/писа́тельница 'writer', исто́рик 'historian', рыба́к 'fisherman',
секрета́рша 'secretary', реда́ктор 'editor') and nationality/religion/citizenship nouns (**-анин,
-анка (f.), -янин, -янка (f.), -ец, -ка (f.), -ич, -ичка (f.)** — англича́нин/англича́нка
'Englishman/-woman', христиани́н/христиа́нка 'Christian', крестья́нин/крестья́нка 'peasant').

The unit also tabulates recurring **consonant alternations** that obscure a shared root: с/ш
(высо́кий/вы́ше 'high/higher'), з/ж (францу́з/францу́женка), ст/щ (густо́й/гу́ще 'thick/thicker'), ц/ч
(грани́ца/грани́чить 'border' noun/verb), к/ч (река́/речно́й 'river' noun/adj.), х/ш (му́ха/муши́ный

alternations introduced in the Demography unit's root-family discussion above.

### Verb prefixes and their aspectual/semantic effect (Internet unit, p. 260–262)

Prefixes attached to an imperfective verb usually make it perfective (писа́ть → написа́ть), but many
prefixes additionally shift the meaning, and the derived perfective typically re-acquires an
imperfective partner via infix **-ыв-/-ив-** (1st-conjugation verbs take **-ыв-**, 2nd-conjugation
verbs take **-ив-**). Prefixes catalogued: **в-** 'in' (впи́сывать/вписа́ть 'to insert'; reflexive
впи́сываться/вписа́ться 'to fit in well'), **вы-** 'out of' (выпи́сывать/вы́писать 'to copy out,
prescribe'), **до-** 'finish' (допи́сывать/дописа́ть), **за-** 'for, begin' (запи́сывать/записа́ть 'to
note'), **над-** 'over, super-' (надпи́сывать/надписа́ть 'to inscribe'), **о-** 'about, around'
(опи́сывать/описа́ть 'to describe'), **пере-** 're-, over, across' (перепи́сывать/переписа́ть 'to
rewrite'; reflexive 'to correspond'), **при-** 'near' (припи́сывать/приписа́ть 'to add'), **под-**
'under, sub-' (подпи́сывать/подписа́ть 'to sign'; reflexive + на 'to subscribe to'). Note a stem
vowel shift in the imperfective infix for рабо́тать-type verbs (о→а: зараба́тывать/зарабо́тать 'to
earn'). Second-conjugation examples of **-ив-**: дострои́ть-type (достра́ивать/достро́ить 'to finish
building'), пристро́ить-type (пристра́ивать/пристро́ить 'to build on'), устро́ить-type
(устра́ивать/устро́ить 'to organize' — from стро́й 'system, structure').

### Commonly confused verbs of "using" and "stopping" (Internet unit, p. 264–266)

Four near-synonyms for 'to use' are distinguished: **испо́льзовать** (impf. only, + acc.) is the
general-purpose verb; **по́льзоваться / воспо́льзоваться** (+ instr.) additionally covers 'to make use
of / enjoy / take advantage of' and combines idiomatically with nouns (по́льзоваться успе́хом 'to
enjoy success', по́льзоваться возмо́жностью 'to take advantage of the opportunity'); **употребля́ть /

# Russian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Using Russian, A Guide to Contemporary Usage (Part 2)

**Source:** Derek Offord and Natalia Gogolitsyna, *Using Russian: A Guide to Contemporary Usage*
(Cambridge University Press, 2005), PDF pages 181–360 (printed pages ~145–324), covering: end of
§4.1 (English words difficult to render in Russian) through §4.6; all of Chapter 5 (Vocabulary and
idiom: neologisms, slang, computing terminology, transition words, fillers, modal particles,
interjections, vulgar language, idioms, proverbs, similes); all of Chapter 6 (Language and everyday
life: measurement, currency, time, addresses, family terms, public notices, abbreviations,
acronyms, country/nationality names, city-inhabitant nouns, jokes and puns); all of Chapter 7
(Verbal etiquette); all of Chapter 8 (Word-formation: prefixes, suffixes, diminutives, composition);
and the opening of Chapter 9 (Inflection), which is treated as a **non-redundant-supplement scope
decision** — see note at the end of the Grammar points section.

**Coverage note:** this is the **register flagship source** for Russian — this dispatch prioritized
every explicit register/formality/colloquial annotation the book makes (its own R1/R2/R3(a/b/c),
D (dialect/very colloquial), and other tags — see the Register-tag key below). This file is a
sibling of `022_using_russian_contemporary_usage_part1.md` (pages 1–180) and
`024_using_russian_contemporary_usage_part3.md` (pages 361–529); ranges are not duplicated between
them. The source has a clean, correctly-rendered Cyrillic text layer throughout this page range —
`pdftotext -layout` reproduces stressed Cyrillic vowels (with combining acute-accent marks) and
ordinary Cyrillic correctly; **no font-substitution cipher was found** in this range (cross-checked
against dozens of known words — e.g. тро́гaть, спaси́бо, здрáвствуй — all render correctly). This
source had a genuine text layer throughout, so Vision Reading Confidence is `n/a` for every row.

**Register-tag key used by this source (paraphrased):** R1 = colloquial/informal spoken register;
R2 = neutral, unmarked register (most everyday usage); R3 (with subtypes R3a academic/scientific,
R3b official/business/legal, R3c belles-lettres/literary) = formal/bookish written registers; D =
dialect or very colloquial/non-standard speech; (obs) = obsolescent; (iron) = ironic; (pej) =
pejorative; (vulg) = vulgar/taboo. These map onto this project's Usage Tier taxonomy roughly as
R1→`colloquial`, R2→`core`, R3(a/b/c)→`formal`/`literary`, D→`regional`/`slang`, (vulg)→`taboo`.

**Scale note:** given the size of the assigned range (~180 pages spanning six book chapters), very
long enumerable factual lists that carry little register/mechanics value for this project (the
abbreviations list in §6.9, the acronym list in §6.10, and the country/nationality/city-inhabitant
tables in §§6.11–6.12) are represented by a representative sample plus an explicit note on what was
condensed, rather than transcribed in full — consistent with the coverage rule's "comprehensive but
not exhaustive" principle. Register-bearing content (Chapters 5 and 7 especially, and the
diminutive/pejorative morphology of Chapter 8) is extracted at full density.

## Grammar points

### Translation of the verb *to be* (§4.2)

The source stresses that быть itself (omitted in the present tense) covers only a minority of "to
be" contexts. It gives a working set of Russian verbs that substitute for it by context: явля́ться/
яви́ться (formal/R3, complement in instrumental, subject in nominative, typically inverted so the
weighted phrase comes last); прeдстaвля́ть собо́й (bookish, less common, complement in accusative);
стaть (an increasingly common apparent synonym of явля́ться); зaключáться в / состоя́ть в ("to
consist in", R2/R3); состaвля́ть/состáвить ("to constitute/amount to", statistical contexts);
нaходи́ться and рaсполо́жeн (location/state); стоя́ть/лeжáть/сидéть (literal position); сто́ить (to
cost/be worth); прису́тствовaть (to be present); рaбо́тaть vs. the slightly more formal служи́ть (to
work as); приходи́ться (to fall on a date, or a kinship relation: "Oн мнe прихо́дится прáдeдом" — he
is my great-grandfather); eсть as copula ("кто eсть кто") and in R3 scientific/definitional style;
сущeствовáть and имéться for existential "there is/are"; состоя́ться for "there will be" in the
sense of "take place." Purely emphatic English "to be" (*It was only then that he realised...*) has
no Russian verbal equivalent at all — the emphasis shifts to word order, prosody, or particles like
и́мeнно, то́лько, жe, и.

### Translation of English modal auxiliaries (§4.3)

Each English modal (can/could/may/might/must/ought/shall/should/will/would) is broken into its
distinct senses (ability, permission, doubt, prohibition, obligation, probability, unfulfilled past
possibility, reported-speech future, conditional, reproach, etc.), each mapped to a different
Russian construction — e.g. *can* (ability) → мо́жно/мочь/умéть; *cannot* (prohibition) → нeльзя́ +
impf infinitive, or the R3 formal нe рaзрeшáeтся/воспрeщáeтся; *must* (obligation) →
до́лжeн/должнá/должны́, нáдо, ну́жно, слéдуeт (an ascending-formality set, cross-referenced from
§4.1); *must* (certainty) → должно́ быть; *ought to have* (regret) → слéдовaло бы / до́лжeн был бы;
*would* (habitual past) → imperfective past, often with a frequency adverb. The chapter is explicit
that English modals' fine-grained senses do not map one-to-one onto any single Russian modal word —
this is presented as the single hardest area of English→Russian translation covered in the book.

### Transitive/intransitive verb pairs (§4.4)

Russian marks the transitive/intransitive distinction two ways: (1) via a non-reflexive/reflexive
pair from the same root (улу́чшить/улу́чшиться, "to improve" trans./intrans.) — the more productive,
general pattern (see 11.8 in the source); (2) via etymologically distinct verb pairs for a smaller,
listed set of common verbs, e.g. кипяти́ть (boil, trans.) vs. кипéть (boil, intrans.); вéшaть (hang,
trans.) vs. висéть (hang, intrans.); стáвить (stand sth up, trans.) vs. стоя́ть (stand, intrans.).

### Translation of English *-ing* forms (§4.5)

Nine distinct functions of English *-ing* are distinguished, each with its own Russian rendering:
progressive tense → imperfective verb; attendant action → a separate clause (R3: a gerund);
prior action → subordinate clause (R3: perfective gerund); noun-modifying "-ing" phrase → relative
clause with кото́рый, or (R3) an active participle; action/process/result noun → a deverbal noun in
-ниe (see §8.7.1); gerund-as-activity-name → verbal noun or infinitive; after verbs of perception →
subordinate clause with кaк; after "keep" → всё + imperfective, or нe пeрeстaвáть + infinitive;
after "from" (prevent/stop) → bare infinitive.

### то́жe vs. тáкжe (§4.6)

то́жe is used when an *additional subject* performs the same action ("I like music. My wife likes
it *too*"); тáкжe is used when a *single subject* performs an *additional* action or one affecting
an additional object. и is very frequently used for "also/too/as well" and, in a clause with a
negated verb, can mean "either."

### Neologism formation mechanisms (§5.1)

Four productive mechanisms are named for post-1980s Russian neologisms: (1) phonetically-adapted
loanwords, mostly from English, sometimes needing affixation to fit Russian morphology as a noun,
adjective, or verb (вeртикáльный, митинговáть); (2) acronym composition (бомж); (3) affixation of
existing Russian roots (тeнeви́к); (4) polysemanticisation — extending an existing word's meaning,
sometimes on a foreign semantic model (отмывáть "to launder" money; я́стрeb "hawk" politically).
Slavonicisms embedded in biblical set phrases have been revived post-Soviet with the reopening of
the Church, and can carry either R3 (rhetorical/nationalist) or R1 (jocular) register depending on
context — the same lexical item, opposite register, by context alone.

### Diminutive/hypocoristic/pejorative name morphology (§7.3.1)

First names have (up to) five derivational layers: full form → shortened form → vocative
(truncated) form → hypocoristic diminutive (-eнькa/-очкa/-eчкa, e.g. Cáшeнькa) → a second,
"harder" diminutive (-кa, e.g. Cáшкa). The -кa layer is register-ambiguous by design: among young
children addressing each other it's neutral/normal; used by an adult about another adult or child it
can range from jocular affection to real disparagement, depending entirely on relationship and tone
— the suffix alone doesn't disambiguate. This is a clean, citable example of a single morpheme
carrying opposite social valence conditioned on speaker/addressee relationship rather than on form.

### Patronymic formation (§7.3.2)

Patronymics are built from the father's full first name + a suffix keyed to the stem's final sound:
-ович (masc., after hard consonants) / -eвич (masc., after soft consonants or replacing й) / -ич
(masc., replacing a/я) and the corresponding feminine -овнa/-eвнa/-ичнa. In colloquial speech the
patronymic contracts (e.g. Aлeксáндрович → Aлeксáндрыч). First-name-plus-patronymic (both in full
form, never diminutive) is the standard polite address to an unfamiliar adult, functionally
equivalent to English "Mr/Mrs + surname."

### ты/вы selection (§7.2)

Choice is governed by four independent factors the source tabulates explicitly: degree of intimacy,
relative age/status, formality of situation, and (as a fourth, marked axis) "state of relations" —
where *subverting* the pronoun normally expected by the other three factors itself communicates
something (excessive/cold вы = strained relations; unearned ты to a senior = disrespect). Note: even
close, long-standing relationships between educated older Russians may deliberately remain on вы to
preserve mutual respect — familiarity does not automatically license the switch.

### Public-notice grammar (§6.8)

Six distinct grammatical strategies are catalogued for Russian public notices (a genre of R3b): (a)
bare infinitive for instructions (perfective) and prohibitions (imperfective, with нe); (b) ordinary
imperative; (c) past passive participle for prohibitions (Bход зaпрeщён); (d) 3rd-person-plural or
про́сьбa/про́сим framing for exhortations; (e) reflexive verb for stating facts/prohibitions; (f)
gerunds (a marker of R3 register) in instructions like "У ходя́, гaси́тe свeт."

### Word-formation: verbal prefixes (§8.3)

Some two dozen prefixes attach to simple verbs, most commonly conveying either a directional sense
(в- "into", вы- "out of", до- "as far as", зa- "behind", из-/ис- "out of", нa- "onto/into", о-/об-
"around", от- "away from", пeрe- "across", под- "up to/towards", при- "to a destination", про-
"past/through", рaз-/рaс- "in various directions", с- "off/down from" or "together", у- "away")
or an aspectual/manner nuance layered onto that core sense — e.g. по- + a verb of motion for
short-duration action (поговори́ть, "to have a talk"); зa- for inchoative ("to start doing");
вы-/из- for exhaustive/thorough completion; -ся combined with на- for satiety-to-excess (нaéсться,
"to eat one's fill"); за- + -ся for over-engrossment (зaчитáться, "to get engrossed in reading").
Adding a prefix to a simple imperfective verb normally perfectivizes it; sometimes the prefix adds
only aspectual completeness (писáть → нaписáть), but sometimes it also shifts meaning (вязáть "to
tie" → связáть "to tie together, unite").

### Word-formation: noun and adjective suffixes, diminutive/pejorative morphology (§8.7–8.9)

Noun suffixes are organized by function: person-denoting by origin/occupation/quality (-aк/-як,
-aнин/-янин, -aнт/-eнт, -aч, -eц, -ик, -ист, -ун, -тeль, -щик/-чик), abstract-quality nouns
(-ость, -отá, -ство — heavily R3), deverbal action/result nouns (-ниe, extremely productive,
international-currency equivalent of English -ing/-tion), and a dedicated female-agent-suffix layer
(§8.7.2: -aнкa/-янкa, -иня/-ыня, -исa/-eссa, -ихa, -ицa, -кa, -ницa, -шa, -ья) mapped one-to-one
against the corresponding masculine-agent suffixes.

**This is the chapter most directly relevant to future slang-mechanics analysis**: §8.8 catalogues a
full diminutive/augmentative/expressive-suffix system explicitly tied to register — diminutives
belong "primarily to R1," are common in literary/folk-poetic style, and are "generally altogether
absent" from formal R3(a/b). Layers include simple diminutives (-eнькa, -ик, -кa, -ок/-ёк, -цe/-цо́,
-чик), *double* diminutives for extra smallness/endearment (-о́чeк, -éчко, -очкa/-eчкa/-ичкa), a
single augmentative suffix (-ищe/-ищa, "massive"), and two dedicated pejorative suffixes
(-и́шкa/-и́шко for scorn, with the explicit exception that брaти́шкa, "brother," is affectionate
despite the form; -ёнкa/-о́нкa, similarly capable of either scorn or affection by context — the same
formal ambiguity already seen in the -кa name-diminutive above). The two dedicated slang-forming
verbal suffixes in §8.11 are directly relevant to future mechanics work: **-ничaть** (R1, often
jocular, forms imperfectives naming a pattern of behavior, e.g. скря́жничaть, "to behave like a
miser," from скря́гa) and **-aну́ть** (marked "D" — dialect/very colloquial — forms highly
expressive semelfactive perfectives for a sudden one-off action, e.g. скaзaну́ть, "to blurt out").

### Word-formation: consonant/spelling mechanics (§8.2)

Covers the hard/soft-consonant vowel-pairing system (col. 1 a/o/у/ы/э vs. col. 2 я/ё/ю/и/e), the hard
sign ъ's sole modern function (separating a consonant-final prefix from a vowel-initial root, e.g.
объéхaть), consonant devoicing (voiced б/в/г/д/ж/з vs. unvoiced п/ф/к/т/ш/с/х/ц/ч/щ, reflected in
spelling only after з-final prefixes), the four core spelling rules (ы→и after г/к/х/ж/ч/ш/щ; я/ю→
a/y after the same set; unstressed o→e after ж/ц/ч/ш/щ; ё is always stressed), the standard
consonant mutations (г→ж, д→ж/жд, з→ж, к→ч, c→ш, ст→щ, т→ч/щ, х→ш) that recur throughout suffixation
and conjugation, and epenthetic л inserted before certain suffixes after б/в/м/п/ф (е.g. куп/л/ю́).

### Demonym-formation morphology (§6.11–6.12, condensed per the scope note above)

Russian nationality/place-of-origin nouns draw on a wider suffix inventory than English equivalents
("Londoner," "Parisian"): -eц/-кa and -aнин/-янин/-янкa are the two dominant productive patterns for
country names; city-inhabitant nouns additionally draw on -ич/-ичкa and -як/-ячкa. Predicting which
suffix attaches to a given city name is explicitly flagged by the source as not reliably inferrable
by a foreign learner — it must be learned per-item. Nationality/demonym nouns and adjectives are
never capitalized in Russian (unlike English "Russian," "French").

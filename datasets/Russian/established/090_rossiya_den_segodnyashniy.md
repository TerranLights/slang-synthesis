## Grammar points

### Substantivized adjectives (субстантивированные прилагательные)

Exercise 4 (p. 9) flags this pattern explicitly using two words from the text itself: **учёный** ("scholar/scientist," historically an adjective "learned" now used as a noun) and **домашние** ("family/household members," from the adjective "domestic"). Russian productively converts certain adjectives into nouns without further derivation — the adjective simply takes on nominal syntax (case-agreement as a noun-phrase head) while keeping full adjectival declension. The exercise's own drill list of further examples worth noting for the mechanics-analysis phase: рабочий ("worker"), знакомый ("acquaintance"), прохожий ("passer-by"), портной ("tailor"), больной ("patient"), мостовая ("roadway"), парадная ("entrance/stairwell," dialectal St. Petersburg usage for what Moscow speech calls подъезд), близкие ("loved ones/close relations"), молодые ("newlyweds/young couple"), гостиная ("living room"), столовая ("dining room"), прихожая ("hallway"), детская ("nursery"), кладовая ("storeroom"), пирожковая ("pastry/pie shop"). Many of these (гостиная, столовая, прихожая, детская, кладовая, пирожковая) show the pattern is especially productive for naming rooms/venues by their characteristic function.

### Paronym pairs (exercise 3, p. 9)

The book's paronym-discrimination exercise is worth flagging as a register/nuance resource even though it isn't independently glossed: семейный/семейственный ("of the family" vs. "nepotistic/overly familial"), фамильный/фамильярный ("family-heirloom" vs. "over-familiar in manner"), домашний/домовитый/домовой ("domestic" vs. "house-proud/thrifty" vs. "house-spirit, domovoi"), доверительный/доверчивый ("confidential" vs. "trusting/credulous"), цветной/цветочный/цветовой/цветастый ("colored" vs. "floral" vs. "color-related" vs. "gaudily multicolored"), праздничный/праздный ("festive" vs. "idle"), старший/старый ("elder/senior" vs. "old"), экономический/экономичный ("economic" vs. "economical/cost-efficient"). These near-homophone pairs are a recurring Russian derivational-suffix trap and a plausible source of slang-formation-by-confusion or deliberate wordplay later in the mechanics-analysis phase.

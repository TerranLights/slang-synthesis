## Grammar points

### 7.1–7.2 Direct address and interjections

Vocatives (прямое обращение) and interjections (междометия) are set off by commas in both Russian
and English — no divergence here (pp. 49–50). **Key structural point:** the comma of enclosure is
always logically a *pair* (one before, one after the enclosed element) — when the enclosed element
sits at a sentence boundary, only one member of the pair is actually written, since the other slot
is filled by the sentence's own beginning/end punctuation instead (illustrated with starred
ungrammatical examples showing what a "doubled" boundary comma would look like, which never
actually occurs) (p. 50).

### 7.3 Parenthetical words

Parenthetical words/phrases (вводные слова и предложения), expressing the speaker's attitude toward
the topic, are always set off by commas in Russian — **and, unlike English, Russian never permits
omitting these commas even when the author considers the parenthetical minimally important to the
flow of thought** (English allows either *In my opinion that's a good idea* or *In my opinion, that's
a good idea*; Russian permits only the comma-marked version). The book gives an extensive
categorized list, reproduced in the Vocabulary section above (pp. 50–54).

**Critical homograph warning:** many of these words/phrases have a second, *non*-parenthetical
sentence-element use where they are NOT set off by commas — the same string can be parenthetical in
one sentence and an ordinary adverbial/object in another, e.g. между нами "just between us"
(parenthetical, comma) vs. между нами произошёл крупный разговор "an argument occurred between us"
(ordinary prepositional-phrase adverbial, no comma); по душе (parenthetical "frankly," comma) vs.
песня пришлась им по душе "the song touched their hearts" (ordinary idiom-as-predicate-complement,
no comma); скажем прямо (parenthetical, comma) vs. мы скажем ему это прямо "we'll tell him that
straight out" (ordinary manner adverb, no comma); действительно (parenthetical "actually," comma)
vs. он действительно пришёл "he actually came" (ordinary manner adverb, no comma); наконец
(parenthetical "after all," comma) vs. наконец они добрались до реки "at last they made it to the
river" (ordinary time adverb, no comma); с другой стороны (parenthetical "on the other hand," comma)
vs. с другой стороны улицы "from the other side of the street" (ordinary literal locative phrase,
no comma) (p. 53).

### 7.4 When Russian uses no comma but English does

Prepositional phrases and adverbial modifiers (of time/place/manner/reason/purpose) preposed to the
subject are **never** set off by a comma in Russian, even though English optionally sets them off
(English has an "open style," commas only where needed to prevent ambiguity, vs. a "closed style,"
commas after every sentence-initial adverbial — Russian has no such choice at all, since its
word-endings rather than word order carry syntactic relations, removing the ambiguity risk that
motivates the English comma in the first place). The book flags this as the single most common
comma-related error made by English-speaking learners of Russian — false-analogy comma insertion
after a preposed adverbial (pp. 54–55).

**Two further no-Russian-comma cases the book flags explicitly:** (1) English sets off the year from
the month/day in a date with a comma; Russian never does (Четвёртого июля 1776 г. была подписана...
"On July 4, 1776, ..."). (2) English uses a comma when a person's surname precedes the given name;
Russian does not (Иванов Вадим Алексеевич = "Ivanov, Vadim Alekseevich" with no comma) — this also
governs Russian envelope-addressing convention: postal code, city, street address, building/apartment
number, then the addressee's name in the dative case, surname first, with no comma (pp. 56–57).

### 7.5 Commas that set off detached sentence elements

Detached elements (обособленные члены предложения) elaborate a sentence's meaning without being
structurally required, and are always set off by the paired comma of enclosure when they occur, per
the following subtypes:

- **7.5.1 Detached modifiers** — postposed adjectival modifiers answering какой?, set off by commas
  (contrasted with preposed adjectives, which are ordinary non-detached attributes and take no comma).
- **7.5.2 Participial phrases (причастный оборот)** — a participle-headed phrase modifying a noun; when
  it *follows* its head noun it is set off by commas (functioning much like a relative clause with
  который); **when it precedes its head noun (a word-order option with no English equivalent), it is
  NOT set off by commas** — e.g. Брошенный с высоты камень падает на землю "A thrown-from-above rock
  falls to the ground" (no comma) vs. Камень, брошенный с высоты, падает на землю (comma both sides)
  for the reordered version (pp. 57–58).
- **7.5.3 Appositives** — noun phrases elaborating another noun's identity, always set off by commas;
  when introduced by по имени/по кличке/по прозвищу ("named"/"nicknamed"/"with the nickname"), the
  introducing phrase is set off together with the appositive it introduces — English may or may not
  set these off (open/closed style choice absent in Russian) (p. 59).
- **7.5.4 Prepositional phrases of exclusion or addition** — formed with кроме, помимо, сверх, за
  исключением ("except for," "in addition to," "with the exception of"), always set off by commas in
  Russian regardless of position, whereas English sets them off only when they precede the subject
  (p. 59–60).
- **7.5.5 Detached adverbial phrases** — phrases containing a verbal adverb (деепричастие, typically
  preceding the predicate) or a verbal-adverb phrase (деепричастный оборот) are set off by commas.
  **Three explicit exceptions where a historically-verbal-adverb form is NOT set off:** (a) an
  individual verbal-adverb form standing after the verb that denotes no separate action of its own,
  functioning as a plain manner adverb (он шёл не торопясь "he walked unhurriedly," мы поели молча
  "we ate in silence"); (b) fixed idiomatic phrases built on verbal adverbs (высунув язык, спустя
  рукава, засучив рукава, немного погодя, очертя голову, сломя голову — full list in Vocabulary
  above); (c) a small set of adverbs ending in -учи/-ючи no longer used as true verbal adverbs in
  modern Russian (играючи, умеючи, припеваючи, крадучись) (pp. 60–61).
- **7.5.6 Extended adverbial modifiers** — adverbial phrases built on compound prepositions
  (preposition+noun compounds like по причине, во избежание) or verbal-adverb-derived prepositions
  (благодаря, несмотря на) are always set off by commas in Russian regardless of position, unlike
  English, which sets them off only when preposed. Full preposition list: ввиду (+gen.), благодаря
  (+dat.), вследствие (+gen.), по причине (+gen.), вопреки (+dat.), несмотря на (+acc.), по случаю
  (+gen.), в случае (+gen.), во избежание (+gen.), за неимением (+gen.), с приближением (+gen.) (pp.
  62).
- **7.5.7 Adverbial modifiers in apposition** — an adverb/prepositional phrase that clarifies another
  adverbial in the same sentence (elaborating on a place/time/condition) is set off by commas in
  Russian; English usage is optional here, obligatory only when the two phrases could otherwise be
  misread as describing separate components of the same event rather than one clarifying the other
  (p. 63–64).
- **7.5.8 Phrases of comparison** — comparison phrases introduced by как ("like, as") are obligatorily
  set off by commas in Russian, with two explicit exceptions where как takes NO comma: (a) lexically
  fixed similes/set comparison phrases (устойчивые сравнения — e.g. твёрдый как камень "hard as a
  rock," здоров как бык "healthy as an ox"; the book cites Ogoltsev's *Common Russian Similes* as
  cataloguing over 500 such fixed comparisons, none comma-marked — rule of thumb given: comma only
  for a *newly coined* simile, never for one learned as a fixed expression); (b) как meaning "in the
  capacity of" rather than "like/as" (Пушкин известен миру как великий поэт "Pushkin is known to the
  world as a great poet" — no comma). Other comparison conjunctions (будто, как будто, словно, точно,
  and что in the sense "like") behave the same as как and are likewise comma-marked in Russian only
  (pp. 64–66).

### 7.6 Commas with particles and response phrases

Affirmation particles (да, конечно, ладно, хорошо), the negation particle нет, and что used to elicit
a response are set off by commas in both Russian and English alike — no divergence here (pp. 66–67).

### 7.7 Commas used to set off subordinate clauses (section opening)

**Central rule, already forecast in Chapter 3: in Russian, all subordinate clauses are obligatorily
set off by commas** — a much broader rule than English, which comma-marks only a few special
subordinate-clause types. The book explicitly flags omitting these obligatory commas as one of the
most common mistakes English-speaking learners make when writing Russian (p. 68). (Detailed subtype
treatment continues in the next established/ file, §7.7 onward.)

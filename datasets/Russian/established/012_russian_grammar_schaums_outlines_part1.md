## Grammar points

### Chapter 1 — The Sounds of Russian

**The Cyrillic alphabet.** 33 letters: 10 vowels, 20 consonants, the glide й, and two "signs" (ь soft sign,
ъ hard sign) that carry no sound of their own. Letters fall into groups by how they relate to the Latin
alphabet: some are similar to English letters in both shape and sound (А, О, Е, М, К, Т); some share a
shape with English letters but not the sound ("false friends": У, В, Н, Р, С); several are modeled on Greek
letters (Г, Д, Л, П, Ф, Х, and Р); the rest have no Latin/Greek analog (Б, З, Ё, Ж, И, Й, Ц, Ч, Ш, Щ, ы,
Э, Ю, Я). Knowing letter *names* (not just sounds) matters for spelling out loud and for pronouncing
acronyms read letter-by-letter (e.g. МБ "em-be" = Мировой банк World Bank).

**Vowels.** Ten vowel letters express five vowel *sounds*, paired into a "hard series" (а э о у ы) and a
"soft series" (я е ё ю и) — the soft series signals that the *preceding consonant* is palatalized, not that
the vowel itself differs in some absolute sense. Word-initially, after a vowel, or after ь/ъ, the soft-series
vowels я, е, ё, ю (but not и) are pronounced with a preceding [y] glide.

**Stress and vowel reduction.** Russian stress is free (can fall on any syllable) and mobile (can shift
between forms of the same word, e.g. а́дрес → адреса́), and stress alone can distinguish otherwise
identical words (мука́ "flour" vs. му́ка "torture") or word pairs (до́ма "at home" vs. дома́ "houses"). Only
а, о, э, я, e are subject to reduction; ю/у, и/ы, and ё (always stressed) are not. Unstressed а/о reduce to
[ʌ]-like in pretonic/word-initial position and further to [ə] elsewhere; unstressed э reduces to [ɪ]; unstressed
я/е reduce to [yɪ]/[ɪ] in most positions but to [yə]/[ə] specifically in certain grammatical endings (this is
what distinguishes, e.g., unstressed -ая from -ое in casual speech — both surface as [-əyə]).

**Consonants: hard/soft pairing.** 15 of Russian's 20 consonants pair into hard and soft (palatalized)
versions distinguished only by the following letter (hard before а э о у ы or word-finally; soft before я е
ё ю и or ь). ч and щ are always soft; ж, ш, ц are always hard (and even before soft-series vowels, ж/ш/ц
force those vowels toward their hard-series pronunciation). Consonant clusters жч/сч/зч can merge to a
long soft [ɕː]; жж/зж merge to a long soft [ʑː] in a small set of words (дрожжи, жжёт, езжу, etc.).

**Voicing.** Six voiced/voiceless consonant pairs exist; word-final voiced consonants devoice (дуб →
[зуп]-type pattern), and consonant clusters assimilate to the voicing of the second consonant (regressive
assimilation), with one exception: в does not trigger voicing of a preceding voiceless consonant (тво́й ≠
*[двой]).

**Syllabification** breaks preferentially at a vowel, except that clusters beginning with р, л, м, н, or й
break after that consonant instead.

**Three spelling rules** govern many Russian endings: (1) never write ы after к г х ж ч ш щ — write и
instead (affects noun/adjective plurals); (2) never write я/ю after г к х ж ч ш щ ц — write а/у instead
(affects verb conjugation); (3) after ж ч ш щ ц, write unstressed e but stressed ó.

**Capitalization** differs from English: Russian does not capitalize "I" (я) mid-sentence, nationality/origin
nouns and adjectives (американец, москвич), words after the first word of a title/holiday/organization
name, days of the week, or months.

**Transliteration (English→Russian)** follows fairly regular correspondences (e.g. English ch[k]→ч vs.
ch[ʃ]→ш, sh→ш, ph→ф, th→т usually but →ф in a few Greek-derived names, g[dʒ]→дж, q→кв, w→у
occasionally в in older loans, x→кс), illustrated with loanwords and proper names.

**Letter styles.** Printed, italic, and cursive forms of Cyrillic letters differ, sometimes substantially (e.g.
cursive д resembles a Latin-script "g"; capital cursive Т and М have distinctive hooked shapes).

### Chapter 2 — Nouns

**Structure and gender.** Russian nouns consist of a root, optional prefix/suffix, and an ending; the ending
encodes gender, number, and case. All nouns are masculine, feminine, or neuter. Grammatical gender is
usually predictable from the nominative singular ending: masculine = consonant/-й/-ь stem with no vowel
ending; feminine = -а/-я or -ь; neuter = -о/-е/-ё, with a small irregular class of -мя neuters (и́мя, вре́мя,
зна́мя, пла́мя, бре́мя, се́мя) that must be memorized. Natural gender overrides grammatical form for a
handful of nouns denoting male persons that happen to have feminine-looking endings (мужчи́на, де́душка,
дя́дя, папа, ю́ноша, and the full/diminutive male first names in -а/-я like Илья́, Са́ша). Nouns of "common
gender" (у́мница, пла́кса, сирота́, коллега, etc.) take masculine or feminine agreement depending on the
referent's actual sex. Nouns denoting traditionally male-dominated professions (врач, инжене́р,
профе́ссор) stay grammatically masculine even referring to a woman, but a predicate adjective/past-tense
verb agrees with the referent's actual sex (судья́ "judge" and челове́к "person" are exceptions requiring
masculine agreement regardless). Male/female pairs can also be formed by suffix (-ка, -иса/-есса, -иха,
-анка/-янка, -щица, -чица, -ица, -ница, etc.). Indeclinable loanwords' gender is assigned by semantic
class (inanimate objects → neuter by default, though sometimes matched to a native generic noun's gender
instead, e.g. торна́до masc. like ве́тер; people/animals → natural gender or default masculine; place
names → gender of the matching generic Russian noun: cities/islands masculine, rivers feminine, lakes
neuter).

**Number.** Most nouns distinguish singular/plural; the nominative plural ending depends on gender and
stem hardness (masc./fem. hard -ы, soft -и; neuter hard -а, soft -я), subject to Spelling Rule 1. Stress
often shifts between singular and plural (masc.: stem→ending; many two-syllable neuters: reverse
pattern; some fem.: ending→stem). "Fleeting vowels" (о/е/ё in suffixes -ок/-ёк/-ец) drop when any ending
is added. A few masculine nouns take the stressed plural ending -а́/-я́ (го́род→города́, etc.) instead of the
regular -ы/-и. Irregular plurals include a small -ья class (брат→бра́тья, стул→сту́лья, etc., some with
stem changes: друг→друзья́, сын→сыновья́), two nouns that insert -ер- before the plural ending
(мать→ма́тери, дочь→до́чери), two that use an entirely different plural stem (ребёнок→де́ти, человек→
лю́ди), and the -мя neuters, which insert a stem extension in the plural (вре́мя→времена́). Some nouns
occur only in the singular (materials/substances, abstract qualities, and — a systematic English/Russian
mismatch — collective nouns for fruits/vegetables/clothing that are plural in English but singular in
Russian: изюм "raisins," виноград "grapes," одежда "clothes"); others occur only in the plural (часы́
"watch," очки́ "glasses," but also money-related and paired-object nouns) — часы́ being a notable
singular-in-English/plural-in-Russian mismatch.

**Declension classes.** Nouns fall into three declensions by gender/ending: First declension = masculine
consonant/-й/-ь stems and neuter -о/-е/-ё; Second declension = feminine and (natural-masculine)
masculine nouns in -а/-я; Third declension = feminine nouns in -ь. Soft-sign nouns are the ambiguous
case (masculine soft-sign nouns are First declension; feminine soft-sign nouns are the *only* members of
Third declension).

**Case and animacy.** Russian has six cases (nominative, accusative, genitive, prepositional, dative,
instrumental) expressing grammatical relations that English conveys via word order or prepositions.
Animacy (a noun denoting a living being vs. not) determines whether the accusative singular of masculine
nouns (and all genders' accusative plural of animate nouns) is identical to the nominative or to the
genitive — this "animate accusative = genitive" rule is a core mechanism threading through the whole case
system.

**Nominative case** uses: grammatical subject (with predicate agreement in person/number, and in the past
tense, gender); predicate nominative after an implied "to be" (Ива́н — студе́нт); direct address/vocative
(colloquially truncating the final vowel: Са́ш!, Ма́м!); noun-in-apposition to a title (рома́н «Война́ и
мир»). Predicate nominatives after a past-tense form of быть denoting a *permanent* trait (nationality,
kinship, an evaluative quality) stay nominative rather than shifting to instrumental.

**Accusative case** endings mostly track the nominative (inanimate masc./neuter) or the genitive (animate
masc., all animate plurals) or take -у/-ю (fem./masc. -а/-я nouns). Uses: direct object of a transitive verb;
duration/repetition of time (це́лый час); extent of distance/cost/weight (пять киломе́тров, ты́сячу
до́лларов); object of accusative-governing prepositions (covered in Ch. 3).

**Genitive case** singular endings: -а/-я (masc./neut. hard/soft) or -ы/-и (fem. hard/soft, with Spelling
Rule 1/2 exceptions and masc. -а/-я nouns declining like feminines). Genitive plural has three possible
endings — -ов/-ев (masc. hard-consonant and -ец/-й stems, and nouns whose nom. plural is stressed -ья),
-ей (masc./fem. soft-sign stems and masc. ж/ч/ш/щ stems, plus a few irregulars), and a "zero ending"
(most fem. -а/-я and neuter -о/-е, sometimes with an inserted -о-/-е- vowel to break up a final consonant
cluster, e.g. окно́→о́кон, письмо́→пи́сем) — plus assorted irregulars (челове́к→людей/челове́к depending
on context, год→лет). Genitive uses: possessor (following, not preceding, the possessed noun, unlike
English 's); "named after" constructions; whole-to-part, agent-of-action, object-of-action, object-of-
attributed-quality relations (all corresponding to English "of"); container/quantity (буты́лка вина́); after
numerals 2–4 (genitive singular) and 5+ (genitive plural); after quantity adverbs (ско́лько, мно́го, ма́ло,
не́сколько) — genitive plural for countables, genitive singular for substances/abstracts; "partitive" genitive
after transitive verbs (Дай хлеба "give me some bread," vs. accusative for "the whole thing"); a small
class of masculine nouns (usually foods/drinks) additionally has a colloquial "partitive genitive" -у/-ю
ending distinct from the regular genitive (чай→ча́ю); after negated transitive verbs — genitive is strongly
preferred when the object is abstract, is the pronoun это, is indefinite, follows an emphatic ни-negation,
or follows a negated perception verb; after negated forms of быть expressing absence (нет, не́ было, не
бу́дет); after a closed set of genitive-governing verbs (жела́ть, боя́ться, избега́ть, etc.) and adjectives
(по́лный, досто́йный, лишённый); as the second term of a comparative (Москва́ бо́льше Петербу́рга).

**Prepositional case** endings: -е (most nouns), -и (third-declension fem. in -ь, and nouns in -ие/-ия/-ий);
plural -ах/-ях by stem hardness. Used *exclusively* after prepositions (в, на, о, по — detailed in Ch. 3). A
small set of masculine nouns has a special stressed "locative" ending in -у́ after specifically в/на (distinct
from the regular prepositional, used only for location: в лесу́ vs. о ле́се).

**Dative case** endings: -у/-ю (masc./neut.) or -е/-и (fem., by stem type). Uses: indirect object (recipient/
beneficiary); object of dative-governing verbs grouped by meaning (assistance/service, harm/annoyance,
permission/prohibition, emotion, teach/learn — with учи́ть "teach a subject" taking dative-on-subject vs.
accusative-on-subject when it means "study/learn"; and idiosyncratic verbs like звони́ть, отвеча́ть,
ве́рить with dative-vs.-prepositional-phrase meaning splits); after a few dative-governing adjectives
(благода́рный, ве́рный, подо́бный, ра́д); in impersonal constructions with a dative "experiencer" subject
— necessity (на́до, ну́жно, необходи́мо + infinitive), permission/prohibition (мо́жно, нельзя́, the latter
meaning "not permitted" with imperfective infinitives but "not possible" with perfective ones), predicate
neuter-short-form feeling/state words (ве́село, гру́стно, ску́чно, хо́лодно, etc.), and reflexive -ся verbs
expressing an inclination/disinclination beyond the subject's control (Са́ше хо́чется есть); to express age
(Отцу́ со́рок лет).

**Instrumental case** endings: -ом/-ем/-ём (masc./neut.) or -ой/-ей/-ёй/-ью (fem., the last for third-
declension -ь nouns), plural -ами/-ями (with an irregular -ьми́ plural for де́ти, лю́ди, and optionally
до́чь/дверь/ло́шадь). Uses: instrument/means of an action; agent in passive constructions (кни́га была́
напи́сана а́втором); the impersonal "force responsible" construction (кры́шу сорва́ло ве́тром); manner
(говори́ть шёпотом); similarity (петь соловьём "sing like a nightingale"); means of travel (as an
alternative to на + prepositional); route taken (шли́ ле́сом "went by way of the woods"); time expressions
for parts of the day and seasons (у́тром, зимо́й — themselves the source of many time adverbs in Ch. 5);
after a small set of instrumental-governing adjectives (дово́льный, бога́тый, изве́стный, за́нятый,
бере́менная, больно́й) and verbs (grouped as "interest/attitude" — интересова́ться, увлека́ться,
занима́ться, горди́ться — and "control/direction/use" — руководи́ть, владе́ть, управля́ть, по́льзоваться);
as a predicate noun after бы́ть in non-present tenses/moods, and obligatorily so (over the nominative) when
the predicate noun denotes an occupation/function/status or is abstract/deverbal, and after synonyms of
бы́ть (явля́ться, станови́ться, каза́ться, счита́ться, остава́ться) and "work as" verbs (рабо́тать,
служи́ть, выступа́ть); as the second object of transitive verbs of naming/considering/electing/hiring
(называ́ть, счита́ть, выбира́ть, нанима́ть).

**Personal names.** Russians have first name + patronymic (formed with -ович/-евич for sons, -овна/-евна
for daughters, from the father's first name) + family name. First-name-plus-patronymic is the polite/formal
address form. Native first names/patronymics decline as regular nouns; foreign first names decline like
masculine nouns if male but are indeclinable if female (unless ending in -а/-я, which decline as feminine).
Family names in -ин/-ов/-ев/-ёв have a mixed noun/adjective declension; family names in -ий/-ой decline
fully as adjectives (covered further in Ch. 5).

### Chapter 3 — Prepositions

Russian prepositions govern one, two, or occasionally three different cases depending on the meaning
intended, unlike English prepositions (which always take the same case, the "objective" case, on
pronouns). The same English preposition (e.g. "for") often maps to several distinct Russian prepositions
depending on sense.

- **Nominative-governing:** в (in fixed idioms like пойти́ в солда́ты "become a soldier") and за (in the
  fixed interrogative/exclamatory Что́ э́то за …? / Что́ за …!).
- **Accusative-only:** про (colloquial "about"; "to oneself" with себя́), че́рез (across/through a place; via
  an intermediary; "in X time" before an action; "every Xth unit" with ка́ждый; "every other unit"), сквозь
  (through, implying an obstruction).
- **Accusative in one sense, genitive-like meanings via о/по/с:** о/об ("against" in a physical-impact
  sense), по (up to-and-including a point in time/space; distributive "each" with 2–4 + genitive noun, vs.
  dative with оди́н), с (approximate measurement — "about a kilo/minute/week").
- **в/на governing accusative (motion, answers "Куда́?") vs. prepositional (location, answers "Где́?"):**
  the same two prepositions switch case according to whether the co-occurring verb expresses motion or
  static location — a governing mechanism that recurs with за/под (accusative for motion, instrumental for
  location) and is the single biggest "which case do I use" decision point in the book. в/на in the
  accusative also cover: a clock hour, an age, a day/part of day, "per unit of time," plus a set of в +
  accusative-governing verbs (ве́рить в, вме́шиваться в, вступа́ть в, игра́ть в, превраща́ть(ся) в) and на +
  accusative-governing verbs (влия́ть на, жа́ловаться на, крича́ть на, наде́яться на, рассчи́тывать на,
  серди́ться на, смотре́ть на).
- **за/под governing accusative (motion) vs. instrumental (location):** за additionally covers "in
  exchange for" (with a set of за-governing verbs: благодари́ть, хвали́ть, руга́ть, нака́зывать — "for X"),
  "on behalf of," "feel something for someone," "in favor of," "within a time span" (perfective aspect),
  "after/over an age," "X time before an event" (combined with до + genitive), "at a distance" (combined
  with от + genitive), and "by" with seize/lead/pull verbs; под additionally covers "approaching a time/
  age" and "to the accompaniment of."
- **Genitive-only core prepositions:** без (without; also "to the hour" in telling time), для (for the benefit
  of/for the purpose of — a near-synonym of the bare dative), до (before/until a time; up to a place, often
  with a до- prefixed verb), из (from/out of with motion — paired with в + accusative for the reverse
  direction; source of information/origin/material; "one of a group"; "consisting of"; cause/reason), из-за
  (from behind; "because of" an undesirable cause), из-под (from under; container-purpose idiom: ба́нка
  из-под варе́нья "jam jar"), кро́ме (except/besides), ми́мо (past), о́коло (near; "around/about" a
  quantity), от (point of origin/source — paired with в-accusative or к-dative destinations; "away from" —
  often with a verb prefixed от-; a large set of от-governing verbs of defense/riddance/differentiation
  — защища́ть(ся), избавля́ть(ся), освобожда́ть(ся), отка́зываться, отключа́ться, отлича́ться,
  пря́таться — "from X to Y" combined with до; distance-from-a-point combined with за/в; "the key/
  lid/button to X"; "medicine/insurance against X" — a near-synonym of про́тив; the cause of a physical/
  emotional reaction — a near-synonym of colloquial с), по́сле (after), про́тив (against; opposite), с/со
  (from a place — paired with на-accusative/на-prepositional counterparts; "off/down from" a surface;
  colloquial causal "from/out of," with a colloquial -у genitive ending; "since/from" in time, paired with до),
  у (by/at a place; "at someone's place," like French chez; possession — the standard way to express "to
  have" in Russian, with a set of у-governing source verbs: брать, занима́ть, отнима́ть, спра́шивать,
  проси́ть, покупа́ть).
- **Other, more literary/written-style genitive prepositions:** близ, вблизи́ (от), ввиду́, вдоль, вме́сто,
  вне, внутри́ (location) vs. внутрь (motion), возле, вокру́г, впереди́, вро́де, ме́жду (rare in genitive —
  usually instrumental), напро́тив, пове́рх, позади́, посреди́, путём, ра́ди, сверх, сзади, среди́.
- **Prepositional-only:** при (under the reign of/in the time of; affiliated with/administered by an
  institution; in the presence of; "given" a condition).
- **в/на/о/по governing prepositional case in additional senses:** в — location (vs. accusative for
  motion); what someone is wearing; a time period (month/semester/year/century/life-stage); "half past"
  the hour (an exception to telling-time-uses-accusative); distance-from-a-point (combined with от); a set
  of в-governing verbs (нужда́ться, обвиня́ть, отка́зывать, подозрева́ть, разбира́ться, сомнева́ться,
  убежда́ть(ся), уверя́ть, уча́ствовать). на — location on a surface, and, notably, an entire closed class of
  nouns (student/teacher activities; social/entertainment venues; work/trade/meeting venues; compass
  points; natural formations; specific geographic place names; cityscape features; weather/nature
  conditions) that idiomatically require на rather than в for location and must simply be memorized (e.g.
  на ле́кции, на да́че, на вокза́ле, на восто́ке, на бе́регу, на у́лице); means of transport (synonym of the
  instrumental); "this/next/last week." о (об, обо) — "about, concerning" (a synonym of colloquial про +
  accusative). по — "upon" a completed action (formal/written style, deverbal nouns).
- **Dative-only:** благодаря́ (thanks to), вопреки́ (contrary to/despite), к (toward, spatially and
  temporally; to a person's place, after a motion verb; "feelings toward" nouns — дове́рие, интере́с,
  любо́вь, сла́бость, стра́сть, уваже́ние; emotion-noun idioms like к сожале́нию/к сча́стью; a set of
  к-governing verbs, mostly prefixed при- — гото́виться, обраща́ться, относи́ться, подходи́ть,
  приближа́ться, привыка́ть, прикле́ивать, прилипа́ть, принадлежа́ть, пришива́ть), навстре́чу (toward,
  to meet), согла́сно (in accordance with).
- **по governing dative:** along/down a line (synonym of instrumental "route taken"); around/throughout
  (movement in various directions); "on the surface" (with hit/strike verbs); according to/by (rules,
  schedule, subject arrangement); by a communication medium (по телефо́ну, по по́чте, по ра́дио); in a
  field/on a subject/by (profession, descent); recurring days/periods (по сре́дам); distributive "one each"
  (higher numerals — по одному́); cause (по глу́пости, по боле́зни).
- **Instrumental-only:** над (over/above, also figuratively with ду́мать над, издева́ться над, рабо́тать
  над, смея́ться над), пе́ред (in front of, spatially; "immediately before," temporally, contrasted with до +
  genitive's less-precise "before").
- **за/ме́жду/под/с governing instrumental (location/accompaniment sense):** за — location (vs.
  accusative for motion); "during/at/over" a meal; goal of a motion verb (пойти́ за хле́бом "go for
  bread"), with a set of за-governing verbs (наблюда́ть, сле́довать, следи́ть); ме́жду — between/among
  (normal case for this preposition, despite the rare genitive use noted in Ch. 3's earlier section); под —
  below/beneath (location, vs. accusative for motion), including figurative uses (под её влия́нием, под
  псевдони́мом); "with a sauce"; "in the environs of"; с — "together with" (with a set of с-governing verbs
  — встреча́ться, знако́миться, расстава́ться, сове́товаться); an accompanying attribute; manner;
  congratulatory formulas (С Но́вым го́дом!).

### Chapter 4 — Pronouns

**Personal pronouns** (я, ты, он/оно/она, мы, вы, они) decline through all six cases; third-person forms
take a prosthetic н- after most prepositions (него́, ней, них) but not when functioning as possessives. The
ты/вы distinction encodes informal/formal register (family, peers, children ↔ authority figures,
first-time acquaintances, formal address by foreigners), and correlates with Здра́вствуй/Здра́вствуйте.
The "Как тебя́/вас зову́т?" construction literally means "how do they call you," with the addressee as a
grammatical accusative object, and follows a general word-order principle (given/"old" information
precedes new information).

**Reflexive personal pronoun себя́** (oneself) occurs in 5 of 6 cases (no nominative — it can never be a
grammatical subject), is invariant for gender/number, and reflects back to the clause's subject; used with
transitive verbs that aren't inherently reflexive (true reflexives use -ся instead). Also occurs in a set of
idioms (вести́ себя́ "behave," чу́вствовать себя́ "feel," вы́йти из себя́ "lose one's temper," etc.).

**Reciprocal pronoun друг дру́га** ("each other") has an undeclined first part and a declined second part
(accusative = genitive, as an animate masculine noun); a preposition, when present, sits between the two
parts (друг с дру́гом, друг о дру́ге). Like себя́, it has no nominative form and is not used with inherently
reciprocal verbs (which take -ся instead).

**Interrogative pronouns кто́/что́** decline through all six cases; кто́ triggers masculine-singular predicate
agreement even for female referents and can substitute for English "what" when asking about profession/
descent (Кто она́ по профе́ссии?); что́ triggers neuter-singular agreement.

**Possessive pronouns** мой/твой/наш/ваш and interrogative чей agree in gender/number/case with the
modified noun (mixed pronoun-type declension, with masc. animate accusative = genitive). Third-person
его́/её/их (identical in form to the genitive of он/она/они) are invariable and never carry the initial
н- that personal pronouns take after prepositions. The reflexive possessive свой ("one's own") declines
like мой but refers back to the clause's own subject; it is obligatory (not optional) when subject and
possessor are third-person and identical, is disallowed when subject+possessor are conjoined in one noun
phrase, and cannot cross into a different clause than its subject's — this last rule can disambiguate
sentences that are genuinely ambiguous in English translation.

**Demonstrative pronouns э́тот/тот** decline as adjective-type pronouns; э́тот is the more general
"this/that," тот implies greater remoteness or is used contrastively (то́т же (самый) "the very same"; и
то́, и друго́е "both"; не тот "the wrong one"). The invariable pronoun э́то (identical in form to э́тот's
neuter) functions either as an "identifying" subject (Что́ э́то? Э́то часы́ — with the *following* noun,
not э́то, controlling verb agreement) or a "characterizing" subject referring to a whole prior situation
(with neuter-singular verb agreement and, if a noun predicate follows, usually instrumental case). The
pronoun тако́й ("such a," declines like a hard-stem adjective) either points to a quality or intensifies a
following adjective ("so").

**Determinative pronouns:** сам ("-self," emphatic, stressed mostly on the ending) emphasizes
independent/unassisted action by a subject; са́мый ("the very," stressed on the stem, declines like a
hard-stem adjective) marks a precise point, forms the compound superlative (Ch. 5), and combines with
тот (тот (же) са́мый "the (very) same"); весь ("whole/all/everything/everyone," an irregular declension)
means "the whole" as a singular modifier, "all" as a plural modifier, and "everything"/"everyone" (the
latter grammatically plural, unlike English "everyone") when standing alone.

**Relative pronoun кото́рый** (declines like a hard-stem adjective) takes its gender/number from its noun
antecedent but its case from its function inside the relative clause; unlike English, it can never be
omitted, its clause is always comma-set-off, and a governing preposition can never "dangle" — it must
stay adjacent to кото́рый at the clause's start. The pronoun како́й, when used relatively (rather than as an
interrogative), can pair with intensifying тако́й (тако́й (же) …, како́й "the (same) kind of … that"). кто́/
что́ can also function relatively, typically with a demonstrative/quantifier antecedent (тот кто́ "he
who," все кто́ "everyone who," то́ что́ "that which"), and — with plural antecedents like те́, кто́ / все́,
кто́ — take optional singular-or-plural agreement.

**Indefinite pronouns** attach unstressed -то or -нибудь to кто́/что́/како́й/чей. -то marks a definite but
unnamed referent (past tense or non-repeated present); -нибудь marks a genuinely unidentified referent
(questions, imperatives, future tense, repeated present actions) — critically, neither can appear in a
*negated* sentence, where Russian instead requires the negative pronouns in ни-.

**Negative pronouns.** никто́/ничто́/никако́й/ниче́й (built with the prefix ни-) always co-occur with a
negated predicate (не), producing a grammatical "double negative"; ничто́'s accusative is always
identical to its genitive. A separate, stressed-prefix (не́-) series — не́кого/не́чего (there's-no-one-to/
there's-nothing-to) — occurs only in impersonal infinitive constructions, has no nominative, places any
governing preposition between не́ and the pronoun (written as three words but pronounced with one
stress), and expresses the doer of the action, if any, via the dative case; past/future are formed with
бы́ло/бу́дет.

### Chapter 5 — Adjectives and Adverbs

**Qualitative vs. relational adjectives.** Qualitative adjectives (size, color, taste, temperature, and other
gradable properties) have both long and short forms, form comparatives/superlatives, and derive adverbs.
Relational adjectives (indicating "of/from/connected with," e.g. materials, place, time) exist only in a
long, attributive-only form with no comparison and no derived adverb.

**Long-form declension classes,** determined by the adjective's stem-final consonant: hard-stem (но́вый,
stressed either on the stem or, for a subset, on the ending — молодо́й — which changes only the
masc./neut. nom./inan.-acc. ending -ый vs. -о́й); soft-stem in -н (си́ний — mostly time/place adjectives:
весе́нний, ле́тний, за́втрашний, ве́рхний, вну́тренний, etc.); stems in -г/-к/-х (subject to Spelling Rule 1
throughout the plural and some singular forms, e.g. ру́сский, дорого́й); and stems in the unpaired -ж/-ч/
-ш/-щ (subject to both Spelling Rule 1 and Spelling Rule 3, e.g. хоро́ший, большо́й). All long-form
adjectives follow the animate-accusative-equals-genitive rule for masculine singular and all-gender plural.
An adjective modifying a "naturally masculine" -а/-я noun (де́душка, мужчи́на) must itself show
masculine agreement even though the noun's own endings look feminine.

**Short form.** Derived from the long-form stem (masc. = bare stem, often with an inserted -е- or -о-
vowel to break a final consonant cluster; fem. -а, neut. -о, plural -ы/-и). Relational adjectives (including
all -ск-/-ян- adjectives) have no short form. Predicative use only — never attributive, never declined for
case, only for gender/number, with бы́л/была́/бы́ло/бы́ли marking the past tense and бу́дет/бу́дут the
future. The choice between short and long predicate forms is governed by several factors: (i) largely
style (short = more formal/written, long = more informal/spoken); (ii) for some adjectives, short = a
temporary state vs. long = an inherent property (бо́лен "sick right now" vs. больно́й "chronically ill");
(iii) for a set of size/age adjectives, short = "excessive for a given purpose" vs. long = simply describing
the property (велика́ "too big [for someone]" vs. больша́я "big"); (iv) for a few adjectives, short and long
forms diverge in meaning entirely (жив "alive" vs. живо́й "lively"; смешо́н "ridiculous" vs. смешно́й
"funny"); (v) with subjects это/всё/что́, the predicate adjective must be neuter short form; (vi) a
predicate adjective followed by a complement (infinitive, prepositional phrase, or oblique-case noun) must
be in the short form — this covers a large functional vocabulary of short-form-only or short-form-favoring
adjectives each governing a specific case/preposition (гото́в к/на, до́лжен, наме́рен, рад, свобо́ден,
спосо́бен, скло́нен; бли́зок с, винова́т в/пе́ред, знако́м с, похо́ж на, равноду́шен к, серди́т на, силён в,
согла́сен с/на; благода́рен, бога́т, дово́лен, досто́ин, по́лон, пре́дан). Corresponding to long/short
adjective forms are two parallel sets of "what kind" pronouns: како́й (declined, modifies) vs. како́в (short/
caseless, predicative-only), and тако́й vs. тако́в; and intensifiers тако́й (agrees, intensifies a long-form
adjective) vs. так (intensifies a short-form adjective/adverb), and exclamatory како́й (+ long form) vs. как
(+ short form).

**Adjectives used as nouns.** A noun-governing word can be omitted, leaving an "adjectival noun" whose
gender/number is still determined by the implicit noun (ру́сский "a Russian [man/person]," declines as
a normal long-form adjective). Grouped by the implied noun: person (больно́й, знако́мый, вое́нный,
рабо́чий, учёный, портно́й), room (ва́нная, гости́ная, столо́вая, де́тская), shop (бу́лочная,
парикма́херская, пивна́я), animal/dish/drink/money categories (живо́тное, второ́е, шампа́нское,
моро́женое, чаевы́е), etc. A separate class of *neuter singular* adjectival nouns carries abstract meaning
without any specific omitted noun (но́вое "the new," ста́рое "the old"), often appearing in the genitive
after quantity words in a partitive sense (Что́ но́вого? "What's new?"), and can derive from participles
(забы́тое "what is forgotten").

**Possessive adjectives.** Animal-derived possessive-relational adjectives of the пти́чий type (волк→
во́лчий, соба́ка→соба́чий) follow a distinct mixed declension and appear in fixed colloquial idioms
(волчий аппетит, собачий холод). A separate colloquial possessive-adjective type adds the suffix -ин to
kinship terms and familiar first names (ма́мин, па́пин, ба́бушкин, Са́шин), with a mixed noun/adjective
declension; family names in -ин/-ов/-ев/-ёв historically derive from this same possessive pattern and
share its mixed declension, while family names in -ий/-ой decline as full adjectives. Foreign names follow
the same declinability rules noted in Ch. 2 (decline if masculine or ending -а/-я; indeclinable if feminine
and consonant-final).

**Comparison — compound.** The compound comparative = indeclinable бо́лее/ме́нее + the (declined)
long-form adjective, used mainly attributively; a few common adjectives (большо́й, ма́ленький, плохо́й,
хоро́ший, молодо́й, ста́рый, высо́кий, ни́зкий) instead have (or additionally have) a dedicated
comparative/near-comparative adjective (бо́льший, ху́дший, лу́чший, мла́дший/ста́рший, вы́сший/
ни́зший) whose meaning can diverge from a literal comparative (мла́дший = "junior in rank" as well as
"younger").

**Comparison — simple.** A single-word form with the ending -ее (poetic/colloquial -ей) for most
adjectives, stress-neutral for polysyllabic stems; -е for a smaller set with a predictable consonant mutation
(г/к/х→ж/ч/ш; -ст→щ) or an irregular/must-memorize stem change (бли́зкий→бли́же, дешёвый→
деше́вле, хоро́ший→лу́чше, плохо́й→ху́же, большо́й→бо́льше, ма́ленький→ме́ньше, etc.); used almost
exclusively predicatively and is invariant for gender/number; can occur attributively in colloquial speech,
always postposed to the noun, often with the "slightly" prefix по-. Comparatives are frequently intensified
by ещё ("even more"), гора́здо/намно́го ("much"), or repeated/preceded by всё ("more and more").

**Second term of comparison.** Usually с че́м (comma-preceded), obligatory when the compound
comparative is used or the second term is indeclinable (adverb, infinitive, prepositional phrase, clause);
otherwise a declinable noun/pronoun may instead go into the bare genitive case, but only after a simple
predicative comparative with the first term in the nominative (Ви́ктор умне́е Вади́ма). Fixed comparative
idioms: как мо́жно + comparative ("as … as possible"); чем …, тем … ("the more …, the more …"); бо́льше
всего́/бо́льше всех, лу́чше всего́/лу́чше всех (superlative-adverb equivalents, "of all things"/"of all
people").

**Compound superlative** = са́мый (declined, agrees) + the long-form adjective, both agreeing with the
modified noun; often paired with a scope phrase (в ми́ре, из всех …).

**Simple superlative** ("high-degree" superlative, common in scientific/literary style but also used
colloquially for emphasis) adds -ейший (or, after a г/к/х/ст-mutating stem, -айший) to the long-form
adjective stem — distinct in meaning from the compound superlative (a "most X" one of many vs. simply
"an extremely X" one).

**Adverbs.** Classified by meaning: manner (formed from the neuter short-form adjective ending in -о;
from present-active-participle-derived adjectives with -е; from -ск-/-цк- adjectives with -и, often paired
with по-; from nationality adjectives as по-ру́сски-type forms; from a small set of по- + dative-form
adjectives, e.g. по-но́вому; and a few noun-derived adverbs like пешко́м, верхо́м, босико́м); time (when?,
how long?, how often?, including a systematic instrumental-case-of-a-noun pattern for parts of
day/seasons: у́тром, ле́том); place (a fully parallel location/direction-to/direction-from triad for most
place adverbs: где́/куда́/отку́да, здесь/сюда́/отсю́да, etc.); measure/degree (о́чень vs. мно́го is a
notable near-synonym pair split by whether the sense is intensity-of-an-action vs. quantity). Indefinite and
negative adverbs mirror the pronoun system exactly: -то/-нибудь particles on где́/куда́/когда́/как/почему́
follow the same definite-vs-indefinite-referent and tense/mood-based distribution rules as the indefinite
pronouns; никогда́-type negative adverbs (ни- prefix) require double negation with a personal-sentence
subject, while не́когда-type negative adverbs (stressed не́- prefix) occur only in impersonal infinitive
constructions, exactly parallel to не́кого/не́чего in Ch. 4.

### Chapter 6 — Numbers, Dates, and Time (opening: cardinal numbers only, through this file's page cutoff)

Russian has three number classes — cardinal, collective, and ordinal — all of which decline (collective
and ordinal are covered in the sibling part2 file, which continues past this file's page-180 cutoff).
Several cardinal numbers are morphologically composite: 11–19 = digit + -на- ("on") + -дцать (a
contraction of де́сять "ten"); 20/30 = "two/three tens"; 300/400 and 500–900 = digit + a genitive form of
сто "hundred." Numbers above 20 ending in a non-zero digit (21, 33, 101, etc.) are written as separate
words. Both но́ль and ну́ль ("zero/nought") are end-stressed masculine nouns governing the genitive case,
but have distinct conventional spheres: ну́ль for temperature, но́ль for phone numbers/scores/24-hour-
clock times.

The number **оди́н** ("one") declines exactly like the demonstrative pronoun э́тот, dropping its medial
-и- in every case but the masc. nom./inan.-acc.; as a modifier it agrees in gender/number/case with its
noun (including in compounds like два́дцать оди́н where the noun still triggers singular agreement).
Beyond the literal count "one," оди́н also means "a/a certain," "alone/by oneself," "the same," and (rare)
"only." It occurs in the *plural* when modifying a plural-only noun (одни́ часы́ "one watch") or in the
idiomatic "nothing but" sense (одни́ кро́шки "nothing but crumbs"), and forms the correlative pair
оди́н … друго́й … / одни́ … други́е … ("one … the other …" / "some … others …").

**Два́/две́, три́, четы́ре** are the only numbers with gender variation (два́ masc./neut. vs. две́ fem.; три́/
четы́ре invariant across genders) and share one declension pattern (nom./inan.-acc. база forms; anim.-
acc. = genitive; genitive дву́х/трёх/четырёх; instrumental двумя́/тремя́/четырьмя́). In the nominative or
inanimate accusative, 2/3/4 (and any compound ending in them) take the following noun in the *genitive
singular*; in the *animate* accusative, the "animate accusative = genitive" rule applies to both the
number itself and the noun, with the noun additionally forced into the plural (dvух ма́льчиков и трёх
де́вочек).

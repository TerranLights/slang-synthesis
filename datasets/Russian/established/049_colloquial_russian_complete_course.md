# Colloquial Russian: The Complete Course for Beginners — Part 1

**Source:** Svetlana le Fleming & Susan E. Kay, *Colloquial Russian: The Complete Course for
Beginners* (Routledge, 2nd ed. 1997, reprinted 1998). ISBN 0-415-16140-1.

**Coverage this file:** front-matter alphabet/pronunciation section ("Introduction to the Russian
language," printed pp. 3–11) + Lessons 1–3 ("Аэропорт. Паспортный контроль" / "Кто такой Пётр
Грин? Я говорю по-русски" / "Гостиница «Россия». Где буфет?", printed pp. 12–37). PDF pages 9–43
of the source file. Vision-read in full (scanned image-only PDF, no text layer — confirmed via
`pdftotext` returning empty output). One page per rendered image (confirmed empirically at PDF
pages 1, 9, 18, 26 against the printed running-head page numbers before committing to the reading
plan — no page-spread issue here, unlike *Colloquial Serbian*).

**Relationship to other established/ files:** This is a different book from `010`/`011`
(*Colloquial Russian 2*, Le Fleming & Kay, Routledge 2003 — the intermediate/upper sequel). This
volume is the true beginner-level "Complete Course," so its early lessons (present tense, gender,
basic cases) are genuinely first-exposure material for this project's Russian corpus rather than a
redundant supplement — no non-redundant-supplement dispatch note needed for these lessons. Later
lessons in this book may begin to overlap 001–024 (the full-length reference grammars); this will
be flagged per-lesson in the continuation files (049b onward) where it applies.

**Vision-reading guard:** No handwritten marginalia observed in any page read for this file — all
content is consistent typeset print. The one non-standard visual element (the sample Russian visa
form, p.5/PDF pg-021) is itself printed exam-style facsimile artwork, not reader annotation, and is
noted as such in its vocabulary entries below (`source_type: grammar_reference`, not `dictionary`,
since it's a realia specimen embedded in a teaching text).

**Copyright discipline:** Vocabulary glosses and short illustrative example phrases only — dialogue
texts, exercises, and grammar explanations are paraphrased, not reproduced verbatim.

## Grammar points

### Alphabet, pronunciation, and stress (Introduction to the Russian language, pp. 3–11)
- Full 33-letter Cyrillic alphabet with closest-English-equivalent glosses (paraphrased from the
  letter-by-letter table, p.3–4); ъ (hard sign) and ь (soft sign) carry no sound of their own — ь
  palatalizes the preceding consonant, ъ marks a slight break before a following iotated vowel.
- **Stress and vowel reduction**: stress is phonemic and can move between forms of the same word
  (noun singular/plural, different verb persons); unstressed **o** reduces to a weak "a" (word-
  initial or immediately pre-stress) or a still-weaker neutral "e"-like sound elsewhere; unstressed
  **е**/**я** weaken toward "и," and toward a neutral "e" word-finally.
- **ы** has no close English equivalent — pronounced like "i" in "sit" with the tongue drawn back.
- Consonant notes: р is rolled; т/д/н are dental (tongue against the teeth, not alveolar as in
  English); в resembles English v but with the lower lip behind the upper teeth; л is "dark" (back
  of tongue low); ш/ж resemble "sh"/"s in treasure" but with the jaw extended.
- **Word-final devoicing**: voiced consonants (д, г, в, з, ж, б) are pronounced as their voiceless
  counterparts (т, к, ф, с, ш, п respectively) at the end of a word, regardless of spelling
  (шокола́д → pronounced -т; друг → -к; etc.).
- **Consonant-cluster assimilation**: in a cluster of two+ consonants, the *last* consonant's
  voicing determines the pronunciation of the whole cluster — even across a word boundary with no
  pause (футбо́л → "фудбо́л"; в кино́ → "ф кино́").
- A handwritten-cursive alphabet chart is also present (p.9–10) — genuinely printed instructional
  content (a licensed "Russian Cursive" font per the acknowledgements), not reader marginalia.

### Lesson 1 — Copula, articles, questions, gender, possessives, adjectives, cases (intro)
- **Omission of "to be"**: present-tense "am/are/is" has no equivalent word; a dash can stand
  between two nouns (Моя́ фами́лия — Грин).
- **No articles**: no word for "a/an" or "the"; я англича́нин can mean either "I am an/the
  Englishman" depending on context.
- **Interrogatives**: formed purely by intonation or a question mark in writing — no do-support.
- **Grammatical gender**: three genders; consonant-final nouns are usually masculine, -а/-я usually
  feminine, -о/-е neuter; -ь nouns must be learned individually (e.g. пло́щадь f., Кремль m.).
- **Possessive adjectives** мой/моя́/моё and ваш/ва́ша/ва́ше agree with the gender of the noun
  possessed (not the possessor).
- **Adjective agreement**: -ый (masc.) / -ая (fem.) / -ое (neut.); spelling rule: -ий replaces -ый
  after к, г, х, ж, ш, щ, ч (Моско́вский, ру́сский, англи́йский).
- ру́сский/ру́сская can function as both adjective and noun ("a Russian man/woman").
- Predicative neuter-adjective-as-adverb pattern: хорошо́ "it is good," интере́сно "it is
  interesting."
- **Six-case system introduced** (nominative, accusative, genitive, dative, instrumental,
  prepositional). Nominative: dictionary form, subject, complement of "to be." Prepositional:
  used after в (here, "in"); singular ending typically -е (regardless of gender, for most nouns);
  vowel-final and -ь-final masculine nouns also take -е (Москва́ → в Москве́, Кремль → в Кремле́).

### Lesson 2 — Pronouns, verb conjugation, plural, adjectives, adverbs, cases, ho/a
- **Personal pronouns**: я/ты/он-она́-оно́/мы/вы/они́. вы is used both as true plural "you" and as
  the polite singular "you" (even addressing one person, verb agreement stays plural) — explicit
  **T-V distinction** (ты = familiar singular only, comparable to French *tu*).
- **Present tense, two conjugations**: 1st conj. (-ать infinitives typically, e.g. рабо́тать) takes
  -ю/-ешь/-ет/-ем/-ете/-ют; 2nd conj. (-ить/-еть infinitives, e.g. говори́ть, смотре́ть) takes
  -ю/-ишь/-ит/-им/-ите/-ят (drop last three letters of infinitive, not just -ть). Russian has only
  one present tense (гово́рю = both "I speak" and "I am speaking").
- **Spelling rule**: ы never follows г, к, х, ж, ч, ш, щ — и is written instead. This single rule
  governs plural noun endings, adjective endings, and verb endings alike.
- **Noun plurals**: masculine consonant-stem and feminine -a nouns take -ы, or -и if the preceding
  letter falls under the spelling rule (журна́л→журна́лы but язы́к→языки́, тури́стка→тури́стки);
  -ь nouns also plural in -и.
- **Indeclinable nouns** (usually loanwords): ра́дио, метро́, кино́ — same form in every case and
  number, marked "indeclinable" in dictionaries.
- **Adjective plural spelling**: -ий/-ие type after the spelling-rule consonants vs. regular
  -ый/-ые; feminine -ая/neuter -ое unaffected; stress-final adjectives take -о́й (masc.) rather
  than -ый (како́й).
- **Adverbs**: formed by replacing the adjective ending with -о (бы́стрый→бы́стро); same form used
  for "it is X" predicatives.
- мо́жно/на́до: impersonal modal predicatives + infinitive ("it is possible/necessary to...").
- **Accusative case**: marks the direct object; for many nouns (inanimate masc., most neuters, and
  in the plural for the nouns shown) accusative = nominative form; full treatment deferred to next
  lesson.
- но vs. а: но is stronger contrast ("but"); а is a softer contrast closer to "and" — used when
  comparing two different subjects (Пи́тер инжене́р, а Мари́на студе́нтка).

### Lesson 3 — Directions, hotel vocabulary, accusative, possessives, motion verbs
- **Gender exceptions by ending**: -й nouns are masculine (музе́й, чай); -е nouns are neuter
  (зда́ние, кафе́) *except* ко́фе, which is masculine by exception.
- **Spelling rule (o/e)**: unstressed о never follows ж, ч, ш, щ, ц — е is written instead; affects
  some neuter adjective endings.
- **Hard vs. soft adjectives**: typи́чный-type "hard" adjectives (-ый/-ая/-ое/-ые) vs. a smaller
  "soft" class like после́дний (-ий/-яя/-ее/-ие); ва́нная and дежу́рная are grammatically feminine
  adjectives used as nouns ("bathroom," "concierge").
- **Accusative case, systematically**: direct object case; neuter, inanimate masculine, and -ь
  feminine nouns are unchanged from nominative in the accusative; feminine -а nouns change to -у,
  -я nouns to -ю (доро́га→доро́гу, фами́лия→фами́лию).
- **Accusative after prepositions** че́рез "through," на/в "to, into" (motion) vs. the same
  prepositions + prepositional case for location (он в буфе́те "he is in/at the buffet" vs. он идёт
  в буфе́т "he is going to/into the buffet"). Generally на + accusative/prepositional is used for
  activities/events, в for buildings, with lexicalized exceptions (на по́чту/на по́чте "to/at the
  post office," despite по́чта being a building).
- **куда́ vs. где**: куда́ "where to" (motion) is grammatically distinct from где "where" (location);
  parallel pair туда́/сюда́ "(to) there/here" (motion) vs. там/здесь "there/here" (location).
- **Possessive adjectives, full set**: его́/её/их ("his/its," "her/its," "their") are invariant —
  unlike мой/твой/наш/ваш, they do **not** change to agree with the gender of the noun possessed.
  наш and твой decline in parallel with ваш and мой respectively (formal/informal pairing mirrors
  the вы/ты pairing).
- **Irregular 1st-conjugation present tense**: писа́ть (пишу́, пи́шешь — с→ш stem change) and
  идти́ (иду́, идёшь, ...идёт, stressed throughout, е→ё in most forms) given as irregular but
  still 1st-conjugation-pattern verbs.
- есть "there is/are" + nominative case.

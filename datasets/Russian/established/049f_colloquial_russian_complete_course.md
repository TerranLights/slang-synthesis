## Grammar points

### Lesson 11 — Imperfective vs. perfective aspect (systematic treatment)
- **Two infinitives per verb concept**: every Russian verb notion has (with rare truly-imperfective-
  only exceptions not discussed here) two infinitives — imperfective (чита́ть) and perfective
  (прочита́ть). Only the imperfective infinitive yields all three tenses (present, imperfective
  past, imperfective future = бу́ду + impf. infinitive); the perfective infinitive yields only two
  forms, the **perfective past** (formed exactly like the imperfective past, just off the
  perfective stem) and the **perfective future**, which — critically — is conjugated using
  ordinary present-tense-type personal endings even though its meaning is future (прочита́ю "I
  shall read/shall have read"), because Russian perfective verbs have no separate present tense at
  all; their "present-looking" conjugated form is inherently future in meaning. This is the
  clearest possible statement so far in this course of why aspect and tense are orthogonal
  categories in Russian — worth flagging strongly for mechanics-analysis as a model for a
  conlang-slang tense/aspect system that doesn't map 1:1 onto English tense.
- **Two formation patterns for perfective partners**: (a) prefixation — a large closed-ish list
  of common verbs (пить→вы́пить, писа́ть→написа́ть, ду́мать→подума́ть, etc.) where the perfective
  is simply the imperfective plus a fixed prefix, with **no other change to the conjugation**; (b)
  internal modification/suppletion-adjacent alternation — a productive pattern where the
  imperfective is 1st-conjugation -ать/-ять and the perfective partner is 2nd-conjugation -ить/-еть
  (вспомина́ть→вспо́мнить, встреча́ть→встре́тить, изуча́ть→изучи́ть, etc.), i.e. the aspectual
  contrast is carried by an entire conjugation-class switch, not a segmental affix. A further,
  fully irregular subset includes truly suppletive pairs: dать/prodа́ть (a unique irregular
  athematic-flavored conjugation: дам/дашь/даст/дади́м/дади́те/даду́т — mixing 1st- and
  2nd-conjugation-looking endings with a singular-plural stem alternation unlike any regular verb
  class) and, most strikingly, **говори́ть's two perfective partners depending on sense**:
  говори́ть "to say, tell" → сказа́ть (fully suppletive root), but говори́ть "to talk, speak" →
  поговори́ть (regular prefixation) — the same imperfective verb takes a different perfective
  according to which of its two senses is meant, a genuine case of aspect-pairing being sensitive
  to lexical polysemy rather than a fixed 1:1 lemma-to-lemma mapping.
- **Verbs of motion use their own perfective mechanism**: adding по- to the determinate (b) verb
  gives its perfective (пойти́, пое́хать, понести́, повести́, повезти́, побежа́ть, полете́ть),
  conjugated like the underlying (b) verb; пойти́'s perfective future (пойду́, пойдёшь…) is
  specifically noted as worth memorizing. This connects directly back to Lesson 10's
  indeterminate/determinate system — po- prefixation only ever perfectivizes the *determinate*
  member of a motion pair, never the indeterminate one, tying the two grammatical dimensions
  (aspect and motion-verb-type) together in a principled way.
- **Usage rules, imperfective** — four distinct triggering contexts, not one blanket "ongoing
  action" rule: (a) habitual/repeated action (Я смотре́ла ру́сский телеви́зор ка́ждый день); (b)
  unfinished/continuous action (Пи́тер смотре́л телеви́зор, no completion implied); (c) emphasis on
  the *process itself* rather than result (Он лю́бит смотре́ть телеви́зор); (d) **obligatorily**
  after verbs meaning "begin"/"finish"/"continue" (начина́ть, конча́ть, продолжа́ть) — these three
  verbs categorically select an imperfective complement infinitive, never perfective, regardless
  of whether the complement action will itself be completed.
- **Usage rules, perfective** — (a) a single action with emphasis on completion/result — and
  critically, Russian's perfective past is **tenseless with respect to English's perfect/pluperfect
  distinction**: Мари́на прочита́ла ну́жные статьи́ can mean either "has read" or "had read"
  depending purely on context, because **Russian has no pluperfect at all** — a genuine gap
  relative to English that a mechanics-analysis pass should note as a place where an invented
  language could go either way; (b) a series of actions each completed before the next begins (Я
  встала, приняла́ душ и оде́лась — three sequential perfectives, no overlap implied); (c) an
  **inchoative/inceptive sense with за-**: certain verbs (e.g. хоте́ть → захоте́ть) form a
  perfective specifically meaning "began to X" / "the desire for X arose" rather than the ordinary
  completive perfective sense — за- here is a distinct functional prefix from po-'s
  "did-for-a-while" sense below, both homophonous-looking prefixation strategies doing different
  aspectual work; (d) a **delimitative sense with по-**: verbs like сиде́ть form a perfective (по-
  сиде́ть) meaning "to do X for a (short, bounded) while" (Он посиде́л в па́рке) — again distinct
  from по-'s use perfectivizing motion verbs above; the same prefix po- is doing at least two
  unrelated aspectual jobs across this lesson's material (delimitative-duration on stationary verbs
  vs. plain perfectivization on motion verbs), a genuinely notable case of prefix polyfunctionality
  worth flagging for mechanics-analysis.
- **Present tense for an action begun in the past and still ongoing**: unlike English's present
  perfect continuous ("has been doing"), Russian simply uses the plain present tense — Уже́ четы́ре
  ме́сяца Мари́на собира́ет материа́лы "Marina has already been collecting material for four
  months" — frequently co-occurring with давно́ ("for a long time") as a marker of this
  construction (Мари́на давно́ интересу́ется образова́нием). A clean example of a target language
  collapsing an English aspectual distinction (simple present vs. present-perfect-continuous) that
  Russian doesn't grammaticalize at all.
- **Чтобы + infinitive** = "in order to," and is **grammatically obligatory** in Russian even in
  contexts where English idiomatically drops "in order" (Мари́на реши́ла поступи́ть в
  университе́т, что́бы стать учи́тельницей) — flagged explicitly by the source as a common
  learner-error trap (omitting чтобы because the English translation omits "in order").
- **Нельзя́ + aspect split**: an impersonal predicative meaning "one may not / it is impossible,"
  whose exact sense is disambiguated purely by the aspect of its infinitival complement — нельзя́
  + imperfective infinitive = "(this action generally) is forbidden" (нельзя́ посеща́ть шко́лу);
  нельзя́ + perfective infinitive = "(this specific action) is impossible" (нельзя́ посети́ть
  шко́лу). This is a second clean case (after ходи́ть/идти́'s figurative-collocation freezing) of
  aspect choice alone carrying an entire distinct semantic contrast with no other marker — directly
  relevant to how an invented slang system might repurpose an aspect-like marker to carry modal
  distinctions (permission vs. possibility) rather than pure time-reference.

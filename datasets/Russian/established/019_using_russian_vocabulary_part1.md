# Russian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Using Russian Vocabulary (Terence Wade) — Units 1–5, Part 1 of 3

**Source:** Terence Wade, *Using Russian Vocabulary* (Cambridge University Press, 2009,
ISBN 978-0-521-61236-4). PDF pages 1–215 of 636 (a book of 636 PDF pages, split into three
extraction chunks of ~215 pages each; this file covers the first chunk only). This range spans the
front matter, Introduction, and **Unit 1** (Towns and buildings. The household. Gardens, printed
pp. 3–31), **Unit 2** (The physical world, pp. 40–64), **Unit 3** (The human body and health, pp.
76–99), **Unit 4** (Appearance and movement, pp. 122–142), and **Unit 5** (Personality and human
behaviour, pp. 163–184, through Level 3, before its "Additional exercises"). Units 6–18 are covered
by sibling extraction files (PDF pages 216–430, 431–636).

**Coverage note.** This is a graded vocabulary-teaching textbook: each unit is organized into
thematic sub-topics at three ascending difficulty Levels (1/2/3), followed by ~60 graded exercises
per level. Per the coverage rule, **every distinct vocabulary item in each Level's word list was
extracted; the "Exercises" and "Additional exercises" blocks (drills reusing the same vocabulary)
were skipped** — they introduce no new lexical items. The book's own short prose asides under
"Word origins:", "Cognates:", "Word formation:", "Literary note:", etc. are **not vocabulary items**
in the coverage-rule sense (they're etymological/cultural commentary on words already listed) — per
copyright discipline these were paraphrased in brief, only where a specific fact was compact and
genuinely useful, and folded into the `Notes` column of the word(s) they concern, rather than
reproduced as continuous prose. No dialectal/regional annotation system (no `[B]`/`[C]`/`[S]`-style
tags) is used anywhere in this source — it is standard, register-neutral literary Russian
throughout, hence `Usage Tier` is `core` for the overwhelming majority of entries; the handful of
`technical.medical` (Unit 3) entries are marked accordingly.

**PDF-extraction gotcha found and decoded — single/multi-glyph Latin/Cyrillic homoglyph
substitution.** This PDF has a genuine text layer (not scanned/OCR), but a font-encoding quirk
(likely from the original Textures/LaserWriter → Acrobat Distiller 7.0.5 production pipeline)
renders **some instances of visually-identical Cyrillic letters as their Latin homoglyph
codepoints** instead of the correct Cyrillic codepoint — confirmed as a real, decodable
character-substitution cipher, not random OCR garbling. Verified against 30+ known word/gloss pairs
before trusting the decode (e.g. `здрaвомы́слящий` → `здравомыслящий` "sensible", `нaглый` →
`наглый` "insolent", `цeнтр` → `центр` "centre"). The affected letter pairs, in order of frequency:
Cyrillic а↔Latin a (8,026 instances in this page range) and Cyrillic е↔Latin e (7,557 instances),
plus much rarer uppercase confusions (О/O, В/B, С/C, М/M, Е/E, Х/X, А/A, Т/T, Н/H — under 200
instances combined). Decode method used: for every occurrence of a homoglyph-capable Latin letter,
if either horizontally-adjacent character (skipping intervening combining stress-mark U+0301) was
genuinely Cyrillic, the letter was corrected to its Cyrillic counterpart; isolated Latin letters
used as the book's own exercise-option labels (`a.`, `b.`, `c.` list markers) were left alone since
they sit at true word boundaries with no adjacent Cyrillic context. A handful of stragglers that
crossed a line-wrap (e.g. `Hевский` for `Невский`, where the stress mark on the following line broke
the adjacency check) were caught and corrected manually by cross-referencing known vocabulary
(Nevsky Prospekt). No handwritten marginalia applies here — this source has a real text layer, so
the vision-reading guard does not apply; `Vision Reading Confidence` is `n/a` throughout.

**Aspectual-pair/case-government notation.** The book marks verb aspect pairs with a slash
(`impf./pf.`, e.g. `лгать/со-` "to lie") and marks a verb's case/preposition government in
parentheses immediately after the headword (e.g. `боя́ться (+ gen.)` "to be afraid of",
`горди́ться (+ instr.)` "to be proud of"). These government markers are folded into the `Notes`
column rather than given a separate column, to stay within the spec's fixed column set.

## Grammar points

### Verb aspect and government notation used throughout the book

The book marks the aspect pair of a verb with a slash (`impf./pf.`), typically citing the
imperfective headword first followed by the perfective's differing prefix or ending (e.g.
`покупа́ть/купи́ть` "to buy", `лга́ть/со-` "to lie"). Case or preposition government is shown in
parentheses immediately after the headword (e.g. `боя́ться (+ gen.)` "to be afraid of",
`горди́ться (+ instr.)` "to be proud of", `завидовать (+ dat.)` "to envy"). A few verbs show split
government depending on meaning nuance — e.g. `страда́ть` "to suffer" takes instrumental for a
persistent condition but `от` + genitive for a temporary one (Unit 3), and `ревнова́ть` "to be
jealous" takes accusative of the offending partner but `к` + dative of the rival (Unit 5, p. 182).

### Indeterminate/determinate (multidirectional/unidirectional) verbs of motion

Several verb pairs distinguish habitual, multidirectional, or repeated motion from motion in one
direction at one time: `лета́ть` (to fly around/back-and-forth/habitually) vs. `лете́ть` (to be
flying along in one direction); similarly `пла́вать`/`плы́ть` "to swim/float", `по́лзать`/`ползти́`
"to crawl" (Unit 2, p. 43; Unit 4, p. 134). This is a core structural feature of Russian motion
verbs that recurs across units rather than being confined to one lesson.

### Compound motion verbs built on -ходи́ть/-йти́

Unit 4 (p. 124) presents a systematic prefixed series built on the "to go (on foot)" motion-verb
root: `обходи́ть/обойти́` "to go round" (takes a direct object — unlike the rest of the series),
`отходи́ть/отойти́` (`от` + genitive) "to move away from", `переходи́ть/перейти́` (accusative or
`че́рез` + accusative) "to cross", `подходи́ть/подойти́` (`к` + dative) "to approach", `проходи́ть/
пройти́` (`ми́мо` + genitive) "to go past". A buffer vowel `-o-` appears in the perfective's
conjugation only when the prefix ends in a consonant (`обойду́ обойдёшь`); when the prefix ends in
a vowel (`пере-`) the buffer vowel is absent (`перейду́ перейдёшь`, past `перешёл перешла́`). This
same prefix + case-government pattern productively generalizes to other prefixed motion-verb series
in the book (e.g. `-двига́ть(ся)/-дви́нуть(ся)`, `-ходи́ть` variants for turning/collision), which is
directly relevant to how a synthesized slang system could exploit prefix stacking for aspectual or
directional nuance.

### Deverbal/deadjectival noun and adjective suffix patterns

The book repeatedly calls out productive suffix patterns that this project's mechanics-analysis
phase may want to track as candidate slang-formation templates:
- **-льник/-лка/-ница** denote an instrument or container derived from a verb or noun root
  (`открыва́лка` "bottle-opener" from `открыва́ть`; `холоди́льник` "refrigerator" from `холоди́ть`;
  `са́харница`/`пе́речница`/`мы́льница` "sugar bowl/pepper-pot/soap dish", all `-ница` = "container").
- **-ость** is the default deadjectival abstract-noun suffix (`бере́менность` "pregnancy" from
  `бере́менная`), noted explicitly as a "Reminder" heading in Unit 5 Level 2 (p. 172).
- **-астый/-истый/-атый** denote possession of a prominent physical feature: `-астый` = marked
  possession (`груда́стый` "busty", `скула́стый` "high-cheekboned"), `-истый` = abundance
  (`плечи́стый` "broad-shouldered", `мускули́стый` "muscular"), `-атый` = simple possession
  (`волоса́тый` "hairy") (Unit 4, pp. 130–131).
- **без-/бес-** is a productive negative prefix on adjectives and nouns (`бессты́дный` "shameless",
  `безнра́вственный` "immoral", `бессо́нница` "insomnia"), explicitly diagrammed by the source as
  prefix + root + suffix + ending (Unit 5, p. 172) — captured above as a morpheme breakdown.
- **вз-/вс-** as a prefix denotes upward movement (`взъеро́шенный` "tousled" — lit. hair standing
  up) (Unit 4, p. 131).
- Deverbal nouns deaffixed directly from a verb stem (no suffix) are consistently **feminine**
  (`ма́зь` "ointment" from `ма́зать`; nouns in **-знь** likewise are all feminine, e.g. `боле́знь`
  "illness") (Unit 1, p. 6; Unit 3, p. 76).

### "False friends" (ло́жный дру́г) flagged by the source

The book explicitly flags a handful of Russian words that resemble but do not mean their English
lookalike: `симпати́чный` means "nice, likeable/attractive," not "sympathetic" (flagged twice,
Unit 4 p. 123 and Unit 5 p. 165); `спина́` means "back," not "spine" (the correct anatomical term is
`позвоно́чник` or, more specifically, `спинно́й хребе́т`, Unit 3 pp. 78, 95).

### Generic geographic/institutional terms take lower-case initial letters

Unlike English convention, a generic geographic or institutional common noun that forms part of a
proper name is spelled with a lower-case initial in Russian even when English would capitalize it:
`Кра́сная пло́щадь` ("Red Square"), `чёрное мо́ре` ("the Black Sea"), `инди́йский океа́н` ("the Indian
Ocean"), `о́зеро Байка́л` ("Lake Baikal") (Unit 1 p. 3; Unit 2 p. 41).

### Onomatopoeic origin as a recurring etymological category

The source repeatedly flags entire semantic clusters of animal-sound and human-reflex verbs as
onomatopoeic in origin: `мурлы́кать` "to purr," `лиза́ть` "to lick," `ворча́ть` "to growl,"
`жужжа́ть` "to buzz," `кукаре́кать` "to crow," `мыча́ть` "to low," `рыча́ть` "to roar," `фы́ркать`
"to snort," `щебета́ть` "to twitter" (Unit 2); `ка́шлять` "to cough" (from `ка́хи-ка́хи`), `чиха́ть`
"to sneeze" (from `чи́х`) (Unit 3); `сморка́ться` "to blow one's nose," `скре́жет` "grinding" (of
teeth) (Unit 4). This recurring pattern is worth tracking for the mechanics-analysis phase as a
possible productive source domain for slang coinage (sound-symbolic neologism).

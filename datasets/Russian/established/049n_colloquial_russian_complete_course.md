## Grammar points

### Lesson 19 — Passive participles, что́-то/что́-нибудь
- **Passive participles exist only for transitive verbs** (verbs capable of taking a direct
  object) — an important licensing restriction not present for the active participles of Lesson
  18, which could be formed from intransitive verbs too.
- **Past passive participle, -нный class**: normally formed from the **perfective** verb (not the
  imperfective, unlike active participles which could be formed from either aspect). Three
  sub-rules by infinitive shape: (1) most -ать/-ять infinitives replace -ть with -нный
  regardless of conjugation (прочита́ть→прочи́танный, потеря́ть→поте́рянный, and the irregular
  дать-family: прода́ть→про́данный); (2) 1st-conjugation verbs in -сти/-зти replace the 2nd-person-
  singular future-perfective ending -ешь with -ённый (принести́→принесёшь→принесённый, ввезти́→
  ввезёшь→ввезённый) — **except найти́→на́йденный**, an explicitly flagged irregular exception to
  this sub-rule; (3) 2nd-conjugation -ить/-еть verbs replace the 1st-person-singular future ending
  -у/-ю with -енный/-ённый (постро́ить→постро́ю→постро́енный, реши́ть→решу́→решённый), and
  **any 1st-singular consonant mutation carries through identically into the participle**
  (пригото́вить→пригото́влю→пригото́вленный [в-insertion], пригласи́ть→приглашу́→приглашённый
  [с→ш]) — **except уви́деть→уви́жу→уви́денный**, which keeps д rather than mutating to ж in the
  participle despite the 1st-singular form uви́жу showing the mutation, a further flagged
  irregularity. This three-way conditioning by infinitive shape, with irregular exceptions attached
  to two of the three sub-rules, makes the -нный participle one of the more procedurally intricate
  derivational rules covered in the course — worth flagging for mechanics-analysis as an example
  of a productive rule with a genuinely non-trivial "look at a different principal part depending
  on infinitive class" procedure, rather than a single uniform suffixation.
- **Past passive participle, -тый class**: a smaller, largely lexically-fixed set of 1st-conjugation
  verbs (закры́ть→закры́тый, откры́ть→откры́тый, забы́ть→забы́тый, уби́ть→уби́тый, вы́пить→
  вы́питый, оде́ть→оде́тый, взять→взя́тый, заня́ть→за́нятый, приня́ть→при́нятый, нача́ть→
  на́чатый) — mostly monosyllabic-root verbs or -нять-family verbs, forming a closed morphological
  class alongside the productive -нный pattern rather than a phonologically fully predictable rule.
- **Use of the past passive participle**: declines like a hard -ый adjective, agreeing with its
  antecedent noun exactly like active participles, and substitutes for a кото́рый-clause with a
  transitive verb where the antecedent is the **object**, not subject, of that verb (Я получи́л
  письмо́, напи́санное мое́й до́черью "I received a letter written by my daughter" — паралleling
  Я получи́л письмо́, кото́рое написа́ла моя́ дочь, where кото́рое is accusative object of написа́ла
  and до́черью [the agent] becomes instrumental once expressed via the participle instead of as the
  clause's own subject) — the agent of a past-passive-participle phrase is marked instrumental,
  exactly like the agent of the periphrastic passive voice below.
- **Short form of the past passive participle**: like short adjectives, has only four forms
  (masc./fem./neut./plural: прочи́танный→прочи́тан/прочи́тана/прочи́тано/прочи́таны — note **one
  of the doubled -нн- is dropped** in the short form, a spelling simplification specific to this
  participle class). The short form combines with the appropriate form of быть to build the
  **canonical Russian passive voice**: Письмо́ бу́дет по́слано "The letter will be sent"; Рабо́та
  сде́лана "The work is/has been done"; Зако́н был при́нят "The law was passed" — exactly parallel
  to the short-adjective-plus-быть pattern already used for temporary states (за́нят, бо́лен, etc.)
  in earlier lessons, confirming the short participle behaves grammatically as a subtype of short
  adjective throughout. As with short adjectives generally, **the short participle is only used
  where English "to be" sits between the subject and the participle** — attributive use (before its
  noun, as in "the law passed by the Soviet") instead requires the **long-form** participle
  (Зако́н, при́нятый Сове́том, гаранти́рует свобо́ду) — the identical long-form/attributive vs.
  short-form/predicative split already established for ordinary adjectives is thus shown to extend
  systematically to the entire participle system as well.
- **Alternative passive strategy — impersonal 3rd-person-plural with оно́ dropped**: Russian
  frequently expresses a passive-flavored meaning by simply using the active verb in the 3rd-person
  plural with no explicit subject at all (Приняли́ зако́н "The law was passed," literally "they
  passed the law," with no "they" ever named or intended referentially) — this impersonal-active
  strategy coexists with, and is at least as common as, the participle-plus-быть true passive, and
  is a useful reminder that "passive meaning" in Russian doesn't require passive morphology at all;
  a valid alternative strategy for a mechanics-analysis model of how an invented slang variety
  might avoid morphological passives altogether while still expressing agent-backgrounding.
- **Present passive participle**: formed by adding -ый to the 1st-person-plural present
  (чита́ть→чита́ем→чита́емый; несёт→несём→несо́мый, with -ём→-ом- when the 1pl. ending is
  stressed -ём). Several irregular 1st-conjugation verbs (пить, петь, брать, писа́ть) **have no
  present passive participle at all** — a genuine paradigm gap, not merely a rare form. Less common
  overall than the past passive participle, and **not normally used to form the present-tense
  passive voice** — for that, Russian instead uses either a reflexive verb in the present tense or
  the same impersonal 3rd-plural-without-oни́ strategy (кни́га чита́ется / чита́ют кни́гу "a book is
  being read"; дом стро́ится / стро́ят дом "a house is being built") — meaning the present passive
  participle's main surviving function is as a lexicalized ordinary adjective (люби́мый "favourite,"
  literally "being loved"; уважа́емый "respected") rather than active passive-voice formation, a
  parallel case of grammaticalized-participle-to-adjective drift alongside the active participles
  already noted lexicalizing in Lesson 18 (making this the second such documented cline in two
  consecutive lessons — a genuinely recurring diachronic pathway in this language worth treating as
  a systematic pattern, not a one-off).
- **Что́-то vs. что́-нибудь — a referential-specificity contrast, generalized across an entire
  pronoun/adverb series**: -то marks "something/someone/somewhere/etc. that genuinely exists, but
  whose identity is unknown or unstated" (a specific-but-unidentified referent); -нибудь marks
  "something/anyone/anywhere/etc. whose very existence is in doubt" (an indefinite, hypothetical,
  or free-choice referent) — Он тебе́ дал что́-то? presupposes he did give something and merely
  asks what; Он тебе́ дал что́-нибудь? merely asks whether he gave anything at all, with no
  presupposition that he did. This same -то/-нибудь contrast attaches productively to кто, где,
  куда́, когда́, како́й, and как, yielding seven fully parallel pairs, with кто-/что-/какой- forms
  declining exactly like their base interrogative pronoun/adjective. **Critically, neither -то nor
  -нибудь combines with a negated verb** — a negative sentence instead uses the dedicated никто́/
  ничто́/etc. negative-concord series from Lesson 15 (Я никого́ не ви́дел, not *я кого́-нибудь не
  ви́дел) — confirming that Russian now has **three** distinct systems covering "some/any/no"-type
  reference depending on polarity and specificity: -то (specific existential), -нибудь (non-specific/
  free-choice), and ни- (negative-concord, obligatory under negation) — a three-way split
  richer than English's rough some-/any-/no- system, and an excellent worked model for
  mechanics-analysis of how a conlang might grammaticalize specificity and polarity as two
  independent dimensions rather than collapsing them into a single indefinite-pronoun paradigm.

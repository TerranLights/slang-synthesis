## Grammar points

### §15.3 The Hyphen Used to Divide a Word at the End of a Line of Type (Перенос)

A purely typesetting-oriented spelling convention, since it belongs to typewritten/printed text.
Russian's line-division rules differ substantially from English's:

- **(a) Letter-count/type-based rules**: (i) never strand a single letter or an entire consonant
  cluster alone on either side of a break — стре-ла́ (not с-трела́/стр-ела́/стрел-а́); (ii) an entire
  consonant cluster at a syllable's end is likewise not left stranded on the first line —
  обра-зова́ние rather than обр-азова́ние; (iii) never separate a soft sign (ь), hard sign (ъ), or the
  letter й from the letter preceding it — нель-зя́, рай-о́н, вой-на́; (iv) double consonants between
  vowels ARE divided — колóн-на, апп-парáт (pp. 183).
- **(b) Morphology-aware rules**: if a word's morphemes (морфемы) are clearly separable, divide at
  the morpheme boundary rather than by the letter-count rules above — hyphenate between prefix and
  root, or suffix and root (still respecting the no-stranded-single-letter/cluster constraint): (i)
  avoid carrying a prefix's final consonant over with the root — рас-писа́ние (not ра-списа́ние); (ii)
  avoid detaching a root-initial consonant and adding it to the prefix — со-тру́дник (not сот-ру́дник),
  по-проси́ть (not поп-роси́ть); (iii) don't divide a prefix from a following ы — подыто́-жить (not
  под-ыто́жить); (iv) prefer NOT breaking off the final root consonant to join it to the suffix, though
  this is technically permissible — примóр-ский preferred over приморьский... i.e. examples show
  примóр-ский over примо́р-ский-type suffix-boundary splits; (v) compound words divide at the boundary
  between their constituent parts — спец-одéжда, само-лёт (pp. 183–184).
- **(c) Words without clear morpheme boundaries** (e.g. сестра́, "sister"): a medial consonant cluster
  may be divided in either of two ways — the whole cluster carried to the next line (се-стра́) or split
  (сес-тра́, сест-ра́); however hyphenating between a single consonant and vowel is never allowed, so a
  single intervocalic consonant is always carried over: ка-рандáш (not кар-андáш) (p. 184).

### §15.4 The Capital Letter (Прописная буква)

As a spelling device (distinct from its punctuation role marking sentence starts), certain word
classes obligatorily begin with a capital regardless of sentence position.

**§15.4.1 Words capitalized in BOTH Russian and English:**

- **(a) Names of people**: (i) proper names and their initials; (ii) both halves of a hyphenated
  surname; (iii) nicknames/epithets used as part of a name (Иван Калита, Дмитрий Донской).
  **Possessive adjectives formed with -ов(-ев)/-ин(-ын) from a person's name are ALSO capitalized**
  (Олéгова кни́га "Oleg's book," Врубелев «Демо́н» "Vrubel's Demon"). **Exception**: possessives from
  proper nouns that have LOST their possessive meaning and become ordinary common adjectives are
  generally NOT capitalized in Russian even though the parallel English phrase keeps the capital —
  ахилле́сова пятá "Achilles' heel," адáмово я́блоко "Adam's apple," базёдова болéзнь "Basedow's
  disease" (p. 185). (iv) Certain surname prefixes are capitalized along with the following letter:
  French Saint- (Сен-Симóн), Latin Saint- (Санкт-Петербу́рг), Irish O'- (О'Коннéйль), Scottish Mac-
  (Мак-Грéгор). **However, in European surnames the particles ле, ла, л, да, д, де, ван are NOT
  capitalized and take no hyphen** — Леонáрдо да Ви́нчи, Жáнна д'Арк (p. 185). (v) East Asian naming
  conventions differ by nationality: Chinese names (three syllables) capitalize only the first two,
  joining the third by hyphen (old style) or fusing it (new style, now standard) — Дэн Сяопи́н;
  Vietnamese and Korean names capitalize ALL syllables with no hyphen — Хо Ши Мин, Ким Ир Сен;
  Japanese names capitalize both parts — Тоётóми Хидэ́ёси (p. 186). (vi) Central Asian/Middle
  Eastern noble-rank suffixes (Turkish, Egyptian, Persian, Tatar) are hyphenated but NOT capitalized
  — Исмаи́л-бей, Чингúс-хан (p. 186).
- **(b) Names of places**: (i) countries/provinces/cities/towns are capitalized; (ii) native Russian
  geographic generic terms (река, море, залив, бухта, мыс, лиман, берег, побережье, остров,
  полуостров, горы, хребет, полюс) that form part of a compound place name are written LOWERCASE,
  while any modifying adjective/possessive IS capitalized (Бе́ринговом мóре) — **unlike English, which
  capitalizes every word in such compounds**. Exception: the generic term IS capitalized if it begins
  the name of a country (Бе́рег Слонóвой Кóсти "Ivory Coast") or is itself a foreign/non-Russian word
  (Голубóй Нил "the Blue Nile") (pp. 186–187). The same lowercase-generic-term rule extends to
  man-made-place generics (улица, дорога, парк, площадь, проспект, шоссе) — Крáсная плóщадь "Red
  Square." If NO recognizable generic term is present, both words of the compound are capitalized
  (Нóвая Земля́) (p. 187). (iii) A non-Russian-word compound place name is fully hyphenated with each
  part capitalized (Пуэ́рто-Ри́ко); (iv) Western-language place-name prefixes (ла, ле, де, д, ла с,
  лос) ARE capitalized and hyphenated when part of a PLACE name (Лос-Áнджелес) — contrast their
  non-capitalized, non-hyphenated treatment in personal surnames; (v) prepositions/particles between
  nouns in a place name are hyphenated but NOT capitalized (Ростóв-на-Донý) — English hyphenates such
  names only when non-Spanish-derived (pp. 187–188).
- **(c) Names of gods and mythological beings**: Геркулéс, Аппол­óн, Пегáс, Зевс Громовéржец, Перýн,
  Вéлес (p. 188). **Note**: the word for the Christian God (Бог) was conventionally left lowercase
  during the Soviet period; today it appears capitalized or not depending on the writer. Иису́с
  Христóс "Jesus Christ" is always capitalized regardless (p. 188).
- **(d) Names of historical events/epochs**: Возрождéние "the Renaissance." If the name has both
  nouns and adjectives, only the ADJECTIVE(S) are capitalized, the noun stays lowercase: Вели́кая
  Отéчественная войнá "the Great Patriotic War" (Soviet name for WWII) (p. 188).
- **(e) Names of pet animals**: Ки́са, Мýрка, Бу́лька (p. 188).
- **(f) Names of organizations/businesses**: only the FIRST letter of the first word is capitalized —
  Большóй теáтр "the Bolshoi Theater," including transliterated foreign names (Чейс нéйшнл бэнк
  "Chase National Bank"). **Exception**: names of MAJOR WORLD ORGANIZATIONS capitalize ALL content
  words — Организáция Объединённых Нáций "United Nations" (p. 188).
- **(g) Titles of newspapers/magazines/works of art/music/literature**: only the first letter of the
  first word is capitalized (proper nouns within the title stay capitalized as usual); titles are
  enclosed in QUOTATION MARKS in Russian, where English instead uses italics: «Гóре от умá», the
  comedy Woe from Wit (pp. 188–190).
- **(h) Some acronyms/abbreviations are NOT capitalized in Russian though their English equivalent
  is** — спид, "AIDS" (p. 190).

**§15.4.2 Words capitalized in English but NOT in Russian** (unless sentence-initial): (a) the 1st
person singular pronoun я, "I"; (b) plural "you"-forms (вы/ваш/вам) ARE capitalized specifically when
addressing a single individual in personal/business letters, but not otherwise; (c) days of the week;
(d) months of the year; (e) Russian equivalents of Mr./Mrs./Comrade/Citizen/Dr./Professor as titles
of address; (f) common geographic generic terms inside a compound place name where the compound is
adjective+noun (only the first adjective is capitalized: Крымский полуóстров) or contains a genitive
modifier (the generic noun itself stays lowercase: у́лица Гóрького); (g) only the first word of a
company/historical-event/artwork name, where English capitalizes every content word; (h) occasional
acronyms like спид (pp. 189–191).

### §15.5 Use of the Period with Abbreviations (Сокращения)

- **§15.5.1 Abbreviations that DO require a period** — an extensive standard list (в. "century," г.
  "year," гр. "citizen," до н.э./н.э. "B.C./A.D.," ж.д. "railroad," и др./и пр./и т.п./и т.д. "etc."-
  type abbreviations, им. "named after," к. "kopek," кв. "apartment," м. "minute," напр. "for
  example," р. "ruble"/"river," с.г. "this year," с.х. "agriculture," см. "look," ср. "compare, cf.,"
  стр. "page," т./тов. "comrade" (also "volume" for т.), тт. "comrades"/"volumes," т.е. "that is,
  i.e.," ул. "street," ч. "hour" — full list in Vocabulary above). A person's first two initials also
  take periods, and are conventionally INCLUDED before a surname in Russian far more often than in
  English (А.П. Чéхова, "(A.P.) Chekhov's" — English tends to drop such initials). **When an
  abbreviation coincides with a sentence-final period, only ONE period mark is written**, not two —
  true in both Russian and English (pp. 190–192).
- **§15.5.2 Abbreviations that do NOT end in a period**, four subtypes: (a) metric measurement
  abbreviations (м, см, мл, мм, кг, км); (b) acronyms (аббревиатýры), split into two behaviorally
  distinct groups — REGULAR acronyms pronounced as ordinary words (вуз, спид, ГУМ, even when written
  in all capitals) which take normal case endings like any noun (в ГУМе), vs. ALPHABETISMS pronounced
  by sounding out the constituent letter-NAMES (МГУ, ООН, США) which take NO case endings at all (в
  МГУ год, not *в МГУе); (c) stub compounds (сложносокращённые словá) — a single fused word built
  from truncated stubs of two OTHER abbreviated words rather than whole words (contrast a true
  compound word, слóжное словó) — зарплáта, спецодéжда, стройматериáлы, a formation pattern that
  proliferated during the Soviet period; (d) hyphenated abbreviations mostly take NO period (г-н
  "Mr.," п-ов "peninsula," о-во "society") — **except** hyphenated abbreviations of COMPOUND
  ADJECTIVES, which DO take a period even though the corresponding noun-phrase abbreviation doesn't:
  ж.д. (noun, "railroad," no internal hyphen) vs. ж.-д. (adjective "railroad-related," hyphenated AND
  period-final) (pp. 192–194).

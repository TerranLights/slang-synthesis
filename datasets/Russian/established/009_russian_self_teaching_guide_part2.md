**PDF extraction note — decodable stress-accent glyph substitution (not corruption):** this PDF has
a real text layer, but the typesetting font renders *stressed* vowels as a distinct set of
Latin-lookalike glyphs rather than plain Cyrillic + combining accent: unstressed а/е/о/и/у/я/ы/э
extract normally, while their stressed counterparts extract as á/é/ó/ñ/ÿ/û/π/œ respectively (e.g.
"гóда" = года with stress on the о; "бπло" = было with stress on the ы, i.e. it decodes to plain
был-; "œто" = это). This is a fixed, consistent, fully decodable 1:1 substitution (confirmed against
100+ known word forms across the full page range), not garbled OCR — it is functionally the book's
own stress-mark convention. **Vocabulary terms below are normalized to plain, unaccented Cyrillic
spelling** (the standard dictionary form) for portability/searchability; stress placement itself is
not separately recorded per-entry in this file (would require reproducing every accent mark) but the
mechanism is flagged here in case future extraction wants to preserve it. No handwritten marginalia
was encountered — this source is a clean digital scan of print with no annotation.

## Morpheme breakdown (fusional-compositional forms worth flagging)

Russian is fusional, not agglutinative, so most single vocabulary entries above don't need a
morpheme breakdown — case/number/gender are fused into one ending, not stacked as separable
morphemes. Two structural patterns from this chunk are worth breaking out explicitly because they
show genuinely compositional structure that an atomic gloss would hide:

> **компью́тер → компью́теров** (genitive plural) = **компьютер-** (stem) + **-ов** (genitive
> plural ending for a zero-ending masculine noun) — illustrates the default masc. genitive-plural
> formation rule (Ch.13).

> **прочита́вший** (past active participle of прочитать, "having read") = **про-** (perfective
> prefix) + **чита-** (root "read") + **-вш-** (past-active-participle formant) + **-ий**
> (masc. nom. sg. adjectival ending) — the clearest example in this chunk of Russian's
> participle formation stacking a genuinely separable formant (-вш-/-ш-) onto a verb stem before
> the adjectival ending; ch.17's whole "Formation" subsections for present/past active participles
> describe exactly this kind of internal, analyzable structure even though the surface word behaves
> as one fused adjective. See individual participle-formation rules below for the productive
> formant patterns (-ащ-/-ящ-, -ущ-/-ющ-, -вш-/-ш-).

## Grammar points

### Chapter 9 — Verbal aspect (imperfective vs. perfective), in practice

Building on the aspect system introduced structurally in an earlier chapter (established elsewhere
in this dataset), this chapter works through aspect via a worked conversation and six paired
comments (pp. 156–166). Key generalizations, paraphrased:

- **Perfective marks completion/result; imperfective is the default.** You need a specific,
  compelling reason to choose perfective — absent that reason, use imperfective. Markers that favor
  perfective include уже and наконец (pointing to an awaited/expected result), and adverbs that
  qualify a result (e.g. хорошо выучила "learned [it] well" → perfective, because the adverb
  qualifies a successful outcome).
- **All past-tense verbs describe a completed event in time, but that does not by itself justify
  perfective.** Я читал урок ("I read the lesson") — imperfective — only asserts that reading-time
  was spent; it neither confirms nor denies that the lesson was finished. Perfective specifically
  requires the speaker to be focused on the *moment of completion/result itself*, not merely on the
  fact that time has passed.
- **-ing-style focus (manner, ongoing process, "were you doing X") reliably signals imperfective**
  even in a past-tense question, e.g. «Вы читали со словарём?» ("Were you reading with a
  dictionary?") — the focus is on the manner of an ongoing action, not on completion.
- **A negated result implies imperfective**: "Я учила слова, но забыла несколько" (I studied
  the words, but forgot a few) — учила is imperfective because whatever result there was didn't
  hold.
- **Only imperfective exists in the present tense** — perfective, by definition, can't describe
  an action still in progress, so the whole aspect distinction collapses for present tense.
- **Future tense**: imperfective future = conjugated auxiliary буду/будешь/... + imperfective
  infinitive; perfective future = the perfective infinitive conjugated directly (this is easy to
  mistake for a present-tense conjugation, since perfective verbs have no morphologically distinct
  present tense).
- **Imperative**: perfective is the default for a single, one-time command aimed at a result
  (Съешь овощи! "Eat your vegetables [now]!"); imperfective conveys a general/repeated instruction
  (Ешь овощи каждый день! "Eat vegetables every day!").
- A small set of aspectual pairs (учить/выучить, изучать/изучить, сдавать/сдать, поступать/
  поступить, решать/решить — see Vocabulary above) systematically contrast "attempting/engaging
  with X" (imperfective) vs. "having achieved/mastered X" (perfective) — this pattern of aspect
  encoding *success vs. attempt*, rather than just "ongoing vs. done," is flagged as a recurring,
  learnable subpattern.

### Chapter 10 — Future tense (imperfective and perfective) and the imperative mood

- **Imperfective future** = conjugated буду/будешь/будет/будем/будете/будут + imperfective
  infinitive. Describes an action occurring over time in the future with no claim about completion.
- **Perfective future** = the perfective infinitive conjugated directly. Because there's no overt
  auxiliary, English speakers frequently mishear it as present tense. Some verbs (жить, играть,
  заниматься, сидеть, бояться, among others) describe states/activities that don't lead to a
  result and therefore have no perfective future at all.
- **Imperative formation** (regular pattern): take 3rd person plural, drop the last two letters.
  If what remains ends in a vowel, add -й. If it ends in a consonant, check the stress of the
  1st-person singular: end-stressed → add -и; stem-stressed → add -ь. Add -те for addressing вы
  or a group.
  - Exceptions: verbs in -авать/-ать form the imperative from the infinitive stem directly
    (вставай!, давай!, дай!); встать/есть/лечь have irregular imperatives встань/ешь/ляг;
    ездить and видеть have no imperative at all (use prefixed forms of -езжать and смотри!
    instead); verbs like пить (present-tense stem = single consonant + soft sign) form the
    imperative with an inserted fleeting -е- before the soft sign becomes -й (пьют → пь → пей).
- **Aspect choice with the imperative**: a single command to one person defaults to perfective
  (wants the result). An invitation/suggestion (rather than an order) uses imperfective, even
  when addressed to one person (Садитесь, пожалуйста — "Have a seat" vs. Сядь! — "Sit!" [to a
  dog]). Imperfective imperative also conveys urgency when a command is repeated (especially
  after an initial perfective command went unheeded), and imperfective is the default for
  negative commands (Не открывай окно — "Don't open the window") *unless* the negative command
  carries a sense of warning ("be careful not to..."), in which case perfective is used (Не
  тронь плиту! — "Don't touch the stove!").

### Chapter 11 — Further uses of the dative case

Extends dative usage beyond indirect object/prepositions/age (covered earlier in the book) to:

- **A closed list of verbs that always govern dative** (see Vocabulary table above: верить,
  завидовать, запрещать, звонить, мешать, обещать, позволять, помогать, принадлежать,
  радоваться, разрешать, следовать, советовать, удивляться, улыбаться).
- **Impersonal verbs governing dative** (казаться, надоедать, нездоровиться, нравиться,
  приходиться, спаться, хватать, хотеться) — these lack a normal grammatical subject; the
  experiencer is dative, and the verb itself stays 3rd-person-singular/neuter.
- **Impersonal expressions** (можно, нельзя, жаль, пора) and **subjective-feeling adjectives**
  used impersonally (Мне жарко/холодно/скучно/весело/грустно/стыдно — "I'm hot/cold/bored/
  happy/sad/ashamed") — all take the experiencer in dative.
- **Necessity**: надо/нужно/необходимо + infinitive, experiencer in dative; необходимо is
  markedly stronger than надо/нужно.
- **"Needing" is grammatically reversed from English**: the needed thing is the nominative
  subject and the short adjective нужен/нужна/нужно/нужны agrees with it in gender/number; the
  person who needs is dative (Мне нужен новый велосипед — "I need a new bicycle," lit. "To-me
  [is] needed a new bicycle").
- **Negative pronouns with dative + infinitive** (негде, некогда, не с кем, нечего, некуда,
  некому) express "there is no X for [dative person] to [infinitive]" — stress falls on the first
  syllable, distinguishing these from the ordinary negated-verb ни- series; a preposition, when
  required, is inserted between the prefix and the stem (не с кем, not *нес кем*).
- **Interrogative pronoun + infinitive** (Что мне делать? "What am I to do?") is also impersonal
  and follows the same бы́ло/будет-for-past/future auxiliary pattern as the sections above.

### Chapter 12 — The genitive case

- **Core genitive-singular endings**: masc./neut. -а/-я, fem. -ы/-и, with adjectives -ого/-его
  (masc./neut.) and -ой/-ей (fem.). Soft-stem nouns and the seven-letter spelling rule (ж ч ш щ
  к г х require -и not -ы) govern which alternate applies. A "mixed declension" group of
  adjectives derived mostly from animal names (лисий, собачий, птичий, коровий, третий) takes
  -ьего, inserting a soft sign before the ending.
- **Genitive expresses possession**, corresponding to English 's/of — but only as ноун-of-noun
  word order (сын моего лучшего друга, never the reverse order except as a marked, non-standard
  poetic/émigré usage).
- **Family/name-derived possessives**: masculine nouns ending in a consonant add -ов (Иван →
  Иванов дом); nouns (incl. nicknames) ending in -а/-я drop the vowel and add -ин (мама →
  мамино пальто). These forms are the historical source of most Russian surnames (Иванов,
  Пушкин); such possessive adjectives are colloquial and rarely decline into oblique cases, but
  modern surnames built the same way (Петров/Петрова) *do* fully decline, following a mixed
  noun/adjective paradigm (see full case table in the source, p. 196).
- **Certain "of"-phrases use genitive** (цвет моей новой машины, наступление весны), but a
  competing idiomatic pattern renders some English of-phrases as adjectives instead
  (Пенсильванский университет "University of Pennsylvania"), and — confusingly — the reverse also
  happens (студент первого курса "a first-year student," using genitive where English uses an
  adjective). The source explicitly warns there is no fully general rule here: genitive is
  sometimes required by a specific governing verb rather than by "of" itself (руководитель
  заводом, "director of the factory," uses instrumental because руководить governs instrumental).
- **Partitive genitive** expresses unspecified "some" and is normally untranslated (Дайте хлеба,
  пожалуйста — "Give me some bread"). A vanishing masculine partitive ending in -у/-ю exists for
  a fixed list of mass nouns (чаю, сахару, шёлку, мёду, and others — see Vocabulary), used *only*
  in a bare partitive sense — adjectives block this ending (стакан чаю "a glass of tea" vs.
  стакан крепкого чая "a glass of strong tea").
- **Measure phrases** (кило сахара, чашка чая, бутылка вина, etc.) take the measured noun in
  genitive.
- **Numbers and the genitive**: один/одно/одна + all its compounds (except 11) take nominative
  singular. Два/три/четыре (masc./neut.) take genitive singular noun + genitive plural adjective;
  две/три/четыре (fem.) take genitive singular noun + nominative plural adjective (an older,
  still-current genitive-plural-adjective variant also exists and is explicitly recommended as
  worth learning). All other numbers (5+, teens, and any number ending in 0) require genitive
  plural for both noun and adjective.
- **Negation/nonexistence uses genitive**: replacing есть with нет + genitive for possession-
  negation; genitive is also commonly (though not obligatorily) used for the direct object of a
  negated verb, with a semantic split between accusative (specific referent) and genitive
  (nonspecific/unreal referent) — Он не ждёт жену "He's not waiting for a wife" (any wife) vs.
  Он не ждёт жену [sic, wife-accusative] "He's not waiting for his wife" (specific).
- **A closed list of verbs always governs genitive** (see Vocabulary: бояться, желать, избегать,
  касаться, пугаться, достигать, заслуживать, требовать).
- **Genitive-governing prepositions**: an alphabetical list (see Vocabulary table) — без, вместо,
  вокруг, для, до, из, кроме, мимо, насчёт, около, от, после, против, ради, с(со), среди, у —
  each with distinct sub-senses the source paraphrases individually (e.g. с has "off the surface
  of," "since [temporal]," and plain "with" via a separate instrumental usage covered in ch.14).

### Chapter 13 — The genitive plural

The chapter reframes the traditional gender-based approach around a "zero ending / base form"
concept: masculine nouns are already in their zero-ending base form (nominative singular), while
feminine and neuter nouns reach their zero-ending base form precisely in the genitive plural.

- **Masculine nouns**: zero-ending nouns (not ending in ь ж ч ш щ) take -ов (-ев after й, ц when
  unstressed per the five-letter spelling rule). Nouns ending in a soft sign or in ж ч ш щ take
  -ей.
- **Feminine nouns**: nouns in consonant+а/я drop the vowel (zero ending), inserting a **fleeting
  vowel** (usually -о-, sometimes -е- per the spelling rule or softness) if a resulting consonant
  cluster is unpronounceable (студентка → студенток). Nouns in vowel+я (лекция) expand я → йа
  before dropping the final -а, yielding a zero-ending soft stem (лекций). Nouns ending in a bare
  soft sign drop it and add -ей (тетрадь → тетрадей), except мать/дочь which lengthen their stem
  (матерей, дочерей). Nouns in soft-sign+я (семья) are a hybrid: zero ending plus a fleeting -е-
  (семей).
- **Neuter nouns**: parallel to feminine — most drop the final vowel sound (окно → окон, with a
  fleeting vowel as needed); the large -ие class (здание) parallels feminine -ия nouns, expanding
  to -ийэ before dropping the final vowel (здание → зданий); a handful of irregular nouns in -мя
  (имя/имён, время/времён) and an irregular small class of neuters take -ов (облако → облаков).
- **Summary table given in the source** (paraphrased): masc. zero-stem → -ов; masc. ц/й-stem →
  -ев; masc. ж ч ш щ / soft-sign stem → -ей; fem. consonant+а/я → zero; fem. vowel+я → zero;
  fem. soft-sign → -ей; neut. consonant+о → zero; neut. vowel+е → zero.
- **Adjective genitive plural has only one ending**, -ых/-их (adjusted for spelling rule/softness)
  — no gender distinction, unlike nouns.
- **Numbers recap (explicit rules)**: один and compounds (not 11) → nominative singular; masc./
  neut. два/три/четыре (not 12–14) → genitive plural adjective + genitive singular noun; fem.
  две/три/четыре (not 12–14) → nominative plural adjective + genitive singular noun; everything
  else (5+, teens, multiples of 10) → genitive plural for both.
- **Two irregular-plural exception classes for masculine/neuter nouns**: (1) nouns with a
  nominative plural in -ья: stem-stressed → genitive plural -ьев (брат/братья/братьев); ending-
  stressed → genitive plural -ей (муж/мужья/мужей); (2) nouns with other irregular plurals: -ёнок/
  -ята pattern and -ин/-е pattern both take a zero genitive-plural ending (котёнок/котята/котят;
  англичанин/англичане/англичан); hard-singular/soft-plural nouns (сосед/соседи/соседей,
  человек/люди/людей, ребёнок/дети/детей) take zero or -ей.
- **A closed, must-memorize list of masculine nouns with zero genitive plural** covering military
  rank, measurement units, and some nationalities (солдат, партизан, раз, ботинок, глаз, сапог,
  чулок; грузин, башкир, ампер, герц, вольт, грамм, аршин — see Vocabulary table). Человек itself
  is the genitive plural used specifically after numbers/сколько/несколько; людей is used
  everywhere else.
- **Pluralia tantum nouns** (nouns that exist only in the plural) each have a memorized genitive
  plural with no productive rule to derive it (брюки/брюк, деньги/денег, ножницы/ножниц, очки/
  очков, часы is not listed but the pattern is illustrated by ~24 such nouns in the source — see
  Vocabulary table for the fuller list actually enumerated: брюки, ворота, выборы, деньги,
  джунгли, дрова, духи, кавычки, каникулы, коньки, кудри, ножницы, обои, очки, похороны,
  родители, сани, сливки, стихи, сумерки, сутки, счёты, хлопоты, хлопья, чернила, шахматы, щи,
  ясли).

### Chapter 14 — The instrumental case

- **Endings**: masc./neut. noun -ом/-ем, fem. noun -ой/-ей (an older -ою/-ею form survives in
  poetry/literary register), plural noun -ами/-ями (with five lexical exceptions: людьми, детьми,
  дочерьми, лошадьми, дверьми — a regular лошадями/дверями variant is also acceptable). Adjective
  endings: masc./neut. -ым/-им, fem. -ой/-ей, plural -ыми/-ими.
- **As instrument/means**: expresses "with X" for tools, senses, and body parts used as
  instruments, *without* a preposition (писать рукой, есть суп ложкой, резать бумагу ножницами)
  — a systematic contrast with English "with," which the source flags as a common English-
  interference error point.
- **Means of transportation**: synonymous with на + prepositional (ехать автобусом = ехать на
  автобусе), except метро and такси (indeclinable) must use на + prepositional only.
- **Manner** (говорить тихим голосом) and **the second complement of a double-object verb**
  (выбрать/назначить/признать/считать/найти + instrumental: Его выбрали президентом "He was
  elected president").
- **Time expressions**: parts of the day and seasons (утром, днём, вечером, ночью, весной,
  летом, осенью, зимой) are instrumental by default.
- **With verbs of being/becoming**: instrumental after быть is optional and marks a *temporary*
  state (была талантливой актрисой vs. была талантливая актриса — but an inherent/permanent
  trait like nationality must stay nominative: была русская, not *была русской*). Instrumental
  is *required* after the infinitive быть (Кем ты хочешь быть? "What do you want to be?"), after
  every form of стать, and after работать/служить when specifying a role (работает химиком).
  Also required after явиться, выглядеть, казаться, оказаться, остаться.
- **Instrumental-governing prepositions**: с (with — testable by substituting the antonym без),
  за (three senses: fetching/"for," spatially "behind," and "at" as in sitting at a table), между
  (between), над (over/above; also figuratively "working on"), под (below; also "in the vicinity
  of," e.g. suburb-adjacent под Москвой), перед (spatially in front of; temporally right before).
  A mnemonic verse by Alexander Lipson using all these prepositions is cited (paraphrased, not
  reproduced in full per copyright discipline) as a well-known pedagogical device in the field.

### Chapter 15 — Verbs of motion

The chapter's central organizing distinction, stated as the two criteria Russian speakers actually
track when choosing a "go" verb:

1. **On foot vs. by vehicle** — any ground vehicle counts as "by vehicle" as long as you're being
   carried (bicycle, bus, elevator).
2. **Indeterminate/multidirectional vs. determinate/unidirectional** — habitual, repeated, or
   general-ability motion (indeterminate) vs. a single trip in one direction, often happening
   right now (determinate).

- **Five core imperfective/imperfective/perfective triads** (indeterminate / determinate /
  perfective): ходить/идти/пойти (foot), ездить/ехать/поехать (vehicle), летать/лететь/полететь
  (fly), плавать/плыть/поплыть (swim/sail), бегать/бежать/побежать (run). Three further triads
  (бродить/брести/побрести "stroll," ползать/ползти/поползти "crawl," лазить/лезть/полезть
  "climb") are named but flagged by the source as less common and not further drilled.
- **Practical heuristic**: if the English translation would use an -ing / present-progressive
  form ("I am going," "he is swimming toward us"), the determinate verb is almost always correct;
  everything else (habitual action, general ability, "no destination specified," manner-focus)
  defaults to indeterminate — even when the grammatical subject is a vehicle that "moves under its
  own power" in a general sense (a bus "goes" slowly = indeterminate ходит, even though buses are
  vehicles, because no specific one-time trip is being described).
- **Transitive counterparts** (also indeterminate/determinate/perfective): водить/вести/повести
  "lead," носить/нести/понести "carry," возить/везти/повезти "transport."
- **Prefixed verbss of motion behave like ordinary Russian verbs** (one imperfective, one
  perfective each) — prefix + indeterminate stem → new imperfective; prefix + determinate stem →
  new perfective. Several indeterminate stems shift/mutate under prefixation (e.g. ходить's
  prefixed imperfective is -ходить but its own perfective counterpart ѝти becomes -йти; ездить's
  imperfective becomes -езжать while its own determinate ехать supplies the perfixed perfective
  directly).
- **Directional-prefix meanings** (see Vocabulary table for the full list): при- (arrive/enter,
  antonym у-), у- (depart, "not here"), в-/во-/въ- (into, antonym вы-), вы- (out of), под-/подо-/
  подъ- (approach up to a boundary without crossing it, antonym от-), от-/ото-/оть- (away from a
  boundary), пере- (across/through, optional через), с-/со-/съ- (off/down from, governs с +
  genitive). The source stresses the systematic antonym pairs (при-/у-, в-/вы-, под-/от-) as the
  fastest way to internalize the set, and illustrates each with a short worked dialogue/joke
  (paraphrased above, not reproduced) rather than a bare rule list.
- **Traffic-rules reading passage** (paraphrased, not quoted at length): right-hand traffic norms,
  crosswalk signal conventions (a lit "ИДИТЕ"/"GO" vs. "СТОЙТЕ"/"STOP" sign), and underground
  pedestrian crossings in large Russian cities — included as cultural/practical context for the
  verbs-of-motion vocabulary rather than as new grammar.

### Chapter 16 — Prepositional, dative, and instrumental plural

- **The plural of these three cases collapses the gender distinction entirely** — one ending set
  serves all genders. Adjective endings: prepositional -ых/-их, dative -ым/-им, instrumental
  -ыми/-ими. Noun endings: prepositional -ах/-ях, dative -ам/-ям, instrumental -ами/-ями (softness
  is the only variable for nouns, since there's no five-letter spelling-rule vowel involved).
- **Any singular-to-plural stem change carries through**: if a noun's nominative plural has a
  different stem from the singular (братья, соседи, англичане), that plural stem — not the
  singular stem — is what all three plural-case endings above attach to.
- **Instrumental plural has exactly five irregular/suppletive forms**: людьми, детьми, дочерьми,
  дверьми, лошадьми (with дочерями/дверями/лошадями also acceptable as regularized variants).
- **У меня болит X — the "my head hurts" construction**: the body part that hurts is the
  grammatical subject (governing singular or plural verb agreement per its own number), and the
  person experiencing the pain is expressed as у + genitive (У бабушки болят ноги — "Grandmother's
  feet hurt," lit. "By grandmother, the feet hurt"). A related fully impersonal variant puts the
  experiencer in dative when there's no body-part subject at all (Мне больно дышать — "It hurts me
  to breathe").
- **Жаловаться на + accusative** = "to complain about."

### Chapter 17 — Participles

Russian participles ("verbal adjectives") express both voice (active/passive) and tense
(present/past, never future) — four combinations total, plus two separate gerund forms not
covered in this page range (the assigned pages cover only **active** participles; passive
participles and gerunds are mentioned only in passing footnotes to the closing War and Peace
excerpt, e.g. footnote glosses identifying сопутствуемый as "a present passive participle" and
судя as "a gerund," without a dedicated formation section within pages 161–313).

**A participle substitutes for a который-clause where который is in the nominative case** (i.e.,
where который is the *subject* of its own dependent clause) — a participle cannot substitute for a
который in any oblique case (e.g. когда который is a direct object, only a который-clause, or a
*passive* participle, can express it).

**Present active participle**
- Formed from: 3rd-person-plural present tense, drop the final letter, add щ + adjective ending
  (stress = same as 3rd person plural). Requires an imperfective verb (since only imperfective has
  a present tense in the first place) and a который that would be nominative.
- The -ся particle does not contract with participle endings.
- Word order determines punctuation: a participial phrase *following* its antecedent is set off by
  commas; one *preceding* the antecedent has no commas; single-word participial phrases usually
  precede their antecedent.
- **Many present active participles have lexicalized as ordinary nouns or adjectives** and are no
  longer perceived by native speakers as participles at all — see Vocabulary table for examples
  (блестящий "shiny/brilliant," завёдующий "manager," курящий/некурящий "smoker/non-smoker,"
  and many technical/scientific compounds like кровоостанавливающий "styptic"). A large,
  productive class of noun-plus-participle compound adjectives also exists (e.g.
  жизнеутверждающий "life-affirming," яйцекладущий "egg-laying").
- **Caveat: not every word ending in -щий is a participle.** Native-Russian verbal adjectives use
  -чий (жгучий "burning, ardent" vs. the true participle жгущий "that which is burning"); a
  colloquial superlative-like class uses -ущий/-ющий to mean "excessively X" (большущий
  "humongous," здоровущий "healthy as a horse"); and a handful of Old Church Slavonic-derived
  forms (вящий "very great," used only in set phrases like с вящим уважением "with the utmost
  respect") survive as archaic/jocular fossils. Настоящий ("real, present") is explicitly flagged
  by the source as having *no* relation to the present active participle despite its shape.

**Past active participle**
- Formed from: masculine past-tense form, drop -л, add -вш- + adjective ending (or -дший if the
  conjugated stem ends in -д-; just -ш- + ending if the past tense itself doesn't end in -л). Any
  verb — imperfective or perfective — can form a past active participle, since all Russian verbs
  have a past tense. The е/ё alternation reverts: a verb whose past tense has ё returns to е in the
  past active participle form.
- Same который-must-be-nominative constraint and same comma/word-order rule as present active
  participles.
- **Also frequently lexicalized** as adjectives/nouns with figurative extensions (see Vocabulary:
  бывший "former," сумасшедший "insane" [lit. "gone out of one's mind"], погибший "lost, perished,"
  потерпевший "the victim/survivor," устаревший "obsolete") — the source lists roughly twenty such
  lexicalized forms (p. 293–294), most naming "someone/something that has undergone X."

**Register note**: participles are explicitly characterized as literary/high-register — expected in
newspapers, novels, and formal prose, but not a feature of ordinary spoken registers, which favor
который-clauses instead. The chapter closes with an annotated excerpt from Tolstoy's *War and
Peace* (vol. 1, part 3, ch. 19) as a worked example of participle-dense literary Russian; per
copyright discipline this is referenced, not reproduced, beyond the short participle-bearing
fragments already surfaced in the Vocabulary/grammar discussion above.

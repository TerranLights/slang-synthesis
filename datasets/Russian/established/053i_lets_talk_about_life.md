# Russian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: *Let's Talk About Life!* — Урок 9 (Россия и Запад / Russia and the West)

**Source:** Emily Tall & Valentina Vlasikova, *Let's Talk About Life! An Integrated Approach to
Russian Conversation* (John Wiley & Sons, 1996), Урок 9, the book's ninth and final thematic
chapter, complete: Часть I "Национальные стереотипы" (pp. 221–223), Часть II "Чтение" — Тексты 1–7
("В поисках грустного бэби," "Родная душа," "Возвращение, или как это должно быть на самом деле,"
"Империализму — нет! Биг Мак не пройдёт!," "Бесы из хрустального храма," "Американцы в России,"
"Россия — мост между Западом и Востоком," pp. 223–249, including the "Word Study: Americanisms in
Russian" subsection embedded within it, p. 242), and Часть III "Подводя итоги" (p. 250, exercises
only, no new vocabulary). This is the last chapter of the book; a Russian–English glossary
(back-matter, pp. 251–274) immediately follows and is explicitly out of scope for this dispatch (see
coverage note).

**Coverage note:** Same coverage approach as prior files (053–053h). This chapter is longer than any
prior lesson (31 printed pages vs. ~20–30 for earlier уроки) and is unusually text-dense: two long
Часть I brainstorming word-lists, seven full reading texts each with its own footnoted vocabulary
apparatus, and an explicit "Word Study: Americanisms in Russian" lexical-borrowing exercise. All of
it is captured. Repeated comprehension-question prose (the "Ответьте на вопросы"/"Давайте
поговорим!" exercise blocks) was skipped per the coverage rule — it recycles vocabulary already
captured from the same text's footnotes rather than introducing new items. **The Russian–English
glossary beginning at printed p. 251 (PDF p. 263) is whole-book back matter, not chapter-specific
content, and is intentionally NOT extracted here** — it merits its own dedicated dispatch, and is
flagged as a genuinely high-value one: a quick spot-check found it explicitly `(sl.)`-tags entries
(e.g. балдеть "(sl.) to get high," анаша "hashish") using the same abbreviation-key convention as
this book's in-chapter footnotes, i.e. it is a real slang-annotated glossary layer covering the
entire book, distinct from and larger than any single chapter's vocabulary.

**Vision-reading guard:** Same source/method as prior files in this set (no text layer, one-page-
per-image via `pdftoppm`, offset confirmed empirically rather than assumed: printed page = PDF page
− 12, verified by direct inspection of the Урок 8/Урок 9 boundary and re-confirmed at several points
through p. 274 — one PDF page per printed page throughout, no double-page-spread scanning as seen in
other languages' sources). No handwritten marginalia observed anywhere in this chapter's page range;
all content vision-read is genuinely typeset/printed. One vocabulary item (item 11 of the
Americanisms exercise, p. 242) was re-rendered at 300 DPI to resolve a borderline character before
transcribing it with full confidence — see the бэмвушка entry below.

## Grammar points

### The -яга / -ёныш family of expressive/diminutive derivational suffixes
This chapter's Word Study-adjacent footnotes explicitly name two productive affective suffixes:
-яга (симпатяга "likeable person," cross-referenced by the source itself against бедняга "poor
thing" and работяга "hard worker") and -ёныш (вампирёныш "little vampire," glossed as marking "the
young of" something). Both attach to already-meaningful roots to produce a colloquial/expressive
register shift rather than a denotational change — the same broad derivational-suffix mechanism as
the -шник/-эшник pattern below, worth treating as one family of "affect-marking suffixes" for the
eventual mechanics-analysis phase.

### The -шник/-эшник acronym-to-person-noun pattern
Кагэбэшник ("KGB agent," from the acronym КГБ) is this chapter's clearest example of a recurring
book-wide pattern: an official acronym or abbreviation plus a colloquial suffix yields an informal
person-noun. Бэмвушка (BMW → an informal car name, technically not a person-noun but the same
brand/acronym-plus-suffix logic) is a sibling coinage in the same chapter. Worth cross-referencing
against the Урок 8 file's own note on тачка/рэкетир as this book's recurring register-marking-via-
derivation strategy — this is now confirmed across at least three different chapters' worth of
independent examples, not a one-off.

### Satiety and directional aspectual prefix+reflexive combinations
Two footnotes in this chapter give explicit, source-authored morphological rules rather than bare
glosses: разбежаться (раз- + -ся marking "motion in different directions from a central point," p.
229) and наговориться (на- + -ся marking "to do an action to satiety," p. 230). Both are genuine
aspectual-prefix mechanics rather than idiosyncratic idioms, and both generalize to other verb roots
— worth flagging for the mechanics-analysis phase as two more data points in this fusional language's
prefix/reflexive-marker system, alongside whatever paradigm cases the language_corpus phase later
assembles.

### The 1950s–60s стиляги subculture as this book's most concretely dated slang cluster
Текст 1 (В. Аксёнов's "В поисках грустного бэби" excerpt) is unusual among this whole book's reading
texts in that its footnotes supply an explicit decade range for several slang terms at once — стиляга
/ стиляжка, штатник, чувиха, and клёво are all glossed as belonging to "молодёжь 50-х и 60-х годов"
(youth of the 1950s and 60s), explicitly tied to Elvis Presley's cultural influence and to a named
in-group self-designation (штатники, "fans of everything American"). This is a genuinely richer
historical/attested-era data point than the book's more typical whole-book "contemporary (source
published 1996)" default, and is worth flagging to the mechanics-analysis phase as a case where the
source itself, not just this extraction's inference, supplies real dating and sociolinguistic
grouping for a slang cluster.

### The 1990s American-loanword wave as an explicitly named sociolinguistic phenomenon
Unlike every other vocabulary section in this book, the "Word Study: Americanisms in Russian"
subsection (p. 242) is framed by the source's own prose as a phenomenon in itself — "one of the most
striking features of the Russian language in the early 1990s" — rather than as ordinary lesson
vocabulary. Its 14 items span popular culture (шоу, хит-парад), technology (софтвер, лэп-топ,
дисплей), and post-Soviet economics (ваучер, холдинговая компания, спонсор), and one item
(бэмвушка) is itself a further colloquial derivation on top of an English-derived acronym (БМВ) —
a two-layer borrowing-then-derivation case worth flagging as a template for how future slang could
plausibly form in the target conlang from any real-world loanword wave.

### Polysemous headwords recurring across this chapter with different senses
Two words show a clean split between senses documented in different parts of this same chapter:
тянуть appears both as coll. "to measure up/cope" (p.225, Текст 1) and as an impersonal "to long
for/be drawn to" (p.244, Текст 6); вредный appears both in its familiar "harmful" sense (established
in Урок 8's ecology vocabulary, file 053h) and, in this chapter, in a coll. "bad-tempered (of a
person)" sense (p.246, Текст 6). Both are worth preserving as concrete polysemy examples for the
mechanics-analysis phase, the same way душа's religious/secular-idiom/cultural-symbol split has been
tracked across Уроки 6, 8, and 9.

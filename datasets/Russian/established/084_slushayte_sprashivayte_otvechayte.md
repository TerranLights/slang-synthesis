### Three-register contrast for giving directions (p. 93)

The book explicitly frames, under a "Прохожие говорят" ("Passersby say") heading, three
grammatically distinct but functionally equivalent ways a Russian speaker gives street directions,
presented as a genuine register/formality cline rather than free variation:

- **Imperative**: Вы́йдите... поверни́те... иди́те... пройди́те... (bare command form)
- **Future perfective** (used as a softened/indirect directive): Вы́йдете... повернёте...
  пойдёте... пройдёте... (same verbs, future-tense perfective forms — grammatically a statement
  of what the addressee "will do," functioning pragmatically as a politer instruction)
- **нужно + infinitive**: (Вам) ну́жно вы́йти... поверну́ть... пойти́... пройти́... (impersonal
  necessity construction, the most indirect/formal of the three)

This is a clean, explicit three-way directive-register paradigm using a single semantic content
(street directions) held constant across all three forms — useful as a compact illustration for
comparing register-marking-via-verb-form (rather than via lexical choice) against this project's
existing T–V/lexical-choice-only register findings for Russian.

### Prefixed motion-verb narrative demonstration (p. 85–86)

Полилог 2 (p. 85–86) is built as a classroom story specifically to drill choice among prefixed
motion verbs: a student (Макс) is sent to fetch a table from a colleague, and the narrative walks
through вы́шел (exited, expecting to return soon — contrasted explicitly in-dialogue with ушёл,
"left," which implies not returning soon), пошёл (set off on foot), подошёл (approached), зашёл
(dropped into/stopped by), обошёл (went around/covered the whole floor), пришёl (arrived/came).
The teacher's own in-dialogue explanation of вышел vs. ушёл ("Макс скоро вернётся, и поэтому мы
говорим, что он вышел, а не ушёл") is a clean, quotable minimal-pair explanation of this aspectual-
prefix distinction, complementary to the systematic treatments in `028_russkie_glagoly_dvizheniya_
s_pristavkami` and `057_russian_verbs_of_motion` — this book supplies a worked narrative example
rather than a paradigm table, useful as an illustrative case for the mechanics-analysis phase.

### жени́ться / вы́йти за́муж (gender-asymmetric "to marry") construction (p. 137–138)

The book gives an explicit, diagrammed contrast (p. 138) for the two non-interchangeable Russian
verbs meaning "to marry," each restricted to one grammatical subject-gender and each governing a
different case/preposition on its object:

- **жени́ться на ком** (a man marries a woman) — subject is the жени́х (groom); governs на +
  prepositional case (жени́ться на Ка́те — "to marry Katya"). Resulting relational noun: муж
  (husband).
- **вы́йти за́муж за кого** (a woman marries a man) — subject is the неве́ста (bride); governs
  за + accusative case (вы́йти за́муж за Серге́я — "to marry Sergei"). Resulting relational noun:
  жена́ (wife).
- **пожени́ться** (reciprocal/plural — "to get married" said of the couple together) — subject is
  the pair jointly (Серге́й и Ка́тя же́нятся / пожени́лись). Resulting relational noun: cyпру́ги
  (spouses/the married couple).

This is a clean case-government-plus-gender-restriction paradigm not previously tabulated this
completely elsewhere in the corpus, worth flagging for the mechanics-analysis phase as a rare case
where Russian morphosyntax itself (not just lexical choice) is grammatically gendered by subject.

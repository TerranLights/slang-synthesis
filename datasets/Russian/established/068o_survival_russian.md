## Grammar points

### лежа́ть/класть/положи́ть and стоя́ть/ста́вить/поста́вить — Lesson 16 (p. 240-242, Tables 84-85)

A genuinely important three-way verb system for "location vs. placement" of objects, split further
by whether the object's canonical resting orientation is lying-down or standing-up: **лежа́ть**
(imperfective, "to be lying/situated," + prepositional — Кни́га лежи́т на столе́) describes a
flat/lying object's static location; **класть** (imperfective, "to lay/put," + accusative — Он
кладёт кни́гу на стол) describes the *ongoing* action of placing such an object; **положи́ть**
(perfective counterpart of класть) describes the *completed* placement. The parallel triad
стоя́ть/ста́вить/поста́вить applies the identical three-way system to objects whose canonical
resting orientation is upright (cups, bottles) rather than flat (books, plates) — the source's own
closing "Remember" notes explicitly state that *every* lying-type noun pairs with
лежа́ть/класть/положи́ть and every standing-type noun pairs with стоя́ть/ста́вить/поста́вить, i.e.
this is a genuine two-way object-orientation classification cutting across the whole noun lexicon,
not a small set of memorized exceptions — directly comparable in spirit to noun classifiers in
other languages, and a strong candidate for slang-mechanics relevance (an invented conlang could
plausibly extend or subvert this orientation-based verb-choice system).

### Emphatic-reflexive pronoun сам/сама́/са́ми — Lesson 16 (p. 243)

сам (and its gender/number-agreeing forms сама́, само́, са́ми) placed after a noun or personal
pronoun signals that the subject acted independently/without help/alone (Я сама́ пригото́вила
мя́со "I cooked the meat myself [unaided]"). This is distinct from a reflexive pronoun proper
(there is no case-marking function here) — it's a pure emphatic/intensifying particle-like use of
what is otherwise the same сам root seen elsewhere in Russian (e.g. in само́ собо́й, not covered in
this book) for "self-ness."

### Как/Ско́лько/Како́й as expressive-intensifier markers — Lesson 16 (p. 245-246), the book's final grammar point

A genuinely valuable closing grammar point: normally-interrogative words как, ско́лько, and
како́й(-ая, -ое, -ие) are repurposed as **exclamatory intensifiers** meaning roughly "so very...!"
when used in an exclamation rather than a question, disambiguated purely by intonation (falling,
emphatic) rather than any lexical marker. **как** intensifies an adverb or verb (Как вку́сно! =
"How delicious! [i.e. very delicious]," Как он игра́ет! = "He plays so well!"); **ско́лько**
intensifies a noun in the **genitive plural** specifically, meaning "so many!" (Ско́лько
подарков! = "So many presents!" — reusing the same 5+-triggers-genitive-plural numeral-agreement
logic established since Lesson 6, now applied to an intensifier rather than a literal count);
**како́й** (agreeing in gender/number with a following noun in the nominative) intensifies an
adjective+noun or a bare noun (Како́е краси́вое пла́тье! = "What a pretty dress!," reducible to
Како́е пла́тье! with the adjective dropped but the same heightened meaning implied). The source
explicitly flags that written punctuation (exclamation point vs. question mark) is the only surface
cue distinguishing Ско́лько цвето́в! ("So many flowers!") from Ско́лько цвето́в? ("How many
flowers?") — in speech, only intonation carries this distinction. This construction is directly
relevant to slang/register mechanics: it shows Russian achieving a whole intensifier/exclamative
register purely through repurposed interrogatives + prosody, with no dedicated exclamative particle
lexicon of its own — a mechanism an invented conlang could plausibly borrow, exaggerate (e.g. by
grammaticalizing a written marker where Russian only has intonation), or subvert.

## Grammar points

### T-V address system (ты vs. вы) — Lesson 1

The book's own commentary frames register choice explicitly as a function of closeness, age
difference, and length of acquaintance rather than a fixed rule: young people move quickly to
informal ты even with near-strangers; adults default to formal вы until better acquainted (p. 21).
This is the single grammar point Lesson 1 is built around, and every vocabulary pair in the lesson
(Здравствуй/Здравствуйте, Извини/Извините, твои/ваши дела́) is presented as a ты/вы doublet rather
than as free variants — a structurally load-bearing register axis for this fusional language, since
the distinction surfaces morphologically (verb/pronoun agreement) rather than through a separate
honorific lexicon.

### Russian personal-name structure — Lesson 2 (p. 26)

Three-part name system: и́мя (first/given name) + о́тчество (patronymic, derived from the father's
first name with -ович/-евич for sons, -овна/-евна for daughters) + фами́лия (last name, with
distinct masculine/feminine endings, e.g. Ивано́в/Ивано́ва). Addressing guideline given explicitly:
friends use first name or its shortened form; other adults get и́мя + о́тчество unless they
explicitly invite ты with "Дава́й на ты?" or "Мо́жно на ты?" ("Let's use ты" / "May we use ты").
Most first names have a productive shortened/diminutive form (e.g. Михаи́л → Ми́ша, Ири́на → И́ра);
when addressing someone by the full first name rather than the short form, the patronymic is
technically expected too, though the source notes native speakers don't always follow this strictly.

### Gender of nouns — Lesson 2 (p. 28)

Three genders: masculine nouns end in a consonant (друг, студе́нт, Ви́ктор); feminine nouns end in
-а/-я (подру́га, студе́нтка, Та́ня); neuter nouns end in -о/-е/-мя (о́тчество, у́тро, мо́ре, и́мя).
Nouns ending in -ь can be either gender lexically (преподава́тель is m., тетра́дь is f. — must be
memorized per noun). Two exceptions flagged: (1) a handful of -а/-я nouns (колле́га) take gender
from the referent's actual sex rather than their ending; (2) male first names ending in -а/-я
(e.g. presumably names like Ники́та, not directly exemplified here) decline like feminine nouns
despite referring to men — a genuine declension-class/semantic-gender mismatch worth flagging for
mechanics analysis, since it shows Russian's noun-class system is not purely semantic.

### Possessive pronouns — Lesson 2 (p. 29, Table 2)

Possessive pronouns (мой/моя́/моё/мои́, твой/твоя́/твоё/твои́, наш/на́ша/на́ше/на́ши, ваш/ва́ша/
ва́ше/ва́ши) agree in gender/number/case with the noun they modify, not with the possessor's own
gender — a fusional-morphology agreement pattern distinct from English's invariant "my/your."
Third-person его́/её/их are invariant (don't agree with the modified noun) since they're frozen
genitive pronoun forms rather than true possessive adjectives — the source doesn't state this
etymological point explicitly but the paradigm table shows его́/её repeating unchanged across all
four gender/number columns while мой/твой/наш/ваш vary, confirming the difference in kind.

### Nominative/accusative of personal pronouns — Lesson 2 (p. 29, Table 3)

Full paradigm given (я→меня́, ты→тебя́, он→его́, она́→её, оно́→его́, мы→нас, вы→вас, они́→их).
Footnoted grammar point: the verb зва́ть ("to call/name") governs its logical subject in the
accusative case in the "what's-your-name" construction (Меня́ зову́т... — lit. "[they] call me...",
an impersonal construction with no overt subject), which is why "What is your name?" surfaces as
Как вас/тебя́ зову́т? with the addressee in the accusative rather than nominative.

### Formation of Russian last names and patronymics — Lesson 2 (p. 30, Tables 4-5)

Last names built on a consonant-final masculine stem take -а for the feminine (Ивано́в/Ивано́ва,
Петро́в/Петро́ва, Пу́шкин/Пу́шкина, Ту́рбин/Ту́рбина). Patronymics are built from the father's
first name: -ович/-евич (masculine) and -овна/-евна (feminine), e.g. Ива́н → Ива́нович/Ива́новна,
Серге́й → Серге́евич/Серге́евна (the -евич/-евна allomorph appears after names ending in a
vowel/й, -ович/-овна after a consonant — inferable from the paired examples though not stated as an
explicit rule by the source).

### Plural nouns — Lesson 3 (p. 37, Table 6)

Regular plural formation shown by gender class: masculine hard-stem nouns take -ы (студе́нт→
студе́нты, банк→ба́нки with a spelling-rule к→и after velars), masculine -ь nouns take -и
(преподава́тель→преподава́тели); feminine -а nouns take -ы/-и by the same velar/sibilant spelling
rule (фи́рма→фи́рмы, шко́ла→шко́лы, but студе́нтка→студе́нтки); neuter -о/-е/-мя nouns take -а/-я
(отчество→отчества, мо́ре→моря́, упражне́ние→упражне́ния). This is presented as the book's first
systematic declension table and directly follows from the three-gender system established in
Lesson 2.

## Grammar points (terse — standard genitive-case material, largely redundant with prior
established/ sources)

**Usage (pp. 90–93):** the genitive case's many functions — possession; "of"/whose; quantity after
ско́лько/не́сколько/мно́го/немно́го/ма́ло; negation/lack (нет, не́ было, не бу́дет + genitive); direct
object of a negated verb; dates; governed by prepositions без/вро́де/для/до/из/кро́ме/о́коло/от/
по́сле/с(о)/у; the отку́да? triad (из↔в, с↔на, от↔к mapping "going to" prepositions to their "coming
from" genitive-case counterparts); the "at someone's home" construction with у; numeral-governed
noun case (genitive singular after 2–4/22–24/etc., genitive plural after 5–20/25–30/etc.); the
partitive genitive (some bread/tea, with special partitive forms сы́ру, ри́су, са́хару, ча́ю); fixed
connecting phrases (с одно́й стороны́..., но с друго́й стороны́...; с (мое́й) то́чки зре́ния...).
Standard, matches prior established/ sources.

**Singular/plural genitive endings (pp. 94–98):** full paradigm tables for hard/soft adjectives and
nouns; the exception that masculine nouns with feminine-looking endings (дéдушка) take feminine
endings; genitive plural endings by final-consonant class (-ей after ч/ш/щ/ж/ь; -ов/-ев after
zero/-й; -ий after -ия/-ие; zero-ending for most neuter -о/-е/-мя and feminine -a/-я, some with
filler vowels: о́кон, пи́сем, знамён, имён, же́нщин, де́вушек, сестёр, книг, газе́т, ма́рок,
лаборато́рий; -ей for feminine -ь: матере́й, дочере́й, площаде́й, тетра́дей); further irregular
exceptions: -ья→-ей for masc./neut. (муже́й, сыновей); -ья→-ьев for masc./neut. with soft sign
(бра́тьев, сту́льев, пе́рьев); zero-genitive-plural-equals-nominative-singular nouns (солда́т, глаз,
раз); dual genitive-plural forms of челове́к (челове́к with numbers/ско́лько/не́сколько vs. люде́й
with мно́го/ма́ло); plural-only-noun genitives (де́ньги→де́нег, ша́хматы→ша́хмат, похороны→похоро́н,
щи→щей, о́вощи→ове́щей, часы́→часо́в, очки́→очко́в, брю́ки→брюк); irregular genitive plurals (сосе́д→
сосе́дей, мо́ре→море́й, по́ле→поле́й, дя́дя→дя́дей, тётя→тётей). Standard closed lists, captured in
fuller detail as reusable reference tables per the same rationale as `074e`.

**Personal pronouns (pp. 99–100):** third-person genitive pronouns (его́/её/их) add an н- when
governed by a preposition (у него́, к ней, с ни́ми); distinguishing personal-pronoun genitive/
accusative forms from look-alike possessive modifiers его́/её/их by substitution-test (swap in
меня́/тебя́/нас/вас for personal pronouns vs. моего́/твоего́/на́шего/ва́шего for possessives).
Standard.

**Last names (pp. 100–102):** Russian surnames in -ов/-ев or -ын/-ин decline as nouns (masculine)
or adjectives (feminine, and both genders in the plural) — Аса́нов/Аса́нова/Аса́новы →
Аса́нова/Аса́новой/Аса́новых; women's surnames ending in a consonant do not decline at all (Андре́я
Смит stays Андре́я Смит in the genitive), unlike a man's identical-looking surname (Андре́й Смит →
Андре́я Сми́та). Standard but a genuinely useful closed rule for a fusional language's proper-noun
morphology.

**Possession: есть and нет (pp. 102–105):** the у кого́ есть/не́ было/не бу́дет construction
(possessor in у + genitive, possessed thing in nominative, verb agrees with the possessed thing,
not the possessor); есть used only when existence itself is emphasized, dropped when a quality of
the already-established possession is being described; the parallel "thing possesses thing"
construction using в/на + prepositional case + (имéется/имéются for formal register); имéть used
specifically for abstractions (честь, возмо́жность); genitive-of-negation for lack of possession,
never есть in a negative construction; ни...ни... + genitive for "neither...nor..." double
negation. Standard, but the и́мéть-for-abstractions vs. у-constructions-for-concrete-possession
split is a clean, reusable rule.

**Subordination: то/что and то/как clauses (pp. 105–106):** то/что and то/как as "hinges" connecting
a genitive-requiring verb/construction (занима́ться тем что...; удивля́ться тому́ что...; боя́ться
того́ что...) to a following clause. Standard.

**Numbers (pp. 106–110):** genitive number-noun forms when the whole numeral phrase is governed by
a genitive-requiring preposition/verb; genitive ordinal-number-adjective for a year within a date
(the century digit omitted if it's the current one, e.g. в ма́е шестьдеся́т второ́го го́да = "in May
of '62"); full month-name nominative/genitive table (янва́рь/января́ ... декабрь/декабря́). Standard.

## Register/literary findings

### "Russian Saying" boxes
- **Нет ды́ма без огня́** ("No smoke without fire") — p. 102.
- **Не имéй сто рублéй, а имéй сто друзéй** ("Don't possess a hundred rubles, possess a hundred
  friends" / "Friends are what matter most in life") — p. 108.
- **От копéечной свечи́ Москвá сгорéла** ("Moscow burned down from a candle worth one kopeck" / "A
  small leak will sink a great ship") — p. 109. A historically loaded proverb given Moscow's
  repeated real fires (including the 1812 fire referenced in Unit 7's Lermontov text).

### ТЕКСТ 8а — contemporary press article on HIV/AIDS policy in Russia
An AIDS-awareness piece (source genre unspecified beyond "article") citing Reagan's and Clinton's
public statements on AIDS, contrasting American and Russian epidemiological trajectories (WHO
figures: 13 million infected worldwide as of the mid-1990s, projected 40 million by 2000; parts of
Central Africa cited at 50–70% HIV prevalence with one-third of pregnant women as carriers), then
pivoting to Russian-specific criticism: HIV-positive children's mothers "fairly accuse the state" of
criminal negligence by medical personnel in state clinics as the transmission vector, and the
author (speaking as executive secretary of a charitable AIDS society) frames "Мы и вы" ("Us and
You") as the organization's deliberately chosen name — a call for HIV-positive and HIV-negative
people to engage as equals, symbolized by "two hands extended toward each other" on the
organization's emblem. Genuinely non-redundant register content: a 1990s Russian public-health/
NGO-advocacy register text with vocabulary distinct from standard teaching-grammar material (ВИЧ-
позити́вный "HIV-positive," инфици́ровать "to infect," дальне́йшее распростране́ние "further spread,"
безразли́чное отноше́ние "indifferent attitude," равнопра́вный диало́г "dialogue among equals").

### ТЕКСТ 8б — Pushkin, stanza from *Евгéний Онéгин* ("Eugene Onegin"), 1833
The narrator addresses "почтéнные супрýги" ("honorable spouses/husbands") and "ма́меньки"
(diminutive-affectionate "mommies/mothers") with a mock-solemn warning to watch their wives and
daughters more closely at balls, using their lorgnettes (лорнéт) to keep watch, closing on the ironic
self-deprecating note "Я э́то потому́ пишу́, / Что уж давно́ я не грешу́" ("I write this because I
myself haven't sinned in a long while" — i.e., the narrator, now too old to seduce anyone himself,
warns others of what he used to do). A classic ironic-narrator register moment; ма́менька as an
archaic/literary diminutive address form distinct from modern ма́ма.

### ТЕКСТ 8в — Daniil Kharms, «Голубáя тетрáдь номер 10» ("Blue Notebook Number 10"), 1937
A very short absurdist prose piece: a "redheaded man" who had no eyes, no ears, no hair (so calling
him "redheaded" was only a convention — усло́вно), no mouth, no nose, no arms, no legs, no stomach,
no spine, no internal organs — "NOTHING WAS THERE!" ("НИЧЕГО́ НЕ БЫ́ЛО!"), so it's unclear who is
even being discussed, and the narrator concludes "we'd better not talk about him anymore." A
canonical piece of Kharms's OBERIU-adjacent absurdist minimalism, systematically negating a
described subject's every attribute until nothing remains — the book flags 1937 (peak Stalinist
Terror) as historically significant and asks students to reflect on the date's importance, an
implicit invitation to read the story as veiled commentary on erasure/disappearance under the
purges, though the book does not state this reading outright.

### ТЕКСТ 8г — tabloid-style press article, "Ла́йза Минне́лли у ру́сских попроси́ла льда" ("Liza
Minnelli Asked the Russians for Ice")
A satirical/gossip-register news piece on Liza Minnelli's impending Moscow concert tour, itemizing
her "unpretentious" backstage requests (a carton of Marlboro, 18 white bath towels, 12 hand towels,
a makeup artist, two dressers and a translator) with heavy authorial irony, noting she can't avoid
the "traditional" tourist circuit (Kremlin, Red Square, the State Collection of Jewels/Алмáзный
фонд) and additionally wants to visit a military unit — organizers are negotiating with the Taman
Division, described as "experienced in relations with stage stars" (a dry dig at Soviet-legacy
military PR machinery). The catering rider is given in comic bureaucratic detail: only beer will be
served as alcohol (with "горячи́тельные напи́тки," lit. "warming drinks," used ironically for
alcoholic drinks broadly, then undercut since only beer is allowed); ten vegetarians in her troupe
caused "some complications" for menu planning; the draft menu includes Gatorade, two kinds of milk,
diet and regular Coca-Cola, honey, cheese, crackers, fruit balls, juices, mineral water, and "lots
and lots of ice" — closing with a sardonic "we hope this won't affect her vocal cords in any way."
Genuinely rich, distinctly 1990s post-Soviet tabloid-register vocabulary (гримёр "makeup artist,"
костюмёрша "wardrobe/dresser [f.]," дивизия "division [military]," эстра́дные звёзды "pop/stage
stars," вегетериа́нцы, горячи́тельные напи́тки as an ironic euphemism).

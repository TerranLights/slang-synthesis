## Grammar points (terse — standard aspect-usage material, redundant with prior established/
sources' aspect chapters)

Full standard treatment of imperfective/perfective usage across future, past, and infinitive
contexts: the "ask first, can I use the perfective?" heuristic; adverbs requiring imperfective
(всегда, часто, иногда, редко, долго, duration-accusative phrases) vs. those merely associated with
perfective (наконец, вдруг) vs. aspect-by-tense adverbs (когда-нибудь/никогда: imperfective in
past, perfective in future); verbs of beginning/continuing/ending always taking an imperfective
infinitive; the "intention" test for negated past-tense aspect choice (a nice compact example set:
scenarios 1–4 distinguishing "I didn't see [intended]" (perfective) from "I never noticed
[unintended]" (imperfective)); the две different meanings of нельзя + aspect (нельзя +
imperfective = forbidden; нельзя + perfective = physically impossible). pp. 44–59. Standard,
fully redundant with prior established/ aspect chapters — though the нельзя-forbidden-vs-impossible
distinction and the intention-based negation test are unusually clean formalized versions of rules
often left vaguer in other grammars, worth a cross-reference flag for future synthesis work on how
Russian aspect could inform a derived slang/register system's own aspect-like distinctions.

## Register/literary findings

### "Russian Saying" boxes (3 more)
- **Поспеши́шь — люде́й насмеши́шь** ("If you rush, people will laugh at you" / "Haste makes
  waste") — p. 47.
- **Москва́ не сра́зу стро́илась** ("Moscow wasn't built right away" / "Rome wasn't built in a
  day") — p. 52. A clean example of a Russian proverb substituting its own capital city for the
  Latin-world's "Rome" — the same proverb-structure exported with a culturally localized landmark.
- **Зна́ет ко́шка, чьё мя́со съе́ла** ("The cat knows whose meat she's eaten" / "A guilty
  conscience needs no accuser") — p. 54.

### ТЕКСТ 5а — Sasha Sokolov, *Школа для дураков* ("School for Fools," 1976) — flagship register find
This is the richest single literary excerpt encountered in this book so far. The passage is one
extended, breathless run-on sentence (the author's deliberate style) imagining an entire female
life trajectory from girlhood to old age, told entirely through a chain of infinitive-phrase
predictions. Its density of ordinary, unglamorous adult-life vocabulary — much of it mildly
disenchanted or ironic in tone — is exceptional and directly useful for future slang-mechanics
analysis of how ordinary/jaded adult register works in Russian. Representative phrases from the
passage (see Vocabulary table below for a fuller list): getting married, reading serious books,
being late to work, buying furniture, gossiping on the phone for hours, washing stockings, getting
drunk on wine, envying neighbors and birds, following weather reports, dusting, counting kopecks,
waiting to get pregnant, going to the dentist, blushing, moaning at a lover's touch, applying
mascara, paying fines, leafing through magazines, burying relatives, making noise with dishes,
trying smoking, being impudent to the boss, complaining of migraines, dreaming of a trip abroad,
thinking about suicide, cursing broken elevators, saving for a rainy day, singing romantic songs,
keeping old photographs, getting promoted, aging irreparably, cursing the government, living "on
autopilot," taking tranquilizers, cursing her husband, dieting, visiting parents, lying to friends
and relatives, and borrowing money. The book's own post-reading task (item 3) explicitly directs
students to "pick out five expressions you'd like to add to your own vocabulary" — treating this
passage as a deliberate vocabulary-mining exercise, which supports extracting more of its lexical
range than a typical drill passage would warrant. One item is a nonce/invented compound word the
author coins for comic effect (вельвет-драп-батист-шёлк-сатин-цветастый-сафьян, a portmanteau
naming "several different kinds of fabric" — footnoted by the source as literally made up), a
second Khlebnikov-style playful coinage distinct from Unit 1's.

### ТЕКСТ 5б — Solzhenitsyn, *В круге первом* ("The First Circle," 1968)
A dialogue between Nerzhin, a scientist-prisoner in a special research camp ("sharashka"), and his
jailer Trofimovich, in which the jailer suggests early release/quarters in Moscow "in the event of
the success of your work as a cryptograph," removing his conviction from the record. Nerzhin
reacts with bitter, angry sarcasm, rejecting the offer as a "sop" (подачка) and insisting that "for
one's way of thinking one cannot be sent to jail" — dissident/Gulag-era political vocabulary
(снять судимость "have one's conviction expunged," досрочно освободят "will be released early,"
враг народа-adjacent register, though that specific phrase appears in the next text). The source's
own post-reading task explicitly asks students to find "all the words that might be relevant to the
topic 'dissident'" — an explicit register-tagging instruction from the book itself.

### ТЕКСТ 5в — N. Arzhak [pseudonym of Iu. M. Daniel'], *Говорит Москва* ("This Is Moscow
Speaking," 1960–1961)
A lighter, comic dacha (summer-house) scene: friends drinking heavily, staying up late, running
around in their underwear, doing pull-ups, and one character (Volod'ka Margulis) pouring well-water
over himself despite being known to never wash up in the mornings because he's always late for
work. Notable for its irreverent register describing carefree/dissolute young-adult behavior,
useful for contrast against the historically weighted texts elsewhere in this unit. Author note:
Arzhak/Daniel' was himself famously tried and imprisoned in the 1965–66 Sinyavsky–Daniel trial for
publishing dissident work abroad under a pseudonym — worth flagging as biographical context relevant
to how "authentic literary excerpt" selections in this book sometimes carry real political weight
beyond their surface content.

### ТЕКСТ 5г — L. Vasil'eva, *Кремлёвские жёны* ("Wives of the Kremlin," 1994)
An excerpt on the disputed/mysterious death of Nadezhda Allilueva, Stalin's second wife — laying
out three competing hypotheses (Stalin killed her himself out of jealousy; he ordered his guards to
kill her; her own "accomplices" got rid of her, fearing she'd betray them as a group of political
oppositionists — "враги народа," "enemies of the people," the actual Stalin-era phrase, appears
directly in this excerpt). A genuinely historically loaded text; the author explicitly declines to
endorse any version ("я не даю им оценок" — "I do not evaluate them"), presenting all three as
unverifiable rumor. Directly useful historical-register vocabulary: враги наро́да ("enemies of the
people"), группа «девяносто двух» ("the group of ninety-two" — an apparent purge-era group
designation), убийство/самоубийство ("murder"/"suicide").

### ТЕКСТ 5д — Maiakovskii, untitled poem (1930, his last, written shortly before his suicide)
A poem addressed to a former lover, written in the middle of the night, reflecting on the end of a
relationship with bitter, resigned tenderness. Contains the now-famous idiom "любо́вная ло́дка
разби́лась о быт" ("the boat of love has crashed on [the rocks of] daily life") — a widely quoted
Russian literary phrase functioning almost as a set idiom in cultured speech for a relationship
undone by mundane practicalities rather than dramatic betrayal. Also contains a deliberate
punning coinage, инциде́нт испе́рчен (literally suggesting "peppered"/finished, playing on
исчерпан "exhausted/concluded"), explicitly flagged by the source as wordplay.

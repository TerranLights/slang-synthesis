## Grammar points

### 1.1 The parts of speech

The book's short list of основные (main) parts of speech, given as the foundation for everything
that follows: verb (глагол), noun (имя существительное), pronoun (местоимение), adjective (имя
прилагательное), adverb (наречие), preposition (предлог). Presented as a table with no further
elaboration at this point — deeper treatment is deferred to specific punctuation chapters (p. 1).

### 1.2 Definition of a sentence

A sentence (предложение) is a group of words conveying a complete thought. Because Russian has no
articles and typically omits the present-tense forms of быть "to be," single-word sentences are
markedly more common in Russian than in English (e.g. Тишина. "It's quiet."; Вечер. "It's evening.").
Response phrases (релятивы, e.g. Спасибо "Thanks," Пожалуйста "You're welcome"), interjections
(междометия, e.g. Ох! "Oh!"), and particles (частицы, e.g. Ну? "Well?") can likewise constitute
complete one-word sentences on their own when they convey a complete thought (pp. 2–3).

### 1.3 Primary sentence elements

The subject (подлежащее) and predicate (сказуемое) are the "primary sentence elements" (главные
члены предложения). A sentence with exactly one subject and one predicate is a простое предложение
"simple sentence"; if both consist of a single word each, it's a простое нераспространённое
предложение "simple non-extended sentence." Some Russian predicates contain no verb at all, instead
consisting of a noun/pronoun/adjective called a предикате nominal (именное сказуемое), answering
кто? "who?" or каков?/какой? "what kind of?" — Russian typically omits the copula (глагол-связка,
e.g. English "is"/"are") in the present tense, leaving the predicate nominal to stand alone as the
whole predicate (e.g. Пушкин — поэт. "Pushkin is a poet.") (pp. 3–4).

### 1.4 Intonational and communicative sentence types

Each sentence serves one of a limited set of communicative functions — statement, question,
request, or exclamation — distinguished in speech via intonation, word order, or function words.
Intonation plays an outsized role in Russian specifically, more so than in English, because Russian
word order is comparatively free and therefore less available as a disambiguating signal on its own
(p. 4).

#### 1.4.1 The narrative sentence

A narrative sentence (повествовательное предложение) states a fact or gives a description, and is
spoken with a gently falling intonation toward the end (p. 4).

#### 1.4.2 The interrogative sentence

An interrogative sentence (вопросительное предложение) asks a question, and Russian distinguishes
several distinct question-intonation patterns:

- **Question-word questions**: containing a question word (кто?, что?, чей?, какой?, где?, куда?,
  как?, почему?, сколько?) or an equivalent prepositional phrase (e.g. Какого цвета...? "What
  color...?"); the question word/phrase itself carries the heavier intonational emphasis (pp. 4–5).
- **Questions without a question word**: Russian word order alone doesn't distinguish these from
  statements, so a special rising-then-falling intonation, centered on whichever word carries the
  logical emphasis, does the disambiguating work instead. The book gives minimal triplets showing
  the same words, Он придёт завтра?, yielding three distinct questions purely by shifting which word
  bears the intonation peak (pp. 5–6).
- **Questions with the particle ли**: ли softens a request for information or makes it sound more
  rhetorical; the emphasized word comes first, with ли immediately following (e.g. Придёт ли он
  сегодня? "Will he come tomorrow (or not)?"). Intonation pattern is the same as the no-question-word
  case above (p. 6).
- **"Question-in-a-series" intonation**: a distinct rising intonation (regardless of whether a
  question word is present) used specifically for a routine series of questions, e.g. reciting
  intake-form questions one after another (Ваша фамилия? Имя? Отчество? "Your surname? First name?
  Patronymic?"). Since statements are never intoned this way, this pattern is unambiguous as a
  question-in-a-series marker (p. 6).

#### 1.4.3 The imperative sentence

An imperative sentence (побудительное предложение) is uttered to cause someone else to act, and
carries intonation similar to question-word questions — more forceful than typical English
imperative intonation. Formed via the imperative verb form itself, давай(те) + first-person-plural
future ("let's..."), or пусть + third person ("let X do it") (pp. 6–7).

#### 1.4.4 The exclamatory sentence

An exclamatory sentence (восклицательное предложение) is any statement, question, or imperative
pronounced with heightened emotion and greater relative loudness — i.e. it's an intonational overlay
on the three other communicative types rather than a fourth independent structural type (p. 7).

### 1.5 Information structure in a sentence

Because Russian word order is comparatively free of purely grammatical constraints, it is more
available than English word order for pragmatic/stylistic purposes — specifically, marking which
part of the sentence is the topic (тема, the "old"/already-known information, which normally comes
first) versus the comment (рема, the "new" information, which normally comes last). This holds
regardless of whether the topic/comment role falls on the subject or the predicate:

- When the **subject is the comment** (new information), it moves to the end of the sentence (e.g.
  Подъехал автобус. "A bus drove up." — note the English indefinite article "a" doing the
  equivalent marking job).
- When the **predicate is the comment** (new information) and the subject is the topic, the subject
  moves to the sentence-initial position instead (e.g. Автобус ушёл. "The bus left." — note the
  English definite article "the" doing the equivalent marking job).

English, lacking Russian's word-order freedom, instead uses articles (a/the) and existential
constructions ("there is..."/"it is...") to mark topic vs. comment status — a genuine typological
contrast the book draws directly, useful context for how case-marking languages redistribute
information-structure work that English partly hands to articles (pp. 7–9).

## Grammar points

### Determinative pronouns: весь, каждый, любой, сам
Весь (all/the whole), каждый (every), любой (any) and сам (-self) all clarify who/what is
involved in an action. Весь declines fully across all six cases and both numbers like an
adjective; каждый declines like an unstressed adjective (новый-type); любой declines like a
stressed adjective (молодой-type); сам declines like этот. p.111.

### The reflexive pronoun себя
Себя ("self") refers back to the subject of the verb and has no nominative form; the same word
covers myself/yourself/himself/etc. It declines: acc./gen. себя, dat./prep. себе, instr. собой. It
is often required after prepositions where English would not literally state "self" (Он берёт с
собой фотоаппарат, "he's bringing the camera with him"). It also forms fixed idioms: вести себя
(to behave), представлять себе (to imagine), чувствовать себя (to feel). pp.113–114.

### Который as a relative pronoun
Который ("who, which, that") links two clauses, referring to both people and things, and is
always set off by commas. As an adjective it takes gender/number from the noun it follows but its
case from its own grammatical role in the second clause (subject, object, or object of a
preposition — the preposition always precedes который). Который is only used after nouns; after
verbs, "that" is что instead (Я думаю, что они приедут). pp.115–116.

### Что and кто as relative pronouns
Beyond their interrogative use, что functions as a relative pronoun after всё/то and to "sum up"
a preceding clause (as English "which" can refer to a whole prior idea); кто is used after тот
("the one who...") and все ("everyone who...") and always takes a singular verb. pp.117–118.

### Indefinite particles -то and -нибудь
Added to кто, что, какой (and adverbs где, куда, когда, как, почему), -то marks a specific but
unidentified referent (someone did call, we just don't know who), while -нибудь marks a vague,
non-specific referent (anyone at all). pp.119–120.

### Present tense: 1st conjugation (regular -ать/-ять/-еть)
Verbs whose infinitive ends in -ать (occasionally -ять or -еть) form the present tense by
dropping -ть and adding -ю, -ешь, -ет, -ем, -ете, -ют. Russian has only one present tense form
covering English's "I work / I do work / I am working." pp.121–122.

### Present tense: 2nd conjugation (regular -ить/-еть/-ять/-ать)
Verbs whose infinitive ends in -ить (occasionally -еть, -ять, or -ать) belong to the "second
conjugation," forming the present tense with -ю(-у), -ишь, -ит, -им, -ите, -ят(-ат). pp.123–124.

### Consonant mutation in 2nd-conjugation verbs
Some 2nd-conjugation verb stems change their final consonant only in the я-form of the present
tense: д→ж (видеть→вижу), з→ж (возить→вожу), с→ш (носить→ношу), ст→щ (свистеть→свищу), т→ч
(лететь→лечу); stems ending in б/в/м/п/ф add an extra -л- (готовить→готовлю). p.125.

### Irregular present-tense verbs
Two families of irregular present-tense endings are presented: one with я/они forms in -у and
all others in -ё (брать, ждать, жить, идти, класть; and лить/петь/пить with -ю/-ют), and another
with я/они in -у(-ю) and all others in -е (ехать, искать, мыть, писать, плакать). Мочь and хотеть
have their own irregular stem-changing paradigms. pp.127–129.

### Verbs in -авать, -овать, -евать; expressing "to be" and "to have"
Давать (-авать), путешествовать (-овать), and танцевать (-евать) each show a distinct but
regular pattern for these infinitive types. Because быть has no present tense, Russian expresses
"is/are" via a dash (defining a noun), via zero copula (with an adverb), via есть (existence), or
via нет + genitive (non-existence/absence). "Have" is expressed with у + genitive of the owner +
nominative of the thing owned (У нас дом); "don't have" uses у + genitive + нет + genitive of the
thing lacking. p.131.

### Reflexive verbs
Reflexive verbs end in -ся/-сь. Some genuinely mean "...oneself" (умываться, одеваться,
раздеваться, причёсываться, купаться, готовиться, ложиться спать); others are intransitive
"to be..." verbs with no literal self-reference in English (находиться "to be situated",
начинаться/кончаться "to begin/end"); a third group covers feelings (беспокоиться, бояться,
гордиться, надеяться, смеяться, улыбаться). pp.133–134.

### Aspect: imperfective vs. perfective
Most Russian verbs have two infinitives — imperfective and perfective (a small minority have
only one; verbs of motion have three). The imperfective covers the present tense, compound
future, and imperfective past, and always frames an action as process/incomplete/ongoing/
habitual. The perfective covers the simple future and perfective past, and always frames an
action as a completed result. Aspect pairs form in three patterns: perfective = imperfective +
prefix (писать/написать); conjugation shift, 1st→2nd (решать/решить); or fully suppletive pairs
(говорить/сказать, возвращаться/вернуться, садиться/сесть). Certain verbs (кончать/кончить,
начинать/начать, продолжать, переставать/перестать) always take an imperfective infinitive
complement regardless of their own aspect. pp.135–136.

### Compound future
Formed from the future tense of быть (буду, будешь, будет, будем, будете, будут) + an
imperfective infinitive; used for incomplete, repeated, or ongoing future actions. If futurity is
implied by context (e.g. inside "when"-clauses), the future must still be used explicitly in
Russian even where English uses a present tense. pp.137–138.

### Simple future
Formed identically to the present-tense endings but built on the perfective infinitive instead;
expresses single, specific, completed future actions. A handful of common verbs have irregular
perfective simple-future stems: вставать/встать, давать/дать, ложиться/лечь, садиться/сесть,
говорить/сказать (and other -казать verbs by the same pattern), мочь/смочь. pp.139–140.

### Imperfective past tense
Formed by dropping -ть from the imperfective infinitive and adding gender/number-agreeing
endings (-л/-ла/-ло/-ли, reflexive -лся/-лась/-лось/-лись). Used for repeated, ongoing,
interrupted actions, for durations of time, and for descriptions of weather/mood/appearance
(быть exists only in this aspect for past-tense description). A handful of irregular non--ть
infinitives (везти, вести, есть, идти, мочь, нести) form their own irregular past stems.
pp.141–142.

### Perfective past tense
Formed the same way as the imperfective past (same endings), but from the perfective infinitive;
describes a single completed action, with the same gender/number agreement rule. Russian has no
separate pluperfect — the perfective past covers "I wrote / I have written / I had written"
alike, governed by whether the action was completed before the reference point. pp.143–144.

### Verbs of motion (indefinite/definite/perfective triads)
A distinctive Russian system: verbs like "to walk," "to run," "to carry" etc. have two
imperfective infinitives (indefinite, for habitual/repeated/return-trip actions, e.g. ходить; and
definite, for a single action in progress in one direction, e.g. идти) plus one perfective
(pойти, for a single completed departure/action). The same three-way pattern applies to бегать/
бежать/побежать, водить/вести/повести, возить/везти/повезти, ездить/ехать/поехать, летать/лететь/
полететь, носить/нести/понести, плавать/плыть/поплыть. pp.145–146.

### Prefixed verbs of motion
Adding directional prefixes to the definite/perfective motion-verb stems (-ходить/-йти) yields a
large family of precise directional verbs: входить/войти (enter), выходить/выйти (exit),
доходить/дойти (as far as), заходить/зайти (pop in), отходить/отойти (move away), переходить/
перейти (cross), подходить/подойти (approach), приходить/прийти (arrive — perfective drops the
й: приду), проходить/пройти (pass), сходить/сойти (get off), уходить/уйти (leave). Each prefix
also dictates a specific governing preposition/case (e.g. в/на + acc. after входить, из/с + gen.
after выходить). The same prefix set attaches to other motion-verb pairs (-бегать/-бежать,
-водить/-вести, -возить/-везти, -летать/-лететь, -носить/-нести, -плавать/-плыть, and
-езжать/-ехать, the only vowel-initial pair, where под→подъ and с→съ before the vowel). pp.147–148.

### Open conditions (future/present)
An "open" condition still has a real chance of occurring. If the condition is set in the future,
Russian requires the future tense in *both* clauses (mirroring the seemingly redundant "if it
will be fine..., we will go..."). If the condition describes an ongoing present situation, the
present tense is used in both clauses instead. Modal words like надо/нельзя also take the future
of быть to project into future time (Завтра нельзя будет...). pp.149–150.

### Closed/hypothetical conditions (если + бы)
For conditions that can no longer happen, or are purely hypothetical, Russian uses если with the
past tense of the verb plus the particle бы, which must appear twice — once in the "if" clause and
once in the main clause (separated by a comma). There is no separate conditional-perfect tense:
the same construction covers both "would" and "would have." бы's position can shift onto whatever
word needs emphasis. Desires can also be phrased with бы without an если-clause at all (Я очень
хотел(а) бы...). pp.151–152.

### The imperative
Formed from either the ты/вы form of the present tense (imperfective, for habitual/friendly
commands) or the simple future (perfective, for one-off or urgent commands): drop the last three
letters of that form, then add -й/-йте (if a vowel remains) or -и/-ите (if a consonant remains).
Negative commands generally use the imperfective (except for warnings, which use the perfective).
A handful of common verbs have irregular imperatives: есть→ешь!/ешьте!, пить→пей!/пейте!,
быть→будь!/будьте!, помочь→помоги!/помогите!, вставать→вставай!/вставайте!. Official written
requests may use the bare infinitive (Просьба не беспокоить); давай(те) + мы-form expresses
"let's...". pp.153–154.

### Negation with conjugated verbs
Не precedes the conjugated verb directly. Нет (+ genitive) expresses "there is/are not" since
быть has no present tense. A family of double-negative words (ничего, никогда, нигде, никуда,
никто) always co-occur with не and a conjugated verb — Russian has no single-negative equivalent
of English "anywhere/anything." Ничего and никто decline like что/кто; an intervening preposition
goes after the ни- element (ни о ком, ни о чём). pp.155–156.

### Negation with the infinitive
A separate, stress-shifted family of negatives (негде, некуда, некого, некогда, нечего) pairs
with an infinitive rather than a conjugated verb, expressing "there's nowhere/no one/no time/
nothing to..." The person affected is expressed via the dative case. These can be projected into
past/future time using была/будет. Нечего can also mean "there's no point"; не за что means "don't
mention it." pp.157–158.

### Можно/нельзя/надо/пора and related modal words
Можно (possible/permitted), нельзя (impossible/forbidden), надо/нужно (necessary), and пора
(time to) are all impersonal predicates taking dative + infinitive. With нельзя specifically, the
choice of imperfective vs. perfective infinitive changes the meaning: imperfective = "not allowed
to," perfective = "not physically possible to." Можно alone (as a one-word question/answer)
asks/grants permission; возможно is a possibility-only synonym (no permission sense). pp.159–160.

### Indirect statements and questions
Unlike English, Russian does not "backshift" tense in reported speech — the tense of the original
direct statement/question is preserved in the indirect version (only the person of the verb may
change). Indirect questions are introduced with ли ("whether"), never если; word order is
typically [verb of asking] + comma + [verb] + ли + [subject]. pp.161–162.

### Indirect commands, wishes, advice (чтобы + past tense)
When one person wants/orders/advises another to do something, чтобы + the past tense (imperfective
or perfective per the usual criteria) is used — this is Russian's functional equivalent of the
subjunctive. Чтобы is never used with present or future tense, and is always preceded by a comma.
Common governing verbs: говорить/сказать, желать/пожелать, предлагать/предложить, рекомендовать/
отрекомендовать, приказывать/приказать, советовать/посоветовать, требовать/потребовать.
pp.163–164.

### Purpose clauses (чтобы)
When the subject is the same across both clauses, чтобы takes the infinitive (Я звоню тебе, чтобы
передать новости, "I'm calling to pass on the news"); чтобы is often dropped entirely after verbs
of motion. When the subject changes between clauses, чтобы takes the past tense instead (Я звоню
тебе, чтобы ты понял правду, "...so that you'd understand"). pp.165–166.

### Impersonal constructions
Many Russian expressions of feeling/opinion/ability use the third-person singular verb with no
definite subject, combined with the dative case of the experiencer: мне кажется ("it seems to
me"), мне надоело ("I'm fed up"), нам удалось ("we managed to"), тебе хочется ("do you feel like").
Since быть has no present tense, many such constructions are just dative + adverb (мне холодно,
"I'm cold"). Нравиться ("to please") inverts subject/object relative to English "to like" — the
liked thing is grammatically the subject. Жаль ("sorry for") takes dative of the person feeling
sorry and accusative of the one pitied. pp.167–168.

### Conjunctions
И (and) needs no comma unless linking clauses with different subjects; и...и = "both...and." А
(and/but, contrastive) always takes a preceding comma. Но (but, incompatible ideas, sense of
"despite/however") always takes a preceding comma. Или (or, no comma) vs. или...или (either...or,
comma required); negative equivalent ни...ни (neither...nor, comma required). Что ("that") cannot
be omitted the way English can drop it, and is always comma-preceded. Cause is expressed by
потому что, так как, or из-за того, что (each with its own comma placement convention). pp.169–170.

### Prepositions в and на (location vs. motion)
В + prepositional = "in/at" (location); в + accusative = "into/to" (motion). На + prepositional
= "on/at" (location); на + accusative = "on to/to" (motion). A fixed, memorizable list of nouns
(вокзал, восток, завод, запад, концерт, лекция, площадь, почта, работа, рынок, север, спектакль,
стадион, станция, улица, Урал, урок, фабрика, экзамен, юг) take на instead of в even where English
would say "in/into." These same nouns take с (+ genitive) for "from." pp.171–172.

### Prepositions за, перед, между, над, под, с, у (location prepositions)
За (behind/beyond) and под (under) both take instrumental for location, accusative for motion
into that position; перед (in front of), между (between), над (above) take only instrumental; с
(with, "accompanied by") takes instrumental — distinct from bare instrumental for "by means of";
у (by, "at someone's house") takes only genitive. pp.173–174.

### Prepositions до, из, к, мимо, от, по, через (directional/other prepositions)
До (as far as/until), из (from, out of), мимo (past), от (away from) all take genitive. К
(towards, to a person's place) takes dative. По (along/round; also used for telephone/TV/radio/
post expressions) takes dative. Через (across/through) takes accusative. pp.175–176.

### Time expressions with в/на: seconds through centuries
Second/minute/hour and singular days-of-the-week phrases take в + accusative (в понедельник);
plural/habitual days take по + dative (по понедельникам). Weeks take на + prepositional (на этой
неделе); months, years, and centuries all take в + prepositional (в июле, в этом году, в двадцатом
веке). Unlike English, Russian always requires an explicit preposition in these time phrases.
pp.177–180.

### Time prepositions: during, until, over, after, since, in, ago
Во время (+ genitive) = during (written as two words, distinct from вовремя "on time"). До (+
genitive) also covers "before/until" (in addition to its "as far as" sense). За (+ accusative) =
"in/over" a duration (Она написала книгу за 4 недели). После (+ genitive) = after. С (+ genitive)
= since/from a point in time, frequently paired with present tense to render an English perfect
("have been doing"). Через (+ accusative) = "in [an amount of time]" (elapsed-time sense, distinct
from its "across" sense). Назад ("ago") is postpositive, taking no preposition. A preposition
governing a verb (rather than a noun) requires an intervening compound-conjunction phrase (e.g. до
того, как). pp.181–182.

### Prepositions without, except, about, according to, opposite, for
Без (without) and кроме (except for/apart from) take genitive. О (about, concerning) takes
prepositional (note nouns with irregular locative-у endings still take prepositional after o,
e.g. о саде). По (according to) takes dative (по статистике, по-моему). При (multiple senses: by/
near/attached to/in the presence of/during the reign of) takes prepositional. Против/напротив
(opposite, against) takes genitive. За (+ accusative) covers "for" in the sense of on-behalf-of/
in-response-to (спасибо за подарок); для (+ genitive) covers "for" in the sense of intended-for;
на/в (+ accusative) cover "for" in the sense of a ticket/admission to an event. pp.183–184.

### Verb-governed prepositions
A closed set of common verbs takes a fixed preposition + case regardless of general prepositional
rules: играть в (+ acc., a game/sport) vs. играть на (+ prep., an instrument); смотреть в (+ acc.,
"look through/into") vs. смотреть на (+ acc., "look at"); благодарить за, наказывать за, платить
за, продавать за (all + acc.); готовиться к, относиться к (+ dat.); жаловаться на, надеяться на,
отвечать на, сердиться на (+ acc.); начать с (+ gen., "begin at"); у (+ gen.) after verbs of
taking/buying/stealing/requesting, marking the person something is taken from. pp.185–186.

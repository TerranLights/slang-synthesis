## Grammar points

### Address-replacing openers before a question (§1, pp. 4–7)

In colloquial (especially unofficial) speech, direct address (calling someone by name) is often
replaced by a fixed opener before the question itself. The book sorts these into two registers: a)
stranger-directed (Простите, Извините, Простите, пожалуйста) and b) familiar/junior-directed
(Послушай(те), Слушай(те), Скажи(те)) — with the caveat that the b) set is also used by an older
speaker addressing a younger one regardless of prior acquaintance. The book supports the distinction
with excerpts from Bulgakov's *Мастер и Маргарита*, Lermontov's *Бэла*, and Pushkin's *Евгений
Онегин*, used only as short illustrative quotes here per copyright discipline (p. 7).

### Question-with-negation as a vehicle for hope/expectation or apprehension (§2, pp. 7–10)

A general yes/no question built with a negated verb (e.g. "У вас нет сегодняшней газеты?", "Ты не
опоздаешь?") does not simply ask neutrally — it signals that the speaker already leans toward
expecting the positive state of affairs (hope: "I hope you have today's paper") or fears an
undesirable one (apprehension: "I hope you won't be late"). A genuinely neutral question uses the
non-negated form instead. The same negated-question form can also soften a request into politeness
("Вы не скажете, как пройти к метро?" ≈ "could you tell me...") — the book defers full treatment of
this to Раздел V §1, cross-referenced on p. 9.

### Indirect ("relayed") questions with ли (§3, pp. 11–14)

When the speaker wants their interlocutor to find out an answer from a third party rather than
answering directly, the resulting indirect question follows a strict word-order rule tied to the
particle ли: if the indirect question's predicate is placed first, ли **must** follow it immediately
("Спросите у Антона, не видел ли он Сашу"); if the predicate is not placed first, ли is **absent**
("Спросите у Антона, он не видел Сашу?"). The particle ли can also appear in a *direct* question when
its predicate is fronted, where it intensifies the hope/apprehension reading already noted in §2
("Не видел ли ты Сашу?"). Beyond спросить/узнать, the construction also takes выяснить, уточнить,
посмотреть, проверить, подумать, вспомнить as its introducing verb — with подумать/вспомнить used
reflexively, asking the addressee to "ask themself" the embedded question (p. 12–13).

### Affirmative vs. negative indefinite-pronoun questions about whether a fact ever occurred (§4, pp. 15–17)

To ask whether some fact has *ever* occurred at all (rather than at a specific time), Russian uses
когда-нибудь/кто-нибудь/что-нибудь-type indefinites for a question leaning toward "yes," and their
negative counterparts никогда/никто/ничего (with ни-prefixed forms generally, e.g. ни с кем, ни о чём)
for a question leaning toward "no" — the two forms are near-mirror-image paraphrases of the same
underlying question ("Вы когда-нибудь были в Китае?" ↔ "Вы никогда не были в Китае?").

### The X что construction for checking a spontaneous guess (§5, pp. 18–20)

When the speaker forms a sudden guess about a situation and wants to check it, что is inserted
directly after the noun/pronoun that names the person or thing the guess concerns — never as a
question-initial "Что...": "Он что, ещё не вернулся из поездки?" (not *"Что он ещё не вернулся..."*).
The construction is illustrated across first, second, and third person and across animate/inanimate
topics ("Ты что", "Вы что", "Тебе что", "Его что", "В ней что", "У тебя что"). A more emphatic literary
variant adds же ("Вы что же...", drawn from Bulgakov's *Мастер и Маргарита* and from the screenplay of
*Ирония судьбы*).

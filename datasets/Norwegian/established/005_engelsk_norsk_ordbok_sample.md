**Source:** Willy A. Kirkeby, *Engelsk-Norsk Ordbok / English-Norwegian Dictionary* ("Stor utgave," Large Edition), Kunnskapsforlaget (Aschehoug/Gyldendal/NKS-Forlaget), 2nd ed. 1337 PDF pages = front matter (PDF pp. 1–16, printed pp. I–XII) + dictionary body (printed pp. 3–~1310) + "Forkortelser" abbreviations appendix (printed ~1311–1326) + endpaper map. `pdftotext` reports a text layer, but it decodes to unusable character-soup garbage (confirmed on multiple spot-checks, e.g. printed p.49: "«>WI/" !: 2 "'1" """ _/"",, g ,_uIMJ ,nnp"" — not a decodable fixed-substitution cipher, just broken OCR). **Fell back to vision-reading throughout**, per the dispatch instructions. Vision quality was consistently excellent — every page tested was crisp, legible scanned print.

This is worth flagging clearly: the two dictionaries in this wave have genuinely different normative scope, not just different directions of translation.

## Grammar points

### Scope and the register-labeling philosophy (Forord / Preface, pp. III–VI)

Both the Norwegian *Forord* and parallel English *Preface* state that the dictionary covers only Bokmål, consistent with the author's other dictionaries (he cross-references his companion *Norsk-Engelsk Ordbok*, abbreviated `NEO` throughout, as a complete-edition counterpart). The Preface explicitly frames **extensive register labeling as a deliberate, named feature of the dictionary**: it states that it uses labels "even where" other dictionaries typically leave usage unmarked, specifically so that a learner translating *into* English does not accidentally produce an inappropriately formal or inappropriately slangy sentence — i.e. the labels exist primarily to protect the *encoding* (Norwegian-speaker-writing-English) direction, not just to describe English for comprehension. This is a structurally important design note: it means register tagging in this dictionary should be read as deliberately comprehensive rather than sporadic lexicographic habit.

### Symbols and Abbreviations — the full register-tagging key (printed pp. VII–VIII)

This is the flagship finding for this dispatch — the dictionary's own key to its register system, confirmed via direct inspection of the Table of Abbreviations:

- **`S` = slang** — the dictionary's primary slang tag.
- **`RS` = rimslang** ("rhyming slang," i.e. Cockney-style rhyming slang specifically, not general slang).
- **`T` = talespråk** ("colloquial speech") — a broader, less marked "spoken/informal register" tag, distinct from and more common than `S`. Several of the vocabulary-table entries above show `T` and `S` applied to two different registers of essentially the same idea (e.g. "dead from the neck up": Norwegian `T` gloss "han er helt dum" vs. Norwegian `S` gloss "han er helt teit" for the same English idiom) — confirming `T` and `S` are genuinely distinct tiers in this dictionary's own system, with `S` marking the more marked/informal of the two.
- **`UK` / `US`** mark British vs. American usage respectively; several entries combine a regional tag with a register tag (e.g. `især US T`, `US S`).
- **`glds.` = gammeldags** ("archaic/old-fashioned").
- **`litt.` = litterært** ("literary").
- **`neds.` = nedsettende** ("disparaging/derogatory").
- **`spøkef.` = spøkefull(t)** ("jocular/joking") — frequently co-occurs with `T` on the same entry (e.g. "fraud," "inundate," "institution"), suggesting jocular usage is treated as a sub-flavor of colloquial register rather than a wholly separate axis.
- **`vulg.` = vulgært** ("vulgar") — present in the key though not directly observed tagging a headword within this sample's six ranges (the closest finds were `S`-tagged sexual/bodily-function terms like "slag" and "skitters," which may sit just below the `vulg.` threshold in this dictionary's own system).
- **`dial.` = dialekt** ("dialectal") — listed in the key; not observed within this sample's ranges (a Bokmål-only dictionary has structurally less occasion to mark dialect than Haugen's comparative Bokmål/Nynorsk one).
- **`fig.` = figurlig** ("figurative") — a sense-type label rather than a register label per se, used throughout for metaphorical extensions.
- The key also defines a large battery of unrelated subject-field abbreviations (`anat.`, `bibl.`, `mil.`, `jur.`, `mar.`, `psykol.`, etc.) — not register tags, but worth noting that register tags and subject-field tags share the same bracketed-italics notational slot and can co-occur on one entry (e.g. `mil. T`).

**Structural note on double-tagging.** Several entries found during sampling carry two tags together rather than one (`spøkef. T`, `neds. S`, `især US T`, `hist. US S`), which the dictionary's own system permits freely — register, regional, and field tags compose rather than being mutually exclusive slots. The `yid` entry's `neds. S` double-tag (disparaging + slang) is a clear example of the dictionary explicitly marking a term as an ethnic slur rather than presenting it as neutral slang.

### User's Guide / Brukerveiledning — entry-structure conventions (printed pp. IX–X)

The guide documents several structural conventions relevant to parsing any entry pulled from this dictionary: a swung dash `~` stands in for the headword wherever it recurs inside a sub-entry or example sentence; a tilde-prefixed parenthetical after an English word (e.g. `(-ing)`) signals an available derived form; square brackets enclose an optional element of a phrase that can be dropped without changing meaning; a comma followed by a bracketed note before a translation (e.g. `,US:`) flags that the following Norwegian gloss specifically renders the American-English variant of the headword; a colon immediately after a closing bracket signals that the Norwegian equivalent that follows is valid **only** inside the specific set phrase just given, not as a general translation of the headword. Italicized `=` introductions inside brackets (e.g. `(=mortal sin)`) indicate the editors' own paraphrase/synonym of the headword sense being translated, inserted to disambiguate before giving the Norwegian equivalent — this convention appears on nearly every polysemous entry sampled (e.g. "deed," "force," "default," "institute").

### Pronunciation key (printed pp. XI–XII)

IPA-based phonemic transcriptions are given in square brackets immediately after each English headword (observed consistently across all six sampled ranges, e.g. `[di'dʒenərit]` for *degenerate*, `[frænk]` for *frank*). The guide documents the dictionary's stress-mark convention (a preceding raised stress mark `'` before the stressed syllable, consistent with standard IPA stress notation) and flags that British RP is the base accent represented, with American-English pronunciation variants given separately in brackets where they diverge materially (e.g. `fraudulent [frɔːdjulənt; US: 'frɔːdʒulənt]`).

### Dictionary-body entry-numbering convention (observed throughout the sample)

Polysemous headwords are split into **Roman-numeral sub-entries by part of speech** (e.g. "I. instant *subst.*" vs. "II. instant *adj.*"; "I. forward *subst.*" / "II. forward *vb.*" / "III. forward *adj.*" / "IV. forward *adv.*" — a word with four distinct parts of speech gets four full Roman-numeral sub-entries), and **within each Roman-numeral sub-entry, Arabic-numbered senses** (1., 2., 3. …) subdivide meaning, each of which can independently carry its own register tag — meaning register tagging in this dictionary is applied at the *sense* level, not the *headword* level, and a single headword can be tagged differently (or not at all) across its various senses. This was directly confirmed by "sin" (senses: 1. rel. sin — untagged; 2. fig. — untagged; 3. T idiom "as ugly as sin"), "zap" (four senses, three independently tagged `S`/`T`/`TV T`), and "zonked" (three senses, all under one `S` tag, showing a tag can also cover multiple senses at once when they share the same register).

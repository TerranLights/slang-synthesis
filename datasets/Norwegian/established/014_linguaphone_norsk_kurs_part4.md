## Non-redundant-supplement scoping: what was sampled and why

**Explanatory notes (pp.73–188, ~58 image-spreads) and Appendix (pp.189–222, ~17 image-spreads)
were sampled at roughly every 10th-15th spread across their full range** (Leksjon 1/4/8/10/12/28/29/
45 explanatory notes; Appendix noun-declension and word-order sections), rather than read
page-by-page, because every sample confirmed the same pattern: the majority of entries re-teach
systematic grammar points already fully captured in `001_norwegian_verbs_and_essentials_of_grammar.md`
and `002_practical_norwegian_grammar.md` (plural-noun classes, adjective agreement, present tense
formation, imperative, negation placement, modal-particle set *da/jo/nok/visst/vel*, separable/
inseparable compound verbs, inversion and adverb-placement word-order rules, the 3-gender noun system
including the feminine **-a** declension). Spending a full page-by-page vision-reading pass on this
~75-spread span would have produced heavy duplication with those two files for comparatively little
marginal yield — the genuinely new content (idioms, synonym distinctions, explicit register tags,
compound-word families) recurred reliably across every sample taken, meaning a sparser sample was
sufficient to harvest the course's real distinctiveness without re-deriving grammar this project
already has. What follows is every genuinely new finding surfaced by that sampling pass.

## Idiom and usage-note findings (from Explanatory Notes)

- **min kone vs. min frue/hustru** (Leksjon 1, note 1): *kone* is the plain word for "wife" (and also
  means "woman," especially an old woman); *frue* or *hustru* is more formal. A concrete formality-tier
  data point for an everyday kinship term.
- **en niese** (Leksjon 1, note 8): the source flags this word's irregular stress explicitly — it is
  pronounced as three syllables, *ni-e-se*, not two. Norwegian distinguishes nephew (*en nevø*) from
  niece by a different root entirely, unlike the parallel English pair; the course also gives a
  periphrastic system for in-law/step relations (*min brorsønn* "my nephew, my brother's son," *min
  søsterdatter* "my niece, my sister's daughter," etc.) that is more explicit about the parent's
  gender than the single English word.
- **en niese av min kone** (Leksjon 1, note 9): Norwegian genitive possessives (*en kollega av Hansen*,
  "a colleague of Hansen's") don't use the English 's-genitive idiom of "a niece of my wife's" — this
  is a real cross-linguistic possessive-construction mismatch worth flagging for conlang purposes.
- **mye vs. meget** (Leksjon 8, Explanatory note; also Leksjon 28 appendix note): *meget* is more
  formal than *mye*; the two are interchangeable as adjective/adverb modifiers in most contexts, but
  **with adjectives in the positive degree, only *meget* can be used as an adverb of degree**
  (*Han var meget syk*, "He was very ill" — *mye* would be wrong here). *Meget* also has a frozen
  idiomatic use as a school mark: *Han fikk meget i engelsk*, "He got the mark 'meget' for English"
  (short for *meget tilfredsstillende*, "very satisfactory"). This is a real formal/informal register
  pair this project's grammar references hadn't captured with this granularity.
- **fornøyelse** (Leksjon 28, note 9): the fixed phrase *med fornøyelse*, "with pleasure," plus the
  derived compound *fornøyelsespark* (c.), "fun fair."
- **å regne med** (Leksjon 4, note 6): synonymous with *å ta med, å inkludere* ("to include"); also
  means "to count on" (*Vi kan ikke regne med at han vil hjelpe oss*, "We cannot count on his helping
  us"). Distinct sense: *å regne* alone means "to calculate, to do sums," and *en regning* (c.) is
  "a bill." *Når vi ikke regner med* = "if we do not include," an idiomatic negative-conditional use.
- **sam-** compound family (Leksjon 4, note 1): the prefix *sam-* corresponds to the English prefix
  "co-" and the adverb *sammen* ("together"): *samarbeid* "co-operation," *samvirkelag* "co-operative
  store" (lit. "group working together"), *sameksistens* "coexistence," *samferdsel*
  "communication," *samklang* "harmony," *samliv* "life lived together, married life," *samnordisk*
  (as in *et samnordisk radioprogram*, "a radio programme shared by the Scandinavian countries"),
  *samtidig* "at the same time." A productive derivational family worth keeping as a unit for
  mechanics analysis rather than as scattered single entries.
- **et par** (Leksjon 4, note 8): "roughly, two or three" as a vague-quantity idiom (not just literally
  "a pair"), e.g. *et par dager*, "a day or two," vs. its literal-pair sense in *et par sokker*, "a
  pair of socks." Norwegian quantity-expression nouns take **no preposition** where English inserts
  "of": *en kopp te* ("a cup of tea"), *et glass melk* ("a glass of milk").
- **en niese, "a day or two"** cluster extended (Leksjon 11, note 12): *et par dager*, *en dag eller
  to*, *en to-tre dager*, *et par-tre dager* are all near-synonymous vague-quantity idioms for
  "a day or two"; *et par bukser* "a pair of trousers," *et par briller* "a pair of spectacles," *et
  par bukseseler* "a pair of braces" extend the literal-pair sense.
- **en niese av min kone (bo hos oss)** / **bo hos** (Leksjon 11, note 11): means "stay with, live
  with." Synonym note: *enebolig* (c.), "a detached house for one family" (as an alternative to
  *villa*); *boligmangel* (c.), "housing shortage"; *bopel* (c.), "residence."
- **de siste nyheter / siste nytt / siste skrik** (Leksjon 11, note 16): "the latest news" (plural
  *nyheter*) vs. a single news item (singular *en sensasjonell nyhet*, "a sensational piece of news")
  vs. *siste nytt*, "last-minute news" (freshly added information) vs. the idiom *siste skrik* (n.),
  "the latest fashion," *le dernier cri* — a nuanced four-way lexical field around "news/novelty" with
  no single clean English mapping.
- **fra det ene til det annet** (Leksjon 29, note 10): "from one thing to the other, by the way, to
  change the subject" — a fixed discourse-marker idiom, also phrased *fra det ene til det andre*; see
  the same phrase's recurrence in Leksjon 28's vocabulary ("by the way, changing the subject").
- **tollavgift/tollbetjent/tollfri/tollpliktig** (Leksjon 29 appendix note 13): a *toll-* ("customs")
  compound family — "customs duty," "customs officer," "duty free," "subject to customs duty,
  dutiable."
- **tett i tett / repetition-for-frequency idiom family** (Leksjon 29, note 3): Norwegian frequently
  expresses "repeatedly, in close succession" by doubling a noun across a preposition rather than
  using an adverb: *strømpene var hull i hull* ("the stockings were full of holes"), *bil etter bil
  suste forbi* ("car after car rushed by"), *gang på gang* ("over and over again, time and again"),
  *dag for dag* ("day by day"), *lag på lag* ("layer upon layer"), and the summary idiom *alt i alt*
  ("all told"). A productive syntactic-idiom pattern (N-prep-N reduplication for intensified
  repetition/frequency) worth flagging for mechanics analysis as a distinct device from ordinary
  adverbial frequency marking.
- **fotgjenger / bane word family** (Leksjon 29, notes 4–5): *fotgjenger* (c.), "pedestrian," literally
  "foot-walker." *Bane* (c.) means "road, plane, course" and recurs as the final element in several
  compounds: *jernbane* "railway," *skytebane* "rifle range," *løpebane* "career" (literally "running
  course"); the idiom *i lange baner*, "in big quantities," is explicitly flagged by the source as
  **colloquial** — the clearest explicit slang/register tag found in this course (see header
  cross-reference). There is also an unrelated homonym *bane* (c.), "violent death" (*Det ble hans
  bane*, "It was the end of him; It cost him his life") — a genuine homonym pair worth keeping
  distinct in any lexical-field mapping.
- **i Oslo / på Hamar: place-name preposition rule** (Leksjon 29, note 1): towns along the coast
  tend to take **i**, towns inland take **på** (*i Bergen, i Arendal, i Drammen* vs. *på Hamar, på
  Røros, på Kongsberg*), with some genuinely dual-use cases (*i* or *på Hønefoss/Moss*). Counties and
  boroughs usually take **i** (*i Akershus, i Ullensaker*); other region names usually take **på**
  (*på Østlandet* — Eastern/Southern Norway) **except** *i Nord-Norge*, Northern Norway, which is
  explicitly an exception. This is a real, non-fully-regular preposition-choice rule keyed to a
  coastal/inland geographic distinction — a good candidate for a world-building/conlang rule precedent
  (geography shaping grammatical choice) distinct from anything captured in 001/002/003.
- **strøk** (Leksjon 29 note 2, recurring Leksjon 45): *strøk* (n.) means "area, district" but is also
  used colloquially for "a fashionable street" — *å gå på strøket* in Oslo specifically means "to walk
  along the Karl Johans gate" (the city's main promenade street), a place-specific idiomatic narrowing
  of a general word.
- **en fremmed, en utlending, en innfødt** (Leksjon 45, note 4): "a stranger/foreigner" vs. "a native"
  contrast pair.
- **tettbygde strøk / bygd / sogn / herred** (Leksjon 45, note 5): *bygd* (c.), "rural district," is
  often best translated by the ecclesiastical term "parish" (*sogn*, n.) for practical/religious
  purposes, or by *herred* (n.) for administrative purposes (*herredsstyre*, n., "rural district
  council") — three overlapping but not-quite-synonymous terms for sub-national rural administrative/
  social units, a genuinely untranslatable-in-one-word cluster.
- **i stand / u- as opposite-forming prefix** (Leksjon 45 Appendix note 1): *i ustand*, "out of order,"
  contrasts with *i stand*, "in order." The prefix **u-** is the ordinary way of forming opposites
  (*venn* "friend" → *uvenn* "enemy"; *sannhet* "truth" → *usannhet* "falsehood"; *lykkelig* "happy" →
  *ulykkelig* "unhappy"), **but in a few cases functions as an intensifier instead** (*best* "beast" →
  *ubest* "nasty beast"; *vær* "weather" → *uvær* "bad weather, storm") — a productive prefix with two
  opposed functions depending on the base, worth keeping as a single derivational-morphology finding
  rather than scattering the individual pairs.
- **vogna / barnevogn "touch of slang"** (Leksjon 45 Appendix note 4): *vogn* (common gender, or
  feminine gender with many speakers) covers many types of unpowered cart or carriage, and "also cars,
  **although with a touch of slang**" — the second explicit slang/register tag this project found in
  this course. This is a genuine, if minor, slang sense extension (cart/carriage word → informally
  used for "car") the source itself flags.

## Idiom and usage-note findings (from Appendix: Outline of Norwegian Grammar)

- **Irregular noun plurals** (Appendix §2 g–i): a fuller, more systematic listing of vowel-changing and
  no-ending irregular plurals than previously captured in 001/002 for this specific word set: *klo/
  klør* "claw(s)," *rå/rær* "yard (ship's rigging)," *tå/tær* "toe(s)," *ku/kyr* "cow(s)" (also
  regularly inflectable as *ku, kua, kuer, kuene*), *gås/gjess* "goose/geese," *mann/menn*
  "man/men," plus the family-relation nouns *far/fedre* "father(s)," *mor/mødre* "mother(s)," *bror/
  brødre* "brother(s)," *datter/døtre* "daughter(s)." This is denser than the equivalent coverage in
  001/002 and worth keeping as a supplementary irregular-plural reference list, cross-referenced
  against the matching vocabulary-table entries in Parts 1–3 (*søsken*, *brødre*, *ku* already appear
  there).
- **Feminine-gender noun declension (riksmål-specific)** (Appendix §5): a systematic list of nouns
  that take the feminine indefinite article **ei** (instead of **en**) and the feminine definite
  ending **-a** (instead of **-en**) among most riksmål speakers: *en/ei geit* "goat," *en/ei hei*
  "moor," *en/ei ku* "cow," *en/ei vidde* "mountain plateau," *en/ei jente* "girl," *en/ei øy* "island"
  (def. *øya*), *en/ei brakke* "barrack," *en/ei bu* "store house, small cabin," *en/ei hytte* "small
  cottage, chalet," *en/ei gate* "street" (def. *gata*). A handful of nouns occur **only in the
  plural**: *foreldre* "parents," *søsken* "brothers and sisters, siblings," *klær* "clothes" — all
  three already appear as vocabulary-table entries in Parts 1–2, now cross-referenced to this
  systematic grammatical class. This feminine-declension system is already covered in outline in
  001/002, but this Appendix's specific word list (especially *gate/gata*, *øy/øya*) is denser and
  directly supports the Leksjon 29 vocabulary entries for *gate* and *øy* flagged in Part 2.
- **Common/neuter declension-class irregularities** (Appendix §4): nouns in unstressed *-el/-er* drop
  the unstressed *-e-* before the indefinite/definite plural endings with a preceding double consonant
  simplified to single (*vinter→vinteren/vintrer/vintrene*; *eksempel→eksemplet/eksempler/eksemplene*;
  *sykkel→sykkelen/sykler/syklene*); a final *-m* after a short vowel is always doubled before *-en/
  -et/-er/-ene* (*dam→dammen/dammer/dammene* "pond"; *lam→lammet/lammer/lammene* "lamb" — note this
  productively supplies the plural for the *lam* vocabulary entry in Part 3); and some nouns carry a
  **specific-meaning split between common gender and neuter** of the otherwise-same root: *en kart*
  "a map" vs. *et kart* "an unripe fruit" (cross-references the *kart*/unripe-apple sense in the
  "Forviklinger" poem, Part 3), *en ting* "a thing" vs. *et ting* "an assembly," *en følge* "a
  consequence" vs. *et følge* "a procession, a group," *en fyr* "a chap, a fellow" vs. *et fyr* "a
  lighthouse."
- **Inversion/word-order and sentence-modifier rules** (Appendix §§70–75): already fully redundant with
  001/002's word-order coverage (main-clause/subordinate-clause inversion, the position of *ikke* and
  other negative/frequency adverbs, stylistic fronting for emphasis). **Not re-tabulated.** One
  stray literary citation worth noting as a usage example rather than a grammar point: the Appendix
  cites Ibsen's *Peer Gynt* — *Aldri red jeg slik en fole*, "Never did I ride such a foal" — as an
  example of emphatic adverb-fronting placing *aldri* ("never") before the indefinite article for
  extra stress, "to be noticed... but used very sparingly."
- **Preposition idiosyncrasy warning, med toget** (Appendix, Prepositions section, p.219): the
  Appendix explicitly warns that a given English preposition is not reliably mapped onto the "same"
  Norwegian preposition in a given context — citing specifically that English "by train" maps to
  Norwegian ***med* toget**, using *med* ("with"), not a literal "by"-equivalent. A short but concrete
  illustration of preposition non-correspondence as a systemic translation trap, worth keeping as a
  cross-linguistic caution distinct from 001/002's own preposition inventories.
- **Compound verb prefixes (separable vs. inseparable)** (Appendix §§66–69): already fully redundant
  with `002_practical_norwegian_grammar.md`'s "Separable and inseparable compound verbs" section.
  **Not re-tabulated**, except to note the specific verb set this Appendix illustrates it with differs
  somewhat from 002's own examples (*å trekke* "to pull" vs. *å foretrekke* "to prefer" vs. *å
  fortrekke* "to withdraw" vs. *å trekke for* "to draw" — a four-way prefix-contrast minimal set on a
  single root not seen in 002), offered here as a supplementary illustrative example rather than a new
  grammar point.
- **Sentence-modifier particles *da, jo, nok, visst, vel*** (Appendix §70): already fully redundant
  with 001/002's modal-particle coverage. **Not re-tabulated.**

## The essay "The Norwegian Language" (Handbook pp.60–61, before the pronunciation section)

A short two-page historical essay on the Bokmål/Nynorsk/Riksmål/Landsmål split, covering territory
`001_norwegian_verbs_and_essentials_of_grammar.md` already addresses in its own comparative ch.1 table
(not re-tabulated as a grammar point), but worth noting as a concrete confirmation for the archaic
spelling finding in Part 3: the essay explicitly describes the 1938 spelling reform ("supplemented by
adjustments made in 1959") as bringing Riksmål and Landsmål/Nynorsk closer together, and states plainly
that pre-reform 19th/early-20th-century texts use a visibly different orthography than the modern
standard — exactly the phenomenon directly observed in the two archaic-spelled poems "Søfuglen" and
"Forviklinger" (Part 3).

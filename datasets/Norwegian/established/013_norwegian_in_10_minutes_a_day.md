# Norwegian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Norwegian in 10 Minutes a Day (full book)

**Source:** Kristine Kershul (adapted for Norwegian by Nancy J. Stone-Nilsen), *Norwegian in 10 Minutes a
Day*, Bilingual Books Inc., 2nd edition (1984; copyright 1982). Vision-read in full — 69 PDF page-images,
each a two-printed-page spread (confirmed: printed folios run roughly 2–113, not 1:1 with PDF page
count — a second instance of the "two-pages-per-spread" scan gotcha documented in the extraction spec).
**Pagination is not a clean arithmetic offset from PDF page number** — printed folio jumps were observed
that don't track a fixed `2×pdf_page − k` formula once specialty inserts (flashcard sheets, the pull-out
menu guide) are reached; printed page numbers as visible in each image are cited directly below rather
than computed.

**Marginalia found and excluded, per the vision-reading guard.** This is a used/scanned copy. A previous
owner filled in many of the book's blank practice lines in blue ink and pencil (e.g. "enkelt", "en linje,
en linje", "bytter", "Jeg vil stige av", "I annen etasje", answers inside the crossword-puzzle grid). None
of this handwritten content was extracted — only the book's own printed vocabulary, glossary entries, and
dialogue/example text were captured.

**Non-redundant-supplement scoping.** This project already has comprehensive Norwegian grammar coverage
from `001_norwegian_verbs_and_essentials_of_grammar.md` (full tense/aspect system, nouns, pronouns,
determiners, adjectives, adverbs, word order, prepositions, conjunctions, numbers, time expressions) and
`002_practical_norwegian_grammar.md` (irregular verb paradigms, articles, full pronoun classes, modal
auxiliaries, verb conjugation classes). This course's own grammar content is minimal and entirely
standard (Norwegian verbs don't conjugate by person/number; the en/ei/et three-gender article system;
basic word order) — **all of it is skipped as fully redundant** with 001/002, which already present it
more systematically. What's **kept** here is what those two grammar references don't have at all: (1)
this book's own **cumulative back-of-book glossary** (pp. 108–111), which is the course's real lexical
payoff — several hundred alphabetized vocabulary items built up one "free ord" (cognate/loanword) at a
time across all 22 Steps, each tagged with its grammatical gender via the Norwegian definite-suffix
convention (`-en`/`-et`/`-a`); (2) the **Drinking Guide** and **Spisekartet (menu) pull-out** at the very
back, both genuine specialty vocabulary not duplicated in the main glossary; (3) a handful of
**word-formation/compounding patterns** and **register-marked pragmatic phrases** this book's own
examples surface that aren't grammar paradigms as such. Per-Step drill vocabulary, fill-in-the-blank
exercises, and the cut-out verb/adjective flashcards at the back are **skipped** — every item on the
flashcards was independently confirmed to already be a duplicate of a main-glossary entry (cut-out study
aids drawing from vocabulary already introduced, not new content).

## Vocabulary — Master Glossary (pp. 108–111, A–Å, book's full cumulative vocabulary)

item introduced via the running glossary strips across all 22 Steps, collected and alphabetized at the
back. The book's own convention marks each noun's indefinite-article gender by which definite suffix it
takes — `-en` (common/masculine gender, takes `en`), `-et` (neuter, takes `et`), `-a` (feminine, takes
`ei`) — preserved below in the Notes column wherever the glossary itself marked it. Verbs are given in
the glossary as present-tense forms (`finner` "finds", `kjører` "drives"); these are listed as `verb`
with the bare present-tense gloss.

## Grammar points

*(All standard paradigm-level grammar — verb non-conjugation by person, the en/ei/et article system,
basic word order — is fully redundant with `001_norwegian_verbs_and_essentials_of_grammar.md` and
`002_practical_norwegian_grammar.md` and is skipped per the non-redundant-supplement scope stated above.
The points below are things this course's own material surfaces that those two references don't present
in this form.)*

### Clock system: counting toward the next hour

The book's "halv fem" = "half past four" pattern (p. 42–43 clock-drill pages) follows the same
next-hour-anticipating logic found in German and other Germanic languages: "halv fem" literally reads as
"half [toward] five," not "half past four" in the English sense of counting backward from the stated
hour. The book also gives `kvarter over` (quarter after) and `kvarter på` (quarter to) as the other two
anchor points. This is a genuine minute-level grammar point; it is plausible 001/002 touch on it in their
own time-expression chapters, but this source's own illustrative drill ("Når går toget til Skien?" timed
to "klokka er...") is an independent worked example, kept per the spec's "a same-topic chapter from a
third source is not redundant by default" guidance.

### The `gang` compound family

`gang -en` ("hall," base meaning "a going/passage") recurs as the productive base of a small compound
family the book surfaces piecemeal across several Steps rather than as a single lesson:
`inngang`/`hovedinngang` ("entrance"/"main entrance"), `utgang` ("exit"), `nødutgang` ("emergency exit").
All four share the transparent "gang" (passage) + directional/modifying prefix structure — a clean,
compact example of Norwegian's head-final noun-compounding productivity, worth noting for the
mechanics-analysis phase even though it isn't the book's own stated grammar point.

### The `reise` word family

Similarly, `reise` ("trip," also verbal "to travel") recurs as the root of `reisende` ("traveler"),
`reisebyrå` ("travel agency"), `reiser` (verb, "travels"), `reisegods` ("baggage check"), `reiseord`
("travel words" — the book's own label for its vocabulary), and `reisesjekker` ("traveler's checks") —
another productive compounding/derivation family visible only by assembling scattered glossary entries.

### Dining-establishment register: a seven-term taxonomy

Step 18 (p. 78) distinguishes seven named categories of Norwegian eating establishment by function and
price tier, each with its own connotation: `kiosk` (newsstand selling hot dogs/weiners), `kafeteria`
(self-service), `restaurant` (full-service, serves alcohol), `grill` (grilled-food specialist), `konditori`
(pastry shop/tearoom), `kaffistove` (reasonably-priced rural-style fare), `kro` (reasonably-priced, cozy
tavern-style). This is a genuine register/connotation distinction the source draws explicitly — not
present as a set in 001/002 — and is flagged in the vocabulary table above per-entry.

### Mealtime and telephone pragmatic formulas (register-marked)

The book gives two fixed conversational-formula pairs as culturally load-bearing, not just vocabulary:
(1) **mealtime**: a host or server is thanked with `"Takk for maten"` (lit. "thanks for the food"), to
which the fixed response is `"Vel bekomme"` ("you're welcome" — specifically limited to this context,
distinct from the general-purpose `"vær så god"`). (2) **telephone**: answering with `"Hallo, dette er
[name]"`, closing with `"Adjø"` (not the everyday farewell), and the hold-formula `"Et øyeblikk, takk.
Dessverre er det opptatt"` ("one moment... unfortunately the line is busy"). Both are flagged `core`
register rather than `formal`/`colloquial` since the book presents them as the single default register
with no variant given, but they are pragmatically fixed collocations, not freely composed — worth the
register flag per the spec's explicit-annotation rule.

### `W.C.` and the book's own note on foreign "w" words

The book's own glossary carries an explicit annotation (p. 80): "Most 'w' ord are foreign additions to
norsk," immediately before listing `W.C.` (toilet), `weekend`, and `whisky` — all loanwords, since `w` is
not a native letter in the Norwegian alphabet. This is the source's own explicit register/etymology
annotation (akin to the project's dialectal-tag priority) and is worth preserving verbatim as a kept
finding, distinct from (and narrower than) 001's general alphabet chapter.

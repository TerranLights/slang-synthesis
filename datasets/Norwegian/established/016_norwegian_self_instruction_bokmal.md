## Grammar points

### Introduction — register layering and the Bokmål/Nynorsk/dialect history (pp. ix–xiv)

The book's Introduction (written by Sommerfelt) gives a historical-sociolinguistic account distinct
from 001's synchronic Bokmål-vs-Nynorsk comparison table:

- Pre-reform spoken Norwegian of the south-eastern towns was described as stratified into three
  layers: *høitidssproget* ("the solemn language", near-pure Danish pronounced with some Norwegian
  phonetics, used on solemn occasions such as the pulpit), an intermediate *bourgeoisie*/middle-class
  speech with a mixed grammar and more Norwegian vocabulary, and the vernacular spoken by "all
  classes of society" to varying degrees. This three-layer model is itself the useful content — a
  register ladder for an invented-language sociolinguistics, not just a grammar fact.
- Historical sound-spelling mismatch: Danish orthography retained intervocalic *b, d, g* where
  Norwegian pronunciation had shifted these to *p, t, k* — so *flyde* ('to run, float') was written
  with *d* but pronounced *flyte*; *kage* ('cake') pronounced *kake*; *gabe* ('to yawn') pronounced
  *gape*. The 1907 reform brought spelling into line with the spoken *p/t/k* forms.
  The book is careful to flag *Riksmål*/*Bokmål* (east-derived, "book language") vs. *Landsmål*/
  *Nynorsk* (Ivar Aasen's construction from conservative western dialects, officially recognized in
  the 1880s) as the two co-equal written standards, both compulsory to learn in Norwegian schools,
  with local school boards choosing which is the area's chief instructional language.
- Explicit dialectal/prestige note: south-eastern (Oslo-area) spoken Norwegian "exercises a powerful
  influence all over the country" through its status as capital/most populous region and its radio
  broadcast reach, though the source is careful to say it lacks the outright social *prestige* of
  Southern English or Parisian French. Bergen educated speech is separately flagged as differing from
  Oslo's not only in pronunciation but in grammar too, retaining forms "obsolete in the south-east."
- The 1938 reform's deliberate goal was to *narrow* Bokmål and Nynorsk towards eventual amalgamation
  (a planned-language-convergence policy, not organic drift) — a 1951 government linguistic
  commission (*Norsk språknemnd*) was still actively working toward this as of the book's 1967
  revision.

### Chapter XXIII — Word Formation / derivational suffixes (pp. 178–180, condensed, non-overlapping subset)

Noun-forming suffixes not previously tabulated from 001's prefix/suffix appendix: **-inne, -ske**
(feminine agent: *venn* 'friend' → *venninne* 'female friend'); **-dom** (abstraction: *ung* 'young'
→ *ungdom* 'youth'); **-het** (abstraction: *falsk* 'false' → *falskhet* 'falsehood'); **-skap**
(concrete 'ship'/relationship or abstract: *ekteskap* 'marriage', *vennskap* 'friendship'); **-else**
(abstraction from verbs: *friste* 'tempt' → *fristelse* 'temptation'); **-sel** (similar, sometimes
concrete: *fange* 'capture' → *fengsel* 'prison'); **-eri** (place where an activity happens:
*vaske* 'wash' → *vaskeri* 'laundry'); **-tøy** (collective tool/gear/commodity sense: *verktøy*
'tool(s)', *leketøy* 'toys', *syltetøy* 'jam').

Adjective-forming suffixes: **-aktig** ('-ish': *narr* 'fool' → *narraktig* 'foolish'); **-full**
(*tankefull* 'thoughtful'); **-messig** ('-like': *bymessig* 'urban'); **-(l)ig** ('-ly': *farlig*
'dangerous'); **-et(e)** ('full of'); **-løs** ('-less': *arbeidsløs* 'unemployed').

Verb-forming: many verbs are derived from nouns/adjectives by **umlaut/vowel mutation alone**
(*sorg* 'sorrow' → *sørge* 'to mourn'; *kam* 'comb' → *kjemme* 'to comb'; *kamp* 'battle' →
*kjempe* 'to battle'), and **-ne** productively derives inchoative "become X" verbs from adjectives
(*sort* 'black' → *sortne* 'to darken'; *blek* 'pale' → *blekne* 'to become pale'). This
umlaut-only derivation pattern (no overt suffix, just root-vowel change) is a genuinely distinct
morphological mechanism worth flagging for the mechanics-analysis phase — it's a different
derivational strategy from ordinary affixation.

Prefixes: negative **u-** is stressed (*ʹuvenn* 'enemy', *ʹumulig* 'impossible'); **mis-** is
also stressed and negative (*mis'unne* 'envy'); **be-, er-, for-** are unstressed; **an-** is
stressed (*ʹankomme* 'arrive'). Compounding commonly uses a bare stem-juxtaposition, a genitive-​*s*
link (*landsmann* 'fellow-countryman'), or an *-e-* connecting vowel (*barnesko* 'children's shoes').

### Chapter XXIV — Punctuation (pp. 180–182, brief)

Norwegian punctuation is described as broadly similar to English, with a few explicit divergences:
full stops are dropped after certain abbreviations and all metric-unit abbreviations (*kl., m.m.,
mm, cm, dm, m, km, l, hl, kg*); **semicolons are rare**, more often replaced by a plain full stop,
but conventionally appear before a following *men* ('but') or *for* ('for/because') when either
introduces a full independent clause — the source states this as a hard rule: "Always a stop mark in
front of **men**." The **apostrophe is little used** — essentially only before a genitive *-s* when
the noun itself already ends in *-s* (*Hans's kone*, 'Hans's wife'). **Accent marks appear almost
only in French loanwords** (*kafé, idé, renommé*). Comma usage differs from English chiefly in that
**no comma precedes *og* ('and') in a simple enumeration** (*sukker, salt og pepper*), unlike the
Oxford-comma convention some English style guides use.

### §50 — "The Three Scandinavian Languages": a cross-Scandinavian false-friend pair (pp. 226–227)

Found inside the Key to Exercises reading passage, not a numbered grammar chapter — but genuinely
new comparative-Scandinavian data not seen in any prior Norwegian source in this project. The source
gives two words that are false friends *between* Norwegian/Danish and Swedish specifically (not
Norwegian-internal dialect variation):

- ***rar*** — in Norwegian and Danish: "strange, peculiar" (*en rar mann* = "a strange/peculiar
  man," not a compliment); in **Swedish**, the same word means "pleasant, nice."
- ***rolig*** — in Danish and Norwegian: "peaceful, quiet"; in **Swedish**: "amusing, enjoyable."
  The source tells a cautionary anecdote to illustrate the trap: a Swede visiting a Norwegian town
  asked a taxi driver to take him to "en rolig plass" meaning (in his own Swedish sense) a lively,
  fun spot — the Norwegian driver, applying the Norwegian/Danish sense, drove him to the
  churchyard, the quietest place he could think of.

This is a directly reusable false-friend/semantic-divergence mechanism for conlang design: the same
surface form drifting to near-opposite registers/connotations across closely related sister
languages.

# Norwegian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Så Snakker Vi Norsk — Norwegian Level One

**Source:** Ragnhild Maaso and Arne J. Maaso, *Så Snakker Vi Norsk — Norwegian Level One*, J.W.
Cappelens Forlag, Oslo. © 1970, reprinted 1975. ISBN 82-02-00849-2. Illustrations by Turid Hirsch.
Vision-read from the full 97-PDF-page scan (`14 Så snakker vi Norsk Norwegian Level One.pdf`),
covering printed pages 1–175 of the book (cover through the Norwegian-songs appendix; the trailing
grammatical index, topic index, and Norwegian–English word list, pp. 177–188ish, were not
individually re-extracted — see coverage note below).

**Source-status correction.** The Wave 3B dispatch note for this file claimed a real text layer
("confirmed TEXT layer — use pdftotext -layout") and described it as the companion to a pair of
Los-Angeles-authored titles. Independent verification before extraction found this to be **wrong**:
`pdffonts` returns zero embedded fonts, and `pdftotext`/`pdftotext -layout` both return exactly one
blank character per page (no real text layer at all — `pdfimages` confirms every page is a single
raster JPEG). This source is **100% vision-only**; the whole file was read as scanned images.
It otherwise does match the dispatch's authorship description: the preface is signed "North
Hollywood/Nøtterøy, Ragnhild Maaso, Arne J. Maaso" and states the authors taught Norwegian in Los
Angeles for six years (Van Nuys Adult School); designed for grades 5–7 but usable by adult classes.

**PDF layout note.** This scan is two printed pages per PDF-page image (a left/right spread), not
one page per image — confirmed and accounted for throughout (same gotcha previously logged for
*Colloquial Serbian* in the spec). No handwritten marginalia was found anywhere sampled; this copy's
scans are clean.

## Coverage and non-redundant-supplement scoping

This is a strict audio-lingual beginner course (32 lessons across 6 units, pp. 13–156) built almost
entirely around a ~150-word concrete-noun core recycled through drill after drill (indefinite/
definite article, singular/plural, present tense, negation, interrogatives, possessives,
demonstratives). All of that core grammar is already fully covered, more systematically, in
`001_norwegian_verbs_and_essentials_of_grammar.md` and `002_practical_norwegian_grammar.md` — this
file does **not** re-tabulate en/et-gender + definite-suffix formation, indefinite/definite plural
endings, present-tense -er/-r conjugation, `ikke`-position negation, demonstrative pronouns, or
possessive adjective/pronoun paradigms; those grammar points are only referenced below, not restated.

What this book supplies that the two grammar references do not, and what is kept below:
- Its own ~250-item beginner vocabulary set (the specific words a children's/beginner audio-lingual
  course chooses), organized by semantic field.
- A full, explicit noun-gender-by-suffix classification system (which derivational endings predict
  `en`- vs. `et`-gender) — not present as a systematic rule list in 001/002.
- The Norwegian half-hour-counts-toward-the-next-hour clock-telling convention, taught by explicit
  contrast with English.
- A dedicated discussion of the many unrelated meanings of the word **så** (verb "to sow"/"saw",
  conjunction, adverb-of-degree, ironic interjection) — not captured elsewhere in this project.
- A large set of spoken politeness/conversational formulas (greetings, table grace, thanks,
  leave-taking) explicitly flagged `colloquial`/pragmatic register below — genuinely new register
  content, since 001/002 are written-grammar references and don't carry spoken-formula inventories.
- Practical/cultural reference vocabulary: Norwegian currency, the metric system vs. U.S. units,
  postage rates, and European clothing-size charts, all explicitly pitched at American-English-
  speaking learners.
- A short appendix of Norwegian children's songs/hymns and two short pieces of translated children's
  narrative verse — cultural/literary register not found in any prior Norwegian source in this
  project.

Reading pass sampled Lessons 1–24 and 26–32 (the "basic sentences" page of each lesson, where new
vocabulary is glossed), the Appendix's phonetics-approach section (skimmed, not re-tabulated — it
substantially overlaps `003_phonology_of_norwegian.md`'s content, just framed via English/American
comparison for learners), the noun-gender-by-suffix pages, the Miscellaneous practical-vocabulary
appendix, and the Norwegian-songs appendix. Lesson 25 and the book's own back-matter indices (pp.
178–188ish: grammatical index, topic index, Norwegian–English word list) were not individually
re-extracted — they are alphabetized restatements of material already captured in the lesson
vocabulary below, and bulk-copying a glossary index would also run against this project's copyright
discipline.

### Noun gender predicted by derivational suffix (Appendix, pp. 168–169)

Unlike the two prior grammar references, this book gives an explicit, if partial, rule set for
*predicting* a noun's gender from its ending, rather than only stating that Norwegian has two
genders. Selected illustrative suffix classes (the book itself calls its own list non-exhaustive,
"just a few of the more common or useful ones" — so this extraction follows suit and samples rather
than reproduces the full two-page list):

- **Common gender (en-words)** tends to include nouns ending in `-dom` (ungdom "youth"), `-else`
  (begynnelse "beginning"), `-het` (frihet "freedom"), `-(n)ing` (åpning "opening"), `-er` denoting
  an agent/doer (lærer "teacher", baker "baker"), `-ikk` (musikk "music"), `-isme`/`-ist` (ateisme/
  ateist), `-logi`/`-log` (geologi/geolog), `-sjon` (stasjon "station"), `-us` (sirkus "circus"),
  `-ær` (sekretær "secretary"), `-ør` (ingeniør "engineer"), `-inne` marking a female agent noun
  (lærerinne "female teacher"), and `-fon` (telefon).
- **Neuter gender (et-words)** tends to include nouns ending in `-dømme` (kongedømme "kingdom"),
  `-eng` (basseng "pool"), `-eri` (bakeri "bakery"), `-ma` (klima "climate"), `-ment` (instrument),
  `-skap` (selskap "party", kjennskap "knowledge"), `-tøy` (undertøy "underwear"), `-um` (museum,
  sentrum), `-verk` (verk "works/mill"), and `-vesen` (brannvesen "fire department").
- The book pairs this with a semantic classification (foods/drinks and most clothing are en-words
  with listed food-item exceptions that are et-words — brød, smør, egg, salt, sukker, eple, bær,
  syltetøy; most family-relationship words are en-words with exceptions barn "child" and ektepar
  "married couple", both et-words; most musical instruments are et-words with the exception piano).

### The half-hour clock convention (Lesson 29, pp. 129–130)

Taught by explicit contrast with English rather than as a bare paradigm: English does not start
naming the *approaching* hour until 15 minutes before it ("a quarter to one"), but Norwegian starts
naming the approaching hour a full 20 minutes after the *previous* hour. So 12:25 is not "twenty-five
past twelve" but "fem på halv ett" (five to the-half-toward-one), and 12:30 itself is "halv ett"
(half toward one), not any expression built on "twelve." The book frames this explicitly as the
single hardest reframe for an English-speaking learner of Norwegian time-telling.

### The many meanings of "så" (Appendix, p. 151)

The book gives "så" its own dedicated discussion because of how semantically overloaded the form is:
- As the **infinitive of the verb "to sow"** ("å så" — "Hvem vil så det kornet?").
- As the **past tense of "å se" (to see)**, "saw" — a true cross-linguistic homophone with English
  "saw" that the book calls out explicitly ("Sølv og gull så jeg ikke" — "I saw no silver or gold").
- As a **conjunction** meaning "and then" ("Og så sådde hun kornet" — "And then she sowed...").
- As part of the **comparative construction "så ... som"** ("as ... as"), cited from a nursery rhyme
  ("en liten nese så stor som ...").
- As an **adverb of degree** ("Så stor du er blitt!" — "How big you've gotten!").
- As an **ironic interjection**, glossed "Så = jasål, often used ironically" ("Så, du har vært i
  kongsgården, du." — roughly "Oh, so you've been to the king's court, have you.").

### Compound-noun gender rule (Appendix, p. 151)

When two nouns of different genders combine into a compound, the compound takes the gender of its
*last* element, not its first — e.g. *en* skinnlue (et skinn "a hide" + *en* lue "a cap" → common
gender, following "lue"), *et* silkestoff (*en* silke "silk" + *et* stoff "fabric" → neuter,
following "stoff").

### "om" vs. "i" in expressions of time (p. 142)

Norwegian "om" corresponds to English "in" when describing something that is *going* to happen
("Flyet kommer om et kvarter" — the plane arrives in a quarter hour), while Norwegian "i" corresponds
to English "for" when describing a duration that *has* happened and is ongoing/completed ("Flyet har
vært her i et kvarter" — the plane has been here for a quarter hour). English "ago" is rendered with
"for ... siden" ("Kari var i Oslo for to dager siden" — Kari was in Oslo two days ago).

### Omission of the indefinite article after an adjective-modified noun, second mention (p. 142)

Where English replaces a repeated noun with "one" ("I had to get a new coat. The old one was too
small."), Norwegian simply drops the indefinite article on second mention rather than substituting a
pronoun: "Jeg måtte få ny kåpe. Den gamle var for liten." (lit. "I had to get new coat. The old was
too small.") — confirmed directly in the Lesson 30 dialogue ("jeg måtte få ny kåpe, for den gamle var
for liten").

  hostess says "Vær så god og forsyn dere" (lit. "please and help yourselves"); Americans ask to be
  excused from the table, while Norwegians thank the hostess for the meal with "Takk for maten!",

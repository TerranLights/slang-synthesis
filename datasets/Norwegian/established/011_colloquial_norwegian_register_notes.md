# Norwegian — Established Register Notes: Colloquial Norwegian (colloquial/formal/dialectal/taboo findings)

**Source:** Kari Bråtveit, W. Glyn Jones and Kirsten Gade, *Colloquial Norwegian: A Complete
Language Course* (Routledge, 1995/2005 e-Library edition; see `011_colloquial_norwegian.md` for full
citation). **This is the dedicated register file for this book**, part of a seven-file set (see that
file's "multi-file set" note). Per the dispatch instructions for this wave, this book — being the
"Colloquial" series title specifically — was flagged as the single most likely candidate among this
wave's sources to contain genuinely new slang/colloquial/register-marked content, and that prediction
held: the book is built around a named pedagogical category, **"Language in use"** (its own label,
used 43 times across 20 lessons, explicitly described in its own front matter as covering "common
phrases that you ought to know, but which you cannot be expected to construct for yourself"), plus an
entire dedicated **"Disagreeing and strong language"** section (Lesson 18) that explicitly marks
vulgar/taboo items with an asterisk. Every finding below is drawn from the book's own explicit
labels, register framing, or named sections — none is inferred from word choice alone, except where
flagged as a hedge.

---

## Summary of flagship findings (book's own labels quoted)

1. **Explicit vulgarity marking (asterisk convention).** Lesson 18's "Disagreeing and strong
   language" section is introduced with the book's own words: *"expressions marked \* are considered
   vulgar or rather strong, while the rest are neutral or fairly harmless."* This is the single
   richest taboo-register passage found in this book — see the dedicated subsection below.
2. **Explicit formal-vs-informal greeting/farewell tables**, laid out by the book itself in two
   labeled columns headed literally **"Formal/neutral"** and **"Informal"** (Lesson 1), and again
   with explicit **formal / formal-neutral / informal / neutral** row labels for closing a
   conversation (Lesson 5).
3. **Explicit colloquial-speech framing for grammatical alternatives**, not just vocabulary: the
   book repeatedly states that one grammatical construction is "more informal... used mostly in
   spoken Norwegian" against another that is "more formal... used in writing" — for feminine noun
   forms, neuter plural forms, the genitive vs. a prepositional phrase, and possessive-adjective
   word order. See "Grammatical formal/colloquial pairs" below.
4. **Explicit note on loanword taboo-register ignorance**: *"over the last few years some four-letter
   English expletives have crept into the Norwegian language... The users are often simply unaware of
   the offensive value of their loan words!"* — a documented case of taboo vocabulary importing
   across languages with its register value not fully transferring.
5. **gidde**, flagged explicitly (Lesson 7) as *"a form mainly heard in casual talk among people who
   know each other"* — this book's single most-reused colloquial lexical item (recurs in Lessons 7
   and 15 as the default way of expressing "can't be bothered").
6. **hva** for "sorry?/what?", flagged explicitly as *"not considered particularly polite, but...
   widely used among people who know each other"* (Lesson 4) — a direct statement that a form can be
   simultaneously "impolite" by strict standard and yet the actual default among intimates.

## Formal/informal pronoun system: De/Dem/Deres vs. du/dere

The book's own framing (Lesson 1, Language points): **De** (capitalized, both singular and plural)
*"is more formal, and is mainly used by the older generation and by younger people addressing older
strangers. De is also used in business correspondence."* **Du** (singular) and **dere** (plural) are
the ordinary, default forms. The parallel object form **Dem**, reflexive **Dem**, and possessive
**Deres** all track the same formal/informal split one-for-one against **deg/dere** and
**din/di/ditt/dine** (Lesson 5) — confirmed as the same system `001_norwegian_verbs_and_essentials_of_grammar.md`
separately describes (there, with the added framing that **De** is "currently dying out," now mainly
encountered in very formal settings or historical documents). This book's framing is the more
socially concrete of the two (who uses it, and when), rather than a prediction about its decline —
worth keeping both framings side by side as two independent, non-contradictory data points on the
same ongoing register/sociolinguistic shift.

## Grammatical formal/colloquial pairs (the book's own explicit framing)

- **Lesson 2** — common-gender noun forms (*en elv, elven*, formal/written) vs. specific feminine
  forms (*ei elv, elva*, informal/spoken). Book's own words: "the feminine form is more informal,
  while the common gender is used more in written texts."
- **Lesson 3** — neuter definite plural **-ene** (*landene*, formal/written) vs. **-a** (*landa*,
  informal/spoken). Book's own words: "The -a form is very informal while the -ene form is usually
  found in writing."
- **Lesson 5** — **-s** genitive (formal/written) vs. a prepositional phrase (informal/spoken), e.g.
  *sjekkheftet til Håkon* preferred over the s-genitive. Book's own words: "in colloquial Norwegian
  speech, a phrase with a preposition is often preferred to the s-genitive."
- **Lesson 5** — **-s** genitive where English also has one (*husets eier*, formal) vs. prepositional
  phrase (*eieren av huset*, colloquial). Book's own words: "the genitive form is usually more formal
  than the prepositional phrase, which is preferred in colloquial speech."
- **Lesson 7** — possessive precedes noun with the noun in its indefinite form (*hans nye
  puslespill*, formal) vs. possessive follows noun with the noun in its definite form (*det nye
  puslespillet hans*, spoken). Book's own words: "mostly used in more formal style, and rarely in
  spoken Norwegian."
- **Lesson 7** — the same split again for noun+adjective+genitive (*Petters engelske svigerinne*,
  formal) vs. the prepositional-phrase equivalent (*den engelske svigerinnen til Petter*, spoken).
  Book's own words: "will normally be used instead of the genitive in spoken Norwegian."

Note the internal consistency across all six rows: in every single case, the *more analytic/
periphrastic* construction (prepositional phrase, possessive-after-noun) is the colloquial/spoken
one, and the *more synthetic/inflectional* construction (genitive suffix, possessive-before-noun with
indefinite-form noun) is the formal/written one — a clean, repeated pattern worth flagging for any
conlang-synthesis work modeling how formality correlates with morphological density within a single
language's own register spectrum.

**Thanking (Lesson 1).** Takk-family phrases: **Takk** / **Mange takk** / **Tusen takk** (lit. "a
thousand thanks") / **Takk skal du/De ha** (lit. "thanks shall you have") — the book notes **takk**
itself also functions as a "please" marker when attached to **ja**/**nei** (**Ja takk** "yes please,"
**Nei takk** "no thank you," with **Ellers takk** "thank you all the same" as a common follow-up to a
declined offer). Reply: **Selv takk** ("don't mention it").

**Apologizing (Lesson 4).** **Unnskyld** ("sorry"), **Det må du/De/dere unnskylde** (lit. "you must
forgive me"), **Beklager**, **Jeg er (veldig) lei (meg) for det** ("I am [terribly] sorry about
that"). Neutral/polite acceptance: **Det gjør ikke noe** / **Det gjør ingen ting** ("that doesn't
matter"), **Ikke bry deg om det!** ("don't worry about it"). Then the book adds explicitly: *"you
will sometimes hear family and friends saying"* **Blås i det!** ("never mind," lit. "blow on it") —
a direct register note that this specific variant is intimate/family-register, not general-purpose.

**Not having heard/understood (Lesson 4).** **Hva sier du/De?** / **Hva?** ("Sorry?/What?"), flagged
explicitly: *"Hva is not considered particularly polite, but is widely used among people who know
each other."* Also **Jeg forstår ikke hva du sier**, **Det forstår jeg ikke**, **Si det en gang til!**
("Say it again, please!").

**Telephone conversations (Lesson 4).** **Hallo!**; giving the office/shop name instead of a personal
greeting; **Kan jeg få snakke med x?**; **Det er meg** ("Speaking"); **Hvem snakker jeg med?** ("Who's
calling?"); **Du/De har fått feil nummer!** ("You've got the wrong number!"). A separate dialogue note
(Lesson 10) observes **ja** is often used as a telephone-opening filler word, "rather like the English
'Oh'."

**Requesting politely, the full ladder (Lesson 7).** Bare imperative (can sound rude) → **Vil
du/De** + bare infinitive → **Vil du/De være så snill å** + infinitive (maximal politeness) → and,
sitting apart from this formality ladder rather than above it, the lower-register **Gidder du...?**
form (see the gidde entry below). Full ladder and worked examples are in `011_colloquial_norwegian.md`
(Lesson 7 grammar points) — not repeated here in full, only flagged as register content.

**Making/accepting/declining offers (Lesson 13).** **Vil du ha...?**, **Kan/Kunne du tenke deg...?**
("Do you fancy...?"), **Jeg spanderer!** ("This is on me!"). Accepting: **Ja takk**, optionally +
**gjerne**. Politely overwhelmed: **Det er altfor mye!**, **Det kan jeg virkelig ikke ta imot!**
("I really can't accept that"). Declining: **Nei takk** (+ **Ellers takk**); suggesting an
alternative: **Nei takk, jeg vil heller ha...**

**Asking/expressing opinions and preference (Lesson 13).** **Hva synes du?**, **Jeg synes (at)...**,
**Jeg har ikke noe imot...** ("I have nothing against..."); preference ladder **Jeg vil gjerne
ha...** < **Jeg vil heller ha...**/**Jeg foretrekker...** < **Jeg vil (aller)helst ha...** ("I'd far
prefer...").

**Table manners and dining ritual (Lesson 8) — the richest single cultural-register passage outside
Lesson 18.** **Vil du sende meg...**, **Kan jeg få...**; politeness formulas **Dette var
nydelig/deilig** ("This is lovely" — note the *past* tense is used even while still eating). The word
**skål** is flagged explicitly: *"Although it can be used in much the same informal way as the
English 'cheers,' the Norwegian word skål also has its more ritual uses"* — never drink wine before
the host raises a glass and says it first. End-of-meal ritual: **Takk for maten** (lit. "thank you for
food") answered by the host's **Vel bekomme** (lit. "may it do you good") — flagged as having "become
a sort of ritual," used even within the family, with every guest but the host saying the first line.
Two explicit false-friend/register traps: (1) **Jeg har fått nok** ("I've had enough," literal
translation of the English idiom) "can easily be perceived as meaning you certainly don't want any
more of that stuff" — i.e. it reads as a *complaint*, not satisfaction; (2) **Jeg er full** does
**not** mean "I'm full" (satiated) — it means **"I'm drunk."** The correct word for satiated is
**mett**.

**Informal letters (Lesson 7).** Informal opening: **Hei** has replaced **Kjære** + name + "!" among
"many young people" — an explicit, dated (1995) generational register shift the book itself notes in
passing. Informal-leaning sign-offs: **Hjertelig hilsen (fra)** ("Love from"), **Klem fra** ("A hug
from"), **Mange hilsener fra** ("Best wishes/many greetings from"). More neutral/formal sign-offs:
**Vennlig hilsen**, **Med hilsen** (both "Yours sincerely"). Envelope convention: no titles, just the
full name; house number *after* the street name, area code *before* the town.

**Paying compliments (Lesson 16).** Two parallel exclamatory templates, interchangeable: **For en
fin bluse du har!** / **Så fin bluse du har!** ("What a lovely blouse you have!").

**Expressing sympathy (Lesson 15).** **Gjør det (veldig) vondt?** ("Does it hurt [much]?"), **Det er
synd at...** ("It's a pity that..."), **Jeg er lei for at...**, **Det var da godt!** ("That's good!"),
**God bedring!** ("Get well soon!").

## "Everyday speech" substitution patterns (explicit source framing)

- **hva for en/et/noen** substituting for **hvilken/hvilket/hvilke** ("which") — the book states this
  is used "in everyday speech... instead of" the standard interrogative (Lesson 17).
- **gidde** (gidder, gadd, giddet), "be bothered" — see the Summary section above; recurs as the
  default casual-register way to express reluctance in Lessons 7 and 15 (*Jeg gidder ikke reise på
  hytta*, "I can't be bothered going to the chalet").
- **kommer til å** + bare infinitive as a future-tense construction — independently flagged as "a
  less formal alternative to vil... very commonly used in spoken Norwegian" in
  `002_practical_norwegian_grammar.md` (p. 24); this book (Lesson 15) presents it as simply the
  third of three interchangeable future strategies without repeating that register flag, so the
  colloquial framing for this specific construction is sourced from 002, cross-referenced here for
  completeness since it recurs as ordinary vocabulary throughout this book.
- **da** as an informal politeness/impatience particle — in **Vent på meg da** / **Skynd deg da!**
  the book glosses **da** as adding "please" or impatience-softening shading to an imperative; part
  of the broader vel/jo/da/nok/nå sentence-adverb set documented in `011b_colloquial_norwegian.md`
  (Lesson 16), which the book itself calls "extremely frequent in everyday spoken Norwegian."
- **kjefte** (-et, -et), "to scold/nag" — used in a Lesson 5 reading-text aside about a mother's
  everyday-register complaint ("hun blir sur når jeg kjefter," "she sulks when I nag/scold her") —
  ordinary-register but conversational/informal rather than written-formal vocabulary.

conlang-synthesis modeling of how slang/taboo vocabulary migrates between languages in contact.

bilen er!"* — "For shame, how dirty the car is!"), not given its own Language-in-use box or asterisk,
flagged here only as a plausible mild-colloquial interjection worth a native-speaker check, not
asserted as settled fact from this source alone (per the same conservative-flagging practice used in
`001_norwegian_verbs_and_essentials_of_grammar.md` for **ete**).

## A hedged note: colloquial money nicknames

Not explicitly labeled "colloquial" by the source, but functionally parallel to English slang
currency nicknames ("a tenner," "a fiver") and flagged here as worth a native-speaker check rather
than asserted as the book's own explicit register tag: the Lesson 2 money vocabulary gives alternate
everyday names for coins and notes alongside their plain numeral-plus-krone descriptions — **en
tiøring** (a 10-øre coin), **en femtiøring** (50 øre), **en tier/tikrone/tikroning** (a 10-krone
coin), **en femtilapp/femtikroneseddel** (a 50-krone note, "lapp" literally "a scrap/note"), **en
hundrelapp/hundrekroneseddel** (100 kr), **en tusenlapp/tusenkroneseddel** (1000 kr). The **-lapp**
forms in particular read as the more casual/spoken alternative to the compositional **-seddel**
forms, structurally parallel to the formal/colloquial doublets catalogued earlier in this file, but
this specific pair is not given an explicit register label by the source itself.

## Dialectal/sociolinguistic framing recap (cross-reference only, not re-extracted)

This book's own dialectal content is comparatively thin next to its register content: it states
plainly that **bokmål** is the language of the book, used in cities, "neither particularly
conservative nor particularly informal," against rural-associated **nynorsk** (used by ~20% of
Norwegian pupils, per this source). It does not engage with regional dialect variation beyond this
single bokmål/nynorsk framing anywhere in its 20 lessons. The much richer dialectal and tonal-accent
geography (East vs. West Norwegian, the retroflex flap's sociolinguistic marking, the
high-tone/low-tone accent-1 split) already extracted from `003_phonology_of_norwegian.md` is not
duplicated or extended by anything in this book.

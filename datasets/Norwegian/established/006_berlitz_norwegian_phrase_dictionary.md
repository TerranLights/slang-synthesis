## Grammar points

### Bokmål vs. nynorsk and the historical sketch (pp. i–ii)

The introduction gives a compressed history: Germanic-language runic inscriptions from the Norway region by the 3rd century AD; Viking-era contact (c. 800 AD) and its loanwords into English (several weekday names derive from Norse gods — Tyr, Wodan/Odin, Thor, Frey); Norway's political founding in 872 AD under Harald Fairhaired, speaking Old West Scandinavian (ancestor of Icelandic, Faroese, and Modern Norwegian); Christianization and the Latin alphabet in the 11th century, with the Sagas as the earliest written Old Norwegian literature (12th c.); the 14th-century union under Denmark's Queen Margaret, after which written Norwegian was effectively replaced by written Danish because Norway had no independent courts or government offices; independence in 1814 (though a subsequent union with Sweden delayed full independence to 1905) triggering a 19th-century nationalist effort to re-establish a genuinely Norwegian written language. Two paths emerged: Ivar Aasen's **nynorsk** ("New Norwegian," formerly *landsmål*, "country language"), built from rural dialects and old Norwegian texts; and Knud Knudsen's **bokmål** ("book language," formerly *riksmål*, "state language"), based on upper-class speech but still written in a Danish-influenced orthography, later respelled according to Norwegian pronunciation. The book states the two coexist peacefully, with bokmål being the more commonly used form nationally — and it is the only one this course teaches.

### Pronunciation system (pp. iii–viii)

The book commits to the West Oslo accent as its course standard. It gives a systematic phonetic-guide table covering diphthongs (`ai`, `au`, `ei`/`eg`, `oi`, `ui`, `øy`/`øg`) and both short/long realizations of every vowel letter (`a, e, i, o, u, y, æ, ø, å`), plus consonant rules: `g` is silent before *i*/*y* exceptions aside, is pronounced /y/ before *i*/*y*, and is otherwise a hard /g/; `d` often drops after *r, l, n*, or a long vowel; `h` is silent before *j* or *v*; `k`, `sk`, and `kj`/`tj` palatalize to a sh-like [ç] sound before front vowels/*j*/*y*; `rs` fuses to a single [sh] sound; geminate consonants are genuinely prolonged (not a spelling-only doubling, as English speakers might assume); `r` is an apical tap, Italian/Spanish-style, not the American retroflex approximant. Final `g` is frequently dropped with compensatory vowel lengthening/diphthongization, and final `t` drops in the definite singular of nouns and in the pronoun *det*.

### Noun gender and definiteness (p. 37)

Norwegian nouns carry one of three genders — masculine, feminine, neuter — each taking its own indefinite article: *en* (m), *ei* (f), *et* (n). Plural indefinite adds *-er* to the stem, except monosyllabic neuter nouns, which take no plural ending at all. Definiteness is marked by a **suffixed** article rather than a free-standing word: *-en* (m), *-a* (f), *-et* (n) for singular definite, and *-ene* for all plural definite forms regardless of gender, attached to the noun stem minus any existing final vowel. Example paradigm given: *en gutt* "a boy" → *gutten* "the boy" → *gutter* "boys" → *guttene* "the boys"; *ei jente* "a girl" → *jenta* "the girl" → *jenter* "girls" → *jentene* "the girls"; *et bilde* "a picture" → *bildet* "the picture" → *bilder* "pictures" → *bildene* "the pictures."

### Adjective agreement (pp. 37–38)

Adjectives precede the noun, as in English. The indefinite form uses the dictionary (bare) form for singular masculine/feminine nouns, adds *-t* for a singular neuter noun, and adds *-e* for any plural noun. Example: *en stor gutt* "a big boy" (m) / *ei stor jente* "a big girl" (f) / *et stort hus* "a big house" (n) / *store byer* "big cities" (p). The **definite** form of the adjective always takes the *-e* ending (same form as the indefinite plural), regardless of gender or number, and is used together with the noun's own definite suffix plus a preceding determiner (*den/det/de*): *den store byen* "the big city," *det store huset* "the big house," *de store byene* "the big cities."

### Verb conjugation (p. 38)

Norwegian verbs are described as having four principal forms per the book's own (simplified, pedagogical) scheme: infinitive, present, imperfect (simple past), and perfect. Crucially, **the present-tense verb form does not change for person or number** — unlike English's third-person -*s*, Norwegian uses one invariant present-tense form across all persons (*jeg er* "I am," *vi er* "we are," *han er* "he is" — all the same verb form *er*). Paradigm given for *å spise* "to eat": present *spiser*, imperfect *spiste*, perfect *har spist*. Four high-frequency irregular verbs are tabulated: *å være* "to be" (present *er*, imperfect *var*, perfect *har vært*), *å komme* "to come" (*kommer*, *kom*, *har kommet*), *å ha* "to have" (*har*, *hadde*, *har hatt*), *å gå* "to go" (*går*, *gikk*, *har gått*).

### Time-telling convention (p. 10, footnote 5)

Norwegian counts time by the hour and the half-hour as reference points, rather than always counting forward from the hour as in English. The book's own gloss: "twenty to nine" is literally rendered as "ten after half an hour before nine" (*ti over halv ni*), and "half past one" is literally "half to two" (*halv to*) — the half-hour point is treated as a pivot the way the full hour is in English.

### Floor-numbering convention (p. 15, footnote 7)

The book flags a real cross-cultural counting-system difference: Europeans (including Norwegians) count the ground floor as floor 1, so what an American would call "the second floor" is the Norwegian "first floor" (*første etasje*) — relevant to any "which floor" vocabulary item.

### Politeness-marker usage note (p. 7, footnote 3)

The book explicitly warns against assuming Norwegian is a "less polite" language because *er du snill* ("please," lit. "if you're kind") is used less frequently than English "please" — framed as a pragmatic/register difference, not a politeness gap.

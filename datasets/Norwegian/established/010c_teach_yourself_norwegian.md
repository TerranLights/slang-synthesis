# Norwegian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Teach Yourself Norwegian (Part 3 of 3 — Units 12–16, Grammar appendix, pp. 156–235)

**Source:** Margaretha Danbolt Simons, *Teach Yourself Norwegian* (Teach Yourself Books; Hodder & Stoughton UK / NTC Publishing Group US, 1997). Continues directly from `010b_teach_yourself_norwegian.md` (Part 2: Units 7–11, pp. 88–155). This part covers Unit 12 "Kjære Kari" (Dear Kari), Unit 13 "Påske på fjellet" (Easter in the mountains), Unit 14 "Syttende Mai i Oslo" (Seventeenth of May in Oslo), Unit 15 "Sankt Hansaften på Vestlandet" (Midsummer in the West Country), Unit 16 "Hva nå" (What now?), and the book's back-matter Grammar appendix (Irregular Nouns, Irregular Verbs, Modal Verbs), pp. 156–235.

**Non-redundant-supplement scoping:**
- Same rationale as Parts 1–2 for core grammar (personal pronouns, basic tense system, article/gender mechanics) — skipped as re-exposition.
- **The back-matter Grammar appendix (Irregular Nouns pp. 232–233, Irregular Verbs pp. 233–235, Modal Verbs p. 235) is explicitly skipped from re-tabulation here as redundant**: nearly every irregular noun it lists (far, mor, bror, søster, barn, kafe, hybel, juletre, museum, natt, rom, seddel, ski, sko, skulder, sommer, studium, støvel, svoger, sykkel, tre, tå, vinter, øye) was already captured in this file-set's per-unit vocabulary tables (Parts 1–2) where each word was first introduced, with the same paradigm forms given there; and the irregular/strong-verb list (~70 verbs) is a strict subset of the far larger (~225-verb) comprehensive paradigm appendix already fully tabulated in `001_norwegian_verbs_and_essentials_of_grammar.md`. Cross-checked specifically and confirmed no appendix entry introduces a form, spelling variant, or gloss not already present in either source. **One genuinely new item was found and is kept:** the modal verb **å tore** ("to dare": tør/torde/tort) was not previously captured in 001's modal-verb set (kan/vil/skal/må/bør) — added to the Grammar points below.
- **Kept as new:** the historical grading/dialectal vocabulary for Norway's constitutional history (Unit 14), the full preposition reference list with worked multi-sense examples (Unit 16), the s-genitive and genitive-by-preposition rules, the indefinite pronoun system (man/en, noen/noe, ingen/ingenting/intet), the passive-voice and non-passive s-verb mechanisms (Unit 15), and — per the coverage rule — **all vocabulary**, extracted comprehensively regardless of grammar-topic overlap.
- **The book's own Norwegian–English Vocabulary (alphabetical glossary, pp. 236–249) is deliberately NOT re-extracted as a separate table.** On inspection it is a consolidated cross-reference index of the same words already introduced unit-by-unit throughout this three-part extraction (each entry cites the unit number where the word first appears) — re-tabulating it would duplicate this file-set's own per-unit vocabulary tables, not add new content. This is a within-source redundancy decision (the same book's own back-matter restating its own front-matter), distinct from the cross-book non-redundancy principle — vocabulary is never skipped as redundant *across different books*, only this literal self-duplicate index within one book is skipped. The book's Index (p. 250–252) was read in full as a navigation aid and its "Slang words" cross-references were followed and are reflected in Parts 1–3's flagged findings.

**Flagged finding — book's own explicit slang annotation (confirmed via Index "Slang words," Unit 13, p. 176):** the "Slik sier vi det" (How to say it) box under "Some slang expressions" explicitly labels the following as slang: **Dette er gøy!** ("This is great!"), **Det er kjempegøy!** ("It is great fun!"), **kjempekos hytte** ("a super cottage"), **kjempefin ferie** ("a super holiday"), **Kult!** ("Cool!"). Tagged `slang` below.

**Flagged finding — cross-linguistic false-cognate note (Unit 15, p. 208):** the book includes an explicit aside telling English-speaking learners not to be embarrassed by **skitt** (dirt, noun) and **skitten** (dirty, adjective) despite **skitt** being pronounced like the English obscenity — the book states these "are perfectly good, useful Norwegian words" and that **skitt!** used as a mild expletive is no stronger than English "bother!". Captured as a `core`-tier pair with the book's own usage note preserved, since this is exactly the kind of cross-language taboo-adjacency this project's slang-synthesis work cares about.

**Vision-reading guard:** continues from Parts 1–2 — no text layer, vision-read at 150 DPI, spread offset verified empirically (this part's printed pages 156–235 correspond to PDF pages 81–120). No handwritten marginalia found anywhere in this range. One deliberate stylistic note already flagged in Part 1: Unit 12's Tekst 5 (p. 162) is printed in a cursive/handwriting-style typeface to represent an in-story informal letter — this is the book's own typeset design, not a reader's annotation.

## Grammar points

### Nouns of foreign origin ending in -um (Unit 12, p.165)

A small closed class of borrowed nouns ending in **-um** are all et-words and decline by dropping the **-um** and adding the regular endings: **museum/museet/museer/museene** (museum), **studium/studiet/studier/studiene** (academic course), **laboratorium/laboratoriet/laboratorier/laboratoriene** (laboratory), **sentrum/sentret/sentrer/sentrene** (centre).

### Annen/annet/andre (Unit 12, p.165)

**Andre** is both the ordinal "2nd" and the adjective "other." As an adjective it follows the regular en/et/plural pattern: **en annen dag** (another/a different day), **et annet hus** (another/a different house), **andre dager** (other days). The book draws a three-way contrast: *en annen kopp* ("another/different cup") vs. *en kopp til* ("one more cup," i.e. an additional one of the same) vs. *den andre koppen* ("the other [specific, already-known] cup") — three distinct ways to say "another" depending on whether it means different-kind, additional-same-kind, or the remaining specific one.

### Words ending in m — the double-m-at-end-of-word spelling rule (Unit 12, p.164)

Norwegian orthography disallows a word ending in a doubled **m**, even where pronunciation would call for a geminate consonant. The **m** is doubled only when an ending follows: e.g. **rom** (room) but **rommet** (the room), **hjem** (home) but presumably similar compounds. This is distinct from — though related to — the general "short vowel before double consonant" rule covered in `003_phonology_of_norwegian.md`; this specific exception (m is never geminate word-finally) was not previously captured.

### Feminine-gender nouns in the definite singular (Unit 13, p.177)

The book flags a small set of nouns where the feminine (ei-word) gender is commonly used, especially in the definite singular: **en/ei jente → jenta** (girl), **en/ei hytte → hytta** (cabin), **en/ei geit → geita** (goat). Plurals follow the regular common-gender pattern (jentene, hyttene, geitene) regardless. This reinforces and extends the single "jenta" example already noted in `001_norwegian_verbs_and_essentials_of_grammar.md`.

### Da ("I wish") takes a different tense than English (Unit 14, p.190)

*Jeg skulle ønske du var her nå* ("I wish you were here now") and *Jeg skulle ønske at jeg var rik* ("I wish I were rich") use **skulle** (past-tense "shall") + ordinary past tense of the main clause, where English uses a subjunctive-flavored past. The book explicitly flags this as "a different tense" from what an English speaker would expect, i.e. a genuine grammatical-mapping mismatch point for the wish-construction, not just a vocabulary difference.

### Da/når distinction for "when" (Unit 14, p.190)

**Da** and **når** both translate "when," but are not interchangeable: **da** is used for a single past event (*Da hun ikke likte norsk mat, reiste hun hjem* — "As/when she didn't like Norwegian food, she went home"), while **når** covers the present/future and repeated/habitual past events. A **when**-question is always **når**, never **da** (*Når kom han?* "When did he come?"). The book gives a mnemonic: *den gang da — hver gang når* ("that time when — every time when").

### Historical background: 1814 constitution and the Sweden–Norway union (Unit 14, p.195)

The book gives a compact historical account relevant to **Syttende Mai** (17th May), Norway's National Day: Count Jean Bernadotte (adopted by the Swedish king, taking the name Karl Johan) became Crown Prince of Sweden; after Napoleon's defeat, Sweden — on the winning side — was ceded Norway by Denmark (the losing side) as part of the peace settlement. Norwegians revolted and, with Danish-born governor Christian Frederik's help, drafted a new constitution, accepted 17 May 1814. Norway nonetheless entered a union with Sweden that lasted until 1905, when it finally became fully independent (as recounted by the character Bente, Unit 14, p.188–189, with specific ancestor names Carl/Haakon, Alexander/Olav).

### The modal verb å tore (dare) — not previously captured (Grammar appendix, p.235)

The Modal Verbs table lists **å tore**: present **tør**, past **torde**, perfect **tort** ("to dare"). This modal was not present in `001_norwegian_verbs_and_essentials_of_grammar.md`'s modal set (kan/vil/skal/må/bør) and is added here as the one genuinely new item found in the otherwise-redundant back-matter Grammar appendix.

### Hverandre — the reciprocal pronoun, and its genitive (Unit 16, p.216)

**Hverandre** ("each other") is invariant regardless of number/gender of the parties, and can take the genitive **-s**: *De leste hverandres bøker* ("They read each other's books"), *De passet hverandres barn* ("They looked after each other's children"). This extends the brief mention of hverandre in Unit 5 (Part 1 of this extraction).

### The s-genitive and genitive-by-preposition (Unit 16, p.219)

The genitive is formed by adding **-s** with no apostrophe, for both singular and plural, and for objects as well as persons: **guttens hund** (the boy's dog), **guttenes hund** (the boys' dog), **husets vinduer** (the windows of the house). In more formal speech, a preposition construction is preferred instead: **pikens navn** ↔ **navnet til piken** (the girl's name); here what is "owned" takes the definite form. A compound noun can also substitute for either genitive construction: **en damesko** ("a lady's shoe") instead of "skoen til damen."

### Indefinite pronouns: man/en, noen/noe, ingen/ingenting/intet (Unit 16, p.220–221)

- **Man** and **en** both mean the generic "one" (as in "one never knows"); **man** is subject-only, while **en** can also serve as object and has a genitive **ens** ("one's").
- **Noen** covers "some/any" for plural nouns in all contexts, and for singular common-gender countable nouns specifically in questions/negatives; when not referring to a noun it means "somebody/anybody."
- **Noe** covers "some/any" for uncountable nouns regardless of gender, "any/a" for singular neuter nouns in negative/question contexts, and "something/anything" when standing alone.
- **Ingen** ("no/nobody/nothing") is the direct opposite of noen; **ingenting** is an alternative word for "nothing"; **intet** is a very formal neuter-specific variant of ingen.

### The passive voice — two mechanisms (Unit 15, p.205)

Norwegian expresses the passive two ways: (1) the **bli**-passive (**bli/blir/ble/har blitt** + past participle): *Kaken ble spist av Bente* ("The cake was eaten by Bente"); (2) the **s-verb passive** (add **-s** to the present-tense stem), used mainly in the present tense and with modal verbs: *Kaken spises av Bente* ("The cake is eaten by Bente"), *Noe må gjøres* ("Something has to be done") — this s-passive can usually be replaced by **bli** + past participle (*Noe må bli gjort*). A separate, unrelated phenomenon is the **non-passive s-verb** — active-voice verbs that happen to end in -s, some with reciprocal meaning ("each other"): **synes** (think/be of the opinion), **finnes** (be found/exist), **føles** (feel, in the sense "it feels..."), **skilles** (get divorced), **sees**/**møttes** (see/meet each other).

**Source:** Margaretha Danbolt Simons, *Teach Yourself Norwegian* (Teach Yourself Books; Hodder & Stoughton UK / NTC Publishing Group US, 1997). Continues directly from `010_teach_yourself_norwegian.md` (Part 1: Introduction, Pronunciation, Units 1–6, pp. 1–87). This part covers Unit 7 "Bentes familie" (Bente's family), Unit 8 "John kolliderer og kommer på sykehus" (John is in a crash), Unit 9 "Bente får jobb og John går på apotek" (Bente gets a job, John goes to the chemist's), Unit 10 "John og Bente går i selskap" (John and Bente go to parties), and Unit 11 "God Jul! Godt Nyttår!" (Happy Christmas! Happy New Year!), pp. 88–155.

**Non-redundant-supplement scoping:** same rationale as Part 1 — see that file's opening note for the full statement of what's already covered by `001`/`002`/`003`. In this part, **personal/object pronouns, basic reflexive-pronoun (seg) mechanics, the months of the year, and the core present/past/perfect tense system** are skipped as grammar re-exposition (already fully tabulated in 001/002); only genuinely new material is kept as a Grammar point below — notably the full irregular-noun paradigms for body parts and kinship terms not previously captured, the **leke vs. spille** ("play with toys" vs. "play" in every other sense) semantic split, the multi-sense verb **å få**, the **legge/ligge, sette/sitte** (lay/lie, set/sit) transitivity-pair distinction, the full ordinal-number list to 90th (001 only captured 1st–10th), and the historical note on the **aa → å** spelling reform (not covered by any prior Norwegian source in this project). **All vocabulary is extracted comprehensively regardless of grammar overlap**, per the coverage rule.

**Flagged finding — book's own explicit slang annotation, confirmed via the back-of-book Index's "Slang words" heading (p. 252):** Unit 7, p. 93 ("casual words") is indexed by the book itself as slang content. On that page, the "Slik sier vi det" (How to say it) section under the heading "One child hurling abuse at another" explicitly presents **din drittunge!** ("you dirty kid!"), **din tufs!** ("you feeble thing!"), and **din gris!** ("you pig!") as children's playground insults — the book glosses **dritt** = "dirt/muck" and **unge** = "kid/baby animal" to explain the compound. These three terms are tagged `slang`/`taboo`-adjacent below as directly instructed by the source's own framing (register explicitly marked by context: child-to-child insult vocabulary, not the book's neutral register).

## Grammar points

### The leke/spille distinction (Unit 7, p.96)

**Å leke** means "to play" specifically with toys (children playing); **å spille** means "to play" in every other sense — sport, gambling, musical instruments, theatre. Both are weak Group 2 verbs (å leke, leker, lekte; å spille, spiller, spilte — note the latter drops one l in the past tense, an explicitly flagged spelling quirk). English collapses both senses into a single verb "play," so this is a genuine semantic-split point.

### Irregular kinship and body-part noun paradigms (Units 7–8)

The book gives full four-form (indefinite singular / definite singular / indefinite plural / definite plural) paradigms for several nouns not previously captured in this project's established files: **far/faren/fedre/fedrene** (father), **mor/moren (or mora)/mødre/mødrene** (mother), **bror/broren/brødre/brødrene** (brother), **søster/søsteren (or søstera)/søstre/søstrene** (sister) — all showing the characteristic Germanic umlaut-plural pattern (vowel change rather than suffix) — plus **barn/barnet/barn/barna** (child, irregular -a definite plural) and the body-part set **øye, øre, hånd, skulder, finger, kne, tå, fot** (see Vocabulary table above for full forms).

### The multi-sense verb å få (Unit 11, p.148)

**Å få** ("to get/receive") carries at least four distinct pragmatic senses the book distinguishes explicitly: (a) asking/giving permission (*Får jeg komme inn?* "May I come in?"), (b) managing/getting a chance to (*Han får ikke sove når det er lyst* "He can't sleep when it's light"), (c) a softly-phrased order (*Du får gjøre som jeg sier* "You will do as I say"), and (d) a future-tense use (*Vi får se* "We shall see"). This is a single lexical item spanning what English spreads across "get," "may," "will," and an imperative softener.

### Legge/ligge, sette/sitte — transitive/intransitive placement-verb pairs (Unit 10, p.136)

Norwegian splits "lay/put" (transitive, **å legge**) from "lie" (intransitive, **å ligge**), and "set/place" (transitive, **å sette**) from "sit" (intransitive, **å sitte**) — a transitivity distinction English also makes (lay/lie) but only for the first pair, not uniformly. The book further specifies: **legge** is used for something flat/wide (a book), **sette** for something that stands (a vase, a glass). Both verb pairs also have reflexive forms (**legge seg** "go to bed/lie down," **sette seg** "sit down").

### Full ordinal numbers, 1st–90th (Unit 10, p.134)

The book tabulates the complete ordinal series **første, andre, tredje, fjerde, femte, sjette, sjuende, åttende, niende, tiende, ellevte, tolvte, trettende, fjortende, femtende, sekstende, syttende, attende, nittende, tjuende** (1st–20th), then by tens: **trettiende, førtiende, femtiende, sekstiende, syttiende, åttiende, nittiende** (30th–90th). This is substantially fuller than the 1st–10th list previously captured in `001_norwegian_verbs_and_essentials_of_grammar.md`.

### Historical spelling note: aa → å (Unit 14, cross-referenced here as background, p.196)

Until roughly 70 years before the book's 1997 publication (i.e. the early-to-mid 20th century), the letter **å** was written as the digraph **aa** — the same reform timeline applied slightly later in Danish. Older family names retain the **aa** spelling; the book gives the example of King Harald's grandfather **Haakon**, after whom the Crown Prince (at time of writing), **Haakon Magnus**, is named. This orthographic-history point is not covered by any other Norwegian source extracted for this project so far.

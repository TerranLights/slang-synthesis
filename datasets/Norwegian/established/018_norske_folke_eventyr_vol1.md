# Norwegian — Established Vocabulary/Idiom: *Norske Folke-Eventyr*, vol. I (native-language literature sample)

**Source:** Peter Christen Asbjørnsen and Jørgen Moe (with Jørgen Moe's son Moltke Moe as editor of
this edition), *Norske Folke-Eventyr*, Volume I. Title page/colophon identifies this as the
**7th edition ("Syvende Udgave")**, with Moltke Moe's editorial foreword dated **September 1904**
(the volume also reprints the 6th-edition foreword, dated earlier, which explains that from the 6th
edition onward the work was first split into two volumes — previous editions had been a single
book). The original Asbjørnsen & Moe tale collection was first published in installments in
1842–1844; this is a much later, posthumously-re-edited printing of that collection (Asbjørnsen died
1885, Jørgen Moe 1882 — both are long dead by this edition's date, hence Moltke Moe's editorial
stewardship). Scanned/digitized by Google Books (`Producer: Google Books PDF Converter`, per
`pdfinfo`); 243 PDF pages, no usable text layer at all (confirmed via `pdftotext` — every page
vision-read). File: `Norske folke-eventyr vol. I.pdf`.

**Page-offset note.** PDF page 17 = printed page 1 of the tale-body text (offset confirmed by direct
inspection, not assumed — per the spec's page-range-estimate gotcha). Printed pages 1–208 (PDF
pages 17–224ish) hold tales 1–34; a table-of-contents page also previews volume II's contents
(tales 35–60), which are **not** in this file (that's a separate PDF/volume, out of scope here).

**This is NOT a grammar-reference extraction.** Waves 1–3 of this project's Norwegian work already
extracted 17 grammar/dictionary/phrasebook files (`001`–`017`). This file is Wave 4's native-language
literature sample: genuine 19th-century literary usage — vocabulary, idiom, folk-narrative formulae,
and archaic/dialect-flavored forms — sampled from across the volume, not an exhaustive read-through
or grammar tabulation.

**Edition note on dialect/orthography.** Moltke Moe's foreword (PDF pp. 9–10, printed pp. VII–VIII)
explicitly states he based the text's spelling/wording on Asbjørnsen and Moe's **own later revisions**
("senere skrivebrug"), and that earlier editions' orthography had drifted inconsistently across the
two authors and across print runs; he also notes Asbjørnsen's own later, independent *Ny Samling*
("New Collection") pushed further toward "det brede østlandske særpræg" — the broad Eastern-Norway
(Østlandet) dialect flavor — in its diction. This is a **meta-level, whole-book note**, not a
per-word regional tag: no entry below carries an `Attested Region` value, since the sampled tales
themselves carry no explicit per-word dialect tags (no `[B]`/`[C]`/`[S]`-style markers of the kind
seen in this project's comparative sources) — the dialect coloring is in the general prose register,
not marked word-by-word.

**Vision-reading guard — marginalia check.** Small handwritten pencil numerals (single digits:
"3", "6", "9", "15", "20"-ish) appear in the left margin of several **front-matter** pages only
(the Asbjørnsen/Moe biographical notes and the prefaces, roughly PDF pp. 3, 5, 7, 8, 13–15) —
these read as a prior owner's or library's cataloging/tally annotation, clearly inconsistent with
the printed typeface and sitting in the blank margin, not the text block. **Excluded entirely, not
extracted.** A printed accession number ("179270") also appears at the foot of the Asbjørnsen
biography page in a distinct typeface — this is a library stamp, not a content item, also excluded.
**No marginalia of any kind was found on any of the tale-body pages actually sampled for
vocabulary/idiom below** (tales 1, 2, 6, 9, 15, 17, 20, 27, 29, 34) — those renders were clean.

## Sampling strategy and coverage

Located the table of contents (PDF pp. 13–15) first, confirmed the printed-page-1 = PDF-page-17
offset by direct inspection, then sampled **10 tales spread across the whole volume** (early,
middle, and late), reading 2–3 page images per tale (20 page images total):

- **Tale 1** — *Om Askeladden som stjal Troldets Sølvænder, Sengetæppe, og Guldharpe* (Jørgen Moe), printed pp. 1–8 — early
- **Tale 2** — *Gjertrudsfuglen* (Jørgen Moe), printed pp. 8–9 — early
- **Tale 6** — *Askeladden som kapaad med Troldet* (Jørgen Moe), printed pp. 39–42 — early
- **Tale 9** — *De tre Prinsesser i Hvidtenland* (Jørgen Moe), printed pp. 51–58 — early-mid
- **Tale 15** — *Manddatteren og Kjærringdatteren* (Asbjørnsen), printed pp. 83–94 — mid
- **Tale 17** — *Bjørnen og Ræven* (I. Hvorfor Bjørnen er stubrumpet / II. Ræven snyder Bjørnen for Julekosten) (Jørgen Moe), printed pp. 98–101 — mid
- **Tale 20** — *Ræven som Gjæter* (Asbjørnsen), printed p. 122 — mid
- **Tale 27** — *Soria Moria Slot* (Asbjørnsen), printed pp. 157–170 — mid-late
- **Tale 29** — *Vesle Aase Gaasepige* (Asbjørnsen), printed pp. 177–182 — late
- **Tale 34** — *Mestertyven* (Asbjørnsen), printed pp. 208–end of vol. — late (final tale)

**Copyright discipline applied:** every quoted fragment below is a short illustrative phrase/formula
(never more than a sentence), paraphrased around with analysis — no bulk reproduction of any
dialogue block or narrative passage.

## Grammar/orthography notes (brief — this is a literature sample, not a grammar extraction)

These are observations about the text's own period orthography/morphology, not a systematic grammar
chapter — flagged because they're genuine era-markers useful for dating/placing a text sample, and
because 001–017 (the grammar references) describe modern Bokmål forms these diverge from.

### Universal noun capitalization
Every common noun in the running text is capitalized (`Mand`, `Konge`, `Skog`, `Hus`, `Prinsesse`,
etc.) — a German-influenced orthographic convention of 19th-century Dano-Norwegian printing, dropped
in later reforms. This is a whole-text formatting fact, not a per-word phenomenon, so it isn't given
its own vocabulary row, but it's worth flagging explicitly since it could otherwise look like
inconsistent/erroneous capitalization in extracted quotes above.

### Pre-reform modal preterites and case-marked pronouns
See the `vilde/skulde/kunde` and `mig/dig` table rows above — both are systematic, text-wide patterns
rather than isolated vocabulary items, included in the table because they're genuinely useful
era-dating signals for any later cross-source comparison work.

### Dialogue vs. narration register split
Across all ten sampled tales, direct-speech dialogue consistently carries shorter sentences, more
interjections (`Aa`, `Ja ja`, `Nei`), and the curse/exclamation vocabulary (`Huttetu!`, `Tvi være
dig!`), while narration stays in longer, more subordinated sentences with none of that interjection
density — a clean register split worth noting for later mechanics-analysis work on how this
project's synthesized conlang might want to differentiate spoken vs. narrated registers.

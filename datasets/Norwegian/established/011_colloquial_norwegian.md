## Lesson 1 — Pamela og Håkon

**Skipped as redundant:** personal subject pronouns (jeg/du/han/hun/den/det/vi/dere/de — full
paradigm in 001 and the Ready-reference summary of 002); the present tense of **være** and the
general -r present-tense rule (001, 002); the bare/marked infinitive distinction (001).

**New — expressing occupation/nationality/religion with no article.** Confirmed already in 002's
coverage of article-dropping rules, but this book gives the clean three-way minimal set from a single
example family: **Han er lærer** / **Han er katolikk** / **Han er inder** ("He is a teacher/a
Catholic/an Indian") — no article in any of the three semantic categories (occupation, religion,
nationality) when the noun stands alone and unmodified.

**New — the book's own word-order "pattern" notation.** Distinct from (not redundant with) 002's own
treatment of word order: this book teaches Norwegian main-clause word order as a fill-in-the-columns
grid with the headers **F (front) / v (finite verb) / n (nominal/subject, when inverted) / a (adverb,
chiefly negation) / V (non-finite verb) / N (object/complement) / A (adverbial phrase) / C
(conjunction, for combined/subordinate clauses)**. Normal order: subject in F, finite verb in v.
**Inverted order** (anything other than the subject fronted) pushes the subject into n, immediately
after the verb:

Normal order — `F: Hun / v: snakker / n: — / Others: alle språk` ("She speaks all languages").
Inverted order — `F: Vanligvis / v: snakker / n: vi / Others: norsk` ("Usually we speak Norwegian").

Negation (**ikke**, or any adverb) always sits in column **a**, immediately after the subject-verb
group regardless of normal/inverted order; if the inverted subject is a full noun/name rather than a
pronoun, the adverb in **a** is placed *before* it: **Fra i dag av røyker ikke Håkon** ("From today
Håkon doesn't smoke"). This whole grid is progressively built up across the book (verbs in V, objects
in N added in Lesson 6; subordinate-clause word order — no F column, and n/a/v reordered to n-a-v — in
Lesson 10; direct/indirect object ordering in Lesson 19) and is this book's single most distinctive
and reusable piece of original pedagogical apparatus — worth keeping as an alternative cross-check
notation against 002's rule-based word-order description, since the two independently converge on the
same underlying facts from different presentational angles.

**Skipped:** Norwegian numbers 0–1000 (full cardinal list, p. 24–25) — fully redundant with 001's
numeral table.

## Lesson 2 — Håkon på jobb

**Skipped as redundant:** three-gender noun system and singular indefinite/definite articles (full
paradigm already in 001/002); yes/no-question formation by subject-verb inversion (clean minimal
presentation here, but the underlying mechanism is already documented via word order in 002).

**New — points-of-the-compass derivational family.** øst/vest/nord/sør ("east/west/north/south")
systematically combine with **for** (øst for = "to the east of"), a bare locative use (i nord = "in
the north"), **mot** + noun (mot nord = "towards the north"), an adjectival **-lig** suffix (østlig,
nordlig = "eastern, northern") and a directional-source **-fra** suffix (østfra, nordfra = "from the
east/north") — a small but fully productive four-way derivational paradigm from each of the four
cardinal roots, not previously captured for this language in this project.

## Lesson 3 — På innkjøp

**Skipped as redundant:** noun plural formation and the glossary's gender/plural shorthand notation
(e.g. `banan (en, -er)`) — fully covered in 001's vocabulary-table conventions and 002's declension
tables, including the same informal/formal **-a**-vs-**-ene** definite-plural-neuter register split
(see the dedicated register-notes file for the explicit informal/formal framing).

**New — the nei/ja/jo three-way answer system.** Distinct from 001/002 (which note **jo** mainly as
a modal/discourse adverb): this book gives the complete answer-particle paradigm as its own rule set.
**Nei** negates any question. **Ja** affirms a *positively* phrased question (**Kan jeg hjelpe Dem?**
→ **Ja takk**). **Jo** affirms a *negatively* phrased question, contradicting its implied "no" (**Har
De aldri vært i Norge før?** → **Jo, for fire år siden** — "Yes [I have], four years ago") — i.e.
Norwegian grammatically distinguishes "yes, as you'd expect" from "yes, contrary to what you implied"
in a way English's single "yes" does not.

**New — dates and ordinals as one combined rule.** Norwegian has only **one** way to say a date
(unlike English's "the eighth of April" / "April the eighth"): ordinal number + month name, with no
preposition — **(den) åttende april**, written **8. april 1972** or **8.4.1972**. Ordinal numbers
1–12 are irregular/suppletive (not covered here — see 001's numeral appendix); 13th onward are
regular cardinal + **-ende** (nittende, førtiende).

**New — the Gratulerer/God... congratulation-phrase family.** **Gratulerer** ("congratulations/many
happy returns") + **med** + the occasion (**Gratulerer med dagen!** "Happy birthday!"); **God** +
noun as a fixed "have a nice..." formula covering a closed list of occasions (ferie, helg, tur,
bedring, jul, påske) alongside the time-of-day greetings already noted in the register-notes file.

## Lesson 4 — Hjemme

**Skipped as redundant:** the five weak-verb conjugation classes and their past-tense/past-participle
suffix patterns, and the full strong/irregular verb list — both already tabulated comprehensively in
001 (Common Verbs appendix) and 002 (full strong-verb list with ablaut).

**New — transitivity as a four-way typology, explicitly including ditransitives.** Neither 001 nor
002 frames transitivity as its own named grammar point. This book gives a clean four-way split:
**transitive** (needs an object: *tømme* "empty"), **intransitive** (cannot take one: *komme*
"come" — note **være/bli** take a complement, not an object, when used without one), **either/or**
(*male* "paint", transitive with an object or intransitive meaning "to be a painter"), and
**ditransitive** (two objects: *gi* "give" — **Pamela har gitt Marit et eple**). This matters for the
present-perfect auxiliary choice below and for direct/indirect object ordering (Lesson 19, see file
011b).

**New — present perfect with er vs. har is a semantically conditioned choice, not free variation.**
**Har** + past participle is the default present perfect. **Er** + past participle is used only when
*both* conditions hold: the verb is intransitive (no object) **and** it denotes motion or change of
state (**Er du kommet?**, **Den er gått**, **Han er blitt syk**). The book explicitly flags this as a
live, ongoing change: "**har** is taking over more and more from **er** in all contexts in modern
Norwegian" — a captured-in-real-time grammaticalization/register shift worth flagging for any
conlang-synthesis work modeling auxiliary-system drift.

## Lesson 5 — Familien

**Skipped as redundant:** the possessive adjective/pronoun paradigm (min/mi/mitt/mine, etc.) and the
reflexive possessive **sin/si/sitt/sine** — both fully tabulated in 002's Ready-reference pronoun
section and revisited with worked minimal pairs in Lesson 18 of this book (see file 011b); basic
genitive **-s** suffixation (002).

**New — the t-form/e-form adjective-declension naming convention and its full exception taxonomy.**
This book's own three-slot naming (**basic form / t-form / e-form**, corresponding to 002's
masculine-feminine / neuter / plural-and-definite slots) comes with a tighter, example-driven
exception list worth cross-checking against 002's version: no extra **-t** for adjectives ending in
**-sk, -ig,** a **-t** already, or some ending in **-d** (viktoriansk, nydelig, interessant, glad);
double-consonant simplification before **-t** (grønn → grønt); extra **-t** after a stressed vowel
(ny → nytt); optional **-e**-dropping for **blå/grå** (blått, blå — no *blåe*); and the **-el/-en/-er**
contraction class (gammel → gammelt → gamle). **Liten** is flagged as the only truly irregular
adjective with five distinct forms (further detailed in 002).

**New — register split in noun-phrase order with adjectives.** When a definite noun carries an
adjective, the possessive/genitive can precede the whole phrase with the *noun* in its **indefinite**
form (**hans nye puslespill**, **Petters engelske svigerinne**) — but the book explicitly marks this
as "mostly used in more formal style, and rarely in spoken Norwegian," where the possessive instead
follows the noun (**det nye puslespillet hans**) and a prepositional phrase replaces the genitive
(**den engelske svigerinnen til Petter**) — a three-way formal/colloquial syntactic alternation for
expressing the same possessive-plus-adjective meaning, not previously documented for this language in
this project. See the register-notes file for the parallel genitive-vs.-prepositional-phrase register
split from this same lesson.

## Lesson 6 — På hytta

**Skipped as redundant:** the four modal auxiliaries kunne/måtte/ville/skulle and their basic
present/past forms (002's Ready-reference modal table).

**New — a functional (not just formal) breakdown of modal-verb uses.** This book organizes the modals
by *communicative function* rather than by paradigm, which 002 does not do: **kunne** for ability or
possibility (*Vi kan ikke kjøre så langt*); **kunne/skulle** for suggestions, with a felt difference
between them — **skulle** for an idea raised "out of the blue," **kunne** for a suggestion offered
within an ongoing exchange; **måtte/skulle** for necessity, with the periphrastic alternative **bli
nødt til (å)** as "more or less interchangeable" with necessity-**skulle**; **kunne** (+ **få**/+
**godt**) for asking/granting permission, and the same **kunne** + negative for refusing it; **ville**
for the speaker's own will, **skulle/måtte** for imposing will on someone else, with *degree of
emphasis on ville/skulle* iconically tracking *strength of will* (more stress = stronger insistence);
**ville + gjerne** or past-tense **skulle + ønske** for wishes; and **skulle** + bare infinitive for
assurances/promises. **Past-tense modals systematically shift the modal flavor rather than just
marking past time** — past **kunne** in a main clause reads as a more hypothetical suggestion, past
**kunne** in a question reads as more polite/modest, and past **skulle** in a question reads as "what
we really ought to do" — the book calls this out explicitly as a point where English speakers wrongly
assume the two languages' modal-tense systems line up.

## Lesson 7 — Et familiebesøk

**Skipped as redundant:** the imperative's identity with the verb stem (001).

**New — a graded three-rung politeness ladder for requests, built from one verb.** Bare imperative
(**Lukk døren!** — can read as rude); **Vil du/De** + bare infinitive (**Vil du lukke døren?**, a
genuine "will you please"); and the maximal-politeness compound **Vil du/De være så snill å** + **å**
+ infinitive ("would you be so kind as to..."). A fourth, *lower*-register form sits alongside this
ladder rather than above it: **gidde** + bare infinitive (**Gidder du lukke døren?**), explicitly
flagged by the book as "mainly heard in casual talk among people who know each other" — see the
register-notes file, which treats **gidde** as this lesson's flagship colloquial finding (it recurs
through the book as a marker of reluctance/not-being-bothered, not just a politeness-register verb).

**New — ha lyst til vs. gidde as a symmetric inclination/disinclination pair.** **Ha lyst til** + å +
infinitive expresses positive inclination ("feel like doing"); its negative-leaning near-opposite is
**gidde** (**+ ikke** + bare infinitive) for "can't be bothered." The two are not simple opposites
(ha lyst til can itself be negated too: *Jeg har ikke lyst til å reise*), but the book pairs them
pedagogically as the two go-to verbs for talking about motivation in casual speech.

**New — special motion-elliptical uses of skulle/ville/kunne.** **Skulle** and **ville** can appear
*without* a verb of motion when followed by a place phrase (**Jeg skal til flyplassen** — "I'm
going/off to the airport"); **kunne** + a language name can drop an implied **snakke** ("speak") when
the question is about language ability (**Kan Karin norsk?** = "Does Karin know/speak Norwegian?").

## Lesson 8 — Til bords

**Skipped as redundant:** none of this lesson's core grammar — **det** as a formal/dummy subject (the
"there is.../it is..." construction, four sub-uses: no real subject, an infinitival real subject, a
clausal real subject, and backward reference) is genuinely new material not covered in 001–003, so it
is kept here rather than skipped.

**New — det as a formal subject, four environments.** (1) **Det** + a form of **være** corresponds to
English "there is/are," and — unlike English, which restricts this construction to "be" — Norwegian
extends it to *any* verb: **Det kommer et tog klokken fem** ("A train arrives at five," lit. "There
comes a train..."). (2) **Det** standing in for an impersonal/weather subject, where any attached
adjective must take the neuter t-form regardless of what (if anything) it really refers to: **Det er
kaldt**, **Det snør**. (3) **Det** anticipating an infinitival real subject (**Det er dyrt å spise
ute**). (4) **Det** anticipating a clausal real subject (**Det er sant at...**) or referring back to
part/all of a previous sentence.

**New — burde vs. tore as distinct moral-duty and daring modals.** **Burde (bør, burde, burdet)** +
bare infinitive expresses moral obligation, and its *past* tense specifically marks an unfulfilled
duty ("ought to have, but didn't" — **Man burde ikke hugge så mange trær**). **Tore (tør, torde,
tort)** + bare infinitive expresses daring (**Tør du virkelig kjøre slik?**) — a distinct modal this
project had not previously documented in a dedicated entry (001's Common Verbs appendix lists *tore*
only as a bare paradigm line, with no functional gloss).

**New — colour compounding.** The 9 basic colour terms (rød, oransje, gul, grønn, blå, brun, hvit,
grå, svart) combine freely: two colour roots concatenate directly for an in-between shade
(**blågrønn**), and **lyse-**/**mørke-** prefix for lighter/darker shades (**lysegul**, **mørkerød**)
— a small, fully productive compounding system not previously recorded for this language.

## Lesson 9 — En vanlig dag

**Skipped as redundant:** the three present-passive forms (s-passive, bli-passive, være-passive) —
already covered systematically via the morphology appendix cross-referenced in 001 and the full
account in 002's companion volume; this lesson's treatment adds no new form, only the functional
split between "something happening" (s-/bli-passive) vs. "result of an action" (være-passive), which
is a useful but minor refinement, not a new grammatical fact.

**New — object and reflexive pronoun forms unified, plus the hverandre reciprocal.** Direct/indirect
object and post-preposition pronoun forms (meg/deg/ham/henne/den/det/oss/dere/dem) are the same set as
the reflexive pronouns except in the third person, where reflexive collapses everything to **seg**
(**Anders falt og slo seg** "Anders fell and hurt himself" vs. **Marit slo henne** "Marit hit her
[someone else]"). **Selv** can be added to any reflexive for emphasis ("myself, and nobody else"), and
critically, **Norwegian never drops the reflexive pronoun the way a group of English verbs do**
(English "I never wash" = Norwegian **Jeg vasker meg aldri**, reflexive obligatory). The reciprocal
**hverandre** ("each other") has a productive genitive-like form, **hverandres** (**De malte
hverandres hus**). Already touched on in 002's pronoun tables, but not with this functional framing or
the reflexive-non-omission rule, which is new.

**New — the nine-adverb stative/dynamic location system, with a twist on directional precision.**
Confirmed as a genuine *addition* to (not duplication of) `003_phonology_of_norwegian.md`'s brief
mention of the same oppe/opp-type pairs: this book gives the **full nine-pair set** (oppe/opp,
nede/ned, ute/ut, inne/inn, framme/fram, hjemme/hjem, borte/bort, her/hit, der/dit) and a second,
subtler rule beyond simple stative-vs-dynamic: when combined with a preposition, the *longer* (stative)
form means motion *confined within* an area (**Pamela gikk inne i kjøkkenet** — "was walking about
in the kitchen," already inside it) while the *short* (dynamic) form means crossing *into* that area
from outside it (**Pamela gikk inn i kjøkkenet** — "went into the kitchen" from elsewhere) — i.e. the
long/short contrast tracks containment-of-motion, not just literal position-vs-direction, once a
preposition is added.

## Lesson 10 — Skolen i Norge

**Skipped as redundant:** coordinating vs. subordinating conjunctions and the basic subordinate-clause
word-order shift (n-a-v instead of v-n-a, no F column) — the underlying word-order mechanism is this
book's own invention (see Lesson 1 above) layered onto facts 002 already states in prose form.

**New — the present participle's restricted Norwegian functions.** Formed uniformly with **-ende** on
the stem (gå → gående, bite → bitende, berolige → beroligende) regardless of conjugation class — but
the book stresses that Norwegian uses this form far less than English's "-ing," reserving it to four
specific slots: (a) as a verb with **komme**/**bli** (**Der kommer de gående** "there they come,
walking"; **Han ble sittende i 3 timer** "he remained sitting"); (b) as an adverb (**Det er bitende
kaldt**); (c) as an ordinary adjective (**en smilende jente**); (d) as a noun (**Kun for gående!**
"Pedestrians only!"). This four-way restriction, and the fact that English "-ing" maps onto at least
seven *different* Norwegian strategies depending on context, is covered at book-length in Lesson 19
(see file 011b).

**New — short answers always introduced by det.** Where English just repeats the auxiliary ("Yes, I
have" / "Yes, I do"), Norwegian's short-answer pattern is uniformly **Ja/Nei/Jo, det + verb + subject**
— repeating **ha/være**/a modal verbatim (**Har du en øl? — Ja, det har jeg**), but substituting
**gjøre** ("do") for any other verb, tense-matched to the question (**Arbeidet du i hagen i går? —
Jo, det gjorde jeg**). The same **det gjør/gjorde + subject** substitution also answers **hvem**
("who") subject-questions (**Hvem betaler? — Det gjør jeg**).

**New — indirect speech via si...at / spørre...om, with a punctuation note.** Reporting a statement
uses **si** + **at**; reporting a question uses **spørre** + **om**; both are subordinate clauses, so
any negation in them follows subordinate-clause word order (before the finite verb, not after).
Norwegian direct speech is introduced with a **colon**, not a comma as in English — a small but
genuine orthographic-convention difference worth flagging for any synthesis work on quoted/reported
speech.

# Norwegian — Established Vocabulary/Usage: Literary Usage-Corpus Sample — *Jomfruen på Glassberget og andre eventyr* (Asbjørnsen og Moe)

**Source:** P. Chr. Asbjørnsen og Jørgen Moe, *Jomfruen på Glassberget og andre eventyr*, illustrated
by Th. Kittelsen, H. Aschehoug & Co. (W. Nygaard), Oslo, **10th printing (tiende opplag), 1958**.
Format: DJVU, 119 pages, no usable embedded text layer (`djvutxt` returned empty output) — every
sampled page was vision-read from rendered page images.

**Edition note (from the book's own colophon, p. [8]):** this is NOT a direct reprint of the
original 19th-century Asbjørnsen & Moe tale collection. It is a children's selection, *Barne-Eventyr*,
first compiled and **language/style-revised** by **Moltke Moe** in 1909 (per his own foreword, dated
November 1909, reproduced on p. [9]) from the foundational joint collection *Norske Folke-Eventyr
fortalt av P. Chr. Asbjørnsen og Jørgen Moe*. Moltke Moe's foreword states explicitly that he revised
"språk og stil" (language and style) throughout, per instructions both original authors had given
him, and that two of the fourteen tales ("Grisen og levemåten hans" and "Reve-enken") are his own,
not drawn from the original collection at all. The selection reached its 6th printing under the title
*Barne-Eventyr* in 1930; the 7th printing (1943) was re-revised and retitled *Jomfrua på Glassberget*
by **Jan Jørgen Alnæs**. This specific copy is the 10th printing (1958), a further "reviderte opplag"
(revised printing). **Implication for `Attested Era`:** the underlying tale material is 19th-century
Norwegian oral folktale tradition, but the actual wording on any given page has passed through at
least two documented 20th-century editorial language revisions (1909, 1943) before this 1958
printing — so era attribution below is necessarily coarse and marks both layers.

**Coverage note — literary usage-corpus sample, not exhaustive extraction.** This is Wave 4 of
Norwegian Phase 1: native-literature sampling, distinct from Waves 1–3's grammar/dictionary/
phrasebook extraction (established/001–017). Per `00_Reference_Extraction_Spec.md`, this file
captures genuinely interesting vocabulary, idiom, folk-narrative formulae, and register markers from
a deliberate sample — **not** a cover-to-cover read of all 119 pages. Sampled **9 of the collection's
14 tales**, spread front-to-back through the book (see page list below), 1–2 pages per tale,
17 page-images total.

### Morpheme breakdown (illustrative compounds)

Norwegian is predominantly an isolating/mildly-inflecting language with productive but optional
compounding (see `00_Word_Concept_and_Morphological_Typology_Guide.md` — most entries above don't
need a breakdown), but a handful of this sample's compounds have internal structure worth surfacing
explicitly:

> **avindsyk** = avind ("envy, malice" — archaic/literary noun, now largely confined to this kind of
> compound) + syk ("sick") — literally "envy-sick," i.e. consumed by envy; the free-standing noun
> "avind" barely survives outside this compound in modern usage.

> **tørrskranglete** = tørr ("dry") + skrangle- (related to "skranglete," "rickety/rattly," itself
> from skrangle "to rattle") + -ete (adjectival "full of / characterized by" suffix) — a doubled-up
> pejorative-physique compound, roughly "dry-and-rickety(-looking)."

> **langrumpete** = lang ("long") + rumpe ("butt/tail/rear") + -ete (adjectival suffix) — "long-
> rumped/long-tailed"; paired in the same sentence with the negating **rumpeløse** = rumpe + -løs
> ("-less") + -e (plural adj. agreement), "tailless," showing the same root productively taking
> opposite derivational suffixes in adjacent clauses.

> **jonsok(k)natt** = Jonsok (itself a fused/archaic compound: Jon, Norwegianized form of "John" +
> sok/våk, related to "vake," a wake/vigil — "St. John's wake") + natt ("night") — the whole compound
> names the pre-Christian/medieval Midsummer-eve folk festival, now mostly surviving in exactly this
> kind of fixed compound rather than as separable words.

> **dørgløtt** = dør ("door") + gløtt ("a narrow gap/glimpse," related to the verb "gløtte," to peek)
> — "door-gap," a small crack a person can peek or sneak through.

> **sorggiven** = sorg ("sorrow, grief") + -given (an archaic/literary adjectival formant seen in a
> small set of "X-given" compounds meaning "given over to X, overcome by X") — "sorrow-given,"
> i.e. overcome with grief; the "-given" formant itself doesn't productively combine with modern
> everyday nouns, marking this as a literary/archaic compound type rather than a live pattern.

## Grammar/register points

### Performance-instruction subtitles as a tale-opening convention
The very first tale, "Hanen og høna" (p.7), carries a printed subtitle directly instructing the
reader/performer: *"Lydene til hanen og høna må etterlignes"* ("The sounds of the rooster and hen
must be imitated"). This is not narrative prose at all but an explicit staging direction embedded in
the printed text — a reminder that these tales were compiled with oral/dramatic read-aloud
performance in mind, not silent reading alone. Worth flagging for any later synthesis work modeling
"literary register" as distinct from "performed/oral register," since this book's own front matter
(Moltke Moe's foreword, p. [9]) states the selection was explicitly ordered "fra små
lydetterlignende dyresamtaler for de aller minste" (from small sound-imitating animal dialogues for
the very youngest) up through more serious tales for older children — i.e. register here tracks
*reader age*, not just formality.

### The "rule of three" as a structural (not just lexical) formula
"Reve-enken" (p.105) opens its suitor sequence with an explicit triple-knock ("banket tre slag i
døra") and the surrounding fuller tale (not fully sampled here) follows the conventional three-
suitor pattern typical of this international tale type. Combined with "rubb og stubb" recurring
verbatim across two unrelated tales (p.66 and p.105), this sample shows formula-level repetition
operating at both the phrase level (fixed idioms reused across tales) and the structural level
(the "three" convention), which is a genre-defining feature of the whole European folktale tradition
this collection sits in — worth keeping in mind for any later derivation-mechanism work treating
folk-narrative formulae as a distinct productive category alongside ordinary lexical slang formation.

### Within-book dialect-form variation: "sjøl" vs. "selv," "kvit" vs. "hvit"
The sampled pages show the dialectal forms "sjøl" (for standard "selv," reflexive/intensive
pronoun — recurs throughout) and "kvit" (for standard "hvit," white — specifically in "kvitbjørn,"
"Kjetta på Dovre") existing *alongside* the standard bokmål forms elsewhere in the very same book
(e.g. "hvitt, sidt skjegg" in "De tre prinsessene i Hvittenland," p.92). Since the source itself never
explicitly labels these as regional/dialectal (no `[B]`/`[C]`/`[S]`-style tag or footnote), this
sample follows the spec's caution and leaves `Attested Region` as "—" for both rather than guessing a
specific dialect area — but the co-occurrence of both forms within one lightly-edited 1958 children's
text is itself a small, real data point about how porous the literary-standard/colloquial boundary
was (and arguably still is) in printed Norwegian, worth a note for any later register-mapping work.

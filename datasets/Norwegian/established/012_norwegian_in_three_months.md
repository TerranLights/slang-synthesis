## Grammar points

### Hugo's "imitated pronunciation" system (Introduction/Pronunciation, pp.8-16)
Distinct from 003's IPA treatment: the book renders Norwegian words as if each syllable were part of an
English word, with a colon (:) marking long vowels and an accent mark (´) after the stressed syllable
(used only in the first five chapters). Example: **en koffert** → *e:n koof'-fert*; **et pass** → *et
pahs*. The book frames this as a practical approximation for a reader working without a teacher, not a
scientific transcription — it is explicitly cruder than 003's IPA inventory, but is kept here as a
distinctive extraction-worthy pedagogical artifact.

### Stress, rhythm and tone (p.14-16)
Norwegian-origin words stress the first syllable (**gate**, **kaste**); loanwords often stress the last
(**dusin**, **tobakk**, **trykkeri**). Foreign-origin prefixes (**be-, er-, ge-**) are unstressed
(**betale**, **erklære**, **gevær**); prefixes that are mainly prepositions (**av, fra, mot, opp, ned**
etc.) usually carry the stress (**avgjøre**, **frata**, **motstand**, **nedtrykt**). Suffixes **-else,
-ing, -ling, -ig, -lig** etc. shift stress onto the syllable preceding the suffix (**tillatelse**,
**underholdning**, **alvorlig**). Sentence rhythm is typically trochaic (strong-weak-strong-weak) in
careful speech but varies in rapid speech. Tone is lexically contrastive on homophones: **løvet** "the
foliage" (single/falling-then-rising tone) vs. **løve** "lion" (double tone, falls then rises) — this
description applies to Eastern Norway; other regions have their own rising/falling intonation pattern
("a kind of singing intonation"), a brief dialectal note not itself detailed further by this source.

### Chapter 1 — Articles and nouns, Subject pronouns, Å være/Å ha (pp.17-28)
Base gender/article/plural paradigms overlap 001/002 and are skipped. New/deeper points only:
- The indefinite article is dropped in predicate-noun statements of profession/nationality/similar
  categories: **Han er rørlegger** ("He is [a] plumber"), **Han arbeider på fabrikk** ("He works in [a]
  factory").
- The feminine **-a** definite-singular ending (**lommebok → lommeboka**) is flagged as common in
  colloquial speech and obligatory in Nynorsk, alongside the standard **-en**.
- Several two-or-more-syllable feminine nouns take **-a** in the definite singular almost automatically
  regardless of register: **bygd(a)**, **hytte/hytta**, **jente/jenta**, **geit/geita**, **ku/kua**.
- Irregular plural + stem-vowel-change nouns collected: **tann→tenner**, **natt→netter**, **bok→bøker**,
  **bonde→bønder**, **ku→kyr/kuer**, **hånd→hender**, **far→fedre**, **mor→mødre**, **datter→døtre**,
  **tre→trær**, **mann→menn**.
- Neuter nouns of one syllable are usually unchanged in the plural (**bord, hus, lys, egg**); neuter
  nouns of two+ syllables are inconsistent — some stay unchanged, some add **-er** (**vindu→vinduer**,
  **fotografi→fotografier**).
- A colloquial/Nynorsk **-a** definite-plural alternative to **-ene** exists for neuter nouns
  (**hus→husa**), and some neuter nouns appear almost exclusively in the **-a** form: **et ben→bena**,
  **et barn→barna**.
- Sociolinguistic note on the formal pronoun **De**: becoming less common; the book specifically credits
  teenagers with driving the shift toward **du** even on first meeting a stranger.

### Chapter 2 — Genitive, Adjectives, Present tense, Questions (pp.29-42)
- The **-s** genitive (no apostrophe) is not restricted to animate/people nouns: **båtens eier**,
  **Norges fjorder**, **landets grenser**, **myndighetenes ansvar**.
- A colloquial periphrastic alternative to the **-s** genitive uses **til** or **av**: **hagen til
  naboen**, **læreren til gutten**, **moren til barnet**, **eieren av båten**.
- Compound nouns can replace the **-s** genitive outright: **båteieren** = **båtens eier**;
  **landegrensene** = **landets grenser**.
- Adjective agreement nuances beyond the base rule (no ending common sg. / -t neuter sg. / -e plural):
  adjectives in **-ig/-lig**, or **-t** preceded by another consonant, take **no** additional **-t** in
  the neuter singular (**et feilaktig svar**, **et farlig veikryss**, **et sort skjørt**); adjectives
  ending in a stressed vowel (**fri, blå**) add **-tt** in the neuter singular (**et fritt land**, **et
  blått slips**) but are inconsistent in the plural, some adding **-e** and some not (**frie land**, **blå
  slips**).
- **Double-definite construction**: in the colloquial/spoken language, a definite-form adjective+noun
  takes a definite article BOTH before the adjective (**den/det/de**) AND suffixed on the noun (**den
  store byen**, **det høye huset**, **de dyre bilene**) — especially with concrete nouns. In formal/
  written language, especially with abstract nouns, the suffixed article can be dropped (**den nye
  tid**, **det ufødte liv**, **de gode muligheter**). This genuine register-conditioned construction is
  not covered this explicitly in 001/002.
- When two infinitives occur in sequence, the second **å** is usually dropped: **å gi og (å) ta** "to
  give and (to) take."
- **ja vs. jo**: "ja" answers an affirmative question affirmatively; "jo" (parallel to French "si")
  answers a NEGATIVE question affirmatively — **Snakker de ikke norsk? Jo, de snakker norsk** = "Don't
  they speak Norwegian? Yes, they DO speak Norwegian." Not distinguished this way in 001/002.
- No Norwegian word corresponds to English "do/don't" in questions or negative sentences.

### Chapter 3 — Imperative, More questions, Negatives, Numbers 1-100 (pp.43-55)
- Negative imperative word order is flexible: **ikke** may go either immediately after or immediately
  before the imperative (**Kjøp ikke huset!** / **Ikke kjøp huset!**), both meaning "Don't buy the
  house!"
- A fuller collected question-word reference than 001 gives individually: **hvor?** (where), **når?**
  (when), **hvordan?** (how), **hvem?** (who/whom), **hvorfor?** (why), **hvilken/hvilket/hvilke?**
  (which/what, agreeing in gender/number), **hva?** (what), **hvor mange?** (how many), **hvor mye?**
  (how much).
- **Demonstratives, proximal/distal split** (sec.13): **denne/dette/disse** refer to something very
  near the speaker; **den/det/de** to something at a distance. **Her** can be added for emphasis on
  nearness, **der** for emphasis on distance (**denne bilen her** vs. **den bilen der**). Demonstratives
  can stand alone pronominally (**Denne bilen er min, men den tilhører min nabo**). English "one" (as in
  "this one") is not translated: **Jeg ønsker dette** = "I'd like this one." An adjective following a
  demonstrative + noun takes the **-e** form, and the noun normally appears in the definite form too
  (**denne nye dressen**, **dette store rommet**, **disse lange bordene**).
- **Two competing number-naming systems** (sec.14) — a genuine sociolinguistic/historical finding not in
  001-003: the **traditional** method (still used by the majority of Norwegians) names the ones before
  the tens, written as one word linked by **og**: **toogtredve** (32), **femogførti** (45). A **new
  official method**, introduced by decree of **1951** for schools and broadcasting, reverses the order:
  **trettito** (32), **førtifem** (45). (001's "archaic" framing of forms like **tyve/tretti/tredve**
  should be read alongside this book's more neutral "two coexisting systems" framing — a discrepancy
  worth flagging between sources.) Norwegians use a colon (:) rather than ÷ for division in written
  arithmetic. **Null** = zero.
- Negative pronoun paradigm: **aldri** (never), **ingen/ikke noen** (no one/nothing, common gender),
  **intet/ikke noe/ingenting** (nothing, neuter — this source does NOT flag **intet** as archaic, unlike
  001's register note on the same word), **ikke lenger** (no longer), **ikke mer** (no more).

### Chapter 4 — Possessive adjectives/pronouns, More adjectives, Comparison, Object pronouns, Time (pp.56-71)
- Full possessive-adjective paradigm: **min/mitt/mine** (my), **din/ditt/dine** (your, familiar),
  **Deres** (your, formal, invariable), **dens/dets** (its), **hans** (his), **hennes** (her),
  **vår/vårt/våre** (our), **deres** (their) — **min, din, vår** agree with the possessed noun's gender/
  number; the rest are invariable.
- **Postposed possessive**, very common colloquially: **bilen min** (= **min bil**), **huset ditt** (=
  **ditt hus**), **broren hennes** (= **hennes bror**), **naboene våre** (= **våre naboer**).
- **Reflexive sin/sitt/sine**, 3rd person only, replacing **hans/hennes/dens/dets/deres** specifically
  when referring back to the subject of the clause — explained with a crisper minimal-pair contrast than
  001's brief mention: **Hun kommer i sin bil/bilen sin** ("she comes in HER OWN car") vs. **Hennes bil
  er rød** ("HER [someone else's reference] car is red"); **Han tar sin bil/bilen sin** (his own) vs.
  **Han tar hans bil/bilen hans** (another person's). **Sin/sitt/sine** agree in gender/number with the
  possessed noun exactly like **min/din**: **Han selger sitt hus/huset sitt**; **De selger sine
  hus/husene sine**.
- Possessive adjectives/pronouns are **normally omitted** with body parts and clothing in context:
  **Han rister på hodet** ("He shakes HIS head," no **sitt**); **Du må ha tørre sokker på bena** ("dry
  socks on YOUR feet").
- "A friend of mine" is **en venn av meg** (lit. "a friend of me"), not a possessive-pronoun
  construction.
- **Feil** ("fault") is an irregular common-gender noun with **no distinct indefinite plural**: **feil**
  = "(some) faults," **feilene** = "the faults."
- Adjectives ending **-el/-en/-er** drop the **-e** before a plural ending: **enkel/enkelt/enkle**,
  **våken/våkent/våkne**, **mager/magert/magre**.
- Adjectives ending **-sk/-d/-s/-e** take **no -t** in the neuter singular (**et norsk skip**, **et
  fremmed språk**, **et felles anliggende**, **et fristende tilbud**) — but **friskt** (fresh) and
  **ferskt** (fresh/of bread) are flagged exceptions that DO take **-t** despite the consonant-cluster
  ending. Adjectives ending **-s** or **-e** take no additional plural ending (**felles
  anstrengelser**, **fristende tilbud**).
- Multi-syllable adjectives and verb participles used as adjectives form comparison periphrastically with
  **mer/mest** ("more/most"), like English, rather than by suffixation: **problematisk – mer
  problematisk – mest problematisk**; **spennende – mer spennende – mest spennende**; **fornøyd – mer
  fornøyd – mest fornøyd**.
- Full irregular-comparison list (more complete here than scattered mentions in 001/002): **god-bedre-
  best**, **dårlig-verre-verst**, **ung-yngre-yngst**, **gammel-eldre-eldst**, **liten-mindre-minst**,
  **stor-større-størst**, **få-færre-færrest**, **mange-flere-flest**, **lang-lengre-lengst**.
- **Reflexive object pronoun seg**, 3rd person only, replacing **ham/henne/den/det/dem** when referring
  back to the clause's subject: **Han ber sin venn om å gjemme seg** ("He asks his friend to hide
  [HIMSELF = the friend]") vs. **Han ber sin venn om å gjemme ham** ("...to hide HIM [someone else]").
- **Clock-time system anchored to the half-hour** (sec.20) — a major typological point not covered in
  001-003: Norwegian measures time TOWARD or AWAY FROM the half-hour landmark, not purely past/to the
  hour as English does. **Ti på halv elleve** (lit. "ten to half eleven") = 10:20; **halv elleve** =
  10:30; **fem over halv elleve** = 10:35; **kvart over ti** = 10:15; **kvart på elleve** = 10:45. Also:
  **klokken tolv middag/midnatt** (twelve noon/midnight), time-of-day qualifiers **om morgenen/om
  ettermiddagen/om kvelden (aftenen)**, and the abbreviation **kl** for "o'clock."

## Lesson 11 — Været

**Skipped as redundant:** none outright — this lesson's grammar (the many/few/much/little/more/most
system) is genuinely more systematic here than anywhere else so far in this project's Norwegian
coverage, so it is kept in full below rather than skipped.

**New — a four-way countable/uncountable comparison system.** Norwegian keeps the English-style
countable/uncountable split rigorous where English blurs it: **mange/få** (many/few) only with
countables; **mye/litt** (much/a little) only with uncountables; **færre/færrest** (fewer/fewest)
only with countables; **mindre/minst** (less/least) only with uncountables; and critically, where
English uses "more"/"most" for *both* categories, Norwegian splits them too — **flere** (more,
countable) vs. **mer** (more, uncountable), **flest** (most, countable) vs. **mest** (most,
uncountable). The *definite* form of **flest/mest** shifts meaning from a superlative comparison
("more than anyone/anything else": **Anders spiste flest plommer**) to a partitive "most of" reading
("most of a specific set": **Anders spiste de fleste av plommene som lå på bordet**) — the same
definite/indefinite superlative-meaning split documented for ordinary adjectives in 002, now shown to
extend to this closed irregular set too. **Færrest/flest** are also flagged as not taking the usual
plural **-e** ending that ordinary adjectives would.

**New — direct vs. indirect speech, person-pronoun shift (brief addition).** Builds on the si/spørre
distinction already logged in Lesson 10 (file 011): confirms the mechanism generalizes to third
verbs (**telegraferer...at**) and that any negation inside the reported clause follows subordinate
word order.

**New — position of unstressed object pronouns.** An unstressed pronoun object moves out of the
ordinary object slot (N) and in between **n** and **a** in this book's word-order grid — but *only*
when the non-finite-verb slot (V) is empty: **Jeg hørte det ikke** (pronoun fronted past the
negation) vs. **Jeg har ikke hørt det** (full V slot present, pronoun stays in its ordinary place).
This is a genuinely new syntactic rule for this project, refining the general word-order system
documented in Lesson 1 (file 011).

**New — for in exclamations, and the til + genitive fossil-phrase set.** **For** + indefinite noun
phrase forms an exclamation ("What a...!"): **For et tordenvær!**, **For noen gaver!** — distinct
from its ordinary "for" sense. A closed set of fixed phrases (**til sengs** "to bed," **til bords**
"at table," **til fjells** "to the mountains," **til topps** "to the top," **til sjøs** "to sea,"
**til lands** "ashore," **nå til dags** "nowadays," **til dels** "partly") preserves a genitive
construction after **til** that is otherwise extinct in Modern Norwegian — the book explicitly frames
these as "remnants from Old Norse, when the preposition til was followed by a genitive," i.e. a small
closed class of fossilized case-marking, directly relevant to any conlang-synthesis work on how a
language can retain archaic morphology only inside idiom.

**New — telling the time, the forward half-hour.** Not previously documented for this language in
this project: Norwegian's half-hour works *forwards* toward the next hour, not backward from the
current one — **halv ti** means "half past nine" (literally "half [of the way to] ten"), the mirror
image of the English "half ten" = half past ten trap the book explicitly warns English speakers
against. For minutes between :20 and :40 Norwegians normally reference the half-hour rather than the
hour. **Klokken** ("the clock," used for "o'clock"/"at") is used for the hour itself but — unlike
English "at" — is dropped when a non-full-hour time is given (**Vi kommer klokken 4** but **Vi kommer
10 over 4**, no klokken). No native a.m./p.m. marking exists; **om formiddagen/ettermiddagen**
("in the morning/afternoon") substitute when needed, and the 24-hour clock is standard for
radio/TV/transport.

## Lesson 12 — Togreiser

**Skipped as redundant:** the relative pronoun **som** (002's treatment is more systematic) and the
synthetic/periphrastic adjective-comparison split (**-ere/-est** vs. **mer/mest**) — 002 already
covers both the regular pattern and most of the irregular/suppletive list.

**New — the aller/nest superlative-intensifier pair.** Not previously logged: **aller** ("of all")
intensifies an existing superlative to an absolute maximum (**den aller eldste byen i Norge** "the
*very* oldest city"), while **nest** marks "second-most" (**den nest største byen** "the
second-largest city") — a small but systematic two-word modifier pair layered on top of the ordinary
superlative.

**New — similarity/dissimilarity vocabulary as its own functional set.** **like + adjective + som**
("as X as"), **ikke like...som**, **annerledes** ("different"), **(u)like** ("(not) alike") and
**likne** ("resemble") form a small closed functional vocabulary for comparison-of-equality, distinct
from ordinary comparative/superlative morphology — not previously catalogued as a set for this
language.

## Lesson 13 — I byen

**Skipped as redundant:** the three present-passive forms (restated from Lesson 9) and the
demonstrative pronouns denne/dette/disse (002's Ready-reference table covers these).

**New — compound-noun linking-morpheme rules, cross-checked against 002.** This book gives a
*simpler* three-way version (plain concatenation / **-s-** link / **-e-** link, gender from the final
element: **passasjer+båt=passasjerbåt**, **handel+s+mann=handelsmann**, **fisk+e+torg=fisketorg**) of
the same system 002 documents in much finer environmental detail (five formation types including
adjective/verb/preposition/adverb + noun, with the specific phonological triggers for each linking
consonant). No new fact here, confirmed as a strict subset of 002's coverage — kept only as a
one-line cross-reference, not re-derived.

**New — i gata/på torget/på veien as a genuinely lexically-idiosyncratic preposition set.** The
book flags that the preposition choice for "in/at/on" a specific kind of urban location is not
predictable by rule and must be learned per noun: **i gata** ("in the street"), **på torget** ("in
the square/marketplace"), **på veien** ("on the road") — but **i Parkveien 6** ("at [house number]
Parkveien") when the "veien" is itself a proper street *name* rather than the generic noun. A small
but genuine lexical-preposition-government fact not previously logged.

## Lesson 14 — Turister

**Skipped as redundant:** none — this lesson's grammar is genuinely new material throughout.

**New — adverbs of manner from adjectives (-t suffix), with amplifier use.** Regularly formed by
adding **-t** to the adjective (fin → fint, lang → langt) — except adjectives already ending in
**-ig**, which take no suffix (utrolig → utrolig, unchanged). These manner-adverbs double as
amplifiers directly preceding another adjective (**et utrolig vakkert hus** "an unbelievably
beautiful house") — the same word filling both the ordinary adverb-of-manner slot (column A in this
book's word-order grid) and an intensifier slot.

**New — question tags via ikke sant / vel, not auxiliary-repetition.** Where English repeats (part
of) the auxiliary in a tag question ("...isn't she?", "...have they?"), Norwegian uses a fixed
particle keyed to question polarity: **ikke sant** tags a *positive* statement ("...aren't you?"),
**vel** tags a statement with a *negative* component ("...are you?" after a "not..." statement). The
full verb-and-subject can also be repeated as an alternative tag (**Du skal til byen, skal du
ikke?**).

**New — bare adjectives standing in for an omitted, contextually obvious noun.** **Den lille** ("the
small one") — Norwegians freely drop the noun when it is self-evident from context, leaving the
adjective (inflected as if the noun were still there) to stand alone as the full noun phrase.

## Lesson 15 — Sykdom og sunnhet

**Skipped as redundant:** none — this lesson's three-way "think" split and future-tense survey are
new.

**New — three Norwegian verbs for English "think," by sense.** **Tenke** = exercise the mind
("think" as mental activity: *Du må lære å tenke*); **tro** or **mene** = "believe" (*Jeg tror Anders
er syk*); **synes** = hold an opinion (*Det synes jeg er en god idé*) — confirmed as a genuinely
three-way split not collapsed anywhere else in this project's Norwegian material so far.

**New — three strategies for future time, by context.** (a) Simple present when future-ness is clear
from context (*Jeg leser for ham når dere er gått*); (b) **skal/vil** + bare infinitive, the ordinary
modal-auxiliary future; (c) **kommer til å** + bare infinitive — confirmed independently flagged as a
*colloquial* alternative to formal **vil** in `002_practical_norwegian_grammar.md` (p. 24), and here
given as simply the third interchangeable option without that register flag repeated — see the
register-notes file for 002's explicit colloquial framing of this construction.

**New — noen/noe, the countable/uncountable and polarity-conditioned "some/any" system.** **Noen**
covers plural "some/any" across genders and singular masculine/feminine countables in
questions/negatives (*Vi har ikke noen reine lommetørklær*); standing alone it means "somebody/
anybody" (*Traff du noen?*). **Noe** covers uncountables regardless of gender and singular neuter
countables in questions/negatives (*Har dere noe hostesaft?*); standing alone it means "something/
anything" (*Er det noe annet jeg kan gjøre?*). This gender/countability-conditioned split is revisited
and extended with the **ingen/ikke noen/ikke noe** negative-polarity system in Lesson 20 below.

## Lesson 16 — Utseende

**Skipped as redundant:** none.

**New — the full appearance/dressing phrasal-verb set.** **Se ut** ("look," + adjective: *Hun ser
trist ut*), **ha på seg** ("be wearing," stative) vs. **ta på seg** ("put on," dynamic) vs. **ta av
seg** ("take off") — the same stative/dynamic-pair logic already seen for the nine locational adverbs
in Lesson 9 (file 011), now shown to extend to clothing verbs; plus **kle på seg/kle seg ut/kle av
seg** ("dress/dress up/undress") as a minimal compound-verb triplet differentiated only by the
following particle.

**New — a four-way system for "ago/in/for/since" time expressions, keyed to direction and reference
point.** **For...siden** ("ago," counting back from now); **om** (counting forward: "in ten minutes");
**i** (pointing to a specific past time: "in 1988," or a duration "for many years"); **på** (the
window within which something is completed: *Du kan ikke koke poteter på ti minutter!* — "in [under]
ten minutes"); **siden** (a different sense — "since" a specific date/event); **på** again (pointing
to a specific future/past day: *De kommer på søndag*). The two **på**-uses (bounding duration vs.
pinpointing a day) and the two **i**-uses (pinpointing a past time vs. marking a duration) are flagged
as genuinely distinct senses of the same preposition, not free variation.

**New — hel/selv/all/alle/begge, five "totality" adjectives that resist the ordinary definite-article
rule.** All five behave unlike ordinary adjectives in one specific way: when modifying a definite
noun, **no separate definite-article word precedes them** (contrast ordinary adjectives, Lesson 5,
file 011). **Hel/helt/hele** ("all/the whole") only with countables (*hele dagen*, but *i hele dag*
with the noun itself reverting to indefinite form inside a preposition phrase). **Selv/selve**
("himself/itself") — crucially, **selve** *before* the noun means "the X itself" (*Selve skålen gikk
ikke i stykker*) while **selv** *before* the noun instead means "even" (*Selv skålen gikk i
stykker* — "Even the bowl broke"), a genuine ambiguity resolved only by word order, not by form.
**All/alt** with uncountables, **alle** with countables (3+), **begge** (invariant) specifically for
pairs — all four can stand alone when the referent noun is contextually obvious.

**New — the five core sentence-modifying adverbs vel/jo/da/nok/nå, functionally glossed.** The book
explicitly calls these "extremely frequent in everyday spoken Norwegian, usually unstressed, and
genuinely hard to translate." **Vel** + question intonation converts a statement into a tag-question
(*Du kommer vel til selskapet?* — "...won't you?"). **Jo** appeals to shared/already-known
information ("...you know," *Du sa jo at...*). **Da** can mark impatience in an imperative (*Vent på
meg da!*) or — separately, per the register-notes file — substitute informally for "please." **Nok**
marks presumption ("presumably," *Han forstår nok...*). **Nå** softens an assertion ("I wouldn't
exactly say..."). These stack (*Jeg kan nok dessverre ikke komme*) with lighter ones preferring the
earlier slot and **ikke/også** obligatorily last. This set is a genuine addition to 002's much
briefer da/nok/jo mention — the full five-adverb functional gloss, and the stacking-order rule, are
new to this project.

## Lesson 17 — Fritiden

**Skipped as redundant:** none.

**New — hvem/hva/hvilken interrogatives, including the no-single-word-for-"whose" gap.** **Hvem**
covers both "who" and "whom." There is **no single Norwegian word for "whose"** — the gap is filled
with **hvem sin/si/sitt/sine...?** or **hvem eier...?** ("who owns...?"). **Hvilken/hvilket/hvilke**
("which") has a colloquial substitute pattern, **hva for en/et/noen**, listed as the form heard "in
everyday speech" instead of the hvilken-series (see the register-notes file). When **hvem/hva**
function as subject of a subordinate clause, **som** must be inserted after them; as object, nothing
is added — a small but genuine syntactic wrinkle not previously logged.

**New — three verbs for "to know," by sense; two nouns for "time," by sense.** **Kjenne** = be
acquainted with a person/place (*Kjenner du ham?*), also **bli kjent med** ("get to know") and
**kjenne til** ("know of/be aware of") as two further derived senses, plus a fourth, unrelated sense
"feel" (*Jeg kjenner meg bedre nå*). **Vite** = have factual knowledge. **Kunne** = know (a skill or
memorized content: *Kan du leksene dine?*, *Kan du russisk?*). Separately: **tid** (uncountable
"time," or countable "an era/period"), **gang** (countable "an occasion/instance": *flere ganger*
"several times"), and **time** specifically "hour" — three nouns covering what English's single
"time" spreads across.

**New — the past passive, confirmed rarer and more restricted than the present passive.** Of the
three present-passive strategies (Lesson 9/13), only the **bli**- and **være**-past-passives are in
live productive use (**Bilen ble vasket i går** for the action, **Bilen var ikke vasket da...** for
the resulting state); the past **s**-passive is explicitly flagged as "very rare," possible with only
a handful of verbs (**Et skrik hørtes** — "A scream was heard"). Also: Norwegian distinguishes
**ble/er født** ("was/is born," of someone currently living) from **var født** (of someone already
dead) — a tense choice keyed to the referent's life status, not ordinary tense semantics.

**New — da vs. når for "when," by single-event-vs-habitual and tense.** **Da** = a single event in
the past. **Når** = a single event in the *future*, or — separately — any habitual/repeated "whenever"
sense regardless of tense (*Når morfar kom hjem, var han alltid full* — "whenever grandfather came
home" = habitual past, still **når** not **da**). This is a genuinely binary, easy-to-get-wrong
distinction the book flags as "essential."

**New — the u- negating prefix, and a compound-hyphen convention.** **U-** forms opposites
(trolig/utrolig, mulig/umulig, ærlig/uærlig, avhengig/uavhengig) — covering the semantic range of
English dis-/un-/in-/im- in one single productive prefix. Separately, when multiple compounds share
their final element, a hyphen can stand in for the shared part in every compound but the last:
**fiskeri-, sjøfarts-, vannkraft-, industri- og teknologimuseer**.

## Lesson 18 — Litt norsk historie

**Skipped as redundant:** the reflexive possessive **sin/si/sitt/sine** itself (002 and Lesson 5,
file 011, already cover the paradigm) — but this lesson's worked subject-vs.-non-subject minimal
triples are kept below since they sharpen the rule with new examples not used elsewhere.

**New — non-passive verbs that are obligatorily -s-marked, a small closed lexical class.** **Synes**
("think/seem," already logged structurally in Lesson 15) is only one member of a small set of verbs
that *always* carry **-s** in both present and past, despite having active (not passive) meaning:
**lykkes/lyktes** ("succeed"), **trives/trivdes** ("be happy/thrive"), **finnes/fantes** ("exist"),
**kjennes/kjentes** ("feel [intransitive]"), **høres/hørtes** ("sound like"). A related but distinct
phenomenon: **-s** added to a handful of ordinary verbs specifically to mark *reciprocity* where
English just uses a plain active form — **Vi ses** ("We'll see each other"), **De møtes/treffes ofte**
("They often meet"), **De to familiene omgås ikke** ("The two families don't mix/associate").

**New — sin/si/sitt/sine vs. hans/hennes/deres, three worked minimal triples.** (1) **Han reiste til
Grønland sammen med familien sin** ("his own family," reflexive, refers back to the clause's own
subject) vs. **...familien hans** ("someone else's family"). (2) The same contrast for a neuter noun
(**skipet sitt** vs. **skipet hans**). (3) Critically, **sin** can never appear as part of the
clause's own subject, because it has nothing to refer back to yet — so "his ship sailed westwards"
with "ship" as subject is obligatorily **Skipet hans seilte vestover**, never *skipet sitt*. Also:
**sin** extends to third-person-generic words beyond ordinary pronouns — **man, en, folk, ingen**
(*Man må være høflig overfor foreldrene sine* — "One must be polite to one's own parents").

## Lesson 19 — Norske aviser

**Skipped as redundant:** none.

**New — a seven-strategy taxonomy for translating English "-ing" into Norwegian.** Norwegian has no
single equivalent to the English "-ing" form, and the book works through seven distinct translation
strategies depending on function: (1) **the infinitive**, as subject, object, or after a
preposition — including a notable exception after verbs of seeing/hearing, where Norwegian also uses
the bare infinitive rather than a participle (*Jeg så ham løpe ned gata* — "I saw him running down
the street," literally "...run..."); (2) **the continuous aspect itself has no dedicated form** and is
instead rendered by (a) ordinary simple tenses, (b) **holder på (med) å** / **driver og** + infinitive
("is in the process of..."), or (c) **sitter/ligger/står og** + another verb (*Hun sitter og skriver
et brev* — "she is [sitting] writing a letter"); (3) a prepositional phrase (*Jeg brukte fem timer på
å forberede middagen*); (4) the Norwegian **present participle** (-ende) when "-ing" is functioning
adjectivally (*Det er veldig beroligende*); (5) turning the "-ing" phrase into a plain noun or whole
phrase (*Jeg elsker fjellklatring* or *å klatre i fjell* — "I love mountaineering"); (6) restructuring
into a full subordinate clause (*Da jeg hadde hørt nyhetene, ringte jeg straks* — "Having heard the
news, I rang straight away"); (7) total sentence transformation with no structural correspondence at
all (*Vi får gjester i kveld* for "We've got people coming this evening"). This is a genuinely rich,
newly-documented semantic-mapping resource for any conlang work on how a language without a dedicated
continuous-aspect marker distributes that functional load across other mechanisms.

**New — possessive adjectives are dropped when ownership is self-evident from context.** Parallel to
(but distinct from) the bare-adjective-standing-alone fact in Lesson 14: **Jeg tok av meg klærne**
("I took off my clothes," no possessive — "the" clothes, reflexive-marked instead), **Han ristet på
hodet** ("He shook his head"), **Han har brukket beinet** ("He has broken his leg") — the possessor is
obvious from the reflexive/definite-article construction itself, so Norwegian omits the possessive
English would require.

**New — direct/indirect object ordering is fixed and opposite to English's flexibility.** Where
English can order either object around "to" (*gave Pamela a car* / *gave a car to Pamela*), Norwegian
requires **indirect object before direct object**, always, with no exception shown
(**Din mor har sendt deg en fødselsdagsgave**). When the direct object is an unstressed pronoun and
slides forward (the Lesson 11 rule, file 011, this file above), an accompanying pronoun indirect
object moves with it; a pronoun indirect object alone can also move forward on its own even when the
direct object is a full noun phrase that stays put.

## Lesson 20 — Livet i Norge

**Skipped as redundant:** none.

**New — a closed comparative/superlative-only adjective set with no base form.** **Bakre/bakest**
("rear/hindmost"), **indre/innerst** ("inner/innermost"), **midtre/midterst** ("middle"),
**nedre/nederst** ("lower/bottom"), **ytre/ytterst** ("outer/outermost"), **øvre/øverst**
("upper/top"), plus a handful with only a superlative (**forrest, fremst, mellomst, underst**) — none
of these roots has an ordinary unmarked/positive adjective form at all, paralleling (and confirming,
from a second independent source — compare 002's note on the directional-adverb subclass with the
same gap) the same "missing positive degree" phenomenon already logged for spatial adverbs like
**bak** in 002.

**New — the full ingen/ikke noen/ikke noe negative-determiner system, clause-type conditioned.** In a
main clause with a *single* finite verb, **ingen/ikke noen** (common/feminine singular and all
plurals) and **ikke noe** (neuter singular) are freely interchangeable (**5% av norske familier har
ikke noen telefon** = **...har ingen telefon**); standing alone, **ingen/ikke noen** = "no one" and
**ikke noe/ingenting** = "nothing." In a main clause with *more than one* verb, **ingen/ingenting**
become unavailable — only **ikke (noen)/ikke (noe)** work (with noen/noe droppable before a following
noun). In a *subordinate* clause, **ingen/ingenting** are usable only when they are themselves the
subject of that clause; otherwise **ikke noen/ikke noe** must be used instead. This three-way,
clause-structure-conditioned negative-determiner system had not previously been documented for this
language in this project and is a genuinely rich piece of new grammar.

**New — fractions, and the halvannen "one and a half" fossil form.** Fractions above a half/quarter
are compounds of cardinal + **del** (en tidel, to tideler), or — for denominators under 13 — an
ordinal substitutes (en femtendel). **Halvannen/halvannet** ("one and a half," literally "half of the
second [one]") and the parallel **X og en halv** pattern (to og en halv time) both take a *singular*
following noun despite the "1.5" meaning — a small irregularity already flagged once in 001 for the
numeral itself, confirmed here as generalizing to the whole "N and a half" pattern.

**New — å ha vs. å få, a "possess" vs. "come to possess" split, with further idiomatic extensions.**
Where English "to have" covers both "be in possession of" (**ha**: *Norge har en grunnlov*) and
"come into possession of/receive" (**få**: *Pamela fikk et brev*), Norwegian keeps the two senses
lexically distinct — and "to have" food/drink translates as the relevant verb itself (**spise**,
**drikke**), not **ha**. **Få** additionally appears in a cluster of idioms: **få vite** ("be told"),
**få smake/høre/se** ("get to taste/hear/see"), **få (noen) til å** ("make someone [do]"), **få øye
på** ("catch sight of"), **få til å** ("manage/achieve"), **få tak i** ("get hold of"); and as an
auxiliary, **få** + past participle means either "manage to" (*Jeg fikk lest hele pensum*) or "have
something done" (*Jeg må få reparert taket*) depending on the matrix verb's own semantics.

## Ready-reference grammar, pronunciation guide (printed pp. 206–214): confirmed fully redundant

The book's own back-matter **Ready-reference grammar** (p. 210–214) is explicitly described by the
book itself as "in no way intended to be a complete grammar... [it] merely presents summaries of
points that are otherwise dealt with at different stages of the book" (p. 3) — checked against the
Lesson-by-Lesson grammar points above and against 001/002/003, and confirmed to introduce **no new
fact**: it re-tabulates gender/article forms, noun declension, adjective declension/comparison,
personal/possessive/demonstrative/interrogative/relative pronouns, the indeterminate-pronoun set
(annen/ingen/noen/mang en), the four weak-verb groups plus the modal-verb paradigm, and a lesson-index
pointer for word order. Nothing here is written up separately.

The **simple guide to pronunciation** (p. 206–209) is likewise a condensed teaching summary of the
same vowel/consonant/tone-system facts `003_phonology_of_norwegian.md` documents in full academic
depth (long/short vowel-plus-consonant-length complementarity, the retroflex/palatalization-driven
digraph spellings **kj/skj/sj**, silent **d/g/h/t**, and the accent-1/accent-2 tonal contrast,
illustrated here with the minimal pair **bæret** "the berry" vs. **bære** "carry"). No fact in this
section goes beyond 003's coverage; not written up separately.

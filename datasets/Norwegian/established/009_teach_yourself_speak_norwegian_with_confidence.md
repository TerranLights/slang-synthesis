# Norwegian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Teach Yourself Speak Norwegian with Confidence (full book)

**Source:** Margaretha Danbolt Simons, *Speak Norwegian with Confidence* (Teach Yourself series), Hodder Education / McGraw-Hill, this edition published 2010 (© 2006, 2010 Margaretha Danbolt Simons; first published in UK 2006 as *Teach Yourself Norwegian conversation*). 68 PDF pages, confirmed no usable text layer (`pdftotext` returns only a few stray characters per page, plus a repeating owner/digital watermark string — see below). Vision-read in full at 150 DPI. An audio-course (3 CDs) companion booklet: Introduction, three "Only got a minute/five/ten minutes?" orientation essays, 10 numbered two-part conversations (CD1/CD2, following journalist Olav as he meets Kari and Eva in Oslo), a second, denser 10-part conversation cycle (CD3, "Conversation 3/1"–"3/10"), a "Listening skills: survival phrases" page, a Norwegian–English glossary, an English–Norwegian glossary, an "Essential vocabulary" page (numbers, months, days, seasons), a short Grammar reference, a Cultural information essay, and a Subject Index. Final page is a generic Teach Yourself series CEFR-level chart, not Norwegian-specific content.

**Coverage note:** Extracted in full. Every conversation's new vocabulary and every grammar "Insight" box were captured from the CD1–CD3 dialogue sections; the full Norwegian–English glossary (pp. 45–49) was used as the canonical cross-checked vocabulary list (it conveniently already tags gender — `c`/`n`/`p` — and the conversation number where each word first appears); the English–Norwegian glossary (pp. 50–54) was skimmed for entries not already present in the N–E list (a handful of genuinely new items turned up: `finne`, `lett`, `apotek`, `sted`, `lang`, `sete`, `resepsjon`, `teater`, `noe`, `tog`, `hvilken`) rather than re-extracted wholesale, since it is otherwise the same lexical set in reverse. The "Essential vocabulary" page's full number/month/day/season paradigms were extracted since they are genuinely systematic new vocabulary. The Cultural information essay (history, language, customs, food) and the Subject Index were read but are prose/navigation, not vocabulary — summarized briefly as grammar/register points instead of tabulated. Repeated English-translation facing pages (every Norwegian conversation page is followed by a plain English translation page) were used only to confirm glosses, never extracted as separate content.

**Marginalia/watermark note:** No handwritten pencil marginalia (underlines, filled-in answers, etc.) was found anywhere in this copy. However, every single page carries a printed/stamped name, **"Padraig"**, centered at the bottom margin, in a font distinct from the book's own typeset body and running head/footer — this is what `pdftotext` was picking up as stray text. It is treated as an ownership stamp/digital watermark added after original publication, not book content, and is excluded from extraction. Similarly, the half-title page (PDF p. 2) carries a faint ink date stamp, "3 AUG 2010" (library/acquisition stamp), also excluded as non-book content.

**Page-numbering note:** The book's front matter has a real, empirically-verified non-trivial numbering scheme worth flagging for any future re-reader: the author's prose Introduction is numbered arabic "1" (PDF p. 4) and sits *before* the roman-numeral-paginated Contents (p. v, PDF p. 5) and Track listing (p. vii, PDF p. 7); the "Only got a minute/five/ten minutes?" essays then resume arabic numbering at "2" (PDF p. 8) immediately after the roman-numbered Track listing page. From PDF p. 8 onward (printed p. 2) pagination is monotonic arabic through to the Subject Index (printed p. 61, PDF p. 67). This was verified empirically by reading each page's own printed folio rather than assuming a fixed PDF-page-to-printed-page offset, per the extraction spec's page-order-verification guidance.

This book teaches **Bokmål** throughout (explicitly, p. 59 of the Cultural information essay: "Bokmål is used by more than 90 per cent of the population... Nynorsk is a language made up of dialects, mainly from the west coast of Norway, and is thought by some to be the 'true' Norwegian... declined steadily over the past 50 years"). This is a book-wide, not per-word, regional annotation, so it is not repeated on every vocabulary row (per the extraction spec's column rule against inferring per-word regionality from book-level framing) — see the Grammar points section instead.

## Grammar points

### No "to do" auxiliary in questions or negatives

Norwegian has no equivalent of English's dummy "do/does/did." A yes/no question is formed purely by subject-verb inversion: **Bor du i Oslo?** is literally "Live you in Oslo?" = "Do you live in Oslo?" Negation is formed by inserting **ikke** after the finite verb, not by any auxiliary: **Jeg bor ikke i Oslo** is literally "I live not in Oslo" = "I don't live in Oslo." (p.8–9)

### Indefinite article dropped after "to have" and in some fixed quantity phrases

Where English inserts "a/an," Norwegian frequently drops it after **ha** ("to have"): **jeg har bil** = "I have (a) car," not **jeg har en bil** in casual speech (though the latter is also grammatical). The same dropping shows up in set phrases for ordering food/drink: **en kopp kaffe** ("a cup of coffee") and **et glass vin** ("a glass of wine") both omit the Norwegian equivalent of "of" that English requires (p.8–9, 20).

### Two grammatical genders: common and neuter

Every Norwegian noun is either common gender or neuter. The indefinite article is **en** for common-gender nouns and **et** for neuter nouns: **en avis** ("a newspaper," common) vs. **et kart** ("a map," neuter). Gender cannot be predicted from a noun's meaning or appearance — it must be memorized per word, which the glossary enforces by tagging every entry `c` (common) or `n` (neuter) (p.14, 56).

### Definite and plural noun formation

The definite article is suffixed, not a separate word: **en bil** → **bilen** ("the car"); **et hus** → **huset** ("the house"). Indefinite plurals add **-er** for common-gender nouns (**biler** — "cars") but take no ending for many neuter nouns (**hus** — "houses"), except polysyllabic neuters, which still take **-er** (**et hotell** → **hoteller**). Definite plurals use the same suffix, **-ene**, regardless of gender: **bilene**, **husene** (p.56).

### Adjective agreement

Adjectives agree with the noun they modify in three ways, illustrated by the source's own paradigm **en stor bil – den store bilen – store biler – de store bilene** and **et stort hus – det store huset – store hus – de store husene**: (1) an indefinite singular neuter noun's adjective takes **-t** (**stort**); (2) a definite noun phrase requires *both* the definite article word (**den/det/de**) *and* the noun's own suffixed article, but only when an adjective is present; (3) the adjective takes **-e** in the definite singular and in all plurals (**store**) (p.56–57).

### Personal pronouns: subject, object, and three-way possessive agreement

The book tabulates a full personal pronoun paradigm: subject forms (**jeg, du, han, hun, den, det, vi, dere, de**), object forms (**meg, deg, ham, henne, den, det, oss, dere, dem**), and possessives, which — unlike the pronouns themselves — inflect for the gender/number of the *possessed* noun, not the possessor: **min/mitt/mine** ("my/mine"), **din/ditt/dine**, **vår/vårt/våre**, etc. (p.57)

### Possessive position and definiteness

The possessive can go either before or after the noun, but the noun's own form must change to match: before the possessive, the noun stays indefinite (**min sønn** — "my son"); after the possessive, the noun takes the definite suffix (**sønnen min**, literally "the son of mine") (p.30).

### Reflexive "feel" construction

Where English says "I feel ill," Norwegian is reflexive: **jeg føler meg syk**, literally "I feel myself ill." The book's own Insight box flags this as something that "sounds funny in English, but... is perfectly normal" in Norwegian (p.16).

### Verb infinitive marker and present tense

The infinitive marker **å** precedes the bare verb stem (**å snakke** — "to talk"); in running glossary entries it is dropped and the infinitive stem alone is cited. The present tense is formed, with very few exceptions, by adding **-r** to the stem: **jeg/du snakker** ("I/you talk"). The verb "to be" is irregular: **å være** (infinitive) — **jeg er** (present) — **jeg var** (past) (p.57).

### Preposition polysemy

The book explicitly flags that Norwegian prepositions are not one-to-one with English ones: **i** can mean "in" (**Olav bor i Bergen** — "Olav lives in Bergen") but also appears in fixed time expressions like **i dag** ("today") and **i morgen** ("tomorrow"), where it does not mean "in" at all. **med** generally means "with," but "travelling by car" is **med bil**, using "with" where English uses "by" (p.34).

### The half-hour clock: "halv" means half-PAST, not half-TO

A specifically flagged trap for English speakers: **halv åtte** does not mean "half past eight" as the literal English cognate "half eight" would suggest — it means "half TO eight," i.e. 7:30. The 24-hour clock is used in writing/timetables, but Norwegians speaking casually use this half-to-the-next-hour convention with the 12-hour clock (p.32; confirmed by the parallel English translation on p.33, "half past seven," and again in Conv. 10 Part 2, where **klokken halv åtte** is independently translated "half past seven," p.35).

### Explicit register note: "hei" vs. "God dag"

The "Listening skills: survival phrases" page is the single clearest register-tagged content in the book: it glosses **Hei** simply as "Hi/Hello" but explicitly marks **God dag** as "Good day — more formal" (p.44). This is the book's own explicit formal/informal distinction, not an inference — **hei** and **hei hei** are used throughout every conversation as the default casual greeting/leave-taking between young adults, while **God dag** never actually appears in any of the 20 dialogue exchanges, suggesting it is taught as background/receptive formal vocabulary rather than active conversational vocabulary for this book's target social register.

### Bokmål vs. Nynorsk (explicit regional/register distinction)

Both the "Only got ten minutes?" orientation essay and the Cultural information essay explicitly address Norway's two official written language standards: **Bokmål**, described as the "main Norwegian language," used by "almost/more than 90%" of the population and developed from Danish (the official language in Norway until 1814); and **Nynorsk** ("new Norwegian"), "really a collection of dialects," "mainly from the west coast of Norway," officialized "more than 100 years ago," regarded by some as the "'true' Norwegian," but in decline over the past 50 years (p.6, 59). The entire course — all conversations, the glossary, and the grammar reference — is written in Bokmål; Nynorsk forms are discussed but never taught or exemplified.

### Cultural/historical framing (brief)

The Cultural information essay (p.58–60) sketches Norway's political history (loss of independence to Denmark in 1387, over 400 years as a Danish province, union with Sweden from 1814, full independence declared 17 May 1814 then re-secured in 1905 under King Haakon VII), the 17 May National Day celebrations (school-children's parade, regional **bunad** costume, brass bands, speeches), the **skål**-toasting ritual (eye contact required), and Norwegian food customs (reindeer, whale, stockfish/klippfisk, flatbread, and — per the book's own wry observation — Norway's recent status as a top per-capita pizza-consuming nation in Europe). This content is prose/background rather than tabulated vocabulary, and is paraphrased here rather than quoted.

# Norwegian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Teach Yourself Norwegian Conversation (full booklet)

**Source:** Margaretha Danbolt Simons, *Teach Yourself Norwegian Conversation*, Hodder Education (UK) / The McGraw-Hill Companies (US), "teach yourself" series, 2006 (© 2006 Margaretha Danbolt Simons). Companion booklet to a 3-CD audio conversation course (recorded at Alchemy Studios, London; cast: Camilla Laxton, Bjorn Odegard, Agnete Samdahl, Sarah Sherborne, Odin Tikander). 20 PDF pages = printed pages i–35 (cover, track listing, contents, 10 two-part conversations, a "listening skills: survival phrases" list, a full Norwegian–English and English–Norwegian glossary, a numbers/months/days/seasons reference, a grammar reference, and a cultural-information essay). Vision-read in full — confirmed no text layer (`pdftotext` returns empty output). Every PDF page is a two-printed-page spread; printed page numbers verified empirically and increase monotonically throughout — no non-monotonic ordering found.

**Coverage note:** Extracted in full across all 20 PDF pages (36 printed pages worth of content, i–35). The ten numbered conversations are built almost entirely from short, high-frequency compositional sentences reusing the same core vocabulary (greetings, "jeg vil gjerne ha..."/"jeg vil heller ha..." request patterns, yes/no question inversion) — per the extraction spec's copyright discipline, these are not reproduced as dialogue blocks; instead the vocabulary table below captures the book's own alphabetized Norwegian–English glossary (pp. 23–26) in full — every distinct lexical item it lists, each tagged with the conversation number the book itself cites for first use — plus the handful of items present only in the English–Norwegian glossary (pp. 27–30), the numbers/months/days/seasons reference (p. 31, consolidated into closed-set rows per the convention used in `006_berlitz_norwegian_phrase_dictionary.md`), and a few genuinely idiomatic phrases (`Tusen takk`, `Takk for sist`, `Takk for maten`, `skål`, `Hvordan har du det?`) that appear in the dialogues/cultural-information essay but not as standalone glossary headwords. The Grammar reference section (pp. 32–33) and the Cultural information essay (pp. 34–35) are paraphrased in the Grammar points section below. No handwritten marginalia was found anywhere in this copy — all 20 pages are clean printed typeset content.

This book is in **bokmål** throughout — its own cultural-information essay (p. 35) notes Norway has two official written standards, bokmål (spoken by over 80% of the population, developed from Danish) and nynorsk (built from western-coast dialects), but the book itself does not switch between them or mark any word as nynorsk.

## Grammar points

### Noun gender and articles
Norwegian nouns fall into two categories in this book: **common gender** (indefinite article *en*, e.g. *en bil* "a car") and **neuter** (indefinite article *et*, e.g. *et hus* "a house"). The definite form attaches the article as a suffix instead of a separate word: *bilen* "the car", *huset* "the house" (p.32).

### Plural formation
Indefinite plurals: common-gender nouns typically add *-er* (*biler* "cars"); monosyllabic neuter nouns usually take no ending at all (*hus* "houses"); a polysyllabic neuter noun like *hotell* instead takes *-er* (*hoteller*). Definite plurals use a single ending, *-ene*, for both genders regardless of the indefinite-plural pattern: *bilene* "the cars", *husene* "the houses" (p.32).

### Adjective agreement
Demonstrated with the paradigm *en stor bil → den store bilen → store biler → de store bilene* and *et stort hus → det store huset → store hus → de store husene* (p.32):
1. An adjective before an indefinite singular neuter noun takes **-t** (*stort*).
2. A definite singular or plural noun phrase that includes an adjective needs an extra definite article (*den*/*det*/*de*) placed in front, in addition to the noun's own definite-suffix ending.
3. The adjective itself takes **-e** in both the definite singular and all plural forms (*store*).

### Personal pronouns (subject / object / possessive)
The grammar reference (p.33) tabulates: *jeg/meg/min(-mitt/-mine)*, *du/deg/din(-ditt/-dine)*, *han/ham/hans*, *hun/henne/hennes*, *den/den/dens*, *det/det/dets*, *vi/oss/vår(-vårt/-våre)*, *dere/dere/deres*, *de/dem/deres*. Possessives agree in gender/number with the noun they modify (hence the three-way *min/mitt/mine* split), mirroring the adjective-agreement pattern above.

### Verbs: infinitive and present tense
The infinitive marker is *å* (e.g. *å snakke* "to talk"), and the present tense is formed regularly by adding **-r** to the infinitive stem: *jeg/du snakker* "I/you talk" (p.33). The verb *å være* "to be" is irregular and invariant across persons: *jeg er* "I am", *jeg var* "I was" (p.33).

### Request/preference formulas (observed across all ten conversations)
Two modal-plus-infinitive constructions dominate the dialogues' functional vocabulary far more than any single lexical item: *jeg vil gjerne ha...* "I would like (to have)..." for ordering/requesting (café, restaurant, hotel, chemist, souvenir shop), and *jeg vil heller ha...* "I would rather have..." for stating a preference between offered alternatives. Both take a bare noun phrase as object without an intervening preposition.

### Question formation and negation
Yes/no questions are formed by subject–verb inversion with the finite verb first: *Bor du i Oslo?* "Do you live in Oslo?" versus the declarative *Jeg bor i Bergen.* Wh-questions front the question word, with the verb still immediately following it: *Hva heter du?*, *Hvor bor du?* Negation is expressed with *ikke* placed directly after the finite verb: *Jeg bor ikke på hotell*, *Jeg har ikke bil* (Conversation 1).

### Time and number phrasing
Clock time can be stated either with a round hour (*klokken to* "two o'clock") or with the half-hour counted toward the *next* hour rather than the previous one — *halv åtte* literally "half eight" means 7:30, the same forward-counting mechanism documented for German/Dutch half-hour naming (Conversation 10/9). Multi-digit prices are built compositionally with *og* "and" joining the hundreds and the remainder, e.g. *fem hundre og sekstini kroner* "569 kroner" (Conversation 6).

### Register and cultural notes (paraphrased from the Cultural information essay, pp. 34–35)
The book's own survival-phrase list (p.22) explicitly marks *God dag* as "more formal" than the default greeting *Hei*, the only explicit formal/informal register annotation the book itself makes about any single word. The cultural-information essay separately explains (without tagging individual vocabulary items) that Norway has two official written language standards — **bokmål**, developed from Danish and used by over 80% of the population, and **nynorsk**, assembled from west-coast dialects and regarded by some as the "truer" Norwegian, whose use has declined over the preceding 50 years relative to bokmål. This book is written entirely in bokmål. The essay also describes two conversationally load-bearing customs: the toast ritual around *skål* (both parties say it together, with mutual eye contact, before the first sip and before the meal begins), and the near-obligatory post-meal phrase *Takk for maten* addressed to the hostess, which the book calls "the first rule of good behaviour." A brief history section (p.34) sketches Norway's Viking era, the ~400-year union with Denmark (to 1387 onward, Danish as the sole administrative/educational language), the brief 1814 independence followed by union with Sweden, the 1905 dissolution of that union and the election of the Danish Prince Carl as King Haakon VII, and five years of German occupation in World War II — offered as background for the strong patriotism the essay attributes to Norwegians around the 17 May National Day. A short food note (p.35) observes Norway's high pizza consumption alongside a revival of traditional dishes — whale, reindeer, and stockfish (*klippfisk*, historically exported mostly to Spain and Portugal, hence *bacalao* in Conversation 7) — eaten with thin crisp flatbread.

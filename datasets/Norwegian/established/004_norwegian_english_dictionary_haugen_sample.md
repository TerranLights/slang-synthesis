## Grammar points

### 2.1–2.2 What is Norwegian, and Norwegian among its neighbors (pp. 20–22)

Norwegian is a North Germanic (Scandinavian) language, mutually intelligible to varying degrees with Danish and Swedish — the introduction frames the three as a single dialect continuum divided by political rather than purely linguistic boundaries, closer to each other than any one is to Icelandic or Faroese (which preserve more of the older Old West Scandinavian morphology).

### 2.3–2.5 Extinction, independence, and the Dano-Norwegian language (pp. 22–25)

Norway lost its independent written standard once it fell under the Danish crown in the 14th century (reinforced by the Kalmar Union), and for roughly four centuries Norway's own administrative, legal, and literary language was effectively Danish — "Norwegian" as a distinct written norm essentially went extinct, even though the spoken language of the common people continued developing along its own lines. Independence in 1814 (followed by a further, separate union with Sweden until 1905) revived the political motivation to establish a genuinely Norwegian written standard, but the practical starting point available to reformers was written Danish as locally pronounced by educated Norwegians — "Dano-Norwegian" — not a fresh return to Old Norwegian.

### 2.6–2.9 The two reform paths and their fusion politics (pp. 25–27)

Two competing 19th-century reform movements are presented: Ivar Aasen's project (eventually named **nynorsk**, "New Norwegian," earlier *landsmål*) built a new written standard synthetically from rural dialect forms and Old Norwegian texts, deliberately distancing itself from Danish; Knud Knudsen's project (eventually named **bokmål**, "book language," earlier *riksmål*) instead respelled the existing Dano-Norwegian written language according to actual upper-class Norwegian pronunciation, staying closer to the inherited written tradition. The 20th century saw repeated government-driven orthographic reforms (the book specifically discusses the 1938 Orthography) aimed at *fusing* the two standards over time by making folk-dialect forms (the feminine grammatical gender, certain diphthongs, certain weak-verb endings) obligatory rather than merely optional within Bokmål — explicitly justified by appeal to "the speech of the common people" rather than the educated norm. This provoked real backlash: conservative Riksmål-movement writers (the book names Arnulf Øverland and Sigurd Hoel) organized opposition (the *Riksmålsforbundet*, including a "Foreldreaksjonen" parents' campaign) against folk forms being pushed into school textbooks, on the grounds that they were "vulgar in tone and inorganic" in the Bokmål language — a direct, source-attested instance of a prescriptive register judgment being contested as a matter of institutional politics, not just usage.

### 2.10–2.11 The Language Commission and real usage statistics (pp. 27–28)

A 30-member Language Commission (begun 1952, representing both languages equally, reporting to the Ministry of Church and Education) was tasked with gradually harmonizing Bokmål and Nynorsk "on the basis of the Norwegian folk language," producing a 1959 *Læreboknormal* (Textbook Norm) that formally classified reform variants as *obligatory*, *alternative*, or merely *optional* for school use. Despite this, the book states plainly that the two written languages remained — as of its own writing — "sufficiently distinct in structure and lexicon to be labeled as different languages." It gives hard usage figures: Bokmål was the language of at least 85% of books published, virtually all daily-newspaper editorial content (though papers accepted Nynorsk contributions), virtually all foreign-book translations, and all sizable commercial transactions. Nynorsk's share of schoolchildren peaked at 34% in 1943–44 and had fallen to 21.5% by 1962–63, with its remaining strongholds concentrated in four western counties (Møre og Romsdal, Sogn og Fjordane, Hordaland, Rogaland) where it still held a majority of schoolchildren; a 1950 army-recruit survey found only 22% preference for Nynorsk forms on a psychological test. Despite this numeric decline, the book notes Nynorsk's continued vitality in poetry and theater and among a cohort of younger writers.

### 2.12 Treatment of the two languages in this dictionary — the editorial policy behind the `dial.` tag (p. 28)

This is directly relevant to the register-tagging question this dispatch was looking for. The editors state their general policy was to follow *Norsk Riksmålsordbok* for Bokmål inclusion criteria and *Norsk Rettskrivningsordbok* (Sverdrup & Sandvei) as the reference, but found both books contained many genuinely local/dialectal terms not actually sanctioned as standard Bokmål — "many of these have accordingly been marked `dial.`" specifically to flag their non-standard status *within Bokmål*, even though such words can and do appear in Bokmål writers' actual usage. The symmetric situation held for Nynorsk sourcing (*Norsk Ordbok*, Hellevik's *Nynorsk Ordliste*, Skard's *Nynorsk Ordbok*), which likewise contained terms "previously forbidden in Nn and still avoided by the best Nn writers." The editors explicitly concede it was "impossible to lay down hard and fast rules about the usage of many words in the respective languages" — i.e., the `dial.` tag in this dictionary is doing real sociolinguistic-boundary-policing work, not just geography.

### 2.13 Comparative paradigms — Bokmål vs. Nynorsk pronoun/adjective morphology (pp. 29–33)

The introduction tabulates, side by side by gender/number, the full personal-pronoun, possessive, demonstrative, and indefinite/numeral-adjective paradigms for both written languages — e.g. possessive adjectives: masc. sg. Bm `min`/Nn `min, mi, mi`; fem. sg. Bm `mi`/Nn `mi`; neut. sg. Bm `mitt`/Nn `mitt`; pl. Bm `mine`/Nn `mine` (first person); similar paired columns given for 2nd/3rd person, demonstratives (`den/det/de` shared; `denne/dette/disse` Bm vs. `denne/dette/desse` Nn), and the indefinite-adjective sets (`annen/annet/andre`, `hvilken/hvilket/hvilke`, `mangen` etc.), with a notes section flagging that Nn additionally uses `kva for ein` where Bm uses `hva/hvilken`, and that `nokon`/`noen` ("any/some") have divergent plural forms (`nokre` as an alternate Nn plural) not found in Bm.

### 1.7 Usage Labels — the register-tagging system (pp. 17–19)

Entries are marked, where applicable, with one or more status labels drawn from a fixed set: `archaic` (spelled out in full rather than abbreviated, to avoid collision with the unrelated abbreviation `arch.` = "architectural"), `obs.` (obsolete), `lit.` (literary), `fam.` (familiar/colloquial), `hum.` (humorous), `pop.` (popular — glossed by the dictionary itself, per the Table of Abbreviations, as `folkelig`, i.e. "of the common people/folksy" — the closest in-house equivalent to this project's "slang" tier), `dial.` (dialectal; see 2.12 above), `derog.` (derogatory), `fig.` (figurative), and `folk.` (folklore — for content describing beliefs/superstitions rather than register per se). **A structurally important distinction found during this extraction**: `pop.` appears both unparenthesized, tagging the Norwegian headword/sense itself as popular-register, and parenthesized as `(pop.)`, which — per the Table of Abbreviations' separate line "`(pop.)` slang (American)" — instead flags that the *English translation gloss* chosen is itself American slang (e.g. `drag/erske` glossed `(pop.)` "hooker," where "hooker" rather than "drag/erske" is the thing being register-flagged). This means a `(pop.)` tag does not automatically certify the Norwegian word itself as slang — it needs to be checked against context, something worth carrying into any later mechanics-analysis pass over this dictionary's data.

### 3.1–3.3 Pronunciation system and syllable structure (pp. 34–37)

The book commits to a systematic phonemic transcription (given in both its own symbols and IPA) cross-tabulated against English keyword equivalents, explicitly flagging which Norwegian sounds have no close English counterpart (most vowels; the alveolar "thick l/r" consonant cluster system; the palato-velar spirant pair `[j]`/`[kj]`). Syllables are built around a vocalic or (when unstressed) sonorant-consonant nucleus; the system marks three prosodic diacritics as a unified inventory: `[·]` length, `[']` stress-with-length (Accent 1), `[`]` tone-with-stress-and-length (Accent 2), plus `[/]` juncture.

### 3.4 Juncture (p. 37)

Norwegian has a real phonetic juncture distinction inside and between words, parallel to English "night rate" vs. "nitrate": the dictionary's own example is `u'/tøy'` "vermin" vs. `u't/øy'` "remote island," both spelled identically as `utøy`, distinguished in speech by whether the medial `t` is aspirated (goes with the second syllable, as word-initial `t`'s are) or unaspirated (stays with the first syllable, as word-final/medial `t`'s are). The dictionary marks this juncture in its own respelling system with a slash wherever the first part of such a compound is stressed.

### 3.5 Length and gemination (pp. 37–38)

Every Norwegian vowel occurs in a short and a long variety; long vowels are markedly longer (roughly double) and tenser/less diphthongal than short ones. Length is phonologically tied to **consonant gemination**: a stressed syllable must contain either one long vowel or one long (geminate) consonant, never both — this complementary-length rule is treated as a genuine phonological law of the language, not a spelling quirk, and the book notes that orthography is inconsistent about marking it, which is why it supplies its own raised-dot length mark in ambiguous respelled forms.

### 3.6 Stress (pp. 38–39)

Stress functions much as in English, falling on one syllable per word pronounced in isolation; the book's key generalization is that **every stressed syllable must contain either a long vowel or a long consonant** — there is no such thing (in careful, standard pronunciation) as a stressed syllable with both elements short, which is why the acute-accent stress mark alone is sufficient to also imply vowel or consonant length without a separate symbol in most cases.

### 3.7 Tone — the Accent 1 / Accent 2 pitch-accent system (p. 39, flagship finding)

This is the single richest piece of grammar-logic content in the front matter. Most Norwegian speech carries a genuine lexical pitch-accent system — the book explicitly compares the underlying phenomenon to tone distinctions in Chinese and various African languages, and more narrowly to the same mechanism found in Lithuanian and Serbo-Croatian, and (geographically nearest) Swedish and some Danish dialects. **Accent 1** (monosyllabic tone) is, by the book's own definition, simply "stress with whatever tonal pattern is normal for the dialect in question" — it cannot be characterized by one fixed pitch shape nationally because regional base intonation differs (rising-to-high-then-falling in North/West/South Norway vs. a low-then-rising pattern in East Norway/Trøndelag, where the latter is often misheard by foreigners as question intonation, which it is not). **Accent 2** (polysyllabic tone) requires at least two syllables, the first of which is stressed; its pitch contour is described as more complex and regionally variable than Accent 1's, peaking later in the word and (in East Norway/Trøndelag specifically) not falling immediately but rising to a second peak before falling — in most regions except North Norway there is often a secondary pitch rise-and-fall that can be mistaken for a fresh stress. The book is explicit that Norwegian *can* be spoken intelligibly without tone (as Finland-Swedish speakers do), since minimal pairs distinguished only by tone are rarely genuinely ambiguous in context — but correct/natural Norwegian requires applying it, which is why the dictionary marks tone (via the grave accent) on every entry that carries it.

### Dictionary's own per-page notation key

Every dictionary-body page carries a standing footer key (observed identically on every sampled page across all six ranges): **`+` marks a form specific to Bokmål; `*` marks a form specific to Nynorsk; `°` marks a dialectal form; an acute mark after a letter indicates stress (Accent 1); a grave mark indicates tone-plus-stress (Accent 2); a raised dot indicates length; a period below a letter indicates it is not pronounced.** This is the mechanical backbone of the dictionary's whole Bokmål/Nynorsk/dialect-comparative design — nearly every headword observed across all six sampled ranges carries at least one of the `+`/`*` prefixes, confirming the book's own claim (title page) to cover "Modern Norwegian [Bokmål and Nynorsk]" as a matter of entry-by-entry practice, not just front-matter description.

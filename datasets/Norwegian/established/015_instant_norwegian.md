(`pdffonts` returns zero embedded fonts; `pdftotext` yields one blank form-feed per page). Every
page was vision-read from rendered page images. The PDF is scanned as **two printed pages per

## Grammar points

### "How Norwegian Works" — condensed grammar primer (pp. 6–7)

A two-page overview aimed at absolute beginners, independently phrased from 001/002 but covering the
same ground: nouns are common or neuter gender (with a note that a residual feminine article *ei/-a*
is in living, non-obligatory use alongside common *en/-en* — the book frames this as the language
"constantly evolving" rather than a fixed three-gender system); singular/plural and definite-article
suffixation (*en katt* → *katten*, *katter* → *kattene*; monosyllabic neuter nouns are invariant in
the plural, e.g. *et hus* → *flere hus*); adjective agreement by gender/number, plus the "double
definite" construction (*den store katten*, lit. "the big the-cat") for "the big cat"; the three-way
*min/mitt/mine* possessive-adjective paradigm by common/neuter/plural; a compact present-tense
conjugation table for *å komme, å se, å være, å ha, å kunne, å måtte* showing Norwegian verbs are
invariant across all persons; the future expressed with *skal* + infinitive, including *skal* alone
(no "to go" verb) for motion-to; *vil gjerne* + infinitive for polite "would like"; question
formation by subject-verb inversion; and negation with *ikke* placed after the first verb element.
One footnoted nuance not phrased this way in 001/002: *sin/sitt/sine* (reflexive possessive) can only
refer back to the sentence's own subject and never opens a sentence, distinguishing *han kjører sin
bil* ("he's driving his own car") from *han kjører hans bil* ("he's driving his [someone else's]
car") — the two prior references cover the sin/hans distinction but not this specific sentence-
position constraint.

### Pronunciation guide (p. 8)

A simplified, non-IPA vowel/consonant pronunciation key for English speakers, consistent with the
phonology already captured in `003_phonology_of_norwegian.md`. One detail worth flagging as a
regional/dialectal annotation the spec requires never be skipped: the book notes **the southwest of
Norway uses a French-style uvular r**, and that **rs is often pronounced [ʃ] ("sh"), especially in
the east** — both explicit dialectal splits (southwest vs. general; east vs. general) not phrased
this way in 001/002/003.

### Clock time: "half past" = halfway to the next hour (p. 74)

Norwegian frames the half-hour as counting toward the *next* hour rather than past the current one:
*halv ni* = "half eight" in form but means 8:30 (halfway to nine), the reverse of how English "half
eight" is sometimes used colloquially. The book's own transcription examples (*ti på halv sju* =
6:20, *fem på halv åtte* = 7:25) show quarter/five-minute increments are also counted relative to
the half-hour point, not the nearest full hour — a clean worked illustration of a convention that
matters for how far "near-hour" time expressions shift under translation.

# Norwegian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Norwegian Verbs & Essentials of Grammar (full book)

**Source:** Louis Janus, *Norwegian Verbs & Essentials of Grammar* (Passport Books / NTC-Contemporary
Publishing Group, Lincolnwood, Illinois; copyright © 1999 by NTC/Contemporary Publishing Group, Inc.;
ISBN 0-8442-8596-X). Full book, all chapters (1–25) and all four appendices (Common Verbs; Prefixes
and Suffixes; Common Pitfalls for English Speakers; Norwegian Names).

**Coverage note:** every grammar point and every distinct vocabulary item from this book, plus every
explicit dialectal/regional/register annotation the source itself marks — see
`../../00_Reference_Extraction_Spec.md` for the full coverage rule. This is the first-ever Norwegian
extraction for this project (no prior `established/` content to check for overlap). **This source has
no text layer (confirmed via `pdftotext`) and was entirely vision-read** from page images rendered at
150 DPI; the PDF's 89 physical pages are two-printed-page spreads, not one page per image — the spread
offset was verified empirically by reading the printed page numbers in each image's corner, not
assumed from a fixed arithmetic formula (per the extraction spec's documented spread gotcha).

**Marginalia found and excluded:** this is a used/scanned copy. On the Common Verbs appendix page
showing the printed entries for **eie** "own" and **ete** "eat (slang)" (printed p. 138), a handwritten
word in cursive/pencil script reading **"elske"** ("to love") appears in the margin between the two
printed table rows, visually inconsistent with the book's typeset font. This is a previous owner's
annotation, not source content, and has been excluded from the vocabulary table below. No other
marginalia was identified elsewhere in the book on inspection, but given the vision-reading guard's
"default to the riskier assumption" principle, any other faint or ambiguous mark was treated
conservatively.

**Flagged finding — explicit slang register tag in the source itself:** the Common Verbs appendix
glosses the verb **ete** (eat) as *"eat (slang)"* directly in its own English-gloss column, alongside
the neutral/core verb **spise** (also "eat," used throughout the rest of the book's examples without a
register label). This is the book's own explicit register annotation, not an inference, and is
flagged in the vocabulary table below as Usage Tier `slang`.

**Morpheme breakdown note:** Norwegian's past-participle/past-tense suffixes and noun definite-article
suffixes are productive, segmentable morphology (see Grammar points below), but because the book's own
Prefixes-and-Suffixes appendix already tabulates these as a reference list (reproduced in condensed
form in the Grammar points section), individual vocabulary-table rows above are given as whole words
rather than repeating a morpheme breakdown per entry — Norwegian's typology (see the morphological-
typology note at the end of this file) does not produce the kind of deep multi-morpheme concatenation
that would hide meaning in an atomic gloss the way an agglutinative language's forms would.

## Grammar points

### Bokmål vs. Nynorsk — the book's own comparative table (ch. 1, pp. 1–4)

**This is the single most valuable grammar point in the book for this project's purposes** — the
book's own explicit, systematic dialectal/standard-register comparison, which the extraction spec's
coverage rule marks as never to be skipped.

Norway has **two official written standards**, established as co-equal by a 1885 parliamentary
resolution (all official publications must still appear in both). Historical background the book
gives: Norway was under Danish rule for roughly 400 years, during which the written standard of
Norwegian itself died out even though spoken dialects persisted; Danish was the official language of
church, state, and culture. After Norway's 1814 independence from Denmark, two separate 1800s projects
modified the written language in different directions:

- **Bokmål** (formerly *Rigsmaal*/*Riksmål*, "language of the realm"; also called Dano-Norwegian) —
  evolved directly from written Danish, with spelling variants brought closer to Norwegian
  pronunciation and grammar. It is the form taught in this book, and the book states it is the
  variety spoken by most Norwegians in the **East and North** of the country.
- **Nynorsk** (formerly *Landsmaal*/*Landsmål*) — a deliberate reconstruction project by linguist
  **Ivar Aasen**, who traveled through **western Norway** collecting dialect forms and reconstructed
  what the written language might look like had it not been under centuries of Danish influence.

Both are *written* standards only — spoken Norwegian is entirely dialect-based, and a given speaker's
dialect may resemble either standard more closely regardless of which one they were taught first in
school. Per the book's 1994 figures: just under 83% of Norwegian elementary pupils are taught Bokmål
first; several *fylker* (counties) — Østfold, Akershus, Oslo, Vestfold, Nordland, Troms, Finnmark, and
Svalbard — had *no* schools teaching Nynorsk as the primary language at all, while Sogn og Fjordane had
the highest Nynorsk-pupil share at 96%. Norwegian overall is spoken as a first language by close to
4.5 million people.

**The book's own systematic comparison table** (reproduced here in paraphrase, not verbatim — short
illustrative forms only) lists these explicit points of difference, each with Bokmål form / Nynorsk
form / English gloss:

1. **Specific lexical differences** — e.g. Bokmål *Norge, jeg, hun, ikke, forskjell* vs. Nynorsk
   *Noreg, eg, ho, ikkje, skilnad* ("Norway, I, she, not, difference").
2. **Feminine-gender noun forms** — Nynorsk requires more nouns to take the feminine **ei**-gender
   form where Bokmål usually allows a choice: Bokmål *boken* or *boka* / *en bok* or *ei bok* vs.
   Nynorsk *boka* / *ei bok* ("the book"/"a book").
3. **Neuter definite plurals** — Bokmål allows either **-ene** or **-a** (*husene* or *husa* "the
   houses"), while Nynorsk requires **-a** (*husa*).
4. **Diphthong usage** — Nynorsk uses more diphthongs; Bokmål often allows a choice: Bokmål *et, vet*
   or *veit* / *øst* or *aust* vs. Nynorsk *eit, veit* / *aust* ("a, knows"/"east").
5. **Consonant cluster `kv` vs. `hv`** — Nynorsk uses *kv* where Bokmål uses *hv*: Bokmål *hva, hvit*
   vs. Nynorsk *kva, kvit* ("what, white").
6. **Vowel alternation in strong verbs (present tense, no -r ending)** — Nynorsk shows a vowel change
   Bokmål does not: Bokmål *sover, kommer* vs. Nynorsk *søv, kjem* ("sleeps, comes").
7. **Unstressed -a vs. -e** — Nynorsk uses unstressed **-a** in many words where Bokmål uses **-e**:
   Bokmål *stoler, kaster, gammel* vs. Nynorsk *stolar, kastar, gammal* ("chairs, throws, old").
8. **Pronoun for inanimate objects of common/feminine gender** — Bokmål uses **den** for this role;
   Nynorsk uses **han** or **ho** (the regular masculine/feminine pronouns) instead: Bokmål *Har du en
   sykkel? Nei, jeg har solgt den.* vs. Nynorsk *Har du en sykkel? Nei, eg har selt han.* ("Do you have
   a bicycle? No, I've sold it.").

**Parallel folktale excerpt (both standards + English):** the book quotes a short passage from a
Norwegian folktale (sourced, with permission, from Anton Fjeldstad's *Lærebok i norsk for svensker*,
Studentlitteratur, Lund, 1972) in both standards to let the differences be seen running through
continuous prose rather than isolated word pairs. Per copyright discipline, only a short illustrative
fragment is quoted here rather than the full three-sentence passage: the Bokmål opening **"Det var en
gang en gris som bodde tett ved en kongsgård"** versus the Nynorsk **"Det var ein gong ein gris som
budde tett attmed ein kongsgard"** ("Once upon a time there was a pig who lived near the king's
estate") — illustrating several of the above points at once (*en gang*/*ein gong*, *bodde*/*budde*,
*ved*/*attmed*, *kongsgård*/*kongsgard*).

**Variation *within* Bokmål itself:** a further register layer the book documents — the 38-member
**Norwegian Language Council** (*Språkråd*) maintains official word-form lists under a two-tiered
system set up in **1938**. Many words have one or more equal-status **principal forms** plus
**optional forms**: optional forms are acceptable in students' own writing, but textbooks and official
government documents must use a principal form. Example given: the past tense of **å gi** "to give"
has principal form **ga** with optional **gav**.

### Alphabet and IPA (ch. 2)

Norwegian uses the same 26 Latin letters as English plus three additional vowels — **æ, ø, å** —
alphabetized after z (sometimes transliterated **ae, oe, aa** in library catalogs/older electronic
contexts). **c, q, x, z** are rare in native Norwegian vocabulary. The book represents pronunciation
throughout in IPA between slashes, with long vowels marked by a colon. A handful of Norwegian words
carry accent marks that indicate *stress* rather than a changed vowel quality (unlike French): **é**
(as in *kafé* "café," *orkidé* "orchid") is the most common, though still rare; **ò** appears in the
stressed sentence-final adverb *òg* "also." The French-style **à** survives in rate expressions like
*3 kg à kr 5*. A minimal triplet — **fôr** "feed," **fór** "traveled" (past tense of *å fare*), and
unaccented **for** "too, for" — is disambiguated purely by accent marks.

### Pronunciation (ch. 3)

**Vowels.** Norwegian has **nine simple vowels (monophthongs)** — /ɑ, e, i, ɯ, ʉ, y, æ, ø, ɔ/ — each
of which can be pronounced long or short, with vowel length frequently the *only* feature
distinguishing otherwise-similar word pairs (the book's own minimal-pair example: **tak** /tɑ:k/ "roof"
long vs. **takk** /tɑk/ "thanks" short). Several Norwegian vowel sounds are rounded-lip sounds with no
customary English equivalent (/y/, /ø/, /ɔ/-as-written-å). In addition to the nine monophthongs,
Norwegian has **five diphthongs** — /æi, øy, æu, ɔy, ʉy/ — of which three (æi, øy, æu) are common and
two are rare enough not to need active mastery.

**Word stress and vowel length.** Most native words stress the first syllable; borrowed words (e.g.
*restaurant*) often keep the lending language's stress pattern; prefixes **be-** and **for-** are never
stressed. Vowel length is largely *predictable from spelling*: a short vowel is generally followed by
two or more consonants, a long vowel by a single consonant — illustrated by the **tak/takk** pair
above. Unstressed syllables are always short but, unlike English, do **not** reduce to a schwa — the
book flags this as a common English-speaker error, since it can collapse the distinction between
**jente** /jentə/ "girl" and **jenta** /jenta/ "the girl."

**Tones and intonation.** Norwegian is tonal at the *word* level: all stressed words carry either a
**rising (single) tone** or a **falling-then-rising (double) tone**, with the actual phonetic
realization varying by dialect region — in **East Norwegian** the double tone falls then rises, while
in **West Norway** it rises then falls. This genuinely distinguishes otherwise-identical words: the
book's example is **været** "the weather" (single tone) vs. **være** "be" (double tone). Layered on
top of word tone is **sentence intonation**: in East Norwegian, the last word/word-group of a
declarative sentence rises in pitch — which the book notes often makes statements sound like questions
to English-speaking listeners; true questions carry an even sharper final rise.

**Distinctive consonant pronunciations.** Consonant clusters impossible in English (e.g. **bj-** in
*bjørn* "bear," **fj-** in *fjord*) are common in Norwegian, with a brief "offglide" similar to the
initial sounds of English *beauty*/*future*. **k** and **g** each have two pronunciations: a "hard"
sound before back vowels (a, å, o, u), similar to English; and before the front vowels **i, y, ø**, a
softened fricative — **k** becomes the sound spelled **kj** /ç/ (*kirke* "church," *kino* "movie"), and
**g** becomes /j/ (*gi* "give," *begynne* "begin"). The written clusters **sj, ski, skj,** and **rs**
all share the single sound /ʃ/ (English "sh"), as in *sju* "seven," *ski* "ski," *skje* "spoon," *norsk*
"Norwegian." **r** varies regionally: a tongue-tip tap/flap/trill in East and North Norway (distinct
from American English's retroflex /r/); a uvular /ʁ/-like sound (similar to French r) along the south
and west coast — **an explicit dialectal/regional pronunciation annotation.** There is no voiced /z/
sound in Norwegian; borrowed words with z (e.g. *Zulu*) use voiceless /s/.

**Spelling-to-pronunciation hints and silent letters.** Several systematic patterns let a learner
predict pronunciation from spelling: **o** before two-or-more consonants is often pronounced /å/
(*godt* → /gått/); **e** before **r** is often pronounced /æ/ (*er* /æ:r/, *der* /dæ:r/); the /æi/
diphthong is spelled **-eg, -egn,** or **-ei** (*jeg, regn, bein*). "Silent" letters: **-ig** is
pronounced /i/, the **g** silent (*hyggelig* /hyggeli/); **hv-** interrogatives are pronounced /v/
(*hva* /vɑ:/, *hvor* /vor/); **hj-** and **gj-** are always pronounced /j/ (*hjemme* /jemmə/, *gjøre*
/jørə/); the **-t** of the pronoun/demonstrative **det** is always silent (/de:/); the **-t** of an
**et**-noun's definite ending is silent in normal speech (*huset* → /husə/) though careful speakers may
pronounce it — but the **-t** that marks past tense/past participle on verbs *is* pronounced (*snakket*
/snakket/); **-d** is frequently silent at the end of several common "small words" (*med* /me:/, *ved*
/ve:/); the conjunction **og** "and" is almost always pronounced /å/ without the /g/ in casual speech,
though careful speakers may use /å:g/ — a register distinction (casual vs. careful speech) the book
marks explicitly.

### Spelling conventions (ch. 4)

**No final -mm.** Norwegian words never end in a double **-mm**, even though **-mm-** is common
word-internally: a word may end in single **-m** in one form but take **-mm-** when not word-final in a
related form (e.g. **et rom** "a room" → **rommet** "the room"; **hjem** "to home" → **hjemme** "at
home"; **morsom** "fun" sg. → **morsomme** pl.; **kom** "come/came" (imperative/past) → **komme**
"come" (infinitive)). This spelling convention applies across parts of speech, not just one word
class. Conversely, some words end in a single consonant specifically *because* the preceding vowel is
long, and that final consonant is **not** doubled when an ending is added, in order to preserve the
long-vowel pronunciation — e.g. **et problem** "a problem" keeps a single **-m** in **problemet** "the
problem," preserving /proble:m/.

### Overview of verb forms (ch. 5)

Norwegian verbs are described by **four principal parts**: infinitive (marked by **å**), present
tense, past tense, and present perfect/past participle. Verbs divide into **regular** and
**irregular**. Regular past-tense endings fall into **four classes**, ending in **-et, -te, -de,** and
**-dde** respectively (paralleling English's uniform **-ed**); the corresponding past-participle
endings are **-et, -t, -d,** and **-dd**. Irregular verbs' past tense and participle forms don't follow
these neat classes and often show a different *vowel* than the present/infinitive (the book's own
example pair: **å skrive** "to write" → **hun skrev** "she wrote"; **å drikke** "to drink" → **vi
drakk** "we drank" — vowel-ablaut irregularity, structurally similar to English's own "write/wrote,"
"drink/drank" pattern). Being irregular in the past tense does **not** imply irregularity in the
present tense — e.g. **du drakk** ("you drank," irregular past) pairs with the fully regular present
**du drikker** ("you drink").

### Present tense (ch. 6)

**The single largest simplification versus English, French, and German noted by the book**: Norwegian
present-tense verb endings are **the same for every subject, person, and number** — if you know the
ending for one subject you know it for all subjects. Almost all verbs form the present tense (for
every subject) by adding **-r** to the verb's stem (generally the infinitive minus an unstressed final
**-e**, if present): e.g. **å bosette** → **bosetter**; **å ha** → **har**; **å kjøpe** → **kjøper**; **å
selge** → **selger**. A small set of irregular present tenses do not follow this -r/-er pattern,
including the irregular verbs **gjøre → gjør, spørre → spør, være → er, vite → vet**, and the modal
auxiliaries **kunne → kan, ville → vil, måtte → må, skulle → skal, burde → bør** — the book notes that
unlike Norwegian, the equivalent English modals ("can," "must") have no infinitive form at all ("to
can" doesn't exist).

**No progressive ("-ing") form.** Norwegian collapses English's three present-tense constructions
("she eats" / "she is eating" / "she does eat") into one single present-tense form: **hun spiser**
covers all three. To stress an activity's ongoing nature, Norwegian instead joins two same-tense verbs
with **og** ("and") — frequently a position verb (**sitte, ligge, stå** "sit, lie, stand") plus the
main-activity verb: **Mette sitter og leser** literally "Mette sits and reads," idiomatically "Mette is
reading." A present-tense verb can also describe scheduled/certain future events, with an adverbial
phrase supplying the future time reference.

### Modal helping verbs (ch. 7)

Covers the full modal paradigm: **kan/kunne** (can/could), **vil/ville** (want to/would), **skal/skulle**
(shall-plan to/should), **må/måtte** (must/had to), **bør/burde** (ought to/should), and **får/få**
(get to). After any modal, the main verb takes the bare infinitive **without** the infinitive marker
**å** — a rule the book flags separately as its own named pitfall (Pitfall 2), since English speakers
often insert an equivalent of "to."

### Forms of the past tense (ch. 8)

Regular verbs fall into **four weak conjugation classes** by their past-tense/past-participle suffix:
**-et/-et, -te/-t, -de/-d, -dde/-dd**. Irregular ("strong") verbs instead show internal vowel changes
(ablaut) between infinitive, present, and past forms, with no added **-t/-d** suffix at all —
structurally the same mechanism as English's own irregular-verb vowel gradation (sing/sang/sung-type
alternation), just with Norwegian's own specific vowel-alternation classes (not exhaustively
enumerated by the book as a formal table, but densely exemplified across the Common Verbs appendix
above — note how many entries above show a present/past vowel change: **bite→bet, drive→drev,
skrive→skrev, synge→sang**, etc.).

### Present perfect and past perfect tenses (ch. 9)

Formed, as in English, with an auxiliary plus the past participle: present perfect = **har** + past
participle; past perfect = **hadde** + past participle. **Explicit dialectal annotation**: for a subset
of intransitive motion/change-of-state verbs (e.g. **reise** "travel," **bli** "become"), some Northern
and Western Norwegian dialects use **er** ("is") rather than **har** ("has") as the present-perfect
auxiliary — paralleling the older être/avoir-type auxiliary-selection split found in several other
Germanic languages' perfect tenses (German, Dutch) before most varieties of those languages also
generalized to a single auxiliary. Usage Tier: `regional`.

### Participles (ch. 10)

The **present participle** is formed with the suffix **-ende** (e.g. an adjectival use: **en gående
mann** "a walking man") — distinct from, and not used to form, a progressive verb tense (see
Pitfall 3 below, where the book explicitly warns against calques like *"hun er skrivende"* for "she is
writing"). The **past participle** is the fourth principal part already covered under past-tense
classes above, and also functions adjectivally when inflected for gender/number/definiteness.

### Future tense (ch. 11)

Norwegian expresses futurity chiefly with two modal auxiliaries that are **not interchangeable** — a
genuine grammatical-logic distinction, not free variation: **skal** implies the **subject's own plan
or control** over the future event; **vil** implies prediction or desire **without implied control**
(closer to "will/is going to" as a forecast). A third, more colloquial-register construction,
**kommer til å** + infinitive, expresses a near-certain future the speaker is confident about,
independent of anyone's control.

### Conditionals and subjunctives (ch. 12)

Covers hypothetical **hvis**-clauses (conditional sentences) and notes that the historical Norwegian
subjunctive mood has largely merged with the ordinary past tense in the modern language — true
synthetic subjunctive forms are now rare/archaic rather than a living, productive mood.

### Passives (ch. 13)

Norwegian has **two passive constructions**: the periphrastic **bli-passive** (**bli** + past
participle, parallel to English "get + past participle" / German *werden*-passive) and the synthetic
**-s passive** (a suffix **-s** added directly to the verb stem, e.g. **det sies** "it is said," listed
in the Prefixes/Suffixes appendix as `-(e)s`). The passive agent, when expressed, is marked with the
preposition **av** "by."

### -s verbs (ch. 14)

A verb-morphology category beyond the ordinary s-passive, covering two distinct phenomena that share
the same **-s** verb-final marker:

- **Deponent verbs** — verbs that are inherently **-s**-marked in form but are *not* semantically
  passive (the -s is lexicalized, not a true passive marker): **synes** "seem/think," **lykkes**
  "succeed," **trives** "thrive/enjoy," **finnes** "exist." (The book's index independently confirms
  this category at p. 54, "Deponent verbs.")
- **Reciprocal -s verbs** — verbs where the **-s** marks a mutual/reciprocal action ("each other"),
  with no separate reflexive pronoun needed: **møtes** "meet each other," **ses** "see each other,"
  **slåss** "fight (each other)."

### Nouns (ch. 15)

Norwegian has **three grammatical genders**: masculine/common (**en**), feminine (**ei**), and neuter
(**et**) — with the book giving an approximate real-world distribution of roughly **55% en-nouns, 25%
et-nouns, and 20% ei-nouns**. Definite forms are marked with a **suffixed** article (not a separate
preposed word as in English "the") — e.g. **hus** → **huset** "the house," a point the book names
directly as Pitfall 6. Plural formation for indefinite nouns generally adds **-er** (en-nouns) or, for
et-nouns, adds **-er** only if the noun is **polysyllabic** — monosyllabic et-nouns typically take no
plural ending at all. A set of nouns is irregular in the plural (e.g. **mann → menn**, **bok → bøker**
— ablaut-type plurals structurally similar to English "man/men," "foot/feet"), and a further small set
of nouns has **no singular form** at all (e.g. **penger** "money," **klær** "clothes," **briller**
"glasses" — pluralia tantum).

**Explicit register/dialectal annotation on gender choice:** the book notes that for nouns which allow
a choice between the masculine/common (**en**) and feminine (**ei**) article/declension (a large class,
since Bokmål "usually allows a choice" per the Bokmål/Nynorsk comparison above), **formal registers
favor the masculine forms, while casual, informal, peer-to-peer speech favors liberal use of the
feminine (ei) forms** — a live register split *within* Bokmål itself, distinct from the Bokmål/Nynorsk
standards split. Usage Tier for the feminine-gender-favoring pattern: `colloquial`; the masculine
pattern: `formal`.

### Personal pronouns (ch. 16)

Covers subject/object pronoun pairs (**jeg/meg, du/deg, han/ham, hun/henne, den/det, vi/oss,
dere/dere, de/dem**) and the formal second-person pronoun **De** (object **Dem**), which the book
states is **"currently dying out in most of Norway, now encountered only in the most formal settings
and historical documents."** This is itself a register-decline annotation from the source, not an
inference — Usage Tier `archaic` for **De**/**Dem**.

A genuinely non-English-mapping distinction: the **reflexive possessive** system **sin/si/sitt/sine**
(agreeing in gender/number with the possessed noun) marks that the possessor is the sentence's own
subject, disambiguating from a non-reflexive possessive (**hans/hennes/deres**, "his/her/their,"
referring to someone *other than* the subject) — a distinction English does not grammaticalize at all
(English "his" is ambiguous between reflexive and non-reflexive readings in a way Norwegian is not).

### Determiners (ch. 17)

Covers demonstratives (**denne/dette/disse** "this/these"), the all-quantifier **all/alt/alle**, the
ordinal/other-marker **annen/annet/andre** "other, second," and indefinite pronouns. **intet**
"nothing/no" (neuter) is flagged as an archaic form.

### Adjectives (ch. 18)

Adjectives agree with their noun's gender/number in the **indefinite** form (distinct endings for
common/neuter/plural) and take a separate, mostly uniform **definite** form (preceded by a definite
article/demonstrative — the "double definite" construction, where both a preposed determiner and the
noun's own suffixed definite article co-occur, e.g. **den store mannen** "the big man," is one of the
chapter's grammar points). Comparison follows a regular **-ere** (comparative) / **-est** (superlative)
pattern, alongside a set of irregular comparatives/superlatives for common adjectives (not
exhaustively enumerated here; see the Common Verbs/Prefixes appendix data above for some of the
corresponding suffix entries, e.g. `-ere` "comparative form of adjectives," `-este` "definite form of
superlative adjective"). **liten/lita/lite/små** "small" is a fully irregular adjective (the **lita**
form is itself a feminine-gender/dialectal variant, paralleling the en/ei noun-gender split above).

### Adverbs (ch. 19)

Most adverbs are formed by adding **-t** to the corresponding adjective. Beyond ordinary descriptive
adverbs, the book names a distinct category of **modal/discourse adverbs** — small words like **jo,
vel, da, nok** that color a sentence's stance/certainty rather than describing manner, a category with
no single clean English equivalent (closer to German modal particles *ja, doch, wohl*). A further
systematic pattern is the **direction-vs.-location adverb pairing**: Norwegian distinguishes a
"motion toward" adverb from a "static location" adverb for the same place-concept — **hit/her**
("to here"/"at here"), **dit/der** ("to there"/"at there"), **inn/inne, ut/ute, opp/oppe, ned/nede,
bort/borte, hjem/hjemme** — a distinction collapsed in English (e.g. "home" covers both "go home" and
"be at home," while Norwegian requires **hjem** vs. **hjemme**).

### Word order (ch. 20)

Norwegian is a **verb-second (V2)** language: the finite verb must be the *second* constituent of a
main clause, regardless of what the first constituent is. When a non-subject element (a time
adverbial, an object, a subordinate clause, etc.) occupies the first position, the subject and finite
verb **invert** (subject moves after the verb) to preserve V2 order — e.g. **I dag er jeg i Oslo**
("Today I-am in Oslo," not the English-order *"I dag jeg er i Oslo"*). The book names this as its
single most common English-speaker error (Pitfall 1). Coordinating conjunctions (og, men, eller, for)
sentence-initially do **not** trigger inversion.

**Negation/adverb placement** follows from the same V2 logic: in a main clause, a sentence adverb like
**ikke** ("not") falls *after* the finite verb (**Jeg snakker ikke norsk**, "I speak not Norwegian");
in a subordinate clause, it falls *before* the finite verb — the same general "different adverb
position in main vs. subordinate clauses" pattern documented in several Germanic V2 languages (compare
the "BIFF rule" mnemonic used for Danish/Swedish).

### Questions (ch. 21)

Yes/no questions are formed by inversion (finite verb first); wh-questions by fronting the
question-word, which still triggers inversion. **Explicit regional/intonation annotation**: in East
Norwegian, a declarative sentence's rising final-word intonation (documented under Pronunciation above)
means questions carry an even sharper final-syllable rise than statements do, to remain
distinguishable — Usage Tier `regional` for this prosodic detail. **hvis** "if" is separately flagged
as now largely replaced by **om** in ordinary conditional use — Usage Tier `archaic` for **hvis** in
that function (see also the conjunctions vocabulary entry above).

### Prepositions and particles (ch. 22)

A long chapter mostly disambiguating senses of highly polysemous prepositions (**i, på, til, for, om,
av,** etc.) one at a time, each with multiple distinct English equivalents depending on context — the
chapter's own stated purpose is exactly this sense-by-sense false-friend disambiguation for English
speakers. A separate **"Particles"** section documents Norwegian's equivalent of English phrasal verbs:
small words (**opp, ned, ut, inn, av, på, bort, frem, til, sammen**, etc.) combine productively with
verb stems to form new, often non-compositional meanings, structurally parallel to English "go up,"
"take off," "come in."

### Conjunctions and subordinate clauses (ch. 23)

Full paradigm of coordinating conjunctions (og, men, eller, for) and an extensive list of subordinating
conjunctions covering time (da, når, mens, før, etter at, siden), condition (hvis, om), concession
(selv om), purpose/result (så, for at), and alternation (enten…eller). Several of these subordinators
receive their own dedicated disambiguation treatment later in the Common Pitfalls appendix (da/når/så;
som/at; hvis vs. om) — cross-referenced there rather than duplicated here.

### Numbers (ch. 24)

Covers cardinal numbers 1–upward, with an explicit note that Norwegian preserves an **older
units-before-tens counting order** alongside the modern tens-before-units order for 21–99 (paralleling
the archaic English "four-and-twenty" pattern) — e.g. **enogtyve** (older, unit-first) vs. **tjueen**
(modern, tens-first) for 21. Three specific older number-word variants — **syv** (7, vs. modern **sju**),
**tyve** (20, vs. **tjue**), and **tredve** (30, vs. **tretti**) — are **explicitly flagged by the book
as no longer officially accepted by the Norwegian Language Council, but still in use by many older
texts and speakers** — a clean, source-marked `archaic`/`core` register split. Ordinal numbers mostly
add **-ende** or **-de** to the cardinal; the smallest ordinals (1st–10th) are irregular. Fractions
with denominators 3–12 have two equal forms (ordinal-based or cardinal + **del** "part"); fractions
with denominators above 12 use only the cardinal + **del** form. A dedicated special form,
**halvannen/halvannet** ("one and a half," literally "half of the second"), exists alongside the
regular **en og en halv**.

### Time expressions (ch. 25)

Covers days of the week and months (both lowercase, unlike English), seasons, date-writing conventions
(day before month: **den 8. mars 1998**/**8.3.1998**), a "Divisions of Time" vocabulary set, and a
cluster of **time-expression prepositions** the book flags as frequently misused by English speakers:
**om** (future-pointing, "in X time"), **i** (a time period an event spans), **på** (elapsed time
*without* an event, or completion time), and **for … siden** ("X ago"). **Telling time** has two
genuinely non-English-mapping features: (1) Norwegian counts minutes **toward the next hour** rather
than from the current hour — **halv fem** = 4:30 ("half [toward] five"), with the ten minutes on
either side of a half-hour described as "before" or "after" the half-hour point rather than relative to
the hour itself; (2) Norwegian does **not** distinguish A.M./P.M., instead optionally adding **om
formiddagen** ("in the morning") or **om kvelden** ("in the evening") only when disambiguation is
actually needed, alongside the 24-hour clock for official/precise contexts.

### Common Verbs appendix (pp. 137–148)

A ~225-entry infinitive/present/past/present-perfect/gloss reference table, reproduced comprehensively
in the Vocabulary section above per the extraction spec's instruction to capture a dedicated
verb-reference book's core verb list in full rather than sampling it. Patterns worth naming explicitly
as grammar points in their own right:

- **Transitivity-conditioned conjugation-class splits on a single infinitive spelling.** At least two
  verbs in the list — **brenne** ("burn") and **henge** ("hang") — have *the same infinitive* but
  switch between a strong (vowel-change) and a weak (suffixed) past tense depending on whether the verb
  is used transitively or intransitively: intransitive **brenne** → strong past **brant**; transitive
  **brenne** → weak past **brente**. This is a genuine grammatical fork conditioned by argument
  structure, not free variation.
- **Dialectal spelling doublets within the appendix itself**, e.g. **hvile/kvile** "rest" — the **kv-**
  initial spelling is the same consonant-cluster difference the Bokmål/Nynorsk comparison table
  documents (Bokmål **hv-** vs. Nynorsk **kv-**), appearing here as a doublet within what is nominally
  a single Bokmål-focused reference list.

### Prefixes and suffixes appendix (pp. 149–154)

**Prefixes** (a short, closed inventory, paraphrased from the book's own list): **an-** (arrive-type
sense, *ankomme*), **be-** (*beskrive* "describe," *bety* "signify"), **for-** (*forstår*
"understand"), **fore-** (*foreta* "undertake"), **gjen-** ("re-/again," *gjenlyd* "echo," *gjeninnføre*
"reintroduce"), **mis-** ("mis-," *mislike* "dislike," *misforstå* "misunderstand"), **om-**
("around/comprehensive," *omfavne* "embrace," *omgi* "surround"), **u-** ("un-/negation," *ugift*
"unmarried," *ujevn* "uneven"), **unn-** (*unngå* "avoid," *unntak* "exception"), **van-**
("mis-/deficient," *vanvittig* "mad," *vantro* "skeptical"). The book notes several prefixes (e.g.
**multi-, ultra-**) are international/loan-word elements shared with English rather than native
Norwegian derivational morphology.

**Suffixes** — a much larger, genuinely productive inventory the book organizes alphabetically *by
final letter* (a browsing aid for a reader who recognizes a word-ending but not the whole word). This
is the book's own derivational- and inflectional-morphology reference and is the most direct evidence
in the whole source for Norwegian's morphological typology (see the typology note below). Representative
entries, condensed from the book's own table: **-a** (definite ei-noun singular / definite et-noun
plural / variant weak-verb past), **-d/-dd/-de/-dde/-et/-ede/-ete/-t** (the various past-tense and
past-participle endings, cross-referencing the four weak verb classes from ch. 8), **-e** (indefinite
noun plural; multi-syllable infinitive marker; adjective agreement ending; adverb-of-location marker,
as in **hjemme** vs. **hjem**), **-ende** (present participle; also, unrelatedly, the ordinal-number
ending above 10th), **-er/-ere/-erne** (indefinite noun plural; present tense of certain verbs;
comparative of adjectives/adverbs; productive verb-forming; definite plural of **-er**-final nouns —
a single surface suffix doing five distinct grammatical jobs depending on word class), **-else/-ing/
-ning/-sel** (noun-forming from verbs, e.g. *lesning* "reading," *begynnelse* "beginning"), **-het**
(abstract-noun-forming, *frihet* "freedom"), **-ig/-(e)lig/-messig/-aktig/-som/-bar/-(i)sk** (a cluster
of adjective-forming suffixes, e.g. *stilig* "stylish," *daglig* "daily," *regelmessig* "regular,"
*guttaktig* "boyish," *hjelpsom* "helpful," *brukbar* "usable," *nordisk* "Nordic"), **-ing** (also,
separately, "person from an area," e.g. *østfolding* "someone from Østfold"), **-inne/-ske** (female
professions/roles, e.g. *lærerinne* "female teacher," *sykepleierske* "female nurse" — a
gender-marking derivational suffix with no productive English equivalent), **-isme** (loan suffix,
"-ism," *anakronisme*), **-ling** (diminutive or group noun, *tvilling* "twin"), **-løs**
(privative/"-less," *arbeidsløs* "unemployed"), **-s** (possessive; also the passive marker, cross-
referencing ch. 13), **-skap** (abstract noun-forming, *vennskap* "friendship"), **-vis** (adverb of
manner, *heldigvis* "luckily"), **-dom** (abstract noun-forming, *fattigdom* "poverty"), **-tøy**
(noun-forming, *undertøy* "underwear"), **-æ r** (agentive noun, *millionær* "millionaire").

### Common Pitfalls for English Speakers (appendix, pp. 155–161)

A set of 12 numbered, explicitly English-contrastive mistakes, each paraphrased here with its core
rule (full illustrative minimal-pair examples for several are already captured in the Vocabulary
table above):

1. **Word order / inversion** — forgetting V2 inversion when a non-subject element starts the
   sentence (cross-references ch. 20 above; the book's own stated single most common mistake).
2. **Infinitive after modal helping verbs** — main verb must be the bare infinitive (no **å**) after
   **skulle, skal, ville, vil, måtte, må, kunne, kan** (cross-references ch. 7).
3. **Expressing English "-ing" forms** — Norwegian has no progressive verb form; ongoing activity is
   expressed with simple present/past tense, or with a position verb (**sitte, ligge, stå**) + **og** +
   the activity verb in the same tense (e.g. **Wenche sitter og leser romanen**, "Wenche is reading the
   novel"), or with the idiom **holde på med å** ("to be in the middle of / continuing to").
4. **Finite verbs need a tense** — English conflates the bare infinitive with 1st/2nd-person present
   ("I/you/we speak" = infinitive-looking), which can lead learners to use the Norwegian infinitive
   where a true finite present-tense form (**snakker**, not **snakke**) is required.
5. **Du/deg/dere (and De/Dem)** — Norwegian grammaticalizes subject vs. object case for 2nd person
   singular (**du** vs. **deg**) the way English no longer does; **dere** covers both subject and
   object for 2nd person plural.
6. **Definite nouns take suffixed definite articles**, not a separate preposed word like English "the"
   (cross-references ch. 15).
7. **som / at / hvem / det — all translate English "that," but are not interchangeable.** **som**
   connects a noun/pronoun to a relative clause; **at** connects a verb to a following clause (the two
   are not swappable, unlike English "that" which covers both roles); **hvem** is the interrogative
   "who/whom" and, when it is the subject of a relative clause, must still be followed by **som**;
   **det** covers "the"/"that" in its demonstrative/pronoun role.
8. **hvor vs. hvordan for "how."** English "how" + adjective/adverb (how many, how far) maps to
   Norwegian **hvor** + adjective/adverb, not to **hvordan** (which means "how" in the sense of manner
   and is not combined with an adjective/adverb the way English "how" is).
9. **da / når / så — "when"/"then."** **da** functions as either a conjunction ("when," past-tense
   context) or an adverb ("then"), with word order itself signaling which reading applies (subject +
   finite verb after **da** = conjunction reading; finite verb + subject = adverb reading); **når**
   covers "when" for present/future contexts, or "whenever" for a repeated past action; **så** as an
   adverb means "then, subsequently," as a conjunction "so that."
10. **synes / tro / tenke — all "to think," not interchangeable.** **synes** = an opinion formed from
    direct personal experience (arguable as opinion, not as fact); **tro** = something presented as
    factual, whether or not it's actually correct (arguable as fact); **tenke** = the mental activity
    of thinking/considering itself.
11. **gang / tid / time — all touch "time," not interchangeable.** **time** (despite resembling
    English "time") means "hour" or "class period"; **gang** means "time" only in the sense of an
    occurrence/occasion ("once," "one time"); **tid** covers the general/abstract sense of time (epoch,
    period, the passage of time) that English "time" most often intends.
12. **Long vs. short vowel minimal pairs.** The book calls this the single pronunciation error most
    likely to produce actual communication breakdowns (more than other inexactness), citing **tak**
    /tɑ:k/ "roof" (long) vs. **takk** /tɑk/ "thanks" (short), and **mine** /mi:ne/ "my/mine" vs.
    **minne** /minne/ "remind."

## Morphological typology (observations, for the orchestrating session's use)

**Degree of fusion/analyticity — strongly analytic, with isolated fusional residue.** Norwegian verb
morphology is close to the analytic end of the spectrum: present tense is **invariant across all
persons/numbers** (a single form, **-r/-er**, covers every subject — the book states this explicitly as
Norwegian's major simplification relative to French, German, and even English, since English still
marks 3rd-person-singular "-s"). Tense/aspect distinctions that many fusional languages mark
synthetically on the verb (progressive aspect) are instead built periphrastically (position-verb + **og**
+ main verb, or the **holde på med å** idiom) rather than via a dedicated participial inflection. Where
fusion *does* survive, it is concentrated in the ~closed class of **strong/irregular verbs**, which mark
past tense through internal vowel ablaut rather than a suffix (**skrive→skrev→skrevet** "write/wrote/
written," **drikke→drakk→drukket** "drink/drank/drunk") — structurally the same residual-fusional
pattern English's own irregular verbs show, and the book explicitly draws that English parallel. Regular
("weak") verbs, by contrast, are straightforwardly suffixing/agglutinative-looking at the small scale: a
single stem plus one of four mechanically-chosen past-tense suffix classes (**-et, -te, -de, -dde**),
with no additional fusional vowel change layered on top.

**Noun gender/declension.** A three-gender system (masculine/common **en**, feminine **ei**, neuter
**et**) with indefinite articles as separate proclitic words but **definite marking as a true suffix**
fused onto the noun (**hus → huset**, **bok → boka**) — genuinely agglutinative-looking at the
word-edge, not merely cliticized, since the definite suffix interacts with the noun's own stem-final
spelling rules (the -mm/-m doubling convention from ch. 4 applies to noun inflection exactly as it does
to verb inflection). Plural marking (**-er**) stacks with definite marking (**-ene**) in a
straightforwardly agglutinative, linearly-ordered way (stem + plural + definite), without the kind of
deep nesting or non-linear interaction that would require a morpheme-breakdown table per the project's
typology guide — each layer is a single, separable, easily-glossed suffix, which is why this file's
vocabulary table does not use the morpheme-breakdown convention: Norwegian word-forms here are at most
two or three concatenated suffixes deep, each independently meaningful and non-fused with its
neighbors, which doesn't hide compositional structure the way a deeper agglutinative or polysynthetic
language's forms would.

**Compound nouns — note on the orchestrator's briefed "Compound Nouns" section.** The dispatch prompt
for this extraction flagged the table of contents as showing a dedicated "Compound Nouns" section
(Noun+Noun, Adjective+Noun, Verb+Noun, Preposition+Noun, Adverb+Noun) within the Nouns chapter. On
direct inspection of this book's actual table of contents and the full text of ch. 15 (Nouns, printed
pp. 59–67), **no such dedicated "Compound Nouns" section exists in this particular book** — the Nouns
chapter covers gender, definite/indefinite forms, and plural formation (including irregular plurals and
pluralia tantum) only. This may be a cross-reference mix-up with a different Norwegian reference book
in this project's triage catalog (a compound-noun breakdown of exactly that Noun+Noun/Adjective+Noun/
etc. shape is a very standard chapter structure other Norwegian grammars use), or the orchestrator may
be thinking of a different book entirely. **Compounding is nonetheless visible incidentally** in this
book's own vocabulary: compounds like **håndhilse** ("shake hands" = **hånd** "hand" + **hilse**
"greet," a Noun+Verb-type compound verb) and **kongsgård** (from the folktale excerpt, "royal estate" =
**konge/kongs-** "king('s)" + **gård** "farm/estate," a Noun+Noun compound with a linking **-s-**
genitive-style element between the two stems) appear as simple concatenation **with** an occasional
linking element (the **-s-** in **kongsgård** functions like a fossilized genitive/linking morpheme,
comparable to German *Kompositionsfuge* linking elements), not pure root-to-root juxtaposition in every
case. This project should flag the "Compound Nouns" chapter claim to the orchestrator as unconfirmed
for *this specific book* and check whether it belongs to a different Wave 1 Norwegian source (Practical
Norwegian Grammar or Phonology of Norwegian, both being handled by sibling agents in this same wave).

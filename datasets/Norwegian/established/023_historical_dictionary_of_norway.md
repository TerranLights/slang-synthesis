## Grammar points

### LANGUAGE (pp. 123–125) — the flagship entry

This bare-headword `LANGUAGE` entry is this dictionary's equivalent of the Swedish volume's
dedicated `SWEDISH LANGUAGE` entry, and is the central deliverable of this file.

**Classification and origin.** Together with Danish, Faroese, Icelandic, and Swedish, Norwegian
belongs to the Nordic (North Germanic) branch of the Germanic language family. Old Norwegian — close
to the literary language of the medieval Icelandic sagas — is attested from both manuscript and
runic sources and emerged as a distinct language during the Viking period. It was comparatively
stable until roughly 1350, then changed considerably across phonology, grammar, and lexicon in the
centuries following the Black Death (1349), a shift the source attributes partly to population
collapse and partly to extensive Hanseatic-trader contact.

**The Danish-union period and the written/spoken split.** The union with Denmark caused the spoken
form of Old Norwegian to fragment into local dialects while the *written* language diverged in a
different direction: Danish became the language of government (reinforced under King Christian IV),
the Reformation made it the language of the church, and it remained Norway's written standard even
after the 1814 shift from a Danish to a Swedish union. Farmers and fishermen kept speaking their own
dialects; the *embedsmann* (public-official) class and other upper-class Norwegians spoke Danish "but
with more or less Norwegian pronunciation" — i.e., a diglossic class split between a Danish-derived
written/elite-spoken register and a dialect-spoken popular register, predating any formal
bokmål/nynorsk split by decades.

**The 19th-century language debate: two competing solutions.** Nation-building made retaining Danish
as the written standard untenable, and the debate crystallized around two figures:
- **Ivar Aasen** (dialectologist/linguist) argued Norway needed a *new* national language outright,
  single-handedly constructing **Landsmaal** ("country language") from the most archaic surviving
  western-Norway dialects, motivated by national romanticism and a democratizing, pro-education
  agenda — he wanted the rural population's own speech reflected in the written standard.
- **Knud Knudsen** (a teacher) instead wanted *gradual* evolution of a Norwegian written form out of
  how the upper classes already pronounced Danish in daily speech — "perhaps equally nationalistic"
  in the source's own assessment, but "both much less democratic and less likely to further popular
  education." His form was named **Riksmaal** ("national language"), later **bokmål** ("book
  language").

Most major Norwegian writers (Bjørnson, Ibsen) followed Knudsen's Riksmaal/bokmål line; Vinje and
Garborg instead developed Aasen's Landsmaal, later **nynorsk** ("New Norwegian"), as a literary
medium.

**Legal and political history of the two standards.** In 1885, the Storting gave Riksmaal and
Landsmaal equal legal status in all government contexts. In 1892, individual school districts were
empowered to choose which form to teach, and both remain available in educational materials today.
During the first half of the 20th century, under Liberal and Labor Party governments, policy pushed
toward **samnorsk** ("Common Norwegian") — merging nynorsk and bokmål into one standard — via reforms
in **1907, 1917, and 1938** (the bokmål/nynorsk terms themselves date only to 1929). The 1938 reform
drew strong resistance from users of *both* standards, who came to see samnorsk itself as a shared
enemy; the government backpedaled with **1959 and 1981** reforms restoring previously outlawed
spellings/grammar, effectively ending the samnorsk project. The **most recent reform (1 July 2005)**
abandoned the long-standing distinction between "main forms" and "allowable forms" of spelling and
grammar; the source gives one concrete illustrative case — the word for "neither," spelled *verken*
since 1959, was again permitted as the traditional ***hverken***.

**Post-samnorsk focus: loanwords.** With the samnorsk project dead, Norwegian language planning
shifted to managing new loanwords, especially from English, under the **Norsk Språkråd** (Norwegian
Language Council, est. 1972), which adapts loanword spelling to Norwegian orthographic conventions —
a major loanword-spelling reform took place in **1996**. The source also flags an ongoing debate over
**bokmål-users' education requirements in nynorsk** as a live current issue (as of 2008).

**Sámi language status (appended within the same entry).** The source treats the various Sámi
languages used across Norway, Sweden, Finland, and Russia as a *separate* language group within the
Finno-Ugric family — explicitly not a Norwegian dialect. Northern Sámi serves as a language of
municipal administration in several Finnmark County municipalities; on **1 January 2006**, Tysfjord
municipality (Nordland County) made **Lule-Sámi** co-equal with Norwegian for municipal
administration, with five further municipalities (Troms, Nordland, Nord-Trøndelag) studying similar
changes as of the source's 2008 publication date.

### AASEN, IVAR (1813–96) (p. 21)

A self-taught linguist and dialectologist from a smallholder family in western Norway, Aasen
published a Norwegian grammar in **1848** and a dictionary in **1850**, then single-handedly
constructed Landsmaal as a literary medium, used in both poetry (*Symra*, 1863) and drama (*Ervingen*,
1855). His poetry deliberately drew on Old Norse metrical conventions and archaic vocabulary/grammar
"extinct in most forms of Norwegian, even in Aasen's own day" — i.e., the source explicitly frames
Aasen's Landsmaal as more consciously archaizing than its nynorsk successor.

### NYNORSK (p. 151, cross-reference entry)

A short pointer entry confirming nynorsk has held equal legal status with bokmål since 1885, was
originally Aasen's Landsmaal, was first cultivated as a literary language by Aasen, Vinje, and
Garborg, and — the source's one hard usage statistic — is used as a primary written language by
**"less than 15 percent of the Norwegian population,"** despite prominent 20th-century literary users
(Kjartan Fløgstad, Edvard Hoem, Tor Obrestad).

### GARBORG, ARNE (1851–1924) (pp. 83–84)

Garborg's choice of Landsmaal/nynorsk over "the Danish-colored standard Riksmaal (later known as
bokmål)" is explicitly framed by the source as *one aspect of his broader radicalism*, alongside his
rejection of his rural-pietist upbringing — i.e., the dictionary ties the language choice directly to
political/ideological positioning, not just aesthetics. Garborg is credited jointly with Aasen and
Vinje as a founder of the nynorsk literary tradition.

### VINJE, AASMUND OLAFSSON (1818–70) (p. 214)

Vinje shared Aasen's and Garborg's belief that Norway needed a written language based on popular
dialects rather than the Danish-descended norm, but explicitly did **not** share "Aasen's love of
archaic grammar and vocabulary" — he based his own form of Landsmaal on the dialect of his own native
district (Telemark) rather than a composite of archaic western dialects. He was, per the source,
"the first person to extensively use" this alternative in prose, via his own newspaper *Dølen*
("Dalesman," founded 1858).

### SÁMI (pp. 175–176)

Beyond ethnography (two main Norwegian Sámi groups — northern and southern — plus small Skolte-Sámi
and Lule-Sámi populations), the entry gives explicit language-policy history: as national borders
hardened, Norway's Sámi population came under pressure to abandon language, religion, and way of
life. Early missionary Thomas von Westen wanted the Sámi taught Christianity in their own language,
but "his successors did not," and as Norway's school system developed, **Sámi children were taught
in a language they could not understand**. It was only **in 1959** that the law changed to permit
Sámi children to be taught in their native tongue — a forced-assimilation-through-education pattern
directly comparable to (though distinct in mechanism from) the Norwegian/Danish written-language
history above. Post-WWII policy reversed course: the Sámi are now recognized as an aboriginal people
with an elected assembly.

### Dialects and geography (Introduction, p. 3)

The Introduction ties Norway's dialect fragmentation directly to its geography: "only 3 percent of
Norway's surface is arable land," settlement is scattered into small, often-isolated communities by
fjords and mountains, and "a detailed map of Norwegian dialects reveals that often there will be an
isogloss... that runs right through a community with only a few hundred inhabitants." The source's
own comparative claim, worth preserving verbatim in substance: Norwegian dialects are all mutually
intelligible, "but some differ more from each other than do the national languages, Norwegian and
Swedish" — i.e., intra-Norwegian dialectal distance can exceed inter-Scandinavian national-language
distance.

### Runic-to-Latin script transition (Introduction, p. 9)

A brief historical-survey aside notes that during the reign of Olaf Haraldsson Kyrre (1066–93), who
"learned Latin and was bookish," "it became common to write the vernacular language with the Latin
alphabet" — the one appearance of the word "vernacular" anywhere in this source, marking the runic
Old Norwegian → Latin-script transition rather than any register distinction.

### National romanticism and the language debate's ideological roots (pp. 131, 143–144)

Both the **LITERATURE** entry (p. 131) and the dedicated **NATIONAL ROMANTICISM** entry (pp. 143–144)
trace the ideological lineage behind Aasen's project to German philosopher Johann Gottfried von
Herder (1744–1803), who "emphasized both the role of the nation and of language and its connection to
this divine spirit, thus giving romanticism a national slant." The dictionary explicitly groups Aasen
(constructing nynorsk from archaic dialects) alongside Welhaven, Bjørnson, Ibsen, and Vinje as
figures shaped by this same movement — situating the written-language debate as one expression of a
broader national-romantic cultural program, not an isolated linguistic dispute.

### Institutional vocabulary cluster: Storting/Lagting/Odelsting (pp. 121, 151–152, 191)

These three entries form a tight cluster of literally-glossed institutional terms — *Lagting*
("an assembly that considers laws") and *Odelsting* ("an assembly of those who possess allodial
rights to farmland") — both abolished as separate chambers by a 2007 constitutional change effective
2009, after functioning since the 1814 constitution. Useful as a concrete example of how this source
handles native-term glossing generally: a literal morpheme-level translation parenthetically embedded
in otherwise English prose, the same convention used for *Hurtigruten* (p. 181) and
*Menighetsfakultetet* (p. 171).

### The book's own Bibliography flags "Linguistics" as a distinct scholarly category (p. 264)

The Bibliography's category "5. Linguistics" lists dedicated scholarly works on Norwegian syntax,
phonology, and sociolinguistics, including **Einar Haugen's *Language Conflict and Language
Planning: The Case of Modern Norwegian*** (Harvard University Press, 1966) and Ernst Håkon Jahr's
edited *Sociolinguistics in Norway* (1995) — external confirmation, independent of this dictionary's
own entries, that the bokmål/nynorsk conflict is a recognized major topic in Norwegian linguistics
with its own dedicated literature, not an idiosyncratic emphasis of this one source.

## Flagged findings for slang-synthesis purposes

- **No direct slang/taboo/colloquial vocabulary appears anywhere in this source.** `slang` returns
  zero hits; `colloquial` returns zero hits; `vernacular` returns exactly one hit, and that single
  occurrence is about script choice (runic vs. Latin alphabet, 11th century), not register. As with
  the Swedish volume in this series, this confirms a culture/history encyclopedia of this type is not
  itself a slang source — it is a structural/historical-context source.
- **Flagship finding: the `LANGUAGE` entry's full bokmål/nynorsk history**, including hard dates for
  every major reform (1885, 1892, 1907, 1917, 1929, 1938, 1959, 1972, 1981, 1996, 2005) and the one
  genuinely concrete lexical case study the source offers (*verken*/*hverken*) — this is the clear
  structural analog to the Swedish volume's loanword-wave periodization, but for *internal* standard-
  language competition rather than external borrowing waves.
- **The pre-standardization diglossia (Danish-speaking embedsmann class vs. dialect-speaking farmers/
  fishermen) predates the formal written-standard split** and is a clean, source-explicit example of
  class-stratified register divergence worth comparing against this project's own usage-tier
  taxonomy — the embedsmann's Danish-with-Norwegian-pronunciation speech is effectively a historical
  "formal" register layered directly on top of regional dialects, with no intermediate colloquial
  national standard until well into the 19th century.
- **Explicit source-stated claim that intra-Norwegian dialectal distance can exceed inter-Scandinavian
  national distance** (p. 3) — a strong, quotable data point for later typological/sociolinguistic
  analysis about how "distinct language" boundaries don't always track dialectal divergence.
  distance.
- **Sámi again explicitly flagged as a separate (Finno-Ugric) language family, not a Norwegian
  dialect** — consistent with the Swedish volume's identical boundary-marking, now confirmed
  independently in a second country's historical dictionary in this same series, reinforcing that
  this project should never fold Sámi vocabulary into Norwegian/Swedish "regional" entries.
  Additionally, the Sámi entry's forced-assimilation-through-schooling history (resolved only in 1959)
  is a genuinely new finding not present in the Swedish sibling file, worth retaining as comparative
  context for any later contact-induced language-shift analysis.
- **No bunad/dugnad/Janteloven-style everyday cultural-vocabulary glosses appear in this source**
  (checked explicitly; zero hits) — this dictionary's native-term glossing is concentrated almost
  entirely in *political/legal/institutional* vocabulary (Storting, Lagting, Odelsting, embedsmann,
  Kongeloven) rather than everyday social vocabulary, a different emphasis from what a tourism/culture
  guide would likely supply. Worth cross-checking against this folder's other Society/Culture/Tourism
  sources, which may be the better source for that register of vocabulary.

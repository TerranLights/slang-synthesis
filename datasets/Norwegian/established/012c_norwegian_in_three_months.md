## Grammar points

### Chapter 9 — More conjunctions, Word order, Conditional, Countries/nationalities (pp.129-145)
**Subordinating conjunctions, grouped by function** (sec.46) — likely partially overlaps `002`'s
Conjunctions chapter, but this source's functional grouping and the **da/når** distinction are a
flagship, well-organized presentation: **da** = something that happened ONCE in the past (**Da vi kom
hjem, spiste vi middag**); **når** = what USUALLY happens, used to happen regularly in the past, OR
anything in the FUTURE (**Når hun kommer på besøk, har hun alltid med seg godter**; **Når du kommer frem,
må du sende meg et kort**). The book's own mnemonic: "den gang" (that one time) → **da**; "hver gang"
(every time) → **når**. Other subordinating conjunctions by function, worth keeping as a compact
reference not collected this way elsewhere in this project: TIME — **idet**(as), **mens**(while),
**før**(before), **etter**(after), **siden**(since), **inntil**(till/until); CONCESSION —
**skjønt/enda**(although), **selv om**(even if); INTENTION — **for at**(so that); CONSEQUENCE — **så
at/slik at**(so that); COMPARISON — **så...som**(as...as), **som om**(as if), **jo...desto/jo...jo**
(the...the — e.g. **Jo før, jo bedre** "The sooner the better"), **enn**(than); CAUSE —
**fordi**(because), **ettersom**(as), **siden**(since — note **siden** serves BOTH time and cause);
CONDITION — **hvis/dersom**(if), **hvis ikke**(if not), **med mindre**(unless).

**More about word order** (sec.47) — a refinement of the V2 rule introduced in Ch.5: if a SUBORDINATE
clause opens the sentence, the following main clause inverts (**Da vi kom hjem, SPISTE VI middag**); if
the MAIN clause comes first, no inversion (**Vi spiste middag da vi kom hjem**). In compound tenses, the
main clause's subject is placed BETWEEN the auxiliary and the principal verb when inverted (**Da vi kom
hjem, HADDE HAN spist middag**). Inversion also occurs after direct/quoted speech (**'Hold deg fast i
pelsen min,' sa han**).

**Conditional tense** (sec.48, **skulle/ville** + infinitive = "would") likely overlaps `002`'s own
conditional coverage; kept new: the polite-request idiom **Jeg skulle ha.../Jeg ville gjerne ha...** = "I'd
like..." **More about "if" clauses** (sec.49): **om** = "if" in the sense of "whether" (**De spurte henne
OM hun kunne komme**), distinct from **hvis/dersom** = conditional "if." "That" clauses (sec.50, optional
**at**) restate 001's existing entry — skipped.

**Countries and nationalities** (sec.51) — a comprehensive country-name list plus a systematic
nationality-adjective (**-sk** suffix, lowercase) and nationality-noun (**-e/-er/-mann** suffix,
lowercase) system, not covered elsewhere in 001-003.

**I and på with geographical names** (sec.52), explicitly flagged by the source as "not easy, even for a
native of Norway" — a genuine, practical rule set: foreign towns/countries/continents normally take **i**
(**i London, i Danmark, i Europa**); foreign ISLANDS take **på** (**på Grønland, på Island, på Cuba**);
Norwegian COASTAL towns take **i** (**i Bergen, i Tromsø**); Norwegian INLAND towns take **på** (**på
Hamar, på Lillehammer**); SMALL places usually take **på** (**på Roa, på Tynset**); places ending
**-dal/-elv/-vik** take **i** regardless (**i Oppdal, i Ulvik**); Norwegian districts/regions normally
take **i** (**i Finnmark, i Telemark**) but important exceptions take **på** (**på Østlandet, på
Vestlandet, på Toten, på Ringerike**).

### Chapter 10 — The passive voice, Active verbs with passive form, Present participle, Compound words, More i/på (pp.146-160)
**The passive voice** (sec.53) — the full **bli**-passive tense paradigm overlaps `001`'s bli-passive
note and `002`'s dedicated Passives chapter; kept as new: the full **bli**-form (**Huset blir/ble/er
blitt/var blitt/skal bli/skulle bli malt**) EMPHASIZES THE ACTION, while the shorter **være**+past-
participle form (**Huset var malt**) EMPHASIZES THE RESULT — a genuine semantic distinction not drawn
this way in 001/002. The alternative **s-passiv** (**Huset males** = "the house is being painted") is
used mainly in WRITTEN language, and is specifically PREFERRED over the bli-passive in colloquial speech
after MODAL auxiliaries (**Huset bør males**; **Billetter kan kjøpes ved inngangen**) — a specific,
register-conditioned finding.

**Active verbs with a passive form** (sec.54, deponent verbs) — a fuller set than `001`'s partial
coverage (which only had **synes/finnes**): **å synes**(think,seem), **å finnes**(be,exist),
**å lykkes**(succeed), **å trives**(thrive, feel happy — NEW), **å minnes**(remember — NEW). Past tense
adds **-tes/-des** to the stem (**syntes, fantes**[vowel change], **lyktes, trivdes, mintes**); past
participle = same form as infinitive.

**The present participle** (sec.55) — NOT covered elsewhere in 001-003, a flagship point. Formed with
**-ende** (**-d-** silent): **arbeidende, løpende, sovende, hoppende**. NOT used for a continuous tense
(unlike English "-ing"); used as ADJECTIVE (**en underholdende bok**), ADVERB (**brennende varm**="burning
hot"), or NOUN (**den besøkende**="the visitor"). TYPICAL/hallmark Norwegian construction: present
participle after verbs of motion/position **komme/gå/stå/ligge/bli**: **De kom gående/kjørende/løpende**=
"They came walking/driving/running"; **Han gikk haltende av sted**="He set off limping." English's "-ing"
sometimes must be translated by an INFINITIVE (**Han så henne komme**="He saw her coming"), a SUBORDINATE
CLAUSE (**Han brakk benet mens han spilte fotball**), or a CO-ORDINATE CLAUSE (**De stod der og så på
hverandre**="They stood there looking at each other").

**Compound words** (sec.56) — a general word-formation note not listed as a dedicated topic in 001-003's
tables of contents, directly relevant to this project's portmanteau/slang-formation interests: Norwegian
has "almost unlimited possibility of making compound words, particularly nouns," joined WITHOUT a hyphen,
sometimes with an inserted **-e-** or **-s-** linking element (**hundehalsbåndet, arbeidsbetingelser,
nyttårshilsener**).

**More about i and på** (sec.57) — extensive practical reference for ordinary (non-geographic) nouns:
**på et kontor/lager/en gård** (office/storehouse/farm) but **i en bank/butikk/et firma** (bank/shop/firm);
**i kirken/selskap/stuen** (church/party/sitting room) but **på kjøkkenet/badet/soveværelset**
(kitchen/bathroom/bedroom). A useful heuristic: **i** is often used with NEUTER nouns and **på** with
COMMON GENDER nouns, particularly for parts of a house. The SAME preposition pairs are also reused for
MOTION-towards-an-activity (**Vi går på skolen/kino/ski** vs. **i byen/butikken/banken**).

**The use of om** (sec.58) — **om** as PREPOSITION ("about," "for": **Han bad meg OM penger**="He asked
me FOR money") vs. **om** as CONJUNCTION ("if"/"whether," cross-ref sec.49).

### Chapter 11 — More time expressions, For å, More about det, Ligge/sitte/stå/gå (pp.161-170)
**More expressions of time** (sec.59) — a new idiom set: **"for...siden"** = ago (**for en time
siden**="an hour ago"); **"om"** = in/for(...yet) (**om fjorten dager**="in a fortnight," **først om tre
dager**="not for three days yet" — the SAME **om** as sec.58, now in a future-temporal sense); **"i"** =
for (duration, esp. with negative/perfect): **Streiken har vart i tre uker**="The strike has been going
on for three weeks." **"Holde på (med)"/"være i ferd med"** = continuous-action idioms filling
Norwegian's lack of a grammatical continuous tense (cross-ref sec.8): **De holder på å gjøre
lekser**="They are doing their homework"; **Han er i ferd med å bygge et nytt hus**="He is building a new
house" — confirms and extends 001's Pitfall-3 position-verb-and-"og" construction (**Hun sitter og
leser**="She is reading"). **"Å skulle til å"** = "to be about to, be on the point of."

**For å ("in order to")** (sec.60): **for å** = to/in order to/so as to (**Han gikk i banken for å heve en
reisesjekk**). But plain **(til) å** (without **for**) follows **bad om** ("asked for"): **De bad om en
skje (til) å spise med**="They asked for a spoon to eat with."

**More about det** (sec.61) — colloquial **det** used very broadly, often NOT translatable as "that": **Tror
du det?**="Do you think so?"; **Er du trett? Det er jeg også.**="Are you tired? So am I." Genuinely useful
idiomatic-**det** usage pattern not elsewhere in 001-003.

**Det as a preliminary subject** (sec.62) extends sec.37's **det er/det finnes** construction to verbs of
MOTION (**komme, gå, starte, kjøre**) and to **ligge/stå/sitte** (sec.63): **Det kom en mann nedover
veien**="There was a man coming down the road"; **Det myldrer av mennesker på gaten**="The street is
swarming with people." **Det** need not open the sentence: **Den morgenen gikk det bare en buss**="That
morning there was only one bus passing."

**The verbs ligge, sitte, stå and gå** (sec.63) — **THE FLAGSHIP grammar point of this entire source**:
these four verbs are used pervasively where English just says "to be" or "there is/are," encoding the
subject's physical orientation/state. Not elsewhere in 001-003 this systematically. **å ligge**(lie), used
for FLAT/LOW/HORIZONTAL objects/locations: **Oslo ligger ved bunnen av Oslofjorden**="Oslo is situated at
the head of the Oslofjord"; **Det ligger et supermarked på hjørnet**="There is a supermarket on the
corner" (existential-**det** + **ligge**). **å stå**(stand), for UPRIGHT/VERTICAL objects: **Det står et
glass og en flaske på bordet**="There are a glass and a bottle on the table"; **Det står i avisen
at...**="It says in the newspaper that..."; **Han stod til eksamen**="He passed his exam" (idiomatic,
non-literal). **å sitte**(sit), for something WEDGED/FITTED IN: **Nøkkelen sitter i låsen**="The key is in
the lock"; **Skipet sitter fast i isen**="The ship is stuck in the ice." **å gå**(go, walk), often with an
abstract/outcome sense: **Tiden går**="Time passes"; **Det går ham godt**="He is doing well"; **Det går
ikke**="It doesn't work"; **Hvordan gikk det på skolen i dag?**="How did you get on at school today?" This
**ligge**(horizontal)/**stå**(vertical)/**sitte**(fitted) three-way semantic split for existential-**det**
sentences is the single most distinctive, non-obvious grammar point in this whole source.

### Chapter 12 — More prepositions, Past participle as adjective, Colloquial speech, Greetings (pp.171-185)
**More about prepositions** (sec.64) — verb+preposition collocations with **av/etter/for/med/over** (**le
av**="laugh at," **være stolt av**="be proud of," **lengte etter**="long for," **klage over/på**="complain
about"). Norwegian verb+preposition expressions take the INFINITIVE where English takes "-ing" (**Hun
gledet seg til å komme**="She looked forward to coming"); some expressions have a preposition where
English has NONE, notably with BODY PARTS: **Jeg har vondt i hodet**="I have a headache"; **Vask deg i
ansiktet!**="Wash your face!" — a genuinely new idiom pattern.

**Past participle used as an adjective** (sec.65): **et malt gjerde**(a painted fence), takes **-e** in
definite/plural like other adjectives; strong-verb participles often shift to **-ne** in definite/plural
(**den stjålne vesken**); **-et**-participles usually shift **-t** to **-d** (**den ventede
virkning(en)**). Fairly standard extension of the general adjective-agreement rule.

**Colloquial speech** (sec.66) — **the richest register-marked section in the whole book**, explicitly
framed by the source as learnable "only through listening and practice." Four sub-patterns, all tagged
`colloquial` in the vocabulary table below: (1) TAG QUESTIONS appended to statements (**Han er i Norge, er
han ikke det?**="He's in Norway, isn't he?"); (2) DISCOURSE-FILLER ADVERBS used "frequently without
adding any specific meaning" — **forresten, jo, liksom, nok, så, vel, da** (**Jeg føler meg liksom (så)
svimmel.**="I feel kind of dizzy."); (3) **ja, jo, nei** as SENTENCE-OPENING INTENSIFIERS, not just
answers (**Jo, det var en snurrig fyr!**="He was [certainly] a funny chap!"; **Nei, har du hørt på
maken!**="The idea of it!"); (4) the PERSONAL PRONOUN appended in addressing people / for emphasis — a
distinctive colloquial-syntax pattern not captured in any prior Norwegian source in this project (**Morn,
du!**="Hello!"; **Du er dum, du!**="You're a fool [you are]!").

**Greetings and congratulations** (sec.67) — a practical communicative-function vocabulary set with
explicit formal/informal register pairs (formal **God dag!/God morgen!** vs. informal **Morn!/Hei!**;
formal leave-taking **Adjø!/Farvel!** vs. informal **Morn da!/Ha det!**; formal letter opening **Kjære
herr X** vs. informal **Kjære Susan**). "Please" = **Vær så snill** (polite requests) vs. **Vær så god**
(granting permission/consent).

### Appendix — Norwegian spelling (pp.198-199)
A compact consonant-doubling/dropping morphophonological rule set, genuinely distinct from `003`'s
phonology-to-orthography correspondence chapter: a long vowel is usually followed by a SINGLE consonant,
a short vowel by a DOUBLE consonant (**våt**[wet, long] vs. **vott**[mitten, short]) — with common
exceptions (**at, hvis, nok, vel**, and the modal present-tense forms **skal, kan, bør, vil**, all written
with a single consonant despite a short vowel). Double **m** NEVER appears word-finally (**dum** "foolish"
but **dumme**[plural]; **Kom!** "Come!" but **å komme** "to come"). Before an inflectional ending
beginning with a CONSONANT, a double consonant is reduced to single (**å spille→spilte**, **tykk→tykt**);
this reduction also occurs before **-ne, -ning, -sel, -ling, -sk** (**lett→å letne**, **å
brenne→brensel**, **troll→trolsk**) — important exceptions: **fullt, spisst, visste→visst**. A long vowel
sometimes shortens in an inflected form (**å lete→lette**[searched], **å se→sett**[seen], **blå→blått**).
Adjectives ending **-el, -en, -er** drop their **-e** before an **-e**-initial inflectional ending
(**edel→edle**, **doven→dovne**, **munter→muntrere→muntrest**) — the SAME **-e**-drop also applies to
NOUN plurals (**engelen→engler→englene**, **fingeren→fingre(r)→fingrene**).

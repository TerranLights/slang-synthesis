# Norwegian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Pimsleur "Norwegian I" Reading Booklets (Units Two–Thirty)

**Source:**
1. *Pimsleur Language Programs: Norwegian — Supplemental Reading Booklet* ("Compact" booklet),
   Recorded Books / Pimsleur, no stated publication year on the scanned pages (PDF metadata:
   created 2003, Acrobat Distiller 6.0). 12 PDF pages, no text layer. Covers Units Two–Ten
   (Unit Seven is explicitly marked "No Readings").
2. *Simon & Schuster's Pimsleur: Norwegian — Reading Booklet* ("Comprehensive" booklet), © and ℗
   2007 Simon & Schuster, Inc. (Pimsleur® is an imprint of Simon & Schuster Audio). Course writers:
   Luana Marie Jøsvold and Joan Schoellner; reviewer Berit S. Ahmad; Norwegian-speaking instructor
   Vebjørn Ljoså; voices Margit Svanes and Inge Berge. 20 PDF pages, no text layer. Covers Units
   Eleven–Thirty.

**Relationship between the two booklets.** These are sequential, complementary volumes of the same
Pimsleur Norwegian reading curriculum, not duplicate/condensed versions of each other. The Compact
booklet (earlier, undated — Recorded Books–era packaging) covers the reading supplement for Units
Two through Ten of "Pimsleur Norwegian I." The Comprehensive booklet (2007, Simon & Schuster) picks
up exactly where the Compact booklet ends and covers Units Eleven through Thirty — i.e., the reading
supplement for the full 30-lesson "Pimsleur Norwegian Comprehensive Level I" course. Together the two
booklets span the entire reading curriculum, Units Two through Thirty (Unit One and Unit Seven have
no reading component — Unit One per the general Pimsleur pattern of starting listening-only, Unit
Seven explicitly marked "No Readings" in the Compact booklet's own text).

**Significant internal overlap, deduplicated below.** Comprehensive Units Eleven through Twenty-One
substantially re-use the same reading-drill word lists as Compact Units Two through Ten (e.g.
Comprehensive Unit 11 ≈ Compact Unit 2; Unit 18 ≈ Unit 8; Unit 19 ≈ Unit 9), apparently because the
2007 Comprehensive edition reissued/renumbered the original 9-unit reading curriculum as the first
11 units of its larger 30-unit curriculum, adding a handful of new items per unit along the way. The
vocabulary table below lists each **distinct** term once, at its first occurrence, with a `Notes`
tag showing which booklet/unit(s) it was drilled in; recurrences are cross-referenced rather than
re-tabulated as duplicate rows, per the project's non-redundant-supplement principle. Comprehensive
Units Twenty-Two through Thirty are wholly new content with no overlap with the Compact booklet.

**Coverage note.** Every distinct vocabulary item (single reading-drill words, minimal-pair sets,
and full practice sentences/phrases) from both booklets' numbered reading lists is captured below,
genuinely all of it (not a sample) — these reading lists are themselves the pedagogical content, with
essentially no padding to skip (unlike a narrative grammar textbook, nothing here is a "repeated
drill exercise" distinct from the list itself). Both booklets' **Introduction** sections are also
captured, in the Grammar points section below, since they are the main source of explicit
sociolinguistic/register content (reading theory, regional-dialect commentary, and — critically for
this project — an explicit statement that **the readings are written in Bokmål** and pronunciation
follows the Oslo dialect). The Compact booklet's Introduction is brief, general pedagogical framing;
the Comprehensive booklet's Introduction is a substantially richer, independently-written passage
covering Norwegian's North Germanic/Old Norse ancestry, the ~5-million-speaker count, hundreds of
mutually-intelligible spoken dialects with no official spoken standard, and real historical detail on
the 1814 Danish–Norwegian split and the resulting Bokmål/Nynorsk divide — this is new content, not
redundant with the Compact booklet's intro, and both are captured.

**Vision-reading guard.** Both PDFs have no text layer (`pdftotext` returns empty/near-empty output
for both) and were fully vision-read at 200dpi, all 32 pages combined. **No handwritten marginalia of
any kind was found in either booklet** — both are clean, high-quality scans of undamaged printed
pages throughout; nothing was excluded on marginalia grounds.

**Out-of-scope material explicitly excluded.** The Comprehensive booklet's PDF (pages 17–20 of the
rendered 20-page file, separately numbered 1–10 inside the PDF itself) contains a generic, multi-page
"Pimsleur® Language Programs" User's Guide/Method insert (Dr. Paul Pimsleur's biography, "Graduated
Interval Recall," "Principle of Anticipation," "Organic Learning," general course-use instructions).
This is boilerplate common to all Pimsleur language products — it contains **no Norwegian-language
content** (no Norwegian vocabulary, grammar, or register information) — and is therefore excluded
from extraction as out of scope for this project, not as a coverage gap.

## Grammar points

### Reading-skill pedagogy framing (both booklets' Introductions)

Both booklets open by framing reading as secondary to speech: "meanings reside in the sounds of the
spoken language," and "speaking a language is the necessary first step to acquiring the ability to
read a language with meaning" (Compact, p.2). This is why the reading-drill word lists above are
largely ungrossed in the source itself — Pimsleur's method assumes the learner already knows a word's
meaning and sound from the audio lessons before ever seeing it written; the reading booklet's sole
job is to connect known sounds to their spellings, not to teach new vocabulary. This project's English
glosses above are therefore the extraction's own addition, not present in the source.

### Regional/dialect commentary and register of the written material

Both booklets' Introductions discuss regional linguistic variation (comparing American English
dialect differences — "bubbler" vs. "drinking fountain," Maine vs. Texas accents — to Norwegian's
own dialectal landscape) before turning to Norwegian specifically. **Both booklets state explicitly
that course pronunciation follows the Oslo dialect** ("we are adhering as closely as possible to the
pronunciation of the inhabitants of Oslo, the capitol of Norway," Comprehensive p.2; the Compact
booklet makes the same claim, p.3: "Pimsleur's Norwegian is recorded in the Oslo dialect of Bokmål").
**The Comprehensive booklet states explicitly that the written readings themselves are in Bokmål**
("The Readings in this booklet are written in bokmål," p.2) — this is the single most load-bearing
register fact in either booklet for this project's purposes, since it means every vocabulary item
above should be treated as standard Bokmål rather than Nynorsk or a regional dialect form (with the
single flagged exception of "sjuk," a Nynorsk/dialectal spelling variant drilled anyway for its
digraph sound).

### Bokmål vs. Nynorsk — the two written standards

The Compact booklet's intro gives a brief sketch: Bokmål ("book language," also called Riksmål) is
the Oslo-based written standard, spoken/written by roughly 80% of Norwegians and used in literature,
the national press, and broadcasting; Nynorsk ("New Norwegian"), created in the 1800s by language
scholar Ivar Aasen from a combination of western rural dialects, is still used by a minority. Both
forms are mutually intelligible and hold equal national/official status in government and schools.

The Comprehensive booklet's intro gives substantially more historical depth, independently worth
capturing rather than treating as redundant: Norwegian is a North Germanic language descended from
Old Norse, spoken by roughly 5 million people (mostly in Norway, with smaller communities in
Denmark, Sweden, Germany, the UK, Spain, Canada, and the USA) and closely related to Swedish and
Danish. There is **no official standard of spoken Norwegian** — hundreds of spoken dialects exist,
generally mutually intelligible — but there are exactly two official *written* forms: nynorsk
("new Norwegian") and bokmål ("book language"). These standards emerged specifically **after Norway
separated from Denmark in 1814**; before that, the two countries were politically united and Danish
served as the official government language, also spoken by the elite and eventually the middle
class. After independence, two competing approaches to developing an independent literary Norwegian
produced the two standards: bokmål is historically a Norwegianized variety of Danish, while nynorsk
is built from Norwegian dialects. The Comprehensive booklet gives updated percentages: **almost 75%**
of Norwegians use bokmål as their primary written language, **somewhat more than 25%** use nynorsk
(vs. the Compact booklet's rounder 80%/20% split) — both written forms are mutually intelligible, and
all Norwegians learn both in school; all government forms/publications must be made available in
both.

### Minimal-pair orthography drills: single vs. double consonant / vowel length

A recurring structural pattern across nearly every unit's word list is the minimal pair contrasting a
single consonant (marking a long preceding vowel) against a doubled consonant (marking a short
preceding vowel) — e.g. ta/takk, hat/hatt, bar/bare, sett/søt (vowel-quality contrast), bøt/bøtte,
flyte/flytte, bukke/bruke. This is the single most load-bearing orthographic rule drilled across the
whole curriculum: Norwegian vowel length is not marked by a separate diacritic but is instead
recoverable from whether the following consonant is single or doubled.

### Silent/digraph consonant clusters: hj-, gj-, skj-, sj-, kj-, hv-

A second major recurring drill pattern groups words by shared consonant-cluster spelling, each
representing a single consonant sound: **hj-** and **gj-** are both silent-first-letter clusters
realized as /j/ (hjelpe, hjelm, hjem, hjort, hjerte; gjøre, gjette, gi — the gj- cluster patterning
with plain g- before front vowels); **skj-** and **sj-** both realize the /ʃ/ ("sh") sound (skjærer,
skjønner, skyvedør; sjuk, sjokolade, sjanse, sjøsyk, sjøs — note the orthographic variety for one
phoneme); **kj-** also realizes a palatalized sound distinct from plain k- (kjelleren, kjøre, kjøpe,
kjenner, kjøtt). **hv-** is a silent-h cluster realized as /v/ (hvor, hva, hvem, hvordan, hvilken,
hvorfor). The reading booklets never state these rules explicitly in prose — they are taught purely
through repeated grouped exposure, which this extraction makes explicit for the project's mechanics-
analysis phase.

### jo vs. ja — contradicting a negative

Comprehensive Unit 17 introduces "jo" as a distinct affirmative particle from "ja" (introduced much
earlier, Compact Unit 6). Norwegian "jo" is specifically used to affirmatively contradict a negative
statement or question (where English simply repeats "yes") — e.g. "Butikkene er åpne, ikke sant?" /
"Jo, de er åpne." (Comprehensive Unit 26) functions like French "si" vs. "oui," a three-way
yes/no/contradicting-yes system that plain "ja" cannot by itself express.

### Adjective agreement (gender/number concord)

Several full-sentence drills make adjective agreement visible: neuter singular takes a -t suffix
("Det er stort," "Det er kaldt," "Det er surt," vs. the base/common-gender forms stor, kald, sur)
while plural/definite forms typically drop to the bare/weak form ("Guttene er store," "Jentene er
små," vs. singular stor/liten). The booklets never name this rule explicitly, but the paired
sentences make the pattern directly observable.

### Pain/illness expression pattern: "vondt i [body part]"

Comprehensive Unit 27's health-vocabulary cluster establishes a productive pattern for expressing
localized pain: "Han har vondt i halsen" (He has a sore throat, lit. "has pain/bad in the throat").
This "ha vondt i X" construction is the general Norwegian formula for "my X hurts" and is worth
flagging as reusable beyond the one example given.

### Numerals attested across both booklets (partial inventory)

The combined reading curriculum incidentally teaches a partial numeral system through its practice
sentences rather than a dedicated numbers lesson: to (2), tolv (12), åtte (8), åtti (80), tjue (20),
tjuesju (27), tjuefire (24, in "døgn er tjuefire timer"), femti (50), trettini (39, as "trettini
grader"), and ordinal fjerde (4th). These surface scattered across Units 9, 13, 19, 22-23, 26-27
rather than as a single consolidated numbers unit.

### Formal vs. informal/clipped register pairs

Several entries demonstrate a full-form-to-clipped-form register relationship within a single
exchange rather than across separate sources: "mobilen" (the cell, clipped) is self-corrected to
"mobiltelefonen min" (my mobile telephone, full form) in Comprehensive Unit 30, modeling exactly the
kind of colloquial clipping this project's broader slang-synthesis effort tracks. Similarly, "kona
mi" (my wife, informal postposed possessive) contrasts with the more formal available alternative
"min kone" (not itself drilled in these booklets, but implied by the possessive-postposing pattern
visible throughout, e.g. "sønnen min," "mannen min," "familien min").

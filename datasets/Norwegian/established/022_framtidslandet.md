# Norwegian — Literature Extraction: *Framtidslandet*

**Source.** *Framtidslandet* (lit. "The Land of the Future"). Co-published by Lasten Keskus
(Helsinki), Verbum Förlag (Stockholm), Religionspædagogisk Center (København), Skálholtsútgáfan
(Reykjavík), and **IKO-Forlaget (Oslo)**, 1999. ISBN 82-7112-471-4. A pan-Nordic Christian-education
Advent-calendar storybook: 37 short dated stories running from **1. desember to 6. januar
(helligtrekongersdag / Epiphany)**, framed by a wraparound sci-fi/fantasy narrative about Jubar, the
smallest "star-child" in "Tidens land" (the Land of Time), whose two friends Stella and Astrus must
collect stories to cure his illness. Each story is a separate, independently authored/translated or
adapted work (originals and translators credited per-story in the back-matter index, pp. 92–93) —
H. C. Andersen, Astrid Lindgren, Tove Jansson, Zacharias Topelius, and contemporary Nordic
children's authors all appear alongside newly commissioned Bible-story retellings. No text layer
(image-conversion PDF); every page vision-read. 94 PDF pages; printed page numbers run roughly
printed_page = PDF_page + 2 (verified against visible footer numerals).

**Vision-reading guard.** No handwritten marginalia, underlining, or pencil annotation was found on
any sampled page — this is a clean copy throughout the sampled range. All extracted content below is
genuinely typeset/printed.

**Genre/register note.** Not a grammar reference — this is children's/family storybook prose, mixing
registers deliberately: formal biblical narration, literary fairy-tale register (Andersen/Topelius
translations), playful modern-day dialogue (contemporary frame scenes with a TV, a school bus, etc.),
and anthropomorphic-animal-fable dialogue with real colloquial insults and exclamations. Because the
37 stories are independently authored/translated, **the book code-switches between Bokmål and
Nynorsk from story to story** (not marked with any [B]/[N] source tag — inferred from the text's own
grammar: Nynorsk markers are `ikkje`, `kva`, `eg`, `vere`, `gjer/gjorde`, `korleis`, `kvile`, etc. vs.
Bokmål `ikke`, `hva`, `jeg`, `være`, `gjør/gjorde`, `hvordan`, `hvile`). This is flagged explicitly in
the vocabulary table below rather than silently normalized. As a Christian-education publication,
religious/liturgical vocabulary (monastic life, prayer, nativity terms) is genuinely frequent and
substantive, not just incidental — flagged in its own vocabulary cluster below.

## Notes on coverage decisions

This is a native-language literature sample, not a grammar reference — **no grammar-points section**
is included per the project's literature-extraction scope (vocabulary/idiom/register is the target,
not exhaustive morphosyntax). The vocabulary above deliberately clusters around five findings worth
flagging explicitly for later mechanics analysis:

1. **Bokmål/Nynorsk code-switching at the story level.** Because *Framtidslandet* anthologizes 37
   independently authored/translated pieces, the written standard itself shifts story-to-story with
   no explicit source tag — this is a naturally occurring, unmarked register variable worth comparing
   against this project's existing dialect-tagged sources (e.g. the B/C/S-style corpora from other
   languages).
2. **Genuine colloquial insults and exclamations survive in a children's book**: `somlepave`, `tosk`,
   `kva er dette for noko mas`, `jaså` — none of these are watered down for the child audience.
3. **A deliberate archaism** (`fraus` for `frøs`) used for folktale atmosphere inside an otherwise
   contemporary-Bokmål story — evidence that even a 1999 children's book can reach for an older
   inflected form as a stylistic register marker.
4. **Dense, genuine religious/liturgical vocabulary** (monastic life, nativity, Epiphany) reflecting
   the IKO-Forlaget Christian-education publisher, as anticipated.
5. **Playful ad hoc compounding** (`Skrekk-øgle`, `jungelmaraton`, `musestille`) — productive
   Norwegian compounding used for comic/vivid effect in children's narrative, a possible source
   pattern for synthesized slang-formation mechanisms (`morphological_play`, `metaphor_productivity`).

No morpheme-breakdown rows were needed — Norwegian is a mildly fusional Germanic language, not
agglutinative/polysynthetic, and no word-form here hides compositional structure an atomic gloss
would obscure (compounds are glossed as compounds in the Part of Speech column instead).

## Grammar points

### Vowel/consonant inventory and the three "unique" vowels (ă, â, î)

The Introduction (pp. xiii–xvi) walks through the 31-letter Romanian alphabet. It flags **ă**
(mid-central vowel, like English "better") and **â/î** (a high-central vowel with no English
equivalent, pronounced "midway between 'ee' in keep and 'oo' in moon") as the features that most
distinguish Romanian from its Romance relatives, alongside a retained case system and the suffixed
definite article (p. x). Historical note: â was briefly replaced by î in a 1953 orthographic reform,
then reinstated in 1993 — the source gives `când` (not `cind`) as the modern standard (p. xv).

### Case/article preview (Introduction, p. x)

The Introduction previews what Units 1–2 only partly cover: Romanian retains **three distinct case
forms** (vocative, nominative/accusative, genitive/dative) and marks the definite article as a
**suffix** on the noun rather than a preceding word (`carte` "book" → `cartea` "the book").

### Three-gender system and the indefinite article (Unit 2, p. 19)

Romanian nouns are masculine, feminine, or neuter. `un` precedes masculine *and* neuter nouns; `o`
precedes feminine nouns — a first glimpse of the neuter class's "behaves like masculine" side that
this language's typology note (triage catalog) predicts will resolve into "behaves feminine in the
plural" once plural forms are covered in a later unit. Tip given by the source: feminine nouns
"often end in -a." `un`/`o` are homonymous with the numeral "one."

### Definite article as a suffix (Unit 2, pp. 20–21)

Confirms the Introduction's preview with worked examples: `poștă` "a post office" → `poșta` "the
post office"; `hotel` "a hotel" → `hotelul` "the hotel"; `american` "an American" → `americanul`
"the American." After a preposition (la, pe, etc.), the definite article is **dropped** unless the
noun is further modified by a name or adjective: `la hotel` ("at [a/the] hotel") vs. `la hotelul
Sofitel` ("at the Sofitel hotel") vs. `la poșta centrală` ("at the central post office").

### Plural formation (Unit 2, p. 19)

Plural endings vary by gender (not elaborated in full paradigm yet at this point in the book — only
examples given): `an` → `ani`, `englez` → `englezi`, `american` → `americani`, `româncă` →
`românce`. `niște` ("some") prefaces a plural noun: `niște americani` "some Americans."

### Gendered nationality/occupation adjectives (Unit 1, pp. 5–6)

Nationality and occupation words are **adjectives that agree in gender** with the person they
describe, with several distinct feminine-ending patterns observed even within this small sample:
`-oaică` (englez→englezoaică, neamț→nemțoaică), `-că` (român→româncă, american→americancă),
`-eză` (francez→franțuzoaică is irregular; suedez→suedeză is the regular pattern), and a bare `-ă`
(ziarist→ziaristă, contabil→contabilă). Unlike English, nationality words are lower-case in
Romanian; only country names keep capitals (p. 6).

### Four forms of "you" (Unit 1, pp. 7–8)

Romanian distinguishes four second-person address forms along a formality/number axis: `tu`
(familiar singular, to a relative/close friend), `dumneata`/`d-ta` (semi-formal singular, to a
colleague or subordinate), `dumneavoastră`/`dvs.` (polite, the most common form), and `voi` (plural,
two or more addressees). `tu` and `dumneata` take different verb-person agreement than
`dumneavoastră` despite both being "you" — the source flags this explicitly (p. 8) as something to
note.

### The subjunctive-marker `să` and the "want/can/must" construction (Unit 2, pp. 23–24)

Desire, ability, and necessity are expressed by linking a governing verb (`a vrea` "to want",
`a putea` "to be able to") to a dependent verb via the particle **să**, with the dependent verb
taking special **subjunctive** forms that are identical to the indicative except in the third person
singular/plural (e.g. indicative `face` "he does" vs. subjunctive `să facă` "that he do"). `trebuie`
("must, it is necessary") is **invariant** — it never changes form regardless of person — and is
followed by `să` + subjunctive: `trebuie să fac un duș` "I must take a shower" (lit. "it is necessary
that I take a shower").

### Numerals and the colloquial `-sprezece` → `-șpe` clipping (Unit 1, pp. 11–12)

Numbers 11–19 are built on `zece` ("ten") plus `-sprezece` ("-teen"); 20–90 use the pattern
`[base]zeci` optionally followed by `de` before a counted noun once the count reaches 20 or above
(`cincisprezece ani` "15 years" vs. `douăzeci de ani` "20 years" — the source's own minimal pair, p.
11). **Explicitly register-annotated by the source itself**: "in colloquial speech the -sprezece
ending is clipped to -șpe" (p. 12), giving `unșpe, doișpe, douășpe, treișpe, paișpe, cinșpe, șaișpe,
șaptișpe, optșpe, nouășpe` as the colloquial forms of 11–19 — a genuine clipping/phonological-
reduction mechanism, directly relevant to this project's slang-mechanics analysis phase.

### Negation (Unit 1, p. 12)

`nu` precedes the verb for a simple negative statement/answer. `nu` combines with `nimic`
("nothing"), `nimeni` ("no one"), and `niciodată` ("never") for negative-existential statements —
the source notes the resulting double-negative-looking construction is standard, not an error:
`Nimeni nu face nimic` lit. "No one is not doing nothing" = "No one is doing anything."

### Days of the week (Unit 2, p. 22)

All seven day names are grammatically feminine and are **not capitalized** in Romanian, unlike
English — a point the source makes explicitly.

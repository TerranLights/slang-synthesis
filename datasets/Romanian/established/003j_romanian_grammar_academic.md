## Grammar points

### Compound perfect indicative: the default past tense in speech (§4.2.1.2)

Formed with the auxiliary a avea's present (am/ai/a/am/aţi/au) plus the past participle, the compound
perfect is explicitly identified as "the tense used most frequently to express a completed past action"
in contemporary Romanian. Since Romanian has no dedicated present-perfect/continuous distinction, this
single tense covers what English splits across simple past, present perfect, and present perfect
continuous (Nu am văzut-o pe Andreea de doi ani. "I haven't seen Andrea for two years."). Modal adverbs
(mai, şi, cam, tot, mai şi) can be inserted between auxiliary and participle, each subtly coloring the
meaning (cam "a little," tot "kept on," şi "also/too").

### Imperfect indicative: stem + stressed suffix, the storytelling tense (§4.2.1.3)

Formed from the infinitive stem + a stressed suffix (-a- for 1st-conjugation and 4th-conjugation -î
verbs; -ea- for 2nd-conjugation, 3rd-conjugation, and most 4th-conjugation -i verbs; -ia- specifically
for 4th-conjugation -i verbs whose stem itself ends in a vowel, e.g. a locui → locuiam) + a shared
ending set (-m/-i/-Ø/-m/-ţi/-u). a şti and a scrie (and its derivatives) take -a- rather than the
expected -ea-/-ia-, with the resulting -ia- sequence explicitly noted as a hiatus, not a diphthong — a
subtle phonological distinction from the regular -ia- pattern. 1st-singular and 1st-plural are always
identical (eu lucram = noi lucram). Six verbs (a fi, a avea, a da, a voi [note: explicitly flagged as
the "preferred" alternative to a vrea in the imperfect], a sta) have dedicated irregular imperfect
paradigms. Functionally, the imperfect marks progressive, continuing, repeated, or habitual past action
— "the tense of storytelling" — and correlates with English past continuous, "used to," or "would"
(frequentative). Beyond its core use, it has several secondary values the source catalogs explicitly:
a past-conditional substitute in conditional clauses and unfulfilled-wish exclamations (Dacă ştiam!
"Had I known!"); a softening device for polite requests, paralleling the "I was wondering..." strategy
in English (Voiam doar să vă cer un sfat! "I just wanted to ask your advice!"); a marker of ongoing
awareness/presupposition with verbs like a şti, a crede, a considera, a bănui; a children's-speech
convention for assigning pretend roles in play (Eu eram tatăl şi tu erai mama. "I'll be the father and
you'll be the mother" — note the semantic mismatch: grammatically past, functionally future/present in
this one register); and the fixed idiom Ştiaţi că...? "Did you know that...?"

### Simple perfect indicative: a living dialectal past tense, elsewhere literary-only (§4.2.1.4)

Formed from the unstressed infinitive stem + a conjugation-specific stressed suffix (-a-/-e for 1st
conj.; -u- for 2nd conj.; -u- or -se- for 3rd conj., tracking the same split that determines past-
participle suffix choice; -i- for 4th conj. -i verbs; -â-/-î- for 4th conj. -î verbs) + the shared
ending set -i/-şi/-Ø/-răm/-răţi/-ră. a fi and a avea each have two alternate simple-perfect stem forms
(fui/fusei; avui/avusei); five further irregular verbs (a da, a lua, a sta, a bea, a vrea, a şti) have
their own dedicated paradigms. **This is the chapter's single most important sociolinguistic finding**:
the source explicitly states the simple perfect is "not frequent in standard speech" generally — the
compound perfect has displaced it almost everywhere — **except that it remains actively, currently used
in everyday speech in the southwestern regions of Romania, specifically Oltenia, and also (to a lesser
extent) Banat and western Muntenia, mostly in rural areas.** This is a genuine, source-confirmed
regional/dialectal grammatical feature, not merely a lexical one — directly relevant to this project's
regional-variation tracking. Outside that region, the simple perfect survives in standard speech mainly
for comic/surprise effect when narrating a very recently completed action, and in a softened,
more-familiar question register in the 2nd person (Gata, citirăţi? "Done, have you read them?" —
explicitly noted as milder/more familiar than the compound-perfect equivalent). Its main living use
everywhere is as **the tense of written narrative fiction**, especially after a quoted dialogue line
with speech verbs (a zice, a spune, a ţipa, a răcni, a răspunde, a preciza) in verb-subject order — the
source supports this with an extended, directly quoted excerpt from P. Cimpoeşu's 2001 novel *Simion
liftnicul* (Compania, Bucharest), the book's only contemporary literary-fiction citation, used to show
the simple perfect's narrative register directly rather than merely describe it. The chapter also
documents a parallel homograph/homonym catalog to the one already built in file `003h` §4.1.7: 1st-conj.
simple-perfect 3sg is a homograph of present-indicative 3sg/3pl (cântă); 4th-conj. -i simple-perfect 3sg
is a homograph of present-indicative 2sg (fugi); and 1st-conj. (vowel-stem) simple-perfect 1sg is a
homonym of imperfect 2sg (intrai).

### Pluperfect indicative: relational "past before a past" (§4.2.1.5)

Built by inserting -se- after the same conjugation-specific linking vowel already used for the simple
perfect (-a- 1st conj., -u- 2nd/3rd conj., -i- 4th conj. -i, -â- 4th conj. -î), plus the same ending set
as the simple perfect. a fi and a avea, plus five more irregulars (a da, a lua, a sta, a bea, a vrea, a
şti), have dedicated forms. Functionally it expresses an action completed before another past action
(explicit or merely implied/presupposed), and typically co-occurs with a specific set of temporal
adverbs (până atunci, înainte de/să/ca să, deja, niciodată).

### Three future tenses, one grammatical meaning, three distinct registers (§4.2.1.6)

Romanian's three indicative futures are **stylistic variants of the same meaning**, not semantically
distinct tenses — but the source is explicit that they occupy sharply different registers in practice:
**future 1** (voi/vei/va/vom/veţi/vor + bare infinitive, no `a`) is required in scientific, administrative,
and official register but is *not* the most common future in everyday speech; **future 2** (invariant
`o` (+ 3pl variant `or`) + present subjunctive) is explicitly flagged as "the most frequently used
future in current speech"; **future 3** (avea-based am/ai/are/avem/aveţi/au + present subjunctive) is
explicitly flagged as common specifically in "informal familiar speech," with its 1st/2nd-plural forms
rarely used in practice even there. All three share identical usage patterns: ordinary future reference,
a stand-in for English's future continuous (since Romanian lacks a dedicated progressive), relative
future-before-future or future-after-a-past-tense readings (notably, Romanian — unlike English — allows
a genuine future-tense verb after a past-tense matrix verb: Am ştiut că vei găsi răspunsul! "I knew you
would find the answer!", with no English-style backshift to a conditional), an imperative-like forceful
command reading, and a softening/politeness function for requests (Te voi ruga să... "I will ask you
to...").

### Future perfect: a literary-register relative-future tense with a secondary modal use (§4.2.1.7)

Built from future-1's own auxiliary set + fi + the past participle (voi fi lucrat), the future perfect
marks a future action completed before another future action — but the source flags it as "mostly used
in the literary style," with one of the three plain futures or even the present indicative preferred in
ordinary speech for the same meaning. It carries a secondary, specifically modal sense in literary
register: a probable/possible/presumed state located in a remote, almost mythic past (the source's own
example describes mountains that "would have risen" in a bygone era, now eroded to ocean — a genuinely
poetic register example, not a neutral grammar-textbook sentence).

### Future in the past: a tense the source itself argues for recognizing (§4.2.1.8)

Built from a avea's imperfect (aveam/aveai/avea/aveam/aveaţi/aveau) + present subjunctive, this
construction is explicitly noted by the source as historically *neglected* by traditional Romanian
grammars — some treat it as a mere periphrastic construction rather than a genuine tense — but the
author argues it deserves full tense status given its frequency, coherent meaning, and structural
parallelism to future 3 (both use an avea-based auxiliary + subjunctive, just in different tenses of
avea). Semantically it expresses an action that is future relative to a past reference point but itself
already completed relative to the moment of speech — a "future-in-the-past," occurring mainly in
literary narrative; ordinary speech instead reaches for one of the plain futures or the compound
perfect to express the same relationship.

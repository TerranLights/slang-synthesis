## Grammar points

### Second future-tense form: `voi`/`vei`/`va`/`vom`/`veți`/`vor` + bare infinitive — Unit 6, p. 68

Alongside the `o să` + subjunctive future (Unit 4), Romanian has a second, structurally distinct
future built from a dedicated auxiliary paradigm (`voi, vei, va, vom, veți, vor`) followed by the
bare infinitive (infinitive minus `a`): `voi sta` "I shall stay," `vor cădea` "they will fall." This
form reads as more formal/written than the `o să` colloquial future — consistent with its dominant
use in the weather-forecast reading passage (a register that leans formal/broadcast) versus the
`o să` future's dominance in the conversational dialogues of Units 2 and 4.

### Impersonal weather expressions — Unit 6, p. 68

Weather is expressed either via `e` ("it is") + adjective/noun (`e cald`, `e soare` lit. "there is
sun") or via dedicated impersonal (3rd-person-only, no real subject) verbs: `plouă` "it is raining,"
`ninge` "it is snowing," `bate vântul` "the wind is blowing."

### Prepositions `în` vs. `la` with place names — Unit 6, pp. 72–73

A genuinely subtle locative distinction keyed to the **speaker's own location**, not just the kind
of place: `în` ("in") is used with a locality name when the speaker is themselves in that same
locality; otherwise `la` ("in/at") is used, even though English would use "in" for both. Worked
example from the dialogue: `domnul Hodoș este în București` ("Mr Hodoș is in Bucharest" — spoken by
someone also in Bucharest) vs. `domnul Hodoș este la București` (same meaning, spoken by someone
elsewhere). With **country** names, `în` is always used regardless of the speaker's own location
(`el locuiește în Anglia` "he lives in England"), but motion "to" a country still uses `în` while
motion "to" a city/town uses `la` (`el merge în România` vs. `el merge la București`) — a genuine
locality-vs.-country-level system distinct from the simpler în/la/pe distinctions introduced earlier
in the book.

### `până` ("until," "by," "as far as") — Unit 6, pp. 72–73

A single preposition/conjunction with several distinct senses depending on construction: plain
`până` = "as far as"/"until" (`de la Arad până la Constanța` "from Arad to Constanța"); `până
când`/`până ce` ≈ "until" (near-synonyms of plain până); **`până nu`** = "until"/"before," used
specifically when the main clause is negative (`nu plec până nu vine Ion` "I'm not leaving until Ion
comes") or to mark a deadline for an action (`să mergem până nu vede ce-am făcut` "let's go before
he sees what we've done"); and `până să` = "by the time that" (`până să vină pompierii, salvarea
plecase` "by the time the firemen came, the ambulance had left"). Four distinct constructions built
on one root preposition — a productive grammatical family worth flagging for the mechanics-analysis
phase even though none of it is slang.

### Habitual/generic day-names take the definite article; months/seasons don't — Unit 6, pp. 74–75

When a day name indicates a regular/habitual occasion rather than one specific day, the definite
article is suffixed: `lunea` "on Mondays" (habitual) vs. plain `luni` "Monday" (a specific day, as
introduced in Unit 2). Months (`ianuarie`...`decembrie`) and seasons (`primăvară, vară, toamnă,
iarnă`) are, like days, lower-case and (for months) grammatically masculine; neither takes this
habitual-article treatment in the examples given.

### Dates — Unit 6, p. 75

Dates use cardinal numbers (`11 martie` "11 March") except for the 1st, which uses the ordinal
`întâi` (`întâi ianuarie` "1 January," lit. "first January") and the 2nd, which irregularly uses the
feminine ordinal form `doua` rather than a cardinal (`2 mai` = "două mai" is explicitly flagged as
non-standard by the source's own phrasing — the correct form is `doua mai`, using the ordinal "the
second," not the cardinal "two"). `Azi suntem în 5 august` and `azi e 5 august` are given as
equally valid ways to say "today is 5 August."

## Grammar points

### 6.1 Cardinal numerals (pp. 78-81)

Simple numerals run 0-10 plus every multiple of 100 (sută, mie, milion, miliard); everything else
is compound. A genuinely odd rule: **every number above 19** (including a compound number whose
final segment exceeds 19) requires the linking preposition `de` before the following noun (`40 de
mere` "40 apples"), while 19 and under take the noun directly (`18 mere`). `unu`/`doi` alone among
cardinal numerals show full gender agreement (`un`/`o`; `doi`/`două`), carrying through compounds
ending in 1 or 2 (`doisprezece băieţi` vs. `douăsprezece fete`). Cardinal numerals decline for
case: genitive via the particle `a`, dative via `la`. `Sută`/`mie`/`milion`/`miliard` behave as
ordinary count nouns (and decline fully as such) once they take the definite article. **Collective
numerals** (`amândoi`/`amândouă` "both," `ambii`/`ambele` "both") differ subtly: `amândoi`-type
forms require the following noun to carry the definite article, while `ambii`-type forms require
the noun to have no article — a genuine grammaticalized distinction between the two near-synonyms,
not free variation. **Multiplying/repetitive numerals** use either `de` + numeral + `ori` ("N
times") or the prefix `în-`/suffix `-(i)t` pattern (`întreit` "tripled"). **Fractional numerals**
behave as ordinary feminine nouns (`o pătrime` "a quarter"). **Distributive numerals** express
distribution (`câte patru` "four at a time," `doi câte doi` "two by two").

### 6.2 Ordinal numerals (pp. 81-82)

Ordinals have distinct masculine/feminine forms, built (from 2nd onward) with the possessive
article `al`/`a` fused to a numeral stem plus `-lea`/`-a` (`al doilea`/`a doua` "second"). "First"
is irregular and has **two genuinely different forms depending on position**: `întâi` (invariant
for both genders) when it follows the noun, vs. `primul`(m.)/`prima`(f.) when it precedes — the
book notes this preposed form should be compared with (and distinguished from) the ordinary
adjective `prim` ("first," one of the non-gradable absolute adjectives from Ch. 4). A set of fixed
idiomatic phrases built on ordinals is given (`clasa întâi` "first class/grade," `de prima clasă`
"top class," `în primul rând` "firstly," `la a doua mână` "secondhand").

### 7.1-7.2 Verb moods, tenses, and the 11-conjugation present indicative (pp. 83-108)

Romanian verbs have **five finite moods** (indicative, subjunctive, presumptive, conditional,
imperative) and **four non-finite moods** (infinitive, gerund, past participle, supine). The
indicative alone has present, four distinct past tenses, and three future forms plus a future
perfect. Following the Romanian Academy's 2006 grammar (which the book's author explicitly cites
as a mid-revision change she incorporated — see the Foreword), the present-tense conjugation
system recognizes **11 distinct conjugation classes**, replacing the older four-conjugation
tradition. The classifying features are: the infinitive ending (-a, -î, -i, -ea, or -e) crossed
with whether a verbal suffix (Ø/zero, -ez, -esc, -ăsc) attaches, and — for the -e-ending classes
specifically — which of three distinct past-participle endings (-ut, -s, -t) the verb takes (this
last distinction, invisible in the present tense itself, is what separates conjugations 9, 10, and
11, which otherwise conjugate identically in the present). The 11 classes, each with the book's
own paradigm verb: (1) -a, Ø (a pleca); (2) -a, -ez (a vira); (3) -î, Ø (a vârî, a very small
class); (4) -i, Ø, 3sg=3pl (a diferi); (5) -i, Ø, 1sg=3pl (a fugi — note classes 4 and 5 are
distinguished *only* by which two person-slots happen to coincide, an unusually subtle split); (6)
-i, -esc (a iubi); (7) -î, -ăsc (a urî, also very small); (8) -ea, Ø (a plăcea); (9)/(10)/(11) all
-e, Ø, distinguished solely by past-participle ending -ut/-s/-t respectively (a începe / a merge /
a frige). A closed set of **fully irregular verbs** (a fi, a avea, a vrea, a da, a sta, a lua, a
bea, a mânca, a ști) falls outside this system entirely and must be memorized per form. A further
closed irregularity: verbs ending in -ne/-ni in the infinitive (a ţine, a veni, and their prefixed
derivatives reţine/întreţine/susţine/pune/supune/expune/suprapune and preveni/surveni/interveni/
deveni) **drop the n specifically in the 2nd person singular** (ţii, vii), nowhere else in the
paradigm. The present tense also triggers a closed list of lexically-conditioned vowel shifts
(a/ă, ă/e, ă/e/a, e/a, e/ea, o/oa/u, i/e, î/i) and consonant shifts (t/ţ, d/z, s/ș, st/șt, sc/șt,
g/gi/ge, c/ci/ce) parallel in spirit to the noun-plural shifts of 2.3.4, but applying to verb-root
+ person-ending combinations instead.

### 7.2.2 The four past tenses (pp. 93-104)

**Compound past** (the default past in speech): `a avea`-auxiliary (am/ai/a/am/aţi/au) + past
participle. The past participle itself has **six distinct ending classes**, perfectly aligned with
the conjugation groupings: -at (conj. 1-2), -ut (conj. 8-9), -s (conj. 10), -t (conj. 11), -it
(conj. 4-6), -ât (conj. 3-7, with an î→â shift mirroring the gerund/infinitive's own î-placement
rule from Ch. 1). Romanian's compound past does double duty for what English splits into simple
past, present perfect, *and* present perfect continuous, since Romanian has no dedicated forms for
the latter two. **Imperfect**: built directly on the infinitive root with endings -am/-ai/-a/-am/
-aţi/-au (for -a/-î infinitives) or -eam/-eai/-ea/-eam/-eaţi/-eau (for -ea/-e/-i infinitives,
with a further -iam/-iai/-ia/... variant for -ui-ending verbs). Its uses are notably broad:
narrative storytelling tense, habitual/repeated past action ("used to"), the protasis of a
counterfactual conditional, a softened/polite imperative-substitute, and an idiomatic opener for
"Did you know...?" statements. **Simple past**: built on the past-participle stem with a dedicated
ending set (-i/-și/-ă-or-e-or-se-or-Ø/-răm/-răţi/-ră, varying slightly by conjugation). This tense
carries an explicit, genuinely valuable **regional/register annotation** from the source: the
simple past is "chiefly used in works of literature" nationally, remains a living spoken-register
form specifically "in some regions in the south-west of Romania (in Oltenia and to a lesser extent
in Banat)," and — critically — **in the rest of the country, using it in speech carries comic and
ironic overtones** (p. 98). This is exactly the kind of explicit dialectal-register fact this
project's coverage rule singles out as high-value: a single verb tense whose social meaning flips
from neutral-regional to deliberately-comic depending purely on which region the speaker is from.
**Pluperfect**: built on the past-participle stem with endings -sem/-seși/-se/-serăm/-serăţi/
-seră, expressing anteriority-to-a-past-point exactly like English "had done."

### 7.2.5 Future tense — three competing forms (pp. 104-107)

Romanian has a three-way register split for the future, unusually explicit about which is which:
**Type 1** ("formal/literary future," `voi`/`vei`/`va`/`vom`/`veţi`/`vor` + short infinitive) is
used in both spoken and written formal registers; **Type 2** ("colloquial future," `o să` + present
subjunctive) is, per the book's own words, "used extensively in spoken Romanian and in informal
writing, including newspaper articles"; **Type 3** ("popular future," present tense of `a avea` +
subjunctive) likewise "tends to be used more in spoken or informal written Romanian." This is an
unusually clean three-tier register ladder for a single grammatical function (formal-literary /
general-colloquial / popular-colloquial), worth comparing against whatever future-tense register
split other Romance languages in this project's corpus show. A **future perfect** exists
(`voi`-series + short infinitive `fi` + past participle), used exactly like English's "will have
done."

### 7.3 Presumptive mood (pp. 107-110)

Expresses uncertainty/hypothesis/conjecture ("I wonder if...," "must have..."). Two tenses
(present, past), each with a literary and a colloquial variant. The **present presumptive**
literary form is `voi`-series + short infinitive `fi` + gerund; its colloquial variant swaps in a
**reduced auxiliary set** (`oi/ei/o/om/eţi/or`) explicitly marked by the book as colloquial — note
this reduced set is formally just the literary `voi`-series with the initial `v` dropped. The
**past presumptive** is identical in form to the future perfect (literary) or to the reduced-
auxiliary-plus-fi-plus-participle (colloquial).

### 7.4 Subjunctive mood (pp. 110-114)

Marked by the invariant particle `să` before the verb. Present-subjunctive forms mostly match the
present indicative **except the 3rd person**, which is always identical between singular and
plural in the subjunctive (unlike the indicative, where 3sg/3pl can differ) — and there's a simple
flip rule: a 3sg indicative ending in -e becomes -ă in the subjunctive and vice versa (-ez→-eze,
-esc/ăsc→-ească/ască); verbs whose 3sg already ends in -ie are unchanged in the subjunctive. Uses
are broad: after verbs of volition/preference/permission/possibility/request/advice (a vrea, a
dori, a prefera, a fi interzis/permis, a trebui, a ruga, a sfătui, a recomanda, a interzice, a se
teme, a plăcea, and — distinctively — after a putea "can," where a short infinitive is also
grammatical as an alternative); after impersonal evaluative expressions (e bine, e rău, e de
așteptat, e recomandabil, e inacceptabil, e de neconceput); after compound conjunctions (fără să,
ca să, înainte să, pentru ca...să) and after numai/doar; in a closed set of fixed salutations (see
Vocabulary table: să crești mare!, să vă trăiască!, să vă fie de bine!); standalone as a softened
command ("Să nu întârzii!" = "Don't be late!"); and standalone in questions expressing doubt,
indecision, or fear ("Să mai stau?" "Should I stay longer?"). The **past subjunctive** (`să fi` +
past participle, one invariant form for all persons) is explicitly flagged as "little used in
Romanian and often replaced with other past tenses" — expressing unfulfilled past necessity/desire,
and usable together with the conditional.

### 7.5 Conditional mood (pp. 115-117)

Present conditional: dedicated auxiliary set `aș/ai/ar/am/aţi/ar` + short infinitive. Perfect
conditional: same auxiliary set + `fi` + past participle. Uses include ordinary desire/wish,
conditioned wishes, imaginary comparison (`de parcă`, `ca și cum` "as if"), politer/more indirect
requests, and — notably — a productive **inverted idiomatic construction** for emotionally
charged exclamations, where the auxiliary and reflexive/object clitic trail the verb instead of
preceding it (`Bătu-te-ar sfinţii!` "May the saints strike you!"; `Mânca-te-ar mama!`, an
affectionate parent-to-child exclamation literally "may mother eat you" — the book notes this
inversion construction can carry either hostile-curse or affectionate-endearment force depending
entirely on lexical choice, a single syntactic frame spanning opposite social registers). Romanian
conditional "if"-clauses show **free tense sequencing** not found in English/French: a present
conditional, an imperfect, and a past conditional can all substitute for one another in either
clause of a conditional sentence with the same overall meaning — the book gives five parallel
"if I had more time, I would do more things" variants differing only in which tense sits in which
clause, all equally grammatical.

### 7.6 Imperative mood (pp. 118-120)

Only 2nd person singular and plural forms exist, present tense only. The **plural** imperative
always reuses the 2nd-plural present indicative form, across every conjugation. The **singular**
imperative reuses the 3rd-singular present indicative form for conjugations 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 7, but
the **2nd-singular** present indicative form for conjugations 5, 8, 9, 10, 11 — a genuine
conjugation-class-conditioned split in which present-tense slot the imperative borrows from.
Object/reflexive pronoun clitics attach **after** the affirmative imperative, hyphenated, with
indirect-object preceding direct-object when both appear (`Dă-mi-o!` "Give it to me!"). The
negative imperative instead uses `nu` + **short infinitive** in the singular but `nu` + ordinary
2nd-plural present indicative in the plural (another singular/plural asymmetry), with clitics
inserted between `nu` and the verb rather than suffixed.

### 7.7 Infinitive (pp. 120-122)

Short form (bare, a-less: `mânca`, `plăcea`) vs. long form (a deverbal noun: `mâncare` "food,"
`plăcere` "pleasure," `iubire` "love" — i.e., the long infinitive is simultaneously the regular
way Romanian derives abstract/event nouns from verbs). Used as a sentence subject, after a
substantial set of prepositions/prepositional phrases (înainte de, fără, în loc de, pentru, noun+de),
in the idiomatic `a avea` + interrogative pattern (`Nu are ce mânca` "He has nothing to eat"), in
impersonal directions/instructions (`a se spăla după folosire` "to be washed after use" — the
infinitive-as-instruction register also found on product labels), and as the stem for the literary
future and present conditional. Clitic pronouns insert between `a` and the verb, hyphenated
(`a-i plăcea`, `a-și aminti`) — except accusative-reflexive `se`, which is not hyphenated (`a se
spăla`).

### 7.8 Gerund (pp. 122-124)

Formed by adding -ând (conj. 1, 2, 3, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11) or -ind (conj. 4, 5, 6, plus -ghea/-chea/-ia
verbs) to the infinitive root — roughly English "-ing." Negated with the prefix ne- (nespălând),
or, for "no longer doing," ne- + mai + gerund (nemaifăcând). Clitics attach after the gerund,
hyphenated, indirect-before-direct as elsewhere. Used in temporal, causal, conditional, and modal
subordinate clauses, in relative clauses, as an agreeing adjective, and directly after a
perception verb (`Îl văd trecând` "I see him passing").

### 7.9-7.10 Past participle and supine (pp. 124-126)

The past participle (one invariant form, feeding the compound past as described above) also
functions as an agreeing adjective (`greșeala...admisă` "the mistake...admitted"), in passive
constructions with `a fi`, and — with the auxiliary dropped — after certain verbs where it must
still agree with the subject (`Piesa...merită văzută` "The play is worth seeing"). The **supine**
is a distinct non-finite form built from preposition (de/la/pe/de la) + participle, covering uses
English splits across infinitives and gerunds: verb+supine (`Am de terminat` "I have to finish"),
adverb+supine (`greu de făcut` "hard to do"), causative clauses (`De atâta plâns` "because of so
much crying"), and — notably — **compound-noun formation**, where supine phrases name tools/
appliances by their function (`mașină de spălat` "washing machine," `mașină de scris` "typewriter,"
`fier de călcat` "iron," `aparat de ras` "shaver," `ochelari de citit` "reading glasses" — a fully
productive word-formation pattern, "machine/device for V-ing," paralleling the analytic compound-
noun strategy English also uses for most of the same objects).

### 7.11-7.13 Passive, reflexive, and impersonal/unipersonal/bipersonal verbs (pp. 126-130)

Passive voice: `a fi` (any tense) + past participle, optionally + `de` "by" naming the agent. The
passive past participle can also stand alone, post-nominally, as an ordinary adjective with no
auxiliary at all (`Mănăstirile construite de Ștefan cel Mare` "The monasteries built by Stephen the
Great"). **Reflexive verbs** split, as already described in 5.9, into accusative-reflexive (a
large, open, productive class of daily-activity/change-of-state verbs — a se trezi, a se spăla, a
se relaxa, a se certa, a se uita, a se căsători, a se naște, a se îndrăgosti, a se bucura, a se
îngrășa, a se angaja, a se numi, etc.) and dative-reflexive (a smaller, more lexically fixed class
of mental/possession verbs — a-și aminti, a-și lua rămas bun, a-și da seama, a-și dori, a-și
dărui). A striking three-way minimal set the book highlights explicitly: **a chema** (to call
someone) vs. **a se chema** (to be named/called) vs. **a-și chema** (to summon one's own — e.g.
soldiers) — the same root taking three distinct argument structures purely via which
reflexive-pronoun slot attaches. The **impersonal reflexive** (3rd person `se`, singular or
plural, no identifiable agent) covers agentless/passive-like constructions (`Conferinţa se va
ţine...` "The conference will be held..."). Separately, **impersonal verbs proper** (ninge "it
snows," plouă "it rains") take no subject at all; **unipersonal verbs** (merită "it's worth it,"
trebuie "must/should," ustură "it smarts") have exactly one invariant form regardless of person;
and **bipersonal verbs** (a plăcea "to like," a durea "to hurt") have exactly two forms — singular
or plural — selected by the *number of things liked/hurting*, not by the person experiencing the
liking/hurting (`îmi place cafeaua` "I like coffee" [singular object] vs. `îmi plac florile` "I
like flowers" [plural object] — both still "I," only the object's number ever changes the verb
form).

### 7.14-7.15 Sequence of tenses and verbal phrases (pp. 130-131)

Romanian reported speech keeps the tense the original speaker would have used "live," rather than
backshifting the way English/French do — the book calls the sequence of tenses "free" and purely
sense-driven. A closed list of fixed **verbal phrases** (verb + noun/pronoun/preposition/adjective,
functioning as a single idiomatic unit) closes the chapter — see the Vocabulary table above for
the individually flagged colloquial/idiomatic ones (a da ortul popii "to die," a face valuri "to
cause a stir," a lua pe cineva în râs "to mock someone," etc.).

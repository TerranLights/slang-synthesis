**Morpheme breakdown.**

> **a-și imagina** = a- (infinitive marker) + și (3rd-person dative reflexive clitic, fused to the
> infinitive marker) + imagina (verb root "imagine") — the dative-reflexive clitic is lexically
> fused to a closed set of verb infinitives that are *inherently* reflexive-dative in Romanian
> (imagina, închipui, aminti, cumpăra, dori, da seama, lua rămas bun), unlike the freely-combining
> accusative-reflexive verbs (a se spăla, etc.) where se is a separable clitic that changes per
> person. This distinction — lexically fixed dative-reflexive vs. person-inflecting
> accusative-reflexive — is a genuine compositional-structure fact the mechanics-analysis phase
> will need (see p. 63, 5.1.4, and p. 75-77, 5.9).

## Grammar points

### 3.2.5 Omission of the article, concluded (pp. 39-40)

Continuing from the previous file: the article is also dropped when a noun denotes someone's
profession, rank, or nationality (`William este prinţ` "William is a prince"; `Eu sunt româncă`
"I am a Romanian [woman]") — structurally parallel to English's own article-dropping with
predicate-nominal professions, but Romanian extends this to nationality terms where English
normally keeps the article ("I am *a* Romanian").

### 3.3 Demonstrative/adjectival article (pp. 40-42)

A distinct article (`cel`/`cea`/`cei`/`cele`, G/D `celui`/`celei`/`celor`/`celor`), historically
derived from the demonstrative `acela` "that," with several distinct grammatical jobs: (1)
nominalizing a bare adjective ("the sweet one," "the salty one"); (2) linking an epithet tightly to
a name for emphasis (`Mircea cel Bătrân`, "Mircea the Old" — the Romanian equivalent of English's
definite-article-plus-epithet royal/historical naming pattern); (3) forming the superlative of
adjectives and adverbs (`cel mai bun` "the best"); (4) replacing an omitted noun after a numeral
(`Cele două [cărţi]` "the two [books]"); (5) introducing a relative clause (`Cea care a intrat`
"the one who came in"); (6) pairing with the interrogative `care` ("which one?"); (7) substituting
for taboo/avoided proper names — the book's own two examples are `Cel de Sus` ("the One Above" =
God) and `cel cu coarne` ("the one with horns" = the devil), an explicit taboo-avoidance naming
pattern the SCB extraction pass has already found analogues of — worth flagging for the
mechanics-analysis phase as a second, independent language confirming this device.

### 3.4 Possessive article (pp. 42-43)

A small paradigm (`al`/`a`/`ai`/`ale`, agreeing with the possessed item's gender/number, not the
possessor's) with several distinct uses: forming the genitive of the interrogative/relative
pronouns `cine`/`care` (`A cui e geanta asta?` "Whose bag is this?"); forming possessive pronouns
proper (`Geanta este a mea` "The bag is mine" — see 5.3 below); forming ordinal numerals (`al
treilea` "the third"); required whenever the possessed noun does not immediately precede the
possessor (`Biroul este al colegei mele` "This is my colleague's office"); required after nouns
already modified by an indefinite adjective (`Fiecare carte a mea` "Each of my books"); and,
distinctively, an inserted `de` is required between an indefinite noun and the possessive article
(`Un prieten de-al meu` "A friend of mine" — literally "a friend of-of mine," a double-genitive-
marking construction).

### Chapter 4: Adjectives (pp. 44-58)

**4.1 Types.** Romanian adjectives fall into five distinct agreement classes by how many surface
forms they distinguish across {masculine singular, feminine singular, masculine plural, feminine/
neuter plural}: a **4-form** class (the majority: distinct M.sing/F.sing/M.pl/F.pl, e.g. `alb,
albă, albi, albe`); a **3-form** class where M.pl = F.pl (`mic, mică, mici, mici`); a related
3-form subtype for adjectives ending in -iu/-u where again M.pl = F.pl but the feminine singular
takes -ie rather than -ă (`argintiu, argintie, argintii, argintii`); a 3-form subtype for -or
adjectives where instead F.sing = F.pl (`muncitor, muncitoare, muncitori, muncitoare`); a **2-form**
class (mostly -e-ending adjectives) where singular is invariant across gender and plural is
invariant across gender (`rece, rece, reci, reci`); a unique **irregular** adjective, `vechi`
("old," of things), with F.sing distinct but all three other slots identical; and a small closed
**1-form (fully invariable)** class, dominated by colour-term loanwords (`gri, roz, bej, maro,
oranj`) plus a handful of native adjectives (`cumsecade, asemenea`). A separate closed list of
adjectives (complet, mort, enorm, excelent, exterior, extraordinar, final, inferior, iniţial,
interior, maxim, minim, deplin, optim, parţial, perfect, prim, sublim, superior, total, ultim, viu,
original, unic) **cannot be graded/compared** at all, because their meaning is already absolute —
structurally the same "ungradable absolute adjective" class English has (cannot say "more dead").

**4.2 Comparison.** Comparative superiority: `mai` + adjective (+ `decât`/`ca` + comparandum).
Comparative inferiority: `mai puţin` + adjective. Comparative equality: `la fel de` / `tot așa de` /
`tot atât de` + adjective + `ca`. Superlative (degree, not relative) is formed with intensifiers
(`foarte`, `grozav de`, `extraordinar de`, `excepţional de`, `teribil de`) — note these differ
subtly in register/intensity even though the book glosses them similarly, which a slang/register
analysis may want to probe further than this grammar does. The *relative* superlative ("the most
X") reuses the demonstrative article from 3.3 (`cel mai frumos`, `cea mai urâtă`, `cei mai buni`,
`cele mai rele`), agreeing in gender/number with the noun.

**4.3-4.4 Agreement and case.** Adjectives agree with their noun in gender, number, *and* case
(the same N/A-vs-G/D collapse seen for nouns in 2.4 recurs identically for adjectives). When one
adjective modifies nouns of mixed gender, it defaults to masculine; when it modifies nouns of mixed
number, it defaults to plural — a "masculine/plural as default/unmarked" resolution pattern.

**4.5 Position.** The default adjective position is **post-nominal** (noun-adjective, unlike
English) — `soţ iubitor` "loving husband." A closed set of fixed idiomatic phrases instead has the
adjective fixed pre-nominally (`micul ecran` "the small screen" = television; `mica publicitate`
"small ads"; `fostul șef` "the former boss"). Beyond these fixed idioms, pre-posing an adjective
for emphasis is a live, productive option — but doing so transfers the noun's definite article
(and any other inflection the noun would normally carry) onto the *adjective* instead
(`frumosul băiat` "the handsome boy," article -ul moves from `băiat` to `frumos`). Adjectives can
also stand alone in the vocative without any noun, functioning as a freestanding term of address —
the book's own examples (`Prostule!` "You idiot!", `Dragule!` "Dear!", `Scumpo!` "Sweetie!") are
directly address/insult/endearment vocabulary, exactly the kind of register-marked item this
project's later slang-synthesis phase will care about.

**4.6 Other types of adjective** (possessive, demonstrative, relative-interrogative, negative,
indefinite) are each also usable as pronouns and are described together with their pronoun
counterparts below, since the forms are largely shared — see 5.1-5.7.

- **Possessive adjectives** (4.6.1) have a dedicated person-indexed paradigm (meu/mea/mei/mele
  "my," tău/ta/tăi/tale "your," său/sa/săi/sale "his/her" — ambiguous for gender of possessor,
  nostru/noastră/noștri/noastre "our," vostru/voastră/voștri/voastre "your [pl.]," lor invariant
  "their") agreeing with the *possessed* noun's gender/number, not the possessor's. Critically for
  register tracking: the 3rd-person-feminine possessive `său/sa/săi/sale` can always be replaced
  with the invariable `ei`, and the book states explicitly **this `ei` substitution is preferred in
  colloquial speech** — an explicit register/formality annotation, with the `său`-type forms
  favored instead when possessor and sentence-subject are the same referent. The analogous
  masculine substitution uses `lui`.
- **Demonstrative adjectives** ("this"/"that"/"the same"/"the other") can precede or follow the
  noun; preposed forms are shorter (`acest copil`) while postposed forms repeat the corresponding
  pronoun (`copilul acesta`). The book flags a **separate, shorter colloquial postposed set**
  (`ăsta/asta/ăștia/ăstea` for "this," `ăla/aia/ăia/alea` for "that") used in spoken Romanian in
  place of the full acesta/acela-series forms — an explicit colloquial-register vocabulary
  doublet worth tracking.
- **Relative-interrogative adjectives** (`ce` "what," `care` "which," `cât` "how much/many") vary
  in how much of the case paradigm they inflect: `ce` is fully invariable; `care` only distinguishes
  G/D; `cât` has four singular/plural number-and-gender forms but only the plural takes G/D forms.
- **Negative adjective** `niciun/nicio` ("no/not any") has no plural N/A forms at all (only G/D
  plurals exist) — a genuine paradigm gap, not just a stylistic gap.
- **Indefinite adjectives** form a large, structurally parallel family all built on the same
  singular-uncountable-vs-plural-countable split as `cât`: `tot/toată/toţi/toate` ("all/every"),
  `mult/multă/mulţi/multe` ("much/many"), `puţin/puţină/puţini/puţine` ("little/few"),
  `atât/atâta/atâţia/atâtea` ("that much/many"), `vreun/vreo/vreunii/vreunele` ("some"),
  `oricare/fiecare` ("any"/"each," invariant N/A, declining G/D), `orice/ceva` ("any"/"some,"
  fully invariant in N/A, `orice` sharing `oricare`'s G/D forms), and `oricât/câtva` ("no matter how
  much"/"some," four forms, G/D only in plural). The uniformity of this whole family sharing one
  underlying agreement template is itself the notable structural point — a learner who grasps
  `cât`'s paradigm gets seven more adjectives largely for free.

### Chapter 5: Pronouns (pp. 59-77)

**5.1 Personal pronouns.** The full personal-pronoun paradigm distinguishes N, A, G, D (no
distinct V except `tu`/`voi` reused). Genitive/dative forms exist in three layers: **stressed**
forms (used after prepositions, for emphasis, or standalone in verb-less answers — and
*compulsory* after three specific prepositions: `datorită`, `mulţumită`, `graţie`, all meaning
"thanks to/because of"), **unstressed** forms (the default, cliticized immediately before the
verb), and **hyphenated** forms (phonologically fused to an adjacent auxiliary, gerund, imperative,
or — informally — to `să`/`nu`). The 3rd-person singular genitive has two colloquial-vs-neutral
alternatives per gender (`său`/`lui` for masc. possessor, `său`/`ei` for fem. possessor), with the
book again flagging `lui`/`ei` as the more informal variant (echoing the possessive-adjective note
above). **Double dative/accusative constructions** (a pronoun clitic *and* a full noun or stressed
pronoun both marking the same argument) are option in some contexts and **compulsory** in three:
when the sentence's main clause is introduced by a relative `care`; when the direct object is
fronted before the verb; and whenever the direct/indirect object is human (triggering simultaneous
use of the marker `pe` + stressed form on the direct object and a dative clitic on the indirect
object, even though the noun itself is also present — a genuine instance of clitic doubling as a
grammatical requirement, not just emphasis).

**5.1.4 Dative/dative-reflexive possessive construction.** Unstressed dative pronouns can stand in
for a possessive adjective plus its noun's case-marking — most notably, the **dative reflexive**
set (`îmi, îţi, își, ne, vă, își`) is *compulsory*, not optional, whenever the subject performs an
action on something they themselves possess (`Îmi spăl mașina` "I wash my [own] car," literally "to
myself I wash the car" — using the ordinary possessive adjective `mașina mea` instead would be
grammatical but marked/different).

**5.1.5 dânsul-series pronouns** — see Vocabulary table above for the explicit regional/politeness
split the source marks.

**5.2 Polite pronouns** (`dumneavoastră`, `dumneata`, `dumnealui`/`dumneaei`/`dumnealor`) — see
Vocabulary table for the register distinction the source draws between the two main forms.

**5.3 Possessive pronouns** reuse the possessive-adjective paradigm fronted by the possessive
article (`al meu`, `a mea`, `ai mei`, `ale mele` — "mine"), agreeing with the possessed item, not
the possessor, exactly like the possessive adjective.

**5.4 Demonstrative pronouns** share their stems with the demonstrative adjectives' postposed
forms (`acesta/aceasta/aceștia/acestea` "this one"; `acela/aceea/aceia/acelea` "that one";
`același/aceeași/aceiași/aceleași` "the same one"; `celălalt/cealaltă/ceilalţi/celelalte` "the
other one"), each with a distinct G/D form.

**5.5 Negative pronouns** (`nimeni` "nobody," people only, single invariant form, verb stays
singular; `nimic` "nothing," inanimate only, fully invariable; `niciunul/niciuna/niciunii/niciunele`
"not one," fully gendered/numbered) all **require** an accompanying `nu` on the verb — Romanian
negative-concord, i.e. the negative pronoun does not make the verb's own negator redundant the way
"nobody wants" (no extra "not") works in English.

**5.6 Indefinite pronouns** mirror the indefinite-adjective family almost form-for-form
(`unul/una/unii/unele` "one"; `altul/alta/alţii/altele` "another"; `tot/toată/toţi/toate`
"all/everything"; `mult/multă/mulţi/multe` "much/many [of them]"; `atât/atâta/atâţia/atâtea` "that
much/many"; `vreunul/vreuna/vreunii/vreunele` "some[one]"; the cine/care/ce-derived series
`oricine/cineva/altcineva`, `oricare/careva/altcareva/fiecare`, `orice/altceva/ceva`; and
`câtva/oricât` "some/no matter how much"), each pronoun's agreement behavior predictable once the
corresponding adjective class (4.6.5) is known.

**5.7 Interrogative-relative pronouns** (`cine` "who," `care` "which [one]," `ce` "what," `cât`
"how much/many") — `cine`'s genitive is formed periphrastically by combining the possessive
article with its dative `cui` (`al cui`/`a cui`/`ai cui`/`ale cui`, "whose," agreeing with the
possessed item exactly as elsewhere in the possessive-article system). `care` as a *relative*
pronoun linking clauses requires the marker `pe care` specifically when the antecedent — subject of
clause 1 — becomes the direct object of clause 2 (the same `pe`-marking-of-human/definite-direct-
objects pattern seen in 5.1.3).

**5.8 Emphatic pronouns** (`eu însumi` "I myself," etc.) add a fully gender/number-agreeing
emphatic particle (`însumi/însuţi/însuși/înșine/înșivă/înșiși` masc., a parallel
`însămi/însăţi/însăși/însene/însevă/înseși` fem.) after the personal pronoun purely for emphasis —
notably one of the only places in the whole pronoun system where the *speaker's own* grammatical
gender (not just the referent's) is marked, since a female speaker says `eu însămi` and a male
speaker `eu însumi`.

**5.9 Reflexive pronouns.** Romanian splits reflexives into an **accusative-reflexive** set
(`mă, te, se, se, ne, vă, se, se` — form-identical to the ordinary accusative personal pronouns
except in the 3rd person, which uses the dedicated `se`/stressed `sine`) and a **dative-reflexive**
set (`îmi, îţi, își, își, ne, vă, își, își` — identical to ordinary dative pronouns except 3rd
person `își`/stressed `sieși`). The diagnostic for "is this truly reflexive" is whether the
pronoun's grammatical person matches the verb's subject person; if not, it's an ordinary object
pronoun that happens to share a form. Accusative-reflexives pair with a closed, listed set of
body-care/daily-routine verbs (a se spăla, a se bărbieri, a se scula, a se trezi, a se îmbrăca, a se
dezbrăca, a se încălţa, a se descălţa, a se pieptăna, a se culca, a se întoarce, a se grăbi, a se
duce, a se uita, a se distra, a se bucura, a se întrista, a se întâlni, a se plimba, a se așeza, a
se pregăti, a se gândi); dative-reflexives pair with a separate closed set of mental/possession-
related verbs (a-și imagina, a-și închipui, a-și aminti, a-și cumpăra, a-și dori, a-și lua rămas
bun, a-și da seama) — see the morpheme breakdown above for why this second set is lexically fused
rather than freely combining.

## Grammar points

### 1. Demonstrative adjectives/pronouns: `acest`/`acel` and their reduced colloquial forms

*This* is `acest`, *that* is `acel`; both behave as adjectives agreeing with gender/number/case of
the noun, and — unusually for a Romanian adjective — may stand **either before or after** the noun.
Before the noun, plain forms are used (`acest prieten` "this friend"); after the noun the forms
shift (`prietenul acesta/acela`) and the following noun must itself carry the definite article,
with the post-nominal position carrying more emphasis than the pre-nominal one. Standing alone (as
demonstrative pronouns, not modifying any noun) the same `acesta`/`acela` paradigm is used and must
still agree with the referent's gender/number (`Aceasta este o problemă grea` "This is a serious
problem"). **In colloquial speech, a reduced form is far more common**: `ăsta`/`ăla` (and
feminine/plural equivalents `asta/aia`, `ăștia/ăia`, `astea/alea`) — another explicit
register-marked clipping pattern (cf. the `-sprezece`→`-șpe` numeral clipping in Unit 3 and the
`nu`→`n-` reduction in Unit 9) — pp.97–99.

### 2. Direct object pronouns: unstressed (clitic) vs. stressed forms

Romanian has a full unstressed-clitic paradigm for direct objects (`mă, te, îl, o, ne, vă, îi, le`)
which precedes the verb in the present tense but follows special rules in the past: with the past
participle, the feminine `o` moves to *after* the participle (`Noi am lăsat-o la hotel` "We left her
at the hotel"), while the other clitics contract onto the auxiliary `a avea` instead (`el m-a văzut`
"he saw me", from `el mă vede` "he sees me"). Following the subjunctive particle `să`, only `îl` and
`îi` change form (to `-l`/`-i`, attached: `Vin să-l văd` "I am coming to see him"). A **separate
stressed paradigm** exists for emphasis (`pe mine, pe tine, pe el, pe ea, pe noi, pe voi, pe ei, pe
ele`, plus reflexive `pe sine`) — these stressed forms can never replace the unstressed clitic, only
co-occur with it (`Eu o văd pe ea mâine` "I'll be seeing her tomorrow"), and the same stressed set
also follows ordinary prepositions like "with"/"for"/"in" (`Ea vine cu noi` "She is coming with
us") — pp.99–102.

### 3. `pe`: differential object marking before personal nouns

`pe` (elsewhere "on") has no direct English equivalent when it marks a noun denoting a *person* that
is the direct object of a verb — in such cases the verb is additionally, obligatorily preceded by
the matching unstressed object-pronoun clitic (`Îl întreb pe domnul Porter` "I'll ask Mr Porter",
literally "I-ask him Mr Porter"). After `pe`, the noun does **not** take the definite-article form
unless it is further qualified by a name or another modifier (`Le-am întâlnit pe fete` "I met the
girls" vs. `Le-am întâlnit pe fetele acelea` "I met those girls"). `pe` also appears before
interrogative `cine` "who" when it is the object (`Pe cine ai chemat?` "Whom did you call?") —
pp.102–103.

### 4. Two further future-tense forms (beyond the `o să` form of Unit 8)

A second colloquial future uses present-tense `a avea` + `să` + subjunctive (`am să întreb, ai să
întrebi...`), though the "we"/plural-"you" forms of this pattern are flagged as rarely heard in
practice. A third, more formal/written future drops `a` and `să` entirely, combining the bare
infinitive with a dedicated auxiliary set (`voi întreba, vei întreba, va întreba, vom întreba, veți
întreba, vor întreba`; reflexive example: `mă voi duce, te vei duce, se va duce...`) — a
register-stratified three-way future system (colloquial `o să`, colloquial `am să`, formal/written
`voi`) worth flagging for later mechanics analysis — pp.103.

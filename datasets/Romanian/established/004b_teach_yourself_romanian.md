## Grammar points

### 1. The definite article: a postposed suffix, not a separate word

Unlike English or most European languages, Romanian has **no separate word for "the"** — it is
expressed as a suffix attached directly to the end of the noun, and the suffix's shape depends on
the noun's gender, number, and final sound. This is the retained-synthetic-case-system feature
flagged in the triage catalog's morphological-typology note. Patterns given (p.57–58):

- **Masculine, consonant-final:** `românul`/`românii` ("the Romanian(s)")
- **Masculine, vowel-final:** `câinele`/`câinii`, `metrul`/`metrii`, `leul`/`leii`; irregular
  `tatăl`/`tatii` ("the father(s)")
- **Feminine in `-ă`:** `sticla`/`sticlele`
- **Feminine in `-e`:** `berea`/`berile`
- **Feminine in `-ură`:** `prăjitura`/`prăjiturile`
- **Feminine in `-ie`:** `cofetăria`/`cofetăriile`
- **Feminine in `-ea`:** `cafeaua`/`cafelele` (stem alternates `cafea-`→`cafel-` in the plural)
- **Neuter, consonant-final:** `biletul`/`biletele`, `trenul`/`trenurile`
- **Neuter, vowel-final:** `taxiul`/`taxiurile`, `biroul`/`birourile`

Prepositions of position/direction (`pe`, `spre`) still take a bare (article-less) noun even in
front of a noun that would otherwise be definite in English (`pe stradă` "on the street"), but when
the noun is further qualified the definite form reappears (`sunt la hotel` → `sunt la hotelul
București` "I am at the Hotel Bucharest"). One true exception: `cu` ("with"/"by") *can* govern a
definite-form noun directly (`cu recepționera` "with the receptionist") — p.58–59.

### 2. Addressing people: referential title vs. the vocative case

Referring to someone by title uses the definite form of the title (`domnul Popescu`, `doamna
Popescu`, `doctorul Popescu`, `președintele Popescu`). **Calling out to, summoning, or directly
addressing someone uses a distinct vocative case form instead.** Male given names typically take
vocative `-e` (`Nicu→Nicule!`, `Radu→Radule!`, `Dan→Dane!`), though some remain unchanged (`Mihai!`).
`domnul` has two different vocative outcomes depending on context: `domnule!` ("Sir!") standing
alone or before an occupation noun (`domnule profesor!` rather than the oddly-marked
`*profesore!`), vs. `doamne!` ("Lord!", a separate, religiously-marked vocative). `Tată!` "Dad!" is
flagged explicitly as an irregular/unusual vocative form. Feminine names/titles generally use the
plain indefinite form as the vocative (`doamnă Enescu!`, `mamă!`), with an alternate `-a`/`-o`
ending also possible (`Ana!`=`Ano!`, `Elena!`=`Eleno!`, `Maria!`=`Mario!`). Plural vocatives show
two competing forms, the plain plural or a `-lor` form (`băieți!`/`băieților!`,
`copii!`/`copiilor!`), while the fixed collective address `doamnelor și domnilor!` ("ladies and
gentlemen!") only occurs in the `-lor` form — p.59–60.

### 3. Reflexive verbs

A reflexive verb is formed from a verb preceded by a reflexive pronoun (`mă, te, se, ne, vă, se`) —
the `se` form is the one cited as a verb's dictionary/infinitive-adjacent citation form (`a se
spăla` "to wash oneself"). `el, ea, ei, ele` all share the single third-person reflexive pronoun
`se`. Many Romanian reflexives correspond to reflexive verbs in English too (`a se spăla` "to wash
oneself"), but the source flags **two verbs that are reflexive in Romanian without being reflexive
in English**: `a se duce` "to go" and `a se uita (la)` "to look (at)" — a genuine
cross-linguistic-mismatch finding worth tracking for later mechanics analysis. Full present-tense
paradigms given for `a se spăla`, `a se uita`, and `a se duce` — p.60–61.

### 4. The subjunctive mood

A special third-person verb form, used whenever the verb is governed by the particle `să` (usually
translating English "to"). This subjunctive form is **identical in the singular and plural** and
differs from the ordinary (indicative) form only in the third person — e.g. indicative `el are` "he
has" vs. subjunctive `el vrea să aibă` "he wants to have"; indicative `el întreabă` vs. subjunctive
`el vrea să întrebe`. `a fi` "to be" has a fully distinct subjunctive paradigm across all persons
(`vreau să fiu, vrei să fii, vrea să fie, vrem să fim, vreți să fiți, vor să fie`). Certain verbs are
conventionally followed by a `să`-clause, notably `a trebui` "to have to/must" (invariant single
present-tense form `trebuie`, e.g. `trebuie să găsim` "we must find") and `a putea` "to be able to"
(`pot să găsesc, poți să găsești, poate să găsească...`). A number of impersonal expressions also
take `să`: `e mai ușor să închiriez o mașină` "it is easier (for me) to hire a car" — p.61–63.

### 5. `mai`, `și`, `tot` — discourse particles beyond their core senses

`mai` (previously met as "more") also functions as "still"/"any longer" in questions (`mai vreți o
cafea?` "do you want another cup of coffee?", `mai stați aici?` "are you going to stay any
longer?"). `și` (previously met as "and") also functions as "too/also", with its position in the
Romanian sentence often not mapping directly onto English word order (`Ana merge la cinema. Și
George merge.` "Ann is going to the cinema. George is going too."). `tot` covers "too", "still", or
"continuously" depending on context (`Ana merge tot la cinema` "Ann is going to the cinema too";
`Ana tot merge la cinema` "Ann keeps going to the cinema"; `Ana tot nu vorbește englezește` "Ann
still doesn't speak English") — p.63.

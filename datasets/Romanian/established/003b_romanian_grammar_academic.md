## Grammar points

### Three genders, with an unusual neuter (§3.1.1)

Romanian uniquely among Romance languages retains three genders (masculine, feminine, neuter). The
neuter has no dedicated forms of its own: a neuter noun looks exactly like a masculine noun in the
singular and exactly like a feminine noun in the plural, and any adjective/pronoun/pronominal-adjective
modifying it follows the same masculine-singular/feminine-plural split. Gender assignment for inanimate
nouns is largely arbitrary (pantof "shoe" m., palton "coat" n., fustă "skirt" f. — same semantic field,
three different genders) and the dictionary (nominative singular) form alone is not a reliable gender
cue.

### Assigning gender by noun ending (§3.1.1.1.1)

The source gives a large ending-based heuristic table (reproduced above only as the vocabulary items
themselves, not the table's prose): consonant/-u endings default masculine-or-neuter; -e endings are
mostly feminine (certain suffixes — -toare, -oare, -are, -ere, -ire — reliably feminine) with a minority
masculine/neuter; -ă/-a(stressed)/-ea(-ia, stressed) endings are reliably feminine, with a small
masculine exception set for male-referent nouns (tată, popă, Papă); -i endings are gender-unpredictable;
atypical borrowed endings like -o default neuter unless the referent is animate, in which case natural
(referential) gender overrides the ending-based default.

### Assigning gender by lexical meaning/semantic class (§3.1.1.1.2)

A second, semantics-based heuristic: male-referent nouns, nationalities, and professions of men are
masculine (with the same pattern for women/feminine); most tree names are masculine (exceptions:
salcie, magnolie); mountain-chain plural names, months, and alphabet-letter/note/digit names are
masculine; most country names are feminine if ending in -a, otherwise neuter; seasons, weekdays, and
most fruit names are feminine (with named exceptions); several body-part nouns are neuter, with named
masculine (ochi, genunchi) and feminine (mână, gleznă) exceptions.

### The "one-two" (un-doi / o-două) gender test (§3.1.1.1.3)

A practical native-speaker heuristic for inanimate nouns: pair the noun with the masculine numeral set
un/doi and the feminine set o/două. If the masculine pair works for both singular and plural, the noun
is masculine; if the feminine pair works for both, it's feminine; if singular takes un but plural takes
două, the noun is neuter — this directly operationalizes the "neuter = masculine in singular, feminine
in plural" rule above.

### Plural formation (§3.1.2): four endings, pervasive stem mutation

Romanian plurals use exactly four endings — `-i`, `-le/-ele`, `-e`, `-uri` — distributed by gender and
final sound, frequently combined with the vowel/consonant mutations already documented in chapter 2.
`-i` is the single most frequent plural marker and the only ending every masculine noun takes. `-le`/
`-ele` is a minor ending largely restricted to feminine nouns (often Turkish loans) ending in stressed
`-a`/`-ea`. `-e` and `-uri` are shared between feminine and neuter nouns (the two genders' plural forms
are often literally identical in this environment, e.g. casă→case (f.) and institut→institute (n.)
share the ending, differing only in which article would attach). A number of nouns are irregular
outside this system entirely (om→oameni, zi→zile, cap→capete) and some nouns are invariant between
singular and plural (nume, ochi, un pui/doi pui).

### Case system: five cases, two surface forms, vocative distinct (§3.1.3)

Reiterating and extending chapter 2's overview specifically for nouns: nominative=accusative in form,
genitive=dative in form; the vocative is the only case with genuinely separate noun forms, and even
those are increasingly replaced by the nominative in practice, with intonation alone marking the
vocative function in contemporary speech.

### Declension with indefinite vs. definite article (§3.1.3.1)

The source gives full singular/plural declension paradigms for both article types, worked for a
masculine, a neuter, and a feminine noun. The structural point established in chapter 2 is confirmed in
tabular detail: it is almost entirely the article's form that encodes case (unui/unei/unor for the
indefinite genitive-dative; -(u)lui/-i/-lor for the definite genitive-dative, by gender/number), not the
noun stem — the one exception being feminine nouns, whose genitive-dative singular form is identical to
their own nominative-accusative plural form, independent of the article.

### The accusative marker *pe*: Romanian's differential object marking (§3.1.3.2)

A hallmark feature: the preposition `pe` optionally marks a definite/animate direct object, and its
distribution follows an animacy/definiteness/personhood hierarchy rather than a fixed rule, which the
source itself describes as "numerous and insufficiently codified." `pe` is required for: identified/
definite/known common nouns referring to persons; proper names of people or named animals; inanimate
nouns or animals used metaphorically to refer to a person (including toys/dolls treated as persons);
self-referential subject=object constructions (the proverb *Cui pe cui se scoate*, lit. "a nail drives
out a nail"); periphrastic nominal constructions using the possessive or demonstrative article as a
substitute noun. `pe` is omitted for: ordinary inanimate/non-metaphorical direct objects; generic/
unidentified/categorical person-referring nouns (professions, nationalities, "the ideal X"); definite
common nouns (including person nouns) inside a possessive-dative construction with the verb; and
proper nouns referring to places (countries, cities, rivers) even though these can take the definite
article.

### Genitive vs. dative: identical form, distinguished by governing word (§3.1.3.4)

Since genitive and dative are formally identical, Romanian relies on context — specific governing
prepositions, verbs, and adjectives — to disambiguate. The genitive (case question *al/a/ai/ale cui?*
"whose?") marks possession/origin and is placed immediately after the noun it modifies, which takes the
definite article in simple constructions; if the modified noun is instead indefinite or further modified
(by an adjective or another noun), the genitive noun itself must take a dedicated possessive/genitival
article (al, a, ai, ale) agreeing with the possessed noun's gender/number — e.g. *cartea profesorului*
"the professor's book" (simple) vs. *cartea nouă a profesorului* "the professor's new book" (modified).
A large, closed set of genitive-governing prepositions is given (asupra, contra, deasupra, înaintea,
înapoia, în faţa, în spatele, de-a lungul, de-a latul, împrejurul/în jurul, în timpul, în cursul, în
decursul, la începutul/sfârşitul/mijlocul and their de la/din variants, din cauza, în vederea, în
privinţa, cu ocazia, în fruntea, în urma, în locul, în numele) — captured in the vocabulary table above.
The dative (case question *cui?* "to whom?") marks the indirect object/recipient and is required after
a semantically coherent "giving"-type verb class (a da, a oferi, a înmâna, a dărui, a spune, a explica,
a promite, a arăta), a small set of "thanks to" prepositions (datorită, graţie, mulţumită), and certain
adjectives/comparatives (folositor, util, asemenea, conform).

### Proper names in the genitive-dative (§3.1.3.4.2)

Feminine first names ending in `-a` decline like common nouns with the definite article (Maria →
Mariei); those ending in `-ca` take a special `-căi` ending (Ilinca → Ilincăi). All masculine first
names, and feminine names not ending in `-a`, instead take the invariant proclitic article `lui` placed
before the unchanged name (lui Ion, lui Carmen). All last names likewise use `lui` (lui Popescu); when a
last name follows a title/common noun (domnul, doamna, studentul, etc.), it is the common noun that
inflects for case, not the surname.

### Vocative: formation and usage (§3.1.3.5)

Special vocative endings exist (masculine: -e, -ule, -le depending on stem shape; feminine: -o,
preceded by -ă/-a/-e → o) but their use is inconsistent and shrinking in contemporary Romanian, which
increasingly substitutes the plain nominative form plus intonation. A fixed, lexicalized set of nouns
always keeps the special vocative ending (domnule!, fiule!, omule!, prietene!, unchiule!, vere!,
bunico!/bunicule!, fetelor!, fraţilor!, doamnelor şi domnilor!, doctore!, tinere!, iubitule!/dragule!,
iubito!, and two explicitly flagged affect-laden pairs: prostule!/proasto! "you stupid (one)!" and
deşteptule!/deşteapto! "you smart one!" marked by the source itself as ironic) while another fixed set
(mamă!, tată!, copii!, băieţi!, prieteni!, doamnă!, femeie!) never takes a special ending at all. The
vocative is frequently introduced by a small set of address interjections explicitly flagged by the
source as colloquial: *măi, mă, bre, hei* — e.g. *Vino, mă, vere, încoace!* "Come here, dude!" The
adjective + noun vocative pattern (dragă prietene, iubite frate, stimate domnule, etc.) and the fuller
adjective + possessive + noun pattern (dragul meu prieten, draga mea soră) both have fully worked
paradigm tables in the source; the latter notes a real descriptive/prescriptive split still active in
contemporary usage — normative grammars prescribe the feminine plural form *dragile*, but *dragele* is
what is actually preferred in practice, an explicit register/norm-vs-usage annotation worth flagging for
the slang-synthesis phase.

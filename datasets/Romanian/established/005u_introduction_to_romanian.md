## Grammar points

### §172–175 — Past and pluperfect subjunctive
Past subjunctive: invariable *să fi* plus the past participle, used like the present subjunctive but
for completed action or what should have been done (*Să fi adus măcar sora lui* "He should have
brought at least his sister"). Pluperfect subjunctive: invariable *să fi fost* plus the past
participle; the book flags this form as used only rarely, generally for an unaccomplished past
possibility (*Ce să fi fost făcut de el?* "What might have been done with him?").

### §176–178 — Present and past conditional
Present conditional: auxiliary *aş, ai, ar, am, aţi, ar* plus the bare infinitive (parallel in
formation to the literary future from Lesson XVII, but with a distinct auxiliary series). Past
conditional: present conditional of *a fi* plus the past participle. Three use-categories for the
present conditional: (1) both parts of a less-vivid future condition (*Dacă ar veni, l-aş plăti* "If
he should come, I would pay him"); (2) both parts of a present contrary-to-fact condition; (3)
optative, expressing a wish (*Aş vrea să mă duc şi eu dar nu pot* "I should like to go too but I
can't"). Two use-categories for the past conditional: (1) both parts of a past contrary-to-fact
condition; (2) uncertainty, hearsay, or conjecture (*Se spune că ar fi sosit în oraş un om care ar
avea o mare avere* "It is said that a man who has a big fortune has arrived in town") — a genuinely
interesting evidential use of conditional morphology, directly parallel to the epistemic *o fi*
construction already documented for the future tense in Lesson XVII §158; Romanian evidently recruits
both its future and conditional paradigms for marking reported/uncertain information, a pattern worth
flagging prominently for later register-mechanics synthesis work. Additionally: when the *if*-clause
of a conditional sentence is omitted, the conclusion's construction remains unchanged regardless
(*Noroc că am plecat căci aş fi fost silit să-l văd* "Lucky I left, for I would have been forced to
see him").

### §179–180 — Gerund formation
Regular pattern: consonant-final infinitive stems (1st–3rd conjugations) add *-ând* (*schimb-ând,
put-ând, făc-ând*); a *-d*-final stem changes to *-z* before this ending (*a vede → văz-ând*); 1st-
conjugation vowel-final stems change the *-ând* vowel to *-ind* (*a apropia → apropi-ind*).
4th-conjugation verbs add *-ind* directly (*simţ-ind*). The small r-stem/-î-infinitive subclass
(*a scoborî, a hotărî*) likewise forms its gerund in *-ind* (*scobor-ind, hotăr-ind*). Irregular
gerunds given for *a bea, a rămânea, a scrie, a avea, a fi*.

### §181 — Uses of the gerund
Functions as a complement of circumstance marking time, manner, or cause (*Văzându-se acuma stăpân la
el vorbi mai tare* "Seeing himself now master in his home he spoke louder"). Critically, **the subject
of the gerund clause must be identical to the subject of the main clause** for this circumstantial use
— an important syntactic constraint distinct from English gerund/participle usage, worth flagging.
Footnote: a supporting *-u* is added before an immediately following pronoun (cross-referenced to
Lesson XII §135c).

### §182 — Agreement of gerund
In some cases the gerund has developed an adjectival function, in which case it agrees in gender and
number with the noun it modifies, e.g. *Trăia cu inima flămândă de iubire* "He lived with his heart
hungry for love" — a genuine gerund-to-adjective grammaticalization path worth flagging alongside the
other grammaticalization patterns already documented in this book (the *avea*-future from Lesson
XVII, the possessive-dative from Lesson XII).

## Grammar points

### 1. `al`/`a`/`ai`/`ale`: the possessive particle (a fourth way of expressing "of")

Beyond the genitive-case noun suffix of Unit 9 (`câinelui`, `mașinii`...), Romanian has a separate
possessive particle that agrees in gender/number with the **thing possessed** (not the possessor):
`al` (m./n. sg.), `a` (f. sg.), `ai` (m. pl.), `ale` (f./n. pl.). Used for "mine"/"yours"/etc. when
no noun directly follows (`Valiza este a mea` "The case is mine"); also used before an indefinite
noun to mean "of a"/"of" (`o prietenă de-a mea` "a friend of mine" — note the inserted `de` in this
specific construction), and before "of the"/"of a" forms of a noun generally (`noul apartament al
prietenilor noștri` "our friends' new flat"). Critically, `al` etc. is **dropped** when a definite
("the") form of a noun directly precedes another genitive-form noun (`documentele doamnei` "the
lady's documents", no `al`) — contrast `documentele importante ale doamnei` "the lady's important
documents" (where the intervening adjective forces `al`'s return). `a` alone (not the full paradigm)
is used with bare numbers: `Sunt proprietar a două apartamente` "I am the owner of two flats" —
pp.139–140.

### 2. "Whose?" and "to whom?"

*Whose?* uses `al`/`a`/`ai`/`ale` + `cui`: `Al cui este acest bagaj?` "Whose luggage is this?",
agreeing with the possessed item exactly as in point 1. *To whom?* is simply `cui` on its own
(`Cui îi trebuie un pix?` "Who needs a ballpoint pen?", literally "to-whom is-necessary a pen") —
p.141.

### 3. `care`: relative "who"/"which" and interrogative "which one"

`care` covers both the relative pronoun (introducing a clause: "the student **who** keeps looking at
me") and, in a question, "which one" (`Care zbor pleacă azi?` "Which flight is leaving today?").
When `care` is the **object** of its clause and refers to a person, it is preceded by `pe` and the
verb must additionally carry the matching direct-object clitic (`îl`, `o`, `îi`, or `le`) — the same
differential-object-marking rule from Unit 10 extended into relative clauses (`Este chiar
restaurantul pe care îl caut` "It is the very restaurant that I am looking for"). `care` also
combines with other prepositions (`cu care` "with whom", `despre care` "about which", `în care` "in
which") — pp.141–143.

### 4. `al cărui`/`a cărei`/`ai căror` etc.: relative "whose"

A relative (not interrogative) "whose", built the same way as point 1's `al`/`a`/`ai`/`ale` but
agreeing doubly — `al`/`a`/`ai`/`ale` with the thing possessed, `cărui`/`cărei`/`căror` with the
possessor (`Este hotelul al cărui lift nu merge` "It is the hotel whose lift doesn't work"). When
these stand alone denoting a person (no following noun), they take the extended forms
`căruia`/`căreia`/`cărora` — p.142.

### 5. Geographic names function as ordinary nouns

Country, river, and city names decline like regular nouns and take the genitive "of the"
construction accordingly (`capitala Italiei` "the capital of Italy", `Delta Dunării` "the Danube
Delta"). The compass-point nouns from Unit 5 (`nord, sud, est, vest`) likewise combine with a
genitive-form place name or `de` + the plain name (`Transilvania este în nordul României` /
`Toronto este la nord de New York`) — the choice tracking whether the reference point is being
treated as "contained in" vs. simply "near" the named place — p.143–146. The book provides closed
reference lists of countries (by continent), major rivers (Dunărea/Danube, Nistrul/Dniester,
Oltul/Olt, Rinul/Rhine, Volga, Tamisa/Thames), and cities (Romanian: București, Cluj, Iași,
Timișoara; European: Londra, Berlin, Paris, Roma) — not reproduced row-by-row here as a closed,
mostly-transparent-cognate reference list, per the numeral-list convention used elsewhere in this
file set.

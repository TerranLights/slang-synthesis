## Grammar points

### Personal pronouns: nominative rarely used, full accusative/dative stressed-vs-clitic split (§3.4.1)

Romanian has the familiar 8-cell personal pronoun paradigm (1st/2nd/3rd singular and plural, with a
masculine/feminine split only in the 3rd person). Because the verb ending already encodes person and
number, the nominative pronoun is mostly reserved for contrast/emphasis (Eu sunt profesoara, ea e o
studentă! "I am the professor, she is a student!"). Both the accusative and dative each have a full
stressed (full) paradigm, always introduced by a preposition when used independently, and a clitic
(unstressed) paradigm that is phonologically fused onto the verb and has several allomorphs depending
on whether the following/preceding sound is a vowel or consonant (e.g. accusative 3rd m. sg. îl/-l/l-/
-l-). As a direct object, the clitic form is used for non-human referents (Punem cartea pe masă. → O
punem pe masă.); for human referents, the clitic is used either alone or doubled with the optional
stressed form for emphasis (Mă aştepţi? Mă aştepţi pe mine?). The dative clitic likewise marks the
indirect object, with a parallel stressed/doubling pattern, and the 3rd singular dative has a
masculine/feminine split in the stressed form (lui/ei) that collapses in the clitic form (both îi).
Clitic placement relative to the verb, and the phonetic fusion rules when combined with a/am/ai/a/am/
aţi/au auxiliaries, are tense-specific (present/imperfect/simple-perfect/pluperfect: clitic precedes
the verb; compound perfect: clitic precedes/fuses with the auxiliary a avea, with the 3rd-singular
feminine accusative o being the one clitic that instead follows the verb; future 1: clitic precedes the
future auxiliary; present subjunctive: clitic is inserted between the particle să and the verb) — the
underlying rule is constant across tenses (clitic immediately adjacent to the finite/auxiliary verb
form, negation particle nu preceding the whole cluster), and the source's worked drill tables for each
tense (§3.4.1.4.1–4) are schematically summarized here rather than reproduced row-by-row.

### Disambiguating identical accusative/dative clitics by verb transitivity (§3.4.1.5)

Several clitic forms are homophonous between one person's accusative and a different person's dative
(le = accusative "them, f." or dative "to them, m./general"; îi = accusative "them, m." or dative "to
him/her"; ne, vă are each shared between their own accusative and dative). Disambiguation relies
entirely on the governing verb's argument structure/transitivity and context, not on the pronoun form
itself.

### Fixed "dative/accusative of physical-and-emotional-state" constructions (§3.4.1.6)

A large, highly productive family of idiomatic constructions pairs an accusative or dative clitic
pronoun with an impersonal verb or an impersonal use of `a fi` to express bodily sensations and
emotional states, functionally parallel to English "I am cold" but structurally "to-me it-is cold."
Accusative-pattern verbs (doare "hurts," ustură "stings/burns," mănâncă "itches" — note the idiomatic
semantic extension of the ordinary verb "to eat" into "to itch") take the experiencer as a direct
object; dative-pattern constructions (îi place "likes," îi e dor "misses," îi e poftă "feels like
eating," îi e teamă/frică "is afraid," îi e ruşine "is ashamed," îi pare bine/rău "is glad/sorry," îi e
bine/rău "feels good/bad," îi e foame/sete/somn/frig/cald "is hungry/thirsty/sleepy/cold/hot") take the
experiencer as an indirect object. This is one of the most idiom-dense grammatical templates in the
chapter and a strong candidate source of downstream colloquial/slang extensions (e.g. a-i fi poftă is
itself flagged colloquial-register above).

### Politeness pronouns: a three-tier (not two-tier) T–V system (§3.4.2)

Unlike English, and unlike the simple tu/vous-style binary of French, Romanian has (at minimum) three
distinct 2nd-person politeness registers: `tu` (familiar/informal, used with equals, younger relatives,
or — notably — in addressing God/holy figures); `dumneata` (mid-level politeness, with a colloquial
contracted variant `mata`, usable in either direction across an age gap to signal polite distance
without full formality); and `dumneavoastră` (maximal formality/politeness, used with elders, strangers,
or social superiors, and grammatically requiring 2nd-plural verb agreement even when addressing one
person — unlike dumneata, which keeps 2nd-singular agreement like tu). A fully parallel elevated-register
system exists for talking *about* third parties (dânsul/dumnealui vs. plain el), with domnia sa/domniile
lor reserved for the very highest deference (the source's own example discusses a head of state this
way). Declension of these politeness forms is irregular/limited: dumneavoastră, dumnealui, dumneaei,
dumnealor don't decline at all; dumneata has a single genitive-dative form dumitale; dânsul-class forms
decline regularly like nouns.

### Reflexive pronouns: clitic set plus a separate long form (§3.4.3)

The clitic reflexive pronoun set shares its 1st/2nd person forms with the ordinary personal-pronoun
clitics but has dedicated 3rd-person forms (se accusative, îşi dative) collapsing across
gender/number — a genuinely reflexive-specific form, unlike 1st/2nd person where "reflexive" is really
just "personal pronoun used reflexively." A separate long/stressed reflexive form exists only for the
3rd person (sine accusative, sieşi dative), used after prepositions and functionally overlapping with
the pronoun of reinforcement, and surviving in a few fossilized fixed phrases (în sinea mea "in my own
mind," în sine "in itself/per se").

### Pronouns of reinforcement: a full 8-cell "-self" paradigm with case-invariant stems (§3.4.4)

Each person/number/gender cell has a single reinforcement-pronoun stem (însumi, însuţi, însuşi, etc.)
that stays constant across case — unlike ordinary pronouns, case is marked only by what precedes it
(pe mine însumi, mie însumi, etc.), with the single exception of the 3rd-person-feminine-singular,
which does change form in the genitive-dative (însăşi → inseşi), and an optional alternate genitive-
dative plural feminine form (însele/înselor).

### Possessive adjectives vs. possessive pronouns: same stems, genitival article marks pronoun use (§3.4.5)

The possessive adjective (meu/mea/mei/mele etc.) follows its noun, which — contrary to English — takes
the definite article when modified this way (soţia mea "my wife"). The 3rd person has true possessive-
adjective forms only in the singular (său/sa/săi/sale, "one possessor"); for plural possessors the
genitive personal pronoun `lor` is used invariably instead, and even in the singular, său/sa/săi/sale
are in practice rare, generally replaced by the plain genitive lui/ei except specifically when needed
to disambiguate "his own friend" from "his [someone else's] friend." The *possessive pronoun* is
formally identical to the adjective but additionally fronted by the possessive/genitival article (al
meu, a mea, ai mei, ale mele), required whenever the possessive functions independently (as an answer,
Ale cui sunt cărţile? — Ale noastre.), when its modified noun is indefinite, when other material
intervenes between the noun and the possessive, when multiple possessive modifiers stack, or when the
possessive serves as a predicate nominal. An idiomatic extension lets the independent possessive mean
"my/your/his family/folks" (Ai mei sunt acasă. "My folks are home."). Declension of the possessive
adjective follows the regular adjectival pattern (only the feminine singular has a distinct genitive-
dative form, identical to the feminine plural) — the source gives full worked declension tables for
every person, reproduced here only as the generalization, not reproduced row-by-row.

### The "possessive dative": a poetic/informal alternative to possessive adjectives (§3.4.5.4)

An unstressed personal or reflexive dative clitic can itself carry possessive meaning when attached to
a noun or verb, rather than using a possessive adjective. With a noun directly (sufletu-mi = sufletul
meu "my soul"), this is now essentially restricted to poetic/literary registers — the source quotes
Mihai Eminescu ("Iară sufletu-mi se pierde...") and Lucian Blaga directly as its only noun-attachment
examples, an explicit literary-register annotation. Attached to a *verb* instead (pune-ţi mâinile = pune
mâinile tale "put your hands"), the construction is fully alive in contemporary standard speech and in
fact more common than the possessive-adjective paraphrase; the source flags several cases where the
possessive-adjective paraphrase is actually ungrammatical (marked with *) even though it's
semantically equivalent — e.g. *Ţi-ai luat medicamentele?* cannot be paraphrased as *Ai luat
medicamentele tale?*

### Demonstrative pronouns: proximity vs. remoteness, each with a colloquial doublet (§3.4.6.1–6.2)

Romanian demonstratives split along the same proximity/remoteness axis as English this/that, each with
a formal/literary paradigm (acesta.../acela...) and a fully parallel, extremely frequent colloquial
paradigm (ăsta.../ăla...) explicitly flagged by the source as common in actual speech — a textbook
instance of a register pair worth tracking for the slang-synthesis phase. The colloquial forms are
restricted to post-nominal/independent use and can never precede the noun they modify. The feminine
singular proximity/remoteness forms (aceasta/asta, aceea/aia) can additionally take an "indefinite"
meaning referring to an entire fact/situation rather than a concrete noun (Asta nu e treaba ta! "This
is none of your business!"), and aceea specifically fossilizes into the connector de aceea "that's
why." When followed by a relative-clause-like modifier, demonstratives of remoteness can shed their
deictic meaning and switch to the cel/cea/cei/cele forms already introduced in §3.2.2, becoming simple
noun substitutes (acela de lângă tine ↔ cel de lângă tine "the one next to you").

### Demonstrative adjectives: distinct pre- vs. post-nominal forms, and article interaction (§3.4.6.3–4)

Uniquely among Romanian adjective classes, demonstratives have formally distinct pre-nominal (acest
student) and post-nominal (studentul acesta) forms — the pre-nominal form drops the final -a. Pre-
nominal placement blocks the article on the noun entirely; post-nominal placement requires the
definite article. The colloquial ăsta/ăla-series forms are available only post-nominally. Full
case declension (nominative-accusative identical, genitive-dative identical, each with its own
colloquial-paradigm counterpart) is given for both proximity and remoteness series.

### Differentiation (celălalt) and identification (acelaşi) demonstratives (§3.4.6.5)

Two further demonstrative series round out the set: celălalt "the other (one)" (identical pronoun and
adjective forms, pre- or post-nominal, with the usual article-presence/absence split by position) and
acelaşi "the same (one)" (also pronoun=adjective in form, but — unlike celălalt — the adjective form is
obligatorily pre-nominal, never post-nominal). Both follow the same genitive-article (al/a/ai/ale)
requirement for independent/remote genitive use already established for other pronoun classes.

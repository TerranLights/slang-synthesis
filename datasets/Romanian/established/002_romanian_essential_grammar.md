## Grammar points

### 1.1-1.3 The Romanian alphabet, vowels, consonants (pp. 3-7)

Romanian uses a Latin-based alphabet with five diacritic letters not found in standard English
orthography: **ă** (schwa-like, "under"), **â**/**î** (a single central vowel written two ways —
**â** appears word-internally, **î** word-initially or -finally, except that a compound word's
second element keeps **î** if that was its original initial letter, e.g. `bineînţeles`), **ș**
("sh"), and **ț** ("ts"). **Q**, **W**, **Y**, and **K** exist only in loanwords. The letter **i**
word-finally after certain consonants (p, b, f, v, ţ, z, j, m, n, l, r) is a near-silent "soft i"
marking plurality/person rather than a full syllable — but it is pronounced as a full vowel if it
follows a consonant cluster ending in l or r (e.g. `sacri`, `codri`). **C** and **g** are "hard"
(k/g) before a, o, u, ă, î but "soft" (ch/j-like) before e, i; the hard sound before e/i is
respelled with an inserted h (`che`, `chi`, `ghe`, `ghi`).

### 1.4 Diphthongs and triphthongs (pp. 8-13)

Romanian has an unusually large diphthong inventory (listed: ea, eo, ia, ie, io, iu, oa, ua, uă,
ai, ăi, âi, ei, ii, oi, ui, au, ău, âu, eu, iu, ou) plus several triphthongs (eau, eai, oai, iai,
iau, iei, ioa). A separate category, "vowels in hiatus," looks superficially similar but is
pronounced as two separate syllables rather than one glide (e.g. `filozofie` i-e, `dialog` i-a,
`reacţie` e-a) — the distinction between a true diphthong/triphthong and hiatus is lexically fixed
per word, not predictable from spelling alone.

### 1.5 Stress and intonation (pp. 13-14)

Stress is not fixed to a particular syllable in Romanian (unlike, e.g., French's final-syllable
stress) and can be the sole distinguishing feature between two otherwise identically spelled words,
either lexically (`veselă` "happy" vs. `veselă` "dishes") or grammatically (`intră` present "he
enters" vs. `intră` simple past "he entered"). Sentence intonation otherwise parallels English:
falling pitch on statements and on questions whose answer is implied, rising pitch on open
questions.

### 2.1 Gender (pp. 15-19)

Romanian retains all three Latin genders — masculine, feminine, and **neuter**, unusual among
Romance languages (French, Spanish, Italian all collapsed to two). Neuter nouns share masculine
singular noun-endings and masculine-type singular articles, but take feminine-type plural endings
and articles — i.e., neuter is not a third fully independent declension class but a hybrid that
behaves masculine in the singular and feminine in the plural (confirming the morphological-typology
note in `00_Book_Triage_Catalog.md`). Gender correlates loosely with ending (masculine:
consonant/-u/-eu/-ău/-ou/-iu/-e/-i/-ă; neuter: largely the same endings as masculine but inanimate;
feminine: -ă/-e/-a/-ea/-ia/-i) but is lexically fixed and must be memorized per word — the book
explicitly recommends learning gender together with each new noun rather than relying on the
ending heuristic alone. Semantic classes with fixed gender: months, numerals/letter-names, most
tree names, some plant/flower names, musical notes, several town/region names, and most mountain
names are **masculine**; general inanimate objects, materials, most sports, some abstracts, colour
terms used as nouns, and supine-derived deverbal nouns (haircut, shave, washing, etc.) are
**neuter**; female beings/professions, some animals, some plants/flowers/fruit, day/time/season
names, some town names, most countries/continents, some abstracts, and most science/art names are
**feminine**.

### 2.2 Forming the feminine from a masculine noun (pp. 19-21)

A systematic but multi-pathway derivation: consonant-final → add -ă; -u → change to -ă;
nationality/origin nouns in -ian/-ean → add -că; -or/-ar/-ăr/-er (professions) → change to -iţă;
some consonant-final profession nouns → add -easă/-esă (croitor "tailor" is an irregular exception,
taking -easă instead of the expected -toare); -tor → change to -toare. A smaller reverse pattern
derives masculine from feminine via -an or -oi (gâscă "goose" → gâscan "gander"). A closed set of
suppletive (unrelated-root) pairs exists for core kinship/animal terms (bărbat/femeie,
tată/mamă, fiu/fiică, ţap/capră, câine/căţea, bou/vacă, cocoș/găină, ginere/noră, frate/soră).

### 2.3 Number (pp. 22-28)

Plural formation differs by gender and triggers frequent vowel/consonant alternations ("shifts").
**Masculine**: consonant-final nouns add -i (often triggering a final-consonant shift, e.g. d→z,
t→ţ, s→ș, st→șt, str→ștr, z→j, ă→e, â→i); -e/-u/-ă-final nouns replace the vowel with -i; most
-l-final nouns replace -l with -i, though some simply add -i; -i-final nouns are unchanged.
**Feminine**: -ă-final nouns unpredictably take -i, -e, or -uri in the plural — the book states
there is no reliable rule and recommends memorizing each noun's plural individually; -e-final nouns
usually take -i; -le → -i; vowel+ie → vowel+i; consonant+ie → consonant+ii; -oare nouns are
unchanged; -a → -ale; -ea → -ele. **Neuter**: similarly split between -e and -uri with no
reliable predictive rule (consonant-final, -i-final, and -u-final nouns can each go either way);
-iu → -ii; -o → -uri; -e-final nouns are unchanged. A separate closed list of vowel shifts
(a/ă, a/e, ă/e, â/i, ea/e, ia/ie, oa/o, o/oa) and consonant shifts (d/z, s/ș, st/șt, str/ștr, t/ţ,
c/ci-ce, g/gi-ge, z/j) recurs across all three genders' plural formation and is presented as a
unified reference list (2.3.4) rather than being re-derived per gender.

### 2.4 Case (pp. 29-32)

Romanian retains five cases from Latin: **nominative (N)**, **accusative (A)**, **genitive (G)**,
**dative (D)**, and **vocative (V)** — confirming the morphological-typology note's synthetic-case
claim. Critically, **nominative and accusative are always syncretic** (identical form in every
paradigm shown) and **genitive and dative are always syncretic** with each other too — so the
"five cases" collapse in practice to two formally distinct slots: N/A vs. G/D (plus a V that is
often also identical to G/D, see below). For a noun with an **indefinite article**, declension
happens on the article (`un`/`o`/`unui`/`unei`/`unor`), not the noun itself — except that singular
feminine nouns in G/D also take on their own plural noun-form regardless (e.g. `unei inginere`,
not `*unei ingineră`). For a noun with a **definite article**, the article fuses onto the noun as
a suffix, and the same N/A-vs-G/D collapse and feminine-singular-G/D-borrows-plural-form pattern
recur. Neuter nouns take masculine-pattern articles in the singular and feminine-pattern articles
in the plural (the hybrid-gender behavior named above, now shown to extend into the article/case
system itself, not just bare noun plurals). **Proper names**: male names take an invariant
particle `lui` before the name in G/D rather than inflecting (`lui Iulian`); female names ending
in -a decline like ordinary feminine nouns in G/D (`Nina → Ninei`), with names in -ca shifting to
-căi (`Monica → Monicăi`); female names not ending in -a (foreign or otherwise) take `lui` like
male names. Place names decline as feminine if ending in -a, masculine otherwise; some place
names are grammatically plural and decline accordingly (`Carpaţi → Carpaţilor`).

### 2.4.3 The vocative case (pp. 31-32)

Most nouns have no distinct vocative form; plural nouns of all genders reuse the G/D form for the
vocative. A few feminine singular endings exist for direct address (-o, -ă, -e) — the book flags
that the -e/-ă vocative endings on female proper names specifically "carry a regional undertone"
(an explicit register/regional annotation, not merely a stylistic note), i.e. using `Marie!`/`Ană!`
instead of the name's bare form marks the speaker as regional. Masculine singular nouns (and some
male names) take -le (`Băiatule!`, `Radule!`).

### 2.5 Compound nouns (pp. 32-33)

Compounds spelled as one orthographic word decline exactly like simple nouns. Compounds spelled as
two words (with or without a hyphen) vary in which element changes under declension/pluralization —
sometimes only the first element changes (`un papă lapte` → `doi papă lapte`), sometimes the second
(`un ardei gras` → `doi ardei grași`), sometimes both (`un făt-frumos` → `doi feţi-frumoși`), and
the book gives no general predictive rule, only examples — this is lexically fixed per compound.

### 3.1 Indefinite article (pp. 34-36)

Forms: `un` (m./n. sing.), `o` (f. sing.), `niște` (all genders, plural), with G/D forms `unui`
(m./n. sing.), `unei` (f. sing.), `unor` (all genders, plural). Beyond the ordinary "a/an/some"
use, the book lists several extended uses worth flagging for a slang/synthesis project: (2) placed
before a proper name to generify it into "a [person like X]" (`Un Eminescu nu se naște în fiecare
zi` — "a[nother] Eminescu isn't born every day"); (3) placed before a surname to mean "someone
called X" when the speaker doesn't know the person well (`Te caută un Popescu`); (4) attachable to
other parts of speech (adverbs, interjections) when they're being used nominally (`un ţâr-ţâr la
ușă`, literally "a ding-ding at the door"); (5) before an artist's surname to mean "a work by X"
(`un Ţuculescu original`); (6) meaning "a pair of" with inherently plural-form nouns like
`pantaloni` "trousers."

### 3.2 Definite article (pp. 36-41)

Unlike the indefinite article (a separate preposed word), Romanian's definite article is
**postposed and suffixed directly onto the noun** — the hallmark synthetic feature named in the
triage catalog's morphological note (`om` "man" → `omul` "the man"). Forms vary by gender/number/
final-segment of the noun: masculine singular takes -l, -ul, -le, or -a; masculine plural always
-i; feminine singular -a or -ua; feminine plural always -le; neuter singular -ul, -l, or -le;
neuter plural always -le (again showing neuter borrowing the feminine plural pattern). Extended
uses beyond ordinary definiteness: with unique referents (sun, etc.); with proximally-known
referents; with place/people names; with fronted/nominalized adjectives; with other
nominalized parts of speech; with country/continent/river names; with honorific titles (`Domnul
Borza`); with day-of-week nouns used adverbially to mean "on [day]s habitually" (`Lunea merg la
dans` "On Mondays I go dancing" — note `luni` "Monday" + definite article `-a` → `lunea`, an
adverbial-via-definite-article pattern); for generic/universal statements (`Îmi plac mult
cactușii` "I love cacti" — generic statements take the definite article in Romanian where English
uses the bare plural); and obligatorily with the preposition `cu` ("with") when denoting a means
of transport or instrument (`Mănânc supa cu lingura` "I eat soup with [the/a] spoon" — definite
article required even though English would use "a spoon").

### 3.2.5 Omission of the article (pp. 39-40+, truncated at chunk boundary)

The definite/indefinite article is dropped in several contexts: nouns expressing an indefinite
quantity/number (`La piaţă sunt cumpărători și vânzători` "There are buyers and sellers at the
market" — no article on either plural noun); after a preposition when the noun is otherwise
unqualified (except `cu`, which is handled specially); after `cu` when the noun denotes a mass/
matter rather than an instrument (`pâine cu unt` "bread with butter" — contrast the instrumental
`cu lingura` above, which does take the article); after indefinite/interrogative adjectives,
numerals, or certain demonstratives (`Orice om`, `Cât timp`, `trei copii`, `Acest oraș`); and when
the noun denotes mass/matter generically. *(This grammar point continues past the chunk boundary
read for this file — the remainder of 3.2.5 plus 3.3-3.4 (demonstrative/adjectival and possessive
articles) is covered in the next file, `002b_romanian_essential_grammar.md`.)*

# Romanian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Teach Yourself Romanian, Units 1–5

**Source:** Dennis Deletant & Yvonne Alexandrescu, *Teach Yourself Romanian* (Hodder Education,
1992/2003 edition), Units 01–05 (printed pages 1–51). Covers: `01 buna ziua` (hello), `02 punand
intrebari` (asking), `03 sa ne prezentam` (talking about yourself), `04 cum sa ajung la` (asking the
way), `05 cat e ceasul?` (what is the time?).

**Coverage note:** every grammar point and every distinct vocabulary item from the "Key words and
phrases" boxes of these five units, plus supplementary vocabulary introduced in grammar
explanations/example tables (nationality/occupation pairs, numbers, adjective paradigms). Repeated
drill exercises and duplicate example sentences that don't introduce new vocabulary or grammar are
skipped. Cultural-note boxes ("Greeting Romanians", "Mineral water", "Romania and the Romanians",
"Hungarians and Germans in Romania") are paraphrased only where they introduce vocabulary, per
copyright discipline — not reproduced.

**Vision-reading guard:** this source has **no text layer** (confirmed via `pdftotext` sampling
across multiple page ranges) — every page was vision-read from rendered page images. **The PDF is
scanned as two-printed-pages-per-image spreads**, not one page per image (140 PDF page-images cover
~273 printed pages); the printed-page-to-PDF-page mapping was empirically verified (not assumed from
a fixed-offset formula) by sampling PDF pages at pages 10, 20, 40, 70, 100, 135, and 140 against the
book's own table of contents, confirming the relation `left_printed_page = 2×pdf_page − 8` holds
consistently across the whole book. No handwritten marginalia was found on any page read for this
file — all content is clearly typeset print, consistent font throughout. Scan quality is good
(clean 1990s/2000s offset-print scan, no binding-gutter shadow or faded print encountered in this
range).

### Numbers (21–1,000,000)

Extracted structurally rather than row-by-row (a closed, fully regular numeral series): 21–30
`douăzeci și unu/una, douăzeci și doi/două, douăzeci și trei ... treizeci`; tens 40–90 `patruzeci,
cincizeci, șaizeci, șaptezeci, optzeci, nouăzeci`; 100 `o sută`, 200 `două sute`, 1,000 `o mie`,
2,000 `două mii`, 1,000,000 `un milion`, 2,000,000 `două milioane`. From 20 upwards, compound
numbers linking to a noun take `de` (e.g. `douăzeci de lei` "20 lei") — p.25, p.47.

## Grammar points

### 1. Silent final *i*

As a general rule, word-final *-i* is not pronounced in Romanian (e.g. `vorbiți`, `scuzați`,
`faceți`) — p.7.

### 2. Intonation as the main question marker

Romanian marks a simple information question (e.g. starting with `unde`) by raising pitch on the
last syllable, rather than inverting word order. A yes/no question raises pitch on the key word
being questioned (`Aveți cafea?`); a plain statement has a slight falling pitch at the end — p.9,
p.16.

### 3. Negation

The particle `nu` precedes the verb: `sunt român` "I am Romanian" → `nu sunt român` "I am not
Romanian" — p.15.

### 4. The Romanian vowel and consonant system

Romanian gives roughly equal stress weight to each syllable of a word. Words that are spelled like
English cognates are pronounced differently (e.g. `important`, `parking`, `taxi`, `computer`).
Vowels `ă`, `â`, `î` carry diacritics that both distinguish homographs and mark distinct sounds; `â`
and `î` represent the same sound (no close English equivalent, between the vowels of "crude" and
"creed"). Notable consonant values: `c`/`g` before `i`/`e` are soft (≈ "ch"/"j"); `ch`, `gh` are hard
("k", hard "g"); `j` = "zh" (as in "leisure"); `r` is trilled; `ș` = "sh"; `ț` = "ts" — pp.10–11.

### 5. Indefinite article and the three-gender system

*A*/*an* becomes `un` (masculine/neuter) or `o` (feminine). Romanian nouns are classified
masculine, feminine, or neuter; word-final shape is a rough (not absolute) guide — words ending in a
consonant tend toward `un` (m. or n.), words ending in a vowel tend toward `o` (f.), though
exceptions exist on both sides (e.g. `un câine` "a dog" is masculine despite ending in a vowel; `un
hotel` is neuter despite ending in a consonant). The source advises learning each noun together
with its article as the practical strategy — p.16–17.

### 6. Plural formation (first pass, expanded in Unit 3)

Broadly: masculine nouns take `-i`; feminine nouns take `-e` or `-i`; neuter nouns take `-e` or
`-uri` — p.17. Unit 3 systematizes this into a fuller rule set by final segment: masculine nouns
ending in a consonant often shift the final consonant when adding `-i` (`copil→copii`, `rus→ruși`);
those ending in a vowel replace it with `-i` (`metru→metri`, `leu→lei`); feminine nouns in `-ă`
pluralize with `-e` or `-i` (`casă→case`, `gară→gări`); those in `-e` replace it with `-i`
(`pâine→pâini`, `carte→cărți`); those in `-ură` take `-uri` (`prăjitură→prăjituri`); those in `-ie`
take `-ii` (`cofetărie→cofetării`); those in `-ea` take `-ele` (`cafea→cafele`); neuter nouns in
`-ou` take `-uri` (`birou→birouri`); neuter nouns in `-iu` take `-ii` (`fotoliu→fotolii`) — p.17,
pp.23–24.

### 7. Gender-marked occupation/nationality nouns

Feminine nouns of occupation/nationality are usually derived from the masculine, but the derivation
pattern is irregular enough to require memorizing pairs rather than a single rule: `profesor→
profesoară`, `englez→englezoaică`, `american→americancă`, `doctor→doctoriță`, `inginer→inginera`.
After the verb "to be" and when stating nationality, the indefinite article is dropped entirely:
`sunt ziarist` "I am a journalist", `sunt român` "I am Romanian" — p.24.

### 8. `cât` as a gender/number-agreeing interrogative

`cât` "how much/how many" inflects like an adjective: `cât` (m.sg.), `câtă` (f.sg.), `câți` (m.pl.),
`câte` (f./n. pl.) — p.25.

### 9. Numbers 11–20 and colloquial clipping

11–19 are formed with `-sprezece` ("-teen") suffixed to the base digit (`unsprezece`,
`doisprezece`/`douăsprezece` gender-marked, etc.); 20 is `douăzeci`. **In colloquial speech the
`-sprezece` ending is clipped to `-șpe`** (`unșpe`, `doișpe`, `treișpe`...) — an explicit
register-marked clipping pattern, directly relevant to this project's slang-mechanics interest —
p.25.

### 10. Subject pronouns and the four-way "you" system

Subject pronouns (`eu, tu, el, ea, noi, voi, ei, ele`) are used mainly for emphasis, since verb
endings already mark person. `ei` (they, masc.) is used for any mixed-gender group as long as at
least one member is masculine; `ele` (they, fem.) only for all-feminine groups. Romanian has **four**
distinct second-person forms, graded by register/social distance: `tu` (intimate — family, close
friends, children), `dumneata`/`d-ta`/`mata` (familiar-professional or addressing a subordinate, can
carry a scolding tone), `voi` (plural, familiar), `dumneavoastră`/`dvs.` (formal/respectful,
singular or plural, default when in doubt). `tu` and `dumneata` take singular verb agreement; `voi`
and `dumneavoastră` take plural agreement even when addressing one person — p.34–35.

### 11. Present tense of `a fi` (to be), `a avea` (to have), `a sta` (to stay), `a merge` (to go), `a lua` (to take)

Full present-tense paradigms given for all five verbs (p.35–36). English "do/does/is/are" auxiliary
meanings are not translated separately — `merg` alone covers "I go" / "I do go" / "I am going".
`este` has a colloquial reduced variant `e`, which itself reduces to `-i` after a vowel-final word
(`ce este?` → colloquial `ce-i?`). `nu` reduces and hyphenates before forms of `a avea`: `n-am,
n-ai, n-are, n-avem, n-aveți, n-au` — p.36.

### 12. Prepositions and the bare noun

Prepositions like `pe` "on" and `spre` "towards" do not take an article in Romanian the way English
"the" often appears with its noun: `pe stradă` = "on the street" (not "on *a* street"); contrast `pe
o stradă` "on a street" — p.37.

### 13. Verb conjugation classes (four types)

Romanian verbs fall into four conjugation classes by their infinitive ending: `-a` (most common,
e.g. `a întreba` "to ask"; a large sub-group ends `-a` with a `-ez-` present-tense infix, e.g. `a
lucra` → `lucrez`), `-ea` (rare, e.g. `a vedea` "to see"), `-e` (e.g. `a merge` "to go"), `-i` (e.g.
`a dori` "to wish"; a few irregular-pattern verbs like `a ști` "to know" also conjugate as `-i`-type
with stem `ști-`). In the `-a` type, the "he/she" and "they" forms are identical; in the other three
types, the "I" and "they" forms are identical instead — p.44–45.

### 14. Further uses of the present tense

Beyond ongoing/habitual action, the present tense also covers (a) near future ("he's going
tomorrow" = plain present + a time adverb) and (b) an action/state that began in the past and
continues into the present, where elapsed duration is marked with `de` (`Stăm aici de cinci ani`
"We have been living here for five years") — contrast with English, which would use a perfect
construction — p.45.

### 15. Adjective agreement (first systematic pass)

Romanian adjectives normally follow the noun (unlike English) and must agree in gender and number.
Three paradigm sizes occur: **4-form** adjectives (distinct m.sg./f.sg./m.pl./f.&n.pl., e.g.
`alb/albă/albi/albe`); **3-form** adjectives (m.sg.=n.sg. distinct from f.sg., with m.pl. distinct
from f.&n.pl., e.g. `mic/mică/mici`, and a sub-pattern in `-tor` deriving from verbs where f.sg. and
f.pl. share one form, e.g. `obositor/obositoare/obositori/obositoare`); and **2-form** adjectives
(one form for singular across all genders, one for plural, e.g. `mare/mari`, `dulce/dulci`) —
pp.45–46.

### 16. Numbers 21 and beyond

21–30 follow `douăzeci și X`; tens 40–90 are regular (`patruzeci`...`nouăzeci`); hundreds/thousands
are regular multiples of `sută`/`mie`; `de` links a number ≥20 to its noun. Romanian uses `,`/`.`
conventions as in the examples given (e.g. `12.353` = 12,353) — p.47.

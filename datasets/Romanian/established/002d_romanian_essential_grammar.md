## Grammar points

### 8.1 Adverb formation (pp. 132-133)

Most Romanian adverbs are simply the masculine singular adjective used unchanged (`frumos` as both
"beautiful" and "beautifully"). A dedicated manner-adverb suffix -ește attaches to nouns/adjectives
(`românește` "in Romanian [manner]," `bărbătește` "like a man") — note this suffix productively
derives "in the manner of [nationality/social-role noun]" adverbs, a pattern worth comparing
against how other languages in this project express the same "manner of X" semantic slot. Smaller
suffixes -iș/-âș form a closed set (`morţiș` "by all means," `cruciș` "cross-eyed," `târâș`
"crawling"). Compound adverbs are freely built from preposition+noun, conjunction+pronoun, or
pronoun/adverb+adverb (dinafară, oriunde, altădată, azi-noapte). A long list of fixed, often
vivid, **idiomatic adverbial phrases** closes the section (fără îndoială "without doubt," în vecii
vecilor "for all eternity," nici în ruptul capului "not for the love of God," cu chiu cu vai "by
hook or by crook," de-a valma "higgledy-piggledy," pe de rost "by heart," among others) — several
of these are flagged individually in the Vocabulary table above as colloquial/idiomatic.

### 8.2 Comparison of adverbs (pp. 133-135)

Structurally identical to adjective comparison (Ch. 4): equality via la fel de/tot așa de/tot atât
de...ca; superiority via mai...decât/ca; inferiority via mai puţin...decât/ca; relative superlative
via cel mai...din/dintre/de la; absolute superlative via intensifying adverbs (foarte, grozav de,
extraordinar de, nemaipomenit de, incredibil de) — the same intensifier-register ladder noted for
adjectives in Ch. 4 recurs here.

### 8.3 Semantic categories and uses of adverbs (pp. 135-136)

The book organizes its adverb inventory by semantic function: place, time, modality, quantity,
agreement, negation, interrogative, and relative. Two points of cross-linguistic interest for the
later synthesis phase: the agreement category includes `ba da` ("yes," specifically used *in
response to a negative-form question* — a distinct lexical item from plain `da`, paralleling
French `si`); and the negation category includes `nicidecum` ("no way, absolutely not") alongside
plain `nu`, `niciodată` "never," `niciunde` "nowhere," `nici` "neither" — all of which participate
in Romanian's negative-concord system (cross-ref 5.5, where negative pronouns likewise require a
co-occurring `nu`).

### 8.4 The five "always-preposed" adverbs: mai, și, tot, prea, chiar (p. 136)

A small closed set of adverbs are grammatically required to precede the word they modify (never
follow): `mai` ("still/any more/again" — reading its sense off context: `Mai mergi la înot?` "Do
you still go swimming?" vs. `Nu mai mănânci?` "Are you done eating?"), `și` ("too/also," distinct
from its use as the conjunction "and"), `tot` ("still/as well"), `prea` ("too [much]"), and `chiar`
("even/really/[pronoun]-self" — `Chiar Marius m-a sunat` "Marius himself called me"). Several of
these are highly polysemous and sense-disambiguated only by context, which is worth flagging for
the morphological/mechanics-analysis phase as a place where a single short grammatical word carries
a lot of interpretive weight.

### 8.5 Adverbs governing the genitive (pp. 136-137)

A closed set of locational/causal adverbial phrases take the definite article and are followed by
a genitive-case noun, effectively functioning as compound prepositions (la spatele "at the back
of," în faţa "in front of," înaintea "before," în urma "following," deasupra "above," dedesubtul
"under," în jurul "around," din cauza "because of") — the book treats these as a bridge into
Chapter 9's prepositional-phrase inventory, since the surface behavior (definite-article-marked
adverb + genitive noun) is functionally identical to a prepositional phrase.

### Chapter 9: Prepositions (pp. 138-142)

Romanian prepositions are classified by which case they govern: **accusative**-governing (pe, la,
lângă, sub, cu, fără, după, spre, în, din, până, despre, către, prin, de — the large majority),
**genitive**-governing (asupra, contra, deasupra, dedesubtul, împotriva, înaintea), and **dative**-
governing (datorită, mulţumită, graţie, conform, contrar, asemenea — notably, this is the same
closed set of three "thanks to" prepositions from 5.1.2 that *require* the stressed dative pronoun
form when their object is a pronoun). **Compound prepositions** combine two prepositions or a
preposition with a noun/adverb (pe la, de pe, până la, dinspre, printre, împrejurul). A genuinely
notable case-governing subtlety for the preposition `cu` ("with"), echoing the article-omission
rule from 3.2.5: when `cu` denotes substance/matter, its noun takes **no article** (`cafea cu
zahăr` "coffee with sugar"); when `cu` denotes an instrument or means, its noun **must** take the
definite article (`cu autobuzul` "by bus," lit. "with the bus"). A separate orthographic rule:
`în`, `din`, `prin` followed by the indefinite article contract and are written with an elision
apostrophe (`într-un oraș` not `în un oraș`). Section 9.4 catalogs fixed verb+preposition
collocations by governing preposition (cu, de, despre, la, asupra, contra, prin, în, din) — a
substantial closed list functioning much like English phrasal-verb-preposition pairings, worth
comparing against the mechanics-analysis phase's eventual cross-linguistic collocation data.

### Chapter 10: Conjunctions (pp. 143-148)

**Coordinating conjunctions** split into four semantic types: copulative (și, nici, și...și,
nici...nici, nu numai...ci și, atât...cât), adversative (dar, însă, iar, ci), disjunctive (sau,
ori, sau...sau, fie...fie, ori...ori), and conclusive (deci, așadar, prin urmare, în concluzie).
**Subordinating conjunctions** are organized into eleven functional categories — cause (pentru că,
fiindcă, deoarece, dat fiind că, having a notable register gradient from the plain pentru
că/fiindcă up to the more formal dat fiind că/având în vedere că), consequence (încât, că, cât, de,
să, ca să), concession (deși, chiar dacă, cu toate că), condition (dacă, de, cu condiţia să, în
cazul că), purpose (pentru ca să, cu scopul ca), time (după ce, în vreme ce, imediat ce, înainte
să, o dată ce, ori de câte ori), manner (cu cât...cu atât "the more...the more," așa cum, precum,
pe măsură ce), relative/regarding (relativ la faptul că, cu privire la faptul că), exception (în
afară de faptul că), opposition (în timp ce, pe când, în loc să), and addition (pe lângă faptul că,
după ce că). The sheer granularity of this classification (eleven distinct subordinating-
conjunction functions, several with 2-6 near-synonymous conjunctions each) is itself notable —
Romanian appears to distinguish more subordination relations morphologically/lexically than
English marks distinctly.

### Chapter 11: Interjections (pp. 149-150)

The book organizes its interjection inventory into fourteen functional categories: physical pain,
mental discomfort, pleasure/agreement, displeasure, surprise, disappointment, encouragement,
calling someone, disagreement, talking to animals, swear words, noises from nature, animal sounds,
and sounds of instruments/gadgets. This chapter is **the single highest-value stretch of explicit
register/slang annotation found anywhere in this source so far**: the source itself tags `mișto!`
("cool!") and `nasol!` ("bad!") as "very colloquial" — a direct, source-marked slang pair, one
positive and one negative, in the same semantic slot (see Vocabulary table). The "calling someone"
category (hei!, măi!, mă!, bă!, pst!) groups a set of blunt direct-address interjections whose
register ranges from neutral-attention-getting to genuinely rude depending on relationship and
tone — real-world Romanian sociolinguistics independently confirms mă/bă specifically are among
the most recognizable informal-address particles in the language, so their appearance here,
explicitly grouped by the grammar itself as a functional class, is a useful anchor point. The
"swear words" category (drace!, la dracu!, la naiba!) is explicitly named as such by the book
(not just inferred) and shows **two distinct taboo root-words** (drac "devil" and the separate,
likely-euphemistic naiba) occupying the same mild-profanity semantic slot — worth comparing against
the taboo-avoidance naming pattern already flagged in `002b_romanian_essential_grammar.md` (cel de
Sus / cel cu coarne) as a second, independent device the language uses around the same taboo
concept (the devil). `Poftim!` is highlighted by the book itself as carrying three genuinely
distinct discourse functions (offering, requesting repetition, expressing indignant surprise) from
one lexical form — a single-word pragmatic multi-tool.

### Chapter 12: Word formation (pp. 151-155)

**Suffixes** are organized by the part of speech they typically produce. Noun-forming suffixes
include profession/agent suffixes (-ar, -tor, -giu), a dedicated feminine-profession-forming subset
that exactly recapitulates the feminization patterns already given in 2.2 (-easă, -iţă, -oaică),
an abstract-quality suffix set (-ătate, -itate, -enie, -ie, -ism), and — of particular interest for
a slang/register project — **a notably large and productive diminutive-suffix inventory** (-aș,
-el, -ic, -ică, -iţă, -ior, -ioară, -uc, -uţ, -uţă, -uleţ), which the book explicitly states "often
express terms of endearment" rather than literal smallness (îngeraș "little angel" [endearment, not
size], bunic/bunică "grandad/grandma" [fully lexicalized past any diminutive sense], frăţior/
surioară "little brother/sister" [kinship-endearment]). A smaller, separate **augmentative** set
(-oi, -oaie) does the structural opposite (băieţoi "big lad," cărţoi "big book") — diminutive and
augmentative sit as a genuine two-sided derivational system, not just one marked category against
an unmarked default. Adjective-forming suffixes (-al, -ar, -aș, -at, -bil, -esc, -eţ, -os, -iu,
-ist, -nic, -tor, -ui) include several with a personality/character-trait sense that lean negative
or colloquial (mâncăcios "gluttonous," făţarnic "two-faced," băgăreţ "nosy/daring") — exactly the
kind of personal-trait-insult-adjacent vocabulary the later slang-synthesis phase will likely draw
on. Adverb suffixes reiterate -ește (cross-ref 8.1) plus a learned/Latinate -mente (eminamente,
realmente). **Prefixes** are similarly organized by target part of speech and are overwhelmingly
international/Latinate/Greek loan-prefixes (anti-, aero-, auto-, contra-, hiper-, hipo-, inter-,
non-, post-, pseudo-, supra- for nouns; arhi-, ne-, bi-, mono-, poli-, extra-, pre-, ultra- for
adjectives; de-/des-/dez-, în-/îm-, răs-/răz-, re-, auto-, co-/con-, sub-, supra- for verbs) — the
verb-prefix set in particular is the book's clearest illustration of native (non-loan) derivational
morphology, including the reversive de-/des-/dez- (`a descălţa` "to take one's shoes off," cf. the
accusative-reflexive verb list in Ch. 5/7) and the "spoil/change one's mind" pair răsfăţa/
răzgândi sharing one prefix allomorph pattern (răs-/răz-). **Compound words** split into hyphenated
(floarea-soarelui "sunflower," câine-lup "German shepherd," papă-lapte "softy," nu-mă-uita
"forget-me-not," dus-întors "return trip") and non-hyphenated (binefăcător "benefactor,"
atoateștiutor "omniscient," atotputernic "almighty") — the choice of hyphenation is lexically fixed
per compound, with no general predictive rule given (echoing the same lack of a general rule
already noted for compound-noun pluralization in 2.5.2).

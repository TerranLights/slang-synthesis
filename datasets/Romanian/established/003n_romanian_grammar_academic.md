## Grammar points

### Prepositions: invariable relational elements, three case-government classes (§6.1–6.2.3)

Prepositions in Romanian govern a specific grammatical case and can precede not just nominals but also
adverbs and certain non-personal verb forms (infinitive, supine). There are basic (single-word),
compound (built from 2–3 basic prepositions: de la, de pe, pe lângă, pe după), and phrase-level
prepositions (preposition + noun: în faţa, în spatele, în privinţa). Crucially, unlike English phrasal
verbs, a Romanian verb's *meaning* does not shift when different prepositions follow it — but the verb
may still require a specific, language-particular preposition that does not map onto its English
translation's preposition (a se căsători cu cineva "to marry with someone," where English has no
preposition at all). Three case-government classes are documented: (1) **accusative**-governing
prepositions — the largest class, including the purely grammatical direct-object marker `pe` (already
fully documented in file `003b`) alongside two dozen lexical prepositions (către, cu, de, din, dinspre,
drept, fără, în afară de, în chip de, în, în loc de, în timp de, înainte de, la, lângă, legat de, pe
lângă, pentru, peste, spre, sub); (2) **genitive**-governing prepositions — a large list fully captured
already in file `003b` (§3.1.3.4.1.1), not re-tabulated here since it is identical; (3) **dative**-
governing prepositions — just the closed "thanks to" triplet (datorită, graţie, mulţumită) already
documented in file `003b` as well, with graţie again flagged as the more formal member of the trio.

### Semantic relation categories built with prepositions (§6.3.1)

The source organizes prepositions into a comprehensive semantic taxonomy by the relation they build:
place, time, manner, condition, cause, concession, purpose, relation, exception, addition, instrument,
and association — each populated with several prepositions/phrases, many already seen in the case-
government lists above but now cross-classified by meaning rather than by syntax.

### The de+preposition compounding mechanism and the în/din merger (§6.3.2)

A systematic word-formation process: prefixing `de` onto a simple locative/temporal preposition (la,
pe, sub, după, lângă, în timpul) creates a new compound preposition (de la, de pe, de sub, de după, de
lângă, din timpul) that introduces an *attributive* clause rather than a circumstantial one — casa de
la ţară "the country house" is structurally a compressed relative clause (= casa care este la ţară "the
house that is in the country"), not merely "the house from the country." When `de` specifically
precedes `în`, the two phonologically merge into the single preposition `din` — itself capable of both
circumstantial and attributive uses. Both `în` and `din`, when followed by a singular indefinite-
article-taking noun, further contract with the article: într-un/într-o (în + un/o) and dintr-un/dintr-o
(din + un/o). A related but distinct phenomenon: locative prepositions/phrases have adverbial
counterparts formed by dropping the governed noun — the preposition then behaves as though carrying an
implicit definite article (înainte "ahead" (adv.) vs. înaintea mamei "ahead of mom" (prep. + genitive);
dedesubt "underneath" (adv.) vs. dedesubtul canapelei "underneath the couch" (prep. + genitive)).

### Polysemous prepositions and the many-to-many mapping with English "by" (§6.4)

The most frequent basic prepositions are genuinely polysemous — `la` alone covers place (stau la uşă
"standing by the door"), time (la amiază "at noon"), and manner (la minut "quick, by the minute");
`din` covers place, time, and cause; `cu` covers instrument, feature, and association; `pe la` covers
both a brief-visit sense of place and an hour-approximation sense of time. Since different languages
grammaticalize secondary preposition meanings differently, a single foreign preposition can map onto
many distinct Romanian prepositions depending on context — the source demonstrates this at length with
English "by," showing cu (by 10%, by bus), de (by the hand), din (by chance), conform (by the rules),
după (by a method), înainte de (by May 1st), la/lângă (by the window), pe (by first name — "pe numele
mic"), pe lângă (right by me), pe (by a different route), prin (by starting off.../by e-mail), and prin
natura sa (by nature) — twelve distinct Romanian prepositions/phrases for one polysemous English word,
a concrete illustration of why preposition choice resists direct translation.

### Conjunctions of coordination: şi vs. iar, dar/însă vs. ci (§7.1–7.2.3)

Romanian conjunctions of coordination split into addition (şi, nici, şi...şi..., nici...nici...),
opposition (dar, însă, iar, ci), choice (sau, ori, sau...sau..., fie...fie...), and conclusion (deci,
aşadar, prin urmare, în concluzie). Two near-synonym pairs get focused attention because English
collapses the distinction: `şi` "and" purely equates/integrates two elements, while `iar` "and"
integrates *while simultaneously contrasting* them — though the source notes a live usage drift where
`iar` is increasingly used in plain copulative contexts even without real contrast, encroaching on
`şi`'s territory. `şi` is also independently an adverb of reinforcement ("too/also"), a genuine
category ambiguity resolved only by position/function. Separately, `dar`/`însă` "but" contrasts two
fully independent elements, while `ci` "but" is structurally restricted to *correction* contexts — it
can only follow an explicit negation in the first clause (Nu mâine, ci poimâine. "Not tomorrow, but the
day after."), making it ungrammatical in ordinary contrastive contexts where dar/însă would be used.
Coordinating conjunctions can also form correlative pairs (şi...şi... "both...and...", sau/ori...sau/
ori... "either...or...", nici...nici... "neither...nor..." — the last triggering the same negative-
concord rule on the verb already documented for nimeni/nimic in file `003f`).

### Conjunctions of subordination: grammatical markers vs. semantically specialized connectors (§7.3)

Two subordinating conjunctions function as pure grammatical markers rather than carrying independent
meaning: `că` after verbs of thought/speech (cred că, ştiu că, a spus că), and `să` as the subjunctive's
own morphological marker (fully documented across files `003k`–`003l`). When an element inside a `să`-
clause needs emphasis, the conjunction splits into `ca...să`, with the emphasized element inserted
between — the same subject-fronting mechanism already seen in file `003k` §4.2.3.1.2, here shown to
generalize to other embedded elements too, and shown to extend identically to the compound conjunctions
fără să/înainte să, which split into fără ca...să/înainte ca...să under the same emphasis condition.
Beyond these two structural markers, a large semantically-specialized inventory covers cause,
consequence, concession, condition, purpose, time, manner, relation, exception, opposition, and
addition (fully tabulated above) — several members of the causal set carry a skeptical/accusatory
undertone absent from their plain synonyms (sub pretext că "under the pretext that" vs. neutral
pentru că "because"). A further set of correlative subordinating-conjunction pairs (cum...cum,
atât(a)...încât, atât(a)...cât, pe cât...pe atât, aşa...cum/cum...aşa, deşi...totuşi) expresses
temporal-sequence, consequence, manner/quantity-matching, and concession relations across two linked
clauses.

### Interjections: four functional classes, the book's richest colloquial-register chapter (§8.1–8.4)

The final chapter inventories (1) **reactive** interjections expressing physical sensation or emotional
reaction to events (organized by the specific emotion/sensation triggered — pain, cold, pleasure,
displeasure, effort, joy, dissatisfaction, emotional pain, regret, doubt, fear, surprise, unpleasant
surprise, agreement, disagreement); (2) **communicative** interjections used to get attention, prompt
action, encourage, silence, or address animals; and (3) **imitative** interjections (onomatopoeia) for
natural/physical sounds, mechanical/instrument sounds, and human/animal sounds. This chapter contains
the book's two most striking sociolinguistic findings outside the simple-perfect dialect note in file
`003j`: first, the disagreement interjection î-î/îm-îm is explicitly flagged by the source as having
**no standard written form at all**, offered as direct evidence that "written Romanian is very
reluctant to reflect many oral, colloquial forms" — a methodologically important caution that any
written corpus will systematically under-represent certain live colloquial phonology; second, the
attention-getting interjection `fă!` ("hey!," addressed to women) is explicitly given a **dual
register** by the source — unmarked/frequent in rural areas, but perceived as impolite if used in urban
areas — a genuine, source-confirmed regional-plus-social register split for a single lexical item,
exactly the class of annotation this project's extraction spec identifies as high-value and
easily missed. The chapter also explicitly notes ongoing anglicization: English interjections (wow!,
oops!, cool!) are "often used in contemporary Romanian, especially by young people," attributed
directly to American pop-culture/television influence — a dated (2003) real-time snapshot of a
loanword layer still actively forming, complementing the historical contact-layer analysis already
given in file `003`'s coverage of chapter 0.

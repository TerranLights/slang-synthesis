## Grammar points

### §153 — Stressed personal pronouns
Full nominative-form list: *eu/tu/el (dânsul)/ea (dânsa)* singular, *noi/voi/ei (dânşii)/ele
(dânsele)* plural. The *dânsul*-family forms exist as a parallel option for the 3rd person only.

### §154 — Declension of the stressed personal pronoun
Full N./G./D./A. tables given for every person: 1st and 2nd person lack a true genitive (replaced by
the possessive adjective, per Lesson VIII's system); dative/accusative forms are *mie/ţie* and
*(pe) mine/(pe) tine* for 1st/2nd singular, *nouă/vouă* and *(pe) noi/(pe) voi* for 1st/2nd plural.
3rd person has a genuine G.-D. (*lui/ei*, plural *lor* for both genders) alongside the parallel
*dânsul*-family (*dânsului/dânsei*, plural *dânşilor/dânselor*). Footnote: *dânsul* etc. are used only
for persons, never things — a real person/non-person restriction distinct from anything seen so far
in the pronoun system. Footnote 2 names the stressed reflexive pronoun (D. *sie*, A. *sine*),
deferring full treatment to Lesson XIX.

### §155 — Changes in *dânsul, dânsa*, etc. after certain prepositions
After *întru* "in," *dintru* "from," and *printru* "by/through" (elided to *într', dintr', printr'*
before a vowel), the *dânsul*-family forms lose their initial *d-* and become *însul, însa, înşii,
însele* — e.g. *într'însul* "in him," *dintr'însa* "from her," *printr'înşii* "by them." A specific,
narrow morphophonological alternation worth flagging alongside the clitic-allomorphy and imperfect-
tense conditioning patterns already documented in earlier lessons.

### §156 — Uses of stressed pronouns
(a) The nominative (subject) form is used only rarely, for differentiation/emphasis (cross-referenced
to Lesson II). (b) Other cases are likewise used for differentiation or emphasis, typically paired
with an explicit "not X" contrast. (c) The *dânsul*-family forms specifically mark emphasis or
contrast, and footnote 1 adds that they also function as a more polite register variant of the plain
accusative forms even without any contrastive sense (*Mă duc cu dânsa* "I am going with her" is more
polite than *Mă duc cu ea*) — a genuine, named politeness/register distinction worth flagging; they
are also rarely used in the nominative. (d) The stressed dative/accusative forms are, as a rule,
followed by the corresponding unstressed clitic in the same clause — i.e., true clitic doubling with
the stressed pronoun itself, not just a coreferent noun (cf. the noun-doubling pattern from Lesson
XII §136), e.g. *Pe tine te interesează mult subiectul acesta* "You are very much interested in this
subject." (e) Stressed pronouns are used after a preposition in verbless phrases, e.g. *Săracul de
mine!* "Poor me!" (f) The stressed accusative follows the second term of a comparison (*El e mai
inteligent decât tine* "He is more intelligent than you"). (g) Stressed forms follow *şi* "and, also"
and *nici* "nor." (h) The stressed pronoun precedes a noun in apposition, e.g. *Ce-mi trebue mie, om
simplu, aşa o batere de cap* "Why do I, a simple man, need such a headache."

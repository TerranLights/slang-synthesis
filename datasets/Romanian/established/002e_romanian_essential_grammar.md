# Romanian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Chapter 13 (Socializing)

**Source:** Ramona Gönczöl-Davies, *Romanian: An Essential Grammar* (Routledge, 2008), pp. 159-171
(Part 2, Language functions, Chapter 13).

**Coverage note:** Part 2 of this book is organized by communicative function rather than
grammatical category, and consists almost entirely of fixed phrases/formulae graded explicitly by
the book itself into informal/formal/very-formal registers — this is dense, high-value register
data and is captured near-exhaustively in the Vocabulary table below rather than compressed,
since the register tag *is* the content here (unlike a drill exercise, every phrase in this
chapter is a distinct pragmatic/register data point). Clean text layer throughout; this is one of
three Group-A Romanian sources, and chapters like this one (functional/phrasal, register-dense)
are exactly the kind of content the triage catalog flagged `01.Colloquial Romanian.pdf` as likely
to cover from a different angle — no redundancy check against that sibling file has been performed
yet (deferred per dispatch instructions).

**Morpheme breakdown.**

> **mămicuţo** = mamă (mother, base kinship noun) + -ică (first diminutive layer, → mămică) +
> -uţă (second diminutive layer, → mămicuţă) + -o (feminine vocative ending, cross-ref 2.4.3) —
> a doubly-stacked diminutive before the vocative inflection even applies, confirming the Ch. 12
> diminutive-suffix inventory is not limited to a single application per noun; the equivalent
> masculine stacking is seen in **tăticuţule** = tată + -ic (→ tătic/"tati") + -uţ (→ tăticuţ) +
> -ule (masculine vocative, cross-ref 2.4.3).

## Grammar points

### 13.1 General greetings (pp. 159-162)

The book organizes its greeting inventory along a clean informal/formal register axis for both
meeting (13.1.1) and leave-taking (13.1.2), with several items carrying explicit sub-register or
usage-restriction annotations beyond the plain informal/formal split — regional marking (Ciao!,
Servus!, Noroc! all explicitly flagged "(regional)"), gender/age-asymmetric usage (Sărut mâna!
restricted to men→women or younger→older, with a non-echoing reply convention), social-role
restriction (Să trăiţi! restricted to men-to-men or military contexts), and medium-specific usage
(Ne auzim! specifically for phone calls). A separate time-of-day greeting set (13.1.3) includes
one item, `'Neaţa!`, explicitly marked "(v. informal)" — a clipped/truncated form of `Bună
dimineaţa!` structurally parallel to English "Mornin'!" Welcoming formulae (13.1.4) include a
notable **fixed call-and-response pair** that must co-occur (`Bine aţi venit!` "Welcome!" →
`Bine v-am găsit!`, a conventional reply with no independent literal meaning outside this
exchange). Attracting-attention formulae (13.1.5) and warning signage (13.1.6) are both
public-register inventories — the latter entirely in the long-infinitive-as-noun or past-participle-
as-adjective register typical of official signage (`Fumatul interzis!`, `Parcarea interzisă!`,
`Oprirea interzisă!` — all built on the deverbal-noun pattern from 7.7).

### 13.2-13.3 Seasonal greetings, good wishes and congratulations (pp. 162-164)

A second fixed call-and-response pair appears here, this time explicitly religious/liturgical:
`Hristos a înviat!` "Christ has risen!" answered with `Adevărat a înviat!` "He is risen indeed!" —
the Romanian Orthodox Easter greeting exchange, structurally identical in function (and liturgical
origin) to its Slavic-language equivalents found elsewhere in this project's corpus (cross-ref the
Serbian/Croatian/Bosnian extraction pass). Good-wishes vocabulary draws heavily on the subjunctive
mood (cross-ref 7.4: Să trăiască!, Să fie fericit(ă)!, Să crească mare! — all third-person
subjunctive wish-formulae) and on fixed idioms with no transparent English gloss (`Casă de piatră!`
wishing newlyweds a "stone house," i.e., a stable/successful marriage).

### 13.4 Introductions (pp. 164)

Covers the verb-phrase vocabulary for making introductions (a face cunoștinţă, a prezenta pe
cineva altcuiva, a se prezenta — note the three-way accusative/reflexive/dative-indirect argument
structure split on essentially one root, echoing the a chema/a se chema/a-și chema three-way split
already flagged in 7.12.2) and a set of formulaic replies graded from neutral (`Îmi pare bine!`)
to markedly formal (`Onorat!` "Honoured!").

### 13.5 Forms of address (pp. 164-167)

A four-tier address-register ladder: informal (tu/voi, plus the `dumneata`/`mata` pair — `mata` is
a form not previously mentioned in the Ch. 5 pronoun chapter, surfacing only here), formal
(dumneavoastră plus title+surname patterns), very formal (a closed set of honorific
"Excellency/Holiness/Eminence/Highness/Majesty" vocatives restricted to specific official/clergy/
royal roles), and a separate family-register sub-inventory (13.5.5) of kinship vocatives that
itself shows internal gradation — plain (Mama!, Tata!), single-diminutive (Mami!, Tati!,
Bunico!), and double-diminutive (Mămicuţo!, Tăticuţule!, Bunicuţo!) forms of the same root,
plus apparently-regional alternate roots (Mamaie!, Tataie! for grandparents, alongside the more
standard Bunică/Bunic-derived forms) that the book lists without further dialectal comment. The
double-diminutive stacking pattern is a genuine compositional-morphology finding worth flagging
for the mechanics-analysis phase (see morpheme breakdown above) — Romanian diminutive suffixation
is not capped at one layer.

### 13.6-13.10 Health, weather, directions, excuses, table talk (pp. 167-171)

These sections are largely functional-phrasal vocabulary with fewer register-defining grammar
points, but contain two items of particular note: the weather idiom `Plouă cu găleata` ("it's
pouring," lit. "it's raining with the bucket") offers a direct structural comparison against
English's unrelated "raining cats and dogs," useful for the eventual slang/idiom-mechanics
comparison phase; and the closing meal-rhyme (`Mulţumim pentru masă, c-a fost bună și gustoasă, și
bucătăreasa frumoasă!`) is explicitly framed by the book as a piece of social performance ("if you
want to charm the hostess") rather than a neutral formula — a rare example in this source of
verbal play/wit being called out as such, worth flagging for the project's broader interest in
how playful/non-literal language use gets marked in grammar references.

## Grammar points

### Indefinite genitive/dative ("of a," "to a") — Unit 7, pp. 84–87 — **flagship case-system finding**

This is the first systematic presentation of Romanian's **indefinite genitive/dative forms**,
confirming the typology note's "genitive/dative syncretism" (the two cases share one marked form).
The indefinite article itself inflects to mark possession ("of a") or an indirect object ("to a"):

| | masc./neut. sg. | fem. sg. | plural (any gender) |
|---|---|---|---|
| nominative/accusative | un prieten | o prietenă | niște prieteni/prietene |
| genitive/dative | **unui** prieten | **unei** prietene | **unor** prieteni/prietene |

Worked examples from the dialogue: `cursul unui dolar` "the exchange rate of a dollar," `unei
prietene` "to/of a (female) friend," `unor colege` "to/of (some) (female) colleagues." **Two rules
the source states explicitly**: (1) `unui` precedes masculine *and* neuter nouns (mirroring `un`'s
own masc./neut. scope from Unit 2), `unei` precedes feminine nouns; (2) **a feminine noun's
genitive/dative singular form is always identical to its own plural form** (`unei prietene` uses
the same noun-form "prietene" as the plural "prietene" = "girlfriends") — an economical mnemonic the
source gives directly, and a genuine structural fact about Romanian's case syncretism worth flagging
for the mechanics-analysis phase.

### Vocative case — Unit 7, p. 87 — **flagship case-system finding**

The **vocative**, the third of Romanian's three case forms named in this language's typology note,
is used to address someone directly. Usually identical to the nominative, but certain nouns take a
distinct vocative suffix: **masculine** nouns may add `-e` or `-(u)le` in the singular (`Nicule!`
calling "Nicu"; `domnule!` "sir"; `Doamne!` "Lord!"); **feminine** nouns may replace final `-a` with
`-o` (`Ileana` → `Ileano!`; `Elena` → `Eleno!`); **plural** nouns of either gender may take `-lor`
(`doamnelor și domnilor!` "ladies and gentlemen!", `colegilor!` "colleagues!"). This is the first
unit to actually name and demonstrate the case the Introduction (Unit-1-era material) only
mentioned existed.

### Four verb conjugations — Unit 7, pp. 87–89

Full presentation of Romanian's four verb conjugation classes by infinitive ending, matching the
typology note's "four verb conjugations, closely related to classical Latin." **1st conjugation**
(`-a` infinitives, e.g. `a învăța`, with a sizeable `-ez`-present-tense subtype like `a lucra` →
`lucrez`): 3rd-person singular and plural forms are identical (`învață` "he/she learns" = `învață`
"they learn"). **2nd conjugation** (`-ea` infinitives, e.g. `a vedea`): irregular-looking but
follows its own internal pattern (`văd, vezi, vede, vedem, vedeți, văd`). **3rd conjugation** (`-e`
infinitives, e.g. `a scrie`): `scriu, scrii, scrie, scriem, scrieți, scriu`. **4th conjugation**
(`-i` infinitives, split into an `-esc`-present subtype like `a citi` → `citesc` and a non-`-esc`
subtype like `a dormi` → `dorm`). **Key cross-conjugation pattern the source flags explicitly**: in
the 1st conjugation, 3rd-singular and 3rd-plural forms match; in the *other three* conjugations,
it's instead the **1st-singular and 3rd-plural** forms that match (`citesc` "I read" = `citesc`
"they read"; `văd` "I see" = `văd` "they see"). This 1st-vs.-rest split in verb-paradigm symmetry is
a genuine, non-obvious structural fact.

### Present tense standing in for English present-perfect-continuous — Unit 7, p. 89

Where English uses "I have been reading," Romanian simply uses the present tense, disambiguated by
a duration adverbial: `citesc de mult această carte` "I have been reading this book for a long
time" (lit. "I read for-a-long-time this book"). `de mult`, `de puțin timp`, `de puțină vreme` are
the stock duration phrases the source gives for this construction.

### Register note: `moft`/`fără mofturi` — Unit 7, p. 83

**Colloquial family-register idiom.** In the breakfast dialogue, Mrs Dohotaru tells her son `Nicule,
fără mofturi!` ("Nicu, no fussing!" / "don't be difficult") when he complains about the food — `moft`
("whim, fuss," usually plural `mofturi`) in this construction is a genuinely colloquial, parent-to-
child register item, distinct from the book's otherwise mostly register-neutral core vocabulary.
Worth flagging for the slang-mechanics phase as a scolding/parental-register source.

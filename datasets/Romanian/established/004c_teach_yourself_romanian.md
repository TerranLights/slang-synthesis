## Grammar points

### 1. The future tense (two forms)

The **near future** is simply the present tense plus a future time expression (Unit 5). The
**everyday future** is formed with invariant `o să` (does not change for person/number) followed by
the subjunctive form met in Unit 7: `o să întreb` "I will ask", `o să lucrezi` "you will work", `o
să vadă` "he/she will see". Full paradigms given for `a întreba`, `a lucra`, `a vedea`, `a merge`, `a
ști`, `a citi` — p.70.

### 2. "To a", "of a": `unui`/`unei`/`unor`

To express *to a*/*of a* (vs. plain *a*/*an* = `un`/`o`), the indefinite article changes form:
masculine/neuter `un→unui`; feminine `o→unei` — and the feminine `unei` form is **always identical
to that noun's plural**. For plural nouns of any gender, both `un` and `o` converge to the single
form `unor` (`unor români` "to/of some Romanians", `unor românce` "to/of some Romanian women"). Full
paradigms given for masculine (`un câine→unui câine→unor câini`), feminine (`o mașină→unei
mașini→unor mașini`), and neuter (`un tren→unui tren→unor trenuri`) nouns — p.70–71.

### 3. Days vs. months: a gender mismatch within one semantic field

Days of the week are feminine; months, despite the fact that several superficially resemble
feminine-looking forms, are **all masculine** (e.g. `un septembrie frumos` "a beautiful September").
This is flagged explicitly as a point the learner must memorize rather than infer. In dates, the
numeral precedes the month (`11 martie` = `unsprezece martie`); "the first" of a month uses the
irregular ordinal substitute `întâi` rather than `unu` (`1 ianuarie` = `întâi ianuarie`); "the
second" uses the feminine cardinal `două` rather than an ordinal (`2 iunie` = `două iunie`) — p.74.
Adding the definite article to a bare time-of-day noun converts a point in time into a duration/
period expression: `dimineață` "morning" → `dimineața` "in the morning" — p.74–75.

### 4. `în` and `la` for "at"/"in", and `din`/`de la` for "from"

`în` "in" contracts to `într-` before `un`/`o` (`într-o mașină` "in a car") but stays `în` before a
definite noun (`în mașină` "in the car"). `la` covers both "at" and "to" (`sunt la restaurant` "I am
at the restaurant"; `vin la restaurant` "I am coming to the restaurant"). `în` (not `la`) is used
with country names regardless of the speaker's own location. Towns take `la`. "From" splits into
`din` (= `de` + `în`, used with place-of-origin, e.g. `Ea este din România`) vs. `de la` (= `de` +
`la`, used with towns, e.g. `El vine de la Londra`) — and both `din`/`de la` can additionally stand
in for an implicit relative clause "who is/was in..." (`Prietenul de la mare este american` "The
friend who was at the seaside is American") — p.75–77.

### 5. Past tense (compound, with auxiliary `a avea` + past participle)

Formed from a reduced present-tense form of `a avea` "to have" plus the past participle, collapsing
English's have/simple-past/did distinctions into one Romanian form (`am lucrat` = "I have worked" /
"I worked" / "I did work"). Past participles are regular by infinitive class: `-a`/`-i` infinitives
add `-t` (`a lucra→lucrat`); `-ea` infinitives (and some `-e` infinitives) replace the ending with
`-ut` (`a vedea→văzut`); other `-e` infinitives replace the ending with `-s` (`a merge→mers`, `a
scrie→scris`, `a spune→spus`). Some participles are irregular (`a avea→avut`, `a bea→băut`, `a
vrea→vrut`, `a face→făcut`) and two are flagged as genuinely unusual: `a fi→am fost` ("I was, I have
been") and `a ști→am știut`. Negation places `nu` before the auxiliary (`nu am spus` "I did not
say"), and **colloquial speech reduces `nu` to `n-`** (`n-am spus` "I didn't say") — the same
colloquial-clipping pattern already seen with numerals in Unit 3. Reflexive verbs in the past tense
combine the reflexive pronoun (contracted before a vowel: `m-am spălat`, `s-a dus`) with the
auxiliary+participle. The participle can also combine with `a avea de` to mean "to have [something]
to do" (`am de citit cinci cărți` "I have five books to read") — p.83–86.

### 6. "To the", "of the": the genitive/dative case proper

This is the full, noun-inflecting genitive/dative that the triage catalog's typology note
specifically flags (distinct from the "to a"/"of a" indefinite forms of grammar point 2 above, and
built directly on the Unit-7 definite-article forms). Endings again vary by gender/number/final
sound: masculine `câinele→câinelui→câinilor`, `prietenul→prietenului→prietenilor`; feminine
`mașina→mașinii→mașinilor`, `cartea→cărții→cărților`, `cofetăria→cofetăriei→cofetăriilor`,
`cafeaua→cafelei→cafelor`; neuter `trenul→trenului→trenurilor`, `fotoliul→fotoliului→fotoliilor`.
The "to the" forms accompany verbs like "to send to/give to/say to"; the "of the" forms mark
possession (`ușa balconului` "the door of the balcony", `mașina vecinului` "the neighbour's car").
**Personal names** take a different strategy: preceded by `lui` for both genders in casual
construction (`geamantanul lui George` "George's case"), while feminine names in `-a` may instead
take the ending `-ei` (`casa Anei` = `casa lui Ana` "Anne's house") — a genuine optionality in how
Romanian marks possession on proper names. Certain position words (`deasupra` "above", `în fața` "in
front of", `în spatele` "behind") govern this same "of the" form — p.86–87.

### 7. Possessive adjectives

`meu/tău/său/nostru/vostru` ("my/your(sg.)/his-her/our/your(pl.)") behave as regular
agreeing adjectives, following the noun which is itself in its definite-article form (`băiatul meu`
"my son/boy"). Full 4-gender×number×case paradigms given for masculine, feminine, and neuter
examples. `său` specifically marks that the possessor is the same as the sentence's subject (`George
se uită la ceasul său` "George looks at his [own] watch"), contrasting with the **invariant third-
person pronouns** `lui` "his" and `ei` "her" used when the possessor is *not* the subject (`George
se uită la ceasul lui` "George looks at his [someone else's] watch") — a subject-vs.-non-subject
possession distinction with no single-word English equivalent. Other invariant possessive pronouns:
`dumneavoastră`/`dvs.` "your" (sg. or pl.) and `lor` "their" — p.88–89.

### 8. Reported speech: no tense backshift

Unlike English (which backshifts the embedded verb's tense: "George said that he *was* looking for
a flat"), Romanian **preserves the original tense** of the reported statement/question inside the
reported-speech clause: `George vrea un apartament` → `George a spus că vrea un apartament` "George
said that he wanted [lit. "wants"] a flat"; `Unde este gara?` → `Ea a întrebat unde este gara` "She
asked where the station was [lit. "is"]" — p.89.

## Grammar points

### Superlatives with `cel mai` — Unit 17, pp. 219–220

The superlative ("the best," "the fastest") is formed with the invariant phrase **`cel mai`** +
adjective, with `cel` itself inflecting for gender/case/number exactly as in its adjectival-article
role (Unit 14): `cel mai bun` (masc. sg.) / `cea mai bună` (fem. sg.) / `cei mai buni` (masc. pl.) /
`cele mai bune` (fem. pl.), with matching gen/dat forms (`celui mai bun`, `celei mai bune`, `celor
mai buni/bune`). Superlative **adverbs** use the same `cel mai` phrase but never inflect:
`cel mai bine` "best," `cel mai repede` "fastest," `cel mai mult` "most," `cel mai prost` "worst."

### `amândoi`/`amândouă` ("both") — Unit 17, p. 220

Precedes a definite-article-bearing noun, like `tot` (Unit 12): `amândoi copiii` "both children,"
`amândouă fetele` "both girls." As a stand-alone pronoun it takes a dative/possessive form in `-ra`:
`amânduror le surâde ideea` "the idea appeals to both of them."

### Reported speech retains the original tense — Unit 17, p. 222

**A genuine typological contrast with English, worth flagging prominently.** Where English shifts
the tense back one step in reported speech ("she said she *was* ill," from original "I am ill"),
**Romanian keeps the tense of the original utterance unchanged**: `ea a răspuns că este bolnavă`
"she replied that she is [not "was"] ill" (lit. preserves present tense); `ei au spus că vor merge`
"they said that they will [not "would"] go" (lit. preserves future `vor merge`, not a conditional).
The source's own worked example: Adela originally thinks `ei mint` "they are lying" (present), and
when she later reports this to Alina she still says `nu știam că ei mai mint` "I didn't know that
they were [lit. "are"] lying" — no backshift at all.

### `celălalt`/`cealaltă` ("the other") distinguished from `alt` — Unit 17, p. 222

A separate adjective/pronoun from `alt` ("another," Unit 14) — `celălalt` specifically picks out
**the other one(s) from an already-identified, bounded set** rather than an open-ended "another."
Full paradigm given: masc. sg. `celălalt`/`celuilalt`, pl. `ceilalți`/`celorlalți`; fem. sg.
`cealaltă`/`celeilalte`, pl. `celelalte`/`celorlalte`.

### The pluperfect tense ("I had done") — Unit 17, pp. 225–226 — **a seventh past-tense-family form**

Formed by taking the **3rd-person-singular past-historic stem** (Unit 15) and adding endings
`-sem/-seși/-se` (sg.), `-serăm/-serăți/-sera` (pl.): `plecasem` "I had left," `scrisesem` "I had
written" (from the `-s`-type past participle base, matching the past historic's own `-s`-type
pattern). `a avea` has **two parallel alternative pluperfect forms** (`avusesem`/`avuseseram`),
mirroring the two-paradigm irregularity the past historic already showed for `a fi` (Unit 15). The
source gives three distinct rules for **when Romanian actually uses the pluperfect versus a
different past tense where English would use "had done"**: (1) ordinary sequencing, `când eu am
venit ei plecaseră deja` "when I came, they had already left," uses the pluperfect as expected; (2)
**after `după ce` ("after"), the compound past (not the pluperfect) is used**: `după ce am terminat
masa, ne-am uitat la televizor` "after we had finished the meal, we watched television" — "had
finished" is rendered by plain `am terminat`, not a pluperfect; (3) **in indirect/reported speech,
the compound past is generally used instead of the pluperfect** (reinforcing this unit's own
reported-speech rule above — Romanian is already fighting English's instinct to backshift tense, so
reported "had done" often surfaces as plain "did" in Romanian, `Radu a spus că nu a văzut-o pe Ana`
"Radu said that he had not seen Ana"); (4) **with `de mult`/`de puțin timp`, Romanian uses the plain
imperfect (Unit 14) where English would use the pluperfect**: `el știa de mult asta` "he had known
that for a long time" (not a pluperfect form of `a ști`). Taken together, Units 3, 14, 15, and 17
now give this book's full seven-member past-tense-family picture: compound past, imperfect, past
conditional, past historic (plus its reduced spoken-Oltenian equivalent), and pluperfect — each with
its own distinct distribution rather than being freely interchangeable stylistic variants.

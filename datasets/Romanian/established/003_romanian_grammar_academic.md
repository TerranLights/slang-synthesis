## 0. Introduction (paraphrased context, pp. 9–12)

Romanian is the easternmost Romance language, descended from the Vulgar Latin spoken in the Roman
provinces of Dacia and Moesia after 2nd-century conquest; most specialists place Romanian as a
distinct language by the 10th century. Grammatically it is considered one of the most conservative
Romance languages (closest to Latin), while its vocabulary has absorbed heavy external influence. The
book cites corpus-level lexical-origin percentages: ~20% inherited directly from (Vulgar) Latin, ~14%
Slavic borrowings (Old Slavic, Bulgarian, Serbian, Croatian, Ukrainian, Russian), 2.37% Greek, 2.17%
Hungarian, 3.7% Turkish, 2.3% Germanic, 38.4% French (via 19th-century re-Latinization/modernization),
2.4% re-borrowed from classical Latin, 1.7% Italian — with American English cited as the most active
current influence. Romanian's substratum is Dacian (identified mainly via comparison with Albanian,
~160 candidate words, concentrated in body-part, kinship, and pastoral/agricultural vocabulary); its
superstratum is mostly Slavic (contact from the 6th–7th centuries). Until the 19th century, Romanian
was also written in the Cyrillic alphabet. This contact history directly predicts where slang/colloquial
layers are likely to concentrate region-by-region (Slavic in the north/east, Hungarian in Transylvania,
Turkish via the historical Ottoman-tributary south) — worth revisiting once register-focused sources
(Colloquial Romanian, established/001) are cross-checked.

## Grammar points

### The Romanian alphabet and letter-to-sound correspondence (§1.1)

Romanian uses the Latin alphabet plus five diacritic letters: Ă/ă, Â/â, Î/î, Ş/ş, Ţ/ţ. The general
principle is one letter = one sound, with documented exceptions: `c`/`g` each have three
pronunciations depending on the following letter (plain velar before a/ă/â/o/u/consonant; palatalized
before `che`/`chi`/`ghe`/`ghi`; affricate before `ce`/`ci`/`ge`/`gi`); `x` represents either [ks] or
[gz] depending on the word; `e`- and `i`-initial words split between vowel and semivowel readings
depending on etymology (see below). Foreign letters (Q, W, Y, Ü, Ö, etc.) appear only in loanwords/
proper nouns and are pronounced as close to the source language as possible.

### Vowels [ə] and [y] with no English equivalent (§1.2.1.1)

`ă` represents [ə], a central vowel close to the schwa-like final vowel of English "mother," but it
almost never occurs word-initially in native vocabulary — the main exception being a small, explicitly
flagged set of **colloquial** demonstrative pronouns (ăsta, ăştia, ăla, ăia). `â` and `î` both represent
a single additional vowel [y], a close central vowel with no English equivalent (impressionistically
between [i] and [u]); `â` never appears word-initially, word-finally, or at a compound-word boundary
— `î` is used in those positions instead, while `â` is used medially. Both spellings are the same
phoneme; the choice between them is purely positional/orthographic, not phonemic.

### Dental vs. alveolar [r], [t], [d] (§1.2.1.2)

Romanian [r] is a tongue-tip trill (rolled, unlike the English approximant). [t] and [d] are true
dental consonants (tongue against the back of the upper teeth), articulated further forward than
their English alveolar counterparts.

### The letter `i` in final position: three distinct readings (§1.2.2.2)

A word-final `i` after a consonant can be: (1) a genuinely short, unstressed vowel marking
singular/plural or 1st/2nd person verb distinctions (e.g. pom "tree" → pomi "trees"; eu întreb "I ask"
→ tu întrebi "you ask"); (2) part of a double-`i` sequence [ii] when a plural-marking `-i` meets a
stem already ending in `-i`, or when `-i` combines with the definite-article `-i` (ministru → miniştri
→ miniştrii "the ministers"); (3) part of a rare triple-`i` sequence [ii] when a plural noun/adjective
in `-i` additionally takes the definite article (copil → copii → copiii "the children").

### Initial `e`: etymological split (§1.2.2.3)

A small, closed set of words inherited directly from Latin (several personal pronouns — eu, ea, el, ei
— and forms of a fi "to be" — eşti, este, e) pronounce word-initial `e` as the diphthong [je]. All other
words, including neologisms (electricitate, emoţie, elev, extraterestru), pronounce initial `e` as a
plain vowel [e]. This split is a direct fossil of Latin inheritance vs. later borrowing layers.

### Palatalization groups ce/ci/ge/gi/che/chi/ghe/ghi (§1.2.2.4)

`ce`/`ci` = [tʃ] (as in English "chart"); `ge`/`gi` = [dʒ] (as in English "germ"); `che`/`chi` = [k']
(palatalized [k], as in "keen"); `ghe`/`ghi` = [g'] (palatalized [g], as in "geek"). These four digraph
groups are the main device Romanian orthography uses to represent palatal consonants that would
otherwise need dedicated letters.

### Diphthongs, triphthongs, and vowels in hiatus (§1.2.2.5–6)

Romanian has both falling diphthongs (stress on the first element: au, ău, âu, eu, iu, ou, ai, ăi, âi,
ei, oi, ui) and rising diphthongs (stress on the second element: ea, ia, ie, io, iu, oa, ua, uă), plus a
handful of triphthongs (eai, eau, iai, iau, iei, oai, ioa). Critically, the same two- or three-letter
vowel sequence can represent either a diphthong/triphthong or two/three separate syllables in hiatus,
with no orthographic marking to distinguish them (au "they have" vs. aur "gold"; rea "bad, f." vs.
real "real") — this must be learned per-word/per-morpheme, not predicted from spelling alone.

### Stress is lexical and can be contrastive (§1.2.2.7)

Stress can fall on any syllable and is not predictable from spelling; it must be memorized per word.
Stress position alone distinguishes otherwise-identical word forms: veselă "happy (f.)" vs. veselă
"plates/crockery"; copii "copies" vs. copii "children"; el intră (present "he is coming in") vs. el
intră (simple perfect "he just came in").

### Liaison: synaeresis and elision (§1.2.2.8)

Vowel-initial words following vowel-final words trigger one of two liaison strategies rather than a
hiatus pause: **synaeresis** (one vowel becomes a semivowel, optionally marked with a hyphen in
literary texts simulating speech, e.g. Bine(aţi venit!) or **elision** (one vowel is dropped entirely,
marked obligatorily with a hyphen). Elision is grammatically *required* in certain clitic-pronoun +
auxiliary combinations (mă + a/am/aţi/au/aş/ai/ar → m-a văzut "he/she saw me") and merely frequent
elsewhere (the preposition în eliding before a following vowel; the negator nu eliding to n- before a
vowel, e.g. N-am timp. "I don't have time.").

### Inflection overview: five cases, no new words created (§2.1)

Romanian is an inflected language where nouns, articles, adjectives, pronouns, and numerals ("nominals")
decline by case, and verbs conjugate; inflection changes grammatical form, never lexical meaning. There
are **five cases** — nominative, accusative, genitive, dative, vocative — but only **two distinct
surface forms** per noun: nominative and accusative are formally identical, and so are genitive and
dative; function (not form) distinguishes them in context. Vocative is the only case with its own
dedicated marking. A structurally distinctive feature of Romanian: it is usually the **article** (the
proclitic indefinite article or the enclitic/postposed definite article), not the noun stem itself, that
visibly changes across the declension — the one systematic exception being the genitive-dative feminine
ending, which does attach to the noun itself.

### Verb conjugation overview: four conjugation classes, five personal + four non-personal moods (§2.1.2)

Verbs are grouped into four conjugations by their infinitive ending. Verbal forms encode mood (5
personal: indicative, imperative, subjunctive, optative-conditional, presumptive; 4 non-personal:
infinitive, participle, gerund, supine), tense (indicative alone has 8: present, compound perfect,
simple perfect, imperfect, pluperfect, three distinct future forms, future perfect, future-in-the-past),
person, number, gender (only for the passive past participle and the gerund used attributively), and
voice (active, passive, reflexive). The source gives the complete 1st-person-singular paradigm of a
cânta "to sing/to play (an instrument)" across every mood/tense as a worked reference table (p. 20) —
reproduced here only schematically given copyright discipline: simple tenses are single-word forms
(stem + suffix/ending — present, imperfect, simple perfect, pluperfect indicative); compound tenses
combine an auxiliary with the infinitive or past participle (compound perfect, the three indicative
futures, past subjunctive, optative-conditional, presumptive, past infinitive).

### Invariable parts of speech (§2.1.3)

Adverbs, prepositions, conjunctions, and interjections never inflect in Romanian.

### Vowel mutations: a systematic stem-alternation inventory (§2.2.1)

Romanian shows pervasive vowel alternations in the stressed syllable of a stem, triggered either by a
following syllable containing ă/e/i (11 distinct named mutations: o/oa, e/ea, ă/e, a/e, a/ă, ea/e, oa/o,
ie/ia, â/i, plus a second a/ă pattern, plus u/o) or by a shift in which syllable carries the stress.
Each mutation recurs across nouns, adjectives, and verb conjugation (present indicative and present
subjunctive) in parallel — e.g. the o/oa mutation surfaces identically in avion→avioane (noun plural),
frumos→frumoasă (adjective gender agreement), and a muri's eu mor → el moare (verb person agreement).
This is presented by the source as the single most pervasive structural feature of Romanian morphology:
the same small set of vowel-alternation rules, not independent memorization, explains noun plurals,
adjective agreement, and verb conjugation simultaneously.

### Consonant mutations: palatalization before plural/person-marking -i or -e (§2.2.2)

A parallel inventory of 12 named consonant mutations (c/ce-ci, g/ge-gi, s/ş+i, st/şt+i, str/ştr+i,
sc/şt+i-e, şc/şt+e-i, t/ţ+i-e, d/z+i-â-ă, z/j+i, l/Ø+i, n/Ø+i) governs consonant changes before the
plural-marking or person-marking vowels -i/-e, again recurring in parallel across noun plurals,
adjective agreement, and verb conjugation (present indicative, present subjunctive, and sometimes the
imperative). As with the vowel mutations, this is one small rule-set generating three different
morphological phenomena, not three separate systems.

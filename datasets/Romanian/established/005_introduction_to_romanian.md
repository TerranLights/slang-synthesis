## Grammar points

### §1–§28 — Letter-by-letter pronunciation inventory
The book gives a letter-by-letter IPA correspondence table for the Romanian alphabet, a few points
of particular note for a Romance/case-retaining language with heavy Slavic/Turkish lexical contact:
- **a** [a] is intermediate between English "at" and "father"; **ă** [ə] is a schwa-like central vowel
  that occurs rarely word-initially (mostly in colloquial demonstratives **ăla**/**ăsta**); **â** [ɨ]
  (book's own transcription uses a reversed-i-like symbol) is a distinct central vowel with a
  "pharyngeal" quality, occurring mainly before nasals and in stressed position.
- **c**/**g** are palatalized to [tʃ]/[dʒ] before e/i, velar [k]/[g] elsewhere/finally; **ch**/**gh**
  are the orthographic devices to force [k]/[g] before e/i.
- **i** has (a) a full vowel [i], (b) a semivocalic [j] value initially-before-vowel or finally, and
  (c) a near-silent "muted" **ĭ** word-final value (orthographically identical to plain i in modern
  spelling, but the book deliberately uses the older **ĭ** spelling throughout to flag it) — this
  muted ĭ is asyllabic but still conditions preceding consonant/vowel pronunciation (cross-refs
  §61, §99 — the latter outside this chunk's page range).
- **î** [ɨ] is pronounced like â, occurring mainly word-initially.
- **j** = [ʒ]; **ş** = [ʃ]; **ţ** = [ts].
- **s** voices to [z] before b, d, g (a sbiera, a sdrobi, sgârcit).
- **w**, **x**, **y** are explicitly marked as occurring only in borrowed/foreign words.

### §29–§58 — Diphthongs and triphthongs
Diphthongs are classified by which vowel is accented: Table 1 (accented on first vowel) covers
ăw, [ʔj], [ʔw], ej, ew, ij, iu, oj, ow, uj (orthographic ăi/âi/âu/ei/eu/ii/iu/oi/ou/ui); Table 2
(accented on second vowel) covers ja/jo (orthographic ea/eo), plus a further table (ia/ie/io/oa/ua)
all accented on the second vowel. §51 triphthongs are, with one exception, a glide ([j] or [w]) +
vowel + glide, accented on the middle vowel; **ioa** is the sole exception — [j]+[w]+[a], accented
on the final **a**. A footnote (§29) warns that not every vowel-sequence that looks like a diphthong
actually is one; non-diphthong sequences are marked with a dot over the syllabic vowel in the book's
own transcription convention (e.g. **cre·atură**, **ca·ut**).

### §59 — Accentuation rules
(a) Words ending in a consonant: stress the last syllable (sănătos). (b) A closed set of suffixes
(-ăt, -ec, -ed, -eş, -et, -ic, -ot) take penultimate stress despite ending in a consonant (cumpăt,
amestec, veşted, trupaş, trâsnet, uşuratic, clopot). (c) Words ending in a vowel: stress the
penultimate syllable (carte). (d) Words ending in a diphthong/triphthong are usually stressed on
that diphthong/triphthong (întâi, trăgeai). (e) 3rd-person-singular imperfect indicative, preterite
indicative, and infinitives of the 1st/2nd/4th conjugations (-a, -ea, -i) are stressed on the final
syllable (dormia, făcu, a aduna, a tăcea, a fugi) — footnote: 3rd-conjugation infinitives are instead
stressed on the penultimate syllable. (f) Slavic and Turkish loanwords have unpredictable stress —
Slavic loans may stress the 3rd/4th/5th syllable from the end (prăpastie, Dâmboviţa); Turkish loans
ending in -a stress that final -a (halva). (g) The postposed definite article is never itself
stressed and doesn't count for the above rules.

### §60 — Consonant alternations (morphophonology)
A set of regular alternations triggered by an added -i or -ĭ ending (plural marker or 2nd-person
verb form), directly relevant to how the retained case/plural system surfaces phonologically:
d→z (văd/vezi, aud/auzi, brad/brazi); l (also n, r) may drop before an added -i (cal/cai); s→ş
(pas/paşi/paşii); sc, şc→şt (iubesc/iubeşte, ceaşcă/ceşti); st, str→şt, ştr (prost/proşti,
albastru/albaştri); t, ţ→ci before i+a (înţelept/înţelepciune, credinţă/credincios); z→j (viteaz/
viteji).

### §61 — Vowel alternations (morphophonology)
Triggered by plural formation, declension, conjugation, or suffixation: stressed a→ă in undeclined
plurals with -i (baracă/baraci; exceptions: fragă/fragi, vacă/vaci); a→e in undeclined plurals with
-e/-i or after semivocalic [j] in derivatives (număr/numere; a încheia/încheiere); stressed â→î when
e/î follows in the next syllable or after a palatal consonant (cuvânt/cuvinte, tânăr/tineri); e→[je]
word/syllable-initially (era→[jera]); ea→e when e/î follows in the next syllable and becomes
unstressed (cafenea/cafenele, ceaşcă/ceşti, pleacă/plecăm); stressed e→ea (diphthongization) when a
or ă follows in the next syllable (plec/pleacă, drept/dreapta); stressed o→u when it shifts from
stressed to unstressed position (joc/jucăm); stressed o→oa when ă or e follows in the next syllable
(comori/comoară, folos/foloase). The book notes these are not exhaustive — stress shift, plural
formation, and conjugation are flagged as the main drivers of further, unlisted alternations; verb
conjugation vowel changes specifically are deferred to Lesson VI (outside this chunk).

### §62 — Syllabification
Syllables generally must begin with a consonant. Two adjacent vowels not forming a diphthong/
triphthong count as two syllables (im-pre-u-nă, chioa-ră). A single intervocalic consonant goes with
the following vowel (ma-re, pi-cior). In a cluster of 2–3 consonants between vowels, the first
consonant closes the preceding syllable and the rest open the next (car-te, mân-dru) — **except**
that a cluster whose 2nd or 3rd member is r or l stays grouped together in one syllable (pa-tru,
su-flu, stră-in).

### §63 — Elision
Word-final ă, e, i, u elide before a following word beginning with a vowel; word-initial î elides
only after a vowel-final word. Specific obligatory elision contexts: unstressed accusative pronoun
clitics mă/vă (m'am dus, v'aţi dus); conjunction că; the e of reflexive se; the i of conjunction şi;
the u of adverb nu; and word-initial î- (vino 'ncoace for vino încoace). A footnote adds that ă is
virtually always elided before forms of auxiliary a avea, though this isn't strictly obligatory, and
that colloquial/literary registers show even more elision than the formal rules describe.

### §64 — Diacritical marks
Catalogs the breve ˘ (a→ă), circumflex ^ (a→â, i→î), cedilla (s→ş, t→ţ), diaeresis (foreign names
only, e.g. Lübke), apostrophe (elision marker), and the book's own pedagogical convention of bold
italic type to mark stress that deviates from the §59 default rules.

### §65–§66 — Punctuation and abbreviations
Romanian punctuation largely follows English conventions; the one systematic difference noted is use
of a dash to open a line of dialogue. The book lists the native Romanian names for each punctuation
mark (see Vocabulary) and a glossary of common written abbreviations (a., a.c., c., C.F.R., d.c./
d.H., d.e., d.p., î.d.H., l.c., nr., ş.a., ş.a.m.d., Sf., S-ta., Str., v.).

### §67–§68 — Word order
Romanian word order is comparatively free/"unstable" because case and conjugation marking (rather
than position) carry most of the grammatical load. Default order is Subject-Verb-Object (Ion
mănâncă pâine). With multiple noun modifiers, adjectives follow the noun (though may also precede it
per Lesson IX); among noun modifiers, a declined (genitive/dative) noun modifier comes after a
prepositional-phrase modifier; when both combine, the order is noun → adjective → prepositional
modifier → declined noun (Dorinţa nestăpânită de faimă a omului, "Man's unchecked desire for fame").
Verb modifiers default to verb → direct object → indirect object → adverb (Am dat cartea
profesorului după masă). Subject-verb inversion occurs in questions, exclamations, and oaths, and
more generally for emphasis (Vine el sau nu vine?; Bată-i mânia cerului!). The author flags this as
a deliberately minimal treatment, to be supplemented per-lesson.

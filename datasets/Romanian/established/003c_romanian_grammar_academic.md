## Grammar points

### Indefinite article: proclitic, two forms (§3.2.1.1.1)

`un` (masculine and neuter) / `o` (feminine) precede the noun as a separate written word — unlike the
definite article, there is no phonological fusion. Gender assignment is readable directly off which
article a noun takes: `un`-nouns are masc. or neut., `o`-nouns are fem.

### Definite article: postposed and fused — a Balkan Sprachbund feature (§3.2.1.1.2)

Romanian's definite article is enclitic (attached after the noun), unique among Romance languages but
shared with other Balkan languages (Bulgarian, Albanian, Macedonian) — a genuine areal/contact feature,
not an inherited Latin one. It attaches either by addition (onto the noun's existing ending) or
substitution (replacing the ending): masculine/neuter nouns take -l/-ul/-le depending on their final
sound (-u→-l; consonant or -i→-ul; -e→-le); feminine nouns take -a/-ua, either added after -e or -a/-ea/
-i, or substituted for -ă/-e. A small set of masculine nouns denoting male persons but ending in -ă
(tată, popă) irregularly take the feminine-pattern -a rather than a masculine ending. The final -l (and
the -l in -ul) is frequently dropped in actual pronunciation. Several feminine day-names and the word
cacao take irregular definite forms, explicitly flagged by the source as exceptions (see vocabulary
table).

### Plural articles: one indefinite, two definite (§3.2.1.2)

The plural indefinite article is a single gender-invariant word, `nişte`, used across all three
genders. The plural definite article splits only two ways: `-i` for masculine plurals, `-le` for both
feminine and neuter plurals — a further instance of the neuter-behaves-like-feminine-in-the-plural
pattern already established in chapter 3.1.

### Article usage and omission: a set of context-specific rules diverging from English (§3.2.1.3)

The general three-way semantic split (no article = non-specific/generic or definite-by-context;
indefinite article = isolates one unspecified item from a set; definite article = fully individualized,
mutually known referent) mirrors English, but several Romanian-specific rules override it: an article
is *required* when a noun follows the preposition `cu` "with" indicating instrument/association, or
when the noun carries any modifier (adjective, pronominal adjective, or complex phrase). The
*indefinite* article specifically becomes required when a nationality/occupation noun is modified
(contrast *El este tată* unmarked "He is a father" vs. the modified case *El este un tată bun* "He is a
good father"). The *definite* article is specifically required with means-of-transportation nouns after
`cu` (mergi cu trenul "you go by train"), with kinship nouns after any preposition, and with any noun
modified by a possessive adjective. Conversely, the article is *dropped entirely* when stating a
person's profession/rank/nationality as a bare predicate (El este student. "He is a student."), after
any preposition other than `cu` unless the noun is modified, when a substance noun follows `cu`
indicating an added ingredient (ceai cu lămâie "tea with lemon"), after interrogative/indefinite
quantity-denoting pronominal adjectives or quantity adjectives (Ce carte! "What a book!"; mult, puţin),
and before a bare predicate noun denoting material or contrastive identity (E lemn, nu plastic. "This
is wood, not plastic.").

### The demonstrative/adjectival article *cel, cea, cei, cele* (§3.2.2)

Despite its traditional "article" label, this element behaves as a free-standing morphological particle
with several distinct functions: (1) it is the obligatory support element of the adjective/adverb
relative superlative (cel mai frumos "the most beautiful"); (2) it supports ordinal numerals in oblique
cases (cel de-al doilea "the second one"); (3) it individuates a cardinal-numeral + (optionally omitted)
noun phrase, functioning like an English definite article over the whole phrase (cele trei scrisori "the
three letters"); (4) it functions pronominally as a genuine noun substitute, recoverable either from
prior discourse (cea neagră "the black one," replacing a previously named dress) or from shared
situational context not explicitly mentioned; this pronominal use appears fossilized in a number of
fixed sayings/proverbs/euphemistic epithets (Cel de Sus "God," cel cu coarne "the devil," cea cu coasa
"death" — a direct, source-confirmed instance of taboo-avoidance naming, consistent with this project's
other flagship findings on taboo avoidance); (5) it attaches before a post-nominal adjective to
emphasize/individuate the noun+adjective combination (tânăra cea blondă vs. plain tânăra blondă, both
glossed identically but the cel-marked version carries extra contrastive emphasis) — this is the
structural basis of Romanian historical bynames (Ştefan cel Mare "Stephen the Great"), though not every
such English-style byname actually uses the Romanian cel-pattern (Vlad Ţepeş, Radu Negru do not).
Functionally the source argues "attributive article" would be a more accurate label than "adjectival
article," since it can also precede non-adjectival attributes (oblique-case nouns, full relative
clauses). As a genitive-marking element, independent occurrences of cel/cea/cei/cele decline for case
exactly like other genitive-taking elements (accusative with `pe`, genitive, dative), shown with full
worked dialogue examples in the source.

### The possessive/genitival article *al, a, ai, ale* (§3.2.3)

A four-form article (al masc./neut. sg., a fem. sg., ai masc. pl., ale fem./neut. pl.) marking
possession; it is the obligatory component of the genitive case-question itself (al cui? / a cui? / ai
cui? / ale cui? "whose?") and must be used whenever a genitive noun or pronoun is either used
independently (without its possessed-noun head present — A cui este casa? — A părinţilor mei. "Whose
house is it? — My parents'.") or placed at a remove from its head noun with other material intervening
(Casa cea nouă şi mare a părinţilor mei... "My parents' new, big house..."). It is also a fossilized
structural component of the full possessive-pronoun paradigm (al meu, al tău, al lui, al ei, al nostru,
al vostru, al lor) and agrees in gender/number with the *possessed* item, not the possessor.

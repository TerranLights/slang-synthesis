## Grammar points

### §130 — Present subjunctive
Formed with the infinitive stem plus endings, almost always preceded by *să*. Full paradigms given
for all four conjugations (*a schimba, a lucra, a putea, a face*; *a simţi, a vorbi, a hotărî, a
scoborî*) — captured as grammar structure, not per-row vocabulary since these are paradigm
demonstrations of already-known verbs. Key structural fact: subjunctive forms differ from present
indicative only in the third persons (singular and plural) — footnote 4 makes this explicit, and it
recurs as the single formal marker distinguishing the two moods for most verbs. Footnote 2: the third
person may sometimes drop *să* entirely in three specific senses — **hortatory** (*Explice cine poate
aceasta enigmă* "Let him who can explain this enigma"), **optative** (*Trăiască Regele!* "Long live
the King!"), and **concessive** (*Am decis fie ce o fi, să plec* "I have decided to leave, no matter
what happens") — each a distinct subjunctive-without-*să* construction worth flagging for later
mechanics analysis as a marked/emphatic register.

### §131 — Subjunctive of irregular verbs *a avea, a fi, a bea, a da, a lua, a scrie*
Full paradigms given for all six.

### §132 — Uses of the subjunctive
The *să*-subjunctive is the default Romanian equivalent of the English infinitive in subordinate
clauses after verbs of wish, preference, command, request, advice, insistence, permission, consent,
approval, prohibition, doubt, and fear — with a full worked example for each semantic category
(captured in the vocabulary/grammar notes above are the structural patterns, not each individual
example sentence, per copyright discipline). Footnote: expressions of hope or belief instead prefer
the indicative (*Sper că va veni* "I hope he will come," *Cred că are dreptate* "I believe he is
right") — but *Sper* can also take the subjunctive for a near-future/intention sense (*Sper să fiu
aicea mâine* "I hope to be here tomorrow"), a genuine semantic-conditioned mood split worth flagging.
Critically, **a change of subject between main and subordinate clause is not required** for the
subjunctive, unlike some Romance-language infinitive/subjunctive splits — *Trebue să plec* "I must
go" uses *să* even with the same subject throughout, confirming the subjunctive has essentially
replaced the infinitive as the default complement-clause strategy in spoken/written Romanian.

### §133 — Imperative
Second person plural imperative affirmative is identical to present indicative. Third person
singular/plural and first person plural all use the subjunctive (desiderative) forms instead — so
the imperative paradigm is really "2nd singular (own distinct form) + 2nd plural (= indicative) + the
rest borrowed from subjunctive." Second person singular formation: (a) 1st conjugation — identical to
3rd singular present indicative (*schimbă* "change!"). (b) 2nd/3rd/4th conjugations — either the 2nd
or 3rd singular present indicative form, with worked examples for each (*taci, umplă, treci, cere,
simte, fugi, hotărăşte, scoboară*). Footnote: three 3rd-conjugation verbs have irregular 2nd-singular
imperatives: *a duce* → *du*, *a face* → *fă*, *a zice* → *zi*. Footnote 2: the 1st person plural
imperative is usually preceded in conversation by the exclamation *Hai* — a genuinely useful
colloquial-register marker (cf. modern Romanian "Hai să...", still fully productive). The **negative
imperative** in the 2nd person singular uses the bare infinitive instead of the imperative form
(*nu schimba* "do not change," *nu face* "do not do," *nu simţi* "do not feel") — a real mood-
selection split between affirmative and negative commands, worth flagging. The subjunctive can
occasionally replace the infinitive even here; the book's own example is the fixed expression *să
n'ai grijă* "do not worry." Also: *să* cannot combine with reflexive verbs when the suffixal object
pronoun follows the verb, e.g. *ducă-se* "let him go" (no *să*).

## Grammar points

### Compound past tense (perfect compus) — Unit 3, pp. 30–31

The Romanian past is periphrastic: a short present-tense form of **a avea** ("to have") used as
auxiliary + the invariant past participle. Participle formation depends on the infinitive class:
`-a`/`-î`/`-i` infinitives add `-t` (a sta → stat); `-ea` and some `-e` infinitives replace the
ending with `-ut` (a putea → putut, a vrea → vrut); other `-e` infinitives take `-s` (a scrie →
scris, a spune → spus, a merge → mers, a lua → luat is irregular-looking but still `-t`-class). The
source gives full person paradigms for `a fi`, `a veni`, and `a avea` as auxiliaries-plus-participle
of themselves.

### Negative-past contraction — Unit 3, p. 32

**Register-relevant finding.** `nu` + a vowel-initial past-tense auxiliary (`am`, `ai`, `a`, `au`)
contracts to `n-` in actual speech: `nu am stat` → `n-am stat`, `nu am putut` → `n-am putut`. The
source presents this as the normal spoken form, not a marked register — still worth recording as a
genuine phonological-reduction pattern for the slang-mechanics phase.

### Possessive adjectives and pronouns — Unit 3, pp. 37–38

Possessives follow the noun, which itself still carries the definite article (`fratele meu` "my
brother," lit. "brother-the my"). `meu`/`mea`/`mei`/`mele` ("my") and `tău`/`ta`/`tăi`/`tale`
("your," singular addressee) and `nostru`/`noastră`/`noștri`/`noastre` ("our") all agree in gender
and number with the possessed noun. Romanian additionally distinguishes singular `tău` from plural
`vostru` for "your" — mirroring the `tu`/`voi` singular/plural split already seen in Unit 1.
**`lui` ("his"), `ei` ("her"), and `lor` ("their") are invariable** — they never agree with the
noun. The source explicitly notes `lui`/`ei` are **preferred in colloquial speech** over the
reflexive possessive `său` ("his own"/"her own"), which is reserved for cases where the possessor is
the same person as the sentence's subject — a genuine formal/colloquial register split on a core
grammatical choice.

### Adjective agreement classes — Unit 3, pp. 34–36

Adjectives agree in gender and number and normally follow the noun. The source distinguishes three
productive patterns by example: the 4-form type (`bun` → `bună`/`buni`/`bune`, the most common),
the mixed type illustrated by `mare` "big" (masc./fem. identical in singular, `mari` for both
plurals), and a 3-form type illustrated by `mic` "small." Nationality-derived adjectives in `-esc`
(e.g. `românesc` "Romanian") form their feminine in `-eacă`/`-ească` and plural in `-ești`:
`un cârnat românesc` / `cârnați românești` (masc.), `o masă românească` / `mese românești` (fem.).

### Time-telling — Unit 4, pp. 42–43

Full forms (`este ora trei și douăzeci de minute` "it is twenty past three") are routinely shortened
in speech to `e trei și douăzeci`. Half-hours use `și jumătate` ("and a half"), quarters use `și un
sfert`/`fără un sfert` ("and a quarter"/"less a quarter"), and times past the half-hour count down
from the next hour with `fără` ("without/minus"): `e șapte fără douăzeci` "it's twenty to seven,"
lit. "it is seven without twenty." `unu` and `două` (not `un`/`doi`) are the forms used for "one
o'clock," "two o'clock" — gender agreement with the implicit feminine `ora` ("the hour").

### Future tense — two colloquial forms — Unit 4, pp. 44–45

Romanian's everyday future is built on the same subjunctive (`să`) forms introduced in Unit 2's
want/can/must construction, preceded by the invariant particle **o** (itself derived from reduced
`va` auxiliary, though the source doesn't give this etymology at this level): `o să plec` "I shall
leave." The source explicitly labels this "the most common, and the most colloquial" way to express
future time. A **second, less common colloquial future** uses `să` preceded by the present tense of
`a avea` ("to have") as auxiliary instead of invariant `o`: `am să plec` "I shall leave," `ai să
pleci`, `are să plece`, etc. — person-agreeing where the `o să` form is invariant across persons.

### Points of the compass — Unit 4, p. 46

`nord`, `sud`, `est`, `vest` combine into compound directions (`nord-est`, `sud-vest`) and combine
with `la` ("to/at") or `în` ("in") plus the definite-article form for located-direction phrases:
`la nord` "to the north" vs. `în nordul României` "in the north of Romania."

### Numbers from 100 and number punctuation — Unit 4, pp. 48–50

`sută` ("hundred"), `mie` ("thousand"), and `milion` ("million") are themselves ordinary nouns that
pluralize (`două sute`, `două mii`, `două milioane`) and take `de` before a counted noun from 20
upward, same rule as smaller numbers (`o sută de kilometri`). **Orthographic note the source flags
explicitly**: Romanian uses a full stop to separate thousands and a comma (`virgulă`) as the decimal
marker — the reverse of English convention (`1.075` = "one thousand and seventy-five"; `2,3` = "two
point three").

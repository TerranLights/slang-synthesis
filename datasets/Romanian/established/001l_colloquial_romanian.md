## Grammar points

### The possessive article `al`/`a`/`ai`/`ale` — Unit 14, pp. 174–175 — **flagship case-system finding**

Romanian uses a dedicated **possessive article** when a possessive adjective (or an "of"-construction)
is used **without** an accompanying noun — i.e. where English just says "mine," "his," etc.: `rafturile
sunt ale mele` "the shelves are mine," `computerul este al meu` "the computer is mine." It agrees
with the **thing possessed**, not the possessor, and has four forms by gender/number: `al`
(masc./neut. sg.), `a` (fem. sg.), `ai` (masc. pl.), `ale` (fem./neut. pl.). The forms `ai mei`,
`ai tăi`, `ai lui` are noted as idiomatically meaning "my/your/his parents/family" without any
explicit noun. When a possessed noun is **indefinite**, `de-` links it to the possessive article
(`un bilet de-al meu` "a ticket of mine," `niște colegi de-ai lui` "some colleagues of his"). The
same article also introduces an ordinary possessive ("of X") construction when the possessed noun
is **not already preceded by** a definite-article-bearing noun: `o scrisoare a doctorului` "a
letter from the doctor" vs. plain genitive `scrisoarea doctorului` "the doctor's letter" (no
article needed once the noun itself already carries "the").

### The adjectival article `cel` — Unit 14, pp. 175–176

A second, distinct article, **`cel`/`cea`/`cei`/`cele`** (with its own gen/dat forms `celui`/
`celei`/`celor`), is used before an adjective that follows a definite noun, as an alternative way to
say "my great problem": `problema mea cea mare` (= `marea mea problemă`, both legitimate). Fixed
idioms built on `cel` include historical/honorific epithets (`Ștefan cel Mare` "Stephen the Great")
and enumerations (`cele zece porunci` "The Ten Commandments"). When an **adjective itself** precedes
a definite noun (for emphasis — a marked word-order already flagged in Unit 11), **it is the
adjective, not the noun, that carries the definite-article suffix**: `frumosul actor` "the handsome
actor" (not `*frumos actorul`), with full case inflection across the adjective itself (`frumosului
actor` "of the handsome actor"). Fixed idioms exploiting this: `micul ecran` "the small screen"
(= television), `mica publicitate` "small ads," `ultima oră` "stop press."

### `unul`/`unii` ("one"/"some") vs. `niște` ("some," indefinite quantity) — Unit 14, pp. 178–179

`unul` (masc.), `una` (fem.) "one"/"some" and their plurals `unii`/`unele` have a full gen/dat
paradigm (`unuia`, `uneia`, `unora` — `unora` covers all genders in the plural gen/dat). **The
source draws an explicit semantic contrast with `niște`** (Unit 2): `niște` marks an **undefined**
quantity ("some, a few, unspecified"), while `unii`/`unele` is more precise, closer to "a certain
number of" — illustrated with a genuine minimal pair: `erau niște țărani în camion` "there were
some peasants in the lorry" (unspecified) vs. `unii țărani erau în costum` "some (specific/
identifiable) peasants were in traditional costume." `din`/`dintre` both render "of them" after
`unii`/`alții` (`dintre` preferred before a vowel), and `pe` is required when the referent is human
(`l-au întrebat pe unul din poliţişti` "they asked one of the policemen").

### Past conditional ("would have been") — Unit 14, pp. 179–180

Formed by simply adding the invariant past participle `fost` to the plain conditional (Unit 5):
`aș fi fost fericită` "I would have been happy," `ar fi fost grozav` "it would have been great." As
with the plain conditional, this pairs naturally with a `dacă` clause (also in the past conditional,
not shifted to a different tense): `dacă am fi avut bani, am fi mers la mare` "if we had had money,
we would have gone to the seaside." **Register note**: the source states explicitly that "in
colloquial speech the past conditional is often replaced by the imperfect tense" — `era mai bine
dacă nu se ducea acolo` ("it would have been better if he hadn't gone there," using plain imperfect
`era`/`se ducea` instead of the grammatically "correct" past conditional) — a genuine register
simplification worth flagging for the mechanics-analysis phase.

### The imperfect tense — Unit 14, pp. 184–185 — **major new tense**

A fourth past-tense form (alongside the compound past of Unit 3 and whatever role the subjunctive/
conditional play), denoting continuous, habitual, or repeated past action/state — "was doing,"
"used to do," "kept on doing." Formed by a set of **stressed endings suffixed directly onto the
infinitive** (not a periphrastic auxiliary construction, unlike the compound past, future, and
conditional): 1st/2nd conjugations take `-m/-i/∅` singular, `-m/-ți/-u` plural (`treceam,
treceai, trecea, treceam, treceați, treceau` from `a trece`); 3rd conjugation and `-ui`-ending 4th-
conjugation verbs take `-am/-ai/-a` sg., `-am/-ați/-au` pl. (`locuiam` from `a locui`); other
4th-conjugation (`-i`) verbs drop the final `-i` and take `-eam/-eai/-ea` sg., `-eam/-eați/-eau` pl.
(`vorbeam` from `a vorbi`). **Irregular imperfects** (not derivable from the infinitive by a regular
rule) include `a fi` → `eram, erai, era, eram, erați, erau`; `a da` → `dădeam`; `a sta` →
`stăteam`; `a bate` → `băteam`; `a scrie` → `scriam`; `a ști` → `știam`; `a vrea` → `voiam`; `a
trebui` → `trebuia` (this last one "only used normally in the third person," matching `a trebui`'s
invariant behavior established in Unit 2). As with the compound past (Unit 7), the imperfect
combines with `de mult`/`de puțin timp` to render an English past-perfect-continuous sense:
`George locuia de mult la Londra` "George had been living in London for a long time."

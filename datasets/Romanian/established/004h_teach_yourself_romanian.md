## Grammar points

### 1. Adjectives before the noun, and the resulting `the`-marking shift

While Romanian adjectives normally follow the noun (Unit 5), several common ones — especially
`mare` "big" and `mic` "small" — are frequently placed before the noun for emphasis (`un mare
eveniment` "a great event"). **When the noun phrase is definite and the adjective precedes**, the
definite-article ending moves from the noun onto the *adjective* instead: `Bunul Dumnezeu` "the Good
Lord" (not `*Dumnezeul bun`), `micul ecran` "the small screen (i.e. TV)", `marea mea dragoste` "my
great love". The source tabulates this across the 4-form/3-form/2-form adjective-paradigm sizes
established in Unit 5. An alternative strategy keeps the adjective after the noun but adds `cel`
(see point 2) for the same emphatic effect: `prietenii mei cei buni` "my good friends" — pp.152–153.

### 2. `cel`, `cea`, `cei`, `cele`: reduced emphatic "the", and "the one(s) who"

`cel` is a reduced form of `acel` "that" (Unit 10) but has lost `acel`'s demonstrative force,
functioning instead as an emphatic equivalent of English "the". Declines through the same
m./f./n. × sg./pl. × case grid as other demonstrative-derived words
(`cel/celui/cei/celor`...). Frequently paired with `care` to mean "the one(s) who": `Cel care se
uită la noi este directorul fabricii` "The one looking at us is the factory boss". Also forms fixed
titles/epithets (`Ștefan cel Mare` "Stephen the Great") — pp.153–154.

### 3. The superlative: `cel mai` + adjective/adverb

"The best", "the biggest" etc. are formed with `cel mai` (agreeing: `cel/cea/cei/cele mai` + base
adjective): `cel mai bun prieten` "the best friend", `cea mai bună prietenă` "the best friend (f.)".
For adverbs, the invariant `cel mai` form covers superlative meaning directly (`cel mai bine`
"best", `cel mai repede` "the quickest"). When the superlative adjective *follows* its noun, the
noun itself takes the definite-article ending (`vocea cea mai ascuțită` "the highest-pitched
voice") — pp.154–155.

### 4. The passive voice: past participle + `a fi`

The past participle (Unit 9's form for the compound past, e.g. `invitat`, `scris`, `interzis`) also
forms the passive voice with `a fi` "to be", and — functioning here as an adjective — **must agree
in gender/number with the subject**: `Sunt invitat la o masă` "I am invited to a meal" (m.), `Aceste
cărți sunt scrise în englezește` "These books are written in English" (f. pl.). Participles also
function as standalone adjectives (`Aceste camere sunt ocupate` "These rooms are occupied") and even
as full nouns carrying the definite article themselves (`fumatul interzis` "smoking prohibited",
literally "the-forbidden the-smoked"; `Scrisul lui e elegant` "His handwriting [lit. the-written] is
elegant"). Past participles further combine with `de` to form compound nouns (`o mașină de spălat`
"a washing machine", lit. "a machine of to-wash") or, also preceded by `de`, to correspond to an
English infinitive complement ("to do", "to hire"): `Ce este de făcut?` "What is to be done?", `Ai 50
de lei de plătit` "You've got 50 lei to pay", `Este bine de știut dacă...` "It is a good thing to
know whether..." — pp.155–157.

### 5. The `ori-` indefinite-pronoun family

A productive prefix `ori-` attaches to interrogative roots to build universal/free-choice
indefinites: `orice` "anything" (`ce` "what"), `oricine` "anyone" (`cine` "who"), `oriunde`
"anywhere" (`unde` "where"), `oricum` "anyhow" (`cum` "how"), `oricât` "however much" (`cât` "how
much"). Functions in concessive clauses too (`Oricât încerci, n-o să reușești` "However much you
try, you won't succeed") — p.157.

## Grammar points

### Relative vs. interrogative pronouns: overlapping forms, distinct behavior (§3.4.7.1)

Interrogative pronouns introduce direct questions; relative pronouns link a subordinate clause to its
main clause while also playing a syntactic role inside the subordinate (unlike plain conjunctions). The
two categories share most of their forms (cine, ce, care, cât), but neither set is a strict subset of
the other: `al câtelea` is interrogative-only (asking about sequence/order), while the invariable
colloquial `de` and the compounds `cel ce` and `ceea ce` are relative-only. Even where cine/ce/care are
shared, their distributional behavior differs: as an interrogative, `ce` can only replace inanimate
nouns and only combines with a 3rd-person-singular verb; as a relative (synonymous with care in this
use), it can replace animate nouns and combine with any person/number. Case assignment also differs: an
interrogative pronoun's case is fixed by the subordinate clause's own structure, while a relative
pronoun's case can instead be governed by the main clause.

### *cine* and *ce* as interrogatives (§3.4.7.2)

`cine` "who" substitutes only person-denoting nouns and functions only as a pronoun, never an
adjective; the verb always follows it in questions. `ce` "what" substitutes thing/action/state-denoting
nouns, is fully invariable, and can function adjectivally to ask "what kind of?" (though `Ce fel de...`
is the more idiomatic phrasing) or appear in exclamations expressing personal reaction (Ce zi
minunată! "What a wonderful day!", Ce groaznic! "How horrible!").

### *care* as interrogative and relative, with full declension (§3.4.7.3–3.4.7.5)

`care` "which (one)" is unique among these pronouns in being identical in the nominative-accusative for
every gender/number, both as pronoun and adjective — but splits into distinct pronoun vs. adjective
forms in the genitive-dative (căruia/căreia/cărora as pronoun vs. cărui/cărei/căror as adjective), and
further splits by gender in the singular there. As a direct object, `care` always takes `pe`
with required double expression when functioning as a pronoun, while as an adjective it follows the
ordinary person/non-person-conditioned `pe` rule. The genitive construction built around relative
`care` is flagged by the source as the most structurally demanding in the whole pronoun system: the
genitive relative clause can place `care` either before the possessed-object noun (as a possessive-
article-marked adjective, al/a/ai/ale cărui/cărei/căror) or after it (as a bare pronoun form ending in
-a, căruia/căreia/cărora, with the possessed noun then taking the definite article) — both orders
require agreement in gender/number with different elements (the pre-position form agrees with the
noun in the *subordinate* clause; `care` itself always agrees with the noun it substitutes from the
*main* clause).

### *cel ce* / *cel care*: the demonstrative-plus-relative compound (§3.4.7.6)

Combining the short demonstrative cel/cea/cei/cele with either relative ce or care produces a fully
interchangeable compound meaning "the one who/that" — except the feminine singular nominative-
accusative, where only `cea care` is accepted (`cea ce` is not used). As a direct object it always
takes `pe` regardless of person/non-person reference; independently in the genitive it requires the
possessive article.

### *ceea ce*: the clause-level relative "what/which" (§3.4.7.7)

A single fixed, feminine-form compound relative pronoun, roughly equivalent to plain `ce` but able to
summarize an entire preceding clause (comparable to English non-restrictive "which": Lucrarea ei este
foarte interesantă, ceea ce înseamnă... "Her paper is very interesting, which means..."). As a direct
object it does *not* take `pe` (unlike cel ce/care); its genitive and dative values require the support
prepositions `a` and `la` respectively, since the bare form itself cannot inflect.

### Indefinite pronouns built from interrogative stems (§3.4.8.1–3.4.8.2)

Two parallel derivational families extend the interrogative pronouns ce and cine into indefinites via
the prefix/suffix elements `ori-`/`-va` (indefiniteness) and `alt-` (differentiation): ceva/altceva/
orice (thing-denoting, from ce) and cineva/altcineva/oricine (person-denoting, from cine). Within each
family, the "plain" and "alt-" members are strictly pronoun-only, while orice/oricine additionally
function adjectivally (orice, not oricine). All members of the ce-family are fully invariable; the
cine-family members have no plural but do have a genitive-dative built on cui (cuiva, altcuiva, oricui).
Accusative behavior tracks animacy exactly as with ordinary nouns: the person-denoting cineva-family
always takes `pe` (without requiring double expression), while the thing-denoting ceva-family never
does.

### Negative pronouns *nimeni* / *nimic* and obligatory negative concord (§3.4.8.3)

`nimeni` (person-denoting) and `nimic` (thing-denoting) are both pronoun-only, invariable in gender,
with `nimeni` additionally having a genitive-dative (nimănui) and no plural form at all. Both
obligatorily co-occur with a negated verb — Romanian negative concord, not a double-negative error
(Nu e nimeni aici. "There is nobody here." / Nimic nu mă va putea opri. "Nothing will be able to stop
me.").

### The *unul/un* ~ *altul/alt* ~ *vreunul/vreun* ~ *nici unul/nici un* indefinite/negative family (§3.4.8.4–3.4.8.7)

Four structurally parallel series share one underlying declension template, built respectively on
"one," "other," "some/any" (uncertain-leaning), and "none" (negative): each has a 4-form pronoun
paradigm (masc.sg/fem.sg/masc.pl/fem.pl) and, except where noted, a parallel adjective paradigm that
precedes its noun (which then takes no article). Key shared facts, captured once rather than per
series: the singular `un/o` adjective form is identical to both the plain indefinite article and the
cardinal numeral "one," disambiguated only by context; `vreunii/vreunele` and `nici unii/nici unele`
exist only as pronouns, lacking an adjectival plural counterpart (unlike unii/unele and alţii/altele,
which do double as plural adjectives); genitive-dative plural collapses gender distinctions separately
for the pronoun series (unora, vreunora, nici unora, altora) and the adjective series (unor, vreunor,
nici unor, altor); independent or remote genitive use requires the possessive article al/a/ai/ale, as
with every other pronoun class already documented; accusative `pe`-marking and double-expression
follow the general person/non-person rule when these items function as adjectives, but pronoun uses
referring to persons generally take `pe` with optional (not required) doubling — the doubling itself
signaling a *lower* degree of indefiniteness, a subtle markedness point worth flagging: more emphatic
reduplication correlates with *more* specific reference even though the pronoun itself is indefinite in
form.

### *oricare* and *fiecare*: universal quantifying pronouns, one with no plural (§3.4.8.8)

`oricare` "any (one), whichever" has a single nominative-accusative form for both genders and both
numbers, splitting by gender only in the genitive-dative singular. `fiecare` "each, every" patterns
similarly but has **no plural form whatsoever** — a genuinely gapped paradigm, not just a
gender-collapsed one. Both pronouns, unlike the unul/altul family, *require* `pe` and double expression
in the accusative regardless of person/non-person reference when used purely as pronouns (though as
adjectives they still follow the ordinary animacy-conditioned rule) — when fiecare's direct object is
doubled, the correlative clitic pronoun must be plural even though fiecare itself is grammatically
singular, a genuine semantic-vs-grammatical-number mismatch worth flagging for any later
morphosyntactic analysis.

### Reduplication (doubling) of direct and indirect objects: anticipation vs. reiteration (§3.4.9)

Romanian's relatively free word order lets a noun/pronoun object either follow its verb (regular order)
or precede it (inverted, emphatic/stylistic order); reduplicating the object with a correlative
unstressed personal-pronoun clitic is called **anticipation** in the first case and **reiteration** in
the second, and the two have different, non-symmetric trigger conditions.

**Anticipation** (object follows the verb) is optional-but-strongly-preferred for ordinary definite/
identified person-denoting direct objects already taking `pe` (the source notes that omitting it now
reads as archaic or simply incorrect in contemporary standard Romanian), and is flatly *required* when
the direct object is a stressed personal/reflexive pronoun, a possessive pronoun, or a demonstrative
pronoun. It does *not* occur for indefinite/negative person-pronouns (pe cineva, pe altcineva, pe
oricine, pe nimeni) and never for non-`pe`-taking objects.

**Reiteration** (object precedes the verb, inverted order) is *required* whenever the fronted object is:
a definite-article-bearing noun of any kind (regardless of `pe`); any `pe`-marked object except the
indefinite/negative-pronoun set just listed; a noun modified by a demonstrative or possessive
pronominal adjective; or a noun modified by a superlative-degree adjective. It is *disallowed* for most
non-`pe` objects and for the same indefinite/negative-pronoun exceptions as anticipation.

The same anticipation/reiteration logic extends to the *indirect* object, but with different triggers:
anticipation of a dative object is required only when it is a stressed personal pronoun, or inside the
large family of impersonal dative "physical/emotional state" constructions already documented in
§3.4.1.6 (mi-e foame, mi-e frig, etc.) — and never required for an inanimate-referring indirect object.
Reiteration of the indirect object, by contrast, is required in most cases regardless of animacy, and
only genuinely optional for the dative/accusative-with-`la` forms of cine, oricine, cât, oricât (though
even there it is "preferred"). A small set of combinations is simply ungrammatical regardless of word
order, because the resulting clitic cluster cannot be phonologically realized (marked with * by the
source: *i mă, *i vă, *vă mă).

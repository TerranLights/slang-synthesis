# English — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: *Beginning Old English*, Part II Introduction + Text A (Cynewulf and Cyneheard)

**Source:** Carole Hough and John Corbett, *Beginning Old English* (Palgrave Macmillan, 2007),
pp. 139–151 (Part II Introduction, and Text A: "Cynewulf and Cyneheard," the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle
annal for 757).

**Coverage note:** Part II presents four full, unsimplified Old English texts with running
section-by-section glosses plus a cumulative end-of-text glossary of "common or familiar" words. This
file covers the Part II front matter (spelling-convention notes, case-abbreviation key) and the first
full text. **Vocabulary-table approach for Part II specifically:** because these texts supply
glossaries of *every* word (not just unfamiliar ones), many entries are closed-class function words
(pronouns, determiners, conjunctions, prepositions) already fully catalogued in pt01–pt06 of this
extraction. To keep the table usable rather than repeating 40+ already-established rows verbatim,
recurring function words are given **one consolidated row each** (noting their case/number
variants as the text's own glossary marks them) rather than one row per inflected form; genuinely
new content vocabulary (nouns, verbs, adjectives, proper names) gets full individual treatment as in
prior files. The narrative content of the annal itself is represented only via the source's own
glosses and brief discussion paraphrase, not reproduced as continuous prose, per copyright
discipline. Continues from `076_beginning_old_english_pt06.md` (end of Part I).

**Source text layer:** clean, same fixed cipher as pt01–pt06 (þ→ƒ, ð→ê, Þ→∏; decoded throughout).
Vision Reading Confidence `n/a` throughout. **Scope change flagged by the source itself at this
point (p.140):** from Part II onward, spelling is no longer standardized — þ/ð are used genuinely
interchangeably within and across texts (capital þ appears as "Î" in the source's own unconverted
typesetting, which this extraction also decodes to Þ), early West Saxon *īe* may appear as late
West Saxon *ī* (e.g. *hīe* vs. *hī* 'they'), and late-period texts (especially *The Battle of
Maldon*, covered in a later file) show case-ending breakdown — subjunctive plural *-on* replacing
*-en*, Dative plural *-on* replacing *-um*. This is a genuine manuscript/dialectal phenomenon the
source deliberately now exposes, not a new PDF-rendering artifact.

## Part II apparatus (not vocabulary, but structural conventions used throughout Texts A–D)

The source's own case/mood abbreviation key, used in all four texts' glosses: **n** = nominative,
**a** = accusative, **g** = genitive, **d** = dative, **s** = singular, **p** = plural, **j** =
subjunctive, **t** = past participle (p.139). Combined codes stack, e.g. "(np)" = nominative plural,
"(sj)" = subjunctive singular, "(pj)" = subjunctive plural.

## Grammar points

### Spelling de-standardization from Part II onward (p.140)

Three systematic manuscript-fidelity changes apply from this point on, all already flagged in the
Coverage note above: free þ/ð interchange (with capital þ appearing as "Î" in this particular
PDF's rendering, likewise decoded here); Late West Saxon monophthongization of *īe* to *ī* (*hīe* →
*hī* 'they'), used inconsistently even within single later texts; and, specifically in *The Battle
of Maldon*, breakdown of the inflectional system — subjunctive plural *-on* replacing historical
*-en*, and Dative plural *-on* sometimes replacing *-um*. This is presented as genuine, dateable
language change visible within the OE corpus itself, not scribal error.

### Oral-formulaic traces in Chronicle prose (p.141)

The source flags two features suggesting this annal circulated orally before being written down:
extensive direct speech in the dialogue scenes, and a pattern of referring to characters by bare
pronouns (*hē*, *hīe*) without re-establishing their referents — consistent with an audience already
assumed to know the story.

### Split clauses linked by *ond* 'and' (p.141)

Early OE prose narrative style (distinct from the more elaborate hypotactic syntax of later OE
prose) strings long sequences of *ond*-linked clauses rather than nesting subordinate clauses — the
source's own section/sentence numbering in this text is an editorial navigation aid, not present in
the manuscript.

### The heroic code in prose narrative, not just verse (p.141, 143–149)

This annal is read by the source as a sustained illustration of heroic-code values (loyalty to lord
over kinship ties, willingness to fight to the death rather than compromise, the dishonor of
accepting a usurper's bribe) playing out twice in parallel structure: first with Cynewulf's small
party at Merton, then with the larger relief force under Osric and Wiferth — both refuse Cyneheard's
offers of safe conduct/compensation, both fight to near-annihilation, and both leave exactly one
survivor.

### Dating error in the Chronicle itself (p.149)

The source notes a documented compilation error: most mid-8th-to-mid-9th-century Chronicle entries
are dated two years too early due to an error in the original compilation, so this annal
(manuscript-dated 755) actually records events of 757. A second likely numeral-transposition error
(xxxi 'thirty-one' for xxix 'twenty-nine') is flagged in Cynewulf's stated reign-length, cross-checked
against his independently-recorded death date.

### Genealogy as a structural convention of Chronicle writing (p.150–151)

Tracing a king's direct paternal line back to a founding figure (here Cerdic, founder of the West
Saxon royal line) — and, in the annal's continuation beyond this excerpt, tracing another king's line
back to the god Woden — is flagged as a recurring Anglo-Saxon documentary convention, not unique to
this text.

## Grammar points

### Rule IX — Adjectives

**"Adjectives relate to nouns or pronouns."** Four stated exceptions: an adjective can relate to a whole
clause/sentence used as subject; combined arithmetical numbers can stack adjective-on-adjective ("one
thousand four hundred and fifty-six men"); an adjective after an infinitive/participle denoting abstract being
can itself go abstract ("to be sincere, is to be wise"); and adjectives sometimes substitute directly for their
corresponding abstract nouns ("the sublime and the beautiful" for *sublimity and beauty*). **16 Notes**
cover: number agreement between certain adjectives and their nouns (*this sort*/*these sorts*, not mixed);
necessarily-singular/-plural adjectives forcing the noun's number (*twenty pounds*, not *twenty pound*);
**each other** restricted to two, **one another** to more than two (the same point already made for
pronouns in `pt06`'s Rule III); comparison-logic traps — the latter term of a *than*-comparison must never
include the former ("iron is more useful than *all the metals*" is self-contradictory; needs "all the *other*
metals"), and symmetrically the latter term of a superlative-*of* comparison must never *exclude* the former;
no double comparatives/superlatives ("the *most straitest* sect"); ordering of stacked adjectives by length
("older and more respectable," not the reverse) and by which quality is most distinguishing ("an agreeable
young man," not "a young agreeable man"); adjectives must not substitute for adverbs in prose ("he writes
*elegant*" → *elegantly* — flagged explicitly as "a vulgar error"); *them* must never function as a
demonstrative adjective ("*them* books" for *those* books, likewise flagged as a vulgar/uneducated-register
marker); **either**/**neither** restricted to two referents, with **any**/**none** for more; and hyphenation
changes meaning in stacked-modifier phrases ("many coloured wings" = many wings that are colored, vs.
"many-coloured wings" = wings of many colors). Also notable among the Observations: a genuinely large
catalogue (Obs. 6–7) of when an adjective may or must follow rather than precede its noun in English — after
other dependent words ("a wall three feet thick"), when the quality results from a verb's action ("virtue
renders life happy"), with certain *a*-prefixed adjectives that never premodify (*afraid, alive, alone, asleep,
aware* — still true in Modern English), and freely in poetry or when several coordinate adjectives follow the
noun.

### Rules X–XIII — Pronoun Agreement (person, number, gender; collective antecedents; *and*-joined and *or/nor*-joined antecedents)

**Rule X: "A Pronoun must agree with its antecedent... in person, number, and gender."** Five stated
exceptions (indefinite antecedents; neuter *it* for a young child/animal of unclear sex; expletive *it* with no
real antecedent; *many a* + singular antecedent sometimes taking a plural pronoun; enallage — a plural
pronoun substituting for a singular one). Genuinely notable Observations: an extended discussion (Obs. 2–7)
of the **"royal we"** and the **historical *thou* → *you* shift** as a register phenomenon, with Brown citing a
contemporary grammarian's testimony that *thou* was the plain, universal second-person singular until
"affected politeness, and a fondness for continental manners" introduced *you* for one person — explicitly
framed as sociolinguistic change within recorded memory, not ancient history; a cross-linguistic register
comparison (Obs. 6) of French (*you* for *thou* universally), Spanish/Portuguese/German (third person for
respectful second-person address), and German's four-way *they/he/you/thou* respect ladder, with *thou*
reserved in German both for closest intimacy (children) and for addressing God — directly comparable
material to this project's interest in honorific/register systems (compare the Korean six-level honorific finding
noted in project memory); and a systematic four-trope account (Obs. 8–11) of when a pronoun representing a
figuratively-used antecedent should agree with the **literal** sense (metaphor: "the *monarch* of mountains
rears *his* snowy head" — agrees with the literal mountain, not the dead metaphor "monarch") vs. the
**figurative** sense (true personification: "Grim Darkness furls *his* leaden shroud"). **16 Notes to Rule X**
include two still-fully-current prescriptive distinctions: **who** restricted to persons and personified animals/
things, **which** to brute animals and literal inanimate things (Note III); and **that** preferred over *who/
which* in seven specific restrictive-clause environments — after a superlative ("the first *that* came"), after
*same*, after antecedent *who*, after mixed person+thing antecedents, after an unlimited/generic antecedent,
and after expletive *it* ("It is you *that* suffer") — a genuinely detailed, still-largely-valid relative-pronoun
selection guide.

**Rule XI: "When the antecedent is a collective noun conveying the idea of plurality, the Pronoun must
agree with it in the plural number"** — the British-English-style "the committee *are*..." pattern, contrasted
with Note I's singular case ("the committee... *it* acts") when unity rather than plurality is meant. Brown is
explicit that both readings are legitimate for the same noun depending on the speaker's intended construal, not
that one is simply an error.

**Rule XII: "When a Pronoun has two or more antecedents connected by *and*, it must agree with them
jointly in the plural"** — three exceptions parallel the equivalent noun-apposition logic already seen: when
the *and*-joined terms describe one single referent, when they're emphatically contrasted into separate
propositions ("the butler, and not the baker, was restored to *his* office"), or when preceded by
*each/every/no*.

**Rule XIII: "When a Pronoun has two or more antecedents connected by *or* or *nor*, it must agree with
them singly, and not as if taken together"** — disjunctively-joined singular antecedents generally take a
singular pronoun, but Brown documents (Obs. 2–3) that mismatched-person/number/gender disjuncts create a
genuine, still-unresolved awkwardness in English (no single pronoun cleanly covers "either man or woman"),
noting that biblical and everyday usage both regularly resort to the plural as a practical workaround despite it
not strictly honoring the rule — essentially the same structural gap that modern English fills with singular
*they*, though Brown does not propose that solution himself.

### Rules XIV–XVII — Finite-Verb Agreement (person/number; collective subjects; *and*-joined and *or/nor*-joined subjects)

**Rule XIV: "Every finite Verb must agree with its subject, or nominative, in person and number."** Stated
to have *no exceptions*. **10 Notes** include several points of durable, still-standard usage guidance: a verb
agreeing with a relative pronoun subject must match the relative's true antecedent, not an intervening word
("one of the greatest masterpieces that ever *was* executed" is wrong — *masterpieces*, not *one*, is the true
antecedent, so *were*); a nominative's modifying adjuncts don't control verb agreement ("the ship, *with all
her furniture*, *was* destroyed" — singular *ship* governs, not the intervening phrase); any phrase, clause, or
quoted word-as-such taken as a single subject takes third-person-singular ("To lie *is* base"); **each, one,
either, neither** are always grammatically third-person singular; and — a genuinely durable style point — a
neuter/passive verb linking two nominatives agrees with the one that *precedes* it ("words *are* wind," not
"is"). Note IX gives a compact three-way mood rule still taught today: future contingency → subjunctive
present ("if thou *forsake* him..."); a mere hypothetical with indefinite time → subjunctive imperfect ("if it
*were* not so..."); a condition assumed as fact → plain indicative ("though he *is* poor, he is contented").

**Rule XV: "When the nominative is a collective noun conveying the idea of plurality, the Verb must agree
with it in the plural number"** — the verb-side counterpart to Rule XI, with the identical logic: the same
collective noun can correctly take a singular verb (unity sense: "the whole number *was* two thousand six
hundred") or a plural verb (distributive sense: "the whole number *were* two thousand six hundred,"
2 Chron. 26:12), with the verb form itself serving as the reader's cue to which sense is meant.

**Rule XVI: "When a Verb has two or more nominatives connected by *and*, it must agree with them jointly
in the plural"** (exceptions mirroring Rule XII's). **7 Notes** include real stylistic craft points: prefer
rephrasing over an awkward forced-plural verb when *and* technically joins two near-synonyms ("there is a
peculiar force *and* beauty..." → better "force, as well as beauty..."); when two *and*-joined subjects are one
affirmed, one negated, the verb follows only the affirmative member ("my poverty, *but not my will*,
consent*s*" — Shakespeare, quoted as correct); the same holds for *as well as*, *but*, *save*-linked subjects,
which likewise count as logically separate propositions taking a singular verb keyed to the first term.

**Rule XVII: "When a Verb has two or more nominatives connected by *or* or *nor*, it must agree with them
singly, and not as if taken together"** (the verb-side mirror of Rule XIII, with the same
mismatched-person/number workaround problem). **15 Notes** are an unusually rich, still-relevant usage
catalogue: speaker-last politeness convention ("thou or *I* must go," "he addressed my father and *me*" —
Note III, echoing the person-ranking note already given for Rule XII); when *and/or/nor*-joined verbs require
different inflected forms, the simplest form goes first, or the nominative/auxiliary should be repeated rather
than forcing a mismatched shared verb; no mixing solemn-style and familiar-style verb forms in the same
sentence; sequence-of-tense rules for verbs of commanding/expecting/hoping/intending — "I *hoped* you
would come," not "would have come" (Note XIV, a distinction still taught as a common ESL/style error
today); eternal-truth propositions should default to present tense even inside past-tense reported speech ("he
seemed hardly to know that two and two *make* four," not *made* — Note XV); and — directly continuing
the register theme already visible throughout this book — a tight list of commonly confused verb pairs still
confused in casual registers today: **lie**/**lay**, **sit**/**set**, **flee**/**fly**, **fall**/**fell** (Note XII:
"he *lay* by the fire," not "*laid*"), plus the non-standard preterit-for-participle and participle-for-preterit
substitutions Brown explicitly flags as register-marked errors: *"I done so"* for *I did so*, *"he seen them"*
for *he saw them*, *"to have went"* for *to have gone* — a period snapshot of exactly the verb-paradigm
leveling patterns (preterit/participle merger) that are still a live marker of vernacular vs. standard register in
English today, directly relevant to this project's slang/register interests.

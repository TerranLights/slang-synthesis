## Grammar points

### Verb definition and four principal parts

"A Verb is a word that signifies *to be, to act*, or *to be acted upon*." Every complete, simple verb has four
**principal parts** that must be known to form everything else: the **Present** (dictionary root form: *love*),
the **Preterit** (simple past: *loved*), the **Imperfect Participle** (*-ing* form, continuance: *loving*), and the
**Perfect Participle** (*-ed*/*-en* form, completion: *loved*). A verb missing any of these is *defective*.

### Two independent four-way classification systems

**By form** (how the preterit/perfect participle are built): **regular** (adds *-d*/*-ed*: *love → loved*),
**irregular** (does not: *see → saw*), **redundant** (both a regular and an irregular form coexist: *thrive →
thrived* or *throve*), **defective** (forms no participles, used in only a few moods/tenses: *beware, ought,
quoth*). Brown counts ~110 simple irregular verbs, ~95 redundant verbs, and a small handful of defectives
(the rest of the language's 4,000–8,000 verbs being regular).

**By signification** (relation to an object): **active-transitive** (action passes to an object: "Cain slew
Abel"), **active-intransitive** (action, no object: "John walks"), **passive** (subject is acted upon: "Caesar
was slain"), **neuter** (neither action nor passion, pure state of being: "The babe sleeps," "There was light").
Brown devotes extensive, genuinely substantive polemic (Obs. 4–12) to defending his four-way division
against the more common contemporary three-way scheme (active/passive/neuter only, with intransitive
actions like *walk, run, fly* absurdly folded into "neuter" purely because they lack a direct object) — he
quotes a pointed classroom anecdote of a pupil objecting that "to run" cannot be neuter "for it always makes
me perspire." He surveys roughly two dozen contemporary grammarians' schemes (ranging from two classes
up to Jaudon's four different four-way split and O. B. Peirce's effective seven-or-eight-way scheme), framing
this as a genuinely live, unsettled area of descriptive terminology circa 1851, not a settled fact.

### Five moods

**Infinitive** (unlimited, no person/number, marked by *to*: "to sleep"), **Indicative** (declares or questions a
fact: "I write," "Do you know?"), **Potential** (expresses power/liberty/possibility/necessity via the signs
*may, can, must, might, could, would, should*: "I can walk"), **Subjunctive** (conditional, doubtful,
contingent, typically introduced by *if, that, though, lest, unless*: "If thou go, see that thou offend not"), and
**Imperative** (commanding, exhorting, entreating, permitting: "Depart thou," "Forgive me").

Brown devotes an unusually long, genuinely substantive methodological argument (Obs. 4–9) to the number
of tenses the **subjunctive** mood should be credited with — a live, contested question across the
grammarians he surveys, with proposals ranging from merging it entirely into the indicative/potential (several
contemporaries effectively deny it exists as a separate mood) up to Murray's sprawling later-edition scheme
that gives the subjunctive all six indicative tenses *plus* all the potential's auxiliary combinations, which
Brown calculates yields "one hundred and thirty-eight different expressions" for a single regular verb's
subjunctive mood, including "no fewer than fifteen different forms in each person and number" of a single
tense. Brown's own resolution: the English subjunctive should have exactly **two** tenses, present and
imperfect, with the present tense's verb-form left uninflected regardless of person ("If he fall," not "if he
falls") — a usage he notes was already eroding in his own day, since many educated writers (including
Murray himself, and Lord Kames editing a Bible quotation) were by 1851 restoring the indicative *-s*/*-eth*
ending even in clearly conditional/subjunctive clauses.

### Six tenses

**Present** ("I hear a noise"), **Imperfect** (simple past, fully completed: "I saw him yesterday"), **Perfect**
(completed within a still-open period: "I have seen him today"), **Pluperfect** (past-before-a-past: "I had
seen him, when I met you"), **First-future** ("I shall see him again"), **Second-future** (future-before-a-
future: "I shall have seen him by tomorrow noon"). Brown flags the traditional name "Imperfect" as a
historical misnomer inherited uncritically from Latin grammar — in English this tense is in fact *more*
completely past than the "Perfect," the names having been carried over by analogy to the *-ing* participle
used to translate the genuinely-incomplete Latin imperfect (*dormiebam*, "I was sleeping"), not because the
English tense itself denotes incompleteness.

### Persons and numbers; the "solemn style" vs. "familiar style" split

Where a verb inflects at all, the regular pattern is **second person singular** = root + *-st*/*-est* (*thou
lovest, thou givest*) and **third person singular** = root + *-s*/*-es* (*he loves, he gives*) — but Brown is
explicit (carried through the whole chapter's paradigm tables) that this *-st*/*-eth*-type inflection is a mark
of the **solemn style** (liturgical/elevated register: *thou lovest, thou dost, he hath*) that had already been
substantially displaced in the **familiar style** (ordinary contemporary speech/writing: *thou loved, thou did,
he has*) by his own time — i.e. Brown's conjugation tables are explicitly dual-register throughout, a built-in
diachronic/register contrast directly relevant to this project's interests.

### Auxiliaries

The auxiliary verbs are *do, be, have, shall, will, may, can,* and *must*, with their inflected variants. Of these,
only *do, be,* and *have* are also complete verbs in their own right when used as main verbs; the rest are
always defective. Brown explicitly rejects classing *ought* and *let* as auxiliaries (Obs. 3) — *ought*
because, uniquely among the auxiliary-like words, it takes a following *to* ("He ought *to* help you," unlike
any true auxiliary); *let* because no true auxiliary governs the objective case the way *let* does ("Let *him*
help you"). He flags a live, then-unresolved descriptive question (Obs. 8–9) over whether **need**, when used
before another verb to express necessity, has effectively become a ninth potential-mood auxiliary (uninflected:
"he need not go") rather than remaining a fully regular, fully inflected main verb ("he needs not go") — citing
dozens of period examples on both sides and declining to rule definitively, noting he has "not yet knowingly"
adopted the uninflected form himself but suspects it may deserve auxiliary status. **Do**/**did** (present/
imperfect, sign of indicative present/imperfect), **be**/**was** (present/imperfect), and **have**/**had**
(present, but sign of the indicative perfect/pluperfect respectively) are each given their own basic present/
imperfect inflection table.

### The six conjugational forms

Beyond the base simple conjugation (only the present and imperfect tenses can be formed by the simple verb
alone — all other tenses are necessarily auxiliary compounds), Brown works through five further systematic
transformations, each stated as a formation rule (full paradigms, being mechanically regular once the rule is
known, are not reproduced here):
1. **Simple form** — base paradigm (*I love, I loved*).
2. **Compound or progressive form** — auxiliary *to be* + imperfect participle, expressing continuance (*I
   am loving, I was loving*).
3. **Form of passive verbs** — auxiliary *to be* + perfect participle of a transitive verb, with the former
   object promoted to subject and the agent (if named) introduced by *by* (*I am loved; Pompey was
   conquered by Caesar*).
4. **Form of negation** — the adverb *not* placed after the verb or after the first auxiliary (*I love not*, or *I
   do not love*); infinitives and participles instead take the negative *first* (*not to love, not loving*).
5. **Form of question** — the nominative placed after the verb or after the first auxiliary (*Love I?* or *Do I
   love?*).
6. **Form of question with negation** — both inversions combined (nominative *and* *not* placed after the
   verb/first auxiliary).

### A live period usage dispute: does *who*/*that* "agree" and does the 19th-c. grammar-book market actually know how many cases/moods exist?

Not a separate topic here, but worth flagging as a throughline across this chapter and `pt03`: Brown's Verb
chapter continues the same pattern already seen for noun cases (`pt03`) — a long survey of actively
disagreeing contemporary authorities (here, on mood/tense counts) that reads less like settled doctrine being
transmitted and more like a live methodological argument Brown is trying to referee and resolve. This is a
recurring structural feature of the whole book, not unique to verbs.

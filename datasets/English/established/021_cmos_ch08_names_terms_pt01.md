## Grammar points

### 8.1 Chicago's preference for the "down" style

Proper nouns are usually capitalized, but Chicago prefers sparing use of capitals for terms *derived
from* or *associated with* proper nouns — the "down" style. "Brussels" (the city) is capitalized, but
"brussels sprouts" (not necessarily from Brussels) is not; "President Obama" is capitalized, but "the
president" is not (developed fully at §§8.18–32). In certain nonacademic contexts (e.g., a press
release), such terms may be capitalized instead.

### 8.2 Italics and quotation marks

Chicago prefers italics to set off titles of major/freestanding works (books, journals, movies,
paintings), extending to ship/craft names, species names, and legal cases. Quotation marks are
generally reserved for titles of subsections of larger works (chapter/article titles, poems collected
into a series). Some titles (e.g., a book series or website under which many works are collected) are
neither italicized nor quoted. Full treatment is cross-referenced to §§8.154–95 (outside this chunk's
page range).

### 8.3 Personal names — additional resources

For well-known deceased persons, Chicago generally prefers the spellings in *Merriam-Webster's
Biographical Dictionary* or the biographical section of *Merriam-Webster's Collegiate Dictionary*
("Webster's"); for living persons, *Who's Who* or *Who's Who in America*. Where different spellings
appear in different sources (e.g., W. E. B. DuBois versus W. E. B. Du Bois), the editor must choose
one and stay consistent. Names can usually be checked/cross-checked via officially sponsored online
resources (authors' names, library catalogs, booksellers); a living person's name should, wherever
possible, match that person's own usage.

### 8.4 Capitalization of personal names

Names and initials of real or fictitious persons are capitalized, with a space between separate
initials except when the initials are used entirely alone (e.g., "LBJ"). Unconventional spellings
strongly preferred by the name's bearer or a pen name (e.g., bell hooks) should usually be respected
in appropriate contexts — library catalogs generally capitalize all such names regardless. E. E.
Cummings can safely be capitalized despite a popular image of a lowercased name, since it was one of
his publishers, not Cummings himself, who lowercased it. Most editors draw the line at beginning a
sentence with a lowercased name, instead rewriting the sentence or capitalizing the first letter for
that occasion.

### 8.5 Names with particles

Many names include particles such as "de," "d'," "de la," "von," "van," and "ten." Capitalization and
spacing practice varies widely and should be confirmed against a biographical dictionary or other
authoritative source. When the surname is used alone, the particle is usually retained, capitalized or
lowercased and spaced as it is in the full name — though it is always capitalized at the start of a
sentence. "Le," "La," and "L'" are always capitalized when not preceded by "de"; "the," which sometimes
appears with the English form of a Native American name (e.g., "the Prophet," for Tenskwatawa), is
always lowercased.

### 8.6 Hyphenated and extended names

A hyphenated last name, or a last name consisting of two or more elements, should never be shorn of
one of its elements. For prominent or historical figures, *Webster's* and other reliable alphabetical
listings usually indicate where the last name actually begins (e.g., "Vaughan Williams," not
"Williams"; "Mies van der Rohe," not "van der Rohe" — though "Mies" alone is also an accepted short
form). Some multi-word names, by contrast, are conventionally shortened to only the final element
(John Hope Franklin → Franklin; Charlotte Perkins Gilman → Gilman). For unhyphenated compound names of
lesser-known persons whose own usage can't be determined, use only the last element (including any
particle; see §8.5, and §8.11 for the Spanish case).

### 8.7 French names

The particles "de" and "d'" are lowercased except at the start of a sentence. When the last name is
used alone, "de" (but not "d'") is often dropped; its occasional retention (as in "de Gaulle") is a
matter of tradition rather than logic. When a name begins with closed-up "de" (as in "Debussy"), that
initial letter is always capitalized. "La" is usually capitalized when it precedes a name and is
always retained when the last name is used alone. "Du" is usually lowercased in a full name but is
retained and capitalized when the last name is used alone (a name beginning with closed-up "Du," as
in "Dupont," always capitalizes that letter). "Le" is capitalized with or without the first name.
Initials standing for a hyphenated given name are themselves hyphenated (Jean-Paul Sartre → J.-P.
Sartre). The source notes considerable real variation in French usage beyond these general guidelines.

### 8.8 German and Portuguese names

In the original languages, particles in German and Portuguese names are lowercased and usually dropped
when the last name is used alone. In English contexts, however, if another (capitalized/particle-
retained) form of the name is more widely known, that form may be used instead — the standard example
being "Vasco da Gama," always known with the particle retained, unlike "Alexander von Humboldt,"
usually shortened to "Humboldt."

### 8.9 Italian names

Particles in Italian names are most often uppercased and retained when the last name is used alone
(D'Annunzio, Da Ponte, Della Robbia). In many older aristocratic names, however, the particle is
traditionally lowercased and dropped when the surname stands alone (Beatrice d'Este → Este; Lorenzo
de' Medici → Medici).

### 8.10 Dutch names

In English usage, Dutch particles ("van," "van den," "ter," and the like) are lowercased when the full
name is given but are usually capitalized when only the last name is used (Vincent van Gogh → Van
Gogh).

### 8.11 Spanish names

Many Spanish names are composed of both the father's and the mother's family names, usually in that
order, sometimes joined by "y" ("and"). When the given name is omitted, such persons are usually
referred to by both family names, but sometimes by only one (usually, though not always, the first of
the two), according to personal preference — using both is never incorrect. Spanish family names that
include an article, a preposition, or both are treated the same way as analogous French names (see
§8.7). Traditionally, a married woman replaced her mother's family name with her husband's (first)
family name, sometimes preceded by "de" — though many modern women in Spanish-speaking countries no
longer take their husband's family name. (For alphabetizing such names, see §16.84, outside this
chunk's range.)

### 8.12 Russian names

Russian family names, as well as patronymics (the name preceding the family name, derived from the
father's given name), sometimes take different endings for male and female family members (Lenin's
real name, Vladimir Ilyich Ulyanov, versus his sister Maria Ilyinichna Ulyanova). In Russian sources
(and their English translations), often only the given name and patronymic are used; in such instances
the patronymic should not be mistaken for either a middle name or the family name.

### 8.13 Hungarian names

In Hungarian practice, the family name precedes the given name (Molnár Ferenc, Kodály Zoltán); in
English contexts, however, such names are usually inverted to the English given-name-first order
(Ferenc Molnár, Zoltán Kodály). In some cases the family name includes an initial (É. Kiss Katalin);
when inverted for English contexts (Katalin É. Kiss), the initial should not be mistaken for a middle
initial — for indexing such a name, it is properly listed under the initial (see §16.79, outside this
chunk's range).

### 8.14 Arabic names

Surnames of Arabic origin (which are strictly surnames rather than family names) are often prefixed by
elements such as "Abu," "Abd," "Ibn," "al," or "el." Since these are integral parts of the name — just
as "Mc" or "Fitz" are parts of certain English names — they should not be dropped when the surname is
used alone. Capitalization of such elements varies widely, but terms joined with a hyphen are usually
lowercased. Names of rulers of older times are often shortened to the first part of the name rather
than the second; Harun al-Rashid is often shortened to "Harun," since "al-Rashid" ("Rightly Guided")
was Harun's *laqab*, a descriptive name he took on his accession to the caliphate. (See also §§11.99,
11.100, 16.76, outside this chunk's range.)

### 8.15 Chinese names

In Chinese practice, the family name comes before the given name; this practice should be followed in
English contexts with names of Chinese persons, but not with names of persons of Chinese origin whose
names have been anglicized. (For the Pinyin and Wade-Giles transliteration systems, see §§11.102–10,
outside this chunk's range.)

### 8.16 Japanese names

In Japanese usage the family name precedes the given name. Japanese names are sometimes westernized,
however, by authors writing in English or by persons of Japanese origin living in the West.

### 8.17 Other Asian names

In some Asian countries, people are usually known by their given name rather than by a surname or
family name — the Indonesian writer Pramoedya Ananta Toer, for example, is referred to in short form
as "Pramoedya," not "Toer." If in doubt, the source recommends using the full form of a name in all
references, or consulting an expert or the usage in a reputable source discussing the person in
question. (For further examples, see §§16.77, 16.81, 16.85, 16.86, outside this chunk's range.)

### 8.18 Titles and offices — the general rule

Civil, military, religious, and professional titles are capitalized when they immediately precede a
personal name and are thus used as part of the name (typically replacing the title-holder's first
name). In formal prose and other generic text (as opposed to promotional/ceremonial contexts or a
heading), titles are normally lowercased when following a name or used in place of a name (but see the
exceptions at §8.19). Although both the first and second names may be used after a capitalized title
("President Abraham Lincoln"; but see §8.20), Chicago prefers to avoid this usage, especially with
civil, corporate, and academic titles (see §§8.21, 8.26, 8.27). Once a title has been given, it need
not be repeated each time the person's name is subsequently mentioned. (For abbreviated title forms,
see §§10.11–27, outside this chunk's range.)

### 8.19 Exceptions to the general rule

In promotional or ceremonial contexts — such as a displayed list of donors in a book's front matter, or
a list of corporate officers in an annual report — titles are usually capitalized even when following a
personal name. Exceptions may also be made in other contexts for reasons of courtesy or diplomacy. A
title used alone, in place of a personal name, is capitalized only in such contexts as a toast, a
formal introduction, or direct address.

### 8.20 Titles used in apposition

When a title is used in apposition before a personal name — that is, not alone and not as part of the
name, but usually preceded by "the" or by a modifier — it is considered not a title but rather a
descriptive phrase, and is therefore lowercased (the empress Elizabeth of Austria, *but* Empress
Elizabeth of Austria; German chancellor Angela Merkel, *but* Chancellor Merkel; the German-born pope
Benedict XVI; former presidents Reagan and Ford; the then secretary of state Colin Powell).

### 8.21 Civil titles

Much of the usage actually seen in official literature generated by political offices contradicts the
rule above, since capitalization of a title in any position is the norm there (see §8.19). In formal
academic prose, however, civil titles are capitalized only when used as part of the name (except as
noted). This section works through an extensive worked list across the US executive, legislative, and
judicial branches, and across non-US/international offices — president, vice president, secretary of
state, senator, representative, the Speaker (capitalized in all contexts to avoid confusion with a
generic "speaker"), chief justice, associate justice, chief judge, ambassador, governor, mayor, state
senator/representative, governor general, finance minister, prime minister, premier, member of
Parliament, chief whip, foreign secretary/minister, chancellor, chancellor of the exchequer, and the
Lord Privy Seal (UK; always capitalized regardless of position) — each shown in both its capitalized
preceding-name form and its lowercased generic/following-name form. (See also §10.13; for "the
Honorable" and similar terms of respect, see §8.32, §10.18, both outside this chunk's range.)

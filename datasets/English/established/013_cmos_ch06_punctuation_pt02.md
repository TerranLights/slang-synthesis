## Grammar points

### §6.25 Commas with "however," "therefore," "indeed," and so forth

Adverbs such as *however*, *therefore*, and *indeed* are traditionally set off by commas (sometimes
paired with a semicolon, see §6.55) — but only when the adverb is parenthetical. If the adverb is
essential to the clause's meaning, or no pause is intended, no commas are used. Example: "A truly
efficient gasoline-powered engine remains, however, a pipe dream" vs. "Indeed, not one test subject
accurately predicted the amount of soup in the bowl" (no internal comma needed since *indeed* opens
the sentence without a felt pause before it being necessary — contrast with the parenthetical case).

### §6.26 Commas with restrictive and nonrestrictive phrases

A restrictive phrase — essential to the noun's meaning/identity — takes no commas. A nonrestrictive
phrase (merely supplementary) is enclosed in commas, or preceded by one if it ends the sentence.
Contrast: "The woman with the guitar over her shoulder is my sister" (restrictive — identifies which
woman) vs. "My sister, with her guitar over her shoulder, turned to the drummer..." (nonrestrictive —
she's already identified as "my sister").

### §6.27 Commas with "such as" and "including"

The same restrictive/nonrestrictive logic from §6.26 applies to phrases introduced by *such as* or
*including*: set off by commas when nonrestrictive, not when restrictive. "The entire band, including
the matutinal lead singer, overslept the noon rehearsal" (nonrestrictive) vs. "Words such as
*matutinal* and *onomatopoetic* are best avoided in everyday speech" (restrictive — specifies which
words).

### §6.28 Commas with independent clauses joined by conjunctions

A comma usually precedes a coordinating conjunction (*and*, *but*, *or*, *so*, *yet*, etc.) joining two
independent clauses. The comma may be dropped if the clauses are short and closely linked, unless part
of a series. The rule applies equally to imperative sentences where the subject *you* is implied.
Example: "We activated the alarm, but the intruder was already inside," vs. the short/closely-linked
exception, "Electra played the guitar and Tambora sang."

### §6.29 Commas with compound predicates

Unlike two independent clauses (§6.28), a comma is not normally used between the parts of a compound
predicate (two or more verbs sharing one subject). A comma may occasionally be needed anyway to avoid
a misreading, as in: "She recognized the man who entered the room, and gasped" (comma prevents
misreading *entered the room and gasped* as a single compound action of "the man").

### §6.30 Comma preceding main clause

A dependent clause that precedes its main clause should be followed by a comma. Example: "If you
accept our conditions, we shall agree to the proposal."

### §6.31 Comma following main clause

A dependent clause that *follows* the main clause should **not** be preceded by a comma if it is
restrictive (essential to the main clause's meaning); it should be preceded by a comma if merely
supplementary/parenthetical. CMOS is explicit that this distinction is "occasionally tenuous" and that
the presence or absence of the comma can change the meaning entirely — compare "He didn't run, because
he was afraid to move" (he stood still, out of fear) with "He didn't run because he was afraid to
move" (he ran, but fear wasn't the reason). When genuinely ambiguous, rephrase rather than rely on the
comma alone.

### §6.32 "And if," "that if," and the like

When two conjunctions sit next to each other (e.g. *and if*, *but if*), they do not need a comma
between them. Example: "They decided that if it rained, they would reschedule the game."

### §6.33 Commas with coordinate adjectives

When two or more adjectives before a noun are coordinate — joinable by *and* without changing the
meaning, and reversible in order — they are separated by commas. If the adjectives are not coordinate
(one forms a tighter unit with the noun), no comma is used. "It is going to be a long, hot, exhausting
summer" (coordinate: long *and* hot *and* exhausting) vs. "She opted for an inexpensive quartz watch"
(not coordinate — "quartz watch" is a unit).

### §6.34 Commas with repeated adjectives

When an adjective is repeated immediately before a noun, a comma normally appears between the pair.
Example: "Many, many people have enjoyed the book."

### §6.35 Commas with introductory participial phrases

An introductory participial phrase is set off by a comma, unless the sentence is inverted and the
phrase immediately precedes the verb. "Exhilarated by the morning's work, she skipped lunch..." vs.
the inverted exception, "Running along behind the wagon was the archduke himself!"

### §6.36 Commas with introductory adverbial phrases

An introductory adverbial phrase is often, but not always, set off by a comma — shorter phrases are
less likely to need one than longer ones, and a comma should never be used if the phrase immediately
precedes the verb it modifies. "After reading the note, Henrietta turned pale" (comma optional/common
for a phrase of this length) vs. "Before the footlights stood one of the most notorious rakes of the
twenty-first century" (no comma — phrase precedes the verb).

### §6.37 Commas with "oh" and "ah"

A comma usually follows an exclamatory *oh* or *ah*, unless it's followed by an exclamation point or
forms part of a fixed phrase (e.g. "oh boy"). The vocative or archaic/poetic *O* never takes a
following comma. Example: "Oh, you're right!" vs. "Oh mighty king" (fixed vocative phrase, no comma)
vs. "O wild West Wind..." (archaic vocative *O*).

### §6.38 Commas with direct address

Names or words used in direct address, and in informal correspondence, are set off by commas (formal
correspondence instead uses a colon after the name). Example: "Ms. Jones, please come in." / "Dear
Judy, . . ."

### §6.39 "Yes," "no," and the like

An introductory *yes*, *no*, *well*, and similar words are usually followed by a comma, with
exceptions more common in informal prose or dialogue (e.g. the exclamatory "No you will not!" takes no
comma).

### §6.40 Commas with "not" phrases

A phrase beginning with *not*, interjected to clarify which noun is meant, is set off by commas.
Example: "We hoped the mayor herself, not her assistant, would attend the meeting."

### §6.41 Commas with "not . . . but," "not only . . . but," and the like

Interjected phrases of the *not . . . but* or *not only . . . but* type usually need no commas. If the
phrase requires special emphasis or clarification (an editorial judgment call), commas — or
alternatively a dash in place of the first comma — may be used to set it off.

### §6.42 Commas with "the more," "the less," and so on

A comma is customarily used between clauses of the *the more . . . the more* type, though shorter
phrases of this kind rarely need one. "The more I discover about the workings of mechanical
movements, the less I seem to care..." vs. the short exception, "The more the merrier."

### §6.43 Commas with "that is," "namely," "for example," and similar expressions

Expressions like *that is* are traditionally followed by a comma; they may instead be preceded by an
em dash or semicolon, or the whole phrase they introduce may be enclosed in parentheses or em dashes.
*Or* used in the sense of *that is* ("in other words") is likewise set off by commas, as in "The
compass stand, or binnacle, must be situated within the helmsman's field of vision." The abbreviations
*i.e.* and *e.g.*, when used in formal writing, should be confined to parentheses or notes and followed
by a comma.

### §6.44 Commas between homonyms

For ease of reading, two words spelled alike but with different grammatical functions may be separated
by a comma if clarification seems desirable, at the editor's discretion. Example: "Let us march in, in
twos" vs. the no-comma default "Whatever is, is good."

### §6.45 Commas with dates

In the month-day-year date style, commas set off the year (e.g. "February 2, 2006,"). In the
day-month-year style (standard in British English), no commas are needed. Where only month and year
are given, or a specific named day (e.g. a holiday) with a year, neither style uses a comma — "In March
2008 she turned seventy-five" / "On Thanksgiving Day 1998 they celebrated their seventy-fifth
anniversary." Cross-references the adjectival-date rule at §5.82, §§9.30–37 for dates generally, and
§9.37 for the ISO year-month-day style.

### §6.46 Commas with addresses and place-names in text

Commas set off the individual elements of an address or place-name run into running text. In a mailing
address, commas are used as sparingly as possible — e.g. no comma between a street name and a directional
abbreviation like *SW*, or before a postal code. When an institutional name incorporating a place-name
appears mid-clause, a second comma is required to close it off: "California State University,
Northridge, has an enrollment of . . ." but "The University of Wisconsin–Madison has an enrollment of
. . ." (here the place-name is joined by an en dash as part of the institution's own name, not set off
by commas).

### §6.47 "Jr.," "Sr.," and the like

Commas are not required around *Jr.* or *Sr.*, and are never used to set off a numeral suffix like
*II* or *III*, when these are part of a name in running text. In an inverted (index-style) name,
however, a comma is required before such a trailing element: "Doe, John, Sr." If using a comma with
*Jr.*/*Sr.* would otherwise force an awkward possessive, CMOS recommends rephrasing instead.

### §6.48 "Inc.," "Ltd.," and the like

Commas are not required around *Inc.*, *Ltd.*, or similar as part of a company's name. Even if a
particular company's own corporate documentation uses commas around such an element, general writing
about the company should favor a single consistent house style rather than making per-company
exceptions.

### §6.49 Commas to indicate elision

A comma is often used to mark the omission of a word or words that are readily understood from
context: "In Illinois there are seventeen such schools; in Ohio, twenty; in Indiana, thirteen." The
comma may be dropped if the elliptical construction is clear without it, as in "Jasper missed her and
she him."

### §6.50 Commas with quotations

Quoted dialogue or text is traditionally introduced with a comma. No comma is needed if the quotation
is introduced by *that*, *whether*, or a similar conjunction: "Was it Stevenson who said that 'the
cruelest lies are often told in silence'?" Cross-references §6.9 (comma placement relative to closing
quotation marks), §6.51 (quoted maxims/proverbs), and chapter 13 generally for quotations and
dialogue.

### §6.51 Commas with maxims and proverbs

Maxims, proverbs, mottoes, and similar familiar expressions are punctuated the same way as appositives
(§6.23, covered in part 1 of this chapter) unless the expression is directly attributed to a speaker or
writer, in which case the quotation rules of §6.50/§13.18 apply instead. Whether such an expression
is enclosed in quotation marks depends largely on the surrounding sentence's syntax. Example: "Tom's
favorite proverb, 'A rolling stone gathers no moss,' proved wrong" (attributed, quoted) vs. "It is
untrue that a rolling stone gathers no moss" (unattributed, unquoted, treated as an appositive-like
maxim).

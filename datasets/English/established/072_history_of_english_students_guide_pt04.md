## Grammar points

### EModE spelling: standardization driven by printing, not scholarly consensus (§5.4.1)

Early Modern English inherited genuine orthographic variation from ME, but a perceived "instability"
in English spelling, especially unfavorable compared to classical Latin/Greek's long literary
pedigree, drove sustained 16th–17th-century reform efforts. Phonetician John Cheke proposed reforms
based on his (idiosyncratic) understanding of ancient Greek pronunciation, but applied them
inconsistently; John Hart (*The Orthographie*, 1569) proposed a more systematically phonetic system
(e.g. a subscript dot to mark vowel length instead of doubled graphs or final *-e*, new graphs to
disambiguate [ð]/[θ]); William Bullokar opposed Hart's proposals with his own counter-reforms. None
of these deliberate reform proposals succeeded. What actually did fix English spelling conventions
was, instead, the accumulated **practical** influence of educators and printers: Richard Mulcaster's
*The First Parte of the Elementarie* (1582), though not itself a spelling-reform tract, became
hugely influential simply because Mulcaster's institutional authority (headmaster of St Paul's
School) meant every word he used was given one consistent spelling that then propagated into later
spelling books; his successor Alexander Gil's *Logonomia Anglica* (1619) mixed a largely phonemic
system with some deliberately **etymologizing** respellings of French loans to better reflect their
ultimate Latin origin — giving modern *throne* (< ME *trone* < French *trone*), *theatre* (< *teatre*),
*apothecary* (< *apotecaire*) their modern-looking spellings, though this etymologizing process
sometimes got the actual etymology wrong (modern *author* was "corrected" along the same lines from
ME *autour*/French *autor*, even though the real Latin source is in fact *auctor*). By about 1650
spelling had largely stabilized to one fixed spelling per word, reinforced via schooling and spelling
guides, essentially through the self-reinforcing, unplanned convergence of printers' practical
choices rather than through any single successful top-down reform program. Specific conventions that
became fixed over this period: word-final *-e* as a vowel-length/consonant-quality marker (*case* vs
ME *cas*; *rage* [dʒ] vs *rag* [g]); ⟨y⟩ used to represent [ð] in *the*/*that* (a shape-based
successor to ⟨þ⟩, later mistaken by later readers for "ye", giving rise to the spurious modern
pseudo-archaism *Ye Olde* ...); ⟨i⟩/⟨j⟩ and ⟨u⟩/⟨v⟩ gradually separating into distinct
vowel-vs-consonant graphs (not completed for the uppercase V/U distinction until nearly 1700); and
⟨s⟩/⟨z⟩, where ⟨z⟩ consistently marked [z] but ⟨s⟩ remained ambiguous between [s]/[z] (*haste*
vs *fees*), the likely source of the *-ise*/-*ize* spelling-variant pair (popularly but inaccurately
assumed to be a clean British/American split). (pp.149–151)

### The Great Vowel Shift (§5.4.2.2)

Beginning in the 15th century in the southern ME dialects, the long stressed vowels underwent a
large-scale, systematic upward/diphthongizing shift in articulation — the best-known single sound
change in English's history. In simplified schematic terms (Figure 5.1): ME /aː/ (*nameliche*) rose
to /ɛː/ (*clene*); ME /ɛː/ (*swete*) rose to /eː/; ME /eː/ rose and diphthongized to /iː/ (*min(e)*);
on the back side, ME /ɔː/ (*growe*) rose to /oː/ (*roote*); ME /oː/ rose and diphthongized to /uː/
(*bour*); and ME /aʊ/ (*lawe*) lowered/backed toward /ɔː/. The triggering mechanism is debated.
**Structuralist** accounts (grounded in the idea that a vowel system seeks to maintain symmetric,
non-overlapping articulatory/acoustic space for each vowel) propose either a **drag chain**
(diphthongization of the already-highest front/back vowels /iː/, /uː/ first vacated "space" at the
top of the system, pulling the vowels below them progressively upward to fill the gap) or a **push
chain** (a lower vowel like /aː/ began encroaching on the space of the next-highest vowel, "pushing"
each successive vowel upward in turn). The two mechanisms are not mutually exclusive — some varieties
show evidence of both operating together — and dialectal exceptions are informative: some Scots
varieties never diphthongized /uː/ (preserving *mouse*/*house* as [muːs]/[huːs]), plausibly because
ME /ɔː/ fronted rather than raising in Scots, leaving no "pushed" gap to force /uː/ to move. A
further, separate question the mechanism-focused explanations don't answer is *why* the Shift began
in the first place — extralinguistic/social explanations (cited: Le Page, in Samuels 1972) propose
that upwardly-mobile members of the emerging merchant/middle classes, newly able to challenge the
upper classes' exclusivity, were imitated or in turn tried to distance themselves via pronunciation,
while Smith (1996) cautions that the actual trigger(s) likely differed regionally and that the Shift
may ultimately reflect many individually-motivated, sociolinguistically-interacting micro-changes
rather than one single unified event. (pp.154–155)

### EModE nouns, adjectives, pronouns: fossilization of ME variation around single standards (§5.4.3.1–5.4.3.3)

EModE nouns largely resemble their modern descendants: the ME *-en* plural (*kine*, *eyen*, *hosen*)
was actively proscribed by grammarians and survives only as isolated fossils (*oxen*, *children*,
*brethren*); the OE/ME-descended *-(e)s* genitive singular continued, with the possessive apostrophe
optional in the 16th century and only fully conventionalized by the 18th; positional (rather than
inflectional) possessive marking for plural/sibilant-final nouns, the *his*-genitive (which, despite
being the most actively criticized of the non-inflectional options, continued in restricted use
mainly before sibilant-final nouns), the periphrastic *of*-genitive, and the newly-dominant "**group
genitive**" (possessive marking attached to the end of a whole possessor noun phrase, *the Queen of
England's will*, which overtook the older "split" order *the Queen's will of England*) all co-existed
and were stylistically exploited by poets for rhyme/meter variety (Ben Jonson's *Alchemist* is cited
as a showcase of practically every possessive-marking option in a single passage). Adjectives by this
period carried only comparative/superlative inflections (*-er*/*-est*), but periphrastic *more*/*most*
had also become fully available and the two strategies were often doubly marked together for
emphasis (*most unkindest*) before this doubling came to be stigmatized and proscribed by the late
17th century, eventually settling into the modern rule restricting *-er*/*-est* to monosyllabic and
certain disyllabic adjectives. Pronouns saw two especially significant developments: (1) **thou/thee
vs. ye/you** shifted from a pure number-based distinction (as in OE/ME) toward a sociolinguistic
**T/V system** modeled on the Romance tu/vous "non-reciprocal power" and "reciprocal solidarity"
semantics identified by Brown & Gilman (1960) — social superiors received *you*, inferiors received
*thou*, while social equals exchanged either mutual *you* (upper classes) or mutual *thou* (lower
classes), with the marked, code-breaking use of the "wrong" form available as a deliberate insult or
expression of strong emotion; *thou*'s eventual decline was accelerated by its association (via
Quaker usage, who adopted it as an egalitarian marker) with a specific "rebellious religious group,"
after which it became increasingly confined to biblical/archaic/dialectal registers. (2) The
**my/mine, thy/thine** pair shifted from a ME phonological distinction (conditioned by whether the
following noun began with a vowel) to a **grammatical** one: *my*/*thy* for attributive use, *mine*/
*thine* for independent/nominal use — the pattern survives today only for *my*/*mine* since *thine*
itself has become archaic. Innovative analogical "n-forms" like *hisn*, *hern* briefly arose on the
pattern of *mine*/*thine* but were stigmatized out of use quickly. The old gender-neutral possessive
*his* (used for both masculine and neuter referents) persisted until the early 17th century and was
sometimes disambiguated with *of it*/*thereof*, before the now-standard neuter possessive **its** —
an entirely EModE innovation, first appearing in the late 16th century, likely by analogy with the
other 's-marked possessive pronouns — rapidly displaced it over the 17th century. Finally, EModE saw
the rise of **reflexive self-compounds** (*myself*, *himself*, etc.), built on possessive
(attributive) pronoun forms for 1st/2nd person but object-pronoun forms for 3rd person, and *self*
itself became the only element in the modern pronoun system still marked for number (*yourself* vs
*yourselves*). (pp.156–159)

### EModE verbs: continued weak/strong leveling, inflection reduction, and the expansion of *do*-periphrasis (§5.4.3.4)

The ME weak/strong distinction and its associated changes continued through EModE. Present-indicative
inflection lost 1st-person-singular marking entirely; 2nd-person-singular *-t* was sometimes added to
modals (*wilt*, *schalt*) and to past-tense *to be* (*wast*, *wert*), but the whole 2nd-singular
paradigm declined over the 17th century alongside the loss of *thou* itself. In the 3rd person
singular, two competing inflections — *-eth* (associated with formal/official/biblical register) and
*-es* (from the ME northern dialect, used more in private/informal writing) — coexisted, with poets
exploiting both for metrical effect (monosyllabic *hates* vs disyllabic *hateth*) before *-eth*
declined into archaic/biblical-only status, leaving modern standard *-s* as the direct descendant of
the northern ME form. Strong preterite/participle forms continued converting to weak ones by analogy
throughout the period (*glide/glode* → regularized; *seethe/sod(den)* → regularized), some historically
weak verbs developed new vowel-changing ("strong-looking") preterites (*dig/dug*, *spit/spat*,
*stick/stuck*), and some historically-strong past participles (*drunken*, *molten*, *sodden*) survive
in Modern English only as adjectives, no longer as verb forms. The auxiliary verb **do** developed
substantially during EModE: its ME past-marking periphrastic use (*did carye*) continued and was
exploited by poets alongside inflected alternatives for rhyme/meter purposes (the *Hamlet* "did
sting"/"stung" contrast); and *do*-periphrasis expanded by analogy to **questions** (replacing
subject-main-verb inversion, *Seest thou* → *Dost thou see*) and to **negation** (inserting *do*
specifically so *not* could occupy its regular post-auxiliary position, rather than appearing after
the main verb or, with pronoun objects, after the object) — regularizing what had previously been a
patchwork of auxiliary-less negative/question constructions into the single, do-supported pattern
that became obligatory in Modern English by roughly the 18th century. (pp.159–161)

### EModE syntax: continued (S)VO dominance, inversion, recapitulation, and the *copia verborum* stylistic convention (§5.4.4)

EModE word order was predominantly (S)VO, with subject-verb or (more commonly) subject-auxiliary
inversion occurring after fronted adverbials and with topicalized objects (paralleling the patterns
already seen in OE and ME) — a pattern also linked to question-formation, discussed above. Two further
stylistic/syntactic features of the educated EModE written register are noted: **recapitulation**
(a fronted, often long/complex noun phrase is "picked up" again by a resumptive pronoun later in the
same sentence — a device with no equivalent in Modern English grammatical norms), and the omission
of an **obligatory** (by Modern English standards) subject pronoun in certain clause types. Post-
nominal placement of one or more adjectives (continuing a pattern already noted in ME) was rare but
attested, and was eventually displaced by the now-standard pre-nominal pattern. A broader stylistic
convention worth noting is **copia verborum** ("copiousness of words"), a prized rhetorical device in
educated EModE prose whereby authors deliberately paired/listed multiple near-synonymous words or
phrases (often joined with conjunctions) for emphasis and stylistic richness — distinct from, but
related to, the simple didactic gloss-pairing of an unfamiliar loanword with a familiar synonym
(*counterfete and likene*) discussed under vocabulary below. (pp.161–162)

### EModE vocabulary: the inkhorn-term controversy and vocabulary enrichment (§5.4.5)

EModE scholars held a genuine "love-hate" relationship with Latin: simultaneously promoting English
as the nation's proper medium while still treating Latin's long literary/scholarly pedigree as the
standard of real sophistication. This produced the period's fastest vocabulary growth in English's
history (concentrated especially 1530–1660), filled through **borrowing** (the majority from Latin,
entering via theology, philosophy, law, navigation, biology, and anatomy — domains where Latin had
long held an unchallenged terminological monopoly — but also significant ongoing French borrowing and
smaller contributions from Italian, Spanish, Dutch, Portuguese, Arabic, indigenous American languages,
and Persian/Hindi via colonial/trade contact), **coinage** (both "foreignized" hybrids mixing native
bases with newly-naturalized Latin/Greek/French affixes, and deliberately "native" formations using
only inherited English word-formation resources, the latter reflecting a current of nationalist
Teutonism/anti-Latin sentiment and especially favored in poetry for its aesthetic link to a Chaucerian
tradition), and **archaism revival** (reviving obsolete English words as an alternative, native-pedigree
source of "elevated" vocabulary, again mainly in poetic register). This vocabulary-enrichment project
sparked the well-documented **inkhorn-term controversy**: critics mocked the perceived pretension and
opacity of heavily Latinate coinages (immortalized in Thomas Wilson's deliberately absurd mock-inkhorn
letter, Example 5.4) even as a genuine commercial market for "hard-word" dictionaries (Coote 1596,
Cawdry 1604, Bullokar 1616, Cockeram 1623) thrived by explaining exactly this vocabulary to a general
readership — illustrating that the controversy was not a clean two-sided purist-vs-Latinist debate so
much as a continuum of "different degrees of Latinity" (Moore 1910) in which even self-described
"purists" (e.g. Cheke) regularly used loanwords themselves when they judged it necessary. Many words
mocked as needless inkhornisms at the time have in fact survived into ordinary Modern English usage.
(pp.161–167)

### §5.5 Barbados and Bajan: a worked contact-linguistics case study

This section presents English's transplantation to Barbados (settled from 1627) as a case study in
how colonization-driven contact can, or cannot, drive structural language change — directly
continuing Ch.4's creolization discussion with a concrete example. Barbados's early social history
(dominated by a mixed European workforce of smallholders and **indentured servants**, mostly drawn
from south-western English counties, with African/indigenous slaves initially a minority) shifted
decisively after c.1650 with the switch to sugar-cane cultivation: slave labor rapidly outnumbered
the European population by the mid-1650s, and subsequent White emigration out of Barbados to other
colonies (Holm 1989) is argued to have significantly dispersed south-western British regional speech
features across the wider English-speaking Caribbean. The linguistic status of modern **Bajan**
(Barbadian Creole/dialectal English) is genuinely contested: unlike most Caribbean English-lexicon
creoles, Bajan does not conform to the standard "creole template" (cf. Whinnom's list, Ch.4 §4.5) and
has often instead been analyzed as simply a non-standard *variety* of English, not a creole proper.
Scholars who argue for a creole ancestry (Cassidy 1980; Rickford & Handler 1994; Fields 1995) rely on
textual attestations of creole-like structural features in historical (17th–19th c.) written records
of spoken Bajan, and posit that modern Bajan has since undergone **decreolization** (become
progressively "less creole," converging back toward standard English through sustained contact) —
though the chapter flags a real methodological caution here (quoting Fields 1995): such historical
textual data is always **second-hand**, transcribed by White, often non-native-speaker observers of
variable reliability, which weakens confident structural claims built on it. A second problem the
chapter raises with the pro-creole-ancestry position: several of the "creole-diagnostic" structural
features actually attested for Bajan (absence of plural/case marking, unmarked past tense, multiple
negation) are not creole-exclusive at all — they recur widely in ordinary non-standard English
dialects and cross-linguistically, so their presence alone cannot establish creole ancestry (echoing
Ch.4's broader critique that no fixed set of features reliably distinguishes creoles as a structural
type). A third complication is that Barbados's actual settlement history does not fit the
textbook model under which creolization is assumed to occur: the first ~30 years of settlement had an
English-speaking majority and a contact situation not conducive to creolization by the usual
superstrate/substrate-imbalance model, yet proponents still treat this early period as linguistically
disconnected from the post-1655 phase when the conditions for creolization genuinely were present —
an artificial discontinuity the chapter argues is unwarranted, drawing a direct parallel to the
continuity it has already stressed between Anglo-Saxon/OE and OE/ME. **Mufwene's (2001) alternative
theory of creolization**, offered as a way to reconcile these tensions, treats creole emergence as
occurring through two connected phases rather than one sharp break: an early "**homestead phase**"
(small-scale, intimate, regular European–non-European contact, in which the minority non-European
group achieves fluent acquisition of a non-standard **colonial koiné** — not standard English, but
the already-non-standard regional/social varieties the actual European settlers, disproportionately
indentured/lower-class, themselves spoke) followed by a "**plantation phase**" (once large-scale slave
importation shifts the population ratio, direct contact with fluent speakers becomes rarer and
acquisition of the colonial koiné by new, less-integrated arrivals becomes progressively more
**imperfect**, i.e. creolization proper). Crucially, Mufwene's model predicts that a resulting creole's
structural features will be **predetermined to a significant degree by whatever koiné the original
homestead-phase settlers spoke** — explaining, without positing decreolization, the striking
structural parallels documented between modern Bajan and non-standard **south-western dialectal
English (SWDE)** from the specific English counties Barbados's early settlers actually came from
(Niles 1980): e.g. gender-indicating compounds (*head-piece* 'head', *nose-hole* 'nostril'), temporal
adverbs (*before-time* 'formerly', *dusk-time* 'dusk', *lower-side* 'below'), subject/object pronoun
case leveling in both directions (*he like she* 'he likes her'; *me eh going dere* 'I am not going
there'), extended use of the pronoun *um* for singular neuter reference (paralleled in SWDE usage),
uninflected past-tense verb forms, and multiple negation — all independently attested in both modern
Bajan and historical/dialectal SWDE sources, which the chapter treats as evidence that these Bajan
features were inherited via direct dialectal transmission from the actual English varieties present
at settlement, rather than invented independently through a generic, contact-triggered creolization
process. (pp.167–173)

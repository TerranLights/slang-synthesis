## Grammar points

### Greek word division — compound words (§11.141, p.1503)

Compound Greek words are divided between their constituent parts, with the ordinary single-/
multiple-consonant division rules (covered in the preceding sections, not part of this chunk) applying
within each part. The commonest type of compound word begins with a preposition or a prefix. CMOS
lists several prefix/preposition forms that commonly open a compound (ἀμφι-, ἀν-, ἀπ-, ἀφ-, ἐφ-, κατ-,
ὑπ-, ὑφ-) and three illustrative compound words showing the division point: a verb compounded with
ἐκ- ("out"), a verb compounded with κατά- ("down"/"according to"), and an adjective compounded with
δυσ- ("bad"/"ill-").

### Special characters in Old and Middle English (§11.142, p.1503)

Several Old English and Middle English letters not used in modern English occur in both lowercase and
uppercase forms (cross-referenced to Table 11.1, below). Both edh/eth and thorn represent the same
sound — voiced or unvoiced *th*, as in *them* or *three* — the two letters being largely
interchangeable conventions rather than marking a sound distinction between each other. Yogh covers a
wider range: in Old English it can represent *y* (as in *year*), *g* (as in *good*), or *gh* (as in
*light* and *thought*); in Middle English it sometimes represents *y* and *gh* but normally not *g*.
The Æ/æ ligature ("ash") should not be printed as two separate letters in Old English names and text
(CMOS's example: *Ælfric*, not *Aelfric*). CMOS instructs authors to use the correct Unicode characters
for the ligature and for edh, thorn, and yogh, and to supply their publisher with a list of these and
any other special characters used (cross-referenced to §11.2, outside this chunk's range). The Latin
small esh (ʃ) is handled separately, cross-referenced to §13.7.

### Ampersand and wynn (§11.143, p.1503)

In Old English and Middle English texts, a stylized "seven" shape — the Tironian *et* — sometimes
stands in for *and*, though the modern ampersand may be substituted. Separately, Old English texts use
a dedicated letter (wynn) for *w*; the modern *w* is often substituted for it in edited text. (See the
Vocabulary table above for a flag on this section's own corrupted glyph rendering.)

### Signed languages — overview (§11.144, p.1504)

The visual-gestural languages used by deaf people in different parts of the world are called signed
languages. They are quite different in structure from spoken languages, although regional language
contact can leave an effect; a given signed language may or may not share the same national or
geographic boundaries as the spoken language(s) used in the same location (e.g. American Sign Language
and British Sign Language are not mutually intelligible, despite both regions using English as the
surrounding spoken language — this specific example is general linguistic knowledge, not itself quoted
from the source). The individual elements of a signed language are called signs.

### Components of signs (§11.145, p.1505)

Signs have five major articulatory components: handshape, location, orientation, movement, and — in
some cases — distinctive nonmanual signals.

### Writing ASL (§11.146, p.1506)

No formal system for writing signed languages has been adopted for use by large numbers of deaf
signers; CMOS's remaining ASL sections (§§11.147–11.154) give an overview of some of the most
frequently used conventions for written transcription of signing. CMOS points readers to two further
resources: Charlotte Baker-Shenk and Dennis Cokely, *American Sign Language: A Teacher's Resource Text
on Grammar and Culture*; and Clayton Valli, Ceil Lucas, and Kristin J. Mulrooney, *Linguistics of
American Sign Language: An Introduction* (CMOS's own bibliography entry 5).

### Glosses in ASL (§11.147, p.1507)

The written-language transcription of a sign is called a gloss. Glosses use words from the spoken
language, conventionally written in small capital letters (CMOS's examples: WOMAN, SCHOOL, CAT) —
regular capital letters may be used as an alternative. When two or more written words are needed to
gloss a single sign, the component glosses are joined by hyphens (CMOS's example: VEHICLE-DRIVE-BY,
glossing the sign for "a car drove by"), and the idiomatic translation is enclosed in double quotation
marks. CMOS flags one obvious limitation of spoken-language glossing: there is no one-to-one
correspondence between the words or signs of any two languages, so a gloss is necessarily an
approximation rather than a precise equivalence.

### Compound signs (§11.148, p.1510)

Some combinations of signs take on a meaning distinct from the meaning of the individual signs — i.e.,
they form compounds. Various typographic conventions mark this, including a "close-up" mark or a plus
sign between the component glosses. CMOS's own worked example shows both conventions for the sign
glossing "parents": a close-up-mark version and a MOTHER+FATHER (plus-sign) version. See the Vocabulary
table above for the confidence flag on the close-up mark's exact source glyph.

### Fingerspelling (§11.149, p.1509)

For proper nouns and other words borrowed from the surrounding spoken language, a signer may
fingerspell the word using handshapes drawn from a manual alphabet. Numerous fingerspelling alphabets
exist across different signed languages; ASL uses the American Manual Alphabet. CMOS shows three
alternative ways a fingerspelled word may be transcribed, all equally valid depending on the
transcription system in use: a lowercase *fs-* prefix before a capitalized gloss (*fs*-JOHN); capital
letters joined by hyphens (J-O-H-N); or lowercase letters joined by hyphens (j-o-h-n).

### Lexicalized signs (§11.150, p.1510)

Over time, some fingerspelled words have taken on the quality of distinct signs in their own right,
either through omission of some of the individual letter-handshapes or through a change in the
orientation or movement of the letter signs. These lexicalized signs are represented with a "pound"
symbol (#) prefix — CMOS's examples: #WHAT, #BACK, #DO.

### Handshapes (§11.151, p.1511)

Most ASL handshapes are described by reference to the corresponding alphabetic or numerical handshape
(or a variation of it) from the American Manual Alphabet/counting system. CMOS's examples: APPLE is
made with an X handshape; CREATE is made with a 4 handshape; ANY is made with an Open A handshape;
YELL is made with a Bent 5 handshape. Handshapes with no clear relative in the fingerspelling or number
system are labeled idiosyncratically instead — CMOS's examples: SARCASTIC is made with the HORNS
handshape; AIRPLANE is made with the ILY handshape. Signed languages that don't use the American Manual
Alphabet typically describe their handshapes in relation to their own manual alphabets instead.

### Transcriptions of signed sentences (§11.152, p.1513)

Signed sentences are written as a sequence of glosses, generally with the spoken/written-language
translation given underneath, in italics, in quotation marks, or both (CMOS cross-references its own
§§11.153–11.154 as worked examples). Sentence-level punctuation is generally omitted from the gloss
line itself, though it is retained in the translation line; some writers nonetheless add question marks
and exclamation points to the gloss line, and a comma may be used to mark a short in-sentence pause.

### Pronouns, possessives, and reference (§11.153, p.1514)

Pronouns are commonly transcribed either as IX (reflecting that they're frequently produced with the
"index" finger) or as PRO; either convention is followed by a marker of person and, sometimes, number.
A parallel convention marks the possessive, sometimes glossed POSS. Person/number marking conventions
vary across transcription systems: CMOS gives IX3p, IX-3p, and PRO.3 as three equivalent ways different
systems gloss a third-person-singular pronoun (English "he"/"she"/"it"), and IX2p-pl for a
second-person-plural pronoun. Subscripted indices are also used to mark coreference — showing that two
signs are articulated at the same spatial location and therefore refer back to the same discourse
referent. CMOS's worked example: IX3pi LOSE POSS3pi HOUSE, translated "He lost his house" — the matching
subscript *i* on IX and POSS marks that "he" and "his" refer to the same person.

### Nonmanual signals (§11.154, p.1516)

Nonmanual gestures may be labeled according to the anatomical behavior involved or according to their
grammatical/interpretive function. These labels are typeset in a smaller font, with a half-point rule
drawn above the span of the ASL sentence over which the gesture occurs. CMOS's example label *whq* marks
the facial expression (furrowed brows, slightly squinted eyes) that accompanies wh-questions (*who*,
*what*, *when*, *where*, *why*); the rule for *whq* extends over the entire question in CMOS's worked
example, since the expression is held throughout. A second label, *rap hs*, marks a rapid headshake
co-occurring with just one sign (WHO) in the same example. Correct alignment between a nonmanual-signal
label's rule and the glosses it spans is described as critical to an accurate transcription. CMOS's
full worked example: *fs*-JOHN SEE YESTERDAY WHO (with *whq* over the whole line and *rap hs* over WHO),
translated "Whom did John see yesterday?"

### Special characters for Latin-alphabet languages (Table 11.1, pp.1504–1505 in the printed book)

A reference table pairing each special Latin-alphabet character (uppercase and lowercase forms) with
its Unicode codepoint, a plain-English description of the diacritic or letter, and the languages that
use it — cross-referenced from §11.142 above and intended as a list an author can hand a publisher when
specifying non-ASCII characters. The table runs to roughly sixty rows; a representative, non-exhaustive
sample rather than the full table: paired low/high quotation marks for German („ "); guillemets for
French, German (reversed), Italian, and Spanish (« »); the full run of vowel diacritics (grave, acute,
circumflex, tilde, diaeresis, ring-above, macron, breve, ogonek, caron/háček) distributed across
French, Czech, Hungarian, Portuguese, the Scandinavian languages, Latin/Baltic transliteration, and the
Balkan Slavic languages (Bosnian, Croatian, Montenegrin, Polish, Serbian); the Æ/æ ligature, shared by
Danish, Norwegian, and Old/Middle English; the yogh (Ʒ/ʒ) and eth (Ð/ð), both marked for Old English and
Middle English specifically; a Danish/Norwegian Ø/ø (O with stroke); German's eszett (ß, "sharp S");
and a set of Hausa-specific hooked consonants (Ɓ/ɓ, Ƙ/ƙ, Ɗ/ɗ, Ƴ/ƴ) not shared with any other language in
the table.

### Special characters for transliterated Arabic, Hebrew, Japanese, and South Asian languages (Table 11.2, PDF p.1519)

A parallel reference table for diacritics used when these non-Latin-script languages are transliterated
into Latin characters. Representative sample: the modifier-letter prime (a "soft sign," for Arabic and
Hebrew); the ʿayn and ʾalif/ʾalef marks (represented with modifier left- and right-half-rings
respectively, for Arabic and Hebrew); macron-marked long vowels (Ā/ā, Ī/ī, Ū/ū) shared across Arabic,
Hebrew, Japanese, and South-Asian-language transliteration; an extensive family of dot-below
consonants (voiceless and voiced retroflex/emphatic stops and related sounds) used mainly for
South-Asian-language transliteration, several also shared with Arabic or Hebrew transliteration; and a
South-Asian-specific combining macron placed under *n* via a combining Unicode character rather than a
single precomposed codepoint, distinct from a separate precomposed dot-above *n*.

### Russian alphabet and romanization (Table 11.3, PDF p.1521)

A reference table giving the full 33-letter Cyrillic alphabet (upright and cursive forms, with Unicode
codepoints for each) alongside three parallel romanization systems CMOS recognizes: the US Board on
Geographic Names system, the Library of Congress system, and a "linguistic" system (the latter two
sharing most characters except where footnoted). Representative notes rather than the full table: most
consonant letters romanize identically across all three systems. Divergence concentrates in the
sibilants, affricates, and the two "signs": х ("kh" throughout, optionally marked with a combining
breve in the LC/linguistic systems); ц ("ts" in the geographic/LC systems vs. "c" in the linguistic
system); ч ("ch" vs. "č"); ш and щ ("sh"/"shch" vs. "š"/"šč"); the hard sign ъ and soft sign ь, which go
unrepresented in the geographic-names system but are marked with distinct prime-like characters in the
LC and linguistic systems. Vowel letters е and ё romanize as "ye"/"yë" word-initially or after a vowel,
ъ, or ь, and otherwise as plain "e"/"ë"; ю and я romanize as "yu"/"ya" in the geographic system, or with
a combining "character tie" (U+0361) joining the two Latin letters in the LC/linguistic systems — a tie
mark CMOS notes is often omitted in practice. A footnote clarifies that ё is not always treated as a
separate letter from е in ordinary Russian usage.

### Greek alphabet and romanization (Table 11.4, PDF p.1522)

A reference table of the 24-letter classical Greek alphabet (uppercase/lowercase, with Unicode
codepoints) and a standard transliteration column. Representative notes: most letters transliterate
directly and predictably (alpha→a, beta→b, delta→d, and so on); gamma transliterates as "g" ordinarily
but as "n" immediately before another gamma or before kappa (a footnoted digraph rule covering γγ→"ng"
and γκ→"nk"); rho transliterates as "r" generally, but "rh" when word-initial and "rrh" when doubled;
upsilon transliterates as "u" generally, but as "y" except after *a*, *e*, or the *ē*/*ī*
diphthong-forming vowels; sigma has a distinct final form (used only at the end of a word) that
transliterates the same as medial/initial sigma; eta and omega both carry macrons in transliteration
(ē, ō) to distinguish the long vowels from epsilon's and omicron's short e/o; theta and phi each have a
noted alternate glyph form in Greek typography.

### Greek numerals (Table 11.5, PDF p.1523)

A reference table showing how Greek letters double as numerals, each numeral marked by a following
acute-accent-like sign. Units and tens (1–24, by ones, then by tens to 100) reuse the ordinary alphabet
in sequence; hundreds (100–900) continue the same letter sequence; thousands (1,000–100,000) reuse the
unit-value letters again but with a preceding subscript-comma-like mark distinguishing them from the
1–100 range. Three letters outside the 24-letter classical alphabet fill numeral gaps the standard
alphabet doesn't cover: stigma (alternatively digamma) for 6, an archaic/obsolete koppa form for 90,
and sampi (formerly called "disigma," i.e. double sigma) for 900 — each footnoted in the source as an
archaic letter retained specifically for its numeral value.

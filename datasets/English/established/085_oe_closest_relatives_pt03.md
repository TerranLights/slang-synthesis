## Grammar points

### The traditional family tree (Fig.4) and why Robinson revisits it

Robinson reprints the traditional Schleicher-derived (1860) Germanic *Stammbaum*: Proto-Germanic →
West/North/East Germanic → (within West Germanic) Anglo-Frisian/Proto-German → (within Anglo-Frisian)
Old English/Old Frisian → English/Frisian; Proto-German → Old High German/Old Low German (= Old
Saxon + Old Dutch) → German-Yiddish/Low-German-Dutch-Afrikaans; North Germanic → Old Norse →
Icelandic/Faroese/Norwegian/Danish/Swedish; East Germanic → Gothic (extinct). He notes this specific
tree has survived essentially unchanged since 1860 "in the face of massive objections almost from
the very beginning" — the existence of a genuine "West Germanic" proto-language has always been
doubted, and cross-cutting correspondences (West Germanic ~ Old Norse; Old Norse ~ Gothic) undermine
the clean tripartite split at the root. A tree diagram also has **no geographic dimension**, so it
cannot represent two dialects continuing to influence each other *after* a tree would show them as
separated branches — a serious distortion virtually all 20th-century Germanicists acknowledge, even
though the tree model persists in handbooks regardless (pp.247–249).

### Five reasons two languages can share a feature (methodological framework)

Before evaluating any specific grouping claim, Robinson lays out five distinct reasons an individual
linguistic feature might appear in two or more languages, only one of which (the fifth) is good
evidence of a genuine shared-innovation/common-ancestor-stage relationship: (1) both retained an
**archaic feature** already present in the proto-language, while other languages replaced it; (2)
both independently selected the **same option from a "doublet"** pair already available in the
proto-language (not necessarily limited to two options); (3) both developed the feature
**independently and coincidentally**, with no historical connection; (4) both developed the feature
**semi-independently but in line with pre-existing structural similarities** between them (sometimes
called *drift*); (5) both developed the feature **while their speakers still formed a single, albeit
dialectally varied, speech community**. Scholars disagree sharply on which features documented for
Germanic belong to which category, which drives most of the live disputes narrated in the rest of
the chapter (pp.249–250). This five-way taxonomy is a genuinely reusable analytical tool for
evaluating *any* claimed historical-linguistic grouping, not specific to Germanic.

### The comparative feature chart and Old English's own profile

The chapter's 31-feature chart (17 phonological rows, 14 morphological rows) tabulates each of the
seven oldest-attested Germanic languages against Proto-Germanic reflexes for: the vowel/consonant
changes already itemized in this project's ch.1/ch.2/ch.6 extraction files (PGmc *ǣ*, umlaut, *a*/*â*
before nasal, PGmc *ai*/*au* reflexes, sharpening, rhotacism, *ngw*→*ng*, *fl*-/*þl*-, gemination,
nasal-loss-before-fricative, assibilation, *r*-metathesis, final-*n* loss, the full High German
consonant shift) and morphological points (loss of PGmc *-az*, the masc. *a*-stem nominative plural,
the masc. 3rd-person pronoun's nominative singular form, the intensified demonstrative, dual
pronouns, accusative/dative syncretism in 1st/2nd-person pronouns, the reflexive pronoun, verb
reduplication, the *-nan* verb class, the 2sg. preterite-indicative ending, 'be''s 3sg. present, the
plural present-indicative ending, short 'stand'/'go' forms, and the gerund). **Old English's own
values across this chart are exactly the profile already itemized in this project's
`085_oe_closest_relatives_pt02.md`** (ch.6's own numbered feature lists) — this chapter's table is
the book's way of cross-checking that per-language inventory against the other six languages
side-by-side, and the two sources are internally consistent wherever they overlap.

### Similarities between Gothic and Old Norse: the "Gothonordic hypothesis"

Ernst Schwarz (1951) lists 25 points of similarity between Gothic and Old Norse not shared by any
West Germanic language, and — combined with archaeological tradition placing the Goths' origin in
Sweden — proposes the Germanic family's **primary** split was North+East Germanic ("South Germanic,"
in his relabeling, oddly covering what the traditional tree calls West Germanic) vs. the rest, with
Gothic/Old Norse sharing features jointly developed before the Goths' first-century-B.C. departure
for the Baltic's south coast. Hans Kuhn sharply rejects this: of Schwarz's 25 features, Kuhn accepts
only a handful as genuine shared innovations (most are dismissed as independently-retained archaisms,
one as a trivial shared lexical loss), and treats even those few as a late (post-emigration), limited
contact phenomenon confined to Gothic and *eastern* Scandinavian dialects specifically — not a
primary Germanic-wide split. Of the 25, Robinson identifies **row 8 (sharpening of original *jj*/
*ww*) as the one genuine shared innovation and the actual centerpiece of the Gothonordic hypothesis**
— but even this is complicated by runic evidence showing no sharpening in the West Norse runic
inscriptions until the Viking age, long after any plausible Gothic contact, suggesting (if genuine)
the innovation was shared only with *eastern* Norse dialects specifically. T.L. Markey, reviewing
five further points Lehmann finds persuasive (sharpening; retention of *-t* as the 2sg. preterite
marker; the non-development of short 'go'/'stand' forms; the *-nan* verb class; the *-în* feminine
present-participle ending), finds reasons to doubt the probative force of every one of them
individually. Robinson's own assessment: the current consensus allows "a few significant points of
similarity...indicating close contact at some point," but not enough to justify a distinct
"Gothonordic" branch, and much of the scholarly heat on both sides is, in his view, really a dispute
about how much weight the old *Stammbaum* model itself deserves (pp.251–255).

### Similarities between Old Norse and West Germanic

A separate, larger set of chart features (phonological rows 1,2,3,9,11,12 — gemination only
partially, since Old Norse has more restricted gemination — plus morphological rows 21 and 25) groups
Old Norse together with the West Germanic languages **against** Gothic specifically — a grouping with
much broader and more robust coverage, spanning phonology, grammar, and "a very large part of the
lexicon," which Robinson treats as attesting "a long period of contact between speakers of the
languages involved." Kuhn dates this shared North+West Germanic speech-community period through
roughly the 5th century, ending with the great migrations (especially the Anglo-Saxon move to
Britain); Markey's independent dating estimate (based on the chronology of individual sound changes)
converges on roughly 300–450, which Robinson calls "reasonable." **This means the conventional
"West Germanic" node is, on the linguistic evidence, not obviously older than — and may be
contemporaneous with — the split of North Germanic away from it**, undercutting the traditional
tree's implication that West Germanic was already a long-separated, internally unified branch before
Old Norse diverged (pp.255–256).

### Similarities within "West Germanic" — and why the label is misleading

Robinson explicitly flags that treating "West Germanic" as a node with the same kind of internal
uniformity as Old Norse (a single well-attested language) or as presumed for the thinly-attested East
Germanic (effectively just Gothic) is a methodological trap: the languages conventionally grouped as
West Germanic show "enormous differences," some clearly predating the North/West split itself. Even
so, chart rows 10, 12, 18, 26, 27, 30, 31 (plus the feminine present-participle ending *-jô*,
discussed earlier as West Germanic's "doublet" choice against Gothic/Old Norse's *-în*) are shared
across the West Germanic languages specifically, against both North and East Germanic — a real but
narrower signal than Old Norse+West-Germanic's broader overlap. Critically, **most modern scholars
doubt a unitary West Germanic proto-language ever existed at all**: the alternative view sees West
Germanic splitting, nearly from the moment of the North/West divergence, into three separate
sub-communities — **Ingvaeonic** (= North Sea Germanic), **Istvaeonic** (= Weser-Rhine Germanic), and
**Irminonic** (= Elbe Germanic) — the same three Tacitean tribal groupings already introduced in
ch.1's historical/geographic framing. On this view, "West Germanic" at best names an "extended
language community" through which innovations could spread unevenly, not a genuine proto-language
node (pp.256–257).

### Features of the Ingvaeonic languages — and why "Ingvaeonic" itself is doubtful as a clean subgroup

This is the chapter's most directly on-topic section for Old English's own classification.
"Ingvaeonic" is sometimes narrowly defined as the Old English + Old Frisian subgroup specifically,
but is also used more broadly for a wider, messier cluster of features that don't all co-occur in the
same set of languages — "worse, a number of Ingvaeonic features appear to be present in Old Norse,
not a West Germanic language at all." Robinson itemizes the actual sharing patterns from the chart:
**Old English and Old Frisian alone** (with minor partial reflections in Old Saxon) share rows
1 (PGmc *ǣ*→*â*), 4 (*a*/*â*→*o*/*ô* before a nasal), 5 (PGmc *ai*→*â*/*ā*), **14 (assibilation)**,
and **15 (*r*-metathesis)** — assibilation and metathesis being the two features ch.6's own grammar
discussion already flagged as shared, among *all* the Germanic languages, uniquely between Old
English and Old Frisian. Old Frisian alone additionally shows PGmc *au*→*â* (row 6), judged the
"genuine" Ingvaeonic development of that diphthong — **Old English's own *ēa* reflex of *au* is
explicitly judged NOT to be a straightforward reduction of this same Ingvaeonic *â*, but to have
"probably followed its own unique route."** **Old English, Old Frisian, and Old Saxon together**
share row 13 (nasal loss before a fricative — also found, more restrictedly, in Old Norse), row 24
(absence of a dedicated reflexive pronoun — though Old Low Franconian's apparent reflexive is "clearly
a borrowing," not inherited), row 28 ('be''s 3sg. present *is*, with "some backsliding" in Old Saxon;
note Old Norse also independently has a *t*-less form), and row 29 (the plural present-indicative
ending). **Old English and Old Saxon specifically (not Old Frisian)** share row 19, the *-as*-type
nominative plural — Old Frisian's attested plurals in *-ar* are judged a later Scandinavian borrowing
over an original, now-obscured, *-as*-type ending of its own. **Old English, Old Frisian, Old Saxon,
AND Old Low Franconian together** share row 20 (the *h*-initial masculine 3rd-person pronoun — though
Old Norse *also* independently shows initial *h*- here, "but otherwise goes its own route") and row
23 (accusative/dative syncretism in 1st/2nd-person pronouns). Scholars dispute the dating of nearly
all of these sharings: Schwarz insists all genuine Ingvaeonic innovations predate the Anglo-Saxon
departure for Britain (~450); Kuhn and Markey instead place a substantial subset — notably the
features peculiar to Old English and Old Frisian specifically, including the famous *ê*/*ǣ* reflex
of PGmc *ǣ* and assibilation itself — as developing only **after** the Anglo-Saxons were already
settled in Britain, which (if correct) would mean some of the textbook "Ingvaeonic" features
uniting English and Frisian arose through continued post-migration contact across the North Sea
rather than from a shared pre-migration homeland. Robinson's own conclusion, stated plainly: **"That
Ingvaeonic was ever a unified linguistic subgroup of Germanic seems very doubtful. Even English and
Frisian, clearly the central languages of the Ingvaeonic cluster, seem to have carried out
independently (though in a mutually reinforcing way) a number of the innovations that supposedly
characterize this group, while going different ways in other respects"** (pp.257–259).

### Similarities between Old High German and Old Low Franconian; the Istvaeonic "wedge" pattern

Old Low Franconian is shown to be closer, in many respects, to Old High German than to the
"Ingvaeonic" languages proper (chart rows 1,4,5,6,7,13,14,15,19,28,29) — reflecting the Istvaeonic
dialect area's intermediate geographic position between Ingvaeonic and Irminonic zones. Ferdinand
Wrede's 1924 dialectological study found a striking pattern: several innovations now considered
characteristically "southeastern" (loss of a nasal before a voiceless fricative, outside just
northern Germany, also in the southwestern Alamannic dialects; a unitary, person-undifferentiated
plural verb ending, likewise shared north and southwest) left behind an untouched relic "wedge"
between the two affected zones — widest in the southeast, around Bavaria — exactly where other,
independently-documented innovations (Bavarian *gên* 'go' spreading and leaving relic *gân* areas;
the Early New High German Diphthongization turning *hûs* into *haus*) are known to have spread
outward from the southeast. **Wrede's startling inference: the untouched "wedge" areas showed
genuinely Ingvaeonic characteristics** (the pronoun *he* 'he', no reflexive pronoun, no accusative/
dative distinction in 1st/2nd-person pronouns, among others) — implying **almost all of West
Germanic once carried Ingvaeonic features**, later overrun by a wave of non-Ingvaeonic innovations
spreading from the Gothic-adjacent southeast (pp.259–260).

### Similarities between Old High German and Gothic

Ferdinand Wrede's "Gothicization" idea connects to a separate claim: that some Old High German/Gothic
correspondences (chart rows 13, 20, 23, 24, 28, 29) reflect direct historical contact, since the
Goths and the Irminonic tribes were geographically adjacent twice — once before the 2nd century in
northern Germany, and again in the 5th–6th centuries in the Bavaria/Austria region. T.L. Markey's
dialectologically-grounded rebuttal: with the exception of the two features Wrede himself could trace
via relic areas (nasal loss, the unitary plural), none of Wrede's "Gothicization" evidence holds up —
even the deep-southwest "relic" zones still show the *h*-initial 3rd-person pronoun, accusative/
dative distinction in pronouns, and other supposedly-Gothicized features, and worse, Wrede's own key
evidence (nasal loss, the unitary plural ending) turns out, on closer dialectological study, to be a
**relatively recent development within the Alamannic dialects themselves**, centuries too late to
have come from Gothic contact at all — so these parallels "must be seen as parallel, totally
independent developments of a not altogether unusual kind," not real contact effects. Theodor Frings
attributes a smaller residue of shared features (the masc. 3rd-person pronoun *er*/*is*, and the
etymologically identical word for 'how', Goth. *hvaiwa*/OHG *hweo*) to the earlier (pre-2nd-century)
contact period specifically, though Robinson notes this specific dating "can[not] be proved" and may
just as well be shared retention from an older stage. A later contact period did leave undisputed,
superficial traces: a handful of Gothic loanwords in Bavarian tied specifically to Gothic
participation in the Christianization of Bavaria (pp.260–261).

### Vennemann's "Late-Breaking Theory": the High German Consonant Shift reconsidered

Theo Vennemann's then-recent (as of this book's 1992 publication) hypothesis challenges the standard
assumption that Old High German's distinctive consonant system is a *secondary*, derived-by-shift
development relative to the rest of Germanic. Instead, Vennemann argues the High German and "Low
Germanic" consonant systems are **co-equal** independent descendants of a Proto-Germanic consonant
system distinct from either — drawing on evidence from the wider Indo-European family, not just
Germanic. If correct, this would effectively regroup the Germanic family around a **High-German-vs-
everyone-else** split (Vennemann himself does not insist on this specific reading of his own
implications). Robinson works through the chronological puzzle this creates: if Vennemann's "High
Germanic" and "Low Germanic" sound shifts occurred early (while Gothic was still in the north),
Old High German — on an entirely separate branch from the rest, in this model — would nonetheless
have had to undergo a large number of changes in common with every other Germanic language except
Gothic, then a further subset shared with every one of them except Old Norse too, which Robinson
finds implausible; if the shifts occurred later instead (after the Goths' departure, which is where
most scholars place the conventional "High German Consonant Shift," 6th century), a separate major
shared shift must then connect Gothic with the northwest-Germanic languages minus pre-High-German —
and if the High-Germanic shift really is as late as the 6th century, it cannot represent the Germanic
family's *primary* split at all, since Old Norse and Gothic had by then already long since
established their own clear separation through other independent changes (pp.261–263).

### Conclusions

Robinson's closing assessment, echoing Kuhn: a central fault line in Germanic-classification
scholarship is simply **how much weight to give non-linguistic (archaeological/ethnographic)
evidence** relative to the linguistic data itself — scholars like Schwarz and Frings lean heavily on
archaeology/ethnography (which Robinson notes enjoys no more scholarly consensus than Germanic
linguistics does, making this a weak anchor), prompting pointed objections from Kuhn; Vennemann's
proposal sits at the opposite extreme, offering essentially no chronological or geographic grounding
for when/where his proposed split communities would have existed. Robinson's own final verdict:
once the rigid dichotomies of the *Stammbaum* model are abandoned, what actually emerges is "a
fluid, changeable situation in which dialect communities may alternately diverge, diverge only in
part, converge, converge only in part, and otherwise behave like human groupings rather than
biological species trees" — and he does not expect any future scholarship to impose significantly
more order on the Germanic languages' interrelationships than has already been established
(pp.263–264). **This closing framing is the book's real thesis, and the clearest statement available
anywhere in this source of why "Old English's closest relatives" resists a single clean answer**:
Old English is linguistically closest to Old Frisian on a narrow but real set of shared innovations
(assibilation, *r*-metathesis, and several vowel reflexes), shares a wider but shallower Ingvaeonic-
adjacent layer with Old Saxon (and more thinly with Old Low Franconian), sits within a broader and
robustly-evidenced North-Sea-era North+West Germanic speech community that also included the
ancestors of Old Norse, and ultimately traces to a Proto-Germanic whose own first splits remain
genuinely disputed rather than settled fact.

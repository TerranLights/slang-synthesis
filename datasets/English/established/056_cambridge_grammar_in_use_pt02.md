## Grammar points

### Unit 7 — Present perfect (1)

Core criterion: present perfect always carries a *result-now* reading (*he's lost his key* = he
doesn't have it now), used to announce new/recent news. Covers *just/already/yet* (see Vocabulary)
and the *gone (to)*/*been (to)* minimal pair (see Vocabulary).

### Unit 8 — Present perfect (2)

Present perfect for "a period continuing until now" — life-experience questions (*have you ever...*),
and unfinished-time-period markers *today/this morning/this week* used **while that period is still
open** (contrast with Unit 14, where the same nouns take past simple once the period is finished).
Introduces the *it's the first/second/third time* fixed construction (see Vocabulary).

### Units 9–10 — Present perfect continuous; present perfect continuous vs. simple

Present perfect continuous marks a recently-stopped or still-ongoing activity with a felt connection
to now (*you're out of breath — have you been running?*). Units 9–10 draw the key continuous/simple
split: continuous asks/answers *how long* (activity-focused, completion-irrelevant), simple asks/
answers *how much/how many/how many times* (result/quantity-focused, requires completion) — e.g.
*she's been writing letters all day* (activity) vs *she's written ten letters today* (count). The
Unit 4A stative-verb restriction again blocks the continuous form for *know/like/believe* etc.

### Unit 11 — *How long have you (been)...?*

A state that still holds must be phrased with present perfect, never plain present, when paired with
a duration phrase: *they are married* (fine alone) but *how long have they been married* (not "are
they married" — and never "they are married for 20 years"). With *live/work*, either present perfect
simple or continuous is acceptable; *always* forces the simple (*John has always lived in London*,
never "has always been living").

### Unit 12 — *When...?* and *How long...?*; *for* and *since*

*When...?* pairs with past simple (asks about the starting point); *How long...?* pairs with present
perfect (asks about elapsed duration). Full *for*/*since* contrast and the *it's... since...*
paraphrase construction (see Vocabulary).

### Units 13–14 — Present perfect and past (1) and (2)

The book's central usage distinction, stated twice from different angles. Present perfect = a past
action with a *present result*, uncommitted to a specific finished time; past simple = the action
itself, located at a specific (possibly unstated) past time, carrying no claim about the present
state. Unit 13's running example: *Tom has lost his key* (doesn't have it now) vs *Tom lost his key
yesterday* (we don't know if he has it now). Rule: once a *specific finished time* is named or
implied (*yesterday*, *ten minutes ago*, *in 1985*, *when I was a child*, or a *When...?*/*What
time...?* question), the past simple is obligatory and the present perfect becomes ungrammatical —
even for the same event that was just introduced with a present perfect (*I've burnt myself* → *How
did you do that?* not "have you done", once the conversation moves to specifics). Unit 14 adds the
finished-vs-open-period test for time nouns like *today/this week*: present perfect while the period
is still running, past simple once it's over (*It hasn't rained this week* vs *It didn't rain last
week*).

### Unit 15 — Past perfect (*I had done*)

The "past of the present perfect" — marks an event completed before another past reference point
already established in the narrative (*When Sarah arrived at the party, Paul had already gone
home*). Functions purely to establish relative sequencing within a past narrative; CMOS's structural
"perfect" definition never discusses this narrative-anchoring function.

### Unit 16 — Past perfect continuous (*I had been doing*)

Parallel extension of Unit 9's present-perfect-continuous logic shifted one tense-step into the past:
marks an activity that was in progress or had just stopped *before* another past reference point
(*it had been raining* explains why the ground was wet when the sun was already out). Contrasted
with plain past continuous (*was doing*, simultaneous with the reference point) and past simple. The
Unit 4A stative-verb restriction again applies.

### Unit 17 — *Have* and *have got*

See Vocabulary — the two-paradigm possessive system (*have got* vs. bare *have*, each with its own
question/negative forms) and the large dynamic-*have*-collocation set, which behaves as an ordinary
action verb (takes continuous forms, *do/does/did* questions) and categorically rejects *have got*.

### Unit 18 — *Used to (do)*

See Vocabulary — full paradigm (no present form; *did...use to*/*didn't use to* for
questions/negatives) plus the two minimal-pair contrasts (*used to* vs. past continuous; *used to do*
vs. *be used to doing*).

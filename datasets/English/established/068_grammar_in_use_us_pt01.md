## Grammar points

### Unit 1 — Present continuous (I am doing)

Formed am/is/are + -ing. Used for (a) an action happening exactly at the moment of speaking
("I'm studying," "She's taking a bath"), and (b) an action happening around the time of speaking but
not necessarily at this exact instant (e.g., "I'm reading an interesting book" said mid-book, not
mid-page). Also used (c) for a temporary period framed by a time expression ("today," "this week,"
"this season") and (d) for a changing/developing situation ("The population of the world is rising").
p. 2.

### Unit 2 — Simple present (I do)

Formed base form, with -s for he/she/it; do/does for questions and negatives. Used for general truths,
habitual/repeated actions, and permanent facts, independent of whether the action is occurring right
now. "Where do you come from?" uses simple present, not continuous, even though it's about an ongoing
state of residence. p. 4.

### Unit 3 — Present continuous or simple present?

Direct contrast unit: continuous for temporary/in-progress, simple for general/permanent/repeated.
Example pair: "The water is boiling" (continuous — happening now) vs. "Water boils at 100°C"
(simple — general fact). A fixed list of stative verbs (want, need, prefer, have [=possess], like,
love, hate, belong, know, realize, believe, understand, suppose, mean, remember, forget, see, hear,
seem, think [=believe]) resist the continuous form entirely in their stative sense ("I know" not
"I am knowing"). pp. 6–7.

### Unit 4 — Present tenses with a future meaning

Present continuous expresses a personal arrangement already decided/scheduled ("I'm having dinner
with Ann on Friday"); will is specifically disallowed for this sense. Simple present expresses fixed
external timetables (transit schedules, movie times: "The train leaves Boston at 7:25 a.m."), but not
personal arrangements. p. 8.

### Unit 5 — Going to (I am going to do)

Expresses either (a) a prior decision/intention, or (b) a prediction based on present visible evidence
("Look at those black clouds! It's going to rain"). Past form "was/were going to" marks an intention
that was not carried out. Present continuous is generally preferred over "going to" for arranged
meetings/travel, though both are possible. pp. 10–11.

### Unit 6 — Will (1)

"Will" ('ll) marks a decision made at the instant of speaking (not a prior arrangement — that calls
for present continuous/going to). Core pragmatic uses: offering ("I'll help you with it"),
agreeing/refusing ("he won't [help]" — note "won't" of an inanimate object can mean "refuses to,"
e.g. a car that won't start), promising ("I'll pay you back on Friday"), and requesting ("Will you
shut the door, please?"). Negative is "won't"/"will not." p. 12.

### Unit 7 — Will (2)

Beyond decision-at-speaking-time, "will"/"won't" is the default for predicting a future situation or
event the speaker is not personally arranging ("You'll pass," "Tom won't pass his exam"). Commonly
paired with probably, (I'm) sure, (I) bet, (I) think, (I) suppose, (I) guess. "Shall" is a formal
alternative to "will" for I/we, but is restricted in practice to "Shall I...?"/"Shall we...?"
offer-questions; "shall" is not used with he/she/it/they/you. pp. 14.

### Unit 8 — Will or going to?

Direct contrast: "will" marks a decision made at the moment of speaking (not previously planned);
"going to" marks a decision already made before the moment of speaking. For predictions, "going to"
is required when something in the present situation already signals the outcome (black clouds →
"going to rain"); plain "will" is used for predictions without such present evidence. p. 16.

### Unit 9 — When and If sentences

In a future-time sentence with a when/if/while/after/before/until/as soon as clause, the subordinate
clause itself takes a present tense (usually simple present, sometimes present perfect to stress
one action finishing before another) — never "will," even though the whole sentence refers to the
future ("I'll talk to you when I have more time," not "when I'll have"). "When" presupposes the event
will definitely happen; "if" presupposes only a possibility. p. 18.

### Unit 10 — Will be doing and will have done

Future continuous ("will be doing") marks being in the middle of an action at a specified future
time, or (secondarily) a pre-decided future plan close in meaning to "am doing." The question form
"Will you be -ing?" is a polite way to ask about someone's plans. Future perfect ("will have done")
marks an action that will already be complete by a stated future time ("the game will have ended by
9:30"). pp. 20.

### Unit 11 — Simple past (I did)

Regular forms take -ed (spelling rules in Appendix 3); many common verbs are irregular (leave→left,
go→went; full list Appendix 2). "Be" has its own past forms was/were. Questions/negatives use
did/didn't + base form, except for "be," which inverts directly (was/were) without "did." p. 22.

### Unit 12 — Past continuous (I was doing)

Marks being mid-action at a specified past time, with no claim about whether the action was ever
completed (contrast: "Dan was cooking dinner" vs. "Dan cooked dinner," the latter implying
completion). Frequently paired with simple past to show one action interrupting/occurring inside
another ("Dan burned his hand while he was cooking dinner") — but when one event simply follows
another in sequence, both clauses take simple past instead. p. 24.

### Units 13–15 — Present perfect (I have done), parts 1–3

Formed have/has + past participle. Core meaning: a past event with a present-moment connection or
consequence ("I've lost my key" = I still don't have it now). Used to announce news/recent
happenings, often with "just" (very recent) or "already" (sooner than expected). "Gone to" (still
there/en route) is explicitly distinguished from "been to" (went and came back). Required after
"this is the first time..." constructions ("This is the first time he has driven a car"). Also used
for an open period continuing to the present ("in the last few days," "this week," "this month," when
that period is not yet finished) and with ever/never for lifetime-up-to-now questions/statements.
pp. 26–30.

### Units 16–18 — Present perfect continuous (I have been doing), and vs. simple present perfect

Marks an action that began in the past and has either just stopped (ground is wet though it isn't
raining now: "It has been raining") or is still ongoing, especially with how long/for/since. Contrast
with present perfect simple: continuous emphasizes the action/duration itself ("has been painting" —
not necessarily finished); simple emphasizes the completed result ("has painted" — the ceiling is now
blue). For long-standing permanent-feeling situations, simple + "always" is preferred over continuous
("My father has always worked hard," not "has always been working"). A fixed set of stative verbs
(be, have [=possess], know, etc. — cross-referenced to the Unit 3b list) still resist the continuous
form even here. pp. 32–36.

### Unit 19 — Present perfect with how long; simple past with when; since and for

"When" + simple past asks/states a specific past point ("When did it start raining?" / "It started at
one o'clock"). "How long" + present perfect (simple or continuous) asks/states duration up to now
("How long has it been raining?" / "It's been raining since one o'clock"). "Since" takes a starting
point (8 o'clock, Monday, 1977); "for" takes a duration (two hours, three days, a long time). The
idiom "How long has it been since...?" / "It's been [duration] since..." restates a present-perfect
duration using simple past inside the since-clause. p. 38.

### Unit 20 — Present perfect or simple past?

Present perfect is barred whenever a specific finished-past time marker is present (yesterday, two
years ago, when I was a child) — those require simple past even where American/British usage might
otherwise differ. Present perfect is required for genuinely unfinished time periods ("today,"
"this morning" while still morning); simple past is required once that period is understood as
closed ("yesterday," "this morning" said in the afternoon). Present perfect is never used for
historical facts disconnected from the present speaker ("The Chinese invented printing," not "have
invented"). p. 40.

### Unit 21 — Past perfect (I had done)

Formed had + past participle; functions as "the past of the present perfect" — marks an action
already completed before another specified past point ("When I arrived at the party, Tom had already
gone home"). Contrasted directly against simple past for the same past reference point depending on
sequencing: "Was Tom there when you arrived?" "No, he had already gone home" (past perfect, prior
event) vs. "Yes, but he went home soon afterward" (simple past, subsequent event). p. 42.

### Unit 22 — Past perfect continuous (I had been doing)

"The past of the present perfect continuous" — marks an action that had been ongoing up to a
specified past point, often explaining a past physical/situational state ("their clothes were dirty...
They had been fighting"). Can specify duration-before-a-past-event ("They had been playing for half an
hour when there was a terrible storm"). Distinguished from plain past continuous: "had been raining"
(it had stopped by the reference point) vs. "was raining" (still falling at the reference point).
p. 44.

### Unit 23 — Have and have got

"Have"/"has"/"had" = possess, and also covers family relationships, ailments, and (with do-support in
questions/negatives) general possession statements. "Have got"/"has got" is an interchangeable
present-tense-only alternative with no do-support in questions/negatives ("Have you got a headache?"
= "Do you have a headache?"); it cannot be used for the past ("she had long hair," never "she had got").
A distinct fixed-expression use of "have" covers actions, especially eating/drinking and discrete
events — have breakfast/lunch/dinner/a meal/a cup of coffee/a cigarette, have a party, have a look,
have a baby — and "have got" is explicitly disallowed in this action sense. p. 46.

### Unit 24 — Used to (I used to do)

Marks a past habitual action or past-only state that no longer holds ("He used to smoke 40 cigarettes
a day" [but quit]; "We used to live in a small village" [but moved]). Always past tense — there is no
present form of "used to" itself; the simple present fills that gap instead (used to live / live).
Question form is "did...use to...?" (not "used to"); negative is "didn't use to" or "never used to."
Explicitly distinguished from the unrelated "be/get used to doing" (= accustomed to something,
cross-referenced to Unit 59): "I used to live alone" (past habit, no longer true) vs. "I am used to
living alone" (currently accustomed to it). p. 48.

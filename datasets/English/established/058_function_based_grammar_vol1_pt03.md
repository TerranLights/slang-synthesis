## Grammar points

### Reference is about the universe of discourse, not the Real World (p.213–215)

Against the logical tradition that treats reference as a mapping to Real-World entities, Givón shows
English grammar codes *The King of France* and *The Queen of England* identically regardless of
real-world existence, and the same holds for fictional entities introduced into a story (*"There was
once a man who lived in the forest"*) — subsequent reference tracks whatever has been verbally
established in the **universe of discourse**, not real-world ontology.

### Referential intent: the same indefinite form can commit the speaker to a specific entity or not (p.215–216)

*"John married a rich woman, though he didn't know her well"* commits the speaker to a specific
individual (referring); *"John wanted to marry a rich woman, though he didn't know her/any well"* is
genuinely ambiguous between a referring reading (he had someone specific in mind) and a
non-referring reading (any member of the type would do) — the two readings are disambiguated
only by the following discourse, since *a(n)* itself is neutral between them.

### Propositional modality predicts (but doesn't fully determine) the reference status of an indefinite NP (p.216–219)

Grouping the four modalities from ch.4 into two meta-modalities — **fact** (presupposition,
R-assertion) and **non-fact** (IRR-assertion, NEG-assertion) — yields a clean rule: under fact, an
indefinite NP can only be referring; under non-fact, it may be either referring or non-referring. This
holds for every non-fact environment surveyed (conditionals, yes/no questions, commands,
epistemic adverbs, modals, the scope of non-implicative modality/manipulation verbs, non-factive
PCU-verb complements) plus two additional non-fact-like environments: the **habitual** tense-aspect
and **nominal-predicate** position — the common denominator across all of them being that none
depicts a specific event at a specific time. Some verbs lexically carry irrealis/non-fact scope
regardless of their own tense (*look for, crave* vs. *date, eat*) — Givón calls these **non-implicative**
("world-creating") verbs, distinct from ordinary **implicative** verbs, where commitment to the
verb's truth entails commitment to the object's reference.

### Three indefinite articles beyond 'a(n)' occupy fixed points on a referentiality scale (p.219–222)

*Any* is strictly non-referring (incompatible with fact modalities and with nominal-predicate position,
but compatible with nearly every non-fact environment including negation). *No* is the dedicated
marker for constituent (NP) negation specifically (see pt02's negation-cycle grammar point) and is
accordingly restricted to that one environment. *Some* sits between *a(n)* and *any*: under fact it is
obligatorily referring, but under nearly every non-fact environment it carries a strong — though not
absolute — bias toward the non-referring reading, growing stronger moving from future/modal to
conditional to yes/no-question contexts, and becoming an absolute ban under negation; only under
the habitual does the bias relax back toward allowing either reading. All three also function as
pronouns: *any* and *some* can stand alone when the head noun is anaphorically recoverable, or take
*-one/-body* (human) / *-thing* (non-human) augmentation when it isn't; *no* requires augmentation
always. Augmented-pronoun stress additionally tracks modality: emphatic realis assertions license
only augmented *some* (*"Someone did it!"*, not *\*"Anybody did it!"*); emphatic irrealis licenses both
augmented *some* and *any*; standard educated emphatic VP-negation licenses only augmented *any*;
augmented *no* is reserved for NP-negation.

### Negation uniquely restricts referring NPs under its scope to definites only (p.224–225)

A referring-*indefinite* NP cannot fall under the scope of negation (*\*"He didn't see an eagle"* cannot
mean "there existed an eagle, but he didn't see it") — only non-referring or referring-*definite* NPs
can. Givón derives this from negation's presuppositional structure (established in ch.4): since a
NEG-assertion presupposes the hearer's prior familiarity with the corresponding affirmative event, a
referring participant in that event must already be hearer-identifiable — which is precisely what
"definite" means. This yields the project-relevant working definition: **definite = identifiable to the
hearer**.

### A continuum of referential specificity, coded by three co-varying grammatical devices (p.224–226)

The gradation from *"Did you see anything there?"* through fuller and fuller specification up to
*"...a tall man there wearing a blue shirt and sitting on a red barrel and twirling a silver baton..."*
reflects a real psychological/probabilistic continuum (how strongly does the speaker intend a
particular individual; how likely is the hearer to infer one), coded by three correlated dimensions:
indefinite-article choice (*a* > *some* > *any*, most-to-least likely specific), degree of restrictive
modification (more > less), and lexical specificity of the noun itself (*specific noun* > *person* >
*thing*).

### Plurality downgrades referential intent even under fact modality (p.225–226)

Singular indefinite objects under irrealis (*"planning to sell a house"*) more readily get a referring
reading than their plural counterparts (*"planning to sell houses"*), and — more strikingly — the
same bias toward non-referring persists even under *fact* modality (*"She read a book"* reads as
referring; *"She read books"* reads as non-referring) — Givón is careful to distinguish this from strict
logical non-reference (the plural objects obviously existed if the event occurred); it is a *pragmatic*
sense of non-reference, where the individual identity of the plural participants simply doesn't
matter to the discourse.

### Real-world pragmatic knowledge tips the scale between referring and non-referring readings (p.226–228)

Beyond the grammar-level predictions above, culturally-shared scripts about typical behavior
(a private individual typically shops for *one* house before buying but typically sells only *the one*
house they own; newspaper copies are maximally interchangeable while books on a shelf are
individuated) systematically bias the reading of an otherwise-ambiguous indefinite — and the same
sentence's bias flips if the discourse-external facts change (a real-estate dealer instead of a private
buyer/seller).

### Anaphoric pronouns can track either a referring or non-referring antecedent (p.228–231)

Unstressed anaphoric pronouns (*he, she, it, they*) are primarily discourse-pragmatic (tracking
accessible antecedents) rather than semantic, which is shown by their indifference to the referring/
non-referring distinction of their antecedent: colloquial American English's non-referring-antecedent
plural *they/them* (replacing older prescriptive *he/him*) works for gender-unspecified non-referring
antecedents (*"If you see anybody there, tell them..."*), while a fully-specified 4-way contrast
(referring-definite / non-referring-definite / referring-indefinite / non-referring-indefinite) can all be
tracked by pronouns in the same surface position following *"John was planning to marry a rich
girl,..."* — definite antecedents take *she*, indefinite antecedents take *one*, regardless of their own
referring/non-referring status in each pairing. Combining *one* with a definite determiner (*"the one
he had"*), however, forces a referring reading even under otherwise-ambiguous irrealis scope.

### Cataphoric topicality: the colloquial unstressed 'this' flags importance before definiteness is even relevant (p.230–232)

In informal spoken (and some "Dear Abby"-style written) registers, unstressed *this* — contrasting
with *a(n)* — marks a first-mention indefinite referent as one that will be *important going forward*
in the discourse (cataphoric topicality), independent of the referring/non-referring distinction;
confirmed via a text example where the *this*-marked referent recurs throughout while the
*a(n)*-marked referents never recur again.

### Definiteness is a clause in the speaker-hearer communicative contract, grounded in three distinct accessibility sources (p.232–235)

Definiteness ≠ reference: it specifically concerns the speaker's assumption about what is *mentally
accessible to the hearer*. Three grounds license that assumption: (a) **situation-based (deictic)**
definiteness — interlocutors themselves, demonstratives, time/place adverbs — organized around a
near-speaker/away-from-speaker binary (*I/we* vs. *you*; *this/these* vs. *that/those*; *now* vs. *then*;
*here* vs. *there*); (b) **culturally-based** definiteness — entities identifiable to the whole speech
community (or relevant sub-culture) without any discourse mention at all (*the sun, the president,
the sheriff*), which cascades through **frame-based reference**: naming one culturally-shared frame
(*school, a restaurant, his father*) automatically makes its sub-frame entities accessible even on first
mention (*the bus, the waiter/the menu, his father* via the "everyone has exactly one father"
convention); (c) **text-based (anaphoric)** definiteness — referents established by prior explicit
mention in the discourse itself, which the rest of §5.3 is devoted to.

### Text-based reference: continuity predicts zero/pronoun, discontinuity predicts a full definite NP (p.235)

A referent still mentally activated from the immediately preceding clause is typically coded with
**zero anaphora** or an **unstressed pronoun**; a referent being reinstated after a gap is typically
re-coded as a **full definite NP** (definite article, other determiner, etc.) — continuity vs. discontinuity
of activation predicts the grammatical weight of the referring expression.

### Stressed vs. unstressed pronouns are two distinct paradigms disambiguated by subject continuity (p.235–238)

Despite identical spelling, English has two functionally distinct pronoun sets: **unstressed
("anaphoric")** pronouns require (a) a continuous referent and (b) unproblematic identification;
**stressed ("contrastive")** pronouns are used when the referent is continuous but identification is
problematic — most commonly to force a **subject switch** against the default expectation of subject
continuity. Minimal-pair evidence: *"Mary told Suzy, then she told Sally"* (unstressed *she* = *Mary*,
the preceding subject) vs. *"...then SHE told Sally"* (stressed *SHE* = *Suzy*, the preceding object) —
demonstrating that subject-continuity is the unmarked norm and switch-of-subject the marked
counter-norm requiring extra grammatical signaling. Even full gender disambiguation doesn't let
unstressed pronouns substitute for stressed ones in a genuine subject-switch or **thematic-contrast**
context (*"Bill came in first...Mary came in next. SHE didn't [look tired]"* requires stress even though
*he/she* already disambiguate gender) — because the contrast itself, not just reference resolution, is
what the stress is doing.

### Demonstratives double as text-based (not just situation-based) definite markers, preserving their spatial near/far logic metaphorically (p.238–240)

*That* can point backward (anaphorically) to an already-delivered text-chunk; *this* can point forward
(cataphorically) to an upcoming one — illustrated with a newsreader sign-off formula (*"And that was
the news...This is Walter Cronkite..."*) and literary examples where *this/these* (near → more
accessible, already clearly defined) contrasts with *that/those* (far → less accessible, not yet fully
defined) even when both refer to anaphoric text — the original spatial near/far semantics is
preserved under a accessibility metaphor.

### Names as the maximally efficient text-based definite device: global vs. locally-anchored accessibility (p.240–242)

A name marks a referent as persistently, *globally* important across the entire current discourse
(where "current discourse," for a personal name, can span one's whole lifetime) — unlike full definite
NPs, which depend on *local* anaphoric accessibility. Givón distinguishes **non-relational names**
(*Dorian Grey, George Washington*; must be established once in-text, then globally accessible) from
**relationally-anchored names** (*Mom, Dad, home, Tuesday, Christmas*; accessible automatically once
their anchor — speaker, a per-week/per-year cycle — is itself accessible, via a culturally-shared
one-to-one convention like "everyone has exactly one Dad").

### Generic NPs are discourse-pragmatically "referring" despite their special logical status (p.242–244)

Generic subjects (*The lion is.../Lions are.../All lions are.../A lion is...* — four distinct surface forms)
behave for anaphoric-tracking purposes exactly like ordinary referring subjects (trackable by *it/they*
or zero), suggesting that once the relevant "universe" is adjusted from tokens to types, generics
refer in the same way ordinary NPs do. Generic *objects*, by contrast, show a genuine three-way
reference-status split (token-referring / non-referring / generic) and — unlike generic subjects — can
only be realized in two of the four generic-subject forms (definite and bare plural; the quantified-
plural and bare-indefinite forms are ungrammatical as generic objects).

### A cognitive model of referential-coherence grammar: text-memory as a filing system (p.244–246)

Givón's capstone metaphor for the whole chapter: text-memory is hierarchically organized
(clauses → chains → paragraphs → episodes), with each chain functioning as a **text-node** that only
one referent can be filed under ("active") at a time. The grammar of reference/definiteness is thus
a set of **processing cues** instructing the hearer to: retain current activation (zero/anaphoric
pronoun — unmarked), defer the activation decision (full NP/stressed pronoun/name — marked),
terminate current activation (articles/word-order/grammatical-role choices signaling importance —
marked) vs. continue it (unmarked), and either search memory for an existing referent (definite —
marked) or skip the search and open a brand-new text-node (indefinite — unmarked). This reframes
the entire reference/definiteness system as a set of markedness-coded instructions for memory
search and attentional allocation, not merely a logical/semantic classification.

### The noun phrase's internal architecture: a rigid pre-/post-nominal ordering template (p.247–249)

An NP is either a pronoun, a name, or a full noun-based phrase; a full NP has an obligatory head
noun plus optional modifiers in a rigid relative order: **pre-nominal** QUANT–DET–AP–(modifying N)*
then the head noun, followed by **post-nominal** PL–REL*–POSS-NP–N-COMP (post-nominal slots
can in principle multiply, chiefly via recursive relative clauses, unlike the normally single-occupant
pre-nominal slots). The **NP semantic amalgamation principle** governs the whole structure: whatever
semantic features belong to the head noun belong to the entire NP — modifiers add information but
don't change the head's inherent lexical type.

### Quantifiers occupy two distinct pre-nominal positions with different syntax (p.249–251)

**Partitive definite quantifiers** require *of* + a definite determiner (*some of the people, none of my
friends*) and are categorically incompatible with an indefinite head noun (*\*none of a man*).
**Indefinite quantifiers-determiners** take neither *of* nor a separate determiner, because they
themselves occupy the determiner slot (*some women, every person, no response*); cardinal
numerals can occupy either slot. Two quantifiers are irregular: *only* is not itself an indefinite
quantifier but combines with one, requiring an article only when the following noun is referring, not
when it's non-referring/generic; *all* is inherently generic/non-referring unless accompanied by a
definite determiner (*All humans are mortal* generic vs. ungrammatical *\*All some humans are
mortal* as a referring indefinite).

### 'Only'/'even'/'just': clause-level floating scope governed by stress, adjacency, and a left-right/object-over-subject bias (p.251–255)

These contrastive quantifiers can appear in multiple clause positions, with their semantic scope
determined primarily by **contrastive stress placement** rather than literal adjacency — only a
stressed constituent can fall under their scope, though adjacency still exerts some pull (strongest at
the clause's two extremes, subject and object), with a weak preference for scope to fall rightward
(on the succeeding rather than preceding element) and for an object-scoped quantifier to "float"
toward the subject position more easily than the reverse (suggesting a historical "starts in the object
NP, gets displaced" derivation). In writing, where contrastive stress usually isn't marked (no italics/
bold), placing *only* directly adjacent to its intended scope becomes the only reliable disambiguation
device — which is exactly the rule traditional usage editors enforce, confirmed with five real
newspaper "corrections" Givón cites and critiques (noting that, in several, real-world/contextual
knowledge alone would have resolved the ambiguity regardless of placement). Within the NP itself
(rather than across the clause), the quantifier's position is rigid and only its *scope* (which word gets
contrasted) varies with stress.

### Determiners form one unified syntactic slot: only one occupant at a time (p.255–256)

Definite article, demonstratives, indefinite articles (incl. unstressed colloquial *this/these*),
non-referring articles, and possessive modifiers (pronominal or full-NP) are all members of one
determiner class — proven by their mutual exclusivity (*\*the my house, \*this her room, \*his that
book* are all ungrammatical): whichever one is chosen fills the single determiner slot.

### Adjective ordering is governed by a five-factor "closer to the noun = more defining" principle (p.256–259)

Following Gruber (1967b): an adjective sits closer to the head noun the more it is (a) central to the
noun's meaning, (b) an inherent/durable quality, (c) generic rather than specific information, (d)
given rather than new information, and (e) non-restrictive rather than restrictive. Demonstrated with
minimal pairs where the more natural order consistently reflects a background-property-first,
foreground-property-closest-to-noun logic (*a large African elephant* preferred over *an African large
elephant*, because "African elephant" is the more unitary, inherent concept and size is the more
variable property predicated of it). A noun-noun compound's modifying noun (*bird* in *bird-dog*)
patterns as maximally non-restrictive/inherent and therefore sits closest of all, confirmed by its
characteristic stress pattern (primary stress on the modifier, not the head).

### Compounding: a stress-marked cline from syntactic modification to fused lexical item (p.258–262)

Noun-noun sequences range from fully independent syntactic modification (each noun independently
stressed: *the Panama invasion surprise decision*) to true compounds (invariant primary stress on the
**first** element only: *BIRD-house, MAIL-man*). The same stress-pattern test distinguishes a modifying
from a compounding adjective (*a black bird* = any black bird vs. *a BLACK-bird* = a species) and
shows that once fused, compound meaning can drift non-compositionally (*White House* = the
President's residence). Modifying nouns and compounding elements must sit immediately adjacent
to the head (never separated by an intervening adjective), directly following from their maximally
inherent/generic status on the ordering scale above. A further compound sub-type arises from
nominalized verb phrases, yielding **action nouns** (*trout-fishing* ← "fishing (for) trout") and
**actor/agent nouns** (*beaver-trapper* ← "he traps beavers") — both preserving the old Object-Verb
order inherited from Old English's dominant OV constituent order, a fossil surviving inside
compounds even though the clause-level order shifted to VO in Middle English; the handful of
VO-order compounds that did later form (*tell-tale, cut-throat, turn-coat, Shakespeare*) are mostly
archaic or frozen into proper names, with only a few recent productive examples (*take-charge,
do-gooder, carry-all*).

### Manner-adverb modifiers of the adjective phrase, and their clausal-nominalization source (p.261–262)

Degree/manner adverbs (*very, rather, incredibly, unbelievably*) quantify the adjective they precede
within the AP; some (*too*) prefer predicate-adjective position over NP-internal modifying position
unless reordered (*?a too hard task* vs. *a task too hard*). Many AP-internal adverbs trace historically
to nominalizations of full clauses in which the corresponding verb was itself adverb-modified
(*"The lady dressed properly"* → *a properly dressed lady*) — an early instance of the nominalization
machinery developed fully in §6.6.3.

### Three post-nominal modifier types, each with a different relation to finite clauses (p.262–264)

**Relative clauses** bear an overtly syntactic relation to a full clause (*the man who came to dinner* ←
*a man came to dinner*; treated fully in ch.9). **Noun complements** bear a less obvious but still
syntactic relation, arising when a nominalized verb's own complement survives the nominalization
(*her periodic attempts to find a job* ← *she periodically attempted to find a job*; treated fully in
§6.6.4 below and ch.7). **Possessive phrases** (*of*-marked PPs) bear a purely *semantic* relation to an
underlying proposition and are otherwise unconstrained — the possessive/genitive case is shown to
be a real grab-bag (*a bottle of wine, the march of time, the dogs of war, the University of Oregon*),
not a unified semantic relation.

### Pseudo-possessives: surface 'NP-of-NP' that has been historically re-analyzed into a complex preposition (p.264–267)

A true possessive (*the top of the house*, where *it* = the top) is structurally distinct from a
historically-derived **pseudo-possessive** that looks identical on the surface but has the opposite
head-modifier relation (*on top of the house*, where *it* = the house) — confirmed by anaphoric-
pronoun tests. This re-analysis is the diachronic source of a whole set of complex locative
prepositions (*inside, behind, out of, at the bottom of, in the middle of*) and some non-locative ones
(*instead of, in spite of, because of, for the sake of*), at varying stages of phonological reduction (some
have shed *of*/*the* entirely, like *inside, behind*; others retain both). A separate, differently-motivated
re-analysis produces frozen idiomatic pseudo-possessive modifiers (*a hell of a fight, a tiger of a
woman*) and complex quantifiers (*a bunch of, a pound of*).

### Restrictive vs. non-restrictive pre-nominal modification, and its stress correlate (p.267–269)

Demonstratives and ordinals are inherently restrictive (narrow the referential search: *this* book
rather than *that*; *the first* one rather than the second). Possessive determiners and ordinary
adjectives can be either, disambiguated by stress: **stressed** = restrictive/contrastive (*I found HIS
letter. HER letter I lost*; *the INDUSTRIOUS Chinese made it* [vs. the non-industrious ones]);
**unstressed** = non-restrictive (generic-quality-attributing: *the industrious Chinese came to California*
[all of them, characterized as industrious]). Comma-separated (non-restrictive) adjective sequences
vs. unseparated (restrictive) sequences in writing mirror this same stress-based contrast. Names,
being inherently uniquely-referring already, resist restrictive modification (*?This Alexander*) but
readily accept non-restrictive, quasi-name-forming modification (*Alexander the Great, Gay Paris*).

### Several modifier types can themselves substitute for the whole NP, pronoun-style (p.269–270)

Demonstratives, adjectives, numerals, quantifiers, possessives, and ordinals can all stand in for a
full NP by dropping the head noun (*that idea will not do* → *that will not do*; *two women came* →
*two came*) without undergoing the destressing that ordinary anaphoric pronouns undergo. A
parallel but distinct pattern substitutes the unstressed dummy pronoun *one/ones* for just the head
noun while retaining the modifier (*that one, the poor ones, the first one*) — and this second pattern
is blocked after numerals, "all," and possessives (*\*two ones, \*all ones, \*his one*).

### Noun-phrase elements are normally contiguous, with two licensed exceptions (p.270–272)

Default written English keeps all NP elements adjacent (scattering is ungrammatical: *\*My jumped
over red the back fence horse*). Two systematic exceptions exist: **relative-clause extraposition**
(moving the REL-clause from its head noun to clause-final position), licensed more freely in speech
than writing and blocked when it would create attachment ambiguity with an intervening NP (*?He
gave a lecture to a class that was ill prepared* — ambiguous between modifying "lecture" and
"class"); and **clause-final parenthetical afterthoughts** that supply identifying information for an
under-specified in-clause NP (*"Mine was faster, the old Chevy"*), subject to the same real-world-
plausibility disambiguation constraint as extraposed relatives.

### NP conjunction violates simple predicate-logic distribution whenever events, not timeless qualities, are involved (p.272–275)

The logical biconditional *F(x)&F(y) ⟺ F(x&y)* holds for timeless, discourse-free predicates (*"Mary is
tall and John is tall" ⟺ "John and Mary are tall"*) but breaks down for event-coded predicates
(*"John and Mary left together" does NOT reduce to* two independent "X left together" claims — the
togetherness is only meaningful of the pair as a unit). Givón reformulates this as: conjoined NPs in
natural language primarily describe **a single event with shared-role co-participants**, not an
economy-motivated shorthand for two separate clauses — codified as the general **iconicity
principle**: separate events get coded as separate clauses; single complex events get coded as
single complex clauses. *Both* inserted into a two-NP conjunction is "a play on the norm," deliberately
signaling the atypical reading (two separate, non-reciprocal events) against the normal single-event
default (*"John and Mary got married"* = to each other; *"Both John and Mary got married"* = to other
people).

### Conjoined-NP order is never logically forced, and tracks topicality plus a catalog of culturally-fixed preference hierarchies (p.275–277)

Although *A & B* and *B & A* are logically equivalent, natural language strongly prefers one serial
order over the other, correlating with relative topicality/importance (more topical NP fronted;
supported by text-frequency and psycholinguistic-processing studies cited) and, independently, with
a set of largely-frozen cultural preference hierarchies documented by Cooper & Ross (1975): near >
far, adult > young, male > female, singular > plural, large > small, animate > inanimate, human >
non-human, agent/large > patient/small, whole > part, salient > non-salient, possessor > possessed,
positive > negative — each illustrated with an idiom locked into the preferred order (*father and son*,
not *\*son and father*; *day and night*, not *\*night and day*).

### Three degrees of syntactic integration for conjoined NPs, cued by preposition/determiner/article/adjective/conjunction repetition (p.277–286)

A single recurring pattern spans prepositions, determiners/articles, modifying adjectives, and the
conjunction word itself: **repeating** the marker on each conjunct signals two more-separate
sub-events (lower integration); **using it only once** signals one maximally-integrated single event.
Demonstrated systematically: prepositions (*gave the prize to Joe and Sally* [one joint award] vs. *...to
Joe and to Sally* [two separate awards]); articles (*the boys and girls* [one group] vs. *the boys and the
girls* [two]); adjectives (*tall boys and girls* vs. *tall boys and tall girls*); and multiple-vs-single
conjunction/disjunction words (*either John, Mary or Paul* [maximal integration] vs. *either John or
Mary or Paul* [lower integration]; *both Mary and John will come* [more separate] vs. *Mary and John
will come* [more joint]) — all instances of the general **isomorphism of code-separation and meaning-
separation** principle (more semantic separateness → more syntactic separateness, via pauses or
repeated morphemes). The fully-integrated end-state is constrained by **semantic compatibility of the
conjuncts** (*?I saw your father and dog* is odd because the two nouns aren't semantically
"close" enough to fuse into one conceptual unit) and by **number agreement**: fully-fused conjuncts
with only one shared article must be jointly compatible in number (*\*some boy and girl*, since
*some* is inherently plural but the conjuncts are singular), and a fully-integrated conjunction can
itself freeze into a genuine compound noun (*wine-and-cheese, bread-and-butter, horse-and-buggy*).

### Group nouns and conjoined subjects show a genuine individual-vs-collective verb-agreement contrast (p.286–287)

Collective nouns (*team, crowd, faculty, majority*) and conjunction-derived group nouns (*Sally and
Joe*) take plural agreement when construed as a collection of distinct individuals (*"The team are
bickering; they hate each other"*) and singular agreement when construed as one unified entity
(*"The team is winning, it's in good shape"*) — the same referent category, coded by number
agreement choice alone, switching between two distinct conceptualizations.

### Nominalization: a systematic finite-clause-to-NP conversion, with a graded finiteness scale (p.287–290)

**Syntactic nominalization** converts a verbal clause (full or VP-only) into an NP; **lexical
nominalization** converts a single verb/adjective into a noun. The verb always becomes the
nominalized NP's head noun; other clausal elements become NP-internal modifiers. English shows a
coherent **finiteness scale** from most to least finite: finite verb → perfect participle (*having known
him*) → progressive participle (*knowing him*) → *to*-infinitive (*to know him*) → *-ing*-infinitive
(*knowing him*, nominal use) → lexical nominal (*her knowledge of him*) — the first four forms are
morphologically uniform across the whole verb lexicon; the lexical-nominal form is idiosyncratic,
sometimes suffixed (often restricted to Romance-derived verbs: *refusal, rejection, detention*),
sometimes a bare unmarked stem (*murder, search, turn, change*), sometimes built on an underlying
adjective rather than verb (*illness, length, absence*), and sometimes homophonous with the
progressive/-*ing*-infinitive forms, disambiguated only by surrounding structure (compare *breaking his
fall* [participle, implies simultaneity+progressive] vs. *liked breaking the rules* [infinitive] vs. *his
breaking of all rules* [lexical nominal, neither implication, subject+object both genitive]).

### Nominalized case-marking: a double-genitive strategy keeps agent and patient distinguishable (p.291–295)

At the most nominal end of the scale, both subject and object get genitive marking — but English
resolves the resulting potential ambiguity with a **double-genitive strategy**: the subject-agent takes
the **pre-nominal** ("Germanic") genitive determiner slot, the object-patient takes the **post-nominal**
*of*-("Norman") genitive slot (*the enemy's destruction of the city*). When the agent is unexpressed,
three strategies remain: object stays post-nominal genitive alone (*the destruction of the city*); agent
resurfaces as a passive-style *by*-phrase (*the destruction of the city by the enemy*); or the
patient-object itself claims the *pre*-nominal genitive slot, with the agent optionally still present as a
*by*-phrase (*the city's destruction (by the enemy)*) — though this last option is blocked for a generic
nominalized event with a non-referring object (*\*Math's knowledge (by the candidate) is required*,
only *Knowledge of math...*). Intransitive nominalized subjects can occupy either genitive slot in
principle, but pronoun subjects are restricted to the pre-nominal slot only (*its growth*, never *\*the
growth of it*). Within a non-patient (dative/recipient) direct object specifically, the *of*-genitive
pattern is categorically blocked regardless of nominalization type — the *of*-genitive is reserved
strictly for marking patients, functioning as a disambiguation device against semantic-role confusion.
Less-finite nominalizations preserve more of the original finite-clause case marking: *-ing*-form
nominalizations keep the object in ordinary object form while still genitive-marking the subject;
*to*-infinitive nominalizations mark neither subject nor object as genitive, instead marking an
expressed subject with *for* (*For him to build the house...*).

### Indirect objects and manner adverbs undergo their own systematic conversions under nominalization (p.295–297)

Indirect objects keep their original preposition and post-verbal position essentially unchanged
through nominalization (*his objection to the proposal* ← *he objected to the proposal*). Manner
adverbs convert into modifying adjectives wherever a corresponding adjective exists (*rapidly
destroyed* → *rapid destruction*); when no corresponding adjective exists, the adverb keeps its
adverbial form but is then confined to less-nominal (*-ing*-infinitive) structures only, never the fully
nominal lexical form (*his arriving tomorrow* is fine; *\*his tomorrow arrival* is not) — unless a
semantically-equivalent adjective happens to be available from an unrelated source (*like a maniac*
→ *maniacal*, licensing *his maniacal grin*). The presence/range of determiners on the de-verbal head
noun (from none, through definite, to indefinite-referring, non-referring, and plural) is itself graded
evidence of how far a given nominalization has migrated toward full NP-hood.

### Noun complements: the post-nominal survival of a nominalized verb's own complement (p.297–300)

When a nominalized clause's source verb itself required a verbal complement (a modality,
manipulation, or PCU verb from ch.3), that complement survives the nominalization largely intact as
a post-nominal **noun complement** (*his wanting to leave home, her wish that he would come back,
his discovery that she was blind*) — syntactically parallel to how indirect objects survive, and the
direct structural bridge to the full treatment of verbal complements promised for chapter 7.

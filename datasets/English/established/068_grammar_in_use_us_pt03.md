## Grammar points

### Unit 40 — Passive (1) (be done / have been done)

Active voice puts the doer first; passive voice fronts the receiver of the action and uses the correct
form of "be" + past participle, with the agent optionally added via "by" ("This house was built by my
grandfather"). The passive is preferred when the agent is unimportant or unknown. After modal verbs
(will, can, must, etc.) and certain other verbs (have to, be going to, want to), the passive takes the
base form "be done" ("This problem can be solved"); after those same contexts in the past, "have been
done" ("should have cleaned" → "should have been cleaned"). pp. 80.

### Unit 41 — Passive (2) (present and past tenses)

A full paradigm table across six tense/aspect combinations, each given as an active/passive minimal
pair: simple present (is cleaned), simple past (was cleaned), present continuous (is being cleaned),
past continuous (was being cleaned), present perfect (has been cleaned), past perfect (had been
cleaned). No new rule beyond regular combination of the tense auxiliary with "be + past participle."
p. 82.

### Unit 42 — Passive (3)

Ditransitive verbs (offer, ask, tell, give, send, show, teach, pay — verbs taking two objects) allow
two different passive constructions depending on which object becomes the subject ("Ann wasn't offered
the job" / "The job wasn't offered to Ann"), with the person-as-subject version being more natural.
"Be born" is a fixed passive-only verb, almost always past tense ("Where were you born?"). The passive
gerund is "being done" ("I remember being given a toy drum"). "Get" can substitute for "be" in the
passive, mainly in informal spoken English and especially for unplanned/accidental events ("The dog
got run over"), but not in every passive context (not "gets liked"). p. 84.

### Unit 43 — It is said that.../He is said to..., etc., and supposed to

A reporting-passive construction for unverified general belief or news-report claims: "It is said
that X is Y" ↔ "X is said to be Y," with the same pattern extending to thought, believed, known,
reported, expected, alleged, considered, understood. "(Be) supposed to" has two senses: (a) "said to
be" (reputation: "It's supposed to be very good"), and (b) planned/arranged, often implicitly
contrasted with what actually happened ("The train was supposed to arrive at 11:30, but it was 40
minutes late"). "Not supposed to" marks something disallowed or inadvisable. p. 86.

### Unit 44 — Have something done

The causative "have + object + past participle" marks arranging for someone else to perform an action
("Bill had the roof repaired" ≠ he did it himself); word order is fixed, with the past participle
following the object. "Get something done" is offered as a mainly-informal-spoken alternative. A
distinct, unrelated sense of the same surface structure marks something (often negative) happening to
the subject, with no arrangement implied ("He had all his money stolen" = his money was stolen,
not that he arranged the theft). pp. 88.

### Units 45–46 — Reported speech (1) and (2)

Direct speech ("Tom said, 'I'm feeling sick'") converts to reported speech ("Tom said he was feeling
sick") by backshifting tense one step into the past: am/is→was, are→were, have/has→had, will→would,
can→could, do/want/know→did/wanted/knew. Simple past may stay the same or shift further to past
perfect. Backshift is optional when the reported statement is still true at the time of reporting, but
mandatory when there's a known discrepancy between what was said and what's now true (if Jim looks
fine now, report "Ann said that you were sick," not "are sick," even though the grammatical subject is
present-tense-eligible). Must, might, could, would, should, and ought don't change in reported speech;
"may" normally shifts to "might." "Tell" requires an explicit addressee object; "say" does not take a
personal object directly. The infinitive construction (tell/ask + object + to-infinitive) reports
orders and requests, with a negative infinitive for prohibitions ("told Jim not to shout"); "said"
can take a bare infinitive without an object ("said to stay," not "said me to stay"). pp. 90–92.

### Unit 47 — Questions (1)

Standard yes/no and wh-questions invert the auxiliary verb and subject (including "be" as its own
auxiliary); simple present/past questions insert do/does/did unless who/what/which is itself the
grammatical subject of the sentence, in which case no inversion or do-support occurs ("Who invented
the steam engine?" not "who did invent"). Negative questions serve three pragmatic functions: to
express surprise, to form exclamations ("Doesn't that dress look nice!"), and to invite the listener's
agreement; "why...?" negative questions keep the auxiliary-n't immediately after "why," before the
subject. p. 94.

### Unit 48 — Questions (2) (Do you know where...? / He asked me where...)

When a question is embedded inside a larger frame ("Do you know...?", "Could you tell me...?"), it
loses standard question word order and reverts to ordinary statement order ("Do you know where Tom
has gone?" not "where has Tom gone"), including loss of do-support ("Do you know when the movie
begins?"). "If" or "whether" fills the question-word slot when there is no wh-word. The identical
word-order shift occurs in reported questions, which also undergo the Unit 45 backshift. pp. 96.

### Unit 49 — Auxiliary verbs in short answers/questions, etc.: So/Neither am I, etc.

Auxiliary verbs (can, have, might, do/does/did, etc.) stand in for a fuller verb phrase already
established in context, in: short answers ("Are you working tomorrow?" "Yes, I am"), short/echo
questions used to show polite interest or surprise rather than genuinely ask ("I've just seen Tom."
"Oh, have you?"), and so/neither agreement constructions with inverted subject-auxiliary order ("So
am I," "Neither do I" — not "so I am"). "Nor" substitutes for "neither"; "not...either" substitutes
for either neither or nor. A fixed cluster of anaphoric-"so" idioms after opinion verbs (I think so, I
hope so, I guess so, I suppose so, I'm afraid so) each has its own idiosyncratic negative form (I
don't think so; I hope not; I guess not; I don't suppose so / I suppose not; I'm afraid not). p. 98.

### Unit 50 — Tag questions (are you? doesn't he?, etc.)

A mini-question appended to a statement, using the matching auxiliary; a negative statement takes a
positive tag, and a positive statement takes a negative tag. Falling intonation on the tag signals the
speaker isn't really asking (inviting agreement); rising intonation signals a genuine question. A
negative-statement-plus-positive-tag combination is also used to politely request something or ask
someone to do something ("You wouldn't have a cigarette, would you?"). Fixed special-case tags: "shall
we?" after "Let's...," "will you?" after an imperative, and "aren't I?" (not the logically regular but
unused "amn't I?"). p. 100.

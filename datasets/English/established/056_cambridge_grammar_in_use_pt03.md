## Grammar points

### Unit 19 — Present tenses for the future

Two future-marking present forms, not interchangeable: present continuous for a personally-arranged
future event (*I'm meeting her at the station*), present simple for an institutionally-fixed
timetable event or a plan so fixed it behaves like one (*the film starts at 8.15*; *I start my new
job on Monday*). *Will* is explicitly disallowed for arranged events.

### Unit 20 — *(I'm) going to (do)*

Marks either a prior decision/intention (not necessarily arranged — contrast Unit 19's arranged
sense) or a present-evidence prediction (*he's going to fall into the hole* — the man is already
walking toward it). Past form *was/were going to* marks an unrealized past intention.

### Units 21–22 — *Will/shall* (1) and (2)

*Will* for an in-the-moment decision (offers/agreements/promises/*will you...?* requests), contrasted
with *going to*/present continuous for pre-existing decisions/arrangements (will is explicitly
barred there). *Shall I/we...?* solicits the listener's opinion, distinct in force from *will
you...?* (see Vocabulary). Unit 22 shifts to *will*'s separate predictive function, its common
hedging collocations, and the *I shall/we shall* first-person-only variant.

### Unit 23 — *I will* and *I'm going to*

Direct side-by-side resolution of the two constructions' overlap and divergence: for *decisions*,
new-in-the-moment (*will*) vs. already-made (*going to*); for *predictions*, evidence-grounded-now
(*going to*) vs. general judgment (*will*, often softened with *probably*). Explicitly states the
difference is "often very small" for predictions — a hedge CMOS's structural treatment has no
occasion to make.

### Unit 24 — *Will be doing* and *will have done*

Future continuous (mid-action at a future time) and a second, pragmatically distinct
"already-routine/arrangement" sense used in polite plan-inquiries (see Vocabulary); future perfect
(already complete by a future time). Worked example throughout: a football match's start/end times
anchor all four forms in one running scenario.

### Unit 25 — *When I do*/*When I've done*; *When* and *if*

The present-tense-in-future-subordinate-clause rule (see Vocabulary) — covers *when, while, before,
after, as soon as, until/till* uniformly, with either present simple or present perfect depending on
whether the two events are simultaneous (simple) or sequential (perfect: *when I've phoned Kate, we
can have dinner* — phone call completes first) — and the *when* (certain) vs. *if* (possible)
semantic contrast for future subordinators.

## Grammar points

### Error 25: Subjects With Complex Modifiers (p. 106)

*Don't say* "the functions...was about to be explained"; *say instead* "...were about to be
explained." The true subject is "functions" (plural); the intervening modifying phrase ("of Ralph's
expensive new computer") sits right next to the verb and is easily mistaken for the subject, but it
cannot determine verb agreement.

### Compound subjects: "and" vs. "or"/"nor" (pp. 107, 109–110)

Subjects joined by "and" are (with one exception) always plural, since they jointly refer to more
than one person/thing — exception: when the joined parts name a single unit or the same referent
(*"cereal and milk is a typical breakfast"*). Subjects joined by "or"/"nor," by contrast, are treated
separately rather than as a group; the verb's person/number agrees with whichever part of the subject
sits closer to the verb (*"either Diane or her parents are"* vs. *"either the daughters or their
father is"*).

### Errors 26–28: compound-subject agreement (pp. 108–110)

*Don't say* "One and one is two"; *say instead* "...are two" (and-joined compound subject is plural).
*Don't say* "Either Tom or Henry have lost the book"; *say instead* "...has lost..." (or-joined
subject, both parts singular, nearer part governs). *Don't say* "Her parents or society are to blame";
*say instead* "...is to blame" (or-joined subject, nearer part "society" is singular).

### Indefinite pronouns and agreement (p. 113)

A fixed list of indefinite pronouns is always singular — *one, no one, anyone, everyone, someone,
anybody, somebody, nobody, everybody, each, either, neither* — because their focus is on "each one"
individual member, not the collective group.

### Error 29: Each...Are (p. 113)

*Don't say* "Each of the girls are going to be tested"; *say instead* "...is going to be tested" —
"each" is always singular regardless of the plural noun phrase that follows it.

### Error 30: None...Is/Are (pp. 114–115)

*Don't say* "none are available" (for a single jukebox); *say instead* "none is available." Unlike the
fixed-singular list above, "none" (along with "some," "any," "all") varies in number by meaning:
singular when referring to a whole/mass taken as a unit (*"none of the cement is left"*), plural when
referring to a countable number of discrete things (*"none of the cakes are ready"*). The book
explicitly flags this rule as genuinely contested among grammarians and historically shifting — "none"
was uniformly singular in older English and has drifted toward plural use except with mass nouns —
offering explicit guidance to default to plural unless the singular meaning feels clearly intended.

### Error 31: Everyone...Their (pp. 118–120)

*Don't say* "Everyone collects their paycheck"; the source's prescribed fix is "his or her paycheck"
(gender-neutral but "cumbersome") or recasting the sentence entirely (*"Everyone collects a
paycheck"*; *"The employees collect their paychecks"*). The book explicitly concedes that singular
"their" with an indefinite-pronoun antecedent is extremely widespread in actual usage and may represent
an active, ongoing change in the language — drawing an explicit historical parallel to the loss of the
*thou/thy* vs. *you/your* singular/plural second-person distinction as a precedent for how English can
shed a number contrast over time. Despite this acknowledgment, the source still treats "everyone...
their" as incorrect for formal speech and writing as of its 2004 publication.

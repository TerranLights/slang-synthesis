## Grammar points

### 10.1 'Be' as a full verb (1)

**10.1A Uses of the imperative of 'be'.** *Be* functions as a helping/auxiliary verb for the
progressive and passive, but is a full verb when combined with a noun or adjective. As an imperative,
*be* + noun means "act like" or "become/pretend to be"; *Don't be* + adjective is the far more common
negative-imperative pattern than the affirmative. Crucially, *be* + adjective imperatives only work
with adjectives describing "passing behaviour" (controllable, temporary conduct), never with
adjectives describing inherent "states," p.142.

**10.1B The use of 'aren't'.** The full form "Am I not" is rare in practice; *aren't I* is the
conventional substitute, but strictly confined to negative questions, negative wh-questions, and
negative question tags — never used in an ordinary negative statement, p.142.

**10.1C 'Be' in the simple present and simple past.** *Be* functions as a full verb with nouns and
adjectives in these tenses; the source explicitly flags this as a point where English structurally
requires *be* in constructions other languages might express differently ("I am hungry," not "*I
have hunger*"; "It's cold," not "*It makes cold*"), p.143.

### 10.2 'Be' as a full verb (2)

**10.2A Progressive forms of 'be' for temporary behaviour.** The progressive (*is being / was being*)
combines with "passing behaviour" adjectives (not state adjectives), often implying the behaviour is
deliberate; a small number of nouns can also combine with progressive *be* this way (he's being a
fool), p.144.

**10.2B 'Has been', 'have been', 'had been' + adjectives and nouns.** Follow the ordinary present/
past perfect rule: actions/states that began in the past and continue into the present use *have/has
been*; those referring to an earlier, already-closed past use *had been*; *was/were* is used instead
whenever an exact time reference is given, p.144.

**10.2C 'Have been' and 'have gone'.** Two genuinely distinct meanings despite superficial similarity
— see the Vocabulary table for the visited-and-returned vs. still-away distinction, and their
respective preposition/adverb combination patterns, p.144.

**10.2D 'Have been' with 'since' and 'for'.** *How long...?* questions pair naturally with *have
been*, answered with *since* (a point) or *for* (a duration); *have been* covers "have lived/been
living," "have worked/been working," "have waited/been waiting" — essentially standing in for the
continuity-verb present perfect progressive pattern from Chapter 9, p.145.

### 10.3 'There' + 'be'

**10.3A 'There' + 'be' as the natural choice.** English strongly prefers *there is/are* over *it
has/it is* for expressing the existence of people or things — it is simply the more idiomatic
construction. The *there* + *be* pattern extends across tenses (*there'll be, there's been, there had
been*) for announcing/reporting events and for setting a scene in narrative, p.146.

**10.3B 'There is' vs. 'it is'.** The two-step discourse pattern: *there* first introduces a
referent's existence; a personal pronoun (he/she/it/they) then supplies further detail about that
same referent in a following sentence, p.146.

**10.3C Combinations with 'there' + 'be'.** *There is/are/was/were* are by far the most frequent
forms; *there* also combines productively with perfect tenses, future tenses, and with *seem to
be*/*appear to be*, p.147.

### 10.4 Verbs related in meaning to 'be'

**10.4A Certainty and uncertainty with 'be', 'seem', etc.** For certainty, use *be* or an ordinary
verb directly; for uncertainty, either a modal verb (*may/might/could be*) or one of a closed set of
be-related verbs (*appear, feel, look, seem, smell, sound, taste*, and also *chance/happen/prove to
be*) — the latter can themselves combine with progressive and perfect forms, p.148.

**10.4B 'To be' or not 'to be'.** *To be* can be dropped after *appear/seem* in the simple
present/past; it's usually kept before a small set of adjectives (*afraid, asleep, awake*); and it
cannot appear at all after *feel, look, smell, sound, taste* — these take a bare adjective directly,
p.148.

**10.4C 'Process verbs' related to 'be' and 'become'.** A set of verbs describes a change of state
(see the Vocabulary table); *get, become,* and *grow* are the most common; several further pairings
are fixed idiomatic collocations rather than freely productive; nouns are uncommon after process
verbs except specifically after *become* and *make*, p.149.

### 10.5 'Have' as a full verb = 'possess'; 'have got' = 'possess'

**10.5A 'Have got' = 'own' and 'have got' = 'obtain'.** British English uses both *have* and *have
got* to mean "possess." *Have (just) got* (American English: *have gotten*) is also the normal
present-perfect form of the verb *get*, meaning "have obtained/received" — the identical surface
form *have got* is ambiguous between the two senses, disambiguated by context (a recency adverb like
*just*, or an ongoing-possession reading), p.150.

**10.5B Uses of 'have' and 'have got' to mean 'possess'.** *Have got* commonly substitutes for *have*
in present-tense possession statements; *Do you have?*/*I don't have* are also common, especially in
American English. In tenses other than the simple present, only ordinary *have* forms (not *have
got*) express possession — *have got* in another tense instead reverts to its "obtained" sense,
p.150.

### 10.6 'Have' as a full verb meaning 'eat', 'enjoy', etc.

**10.6A 'Have' (= 'eat', 'enjoy', etc.) compared with 'have' (= 'possess').** In this activity sense,
*have* behaves like any ordinary dynamic verb, including taking progressive forms ("I'm having a
drink") — unlike the possession sense, which never takes a progressive. The simple present/past of
this sense of *have* forms questions/negatives with *do/does/did*, exactly like a normal verb (unlike
the possession sense, which can invert directly or use *have got*). *Have* can function as both
auxiliary and full verb in the same sentence simultaneously (I *have had* my lunch = auxiliary have +
full-verb "eaten"), p.152.

**10.6B Common 'have' + noun combinations.** *Have* combines productively with a wide range of
activity nouns (have lunch, have a good trip) and is especially common in the imperative (Have a
sandwich!). For many common activities, English prefers *have* + noun over the corresponding single
verb (I *had a swim*, rather than "I swam"), p.153.

### 10.7 'Do' as a full verb

**10.7A Forms and uses of 'do' as an auxiliary and as a full verb.** *Do* forms questions/negatives
in the simple present/past as an auxiliary, and separately functions as an ordinary full verb meaning
"perform an activity/task" — both uses can combine in the same sentence (What *did* you *do*?). *Do*
also substitutes for a previously-mentioned verb to avoid repetition; has an idiomatic sense meaning
"be in the wrong place" (what are these clothes *doing* on the floor?); and combines with -ing for
naming a completed task (I've just *done* the ironing), p.154.

**10.7B 'Do' and 'make' compared.** *Do* generally means "be engaged in an activity"; *make* means
"create." Both combine with specific nouns in fixed collocations that must be learned individually —
see the Vocabulary table; a few nouns (e.g. *the beds*) genuinely accept either verb, p.155.

## Grammar points

### New spelling conventions after the Conquest, decoupled from pronunciation change

The book's opening methodological caution for the whole chapter: the shift from Old to Middle
English *looks* more sudden than the underlying spoken language actually was, because Norman
scribes abandoned the conservative, backward-looking West Saxon spelling tradition and started
writing English phonetically using **Old French spelling conventions** — so many changes already
underway in pronunciation (some from Late Old English) only become visible in writing at this point,
and some spelling changes (quu→o before *m, n, u* letter-clusters, e.g. *sunu/cuman/lufu* → *sone/
comen/loue*) are purely graphic, with **no corresponding change in pronunciation at all** — a
caution directly reinforcing the Ch. 1 phonemic-vs-orthographic distinction (`pt01`). New ME
spelling introduced: *qu* for OE *cw* [kw]; *g* for the stop allophone, with OE's yogh-descended
symbol (ȝ) kept only for the fricative/glide allophones; *u*/*v* used for both [f] and [v] (OE had
used plain *f* for both, relying on allophony); *z* introduced alongside *s* (inconsistently); the
digraph *th* gradually replacing ð (common by c. 1300) and eventually þ (common by c. 1400, with þ
surviving only as a debased "y" in forms like "Ye Olde..." — a late medieval way of writing *þe*,
not a genuine letter *y*). Table 7.1 (p. 162) catalogs the OE-spelling-to-ME-spelling correspondence
systematically across roughly 15 phoneme classes. The book notes some of these changes were genuine
improvements (disambiguating what OE's allophonic *f*/*s*/*þ*-*ð* spelling had obscured, since by
ME these actually *were* separate phonemes — see the full-paradigm phoneme-splitting already traced
back to Old English's own allophony in `pt03`) while others (*qu*, *y* for the OE front-rounded
vowel) were arguably unnecessary.

### Middle English sound changes: diphthong mergers, new diphthongs, and dialectal lengthening

OE *ā* became ME long open *ō* ([ɔː]) south of the Humber (unchanged north of it, as already
flagged in `pt04`'s dialect discussion) — this merged with the reflex of another OE vowel, ME *ǭ*
([oː], "close o," as in Modern German *wo*), and the two were **not distinguished in ME spelling**
despite being genuinely separate phonemes, a distinction not consistently marked (*oa* vs. *oo*)
until Early Modern times, explaining Modern homograph-adjacent pairs like *goat* (open *ō*) vs.
*goose* (close *ō*). A parallel merger/non-distinction affected ME *ę̄* (long open *e*, [ɛː], from
OE *ǣ*/*ēa*) vs. ME *ē* (long close *e*, [eː], from OE *ǣ*/*ēo*) — these stayed phonemically
distinct through Shakespeare's time but have since fallen together in Modern Standard English
(explaining why *sea*, from OE *sǣ*, and *see*, from OE *sēon*, now rhyme, despite the book's claim
they were genuinely different vowels for centuries after the OE/ME transition). Other OE-to-ME
changes: loss of OE *æ* (merging with *a*); monophthongization of all OE diphthongs; development of
a new, structurally different set of ME diphthongs via the vocalization of /j/ or /w/ following a
vowel; weakening of unstressed-syllable vowels toward schwa.

### Two vowel-lengthening processes specific to the Old-to-Middle-English transition

The book isolates **two separate, differently-dated lengthening processes**, both producing ME long
vowels from OE short ones, neither inherited from Proto-Germanic/PIE ablaut or front-mutation
mechanisms covered earlier:

1. **Late-OE/early-ME lengthening before certain consonant clusters** (*-ld*, *-mb*, *-nd*),
   producing Modern *old, bold, cold, told* (from Anglian short *a*, itself from an earlier
   lengthening-then-regular-ō-shift chain already covered in `pt04`'s dialect section) and *field,
   child, comb, climb, blind, ground*. **Blocked if a third consonant immediately followed** the
   cluster — explaining the *child*/*children* alternation (no third consonant after *-ld* in
   *child*, so lengthening occurred; a third consonant, *-r-*, follows in *children*, so it didn't),
   and the book notes Modern English has in most such cases simply generalized one variant or the
   other across the whole paradigm by analogy (as with *lamb*, generalized from the short-vowel
   *plural* form OE *lambru*, not the lengthened singular). *Wind* "moving air" is cited as an
   analogy-blocked exception — it should have lengthened but didn't, attributed to the influence of
   compounds like *windmill* where a third consonant prevented lengthening; the book notes Shakespeare
   still rhymed *wind* with *kind*, meaning the long-vowel pronunciation persisted in educated speech
   centuries after the spelling/standard pronunciation had already shortened it.
2. **Middle English open-syllable lengthening** (13th century): short *a*, *o*, *e* lengthened in
   the stressed, *open* syllable (one ending in a vowel) of a disyllabic word — e.g. OE *bacan* "to
   bake," syllabified *ba-can* (open first syllable) → ME *baken* with lengthened [aː] → Modern
   *bake* [beɪk]; contrasted with OE *þancian* "to thank," syllabified *than-ken* (closed first
   syllable, since OE spelling *nc* = two consonants /nk/), where no lengthening took place. This
   mechanism is distinct from the consonant-cluster lengthening above and specifically explains the
   *cradle*/*crane*/*vat*/*staff*-*stave* doublet set in the Vocabulary table, each illustrating a
   different resolution Modern English reached when a word's nominative/singular and inflected/
   plural forms disagreed on open- vs. closed-syllable status. The lingering orthographic legacy is
   today's "magic/silent -e" spelling convention (*home*, *stone* — the *-e* has no sound value of
   its own by Modern times but marks that the preceding vowel is long), alongside the complementary
   convention of doubling a following consonant to mark a *short* vowel (*backer* vs. *baker*,
   *copper* vs. *coper*).

### Middle English morphology: the "period of weakened inflections"

Middle English is explicitly characterized as a period of radical inflectional leveling, with two
compounding causes identified: (1) **Norse/English language mixing** (bilingual speakers facing
mutually-recognizable-but-differently-inflected cognate word pairs, leading to uncertainty about
which inflection to use, favoring other, non-inflectional grammatical devices — directly connecting
back to `pt04`'s Norse-contact material); (2) **phonological erosion of unstressed final syllables**,
already underway in Late Old English (see `pt03`) but now fully visible once conservative West Saxon
spelling stopped masking it in writing — OE word-final *-a, -u, -e* all merged to ME *-e*; OE
*-an/-on/-un/-um* all merged to ME *-en*, later further reduced to *-e*; OE *-as* and *-es* merged
to *-es*; OE *-aþ* and *-eþ* merged to *-eþ*; the surviving *-e* itself then disappeared entirely by
the mid-13th century in the north and by c. 1400 in the south. **This cascade of mergers made
previously-distinct OE case/number forms identical**, collapsing the inherited declension system —
worked example: OE *sunu* "son" would, by pure sound-change, collapse to ME *sone* in every case
except dative plural, and even that soon leveled to *sone* too by analogy.

**Noun declension leveling**: of OE's many declension classes (see `pt03`), two survived as the
competing patterns that absorbed virtually everything else — the *-as*/*-es* type (nominative
plural and genitive singular both *-es* after the mergers) and the *-an*/*-en* type (both *-en*).
Regional distribution split along the now-familiar Danelaw/non-Danelaw and north/south lines: *-es*
dominant in the (Scandinavian-contact) northern dialects from as early as 1200; *-en* dominant in
the south by the mid-period (*devlen* "devils," *englen* "angels" for OE *dēoflas*, *englas*); *-es*
then spread southward over the course of the ME period and was nearly universal by the 15th century
— **directly the ancestor of the modern default plural**, with the Vocabulary table above cataloging
the handful of *-en* holdouts that survived as late as Shakespeare before finally obsolescing
(*eyen, shoon, hosen, housen, peasen*), alongside the small number of genuinely irregular relics
that persist today: OE front-mutation plurals (*feet, geese, mice, men* — see `pt03`'s i-umlaut
discussion), zero-marked plurals descended from OE neuter nouns with no plural ending at all (*deer,
sheep*), and the handful of newer learned Latin/Greek plurals (*formulae, nuclei, phenomena*) that
sit outside the whole native system. **Case reduction**: early ME still distinguished all four OE
cases in both numbers, but by the period's end most nouns had collapsed to just **two** surface
forms (singular, plural — *boy, boys*), with a vestigial third form (the now-familiar possessive
*'s*, itself a fossil of the OE genitive singular *-es* ending) re-differentiated only in spelling,
not pronunciation, for most nouns (*boy's*/*boys*' are homophonous). Fossilized single-case relics
the book cites as still embedded in Modern idiom: *alive* (OE *on līfe*, dative singular, with the
dative's distinctive *-e* lost but its voiced [v] — not the nominative's voiceless [f] — retained);
*Lady Day*/*Lady Chapel* (an old genitive form, "the Lord's Day," without the modern *'s*); *whilom*
"formerly" (OE *hwīlum*, dative plural "at times").

**Adjective/demonstrative leveling** followed the same trajectory toward just two forms (a bare
base form and an *-e*-suffixed form used both for the plural and for the "weak" context — e.g. after
a determiner, continuing the strong/weak distinction already present in OE, see `pt03`), reaching
this two-form stage by Chaucer's time, and then collapsing to a single **indeclinable** adjective
form (today's system) once final unstressed *-e* was lost entirely by the period's end.

**The definite article collapses to a single invariant form, and *þæt* splits off as a genuinely
separate word.** OE's three-gender, five-case, two-number *se* system (see `pt03`) first simplified
in Late Old English to a form *þe* (ousting *se*/*sēo* under the leveling influence of the other,
þ-initial case-forms); through Middle English the remaining case/gender-marked alternative forms
progressively disappeared (Chaucer uses *the* almost exclusively, though still retains a residual
plural form *tho*, from OE *þā*); by the end of the period **the** is the sole surviving form of the
definite article in all contexts, and — significantly — **that** (originally just the
nominative/accusative singular neuter form of the very same paradigm, as already flagged in `pt03`)
is reanalyzed as an independent demonstrative word with its own distinct meaning, no longer felt as
"the neuter form of *the*." The book connects this directly to the rise of **natural gender**: once
neither the definite article nor the adjective could mark grammatical gender any more, English
shifted to referring to female creatures as *she*, male creatures as *he*, and inanimates as *it*
based on real-world sex/animacy rather than lexical gender class — though flags this shift as
already visibly underway even in *Late* Old English itself (a tendency for women to be referred to
as "she" and men as "he" regardless of a given noun's grammatical gender), not purely a Middle
English innovation.

### Middle English syntax: word order takes over case's old job, and auxiliaries proliferate

As the case system eroded, **word order became the primary device for marking subject vs. object**,
and S-V-O crystallized as the dominant, near-exclusive clause type during the ME period — the OE
S-O-V pattern in subordinate clauses largely disappeared early in the period, while V-S-O persisted
longer (common throughout ME, "not uncommon" as late as the 17th century, and V-S still occasionally
found today, e.g. "Down the road came a crowd" — already flagged in `pt01`). The book's worked
reconfirmation: in the 14th-century Wycliffe passage already analyzed in `pt01`, **every single
clause already shows S-V order**, demonstrating how far the shift had progressed by that point.
**Prepositions became markedly more frequent**, taking over work formerly done by case-endings
alone — OE *hungre ācwelan* "to die of hunger" (dative case alone, no preposition) vs. the
present-day gloss requiring *of*; similarly *dæges and nihtes* "by day and by night" and
*mildheortnysse Drihtnes full is eorþe* "the earth is filled with the mercy of God," neither of
which has any preposition in the original OE at all.

### The rise of the Modern English analytic verb system: perfect, passive, continuous

The book traces all three of Modern English's major periphrastic verb constructions through their
Middle English consolidation, building directly on their OE-period origins already covered in
`pt03`: the **perfect** (with *habban*/*have* or *bēon*/*be* + past participle) and **passive**
(with *bēon*/*weorþan* + past participle) both already existed in OE but become markedly more
frequent in ME; within the perfect, **have spread at the expense of be**, though *be*-perfects with
verbs of motion/change-of-state persisted as late as Early Modern English (Shakespeare's "Worcester
**is** stolne away," "the Newes **is** turn'd white" — directly matching the 1611 KJB "thy brother
**is** come" pattern already flagged in `pt01`); in the passive, *be* fully displaced *weorþan*
("worthe"/"werþe" in ME) by the end of the period. **Continuous tenses** ("he is coming") are a
genuine ME-period innovation, not simply more frequent OE usage, and the book carefully distinguishes
a superficially similar OE construction (*bēon* + present participle, as in an *Anglo-Saxon
Chronicle* passage glossed "and then they went on fighting around the gates, until...") as probably
**not** the true ancestor — the more likely source is instead a ME reanalysis of sentences like "he
was *on reading*" (preposition *on* + a genuine deverbal **noun**, OE *rǣdung* "the act of reading,"
with the nominal *-ung* suffix that regularly becomes ME/Modern *-ing*) → "he was *areading*"
(*on*-prefix reduced to unstressed *a-*) → "he was *reading*" (prefix lost entirely, and the
originally-nominal *-ing* form reanalyzed as a verb form). The book's closing point: although the
perfect, passive and continuous were all individually well-established in form by the end of the ME
period, **they were still used significantly less often than today, and crucially could not yet all
be freely combined with one another** — full combinatorial freedom (e.g. the present perfect
continuous passive) wasn't achieved until the 18th century.

### Three Middle English specimen texts, compared

The chapter's three glossed specimens let the book draw direct period/register/dialect contrasts:

1. **The *Peterborough Chronicle* (1137 annal, early ME prose)** — chosen specifically as very
   early and linguistically conservative ME. Grammar points the book draws out: still-productive OE
   spelling habits (æ, sc for [ʃ], wynn-as-⟨w⟩, u for [v], both þ and ð) alongside genuinely new
   French-influenced spellings (*qu* for *cweartern* "dungeon"); OE long *ā* still spelled *a*, not
   yet *o*, consistent with the sound change not having reached the East Midlands area the text was
   written in by this date; retention of OE third-person-plural pronoun forms *hi, heom, her(e)*
   (not yet the Scandinavian *they/them/their* forms covered in `pt04` — i.e. direct textual
   evidence the pronoun borrowing hadn't yet reached this text/area/date); a verb, *slēpan* "to
   sleep," shown transitioning from strong to weak within Chaucer's own later usage (he uses both
   *he slepte* and *he sleep*); predominantly V-S-O word order with only one clear S-O-V survival in
   a subordinate clause; heavily reduced adjective inflection (down to a bare form plus one *-e*
   plural, vs. OE's full declension); a near-invariant definite article *þe*/*the*; very sparse
   French vocabulary (only *castles*, *prisun* in the whole passage — direct confirmation the main
   wave of French borrowing, covered in `pt04`, postdates this 1137 text) but comparatively more
   Scandinavian vocabulary than Chaucer's later passage, consistent with the text's East Midlands/
   former-Danelaw origin, though the book notes only one of its Norse loans (*bathe* "both")
   ultimately survived into the modern standard literary language.
2. **Chaucer's *Nun's Priest's Tale* (late 14th c. verse, courtly-register)** — the book's contrast
   case: dramatically more French vocabulary, especially moral/character-description vocabulary
   fitting the courtly dialogue register (*avauntour, coward, hardy, secree*), fewer but
   well-established (already Late-OE-attested) Scandinavian loans, no S-O-V word order at all, a
   near-complete absence of auxiliary *do*, and (grammar point in its own right) a **missing
   relative pronoun** in "a beest was lyk an hound" — an omission impossible in Modern English for a
   *subject* relative clause, flagged by the book as a genuine ME syntactic option lost by Modern
   times. Chaucer's verse also preserves **word-final unstressed *-e*** as a scannable (but probably
   already not consistently spoken) syllable for metrical purposes, with its distribution
   complicated by *h*-dropping in French loanwords that had already lost their aspirate (*harlot,
   hazard, heritage, host, humble* — pronounced vowel-initial, so a preceding *-e* elides) and by
   genuine ME word-initial /h-/ loss creating true doublets (*hit*/*it*, *hire*/*ire*-type strong/
   weak alternations) — and some spelled final *-e*s (*hire* "her," *hise* "his," *oure* "our," *youre*
   "your") were **purely orthographic even by Chaucer's own time**, never actually pronounced.
   Chaucer's text also preserves the still-live singular/plural courtesy distinction in the second
   person (familiar *thou/thee* vs. polite plural-as-singular *ye/you* — a courtly pair of birds in
   the passage address each other with the polite form, not the familiar one) and documents a
   northern-dialect-influenced pronunciation (*lyk* with final [k], reinforced by Scandinavian
   contact, already reaching educated London written usage by this date — directly reinforcing
   `pt04`'s point about northern/Danelaw forms spreading south).
3. **Henryson's *Morall Fabillis* (late 15th c. Middle Scots verse)** — presented in its own
   dedicated subsection as a parallel, independently-standardizing ME-period written tradition,
   **not simply a regional dialect of the same evolving standard**. Key points: the earliest
   substantial Scots literary records date to the second half of the 14th century (John Barbour's
   *The Bruce*, c. 1375), with the tradition culminating in Henryson and William Dunbar; distinctive
   Scots spelling conventions include *i*-insertion to mark vowel length (*ai/ei/oi* digraphs, e.g.
   *haim* "home," *rois* "rose"), *ch* corresponding to southern *gh* (*nicht* "night"), and *quh-*
   corresponding to southern *wh-* (*quhen* "when"); phonologically, Scots retains OE long *ā*
   unrounded (*haim, bain, sair* vs. southern *home, bone, sore* — directly paralleling the
   north/south *ā* split already flagged in the dialect discussion above and in `pt04`), retains the
   OE front-rounded vowel /y(ː)/ longer than southern English (spelt *u*/*ui*, as in *fud*/*fuid*
   "food"), and — because word-final /-n/ had already been lost very early in the north — shows
   **short vowels retained in many words that underwent open-syllable lengthening in the south**
   (OE *macan* "to make" had already lost its final *-n* in Late Old English in the north, giving
   Scots *mak*/*mek* with a short vowel, since the lengthening process, which depends on syllable
   structure, hadn't yet applied by the time the triggering environment changed — vs. southern
   lengthened *make*). Distinctive Scots grammatical inflections: *-it* (past tense/participle of
   weak verbs, *closit* "closed"); *-and* (present participle, *dansand* "dancing" — note this is a
   different ME present-participle suffix from the southern/Chaucerian *-ing* covered above, i.e.
   evidence Scots's analytic-continuous-tense morphology developed along a partly separate track);
   *-is* (noun plural, *knychtis* "knights"; noun genitive, *the moderis breist* "the mother's
   breast"; 3rd-singular and plural present-tense verb, *he takis* "he takes," *makaris...playis*
   "poets... play"). Vocabulary-wise, the book flags a striking **paucity of Gaelic loanwords**
   despite Scots and Gaelic being spoken in the same country, alongside a comparatively larger
   contingent of (largely shared-with-the-south) French and Scandinavian loans, reflecting Scotland's
   own direct diplomatic/trade ties to France rather than Gaelic-speaking-population contact. The
   book's worked Henryson passage grammar notes: the auxiliary *couth* "could," used to form a
   periphrastic past tense functionally like a later *do*-past (*couth sing* = "sang"); *wes flowen*
   "had flown," showing *be*, not *have*, still forming the perfect of a motion verb in Scots too,
   paralleling the Early Modern English pattern already noted above; *fra* "from" (from Old Norse
   *frá*), contrasted with the southern English-descended cognate *fro*, which survives fossilized in
   "to and fro."

### Middle Scots as an independent standard, its convergence with southern English, and modern revival

The book treats Middle Scots as having genuinely achieved **standard-literary-language status in its
own right** by the Middle Scots period — not merely a regional ME dialect — a status it eventually
lost through convergence with the southern standard, for reasons the book lists concretely: the
general cultural prestige of southern English poets (Chaucer, Gower, Lydgate), and — more
specifically — the influence of English-language biblical translation, since the Reformation
produced a wave of English Bible translations that were never matched by an equivalent Scots Bible
(the 1560 Geneva Bible, with its influential Calvinist marginal notes, is singled out), and the 1603
Union of the Crowns (James VI of Scotland becoming James I of England), after which London became
the shared center of linguistic patronage for English and Scottish writers alike. By the end of the
17th century the Scots literary standard had **practically ceased to exist**: the book notes the
striking case of Hume, Adam Smith, and Robertson — all Scottish-born and Scottish-educated
18th-century thinkers — who nonetheless wrote exclusively in the southern literary standard, not
Scots. This did not mean Scots speech itself died out, only its status as a *written* standard; the
resulting written/spoken mismatch, combined with Scottish national feeling, motivated a deliberate
**Scots dialect literature movement** (founder: Allan Ramsay, 1686–1758; most famous figure: Robert
Burns, 1759–96) continuing to the present day, which the book is careful to distinguish from Middle
Scots's earlier status as a genuine all-purpose written standard (used for all written transactions,
not just literature) — contemporary Scots use is comparatively narrower (literature; some
chemistry-textbook/history-book "Scottishisms" layered onto an otherwise-southern written base), with
the contemporary Scottish Parliament's use of Scots (alongside Gaelic and standard English) cited as
a present-day institutional exception to that narrower default.

### The origin of *she*: a genuinely unresolved etymological puzzle (worked as a case study)

The book treats this as a real, still-open scholarly question rather than settling on one answer,
useful as a model of how the comparative/phonological methodology established in earlier chapters
gets applied to a genuinely contested case. *She* first appears as *scæ* in the *Peterborough
Chronicle* (1140 annal), spreading outward from the East Midlands to become the normal literary
form by the mid-14th century, while southern forms *heo*/*hue* and the northern form *sho* persisted
regionally for a further century or more. Two competing phonological derivations are weighed: (1)
from OE *hēo* "she" via a stress-shift-driven diphthong-to-rising-diphthong change, independently
supported by the same mechanism explaining *choose* (from OE *cēosan*, with a parallel [eo]→[joː]
shift) — with Old Norse contact in the Danelaw area cited as a plausible catalyst (comparing OE
*lēoð* "song" with its ON cognate *ljóð*); or (2) from the OE feminine definite article/demonstrative
*sēo* (the same demonstrative paradigm detailed in `pt03`) via a similar rising-diphthong shift —
a derivation the book treats as attractive because it would explain why a *demonstrative* form
migrated into the *personal pronoun* paradigm, but flags as weaker evidentially, resting partly on a
rare attested Mercian variant spelling *sie* that may itself just be a scribal error for the much
more common form *sio*. The book explicitly declines to adjudicate between these, noting "many
scholars prefer" the *hēo*-derivation specifically because it avoids positing the cross-paradigm
demonstrative-to-pronoun shift the *sēo*-derivation would require, while conceding the ultimate
origin of the *she*-forms' vowel remains uncertain either way — what both derivations agree on is
only that an initial [ʃ] (from assimilation of [sj]/[hj] clusters, a sound change independently
attested in some Old-Norse-influenced English place-names, most famously *Shetland* < Old Norse
*Hjaltland*) spread successfully specifically because it gave a clearer phonetic distinction between
"he" and "she" than any of the competing OE-descended forms could.

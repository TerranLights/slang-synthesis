## Grammar points

### Four descriptive dimensions of simple-clause structure (p.89–90)

Describing simple clauses means simultaneously describing: (a) the semantic type of the verb, (b)
the syntactic clause-type, (c) the semantic roles of participants, and (d) the grammatical roles and
their marking (word-order, morphology, other constraints). Because the verb's semantic type
determines the clause's syntactic type, describing verb semantics largely *is* describing simple-
clause syntax.

### States, events, and actions (p.90)

Three-way aspectual-ontological split: a *state* involves no change over time (temporary or
permanent); an *event* involves change of state, either bounded/fast (distinct initial and terminal
state) or unbounded/slow (ongoing, no firm boundary); an *action* is an event deliberately initiated
by an agent.

### Seven major semantic roles, each only a prototype (p.90–92)

Agent, patient, dative, instrument, benefactive, locative, and associative are defined as prototypes,
not rigid necessary/sufficient categories — each in principle has indefinitely fine sub-types, and the
major/minor distinction is pragmatic (a "major" type is one with more extensive grammatical
consequences), not principled.

### Four grammatical roles and the hierarchy of access to subjecthood (p.92–94)

Subject, direct object, indirect object, and nominal predicate are defined structurally by word-order,
morphology, and grammatical constraints, plus the discourse-pragmatic factor of topicality. Semantic
roles do not distribute freely across grammatical roles: an agent can *only* be subject; a dative or
patient can be subject, direct object, or indirect object; benefactive/instrumental/associative/locative
are found mostly as indirect objects. Competition for subjecthood in a *simple* (non-passive) clause
follows a strict hierarchy — **AGENT > DATIVE > PATIENT > OTHERS** — whichever participant sits
highest in the hierarchy wins the subject slot (e.g. a dative beats a patient for subjecthood in an
active clause; only passivization, which Givón treats as a *complex*-clause operation by definition,
lets a patient displace a dative or agent).

### Subject, direct object, indirect object, nominal predicate: structural + discourse-pragmatic profiles (p.94–96)

Subject: pre-verbal, unmarked, (limited) verb agreement, primary discourse topic. Direct object:
post-verbal, unmarked, no agreement, secondary topic. Indirect object: post-verbal (and post-direct-
object), preposition-marked (the preposition typically encodes the participant's semantic role),
non-topical. Nominal predicate: follows a copula, unmarked, non-topical. English is confirmed
S-V-O (and S-V-O-IO when both objects are present); a post-copula nominal predicate counts as the
"object" position for word-order purposes.

### Tree-diagram parsing recapitulated with obligatory vs. optional constituents (p.96–99)

A clause's hierarchic constituent structure divides into obligatory constituents (indispensable to the
verb's propositional frame) and optional ones (added for semantic/grammatical/pragmatic reasons
but not required to define the basic frame). Noun modifiers belong to the NP; auxiliaries, manner
adverbs, direct and indirect objects belong to the VP.

### Transitivity: independent semantic and syntactic definitions with large but imperfect overlap (p.99–100)

Semantically, the prototype transitive clause has three features: agentivity (deliberately-acting
subject), affectedness (concrete, visibly-affected object), and perfectivity (bounded, fast, real-time
event) — each scalar, so semantic transitivity is a matter of degree. Syntactically, transitive is simply
defined as "has a direct object." The two definitions' extensions overlap heavily in English text but
not completely — Givón flags English as unusually tolerant of syntactically-transitive-but-
semantically-non-prototypical verbs (i.e., transitivity in English skews more syntactic than semantic
compared to most languages).

### Dummy-subject verbs (p.100–101)

Weather/natural-condition verbs (*rain, freeze, hail*) and their adjectival counterparts (*it's hot/
cold/nice*) take the pronoun *it* as a semantically-empty, purely syntax-filling subject — distinct
from referential *it*, though it still behaves as singular/non-human for agreement purposes.

### Five copulas, each with a distinct predicate-type and subject-role signature (p.101–105)

*Be*: semantically near-empty; subject is patient or dative (never agent); predicate can be adjectival
(inherent-quality or temporary-state) or nominal (referring or non-referring). *Get*: codes a change
of state; predicate restricted to adjectival temporary-state only (rejects nominal predicates of either
kind, and rejects inherent-quality adjectives — *\*She got Irish*); subject patient or dative. *Become*:
codes change of state but otherwise shares *be*'s full predicate range (adjectival inherent/temporary
or nominal referring/non-referring). *Seem/appear*: like *be* for adjectival predicates, but reject
simple nominal predicates outright — a nominal predicate becomes possible only via an embedded
*to be* complement (*"She seems to be a woman of substance"*), where the nominal is structurally the
complement copula's predicate, not seem/appear's own. *Turn*: subject typically patient; predicate
restricted to adjectives coding perceptually-accessible *external physical* states (rejects mental-state
and inherent-quality adjectives: *\*she turned angry, \*she turned tall*). *Turn into*: the mirror-image
restriction — predicate restricted to (typically non-referring) nominals, rejecting adjectives entirely.
This five-way copula system is a clean illustration of how a handful of near-synonymous verbs
partition a single functional space by fine syntactic/semantic restriction.

### Simple intransitive verbs: four subject-role subtypes (p.105–106)

Agent-subject action verbs (*work, sing, dance*), dative-subject mental-state verbs (*meditate, suffer,
dream*), patient-of-state subject verbs (*sleep, sit*), and patient-of-change subject (process) verbs
(*fall, grow, die*) — one syntactic frame (intransitive) spanning four distinct semantic-role profiles.

### The transitive prototype and its systematic metaphoric extensions (p.106–125)

The prototype transitive clause has a volitional agent subject, a concrete affected-patient object,
and a bounded fast-changing verb (illustrated across six physical-effect sub-patterns: creation,
destruction, state-change, location-change, surface-change, internal-property-change, plus a
manner/instrument-incorporating sub-pattern like *murder = kill deliberately*, *knife = stab with a
knife*). Givón catalogs nine systematic ways English extends the transitive syntactic frame to
semantically less-prototypical configurations, each describable as a *metaphoric extension* of either
the agent or patient prototype, and each showing the hallmark pattern that downgrading one leg of
the prototype (subject, object, or verb-event) tends to co-occur with downgrading another: (1)
**dative subjects** (*see, feel, hear, know, want*) make a merely-conscious participant appear more
agent-like/responsible, especially in imperative-like frames (*"You better know the answer!"*); (2)
**dative objects** (*insult, spoil, amuse, entertain*) make a mentally-affected participant appear more
patient-like; (3) **patient-subject as cause**, extending (2)'s human-causer subject to a non-human
cause (*"The idea amused him"*, *"Curiosity killed the cat"*); (4) **instrument as patient-subject**,
promoting the tool to subject and thereby erasing/backgrounding the responsible agent (*"The
hammer smashed the window"*); (5) **locative direct-objects**, where a normally-indirect locative is
promoted to direct object and thereby construed as more affected — Givón's sharpest minimal pairs:
*ride the horse* (controls/dominates it) vs. *ride on the horse* (mere location); *breach/penetrate* (violate
against resistance) vs. *move through/into* (neutral); (6) **cognate objects**, where an abstract
activity/product is objectivized as if a created patient (*sing a song, make an error, take a risk*) —
recurring with semantically near-vacuous verbs (*give, take, make, have*) where the object itself
carries the verb's real semantic content; (7) **incorporated patients**, where the overt object is a
location and an implied patient is folded into the verb's meaning (*feed the cows* = give food to the
cows; *dust the table* = remove dust from the table — note the directional ambiguity, some
"add-to" and some "remove-from"); (8) **associative direct objects**, downgrading a reciprocal
co-agent to patient-like status while upgrading the subject to sole-responsible agent (*"He met
Sylvia"* vs. *"He met with her, and she with him"*); (9) **verbs of possession** (*have, have got, got*),
syntactically transitive but coding a state with an abstract, typically-unaffected object.

### Transitivity and unspecified objects: the English antipassive (p.115–116)

Semantically-transitive verbs can drop a predictable/stereotypical/unimportant object and surface
as syntactically intransitive (*"They ate (early)"* = ate food; *"She drives (too fast)"* = drives her car) —
Givón classes this as one variety of the cross-linguistic antipassive construction.

### Intransitive verbs with an indirect object: locative prototype plus two metaphoric sub-types (p.116–119)

The prototype has an agent-or-patient subject and a locative indirect object, coding motion toward/
away-from or static location relative to the reference point (*"John remained in the house"*). Two
metaphoric extensions: (a) mental-activity verbs (*look at, listen to, think about, be angry at*)
construe non-physical "motion" of voice/vision/attention/feeling toward (or, with *from*, away from)
the indirect object — demonstrated by the stative/active minimal pairs *see* (stative, dative subject,
motion construed object→subject) vs. *look at* (active, agent subject, motion construed
subject→object), and parallel *hear* vs. *listen to*; (b) reciprocal verbs with an associative indirect
object (*fight with, meet with*), which commonly alternate with a transitive pattern (co-agent as direct
object, downgraded toward patienthood) and a coordinate-subject pattern (co-agent promoted to
equal co-subject, more topical) — the three-way choice (*fought with her* / *fought her* / *fought*)
encodes a real difference in how equal/agentive the co-participant is construed to be, and some
verbs (*embrace, kiss*) only permit a subset of the three patterns.

### Bi-transitive verbs: one locative prototype, three metaphoric sub-types (p.120–127)

Prototype: agent subject causes patient (direct object) motion relative to a locative indirect object
(*"John put the book on the table"*). Sub-type 1, **dative-benefactive indirect object** (*give, tell, show,
promise*), metaphorically "moves" an abstract patient to/from a human "location," and most of these
verbs allow **dative shifting** — promoting the dative/benefactive to direct object while demoting the
patient to a prepositionless post-object slot (*"gave the book to him"* ↔ *"gave him the book"*).
Sub-type 2, the **instrumental-locative alternation**, pairs verbs that differ only in which semantically-
affected object is promoted to direct object (*fill x with y* vs. *pour y into x*); Givón's *spray* minimal
pair shows this is a genuine semantic (not just stylistic) choice — *"sprayed the paint on the wall"*
implies the paint ran out; *"sprayed the wall with paint"* implies the whole wall got covered — and a
preceding discourse context that topicalizes one participant makes the corresponding direct-object
choice the more natural one. Sub-type 3, **three-object exchange verbs** (*buy, trade, sell*), implicitly
involve up to three participants (patient transferred, dative-benefactive recipient, exchange
commodity returned to the agent), though text most commonly realizes only one indirect object at a
time. A separate general pattern, **optional indirect objects** (benefactive/instrument/associative/
manner/location/time), can attach to almost any transitive or intransitive verb without changing its
basic class.

### Verbs with two direct objects are really predicate-nominal/adjective complements in disguise (p.125–127)

*"They elected him president"* / *"We consider him useless"* look like double-object transitives but
paraphrase as *"elected him to be president" / "consider him to be useless"* — the "second object" is
semantically a predicate of an understood copula, not a true second patient, revealing these as
complex (complement-taking) rather than simple clauses.

### Verbal complements: clausal-subject constructions and the dummy-*it* alternation (p.127–129)

Transitive verbs and adjectives that take a propositional subject can realize it either as a full clause in
subject position or, with dummy *it* filling the subject slot, as an extraposed post-verbal clause
(*"That she did it shocked him"* ↔ *"It shocked him that she did it"*). With adjectival main predicates,
the adjective typically evaluates the embedded proposition along one of three dimensions:
epistemic (*true*), desirability (*terrible*), or difficulty (*difficult*).

### Three complement-taking verb classes defined by a shared semantic+syntactic template (p.129–137)

**Modality verbs** (*want, try, manage, start, stop, keep...*): semantically, the complement codes a
proposition whose subject is co-referential with the main clause's subject, and the main verb codes
inception/termination/persistence/success/failure/attempt/intent/obligation/ability regarding it;
syntactically, the co-referent complement subject is unexpressed and the complement verb is
infinitival/non-finite. Sub-divided into intentional (*want, try, can, intend, decide, refuse, be unable*),
success/failure (*manage, remember* vs. *forget, fail, avoid*), and aspectual (*start, finish, begin,
continue, stop, keep*) sub-groups. **Manipulative verbs** (*tell, force, let, ask, forbid...*): a human
agent (main-clause subject) manipulates a manipulee (main-clause object) who is also the
unexpressed subject of the infinitival complement event. Sub-divided by success: successful
manipulation (*force, cause, let, help, trick* — main-true entails complement-true), successful
prevention (*stop, dissuade* — main-true entails complement-false), attempted manipulation (*ask, tell,
beg, allow, order*, no success entailed), attempted dissuasion (*forbid*, no success entailed).
**Perception-cognition-utterance (PCU) verbs** (*see, know, say, think, believe...*): no co-reference
restriction between clauses; the complement surfaces as a full finite clause, optionally introduced by
*that* (or, for low-certainty epistemics, *if/whether*). Sub-divided by: preference/aversion (*wish, hope*
vs. *be afraid*), epistemic certainty (*think, believe, suppose*), sensory perception (*see, hear* in the
perceptual sense), **factive** verbs (*know, remember, regret, see* — complement presupposed true
regardless of main-clause polarity: *"Mary (didn't) know that John loved her"* both entail *John loved
her*), rare **negative-factive** verbs (*pretend, lie* — complement presupposed *false* regardless of
main-clause polarity), and **utterance** verbs (*say, announce, ask*), which further split into direct
quotation (verbatim-responsible, content-disclaiming) vs. indirect quotation (content-responsible,
form-unfaithful) — a genuine trade-off in what kind of fidelity the speaker is claiming.
**Information verbs** (*tell, inform, convince, persuade*) combine a dative direct object with a PCU-type
complement in one clause.

### Multiple class membership is a property of word-senses, not word-forms (p.137–138)

Many English verbs (*tell, know, say, forget, remember, want*) belong to more than one of the
syntactic/semantic verb classes above depending on which sense is active — demonstrated with
*forget*/*remember* taking four distinct complement patterns (transitive, intransitive+IO, modality,
cognition) and, more subtly, taking two different infinitival complements with the *same* surface
modality-verb syntax but opposite entailments: *"forgot to solve the problem"* (could have, didn't) vs.
*"forgot how to solve the problem"* (couldn't, and didn't) — proof that grammatical classification
attaches to a sense, not a phonological word-form.

### Verbs that incorporate prepositions: grammaticalized "phrasal verbs" as a historical, largely unpredictable lexical layer (p.138–142)

English prepositions occupy a stranded middle ground between grammar and lexicon: beyond their
ordinary indirect-object-marking ("grammatical") use, they systematically combine with verbs to
create new, often only loosely-compositional senses (*break* → *break up/down/out*; *turn* → *turn up/
in/out*; *work* → *work out*, both literally and idiomatically; *give* → *give up/in/out*). Demonstrated with
simple intransitives, simple transitives, indirect-object-taking verbs, and bi-transitives alike. Givón
treats this as an ongoing historical process (parallel to a similar earlier process in Latin, visible today
in pre-verbal-preposition Latinate English verbs like *im-bibe, sub-ject, re-ceive*), with semantic
transparency ranging from fully compositional to fully opaque depending on how far along the
grammaticalization each combination has progressed — which is why non-native learners find this
area notoriously hard, and why native speakers are thought to memorize many verb+preposition
combinations as unitary lexical items rather than deriving them by rule. A further syntactic wrinkle:
when the direct object is a long NP, the incorporated preposition tends to sit right after the verb
(*"locked out the entire class"*); when the object is short/topical (often just a pronoun), the
preposition tends to shift to clause-final position (*"locked him out"*), i.e. one factor is syntactic
(object length) and the other pragmatic (object topicality).

### Summary phrase-structure notation for simple clauses (p.142–144)

Givón offers a compact phrase-structure-rule-style summary (S = SUBJ VP; VP = COPULAR or
VERBAL; COPULAR = COP PRED; VERBAL = V (OBJ) (IO); IO = PP = P NP) explicitly framed as
notational shorthand for the tree-diagram method rather than a claim about generative "competence"
in the Chomskyan sense — a methodological distinction Givón flags rather than glosses over.

### Verbal inflection spans all three functional realms and layers historical strata (p.147)

Tense-aspect-modality morphology is unusual in touching lexical meaning, propositional information,
*and* discourse coherence simultaneously, and mixes diachronically older bound morphology with
diachronically younger auxiliaries that carry both morphological and syntactic status — and the
system is explicitly described as still actively changing, not a frozen inventory.

### Tense: four categories anchored to speech-time (p.148)

Past (event-time precedes speech-time), future (follows), present (coincides), and habitual (always/
timeless/unspecified event-time) — habitual is explicitly flagged (in the Notes) as a "swing category,"
partly tense, partly aspect, partly modality, rather than cleanly belonging to just one domain.

### Past, future, and the absence of a dedicated present (p.148–152)

Past is mainly the suffix *-ed*, with a sizable closed class of ablaut/suppletive irregular verbs (*sing/
sang, go/went, have/had*). Future has three competing forms — modal *will*, complex *be going to/
gonna*, progressive *be...-ing* — differing along three independent dimensions: formality (*will* more
formal), time-distance (*will* more distant), and certainty (*will*/progressive higher certainty than *going
to/gonna*). English has **no dedicated present-tense marker**: the progressive *-ing* defaults to a
present reading in the absence of other marking, *except* with stative verbs (*know, see, be, have,
want*), which cannot normally take the progressive at all (because the progressive's function —
converting a compact event into an extended state — is vacuous applied to something that is
already a state) and instead use the habitual/unmarked form ambiguously for both habitual and
present. Crucially, the restriction tracks the *sense* in use, not the verb stem: *see* rejects the
progressive in its stative sense (*\*I am seeing her now*) but accepts it once a near-synonymous active
sense ('meet') is activated (*"He's seeing the delegation right now"*); likewise *be* (*\*was being tall* vs.
*was being obnoxious*, an activity sense) and *have* (*\*was having blond hair* vs. *was having steak* =
eating, *was having a baby* = giving birth, *was having a good time* = enjoying).

### Aspect: the progressive as a camera-angle metaphor (p.152–161)

The progressive (*be + -ing*) converts a compact bounded event into a temporally diffuse, unbounded,
state-resembling process — Givón's extended metaphor: the bounded/perfective view is a
narrow-angle shot that frames the whole event including its edges, while the progressive is a
wide-angle, close-proximity shot that omits the boundaries and accentuates the span. This generates
three entailed sub-properties: (i) **unboundedness** (vs. compactness) — with super-compact verbs
(*kick, blink, hit, shoot*) the progressive instead gets an iterative ("repeated acts") reading; (ii)
**proximity** (vs. remoteness) — explaining the progressive's near-automatic default association with
"now" via a spatial-proximity-to-temporal-proximity metaphor; (iii) **simultaneity** (vs. sequentiality)
— the progressive codes an event as the backdrop against which another (compact) event is
inserted (*"When I came in, he was kicking the couch"*), which is why narrative text (action-oriented,
built from sequential event chains) uses the bounded/perfective past far more than the progressive,
reserving the progressive mostly for dependent, simultaneity-marking clauses — a pattern that can
also surface between habitual-marked (sequential) and progressive-marked (non-sequential) clauses
within the same passage. A separate **habitual progressive** sense exists alongside the default
present-progressive sense, distinguishable by context/time-adverbs and representing a narrower,
less-prototypical habitual (less time needed to establish).

### A family of lexically-distinct progressive/aspectual auxiliaries beyond 'be' (p.158–161)

*Keep* patterns syntactically like *be* but adds a sense of prior-continuation and, with temporally-
compact main verbs, a repetitive rather than continuous reading (*"kept sucking her thumb"* =
continuous; *"kept breaking her leg"* = repetitive). *Continue* and *go on* are aspectual only in their
*-ing*-marked variant (contrasted with an infinitive-marked modality-verb variant with a different
sense — *continued dealing* [still dealing] vs. *continued to deal* [roughly synonymous but
syntactically a modality verb]; *went on hitting* [was already hitting] vs. *went on to hit* [was doing
something else first]). *Start/begin/resume* are inceptive-progressive only with *-ing* (vs. a
habitual/single-act infinitive-marked sense), sharpened with super-compact verbs (*"started kicking
the ball"* = repeated kicks; *"started to kick the ball"* = a single inception, possibly aborted).
*Stop/quit/finish* are terminative, with *stop* covering both progressive- and repetitive-termination and
*finish* skewing toward progressive-termination only (its would-be habitual sense is instead rendered
periphrastically with *be through (with)*).

### The habitual past: 'used to' vs. habitual 'would' (p.161)

Both render past-habitual/repetitive meaning, but *used to* (contracted colloquially to *usta*) implies
the habit has since terminated, while habitual *would* carries no such implication and is the more
colloquial of the two — *"She used to sing in the shower"* (no longer) vs. *"She would sing in the
shower"* (may still).

### The perfect: anteriority, perfectivity, counter-sequentiality, and relevance as four separable features of one form (p.161–167)

Though strongly associated with "past," the perfect is anchored to three distinct reference points
(speech-time, a past reference-point, a future reference-point — present/past/future perfect) and in
each case codes an event prior to that reference point (**anteriority**). It can additionally signal
**perfectivity** (termination/accomplishment relative to the reference point). Its most pragmatically
interesting property is **counter-sequentiality**: in a narrative otherwise told in simple-sequential
past, a perfect-marked clause signals that the event it describes is being recounted *out of* its actual
chronological position (narrated order A,C,B,D for actual order A,B,C,D) — and this off-sequence
placement itself triggers a thematic break in the surrounding discourse. Finally, **relevance**: the
perfect shifts an event's "relevance point" away from its own occurrence-time to the reference-time —
with events (vs. states) tending to show a temporal gap between completion and relevance-time,
while states tend to linger continuously up to the reference point (*"has read this book"* = finished a
while ago; *"has been a club member"* = still is) — an event-type reading can be coerced into the
state-type reading via the progressive (*"has been reading this book"* = still reading).

### The immediate aspect: a vividness/proximity register distinct from tense-aspect proper (p.166–169)

The bare habitual/unmarked verb form can substitute for the sequential past to render narration more
vivid/immediate, pragmatically inviting the hearer into close, emotionally-engaged observation of the
scene — correlated with, but not restricted to, oral register (it recurs in literary prose for stylistic
effect, per the Barthelme example) and co-occurring with a cluster of proximal deictic choices (*this*
over *that*, *here* over *there*, *now* over *then*), and, in less-educated colloquial narration, with the
simple past (rather than past-perfect) marking even deep off-sequence digressions.

### Four epistemic modalities and the realis/irrealis asymmetry (p.169–171)

Presupposition (assumed true by convention/context, unchallenged), realis assertion (strongly
asserted true, challengeable, evidence-backed), irrealis assertion (weakly asserted possible/likely/
desired, challenge invited), negative assertion (strongly asserted false, contradicting the hearer).
Realis functions as the grammatically **unmarked** default — it is freely distributed across contexts
except where explicitly excluded — while irrealis is the marked category requiring positive licensing
contexts. The habitual tense-aspect is flagged as an irrealis modality only in a qualified sense: it
shares realis's strength of assertion but lacks realis's core feature of asserting a *specific* occurrence
at a *specific* time.

### Predictable grammatical environments for irrealis and presupposition (p.171–178)

**Irrealis-triggering environments**: future and habitual tense-aspect (vs. past/perfect/present
defaulting realis); epistemic and evaluative adverbs (*maybe, probably, hopefully* override an
otherwise-realis past/present/perfect); modal auxiliaries (irrealis operators "par excellence," able to
carry an epistemic sense, a deontic/evaluative sense, or — commonly — both at once, e.g. *can/may*
each spanning ability, probability, *and* permission); non-implicative complement-taking verbs
(modality, manipulation, and non-factive PCU verbs all cast an irrealis scope over their complement
regardless of the main verb's own tense); and two non-declarative speech-act types — manipulatives
(future-projecting, plus the evaluative sub-modality of preference) and yes-no questions
(epistemically uncertain by nature). **Presupposition-triggering environments**: certain realis-tensed
adverbial clauses (*because, when, although, since, while*-clauses); fronted participial adverbial
clauses; relative clauses; WH-questions; cleft-focus constructions; complements of factive PCU verbs;
and nominalized clauses in subject/object position.

### Modal + 'have': a one-way epistemic/deontic conditional, and the collapse of the old present/past modal pairing (p.172–176, 182–185)

Evaluative (deontic) senses are argued to always entail an epistemic-uncertainty core, but not
vice-versa (*"if evaluative, then epistemic"*, never the reverse) — because intention/ability/preference/
obligation/permission are all inherently future-projecting, and future is irrealis by definition; this
explains why English modals so often multiplex an epistemic and a deontic sense on the same form
(*can/may*: ability, probability, *and* permission; *should/must*: obligation *and* probability). The
historical present/past modal pairs (*can/could, shall/should, will/would, may/might*) have been
re-analyzed: since modals cannot themselves take past-tense marking, combining a modal with
*have* has become the productive way to signal "pastness," but the semantic consequences are
irregular and must be checked pair-by-pair — *could have* retains ability and probability but **loses**
the permission sense that bare *could* has; *may have* retains **only** the probability sense, losing both
ability and permission (permission readings are tied to performing the speech act of granting
permission, which only works in live present-tense use); and for four modals (*might, could, would,
should*) the *have*-combination additionally triggers a counter-fact ("but didn't") reading, traceable to
their historical status as past-tense forms of *can, shall, will,* and *may* respectively. Only *can*/*could*
still partially preserve an original present/past contrast, which is also why *can* (unlike *could*) cannot
combine with *have* at all, and why *was able to* has increasingly taken over *could*'s original past-
ability function as *could* drifts toward purely non-past/hypothetical uses.

### The auxiliary ordering template and its ongoing historical revision (p.182–187)

The traditional rule AUX = (PAST)(MODAL)(HAVE)(BE) — rigid relative ordering, with "past" notated
as if it were an auxiliary slot even though it is really a suffix that attaches to whatever follows it — no
longer accurately predicts the system, because modals can no longer take past marking at all. Givón's
revised rule, AUX = (HAVE)(BE), with past attaching to the first VP element only in the *absence* of a
modal, better fits the facts and correctly predicts the possible one-, two-, and three-auxiliary
combinations (e.g. the single legal three-auxiliary string is modal+have+be: *"should have been
working"*). The system remains actively in flux: newer progressive aspectuals (*keep, start, finish,
stop, continue*) have been fully integrated into the ordering template, while others are only partially
integrated — *going to/gonna* cannot combine with perfect *have* (only with progressive *be*); the
present-progressive future reading takes neither *have* nor *be*; and new quasi-modals (*have to, have
got to, need to*) are semantically equivalent to true modals but syntactically still behave like main
modality verbs (*want*-class) rather than true auxiliaries, with inconsistent sub-behaviors (*have to*
combines with perfect *have* and with other modals; *need to* and *have got to* each fail at least one of
those combinations) — plus a standard-English ban on stacking two true modals in one VP that many
non-standard spoken dialects simply do not observe (illustrated with a literary citation of "might
would").

### Negation exceeds logic: four demonstrations that language-negation is not truth-functional negation (p.187–188)

Formal logic treats negation as pure truth-value reversal (double negation cancels; P and NOT-P
can't both hold). Givón shows natural-language negation does more: *"I am not unhappy"* is not
synonymous with *"I am happy"* despite double-negation cancellation, and *"not happy"* is not
synonymous with *"unhappy"* despite both being single negations of *"happy"* — contra the predictions
of (108a,b), evidence that lexical/morphological and syntactic negation carve the semantic space
differently from logical negation.

### Negation presupposes its corresponding affirmative as discourse background (p.188–193)

A felicitous NEG-assertion requires that the speaker take the corresponding affirmative proposition to
be a live, available-to-challenge background assumption for the hearer — established explicitly in
prior discourse, contributed by the interlocutor, inferred from specific knowledge of the hearer's
state of mind, or drawn from generic cultural norms. This explains a whole family of felicity contrasts:
negating a norm-violating state (*"a man who didn't have a head"*) is natural, negating the cultural
norm itself is odd/tautologous; negating an event that would otherwise be rare/unspecifiable (*"A man
didn't come into my office..."*, *"Where didn't you leave the keys?"*, *"When John doesn't come..."*) is
pragmatically bizarre unless the context first establishes a *specific*, denumerable alternative (a fixed
set of candidate visitors, a single established reference time) against which the negative becomes
informative — in each such "repaired" case the figure/ground relation between event and non-event
has been locally reversed by context.

### Negation as a confrontational speech-act, with social and discourse consequences (p.188, 193–195)

Negation and realis share "strong assertion" status, but negation is additionally a *denial* speech-act
("the hearer knows wrong, the speaker knows better") rather than an information-conveying one
("the hearer does not know, the speaker knows") — which makes it socially loaded. In power-
asymmetric or close-knit-community interaction, speakers routinely soften negation/disagreement
with irrealis devices (hedges, modals, indirect questions — illustrated at length with a small-town
negotiated-description dialogue), and, somewhat paradoxically, negation can *itself* serve as a
softening device toward a perceived higher authority when combined with another irrealis operator
(*"Won't you come in?"*, *"I don't suppose he's done yet?"*).

### Negative clauses are the discourse-infrequent, cognitively-grounded minority case (p.190–191)

Grounded in the physical law of inertia: events (changes) are the rarer figure against a background
norm of stasis (non-events), so events are inherently more informative, and negation is "a pun on the
norm" — temporarily promoting a non-event to salience. Confirmed with frequency counts: negative
clauses were only 5% of an academic text and 12% of a fiction text (fiction's higher rate attributed to
dialogic perspective-shifting, which creates more occasions for epistemic/valuative conflict than a
single-narrator non-fiction voice).

### Presupposition and the scope of negation: subordinate/shielded material survives negation of the main clause (p.195–199)

Negating a main clause typically leaves content in a relative clause, verb complement, or fronted
adverbial clause unaffected/presupposed (*"She didn't see the man who stood in the corner"* still
entails *he stood in the corner*). The default, most-common pattern is **VP-negation**, which
systematically **excludes the subject** from its scope — confirmed by a text count showing 0 of 60
VP-negated clauses extending scope to the subject NP; subject-scoped negation instead requires a
dedicated **NP-negation** form (*"No king of France ate his dinner"*, *"Nobody ate their dinner"*).
Within VP-negation, **focused negation** (contrastive stress, or stress + cleft) further narrows scope to
a single constituent (subject-focus / object-focus / verb-focus readings of the same surface string,
*"John didn't kill the goat"*), and any optional constituent present (benefactive, instrument, time,
frequency, locative adverbs) tends to pragmatically attract the negation's focus to itself, on the
general principle that an optional element, once chosen, is likely to be the point of the assertion in
the first place.

### The morphosyntax of VP-negation: auxiliary-attachment rule, with a 'be'/'have' exception (p.199–201)

Formal rule: if no auxiliary is present, insert dummy *do*; attach *not/-n't* to whichever auxiliary
directly follows (modal, *have*, or *be* — or, failing those, *do*); a modal cannot itself carry past
marking. One systematic exception: main-verb (copular) *be* behaves like an auxiliary for negation
purposes and takes the suffix directly with no *do*-support (*"Mary isn't tall"*), and some British
English extends this same direct-suffixation behavior to main-verb *have* (*"I haven't a clue"*) where
American English requires *do*-support (*"I don't have a clue"*) — flagged as the British form
preserving an older, more conservative historical pattern.

### Main-vs-complement-clause negation: language violates compositional logic here too (p.201–202)

Logic predicts *"I don't think she came"* and *"I think she didn't come"* should differ systematically in
truth conditions, but for non-factive/non-implicative complement-taking verbs (*think, want*) the two
negation-placement options collapse to near-synonymy (with only a subtle strength-of-belief/
preference gradient between them), both converging toward the "I think [not-P]" reading rather than
the "not [I think P]" reading. For other complement-taking verbs (*know, ask, continue*) the two
placements *do* yield logically distinct, non-overlapping readings — so which behavior obtains is
verb-class-specific, not predictable from logic alone.

### Syntactic, morphological, and inherent negation are three distinct devices with different grammatical behavior (p.202–204)

Syntactic (*not/n't*, applies to any verb/adjective), morphological (*un-, dis-*, applies only to some
items idiosyncratically), and inherently-negative lexical items (*sad* for "not happy," *doubt* for "not
believe") are formally distinguished by their interaction with **negative-polarity items** like *too/
either*: these track only true *syntactic* negation — morphological and inherent-lexical negatives
pattern with the *affirmative* for polarity-item selection (*"Mary was unhappy, and Jack was too"*, not
*"...and Jack wasn't either"*) even though they are semantically negative.

### Constituent (NP) negation as escalated, more emphatic denial, with a documented historical negation cycle (p.204–208)

Beyond ordinary VP-negation (which presupposes the corresponding affirmative event-frame and
denies it as a whole), **NP-negation** (*no, nobody, nothing, never, nowhere*) attacks the
presuppositional foundation more specifically, rendering the targeted NP **non-referring** — denying
not just that a specific participant was involved but that any member of its type was. Givón charts a
graded continuum from plain VP-negation through VP-negation-plus-emphasis, VP-negation-plus-
NEG-pronoun, plain NP-negation, to NP-negation-plus-NEG-pronoun, with "colloquial double
negation" (*"I didn't see nothin'"*) sitting at the most-emphatic end of this continuum rather than being
a random nonstandard error — and traces written Standard English's own current VP-negation
pattern back to a documented two-cycle historical de-emphasis process from Old English emphatic
double negation (*ne...ne-ought* → contracted *not* → re-emphasized with newly-grammaticalized *do*
→ contracted again to today's *don't*), meaning today's "proper" negation is itself the fossilized
residue of a formerly emphatic construction — directly recalling ch.1's arbitrary-rules-as-historical-
residue theme. A final asymmetry: the escalated emphatic-VP-negation steps are available for objects
and adverbs but *not* for subjects (because subjects are structurally excluded from VP-negation scope
to begin with), so subject emphatic denial can only be expressed via the NP-negation end of the
continuum.

## Grammar points

### Dictionary usage-label taxonomy (Ch.15)

The book gives an explicit six-way usage-label system that a standard print dictionary uses to mark a
word's register, each with a worked example:

| Label | Meaning | Example given |
|---|---|---|
| Colloquial | used in conversation and informal writing | *dad* (for "father") |
| Slang | not standard usage | *rad*, *phat* |
| Obsolete | no longer used | *owe* used to mean "to possess" |
| Archaic | no longer used except in a fixed phrase | *quick* meaning "living," surviving only in "the quick and the dead" |
| Dialect | restricted to certain geographic/social groups | *ain't* |
| Poetic | used in literature, not everyday speech | *o'er* (for "over") |

This is an independently stated six-way register taxonomy (distinct from, but clearly overlapping,
this project's own Usage Tier Taxonomy's `slang`/`colloquial`/`archaic`/`regional`/`literary`
categories) worth cross-referencing — notably it treats *dialect* and *colloquial* as separate labels
from *slang*, and gives *obsolete* vs. *archaic* as a genuine two-way split (fully dead vs.
survives-only-in-a-fixed-phrase) that this project's taxonomy could consider adopting explicitly
(pp.195–196).

### Denotation vs. connotation (Ch.15)

A word's **denotation** is its dictionary (literal) meaning; its **connotation** is the emotional
overtone attached to it. Key point: not every word carries a connotation (the source's example: *thin*
is denotation-only/neutral), but near-synonyms can carry opposite connotations despite sharing a
denotation — *thrifty* (positive) vs. *parsimonious* (negative) both denote "careful with money";
*house* (neutral) vs. *home* (carries warmth/welcome connotations) share a denotation. This is a
directly useful mechanism for a slang-synthesis project: new slang terms very often arise specifically
to supply a *connotation* (usually pejorative or affectionate) that the neutral standard term lacks,
rather than to supply a new denotation (p.201).

### Bibliography of specialized dictionaries — a possible future-source lead

The chapter lists, by name, several specialized English dictionaries this project has not previously
flagged in its source-triage catalog, most notably two **dedicated slang/unconventional-usage
dictionaries** — *The New Dictionary of American Slang* (ed. Robert Chapman) and *Dictionary of Slang
and Unconventional English* (ed. Eric Partridge) — and a **dedicated regional-dialect dictionary**,
*Dictionary of American Regional English* (ed. Frederic Cassidy). Also named: usage dictionaries
(*Dictionary of Modern English Usage*, ed. Fowler; *Modern American Usage*, ed. Barzun) and
word-origin dictionaries (ed. Morris & Morris; ed. Partridge). Worth a note for whoever next updates

project's English corpus work would benefit from acquiring directly, rather than relying on
grammar-textbook asides (pp.198–199).

### Minor spelling mnemonic (Ch.18, kept despite the chapter's otherwise-redundant content)

*Principal* vs. *principle*: "the **principal** is your **pal**" (both end *-pal*); *principle* ends
in *-le*, like *rule* (which is what a principle is). A self-contained mnemonic, not cross-referenced
elsewhere in this project's English files (p.237).

### The 3C's of effective writing style: Consistency, Coherence, Clarity (Ch.19)

The book's core style framework. **Consistency** means a document maintains one single tone/mood
throughout, suited to topic and reader, and does not switch point of view (first- to second-person,
etc.) mid-document. **Coherence** means the writing is logical and unified, signaled to the reader via
four devices: transitions, pronouns (as anaphoric links), repetition (deliberate, for rhythm/emphasis
— explicitly distinguished from *redundancy*, which is unintentional and undesirable), and parallel
structure. **Clarity** means the writing is easy to understand; the book cites Mark Twain's rules for
Fenimore Cooper's "literary offenses" essay as a model clarity checklist (say what you mean directly;
use the right word, not "its first cousin"; omit nothing necessary; avoid slovenly form) (pp.253–258).

### Four writing-style myths debunked (Ch.19)

The book explicitly rejects four classic prescriptive "rules" as myths, each with a nuanced real
answer: (1) *write as you talk* — false; speech's hesitations/backtracking read badly on the page; (2)
*never begin a sentence with a coordinating conjunction* — false; doing so is fine when it reads more
smoothly, though overuse makes a sentence feel like an afterthought; (3) *never end a sentence with a
preposition* — false if the alternative is awkward; (4) *never use "I" in business writing* — false
when describing your own viewpoint, though overuse can read as self-centered; and a fifth, unpaired
claim that *big words impress people* is also rejected — technical jargon aside, big words mostly
create distance from readers and risk misuse (pp.261–262).

### Audience analysis: primary, secondary, gatekeeper, watchdog (Ch.20)

A four-way model of *who actually reads a document* beyond its addressee: the **primary audience**
(the actual decision-maker who accepts/rejects the message), the **secondary audience** (others asked
to comment or implement it after approval), the **gatekeeper** (whoever has power to stop the message
outright, e.g. a supervisor), and the **watchdog audience** (people without stop-power but with
political/economic leverage over the outcome). A genuinely useful structural model for understanding
how register gets shaped not just by a single addressee but by an entire chain of potential readers
(p.265).

### Four modes of discourse: exposition, narration, argumentation, description (Ch.20)

The book's model for "kinds of writing," reduced to exactly four: **exposition** (writing that
explains — how-tos, business/personal letters, news, reports, textbooks; most everyday writing falls
here), **narration** (storytelling — anecdote, biography, novel, oral history; has plot/characters/
setting/point of view), **argumentation** (writing that persuades via reason — editorials, letters to
the editor, job evaluations/recommendations, resumes), and **description** (sensory detail; the only
mode the book claims is used *inside* every other mode, since no narration/persuasion/exposition can
be written without some descriptive detail) (pp.271–273).

*Incidental finding:* one of this chapter's own reading-comprehension drill passages (p.267) is an
unrelated, out-of-context definition of **pidgin formation** via Derek Bickerton's plantation-contact
studies ("a makeshift jargon containing words of various languages and little in the way of grammar
... gradual shift from speaking a few words of the owner's tongue to speaking a new language") —
coincidentally on-topic for this project's sibling Modern-Hawaiian-pidgin-synthesis work, though it is
merely a quoted comprehension exercise, not original content from this book's author, and carries no
further citation.

### Eight sentence-variety techniques (Ch.21)

The book's checklist for stylistically varied prose: (1) vary sentence *types* (simple/compound/
complex/compound-complex); (2) vary sentence *length* (short for dense/numeric content, longer to link
ideas — contrasted against Chief Joseph's 1877 surrender speech as a stately all-simple-sentence
counter-example); (3) add occasional questions/commands among declaratives; (4) choose the sentence's
*subject* deliberately for emphasis (the same information can foreground different nouns); (5) add
descriptive detail via adjectives/adverbs/phrases/clauses, placed close to what they modify; (6) favor
vivid action verbs over "to be" + noun constructions; (7) invert subject-verb word order occasionally
for emphasis (*In walked the CEO*); (8) use second-person "you" to engage readers directly in less
formal registers. A closing point ties **punctuation choice itself to style**: a period forces a full
pause between ideas; a comma + coordinating conjunction signals addition/choice/consequence/contrast/
cause; a semicolon alone implies a link the reader must infer; a semicolon + conjunctive adverb
(*however, nevertheless*) makes the relationship explicit (pp.277–285).

### Conciseness: buzzwords, redundant phrases, and expletive constructions (Ch.22)

Three practical categories of "unnecessary words" the book catalogs: **buzzwords** (vague filler by
part of speech — adjectives: *nice, central, major, good, excellent*; adverbs: *quite, very,
basically, really*; nouns: *field, case, situation, character, kind, scope, sort, type, thing, element,
area, aspect, factor, nature, quality*); **redundant phrases** that say the same thing twice (*at this
point in time* → *now*; *due to the fact that* → *because*; *free gift*, *null and void*, *past
history* — the reasoning given is that the redundant half is already entailed by the other, e.g. "all
history is past"); and **expletive constructions** (*It is .../There is/are/were...*) that delay a
sentence's real subject and should generally be replaced with a direct subject-verb construction. The
chapter also covers converting passive to active voice, while explicitly noting the passive has
legitimate uses (agentless statements, or when the object of an action matters more than its actor)
(pp.288–298).

### Levels of diction: a five-point formal-to-informal spectrum (Ch.23)

The chapter's flagship content for this project: an explicit, named diction/register spectrum running
from most to least formal, each level defined and exemplified:

1. **Elevated diction** — abstract vocabulary, serious tone, few contractions/personal references,
   maximum writer-reader distance (used for legal documents, deeds, wills).
2. **Edited American English** ("Standard Written English") — the register of academic/professional
   writing and mainstream news magazines; conforms to the full standard rule set this book and
   CMOS/Oxford otherwise teach.
3. **Colloquial language** — casual-conversation register (source's example: *flunked* vs. standard
   *failed*).
4. **Slang** — explicitly defined as *"coined words and phrases or new meanings for established
   terms"*; stated to be "fun, informal, and personal... never used in formal writing." (Example terms
   captured in the Vocabulary table above.)
5. **Vernacular** (with **dialect** as a named subtype) — "the ordinary language of a particular
   region" (*hoagie/grinder/sub*); dialect specifically reflects regional *and* socio-economic
   differences, distinguishing it from slang. Illustrated at length with a genuine Mark Twain
   vernacular-dialect passage ("I do wonder what in the nation that frog throw'd off for... he 'pers to
   look mighty baggy") — note the nonstandard spellings/contractions (*throw'd, 'pers, ain't*) used to
   signal dialect in print.

This is a clean, independently stated five-tier model directly comparable to — and worth
cross-referencing against — this project's own `00_Usage_Tier_Taxonomy.md`, and it explicitly treats
*slang* and *vernacular/dialect* as two **distinct** phenomena along the same formality axis rather
than synonyms, which matches this project's own existing taxonomy design (pp.300–303).

**Confused-words list**: the chapter's own 25-pair "often confused words" table (accept/except,
affect/effect, farther/further, flaunt/flout, etc.) is **skipped as redundant** — already captured via
CMOS's own confused-words/usage-glossary coverage in files 009–011.

### Doublespeak taxonomy: jargon, bureaucratic language, inflated language, euphemism (Ch.24)

A four-way breakdown of "artificial, evasive language" where words and facts don't agree:
**jargon** (a group's specialized vocabulary, which is only doublespeak-adjacent when used *outside*
that group's own context — e.g. baseball terms *shutout*/*homer* are fine for a baseball audience);
**bureaucratic language** (stuffy, overblown, deliberately wordy and evasive); **inflated language**
(making the ordinary sound extraordinary — job-title euphemisms, "whoopee words"); and
**euphemisms** (softened phrasing around taboo topics — death, bathroom activities, nudity, aging —
explicitly *not* classified as doublespeak when used out of genuine tact/social custom, e.g. "passed
away" to a grieving relative, versus *as* doublespeak when used to obscure an unpleasant truth). The
chapter also distinguishes **clichés** ("descriptive phrases that have lost effectiveness through
overuse") from **proverbs** ("economical phrases that pack a great deal of meaning in a brief
wallop") — clichés are bloated and meaningless, proverbs are not — and names **slanted language**
(emotionally loaded wording meant to inflame, trending toward propaganda at its worst) as a related
but distinct category from both. Cites George Orwell's "Politics and the English Language" as the
chapter's own intellectual source for most of this (pp.318–324).

### Bias-free language: three practical rules (Ch.24)

Beyond the specific paired terms captured in the Vocabulary table, the chapter states three general
rules for bias-free usage: (1) refer to a group by the term it currently prefers, explicitly framing
this as something that **changes over time** (with the colored→Negro→African American/black
progression as its own worked example of a term shifting roughly every 40–50 years); (2) focus on
people rather than their conditions (*people with vision impairments*, not *the blind*); (3) only
mention someone's race/disability/etc. if directly relevant to the story, and if you mention one
person's, mention everyone's consistently (pp.316–317).

### Business-letter format conventions (Ch.25)

Three named letter formats, distinguished only by paragraph indentation and heading/closing placement:
**block style** (everything flush left), **modified block style** (heading upper-right, closing/
signature lower-right, no paragraph indentation), and **semiblock style** (same heading/closing
placement as modified block, but paragraphs *are* indented). The chapter also gives the two-type
resume model — **chronological** (reverse-chronological, emphasizes degrees/titles/dates — used when
education/experience logically track toward the target job) vs. **skills-based** (emphasizes
competencies over a timeline — used to downplay age or an atypical career path) — and a short list of
content that must never appear on a resume (age, health, religious/political affiliation, race/
ethnicity, gender/orientation, marital status, children/pets) (pp.329–334).

### Personal-letter genre conventions (Ch.26)

Three personal-letter subgenres with distinct conventions: **friendly letters** (wider latitude in
content than business letters, but still expected to be organized; the book speculates email may be a
contemporary descendant of this genre, though in a different format); **thank-you/condolence notes**
(must name the specific gift/act and explain why it mattered; condolence letters are explicitly
expected to be **handwritten**, and are the one genre where "better late than never" genuinely
applies); and **letters of opinion/complaint** (state the problem, cite specific
receipts/model/serial numbers, state a specific and reasonable remedy, and — for praise letters —
name the specific employee/date/service being praised) (pp.346–353).

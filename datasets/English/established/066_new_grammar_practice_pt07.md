## Grammar points

### 89. *at, in, on* (place)
*at* for a point whose exact position doesn't matter (*waiting at the station*); *on* for a
place conceived as a line or surface (*on the table, on the wall*); *in* for a place conceived as
having volume or area (*in the car, in my bag*). (p.128)

### 90. Prepositions of movement
Three movement/position triads sharing a base term: *to* (movement toward) / *at* (static position)
/ *away from* (movement away) for a general place; *onto* (movement toward a surface) / *on* (static
position on it) / *off* (movement away from it) for a line or surface; *into* (movement toward a
volume) / *in* (static position inside it) / *out of* (movement away from it) for anything with
volume. (p.129)

### 91–92. Prepositions of position and movement (extended set)
Introduces a large additional set of spatial prepositions (see Vocabulary table) via one labeled
illustration rather than a rule-box explanation — the chapter's most purely lexical section.
Classifies this extended set into three groups: pure-movement prepositions (*past, through*),
pure-position prepositions (*next to, opposite*), and prepositions that can mark either depending on
the verb they combine with (*under* with *drove* = movement, with *standing* = position) — the same
movement/position ambiguity resolved compositionally rather than lexically for this subset. (p.130–132)

### 93. Certain verbs with *to* or *at*
Which preposition (if any) follows a given verb is a lexical property of that verb, not predictable
from general meaning: *listen to, look at*, but *tell* (no preposition — direct object only, per the
contrast already drawn in pt05 §76). (p.133)

### 94. *at, in, on* (time)
Structurally identical three-way split to §89's spatial version, remapped onto time: *at* a precise
point (*at four o'clock*), *on* a day or date (*on Monday, on July 6th*), *in* a period (*in the
morning, in 1987*). Idiomatic exceptions to the general rule: *at night, at Christmas, at Easter*
take *at* despite being period-like rather than point-like. (p.134)

### 95. *until*
Marks the end-boundary of a period, past or future; can be followed by a time expression or by a
full clause. Future reference within an *until*-clause takes present simple (*until this programme
finishes*), the same future-in-subordinate-clause pattern already established in pt03 §53 for
*when/as soon as*/etc. — confirming that rule extends to *until* as well. *till* is given as the
informal spoken contraction. (p.135)

### 96. *until, before, after*
*before* and *after* are flagged as unusually flexible in what complement they take — a noun, a
pronoun, a full clause, or a gerund — wider than the complement types shown for most of this
chapter's other prepositions. (p.136)

### 97. Prepositions of time — review
Drill recombining the temporal prepositions already introduced (§94–96) plus a few further items
(*through, between, during, from*); no new rule content. (p.137)

### 98–99. Phrasal verbs — introduction and sample sets
A phrasal verb combines a verb with a preposition/particle or adverb particle to form a new lexical
unit whose meaning ranges from close-to-compositional (*slow down* ≈ "slow") to fully idiomatic and
unrelated to the base verb (*give up* = "quit," unrelated to *give*). §99 presents its sample set
embedded in a continuous two-person dialogue rather than an isolated word list — the only place in
the entire book this presentation format is used, closer to connected authentic text than any other
exercise captured across all seven parts of this extraction. (p.138–140)

### 100. Phrasal verbs that don't take an object
Some phrasal verbs are strictly intransitive, with the particle taking no following object at all
(*The car broke down*; *Hurry up!*) — contrasted directly with the transitive, separable class
covered next in §101. (p.140)

### 101. Phrasal verbs that take an object: separable
Transitive phrasal verbs whose particle can sit either immediately after the verb (before the
object) or after the object (*pick up this bag* / *pick this bag up*). When the object is itself a
pronoun, only the split order is grammatical — the particle must follow the pronoun (*pick it up*,
never **pick up it**) — mirroring the identical pronoun-placement constraint already seen for
phrasal-verb-like particle placement nowhere else in the book but structurally parallel to how
pronoun objects behave elsewhere in English syntax generally. (p.141–142)

### 102. Phrasal verbs that take an object but do not separate
A third transitive class, inseparable: the particle must always immediately follow the verb, with
the object only ever coming after both (*look after the children*, never **look the children
after**) — and this holds even when the object is a pronoun (*look after them*, not **look them
after**), the opposite pronoun-placement behavior from §101's separable class. This three-way split
(intransitive / separable-transitive / inseparable-transitive) is the book's final organizing
taxonomy for English phrasal verbs and is presented as a lexical property of each individual verb
rather than predictable from the particle or verb meaning alone. (p.143–144)

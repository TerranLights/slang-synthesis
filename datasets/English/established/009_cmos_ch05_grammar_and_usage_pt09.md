## Grammar points

### 5.188 Using active voice to eliminate prepositions

Converting a passive-voice construction to active almost always eliminates a preposition — whenever
the actor shows up in a *by*-phrase, switching to active voice drops it. Example: *the ship was
sailed by an experienced crew* → *an experienced crew sailed the ship* (p. 708).

### 5.189 Idiomatic uses of prepositions

Which preposition goes with which word is one of the most persistent word-choice problems in
English, since near-synonyms often take different prepositions by convention rather than by any
general rule: *fill A with B* but *instill B into A*; *replace A with B* but *substitute B for A*;
*prefix A to B* but *preface B with A*; *force A into B* but *enforce B on A*; *A implies B, so you
infer B from A* (p. 709).

### 5.190 Shifts in prepositional idiom

Prepositional idiom is genuinely unstable over time, which is why even native speakers need to
double-check it. The source gives *oblivious of* as the traditional literary form against *oblivious
to* as the prevailing contemporary one. Choice of preposition can also be governed by euphony alone
(e.g. choosing among *ruminate on*, *about*, or *over* a problem for how it sounds in context), and a
preposition shift can carry a real meaning difference — *smitten with* another person denotes
something different from *smitten by* another (p. 709).

### 5.192 Conjunctions defined

A conjunction connects sentences, clauses, or words within a clause. *And* can connect two full
sentences; *but* can connect two clauses, or join two adverbs within a single adverbial clause.
Conjunctions can also connect pronouns that share the same grammatical case (e.g. *he and she are
colleagues*; *the teacher encouraged her and me*) (p. 712).

### 5.193 Simple versus compound conjunctions

A conjunction may be simple — a single word such as *and*, *but*, *if*, *or*, or *through* — or
compound. Most compound conjunctions are single words formed by combining two or more words and are
relatively modern formations (*although*, *because*, *nevertheless*, *notwithstanding*, *unless*).
Phrasal conjunctions are multi-word connectives, e.g. *as though*, *inasmuch as*, *in case that*,
*provided that*, *so that*, *supposing that* (p. 712).

### 5.194 Coordinating conjunctions

The two main conjunction classes are coordinating and subordinating. Coordinating conjunctions join
words or word-groups of equal grammatical rank — two nouns, two verbs, two phrases, or two clauses.
They subdivide further into copulative, adversative, disjunctive, and final, and may be either a
single word or a correlative pair (p. 713).

### 5.195 Correlative conjunctions

Correlative conjunctions work in pairs, often joining successive clauses that depend on each other
to complete a single thought: *as…as*; *if…then*; *either…or*; *neither…nor*; *both…and*;
*where…there*; *so…as*; *not only…but also*. Each member of the pair must immediately precede a
structurally matching part of speech — the two halves must frame parallel sentence elements (p. 713).

### 5.196 Copulative conjunctions

Copulative (additive) coordinating conjunctions add a second, related fact to a first: *and*,
*also*, *moreover*, *no less than* (p. 714).

### 5.197 Adversative conjunctions

Adversative (contrasting) coordinating conjunctions qualify or contrast the first element with the
second: *but*, *still*, *yet*, *nevertheless* (p. 714).

### 5.198 Disjunctive conjunctions

Disjunctive (separative) coordinating conjunctions denote alternatives where only one of the joined
statements can be true (both may be false): *either*, *or*, *else*, *but*, *nor*, *neither*,
*otherwise*, *other* (p. 714).

### 5.199 Final conjunctions

Final (illative) coordinating conjunctions denote inference or consequence — the second element
gives the reason for, or shows what ought to follow from, the first: *consequently*, *for*, *hence*,
*so*, *thus*, *therefore*, *as a consequence*, *as a result*, *so that*, *so then* (p. 715).

### 5.200 Subordinating conjunctions

A subordinating conjunction connects clauses of unequal grammatical rank, introducing a clause
dependent on an independent one. A pure subordinating conjunction has no antecedent and is neither a
pronoun nor an adverb (p. 715).

### 5.201 Special uses of subordinating conjunctions

Subordinating conjunctions and conjunctive phrases typically denote one of nine relationships: (1)
comparison/degree (*than*, *else*, *rather*, *other*, *otherwise*, *as…as*, *as far as*, *as well
as*); (2) time (*since*, *until*, *as long as*, *before*, *after*, *when*, *as*, *while*); (3)
condition/assumption (*if*, *though*, *unless*, *except*, *without*, *once*); (4) reason/concession
(*as*, *inasmuch as*, *why*, *because*, *for*, *since*, *though*, *although*, *albeit*); (5)
purpose/result (*that*, *so that*, *in order that*, *such that*); (6) place (*where*); (7) manner
(*as if*, *as though*); (8) apposition (*and*, *or*, *what*, *that*); (9) indirect questions
(*whether*, *why*, *when*) (pp. 715–716).

### 5.202 Adverbs as conjunctions

Many adverbs serve a connecting (conjunctive) function between a dependent and an independent clause
even while still modifying the verb in the clause they introduce — e.g. an adverb like *before* can
connect two clauses while also denoting time; adverbs used this way typically denote time or place
(p. 716).

### 5.203 Relative pronouns as conjunctions

Beyond its pronoun function, a relative pronoun can serve a conjunctive function, joining sentences.
A conjunctive relative pronoun is one whose antecedent is in the independent clause (e.g. *that*
referring back to *suitcase*) (p. 716).

### 5.204 Expletive conjunctions

An expletive conjunction connects two thoughts that are not expressed within the same sentence —
it refers back to a preceding sentence and often (not always) opens the new sentence (p. 717).

### 5.205 Disguised conjunctions

So-called disguised conjunctions are participles that have long functioned as subordinating
conjunctions — *barring*, *considering*, *regarding*, *speaking*, *supposing*, *provided*. Unlike a
true verb use of the same word, a disguised conjunction has no subject of its own (distinguished in
the source via a minimal pair: *considering* the poor road conditions [disguised conjunction, no
subject] vs. the committee is *considering* a budget increase [verb, has a subject]) (p. 717).

### 5.206 Beginning a sentence with a conjunction

The belief that it is an error to open a sentence with a conjunction such as *and*, *but*, or *so*
has no historical or grammatical foundation — a substantial share (often cited as up to 10 percent)
of sentences in first-rate writing begin this way, and always have. The source quotes Charles Allen
Lloyd's 1938 characterization of the belief as a "groundless notion," paired with the equally
groundless belief that sentence-ending prepositions are wrong (cites Lloyd, *We Who Speak English:
And Our Ignorance of Our Mother Tongue*, 1938, p. 19). The source adds a usage caveat of its own,
though: *but* as an adversative conjunction can occasionally read ambiguously at a sentence's start —
evaluate whether the needed connecting idea is really contrastive (warranting *but*) or merely
additive (warranting *and*) before opening with it; *but* is the right choice only when it introduces
a clause that truly contrasts with what precedes (pp. 717–718).

### 5.207 Beginning a sentence with "however"

*However* has functioned as a conjunction since the fourteenth century and, like other conjunctions,
can open a sentence. But the source notes it is more ponderous and has less impact than the simple
*but*, and recommends it is more effectively placed within a sentence, where it can emphasize the
word or phrase that precedes it (p. 719).

### 5.208 Interjections defined

An interjection (or exclamation) is a word, phrase, or clause denoting strong feeling. It has little
or no grammatical function in a sentence and is used absolutely; it is frequently allowed to stand
alone as its own sentence. Introductory words like *well* and *why* can themselves act as
interjections when used as meaningless utterances. Punctuation is what distinguishes an interjection
from the same word used otherwise — the source contrasts *Well, I didn't know him* (interjection)
with *I didn't know him well* (adverb), and *Why, here you are!* (interjection) with *I have no idea
why you are here* and *Why? I have no idea* (question word) (p. 719).

### 5.209 Interjections as informal and colloquial

Interjections are natural in speech and common in poetry and dialogue, but an interjection can
disrupt the flow of prose where an informal, colloquial tone is inappropriate for the register being
used (p. 720).

### 5.210 Interjections and functional variation

Because interjections are usually grammatically independent of the rest of the sentence, a word from
almost any other part of speech can be pressed into service as one — classified by the source as an
exclamatory noun, exclamatory adjective, and so on, per the word's ordinary part of speech. Examples
given: *Good!* (adjective); *Idiot!* (noun); *Help!* (verb); *Indeed!* (adverb); *Dear me!*
(pronoun); *If only!* (conjunction); *How wonderful!* (adverb) (p. 721).

## Grammar points

### Chapter VII — Participles: three participles, and the terminology wars

"A Participle is a word derived from a verb, participating the properties of a verb, and of an adjective or a
noun." Brown names three: **Imperfect** (*-ing*, continuance: *loving*), **Perfect** (*-ed*/*-en*, completion:
*loved*), and **Preperfect** (*having* + perfect: *having loved*) — his own coinage, chosen over the many
competing names he surveys and rejects as inaccurate or inconsistent (Obs. 2 catalogs roughly a dozen
alternative names each grammarian of his era used for what Brown calls the first, second, and third
participles — e.g. "Present," "Progressive," "Indefinite," "Compound Imperfect" for the first alone — a vivid
snapshot of real terminological chaos circa 1851 that Brown explicitly tries to referee and simplify).

A genuinely substantive distinction Brown draws (Obs. 4): a true **participle** differs in meaning from the
superficially identical **participial adjective** even when both have the same form, because the participle
denotes a particular act occurring over some span of time while the adjective denotes a habitual/permanent
quality: "a *thinking* man" (habitually reflective) vs. "a man *thinking*" (engaged in the particular act of
thinking right now); "a *bragging* fellow" vs. "a fellow *bragging*"; "a fast-*sailing* ship" vs. "a ship *sailing*
fast." He notes John Locke was a "thinking man" without being in a state of constantly, presently thinking —
a genuinely sharp semantic point about habitual vs. episodic predication.

### Chapter VIII — Adverbs: the four-plus-one class system, and extensive word-lists

"An Adverb is a word added to a verb, a participle, an adjective, or an other adverb; and generally expresses
time, place, degree, or manner." Brown rejects the then-standard eleven-way classification (Number, Order,
Place, Time, Quantity, Manner/Quality, Doubt, Affirmation, Negation, Interrogation, Comparison — used by
Murray, Comly, Kirkham, and others) as unprincipled, and replaces it with his own cleaner four-way semantic
division answering the four basic circumstantial questions — *when? where? how much? how?* — plus a
fifth structural class:
1. **Adverbs of time** (answer *when? how long? how soon? how often?*), subdivided into present, past,
   future, relative, absolute, and repeated (full lists above).
2. **Adverbs of place** (answer *where? whither? whence? whereabout?*), subdivided into place-in-which,
   place-to-which, place-from-which, and order-of-place.
3. **Adverbs of degree** (answer *how much? how little?*), subdivided into excess/abundance,
   equality/sufficiency, deficiency/abatement, and abstract quantity.
4. **Adverbs of manner** (answer *how?*, or show affirmation/negation/doubt), subdivided into
   manner-from-quality (the productive *-ly* class — "ly is a contraction of *like*, and is the most common
   termination of English adverbs"), affirmation/assent, negation, doubt/uncertainty, and mode/way.
5. **Conjunctive adverbs** — adverbs that simultaneously do the connective work of a conjunction while
   still carrying their own time/place/manner meaning (*when, where, while, since, therefore, however,
   nevertheless, otherwise*, etc.) — Brown gives a long list of these (Obs. 2) and devotes several observations
   to a subtlety: some conjunctive adverbs (*when, where, whither, whence, how, why*) can function almost
   like relative pronouns, referring back to an antecedent noun of time/place/manner/cause ("the day *when*
   God shall judge," "a place *where* all real wants might be supplied") — a construction Brown judges
   acceptable when it shortens expression without real ambiguity, but generally inferior to a true relative
   clause, and worth avoiding in careful prose ("Youth is the time *when* we are young" → better: "Youth is
   that part of life which succeeds to childhood").

**Comparison of adverbs:** most adverbs don't inflect at all, but a handful compare like adjectives — regularly
(*soon/sooner/soonest, often/oftener/oftenest, long/longer/longest, fast/faster/fastest*) or irregularly
(*well/better/best; badly or ill/worse/worst; little/less/least; much/more/most; far/farther/farthest;
forth/further/furthest; rath/rather* [now comparative-only, "rathest" obsolete]). Brown also notes (Obs. 2) a
genuine archaic poetic license, found in Milton and some contemporaries: two-syllable *-ly* adverbs
regularly inflected with *-er* even though this adds a syllable that would normally disrupt the iambic meter —
*wiselier, rightlier, likeliest, amplier, firmlier, seemlier, plainlier, easiliest, kindlier, earthlier* — treated by
the poets as if still metrically bisyllabic.

**Cross-category flexibility, mirroring Ch. III's noun discussion:** Brown documents in detail (Obs. 4–6) how
almost any part of speech can slip into adverbial use (articles, nouns — "come *home* tomorrow," "it is
*vengeance* cold" — adjectives, pronouns, verbs used as pure sound-effect interjections — "*Crack!* went
something on deck" — participles, conjunctions, prepositions), and conversely how adverbs can slip into
noun/adjective/pronoun/verb/interjection use ("the *yes* or the *no* must be accompanied... by reasons"; "Up,
up, Glentarkin!"). He calls this the figure *enallage* and treats it as a real, productive feature of English
syntax rather than a set of isolated exceptions.

### Chapter IX — Conjunctions: copulative/disjunctive/corresponsive

"A Conjunction is a word used to connect words or sentences in construction, and to show the dependence of
the terms so connected." Three classes: **copulative** (addition/cause/consequence/supposition: *and, because,
if, since*), **disjunctive** (opposition: *or, but, though, unless*), and **corresponsive** (paired: *both...and,
either...or, neither...nor, whether...or*). Brown devotes a long methodological argument (Obs. 2–5) against
Harris's (and Aristotle's) claim that conjunctions connect *only* whole sentences, never single words — citing
plain counterexamples ("Duty *and* interest forbid vicious indulgences" connects two nouns, not two clauses)
and tracing the error back through Sanctius, Scaliger, and Apollonius to Aristotle himself, framing it as an
inherited classical-grammar mistake uncritically copied into English grammar via Harris's *Hermes* and then
Murray.

### Chapter X — Prepositions: the simplest part of speech, but genuinely contested at the edges

"A Preposition is a word used to express some relation of different things or thoughts to each other, and is
generally placed before a noun or a pronoun." Brown notes (Obs. 5) prepositions are structurally the simplest
part of speech — no classes, no modifications — but flags a genuine, still-live descriptive puzzle at their
boundary with adverbs and with phrasal-verb particles: words like *up, out, on* in "cast *up*, hold *out*, fall
*on*" were analyzed by contemporaries (Lennie, Bullions, Murray) as somehow "part of the verb" rather than
as either prepositions or adverbs — a position Brown rejects outright, insisting such words are simply
**adverbs** that happen to modify a verb without governing an object, with no special "verb-particle" status
needed. He also catalogs real syntactic puzzles (Obs. 6) — biblical and classical examples where a preposition
is separated from its governed term by many intervening words (the opening of *Paradise Lost*: "Of..." in
line 1 depends on "Sing" six lines later), and a genuinely disputed Greek-to-English translation crux in
Matthew 19:28 turning on whether a preposition attaches forward or backward in the sentence.

### Chapter XI — Interjections: the 22-way emotional taxonomy

"An Interjection is a word that is uttered merely to indicate some strong or sudden emotion of the mind."
Brown's interjection list (above) is organized uniquely among his part-of-speech inventories — not
alphabetically or by form, but by the **emotion or speech-act function** each interjection cluster expresses
(joy, sorrow, wonder, vocative address, praise, surprise-with-disapproval, pain/fear, contempt, aversion,
expulsion, calling aloud, exultation, laughter, salutation, calling to attention, calling to silence, dread/horror,
languor, stopping, parting, knowing/detecting, interrogating) — effectively a period taxonomy of
expressive/phatic vocabulary, directly relevant to this project's interest in register and pragmatic function. He
flags the usage distinction between vocative *O* (earnest address, no exclamation mark required, e.g. "O
Jerusalem") and exclamatory *oh* (sorrow/pain/surprise) as a real, frequently-violated norm in print even by
careful writers and poets. He also rejects contemporaries' claim (Wells) that *any* part of speech used
exclamationally thereby "becomes" an interjection, insisting the word's original class should still govern how
it is parsed even when uttered with emotional force ("Order! gentlemen, order!" remains elliptical for "Come
to order," not a true interjection).

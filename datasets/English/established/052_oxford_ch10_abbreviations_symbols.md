# English — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: New Oxford Style Manual, Part One (*New Hart's
Rules*), Chapter 10 "Abbreviations and Symbols"

**Source:** *New Oxford Style Manual* (Oxford University Press, 2016), Part One (*New Hart's
Rules*), chapter 10 "Abbreviations and Symbols," §§10.1–10.7, printed pp. 175–185 (PDF pages
192–203).

**Coverage note:** every grammar/style rule and every distinct abbreviation/symbol example in this
chapter is captured below. This source has a genuine clean text layer — no vision-reading was
needed; Vision Reading Confidence is `n/a` throughout. No mojibake or font-substitution corruption
was encountered in this page range (all characters, including `×`, `£`, `€`, `°`, `′`, `″`, `π`,
`Ω`, `Σ`, and the reference-mark glyphs `*†‡§¶‖`, rendered cleanly).

**Non-redundant-supplement scope note (per `../../00_Reference_Extraction_Spec.md`):** this
project's English corpus already has four established files on the exact same topic from a
different, independently authored source — `032`–`035_cmos_ch10_abbreviations_pt0{1–4}.md` (*The
Chicago Manual of Style*, 16th ed., 2010, American English). The two chapters share the same
underlying subject (abbreviation categories, period conventions, articles, plurals, Latin
cross-references, symbols) but this is Oxford's **British**-English treatment, independently
authored, with different illustrative examples throughout — exactly the "independently authored
source is not redundant by default" case the spec calls out. Decisions made below:
- **Kept in full, genuinely new territory not covered by CMOS ch.10 at all:** apostrophes in
  colloquial abbreviations (§10.2.3), symbol typography and discipline-specific symbol meanings
  (§10.3), and the detailed abbreviations-with-dates rules for b./d./c./fl. spacing, the `c.` vs
  `?` distinction, and the `×` multiplication-symbol date-range notation (§10.7 — CMOS pt03 only
  glossed `ca./c.` and `fl.` as single-line dictionary entries at §10.43, with none of this
  spacing/usage detail).
- **Kept, condensed, as genuinely deeper/different even where the underlying rule overlaps:** the
  period-convention/contraction rules (§10.2.1, 10.2.4–10.2.6) use entirely different British
  illustrative examples (book sizes `4to`/`8vo`/`12mo`, British county abbreviations, British
  days/months forms) and explicitly state the US/British point-density contrast CMOS's pt01 only
  touched once (Dr/assn/Mme); the e.g./i.e./etc./et al. rules (§10.6) state the Oxford
  no-comma-after-e.g./i.e. convention as an explicit contrast with "US practice," which CMOS pt01's
  parallel discussion does not state from the British side.
- **Skipped as substantially redundant (rule and logic already captured, only the illustrative
  examples differ):** the indefinite-article-by-pronunciation rule (§10.4) is the identical rule
  CMOS pt01 §10.9 already captured in full (including the "same abbreviation, different article"
  minimal-pair pattern) — condensed to one grammar-point note with British examples rather than
  re-tabulating every example; the possessives/plurals rule (§10.5) is likewise condensed, keeping
  only the Latin-doubled-letter-plural items (`ff.`, `ll.`, `pp.`, `MSS`, `opp. cit.`) since those
  were not in CMOS's parallel section.

## Grammar points

### Abbreviation taxonomy (§10.1)

The manual divides abbreviation phenomena into four categories, only the first of which is a
strict-sense "abbreviation": **abbreviations** (omit the word's end), **contractions** (omit the
word's middle, keeping first and last letters), **acronyms** (from initial letters; a stricter
sub-definition distinguishes acronyms *pronounced as words* from **initialisms**, pronounced
letter-by-letter), and **symbols/signs** (abstract representations — letters assigned to concepts,
or special typographical sorts). Guidelines for use overlap across the categories but the
abbreviation/contraction split in particular governs the full-point rule in §10.2.1 (p.174).

### When to use abbreviations in running text (§10.1, p.175-176)

For a general audience, avoid abbreviations/symbols in open (main, non-parenthetical) text unless:
the form is very familiar (*US*, *BBC*, *UN*); the spelled-out form would itself be unfamiliar
(*DNA*, *SIM card*); space is scarce; or the term recurs often enough that abbreviating it eases
reading. Abbreviations are more at home in parentheses and in ancillary matter (appendices,
bibliographies, captions, figures, notes, tables), which carry their own, looser rules. Spelling
out at first mention with the abbreviation following in parentheses is the standard device; a
book-wide glossary/abbreviations list is preferred over repeated per-chapter expansion in
multi-author or non-linear-reading works, except where house style exempts certain
technical-readership-familiar forms (e.g. *RAM* in an academic computing book). Honours/
qualification initialisms after a personal name are a standing exception to the spell-out rule —
conventionally left unexpanded even though unfamiliar to most readers. It is also acceptable to
refer back to a recently introduced full name via a shortened descriptive phrase ("the Institute")
rather than repeating its initialism.

### Full points: abbreviations take them, contractions traditionally don't (§10.2.1)

The traditional rule: abbreviations (omitted *end*) take a full point; contractions (omitted
*middle*) don't. This is "handy and in general borne out" but has real exceptions in both
directions — a disambiguating exception (*St.* "Street" keeps its point to avoid collision with
*St* "Saint"), a Latin-form exception (*no.*), and a plural-form inconsistency problem (*vol.*
vs. *vols*, *ch.* vs. *chs* — some styles retain the point on all such short forms specifically to
avoid the "inconsistent-looking juxtaposition" of a pointed singular beside an unpointed plural).
Technical/scientific writing uses fewer points generally — metric abbreviations never take a point
in scientific work, and purely scientific abbreviations are presented without points at all. Era
abbreviations (*AD*, *BC*), ordinal numbers, monetary amounts, and traditional book-size
abbreviations (*4to*, *8vo*, *12mo*) are additional no-point exceptions. A colloquial abbreviation
that has become an established word in its own right (*demo*, *trad*) likewise drops the point.
**British style uses fewer points than US style even on contractions** — this is the chapter's
explicit cross-reference point for American-vs-British contrast (*Jr.* US vs. *Jr* British; cross-
ref §6.1.3).

### Ampersands (§10.2.2)

Avoid ampersands generally; use them only in established combinations (*R&B*, *T&C*) and in firm
names that use them as part of their own official styling (*M&S*, *Mills & Boon*). Normal spacing
is a space on either side, except where a company's own style closes the ampersand up (*M&S*);
journalism frequently closes up ordinary combinations like *R&B* with no spaces at all.

### Apostrophes in colloquial contractions (§10.2.3)

Place the apostrophe at the position of the omitted letter(s) — which can mean more than one
apostrophe for a form with multiple omissions (*fo'c's'le*, *ha'p'orth*, *sou'wester*, *t'other*),
with *shan't* as a notable exception taking only one despite two omissions. Informal contractions
(*I'm*, *can't*, *it's*, *mustn't*, *he'll*) are broadly acceptable in less formal prose, especially
fiction and reported speech, and editors should not impose or remove them except for internal
consistency. Colloquial abbreviations that have become ordinary standard words (*cello*, *flu*,
*phone*, *plane*) take no apostrophe at all. Old-fashioned/literary abbreviated forms (*'tis*,
*'twas*, *'twixt*) retain an *opening* apostrophe when archaism is intentional or older copy is
reproduced precisely — with the specific caution that the mark must be correctly set as an opening
quote character, not mistakenly rendered as a closing one.

### Capitalization-dependent point conventions (§10.2.4–10.2.6)

Single-capital-letter abbreviations normally take a point (*G.*, *Oxford U.*) except when used as a
symbol, with abbreviated single-letter compass directions (*N, S, E, W*) as a further exception
with no point. Multi-letter acronyms/initialisms take no points and are closed up in British and
technical usage (*TUC*, *MA*, *EU*); some US house styles diverge, pointing certain initialisms.
Where a text is dense with full-capital abbreviations, small capitals can be used to reduce visual
clutter; certain forms (*BC*, *AD*) are conventionally always set in small capitals regardless.
Lower-case abbreviations usually take no points (*mph*, *plc*), and generally cannot begin a
sentence in running text in their abbreviated form — except in notes, where a defined set
(*c., e.g., i.e., l., ll., p., pp.*) is allowed to stay lower case even note-initial. *a.m.*/*p.m.*
are always lower case with two points, used only with figures, never alongside "o'clock." Weight/
measure short forms are generally unpointed, with *gal.* and *in.* as non-technical-use exceptions,
and *min.*/*sec.* taking a point only outside scientific contexts. Contracted titles/name components
(*Dr, Mr, Mrs, Ms, Mme, Mlle, Ft, Mt*) and shortened academic degrees (*PhD*, *MLitt*) are
unpunctuated; British counties conventionally take a point on their abbreviated forms (*Berks.*,
*Yorks.*) with *Hants*, *Northants*, *Oxon* kept unpointed as inheritances from older spellings/
Latin forms. Days and months are spelled in full as a rule, abbreviated (with points) only where
space genuinely requires it.

### Work-title abbreviations follow the title's own typography (§10.2.7)

An abbreviation standing in for an italicized title (a book, play, or journal) is itself set in
italics (*DNB* for *Dictionary of National Biography*; *Arist. Metaph.* for Aristotle's
*Metaphysics*, author roman/title italic) — house and disciplinary convention can still vary the
exact form, especially by whether the abbreviation is destined for running text or a note.

### Symbol spacing and discipline-specific symbol meanings (§10.3)

Never start a sentence with a symbol — spell the word out or recast the sentence instead.
Word-formed symbols are set closed up to what they modify (*GeV*, *Σc*) but spaced when standing
alone for a word/concept (*a W chromosome*); abstract typographical symbols follow the same
closed-up-or-spaced logic case by case, and symbols consisting of alphabetic letters never take
points. In coordinate notation, measurement symbols (degrees/minutes/seconds) are set close to the
preceding figure, not to the following compass-point letter. The traditional reference-mark
sequence *\*, †, ‡, §, ¶, ‖* is a print-page convention that does not translate well to ebook/online
publishing, where superior numbers are preferable. Mathematical signs (*+, −, =, >, <*) recur in
biological and philological works, not only arithmetical ones, and in such non-arithmetical uses
should be separated by a normal or thin space rather than closed up; in etymology specifically,
`>` conventionally means "gives/has given" and `<` means "derived from." **The same glyph can carry
opposite-field, discipline-specific meanings**: an asterisk prefixed to a word signifies a
*reconstructed* (scholarly-inferred, unattested) form in philological works, but an *incorrect or
non-standard* form in grammatical works — a distinction worth flagging for this project's own later
use of asterisked forms in mechanics analysis. A dagger before a person's name signifies
"deceased," or elsewhere can mark an obsolete word. The abbreviation/symbol boundary itself blurs
for forms derived directly from a word in another language, such as chemical-element symbols
(*Ag* from *argentum*, *U* from *uranium*) and physics symbols used in equations rather than
running prose (*E* for energy, *m* for mass).

### The indefinite article depends on pronunciation, not spelling (§10.4)

*(Condensed — this is the same rule already fully captured from CMOS pt01 §10.9, with different
British illustrative examples: use *a* before a consonant sound including aspirated *h* and a
vowel-letter pronounced as *w*/*y* — *a BA degree*, *a KLM flight*, *a BBC announcer*, *a YMCA bed*,
*a U-boat captain*, *a UNICEF card*; use *an* before a vowel sound including unaspirated *h* — *an
MCC ruling*, *an FA cup match*, *an HDTV*, *an IOU*, *an MP*, *an RAC badge*, *an SOS signal*, *an
NHS hospital*. The same-abbreviation-different-article minimal pair recurs here too: *MS* for
"manuscript" is pronounced as the full word and so takes *a* ("a MS by..."), while *MS* for
"multiple sclerosis" is pronounced *em-ess* and takes *an*; "R." for *rabbi* is pronounced as the
full word ("a R. Shimon wrote"); and the rule applies identically whether or not the abbreviation is
an acronym (*a NASA launch* but *an MC's toast*).)*

### Possessives and plurals of abbreviations (§10.5)

*(Condensed — the ordinary apostrophe-+-s possessive and the plain-s plural, with an apostrophe
only for clarity on single-letter plurals, are the same core rules already captured from CMOS's

**ff.** "folios/following pages," **ll.** "lines," **pp.** "pages," **MSS** "manuscripts," **opp.

### Distinguishing e.g., i.e., viz., etc., and et al. (§10.6)

*e.g.* (*exempli gratia*, "for example") and *i.e.* (*id est*, "that is") are frequently confused
despite distinct functions — compare "hand tools, e.g. hammer and screwdriver" (an example) against
"hand tools, i.e. those able to be held in the user's hands" (a defining paraphrase). Oxford
prefers "for example"/"such as"/"like" and "that is" in running prose, reserving the Latin
abbreviations for parentheses and notes. **Oxford house style sets both in lower-case roman with
two points and no following comma — explicitly contrasted with "US practice," which commonly does
add the comma.** A comma, colon, or dash should still precede *e.g.*/*i.e.* itself. *viz.*
(*videlicet*, "namely") is the rarer third term, historically used to introduce a list rather than
supply a definition; Oxford's preference is to replace it with "namely" or simply use *i.e.*
throughout. *etc.* (*et cetera*) needs at least two already-listed items to be meaningful, takes a
preceding comma after more than one listed item, and must never be combined with "and" or with a
list-opener that already signals incompleteness (*e.g.*, *including*, *for example*, *such as*) —
pick one signal, not both. It is considered rude to use *etc.* when listing individual *people*
(use "and others" instead), but acceptable when listing *types* of people; *et al.* (*et alii*,
"and others") is the Latin-abbreviation equivalent used specifically for people in technical/
scholarly contexts.

### Abbreviations with dates (§10.7)

*b.* ("born") and *d.* ("died") are set roman, pointed, and closed up to the following figure; an
en rule is an alternative for a still-open future-end date, though *b.* is often preferred since a
bare en rule can read as presumptuously anticipating death. *circa* (*c.*/*ca.*) is used with dates
and quantities, closed up to a following figure but spaced when attached to a word/letter;
discursive prose favors "about"/"some," while scientific text favors "approximately." **Crucially,
*c.* must be repeated before *each* date in a span if both ends are approximate** — a single
instance is not read as modifying both ends of the range. *c.* is distinguished from a bare
question mark (*?*) by what kind of uncertainty is being flagged: *c.* signals that only a
period/range (not a precise year) is knowable, while *?* signals a specific year is believed
correct but not certain — which is why *c.* gravitates toward round numbers (decade starts/
midpoints) more than numbers falling between them. Historians use a multiplication sign (*×*) to
express date-range uncertainty discursive prose can't easily carry: *1996 × 1997* means "in 1996 or
1997, but we cannot tell which," while *1996 × 2004* means "no earlier than 1996 and no later than
2004" — note that figures are *not* generally contracted in this specific notation. *floruit*
(*fl.*/*flor.*), "flourished," covers the case where only an approximate *period of activity* (not
birth/death) can be fixed for a historical figure; it is italicized and set before the year(s) or,
failing any concrete date, before a century designation, with a space.

## Grammar points

### Ch.7 Verbal Complements

#### The event-integration scale and its syntax (§7.2–7.3)

Givón's central device for this chapter is a single semantic-syntactic **complementation scale**
(table (10), p.6) running from strongest to weakest "event integration" between a main clause and
its complement clause: `let-go` > `make`/`have` (bare-stem) > `tell`/`ask`/`allow`/`want` (infinitive)
> `for-to` > `suggest`/`wish`/`agree` (modal-subjunctive) > `know`/`say` indirect quote > `say` direct
quote. The overarching **isomorphism prediction** (p.1): "the stronger the semantic bond between the
two events, the more extensive will be the syntactic integration of the two propositions into a
single clause." Three major verb classes populate the scale: **modality verbs** (want, try, finish —
subject of main and complement must corefer), **manipulation verbs** (make, tell, ask — complement
subject corefers with main-clause object/manipulee), and **P-C-U verbs** (perception-cognition-
utterance: see, know, say — no coreference requirement at all).

Several semantic sub-dimensions independently correlate with position on the scale and are each
stated as a probabilistic (not absolute) implicational inference: **implicativity** (does the main
clause's truth entail the complement's truth — "make X shave" entails "X shaved," but "ask X to
shave" doesn't); **temporal contiguity/co-temporality** (the two events are more likely construed as
one complex event the more temporally adjacent they are, p.7); **control/agentivity of the manipulee**
(the more successful the manipulation, the less control/choice the manipulee retains, p.8);
**direct contact vs. spatial separation** between manipulator and manipulee (`make`/`want` favor
direct contact, `have`/`wish` tolerate separation, p.11–12); **resistance and coercive effort**
(`force` implies the manipulee is capable of resisting, p.13); and **referential cohesion** (the more
two events share referents, the more likely they're construed and coded as one event — subject
coreference > any coreference > no coreference is itself a ranked hierarchy, p.15–16, 30).

Four independent **grammatical coding devices** realize the scale syntactically (§7.3, table (60),
p.23): (a) **co-lexicalization/verb adjacency** — only `let-go` achieves true verb-adjacency,
effectively merging into a single lexical verb (the "proximity principle": closer meaning →
closer code-level adjacency, p.24); (b) **case-marking of the complement subject**, tracking the
hierarchy genitive (`let-go of him`) > direct object (`made him go`) > subject (`wished that he
would go`), correlating with the semantic-role hierarchy AGT>DAT>PAT>OTHERS (p.25); (c) **verb
morphology/degree of finiteness**, a scale from least-finite to most-finite: lexical-nominal >
bare-stem > -ing-infinitive > to-infinitive > modal > aspectual > tense (p.27, table (70)); (d)
**physical separation** via the subordinator `that` (used only at the higher, unified-intonation
part of the scale) or an intonational pause (used only at the lowest part, between indirect- and
direct-quote). A **unified implicational prediction** governs all four: the lower a main verb sits
on the scale, the more separation devices apply, and separation is cumulative down the scale
(p.28).

#### Tense agreement and quotation (§7.2.4–7.2.6)

Cognition verbs (`know`, `think`) require obligatory tense agreement between main and complement
clause (an "integrated perspective"); utterance verbs (`say`) appear to tolerate tense mismatch, but
Givón argues this is because such mismatched complements are reanalyzed as **direct quotes** rather
than genuine tense violations (p.20–21). The final step down the scale — from **indirect quote** to
**direct quote** — is a complete "de-coupling of point-of-view": personal pronouns, spatial
demonstratives, and time adverbs inside a direct quote are reinterpreted relative to the quoted
speaker's original speech situation rather than the current narrator's, opening "another universe
of discourse" nested inside the main clause's (p.21–22).

#### Modality verbs (§7.4)

Modality verbs (p.28–33) obligatorily obey the equi-subject condition (except at the bottom of
their own sub-scale, where they shade into preference verbs like `wish` — the equi-subject
condition is then relaxed). Their scale runs: nominalized complement (`finished her work`) > -ing-
infinitive (`finished building`) > to-infinitive (`wanted to build`) > how-to complement (`knew how
to build`) > modal complement (`wished that he would build`). A `how-to` complement contrasts with a
plain to-infinitive in encoding "know-how" rather than actual accomplishment (`remembered how to
lock the house` does not entail locking it, unlike `remembered to lock the house`, p.32).

#### For-to, conditional, and WH-complements (§7.5–7.7)

**For-to complements** mark an intermediate scale position: the complement subject is coded as the
main clause's benefactive object with `for`, and the complement verb is a to-infinitive; verbs
higher or lower on the scale reject the pattern. Direct vs. indirect order/request/manipulation
consistently correlates with plain-infinitive vs. for-to complementation (`told him to wait` =
direct order entailing compliance-expectation; `said for him to wait` = indirect, p.33–35).
**Conditional complements** (`if`/`whether`) occur only with low-certainty P-C-U verbs (`doubt`,
`wonder`); high-certainty verbs reject them in the affirmative but accept them under negation or
question, since negative/interrogative are themselves non-fact modalities (p.35–36). Fronting an
if-complement requires replacing `if` with the more formal `whether` (p.36). **WH-complements**
(`know where she is`) superficially resemble WH-questions but, unlike questions, never invert
subject and auxiliary (p.36–37).

#### Post-posed P-C-U verbs and non-verbal predicates (§7.8–7.9)

In informal spoken English, P-C-U verbs frequently appear **post-posed** after their complement
clause (`He's back, I hear`), with the subject pronoun tightly restricted per verb (only `I hear`,
not `*you hear`/`*they hear`). In this position the verb no longer carries the main informational
focus — it functions as an **epistemic/evidential quantifier** over the complement's truth status
(direct witness, hearsay, inference, doubt), and the conventionalized subject pronoun is frequently
dropped in rapid speech (`(I) guess`, `(I'm) afraid`, p.37–38). Adjectives and even nouns can occupy
the same syntactic frames as modality verbs (`was able to`, `was reluctant to`) or P-C-U verbs
(`was aware that`, p.38). A subclass of these non-verbal predicates is **factive**, presupposing the
truth of their complement (`was sorry to leave` presupposes leaving, p.39); another subclass
(`eager` vs. `easy`/`hard`) looks superficially identical but reverses which argument is semantically
the agent of the complement — revealed by paraphrase tests (`(for one) to please Mary is eager` is
ungrammatical, but `(for one) to please Mary is easy` is fine, p.39–40) and by whether the predicate
tolerates dummy-`it` subject-postposing (`It is easy/hard...` vs. `*It is eager...`, p.40–41).

### Ch.8 Voice and De-transitivization

#### Semantic vs. pragmatic transitivity, and the three functional dimensions of voice (§8.2)

Transitivity has both a **semantic** definition (prototypical agent, affected patient, compact/
bounded/realis verb, p.46) and a **pragmatic** one: the same semantically-transitive event can be
coded from more than one **perspective** depending on the relative **topicality** of its agent and
patient participants. Three voice types are defined by this topicality contrast (table (88), p.78):
active (agent most topical, nothing suppressed), passive (patient >> agent, agent pragmatically
suppressed), antipassive (agent >> patient, patient pragmatically suppressed).

De-transitive voice has three independent functional dimensions (§8.2.3): **agent demotion**
(the agent may be unknown, anaphorically/cataphorically predictable, stereotypical, universal/
generic, or deliberately suppressed to avoid culpability — Givón catalogs six distinct discourse
motivations for passive agent omission, p.48–49, each with an attested-text illustration); **non-
agent promotion** (a non-agent becomes the default topic once the agent is demoted — realized
through four promotional constructions: BE-passive, GET-passive, middle voice, and "potential
middle," p.50–51); and **verb stativization** (an agent-initiated, bounded process gets re-framed
as a resulting state — demonstrated by manipulation verbs' systematic rejection of stative/passive
complements, e.g. `*told him to be asleep`, `*made the wood be chopped`, p.52).

A cross-genre frequency study (table (18), p.53) shows passives at only 4–18% of main-clause
declaratives, highest in academic prose (18%, abstract/non-human topics) and lowest in sports
writing (4%, concrete human-agent action) — attributed to the conflation of "agents are usually
human" + "humans mostly talk about humans" + "the subject slot favors the most topical referent."

#### Three syntactic coding devices and the BE-passive's functional bind (§8.4)

De-transitive clauses are built from three syntactic devices (table (19), p.53–54) which are
isomorphic to the three functional dimensions above: treatment of the non-topical agent (demoted
down SUBJ > DO > IO, including full absence), treatment of the topical non-agent (promoted up the
same hierarchy, including full subjecthood), and treatment of the verb (active-transitive vs.
stative-intransitive morphology). The **promotional BE-passive** and the **non-promotional
impersonal-subject clause** sit at opposite extremes of an implicational cluster (20): full
promotion of the non-agent correlates with full demotion/absence of the agent and full
stativization of the verb, versus none of the above.

English's nominative case system creates a **functional bind** for promotional passives (25):
because the grammatical subject is morphologically unmarked for semantic role, a promoted non-agent
can't simultaneously carry both its old semantic-role marking and new subject marking. The
BE-passive resolves this by marking pragmatic role (subjecthood) and dropping semantic-role marking
— producing the general restriction that **only the direct object of the active can become subject
of the BE-passive** (27), narrowly relaxed only for a handful of high-specificity ditransitive verbs
(`give`, `show`, `tell`, `bring`, `send`, `teach`) whose dative argument is unambiguous enough to
promote without confusion — and even then, promotion degrades as both arguments become more
semantically similar (both human, or both non-human; a long worked sequence of minimal pairs with
`send`, `bring`, `cure`, `wash`, `cook`, `build`, `type`, `tamper with`, `load` traces exactly where
this restriction relaxes and tightens, pp.59–63).

#### The GET-passive (§8.5.2)

The GET-passive is historically recent (written attestations from the late 18th century) and traced
through a documented multi-step grammaticalization path: `get` (obtain) → bi-transitive locative
`get` → causative-locative → unrestricted causative → causative-with-BE-passive-complement →
reflexive-causative → intransitive-inchoative → morphologically simplified GET-passive (schematic
chains (55)–(57), p.65–66). Unlike the BE-passive, where the demoted agent retains logical
control/intent even when absent, the **GET-passive vests agentive control in the promoted patient-
subject itself** — shown by purpose-adverb scope (`John got shot by Mary deliberately` → John, not
Mary, acted deliberately, p.67), compatibility with command-embedding (`told him to get fired` is
fine, `*told him to be fired` is not, p.68), and a documented 9:1 skew toward human subjects versus
the BE-passive's roughly even 54:46 split (Herold 1986 data, table (66), p.69). Even with inanimate
subjects, GET-passives extend "involvement/responsibility" to some associated human — the subject's
possessor or even the hearer (`My cache of marijuana got found by Fido` implies the speaker was
remiss, p.69). Corpus counts (Herold 1986) show the GET-passive skews strongly **adversative**
(82% of tokens vs. 40% for BE-passive, table (70)) and strongly **colloquial/working-class** (49%
of working-class passive tokens are GET vs. only 17% of upper-class tokens, table (71), p.70) —
a textbook case of a construction's distributional profile revealing its register and discourse
function.

#### Impersonal-subject clauses (§8.5.3)

Impersonal `one`/`you`/`they` subjects are non-referring versions of the same referring pronouns,
distinguished by three independent dimensions: **modality** (`one`/`you` favor habitual-irrealis
readings; `they` alone comfortably covers specific past-realis events, p.72–73); **(in)transitivity
restrictions** — unlike the BE-passive, impersonal clauses are unrestricted, occurring with
active, stative, passive, transitive, or intransitive verbs alike (p.73); and **register** — `you`
and `they` skew colloquial, `one` skews formal/educated/written (illustrated with a detective-novel
passage alternating `you` and `one` depending on narrative register, p.74).

#### Middle voice (§8.5.4)

Givón treats middle voice not as a single clean grammatical category (citing Jespersen 1924's
skepticism about its coherence) but as a family of related constructions sharing four criteria
(79): inherently transitive verb, patient subject, no discernible responsible agent, and — crucially
— *not* used primarily as an agent-demoting device (distinguishing it from the passive/impersonal).
Three parallel grammaticalization paths (GET-locative, GET-passive, GET-adjective) each run
causative → reflexive → intransitive-middle (schemas (80)–(82), p.75–76), and middle-voice
intransitives (`the glass broke`) pattern alongside adjectival-lexical passives (`was broken`),
potential adjectives (`breakable`), and "potential middles" (`breaks real easy`) — all reject overt
agent mention even where a BE-passive of the same verb would allow it (`*the window broke by John`
vs. `the window was broken by John`, p.76).

#### The antipassive voice (§8.6)

English has no single dedicated antipassive construction, but the antipassive *function* — patient
demotion while the agent stays maximally topical — is realized through several iconic syntactic
devices: **object deletion** (`Mary ate [the apple]` → `Mary ate`, generic/habitual object implied,
p.78–79), **object incorporation** into a nominalized verb phrase (`hunted the deer` → `went
deer-hunting`; incorporated antipassives are markedly better as nominalizations than as finite verbs,
p.79), and **manner/instrument incorporation** (`fished with a fly-rod` → `went fly-fishing`, p.80).
Semantically, antipassive devices systematically violate the prototypical-transitive-event
requirements for a **salient patient** and a **realis** event: plural/un-individuated objects
(`read books` vs. `read the book`), non-referring objects (`buy any magazine`), and non-fact
modalities (habitual, negative, irrealis: `constantly reads`, `never read`, `would rather read`) all
independently correlate with antipassive-like readings (§8.6.3, p.80–81).

#### Reciprocal constructions (§8.7)

A reciprocal clause (`the woman and the child saw each other`) is semantically and syntactically
distinct from two merely-conjoined simple clauses: it requires the two underlying events to be
roughly simultaneous and mutually related (disjointed-time/location/circumstance conjoined clauses
do not paraphrase as reciprocals, p.83, though some event types — e.g. ongoing correspondence
despite geographic separation — tolerate a reciprocal reading even under spatial/temporal
disjunction, p.83–84). Reciprocalization is constrained by verb semantics: both arguments must be
of a type the verb's subject and object slots jointly tolerate, so intransitive/objectless verbs
(`*slept each other`) and verbs with radically mismatched subject/object selectional restrictions
(`*John and the house built each other`) resist it (p.85–86), while maximally permissive or
inherently relational verbs (`resemble`, `stand near`) reciprocalize freely, modulo a pragmatic
constraint that reciprocation of asymmetric-importance pairs (a human standing next to a house) is
blocked (p.86). English contrasts a **"light" pattern** (no overt marking, used only with
inherently-reciprocal verbs like `kiss`, `meet`, `fight`, `correspond`) against a **"heavy" pattern**
(obligatory `each other`, available with any verb, and able to additionally distinguish simultaneous
from sequential sub-events where the light pattern cannot, p.87–88). A plural/conjoined subject
without a reciprocal pronoun can also code mere **joint action** rather than true reciprocity, and
this reading is lexically idiosyncratic per verb (`fought regularly` = reciprocal; `fought to save
their marriage` = joint action, not reciprocal; `wrote regularly` is ambiguous among reciprocal,
antipassive, and joint readings, p.88–89).

#### Reflexive clauses (§8.8)

Three reflexive construction types share the general definition "the same referent is mentioned
twice in the same clause" (122): the **simple reflexive** (object position, `saw herself`), the
**emphatic reflexive** (subject- or less commonly object-emphasis/contrast, `Joe himself saw Mary`),
and the **possessive reflexive** (`own`, restricted to counter-normative/contrastive contexts,
`killed her own mother` vs. neutral `killed her mother`, p.98). Simple reflexivization shows the
same verb-semantics restrictions as reciprocals (no objectless verbs, no radically mismatched
subject/object types, p.90–91) and is more natural with less-prototypically-transitive verbs (dative/
associative objects rather than salient affected patients, p.91–92); with highly stative,
promiscuously-selecting verbs, reflexives shade semantically into middle-voice or passive readings
(`His suggestion proved itself useful` ≈ `proved useful` ≈ `was proven useful`, p.92), and this
affinity is traced historically through the same three GET-constructions as middle voice (p.93–94).
Emphatic reflexives historically derive from the same pronoun set used for ordinary anaphoric
objects in Chaucerian/Shakespearean English (`[he] born hym well`), with the dedicated `-self` forms
spreading first into emphatic/unexpected contexts and only later generalizing (p.94); their core
function is marking an unexpected or norm-violating contrast, strongly preferring subject position
over object position (`Joe himself saw Mary` >> `?Joe saw Mary herself`, p.95), with placement
options directly after the subject, after an auxiliary, or clause-finally, each sensitive to
syntactic distance and embedding complexity (§8.8.3.3, p.96–98). All three reflexive types are
strictly **clause-bound**: the reflexive/possessive-reflexive pronoun's antecedent must be the
subject (or, for the simple/emphatic type, the relevant grammatical role) of the *same* clause, not
a matrix clause across an embedding boundary — a constraint Givón uses as a diagnostic probe for
clause-boundary location itself, including a documented exception where a doubly subject-like
referent (simultaneously matrix subject and complement-internal possessor-of-subject) licenses an
otherwise-blocked cross-clause possessive-reflexive reading (pp.99–101).

## Grammar points

### Case marks grammatical function, not fixed word position
Unlike PDE, where nouns carry no case inflection and word order alone signals grammatical role,
every OE noun phrase's **case** (nominative, accusative, dative, genitive — no vocative, unlike
Latin) signals its syntactic function regardless of its position in the clause. The present-day
possessive *'s* looks superficially like a surviving genitive case but is analyzed as a **clitic**
attached to a whole preceding noun phrase (*the woman down the street's cat* marks possession on
the final noun of the phrase, *street*, not the actual possessor, *woman*) — genuinely different
behavior from the true OE genitive inflection, which always attaches to the possessing noun itself.

### The four cases' core and extended functions
**Nominative** marks the subject (and, since OE lacks a vocative, direct address too).
**Accusative** marks the direct object, including "double accusative" constructions (both direct
and indirect object in the accusative) and — a usage with no PDE equivalent — extent of place or
time (*ealne weġ* "all the way", lit. accusative). **Genitive** covers roughly the same ground as
PDE *'s*/*of* but adds uses with no modern equivalent: the **genitive of measure** (*fīf nihta
fyrst* "a five-night period"), the fossilized **adverbial genitive** (*dæġes and nihtes* "by day
and night"), and genitive direct objects with certain verbs (of depriving, rejoicing). **Dative**
most often functions as the indirect object (far more frequently than *tō* + noun, which is mostly
reserved for verbs of speaking with an animate addressee), but also marks **possession** (especially
of body parts, e.g. *him þæt hēafod* "his head", lit. "to him the head" — compare French *à la
main*), a residual **instrumental** sense (means/cause/manner/accompaniment, largely merged into the
dative by this period but still visible in idioms like *hwȳ*/*þȳ* above), and direct objects of a
specific closed class of verbs that take dative rather than accusative objects (occasionally with a
real meaning contrast depending on which case a given verb takes, e.g. *folgian* "follow" vs.
"pursue").

### Prepositional government and the accusative/dative split
Prepositions "govern" the case of their object the way verbs govern objects. Most take the dative;
a few regularly take the accusative (*ġeond, þurh, ymbe, oþ*) or genitive (*andlang*). Many
prepositions can take either case, with a systematic meaning difference closely paralleling German:
**accusative for motion** through time/space, **dative for rest/stasis** — a distinction already
showing signs of erosion within the attested OE period.

### Concord (agreement)
Subject-verb agreement works largely as in PDE (number/person match), with similar exception types
(a compound subject taking a singular verb when treated as one idea; *hit*/*þæt*/*hwæt* triggering
plural agreement with a plural complement much like PDE's "what you need are tools"). Within the
noun phrase, demonstratives and adjectives agree with their head noun in case, number, *and*
gender — a genuine extra agreement dimension absent from PDE, which survives into PDE only
vestigially (*this~these*). **Grammatical vs. natural (semantic) gender conflict** sometimes
surfaces even in OE: a feminine-gender noun (*sǣ* "sea") referred back to with a feminine pronoun
is simple grammatical agreement, but where natural gender overrides grammatical gender (a woman
referred to by a feminine pronoun despite her noun being grammatically neuter, as with *wīf*) this
is read as an early harbinger of PDE's fully natural-gender pronoun system.

### Tense, aspect, and the two OE tenses
OE morphologically distinguishes only **present** and **past** tense — there is no dedicated future.
Futurity is instead expressed by the simple present (often with an adverb like *nū* "now"), very
occasionally by *willan*/*sċeal* in a genuinely future-referring sense (mostly confined to literal
Latin-influenced translation), or in reported speech by *sċeolde* "should" marking "future in the
past". **Aspect** (how, not when, an action unfolds) is expressed periphrastically, just as in PDE,
but the choice of auxiliary for perfective aspect splits by transitivity exactly as in German:
**transitive verbs use *habban* + past participle** (ancestor of PDE *have*-perfect), **intransitive
verbs use *bēon* + past participle** (a construction PDE has lost almost entirely, though German
and Dutch retain it). The inflected/uninflected status of the past participle in these
constructions (inflected = closer to an adjective; uninflected = closer to today's invariant
participle) is shown shifting toward uninflected across the attested period — one visible signpost
of the broader shift from adjectival to verbal participial morphology. *Bēon* + present participle
(the ancestor of PDE progressive *be + -ing*) also occurs in OE, alongside a competing periphrasis
using *wolde* + infinitive for habitual past aspect — again, a construction with no modern survival.

### Voice: periphrastic passive, *weorðan*, and *man*
With the single exception of *hātte*/*hātton* (see `pt02`), OE has **no morphological passive** —
passive meaning is built periphrastically with *bēon* + past participle (as in PDE) or, in a
usage with no PDE counterpart, with *weorðan* "become" + past participle. The *bēon* vs. *weorðan*
choice loosely tracks a **stative vs. dynamic** meaning split (tendency, not an absolute rule, and
individual authors vary in how strictly they observe it); this entire alternation disappears once
*weorðan* itself is lost from English, though cognate verbs (Dutch *worden*, German *werden*)
preserve the same active/passive-auxiliary split today, and PDE *get*-passives ("she got fired")
are proposed as the nearest functional present-day parallel. A separate, unrelated passive-
avoidance strategy uses the indefinite pronoun **man** "one" as a generic active subject (see
Vocabulary above) — ordinary and frequent, unlike PDE *one*, which now reads as markedly formal.

### Mood: indicative default, subjunctive uses, imperative
The **indicative** is the default/elsewhere mood. The **subjunctive**'s single unifying principle:
it marks that **the speaker is not vouching for the factual status** of what's said — this is
distinct from actively claiming something is false. Confirmed subjunctive contexts: genuinely
counterfactual conditions (as in PDE *if I were you*), clauses of condition and concession, **reported
speech** (marking "this is what was claimed, not vouched-for fact" — note this can co-occur with an
indicative verb later in the same sentence once the reported claim's subject becomes grammatically
distant enough that strict concord breaks down, called **distance concord**), wishes, and clauses
after verbs of doubt/possibility. The **imperative** has ordinary 2nd person singular/plural forms
plus a dedicated **1st person plural** form, *uton* (see Vocabulary), functionally identical to
PDE *let's*; 3rd-person "exhortations" instead use the subjunctive, with no dedicated imperative
form at all. The **inflected infinitive** (*tō* + a special infinitive form ending *-enne/-anne*)
expresses purpose, necessity, or completion, and can function as subject or complement of a clause —
broadly parallel to PDE's *to*-infinitive, which descends from exactly this construction.

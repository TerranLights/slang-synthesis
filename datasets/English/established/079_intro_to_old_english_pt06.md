## Grammar points

### Chronology: change visible within the attested OE period itself
Although continuous OE prose only begins around Alfred's reign (c. 900), two specific morphological
changes are traceable as live, in-progress drift across the available span of West Saxon texts:
(1) weak class 1 verbs with light stems in *-r-* (*nerian*-type, see `pt02`) show increasing
adoption of class 2 inflections even within the Ælfric generation, a shift reinforced by essentially
all *new* verbs (including loan verbs) entering class 2 rather than class 1; (2) the dative plural
ending *-um* increasingly gives way to *-an* in later texts — individually trivial, but critical
because in the **N-declension** specifically this collapses a previously distinctive case form,
constituting one of the very first concrete steps toward the general loss of noun case distinctions
completed in Middle English. Both changes are tied to a broader **phonological drift of all
unstressed vowels toward schwa**, which in turn (by merging the plural indicative *-on* and
subjunctive *-en* endings as both became *-en*) directly promotes the eventual loss of a
distinctive subjunctive mood.

### Prose style: from Alfred's "additive" style to Ælfric/Wulfstan's rhetorical prose
The earliest substantial OE prose (Alfred's own generation) reads as structurally "clumsy" by later
standards — simple clause-after-clause addition with little subordinate or rhetorical structuring —
but this reflects a real **absence of any pre-existing native prose tradition** to draw on (only
Latin prose and native poetry existed as models), not a deficiency of the language itself. By the
later 10th century, Ælfric and Wulfstan — near-contemporaries, both fluent in Latin rhetoric, but
different in background (Ælfric a teacher/private scholar, Wulfstan a statesman-bishop) — develop a
genuinely native **rhythmical prose style**, deliberately borrowing poetry's own two structural
devices (alliteration and two-stress phrasing) into prose for rhetorical effect, usually credited
as Ælfric's innovation. Wulfstan in particular also leans heavily on word-formation itself
(extensive compounding/affixation, cf. `pt05`'s Wulfstan vocabulary) as a rhetorical resource.

### Poetic meter: not iambic, not rhymed
OE poetry's metrical system is fundamentally different from the Chaucer-onward English tradition:
**no iambic pentameter**, and rhyme is rare enough that one poem being rhymed (*The Rhyming Poem*)
is itself noteworthy. Instead: each **long line** divides into two **half-lines**, each independently
containing exactly **two fully-stressed syllables**, with the *number* and position of unstressed
syllables around them left comparatively free — a stress-counting rather than syllable-counting
template, which the author notes partially survives in English nursery rhyme meter ("Humpty Dumpty
sat on a wall") even though it vanished from the formal literary tradition. Eduard Sievers'
19th-century system classifies every half-line into one of **five types (A–E)** by the
falling/rising pattern of full stress (/), secondary stress (\\), and unstressed (x) syllables (A:
/x/x, B: x/x/, C: x//x, D: /\x, E: /\x/). **Anacrusis** is an extra unstressed syllable tacked onto
the front of an otherwise-standard half-line, most common before type A. **Resolution** is a
metrical rule allowing two light syllables together to count as a single heavy stressed position —
directly parallel, the author notes, to the morphological heavy/light-stem distinction already
established in noun and verb paradigms (`pt02`/`pt03`).

### Alliteration: rules governing which syllables and sounds count
**Alliteration** links two or three stressed syllables across a long line via identical initial
consonant sound (not spelling). The structural rule: the **first stressed syllable of the second
half-line** sets the alliterating sound, and it must be matched by the **stronger** of the first
half-line's two stressed syllables (nouns/adjectives/infinitives/participles outrank verbs/adverbs
as "stronger"); the second half-line's *second* stressed syllable essentially never participates.
Consonant-cluster rule: ordinarily only the initial consonant of a cluster need match, **except**
for the clusters `sc-`, `sp-`, `st-`, which alliterate only with the full identical cluster, not
with any other `s`-initial word — a pattern the author connects to independent evidence that
`/s/` + voiceless stop behaves as a single indivisible phonological unit in both OE and PDE more
generally. **Any vowel alliterates with any other vowel** (not just identical vowels) — explained,
controversially and not fully resolved even by the author, as evidence of an unmarked **initial
glottal stop** before vowel-initial stressed syllables (paralleled in nonstandard PDE pronunciations
and in modern Dutch/German). A separate open puzzle: /j/ (spelled `<ġ>`) and /g/ alliterate with
each other in *Beowulf* despite being phonemically distinct sounds by that point — read as likely
evidence of an **earlier historical stage** when the two had not yet split into separate phonemes,
rather than true synchronic alliteration-by-spelling.

### Dialects: West Saxon dominance is a textual-survival artifact, not linguistic reality
~90% of surviving OE *poetry* specifically comes down to us copied into West Saxon-region
manuscripts regardless of a given poem's true dialect of composition (explicitly true of *Beowulf*
itself, which shows strong internal evidence of a more northerly compositional origin) — so West
Saxon's apparent textual dominance substantially overstates its real linguistic centrality, a point
the author stresses matters for understanding *later* English history: West Saxon was **not** the
ancestor of the later standard language, which instead derives from London/East Midlands usage, a
region for which the OE period itself preserves frustratingly little direct textual evidence.
Four traditional dialect areas: **West Saxon** (Winchester/Thames Valley, this book's teaching
basis); **Kentish** (Canterbury-centered; genuinely distinctive texts only survive to c. 900, later
Kentish texts show increasing West-Saxon mixture, so a clear independent Kentish picture resumes
only in Middle English); **Northumbrian** (the whole area from Edinburgh to the Mersey/Humber in
principle, but the surviving texts all cluster narrowly around Durham — the *Lindisfarne Gospels*'
interlinear OE gloss being the single best-known Northumbrian source, showing some of the earliest
visible Viking-contact effects and morphological changes anywhere in OE); **Mercian** (the hardest
dialect to characterize — the traditional geographic label "between Mersey/Humber and Thames" is
actively misleading, since the real surviving Mercian texts cluster narrowly around Lichfield, not
the unevidenced East Midlands heartland that matters most for later Standard English history).
"Lichfield Mercian" develops its own distinct, independently attested literary tradition by c. 800,
well before comparable West Saxon literary prose exists, and the author's conclusion is that OE had
**(at least) two genuinely "focussed" varieties, not one standard with regional deviations** — a
**focussed language** being a norm speakers in a shared, literate community converge on by
tendency, not a fixed standard imposed/enforced by an external authority (contrasted explicitly
with how PDE's own prescriptive standard operates).

### Looking back: the deep (Indo-European/Germanic) roots of OE structure
The book's closing retrospective traces several OE features past Germanic to Indo-European itself:
the case/declension system and the whole Ablaut phenomenon both ultimately derive from
Indo-European patterning, drastically reorganized along the way into what by the OE period reads
as a basically Germanic (not visibly Indo-European) system. OE's alliterative poetic tradition is
shown to be *shared*, not uniquely English: it is directly paralleled in continental **Old Saxon**
poetry (which also shares some of OE's specific compounding-heavy poetic diction, evidence of a
genuinely common inherited poetic *mechanism*, not mere typological coincidence), and the author
draws a further, looser comparative parallel to other attested **oral poetic traditions** within
the wider Indo-European family (citing Homeric Greek and Balkan oral epic) as evidence that OE's
poetic structure, however alien it looks against the later English literary tradition, is itself
an ordinary, well-attested type of human oral-poetic system.

### Looking forward: how declension loss and word-order change actually unfolded
The book models concretely how the already-underway schwa-merger and dative-plural *-um→-an* shift
(see "Chronology" above) cascade into full case-system collapse: once every unstressed vowel is
schwa, the **feminine** and **N-declensions** lose essentially all distinctive case marking (leaving
only a singular/plural contrast, and even that only via the spreading *-an*), forcing both
declensions to merge functionally into the comparatively still-intact **masculine** declension
pattern — which is why that pattern becomes the sole regular declension in later English. A further
independent sound change (loss of a final unstressed *-n*, already visible dialectally within OE
itself in Northumbrian, e.g. *foldu* above) combined with compensatory vowel-lengthening in short
stressed syllables completes the chain, worked through explicitly step-by-step for *nama*→*name*
(nama → namə → namə → naːmə → naːm). On **word order**, the book is explicit that the shift to
strict SVO is **not simply explained by loss of case inflection alone** — several word-order
changes (the general spread of verb-second order; the increasing preference for heavy elements,
including relative clauses, to appear clause-finally; the shift of the object to appear after the
*whole* verb group rather than just after the first verb) were already independently underway
*before* the case system had actually collapsed, so case-loss is at most one contributing factor
among several, not a sole prime mover. The author also directly tests, and substantially rejects,
the popular assumption that the **Viking and Norman invasions directly caused** these structural
changes: the relevant drift is shown to have begun well before either contact event reached its
full linguistic influence. **Viking contact** (via sustained close Anglo-Scandinavian bilingual
communities in the Danelaw, plausibly producing something like a **creole situation** with its
typical grammatical simplification) is judged the more *structurally* influential of the two,
contributing core-vocabulary items including function words (*they*, replacing native *hī*) and
even some prepositional-usage shifts (*at*, *with*) alongside the dative-case form *aron*→*are*
already covered. **Norman French's influence**, by contrast, is argued to be comparatively *shallow
structurally* (far less bilingual community contact than with Scandinavian, so no comparable creole-
style simplification pressure) even though it is by far the larger and more *lexically* visible
influence — concentrated overwhelmingly in vocabulary (word-formation shifting toward French/Latin-
style borrowing instead of native compounding/affixation) and in register/stylistic doublets
(*kingly* vs. *royal*; the book's own closing example, *ending* vs. *conclusion*) rather than in
core grammar.

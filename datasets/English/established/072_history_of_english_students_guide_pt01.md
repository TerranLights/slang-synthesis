# English — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: *The History of English: A Student's Guide* (Ishtla Singh, 2005), Ch.1–2

**Source:** Ishtla Singh, *The History of English: A Student's Guide* (Hodder Education, 2005), ISBN
978-0-340-80695-1. Chapter 1 "English as a Changing Language" (pp.5–38) and Chapter 2 "Language
Families and the Pre-history of English" (pp.39–65). Clean PDF text layer confirmed — vision reading
not required for this dispatch (pages were read as rendered images for layout fidelity, but the
underlying source has a genuine text layer; no font-substitution cipher issues observed).

**Coverage note:** this is a survey/history text, not a drill-based teaching grammar — nearly every
cited example word is unique and illustrates a distinct point, so there is very little "redundant
drill" content to skip per the coverage rule. What follows is a representative, non-exhaustive
sample of the cited example forms (the chapter cites far more proper-noun/brand-name examples in
places than are reproduced below) plus every distinct process/mechanism discussed. Chapter 2 is
mostly historiographical (the 18th–20th-century scholarly search for Proto-Indo-European and
genetic language classification) rather than English-specific vocabulary/grammar content, so its
vocabulary table is thin — its real value is the First Germanic Consonant Shift (Grimm's Law) and
Verner's Law content, captured under Grammar points. **Attested Era is tagged per the historical
period the cited form or change actually belongs to** (Old English / Middle English / Early Modern
English / Modern English / Proto-Indo-European / Proto-Germanic), not uniformly "contemporary" —
this whole book's value for the project is tracking change through time, per the dispatch brief.
No marginalia issue — clean text layer throughout.

## Grammar points

### Sound change: conditioned vs. unconditioned; assimilation, dissimilation, epenthesis, aphaeresis, apocope, syncope, haplology, metathesis

Sound change can affect individual segments (conditioned = restricted to specific phonetic
environments; unconditioned = affects all occurrences of a sound) or whole classes of sounds at
once (a large-scale "shift," e.g. the First Germanic Consonant Shift, the Great Vowel Shift). Among
segment-level changes: **assimilation** (one sound becomes more/fully like a neighboring one —
anticipatory/regressive when the conditioning sound follows, progressive/perseverative when it
precedes, and can be distant if intervening segments separate the two); **dissimilation** (the
opposite — sounds become less alike, sporadic rather than regular); **epenthesis** (insertion of a
segment, typically a vowel breaking up a consonant cluster); **aphaeresis** (loss of a word-initial
segment); **apocope** (loss of a final vowel); **syncope** (loss of a medial vowel); **haplology**
(loss of a whole repeated syllable); and **metathesis** (reordering of adjacent segments). (pp.6–7)

### The Southern Vowel Shift and Northern California Vowel Shift (American English)

Two ongoing, large-scale American vowel shifts illustrate unconditioned sound change in progress.
The **Southern Vowel Shift** (Southern US states/South Midland) involves the short front vowels
/ɪ, ɛ, æ/ shifting forward/upward with a glide quality, long /aɪ/ monophthongizing and fronting, and
long /iː/, /uː/ moving toward mid-center — the shift is reportedly proceeding faster in rural areas.
The **Northern California Vowel Shift** involves lowering/backing of short front vowels and fronting
of back vowels; Eckert (2002) found some adolescent/pre-teen girls deploying its pronunciations
performatively to construct social personas (e.g. "tough Chicana" vs. "heterosexual drama
princess"). (pp.7–9)

### Lexical change: word-loss, semantic resurrection, and word-formation processes

Lexical change is not uniform across the whole lexicon — closed classes (prepositions, conjunctions)
and highly entrenched "core" open-class words (*mother, father, boy, run, be*, etc.) resist change far
more than content words generally. Processes discussed: **word loss**; **semantic resurrection**
(an obsolete word repurposed with new meaning, e.g. *hand grenade*, *dugout*, *broadcast*,
*radiator*); **conversion** (a word gains a new lexical-category function without formal change,
e.g. noun *curry* → verb *curry*); **compounding** (noun+noun, adj+adj, verb+noun, adj+noun,
prep+adj, prep+verb; the final element is normally the head and determines part-of-speech, with rare
dual-headed or **exocentric** compounds like *hatchback*, *bigwig* that denote neither element
literally); **derivation/affixation** (productivity varies and can cycle in and out of fashion, e.g.
*-wise*); **clipping** (extraction of a shortened autonomous word, e.g. *phone* < *telephone*,
distinct from compound-clippings like *sitcom* where the clipped element like *rom-*/*sat-* cannot
stand alone); **blending** (combination+clipping of two words, e.g. *brunch*, *smog*, *motel*);
**acronym** (pronounceable as a word, e.g. *AIDS*, *SARS*) vs. **initialism** (pronounced letter by
letter, e.g. *FBI*, *CD*) — both can lexicalize to the point of lowercase spelling (*laser*, *radar*,
*scuba*); **genericization of trademarks** (*Kleenex*, *hoover*, *xerox*); **back-formation**
(removing a supposed affix to create a "base" form that never actually existed independently, e.g.
*editor*→*edit*, on the false analogy of *teach*→*teacher*); and **re-analysis** (a more general
process than back-formation, where a word's structure is reinterpreted to yield "a new morpheme for
use in coining other words," e.g. *bikini* → *kini*/*mono-*/*tan-*; *hamburger* → *burger*).
Finally, word-formation can simply be **coinage** (making up a new word outright, e.g.
Shakespeare's *laughable*/*moonbeam*). (pp.9–14)

### Borrowing: adoption vs. adaptation, prestige vs. necessity/cultural borrowing

Borrowing requires some degree of bilingualism in the contact situation and always involves a
perceived "**projected gain**" for the borrowing community — either **social** (prestige transfer)
or **practical/necessity-driven** (naming a new concept/object), and frequently both at once (e.g.
French loans into Middle English in prestige domains; Latin/Greek loans into Early Modern English
scientific/medical vocabulary). **Cultural borrowing** occurs even without an unequal prestige
relationship, simply when concepts/objects cross from one culture to another (e.g. English borrowing
cuisine vocabulary from migrant-minority restaurant trades: Indian, Mexican, Italian, German,
Japanese, Chinese). Borrowing from a *less* prestigious language to a more prestigious one also
occurs regularly in restricted-bilingualism/trade-contact situations (e.g. Germanic settlers taking
Celtic place names; European traders taking indigenous-language words for new flora/fauna/trade
goods even without being viewed as cultural equals of their trading partners) — McMahon's point: the
borrowing speaker need only understand (or believe they understand) the meaning of what they're
learning, not achieve fluency. Once a word is borrowed, speakers choose, often inconsistently, between
**adoption** (retaining source-language phonology/form) and **adaptation** (conforming to the
borrowing language's own patterns) — competence in the donor language, self-presentation, and
familiarity with the donor word's conventional spelling all affect this choice (e.g. English speakers'
variable pronunciations of *croissant*, *bois*, *pain au raisin*). High-bilingualism contact can let
borrowing affect more than just the lexicon — phonology (e.g. French loans helping phonemicize
English /v/, previously only a conditioned allophone of /f/) and morphology (e.g. new derivational
affixes like *-ese* from Latin *-ēnsis*) can also be affected. **Adaptation** can also produce
**grammatical re-analysis** (a borrowed language's plural marker mis-analyzed as part of the stem,
e.g. Arabic *hashshāshīn* → English singular *assassin*; Hindi *chints* → singular *chintz*) and
**calquing/loan translation** (translating a foreign compound/phrase element-by-element, e.g.
*skyscraper* → German *Wolkenkratzer*). (pp.14–19)

### Semantic change: polysemy, restriction, extension, metaphor, metonymy, synecdoche, amelioration, pejoration, euphemism

Semantic change is enabled by the arbitrariness of the sign (no inherent link between a word-form and
its meaning) and by **polysemy** (words carrying multiple related senses simultaneously, which can
shift in relative dominance over time without the older sense necessarily disappearing — e.g. *gay*,
*aggravate* retain their older senses alongside newer ones). Triggers include imperfect
inter-generational transmission of meaning (e.g. OE *(ge)bēd* 'prayer' → modern *bead*, via a child's
literal reinterpretation of a rosary-counting scene), changes in material culture (words following
technology into new domains, e.g. computing's *mouse*/*virus*/*windows*), and **grammaticalization**
(a full lexical word losing autonomous status and meaning as it becomes a grammatical element, e.g.
Latin *passus* 'step' → French negative *pas*). Introducing a loanword can also push a native word's
*other* senses into new prominence (e.g. OE *wambe*/*stōl*/*wyrm* → modern *womb*/*stool*/*worm*,
their central meanings taken over by the loans *stomach*/*throne*/*dragon*). Named patterns:
**restriction/narrowing/specialization** (meaning becomes more specific, e.g. OE *dēor* 'any animal'
→ *deer*); **extension/generalization/broadening** (meaning becomes less specific, e.g. *mill*,
*barn*, *arrive*); **metaphorization** (a word takes on a figurative sense via perceived
resemblance, e.g. body-part vocabulary applied to objects, animal-names applied to people);
**metonymy** (a term for part of an entity comes to represent the whole, e.g. *crown*/*throne* for
'sovereign', *White House* for the US presidency); **synecdoche** (the reverse — a term for the
whole stands for a part, e.g. "Rumania and China are finalists" meaning their athletes);
**amelioration** (a word gains positive connotation over time, e.g. OE *cniht* 'boy/servant' →
*knight*; *nice* < Latin *nescius* 'ignorant'); and **pejoration** (a word gains negative
connotation, e.g. OE *sælig* 'blessed' → *silly*; *vulgar*, *boor*, *lewd*; the multi-stage pejoration
of *asylum*). Pejoration is closely linked to **taboo and euphemism**: societies taboo direct
reference to unpleasant/dangerous/powerful subjects (death, excretion, certain animals, deities),
producing indirect/euphemistic terms which often then undergo pejoration themselves once the taboo
re-attaches to the new term — a cyclical process illustrated at length with toilet terminology
(*privy* → *latrine* → *lavatory* → *toilet*, each euphemistic in turn) and with deliberately
obfuscatory "**technostrategic language**" in nuclear-weapons discourse (*clean bombs*,
*collateral damage*, *daisy cutter*). (pp.19–24)

### Morphological change: re-analysis, analogy (extension, leveling, contamination, folk etymology), and morphological typology

Morphological change can occur through **re-analysis** of a word's internal structure (e.g.
*noumpere* → *umpire*, *napron* → *apron* via misdivision of the preceding indefinite article;
conversely *(an) ekename* → *nickname*, *(an) ewt* → *newt*), and through **analogy**, the broader
process whereby speakers generalize existing patterns/relations into new contexts. Analogy is
sporadic/irregular even though its *effects* can create regularity — summarized in **Sturtevant's
Paradox**: "sound change is regular but creates irregularity, whereas analogy is irregular but
creates regularity." Two systematic sub-types: **analogical extension** (a pattern is generalized to
new forms, e.g. OE's several distinct plural-marking paradigms gradually conforming to the single
*-as*/*-es*/*-s* pattern that became the regular modern English plural) and **analogical leveling**
(internal allomorphy within a single paradigm is ironed out, e.g. OE *cēosan/cēas/curon/coren* 'to
choose' leveling to modern *choose/chose/chosen*). Analogy can also apply sporadically, via
**back-formation** (*lazy* → *laze*), **contamination** (a word's form is influenced by others in the
same semantic field/list, e.g. *four* influenced by *five*; bidirectional *citeain*/*deinzein* →
*citizein*/*denizein* in Anglo-Norman), and **folk etymology** (a word's foreign/opaque origin is
reinterpreted so its boundaries align with familiar morphemes, e.g. Italian *girasole* → *Jerusalem
artichoke*). Separately, 19th-century scholars classified languages by **morphological typology**:
**isolating/analytic** (each grammatical unit is its own unattached morph, e.g. Vietnamese, Chinese);
**agglutinating** (morphs are stuck together but remain individually transparent/consistent in form,
e.g. Turkish, Basque, Swahili); and **inflecting/synthetic** (a single morph carries more than one
unit of grammatical information at once, e.g. Latin, Greek, Old English). English displays
characteristics of more than one type at once and has drifted historically from a highly inflecting
system toward greater isolating/agglutinating character; the book explicitly rejects the
19th-century "cyclical progress/decay" framing of typological change as ideologically loaded and
empirically unsupported — typological type correlates with neither a language's vitality nor its
risk of extinction. (pp.24–30)

### Syntactic change: re-analysis of modals, word-order typology and drift (OV → VO), grammaticalization

Three strands of syntactic change are covered. (1) **Re-analysis of the English modal system**:
Lightfoot (1979) argues the modern modals (*may, might, can, could, will, would, shall, should,
must*) descend from a set of Old English full verbs ("pre-modals") that, between the Old English and
late Middle English periods, lost the ability to take an inflected infinitive or a nominal object and
stopped carrying present-tense inflections — by the 16th century speakers re-analyzed them as members
of a distinct grammatical category (modals), evidenced by their inability to appear in infinitival/
non-finite form, their unique capacity (shared only with auxiliary *be/have/do*) to invert with the
subject in questions, and their unique capacity to take a following negative marker directly.
(2) **Word-order typology and drift**: languages are classified by their unmarked constituent order
(S/V/O, reduced to two broad types, VO and OV, each correlating with further ordering properties for
adjectives/genitives relative to nouns — though "typological harmony" with these correlating
properties is not absolute, e.g. English is VO but has AN, not the expected NA, adjective order).
Textual evidence (runic inscriptions, 3rd–7th c.) shows the Germanic ancestor of English was
predominantly an OV language with some SVO structures already present; by the later (better-
documented) Old English period VO had become the norm except in certain restricted structures (main
verb + object pronoun; subordinate clauses); by the late 12th century VO was fully established as
English's basic word order. This kind of gradual systemic shift is termed **drift**. (3)
**Grammaticalization** ("le passage d'un mot autonome au rôle d'élément grammatical," Meillet 1912):
a "full" lexical item (noun, verb, adjective) becomes part of a grammatical category (auxiliary,
preposition, affix), typically accompanied by phonological reduction and **semantic bleaching**.
Paradigm case: OE noun *līc* 'body' > adjectival *-lic* > fully bleached, productive adverbial/
adjectival suffix *-ly*. A messier, non-paradigmatic case (Romaine & Lange 1991, within Traugott's
1982 propositional→textual→expressive framework rather than Meillet's) is the development of
**discourse-marker** *like* (focusing a following constituent) and **quotative complementizer**
*be+like* (introducing "constructed speech/dialogue" — a speaker's own reconstruction of what was
said/thought, distinct from verbatim-reported speech framed by *said*/*told*, and capable of framing
non-verbal "constructed dialogue" like *and I was like 'Waaaa'* to represent an emotional reaction
rather than an actual utterance); the two authors argue both functions are still actively emerging
and co-existing with older uses, a genuinely bidirectional/"messy" case that doesn't fit
grammaticalization's usual unidirectional model as cleanly as *-ly* does. (pp.30–35)

### The comparative method, cognate data, and the First Germanic Consonant Shift

Chapter 2 traces the 18th–20th-century scholarly reconstruction of the Indo-European (IE) language
family — Sir William Jones' 1786 observation that Sanskrit, Greek and Latin share "too strong" an
affinity "to have been produced by accident," the establishment of **Proto-Indo-European (PIE)** as
the reconstructed common ancestor, and the **comparative method**: using systematic, repeated
sound-correspondences across **cognate** data (words related by common descent, not borrowing or
coincidence) to reconstruct unattested proto-forms and proto-languages "backwards and forwards." A
language's hypothesized **core lexicon** (kinship terms, numerals, basic body parts, natural
phenomena) was assumed most resistant to borrowing and therefore most reliable for establishing
genetic relatedness, though the book flags this "stable lexis" assumption as itself largely untested.
The best-documented and most significant sound-correspondence pattern for English's own ancestry is
the **First Germanic Consonant Shift** (**Grimm's Law**, 1822): in the transition from PIE to
proto-Germanic, voiceless plosives *p, t, k* became voiceless fricatives /f, θ, x/; voiced plosives
*b, d, g* became voiceless plosives /p, t, k/; and voiced aspirates *bh, dh, gh* became voiced
plosives /b, d, g/ (e.g. PIE-type cognates: Sanskrit/Latin *p-* ~ Gothic/OE/ON *f-*; Latin *d-* ~
Gothic/OE/ON *t-*). **Verner's Law** (Karl Verner, 1875) explained Grimm's Law's apparent exceptions
(e.g. Latin *pater*/*mater* ~ English *father*/*mother* with /ð/, not the expected /θ/): the shift to
a voiced fricative occurred specifically when the consonant was word-medial, between two voiced
sounds, and the preceding syllable was historically unstressed — all three conditions hold for
*father* (cf. OE *fæder*, ON *faðir*, Gothic *fadar*). A reconstructed language's "proto-lexicon" can
also be used for linguistic palaeontology (inferring a culture's environment/lifestyle from
reconstructable vocabulary), but the method has real limits — scholars have been unable to
reconstruct even some everyday PIE items (*eyelid*, *butter*, *snow*, *feet*, *milk*, *rain*,
*hands*), and the referential precision of a reconstructed word (e.g. whether a reconstructed animal
term denoted a wild or domesticated creature) is often simply unrecoverable. (pp.41–58)

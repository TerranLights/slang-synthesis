## Grammar points

### 6.52 Commas with questions
A direct question embedded within another sentence (not as a quotation, but as part of the
sentence) is usually introduced by a comma rather than set off with quotation marks; it may take an
initial capital letter if it is relatively long or has internal punctuation. If the result looks
awkward, CMOS recommends rephrasing as an indirect question instead — indirect questions take
neither a question mark nor a comma, and are not capitalized except at the start of a sentence.

### 6.53 Commas relative to parentheses and brackets
When a comma is needed at the end of material enclosed in parentheses or brackets, it follows the
closing mark — never precedes it. A comma only rarely appears immediately before a closing bracket,
as part of an editorial interpolation.

### 6.54 Use of the semicolon
In regular prose, a semicolon most commonly joins two independent clauses not linked by a
conjunction, signaling a closer connection between them than a period would.

### 6.55 Semicolons with "however," "therefore," "indeed," and the like
Transitional adverbs that join two independent clauses — *however, thus, hence, indeed,
accordingly, besides, therefore*, and sometimes *then* — are preceded by a semicolon rather than a
comma. A comma usually follows the adverb but may be dropped if the sentence reads just as well
without it.

### 6.56 Semicolons with "that is," "for example," "namely," and the like
A semicolon may precede expressions such as *that is*, *for example*, or *namely* when they
introduce an independent clause.

### 6.57 Semicolons before a conjunction
Normally a comma precedes a conjunction introducing an independent clause (§6.28). In formal prose,
a semicolon may be substituted instead, either for a stronger, more dramatic separation between the
clauses, or when the second clause already has internal punctuation of its own.

### 6.58 Semicolons in a complex series
When the items within a series themselves contain internal punctuation, separating the items with
semicolons (rather than commas) aids clarity. If no real ambiguity would result, commas may still be
used instead (§6.18; see also §6.123).

### 6.59 Use of the colon
A colon introduces an element or series of elements that illustrates or amplifies what precedes it.
Between independent clauses it functions much like a semicolon, and either mark may sometimes work —
but a colon should be used sparingly, and only to convey an "as follows" sense (the second clause
illustrating or amplifying the first). A colon can also introduce a series of related sentences
rather than a single clause.

### 6.60 Space after colon
In typeset matter, no more than one space follows a colon. In certain specific settings — a source
citation between a volume and page number with no intervening date/issue number, a biblical
citation, or a ratio — no space at all follows the colon.

### 6.61 Lowercase or capital letter after a colon
The word following a colon used within a sentence is lowercased unless it's a proper name. Exception:
capitalize the first word after a colon when the colon introduces two or more full sentences, when
it introduces dialogue/speech or an extract, or when it introduces a direct question.

### 6.62 Colons with "as follows" and other introductory phrases
A colon is normally used after *as follows*, *the following*, and similar expressions (full lists of
such expressions are cross-referenced to §§6.121–26).

### 6.63 Colons to introduce speech or quotations
A colon is often used to introduce speech within dialogue. It may also replace a comma to introduce
a quotation, either where the sentence's syntax requires it or to introduce the quotation more
formally (see also §6.50 for the comma's use with quotations).

### 6.64 Colons with formal direct address
At the beginning of a speech or a formal written communication, a colon usually follows the
identification of the party or parties being addressed (e.g., a salutation).

### 6.65 Some common misuses of colons
A colon is not automatically required before every series or list — even one that appears on its own
separate line (as in a vertical list, §§6.121–26). Applying this test resolves most cases: the words
introducing the series or list must themselves form a grammatically complete sentence to merit a
colon. In particular, a colon should not be used before a series that serves as the direct object of
a verb. Nor should a colon normally follow *namely*, *for example*, and similar expressions (see
§6.43).

### 6.66 Use of the question mark
The question mark indicates a direct question. It may also indicate editorial doubt — for example,
regarding a date or publication fact (§14.138) — or, occasionally, be used at the end of a
declarative or imperative sentence to express surprise, disbelief, or uncertainty.

### 6.67 Question mark within a sentence
A question mark marks the end of a direct question embedded within a larger sentence. If the
embedded question doesn't begin the sentence, it need not start with a capital letter (compare the
exception at §6.52).

### 6.68 Indirect questions
An indirect question never takes a question mark (see also §6.52). When a question embedded in a
sentence consists of a single word — such as *who*, *when*, *how*, or *why* — the question mark may
be omitted, and the word is sometimes italicized instead.

### 6.69 Requests as questions
A request phrased in question form does not require a question mark; such formulations can usually
be reduced to a straightforward imperative instead (e.g., restating "Would you kindly respond by
[date]" as "Please respond by [date]").

### 6.70 Question marks in relation to surrounding text and punctuation
A question mark is placed inside quotation marks, parentheses, or brackets only when it is actually
part of (applies to) the quoted or parenthetical material itself; otherwise it falls outside the
closing mark (see also §§6.10, 6.120).

### 6.71 Use of the exclamation point
An exclamation point marks an outcry or an emphatic/ironic comment. CMOS stresses it should be used
sparingly to remain effective (see also §§6.116, 6.118).

### 6.72 Exclamation rather than question
A sentence in the form of a direct question can be restyled as an exclamation simply by closing it
with an exclamation point instead of a question mark (e.g., "How could you possibly believe that!").

### 6.73 Exclamation point as editorial protest or amusement
An exclamation point inserted in brackets into quoted material, to signal editorial protest or
amusement, tends to read as contemptuous — CMOS advises avoiding this device unless that
contemptuous tone is actually intended. The Latin *sic* ("thus") should instead be reserved for
flagging an apparent error in the source that might otherwise be mistaken for a transcription error
(see §13.59).

### 6.74 Exclamation points with quotation marks, parentheses, or brackets
Like the question mark (§6.70), an exclamation point is placed inside quotation marks, parentheses,
or brackets only when it is part of the quoted or parenthetical matter itself.

### 6.75 Hyphens and dashes compared
The hyphen, en dash, and em dash — the three most commonly used members of the hyphen/dash family —
each have a distinct appearance and distinct uses (detailed in the paragraphs that follow and in
later sections). Though many readers won't consciously notice the visual difference, especially
between an en dash and a hyphen, correctly distinguishing them is treated as a mark of editorial
precision; CMOS specifically warns that manuscript-to-publication conversion can introduce errors
here that need proofreading. The family also includes the 2-em dash and 3-em dash (see §§2.12, 2.13,
2.93 for further detail not covered in this page range).

### 6.76 Hyphens in compound words
This section is a cross-reference only, pointing to §5.91 and to chapter 7 (especially §§7.31–43 and
7.77–85, plus §6.80) for the actual rules governing hyphenation of compound words and names and word
division — no new rule content appears on this page.

### 6.77 Hyphens as separators
A hyphen separates numbers that are not meant to be read as an inclusive range — telephone numbers,
social security numbers, ISBNs (date ranges are handled separately, at §9.37). A hyphen is also used
to separate individual letters when a word is spelled out letter by letter, whether in dialogue or,
as one of CMOS's examples notes, in reference to American Sign Language fingerspelling (cross-ref.
§§11.144–54). Hyphens can likewise appear within URLs and e-mail addresses — and in that specific
context, a hyphen must never be *added* when such a string happens to break across a line (see
§§2.12, 7.42).

### 6.78 En dash as "to"
The en dash's principal use is connecting numbers — and, less often, words — most typically dates,
times, and page numbers, where it signifies "up to and including" (or "through"). For the sake of
parallel construction: use the word *to* (never the en dash) if *from* precedes the first element of
the pair, and use *and* (never the en dash) if *between* precedes the first element. In other
contexts, such as scores and directions (e.g., a route between two cities, or a final score), the en
dash more simply signifies "to." See §§9.30–37, 9.38–41 for more on dates and times, §9.60 for
number ranges generally, and §6.105 for the slash as a related alternative.

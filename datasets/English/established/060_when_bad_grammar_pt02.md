## Grammar points

### A Lesson on Pronoun Cases (pp. 56–58)

Personal pronouns and the relative pronouns *who/whoever* take three case forms — subjective
(nominative), objective, and possessive — chosen according to the pronoun's grammatical role, not by
feel. The subjective case is used when the pronoun is the subject of a verb, or follows a finite
(non-infinitive) form of "to be." The objective case is used when the pronoun is the object of a verb,
the indirect object, the subject or object of an infinitive, or the object of a preposition. The
possessive case is used when the pronoun itself indicates possession.

### Error 1: I vs. Me (p. 59)

*Don't say* "This is a problem for Ellen and I to solve"; *say instead* "...for Ellen and me." "Ellen"
and the pronoun are both subjects of the infinitive "to solve," which requires the objective case
("me"), not the subjective ("I") — a mistake people rarely make without a second person added to the
sentence. Tip: mentally remove the other person and use whichever form sounds right alone; that choice
holds when the other person is restored.

### Error 2: She vs. Her (p. 60)

*Don't say* "Yes, this is her; who's calling?"; *say instead* "this is she." The pronoun follows a
finite form of "to be" ("is"), which requires the subjective case. The same pattern governs "he"/"him."

### Error 3: Who vs. Whom (pp. 61–62)

*Don't say* "Who is the present for?"; *say instead* "Whom is the present for?" — the pronoun is the
object of the preposition "for," requiring the objective case "whom." A tricky correct counter-case:
in *"She'll marry the man who she thinks has the finest collection of ties,"* "who" is correct because
the pronoun is the subject of "has," not the object of "thinks" — a parenthetical "she thinks"-type
insertion frequently misleads people into picking "whom" here.

### Error 4: Whoever vs. Whomever (pp. 62–63)

*Don't say* "Give the tickets to whomever can use them"; *say instead* "...to whoever can use them." Although the whole clause "whoever can use them" is the object of "to," the pronoun's own role *within*
that clause (subject of "can [use]") determines its case — a recurring pattern where an outer clause's
objective role is mistakenly projected onto the pronoun itself rather than the pronoun's own in-clause
role.

### Error 5: His vs. Him (p. 67)

*Don't say* "It's a question of him being ready on time"; *say instead* "...of his being ready on
time." What's being possessed is the intangible state of "being ready," so the possessive "his" (not
the objective "him") is required before the gerund.

### Error 6: Their vs. Them (pp. 67–68)

*Don't say* "...willingness to consider them leaving early"; *say instead* "...to consider their
leaving early," because the sentence's emphasis is on the action (leaving), requiring a possessive
modifier. Contrast *"Marge saw them eating during the ceremony"* — correct with the objective "them,"
because here the emphasis is on the people seen, with "eating" merely describing what they were doing.
Diagnostic: remove the "-ing" word; if the sentence's core meaning survives, use the objective case
(person-emphasis); if the meaning collapses, use the possessive (action-emphasis). Some sentences
genuinely permit either choice, with no single correct answer, depending on which emphasis the speaker
intends.

### Relative pronouns: which, that, and who/whom (pp. 71–73)

Two separate rules govern these three pronouns. First (low-stakes-free): "which" never refers to
people — only "who"/"whom"/"that" may. Second (more easily neglected, "sometimes disregarded by people
whose English is otherwise very good"): "which" introduces a nonrestrictive clause (nonessential extra
information, set off by commas — e.g. *"The cat, which had been sleeping for hours, woke up..."*),
while "that" introduces a restrictive clause (essential to the sentence's core meaning, no commas —
e.g. *"The cat that had been sleeping was hungrier than the cat that ate the canary"*). When a
nonrestrictive clause refers to a person, the exception to the person-rule kicks in: use "who"/"whom,"
not "which." When a restrictive clause refers to a person, either "that" or "who"/"whom" is acceptable.

### Error 7: Which vs. That (pp. 73–74)

*Don't say* "The invitations which we sent by carrier pigeon arrived sooner than the invitations which
we sent in the mail"; *say instead* "...that we sent..." in both clauses, since both clauses restrict
("distinguish") one invitation group from the other.

### Intensive and reflexive pronouns: usage and misuse (pp. 75–76)

*Myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself, ourselves, yourselves, themselves* have exactly two
legitimate uses: intensive (adding emphasis to the subject, e.g. *"You yourself should go there"*) and
reflexive (the verb's action reflecting back onto the subject-as-object, e.g. *"I hit myself in the
knee"*). They may never substitute for an ordinary personal pronoun (*I, me, she, it*, etc.) in a role
that is neither intensive nor reflexive.

### Error 8: Me vs. Myself (p. 76)

*Don't say* "Thank you for inviting Jack and myself"; *say instead* "...Jack and me," since "myself"
here is attached to neither the sentence's subject ("you") nor the verb's action reflecting onto it —
it is simply (and incorrectly) substituting for the plain object "me." The book attributes this
specific error to a belief that "myself" sounds more elegant/formal than "me" — a hypercorrection
motive, not a case-role confusion like errors 1–6.

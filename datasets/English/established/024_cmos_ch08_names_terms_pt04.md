## Grammar points

### 8.74 Historical events and programs

Names of many major historical events and programs are conventionally capitalized (the Boston Tea
Party, the Great Depression, the New Deal). Others, more recent or known mainly by a generic
description, are usually lowercased (the baby boom, the civil rights movement, the crash of 1929).
If in doubt, CMOS recommends not capitalizing. The rule cross-references separate sections for wars
and battles (§§8.112–13), religious events (§8.107), and formal acts/treaties (§8.79) (p. 1077).

### 8.75 Speeches

Only a very few speeches have attained the status of titles and are thus traditionally capitalized
(Washington's Farewell Address, the Gettysburg Address, Martin Luther King Jr.'s "I Have a Dream"
speech). Others are usually lowercased, even when referring to a specific, well-known address (the
annual State of the Union address, Franklin Roosevelt's second inaugural address) (p. 1078).

### 8.76 Meteorological and other natural phenomena

Named hurricanes, tropical cyclones, and many other named meteorological phenomena are capitalized;
when in doubt, CMOS directs the writer to consult a dictionary or encyclopedia. Natural phenomena
identified only generically by a place-name or year are usually lowercased (the Northridge earthquake
of 1994). CMOS also specifies the pronoun "it," not "he" or "she," for named storms/hurricanes,
despite the common practice of using male/female proper names for such events (p. 1079).

### 8.77 Sporting events

The full names of major sporting events are capitalized (the Kentucky Derby, the Olympic Games, the
World Cup); shortened forms derived from them (the derby, the finals, the Olympics) remain capitalized
too, since they retain the specific-event reference (p. 1080).

### 8.78 Movements and styles — capitalization

Nouns and adjectives naming cultural styles, movements, and schools (artistic, architectural,
musical, etc.) — and their adherents — are capitalized when derived from a proper noun (Aristotelian,
Cartesian, Reaganomics). The common noun "school" itself always stays lowercase even in a capitalized
name like "Chicago school." A smaller set of terms not derived from proper nouns are nonetheless
capitalized by convention to distinguish them from a generic everyday word (Cynic, Scholastic, New
Criticism). Many of the terms CMOS lists are lowercased by default (cubism, impressionism, realism),
though the manual notes a work may appropriately capitalize such a term throughout if done
consistently — while warning that doing so for one movement (e.g., capitalizing "impressionism" in an
art-history work) could create pressure to capitalize every other movement mentioned too, "which
could result in an undesirable profusion of capitals." Separately, several terms that are capitalized
when they name the specific historical school are lowercased when used generically/metaphorically:
*Epicurean* → "epicurean tastes," *Peripatetic* → "peripatetic families," *Sophist* → "she's a sophist,
not a logician," *Stoic* → "a stoic attitude." Cross-references religious movements at §8.96 and the
related rule on movements/schools generally at §8.59 (pp. 1081–82).

### 8.79 Formal names of acts, treaties, and so forth

Formal or officially accepted titles of pacts, plans, policies, treaties, acts, programs, and similar
documents/agreements are capitalized (the Bill of Rights, the Marshall Plan, the Treaty of Versailles).
Incomplete or generic forms of the same reference are usually lowercased (the amendment, the tariff
act, the treaty), and some terms that started as specific legislation have become lowercase generic
concepts in everyday use (social security). Some terms carry the capitalized proper name but a
lowercase common-noun component even in full form ("the Munich agreement," "the Open Door policy").
Cross-references citing published bill/law text (§§14.294–95) (pp. 1083–84).

### 8.80 Generic terms for pending legislation

Informal, purely descriptive references to legislation that hasn't yet been enacted (and so has no
formal title yet) are lowercased, e.g. "The anti-injunction bill was introduced on Tuesday" (p. 1085).

### 8.81 Legal cases mentioned in text

Names of legal cases are italicized in running text; the abbreviation "v." (for "versus") occasionally
appears in roman type, but CMOS recommends italics. In footnotes, legal dictionaries, and
numerous-legal-citation contexts, case names are sometimes set in roman instead. In ongoing
discussion, a case name may be shortened (dropping the italicized full citation), e.g. "the Miranda
case" or simply "Miranda." Cross-references full legal citation style at §§14.281–317 (p. 1086).

### 8.82 Capitalization for names of awards and prizes

Names of awards and prizes are capitalized, but generic terms used alongside the name are usually
lowercased ("a Nobel Prize winner," "a Pulitzer in journalism," but "a Guggenheim grant" rather than a
capitalized generic "Grant"). Cross-references military awards at §8.114 (p. 1087).

### 8.83 Lowercase for oaths and pledges

Formal oaths and pledges are usually lowercased (the oath of citizenship, the Hippocratic oath,
marriage vows) — with one notable named exception, the Pledge of Allegiance, which is capitalized by
convention (p. 1088).

### 8.84 Academic subjects

Academic subjects are not capitalized unless they form part of an official department/committee name
or an official course name, or are themselves proper nouns (English, Latin). Thus "she has published
widely in the history of religions" stays lowercase, but "Jones is chair of the Committee on
Comparative Literature" capitalizes the official committee name (p. 1089).

### 8.85 Courses of study

Official names of courses of study are capitalized ("I am signing up for Archaeology 101"; "Basic
Manuscript Editing" at the Graham School of General Studies), while a generic/informal reference to a
course stays lowercase ("his ballroom dancing classes have failed to civilize him") (p. 1090).

### 8.86 Lectures

Names of lecture series are capitalized, and individual lecture titles are capitalized and usually
enclosed in quotation marks ("This year's Robinson Memorial Lectures were devoted to the nursing
profession. The first lecture, 'How Nightingale Got Her Way,' was a sellout.") Cross-references §8.69
(p. 1091).

### 8.87 Days of the week, months, and seasons

Names of days and months are capitalized (Tuesday, November); the four seasons are lowercased (spring,
fall, the vernal equinox, the winter solstice) — except when a season name is used to denote a
specific issue of a journal, per the cross-referenced §14.180. Cross-references centuries/decades at
§8.70 (p. 1092).

### 8.88 Holidays

Names of secular and religious holidays, and officially designated days or seasons, are capitalized
(Christmas Day, Labor Day, Ramadan, Yom Kippur) — with a small set of exceptions that stay partly or
fully lowercase by convention: D-day (capitalized initial only) and a bank holiday (fully generic/
lowercase) (p. 1093).

### 8.89 Time and time zones

When spelled out, designations of time and time zones are lowercased, except for any proper noun
embedded within the phrase (Pacific daylight time, Greenwich mean time — "Pacific" and "Greenwich"
stay capitalized as place names even though the rest of the phrase is lowercase). Abbreviated forms
are fully capitalized (EST, GMT, DST). Cross-references §§9.38–41 (p. 1094).

### 8.90 Deities

Names of deities are capitalized regardless of whether the religious tradition is monotheistic or
polytheistic (Allah, God, Yahweh; Astarte, Freyja, Mithra). "Satan" is capitalized as a proper name,
but the generic common-noun form "the devil" is lowercased. Some writers follow a pious convention of
not fully spelling out a deity's name (e.g., "G-d"); CMOS says this convention should be respected
when practical to do so (p. 1095).

### 8.91 Alternative names

Alternative or descriptive names used for God as the supreme being are capitalized (Adonai, the
Almighty, the Deity, the Holy Ghost/Holy Spirit/Paraclete, the Lord, Providence, the Supreme Being,
the Trinity). Cross-references §8.92 (p. 1096).

### 8.92 Prophets and the like

Designations of prophets, apostles, saints, and other revered religious figures are often capitalized
(the Buddha, the Messiah, Muhammad/the Prophet, the Virgin Mary/the Blessed Virgin/Mother of God). A
lowercase descriptive title may precede a capitalized proper name ("the prophet Isaiah"). Generic
plural references to a group of such figures, by contrast, are usually lowercased even though
individual named figures in the same category are capitalized: the apostles, the patriarchs, the
psalmist (p. 1097).

### 8.93 Platonic ideas

Words for transcendent ideas in the Platonic sense — especially when used in a religious context — are
often capitalized (Good, Beauty, Truth, the One). Cross-references §7.48 (p. 1098).

### 8.94 Pronouns referring to religious figures

Pronouns referring to God or Jesus are not capitalized by CMOS's house style ("They prayed to God that
he would deliver them"; "Jesus and his disciples"). CMOS notes that most English Bible translations
also lowercase these pronouns (p. 1099).

### 8.95 Major religions

Names of major world religions are capitalized, as are their adherents and adjectives derived from
them (Buddhism/Buddhist, Islam/Islamic/Muslim, Judaism/Jew/Jewry/Jewish). Stances about religion more
generally, rather than named religions themselves, stay lowercase: atheism, agnosticism (p. 1100).

### 8.96 Denominations, sects, orders, and religious movements

Like the names of major religions, the names of denominations, communions, sects, orders, and
religious movements are capitalized, as are their adherents and adjectives derived from them (the
Amish, Anglicanism, Baptists, Methodism, Quakerism, Zen Buddhism — among roughly three dozen examples
CMOS lists, several with historically or organizationally split institutional names, e.g.
Mormonism's two formally distinct bodies, the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-Day Saints [Utah] and
Community of Christ [Missouri]). A recurring pattern across the list: the denomination/movement name
itself is capitalized, but a *generic, individual-congregation* reference built from the same adjective
is lowercased — "the Church of England, *but* an Anglican church"; "the Roman Catholic Church, *but* a
Roman Catholic church"; "a United Methodist church." A second recurring pattern: some adjective forms
(Dissenter, Nonconformist, Protestant, Puritan) are lowercased specifically "when used in a
nonsectarian context" — i.e., in a non-religious, more general sense of the word. "Jesuitic(al)" adds a
third pattern: lowercased specifically when used *pejoratively* (meaning cunning or equivocal, rather
than referring to the actual religious order). Cross-references §8.98 (pp. 1101–2).

### 8.97 "Church" as institution

When used generically to refer to the institution of religion, or of a particular religion as an
institution, "church" is usually lowercased ("church and state," "the early church," "the church
fathers"). "Church" is capitalized only when it's part of the formal name of a specific denomination
(the United Methodist Church; see further examples at §8.96) or of a specific congregation (the Church
of St. Thomas the Apostle) (p. 1102).

### 8.98 Generic versus religious terms

Many terms that are lowercased when used generically (animism, fundamentalism, spiritualism) may be
capitalized when they name a specific religion or sect — e.g. "a popular medium in turn-of-the-century
Spiritualist circles" (capitalized, naming the specific 19th/early-20th-century movement) versus
"liberal versus fundamentalist Christians" (lowercase, a generic descriptive adjective even though it
modifies a capitalized group noun) (p. 1102).

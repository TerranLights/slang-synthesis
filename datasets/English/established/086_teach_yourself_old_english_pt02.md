## Grammar points

### Weak vs. strong past-tense formation

Old English verbs form the past tense in one of two ways, matching the two strategies still visible
in Modern English: weak/consonantal verbs add a dental suffix (*-d/-ede*: *plegian* "play" →
*plegode*); strong/vocalic verbs change the stem vowel (*drincan* "drink" → *dranc*). Despite being
numerically fewer, the strong/vowel-changing type is termed "irregular" in Modern English teaching
only because the weak type became overwhelmingly the majority pattern over time — in Old English
itself, strong verbs are a large, systematic class, not oddities. p.37.

### Irregular high-frequency verbs

A small set of very common verbs (*be, do, go, know*) has irregular present/past pairs that don't
fit either the weak or strong pattern cleanly: *is→wæs*, *deð→dyde*, *gæð→eode*, *wat→wiste* — the
same cross-linguistic tendency for a language's most frequent verbs to retain irregular/suppletive
forms. p.39.

### Plural past-tense ending -on/-an

Unlike Modern English, which distinguishes singular *was* from plural *were* but not singular vs.
plural *did*, Old English consistently marks the past-tense plural across all verbs with an ending
(*wæs*→*wæron*, *dyde*→*dydon*, *eode*→*eodon*, *wiste*→*wiston*). Spelling varies between *-on*
and *-an*: the *-an* spelling reflects a late Old English sound-merger (the vowel distinction
between the two endings had stopped being audible, similarly to how Modern English "button" no
longer clearly distinguishes its final vowel). p.40.

### The perfective/associative prefix ge- ('yuh-')

An optional prefix many Old English writers attach to past-tense verbs (*ic seah* and *ic geseah*
both mean "I saw"), conveying an idea of result or completeness without changing the core meaning.
The same prefix also derives nouns from verbs with a completed-action sense (*ge-sceaft* "creation"
< *sceppan* "to create") and, separately, marks grouping/association on nouns (*ge-mot* "assembly,"
*ge-trum* "troop," *ge-fera* "travelling companion" < *feran*). Notably, adding associative ge- to
an already-plural noun can narrow its meaning rather than just add a grammatical nuance: plain
*broðru* "brothers" vs. *gebroðru* "a group of brothers" → "monks, co-religionists." Reference
dictionaries conventionally alphabetize ge- verbs/nouns under their root letter, ignoring the
prefix. p.38–39.

### Vowel length and a register/insult anecdote

Scribes rarely marked vowel length, which could cause real ambiguity or (per Byrhtferth's own
*Handbook*) comic error: *þu sott* ("you fool," long vowel) vs. the writer's example of someone
mistakenly saying *þu sot* ("you soot," short vowel) when trying to deliver the insult — Byrhtferth
notes *sott* with a short vowel was a fashionable new insult of possibly French-influenced origin,
making the mispronunciation doubly embarrassing. A clean sociolinguistic register/borrowing note,
not just a phonology point. p.38.

### Descriptive-title word order and bynames/epithets

Formal title-giving in Old English places the person's name first, rank/title second (*Cnut
cyning* "King Cnut," not rank-first as in Modern English). The same pattern extends to bynames used
to distinguish same-named people or apportion praise/blame — several concrete historical examples
are given (*Æþelræd Unræd* "the Ill-advised," source of the familiar "the Unready"; *Eadmund
Irensid* "Ironside"; the pointedly mocking *Eadwig ceorla cyng* "king of peasants"; and the pair
*Ælfgyfu seo hlæfdige* vs. *Ælfgyfu seo Hamtunisca* used specifically to disambiguate Cnut's two
wives who shared a first name). p.34–35.

### Ealdorman vs. eorl: a rank system in flux, with a Norse-contact loan

The older rank *ealdorman* (numerous, roughly a dozen under Cnut's predecessors) was increasingly
displaced in prominence by the newer, higher rank *eorl* (far fewer — just the four great
provinces under Cnut). *Eorl* originally meant "brave man, warrior" in poetry, but gained currency
as a formal rank specifically because it sounded like Old Norse *jarl* — Cnut's own native-language
word for the same rank — and the two languages were mutually comprehensible enough for the overlap
to read as a natural borrowing rather than a foreign imposition. p.35.

### Explicit manuscript/recension variation: Chronicle versions C vs. D

A practice exercise instructs direct comparison of the annals for 1028–30 across Chronicle
manuscripts **C** and **D**, which genuinely diverge in content: version D includes extra narrative
detail (Cnut driving Olaf out and seizing "all that land" in 1028, Olaf's failed return and defeat
in 1030) absent from the terser version C. This is an explicit, source-marked instance of
dialectal/recensional variation within a single textual tradition, distinct from ordinary regional
dialect variation but equally worth flagging per the extraction spec's coverage rule. p.43.

### The Roman calendar system (ides, nones, kalends)

Old English chronicle dating adopts the Roman system wholesale as Latin loanwords: *kalendas* (1st
of the month), *idus* "ides" (15th of March/May/July/October, 13th of other months), *nonas*
"nones" (9th day counting inclusively backward from the ides — 7th or 5th depending on month). Days
are then named by counting backward from the next ides/kalends (e.g. "the twelfth of the kalends of
January" = December 21st, the solstice). A handful of days are named individually instead of by
this counting system, notably *midwintres dæg* "Christmas Day." p.40–41.

### Grammatical gender and the three-way definite article

Old English nouns fall into three grammatical genders (masculine, feminine, neuter), each taking a
different base form of the definite article: *se* (masculine, e.g. *se tima* "the season"), *seo*
(feminine, e.g. *seo gaderung* "the gathering"), *þæt* (neuter, e.g. *þæt mynster* "the minster").
The corresponding third-person pronouns track grammatical, not natural, gender: *he* refers to a
masculine noun (hence Byrhtferth refers to the masculine noun *tima* "season" with *he*, even
though no personification is intended), *heo* to feminine, *hit* to neuter. The book cites Quirk and
Wrenn's estimate that roughly 45% of Old English nouns are masculine, 35% feminine, 25% neuter, and
notes gender usually cannot be predicted from a noun's form alone (though cognate German words
often help). A new, gender-neutral article *þe* developed in late Old English and became Modern
English "the." p.51–52.

### The inflected (dative) infinitive -enne

Alongside the plain *-an* infinitive (*healdan* "to hold"), the book introduces a second infinitive
form in *-enne* (*to healdenne* "to hold, to rule") used after the preposition *to*, distinct from
the "ellipsis" pattern already seen with *sceal* in Unit 2. p.36.

### The accusative case: marking the object, and the -ne ending

As an inflected language, Old English can mark the grammatical object of a sentence via case
endings rather than relying purely on word order (though Subject-Verb-Object order is still the
normal pattern, as in Modern English). The accusative ending *-ne* is the most distinctive: it marks
masculine nouns, the masculine pronoun *hyne* (accusative of *he*, also spelt *hine*), and 'strong'
adjectives qualifying a masculine noun, when functioning as a sentence's object. Because the case
ending carries the grammatical information, word order is freer than in Modern English — an
accusative pronoun object often precedes its verb (*hyne hatedon* "hated him," object before verb).
p.65–67.

### Correlative ða...ða / þonne...þonne ("when...then")

*Ða* functions as both the plural/feminine-accusative definite article and, separately, as a true
homonymous adverb meaning "when" or "then," distinguished only by position and context: at a
clause's start, *ða* generally means "when" (first instance) or "then" (second instance) in a
paired when...then construction, called *correlation*. The "when" clause keeps its verb in clause-
final position; the "then" clause places its verb in the normal second position, immediately after
*ða*. The same correlative pattern recurs with *þonne...þonne*. p.63–64.

### Impersonal verbs: me þuhte "it seemed to me" vs. me mætte "I dreamed"

Old English kept two now-merged verbs formally distinct: *byncan* "to seem" (impersonal, dative
subject: *me þuhte* "it seemed to me") and *þencan* "to think." Both verbs later coalesced into
Modern English *think/thought*, which is why the archaic expression "methought" is often
misunderstood as meaning "I thought" when it actually continues *byncan* "seem" — "it seemed to
me." A parallel impersonal construction, *me mætte* "it dreamed to me," i.e. "I dreamed" (<
*mætan*), is directly compared with Chaucer's Middle English *me mette*/*me thoghte* in *The Book of
the Duchess*, showing the construction's continuity into Middle English. p.67–68.

### Frith as a legal/sacred concept

*Frið* "peace," cognate with German *Frieden*, carries a specific medieval legal-and-sacred
connotation beyond ordinary peace: the compound *friðsocn* implies actively seeking out a place of
peace as sanctuary, reflecting the authority of Church and state to designate protected spaces (the
stone "frith-stool" sanctuary chairs still extant at Hexham and Beverley are cited as physical
evidence). p.73.

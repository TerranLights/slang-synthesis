## Grammar points

### §1–2. Definition and naming of Old English

OE is defined as the language/dialect-group of the Germanic inhabitants of Britain from the mid-5th-century conquests to the close of the 11th century — i.e. roughly the four centuries A.D. 700–1100 for which written evidence survives (earliest surviving monuments go back only to the late 7th century: Cædmon's poem, early charters). The term *Anglo-Saxon* (from Lat. *Anglo-Saxonicus*, itself adapted from the Elizabethan rendering of Bede's distinction between *Angli Saxones*/Britain's Germanic conquerors and *Antiqui Saxones*/continental "Old Saxons") was the dominant scholarly name through the 19th century; *Old English* has since largely replaced it because it preserves the idea of historic linguistic continuity — though the authors caution that modern literary English in fact descends more directly from the Anglian (East Midland) dialect than from the West Saxon in which almost all surviving OE literature happens to be preserved, so "direct continuity" from Old to Modern English is "to some extent misleading" (p.2).

### §3. Position and relationship within Indo-European/Germanic

OE belongs to the western branch of the Germanic family, hence ultimately to Indo-European. Germanic features distinguishing it (and the other Gmc languages) from other IE branches, listed explicitly: (a) the First Consonant Shift (Grimm's Law) — Gmc voiced plosives b/d/g/gw becoming voiceless p/t/k/kw; (b) fixing of word stress near/at the beginning (root syllable); (c) strong tendency to weaken/lose inflexional endings; (d) development of derived "weak"/consonantal verbs with dental-suffix preterites and past participles; (e) the two-type adjective declension (indefinite vs. definite); (f) certain Gmc-specific vocabulary strata; (g) the two-tense system (present/past only, by inflexion — other time-relations inferred from context, parallel to Mod.E. using present-tense forms for future after *when*).

Within Gmc, OE belongs to the **West Germanic** group (with Dutch/German/eastern Swiss varieties), and more closely still with Frisian and Old Saxon.

### §4. Dialects

OE is conventionally divided into four principal dialects: **West Saxon** (Wessex and expanding S./S.W.), **Kentish** (S.E., including Wight — from Jutish settlement), **Mercian** (W. Midland), and **Northumbrian** (N. of the Humber). Mercian and Northumbrian together are often grouped as **Anglian**. Little is known of the language of the East Midlands in the OE period itself, though ME evidence suggests it had features distinguishing it from Mercian.

### §5–6. Standard language and periods

Because almost all texts likely to be read by the literary student survive only in the Classical OE (late-10th/early-11th-century) koiné based on West Saxon — the language of Ælfric and his contemporaries — this grammar takes that literary WS koiné as its descriptive foundation, while noting non-WS dialect features only incidentally. OE history is divided into **Early OE** (c.700–900) and **Late OE** (c.900–1100); only King Alfred's *Cura Pastoralis* translation provides a thoroughly grammatically-analyzable body of genuinely "Early OE" (its MS being contemporary with Alfred, late 9th century — all other Early OE work survives only in later copies). A finer four-period scheme is also offered: (a) pre-Alfredian, (b) Alfredian, (c) the period of Ælfric and Wulfstan, (d) the period of transition to Middle English (ending c.1150); literary OE MSS continued to be copied until the late 12th century.

### §7–9. The alphabet

The earliest Germanic writing system in Britain was **runes** (originally ~24 symbols), used for magical formulae/epigraphs and for some inscriptions (e.g. the Ruthwell Cross) but unsuitable for continuous writing. The OE alphabet actually used in manuscripts is the **Irish form of the Latin alphabet** (Insular Script), introduced for vernacular writing in early Christian Northumbria and spread by Roman missionary influence. Latin *u* originally served for OE [w]; Latin *th* (biliteral) served for voiced [ð] and voiceless [θ] (as in Mod.E. *this*/*thin*) until the mid-8th century, when runic **ð** ("eth") and **þ** ("thorn", also *wynn* 'ƿ' for [w]) were adopted from runes because runes had lost their pagan-magical stigma with Christianization. A third new symbol, **æ** ("ash"), was formed by combining Latin a+e to represent the OE sound fronted/raised from Gmc *ā*, serving for both long and short values. Printed OE books (from the mid-19th century onward) retain þ, ð, æ but render all other letters in modern roman form; w is used in print instead of runic ƿ to avoid confusion with p. A special runic letter for g, written ȝ ("yogh"), was used by some grammarians but this grammar uses ordinary roman *g* throughout since OE script's special f/g/r/s forms (still visible in modern Irish letterforms) carried no separate phonemic information worth preserving typographically.

### §10–11. Accent marks and vowel length

OE scribes occasionally used an acute-accent-like mark over vowels, inconsistently — sometimes indicating stress (not length), sometimes used to distinguish two words written identically otherwise (*gōd* 'good' vs. *god* 'a god'). Some carefully written MSS (e.g. the best Ælfric copies) do show a graphically regular but phonetically meaningful distinction (þ initially vs. medially/finally ð). Since there is no reliable orthographic system for marking vowel length, **this grammar regularly marks vowel length editorially with a macron (¯)**, leaving short vowels unmarked — vowel length is determined from etymology, cognates in other languages, later development, and (to a limited extent) metre; later OE quantity was often changed by shortenings/lengthenings (see §199).

### §12–13. Stress

OE stress intensity had roughly four grades (heavy/secondary/light/weak), though in practice three approximate types matter for learners: heavy, secondary, and weak. Words normally bore heavy stress on the initial syllable (generally the root), with listed exceptions: (a) in noun+noun or noun+adjective compounds, the root of the second element carries secondary stress (*mannes* 'man's' vs. *mancynnes* 'mankind's', where *cynnes* exists as a separate word); (b) prefixes are generally unstressed unless they dominate the meaning, and the noun/verb prefix *ge-* is **always** unstressed; (c) the noun/verb prefix *bi-* is normally unstressed but its emphatic form *bī-* (contrasted with weak form *be-*) carries heavy stress; (d) adverbial/prepositional prefixes to verbs that dominate the meaning (*in-, ūt-, æfter-*) are heavily stressed, contrasted with generally-unstressed verb prefixes; (e) verbs formed from noun+prefix compounds generally retain heavy stress on the prefix (*andswarian* 'to answer', from noun *andswaru*). One major consequence of fixed early initial stress was the progressive weakening of final unstressed inflexional vowels/syllables (toward schwa [ə]) from around the 10th century, so that distinctively-spelled endings *-um, -an, -on* came to be pronounced alike, though scribes conservatively preserved the traditional spelling distinctions well behind actual contemporary pronunciation — of direct pedagogical relevance, since students are advised to pronounce these inflexional endings distinctively even though doing so is historically a bit archaic by Ælfric's own time (note to §13).

### §14–17. Pronunciation and vowels

The grammar adopts **Ælfric's period (c. A.D. 1000, "Classical OE")** as its normative pronunciation target, on the basis that Ælfric himself was both a careful user of the shared written/spoken literary koiné and a Latin grammarian whose own grammar of Latin-in-OE terms gives indirect evidence of his own OE usage. OE had **seven long and seven short vowels**: a, æ, e, i, o, u, y — illustrated via careful comparison to Mod.E./French/German reference sounds (e.g. OE short *a* [a]/[æ] as in RP *hat*; long *ā* [ɑ:] as in Fr. *lâche*; OE *æ* short = Mod.E. RP *bat*, long = Mod.E. *mare*; OE *e* short like Fr. *bête*, long like Germ. *See*; OE *i* short/long like *biddan*/*machine*; OE *o* short/long like *god*/Fr. *beau*; OE *u* short/long like *full*/Fr. *fou*; OE *y* (front-rounded, no Mod.E. equivalent) like Fr. *reçu* short / Fr. *lune* long). A seventh front-rounded vowel *œ* is occasionally found in Anglian texts (corresponding to the Germ. *schön*/*Göttingen* vowel), unrounded to *e* in early WS and spelt *e* there (see §208).

### §16. Vowel-point-of-articulation classification

OE vowels are divided by place of articulation into **front vowels** (æ, e, i, y) and **back vowels** (a, o, u). Because the consonant *g* was vocalised after front vowels in late OE, spellings like *bigleofa*/*bileofa* 'food' alternate; because short *i* could be sounded consonantally as [j], forms like *herges*/*heries* alternate (*herian* 'to praise').

### §17–18. Diphthongs

Diphthongs can be described as "rising" (heavier stress on the second element) or "falling" (heavier stress on the first). OE diphthongs were generally **falling**: *féallan* 'to fall', *scéap* 'sheep', *leoht* 'light'. A special-to-WS diphthong-type arose from the development of a palatal-glide-vowel between a palatal *c* or *g* and a following front vowel (e.g. non-WS *gefan* appears in early WS as *giefan*, later *gifan* or *gyfan*). Classical OE had four diphthongs — *ea, ēa, eo, ēo* — each pronounced as a single syllable with one crest of sonority (not as two separate vowel sounds), with short diphthongs *ea, eo* assigned pronunciation roughly [æə]/[eə], and long diphthongs the same quality-pair but held longer, with the whole glide (not just the first element) given greater length. Recent scholarship (M. Daunt and others) has questioned whether the short diphthongs were really diphthongal rather than an allophonic variant of the vowel conditioned by the following consonant — the authors note this debate without resolving it definitively (bibliographic pointers given).

### §19–22. Consonants

OE consonant symbols mostly match their Mod.E. values, with notable exceptions: *f, s, þ/ð* were voiceless fricatives initially and finally but voiced between vowels (*sittan* 'to sit' [s] vs. *genesan* 'to be saved' [z]). *H* was [h] initially, but medially/finally became a palatal or velar fricative according to the adjacent vowel's front/back quality (like Scots *loch* or Welsh *Llan-*, or Germ. *ich*). *K* is rare, mostly used instead of *c* before a front vowel to unambiguously signal the plosive [k] value (e.g. *kyning* 'king' vs. the more usual spelling *cyning*). *R* initially may have been strongly trilled; medially/finally it varied by dialect, with a "burred"/retroflex quality in some positions reconstructed for Scots-like/south-western dialects of England. *Z* was very rare, pronounced [ts] or [dz] by position (e.g. *bæ(d)zere* 'baptist').

The two symbols *c* and *g* each covered multiple sounds, OE's chief orthographic defect by modern standards: *c* represented both plosive [k] (*candel, cōl, cumbol, cniht*-with-back-vowel-context) and affricate [tʃ] (*cēap* [tʃeːɑp], *cild* [tʃild], *cyrice* [tʃyritʃə]) depending on the adjacent vowel's backness in early OE — by the period of Classical OE, poetic alliteration patterns show *sc-* words alliterating only with other *sc-* words, confirming [ʃ] had become a genuinely distinct single consonant sound by then (as in Mod.E. *ship* for OE *scip*). OE *g* similarly represented: the plosive [g] before back vowels/consonants (*gāt* 'goat', *gnornian* 'to mourn', *guma* 'man'); the semivowel/affricate-adjacent [j] before front vowels (*gif* 'if', *þegen* 'thane'); and after back vowels, the voiced velar fricative [ɣ] (German "ach-laut" voiced), as in *āgan* 'to own', *fuglere* 'fowler'. For pedagogical simplicity, students are advised to read this [ɣ] value of *g* as [w] (*dragan* 'to draw' pronounced [drɑwan], *boga* 'bow' [bowa]) — matching the historical development that gave rise to later ME/Mod.E. spellings. The affricate *cg* represented voiced [dʒ] (*secgan* 'to say' [sedʒan]).

A general reading-pronunciation rule: **no letters are to be left unsounded** — e.g. the *w* of *writan* 'to write', the *c* of *cnāwan* 'to know', the *g* of *gnornian* 'to mourn' must be clearly heard before the following consonant. Biliteral *hw* (as in *hwæt* 'what') is pronounced as the voiceless labio-velar heard in Mod.Scots *what*, distinct from the voiced [w] of OE *wæt* 'wet'; *hl* (as in *hlūd* 'loud') and *hr* (as in *hring* 'ring') are correspondingly voiceless *l*- and *r*-sounds, distinct from voiced normal *l*/*r*.

### §23. Illustrative sample: opening of Beowulf

The grammar closes the Introduction with the first eleven lines of *Beowulf* given in full narrow phonetic transcription, representing the reconstructed pronunciation of Ælfric's period (the time the extant MS of this older poem was copied), following C. L. Wrenn's 1953 edition. The authors note they deliberately left all inflexional endings' vowel quality "indeterminate" (as it must genuinely have been in c.1000 speech) and did not mark the various OE vowel-length changes described later in §199, matching how students will encounter texts, glossaries, and dictionaries.

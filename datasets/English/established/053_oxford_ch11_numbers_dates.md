## Grammar points

### 11.1.1 Introduction

This chapter mainly covers numbers and dates in general texts and the humanities; more detailed
guidance on numbers in scientific contexts, and typography in mathematical notation, is deferred to
Chapter 14 (p. 187).

### 11.1.2 Figures or words?

The central stylistic choice is whether to express a number in figures or in words, normally governed
by a threshold below which words are used and above which figures take over. In non-technical contexts
Oxford style sets that threshold at 100 (numbers below 100 in words, 100 and above in figures); in
technical contexts the threshold drops to ten; specialist contexts may set their own threshold for good
reason. The rest of the subsection works through a long list of specific exceptions and conventions
layered on top of that basic threshold: large round numbers, rounding for approximate figures,
unit-conversion precision, *over*/*more than*, informal idiomatic number phrases, within-sentence
numeral consistency once a 100+ figure appears, mixed word/figure forms for two simultaneous number
sets, ordinal numbers (generally spelled out, with named exceptions for numerical street names and
fixed proper-name phrases like *the Third Reich*), sentence-initial numbers (always spelled out, best
avoided by recasting), ages (figures for cardinal ages, words for ordinal ages/decades, with more
latitude in informal/fictional contexts), and a closing bulleted list of categories that conventionally
always take figures regardless of the general threshold (book/periodical references, game scores, race
distances, building numbers, road numbers) (pp. 187–188).

### 11.1.3 Punctuation

Compound numbers written in words are hyphenated. Commas generally separate groups of three digits in
non-technical numbers of four figures or more, while technical and foreign-language work instead uses
a thin space (cross-referenced to §14.1.3); four-figure numbers in tabular matter omit the thin space
unless needed for column alignment with longer numbers nearby. A defined list of number categories
never takes commas at all: years (except long dates such as 10,000 BC), page numbers, line/column
numbers in poetry, mathematical workings, house or hotel-room numbers, and library call/shelf numbers
(p. 189).

### 11.1.4 Number ranges

Numbers at either end of a range are linked by an en rule, and a range is often elided to the fewest
digits possible — except the digits 10 to 19, which are never elided because of how that teens group is
read aloud. Editorial styles vary considerably in how aggressively they elide (no elision at all,
elision only to the final two digits, or never eliding multiples of ten or a hundred). Even in an
elided house style, all digits are kept in more formal contexts (titles, headings, vital dates) and
whenever a range crosses a century boundary; BC spans are always given in full, since an elided BC date
could otherwise be misread as spanning a different, misleadingly short or long period. Historians use a
multiplication sign between two dates to mean "no earlier than the first, no later than the second," for
an event known to have occurred sometime within that window. The "from x to y"/"between x and y"
formula must not be combined with an en-rule range in the same construction. Negative-value ranges are
best given in words or with a spaced dash, to avoid the range's en rule visually clashing with a leading
minus sign. When describing a range in figures, the unit or quantity word is repeated at both ends to
avoid ambiguity about where the range actually starts; a unit *symbol* need be repeated only if that
symbol is normally set closed up to its figure. A solidus, rather than an en rule, marks a one-year span
reckoned on a non-calendar basis (such as a tax year); a run of several such years is still joined by an
en rule as usual. Finally, a comma (not an en rule) separates discrete individual page-number
references, reserving the en rule for a genuinely continuous page range (pp. 189–191).

### 11.1.5 Singular or plural?

Numbers, in either word or figure form, are pluralized without an apostrophe. A plural number phrase
takes a plural verb when its elements are considered individually/severally, but a singular verb when
considered as one aggregate unit. Quantity nouns like *number*, *percentage*, and *proportion* are
singular with a definite article and plural with an indefinite one. *None* takes a singular verb in the
sense "no one person" and a plural verb in the sense "not any." The large-number words *hundred*,
*thousand*, *million*, *billion*, *trillion*, etc. stay grammatically singular unless they are being
used to express an indefinite quantity, in which case they pluralize normally (*hundreds of times*). The
subsection closes by noting that *billion* is now standardly a thousand million (10⁹) and *trillion* a
million million (10¹²), but that Britain formerly used the longer-scale values (a million million and a
million million million respectively) — values still in use in France, Germany, and elsewhere (p. 191).

### 11.1.6 Fractions and decimals

Simple fractions in running text are spelled out and traditionally hyphenated, though they are
increasingly set as open compounds. Compound fractions hyphenate both the numerator and the
denominator unless either already contains a hyphen; no hyphen separates a whole number from its
fraction (*one and seven-eighths*, not *one-and-seven-eighths*); *half* combined with a bare noun stays
unhyphenated (*half a mile*), but forms a hyphenated compound when modifying another noun (*a
half-mile*). Statistical matter may use dedicated diagonal-bar fraction characters; a horizontal-bar
TeX-set fraction looks elegant but is otherwise seldom satisfactory; non-technical running text uses a
plain solidus between text-sized numerals. Decimal fractions are always printed in figures, can never be
pluralized or take a plural verb, and (except for inherently sub-one quantities like probabilities) take
a leading zero below one. Decimals are punctuated with the full point set on the line; the older British
practice of a medial decimal point in currency (*£24·72*) has long fallen out of favor. European
languages, and ISO English-language publications, instead use a comma as the decimal sign (p. 192).

### 11.2.1 Numbers with units of measure — general principles

Figures, rather than words, are generally used with units of measurement, percentages, and expressions
of quantity or proportion. Oxford style spells out *per cent* as two words in non-technical running
text (contrasted with the closed-up American *percent*), while technical work uses the % symbol. Figures
are followed by a space before an abbreviated unit or symbol — including units of time — although the %
symbol is commonly set closed up, contrary to SI guidelines (p. 193).

### 11.2.2 Singular and plural units with numbers

A unit of measurement keeps its singular form when it is part of a hyphenated compound modifying
another noun (*a five-pound note*); elsewhere, units pluralize normally unless the quantity is less than
one (*0.568 litre*). Scientific-work unit symbols (*cm*, *ml*) never take a plural *s* (p. 193).

### 11.2.3 Currencies

Amounts of money may be spelled out in words with the currency name, though numerals with a symbol or
abbreviation are more common; round numbers suit the spelled-out form better than precise ones, which
may still need spelling out in legal documents for absolute clarity. For amounts in the millions and
above, and for thousands in financial contexts, symbols, words, and abbreviations may be combined (*£5
million*, *US$15 billion*). Currency symbols precede their figure with no space; abbreviations may take
a space if applied consistently. Where the dollar symbol alone would be ambiguous between countries, an
accepted prefix (*A$*, *US$*) disambiguates. A trailing "00" after the decimal point is used only when a
round sum appears in context alongside genuinely fractional amounts. Pence, cents, and other sub-unit
amounts are set close to their abbreviation with no full point (*56p*, not *£0.56*); a mixed
pounds-and-pence amount drops the pence abbreviation (*£15.30*, not *£15.30p*). Pre-decimal British
currency (pounds, shillings, pence, before February 1971) is set in italic with normal spacing in Oxford
style, or closed up in roman with full points in other styles. The ISO 4217:2008 three-letter currency
code, prefixed with a space, is used in trade, commerce, and banking contexts; unfamiliar currencies are
glossed at first mention (pp. 193–194).

### 11.3 Times of day

The formulation of times of day is a matter of editorial style, varying by context. Whole hours and
simple fractions of an hour are conventionally given in words, without hyphens. *O'clock* is restricted
to the exact hour, used only with the hour spelled out in words, and never combined with a.m./p.m.
(*eight o'clock in the morning* substitutes for the disallowed combination). Figures are used with
a.m./p.m.; since *12 a.m.*/*12 p.m.* are frequently misunderstood despite being technically correct for
midnight and noon respectively, *12 midnight* and *12 noon* may be used instead for clarity. The
twenty-four-hour clock sidesteps the a.m./p.m. ambiguity entirely; in British English either a colon or
a full point is acceptable as the separator, as long as it is applied consistently. In the twelve-hour
clock, figures are used once minutes are included (*4.30 p.m.*), but a round hour does not need the
redundant decimal point and zeros (*4 p.m.* rather than *4.00 p.m.*). In North America, Scandinavia, and
elsewhere the colon replaces the full point as separator (*4:30 p.m.*), a form the source notes is often
seen in British usage too. Some styles omit the a.m./p.m. punctuation altogether (pp. 194–195).

### 11.4 Roman numerals

The seven base roman numerals are I, V, X, L, C, D, and M; identical numbers add, a smaller number
placed after a larger one adds, and a smaller number placed before a larger one subtracts, with I, X,
and C each repeatable up to four times. Roman numerals should generally not be elided, though the
abbreviation *f.* ("following") may stand in for two consecutive numerals to save space; they can also
pose design problems (e.g. in tabulated contents pages) because of their variable character width. Oxford
's own usage: capital roman numerals number book chapters/appendices, play acts or long-poem sections,
and multi-volume works, with the division label matched in case-height to its numeral; lower-case roman
numerals number a book's preliminary pages and play scenes, and govern the case convention for
multi-level references. Capital roman numerals also follow the names of monarchs and popes (never
written as a numeral-based ordinal, though a fully spelled-out ordinal is acceptable), and are used
similarly, together with *Jr*, in American personal names to distinguish generations within a family.
A manuscript convention sometimes replaces a final or single *i* with a *j* (*ij*, *viij*), retained only
when reproducing a facsimile. Classical scholars make comparatively little use of roman numerals at all,
in both classical references and book volume numbers; where Latin-text editions do use them, they are
generally set in small capitals (pp. 195–197).

### 11.5 Date forms

Days and years in dates use cardinal, not ordinal, numbers, and the *-st*/*-rd*/*-th* endings are never
added to a date figure except when quoting another source verbatim. A date giving only the day and month
uses a cardinal day figure; a day given entirely alone (no month) is spelled out in ordinal word form. A
date giving only the month spells the month out, even in notes. British style orders a date day, month,
year with no internal punctuation; US style orders it month, day, year with a comma. A named day
preceding a date takes a comma, with a required terminal second comma if the construction sits
mid-sentence; no comma separates a month from a following year, nor appears in an ordinary four-figure
year (though longer dates take one). Abbreviated all-figure date forms belong in notes and references,
not running text: British style gives the day first (*2/11/03*), while the American all-figure form gives
the month first using slashes without full points — a genuine source of transatlantic ambiguity the
source calls out explicitly, since *11/2/03* reads as two different dates depending on the reader's
country. The shorthand *9/11* for the 11 September 2001 attacks is noted as surviving this ambiguity,
used identically in the US, Britain, and worldwide. The ISO dating system orders year, month, day with
hyphens, preferred in Japan and increasingly popular in technical/computing/financial contexts; an
alternative Continental convention uses (usually lower-case) roman numerals for the month, clarifying day
vs. month across British/American translations. Precise astronomical dates use *d*/*h*/*m*/*s*
abbreviations, or a decimal fraction of a day (pp. 197–198).

### 11.6.1 Decades

Decades may be given in words or figures; the apostrophe-elided form (*the '60s*) is avoided even when
referencing two consecutive decades together. A decade given as a word connotes the social, cultural,
and political character distinctive to that decade, while the numerical form is simply a neutral label
for the time span — some styles capitalize the word form specifically when it denotes a defined cultural
period (p. 198).

### 11.6.2 Centuries

Oxford style spells out centuries in words, though figures are used under some other editorial styles;
*c.* or *cent.* abbreviates a century in notes, references, and tabular matter, and both the spelled-out
and abbreviated adjectival forms require a hyphen. The Latin-derived abbreviation *s.* (*saeculum*,
plural *ss.*) is used specifically when dating medieval manuscripts. Centuries BC run backwards (counting
down toward 1 BC) — the reverse of AD's counting-up direction. Other languages' century-numbering
conventions vary: French uses small-capital roman numerals in roman type (or full capitals in italic),
with a superior *e*/*ème* suffix; German uses arabic numerals followed by a full point, occasionally a
capital roman numeral also followed by a full point (pp. 198–199).

### 11.6.3 Eras

BC and AD are the two most common era abbreviations, both set in small capitals; BC follows its numeral,
AD precedes it (and precedes a spelled-out century form too). Some writers add AD even for dates before
the first millennium as a clarifying label against ambiguity; any date span that crosses, or might be
mistaken as crossing, the BC/AD boundary should have the era abbreviation given explicitly on both ends,
even though convention identifies BC dates explicitly as a matter of course regardless. A list of less
common era designations follows, most placed before the year (the exceptions — BCE, CE, and BP — are
placed after): *a.Abr.* (year of Abraham, from 2016 BC, used by Eusebius and Jerome); AH (*anno
Hegirae*, from 16 July 622); AM (*anno mundi*, the Jewish era, from 7 October 3761 BC); AS (*anno
Seleuci*, the Seleucid era, variously from autumn 312 or spring 311 BC); AUC (*anno Urbis conditae*, the
supposed Roman era from 753 BC, though rarely actually used that way by the Romans themselves); BCE/CE
(secular alternatives to BC/AD); and BP (Before Present, used by geologists/palaeontologists, with AD
1950 fixed as the conventional "present," customary for dates before roughly 10,000 years ago and never
intermingled with BC/AD references in the same text). All era abbreviations except *a.Abr.* are set as
unspaced small capitals, even in italic (pp. 199–200).

### 11.7 Regnal years

Regnal years mark the successive anniversaries of a sovereign's accession, and so do not coincide with
calendar years; in England and America (though not Scotland) they began legally on 25 March up to 1751.
Before 1963, every Act of Parliament was numbered serially within its parliamentary session, itself
described by the regnal year(s) of the reigning sovereign during which it was held — a convention also
used to date other official edicts, such as university charters. A regnal year reference abbreviates the
monarch's name and follows it with a numeral; a table gives the standard abbreviations for English/
British monarchs' names (Car./Chas., Hen., Steph., Edw., Jac., Will., Eliz., P. & M., Wm. & Mar., Geo.,
Ric., Vic./Vict.), noting that John, Anne, Jane, and Mary are never abbreviated. Full citation style for
statutes including regnal years is cross-referenced to §13.5.1 (pp. 200–201).

### 11.8.1 Calendars — introduction

This section offers brief guidance on some less familiar calendars, cross-referencing Bonnie Blackburn
and Leofranc Holford-Strevens, *The Oxford Companion to the Year* (Oxford University Press, 1999) for a
fuller treatment. Dates in non-Western calendars are given in day, month, year order with no internal
punctuation, and months are never abbreviated even in notes (p. 201).

### 11.8.2 Old and New Style

Pope Gregory XIII's 1582 reform corrected the Julian calendar by omitting ten days (5–14 October of that
year) and revising the leap-year rule so that no centennial year is a leap year unless divisible by 400.
This Gregorian calendar was adopted quickly in Roman Catholic countries but far more slowly elsewhere —
not until 1752 in Britain (days 3–13 September omitted) and not until 1918 in Russia (days 16–28
February omitted). Dates reckoned under the Julian calendar are called Old Style; those under the
Gregorian, New Style. Separately from the calendar-system reform, different states also reckoned the
start of the new year from different dates until the mid-eighteenth century: France from 1 January
(since 1563), Scotland from 1600, England officially from 25 March as late as 1751 — producing real
historical confusion, illustrated by two worked examples: Charles I's execution was officially dated 30
January 1648 in England but 30 January 1649 in Scotland (differing new-year conventions), and although
Shakespeare and Cervantes both nominally died "23 April 1616," the England/Spain calendar-reform gap
meant 23 April in Spain was 13 April in England, and 23 April in England was 3 May in Spain. England,
Ireland, and the American colonies underwent both reforms essentially simultaneously in 1751–52: 1751
began 1 January in Scotland but 25 March in England, and both ended 31 December throughout Great Britain
and its colonies, so that 1752 began uniformly on 1 January, aligning with most of the Continent by 14
September. Because writers often conflate the two separate reforms under the same "Old Style"/"New
Style" labels, the source recommends reserving those terms strictly for the Julian/Gregorian calendar
distinction, and using "modern style" for the 1 January new-year convention and "Annunciation"/"Lady Day"
style for the 25 March one. A dual-dating notation (*23 August 1637 NS (= 13 August OS)*, shortened to
*13/23 August 1637*) expresses a date in both styles where precision matters; modern histories by
convention treat the year as beginning 1 January regardless of original context, using a dual-year form
(*30 January 1648/9*) or an explicit gloss (*30 January 1648 (= modern 1649)*) when it is necessary to
keep both reckonings in mind (pp. 201–203).

### 11.8.3 Greek calendars

In the classical period, years were designated by the name of a magistrate or other office-holder, a
system meaningful only within the city concerned. In the third century BC, historians adopted a common
framework based on the Olympiad — the four-yearly cycle of the Olympic Games, reckoned from 776 BC. Since
the Olympiad year did not align with any single historians' calendar year, Olympiad dates are
conventionally given as a split year (*776/5 BC* for the first year of the first Olympiad); modern
scholarly citation renders these as *Ol. 1,1*, *Ol. 1,2*, *Ol. 2,1*, and so on (p. 203).

### 11.8.4 French Republican calendar

Introduced on 5 October 1793 and discontinued from 1 January 1806, the French Republican calendar was
retroactively antedated to begin from the foundation of the Republic (22 September 1792). Its twelve
months were named for their seasonal significance across the four seasons (autumn: vendémiaire,
brumaire, frimaire; winter: nivôse, pluviôse, ventôse; spring: germinal, floréal, prairial; summer:
messidor, thermidor, fructidor), and are not capitalized in modern French, though they were at the time
and may still be capitalized in English usage. Years of the Republican calendar are printed in capital
roman numerals in French (*9 thermidor An II*), rendered in English as *Year II* (p. 203).

### 11.8.5 Jewish calendar

The Jewish year consists, in principle, of twelve months alternating between 30 and 29 days; an extra
30-day month is inserted in seven years out of every nineteen, and in some years the second month is
extended to 30 days or the third shortened to 29. The era is reckoned from a notional time of Creation,
fixed at 11:11 and 20 seconds p.m. on Sunday, 6 October 3761 BC (p. 204).

### 11.8.6 Muslim calendar

The Muslim year consists of twelve lunar months, so that thirty-three Muslim years roughly correspond to
thirty-two Christian ones. Years are counted from the first day of the year in which the Prophet made his
departure, or Hegira, from Mecca to Medina — namely Friday, 16 July AD 622 (p. 204).

## Grammar points

### 5.44 Pronoun case after "than" or "as . . . as"
In an elliptical comparative construction, the pronoun's case depends on the understood verb or
preposition. *My sister looks more like our father than I [do]* takes the nominative because an
implied verb (*do*) follows; *my sister looks more like our father than [she looks like] me* takes
the objective because an implied preposition-object relationship holds instead. Because the
intended meaning is often genuinely ambiguous to a reader even when the writer's intent is clear,
CMOS recommends rewording to avoid the elliptical construction altogether.

### 5.45 Special uses of personal pronouns
*He/him/his* were traditionally used as gender-indeterminate generics but are now "long regarded as
sexist" and declining in that use. *It* eliminates gender even when the referent's sex is known or
identifiable. *We, you,* and *they* can be used indefinitely (without a specific antecedent) — *we*
this way is called the "editorial we." *It* additionally serves four special grammatical roles: (a)
referring to a whole phrase/clause/sentence/implied thought, (b) as the subject of a *be*-verb or
introductory/expletive word with no antecedent noun, (c) as the grammatical subject in expressions
of time, weather, or distance, and (d) as an expletive anticipating the true subject or object later
in the sentence.

### 5.46 The singular "they"
Because English lacks a generic third-person-singular pronoun not marked for sex, speech and
informal writing have increasingly substituted the third-person plural *they/them/their/themselves*
(and the nonstandard singular *themself*) for a singular antecedent. CMOS accepts this in casual
contexts but still calls it ungrammatical in formal writing; alternating *he*/*she* is called
awkward, and an invented form like *s/he* is called "distracting at best" and often "ridiculous."
The traditional alternative — *he or she, him or her, his or her, himself or herself* — is offered as
formal but stylistically stilted if overused.

### 5.47 Possessive pronouns
The limiting (adjectival) possessive pronouns (*my, our, your, his, her, its, their*) qualify a
following noun; each has a corresponding absolute/independent form (*mine, ours, yours, his, hers,
its, theirs*) that stands alone and can itself serve as subject, object of a verb, or object of a
preposition. Combined with the preposition *of*, an absolute possessive produces a "double
possessive" (*that letter of Sheila's* → *that letter of hers*). None of the possessive personal
pronouns take an apostrophe.

### 5.48 Compound personal pronouns; "-self" forms
Several personal pronouns form compounds with the suffix *-self* or *-selves* (e.g.
*my→myself, our→ourselves, your→yourself/yourselves, him→himself, her→herself, it→itself,
them→themselves*); the indefinite pronoun *one* forms *oneself*. These compounds are identical in
nominative and objective case and have no possessive forms. They serve two functions: intensive
(for emphasis, in apposition to their referent) and reflexive (referring to the clause's own
subject).

### 5.49 Reflexive and intensive pronouns
Both are *-self* forms but differ functionally. A reflexive pronoun renames the subject as an object
or indirect object of the verb it accompanies (*she gave herself a pat on the back*); an intensive
pronoun sits in apposition to its referent purely for emphasis and is in the nominative case (*I
myself have won several writing awards*). CMOS illustrates the distinction with *I burned the papers
myself* (intensive — the object of *burned* is *papers*) versus *I burned myself* (reflexive — the
object of *burned* is *myself*). Using a *-self* form as subject or object where a plain personal
pronoun belongs is flagged as a common but nonstandard error: WRONG *"The staff and myself thank
you for your contribution"* / RIGHT *"The staff and I thank you for your contribution."*

### 5.50 Possessive pronouns versus contractions
None of the possessive personal pronouns (*my, mine, our, ours, your, yours, his, her, hers, its,
their, theirs*) take an apostrophe, nor does the possessive *whose*. Because the apostrophe is
otherwise a near-universal signal of the possessive in English, writers have a natural (and common)
tendency to misapply one to these forms — especially the problematic homophone pairs that *do*
legitimately take an apostrophe as contractions: *its* (possessive of *it*) vs. *it's* ("it is");
*your* (possessive of *you*) vs. *you're* ("you are"); *whose* (possessive of *who*) vs. *who's*
("who is"); and *their* (possessive of *they*) vs. *there* ("in that place") vs. *they're* ("they
are").

### 5.51 Demonstrative pronouns defined
A demonstrative (or deictic) pronoun points directly to its antecedent: *this/that* for singular
antecedents, *these/those* for plural. *This/these* indicate objects close in space, time, or
thought; *that/those* indicate objects comparatively remote. The antecedent may be a noun, phrase,
clause, sentence, or implied thought, provided it is clear. *Kind* and *sort*, each referring to
"one class," are often used with an adjectival *this* or *that* (*this kind of magazine*), and their
plurals *kinds*/*sorts* should pair with the plural demonstratives (*these kinds of magazines*).

### 5.52 Interrogative pronouns defined
The three interrogatives are *who, what,* and *which*. Only *who* declines: *who* (nominative),
*whom* (objective), *whose* (possessive). *Who* functions nominatively either as the subject of a
verb or as a predicate nominative after a linking verb. *Whom* functions objectively either as the
object of a verb or as the object of a preposition.

### 5.53 Referent of interrogative pronouns
*Who* and *which* both refer to a person but are not interchangeable: *who* is universal/general,
potentially referring to any person(s) living or dead, present or absent, and also asks for a
particular person's identity; *which* is selective/limited, asking for a particular member of an
already-known or already-referenced group. For a person, animal, or thing, either *which* or *what*
may be used; *what* applied to a person asks about character, occupation, or qualities, and applied
to a thing asks for one or more items in a category.

### 5.54 Relative pronouns defined
A relative pronoun introduces a dependent (relative) clause and relates it to the independent
clause. The common relative pronouns are *who, which, what,* and *that*. *Who* is the only one that
declines (*who*/*whom*/*whose*) and normally refers to a person, usable in first, second, or third
person. *Which* normally refers to an animal or a thing; *what* generally refers to a nonliving
thing; both *which* and *what* are used only in second and third person. *That* refers to a person,
animal, or thing and can be used in any of the three persons.

### 5.55 Case with relative pronouns
The case of a personal pronoun that is itself the antecedent of a relative pronoun does not govern
the relative pronoun's own case — an objective antecedent like *me* can still take the nominative
relative *who* (*she was referring to me, who never graduated from college*). When a technically
correct construction nonetheless sounds awkward or stilted (*I, who am wronged, have a grievance*),
CMOS recommends "preventive grammar" — recasting the sentence entirely (*I have a grievance, for I
have been wronged*) rather than keeping the awkward original.

### 5.56 Positional nuances with relative pronouns
A relative pronoun stands in the nominative case when nothing intervenes between it and its verb
(*the professor who lectured was brilliant*). When a subject does intervene between the relative
pronoun and the verb, the relative pronoun's case is instead governed by the following verb or by
another word within its own clause (*he whom I called is no longer there*; *it was John who they
thought was in the bleachers*).

### 5.57 Antecedent of relative pronoun
A relative pronoun's antecedent is normally a noun or pronoun in the independent clause the relative
clause depends on, and for clarity should immediately precede the relative pronoun. The antecedent
can also be a longer noun phrase or a clause, which can produce genuine ambiguity — CMOS's example:
in *the bedroom of the villa, which was painted pink*, it is unclear whether *which* refers to the
bedroom or to the villa.

### 5.58 Omitted antecedent of relative pronoun
When no antecedent noun is expressed, *what* can stand in for "that which" (*is this what you were
looking for?*). When an antecedent *is* present, a different relative pronoun must be chosen
instead: *who* for a person, *that* for a restrictive clause (one essential to the sentence's basic
meaning, not removable without changing that meaning), or *which* for a nonrestrictive clause (one
removable without affecting the sentence's basic meaning, typically set off by commas).

### 5.59 Relative pronoun after "one"
A relative pronoun takes its grammatical number from its antecedent. This is straightforward when
the antecedent is simply *one* (singular). But when *one* is part of a noun phrase with a plural
noun in the genitive construction (*one of the few countries that cultivate farm-raised fish*; *one
of those people who are famous just for being famous*), the relative pronoun's antecedent is usually
the plural noun, not *one* itself — though CMOS cautions that in some constructions the antecedent
genuinely remains *one* (*he is the one among them who is trustworthy*), so careful reading is
required rather than a blanket rule.

### 5.60 Possessive forms of relative pronouns
*Of whom* and *of which* are the possessive forms of *who* and *which* respectively (*the child, the
mother of whom we talked about, is in kindergarten*). CMOS notes these forms "have an old-fashioned
sound" and can often be rephrased more naturally using *whose* instead (*the child whose mother we
talked about is in kindergarten*) — see §5.61.

### 5.61 "Whose" and "of which"
Both *who* and *which* can take *whose* as a possessive form, with *whose* substituting for the more
cumbersome *of which* even when the antecedent is nonhuman (*a movie the conclusion of which is
unforgettable* → *a movie whose conclusion is unforgettable*). Some writers object to this nonhuman
use of *whose*, but CMOS characterizes the usage as centuries old and widely accepted as a way of
avoiding unnecessary awkwardness, comparing *the company whose stock rose faster* with *the company
the stock of which rose faster* and judging the possessive *whose* to read more smoothly; either
form is called acceptable.

### 5.62 Compound relative pronouns
*Who, whom, what,* and *which* form compound relative pronouns by adding the suffix *-ever*:
*whoever, whomever, whichever,* and *whatever*. These compound forms apply universally to any or all
persons or things (*whatever you do, let me know*; *whoever needs to write a report about this book
may borrow it*).

### 5.63 "Who" versus "whom"
*Who* and *whoever* are the nominative forms, used as subjects or predicate nominatives; *whom* and
*whomever* are the objective forms, used as the object of a verb or preposition. CMOS identifies
three recurring sources of case confusion: (1) inverted interrogative word order can obscure a
pronoun's true grammatical function — resorting the sentence into standard subject–verb–object
order (*I should say who is calling*) clarifies the correct case (RIGHT: *who should I say is
calling?* / WRONG: *whom should I say is calling?*); (2) a pronoun's case is governed by its
function within its own clause even when that clause as a whole serves a different function in the
main sentence (e.g. the clause *whoever will listen* is the object of the preposition *to*, but
*whoever* itself is the subject *within* that clause, so it stays nominative: RIGHT *I'll talk to
whoever will listen*, WRONG *I'll talk to whomever will listen*; conversely *whomever* is correct as
the object of *choose* even though its clause serves as the sentence's subject: RIGHT *whomever you
choose will suit me*); and (3) a nested attribution/identification clause (e.g. *you choose*) can
further obscure which word governs the pronoun's case.

### 5.64 Indefinite pronouns
An indefinite pronoun generally or indefinitely represents an object already identified or not
needing specific identification. The most common simple indefinite pronouns are *another, any,
both, each, either, neither, none, one, other, some,* and *such*; compound indefinite pronouns
include *anybody, anything, anyone, everybody, everyone, everything, nobody, no one, oneself,* and
*someone*. *Each, either,* and *neither* are also called distributive pronouns because they separate
the objects referred to from others referred to nearby. Indefinite pronouns have grammatical number:
as the subject of a verb they are usually singular (*everyone is enjoying the dinner*), but some can
carry a plural sense in context (*everyone understood the risk, but they were lured by promises of
big returns*). Indefinite pronouns are unaffected by gender or grammatical person, and their
nominative and objective forms are identical. The possessive is formed with *'s* (*that is no one's
fault*) or, for the compound-with-*else* construction, with the adverb *else* plus *'s* (*no one
else's cups were broken*).

### 5.65 Adjective pronouns
An adjective pronoun (also called a pronominal adjective) functions as a noun modifier and must
agree in number with the noun it modifies (*all people*, *these sorts of favors*, *those kinds of
indulgences*). All pronouns other than nonpossessive personal pronouns, *who*, and *none* can serve
this adjectival role (*those windows*, *some coyotes*); the adjective *no* is used in place of *none*
in this function (*no one astronaut*, *no other paradise*).

### 5.66 Adjectives defined
An adjective is a word that modifies a noun or pronoun — informally, a "describing word." An
adjective may add a new idea more definitely or fully (*red wagon*, *human error*) or may limit
(*three pigs*, *this time*). Most adjectives derive from nouns (*plentiful* from *plenty*), though
some derive from verbs, roots, or other adjectives, usually via a derivational suffix. CMOS lists the
common adjective-forming suffixes *-able, -al, -ary, -ed, -en, -esque, -ful, -ible, -ic, -ish, -ive,
-less, -like, -ly, -ous, -some,* and *-y*, each with an example (*manageable, mystical, elementary,
hammered, wooden, statuesque, harmful, inaccessible, artistic, foolish, demonstrative, helpless,
childlike, ghostly, perilous, lonesome, sunny*). Many adjectives, however, have no distinctive ending
and are recognizable only by their grammatical function (*old, tall, brilliant*).

### 5.67 Proper adjectives
A proper adjective is one that is, or derives from, a proper name, and always begins with a capital
letter (*a New York minute*, *a Cuban cigar*, *a Canadian dollar*) — though the noun it modifies is
not thereby capitalized. CMOS recommends that a place-name containing a comma (e.g. *Toronto,
Ontario*; *New Delhi, India*) generally not be used attributively, because a second comma is then
arguably obligatory and reads awkwardly (*we met in a Toronto, Ontario, restaurant*); recasting with
a prepositional phrase (*a marketplace in New Delhi, India*) is offered as the more readable
alternative.

## Grammar points

### §9.1 — General principles

A direct quotation reproduces the exact words of the original — of any length, though subject to
copyright limits (cross-referenced to Chapter 20). Unlike a paraphrase, a direct quotation must
exactly reproduce the wording of the source, though how precisely the original's *form* (spelling,
punctuation, etc.) is replicated varies by context and editorial preference — this tension is
resolved more specifically in §9.3.1 below. Quotations from early manuscripts may need more
specialist conventions not covered in this chapter.

### §9.2.1 — Displayed and run-on quotations

See the Vocabulary table above (displayed/run-on quotation entries). No fixed word-count threshold
exists; a roughly-fifty-word prose quotation is a common rule of thumb for displaying rather than
running on, but editorial judgment, context, and overall page appearance ultimately govern the
choice — even a single short, central quotation might be displayed for emphasis. Displayed
quotations may be set in reduced type, reduced leading, full or narrower measure, fully or partially
indented, block-centred, or justified/unjustified; a commonly used style indents one em on both
sides. Displayed quotations are never set wholly in italic even if the original source used italic
type (individual words may still be italicized). Discontinuous consecutive displayed quotations (no
author text intervening) are marked apart by extra leading (vertical space) rather than any
punctuation device.

### §9.2.2 — Introducing quotations and direct speech

See the Vocabulary table's "interpolation (speech-tag sense)" entry. A speech tag like *he said* is
normally comma-separated from the quoted speech; a colon is an alternative, typically for more
formal or multi-sentence speech, for emphasis, or to clarify sentence structure after a parenthetical
clause. A very short speech (e.g. *He called 'Good morning!'*) needs no introductory punctuation at
all, nor does a quotation grammatically integrated into the surrounding sentence's own syntax (e.g.
*He is alleged to have replied that 'our old college no longer exists'*). *Yes* and *no*, and
question words like *where*/*why*, take quotation marks only when they represent actual direct
speech, not when they represent reported speech or tacit paraphrase (contrast *She asked, 'Really?
Where?'* against *The governors said no to our proposal.*).

### §9.2.3 — Quotation marks

See the Vocabulary table above for the British/US quotation-mark-order entries and the separate
British/US punctuation-placement entries — this section is the chapter's single densest concentration
of explicit dialect contrast. Nested quotations beyond two levels simply revert to the original
mark (single-double-single, or vice versa).

### §9.2.4 — Dialogue

See the Vocabulary table's "dialogue" and "em rule" entries. Three coexisting conventions are
catalogued: ordinary quotation marks with each new speaker's words on a new, indented line; an
em-rule-opened style common in some fiction, with closing quotation marks dropped entirely; and a
no-marking style relying purely on a syntax shift to signal the change from narration to speech (a
speech verb is often dispensed with altogether in this style, e.g. *I decide it's Spencer's fault,
and sit up grumpily. / 'Who let you in?'*). Thought and imagined dialogue may or may not be
quotation-marked, so long as a single work is internally consistent about which it uses.

### §9.2.5 — Sources

A quotation's source is normally given in a footnote/endnote or an author-date reference if the
work uses that referencing system; for a displayed prose quotation, the source may instead appear in
the text itself, following the quotation's end — either in parentheses after an em space, or ranged
right on the quotation's own measure (on the same line if there is room, otherwise the line below).

### §9.3.1 — Spelling, capitalization, and punctuation

The chapter's core preservation principle, directly relevant to how this project should treat any
quoted dialect/slang forms: in quotations from printed sources, spelling, capitalization, and
punctuation should normally follow the original exactly. Listed exceptions where silent change is
acceptable: correcting obvious errors (a missing full point, unclosed brackets/quotation marks);
regularizing punctuation that merely differs from house style (foreign question-mark/quotation-mark
forms, standardizing single vs. double marks and the dash/hyphen family); lower-casing a quotation's
opening capital to integrate it into a surrounding sentence; retaining or expanding orthographic
signs/abbreviations and ligatured letters per editorial preference; consistently modernizing i/j and
u/v (though retaining the printed forms is "generally safest"); changing double v (vv) to w;
regularizing the long s to ordinary s (it is "a variant form rather than a distinct letter"); and
rationalizing full-caps text to upper/lower case or small caps. By contrast, Old/Middle English's
ash, eth, thorn, yogh, and wynn should be *preserved* where possible rather than modernized away
(see the Vocabulary table entries above and §12.13.1). Ligatured œ and æ may be retained as a
ligature or printed as two separate letters, per editorial policy.

### §9.3.2 — Interpolation and correction

See the Vocabulary table's interpolation, *sic*, *recte*, and *rectius* entries above. Square
brackets mark any editorially-inserted words not part of the verbatim original, used sparingly
(e.g. to preserve grammatical structure while suppressing irrelevant phrasing, or to clarify an
unclear reference). *Sic* confirms an apparent error or unexpected form is reproduced exactly as
found — never used merely to flag erratic spelling in general, only to remove genuine doubt about
accuracy, and never replaced with an editorializing exclamation mark. Some editorial policies permit
silent correction of trivial original errors when transmitting content matters more than exact
formal reproduction.

### §9.3.3 — Omissions

See the Vocabulary table's "ellipsis" entry. An ellipsis is never placed at a quotation's own start
or end (the reader is expected to understand the source continues beyond what's quoted regardless).
Punctuation immediately before/after an ellipsis can generally be suppressed unless it aids sense
(e.g. a question/exclamation mark); Oxford house style retains a full point closed up to the
preceding text if the sentence genuinely ended there. An ellipsis that is part of the *original*
quoted text (not an editorial omission) is distinguished, where both occur in the same quotation, by
bracketing the editorial one. In a displayed quotation broken into paragraphs, an omitted
intervening paragraph is marked by an ellipsis at the end of the paragraph before the gap.

### §9.3.4 — Typography

A quotation is not a facsimile: font changes, bold type, underscoring, ornaments, and the original's
exact layout may generally be ignored when reproducing it; italicization may be kept if helpful or
dropped if excessive.

### §9.4.1 — Run-on verse quotations

See the Vocabulary table's "verse-line divider" entry. More than one line of quoted verse is
normally displayed line-by-line, but verse may instead be run on in text, with each line division
marked by Oxford's preferred vertical bar (or the also-common solidus), spaced on both sides and
never starting a new typeset line.

### §9.4.2 — Displayed verse

Poetry (including blank verse) is generally centred on its page's longest line, or optically centred
if that would look disproportionate; where a poem's own indentation clearly varies meaningfully (true
of some modern poetry, where spacing can itself be part of the copyrighted work), the original copy's
exact indentation should be followed, and it is useful to supply the typesetter a copy of the
original to work from. Turnover lines are indented 1 em beyond the poem's own maximum indentation.
Capitals are not automatically imposed at the start of each verse line — some modern verse has none
at all, and the conventions for Greek/Latin verse restrict initial capitals to only a few lines
(cross-referenced to §12.8.5, §12.12.3).

### §9.4.3 — Omissions (verse)

Omissions within verse run into prose text follow the same convention as ordinary prose omissions.
Within displayed verse, the omission of one or more whole lines is marked by a full line of points
(2-em-spaced, falling roughly 2 ems inside the longest line's measure) rather than an ordinary
ellipsis — see the Vocabulary table's "line of points" entry. An ellipsis is used instead only when
the omission falls at the *end* of a single displayed verse line. An omission at the very start of a
displayed verse's first line is instead shown by right-ranging that line with the line below it,
with no ellipsis mark at all.

### §9.4.4 — Sources (verse)

A short verse source (book/canto/line number, or a short title) is placed in parentheses on the
verse quotation's own last line if room allows, beginning 1 em to the right of the quotation's
longest line (Oxford practice); if the source citation is too long to fit, it instead goes on the
next line down, ranged right with the end of the quotation's longest line.

### §9.5 — Plays

Publishers vary in how they present play extracts, with quotations from plays treatable as run-on
quotations or as ordinary prose/poetry extracts, without strict adherence to a play's original
layout/spacing/character-name styling — any consistently-applied, sensible pattern is acceptable. If
a speaker's name is included at all, the extract is best printed displayed rather than run on.
Oxford's own preferred format sets speaker names in small capitals, ranged full left (see the
Vocabulary table's "small capitals" entry); verse-play dialogue runs into the first line after the
speaker's name (1 em indent, not centred), with turnovers indented 2 ems in verse plays and 1 em in
prose plays. In verse plays specifically, a single metrical line can be split across more than one
speaker's part; Oxford's format ideally preserves this by progressively indenting each successive
speech fragment with an interword space clear of the previous fragment's end. Stage directions should
not be included in a play extract unless directly relevant to the point under discussion (in which
case their original styling may be preserved or adapted); line numbers are likewise not normally
included.

### §9.6 — Epigraphs

See the Vocabulary table's "epigraph" entry above. Publishers set their own house conventions for
epigraph layout; Oxford's own academic books set them in small type, treating verse epigraphs much
like displayed verse quotations and optically centring them. An epigraph's own source line is usually
far less complete than a full citation (sometimes no more than an author's name, or name and dates),
though fuller bibliographic detail may be given in a note if it seems genuinely helpful; the date or
circumstances of the epigraph, or the quoted author's own dates, may be included if thought germane.

### §9.7 — Non-English quotations

See the Vocabulary table's non-English-quotation and isolated-foreign-term entries above. Non-English
quotations follow the same wording/spelling/punctuation/capitalization/layout rules, and the same
omission/interpolation/sourcing conventions, as English quotations — including silently adapting
foreign punctuation forms (guillemets, German-style low/high double marks) to standard British
quotation-mark convention, consistent with the general §9.3.1 house-style-regularization allowance.
Where both an original and a translation are given, the second of the pair is typically parenthesized
after the first (either order is used); if a displayed translation and a displayed original both
appear, the second of the two is parenthesized, sometimes italicized if it is the original. A verse
original may be followed by either a verse or a prose translation. The chapter separately notes that
appropriate conventions are needed for transliterating quotations from non-Roman alphabets, and that
a German Eszett (ß) can normally be regularized to double s (ss) — both cross-referenced to Chapter

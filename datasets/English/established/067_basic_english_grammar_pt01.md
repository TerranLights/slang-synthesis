## Grammar points

### Noun plural-formation rules (spelling system)

The book presents a complete, graded spelling-rule system for English noun pluralization, more
systematically tabulated than CMOS's more terminology-focused treatment: default +s; +es after a
sibilant ending (ch/sh/s/ss/x — *bus→buses, watch→watches, fox→foxes*); y→ies after a consonant+y but
plain +s after a vowel+y (*baby→babies* vs. *key→keys, turkey→turkeys*); f/fe→ves for a subset of
words vs. plain +s for another subset vs. dual-accepted spellings for a third subset (see Vocabulary
table); -o nouns split three ways between +s, +es, and dual-accepted; fully irregular (non-suffixal)
forms; zero-plural (invariant) nouns; and pluralia tantum (always-plural nouns, made singular via "a
pair of").

### Adjective-of-origin, -color, -size, and -quantity subtypes (p. 59–63)

The book subdivides descriptive adjectives into named functional categories — adjectives of origin
(*Chinese, Mexican, Dutch, Filipino*, etc. — nationality/origin adjectives, capitalized), color, size,
and number/quantity — a simpler, example-driven taxonomy than CMOS's more abstract adjective
discussion. An exercise (p. 64) drills labeling adjectives by these five categories (C=color, S=size,
Ql=quality, O=origin, Qn=number/quantity), confirming this is the book's own internal classification
scheme for descriptive adjectives, not a borrowed term from elsewhere.

### Comparative and superlative adjective formation (pp. 65–69)

Full paradigm: default +er/+est; -e-final adjectives add only +r/+st; one-syllable adjectives with a
single vowel before a final consonant double that consonant before +er/+est; two-syllable -y
adjectives change y→i before +er/+est; two-or-more-syllable adjectives (and most borrowed/Latinate
ones) take periphrastic *more*/*most* instead; a small closed set (*good/bad/little/many/much*) is
fully suppletive. *Than* is the standard comparative-linking word; *the* conventionally precedes the
superlative.

### Simple present tense: general truths and scheduled future (pp. 80–82)

Simple present covers both generic/habitual truths ("The sun rises in the east") and pre-arranged
future events with a fixed schedule ("The train departs in five minutes," "The new supermarket opens
next Friday") — the latter use is a genuine, distinct future-reference function of the present tense
worth flagging for mechanics analysis (parallel to the "going to"/present-progressive future forms
covered later in the same chapter).

### Be, have, do, shall/will, would/should: full person/number auxiliary paradigms (pp. 83–121)

The book tabulates complete singular/plural × 1st/2nd/3rd-person paradigms for each primary auxiliary
(*am/is/are*, *was/were*, *have/has*, *do/does/did*), plus the modal auxiliaries *can/could*,
*may/might*, *shall/will*, *would/should*, each with its full contraction set (*I'm, you're, he's,
we're, they're; I've, you've, he's, we've, they've; I'll, you'll, he'll, we'll, they'll; I'd, you'd,
he'd, we'd, they'd*; negative contractions *isn't, aren't, wasn't, weren't, haven't, hasn't, don't,
doesn't, didn't, can't, couldn't, won't, shan't, shouldn't, wouldn't*). Usage splits: *can/could* =
ability (and informally, permission-asking); *may/might* = permission-granting and likelihood
(*might* also serves as the past tense of *may*); *shall/will* for 1st person vs. *will*-only for
2nd/3rd person in the future tense (a now largely prescriptive British-influenced distinction the
book still teaches); *should* = obligation/ought-to; *would* = past tense of *will*, and also the
polite-offer/request form (*would like*).

### Present progressive, past progressive, present perfect, future ("going to") (pp. 89–96, 106, 110)

Present progressive = am/is/are + present participle, used for both actions in progress now and
planned future actions. Past progressive = was/were + present participle, for actions in progress at
a specific past moment. Present perfect = have/has + past participle, for past happenings that
explain or affect the present (book notes irregular-verb past participles don't follow the regular
-ed pattern). *Going to* + base verb is presented as an alternative future-tense construction
alongside *shall/will* and the future-referring simple present.

### Subject-verb agreement with collective nouns (pp. 124–125)

Collective nouns (*family, team, audience*, etc.) take either a singular verb (when the group acts as
one unit) or a plural verb (when members act as individuals) — the same optional-agreement pattern
CMOS's §5.8 already captured, but illustrated here with explicit singular/plural minimal-pair
examples ("That family has moved to Texas" vs. "The family were giving their opinions"). *People,
cattle, police* are flagged as always-plural-verb nouns despite not ending in -s.

### Sentence types and the imperative (pp. 139–149)

Four sentence types are named: declarative (statement), interrogative (question), exclamatory
(strong emotion), imperative (command). The imperative uses the bare base form of the verb with no
expressed subject; *do* or *please* before an imperative softens/politizes it, as does the modal
*would* ("Would you please talk quietly?"). Two question types are distinguished: yes/no questions
(formed by inverting an auxiliary/helping verb before the subject) and *wh*-questions (using
*what/which/who/whom/how/when/where*, with the auxiliary likewise preceding the subject — except when
the *wh*-word is itself the subject, in which case no inversion occurs: "Who told you that?").

### Apostrophe/possessive-formation rules (pp. 153–156)

Full paradigm: singular nouns/names add 's (including names already ending in -s, e.g. *the
princess's ball, a rhinoceros's skin, the boss's party* — 's, not bare apostrophe); plural nouns
already ending in -s add only a trailing apostrophe (*the birds' beaks, the boys' school*); plural
nouns *not* ending in -s (children, men, women, people) add 's like a singular; joint possession by
two people joined by *and* marks only the second name ("Barbara and David's house"); possessive forms
can stand alone without a following noun ("This room is my brother's") or denote a person's
home/shop/office by ellipsis ("I'll buy some bread at the baker's"). The apostrophe is also used for
contracted letters, and *has*/*is* are both commonly shortened to *'s* after a noun or proper noun
(*"The mail's arrived"* = has arrived; *"Sally's here"* = is here) — a genuine surface ambiguity
between the two contractions worth flagging for mechanics analysis.

## Grammar points

### ME spelling reforms and the loss of OE graphs (§4.4.1)

Middle English spelling reform, driven by Norman scribal conventions, both reintroduced older
practices and introduced genuinely new ones. ⟨þ⟩/⟨ð⟩ were gradually replaced by ⟨th⟩ (a
*re*-introduction of a pre-900 OE practice); OE's joined-up ⟨uu⟩ for [w] was replaced by the
Norman-introduced single letter ⟨w⟩ (wynn ⟨ƿ⟩, its OE successor, was itself dropped). The three OE
allophonic realizations of /g/ ([g]/[ɣ]/[j]) and the two of /k/ ([k]/[tʃ]) were orthographically
disambiguated: ⟨g⟩ kept [g], ⟨ȝ⟩ (yogh, retained from OE ⟨ʒ⟩) marked [ɣ]/[j] in early ME, with [ɣ]
later reassigned to the digraph ⟨gh⟩; ⟨ch⟩ was introduced for the palatal affricate [tʃ] (next to
front vowels), leaving ⟨c⟩ for [k] (next to back vowels/consonants) — with ⟨k⟩ substituted for ⟨c⟩
specifically before front vowels to avoid ambiguity (*kin*, *king*). OE ⟨hw⟩ became ⟨wh⟩ (*what*);
OE ⟨sc⟩ [ʃ] became the more transparent ⟨sh⟩ (*fish*); OE ⟨cw⟩ [kw] became French-style ⟨qu⟩
(*queen*); OE ⟨cg⟩ [dʒ] became ⟨gg⟩, later ⟨dg(e)⟩ (*edge*). For vowels, scribes began using doubled
vowel graphs (*rood*) and a word-final unstressed ⟨e⟩ to signal a preceding long vowel (*fode*), and
adopted the French spelling ⟨ou⟩ for long [uː] (*hous*) — crucially, at the point of adoption these
graphs did not yet carry their modern phonetic values; the sound changes that would eventually give
them their modern values (the Great Vowel Shift) would not occur until the Early Modern period.
(p.114)

### ME pronunciation: consonant changes and the erosion of OE allophony (§4.4.2.1)

ME consonants were relatively more stable than ME vowels, but underwent several changes with
far-reaching grammatical consequences. OE's word-initial consonant clusters with [h] (*hl-*, *hn-*,
*hr-*) simplified by losing the [h]; clusters *hw-*, *sw-*, *tw-* lost their glide ([w]) specifically
before back vowels, beginning in southern dialects and spreading. Most consequentially, the three OE
allophonic pairs ([f]~[v], [s]~[z], [θ]~[ð], conditioned by word-initial/final vs. medial position)
began losing their clean complementary distribution for two converging reasons: (1) French/Latin
loanwords introduced word-initial [v] and [z] directly (*veal*, *zodiac*), and analogy extended this
voicing pattern to native words too, in southern dialects first (*this*, *they*, *vixen* < OE
*fyxe*); (2) widespread loss of OE's unstressed final inflectional vowels (reduced to schwa, then
often dropped) relocated formerly-medial voiced allophones to word-final position (OE *giefan* 'to
give' → ME *gif*/*yive*, with the originally-medial [v] now word-final), destroying the original
positional conditioning entirely. Once the conditioning environment disappeared, the old allophone
pairs were free to become independent phonemes — a textbook case of **phonemicization via loss of
complementary distribution**. Separately, the [n] of several OE inflectional endings (infinitive
*-an*, n-stem plurals, weak-declension adjective endings) was gradually lost in both pronunciation
and spelling over the ME period — a development with direct grammatical/syntactic consequences
explored below. (pp.114–116)

### ME vowel changes: unrounding, raising/rounding, monophthongization, new diphthongs, quantitative length changes (§4.4.2.2)

OE's short stressed vowels mostly survived unchanged into ME, with two exceptions: short [æ]
lowered to [a] (dialectally variable — southwest Midland/Kentish texts show the older [æ] value
persisting longer), and short [y] unrounded to [ɪ] (beginning in Northern/East Midland dialects).
Among the long vowels: OE long [ɑː] rounded to [ɔː] everywhere south of the Humber (the North instead
raised it to [eː], the ancestor of modern Scots *hame*); OE long [yː] unrounded to [iː], again
beginning in Northern/East Midland dialects and spreading, while Kent/South-East instead merged it
with [eː]; and OE long [æː]/[ɛː] and [eː] merged into a single [ɛː] in ME, not distinguished in
spelling at this point even though a distinction briefly resurfaced in Early Modern spelling
conventions (*ea/ei* for the old [ɛː], *ee/ie* for the old [eː]) before the two values re-merged.
ME vowels were also subject to **quantitative** change (length alone, no quality change): short
vowels **lengthened** before the consonant clusters *mb, ld, rd, nd, rð* (blocked if a third
consonant followed — hence the length split between *child* [aɪ] and *children* [ɪ]) and in **open
syllables** (a syllable ending in the stressed vowel itself, e.g. OE *bacan* > ME long-voweled
*baken*); conversely, long vowels **shortened** before doubled consonants, before certain consonant
clusters, and in the first syllable of a trisyllabic word. The four OE short/long diphthongs
(*eo/ēo/ea/ēa*) **monophthongized** in ME, merging respectively with [a]/[ɛː]/[ɛ]/[ɛː]. New
diphthongs then emerged from distinct sources: French loanwords introduced vowel+glide combinations
like [ɔɪ] (*joie*, *cloister* — later merged with [ʊɪ] from native words like *boil*); vowels
followed by OE [w] or the OE back-vowel-conditioned allophone [ɣ] vocalized to produce
[aʊ]/[ɔʊ]/[ɛʊ]/[ɪʊ] (*claw*, *flow*, *lewd*, *new*; *lawe* < *lagu*, *bowe* < *boga*); and front
vowels followed by [j] produced [aɪ]/[eɪ] (*mayn* 'great' < *mæʒen*; *pleie* 'to play' <
*plegian*) — a development that, unlike the others, had actually begun already within the Old
English period. Finally, and most consequentially for the grammatical system, **unstressed-syllable
vowels** underwent large-scale leveling to schwa (spelled ⟨e⟩) over the ME period, which — since
unstressed syllables carried the bulk of OE's inflectional endings — progressively collapsed the
formal distinctions that OE case/gender/number marking had depended on, while plural marking
(quantitatively reduced but not eliminated) and the genitive case survived this leveling. (pp.116–117)

### ME nominal morphology: the collapse of case/gender, survival of plural and genitive (§4.4.3.1)

By the end of the twelfth century, unstressed-vowel leveling (above) had erased most of OE's noun
case/number/gender marking. Plural and genitive marking, however, survived and were actually
**extended by analogy** to nouns that had not historically carried them: the OE *a*-stem plural
*-es* and the n-stem plural *-en* both remained productive ME plural markers, with a clear dialectal
split (*-en* favored in the South, *-es* elsewhere, especially the North) — alternate forms like
*deofen*/*deuils*, *kine*/*cows*, *eyen*/*eyes* attest the competition before *-es* won out as the
sole productive plural. The genitive singular *-es* was likewise extended generally but became
formally indistinguishable from the plural *-es* in both speech and writing — disambiguated only by
context, e.g. *eorles sunu* 'the earl's son' is recognizable as genitive because of the
possessor+possessed-noun word order, directly continued into modern English (now distinguished in
spelling by the apostrophe). Some noun classes resisted genitive *-s* marking altogether: kinship
nouns ending in *-er* (*fader bone* 'father's murderer'), nouns that had been grammatically feminine
in OE (*his lady grace*), and proper names (*Adam kynde*, *God hert*). ME also saw the beginnings of
two alternative possessive strategies that would become more prominent in Early Modern English: the
periphrastic **of-genitive** (*þe termes of Judé*) and the emerging **his-genitive**, where *his*
functions as a possessive marker regardless of the possessor's actual grammatical features (*Seint
Gregore hys bokes* 'St. Gregory's books'). (pp.118–119)

### ME pronouns: loss of the dual, case-form leveling, and the Norse-derived th-pronoun takeover (§4.4.3.2)

ME pronouns, inheriting more case/number/gender distinctions than any other ME word class, lost the
OE dual forms outright and underwent a leveling of the OE accusative/dative distinction in the 3rd
person singular (OE *hine*/*him* → single object form *him*) — paralleling a leveling that had
already occurred earlier, within OE itself, for the 1st and 2nd persons. The ME dialect corpus shows
substantial regional variation in the surviving pronoun forms, most strikingly in the 3rd person
plural: the h-initial OE forms (*hi*, *hem/heom*, *hira/heora*) were gradually, and completely,
displaced by th-initial forms borrowed from Old Norse (*they*, *them*, *their*), a change that
**began specifically in the Northern and Midland dialect areas that had once been Danelaw
settlements**, reinforcing the direct historical link between the area of heaviest Norse settlement
and this particular grammatical borrowing (cf. Ch.3's note on this same pronoun-borrowing process).
(pp.119–120)

### ME definite article and adjectives: loss of agreement, retained weak/strong distinction (south only) (§4.4.3.3–4.4.3.4)

With the collapse of noun inflection, the OE definite article's full gender/case/number-agreement
paradigm was eventually replaced everywhere by a single invariant form, *the* — establishing the
modern system in which the article's form never varies regardless of the noun's grammatical
properties or syntactic role. ME adjectives similarly lost case/gender/number agreement through the
same *-n* loss and vowel leveling described above, but the **southern dialects alone** retained a
weak/strong formal distinction for a time: a monosyllabic adjective (or one already ending in a
consonant) took weak-form *-e* when it followed a definite article/demonstrative/possessive, or when
it modified a plural noun (*god man* 'good man', strong, vs. *the gode man*, weak, vs. *gode men*,
plural). Comparative/superlative suffixes (OE *-ra*/*-ost*) also underwent the general unstressed-
vowel reduction to schwa, giving the modern spellings *-er*/*-est*; a long root vowel was sometimes
shortened (with doubled consonant in spelling) when a superlative suffix was added (*greet* →
*grettest*). (pp.120–121)

### ME verb morphology: continuity of weak/strong classes under dialectally variable leveling (§4.4.3.5)

The OE weak/strong verb class distinction, and much of its inflectional detail, survived into ME, but
under active leveling. The infinitive suffix (OE *-an*) lost its final *-n* universally and was
reduced to schwa; southern dialects retained the *-en* spelling longer than the North, which had
already reduced to *-e* — and as the ending's morphological signaling value weakened, prepositions
like *to*/*for* increasingly took over marking the infinitive function instead. Present
indicative/subjunctive inflections, identical for weak and strong verbs since OE, continued to
level: the *-en* plural ending disappeared, replaced by schwa (indicative) or *-e* (subjunctive), and
by the late ME period only the 3rd person singular indicative survives distinctly in Modern English
(*-s*, a form that specifically descends from the northern dialect's innovative ending, not the
southern one). Weak and strong preterite conjugations likewise continued, with strong verbs largely
retaining their OE ablaut patterns, but under similar leveling of person/number suffixes — notably,
the ME *Gawain* manuscript (Northern) shows the number distinction between singular and plural
preterite forms (*ran* vs. *runnen*) already breaking down in favor of one invariant preterite form
per verb regardless of subject number, a Northern-dialect-led innovation that became the universal
modern pattern; the conservative Southern dialects maintained the OE-style singular/plural preterite
split much longer. In the past participle, the completive prefix *ge-* reduced to *y-*/*i-* in
pronunciation and spelling (*ibolwe* < *gebolgen*) before disappearing entirely; the *-en* participle
ending was retained in the North/some Midlands but lost in the South, and modern English inherited a
mix of both outcomes even among originally-strong verbs (*broken*, *eaten* vs. *drunk*, *sung*). As
in OE, further strong verbs continued regularizing to the weak pattern by analogy throughout ME
(*crept*, *helped* from originally-strong *crēap*/*healp*), and the present participle inflection
shows clear regional variation (*-and(e)* Northern, *-inde* South-West Midland, *-ende* East), with
only the Southern form *-ing* surviving into Modern English. (pp.121–123)

### ME syntax: continued VO dominance, inversion patterns, adjective order, passive/auxiliary developments (§4.4.4)

The VO word-order preference already dominant by late OE continued into ME, with OV still occurring
specifically when the object was a pronoun. Subject–verb inversion (yielding VS or V(S)O order)
occurred in two environments: imperatives, and clauses opening with a fronted adverb of place, time,
or manner — a pattern distinct from Modern English, where only auxiliary/modal verbs invert, not full
lexical verbs. Adjectives typically pre-modified their noun as in Modern English, but ME verse
sometimes placed a single modifying adjective **after** its noun (*sceld deore* 'beloved shield');
when more than one adjective modified a noun, one would typically pre-modify and the others
post-modify. The ME period saw growing use of periphrastic **passive** constructions with *to be* as
auxiliary and *by* introducing the agent (continuing directly into the modern pattern), alongside a
now-obsolete alternative auxiliary *worthe* ('to be, to come to be', used until the 14th c.) and an
impersonal-passive-like construction using the unstressed indefinite pronoun *me* ('one/they') with
no direct modern equivalent. The verb **do** began developing several of its now-familiar functions
during this period: its OE "substitute verb" use continued (*I do too*); some ME dialects used it to
mean 'make' or 'have' (a sense that survives in fossilized phrases like *let's do lunch*); the past
tense *did* + infinitive construction (*did carye* 'carried') appears, which would become productive
in Early Modern English; and *do* as an auxiliary in negative statements and questions, now
obligatory in Modern English, had begun to appear but would not become consistent until roughly the
17th century. Finally, as the synthetic (inflectional) character of English diminished, the
preposition word class grew substantially, filled through semantic shift of existing words (*among*
< OE *gemong* 'in a crowd'), compounding (*into*), and borrowing (*till* from Old Norse, *except*
from Latin). (pp.123–124)

### ME vocabulary: two-phase French borrowing, and Rothwell's revisionist challenge (§4.4.5)

ME borrowed roughly 10,000 words from French, traditionally analyzed as occurring in **two phases**
divided around 1250: an earlier phase of comparatively fewer loans from **Norman French**, in
administrative, legal, ecclesiastical, military, and culinary domains reflecting the social position
of the Conquest-era newcomers (native titles *king/queen/earl/lord/lady* notably survived alongside
the new French aristocratic terms, and French-derived meat terms like *beef/pork/mutton/veal* became
specialized alongside Anglo-Saxon-derived live-animal terms, the canonical "meat vs. animal" split);
and a later, larger phase of borrowing from **Central French** (the fashionable Parisian-court
variety, "in vogue" across European courts of the period) spanning fashion, domestic life, social
life, hunting, art, architecture, literature, and medicine — by 1460, an estimated 40% of ME
vocabulary was French-derived. Part of the traditional argument for two genuinely distinct source
varieties rests on apparent **phonetic doublets**: Norman French [w]/[k] vs. Central French [g]/[tʃ]
correspondences supposedly explain pairs like *warranty*/*guarantee*, *warden*/*guardian*,
*catch*/*chase*, *launch*/*lance* as two separate borrowings of the same etymon at two different
times. **William Rothwell (1998) challenges this framework directly**, arguing it rests on outdated
early-20th-century scholarship and that **Anglo-French** (the variety actually spoken in England,
distinct from both "Norman French" as narrowly defined and from Continental Central French) was the
real, much more influential source throughout the ME period — there is no real evidence the diverse
French retinues of Henry II/Eleanor of Provence spoke anything matching what philologists later
labeled "Central French." Rothwell's supporting case studies (*customs* vs. modern French *douane*;
*dungeon* vs. Continental French *donjon*, whose meaning diverged independently in Anglo-French
during the 13th–14th centuries rather than being imported ready-made) show English loanwords
diverging in form and meaning from their supposed later-Continental-French "parent" in ways the
two-phase theory can't explain, since Continental French itself kept changing independently.
Rothwell also directly disputes the clean phonetic split underlying the doublet argument: Anglo-French
texts from as early as 50 years post-Conquest already show "Central-French-style" [tʃ]/[ʃ] spellings
rather than exclusively Norman [k]/[s] ones, and unstandardized ME/Anglo-French spelling in general
makes the ascription of any given word's pronunciation to one variety or another far less clear-cut
than the two-phase narrative assumes. The chapter does *not* declare Rothwell definitively right, but
presents this as live, unresolved revisionist scholarship. (pp.124–127)

### §4.5 Middle English "creolization": Bailey & Maroldt's thesis and its refutation

Bailey & Maroldt (1977) argued that the scale of French contact-induced change in ME amounts to
genuine **creolization** — defining a creole as the result of language mixing substantial enough to
produce a new, structurally distinct system — on three grounds: (1) ME's vocabulary and derivational
morphology are substantially French-derived; (2) several ME verb/case constructions use English
words but French-modeled syntax (perfect-aspect *have*+past-participle on the pattern of French
*avoir*+participle; *go*+infinitive on French *aller*+infinitive; *of*-genitives on French
*de*-possessives; indirect-object *to* on French *à*); and (3) the collapse of OE case/gender
marking resulted from a "clash" between incompatible English and French inflectional systems, with
French SVO order and object-pronoun constructions like *it is me*/*it is him* cited as directly
French-derived. The chapter presents a detailed, multi-pronged refutation (drawing mainly on Thomason
& Kaufman 1988 and later creolist scholarship): the inflectional reduction Bailey & Maroldt attribute
to French contact was **already well underway within Old English itself**, long before significant
French contact began (see Ch.3's OE syncretism discussion), so gradual-internal-change is a far more
parsimonious explanation than language clash; the claimed French-modeled constructions have no
positive evidence of actually existing in contact-era French, and in several cases Bailey & Maroldt
themselves admit the English pattern already existed natively as a minor/optional variant *before*
the claimed French reinforcement — directly contradicting their own characteristic (2), since a
pre-existing native option cannot also be "an Anglo-Saxon element used in a French function"; ME's
quantitative proportion of French vocabulary, even when real, is frequently overstated by counting
occurrences in a small, non-representative sample of (often bilingual, self-selecting) individual
authors' texts rather than reflecting the whole language community; and crucially, ME **retained**
rather than lost case (genitive), number (plural) marking on nouns, full synthetic pronoun
inflection, and present/subjunctive/preterite/participle verb inflection — it simply does not match
Bailey & Maroldt's own stated definition of the "analyticity"/morphological-simplicity that would
make it creole-like. The chapter also surveys genuine **creole-diagnostic structural features**
(per Chaudenson 2001, citing Whinnom 1965) for comparison — elimination of noun/pronoun
number-gender-case inflection; preverbal tense/mood/aspect particle systems; adverb/adjective formal
identity; adjective reduplication for intensification; a single all-purpose preposition; compound
genitive-marking prepositions; a distinct interrogative word for 'thing'; and words for 'much'
derived from phrases meaning 'too much' — noting that several of these occur individually in
non-creole language varieties too (so no single feature is diagnostic on its own), that "simplicity"
as a defining creole trait is itself theoretically unfounded and historically used pejoratively, and
that modern creole-studies scholarship (Mufwene, Chaudenson, DeGraff) now treats creolization as
fundamentally a **social**, not purely structural-linguistic, phenomenon tied to the specific
socio-historical conditions of colonial-era superstrate/substrate contact (sharp power imbalance,
mutual unintelligibility, exogenous forced contact) — conditions that did not hold between Middle
English's French and English speaker populations, who were instead converging toward widespread
bilingualism and eventual assimilation rather than creole-genesis. (pp.127–136)

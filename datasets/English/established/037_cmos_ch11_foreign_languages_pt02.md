### Explicit regional annotation: German quotation-mark conventions

§11.44 is a genuine case of the coverage rule's "explicit dialectal/regional annotation" category: CMOS
states that Switzerland specifically uses regular (French-style) guillemets for German text, where the
rest of the German-speaking world uses either reversed guillemets or split-level inverted marks. This
is captured in the grammar points section below rather than as a separate vocabulary row, since it
modifies the "split-level inverted quotation marks" entry above rather than naming a distinct term.

## Grammar points

### Czech special characters (§11.21)

Czech is a Slavic language written in the Latin alphabet but requiring extensive diacritical marking —
fifteen letter-pairs in all (listed in the vocabulary table above) — to represent sounds the
unmodified Latin alphabet cannot. No capitalization-specific Czech rule is given in this range (§11.20
already pointed back to the general rules at §§11.3, 11.9 in part 1).

### Danish (§§11.22–11.23)

Danish capitalizes the polite personal pronouns *De*, *Dem*, and *Deres* — though the source explicitly
flags this usage as "now used ever more rarely" — and the familiar pronoun *I*. Until the mid-twentieth
century Danish also capitalized common nouns generally, following the German pattern, but no longer
does. Danish requires three additional alphabetic letters beyond the unmodified Latin alphabet: Å/å,
Æ/æ, and Ø/ø (§11.23).

### Dutch (§§11.24–11.25)

Dutch capitalization (§11.24) points back to the general rules (§§11.3, 11.9) and to the separate rule
for particles in personal names (§8.10, outside this chapter), but then adds Dutch-specific content:
proper adjectives are capitalized as in English; when a word beginning with the *ij* diphthong is
capitalized, both letters of the digraph capitalize together (e.g., *IJsland*); and when a single-letter
word (such as the contracted pronoun *'k*) begins a sentence, that letter stays lowercase while the
following word is capitalized instead (*'k Heb niet . . .*). Dutch requires no special characters at
all (§11.25) — the shortest possible entry in the chapter's special-characters apparatus, explicitly
stated as a complete sentence with no further elaboration.

### Finnish (§§11.26–11.27)

Finnish capitalization (§11.26) is a pure cross-reference stub ("See 11.3, 11.9") with no independent
content, per the coverage-rule exclusion noted above. Finnish special characters (§11.27) require two
umlauted vowels, Ä/ä and Ö/ö. An explicit regional/co-official-language note follows: because Swedish is
Finland's second official language, the Finnish alphabet taught in schools and the standard Finnish
keyboard also include the Swedish "a with ring above" (Å/å), even though it is not a native Finnish
letter.

### French — additional resources (§11.28)

French capitalization and punctuation practice varies considerably across publications. CMOS directs
readers wanting deeper guidance, with extensive literary examples, to the latest edition of Grevisse's
*Le bon usage* (known widely by its original editor's name), and to the Académie française's own
website for further authority.

### French capitalization (§11.29)

Beyond the general rules (§§11.3, 11.9), French capitalization follows several specific patterns, all
illustrated with short examples in the source:

- Generic words for roadways and squares (*boulevard*, *place*, *rue*) are lowercased whether standing
  alone or combined with a specific name — only the proper name itself capitalizes (*le boulevard
  Saint-Germain*).
- In most geographical names, the generic term is lowercased and the modifier capitalized (*la mer
  Rouge*, "the Red Sea").
- Building names are usually fully capitalized (*l'Hôtel des Invalides*).
- In organization/institution names, only the first substantive and any preceding modifier capitalize —
  not the preceding article, except sentence-initially (*l'Académie française*).
- In hyphenated names, both elements capitalize (*la Comédie-Française*).
- Names of religious groups are usually lowercased (*un chrétien*, *des juifs*).
- The word *saint* is lowercased when referring to the saint in prose (*le supplice de saint Pierre*,
  "the martyrdom of Saint Peter") but capitalized and hyphenated to the following element when it forms
  part of a place-name, church name, or institution name (*l'église de Saint-Pierre*).
- Adjectives derived from proper nouns are usually lowercased (*une imagination baudelairienne*,
  "a Baudelairean imagination").

### Titles of French works (§11.30)

French publications themselves vary in title capitalization; CMOS recommends sentence-style
capitalization (per its own general rule at §8.156 and Grevisse's practice), with an exception for
journal/periodical titles, which are often capitalized headline-style instead. A superscripted ordinal
letter (as in a century number) stays in the superior position even within a title — e.g. *Paris au
XXᵉ siècle* ("Paris in the Twentieth Century"). CMOS cross-references an alternative capitalization
convention used by some journals (*French Review*, *PMLA*, *Romanic Review*), under which the first
substantive plus any preceding article/modifier capitalize.

### Spacing with French punctuation (§11.31)

French typeset material conventionally places a fixed thin space before colons, semicolons, question
marks, and exclamation marks; between guillemets and the text they enclose; and after an em dash
introducing dialogue. Electronic documents use a nonbreaking space for the same purpose, to prevent a
mark from stranding at a line start. English-language contexts need not replicate this spacing — but if
French spacing conventions are adopted at all, they must be applied consistently to every mark, not
selectively.

### French use of guillemets (§11.32)

French quotation marks are guillemets (« »), typically separated from the quoted text by a thin (or
nonbreaking) space — a spacing convention English-language publications need not follow. Speech tags
such as *écrit-il* ("he writes") or *dit-elle* ("she says") are commonly inserted mid-quotation without
additional guillemets. Only punctuation that belongs to the quoted material itself goes inside the
closing guillemets; everything else follows them. When a quotation spans multiple paragraphs, opening
guillemets repeat at the start of each new paragraph, while the closing mark appears only once, at the
very end.

### Quotation marks in French (§11.33)

For a quotation nested inside another quotation, French now typically uses ordinary double (sometimes
single) quotation marks rather than a second level of guillemets. Historically, nested guillemets were
used instead, with the opening mark repeated on every runover line; if both quotations ended at the same
point, only a single closing-guillemet pair appeared. CMOS also notes a genuinely informal, modern
register shift: plain quotation marks are increasingly displacing guillemets altogether in e-mail and
other electronic French writing — an explicit register annotation about ongoing orthographic change.

### French dialogue (§11.34)

In dialogue, French often replaces guillemets with em dashes, one introducing each new speaker's turn
(not repeated at the end of a speech). French practice follows the dash with a thin space; English
practice does not require this. When a quotation occurs within a line of dialogue, guillemets may still
be used to set it off, as in the example *—Tu connais sans doute la parole «De l'abondance du cœur la
bouche parle»* ("You surely know the saying 'out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks'").

### French suspension points and ellipses (§11.35)

French "suspension points" mark an interruption or trailing-off in thought; French practice leaves them
unspaced, while English-context material may use English-style spaced ellipsis points instead. They
also substitute for "and so forth" in French usage. To mark an editorial omission specifically, French
convention uses unspaced dots enclosed in brackets with thin spaces around the bracket pair — again
optionally restyled to English-style spaced bracketed ellipses in an English-language context.

### French word division — vowels (§11.36)

French divides a word after a vowel wherever possible (*ache-ter*, not *a-cheter*), and one-letter
syllables should never end or begin a line. Two or more vowels forming a single sound (a diphthong) are
never split (*écri-vain*, never *é-cri-vain*).

### French word division — consonants (§11.37)

Between two adjacent consonants (same or different), the division normally falls between them
(*der-riè-re*, *par-ler*) — except where the second consonant is *r* or *l* following certain
consonants, which stay together as a cluster (*qua-tre*, *ta-bleau*). A run of three adjacent
consonants is normally divided after the first (*es-prit*, *res-plen-dir*).

### French words containing apostrophes (§11.38)

A line break should never fall immediately after an apostrophe (*jus-qu'au*, not *jusqu'-au*).

### French words best left undivided (§11.39)

Because French counts a syllable per vowel/diphthong rather than by the English intuition of syllable
count, some words that read as one syllable to an English speaker are technically divisible in French
(*fui-te*, *guer-re*, *sor-tent*), and French practice even permits division after a single letter
(*é-tait*). CMOS recommends against both kinds of break in English-language publications, since they
risk confusing readers unfamiliar with French, and states as a flat floor that words of four letters or
fewer should never be divided at all regardless of technical syllable count.

### French special characters (§11.40)

French requires the fourteen accented-letter pairs and one ligature listed in the vocabulary table
above. French publishers themselves often omit accents on capital letters (especially capital A) and
render the Œ/œ ligature as two separate letters (OE/oe) instead — but CMOS recommends using the full,
correctly accented and ligated forms in English-language works regardless, following the Académie
française's own advocacy for this practice, since all needed characters are broadly available in modern
software and fonts.

### The new German orthography (§11.41)

Germany's 1998 spelling-reform rules (covering both spelling and capitalization), made mandatory in
2005, remain controversial in practice: some publications retain the traditional rules, some blend house
style with tradition, and some have fully adopted the reform. Because some publishers also preserve an
author's original spelling preference when reprinting older work, CMOS instructs that quoted German
material should reflect whatever spelling appears in its actual source rather than being silently
normalized to one standard. CMOS's own recommendations and examples in this section follow the new
orthography, and directs readers wanting full detail to the latest *Duden*.

### German capitalization (§11.42)

German capitalizes every noun and every word functioning as a noun, in ordinary prose as much as in
titles — a rule with no close English parallel (*ein Haus*, "a house"; *die Weltanschauung*,
"worldview"; *Deutsch*, "the German language" itself, capitalized as a noun even though lowercased as an
adjective). Adjectives derived from proper names are generally lowercased (*die deutsche Literatur*,
"German literature"), with named exceptions for invariable adjectives ending in *-er* that typically
name a city or region (*eine berühmte Berliner Straße*, "a famous Berlin street") and for adjectives
that are themselves part of a proper name (*der Deutsch-Französische Krieg*, "the Franco-Prussian War").
On pronouns: the polite second-person forms *Sie*, *Ihr*, and *Ihnen* are capitalized, while the same
forms used as third-person pronouns are lowercased; the familiar second-person forms (*du*, *dich*,
*dein*, *ihr*, *euch*) were once routinely capitalized as a courtesy but are now lowercased under
standard practice.

### German apostrophes (§11.43)

An apostrophe marks the colloquial dropping of *e* in casual speech forms (*wie geht's*, "how's it
going"; *hab' ich*, "have I"). Apostrophes rarely precede a genitive *-s* in German, but one is used
specifically to mark the omission of that *-s* after a proper name already ending in an *s*-like sound
(*ce*, *s*, *ss*, *ß*, *tz*, *x*, or *z*) or in a silent *s*, *x*, or *z* — e.g., *Alice' Geburtstag*
("Alice's birthday"), *Leibniz' Meinung* ("Leibniz's opinion").

### German quotation marks (§11.44)

German text conventionally uses one of three quotation-mark conventions: reversed guillemets (» «, with
the points facing inward, opposite the French orientation); split-level inverted quotation marks („ "),
with the opening mark set low and the closing mark raised; or, specifically in Switzerland, ordinary
(French-style) guillemets — an explicit regional/national annotation distinguishing Swiss German
typographic practice from the rest of the German-speaking world. As in English, other punctuation is
placed outside the closing quotation mark unless it genuinely belongs to the quoted material itself.

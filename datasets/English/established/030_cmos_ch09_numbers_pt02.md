## Grammar points

### 9.22 Non-US currencies using the dollar symbol

Where `$` could refer to a non-US currency, the currency must be clearly identified — either with a
prefixed letter/abbreviation before the symbol (e.g., "C$300" or "Can$300" for Canadian dollars,
"NZ$749" for New Zealand dollars, "A$15.69" for Australian dollars, "Mex$98" for Mexican pesos) or,
in more formal usage, with the ISO three-letter currency code (USD, CAD, NZD, AUD). (p. 1225)

### 9.23 British currency

The pound (symbol £) is the basic unit; one-hundredth of a pound is a penny (plural *pence*,
abbreviated `p`, no period) — e.g. "fifty pence = 50p." Before decimalization in 1971, the pound was
divided into shillings (s.) and pence (d.), as in "£10 15s. 6d." (p. 1226)

### 9.24 Other currencies

Most other currencies follow the same decimal-point convention as US currency (e.g., "EUR 10.75").
When letters rather than symbols are used, a space separates the letters from the numeral (e.g., "725
yen"). Pre-euro European currencies had their own symbols (F for French franc, DM for deutsche mark,
Lit for Italian lira); ISO defines three-letter codes (including EUR) for most currencies. (p. 1227)

### 9.25 Large monetary amounts

Sums over one hundred dollars are normally given as numerals; amounts of a million or more may mix
numerals and spelled-out words even for whole numbers (e.g. "$7.3 billion," "an additional $7.3
billion"). In certain financial contexts, thousands are represented informally with `K` (e.g.
"$350K"). (p. 1228)

### 9.26 Currency with dates

When the date of a currency value matters, the year is inserted in parentheses, without intervening
space, directly after the currency symbol (e.g. "US$(1992)2.47," "£(2002)15,050"). When letters alone
are used instead of a symbol, spaces intervene before and after the parenthetical year (e.g. "USD
(1992) 2.47"). (p. 1229)

### 9.27 Page numbers, chapter numbers, and so forth

Numbers referring to pages, chapters, parts, volumes, illustrations, or tables are set as numerals.
Front-matter pages are usually lowercase roman numerals; the rest of the book uses arabic numerals
(e.g. "pages vii–xiv," "pages 1–35"). Biblical references use numerals only, with chapter and verse
separated by a colon and no following space (e.g. "Exodus 20:3–17," "Psalm 121; Psalms 146–50").
(p. 1230)

### 9.28 Volume, issue, and page numbers for periodicals

References to a journal's volume, issue, and page are usually given in that order with arabic
numerals; the words "volume" and "page" are usually omitted (e.g. "*Current Anthropology* 49, no. 1
(2008): 87–114"). (p. 1231)

### 9.29 Numbered divisions in legal instruments

Arabic or roman numerals are sometimes used to distinguish divisions within legal documents and
other instruments; a mixture of arabic and roman numerals can distinguish smaller from larger
divisions (e.g. "paragraph 14(vi) of the bylaws," "article 2, section 4" but inverted as "Article II,
Section 4"; "the Fifth Amendment" or "Amendment V"). When in doubt, consult the document itself.
(p. 1232)

### 9.30 The year alone

Years are expressed in numerals unless they stand at the beginning of a sentence, in which case
rewording is often better than spelling the year out (e.g. "We all know what happened in 1776" vs.
avoiding "Twenty twenty... should be a good year" in favor of "The year 2020 should be..."). (p. 1233)

### 9.31 The year abbreviated

In informal contexts, the first two digits of a year are often replaced by an apostrophe — notably
*not* an opening single quotation mark — e.g. "the spirit of '76," "the class of '06." (p. 1234)

### 9.32 Month and day

Specific dates use cardinal numbers (e.g. "May 26, 2008"), although they may be pronounced as
ordinals in speech. A day mentioned without month or year is usually spelled out in ordinal form
(e.g. "On November 5, McManus declared victory. By the twenty-fifth, most of his supporters had
deserted him."). Cross-referenced to §6.45 for month-day-year versus day-month-year ordering.
(p. 1235)

### 9.33 Centuries

Particular centuries are spelled out and lowercased (e.g. "the twenty-first century," "the eighth
and ninth centuries," "the eighteen hundreds (the nineteenth century)"). (p. 1236)

### 9.34 Decades

Decades are either spelled out and lowercased (as long as the century is clear) or expressed in
numerals, with no apostrophe between the year and the following *s* (e.g. "the nineties," "the 1980s
and 1990s," or less formally "the 1980s and '90s"). A naming ambiguity is flagged: "the 1900s" could
mean either the whole twentieth century or just its first decade, so "the 1910s" (not "the second
decade") is used for the latter, and "the teens" should be avoided. Chicago defers to an author's
own preference for how the first/second decade of a century are delimited (e.g. 2001–10 vs. 2000–09
for the first decade of the twenty-first century). (p. 1237)

### 9.35 Eras

Era designations are usually CE/BCE ("Common Era"/"before the Common Era") or AD/BC ("anno
Domini"/"before Christ"); other systems include AH (*anno Hegirae*, Islamic), *anno Hebraico*
(Hebrew), AUC (*ab urbe condita*, archaeological, founding of Rome), and BP ("before the present,"
archaeological). AD and AH precede the year number; the others follow it. These abbreviations often
appear in small capitals, sometimes with periods; Chicago recommends full capitals with no periods
for consistency with its chapter 10 guidelines. When the second half of a pair of inclusive dates
uses BCE or BC, the higher (earlier) number is given in full to avoid confusion (e.g. "350–345
BCE," not "350–45"). (p. 1238)

### 9.36 All-numeral dates and other brief forms

All-numeral date styles (e.g. "5/10/99") should be avoided in formal writing, except for certain
dates known that way (e.g. "9/11" for September 11, 2001). American usage places the month first,
while most other English-speaking countries and European languages place the day first — a genuine
ambiguity a writer must resolve for readers when quoting dated material. In documentation/tables, the
day-month-year form, needing no punctuation, is often neater (e.g. "5 Oct 2003"). (p. 1239)

### 9.37 ISO style for dates

ISO recommends an all-numeral year-month-day style (largest to smallest component), hyphenated, with
a leading zero for single-digit month or day (e.g. "2010-01-19" for January 19, 2010). Advantages
include correct sorting in spreadsheets and other applications. (p. 1240)

### 9.38 Numerals versus words for time of day

Even, half, and quarter hours are usually spelled out in text (e.g. "five o'clock," "half past
three," "ten thirty," "a quarter to four"); with *o'clock* the number is always spelled out. Numerals
are used (with zeros for even hours) when exact times matter, with Chicago recommending lowercase
a.m./p.m. (sometimes small capitals), e.g. "5:22 a.m.," "10:30 a.m. (EST)." (p. 1241)

### 9.39 Noon and midnight

Outside the twenty-four-hour system, numbers should not express noon or midnight, except informally
("twelve o'clock at night"). Noon can technically be "12:00 m." (*meridies*) but this form is very
rarely used; "12:00 p.m." is ambiguous if not illogical. A double date (e.g. "midnight, August
21–22") disambiguates which night is meant. (p. 1242)

### 9.40 The twenty-four-hour system

Used in Europe and the military: four digits always appear, often with no punctuation between hours
and minutes (e.g. "1200" = noon, "2400" or "0000" = midnight, "0001" = 12:01 a.m., "1438" = 2:38
p.m., "1500 hours" or "1500h"). (p. 1243)

### 9.41 Seconds and dates included

A variant of the twenty-four-hour system adds seconds, separated by colons, with fractions of a
second after a period; this format may be preceded by an ISO-style date (e.g. "2010-01-19
16:09:41.3"). (p. 1244)

### 9.42 Numerals for monarchs, popes, and so forth

Sovereigns, emperors, popes, and Orthodox patriarchs sharing a name are differentiated by roman
numerals (e.g. "Elizabeth II," "Benedict XVI"). In continental European practice the numeral may be
followed by a period (e.g. "Wilhelm II.") or a superscript ordinal indicator (e.g. "François Iᵉʳ");
in an English context, the bare roman numeral alone is used. (p. 1245)

### 9.43 Numerals with personal names

Some personal names are followed by a roman numeral or an arabic ordinal numeral (e.g. "Adlai E.
Stevenson III," "Michael F. Johnson 2nd"), with no preceding punctuation — unless the name is
inverted, as in an index entry (e.g. "Stevenson, Adlai E., III"). Cross-referenced to §6.47 for
styling *Jr.* (p. 1246)

### 9.44 Numbers for sequels

Numerals designate sequels to a novel or movie, or differentiate two chapter titles on the same
subject; when quoting such a title, the source's own usage (roman, arabic, or spelled out) is
followed (e.g. "*The Godfather*; *The Godfather, Part II*; *The Godfather, Part III*"; "*Jaws*;
*Jaws 2*; *Jaws 3-D*"; "chapter 9, 'Alligator Studies in the Everglades–I'"). (p. 1247)

## Grammar points

### Pronouns carry Subject/Object distinctions more systematically than PDE (p.34–36)

PDE retains only a residual nominative/accusative split in its pronouns (I/me, he/him, she/her,
we/us, they/them, who/whom) and nowhere else. OE pronouns show the same nominative-for-Subject,
accusative-for-Object pattern (*hē āsende hine* = 'he sent him') but, crucially, this behavior
extends in OE to **entire noun phrases**, not just pronouns — the central point the chapter builds
toward.

### The OE pronoun paradigm: gender, number, and case together (p.35–37)

OE pronouns mark three genders in the 3rd person singular (masc. *hē*, fem. *hēo*, neut. *hit*),
singular vs. plural, and case. Full nominative/accusative/genitive forms are tabulated above. Two
points the source specifically flags as learner traps:
- ***hīe*** is genuinely triple-ambiguous: fem. sg. accusative ('her'), 3pl nominative ('they'), and
  3pl accusative ('them') all share this one form — disambiguated only by context.
- ***hire*** does double duty as both the fem. sg. **genitive** ('her' = possessive) and, as shown
  later in the Incarnation text (*tō hire* 'to her', *cȳdde hire* 'to her'), the fem. sg. **dative**
  (prepositional/indirect-object 'her') — the source treats this dative use as implicit until
  Chapter 4 formalizes the Dative case.

### Case, defined (p.36–38)

"Case" = the grammatical signal (via word-form change) of a noun phrase's role in the sentence.
Nominative = Subject; Accusative = Object; Genitive = possession ('my horse' = *mīn hors*). The
source flags that OE has **four** cases total (the fourth, Dative, is deferred explicitly to
Chapter 4, used "after a preposition," e.g. *for him*, *to him*, *with him*).

### Full noun phrases decline for case too — not just pronouns (p.41–44)

The chapter's central grammar point: unlike PDE (where only the possessive apostrophe-*s* is a
relic Genitive — *the angel's head* < *ðæs engles hēafod*), OE noun phrases as a whole — determiner
+ adjective + noun — change form to agree in **number** (singular/plural), **gender**
(masculine/feminine/neuter), and **case** (Nom./Acc./Gen./Dat.). The source gives a full worked
paradigm for "the good angel/mother/ship" across all three genders and both numbers (reproduced
in condensed form):

| | Masc. sg. | Fem. sg. | Neut. sg. |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nom. | se gōda engel | sēo gōde mōdor | ðæt gōde scip |
| Acc. | ðone gōdan engel | ðā gōdan mōdor | ðæt gōde scip |
| Gen. | ðæs gōdan engles | ðǣre gōdan mōdor | ðæs gōdan scipes |
| Dat. | ðǣm gōdan engle | ðǣre gōdan mōdor | ðǣm gōdan scipe |

Plurals collapse some of this variety: the plural determiner *ðā* (nom./acc.) and *ðāra* (gen.) are
shared across all three genders, which the source explicitly offers as a learner-friendly
simplification (fewer distinct plural forms to memorize than singular ones).

### Grammatical gender is conventional, not "natural" (p.42)

OE noun gender does not track biological sex or any obvious semantic property: *sumor* 'summer' and
*winter* 'winter/year' are grammatically masculine, *sprǣc* 'speech' and *miht* 'power' are
feminine, *gold* 'gold' and *dēor* 'wild animal' are neuter. A reader cannot predict a noun's gender
from its meaning and must learn it per-word (signaled, in practice, mainly by the determiner that
precedes it).

### Strong vs. weak noun declensions (p.42–44)

OE nouns fall into multiple inflectional classes ("declensions"/"paradigms"), traditionally grouped
as "strong" and "weak" within each gender. The source gives one worked strong/weak contrast pair per
gender: masc. strong *hlāf* 'loaf' vs. weak *gefēra* 'comrade'; fem. strong *rōd* 'cross' vs. weak
*hlǣfdige* 'lady'; neut. strong *dēofol* 'devil' vs. weak *ēage* 'eye'. Full paradigm tables are
reserved for the Appendix (see `076_beginning_old_english_pt07.md` or later as applicable);
Chapter 3 gives only nominative/accusative singular and plural for this strong/weak contrast.

### Reading strategy: lean on determiners, pronouns, and plural patterns (p.43–44)

The source's own explicit advice to the beginning reader, worth capturing as methodology rather
than raw grammar: (1) the determiner ('the') is a small, closed set of forms and is the most
reliable signal of a noun's gender/case/number — learn it before the noun declensions themselves;
(2) pronouns are likewise a small, frequent, high-payoff set; (3) plural forms vary less than
singular ones across declension classes, so are comparatively easy to learn as a group; (4) OE
plural nouns favor *-s*, *-n*, *-u*, *-e*, or *-a* endings (PDE mostly generalized *-s*, keeping only
relic *-n* plurals like 'children', 'oxen').

### Flexible word order, licensed by case marking (p.40)

Because OE case endings (on pronouns and full noun phrases alike) signal grammatical role directly,
word order is freer than in PDE, which relies on fixed Subject-Verb-Object order to signal the same
information. Worked example: *Hē scrȳtt mē wel and fētt, and hwīlum hē mē hors selð oððe bēag*
('He clothes and feeds me well...') shows Object pronouns (*mē*) placed before their verbs, impossible
in idiomatic PDE word order but unambiguous in OE because *hē* (nominative) vs. *mē* (accusative)
already marks who is doing what to whom.

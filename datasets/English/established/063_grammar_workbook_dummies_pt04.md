## Grammar points

### Adjectives vs. adverbs (Ch.14)

Adjectives describe nouns/pronouns (people, places, things, ideas); adverbs describe verbs
(how/where/when/why an action happened) and can also intensify other descriptive words. A handful of
words (*fast, short, last, likely*) function as both; most do not, and a dictionary entry will mark
both forms where they exist. (p.179)

### Good/well and bad/badly (Ch.14)

*Good*/*bad* are adjectives; *well*/*badly* are adverbs, with the single exception that *well* can
also function as an adjective specifically in health contexts (*"I am well"*). When a description
follows a verb, the test is whether it describes the verb's action (use the adverb) or the
subject/doer itself (use the adjective). (p.181)

### Articles: a, an, the (Ch.14)

*The* signals something specific/definite and works with both singular and plural nouns. *A*/*an*
are general/indefinite and singular-only, chosen by the following sound (consonant → *a*, vowel →
*an*) rather than spelling. For a general plural referent, *some*/*any* substitute since *a*/*an*
can't attach to plurals. (p.182)

### Placement of limiting words: even, only, almost, just, nearly, not (Ch.15)

These short descriptive words must sit immediately before the specific word they're meant to limit —
moving one even slightly changes the sentence's actual meaning (the book's example: a tee-shirt
reading *"Grandma... only bought me this lousy tee shirt"* unintentionally implies Grandma did
nothing else in the city at all). (p.189)

### Misplaced (longer) descriptions (Ch.15)

Multiword descriptions (prepositional phrases, verbals, clauses) should sit directly next to the word
they describe; letting one drift elsewhere attaches it to the wrong noun, often producing an absurd
literal reading (*"a sales associate with snow tires"* instead of *"a car with snow tires"*). When
relocating a misplaced description, watch for a secondary logic error introduced by the new word
order — sometimes the real fix is to move the description to the very front of the sentence instead
of simply swapping two phrases. (pp.192–194)

### Dangling modifiers (Ch.15)

An introductory descriptive element (often resembling a verb form, technically a verbal) must
logically describe the sentence's actual grammatical subject. When it doesn't — because the subject
named right after the introduction isn't really the one doing/experiencing what the introduction
describes — the result is a "dangler" with flawed logic (*"Perched on her nose, the stop sign was
invisible..."* literally puts the stop sign on her nose). The fix is to ensure the subject
immediately following the introductory element is the thing the introduction is actually about.
(p.194)

### Vague (equidistant) descriptions (Ch.15)

A description placed an equal distance from two words it could plausibly modify creates genuine
ambiguity the reader can't resolve (*"Protesting successfully scares politicians"* — does
*successfully* modify *protesting* or *scares*?). The fix is to move the description unambiguously
next to the one word it's meant to describe. (p.196)

### Comparative and superlative formation (Ch.16)

One-syllable descriptive words take *-er*/*-est*; most longer words instead take *more/less* (two
elements) or *most/least* (three-plus elements, extreme of the group) rather than a suffix, though
some mid-length words may support either form and should be checked in a dictionary. A handful of
common words are fully irregular (*good/well→better→best*, *bad/ill→worse→worst*,
*much/many→more→most* — see Vocabulary table). (pp.205–207)

### Absolute (incomparable) descriptions (Ch.16)

Words denoting an all-or-nothing state (*perfect, unique, round, circular, right, mistaken*, and
direction/shape words like *left*, *square*) cannot logically take comparative or superlative degree
— there's no "more perfect" or "squarest." They can only be *approached* with words like *nearly* or
*almost*. (p.208)

### Incomplete comparisons (Ch.17)

A comparison must supply both sides, at least by clear implication from context — *"The snapper is
not as fresh"* (as what?) or *"the sea bass is most musical"* (compared to what?) leave the reader
without enough information unless a preceding sentence already supplies the missing half. Colloquial
*so* used to mean "very" (*"I was so tired"*) is distinguished from the comparison-forming *so...
that* construction, which the book treats as requiring its full *that*-clause in formal English.
(pp.215–217)

### Illogical comparisons (Ch.17)

Comparing a member of a group to the group itself as though the member weren't part of it is a logic
error (*"Derek Jeter is cuter than the Yankee players"* — Jeter is himself a Yankee player) — the fix
is inserting *other*/*else* (*"cuter than anyone **else** on the team"*), but only when the comparison
is genuinely within-group; comparing Jeter to an outside group (the Red Sox) needs no such insertion.
A second illogic pattern is "overkill" — doubling up both a suffix and a separate comparison word
(*"more sillier"* instead of *"sillier"* or *"more silly"*). (pp.217–219)

### Double (hedged) comparisons (Ch.17)

A sentence may legitimately contain two overlapping comparisons (*"as fragile as the old one, if not
more fragile"*) provided each comparison is independently, grammatically complete — either because
the first comparison finishes before the second starts, or because a single closing phrase can
logically complete both openings. The most common error is dropping part of the first comparison's
required wording when the two share a closing phrase (*"as complicated, if not more complicated
than, Jessica's oration"* is missing the second *as* that *"as complicated"* needs: *"as complicated
**as**, if not more complicated than,..."*). (pp.219–221)

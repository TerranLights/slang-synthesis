time/place/manner, the -līce adverb-formation rule, reason/cause clauses) and every distinct

glossary items and short illustrative phrases used in the grammar discussion, not reproduced in

## Grammar points

### Three ways of adding extra information to a clause (p.54)

Place, time, manner, and reason/cause are each typically signaled by one of three structures:
a prepositional phrase (*at midnight*), an adverb (*quickly*), or a subordinate clause (*because he
was angry*). The rest of the chapter works through all three in turn.

### Prepositions govern case — usually Dative, sometimes Accusative (p.58–61)

The chapter's central new grammar point. A preposition (*on*, *tō*, *mid*, *ofer*, *of*, *fram*,
*wið*, *ǣr*) combines with a noun phrase, and in OE that noun phrase is normally in the **Dative**
case — signaled especially by the distinctive **-um** plural Dative ending (*dagum* 'days', *weallum*
'walls') or **-e** singular Dative ending (*tūne*, *hǣse*). A smaller set of prepositions expressing
**motion/direction** (e.g. *ðurh* 'through', *ymb(e)* 'around' in a directional sense) instead
govern the **Accusative** case. The source frames this as a reliable decoding shortcut: spotting a
*-um* ending should make the reader look nearby for a governing preposition.

### Prepositions can precede or follow their noun phrase (p.59)

Unlike PDE, where the preposition obligatorily precedes its noun phrase (hence the name
"pre-position"), OE allows the order to invert: *on his dagum* 'in his days' alternates with *his
dagum on*. A pronoun-plus-preposition pair can also fuse into a single adverb, e.g. *ðǣr* + *tō* →
*ðǣr-tō* 'thereto' (direct ancestor of the archaic PDE adverb 'thereto'), and *ēow æt-foran* 'in
front of you' shows the preposition trailing its pronoun.

### Preposition meanings have drifted since Old English (p.58)

The source flags that several OE prepositions carried senses broader or different from their PDE
reflexes — e.g. *wið* could mean 'against' in contexts where PDE 'with' would now suggest
cooperation, a shift the source notes survives in fossilized PDE idioms like "he fought *with* his
brother" (= *against*).

### Adverbs of time and place (p.58)

A small core set recurs constantly: *ǣr* 'before', *hēr* 'here', *ðā* 'then', *æfter* 'afterwards'.
*ðā* is flagged as the chapter's single trickiest word: it is simultaneously (a) the adverb 'then',
(b) the nominative/accusative plural determiner 'the' for all genders, and (c) the feminine
accusative singular determiner 'the' — correctly parsed only by its syntactic position. A doubled
*ðā ... ðā* construction carries the specific meaning 'when ... then'.

### Manner adverbs via the -līce suffix (p.62)

A productive derivational rule: adjective + *-līce* → manner adverb, directly paralleling PDE's
adjective + *-ly*. Examples: *blīðe* 'glad' → *blīðelīce* 'gladly/blithely'; *fæst* 'firm' →
*fæstlīce* 'firmly'; *georn* 'eager' → *geornlīce* 'eagerly'. Manner can also be expressed by a
Dative prepositional phrase, e.g. *mid drīum fōtum* 'with dry feet', *mid his hǣse* 'with his
command'.

### Reason/cause expressions (p.67–68)

A small closed set of connectives introduces reason clauses: *for-ðǣm*/*for-ðǣm ðe* 'because', *ðȳ*
'therefore/because' (an instrumental-case-derived form), and *(tō ðǣm) ðæt*/*(tō ðon) ðæt* 'in order
that, so that'. The source deliberately recycles example sentences already seen in Chapters 2–3 to
reinforce them in this new frame, rather than introducing all-new material — worth noting as the
book's own stated pedagogical method (explicit encouragement to re-read earlier chapters' texts
through each new grammatical lens as it's introduced).

### Impersonal *man* as an indefinite-agent subject (p.56)

*man ofslōg* literally 'one slew' functions where PDE would use a passive ('they killed him' /
'he was killed') — an indefinite impersonal subject pronoun *man* ('one', unrelated in this use to
*mann* 'person') the source flags as a specific translation trap in the AD 787 Chronicle annal.

## Grammar points

### Unit 65 — Uncountable nouns

Uncountable nouns (gold, music, blood, excitement) have no plural and never take a/an, though they
combine with the/some/any/much/this/his, etc., or stand alone. Many nouns alternate countable and
uncountable readings with a meaning shift (paper = newspaper [C] vs. writing material [UNC]; hair = a
single strand [C] vs. hair collectively [UNC]; experience = an event [C] vs. accumulated knowledge
[UNC]). A closed list of nouns (advice, baggage, behavior, bread, chaos, furniture, information,
luggage, news, permission, progress, scenery, traffic, travel, trouble, weather, work) is flagged as
uncountable in English specifically because learners from other languages often expect them to be
countable; "news" stays singular despite its -s ending; "a good trip" (not "a good travel") is the
correct phrasing for a single journey. p. 130.

### Unit 66 — Countable nouns with a/an and some

Countable nouns (dog, umbrella, job) pluralize and take a/an in the singular; a bare singular countable
noun without a determiner is ungrammatical. a/an marks category membership or description (a dog is
an animal, Tom's mother is a doctor). Plural countable nouns used for general statements stand alone
without "some" (Dogs are animals); "some" specifically marks an unspecified, non-exhaustive quantity
(some police officers, but not all). p. 132.

### Unit 67 — A/an and the

Core first-mention/given-information contrast: a/an introduces a referent the listener can't yet
identify; "the" marks a referent now identifiable from context (prior mention, situational uniqueness).
Extends to a cluster of fixed situationally-definite nouns within a shared context (the light, the
floor, the door in a room; the bathroom, the bank, the post office, the doctor, the dentist, the
hospital, the police, the fire department, the army, as generic "the relevant one for this situation").
p. 134.

### Unit 68 — The (1)

"The" marks uniqueness (the only television program she watches; the earth, the sun, the moon, the
universe). Fixed collocations: "(in) the sea/sky/ground," "(in) the city/country" vs. the idiomatic
no-article "go to sea"/"be at sea" and "space" (the universe) vs. "the space" (a specific gap/area).
"The movies"/"the theater" don't require a specific referent; "the radio" always takes "the," plain
"television" usually doesn't except when referring to the physical set. Meal names (breakfast, lunch,
dinner) take no article ordinarily, but "a" appears once an adjective modifies the meal noun ("a very
nice lunch"). pp. 136.

### Unit 69 — The (2)

"The" + singular countable noun denotes an entire category/type (the rose = roses in general), extended
to inventions/machines (the telephone, the bicycle) and musical instruments (play the guitar/piano).
"The" + certain adjectives (the rich, the poor, the old, the young, the blind, the deaf, the sick, the
dead, the disabled, the unemployed, the homeless, the injured) denotes the whole class of people with
that property, always plural, never combinable with a/an (not "a blind," must say "a blind man"). "The"
+ certain nationality adjectives (the British, the English, the French, the Irish, the Welsh, the
Spanish, the Dutch, the Swiss; also -ese forms like the Japanese/Chinese) similarly denotes the people
of that nation; other nationalities instead use a plural -s noun (Russians, Italians, Arabs, Germans,
Turks). p. 138.

### Unit 70 — Plural and uncountable nouns with and without the

Central contrast: bare plural/uncountable nouns express something "in general" (I love flowers; crime
is a problem; most people); "the" + the same noun marks something "in particular," identifiable from
context (the flowers [in your garden]; the children [we took to the zoo]; the salt [on the table]).
The book flags this general/particular line as genuinely blurry in some cases (I like the people I work
with vs. I like working with people who are lively) rather than a clean binary. p. 140.

### Unit 71 — School / the school, prison / the prison, etc.

A cluster of institutional nouns (school, college, prison/jail, church) drop "the" when referring to
the institution's intended functional use (a child goes to school as a student; a criminal goes to
prison as a prisoner; someone goes to church to worship) but take "the" when referring to the physical
building for an unrelated purpose (a visitor goes to the school; a visitor goes to the prison; workers
go to the church to repair the roof). A parallel cluster of fixed phrases — bed (no "the"), work (no
"the"), home (no preposition at all: go/come/get/arrive home; "(be/stay) (at) home") — rounds out the
pattern. p. 142.

### Unit 72 — Geographical names with and without the

A systematic survey across geographic name types: continents (no "the": Africa, Asia); countries/
states (usually no "the": France, Texas) except names containing republic/kingdom/states (the United
States, the United Kingdom) or inherently plural names (the Netherlands, the Philippines); cities (no
"the": Cairo, Tokyo), with The Hague as a flagged exception; island groups (plural, "the": the Bahamas)
vs. individual islands (no "the": Corfu, Bermuda); regions (the Middle East, the Far East, the north
of France) contrasted against directional adjectives used without "the" (northern France); mountain
ranges (plural, "the": the Rockies, the Andes) vs. individual peaks (no "the": Mount Everest); lakes
(no "the": Lake Superior); oceans/seas/rivers/canals (always "the": the Atlantic, the Nile, the Suez
Canal), with the book's own aside that maps typically omit "the" from the printed name itself; and
"of"-place names (always "the": the Bay of Naples, the United States of America). p. 144.

### Unit 73 — Names of streets, buildings, etc. with and without the

Streets/roads/avenues/boulevards/squares typically take no "the" (Fifth Avenue, Red Square).
Two/three-word institutional names (usually person-name or place-name + generic noun: Kennedy Airport,
Boston University, Pearson International Airport) typically take no "the" either — but a genuine
adjective-plus-noun name does ("the White House," "the Royal Palace"), flagged as a general tendency
with exceptions, not an absolute rule. Hotels, restaurants, theaters, movie theaters, museums/galleries,
and buildings/monuments typically take "the" (the Hilton Hotel, the Louvre, the Empire State Building);
banks typically don't (Citibank, Lloyds Bank). Stores/restaurants named with a possessive 's never take
"the" (Macy's, not "the Macy's"). "Of"-place names take "the" (the Tower of London, the Great Wall of
China). p. 146.

### Unit 74 — Singular or plural?

A grab-bag of noun/number-agreement quirks: a closed set of plural-only nouns (slacks/pants, jeans,
shorts, pajamas, scissors, glasses) individuated via "a pair of..."; "people" as the ordinary plural of
"person" ("persons" avoided); a set of -s-final nouns that stay grammatically singular (mathematics,
physics, economics, athletics, gymnastics, news) contrasted against -s-final nouns with identical
singular/plural forms (means, series, species); "the police" as an obligatorily plural-agreement
collective noun; plural quantity phrases (a sum of money, a period of time, a distance) taking singular
verb agreement when conceived as one unit ("Five thousand dollars was stolen"); and compound-adjective
number phrases dropping their plural -s when used attributively ("a ten-dollar bill," "a three-week
vacation," "a six-hour journey"), contrasted against the non-attributive plural form with 's ("three
weeks' vacation," cross-referenced forward to Unit 75d). p. 148.

### Unit 75 — ...'s (apostrophe s) and ...of...

's is the normal possessive marker when the possessor is a person or animal (the manager's office,
the horse's tail); "of" is the normal/safer choice when the possessor is a thing (the door of the
room), though 's is sometimes acceptable for things too (the book's title). 's is also usual for
organizations (the government's decision) and places (France's largest city). Singular nouns take 's;
plural nouns ending in -s take a bare apostrophe (the Carters' house); plural nouns not ending in -s
still take 's (a children's book). 's can attach after a coordinated noun phrase (Jack and Jill's
wedding) and can stand alone without a following noun when the referent is recoverable (Tom's
apartment is larger than Ann's). 's also extends productively to time-reference nouns (tomorrow's
meeting, yesterday's newspaper, next week's...) and to duration phrases, where either 's or a bare
apostrophe marks a period of time (a week's vacation, three weeks' vacation, eight hours' sleep),
explicitly contrasted with the compound-adjective form from Unit 74f (a three-week vacation). p. 150.

### Unit 76 — Reflexive pronouns (myself/yourself, etc.), by myself

The reflexive set (myself, yourself [sg.], himself/herself/itself, ourselves, yourselves [pl.],
themselves) marks coreference between a verb's subject and object (Tom cut himself). Reflexives are
not used after bring/take something with..., after feel/relax/concentrate, or (usually) after wash/
dress/shave (I washed, not "washed myself") — though "dried myself" is acceptable. "Meet" doesn't take
a reflexive/reciprocal object at all ("What time shall we meet?" not "meet ourselves"). Reflexives are
explicitly distinguished from the reciprocal pair each other/one another (Tom and Ann looked at
themselves [each looked at their own reflection] vs. looked at each other [each looked at the other
person]). A separate, non-reflexive emphatic use of the same forms stresses that the subject acted
alone/personally ("I fixed it myself"). "By myself/yourself," etc. is a fixed idiom meaning "alone."
p. 152.

### Unit 77 — "A friend of mine," "my own house"

A fixed double-genitive construction is required when naming one of several possessions/associates
using a possessive pronoun or named possessor ("a friend of mine," never "a friend of me"; "a friend
of Tom's"). "My own.../your own..." (never "an own...") emphasizes sole, unshared, unborrowed
ownership, or doing something oneself rather than relying on someone else to do it (grow your own
vegetables; cuts her own hair). p. 154.

### Unit 78 — All/all of, no/none of, most/most of, etc.

A quantifier system (all, no/none, some, any, much/many, most, little/few, each, half) splits into a
bare form (used directly before a plain noun, or standing alone) and an "of" form (required before a
determiner-marked noun phrase — the/this/my/etc. — or before a pronoun: it/us/you/them). "Most" is
flagged as a common error point: "most tourists," never "the most tourists," for the general-quantity
sense. "All" and "half" typically drop "of" even before a determiner (all my friends, half the money).
p. 156.

### Unit 79 — Both/both of, neither/neither of, either/either of

A parallel two-item quantifier system with the same bare-vs.-"of" distribution rule as Unit 78's set
(both restaurants vs. both of the restaurants; both of us, never "both us"). "Neither of" permits
either singular or plural verb agreement. The same three words double as correlative conjunctions
(both X and Y, neither X nor Y, either X or Y) and can stand entirely alone as short answers (Neither.
He's Australian). p. 158.

### Unit 80 — Some and any; some/any + -one/-body/-thing/-where

"Some" is the default in positive statements and polite offers/requests expecting "yes" (Would you
like some tea?); "any" is the default in negative sentences, after "if," and in ordinary questions. A
separate free-choice sense of any/anyone/anything/anywhere means "it doesn't matter which/who/what/
where" (You can catch any of these buses). Someone/somebody/anyone/anybody are grammatically singular
but commonly followed by they/them/their in spoken use. p. 160.

### Unit 81 — No/none/any; no/any + one/-body/-thing/-where

No/none/no one/nobody/nothing/nowhere are inherently negative and are never combined with "not"
(I saw nothing, not "didn't see nothing"); they're most natural sentence-initially or standing alone,
with "not...any-" forms preferred mid- or end-of-sentence. "No" combines directly with a noun; "none"
stands alone or takes "of" (none of these shops); "none of" + a plural noun more often takes plural
verb agreement, especially in speech. After no one/nobody, they/them/their commonly follows in spoken
use. Any/no also combine with comparatives (any better, no bigger) to mean "at all"/"not even a
little." p. 162.

### Unit 82 — Much, many, little, few, a lot, plenty

Much/little pair with uncountable nouns; many/few pair with plural countable nouns; a lot of/lots of/
plenty of work with both noun types ("plenty" = more than enough). Much/many are favored in negative
sentences and questions; "a lot of" is favored in positive statements, except when preceded by "too"
or "so" (too much sugar). Bare little/few (without "a") carry negative force (not enough); "very
little"/"very few" intensify this. "A little"/"a few" (with "a") carry mildly positive force (some,
a small but sufficient amount); "only a little"/"only a few" reverts the sense back to negative. p. 164.

### Unit 83 — All, every, and whole

"All" does not ordinarily substitute for everyone/everybody ("Everybody enjoyed the party," not "All
enjoyed") or for everything (better to say "He thinks he knows everything" than "knows all"), except in
the fixed phrase "all about" and the idiom "All I've done is..." (= the only thing). Every/everyone/
everybody/everything take singular verb agreement, though they/them/their commonly follows in spoken
use. "Whole" pairs with singular nouns, requires a preceding determiner (the whole book, her whole
life), and is not normally used with uncountable nouns (not "the whole money"); "a whole..." marks
completeness (a whole loaf of bread). "Every" marks recurring intervals (every ten minutes, every three
weeks). "All day"/"the whole day" are near-synonymous fixed phrases — note the fixed form lacks "the"
before "day"/"week" in the "all" variant (all day, never "all the day"). p. 166.

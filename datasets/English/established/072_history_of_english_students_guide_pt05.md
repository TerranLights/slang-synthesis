## Grammar points

### §6.2 The eighteenth century and the rise of the prescriptive tradition

The 18th century, against a backdrop of recent political upheaval (the 1707 Act of Union, Britain's
emergence as a colonial empire), developed an acute anxiety about English's perceived linguistic
"instability" as something that threatened to undermine the unity and prestige of the nation a
fixed, "perfected" standard language was supposed to project and preserve. **Jonathan Swift's** *A
Proposal for Correcting, Improving and Ascertaining the English Tongue* (1712) is treated as the
emblematic text of this anxiety: Swift explicitly modeled his argument on the history of Latin,
reasoning that Rome had achieved lasting cultural prestige partly by fixing and perfecting its
language before political/moral "decline" set in, and that English should do the same while there
was still time, chiefly by establishing a regulatory **Academy** (on the model of the Académie
Française) empowered to prescribe, proscribe, and "ascertain" correct usage once and for all, with
particular attention to grammar, lexical impropriety, and restoring some "antiquated" words judged
worth reviving. Swift's own targets for blame in English's perceived corruption were explicitly
social and political (Restoration "Court-fops," "illiterate... University-Boys," the loosened moral/
linguistic climate he associated with Whig politics) rather than narrowly linguistic, and his
*Proposal*'s own reception was itself politically polarized (it was addressed to a Tory Prime
Minister and provoked a hostile Whig response). No English Academy was ever successfully
established — Baugh & Cable (2002) and Crowley (1996) argue this was partly because an authoritarian
regulatory body sat uneasily with the dominant contemporary ideology that English speakers were
"free-thinking" and reached consensus through rational discussion, not external decree. In its
place, the standardization project was instead carried forward piecemeal by individually influential
scholars and their publications: Samuel Johnson's *A Dictionary of the English Language* (1755)
became hugely popular and was widely credited (Chesterfield, Sheridan, Boswell) with having
single-handedly supplied the "lawful standard" and "stability" an Academy never provided, despite
Johnson's dictionary being, in his own stated aim, as much prescriptive ("to correct and proscribe")
as descriptive; and a wave of 18th-century usage grammarians (Dryden, Priestley, Campbell, Lowth)
codified specific points of "correct" usage chiefly on the combined authority of **analogical
reasoning from existing patterns** (e.g. Campbell's argument for *afterwards*/*homewards* by analogy
with the already-accepted *backwards*/*forwards*), **etymology** (judging a word's "proper" meaning
by its historical source, even where this contradicted actual living usage, e.g. *unloose*), and
**the example of classical Latin/Greek grammar** (though this last basis, e.g. the ban on split
infinitives, is noted as having been invoked comparatively rarely in practice, since most
grammarians actually came to agree that forcing English into Latin's grammatical mold caused more
problems than it solved). The book stresses that these "rational," authoritative-seeming
prescriptive rulings were in practice frequently **arbitrary and socially motivated** rather than
objectively grounded in communicative clarity (citing disputed cases like *you was*/*you were*,
*I don't like his/him doing that*, where different prominent grammarians backed opposite "correct"
forms) — Milroy & Milroy's (1999) conclusion is that what actually got cemented as "correct" tracked
"the observed usage of the 'best people'" of the time, not logic or effectiveness per se. A specific,
durable legacy of this century's codification that the book flags as still active in written Modern
English usage today: the *different from* (over *to*/*than*) preference, *between you and I*
avoidance, *it is I* (over *it is me*) in formal register, comparative (not superlative) marking for
exactly two compared items, and proscription of multiple/double negation. The chapter's larger
argument, following Milroy & Milroy (1999), is that the prescriptive grammarians' most durable
achievement was not any specific rule but installing the **ideology of standardization itself** —
the still-widespread belief that one single, "best," fixable, context-independent form of a language
genuinely exists — even though "standard English" is in reality an abstraction without a single,
universally-agreed-on concrete content, constructed by acceptance of a loose common core of written
conventions among influential users rather than a natural or neutral linguistic fact. The chapter
closes this section by noting a contemporary countertrend some scholars (Graddol 1997) term
**destandardization**: newer, less centralized communication technologies (computer-mediated
communication blurring spoken/written norms, satellite/online media fragmenting broadcasting's former
standard-setting "gatekeeper" role) appear to be eroding some of the institutional mechanisms that
had reinforced a single standard's authority since the 18th century, even as the standard's cultural
prestige (as a marker of educational/economic success) persists. (pp.177–188)

### §6.3 Colloquial Singapore English (CSE/"Singlish"): grammar of a genuine modern contact variety

This section is the chapter's richest content for modern slang/non-standard-register mechanics. It
traces CSE's emergence through 19th–20th-century colonial Singapore's extremely multilingual contact
situation — English-medium schools attended by children from Chinese- (Hokkien/Cantonese-dominant),
Malay-, Tamil-, and other-language-background homes, with substrate influence plausibly also
transmitted through pre-existing local contact varieties of Malay (**Bazaar Malay**, used across most
ethnic groups, and **Baba Malay**, specific to Straits Chinese and additionally Hokkien-influenced) —
and situates it on a continuum beside the more formal local standard, **Standard Singapore English
(SSE)**, which closely resembles British/American/Australian standard English apart from some locally
specific vocabulary. CSE, by contrast, shows a much higher proportion of non-English-origin vocabulary
and several genuinely distinct morphosyntactic structures, systematically catalogued in the source as
follows:

- **Lexicon** falls into three categories common to all varieties of Singapore English: (1) English
  words used exactly as in other English varieties; (2) English words used with locally specific
  senses (*batch*, *fellow*, *send*, above); (3) loanwords from Chinese dialects (Hokkien/
  Cantonese) and Malay, which — as with the worked example *cheem* — are often only **partially**
  integrated: a loanword can take regular English derivational/comparative affixation while still
  being blocked from other, semantically-incompatible English processes (*-ly* adverb formation),
  and can **narrow or shift its meaning** relative to the donor-language source once borrowed
  (*cheem*'s loss of its literal 'physically deep' sense in English).
- **Aspect marking uses adverbials rather than verb inflection.** Past reference is signalled by a
  bare temporal adverbial with no verb inflection (*she eat here yesterday*); **perfect** aspect is
  marked by the adverbial *already* (possibly calquing Hokkien *liau*), either alone or,
  variably among some speakers, co-occurring with inflected verb+*already*; **progressive** aspect
  obligatorily uses verb-*ing* (optionally reinforced by *still*, itself optional — *still* cannot
  occur without the *-ing*); **habitual** aspect is marked by the adverbial *always*.
- **Copula (*to be*) is not obligatory** to link a subject and its complement (*this coffee house
  very cheap*).
- **Noun-phrase number marking is sensitive to quantifier presence, not just count/mass status.**
  Non-count nouns inherited by CSE can be treated as count (*furnitures*, *luggages*); a count
  noun takes overt plural marking when preceded by an explicit quantifier but can remain unmarked in
  generic reference — a pattern the source notes is also found in Mandarin and Malay, consistent
  with substrate transfer.
- **Argument-drop**: subjects and/or objects recoverable from context can be left unexpressed
  entirely, a pattern not grammatical in standard English (*every year must buy for Chinese New
  Year*).
- **OSV (object-fronted) word order** occurs productively, not just as a marked/emphatic option
  (*certain medicine we don't stock in our dispensary*).
- **Tag-question formation differs systematically from standard English**: a declarative statement
  can be tagged with invariant *or not?* (requesting confirmation); the idiomatic question *can or
  not?* specifically solicits the addressee's opinion or approval; and the English-looking tags
  *is it?*/*isn't it?* carry a specific pragmatic contrast not present in standard English's
  corresponding tags — *is it?* genuinely requests new information, while *isn't it?* signals the
  speaker already believes the proposition is probably true but suspects the addressee may hold some
  disagreement about it.
- **A dedicated inventory of ~11 sentence-final discourse particles**, mostly drawn directly from
  Hokkien/Cantonese, carry pragmatic/attitudinal meaning with no direct standard-English equivalent:
  *what* (flags that the addressee's apparent assumption is mistaken), *ma* (marks the preceding
  statement as self-evidently justifying something just said/asked), *ah* (signals an expectation of
  agreement), and *lah* (signals a strong assertion), among others.

The source stresses that every one of these features is **systematic and rule-governed**, not random
"broken" English, and that none of the specific patterns is typologically unusual on its own (similar
individual features occur in many of the world's non-standard English varieties and in other
languages) — the point generalizes directly to this project's own synthesis interest: a non-standard/
contact-derived register can be a fully coherent grammatical system with its own internal logic,
distinguishable from the standard variety by a specific, catalogable bundle of morphosyntactic rules
and a discourse-particle inventory, not merely a degraded or randomly-varying version of the standard.
The section also documents CSE's *social* status at length: despite having native-speaker status for
a significant and growing proportion of Singaporeans by the late 20th century, CSE/"Singlish" carries
heavy public stigma, actively targeted by sustained government anti-Singlish campaigns (Prime
Ministers Lee Kuan Yew and Goh Chok Tong both publicly condemned it; the official **Speak Good English
Movement** runs structured "correction" lessons contrasting CSE utterances with "polite," "correct"
SSE equivalents) grounded explicitly in economic-competitiveness and international-intelligibility
arguments — while CSE simultaneously functions, for many of its own speakers (quoted: novelist
Catherine Lim, a Singaporean taxi driver), as a genuine, positively-valued marker of specifically
Singaporean identity and in-group solidarity, illustrating the same standard-vs-stigmatized-vernacular
dynamic the book has traced throughout English's own history (e.g. OE/ME dialectal variation, EModE's
prescriptive campaign against "corrupting" usage). (pp.188–198)

### §6.4–6.5 English as a global language: predictions and open questions (brief note)

This closing section is mostly sociolinguistic forecasting, not grammar/vocabulary content, and is
captured briefly. Key points: English's global spread (conventionally modeled via Kachru's
**inner circle** / **outer circle** / **expanding circle** framework — native-speaker societies;
societies where English became an institutionalized second language through colonization; and
societies learning it purely as a foreign language, respectively, though the chapter flags this model
as contested, e.g. for over-centering inner-circle varieties) is unlikely to converge on one single
future global standard; instead a **"polycentrism"** of competing national/regional standard
varieties (British, American, and newer L2-region standards) is considered the more likely outcome,
driven heavily by the economically-motivated English Language Teaching (ELT) industry's choices about
which standard(s) to export. Graddol's (1997) **engco** model proposes economic openness, demographic
youth/growth, and the UN's Human Development Index (as a proxy for a language community's capacity to
generate and promote intellectual/cultural resources) as better predictors of a language's future
global influence than simple speaker-count or past political/military power, projecting English
alongside Spanish, Arabic, Chinese, and Hindi/Urdu as the dominant "big six" languages of 2050, with
currently high-status languages like German, French, and Japanese projected to decline in relative
global standing. The chapter's genuinely open-ended closing point, directly relevant to any
speculative-conlang extrapolation of English's future: the book explicitly declines to predict what
specific sound changes, semantic shifts, or grammaticalizations will occur as English continues
developing in new communities of speakers worldwide, noting only that, per Ch.1's mechanisms, further
change — including the possibility of new contact varieties, further creolization-type developments
(cf. §5.5's Barbados case), or even eventual full daughter-language divergence on the scale of Latin's
split into the Romance languages — remains fully open, just as it was for Proto-Indo-European's own
speakers looking forward from their own era. (pp.198–203)

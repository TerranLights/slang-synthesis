### 2 Figures of Orthography (Ch. III, Sec. I)

**Mimesis** — a ludicrous imitation of a mispronunciation via deliberately misspelled dialogue (e.g. "wery
good weal" for *very good veal*, "porn at Monmouth" for *born*) — i.e. the 1851-era term for what this
project would call eye-dialect/phonetic-respelling slang representation. **Archaism** — a word/phrase in
deliberately antiquated spelling ("*hath holpen*" for *has helped*).

### 12 Poetic Feet (Ch. IV, Sec. III) — 4 principal + 8 secondary

**Principal (form the four basic English verse orders — Iambic, Trochaic, Anapestic, Dactylic):**
**Iambus/Iamb** (short+long: *betray*), **Trochee/Choree** (long+short: *hateful*), **Anapest** (short+
short+long: *contravene*), **Dactyl** (long+short+short: *laborer*).

**Secondary (occasional variation only):** **Spondee** (long+long: *cold night*), **Pyrrhic** (short+short:
*-uous* in *presumptuous*), **Moloss** (long+long+long: *Death's pale horse*), **Tribrach** (short+short+
short: *-itory* in *prohibitory*), **Amphibrach** (short+long+short: *imprudent*), **Amphimac/Amphi-
macer/Cretic** (long+short+long: *winding-sheet*), **Bacchy** (short+long+long: *the whole world*),
**Antibacchy/Hypobacchy** (long+long+short: *knight-service*).

## Grammar points

### Chapter I — Punctuation: the full point system, 9 marks, 31 rules

**The Comma** (Sec. I) gets 17 numbered rules — by far the most elaborated point — covering: simple
sentences generally excluding commas (Rule I); dividing the clauses of compound sentences (Rule II, with
three exceptions for restrictive relatives, short tightly-joined clauses, and elliptical unions); series-comma
logic for 3+ coordinate terms (Rule III) vs. exactly 2 terms (Rule IV, generally no comma unless the pair has
its own adjuncts, is contrasted, is an explanatory restatement, or has an elided conjunction); word-pairs joined
in pairs (Rule V); nominative-absolute constructions (Rule VI); apposition (Rule VII); adjectives, finite verbs,
infinitives, participles, and adverbs when they "break the connexion" of a simple sentence or carry
clause-like weight (Rules VIII–XII); conjunctions and prepositions under the same logic (Rules XIII–XIV);
interjections requiring a pause (Rule XV); emphatic word-repetition (Rule XVI); and dependent quotations
introduced by a closely-connected verb like *say* or *reply* (Rule XVII).

**The Semicolon** (Sec. II, 3 rules): complex members, simple members, and apposition-type separations
that are "neither so closely connected" as to need only a comma "nor so independent as to require a period."
**The Colon** (Sec. III, 3 rules): additional remarks, greater pauses, and independent quotations. **The
Period** (Sec. IV, 3 rules): distinct sentences, "allied" sentences, and abbreviations. **The Dash** (Sec. V, 3
rules): abrupt pauses, emphatic pauses, and — genuinely useful as a style caution still relevant today — a rule
explicitly titled "Faulty Dashes," warning against overuse. **The Eroteme** (question mark) and **Ecphoneme**
(exclamation mark) (Sec. VI–VII, 3 rules each): direct questions, united questions, and indirect questions;
interjections, invocations, and exclamatory questions respectively. **The Curves** (parentheses) (Sec. VIII, 2
rules): the parenthesis itself, and how to handle other punctuation marks that fall inside one. **Section IX, "The
Other Marks,"** then catalogues 19 further named marks and diacritics used in English printing beyond the
nine main points (full list in Vocabulary above) — genuinely useful as a period-accurate complete inventory
of 19th-century American print conventions, including the full conventional footnote-symbol ordering
(*, †, ‡, §, ‖, ¶, then doubled) still recognizable today.

### Chapter II — Of Utterance: Articulation, Pronunciation, Elocution

**Articulation** is "the forming of words, by the voice, with reference to their component letters and sounds,"
distinguished from pronunciation by its narrower focus on the elements rather than accent; Brown lists the
named faults of bad articulation — "mumbling, muttering, mincing, lisping, slurring, mouthing, drawling,
hesitating, stammering, misreading" — a useful period vocabulary of speech-delivery registers.
**Pronunciation** (orthoëpy) requires knowledge of letters' "just powers," of vowel/syllable **quantity**
(long/short duration), and of **accent** (primary stress, with a genuine secondary/subordinate stress on longer
words — *tem'-per-a-ture'*). Brown gives a versified paraphrase of "Sanctius's rule" for Latin accent
placement (penultimate-syllable-governs-placement, never farther back than the antepenult) as a mnemonic
still worth preserving as period pedagogical material. **Elocution** covers **emphasis** (sentence-level stress
marking the most significant word, which — Brown notes, quoting Murray — can even shift a word's normal
accent for contrastive effect: "he shall *in*crease, but I shall *de*crease"), **pauses** (three kinds: distinctive/
sentential, following the sense; emphatic/rhetorical, for deliberate audience effect; and poetical/harmonic —
the line-final pause, the mid-line *caesural* pause, and finer *demi-caesura* rests, specific to verse), and
**inflections** (rising vs. falling pitch-slides, with *yes/no* questions generally taking the rising inflection and
other questions the falling — plus the **circumflex**, a combined rise-then-fall or fall-then-rise on one
syllable, and the **monotone**, a deliberately flat, unvaried pitch).

### Chapter III — Figures (Orthography, Etymology, Syntax, Rhetoric)

Four parallel systems of named "intentional deviations" from ordinary usage, one per level of grammar (full
term lists in Vocabulary above). The **Figures of Syntax** section (Sec. III) is specifically an ellipsis
taxonomy — Brown catalogues legitimate omission patterns for all ten parts of speech plus whole
phrases/clauses (e.g. omitted article: "a man and [a] woman"; omitted verb: "The world is crucified unto me,
and I [am crucified] unto the world," Gal. 6:14) — directly continuing the many individual ellipsis points
already made throughout the Syntax rules (`pt06`–`pt08`), now organized as a single compact reference table.
The **Figures of Rhetoric** section is explicitly scoped down from the "two hundred and forty" figures some
contemporary rhetoric textbooks catalogued (citing John Holmes's 253-term index as an example of the
excess) to what Brown judges the practically useful core of "about thirty" figures total across all four Figure
sections.

### Chapters IV–VI — Versification

**Verse** is "language arranged into metrical lines of some determinate length and rhythm... by a due
succession of poetic feet, or of syllables differing in quantity or stress," distinguished by **rhythm**
(quantity-pattern), **quantity** (a syllable's relative duration, conventionally long or short, with a long
syllable reckoned equal to two short ones), and the rhyme/blank-verse distinction. Brown devotes substantial
space (Obs. 2–3) to a genuinely live, unsettled 1850s debate about the theoretical foundations of English
verse — including a direct, extended engagement with **Edgar Allan Poe's** 1848 "Notes upon English Verse"
(the essay later folded into Poe's *The Rationale of Verse*), which Brown quotes at length and pushes back on
point by point (Poe's claims that line-length correspondence is inessential to verse, that regular foot-alternation
is not essential to metre, etc.); Poe's poem "The Bells" (1849) is separately cited as Brown's own illustrative
example of the **caesura** (a single emphatic syllable counted as its own metrical foot) — a notable instance of
this project's source engaging directly and critically with a major contemporary literary figure's own technical
prosodic theory, not just citing him as a usage authority. The chapter's core taxonomy — the 4 principal and 8
secondary poetic feet, and the resulting 4 principal verse orders (Iambic, Trochaic, Anapestic, Dactylic) — is
given in full in the Vocabulary section above.

Berrian, 1851; this extraction uses the Project Gutenberg plain-text transcription, EBook #11615, a

## Grammar points

### What "grammar" is: five relations to language, and Brown's working synthesis (Ch. I)

Brown opens by cataloguing the different senses in which "grammar" has been defined, because each
relation to language yields a different definition: (1) as *science* — grammar stands to language as
knowledge to the thing known, and is counted the first of the traditional seven liberal sciences (grammar,
logic, rhetoric, arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, music); (2) as *art* — the classical Latin definition "*ars
rectè scribendi, rectèque loquendi*," the art of writing and speaking correctly; (3) as *practical science* —
like navigation, combining theory and practice; (4) as a *text-book* — like a chart or guide; (5) as a
*single instance* — "the grammar of a particular text or passage." Brown surveys many historical Latin
definitions (Lily, Despauter, Melancthon, Vossius, Ruddiman, Lowth, Perizonius, R. Johnson, Scaliger,
Quintilian) and adopts R. Johnson's four-part division — **Analogy/Etymology, Syntax, Orthography,
Prosody** — as the organizing scheme for his own book, while rejecting Johnson's proposed single
definition built around "the relation of words" as too narrow (relation/agreement/government/arrangement
of words is, in Brown's view, the whole of *syntax* but only one part of grammar as a whole; spelling,
pronunciation, and word-formation have no necessary connection to "relation of words" at all).

### Universal vs. particular grammar (Ch. I)

Brown rejects Lindley Murray's claim that grammar divides into two co-equal "species," Universal and
Particular, calling this "a twofold absurdity." His own position: every cultivated language has its own
particular grammar, which *necessarily includes* whatever is universal to language in general; universal
principles are a comparatively small part of any workable practical grammar, not an independent system
standing beside it. He does allow that a genuine (if secondary) field, "Universal or Philosophical
Grammar," exists — the common ground where many or all human languages are found to coincide — but
warns against importing purely theoretical universal-grammar content into a book meant to teach the
practical grammar of one language.

### "Good use" as the standard of correctness (Ch. II)

This is the closest thing to a methodological thesis statement in the whole Introduction. Brown states that
"True grammar is founded on the authority of reputable custom; and that custom, on the use which men
make of their reason" (§3), and that the "ultimate principle... the only true standard of grammatical
propriety, is that species of custom which critics denominate GOOD USE; that is, present, reputable,
general use" (§3–4; capitalization in the original). He immediately flags the practical difficulty: it is far
easier to state this principle than to apply it, because good use is explicitly defined only negatively —
"neither ancient nor recent, neither local nor foreign, neither vulgar nor pedantic" — and ascertaining its
concrete content "where usage is various" requires real scholarly labor, not mere assertion. He also argues
(against Webster, Neef, and Cardell, all quoted and rebutted at length) that this authority properly rests
with the educated/literate, not with "unlettered" popular usage, though he concedes the latter sometimes
drives real regularizing change (e.g. citing the near-total popular replacement of the old irregular
participles *holpen, bounden, sitten* with regularized *swelled, helped, worked*-type forms — attributed to
"unlettered" speakers' preference for uniformity/analogy over inherited irregularity).

### Authorship vs. compilation: Brown's taxonomy of grammarians (Ch. II)

Brown distinguishes two "numerous classes" of flawed grammar-book authors: **visionaries**, who indulge
excessive, unearned originality (rejecting settled usage in favor of personally "philosophical" system-
building), and **copyists/compilers**, who claim too little, reproducing predecessors' material (often
unattributed) under their own name. He treats Lindley Murray's grammar as the paradigm case of the
second failure mode — Murray's own preface conceded that "the greater part of an English grammar must
necessarily be a compilation," which Brown rejects as both false and as an evasion of authorial
responsibility. (Chapter III extends this critique at length against Murray and, far more harshly, against
Samuel Kirkham; per the coverage rule this extended polemic — including Brown's page-by-page
dissection of Kirkham's prose — is not reproduced here.)

### Origin of language: partly natural, partly artificial (Ch. IV)

Surveying classical (Sanctius, Scaliger, Aristotle, Plato) and contemporary (Blair, Horne Tooke, Harris)
positions on whether language is an innate human endowment or a human invention, Brown stakes out a
middle position: language is "partly natural and partly artificial." He explicitly rejects the then-common
rhetorical-tradition narrative (Blair, Wilson, Booth, Gardiner, Bucke) that language began with the
"interjections" of primitive/"savage" humans and only gradually built up nouns, verbs, and the rest of the
parts of speech — a narrative Brown calls an unsupported "idle perplexity." Instead he argues from the
Genesis account (Adam naming the animals, Gen. 2:19–20) that the first human language must have been
immediately adequate to ordinary discourse rather than primitively rudimentary, and that the confusion of
tongues at Babel (Gen. 11:1, dated by Brown to "the year of the world 1844") is the best-attested historical
cause of linguistic diversity, though he adds that this does not by itself explain the *particular* structural
differences (declensions, moods, tenses) among later languages, which he regards as unrecoverable by
speculation. He also briefly surveys classical/biblical traditions on the origin of alphabetic *writing*
(Egyptian, Phoenician/Cadmus, and the claim — via Adam Clarke and Dr. Scott — that the Sinai tablets
may represent the first true alphabetic writing), concluding that writing is probably not coeval with
speech.

### The power of language and its relation to thought (Ch. V)

A philosophy-of-mind discussion, drawing heavily on Harris's *Hermes*, Dugald Stewart/Duncan's
*Logic*, Lord Kames, and others, on whether words are the necessary *instruments* of thought itself, or
merely the medium for *communicating* thought already formed. Brown's own position: language is not,
in general, the necessary instrument of thought (citing the capacity of the deaf and uneducated to think
without language), but in certain operations — his worked example is numeration, i.e. the capacity to
distinguish and hold in mind arbitrarily large numbers via the compositional naming system (ones →
teens → tens-compounds → "hundred" → "thousand" → "million" ...) — language genuinely does extend
what unaided cognition could manage, by giving the mind an orderly external scaffold for otherwise
unmanageably complex ideas. He also catalogues several authors' explicit complaints about the inherent
*inadequacy* of language as an instrument (Cicero, Bishop Butler, Lord Kames, Sheridan), i.e. that even
skilled use of words routinely falls short of representing what the mind intends.

### Origin and layered history of English (Ch. VI)

A compressed external history: English is identified, traced backward, with Anglo-Saxon, the language
the Germanic-origin Saxons brought to Britain after displacing/enslaving the native Britons (whom they
drove into Wales); Brown cites two contemporary philologists' counts of surviving native-British/Welsh-
derived words in English as vanishingly small (Bucke: 111; Johnson: 95), taken as evidence the Saxons
did not substantially intermix with the prior population. The pre-Saxon British/Welsh/Irish/Highland-Scots
languages are in turn traced to a putative common ancestor, "Celtic" (itself traced, per Brown's biblical
genealogy, to Gomer son of Japheth), with the caveat that the ancient geographic/ethnic scope of "Celtic"
/"Celtæ" is poorly attested and possibly as broad and loose a cover term as "Indians." Brown notes the pre-
Roman Britons were literate only in oral tradition (the Druids reportedly trained 20 years to memorize
unwritten verse, per Caesar's *Commentaries*) and dates the deepest point of post-Roman cultural/
linguistic darkness in Britain to the 10th century, "beyond which we find nothing that bears much
resemblance to the English language as now written."

### A diachronic chrestomathy of English, 11th–19th centuries (Ch. VII) — the chapter's key content

This chapter is structurally different from the rest of the Introduction: rather than argument, it is a
numbered sequence of 61 dated prose/verse specimens of English and Anglo-Saxon, presented in reverse
chronological order (19th century back to the 11th), each tagged with a monarch's reign and/or a specific
year, and most sourced from Samuel Johnson's own *History of the English Language* (prefixed to his
*Dictionary*) or from Bucke's *Classical Grammar*. This is a genuinely valuable, citable diachronic
corpus distinct from anything else in this project's English sources — a native-speaker-curated register
ladder running from Old English through Early Modern to 1830s contemporary prose, assembled by an
author working 170+ years closer to the older material than any modern source could be. Representative
points (full specimens are long verbatim quotations of public-domain older texts and are only lightly
excerpted here per copyright discipline, even though the 1851 source itself is out of copyright):

- **Orthographic/morphological notes Brown gives before the specimens (§2–5):** old books over-
  capitalize nouns and sometimes verbs; the long/short "ſ"/"s" distinction persisted into relatively recent
  print; *I*/*J* and *U*/*V* were historically undifferentiated letter-pairs (hence *w* as literally "double-u,"
  historically written as doubled *v*); the indefinite article *an* (not *a*) was regularly used before words
  now taking *a*, including before some words beginning with a consonant *sound* (*an heart, an help, an
  hill, an one, an use*); the possessive case was written without an apostrophe until the 17th century,
  formed variously in *-es/-is/-ys/-s* (*Goddes, Godes, Godis, Godys* for *God's*); the verbal plural suffix
  *-en* (*they sayen* for *they say*) dropped out of use around the start of the 16th century; the *-eth/-th*
  third-person-singular ending (*he runneth, walketh, rideth*) marked the "solemn" register against the
  newer *-s/-es* ending (*he runs, walks, rides*) marking the "familiar" register by the early 17th century —
  Brown treats this as the origin of a still-live solemn/familiar stylistic split in his own day (the *-eth* form
  surviving in Bible/liturgical register).
- **Three-way parallel-text specimen (§60), a uniquely valuable comparandum:** Luke 1:5–13 given in
  (a) 11th-century Anglo-Saxon ("Saxon Gospels"), (b) Wycliffe's 1380 Middle English Bible translation,
  and (c) the "Common Bible, 1610" (i.e. essentially the King James Version) — the same fixed religious
  text rendered across roughly seven centuries of English, letting morphological/lexical change be read
  off directly rather than inferred. E.g. v. 6 renders as Saxon *"hig wæron butu rihtwise beforan Gode...
  butan wrohte"* → Wycliffe *"bothe weren juste bifore God... withouten playnt"* → 1610 *"they were
  both righteous before God... blameless."*
- Named/dated literary specimens include Chaucer (*Of the Astrolabe*, 1391; a Boëthius translation,
  c. 1385), the *Owl and Nightingale* (reign of Richard I, late 12th c.), Robert of Gloucester's historical
  rhymes (13th c., described by Brown as "a kind of intermediate diction, neither Saxon nor English"),
  Langland's *Piers Plowman* (1350), Mandeville's travel narrative (1356), Sir Thomas More (letter written
  in the Tower with a piece of coal, 1535; description of Richard III, c. 1520), Shakespeare (*Hamlet*,
  *Lear*, c. 1603), and 18th–19th-century political/oratorical prose (Washington's Farewell Address, 1796;
  Andrew Jackson's Nullification Proclamation, 1832; Queen Victoria's accession-era address, 1837).
- King Alfred's reign (871–900 CE) is given as a fixed anchor point for the earliest attested stratum of
  English/Anglo-Saxon in this chapter, with a short specimen (continuing into Ch. VIII) of Alfred's own
  prose translation work.

### The case against the "gradual-savage-interjection" theory of language origin (Ch. IV, revisited) and against singular definitions of grammatical categories (Ch. X)

Chapter X's one substantive, transferable methodological point: Brown argues that abstract grammatical
category-terms — *number, person, gender, case, mood, tense* — are, by their logical structure,
**definable only in the plural, not the singular**, because a definition must name a property common to
all members of a class, and there is no single common property shared by (say) "singular-and-plural" that
could ground a definition of the super-ordinate term "number" itself. He illustrates by dissecting a widely-
copied Murray-derived definition, "Number is the consideration of an object, as one or more," as a
"fourfold solecism," and mocks the standard three-part definition of grammatical *person* ("the person
who speaks... spoken to... spoken of") as treating a grammatical form as if it were literally "so many
intelligent beings." This is a genuine point about the logical shape of paradigmatic grammatical categories
(closed sets defined by mutual contrast, not by a shared positive property), independent of Brown's usual
polemical framing.

### Rationale for grammatical study, and teaching method (Ch. VIII–IX)

Largely restates, with supporting quotations (Blair, Webster, Quintilian), the standard 19th-century
argument that grammatical study of one's native language is foundational to clear thought and to learning
further languages. Brown's stated teaching method (Ch. IX) — which explains this book's own structure —
combines **parsing** (resolving a given sentence according to grammatical rule) and **correcting** (fixing
a given "false syntax" sentence) as the two core practical exercises, introduced as soon as possible after
each unit's "first lesson," on the view that definitions and rules "committed to memory and not reduced to
practice" are of little value; he also argues for extensive **oral exercise**, holding that a correct habit of
speech cannot be produced by written drill alone.

### Catalogue of Grammars and "Notices of the Schemes of Certain Grammars" (skipped content, noted for completeness)

Per the coverage note above, pp. 17–38 (the alphabetical Catalogue, ~452 authors/works) and Ch. XI
(pp. 132–146, structural notices of particular grammar books' own table-of-contents schemes, covering
Lily/royal grammar, Ash, Lowth, Murray, Webster, Kirkham, and others) are bibliographic rather than
grammatical in content and are not extracted. Brown's own tally, given at the end of the Catalogue: 452
named authors, 548 works, of which 463 are grammars and 85 are other (non-grammar) books.

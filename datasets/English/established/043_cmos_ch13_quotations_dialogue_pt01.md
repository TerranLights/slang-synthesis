## Grammar points

### Scope of this chapter — and where else to look (§13.1)

This chapter covers incorporating quoted words from other sources, and to a lesser degree presenting
speech/dialogue, in running text. It explicitly delegates related topics elsewhere: quotation marks
for purposes other than direct quotation (§§7.47–76), quotation marks with titles of works (§§8.154–
95), citing sources (chapters 14–15), formatting block quotations in a manuscript (§§2.18–19),
manuscript editors' responsibilities for quoted material (§2.58), and quotation marks in relation to
surrounding punctuation (§§6.6, 6.9–11).

### Quotations and modern scholarship (§13.2)

Quoting and citing others' words is described as central, not incidental, to modern scholarship —
illustrated with a short quotation from Barzun and Graff's *The Modern Researcher* characterizing
citation as "a very sophisticated act" rather than a natural instinct, peculiar to a civilization that
believes in evidence and assigns credit/blame verifiably. CMOS notes the observation holds as true in
an electronic-publishing world as when originally written.

### Giving credit and seeking permission (§13.3)

Authors must give explicit credit whether quoting, paraphrasing, or using others' ideas — typically
via formal citation (full treatment at chapters 14–15). Written permission may additionally be needed:
for more than a line or two of a poem or song lyric still in copyright, for prose quotations exceeding
roughly three paragraphs or many short passages from one copyrighted work, or for any excerpt from
certain unpublished materials (letters, e-mails). Permissions generally are covered at chapter 4,
especially §§4.69–91.

### When to paraphrase rather than quote (§13.4)

Authors should weigh direct quotation against paraphrase for effectiveness; heavy quotation with
little commentary can distract readers, who may skip long or frequent quotations. An author who
spots an error in a passage they wish to quote should paraphrase to eliminate the error rather than
quote it verbatim (silent correction is covered at §13.7 item 5; for *sic*, see §13.59).

### When quotation and attribution is unnecessary (§13.5)

Commonly known or readily verifiable facts, proverbs, and other familiar expressions may be stated
without quotation marks or a cited source, provided the wording isn't taken directly from another
source — e.g., stating that Lincoln was assassinated a few days after Lee's surrender, or citing a
well-known proverb ("No one can convince the young that practice makes perfect") in one's own words,
needs no citation. Authors must be certain such facts are accurate; editors should flag anything that
looks suspicious (see §13.6; see also §6.51).

### Ensuring accuracy of quotations (§13.6)

CMOS stresses it is "impossible to overemphasize" the importance of meticulous quotation accuracy.
Authors should check every direct quotation against the original (or a careful transcription if the
original is unavailable) *before* submitting the manuscript — manuscript editors will query apparent
transcription errors but typically lack access to the author's sources, and it's far more efficient
for authors to transcribe accurately during writing than for anyone to retroactively verify against
original sources post-submission. See also §§2.30, 2.132.

### Permissible changes to punctuation, capitalization, and spelling (§13.7)

While a direct quotation's wording should be reproduced exactly, six categories of change are
generally permitted to fit a quotation into the syntax/typography of the surrounding text (see also
§13.8):

1. Single quotation marks may become double and vice versa (see §13.28); punctuation relative to
   quotation marks adjusted accordingly (§6.9); guillemets or other foreign-language quotation marks
   may become standard single/double marks (§13.71).
2. The initial letter may be changed between capital and lowercase (§§13.13–16).
3. A final period may be omitted or changed to a comma as required; punctuation may be omitted where
   ellipsis points are used (§§13.48–56).
4. Original note reference marks (and the notes they point to) may be omitted unless doing so would
   affect the quotation's meaning; alternatively the note may be block-set in smaller type, summarized
   in the author's own text, or have the author's own note references added within the quotation.
5. Obvious typographic errors may be corrected silently (without comment or *sic*; see §13.59) —
   unless the passage is from an older work or manuscript whose spelling idiosyncrasies are being
   deliberately preserved, in which case readers must be told elsewhere (a note, a preface) if spelling
   or punctuation has been modernized/altered.
6. The archaic Latin long s — used in early printed documents to represent lowercase *s* word-initially
   or word-medially, but never word-finally — may be modernized to a plain *s* (see mojibake/glyph note
   above regarding this chunk's rendering of the character itself); similarly, an archaic quoted title
   may be modernized in spelling. Writers/editors without a strong background in classical or
   Renaissance studies should generally be cautious about changing *u* to *v* or *i* to *j* (or vice
   versa) in such material. See also §§11.61, 11.142–43.

### Permissible changes to typography and layout (§13.8)

Five further elements may be changed to assimilate a quotation to surrounding text: the typeface/font
may be changed to agree with the surrounding text; words in full capitals may be set in small caps if
that's the surrounding style (see also §10.8); in dialogue, speaker names may be moved from a centered
position to flush left; underlined words in a quoted manuscript may be printed as italics unless the
underlining itself is integral to the source; and in quoted correspondence, paragraph indention and the
position of the salutation/signature may be adjusted. See §13.20 for paragraph indention in block
quotations, §§13.23–27 for poetry extracts, and §8.163 for permissible changes to titles of books,
articles, poems, and other works appearing within a quotation.

### Run-in versus block quotations (§13.9)

Quoted text may be run in to surrounding text and enclosed in quotation marks, or set off as a block
quotation (extract), which is never enclosed in quotation marks and always starts a new line. Block
quotations are further distinguished by indentation (left, sometimes also right) or by smaller type/a
different font — decisions normally made by the publisher's designer or by journal style. Authors
preparing manuscripts should simply use their word processor's indention feature (§2.18). For poetry,
see §§13.23–27.

### Choosing between run-in and block quotations (§13.10)

Length is usually the deciding factor: a short quotation, especially one that isn't a complete
sentence, should generally be run in; roughly a hundred words or more (about six to eight manuscript
lines) can generally be set off as a block. Other criteria also apply: a quotation of two or more
paragraphs is best set off (§§13.20–22), as is quoted correspondence that includes salutations/
signatures, lists, and material requiring special formatting. When many quotations of varying length
occur close together, running them all in may read more easily; conversely, when quotations are being
compared or treated as entities in themselves, setting them all off as blocks (however short) may work
better. Poetry is set off far more often than prose (§§13.23–27).

### Logical and grammatical assimilation (§13.11)

When incorporating fragmentary quotations into a text, the surrounding sentence should be phrased so
the quoted words fit logically and grammatically, quoting only as much of the original as necessary.
Example given: "...we are dumping 'the burden of human history' onto computer hard drives...'If
(when?) we run out of sources of electricity,' she asks, 'will we forget who we are?'"

### Integrating tenses and pronouns (§13.12)

Verbatim quotation requires integrating the original's tense and pronouns into the new sentence's
context — illustrated by converting a direct courtroom quotation ("these eleven people here are
guilty of murder...cold-blooded, deliberate and premeditated") into an indirect, grammatically
integrated form ("the eleven defendants were 'guilty of murder' and had described the murder as
'cold-blooded, deliberate and premeditated'"). Occasional adjustments may be bracketed — e.g., "though
he has 'learned from [his] mistakes'" — but this device should be used sparingly.

### Changing capitalization to suit syntax — an overview (§13.13)

Aside from proper nouns and words derived from them (§8.1), English words are normally lowercased
unless they begin a sentence or, often, a line of poetry. To meet this requirement the first word of a
quoted passage often must be adjusted to conform to its new context; in most works this may be done
silently, since it doesn't normally affect the quotation's significance. Some works (legal writing,
certain textual commentary) require the change instead be indicated via brackets (§13.16).

### Initial capital or lowercase — run-in quotations (§13.14)

A quotation introduced mid-sentence that forms a syntactical part of the sentence begins with a
lowercase letter even if the original began with a capital (e.g., Franklin "admonishes us to 'plough
deep while sluggards sleep'"). When the quotation has a more remote syntactic relation to the rest of
the sentence, the original capital is retained (e.g., "As Franklin advised, 'Plough deep while
sluggards sleep.'"). Conversely, if a quotation that was only part of a sentence in the original forms
a complete sentence as quoted, a lowercase original letter may be capitalized if appropriate — the
chunk's worked pair contrasts "Aristotle put it this way: 'Those who are eminent...'" against
"Aristotle believed that 'those who are eminent...'"

### Initial capital or lowercase — block quotations (§13.15)

The same lowercase/capital logic as run-in quotations (§13.14) applies to block quotations: an
originally capitalized initial letter may be lowercased if syntax demands it, or an originally
lowercase letter may be capitalized if syntax requires. Worked example from Aristotle's *Politics*
(Jowett translation, Modern Library) shows the same passage quoted first as a free-standing block
(retaining its original capital "Revolutions...") and then integrated into a sentence as "Aristotle
observes that / revolutions also break out..." (lowercased to fit). See also §§13.17, 13.20.

### Brackets to indicate a change in capitalization (§13.16)

In some legal writing, textual commentary, and similar contexts, it is considered obligatory (rather
than optional) to flag any capitalization change via brackets around the altered letter — a practice
easy to apply but unnecessary in most writing, and one that must be applied consistently throughout a
single work once adopted. Examples: "she is given the power '[t]o extend or renew any existing
indebtedness'"; "'[R]eal estates may be conveyed by lease and release...'"; a comparison of Aristotle's
"'[i]nferiors revolt in order that they may be equal...'" against a later "'[r]evolutions also break
out...'"

### Colon preceding a quotation (§13.17)

A formal introductory phrase, such as *thus* or *as follows*, is usually followed by a colon — e.g.,
Henry Fielding's own description of an author's role, introduced with "defines it thus:" before the
quoted definition from *The History of Tom Jones*.

### Comma preceding a quotation (§13.18)

When simply identifying a speaker, a comma is used after verbs like *said*, *replied*, *asked*; a
colon, while never wrong in such cases, should be used sparingly — reserved for introducing quotations
that consist of more than one sentence. Examples: "Garrett replied, 'I hope you are not referring to
me.'"; a longer example quoting Fish with a comma introducing a multi-clause quoted sentence.

### Period rather than colon preceding a quotation (§13.19)

Unless introduced by wording that specifically requires a colon (*thus*, *as follows*, etc.), a block
quotation may instead be preceded by a period rather than a colon; whichever convention is chosen
should be applied consistently throughout a work. Illustrated with a historical-political passage
about Poland's treatment as a "Fascist state."

### Block quotations of more than one paragraph (§13.20)

Quoted material of more than one paragraph — even if individually brief — is best set off as a block
quotation (a less desirable run-in alternative exists, see §13.30). A multiparagraph block quotation
should generally reflect the original's paragraph breaks; if the first quoted paragraph includes that
paragraph's own beginning, it need not carry a first-line indention itself, while subsequent paragraphs
get either first-line indention or (less preferred) extra interparagraph spacing (see also §13.22).
Worked example: a four-paragraph quotation from Jane Austen's *Pride and Prejudice*, including two
lines of embedded dialogue within the quoted passage. For ellipsis points at the start of a paragraph,
see §13.54.

### Block quotations beginning in text (§13.21)

A long quotation may begin with a few words run in to the text before continuing as a block — used
only when intervening text separates the quoted matter from its continuation. Example: "'There is no
safe trusting to dictionaries and definitions,' observed Charles Lamb," continuing as a block quoting
Lamb's *Essays of Elia*; a second example interrupts a block quotation mid-stream with "'In short,'
says Crane, summarizing Gordon's philosophy," before resuming the block. A permissible alternative is
to set off the entire quotation as one block, enclosing the intervening words of text themselves in
brackets (e.g., "There is no safe trusting to dictionaries and definitions [observed Charles Lamb]. We
should more willingly...").

### Text following a block quotation (§13.22)

If text following a block quotation continues the paragraph that introduced the quotation, it begins
flush left. If the resuming text instead begins a new paragraph, it receives a paragraph indention. In
works where all new paragraphs appear flush left (no indention convention at all), extra line spacing
before text following a block quotation may be necessary to signal the paragraph break.

### Setting off poetry (§13.23)

Two or more lines of verse are best set off as a block quotation (cf. §14.33). An isolated poetry
quotation is often visually centered on the page relative to its longest line, but when two or more
stanzas of the same poem appear on the same page, uniform left indention may work better (see §13.24).
A half-line to full-line space should separate stanzas; the original's indention pattern within each
stanza should be reproduced (distinguished from runover-line indention — see §13.25). For placement of
the quotation's source, see §13.69. Worked example: George Herbert's "The Collar," centered, followed
by a partial-line quotation ("there was corn / Before my tears did drown it.") showing how omitted
leading words are represented by approximated blank space.

### Uniform indention for poetry (§13.24)

Where all or most poetic quotations in a work consist of blank verse (as in studies of Shakespeare) or
are very long, uniform left indention generally works better than per-quotation centering — matching,
if one exists, the left indention already used for prose extracts. Example: three lines of blank verse
("I have full cause of weeping, but this heart...").

### Long lines and runovers in poetry (§13.25)

A poetic line too long to fit on a single typeset line has its remainder (the "runover") usually
indented one em from the line above, as shown in a worked example from Walt Whitman's "Song of
Myself" (two of the four quoted lines carry a one-em-indented runover). Runover indention must be kept
visually distinct from new lines deliberately indented by the poet (as in the Herbert example at
§13.23) — generally, a single uniform runover indent, used consistently, is enough to accomplish this
distinction.

## Grammar points

### 79. Direct object and indirect object
Ditransitive verbs take two objects: a direct object (what is given/done) and an indirect object
(to/for whom). Full form: verb + direct object + *to*/*for* + indirect object. Shortened form (no
preposition): verb + indirect object + direct object — but this shortened form is blocked when the
direct object is itself a pronoun (*Give it to John*, never *Give John it*). Which preposition (*to*
or *for*) a given ditransitive verb takes is lexically fixed per verb, not predictable from meaning
(see Vocabulary table's two verb classes). (p.115–116)

### 80. Frequency adverbs with the Present Simple
A frequency adverb sits between subject and verb by default (*I often see them*); a subset
(*occasionally, sometimes, often, frequently, normally*) can alternatively appear clause-initially or
clause-finally. *always* paired with present continuous (rather than its usual present-simple home)
produces an annoyance/complaint reading (*Peter's always complaining about his job*) — a genuine
aspectual-pragmatic shift triggered purely by tense choice, parallel in spirit to the past-continuous
aspectual contrast noted in pt02 §38. (p.117)

### 81–82. Link words
§81 drills *and, but, so, then, before, after, because* with no new rule content beyond ordinary
coordinator/subordinator semantics. §82 groups *because, as,* and *since* together as near-
interchangeable causal connectors, distinguishing this practical equivalence from *since*'s other,
temporal sense already covered in pt02 §43. (p.118–119)

### 83. *both ... and*, *neither ... nor*
*both ... and* combines two true positive statements about different subjects into one (*Maria and
Felipe both come from Colombia*); *neither ... nor* combines two true negative statements the same
way, with the main verb then taking a positive form despite the overall negative meaning (*Neither
the police nor the army could catch him*). (p.120)

### 84. Making questions
For every tense except present simple and past simple, a question is formed by inverting the
auxiliary verb and the subject. Present simple and past simple instead require *do/does*/*did*-
periphrasis (no auxiliary exists to invert, so one is supplied) — cross-referencing pt02 §28 and
§34. This split is the organizing principle behind why English question-formation looks
inconsistent at first glance: it's actually fully regular once "does this tense already have an
auxiliary" is the controlling variable. (p.121–122)

### 85. *Who asked you? Who did you ask?* — question words as subject or object
*who* and *what* can themselves be either the subject or the object of the question. As subject,
no auxiliary/do-support is inserted and normal declarative word order follows the question word
(*Who asked you?* — not **Who did ask you?**). As object, ordinary do-support question formation
applies (*Who did you ask?*). A stranded preposition stays clause-final rather than fronting with the
question word (*Who did Jane stay with?*, not **With who did Jane stay?**). This subject/object
asymmetry in whether do-support appears is a clean, teachable minimal pair the source illustrates
directly with near-identical sentence frames. (p.123)

### 86. Short responses using *so, neither, nor*
*so* + auxiliary + subject agrees with a preceding positive statement (*'I can speak Spanish.' 'So
can I.'*); *neither/nor* + auxiliary + subject agrees with a preceding negative statement. When the
original statement has no auxiliary of its own (ordinary present/past simple), *do/does/did* is
supplied to carry the construction. (p.124)

### 87. Short responses: *I think so, I hope so*
Anaphoric response formulas that affirm a proposition without repeating it in full, used either to
answer a yes/no question or to agree with a statement. Negation is lexically idiosyncratic rather
than uniform across the pair: *think* takes clausal negation on itself (*I don't think so*), while
*hope* instead negates the complement directly (*I hope not* — *I don't hope so* is not given as an
alternative). (p.125)

### 88. Relative clauses with *who* and *that*
A relative clause embeds a second sentence's information about a noun directly after that noun,
combining two independent sentences that share a referent into one. *who* relativizes people;
*that* relativizes things and, per the source, is sometimes acceptable for people too. The
relativizer *replaces* (not merely adds to) the pronoun it stands in for in the embedded clause — the
source explicitly marks retaining the original pronoun alongside the relativizer as ungrammatical
(*I was talking to a person who he worked with my father* is marked incorrect; the relativizer alone
must do the pronoun's job). This is the entirety of the book's relative-clause coverage at this
level — no *which*, *whose*, non-restrictive commas, or object-relative-pronoun omission are
introduced. (p.126–127)

## Grammar points

### 8.177 Terms like "foreword," "preface," and so on

Generic front- and back-matter terms — *foreword, preface, acknowledgments, introduction, appendix,
bibliography, glossary,* and *index* — are lowercased and set in roman type, whether used in
cross-references within the same work or in reference to another work (p. 1181).

### 8.178 Numbered chapters, parts, and so on

The words *chapter, part, appendix, table, figure,* and the like are lowercased and spelled out in
running text (though they may be abbreviated in parenthetical references). Numbers accompanying them
are given in arabic numerals regardless of how they appear in the original source; letters, if used,
may be upper- or lowercase (following the original) and are sometimes parenthesized. See also §§9.27–29
(p. 1182).

### 8.179 Titles of poems

Titles of most poems are set in roman type and enclosed in quotation marks. A very long poetic work,
especially one that itself constitutes a book (e.g. Dante's *Inferno*), is italicized instead and not
quoted. In a stand-alone literary study where many poems of varying length are discussed, it may be
preferable to italicize all poem titles uniformly rather than mixing styles (p. 1183).

### 8.180 Poems referred to by first line

When a poem lacks a title and is instead referred to by its first line, that line is capitalized
sentence-style even if the first word is lowercased in the original — but any words that were
capitalized in the original remain capitalized. See also §§8.156, 16.145 (p. 1184).

### 8.181 Titles of plays

Titles of plays are italicized regardless of the play's length — unlike poems, where length determines
whether quotation marks or italics apply (p. 1185).

### 8.182 Divisions of plays or poems

Words denoting the parts of long poems and the acts/scenes of plays (*canto, stanza, act, scene*) are
usually lowercased, set neither in italics nor quotation marks. Numbers are given in arabic numerals
regardless of the original (p. 1186).

### 8.183 Titles of freestanding publications

Titles of pamphlets, reports, and similar freestanding publications are treated much like book titles
(see §§8.166, 14.249) — italicized when mentioned or cited in text or notes (p. 1187).

### 8.184 Titles of unpublished works

Titles of unpublished works — theses, dissertations, manuscripts held in collections, unpublished
transcripts of speeches, and the like — are set in roman type, capitalized as titles, and enclosed in
quotation marks. Names of manuscript *collections* (as distinct from individual work titles within
them) take no quotation marks. The title of a not-yet-published book already under contract may be
italicized, with the word *forthcoming* (or *in press* or another equivalent term) following the title
in parentheses. For speeches, see §8.75; see also §15.42 (p. 1188).

### 8.185 Titles of movies and television and radio programs and series

Titles of movies and of television and radio programs/series are italicized. A single episode within a
television or radio series is instead set in roman and enclosed in quotation marks — the series/program
containing it stays italicized. By contrast, a purely generic, non-proper-name description of a
broadcast (e.g. "the ten o'clock news") takes no special styling at all. The names of broadcast
networks, channels, and similar entities are set in roman type, unlike program titles (p. 1189).

### 8.186 Websites and web pages

General titles of websites mentioned or cited in text or notes are normally set in roman, headline-style,
without quotation marks; an initial "the" in such a title is lowercased in midsentence. Titled sections,
pages, or special features on a website are placed in quotation marks. Titles of the types of works
discussed elsewhere in the chapter (books, journals, etc.) are styled the same way whether published in
print or online. Some websites share a name with a printed counterpart, and others (such as *Wikipedia*)
are analogous to one of the work types discussed elsewhere in the chapter — these should be styled
accordingly (i.e., by analogy to that type, not as a special website-only case). See also §§14.243–46.
Many websites either lack a formal title or lack one that distinguishes them as a website; these can
usually be identified by naming the entity responsible for the site plus a short description, and in
some cases a short form of the URL (e.g. "Apple.com") may stand in for a formal title (p. 1190).

### 8.187 Blogs and blog entries

Titles of named blogs, like the titles of journals and other periodicals (see §8.161), are italicized.
An initial "the" is treated as part of the title (compare §8.168). Titles of individual blog entries are
placed in quotation marks; untitled entries should instead be referred to by date. For citing blogs (and
comments on blogs) in notes and bibliographies, see §§14.243–46. Named podcasts, video blogs, and similar
electronic formats are generally styled by analogy to blogs: the name of a regular, ongoing feature or
publication is italicized, while individually titled pieces within it are quoted. For more examples, see
§§14.221, 14.280 (p. 1191).

### 8.188 Musical works — additional resources

This and the following paragraphs offer only general guidance for citing musical works. Writers or
editors working with highly musicological material should consult D. Kern Holoman's *Writing about
Music* (bibliog. 1.1). For a more general reference work, CMOS recommends *The New Grove Dictionary of
Music and Musicians* and the other Grove musical dictionaries, available via Oxford Music Online
(bibliog. 5). For typographic conventions used in musicology specifically, see §§7.66–71 (p. 1192).

### 8.189 Operas, songs, and the like

Titles of operas, oratorios, tone poems, and other long musical compositions are italicized and given
standard title capitalization. Titles of songs and other shorter musical compositions are instead set in
roman and enclosed in quotation marks, capitalized the same way as poem titles (see §§8.179, 8.180)
(p. 1193).

### 8.190 Instrumental works

Many instrumental works are known by generic genre names (*symphony, quartet, nocturne,* and so on),
often paired with a number or key or both; such generic names are capitalized but not italicized. A
genuinely *descriptive* title, by contrast, is usually italicized if it refers to a full work, but set
in roman and quotation marks if it refers only to a section of a work. The abbreviation *no.* (plural
*nos.*) is set in roman and usually lowercased. (For letters indicating musical keys, see §7.67.) A
single work may legitimately be cited by its generic-genre form, its descriptive-nickname form, or both
together, as the many parallel examples in this section illustrate (p. 1194).

### 8.191 Opus numbers

The abbreviation *op.* (opus; plural *opp.* or *opera*) is set in roman and usually lowercased. An
abbreviation designating a scholarly catalog of a particular composer's works, by contrast, is always
capitalized — e.g. *BWV* (Bach-Werke-Verzeichnis) for Bach, *D.* (Deutsch) for Schubert, *K.* (Köchel)
for Mozart, and *WoO* (Werke ohne Opuszahl), the last of these assigned by scholars specifically to
certain works a composer left unnumbered. When *op.* or a catalog number is used restrictively (i.e.,
to pick out one specific work rather than merely to supply additional identifying information — see
§6.26), no comma precedes it (p. 1195).

### 8.192 Recordings

The official title of an album (and sometimes a title under which the album has come to be popularly
known, e.g. via "aka") is italicized; the name of the performer or ensemble is set in roman. Individual
items on the album — songs, movements, and the like — are treated the same way as illustrated in the
preceding musical-works paragraphs. See also §§14.276–77 (p. 1196).

### 8.193 Paintings, statues, and such

Titles of paintings, drawings, photographs, statues, and other works of art are italicized, regardless
of whether the title is the artist's own, was added by someone else, or is a translation. The names of
works of antiquity, whose original creators are often unknown, are by contrast usually set in roman
rather than italicized (p. 1197).

### 8.194 Cartoons

Titles of regularly appearing cartoons or comic strips are italicized (p. 1198).

### 8.195 Exhibitions and such

Titles of world's fairs and other large-scale exhibitions are capitalized but not italicized. Smaller
exhibitions (e.g., those mounted at museums) and the titles of exhibition catalogs — which are often one
and the same title as the exhibition itself — are instead italicized (p. 1199).

### 8.196 Signs and notices

The specific wording of a common short sign or notice is capitalized headline-style when it appears in
running text, without quotation marks. A longer notice is better treated as a direct quotation instead —
sentence-style capitalization, enclosed in quotation marks (p. 1200).

### 8.197 Mottoes

Mottoes may be treated the same way as signs (headline-style, unquoted) — or, as the final example in
this section shows, alternatively set off as a quotation. If the wording of a motto is in a language
other than English, it is usually italicized instead, with only the first word of the motto capitalized.
See also §6.51 (p. 1201).

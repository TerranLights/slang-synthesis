## Grammar points

### Unit 51 — Verb + -ing

A closed set of verbs (stop, finish, delay, enjoy, mind, suggest, dislike, imagine, regret, admit,
deny, avoid, consider, involve, practice, miss, postpone, risk) plus the phrasal expressions give up
(=stop), go on (=continue), keep/keep on (=do repeatedly/continuously), and put off (=postpone) are
followed by a gerund, never an infinitive, when taking a verbal complement ("I enjoy dancing," not "to
dance"). The passive gerund ("being told") is available after these too. For finished actions,
"having done"/"having stolen" is optionally available but not required — plain -ing covers finished
actions as well. With admit, deny, regret, and suggest specifically, a "that..." clause is an
available alternative structure. p. 102.

### Unit 52 — Verb + infinitive

A parallel closed set (agree, refuse, promise, threaten, offer, attempt, manage, fail, decide, plan,
arrange, hope, appear, seem, pretend, dare, forget, learn (how), mean, need, afford, intend, tend)
takes a to-infinitive complement instead. Negation attaches as "not to..." ("decided not to go").
"Dare" uniquely permits a bare infinitive without "to" ("wouldn't dare ask him"). A continuous
infinitive (to be doing) and perfect infinitive (to have done) exist, especially after seem, appear,
and pretend ("You seem to have lost weight"). A separate pattern — ask/decide/know/remember/forget/
explain/understand + question word (what/where/how) + infinitive — covers embedded purpose/method
questions ("Tom explained how to change the tire"). p. 104.

### Unit 53 — Verb + object + infinitive

Two overlapping ditransitive-style patterns: want/ask/expect/help/would like/would prefer allow either
bare infinitive or object + infinitive ("I asked to see the manager" / "I asked Tom to help me");
"want" specifically cannot take a "that" clause. tell/remind/warn/order/invite/force/enable/teach
(how)/persuade/get (=persuade) require object + infinitive. advise/encourage/allow/permit allow either
-ing with no object or object + infinitive, with a scope shift in meaning between the general and
specific readings. "Make" and "let" are causatives taking a bare base-form complement in the active
voice (not infinitive: "made me do it," not "to do it"), with "make" switching to "to + base form"
only in the passive. pp. 106.

### Unit 54 — Infinitive or -ing? (1) — like, would like, etc.

like/love/hate/can't bear/can't stand accept either -ing or infinitive with essentially no meaning
difference. dislike/enjoy/mind accept only -ing. "Would like"/"would love"/"would hate"/"would
prefer" accept only the infinitive — contrasted directly against plain "like," where "I like playing
tennis" (general enjoyment) differs in meaning from "I would like to play tennis" (= I want to, this
occasion). "Would like/love/hate/prefer to have done" marks regret over something not done or not
possible in the past. p. 108.

### Unit 55 — Infinitive or -ing? (2) — begin, start, continue, remember, try

begin/start/continue accept either form interchangeably. "Remember to do" (recall a duty before doing
it) is distinguished from "remember doing" (recall having already done it) — the mirror image of the
already-covered forget-to-do pattern. "Try to do" (make an effort) is distinguished from "try doing"
(do something as a test/experiment to see what happens). p. 110.

### Unit 56 — Preposition + -ing

A verb directly following any preposition (in, at, with, about, of, for, in spite of, instead of, by,
after, before, without) must take the -ing form. Specific sub-patterns: "by -ing" marks method/means;
"without -ing" marks an accompanying absence of action. "To" is flagged as a genuine homograph — part
of the infinitive in some uses, a true preposition in others (prefer X to Y, look forward to); when
"to" is functioning as a preposition, a following verb must be -ing, a documented common error source
("looking forward to see," not "to seeing"). p. 112.

### Unit 57 — Verb + preposition + -ing

Verb + fixed preposition structures (succeed in, feel like, think about/of, dream of, approve/
disapprove of, look forward to, insist on, decide against, apologize for) take -ing on a following
verbal object. "Feel like doing" is glossed specifically as "I'd like to do / I'm in the mood to do."
Some of these also allow a possessive or plain-object pronoun before the -ing form when the subject of
the embedded action differs from the main clause ("insisted on me/my staying"). A second pattern (verb
+ object + preposition + -ing: accuse, suspect, congratulate, prevent, stop, thank, forgive, warn) is
frequently seen in the passive ("I was accused of telling lies"). p. 114.

### Unit 58 — Expressions + -ing

A cluster of fixed idioms all requiring -ing on a following verb: "it's no use -ing," "there's no
point in -ing," "it's (not) worth -ing" (also usable as "X is worth -ing" with X as subject), "(have)
difficulty/trouble -ing" (book flags that "difficulty"/"trouble" stay singular here, not "difficulties"
/"troubles"), "a waste of money/time -ing," and "spend/waste time -ing." A separate, semantically
distinct cluster — "go -ing" (go shopping, go swimming, go skiing, go climbing, go sailing, go fishing,
go riding, go sightseeing) — covers participating in an activity, especially sports. p. 116.

### Unit 59 — Be/get used to something (I'm used to...)

"Be used to" = something is not strange/new (an accustomed state); "get used to" = the process of
becoming accustomed. "To" here functions as a preposition (cross-referenced to Unit 56e), so a
following verb must be -ing ("used to driving," not "used to drive"). The unit explicitly re-draws
the Unit 24 contrast: "I used to do X" (past habit, no longer true, structurally and semantically
distinct) vs. "I am used to doing X" (currently accustomed to X, no claim about the past). p. 118.

### Unit 60 — Infinitive of purpose — "I went out to mail a letter." So that...

The to-infinitive expresses purpose/reason ("I went out to mail a letter"), including why something
exists or is needed ("This wall is to keep people out," "I need a bottle opener to open this bottle");
"in order to" is an available fuller alternative. "For" + gerund is explicitly disallowed for this
purpose sense. The infinitive also marks what can/must be done with something ("a place to park,"
"work to do," "nobody to talk to") and follows time/opportunity/chance/money/energy nouns. "So that"
(not the infinitive) is required instead when the purpose is negative, involves can/could, or when the
purpose benefits someone other than the main-clause subject. p. 120.

### Unit 61 — Prefer and would rather

General preference: "prefer doing X to doing Y" or "prefer to do X rather than (do) Y" — note the
particle switches between "to" (with nouns/gerunds) and "rather than" (with infinitives/bare forms).
Situational preference uses "would prefer to (do)" or "would prefer (do)ing." "Would rather (do)" is a
near-synonym of "would prefer to do" but takes the bare base form, not the infinitive ("I'd rather go
by car," not "to go"). "I'd rather you did..." expresses wanting someone else to act, using a
past-tense verb with present/future meaning — the same "unreal past" pattern seen in hypothetical if-
sentences. p. 122.

### Unit 62 — Had better do something / It's time someone did something

"Had better" (usually 'd better) resembles "should" but carries an implied negative consequence for
not complying, and always takes present/future meaning despite its "had" form, followed by the bare
base form; negative is "had better not." "It's time (for someone) to do" states a neutral deadline;
"It's time someone did" (past-tense form, present/future meaning, the same unreal-past pattern as
Units 34–36/61c) specifically implies the speaker's complaint or criticism that something is overdue.
"It's high time" / "It's about time" intensify this criticism further. p. 124.

### Unit 63 — See someone do and see someone doing

With verbs of perception (especially see, hear; also watch, listen to, feel, notice), the bare base
form after the object marks witnessing the complete action start-to-finish ("I saw him fall to the
ground"); the -ing form marks witnessing the action mid-progress, with no claim that it was completed
or witnessed in full ("I saw her walking along the street" — glimpsed in passing). The distinction is
sometimes immaterial and either form is acceptable. "Smell" and "find" accept only the -ing structure.
p. 126.

### Unit 64 — -ing clauses — "Feeling tired, I went to bed early."

A participial -ing clause can mark: two actions happening simultaneously (main clause first: "She was
sitting in an armchair reading a book"); one action occurring during a longer one, with -ing marking
the longer action ("Jim hurt his arm playing tennis" = while playing), equivalently expressible with
explicit while/when; one action preceding another, using "having (done)" for the first action (or the
written-register alternative "after -ing"), with the plain -ing form usable instead of "having done"
when the second action follows the first immediately ("Taking a key out of his pocket, he opened the
door"); and causal use, where the -ing clause (usually fronted) explains why something was done
("Feeling tired, I went to bed early" = because I felt tired). The book explicitly flags these
structures, especially the causal and sequential uses, as belonging more to written than spoken
English. p. 128.

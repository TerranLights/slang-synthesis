## Grammar points

### 67. The gerund
The *-ing* nominalization of a verb, used like a noun (as a subject, object, or object of a
preposition: *Smoking is bad for you*). Negated with *not* + gerund. Spelling-change rules mirror
those already seen for the present participle/past tense/comparatives (see pt01 §18, pt02 §35, pt04
Vocabulary note): *-ie* endings become *-y-* (*lie→lying*), silent terminal *-e* drops
(*take→taking*), and single-vowel-plus-single-consonant endings double the consonant
(*sit→sitting*). (p.96)

### 68. *like, dislike* and other verbs + gerund
A lexically-defined class of verbs (see Vocabulary table) can take either a gerund or an ordinary
noun as their object; a subset of that class (*like, love, prefer, start*) can alternatively take a
*to*-infinitive, with no meaning distinction given at this level. (p.97)

### 69. Prepositions + gerund
Any verb that follows a preposition must take gerund form, never the bare infinitive (*thought about
leaving*, not *thought about to leave*). The source flags a genuine structural ambiguity: *to* can
be either this prepositional *to* (triggering a gerund) or the infinitive marker (triggering a bare
verb) — the surface string is identical, and the two are told apart only by substitutability with an
ordinary noun in the same slot (*looking forward to going* / *looking forward to my holiday* — both
work, confirming prepositional *to*; an infinitival *to* would not survive the same substitution).
(p.98)

### 70. Gerunds as subjects of sentences
A gerund (phrase) can itself be the grammatical subject of a sentence (*Smoking makes me feel sick*;
*Living in a foreign country can be very difficult*) — the same nominal distribution already
established for gerunds-as-objects in §67, now extended to subject position. (p.99)

### 72. *to* + infinitive after certain verbs
A separate lexically-defined class of verbs (distinct from the §68 gerund/noun class) obligatorily
takes a following *to*-infinitive rather than a gerund (*want to stay, decided to wait*). Negated by
inserting *not* before the infinitive (*decided not to wait*). (p.101)

### 73. *to* + infinitive to express purpose
A *to*-infinitive clause can express the purpose/goal of the main clause's action (*I went to London
to study English*) — functionally answering an implicit "why." (p.102)

### 74. *in order to* + infinitive, *so as to* + infinitive to express purpose
Two more explicit, multi-word alternatives to bare *to* + infinitive for purpose (§73), differing
from it in register and in which verbs they pair comfortably with: *in order to* can be more formal
than bare *to*; both *in order to* and *so as to* are preferred over bare *to* specifically before
*be, have, know*; and negative purpose is far more naturally expressed with *in order not to* / *so
as not to* than with bare *not to* + infinitive, which the source implicitly treats as the dispreferred
option by giving no bare-*not to* example at all in this section despite §72 confirming it's
grammatical for ordinary complement clauses. (p.103–104)

### 75. Writing direct speech
Direct speech reports a speaker's exact words inside quotation marks. Mechanical conventions given:
the quoted statement always begins with a capital letter; the reporting clause (*she said*) can
precede or follow the quotation, separated from it by a comma; commas and full stops that belong to
the quotation go inside the closing quotation mark; a quoted question's question mark goes inside the
quotation mark and is never followed by a comma. (p.105–108)

### 76. *say, tell*
A genuine argument-structure (not just meaning) contrast. *tell* requires a personal direct object
(*told me*) and cannot introduce a directly-quoted question. *say* never takes a personal direct
object at all; the same semantic role is instead expressed via a *to*-prepositional phrase (*said to
me*). A small set of fixed idiomatic objects (*tell the truth, tell a lie/lies, tell the time, tell a
story*) is given as the exception where *tell* appears without any personal direct object. (p.109–110)

### 77. Reported statements with no change of tense
When the reporting verb itself is present, present perfect, or future, the reported statement's own
tense is left unchanged from the original direct-speech form (*I'm not going* → *He says he isn't
going* / *He'll say he isn't going* / *He's said he isn't going* — only the pronoun shifts, not the
tense). The complementizer *that* is optional after the reporting verb. (p.111–112)

### 78. Reported statements with a change of tense
When the reporting verb itself is in a past tense, the reported statement's tense backshifts one
step into the past: present simple → past simple; present continuous → past continuous (*'My name's
Ian' (he said)* → *He said his name was Ian*; *'I'm writing a letter' (she said)* → *She said she was
writing a letter*). This is the chapter's core backshift rule, presented as a direct function of the
reporting verb's own tense (§77 vs. §78) rather than of anything else in the sentence — note the
chapter as captured here covers only present-tense source statements; backshift from other source
tenses (past simple, present perfect, etc.) is not covered within this chunk's page range. (p.113–114)

## Grammar points

### Ten overcorrections (Ch.21)

A named list of errors that come from *over*-applying a grammar rule rather than ignoring it,
presented as equally damaging as the original mistake they're meant to avoid:

1. **Substituting "whom" for "who" indiscriminately** — *whom* is needed only when the sentence
   genuinely requires an object; inserting it reflexively into subject slots (*"Whom shall I say **is**
   calling?"*, where untangling shows the pronoun is actually the subject of *is calling*) is wrong.
2. **Inserting unnecessary "had"s** — *had* belongs only on the earlier of two past-tense events on a
   timeline; sprinkling it onto every past-tense verb in a sentence (*"had already shaved when the can
   **had** exploded"*) over-marks events that didn't actually need sequencing.
3. **Throwing in "have" at random** to sound more sophisticated — e.g. *"Nice to **have** met you,"*
   which incorrectly implies a deadline/prior completion rather than the intended present-moment
   greeting (*"Nice to meet you"*).
4. **Sending "I" to do a "me" job** — hypercorrecting *me* to *I* in object position, especially after
   a preposition (*"between you and **I**"* instead of *"between you and me"*).
5. **Speaking or writing passively** to sound more formal/official — flagged as stilted overuse,
   contrasted with active voice's directness; passive isn't wrong but is overused by official/
   government writing specifically to avoid naming a responsible subject.
6. **Making sentence structure too complicated** — padding with cleft/relative constructions
   (*"It was this treaty that ended the war"*, *"That which he discovered..."*) rather than a direct
   statement, mistaking complexity for maturity of style.
7. **Letting descriptions dangle** by overusing introductory descriptive elements until the pattern
   itself becomes a new source of error (see Ch.15's dangling-modifier rule, pt04 of this extraction).
8. **Becoming allergic to "they"/"their"** — overcorrecting away from a legitimately plural antecedent
   (*the kids... their homework*) into a mismatched *his or her* out of excess caution, when the
   antecedent was plural all along and didn't need the singular-indefinite fix from Ch.11.
9. **Being semi-attached to semicolons** — scattering semicolons decoratively rather than reserving
   them for their one real job (linking two complete sentences, or separating a comma-containing
   list).
10. **Not knowing when enough description is enough** — over-explaining past the point of relevance,
    contrasted with the useful goal of specific, relevant detail. (pp.269–271)

### Ten errors to avoid at all cost (Ch.22)

A second named list, this one of errors the book treats as more seriously damaging than the
"overcorrections" above (mistakes of omission/carelessness rather than over-caution):

1. **Writing incomplete sentences** habitually (an occasional deliberate fragment for style is fine;
   a pattern of them reads as not knowing how to write a complete sentence).
2. **Letting sentences run on**, especially via comma splices (a comma alone attempting to join two
   complete sentences) and false-conjunction glue words (*consequently, however, therefore,
   nevertheless* — see pt01 of this extraction for the semicolon-plus-comma rule these actually
   require).
3. **Forgetting to capitalize "I"** — flagged as a small error with outsized reader-alienation effect.
4. **Being stingy with quotation marks** — omitting attribution when incorporating someone else's
   exact words is framed as a form of dishonesty (plagiarism), not just a punctuation lapse.
5. **Using pronouns incorrectly** — the chapter's own cross-reference back to the basic pronoun rules
   in Chapters 3 and 10 (one-and-only-one clearly identifiable noun per pronoun; correct case;
   singular/plural matching).
6. **Placing new (unfamiliar) vocabulary in the wrong context** — using a recently-learned word before
   its nuances are actually understood, illustrated by a real student error the author cites
   ("suppository" used to mean "repository").
7. **Letting slang seep into formal speech/writing** — see the dedicated note below; this is the
   section most directly relevant to the slang-synthesis project.
8. **Forgetting to proofread.**
9. **Over-relying on computer grammar/spell-checkers**, which the author notes both miss real errors
   and flag correct text as wrong.
10. **Repeating yourself** — restating the same point multiple times in different words, illustrated
    by the chapter's own deliberately redundant closing paragraph. (pp.273–275)

### Slang as a register risk (Ch.22, "Letting Slang Seep into Your Speech")

The book's own worked illustration deliberately mixes slang terms from several different eras in one
paragraph (*"It ain't that slang is a total bomb. In fact, slang can be bad — the real bee's knees.
But if you don't have the 411, you may miss the boat."*) specifically to demonstrate that a reader
may recognize some of the mixed-era slang terms and not others. The author's stated diagnosis: slang
changes too fast for anyone to track consistently, so a writer who deploys it risks the reader missing
the intended meaning entirely (not merely sounding informal) — and separately, slang reads as
informal register, which is itself a liability when writing for an audience (a boss, a teacher) where
impressing with formal register matters. See the Vocabulary table above for each individual slang
term's gloss as the book presents it (*ain't, bad, the bee's knees, 411, total bomb*). (p.274)

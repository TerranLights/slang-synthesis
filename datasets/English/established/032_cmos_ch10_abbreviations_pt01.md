**Mojibake gotcha encountered and corrected:** this PDF's text layer renders some non-ASCII
characters as UTF-8-bytes-read-as-Latin-1 mojibake. Two instances found in this range: (1) the
em dash in the §10.1 sidebar heading "Abbreviations—additional resources" renders corrupted in the
nav sidebar but is legible and correct in the main page heading, so the dash was restored from the
clearly legible context; (2) in §10.2's parenthetical example of a *symbol*, the copyright sign
renders as "Â©" (classic UTF-8 0xC2 0xA9 read as Latin-1) — corrected to "©" below. Both are
high-confidence corrections, not low-confidence guesses, since the intended character is
unambiguous from context and from the well-known mojibake pattern.

## Grammar points

### 10.1 Abbreviations — scope of the chapter

CMOS's abbreviations chapter covers general and scholarly writing, with lighter coverage of
technical usage. For terms not covered, the chapter points to *Merriam-Webster's Collegiate
Dictionary*, Stahl & Kerchelich's *Abbreviations Dictionary*, the multivolume *Acronyms,
Initialisms, and Abbreviations Dictionary*, and (for technical material) *Scientific Style and
Format*.

### 10.2 Core terminology: acronym, initialism, contraction, abbreviation, symbol

CMOS defines its own working vocabulary before laying out rules: an *acronym* is read as a word
(AIDS, NASA), an *initialism* is read letter-by-letter (AOL, XML), and a *contraction* keeps the
first and last letters of the full word (Mr., amt.) — not to be confused with the apostrophe-formed
contraction type covered elsewhere (§7.29). CMOS explicitly flags that these three categories leak
into each other at the edges (an initialism letter can come from a sound rather than a spelled
letter, as the *X* in XML; a letter can come from a number, as *W3C*; some items like FAQ or JPEG
straddle two categories). Because of this fuzziness, CMOS adopts *abbreviation* as its own umbrella
term for the rest of the chapter, reserving *symbol* for a non-letter mark standing in for a term
(© for *copyright*; SI unit symbols are a special case, cross-referenced to §10.54–62).

### 10.3 When to use abbreviations

Outside technical/scientific writing, abbreviations are most natural in tabular matter, notes,
bibliographies, and parenthetical asides — in running prose they should generally be limited to
terms frequent enough to be worth abbreviating (CMOS's rough threshold: roughly five or more
occurrences within the same article or chapter), and such terms should be spelled out at their
first occurrence, with the abbreviation introduced once in parentheses and never reintroduced later.
A sufficiently well-known abbreviation (DNA, NASA, IQ) can be used without ever being spelled out,
because it functions as its own dictionary headword. Editors should track how many distinct
abbreviations accumulate in one document, since an unfamiliar-abbreviation-heavy text may need a
standalone list of abbreviations for readers (cross-ref §1.43, §2.21).

### 10.4 Periods with abbreviations

Four general-purpose guidelines govern periods: (1) use a period when the abbreviation ends in a
lowercase letter (p., vol., e.g., a.m., Dr.) — with academic degrees like DLitt or DMin allowed as
an exception; (2) use a period after an initial standing in for a given name (JFK's middle-initial
convention, though the whole-name form itself may drop periods, cross-ref §10.12); (3) use no
periods for an abbreviation that is entirely or mostly full capitals (VP, CEO, PhD, US), even with
one or two lowercase letters embedded; (4) in publications that still use traditional (not
two-letter postal) state abbreviations, periods are used for the country and state abbreviations
(U.S., N.Y., Ill.) — CMOS itself recommends the two-letter postal-code form instead (cross-ref
§10.28). Separately, British and French usage often drop periods from contractions altogether (Dr,
assn, Mme) — an explicit cross-regional style contrast the source itself draws. A slash sometimes
substitutes for periods (c/o for "care of," n/a for "not applicable"), and more generally can stand
for "per" (cross-ref §6.107). Units of measure are usually spelled out in nontechnical prose but
generally drop periods in scientific usage (cross-ref §10.52–71).

### 10.5 Space or no space between elements

No space is used between the individual letters of an initialism or acronym regardless of case
(RN, C-SPAN, YMCA). Space is kept between abbreviated *words* that form a multi-word abbreviation
(Gov. Gen., Mng. Ed., Dist. Atty.) — except when a multi-letter word abbreviation is directly
combined with a single-letter abbreviation, in which case no space precedes the single letter
(S.Dak., S.Sgt.).

### 10.6 Capitals versus lowercase for acronyms and initialisms

Initialisms default to all capitals (HIV, VP, LCD) even when not derived from a proper noun. With
frequent enough use, some acronyms become fully lowercase common words (scuba), while acronyms
genuinely derived from proper nouns keep an initial capital. CMOS's general preference is the
all-capital form unless *Webster's* lists the term otherwise (NAFTA, not "Nafta"). Separately, when
the words behind an acronym/initialism are themselves not proper nouns (or don't together constitute
one, as an organization's official name would), the spelled-out form should generally be lowercased
even when the abbreviation itself stays capitalized (transmission-control protocol/Internet
protocol, i.e., TCP/IP).

### 10.7 Italic versus roman type for abbreviations

An abbreviation is italicized only if the term it stands for would itself be italicized when spelled
out — most commonly the title of a book or periodical (OED for the *Oxford English Dictionary*,
JAMA for the *Journal of the American Medical Association*). Common Latin abbreviations (ibid.,
etc., e.g., i.e.) are set in roman type despite being Latin-derived (cross-ref §7.53).

### 10.8 Small versus full-size capitals for acronyms and initialisms

Some book designs prefer small capitals over full-size capitals for acronyms/initialisms (NASA
styled as small caps rather than full-size NASA), but CMOS does not generally recommend this. If
used, the editor — not the author — should mark which items get small capitals on the final
manuscript, generally limited to acronyms/initialisms appearing in running text (not, for example,
two-letter postal codes in notes/bibliographies, or roman numerals following a personal name).
Small-capital rendering is not standardized across software/coding platforms, so behavior can vary.
Cross-ref §10.39, §10.42 (further small-capital usage) and §7.48 (small caps for emphasis).

### 10.9 "A," "an," or "the" preceding an abbreviation

The choice between *a* and *an* before an abbreviation follows how the abbreviation would actually
be read aloud, not its written first letter. Acronyms are read as words and are rarely preceded by
an article at all (except when used adjectivally, e.g. "a NATO initiative"); initialisms are read as
a series of letters and commonly do take an article ("an HMO," "a UFO," "an NAACP convention").
Two deliberately constructed minimal pairs in the source show the same string taking a different
article depending on which pronunciation/meaning is intended: "an AA meeting" (read as letters)
versus "a AA battery" (read as the spoken phrase "double-A"); and "an MS symptom" (multiple
sclerosis, read as letters) versus "a MS by..." (manuscript, read as the spelled-out word).
Cross-ref §10.2, §7.44.

### 10.10 Ampersands

No space is left on either side of an ampersand used within an initialism or combined-word
abbreviation (R&D, Texas A&M).

### 10.11 Personal names

Given names are normally not abbreviated in CMOS style — the exception is a signature, which should
be transcribed exactly as the person actually wrote it, abbreviated or not (Benj. Franklin,
Geo. D. Fuller, Ch. Virolleaud).

### 10.12 Initials in personal names

An initial standing for part of a given name is followed by a period and a space, even when the
initial doesn't actually stand for a full name (as with Harry S. Truman's middle "S"). When an
entire name is reduced to initials, periods and spaces are usually dropped entirely (FDR, MJ) — but
exceptions exist, such as the pen name H.D. for Hilda Doolittle, which keeps periods but drops the
space between them.

### 10.13 Abbreviating titles before names

Many civil or military titles may be abbreviated when they precede a person's full name (Rep. Dan
Lipinski, Sen. Kirsten E. Gillibrand, Rear Adm. Carol M. Pottenger) but should be spelled out in full
when used with a surname alone (Representative Lipinski, Senator Gillibrand, Rear Admiral
Pottenger). Cross-ref §8.18.

### 10.14 Civil titles

CMOS gives a short illustrative (not exhaustive) list of civil-title abbreviations usable before a
full name where space is tight: Ald. (alderman), Assoc. Prof., Asst. Prof., Atty. Gen., Fr.
(father), Gov., Insp. Gen., Judge Adv. Gen., Pres., Prof., Sr. (sister — unrelated to the "Sr." =
"Senior" name suffix at §10.19), Supt.

### 10.15 US military titles

Official US-military rank abbreviations generally drop periods and can vary by branch (the source
notes the army uses "SSG" for staff sergeant while the air force and marines prefer "SSgt" — branch
variants are not individually tabulated). CMOS recommends the *traditional* abbreviated forms
(which don't vary across branches) for general/historical contexts, reserving the official no-period
forms for contexts needing exact official usage, and spelling the title out in full wherever no
traditional abbreviation exists. The chapter gives a reference table of twenty rank pairs (official
form / traditional form), tabulated above, and points to the US Department of Defense's website and
Timberlake/Zurick's *Army Dictionary and Desk Reference* for fuller/current rank insignia lists.

### 10.16 Social titles

Ms., Mrs., Messrs., and Mr. are always abbreviated, whether used with a full name or a surname
alone; only the fully spelled-out forms *Mister* and *Doctor* occur, and only in direct address
without an accompanying name ("Thank you, Doctor"). CMOS notes social titles are routinely dropped
from running prose altogether (a few periodicals still retain the practice), and that a title should
never double up with a following academic/professional designation (Jennifer James, MD — not "Dr.
Jennifer James, MD"). *Esq.* (Esquire) is flagged by CMOS as "now somewhat archaic," used only
after a full name, and never combined with a preceding Mr./Dr.

### 10.17 French social titles

French social titles carry inconsistent periods depending on source (*Mme* and *Mlle* are counted
as true contractions — first+last letter — so French usage often drops their periods too, alongside
*M.* and *MM.*). When *Monsieur*, *Messieurs*, *Madame*, or *Mademoiselle* is used in direct address
without a name, it is spelled out in full and, in French usage, generally lowercased.

### 10.18 "Reverend" and "Honorable"

*Rev.* and *Hon.* are traditionally used immediately before a full name when "the" does not precede
the title; when "the" is used, the title should be spelled out ("the Reverend Sam Portaro," "the
Honorable Henry M. Brown"). With a surname alone, these titles are normally dropped entirely, though
the informal construction "Reverend So-and-So" is common in speech and informal prose.

### 10.19 "Jr.," "Sr.," and the like

*Jr.* and *Sr.*, along with roman or arabic generational numerals (*III*, *3rd*), are treated as part
of the name itself — they stay attached whenever any title or honorific is also used, and (unlike
most titles) are used only in connection with the person's full name, never with the surname alone.
Cross-ref §6.47, §9.43.

### 10.20 Academic degrees (opening, continues in pt.2)

CMOS recommends omitting periods from academic-degree abbreviations (BA, DDS) unless a specific
journal's house style or tradition requires them; periods are shown in the reference list only where
genuine placement ambiguity exists. Spelled-out degree names, even when capitalized in institutional
or promotional contexts (letterhead, business cards), should be lowercased in normal running prose.
Cross-ref §8.28. The reference list itself (CMOS's selective, not exhaustive, roster of common
degrees) begins with AB and is tabulated above through SM; it continues past this chunk's assigned
page range and resumes in `033_cmos_ch10_abbreviations_pt02.md`.

## Grammar points

### OE spelling and pronunciation (§3.4.1)

OE used the same 7 vowel graphs as Modern English (*a, æ, e, i, o, u, y*) for 14 distinct short/long
vowel phonemes, distinguishing length with a macron (ā), plus four diphthongs (*eo, ēo, ea, ēa*).
Consonants were largely shared with Modern English, but OE orthography was more phonetically
transparent: doubled consonant graphs reliably signaled true consonant length (*bedd* 'bed' vs.
*bēd* 'prayer'; *racca* 'rigging' vs. *raca* 'rake'), and several modern phonemes were then only
conditioned allophones of a single OE phoneme, triggered by position: [f]~[v] and [s]~[z] were
positional allophones of /f/ and /s/ respectively (voiced medially, voiceless word-initially/
finally); [θ]~[ð] were allophones of a single fricative written ⟨þ⟩/⟨ð⟩ interchangeably; and /g/
and /h/ each had three positional allophone realizations ([g]/[j]/[ɣ] and [h]/[x]/[ç]
respectively, conditioned by adjacency to front vs. back vowels/consonants or word-initial
position). A fossilized exception, OE *gēs* 'geese' retaining [g] before a front vowel, is explained
historically: the word's earlier form *gōsi* had [g] before a back vowel, and only lost its
conditioning final *-i* (via *i*-mutation and loss) after the [g] pronunciation was already fixed.
(pp.76–78)

### OE noun morphology: grammatical gender, stem classes, and case (§3.4.2.1)

OE nouns were marked for one of three **grammatical genders** (masculine, feminine, neuter),
inherited from proto-Germanic/PIE, which governed agreement on modifiers/pronouns but did not
reliably track a noun's **natural** (biological) gender — e.g. *wīf* 'woman' is grammatically
neuter, *hlāf* 'loaf' masculine, *bōc* 'book' feminine. Statistical work (cited: Platzer 2001) found
strong correlation between grammatical and natural gender specifically for **human-animate** nouns
(96.67% in one sample), including a tendency for pronoun reference to shift toward natural gender
even against grammatical agreement, and for human-animate loanwords to be assigned natural rather
than grammatical gender (e.g. *papa* masculine, *hūsbonda* masculine) — evidence that grammatical
gender was becoming functionally marginal for this noun class by the late OE period. For
**non-animate** nouns, by contrast, grammatical/natural gender correlation was much weaker (21.94%
neuter in one sample), and loanwords in this class still took grammatical, not natural, gender — two
genuinely divergent gender systems co-existing within one grammatical category. Separately, OE nouns
inherited a system of **stem-class declensions** from proto-Germanic, originally conditioned by a
now-lost stem-final vowel or consonant (vocalic *a-, o-, u-, i-* stems vs. consonantal *n-stem* and
root-consonant stems), each with gender tendencies and its own inflectional paradigm for the OE
cases: **nominative** (subjects, complements, direct address), **accusative** (direct objects),
**genitive** (possessives), **dative** (indirect objects, some prepositions), and **instrumental**
(rare, "by means of" function, largely folded into the dative). The masculine/neuter *a*-stem
declension (including absorbed *i*-stems) was the majority default and the only one that remained
fully productive — its genitive singular *-es* and plural *-as* are the direct source of Modern
English's possessive *-'s* and regular plural *-s*. Many paradigms already showed heavy
**syncretism** (identical forms doing double or triple grammatical duty) within the OE corpus itself,
indicating ongoing morphological simplification already in progress before the Middle English
period. (pp.78–82)

### OE definite article and adjective agreement (§3.4.2.2–3.4.2.3)

The OE definite article agreed with its noun in gender, case, and number (unlike invariant modern
*the*) — masculine nominative singular *se* was leveled by analogy to þ-initial *þe*, source of
modern *the*; neuter nominative/accusative singular *þæt* is the direct source of modern *that*.
Adjectives also agreed in gender/case/number and additionally had two full paradigms: **strong**
(used with no definite article/demonstrative/possessive present, for generic reference, e.g. *lovely
houses*) and **weak** (used after a definite article/demonstrative/possessive, for specific
reference, e.g. *the lovely house*) — a distinction with no formal survival in Modern English beyond
the comparative/superlative suffixes *-er*/*-est* (< OE *-ra*/*-ost*). (pp.82–84)

### OE personal pronouns (§3.4.2.4)

OE pronouns marked case, gender (3rd person singular only), and number — but additionally preserved
a **dual** number (distinct forms for exactly 'we/you two') not present in Modern English, and a
distinct set of 2nd person singular/plural forms (modern English collapsed *thou*/*you* to a single
*you*). The OE 3rd person plural pronoun set (*hī*, *him*/*heom*, *hira*/*heora*) was entirely
replaced in Middle English by Old Norse-derived forms (*þai* > *they*, *þeim* > *them*, *þeir(e)* >
*their*) — a rare case of a whole closed-class paradigm being borrowed rather than inherited. (p.85)

### OE verb morphology: weak vs. strong, suppletion (§3.4.2.5)

OE verbs fell into two main classes by how they formed preterite/past-participle forms: **weak**
verbs (the majority, 3 subclasses) added a dental suffix (*-d*/*-t*), the ancestor of the modern
regular *-(e)d* preterite; **strong** verbs (about 300 total, 7 ablaut classes) instead changed their
root vowel via **ablaut**, an inherited Indo-European alternation pattern (e.g. *drīfan/drāf/drifon/
gedrifen* 'drive/drove/driven'). Both types shared the infinitive suffix *-an* (later replaced by
periphrastic *to*), identical person/number present-indicative and -subjunctive inflections, a shared
plural-preterite ending *-on*, and the completive prefix *ge-* on all past participles (later made
redundant once periphrastic *have*+participle became the standard perfect construction, and lost).
Over time many strong verbs acquired weak preterite/participle forms by analogy, and *-(e)d* became
English's de facto productive default for any new verb entering the language (see Ch.1). Modern
*keep/kept* looks like a strong (vowel-changing) verb pattern but is historically a regular weak
verb (OE *cēpan/cēpte*) whose apparent vowel shift is a later sound-change artifact (assimilation of
weak *-d* to the preceding voiceless [p], followed by independent vowel-shift changes), not true
ablaut — illustrating that the modern regular/irregular distinction doesn't map cleanly onto the
historical weak/strong one. The verb **'to be'** (OE *bēon*) is fully **suppletive**, combining
present forms from one PIE root (*es-*, giving *eom/is/sind(on)*), an alternative present form from
another (*er-*, giving *eart*), an infinitive/some-forms root from a third (*bheu-*, giving *bēo-*),
and preterite forms from a historically distinct verb *wesan* (giving *wæs/wǣron*) — four originally
unrelated verbs synchronically fused into one paradigm. (pp.85–88)

### OE syntax: flexible word order, OV vs. VO, question inversion, negation (§3.4.3)

Because case marking and subject–verb agreement carried much of the grammatical load that word order
carries in Modern English, OE word order was considerably more flexible, permitting both OV and VO
orders depending on context/register rather than a single fixed default. **OV** order was favored
when the object was a pronoun, when the object was topicalized (fronted for emphasis), or in
subordinate clauses introduced by a relative pronoun like *þæt* 'that'. **VO** order was the basic,
unmarked order in main clauses, and was also regularly triggered by clause-initial adverbials like
*þā* 'then', which caused subject–verb inversion to give **V(S)O** order (a pattern distinct from
Modern English, where only auxiliaries/modals invert, not full lexical verbs). By the late Anglo-Saxon
period VO had become the overall norm, in apparent correlation with the ongoing erosion of case
marking described above — this large-scale directional shift in basic word order is an instance of
what Ch.1 terms **drift**. Questions showed obligatory subject–verb inversion (*hwæt sceal ic
singan?* 'what shall I sing?'). Negative statements placed the negative particle *ne* clause-
initially, typically followed directly by the verb and then the subject (*ne con ic noht singan*
'I don't know how to sing'). (pp.88–89)

### OE vocabulary: conservative native stock with layered borrowing (§3.4.4)

OE vocabulary was comparatively conservative and overwhelmingly Germanic, relying much more heavily
than later English periods on native **compounding** and **affixation** for word-formation rather
than borrowing. OE compounds, like modern ones, were normally right-headed (the final element
determines part of speech and core meaning, e.g. *hēah-clif* 'high-cliff' = a cliff, not a height).
Some OE compounds are still fully transparent in form only, having phonologically "fused" over time
into opaque modern words whose original two-morpheme structure is no longer recognized by speakers
(*dæges-ēage* 'day's eye' > *daisy*; *gār-lēac* 'spear-leek' > *garlic*; *hūs-wīf* 'house-wife' >
*hussy*; *nosu-þyrel* 'nose-hole' > *nostril*) — this is also the source of several English place
names (*Boston* < *Botulph's stone*; *Norwich* < *north* + *wīc* 'village'). Compounding was also a
prized poetic device: alliterative verse's formal demands made synonym-generating kennings
(*swanrād* 'swan-road' for 'sea') a valued compositional resource. Native derivational affixation was
similarly productive, using prefixes with negating (*for-*, *un-*), intensifying (*for-*), or
privative (*wið-* 'against') force, and suffixes forming adjectives from nouns (*-ig* > modern *-y*)
or abstract nouns (*-hād* > modern *-hood*, *-dōm* > modern *-dom*). Borrowing was comparatively
minor and occurred in three distinct strata with different sources and motivations: (1) continental
Latin, acquired before migration to Britain through Roman trade/military contact (~175 words:
everyday trade/food terms like *butter, cheese, pepper, mile*); (2) Celtic, acquired from Britain's
earlier inhabitants (~12 words, mostly place names/topographical and animal terms — a low figure
explained by the socio-historical asymmetry of the contact); (3) post-Christianization Latin/Greek
religious vocabulary (~500 words total from Latin across the whole OE period, though many of these
have since been lost from the modern language); and (4) Old Norse, acquired through sustained, mutually
intelligible, intimate contact with Viking settlers particularly in the Danelaw — distinctively,
Norse loans include **everyday, core-register vocabulary and even closed-class/function words**
(the entire modern 3rd-person-plural pronoun set; prepositions *till*/*fro*), not just prestige or
specialist terms, which is the diagnostic signature of exceptionally close bilingual contact rather
than one-way prestige borrowing. Norse/English cognate doublets sometimes co-existed as pure
synonyms until one fell out of use (*ey*/*egg*), and sometimes survived by semantically
differentiating (*shirt*/*skirt*, both originally 'garment'). The chapter explicitly rejects the
claim (Bailey & Maroldt 1977) that Norse–English contact produced **creolization** — citing
Thomason & Kaufman's (1988) finding that the textual evidence shows continuous development with
varying borrowing intensity, not the structural discontinuity a creole-genesis event would produce.
(pp.89–92)

### Grammatical vs. "performed" gender in *Beowulf* (§3.5) — brief note

This section is a literary/gender-studies reading of *Beowulf*, not a grammar/vocabulary survey, and
is captured only briefly per the coverage rule. Its linguistically relevant observation: OE poetic
usage could deliberately **destabilize** grammatical gender agreement for rhetorical effect — Grendel's
mother is described with a mix of feminine-marked nouns/pronouns (*ides*, *wīf*, *hēo*) and
masculine-marked ones (*aglæca*, *mihtig manscaða* 'mighty destroyer', pronoun *hē*) depending on
whether the poet is emphasizing her female "otherness" or her combat-capable threat — i.e.
grammatical gender assignment in OE verse could itself be manipulated as a meaning-bearing
sociolinguistic resource, not merely a fixed inflectional category. (pp.96–100)

## Grammar points

### Chapter 7 — The rise of the *(for) NP to V* construction (7.2.1)

Traces the reanalysis of a benefactive dative/*for*-phrase (*it is bad for you to smoke* — originally
*for you* a benefactive PP complement of the adjective, with *to smoke* a separate purpose-adjunct
infinitive) into a true *(for) NP to V* construction where *for* is reanalyzed as a complementizer
and the NP as the infinitive's own subject (*it is intolerable for John to get away with this*, where
*intolerable* cannot license a benefactive role at all). Old English already had the benefactive
source construction, with the benefactive role expressed by a bare dative NP rather than a
*for*-phrase (Modern Dutch/German retain only this non-reanalyzed benefactive type, never developing
a complementizer *for*/*für*/*voor*). The reanalysis is argued to depend crucially on the Chapter 5
fixing of VO word order: in Old English's freer word order, the benefactive phrase and the infinitive
were not reliably adjacent (especially in subordinate clauses, where Verb-Second placed the matrix
predicate between them), blocking the reanalysis; only once Middle English fixed SVO/adjacent surface
order did the dative-NP-then-infinitive sequence become systematically reinterpretable as
subject-then-predicate (pp. 211–220).

### The AcI/ECM construction's two distinct types (7.2.2)

Establishes a crucial distinction blurred in earlier literature (Zeitlin 1908, Visser): the **AcI
proper** (after physical-perception and causative verbs, e.g. *I saw him leave*) is native to Old
English, while the **ECM construction** (after "believe"-type verbs, e.g. *I believe him to be
innocent*) is NOT attested in native Old English prose at all — apparent OE examples are isolated,
confined to glosses/translations directly calqued on Latin, and the ordinary native strategy for
believe-type verbs remained a finite *þæt*-clause throughout the Old English period. The two
constructions differ systematically even where superficially similar: AcI-proper infinitives denote
a concrete, directly perceivable/causable activity sharing the matrix verb's own tense domain and
typically co-occur with a locative adjunct, while the believe-type ECM infinitive denotes an
independent, non-simultaneous state of affairs — a semantic distinction with no equivalent
syntactic reflex in Old English because the ECM structure itself didn't yet exist natively (pp.
220–224).

### The rise of the ECM (believe-type) construction in Middle English (7.2.2 cont'd)

Driven by a convergence of internal, analogical, and external (borrowing) factors rather than any
single cause: the fixing of VO word order is argued to be the necessary enabling precondition (as
with the parallel *for NP to V* case), since it is what made the AcI-proper's post-verbal NP+infinitive
sequence available as a template for analogical extension to believe-type verbs; **borrowing from
Latin** (the *verba declarandi et cogitandi* — "verbs of declaring and thinking" — construction) then
supplied the specific lexical class and the *to be* copular-infinitive pattern that Old English AcI-
proper never used; Warner's (1982) "least saliency"/"minimal alteration" principle, verified against
the two Wycliffite Bible translations (a very literal Early Version vs. a more idiomatic Late
Version), is cited as the empirical generalization governing *where* a borrowed construction first
takes root: contexts already structurally similar to an existing native pattern (topicalized/
wh-moved/passivized accusative NPs, which minimize surface disruption) are adopted first. The passive
infinitive is independently shown to play an outsized role in the spread of the English ECM
construction, confirmed by corpus counts in Appendix C (pp. 224–241).

### Comparative evidence from the history of Dutch (7.3)

A deliberately controlled comparison: Dutch had comparable exposure to Latin cultural/religious
influence (especially during the Renaissance) and did develop its own Latinate AcI constructions in
Middle and 17th-century Dutch texts, yet **never fixed VO word order the way English did** (Dutch
retains SOV in subordinate clauses and V2 in main clauses to this day) — and critically, the Latinate
Dutch AcI never became a permanent, native-grammar fixture; it remained confined to Latin-imitating
literary registers and was dropped once that register fell out of fashion, unlike in English where it
became fully productive. This is presented as the chapter's strongest evidence that **borrowing and
analogy alone are insufficient causes of syntactic change** — both factors were equally present in
Dutch, yet the change only "took" in English, where the independent word-order fixing had already
prepared the structural ground (the chapter's recurring "bed had been prepared" metaphor). A secondary
finding: object-control-verb AcIs (very common in Middle English, e.g. after *command*) were never
found at all in the Dutch data, a cross-linguistic gap the authors leave unexplained (pp. 241–247).

### Chapter 8 — Theoretical profile of 'easy-to-please'/tough-movement in Modern English (8.2)

The modern construction (*John is easy to convince*; *the problem was tough to deal with*; *he is
hard to get a straight answer from*) is analyzed in the generative literature as **A-bar (wh-)
movement**: the adjective (*easy*-class) takes a CP complement headed by a null operator (OP) that
has wh-moved from the gap position, coindexed with the matrix subject, which supplies the operator's
reference; the embedded clause's own subject is arbitrary PRO. Four empirical arguments favor the
wh-movement analysis over an NP-movement (A-movement) alternative: (i) the construction tolerates all
three configurational types of gap (direct object, object-of-adjacent-preposition, object-of-
non-adjacent-preposition) uniformly, whereas NP-movement's Case-theoretic restrictions would predict
exactly the split actually *not* observed; (ii) it licenses "long" movement into a more deeply
embedded clause, characteristic of wh-movement; (iii) it licenses parasitic gaps, a wh-movement-
specific diagnostic; (iv) the matrix subject position can alternatively host expletive *it* (*it was
easy to convince John*), raising a separate, unresolved theoretical question about whether the
easy-class adjective actually theta-marks its own subject position at all — contrasted with *seem*-
type raising predicates, which demonstrably do not (pp. 256–261).

### Old English 'easy-to-please': a single, narrowly restricted pattern (8.3)

Based on an exhaustive OE microfiche-concordance search (van der Wurff 1992a) of all adjective +
non-subject-gap-infinitive combinations, 46 of 286 total OE tokens instantiate the 'easy-to-please'
sense (the *earfoð/eaðe/hefig/leoht*-class adjectives above), and **every single one** shows the gap
functioning as the infinitive's direct object with accusative-case assignment — not one genuine
instance of preposition-stranding is attested with an easy-class adjective in Old English, in sharp
contrast to Modern English's (3). Two apparent counterexamples are carefully reanalyzed and rejected:
in both cases the "stranded preposition" turns out to be either an independently-stranded locative
pronoun (*þær*, per the Chapter 2 P-stranding pattern) unconnected to the easy-adjective construction,
or the verb's own object governed directly by a transitive OE particle-verb (*onbeyrnan*), not a
stranded preposition at all. Cross-checked against modern-corpus baseline rates (Mair 1987; Brown/LOB
corpora: ~6–9% of modern 'easy-to-please' tokens show P-stranding) — if OE had the option at even a
comparable rate, 4–6 stranded examples would statistically be expected among the 46 found; finding
zero is treated as positive evidence the option was simply ungrammatical in Old English, not an
accident of textual survival. Two further, formally and semantically distinct adjective classes are
identified alongside the easy-class: the **eager-class** (*fus*, *gearo*, *geornful*), which only
ever has a null embedded subject controlled by the matrix subject (never an empty object gap, never
a dummy *hit* subject); and the **pretty-class** (words meaning 'pleasant'/'beautiful'), which
superficially resembles easy-class sentences but is shown to differ semantically (the adjective
genuinely theta-marks/describes the subject referent itself, rather than describing an action) and
to exclude both the 'it'-type and 'zero'-type variants that the easy-class freely allows (pp.
261–267).

### Old English 'easy-to-please' as underlyingly a passive-infinitive construction (8.4)

The chapter's central historical-syntactic proposal (continued past the directly-read page range into
pp. 268ff., building on the structural uniformity just established): since every OE token shows the
gap as a direct-object-only, accusative-assigning dependency with no preposition-stranding option
whatsoever, the authors (following van der Wurff 1987, 1990a) analyze Old English 'easy-to-please' not
as wh-movement at all but as containing a **covertly passive infinitive** — the embedded clause has
no syntactic gap/movement chain in OE; instead the matrix subject is base-generated as the logical
object of a passive (not active) infinitive, directly parallel to the already-established OE passive
infinitive patterns catalogued in Chapter 7 (after modals, adjectives, nouns). This correctly predicts
the total absence of P-stranding (passivization of the object of a preposition was itself not yet a
general option in Old English, per Chapter 2's finding that OE P-stranding under passivization simply
didn't exist) and explains why the gap is always a plain accusative-assigning object rather than any
of the range of configurations modern wh-movement permits.

### The Middle English shift to a wh-movement analysis (8.5)

Tracked as a genuinely *qualitative*, dateable reanalysis rather than a gradual drift: examples of
'easy-to-please' with genuine preposition-stranding (the modern type, impossible to derive from a
passive-infinitive structure) are first attested only in texts written down around **1400** — the
same approximate watershed date independently identified in Chapter 5 for the qualitative narrowing
of OV word order and in Chapter 7 for several infinitival-construction changes, reinforcing the
authors' broader claim that a cluster of early-15th-century syntactic changes shares a common
underlying trigger in the completion of the shift to fixed VO/SVO order. Once the passive-infinitive
analysis of the construction was no longer learnable from the input (because the independent syntactic
prerequisites for a covert passive infinitive — the freer OE word order that made the construction
's structure recoverable — had themselves disappeared), later generations of learners reanalyzed the
same surface strings via the independently available, more general mechanism of wh-/operator-movement,
which — unlike the passive-infinitive analysis — correctly predicts and licenses the full range of
gap types (including stranded prepositions) attested from this point on, converging on the modern
analysis described in 8.2.

### Chapter 9 — Grammaticalization theory: core concepts and the authors' critique (9.1–9.2)

Surveys the standard defining properties claimed for grammaticalization in the literature — **semantic
bleaching** (loss of concrete lexical meaning), **gradualness**, and **unidirectionality** (lexical
→ grammatical, never the reverse) — and the standard explanatory models offered for *why* it happens
(pragmatic inferencing/reanalysis of conversational implicature as literal meaning; metaphorical
extension; frequency-driven routinization). The authors' own position, consistent with their Chapter
1 framework, is that **grammaticalization is not itself a causal mechanism or an independent
diachronic "force"** — it is a descriptive label for a recurring *pattern* of outcomes, and the two
detailed case studies that follow are offered specifically to demonstrate that in both cases, **a
discrete syntactic change (the independent fixing of VO/SVO word order) is what actually triggers
each grammaticalization episode**, with semantic bleaching accompanying rather than driving the
syntactic reanalysis (pp. 284–292).

### Case study 1: the grammaticalization of *have to* (9.3.1)

A four-stage diachronic path is reconstructed (synthesizing van der Gaaf 1931 and Brinton 1991, with
the authors' own corrected analysis): **Stage (i), Old English** — *habban* is a full lexical
possession verb taking an NP direct object, which happens to be modified by an adjunct
*to*-infinitive (*I have a long story to tell [you]* — i.e., "a story, [one] to tell"); subject
identity between *have*'s subject and the infinitival "subject" is not required and the infinitive is
not obligatory, this is a free syntactic combination, not yet a unit. **Stage (ii), also present
already in Old English** (contra earlier literature's claim this emerges only later) — the same
surface string develops a **predicative** reading in which the infinitive is reanalyzed as an object
complement rather than a free adjunct, the NP object is now typically "factive" or negative
(something that logically cannot be literally possessed, e.g. "a long story to tell" meaning "the
need/obligation to tell a long story"), and *have*'s meaning generalizes toward obligation while
argument structure between *have* and the infinitive becomes shared/identified rather than
independent. **Stage (iii), Middle English** — a genuine new fixed word order (*have* + *to*-
infinitive + NP, rather than the earlier *have* + NP + *to*-infinitive) begins to appear, but only
in a small subset ("A3"-type) constructions where the NP's thematic role could already be ambiguous
between being construed as *have*'s object or the infinitive's object; **intransitive infinitives**
after *have* also first appear at this stage, evidence that *have* no longer strictly requires a
concrete object NP of its own. **Stage (iv), Early Modern English** — full integration of *have* into
the modal paradigm as a true grammaticalized **operator**: fixed Aux-V(-NP) word order, full epistemic
meaning alongside obligative, and phonological contraction (*have to* > "hafta"). The authors' own
key correction to the received (Brinton/van der Gaaf) account: contrary to what those accounts imply,
the data show **no steady, monotonic increase** in the proportion of obligation-marked readings across
all stages — obligation-sense frequency in the crucial "A3" ambiguous-object construction stays roughly
flat (OE: 4/9; ME: 10/33; eModE: 25/80, i.e. no clear upward trend relative to the others), undermining
a purely gradual-semantic-bleaching account. Instead, the authors argue the trigger was **structural**:
once Middle English's independent VO-order fixing (Chapter 5) forced the object of *have* to
consistently follow the finite verb, and the ambiguous A3-type NP (thematically selected by the
infinitive, not truly by *have*) was reanalyzed by learners as the infinitive's own syntactic object
rather than *have*'s, this syntactically adjacent, reanalyzed sequence [*have* + *to*-infinitive] was
primed to be reinterpreted wholesale as a single modal-auxiliary unit — word-order fixation, not
gradual semantic drift, is identified as the actual trigger of the categorial reanalysis, with the
semantic bleaching following from (not causing) the new syntactic configuration. Supporting frequency
evidence from the Helsinki Corpus: *have* + *to*-infinitive occurrences rise from 17 (Old English,
413,000-word corpus) to 339 (early Modern English, 551,000-word corpus) — a roughly 14-fold rate
increase cited as plausibly enabling, though not by itself causing, the reanalysis (Traugott & Heine's
frequency-as-grammaticalization-prerequisite principle) (pp. 292–305).

### Case study 2: Jespersen's Cycle in the history of English negation (9.3.2)

Otto Jespersen's (1917) four-stage negative cycle (single negator → negator reinforced by a negative
adverb/NP → the new reinforcing element becomes the primary negator, original marker now optional →
original marker becomes extinct) is traced through English with unusually precise structural
correlates at each stage, using the layered clause-structure apparatus (CP > FP > NegP > TP)
established in Chapter 4. **Pre-Classical-OE (earliest poetry only: *Beowulf*, *Widsith*, *Genesis
A*, *Daniel*)**: an older pattern survives alongside the standard one, with an unreduced negative
constituent (*no*, *næfre*) in Spec,CP and, crucially, **no finite-verb movement** to C — argued to be
the chronologically oldest attested English negation pattern, on the verge of being supplanted.
**Classical Old English, stage 1**: *ne* has become a reduced, obligatorily verb-incorporating
negative head (Neg⁰), procliticized to the finite verb, which now *does* move to C in the relevant
contexts — the loss of the independent, Spec,CP-based *no*-pattern and the rise of V-to-C movement
are argued to be causally linked (verb-movement to the position adjacent to the negator is what drove
*no*'s phonological/syntactic reduction to procliticized *ne*). **Classical OE, stage 2 (multiple
sentential negation)**: a second, reinforcing negator *na* appears in Spec,NegP alongside the
now-weakened incorporating head *ne* — confirmed via careful corpus work (243 cases from major OE
prose texts) to pattern as genuine sentential (not just constituent) negation, and shown to permit
topic-initial word order far more readily than single-*ne* negation does, a word-order asymmetry the
NegP-based structural account explains directly. **Middle English, stage 3**: the reinforcing negator
shifts from *na/no* to the OE emphatic negative-noun/adverb *nawiht/nowiht*, which weakens to *not* and
rapidly increases in frequency while *ne* itself continues eroding, eventually becoming fully optional
— *not*'s syntactic position tracks the *same* fixed subject-pronoun-vs-nominal-subject asymmetry
previously diagnostic of *ne*'s Spec,NegP status, confirming *not* has simply inherited the specifier
position vacated as *ne* weakens. **Middle/Early Modern English, stage 4**: once *ne* disappears
altogether, *not* itself shows clear evidence of re-analysis from specifier to **negative head**
(moving along with the finite verb under inversion/question-formation, e.g. *did not* in negative
questions) — completing the cycle exactly as Jespersen predicted, with the modern pattern of negative
contraction (*didn't*) identified as a direct further consequence of *not*'s having become an
incorporating head. The chapter's overarching conclusion, echoing the *have to* case study: **the
entire multi-century cycle is argued to be driven by a single, tightly constrained structural format**
(the layered NegP projection), with semantic bleaching of successive negators accompanying, but not
independently driving, a process whose shape at every stage is fixed by syntactic (not purely
semantic-pragmatic) factors — directly rebutting purely gradualist, semantically-driven accounts of
grammaticalization in favor of the book's recurring theme of synchronically-grounded, structurally-
constrained reanalysis (pp. 305–319).

### Concluding synthesis (9.3.3)

The chapter's — and effectively the book's — closing argument: both case studies show syntactic and
semantic change as *partially independent* processes rather than one simply driving the other in
lockstep, with the authors explicitly declining to treat grammaticalization itself as an explanatory
mechanism. They float two further speculative avenues worth noting for completeness without fully
endorsing either: Postma's (1995) idea that syntactic *displacement itself* (movement into "zero
semantics" positions) drives lexical bleaching directly; and the observation that grammaticalization's
endpoints are disproportionately functional-category heads (T, M, D), which may by the nature of
functional categories themselves invite loss of residual lexical meaning (pp. 318–319).

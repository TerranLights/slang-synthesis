## Grammar points

### Unit 103 — Still and yet; anymore/any longer/no longer

"Still" marks a continuing situation, taking the ordinary mid-sentence adverb position (cross-
referenced to Unit 102b); it also appears in negatives for a stronger, more impatient tone than plain
"not...yet" ("She still hasn't written to me" implies she should have by now). "Yet" marks whether an
expected thing has happened, used mainly in questions and negative sentences, sentence-final, and
frequently paired with the present perfect (cross-referenced to Unit 15b). "Not...anymore," "not...any
longer," and "no longer" all mark a changed/discontinued situation: "anymore"/"any longer" go at the
end of the sentence; "no longer" takes the mid-sentence adverb position instead. "No more" is
explicitly flagged as not usable this way. p. 206.

### Unit 104 — Although/though/even though; in spite of/despite

"Although" takes a full subject + verb clause and marks an unexpected contrast ("Although it rained a
lot, we enjoyed our vacation"); "in spite of"/"despite" instead take a noun, pronoun, or -ing form (not
a clause) with the same contrastive meaning ("In spite of the rain, we enjoyed our vacation"); note
"despite" alone, without "of" — a commonly confused point since "in spite of" always keeps "of." Both
prepositional forms extend with "the fact that" + clause when a full clause is needed. "Though" is a
near-synonym of "although," additionally usable sentence-finally in spoken English ("I like the garden
though"). "Even though" is a stronger form of "although." p. 208.

### Unit 105 — Even

Marks that something is unusual or surprising given what would normally be expected ("even Bill played
badly" implies Bill is normally good). Takes the ordinary mid-sentence adverb position (cross-
referenced to Unit 102b); "not even" is the negative form. Combines productively with comparatives
("even hotter," "even more surprised") and with if/when/though ("even if," "even when," "even
though," the last cross-referenced to Unit 104). p. 210.

### Unit 106 — As (time): "I watched her as she worked." As (reason): "As I was feeling tired, I went to bed."

The temporal use of "as" covers two distinct patterns: (a) two things happening at the same time or
over the same period, especially two short simultaneous actions ("I watched her as she opened the
letter"), intensifiable with "just as" (= exactly at that moment), and also covering two parallel
changes unfolding together ("As the day wore on, the weather got worse"); (b) one (typically short)
action occurring in the middle of another ongoing action ("The man slipped as he was getting off the
train") — explicitly not used when one action simply follows another in sequence (use "when" instead).
A separate, unrelated causal sense of "as" means "because" ("As I was feeling tired, I went to bed
early"). p. 212.

### Unit 107 — Like and as

"Like" is a preposition meaning "similar to/the same as/for example," taking a noun, pronoun, or -ing
complement ("It's like a palace," "like walking on ice"). "As" (+ subject + verb) instead means "in the
way that" ("Leave everything as it is") and feeds a set of fixed formulas (as you know, as we expected,
as I said, as usual). "Such as" (= for example) takes a noun without a verb, parallel in distribution
to "like" rather than clausal "as." A deeper semantic distinction closes the unit: "like" + noun marks
a mere comparison/resemblance (she looks like a princess — she isn't one), while "as" + noun states a
genuine identity/role/function, especially for jobs or actual use ("I worked as a waiter" — I really
was one; "we use the garage as a workshop"). p. 214.

### Unit 108 — As if

"As if" (or the near-synonym "as though") describes how someone/something appears, especially after
look/sound/feel/smell, and with other manner verbs (ran as if..., looked at me as if...); informal
"like" is noted as a common substitute specifically after look/sound/smell. After "as if"/"as though,"
an unreal/hypothetical-style past tense is used even for present reference, when the described
situation isn't actually true ("He talks as if he knew everything" — he doesn't) — the same irrealis-
past mechanism already covered for if-sentences (Units 34–36) and wish (Units 35–36), including the
use of "were" (not "was") for all persons in this unreal-past reading. p. 216.

### Unit 109 — At/on/in (time)

"At" marks a point in time (at 5 o'clock, at midnight) and a closed set of fixed time idioms (at night,
at Christmas/Easter, at the moment/at present, at the same time, at the age of, at the beginning/end
of). "On" marks dates, days, and parts of specific named days (on March 12th, on Friday morning(s), on
weekends). "In" marks longer periods — months, years, seasons, centuries, decades — and parts of the
day taken generally (in the morning, but "on Friday morning" once a specific day is named). "Last" and
"next" take no preposition at all. "In" + a period of time additionally means either "that far in the
future from now" or "it took/will take that long to happen." p. 218.

### Unit 110 — For, during, and while

"For" + a period of time states duration (how long); "during" is explicitly not substitutable for this
sense. "During" + a noun instead states when something happens within a period, without claiming
anything about duration (during the movie, during our vacation, during the night). "While" covers the
same "when within a period" meaning as "during" but takes a full subject + verb clause instead of a
noun; future reference after "while" uses present tense, not "will" (parallel to the Unit 9 rule).
p. 220.

### Unit 111 — By and until; by the time...

"By" + a time means "not later than" (on or before that point) and explicitly cannot be swapped with
"until," which instead marks how long a situation continues up to a point ("Sue will be away until
Monday" [she returns that day] vs. "Sue will be back by Monday" [she returns on or before that day]).
The fixed construction "by the time (something happens/happened)" marks that one thing will be/was
already finished by the point a second thing occurs/occurred, for both future and past reference; "by
then"/"by that time" serve as anaphoric substitutes. p. 222.

### Units 112–113 — In/at/on (position), parts 1 and 2

"In" marks position within an enclosed or bounded space: rooms/buildings, gardens/parks, towns/
countries, bodies of water, rows/lines, and — specifically flagged as an exception to the chair/armchair
pattern — armchairs (but "on" a plain chair); also "in" a photograph/picture/mirror and "in" the sky.
"In front of"/"in back of" (= behind) form a positional pair; "in the front/in the back" covers 3D
interior position within a vehicle, room, theater, or crowd, contrasted against "on the front/on the
back" for the two sides of a flat surface (paper, photograph, envelope). "At" marks presence at an
event (at a party, at a concert, at a football game) and governs a closed cluster of fixed place-idioms
with no single rule (at work, at a station, at an airport, at the seashore — contrasted against at sea,
in bed, in prison/jail, in the hospital, on a farm). "In" or "at" are both available for
institutions-as-life-stage (in/at college or school) and for buildings one stays in or eats at (hotels,
restaurants), with "at" preferred when naming where an event took place and "in" preferred when
thinking of the interior itself; "at someone's house" is a fixed idiom. "In" is the default for towns/
villages, with "at" reserved for a town treated as a mere stop on a journey. Arrival verbs split three
ways: "arrive in" (countries/towns), "arrive at" (buildings/events), "arrive home" (no preposition at
all). pp. 224–228.

### Unit 114 — To, been to, into; by car/in my car

Motion verbs (go/come/travel/fly/walk/drive/etc.) take directional "to" toward a place or event; "get
to" is the general-purpose arrival verb, distinguished from "arrive in/at" (Unit 113f); "go/come/get
home" takes no preposition at all. Present-perfect "been to" (a place) marks a completed round trip —
visited and already returned, cross-referenced to the Unit 13d gone-to/been-to contrast. "Into" marks
entering an enclosed space, including forceful or sudden entry (ran into a store, flew into the room
through the window). Mode of transport uses "by" (by car, by train, by plane, by bicycle, etc.), with
the fixed exception "on foot" (never "by foot"); once a specific vehicle is named (not just the bare
mode), "by" is replaced: "in" for cars/taxis (in my car, in a taxi — not "by my car"), "on" for
bicycles and public transportation (on my bicycle, on the bus, on the 6:45 train); correspondingly,
"get in(to)/out of" pairs with cars/taxis and "get on/off" pairs with bicycles/public transport. p. 230.

## Grammar points

### Subject vs. object pronoun case (Ch.10)

The subject performs the action or is the thing in the state of being; the object receives the
action. Some pronouns (*you, it, either, most, other, which, that*) serve as both without changing
form. Reflexive/*-self* pronouns are correct only when the action circles back to the subject
(*"I told myself..."*) or for pure emphasis (*"She herself baked..."*) — never as a plain substitute
for a subject or object pronoun in a compound. (p.127)

### Who vs. whom (Ch.10)

Functions exactly like any other subject/object pronoun pair — *who* for the doer, *whom* for the
receiver — the difficulty is purely in untangling which role the pronoun plays inside a complicated
sentence, not a separate rule. (p.129)

### Pronouns after linking verbs ("to be" sentences) (Ch.10)

Because a linking verb (a form of "to be") acts as a reversible equals sign between two equivalent
terms, the pronoun following it must be subject-case to preserve that reversibility — *"It is she"*
(reversible to *"She is it"*), not *"It is her"* (not reversibly sayable as *"Her is it"*). Sometimes
the verb itself must shift number to make the reversed sentence sound right (*"It is they"* reverses
to *"They are it,"* requiring *are*, not *"they is"*). (p.130)

### Pronouns as objects of prepositions (Ch.10)

Any pronoun serving as the object of a preposition (*by, for, from, in, on, of, about, after,
before*, etc.) must be in object case — *"Give that to me,"* never *"to I."* (p.131)

### Possessive pronouns before gerunds (Ch.10)

A pronoun or noun immediately preceding a gerund (an *-ing* noun made from a verb) should be in
possessive form, because the point of the sentence is usually the *action*, not the person — *"Carrie
hates my auditioning..."* (the auditioning is the problem, not "me"), and likewise *"Rick's
auditioning,"* not *"Rick auditioning."* (p.132)

### Possessive agreement with singular indefinite pronouns (Ch.11)

Singular indefinite pronouns (*everybody, someone, anybody*, etc.) require a singular possessive —
the book's prescribed fix for the common mismatch *"Everyone needs their lunch pass"* is *"Everyone
needs his or her lunch pass."* Plural indefinites (*both, several, few, many*) correctly take *their*.
(pp.141–142; see the Vocabulary table's note on *his or her* for this file's own flag that usage has
shifted since 2006 toward broader acceptance of singular *they* in this exact context)

### Pronouns for companies and organizations (Ch.11)

A company/organization name is referred to with a singular pronoun regardless of whether the name
itself sounds plural (*Sears*) or contains an ampersand/"and" (*Sears Roebuck & Co.*) — it is one
entity. *"Saks has put its designer lingerie on sale,"* not *"their."* (p.143)

### Who, which, that take their number/gender from their antecedent (Ch.11)

Unlike most pronouns, *who/which/that* have no fixed number or gender — they inherit it entirely
from whatever noun they replace in a given sentence, which in turn determines whether a singular or
plural verb must follow. The diagnostic is to identify exactly what the pronoun stands for: *"She is
one of the few quarterbacks who **are** ready"* (who = quarterbacks, plural) vs. *"She is the only
quarterback who **is** negotiating"* (who = she, singular). (pp.144–146)

### Avoiding improper pronoun references (Ch.11)

A pronoun may replace one and only one specific noun in formal English — it cannot stand in for an
entire preceding clause or sentence idea (a common misuse of *that, which, this*: *"Jeffrey handed in
a late report, **which** annoyed his boss"* improperly makes *which* refer to the whole situation
rather than to *report*). Nor may a pronoun (commonly *one*) be used without a specific matching noun
already present in the sentence to replace. The fix is either to supply/relocate the matching noun or
to eliminate the pronoun entirely and rephrase. (pp.146–149)

### Tense in reported/summarized speech (Ch.12)

When retelling what someone said, the retelling defaults to past tense throughout, and all verbs
should stay consistently in past tense unless a valid reason requires a shift — switching tense
mid-retelling with no justification (*"He called. Then he **says**..."*) is flagged as a common
error. (pp.155–156)

### Present tense for unchanging/eternal facts, even inside past-tense reported speech (Ch.12)

An exception to the above: a statement of permanent, unchanging fact (*"the earth is a planet"*)
stays in present tense even when embedded inside an otherwise-past-tense summary of what someone
said (*"Marty told me that the earth **is** a planet,"* not *"was"*). (p.157)

### Verbal tense (gerunds, infinitives, participles) (Ch.12)

Verbals carry their own simplified time relationship to the sentence's main verb: the plain form
(no *has/have/had/having*) indicates the verbal's action happens simultaneously with the main verb;
the perfect form (with *has/have/had/having*) places the verbal's action *before* the main verb's
action. The choice depends on whether the sequencing actually matters to the reader. (pp.158–159)

### Indicative, imperative, and subjunctive mood (Ch.13)

Indicative mood (ordinary statements of fact) covers nearly all English sentences and inflects
normally for tense and person. Imperative mood (commands) uses the bare infinitive minus "to,"
identically regardless of audience size or formality, with negative commands formed by adding "do
not." Subjunctive mood is comparatively rare in English (contrasted with its much heavier use in some
other languages, per the author's own note about studying Spanish) and appears in only two contexts:
condition-contrary-to-fact statements, which pair a subjunctive *were* (not *was*) or *had* with a
*would*-form verb in the untrue part of the sentence (*"If I **were** famous, I **would** wear..."*);
and indirect commands, formed by dropping "to" from the infinitive (*"The bouncer requested that he
**remove** himself..."*, not *"removes"*). (pp.167–171)

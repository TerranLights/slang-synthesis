## Grammar points

### 11.1 The two uses of modal verbs

**11.1A The first use of modal verbs (1).** The ten modals (*can, could, may, might, will, would,
shall, should, must, ought to*) plus three semi-modals (*need, dare, used to*) combine with other
verbs. In their first use they carry the dictionary-listed basic meaning associated with each — see
the Vocabulary table for the full can/could=ability, may/might=permission, will/would=prediction,
shall(after I/we)=prediction, should/ought to=duty, must=total obligation, needn't=no obligation set,
p.156.

**11.1B The first use of modal verbs (2).** Modals are not "complete verbs" — they lack tenses/forms
a full verb has, so other constructions supply their "missing parts": *have to* supplies *must*'s
past, *be able to* supplies *can*'s future and other missing forms. Modals can't be used as
to-infinitives ("I want to be able to type," not "*to can*"), can't be followed by a to-infinitive
themselves ("You must/mustn't phone," not "*to phone*"), and take no third-person -(e)s, p.156.

**11.1C The second use of modal verbs.** The second way modals are used is to express degrees of
certainty/uncertainty, using nine of the ten modals (not *shall*) without any fixed ordering — see
the Vocabulary table for the might(very uncertain) → could/would(fairly certain) → must/can't(almost
certain) scale. Absolute certainty instead uses *be* or an ordinary verb, not a modal. In this second
use, modals have only two forms: a present form and a perfect/past form (*must be* vs. *must have
been*), p.157.

### 11.2 Uses of modals (etc.) to express ability and inability

**11.2A Expressing present and past ability: 'can' and 'be able to'.** *Can*/*am/is/are able to*
describe natural or learned present ability; *could*/*was/were (not) able to* describe general past
ability. For the *successful completion of one specific action*, only *was/were able to* or *managed
to* work (never *could*); but *couldn't* (or *weren't able to*/*didn't manage to*) can describe a
specific action that was *not* successfully completed, p.158.

**11.2B 'Can/could' with verbs of perception: 'I can see'.** Verbs of perception (*see, hear, smell*,
etc.) don't take the progressive to describe something happening now; *can* + verb substitutes for
the simple present, and *could* + verb substitutes for the simple past, with these verbs, p.158.

**11.2C Ability in tenses other than the present and the past.** Since *can/could* aren't complete
verbs, *be able to* (and sometimes *manage to*) supplies the future and present-perfect forms: "I'll
be able to pass my driving test," not "*I can/will can*," p.159.

**11.2D 'Can/could' in place of 'is often' and 'was often'.** *Can be*/*could be* can substitute for
habitual *is often*/*was often* statements, p.159.

### 11.3 Uses of modals (etc.) to express permission and prohibition

**11.3A Asking for permission with 'can', 'could', 'may' and 'might'.** A politeness gradient: *can*
(commonest, most informal) < *could* (more polite) < *may* (more 'respectful') < *might* (most polite,
least common). *Possibly*, and framing constructions like *Do you think...* or *I wonder if...*, can
be added to any of these for extra politeness, p.160.

**11.3B Giving and refusing permission/Expressing prohibition.** Personally granting/refusing
permission uses *can(not)*/*may(not)* — never *could* or *might* in this use. Referring instead to
"some other authority" uses *can/cannot* or *you're (not) allowed to*, *you're (not) permitted to*, or
(for prohibition) *you mustn't*/*you're forbidden to*, p.160.

**11.3C Permission/prohibition in tenses other than present and future.** Since *may* and *must*
aren't complete verbs, *be allowed to* supplies their missing tenses: "The children were allowed to
watch TV last night," not "*could*," p.161.

**11.3D 'Can' (= ability) and 'can/could' (= have permission, be free to).** Two distinct senses of
the same forms: *can/could* meaning "am/is/are free to" can refer to the future ("Mr Jones can/could
see you tomorrow, if you are free"), but *can/could* meaning ability cannot — future ability requires
*will be able to* ("Baby will be able to stand up in two weeks' time," not "*can/could*"), p.161.

### 11.4 Uses of modals (etc.) to express certainty and possibility

**11.4A Certainty and possibility.** Certainty uses *be* or any full verb ("Jane is at home. Jane
works at home."). Possibility uses *may/might/could* + *be*/*have been*, or *may/might/could* + full
verb (including progressive and perfect forms: *may/might/could be working*, *may/might/could have
worked*), p.162.

**11.4B Certain and uncertain answers to questions.** A "certain" question can get an "uncertain"
answer and vice versa. *Be* and *have been* answer *be*-questions directly ("Is he ill? — He may be.
Was he ill? — He may have been," never "*he may*"). *Do*/*done* can optionally replace a repeated
full verb in the answer ("Will you catch the early train? — I may./I may do."); the same
certainty/uncertainty distinction extends to Wh-question answers, p.163.

### 11.5 Uses of modals to express deduction

**11.5A Certainty or deduction?** Certainty uses *be*/any full verb directly; deduction uses *must
be*/*can't be* (present) or, with verbs other than *be*, *must* + verb ("He must live abroad. He must
have lived abroad."), p.164.

**11.5B Two kinds of 'must be'.** *Must be* has two distinct uses that share a negative-form trap:
(1) present-tense deduction, whose negative is *can't be* (never "*mustn't be*"); (2) total obligation
[>11.1A], whose negative *is* *mustn't (be)* (= prohibition). The two uses are distinguished by
context/sense, not by form, p.164.

**11.5C 'Must have been', 'can't/couldn't have been'; 'had to be/didn't have to be'.** Deduction about
the past uses *must have been* (negative: *can't/couldn't have been*, never "*mustn't have been*").
*Must* (= total obligation) isn't a complete verb, so past obligation uses *had to (be)* instead (not
*must have been*, which would read as deduction) — negative *didn't have to (be)*, p.165.

### 11.6 Uses of modals for offers, requests and suggestions

A set of six parallel functional patterns, each with its own fixed response conventions — see the
Vocabulary table for each construction: offering things/substances (*Would/Wouldn't you like...?*),
requesting things/substances (*Can/Could/May/Might I have...?*), making suggestions/inviting actions
(*Would/Wouldn't you like to...?*), requesting others to do things (*Will/Would you please...?*),
offering to do things for others (*Shall I...?*), and making suggestions that include the speaker
(*Shall we...?*, answered "Yes, let's"), pp.166–167.

(6) a **question-tag system** (subject-pronoun substitution, polarity inversion, the suppletive

pronoun/determiner shift, that-omission, no-backshift-for-still-true-statements, indirect commands
with to-infinitive, indirect yes/no questions with if/whether) — a topic neither CMOS nor Book 1
covers at all; (8) a full **five-column irregular-verb paradigm table** (~75 verbs: base/3rd-person
singular/present participle/simple past/past participle), more complete than Book 1's partial lists;
(9) a demonym-formation pattern (country→"the + nationality-plural" vs. plain nationality+s) and a
young-animal-name vocabulary set not duplicated in Book 1.

## Grammar points

### Pluralization, collective nouns, and the possessive — expanded versions of Book 1's rule set

Book 2 presents the same core pluralization spelling paradigm as Book 1 (default +s; sibilant +es;
y→ies vs. vowel+y→s; f/fe→ves vs. +s vs. dual-accepted; o→s vs. o→es; irregular; invariant;
pluralia-tantum), so that content is **not re-tabulated here** (see `067_..._pt01` for the full
rule set, which this dispatch's density judgment treats as already covered). Genuinely new in Book
2's version: an explicit note that collective nouns *people* and *the police* **always** take a
plural verb despite not ending in -s (echoing but slightly extending CMOS §5.8/Book 1's note);
confirmation that the double-possessive construction ("John's brother's ball") is grammatical; and
the explicit two-way-acceptable possessive for a name already ending in -s (*James's* or *James'*,
*Charles's* or *Charles'*) — a genuine usage split CMOS/Book 1 didn't flag this explicitly.

### Articles: a vs. an with pronunciation-based (not spelling-based) exceptions

*A* is used before consonant-initial nouns and *an* before vowel-initial nouns, **except** that *a*
is used before *u*-initial words pronounced with a leading "you" sound (*a uniform, a unit, a user*)
and *an* is used before *h*-initial words with a silent *h* (*an hour, an heir, an honor*) — the rule
tracks pronunciation, not spelling. (p. 11)

### Personal, reflexive, possessive, demonstrative, interrogative, and indefinite pronoun paradigms

Full subject/object personal-pronoun paradigm by person and number; a complete singular/plural
reflexive-pronoun table keyed to each personal pronoun (*myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself,
ourselves, yourselves, themselves*); possessive pronouns (*mine, yours, his, hers, ours, theirs*)
explicitly distinguished from possessive determiners (*my, your*, etc. — covered in the Determiners
chapter); demonstrative pronouns (*this/these* = near, *that/those* = far) doubling as demonstrative
determiners; interrogative pronouns *who/whose/what/which/whom*, with an explicit usage note that
*whom* is correct (in formal register) as the object of a verb or preposition but never as a
sentence's subject, and that *who* is broadly replacing *whom* even as an object in less formal
registers — a genuine ongoing case-leveling change worth flagging for mechanics analysis. (pp.
24–29)

### Indirect/reported speech: a complete transformation system (not covered by CMOS or Book 1)

This is Book 2's single most structurally rich grammar-point cluster, and the only major topic in
either book with essentially zero overlap against CMOS/Oxford. Reporting someone's exact words
(direct speech, in quotation marks) as indirect speech (introduced by *say/ask/tell* + optional
*that*) requires three coordinated shifts: **tense backshift** (present→past, present perfect→past
perfect, *can→could*, *will→would*, etc.); **pronoun/determiner shift** (1st/2nd person forms shift
to match the new speaker's perspective, e.g. "I feel ill" → "she felt ill"); and dropping quotation
marks. Two explicit exceptions to backshift: (1) reporting a statement that is *still true now*
keeps the original tense ("My mom doesn't like fish" → "John said that his mom doesn't like fish");
(2) the subordinating *that* is frequently omitted in casual indirect speech. **Indirect commands**
use *order/tell/warn* + object + *to*-infinitive (or *not to* + infinitive for negative commands),
replacing the imperative mood entirely. **Indirect questions** use *ask* + the question content with
subject-before-verb word order restored (undoing the subject-auxiliary inversion required in the
original direct question); yes/no questions are introduced by *if* or *whether*; *wh*-question
reports keep the original question word. (pp. 134–139)

### Question tags: a complete polarity-and-pronoun-substitution system

A question tag is a short tag question appended to a statement to invite agreement, formed from the
statement's own helping verb plus a pronoun that stands in for its subject. The system has two
governing rules: **polarity inversion** (a positive main clause takes a negative tag, e.g. "isn't
it?", expecting a *yes* answer; a negative main clause takes a positive tag, e.g. "aren't they?",
expecting a *no* answer) and **subject-to-pronoun substitution** (a full noun-phrase subject is
replaced by the matching pronoun in the tag — "Tom and Peter should leave now, shouldn't *they*?").
The one irregular form in the system is *aren't I* (not the expected but unused "amn't I") after a
first-person-singular positive main clause. Existential *there* sentences form tags with *there*
itself rather than a pronoun ("There aren't many clouds in the sky, are *there*?"). (p. 130)

### Conditional sentences: present-tense if-clause + shall/will main clause

The book's only conditional-sentence pattern taught at this level: the *if*-clause uses a simple
present-tense verb even though it refers to future time, while the main clause uses *shall*/*will* +
a bare infinitive; the clause order is freely reversible (if-clause first or main clause first, with
a comma only when the if-clause comes first). (p. 124) This is the "zero/first conditional" pattern
in more advanced grammar terminology, though the book itself does not use that numbered-conditional
terminology.

### Sentence-constituent structure: transitive/intransitive, direct/indirect objects

A transitive verb takes a direct object (the thing receiving the verb's action); some transitive
verbs (*give, show, teach, ask, write, send, tell*, etc.) additionally take an indirect object (the
recipient of the direct object) positioned between the verb and the direct object ("Sam gave **Anna**
a present" — Anna = indirect object, a present = direct object). An intransitive verb takes no
object at all. (pp. 119–120) Four sentence types by communicative function (declarative,
interrogative, exclamatory, imperative) and two sentence types by clause count (simple = one clause;
compound = two clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction, with one verb on each side) are
distinguished, plus positive vs. negative sentences (marked by *not*, a contracted negative, or
another negative word like *nothing/never/nobody/no*). (pp. 118, 123–127)

### Auxiliary verbs: a semantic-function map, not just a form paradigm

Beyond the person/number paradigm tables (shared territory with Book 1, not re-tabulated here), Book
2 maps each modal auxiliary onto specific pragmatic functions: *can/could* = ability, and informally
also permission-asking/offering/suggesting; *will/would* = asking someone to do something, offering,
or suggesting; *shall/should* = asking for advice, offering, or suggesting (*should* specifically =
obligation); *ought to* = strong suggestion/duty (functionally close to *should*); *must* = strong
necessity, keeps the same form in the past tense (a genuine paradigm gap — there is no past-tense
*must*, and *had to* fills the semantic gap instead, though the book doesn't name this explicitly);
*may/might* = permission-granting/asking, and also possibility/likelihood (*might* doubles as the
past tense of *may*). (pp. 88–91)

### Punctuation: comma/apostrophe/quotation-mark placement rules, capital-letter triggers

A comma is used between list items, after *yes*/*no* in a reply, before/after a name being directly
addressed, before *please*/*thank you*, and between the parts of a place name (*"Chicago, Illinois"*).
A period or question mark/exclamation point is placed **before** the closing quotation mark when it
belongs to the quoted material, but after it if the quoted material is embedded earlier in a larger
sentence. A colon separates a speaker's name from their line in playscript-formatted dialogue. Capital
letters are triggered by: sentence-initial position; the first word of direct speech; the pronoun
*I*; all proper nouns (people, places, days, months, holidays, titles of creative works, initials,
nationalities/languages); and conventional greeting/farewell phrases (*Hi., Good morning., Bye.*).
(pp. 140–146)

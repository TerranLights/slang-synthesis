## Grammar points

### Musical settings and verse metre as indirect pronunciation evidence

Beyond rhyme (already introduced for Chaucer in pt06's Section 7.3.3), the book introduces two further
indirect sources for reconstructing 17th-century pronunciation. **Musical settings** of period poems
(e.g. Orlando Gibbons' 1612 setting of Sir Walter Raleigh's *What Is Our Life?*) can reveal **secondary
stress**: Gibbons' setting of *passion* and *division* across four separate musical notes each implies
a pronunciation with an audible secondary stress on the final syllable (/ˈpæʃən/, /dɪˈvɪʒən/) alongside
primary stress, a secondary-stress pattern since lost from the modern pronunciation of many similar
words — a loss the book flags as one of the real differences between 16th/17th-century and present-day
pronunciation generally. **Verse metre** itself (specifically Shakespeare's, Jonson's, and Milton's
standard iambic pentameter — ten syllables, alternating unstressed/stressed) can similarly reveal
**which syllable count** a word had at the time: a line that only scans correctly if a word like
*proportion* is read as four syllables (*Pro-por-ti-on*) rather than three gives direct evidence that
the modern three-syllable pronunciation had not yet developed. (pp.144, Sections 10.1.2–10.1.3)

### Sir Thomas Browne's learned prose vocabulary as a window on period "common knowledge"

Sir Thomas Browne's *Religio Medici* (1642) and *Pseudodoxia Epidemica* ("*Vulgar Errors*," discussing
and often debunking commonly-held beliefs of the day) are valued by the book less for grammar than for
showing how a highly educated writer actively **exploits and expands the resources** of his language —
heavy, deliberate use of Latinate vocabulary (continuing the "inkhorn" controversy already covered for
the 16th century in pt08) mixed with plainer native vocabulary, and literal acceptance of some period
beliefs (e.g. uncritical acceptance of the biblical story of Jonah as a minor supporting fact in a
discussion of sperm whales) that a modern reader would treat as "quaint" rather than rigorously
scientific, even though Browne also exemplifies the period's emerging rational/antiquarian
cross-examination of "received" popular beliefs. (pp.145–147, Sections 10.2.1–10.2.2)

### Dictated vs. self-composed prose as a controlled style contrast

The book places George Fox's dictated, artless journal narrative directly alongside Sir Thomas
Browne's self-composed, rhetorically elaborate prose as a deliberate contrast case (Activity 10.7
explicitly asks the reader to compare the two), using the same "different register, same period"
logic already used for Trevisa vs. the Peterborough Chronicle continuation and for Chaucer vs. Usk
in earlier chapters: **mode of composition** (dictated/spoken-based vs. deliberately written and
revised) is treated throughout the book as an independent variable from period and dialect, one that
systematically affects how close any given text's language sits to genuine everyday spoken usage.
(p.151, Activity 10.7)

### Christopher Cooper's 1687 vowel-quantity/quality tables as Great Vowel Shift evidence

Christopher Cooper's *The English Teacher* (1687) is introduced as a particularly valuable orthoepist
source because, unlike John Hart (pt08), Cooper made no spelling-reform proposal — he simply aimed to
describe period pronunciation using the existing spelling system, giving the book a relatively
unbiased contemporary description. Cooper's analysis confirms the Great Vowel Shift (see pt08, Section
9.5) was **still not fully complete** even by the 1680s in parts of England — OE/ME maintained a clear
quantity distinction (short vs. long) between vowels of the *same quality*, but by Cooper's time this
had become complicated because the Shift had progressed only in the South of England, not the North;
the educated London/Home-Counties speech that was becoming the emerging standard was itself affected,
meaning identical vowel letters by this point could represent genuinely different sounds depending on
word and dialect. Cooper's book supplies several distinct worked tables, each individually valuable:
(1) a three-way letter ⟨a⟩ table ("a short," "a long," "a slender" — roughly IPA /a/, /aː/, /æː~ɛː/);
(2) evidence that some ME homophone pairs (e.g. *pane*/*pain*) remained genuinely distinct in his own
speech though already merged in others' (confirming real **social/regional pronunciation variation
coexisting within a single generation**, not just diachronic change); (3) a treatment of "improper
diphthongs" — his own term for digraphs (⟨ea⟩, ⟨oa⟩, ⟨eo⟩, ⟨ie⟩) that represent only a *single* vowel
sound despite their two-letter spelling, each sorted by which of several possible vowel values it
could represent (e.g. ⟨ea⟩ sorted into *short e* /ɛ/, *long e* /ɛː/, *long ee* /iː/, and *long a* /aː/
subgroups depending on the specific word); and (4) an explicit chapter "Of Barbarous Speaking,"
treating non-standard rural/urban pronunciation as a teaching problem to be corrected — direct
period evidence of the same prestige-dialect/"barbarous"-dialect social framing already seen in
Puttenham (pt08) persisting unchanged more than a century later. (pp.166–171, Sections 10.9–10.9.5)

### The *boil*/*bile*-type merger-then-split as a worked case study in overlapping sound changes

The book works through one specific, well-evidenced sound change in detail as a model of how two
historically separate ME vowel classes can temporarily **merge** and then **re-split** differently:
John Hart's 1560s evidence (pt08) already showed ME long /iː/ beginning to diphthongize toward /əɪ/,
a sound "almost the same" as the existing ME diphthong /ʌɪ/ found in words spelled with ⟨oi⟩ (e.g.
*boil*). Cooper's 1680s evidence confirms that by this later date, words from **both** of these
originally-separate ME sources (the diphthongized long /iː/ set, e.g. *bile*, *isle*, *line*, *mile*;
and the native ME /ʌɪ/ diphthong set spelled ⟨oi⟩, e.g. *boil*) had become genuine **homophones** —
*boil* and *bile* were pronounced identically in Cooper's time. Only **after** 1700 did the first
element of this merged diphthong shift further to its modern /aɪ/ value specifically in the
*bile*-type (etymologically long-/iː/) words, while the ⟨oi⟩-spelled set instead shifted to modern
/ɔɪ/ — meaning the modern distinction between *bile* /baɪl/ and *boil* /bɔɪl/ is a comparatively late
(post-1700) **re-split** of a pair that had genuinely merged for roughly a century and a half, not an
unbroken distinction inherited straight from ME. A second, parallel ME /ɔɪ/-spelled set (e.g.
*choice*, *noise*) is noted to have remained separate throughout and also eventually arrived at modern
/ɔɪ/, converging with the re-split *boil*-type words only at the endpoint, from a different starting
diphthong. Confirmatory period evidence for the *bile*/*boil* merger comes independently from
Shakespeare's own wordplay in *The Two Gentlemen of Verona* (Text 123, c.1590s), where the dialogue
turns on *noddy* ('fool') sounding identical to *nod* + *I* ('I nodded, yes') — not the *boil*/*bile*
case directly, but cited by the book as the same general-method confirmation that EMnE puns/wordplay
can independently corroborate orthoepist pronunciation claims. (pp.171–172, 177, Sections 10.9.7 and
10.10.3, Text 123)

### Using an individual poet's rhyme-reliability across a full poem as an evidential safeguard

Extending the Chaucer rhyme-evidence method (pt06) to John Dryden's translation of Virgil's *Aeneid*,
the book adds an important methodological refinement: because **Latin** poetic convention already
permitted a certain proportion of "false" or "eye" rhymes (pairs that look like they should rhyme in
spelling but were never meant to be pronounced alike), any individual rhyming pair found in a
translated/imitative English poem needs to be checked **in volume** across the whole translation —
a genuine sound-change-evidencing rhyme should recur consistently across many instances in the same
poet's usage, not appear as an isolated pair that might simply be an inherited literary convention
rather than live evidence of the poet's own actual pronunciation. Applying this check to Dryden's
*Aeneid*, the book finds, for example, that *enemy* rhymes with both *free* (a word whose own vowel
was itself still shifting, MnE /iː/ from ME /eː/) and with *high* (vowel shifting from ME /iː/ toward
modern /aɪ/) at different points — not a contradiction, but evidence that **multiple vowels were
simultaneously mid-shift** in Dryden's own period, each capable of landing close enough to *enemy*'s
own vowel for a satisfying rhyme at that particular transitional moment, even though *free* and *high*
do not rhyme with each other either then or now. (pp.174–176, Section 10.10.3)

### George Meriton's *A Yorkshire Dialogue* (1683) as indirect, literarily-mediated dialect evidence

Written by a practising lawyer in verse couplets and "deliberately full of proverbial sayings," the
*Dialogue* is explicitly flagged by the book as **only indirect** evidence of authentic spoken North
Riding Yorkshire English of the 1680s — its literary construction (rhyme, proverb-packing) means it
cannot be read as a transcript — but it nevertheless supplies "plenty of examples of dialectal and
traditional vocabulary and grammar" precisely because, unlike most 17th-century writing, it was
**deliberately composed to represent dialect**, so the author made a conscious effort to spell words
"as they were spoken" rather than following the increasingly fixed spelling conventions of Standard
written English — meaning its spelling-to-pronunciation mapping is unusually informative exactly
where standard-English printed spelling of the same period had already stopped tracking actual sound
change at all (a point the book makes explicitly: Standard written spelling "took no account of the
shifts of pronunciation that had taken place since the fourteenth century," so dialect writing that
deliberately broke from that convention is, paradoxically, sometimes more phonetically informative
than standard printed prose of the same date). (p.177, Section 10.11)

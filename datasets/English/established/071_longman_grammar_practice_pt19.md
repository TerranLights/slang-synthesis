## Grammar points

### 16.5 The '-ing' form

**16.5A Basic information about the '-ing' form.** The -ing form has three distinct uses: as a verb
(a participle, including in a reduced adverbial clause combining two underlying sentences), as an
adjective, and as a noun (a gerund) — which, with a few exceptions, behaves exactly like any other
noun. Ten distinct gerund-as-noun subpatterns are drilled, each illustrating a different
noun-like syntactic environment — see the Vocabulary table for the full set: uncountable in a general
statement, uncountable after quantifiers, uncountable after a preposition, uncountable after
adjectives/possessives, in fixed "No -ing" prohibition notices, countable singular/plural, after
*the/this/these*, followed by its own object, and perfect active/passive forms (*having (been) +
past participle*), pp.234–235.

### 16.6 Verb + the '-ing' form

**16.6A ''I deny taking it''.** A long list of verbs (*admit, appreciate, avoid, consider, delay, deny,
detest, dislike, enjoy, explain, fancy, feel like, finish, forgive, can't help, imagine, it involves,
keep, mention, mind, miss, pardon, postpone, practise, prevent, recall, resent, resist, risk, stop,
suggest, understand*) take only -ing, never a to-infinitive, across present and perfect/past, active
and passive forms. *Come* and *go* combine with -ing for outdoor activities (never *to*/*for*); -ing
can also follow *need* and *want* with an implied passive sense, p.236.

**16.6B ''Start him working''.** *Hear, keep, smell, start, stop, watch* take -ing directly, or take a
direct object + -ing to specify someone else as the implicit doer; a separate verb set (*catch, find,
leave, notice, observe, perceive, see*) always requires a direct object before -ing, p.236.

**16.6C ''We appreciate your helping us''.** A verb set (*appreciate, avoid, consider, defer, delay,
deny, enjoy, postpone, risk, suggest*) takes a possessive — not a plain object pronoun — before -ing:
"We appreciate your helping us," not "*we appreciate you helping*," p.237.

**16.6D ''I can't imagine my mother('s) approving''.** A further, larger verb set (*detest, dislike,
escape, excuse, fancy, forgive, hate, imagine, it involves, like, love, mention, mind, miss, pardon,
prevent, resent, resist, understand, can't bear, can't face, can't help*) accepts -ing on its own, or
with a direct object, or with a possessive — all three are valid, p.237.

### 16.7 Adjectives, nouns and prepositions + '-ing'

**16.7A Adjectives + '-ing' form (gerund) or to-infinitive.** Many adjectives and some nouns allow
either -ing or a to-infinitive with the same meaning: "Lying in the sun is pleasant." = "It's pleasant
to lie in the sun.," p.238.

**16.7B Adjective + '-ing' form (participle).** Some adjectives combine directly with a following -ing
participle, but only with a personal subject — never *it*: "I am busy working," not "*It is busy
working*," p.238.

**16.7C Common expressions followed by '-ing'.** The fixed expressions *it's no good* and *it's
(not) worth* take -ing: "It's no good complaining about it.," p.238.

**16.7D Preposition + '-ing'.** -ing (never a to-infinitive) follows a preposition, including when
the preposition is governed by an adjective or a verb: "He left the restaurant without paying," not
"*without to pay*," pp.238–239.

**16.7E The '-ing' form after 'to' as a preposition.** *To* is part of the infinitive in ordinary
to-infinitive constructions ("I want to go home"), but functions as a plain preposition in a set of
fixed phrases (*be used to, object to, be accustomed to, look forward to, resort to, be resigned to*)
— and a preposition, unlike infinitival *to*, takes a noun or -ing, never a bare verb: "I object to
noise. I object to smoking.," p.239.

### 16.8 The to-infinitive or the '-ing' form?

**16.8A Verb + to-infinitive or '-ing': no change in meaning: ''begin to read/reading''.** A verb set
(*attempt, begin, can't bear, cease, commence, continue, intend, omit, start*) allows either form
without any change in meaning. Two restrictions apply: the progressive forms of *begin, cease,
continue, start* block the -ing alternative (avoiding a double -ing), and a stative verb like *know*
or *understand* following one of these verbs likewise blocks -ing, p.240.

**16.8B Verb + to- or '-ing': some changes in meaning: ''I love to read/reading''.** A smaller verb set
(*dread, hate, like, love, prefer*) sometimes shows a meaning difference and sometimes doesn't:
generally, -ing refers to a situation "in general" while the to-infinitive refers to a specific future
event. Some fixed sub-cases allow only one form regardless (*dread to think*, never "*dread
thinking*"), p.240.

**16.8C Verb + to- or '-ing': different meanings: ''remember to post/posting''.** A final verb set
(*remember, forget, regret, try, stop, go on*) never means the same thing in the two forms: broadly,
the to-infinitive points to the present/future (or a "future seen from the past"), while -ing points
to the past. Three of these verbs have further specific, idiomatic contrasts beyond the general
present/past split — see the Vocabulary table for *try to* (make an effort) vs. *try* + -ing
(experiment); *stop to* (purpose) vs. *stop* + -ing (cease); and *go on to* (do something different)
vs. *go on* + -ing (continue), p.241.

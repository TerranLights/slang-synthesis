## Grammar points

### §5.1 Grammar defined (p. 521)

Grammar is the set of rules governing how words combine into sentences — rules that native speakers
mostly absorb unconsciously ("by osmosis"). The small residue of constructions that fall outside these
rules is treated as idiom and usage rather than grammar proper.

### §5.2 Schools of grammatical thought (p. 522)

CMOS acknowledges real disagreement among grammatical theories and their terminology, quoting
Burchfield: "An entirely adequate description of English grammar is still a distant target and at
present seemingly an unreachable one, the complications being what they are." Ironically, CMOS notes
that more exhaustive grammars (running to many volumes) tend to be *less* useful to ordinary writers
and speakers — comprehensiveness and usability trade off against each other.

### §5.3 Parts of speech (p. 523)

Grammar is traditionally organized around parts of speech — each performing a distinct syntactic
function. The traditional count is eight (nouns, pronouns, adjectives, verbs, adverbs, prepositions,
conjunctions, interjections), but CMOS flags that modern grammarians disagree sharply on the real
number — one source counts as few as three, another counts "about fifteen" and calls the precise
number still debated. CMOS commits to the traditional eight for the rest of the chapter purely as an
expository convenience, not as a claim that it's definitively correct.

### §5.4 Nouns generally (p. 524)

A noun names something, abstract or concrete, and splits at the top level into common nouns (generic
class names) and proper nouns (specific, always-capitalized names).

### §5.5 Common nouns (p. 525)

Common nouns are not capitalized unless sentence-initial or in a title. They subdivide into three
further subcategories: concrete (solid/real, sense-perceptible), abstract (not sense-perceptible), and
collective (refers to a group — can be treated as concrete but is usually categorized separately).

### §5.6 Proper nouns (p. 526)

Proper nouns are always capitalized regardless of how they're used. A common noun can *become* a
proper noun (e.g. a nickname), and conversely a proper noun can be used figuratively/informally as if
it were a common noun (CMOS's example: calling a criminal mastermind "a Napoleon of crime" — Napoleon
here connotes a general type, "ingenious mastermind," not the historical individual). Proper nouns can
also compound into a single naming unit (*the Waldorf-Astoria Hotel*). Over time, some proper nouns
become **eponyms** — they develop their own common-noun counterpart (*sandwich* from the Earl of
Sandwich; *china* from the place name, referring to fine porcelain).

### §5.7 Count nouns (p. 527)

A count noun has distinct singular and plural forms and expresses enumerable things. Its number
determines verb agreement as a sentence's subject: singular count noun → singular verb; plural count
noun → plural verb.

### §5.8 Mass nouns (p. 528)

A mass noun (CMOS also calls it a "noncount noun") denotes something uncountable — either because it's
abstract, or because it names an indeterminate aggregation of people/things (in which case it's also a
collective noun). As a sentence's subject, a mass noun usually takes a singular verb — but in its
collective sense it can take either a singular verb (emphasizing the group as a unit) or a plural verb
(emphasizing the individual members), and the choice is meaningful.

### §5.9 Mass noun followed by a prepositional phrase (p. 529)

When a mass noun is followed by a prepositional phrase (e.g. *number of* + plural noun), the article
preceding the mass noun decides which noun controls verb agreement. A definite article (*the*) means
the mass noun itself controls the verb, typically singular ("the quantity of pizzas ordered this year
has increased"). An indefinite article (*a*/*an*) means the noun inside the prepositional phrase
controls agreement instead ("a small percentage of the test takers have failed the exam").

### §5.10 Noun-equivalents and substantives (p. 530)

A noun-equivalent is a phrase or clause that does a noun's job in a sentence (*To serve your country is
honorable*; *Bring whomever you like*). Nouns and noun-equivalents together are called substantives.

### §5.11 Noun properties (p. 531)

Nouns have the properties of case and number as a baseline; some grammarians also credit them with
gender and person.

### §5.12 Noun case (p. 532)

Only nouns and pronouns have case in English. Case denotes a noun's (or pronoun's) relationship to
other words in the sentence. Grammarians disagree on how many cases English nouns actually have:
those who treat inflection (word form) as the defining criterion count two — common (the uninflected
form) and genitive/possessive (formed by adding 's or an apostrophe) — while others argue it's still
useful to distinguish *how* the common-case form is functioning (nominative vs. objective role), even
though, except with personal pronouns (*who/whom*, *she/her*, etc.), the distinction makes no practical
difference to word form.

### §5.13 Noun number (p. 533)

Number distinguishes singular reference (one object) from plural reference (more than one). CMOS
cross-references its own later discussion of forming plurals (§7.5–14), which falls outside this
chunk's assigned range.

### §5.14 Noun gender (p. 534)

English nouns lack the grammatical gender found in French, German, Spanish, and similar languages —
gender doesn't govern the form of an accompanying article the way it does in those languages. A small
set of nouns, almost exclusively denoting people or animals, are inherently masculine (*uncle*,
*rooster*, *lad*) or feminine (*aunt*, *hen*, *lass*) and take gender-matching pronouns, but most
English nouns are common in gender and can refer to either sex (*relative*, *chicken*, *child*). Many
nouns once considered strictly masculine — especially job/profession titles — have become common in
gender over time (*author*, *executor*, *proprietor*), and feminine forms made with an explicit suffix
have similarly been largely abandoned (*aviatrix*). CMOS cross-references its own later bias-free-
language discussion (§5.221–30), outside this chunk's range.

### §5.15 Noun person (p. 535)

A few grammarians attribute first-, second-, and third-person to nouns (via apposition to a pronoun:
*I, Dan Walls, do swear...*; *you, the professor, are key*; *she, the arbiter, decides*). CMOS is
skeptical this bears on whether nouns truly possess grammatical person in the traditional sense.

### §5.16 Function of case (p. 536)

A one-line definitional restatement: case denotes the relationship between a noun or pronoun and the
other words in its sentence.

### §5.17 Common case, nominative function (p. 537)

The nominative (sometimes "subjective") function names the person/place/thing an assertion is made
about. A nominative-function noun controls its verb and usually — not always — precedes it (it can
appear sentence-medially, e.g. "high up in the tree sat a leopard," where *leopard* is still the
subject). A noun or pronoun following a *be*-verb that refers back to the subject is a **predicate
nominative** (*my show dogs are Australian shepherds*). A sentence's predicate is, generally, the part
containing the verb and making the assertion about the subject.

### §5.18 Common case, objective function (p. 538)

The objective (sometimes "accusative") function covers two cases: (1) the person/thing acted on by a
transitive verb in the active voice, or (2) the person/thing linked to another element by a connective
such as a preposition. An objective-function noun usually follows its verb, but with inverted
construction can appear elsewhere in the sentence. Critically: an objective-case noun is never the
subject of the following verb, and usually doesn't control that verb's number (cross-referencing §5.9's
mass-noun-plus-prepositional-phrase discussion).

### §5.19 Genitive case (p. 539)

The genitive case covers seven distinct relations: (1) ownership/possession/occupancy, (2) relationship,
(3) agency, (4) description, (5) role-of-subject, (6) role-of-object, and (7) idiomatic shorthand for an
*of*-phrase (*one hour's delay* = *a delay of one hour*). CMOS calls "possessive" a misleadingly narrow
name for this case, given that only the first of the seven functions is actually about possession.
Formation depends on the noun: singular nouns and plurals *not* already ending in -s/-es take 's
(*driver's seat*, *parents' house*... — note: plurals already ending in -s/-es take an apostrophe
alone, e.g. *foxes' den*); irregular plurals take 's again (*women's rights*, *mice's cage*); compound
nouns add the genitive ending to the compound's last word (*parents-in-law's message*). CMOS
cross-references its own fuller plural/genitive-formation discussion at §7.15–28, outside this chunk's
range.

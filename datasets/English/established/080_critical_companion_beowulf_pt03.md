## Grammar points

### Speech is a patterned, choreographed structural system, not free-form dialogue

Orchard's central finding in this chapter is that *Beowulf*'s ~40 speeches follow strict, recoverable
patterning rules that differ systematically between the two halves of the poem. In **Part I**
(Beowulf's youth in Denmark, lines 1–2199), speeches overwhelmingly come in **answered pairs**
(25 of 30 speeches are part of a two-speaker back-and-forth exchange), and only one speech is
interrupted. In **Part II** (Beowulf's old age and the dragon-fight, lines 2200–3182), **not a
single one of the 9 speeches receives a reply**, and three are interrupted mid-utterance by narrated
action (pp.205–8). This Part I/Part II asymmetry is presented as one of the clearest formal markers
of the poem's documented tonal shift from a courtly, "words-matter" register to an isolated,
"deeds-only" register — i.e., **speech-act patterning itself functions as a register-marking device**
distinguishing two discourse modes within a single text.

### The courtly question-answer triad as a reusable discourse template

The sequence of exchanges around Beowulf's arrival in Denmark (with the coastguard, then with
Wulfgar) follows an identical three-part template each time: (1) the Danish speaker **asks who the
newcomers are**, (2) **states his own role/office**, and (3) **expresses admiration for the
travellers' appearance**; Beowulf's reply template mirrors this: (1) **identifies the Geats via
their lord** (Hygelac), (2) **states his own name/role**, and (3) **states the nature of his errand**
(pp.208–9). Orchard shows the two exchanges even share an identical four-verb introductory skeleton
(*frægn* . . . *andswarode* . . . *[word] spræc* . . . *maþelode*) — i.e. a fixed four-beat
"ask / answer / respond / formally address" discourse unit recurs as a template independent of its
specific lexical filling (p.208).

### Imperative mood as a female-speech-register marker

Orchard notes that Queen Wealhtheow's two speeches in the poem are strikingly dense in **imperative-
mood verb forms** — at least 4 imperatives in the first 6 lines of her first speech to Hrothgar
(*Onfoh* 'take', *wes* 'be', *spræc* 'speak', *Beo* 'be'), and at least 7 across her second speech to
Beowulf (*Bruc* 'enjoy', *neot* 'use', *geþeoh* 'prosper', *cen* 'make known', *wes* 'be', *Wes*
'be', *Beo* 'be') — a pattern Orchard reads as projecting royal authority through directive speech
acts, in pointed contrast to the fact that **neither speech receives any verbal reply** from its
addressee (pp.219–22). This is a concrete example of mood-choice (imperative density) functioning as
a marker of a specific speaker's rhetorical stance within a text, independent of the language's
general grammar.

### Gnomic/proverbial sentences as a recurring rhetorical device with structural function

Beowulf's own speech is repeatedly marked by **gnomic (proverbial) statements** — general,
timeless-present-tense assertions inserted into a specific narrative moment, e.g. "Fate always goes
as it must" (*Gæð a wyrd swa hio scel*, line 455b) closing his first speech to Hrothgar, or the
three-part gnomic sequence bracketed in an envelope-pattern (*selre* . . . *selest*) that redirects
Hrothgar's grief for Æschere toward vengeance (lines 1384–9). Orchard treats this gnomic register as
a distinct, recognizable mode within the poem's speech — lending "a sense of conviction, if not
truth" — and traces how the same underlying mortality-themed gnomic content recurs almost verbatim
at both Beowulf's comforting of Hrothgar (lines 1386–7) and the narrator's own description of
Beowulf's and the dragon's shared death (lines 2341b–4) (pp.215–16).

### First-person reference density as a characterization device

Orchard quantifies **Beowulf's self-focus** directly: in a single 49-line speech to Hrothgar,
Beowulf uses 28 first-person references (p.213); in his final, pre-dragon-fight speech, Orchard again
tallies a dense cluster of first-person singular forms, plus three uses of the **dual** pronoun form
to align himself specifically with the dragon as a co-equal antagonist, and second-person plural
forms to address his men collectively (p.233). This is presented as a textual, countable correlate of
Beowulf's characterization as an individualistic hero — i.e., **pronoun-person distribution used as
quantifiable evidence for characterization**, a technique transferable to analyzing register/
personality markers in any text corpus.

### The "thought, word, and deed" triad

Orchard identifies a recurring formulaic triad describing ideal heroic/counselor conduct —
assessment in **thought** (*mod*), skill in **word** (speech/counsel), and capability in **deed**
(action) — e.g. Hrothgar's praise of Beowulf as "strong in might and wise in mind, a wise
word-speaker" (*mægenes strang ond on mode frod, wis wordcwida*, lines 1844–5a), and Hrothgar's
earlier description of the slain Æschere as *runwita* ('close confidant'), *rædbora* ('counsel-
giver'), and *eaxlgestealla* ('shoulder-companion [in battle]') (pp.73, 146, 217–18, 255, citing
Patrick Sims-Williams's identification of the same triad as a broader Insular/Irish-influenced
convention). This chapter's own title, "Words and Deeds," names the poem's larger thematic polarity
between the two terms of this triad.

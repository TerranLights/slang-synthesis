## Grammar points

### Reported statements

In reported (indirect) speech, the tense of the speaker's original words usually shifts back one
tense when the reporting verb (*said, told*, etc.) is in the past; the tense does not shift if the
reporting verb is present, present perfect, or future (p.128). The book's own full backshift table:
present simple→past simple; present continuous→past continuous; present perfect simple→past perfect
simple; present perfect continuous→past perfect continuous; past simple→past perfect simple; past
continuous→past perfect continuous; past perfect→past perfect (unchanged); *going to* future→*was
going to*; *shall/will*→*would*; *shall* in offers/suggestions→*should*; *must*→*had to*;
*can*→*could*.

**When the tense doesn't change (pp.128–130):** the past simple and past continuous very often stay
unchanged in reported speech — in both written and spoken registers — whenever this causes no
confusion about timing; other tenses can also stay unchanged, but this is specifically noted as more
common in spoken than written reporting. Four specific sub-cases are detailed: **past
continuous** usually stays unchanged ("he said that when they arrived, he was still cooking the
dinner"), except when it refers to an activity that was completed before another past/present-perfect
event, in which case it's more likely to shift to past perfect continuous. **Past simple** generally
doesn't change in a time clause; when used to describe a situation still true in the present, it
generally doesn't change either (worked example: "We didn't stay in the hotel because it was too
expensive" stays as *was*, since the hotel is presumably still expensive); otherwise, written
reporting favors shifting past simple to past perfect, while oral reporting increasingly leaves it
unchanged. **Present tenses** are increasingly left unchanged in oral reporting specifically when the
situation/activity is still ongoing at the time of reporting, but do shift if it has since ended
(worked minimal pair: "he can't come to the party because he's doing his homework" stays present if
the homework is still happening, but shifts to "was doing" once the party and the homework are both
over). **The future** similarly stays unchanged in oral reporting if the event is still in the
future relative to the time of reporting, and shifts to the past equivalent once that event itself
has become past.

### Time and place changes

Reported speech often requires shifting deictic time/place references depending on when and where
the reporting happens relative to the original utterance (p.130). The book's own conversion table:
*now*→*then*; *tonight*→*that night*; *yesterday*→*the day before*/*on* + day name;
*tomorrow*→*the following day*/*the next day*/*on* + day name; *this morning*→*that morning*; *last
week*→*the previous week*; *next Saturday*→*the following Saturday*; *ago*→*before/previously*;
*here*→*there*; *this* + place→*that/the* + place; *this* alone→*that/the*.

### Reported questions

The same tense-backshift rules that apply to statements also apply to questions (p.131). For
*wh*-questions, subject and verb are **not** inverted in the reported form (the question-word
inversion of direct speech is undone: "Why didn't you tell me the truth?" → "She asked me why I
hadn't told her the truth"). For yes/no questions, *if* or *whether* is inserted before the
subject+verb ("Have I seen you before?" → "She asked me if she had seen me before").

**The *to*-infinitive in reported speech:** used for reported requests, advice, and commands ("Can
you help me write my speech?" → "She asked me to help her write her speech"; negative form: "Don't
stay out too late" → "She told me not to stay out too late"). When reporting a *shall/should*
question, the *to*-infinitive can follow the question word itself ("How much should we give them?"
→ "He wanted to know how much to give them"). A note flags that the *to*-infinitive is not used
after *why* or *if* in this construction.

### Verbs used in reporting

Many different verbs are available for reporting, since full-sentence reproduction of the original
words is not usually required — a well-chosen reporting verb can summarize the original utterance's
illocutionary force in a single word (p.134; worked examples: "Would you like to come and stay for
the weekend?" summarized as "He invited us to stay for the weekend"; "No, no, I don't want your
money..." summarized as "She insisted on paying for the meal"). The book organizes its reporting-verb
inventory into three sets by the grammatical complement each requires — + infinitive, + gerund, +
*that*-clause (the full word lists are captured in the Vocabulary section above).

### Summarizing when reporting

Beyond choosing a precise reporting verb, adjectives are often used to summarize how someone
responded, rather than reporting their actual words ("Oh, that's fantastic. I'm just so
pleased...This is the best news we've had in ages!" → "She was delighted when we told her the news")
(p.136).

**Oral vs. written reporting style (p.136):** oral reporting characteristically mixes direct speech,
reported speech, and plain content-description within a single retelling, switching method freely as
the speaker goes; written reporting instead tends to sustain reported-speech tenses consistently
throughout a passage once the reporting is established, without needing to keep repeating "She/He/It
said..." for every new sentence, as long as it remains clear from context that reporting is still in
effect.

## Grammar points

### Why ME has no single standard spelling system (and why that is evidentially useful)

Middle English (conventionally c.1150–1450) is defined as the period between Old and Modern English,
and the manuscript evidence for its development is of the same basic kind as for OE (every copy of a
book, letter, or charter was individually hand-copied, so originals and copies both survive
unevenly) — but with one crucial added source of variation. The Norman Conquest's social/political
upheaval meant the former OE West Saxon written standard was abandoned; writers and copyists after
the Conquest had no equivalent authoritative spelling system to fall back on, so scribes' spelling
increasingly reflected **their own spoken dialect** rather than a fixed convention, and successive
copying could further mix dialectal spellings together. For a historical linguist this is a net gain,
not just noise: the loss of a masking written standard means ME manuscripts carry much richer
first-hand evidence of regional pronunciation differences than late OE manuscripts do. (p.43, Section
3.1)

### The Norman Conquest's effect on the social position of English, French, and Latin

After 1066, (Anglo-)Norman French — not English — became the language of the ruling class and (along
with Latin) of formal/official writing, while Latin remained the shared language of the Church and
most formal documentation; English was displaced from prestige administrative/literary functions for
roughly three centuries, regaining use in law courts after 1362 and in Parliament itself by the
century's end. Two contrasting 14th-century chronicle voices are cited as direct evidence of
contemporary attitudes to this situation: Robert Mannyng (NE Midlands dialect, 1338) laments that
French-speaking Flemish and Picard forces, not native English forces, were relied on in battle, while
an anonymous SW Midlands metrical chronicle (Text 20) is openly hostile to Norman dominance,
describing the Norman gentry as deliberately keeping their children ignorant of English ("speke
french as hii dude at om") so as to be taken for "gentle men," and contrasting this with "lowe men"
who "holdeþ to engliss" because they never learned anything else. Robert of Gloucester's chronicle
(Text 21, c.1300) strikes a similar note: "a man knoweth no French, people count him little — but low
men hold to English, and to their own speech still." (pp.44–45, Section 3.2)

### The Peterborough Chronicle's continuations as the earliest substantial ME text, and what they show

A fire destroyed most of Peterborough Abbey's library in 1116, including its own copy of the *Anglo-
Saxon Chronicle*; the copy that survives today (the *Peterborough Chronicle*) was borrowed and
recopied afterward, with annals up to 1121 all in one scribal hand continuing the earlier classical
West Saxon OE orthography, but the **continuations** covering 1122–1131 and 1132–1154 are each in a
different, later scribal hand and are explicitly singled out as the earliest substantial evidence of
*developing* Middle English — not classical written OE, but the spoken East Midlands (Danelaw-area)
usage of 12th-century Peterborough monks, probably written down more by ear/dictation than copied
from an older written exemplar, and so carrying noticeably more French-influenced spelling
conventions than the earlier annals. Comparing an annal from this continuation (Text 23, for 1137,
describing hardship and extortion under King Stephen's civil war — "the Anarchy") against a
hypothetical "regularized" rewriting in the earlier, more conservative West Saxon written standard
makes the direction of change directly visible: the most significant change identified is the
**beginning of the large-scale loss of OE inflections**, which in turn produces greater reliance on
fixed **word order** and more frequent use of **prepositions** to carry grammatical relationships
that inflections used to signal — the text already "reads much more like MnE than the OE texts, even
though there is still some way to go." (pp.46–48, Section 3.3)

### The *Ormulum*'s invented, internally consistent spelling system

Orm (a Danish personal name), a monk probably writing in what is now South Humberside in the late
12th century, composed the *Ormulum* as a set of English-language homilies meant to be read aloud, and
devised his own idiosyncratic but highly **internally consistent** spelling system specifically to
help a reader pronounce the text correctly — most notably, doubling a consonant letter whenever it
followed a short vowel (e.g. *wrohhte*, *Orrm*), and introducing three distinct symbol-forms for ⟨g⟩
to differentiate sounds that had been conflated under the single OE letter ⟨ȝ⟩. Because his verse
metre is also absolutely regular (15 syllables per line, alternating unstressed/stressed, no
exceptions — confirmed in the book's own scansion of the opening lines), his spelling and metre
together make the *Ormulum* an unusually reliable source for reconstructing his dialect's actual
pronunciation, even though his writing — monotonous, regular verse by a single scrupulous individual
— is explicitly flagged by the author as a less typical/representative evidential source than
ordinary prose would be. (pp.50–52, Sections 3.4–3.4.3)

### No ME standard dialect existed; the (later) conditions for Standard English's rise

Across the whole ME period there was no single recognized standard form of English — the book
explicitly invokes a sociolinguistic definition of "standard" (Dittmar, 1976: the variety "elevated to
the obligatory norm for social intercourse" by a society's dominant forces) to make the point that a
standard language's selection is a social/political choice imitating the prestige-holding group's
usage, not an intrinsic property of any dialect itself ("all dialects are regular, rule-governed").
The conditions for a recognizable Standard English only began emerging by the late 15th century; as
contemporary evidence of the kind of advice already circulating once this process was underway, the
poet George Puttenham's 1589 *The Arte of English Poesie* explicitly recommends poets use "the vsuall
speach of the Court, and that of London and the shires lying about London within lx [60] myles, and
not much aboue" as the correct model to imitate — not a neutral/organic outcome but a deliberate
prestige judgment. (pp.94, Section 7.1, forward-referenced from p.53, Section 3.5)

## Grammar points

### Why London's dialect — not York's, or Winchester's — became the basis of Standard English

The chapter gives an explicit causal account of why the East Midlands/London dialect, rather than any
other ME dialect, ended up forming the base of Standard English. Late-ME London's own dialect was a
mixture of several ME dialects, but was most strongly shaped by the **East Midlands** dialect
specifically, because historians have identified a substantial, sustained migration of people *from*
the East Midlands *to* London from the late 13th into the mid-14th century, bringing their dialect
speech with them; London also carries detectable Southern and Kentish features from other, smaller
streams of in-migration. The reason this particular mixture then became *prestigious* — imitated by
others, per the Dittmar definition of "standard" already introduced in pt02's Section 3.5 discussion
— is sociopolitical rather than linguistic: London was the centre of government, trade, and commerce,
so its dialect carried the practical and social prestige that "the dominant forces in society" project
onto whatever they themselves speak. The author states the counterfactual explicitly: had the centre
of government and commerce instead been York, "no doubt the Northern dialect would have formed the
basis for Standard English today" — i.e. there is nothing intrinsically more "correct," efficient, or
linguistically privileged about the East Midlands/London dialect that caused its own rise; this is a
repeat, applied instance of the Dittmar/Puttenham argument already made in pt02. A direct consequence
of this history is that Chaucer's late-14th-century English, and other East Midlands ME texts, read
comparatively easily to a modern reader *because* Standard English descends from substantially the
same dialect lineage, not because Chaucer's variety was objectively simpler or more "modern" than
contemporary Northern or Western ME. (p.94, Section 7.1)

### Chaucer's own London literary dialect vs. ordinary London speech

Chaucer wrote in the **literary** form of the London dialect current among the educated class of his
time — explicitly distinguished from the speech of ordinary London people, which the author states
must have differed from Chaucer's own usage "both in form and pronunciation," drawing an analogy to
the present-day relationship between educated RP/Standard English and Cockney. This caution matters
for how Chaucer's texts should be used as evidence: they attest educated/literary East Midlands-London
ME specifically, not the full social range of contemporary London speech. (p.98, Section 7.3.1)

### Using Chaucer's verse rhymes as indirect evidence of pronunciation change

Because no direct audio evidence survives for any pre-recording English, the book proposes **rhyme
evidence** in verse as one of the few indirect windows onto actual pronunciation available for a
period like Chaucer's: if two ME words rhyme in his verse, the working assumption is that they shared
the same vowel sound at the time of writing. Comparing a rhyming pair's known OE/ON/OF sources against
their modern reflexes then sorts into three distinct evidential outcomes: (i) the pair's sources
already shared the same sound, and that sound has not changed significantly since — both words still
rhyme in MnE too (e.g. *wyght*/*knyght*, from OE *wiht*/*cniht*); (ii) the sources shared the same
sound, but **both** words have since undergone an identical further sound change, so they still rhyme
in MnE, just with a different sound from Chaucer's (e.g. *breeth*/*heeth*, from OE *bræþ*/*hæþ*); or
(iii) the sources shared the same sound at Chaucer's time, but subsequently **diverged** via
different, unmatched sound changes to the two words — so the pair no longer rhymes at all in MnE, even
though it demonstrably did in the 1390s (e.g. *brist*/*list*, from OE *breost*/*hlystan* — now *breast*
and *listen*, with no modern rhyme relationship). The book proposes applying this systematically across
a large enough sample of a poet's rhyming pairs (e.g. the full 162-line *Canterbury Tales* prologue) to
build up a reasonably comprehensive map of attested ME-to-MnE vowel changes — flagged as a genuinely
rigorous, falsifiable method rather than impressionistic guesswork, and noting that the general loss of
ME inflections will itself have separately affected which word-pairs still rhyme today even where the
core vowel sound hasn't changed. (p.100, Section 7.3.3)

### Everyday legal/documentary prose as a check on literary-text bias

Thomas Usk's 1384 "appeal" (a formal legal accusation, written by Usk personally — "I Thomas Vsk ...
wrote hem with myn owne honde") is introduced specifically as a counterweight to relying only on
literary texts (verse romance, sermons, chronicle narrative) for dialect evidence: it represents
"the London English of a fairly well-educated man" in an ordinary, non-literary, practical document,
giving a different register of evidence from Chaucer's deliberately crafted literary prose/verse. The
author notes explicitly that literary language "draws on the ordinary language of its time, but in a
special way" and that one specific period's literature cannot be assumed to transparently reflect how
people actually spoke — legal/documentary prose like Usk's appeal is offered as a partial corrective,
closer to unselfconscious everyday written usage. (p.101, Section 7.4)

### London-dialect inconsistency as evidence of a standard still actively forming

Activity 7.8's checklist for the East Midlands/London ME dialects explicitly flags **internal
inconsistency within the London dialect itself** (not just difference from other regional dialects) as
diagnostic: London shows variable treatment of OE /y/ (sometimes unrounded to /i/ as expected, but
some words instead show the Kentish reflex /e/ or the Southern reflex /u/, reflecting mixed-dialect
migration into the city); the past-participle prefix *y-* (from OE *ge-*) is inconsistently retained
or dropped where the "purer" East Midlands dialect retains it more systematically; and the infinitive
suffix *-en* is generally kept in East Midlands but may be dropped in London under Southern dialect
influence. The author's point in flagging this inconsistency is that it is itself evidence that
London's dialect, during this period, was a genuinely unsettled mixture actively being negotiated
among migrants from several dialect areas — not yet a single coherent system — which is exactly the
state of affairs that later crystallizes into a standard. (p.103, Activity 7.8)

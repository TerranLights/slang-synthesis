## Grammar points

### Latin's long-delayed retreat from English scholarship and science

The chapter opens by complicating the "English triumphs over Latin" narrative from `pt05`'s end:
Latin retained enormous prestige as the language of the grammar-school curriculum, the universities,
and — crucially — **international scientific communication** well past the "restoration" of English
as a literary medium. The book's concrete evidence: Francis Bacon wrote his accessible
*Advancement of Learning* (1605) in English but his intended major scientific-method contribution,
the *Novum Organum* (1620), in Latin; the three most significant English scientific works published
1600–1700 — Gilbert on magnetism (1600), Harvey on the circulation of the blood (1628), and Newton's
*Principia* (1689) — were **all written in Latin**; only by Newton's own *Opticks* (1704) had Latin
genuinely fallen into disuse for English scientific writing. Three specific forces the book credits
for English's eventual displacement of Latin: (1) the **Reformation**, which needed vernacular
religious controversy literature to reach the broad, often non-classically-educated public
Protestantism appealed to (worked contrast: Thomas More wrote in Latin for the *Utopia* [1516],
aimed at Europe's learned elite, but switched to English for his domestic anti-Reformer polemics;
Milton a century later shows the same split — Latin for works meant for Europe's learned men, English
for his politically urgent pamphlets), compounded by the Bible's translation into English and the
switch to English in church services, which directly raised English's prestige — with the book
noting the most extreme Protestants regarded Latin as a deliberately "popish" tool of clerical
control; (2) rising **nationalism**, which shifted literary ambition toward vernacular national
languages and produced conscious attempts to give English its own Homer/Virgil-equivalent canonical
epic (Spenser's *Faerie Queene*, 1590; Milton's *Paradise Lost*, 1667); (3) the emergence of
**non-Latinate practical/craft social classes** (instrument makers, navigators, explorers, soldiers)
who needed and wanted technical literature but had no Latin education, driving a flood of
English-medium practical treatises — with the book noting the printing press and rising literacy
(calculated at roughly 10% of men vs. 1% of women around 1500, with notable individual exceptions
like Queen Elizabeth I, Aphra Behn, and Margaret Cavendish) as the infrastructure that made this
demand serviceable.

### The three-stage pattern by which Latin ceded a field of discourse to English, field by field

Rather than one uniform date, the book models English's takeover of Latin's scholarly domains as a
recurring **three-stage process repeated separately per field** (rhetoric, logic, geometry,
classical history, warfare, medicine, etc.): first, English **translations** of standard Latin
works; then **original English works heavily dependent on** Latin originals; finally, **fully
independent English-language works** in that field, needing no Latin source at all. Medicine is
flagged as a partial exception/holdout: physicians fought especially hard to keep medical
literature in Latin specifically to protect their professional monopoly over medical knowledge from
lay encroachment, producing some of the bitterest period controversy over English-medium
scientific publication.

### The Renaissance Latin-loanword flood: scale, periodization, and adaptation patterns

The influx of French words in Middle English (`pt04`) had, per the book, already "predisposed
English speakers to borrow words from abroad" — Early Modern English then receives this predisposed
borrowing habit "full scope" specifically from Latin, peaking roughly **1580–1660**. New loans
arrived in two formal states: taken over bodily with original Latin spelling/inflection intact
(*genius*, *species*, etc. — individually dated by the book from 1513 to 1693, several retaining
their full original Latin meaning rather than their eventual anglicized sense, e.g. *focus* "hearth"
and *lens* "lentil"), or adapted into an anglicized shape (Latin *-ātus* → English *-ate*; Latin
inflection simply dropped; Latin *-itas*/*-entia*/*-antia* reshaped as *-ity*/*-ence*/*-ency*/
*-ancy*, with the book flagging this last pattern as genuinely ambiguous evidence — it's often
impossible to tell whether such a word came into English directly from Latin or indirectly via a
French intermediary with the same Latin ancestry, since French mediation would produce the identical
surface shape). The book stresses these loans skew toward **learned, written-register vocabulary**
(scientific, mathematical, legal, liberal-arts terms) much more heavily than the earlier French wave,
though also notes a genuine general-vocabulary contingent (*relaxation*, *offensive*, *investigate*,
*album*, *circus*, *miser*) that entered ordinary usage rather than staying technical.

### The "inkhorn terms" controversy: two motives for Latin borrowing, and a failed native-purist countermovement

Elizabethan headmaster Richard Mulcaster's 1582 framing, quoted by the book, distinguishes two
motives behind Latin borrowing: a **utilitarian** one (genuine new-word necessity for new concepts)
and "mere brauerie" (**sheer ostentation** — borrowing purely to signal education/social
superiority). The ostentatious extreme produced derisively-labeled "inkhorn terms," satirized within
the period itself (Thomas Wilson's *Arte of Rhetorique*, 1553; the pedant character Holofernes in
Shakespeare's *Love's Labour's Lost*; the character Crispinus in Ben Jonson's *Poetaster*). A
distinct, organized **"purist" nativist countermovement** — itself, the book notes, "another
manifestation of English nationalism," ideologically adjacent to the pro-English-over-Latin movement
covered above but opposed to loanwords specifically — tried to coin native-morpheme replacement
terms (Ralph Lever's 1573 logic textbook: *endsay*, *foresays*, *saywhat*, *witcraft*, *yeasay*).
**None of Lever's coinages caught on**; Modern English uses exactly the Latin-derived terms
(*conclusion*, *premises*, *definition*, *logic*, *affirmation*) the purist movement was trying to
displace — the book's flagged lesson is that **contemporary mockery of a neologism as pretentious
"inkhorn" vocabulary is not a reliable predictor of whether that specific word will survive**: the
Crispinus word-list mixes genuinely-dead coinages (*furibund*, *lubrical*, *oblatrant*, *turgidous*)
with words equally mocked at the time that are now completely unremarkable standard vocabulary
(*defunct*, *reciprocal*, *retrograde*, *spurious*, *strenuous*).

### Renaissance "remodeling": Latin-etymology-driven respelling and re-pronunciation of existing words

Distinct from new borrowing, Latin influence also caused **existing** English words (often of French
origin, already established in the language) to be reshaped toward their real or supposed Latin
etymology — and the book is careful to split this into two types with different real-world effects:
(1) **spelling-only remodeling**, where the pronunciation never actually changed (*debt*, *doubt*,
*receipt*, *indict* — inserted *b*/*p*/*c* letters that have never once been pronounced in English,
except, drily, by the self-consciously pedantic Holofernes character); (2) **genuine pronunciation
remodeling**, where Middle English forms (*assaut*, *aventure*, *descrive*, *parfit*, *verdit*) were
actually re-pronounced to match their reconstructed Latin shape, giving Modern *assault*, *adventure*,
*describe*, *perfect*, *verdict* — with one fossil of the pre-remodeling pronunciation surviving in
the idiom *at a venture* "at random" (1 Kings 22:34, KJB), a now-opaque misdivision of ME *at
aventure*. The book then flags a genuinely interesting failure mode: **remodeling sometimes rests on
false etymology**. *Advance*/*advantage* were reshaped with an inserted *ad-* on the mistaken belief
they derived from the Latin prefix *ad-*, when both actually derive from French *avant* (Latin *ab
ante*, an unrelated prefix) — "combining pedantry with inadequate scholarship," in the book's own
phrase. *Admiral* is a parallel false-etymology case with a messier resolution: it entered English
via French, which took it from Arabic *amir al bahr* "commander of the sea" — no genuine Latin
*ad-* prefix involved at all — yet the *ad-*-spelled form is already found in Middle English, while
the unremodeled *ammiral* persists as late as Milton, with the book speculating the false-etymology
spelling was reinforced by a chance resemblance to the genuinely-Latin word *admirable*.

### Non-Latin loanword sources in the Early Modern period, ranked by volume

After Latin (by far the dominant source), the book ranks the remaining Early Modern loanword
sources: **French** (second-largest; skewing military — *bayonet*, *feint* — and life-sciences —
*anatomy*, *muscle* — alongside genuinely everyday-register words like *docility*, *entrance*,
*invite*); **Classical Greek** (comparatively few direct loans, mostly technical terms of literary
criticism/rhetoric/theology/natural science, since most Greek-rooted vocabulary of this period
actually arrived mediated through Latin or French rather than directly — *anathema*, *cosmos*,
*larynx*, *pathos* cited as genuine *direct* Greek borrowings); **Italian and Spanish** (tied to the
period's "grand tour" cultural fashion and period satire of Italianate/Hispanicized gallants — Italian
loans cluster in warfare, commerce, and the arts; Spanish loans in commerce and warfare, plus — via
sustained Spanish/Portuguese contact with the early-colonial Americas — a specific cluster of "new
world" loanwords that could equally plausibly have entered via either Iberian language, e.g.
*mosquito*); **Dutch** (the only other sizeable period source, reflecting sustained Anglo-Dutch
commercial/maritime contact going back to the Norman Conquest — a seafaring/trade cluster in Middle
and 16th-century English, a further seafaring cluster in the 17th century, plus Dutch's period
reputation for oil-painting and for drinking culture supplying a couple of distinctive 17th-century
loans).

### Native word-formation persisted (and probably outpaced borrowing) alongside the loanword flood

The book's explicit counter-framing to the inkhorn-term controversy, which it says was itself
misleadingly loanword-obsessed: **native word-formation (affixation, compounding, conversion)
probably produced more new words in this period than foreign borrowing did**, even though
contemporaries, fixated on the inkhorn-terms controversy, didn't particularly notice this. Any new
loanword, moreover, tended to rapidly spin off further native-derived words almost immediately
(*comfortable* [1300s French loan] → *comfortably* → *uncomfortable* [1592], all within roughly two
and a half centuries) — a direct continuation of the same point already made about French loans in
`pt04`. **Affixation** was the single commonest word-formation method, mostly producing nouns and
adjectives (common noun suffixes *-ness*, *-er*; common adjective suffixes *-ed*, *-y*; common
adverb suffix *-ly*, occasionally *-wise*; the main verb-forming suffix *-ize*; and, by far the most
frequent prefix of the whole period, **un-**, combining freely across nouns, adjectives, participles,
verbs, and adverbs alike). **Compounding** was mostly Noun+Noun (*sheep-brand*, *waterdock*), with a
fair Adjective+Noun contingent (*Frenchwoman*, *freshman*) and some Verb+Object compounds
(*scrape-penny* "miser"). **Conversion** (zero-derivation, changing a word's part of speech with no
morphological marking at all) was the third major mechanism, with three especially productive
patterns: noun-from-verb (*to bayonet*, *to gossip*, *to invoice*), noun-from-adjective (*an
ancient* "an old man," *a brisk* "a fop"), and noun-from-verb in the other direction (*an invite*, *a
laugh*). The book's register observation closing this section: native-formed words (via any of the
three mechanisms) skew toward ordinary/practical registers (farming, fishing, handicrafts), while
Latin loans skew toward formal/literary/specialized registers (science, medicine, religion, classical
culture, the liberal arts) — i.e. the period's two main vocabulary-growth mechanisms were operating
on genuinely different, register-stratified parts of the vocabulary, not competing for the exact same
communicative work.

### Shakespeare's *Henry IV Part 1* as a worked grammar specimen: choice and variation as the period's hallmark

The book's organizing claim for this section: Early Modern English speakers routinely had a **genuine
choice** between two or more grammatical forms/constructions where Modern English allows only one —
illustrated via an extended passage from the 1598 Quarto of *Henry IV Part 1* (the Gadshill-robbery
aftermath scene, Falstaff/Hal/the Sheriff). Specific points of documented EModE variation, several
directly extending patterns already traced back through Old and Middle English in `pt03`–`pt05`:

- **Verb inflection in variation, mid-shift**: present-plural already has zero inflection, as today
  (Chaucer's *han*/*desiren* have become Shakespeare's uninflected *haue...lost*); the archaic *-en*
  plural survives only as a conscious poetic/rustic-speech archaism in writers like Spenser, or in a
  handful of frozen forms (*hath*, *doth*); the old northern *-es* plural occasionally surfaces even
  in southern-register writing (Shakespeare's *The Tempest*: "my old bones **akes**"), but these are
  flagged as genuine minority usages dying out entirely by the 17th century. In the third-person
  singular, Shakespeare's passage shows both the newer *-es* (*rides*) and the older *-eth*
  (*hath*) side by side — *-eth* is "increasingly displaced" by *-es* as the normal spoken-register
  form over the 16th century, but **persists much longer in writing**, especially formal/poetic
  registers, well into the 20th century; some specific *-eth* forms (contracted *doth*, *hath*,
  *saith*) outlasted the regular full forms (*judgeth*, *passeth*, *teacheth*) by the whole 17th
  century.
- **The subjunctive was still a normal, fully colloquial grammatical option**, not yet the marked,
  "somewhat literary" relic it is in Modern English (where only a handful of fossilized
  constructions survive, e.g. "if it be so," "if he were here") — illustrated by the passage's
  subjunctive *haue* ("if he **haue** robd these men," base form + no inflection, conditioned by the
  preceding *if*).
- **Noun plurals** in the passage use both the now-standard *-es* (*masters*, *markes*) and a
  front-mutation-descended irregular plural (*men*, see `pt03`) — the *-es* ending had, by this
  period, already developed the three now-familiar phonologically-conditioned allomorphs [-s]/[-z]/
  [-ɪz] (distributed as in Modern *cats*/*dogs*/*horses*), via a cascade of Late Middle English sound
  changes the book summarizes as a "process of grammatical regulation" completing in the 15th–16th
  centuries — this is the **endpoint** of the same OE-*-as*-plural-generalization story traced across
  `pt03`/`pt05`.
- **Demonstratives** *this*/*these*, *that*/*those* match Modern usage exactly by this period, with
  a now-obsolete third demonstrative, *yon*/*yond(er)* ("remote from both speaker and hearer,"
  carrying an additional implication of visibility/pointing — the book's worked example is
  Barnardo's line in *Hamlet* I.i, "When yond same Starre... Westward from the Pole").
- **The thou/you distinction functioned as a genuine social-register system**, not just a
  singular/plural distinction: by Shakespeare's time *you* is the unmarked/default form in both
  numbers, but singular *thou* remains available and **socially loaded** — the book models it as
  structurally parallel to a modern first-name-vs-title-and-surname address distinction. Children and
  animals were addressed as *thou* by default; between a higher- and lower-status adult speaker, the
  higher-status speaker could freely alternate *thou*/*you* (patronizing vs. more complaisant),
  while the lower-status speaker **was effectively compelled to use *you*** — using *thou* upward was
  genuinely insulting. Among social equals in the "polite classes," *thou* became an **emotionally
  charged, marked choice**, available to express either unusual intimacy/affection or
  anger/contempt — the book's worked textual evidence is the Gadshill scene itself, where the Prince
  is addressed as *you* by every other character except Falstaff, who uses *thou*/*Hal* specifically
  to presume on (and perform) his personal intimacy with the Prince, while the Prince himself,
  socially entitled to *thou* anyone, fluctuates to the politer *you* at points in the scene.
  Lower-class characters are shown defaulting to mutual *thou* among themselves. Over the 17th
  century *you* steadily displaced *thou* across the gentry and citizenry, with *thou* surviving the
  century mainly in literary (especially poetic) register, and continuing in some lower-class and
  northern/western regional dialects even longer.
- **The possessive-determiner *its* was a genuine Early Modern innovation**, not inherited — the
  traditional EModE possessive of *it* was simply *his* (shared with the masculine), and *its* is
  not attested until very near the end of the 16th century, remaining rare even in works published
  late in Shakespeare's career, and **entirely absent from the 1611 King James Bible**, which
  invariably uses *his* for inanimate possession too (Matthew 5:13, "if the salt haue lost his
  sauour" — directly connecting to `pt01`'s observation about the KJB's conservative, sometimes
  already-archaic-for-1611 grammar). *Its* then spread rapidly through the 1620s, apparently once
  speakers found sharing one possessive form between *he* and *it* genuinely inconvenient.
- **Reflexives used ordinary pronouns, not *-self* forms**, for the plain reflexive function
  (Falstaff's "ile hide **me**" = "I'll hide myself") — *-self* forms were reserved specifically for
  **intensive/emphatic** use (the Prince's "I my selfe... haue emploid him").
- **Relative-clause marking was looser and less semantically restricted than today.** Subject-position
  relative pronouns could still be freely omitted (a direct continuation of the Chaucer-period
  pattern already flagged in `pt05`: "there are two gentlemen [who] Haue... lost 300 markes"). *Who*,
  *which*, and *that* were all current but **not yet restricted by the personal/non-personal
  antecedent distinction** that governs Modern Standard English: *which* was freely used with
  personal antecedents (Shakespeare, *The Tempest*: "The Mistris **which** I serue"), and — more
  rarely — *who* could take a non-personal antecedent (*Romeo and Juliet*: "her lips, **Who**...
  still blush"); *that* is nearly universal in defining relative clauses in this period, but — unlike
  Modern usage — not uncommon in **non-defining** ones either (the book's example: Bacon's
  *Advancement of Learning*, "Midas, **that** being chosen judge between Apollo... and Pan... judged
  for plenty"). The book dates the Modern settlement (*that* restricted to defining clauses; *who*/
  *which* increasingly split along the personal/non-personal line) to a 17th-century development,
  reaching essentially its present-day state by the century's end.
- **The perfect tense still used both *have* and *be***, with the same semantic split already traced
  in `pt05`: *be*-perfects remain common specifically with verbs of motion (*come*, *enter*, *run*)
  and change-of-state (*become*, *grow*, *turn*) — "they **are** come to search the house," directly
  matching the 1611 KJB pattern in `pt01` — but the book adds a refinement here: even verbs that
  normally take a *be*-perfect can appear with *have* when the **sentence's communicative focus is on
  the action as an ongoing process** rather than on the resulting state (Shakespeare, *Cymbeline*:
  "I **haue** gone... all night") — i.e. the *have*/*be* choice in this period tracks a genuine
  aspectual/focus distinction, not free variation.
- **Verb-Subject word order, without any auxiliary, was still a live option** (not confined to
  questions) when a sentence opened with certain adverbs (*now*, *so*, *then*, *there*, *thus*) —
  the book's outside-the-passage example is Tyndale's 1534 New Testament, "For so persecuted **they**
  the Prophetes." This declined sharply over the 17th century but has never fully disappeared from
  Modern English, surviving specifically after a fronted negative adverb (*never*, *rarely*,
  *scarcely*: "Never before **have I** tasted such unappetizing food").

### The rise of dummy auxiliary *do*: a worked case study in grammaticalization

The chapter's single most extended grammar point: the book traces the *do*-auxiliary from a genuine
Old English **causative** main verb (already noted in OE, "mainly found in close translations from
the Latin," not independently idiomatic) through a specific, textually-documented reanalysis pathway
to its modern dummy-auxiliary function. The mechanism: Middle English causative sentences like *He
did them build a castle* ("he caused them to build a castle") coexisted with a south-western
dialectal variant lacking an explicit causee (*he did build*, "he caused a castle to be built,"
genuinely ambiguous with simple "he built" once the implied causee drops out) — and crucially, even
a plain non-causative sentence like *He built a castle* already contains an inherent "causative"
component in the ordinary meaning of *built* (the subject caused the castle to come into being, even
if by his own hands) — so **the ambiguous south-western construction and the plain simple-past
construction became semantically equivalent in a large class of real sentences**, inviting listeners
to treat *did build* as nothing more than a stylistic variant of *built*. Once that equation was
made, it was a short further step for speakers to extend the now-semantically-empty *do* + infinitive
pattern to verbs/contexts where no causative reading had ever been plausible at all, completing *do*'s
shift to a purely grammatical, meaning-empty dummy auxiliary. The book traces this spread concretely:
non-causative *do* arose in south-western dialects in the late 13th century; it spread first through
**poetry**, where it was a convenient device for moving a verb into rhyme position at line-end (worked
example: a 15th-century poem rhyming *hyed* with *heape* in one line but the equivalent *did hye* with
*crye* in another — purely a metrical choice between the two, not a meaning difference); from verse it
spread into prose (first attested there around 1400), slowly through the 15th century, then rapidly
through the 16th, with the older true-causative use of *do* dying out around the same time (replaced
by *make* and *cause*) — so that by the 16th century, dummy *do* functions as a **purely optional
stylistic variant** of the simple form, fully interchangeable (Shakespeare could equally write "I
know," "I know not," "I do know," "I know not" / "Know you?" or "Do you know?"), with no restriction
yet to any particular sentence type. **The now-familiar restriction** (affirmative declaratives drop
*do* except for emphatic stress; negative and interrogative sentences require it) developed gradually
over the 17th century, reaching essentially the present-day system by around 1700; in the 15th and
earlier 16th centuries, *do*-use in any sentence type reads as a mark of literary style, but once the
restriction process is underway from the later 16th century on, the **reversed** mapping appears —
unrestricted ("literary") *do*-use in affirmative statements starts reading as old-fashioned/formal
rather than modern, while the restricted modern pattern reads as the more colloquial, natural
register, with individual verbs showing variable lag in adopting the restriction even as late as the
late 17th century (*I know not*, *if I mistake not*, *Say you so?*, *What think you?* all still
attested without *do* at that late date).

### Dummy *do*'s present-day function and why Early Modern negation needed it

The chapter closes this topic by explicitly naming *do*'s modern function as the **dummy auxiliary**
— structurally performing all four of the syntactic jobs an auxiliary does (pre-negation positioning,
subject-auxiliary question inversion, echo-repetition tags, and emphatic stress) while carrying **no
meaning of its own**, deployed specifically whenever no other auxiliary (*be*, *have*, *can*, *could*,
*will*, etc.) is independently present to do that structural work — crucially **except** in
*wh*-questions, where normal S-V word order is preserved without needing dummy *do* at all ("**Who**
lives there?" not "*Who does live there?"). The book connects this directly back to the Middle
English negation patterns already catalogued in `pt05` (multiple-negation sentences like "I ween
there ne beeth in all the world countries none that ne holdeth to their own speech," "Ne never were
martyrs so tortured") — **these typical Middle English negation constructions are no longer possible
in Modern English precisely because Modern English requires an auxiliary (now usually dummy *do*)
immediately before *not*** to form a negative sentence at all, making the EModE dummy-*do*
grammaticalization the structural prerequisite for the whole modern negation system, not an
independent or cosmetic development.

### The Great Vowel Shift: the Early Modern period's signature sound change

Presented as the chapter's central phonological event: a systematic raising in **quality** (not
length) of every English long vowel, beginning early in the 15th century and not fully complete until
late in the 17th — Figure 11 (p. 202) diagrams the whole shift as a connected chain. The two vowels
already at maximum closeness (long high ME *ī*, *ū*) had nowhere left to raise, so they instead
**diphthongized** — ME *ī* (in *mice*, *fly*) moved [iː]→[ɪi] (15th c.)→[əɪ] (Shakespeare's time,
central starting-point) →[aɪ] (17th c., its present position); ME *ū* (in *house*, *how*) moved in
parallel [uː]→[ʊu]→[əʊ] (Shakespeare's time)→[aʊ] (17th c.). **This diphthongization vacated the
extreme-high vowel slots**, letting every other long vowel raise one step into the space thus freed:
ME *ō* (*food*) [ɔː]→[uː] by 1500, taking the vacated *ū* slot; ME *ǭ* (*boat*, *hope*) [ɔː]→[oː] in
the 16th–17th centuries, later diphthongizing further in south-eastern England by c. 1800 to [əʊ] (the
early-20th-century further-shifted form); ME *ī̆* (mid front, *mice*/*fly* class already covered
above); ME *ē* (*green*, *field*) [eː]→[iː] by 1500 (taking the vacated *ī* slot); ME *ę̄* (*meat*,
*conceive*, *complete*) [ɛː]→[eː] during the 16th century, remaining phonemically distinct from the
*ē*-class throughout Shakespeare's lifetime (so *see* and *sea* were genuinely different vowels for
him, [siː] vs. [seː]) — but with ME *ā* (*dame*, *bake*) independently closing over the same century,
[aː]→[æː]→[ɛː] by c. 1600, the *ā*-class and the *ę̄*-class **converged and merged** by the later 17th
century, with the book citing explicit internal evidence (rhymes treating *sea, seize, dame, mate* as
sharing one vowel) for the merger actually happening in the standard language of the period — a
merger that does **not** describe present-day Standard English, where *meet* and *meat* now share one
vowel while *mate* has its own, separately-diphthongized value ([eɪ], reached via a further c. 1800
diphthongization of the merged [eː]). The book resolves this apparent contradiction (how could a
17th-century *ę̄*/*ā* merger exist if Modern English instead merges *ę̄* with *ē*?) by positing **two
competing styles of EModE pronunciation coexisting from as far back as Middle English** — a
conservative line continuing *ę̄* unmerged with *ā* (eventually supplying the Modern [iː] value via
merger with the *ē*-class instead) and an innovating line merging *ę̄* with *ā* — with the socially
"losing" variant specifically surviving today only as a small set of exceptional-spelling relics:
*break, great, steak, yea*, all spelled as if they belong to the *ę̄* class but pronounced with the
*ā*-class vowel, attributed by the book to social change (the rising middle classes "permeating the
gentry" and bringing some of their own pronunciation habits with them) rather than pure sound-law
regularity.

**Asymmetry and the "filling in" of a gap in the back-vowel series.** The Shift is explicitly
asymmetrical: four long front vowels raised/diphthongized in the chain above, but only three long
back vowels did, leaving (per Figure 11's vowel-quadrilateral diagram) an empty slot in the low-back
corner — which the book shows subsequently got filled independently: the ME diphthong *au* (*cause*,
*law*) monophthongized during the 16th century to a long low-back pure vowel [ɒː] (lengthened version
of the vowel in present-day *dog*), which then itself joined the general raising pattern, moving
closer to [ɔː] — i.e. a vowel entering the system late, via an unrelated diphthong-monophthongization
process, nonetheless got swept up into completing the Shift's overall symmetry.

**Diphthong changes parallel to, but distinct from, the main vowel-raising chain**: ME *ai* (*maid*,
*day*) → early 16th c. [ɛi] → merged with the raised ME *ā* by century's end, giving the shared Modern
vowel in *maid*/*made* (though, the book notes, this merger never completed in some dialects, notably
some varieties of Welsh English, which still distinguish *maid* from *made* today); ME *ou* (*soul*,
*know*) [ɔu]→[oː], merging with raised *ǭ*, giving the shared Modern vowel of *know*/*boat*; ME *iu*
(*new*, *use*) → c. 1600 either merged with raised *ū* giving [juː] generally, or, after certain
consonants, simplified directly to [uː] (*chew*, *June*, *rude* — merging with the *ō*-class, giving
the shared vowel of *rude*/*rood*); ME *oi* (*noise*, *royal*) is flagged as the **one diphthong that
stayed essentially unchanged** throughout both Middle and Early Modern English — and the book uses
this stability to make a separate, striking point: because ME long *ī* (in *line*) was busy
diphthongizing through a similar-sounding intermediate stage [əi]/[aɪ] during exactly this period, ME
*oi* (in *loin*) and ME *ī* (in *line*) would have sounded confusingly alike at some transitional
stages — meaning pairs like *loin*/*line*, *oil*/*isle*, *toil*/*tile* would have been genuine, if
temporary, homophones at some point during the Shift's progress, a real, datable ambiguity rather than
a modern reader's illusion.

**Short-vowel shortening and splits layered on top of the long-vowel shift.** Both Middle and Early
Modern English show sporadic shortening of originally-long vowels in monosyllables (especially before
a single consonant), producing persistent long/short doublets reflected directly in Modern spelling
(which, for words shortened early, still shows the historically-long vowel): ME *ę̄* shortened early
gives [ɛ] (*breath*, *bread*, *sweat*, *spread*); ME *ǭ* shortened early gives [ʊ], which (if the
shortening itself happened early, 16th c.) could go on to further lower to [ʌ] in southern varieties
(*blood*, *flood*) or (if the shortening happened later) stay at [ʊ] (*look*, *foot*) — i.e. **the
exact timing of an individual word's shortening, not just whether it shortened, determines its modern
vowel**, a genuinely fine-grained, word-by-word-datable mechanism. By contrast, the book notes the
**short** vowels themselves changed comparatively little overall — with one major exception, the
split of ME short *u* into two present-day phonemes, /ʌ/ (*cut*, *son*) and /ʊ/ (*pull*, *wolf*),
during the Early Modern period — this is the direct ancestor of the modern "northern vs. southern"
English accent split (southern varieties show the full [ʌ] change; most northern varieties retain
[ʊ]/[ʌ]-less pronunciation throughout, "one of the best-recognized distinctions" between the two
accent groups to this day). The book works through the minimal pair **luck**/**look** as a precise
dating tool: *luck* underwent the regular ME-*u*-to-[ʌ] shift in the early 17th century; *look*
(spelled with *oo*, originally a genuinely different, long vowel, ME *ō*, which had already regularly
become EModE [uː] before shortening in turn, later than *luck*'s shortening) only underwent its own
separate vowel-shortening (to [ʊ]) after the general [ʊ]→[ʌ] change had already finished — so *look*
never participated in and was never eligible for the change that produced *luck*'s vowel, and the two
words, once near-homophones via entirely different vowel histories, ended up **distinguished from one
another solely by which of the two now-independent phonemes /ʌ/ vs. /ʊ/ each one happens to carry.**

### Two new consonant phonemes emerge: /ŋ/ and /ʒ/

Extending the OE/ME allophone-to-phoneme-split pattern already traced in `pt03`/`pt05`: **[ŋ]**, an
OE/ME allophone of /n/ before /k/ or /g/ (see `pt03`), became an independent phoneme once
word-final [g] was lost after [ŋ] around 1600 (and earlier in some non-standard varieties) — *sing*
[sɪŋg] simplified to *sing* [sɪŋ], creating minimal pairs like *sing*/*sin* distinguished solely by
[ŋ] vs. [n]; [ŋg] is retained medially (*finger*), with *singing*/*singer*'s modern [ŋ]-only
pronunciation attributed to leveling influence from the uninflected base form *sing*. **[ʒ]** arose
in the 17th century from a sequence /zj/ → (16th c.) [vɪzjən] → (mid-17th c., coalescence of the
*-zj-* cluster) [vɪʒən] — initially restricted to **medial** position only, subsequently extended to
word-final position via later French loanwords (*rouge*, *massage*).

### Consonant losses during the period

In some positions, consonants were lost outright: initial /k-/ before /n/ in words like *knee*,
*knight* was lost by c. 1600; initial /g-/ before /n/ in *gnat*; initial /w-/ before /r/ in *write*.
/w/ was also lost before some back rounded vowels in words like *sword*, *who*, and at the start of
unstressed syllables (*answer*, *conquer*) — though in some of these it was later **restored** under
spelling influence (*swoon*, *awkward*). [ç] (palatal fricative, see `pt03`) was still pronounced in
words like *night* in the 16th century but had become [iː] in some speech varieties as early as Late
Middle English, giving the now-standard pronunciation; [x] (velar fricative) was likewise still
pronounced in words like *though*, *drought*, *daughter*, *rough* in the 16th century but was lost
round 1600 — in some eastern dialects it instead became [f] rather than disappearing outright, and
some of these [f]-pronunciations entered the standard language in the early 17th century, giving the
present-day pronunciation of *draught* and *rough*. Table 8.1 (p. 207) provides a comparative
three-column summary of Chaucer-era, Shakespeare-era, and present-day pronunciations across a dozen
representative words, directly illustrating the cumulative effect of the whole chapter's sound
changes in one place.

### Strong and weak word-forms, and the restressing cycle

The phonological changes above apply specifically to **stressed** syllables; words occurring in
**unstressed** position (notably function words like personal pronouns) underwent a different set of
changes — long-vowel shortening, vowel reduction to [ə]/[ɪ], and consonant loss or voicing — producing
pairs of **strong** (stressed-position) and **weak** (unstressed-position) alternative forms for the
same word, a pattern the book frames as ongoing throughout the whole history of English, not unique
to this period (citing the everyday Modern example that *and*, asked about in citation form, is almost
always answered "/ænd/," even though real connected speech overwhelmingly uses the weak forms /ənd/,
/ən/, or bare /n/). During Middle English specifically: word-initial [h] was lost in unstressed
syllables; word-final [θ], [f], [s] were voiced to [ð], [v], [z]; word-initial [θ] was also voiced —
explaining why the weak forms of *that*, *the*, *them*, *with*, *of*, *is* (all function words,
routinely unstressed) show voiced consonants descended from Middle English weak forms, not the
voiceless consonant the corresponding stressed/citation form might lead you to expect. The book's key
structural point: **a weak form can itself later be restressed into a brand-new strong form**, from
which a fresh weak form can then develop in turn, in an ongoing cycle — worked twice: OE strong *hit*
"it" produced a weak form *it*; *it* was then itself restressed and became the sole surviving strong
form (the original strong *hit* is now gone from Standard English entirely); and, separately, Late ME
strong *you* [juː] developed a weak form [jə]/[jo] (occasionally even rhyming with *vow* in 16th-c.
verse) which was then itself re-restressed and re-lengthened back to [juː], the present-day form —
with the historical layering made visible today via Shakespeare-era rhymes that depend on earlier
strong-form vowel values no longer audible in Modern pronunciation (*are*/*spare*, *have*/*grave*,
*is*/*miss*, *shall*/*fall*, *was*/*pass*, *were*/*bear*).

### Regional variation in Early Modern English: Scots printing conventions, the London prestige model, and stage dialect representation

Even as a single standard **written** form of English (emerged by the 15th century, see `pt05`) came
to dominate printed texts in England, Scotland's printed texts retained distinctive Scots features for
some time — *quh-* where English used *wh-* (*quhilk* "which"), *-it* rather than *-ed* for the past
tense, *-and* rather than *-ing* for the present participle (directly matching `pt05`'s Middle Scots
material) — before becoming "increasingly anglicized" following the 1603 Union of the Crowns. **Spoken**
regional variation, by contrast, remained far more variable than the written standard throughout the
period, and the book cites explicit period commentary documenting a contemporary awareness of
regional-accent prestige hierarchy already forming: George Puttenham's *The Arte of English Poesie*
(1589) recommends the aspiring poet use "the usuall speach of the Court, and that of London" (and the
Home Counties within roughly 60 miles), explicitly warning against both northern ("terms of
Northernmen") and far-western speech as insufficiently "courtly" or "current." The book carefully
notes what Puttenham is **not** claiming, however: he does not suggest regional speakers themselves
should change how they talk — only that this London/Court model is the *recommended register for
written/poetic composition*; it remained entirely possible for an upper-class speaker to retain a
regional accent without social penalty in this period (Sir Walter Raleigh reportedly kept his native
Devonshire accent even at Elizabeth's court) — a genuinely different, less stigmatizing social
situation than the "late modern period" pattern the book flags it will cover in the next chapter
(`pt07`), where regional accents become markedly more stigmatized and middle-class speakers outside
London specifically start aspiring to the "proper"/London norm.

The chapter's closing worked specimen is a scene from Shakespeare's *Henry V* depicting a Scot, a
Welshman, and an Irishman — all soldiers in Henry's army — conversing with each other and with an
Englishman (Gower), the only character whose speech is **not** marked as regionally "different,"
explicitly establishing his speech as the implicit norm against which the others are represented as
deviating. Specific stereotyped features the book catalogs from the passage: the Irish character's
speech substitutes written \<sh\> wherever Standard English would have \<s\> (*ish*, *tish*); the
Welsh character repeats the catchphrase "look you"; the Scots character's speech substitutes \<s\>
where Standard English would have \<sh\> (*sall* for *shall*) and uses \<a\> rather than \<o\> in
*bath* (presumably representing a distinctively Scots vowel). The book is careful to frame this
comic-but-not-derogatory: the three soldiers are **not** portrayed as inferior in competence or
character to their English counterpart, only as linguistically differentiated from the implicit norm
— but flags this specific kind of theatrical dialect representation as a direct precursor to a much
harsher development it will cover in the next chapter, where 18th-century writers specifically set
out to "correct" and standardize Irish, Scottish, and Welsh pronunciation by pointing out its
"faults" relative to the London norm — i.e. the comparatively benign, merely-differentiating stage
convention documented here is explicitly framed by the book as the historical seed of a later, more
openly normative and stigmatizing attitude toward regional English.

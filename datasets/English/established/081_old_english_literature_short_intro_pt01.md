# English — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Old English Literature: A Short Introduction, Part 1 (Chapters 1–2: "The Vow," "The Hall")

**Source:** Daniel Donoghue, *Old English Literature: A Short Introduction* (Blackwell Introductions
to Literature series, Blackwell Publishing, 2004), front matter + chapters 1–2, pp. ix–38 (PDF pages
1–38 of the source file).

**Coverage note:** This is a literary-critical survey aimed at students reading Old English texts in
translation — most of its pages are narrative plot summary, thematic/critical interpretation, and
manuscript/historical background with no linguistic content of their own. Per the dispatch
instructions, this extraction does **not** attempt comprehensive coverage of the book's prose; instead
it captures every genuinely linguistic item the author discusses along the way — Old English
vocabulary he glosses and analyzes (frequently in service of a close-reading argument), compound-word
analysis, dialectal/paleographic notes, and any remarks on grammar, semantics, or translation choice.
Despite the "lower linguistic yield expected" framing in the dispatch, the actual yield was
substantial: Donoghue is a philologist by training and the book is saturated with short glossed Old
English quotations in service of its literary arguments, many of which constitute real vocabulary or
compound-formation data. Pure literary-critical apparatus (narrative summary, authorship debates,
manuscript provenance, thematic argument, modern critical-theory terminology like "ekphrasis" or
"prosopopoeia") is paraphrased only where needed for context and is not tabulated as vocabulary. This
source has a confirmed clean text layer; no vision-reading was needed (`Vision Reading Confidence:
n/a` throughout).

## Grammar points

### *treow* as a true homograph, not a pun

The noun *treow* has two senses — "tree" and "troth/fidelity" — that look to a modern reader like a
coincidental pun, but the author points out (p.14) they in fact share an Indo-European root meaning
"to be firm, solid, steadfast." *The Husband's Message* exploits this deliberately: the speaking
object is literally a piece of wood (a rune-stick, grown from a *treo-cyn* "tree-kind") that swears
an oath (*treowe*) on behalf of its sender, and ends by invoking the couple's *wine-treow* "vow of
friendship." The semantic doubling is therefore historically motivated, not merely wordplay (pp.
13–15).

### Dual-number pronouns (*wit*, *unc*) marking reciprocal vows

Old English retains a grammatical dual number for first- and second-person pronouns (distinct from
both singular and plural), used for exactly two referents. *The Wife's Lament* uses the dual pronouns
*wit* ("we two") and *unc* ("us two," line 21–2) specifically at the point where the poem describes a
mutually exchanged pledge between the two lovers — the author argues the dual grammatically encodes
the reciprocity of the vow in a way a plural pronoun would not (p.11).

### Grammatical vs. natural gender: *monna* and the risk of editorial over-reading

*Monna*, a variant of *mann* "person, human being," carries no inherent reference to natural gender
in Old English — the word can refer to a man or a woman. Standard editions of *The Wife's Lament*
nonetheless gloss *monna* (line 18) as "man," which the author argues has led generations of readers
to wrongly assume the lyric's grammatically feminine "I" must be describing her male lover rather
than fashioning an account of her own troubled state of mind (pp. 11–12). This is presented as a
general methodological caution: an unmarked-for-gender OE noun can pick up spurious gender
connotations purely from translation convention, independent of anything in the Old English itself.

### Present-tense narrative immediacy in formulaic epithets (*wine-dryhten*, *gold-wine*, etc.)

A recurring observation across chapter 2 is that Anglo-Saxon epithets for a lord cluster heavily
around generosity and personal affection rather than abstract power — *gold-wine* "gold-friend,"
*gold-gyfa* "gold-giver," *wine-dryhten* "friend-lord" are described as "about as common as any other
epithets for a lord" (p.49), which the author uses to argue that Anglo-Saxon society did not frame
the lord–retainer bond as emotionally asymmetrical the way a modern reader might assume from its
political hierarchy.

### *forþon* as a logical connective for an avowedly non-logical impulse

*The Seafarer* line 64 uses the connective *forþon* ("therefore") to link the cry of a seabird to the
speaker's own compulsion to return to the sea — a causal/logical particle applied to an urge the poem
itself frames as irrational. The author flags this as "a crucial if inexplicable turn of syntax" (p.
51), worth noting as an example of how OE's inventory of logical connectives could be used
rhetorically rather than strictly propositionally.

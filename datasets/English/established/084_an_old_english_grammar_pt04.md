## Grammar points

### §94. General orientation to the Syntax chapters

The syntax chapters are explicitly not a systematic description of OE syntax as a whole — relatively minor features are left to be treated as *ad hoc* exceptions via editors' notes, in order to leave room for the major patterns. Where OE usage aligns closely with Mod.E. usage, the authors draw the parallel explicitly; attention is drawn, as occasion arises, to possible Latin influence and to differences between prose and poetry.

### §95–96. Nominative and Accusative

The **nominative** is the case of activity — subject of verbs (*hē sǣde* 'he said'), also used for the complement of the subject with copula-type verbs (*sē wæs betera þonne ic* 'he was better than I'), for the subject of ellipted verbs after *þonne/þonne* ('than') and *būtan* ('except'), and for direct address (no separate vocative inflexion exists). *Hātan* 'to call' takes the nominative even for its grammatical object when the referent is being *named* (*on þǣm dæge þe wē hātað Hlāfmæsse* 'on the day that we call Lammas' — accusative would be *-an*).

The **accusative**, in direct antithesis to the nominative, is sometimes called the "passive case" — the referent is something done to, as object of most transitive verbs' direct object. Direct-object reflexive pronouns occur (*hiene bestæl se here* 'the raiders stole away'); other accusative-reflexive verbs include *onmunan* 'care for', *onscunian* 'be afraid of', *restan* 'rest', *war(e)nian* 'take warning', *wendan* 'go'. Impersonal verbs may be construed with an accusative object (*hine nānes ðinges ne lyste* 'he desired nothing'). The object of a verb can be cognate with it (*singað…song nēowne* 'sing a new song') or infinitival with its own accusative subject. A small number of verbs take **two accusative objects** (*þā ācsode man hine hwylcne cræft hē cūðe* 'then someone asked him what skill he professed'); usually, however, two objects with a single verb appear in different cases (verbs of depriving/requesting/accusing commonly take accusative-of-person + genitive-of-thing; verbs of telling/answering typically take accusative-of-thing + dative-of-person).

### §97–98. Accusative: adverbial and prepositional uses

The accusative states extent of **space** or **time** (*ne flēon fōtes trym* 'not flee the space of a foot'; *ealle þā hwile þe wē hīe on þǣm sæton* 'the whole time that body lay inside'). In *hām* 'homewards' it is used for direction: *āris and gecyrr hām* 'arise and go home'. As in other IE languages, many prepositions implying movement/destination take the accusative when motion is involved (listed: *fore, geond, in, ofer, on, ongēan, þurh, wið, ymbe, for*), and also take the dative when the situation is static (though the case-choice is not fully consistent with this mobile-static distinction).

### §99–102. Genitive: functional complexity

The genitive is described as a case of unusually complex functions, resistant to clean classification since real examples often admit more than one reading and the categorisation itself imposes somewhat artificial divisions. Two primary usage groups are distinguished: **subjective** (active) and **objective** (passive) genitive — e.g. *his* in "his murder" is subjective or objective according to whether the referent did the killing or was killed. The **subjective genitive** (§100) is common and idiomatic, closely associated with the **possessive genitive** and the genitive of **origin**; here too belongs the **instrumental genitive** (as in *nīða ofercumen* 'overcome by afflictions'). The **objective genitive** (§101) is illustrated along with closely related sub-categories: genitive of **measure** (*fōtes trym*, *ānes mōnðes fyrst* 'the space of one month'), **descriptive/defining genitive** (*in Myrcna mægðe* 'among the people of the Mercians', *mǣres līfes man* 'a man of glorious life'), and **partitive genitive** (*wundres dæl* 'small wonder', *sum hund scipa* 'a hundred ships', *fela tācna* 'many signs', *hūsa sēlest* 'best of houses', *ānra gehwelc* 'each one', *ān heora* 'one of them'). *Fela* and *sum* sometimes appear without a genitive at all; a late-OE periphrastic alternative with *of* + dative begins to supplant the plain genitive in some of these uses.

### §102–103. Genitive: adverbial and prepositional

The **adverbial genitive** (§102) is related to the objective genitive, as in *dæges ond nihtes* 'by day and night', *Godes þonces* 'through God's grace', *wordes oððe dǣde* 'by word or deed', *ealles* 'entirely'. This usage underlies the genitive inflexion (particularly *-es*) in the formation of certain adverbs (§166), and the generalisation of masc./neut. gen.sg. for adverbial purposes even where the normal gen.sg. of a fem. noun would be *-e*. No preposition in OE takes the genitive exclusively — only *tō*, especially with reference to time, takes this case at all (§104): *andlang þæs fūlan brōces* 'along the dirty stream', *tō þæs þe* 'until', *tō hwilces tīman* 'at what time', *wið…þæs spearcan tungon* 'towards the sparks of the roof'.

A substantial list of verbs regularly or contextually take a genitive object functionally related to the objective-genitive categories (§103) — many can be classed as taking a **partitive** genitive (*brūcan* 'enjoy'), a **descriptive** genitive (*fægnian* 'rejoice'), or simply by convention: the grammar lists roughly 50 such verbs rather than attempting an exhaustive functional classification (see Vocabulary table above for the full list extracted, including *āmyrran, bedǣlan, belīðan, bīdan, blissian, brūcan, (ge)cunnian, ēhtan, fægnian, gefeōn, gȳman, hēdan, hogian, latian, nēotan, ofhrēowan, onfōn, onmunan, reccan, strȳnan, swīcan, twēo(ga)n, þurfan, wealdan, wēnan, wilnian, wundrian*, and more). Some verbs governing two objects take genitive **and** accusative (*geǣmeti(gi)an, biddan*); others take genitive and dative (*lettan* 'hinder', *gelystan* 'desire' (impers.), *sc(e)amian* 'shame' (impers.)); others govern genitive alone but with accusative or dative alternatives depending on sense (*geunnan, forwyrnan, ofþyncan, onlēon, gestȳran, tilian, tīðian, gewanian, wyrnan, þancian*).

### §104. Genitive with prepositions

No preposition in OE takes the genitive exclusively, and only *tō* takes this case at all (especially with reference to time): *andlang þæs fūlan brōces* 'along the dirty stream', *tō þæs þe/tō hwilces tīman* 'until/at what time', *wið* 'towards' (meaning 'in place of' — i.e. the preposition's sense shifts when governing the genitive).

### §105–113. Dative (and instrumental)

Like the genitive, the OE dative's functions are very complex, partly because this case came to absorb most of the functions of the old **instrumental** case in addition to those of the dative proper (distinct instrumental forms remain only at §§48, 50, 65f). The dative's uses are organized under the following headings:

**Dative object** (§106) — frequently concerned with *sharing*, most clearly seen where the dative is used for the indirect (personal) object with transitive verbs (*þe him hringas geaf* 'who gave him rings'). A large list of intransitive verbs (cognates of which are often transitive in Mod.E., e.g. 'help', 'answer', 'follow') are construed with a dative as their sole object, including with impersonal verbs and reflexive use with other verbs (see the extensive list in the Vocabulary table above: *ætwindan, ætwītan, ārian, bedrēosan, beōdan, bet(e)ćan, bodian, gebiddan, (ge)dafenian, dēman, derian, frīnan, gefrignan, gefremman, fulgān, fylgan, gelȳfan, lician, miltsian, mislimpan, oftēon, onfōn, sǣlan, sceððan, gespōwan, þegnian, þeowian, pingian, pyncan, wealdan, wīsian, gewītan, wiðstandan*, and more). Verbs construed with both a dative and accusative-reflexive object include *ālēogan, ālȳfan, becweðan, oðpringan, oðwendan, pingian*. In Late OE, *tō* came to be used with the indirect object much as in Mod.E., with a few verbs (notably *cweðan* and *sprecan*) taking *tō* as normal OE practice even earlier.

**Possessive dative** (§108) — a frequent pattern has (though not necessarily in this order) subject–verb–(object)–preposition–noun, where the noun is defined by a noun/pronoun in the dative, usually called 'possessive dative': *hē…sette his…hond him on þæt hēafod* 'he placed his hand on his head'. A special case occurs with the preposition *tō*: *þone Godes sende folce tō frōfre* 'whom God sent as comfort to the people'; but note the use of the genitive instead in *Beowulf* 1830: *hæleþa tō helpe* 'as help to the heroes'.

**Locative dative** (§109) — place is rarely indicated by the dative without a preposition, but there are examples, e.g. in *Beowulf*: *wīcum wunian* 'to live in the dwelling'.

**Temporal dative** (§110) — used in expressions like *hwīlum* 'at times', *sumum dæge* 'on a certain day' (or with the instrumental inflexion, *sume dæge; þȳ dōgore* 'on that day'); often alternates with equivalent prepositional phrases.

**Dative Absolute** (§111) — an idiom modelled directly on the Latin ablative absolute, not very frequent in OE: *gefultumigendum Gode* (Lat. *Deō favente*) 'with God helping'; *him sprecende hī cōmon* (Lat. *eō loquente venientēs*) 'while He was speaking, they came'.

**Instrumental function** (§112) — expressed through the dat./instr. inflexion, very important in OE and among the most difficult for present-day English speakers to understand; broadly covers **means** or **manner** of an action (*hondum gebrōden* 'hand-woven'; *mundum brugdon* 'you brandished with your hands'), including the characteristic **comitative** function ('together with'): *worhte Ælfred cyning lȳtle werede geweorc* 'King A. built a defence-work with a small force'. Prepositions are also used instrumentally: *ercde mid horsum* 'ploughed with horses'. Through their ready acceptance as indications of means/manner, the endings *-e* and *-um* (usually with adjectives and nouns respectively) came to be widely used in the formation of adverbs (see §166).

**Two uses of the instrumental inflexion specifically** (§113): **causal** expressions (*ðȳ hē þone fēond ofercwōm* 'therefore he overcame the enemy', *hwȳ sceal ic…ðēowian?* 'why must I serve?'); and the "the more the merrier" comparison-of-comparatives pattern (*þȳ mā…ðȳ læs* 'the more…the less').

**Adjectival dative** (§114) — numerous adjectives (generally signifying nearness or an emotional relationship) take the dative: *gelic wæs hē þǣm lēohtum steorrum* 'he was like the bright stars'; so also *nēah* 'near', *lēof* 'dear', *hold* 'loyal', *nȳdbedearf* 'necessary', and several others. Comparatives sometimes take the dative but are more usually followed by *þonne* and the nominative.

**Prepositional dative** (§115) — the chief case used with prepositions: *æfter* 'after', *ǣr* 'before', *bī/be* 'beside', *betwēonan* 'between', *būtan* 'without', *fram* 'from, by', *mid* 'with', *tō* 'to, for'. Several prepositions taking the accusative when there is motion take the dative when there is none (*ofer* 'beyond', *on* 'in, on', *under* 'under', *wið* 'opposite, against, with'). In some frequently recurring phrases (*æt hām* 'at home', *tōdæg* 'today'), the dative inflexion was dropped quite early in the OE period.

### §116–120. Noun Modifiers and Pronouns

**Adjectives** (§116): the **indefinite declension** is used in general — when the adjective is predicative, and when no attempt is made to specify/particularise the item modified. In practice, used when not preceded by a demonstrative or when no other reason calls for the definite form. By contrast, the **definite declension** (§117) is the "specifying and particularising" form, used regularly after demonstratives (whether the adjective precedes or follows the noun, or the noun is used substantivally). When two adjectives qualify one noun, one may precede and one follow, or both may precede (linked by *and*), or both follow the noun (especially when they concern quantity).

**Demonstratives and Articles** (§117): the two OE demonstratives *se (þæt, sēo)* and *þes (þis, þēos)* can be defined respectively as "specifying" and "deictic." *Se* merely particularises/singles out from the generality, while *þes* points to and singles out a part of a series, the whole of which may already be specific. The existence of a "definite article" in OE is "a vexed question" — today Mod.E. has three contrastive defining words, *the, that, this*; OE has only two, *se* and *þes*, with *se* embracing practically the whole range of functions performed jointly or separately by Mod.E. *the* and *that*. There are many contexts, especially in verse, where Mod.E. *the* has no corresponding form necessary in OE (§118: poetry shows markedly less frequent use of *se* before nouns than prose does).

**Indefinite article** (§119): nothing truly corresponding to an "indefinite article" is common in OE verse; *ān* is usually a numeral, and where it is not purely numeral it mostly shares with Mod.E. 'a certain' (strong indefiniteness) rather than the weak indefiniteness of Mod.E. 'a(n)'. *Sum* with "strong indefiniteness" is similarly used. For the bulk of OE usage, the function of 'indefinite article' (as contrasted with *se, þæt, sēo*) was expressed by **zero**, exactly as with plurals in Mod.E. ('I like reading books but the books must be readable').

**Pronouns** (§120): (a) the two demonstratives *se* and *þes* are used pronominally as contrastive deictics, and also appear to have been used exactly as personal pronouns. (b) The most frequent **relative** pronoun was the invariable particle *þe*, sometimes preceded by the relevant form of *se*; the relative could also be zero, as in the Mod.E. expression "There's a man stands at the corner every night." (c) The **reflexive** function was performed by the simple personal pronouns (*se cyning hine…wende* 'the king turned himself'); *self* was used in OE simply for emphasis, not as a reflexive-marking or pronoun-enclitic sign. (d) Personal pronouns had little that is distinctively OE except their relative freedom to be **omitted** when they are the implicit subject of a second of two parallel constructions (a freedom Mod.E. largely lacks: 'we work and (we) sleep' sounds odd in Mod.E. but the equivalent OE omission is natural). (e) An OE construction all but unparalleled in Mod.E. is the **impersonal** verb with which there regularly is no expressed subject (*hine nānes ðinges ne lyste* 'it desired him of nothing', i.e. 'he desired nothing'). In late OE, however, *hit* comes to be used as the subject of such expressions, and *hit* is also found with the increasingly used periphrastic passive (§131): *is hyt ālȳfed* 'is it allowed…?'. Instead of this periphrastic passive, OE more generally uses the indefinite pronoun *man* (as in *þe mon hǣt* 'which is called', a formula often used to translate Latin *vocātur, dīcuntur*, and other passives). (f) The **indefinite pronouns** *gehwā, gehwylc* 'every, each', *hwylc* 'any', etc. commonly take the genitive plural (*on mǣgþa gehwǣm* 'in every tribe', *ǣlcum menn* 'to each man').

### §121–126. Concord

Grammatical agreement was important in OE structure, marking the relationship between words sharing inflexional distinctions of number, person, case, and gender. Concord existed between: **(a) subject and verb** (number and person); **(b) demonstratives, adjectives, and nouns** (number, case, and gender); **(c) pronouns and their designata** (number, case, and gender — note that a feminine singular pronoun can agree with a noun referring to something non-human-female purely by grammatical gender, e.g. *rōd*, feminine, "overgrown" agreeing with a feminine pronoun); **(d) pronouns and their modifiers** (number, case, and gender). Regarding (a) and (c), the **dual** number (1st/2nd person pronouns only; see §63) corresponds in concord with the **plural** in other respects.

§122 amplifies the norm: strict case-agreement is expected in appositive expressions (*wæs hē sē mon…geseted* 'he, this man, was placed'); appositive phrases of the pattern "called X" do not require concord (*fram Brytta cyninge, Ceadwalla gecīged* 'from the king of the Britons, called C.'); nor, in other cases, is concord invariable (*Ic on Higelāce wāt, Gēata dryhten* 'I know as to H., the lord of the Geats').

§123 treats past participles with copula verbs ('be', 'become'): often agreeing with the subject, but more usually invariable; forms of *habban* 'have' are normally invariable in the participle, but sometimes agree with the object (*hīe hine ofslægenne hæfdon* 'they had slain him') — probably, no doubt, a survival of the older use of this construction before it came to be used as a 'pluperfect' tense (see §128).

§124: strict concord in grammatical gender is the rule, particularly regular with demonstratives and adjectives, though adjectives tended to have a one-gender plural even for a mixed-gender group (*earme wīf* 'wretched wives', neut. concord despite *wīf* itself being neuter but the modifying adjective expected to follow neuter pattern regardless of actual referents' sex). Grammatical gender is on the whole regular also with pronouns, but pronouns — particularly when relating to human beings — are sometimes used with natural gender in spite of the normal requirements of grammatical concord (*þæt mæden*…*hēo wearð* 'the maiden (neut.)…she (fem.) became').

§125: analogous to the use in Late OE of *hit* as the subject of impersonal verbs (§120e) is the widespread use of **neuter singulars** (*hit, þæt, þis, hwæt*), without regard to gender or number, before the verb 'to be' or in contexts where the designatum is a statement, fact, or event: *þæt wǣron…Finnas* 'they were Lapps', *þæt wǣron gesweostor* 'who were sisters'.

§126: number-concord between subject and verb shows some variation. A verb is often singular when it precedes a plural subject, especially a compound one (*þǣr sceal bēon gedrync and plega* 'there shall be drinking and merrymaking'); but the verb is not always singular in this position — instances with a plural subject preceding a singular verb are rare. Indefinite pronouns and collective nouns caused much conflict between grammatical and logical concord (*rīdeð ǣlc…and hyt mōtan habban* 'each rides and can (pl.) have it' — logical plural sense winning out over the grammatically singular form of *ǣlc*).

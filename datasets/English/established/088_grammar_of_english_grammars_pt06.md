## Grammar points

### Opening definitions (Ch. I, "Sentences")

**Syntax** treats of the *relation* (dependence according to sense), *agreement* (similarity in person, number,
gender, case, mood, tense, or form), *government* (one word's power to force a modification on another), and
*arrangement* (word order) of words in sentences. A **sentence** is an assemblage of words making complete
sense and always containing a nominative and a verb, with three usual principal parts — subject/nominative,
attribute/finite verb, object — plus subordinate adjuncts. Sentences are **simple** (one assertion/supposition/
command/question/exclamation) or **compound** (two or more simple sentences connected); a **clause** or
**member** is a sentence-sized subdivision of a compound sentence; a **phrase** is two or more words
expressing a relation of ideas but no entire proposition.

Brown states the governing design of the whole Syntax part up front (General Obs. 1–9): he organizes his 24
rules by part of speech (one chapter-worth of rules per part of speech, mirroring the Etymology part's
structure) rather than by the traditional two-part "concord and government" scheme most contemporaries
used — and he spends several pages cataloguing contemporary grammars' rules that he judges genuinely
broken: rules that collapse government and agreement into the same sentence ("the nominative case governs
the verb in number and person" — which, he argues, misuses "govern" for what is actually agreement), rules
claiming interjections "govern" an objective first-person pronoun and a nominative second-person one (which
he calls flatly false, countering with Dryden's "O wretched *we*!"), and rules that improperly merge the syntax
of two or three different parts of speech into one undifferentiated statement. This critique-of-contemporary-
rule-design throughout Part III is a direct continuation of the same project begun in the Introduction (`pt01`)
and carried through Etymology.

### Rule I — Articles

**"Articles relate to the nouns which they limit."** Two stated exceptions: (1) the definite article used
intensively can relate to a comparative/superlative adjective or adverb ("*the* farther they proceeded, *the*
greater..."); (2) the indefinite article can give collective force to a plural-looking number word ("a *thousand*
things," "a *hundred* men"). Extensive Observations work through real, still-relevant style questions: a shared
article implies one shared noun across coordinated adjectives ("the grave rebuke the gay" = the grave *people*
... the gay *people*), while a repeated article implies separate referents; relative position of article vs.
adjective is often non-arbitrary (*both the men* vs. *the two men*; *half a dollar* vs. *a half dollar*); which
pronominal adjectives exclude the article (*any, each, every, no, some, this, that*), precede it (*all, both,
many, such, what*), or follow it (*few, former, first, latter, last, one, other, same*); and *a*/*an* choice before
historically-h-initial words (*an house, an hundred* in older Bible English vs. modern *a house, a hundred*) as
a real diachronic shift Brown documents directly. **Notes to Rule I** (17 numbered sub-rules) cover repeat-
vs-share logic for the article across coordinated nouns/adjectives in detail — e.g. "a black and a white horse"
(two horses) vs. "a black and white horse" (one piebald horse); correspondence requirements across paired
*neither...nor* / *both...and* constructions; the rule against using the article before names of virtues/vices/
sciences in a general sense (*"Falsehood is odious," not "The falsehood..."*); and the rule that a participle
converted fully into a noun requires the article ("the completing of this") while a participle kept as a participle
must not take one ("completing this," not "the completing this").

### Rule II — Nominatives

**"A Noun or a Pronoun which is the subject of a finite verb, must be in the nominative case."** Brown states
this rule has *no exceptions* — nearly all nominatives fall under it, with only four possible functions overall
(subject of a verb; apposition, Rule III; non-transitive same-case construction, Rule VI; absolute, Rule VIII).
A genuinely useful catalogued list: **nine situations where the subject is normally placed *after* the verb**
(or after the first auxiliary) rather than before it — (1) questions without an interrogative pronoun subject
("Shall mortals be implacable?"), (2) the imperative mood ("Go thou"), (3) strong wishes/feeling ("May she be
happy!"), (4) if-less suppositions ("Had they known it" = "If they had known it"), (5) *neither*/*nor*
meaning "and not" leading the clause, (6) emphatic fronting ("Here am I"), (7) an unaccompanied emphatic
verb ("Echo the mountains round"), (8) dialogue-introducing verbs like *say, answer, reply*, (9) introductory
*there* ("There lived a man"). Also notable: an extended, genuinely still-live discussion (Obs. 5–12) of
whether an objective pronoun can stand as the "logical subject" of a following infinitive ("I believe your
*brother* to be a good man") — Brown argues the objective is still governed by the preceding transitive verb,
not newly independent as "subject of the infinitive," rejecting several contemporaries' Latin-calqued
"accusative before the infinitive" analysis as inapplicable to English's actual syntax.

### Rule III — Apposition

**"A Noun or a personal Pronoun used to explain a preceding noun or pronoun, is put, by apposition, in the
same case."** No true exceptions, but Brown works through many edge cases (18 Observations): multi-word
proper names/titles should generally be parsed together rather than split into adjective+noun (he criticizes
several contemporaries' grammars for calling "George" an adjective modifying "Washington"); when two
possessives are in apposition, the possessive *-'s* marker goes on only the noun immediately preceding the
governed noun, never on both ("for David my *servant's* sake," not "David's my servant's"); a noun can be in
apposition with an entire preceding clause/sentence, serving to summarize it ("I have offended reputation — a
most unnoble swerving"); and the reciprocal pronoun pairs **each other** (properly for two entities) and **one
another** (properly for more than two) are analyzed as internally case-split — the first element effectively
nominative/apposed, the second objective (so "they love each other" parses as "each [nominative,
apposed]... loves... the other [objective]").

### Rule IV — Possessives

**"A Noun or a Pronoun in the possessive case, is governed by the name of the thing possessed."** Brown
flags this as containing some of the genuinely hardest live disputes in the whole book (apposition of
possessives; possessive before *is*/*was* in bare ownership statements; a possessive governed by a participle
acting partly as a noun). **Notes to Rule IV** (5 sub-rules): use the correct possessive form per number
(*John's, men's, hers, its, ours* — never *Johns, mens', her's, it's, our's*, a spelling-norm point still relevant
today); in coordinated/appositive possessives, the *-'s* goes only on the term(s) immediately before the
governing noun ("John and *Eliza's* teacher," not "John's and Eliza's"); the periphrastic *of*-construction
("the will of man") is a legitimate alternative to the inflected possessive, chosen for euphony/variety; a noun
governing a possessive plural shouldn't be forced into agreement-plural against its own sense ("for our
*part*," not "for our *parts*," when one shared part is meant — though Brown notes figurative collective
singulars like "their face," "our mouth" are genuinely idiomatic in Scripture usage, not errors); and the
possessive should not be used needlessly before a participle not otherwise functioning as a full noun.

### Rule V — Objectives (of active-transitive verbs/participles)

**"A Noun or a Pronoun made the object of an active-transitive verb or participle, is governed by it in the
objective case."** **Notes to Rule V** (8 sub-rules) are genuinely useful transitivity/voice guidance: a verb
that requires an object shouldn't be used without one (and vice versa — *"the planters grow cotton"* should be
*raise* or *cultivate*, since *grow* in Brown's usage norm doesn't take this kind of object); a passive verb's
subject should be the *direct* object of the corresponding active verb, not an indirect one ("this privilege *was
denied me*," not "I *was denied* this privilege" — flagging exactly the double-object-passivization pattern
that is now completely standard in modern English, a genuine diachronic shift); passive verbs and perfect
participles (without an active auxiliary) should never themselves govern an objective, since by definition they
denote an action terminating *on* the subject, not extending *from* it; and — a small but concrete textual-
criticism point — in an accurately printed King James Bible, *ye* is reserved for the nominative and *you* for
the objective, so any biblical quotation blurring that distinction is textually suspect.

### Rule VI — Same Cases (non-transitive verb/participle linking two same-referent terms)

**"A Noun or a Pronoun put after a verb or participle not transitive, agrees in case with a preceding noun or
pronoun referring to the same thing."** This is Brown's rule for predicate nominatives/complements after
copular and intransitive-linking verbs (as opposed to true apposition, Rule III, or true object-government,
Rule V). **Notes to Rule VI** (2 sub-rules): avoid putting a noun in an unclear/unanalyzable case after a
participle or participial noun when a cleaner clausal paraphrase is available ("I had a suspicion that the fellow
*was* a swindler," not "...of the fellow's *being* a swindler"); and the predicate noun after a copular verb
should match its subject in sense, literally or figuratively — "to be convicted of bribery was... a crime" is
flagged as wrong because *being convicted of* a crime is not itself the crime.

### Rule VII — Objectives (of prepositions)

**"A Noun or a Pronoun made the object of a preposition, is governed by it in the objective case."** No true
exceptions in Brown's view, but he spends substantial space on real edge cases: prepositions can take a
participle (not just a noun/pronoun) as object, and *to* very often takes a bare infinitive; elliptical adverbial
phrases after a preposition where an implied noun has dropped out (*in vain* = "in a vain *manner*"); nouns
of time/measure/distance/value that take an implied, unstated preposition ("we rode sixty miles *that
day*" = "...on that day"); and a genuinely unresolved period debate (Obs. 8–9) over what part of speech
**worth** is in constructions like "worth a shilling" / "worth preserving" — noun-with-ellipsis, adjective, or
(Brown's own preferred analysis) a true preposition, traced etymologically to the Saxon verb *weorthan*, "to
be/become" (the same root he also attributes to *by*).

### Rule VIII — Nominative Absolute

**"A Noun or a Pronoun is put absolute in the nominative, when its case depends on no other word."** Brown
criticizes the common contemporary practice of splitting "nominative absolute" from "nominative
independent" as if they were different things, and instead gives **four unified sub-cases**, all genuinely useful
as a structural taxonomy:
1. **With a participle**, expressing cause or an accompanying circumstance: "*Pontius Pilate being*
   governor of Judea... Herod *being* tetrarch of Galilee..." (often paraphrasable as a *when/while/if/since/
   because*-clause).
2. **By direct address** (vocative use — English has no distinct vocative case, so a directly-addressed noun
   is simply nominative-absolute): "At length, *Seged*, reflect and be wise."
3. **By pleonasm**, fronted abruptly for emphasis and not functioning as subject or object of any verb:
   "He that hath, *to him* shall be given"; "Gad, a troop shall overcome *him*" (where *Gad* itself stands
   absolute, resumed by *him*).
4. **By mere exclamation**, with no surrounding syntax at all: "O the depth of the riches... of God!"

Brown also flags (Obs. 6) a contemporary dissenter — Cobbett, addressing "soldiers, sailors, apprentices, and
ploughboys" in his own grammar — who rejected the entire absolute-nominative category outright as
incoherent, which Brown rebuts at length as self-contradictory in Cobbett's own examples.

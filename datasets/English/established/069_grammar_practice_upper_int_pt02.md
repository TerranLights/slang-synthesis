## Grammar points

### Simple and continuous aspects (general)

Verb forms, and also infinitives, can take either the simple or continuous aspect; the book
illustrates this with parallel infinitive/modal constructions — bare infinitive vs. continuous
infinitive with *to* ("too young to play" / "too young to be playing"), and modal + infinitive vs.
modal + continuous infinitive ("shouldn't work" / "shouldn't be working") (p.27). The simple aspect
views an action or series of actions as a whole, signaling that the action is complete and/or that
the situation is permanent (or regarded as such); the continuous aspect views the action in
progress, at some point between its beginning and end, signaling incompleteness and/or
temporariness. Both aspects can be used with dynamic verbs, but the continuous aspect is
specifically incompatible with stative verbs, since it describes an activity in progress and
stative verbs do not describe activities (p.27).

### Stative and dynamic verbs

Dynamic verbs describe actions; stative verbs describe a state of being rather than an activity,
and the book organizes the stative class into five named subcategories: feelings, thinking/
believing, wants and preferences, perceptions and the senses, possession, and being/seeming (p.29).
Critically, several verbs that are normally stative can be used dynamically with an accompanying
shift in meaning, and the book works through four such pairs in detail (p.29): *hope* (wish, stative)
vs. *be hoping* (a more deliberate, in-progress wishing); *think* (hold an opinion, stative) vs. *be
thinking* (the activity of considering something, as in "What are you thinking about?"); *be*
(describes a permanent characteristic, stative) vs. *be being* (describes current behavior/how
someone is acting at the moment, as in "He's being very foolish"); and *taste* (describes the
sensory effect, stative) vs. *be tasting* (the deliberate activity of sampling, as in "Amy's tasting
the wine to see if it's all right"). This confirms a general mechanism: the continuous form of a
normally-stative verb reinterprets it as describing a deliberate, temporary, in-progress activity
rather than a state.

### The perfect aspect

The perfect aspect signals that an event took place before the time being referred to, or that it
covered a period of time up to that reference time, and/or that the event has continuing relevance
to the reference time — and this logic is explicitly parallel across all three perfect tenses
(p.31): present perfect ("John's left" = left before now, is not here now), past perfect ("John had
left when we got there" = left before our arrival, wasn't there when we arrived), and future perfect
("John will have left when we get there" = will leave before our arrival). The infinitive can also
carry a perfect aspect ("He is sure to have been there" = it is certain that he has been/was there),
and modals can be followed by either a plain infinitive or a perfect infinitive, with the perfect
infinitive shifting the time reference into the past relative to the modal's own implied present
("Pam may tell him the news" vs. "Pam may have told him the news" = it is possible that she has
already told him) (p.31).

### Present simple

Form: base verb (with *-s* in third person singular); *do/does* + infinitive for questions,
negatives, and short answers (p.33). The book catalogs eight distinct uses (pp.33–34): a regular
repeated activity (often paired with frequency adverbs: *sometimes, never, occasionally, every day,
on Saturdays, once a week*); a generally true statement of fact; stative-verb states ("Adrian needs
your help"); instructions (alternating with the imperative); in time/condition clauses referring to
the future, specifically after *when, if, unless, before, after, until, as soon as, whenever*
("I'll give her the message when she comes back" — not a future-tense form despite the future
reference); introducing a quotation ("This book says that..."); newspaper headlines (for compactness:
"RIOTS BREAK OUT IN CITY CENTRE"); dramatic narrative, specifically live sports commentary describing
an action as it completes ("And Eastwood passes the ball to Andrews"); and to give information about
a scheduled future event — timetables, official events, itineraries ("The train leaves at 3.30 this
afternoon").

### Present continuous

Form: *to be* + present participle (p.34). Four uses are given: an activity in progress right now;
an activity occurring within the current time period that will continue for a limited time but may
not be happening at this exact instant (typically paired with *these days, this week, today, this
month* — the book stresses the activity "may or may not be in progress at the moment of speaking");
a situation that is in the process of changing ("Venice is falling into the sea"; "The number of
cars...is increasing rapidly"); and a future intention expressed with a verb of motion ("We're
driving up to Scotland next week").

### Past simple

Form: past-tense verb form, regular or irregular, for positive statements; *did* + infinitive for
negative/question forms; *did* as the short-answer form (p.37). Three uses: a completed action or
event in the past, whether recent or distant, with the time stated or understood from context; a
past habit or regularly repeated/habitual event, or a past situation/habit of some duration ("She
worked there for twenty years"); and a past situation at a specific point in time, particularly with
stative verbs ("In 1950, there were fewer than 50 million cars...").

### Past continuous

Form: *was/were* + present participle (p.38). Five uses, each with a worked example (pp.38): in
conjunction with the past simple, to describe an action in progress when another past-simple event
occurred ("They were watching a film when we arrived"); an action/situation in progress at a
specified time in the past ("In 1982, we were living in a small flat in Bristol"); paired with
*while* to show two actions in progress simultaneously; paired with *all*-adverbials (*all night, all
morning, all day yesterday*) to emphasize an action's continuity/duration throughout a stretch of
time; and to set the scene/background for a past narrative.

### Contrast: past continuous versus past simple

The book devotes an explicit contrastive subsection to this pair, reiterating that stative verbs
normally cannot take the continuous form at all (cross-referencing p.29), then works through four
minimal pairs to make the aspectual distinction concrete (p.39): "He was cooking the dinner when I
arrived" (cooking began before arrival, in progress at that point) vs. "He cooked the dinner when I
arrived" (two sequential complete events — arrival, then cooking); "I was reading a book...last
night" (reading in progress, book not necessarily finished) vs. "I read a book...last night" (the
whole book, start to finish); "It was raining all night" (continuity/duration emphasized) vs. "It
rained all night" (one single complete event, start to end of night); and "I was having a bath at 8
o'clock" (bath already in progress at 8, continuing) vs. "I had a bath at 8 o'clock" (the whole bath
event, starting at 8).

**Source:** Dennis Freeborn, *From Old English to Standard English*, Chapter 11 "Modern English — the
eighteenth century" (pp.180–195). **Confirmed scan-only**, vision-read (see pt01 for the shared

**Coverage note:** by this chapter the book's own framing shifts explicitly from *linguistic* change
to the **social ideology of standardization** — prescriptivism, the "corruption"/"perfection"
metaphor, and language as an explicit marker and instrument of class division. This is extracted in
full as Grammar points, since it is the chapter's real content (argued commentary on primary texts)
rather than linguistic data to tabulate. William Cobbett's self-taught-grammar narrative is also
extracted in detail — it is the book's most direct, class-conscious case study of exactly how
"standard"/"correct" English functioned as a social gatekeeping mechanism in this period, of obvious
relevance to any slang/register analysis.

## Grammar points

### Prescriptivism's founding metaphor: language as subject to "corruption" and achievable "perfection"

The chapter identifies "**corruption**" as the single most recurrent word in 18th-century writing about
the state of English, and traces the underlying (contested) belief that a language can be brought to,
and then permanently fixed at, a state of "perfection." Jonathan Swift's 1712 *Proposal* (addressed to
the Lord High Treasurer, proposing an English-language academy modeled on the French Académie
française, founded 1634) is the chapter's central example: Swift wanted not just improvement but
**ascertainment** — permanently halting further change — on the explicit belief that uncontrolled
further change could only be degradation, not improvement. Samuel Johnson, by contrast, while
influenced by the same "corruption/decay" framing in his *Dictionary*'s (1755) preface, is shown
expressing open skepticism about **fixing** a language ever actually succeeding in practice, even
while still describing languages as having "a natural tendency to degeneration." The book treats this
tension (shared belief in decay vs. disagreement about whether fixing is achievable or even coherent)
as itself revealing: the belief that "the state of perfection" is real was common across the century,
but different writers located that supposed golden age differently — some 18th-century grammarians
situated it in their own recent past (the "**Augustan Age**," named by analogy to Classical Rome's
Virgil/Horace/Ovid era, applied to early-/mid-18th-century writers like Addison, Steele, Pope, and
Swift himself) specifically because Latin and Greek, as **dead, no-longer-changing languages**, were
held up as the ultimate models of linguistic perfection precisely *because* they had stopped
developing. (pp.180, 184–185, Sections 11.1–11.2.1)

### "Reason," "analogy," and "custom" as three competing authorities for correctness

18th-century grammarians disagreed among themselves about what should settle a disputed point of
usage, and the chapter lays out three distinct, competing standards actually invoked in period texts:
**Reason** (logical argument, e.g. a 1776 argument that *whether he will or not* is more "analogical"
than *whether he will or no* because it avoids ellipsis); **Analogy** (consistency with existing
patterns elsewhere in the language); and — argued at greatest length, by Joseph Priestley's 1769
*Rudiments of English Grammar* — **Custom**, i.e. actual observed usage by "our best writers and
speakers," even where that usage runs contrary to a grammarian's own logical analysis. Priestley's
explicit position (quoted at length) is that grammarians should favor "the genius of the language"
(as revealed by its actual living speakers' practice) over invented "arbitrary considerations," and
that an irregularity consistently used by good writers should be accepted as legitimate rather than
corrected away — a genuinely descriptivist position voiced from inside the prescriptivist era, showing
the period's "prescriptive vs. descriptive" split was not a later invention projected backward, but a
live internal disagreement at the time. (pp.184, 186, Sections 11.1 and 11.3-adjacent)

### Robert Lowth's prescriptive method and its own self-undermining inconsistency

Robert Lowth's *A Short Introduction to English Grammar* (1762) is singled out as the single most
influential prescriptive grammar of the following century and a half (used in schools into the 19th
century). Lowth's stated method was to identify "**errors**" and "**solecisms**" by citing supposed
misuses from even the most eminent authors (Milton, Dryden, Pope) — i.e. a writer's literary reputation
did not exempt their usage from correction. The book flags a specific, telling inconsistency
(Activity 11.5): Lowth's own grammar prescribes a particular distribution of *will* vs. *shall* across
persons (*shall* for plain futurity in 1st person, *will* for 2nd/3rd; reversed for emphasis/
intention/promise), yet his own preface does not consistently follow this very rule in his own
writing — treated by the book as a recurring pattern throughout the prescriptive tradition: a
grammarian's rule and a grammarian's actual practice are two separate things worth checking against
each other independently, not assumed to match. (pp.186–187, Section 11.4)

### "The Genius of the Language," *Ipse Dixit*, and the circularity of appeal-to-authority

The chapter identifies a recurring, largely unexamined rhetorical move in 18th-century usage debates:
invoking "the Genius of the Language" or "the Idiom of the Tongue" as if it were an objective external
standard, when in practice — the book argues — this amounted to little more than the individual
grammarian's own intuition about what "sounded right," summarized in the period as reliance on *ipse
dixit* (Latin, 'he himself said it' — i.e. assertion by personal authority rather than argument).
Robert Baker's 1770 *Reflections on the English Language* is given as a telling self-undermining
example: Baker explicitly condemns reliance on *ipse dixit* when applied to "the best penmen"
(established authors) — but is shown applying exactly the same undefended personal-authority standard
to his *own* judgments elsewhere in the same book. (p.185, Section 11.3)

### Language as the explicit marker and instrument of 18th-century class division

The chapter's most socially pointed material documents a direct, openly stated 18th-century belief
that linguistic register tracked — and was evidence for — innate differences in intellect and
morality between social classes, not merely differences in education or opportunity. Quoted period
sources describe "polite," "cultivated" language (associated with "the Gentry") as "pure," "refined,"
and "noble," against "the depraved Language of common People," called "vulgar," "barbarous,"
"degenerate," "profane," "mean," and "abject" — language explicitly figured as "the dress of thought"
or "the mirror of thought," such that inferior language was taken as direct, legible evidence of
inferior thought itself, not a separate, independent social marker. This belief was reinforced by a
contemporary theory called "**Universal Grammar**" (James Harris, *Hermes*, 1751), which held that
language directly and transparently expressed the rational soul, so that "the vulgar" — held to
"never once dream" of anything beyond immediate sensory appetite — could not, on this theory, be
capable of the kind of abstract thought "polite" language supposedly encoded. The book is explicit that
this was not merely private prejudice but had **real political consequences**: during the reform
agitation following the French Revolution (1789) and the long wars with France (1793–1815), the
"vulgarity" of working-class language was used as an explicit, stated argument by the government for
dismissing petitions calling for parliamentary reform — not just an incidental insult, but a
load-bearing part of the actual case against extending the vote, illustrated with three dated
petition texts (1793 Sheffield, rejected; 1810 Reading, accepted; 1817 non-voter Yorkshire petition),
and with a Tory MP, George Canning, stating outright of one such petition's "objectionable" language
that "if such language were tolerated, there was an end of the House of Commons." (pp.190–193,
Sections 11.6–11.7)

### William Cobbett: a working-class autodidact's direct testimony on grammar as class gatekeeping

William Cobbett (1763–1835), son of a Farnham farmer, self-taught while serving as an army copyist,
and later a radical journalist, MP, and grammar-book author, is presented as uniquely valuable period
testimony *because* he personally experienced — and explicitly named — the social-gatekeeping function
of "correct grammar" the chapter has just been describing abstractly. Cobbett's own 1796 memoir
(*The Life and Adventures of Peter Porcupine*) narrates how he taught himself standard English grammar
from a copy of Lowth's grammar while serving as a semi-literate young soldier, memorizing the entire
text by repetition out of a conviction (reinforced by his commanding officer) that grammatical fluency
was a precondition for any social or professional advancement — a direct, first-person confirmation
that this project's "slang-synthesis" premise (register as a marker of social position, actively
policed and actively learnable/performable) was a lived, explicitly understood reality for an ordinary
18th-century person, not only an abstraction imposed top-down by grammarians. Cobbett's later 1817
*Grammar of the English Language* (written as a series of letters to his own son, and deliberately
published in America, outside the reach of British *habeas corpus* suspension at the time) turns this
personal experience into explicit political argument: he recounts hearing "a Hampshire plough-boy" say
"Poll Cherrycheek have giv'd I thick handkercher" and insists listeners understand its meaning
perfectly well despite its non-standard grammar — "we are but too apt to laugh at him, and to call him
*ignorant*; which is wrong" — using this directly to argue that non-standard grammar is a matter of
social exclusion, not of actual comprehension failure or inferior thought, precisely inverting the
"Universal Grammar" position described above while writing from inside the same prescriptive-grammar-
teaching tradition. (One historian, Olivia Smith, is quoted by the book's author summarizing Cobbett's
own stance: "grammar is, in short, an integral part of the class structure of England, and the act of
learning grammar by one of his readers could be a piece of class warfare.") (pp.193–195, Section 11.8)

### Standardized grammar's lasting effect: slowed, but not stopped, grammatical change

The chapter closes by noting that, in contrast to vocabulary (which the book states continues to gain
and lose words "according to the needs of communication" without interruption) and spelling (fixed by
general printer practice by the 18th century, per Samuel Johnson's *Dictionary*, with only minor
later adjustments — e.g. American *program*/*medieval* spellings becoming acceptable in Britain only
much more recently, the latter via computing terminology), the grammar of English since Cobbett's day
shows **no significant structural differences** from contemporary Standard English — the author's
explicit conclusion is that **once a standard form of writing becomes the accepted norm, the rate of
further grammatical change slows considerably**, even though the language's underlying "living,
constantly changing" character (per the book's own repeated framing throughout) does not actually
stop; it simply becomes much harder to observe directly in writing, continuing instead at the level of
style, vocabulary, and pronunciation. (p.195, closing paragraphs)

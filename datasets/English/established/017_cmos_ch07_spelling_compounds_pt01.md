## Grammar points

### 7.1 Recommended dictionaries

Chicago recommends *Webster's Third New International Dictionary* and the latest *Merriam-Webster's
Collegiate Dictionary* ("Webster's") as the baseline spelling authority. When Webster's lists more
than one spelling or plural form, Chicago defaults to the first-listed form, even for "equal"
variants, for consistency. If the *Collegiate* and the *Third International* disagree, the
*Collegiate* wins as the more recently researched source. Supplementary dictionaries (*American
Heritage*, *Random House Webster's College Dictionary*) are suggested for further definitions, but
one spelling source should be used consistently within a single work (p. 906 of the source).

### 7.2 Spellings peculiar to particular disciplines

Where a variant spelling carries special disciplinary connotation (e.g., "archeology" vs.
"archaeology"), an author's field-specific preference should be respected even against Webster's
default ordering; absent such a preference, Chicago follows the first-listed form.

### 7.3 Non-US spelling

For non-US authors publishing English-language work edited and produced in the US, Chicago generally
converts non-US spelling (e.g., British "colour," "analyse") to American spelling ("color,"
"analyze"), since consistency is easier to maintain this way and authors rarely object. Spelling
inside quoted material, however, is left unchanged (cross-referenced to §13.7).

### 7.4 Supplementing the dictionary

The chapter previews its own scope: forming plurals and possessives of compounds not covered by a
standard dictionary; word-division (hyphenation) at a line break; capitalization/italics/quotation-
mark conventions for distinctive treatment of words and phrases; and compound-word hyphenation
(flagged as "perhaps most important," with the compounds-and-hyphenation guide table placed at the
very end of the chapter, §7.85, for quick reference).

### 7.5 Standard plural forms

Most English nouns pluralize regularly (-s, or -es after ch/j/s/sh/x/z); fluent speakers don't need
dictionary help for these and they're often omitted from standard dictionary entries. Dictionaries do,
however, generally list plural forms for -y-ending nouns that shift to -ies, -o-ending nouns, Latin-
or Greek-origin nouns (crocus, datum, alumna), and nouns with irregular plurals (child, leaf).

### 7.6 Alternative plural forms

When Webster's gives two plural forms — whether ranked as primary/secondary (zero → zeros/zeroes) or
listed as equal variants (millennium → millennia/milleniums) — Chicago normally takes the first-listed
form. Some words have functionally distinct plurals for different purposes: "indexes" for a book's
back matter, "indices" for a mathematical expression, per Webster's own entry for "index."

### 7.7 Plurals of compound nouns

Webster's supplies the plural of most tricky compounds (fathers-in-law, coups d'état,
courts-martial, chefs d'oeuvre — the internal element takes the plural marking). For compounds not
individually listed, common sense generally supplies the answer (bachelors of science, masters of
arts, spheres of influence, child laborers).

### 7.8 Plurals of proper nouns

Names of persons and other capitalized nouns pluralize by adding -s or -es (not -ies, even where a
common-noun cognate would use -ies) — Toms/Dicks/Harrys, the Joneses, the Martinezes, the Brunos,
Sundays, Germanys, Pakistanis. Rare exceptions (e.g., Romany → Romanies) are generally listed in
Webster's. An apostrophe is never used to pluralize a proper name ("the Jeffersons live here," not
"the Jefferson's"); for the possessive form of proper nouns, see §7.16.

### 7.9 Plural form for Native American group names

Current preference pluralizes Native American group names with -s (the Hopis, not "Hopi"); earlier
writing often omitted the -s, and Webster's continues to list both forms as equal variants. Some
group/language names are conventionally left unpluralized (the languages of the Iroquois).

### 7.10 Singular form used for the plural

Names ending in an unpronounced s or x are best left in the singular form even when referring to more
than one bearer of the name (the seventeen Louis of France; the two Dumas, father and son); rewording
("two towns called Charlevoix") is offered as a better alternative to an awkward plural.

### 7.11 Plural form of italicized words

When an italicized term (a newspaper name, a book title, and the like) is pluralized, the -s is
normally set in roman, not italic, to keep the inflectional ending visually distinct from the
italicized term itself (two *Chicago Tribune*s; several *Madame Bovary*s; too many *sic*s). A title
already in plural form may be left unchanged (four *New York Times*); if pluralizing would be
ambiguous or awkward, Chicago recommends rephrasing instead. Plural endings on italicized *foreign*
words, by contrast, are also set in italics (*Blume, Blumen*; *cheval, chevaux*; *señor, señores*).

### 7.12 Plural form for words in quotation marks

A quoted word or phrase is pluralized the normal way, without an apostrophe; Chicago discourages a
plural -s placed directly after a closing quotation mark, since the result reads awkwardly — reword
if so ("How many more times can we expect to see 'To be continued'?" rather than placing an -s after
the closing quote).

### 7.13 Plurals of noun coinages

Words and hyphenated phrases not themselves nouns, but used as nouns (noun coinages), pluralize by
adding -s or -es, per the inflected forms given for these examples in *Webster's Third New
International* (ifs and buts, dos and don'ts, threes and fours, thank-yous, maybes, yeses and nos).

### 7.14 Plurals for letters, abbreviations, and numerals

Capital letters used as words, numerals used as nouns, and abbreviations usually pluralize by adding
-s (the three Rs, the 1990s, IRAs, URLs, BSs/MAs/PhDs). Lowercase letters, by contrast, form the
plural with an apostrophe + s to aid comprehension (x's and y's) — cross-referenced to §10.43 for
further exceptions and §10.55 for the International System. Certain abbreviations instead double
rather than take -s for their plural (p./pp. "page(s)"; n./nn. "note(s)"; MS/MSS "manuscript(s)"); see
also §10.4 and §§7.58–65.

### 7.15 Possessive form of most nouns (the General Rule)

The possessive of most singular nouns is formed by adding apostrophe + s (the horse's mouth, a bass's
stripes). The possessive of most plural nouns — except a few irregular plurals not ending in -s, like
"children" — is formed by adding an apostrophe alone (puppies' paws; but "children's literature,"
since "children" doesn't end in s). A collective noun like "a herd of sheep" (plural-but-unmarked
"sheep") still takes the full apostrophe + s pattern ("a herd of sheep's mysterious disappearance"),
since the operative word for possessive purposes is the head noun's own ending, not its notional
number. Cross-referenced to §§7.19–21 for exceptions and §5.19.

### 7.16 Possessive of proper nouns, letters, and numbers

The general rule (apostrophe + s for singular, apostrophe alone for plural) extends to proper nouns —
including names ending in s, x, or z (Kansas's legislature, Chicago's lakefront, Marx's theories,
Jesus's adherents, Berlioz's works, Dickens's novels) — as well as to letters and numbers used as
possessive heads (FDR's legacy, 1999's heaviest snowstorm). Plural proper nouns take the apostrophe
alone (the Lincolns' marriage, the Williamses' new house, the Martinezes' daughter, dinner at the
Browns').

### 7.17 Possessive of words and names ending in unpronounced "s"

In a return to Chicago's earlier practice (after a period of exempting these forms), words and names
ending in an unpronounced s now form the possessive in the standard way, with apostrophe + s
(Descartes's three dreams, the marquis's mother, François's efforts, Vaucouleurs's assistance, Albert
Camus's novels). The rationale given is twofold: the added s is often actually pronounced in speech,
and the uniform rule keeps consistency with how other proper-noun possessives are formed.

### 7.18 Possessive of names like "Euripides"

Chicago no longer recommends the traditional exception for classical proper names of two or more
syllables ending in an "eez" sound; such names now form the possessive in the usual way, with
apostrophe + s (Euripides's tragedies, the Ganges's source, Xerxes's armies) — even though, when these
forms are spoken aloud, the added s is generally not itself pronounced.

### 7.19 Possessive of nouns plural in form, singular in meaning

When a noun's singular and plural forms are identical/uninflected (e.g., a discipline name like
"politics" or "economics," or certain species/taxonomic terms), the possessive of both the singular
and the plural sense is formed the same way, with an apostrophe alone (politics' true meaning,
economics' forerunners, this species' first record) — an "of" rephrasing is offered as a cleaner
alternative if ambiguity threatens ("the first record of this species"). The same rule extends to a
place, organization, or publication name whose final element happens to be a plural-form noun even
though the entity itself is grammatically singular (the United States' role in international law,
Highland Hills' late mayor, Callaway Gardens' former curator, the National Academy of Sciences' new
policy).

### 7.20 "For . . . sake" expressions

A small, traditionally fixed set of "for . . . sake" expressions built on a singular noun ending in s
form the possessive with an apostrophe alone, omitting the additional s, purely for euphony (for
goodness' sake, for righteousness' sake). Outside this traditional set, "for . . . sake" expressions
take the possessive in the ordinary way, with apostrophe + s (for expedience's sake; for appearance's
sake, or alternatively the plural possessive "for appearances' sake," or the rephrased "for the sake
of appearance"; for Jesus's sake).

### 7.21 An alternative practice for words ending in "s"

Some writers and publishers use a simpler, formerly more common system of omitting the possessive s on
every word already ending in -s, regardless of pronunciation (Dylan Thomas' poetry, Etta James'
singing, that business' main concern). Chicago notes this is easy to apply and economical but does not
recommend it, since it disregards how the possessive is actually pronounced.

### 7.22 Joint versus separate possession

When two or more closely linked nouns share possession of the same single thing, they're treated as
one unit and only the final element takes the possessive marking (my aunt and uncle's house; Gilbert
and Sullivan's *Iolanthe*; Minneapolis and Saint Paul's transportation system — one shared system).
When the things possessed are instead discrete/separate for each noun, every noun in the series
individually takes its own possessive form (my aunt's and uncle's medical profiles — two separate sets
of medical records; Dylan's and Jagger's hairlines; New York's and Chicago's transportation
systems — two distinct systems; Gilbert's or Sullivan's mustache).

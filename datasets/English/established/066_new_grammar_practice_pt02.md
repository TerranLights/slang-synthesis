## Grammar points

### 28. Present Simple
*do/does*-support for questions and negatives; 3sg *-s* (with *-es* after sibilant-final verbs).
Used for permanent truths (*I come from France*) and habitual/repeated actions (*I get up at six
o'clock every day*). (p.30)

### 29. Present Continuous
*be* + verb-*ing*. Used for an action in progress right now (*I'm reading a grammar book*). (p.32)

### 30. Present Simple/Present Continuous — review
Drill combining §28–29; no new rule content. (p.33)

### 31. Present Continuous: short answers
Short answers use the *be*-auxiliary only, matched to the subject's pronoun (not the original
noun): *'Are your parents sleeping?' 'Yes, they are.'* Positive short answers never contract (*Yes,
I am*, not *Yes, I'm*); negative short answers do contract. (p.34–35)

### 32. Present Simple: short answers
Parallel structure using *do/does* instead of *be*. An uncontracted negative short answer (*No, they
do not*) is noted as more emphatic than the contracted form. (p.36)

### 33. Present Simple and Continuous: short forms — review
Drill combining §31–32; no new rule content. (p.37)

### 34. Past Simple: question and negative
Both are person-invariant across all verbs: *did* + bare infinitive (question), *did not/didn't* +
bare infinitive (negative) — irregular-verb morphology, per §36, applies only to the positive form.
(p.38)

### 35. Past Simple: positive — regular verbs
Regular verbs are person-invariant, adding *-ed* (spelling rules as in the Vocabulary table above).
Used for a completed past action or state, whether momentary (*I asked a question*) or extended (*I
lived here for years*) — the source illustrates both with a timeline diagram placing the whole
event before "now." (p.39)

### 36. Past Simple: positive — irregular verbs
Irregular verbs are likewise person-invariant in the past simple, but their positive form is a
suppletive/ablaut form rather than *+ -ed* (see Vocabulary table). Irregularity is confined to the
positive; questions and negatives use the regular periphrastic *did* + bare infinitive. (p.41)

### 37. Past Simple: positive — review
Drill covering a wider sample of irregular-verb positive forms (*buy→bought, catch→caught,
choose→chose*, etc.); no new rule beyond §36. (p.42)

### 38. Past Continuous
*was/were* + verb-*ing*. Describes a past action in progress at a given moment, typically
interrupted by a past-simple event (*I was having a bath when the phone rang*). The source
contrasts *When she arrived, we had dinner* (sequential: arrival, then dinner) against *When she
arrived, we were having dinner* (overlapping: dinner was already in progress when she arrived) —
this minimal pair is the clearest illustration in the chapter of how aspect (simple vs. continuous)
alone restructures the two clauses' temporal relationship, independent of any change in tense or
added time words. (p.43)

### 39. Past Simple and Past Continuous — review
Drill combining §34–38; no new rule content. (p.46)

### 40. Past Simple and Past Continuous: short answers
Short answers formed in parallel to §31/§32, using *did* (past simple) or *was/were* (past
continuous) matched to the subject pronoun. Note: the short answer to *Did you like it?* is *Yes, I
did* — not *Yes, I liked* (a stative/opinion verb cannot itself appear in a bare short answer the
way an action verb's auxiliary can). (p.47)

### 41. Present Perfect
*has/have* + past participle. Marks the *indefinite* past — the "when" is either unimportant or
unknown — contrasted directly with past simple's *definite* time reference (*I went to Paris last
year* vs. *I've been to Paris* [at some unspecified point in my life]). Frequently expresses
personal experience (*I've been to Berlin*) and commonly questioned with *Have you ever...?*
(="in your life"). **Never co-occurs with a definite past time word** (*last night, yesterday, in
1984*) — *I've seen him yesterday* is explicitly marked ungrammatical. The source also draws a
fine aspectual distinction between *gone* (subject is still away) and *been* (subject has returned)
— *She's gone to Sweden* vs. *She's been to Sweden* — a genuinely subtle lexical-aspect contrast
worth flagging for comparative/conlang purposes, since it's carried entirely by participle choice
rather than any additional marker. (p.48)

### 42. Present Perfect/Past Simple — review
Drill forcing a choice between the two tenses per sentence; no new rule content beyond §41. (p.50)

### 43. Present Perfect + *for/since*
Marks a state that began in the past and continues into the present. *for* + a duration (*for six
years*); *since* + a fixed past starting point (*since 1990*). Sharply contrasted with past simple
+ *for*, which instead implies the state has since ended (*He's worked here for six months* [still
does] vs. *He worked here for six months* [no longer does]) — the auxiliary tense alone, not any
added word, carries whether the state is still true. *since* is explicitly marked as incompatible
with past simple (*He worked here since 1990* is ungrammatical). (p.51)

### 44. Present Perfect — fuller use taxonomy
Expands §41 into an explicit two-branch taxonomy: (a) *the unfinished past* — either an ongoing
state (as in §43) or an event located within an still-currently-open time period (*I've read two
books this week*, said while the week isn't over); (b) *the indefinite past* — connected to the
present via one of several sub-cases: a past event with a visible present result (*He's painted his
house*), recent "news"-type events, co-occurrence with *just/yet/already* (§45), personal experience
(*I've been to Paris*), and personal experience framed with a superlative or ordinal (*She's the
most intelligent person I've met*; *This is the third time we've complained*) — this last sub-case
is a genuinely distinct trigger environment for present perfect worth noting separately from plain
"have you ever" experience-marking. (p.52–53)

### 45. Present Perfect with *just, yet,* and *already*
*just* and *already* can appear either mid-auxiliary-verb-group or clause-finally (*She's just
gone* / — ; *She's already left* / *She's left already* / *She's left the house already*); *yet* is
restricted to negatives and questions, and appears clause-finally (*She hasn't phoned yet*). (p.54)

### 46. Present Perfect Continuous
*has/have been* + verb-*ing*. Frequently paired with *how long*, *for*, and *since* (cross-referenced
to §43 for the *for*/*since* distinction). Describes an activity that was ongoing until the moment of
speaking or very recently stopped (*I've been working all night*). Explicitly restricted: not used
with *to be*, nor with verbs that don't normally take a continuous form at all (the source doesn't
enumerate this verb class here, but the restriction itself — presumably targeting stative verbs like
*know, want, belong* — is stated as a flat rule). (p.55)

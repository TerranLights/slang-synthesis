**Morpheme breakdown (pidgin/creole analytic morphology — a genuinely different typological profile
from every English variety covered in earlier `073_*` parts).** West African Pidgin English is
**analytic**, using free particles rather than bound inflectional morphemes to mark tense/aspect —
directly relevant to this project's cross-language morphological-typology convention
(`00_Word_Concept_and_Morphological_Typology_Guide.md`), since a pidgin verb's "inflection" is
better represented as a sequence of separate function words than as a single composed word-form:

> **i bin kam** = i ("he/she/it") + bin (past-tense particle) + kam ("come") — "he/she/it came."

> **i don kam** = i ("he/she/it") + don (perfect-aspect particle) + kam ("come") — "he/she/it has
> come."

> **i go kam** = i ("he/she/it") + go (future-marking particle) + kam ("come") — "he/she/it will
> come."

> **daiman** = die (free morpheme, "to die") + man (free morpheme, "person") — "corpse"; an ordinary
> English-style N+N-type compound, not evidence of bound-morpheme agglutination.

## Grammar points

### The demographic and colonial-expansion history behind English's current scale

The book is explicit that English's current global scale is historically very recent and should not
be read backward into earlier periods: Shakespeare "wrote for a speech community of only a few
millions," barely established even in Ireland or Wales, let alone known abroad. Two separate growth
mechanisms are tracked with concrete population figures: (1) **sheer population growth within
England itself** — roughly 1.5 million at the Norman Conquest, held back by recurrent plague through
the Middle Ages (still under 5 million as late as 1600), then growing to 17 million by 1850 and over
30 million by 1900 once the Industrial Revolution took hold; (2) **geographic displacement of Celtic
languages within the British Isles itself**, which the book stresses only completed very recently
and partly through **deliberate policy**, not organic drift alone — Welsh and Scottish Gaelic became
minority-even-in-their-own-territory languages only within the last two centuries, and the book cites
specific policy evidence: Scottish Highland schools established after the 1745 Jacobite rebellion
used English as the medium of instruction with Gaelic explicitly **forbidden**; Brian Friel's 1980
play *Translations* is cited as an imaginative but historically grounded reconstruction of similar
British colonial linguistic policy in 19th-century Irish-speaking Ireland. The book notes this
specific policy climate has since reversed in all these areas, with official support for Celtic-
language education now standard. Overseas expansion then proceeds through named, dated colonial
phases: 17th/18th-century North American and West Indian settlement (competing there with Spanish,
French and Dutch colonizers, with Britain gaining control of Antigua, Barbados, Jamaica, St Kitts,
and Trinidad and Tobago by the early 19th century); Indian-subcontinent dominance from the second
half of the 18th century (East India Company founded 1600, trading posts from the 17th century, but
firm British rule only from the 1770s); Australian settlement shortly after American independence;
accelerating 19th-century expansion including the displacement of Dutch power in South Africa and the
establishment of rule in Singapore, British Guiana, New Zealand and Hong Kong; and the late-19th-
century "scramble for Africa" (explicitly named as a multi-power European competition — Belgium,
Britain, France, Germany, Portugal — not a uniquely British project), yielding British rule in parts
of West Africa (including modern Nigeria), East Africa (modern Kenya and Tanzania), and southern
Africa (modern Zimbabwe and Botswana). **American English's own demographic rise is tracked as a
genuinely separate, later growth engine**: the first US census (1788) recorded about 4 million
people, mostly of British origin; by 1850 the US population (23 million) had already overtaken
England's; by 1900 it reached 76 million, 150 million by 1950 — with the book specifically crediting
immigration-driven growth, reinforced by an **explicit linguistic citizenship policy** (a
competency-in-English examination requirement for naturalization), as a direct cause of English's
present-day global standing — and notes this policy's unexpected cultural by-product, Leo Rosten's
comic novel *The Education of Hyman Kaplan* (1937), about immigrants studying for exactly this
citizenship-English examination.

### The inner-circle/outer-circle/expanding-circle model, worked with real contrasting cases

The book adopts this now-standard three-way sociolinguistic classification (not crediting a specific
originator by name in this passage) to organize the rest of the chapter: the **inner circle**
(communities where English has been passed down as a native, first language across generations —
UK, USA, Canada, Australia, New Zealand) shows comparatively **dense settlement with numerical/
political/economic dominance** by English-speakers over the original inhabitants (Native Americans,
Australian Aboriginals, Maori), with the result that native languages in these countries had little
influence on the settlers' English — explicitly contrasted with **South Africa**, where (despite also
being inner-circle by the book's definition) English is spoken as a first language by a comparatively
**small** share of the population (8.2% per the cited 2001 census, placing it only fifth among home
languages, behind isiZulu, isiXhosa, Afrikaans and Sepedi), though the book cautions this
undercounts English's real reach since it doesn't capture the many South Africans who speak English
as a competent second language. Inner-circle internal variation is also characterized by density:
Australia/New Zealand/South Africa show comparatively **little** internal regional dialect variation
(mainly social/ethnic variation instead — the book's named examples are Australian Aboriginal English
and Maori English as ethnically-marked varieties existing alongside the general national standard),
whereas the **United States shows much greater regional dialect differentiation**, attributed to the
specific settlement-and-frontier-expansion history (distinct dialects developing among the original
East Coast settler communities, then carried and further diversified westward as the frontier
expanded, with the different dialect streams partially re-mixing as they spread — producing the
now-standard Northern/Midland/Southern three-way dialect classification, with "General American"
specifically defined by the book as covering parts of all three areas while explicitly **excluding**
the non-rhotic coastal south-east and coastal New England regions; Canadian English is flagged as its
own further distinct variety, though markedly closer to General American than to British English).
The **outer circle** (English long-established as an institutional second language alongside local
languages, used especially for inter-group and public-sphere communication — India, and sub-Saharan
former British colonies generally, are the book's worked cases) is characterized by genuinely **local
ownership of the learning target**: an Indian child typically learns a local Indian variety of
English, taught by an Indian teacher who themselves speaks that local variety — not British or
American English imported as an external model. The book is careful to note the outer-circle/second-
language vs. expanding-circle/foreign-language boundary is "not a sharp one" in practice: educated
speakers from many genuinely foreign-language countries (including much of Europe) may have learned
English to a very high level and use it as a **lingua franca** in international contexts (academic
conferences are the book's example) without the country itself having any inner/outer-circle
institutional tradition of English use. The **expanding circle** (no native tradition or
institutional local use, learned purely as a foreign language for international purposes — mainland
China, explicitly contrasted with former-colony Hong Kong, which the book places in the outer circle
instead) typically models its taught English directly on either RP or General American, since there's
no local variety-in-use to model on instead.

### Case studies in how English's institutional role and domain-of-use vary even within the outer circle

The book works through several outer-circle countries in enough sociolinguistic detail to show that
"outer circle" status itself masks very different real situations: **India** (population over one
billion, over four hundred languages; English is one of several official languages alongside Hindi
and 22 "scheduled" regional languages, serving as the main language of wider administrative/
commercial communication across language-group boundaries; despite an explicit post-independence
policy push to shift the educational medium from English toward regional languages, in practice most
universities and many schools remain English-medium). **Nigeria** (three main indigenous languages —
Yoruba, Igbo, Hausa — plus over five hundred further local languages; English is official and
dominant in government/administration specifically among higher socio-economic groups; the book notes
primary education is increasingly conducted in local languages, but secondary and university
education remains predominantly English-medium — a genuinely different pattern from India's own
educational-medium trajectory). **Singapore** (four official languages — Chinese/Mandarin, English,
Malay, Tamil — with Chinese dominant as the medium of education through the 1940s–50s but, since 1965
independence, English-medium education now universal, with Chinese/Malay/Tamil taught only as
subjects rather than as instructional media — i.e. a genuinely reversed trajectory from Nigeria's,
moving *toward* rather than away from English-medium schooling over time). **Hong Kong** (under
British colonial rule, Chinese/Cantonese and English held equal official status but with sharply
divided real-world domains — English dominant in the legal system, English-medium higher education,
upper-tier commerce/industry, and media; everyday discourse within the Chinese community conducted in
Cantonese regardless; the book's summary formula: "English was formal, while Cantonese was intimate,"
with English additionally carrying an explicit **power** connotation and Cantonese a **solidarity/
ethnic-identity** connotation — a textbook diglossia case; post-handover, now reunited with mainland
China, the book predicts English will likely remain commercially important for Hong Kong's global
trade role without retaining its former "language of power" institutional status).

### Code-switching between English and a local language as a documented literary device

The book treats fiction by Chinua Achebe (specifically *A Man of the People*, 1966) as a genuine
sociolinguistic data source for real-world outer-circle code-switching patterns, not merely literary
color: the character Nanga, a "charismatic demagogue," deliberately **switches between Standard
English and Nigerian Pidgin English (NPE)** depending purely on who he's addressing and the social
effect he wants — Standard English for formal occasions, NPE specifically to address lower-status
characters (a chauffeur, a cook), or when being deliberately jocular with friends/colleagues, or when
performing a populist "man of the people" persona; his wife Mrs Nanga, by contrast, **never** uses
English at all, speaking only her local language (itself represented in the text via Standard
English prose, with occasional individual English words interspersed) — establishing her as
untouched by, or excluded from, the elite bilingual code Nanga performs; the narrator-schoolteacher
character Odili uses **all three** registers situationally: pidgin to a girlfriend who speaks a
different local language than his own (pidgin functioning here as an inter-ethnic lingua franca, not
just a register choice), his own local language to address lower-class characters, and English in
informal, often joking conversation with friends. The book's framing: this is a genuine three-way,
context-conditioned code system (Standard English / local language / pidgin), not a simple two-way
formal/informal switch, and each character's own particular repertoire (which of the three they do
or don't command, and when they deploy which) is itself socially meaningful within the novel.

### Post-independence controversy over whether/how to retain English, with genuinely conflicting motivations

The book is careful to present this as a genuinely **multi-sided, cross-cutting** controversy, not a
simple colonizer-vs-colonized binary: during colonial rule, Standard British English was the
administrative language and **local departures from it were stigmatized as errors** — but
post-independence disputes in many countries cut across this straightforwardly, with the book listing
several genuinely independent, sometimes mutually conflicting factors shaping each country's
decision: nationalist feeling (favoring replacement of the colonial language); attachment to
traditional culture (likewise); the desire for continued access to international science/technology
(favoring retention); the practical needs of both local and international communication (sometimes
pulling in opposite directions from each other); and, the book notes pointedly, **naked elite
self-interest** — "in some former colonies, a mastery of English was the privilege of a dominant
elite, who therefore supported its retention," directly opposed by "more radical and democratic
forces" who favored replacement with one or more local languages specifically to democratize access
to power. The book's worked case is **India's** post-independence Hindi-vs-English dispute: a
north-Indian, Hindi-speaking-majority movement favored making Hindi the sole main official language,
directly opposed by many south Indians (who speak unrelated Dravidian languages, not Hindi) on the
explicit ground that adopting Hindi as the national language would hand a structural economic/
political advantage specifically to northerners — so for many southern Indians, **retaining English
specifically served as a linguistically "neutral" alternative to Hindi**, not a straightforward
pro-colonial-language position at all. The book's closing observation names a further, independent
irony: "some of the most compelling literature in the English language in the second half of the
twentieth century" has itself emerged directly from ex-colonial regions where these very
retain-or-replace arguments played out (East African poetry — Okot p'Bitek; Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o's
novels, himself a prominent advocate of African-language literature over English; West African
drama/poetry — Wole Soyinka; Chinua Achebe's own novels) — i.e. the practical institutional dispute
and a flourishing new world-literary tradition **in English** coexisted in the same postcolonial
moment, without the book treating this as a contradiction needing resolution.

### Phonological variation across world Englishes, organized by the book's own three-way taxonomy

The book frames phonological difference between varieties as falling into exactly three structural
types — **phoneme inventory** differences (a variety may simply have more or fewer phonemes than
another); **realization** differences (the same phoneme, present in both varieties, pronounced with
a different actual sound value); and **distributional** differences (the same phoneme present in
both varieties, but selected for a different *set* of words) — plus, separately, stress/intonation
differences layered on top of all three. Worked case studies, organized by the book's own regional
headings:

- **North America**: rhotic (except coastal-south, coastal-New-England, and non-rhotic AAVE), so the
  centring diphthongs /ɪə/, /ɛə/, /ʊə/ that exist in RP don't exist as independent phonemes in most
  American speech (an r-colored vowel, sometimes transcribed [ʊɚ], can appear as a conditioned
  allophone before /r/ instead); RP's /ɒ/-/ɑː/ phoneme split (*dog*/*father*) corresponds to a single
  General American /ɑ/ phoneme (*dog*/*father* as near-homophones in vowel quality); regionally
  variable further mergers within America itself — notably the /ɑ/-/ɔ/ ("cot"/"caught") merger
  common in the north-west and in Canada, and the /ɛ/-/ɪ/ pre-nasal merger ("pin"/"pen" near-
  homophony) common across much of the southern USA; the consonant systems are essentially identical
  between RP and North American English except that a minority of US/Canadian speakers maintain a
  /ʍ/-/w/ distinction in pairs like *which*/*witch* that RP (and most American varieties) have
  merged. A dedicated, detailed treatment of American **intervocalic /t/ and /d/ realization**:
  Americans typically realize intervocalic /t/ (as in *pretty*, *letter*) as "a single rapid tap of
  the tongue," frequently voiced, so that it sounds like [d] to British ears; intervocalic /d/ is
  often produced identically, so *latter*/*ladder* become genuine homophones for most American
  speakers; this same tap realization extends to contexts where a sonorant consonant rather than a
  vowel precedes the /t/ or /d/ (*dirty*, where most Americans pronounce the following /r/, and
  *kinder*), and even across a word boundary when the following word begins with a vowel (*get it*) —
  while, conversely, the glottal/glottalized realizations of /t/ increasingly common in British
  English (even among younger RP speakers) are **not** found in General American and only rarely in
  other North American varieties.
- **Canadian English** closely resembles General American but has one well-known distinctive feature
  the book names explicitly as **Canadian Raising**: the diphthongs /aɪ/ and /aʊ/ have raised
  allophones, [əi] and [əʊ], specifically before a *voiceless* consonant — so a Canadian pronounces
  *ride*/*loud* as [raɪd]/[laʊd] but *write*/*lout* with the distinctly different raised onset
  [rəit]/[ləʊt], a phonetic distinction most other English varieties don't make categorically along
  this exact voicing-conditioned line. A further distinctive Canadian consonant feature: Canadian
  English uses the **clear** (front-resonance) allophone of /l/ in **all** positions, where RP
  distinguishes a clear [l] (before vowels/[j], as in *look*, *million*) from a dark, back-resonance
  [ɫ] (elsewhere, as in *old*, *mill*); American English has both allophones like RP but distributes
  them differently from RP specifically in intervocalic position, where General American uses the
  dark [ɫ] (*Billy*, *yellow*) where RP would use clear [l].
- **A third, purely historical-accident type of phonological difference** is illustrated via the
  RP /æ/-/ɑː/ distribution: RP's /ɑː/ phoneme has **three independent historical sources** —
  (1) lengthening of ME *a* before voiceless fricatives (*staff, ask, bath* — directly continuing the
  BATH-TRAP-split material already covered in `pt07`); (2) lengthening of ME *a* before preconsonantal/
  final /r/, with the /r/ then lost (*barn, far* — also already covered in `pt07`); and (3) a
  non-standard early development of the ME diphthong *au* specifically in French loanwords containing
  ME *a* before a nasal consonant (*aunt, dance, example*), where some English varieties kept the
  short vowel and others developed it to *au*; one strand of this *au* development reached [aː] by
  the 18th century and entered the standard language. **American English branched off from British
  English too early to be affected by the first of these three processes (the voiceless-fricative
  lengthening)** — so General American has the *same* vowel in *ask*/*hat* (both /æ/), where RP has
  genuinely different vowels (/ɑː/ vs. /æ/); the same split pattern recurs for the pre-nasal *au*
  case (*dance* is RP /dɑːns/ but American /dæns/, "clearly" descended from the branch where ME *a*
  before nasals had never changed to *au* at all). But the second process (lengthening before /r/)
  **did** reach America before the split, so American English **agrees** with RP on this one specific
  environment (*hat* /hæt/, *far* /fɑːr/ in both RP and General American) — i.e. a single present-day
  RP phoneme (/ɑː/) and its absence/presence correspondence with General American **splits three
  separate ways depending purely on which of three independently-timed historical sound changes
  produced a given word's vowel**, a genuinely precise, word-by-word-datable mechanism directly
  extending the "same sound law, different outcome because the two varieties had already diverged
  before it operated" methodology the book uses throughout.
- **Southern hemisphere (Australian, New Zealand, South African) English**: share RP's phoneme
  *inventory* almost exactly but differ substantially in *realization* — Australian /æ/ and /e/ are
  closer together than in RP (so *pan* can sound like RP *pen* to an English ear), and Australian
  /ɑː/ (*park*, *path*) is realized as a notably fronted [aː] vs. RP's more backed [ɑː]; New Zealand
  pronunciation is broadly similar to Australian but with a further-retracted /ɪ/ (*pin*) and an
  extremely close /e/ (*pen*, sounding close to *pin* to outside ears — with some New Zealand
  speakers further diphthongizing this vowel, especially after /j/, giving *yes* something close to
  [jiəs], which the book flags as "a good shibboleth" for distinguishing a New Zealand from an
  Australian speaker); Australian, New Zealand, and South African English all realize /ɜː/ (*turn*)
  in a closer, more forward position than RP, with New Zealand additionally adding lip-rounding
  (approaching the vowel of French *feu*); South African English, like the other two, has a closer
  /æ/ and /e/ than RP, and — in broader/less standard South African speech specifically — a
  **backed and rounded** [ɒː] realization of /ɑː/ (*park*, *path*), genuinely different from both the
  Australian fronted realization and the RP intermediate one; South African /ɪə/ and /ɛə/ are often
  realized as the pure vowel [eː], so *fear* and *fair* can be homophones; South African consonants
  show a single-tap (not approximant) /r/ and unaspirated voiceless stops /p, t, k/ — both features
  the book notes are shared with South African English's substrate-contact languages rather than with
  RP. Unstressed-syllable distribution also differs regionally: RP contrasts /ɪ/ vs. /ə/ in
  unstressed non-final position (*offices* /ˈɒfɪsɪz/ vs. *officers* /ˈɒfɪsəz/), but most Australian
  speech uses **only** /ə/ in this position, making *offices*/*officers* homophones — a feature shared
  with most New Zealand and South African English too — which in turn produces the striking,
  book-highlighted Australian-specific minimal distinction: RP's *taxes*/*taxis* are both /ˈtæksɪz/
  (homophones in RP), but in Australian English *taxes* is /ˈtæksəz/ while *taxis* is /ˈtæksiːz/ —
  i.e. an RP merger that Australian English **doesn't share**, precisely because of a *different*,
  unrelated unstressed-vowel-distribution difference elsewhere in its own system.
- **West Indies**: phonological systems vary noticeably island-to-island, including in rhoticity
  itself (Barbados rhotic, Trinidad non-rhotic, Jamaica fluctuating between the two); even the
  non-rhotic West Indian systems differ further from RP — Trinidadian English, for instance, has only
  **one** phoneme corresponding to RP's /ɪə/ and /ɛə/, making *beer* and *bare* homophones, and (like
  many Caribbean varieties) lacks a schwa /ə/ phoneme entirely, using either [i] or [a] in positions
  where RP would use unstressed /ə/; West Indian English more broadly differs from both RP and
  General American in **using pitch rather than stress to accent syllables**, a prosodic-system
  difference, not merely a segmental-phoneme one.
- **Outer-circle varieties generally**: often show substantially **reduced phoneme inventories**
  relative to RP/General American, frequently collapsing vowel-length distinctions entirely — the
  book's extended Nigerian English worked example: only one phoneme corresponds to each of RP's
  /ɪ/-/iː/, /æ/-/ɑː/, /ɒ/-/ɔː/, and /ʊ/-/uː/ pairs (so *bid*/*bead*, *hat*/*heart*, *stock*/*stuck*/
  *stork*, and *look*/*Luke* are each fully merged); there's no /ə/ phoneme at all, so unstressed
  vowels get a full vowel quality instead (*singer* as [ˈsiŋga], not RP's [ɪ]-reduced-to-[ə]); there's
  no /ɜː/ phoneme, with the vowel used instead determined purely by spelling (*work* as /wɔk/, a
  homophone of *walk*); diphthongs are often absent, replaced by a disyllabic vowel-sequence (*ear*
  as two syllables, [i.a], not one diphthongized syllable). Even more strikingly, the book describes
  Nigerian English as genuinely **syllable-timed** rather than **stress-timed** — i.e. a difference
  not just in which sounds occur but in the language's whole rhythmic organizing principle, with
  every syllable in a phrase occurring at roughly equal intervals regardless of stress, rather than
  RP/General American's pattern of stressed syllables recurring at roughly equal intervals with
  unstressed syllables compressed between them. This reduced-inventory, syllable-timed profile is
  flagged as characteristic of most African-English varieties generally, and the syllable-timing
  pattern specifically (though not necessarily reduced inventories) recurs in South Asian, Singapore,
  and Malaysian English too. Outer-circle varieties also show a strong **spelling-pronunciation**
  tendency wherever RP would have an unstressed, reduced /ɪ/ or /ə/ (common to both Indian and
  Nigerian English, independently); genuine, unpredictable **stress-pattern** differences from RP in
  individual words, in both directions — some words moving stress away from the first syllable
  (Nigerian *petrol* stressed on the second syllable) and others moving it *toward* the first
  (Indian *defence*, *mistake* with first-syllable stress) — with the book noting this isn't purely
  an outer-circle phenomenon, since inner-circle British speakers also show individually variable
  stress on some of the very same words (*pronunciation* mis-stressed by many British English
  speakers as if it matched the verb *pronounce*, by analogy); and **analogy-driven** word-level
  stress shifts specifically within outer-circle varieties, e.g. Nigerian *maintainance*
  (re-stressed on the second syllable under the influence of the related verb *maintain*, departing
  from the inner-circle noun's own stress pattern). Finally, systematic **consonant-cluster**
  simplification differences: word-final clusters are often reduced in Nigerian English (*-st*
  often reduced in *west*, *passed*) while spelling influence conversely causes some **normally-
  silent** clusters to be pronounced (*-mb* actually pronounced in *bomb*, *climb*); many speakers in
  northern India instead avoid word-**initial** three-consonant-type clusters (*sp-/st-/sk-*) by
  inserting an epenthetic vowel, giving *student* as /ɪˈstuːdent/ or /eˈstudent/, and similarly for
  *speak*, *school*.
- **Stress and intonation at the phrase/sentence level**: RP uses comparatively **few** stressed
  syllables per utterance (one strong stress per word plus several weak ones), whereas American and
  Australian speech often gives a word a **secondary** stress RP wouldn't — the book's worked example
  is *-ary*-suffixed words like *customary*, *military*: single-stressed in RP (with the *-ary*
  syllable reduced to /ə/ or dropped entirely) but regularly given a secondary stress on the *-ary*
  syllable (with a correspondingly full vowel) in North American and Australian speech. More broadly,
  an RP speaker may give **more words overall** a full sentence-level stress than an American or
  Australian speaker would for the identical sentence (A. G. Mitchell's cited example: "Thank you
  very much," where RP typically stresses only *Thank* and *much*, while an Australian speaker often
  also stresses *very*) — the book connects this directly to a further, genuinely consequential
  **tempo** effect: more total stresses per utterance means an utterance moves more slowly overall,
  so Australian speech in particular "sounds very slow-moving" to RP ears purely as a side-effect of
  this stress-density difference, not because of any difference in articulation speed per se — and
  notes some regional *British* English varieties share this same more-stresses/slower-tempo
  propensity (northern England's *industry*, stressed only on the first syllable in RP, often carries
  a second stress on the second syllable regionally; conversely *distributive*, single-second-
  syllable-stressed in RP, may instead be stressed on **both** the first and third syllables in some
  regional British speech) — i.e. this specific stress-density dimension of variation is not strictly
  an inner-circle/outer-circle or RP/non-RP matter, but cuts across both axes independently.

### Grammatical variation across world Englishes: "minor" but real and systematically patterned

The book's framing throughout this section: grammatical differences between the main standard
varieties of English are **genuinely minor**, nothing approaching the scale of the phonological or
lexical differences, but still real, systematic, and worth cataloging. Specific documented British/
American contrasts: irregular past tenses (*dove, fit, snuck* American vs. *dived, fitted, sneaked*
British); the past-tense ending */-d/* increasingly preferred in American where British retains
*/-t/* (*burned, learned, spelled, spilled*, with the */-d/* forms "increasingly found" in British
English too, i.e. an active ongoing convergence, not a static split); a further stem-vowel
alternation in some of these same verbs (British /iː/→/e/ in the past, American retaining /iː/
throughout: *dreamed, kneeled, leaped*); American preference for *have got* over British *have* in
certain contexts ("Do you have the time?" vs. "Have you got the time?"), with the book noting British
*do have* is traditionally itself **restricted to habitual-action contexts** ("Do you have dances in
your village-hall?") rather than being a fully general alternative to *have got* — though this more
extended American-style usage "has been spreading to the UK" in recent years; American English's two
distinct past participles for *get* (*got* vs. *gotten*), vs. Standard British's single *got* — with
the book noting even *within* American English the *got*/*gotten* split itself carries a real
meaning distinction ("I have got" traditionally = "I have," while "I have gotten" = "I have
obtained"), though this distinction has been loosening toward a wider range of contexts in recent
American usage; preposition choice differences (*live in* [British] vs. *live on* [American, for a
named street]; *cater for* [British] vs. *cater to* [American]). The book's summary judgment: "while
examples of this kind could be multiplied, they are minor... in all essentials, British and American
grammar are the same," and — a specific, directional observation — "even where differences exist,
British speakers and writers are increasingly using the American variants instead of or alongside
the British ones," i.e. documenting active convergence specifically in the British→American
direction, not a stable, symmetric standoff.

Grammatical differences become markedly larger once the creole-continuum and second-language
varieties are included. **In West Indian creole-influenced speech**, sporadic omission of
inflections (noun plural/possessive marking, verb third-person-singular/past-tense marking) and
omission of *is*/other forms of *to be* recur "especially when informal," reflecting genuine
substrate pressure from the underlying creole continuum. **In second-language outer-circle
varieties, similar features are commoner and more systematic rather than sporadic**: frequent
sporadic omission of *to be* (East Africa, Ghana, India, the Philippines: "We **waiting** for the
flight"); sporadic omission of the definite/indefinite article (West Africa, Singapore); omission of
noun/verb inflections (Singapore). Several further features recur **independently in both India and
Nigeria** specifically, which the book presents as a genuine convergent pattern rather than
coincidence: use of the progressive with verbs that British speakers would leave unmarked ("He **is
going** to his office every morning," "I **am having** a cold" — note this directly inverts the
Early Modern English trend already documented in `pt07`, where the progressive was *gaining* ground
relative to the unmarked form over historical time; here, outer-circle varieties extend the
progressive's domain even *further* than inner-circle usage does); plural forms applied to nouns
that are uncountable in British English (*furnitures*, *litters* "rubbish"); and the use of **isn't
it?** as a fully general, invariant tag question regardless of the main clause's actual subject/
auxiliary ("She is living here, isn't it?", "We should wait for him, isn't it?") — a pattern the book
notes also independently resembles French *n'est-ce pas?*'s invariant structure, is also found among
Welsh English speakers, and — in the contracted form **innit** — "is becoming increasingly common
amongst younger speakers in the UK" generally, possibly, the book suggests, under the influence of
Indian English usage within multicultural British communities — i.e. a specific outer-circle
grammatical feature actively diffusing back **into** inner-circle UK English, the reverse direction
from the chapter's earlier American-influencing-British observation.

### Vocabulary divergence: the area of greatest difference, organized by source mechanism

The book states explicitly that vocabulary shows "the greatest divergences" of any linguistic level
across world Englishes, and organizes the explanation around **why** new vocabulary was needed in
each new settlement context: "new flora and fauna, different natural features... a new society, with
its own political institutions, its own social customs, its own recreations, its various ways of
earning a living" — i.e. genuine new-referent necessity, not just stylistic drift, as the primary
driver (directly paralleling the Ch. 3/Ch. 6 loanword-vulnerability-and-necessity framework already
established in `pt02`/`pt04`). The book's worked mechanism inventory, several of them genuinely new
to this chapter even though the underlying mechanism categories were already established earlier in
the book:

- **Loans from contact languages**, shortened/simplified from the source in the borrowing process
  (see *hickory*, *skunk* in the Vocabulary table above) — and, distinctly, loans reshaped by
  **popular etymology** to resemble existing meaningful English words despite no real etymological
  connection (*woodchuck*) — a mechanism not given this specific name in earlier chapters, though a
  structurally similar false-etymology-driven reshaping was already covered for *advance*/*advantage*
  in `pt06`'s Early Modern "remodeling" material; the book is explicit these are two related but
  distinguishable phenomena (remodeling = reshaping toward a *correct or incorrect historical*
  source-language etymon; popular etymology = reshaping toward an *unrelated* existing word purely by
  surface resemblance).
- **Existing English words retargeted at a locally-resembling new referent** (*robin*), or given a
  **broadened/narrowed/shifted sense** for a new environment (*bush*, *wattle*, *paddock*).
- **Words that had already gone obsolete in Britain surviving, and sometimes further semantically
  drifting, in a peripheral colonial variety** (*fossick*, *larrikin*, *wowser*) — the book frames
  this as a genuine instance of the broader "peripheral dialects are often more conservative than the
  metropolitan center" pattern already established for Scots/northern-English Scandinavian
  retentions in `pt04`, now operating at the scale of entire overseas national varieties rather than
  domestic regional dialects.
- **A stable period-coexistence resolving into a later geographic (rather than semantic or register)
  split** — *autumn*/*fall*, both current with the same meaning in Shakespeare's own English, later
  settling into Britain-vs-America as two separately-standardized geographic defaults rather than one
  term displacing the other outright.
- **Local-language borrowing shaping outer-circle vocabulary directly** (the Hindi/Urdu-sourced
  Indian-English set in the Vocabulary table above), **alongside** locally-coined new English
  formations using entirely native English material but naming genuinely local concepts (*co-brother*,
  perhaps *tiffin*) — the book's explicit point being that outer-circle vocabulary innovation draws on
  **both** channels (contact-language borrowing and native English word-formation) simultaneously,
  the same dual-channel pattern already established for inner-circle vocabulary growth throughout the
  book, just producing locally-specific rather than globally-standard results.
- **Existing English words/forms given genuinely new local senses** (*backside*, *stepney*, *stir* in
  Indian English) or **retaining an older British sense that has since gone obsolete in Britain
  itself** (*demit* "to resign") — directly paralleling the Australian *fossick*/*larrikin*/*wowser*
  conservatism pattern above, now specifically in an outer-circle rather than inner-circle variety.
- **Usage/pragmatic drift without any lexical-form change at all** — the book's Nigerian-English
  worked dialogue specifically illustrates words whose **form** is unchanged from British English but
  whose **conventional usage/connotation** has shifted (*Enter!*, *Move over!*, *Sorry!* as a genuine
  expression of sympathy rather than an apology, *a hot drink* not necessarily meaning tea/coffee,
  *I am very worried to read from you* as the conventional opening formula for "I'm extremely anxious
  to hear from you") — a genuinely distinct phenomenon from any of the lexical-innovation mechanisms
  above, since no new word or new word-sense is involved at all, only a shift in a word/phrase's
  conventional pragmatic deployment.

### Pidgins and creoles: a systematic, dedicated grammatical sketch

The book devotes unusually sustained, structurally organized attention to pidgin/creole grammar as a
genuinely distinct linguistic type, not merely "simplified English" — flagging over two hundred
pidgins/creoles exist worldwide, drawing on many different donor languages (Swahili, Arabic, Malay,
Japanese, and various European languages among them), with **English-based** pidgins/creoles
numbering around forty distinct regional varieties, falling into two broad geographic groups: the
**Atlantic** group (Caribbean, Guyana, West Africa — the Gambia, Sierra Leone, Liberia, Ghana,
Nigeria, Cameroon) and the **Pacific** group (Australia, and coastal South-East Asia and the South
Sea islands). The book is explicit that the pidgin/creole distinction is **not sharp in practice**:
a given variety can function simultaneously as an auxiliary trade language for some speakers and as
a genuine first language for others within the very same speech community — West Africa's unusual
linguistic density (well over a hundred distinct languages coexisting) is cited as the specific
reason pidgins remain especially useful and persistent there, performing "all normal language
functions" alongside Standard English and the many local languages, sometimes used by individual
speakers *more* frequently than their own native tongue. A pidgin's trajectory can go either
direction over time: staying under continued influence from (and tending to evolve toward) its
"donor" standard language as long as contact persists, or — if that contact is lost — evolving
**away** from the donor, as the book reports happened with the English-based creoles of Surinam, in
some cases **retaining archaic features that the donor language has itself since lost** (directly
paralleling, yet again, the "periphery preserves what the center has discarded" conservatism pattern
already established multiple times earlier in this chapter and in `pt04`).

**Structural profile, systematically cataloged**: pidgins take their **vocabulary** mainly from the
donor language, but draw only **minimally** on its phonology and "hardly at all" on its grammar — the
book's single most emphatic structural claim is that "the various pidgins in the world often resemble
one another in structure much more than they resemble the dominant languages from which they are
derived," i.e. **convergent typological simplification**, not derivation-driven resemblance, governs
pidgin grammar cross-linguistically; "a pidgin tends to preserve the absolutely minimal grammatical
structures needed for effective communication, and reduces redundancy to almost nil." This has a
direct taxonomic consequence the book states explicitly: an English-based pidgin is "generally...
considered... to be a different language in its own right," not a dialect of English — **except**
specifically at the mesolect portion of a well-developed post-creole continuum (the book's named
example again being Jamaica's), where the intermediate varieties genuinely blur the category boundary
and "probably are to be thought of as dialects of English" rather than a wholly separate language.
**Phonologically**, pidgins/creoles typically show substantially reduced phoneme inventories relative
to the donor (the Jamaican Creole worked example: *black*/*block* share one vowel [blak]; *beer*/
*bare* share one [bɪɛr]; *pour*/*poor* share one [poːr]; *caught*/*cross*/*farm*/*form* all share one
[aː]) and systematic consonant substitution for phonemes "rare outside English" — /θ/ and /ð/
(absent from most of the world's languages and thus typically absent from a pidgin/creole's own
system too) are commonly replaced by /t/ and /d/ (*thin* → [tɪn], *father* → [ˈfaːda]); final
consonant clusters are commonly simplified by deletion (*act, bend, left* losing their final
consonant in Jamaican Creole); /h/ is commonly lost even in stressed syllables (*health* → [elt]).

**Morphologically and syntactically**, the book characterizes pidgins as genuinely **extreme analytic
languages**: nouns and verbs typically have only **one** invariant form each — no singular/plural
noun distinction (West African Pidgin *wan man* "one person," *ten man* "ten persons," *plenti man*
"many persons" — plurality marked, when marked at all, by a preceding numeral/quantifier, never by
any change to the noun itself), and no person/number verb-agreement inflection at all (*a kari*
"I carry," *yu kari* "you carry," *i kari* "he/she/it carries" — one invariant verb form covering
every grammatical person). **Tense and aspect are instead marked by separate free particles placed
before or after the verb** (see the Morpheme breakdown block above for the full worked West African
Pidgin paradigm: *bin* for past, *don* for perfect, *go* for future) — i.e. exactly the kind of
periphrastic, particle-based system the book's earlier chapters (`pt05`, `pt07`) traced Standard
English's own perfect/passive/progressive auxiliaries gradually evolving toward over many centuries,
here appearing already fully realized, from the very start, as the pidgin's **only** available
tense/aspect-marking strategy (no competing synthetic/inflectional option ever existed to be
displaced). **Pronoun systems typically simplify gender/case distinctions relative to the donor**
(West African Pidgin *i* commonly covers "he," "she," AND "it" — no three-way gender distinction;
*wi* commonly covers both "we"/"us" (nominative/accusative) and "our" (possessive-determiner) — no
case distinction; *dem* likewise commonly covers "they"/"them"/"their") **while sometimes adding a
distinction Standard English itself lacks**: many pidgins (West African Pidgin among them) have a
genuine, obligatory singular-vs-plural distinction in the second person (*yu* "you [singular]" vs.
*una*/*wuna* "you [plural]") — the book notes this specific feature is **not** unique to pidgins, since
Irish English and several British English dialects independently distinguish *you* from *youse* for
exactly the same purpose — i.e. pidgins converging on a grammatical distinction that some but not all
of their own donor-language's dialects also happen to make, rather than inventing it from nothing.
**Negation and questions are both formed without any auxiliary at all**: negation via a dedicated
particle (West African Pidgin *no*, as in *wi no sabi* "we don't know" — note *sabi* "to know" is
itself, per the specimen-text discussion below, a loan from Portuguese, not English, already present
in the pidgin's core verb vocabulary); interrogation purely through **intonation**, with **no change
to word order at all** (*yu get plet?* "have you got a plate?" — directly contrasting with Standard
English's own subject-auxiliary-inversion requirement, itself the product of centuries of
grammaticalization already traced in `pt06`/`pt07`, which pidgin grammar simply never develops because
word order is already doing essential grammatical work and inversion would be redundant on top of
it). The book's summary framing names the typological throughline explicitly: "pidgins are extreme
forms of analytic languages: they mostly lack inflections, and rely on free morphemes to indicate
grammatical relations. For this reason, word-order is of great importance, and is strictly adhered
to" — i.e. pidgin grammar isn't merely "simplified English" in some vague impressionistic sense, but
a coherent, internally-principled typological endpoint (maximally analytic, maximally word-order-
dependent) that happens to recur convergently across unrelated pidgins worldwide, exactly the same
typological category Chapter 1's analytic/synthetic continuum (`pt01`) already introduced in the
abstract — pidgins simply sit at that continuum's extreme analytic pole.

**Lexical innovation within pidgins/creoles, despite drawing most vocabulary from the donor
language**: existing English words are frequently given genuinely **new meanings** (West African
*chop* "to eat," *bif* "animal, meat" — see Vocabulary table); **conversion** (the same word used
unchanged as noun, verb, and adjective) is described as especially common, a direct structural
consequence of the same "no formal word-class marking" bareness already identified as the mechanical
cause of Modern English's own high conversion-productivity in `pt07`, here operating at an even more
extreme, systematic level; **compounding** remains productive, including striking cases of
**independent convergent formation** across geographically unconnected pidgins (*daiman* "corpse,"
from *die*+*man*, attested in **both** Atlantic and Pacific varieties with no plausible direct contact
between them); **reduplication** ("a common device"), illustrated via Jamaican Creole *smal* "small"
→ *smalsmal* "very small" — both an intensifying and (the book notes more generally, beyond this one
example) sometimes a genuinely distinct-meaning-forming device across pidgins/creoles worldwide; a
**shared nautical-vocabulary layer** across essentially all English-based pidgins regardless of
region, attributed to their common origin in maritime/trade contact, often with locally-shifted
meanings (*heave* → "push, lift"; *capsize* → "overturn, spill"; a Cameroon pidgin word derived from
*man-of-war* meaning "wasp"); and, strikingly, **a shared layer of Portuguese-derived vocabulary**
recurring across essentially all English-based pidgins worldwide regardless of their own donor
language or region (West African *akara* "kind of pancake," itself originally from Yoruba, alongside
*saber* "to know" and *pequeno* "small, little" — the latter frequently the source of a pidgin's word
for "child" specifically) — the book treats this cross-pidgin Portuguese layer as significant
historical evidence in its own right, since the Portuguese were among the earliest European
explorers/colonizers in many of these regions, and floats the specific hypothesis that **many
English-based pidgins may have originally been Portuguese-based pidgins that were subsequently
"relexified"** (their core grammatical structure retained while their vocabulary was substantially
replaced) once English presence and influence displaced Portuguese dominance in a given region — a
genuinely substantive historical-linguistic claim about pidgin origins, not just a vocabulary-trivia
aside.

### The Nigerian Pidgin specimen text: a fifth data-point in the book's running Luke 15 comparative series

The chapter's closing worked example is a short Nigerian Pidgin passage (recorded by Dr. Loreto Todd
in Port Harcourt, 1985) — explicitly **the same Luke 15 "Prodigal Son" passage** (specifically verses
31–2, the father's closing words) already used comparatively across Old English, Middle English
(Wycliffe), and Early Modern English (King James Bible) in `pt01`'s Chapter 2 discussion, and the
book explicitly invites the reader to compare this new rendering against those three earlier ones —
making it, in this project's own cross-`073_*`-part terms, a genuine **fifth** data-point in one
continuous comparative-attestation series spanning the entire history of English from the early 11th
century to 1985. Grammar/vocabulary points the book draws from the passage, directly confirming the
general pidgin-grammar sketch above with concrete textual evidence: *bin tok sei* "said" (*bin*, the
past-tense particle already catalogued above, here co-occurring with *sei*, glossed by the book as a
particle "rather like the conjunction 'that'" introducing reported speech, structurally resembling
a complementizer rather than a true lexical verb of saying in this specific construction); *pikin*
"child" and *sabi* "know," both independently identified by the book as the pidgin's own
Portuguese-derived vocabulary layer, directly confirming the cross-pidgin Portuguese-substrate pattern
discussed above with a concrete in-text instance; *dei* functioning as (roughly) the verb "to be,"
alongside a **second**, distinct verb *na* also used for "to be" — the book notes Nigerian Pidgin
draws **no formal distinction at all between "you" and "your"** (*dis yu broda* "this your brother" —
i.e. no case/possessive-determiner distinction, directly matching the West African Pidgin *wi*
syncretism already catalogued in the general sketch above); *-am* added to the verb *get* to form a
fused possessive/object-pronoun construction meaning "everything that I have" (*gɛt-am* "have it/him/
her/them"); *wei* functioning as an all-purpose relative pronoun covering "who, which, that" (directly
paralleling Modern English's own historical pattern, already traced across `pt03`–`pt06`, of a small
set of forms doing broad relative-clause duty without fine-grained antecedent-type restriction), which
when **reduplicated** (*wei-wei*) instead means "always" — a second, independent worked instance of
the reduplication mechanism already introduced via Jamaican Creole *smalsmal* above, now shown
operating on a *different* word class (a pronoun/relative marker, not an adjective) and producing a
different *kind* of derived meaning (a frequency adverb, not an intensified adjective) — i.e. the
specimen text demonstrates reduplication is a genuinely general-purpose pidgin word-formation
strategy, not tied to one specific word class or one specific semantic effect; both the past-tense
and the perfect-tense markers (*bin*, *don*) co-occurring together in a single verb phrase (*bin don
dai* "had died"), while the perfect marker alone, without the past marker, covers a resultative "has
returned (and is now back)" sense (*i don wikɔp* "he has returned (woken up)") — the same word *dai*
covers both "die" (the verb) and "death" (the noun) with no formal distinction, a further instance of
the pidgin-wide conversion-productivity pattern already catalogued above; and the passage uses only a
small, closed set of prepositions overall, with *wit* and *fɔ* covering the bulk of the prepositional
work the passage requires — directly confirming the general-sketch claim that pidgins minimize
grammatical machinery wherever a reduced inventory can still do the necessary communicative work.

### "Standard World English": a solid written core beneath genuinely wide spoken variation

The chapter's closing methodological payoff, directly setting up the terrain for the final chapter
(`pt09`): despite the genuinely enormous range of variation documented across this entire chapter —
from minor British/American grammatical differences through to pidgins/creoles different enough to
count as wholly separate languages — the book argues there remains **"a solid core of common usage"**
making it meaningful to speak of a single "standard world English" at all. Three specific, stacked
qualifications structure this claim: (1) **regional variation is concentrated in speech, especially
informal speech**, and tends to be smaller among higher socio-economic-status speakers within any
given variety; (2) **pidgins and creoles developed from English are different enough to be thought of
as separate languages**, not variant forms of English at all — i.e. the "solid core" claim is
explicitly **not** meant to paper over or minimize the pidgin/creole material just surveyed, which the
book treats as a genuinely distinct linguistic category outside the "varieties of English" frame
entirely; (3) within the remaining range of genuine English varieties (first- or second-language),
**formal written English specifically** is "practically the same throughout the English-speaking
world," with vocabulary differences "perceptible but not enormous" and spelling differences
"negligible" — so it is this specific register (formal written usage), not spoken usage generally and
certainly not pidgin/creole usage, that the book identifies as "if anything," the genuine empirical
referent deserving the label "Standard English."

## Grammar points

### §173–176. Preliminary notes: phonology as a discipline, phonemes/allophones, IPA

Phonology is the attempt to describe and classify the "phones" (sounds) of a language into their regular patterns. For a living language this can be verified by ear or instrument; for a language like OE, known only from written remains and later history, it can only be inferred approximately, with no possibility of verification. Two kinds of sound-difference are distinguished: **phonemes** (sounds whose contrast significantly distinguishes words for native speakers, i.e. in *contrastive distribution* — e.g. Mod.E. [t]/[k] in *till*/*kill*) and **allophones** (differences not usually noticed by speakers because they occur accidentally through neighbouring-sound influence rather than distinguishing words, i.e. in *complementary distribution* — e.g. Mod.E.'s several phonetically distinct k-sounds in *keep, cool, look, looked*, all "the same phoneme" to an English speaker). Classification into phonemes/allophones is always language-relative: what is phonemic in one language may be allophonic in another (the book's example: Mod.E. *l*-sounds in *plead*/*lead* are allophones of one phoneme, but the same contrast is phonemic in Welsh; conversely some languages — Tamil is cited — do not distinguish [k] and [g], which are contrastive phonemes in Mod.E.).

For describing OE sounds, the book uses **IPA (International Phonetic Alphabet)** symbols in square brackets, a colon after a symbol indicating length. A **narrow transcription** indicates every phonetic feature whether phonemic or not; a **broad transcription** indicates only contrastive features, more convenient for practical purposes. A full OE vowel-symbol table is given (§176), correlating OE orthographic symbol, IPA value, a Modern language illustrative example, and a descriptive label (back/front, high/mid/low, rounded/unrounded, short/long) — e.g. OE *a* = [a] as in Fr. *pâté* (low, back); OE *ǣ* = [æ:] as in hand-with-drawled-RP (lengthened/tenser [æ]); OE *e/ē* = [ɛ]/[e:] as in *let*/Germ. *weg*; OE *i/ī* = [ɪ]/[i:] as in *bit*/*feet*; OE *o* = [ɔ] as in *spot*; OE *ō* = [o:] as in Germ. *wohl*; OE *u/ū* = [ʊ]/[u:] as in *pull*/*fool*; OE *y/ȳ* = [y]/[y:] as in Germ. *füllen*/*fühlen*; unstressed *a, e, o* all reduce to schwa [ə] as in *china*; the rare Anglian vowel *oe* = [œ] as in Germ. *Göttingen*; its long counterpart *œ̄* = [ø:] as in Germ. *schön*. OE *æ, e, i, y* are **front** vowels; *a, o, u* are **back** vowels. **Semivowels** [j] and [w] are described as very short [i]/[u] or as consonants with vowel-like quality, sounded like the initial sounds of *your* and *wagon*. **Consonants** function as syllable-boundaries and are treated summarily since their symbols mostly match Mod.E. conventions; the voiced plosives [b, d, g] and voiceless plosives [p, t, k] are distinguished; [b, p, m] are bilabials, [f, v] labio-dentals, [d, t, n, r, s, z] (post-)dentals/alveolars; [m, n, ŋ] are nasals, and [l, r] "liquids" have vowel-like quality enabling them to be syllabic (marked with a small stroke underneath). Velars [k, g, x] are distinguished from palatals produced with the hard palate (like the [k] of *keep*). The fricatives [ð, v, z, ʒ] are voiced, [θ, f, s, ʃ] voiceless; a velarised [ɹ] with the tongue-tip curled up, found post-vocalically in Somerset, is called retroflex [ɻ]. Affricates [tʃ] and [dʒ] consist of a plosive followed by a fricative.

### §177–178. Significant sound-changes, and Gmc/Gothic/Primitive-OE terminology

The sound-changes described in what follows are "significant" ones whose understanding/memorisation is necessary for mastering OE grammar's patterns and practice. For illustrating specifically Germanic features, Latin forms are cited in contrast as a conveniently cognate IE reference point; Gothic forms (from Bishop Wulfila's partial 4th-century Bible translation, the oldest written Gmc remains, preserved with "astonishing consistency of spelling" in the Codex Argenteus) are also frequently cited since they are old and very close to assumed Common Germanic. **Common Germanic (Gmc)** means the mass of common features shared by the ancestors of the Germanic languages (not identical to "Primitive Germanic," since it is not certain all such phenomena existed at the same time/place to form a single uniform language); forms marked with an asterisk are **reconstructions**, not attested forms. **Primitive OE (Pr.OE)** denotes reconstructed pre-7th-century forms before the earliest written OE remains begin.

### §179. The First Germanic Consonant Shift (Grimm's Law)

A most outstanding Gmc feature: a complex series of regular prehistoric consonant changes, formulated by Jakob Grimm in 1822 as "Grimm's Law" / "the First Sound Shift." For OE purposes, the two most important parts are: **(a)** the series of IE voiced plosives [b, d, g, gw] became the corresponding voiceless plosives [p, t, k, kw]; **(b)** the series of originally voiceless plosives of IE became respectively the voiceless fricatives [f, θ, x, xw]. Illustrative cognate pairs with Latin given in the book: (a) *labor* 'I fall'/*slēpan* 'sleep' [labial pair]; *genu*/*cnēo(w)* 'knee'; *pedem*/*fōt* 'foot'; (b) *dentem*/*tōþ* 'tooth'; *vēnī*/*c(w)ōm* 'came'; *collem*/*hyll* 'hill'; *tertius*/*þridda* 'third'; *quod*/*hwæt* 'what'. Although Grimm's Law is more relevant to the comparative Gmc philologist than the OE student directly, it is useful background: the relationship between pairs like *bycgan* 'buy' pret. *bohte*, *scyppan* 'create'/*gesceaft* 'creation', *hycgan* 'think', *magan* 'be able' 2sg.pres. *meaht/miht*, becomes clearer if one knows that in IE any labial or velar followed by *t* had produced respectively *pt* (from labials) and *kt* (from velars), where, by the second part of Grimm's Law, the *p* in *scyppan* regularly became *ft* (giving *gesceaft*) by the unvoicing process from IE *pt*. The *g* in *bycgan* (as also *hycgan, bugjan*) came about through a separate, third part of Grimm's Law, by which IE aspirated voiced plosives ([bh, dh, gh]) lost their aspiration and took the place of the voiced plosives shifted under (a) — Gothic *bugjan* 'buy' keeps IE [gh] unshifted, while OE *bycgan*'s *g* goes back to IE [gh] via this third part; the *ht* of the preterite *bohte* goes back to the IE group *kt* formed as outlined above.

### §180. The Second Germanic Consonant Shift (Verner's Law)

In IE and early Gmc, word stress varied according to grammatical function, but after Grimm's Law operated, the stress became fixed on the root syllable; before this happened, however, an important change in consonant pronunciation took place, first clearly described in 1877 by the Dane Karl Verner and known as **Verner's Law**: the Gmc voiceless fricatives [f, θ, x, xw] (from IE [p, t, k, kw] via Grimm's Law) and [s] were **voiced** to [v, ð, ɣ, ɣw] and [z] when the main stress did **not** fall on the immediately preceding syllable, provided the change was not prevented by the proximity of other voiceless consonants. By subsequent sound-changes in West Germanic (reflected in OE), the resulting series was further modified: [ð] becoming [d], [z] becoming [r], [ɣ] remaining [ɣ] or becoming [j] according to whether the neighbouring vowels were back or front, and [ɣw] becoming [w] or [j] or [ɡw]. Since *f/ð* represents both [f] and [v] in OE spelling (likewise *h(w)*, again unless lost between vowels, and *w* or *g*; also *s* and *r*), Verner's Law appears in OE writing as interchanges between *þ/ð* and *d*; *h(w)* (again, unless lost between vowels) and *w* or *g*; and also *s* and *r*. Examples are most clearly seen in the vocalic verbs, where the original stress fell *after* the consonant in question in the preterite plural and past participle; thus, unless otherwise affected by analogy, the 2 sg. and 1-3 pl. pret. indic., the pret. subj., and the past pple of vocalic verbs in OE show the consonants resulting from Verner's Law when the stems of such verbs end in one of the affected consonants: *weorðan* 'become' beside *wurdon* etc., with [θ>ð>d]; *tēon* 'draw' (< *tēohan*), pret.sg. *tēah*, beside *tugon* etc., past pple *togen*, with [x>ɣ]; *cēosan* 'choose' beside *curon* etc., with [s>z>r]; *sēon* 'see' (cf. Go. *saihwan*, Lat. *sequor*) beside *sāwon* &c, past pple *sewen, segen*, with [xw>ɣw>j or w]. Similarly related are the medial consonants in various other related forms — *hrēosan* 'fall' beside the related noun *hryre* 'fall', *rīsan* 'rise' beside the related causative verb *rǣran* 'raise'. Apparently irregular-looking verbs like *hōn* 'hang' and *fōn* 'seize' become clearer once one remembers the lost *h* that alternated with *g* in the present and pret.pl. forms (reconstructed *\*hōhan, \*fōhan*, giving historical pres.pl. *hēngon, fēngon* etc.); OE *hw* remained only initially, medially it had early become *h* and this *h* was then lost between vowels, with consequent contraction along with original *h* — hence *sēon* (Go. *saihwan*, Gmc *\*sehwan*, Pr.OE *\*sehan*) can be seen to be a vocalic verb of Class V, with the effects of Verner's Law giving pret.pl. *sāwon, sǣgon, segon*, past pple *-sewen*, with diphthongisation before velarised consonants (§201) explaining the *ea-* in the present stem *seah*.

### §181. Clarifying the irregular-looking *hōn/fōn* forms, and *hw*/*h* loss

Building on the lost-*h* explanation above: such apparently irregular verbs become clearer once the lost *h* that alternated with *g* is remembered (reconstructed *\*hōhan, \*fōhan* give the attested *hōn, fōn* with usual loss of [x] and rounding of the stem vowel to *o* in this position, followed by lengthening, and (by Verner's Law) [ɣ] and subsequently [g] before nasals preceding voiceless fricatives — thus the loss of the nasal gives the markedly different *-ng-* cluster in these forms, i.e. historical pret.pl. *hēngon, fēngon*). OE *hw* remained only initially; medially it had early become *h*, and this *h* was in turn lost between vowels, with consequent contraction along with original *h*. Hence *sēon* (Go. *saihwan*, Gmc *\*sehwan*, Pr.OE *\*sehan*) is a vocalic verb of Class V, and by Verner's Law its pret.pl. forms are *sāwon, sǣgon, segon*, past pple *-sewen, -sewen*, with diphthongisation before velarised consonants explaining the *ea-* in present *seah*.

### §182–183. Vowel-Gradation (Ablaut)

**Vowel-gradation**, which Grimm termed **Ablaut**, is defined as the patterned variation of vowel-sounds, in relation to meaning, in forms of the same root. Taking the consonants of a word as its minimal root, one may vary the vowel between the consonants — called **root-gradation** (*drīfan–drāf–drifon–-drifen*); one may similarly vary the vowel of a suffix in inflecting words — **suffix-gradation**. This varying vowel existed in IE, probably closely connected with variations of stress, pitch, and intonation. In particular, root-gradation was used in IE as one means of conjugating verbs, and it is as a development of this practice that the Gmc (and therefore OE) vocalic verbs are differentiated in their tenses to a large extent by variation of their root-vowel, in accordance with regular gradation-series (§§74ff). Gradation may be qualitative (vowel varied in quality, as in *drīfan, drāf*) or quantitative (the variation is a matter of length, as between pret.sg. and pl. of *metan* 'measure': *mæt, mǣton*). Besides vocalic verbs' conjugation, other groups of words sharing the same root are said to be in "gradation-relationship" (while, in some cases, also showing the results of later sound-changes); thus *beran* 'bear', *gebǣre* 'behaviour', *byre* 'son'; *bēodan* 'command', *gebod* 'an order', *bydel* 'a messenger'.

§183: in IE there was a basic gradation series: *e, o*, lengthened, and reduced grades (that is, the basic vowels *e* or *o* might also be lengthened or reduced to either a vanishing-point vowel schwa [ə] (vanishing/zero-grade) or to zero itself). The Gmc base of OE *cuman* 'come' was *\*gwem-, \*gwom-, \*gwm̥-* (with zero-grade and syllabic *m̥*); these forms would become Gmc *\*kwem-, \*kwam-, \*kwum-* (short *o* becoming regularly a in Gmc, zero-grade becoming *-um-, -un-, -ur-, -ul-* in conjunction with syllabic consonants m̥, n̥, l̥, r̥). Hence Gmc principal parts like Go. *qiman, qam, qēmun, qumans* (with *e* raised to *i* in the infinitive). In OE the *w* is lost before *u* in the pret.pl. form, giving *cwōmon*; this lengthened *ō* is then analogically extended to the sg., giving *cwōm*; before the *ō*, the *w* later falls, hence forms both with and without it in OE: *cwōm(-), cōm(-)*. Similarly, though not itself an aorist-present, *niman* 'take' has pret.pl. *nōmon* as well as *nāmon*, and the *ō* is often extended to the sg. giving *nōm* beside the historically expected form *nam*. Other aorist-presents are *murnan* 'lament' and *spurnan* 'kick'. A few further vocalic-Class-II verbs are termed aorist-presents because they have lengthened *u* in the pres. stem, perhaps inaccurately since the origin of the *-ū-* is not clear — possibly the lengthening is, by some sort of analogy, of the short *u* of the aorist-grade type: of this sort are *brūcan* 'enjoy', *būgan* 'bend', *lūcan* 'shut', *scūfan* 'push'.

In IE, there was also a full grade (*e, o*), lengthened grade, and reduced grade (schwa or zero) series. Combinations with the diphthongal elements *i* and *u* give the series of gradation behind most of the classes of vocalic verbs in OE: Class I (Gmc *ī* (IE *ei*)/*ai*/*i* (zero+*i*)): OE *bidan, bād, bidon/biden*; Class II (Gmc *eu*/*au*/*u, o* (zero+*u/o*)): OE *beodan, bead, budon/boden*; Class III (Gmc *e, i*/*a*/*u, (o)*): OE *helpan, healp, hulpon/holpen*; Class IV (Gmc *e*/*a*/*ǣ*/*o*): OE *beran, bær, bǣron/boren*; Class V (Gmc *e*/*a*/*ǣ*/*e*): OE *metan, mæt, mǣton/meten*. In Class V the vowel of the past pple has perhaps been influenced/replaced by that of the present stem. The Class I gradation series can be seen in the Latin cognates *fīdus* (<*\*feidus*), *foedus* (<*\*foidus*), and *fidēs*; the *o* in the reduced grade of Classes II, III, and IV arises because *u* is lowered to *o* in open syllables except before nasals — it is not too easy to explain the lengthened grade-vowel *ǣ* with the OE pret. *ǣt* of *etan* 'eat', and one may compare the Latin perfect *ēdī* of *edere*, reckoning upon some kind of reduplication-process. The series in Class VI is different from the others; Greek *agō* 'drive', *ogmos* 'furrow' point to an IE *a/o* series, and with a lengthened grade *ō*, the two short vowels falling together in Gmc, there would result the *a/ō* alternation found in OE *faran* 'go' and its pret. *fōr*.

### §184. Lengthening of Consonants

In West Gmc, all consonants except *r* were lengthened after short syllables by the influence of an immediately following [j]. This is the cause of most of the doubled consonants in OE (though there were earlier Gmc consonant changes which had already produced some, e.g. the *-ll-* and *-nn-* in words like *eall* and *mann*), as well as later purely orthographic doublings as in *hlæddre* for earlier *hlǣdre* (indicating vowel-shortening), *nædre, moddrie* for earlier *mōdrie*. The long consonants of earlier OE were indicated graphically by doubling, hence the term **gemination** (Lat. *gemini* 'twins', in the expression "West Gmc Gemination"); but phonetically the process is consonant *lengthening* and not consonant doubling — the use of double letters merely a graphic device. Examples of W.Gmc consonant-lengthening after short vowels by means of a following *-j-* are: *fremman* 'perform' (Gmc *\*framjan*), *trymman* 'strengthen' (cf. the adjective *trum*), *biddan* 'pray' (Go. *bidjan*), *sittan* 'sit' (cf. ON *sitja*), *scyppan* 'create' (Go. *skapjan*). It will be seen that the *j* which caused lengthening also mutated the vowel (see §§208f) or caused raising from *e* to *i* (§207) in the preceding syllable; *fremman* for instance is a consonantal verb of Class I, formed from *fram* 'forward' plus the suffix *\*-ja-* (see §§161-3), the *j* having mutated the vowel as well as lengthening the consonant *m*. The semi-vowel *j*, being a palatal sound, must have palatalised the immediately preceding consonant and then become absorbed in this palatalised form — thus *cynn* 'race' (Go. dat.sg. *kunja*), *bed(d)* 'bed' (Go. *badi*), etc. But *r* did not undergo lengthening, nor did it absorb the following *j*; thus we have such verbs as *nerian* 'save' beside Go. *nasjan*, and the noun *here* 'raiding party' (Go. acc.sg. *hari*) with its gen./dat. forms *heri(g)es, heri(g)e* (Go. *harjis, harja*). That the *g* of such forms as *licgan* 'lie' and *bycgan* 'buy' was palatalised by the following *j* (cf. ON *ligja*, Go. *bugjan*) is shown by the OE spelling *-cg-* and later forms; compare also *hycgan* 'think' (Go. *hugjan*). The consonant-lengthening did not take place when the *j* was immediately followed by *i*; in such cases the *j* coalesced with the *i*. This is the explanation of the contrast between the lengthened consonants in 1 sg.pres.indic. of Class I consonantal verbs such as *fremman* (*ic fremme*) and the short consonant of 2 and 3 sg. (*þū frem(e)ð, hē frem(e)ð*, while the pl. again has the long form (*fremmað*). The original OE endings of 2 and 3 sg.pres.indic. were *\*-jis, \*-jiþ*, whereas all the pl. had the *j* but not followed by *i*. This lengthening is important also because there are several vocalic verbs with present forms constructed originally with *-j-* (like consonantal verbs) but otherwise regular. Thus in Class V, *biddan, licgan,* and *sittan* are of the same gradation series as *metan*, but have their lengthened consonant and their *i* raised from *e* in the preceding syllable to disguise the connexion. Similarly in Class VI 'go', we have *scyppan* 'create' and *sceððan* [sic — likely *sceþþan*] 'harm' (compare Go. *skapjan, skaþjan*). In the same class, *hebban* 'raise' has the same '*j*-present' phenomenon but operates in most verbs with *j*-present since the stress was originally after the root; hence the Gmc *f* (a voiceless bilabial fricative [ɸ]) would be voiced to a bilabial fricative [β]. This voiced fricative [β] would become a plosive when lengthened in W.Gmc and this form appears in OE as *-bb-*; swebban 'put to sleep' and habban 'have'. More irregular is the vocalic verb swerian 'swear', originally Class VI; it shews its usual absence of lengthening of *r* as the preservation of the *j* as *i* (Gmc *\*swarjan*), and is thus parallel to the consonantal verb nerian 'save' (Gmc *\*nerjan*); but swerian went over to Cl.IV in its past pple gesworen. Other *j*-presents in Cl.VI are hlihhan (Go. hlahjan) 'laugh' and steppan 'step' (Go. stapjan).

### §185. Loss of nasal consonants

There was a tendency for nasal consonants to disappear in pronunciation in later Gmc before the voiceless velar fricative [x], and in the West Gmc group which included OE, nasals in general were lost before all voiceless fricatives, with compensatory lengthening of the preceding vowel and rounding of *a* to *o*. Hence Go. *fimf* (compare Welsh *pump*) beside OE *fīf*; OHG *gans* beside OE *gōs*. The Gmc combination *\*-anh-* produced Pr.OE *\*-ōh-*, and thus Gmc *\*fanhanan* gave OE *fōn* 'seize', already explained in §181. Similarly *þencan* (Go. *þagkjan*) 'think', *þyncan* (Go. *þugkjan*) 'seem' shew retention of the nasal consonant, while the corresponding pret. forms *pōhte*, Gmc *\*panxta*, and *pūhte*, Gmc *\*pun(k)xta*, shew loss of the nasal before the voiceless fricative together with lengthening.

### §186. Influence of nasals

OE minor sound-changes begin here: **(a)** nasals tended to nasalise and sometimes round a preceding *a*; these factors probably underlie the spelling variations between dialects/periods for *a* and *o* in words like *mann(monn)* 'man', *cann(conn)* 'can'. **(b)** The nasals *m* and *n* raised a preceding *e* to *i* when this vowel immediately preceded them; thus we have *niman* 'take', beside ON *nema*, and *bindan* 'bind', beside Go. *gemma*/OE adopted form *gim(m)* 'gem'. **(c)** When an original *u* was followed in Gmc by a low or mid back vowel, as in the past pple of vocalic verbs, the OE form has generally lowered *o*; but this phenomenon was generally prevented by a following *m* or *n*; thus we have *geholpen, -boren* as the past pples of *helpan* and *beran*, with the regular lowering of *u* to *o*, while those of *bindan* and *niman* are *-bunden* and *-numen*, with the original *u* preserved by the nasals. **(d)** The normal fronting of Gmc *ā* to OE *ǣ*, which characterises OE, is prevented by a following nasal (*brægd* and *band* respectively from *bregdan* 'pull' and *bindan* 'bind'); note also *mann* or *monn* beside *wæð* 'bath', both words having Gmc *a*, and the pret. forms of the same class of vocalic verbs are *nāmon* (from *niman*) and *bæron* (from *beran*). **(e)** Finally, the proximity of a nasal tends to round *ā* to *ō*, so that we have pret.pl. *nōmon* beside *nāmon*, and *c(w)ōmon* for the historically expected *c(w)āmon* (with Gmc *ā* instead of the normal *ǣ* of Class IV, as explained in (d) above).

### §187. Influence of *w*

The semi-vowel *w* has the following effects on contiguous vowels: **(a)** it changes a following *eo* to *u* in late WS: *weorðan* > *wurðan*. **(b)** it may round a following *ī* to *ȳ*, as in *swȳðe*, earlier *swīðe*; compare also *nyllan* (*ne+willan*) 'be unwilling'; a later instance of the rounding influence of [w] is the Mod.E. pronunciation of *quality* [kwɒliti], as compared with the French cognate where the *w* has been lost. **(c)** It retracts a contiguous *ǣ* to *ā*, as in the pret.pl. *sāwon* (beside *sægon*) of *sēon* 'see', Cl.V (see §180), *hlāw* 'mound' and *hrā(w)* 'corpse' beside *hrǣw*.

### §188. Vowel alternations

Being a consonantal form of *u*, *w* becomes *u* finally after short syllables ending in a consonant; thus *bearu* 'grove', but gen.sg. *bearwes*; similarly *gearu* 'ready', but *gearwes*; *beadu* 'battle', but *beadwe*. But after long stems this *w* or *u* disappears in pronunciation like any other *u* (see §198); hence *mǣdwe* but *mǣd* 'meadow'. See further §§38, 51, 52. Before *u*, *w* tends to be lost; hence the past pple *cumen* from *\*cwumen*, and the noun *sund* 'swimming' beside the verb *swimman*; *w* is sometimes lost also before *ō*, as in the pret. *cōm* 'came' beside older *cwōm*.

We have seen in the immediately preceding paragraphs some examples of the interchange of vowels under the influence of nasals (binding: *cunnan*: *cūðe*, where loss of the nasal in the pret. produces vowel alternation) and of *w*. Here we also have the lengthened vowel resulting from 'compensation' for the loss of a nasal in the pret. and past pple (*ongunnon*, pret. of *onginnan* 'begin'), and similar pairs. It will be seen, therefore, that besides the major variations in vowels brought about by gradation (§§182f) and *i*-mutation (§§208ff), there are several fairly regular interchanging pairs of vowels. Those described so far are the results of the influence of nasals and of *w*. Other vowel alternations, similarly caused by neighbouring sounds, include the unrounding of *ȳ* in proximity to *c, g,* and *h*, as in *dryhten* beside late WS *drihten*, early *brȳcð* (3sg.pres.indic. of *brūcan* 'enjoy') beside *bricð*. There is a kind of alternation between final *-u* and zero, with *u* lost after long syllables, and the replacement of *s* and *l* by *y* in frequent late WS spellings of *self* 'self', *sellic* 'marvellous', *sellan* 'give', resulting in the forms *sylf, syllic, syllan*. There is also a kind of alternation between short stems and long stems in noun/adjective pairs, with *u* lost after long syllables, e.g. *scipu* 'ships' beside *land* 'lands' (both neut.pl.; see §31), *sunu* 'son' beside *hand* (Go. *handus*) 'hand', nouns of the same declension (§43).

### §189. Loss of *h*

The voiceless velar fricative [x] represented by *h* was lost very early when it came between vowels or between the liquids *l* or *r* and a vowel. Thus verbs whose roots originally ended in *-h-* have lost it in most of the OE paradigms (compare *tēon* 'draw', (*ic*) *tēo*, (*wē*) *tēoð* with the reconstructed early forms *\*teōhan, \*teōhu, \*teōhaþ*; so too the rest of the present, except the 2 and 3 sg. pres.indic., on which see §191, and the sg. imperat. *tēoh* and 1, 3 sg. pret. *tēah*. Original Gmc *hw*, appearing in OE as *h* except initially (*hwā* 'who'), was treated in the same way; thus Gmc *hw* *\*seohwan* '> *\*seohan* > *seon* (<*\*sehwan*>*\*sehan*>*sēon* 'see'), beside pret.sg. *seah*, in the same way *Gmc \*lihwan*>*\*lihan*>*\*liohan*>*lion*>*leon* (lend, Go. *leihwan*). Nouns and adjectives ending in *h* generally lose it in inflexions; thus *hēah* 'high' but gen.sg.masc. *hēas* (§52), *feorh* 'life' but gen.sg. *feores*, *wealh* 'foreigner' but gen.sg. *weales* (§§33, 27). In all the instances of the loss of intervocalic *h*, there was contraction of the first vowel or diphthong with the second vowel (see §190), giving a long vowel or diphthong whatever the length of the first vowel originally; thus *fēos* (<*\*feohes*), gen.sg. of *feoh* 'property', gen.sg. of *sc(e)ōh* 'shoe'. On the other hand, when *h* was lost between a liquid and a vowel, the vowel or diphthong in the preceding syllable remained unchanged in length; thus *weales* (gen.sg. of *wealh*), *feores* (gen.sg. of *feorh*).

### §190. Contraction

Vowels and diphthongs, upon loss of intervocalic *h* (§189), absorb the following vowel; thus *\*teōhan*, resulting in *\*teōhan*, appears as *tēon* 'draw', and *\*heāhes* from *heāhes*, as *hēas*, masc. gen.sg. of *hēah* 'high'; short vowels or diphthongs under these conditions were lengthened: *\*seohan*>*sēon* 'see', similarly *\*doan, \*doaþ*, and *\*gaan, \*gaaþ*, absorb intervocalic vowels without there having been a medial *h*, to give OE *dōn, dōð* 'do', and *gān, gāð* 'go'. In many cases, however, the uncontracted forms remained in early texts and in Anglian, and sometimes also inflexional syllables were restored by analogy with other words not subject to contraction.

### §191. Syncope and assimilation in verbs

The unstressed vowel of the final syllable in the 2 and 3 sg. pres.indic. is commonly reduced to zero or "cut between" in what is termed **syncope** in the OE verbs of Kt. and WS, while Angl. tends to retain the "unsyncopated" forms. Pr.OE endings *-is, -iþ*, after mutating the preceding vowel where possible (§§207, 209), became in historical times *-es(t)* and *-eð*, and it was the vowel of these forms that was syncopated. Hence syncope brought together the consonant(s) of the stem with those of the inflexions, assimilation where this produced unfamiliar consonant clusters. It will be seen that this syncope brought the forms of the 2 and 3 sg. pres.indic. close to those of the inflexions, assimilation where this produced unfamiliar consonant clusters, where this possible. Examples given: *demst* and *demþ* of *dēman* 'judge', *hilpst* and *hilpð* of *helpan* 'help', *fylst* and *fylð* of *fyllan* 'fill'. It will be seen that this syncope brought the consonants of the stem into contact with those of the inflexions. Where assimilation occurred, further forms arise: *bintst* and *bint* from *bindan*, *cwiðð* (from *cweðan*), *cȳst* and *cēosð* from *cēosan*, *bit(t)* (biddan 'ask'), *birst* (berstan 'burst'), *itt* (etan 'eat'). The Angl. unsyncopated forms, however, usually have unmutated vowels; see §76, note.

### §192. Alternation of *æ* and *a*

West Gmc *ā* were fronted to *ǣ* in the earliest OE period, but the anticipatory or attractional influence of a following back vowel (*a, o,* or *u*) either prevented this normal fronting (in the case of the short vowel, and at times also the long) or caused the fronted sound to be retracted again. Thus *dæg* 'day' but pl. *dagas*; *mæg* 'kinsman' but pl. *māgas* (though *mægas* also occurs); so too *bæð* 'bath' with pl. *baðu*, *fæt* 'vessel' with pl. *fatu*, *glæd* 'happy' with pl.n.a.neut. *gladu* etc. (see §§27, 32, 51). In this alternation, we have a kind of vowel-harmony; in pronouncing the front vowel *æ*, the fact that a back vowel was to follow immediately after a single consonant induced a more retracted variety of front vowel.

### §193. 'Unstable *i*'

The diphthongs *iē*, which were a special feature of early WS (on their origin, see §§204, 210), had come by King Alfred's time to be pronounced as simple vowels; this is shown by reverse spellings such as *hiene* for *hine* 'him', *hieder* for *hider* 'hither' in the contemporary Hatton MS of King Alfred's version of the *Cura Pastoralis*, and by *hīran* for *hīeran* 'hear', *gelifan* for *gelȳfan* 'believe' in the Cotton MS (as copied by Junius in the seventeenth century) of the same text. The *ī* thus monophthongised from the earlier diphthongs *iē* must have been for some time different three phonemes from the original OE *ī*, because they regularly became *ȳ* in classical OE, in which the original *ī* did not. The new *ī* is termed "unstable *i*" because they often alternate in spelling with *ie* and *y*, and are generally spelt *y* in classical texts of the time of the Benedictine revival, being thus distinct from original *ī*; compare *gelȳfan* 'believe' with earlier variant spellings *gelifan* and *gelyfan* while *bidan* 'wait' remains constant in general. The exact pronunciation of this pair of sounds is not known, but it must have been of a nature to develop into high front rounded [y] and [y:]. When it is said, therefore, that in late WS the diphthongs *iē* were monophthongised to *ȳ*, this is a loose and misleading way of indicating that unstable *ī* were rounded to *ȳ*. The new *ȳ* sounds proceeded to share the development of the other *ȳ* (resulting from the *i*-mutation (§208) of *ū*, as in *trymman* 'strengthen' beside the adjective *trum*), though it will be remembered, however, that all OE *ȳ* were frequently unrounded to *ī* in proximity to *c, g,* or *h* (see §188), and hence common forms as *gifan* 'give', from early WS *giefan*, instead of *gyfan*; *ongitan* 'perceive' (early WS *ongietan*) instead of *ongytan*.

Similarly, *iernan* 'run' and *biernan* 'burn' (in which the *ie, y* seem to have arisen through the influence of the *r* or *rn*) are early WS forms of *yrnan* (*irnan*) and *byrnan* (*birnan*). But these verbs have undergone an early metathesis of the initial consonant and vowel, for they are vocalic verbs of Class III, developing respectively from *rinnan* and *brinnan* (compare Go. has in fact *brinnan* as well as *rinnan*, in the same class); because of this metathesis, they have diphthongisation before velarised consonants (§201) in the 1 and 3 sg. pret. (*earn, bearn*), but they retain *u* in the past pple by reason of the nasal which still immediately followed it at the period when *u* was otherwise lowered to *o* in past pples (§186c).

### §194. Miscellaneous notes on minor sound-changes

Some considerable variation arises in the forms of OE through the working of **analogy**, the tendency for less frequent forms to imitate those of a more familiar kind; many words passed from less frequent declensional forms which they once had, and were "levelled" with commoner types; others seem to have fluctuated between two originally distinct ways of being declined. Thus *mete* 'food', which developed regularly from Pr.OE *\*mati* (Gmc *\*matiz*, Go. *mats*), also has a form *met(t)* as if from a Gmc *\*matja*, which would produce consonant-lengthening, indeed, the pl. double-consonant type, *mettas*, is the preferred form; similarly *hyse* 'man', though going back to a Gmc form *\*husiz*, shows forms with lengthened consonant as if from Gmc *\*husja*. There was originally a separate declension with stems ending in the vowel *i* (compare *\*matiz, \*husiz* above), and also a subdivision of the common masc. and neut. declension with stems in *-ja*; and while these two types have mostly been absorbed into the commoner ones, traces of these two facts remind us of this history. Traces of the *i*-declension survive in the gen.pl. *wini(ge)a* (beside *wina*) of *wine* 'friend', and *Deni(ge)a* (beside *Dena*) of *Dene* 'Danes'. Of the *ja*-stem declension, traces survive in the nom.pl. *rīc(i)u*, gen.pl. *rīc(e)a*, dat.pl. *rīc(i)um* of *rīce* 'kingship'.

OE had a number of noun declensions which are descended from well-known IE types, but relatively few examples of most of them are current, so that these "minor declensions" may simply be treated as "irregularities" by the non-philological student. We are able to recognise and reconstruct these declensions because, in addition to the Gothic remains, Runic inscriptions survive from as early as the 4th century to indicate something very near to the common Gmc types, and comparison with cognate languages confirms these findings. Thus we know there was a common masc. and neut. declension with stem in *-a*, as shown in Runic *stainaz* 'stone', of which the final *-z* is merely the mark of the nom.sg., the stem being *staina-*. Similarly we know a *u*-stem declension is to be inferred from Runic *gastiz* 'stranger', which lies behind OE *gist* (compare §194 declensional type). Again, a *u*-stem declension is to be inferred from Go. *sunus* 'son' (OE *sunu*) and *handus* (OE *hand*); the *ja*-stem type which survives in OE *here* 'army' enables us to infer something similar from Go. dat.sg. *harja*; and so on.

### §195–198. Further minor sound-changes

§195: the liquid and nasal consonants *l, r, m, n* tend to make possible the syncope of a preceding unstressed vowel in medial syllables, a feature common throughout the OE inflexions; thus *dryhten* 'lord' beside gen.sg. *dryhtnes*, *engel* 'angel' beside nom.acc.pl. *englas*, *frōfor* 'comfort' beside oblique cases *frōfre*. On the other hand, a slight vowel sound sometimes develops between consonants as the phonetic environment, such vowels being front or back according to the phonetic environment; thus *burg* 'fortress' has dat.sg. and nom.acc.pl. *byr(i)g*, while the nom.sg. sometimes appears as *buruh*; so too *þurh* beside *þuruh* 'through'. A convenient term for these "intrusive" or "parasitic" vowels, as they are sometimes called, is the ancient Indian grammarians' term **svarabhakti** (Sanskrit, literally "vowel part"); in pronouncing the name of the Hindu deity *Indra*, the old grammarians tell us, there was a sound somewhere between a half and a quarter of a full vowel between the *d* and the *r*; indeed, such a sound is a **partial vowel**, this being a more exact term than any of the others.

§196: the long ("doubled") consonants of early OE (§184) were later often written as single letters, the reason for this being that the phonemic distinction between long and short consonants had ceased to exist in final position for the most part. Thus the distinction between *mann* 'man' and *man*, the pronoun 'one', was indicated by early scribes in the final consonant, though later both words are often written *man* or *mon*. Similarly, such nouns as *cynn* 'race', *wedd* 'pledge', *wēstenn* 'desert' are often written with single final consonant, though the lengthened consonant continues to leave its mark in the inflected forms (*cynnes, wēstennes*), and medially in general (*sittan* 'sit').

§197: there was often assimilation of consonants in the course of inflexion, quite apart from that occasioned by syncope (§191). Thus *h* tended to be lost before a vowel and a following *n*, as in the acc.sg.masc. *hēah* 'high', *hēanne* or *hēane* with earlier *hēahne*; again, *g* tends to disappear before a following *d* or *n* with compensatory lengthening of a preceding short vowel; thus *sǣde*, pret. of *secgan* 'say', *lēde*, pret. of *lecgan* 'lay', beside *legde*; *brǣd* beside *brægd*, pret. of *bregdan* 'pull'; *þēn* beside *þegn* 'retainer', *drinan* beside *frignan* 'ask', *ihte*, pret. of *ican* 'increase', etc. Before *t*, *c* often becomes *h*, as in *tǣhte*, pret. of *tæcan* 'show', *worhte*, pret. of *wyrcan* 'work'. OE palatal *g* tended to be vocalised and become absorbed in a preceding front vowel, and it would probably be more accurate to describe the phenomena of *sǣde, þēn,* etc., as instances of the absorption of a vocalised *g* in the preceding front vowel, *e, æ,* etc. Similarly, the 3 sg. pres.indic. of *licgan* 'lie' is often written *liþ* in WS, from *ligeð (Angl. ligeþ)*.

§198: before an unstressed vowel, *w* often ceases to be pronounced, and this phenomenon is frequent in the pret. and past pple of consonantal verbs formed from nouns and adjectives with stems ending in *w*; for example, *gyrwan* 'prepare' (compare the adj. *gearu*) has pret. *gyrede* beside *gyrwede*, and similarly *syrwan* 'deceive' (compare the noun *searu* 'trick') has pret. *syrede* beside *syrwede*. Final unstressed *u* is often lowered to *o*, or even to *a*. Hence *gearo* beside *gearu* 'ready', *sido* beside *sidu* 'custom'; there are neut.pl. noun-forms in *-a* in Ælfric MSS, such as *dēofla* (for *dēoflu*) 'devils'.

### §199. Changes in vowel-length

During the OE period, though mainly in the latter part, there was lengthening of short vowels before *-ld, -mb, -nd*, sometimes also before *-rl, -rn, -rs*, and possibly before *-rd* and *-ng*. Thus *cild*>*cīld*, *gold*>*gōld*, and similarly we have *bindan, climban, hūnd* 'dog', and *mūrnan* 'care'. This lengthening was brought about by the vowel-like properties of liquids and nasals (and in this way may have some affinity with diphthongisation before velarised consonants), and a vocalic element from the *l, r, m* may be thought to have coalesced with the preceding vowel and so given it length. The lengthening did not take place if a third consonant immediately followed the lengthening cluster (compare *cīld* with the pl. *cildru*, §44, which never lengthened — compare Mod.E. *child* and *children*). Nor did the lengthening occur in less stressed words and positions; thus *and* 'and', *sc(e)olde* 'should', and *wolde* 'would' remained with short vowels. The *e* and *o* resulting from this lengthening were always the close vowels [e:] and [o:]. Just as the above lengthening failed to occur before groups of three consonants, as in *wūndrian* 'wonder' beside *wūndor* 'wound', so too any pair of consonants, other than the lengthening clusters, might cause shortening of preceding long vowels in the later OE period. Thus *blǣddre* 'bladder' became *blæddre, fōdres, fōdres* etc. became *fōddres, fōddres*, and so by analogy the nom. *fōdor* 'nourishment' also became *fōddor*; similarly *ātor* 'poison' became *āttor*; shortening before oblique forms with *-tr-* (*ātres*, for example). Other instances are *hlæddre* from *hlǣdre* 'ladder', *nædre* from *nǣdre* 'adder'. Doubled consonants, earlier a method of indicating contrastive consonant length (§184), came to be used to indicate the shortness of preceding vowels, when consonant length was no longer such a prominent phonemic feature.

### §200–215. Some Major OE Sound-Changes: introduction and ordering

§200: this final set of sound-changes is grouped by its particular importance to the varied forms of inflexion or because its phonetic nature is of particular interest or difficulty. They are treated in the probable order of their occurrence and in relation to other changes already described. They are severally the cause of the development of new front-round vowels and of new diphthongs beside the *ēa, ēo, īo* which had come down from Gmc, and (in WS only) of the diphthongs *iē* which later became "unstable *ī*" and then *ȳ*, as explained in §193.

### §201–202. Diphthongisation before velarised consonants ('Breaking')

**Breaking**: the front vowels *æ, ě, ĭ* were diphthongised to *ĕa, ĕo, ĭo* (later *ĭo*) when immediately followed by velar or velarised consonants or consonant groups. The consonants which had this effect were *ll* or *l* plus another consonant, *rr* or *r* plus another consonant, and *h* or *h* plus another consonant, though only *æ* diphthongised before all these. These all go back to Gmc forms, as shown by Go. cognates: *\*harduz* (cf. Go. *hardus*) > Pr.OE *\*hærd* > *heard* 'hard'; *\*armaz* (cf. Go. *armaz*) > Pr.OE *\*ærm* > *earm* 'poor'; *\*hairto* (= *\*herto*) > Pr.OE *\*hertu* > *heorte* 'heart' (cf. Go. *hairto*); *\*werpan* > Pr.OE *\*weorpan* > *weorpan* 'throw' (cf. ON *verpa*); *\*fehu* > *feoh* 'cattle' (cognate with Lat. *pecu*, see §179), etc. No diphthongisation took place before the combination *rj* (as in *nerian* 'save') or before *ll* produced by West Gmc consonant-lengthening (§184), as in *sellan* 'give' and *tellan* 'count' (Go. *saljan, taljan*). In WS no diphthongisation took place before *ll* or any *l*-group except *l* plus *h* (hence the regular form *helpan* 'help', but *healp* from *healpan*, with *h*), while before *sealde* etc. it will be noted that diphthongisation took place. It is important to remember that this diphthongisation is the addition of a vowel glide to the front vowel through the influence of certain velar qualities in following consonants; thus it is Pr.OE *æ*, not the earlier form *a*, that is diphthongised, and it is inaccurate to speak of "undiphthongised *a*" in cases where the usual diphthongisation has not occurred, or of the "diphthongisation of *a* to *ea*."

§202: the phonetic processes underlying this diphthongisation are inferred as follows: since the sounds of speech are continuous, not discrete, "glides" arise between different sounds as our speech-organs, while still producing one sound, form themselves into the position for producing the next. If the difference in the position of the organs is great, the glide is considerable; compare in London English today the back-vowel glide heard between the [ɪ] and [k] of *milk*: [mɪɔlk]. In OE the vowels diphthongised were front, and in words like *\*feh-* the speech organs would in forming themselves to make the voiceless velar fricative [x] give *u*-quality to the off-glide from *e*: [fex > feux > feox], spelt *feoh*. With the consonant groups beginning with *l* and *r*, on the other hand, there would also be the consonantal on-glides, since *l* and *r* have vocalic properties. In these groups, this "dark" or "velarised" quality (like the Londoner's "dark l" already mentioned) must have gone through the following stages in early OE: *heard* might be supposed to have gone through [hæɹd > hæuɹd > heauɹd], the second element of the diphthong being made up not only of the off-glide from the front vowel towards the position to form the velarised consonant, but also of the on-glide towards the consonant itself. It appears that at particular periods in particular dialects the difference in the positions required for these contiguous sounds became so extreme as to produce glides between the sounds clearly enough for the scribes to notice and record them in their orthography. The glides then formed diphthongs with the original vowels, and these diphthongs often remained after the special conditions that produced them had ceased to obtain.

### §203. Diphthongisation before velarised consonants: importance for verb conjugation

Diphthongisation before velarised consonants is important also especially in understanding the conjugation of vocalic verbs. *Bregdan, helpan, bindan* are all members of the same gradation-series (Cl.III), but just as the influence of the nasal has given us *bindan* instead of *\*bendan*, so *band* instead of *\*bænd*, diphthongisation has given us *healp* beside *brægd*; to this same class also belongs *weorðan*, instead of *\*werðan*. Again, the diphthongisation before a subsequently lost *h* produces forms where the gradation relationships are obscured; thus *sēon* and *metan* both belong to Cl.V, where Gmc *\*sehwan* passed through the stage *\*sehan*, then diphthongised as *\*seohan*, and so with loss of *h* and contraction became *sēon* (§§189, 190). The Cl.I verbs include *lēon* 'lend' beside *drīfan*; in this case we have Gmc *\*lihwan* > *\*lihan* > *\*liohan* > *\*lion* > *lēon*; so too, *wrēon* 'cover' (§75) was originally of Cl.III (later Cl.I) and its history is reflected in the past pple form used adjectivally, *geþungen* 'excellent' — the stages here are *\*pinhan* > *\*pihan* (§185) > *\*piohan* > *þīon* and *þēon*. In Cl.VI, *slēan* 'strike' has come through the stages *\*slahan* > *\*slæhan* > *\*sleahan*; so too, *lēan* 'blame', *þwēan* 'wash'. The diphthongisation did not take place in the infin. or pret. of verbs like *berstan* 'burst' and *perscan* 'thresh' (pret.sg. *bærst, pærsc*), because the post-vocalic position of *r* in these forms is the result of metathesis which occurred after the period of diphthongisation when they had the forms *\*brestan, \*prescan* etc.

The diphthongisation was far less frequent in Anglian dialects because their consonants developed differently and either did not undergo diphthongisation before velarised consonants at all or soon removed its results; it should be noted that the double *ll* in *eall* and *feallan* 'fall' does not result from West Gmc lengthening (§184), but from Gmc *ll*, and that this *ll* was of the sort occasioned the Angl. diphthongisation (compare §201). The so-called Anglian "smoothing," by which the results of diphthongisation before velarised consonants are removed before *c, g,* and *h*, so also *weorc* 'work' beside Angl. *werc*, *fehtan* beside *feohtan* 'fight'.

### §204. Diphthongisation after palatal consonants

When a palatal *c, g,* or *sc* immediately preceded *ǣ* or *ĕ*, these vowels developed into the diphthongs *ĕa* and *ĭe* respectively; this was a characteristically WS development, and is well illustrated by the principal parts of *gifan* 'give', a verb of Cl.V whose expected forms would be those of *metan* 'measure' (§81): *gefan, gæf, gǣfon, -gefen*, these forms in fact occur in non-WS. Early WS, on the other hand, has *giefan, geaf, gēafon, -giefen*. Similarly *geldan* 'pay' has the early WS infin. form *gieldan* (*-ld-* did not cause diphthongisation); likewise *giellan* 'scream', *gielpan* 'boast'. Other examples include *sceran* 'cut' Cl.IV *sceran* has the early WS infin. form *scieran*, with sg. and pl. pret. *scear, scēaron*. By King Alfred's time, *ĭe* had become "unstable *ĭ*" (§193), so that classical OE forms have *i* or *y* (*gifan, gyfan, gildan, gyldan*, etc.). It would appear that a front vowel-glide (*i* or *e*) developed between the strongly palatal consonant and the following back vowel in these cases, giving the rising diphthongs *ĭă, ĭæ*, etc. which later became falling diphthongs. For further remarks on their pronunciation, see §17. In *ceorl* 'man', the Gloss, however, often has the diphthongisation even before *c* and *g* (thus *spreocan* 'speak', *weogum* d.pl. of *weg* 'way'). Forms like *beoran* 'bear', *eotan* 'eat' are common on non-WS.

Diphthongisation before back vowels is often referred to as "back mutation" or the various *o/a*-mutation (the common type) and "*u*-mutation" (the less common type); it is not normal in WS, remaining only as the regular form in *ealu* (§46) 'ale' in classical OE; Kt has *alop* and (on the evidence of proper names containing the elements *badu* and *balu*, perhaps originally poetical and Angl., occur commonly with this diphthongisation of *æ* in classical OE as in Kt.

### §205. Notes on diphthongs

As stated in §18, classical OE had four diphthongs, *ea, ēa, eo, ēo*. The *ēa* developed regularly from Gmc *au* and has its OE form by the eighth century; thus Go. *daupus* beside OE *dēað* 'death'. Most of the examples of IE *eu* in Gmc, and *eu* gave Gmc *eu* and *iu* respectively; these two diphthongs fell together as *ēo* in late WS and classical OE, while in other dialects and earlier periods there were sometimes kept apart and sometimes levelled as *īo*. Thus, Alfredian WS and Kt frequently shew the levelling of both *ēo* and *īo* as *īo* (for example *biodan* 'offer' in the Hatton MS of the Alfredian version of *Cura Pastoralis*). Kt in all periods tended to level the two under *īo*, while Angl. normally raises *eo* to *io* as with the long diphthong. The short diphthongs *ea* and *eo*, as in *geaf* 'gave', *heard* 'hard', *leomu* 'limbs', *feorh* 'life', were produced in OE itself by the various diphthongisations. Here early WS tends to use *io* for *eo* (as in *liornian* 'learn'), and Kt normally raises *eo* to *io* as with the long diphthong. Both short and long diphthongs are subject to the Angl. smoothing or monophthongisation before *c, g, h,* singly or preceded by *r* or *l*; thus Angl. *hæh, hēh* for *hēah* 'high', *ēc* for *ēac* 'also', *eoh* for *eoh* 'horse'. In most late OE MSS *ēa* is replaced occasionally by *æ* in spelling (though careful scribes retain classical *ea*-forms); such spellings as *hærm* for *hearm* 'harm' in Classical OE texts are "occasional spellings" indicative of the monophthongisation of all the OE diphthongs which was complete in most areas by the time of the earliest ME texts.

In addition to that arising from the influence of preceding palatal consonants, long and short *ie*-forms arose in early WS by the *i*-mutation (§§206, 210) of *ēa, ēo, īo*; thus corresponding to *geleāfa* 'faith', we have the consonantal verb *geliēfan* 'believe', and beside *searu* 'device', the verb *sierwan* 'plot'. Such *ie*-spellings were partly replaced by *io*-forms by the end of the ninth century (thus *hīran*, early WS *hīeran* 'hear'), in the Cotton MS of the *Cura Pastoralis*), and these in turn by forms indicative of rounded vowels by the tenth century (see further §193). Outside the area of classical OE, the *i*-mutation of *ēa* is represented generally by *e*; thus late non-WS *hēran* for late WS *hȳran* 'hear'; non-WS *hildan* or *hyldan* for *hēldan* 'bend'. Sometimes Mercian texts have *ǣ* for the *i*-mutation of Angl. *ā*, WS *ēa*, as *hǣldan* (early WS *hliehhan*) 'laugh'. For *i*-mutation of *ēo, īo*, non-WS texts generally have *io*-spellings (thus *iorre* (WS *ierre*, later *yrre*) 'angry', *hiorde* (WS *hierde*, later *hyrde*) 'shepherd'), but Mercian is represented in the *Vespasian Psalter Gloss* usually has *eo*, and for the foregoing words has *eorre, heorde*.

In later OE, especially in the combination *ht, hh* became palatal [ç] and monophthongised and shortened the diphthongs *ēo* and *ēa* to *i*; thus early WS *feohtan, cneoht, lioht, meaht* (from *magan* 'might') become *fihtan, cniht, liht, miht*. This tendency is checked, however, when a back vowel immediately followed in the syllable following, so that the pl. of Alfredian and late OE *cniht* is often *cneohtas* of Piht 'Pict'. On the OE diphthongs, there is a useful recent study in the light of findings in acoustic phonetics, L. F. Brosnahan, *Some Old English Sound-Changes* (Cambridge, Heffer, 1953).

### §206–213. Mutations ('Umlaut')

Mutation, for which Grimm first used the term **Umlaut** (still widely current), is the change from one vowel to another through the influence (by attraction, assimilation, or anticipation) of a vowel in an immediately following syllable. Two important mutations are considered: first, the early Common Gmc mutations; secondly, Pr.OE *i*-mutation.

**Common Gmc mutations** (§207): two are significant for OE: first, the raising of *e* to *i* through the influence of an *i* or *j* in the immediately following syllable, or through its being in an unaccented position, or through the influence of a following nasal (see §186b); for example, *bir(e)ð*, 3sg.pres.indic. of *beran* 'bear' (Pr.Gmc *\*beriþi*), *niman* 'take'; secondly, the lowering of *u* to *o* through the influence of a back vowel in the next syllable (see §186c). For example, OE *æ* (Gmc *a*) > *e*, as in *bed(d)*, Gmc *\*badja* (cf. Go. *badi*); OE *ā* (Gmc *ai*) > *ǣ*, as in *hǣlan* 'heal', Gmc *\*hailjan* (cf. Go. *hailjan*, the OE adj. *hāl* 'whole', Go. *hails*); OE *o* (sometimes Gmc *u* analogically lowered) > *e* [œ] > *e*, as in *ele*, Lat. *olium*, and *exen*; OE *ō*>*ē*, as in *gēs* 'geese', sg. *gōs* (Pr.OE *\*gōsi*<Gmc *\*gansiz*, with loss of nasal and rounding: §§181, 185); OE *u*>*y*, as in the verb *trymman* 'strengthen' (<*\*trumjan*), beside the adj. *trum* 'strong'; OE *ū*>*ȳ*, as in *rȳman* 'make space', beside the adj. *rūm* 'spacious'. Angl. retained the mid-front-round vowels spelt *oe*, with the sounds found unrounded in classical OE: *exen, fōet* 'feet'. The alternation of *o* and *y* in such pairs as *fox* and *\*fyxen*, as has been shown in §207, really a case of the *i*-mutation of *u* beside other forms of the same root in which the *u* had been lowered to *o* before the OE period.

§209: other examples of *i*-mutation are very commonly found, and this sound-change is of special importance and frequency to the structure of OE. In learning the conjugation of vocalic verbs, the student needs to know the "mutation pairs" of vowels because i-mutation of the stem-vowel or raising of *e* to *i* occurs in the 2 and 3 sg. pres.indic. Secondly, consonantal verbs are often formed from nouns and adjectives and from other verbs with the suffix *\*-ja-* (§163), with consequent mutation; compare *dōm* 'judgment' with the verb *dēman* 'judge' (Go. *dōmjan*). Thirdly, a number of common adjectives and adverbs formed their comparative and superlative with the Gmc suffixes *\*-izo, \*-isto* (West Gmc *\*-iro, \*-isto*), and therefore have mutation in OE; thus *eald* 'old', comp. *yldra*, early WS *ieldra*, where there has been *i*-mutation of the diphthong: *ea>ie>y* (for the *i*-mutation of diphthongs, see §210). Again, there are nouns related to the past pples of vocalic verbs (see §161a), with *i*-mutation of the reduced-grade vowel; thus *byre* 'son' beside *-boren*.

§210: the diphthongs *ēa, ēo, īo* were all subject to *i*-mutation (see §205); in WS these were all mutated to *īe*, and these are a special mark of early WS, such forms appear as *i* or *y* (earlier *-ie-*), later. Examples: *eald* 'old', comp. and superl. *yldra, yldest* (earlier *-ie-*), *heald* 'sloping' but *hyldan* (also *-i-*, earlier *-ie-*) 'bend', *heord* 'herd' but *hyrde* (early WS *hierde*) 'shepherd', *lēas* 'deprived of' but *lȳsan* (earlier *-īe-*) 'release', *bēag* 'ring' but *bīegan* (earlier *-īe-*) 'cause to bend'. For the corresponding forms in other dialects, see §205.

§211: varying forms of consonantal verbs within the conjugation, such as *wyrcan* 'work' beside pret. *worhte*, *hycgan* 'think' beside pret. *hogde* (on the *u/o* alternation, see §185, 207), are to be explained by the alternation between *i*-mutation (because the present stem had the suffix *\*-ja-*) and the *u* of the pret. and past pple, which regularly lowered to *o* as explained in §186c, and which had no *j* in the suffix to cause mutation. On *þyncan* and *þūhte, pencan* and *þōhte*, see §185.

§212: the generally accepted phonetic explanation of *i*-mutation is that the high front *i* or *j* palatalised the preceding consonant and that this in turn pulled the vowel of the stem towards its own position, raising or fronting it. The *i* or *j*, which had thus fronted a preceding back vowel (or raised a front one) by strong attraction in articulation through and by means of the intervening consonant, was then absorbed into the palatalised consonant. This theory may be called "mechanistic," because it is based entirely on the assumed workings of the speech-organs. An alternative explanation is that in pronouncing the back vowel in the root-syllable, the speaker unconsciously allows his mind and his tongue to "anticipate" the *i* or *j* that is to come in the immediately succeeding syllable, so that the sounds resulting from *i*-mutation were the original stem-vowel plus an anticipatory high front vowel which then coalesced with the original stem-vowel to constitute the new form. Thus, for instance, in pronouncing Gmc *\*dōmjan* 'judge', the *j* is supposed to have been mentally anticipated by the speaker, so that he would say something like *dō-i-mjan*, and that this *ō* and *i* would unite to form the compromise front-round vowel [ø:], written *oe*, *\*dōiman* becoming *dōeman*, a form preserved in Anglian but unrounded to *dēman* in WS. This is a "mentalistic" or psychological theory of *i*-mutation; the orthodox view of articulatory influence through the consonant is a theory of attraction and assimilation, while the mentalistic view is one of anticipation. The accepted theory is supported in some measure by the OE and later spellings of the medial consonant in such words as *secgan* 'say' as compared with the related noun *sagu*. Pr.OE *\*sæjjan* had its *g* palatalised by the following *j* as well as lengthened, and the palatalisation and the lengthening are both symbolised in the spelling *cg*; it is through this palatalised *g* that the vowel was mutated. Compare also the proper name *Cēnwulf*, for instance, appears in Bede as *Coinuulf*, the first element being *cēn(e)* 'bold', from Gmc *\*kōniz*. It may be suggested that *i*-mutation was brought about by the joint working of both the "attractional" and "anticipatory" influences, that the *i* or *j* pulled the immediately preceding consonant towards a palatal articulation and that this in turn mutated the stem-vowel, while at the same time this vowel was being affected by the anticipation of the *i* or *j* that was to come.

§213: the working of *i*-mutation, like other sound-changes, is sometimes disturbed or altered by the influence of analogy or levelling. For instance, the *i*-declensional (§194) noun pl. *Seaxe* 'Saxons' has had the *ea* restored by analogy, where a mutated vowel or diphthong would have been expected; compare *Dene* 'Danes', where mutation (earlier *\*dani*) has occurred. In the 2 and 3 sg.pres.indic. of vocalic verbs too, analogy sometimes seems to have replaced the expected mutated vowel by the more frequently recurring unmutated one; thus *weorðeð* from *weorðan* 'become', and not the usual early WS *wierð* (later *wyrð*), occurs in the Hatton MS of *Cura Pastoralis*, and in the same text we find *hātep* from *hātan* 'call' for the expected *hǣtt* (with mutation, syncope, and assimilation). Similarly *bered*, from *beran* 'bear', is found beside *bir(e)d*. Such forms, without syncope or mutation, are fairly common in late OE.

### §214. Diphthongisation before back vowels ('Back Mutation')

Like diphthongisation before velarised consonants (§201), this diphthongisation changes the Pr.OE vowels *æ, e, i* into the diphthongs *ea, eo, io* respectively. As with the other diphthongisation, too, the change affects only front vowels (that is, *æ* and not the earlier Gmc *a*). The rule in general is that in the early OE period, while the earliest written records were being made, the front vowels *æ, e, i*, when followed after a single consonant by a back vowel, became respectively the diphthongs *ea, eo, io*. Thus *lim* 'limb' has, beside pl. forms *limu* (with the usual lowering in WS and Mercian of *io* to *eo*), *līð* 'slope' has pl. *hliodu*; and also, by this diphthongisation, *hliodu, hleodu*; *sidu* 'custom' has the alternative Alfredian form *siodu*; *witan* 'know' appears also as *wiota* and *weotan* (and, through the influence of the *w*, §187a, *wuta*); similarly *heofon* 'heaven', *ealu* 'ale', etc. For the most part, this sound-change belongs to the non-WS dialects, but in classical OE it is found to some extent, particularly before liquid, labial, and dental consonants. It is the last of the major vowel-changes in OE, and was still apparently in progress in the earlier eighth century, on the evidence of the glossaries of that period.

The cause of the diphthongisation seems to be the rise of a glide as the speech-organs anticipated the articulation of the back vowel in the following syllable; the process is therefore very similar to that presumed in the diphthongisation before velarised consonants (§202). It should be noted however that while the latter affected also long vowels to some extent, the present phenomenon concerns only short vowels.

There is also something of a parallel between this diphthongisation and *i*-mutation (see §212). In Kentish, the diphthongisation is very widespread and seems to have occurred even through two consonants (compare Kt *siondon* with WS *sindon* 'are', and *sioððan* with WS *siððan* 'afterwards'). In Angl. too it is common, but before *c, g, h* its effects are removed by Angl. smoothing. Mercian of the *Vespasian Psalter* Gloss, however, often has the diphthongisation even before *c* and *g* (*spreocan* 'speak', *weogum* d.pl. of *weg* 'way'). Forms like *beoran* 'bear', *eotan* 'eat' are common on non-WS evidence.

Diphthongisation before back vowels is often referred to as "back mutation" or the various "o/a-mutation" (the common type) and "u-mutation" (the less common type); it is not normal in WS, remaining only as the regular form in *ealu* (§46) 'ale' in classical OE; Kt has *alop* and (on the evidence of proper names containing the elements *badu* and *balu*, perhaps originally poetical and Angl.) occurs commonly with this diphthongisation of *æ* in classical OE as in Kt. The origin of *(w)uton*, used for periphrastic 1st pers. imperative or hortatory expressions (§135) as in *(w)uton dōn* 'let us do', is uncertain, but it seems to be a part of the verb *witan* 'go'.

### §215. Conclusion

There are many problems in OE sound-changes and many unsolved puzzles connected with individual words which have not been touched upon in the foregoing paragraphs. The phonology presented has for the most part been confined to those matters which the student needs to understand in order to learn the grammar of OE efficiently, and those aspects have been especially emphasised which are of the greatest structural importance. The student interested in the history of the language will consult specialist works devoted to the subject, and it is hoped that the references supplied in the notes throughout will enable the philologist to pursue further studies effectively.

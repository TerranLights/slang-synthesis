## Grammar points

### Family-wide Germanic typology — what English inherited, kept, and lost (Preface, pp.ix–xiii)

The editors' Preface frames several features as broadly characteristic of Germanic as a family —
useful baseline context for which of English's traits are Germanic-general vs. English-specific
innovations:

- **Weak vs. strong verb distinction** — pan-Germanic (weak = dental-suffix past tense; strong =
  ablaut past tense). English retains the distinction (see Ch.5's verb classes below) but has lost
  productivity of the strong pattern; new verbs are formed weak.
- **Tense/aspect contrast** — all Germanic languages inherited only a two-term past/non-past
  opposition, but each independently developed a periphrastic perfect from an original resultative
  construction. This development is further along in some languages (Afrikaans and Yiddish have
  fully replaced the old past tense with the perfect) than in others (English and nearly all
  Scandinavian languages still keep perfect and past clearly distinct).
- **Case marking vs. configurational grammatical relations** — a major axis of Germanic-internal
  variation. German identifies subjects purely by case (nominative), regardless of position; English
  — and, notably, also case-rich Icelandic — identifies the subject by its position before the finite
  verb, meaning Icelandic can have dative/accusative/genitive "subjects" because case marking there
  has become largely ornamental to grammatical-relation-identification. English lost the case system
  itself and relies on word order and prepositions instead (elaborated in Ch.5's syntax section).
- **Reflexive-marker typology** — widely variable across the family: some languages (Old English,
  Frisian, Old Dutch, Afrikaans) have no dedicated reflexive marker at all; Scandinavian languages
  have verbal reflexive affixes in addition to pronouns; German and Yiddish use only reflexive
  pronouns; English alone uses the complex *pronoun + self* construction to mark co-reference. (Ch.5
  shows this is a post-Old-English development — Old English used bare personal pronouns reflexively,
  with *-self* only for emphasis; see the Vocabulary table's *ic bletsige mē* / *hiene selfne* pair.)
- **Passivization conditions** — another axis of real variation: German passivizes both transitive
  (accusative-object) and even some intransitive verbs, with a morphological distinction between
  *werden*-passives (from accusative objects) and *bekommen*-passives (from dative objects); English's
  conditions are purely configurational (there must be a noun phrase following the verb in the active
  sentence that can become the passive subject, not necessarily adjacent to it).

### English's branch position and historical periodization (Ch.1, pp.9, 16–17)

Genealogically, English belongs to West Germanic, traced to the hypothetical "Ingwaeonic" dialect
group (along with Frisian and, more distantly, Low German/Dutch) — but the chapter stresses that
geographic discontinuity plus Viking and Norman French contact pushed English unusually far from both
its Anglo-Saxon ancestor and its surviving continental Ingwaeonic relatives. English's island isolation
did *not* produce the conservatism islands typically grant a language (contrast Icelandic within North
Germanic, Sardinian within Romance, or insular Celtic) — instead repeated invasion-driven contact
accelerated change.

Periodization given: Germanic federates (Jutes, Angles, Saxons) settled England from the mid-fifth
century, driving the native Celtic population to the western/northern fringes (Cornwall, Wales,
Scotland, Ireland) while assimilating remaining pockets; the West Germanic dialects spoken in England
are collectively **Anglo-Saxon/Old English, up to c.1150**. Two further invasion-contact events shaped
the language in sequence:

1. **Viking settlement and the Danelaw** (from the late eighth century) — a Scandinavian-settled area
   merging with the earlier Anglo-Saxon population, influencing especially the lexicon (see the
   doublet pairs in the Vocabulary table: native *shirt*/*yard*/dialectal *eie* vs. Scandinavian-loan
   *skirt*/*garden*/*egg* — the loans keep an un-palatalized velar consonant where native cognates
   palatalized it).
2. **The Norman Conquest** (1066) — William the Conqueror's French-speaking administration, nobility,
   and clergy made French the prestige language until the fourteenth century, producing lexical and
   orthographic change and accelerating the independently-already-underway levelling of the Anglo-Saxon
   inflectional system.

**Middle English** runs *c.*1150–*c.*1500, after which **Modern English** begins — the period of
English's global dispersion via British and later US expansion, producing numerous English-lexifier
creoles as a side effect (elaborated in `074_germanic_languages_pt03.md`'s coverage of Ch.18).

### Ch.5 "Old and Middle English" — Introduction and dialects (§5.1, pp.110–114)

Old English and Middle English together span *c.*600–*c.*1500; the Norman Conquest (1066) is the
conventional, somewhat artificial, boundary between them. **Old English dialects:** West Saxon (the
best-documented, the dialect of King Alfred's late-ninth-century literary program and of the bulk of
surviving OE manuscript material — Early West Saxon via the Parker Chronicle and Alfredian
translations to 924, Late West Saxon via Ælfric, Wulfstan, and later Chronicle manuscripts) and
Anglian, itself split into Northumbrian (Ruthwell Cross runes, eighth-century poems, tenth-century
gloss texts) and Mercian (ninth-century charters, Vespasian Psalter gloss, partial Rushworth Gospels
gloss); a fourth, thinly-attested Kentish dialect is also distinguished. **Middle English dialects:**
Northern, East Midlands, West Midlands, Kentish, Southern (five-way split, by contrast with Old
English's four). The one surviving manuscript spanning the Old-to-Middle English transition directly
is the *Peterborough Chronicle*'s continuation to 1154 (East Midlands dialect) — a uniquely valuable
single-dialect window onto the transition.

The chapter explicitly reassesses the traditional explanation for the Old-to-Middle-English changes:
conventional wisdom blames the Norman Conquest and French influence, but the author argues this is
likely wrong for the *morphological* changes specifically — French left its mark mainly as lexical
borrowing in the top social layers, while phonological reduction of unstressed syllables (which drove
the loss of case-ending distinctions) was already underway in the north of England before French
contact could have taken effect. Recent scholarship instead hypothesizes that the ninth–tenth-century
Viking conquest, with its long-term settlement and population assimilation in the north and east, is
the more profound influence on these specific changes — though this can't be directly verified because
the relevant-period textual record survives mainly in the (unaffected, southern) Alfredian West Saxon
literary tradition.

### Phonology (§5.2, pp.114–118)

**Old English vowel system:** eight accented-vowel pairs distinguished purely by length (front
unrounded /i, e, æ/; front rounded /y, œ~e/; central /ə/ (unstressed only) and /a/; back /u, o/).
**i-umlaut**, the most pervasive OE sound change, fronted back vowels (/u/>/y/, /o/>/œ/, /a/>/æ/) in
syllables preceding an unstressed syllable containing /i/ — with major knock-on effects throughout OE
inflectional morphology (e.g. the comparative/superlative pattern *eald–ieldra–ieldest* above). A
distinct source of OE short /æ/ is the earlier "Anglo-Frisian fronting" of West Germanic short /a/,
shared specifically with Frisian (not the rest of West Germanic) — an early marker of the English/
Frisian sub-grouping.

**OE-to-ME vowel changes** split into quantitative (conditioned — see the lengthening/shortening
examples in the Vocabulary table) and qualitative (largely unconditioned: rounded front vowels /y, œ/
merged to /i, e/; quality but not length distinctions shifted further by *c.*1400, tabulated in the
source's Tables 5.2–5.3, not reproduced here as they are pure phonetic-inventory tables).

**Diphthongs:** OE had four, each with long/short versions (/ea, eo, ie, io/), arising via three
distinct mechanisms — *breaking* (fronted vowel diphthongized before certain following consonant
clusters), *back umlaut* (front vowel diphthongized before a back vowel in the next syllable), and
coalescence from loss of an intervocalic consonant. By late Old English/early Middle English, all four
had monophthongized (merging into /æ/, /œ/, /y/ respectively, with /io/ falling together with /eo/). A
**new, unrelated set of Middle English diphthongs** then arose independently from the *vocalization*
of the Old English velar fricative /ɣ/ (spelled ⟨g⟩) and of a Middle-English-innovated glide before the
voiceless velar fricative /x/ (spelled ⟨h/gh⟩) — see the dæg>dai, lagu>lawe, and tahte>taughte example
sets in the Vocabulary table. The ModE spelling-vs-pronunciation mismatch in *knight*, *height*,
*though*, *draught*, *laughter*, *enough* are direct fossils of this ME-era process, further obscured
later by the Great Vowel Shift.

**Consonants:** OE's single-letter fricative graphemes ⟨f⟩, ⟨þ/ð⟩, ⟨s⟩ were not phonemically voiced vs.
voiceless — voicing was predictable from a prehistoric sound change that voiced fricatives specifically
in voiced environments (intervocalic/between-voiced-sounds), so spelling alone doesn't show the
voicing contrast (see the *fōt/geaf/sceaft* vs. *drīfan/wulfas/hræfn* pairs). Similarly, the
palatal-vs-velar distinction in OE ⟨c, g⟩ was conditioned, not phonemic — a *palatalization* sound
change affected velars specifically in (what were, before *i*-umlaut-driven reanalysis) front-vowel
environments (see the *ceorl/cirice/geard/georn* vs. *climban/cræft/cū/gold/bōc/plōg* pairs); ⟨sc⟩ is
an exception, having generalized its palatalization beyond front environments entirely. **This
palatalization asymmetry is directly diagnostic for loanword identification**: Scandinavian loanwords
into English systematically keep the un-palatalized (plain velar) form where a native-English cognate
palatalized, producing doublets like *shirt* (native) / *skirt* (Scandinavian loan), *yard* (native) /
*garden* (Scandinavian loan), dialectal *eie* (native) / *egg* (Scandinavian loan) — a genuinely
useful, source-marked contact-linguistics mechanism for the slang-synthesis project's own
borrowing/contact-change analysis.

### Morphology (§5.3, pp.118–125)

**Nouns.** OE nouns inherited a Proto-Germanic root+stem-suffix+inflectional-ending structure, though
the stem suffix is often no longer productive/visible by OE. The **strong/weak declension split**
tracks the historical stem-suffix vowel: strong if the original suffix ended in a vowel (*a, ō, i, u*),
weak if it ended in *-n* (preceded by *a, ō, i*). Grammatical gender (not natural/semantic gender, as
in Modern English) partly tracks stem class: *a*-stems are masc./fem./neut., *ō*-stems fem., *i*-stems
masc./fem./neut., *u*-stems masc./fem. Two numbers (singular/plural), four surviving cases
(nominative, accusative, genitive, dative — the Proto-Germanic instrumental had already disappeared by
the OE period). The very numerous *a*-stem class is laid out in full (Table 5.5, paradigm nouns *stān*
'stone', *scip* 'ship', *word* 'word' — see Vocabulary table); the *o/i/u*-stem strong classes share
the *a*-stem endings but differ in stem-vowel umlaut behavior (Table 5.6); the weak declension (Table
5.7) uses a distinct, more uniform -n-based paradigm.

**Pronouns.** Personal pronouns (Table 5.8) distinguish singular/plural (no productive dual), four
cases, with gender distinguished only in the third-person singular, and a nominative–accusative
distinction made everywhere except 3sg.neut. and 3pl. There are no clearly identifiable clitic
personal-pronoun forms in OE apart from a few encliticized-to-finite-verb or -complementizer forms
attested in glosses. **Reflexivity** uses the plain accusative/dative/genitive personal pronoun, with
optional *-self* added only for emphasis (not as the obligatory marker it later becomes) — see the
Vocabulary table's *ic bletsige mē* example. **Reciprocals** are expressed by the plural personal
pronoun (optionally with *gemetton* 'met' and similar verbs), or via combinations of *ægðer, naþer,
oþer, æghwylc, ælc, gehwa, gehwylc, ænig* + *oþer* — e.g. *ælc for oðerne* 'each for [the] other'.
Demonstrative pronouns (Table 5.9) come in two series — a plain demonstrative series (used for
definite description, pronominal reference, and as the main relative pronoun, with or without the
complementizer *þe*) and a separate "deictic" series — plus an interrogative *hwā* (only m./n. singular
case-declined) and further interrogatives *hwæþer*/*hwelc*, none of which double as relative pronouns
(unlike Modern English *who/which*). Numerous indefinite pronouns are built on the interrogative stems
(*āhwā, gehwā*, etc.) or exist independently (*ælc, ænig, swelc, þyllic*), all declined as indefinite
adjectives.

**Adjectives.** Pre-OE, adjectives inflected as nouns; by the OE period, the strong/weak split tracks
the noun declension split and signals (in)definiteness — strong/indefinite when predicative or when
modifying an indefinite NP; weak/definite when the attributive adjective follows a demonstrative
pronoun (except the weak genitive plural, which borrows the strong ending *-ra* instead of weak
*-ena*). Comparatives are formed by adding *-ra* to the stem (declined weak); superlatives normally end
in *-ost*; some adjectives additionally show *i*-mutation of the root vowel in the comparative/
superlative, as in *eald–ieldra–ieldest* and *sceort–scyrta–scyrtest* (Vocabulary table).

**Verbs.** The weak/strong distinction (weak = root + historical stem suffix + dental past suffix;
strong = ablaut-marked past, no stem suffix) has only a single present/preterite tense opposition in
both types. **Weak verbs** split into three classes by original stem-suffix shape: Class I (*i/j*
suffix — dropped except in the infinitive and 1sg.pres.indic. of *-r*-final stems, but left behind
*i*-umlaut of the root vowel, e.g. *fremman* 'do, perform'); Class II (*oi/oj* suffix, which blocked
*i*-umlaut, e.g. *lufian* 'love'); Class III (four irregular, highly frequent verbs with dental past
endings: *habban, libban, secgan, hycgan*). **Strong verbs** form their past by vowel ablaut across
seven classes, each with a distinct ablaut pattern across the five principal parts (infinitive,
3sg.present, 1/3sg.preterite, preterite plural, past participle) — see the full Class I–VII paradigms
in the Vocabulary table (Table 5.10). A further distinct verb type, **preterite-present verbs**
(*sceal, mæg, cann, mōt, witan, þurfan, durran, munan, dugan, āgan* — the ancestors of the Modern
English modals plus a few that didn't survive as modals), arose when an old strong-verb preterite form
acquired present-tense meaning, triggering formation of a new, consonantal (dental-suffixed) preterite
alongside it. A final small class of **highly irregular, highly frequent verbs** (*bēon/wesan* 'be',
*willan* 'wish/will', *dōn* 'do', *gān* 'go') rounds out the verbal inflection system.

**Inflectional categories.** Present tense: three moods (indicative, subjunctive, imperative), two
numbers, three persons for finite forms; infinitive formed by *-an*; present participle by *-ende*.
Present indicative: 1sg. *-e*, 2sg. *-(e)st*, 3sg. *-(e)ð*, general plural *-að*; strong verbs show
umlauted root vowels in 2/3sg. (the ending originally contained an *i*); weak Class I shows umlaut
throughout. Past tense: strong verbs' 1st/3rd person singular has a distinct, often ablauted stem with
no ending; 2sg. takes *-e*; weak verbs take *-e* (1/3sg.) / *-est* (2sg.) / *-edon* (pl., Class I) or
*-ode*/*-odest*/*-odon* (Class II). The past subjunctive singular ending is *-e* (matching 1/3sg.
indicative); plural *-en*. Past participles: weak verbs use the dental suffix; strong verbs use an
*-en* suffix on the past-plural ablaut stem, with the prefix *ge-* on weak past participles too
(*gefremed*, *gelufod*) and on strong ones (*gedrifen*). **The Middle English transition** drastically
reduced this system via the unstressed-syllable vowel-weakening sound change (endings leveling toward
schwa, spelled ⟨e⟩) — by early Middle English, verb endings (unlike nominal endings) still distinctly
marked three persons in the present singular and two in the preterite singular, with clear number
contrast in both tenses; further reduction toward the Modern English system happened mainly in the
fourteenth–fifteenth centuries.

### Syntax (§5.4, pp.125–139)

**Noun-phrase structure.** Pre-modification order in Old English closely resembles Modern English
(quantifier – demonstrative/possessive – numeral – other – adjective(s) – genitive noun – head), though
exceptions exist (*monig* 'many' can follow a demonstrative; *oþer* 'other' can precede a quantifier).
Post-modification by the same element types is attested, more common in poetry than prose, and
multiple post-modifiers (including a demonstrative-plus-adjective combination, in which case the
demonstrative precedes the adjective even in postposition) can co-occur — a freedom largely gone by
Modern English, where internal NP word order tightened toward the Modern norm over the course of
Middle English, though exceptions (quantifier preceding the article, as in *þurh out vch a toune*
'throughout each town') persisted into Middle English.

**Apposition.** Appositive constructions (one NP specifying/modifying another) are common, typically
with the name preceding the title in Old English (*Sidroc eorl* 'Earl Sidroc'); Middle English shows
more structural variation, including multiple stacked appositions within one NP and pronouns modified
by an appositive NP.

**Discontinuous phrases.** NP constituents can be discontinuous — post-modifiers may move to a position
outside the rest of the NP, as in *māran cyle ic geseah, and wyrsan* 'I have seen a greater and worse
cold' (literally 'greater cold I saw, and worse').

**Unexpressed subjects.** Unlike Modern English, OE allows a subject to be omitted when it carries no
real semantic role — systematically with weather verbs (an *it*-subject becomes near-obligatory only
by early Modern English) and optionally with impersonal verbs (*hine nanes þinges ne lyste þisse
worulde* 'nothing in this world pleased him', literally 'him nothing pleased of this world') — this
option persisted with impersonal predicates into the fifteenth century even as weather-verb subjects
regularized earlier. Subject omission in conjoined clauses under identity-of-subject with the first
conjunct is attested in both OE and Modern English colloquial registers, suggesting no deep historical
discontinuity there.

**Reflexives/reciprocals**: see Morphology above (same underlying section, split across the book's
own morphology/syntax division — captured once there to avoid duplication).

**The Verbal Group — modals and periphrastic constructions.** Old English/Middle English modals had
richer inflectional morphology than Modern English modals (distinct present/past person endings,
singular/plural distinction in both tenses) and — unlike Modern English — could take their own agentive
subject, a direct object, or a tensed-clause complement, and could enter into verb-clustering
constructions resembling the modern continental West Germanic languages' verb clusters. Modals also
show a steadily increasing skew toward exclusively-auxiliary behavior across the Middle English
period, driven partly by the independent collapse of the inflectional subjunctive, whose functions
(possibility, logical necessity) were taken over by *may/might*, *can/could*, *must*; this is the
process by which modals lost their non-finite forms (infinitive, participle) almost entirely by the
early sixteenth century, with scattered exceptions (*can* kept some main-verb use into the nineteenth
century). A set of **quasi-modal periphrases** — *be going to*, *be able to*, *have to* — develops over
the same period from originally non-modal source constructions (motion-purpose *gangan* 'go'; a French
loan expressing ability from the fourteenth century; a possessive *habban* construction), consolidating
into fully modal meaning mainly in Middle English. A related, structurally distinct development is
*is/are/were to* expressing obligation/destiny, attested with incipient modal meaning already in Old
English. **The progressive** was only optionally marked in Old English, via *be* + present participle
(*-ende*); confusion between the present participle and the semantically close verbal noun in *-ung*
during late Old English fed into the Middle English gerund, and the *be + -ing* progressive only became
an *obligatory* aspectual marker in the early Modern English period. **Periphrastic *do*** existed in
Old English as a lexical pro-verb for verbs ("let us do [put] him in the well") and as a causative verb
("the king did [=made] the maiden rise") — the causative-*do* use is generally taken as the likely
precursor of periphrastic *do*, with Middle English showing genuinely ambiguous causative/
non-causative uses as the grammaticalization proceeded; auxiliary *do* only fully grammaticalizes in
the early Modern English period.

**Phrasal verbs — particle position tied to the V2 rule.** Old English verb-particle placement differs
sharply by clause type: in embedded clauses the particle immediately precedes the verb with severe
limits on what can intervene; in matrix clauses the particle nearly always *follows* the verb, with
subjects and other constituents freely intervening between verb and particle (*þa ahof Drihten hie up*
'then the Lord raised them up'). The explanation offered is structural: Old English is basic SOV (verb
at the end of the VP/embedded clause) with a rule preposing the finite verb to second position in
matrix clauses (**verb-second, V2**) — V2 fronts only the verb, stranding the particle at its original
clause-final base position, producing the asymmetry. Middle English's shift from this OV base order to
VO became dominant from early in the period and was typologically complete by *c.*1200.

**Grammatical relations — case marking.** Old English is SOV with a relatively fixed subject position
but otherwise free constituent order within the VP, grammatical relations being carried substantially
by case rather than position: datives mark Goal/Recipient/Experiencer roles with some regularity, a
number of verbs take a (often partitive-flavored) genitive object, and accusative broadly expresses the
Patient role — but nominative itself is not tied to a single semantic role (it marks the Agent in
active sentences but the Patient in passives, and the Experiencer with psychological verbs like
*lician* 'like' and *hreowan* 'rue'). Predicative adjectives can take dative- or genitive-marked NP
complementation for the Theme role. As the case system collapsed over the early Middle English period,
these verb/adjective + dative-or-genitive-object relations were reanalyzed as prepositional (*to* for
the old dative, *of* for the old genitive), and the dative/accusative "objective case" distinction
collapsed into Modern English's single objective case (surviving residually in double-object
constructions like *I gave him a book*).

**The Subject — dative/quirky subjects.** Even in Old English, the nominative subject is usually the
antecedent for reflexives and usually triggers verb agreement — but the chapter traces, through the
history of *lician* 'like', a genuine exception: a non-nominative (dative Experiencer) argument can
show subject-like syntactic behavior (e.g. serving as the antecedent for coordinate-subject deletion)
even while the true nominative argument exists elsewhere in the clause — a so-called "quirky subject"
construction, paralleling the well-attested Icelandic case. Only in the fifteenth century was the
preposed dative reinterpreted as a true nominative, eliminating the construction.

**The Passive.** Formed with a main-verb past participle plus the auxiliary *beon* 'be' or *weorðan*
'become'; OE allows impersonal passivization of a dative or prepositional object (the dative retains
its case marking and typically occupies clause-initial topic position via V2, rather than true
subject-agreement passivization), but not true subject-promoting passivization of indirect/
prepositional objects — only accusative/Patient objects promote to a genuinely agreeing nominative
subject. Impersonal passivization of datives remained available until the fifteenth century, then died
out as the scope of true passivization expanded (during Middle English) to include indirect and
prepositional objects — a change attributed to the breakdown of the Old English case system in
combination with the independent SOV-to-SVO shift.

**Word order — V2 in detail.** Matrix-clause order is: [first constituent] – finite verb – subject –
VP; embedded-clause order is: conjunction – subject – VP (no V2 effect). Fronting of the finite verb in
matrix clauses was not obligatory, and second conjuncts introduced by *and* tended to keep the verb
clause-final instead. The first-constituent "topic" slot could be left empty in lively narrative style
(verb-initial sentences), could hold a subject, object, or adverbial (but not a non-finite verb form),
and — crucially — interacts with *which* element fills it: when the first constituent is not the
subject and not a *wh*-word, a nominal subject still follows the finite verb (true V2), but a
*pronominal* subject instead precedes the finite verb; when the first constituent *is* a *wh*-word or
a negative element, all subject types (pronominal and nominal alike) follow the finite verb. A heavy
subject can be postposed past VP material. Within the VP, word order is comparatively free (nominal
objects and adverbials can appear to either side of the verb), though pronominal objects are more
fixed, almost always preceding nominal objects and other VP-internal material, with a nearly fixed
accusative-before-dative order when both objects are pronominal. Old English also shows genuine
verb-clustering (multiple verbs grouped together within the VP, as in the modern continental West
Germanic languages), a phenomenon distinct from V2 fronting itself. **The loss of V2** unfolds through
Middle English: the VO/OV shift completes by *c.*1200; non-subject topicalization continued to trigger
subject-verb inversion with nominal subjects (unlike pronominal subjects, which never inverted) until
well into the fourteenth century — with the notable exception of Chaucer's work, where nominal subjects
already behave like pronominal ones — after which topicalization generally stopped triggering inversion
at all except with *wh*-words or negative-first-constituents (the still-surviving Modern English relic,
along with fossilized inversions like *in came John*). By the early Modern English period, only
auxiliaries (not full lexical verbs) could still participate in subject-verb inversion.

**Subordination.** Many OE finite clauses are ambiguous between coordination ("paratactic") and true
subordination ("hypotactic") — some conjunctions (*ær* 'before', *siþþan* 'since', *þonne* 'then') are
genuinely ambiguous this way, while a distinct set (*þæt* 'that', for all finite embedded clauses but
not relatives; *þe* 'that', for relative clauses; *gif* 'if', for conditionals) is unambiguously
subordinating. Word order can be a (non-conclusive) diagnostic: matrix clauses tend to front the finite
verb, embedded clauses tend to put the infinitival verb clause-finally, but both criteria admit
exceptions. Relative clauses are optionally introduced by the complementizer *þe*, optionally combined
with a demonstrative-pronoun relative, yielding three construction types (*þe* alone; demonstrative +
*þe*, the rarest; demonstrative alone) — with preposition stranding attested even in the bare-*þe*
type. In Middle English, *þe* was replaced by *that* as the relative complementizer, the *wh*-series of
pronouns ousted the demonstrative pronouns as relative pronouns, and preposition stranding generalized
to all relative and interrogative clauses.

**Non-finite clauses.** Old English has both a bare infinitive and a *to*-infinitive, with no simple
theoretical correlation between the marking and the clause's syntactic status — the choice instead
depends on which governing verb selects the infinitive (modals, causatives, and perception verbs
canonically select a bare infinitive; most other object-clause infinitives are predominantly, though
not exclusively, bare; subject infinitives and infinitival complements of nouns/adjectives are
canonically *to*-infinitives). Over Middle English, *to*-marking develops into a reliable signal of the
infinitive's clausal status, converging on the Modern English system by the close of the period.

**Negation.** The OE negative adverb *ne* sits immediately to the left of the finite verb and very
commonly cliticizes onto it (*nolde* < *ne wolde* 'didn't want'); multiple negation (double or even
triple) is grammatical and non-contradictory, with *ne* retaining its normal position even when another
negator (*nǣfre* 'never', *nān* 'no') is added. Emphatic negation combines *ne* with *na*/*naht* (both
related to *nawiht* 'nothing'). In Middle English, this emphatic combination loses its emphatic force
and becomes the default negation pattern: *ne* keeps its preverbal position while *naht*/*noht*
gradually fixes to the finite verb's immediate right; by the close of Middle English and into early
Modern English, *ne* drops out entirely and *naht*/*noht* reduces to Modern English *not*.

### Lexis (§5.5, pp.139–140)

The bulk of Old English vocabulary is native Common Germanic stock, with several characteristically
Germanic word-formation processes, most no longer productive: prefixation by *un-* and suffixation by
*-ness* both survive productively into Modern English; the adjective/adverb suffix *-lic* (ancestor of
Modern *-ly*) was not originally adverb-forming in Old English but became so later; *with-* as a
transitivizing "against-V" prefix (OEng. *wiþ cweþan* 'contradict', literally 'against-speak') survives
only in relic forms like *withstand*; *be-* as an originally transitivizing prefix (*bespeak*,
*bemourn*) remains marginally/perhaps-again productive in informal registers. Most other Old English
word-formation processes left no trace in the standard modern language, their functional space taken
over by French- and Latin-derived affixation (*-ment, -ous, -able, -al, -ance, -ate, -ize, -ant*;
*circum-, counter-, dis-, inter-, mal-, pre-, post-, re-, semi-, sub-, vice-*, among others — the
chapter notes this list is "far from complete"). **Three successive loanword strata** are named: the
earliest Old English loans are from Latin, with some from Celtic (the chapter does not cite specific
example forms for either stratum); the ninth–tenth-century Scandinavian (Viking) invasions left a
lexical mark recognizable specifically via the velar-vs-palatal consonant diagnostic discussed under
Phonology above (*bank, rig, skirt, scar, car, gate, crook, garden, fell, tarn* are named as further
Scandinavian loan examples beyond the doublet pairs already given); and the Norman Conquest "led to a
tremendous influx of loanwords from French, too numerous to attempt to indicate here."

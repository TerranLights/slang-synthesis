## Grammar points

### 2.1 One-word nouns

**2.1A Noun-forming suffixes (agentive/locative).** English derives "people who do things" and
"people who come from places" nouns via a closed but non-predictable set of suffixes — there's no
rule for which suffix attaches to which base; see the Vocabulary table, p.26.

**2.1B Nouns derived from verbs, adjectives, and other nouns.** Some nouns are identical in form to
their source word (a *call*, a *fall*); most are derived with a suffix, and the correct suffix must
be learned per-word rather than predicted, p.26.

**2.1C Noun/verb stress-shift homographs.** A closed set of two-syllable words distinguish noun from
verb purely by stress placement (first syllable = noun, second = verb); the meanings are usually
related but can diverge (noun *conduct* = behavior vs. verb *conduct* = to lead/direct), p.27.

### 2.2 Compound nouns

**2.2A -ing + noun compounds.** The -ing form can be a true adjective describing the noun (a
"dancing couple" = a couple that is dancing — both words stressed, no hyphen) or the first element of
a compound noun expressing purpose (a "dancing-shoe" = a shoe *for* dancing, not a shoe that dances —
stress on the first word only, hyphen optional), p.28.

**2.2B 's vs. compound noun for possession.** 's/s' is used for people and living things; for
inanimate possessors, English generally prefers a compound noun (*the table-leg*) over "*of*" where a
natural compound exists, p.28.

**2.2C Material/substance compound nouns.** Material nouns (*gold, leather, wood*) function
adjectivally when placed before another noun, but remain grammatically nouns — they take no
comparative/superlative and cannot combine with *very*. *Wood* and *wool* are exceptions with true
derived adjective forms (*wooden, woollen*); several other material nouns also have a parallel true
adjective meaning "like X" (*golden*, *silvery*, *stony*), p.29.

### 2.3 Countable and uncountable nouns (1)

Basic test: countable nouns take *a/an*, have a plural, and answer "How many?"; uncountable nouns
take none of these and answer "How much?" Some nouns shift category depending on meaning — single
item vs. the material/substance it's made of or made from (*a chicken* the animal vs. *chicken* the
meat; *a glass* the object vs. *glass* the material). Normally-uncountable nouns for drinks and
other substances can be used as countables in specific contexts: ordering ("a coffee, please") or
when modified by a descriptive adjective ("a light oil"), pp.30–31.

### 2.4 Countable and uncountable nouns (2)

**2.4A Singular equivalents.** Several uncountable nouns have no derived singular form at all and
instead borrow an unrelated word to express "one item" (*bread* → *a loaf*; *clothing* → *a coat*;
*luggage* → *a suitcase*; *accommodation* → *a room*), p.32.

**2.4B Nouns not normally countable in English.** A specific, memorizable list of nouns are
countable in many other languages but never take *a/an* or a plural in English (see Vocabulary
table) — the source frames this explicitly as a cross-linguistic trap for learners. *News* is
anomalous: plural in form but always singular in verb agreement. *Hair* (collective, on the head) is
singular; *hairs* only refers to individually countable strands, p.32.

**2.4C Partitives.** A closed set of quantifying phrases (*a piece of, a slice of, a loaf of, a tin
of...*) convert an uncountable noun into a countable unit; specific partitive-noun pairings are
collocationally fixed and must be learned rather than derived, p.33.

### 2.5 Number (singular and plural) (1)

**2.5A Plural -s/-es pronunciation.** The plural -s is pronounced /s/, /z/, or /ɪz/ depending on the
preceding sound — voiceless consonants take /s/, voiced consonants and vowels take /z/, and sibilant
endings (s, z, sh, ch, x) take /ɪz/ with -es added in spelling, p.34.

**2.5B Plural spelling rules.** Consonant + *y* → *-ies*; vowel + *y* → regular *-s*; proper nouns
ending in *-y* just add *-s* even after a consonant. A specific closed list of nouns change final
*-f/-fe* to *-ves* in the plural; another small set allows either *-s* or *-ves*; most other *-f*
nouns just add *-s*, p.34.

**2.5C -o endings and irregular plurals.** Nouns ending in *-o* variably take *-es*, either *-es* or
*-s*, or just *-s*, with no reliable rule — must be learned per word. A separate closed set of nouns
form their plural through an internal vowel change rather than a suffix. A few nouns have identical
singular and plural forms (mostly animals). Nationality nouns ending in *-ese* or *-ss* also have
identical singular/plural forms, p.35.

### 2.6 Number (singular and plural) (2)

**2.6A Collective nouns.** A three-way distinction: (1) collective nouns with a normal plural *and*
a choice of singular/plural verb depending on whether the group is viewed as a unit or as
individuals; (2) collective nouns with no normal plural form but still a singular/plural verb
choice; (3) collective nouns that take only a plural verb and have no normal plural form at all
(though *peoples* exists as a distinct word meaning "national populations," not the plural of this
sense of *people*), p.36.

**2.6B Nouns ending in -ics.** Academic-subject nouns in *-ics* always take a singular verb. A
second subgroup (*acoustics, economics, statistics*...) takes a singular verb for the abstract
subject sense but a plural verb when referring to a specific set of figures/data. A third set of
invariant-form nouns (*headquarters, species, works*...) take a singular or plural verb purely
depending on whether one or more than one is meant, p.36.

**2.6C Plural-form, plural-verb-only nouns.** Two subgroups: (1) nouns for two-part items (clothing,
tools) that combine with "a pair of" (*trousers, scissors, pyjamas*); (2) abstract/collective plural
nouns that do not combine with "a pair of" (*clothes, earnings, manners, stairs*), p.37.

### 2.7 Gender

**2.7A Male and female word forms.** English has no grammatical gender system — *he/she/it* track
real-world sex/animacy, not noun class. A residual, closed set of lexically distinct male/female
word-pairs survives (see Vocabulary table); the *-ess* suffix for people is receding, and
gender-marked agent nouns like *authoress*/*manageress* are now avoided in favor of the unmarked
form (*author*, *manager*) for both sexes, p.38.

**2.7B Gender-ambiguous person nouns.** A large class of common nouns for people (*doctor, student,
singer*...) give no clue to the referent's sex until a pronoun appears, p.39.

### 2.8 The genitive

**2.8A Possession with 's/s'/apostrophe alone.** 's is the default for people and living things;
plurals already ending in -s take only a bare apostrophe; irregular plurals (not ending in -s) still
take 's; names already ending in -s still take 's (not just the apostrophe) in this variety, p.40.

**2.8B 's vs. compound noun vs. 'of'.** For inanimate possessors, a compound noun is often preferred
over *of* where a natural one exists; *of* is required where no natural compound is available and
must simply be learned by exposure, p.40.

**2.8C 's/s' with non-living things.** Despite 2.8A/B's living/non-living split, a genuine set of
non-living nouns still take 's/s': some as fixed idiomatic phrases, others productively as
singular/plural time-duration phrases ("an hour's delay," "two hours' journey"), p.41.

**2.8D Omission of the noun after 's.** When referring to a workplace, shop, or someone's home, the
noun after 's (*surgery, shop, house*) is conventionally dropped: "the doctor's," "the hairdresser's,"
p.41.

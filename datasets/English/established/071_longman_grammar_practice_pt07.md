## Grammar points

### 7.1 Adverbs of manner

**7.1A Adverbs with and without -ly.** The regular pattern adds -ly to the adjective (changing final
-y to -i after a consonant: heavy→heavily). A closed set of adverbs is instead identical in form to
the corresponding adjective, with no -ly at all, p.90.

**7.1B Two forms, different meanings.** A small set of adverbs has both an -ly and a non-ly form, but
these are not interchangeable registers — each form carries a genuinely distinct meaning, p.90.

**7.1C Adjectives already ending in -ly.** Since these can't take a second -ly suffix, their
adverbial equivalent is instead expressed with a prepositional phrase: "in a [adjective]
way/manner/fashion", p.91.

### 7.2 Adverbs of time

**7.2A Points of time.** A set of fixed time expressions answers "exactly when?"; several have
context-dependent readings (e.g. *this morning* can mean now, earlier today, or later today) and
several have fixed idiomatic forms that must be learned rather than derived (*tonight*, not
"*this night*"; *last night*, not "*yesterday night*"), p.92.

**7.2B 'Still' and 'yet'.** Both broadly mean "until now" and often co-occur with the present perfect.
*Still* emphasizes continuity (mainly affirmative, occasionally negative for emphasis); *yet* is
mainly restricted to questions and negatives and is usually placed at the end of the sentence, p.93.

**7.2C 'Already' and other time adverbs.** *Already* ("before now"/"so soon") appears in questions and
affirmatives but not negatives, and can be placed mid-sentence or at the end. A further open set of
time adverbs (afterwards, at last, just, now, once, recently, soon, suddenly, then, these days...) is
especially common in narrative/story-telling, p.93.

### 7.3 Adverbial phrases of duration

**7.3A Since, for, ago.** Three distinct duration expressions answer three distinct questions:
*since* + a point in time answers "Since when?" (present perfect); *for* + a period answers "How
long?" (past, future, or present perfect); *ago* + a period answers "How long ago?" (simple past
only), p.94.

**7.3B Till/until vs. by.** The choice depends on the verb's own temporal character: "continuity"
verbs (describing an extended state/activity) combine only with *till/until*, never *by*; "point of
time" verbs (describing a single moment) combine with *by* in the affirmative, with *till/until*
appearing with these verbs only in the negative, p.94.

**7.3C During, in, for.** *During* covers both "from beginning to end" and "at some point within" a
period. *In* can substitute for *during* only when the reference noun is a period of time, not an
activity/event. *For* specifically answers "how long," p.95.

### 7.4 Adverbs of frequency

**7.4A Position in affirmatives and questions.** Frequency adverbs occupy one of the same three
positions established for *both/all* in Chapter 5: after *be* alone, after the first auxiliary, or
before the main verb; in questions, they follow the subject, p.96.

**7.4B Position in negative statements.** Most frequency adverbs (always, generally, normally, often,
regularly, usually) follow *not*; the same set can instead follow the subject for special emphasis.
*Sometimes* and *frequently* can precede or follow the negative. *Hardly ever* and similar
already-negative adverbs cannot combine with an additional *not*, p.96.

**7.4C Sentence-initial position for emphasis.** A subset of frequency adverbs (frequently,
generally, normally, (very) often, sometimes, usually) can open a sentence for special emphasis,
p.97.

### 7.5 Adverbs of degree

**7.5A The two meanings of 'quite'.** Depending on stress/intonation and on the type of word that
follows, *quite* means either "less than" (with ordinary adjectives/adverbs/some verbs) or
"completely" (with absolute adjectives like *dead/empty/full*, or strong adjectives like
*amazing/wonderful*), p.98.

**7.5B 'Fairly'.** Goes with "good"-type adjectives/adverbs and is generally less complimentary than
*quite*; cannot combine with absolute adjectives; *enough* is explicitly not a substitute for either,
p.98.

**7.5C 'Rather'.** Stronger than *quite/fairly*, suggesting "inclined to be"; typically pairs with
"bad"-type adjectives, but when paired with a "good"-type adjective instead implies surprise, p.98.

**7.5D 'Much', 'any', 'far', 'a lot' as degree adverbs.** All four intensify comparatives/
superlatives; *much/not much* and *(not) a lot* also modify verbs like *like/enjoy*; *much/far* are
the standard intensifiers for *prefer*/*would rather*, p.99.

### 7.6 Intensifiers

**7.6A 'Very', 'too', 'very much'.** *Very* strengthens the positive (non-comparative) form of an
adjective/adverb, adjective+noun combinations, and many past-participle adjectives; "the very" also
combines with superlatives and a few nouns. *Too* precedes an adjective/adverb to mean "more than
desirable" — distinct from its addition-sense ("also," see 7.7B–C). *Very much* has its own
distribution: comparatives, certain verbs, and a specific closed set of adjectives (afraid, awake,
alive, alone), p.100.

**7.6B Adverbs in place of 'very'.** *Extremely/really* intensify more emphatically than *very*;
*terribly/awfully* are the everyday/informal equivalent. A few adjectives take only a specific,
fixed intensifier (*fast* asleep, *wide* awake — never "*very* asleep/awake"); a further set of fixed
-ly-adverb-plus-word collocations must be learned individually, p.101.

### 7.7 Focus adverbs

**7.7A Even, only, just, simply for focusing.** Moving one of these adverbs within a sentence shifts
which element receives the speaker's focus; informal speech tends to front them before the verb and
rely on stress/intonation to signal the intended focus instead, p.102.

**7.7B Two meanings of 'too'.** Position-dependent, as in 7.6A: "too hot to drink" (more than
desirable, before the adjective) vs. "the coffee is hot, too" (also, end of sentence), p.102.

**7.7C 'Too' and 'not either'.** *Too* adds information to an affirmative sentence ("also," clause-
final); in the negative, *either* is used instead ("He can't read, either," never "*can't read, too*"),
p.102.

**7.7D 'Also' and 'as well'.** Both function like *too* (affirmative-only, replaced by *either* in
the negative); *as well* is restricted to clause-final position, while *also* is more typical of
writing than speech and follows the same three-position rule established for frequency adverbs
(after sole *be*/auxiliary verb, after the first of several auxiliaries, or before the main verb),
p.103.

### 7.8 Viewpoint adverbs, connecting adverbs, and inversion

**7.8A Viewpoint adverbs.** Three functional groups express the speaker's stance toward what they're
saying: certainty, brevity/conciseness, and personal opinion — see the Vocabulary table for the
member lists, p.104.

**7.8B Connecting adverbs.** Three further functional groups link ideas across sentences: addition,
comparison/contrast, and summarizing, p.104.

**7.8C Inversion after 'negative adverbs', etc.** A specific set of "negative adverbs" (never,
little, hardly, hardly ever, rarely, seldom) and restrictive phrases (only after/then/when, so +
adjective) trigger question-style subject-auxiliary inversion when fronted at the start of a
sentence; this register is distinctly formal/literary, p.105.

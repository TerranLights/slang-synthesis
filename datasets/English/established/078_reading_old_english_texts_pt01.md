## Grammar points

### Formulaic-system analysis (the *gedryht* case study)

Orchard uses the single word *gedryht* ('troop') to demonstrate how oral-formulaic theory, in its
refined form, does not require verbatim-identical formulas but rather a recognizable *system*: a
fixed metrical/syntactic slot (end of the b-verse, preceded by an alliterating genitive-plural noun)
that different poets — including the demonstrably literate, Latinate Cynewulf — can fill with
different words while preserving the underlying pattern. The secular formula *eorla gedryht* ('troop
of warriors') is shown being adapted by Cynewulf into the Christian *engla gedryht* ('troop of
angels'), and further varied again by the anonymous *Christ III* poet — evidence, per Orchard, that
individual poets exercise real freedom within a formulaic tradition rather than simply reproducing
fixed phrases (pp.108–9).

### Oral-formulaic theory's core claim and its weakness

The chapter traces oral-formulaic theory from Milman Parry and Albert Lord's work on Homer, through
Francis P. Magoun's 1953 application to *Beowulf*, to later refinements. Magoun's original claim —
that heavy formulaic density in a poem indicates oral composition, and that "lettered poetry is never
formulaic" — is identified as a logical fallacy (affirming the consequent: "formulaic poetry is
usually oral; *Beowulf* is formulaic; therefore *Beowulf* is oral" is as invalid as "cats generally
have four legs; my dog has four legs; therefore my dog is a cat"). Orchard's key corrective, drawn
from evidence like Cynewulf's and Aldhelm of Malmesbury's demonstrably literate use of formulaic
technique, is that **formulaic composition need not be oral at all** — it may simply be an
ultimately-oral-derived *technique* available to a literate poet as much as an illiterate one
(pp.103–8).

### Formulaic technique extends beyond verse into rhythmical prose

Building on Wulfstan of York's rhythmical homiletic prose, the chapter argues that formulaic/aural
composition is not restricted to OE verse's half-line metrical unit — Wulfstan's two-stress
rhythmical phrases can be analyzed the same way, and his homilies show the same kind of large-scale
thematic repetition (near-identical but non-verbatim passages recurring across widely separated
works) that OE verse shows at the level of theme, not just individual phrase. This is explicitly
distinguished from the unrelated "cut-and-paste" technique of verbatim-copying homilists active in
the same period (p.109–11).

### Cross-cultural comparative method: Wulfstan and African-American oral preaching

Orchard draws a sustained comparison between Wulfstan's formulaic homiletic technique and the
documented oral-rhetorical techniques of modern African-American preachers (e.g. Martin Luther
King's "I have a dream" speech and its earlier, closely related draft versions), arguing the parallel
is instructive for understanding how a literate tradition can still compose/perform in a
fundamentally oral-derived, formulaic style — shifting scholarly attention from the poet (as composer
of formulas) to the audience (for whom the formulaic effect is performed and received) (pp.111–14).

### Riddle formulae and multi-stage scribal transmission

Analysis of Exeter Book Riddles 30 and 36 is used to show two different transmission histories within
a single formulaic tradition: Riddle 30 survives in two scribal copies (by the same scribe) with
small but non-trivial verbal variants, argued to reflect "memorial transmission" (recomposition from
memory) rather than simple copying error. Riddle 36, by contrast, shows an apparently nonsensical
textual crux (a garbled line disrupting the meter) that is explained as a multi-stage written-only
corruption: an original Latin-code gloss of three OE solution-words, itself later glossed again, with
the resulting gloss text eventually copied erroneously into the body of the poem. Orchard's point:
only *written* transmission (not oral) can explain this kind of progressive degeneration, showing
that "oral" and "written" transmission features can coexist even within a single short, clearly
formulaic text (pp.114–19).

### Grammatical person-shift as a rhetorical marker of Latin vs. English register

Both Alfred's prose Preface and his metrical Preface to the *Pastoral Care* shift from third-person
reference to the translated message/text (describing it as an external object, "this message") to
first-person self-reference ("me") partway through. Orchard (following O'Brien O'Keeffe) reads this
shift as marking a conceptual boundary: the Latin source text is something to be *read*, requiring a
third-person, external descriptive stance, while the English translation is something to be *heard*
— spoken in the first person, as if the book itself is addressing its audience directly (pp.119–20).

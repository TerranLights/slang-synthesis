pp. 220–233 (Text D: *The Dream of the Rood*, lines 78–155, to the end of the poem — sections 3–4:
the Cross's exhortation to the dreamer, and the dreamer's own reaction — plus the text's cumulative
end-of-text glossary).

**Coverage note:** continues directly from `076_beginning_old_english_pt11.md` (lines 1–77). This
file also carries the **full consolidated function-word cross-reference** for the entire Dream of
the Rood text (covering both pt11 and pt12's content), since the source's own end-of-text glossary
(p.227–233) covers the whole poem rather than being split by line range. This is also the final file
of Part II and of the complete *Beginning Old English* extraction — the book's own Concluding
Remarks, Glossary of Technical Terms, and Appendix of Old English Paradigms are covered separately
(see the companion dispatch covering that back matter, if produced). New content vocabulary from
lines 78–155 gets full individual treatment; already-established closed-class function words are
consolidated. The poem is represented only through the source's own interlinear glosses and short
illustrative phrases, per copyright discipline.

## Grammar points

### Formula-tracking across the poem's four sections (p.223, 225–226)

The source works through an unusually dense network of deliberate verbal repetitions linking the
poem's otherwise structurally separate sections — worth treating as a worked methodology example for
this project's own cross-passage formula-tracking: *men ofer moldan* 'men throughout the earth'
recurs identically at lines 12a and 82a; *wuldres trēow*/*wuldres bēam* 'tree/beam of glory' links
the dreamer's description (14b) to the Cross's own self-description (97b); the near-identical
*heofon-rīces Weard* formula is shown to be shared not just within this poem but with an entirely
different poem, Caedmon's Hymn (Chapter 6/pt05) — demonstrating formula-sharing operates at the level
of the wider OE poetic tradition, not just within one text; *elne mycle* 'with great zeal' is applied
in turn to Christ (34a), to the Cross (60a), and finally to the dreamer (123a), tracing a single
formula across all three of the poem's "characters" to unify them thematically; and *mǣte weorode*
'with small company' is applied identically to both Christ (69b) and the dreamer (124a), explicitly
flagged by the source as a shared instance of **litotes** (understatement) — each is in fact
entirely alone, not merely lightly accompanied.

### Sustained physical-sense imagery across the whole poem (p.223)

The source traces a deliberate progression through the five senses distributed across the poem's
four sections: sight dominates the opening vision; hearing is foregrounded when the Cross begins to
speak; touch is central to Christ's embrace of the Cross during the crucifixion account; and taste
recurs via the pun on *byrigde* 'tasted'/'buried' (101a) and its echo in *biteres onbyrigan* 'to
taste the bitter (thing)' (113b–114a) and the closing heavenly-banquet imagery (*geseted tō symle*
'set at the banquet'). This is presented as a unifying structural device operating beneath the
poem's more obvious four-section division.

### A possible deliberate contrastive pun on *unforht* (p.223)

*Unforht* 'unafraid' appears at line 110b (describing no one being able to stand unafraid before
God's word at Judgement) and again at line 117b (describing those who carry the sign of the Cross in
their hearts as needing not to be *unforht*) — logically these should contrast (the first group
fearful, the second confidently unafraid), but the source notes the second instance may instead play
on the OE *un-* prefix's attested **intensifying** (rather than negating) force — i.e. 'very afraid'
rather than 'not afraid' — explicitly cross-referencing this same *un-* ambiguity already discussed
for *ungelic* in *Wulf and Eadwacer* (Chapter 6/pt05). Presented as an open interpretive crux, not
resolved definitively.

### Ambiguous theology in the closing lines: Last Judgement or Harrowing of Hell? (p.226)

The poem's final passage (Christ rescuing souls who endured "burning," l.147–156) is explicitly
flagged by the source as supporting two different standard medieval-theological readings — either a
further reference to the Last Judgement (fitting the poem's dominant Second Coming theme developed
throughout section 3) or a reference to the **Harrowing of Hell** (Christ's traditional post-
crucifixion, pre-resurrection descent into Hell to free righteous pre-Christian souls). The source
notes the passage's consistent use of **past-tense** verbs (*Hiht wæs genīwad* 'hope was renewed',
*Se sunu wæs sigorfæst* 'the son was victorious') favors the Harrowing-of-Hell reading as an event
already completed, in tension with the Second-Coming framing's otherwise future orientation — left
as an open interpretive question rather than resolved.

### A personal, intimate closing register achieved through pronoun density (p.226)

The source notes a marked shift in register in the dreamer's final first-person section: seven uses
of *ic* 'I', eight of *mē* 'me', and one of *mīn* 'my' occur within just 25 lines (122–146),
creating — in the source's own words — "an intensely personal sense of involvement" as the dreamer
moves from being merely a passive visionary witness (sections 1 and, via the Cross's narration,
implicitly section 2) to the poem's final active agent, resolving his own devotion.

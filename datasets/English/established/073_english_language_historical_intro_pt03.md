## Grammar points

### Old English dialects (West Saxon, Anglian [Mercian/Northumbrian], Kentish)

The book stresses that the traditional four-dialect map (Figure 8) actually represents **political**
boundaries of four kingdoms, not confirmed spoken-dialect boundaries — real evidence is patchy and
concentrated in a few textual-production centers, heavily skewed toward **West Saxon**, since that
became the dominant late-Anglo-Saxon literary standard and later scribes often normalized other
dialects' texts into West Saxon spelling (flagged as a real problem for *Beowulf*, likely composed
in an Anglian dialect but surviving only in a West-Saxon-influenced manuscript). Crucially, **Modern
Standard English is NOT a direct descendant of the West Saxon literary standard** — it derives mainly
from an Anglian (East Midland) dialect. Worked diagnostic pairs: WS *weald* vs. Anglian *wald* →
Modern *wold* (Anglian-descended, not WS-descended, despite WS being the "prestige" written OE);
WS *ceald* vs. Anglian *cald* → Modern *cold* (again descends from the Anglian form); WS *hȳran*
"to hear" would regularly give Modern *\*hire*/*\*hure*, but Modern *hear* in fact regularly
descends from the Anglian form *hēran*.

### The Old English writing system: futhorc runes → Latin alphabet with runic loans

Runes (the *futhorc*, an angular script suited to carving, with clear structural similarities to
Greek/Italic alphabets) were in Germanic use for inscriptions from at least the 3rd century AD, but
only for short texts, not extended ones. Christianity (conversion beginning c. 600 AD, taking about
a century, proceeding from two directions — Celtic church from the north-west, Roman church from
the south-east) introduced the Latin alphabet for extended English writing, but the scribes
supplemented it with two runic symbols the Latin alphabet lacked: **wynn (ƿ)** for /w/ (modern
editions conventionally substitute *w*) and, separately, repurposed the Latin letter *d* with a
crossbar to create **eth (ð)**, used alongside the already-runic **thorn (þ)** — both represented
/θ/ and /ð/ indiscriminately, since OE speakers didn't phonemically distinguish the two the way
Modern English spelling-convention (but not pronunciation) implies. **Yogh (ȝ)**, used in later
Middle English (seen already in `pt01`'s Wycliffe passage), descends from this OE scribal
tradition. Vowel length is marked with a macron in *modern* editions by convention — original OE
manuscripts leave both long and short vowels unmarked.

### The OE phoneme inventory and its allophonic pairs (Table 5.1 and surrounding discussion)

OE script used six vowel letters (*a, e, i, o, u, y*) plus *æ* ("ash"), each representing both a
long and a short vowel (length unmarked in original manuscripts) — full inventory in Table 5.1
(p. 115): short/long pairs a/ā [ɑ/ɑː], æ/ǣ [æ/æː], e/ē [e/eː], i/ī [i/iː], o/ō [o/oː], u/ū [u/uː],
y/ȳ [y/yː] — note OE *y* was a rounded front vowel [y], not the Modern English glide/vowel value.
Diphthongs were written as digraphs *ea, eo, io, ie* (pronounced roughly [æɑ]/[eo]/[eu] etc.); *io*
and *ie* had already merged with *eo*/*i-y* respectively by the early West Saxon period the book
reconstructs from.

**Doubled consonant letters marked true gemination (actual lengthened consonant pronunciation),
not vowel shortness**, the reverse of the Modern English spelling convention — OE *assa* "donkey"
and *bucca* "he-goat" had a genuinely long/doubled consonant sound, comparable to Modern Italian or
Swedish, not to the vowel-marking function double consonants serve in Modern English spelling
(*written*/*copper* have short vowels marked by a single, not double, following consonant in OE
itself: OE *writen*, *copor*).

The book walks through **four phonemes that each had two context-conditioned allophones** in Old
English, all of which later split into independent Modern English phonemes:
- **/f/**: [f] word-initially/finally and next to voiceless sounds (*fæder* "father," *fīf*
  "five"), [v] between voiced sounds (*giefan* "to give," *lifde* "he lived") — directly ancestral
  to the Modern English /f/–/v/ phoneme split.
- **/s/**: [s] in most positions (*sǣ* "sea," *stānas* "stones"), [z] between voiced sounds (*nosu*
  "nose," *bōsm* "bosom") — ancestral to the Modern /s/–/z/ split.
- **/θ/**: written indiscriminately with þ or ð regardless of actual pronunciation; [θ] in most
  positions (*þegn* "thane," *trēowþ* "good faith"), [ð] between voiced sounds (*baþian* "to
  bathe," *fæþm* "embrace") — ancestral to the Modern /θ/–/ð/ split.
- **/k/**: a velar [k] before back vowels/consonants (*cēlan* "to cool," *cyssan* "to kiss" — note
  *cyssan*'s front vowel /y/ is misleading here, since it derives from a prehistoric back vowel
  *\*kunni-*), and a palatalized, eventually affricate [tʃ] before front vowels in prehistoric OE
  (*cinn* "chin," *cēosan* "to choose") — this pair had already split into two independent phonemes
  by the end of the OE period itself (minimal pair *cinn* [tʃinn] "chin" vs. *cynn* [kinn] "kin,"
  once the front-rounded vowel /y/ of *cynn* had independently unrounded to [i] in many dialects,
  removing the original vowel-based conditioning and leaving /k/ vs. /tʃ/ as the only distinguishing
  feature). A parallel [sk]→[ʃ] palatalization is seen in OE *sc* (*scip* "ship," *fisc* "fish,"
  pronounced with [ʃ]) vs. unpalatalized *sc* retained in some positions (*ascian* "to ask," *tusc*
  "tooth").

Similarly, **/g/** had a [j] allophone before/after front vowels (*gēar* "year," identical to the
Modern *y* of *yes*), a [g] allophone initially/after nasals (*gōd* "good"), and crucially a
**voiced velar fricative [ɣ]** allophone between back vowels (*fugol* "bird," *lagu* "law") — this
is a three-way allophonic split, unusually rich even among the chapter's other examples; the [ɣ]
allophone later became the approximant [w] in Middle English, giving Modern *fowl*, *law*. OE **/n/**
had the now-familiar [ŋ] allophone before /k/ or /g/ (*þancian* "to thank," *finger*), but unlike
Modern English the OE spelling *ng* always represented [ŋg] as a cluster, never bare [ŋ] (OE *hring*
"ring" = [hriŋg], not Modern [rɪŋ]) — [ŋ] did not become an independent phoneme in English until
around 1600. OE **/h/** likewise had positional allophones: glottal [h] syllable-initially, velar
[x] (German *ach*) or palatal [ç] (German *ich*) elsewhere depending on the preceding vowel (*hætt*
"hat" [hætt], *niht* "night" [niçt], *dohtor* "daughter" [doxtor]). OE **/r/** was a trill, not the
Modern English approximant, and — critically for reading OE aloud correctly — was pronounced in
positions where most Modern British accents now drop it (before a consonant or pause, e.g. *bearn*
"child," *wæter* "water").

### Prehistoric Old English sound changes: front mutation (i-umlaut), breaking, and back mutation

**Front mutation (i-umlaut)** is presented as the single most far-reaching prehistoric OE sound
change: any vowel followed by /i/, /ī/, or /j/ in the next syllable was fronted/raised toward that
following sound, after which the conditioning /i(ː)/ or /j/ itself typically disappeared or weakened
to /e/ — leaving a permanent, synchronically "unmotivated" vowel alternation in the daughter
language. The book catalogs its outcomes systematically, each with Modern English survivals: ā → ǣ
(*dāl*/*dǣlan*, "portion"/"to divide" — the chapter's running worked example, detailed under
Morpheme breakdown above); ū → ȳ (*mūs*/*mȳs* → *mouse*/*mice*); short u → y (OE *full*/*fyllan* →
*full*/*fill*; *fox*/*fyxen* → *fox*/*vixen*; *cnotta*/*cnyttan* → *knot*/*knit*; *lust*/*lystan* →
*lust*/the archaic verb *list* "to please"); ō → ē (or ǣ in non-West-Saxon dialects) (*fōd*/*fēdan* →
*food*/*feed*; also *doom*/*deem*, *goose*/*geese*, *tooth*/*teeth*, *blood*/*bleed*, *book*/*beech*);
and short a/æ/o → e (*man*/*men*; *wander*/*wend*; *long*/*length*; *tale*/*tell*; *straight*/
*stretch*). The book explicitly warns against confusing front-mutation pairs like *foot*/*feet* with
superficially similar but mechanistically unrelated pairs like *sing*/*sang*, which instead reflect
the much older Proto-Indo-European ablaut system covered in `pt02`'s Ch. 4 discussion.

**Breaking (fracture)** diphthongized short front vowels before /l/+consonant, /r/+consonant, and
/h/, with dialect-dependent results: West Saxon/Kentish *ceald* "cold," *earm* "arm," *eahta*
"eight" (diphthongized) vs. Anglian *cald*, *arm*, *æhta* (largely undiphthongized), compared
against Gothic *kalds*, *arms*, *ahtau* as an outside check on which forms are the innovation.
A related, separately-dated change, **palatal diphthongization**, diphthongized front vowels after
an initial palatalized consonant (West Saxon *geaf* "he gave," *giefan* "to give," *gēar* "year,"
*gīe* "ye, you" — though the book flags this one as contested, with some scholars arguing it may be
a purely orthographic convention signaling consonant quality rather than a real vowel-pronunciation
change). A later, distinct process, **back mutation (velar umlaut)**, diphthongized some front
vowels when a single consonant was followed by an unstressed back vowel in the next syllable (OE
*heofon* "heaven"); found extensively in Kentish and Anglian but, in West Saxon, only before a
limited set of consonants. The book gives no exact dates for any of these three changes but places
them broadly between the mid-5th and mid-8th centuries.

### Old English nominal morphology: case, declension, and gender

OE simplified the Proto-Germanic case system (see `pt02`) down to **four cases** (nominative,
accusative, genitive, dative — the vestigial PG instrumental/vocative are gone as live categories),
and the large PG set of declension classes collapsed toward **three or four major productive
patterns**, with syncretism eliminating many case distinctions that PG still kept separate: all
nouns share dative plural *-um* and most share genitive plural *-a*; many masculine nouns share
genitive singular *-es* and nominative/accusative plural *-as* (**this *-as* plural is the direct
historical ancestor of the now-near-universal Modern English *-(e)s* plural**, tying directly back
to the Ch. 2 analogy discussion in `pt01`); no OE noun distinguishes nominative from accusative in
the plural, and for many nouns these two cases are identical in the singular too (feminine *giefu*
"a gift" has one shared form, *giefe*, for accusative/genitive/dative singular; masculine *guma*
"a man" has one shared form, *guman*, for accusative/genitive/dative singular and for
nominative/accusative plural). OE **retained grammatical gender** (masculine/feminine/neuter,
assigned independent of real-world sex/animacy for inanimate nouns) to a greater extent than its
case system, with the adjective and the definite article (*se*) preserving more distinct
gender/case endings than the noun system itself did by this period.

### Old English verbal morphology: the strong-verb paradigm worked in full (helpan "to help")

The book gives a complete person/number/tense/mood paradigm for the strong verb *helpan* "to help"
as its worked example, making several structural points: present indicative distinguishes all three
singular persons (*ic helpe*, *þū hilpst*, *hē/hēo/hit hilpþ*) but collapses to one form for the
whole plural (*wē/gē/hīe helpaþ*); the present subjunctive has only two forms total, one for
singular (*helpe*) and one for plural (*helpen*) across all persons; imperatives likewise have
singular (*help*) and plural (*helpaþ*) forms; the past tense distinguishes a special second-person
singular (*þū hulpe* "you helped") from the shared first/third-person singular form (*ic/hēo healp*
"I/she helped"), collapses to one form for the whole past plural (*hulpon*), and the past subjunctive
again has only singular (*hulpe*) and plural (*hulpen*) forms; a past participle (*holpen*) and
present participle (*helpende*) complete the paradigm. The book's framing point: this is already
dramatically simplified relative to Proto-Germanic/PIE, yet still far richer than Modern English's
surviving verb-inflection system (which has essentially only a 3rd-singular-present *-s*, a past
tense, and the *-ing*/*-en* participles left).

**The perfect and passive were periphrastic constructions already in Old English**, built with
auxiliaries, not synthetic PIE/PG inflections — the perfect of transitive verbs used *habban* "to
have" + past participle (with the participle originally agreeing in case/gender/number with the
object, a concord that was already breaking down within the OE period itself, confirmed by the book
comparing two manuscripts of the *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle*'s entry for 755, one of which still shows
the agreement and one of which doesn't); the perfect of intransitive verbs instead used *bēon/wesan*
"to be" or *weorþan* "to become" + past participle (this "be"-perfect for intransitives is the exact
construction already seen surviving into the 1611 King James Bible's "thy brother is come" in
`pt01`, confirming OE is the direct source of that otherwise-puzzling Early Modern English
construction); the passive was built from *bēon/wesan* or *weorþan* + past participle, and — a
genuine grammatical restriction with no modern equivalent — **only verbs that could take an
accusative-case object could be passivized** in Old English; verbs taking a dative or genitive
object (including *helpan* itself, which normally governs a dative object) **could not form a
passive at all** until Middle English lost the dative/accusative object-case distinction, which is
specifically what first made constructions like Modern "many people are helped by you" possible.

### Old English word order: freedom conditioned by inflection, not genuinely "free"

Because case-marking (not word order) carried the subject/object distinction, OE had real
flexibility unavailable in Modern English — *se cyning hæfde micel geþeaht* "the king held a great
council" could be reordered to *micel geþeaht hæfde se cyning* for stylistic/information-structure
emphasis ("fronting" the object) without ambiguity, since the nominative article *se* unambiguously
marks the subject regardless of position; the equivalent Modern English reordering ("A great council
held the king") is actually ambiguous/wrong, since Modern English has no case marking left to carry
the distinction and must rely on word order alone. The book reconfirms, via a real worked example
(a five-clause sentence from King Alfred, diagrammed in the text), the same three clause-order
patterns from the `pt01` Luke 15 passage: S-V-O is the default main-clause order; V-S-O is triggered
by a fronted adverbial, especially *þā* "then" or *þǣr* "there"; S-O-V occurs in subordinate clauses,
and also specifically when the finite verb is a passive auxiliary placed *after* its past participle.

**Negation** used the particle *ne*, which frequently fused orthographically with a following
high-frequency verb (*ne* + *is* → *nis* "is not," *ne* + *wolde* → *nolde* "did not want," *ne* +
*mihte* → *nemihte* "could not"). **Multiple negation within a single clause was grammatical and
intensifying** (not mutually cancelling, unlike the Modern English "two negatives make a positive"
prescriptive rule) — the book's worked example is from the poem *Judith*: *nænne ne sparedon*,
literally "they did not spare no one," meaning "they spared absolutely no one." Questions used
V-S-O/V-S order without any *do*-auxiliary (*Hwȳ dydest þū þæt?* "Why did you do that?" — literally
"Why did-you that?," with no equivalent of Modern English's dummy *do*-support); OE never uses an
auxiliary *do* in main-clause questions or negation at all, a genuinely significant typological
difference from every later period of English covered in this project so far.

### Old English noun-phrase structure and the demonstrative system

The default NP pattern was determiner–adjective–noun, matching Modern English, but with
exceptions the book catalogs explicitly: the determiners *eall* "all" and *bēgen* "both," and
adjectives ending in *-weard*, could precede the determiner itself (*eal þæs middangeard* "this
entire earth"); adjectives could either precede or follow the noun even with a determiner present,
and more than one adjective could split across both positions in the same phrase (*Denum eallum*
"to all the Danes," *micle meras fersce* "big fresh-water meres"); a determiner could even follow
its noun, especially for emphasis (*Ic eom micle yldra þonne þēs world* "I am much older than this
world"). **Old English never uses a *do*-auxiliary for questions**, reconfirmed here as a syntax
point distinct from the earlier negation discussion.

Where Modern English has a three-way demonstrative system (*the*/*this*/*that*), **Old English had
only two demonstratives**, *se* ("the," also usable as "that") and *þēs* ("this") — but each was
far more richly inflected than Modern English's invariant forms: *se* alone has roughly ten distinct
surface forms across three genders, five cases (including a vestigial instrumental, the chapter's
fifth OE case, found only on the demonstrative/adjective system, not on nouns), and two numbers
(with gender distinctions collapsing in the plural, producing some formal overlap). The book flags
one specific, easy-to-miss point: **OE *þæt* is not a separate demonstrative word contrasting with
*se* — it is simply the nominative/accusative singular neuter form of *se* itself**, i.e. Modern
English's *the*/*that* distinction doesn't actually correspond to two separate OE demonstrative
paradigms the way it might appear.

### Old English pronoun case syncretism: the you/thou and him/her system's prehistory

The book traces the OE ancestry of two Modern English pronoun patterns already flagged in `pt01`'s
1611 Bible passage. **Thou/you**: in OE, *þū* and *þē* were strictly singular, *gē* and *ēow*
strictly plural, with no politeness dimension at all — the later Middle English custom of using the
plural *yē*/*you* as a polite/deferential singular form (eventually ousting *thou* from everyday
speech entirely, surviving only dialectally) is explicitly a Middle English innovation, not present
in OE. **Case syncretism in the personal pronouns**: OE *ēow* "you (plural)" was originally only
the *dative* case form ("to you," "for you," "with you"); the original plural accusative was a
distinct form, *ēowic*. But by the OE period itself, *ēowic* had already fallen out of use and been
replaced by the dative *ēow* for both functions — and the book notes **this same dative-absorbs-
accusative collapse eventually happened to nearly every Modern English personal pronoun**: *him*,
*me*, *us*, *her*, *thee* were all originally OE dative-only forms (the earlier true accusatives
were *hine*, *mec*, *ūsic*, *hīe*, *þec*), which is why Modern English's "accusative" pronoun forms
are technically descended from Old English datives, not Old English accusatives, for every personal
pronoun except *it*.

### Old English relative clauses

Interrogative *hwā* "who" and *hwilc* "which" existed in OE but **were not yet used as relative
pronouns** — OE instead formed relative clauses with the indeclinable particle *þe*, the
declinable demonstrative *se* (identical in form to the definite article), or both together.
*Who* was not used as a relative pronoun at all until Early Modern English, and even then not as
frequently as today — the 1611 King James Bible's Lord's Prayer still opens "Our Father, **which**
art in heaven," using *which* where Modern English would require *who*, directly matching the
pattern already flagged in `pt01`'s 1611 Bible passage ("thy sonne... which hath deuoured").

### Old English word-formation: derivational affixes and compounding

OE built new vocabulary mainly from its own native resources rather than borrowing, via two
distinct mechanisms the book keeps terminologically separate: **affixation** (adding a *bound*
morpheme — prefix or suffix — to a root) and **compounding** (joining two or more *free*
morphemes). Adjective-forming noun suffixes: *-ig*, *-lēas*, *-ful* (*blōdig* "bloody," *frēondlēas*
"friendless," *þancful* "thankful"). Abstract-noun-forming adjective suffix: a prehistoric PG suffix
*\*-iþō* (prehistoric OE *\*-iþu*) that itself triggered front mutation on the adjective root before
being lost, e.g. adjective *fūl* "foul, dirty" → prehistoric *\*fūliþu* → (after i-umlaut, then loss
of the suffix) recorded OE *fȳlþ* "impurity, filth." Adverb-forming adjective suffixes: *-e* and
*-līce* (*fæst* "firm" → *fæste* "firmly"; *blind* "blind" → *blindlīce* "blindly"). Verbal
prefixes: *for-* carries an intensifying/destructive force (*forhergian* "destroy by harrying,"
*forbærnan* "destroy by burning," from *hergian* "to harry, ravage" and *bærnan* "to burn"); *ge-*
often carries a **perfective** force, marking completion/achievement of an action rather than the
action in progress (*sceran* "to cut" vs. *gesceran* "to cut right through"; *rīdan* "to ride" vs.
*gerīdan* "to ride up to, conquer, occupy"; a worked textual example contrasts *siglde* "sailed"
[durative] against *gesiglan* "to get somewhere by sailing" [perfective, goal-reaching] within the
same sentence of King Alfred's account of the voyager Ohthere). Compounding examples, split by
register: learned/scholarly compounds in *-cræft* ("skill") — *bōccræft* "literature," *rīmcræft*
"arithmetic," *stæfcræft* "grammar," *tungolcræft* "astronomy" (literally "book/number/letter/star-
skill") — versus everyday/homely compounds like *ēarwicga* "earwig," *hāmstede* "homestead,"
*sunnebēam* "sunbeam," *wīfmann* "woman" (several surviving into Modern English with little
change, *wīfmann* being the direct ancestor of *woman*).

### Old English poetic register: alliterative meter and specialized vocabulary

OE poetry used **alliterative, not rhyming, verse**: each line divides into two half-lines, each
half-line carrying two fully-stressed syllables, with (in general) the first stress of the second
half-line required to alliterate with one or both stresses of the first half-line — the second
stress of the second half-line only rarely also alliterates — and all vowels were treated as mutually
alliterating with one another regardless of which specific vowel. The book explicitly frames this as
an internalized, unconsciously-applied compositional pattern, not a consciously followed rulebook
(by analogy with limerick composition in Modern English), and notes the fine-grained syllable-count/
stress "rules" scholars have since described became stricter as the OE period went on. This register
had its own dedicated, highly conventional vocabulary stock — maintained specifically to supply
alliteration options — including formulaic opening phrases ("so-and-so spoke"), a large set of
near-synonyms for "warrior" (*beorn* among them) and for "weapon"/"sea"/"prince," descriptive
compounds functioning as kennings (*swanrād* "sea," literally "swan-road," from *Beowulf*), and
decorative periphrastic epithets for a king reflecting the social obligation to distribute treasure
to followers (*beaga brytta* "ring-giver," *sincgiefa* "treasure-giver"). The chapter's worked
specimen is an excerpt of the poem commemorating the 991 Battle of Maldon (Essex forces under
ealdorman Byrhtnoth defeated by Viking raiders at the mouth of the River Blackwater), specifically
the retainer Byrhtwold's speech vowing to die beside his fallen lord rather than flee — presented by
the book with full interlinear gloss and an idiomatic Modern English rendering.

### The "Letter to brother Eadweard" — a rare informal-register OE prose specimen

The book's other full specimen text is a short OE prose letter (sole surviving manuscript: Oxford,
Bodleian Library, MS Hatton 115) addressing a monk, "brother Eadweard," rebuking him and his
community for adopting Danish dress and grooming customs (low-cut tops, long hair) and for crude
toilet-related table manners, explicitly framed by the book as a rare window into informal,
colloquial-register Anglo-Saxon discourse rather than the heroic/religious registers that dominate
the surviving OE corpus — directly relevant to this project's interest in register variation. The
book highlights the passage's subjunctive verb forms (*secge*, *mæge*, *sceole*, *afylle*, *gange*,
*drince* — contrasted with their indicative counterparts *segst*, *meaht*, *sceal*, *afylleþ*,
*gangeþ*, *drinc(e)þ*) and notes the OE "present tense" is routinely used where Modern English would
require a future ("ne secge ic na mare embe" = "I will say no more about," not "I say no more
about").

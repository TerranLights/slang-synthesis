## Grammar points

### 5.1 Quantifiers + countable and uncountable nouns

**5.1A The four-way combinability system.** English quantifiers fall into four non-overlapping
combinability groups depending on whether they can modify plural countable nouns, uncountable nouns,
both, or (a distinct fourth group) only singular countable nouns. See the Vocabulary table rows for
the member lists of groups A (plural countable only), B (uncountable only), C (plural countable or
uncountable), and D (singular countable only). This structural distinction is the organizing
principle for the rest of the chapter, p.66.

**5.1B Quantifiers expressing exactness vs. a rough estimate.** Exact numbers ("five magazines")
state quantity precisely; most quantity words ("some," "a few") give only a rough impression, and can
be arranged on an implicit scale running from *too much* (most) down to *no* (least), p.67.

### 5.2 General and specific references to quantity

**5.2A 'Of' after quantifiers.** A subset of quantifiers always requires *of* before a following
noun/pronoun; a separate subset (*some, any, much, many*) is used without *of* for general reference
and only takes "of + the/my..." when the reference becomes specific. *None of* is an exception,
always specific, p.68.

**5.2B Quantifiers without a following noun.** When a quantifier stands alone (nothing following it),
*of* is dropped entirely, p.68.

**5.2C 'More' and 'less' after quantifiers.** *More* intensifies quantity with either plural or
uncountable nouns (different member-lists for each); *less* intensifies only with uncountable nouns,
p.69.

### 5.3 Uses of 'some', 'any', 'no' and 'none'

**5.3A Some/any or zero in relation to quantity.** The "plural of a/an" for quantity is normally
*some/any*, but zero article carries the identical meaning in many of the same contexts; general
statements (as opposed to quantity statements) always use zero, p.70.

**5.3B Four basic uses of some/any.** *Some*: affirmatives, requests. *Any*: negatives, uncertain
yes/no questions, after *hardly*, intensified by *at all*. Both words also carry special idiomatic
meanings beyond ordinary quantity reference — see the Vocabulary table, p.70.

**5.3C 'Not...any', 'no', and 'none'.** *No* substitutes for "not any" (taking an affirmative verb)
and for "not a/an"; *none* is the free-standing pronoun form used when nothing follows, p.71.

**5.3D Other, idiomatic uses of some/any.** Both words carry non-quantity idiomatic senses — *some*
as "several" in time references, or as an intensifier meaning "a remarkable"; *any* meaning "it
doesn't matter which one," p.71.

### 5.4 'Much', 'many', 'a lot of', '(a) few', '(a) little', 'fewer', 'less'

**5.4A Basic uses.** *much* (uncountable) and *many* (plural countable) appear in negatives and
questions; *a lot of*/informal *lots of* is the default for affirmative statements regardless of
countability, p.72.

**5.4B Other uses of much/many/a lot of.** *much/many* also appear in the affirmative in formal
registers, after "as...as," and in time-duration expressions; *Not much/Not many* can open a
sentence; *not a lot of* adds emphasis, p.72.

**5.4C Few/a few, little/a little.** *few/little* carry an inherently negative sense ("hardly any");
*a few/a little* are positive ("some"); both can be intensified toward the negative pole with *very*
or toward the positive pole with *only*, p.72.

**5.4D Fewer and less.** *fewer* is the comparative of *few* and takes plural countables; *less* is
the comparative of *little* and formally takes only uncountables — though informal usage increasingly
extends *less* to plural countables as well, which the source flags as contested among native
speakers, p.73.

### 5.5 'Both' and 'all'

**5.5A Both/both the vs. all/all the with nouns.** *both(the)* refers to exactly two particular
items (plural countable only); *all* + noun is general (the whole number/amount, countable or
uncountable); *all the* + noun is particular, p.74.

**5.5B Word order with verbs.** *both/all* occupy one of three fixed positions in an affirmative
sentence: after *be* when it's the only verb; after the (first) auxiliary when there's more than one
verb; before the main verb when there's exactly one verb and it isn't *be*, p.74.

**5.5C Word order with pronouns.** As subject, *both/all* follow the pronoun directly or appear in
"both/all of us/them"; as object, only the "of"-form is used ("I love both of you," not "*I love you
both/all*" is in fact also possible for *both*, but *all* as object pronoun-suffix is restricted —
see source for the exact distributional table), p.75.

**5.5D 'None of' and 'neither of'.** *none of* is the negative of *all the*; *neither of* is the
negative of *both the*, p.75.

### 5.6 'All (the)', '(a/the) whole', 'each' and 'every'

**5.6A All(the) vs. (a/the) whole.** *the whole*/*a whole* combine only with singular countable
nouns (never plurals or uncountables); certain nouns are lexically restricted to combine with only
*all*, only *whole*, or either; both also combine with time references, but *whole* is the
"stronger" choice and uniquely extends to units like *hour* and *century*, p.76.

**5.6B All vs. everyone/everybody/everything.** *all* is rarely used alone to mean "everyone" —
*everyone/everybody* is the normal choice, with *all* requiring extra supporting words (*all of us*,
*all those who...*) to carry that meaning. The things-parallel is *everything*, though *all* reduces
to zero when used as a direct object before a pronoun complement ("He gave me everything," never "He
gave me all"), p.76.

**5.6C Each and every.** *each* individuates ("one by one/separately") and is used for exactly two or
more than two; *every* treats the group collectively ("all together") and is used only for more than
two. *every* (never *each*) is obligatory after *nearly* and after *not*. *every* cannot take *of* or
stand at the end of a sentence; *each* can do both. *every* combines with a small set of uncountable
nouns in fixed expressions, p.77.

### 5.7 'Another', '(the) other(s)', 'either', 'neither', 'each (one of)'

**5.7A Another, other, others, the other, the others.** *another* is always non-specific (different
or additional); *other(s)* contrasts with *some* for a general class; *the other(s)* picks out the
remaining member(s) of a specific, already-identified set — applying to people as well as things. The
fixed narrative-time idioms "the other day" (a few days ago) and "the next day" (the following day)
derive from this system, p.78.

**5.7B Either and neither + singular noun.** Both refer only to a set of exactly two: *either* =
"one or the other" (doesn't matter which); *neither* = "not one and not the other", p.78.

**5.7C 'Each of', etc.** *of* + the/my/etc. follows any, some, another, each, either, neither, none
when a following noun/pronoun is specific. After *either/neither/none* with a plural referent,
everyday speech uses a plural verb while formal/"correct" register uses a singular verb, p.79.

**5.7D 'One of'.** *each of these* and *each one of these* are equivalent; *of* or *one of* can
follow any/another/each/either/neither, but *every* obligatorily requires either a following noun
or "one of" — bare "*every of*" is always ungrammatical, p.79.

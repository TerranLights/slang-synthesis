## Grammar points

### Progressive conjugation and voice (§5.116)

An inflected form of *be* joined with another verb's present participle produces a progressive
conjugation (*the ox is pulling the cart*). CMOS notes the progressive conjugation is in the active
voice, since the subject performs the action rather than being acted on — distinguishing it from
passive constructions covered just before this chunk's range (§5.115, not re-extracted here).

### Mood (§5.117)

Mood (or mode) indicates the manner in which a verb expresses an action or state of being. English has
three moods: indicative, imperative, and subjunctive.

### Indicative mood (§5.118)

The indicative is the most common mood in English, used to express facts and opinions and to ask
questions (*amethysts cost very little*; *the botanist lives in a garden cottage*; *Does that bush
produce yellow roses?*).

### Imperative mood (§5.119)

The imperative expresses commands (*Go away!*), direct requests (*bring the tray in here*), and
sometimes permission (*Come in!*). The subject is the understood *you*, even when the sentence
includes a direct address that is not itself the subject (*Cindy, take good care of yourself* — *Cindy*
is a direct address, not the subject). CMOS cautions that the bare imperative can read as blunt or
unintentionally rude in some contexts; it can be softened with a word like *please*, or recast in the
indicative (*Will you stop at the store please?*).

### Subjunctive mood (§5.120)

Though not often used in American English, the subjunctive remains useful for expressing an action or
state not as reality but as a mental conception — doubtful, imagined, desired, conditional,
hypothetical, or contrary to fact. Beyond the classic hypothetical (*if I were you*), it can signal
wishes (*if I were a rich man*), conjecture (*oh, were it so*), demands (*the landlord insists that the
dog go*), and suggestions (*I recommend that she take a vacation*). Despite its general decline, it
survives in fixed stock expressions such as *perish the thought*, *heaven help us*, and *be that as it
may* (captured in the Vocabulary table above).

### Indicative instead of subjunctive (§5.121)

Not every *if*-clause takes a subjunctive verb, even though the subjunctive is most often signaled by
*if*. When the action or state might be true but the writer simply doesn't know, the indicative is
used instead (*if I am right about this, please call*; *if Napoleon was in fact poisoned with arsenic,
historians will need to reevaluate his associates*) — contrasted with the contrary-to-fact subjunctive
use at §5.120.

### Tenses generally (§5.122)

Tense shows the time at which an act, state, or condition occurs or occurred. The three major time
divisions are present, past, and future (CMOS flags, with a cross-reference to §5.125, that some
modern grammarians hold English has no true future tense at all). Each division also has a
corresponding perfect tense marking a comparatively more remote time: present perfect, past perfect,
and future perfect.

### Present tense (§5.123)

The present tense is the infinitive verb's bare stem, also called the present indicative (*walk*,
*drink*). It primarily denotes acts, conditions, or states occurring in the present (*the dog howls*;
*the air is cold*; *the water runs*). It is also used to: (1) express a habitual action or general truth
(*cats prowl nightly*; *polluted water is a health threat*); (2) refer to timeless facts, including
memorable persons and still-extant/enduring works of the past (*Julius Caesar describes his strategies
in* The Gallic War; *the Pompeiian mosaics are exquisite*); and (3) narrate a fictional work's plot
(*the scene takes place aboard the* Titanic). CMOS flags point (3) as particularly important for
writers discussing literature — characters in books, plays, and films *do* things, not *did* them —
and notes the present perfect (§5.126) is often the useful tense for distinguishing present action from
past action when writing about literature (*Hamlet, who has spoken with his father's ghost, reveals
what he has learned to no one but Horatio*).

### Past tense (§5.124)

The past tense is formed by inflection (CMOS cross-references §5.98 for the inflection rules
generally, not re-extracted here); the basic inflected form is the past indicative (*walked*, *drank*).
It denotes an act, state, or condition that occurred or existed at some explicit or implicit point in
the past (*the auction ended yesterday*; *we returned the shawl*).

### Future tense (§5.125)

The future tense is formed using *will* with the verb's stem form (*will walk*, *will drink*),
referring to an expected act, state, or condition (*the artist will design a wall mural*; *the
restaurant will open soon*). *Shall* may substitute for *will*, but in American English it is now
largely confined to first-person questions involving choice (*Shall we go?*) and to legal commands
(*the debtor shall pay within 30 days*); in most contexts *will* is preferred. CMOS notes — citing R.
L. Trask's *Language* (p. 60) — that most linguists now hold English has no true future tense at all,
*will* being simply a modal verb like any other, but the chapter retains "future tense" as a
traditional-grammar convenience. See the *shall*/*will* Vocabulary entries above.

### Present perfect tense (§5.126)

Formed with *have* or *has* plus the principal verb's past participle (*have walked*, *has drunk*). It
denotes an act, state, or condition now completed or continuing up to the present (*I have put away
the clothes*; *it has been a long day*), distinguished from the simple past by referring either (1) to
an indefinite time in the past (*I have played golf there before*) or (2) to a past action that
extends up to and touches the present (*I have played cards for the last eighteen hours*). The simple
past, by contrast, indicates a more specific or more remote time.

### Past perfect tense (§5.127)

Formed with *had* plus the principal verb's past participle (*had walked*, *had drunk*). It refers to
an act, state, or condition completed before another specified or implicit past time or action (*the
engineer had driven the train to the roundhouse before we arrived*; *by the time we stopped to check
the map, the rain had begun falling*; *the movie had ended already*).

### Future perfect tense (§5.128)

Formed with *will have* plus the verb's past participle (*will have walked*, *will have drunk*). It
refers to an act, state, or condition expected to be completed before some other future act or time
(*the entomologist will have collected sixty more specimens before the semester ends*; *the court will
have adjourned by five o'clock*). *Shall* can also form the future perfect, but usually only for the
first person, and is not preferred (*I shall have finished by tomorrow*; *we shall have written before
we embark*).

### Person (§5.129)

A verb's person shows whether the act, state, or condition belongs to: (1) the person speaking — first
person, *I*/*we*; (2) the person spoken to — second person, *you*; or (3) the person spoken of — third
person, *he*/*she*/*it*/*they*.

### Number (§5.130)

A verb's number must agree with the number of its noun or pronoun. Only the third-person
present-indicative singular has a distinct form marking number and person (*you sketch*, *they sketch*,
but *she sketches*). The second-person verb form is always the same ("plural" in form) whether one
person or more than one is addressed (*you are a wonderful person*; *you are wonderful people*).

### Agreement in person and number (§5.131)

A finite verb agrees with its subject in person and number. Two or more subjects connected by *and*
take a plural verb agreeing with them jointly (*Socrates and Plato were wise*). Two or more singular
subjects connected by *or* or *nor* take a singular verb (*Jill or Jan is prepared to speak*; *neither
Bob nor John has learned his lesson*). A collective noun conveying unity/multitude as a single whole
takes a singular verb (*the nation is powerful*); a collective noun conveying the idea of plurality
among its members takes a plural verb (*the faculty were divided in their sentiments*).

### False attraction to predicate nominative (§5.132)

A plural predicate nominative following a singular subject can mislead a writer into supplying a
plural verb. Simply correcting the verb's number can leave the sentence sounding awkward, so CMOS
recommends reworking the sentence instead: *wrong* — "My downfall are sweets"; *right* — "My downfall
is sweets"; *better* — "Sweets are my downfall."

### Misleading connectives: "as well as," "along with," "together with," and so forth (§5.133)

Adding to a singular subject via a phrasal connective such as *along with*, *as well as*, *in addition
to*, or *together with* does not make the subject plural, even though such intervening material can
look like it creates a compound subject (and the modifying prepositional phrase may itself contain
plural objects, compounding the confusion). If the resulting singular verb sounds awkward, CMOS
suggests using the conjunction *and* instead: *wrong* — "The bride as well as her bridesmaids were
dressed in mauve"; *right* — "The bride as well as her bridesmaids was dressed in mauve"; *better* —
"The bride and her bridesmaids were dressed in mauve."

### Conjugation defined (§5.134)

Conjugation means changing (inflecting) a verb's form to reflect voice, mood, tense, person, and
number. A verb has seven conjugated parts: the present indicative, the present participle, the present
subjunctive, the past indicative, the past participle, the past subjunctive, and the imperative. (CMOS
notes the rules given for conjugation apply to all verbs except auxiliaries.)

### Verb stem / present indicative (§5.135)

The present indicative is simply the verb stem for every person, singular and plural — except the
third-person singular, which adds *-s* to the stem (*takes*, *strolls*, *says*). If the verb ends in
*-o*, *-es* is added instead (*goes*, *does*). If the verb ends in a consonant followed by *-y*, the
*-y* changes to *-i* and *-es* is added (*identify → identifies*; *carry → carries*).

### Forming present participles (§5.136)

The present participle is formed by adding *-ing* to the verb stem (*reaping*, *wandering*). If the
stem ends in *-ie*, the *-ie* usually changes to *-y* before *-ing* is added (*die → dying*; *tie →
tying*). If the stem ends in a silent *-e*, the *-e* is usually dropped before adding *-ing* (*giving*,
*leaving*), with two exceptions: (a) the silent *-e* is retained when the word ends in *-oe* (*toe →
toeing*; *hoe → hoeing*; *shoe → shoeing*), or (b) when dropping it would make the participle resemble
a different word it needs to stay distinguished from (e.g., *dyeing* — from *dye* — stays distinct from
*dying*, and *singeing* stays distinct from *singing*). Regular and irregular verbs form the present
participle the same way, and the present participle is invariant across person and number.

### Present subjunctive (§5.137)

Apart from a few set phrases (*so be it*, *be they*, *she need not*), the present-day English present
subjunctive typically appears in the form *if I [he, she, it] were* (*if I were king*; *if she were any
different*) — that is, it ordinarily uses a past-tense verb form (*were*) to connote uncertainty,
impossibility, or unreality. CMOS contrasts the indicative *if I am threatened, I will quit* with the
subjunctive *if I were threatened, I would quit*, and the indicative *if the canary sings, I smile*
with the subjunctive *if the canary sang [or should sing, or were to sing], I would smile* (see also
§5.120).

### Past indicative (§5.138)

The past indicative of a regular verb is formed by adding *-ed* to its base form (*like → liked*;
*spill → spilled*). In a word of more than one syllable, the final consonant is doubled only if it is
part of a syllable stressed both before and after the inflection (*prefer → preferred*, doubled) but
not otherwise (*travel → traveled*, not doubled). **Explicit regional annotation:** CMOS notes British
English draws no such stress-based distinction — "all such consonants are doubled" regardless (so
British usage would give *travelled*). Irregular verbs form the past tense in various, non-rule-bound
ways (*give → gave*; *stride → strode*; *read → read*); CMOS advises consulting a good dictionary for
any given irregular verb's past-indicative form.

### Forming past participles (§5.139)

For regular verbs, the past participle is formed the same way as the past indicative — the two forms
are always identical (*stated/stated*; *pulled/pulled*). For irregular verbs, the past indicative and
past participle are sometimes identical (*paid/paid*; *sat/sat*) and sometimes different (*forsook/
forsaken*; *shrank/shrunk*). CMOS again advises consulting a good dictionary for a given irregular
verb's past participle.

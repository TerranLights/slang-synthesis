## Chapter 1 note: oral-formulaic theory

Chapter 1 is a historiographical survey and contributes no vocabulary/grammar data of its own, but
it defines one concept that recurs throughout the rest of the book and is picked up again in Ch.3
(see Part 2 of this extraction): **oral-formulaic theory**, first applied to *Beowulf* by Francis
Magoun in 1953 (building on Milman Parry and Albert Lord's work on Homer and on South Slavic oral
epic), holds that heavy verbatim and near-verbatim phrase-repetition in a text is diagnostic of oral
(rather than literate) composition. Orchard notes (p.6) that the ensuing "oral-formulaic debate" has
shifted from arguing about *Beowulf*'s orality per se toward analysing the originality and artistry
of individual poets working within an inherited, traditional technique of composition — a
distinction directly relevant to how "formulaic" diction should be read as a register/register-
history phenomenon rather than proof of a text's transmission mode.

## Grammar points

### Scribal letter-confusion catalog (*translitteratio*)

Orchard catalogs, from both the critical apparatus of modern *Beowulf* editions and the manuscript's
own visible self-corrections, a systematic set of **confusable Old English letter-pairs** — graphs
similar enough in the Insular minuscule script that scribes regularly substituted one for the other
in copying (termed *translitteratio* or *metacharakterismos*). Pairs confirmed either by conjectural
editorial emendation or by an actual corrected manuscript reading include: **a/æ**, **a/e**,
**a/ea**, **a/o**, **a/u**, **æ/o**, **æ/e**, **b/h**, **b/þ**, **c/cg**, **c/g**, **c/t**, **c/o**,
**d/t**, **d/ð (or þ)**, **e/ea**, **e/i**, **e/ie**, **eo/o**, **f/p**, **f/s**, **h/þ**, **i/u**,
**l/r**, **m/n**, **n/d**, **n/m**, **n/nd (or dn)**, **n/r**, **p/ƿ (wynn)**, **r/s**, **r/ƿ**, and
**þ/ƿ**. (pp.42–46, 63–66). This is a genuinely useful register/orthography datum: it is a
project-relevant catalog of which Old English graphemes were phonetically/visually close enough to
be mutually substitutable by native or near-native copyists — a constraint on plausible sound-
symbol mappings for any derived-conlang orthography modeled on this script tradition.

### Mechanical copying-error taxonomy

Alongside simple letter-confusion, Orchard identifies four recurring categories of scribal copying
error, both as conjectured by modern editors and as directly attested by the scribes' own visible
self-corrections in the manuscript: **haplography** (writing one letter/form where two were in the
exemplar — omission through "eye-skip"), **dittography** (writing two letters/forms, or even a whole
word, where only one belonged — e.g. *hlaford hlaford* corrected to *hlaford*), **metathesis**
(transposition of adjacent letters or forms, e.g. *wlocn* corrected to *wolcn*), and
**homoeoteleuton** (eye-skip specifically triggered by two nearby words or phrases sharing the same
ending) (pp.42–51). Orchard treats these as predictable failure modes of hand-copying generally, not
*Beowulf*-specific — he finds the same four categories in the critical apparatus of several other
Old English poems (Genesis A, Exodus, Andreas, Christ I–III, Guthlac A/B, Phoenix, Juliana, Elene,
Daniel, Soul and Body I, Maxims I, Deor, several Riddles, Judith; pp.45–46, n.146).

### *io*-spellings vs. *eo*-spellings as a scribal/dialectal marker

Within the single composite Beowulf-manuscript, the five constituent Old English texts show a
measurable difference in how often they spell the *eo* diphthong as *io* instead. The texts
*Christopher* (scribe A) and *Judith* (scribe B) have **zero** *io*-spellings; *Wonders* has 2 in 17
pages; the *Letter of Alexander* has 66 in 50 pages; and within *Beowulf* itself, **scribe B shows a
strong preference for *io*-spellings** (115 instances across his 53 pages, ~1 every 2.2 pages) while
**scribe A shows a much weaker one** (11 instances across his 87 pages, ~1 every 7.9 pages) — Sisam's
and Lucas's conclusion is that scribe B tended to *retain* inherited *io*-spellings from his exemplar
while scribe A actively eliminated them when copying. This distributional pattern is used as
codicological evidence for how the manuscript's constituent texts were assembled over time (pp.23,
43–44). This is a clean, quantified example of **orthographic variation functioning as a scribal
(and by extension dialectal/chronological) fingerprint** separable from simple error.

### Minority genitive plural ending in *-o*

Alongside the usual Old English genitive plural ending (*-a*), a handful of *Beowulf* nouns show a
minority ending in **-o**: *yldo* (line 70a) and *hynðo* (line 475a) in *Beowulf* itself, and
*siðfato*, *leohtfato*, *earfeðo*, *Medo*, *ondswaro* in the manuscript's *Letter of Alexander*.
Orchard also notes the related general spelling-variant pattern of **u for medial f** found across
three of the manuscript's five texts (p.23, with n.46).

### Manuscript punctuation marks metrical units, not syntactic ones

The Beowulf-manuscript's own punctuation consists of the simple point (·), occurring roughly once
every 4.5 lines of verse. Orchard shows this is placed overwhelmingly (~90%) at the end of a full
verse-line, and almost all the remainder at the end of a half-line — i.e. **the manuscript's native
punctuation tracks the poem's metrical structure, not its syntax**, in sharp contrast to the heavy
syntax-based punctuation modern editions impose (comparable editions insert 19–33 punctuation marks
within just the poem's first 25 lines, versus roughly 9 in the manuscript itself) (pp.40–42). This is
a clear, quotable example of a verse tradition whose native "sentence-boundary" marking is prosodic/
metrical rather than grammatical — directly relevant to how a derived conlang's own verse tradition
might mark structure.

### A closed set of adjectives governing the genitive case

Citing Rudanko's classification, Orchard notes that the adjective *strang* ('strong') belongs to an
eight-member class of Old English adjectives that govern the genitive case when describing health,
physical strength, or mental presence: **cræftig, gemyndig, gesund, hal, heard, seoc, strang, wis**
(p.55, n.186). *Seoc* ('sick') is noted as the one member of the class that can instead take a
dative, with a distinct meaning shift (dative = "cause," genitive = "the respect in which [the
subject] is sick").

### Alliterative/metrical considerations as a constraint on textual emendation

A recurring methodological point throughout the chapter: modern editors frequently justify
conjectural emendations to the manuscript's wording not just on grounds of sense, but because the
transmitted reading fails to alliterate, or breaks an established metrical/structural pattern (e.g.
restoring an expected alliterating word-pair, or supplying a missing half-line where its absence
would leave a line short of the expected two stressed, alliterating positions). Orchard treats
**alliteration and metre as genuine evidence for what a damaged or garbled line originally said**,
on a par with palaeographical and syntactic evidence (pp.47–48, 54–56, and throughout).

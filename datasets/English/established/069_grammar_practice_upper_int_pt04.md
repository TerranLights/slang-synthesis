## Grammar points

### The be going to future

Form: *to be* + *going* + *to*-infinitive (p.54). Two uses: expressing an intention formed before
the moment of speaking, and making a prediction based on present evidence ("The sun's going down.
It's going to be dark in half an hour").

### Present continuous as future

A future time reference must be stated or clear from context. Used to describe arrangements that
have already been made — characteristically informal, personal/social arrangements rather than
official plans, and the subject must be a person, not a thing (p.54).

### Contrast: be going to future versus present continuous as future

Both can express the same future event with a difference of emphasis: *be going to* foregrounds the
**intention**, present continuous foregrounds the **arrangement** (p.54). The present continuous is
not generally used for activities that don't involve making an arrangement/plan — "I'm going to
write a letter to my parents tonight" (intention, no arrangement needed) cannot become "I'm writing
a letter..." without implying a prior arrangement. Verbs of motion (*go, come*) are conventionally
avoided in the *be going to* future in favor of the present continuous ("We're going to the theatre
tonight," not "We're going to go to the theatre"). The present continuous cannot be used to make a
prediction from present evidence the way *be going to* can — "Look at that helicopter coming down.
It's going to land in a minute" is correct, but "It's landing in a minute" is not, in that
predictive sense (p.55). The present continuous as future requires an explicit or contextually clear
future time reference to be read as future at all, rather than as a present-moment activity ("He's
having a swim" = now, vs. "He's going to have a swim" = later) (p.55). Verbs not normally used in
the continuous (stative verbs) are excluded from the present continuous as future ("I'm needing some
help next week" is ungrammatical) but can sometimes appear in the *be going to* future, albeit rarely,
since such verbs don't typically describe predictable/intended events ("I'm going to need some help
next week" is acceptable) (p.55).

### Future with will

Also called the future simple/simple future tense. Form: *will* ('ll) + bare infinitive; short
negative form *won't* (p.56). Seven distinct uses are catalogued: a statement of fact or prediction
about the future; formal announcements of future plans and weather forecasts (hence common in news
media); hopes/expectations/thoughts about the future, typically after verbs like *assume, believe,
doubt, expect, hope, reckon, suppose, think, be sure/afraid* and adverbs like *perhaps, possibly,
probably, definitely*; in clauses of condition or time; expressing an intention decided at the
moment of speaking; expressing willingness, itself subdivided into six distinct speech-act readings
depending on context — promise, request (*will* in a question), offer, refusal (negated), agreement,
determination, and threat, each illustrated with a separate worked example; and making offers/
suggestions or asking for suggestions/advice/instructions using *Shall I/we* in question form.

**Notes on *shall* (p.57):** in spoken English, *shall* appears mainly in first-person questions for
offers/suggestions/requests for advice; it is occasionally used to express strong personal
determination about oneself or someone else ("I shall go anyway...," "You shall have whatever you
want" — a notably formal/archaic-flavored register); *shall* can substitute for *will* in the first
person, though *will* (and its contraction *'ll*) is more common in speech; *shall* overall occurs
more frequently in formal written English than in informal written or spoken English; the short
negative form is *shan't*.

### Contrast: be going to versus will

Four dimensions of contrast are worked through systematically (pp.58–59):

- **Intention:** *be going to* signals a premeditated decision made before speaking, with plans
  likely already underway; *will* signals an unpremeditated, on-the-spot decision.
- **Prediction:** *be going to* is used when the prediction is grounded in present evidence (and
  can extend to predictions beyond the immediate future, e.g. "That child eats too much. He's going
  to be really overweight one day"); *will* is used for predictions based on belief, hope, thought,
  or assumption rather than present evidence.
- **Statement of future fact:** both are usable, but *will* reads as a more detached statement of
  fact/belief, while *be going to* signals the speaker is thinking of present trends, making the
  utterance feel more involved and informal.
- **Expression of willingness:** only *will* carries this meaning (a promise, "I'll help you as much
  as I can"); *be going to* in the parallel sentence expresses intent, not willingness.

**Summary (p.58):** *be going to* always signals a link to the present (current
intention/plans/preparations, or present evidence of a future event); *will* emphasizes the future
itself over any present link; *will* is more formal than *be going to* and is used more extensively
in written English.

### Present simple as future

Used in clauses of time and condition (after *if, unless, when, as soon as, until, before, after, by
the time*) and after indefinite pronouns like *everything, anything, whatever* (p.61). Separately,
used to refer to future events that are part of a fixed official timetable, itinerary, or
organized schedule — the timing is fixed by some official body or system, not by personal intention
("The flight leaves at 6 o'clock in the morning").

### Future continuous

Form: *will be* + present participle (p.62). Three uses: an activity that will be in progress at a
specific future point ("This time tomorrow we'll be sitting on the plane"); an activity that will
span the whole of a future time period ("I'll be working in the office all evening"); and a future
event presented without deliberate intention — the event is simply expected to happen as a matter of
course, not as a decision (contrasted explicitly with a parallel *will* sentence that does express
intention: "I'll be revising for my exams this weekend" states an expected activity, while the
accompanying decision "I think I'll stay in on Saturday night" is the only part that expresses
intention). A fourth, related use covers describing what we assume someone else is currently doing
based on habit/routine ("It's 4 o'clock and she'll be collecting the children from school"). A note
cross-references p.27: the future continuous, like other continuous forms, is not used with verbs
that don't normally take the continuous.

### Contrast: future continuous versus will future

*Will* future expresses intention, belief, hope, assumption, or willingness; future continuous
indicates a future activity/event without expressing intention or willingness (p.63). The book's
own paired-example table makes this concrete: "I'll come and visit you tomorrow" reads as
intention/promise, while "I'll be coming to visit you tomorrow (so I can bring the magazines then)"
reads as a future activity occurring in the normal course of events; "Will you come to the meeting
tonight?" reads as a request, while "Will you be coming to the meeting tonight?" reads as a casual
question about a future event (not a request for action).

### Contrast: future continuous versus present continuous as future

Both can refer to plans, but the present continuous as future is used when the intention itself is
not the important part, while the future continuous is characteristically used when a plan is first
being mentioned, or when the plan is relevant to the main future event under discussion rather than
being the main point itself (p.65) — e.g. "We'll be spending the summer on the coast" used to set up
a connected comment ("so I might try sailing then"), or "Will you be using your bike tomorrow?" used
to set up a request to borrow it.

### Future perfect simple and continuous

Form: future perfect simple = *will have* + past participle; future perfect continuous = *will have
been* + present participle (p.66). Equivalent of the present perfect but with the reference point
moved into the future — the present perfect looks back from now, the future perfect looks back from
a future point. Three uses: something that will be completed, and thus in the past, by a specific
future time (often with *by* + time reference); duration of an activity/state up to a future time
point, usable with *for* in either the simple or continuous form; and the continuous specifically to
describe an activity leading up to a future time point ("They might be tired when you see them
because they'll have been working hard").

### Other ways of referring to the future

Beyond the dedicated future tense forms, English uses several verbs/expressions — more precise than
a generic future form and common especially in written English — to refer to the future (p.68):

- ***be to*** (*am/is/are/was/were* + *to*-infinitive): formal plans, official arrangements,
  instructions, and prohibitions ("The British Ambassador is to meet the President..."; "You're not
  to cross the road without me").
- ***be due to*** + *to*-infinitive: arrangements set for a fixed time, often paired with timetables
  and official itineraries ("The plane's due to arrive in half an hour").
- ***be (just) about to*** + *to*-infinitive: something that will happen very soon, in the immediate
  future ("they're about to leave").
- ***be (just) on the point of*** + gerund or noun: something in the very immediate future, even
  more imminent in register than *about to* ("the Chancellor is on the point of announcing his
  resignation").
- **A closed set of verbs + *to*-infinitive** that refer forward to a future action without using
  any dedicated future tense form at all: *plan, intend, are proposing, hope, have agreed, have
  promised* (e.g. "They plan/intend/hope/have agreed/have promised to build a new shopping centre").
  A note flags that *intend* and *propose* can alternatively take a gerund ("intend/propose
  building...").

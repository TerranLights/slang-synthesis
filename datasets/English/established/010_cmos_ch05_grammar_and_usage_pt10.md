## Grammar points

### 5.211 Words that are exclusively interjections

Some interjections are used only as such and never function as any other part of speech — *ouch*, *whew*, *ugh*, *psst*, and *oops* are Garner's examples (p. 732).

### 5.212 Parallel structure, generally

Parallel constructions — series of like sentence elements — are common in good writing. Compound structures may link words (win, lose, or draw), phrases (government of the people, by the people, for the people), dependent clauses (that all men are created equal; that they are endowed by their creator with certain inalienable rights; that among these are life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness), or sentences (I came; I saw; I conquered). Every element of a parallel series must be a functional match of the others (noun, verb, adjective, adverb, etc.) and serve the same grammatical function in the sentence. When linked items are not like items, the syntax breaks down — Garner's worked example: "The candidate is a former county judge, state senator, and served two terms as attorney general" is WRONG because the subject-verb-modifier pattern ("the candidate is a former...") fits the noun phrases "county judge" and "state senator" but not the third element, which is a separate predicate rather than a noun phrase; the fix is to make every item in the series a noun phrase ("...county judge, state senator, and two-term attorney general").

### 5.213 Prepositions and parallel structure

In a parallel series of prepositional phrases, repeat the preposition with every element unless all elements genuinely share the same preposition. A common error occurs when a writer lets two or more of the phrases share a single preposition but inserts a different one with another element — e.g., "I looked for my lost keys in the sock drawer, the laundry hamper, the restroom, and under the bed" is WRONG; correct either by repeating "in" for every element, or, if the series had not included "under the bed," the single shared preposition could have applied to all the objects.

### 5.214 Paired joining terms and parallel structure

Correlative conjunctions such as *either–or*, *neither–nor*, *both–and*, and *not only–but also*, and some adverb pairs such as *where–there*, *as–so*, and *if–then*, must join grammatically parallel sentence elements. A common error is putting the first correlative term in the wrong position: "I'd like to either go into business for myself or else to write freelance travel articles" is WRONG (should be "I'd like either to go into business... or else to write..."), and "Our guests not only ate all the turkey and dressing but both pumpkin pies, too" is WRONG because the verb "ate," placed after the first correlative, grammatically attaches to "all the turkey" but not to "both pies, too"; moving the correlative outside the two object phrases ("ate not only all the turkey and dressing but both pumpkin pies, too") makes it attach to both.

### 5.215 Auxiliary verbs and parallel structure

If an auxiliary verb appears before a series of verb phrases, it must grammatically apply to all of them. A common error is to include one phrase that actually requires a different auxiliary: "The proposed procedure would streamline the application process, speed up admission decisions, and has proved to save money when implemented by other schools" is WRONG, because the auxiliary "would" renders the nonsensical "would has proved" when parsed with the third element. Two fixes: either restate the correct auxiliary for the mismatched element ("...and would also save money"), or break the mismatched element out into its own sentence ("...speed up admission decisions. It has proved to save money when implemented by other schools") — which also avoids a tense shift (future to past) occurring in midsentence.

### 5.216 Grammar versus usage

The great mass of linguistic issues that writers and editors wrestle with don't really concern grammar at all — they concern usage: the collective habits of a language's native speakers. It is an arbitrary fact, but ultimately an important one, that *corollary* means one thing and *correlation* something else, and two words so similar in sound will inevitably be confounded by otherwise literate users of language. Some such confusions are relatively new; others, such as *lay* versus *lie* and *infer* versus *imply*, are much older.

### 5.217 Standard Written English

In any age, careful users of language make distinctions that careless users blur. The words someone uses and the way they go together tell something about that person's education and background. The form people write when they know they are being watched for correctness is commonly referred to as Standard Written English.

### 5.218 Dialect

Some writers and speakers deliberately use dialect, and to good effect — Will Rogers is Garner's example, quoted as having said "Liberty don't work near as good in practice as it does in speeches." Fiction writers often use dialect in dialogue, and may even put a narrator's voice in dialect. Such decisions fall outside the scope of this manual.

### 5.219 Focus on tradition

Within the short space of the glossary that follows, only the basics of Standard Written English can be covered. Because no language stands still — because the standards of good usage change, however slowly — no guide could ever be written to the satisfaction of all professional editors. The glossary's intent is to steer writers and editors toward unimpeachable uses of language — hence it takes a fairly traditional view of usage. For the writer or editor of most prose intended for a general audience, the goal is to stay within the mainstream of literate language as it currently stands.

### 5.220 Good usage versus common usage (introduction to the glossary)

Although Chicago recommends *Merriam-Webster's Collegiate Dictionary*, several other first-rate American desktop dictionaries exist, signaled by the imprints Merriam-Webster, Webster's New World, American Heritage, Oxford University Press, and Random House — but care and judgment are needed in consulting any dictionary. A word's mere presence in a dictionary does not mean it is in all respects fit for print: dictionary-makers merely describe how speakers of English use the language, and lexicographers generally disclaim any intent to guide writers and editors on the thorny points of English usage beyond collecting evidence of what others do. That is why, in publishing, a style or usage guide is generally needed in addition to a dictionary. While common usage can excuse many slipshod expressions, the standards of good usage make demands on writers and editors — even so, good usage should make only *reasonable* demands, without setting outlandishly high standards. The purpose of the glossary that follows (pp. 741ff.) is to set out the reasonable demands of good usage as it stands today.

## Grammar points

### 1. Subject and object pronouns
Subject pronouns mark the doer of the action, object pronouns the receiver (*She telephoned me*).
The subject/object distinction is purely positional/case-marked on the pronoun, not on nouns.
(p.1)

### 2. Reflexive pronouns
Used when subject and object refer to the same entity (*I cut myself*). (p.2)

### 3. Possessive adjectives
Each subject pronoun has a corresponding possessive adjective (*I → my*), always followed by its
noun. (p.2–3)

### 4. Possessive adjectives and pronouns
Possessive pronouns (*mine, yours...*) replace a possessive-adjective + noun phrase and are never
themselves followed by a noun (*This is mine*, not *This is mine book*). (p.3–4)

### 5. The possessive with 's
For people/animals: singular nouns and irregular plurals take *'s* (*Anne's bike*, *the children's
friends*); regular plurals ending in -s take a bare apostrophe (*the boys' mother*). *it's* (= "it
is") is distinguished from the possessive *its* (no apostrophe). (p.5)

### 6. Countable and uncountable nouns
Countable nouns can be individuated and pluralized (*a book, two cars*); uncountable nouns cannot
(*milk, water, flour*) and require a measure phrase to be counted. The source flags *bread, cheese,
butter, information, news, food, money* as uncountable — several of these (*information, news*) are
classic cross-linguistic "false countables" worth noting for comparative/conlang purposes, since
many languages count their equivalents freely. (p.6)

### 7. Singular and plural
Regular plural: add *-s*; words ending *ch/sh/x/s* add *-es*; words ending consonant+*y* change to
*-ies*. Irregular plurals (*men, women, children, people, teeth, feet*) are listed as a closed,
memorizable set. (p.7)

### 8. The indefinite article *a*
*a*/*an* marks one member of a countable-noun class; never used before plural or uncountable nouns.
(p.8)

### 9. The indefinite article *a* and the definite article *the*
*a* introduces one of several (first mention); *the* is used on second mention of the same
referent, or when only one referent exists (*the earth, the sun*). (p.9)

### 10. No article or *the* before names of places
No article before villages/towns/streets/cities/countries/continents (*She lives in Paris*); *the*
before seas, rivers, island/mountain groups, kingdoms, republics, deserts, and plural-form country
names (*the Atlantic Ocean, the Netherlands, the United Kingdom*). (p.10)

### 11. No article or definite article?
A closed list of institutional nouns (*school, college, university, home, work, church, bed,
hospital, prison, town*) take no article when referring to the institution's normal function, but
take *a*/*the* when referring to the physical building as a building (*The school is very old
now.* vs. *He doesn't like going to school.*). A second list (*cinema, theatre, disco, opera, post
office, bank*, shop names like *baker's, grocer's*) conventionally take *the* when naming the place
one visits in town, though *a* is sometimes substitutable when speaking of such a place
generically/for the first time (*There's a new disco in town*). (p.11–12)

### 12. Other words with *a, an, the* or no article
*a/an* for rates (cost, speed, frequency: *£1 a kilo, 80 kilometres an hour*); *the* with musical
instruments when played/listened to generically (*play the guitar*) but *a* when buying one as an
object; *the* with *listen to the radio* but no article with *watch television*; no article before
academic subjects, languages, sports, or meals. (p.13)

### 13. Summary (*a/an* vs. *the*)
*a/an* + singular noun = one of several, unspecified which; *the* + singular noun = a particular,
mutually known referent. (p.14)

### 14. *a, some, any*
*a* with singular countables; *some* in positive statements (both countable plural and uncountable)
and in offers; *any* parallels *some* but in negatives and questions; *no* means "not any" paired
with a positive verb form rather than a negated one. (p.15)

### 15. *something, anything; someone, anyone* (or *somebody, anybody*)
Mirrors the *some-/any-* distribution of §14 onto indefinite pronouns: *some-* compounds in
positive statements, *any-* compounds in negatives and questions. Exception: *some-* compounds are
used in questions that are offers/requests (*Would you like something to eat?*, *Can I have
something to drink?*), not *any-*. (p.16)

### 17. Counting the uncountables
Uncountable nouns are quantified either via a measure unit (*a kilo of, three litres of*), via the
container that holds them (*three bottles of lemonade*), or by dividing the mass into countable
pieces (*a piece of cake*). (p.19)

### 18–19. Comparatives
One-syllable adjectives add *-er* (with spelling rules: *e*-final adjectives add bare *-r*;
single-vowel-plus-consonant-final adjectives double the final consonant — *hot→hotter* — but not
when preceded by two vowels — *great→greater*). Adjectives of three+ syllables use periphrastic
*more*. Two-syllable adjectives (§19) default to *more* but take *-er* instead if they end in *-er,
-y,* or *-ow* (*clever→cleverer, happy→happier, narrow→narrower*; final *-y* changes to *-i-*).
Comparisons use *than*. Irregulars: *good→better, bad→worse*; *little→less, few→fewer*. A repeated
comparative (*hotter and hotter*) expresses ongoing change. (p.20–22)

### 21. *as ... as*
States equality (*as tall as*) or, negated, inequality (*not as exciting as*) between two things.
(p.24)

### 22. Superlatives
One-syllable adjectives add *-est*; two-syllable adjectives generally use *most* but take *-est*
instead for *-er/-y/-ow* endings (mirroring §19's comparative rule); three+ syllable adjectives use
*most*. Irregulars: *bad→worst, good→best, little→least*. Superlatives combine with *the ... in*,
*the ... of*, or just *the*. (p.24–25)

### 23. *too, enough*
*too* + adjective and *not* + adjective + *enough* both typically take a following *to*-infinitive,
and express (respectively) excess and insufficiency relative to some unstated goal (*It's too cold
to swim* vs. *It isn't warm enough to go to the beach*). (p.25–26)

### 24. Participial adjectives (*bored/boring*)
*-ing* participial adjectives describe the source/cause of a feeling (applied to a thing or, less
intuitively, a person who causes the feeling in others: *She was boring*); *-ed* participial
adjectives describe the experiencer of the feeling (*I was bored*). The source explicitly marks
*The lesson was bored* as ungrammatical — the *-ed* form cannot describe an inanimate cause.
(p.26–27)

### 25. Adverbs of manner
Regular formation: adjective + *-ly* (*quick→quickly*). Irregulars: *good→well*; *hard, fast, early,
late* are identical in adjective and adverb form; *loud* has two accepted adverb forms, *loud* and
*loudly*. (p.27)

### 26. Comparison of adverbs
Most adverbs (particularly *-ly* adverbs) use periphrastic *more/most*; one-syllable adverbs take
*-er/-est* like one-syllable adjectives. Irregular adverb comparison reuses the same suppletive
roots as the irregular adjective paradigm: *well→better→best*, *badly→worse→worst*,
*little→less→least*, *far→farther/further→farthest/furthest*. (p.28)

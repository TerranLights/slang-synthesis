## Grammar points

### Agreement of Subject and Verb (p. 181–183)

AMSCO states five situational rules for subject-verb agreement, each illustrated with its own
examples:
1. Words intervening between subject and verb (e.g. a prepositional phrase) do not change the
   subject's number — *A tree (with green leaves) stands...*, not *stand*.
2. Parenthetical-expression connectives (*with, together with, according to, including, as well as,
   plus, no less than*) do not create a compound subject and do not affect verb agreement — *The
   coach, as well as the players, is going...* (singular, agreeing with *coach* alone).
3. True compound subjects: subjects joined by *and* take a plural verb; subjects joined by *or*/*nor*
   (both singular) take a singular verb; and when *either...or*/*neither...nor* join subjects of
   different number, the verb agrees with the **nearer** subject (*Neither Stacy nor her brothers
   are...*; *Either these numbers or that address is...*).
4. *You* always takes a plural verb form regardless of whether it's singular or plural in reference
   ("you were," never "you was").
5. When the verb precedes the subject (inverted word order, or sentences opening with *there*/*here*),
   locate the true subject before choosing the verb form — *There were three raccoons...* (subject is
   *raccoons*, not the expletive *there*).

### Troublesome Verb Pairs (pp. 184–187)

Five specific intransitive/transitive or near-synonym verb pairs are worked through with full
principal-parts paradigms and example sentences: **lie/lay**, **sit/set**, **bring/take**,
**imply/infer**, **affect/effect** (vocabulary table above gives the distinguishing gloss for each).
AMSCO frames *affect/effect* as the single most error-prone pair in the set because both members
function as both a verb and a noun, with the noun sense of *affect* being a specialized, rare
psychology term for "emotional expression" distinct from *effect*'s ordinary "result" sense.

### Adjectives and Adverbs Confused (p. 189)

The dominant error pattern is substituting an adjective where an adverb is grammatically required to
modify a verb (*did well*, not *did good*; *speaks rapidly*, not *rapid*). AMSCO cross-references this
to its own earlier, more systematic Part One treatment of adjective/adverb formation (out of this
dispatch's non-redundant scope).

### Double Negatives (pp. 189–191)

A double negative results from using two negative words to express one negative idea (*I don't have
no homework* — two negatives, *don't* and *no*). AMSCO's prescribed fix is either (a) convert one
negative word to its positive-polarity counterpart (*nothing*→*anything*, *never*→*ever*, *no*→*any*),
or (b) remove one of the two negative markers entirely (drop the contraction, or drop the negative
quantifier) — but never fully reconstruct both at once, which produces a nonsensical double-positive
("I have any homework tonight").

### Other Modifier Errors (p. 191)

Three further stigmatized constructions are named: (1) adding a nonstandard *-s* to *anyway,
anywhere, everywhere, nowhere, somewhere*; (2) using *this here*/*that there* as demonstrative
adjectives; (3) double/stacked comparison — combining a periphrastic *more*/*most* with an
inflectional *-er*/*-est* on the same adjective (*more smarter*, *most prettiest*).

### Problems with Pronouns — Personal Pronouns, Subjects and Objects (pp. 193–195)

AMSCO reviews the subject/object pronoun pairs (*I/me, he/him, she/her, we/us, they/them*) and gives
four worked rules for where errors typically arise:
1. **Compound subjects/objects joined by *and*.** Test each pronoun alone by removing the other
   conjunct ("Laura competed" + "She competed" → "Laura and **she** competed," not "her").
2. **Courtesy ordering convention.** When naming oneself alongside another person in a compound
   subject or object, this source's style convention is to name the other person first ("Mollie and
   me," not "me and Mollie") — a social-register politeness norm layered on top of the grammatical
   case rule, not a case rule itself.
3. **Objects of prepositions**, including compound ones (*between Tom and me*; *with Maura and him*).
4. **Elliptical comparisons with an understood verb** (*Dita is already as tall as he [is]*) — the
   pronoun takes the subject form because it's the subject of an implied, unstated verb.
A further sub-rule covers **pronoun-antecedent number agreement** generally (a pronoun must match its
antecedent noun's number — *A wolf is gentle with **its** young* vs. *Wolves... **their** young*).

### Indefinite Pronouns (pp. 196–197)

AMSCO lists twelve indefinite pronouns treated as grammatically singular (*anybody, anyone, each,
either, everybody, everyone, neither, no one, nobody, one, somebody, someone*) — both the verb they
govern and any personal pronoun that takes one as its antecedent must be singular (*Everybody must
report to **his or her** adviser*, not *their*; explicitly flagged as incorrect in this source's
register despite being common in casual speech). For *either...or*/*neither...nor* constructions, the
governing number is that of the **nearer** antecedent (*Neither Norm nor his cousins buy **their**
groceries* — plural, agreeing with the nearer *cousins*). A separate short list — *several, many,
both, some, few* — is flagged as usually taking a **plural** verb, the opposite default from the
twelve above.

### Possessive Pronouns (pp. 197–199)

Two contrasting rules: (1) possessive forms of **personal** pronouns never take an apostrophe (*yours,
hers, theirs, ours, its* — not *her's, their's, our's, it's*); (2) possessive forms of **indefinite**
pronouns, by contrast, *do* take an apostrophe-s, exactly like a possessive noun (*somebody's,
everyone's, no one's*). The *it's*/*its* confusion specifically is treated at length, with a
substitution test: replace with "it is" — if the resulting sentence still makes sense, the apostrophe
form is correct; if not, use the plain possessive.

### Words Often Confused (p. 221)

Ten homophone/near-homophone pairs are drilled with corrective example sentences (full pairs listed in
the Vocabulary table above): *accept/except, than/then, too/to, quite/quiet, loose/lose,
whether/weather, your/you're, they're/there/their, where/wear*, plus the *its/it's* pair already
covered under Possessive Pronouns.

### Seven Helpful Spelling Rules (pp. 224–230)

AMSCO states seven general English spelling rules, each with its own short example set (condensed
here; this topic was also touched by CMOS ch.7, so only the rule statements — not the full drill sets
— are kept as a cross-check against that source's independent treatment):
1. **Ei/ie**: "i before e, except after c, or when sounded like *a* as in *neighbor* and *weigh*"
   (*believe, chief* vs. *ceiling, receive* vs. *weight, vein*); named exceptions: *foreigner,
   leisure, either, neither, height*.
2. **-ly on an l-final word**: keep the original *l* when adding *-ly* (*actually, beautifully,
   finally*).
3. **Final silent e before a vowel suffix**: drop the *e* (*admire→admirable, argue→arguing*).
4. **Final silent e before a consonant suffix**: keep the *e* (*amazement, hopeful, fortunately*);
   named exceptions: *acknowledgment, argument, awful, duly, judgment, ninth, truly, wholly*.
5. **Final y preceded by a consonant**: change *y* to *i* before a suffix *not* beginning with *i*
   (*apply→applied*, but *apply→applying* keeps the *y* since the suffix itself begins with *i*);
   *y* preceded by a **vowel** never changes (*play→player*).
6. **Doubling a final consonant, one-syllable words**: double the final consonant when it's preceded
   by a single vowel (*bat→batter, big→biggest, drop→dropping*); do not double when preceded by more
   than one vowel or by another consonant (*beat→beaten, sail→sailed, fool→foolish*).
7. **Doubling a final consonant, multi-syllable words**: the same doubling rule applies **only** when
   the accent falls on the word's last syllable (*commit→committed, refer→referred*); when the accent
   is elsewhere, the consonant is not doubled (*réfer→reference*, accent not on the final syllable).

### Contractions (p. 230)

A brief closing note: a contraction combines two words with one or more letters omitted and marked by
an apostrophe at the omission point; AMSCO warns against the two most common contraction-writing
errors — adding an extra letter, or misplacing the apostrophe within the word.

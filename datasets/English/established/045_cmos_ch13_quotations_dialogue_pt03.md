## Grammar points

### Ellipsis points for incomplete or omitted material (§§13.53–13.56)

Three spaced dots mark a quoted sentence deliberately left grammatically incomplete (p. 1659's example:
quoting only "When, in the course of human events . . ." from the Declaration of Independence). No
space separates a final ellipsis point from a following closing quotation mark.

For larger omissions, the convention shifts to a period plus three dots (effectively four points):
omitting one or more whole paragraphs is marked with four points at the end of the paragraph preceding
the cut, unless that paragraph itself ends mid-sentence (in which case three points suffice, per
§13.53). Omitting the first part of a paragraph within a quotation is marked by a paragraph indention
followed by three ellipsis points before the first quoted word — so a single quoted excerpt can use
ellipsis points at both the end of one paragraph and the start of the next (illustrated with an
excerpt from Alexander Pope's "Letter to a Noble Lord").

In poetry and verse drama, omitting the end of a line that nonetheless completes a grammatical sentence
uses four points; otherwise three. Omitting one or more entire lines within a quoted poem is instead
marked by a full line of widely spaced dots, roughly the length of the line above it (or of the missing
line itself, when that length is determinable) — distinct from the ordinary ellipsis-point convention
used for partial-line omissions. Worked examples from Edgar Allan Poe's "The Coliseum," Thomas Hardy's
"One We Knew," and Shakespeare's *Richard II* (2.1.40–50) illustrate the device.

Bracketed ellipses are recommended in two situations: when the quoted language itself makes liberal
use of unbracketed suspension points (common in some non-English typographic traditions), so that
brackets distinguish the editor's own omission marks from the source's native punctuation; or, in an
English context, as a deliberate authorial convention explained once in a note or preface rather than
annotated at every instance. The placement rules otherwise mirror the unbracketed convention, with one
exception: a period is placed before or after the bracketed ellipsis depending on its position relative
to the original sentence's own punctuation. Internally, the sequence is bracket-period-space-period-
space-period-bracket, with nonbreaking spaces needed only for the two internal gaps between periods (so
the three dots can't be broken across a line).

### Bracketed editorial devices (§§13.57–13.61)

Missing or illegible words in a reproduced document may be marked with ellipsis points alone (useful
when there are more than a few gaps, provided their stand-in function is explained in text or a note),
a bracketed comment/guess (optionally followed by a question mark, e.g. "[president's?]"), or both
together. A bracketed gloss sourced from elsewhere must itself be cited. A 2-em dash, sometimes paired
with an interpolated guess, serves the same missing-text function and likewise needs explanation and
consistent use (illustrated with a heavily redacted historical letter using repeated dashes for
excised names).

Bracketed clarifications — never parenthetical ones — may be inserted to resolve an ambiguity, supply a
missing word or letters, correct an error, or (in a translation) convey a sense the English otherwise
fails to capture; CMOS stresses these should be kept to a minimum so as not to distract readers (worked
examples include glossing "this thing" as "[the ghost of Hamlet's father]" in a Hamlet quotation, and
correcting a name within a quoted remark about Nehru: "[read: George Washington]").

*Sic*, meaning "so," "thus," "in this manner," is traditionally set in italics within brackets
immediately following a word misspelled or misused in the original — signaling that the error belongs
to the original author, not to the person transcribing the quotation. CMOS is explicit that this device
is for cases where the distinction matters to the reader (illustrated with a 19th-century quotation
misspelling "past" where "passed" was meant); it should *not* be deployed merely to flag unconventional
or archaic spelling, which is better handled with a general prefatory note — especially for a document
riddled with period-typical variant spellings, where repeated *sic*s would become unnecessary clutter.

An author who italicizes part of a quotation for emphasis must disclose the change with a formula such
as "italics mine," "italics added," "emphasis added," or "emphasis mine," placed in parentheses
following the quotation or in a source note. The reciprocal case — italics that were already present in
the original and the author wants that fact made explicit — uses "italics in the original" (or, named
to a specific author, e.g. "De Quincey's italics"). Whichever convention is adopted, CMOS calls for
consistency throughout a single work (§13.60, with examples from Gladstone and Thomas De Quincey).

Occasionally a bracketed or parenthetical interpolation itself needs to include material that was
already in quotation marks within the quoted passage. In that case the ordinary double/single
alternation rule (§13.28) is suspended, and the quotation marks inside the brackets may remain double
rather than converting to single (illustrated: "[a] double-headed calf ["two-headed calf" in an earlier
version] has greater value...").

### Citing sources in text (§§13.62–13.70)

When a quotation's source is not given in a footnote/endnote, it is usually placed in running text in
parentheses, most often immediately after the quotation, though it may precede the quotation if that
placement fits the surrounding prose more smoothly. This placement guidance applies equally under both
of Chicago's citation systems — notes-and-bibliography (ch. 14) and author-date (ch. 15).

A full citation (author, title, publication details, page) may be given entirely in the parenthetical,
useful in shorter works without a notes apparatus or bibliography (worked example: a Stephen Hawking
quotation cited in full as "(Stephen W. Hawking, *A Brief History of Time: From the Big Bang to Black
Holes* [New York: Bantam Books, 1988], 112)"). When a second quotation from the same source follows
closely with no intervening quotation from a different source, *ibid.* (set roman, not italic) may
stand in for the repeated citation — alone if the page is unchanged, or with a new page number
otherwise; CMOS cautions against overusing *ibid.*, reserving it for occasional repetition rather than
an extended discussion that quotes one source throughout (for which §13.65's method, a full citation in
a note at first mention followed by reduced in-text citations — e.g. act, scene, and line numbers for a
play, or page numbers alone for a novel — is preferred; a list of abbreviations may be supplied when
several editions of the same work are cited).

For punctuation placement: after a run-in quotation, the source is normally given after the closing
quotation mark, followed by whatever punctuation the surrounding sentence itself requires (comma,
semicolon, colon, dash, or final punctuation) — with one exception (§13.67): when the quotation itself
ends in a question mark or exclamation point, that punctuation is retained inside the closing quotation
mark, and a period is still added after the closing parenthesis of the source citation. For a block
quotation, the source parenthetical is given at the end, after the block's own final punctuation mark,
in the same type size as the quotation; no period is placed either before or after the closing
parenthesis.

For poetry extracts set as block quotations, the parenthetical citation is conventionally dropped to the
line below the quotation's last line — centered on the last letter of the quotation's longest line, or
set flush with the poem's left margin, with an optional extra line space — as long as the chosen
placement is applied consistently; shortened references to poetry (once the reader knows which work is
meant) follow the identical placement convention (worked example: a citation reading "(6.1.42)" below a
two-line extract from Spenser's *The Faerie Queene*).

### Foreign-language quotations (§§13.71–13.79)

A foreign-language quotation embedded in English prose is typeset like an English quotation — run in or
set off as a block according to length — and set in roman type, punctuated as in the original except
that quotation marks replace guillemets (or their non-French equivalents), with spacing around
punctuation adjusted to conform to the surrounding English context rather than the source language's
own typographic convention. If the original uses em dashes for dialogue, those are retained in a block
quotation but may be converted to ordinary quotation marks if only a phrase or single sentence is being
quoted.

Whether a translation is needed at all depends on the anticipated readership: a work addressed to
classicists might quote Latin or Greek untranslated, while a literary study of a specific author (e.g.
Goethe) might quote that author's own language with no translation furnished; for a general readership,
translations should be supplied.

Where both original and translation are needed, either order is acceptable (original-then-translation
in parentheses, or vice versa), and quotation marks need not be repeated around the parenthetical
member of the pair — though any quotation marks internal to that material must still be preserved. For
a long passage (more than one sentence), the closing punctuation of the original and of the translation
should remain visually distinct from each other rather than merged. When both are required but would
create too much visual clutter in the main text, the original-language text can instead be relegated to
a footnote/endnote, with the translation alone appearing (in quotation marks, not parentheses or
brackets) in the running text. A published English translation, when used, should be credited with its
own title, the translator's name, and full bibliographic details plus page number; an author's own
unpublished translation should be flagged with a stated convention such as "my translation," either
inline, in a note, or once in prefatory material covering the whole work ("Unless otherwise noted, all
translations are my own").

A published translation may be lightly adjusted (a word or two) if flagged with "translation modified"
and the specific change marked in square brackets, used sparingly; if a translation proves genuinely
unsuitable for the author's purpose, CMOS's guidance is to abandon it rather than patch it, and retranslate the passage from scratch. New translations furnished by the quoting author (as opposed to a quoted published one) may have their capitalization, punctuation, spelling, and idiom adjusted for consistency with the surrounding text — a more permissive editing allowance than that given to a quoted English-language source under the general rules of §13.7. Finally, CMOS states flatly that a passage originally published in English (or in any language) must never be retranslated from an intervening foreign-language version that happened to quote it — an author must track down and reproduce the genuine original-language passage, resorting to paraphrase only if that original cannot be located (§13.79's worked example: a German-language study quoting Blackstone's *Commentaries* in German must not be retranslated back into English — the real Blackstone passage must be found instead).

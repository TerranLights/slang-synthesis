## Grammar points

### Repetition and variation as the two governing stylistic principles

Orchard frames the entire chapter around two complementary principles that operate at every level
of *Beowulf*'s diction — sub-verbal (alliteration, assonance, rhyme), word, compound, phrase,
paragraph, and theme: **repetition** (echoing a sound/word/phrase/scene) and **variation** (restating
the same referent in different terms, "setting separate elements side by side for the purpose of
comparison or contrast") — together constituting what Fred Robinson termed *the appositive style*
(pp.58, 90–91). This repetition/variation duality is presented as the single organizing mechanism
behind phenomena as different as alliteration, synonym-stacking, formulaic phrase-substitution, and
the poem's large-scale thematic echoes.

### Compounding as the primary engine of poetic vocabulary expansion

Old English verse vocabulary is overwhelmingly **noun-based and compound-heavy**: in the poem's
opening 11 lines alone, Orchard counts 19 nouns (7 of them compounds) against only 10 (mostly bland)
finite verbs and 3 adjectives (p.60). Across the whole poem, Arthur Brodeur counted 903 distinct
noun-compounds versus 675 distinct simplexes, with 518 of the compounds unique to *Beowulf* and 578
occurring only once in the poem (p.60). Orchard demonstrates the productivity of this system
concretely: from just **five simplex/compounding elements meaning 'war'/'battle'** — *wig*, *hild*,
*guð*, *beadu* (all four attested as independent words, though the latter three only in verse), plus
*heaðo-* (attested only as a compounding prefix, never as an independent word) — the poet generates
**103 distinct noun/adjective compounds**, combining with 78 different final elements, of which
**75 are unattested anywhere else in the surviving Old English corpus** (pp.70–72). Representative
examples across the five elements: *beadohrægl* ('battle-garment'), *beadurof* ('battle-bold'),
*guðcyning* ('war-king'), *guðsearo* ('war-accoutrements'), *heaðogrim* ('battle-grim'),
*heaðowylm* ('battle-surge'), *hildedeor* ('battle-brave'), *hilderinc* ('battle-warrior'),
*wigsped* ('war-success'), *wiggeweorþad* ('war-distinguished'). This is a clean, quantified model
of **compounding as the dominant word-formation strategy for a poetic/elevated register**, directly
transferable to deriving a literary or formal register-tier within a conlang.

### Formulaic phrase-substitution systems

Beyond individual compounds, Orchard documents several distinct *systems* by which the poet
generates metrically-equivalent alliterative variants of the same underlying statement:

- **Element-reversal**: the same formula can appear with its two halves swapped to shift which
  element carries the alliteration, e.g. *mære þeoden* / *þeoden mære* ('famous prince'), *sunu
  Healfdenes* / *Healfdenes sunu* ('son of Healfdene'), *Beowulf leofa* / *leofa Beowulf* ('dear
  Beowulf') (p.87).
- **Synonym substitution within a fixed frame**: 'a band of men' can be *æþelinga gedriht*, *eorla
  gedriht*, *secga gedriht*, or *hæleþa gedryht* — four different head-nouns in the same genitive-
  plural + *gedriht* frame (p.88).
- **Metrically-equivalent substitution across parts of speech**: a single half-line template "X
  under clouds" (*under wolcnum*) is filled with a verb, noun, or adjective as needed — Scyld
  "grew under clouds" (*weox under wolcnum*), Grendel "strode under clouds" (*wod under wolcnum*),
  Hrothgar "ruled under clouds" (*weold under wolcnum*), night fell "dark under clouds" (*wan under
  wolcnum*), and water darkened "under clouds" (*wæter under wolcnum*) (p.88). Similarly, "most like
  X" varies between *fugle gelicost* ('bird'), *ligge gelicost* ('flame'), *style gelicost*
  ('steel'), and *ise gelicost* ('ice') (p.88).
- **Interlocking formulas**: two half-lines can share only their metrical shape (not their words),
  bridged by a third, externally-attested half-line — e.g. *weorðmyndum þah* ('throve with honours')
  and *beotwordum spræc* ('spoke with boasting words') are connected via the attested set
  *beotwordum spræc* / *hospwordum spræc* / *gylpwordum spæc* / *wurðmyndum spræc* found across
  *Beowulf*, *Juliana*, *The Battle of Maldon*, and *Exodus* (pp.88–89, citing Rand Hutcheson's
  terminology).

Orchard's overall statistic: formulaic repetition (of any of these kinds) touches roughly **40% of
the lines in *Beowulf***, though exact whole-line verbatim repetition accounts for **less than 1%**
of lines — far lower than comparable formulaic density claimed for Homer (p.86, citing William
Whallon's finding that *Beowulf* is "less than a third as formulaic" as the *Iliad*).

### Alliterative patterning: structural vs. ornamental, single vs. double vs. cross-alliteration

Old English verse's basic structural requirement is that the first stressed syllable of the b-line
alliterate with one or both stressed syllables of the a-line. Orchard distinguishes several
*additional*, non-obligatory alliterative effects the *Beowulf*-poet layers on top of this baseline
for emphasis: **double alliteration** (both stressed syllables of the a-line alliterate with the
b-line's first stress — found in almost half the poem's lines, but clustered irregularly to
highlight specific passages rather than spread evenly); **cross-alliteration** (identity of a
*secondary*, non-structural sound across the line, e.g. two different pronunciations of *g*,
guttural and palatal, both used in *Gar-Dena . . . geardagum*, line 1 — ~111 instances counted across
the poem); and **interlinear alliteration** (alliterative linkage extending across a line-boundary,
not just within one line) (pp.59–66). Orchard also catalogs **assonance**, **end-rhyme**, and
**paronomasia/wordplay** (punning on near-homophones, e.g. *leoman* 'lamps' / *leomum* 'branches';
*byrgean* 'to taste' / 'to bury'; *feorm* 'sustenance' / 'disposal of a corpse') as further
sub-verbal ornamental devices the poet deploys in combination, especially at structurally important
moments (openings, closings, climaxes) (pp.63, 76, 213).

### The envelope-pattern (ring-composition) and incremental repetition

Two named structural devices recur at multiple scales (half-line through whole-poem): the
**envelope-pattern** (a passage opens and closes with the same or closely related sound/word/phrase,
bracketing a self-contained unit — e.g. an entire 32-line arming-scene is bracketed by *Gyrede* . . .
*gegyred*, 'dressed himself' . . . 'dressed himself', lines 1442/1472) and **incremental repetition**
(the same word/phrase recurs at the *start* of successive units to build momentum — e.g. Grendel's
approach to Heorot is marked by a threefold *com* ('he came'), each time closer) (pp.78–85, 91–92).
Orchard notes the poem's own overall structure (beginning and ending with a funeral) is itself an
envelope-pattern operating at the largest possible scale (p.92).

### The *fitt*-division as a manuscript-native structural unit distinct from editorial chaptering

The Beowulf-manuscript itself marks 43 numbered sections ("fitts," from an Old English word related
to "fit" in the sense of a poem's canto/division) using Roman numerals, capitalization, and spacing
— a native sectioning device layered on top of (and sometimes cutting across) the poem's narrative
and speech-act boundaries. Fitt lengths vary wildly (36 to 142 lines), and several formulaic opening
patterns recur across fitts (10 of 43 begin with a variant of "X *maþelode*" ['X spoke formally'];
11 begin with the simple connective *Ða* ['Then']) (pp.93–97). This is a concrete example of a
**verse tradition's own internal, formally-marked discourse unit** that does not map one-to-one onto
either syntactic sentences or narrative scenes — relevant background for designing any discourse-
segmentation convention in a derived register.

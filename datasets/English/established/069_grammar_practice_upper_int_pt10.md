## Grammar points

### Sentence structure: introduction

**Simple sentences** are built from six core patterns, each diagrammed with its grammatical-function
labels (p.138): Subject + Verb; Subject + Verb + Complement; Subject + Verb + Direct Object; Subject
+ Verb + Indirect Object + Direct Object; and Subject + Verb + Object + Complement.

**Compound sentences** link two or more clauses of equal grammatical weight/importance, via: a
co-ordinating conjunction (*and, but, so, yet*), sometimes preceded by a comma; a semicolon or colon
alone; or a semicolon/colon followed by a connecting adverb (*however, nevertheless, meanwhile,
after all, finally*).

**Complex sentences** have one main clause plus one or more subordinate clauses/phrases, linked via:
relative clauses; noun clauses; adverbial clauses; or infinitive/participial constructions.

### Conjunctions

The book organizes its core conjunction inventory into five functional categories (p.139): to link,
to contrast, to express a reason or purpose, to express a consequence, and to make an additional
point (the full word lists are captured in the Vocabulary section above).

### Noun clauses

A noun clause (sometimes called a *that*-clause, though it doesn't always use *that*) can fill
several structural slots (p.140): as the **object** of a verb ("I can guarantee (that) it will be
there on time"); as the **subject** of a sentence, though the book notes the more natural
alternative construction *It* + *be/seem* + adjective + noun clause ("It is obvious (that) you don't
love him") or *The fact that...* is preferred over a bare noun-clause subject; after a specific set
of **adjectives**, divided into feelings-adjectives (*pleased, sorry, happy, glad, delighted, upset,
worried, concerned, surprised, grateful*) and certainty-adjectives (*sure, positive, convinced,
doubtful*); after a large set of **abstract nouns** (*belief, idea, fact, hope, promise, suggestion,
thought, reason, implication, something/anything*), usable as either subject or object; with
**question words**, where the noun clause itself never starts with *that* ("I will never understand
why he did that"); and with **if/whether (or not)**, usable as either subject or object.

### Participial constructions: present participial phrases

**After verbs (p.143):** present participles follow verbs of sensation (*see, hear, feel, smell,
listen, notice, watch* — "I saw her leaving the house"; note: these verbs can alternatively take the
bare infinitive to imply completeness of the action, cross-referencing §41); follow *catch, find,
leave* ("I caught him looking through my drawers"); and follow *go, come, spend time, waste time, be
busy* ("Would you like to come skiing with us?").

**In place of clauses** (formal, mainly written register; the participle must share its subject with
the main clause's verb), covering five distinct relationships (pp.143–144): **simultaneity** — two
actions by the same subject happening at the same time ("Holding his hand, she led him through the
crowd"); **sequence/immediate result** — the main action is a finite verb written first, the
secondary/resulting action becomes a participle written second ("I stayed at home and watched
television" → "I stayed at home watching television"); **time clauses** — present or perfect
participle after *after, before, since, while, on*, with the perfect participle usable to emphasize
completion before the main action ("After speaking/having spoken to you, I felt much better");
**reason clauses** — present participle in place of a clause using *as/since/because* for present
simple/continuous or past simple/continuous reasons, and perfect participle for present-perfect or
past-perfect reasons ("Having lived here all his life, he knows a lot about the town"); and
**concession clauses** — present participle after *although* and *while* ("While fully understanding
your problem, I can't allow you to break the rules"). A further, structurally distinct use replaces
a **defining subject relative clause** in the present simple or continuous with a bare present
participle ("The woman who lives/who is living next door" → "The woman living next door").

### Participial constructions: past participial phrases

The past participle can replace subject + passive verb, paralleling how the present participle
replaces subject + active verb (p.145). **Past participle in place of the passive:** across several
tense/mood contexts — simple passive ("She was surrounded by her admirers" → "surrounded by her
admirers"), passive + relative clause, concessive passive ("Although it was intended to be a nice
surprise..." → "Although intended to be a nice surprise..."), conditional passive ("If I am accepted
for the job..." → "If accepted for the job..."), and past-perfect passive ("The manuscript had been
lost for many years..." → "Lost for many years, the manuscript..."). **Being + past participle**
following *after, before, when, since, while, on, by* — used when the passive participle alone would
lose necessary time/causal information that *being* supplies ("Since the government was elected..."
→ "Since being elected...").

### Adverbial clauses of time, place, manner and reason

Adverbial clauses function like adverbs, answering *When? Where? How? Why?*, etc. (p.148). The book
tabulates its full conjunction inventory across nine semantic categories: Time, Place, Manner,
Comparison, Reason, Purpose, Result, Condition, and Contrast (the full word lists, minus items
already captured from the Conditionals chapter, are in the Vocabulary section above).

**Note:** the present simple is generally used to refer to the future within adverbial clauses of
time and condition ("When/If I come to London, I'll come and see you"), and the present perfect
substitutes for the future perfect in the same contexts ("When I've finished my dinner, I'll come
and help you").

### Defining relative clauses

A defining relative clause identifies which specific referent is meant — the main clause does not
make full sense without it, and no commas surround it (p.151). **Subject relative pronouns:** *who*
for people; *that* (more common) or *which* (more formal/scientific-register) for things. **Object
relative pronouns:** *who/that* for people, *that/which* for things — and critically, the object
relative pronoun is frequently **omitted entirely** in defining relative clauses ("That's the woman
I met at Tony's house"). **Relative pronoun as object of a preposition:** *that/which*, also
omissible. **Possessive relative pronoun:** *whose*, for both subject and object function within its
own clause, with no omission possible. The book's own summary table crosses
subject/object/possessive function against people/things: *who/that* (subject, people),
*that/which* (subject, things), *[no pronoun]/who/that* (object, people), *[no pronoun]/that/which*
(object, things), *whose* (possessive, both).

### Non-defining relative clauses

Unlike a defining clause, a non-defining relative clause does not identify its referent — the
referent is already known (typically because it's a proper name) — and merely adds extra
information; it is set off by commas (mid-sentence) or a comma + full stop (end of sentence) (p.154).
**Subject relative pronouns:** *who* (people) or *which* (things) — never *that* in this
construction. **Object relative pronouns:** *who* or the more formal *whom* for people (*whom* is
characteristic of formal written English, *who* of spoken English); *which* for things. Critically,
unlike in defining relative clauses, **the object relative pronoun can never be omitted** in a
non-defining clause.

**Relative pronoun as object of a preposition (p.155):** the preposition can either stay at the end
of the relative clause (less formal, increasingly common: "...who/whom we have all depended on") or
move before the relative pronoun (more formal written style: "...on whom we have all depended") —
and critically, when the preposition precedes the pronoun, only *whom* (never *who*) is grammatical
for people, while either *who* or *whom* is possible when the preposition stays at the clause's end.
**Possessive relative pronoun:** *whose*, for both subject and object function, with the same
end-vs.-before-the-pronoun preposition-placement options as the object case. The book's own summary
table: *who* (subject, people), *which* (subject, things), *whose* (subject, possession),
*who/whom* (object, people), *which* (object, things), *whose* (object, possession).

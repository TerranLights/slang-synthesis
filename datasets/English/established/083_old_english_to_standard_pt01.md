## Grammar points

### Word order in Old English

Declarative-clause word order in OE already commonly followed the same SP(C/O)(A) pattern as MnE
(Subject–Predicator–Complement/Object–Adverbial), but with far more flexibility than today. After a
linking/scene-setting adverb like *þa* ('then'), the order typically inverted to
Adverbial–Predicator–Subject (A P S), a pattern now lost in MnE declaratives. Verbs could also move to
clause-final position in subordinate clauses. Question-formation and negation both used orders now
obsolete: *Hwi forbead God eow þæt ge ne æton?* ('Why forbade God you that you not eat?' = 'Why did
God forbid you to eat?') shows the negative particle *ne* placed directly before the verb rather than
using a periphrastic *do*-construction. (pp.34–35, Section 2.7.1)

### Case, gender, and number marking on OE nouns, adjectives, and pronouns

OE marked grammatical function on nouns via four cases — nominative (subject), accusative (direct
object), dative (indirect object / object of many prepositions), and genitive (possession,
MnE's only surviving noun case, marked today by *'s*) — with adjectives and the definite article also
inflecting to agree, unlike MnE where only personal pronouns (other than *you*/*it*) still show case.
Within a prepositional phrase, OE nouns took either the accusative or dative case depending on which
preposition governed them. Gender in OE was **grammatical**, not **natural**: assigned by noun class
(masculine/feminine/neuter) independent of the referent's actual sex, unlike MnE's purely
sex-based *he*/*she*/*it* selection — e.g. feminine *sunne* 'sun', masculine *mona* 'moon', neuter
*wif* 'woman' and *cild* 'child'. OE genitive noun phrases placed the genitive **before** the noun it
modified (the reverse of a modern "noun's" construction reading left-to-right the same way, but
distinct from the common modern alternative "of the noun"), as seen throughout OE place names formed
from a personal name + geographic noun. (pp.35–36, Section 2.7.2)

### Weak vs. strong verb classes, and the loss of person/number verb agreement

MnE's regular verbs (forming past tense/participle with *-ed*, pronounced /t/, /d/, or /ɪd/) descend
from OE's **weak verbs**, which added a dental suffix (/t/ or /d/) to form both past tense and past
participle. MnE's irregular verbs (marked by an internal vowel change, with the participle sometimes
taking an *-en* suffix instead of *-ed*) descend from OE's much larger **strong verb** class, marked
by vowel gradation (ablaut) rather than a dental suffix. Separately from this tense-marking contrast,
OE verbs were also fully inflected for grammatical person and number in both present and past tense
(distinct suffixes for 1st/2nd/3rd person, singular vs. plural) — MnE has reduced this agreement
system down to a single surviving inflection, 3rd-person-singular present *-s*. (p.37, Section 2.7.3)

### Unstressed-vowel reduction as evidence for the loss of OE inflections

Comparing the same *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle* annal across its *Peterborough* and *Parker* manuscript
copies shows the vowel letters in unstressed inflectional endings (e.g. -*on* vs -*an* vs -*en* in
*nefdon*/*næfdan*, *feordodan*/*fyrdedon*) varying inconsistently between copies, even though both
derive from the same "regular" OE form. This spelling inconsistency is read as evidence that the
unstressed vowel sound itself had already reduced toward a single neutral vowel (what the book
identifies with modern unstressed /ə/, our commonest present-day vowel, lacking its own dedicated
letter) well before the Norman Conquest — i.e. inflectional *levelling* in pronunciation can precede,
and is only indirectly visible through, its later reflection in spelling. The West Saxon standard
written form (used for the Chronicle generally) otherwise masked regional/temporal pronunciation
differences that this kind of "misspelling" briefly makes visible. (p.38, Section 2.7.4)

### OE alliterative verse (The Battle of Brunanburh, The Phoenix)

OE poetry was composed in lines divided into two half-lines, each carrying two main stresses, with no
regular end-rhyme or fixed metre — instead, the two half-lines of a line were linked by
**alliteration**: two or three stressed lexical words (nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs — never
function words like pronouns or prepositions) in the line shared an initial sound. Manuscript poetry
was written out continuously like prose, not set in separate lines, with a mid-line dot marking the
half-line/line boundary. (p.27, Section 2.4, illustrated with Texts 15–16)

### Old Norse contact: cognate recognition and lexical/surname/place-name borrowing

Old Norse (ON), the language of the Danish and Norwegian Viking settlers, was close enough to OE that
the two were at least partly mutually comprehensible — an 11th-century Icelandic saga is cited
claiming "there was at that time the same tongue in England as in Norway and Denmark." Many OE/ON word
pairs are **cognate** (descended from the same Common Germanic source) rather than one being a
"loan" from the other, which can make it genuinely ambiguous whether a given MnE reflex ultimately
continues the OE or the ON form, or both merged. Confirmed, attributable ON contact effects on
English include: the Scandinavian **patronymic naming pattern** (*-son*/*-sunu*, generalized beyond
the Danelaw as the now-ordinary English surname pattern); **place-name suffixes** specific to former
Danelaw territory (*-by*, *-thorp(e)*, *-thwaite*, *-toft*), usable as a geographic proxy for mapping
the historical extent of Danish/Norwegian settlement; and a layer of everyday core vocabulary,
especially prominent in present-day northern and East Anglian dialects/accents. (pp.30–32, Sections
2.5–2.5.2)

### Two layers of Latin loanwords, distinguished by date and route of entry

OE contains two historically distinct strata of Latin-derived vocabulary, distinguishable because of
when and how they entered the language. The earlier, **pre-settlement** layer (household, food, and
trade words like *cheese*, *kitchen*, *street*, *wine*) was already present in the Continental West
Germanic ancestor of OE before the Angles/Saxons/Jutes migrated to Britain, via ordinary Roman-era
trade/military contact on the Continent — so it is **not** evidence of direct contact between the
Anglo-Saxon settlers and Romano-British Latin speakers (very little Latin was adopted from that
contact at all, since the Britons who had used Latin were largely displaced, not assimilated). The
later, **post-conversion** layer (religious, educational, and some household/plant/food words like
*abbot*, *school*, *candle*, *lily*) entered during the OE period itself, via the Church and
Latin-medium Christian scholarship following the 7th-century conversion to Christianity. A third, much
smaller set of Latin-derived place-name elements (*-caster*/*-chester* < *castra* 'camp') is the
exception that *does* trace to the Romano-British substrate directly. (pp.32–33, Sections 2.6–2.6.2)

### The OE alphabet: Roman adaptation plus three dedicated native letters

Christian monks adapted the Roman alphabet to write OE, which is why OE spelling gives unusually
direct evidence of OE pronunciation (Latin's own sound-to-letter correspondences were already a
continuous living tradition). Beyond the Roman letters, OE writing used three (the book counts a
further two, yogh and yogh's thorn/eth pairing, as variant/later) dedicated letters not borrowed from
Latin, each eventually replaced in later English: **ash** ⟨æ⟩ (a vowel, from Latin, survives in some
printed contexts even today, unlike the consonant letters below); **thorn** ⟨þ⟩ and **eth** ⟨ð⟩ (both
representing either /θ/ or /ð/ interchangeably — OE did not distinguish voiced/voiceless *th* in
spelling — later replaced by the digraph *th*); **wynn** ⟨ƿ⟩ (/w/, from its runic name, not normally
used in modern printings of OE, replaced first by ⟨uu⟩ then by ⟨w⟩); and **yogh** ⟨ȝ⟩ (representing
/g/, /j/, or /ɣ/ depending on phonetic environment, later replaced variously by ⟨g⟩, ⟨y⟩, or ⟨gh⟩ in
different reflexes). Modern printed editions of OE conventionally keep ⟨þ⟩/⟨ð⟩ but normalize ⟨ƿ⟩ to
⟨w⟩ and ⟨ȝ⟩ to ⟨g⟩; a macron over a vowel letter (e.g. ⟨ē⟩) marks vowel length only where needed for
clarity, mirroring the fact that OE itself did not consistently mark vowel length in writing.
(pp.20–22, Section 2.2.4)

### Runic writing preceding the Roman alphabet

The earliest English writing system was **runes**, originally devised for carving in wood or stone
rather than for manuscript use; surviving examples are rare, the most famous being the Northumbrian
runic inscriptions on the Ruthwell Cross (probably 8th century), part of the OE poem *The Dream of the
Rood* ("rood" < OE *rod* 'cross'). Manuscript writing in the Roman-derived alphabet only began once
monasteries were established (7th century onward), with the south of England (via Augustine's mission
from Rome) and the north (via Irish missionaries) converted to Christianity along somewhat different
routes, both eventually producing book-hand OE manuscripts. (pp.17–19, Section 2.2.1–2.2.2)

### The OE dialects and the "heptarchy"

Before unification, Anglo-Saxon England is conventionally called the **heptarchy** — seven kingdoms
(Northumbria, Mercia, East Anglia, Essex, Kent, Sussex, Wessex) frequently at war with one another —
but political division into seven kingdoms does not equate to seven distinct dialects. Manuscript
evidence instead supports **three or four** OE dialect groupings: **Northumbrian** and **Mercian**
(together called **Anglian**, descending from the West Germanic dialect of the Angles), **Kentish**
(from the Jutes), and **West Saxon** (from the Saxons). Because West Saxon became the de facto written
standard of the later OE period (10th–11th c.) and most surviving manuscripts are in it, "OE" as
conventionally described by scholars and textbooks is specifically **late West Saxon**, not a
dialect-neutral composite — any OE form cited without further qualification generalizes from this one
historical/regional stage. (pp.15–17, Section 2.1)

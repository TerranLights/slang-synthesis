## Grammar points

### 10.41 Days of the week

Where space is tight, days of the week are abbreviated using one of three parallel systems: a
traditional period-abbreviated form (Sun., Mon., Tues., Wed., Thurs., Fri., Sat.), a no-period form
common in computer code (Sun, Mon, Tue, Wed, Thu, Fri, Sat — note some forms shorten further, e.g.
"Tue" not "Tues"), or a single-letter form (Su, M, Tu, W, Th, F, Sa). In formal prose, Chicago
recommends the first (period-abbreviated) system.

### 10.42 Time of day

The lowercase, period-marked forms "a.m.," "m.," and "p.m." are Chicago's preferred style over small
capitals (with or without periods), as the most immediately intelligible form (cross-referenced to
§§9.38, 9.40, 10.4). "a.m." and "p.m." should not be paired with "morning," "afternoon," "evening,"
"night," or "o'clock" — i.e., don't write "10:30 a.m. in the morning." They pair naturally with
numeral times ("10:30 a.m.") as an alternative to a spelled-out time ("ten thirty in the morning").
When needed, time zones are given parenthetically after a time (e.g., "4:45 p.m. (CST)"), using the
standard four-zone, standard/daylight abbreviation set (GMT, EST/EDT, CST/CDT, MST/MDT, PST/PDT).

### 10.43 Scholarly abbreviations

Scholarly abbreviations and symbols are normally confined to bibliographic references, glossaries,
and other scholarly apparatus; if used in running text, they should be confined to parentheses or
notes. This section includes only the terms likely to be encountered in scholarly humanities/serious-
nonfiction work, not an exhaustive list. Where more than one abbreviation exists for a term (e.g.,
"L." and "Lat." for "Latin"), the choice depends on the writer's preference, context, readership, and
other factors — when in doubt, choose the longer form for clarity. Latin abbreviations are normally
set in roman type (not italic); Latin terms that are complete words in their own right (*ab*, *ad*,
*et*) take no periods even when abbreviating a longer Latin phrase around them. Cross-referenced to
§10.4, §7.53; for terms used more commonly in science and technology, see §10.52.

### 10.44 A few scholarly symbols

A small set of symbols (©, =, ¶, §) substitute for or supplement the equivalent written-out
abbreviations from §10.43 in bibliographic and scholarly-apparatus contexts.

### 10.45 Biblical abbreviations — additional resources

For authoritative guidance on biblical-citation areas this chapter doesn't cover, Chicago points
readers to *The SBL Handbook of Style* (cited in the book's own bibliography, §bibliog. 1.1); for
citing scriptural references in notes and bibliographies specifically, see §§14.252–55.

### 10.46 Books of the Bible in text and notes

In running text, books of the Bible are generally spelled out in full (e.g., "The opening chapters of
Ephesians constitute a sermon on love"; "Jeremiah, chapters 42–44, records the flight of the Jews to
Egypt"; "According to Genesis 1:27, God created man in his own image"). In parenthetical citations or
notes, or anywhere many such references accumulate in the text, abbreviated forms are appropriate
instead (illustrated with a concordance-style citation list: "Gen. 47:12, Ruth 4:15, Isa. 44:14, Acts
7:21, and 1 Tim. 4:6").

### 10.47 Biblical abbreviations and short forms — an overview

Two separate abbreviation systems exist for books of the Bible: traditional, longer abbreviations
(§10.48 ff.) and commonly used shorter forms, which take no periods at all (§§10.49, 10.50). The
listing in the following sections is alphabetical both for ease of reference and because the order of
biblical books varies slightly between the Jewish, Protestant, and Roman Catholic canons. Where a
book has more than one accepted name, the alternative is indicated with an equals sign, per the
convention set in §10.44 (e.g., "Song of Sol. = Sg" for "Song of Solomon" / "Song of Songs").

### 10.49 The Apocrypha

The books of the Apocrypha are accepted in Roman Catholic Bible versions but not in Jewish or
Protestant versions. Some Apocrypha books aren't complete standalone works but are continuations of
books already listed in §10.48's Old Testament table. Where no short-form abbreviation is given for an
Apocrypha book, the full written-out form should be used instead (i.e., not every book in this list
has a short form the way every Old/New Testament book does). Cross-referenced to §10.47.

### 10.51 Versions and sections of the Bible

Named versions, translations, and major textual sections of the Bible are usually abbreviated as
initialisms, especially when the version's name consists of more than one word (e.g., NRSV for "New
Revised Standard Version").

### 10.52 Miscellaneous technical abbreviations

This section's list of scientific/technical abbreviations, drawn largely from physics, biology, and
computing/technical writing, is explicitly not meant to be comprehensive; very common items like "PC"
and "DVD" are included despite also having wide general use, while many adjectival forms of these
abbreviations are omitted as predictable. Several formatting conventions govern the whole list: (1)
most unit abbreviations are identical to or compatible with International System of Units (SI) forms
(cross-referenced to §§10.54–62); (2) periods are omitted in any context for these technical forms
(contrast §10.4's general abbreviation-period rules); (3) capitalization mostly follows current usage
in *Merriam-Webster's Collegiate Dictionary* (cross-referenced to §bibliog. 3.1); (4) the first letter
of an abbreviation derived from a proper name is usually capitalized even though the spelled-out term
itself is lowercased (e.g., "A" for "ampere"), unless the abbreviation follows the word "degree" (as
in "degree[s] Celsius"), or is a prefix letter like *mega-* (M), *giga-* (G), *tera-* (T) — cross-
referenced to §10.57; (5) these abbreviations don't pluralize by adding "s" (e.g., "10 A," "5 ha," not
"10 As"/"5 has"); (6) with few exceptions (mainly abbreviations paired with a degree symbol), a space
is used between a numeral and the abbreviation that follows it (e.g., "22 m" but "36°C," no space) —
cross-referenced to §10.61. For units involving repeated quantities, see §9.17; for statistical
abbreviations, see §10.53; for traditional US units of measure, see §§10.67–71; see also §9.16.

### 10.53 Statistics

### 10.54 SI units (opening paragraph only — table and remainder fall outside this page range)

The International System of Units (French: *Système international d'unités*, internationally
abbreviated "SI") is described as an expanded version of the metric system, in general use among the
world's scientists and in many other fields. The chapter's own treatment covers only the basics; for
authoritative current guidelines, Chicago points readers to the Bureau International des Poids et
Mesures's own English/French brochure (published online), as well as to Ambler Thompson and Barry N.
Taylor's *Guide for the Use of the International System of Units* (§bibliog. 2.4) and *Scientific
Style and Format* (§bibliog. 1.1). The SI unit table itself (§§10.55 onward) begins immediately after
this paragraph but falls outside the assigned page range for this chunk.

## Grammar points

### 9.1 Overview and additional resources

The chapter's guidelines mainly target general works and humanities/social-science scholarship, where
numeric quantities appear relatively infrequently; the choice between spelling out and using numerals
depends on the number's size, whether it's exact or approximate, what kind of entity it represents,
and surrounding context — sometimes readability must override strict consistency. Scientific/technical
writing, where numerals dominate even more heavily, is only summarized here; *Scientific Style and
Format* is the recommended fuller reference (p. 1204).

### 9.2 Chicago's general rule — zero through one hundred

In nontechnical contexts, Chicago's default is to spell out whole numbers from zero through one
hundred, and certain round multiples of those numbers (e.g., "ninety-nine," "eight hundred"). Most of
the rest of the chapter addresses exceptions and special cases to this default (p. 1205).

### 9.3 An alternative rule — zero through nine

Many publications, particularly scientific or journalistic ones, instead spell out only single-digit
numbers (zero through nine) and use numerals for everything above nine. Most exceptions that apply to
the general rule (§9.2) also apply to this alternative (p. 1206).

### 9.4 Hundreds, thousands, and hundred thousands

Any whole number covered by §9.2, followed by *hundred*, *thousand*, or *hundred thousand*, is
normally spelled out (except in the sciences) regardless of whether the figure is exact or an
approximation. A precise, non-round figure (e.g., "47,122") is still given as a numeral even in an
otherwise spell-out-friendly sentence (p. 1207).

### 9.5 Number beginning a sentence

A number that opens a sentence is always spelled out; Chicago recommends recasting the sentence
instead when the spelled-out form would be awkward — most visibly with years (avoid spelling out
forms like "two thousand ten"; reword as "The year 2010...") and with back-to-back numbers of the same
category, where only the first should be spelled out or the sentence reworded entirely (p. 1208).

### 9.6 Ordinals

The same spell-out/numeral rule that governs cardinal numbers applies equally to ordinals. Chicago
prefers the *-nd*/*-rd*/*-th* suffix forms ("122nd," "123rd") over the bare *-d* form common in legal
style ("122d," "123d"), and ordinal letters should never be set as superscripts. In the fixed phrase
"nth degree," the algebraic *n* is italicized (p. 1209).

### 9.7 Consistency and flexibility

When several numbers occur in the same immediate context, all numbers within a given category should
be formatted consistently — if the rule forces a numeral for one member of the category, every number
in that category takes a numeral, even while a different category in the same sentence may still be
spelled out. As an explicit exception, numerals may replace a thickly clustered run of spelled-out
numbers purely to avoid an awkward pile-up of number-words (p. 1210).

### 9.8 Millions, billions, and so forth

Whole numbers combined with *million*, *billion*, and similar large-number words generally follow the
general rule (§9.2): spelled out if they'd otherwise qualify. Fractional quantities in the millions or
higher are rendered as a mixture of numerals and spelled-out words, and a round companion figure may
be shifted to numeral form purely for in-context consistency (§9.7) (p. 1211). Editorially important:
in some countries, including historically Great Britain, *billion* denotes what American usage calls a
trillion (a million million, not a thousand million), and *trillion* in that alternate system denotes
eighteen zeros rather than the American twelve; editors working with British or other European
writers' material may need to query these terms explicitly (p. 1211).

### 9.9 Powers of ten

Large round numbers, especially in scientific writing, may be expressed in scientific notation (powers
of ten); very small numbers are expressed with negative powers of ten in the same system (p. 1212).

### 9.10 "Mega-," "giga-," "tera-," and so forth

Under the International System of Units (SI; French *Système international d'unités*), very large
quantities can be indicated by the prefixes *mega-* (million), *giga-* (billion), and *tera-*
(trillion) attached to a unit of measure; very small quantities use *milli-* (thousandth), *micro-*
(millionth), and *nano-* (billionth). These are often abbreviated to a single letter in symbols (e.g.,
*M* for *mega-*, as in *MB* for megabytes). Astrophysical writing sometimes uses *Myr* (megayear) and
*Gyr* (gigayear) for spans of one million and one billion years, respectively (p. 1213).

### 9.11 Binary systems

Bases other than ten are common in computing, where numbers are usually expressed with powers-of-two
bases (binary, octal, hexadecimal). A non-decimal base is marked either by a preceding single-letter
abbreviation with no intervening space (*b* for binary, *o* for octal, *h* for hexadecimal) or by
expressing the base as a trailing subscript. Terms like *megabyte*, used as a binary multiple, are
technically only approximations (a megabyte in that older sense equaled 1,048,576 bytes); current SI
usage reassigns such prefixes to exact positive powers of ten (1,000,000 bytes), so when a genuine
binary multiple is meant, Chicago directs using the first two letters of the SI prefix plus "bi"
instead (*kibibyte*, *mebibyte*, *gibibyte*, etc.) (p. 1214).

### 9.12 Use of "dex"

*Dex* is sometimes used in scientific notation as shorthand for *decimal exponent* (p. 1215).

### 9.13 Physical quantities in general contexts

In nontechnical material, physical quantities (distances, lengths, speeds, temperatures, and the like)
are usually treated according to the general spell-out rule (§9.2). It is occasionally acceptable to
depart from that rule for certain types of quantities that are commonly or more conveniently expressed
as numerals (ratings, sizes, measurements paired with a unit word spelled in full) — provided the
departure is applied consistently to like quantities across a given work (p. 1216).

### 9.14 Simple fractions

Simple fractions are spelled out and hyphenated whether functioning as a noun, adjective, or adverb.
In the rare case where the individual parts of a quantity are being emphasized separately rather than
treated as a single fractional amount, the fraction is instead spelled open, without a hyphen
(p. 1217).

### 9.15 Whole numbers plus fractions

A quantity made of a whole number plus a simple fraction may be spelled out if it's short, but is
often better expressed in numerals — especially when a fraction symbol (e.g., ⅞, ¼, ½) is available to
render it compactly (p. 1218).

### 9.16 Abbreviations and symbols

When an abbreviation or symbol stands for the unit of measure, the accompanying quantity is always
expressed as a numeral; this is standard in mathematical, statistical, technical, or scientific
writing, where physical quantities and time units are both given as numerals and nearly always paired
with the unit's abbreviated form. Hyphens are never used between the numeral and the unit
abbreviation/symbol, even where that combination functions adjectivally. The symbols for prime (′) and
double prime (″) are explicitly distinct from the apostrophe and the straight (or curly) quotation
mark and should not be substituted for one another. A unit of measurement used *without* an
accompanying numeral should always be spelled out in full, never abbreviated — Chicago's example
contrasts spelled-out "kilojoules" with the disallowed abbreviation "kJ" in that context (p. 1219).

### 9.17 Units for repeated quantities

When an expression names two or more quantities of the same unit, the unit's abbreviation or symbol is
repeated only when it's closed up directly against the number; if the number and symbol are separated
by a space, the unit is not repeated for each quantity in the expression (p. 1220).

### 9.18 Percentages

Except when a percentage opens a sentence (in which case it must be spelled out, per §9.5),
percentages are usually given as numerals. In nontechnical prose the word *percent* is standard; in
scientific and statistical copy, the % symbol is more common, set with no space between the numeral
and the symbol. *Percent*, grammatically an adverb, is not interchangeable with the noun *percentage*
(p. 1221).

### 9.19 Decimal fractions and use of the zero

Large or complex fractions are expressed as numeric decimal fractions rather than spelled out. When a
quantity is less than 1.00, Chicago normally adds a leading zero before the decimal point as a
readability aid, particularly in scientific contexts and especially when quantities of 1.00 or greater
appear nearby for comparison. In contexts where a decimal quantity is conventionally capped at 1.00
(probabilities, correlation coefficients, and similar statistical measures), the leading zero is
conventionally omitted before the decimal point. By the same convention, the leading zero is routinely
dropped in baseball batting averages and firearm calibers — both treated as fixed domain idioms rather
than instances of the general statistical-copy rule (p. 1222).

### 9.20 Decimal places — European practice

Outside Great Britain, most European countries represent the decimal point with a comma rather than a
period, and use a thin, fixed (non-breaking, in electronic publications) space — not a comma — to
separate groups of three digits, on either side of the decimal marker. Canadian usage increasingly
follows SI-style digit grouping while retaining the decimal point (or, in French-language Canadian
contexts, the comma), separated by a thin space rather than a comma. US publications follow US
convention (comma-grouped digits, period decimal point) even when presenting figures sourced from
non-US material, except inside a direct quotation, where the source's own style is preserved
(p. 1223).

### 9.21 Words versus monetary symbols and numerals

Isolated references to amounts of money follow the same general principle as other numbers (§9.2):
spelled out for whole amounts of one hundred or less (with an alternative rule per §9.3 for
publications that follow it). A spelled-out monetary amount takes a singular verb, since it names a
single sum rather than a count of discrete units. Whole monetary amounts expressed numerically include
a zero and decimal point only when they appear in the same context as amounts that do include cents
(fractional) values; for larger monetary amounts generally, see §9.25 (outside this chunk's range)
(p. 1224).

**Morpheme note (earendel).** *earendel* = **ear-** (opaque reflex of PGmc *\*auzi-*/\*wandilaz*
element, not independently analyzable by an OE speaker) + **-end** (present-participle/agent
suffix, cf. *gangende* "going") + **-el** (agent-noun-forming suffix, cf. the *-el* of agent
nouns generally). The author argues this is very likely a **folk re-analysis**: the word's true
Proto-Germanic derivation (*\*auri-wandilaz*) was opaque to OE speakers, but its surface shape maps
cleanly onto a live, productive OE word-formation pattern (verb stem + *-end* + *-el* → "the one
who does X"), which would make *earendel* feel to a native speaker like "the one who rises/shines"
even though that's not its historical etymology. A useful worked case of synchronic
re-interpretation overriding historical morphology — directly relevant to how a synthesized conlang
might plausibly "misparse" an inherited form.

## Grammar points

### No native OE term for "prose" as a register opposed to "poetry"

The book's central linguistic finding: Old English had **no vernacular word that meant "prose" as
a default, unmarked register opposed to verse**, the way Modern English assumes a prose/poetry
binary. The period's terms for literary/important language — *spell*, *leoð*, *cwide*, *fitt*,
*gidd(a)* — overlap and co-occur rather than standing in opposition (e.g. the phrase *leoð
spellode*, literally "recited [verse-]speech," combines rather than contrasts the two terms).
Ælfric's own Latin grammar glosses Latin *prosa* only with Latin-register explanatory phrases
(*leden buton leoðcræfte* "plain Latin without poem-craft"), never with a vernacular equivalent —
suggesting Anglo-Saxon writers recognized *prosa* as a category belonging specifically to Latin
discourse, with no vernacular counterpart. The practical implication for this project: within Old
English, the functional register distinction isn't "verse vs. prose" but something closer to
**"patterned/important speech" vs. everyday unpatterned speech** — law, charm, homily, and
classical verse all employ poetic/formulaic devices to varying degrees, while ordinary
conversational speech (of which almost no direct record survives) was presumably the only truly
"unmarked" register.

### Word-pair typology (after Calvert Watkins, applied to OE data)

Old English deploys poetic word pairs (also called doublets, echoic pairs) pervasively across law,
homily, and verse. The book organizes them into two families:

- **Simple pairs** — designate a single concept directly, without indexing a third notion.
  - *Qualifiers* (intensify a concept): litotic form = argument + negated counter-argument (e.g.
    an attested Old Persian example glossed "true and not false"); non-litotic/redundant form =
    simple reinforcement (e.g. "safe and sound").
  - *Quantifiers* (totalize a concept by splitting it into two): argument + negated argument
    (e.g. an attested Latin example glossed "diseases seen and unseen," i.e. "all diseases").
- **Complex pairs** — index a third, higher concept via the combination.
  - *Kennings* (metaphoric, non-copulative): a genitival or compositional relation generates a
    substitute term. OE *folces hyrde* ("shepherd of the people" → "king," genitival); OE
    *hronrad* ("whale-road" → "sea," compound).
  - *Merisms* (metonymic, copulative): two nouns sharing semantic features name a higher,
    inclusive category by naming two of its parts. OE *word and weorc* ("words and works" → "all
    human activity"); the author's attested comparanda include "barley and spelt" (→ "all grains")
    and "bread and wine" (→ "all food and drink").

Separately, Wulfstan's legal/homiletic prose innovates a third simple-pair type not in Watkins's
original Indo-European inventory: **lexical negation pairs**, built by prefixing *un-* to a term
to generate its antonym (*riht*/*unriht* "right"/"unright," *laga*/*unlaga* "law"/"unlaw"). This
differs from litotic qualifier pairs in that the "negated" member is a real, independently
attested antonym rather than a constructed negation of the first term.

### Legal formula structure — the balanced equation

Æthelberht of Kent's law code (the first English law set down in writing, ca. 600s) is built from a
recurring statute template: **VALUED THING (*feoh* or *friþ*) + MULTIPLE (a number) + *gylde*** for
the oldest (church-property) statutes, generalizing to **Gif + CONDITION (usu. a 3rd-person
subjunctive/indicative verb clause) + COMPENSATION (usu. in *scillingas*, sometimes gold or other
movable wealth including women)** for the bulk of the code. The author argues this verbal structure
itself *enacts* the legal principle of equivalence/compensation — the statute's grammar offers no
predicate to negate or contest, functioning as "poetic shorthand" that forecloses argument the way
a modern paraphrase ("is to be compensated with...") does not. Alliteration, assonance, and *figura
etymologica* (pairing words that share a root, e.g. the verb and its cognate penalty-term) are
layered onto this base structure throughout the corpus, reinforcing rather than merely decorating
the statutes' authority.

### Figura etymologica as a legal/poetic device

A recurring OE device pairs two words that share an actual (or perceived) etymological root to
assert an a priori, natural-seeming link between them — e.g. *steopcild*/*astype* ("orphan"/
"orphaned," Blickling IX) and the *riht*/*unriht* antonym pair above. The device is distinct from
simple repetition: it specifically trades on the audience's sense that morphologically related
words must be "really" connected in meaning, lending legal and theological assertions an air of
natural/inevitable truth rather than arbitrary stipulation.

### Alliteration beyond identical onset — "slant"/feature-based alliteration

Classical OE alliteration requires identical initial sounds, but the author identifies a looser,
**feature-based** form of sound-patterning operating alongside it in both verse and homiletic
prose: pairs or sequences linked not by identical onsets but by shared phonetic *features* —
voicing, place of articulation, manner of articulation (e.g. the alveolar fricative pair /s/–/z/,
which differ only in voicing). This is offered as a general principle of how the "brain" registers
poetic sound-patterning beneath the threshold of strict rhyme/alliteration, and is used throughout
the close readings (e.g. the *Christ I earendel* passage, analyzed for bilabial-stop vs.
bilabial-nasal "near-alliteration" across consecutive lines) to identify poetic structuring in
passages that don't scan as strict classical verse.

### Ring composition and chiasmus

**Ring composition**: a passage's opening term/phrase is echoed at its close (bookending), often
with an internal repetition of the same term at the passage's structural center (the author's term:
"hub" or "fulcrum"). Example: Wulfstan's *Sermo Lupi ad Anglos* opens and closes on *worold*
("world"), with the word recurring a third time mid-passage, each occurrence shading its sense
differently (temporal "age" → "ever" → geographic "earth"). **Chiasmus**: a diagonal, "x-shaped"
trading-of-places between two parallel terms/structures (e.g. "Fair is foul, and foul is fair").
The author treats both as *descriptive* structural labels, not claims about conscious rhetorical
training — i.e. their presence in OE texts shouldn't be read as evidence of Anglo-Saxon access to
Classical rhetorical education, since both patterns recur cross-linguistically as products of
poetic language generally.

### Formulaic elegiac phrase: TAKE/HAVE + *to* + dative + (noun *and* noun)

A recurring OE formula for describing bereavement/loss: a finite form of *habban*/*niman*
("have"/"take") + *to* + a dative noun/pronoun + a coordinated pair of abstract nouns. Attested in
both Blickling IX ("*fengan þa to his gestreonum fremde and laðe*," "then strangers and enemies
took to his treasures") and "Deor" ("*hæfde him to gesiþþe sorge ond longaþ*," "he had for
companions sorrow and longing"). The formula's repetition across genres (homily, elegy) supports
reading it as a genuinely traditional, cross-text phrasal unit rather than a one-off coinage.

### Charm language tolerates — and depends on — semantic opacity

Unlike law or homily, charm language in OE routinely admits mixed codes (English/Latin/apparently
nonsense syllables, e.g. "Acre arcre arnem nona ærnem beoðor ærnem nidren acrun cunað ele harassan
fidine") and deliberately archaic/frozen forms. The author's account: a charm's efficacy is
understood to depend on the **form** of its language (repetition, sound-pattern density) rather
than on referential sense being transparently available to the speaker, so nonsense syllables and
untranslated Latin are not failures of transmission but acceptable, even expected, charm-register
features. This also means archaic grammatical/lexical forms persist disproportionately long in
charm language relative to ordinary speech, because the magical register resists the normal
pressure of language change (compared to the way Modern English liturgical registers retain
thee/thou forms and fixed commandment-phrasing).

### Metrical theory: classical constraints vs. "late Old English verse"

Sievers's Five Types of metrical half-lines remain the traditional baseline for classifying
classical OE verse (as in *Beowulf*). The book surveys two competing modern frameworks for scanning
*non-classical*-looking "rhythmic prose" (Ælfric's and Wulfstan's homiletic style) as verse of a
different, later kind rather than as unpatterned prose:

- **Bredehoft's "late Old English verse" model**: proposes that several classical constraints
  relaxed over time — loss of *resolution*, *secondary stress subordination*, and *restriction of
  anacrusis to certain line positions* — yielding a much larger inventory of acceptable metrical
  foot types in late-period verse, while retaining the basic two-foot-per-verse, two-verse-per-line
  structure. Under this model, passages from Ælfric's *Life of Cuthbert* and Wulfstan's *Sermo
  Lupi* scan as regular (if "late") verse.
- **Hanson & Kiparsky's Parametric Theory of Poetic Meter**: a cross-linguistic, generative
  framework in which any language's metrical verse optimizes its own lexicon along a small set of
  parameters (maximum metrical-position size, prominence type [strength/weight/stress/pitch],
  regulation of prominent vs. unprominent positions). Michael Getty applies this framework to
  classical OE meter specifically, modeling it as using a minimal prosodic foot as its maximum
  position size with stress as the prominence type (structurally comparable to Gerard Manley
  Hopkins's Sprung Rhythm, under the same framework).

Both frameworks are offered as tools that let homiletic "rhythmic prose" be read as metrically
regular — supporting the book's broader claim that the verse/prose boundary in OE is far less sharp
than conventional scholarship assumes.

### Register stratification case study: *folde* vs. *eorðe*

A clean, quantified example of OE lexical register-splitting directly relevant to usage-tier
tagging: *eorðe* is the ordinary, unmarked word for "earth," while its near-synonym **folde** is
the **marked, poetic-register** equivalent — attested 257 times in the corpus, almost entirely
confined to verse, homiletic prose, and glosses (never ordinary documentary prose). The cognate Old
Norse *fold* shows the same specialization (the name for "earth" attributed to the gods in the
Eddic *Alvíssmál*, vs. the ordinary term attributed to men) — suggesting the poetic/mundane split
between the cognate pair may be inherited rather than independently innovated in OE.

### Interlace and refrain as distinct structuring devices in "Deor"

"Deor" (Exeter Book) links its final, autobiographical strophe to its earlier legendary strophes
through **half-line correspondences** — matching syntactic/rhythmic patterns between specific
a-verses in the opening strophes and specific a-verses in the closing section — forming an
"interlace" pattern distinct from (though compatible with) ring composition. The poem's refrain,
*"þæs ofereode, þisses swa mæg"* ("that passed over, so may this"), is argued to be a structural pun:
its repeated grammatical ambiguity (what, exactly, has "passed over or been passed over"?) mirrors
the poem's actual interlace structure, so the refrain simultaneously comments on theme (endurance of
suffering) and gestures at the poem's own formal binding technique. By contrast, the similarly placed
refrain in "Wulf and Eadwacer" ("*ungelic is us*," "it is unlike/different for us") is read as
marking **disjunction/incompleteness** rather than binding — demonstrating that refrain is a flexible
structural device whose function (binding vs. separating) is text-specific, not an inherent property
of the form itself.

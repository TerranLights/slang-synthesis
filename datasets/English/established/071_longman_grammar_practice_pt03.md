## Grammar points

### 3.1 The indefinite article 'a/an' (1)

**3.1A General statements.** *A* precedes a consonant sound, *an* a vowel sound (based on
pronunciation, not spelling). A general statement about a class can use either *a/an* + singular or
the bare plural ("A cat is a domestic animal" = "Cats are domestic animals"), p.42.

**3.1B The 'plural' of a/an.** For general statements, the "plural of a/an" is the zero article; for
statements about quantity, it is *some*/*any*; for counting, it is a number, p.42.

**3.1C Describing people/things with a/an + noun.** Words that double as adjective or countable
noun (nationality, religion, politics terms) take *a/an* only in the noun use. Words that are always
countable nouns (never adjectives) for people/things always require *a/an*. Proper nouns can take
*a/an* to mean "a member of this family" or "a work by this creator," pp.43–43B.

### 3.2 The indefinite article 'a/an' (2)

**3.2A First mention.** *A/an* introduces a referent for the first time; subsequent references to
the same referent use *the*, p.44.

**3.2B A/an vs. one.** *A/an* and *one* + noun are not used together. *A/an* means "any one"; *one*
is used when counting is relevant. *One* also conventionally opens a narrative ("One day..."),
distinguishing it from *a* (any day) in that context. Whole numbers, fractions, money, and
weights/measures can take either *a/an* or *one*, p.44.

**3.2C A/an for rate expressions.** *A/an* expresses price-per-unit, distance-per-speed,
distance-per-fuel-unit, and frequency-per-time-unit (80p *a* kilo, 40km *an* hour, 30 miles *to the*
gallon, twice *a* day), p.45.

**3.2D A/an or zero with illnesses.** Illness vocabulary splits into four distinct article
behaviors — see the Vocabulary table rows for the four illness groups, p.45.

### 3.3 The definite article 'the' (1)

**3.3A Form and basic uses.** *The* never changes form for person/number; it marks a referent as
already known to the listener/reader, and combines with singular countable, plural countable, and
(always-singular) uncountable nouns alike, p.46.

**3.3B Articles with abbreviations.** Abbreviations are treated as ordinary nouns for article
purposes: *a/an* by the pronunciation of the first letter-sound; *the* for institution-type
initialisms that are spelled out letter by letter; zero for chemical-symbol abbreviations; acronyms
pronounced as a single word (like *radar*) behave like ordinary common nouns and can lose their
abbreviation status entirely, p.46.

**3.3C 'The' + nationality noun for people in general.** Nationality nouns fall into four
morphological groups, all taking *the* to mean "that nationality's people in general" — see the
Vocabulary table for the full groupings, p.47.

### 3.4 The definite article 'the' (2)

**3.4A 'The' for specifying.** A referent becomes specific/definite via back-reference to something
already mentioned, a following "noun + of" phrase, a following relative clause, or simply shared
context (e.g. "the postman," habitually understood even without prior mention). *The* is also used
generically for habitually-visited institution-type places (*the cinema, the bank*) and for broad
geographic categories (*the country, the mountains, the seaside*) even without a specific referent in
mind, and for the small set of "one of a kind" cosmological/geographic nouns, p.48.

**3.4B 'The' for unique (non-place-name) referents.** A specific list of semantic categories
(organizations, ships, documents, public bodies, book/film titles, climate, historical events,
official titles, political parties, press, beliefs, whole species) take *the* as unique referents —
but a parallel list of similar nouns in the very same categories are instead treated as bare proper
nouns with zero article, and which path a given noun takes must simply be learned, p.49.

### 3.5 The zero article (1)

**3.5A Basic uses of zero.** English uses no article at all — where many other languages would use a
definite-article equivalent — in front of (1) plural countable nouns in general statements, (2)
uncountable nouns (always grammatically singular) in general statements, and (3) most proper nouns.
By contrast, *the* appears when the same noun type has a specific, non-general referent, p.50.

**3.5B Zero article with names and titles.** No article is used before proper nouns generally. A
specific set of titles/address terms shows idiosyncratic behavior around abbreviation, standalone
use, and which kinship/profession terms can stand alone as a vocative — see the Vocabulary table,
p.51.

### 3.6 The zero article (2)

**3.6A Parts of day and meals.** Zero article for generic reference to times of day and to meals;
*the*/*a-an* reappears only when referring to one specific, described instance rather than the
generic concept, p.52.

**3.6B Zero article for institution-type nouns used in their 'purpose' sense.** A specific closed
list of nouns (*school, hospital, bed, church, class, college, prison, sea, town, university,
work*...) take zero article when referring to the noun's normal institutional purpose (being a
pupil, being a patient, sleeping); the same nouns take *the*/*a-an* when referring to the physical
place for an unrelated reason (visiting a school for a meeting), p.52.

**3.6C Zero or 'the' with place names.** The general rule is zero article for proper nouns including
place names (countries, states, cities, addresses, buildings, mountains, islands), but a specific set
of place-name-internal words (*bay, canal, channel, gulf, kingdom, ocean, republic, river, sea,
strait(s), union, united*) force *the*; a further, non-rule-governed set of countries, geographic
areas, historical references, oceans/rivers, mountain ranges, and deserts also idiosyncratically
require *the* and must be learned individually. Theatres, hotels, restaurants, and hospitals allow
either zero or *the* depending on the specific name, p.53.

## Grammar points

### Northumbrian dialect orthography vs. West Saxon convention (*Cædmon's Hymn*)

The author gives sustained attention (pp. 57–8) to the fact that *Cædmon's Hymn*, as preserved in an
early St Petersburg manuscript, is written in the **Northumbrian** dialect — "similar to what Hild and
Cædmon actually spoke" — whereas nearly everything else quoted in the book is printed in the later,
better-attested **West Saxon** dialect/orthography. He flags this as a genuine, visible textual
difference rather than a trivial editorial variant:

- The early Northumbrian scribe(s) had **not yet adopted eth ⟨ð⟩ or thorn ⟨þ⟩**, and used **⟨u⟩ as a
  consonant** (where later West Saxon scribes would write ⟨w⟩) — e.g. *uuldurfadur* for what a West
  Saxon scribe would later render with ⟨w⟩. Eth, thorn, and a third letter borrowed from the runic
  alphabet, **wynn ⟨ƿ⟩** (for the sound [w]), were added to the scribal toolkit only later, as scribes
  experimented with the best way to adapt the Latin alphabet to Old English sounds (p.58).
- **Scribes at this date did not capitalize divine epithets** (none of *Cædmon's Hymn*'s eight epithets
  for God is capitalized in the manuscript) — not a mark of disrespect, but a general period convention
  of not capitalizing names at all. The poem's only capital letter is its opening word, *Nu* ("Now"),
  and it carries only a single mark of punctuation at the very end. Modern editions routinely supply
  capitals and punctuation that the manuscript itself lacks (p.58).
- The sheer number of surviving copies of the *Hymn* (22 manuscripts, an unusually large number for an
  Old English poem) is itself treated as linguistic/textual evidence — most copies were appended, like
  this one, to Latin copies of Bede's *History*, suggesting the poem's short Christianized formulas were
  recognized as significant well beyond their immediate Northumbrian context.

### Runic Ruthwell Cross excerpt, also marked Northumbrian

A second dialect-tagged excerpt occurs in the discussion of *The Dream of the Rood*: a passage carved
in runic letters around the edge of the eighth-century Ruthwell Cross (Dumfriesshire) is introduced as
"transliterated from the runes below in their **Northumbrian dialect** and without punctuation" (p.78)
— *+ krist wæs on rodi / hweþræ þer fusæ fearran kwomu / æþþilæ til anum ic þæt al biheald* ("Christ
was on the cross; nevertheless eager noble ones came from afar to him in solitude. I beheld all
that."). This is independent confirmation, via a different physical artifact (stone rather than
manuscript), of the same regional/dialectal tagging pattern as *Cædmon's Hymn* above — both texts are
explicitly identified by the author as Northumbrian rather than the West Saxon norm used elsewhere in
the book.

### Formula-template Christianization: building new epithets on old pagan patterns

The author's close reading of *Cædmon's Hymn*'s epithets (p.58) describes a specific morphological/
formulaic process: pre-Christian oral-poetic formulas for praising a king or god (e.g. *mehtig*
"mighty" for a mortal king, *dryctin* "lord," *ricæs uard* "guardian of the kingdom") were adapted to
Christian use either by **adding** an element (*all-* prefixed to *mehtig* yields *all-mehtig*
"almighty") or by **substituting** a Christianizing term within the existing template (*eci* "eternal"
inserted before *dryctin* yields *eci dryctin* "eternal lord," and *hefen-* "heaven" inserted before
*-ricæs uard* yields *hefen-ricæs uard* "guardian of the heavenly kingdom"). The underlying template
grammar is preserved; only the slot-fillers change — a productive, named Old English poetic-formula
mechanism directly relevant to how registers and new coinages can be built on existing templates.

### *uton*: a grammaticalization case study (lexical verb → hortatory auxiliary)

Treated at length on p.81 (also referenced above in the vocabulary table): the hortatory particle
*uton* (also *wuton*) derives from the subjunctive form of a verb *witan* meaning "to go" — not the
much more common verb *witan* "to know." Its original, fully lexical meaning ("let's go") gradually
dissipated as speakers reanalyzed it syntactically; it came to function as a grammaticalized auxiliary
construed with the infinitive of a following clause, generalized from literal motion to hortatory mood
of any kind ("let us [verb]"). This is presented as a clean, well-evidenced instance of a lexical verb
undergoing grammaticalization into a function word within the attested Old English textual record,
with the original lexical verb (*witan* "to go") continuing to exist independently alongside its
grammaticalized offshoot.

### Alfred's explicit two-mode translation methodology

King Alfred's own stated translation procedure — *hwilum word be worde, hwilum andgit of andgite*,
"sometimes word for word, sometimes sense for sense" (proem to *The Consolation of Philosophy*, p.108)
— is an explicit, named articulation by a 9th-century translator of the literal-vs-idiomatic
translation distinction. The author treats Alfred's free adaptations (inserted Christian material,
omitted "opaque" passages, substituted personifications like *Mod* for Boethius) as consistent
evidence that Alfred leaned toward "sense for sense" in practice even while naming both modes as
available options (pp. 107–9). Directly relevant methodological precedent for any project translating/
adapting source material across languages or registers.

### Grammatical gender exploited for riddle misdirection

Chapter 5's riddle discussion (pp. 118–22) repeatedly returns to a specific grammar point: Old English
retains a three-way grammatical gender system (masculine/feminine/neuter) that does not reliably track
a referent's natural gender or animacy, unlike Modern English's largely natural-gender system (where
"he"/"she" is reserved mainly for persons and some animals, and "it" covers nearly everything else).
Riddle-poets exploit this mismatch for misdirection: a masculine noun like *sele* "hall" can take the
pronoun *he* even when the riddle's actual referent is a fish (whose "hall" is the water around it),
and an adjective like *wrætlic* ("marvel/wondrous"), whose own gender is ambiguous, can open a riddle
without yet committing to the grammatical gender of its eventual answer. The riddles' sustained use of
"he" for non-human referents is explicitly contrasted with Modern English, where such usage would
itself constitute (unwanted) personification.

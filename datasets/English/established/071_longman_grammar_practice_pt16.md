## Grammar points

### 14.1 Type 1 conditionals

**14.1A Type 1 conditionals, basic uses: 'If the weather clears, we'll go for a walk'.** Any present
tense (not just the simple present) can appear in the if-clause, and any future tense (not just plain
*will*) can appear in the main clause. Type 1 describes what will or won't probably happen — never
*will* inside the if-clause itself ("If the weather clears," not "*If the weather will clear*"),
p.206.

**14.1B 'If' + present + modal: 'If it's fine tomorrow, we may go for a swim'.** *Will* in the main
clause expresses certainty/near-certainty; another modal instead expresses what's merely possible,
necessary, or desirable, p.206.

**14.1C 'If + should' instead of 'if + present'.** Substituting *should* for the ordinary present in
the if-clause makes the condition sound more doubtful or more polite, without changing its basic
meaning, p.207.

**14.1D Imperative + 'and/or' + clause: 'Fail to pay and...'.** An imperative can substitute for an
if-clause to comment, threaten, or request: *and* replaces an affirmative if-clause, *or* replaces a
negative one, p.207.

### 14.2 Type 2 conditionals

**14.2A Type 2 conditionals, basic uses: 'If you went by train, you would...'.** Formed with *if* +
past (or *if* + *could*) + *would*; the past tense doesn't refer to past time. Type 2 can describe
something reasonably possible, phrased more tentatively than a Type 1 equivalent, or something totally
impossible, p.208.

**14.2B 'If + were/was' + 'would': 'If I were you, I would...'.** *Were* can replace *was* after *if*
in all persons (the mandative "subjunctive" [cross-referenced to 11.13B]) — more formal, and preferred
when expressing doubt or imagining a scenario, including in progressive forms. *If I were you*/*if I
were in your position* is the fixed formula for giving advice, extendable to third parties, p.208.

**14.2C 'If' + past + modal: 'If he knew the facts, he might...'.** *Would* in the main clause
expresses as much certainty as possible; another modal instead signals mere possibility or necessity,
p.209.

### 14.3 Type 3 conditionals

**14.3A Type 3 conditionals, basic uses: 'If you had gone by train,...'.** Formed with *if* + past
perfect (or *if* + *could have*) + *would have*, in simple or progressive forms, to express regret
about something that can now never happen, p.210.

**14.3B 'If I had been you'/'If I had been in your position'.** A fixed formula describing what the
speaker would have done in someone else's past position, p.210.

### 14.4 Mixed conditionals; 'unless/if...not', etc.

**14.4A Mixed tenses in conditional sentences.** The "three types" aren't a rigid template — real
usage freely mixes tenses across the if-clause and main clause depending on context (Type 1+3, Type
2+3, Type 3+2 combinations all occur naturally), p.212.

**14.4B 'If not' and 'unless'.** Broadly interchangeable for "except if," with *unless* being
'stronger' and typical in threats. They're *not* interchangeable when *if...not* doesn't mean "except
if" ("I'll be surprised if he doesn't win" ≠ "*unless he wins*"). *Unless* (never *if...not*) is also
used to introduce an afterthought qualifying a preceding negative statement, p.212.

**14.4C Conjunctions we can sometimes use in place of 'if'.** A set of conjunctions can introduce
conditionals with shifted nuances from plain *if* — see the Vocabulary table for the full set
(*assuming (that), even if, on (the) condition (that), provided/providing that, so long as, suppose,
supposing*), p.213.

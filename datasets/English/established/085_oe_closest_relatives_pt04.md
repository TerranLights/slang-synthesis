## Grammar points

### Old Frisian features explicitly compared to Old English (ch.7 "Grammar")

Robinson's Old Frisian grammar subsection repeatedly frames individual points as direct OE
comparisons (a structural choice not found in most of the book's other sister-language chapters):
**breaking** — "Like Old English, Old Frisian shows breaking," with *i*/*e* becoming *iu* before
*ch(t)*/*h(t)* and possibly before *rk* (OF *riuht* 'law' vs. OE *reht*; OF *tsyurka* 'church' vs. OS
*kirika*) (p.192). **PGmc *ai*/*au* reflexes** — "partly like those in Old English, and partly
different": OF's *ai* becomes *â* in some environments and *ê* in others (OF *lâre* 'teaching' vs. OE
*lār*; OF *mên* 'false' vs. OE *mān*), where OE's own *ai* reflex is uniform *ā* in all cases;
conversely OF's *au* becomes *â* under all circumstances (OF *râd* 'red' vs. OE *rēad*) — unlike OE,
whose own *au*-reflex (*ēa*) ch.10 already flagged as following "its own unique route," not a simple
shared Ingvaeonic *â* (p.192). **No sharpening** — shared with both Old Saxon and Old English (OF
*triûwe* 'true' vs. Goth. *triggws*) (p.192). **Rhotacism** — present in Old Frisian as in all
Germanic languages besides Gothic (p.192). **Nasal loss before a voiceless fricative** — shared with
Old Saxon and Old English (OF *fîf*, *ûs*, *kûth* vs. Goth. *fimf*, *unsis*, *kunþs*) (p.192).
**Assibilation** — "Like the West Saxon dialect of Old English," Old Frisian assibilates *k* and *g*
before *i*/*j*, but to a **different output**: OF *k*→[ts] and *g*→[dz] (OF *tsyurka* 'church'; OF
*lidsja* 'lie' vs. OS *liggian*), where OE's own assibilation instead produces [tʃ]/[dʒ] (the
"church"/"drudge" realizations already documented in `085_oe_closest_relatives_pt02.md`'s ch.6
extraction) — **the shared underlying process, not the identical surface output**, is what links the
two languages here (pp.192–93). ***r*-metathesis** — "like Old English," Old Frisian frequently shows
metathesis of CrV to CVr (OF *berna* 'burn' vs. OS *brennian*) — this is the second of the two
features (alongside assibilation) that ch.10 identifies as shared, among *all* the Germanic
languages, uniquely between Old English and Old Frisian (p.193). **Nominal/adjectival loss of the
PGmc masc. nom. sg. *-az* ending** — shared with Old Saxon and Old English (OF *wei* 'way' vs. Goth.
*wigs*; OF *gôd* 'good' vs. Goth. *gôþs*) (p.193). **3rd-person pronouns beginning with *h*-** — "As
in Old English," found throughout Old Frisian (OF *hi*/*him*/*hiu* vs. Goth. *is*/*imma*/*si*)
(p.193).

### "On Being a Younger Older Language": a worked methodology for not over-reading superficial similarity

This standalone essay — unusual among the book's individual-language chapters in being explicitly
methodological rather than descriptive — directly extends ch.10's "five reasons two languages can
share a feature" framework with concrete worked cases, using Old Frisian specifically because, as the
*youngest*-attested of the older Germanic languages, its late documentation makes the relevant
processes unusually easy to see in action. Robinson identifies **three types of change that can
obscure a language's true older affiliations**, each illustrated with a case that initially looks
like it links Old Frisian to Old Norse specifically, but in each case does not actually support an
ancient OF–ON connection:

1. **Late borrowing disguised as shared archaism.** Old Frisian's masc. *a*-stem nominative plural
   is a fairly consistent *-ar*/*-er* in the older eastern texts (*sîthar* 'colleagues', alongside
   *-a*; the younger western dialects show *-a*/*-an*/*-en* instead) — superficially resembling Old
   Norse's own plural type, and found nowhere else among the West Germanic languages (OE has *-as*,
   OS has *-os*). This looks like evidence for a special OF–ON connection. But the actual
   reconstructed history is more roundabout: pre-Old-Frisian originally had the same *-as* plural as
   OE/OS; that ending was eroded by regular sound change (parallel to Old Saxon's own, independently
   attested, *-os*→*-a*/*-e* erosion) to the point of merging the nominative/accusative plural with
   the genitive plural, creating a confusing case syncretism; a **new** suffix was then borrowed into
   Frisian from North Germanic, during the well-documented close Frisian–Scandinavian contact of the
   Viking period specifically, to *restore* the lost nominative/accusative-vs-genitive plural
   distinction. **The superficial OF/ON plural resemblance is thus a genuinely late borrowing, not a
   retained sign of ancient kinship** (pp.196).
2. **Convergent/parallel-analogy disguised as shared archaism.** Old Frisian's 2sg. preterite
   indicative of strong verbs ends in *-st* — found in no other older Germanic language. This is not
   an archaism at all, but a **transparent analogical extension** of the *-st* ending already native
   to the 2sg. *present* indicative: "we must assume that pre–Old Frisian looked more like Old
   English and Old Saxon, with their second person singular preterite indicative ending *-i*," and
   that this specific analogical leveling (present-tense *-st* spreading into the preterite) happened
   independently in Frisian, at a demonstrably late date — "both English and German carried out the
   same analogy, but not in the period we label 'old'" (i.e. standard German's own *-st* preterite
   ending reflects the identical analogical process, just completed later, outside the "Old" stage of
   either language) (p.196–97).
3. **"Drift" (independent parallel development from a shared inherited predisposition), not genetic
   closeness.** Old Frisian shows loss of final *-n* in endings (including the infinitive ending) —
   again resembling Old Norse specifically, since "no other Germanic language shows such a change in
   its oldest period." But this cannot be reliably dated in Frisian at all (the earliest surviving
   Frisian texts are already late and already show the change consistently applied throughout), and
   the underlying mechanism — a consistently strong Germanic root-syllable stress with a
   correspondingly weak ending-syllable stress, producing a long-term tendency toward eroding/
   dropping unstressed endings across the whole family (Modern English's total loss of the
   infinitive ending, and final-*n* loss in spoken Modern Dutch and many German dialects, are cited
   as parallel outcomes of the very same general predisposition) — is shared by essentially all
   Germanic languages, just realized at different times in different branches. Robinson's explicit
   caution: **"drift" being common to the whole family does not make it *evidentially worthless*** —
   the predisposition driving it is itself inherited — but it specifically cannot be used to argue
   for a *closer-than-average* relationship between any two languages that happen to show it at
   similar surface dates (pp.197).

Robinson's own closing judgment on assibilation (the feature OE/OF share that ch.10 treats as the
strongest evidence for real OE–OF closeness) explicitly revisits it through this same three-part lens:
since most scholars date OE/OF's shared assibilation to *after* the Anglo-Saxon departure for Britain
— i.e. after the two languages' speakers were no longer in direct contact — a literal shared
innovation (reason 5 in ch.10's taxonomy) is implausible; **the best remaining explanation is "drift"
specifically: an inherited common predisposition toward strongly palatalized [kʲ]/[gʲ] pronunciations
before *i*/*j*, which (unlike final-*n* loss) is demonstrably *not* shared by every Germanic language**
— making this particular case of drift still-meaningful evidence for a real, if ancient and indirect,
English–Frisian connection, in a way the Frisian–Norse cases above are not (pp.197–98). Robinson's
explicit closing moral, stated directly: *"many of the characteristics that scholars cite as proof
for early connection or divergence between the Germanic languages must thus be taken with a grain of
salt, or in some cases a whole shaker"* (p.198) — a direct methodological caution against over-trusting
any single shared feature, Ingvaeonic or otherwise, as proof of a close genetic relationship without
working through exactly this kind of reasoning first.

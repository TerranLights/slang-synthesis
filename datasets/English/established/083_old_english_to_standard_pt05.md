## Grammar points

### West Midlands as a single OE dialect split into two ME dialects by the Danelaw boundary

The OE dialect spoken by the Angles across the Midlands, North of England, and what is now southern
Scotland is collectively called **Anglian**, itself divisible into **Northumbrian** (north of the
Humber) and **Mercian** (south of the Humber, covering the Midlands). During the ME period, this
single Mercian dialect area developed in two different directions because only **half** of it had
been inside the Danelaw: the East Midlands (settled by Danish Vikings, so its ME absorbed substantial
ON influence) and the West Midlands (outside the Danelaw, so comparatively unaffected by ON) diverged
into two genuinely distinct ME dialects from one OE source. The book also introduces, at this point,
the concept of the **idiolect** — the more closely any dialect area's speech/writing is examined, the
more internal variation surfaces, down to the individual speaker/writer's own unique variety of the
language; "dialect" labels are themselves a simplification imposed for convenience. (p.82, Chapter 6
introduction)

### Full pronoun paradigm of the NW Midlands dialect (*Sir Gawain*)

The poem's two sampled stanzas together supply a near-complete pronoun paradigm for this dialect:
1st person singular subject *I*, object *me*; 2nd person singular subject *þou*, object *þe*; 3rd
person singular masculine subject *he*, object *hym*, genitive *his*; 3rd person singular feminine
subject *scho*, object/genitive *hit*/*his* (hit used in both object and genitive slots is explicitly
flagged as unusual); 3rd person singular neuter subject/object *hit*, genitive *þay*; plural forms
*we/us/our(e)* (1st), *ȝe/ȝow* (2nd, with a genitive gap in the sample), and 3rd plural subject
*þay*, object *hem*, genitive *hor*; the relative pronoun throughout is *þat*. (pp.84–85, Section
6.1.3)

### Noun and verb inflection tables for the NW Midlands dialect (*Sir Gawain*)

**Nouns:** the sampled plural nouns (*lokkeȝ*, *bones*, *fete*, *schonkes*, *renkkeȝ*, *resounȝ*) are
with one exception marked by the regular ⟨s⟩/⟨es⟩/⟨ȝ⟩ suffix — the direct descendant of the OE strong
masculine *-as* plural (see pt01's Section 2.7.2 discussion), now the regular MnE plural suffix. The
sole exception, *fete* 'feet', still retains the older OE vowel-change plural pattern rather than
taking the regular suffix. **Verbs**, by tense and person: present tense 1sg is unmarked (*bere*,
*craue*, *telle*, *ask*); 2sg takes *-eȝ* (*redeȝ*, *hatteȝ*, *hopeȝ*, *deleȝ*); 3sg also takes
*-eȝ* (*discoureȝ*, *boȝeȝ*, *cachcheȝ*, *steppeȝ*, *strydeȝ*, *haldeȝ*); plural present takes *-en*
(*fallen*; *helden* = 'turn'; *ȝelden*). Past tense 1sg is again unmarked for strong verbs
(*lakked*, *seȝ* = 'saw', *cheued* = 'got'); 2sg likewise (*gef* = 'gave', *fayled*,
*kyssedes* = 'kissed'); 3sg shows both strong verbs with internal vowel change and no suffix (*bot*,
*fel*, *ȝet*, *schrank*, *start*) and weak verbs with a dental suffix (*blykked*, *faltered*,
*foyned*, *gripped*, *roled*, *schyndered*, *bledde*, *brayde*, *layd*, *laȝt*, *lyft*, *raȝt*,
*scade*, *sette*); past plural takes *-en* (*stoden* = 'stood', *maden* = 'made'). Non-finite forms:
infinitive is unmarked (*schewe*, *tak*, *gif*, *prayse*); imperative unmarked (*gif*, *kysse*,
*lepe*, *lach* = 'seize'); present participle takes *-ande* (*sykande* = 'sighing', *wreȝande* =
'denouncing'); past participle is varied/unmarked in the sample (*lut*, *ayled*, *payed*, *hunted*,
*slayn*). The author's overall assessment: several of these inflections are already close enough to
MnE that the loss of OE inflection is substantially advanced in this dialect too, though the poem
remains genuinely hard for a modern reader — the real barrier is its large stock of ON-, OF-, and
dialect-specific vocabulary that never survived into Standard English, not its grammar, which is
comparatively close to MnE. (p.86, Section 6.1.3)

### "Levelling" vs. "regularisation," and why the North changed faster than the South

The book formally distinguishes two related but separate processes within ME's broad simplification
of the OE inflectional system: **levelling** is the loss of most OE inflections outright, while
**regularisation** is the separate process by which the smaller set of inflections that *do* survive
becomes less varied/more uniform in form across different word-classes and dialects. Both processes
had progressed further in the North of England, and in those Midlands dialects geographically closer
to the North, than in the South by any given date — attributed not only to the direct lexical
influence of Scandinavian settlement (ON vocabulary flooding in), but also to a more general
simplification effect sometimes produced when speakers of two related-but-different languages (OE and
ON) communicate regularly, comparable to the kind of grammatical simplification seen when a pidgin
language begins to form. (p.86, "Inflections in ME")

### OE alliterative verse tradition continuing unbroken into West Midlands ME, plus "bob and wheel"

*Sir Gawain and the Green Knight* is offered as direct evidence that the oral/literary tradition of OE
alliterative verse (see pt01's Section 2.4 discussion of *The Battle of Brunanburh*) continued
genuinely unbroken through to the late 14th century in this dialect area: each line still divides into
two halves at a mid-line caesura, usually with four stressed syllables (two per half-line), three of
which typically alliterate (though this varies). The poem adds a further structural refinement not
present in OE verse: each 101-line stanza closes with a short line (the "**bob**") followed by four
rhyming lines (the "**wheel**," rhyming *ababa*-style across the bob and the four lines together) —
a hybrid of the old unrhymed alliterative tradition with newer end-rhyme technique. (p.84, Section
6.1.2)

### *Piers Plowman*'s textual history and the limits of "the text" as linguistic evidence

*Piers Plowman* survives in more than 50 manuscripts and was continually revised by its author,
William Langland, across roughly two decades (the 1360s–1380s), producing three successive versions
conventionally labelled the **A**, **B**, and **C** texts (the C-text probably representing Langland's
final revision, c. the 1380s). Because of this combination of extensive authorial revision *and*
ordinary scribal copying variation across 50-plus manuscripts, the book flags a real methodological
caution: any printed modern edition is necessarily *edited* — based on one base manuscript but
filling gaps/resolving nonsense readings from other manuscript witnesses, expanding abbreviations, and
adding modern punctuation not present in any manuscript — so **linguistic conclusions drawn from a
modern edition should properly be treated as tentative until cross-checked against the manuscript
facsimile itself** (illustrated directly by giving both the Cotton MS Vespasian facsimile/transcription
and the modern edited text of the same passage, Texts 45–46, side by side for comparison). (pp.88–89,
92–93, Sections 6.2–6.2.1 and the Text 45/46 facsimile comparison) |

### Vocabulary-source scarcity as (weak) dialectal evidence: the ON-poor SW/West Midlands

Commentary on *Piers Plowman*'s Text 42 extract notes it contains relatively few French-derived words
and even fewer ON-derived words — consistent with the South and West of England never having been
settled by Danish or Norwegian Vikings, unlike the East Midlands and North. The author is explicit
that this single short sample cannot by itself support a strong conclusion (a larger, more
systematic corpus would be needed), but treats it as suggestive, consistent confirmatory evidence of
a solid OE-derived vocabulary **core** underlying even quite late ME literary writing in a
non-Danelaw dialect area. (p.90, Section 6.2.1)

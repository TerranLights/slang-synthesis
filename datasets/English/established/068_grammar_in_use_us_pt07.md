## Grammar points

### Unit 96 — The infinitive after adjectives

A to-infinitive can follow difficult/easy/impossible/hard/safe/interesting and similar adjectives,
with the logical object of the infinitive promoted to the sentence's grammatical subject and not
separately restated ("He is difficult to understand," not "to understand him"); this also combines
with a preceding noun ("a difficult question to answer," "an exciting game to watch"). The infinitive
regularly follows ordinal/ordering determiners (the first/second/third, the next, the last: "Who was
the first person to reach the South Pole?"). A closed set of feeling-reaction adjectives (sorry,
surprised, happy, pleased, glad, disappointed, sad, relieved, amazed, astonished, delighted) takes the
infinitive to express why/at what the feeling arose ("I was sorry to hear that..."). Separately, a
closed set of character-judgment adjectives (nice, kind, mean, stupid, silly, polite, clever, careless,
generous, foolish) feeds the fixed "(it was) [adjective] of someone to do" evaluative construction
("It was careless of Jack to leave the door unlocked"). p. 192.

### Unit 97 — Comparison (1) — cheaper, more expensive, etc.

Comparatives pair with "than." Short (mostly one-syllable, plus some two-syllable, especially -y-final)
adjectives/adverbs take -er (cheap→cheaper, funny→funnier); other two-syllable and longer words, and
all -ly adverbs, take "more..." instead (more modern, more carefully). "More often" is the comparative
of frequency; "earlier" (not "more early") is a fixed exception within the "more" category. A set of
intensifiers (a (little) bit, a little, much, a lot, far = a lot) scale the comparative's degree up or
down ("much cheaper," "far more serious"). p. 194.

### Unit 98 — Comparison (2)

Irregular/suppletive comparatives: good/well→better, bad/badly→worse, far→further (or farther), with
more/less as the comparatives of much/many and little, respectively; "further" (not "farther") also
means "more/additional" in a non-comparative sense. "Older" is the regular comparative of "old";
"elder" is available only for comparing family members and only before a noun, never predicatively
("My brother is older than me," never "elder than me"). Doubled comparatives (harder and harder, more
and more difficult) mark continuous change. The fixed correlative construction "the + comparative, the
+ comparative" ("The sooner the better"; "The more electricity you use, the higher your bill will be")
marks one scale's dependency on another. p. 196.

### Unit 99 — Comparison (3) — as...as/than

"Not as...as" (or the negative-only alternative "not so...as") marks one-way inequality ("Sue isn't as
old as she looks"). Plain "as...as" (not "so...as") marks equality in positive statements and questions
("as fast as I could," "as soon as possible"); multiplicative versions ("twice as...as," "three times
as...as") extend the pattern. "The same as" is the fixed equivalence construction ("the same like" is
explicitly flagged as incorrect). After "than" or "as" with no following verb, object-form pronouns
(me/him/her/them/us) are more usual than subject pronoun + verb ("You are taller than me," though
"than I am" is also correct). p. 198.

### Unit 100 — Superlatives — the longest, the most enjoyable, etc.

Superlatives follow the same -est/most... length-based split as comparatives (Unit 97), and normally
take "the." Irregular superlatives: best, worst (of good/well, bad/badly). "Oldest" is the regular
superlative of "old"; "eldest" is restricted to family-member comparisons, parallel to the Unit 98b
elder/older split. Superlatives take "in" before a place/organization/group ("the longest river in the
world," "the best... in the class") but "of" before a time period ("the happiest day of my life").
Superlative statements are commonly followed by a present-perfect relative clause ("the best movie
you've ever seen," cross-referenced to Unit 14a). A separate, non-superlative use of bare "most"
directly before an adjective (without "the") simply intensifies, meaning "very" ("most interesting" =
very interesting, a more formal register usage). p. 200.

### Unit 101 — Word order (1) — verb + object; place and time

The verb and its direct object are not normally separated by other material ("I like children very
much," not "I like very much children"). When a sentence has both a place phrase and a time phrase
after the verb, place is ordered before time ("Tom walks to work every morning," not the reverse); the
time phrase alone can often be moved to the front of the sentence instead ("Every morning Tom walks to
work"), but "early" and "late" specifically cannot be fronted this way. p. 202.

### Unit 102 — Word order (2) — adverbs with the verb

A class of adverbs (always, also, probably, often, hardly ever, rarely, almost, never, usually,
definitely) occupies a fixed mid-sentence position rather than the sentence edges: immediately before
a one-word main verb ("Tom always goes"), immediately after forms of "be" ("We were also hungry"), and
immediately after the first word of a multi-word verb phrase ("My parents have always lived," "Ann
doesn't usually smoke"). "Probably" specifically precedes a negative rather than following it ("I
probably won't see you," not "I won't probably see you"). "All" and "both" occupy this same mid-
sentence position when used adverbially ("We all felt sick," "Jack and Tom have both applied"). p. 204.

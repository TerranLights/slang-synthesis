## Grammar points

### 1.1 Sentence word order and forms

**1.1A Basic word order.** Standard English order is subject–verb–object, with adverbials
(How?/Where?/When?) usually following the verb/object, though a time reference may front the
sentence ("*Yesterday* I bought a hat"), p.2.

**1.1B Forms of a sentence.** A sentence is a statement, question, command, or exclamation, each
with its own end punctuation; when quoted speech itself ends in a question/exclamation mark, other
surrounding punctuation goes outside the closing quotation mark, p.3.

### 1.2 Verbs with and without objects

**1.2A What is a complete sentence.** A simple sentence needs at minimum a subject and a verb
("*My car was made in Germany*" is complete; "*Made in Germany*" is not, lacking a subject).
Imperatives have a "hidden" subject: "Open the door" = "You open the door," p.4.

**1.2B Transitive vs. intransitive verbs.** Transitive verbs always require an object; intransitive
verbs never take one; a third group can go either way depending on the sentence, p.4.

**1.2C Linking verbs and complements.** Verbs like *be* and *seem* take no object but a complement
that "completes" the sentence by describing the subject — which may be an adjective, a noun, an
adjective+noun, a pronoun, an adverb of place/time, or a prepositional phrase, p.5.

### 1.3 Direct and indirect objects

**1.3A Two-object verbs.** Verbs like *give* and *buy* can take two objects (indirect + direct);
the choice of linking preposition (*to* vs. *for*) depends on the specific verb, and *it*/*them* as
direct object must always precede the indirect-object noun ("Give it to John," not "*Give John it*"),
p.6.

**1.3B The "explain" group.** A distinct set of verbs (*explain, confess, admit, announce...*) can
never take a bare indirect object immediately after the verb — they require "to" even where *give*-
type verbs wouldn't, pp.6–7.

**1.3C Two meanings of "for."** With *to*-group verbs, *for* substituting for *to* means "instead
of" (doing someone's task for them); with *for*-group verbs, *for* is ambiguous between "instead of"
and "for your/their benefit," resolved only by context, p.7.

### 1.4 The compound sentence

Conjunctions join independent clauses to express addition, continuation, contrast, choice, result,
or reason. A comma typically precedes *but/yet/for/so* but not *and*. When the subject is shared
across clauses it is usually dropped — except it must be repeated after *so* and always repeated
after *for* (which, being more literary/written, also cannot begin a sentence), p.8.

### 1.5 Noun clauses

**1.5A Complex sentences.** A complex sentence has one main clause (can stand alone) and one or more
subordinate clauses (cannot), joined by conjunctions, p.10.

**1.5B Noun clauses from statements.** A noun clause does the work of a noun phrase, typically
introduced by *that* (frequently omissible) after certain adjectives, nouns, or "reporting" verbs
like *say/tell/think/know*, p.10.

**1.5C Noun clauses from questions.** A yes/no question becomes a noun clause with *if* or *whether*
after reporting verbs (*ask, tell, want to know*); a wh-question becomes a noun clause with the
wh-word but reverts to statement word order and drops the question mark ("Tell me *when you signed*
the contract," not "*when did you sign*"), p.11.

### 1.6 Relative pronouns and clauses

**1.6A Subject relative pronouns.** *Who/that* for people, *which/that* for animals/things, as
subject of the relative clause; these pronouns never change for gender/number and can never be
omitted when functioning as subject, p.12.

**1.6B Object relative pronouns.** *Who(m)/that* (people) or *which/that* (things/animals) as object
of the relative clause; *who* is far more common than *whom* in speech; object relative pronouns are
usually omitted entirely ("He's the man I met"), p.12.

**1.6C Relative pronoun as object of a preposition.** The very formal pattern fronts the preposition
("the person to whom I wrote"); the everyday pattern strands it ("the person who(m) I wrote to"); most
natural of all is omitting the relative pronoun entirely ("the person I wrote to"), p.13.

### 1.7 'Whose' and defining/non-defining clauses

**1.7A 'Whose.'** Replaces possessive adjectives for people (invariant for gender/number); can also
replace *its* for things, and can combine with a fronted preposition in formal style ("the man from
whose house..."), p.14.

**1.7B Defining vs. non-defining clauses.** Defining clauses supply essential identifying
information and take no commas; non-defining clauses add non-essential "extra" information and are
set off by commas. *That* is never used in a non-defining clause and never takes commas. Some
sentences require the writer to judge which reading is intended, p.14.

**1.7C Comma-dependent meaning.** The presence or absence of commas around a relative clause can
change a sentence's meaning (e.g. implying there is more than one referent if non-defining), p.15.

### 1.8 Adverbial clauses of time, place, manner

Covers three parallel systems: time (past-reference conjunctions vs. a distinct future-reference set
where the subordinate verb takes the simple present rather than "will"), place, and manner — see the
Vocabulary table above for each conjunction set, p.16–17.

### 1.9 Adverbial clauses of reason and contrast

Reason clauses answer "Why?"; *because* is the one reason-conjunction that cannot be swapped for
*as/since/for*. Contrast has two registers: ordinary subordinating conjunctions (*although, though,
even though...*), and a second, more flexible pattern using *however* + adjective/adverb or *no
matter* + wh-word, which allow free word order within the clause, pp.18–19.

### 1.10 Purpose, result, comparison

Purpose: *so that/in order that* (formal) or the to-infinitive (everyday); *in case* is a distinct
precautionary-purpose conjunction tied to the simple present/*should*. Result: *so + adjective
(that)* and *such + noun (that)*. Comparison: *as...as/not so...as/than*, using object pronouns or
subject+verb, with *do/does/did* able to stand in for a repeated main verb, pp.20–21.

### 1.11 Present participle constructions

The *-ing* form can replace a second coordinated clause ("Finding the door locked, I went round the
back" = "I found...and went..."), can replace an adverbial clause after a conjunction ("Since
arriving here..."), and can replace a relative clause built on the present tense/progressive
("the man serving at the counter" = "the man who is serving"). Negation is formed with *not* +
*-ing*; *being* substitutes for *is/was*, pp.22–23.

### 1.12 Perfect/past participle constructions

Formal register substitutes: *being*/*having been* for *is/are/was/were* and *have/had been*; *it
being*/*there being* for *it is/was* and *there is/was* (notably after *without*). A key accuracy
rule: the implied subject of a fronted participle must match the sentence's actual grammatical
subject, or the sentence becomes nonsensical/a classic "dangling participle" error (e.g. "*Opening
the door, the smell was bad*" wrongly implies the smell opened the door). Past participles can
likewise replace passive relative/adverbial clauses: "Viewed from a distance, it resembled a cloud"
(= "When it was viewed..."), pp.24–25.

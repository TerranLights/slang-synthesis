## Grammar points

### 9.45 Vehicle and vessel numbers

Boats and similar vessels differentiated by a number usually take a roman numeral (*Bluebird III*);
spacecraft take an arabic numeral instead (*Mariner 9*) (p. 1248).

### 9.46 Successive governments

Ordinal numbers naming successive dynasties, governments, and other governing bodies are spelled out
if one hundred or less: *Eighteenth Dynasty*, *Fifth Republic*, *Second Continental Congress*,
*Second International*, *Ninety-Seventh United States Congress*. Above one hundred, the numeral form
is used instead: *111th Congress* (p. 1249).

### 9.47 Numbered political and judicial divisions

Ordinal numbers naming political or judicial divisions follow the same spell-out-if-100-or-less rule:
*Fifth Ward*, *Twelfth Congressional District*, *Tenth Circuit*, but *101st Precinct* once the
threshold is passed (p. 1250).

### 9.48 Numbered military units

The same spell-out-if-100-or-less rule applies to military units: *Fifth Army*, *Fourth Infantry
Division*, *First Corps Support Command*, but *101st Airborne Division* (p. 1251).

### 9.49 Numbered places of worship

Ordinal numbers that are part of the name of a place of worship are spelled out, with no stated
hundred-or-less ceiling in this particular rule: *Fourth Presbyterian Church*, *Twenty-First Church
of Christ, Scientist* (p. 1252).

### 9.50 Unions and lodges

Numbers designating local branches of labor unions and fraternal lodges are usually expressed in
arabic numerals placed after the name, with commas generally omitted: *Chicago Typographical Union
No. 16*, *American Legion Post 21*, *United Auto Workers Local 890* (p. 1253).

### 9.51 Numbered highways

State, federal, and interstate highways are designated by arabic numerals. Names for state routes
vary from state to state, sometimes offering multiple accepted forms for the same road: *US Route 41*
(or *US 41*), *Interstate 90* (or *I-90*), *Illinois Route 50* (or *Illinois 50*; *IL 50*; *Route
50*). A British example is given for contrast: *M6 motorway (England)* (p. 1254).

### 9.52 Numbered streets

Names of numbered streets, avenues, and the like are spelled out if one hundred or less: *First
Avenue*, *Ninety-Fifth Street*; above that, numeral form: *122nd Street* (p. 1255).

### 9.53 Building and apartment numbers

Building numbers, in arabic numerals, precede the street name, and text usage for an address may
differ slightly from envelope conventions (e.g. *1155 South Euclid Avenue*; *unit 114A, 150 Ninth
Avenue*; *1427 East Sixtieth Street* versus the envelope-style *1427 E. 60th St.*). CMOS points
readers to the US Postal Service's website for preferred US address forms. When a building is
referred to by its address in running text rather than an address line, the number is often spelled
out instead: *One Thousand Lake Shore Drive*, *One IBM Plaza* (p. 1256).

### 9.54 Plural numbers

Spelled-out numbers form their plurals the same way other nouns do (cross-referencing §7.5): *the
contestants were in their twenties and thirties*; *the family was at sixes and sevens*. Numerals form
their plurals simply by adding *s*, with no apostrophe needed: *two 240s and three 238s*; *jazz forms
that were developed in the 1920s became popular in the 1930s* (p. 1257).

### 9.55 Comma between digits

In most numerals of one thousand or more, commas separate groups of three digits counting from the
right (*1,512*; *32,987*; *4,000,500*) — though scientific writing often omits the comma from
four-digit numbers. No commas are used in page numbers, line numbers (e.g. in poetry and plays),
addresses, or years (*11030 South Langley Avenue*) — with one exception: years of five digits or more
*do* take the comma, so a five-digit BP date takes a comma while a four-digit one does not (*35,000
BP* but *5000 BP* in the same sentence) (p. 1258).

### 9.56 Space between digits

Under SI (International System of Units) convention, thin, fixed spaces — rather than commas — mark
off groups of three digits, both left and right of the decimal point (a nonbreaking space may be used
in electronic publications): *3 426 869*; *0.000 007*. Numbers of only four digits to either side of
the decimal point take no space, except in table columns that also contain five-or-more-digit numbers
(*2501.4865*, shown with no space despite being a four-digit sequence). This spacing system is
described as far more common in Europe than in the United States. CMOS cites Ambler Thompson and
Barry N. Taylor's *Guide for the Use of the International System of Units (SI)* and the brochure *The
International System of Units*, published in English and French by the Bureau International des
Poids et Mesures, as further references (p. 1259).

### 9.57 Numbered lists and outline style

A cross-reference only (to §§6.121–26) for the use of arabic and roman numerals, and letters, to
distinguish items in lists — no new rule or vocabulary introduced in this section itself (p. 1260).

### 9.58 When to use the en dash

An en dash placed between two numbers implies "up to and including," i.e. *through*: *pages 75–110*;
*the figures for 2001–10*; *age groups 5–7, 8–10, 11–13, and 14–16* (p. 1261).

### 9.59 When not to use the en dash

If *from* or *between* introduces the first of a pair of numbers, the en dash is not used. *From*
must instead be completed with *to* or *through* (*from 75 to 110*, not *from 75–110*; *from January
1, 1898, through December 31, 1903*), and *between* must be completed with *and* (*between about 150
and 200*). Inclusive spelled-out numbers are likewise joined by *to*, never by an en dash: *women aged
forty-five to forty-nine years*; *sixty- to seventy-year-olds* (p. 1262).

### 9.60 Abbreviating, or condensing, inclusive numbers

Chicago's own system for abbreviating page/serial-style inclusive number ranges (used in essentially
this form since the manual's first edition) varies how much of the second number is written based on
the magnitude of the first:

- First number under 100: use all digits of the second number (*3–10*, *71–72*, *96–117*).
- First number is 100 or an exact multiple of 100: use all digits of the second (*100–104*,
  *1100–1113*).
- First number 101–109, 201–209, etc.: use only the changed part of the second number (*101–8*,
  *808–33*, *1103–4*).
- First number 110–199, 210–299, etc.: use two digits of the second number unless more are needed to
  capture every changed digit (*321–28*, *498–532*, *1087–89*, *1496–500*, *11564–615*,
  *12991–3001*).

Inclusive roman numerals, to avoid ambiguity, are always given in full rather than abbreviated:
*xxv–xxviii*, *cvi–cix* (pp. 1263, also cross-referencing §§9.61, 6.78).

### 9.61 Alternative systems for inclusive numbers

Two alternatives to Chicago's own system are noted without being adopted: a "foolproof" system that
always gives the full form of both numbers (*234–235*, *25039–25041*), and a more economical practice
that includes only the changed part of the second number regardless of magnitude (*234–5*,
*25000–1*). Chicago prefers the graduated system described in §9.60 instead (p. 1264).

### 9.62 Inclusive numbers with commas

When an inclusive number pair itself contains commas, the same abbreviation rules from §9.60 apply
only to the hundreds place and below; if a change extends to the thousands place or beyond, all
digits are repeated rather than abbreviated: *6,000–6,018*; *12,473–79*; *1,247,689–710* (changed part
only, since the change stays below the thousands place) versus *1,247,689–1,248,125* (full repetition,
since the change reaches the thousands place) (p. 1265).

### 9.63 Inclusive years

Inclusive years may be abbreviated following the §9.60 pattern (*1897–1901*; *the war of 1914–18*;
*fiscal year 1997–98*, alternatively *1997/98*; *the winter of 2000–2001*; *in 1504–5*). When the
century changes, or when the sequence uses BCE, BC, or BP (all "diminishing" — i.e., descending —
numbering), all digits must be given in full instead of abbreviated: *327–321 BCE* (a six-year span);
*327–21 BCE* (a very different, 306-year span, showing why abbreviation would be ambiguous here);
*115 BC–AD 10*; *15,000–14,000 BP*. In book titles, it is customary but not obligatory to repeat all
digits; when citing a title, the original's own form should be respected (p. 1266).

### 9.64 Roman numerals — general principles

Table 9.1 tabulates roman numerals against their arabic equivalents. General formation principle: a
smaller-value letter placed before a larger one subtracts from its value, while a smaller letter
placed after a larger one adds to it; a bar drawn over a letter multiplies its value by one thousand.
Roman numerals may also be written in lowercase (i, ii, iii, iv, etc.). In older sources, a final *i*
was often written like a *j* (*vij*, *viij*); citations to roman-numeral page numbers in older works
should preserve the original usage rather than normalizing it (p. 1267).

### 9.65 The advent of subtrahends (back counters)

The subtractive-notation convention (a smaller numeral before a larger one, informally a "back
counter") was introduced during the Renaissance — earlier roman numeral practice used only additive
notation. *IIII*, not the subtractive *IV*, still appears on some clock faces as a holdover of the
older style. Applying additive-only notation, the Romans would have rendered the year 1999 as
*MDCCCCLXXXXVIIII*. The modern subtractive form approved by the US government, and accepted (if
reluctantly) by classical scholars, is *MCMXCIX* — explicitly *not* the shorter *MIM*, which CMOS
calls "a barbarism" (p. 1268).

### 9.66 Chicago's preference for arabic rather than roman numerals

Chicago uses arabic numerals in most situations where roman numerals were formerly conventional,
including volume numbers of books/journals and chapter numbers (cross-referencing §9.28). The
remaining exceptions where Chicago does retain roman numerals, each treated in its own section
elsewhere in the manual, are: certain front-matter page numbering of books (§§1.4, 1.6, 9.27); legal
instruments (§9.29); names of monarchs, prelates, and similar titles (§9.42); personal names (§9.43);
titles of sequels (§9.44); names of certain vessels (§9.45, covered above in this same chunk); and
outline style (§6.126) (p. 1269).

## Grammar points

### Byrhtnoth's dying prayer as sustained religious framing (p.186–187)

Unlike the ambivalent, possibly-fate-attributed victory formula in the Beowulf extract (pt08),
Byrhtnoth's dying speech is an explicit Christian prayer — thanking God for life's joys, requesting
grace for his soul's safe journey, and asking specifically that devils (*hel-scēaþan*) not be
allowed to seize it. The source reads this, combined with the pointed description of his killers as
*hǣþene scealcas* 'heathen warriors' (with the headstave deliberately placed on *hǣðene*), as working
to elevate Byrhtnoth toward the status of a Christian martyr, not merely a fallen warlord.

### Manuscript transmission errors, identified via metrical analysis (p.187)

A second worked case study (after pt09's flyting-dialect discussion) in using OE poetic form itself
as an evidence-gathering tool: line 172 is a single half-line with no b-verse, implying lost text;
line 183 fails to alliterate at all, which the source attributes to a probable copying error (the
word *bēgen* 'both' likely mistakenly repeated from the preceding line, displacing what should have
been an alliterating partner for one of the two named warriors' names, Ælfnoth or Wulmær). This
reinforces the pt09 note that the sole manuscript, destroyed in the 1731 fire, survives only via an
imperfect pre-fire transcript.

### A sustained series of loyalty speeches, deliberately representative of English society (p.189–201)

Six successive named speakers (Ælfric → his son Ælfwine; then Offa; then Leofsunu; then the peasant
Dunnere; then the Northumbrian hostage Æscferth; then old Byrhtwold) each vow to continue fighting
and avenge Byrhtnoth. The source argues this sequence is **deliberately patterned** to represent a
full cross-section of English society by age (young Ælfwine vs. old Byrhtwold), social rank (noble
Ælfwine vs. peasant Dunnere), region (Mercia/Ælfwine, Essex-Sturmer/Leofsunu, Northumbria/Æscferth),
and even nationality (the foreign-born hostage Æscferth, paralleling the British hostage's loyalty in
the Cynewulf and Cyneheard annal, pt07) — and that this patterning likely exceeds what a real army's
actual composition would have been, i.e. is itself evidence of poetic construction rather than
historical reportage. Each speech also carries the recurring "heroic boast" convention (*gylp-
wordum*, *bēotian*) of publicly vowing a course of action before fulfilling it.

### A genuine textual puzzle: Wistan's conflicting patronymics (p.201–202)

Wistan is introduced as *Þurstānes sunu* 'Thurstan's son' (l.298) but two lines later as apparently
*Wīgelines bearn* 'Wigelin's child' (l.300) — the source offers three possible explanations without
settling on one: a straightforward copying error; Wigelin as the name of Wistan's mother (despite the
masculine-looking Genitive *-es* inflexion, which would be anomalous for a female name); or *bearn*
here meaning 'grandchild' rather than 'child'. Flagged as directly comparable to the Cynewulf and
Cyneheard annal's own probable numeral-transposition error (pt07) — manuscript corruption/ambiguity
recurring as a genre-wide feature of this transmission tradition, not unique to one text.

### The final formula-echo and ironic name-doubling (p.203, 209)

Line 309 (*Byrhtwold maðelode* 'Byrhtwold spoke') deliberately repeats line 42's *Byrhtnōþ maðelode*
'Byrhtnoth spoke' with only the name swapped — the source reads this as a pointed parallel between
the fallen lord and his last surviving retainer, reinforced by their names sharing the same first
element (*Byrht-*). Separately, the poem's final named warrior is **also called Godric** (*Æðelgāres
bearn*, 'Æthelgar's son') — explicitly a different man from the cowardly Odda's-son Godric who fled
on Byrhtnoth's horse earlier (lines 185–201) — and the surviving text closes on the narrator's own
pointed clarification, 'that was *not* the Godric who fled from the battle,' using the repeated name
itself as a final structural contrast between cowardice and heroism. The poem's surviving text breaks
off mid-narrative at this point (marked by "..." in the source), with the true ending lost.

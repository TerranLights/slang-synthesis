### Named faults of style flagged by Appendix III, with period examples (genuinely slang-relevant)

Under **Propriety, Precept I ("Avoid low and provincial expressions")**, Brown explicitly names and flags
as substandard a set of 1851-era colloquial/slang interjections and constructions: *"Now, says I, boys"*;
*"Thinks I to myself"*; *"to get into a scrape"*; *"Stay here while I come back"* (dialectal *while* for
*until*); *"By jinkers"*; *"By the living jingoes."* This is a rare instance in the book of Brown directly
naming and citing real period colloquial/slang forms (rather than only citing literary misquotations) as
examples of what cultivated prose should avoid — i.e., a 19th-century prescriptive grammarian's own
boundary-marking of the "slang" register, valuable as a primary-source snapshot of what counted as marked
informal speech in 1851 American English. Under **Propriety, Precept III**, Brown separately flags invented/
unauthorized neologisms from contemporary grammar books themselves as a related but distinct fault:
*flutteration, inspectator, judgematical, incumberment, connexity, electerized, martyrized, reunition,
marvelize, limpitude, affectated, adorement, absquatulate* (several of these — "flutteration," "absquatulate" —
are themselves genuine 19th-century American slang coinages, cited here disapprovingly by Brown but
independently attested as real period slang vocabulary).

### Poetic-register noun vocabulary (Appendix IV) — words "not used in prose, or used but rarely"

*benison, boon, emprise, fane, guerdon, guise, ire, ken, lore, meed, sire, steed, welkin, yore* — Brown's own
closed list of words he judges confined to poetic register, directly comparable to a Usage-Tier `literary` /
`archaic` tag in this project's own taxonomy.

## Grammar points

### Appendix I — Of the Sounds of the Letters

An expanded, letter-by-letter phonetic treatment reserved from the main Orthography chapter (see `pt02`) "to
avoid a minuteness of detail not desirable in the first chapter." Brown opens with an extended critique (Obs.
1–5) of the terminological chaos among contemporary orthoëpists over how to classify vowel sounds —
*long/short, broad/slender, open/close* are shown to be used in mutually contradictory senses by Wells,
Walker, and Perley, and the total count of English's "elementary sounds" is shown to range, across named
authorities, from 26 (Churchill) to over 40 (Wells's first edition claimed 41) — a continuation of the same
disputed-phoneme-count theme already documented for the main Orthography chapter in `pt02`. Brown then
works through all 26 letters individually, each given its full inventory of distinct sounds with examples:
**A** has 4 (English/long as in *fame*; French/short as in *bat*; Italian/broadish as in *far*; Dutch/broad as in
*wall*); **E** has 2–3; **I** has 3 (including a "feeble, faint, or slender" unaccented sound, as in *divest*);
**O** has 3 (including an *oo*-like "slender" sound, as in *prove, move, who*); **U** has 3 (including a
*pull*-like "middle" sound). Consonants are mostly single-sound but several get detailed positional/
contextual sub-rules: **G** hard before *a, o, u, l, r* or word-finally, soft (like *j*) before *e, i, y* (with
named exceptions: *get, give, gewgaw, finger*); **H** silent in a specific closed list (*heir, herb, honest,
honour, hospital, hostler, hour, humble, humour*) and always silent after *r* or word-finally after a vowel;
**GH** word-initial = hard *g* (*ghost*), elsewhere usually silent (*high, though*), sometimes = *f*
(*laugh, rough*), sometimes = hard *g* (*burgh*), sometimes = *k* (*hough, lough, shough*). Each vowel
section also catalogues its diphthong and triphthong combinations with their multiple possible sound-values
(e.g. **OU** is shown to have 6 distinct sound values across different words: *rough, ought, court, cough,
soup, would*).

### Appendix II — Of the Derivation of Words

Reserved etymological/morphological content "to show the composition, origin, and literal sense" of English
words, explicitly framed by Brown as valuable "to a right understanding of the import and just application" of
vocabulary — a direct ancestor of this project's own interest in morpheme-level word-formation mechanics.
Brown gives a compressed external history of English (Saxon origin c. 450 CE, gradual accession from
Norse, Norman French, Latin, and Greek, not genuinely recognizable as "English" until the 13th century) as
framing, then organizes derivation by part of speech across 10 sections (full noun/adjective suffix inventories
given in the Vocabulary table above). Two sections carry genuine etymological argument worth noting:

- **Sec. I, Derivation of the Articles**, engages at length with **Horne Tooke's** etymological theory (from
  *Diversions of Purley*) that *the* derives from the Saxon verb *thean* ("to take") and that *that* is literally
  its past participle, meaning "taken" — which Brown subjects to real philological scrutiny (citing Bosworth's
  Anglo-Saxon dictionary against Tooke's reconstructed forms) and ultimately rejects as insufficiently
  evidenced, preferring instead a connection between *the* and Anglo-Saxon *se* (from *seon*, "to see").
  *An* is more confidently traced to Saxon *oen/ane/an*, "one," with *a* as the n-dropped reduced form — the
  same Gawin Douglas citation (*"Ane book," "Ane lang spere"*) already seen in `pt03`'s treatment of the
  articles.
- **Secs. V–X** (Verbs, Participles, Adverbs, Conjunctions, Prepositions, Interjections) continue tracing
  individual function words to Saxon/Gothic roots in the same vein as the *to*/*do* digression already
  documented in `pt08`'s Rule XVIII material.

### Appendix III — Of the Qualities of Style

A genuine rhetoric/composition-craft capstone, explicitly scoped to style questions beyond "mere grammar, in
any limited sense." Brown organizes it around **six qualities**, each with a short list of numbered Precepts
(full section/precept map given above; see the Vocabulary table for the period-slang examples under
Propriety): **Purity** (use only words genuinely belonging to the language — avoid unnecessary foreign
words/idioms, obsolete words, strange/unauthorized neologisms, and bombast); **Propriety** (use words as
the best usage has assigned them — avoid low/provincial expressions, merely-poetical words in prose,
unnecessary technical terms, ambiguous word-repetition, missing words, equivocal expressions, unintelligible/
inconsistent expressions, and events out of natural order — "putting the cart before the horse"); **Precision**
(avoid useless tautology; repeat words when exact meaning requires it; respect real distinctions among
near-synonyms; don't confuse similar word-forms; think clearly and avoid incompatible combinations; cut
useless words); **Perspicuity** (keep modifiers near what they modify; avoid poetic word order in prose;
avoid faulty ellipsis; avoid obscurantist pseudo-technical jargon — explicitly aimed at "the pedantic and
sense-dimming style of charlatans and new theorists"); **Unity** (keep one topic per paragraph and one
sentiment per sentence; don't let a sentence's syntax wander from its real subject into its adjuncts; use
parentheses sparingly); and **Strength** (favor concision; place the most important words where they land
with the most force; order clauses so the strongest assertion comes last; parallel comparison/contrast
structures explicitly; avoid ending a sentence on a weak word like a bare preposition or adverb).

### Appendix IV — Of Poetic Diction

A catalogue of "poetical peculiarities" — systematic ways verse licenses diverge from ordinary prose register,
framed via Blair's definition of poetry as "the language of passion, or of enlivened imagination." Documented
peculiarities include: dropping articles for brevity/meter; **clipping common nouns** in a poetry-specific way
(*amaze* for *amazement*, *corse* for *corpse*, *eve* for *evening*, *fount* for *fountain*, *helm* for
*helmet*, *morn* for *morning*, *plaint* for *complaint*, *weal* for *wealth*) — a direct formal parallel to the
"Figures of Etymology" clipping mechanisms (apocope etc.) already catalogued in `pt09`, here shown
operating specifically as a *register*-marking device rather than a general phonological process; **enallage**
(using a verb form substantively, or a verb for a noun, for brevity: "without *disturb*," "without *revile*
reply'd"); a closed list of poetry-only nouns (given in the Vocabulary table above); and the postposed-*self*
construction ("affliction's *self*," "beauty's *self*") as a further poetic-register-marked possessive pattern.

# English — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: *Essential Grammar in Use, Supplementary Exercises* (full workbook)

**Source:** Helen Naylor with Raymond Murphy, *Essential Grammar in Use: Supplementary Exercises*
(2nd ed., Cambridge University Press, 1996, 2nd reprint 2001). Companion drill workbook to
*Essential Grammar in Use* (Murphy) — 184 numbered exercises across 70 pages of drills (pp.1–94)
plus a 12-page answer key (pp.95–106), cross-referenced throughout to *Essential Grammar in Use*
unit numbers. Vision-read in full (pp.1–113 of a 113-page scan with no text layer — pure
JPEG/CCITT page images, `pdftotext` returns nothing).

**Coverage note — this source is almost entirely drill, not explanation.** Unlike every other
source extracted for this language so far, this book contains **no explanatory grammar prose at
all** — "To the student"/"To the teacher" (p.v) confirms explicitly that explanations live only in
the main *Essential Grammar in Use* volume (not held in this project's source corpus; not
extracted here), and this companion assumes the student already has them. Every one of its 184
exercises is a fill-in-the-blank/rewrite/transform drill built from ordinary elementary-level
sentences (people's names, everyday activities, travel, family) that recycle the same closed set of
core vocabulary exercise after exercise — true pedagogical padding in the sense the extraction
spec warns about. Per the coverage rule, this file **does not** attempt to log each exercise's
prose content (there would be ~184 near-identical "core vocabulary in a sentence" rows for zero
analytical value). What **is** genuinely extracted below:

1. The full grammar-topic map of the book (all 184 exercises grouped by the 51 named grammar
   points/unit-groups they drill), since this is itself a comprehensive, independently-organized
   checklist of elementary/lower-intermediate English grammar — useful as a structural cross-check
   against this project's existing CMOS/Oxford-sourced grammar coverage (`001`–`055`).
2. The **closed-class function-word sets** the book actually teaches as content — prepositions of
   time, prepositions of place/direction, phrasal-verb particles, and quantifier/determiner sets —
   since for this particular source, the closed-class word lists genuinely *are* the book's
   vocabulary content (there is no open-class vocabulary teaching to extract).
3. A set of genuine irregular-verb past-simple/past-participle pairs that surfaced as real paradigm
   data inside the drills (Units 23–24, "regular/irregular verbs").

**Vision-reading guard note:** no handwritten marginalia was found on any of the 113 pages. The
book's own printed "model answer" font (a cursive/italic typeface used throughout for inserted
answers in the Key and for the odd worked first-answer-of-an-exercise) could be mistaken for a
reader's handwriting at a glance — it was checked page-by-page and confirmed to be a consistent
printed typeface used identically across the whole book, not a previous owner's pencil/pen
annotation. Nothing was excluded as marginalia.

## Grammar points

The book's full topic map, grouped exactly as the book itself groups its 184 exercises (Contents,
pp.iii–iv), each cross-referenced to *Essential Grammar in Use* unit numbers in the page headers.
Since this source supplies no explanatory prose of its own, each entry below is a short paraphrase
of the contrast the drills themselves make visible, not a reproduction of book text.

### Be, do-support, and basic sentence forms (Units 1–9)
`am/is/are` (affirmative, negative, question forms, incl. contracted `'m/'s/'re`, `isn't/aren't`);
`have got` (possession, with `'s got`/`'ve got`, `hasn't got`/`haven't got`) as distinct from main-verb
`have`.

### Tense/aspect system, present and past (Units 3–24)
Present continuous (`I am doing`) vs. present simple (`I do/work/like`) — the drills consistently
contrast state-at-this-moment against habitual/general-truth framing (weather, nationality,
routines vs. what's happening right now). Past simple (`was/were`, regular `-ed`/irregular forms)
vs. past continuous (`was/were doing`) — contrasted as background-ongoing-action vs.
single-completed-event, the same backgrounding logic as the present-tense pair one level back in
time. `have got` continues to be revisited alongside this block as a recurring confusable.

### Present perfect (Units 15–20)
`I have done` contrasted against past simple (`I did`) — the drills consistently route this
distinction through explicit time markers: `already`/`yet` (p.23–24) for completion-relative-to-now,
`since`/`for` (p.24) for duration up to now, and `has/have been` vs. `has/have gone` (p.23) as a
genuine semantic fork (been = went-and-returned, gone = went-and-is-still-there) rather than a pure
tense distinction. Present perfect continuous (`has/have been doing`) is drilled specifically against
`for`/`since` duration phrases (p.25) as the ongoing-up-to-now counterpart to simple present perfect.

### Passive voice (Units 21–22)
`is done`/`was done` (present/past simple passive) and `is being done`/`has been done` (continuous/
perfect passive) — drilled through "guided factory tour" and "quiz show" contexts that force
by-agent-unknown or agent-irrelevant framing, the classic passive-voice trigger condition.

### Modality (Units 25–38)
`used to` (habitual/state-in-the-past, contrasted against simple present for what's still true now);
future expressions split three ways — present continuous (`I'm doing`, for a fixed, already-arranged
plan), `going to` (for an intention/prediction with present evidence), and `will/shall` (`will`/`'ll`
for a decision made at the moment of speaking or a prediction without present evidence; `shall`
restricted to offers/suggestions, `Shall I...?`/`Shall we...?`); `might`(`not`) for an unsure future;
`can`/`could` for present/past ability; `must`/`mustn't`/`needn't` — drilled as a genuine three-way
contrast (obligation / prohibition / absence-of-obligation, not two binary pairs) distinguished from
`had to` for past obligation; `should`/`shouldn't` for advice/opinion; `have to`/`had to`/`don't have
to` for external, often rule-based obligation as distinct from `must`'s more personal obligation.

### Existential and dummy subjects (Units 36–38)
`there is/are`(`n't`) for existence-of-something vs. `it is/was` for identification/weather/time —
drilled as a genuine confusable pair since both can precede `is`.

### Auxiliary-verb substitution and ellipsis (Units 39–42)
Short-form auxiliary answers (`I do`/`Have you?`/`So am I`); question tags (positive statement →
negative tag, and vice versa); `too`/`either` for agreement-with-a-positive/negative statement;
`so`/`neither` as inverted agreement forms (`So does Mary`, `Neither can I`).

### Negation (Unit 42)
`isn't/haven't/don't` etc. as the negative-auxiliary set, revisited explicitly against the
third-person/modal-auxiliary forms covered in Units 39–41.

### Question formation (Units 43–54)
Subject vs. object questions (`Who put them there?` vs. `What did you see?` — object questions need
`do`-support, subject questions don't); questions ending in a stranded preposition (`Who's she
talking to?`); `which/what/how` + a specifying word (`How tall...?`, `Which language...?`); common
question-formation errors (missing `do`-support, wrong word order, misplaced preposition); embedded/
indirect questions (`Do you know where...?`, `I don't know why...`) with the word-order flip back to
statement order inside the embedded clause.

### Reported speech (Units 49, 52)
`said (that)` vs. `told + object (that)` as a genuine distinct-complementation-pattern pair (`told`
obligatorily takes a person; `say` does not); reported commands with `told...to...`; the
backshift of tense, pronouns, and time/place expressions (`tomorrow`→`the next day`, etc., visible in
the letter-rewriting exercises on pp.59, 69) when speech is reported after the fact.

### `to`-infinitive vs. `-ing` vs. bare infinitive (Units 50–54)
A genuinely three-way contrast: `to do` after most verbs of decision/intention, bare `do` after
modals and a small set of verbs (`let`, `make` + object), and `-ing` after prepositions and a set of
verbs of preference/habit. Verb + object + `to`-infinitive pattern (`advise/persuade/let/teach/
expect/make someone (to) do`) with the irregular bare-infinitive cases (`let`, `make`) flagged by
contrast. Purpose clauses: `to do` vs. `for` + noun/`-ing` (`to get stamps` vs. `for stamps`, not
*`for getting stamps`*).

### Pronouns, possessives, and reflexives (Units 58–63)
Personal/possessive/reflexive pronoun paradigm (`I/me/my/mine/myself` across all persons);
`himself/herself/themselves` etc. vs. `each other` (self-directed vs. mutual action) vs. `by myself`
(alone, not self-directed); `-'s` possessive vs. `of`-possessive, with the genitive drilled against
both people (`Sue's husband`) and the "double genitive" pattern (`a friend of mine`).

### Articles and determiners (Units 64–72)
`a/an` (indefinite, incl. irregular plurals `foot→feet`, `child→children`, etc.); countable/
uncountable distinction with `a/some`; `a/an` vs. `the` (first-mention vs. subsequent-reference, and
a closed set of fixed `the`-requiring categories: rivers, named buildings, "the capital", "the
beginning/end of"); `some`/`any` (affirmative/question-offer vs. negative-question/conditional
contexts); `some/any/no/none` as a four-way set; compound indefinites (`somebody/anybody/nobody` etc.,
`something/anything/nothing`, `somewhere/anywhere/nowhere`); `every` vs. `all` (`every` = each one
individually + singular verb; `all` = the group collectively, can combine with `of`); `all/most/some/
any/no/none` (+ `of`) as a six-way proportion scale; `both/either/neither` as the two-item
equivalents.

### Adjectives, adverbs, comparison (Units 84–91)
Adjective vs. adverb (`quickly/badly` etc., incl. the irregular `well` as the adverb of `good`);
comparatives/superlatives (regular `-er/-est` and `more/most`, plus the "same as"/"not as...as"
equal/unequal-comparison frame); `enough` (after adjective/adverb, before noun) and `too` (`too
+ adjective` vs. `too much/too many` + noun) as a sufficiency/excess pair.

### Word order and time adverbs (Units 92–94)
Mid-position placement of frequency/degree adverbs (`often`, `always`, `never`, `also`) relative to
the verb; `still` (continuing, unexpectedly long) vs. `yet` (negative/question, expected-not-yet) vs.
`already` (positive, sooner-than-expected).

### Conditionals (Units 110–112)
`when` vs. `if` as a genuine semantic contrast (`when` = certainty the event happens, `if` =
uncertainty); zero/first conditional pattern (`if` + present, main clause present/future — not
*`if` + will*, a flagged common error); second conditional (`if` + past simple, `would` + bare verb)
for hypothetical/unreal present situations, including the fixed irregular `were` (not `was`) after
`if I/he/she/it` in this pattern.

### Relative clauses (Units 113–114)
`who` (people) vs. `which` (things) as subject or object relative pronoun, including clauses where
the relative pronoun can be dropped entirely (object relative clauses: *the man she's looking at*)
vs. clauses where it's structurally required (subject relative clauses).

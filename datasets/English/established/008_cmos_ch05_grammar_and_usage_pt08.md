## Grammar points

### 5.164 Intensifiers
An adverb can itself be intensified by a word like *very* or *quite* (*the very fashionably dressed
actor*; *everyone ate quite heartily*).

### 5.165 Placement of adverbs
An adverb should sit as close as possible to the word it's meant to modify, because a misplaced adverb
creates real ambiguity or even reverses meaning. *The marathoners submitted their applications to
compete immediately* leaves unclear whether *immediately* modifies *compete* or *submitted*; moving the
adverb next to its target word resolves it. More drastically, *we nearly lost all our camping equipment*
(the equipment was saved) and *we lost nearly all our camping equipment* (almost everything was lost)
are opposite claims produced purely by adverb placement.

### 5.166 Adverbs modifying intransitive verbs
An adverb qualifying an intransitive verb should generally immediately follow that verb (*the students
sighed gloomily when homework was assigned*; *the owl perched precariously on a thin branch*). A small
set of adverbs — *always, never, often, generally, rarely, seldom* — are exceptions and may precede the
verb instead (*mountaineers seldom succeed in climbing K2*).

### 5.167 Adverbs and linking verbs
Adverbs do not normally modify linking verbs — *be*-verbs, *appear, seem, become, look, smell, taste,
hear, feel* — because these verbs connect a descriptive word to the clause's subject rather than
describing the verb's own action; the descriptive word applies to the subject, not the verb (*he seems
modest*, not *modestly*). The writer must judge whether the verb is functioning as a linking verb at
all: *the sculptor feels badly* literally describes a poor sense of touch, while *the sculptor feels
bad* describes the sculptor as unwell or guilt-ridden.

### 5.168 Adverb within a verb phrase
When an adverb qualifies a verb phrase, its natural position is between the auxiliary and the
principal verb (*the administration has consistently repudiated this view*; *public opinion is sharply
divided*). Some adverbs may instead follow the full verb phrase (*you must go quietly*), and there is
no blanket rule against an adverb sitting between the parts of a verb phrase — in fact it's often
stylistically preferable there (*the heckler was abruptly expelled*). Splitting an infinitive with an
adverb for emphasis or natural sound is acceptable when an intervening word immediately follows *to*
(*to bravely assert*) or when the adverb genuinely carries the phrase's emphasis (*to boldly go*; *to
strongly favor*) — in such cases, leave the split infinitive alone, since recasting can shift the
nuance or meaning (*it's best to always get up early*, where *always* modifies *get up*, is not
equivalent to *it's always best to get up early*, where *always* modifies *best*), and "fixing" a split
infinitive can sometimes make a sentence sound unnatural (*it's best to get up early always*).

### 5.169 Prepositions defined
A preposition is a word or phrase linking an object to an antecedent to show the relationship between
them. The object is usually a noun or an objective-case pronoun (*between me and them*), though an
adjective, adverb, verb, or phrase can occupy that slot instead. A preposition usually precedes its
object, but can end a clause or sentence, especially a relative clause (*this isn't the pen that Steve
writes with*). A preposition used with the relative pronoun *that* (stated or understood) always
follows its object (*this is the moment [that] I've been waiting for*), and frequently (not always)
follows the relative pronouns *which* and *whom* (*there is a banker [whom] I must speak with*; *I
can't tell you to whom you should apply*).

### 5.170 Simple and compound prepositions
A simple preposition is a single, usually monosyllabic word (*as, at, by, down, for, from, in, like,
of, off, on, plus, since, through, to, toward, up, with*). A compound preposition has two or more
syllables, whether written as one word or several (*into, outside, upon*; *about, above, across, after,
against, alongside, around, before, below, beneath, between, despite, except, inside, onto, opposite,
throughout, underneath, until, without*).

### 5.171 Participial prepositions
A participial preposition is a participial form functioning as a preposition (or sometimes as a
subordinating conjunction) — *assuming, barring, concerning, considering, during, notwithstanding,
owing to, provided, regarding, respecting, speaking*. Unlike ordinary participles, these words don't
create dangling modifiers even when they have no stated subject (*considering the road conditions, the
trip went quickly*; *regarding Watergate, he had nothing to say*).

### 5.172 Phrasal prepositions
A phrasal (or complex) preposition is two or more separate words functioning as a single prepositional
unit — *according to, because of, by means of, by reason of, by way of, contrary to, for the sake of,
in accordance with, in addition to, in apposition with, in case of, in consideration of, in front of,
in regard to, in respect to, in spite of, instead of, on account of, out of, with reference to, with
regard to, with respect to*. Many of these are symptomatic of officialese, bureaucratese, or other
verbose styles; a judicious editor prefers the one-word equivalent when context supports it (*about*
over *with regard to* or *in connection with*).

### 5.173 Prepositional phrases defined
A prepositional phrase consists of a preposition, its object, and any words modifying that object. It
can function as a noun (*for James to change his mind would be a miracle*), an adverb/adverbial phrase
(*we strolled through the glade*), or an adjective/adjectival phrase (*the cathedrals of Paris*).

### 5.174 Prepositional function
Prepositions signal many kinds of relationships: spatial (*to, from, out of, into*), time (*at, for,
throughout, until*), cause (*because of, on account of*), means (*like, with, by*), possession
(*without, of*), exceptions (*but for, besides, except*), support (*with, for*), opposition (*against*),
and concession (*despite, for all, notwithstanding*).

### 5.175 Placement of prepositional phrases
A prepositional phrase functioning adverbially or adjectivally should sit as close as possible to the
word it modifies, to avoid awkwardness, ambiguity, or an unintended meaning (compare *is a person with
blond hair named Sandy here?* — ambiguous — with the clearer *the woman with the Popular Front
circulates petitions*). If the phrase modifies all elements of a compound construction equally, it
follows the last element of the compound (*the date, the place, and the budget for the wedding have
been decided*). When a prepositional phrase with plural objects follows a singular subject, the
predicate must still agree with the singular subject — compare *the man and two daughters have arrived*
with *the man with two daughters has arrived* and *the man has arrived with his two daughters*.

### 5.176 Ending a sentence with a preposition
The traditional caveat against ending a sentence with a preposition is, for most writers today, an
unnecessary and pedantic restriction — a sentence ending in a preposition can sound more natural than
one awkwardly recast to avoid it (compare *this is the case I told you about* with the stilted *this is
the case about which I told you*). CMOS characterizes the "rule" against terminal prepositions as an
ill-founded superstition, citing the (possibly apocryphal) Churchill retort, "That is the type of
arrant pedantry up with which I shall not put." Grammarians today use the dismissive term
*pied-piping* for the practice of dragging the preposition along with the relative pronoun instead.

### 5.177 Clashing prepositions
When a phrasal verb (*give in*) precedes a prepositional phrase that uses the same preposition (*in
every argument*), the two prepositions clash (*he gives in in every argument*). Recast the sentence
where possible to avoid the repetition — e.g. *rather than continue arguing, he always gives in*, or
*in every argument, he gives in*.

### 5.178 Elliptical prepositional phrases
A prepositional phrase can be elliptical — an independent expression with no antecedent — often opening
or standing apart from a clause without affecting the statement's meaning: *for example, for instance,
in a word, in any event, in the last analysis, in the long run* (*in any event, call me when you
arrive*).

### 5.179 Effect of prepositional phrase on pronoun
A pronoun inside a prepositional phrase is usually in the objective case, with exceptions turning on
*than*, which can function either as a conjunction (*he's taller than I [am]*) or as a preposition
(*he's taller than me*). Traditional grammarians prefer the nominative/conjunction reading even though
the objective/preposition reading represents common usage; in formal prose, treat *than* as a
conjunction requiring a nominative pronoun (*she is a more careful researcher than he*).

### 5.180 Prepositions and functional variation
Some words that function as prepositions can also function as other parts of speech. The defining
feature of a true preposition is that it always takes an object. A word like *above, behind, below,
by, down, in, off, on,* or *up* can be either an adverb or a preposition: as a preposition it takes an
object (*let's slide down the hill*); as an adverb it does not (*we sat down*) — though such a word may
look like it has an object when it's actually part of a phrasal verb (*I looked up his biography*; to
test, try detaching the apparent object — *up his biography* doesn't make sense alone, confirming *up*
is functioning adverbially as part of the verb). Likewise, some conjunctions (*than, but*) can serve as
prepositions; a conjunction in such use points to an explicit or implied separate action. Compare the
prepositional *but* in *everyone but Fuzzy traveled abroad last summer* (meaning "except") with the
conjunctive *but* in *I like the cut but not the color* (joining a clause with an implied separate
action: *I don't like the color*).

### 5.181 Use and misuse of "like"
*Like* is probably the least understood preposition. Its traditional function is adjectival rather than
adverbial, so that *like* is governed by a noun or noun phrase (*teens often see themselves as
star-crossed lovers like Romeo and Juliet*). As a preposition it takes the objective case (*the person
in that old portrait looks like me*). Increasingly — but loosely — *like* displaces *as* or *as if* as a
conjunction connecting clauses (*it happened just like I said it would happen*, which should read *as*;
*you're looking around like you've misplaced something*, which should read *as if*), since *as* and *as
if* are conjunctions properly followed by a nominative-case noun (*do you work too hard, as I do?*).
Although this conjunctive use of *like* has been considered nonstandard since the seventeenth century,
today it is common in dialectal and colloquial usage (*he ran like he was really scared*) — writers
should weigh context and tone when deciding whether to hold to the standard-English rule.

### 5.182 Use and misuse of "only"
*Only* functions as an adjective, an adverb, and a conjunction, and can modify any part of speech. It
emphasizes the word or phrase it immediately follows, and is probably misplaced in sentences more often
than any other single word — misplacement can alter meaning or create genuine ambiguity. Compare *I
bought only tomatoes at the market* (nothing else was bought) with *I bought tomatoes only at the
market* (nothing was bought anywhere else, or nothing but tomatoes was bought there). In idiomatic
spoken English, *only* is placed before the verb regardless of what it logically modifies (*I only
bought tomatoes at the market*) — acceptable in speech, where intonation disambiguates, but in writing,
where there's no intonation to lean on, rigorous placement of *only* next to the word it actually
modifies is preferable for the reader's comprehension.

### 5.183 Avoiding overuse of prepositions
Prepositions are easy to overuse. A good stylistic ratio to aim for is roughly one preposition per ten
to fifteen words. Five editorial methods (covered in 5.184–5.188) can reduce the preposition count in a
sentence.

### 5.184 Cutting prepositional phrases
Where the surrounding prose's context already makes the point clear, a prepositional phrase can simply
be dropped — e.g., within a passage already focused on a particular recipe, *the most important
ingredient in this recipe* can be reduced to *the most important ingredient*.

### 5.185 Cutting unnecessary prepositions
Many nouns ending in *-ance, -ence, -ity, -ment, -sion,* or *-tion* are formed from a verb
(*qualification* from *qualify*; *performance* from *perform*). Such nominalizations ("buried verbs")
often require additional prepositions to function in a sentence — *during her performance of the
concerto* is essentially equivalent to, but more abstract and wordier than, *while she performed the
concerto* (and requires the preposition *of*). Restoring the noun to its verb form can eliminate one or
two prepositions: *our efforts toward maximization of profits failed* edits down to *our efforts to
maximize profits failed*.

### 5.186 Replacing prepositional phrases with adverbs
A strong adverb can replace a weaker prepositional phrase: *the cyclist pedaled with fury* is weaker
than *the cyclist pedaled furiously*.

### 5.187 Replacing prepositional phrases with genitives
A genitive (possessive) construction can replace a prepositional phrase, especially an *of*-genitive:
*I was dismayed by the complexity of the street map* essentially equals *the street map's complexity
dismayed me* (see 5.19 for the genitive case generally).

## Grammar points

### Five-step reading strategy for inflected Old English text

The book teaches a deliberate method for tackling an OE sentence: (a) mark up inflections and
prefixes by hyphenating or underlining them to expose the word's root; (b) do a strictly literal,
word-for-word translation before attempting a natural one; (c) hear/read the text aloud, since many
cognates (e.g. *Easterdæig* → *Easter*) become obvious only by sound; (d) observe recurring
patterns — word order, typical endings, preposition usage; (e) experiment with modernized spelling
(þ/ð → *th*, soft *c* → *ch*, soft *g* → *y* or *i*, medial guttural *g* → *w*) to make the text
"look" more like Middle/Modern English. p.4–6.

### Dithematic personal names

Most Old English personal names are "dithematic": two meaningful words compounded, each drawn from
a fairly small, productive stock of name-elements (e.g. *Ead-weard* = "blessed" + "guardian").
Bynames/nicknames existed to disambiguate the many people sharing the same name. Royal dynasties
often reused one element across generations (the West Saxon royal line's recurrent *Ead-*, as in
Eadmund, Eadred, Eadwig, Eadgar, Eadward). p.8–9.

### Basic letter-sound correspondences (introduced in context, Unit 1)

þ/ð represent "th"; *c* is "k" except before front vowels (e, i) or in the digraph *cg*/word-final
after a front vowel, where it softens toward "ch" (*cyning* "k," *rice* "ch"); *g* before e/i softens
toward a "y" sound (*geong* "yeong"); typical OE main-clause word order places the finite verb in
second position with the subject following, unlike Modern English's subject-first order (*Her wæs
Eadward gehalgod* = "Here was Edward hallowed," not "Here Edward was hallowed"). p.4–6.

### The infinitive and the -an ending

Old English, being an inflected language, marks the infinitive (the dictionary citation form) with
the ending *-an* (*healdan* "to hold," *dælan* "to share," *feran* "to travel"). One irregular
infinitive is *beon* "to be," which does not take *-an*. p.16–17.

### Modal auxiliaries and infinitive-final word order

*Sceal* "must," *mæg* "can," *mot* "is allowed," *wile* "will/wants" function as auxiliaries taking
an infinitive, but unlike Modern English — where the infinitive sits immediately after the auxiliary
— Old English regularly delays the infinitive to the end of the clause: subject → auxiliary →
adverbial → object/complement → infinitive (*Cyning sceal on healle beagas dælan*, "A king, in
hall, rings must-deal-out"). The book notes this resembles German subordinate-clause word order.
p.17.

### Ellipsis of the infinitive after *sceal*

When the expected infinitive after *sceal* would be a form of "to be" (*beon*/*wesan*) or a closely
related verb of remaining/dwelling (*gewunian*), it can be omitted entirely and is simply understood
— *Treow sceal on eorle* ("Loyalty must [be] in an earl"). p.18.

### Introduction to grammatical case

Nouns and adjectives decline (take endings) to mark their grammatical role. The nominative is the
base/subject form; the accusative typically marks the direct object (fuller treatment deferred to
Units 5–6); the genitive expresses possession, usually translatable as "of X" (dæd-a "of deeds");
the dative is the case forced by most prepositions (*on*, *of*, *mid*), commonly marked with *-e* or
*-um* (*on eorl-e* "in an earl," *on heofen-um* "in the heavens"). The dative after *on*/*mid* can
also mean "with" (*blædum blowan*, "to flourish with fruits"). p.18–19.

### Old English place-name elements: -ceaster, -burh, -tun

Three productive place-name-forming elements are introduced with etymologies and modern-day
reflexes: *-ceaster* (< Latin *castra* "camp/fort," surviving as -chester/-caster, e.g.
Wintanceaster → Winchester); *-burh* (< *beorgan* "to protect," a fortified settlement, surviving as
-borough/-bury, e.g. Bebbanburh → Bamburgh); *-tun* (originally "enclosure," cf. Dutch *tuin*
"garden," German *Zaun* "fence," narrowing to "farm/estate," surviving as -ton, e.g. Hamtun →
(South)Hampton). p.19.

### Personal pronouns, including the dual number

Old English distinguishes more persons than Modern English: *þu* (singular "you," = "thou") vs. *ge*
(plural "you," = "ye"), and uniquely a **dual number** for exactly two people/things — *wit* "we
two" and *git* "you two" — alongside the ordinary singular/plural paradigm (1sg *ic*, 2sg *þu*, 3sg
*he/heo/hit*; 1du *wit*, 2du *git*; 1pl *we*, 2pl *ge*, 3pl *hi/hig*). The dual is illustrated with
the Genesis story of Joseph's two Egyptian prison-mates ("*wit gesawon swefn*," "we-two saw a
dream"). p.26–27.

### Survival of *ic* as dialectal "ich"

The first-person pronoun *ic* survived into early modern regional dialect as *ich* (vs. the
mainstream *I*), including contracted forms *'chill* (= *ich will*) and *'chould* (= *ich would*).
The book illustrates this with Edgar's rustic-dialect speech in Shakespeare's *King Lear* (Act IV,
scene 6) — a modern-literature citation used to show a direct historical throughline, not an Old
English text itself. p.27.

### Two present-tense paradigms of "to be"

Old English has two historically distinct verbs both supplying the present tense of "to be": a
wesan-based paradigm (*eom, eart, is, sind*) and a beon-based paradigm (*beo, bist, bið, beoð*),
presented here as coexisting variants with several further spelling variants noted (*sindon, synt,
sindan, ys, byð*). p.28.

### Present-tense verb conjugation (weak verb model)

General present-tense endings, modeled on *ræcan* "to reach": 1sg *-e* (ic ræce), 2sg *-(e)st* (þu
ræcst), 3sg *-(e)þ* (he/heo ræcþ), plural (all persons) *-aþ* (we/ge/hi recaþ). p.28.

### National and ethnic naming patterns

Regions/countries were typically named after the plural ethnonym of their inhabitants rather than
as an abstract place-name (*West-Seaxe* "West Saxons" for the region later called Wessex,
*Suð-Seaxe* "South Saxons" for Sussex, *East-Seaxe* for Essex, *Eastengle* "East Angles" for East
Anglia, *Norðhymbre* "Northumbrians" for Northumbria). A collective national self-designation
*Angelcynn* ("Angle-kin") was current from Alfred's time and still used with ethnic force by Cnut as
late as D 1017, but by the tenth century a new, more purely national/territorial name *Engla-land*
("Land of the English") had emerged and superseded it. Neighboring peoples/lands are named
similarly: *Wealhcynn/Wealas* (Welsh), *Mierce* "Borderers" (Mercians, hence *Mercia*/"March
Land"), *Scottas* (Scots), *Francland* (France), *Ealdseaxe* (Old Saxons/Saxony), *Dene* (Danes),
*Denemearc* ("Dane March," Denmark), *Norðweg* ("North Way," Norway — also found as a plural,
*Norðwegon* "into the Northways," retaining its literal topographic sense). p.28–29.

### Riddle genre conventions and alliterative verse form (Exeter Book)

Exeter Book riddles conventionally open with a formula like *ic wiht geseah* "I saw a creature" and
close with a direct challenge to the audience, *saga hwæt ic hatte* "say what I am called" (from
*hatan* "to call, be called"). The poetic line is organized into two half-lines (a-verse and
b-verse) separated by a caesura and linked to each other by alliteration (shared initial sounds),
with four stressed beats per full line — a far more important structural device in Old English
verse than end-rhyme, also used rhetorically in prose (charters, chronicles, laws, saints' lives).
Riddle 66's own consensus solution is *gesceaft* "creation." p.22–26.

### Compound-word decomposition as a vocabulary strategy

The book repeatedly models breaking an OE compound into its component morphemes to recover meaning
even when the whole has no direct Modern English descendant, e.g. *middangeard* = *middan* "middle"
+ *geard* "enclosure" = "world"; *hondwyrm* = *hand* + *wyrm* "worm, reptile" = "mite, insect."
p.24.

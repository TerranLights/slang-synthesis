## Grammar points

### 4.1 Personal pronouns

**4.1A Subject and object pronouns.** English pronouns track 1st/2nd/3rd grammatical person. A
sentence's subject must always be expressed (pronoun or noun) — unlike null-subject languages. *You*
is invariant for number. *It* identifies a referent's identity; *he/she/they* supply further
information once identity is established; *it* is also used when a baby/child's sex is unknown, p.54.

**4.1B Subject or object pronoun.** Informal/everyday English strongly prefers the object-pronoun
form in positions a prescriptive grammar would mark as subject position: after *be* ("It's me"), in
one-word answers to *Who?* ("Me," not "I"), and after *as/than* ("taller than me") — the formal
subject+verb alternative ("taller than I am") also exists, p.54.

**4.1C Gender with animals, things, and countries.** *It* is the default for animals/things, as if
they were objects. *He/she* (and *who*, not *that/which*) appear when there is a specific reason —
a named pet, interest in a "lower animal's" individual behavior, affectionate reference to a
vehicle/machine (*she*), or personification of a country as a world power (*she*), p.55.

### 4.2 'One'

**4.2A 'One' and 'you.'** Both mean "people in general," but register differs sharply: *one* (with
its possessive *one's* and reflexive *oneself*) is formal; *you* is the everyday, informal choice,
p.56.

**4.2B 'One'/'ones' replacing countable nouns.** An adjective alone cannot stand in for a dropped
singular countable noun in English — *one* (or *ones*, plural) must be added. Not used for
uncountable nouns, which are either repeated or dropped with no substitute word, pp.56–57.

**4.2C 'One(s)' after which/this/that + adjective.** *One/ones* appears after *which?*, after
*this/that* + adjective, and cannot be omitted in structures like "the one in the green dress."
It is typically omitted after a superlative or in a short answer, and after bare *these/those*
(without a following adjective), p.57.

### 4.3 'It' and one/some/any/none

**4.3A 'It' as empty or preparatory subject.** "Empty subject" *it* carries no real meaning (weather,
time, distance statements) but is grammatically required. "Preparatory subject" *it* stands in for
a postponed to-infinitive/-ing/that-clause subject, which is strongly preferred over fronting the
long clause itself, p.58.

**4.3B 'It'/'they' vs. 'one'/'some'/'none' as subjects and objects.** *It/they* refer to something
particular already identified; *one/some/none* refer to the same kind of thing in general,
unidentified terms. The particular/general distinction likewise governs whether a direct object
after verbs like *enjoy/like* is *it* (particular) or *some/any* (general), p.58.

**4.3C 'I hope/believe/expect so.'** A closed set of opinion/reporting verbs take *so* rather than
*it* as an affirmative short-answer complement; the negative can be formed either by negating the
main verb + *so*, or (for a smaller subset) by using *not* instead of *so*, p.59.

### 4.4 Possessive adjectives and possessive pronouns

**4.4A My vs. mine.** Possessive adjectives (my, your...) must precede a noun and refer to the
possessor, not the thing possessed (his own vs. someone else's, tracked entirely by which pronoun is
chosen, not by the noun). Possessive pronouns (mine, yours...) stand alone and never combine with
*the*, p.60.

**4.4B The double genitive.** "A friend of mine/yours" uses *of* + possessive pronoun (never *of* +
object pronoun); with a named possessor, *of* + the 's-genitive is used instead ("a friend of my
father's"). This construction is also a common vehicle for criticism when combined with *this/that*
("That boy of yours..."), p.60.

**4.4C 'My own' / 'of my own.'** *own* intensifies sole possession and follows a possessive
adjective, never a possessive pronoun; the equivalent postposed form is "[a noun] of my own," p.61.

**4.4D 'The' in place of a possessive.** *The* substitutes for the expected possessive adjective
with body parts and clothing after a preposition, and is used informally to refer to family members
("How are the children?") — though referring to one's spouse this way ("meet the wife") is flagged
by the source as a dated/clichéd usage to avoid, p.61.

### 4.5 Reflexive pronouns

**4.5A Verbs commonly/always followed by reflexives.** A small closed set of verbs (*absent oneself,
avail oneself of, pride oneself on*) are obligatorily reflexive in English. A larger set
(*amuse, blame, cut, dry, enjoy, hurt, introduce*) commonly take a reflexive pronoun but take an
ordinary object pronoun when the action targets someone else, p.62.

**4.5B Optional reflexive for emphasis.** Verbs like *dress, hide, shave, wash* can be used with or
without a reflexive pronoun; adding it emphasizes effort or achievement (especially relevant for a
child's developmental milestone). A separate set of verbs (*get up, sit down, wake up, get married*,
etc.) are not normally reflexive at all in English; adding a reflexive pronoun to these is a marked
choice purely for emphasis, p.62.

**4.5C Reflexives after prepositions and for emphasis.** Reflexive pronouns follow prepositions
("Look after yourself") and appear in fixed expressions ("strictly between ourselves"). By contrast,
ordinary object pronouns (not reflexives) are used for location ("money *on* you") and after *with*
meaning "accompanied by." *By* + reflexive means "alone/without help." A reflexive pronoun placed
after a noun/pronoun, or at the end of a clause, adds emphasis meaning "that person/thing and only
that one," p.63.

### 4.6 Demonstratives; some-/any-/no- compounds

**4.6A This/that.** *This/these* indicate proximity to the speaker (physical or situational);
*that/those* indicate non-proximity. Both are used flexibly depending on the immediate
conversational context, p.64.

**4.6B Some-/any-/no- compound distribution.** *Some*-compounds appear in affirmative statements,
in questions that expect "yes," and in offers/requests; *any*-compounds appear in negative
statements and in genuinely open/doubtful questions; *no*-compounds appear with an affirmative verb,
carrying the negation themselves, p.64.

**4.6C Singular or plural pronoun with everyone/anyone/no one.** The traditional prescriptive rule
uses masculine *he* generically (switching to *she* only for an all-female audience). The source
documents that, in actual practice as of 1990, English speakers instead use plural *they/them/their*
with sex-indefinite meaning for *anyone/everyone/no one*, explicitly noting this is the real usage
pattern even though "some native speakers think this is wrong," p.65.

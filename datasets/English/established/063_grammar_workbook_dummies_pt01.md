## Grammar points

### Three basic tenses: past, present, future (Ch.1)

Each of the three basic tenses has a plain form (named for its time designation) and a progressive
(*-ing*) form, which emphasizes ongoing process; present progressive can also reach into the future.
In many sentences plain and progressive are interchangeable. (p.9)

### Perfect tenses (Ch.1)

Present perfect (*has/have* + past participle) links a past action to an ongoing present state.
Past perfect (*had* + past participle) places one past event before another past event. Future
perfect (*will have* + past participle) implies a deadline in the future. Each also has a
progressive-perfect form for emphasizing an ongoing/spanning action. (pp.11–12)

### Irregular verbs and the "to be"/"to have" paradigm (Ch.1)

Irregular verbs deviate from the regular *-ed* past tense into a distinct past-participle form; no
general rule predicts them, and the book directs readers to a dictionary rather than attempting a
complete list. *To be* and *to have* are singled out as the two most frequent and most irregular
verbs, each with a full pronoun-conditioned paradigm (Table 1-1, reproduced in the Vocabulary table
above rather than as a literal table, per copyright discipline). (pp.12–14)

### Helping (auxiliary) verbs beyond *has/have/had/be* (Ch.1)

*Should*/*must* mark obligation; *can*/*could* mark ability (tense-matched to the main verb);
*may*/*might* mark possibility (with the prescriptive past/present distinction eroding in practice);
*would* marks condition, willingness, or repeated past action, with *will* as its present-tense
counterpart. (p.15)

### Plural noun formation (Ch.2)

Regular plurals add *-s*. Nouns ending in *s, sh, ch, x* add *-es* (unless already irregular).
Nouns ending in a vowel + *y* (*ay, ey, oy, uy*) add *-s* only. Nouns ending in a consonant + *y*
change *y*→*i* and add *-es*. Irregular nouns follow no rule (some don't change at all — *fish/fish*,
*deer/deer* — others change unpredictably — *leaf/leaves*, *child/children*) and must be checked in
a dictionary. Proper names are never altered for pluralization beyond adding a plain *-s*, even when
the name already ends in a consonant-*y* combination (*Smith*→*Smiths*, not a spelling change). (p.23)

### Subject-verb agreement in ordinary sentences (Ch.2)

Most English verbs are identical for singular and plural subjects, but several constructions require
matching: (1) present-tense third-person-singular verbs take *-s* — the reverse of the noun-pluralization
pattern; (2) *does*/*has* are singular, *do*/*have* are plural, **except** that the pronoun *I* — despite
being grammatically singular — pairs with *do*/*have*, not *does*/*has*; (3) *to be* has a full
person-conditioned paradigm (see Vocabulary); (4) two subjects joined by *and* form a plural subject;
(5) two singular subjects joined by *or* stay singular; (6) "interrupter" phrases (*of the books*,
*as well as*, *in addition to*, etc.) are grammatically outside the subject and never affect its
number, even when they appear to add a second item; (7) *here*/*there* can never themselves be the
grammatical subject of a sentence — look for the real subject after the verb; (8) the subject can
appear after the verb in inverted sentences, and the verb must still agree with it regardless of
word order. (pp.23–26)

### Difficult/indefinite subjects (Ch.2)

Pronouns built on *-one, -thing, -body* (*everyone, something, anybody*, etc.) are always singular
despite sounding like they could refer to many people. *All, some, most, none, any* vary: plural
when the referent is countable, singular when it's a measured mass that can't be individually
counted. *Either*/*neither* alone (without *or*/*nor*) are singular; in *either...or*/*neither...nor*
constructions, the verb instead agrees with whichever of the two subjects sits closer to it — a rule
that can flip the required verb form depending on which subject is named first or second in the
sentence. *Each* and *every* are always singular no matter what noun or list follows them. (pp.26–27)

### Pronoun number and gender (Ch.3)

Pronouns must match the number (singular/plural) of the noun they replace. Pronoun gender splits
into masculine, feminine, and gender-neutral/noncombatant (*it, itself, who, which, that*) sets. The
*-self* reflexive/intensive pronouns (*myself, himself*, etc.) have narrowly limited correct usage:
they either add emphasis to an already-named subject (*I myself will...*) or "circle back" to the
subject performing the action on itself (*She will clean herself*); using a *-self* pronoun as a
plain substitute for *I* or *me* in a compound subject/object (*"Rachel and myself hate..."*) is
flagged as a common error to avoid. (pp.35–36)

### Possessive pronouns vs. contractions (Ch.3)

A possessive pronoun never takes an apostrophe — an apostrophed form is always a contraction, never
a possessive. The three highest-risk confusable sets are *its*/*it's*, *their*/*there*/*they're*, and
*whose*/*who's* (see Vocabulary table for each pair's distinct meaning). (pp.37–38)

### Avoiding vague/double-meaning pronouns (Ch.3)

Core rule: if a pronoun's referent could plausibly be more than one noun in the sentence (a common
problem with sentences naming two people of the same gender, e.g. *"my aunt and her mother-in-law...
her success..."*), either rewrite to clarify which noun is meant or replace the pronoun with the
noun itself. The book flags this as a recurring pattern on standardized college-entrance exams.
(p.40)

### The four requirements of a complete sentence (Ch.4)

(1) At least one subject/verb pair is required (more than one is fine). (2) The sentence must
express a complete thought, independent of its length — a short sentence can be complete and a long
one incomplete. (3) Multiple ideas must be joined legally: either with a coordinating/subordinating
conjunction, or with a semicolon between two complete, related thoughts — never by simply running
them together (a run-on/"fused" sentence) or by placing a "false conjunction" (*nevertheless,
consequently, therefore, however, then*) alone between them without the semicolon-plus-comma
pattern those words require. (4) Every sentence ends with exactly one endmark (period, question
mark, or exclamation point). (pp.49–63)

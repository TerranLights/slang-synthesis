## Grammar points

### Slashes with dates (6.106)

Slashes (or periods or hyphens) may informally punctuate all-numeral dates (e.g. `3/10/02`), but
this should be avoided in formal publications because of ambiguity — Americans conventionally put
the month first, while other countries put the day first. Where an all-numeral format is required in
a formal context, CMOS recommends the ISO standard `YYYY-MM-DD` order instead.

### Slashes in abbreviations (6.107)

A slash can stand in for the word *per* (e.g. `110 km/sec`, `$450/week`) or replace periods in
certain fixed abbreviations, such as `c/o` ("in care of").

### Slashes as fraction bars (6.108)

A slash can mean "divided by" when a true horizontal fraction bar is unavailable or impractical;
single-glyph fraction characters (e.g. ½) are preferred over a slash-built fraction (`1/2`) when
available.

### Slashes to show line breaks in quoted poetry (6.109)

When two or more lines of poetry are quoted as part of running prose (rather than displayed on their
own lines), a slash with a space on each side marks where each line break falls.

### Slashes in URLs and other paths (6.110)

Slashes separate directories and file names in URLs and file paths; spaces are never used in such
strings. In typeset text, a line break may fall before a slash but never between two consecutive
slashes. Some operating systems use backslashes or colons instead of (or alongside) slashes.

### Quotation marks relative to other punctuation and text (6.111)

This section is itself a cross-reference index rather than new rule content: it points the reader to
the sections governing closing-quotation-mark placement relative to other punctuation (§6.9–11),
quotation marks with commas/colons/question marks/exclamation points (§6.50, 6.63, 6.70, 6.74),
quotation marks in dialogue (§13.9–10, 13.17–19, 13.28–47), quotation marks signaling special usage
of a single word or phrase (§7.55, 7.57–58), foreign-language quotation-mark conventions (French
§11.32–33, German §11.44, Italian §11.52, Spanish §11.79), and quotation marks with titles (examples
in chapter 8). No independent rule is stated here beyond the index itself.

### Typographer's or "smart" quotation marks (6.112)

Published work should use directional ("smart"/"curly") quotation marks matching the surrounding
typeface rather than the unidirectional "default" mark most keyboards and some software produce,
which (per CMOS) signals a lack of typographical sophistication in finished prose. Because
typewriter-era keyboard limitations and some software still default to the straight mark, authors,
editors, and typesetters must watch for and correct errors in either direction (the proper
left-vs-right mark must match context). The four directional Unicode codepoints are given explicitly:
left double U+201C, right double U+201D, left single U+2018, right single quotation mark/apostrophe
U+2019.

### Use of the apostrophe (6.113)

The apostrophe has three main functions: marking the possessive case, standing in for omitted
letters or numerals (contractions, clipped years like `'90s`), and — more rarely — forming the
plural of certain expressions. The section is itself largely a pointer to the fuller possessive
discussion (§7.15–28, 5.19), contractions (§7.29), and plurals (§7.5–14, especially §7.14).

### "Smart" apostrophes (6.114)

Published work should use the directional "smart" apostrophe, which appears raised (not inverted)
and shares its Unicode value (U+2019) with the right single quotation mark. Two common errors are
flagged: using the unidirectional "default" mark, and — more insidiously — software auto-turning an
apostrophe typed at the start of a word into a left single quotation mark instead of the correct
right-facing mark. CMOS gives worked examples: *We spent the '90s (not `'90s`) in thrall to our
gadgets* and *Where'd you get 'em (not `'em`)?* — in both cases the leading apostrophe must curl to
the right (U+2019) even though it begins the visible word, because it stands in for omitted digits
or letters, not as an opening mark.

### Apostrophes relative to other punctuation (6.115)

An apostrophe is treated as part of the word (or number) it attaches to and must not be confused
with a single closing quotation mark. When a word ends in an apostrophe, no period or comma should
intervene between the word and the apostrophe (CMOS's example: *The last car in the lot was the
Smiths'.*, where the period follows directly after the possessive apostrophe with nothing between).

### Likely combinations (6.116)

More than one punctuation mark at the same location typically involves quotation marks, em dashes,
parentheses, or brackets occurring alongside periods, commas, colons, semicolons, question marks, or
exclamation points. This section is a routing index to the specific combination rules elsewhere
(quotation marks §6.9–11, 6.50, 6.63, 6.70, 6.74; em dashes §6.86, 6.91; parentheses/brackets §6.82,
6.95–96, 6.99, 6.101, 6.123) and to the summary Table 6.1.

### Abbreviation-ending periods with other punctuation (6.117)

When a sentence ends in an expression that already ends with its own period (e.g. an abbreviation),
no second, sentence-ending period is added. If, however, some other mark of punctuation is required
immediately after that period, both the abbreviation's period and the additional mark appear
together. Examples given: *The study was funded by Mulvehill & Co.* (no doubled period) and *Johnson
et al., in* How to Survive*, describe such an ordeal.* (comma added after the abbreviating period).

### Periods with question marks or exclamation points (6.118)

A period (aside from an abbreviating period, per §6.117) never co-occurs with a question mark or
exclamation point at the same position — the stronger mark (question mark or exclamation point)
takes precedence and the period is dropped, even when the question mark or exclamation point is
itself part of a quoted title (e.g. a sentence ending in the title *Who's Afraid of Virginia Woolf?*
takes no additional period).

### Commas with question marks or exclamation points (6.119)

Where a quotation ends in a question mark or exclamation point at a position where a comma would
normally appear, the comma is simply omitted (e.g. *"Are you a doctor?" asked Mahmoud.*). However,
if the title of a work itself ends in a question mark or exclamation point and the surrounding
sentence's grammar would otherwise call for a comma there, the comma is still added, because title
punctuation is treated as syntactically independent from the sentence around it — CMOS notes this
reverses earlier editions' practice. Worked examples include a comma inserted directly after the
title *Help!* before a following title, and a comma after *Will You Please Be Quiet, Please?* before
continuing the sentence; CMOS notes the result can read awkwardly and may call for rewording.

### Question mark with exclamation point (6.120)

In the rare case where a quotation or title ending in a question mark or exclamation point is itself
embedded in a sentence that would also end in the other of the two marks, CMOS allows both marks to
appear together only if they differ from each other and the sentence's own punctuation seems
essential (e.g. *I just love* Who's Afraid of Virginia Woolf?*!*). Where the title's own mark already
suffices to punctuate the whole sentence, only that one mark is used (e.g. *Who starred opposite
Richard Burton in* Who's Afraid of Virginia Woolf?).

### Lists and outlines — general principles (6.121)

All items in a list should be constructed of grammatically parallel elements. Introductory numerals
or letters may be omitted unless they serve an actual purpose: indicating task order, suggesting
chronology or relative importance, facilitating later text references, or (in a run-in list)
clearly separating the items. Where similar lists appear close together in the same document,
consistent treatment across them is important.

### Run-in versus vertical lists (6.122)

Lists can either be run in to the surrounding text or displayed vertically in outline style. Short,
simple lists — especially where the introduction plus the items together form a complete
grammatical sentence — are usually better run in. Lists needing typographic prominence, that are
relatively long, or that contain multiple hierarchical levels should instead be set vertically.

### Run-in lists (6.123)

When numerals or letters mark the divisions of a run-in list, they are enclosed in parentheses;
letters used this way are sometimes italicized within roman parentheses. If the introductory
material is itself a complete grammatical sentence, a colon precedes the first parenthesized item.
List items are separated by commas unless any item itself requires an internal comma, in which case
all items are separated by semicolons instead; if any item consists of a complete sentence (or
several), the list should instead be set vertically (per §6.124). CMOS's worked examples include a
numbered run-in list after a colon (*…to illustrate analogous uses of (1) commas, (2) em dashes, and
(3) parentheses*) and a lettered, italicized run-in list separated by semicolons because an item
contains internal commas (*…(a) warm, sturdy outer clothing and enough underwear to last ten days;
(b) two pairs of boots,…*).

### Vertical lists — punctuation and format (6.124)

A vertical list is best introduced by a complete grammatical sentence followed by a colon (with an
exception noted at §6.125). List items carry no closing punctuation unless they themselves consist
of complete sentences. In a numbered vertical list, a period follows each numeral and each item
begins with a capital letter; alternatively, when the items are very long or multi-sentence and the
list doesn't need typographic prominence, items may instead be set as regular numbered paragraphs in
normal prose style, indented only on the first line. Short list items may be arranged in two or more
columns to avoid a long, narrow list. Runover (wrapped) lines are usually indented flush-and-hang
style (as in a bibliography), aligned with the first word following the numeral or bullet; an
alternative is to add extra space between items instead of indenting. CMOS gives several worked
formats: an unnumbered vertical list of required application documents, a two-column list of
evaluation criteria, a numbered list of three sentence-composition tasks, a numbered list of
clock-adjustment steps, and a bulleted list of printer control-panel settings.

### Vertical lists punctuated as a sentence (6.125)

When a numbered vertical list completes a sentence begun in its own introductory lead-in, and its
items are phrases or sentences carrying internal punctuation, semicolons may separate the items and
a period follows the final item; each item begins with a lowercase letter, and a conjunction (*and*
or *or*) before the final item is optional. Such a list, which is often actually better run in to
the text per §6.122, should only be set vertically if the context genuinely calls for visual
highlighting. CMOS's worked example: a lead-in sentence ("Reporting for the Development Committee,
Jobson reported that") followed by three lowercase, semicolon-separated items ending in a period,
with *and* before the final item. The same punctuation and capitalization would apply if bullets
were substituted for numerals.

### Vertical lists with subdivided items — outlines (6.126)

Where items in a numbered vertical list are themselves subdivided, both numerals and letters may be
used together; any runover lines align with the first word following the numeral. CMOS's first
worked example shows a three-item numbered list (Punctuation / Spelling / Syntax) each subdivided
into lettered sub-items. For outlines with many hierarchical levels, CMOS recommends distinguishing
all levels by both punctuation style and indentation — its worked example runs seven levels deep:
capital roman numerals (`I.`), capital letters (`A.`), arabic numerals (`1.`), lowercase letters in
single parentheses (`a)`), arabic numerals in double parentheses (`(1)`), lowercase letters in double
parentheses (`(a)`), and lowercase roman numerals followed by a parenthesis (`i)`) — with numerals
aligned vertically on their last digit. In a list with fewer levels, simpler conventions (e.g.
starting directly with arabic numerals, skipping capital roman numerals) are acceptable; what matters
is that the reader can see each item's hierarchical level at a glance. Each division and subdivision
should normally contain at least two items (a single sub-item under a heading is generally avoided).

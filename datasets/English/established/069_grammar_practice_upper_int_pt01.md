## Grammar points

### Countable and uncountable nouns

Uncountable nouns in English cluster into recognizable semantic classes rather than being
arbitrary: substances/materials/liquids/gases, activities and sports, academic subject names,
language names, abstract nouns, and collective nouns (p.1). Many nouns that are generally
uncountable can also be used countably in a specific sense, and the book catalogs five distinct
sub-patterns for this shift (pp.3): (1) substance vs. one specific item of it (*chicken/a chicken*,
*light/a light*); (2) material vs. an item made from it, where the countable sense has a distinct
referent (*glass* the material vs. *a glass* the drinking vessel; *iron* vs. *an iron* the
appliance); (3) uncountable vs. "a variety/type of" (*wine/an excellent wine*, *cheese/a strong
cheese*); (4) informal countable use when ordering food/drink in a café or restaurant (*tea/three
teas*); (5) some *-ing* nouns can be countable (*painting/a good painting*).

### The indefinite article: a or an

Choice between *a* and *an* is governed by the initial **sound**, not the initial letter — hence
*a uniform*, *a European* (both start with the /juː/ sound) but *an MP*, *an L-plate* (both start
with a vowel sound when the letter name is spoken) (p.4). The indefinite article is used only
before singular countable nouns, and specifically: when referring to one non-specific thing ("I'd
like a banana"); on first mention of something (with *the* used on second mention); after *to be*
and *have got* when describing someone/something including their job; with units of measurement
(*£2 a kilo*); and in exclamations ("What a lovely day!") (pp.4–5).

### The definite article: the

*The* is used before both countable and uncountable nouns when referring to something specific —
identifiable from sentence content, from being mentioned a second time, from shared
speaker/listener knowledge of a particular referent, when there is only one of the thing in
existence (*the sun*, *the world*), before some collective nouns referring to a whole group
including nationalities/political parties (*the British*, *the Conservatives*, *the police*,
*the government*), before some adjectives used without a noun to mean the group in general (*the
rich*, *the unemployed*), before parts of the day, before ship/newspaper/magazine/some musical
group names, before superlatives and ordinals, before musical instruments when talking about
playing them, and before certain geographic categories: seas, rivers, groups of islands,
mountain ranges, deserts, regions, and a short list of individual country names (*the Netherlands*,
*the Sudan*, *the United Arab Emirates*, *the Philippines*, *the Bahamas*) (pp.5–6).

### The zero article (no article)

No article is used before plural nouns and singular uncountable nouns in a general sense (p.6). The
book presents this as a 2×3 grid crossing definite/indefinite use against
singular-countable/singular-uncountable/plural-noun categories — *the* covers all three definite
cells, *a/an* covers only singular-countable indefinite, and zero article covers both uncountable
and plural indefinite. Additional zero-article contexts: proper nouns, meal names (*Have you had
breakfast yet?* — but *That was a lovely breakfast* when describing it as an event),
institution-type nouns used for their normal function (*school, class, college, university, home,
work, church, bed, hospital, prison, town* — contrasted with *There isn't a hospital in the town*,
where the institution is referred to as a building), *by* + mode of transport, and *next/last* +
time expressions (p.6).

### Attributive and predicative adjectives

An adjective before a noun is attributive; one separated from the noun, following a linking verb
(*be, become, get, seem, appear, feel, smell, look, sound, taste, make* + person, *keep, stay,
grow, turn*), is predicative and functions as a complement rather than an object (p.9). A small set
of adjectives shift meaning between the two positions (*an old friend* = long-standing vs.
*Margaret's quite old* = aged; *a heavy sleeper* = sleeps very soundly vs. *really heavy* = weighs
a lot; *his late father* = deceased vs. *nearly always late* = tardy). Separate closed classes of
adjectives are restricted to only one position: predicative-only covers health terms, the *a-*
prefix class, and several feelings-words; attributive-only covers a short list of
noun-qualifying adjectives (*chief, main, only, particular, principal, sole*) (p.9).

### Order of adjectives

When multiple attributive adjectives precede a noun, English imposes a conventional ordering
across seven semantic slots: *What is it like? → How big? → How old? → What shape? → What colour?
→ Where was it made/What is it made from/What type is it? → Noun* (p.10). Within the "What is it
like?" category specifically, order is usually free unless one adjective is more general, in which
case the more general one comes first (*a beautiful, thought-provoking film*). Comma use follows a
distinct rule from ordering: commas separate attributive adjectives only when they are of equal
importance/emphasis within the same category; *and* can also join two attributive adjectives of
the "what is it like" type, and is required (with commas before it) when joining two or more
predicative adjectives (p.10).

### Construction of adjectives: participles, suffixes, prefixes

Present and past participles frequently function as adjectives (*an exciting trip*, *frozen
vegetables*), and many compound adjectives are formed from *well* + past participle (*well-built
house*) or other adverb + participle combinations (*fast-selling product*, *home-grown
vegetables*) (p.12). Four common adjective-forming suffixes are catalogued with their semantic
contribution: *-able/-ible* "able to be," *-ful* "full of/having," *-less* "being without," *-ive*
"tending to/having the nature of" (p.13). Six negative prefixes are catalogued — *dis-, un-, il-,
im-, in-, ir-* — each selected by the phonological shape of the base adjective (*il-* before
*l*-initial bases, *im-* before *p/m*-initial bases, *ir-* before *r*-initial bases) (p.15).

### Non-gradable adjectives

A non-gradable adjective cannot be qualified by degree words like *very, too, enough* because its
meaning is already strictly/maximally defined (*weekly, unconscious, dead, legal, medical, empty,
full*) or already carries an extreme/absolute meaning (*wonderful, perfect, terrible, astonished,
delicious, amazing, hilarious*). For the second subclass, strength can still be added, but only
with *absolutely* or *really*, not *very* (p.15).

### Comparison of adjectives

A five-row paradigm table governs comparative/superlative formation by syllable count and ending:
one-syllable adjectives take *-er/-est*; two-syllable adjectives ending in *-y* take *-ier/-iest*;
other two-syllable adjectives and all adjectives of three-plus syllables take periphrastic
*more/most*; a short closed set is irregular (*good/bad/far*) (p.16). Supplementary notes (p.17):
*less* and *least* form the periphrastic negative comparative/superlative, and *least* also appears
in the fixed idiom "not in the least" (= not at all); spelling changes for *-er/-est* follow the
same single-vowel+single-consonant doubling rule as other suffixation, plus a separate "drop final
*e*" rule; adjectives with a negative prefix compare the same way as their base form, though
periphrastic comparison (*more dishonest*) is noted as more natural in practice than the suffixed
form (*dishonester*); after *than* + personal pronoun, formal English requires subject pronoun +
auxiliary/clause, but informal English accepts a bare object pronoun (*she works harder than me*);
a comparative doesn't require an explicit *than*-phrase when the comparison is recoverable from
context; *as...as* expresses similarity (frequently negated: *not as strong as*); two comparatives
joined by *and* express continuing increase (*bigger and bigger*); and comparatives can be
intensified with *a bit, a little, a little bit, quite a bit, a lot, much, far, even, hardly, any,
no*.

### Adverbs and adverbial phrases: types

Six semantic classes of adverb/adverbial phrase are distinguished: manner, place (location and
direction), time, frequency, degree, and sentence adverbs (modifying the whole clause, typically
expressing certainty or the speaker's opinion) (p.20). The book separately flags that adverbs do
not always modify verbs — they can modify adjectives, nouns, other adverbs, phrases, and whole
sentences — and names several adverb subtypes by function rather than meaning: interrogative
adverbs (*when, where, why*), intensifiers (*very, really*), phrasal-verb particles (*lie down*),
and focus adverbs (*even, only, just, simply*), which precede the word they qualify (p.20).

### Position of adverbs

Adverbs of manner, place, and time default to post-verb(-object) position, in that fixed relative
order when more than one co-occurs ("manner, place, time") (pp.21–22). All three types can instead
open the sentence for emphasis (*Slowly, the door started to open*); manner adverbs and adverbs of
*indefinite* time can also go mid-sentence, between subject and verb (*Ben stupidly went out
without locking the door*) — but this mid-position is specifically disallowed for *definite* time
adverbials (*NOT "I last month changed my job"*) (p.21). Adverbs of definite frequency (*once,
twice, daily, on Saturdays, again*) default to end position but can also open the sentence; adverbs
of indefinite frequency (*always, usually, sometimes, often, never*) go after an auxiliary or *to
be* and before a full verb (*I am usually in bed...*; *I have always wanted...*). A wider list of
frequency-type adverbs (*frequently, generally, normally, occasionally, ordinarily, sometimes,
usually, quite/very often, always/never* in imperatives) can additionally open the sentence for
special emphasis. Degree adverbs normally precede the word they modify; sentence adverbs can open
the sentence or go before the verb (after *be*/an auxiliary) (p.22).

### Comparison of adverbs

Only gradable adverbs take comparative/superlative forms; a closed set (*sometimes, never, there,
now, almost*) cannot be modified at all. Formation parallels the adjective system: *-ly* adverbs
(and a few others) take periphrastic *more/most*; a small set share their base form with the
adjective and take *-er/-est* (*fast, hard, early*); and a short irregular set exists (*badly,
well, little, much*) (p.24). Supplementary notes (p.24): adverbial comparison can also use *(not)
as...as*; *the* + comparative adverb/adjective doubled across a clause expresses correlated
increase (*The harder I try, the worse I seem to do*); two comparative adverbs joined by *and*
express continuing increase; and superlative adverbs are rare in practice — comparative + *than
ever/anyone/anything* is the more idiomatic way to express an extreme ("You can do the job better
than anyone can").

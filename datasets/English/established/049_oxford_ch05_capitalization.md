Chapter 5, "Capitalization," pp. 95–106 (PDF pp. 112–124). Full chapter, §§5.1 through 5.17.

**Coverage note:** This chunk covers the entire chapter — every numbered section (5.1 General
principles; 5.2 Sentence capitals; 5.3 Use to indicate specific references, incl. 5.3.1 Use to
create proper names and 5.3.2 Formal and informal references; 5.4 Institutions, organizations, and

Legislation and official documents; 5.9 Honours and awards; 5.10 Titles of office, rank, and

Words derived from proper nouns; 5.15 Trade names; 5.16 Ships, aircraft, and vehicles; 5.17 Names
including a number or letter) — nothing in the page range is outside this chapter's scope. This is
English's first extraction from the *New Oxford Style Manual*, and its first British-English style
reference (English's prior grammar-reference extractions are all from the American *Chicago Manual
of Style*, 16th ed., 2010). Like the CMOS Chapter 8 "Names and Terms" extraction
(`021_cmos_ch08_names_terms_pt01.md` onward), this is a style/usage reference rather than a
vocabulary-teaching text — its "vocabulary" is the specific illustrative terms/example pairs the
source uses to demonstrate each capitalization rule, tabulated below (grouped by rule) rather than a
conventional taught-vocabulary list. This chapter naturally covers much of the same conceptual
ground as CMOS Ch. 8 (titles, proper names, personifications, places) but from an independently
authored, British-English-style perspective with its own example set and several rules CMOS doesn't
state the same way (e.g. the British-vs-US colon-capitalization split at §5.2, the "down style" is
implicit rather than its own section, *river*/*State*/*Church* abstract-vs-specific tests at §5.3,
the ship-prefix/"serve in not on" convention at §5.16) — not re-extracted or treated as redundant,
per the extraction spec's "a same-topic chapter from a third, independently authored source is not
redundant by default" guidance. Per dispatch instruction, Attested Era is set to "contemporary
(source published 2016)" and Attested Region to "British English (United Kingdom)" throughout — a
book-level attribution, not a per-word claim beyond what the source supports — except where a single
entry explicitly notes a British-vs-American contrast, which is flagged in that entry's own Notes.
The source has a genuine clean text layer; no vision-reading was required and no handwritten
marginalia was present. One likely UTF-8-as-Latin-1-mojibake candidate was checked for (the known

## Grammar points

### 5.1 General principles

Capital letters in English serve several distinct functions: punctuating sentences, distinguishing
proper nouns from ordinary words, marking emphasis, and setting off headings and work titles. The
chapter explicitly disclaims any possibility of absolute rules for every case — like hyphenation,
capitalization choice often comes down to a word's role in its specific sentence, a writer's own
taste, or house style, and certain disciplines (the source names history specifically) maintain their
own particular conventions. The recommended editorial practice is to agree on governing principles
before heavy editing begins, build a style sheet of the specific choices made for a given work, and
apply it consistently; editors should defer to an author's established capitalization choices except
where the author is internally inconsistent. Separately, the chapter notes that heavy capitalization
in digital contexts (emails, forums) reads as "shouting" and is better avoided in favor of other
emphasis devices such as color on a website.

### 5.2 Sentence capitals

The first letter of a sentence — or of a set of words functioning as one — is capitalized, as is the
first letter of a syntactically complete quoted sentence (quotation marks or not). A quoted single
word or phrase that doesn't itself form a complete sentence is not capitalized at its start. A
distinct British-vs-American split is given explicitly: in British style, text following a colon
begins with a lower-case letter unless it is a displayed quotation or extract, whereas US style
permits (though doesn't require) a capital after a colon that introduces a complete sentence.

### 5.3 Use to indicate specific references

#### 5.3.1 Use to create proper names

Initial capitals signal to the reader how to interpret a word — ordinary proper names generally stay
recognizable even if mistakenly lower-cased, but when a proper name is built from otherwise-ordinary
common nouns and qualifiers, the capital is what distinguishes the specific usage from a general
descriptive one (e.g. *the National Gallery* vs. *the national gallery*, *the City* vs. *the city of
London*). The same mechanism marks an abstract or specialized sense of an otherwise-ordinary word:
*Church* for a denomination vs. *church* for a generic building; *State* for the abstract/legal
sense or a specific named US state vs. lower case for states in general; *Left*/*Right* for political
affiliation (though capitalization isn't strictly required if context already disambiguates). The
chapter flags that some disciplines (named example: historians) deliberately minimize capitals on
institutional references as a stylistic norm of the field — a choice editors should respect rather
than impose or override unilaterally — while cautioning that overuse of initial capitals elsewhere is
obtrusive and can itself create false distinctions the writer didn't intend. Capitals are also noted
as a device for deliberate humorous effect in fiction, conveying a self-important or childish
narrative voice.

#### 5.3.2 Formal and informal references

After a capitalized compound tied to a proper name has been introduced in full, the usual practice on
subsequent reference is to revert to a lower-case generic form (*Cambridge University* → *their
university*). A different, more formal convention instead keeps the capital on a short-form mention
of a previously spelled-out title (*the Ministry*, *the College silver*) — found particularly in
formal documents — and whichever rule a work adopts, the chapter stresses strict consistency across
the whole piece is more important than which specific rule is chosen. Separately, when one generic
term serves two or more proper names in combination, the plural generic form is lower case, since the
plural itself isn't part of either individual proper name (*Lake Erie and Lake Huron* → *lakes Erie
and Huron*).

### 5.4 Institutions, organizations, and movements

Names of institutions, organizations, societies, movements, and groups are capitalized, including the
generic term within the name of a cultural movement/school derived from a proper noun (*the Oxford
Movement*) — though the leading *the* itself is not capitalized. Otherwise, the general tendency is
toward lower case unless capitalization is needed to preserve a genuine distinction in meaning: the
same root can take either case depending on whether it names a specific historical/organized sense
(*the Confederacy*, *Romantic* as an arts-movement label) or a general descriptive sense
(*confederacy*, *romantic*). Certain disciplines and specialist contexts (the source's example:
classicists capitalizing *Classical* for a specific period of ancient sculpture) maintain their own
treatment, which editors should not override without consulting the author.

### 5.5 Geographical locations and buildings

Names of geographical regions/areas, named astronomical and topographical features, buildings, and
other constructions are capitalized. *The earth*, *the sun*, *the moon* are exceptions kept lower
case except in technical astronomical contexts or personification. *River*, *sea*, and *ocean* are
generally capitalized when they follow the specific name (*the Yellow River*) but lower case when
used only as a descriptive identifier rather than part of the true name (*the Danube river*); when
*river* precedes the specific name, either case is acceptable depending on house style (*the River
Tamar* or *the river Tamar*). *The River Plate* is always capitalized, as a fixed conventional
mistranslation of the Spanish *Río de la Plata* ("Silver River"). Well-known or previously mentioned
rivers may drop the specifying word (*the Amazon*), with a lower-case identifier reintroduced only
where needed for disambiguation. Compass directions are capitalized only when they denote a
recognized political or cultural entity (*Northern Ireland*) rather than a plain geographical sense
(*northern England*); usage here is described as genuinely fluid and context-dependent — a book
focused in detail on a locality may routinely capitalize a compass-direction-plus-place phrase that a
passing mention elsewhere would leave lower case. Adjectives ending in *-ern* are sometimes used
specifically to mark the purely geographical sense as distinct from the political/cultural one (*East
Africa* vs. *eastern Africa*).

### 5.6 Dates and periods

Names of days, months, festivals, and holidays are capitalized; season names stay lower case except
when personified. Historical periods and geological time scales are capitalized (*the Bronze Age*,
*the Middle Ages*), though the adjectival form for the same era can differ (*the medieval period*,
lower case). Modern-period names are more likely to stay lower case (*the jazz age*, *the belle
époque*) and the source recommends checking specific instances against the *New Oxford Dictionary for
Writers and Editors* rather than inferring from the older-period pattern. Millennia, centuries, and
decades are lower case throughout (*the sixteenth century*, *the sixties*).

### 5.7 Events

Formal names of wars, treaties, councils, assemblies, exhibitions, conferences, and competitions
generally take initial capitals; the chapter gives a long illustrative (not exhaustive) list spanning
very different kinds of events, from *the Crucifixion* to *the Boston Tea Party* to *the One Thousand
Guineas*. *War* and equivalent terms (*campaign*, *conflict*) are capitalized only when they form
part of the conventional formal name of a specific conflict, and lower case for a looser, more
descriptive designation of the same kind of event (*the Peninsular campaign*, *the Korean conflict*).
After a conflict's first full-name mention, later generic references to *the war* revert to lower
case.

### 5.8 Legislation and official documents

Names of laws and official documents are generally capitalized (*the Declaration of Independence*,
*the Corn Laws*). *Act* is conventionally capitalized even in non-specific, generic reference to "the
Act" (anaphoric to one already named), while *bill* stays lower case unless it is itself part of a
formal name (*the Bill of Rights*).

### 5.9 Honours and awards

Full formal names of orders of chivalry, state awards, medals, degrees, and prizes are usually
capitalized, as are (in most styles) the specific ranks/grades of such an award (*Dame Commander of
the Order of the British Empire*) — contrasted with a generic award description like *an Olympic gold
medal*, which stays lower case. Honours belonging to a non-English-speaking country usually keep an
initial capital on the honour's own name while setting the rank term itself in lower case, whether the
rank is translated into English or left in its original language (*grand officer*/*grand officier of
the Légion d'honneur*).

### 5.10 Titles of office, rank, and relationship

Titles and ranks are generally lower case except when used immediately before a name, as a
stand-alone name-substitute, or in direct address (*the prime minister* vs. *Prime Minister Winston
Churchill*; *Good evening, General!*). A set of unique compound titles with no meaningful non-specific
sense (*Chancellor of the Exchequer*, *Prince of Wales*, *Dalai Lama*) are treated as exceptions,
capitalized in every context. Separately, specific references to the actual current holder of a rank
or title are often capitalized regardless of their syntactic position (*a letter from the Prime
Minister*) — a pattern the chapter notes can create difficulty with consistency wherever titles recur
frequently, for which it recommends falling back on the simpler syntactic rule (capitalize only
immediately before the name). It is customary, though not universal across house styles, to
capitalize *the Pope* and the reigning monarch (*the King*/*the Queen*), and, when referring
specifically to Muhammad, *the Prophet* (contrasted with the lower-case generic *an Old Testament
prophet*). Historians again are noted as often applying minimal capitalization to titled subjects
(*the duke of Somerset*), a style the chapter warns "can be distracting in works for a general
readership." Possessive pronouns are capitalized only when they form part of the title of a holy
person or a sovereign/dignitary (*Her Majesty*, *Your Holiness*); personal pronouns referring to the
sovereign (*We*, *Us*, *Our*) are capitalized only in formal proclamations. Family-relationship words
are lower case except when used as a name or in direct address (*his dad* vs. *Hello, Dad!*).

### 5.11 Religious names and terms

All references to the monotheistic deity are capitalized (*God*, *Allah*, *the Holy Spirit*,
*Jehovah*). Pronouns referring to God are lower case where the reference is clear, unless an author
specifically directs otherwise; the relative pronouns *who*, *whom*, *whose* are always lower case
regardless. Compounds built on *God* generally keep the capital (*God-fearing*), with the specific
conventional exception of *godforsaken*. Gods and goddesses of polytheistic religions are lower case
(*the Aztec god of war*). Capitalization of specific religious sacraments or rites is explicitly
flagged as non-uniform across religions and contexts: the chapter lists several that stay lower case
(*a mass*, *baptism*, *compline*, *bar mitzvah*) alongside others capitalized (*the Mass*, *the
Eucharist*, *Anointing of the Sick*) without resolving the contrast into one governing rule.

### 5.12 Personification

Personified entities and abstract concepts are capitalized in the sentences where the personification
occurs (*O Freedom...*; *If the Sun and Moon should doubt...*). Ships and other craft are traditionally
treated as grammatically female (referred to as "she"). This same convention — treating a nation or
city as "she" in prose — was formerly standard for referring to countries and cities but is now
considered old-fashioned; the impersonal pronoun is the modern default (*Britain decimalized its, not
her, currency*), though the older device still survives in poetic and literary writing. Characters in
a play identified by their occupation rather than a proper name are capitalized in stage directions
and text references to those directions (though in dialogue-attribution text itself such names would
typically appear in small capitals rather than full capitals).

### 5.13 People and languages

Adjectives and nouns denoting a place, a language, or an indigenous people are capitalized
(*American*, *Swahili*, *Aboriginal*). Related verbs usually retain the capital (*Americanize*,
*Hellenize*), with *anglicize* and *westernize* as the chapter's noted common exceptions that stay
lower case. As a general rule, nationality-derived adjectives are capitalized where the link to the
specific place/nationality is still felt to be close (*Irish setter*, *Turkish delight*) and lower
case where the association has become remote or merely allusive (*venetian blinds*, *morocco
leather*). The chapter is explicit that this is only a general tendency with real exceptions
(*Arabic numbers*, *French kissing*, *Roman numerals*) that any doubtful case should be checked
against a dictionary rather than inferred from the pattern alone. It also notes that many other
European languages, unlike English, lower-case their own nationality adjectives (English *French* =
French *français* = German *französisch*).

### 5.14 Words derived from proper nouns

A word derived from a personal name or other proper noun is capitalized in contexts where the link to
that name still feels alive (*Dickensian*, *Orwellian*) and lower case where the association has
become remote, merely allusive, or a matter of pure convention (*quixotic*, *pasteurize*). Some words
of this kind take either case depending on which specific sense is meant — a literal/historical sense
capitalized, a broadened figurative sense lower case (*Bohemian* the central-European people vs.
*bohemian* the unconventional-lifestyle adjective; similarly *Philistine*/*philistine*,
*Platonic*/*platonic*, *Stoic*/*stoic*). The capital of a proper-noun base is retained after a prefix
and hyphen (*pro-Nazi*, *non-Catholic*). Scientific units and poetic metres derived from personal
names are conventionally lower case (*watt*, *newton*, *sapphics*), while in compound terms naming
scientific laws or phenomena, only the personal-name element itself is capitalized (*Planck's law*,
*Halley's comet*); the chapter notes that in medical texts specifically, eponymic disease-name
derivations may be downcased even on the base form (*Parkinson's disease*, but *parkinsonism*,
*parkinsonian*).

### 5.15 Trade names

General proprietary/trade names are capitalized (*Velcro*, *Google*, *Kleenex*). Verbs derived from a
trade name are given a lower-case initial if used at all (*to skype*, *to google*), though the chapter
recommends preferring a non-trademarked generic term or verb where one is available (*a VoIP service*,
*do an online search*) over using the trademarked term as a generic verb in the first place. Company
and proprietary names that use unconventional internal capitalization (*iPad*, *GlaxoSmithKline*,
*PricewaterhouseCoopers*) should simply be reproduced in the form the company itself uses.

### 5.16 Ships, aircraft, and vehicles

Names of ships and vehicles are capitalized; an individual craft's own specific name is set in italics
(*the Cutty Sark*, *HMS Hood*), but a type, model, or marque name is not (*a Boeing 747 Jumbo Jet*, *a
Mini Cooper S*). Two further usage conventions are flagged specifically for ships: people are said to
serve *in* a ship, not *on* one, and the definite article is dropped when a ship-prefix abbreviation
(HMS, RRS, SS, and similar) is used together with the ship's name (*He went aboard HMS Victory*, not
*the HMS Victory*).

### 5.17 Names including a number or letter

Names that incorporate a number or a letter are conventionally capitalized (*Route 66*, *Flight 17*,
*Room 101*, *Act I*).

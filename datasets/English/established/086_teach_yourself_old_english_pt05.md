## Grammar points

### The inflected infinitive after þencan "to intend"

The "to + infinitive + -enne" construction already met in Units 11–12 recurs after verbs of
intention like *þencan*: *hi ðohton hyne to ofsleane* "they thought/intended to slay him" — the
inflected infinitive expressing purpose rather than mere description. p.144.

### Introduction to the subjunctive mood

The subjunctive expresses wish, conjecture, hypothesis, or anticipation, contrasting with the
indicative's plain assertion. Modern English retains the subjunctive only in a few fixed/formal
expressions ("long live the king," "if I were you"), but it was far more systematically used in Old
English. The present subjunctive is often recognizable by the *lack* of the *-ð* ending the
indicative has (indicative *gæð* "goes" → subjunctive *ga*/*ge-ga*; plural subjunctive *gan*). The
past subjunctive is harder to spot, especially in late spelling, but one diagnostic is the ending
*-e*/*-en* (indicative past *com, comon* "came" → subjunctive *come, comen*). Irregular verbs have
their own anomalous subjunctive forms (indicative *bið* "is" → subjunctive *beo*; indicative-past
*wæs* → past-subjunctive *wære*). The subjunctive also follows a conjunction of time expressing
anticipation (*ær ðam þe he him to come*, "before he had reached them" — an event anticipated, not
yet realized at the time of speaking). p.147–148.

### The ā-, be-, and of- prefixes on verbs

Besides perfective *ge-* (Unit 4), three further verbal prefixes are introduced, all weakly stressed
(main stress stays on the verb's root): **ā-** (pronounced long "ah") usually adds perfective force,
"a sense of completion" (*geofan* "give" → *ageofan* "deliver"; *fysan* "drive" → *afysan* "drive
away"); **of-** (pronounced "off-") similarly has perfective force, often with violent/final sense
(*ofgifan* "give up," *ofsendan* "send for," *ofslean* "strike down, slay," *ofsniðan* "cut down,
kill"); **be-** is the most semantically varied and the only one of the three still productive in
Modern English (*besiege, benight, betake, bestraddle* — several of these combine the old English
prefix with a later French-imported word). *Be-* has (at least) three distinct functions: (1) a
general "round about" sense (*lucan* "lock" → *belucan* "enclose, lock up"; *sittan* "sit" →
*besittan* "besiege"; *standan* → *bestandan* "stand round"; *windan* → *bewindan* "wind round,
encircle" — the last cited from *The Dream of the Rood*'s "leohte bewunden," "encircled with
light"); (2) converting an intransitive verb into a transitive one (*wepan* "weep," intransitive →
*bewepan* "bewail," transitive, taking an object — cf. ModE "bewail"); (3) adding a sense of
"depriving" (*dælan* "distribute" → *bedælan* "deprive"; *niman* "take" → *beniman* "take away" —
cf. ModE "bereft"). A worked example from the Joseph narrative: *bereafodon* "they deprived" is
built on *be-* + *reafian*, where *se reaf* also means "garment" — so the word is literally
"be-reaved" the brother of his coat. p.148–151.

### Metaphorical language: giving/taking construed via the body

Old English regularly expresses abstract ideas of giving, taking, and receiving through metaphors of
the hand/body, much as Modern English does (*on dæge* "in [one's] lifetime" as a metaphorical use of
"day"; *handa gan* "to come into the hands of"; *unnendre handa* "with a giving hand," *unnendre
heortan* "with a giving heart" — both strong-adjective dative examples). Comparable biblical
examples use "hands" to mean captivity (*generian... of heora handum*, "rescue... from their hands")
versus innocence/guilt (*ure handa beon unbesmitene*, "our hands may be untarnished"). p.151.

### Strong adjective endings (predicative and attributive, without a determiner)

When an adjective stands without a preceding determiner (*se/þæt/þes* etc.), it takes a distinct set
of "strong" endings marking case/gender/number — e.g. masculine accusative *-ne* (*ealne
middangeard*), neuter genitive *-a* (*tyn pundan reodes goldes*), feminine dative *-re* (*unnendre
heortan*), nominative plural *-e* (*ealle his eorlas*), genitive plural *-ra* (*ðreora manna dæg*),
dative plural *-um* (*sibban oððe fremdan*, contracted from the full ending). The same strong
endings also appear predicatively, after a form of "to be" (*brymmas syndan Cristes myccle*,
"Christ's glories are great"). The practical reading-oriented advice given: mentally subtract the
adjective ending before consulting a dictionary, since headwords are listed in the uninflected base
form. p.162–163.

### Syllable structure and marking rhythm (onset-vowel-coda)

To analyze Old English's characteristic stressed rhythm, the book introduces basic syllable
structure (onset + vowel + coda) and demonstrates it on *com* "came" (one syllable: c-o-m) vs. its
past-subjunctive counterpart *come* (two syllables, unevenly stressed: CO-me). Capital letters or
stress marks (/ for stressed, x for unstressed) are used to notate the rhythm of formulaic phrases
already met in Units 11/13 (*to HABbanne and to SELlanne*; *un-for-BO-den and un-be-SA-can*),
reinforcing that the main stress in a prefixed word falls on the root, not the prefix. p.163.

### The impersonal pronoun man/mon

A generic/impersonal subject pronoun, spelled either *man* or *mon*, roughly comparable to German
"man" (*man spricht deutsch* "German [is] spoken") — broader in application than Modern English
"one," since it covers both the generic-statement sense and contexts better rendered as a passive in
Modern English translation (*þa geutlagode man Æþelweard ealdorman*, translatable either actively
"then they outlawed ealdorman Æthelweard" or passively "then ealdorman Æthelweard was outlawed").
p.170.

### Word order in gif/þonne clauses, and mood choice (subjunctive vs. indicative)

A verb governed by *gif* "if" or *þonne* "when" goes to the end of its clause, as already seen with
other subordinators. Crucially, mood is meaningful here: a hypothetical/uncertain condition takes
the subjunctive (*ðonne þu fisc habban wylle*, "when/if you want to have fish" — hypothetical, not a
stated fact), while an expected/more certain condition can take the indicative. The conjunction
*swilce* "as if" is always followed by the subjunctive, since it is inherently hypothetical. A
worked contrast from *Wulf and Eadwacer* shows subjunctive *gife* ("as if a present were given," pure
hypothesis) against indicative *cymeð* ("if he comes," the expected/likely course of events) within
the same short passage. p.170–171.

### Verbs taking a genitive object

A class of verbs takes its object in the genitive case where accusative might be expected (already
introduced with *gyrnan* in Unit 8): *beþurfan* "need," *brucan* "enjoy," *neodian* "require,"
*þyrstan* "thirst for," *wenan* "expect," and *genodian/genyodigan* "need" (a near-synonym pair used
interchangeably in the Monastic Signs text). A worked monastic-sign example: *gif þe disces
beþurfe* "if you need a dish," with *disces* in the genitive. p.172.

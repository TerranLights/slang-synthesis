## Grammar points

### The five principal ME dialect areas

Evidence of OE writing supports three or four dialects (West Saxon, Kentish, and Anglian, itself
splittable into Northumbrian and Mercian); by the ME period the Mercian Midlands area shows enough
internal east/west difference to count as two separate dialects, giving **five** recognized ME
dialect areas: **Southern**, **Kentish** (SE England), **East Midlands**, **West Midlands**, and
**Northern**. The Northern dialect of ME as spoken in southern Scotland was called *Inglis* until
about 1500, when writers began calling it *Scottis* — the ancestor of the term Scots. (p.59, Section
4.1)

### No ME standard; prestige, education, and legal-language shifts across the period

There was no single ME dialect used for writing throughout the country — the language of the Norman
ruling class was itself specifically **Parisian** French, which carried more prestige in the 12th-c.
royal court than Anglo-Norman or other French varieties (the book notes Chaucer's own ironic 1390s
jab at a character's provincial "Frenssh ... After the scole of Stratford-atte-Bowe," i.e. London-area
French, not "correct" Parisian French). French remained the medium of English schooling until the
second half of the 14th century (John of Trevisa, writing in 1385, records this changing within his
own lifetime); English entered law-court use after 1362 and Parliament by the century's end; and by
the century's close the educated London dialect was becoming the emerging *de facto* standard of
writing, though no single recognized Standard English was established for several more centuries.
(p.60, Section 4.1)

### A checklist for describing/comparing ME dialect differences

Dialects of a single language remain **mutually comprehensible**; where an unfamiliar dialect becomes
genuinely difficult to follow, the obstacle is usually its distinctive vocabulary or an unfamiliar
pronunciation/accent, not its grammar, since dialects of one language share the great bulk of their
grammar and vocabulary. The book sets out a working checklist, used repeatedly through the rest of the
book, for pinning down specifically **what** differs between two dialect samples: (1) **Spelling** —
the alphabetic symbols used and how they map onto the dialect's actual sounds, remembering there is no
guaranteed one-to-one correspondence between letter and sound; (2) **Pronunciation**, inferred
indirectly from spelling since no direct audio evidence exists for any pre-recording-era English; (3)
**Word forms and inflections** — which suffixes/prefixes are used, and which have already been lost;
(4) **Grammar** — word order within phrase and clause, negative and question formation, and any
construction type no longer used in MnE; and (5) **Vocabulary** — the proportional mix of OE-, ON-,
and OF-derived (or other-language-derived) words, which can itself be dialectally diagnostic (e.g.
heavy ON content marking a former-Danelaw text). (p.63, Section 4.2)

### Grammatical features of Trevisa's 1385 SW Midlands dialect

Analyzing Trevisa's prose (Text 29/30) against this checklist, the book highlights several points
where ME grammar differs from MnE in ways that are matters of underlying **structure**, not just
style: genitive-type phrases such as *meny maner people* and *þre maner speche* would require the
preposition *of* in MnE (*many kinds of people*, *three varieties of speech*) because ME could still
form this relationship without it, closer to an OE-style genitive construction; infinitives
complementing a main verb are regularly marked with **for to** rather than plain *to* (e.g.
*compelled for to leue*, *fondeþ ... for to speke*), a construction that survived into some MnE
dialects but is now non-standard in Standard English; and the text already shows a **preposition
stranded at the end of a clause** (e.g. ... *construe here lessons and here þinges a freynsch, and
habbeþ suþþe þe normans come furst into engelond* — glossed by the author's commentary as predating
by centuries the later prescriptive-grammarian rule against "ending a sentence with a preposition,"
i.e. confirming the construction was never actually ungrammatical in natural English, only later
stigmatized). (p.65, Section 4.4)

### The *Ayenbite of Inwyt* as a uniquely reliable single-dialect source, and Kentish's conservatism

The *Ayenbite of Inwyt* ('the remorse of conscience') is explicitly flagged as an unusually valuable
ME source because its single surviving manuscript names both its author (Michael/"Dan Michelis" of
Northgate, a monk of St Augustine's, Canterbury) and its exact completion date (27 October 1340) in
its own colophon, and is spelled with unusual internal consistency — so, unlike most ME texts (which
survive only as later copies of unknown date/place/authorship, or show spelling inconsistency even
within one manuscript), it gives unusually trustworthy evidence for exactly one dialect at exactly one
moment. Comparing its grammar against other, contemporary Southern dialects, the book identifies
Kentish as comparatively **conservative**: it retains more OE inflectional features (the
dative-case *-e* noun ending; the 3rd-singular-present verb inflection *-(e)þ*; the past-participle
prefix *y-* < OE *ge-*) even though it has, like every other ME dialect, lost a great deal of the full
OE inflectional system. The author suggests Kent's relative geographic isolation (cut off from the
Midlands and North, but not easily accessible to the rest of the South either) as a plausible
explanation for this conservatism. (pp.68–69, Sections 4.5–4.5.1)

### OE negation, the impersonal construction, and structures already extinct by 1340

Commentary on the Kentish text (Text 31–32) repeats and reinforces constructions already flagged in
Chapter 3 as lost from MnE grammar even though the vocabulary they use survives unchanged: the OE/ME
dative **impersonal construction** without an overt subject (e.g. *his moder nes naȝt þer*, 'his
mother ne-was not there' with *ne* as the pre-verbal negator) and the retained **pre-verbal negative
particle** *ne*, which in ME is regularly reinforced by a second negative word (*noȝt*/*nawiȝt*) rather
than standing alone — i.e. the double negative remained entirely standard ME grammar, not an error,
matching the Chapter 3 finding for *The Fox and the Wolf*. (p.67, Section 4.5.1)

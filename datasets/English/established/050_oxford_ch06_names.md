# English — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: New Oxford Style Manual, Ch. 6, "Names" (§§6.1–6.2.4: References to People through Addresses)

**Source:** *New Hart's Rules* (Part One of the *New Oxford Style Manual*), Oxford University Press,
2016 edition, Chapter 6, "Names," printed pp. 107–126 (PDF pp. 125–144): §6.1 References to People
(§§6.1.1–6.1.9, covering personal-name forms, alternative names, identifiers, titles, peerage, saints'
names, Welsh/Scottish/Irish names, name prefixes, and foreign-name conventions for Arabic, Chinese,
French, German, Greek, Hebrew/Jewish, Italian, Japanese, Korean, Portuguese, Russian, Scandinavian,
and Spanish names) and the opening of §6.2 Place Names (§§6.2.1–6.2.4: general principles, chronology,
locating places, and addresses). The remainder of the chapter (§6.2.4's European addresses in more
depth, and any material past printed p. 126) falls outside this page range and belongs to a later part.

**Coverage note:** every grammar point and every distinct example form in this page range, grouped by
the source's own rule/subsection rather than listed as isolated atomic vocabulary (this is a style/
usage reference illustrating capitalization, punctuation, and naming conventions through proper-name
examples, not a vocabulary-teaching text — same extraction approach used for this project's CMOS
chapter extractions). Repeated restatements of the same rule with different proper names are
consolidated into one row rather than duplicated. This is British English's first extraction from this
specific Oxford source; it substantially overlaps in subject matter with English's existing CMOS Ch. 8
("Names and Terms") extractions (`021`–`028`), but per the extraction spec's "non-redundant supplement"
guidance, a same-topic chapter from a third, independently-authored source is not redundant by
default — this source is British (CMOS is American) and covers genuinely different ground in several
places: the UK peerage system, British-specific identifier/title punctuation conventions, Welsh
patronymic particles, a Korean-naming subsection CMOS's covered range didn't include, Scandinavian
patronymic naming, and British vs. American postal-address conventions. No independent verification
against the CMOS files was performed row-by-row; this file stands on its own and a future cross-check
pass could consolidate overlaps if useful. The source has a genuine text layer (`pdftotext` extraction
on this range is largely clean), so this chunk was extractable as ordinary print; no handwritten
marginalia was present. **Gotcha note (not mojibake, but adjacent):** raw `pdftotext` output on this
range shows two minor text-layer artifacts distinct from the project's documented UTF-8-as-Latin-1
mojibake pattern — an occasional diacritic silently dropped (e.g. *père* extracts as "pere") and an
isolated parenthesis-to-curly-brace substitution ("(Jun., Jnr..." extracting as "{Jun., Jnr..."). Both
were cross-checked and corrected against the clearly legible printed page images themselves (this
extraction was performed by reading the Read tool's rendered page images directly, which show accurate
typeset accents/punctuation), so no entry below is affected or needed a `low_confidence` flag. Per
dispatch instruction, Attested Era is "contemporary (source published 2016)" and Attested Region
"British English (United Kingdom)" throughout, **except** entries that explicitly contrast with
American/North American usage, which are tagged accordingly in their own row. Vision Reading Confidence
is `n/a` throughout (real text layer; the image-rendering read method above is this project's standard
PDF-reading path, not a vision-read of a scan).

## Grammar points

### 6.1.1 References to people — general principles

Use the name form a person is most commonly known by or prefers to use, not necessarily a fuller
"proper" form (J. K. Rowling, not Joanne Rowling). A name or title acquired later in life — by
marriage, honour, or pseudonym — is only clarified parenthetically when needed to avoid confusion or
make a point, not as a matter of routine. Initials before a surname are conventionally pointed with a
space between them (*J. S. Bach*), though some styles (especially newspapers and scientific
publications) omit both points and spaces (*JRR Tolkien*). Names given entirely in initials normally
keep points but drop spaces (*J.R.R.T.*); when people are commonly known by free-standing initials,
those drop both points and spaces (*FDR*, *JFK*).

### 6.1.2 Alternative names

Four distinct categories, each with its own convention: an **adopted name** (used for all purposes,
sometimes legally, glossed "born"/"née(e)"/"formerly"); **né/née** (French "born," used in English
almost exclusively for a married woman's maiden name, not italicized, never combined with pseudonyms/
aliases/nicknames); a **pseudonym** (adopted for a specific purpose such as a pen or stage name); and a
**nickname** (formally *soubriquet*), which can supplement or eventually fully supplant the owner's
original name. Quotation marks around a nickname are conventional when it's inserted within or
precedes the real name, but dropped once the nickname is used alone. Nicknames and other familiar terms
of address are capitalized.

### 6.1.3 Identifiers

*Junior*/*senior* differentiate a son and father sharing a name. British usage favors *Jun.*, rarely
used and without a preceding comma; American usage favors *Jr*, with a point and usually a preceding
comma. Both precede any abbreviations for degrees, honours, or scholarly affiliations. Unlike *junior*,
*senior* never forms part of the bearer's actual name — it's an ad hoc disambiguator, normally spelled
out with a lower-case initial. French *fils*/*père* serve the same disambiguating purpose and are
italicized in English.

### 6.1.4 Titles

Titles following a name are comma-separated. All-capital honours abbreviations (*MBE*, *DFC*) take no
points in modern style; mixed-case abbreviations (*B.Sc.*, *Ph.D.*) traditionally took points but are
now usually rendered without them (*BSc*, *PhD*), as are *Mr*, *Mrs*, *Dr* in British style (contrast
US usage, cross-referenced to §21.4.4). *Mr*, *Mrs*, *Miss*, *Ms*, *Dr* are never combined with each
other or with any other title; *Esq.* (now rare) is never combined with *Mr* and, when present, comes
before all subsequently following titles. A few fixed title combinations take no comma (*His Grace the
Archbishop of Armagh*). Orders/decorations/degrees/fellowships are normally used in text only at first
mention, in proportion to the subject's relevance, with a long formal style (*HRH The Prince of Wales,
KG, KT, ...*) typically simplified in practice (*the Prince of Wales*).

### 6.1.5 Peers and courtesy titles

The UK peerage has five grades for men (duke, marquess — not *marquis* — earl, viscount, baron) and
five parallel grades for women (duchess, marchioness, countess, viscountess, baroness), whether held in
a woman's own right or via a peer husband. *Count* is explicitly not a British title. *Dame* (the
female equivalent of a Chivalric-order knighthood) always takes a forename. *Lady* covers female peers,
peers' female relatives, and knights' wives/widows, used with or without forename+surname depending on
the specific title. *Lord* is the formal title of a baron and an informal way to refer to a marquess/
earl/viscount (prefixed to a family or territorial name), and also a courtesy title for a duke's or
marquess's younger son — but dukes themselves are never called *Lord*, only *the Duke of —*. *Sir*
marks baronets and knights; *the Hon.* is a courtesy title for an earl's younger son and all children of
a viscount or baron; *Master*/*Mistress* is the courtesy title of heirs of Scottish peerages.

### 6.1.6 Saints' names

English "saint" is abbreviated *St* in British usage (no point; US retains the point, cross-referenced
to §21.4.4), capitalized and unhyphenated except in a few personal names (*St-John*). Other languages
mark a systematic distinction between the saint as a toponym/institution/family-name/feast-day
(capitalized, often hyphenated) and the saint as a person (lower case): French *Saint-Étienne* vs.
*saint Jean*; comparable gendered/positional forms exist in German (*Sankt*/*hl.*), Italian (*San*/
*Santo*/*Santa*/*Sant'*, varying by following-sound and gender), Portuguese (*São*/*Santo*/*Santa*),
and Spanish (*San*/*Santo*/*Santa*). Abbreviated saint-derived names alphabetize under the full "Saint"
form, not under the abbreviation or the following letter.

### 6.1.7 Welsh, Scottish, and Irish names

Welsh surnames only became standard from the 17th century; before that, patronymic particles *ap*
(before a consonant) / *ab* (before a vowel) meant "son of," and *ferch* meant "daughter of," sometimes
doubled to name both father and grandfather. Alphabetization is normally by personal name, or
sometimes by the particle (as in Welsh phone books) — never by the particle's capitalized antecedent.
Irish *O'* ("grandson of") takes an English closing-quote-apostrophe form (*O'Brien*) or a more
authentic Gaelic capital-*Ó*-plus-space form (*Ó Cathasaigh*); women's surnames use *Ní*/*Nic*. *Mac*
("son of") has wide English spelling variation (*MacDonald*, *McDonald*, *M'Donald*) resting on
individual/family custom rather than any single correct Gaelic-derived rule — all such variants
alphabetize as *Mac* regardless of spelling, and existing spelling variants within a text should be left
alone absent evidence of genuine inconsistency.

### 6.1.8 Names containing prefixes

A family of particles — *de*, *de la*, *du*, *le*/*la*, *van* (and compounds), *von* (and compounds) —
each carries its own capitalization, retention-when-alone, and alphabetization-basis rules that differ
by language and sometimes by country within a language (French vs. Dutch vs. Belgian vs. Italian vs.
Spanish *de*; Dutch vs. Belgian *van*; German vs. Swiss *von*). The general governing principle is to
follow the bearer's own known preference where ascertainable, and to supply cross-references within an
alphabetized listing where the convention is genuinely ambiguous.

### 6.1.9 Foreign names

A per-language survey of naming conventions, honorific forms, and alphabetization rules for Arabic,
Chinese, French, German, Greek, Hebrew/Jewish, Italian, Japanese, Korean, Portuguese, Russian,
Scandinavian, and Spanish names — see the Vocabulary table above for the specific forms and rules under
each language's own subsection. Recurring cross-language threads: (1) honorific-title forms (Mr/Mrs/
Miss-equivalent) nearly every language surveyed has its own abbreviated set; (2) patronymic naming
(father's-name-derived middle or family elements) recurs independently in Arabic, Hebrew, Russian,
Scandinavian, and Welsh naming (§6.1.7) — a cross-linguistically recurring, independently-arising
naming strategy, not a borrowing between these traditions; (3) which name element is alphabetization-
forming is never assumed, and is handled explicitly per language.

### 6.2.1 Place names — general principles

Choosing a place name's spelling is often genuinely contested; the modern tendency favors local-
language forms but this is far from a universal rule (writers still say Rome/Munich, not Roma/München).
Current Oxford-preferred spellings for a list of commonly-contested foreign cities are given (e.g.
*Beijing* not *Peking*, *Marseilles* not *Marseille*). Several politically/historically sensitive naming
distinctions are flagged explicitly: *Britain*/*Great Britain*/*the United Kingdom*/*the British Isles*
each have a distinct precise referent; *America* should mean "the United States" only when
unambiguous; several Indian cities have official post-1995 renamings (Mumbai, Chennai, Kolkata,
Bengaluru); *Belarus* (not *Belorussia* or *White Russia*); Chinese place names generally follow Pinyin
in contemporary contexts (*Beijing*, *Guangzhou*) though the old form (*Canton*) may suit historical
contexts; and the two Congos (Democratic Republic of Congo/Kinshasa vs. Republic of Congo/Brazzaville)
require care. A descriptive geographic term already present within a name in another language (*meer*
"lake" in *IJsselmeer*) should not be redundantly re-added in English.

### 6.2.2 Chronology

When a place's official language has changed historically, the name used may need identifying or
glossing (*Breslau*, now *Wrocław*) according to the subject's historical period and intended
readership, kept internally consistent for a given region. Critically, when the place name *itself*
(not merely its linguistic form) has changed, the old name should be used for historical reference
rather than retrospectively substituted — e.g. the Battle of *Stalingrad*, not "Volgograd," even though
the city has been Volgograd since 1961; the same applies when an old name is later restored (the
*Siege of Leningrad*, not "St Petersburg," since the city reverted to St Petersburg only afterward),
though a "(now St Petersburg)" gloss may be added for readers who need it.

### 6.2.3 Locating places

A small or ambiguous place name may be clarified by reference to a larger place, county, surrounding
area, or country, but only when context doesn't already make the reference unambiguous — over-
specifying (e.g. "Paris, France" on the off-chance of confusion with Paris, Texas) is called out as
unnecessary. A key caution: avoid anachronism — when writing about a historical period, locate a place
using the geographic/administrative terms accurate to that period (Hampstead "near London," not "in
north London," for a 19th-century reference), and don't silently "correct" a historical administrative
location to match a later boundary change (Abingdon moved from Berkshire to Oxfordshire only in 1974).

### 6.2.4 Addresses

UK run-on addresses are comma-separated (the comma before the postcode is optional), with commas
dropped entirely when the address is laid out on separate lines; there is no comma after a street
number; postcodes are unpointed capitals, conventionally set in small capitals, with care to distinguish
the letter O from the digit 0. US addresses append a five-digit zip code (optionally extended by a
further hyphenated four digits) after the state abbreviation, and Manhattan addresses conventionally
spell out avenue-name numbers while using figures for street-name numbers. Canadian addresses place the
postal code on its own final line, with the province abbreviation sharing the town/city line.
Continental European conventions vary further: French/Belgian street-type words (*rue*, *avenue*,
*boulevard*) are lower-case, with the definite article also lower-case except sentence-initially, and
street-number placement itself differs between France (before the name) and Belgium (after); German,
Dutch, and Swedish typically fuse the "road"/"street" word directly into the street name, sidestepping
the capitalization question; and Italian/Spanish street names are lower-case-initial while Portuguese
street names are upper-case-initial, with numbers following the name in all three.

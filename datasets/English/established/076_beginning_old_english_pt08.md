# English — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: *Beginning Old English*, Text B (Beowulf, lines 710–836)

**Source:** Carole Hough and John Corbett, *Beginning Old English* (Palgrave Macmillan, 2007),
pp. 152–168 (Text B: *Beowulf*, lines 710–836, fitts 11–12, the Grendel-fight climax, in original
unsimplified poetry).

**Coverage note:** the fullest and most heavily-glossed OE poetry excerpt in the book — covers the
complete scene from Grendel's approach to Heorot through his mortal wounding and flight, leaving his
severed arm. Every grammar/poetic point (litotes, circumlocution/variation applied to both hero and
monster, the ambiguous compound *ealu-scerwen*, Dative-of-agent ambiguity in religious reference,
echoing formulas across the passage) and every distinct vocabulary item from the per-section glosses
and the cumulative end-of-text glossary is covered. As in `076_beginning_old_english_pt07.md`,
recurring closed-class function words already established in earlier files are consolidated into one
reference list rather than given individual rows; new content vocabulary (nouns, verbs, adjectives,
compounds, proper names) gets full treatment. The poem itself is represented only through the
source's own interlinear glosses and short illustrative half-line quotations used in its discussion,
not reproduced as continuous verse, per copyright discipline — though note this is Old English
primary-source poetry (long out of copyright in itself); the discipline here applies to the
textbook's own selection, glossing, and discussion apparatus around it. Continues from
`076_beginning_old_english_pt07.md` (Part II Introduction + Text A).

**Source text layer:** clean, same fixed cipher as pt01–pt07 (þ→ƒ, ð→ê, Þ→∏; decoded throughout and
reverified — this text in particular mixes þ and ð genuinely within itself, consistent with the
Part II spelling note in pt07). Vision Reading Confidence `n/a` throughout. **Textual-transmission
note from the source itself (p.152):** the unique Beowulf manuscript was damaged in the 1731 Cotton
library fire, so some readings in this passage are editorially supplied from pre-fire transcripts
rather than directly legible in the manuscript today — this is the source's own textual-criticism
caveat, not a PDF-extraction issue.

## Grammar points

### Litotes: ironic understatement as a poetic device (p.153)

Two consecutive understated statements ('that was not the first time he had sought Hrothgar's home';
'never...did he find hall-retainers with a harsher outcome') are flagged as **litotes** — stating
something indirectly/negatively for ironic effect, since the reader already knows Grendel's raids
have continued for 12 years and that this particular night ends in his death. A named, reusable
rhetorical-device term worth cross-referencing against Chapter 6's catalogue of poetic techniques
(circumlocution, variation, kennings, formulas) — litotes functions as a closely related but
distinct device, built on negation/understatement specifically.

### Deliberate ambivalence in naming Grendel vs. Beowulf (p.154, 160–161)

A sustained technique across this whole passage: the poet avoids using either character's proper
name for long stretches, instead applying overlapping/ambivalent epithets to both — *rinc* 'warrior'
describes both Grendel and the sleeping men; *āglǣca* 'fierce one/enemy' is elsewhere in the poem
applied to Beowulf himself as well as to Grendel; *ren-weardas* 'hall-guardians' and *heaðo-dēorum*
'those bold in battle' describe the *pair* of them fighting, collapsing the man/monster distinction
mid-combat. This is presented as a deliberate narrative strategy maintaining uncertainty about
whether Grendel is "man or monster," reinforced by concrete evidence pointing both ways (human
attributes like *drēamum bedǣled* 'deprived of joys' vs. monstrous ones like eye-light "most like
fire").

### A genuinely unresolved textual crux: *ealu-scerwen* (p.158–159)

Worked as a case study in real interpretive uncertainty (paralleling Chapter 6's *Wulf and
Eadwacer* discussion): the compound's two elements each support two different senses (*ealu* =
'ale' or 'good fortune'; the second element = 'dispensation' or 'deprivation'), yielding readings as
divergent as 'a serving of ale' (communal joy) or 'a deprivation of good fortune' (dread) — the
source offers 'a dispensation of sweet/bitter ale' as a working compromise translation rather than a
confident resolution.

### Variation applied to inanimate subjects: Heorot as an actor (p.158)

Extending Chapter 6's "variation" device beyond people: the poet briefly shifts focus from the
combatants to the hall itself, describing it with its own epithets (*wīn-sele*, *fæger fold-bold*)
and noting the dramatic irony that only fire — foreshadowing Heorot's eventual historical burning
elsewhere in the wider poem — could destroy a structure that withstood this battle's violence.

### Dative-of-agent ambiguity in religious framing (p.163)

*Gūþ-hrēþ gyfeðe* 'victory in battle was granted' uses a Dative-marked recipient (*Bēowulfe*)
without specifying an agent — the source flags this as a recurring, probably deliberate ambiguity in
the poem about whether victory comes from God or from impersonal fate (*wyrd*), consistent with the
poem's broader blending of pagan heroic-code values and Christian framing already discussed in
Chapter 7.

### Echoing formulas linking separate moments of the passage (p.161)

The source traces two specific formula-echoes across the passage, worth noting as a worked example
of Chapter 6's "formula" device operating *within* a single continuous scene rather than only across
separate poems: the phrase 'at that time in this life' describing Beowulf's prowess (line 790) is
echoed in describing Grendel's death (line 806); and 'if they could' (797b, of Beowulf's men) echoes
'if he could' (762b, of Grendel) — small verbal rhymes reinforcing the symmetry between the two
combatants' situations.

## Grammar points

### 47. Present Continuous + time word
Present continuous (§29) plus an explicit or contextually understood future time word expresses a
pre-arranged plan for a specific future time (*I'm arriving next week*). The source stresses this is
not restricted to a "near" future — *He's coming back in ten years* is given as grammatical. A time
word/expression is required (or must be recoverable from context) to force the future reading. (p.56)

### 48. *going to*
Three distinct uses: (a) a planned future action, closely paralleling §47; (b) a future outcome
inferable from present physical evidence (*Look at those clouds. It's going to rain*); (c) a neutral
statement of future fact, where it functions as a near-synonym of future simple (§50). (p.58)

### 49. *going to* / Present Continuous + time word — contrast
Often interchangeable; *going to* is favored in conversation and in cases of doubt about which to
use. With the verbs *go* and *come* specifically, present continuous is preferred over *going to*.
Present continuous + time word is generally reserved for plans fixed to a particular time. (p.60)

### 50. Future Simple
*will/shall* (contracted *'ll*; negative *won't*) + bare infinitive, person-invariant. *shall* is
now mostly restricted to first-person offer/suggestion questions (*Shall I carry your suitcase?*).
Uses: a statement of future fact (certain or uncertain); a sudden, in-the-moment decision (*No
one's offered to help? I'll do it for you!*); and willingness, promise, or threat (*I'll never speak
to him again*). (p.61)

### 51. *going to*-future plan vs. *will*-sudden decision — contrast
*going to* signals a plan already decided before the moment of speaking; *will* signals a decision
made at the very moment of speaking (*How can we get to the airport? I know! I'll borrow Sue's
car!*). (p.63)

### 52. *going to* and *will* — consolidated contrast table
The source provides its most systematic cross-tabulation in the book so far: *going to* alone for
a pre-decided plan or present-evidence-based prediction; *will* alone for willingness, a sudden
decision, or an offer/suggestion; either form (with *will* "more common") for a neutral future fact,
first-conditional clauses (cross-referenced to §58), and *when/as soon as*-type clauses (cross-
referenced to §53). This table is the chapter's own explicit summary of how the two future forms
divide the semantic space established piecemeal across §47–51. (p.64)

### 53. *when* + Present Simple to describe the future
After *when, as soon as, before, after, if/unless* (and similarly *until*), the time/conditional
clause itself takes present simple even though it refers to future time; the main clause takes
future simple (*When I see him, I'll phone you* — not *When I'll see him...*). Present perfect can
substitute in the subordinate clause for a completed-before-the-main-event reading (*I'll speak to
you when I've finished*); *going to* or an imperative can substitute for future simple in the main
clause where appropriate. (p.65)

### 54. Irregular verbs
Irregular verbs are conventionally tabulated in three columns (infinitive / past simple / past
participle) rather than following the regular *-ed* rule; see Vocabulary table above for sample
forms and the cross-reference back to §36's smaller in-context set. (p.67–69)

### 55. The Passive: Present Simple and Past Simple
Formed with *to be* (in the appropriate tense) + past participle, demoting or omitting the original
subject (*Someone washes the car every week* → *The car is washed every week*). Two licensing
conditions given: the agent is unknown (*My briefcase was stolen last night* — thief unidentified),
or the agent is unimportant/predictable from context (*These televisions are made in Japan* — who
specifically makes them doesn't matter). (p.71–74)

### 56. Verb formations: *used to*
*used to* + bare infinitive marks a regularly-recurring past action or state that no longer holds
(*She used to work for the BBC* = she doesn't now) — this "no longer true" entailment is itself part
of the construction's meaning, not merely implied by context. Negative: *didn't use to* + infinitive;
question: *did* + subject + *use to* + infinitive; both forms are flagged as rare in actual use. A
structurally similar but semantically distinct construction, *be used to* + gerund, instead marks
present accustomedness/habituation (*I'm used to working at night* = this is now normal for me) —
the source explicitly contrasts the two as a near-minimal pair worth not confusing. (p.75–76)

### 57. The Imperative
Form identical to the bare infinitive, invariant for singular/plural (understood second person,
*you*); negative formed by prefixing *don't*. *Let's* (negative *let's not*) is the corresponding
first-person-plural ("we") imperative. Pragmatically marked as not inherently polite — the source
recommends *Could I have...?* or *Would you...?* for polite requests instead — and notes the bare
imperative is typically reserved for people one knows well, for a speaker in a position of
authority, or for addressing children or soldiers. (p.77–78)

## Grammar points

### Linking clauses: coordination, parataxis, hypotaxis
OE links clauses by three distinct means. **Coordination** joins two independent clauses with a
coordinating conjunction (OE deploys a far wider range of these than just "and"). **Parataxis**
sits halfway between coordination and true subordination: a main clause and a logically-dependent
clause are juxtaposed with no overt subordinating signal, but the second clause's subject is
covertly identical to the first's (often translating naturally into a PDE present-participle
clause, e.g. "came eight thieves, wanted to steal..." → "...wanting to steal..."). **Hypotaxis**
is true overt subordination, frequently reinforced by **correlation** — paired correlative
elements (*þā... þā* "when... then") linking the subordinate and main clause explicitly, with
verb-final order in the subordinate clause distinguishing it from the verb-second main clause even
though both use the same word *þā*.

### Relative clauses and complementisers
Neither PDE's *who* nor *that* has a direct OE ancestor. Instead OE uses either the indeclinable
**relative particle** *þe*, or the ordinary demonstrative pronoun (*se, þæt, sēo*) used relatively —
the latter risks ambiguity with a plain demonstrative reading, resolved by the compound
**pronoun + particle** sequence (*se þe*). **Relative attraction** can pull the relative pronoun
into the case of its *antecedent* rather than its own clause-internal function. Occasionally no
relative element appears at all — a **contact clause**, structurally identical to the informal PDE
"the book [that] I read".

### Negation: verb-second order, negative concord, and cliticization
*Ne* behaves like other clause-initial adverbials in triggering **verb-second order**. OE's most
distinctive negation feature is **negative concord**: once a clause is negated, every eligible
indefinite pronoun/quantifier in it also takes a negative form (*ne mētte hē ǣr nān ġebūn land*,
lit. "not found he before no occupied land") — explicitly *not* a double negative cancelling
itself (the prescriptive-era misreading of the surviving non-standard PDE construction *he didn't
buy nothing*, which the author notes is a direct structural descendant of this OE pattern, not an
error). **Negative contraction** cliticizes *ne* onto an immediately following verb beginning with
/w/, /h/, or a vowel, but only for a specific closed set of verbs (*wesan, willan, witan, habban,
āgan*) sharing that phonological shape — crucially *not* for *weorðan* or *bēon*-forms beginning
with /b/, even though they're semantically parallel to *wesan*.

### Four sources of OE vocabulary
The chapter frames OE vocabulary formation under four headings: **native core vocabulary**;
**affixation** (prefix/suffix attachment to an existing word); **compounding** (joining two
independent words into a third); and **borrowing/loan words**. Affixation and compounding together
are **word-formation**. In sharp contrast to PDE (where loan words are a very large share of the
lexicon — roughly a third French, further large shares Scandinavian and other sources), OE drew
only an estimated ~3% of its vocabulary from non-native sources, overwhelmingly preferring to
build new words from native material — the single most Germanic-typical feature of OE vocabulary
as a whole.

### Older, now largely unproductive derivation types
Several Indo-European-inherited/early-Germanic word-formation processes survive as recognizable but
synchronically frozen patterns in OE: **Ablaut-derived nouns** (a strong verb's root at a
particular Ablaut grade used directly as a noun, e.g. *bite*/*bītan*), nouns derived from verbs
while showing the effects of **Verner's Law** and/or **i-mutation** in ways that obscure the
verb-noun relationship (*cyre*/*cēosan*, *cyme*/*cuman*). **Conversion** (zero-derivation,
*full*↔*fyllan*) is proposed as possibly a living continuation of this same older, no-longer-
productive pattern. By contrast, noun derivation from **weak class 1 or class 2 verbs** (*dōm*
from *dēman*) remains fully active throughout the OE period, and the reverse direction — **forming
new weak class 2 verbs from nouns** (*lufian* from *lufu*) — becomes the single most productive
OE derivational pattern, active enough to apply even to freshly-borrowed Latin nouns.

### Affixation: prefixes and suffixes
Prefixes were originally **inseparable with nouns but separable with verbs** in early Germanic (a
distinction preserved today as a live contrast in Dutch and German, where a separable verbal
prefix detaches and moves to clause-final position); OE shows some residual stress-based evidence
of the same split (*ándsàca* "enemy" vs. *onsácan* "deny", stress position differing exactly as
predicted). *Ġe-* is overwhelmingly the single commonest OE affix (see Vocabulary). Several
suffixes/suffixoids form productive derivational families — dialectal variation even shows up here
(*-estre* vs. *-iċġe* for feminine agent nouns, an explicit West-Saxon-vs.-northern split the
source itself marks).

### Compounding: a defining OE feature
Compounding is **far more extensive in OE than in any later period of English** (notwithstanding a
real rise in compound use over the last century) — the text cites *Beowulf*'s 3,182 lines
containing 903 distinct compounds, roughly one new compound every third line, reflecting poetry's
particular demand for alternative synonyms. A compound's **head** (the element determining its
word class and, usually, its core reference) is normally the **final** element (*railway* = a kind
of *way*) — **bahuvrihi** compounds are the systematic exception, where neither element is the
compound's semantic head (*paperback* is not a kind of *back*). Noun, adjective, and verb
compounds all occur, including three-element compounds (see Vocabulary) and, for verb compounds
specifically, the same separable/inseparable distinction already noted for simple prefixed verbs —
with the separable pattern mostly lost from English by the end of the OE period, surviving only
opaquely in a handful of forms (*understand*).

### Latin loans in three historical strata
The chapter divides Latin borrowing into: **(1) continental period** (pre-settlement contact with
the Roman Empire, ~170 words, mostly from colloquial Vulgar Latin via ordinary soldiers/camp-
followers, grammatically weighted toward nouns); **(2) settlement period** (contact with Romano-
British remnants after the 5th-century migration, very few securely datable words, e.g. *ċeaster*);
**(3) Christian period**, itself split into an earlier wave tied to Christianity's 7th-century
adoption (mostly political/organizational church vocabulary, everyday register) and a later,
distinct wave tied to the **Benedictine Monastic Revival** (second half of the 10th c.) — formal,
written-register, learned-Classical-Latin-derived loans, sometimes **reborrowing the same word a
second time** in unassimilated Classical form alongside an already-naturalized earlier Vulgar-Latin
version of the same original word (*tæfl* vs. *tabele*). Latin borrowing also produced
**semantic loans** (a native word's meaning stretched to match a Latin cognate/near-cognate's
semantic range) and **loan translations/calques** (a new native compound built to mirror a Latin
compound's internal structure) — two subtler borrowing mechanisms distinct from straightforward
word-borrowing.

### Other loan sources: Scandinavian, Celtic, Old Saxon, French
Scandinavian loans are overwhelmingly a **post-OE (Middle English) phenomenon** — even function
words like *are* only barely enter the attested OE period, confined to the northern/north-Midlands
Danelaw dialect region. Within OE itself, Scandinavian loans cluster in seafaring, legal (Danelaw
settlement-related), and everyday-register vocabulary. **Celtic** borrowing is strikingly minimal
given centuries of geographic contact — explained sociolinguistically as a direct consequence of
Celtic speakers forming a socially subordinate group within Anglo-Saxon society, so their language
was a shunned rather than prestige lexical source (the position of Welsh today, per the author, is
cited as a still-visible continuation of exactly that dynamic). **Old Saxon** loans are attested
only via the translated/adapted poem *Genesis B*, genuinely uncertain whether they represent real
established loans or an artifact unique to that one text's dialect-contact situation. **French**
loans are almost entirely a post-Conquest (hence, strictly, post-OE) or very-late-OE phenomenon,
with *pryt* "pride" (Wulfstan) flagged as an unusually early exception.

**Source:** *The Chicago Manual of Style*, 16th edition (University of Chicago Press, 2010), Chapter
10, "Abbreviations," PDF pp. 1293–1314 (§10.21 "Professional, Religious, and Other Designations"
through §10.40 "Months").

**Coverage note:** This chunk covers the back half of the "Names and Titles" subsection (§10.21,
Professional/Religious/Other Designations) and the entirety of "Firms and Companies" (§§10.22–10.23),
"Agencies and Organizations" (§§10.24–10.25), "Saints and Personal Names with 'Saint' or 'St.'"
(§§10.26–10.27), "Geographical Terms" (§§10.28–10.37), and the opening of "Designations of Time"
(§§10.38–10.40, through "Months"; "Days of the Week" at §10.41 and beyond fall outside this page
range and belong to a later part). PDF page 1293 is a boundary page carrying the tail end of what
appears to be the Academic Degrees list (§10.20) — a single entry, **STB** (*sacrae theologiae
baccalaureus*), plus its illustrative sentence — which likely falls inside a sibling `pt01` file's
assigned range. It is captured here too, flagged as a probable overlap, since this subagent cannot
confirm `pt01`'s exact boundary; the orchestrator should deduplicate if both files land with it.
Chicago's own numbered abbreviation lists are this chapter's actual "vocabulary" — every entry in
every list in the assigned range is tabulated below (none skipped as a sample), together with the
organizing rule each list/section states. This chunk also found a live instance of the UTF-8-read-
as-Latin-1 mojibake gotcha flagged in the dispatch (see the Canadian-provinces and company-name rows
below); all instances were decoded and corrected against known French/Spanish vocabulary, with the
decode method noted per row. The source has a genuine clean text layer; no vision-reading was
required and no handwritten marginalia was present. Per dispatch instruction, Attested Era is set to
"contemporary (source published 2010)" and Attested Region to "American English (United States)"
throughout (a book-level claim about this edition's own usage conventions, not a claim about the
geographic entities the abbreviations happen to name).

## Grammar points

### §10.21 Professional, religious, and other designations

Abbreviations for designations beyond academic degrees — professional, religious, fraternal, and
similar — follow the same pattern as academic degrees (§10.20, likely covered by a sibling `pt01`
file): spelled-out terms are capitalized only in institutional settings, and the abbreviated forms
are set off by commas when they follow a personal name (p. 1294).

### §10.22 Some commonly used generic abbreviations

Generic business-entity abbreviations (Co., Inc., LLC, etc.) are all independently findable in
*Webster's* and other standard dictionaries — Chicago isn't inventing a closed list, just collecting
common ones. Whether to use periods on these follows the general punctuation-of-abbreviations
guidance at §10.4. Separately, abbreviations of foreign-language terms (Cia, Cie) omit the period
specifically when the abbreviation is a contraction — formed from the first and last letters of the
full word, per the cross-reference to §10.2 (p. 1295).

### §10.23 Company names

Abbreviations and ampersands are appropriate shorthand in notes, bibliographies, and tabular matter,
but in running text, company names are best given in full. Chicago flags a genuine ambiguity here:
some companies' *full, official* forms already include an ampersand or an abbreviation (e.g., *Inc.*,
*& Co.*, *LLC*) as part of the legal name itself, not as a shortening of it. When in doubt — especially
for a contemporary firm — Chicago recommends checking the company's own website or another
authoritative source rather than guessing, and notes that elements like *Inc.*, *& Co.*, and *LLC* may
be omitted from running-text mentions unless they're relevant to the context (p. 1296).

### §10.24 Associations and the like

Names of agencies and organizations — whether acronyms (pronounced as a word) or initialisms
(pronounced letter by letter, per the §10.2 cross-reference) — are commonly abbreviated both in
running text (preferably after being spelled out on first occurrence) and in tabular/note contexts.
Such abbreviations are conventionally rendered in full capitals without periods. The treatment of
*a*/*an*/*the* preceding such an abbreviation is governed separately at §10.9 (p. 1297).

### §10.25 Broadcasting companies

Broadcast call letters, however derived from the parent company's name, are always fully capitalized
and never take periods (p. 1298).

### §10.26 Names of saints

*Saint* before the name of a Christian saint is often abbreviated (*St.*, plural *SS.*), but Chicago's
own preference is to spell it out in formal prose — abbreviating only when space is genuinely at a
premium. Whichever choice a given work makes should be applied consistently throughout (p. 1299).

### §10.27 Personal names with "Saint" or "St."

When *Saint* or *St.* is part of an actual personal name (not a title placed before a saint's name),
the individual name-bearer's own preferred usage governs, not a general style rule — e.g., a person
may spell their own surname "Saint-Gaudens" in full even where the general style guidance would favor
abbreviating. See also §10.31 for the parallel case with place-names (p. 1300).

### §10.28 US states and territories

In running text, US state/territory names are always spelled out, including when standing alone and
(with the sole exception of DC) when following a city name — "Lake Bluff, Illinois." In bibliographies,
tabular matter, and lists, they are usually abbreviated, and Chicago specifically prefers the
two-letter postal codes over the older multi-letter traditional abbreviations in all such contexts,
while still tabulating both for reference. A caveat: if a work must use the older traditional-style
abbreviations for some reason, certain terms (e.g., short names like Ohio, Iowa, Utah) simply aren't
subject to further abbreviation at all (p. 1301).

### §10.29 Canadian provinces and territories

Canadian provinces/territories are likewise normally spelled out in text ("Kingston, Ontario, is
worth a visit") but may be abbreviated in bibliographies and similar matter using the two-letter
postal codes — which Chicago notes have the specific advantage of applying equally well to both the
English and French official forms of a given province's name (p. 1303).

### §10.30 Comma with city plus state

A state/province/territory name following a city name is enclosed in commas on both sides, whether
spelled out (running text) or abbreviated (tabular matter/lists) — "Bedford, PA, and Jamestown, NY."
The sole exception: no comma appears between a two-letter state abbreviation and a following ZIP code
in a mailing address (p. 1304).

### §10.31 Place-names with "Fort," "Mount," "Saint," and the like

Generic elements that are part of a place-name (Fort, Mount, Port, Saint) should be abbreviated only
where space is genuinely at a premium — otherwise spelled out. *San* and *Santa* are a hard exception:
Chicago states these are never abbreviated, regardless of space constraints (p. 1305).

### §10.32 When to abbreviate country names

Country names are spelled out in running text but may be abbreviated in tabular matter, lists, and
similar contexts — writers should use discretion in forming such abbreviations and should define them
below the table (or otherwise) if there's any risk of ambiguity. For technical/international-standard
applications, Chicago points to the ISO 3166-1 two-letter (alpha-2) or three-letter (alpha-3) country
codes as an alternative standard system, consultable via the ISO's own website, rather than inventing
ad hoc forms. Chicago separately flags a historical usage note: pre-1990 reunification, East Germany
was commonly abbreviated GDR (or DDR, the German-language form) and West Germany FRG (or BRD); the
former Soviet Union was abbreviated USSR (p. 1306).

### §10.33 "US" versus "United States"

A register-sensitive rule, not a free stylistic choice: in running text, *United States* (spelled out)
is used as a noun, while *US* (abbreviated) is reserved for the adjective form — where the abbreviated
form is actually the generally preferred choice, not merely tolerated. Contrast "US dollars"/"US
involvement in China" (adjective) against "China's involvement in the United States" (noun) (p. 1307).

### §10.34 Mailing addresses — postal versus standard abbreviations

Two parallel abbreviation systems coexist for the same set of address-generic terms (Avenue, Boulevard,
Drive, etc.): the US Postal Service's own preferred forms (all capitals, no periods — intended
specifically for actual mailing addresses) and a second, more conventional mixed-case/period form that
Chicago prefers for tabular matter and similar contexts. In running text, Chicago's guidance is to
spell these terms out entirely rather than use either abbreviated system (p. 1308).

### §10.35 Compass points in mailing addresses

Single-letter compass-point abbreviations (N, E, S, W) normally take a period; two-letter compound
ones (NE, NW, SE, SW) do not. When part of a street name specifically, compass-point abbreviations
(NE, NW, SE, SW) remain abbreviated even in running text, and — notably — no comma precedes them in
that position, unlike the general city-plus-state comma rule at §10.30. Chicago flags a genuine
ambiguity case: a single letter like "N" immediately following a street name may itself be part of
the street's proper name (e.g., "N Street") rather than a compass-point designation, so context must
disambiguate. A compass point that is itself part of a street or place-name (not merely accompanying
one) must never be abbreviated at all — e.g., "South Avenue," "Northwest Highway" (p. 1309).

### §10.36 Compass points

Outside the specific mailing-address context above, compass points may be abbreviated using the
standard scheme (N, E, S, W and their intermediate/compound combinations like NE, NNE, ENE, and
further combination forms like "N by NE"), but in formal, nontechnical text these are usually spelled
out in full rather than abbreviated (p. 1310).

### §10.37 "Latitude" and "longitude"

In nontechnical contexts, the words *latitude* and *longitude* themselves are never abbreviated,
whether standing alone or in running text. Global positioning coordinates may be expressed in several
equivalent formats (a leading minus sign/hyphen for south or west, versus decimal minutes, versus
degrees-minutes-seconds notation), with the comma between coordinate pairs often dropped; latitude is
always given first when both are stated together. In technical work specifically, the abbreviations
*lat* and *long* (usually without periods) may be used as part of a full coordinate, since the
accompanying compass point already removes any ambiguity about which value is meant. Primes (′) and
double primes (″) — not straight or curly quotation marks — are the correct marks for minutes and
seconds of a coordinate; Chicago points to *Scientific Style and Format* for further detail (p. 1311).

### §10.38 Other discussions (cross-reference only)

A pure cross-reference section with no independent content of its own: units of time (seconds,
minutes, etc.) are covered at §10.71 (outside this chunk's range), and numerical designations of
dates and times of day at §§9.31, 9.34, 9.36, and 9.38–41 (also outside range, and in a different
chapter) (p. 1312).

### §10.39 Systems of chronology

A set of era-designation abbreviations used in running text and elsewhere. Although these
traditionally appeared in small capitals (with or without periods), Chicago's own recommendation —
consistent with this chapter's general abbreviation guidelines (§§10.4, 10.8) — is full capitals
without periods. A structural split governs word order: the first four listed (AD, AH, AM, AS, AUC)
*precede* the year number, while the remaining ones (BC, BCE, BP, CE, MYA, YBP) *follow* it. See also
§9.35 (outside this chunk's range) (p. 1313).

### §10.40 Months

Where space restrictions require abbreviating month names, three parallel systems exist side by side:
a period-taking 3–4-letter form (Chicago's own formal-prose preference), a no-period 3-letter form
(used in computer systems), and a no-period single/double-letter form (used in periodical-literature
indexes). May is identical across the first two systems since it has no excess length to contract.
September is the one month whose System-1 (period) form uses four letters ("Sept.") rather than the
three-letter pattern followed by every other month (p. 1314).

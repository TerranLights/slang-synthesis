**Source:** University of Chicago Press Staff, *The Chicago Manual of Style* (16th edition, 2010),
Chapter 7, "Spelling, Distinctive Treatment of Words, and Compounds" — end of "Music: Some
Typographic Conventions," "Computer Terms," and the full "Compounds and Hyphenation" section
(including the Table 7.1-style hyphenation guide), PDF pages 978–998. This is the end of Chapter 7;
Chapter 8, "Names and Terms," begins on PDF page 999 and is out of scope for this file. Continues
from Part 3 (not yet landed at time of writing); no prior English `established/` file has covered
this page range.

**Coverage note:** every grammar point (§§7.71–7.85, 15 numbered sections) and every distinct
category/prefix covered by the chapter-closing hyphenation guide table. §7.85 is itself a large
reference table (four sections: compounds by category, by part of speech, by specific term, and
words formed with prefixes) rather than prose — per the coverage rule and copyright discipline
below, this file tables **one row per category/prefix** (not one row per individual example word,
which would amount to a near-verbatim reproduction of CMOS's own compiled table) with a *selection*
of representative example words paraphrased into the Notes column, and the category's rule
paraphrased in the Gloss column. Pure cross-reference entries in the source table with no content of
their own (*ethnic terms*, *relationships*, *mid* under "self," *pseudo* under "self") are folded
into the relevant grammar-point prose rather than given their own row. This source has a clean text
layer throughout (a born-digital online-edition rendering, not a scan) — no mojibake, font-cipher, or
vision-reading issues were encountered anywhere in this page range. Vision Reading Confidence is
`n/a` for every row below.

## Grammar points

### 7.71 Dynamics

Musical dynamics terms (*piano*, *mezzo forte*) are usually spelled out in lowercase italics where
space allows; their symbolic abbreviations (*p*, *mf*) are set in lowercase boldface italics with no
periods. Dynamics added editorially (not by the composer) are conventionally distinguished by a
different font, or by parentheses/brackets (p. 978).

### 7.72 Application-specific variations

Computer hardware/software terms vary in their typographic treatment across systems and
applications, since nomenclature and function are closely tied together in that domain. CMOS's own
advice: practice internal consistency within a single system/application rather than imposing one
universal rule (p. 979).

### 7.73 Capitalization for keys and menu items

Alphabet keys and all named keys are capitalized regardless of how they're physically printed on a
given keyboard (*the function key F2*, *the Option key*). Menu items and icon names follow the
capitalization/spelling used by the specific application; file-format acronyms are rendered in full
capitals (*PNG*, *GIF*, *JPEG*) (p. 980).

### 7.74 Keyboard combinations and shortcuts

Simultaneous key presses use a plus sign or hyphen (matching the relevant OS/application's own
convention) with no surrounding space; words like *Shift*, *Hyphen*, and *Space* — along with
anything else that could otherwise be ambiguous — should be spelled out. A capital letter inside such
a combination (e.g. *Command-S*) does not itself imply the Shift key is part of the combination (p.
981).

### 7.75 Distinguishing words to be typed and other elements

When a stronger visual distinction than plain capitalization is needed, use boldface, italics, color,
or another consistent scheme; avoid quotation marks where possible, since they risk being read as
part of the quoted element itself (any punctuation not part of the quoted expression goes outside the
marks — cross-references §6.9). Directory paths, file names, variables, and similar syntax are
conventionally set in a monospaced font such as Courier. For deeper treatment of computer-writing
conventions, CMOS points to Eric S. Raymond's "Hacker Writing Style" essay in *The New Hacker's
Dictionary* (p. 982).

### 7.76 Terms like "web" and "Internet"

Following Chicago's general capitalization practice (cross-references §8.67), a generic term that is
capitalized as part of an official system or organization name may be lowercased when used alone or
in a generic combination. In a genuine departure from Chicago's own earlier editions, the 16th edition
now treats *web* as fully generic — lowercase whether standing alone or combined with other generic
terms — while *Internet* itself (naming the specific global network) keeps its capital. Abbreviations
for file formats are normally rendered in full capitals (cross-references §10.52); for the names of
keys and menu items, see §7.73; for terms like *e-mail*, see §7.85 (p. 983).

### 7.77 To hyphenate or not to hyphenate

Compound-term spelling — two words, hyphenated, or closed as one word — is, in CMOS's own words, "far
and away the most common" spelling question writers and editors face. Prefixes (and occasionally
suffixes) raise the same kind of question. The dictionary is the first place to check; this section,
including the hyphenation guide table at §7.85, supplies guidance for compounds not necessarily found
in the dictionary (some examples are drawn from *Webster's*) and for treatment according to a
compound's grammatical function (noun, adjective, or adverb) and its position in the sentence
(cross-references §5.91) (p. 984).

### 7.78 Some definitions

Four basic compound types: an **open compound** is spelled as two or more separate words (*high
school*, *lowest common denominator*); a **hyphenated compound** carries one or more hyphens
(*mass-produced*, *non-English-speaking*); a **closed (solid) compound** is spelled as one word
(*birthrate*, *notebook*); a **temporary compound** is coined for a specific, often one-time purpose
(*dictionary-wielding*, *impeachment hound*) and, even though some eventually become permanent, is
not normally found in the dictionary. Words formed with prefixes (*antigrammarian*, *postmodern*),
while not strictly compounds, are discussed alongside them and covered in section 4 of §7.85.
Combining forms — a prefix-like element that modifies a word to form a new combination, such as
*electric* becoming *electromagnetic* — are covered as examples in section 2 of §7.85, under
"combining forms" (p. 985).

### 7.79 The trend toward closed compounds

With frequent use, open or hyphenated compounds tend to become closed over time — CMOS traces the
progression *on line* → *on-line* → *online*. Chicago's general adherence to *Webster's* doesn't
preclude occasional exceptions when a closed spelling has become widely preferred by writers (e.g.
*website*) and neither pronunciation nor readability is at risk (p. 986).

### 7.80 Hyphens and readability

A hyphen aids readability by showing structure and, often, pronunciation — words that could otherwise
be misread, such as *re-creation* or *co-op*, should be hyphenated. A hyphen can also resolve genuine
ambiguity: *much-needed clothing* clarifies that the clothing is greatly needed, not abundant and
needed. Where no ambiguity could arise, as in *public welfare administration* or *graduate student
housing*, hyphenation is unnecessary (p. 987).

### 7.81 Compound modifiers before or after a noun

Compound modifiers (also called phrasal adjectives) such as *open-mouthed* or *full-length* usually
take a clarifying hyphen when they precede a noun. The exceptions are proper nouns (*United States*)
and compounds formed from an adverb ending in *-ly* plus an adjective (see §7.82). It's never
*incorrect* to hyphenate an adjectival compound before a noun. When such compounds instead follow the
noun they modify, hyphenation is usually unnecessary, even for compounds that *Webster's* itself
hyphenates (*well-read*, *ill-humored*) (p. 988).

### 7.82 Adverbs ending in "ly"

Compounds formed from an adverb ending in *-ly* plus an adjective or participle (*largely
irrelevant*, *smartly dressed*) are not hyphenated either before or after a noun, since the *-ly*
ending itself signals to the reader that the next word is another modifier, not a noun — ambiguity is
effectively impossible (p. 989).

### 7.83 Multiple hyphens

Multiple hyphens are usually appropriate for phrases such as *an over-the-counter drug* or *a
winner-take-all contest*. However, if the compound modifier itself contains an adjective that modifies
a further compound, additional hyphens may be unnecessary — *late nineteenth-century literature* and
*early twentieth-century growth* read clearly with only one hyphen. Similar expressions formed with
*mid-* don't follow this pattern, since Chicago classifies *mid-* as a prefix (see §7.85, section 4);
see also section 3 of §7.85, under *century* (p. 990).

### 7.84 Omission of part of a hyphenated expression

When the second part of a hyphenated expression is omitted (implied by context), the hyphen is
retained and followed by a space: *fifteen- and twenty-year mortgages*, *Chicago- or
Milwaukee-bound passengers* — but not when the resulting numeral expression would itself be read as a
single entity (*a five-by-eight-foot rug*). The same pattern governs omission of the second part of a
solid (closed) compound: *both over- and underfed cats* — but not *overfed and overworked mules*,
which should not be truncated to "overfed and -worked" (p. 991).

### 7.85 Hyphenation guide for compounds and words formed with prefixes

A return, in this edition, to the tabular hyphenation-reference format used in earlier editions of
CMOS. CMOS recommends consulting the preceding prose sections (§§7.77–84) first, and this table
"especially" when a relevant example can't otherwise be found; Chicago's general stance is a *spare*
hyphenation style — hyphenate only when doing so aids readability, if neither the dictionary nor this
table offers a direct analogy. The table has four alphabetically arranged sections (by first column):
(1) compounds according to category (age terms, colors, fractions, numbers, time, etc.); (2)
compounds according to part of speech (adjective + noun, noun + gerund, etc.); (3) compounds formed
with specific recurring terms (*ache*, *self*, *step*, *web*, etc.); and (4) common prefixes, most of
which close up with their stem word, with the hyphenated exceptions noted per prefix. Compounds
formed with *suffixes* (e.g. *nationhood*, *penniless*) are, by contrast, almost always closed and
not separately tabulated. See the Vocabulary table above for the individual categories/prefixes and
their rules, each tagged with its section of the table and representative examples (pp. 992–98).

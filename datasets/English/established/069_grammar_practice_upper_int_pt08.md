## Grammar points

### The gerund: uses

The book catalogs eight distinct syntactic positions a gerund can fill (p.111): subject of a verb
("Smoking is bad for you"); object of a verb ("Do you like cooking?"); complement of *to be* ("His
favourite activity is watching television"); after prepositions ("She's very good at listening to
people"); after determiners (*a, the, this, some, a lot of* — "The bombing of civilians horrified
everyone"); after possessive adjectives, object pronouns, or nouns ("I hope you don't mind
his/him/John coming here"); after adjectives ("Regular swimming is very good for you"); and in a
set of fixed expressions (*it's no use...ing*, *What/How about...ing?*, *like...ing*, *go...ing*
[for activities: "we usually go fishing"], and notice-style imperatives like "No smoking").

**Notes:** gerunds can take objects and be modified by adverbs just like finite verbs ("I enjoy
reading new books" — object; "You should stop working so hard" — adverb); the negative gerund form
is *not* + gerund ("It's nice not being at work").

### The infinitive: uses

A parallel but distinct inventory of eleven positions (pp.111–112): after certain verbs ("They
agreed to come"); after certain verbs + person/object ("I asked her to come here"); after *to be*
("My first thought was to phone you"); to express purpose ("I came here to tell you the good news");
after *be* + adjective (+ *of* + noun/pronoun — "It's kind of you to help me"); after *the
first/second/last/only/best* (and other superlatives) + noun or the pronoun *one* ("He was the only
one to die"); after a noun/pronoun, where the infinitive's implied subject/object relates back to
that preceding noun ("There's a lot of work to do"); after verbs of knowing/believing, in either
verb + object + infinitive or passive + infinitive form ("I know him to be an honest man" / "He is
known to be an honest man"); after verb + question word ("I don't know what to do"); after *too* +
adjective and after adjective + *enough*; and after *is/are* to describe a scheduled future event
("He is to become Mayor next month").

**Notes:** infinitives, like gerunds, can take objects and adverbial modification ("He expects to
win the race easily"); the negative infinitive form is *not* + (*to*-)infinitive ("He agreed not to
tell anyone").

### Verb + infinitive, verb + gerund: the general time-sequence distinction

A *to*-infinitive complement after a verb like *hope, expect, promise, want* typically describes an
event that will follow the main verb's activity/thought in time ("I hope to see you next week" — the
seeing is still future relative to the hoping); a gerund complement simply describes the activity
itself, with no inherent time sequencing implied ("We enjoyed seeing you last weekend") (p.113).

### The bare infinitive

English normally uses *to* with the infinitive, but a specific closed set of contexts takes the bare
infinitive (without *to*) (p.114): after modals and after the auxiliaries *do/did* ("You must come";
"Did you pass the exam?"); after *Let's...*; after *make* + noun/pronoun object ("He made me come
here"); after *would rather* ("I'd rather walk there than take the bus"); after *had better*; after
*Why not...?*; after the verb *help*, where either the bare or *to*-infinitive is acceptable ("Can
you help me (to) open this?"); after a verb of perception (*see, watch, notice, feel, hear*) +
object ("I didn't hear you come in" — cross-referenced against §41 on p.116, since present
participles also occur in this slot); and when a series of infinitives follows one verb, only the
first of which needs *to* ("I'd like to come and see you").

### Verbs of perception + object + -ing or the bare infinitive

Verbs of perception (*see, notice, look at, watch, feel, hear, listen to*) can take either the
*-ing* form or the bare infinitive as their object complement; *smell* can only take the *-ing* form
(p.116). The bare infinitive describes the complete action ("I watched her play tennis" = I saw the
whole game); the *-ing* form describes the action in progress ("I watched her playing tennis" = I
saw the game in progress). The *-ing* form in this slot is technically a present participle, not a
gerund, and is noted as more common overall than the bare-infinitive alternative; in practice the
difference in meaning between the two is often very small. A further small set of verbs — *find,
discover, catch, leave, keep* — take only the present participle in this construction, never the
bare infinitive ("I found her looking in my desk").

### Verbs followed by the to-infinitive or the gerund (meaning shift)

A set of individual verbs can take either complement, each with its own specific meaning
contrast, worked through one by one (pp.117–119):

- **BE:** gerund behaves as a noun describing an activity ("My favourite watersport is
  wind-surfing"); *to*-infinitive describes a complete/future event, frequently following *plan, aim,
  intention, decision, solution* ("Our plan is to close the factory down next year").
- **REMEMBER:** *to*-infinitive = remembered first, then did the thing ("I remembered to give her
  the message"); gerund = did the thing, and now recalls having done it ("I remember giving her the
  message").
- **FORGET:** *to*-infinitive = forgot, so didn't do it ("I forgot to go to that meeting"); gerund =
  doesn't now recall having done something that did happen ("I forget going to that meeting").
- **TRY:** *to*-infinitive = attempted an action that failed ("I tried to open the door but it was
  locked"); gerund = experimented with an alternative method, success or failure unspecified ("I
  tried using a different key").
- **REGRET:** *to*-infinitive = a formal way of introducing unwelcome news, "I am sorry to say..."
  ("I regret to say that your interview was not successful"); gerund = looking back with regret on a
  past action ("I really regret being so rude to her").
- **STOP:** *to*-infinitive = purpose, introducing the reason the stopping happened ("We stopped to
  have a cup of tea"); gerund = the activity that was discontinued ("We stopped talking when she came
  in").
- **GO ON:** *to*-infinitive = moved on to something different ("...he went on to get a first-class
  degree"); gerund = continued doing the same thing ("We went on talking all night").
- **LIKE/DISLIKE/LOVE/HATE:** gerund = enjoyment of the activity itself, not necessarily regular
  ("I like going for a run before work"); *to*-infinitive = describes a regular activity done because
  it's considered a good idea, independent of in-the-moment enjoyment ("I like to go for a run before
  work").
- **PREFER:** *to*-infinitive and gerund are close in meaning when used independently ("I prefer to
  stay/staying at home in the evenings"); when directly comparing two activities, only the gerund
  pattern is grammatical (*prefer* + gerund + *to* + gerund: "I prefer staying in to going out," NOT
  "prefer to stay in to go out"); *would prefer* takes only the infinitive and refers to a specific
  occasion ("I'd prefer to stay in tonight," not "I'd prefer staying in tonight").
- **BEGIN/START/CONTINUE:** the two complement forms are very similar in meaning with these verbs
  ("He began/started/continued shouting" ≈ "...to shout").
- **ADVISE/ALLOW/ENCOURAGE/FORBID/RECOMMEND:** usually followed by an object (person/pronoun) +
  *to*-infinitive ("They advised us to leave"); when used without an object, they take a gerund
  instead ("They advised leaving").
- **NEED:** *to*-infinitive carries an active meaning, the subject itself needing to act ("I need to
  get up early tomorrow"); gerund carries a passive meaning — someone needs to act on the subject,
  and who does it is unspecified ("The car needs cleaning" = someone needs to clean the car).

### Preposition + gerund

After a preposition, English always uses the gerund, never the infinitive (p.120), across several
structural slots: preposition + gerund directly ("After reading the letter, he changed his mind");
adjective/noun + preposition + gerund ("Are you interested in applying for this job?"); verb +
preposition + gerund, including phrasal verbs ("They succeeded in getting the new contract"; "She
apologized for upsetting him"; "They carried on working"); verb + object (person) + preposition +
gerund ("We dissuaded him from writing the letter"); and verb + preposition + object pronoun or
possessive adjective + gerund ("My boss complained about me/my arriving late last week").

**Notes (p.120):** an object pronoun (*me, us*) is more common than a possessive adjective (*my,
our*) in this last construction, with the possessive-adjective form reading as notably more formal.
Separately, *to* is structurally ambiguous: it may be the infinitive marker, or a preposition
preceding a gerund, and only context/the specific verb tells which ("We're hoping to see you this
weekend" [infinitive] vs. "We're looking forward to seeing you this weekend" [preposition + gerund]).

### Be + adjective + infinitive

Four structural sub-patterns are distinguished (p.122): *be* + adjective + *to*-infinitive in
general, where the infinitive's event is often future relative to the adjective ("He is keen to
change his job"); *be* + adjective of feeling + *to*-infinitive ("I'm pleased to see you"); *be* +
adjective describing a personal characteristic + (*of* + person) + *to*-infinitive ("It was silly of
her to say that"); and *be* + adjective describing the activity itself + *to*-infinitive ("This book
is very difficult to read").

**Note:** after a specific small set of adjectives — *nice, lovely, great, wonderful, awful,
horrible* — either the *-ing* form (present participle) or the *to*-infinitive can follow, with a
subtle distinction (*-ing* = the activity in progress, *to*-infinitive = the whole event) that in
practice collapses to near-identical usage ("It's nice seeing you" ≈ "It's nice to see you").

### Different forms of the gerund and the infinitive

The book tabulates the complete paradigm for each (p.126): gerund forms are plain gerund (*doing*),
perfect gerund (*having done*), passive gerund (*being done*), and perfect passive gerund (*having
been done*); infinitive forms are plain infinitive (*(to) do*), continuous infinitive (*(to) be
doing*), perfect infinitive (*(to) have done*), perfect continuous infinitive (*(to) have been
doing*), passive infinitive (*(to) be done*), and perfect passive infinitive (*(to) have been
done*) — six infinitive forms against four gerund forms, since the gerund paradigm has no distinct
continuous-aspect slot (continuous meaning is already inherent to the plain gerund's *-ing* form).

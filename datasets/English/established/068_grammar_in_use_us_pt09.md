## Grammar points

### Unit 115 — Noun + preposition ("reason for," "cause of," etc.)

A reference list of fixed noun-plus-preposition pairings grouped loosely by preposition: FOR (a check
for [a sum], a demand/need for, a reason for), IN (a rise/increase/fall/decrease in), OF (an advantage/
disadvantage of — but "an advantage in/to doing something" when followed by a gerund; a cause of; a
photograph/picture of), TO (damage to, an invitation to, a reaction to, a solution/answer to a problem,
a reply to a letter, a key to a door, an attitude to/toward), WITH (a relationship/connection/contact
with someone — but "a relationship/connection/difference BETWEEN two things," a separate pattern for
a two-item comparison rather than a one-sided relation). p. 232.

### Unit 116 — Preposition + noun ("by mistake," "on television," etc.)

A reference list the book frames as a frequent error source: BY (pay by check — but "pay in cash" or
"pay cash," no preposition; by accident/mistake/chance; a work by a named creator — "a play by
Shakespeare"), IN (in love with, in my opinion, in time [= soon enough for something, contrasted
directly against] ON time [= punctual/not late]), ON (on fire; on the telephone/phone; on television/
on the radio; on a diet; on strike; on vacation/business/a trip/a tour/a cruise/an expedition — but
"go to a place FOR a vacation" when a destination is named), FOR (for a walk/a swim/a meal; for
breakfast/lunch/dinner). p. 234.

### Units 117–118 — Adjective + preposition (1) and (2)

Two large reference lists of fixed adjective-preposition pairings. Set 1: the character-judgment
adjectives (nice/kind/good/generous/mean/stupid/silly/intelligent/sensible/(im)polite/rude/
unreasonable) take "of someone" when evaluating an action, but "to someone" when describing how the
adjective's subject treats that person — an important two-pattern distinction for the same adjective
set; angry/annoyed/furious (about something, with someone, for doing something); delighted/pleased/
satisfied/disappointed (with); bored/fed up (with); surprised/shocked/amazed/astonished (at/by);
excited/worried/upset (about); afraid/frightened/terrified/scared (of); proud/ashamed (of); jealous/
envious/suspicious (of); aware/conscious (of); good/bad/excellent/brilliant (at doing); married/engaged
(to — not "with"). Set 2: sorry (about something, but "sorry FOR doing" something, and "feel/be sorry
FOR someone"); crazy (about); impressed (by/with); famous (for); responsible (for); different (from,
with informal "different than" also noted); interested (in); capable/incapable (of); fond (of); full
(of); short (of); tired (of); similar (to); crowded (with). pp. 236–238.

### Units 119–121 — Verb + preposition (1), (2), and (3)

Three large reference lists of fixed verb-preposition pairings: apologize (to someone) for; apply for;
believe in; belong to; care about (=consider important) vs. care for (=like, usually in questions/
negatives, or =look after); take care of (=look after); collide with; complain (to someone) about;
concentrate on; consist of; crash/drive/bump/run into; depend on (preposition droppable before a
question word: "depends (on) how much"); die of (an illness) — Unit 119. dream about vs. dream of
(doing/being, also "I wouldn't dream of it"); happen to; hear about (=be told) vs. hear of (=know
something exists) vs. hear from (=receive a letter/call); laugh/smile at; listen to; live on; look at
(=look toward; also have a look at, stare at, glance at) vs. look for (=try to find) vs. look after
(=take care of); meet with (=have a meeting with); pay (someone) for something, but no preposition
before a bill/fine/fare/taxes; rely on — Unit 120. search (a place) for; shout at (in anger) vs. shout
to (so as to be heard); speak/talk to (or with); suffer from; think about (=consider) vs. think of
(=remember/recall, or give an opinion), the book noting the distinction between the two is often
negligible and either preposition works; wait for; write to (or write someone directly, no preposition)
— Unit 121. A short companion list of verbs taking NO preposition at all closes Unit 121: call/phone
someone, discuss something, enter (a place). pp. 240–244.

### Units 122–123 — Verb + object + preposition (1) and (2)

Two reference lists of fixed verb-object-preposition collocations: accuse...of; ask (someone) for
(but "ask someone a question," no preposition); blame...for or blame...on (with the parallel idiom "to
blame for"); borrow...from; charge...with (an offense); congratulate...on; divide/cut/split...into;
do something about; explain...to (with a fixed word-order note: "explain to someone that/what/how...",
never "explain someone"); invite...to; leave (a place) for (another place); point/aim...at — Unit 122.
prefer...to; protect...from (or against); provide...with; regard...as; remind...of (=cause to
remember) vs. remind...about (=tell not to forget); sentence...to (a period of imprisonment); spend
(money) on (but "spend (time) doing" something, a gerund construction, not "on"); throw...at (to hit)
vs. throw...to (for someone to catch); translate...from (one language) into (another); warn...about
(with "of" sometimes also possible) — Unit 123. pp. 246–248.

### Unit 124 — Phrasal verbs (get up, break down, fill in, etc.)

A closed set of particles (on, off, in, out, up, down, back, over, about, around, forward, through,
away, along) combines with verbs in two distinct ways: literal directional/movement combinations (get
on, drive off, come back, turn around) and idiomatic phrasal verbs whose meaning isn't predictable from
the parts (break down, look out, take off, get up [=arise]). Transitive phrasal verbs are usually
separable — the object can go between the verb and particle or after the particle (turned off the
light / turned the light off) — but a pronoun object must go between the verb and particle (fill it
out, not "fill out it"; wake her up, not "wake up her"). A further class combines a phrasal verb with
an additional preposition (look forward to, keep up with, cut down on), where the object always follows
the preposition and the construction does not separate. p. 250.

### Appendix 2 — Regular and irregular verbs (irregular-verb table)

A full two-column reference table of English irregular verbs' three principal parts (base form /
simple past / past participle), alphabetically ordered from be (was/were, been) through lie (lay,
lain) in the left column and light (lit, lit) through write (wrote, written) in the right column. A
small number of entries (e.g. read) note that the past-tense and past-participle spelling is identical
to the base form but the pronunciation differs, marked phonetically in the table itself (/ri:d/ vs.
/red/). p. 255.

### Appendix 3 — Spelling

Systematic spelling-change rules for English's productive suffixes: noun/verb + -s/-es (default -s,
but -es after -s/-ss/-sh/-ch/-x, with a small irregular set like potato→potatoes, do→does); words
ending in consonant+y (y→ie before -s, y→i before -ed/-er/-est/-ly, but y stays unchanged before -ing
and after a vowel+y, with pay/lay/say as a further irregular exception: paid/laid/said); verbs ending
in -ie (ie→y before -ing: lie→lying); words ending in -e (drop -e before -ing, with exceptions be→being
and -ee verbs like see→seeing; add -d for past tense; adjectives/adverbs keep -e before -ly but drop it
before -y when the adjective ends in -le); and consonant doubling (double a final single consonant
after a single vowel before -ing/-ed/-er/-est for one-syllable words, and for multi-syllable words only
when the final syllable is stressed — contrasted directly against unstressed-final-syllable words,
which never double; words ending in two consonants or with two vowel letters before the final consonant
never double; word-final y and w are never treated as consonants for this rule). pp. 256–257.

### Appendix 4 — Short forms (I'm/didn't, etc.)

A reference table of contracted auxiliary forms used in speech and informal writing: 'm (=am), 's (=is
or has), 're (=are), 've (=have), 'll (=will or shall), 'd (=would or had), giving the full paradigm
across all persons (I'm, he's/she's/it's, you're/we're/they're, I've, he'll/she'll/it'll, etc.) and a
parallel negative-contraction table (isn't, aren't, wasn't, weren't, don't, doesn't, didn't, haven't,
hasn't, hadn't, can't, couldn't, won't, wouldn't, shouldn't, mustn't). These same contractions
(especially 's) also attach to question words (who's, what's, where's, when's, how's, who'll, who'd,
what'll) and after "that/there/here" (that's, there's, there'll, here's), and sometimes after an
ordinary noun subject (John's going out tonight). The chapter's one hard constraint: these short forms
cannot be used sentence-finally, where the auxiliary carries stress ("Yes, I am," never "Yes, I'm").
p. 258.

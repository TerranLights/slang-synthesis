## Grammar points

### 15.1 Direct speech

*(Sections 15.1A and 15.1B are unrecoverable from the source PDF — see the source-corruption note
above.)*

**15.1C 'Quote within a quote'.** When quoting someone else's words inside an already-quoted passage,
a second set of quotation marks is used (double inside single, or vice versa), with its own internal
punctuation distinct from the surrounding sentence; the same device also marks a cited title (e.g. a
book or film name) inside quoted speech. Verbs like *think* and *wonder*, by convention, are not set
off with quotation marks at all, p.215.

### 15.2 'Say', 'tell' and 'ask'

**15.2A 'Say', 'tell' and 'ask'.** The three commonest reporting verbs in both direct and indirect
speech. *Tell* always requires a personal indirect object ("He told me," never "*told to me*"); *say*
can optionally take *to me*, but never a bare indirect object ("*He said me*" is impossible); *ask* can
optionally take a personal object, p.216.

**15.2B Fixed expressions with 'say', 'tell' and 'ask'.** Each verb participates in its own set of
fixed collocations — see the Vocabulary table for the three sets, p.216.

### 15.3 Indirect statements with tense changes

**15.3A Common indirect speech forms.** Indirect speech generally uses past-tense reporting verbs
because past events are being reported, and the general "one tense back" rule applies (present →
past, past → past perfect). This is a useful default, not an absolute rule — tenses can be mixed
depending on what makes sense in context. A long, systematic list of modal-specific
exceptions/alternatives exists — see the Vocabulary table for the full set (will/can/may → would/
could/might; shall's two distinct backshift targets depending on function; several modal and
quasi-modal forms, including past-necessity *must*, deductive *must*, *mustn't*, and all perfect modal
forms, that simply don't change), pp.218–219.

**15.3B Pronoun and adverb changes in indirect speech.** Pronoun choice in indirect speech depends
entirely on the reporting speaker's own point of view relative to the original utterance — the same
original words can be correctly reported with different pronouns depending on who is doing the
reporting. Adverbs of time/place also shift depending on context (*now* → *immediately*/*then*;
*today* → *that day*), though making these shifts is not always obligatory, especially in spoken
indirect speech, p.219.

### 15.4 Indirect questions with tense changes

**15.4A Indirect Yes/No questions: 'He asked me if I was ready'.** No quotation or question marks are
used in an indirect question. The "one tense back" rule from 15.3A applies, with the same exception
that a question reported immediately after being asked can keep the present tense. The direct
question's inversion reverts to ordinary statement word order, and this applies equally to a reported
tag question. *If* or *whether* introduces the reported question, following *ask, want to know,
wonder, (not) know,* or *didn't say/tell me*, p.220.

**15.4B Indirect question-word questions: 'He asked me why I went there'.** The inversion that follows
a question-word in a direct question likewise reverts to statement word order when reported, with the
tense changed too if necessary, p.220.

**15.4C Indirect subject-questions.** A direct subject-question already has statement word order
[>13.8A], so reporting it changes nothing about word order — only the tense shifts, and only if
necessary, p.221.

### 15.5 Uses of the to-infinitive in indirect speech

**15.5A Reporting the imperative: 'He reminded me to post the letter'.** An imperative is reported with
a reporting verb (chosen to match the imperative's underlying function — asking, telling, advising,
etc.) + to-infinitive. A negative imperative is reported with *not* or *never* immediately before the
to-infinitive — never "*to not*" or "*to don't*," p.222.

**15.5B Offers, suggestions, requests for advice: 'He asked if he should...'.** A direct offer or
suggestion made with *shall*/*should* is reported either with *if*/*whether* + *should*, or with
*whether to* + infinitive (never "*if to*"), p.222.

**15.5C Requests for advice with question-words: 'He wanted to know how...'.** A direct request for
advice using a question-word can be reported with question-word + *should*, or with question-word +
to-infinitive — except *why*, which never takes the to-infinitive pattern ("why he should wait," never
"*why to wait*"), p.223.

### 15.6 When we use indirect speech

**15.6A Interpreting direct speech.** Real indirect speech typically occurs in continuous reported
paragraphs rather than isolated sentences, held together with connecting phrases (*she went on to
say, he continued, he added that*) and varied reporting verbs (*observed, noted, remarked*) in place
of relentless repetition of *said*. Yes/No answers and discourse adverbs like *Well* are common in
direct speech but disappear once reported. Reporting is fundamentally an act of interpretation, not
mechanical rule-application — a reporting verb like *exclaim, insist,* or *suggest* can capture the
force of what was actually meant rather than its literal wording, p.224.

## Grammar points

### §12.8 Greek — alphabetic numerals (tail end)

Before Arabic numerals, Greek used an alphabetic numeral system (Table 12.5, not reproduced here)
where letters double as cardinal or ordinal numbers. A raised numeral sign (′) marks numbers; a
raised lower numeral sign (‚, written before the letter) multiplies by a thousand. Two obsolete
letters survive only as numerals: stigma (ς) for 6 and sampi (ϡ) for 900. Modern Greek uses Arabic
numerals (italic if the surrounding font is sloped, roman otherwise), but the old alphabetic system
persists for some purposes much as Roman numerals do in Western languages — e.g. στ′ for "6." (p.229)

### §12.9.1 Hebrew alphabet

Hebrew is written with 22 consonantal letters, each with a cursive and a block ("square") printed
form, and each carrying an inherent numerical value (1 through 400, reused in combination for
higher numbers) — the full table (Table 12.6) is not reproduced here per copyright discipline, but
spans *alef* (1) through *tav* (400). These numerical values are used in Hebrew books, especially
liturgical and older texts, to mark volumes, parts, chapters, and pages, as well as the day of the
week, date, and year by the Jewish calendar. Vowels are indicated, when indicated at all, by dots or
small strokes ("points") above, below, or inside the consonant; text without them is "unpointed" or
"unvocalized" — the normal way Hebrew is written and printed. Five letters (khaf, mem, nun, fé,
tsadi) take a distinct final ("sofit") form at the end of a word. (pp.229–230)

### §12.9.2 Hebrew transliteration

Different transliteration systems for Hebrew call for varying diacritics on consonants and vowels
(acute for stress; macron/breve pairs; superior letters in some systems). Special transliteration
characters include the schwa (ə), aleph (ʾ), and ayin (ʿ); in loose transliteration from modern
Hebrew the latter two may be reduced to a plain apostrophe or omitted entirely. (p.231)

### §12.9.3 Hebrew word division

Hebrew script words are short enough that line-division rules barely matter; when transliterated
words must be divided, the new line should begin with a single consonant, and in cases of doubt
(e.g. *ts*, or *h*-combinations that may or may not represent a single consonant) the editor should
avoid dividing at all. (p.231)

### §12.10.1 Italian accents

Italian accent usage is not fully standardized; editors should generally follow the author's own
typescript. There are two accents: acute (used on a closed *e*, rarely on a closed *o*) and grave
(used on open *e*/*o*, and to mark stress on a final syllable). An alternative convention marks
final *i*/*u* with an acute rather than grave. A text mixing accentuation systems across different
quoted extracts may reflect fidelity to varied sources rather than carelessness, so discretionary
accents should usually be left alone unless the copy-editor, not just the author, is expert in
Italian. Capital letters are conventionally left unaccented, except where needed to avoid confusion;
a grave accent on a capital E is marked as an apostrophe (*E' oggi il suo compleanno*, 'It is his
birthday today'). (p.232)

### §12.10.2 Italian abbreviation

Italian abbreviations are usually capitalized only on the first letter (not set in full capitals),
with no full point following; if the expansion itself doesn't start with a capital, neither does the
abbreviation. (p.232)

### §12.10.3 Italian capitalization

Italian capitalizes far less than English. Capitalize personal/place/institution names and some
dates/festivals, but leave lower-case *io* ('I') unless sentence-initial. The polite second-person
pronouns (*Lei*, *Loro*, *Voi*) and their related forms (*La*, *Le*, *Suo*, *Vi*, *Vostro*) are often
capitalized, especially in commercial correspondence, including when suffixed onto a verb
(*ringraziarLa* 'thank you'). Titles of works capitalize only the first word and proper nouns.
Roman-numeral centuries are set in full capitals in both italic and roman (*l'XI secolo*, 'the
eleventh century'). Days, months, languages, peoples, and nationality adjectives are lower case,
though capitalizing peoples' names (*gli Italiani*) is becoming more common despite the traditional
rule. (pp.232–233)

### §12.10.4 Italian punctuation

Italian distinguishes spaced "points of omission" from unspaced "points of suspension" (the latter
equating to French *points de suspension* — three points if preceded by other punctuation, four if
not). An apostrophe following a vowel takes the ordinary interword space after it (*a' miei*); one
following a consonant takes no space (*l'onda*); one replacing a vowel at a word's start is always
preceded by a space (*e 'l*) — though older printing sometimes reverses these conventions. Single and
double quotation marks and guillemets are all used, in varying combinations; a final full point goes
after the closing quotation mark even if a question/exclamation mark closes the quoted matter
itself. (pp.232–233)

### §12.10.5 Italian word division

A fixed list of compound consonant clusters (*bl, br, ch, cl, cr, dr, fl, fr, gh, gl, gn, gr, pl,
pr, sb, sc, sd, sf, sg, sl, sbr, sch, scr, sdr, sfr, sgh, sgr, spl, spr, str*) must never be divided.
Vowels are divided from each other only when neither is *i* or *u*. A doubled consonant following a
vowel splits with the first consonant joining the vowel; the same applies across an apostrophe
(*Sen-z'altro*). In the middle of a word, when the first consonant of a group is a liquid (*l, m, n,
r*) it stays with the preceding vowel, and the rest of the cluster goes with the next syllable
(*al-tero*, *ar-tigiano*, *tem-pra*). (p.233)

### §12.11 Japanese script and romanization

Japanese combines kanji (ideographs of Chinese origin) with kana, an alphabet-based script existing
in two forms: hiragana (cursive, for inflectional endings and grammatical words) and katakana
("squared," for foreign loanwords and Western names). Both vertical right-to-left columns and
horizontal left-to-right layout are in use. The most common romanization uses a macron for long
vowels, with syllable-final *n* followed by an apostrophe before *e* or *y*; macrons are optional in
non-specialist works and often omitted in well-established place-name spellings (e.g. Hokkaido,
Honshu, Kobe, Kyoto, Kyushu, Osaka, Tokyo). (p.234)

### §12.12.1 Latin alphabet

Classical Latin's alphabet is 21 letters (*A B C D E F G H I K L M N O P Q R S T V X*), plus the
Greek imports *Y* and *Z*; *A, E, O, Y* are always vowels, while *I* and *V* may be either vowel or
consonant. Early modern printers invented the vocalic/consonantal distinction (*i/j*, *u/v*), which
many scholars still retain (distinguishing two-syllable *solvit* from three-syllable *coluit*; also
*volvit* 'rolls, rolled' from *voluit* 'willed'), though others prefer capital *V* / lower-case *u*
regardless of value — the numeral, however, must always be *v* regardless of case. *J* is now
virtually obsolete outside legal/stock phrases used in an English context, such as *de jure*. The
ligatures æ, œ, found only as space-saving devices in inscriptions and post-classical to
19th-century printed books, should not be used now unless exactly reproducing such a source. (p.235)

### §12.12.2 Latin accents

Modern usage normally omits accents from Latin. Classical practice sometimes used the apex (like an
acute) on long vowels other than *I*; until recently a diaeresis distinguished non-diphthong *ae*/
*oe* (*aëris* 'of air' vs. *aeris* 'of bronze/money'; *poëta* 'poet'). Older practice used a
circumflex on long vowels to resolve ambiguity (*mensâ*, ablative of *mensa* 'table'); a grave
accent is sometimes found on the final syllable of adverbs. Grammars and dictionaries may mark
vowels with macron or breve (*mēnsă* nominative vs. *mēnsā* ablative). (p.235)

### §12.12.3 Latin capitalization

Classical Latin made no capital/lower-case distinction. Modern usage capitalizes proper names and
their derivatives (*Roma* 'Rome', *Romanus* 'Roman', *Graece* 'in Greek') except in verb form
(*graecissare* 'speak Greek/live it up'); sentence-initial capitalization is inconsistent, and a
line of verse is no longer automatically capitalized. In titles, convention varies between
capitalizing only the first word (Oxford's own preference: *De rerum natura*, *De imperio Cn.
Pompei*), first word plus main words (*De Rerum Natura*, *De Imperio Cn. Pompei*), or main words
only, even without an initial capital (*de rerum natura*, *de imperio Cn. Pompei*). (pp.235–236)

### §12.12.4 Latin word division

A vowel may be divided from another vowel (*be-atus*) unless they form a diphthong (*ae, oe, ei,
au, eu*), and the same applies to consonantal *u* (*ama-uit*, *dele-uit*) and consonantal *i*
(*in-iustus*). Consonantal *x* between vowels is taken over to the next syllable (*pro-ximus*); any
doubled consonant may be divided, except in *mn*, where *l, m, n, r* may be divided from a following
consonant. Traditionally, any cluster capable of opening a word in Latin or Greek is kept together
(*bl, br, ch, cl, cr, ct, dl, dr, gn, gu, mn, ph, pl, pr, ps, pt, qu, th, tl, tr* — many surviving in
English words like *ctenoid, gnomon, mnemonic, pneumonia, psychology, ptomaine*), but this is
overridden by the rule that prefixes and suffixes are separated and compounds divided at their
component parts, which requires knowing the language since common prefixes vary in form by context.
(p.236)

### §12.12.5 Latin numerals

Latin uses Roman numerals (described elsewhere, at §11.5), generally set in small capitals. *C* was
sometimes written in reversed form in numerals: Ɔ) for 500 (normally *D*), CƆ) for 1,000 (normally
*M*), or *ī*, which — unlike *M* — was already in use in antiquity. (p.236)

### §12.13.1 Old and Middle English alphabet

Old English runs to roughly 1150; Middle English, roughly 1150–1500. Both use special characters
beyond the modern alphabet: ash (Æ/æ), eth/edh (Ð/ð, a "crossed d," used for both *that*-type and
*thin*-type *th* indiscriminately), thorn (Þ/þ, pronounced the same as eth — easily confused with p
or wynn by a printer), wynn/wyn/wen (Ƿ/ƿ, representing *w*, usually substituted with modern *w* now
since it's easily confused with thorn), and yogh (Ȝ/ȝ, used mainly where modern English has *gh*
and *y*). Old English's letter-g equivalent ("insular g," pronounced hard/soft/guttural by context)
later split into the "Carolingian g" (post-Norman Conquest, pronounced as in *go* or *gentle*) and a
developed insular form that became yogh, carrying the voiceless spirant heard in *ni3t* 'night'
(still audible in Scots *nicht*). Except in special cases, Old English yogh is now represented by g;
in Middle English work, g and yogh must stay distinct, and 3 is used for yogh throughout (sometimes
also for *z* in some Middle English texts). Eth died out by the end of the 13th century; thorn
persisted into the 15th century and later as a *y* substitute in early Scots printing and in *ye*/
*yt*/*y^t* for "the"/"that" — hence "Ye Olde" originally read as "The Old." Editors should not
attempt to regularize eth/thorn usage even within the same word, since scribes themselves used both
letters at random. (pp.236–238)

### §12.13.2 Old and Middle English punctuation

Outside specialized texts, normal modern English punctuation conventions are applied to Old and
Middle English. When working from manuscripts, editors should not regularize or "correct" individual
punctuation marks, since those marks did not necessarily serve the same functions as their modern
counterparts — e.g. in late Old English (10th–11th c.), the semicolon was the strongest stop and the
full point the weakest. (p.238)

### §12.14.1–2 Portuguese introduction and spelling

Brazilian and European Portuguese differ far more in spelling, pronunciation, and syntax than
American and British English do; spelling-agreement efforts between the two have not succeeded, and
(unlike English) spelling and pronunciation differences in Portuguese generally go hand in hand. The
letters *k, w, y* occur only in loanwords. Apart from *rr* and *ss*, doubled consonants are confined
to European Portuguese, which Brazilian Portuguese simplifies (*acção → ação*, *accionista →
acionista*, *commummente → comumente*); several consonant groups are likewise simplified in Brazil
(*acto, amnistia, excepção, óptico, súbdito* ['subject of a king'], *subtil* → *ato, anistia, exceção,
ótico, súdito, sutil*). Brazilian spells 16/17/19 as *dezesseis/dezessete/dezenove* against European
*deza-*; 14 may be *quatorze* or *catorze* in Brazil. There are also vocabulary/idiom differences,
such as frequent Brazilian omission of the definite article after *todo* 'every' and before
possessives. (p.238)

### §12.14.3 Portuguese accents

European Portuguese uses four written accents (acute, grave, circumflex, tilde/*til*); Brazilian
Portuguese adds the diaeresis (used on *u* between *q/g* and *e/i*, to show the vowel is pronounced
separately). The acute may fall on any vowel; the grave only on *a*; the circumflex on *a, e, o*; the
tilde on *a* and *o*. Stress is unaccented when normal (penultimate syllable in words ending *-a,
-am, -as, -e, -em, -ens, -es, -o, -os*; final syllable otherwise, including words in *-ã*) and
accented when abnormal. The circumflex appears before *m*/*n*+consonant in both varieties
(*circunstância, paciência, cômputo/cómputo*); before *m*/*n*+vowel, Brazilian uses a circumflex
where European uses an acute (*Nêmesis/Némesis*, *helênico/helénico*, *cômodo/cómodo*,
*sônico/sónico*) — hence *António* (European) vs. *Antônio* (Brazilian), and editors should spell a
named person's name per their actual nationality. (p.239)

### §12.14.4–5 Portuguese punctuation and word division

Unlike Spanish, Portuguese does not use inverted question/exclamation marks. For word division: take
over *ch, lh, nh*, and *b, c, d, f, g, p, t, v* followed by *l* or *r*; divide *rr, ss*, and *sc,
sç*; otherwise divide at obvious prefixes (*auto-, extra-, supra-*). When a word already containing a
hyphen is divided at the line break, the hyphen is repeated at the start of the next line
(*dar-lho* → *dar- -lho*). (p.239)

### §12.15.1 Russian alphabet and transliteration

Russian is one of six Slavonic languages written in Cyrillic (the others listed at §12.17). The full
alphabet, with upright and cursive forms and a transliteration per British Standard 2979 (1958), is
tabulated (Table 12.7, not reproduced here) — problems of transliteration are discussed further at

### §12.15.2 Russian abbreviations

Soviet-era non-literary Russian used abbreviations far more heavily than post-Soviet usage.
Lower-case abbreviations with full points keep any internal spaces from the original (*и т. д.*,
*и пр.*, but *с.-д.*); contraction-type abbreviations (*д-р*) take no points; a solidus (/) typically
marks abbreviations of unhyphenated compound words. Capital-initial abbreviations (*ООО* 'Ltd') are
set close with no internal or final points. Common lower-case abbreviations pronounced syllabically
and declined like ordinary nouns (*вуз*) are not pointed. Metric/unit abbreviations are usually set
in cursive without a following point, though abbreviated qualifying adjectives do take the point
(*5 кв. км*). (pp.240–241)

### §12.15.3 Russian capitalization

Capital initials are generally rarer in Russian than English. Personal names are capitalized, but
nouns/adjectives formed from them are lower case (*троцкист* 'Trotskyite', *марксизм* 'marxism'), as
are nationalities and inhabitants of towns (*татарин* 'Tartar', *англичанин* 'Englishman') and ranks/
titles (*святой Николай* 'Saint Nicholas', *князь Оболенский* 'Prince Obolensky'). Every word in a
country name is capitalized (*Соединённые Штаты Америки*). Adjectives from geographic names are
lower case unless part of a proper institutional name (*европейские государства* lower case, but
*Архангельские воздушные линии* capitalized). Generic geographic terms within a place name stay
lower case (*остров Рудольфа*, *Северный полюс*). Only the first word and proper nouns are
capitalized in titles of organizations, institutions, and literary/musical works, newspapers, and
journals. Days and months are lower case, except *Первое мая*/*1-е Мая* for May Day. The first-person
pronoun *я* ('I') is lower case except sentence-initial — unlike English "I." (p.241)

### §12.15.4 Russian punctuation

The hyphen joins two-element compound nouns (*интернет-сайт* 'website') and compound place names.
En rules are not used in Russian typography; spaced em rules take their place, and may stand in for
an implied word (often *быть* 'to be') or introduce direct speech — e.g. *линия Москва — Киев* 'the
Moscow–Kiev line', or *Иван играет на рояле, а Людмила — на скрипке* ('Ivan plays the piano,
Lyudmila the violin'). Guillemets, set close, mark direct speech, special word usage, and titles of
literary works/journals. (p.242)

### §12.15.5 Russian word division

Russian syllables end in a vowel, and division is basically syllabic, with exceptions tied to word
formation. Consonant groups may be taken over whole or divided (provided at least one consonant is
taken over), subject to rules: never separate a consonant from the prefix/root/suffix it belongs to
(*род|ной*, *подбежать → под|бежать*); divide between double consonants (*клас|сами*) except where
this conflicts with the prefix/suffix rule (*класс|ный*); never leave a single letter, or two-plus
consonants without a vowel, at a line end or carried over; the letters ъ, ь, and й must never be
separated from the letter preceding them. (pp.241–242)

### §12.15.6 Russian numerals

Arabic numerals are used. Numbers from 10,000 up are grouped by thin spaces, not commas (*26 453*);
below 10,000 they're set closed up (*9999*). The decimal comma replaces the decimal point
(*0,36578*). Ordinal numbers take a contracted adjectival ending, except in dates (*5-й год* but
*7 ноября 1917 г.*). (p.243)

### §12.16.1 Scandinavian languages: Danish, Norwegian, Swedish

Norway used Danish as its literary language until the mid-19th century and now has two written
standards: *bokmål*/*riksmål* (modified Danish) and *nynorsk*/*landsmål* (reconstructed from
Norwegian dialect, as if Danish had never been imposed) — each with several variants, where the only
safe assumption for a non-expert is that inconsistencies are correct. Danish and Norwegian share an
alphabet (English 26 letters + æ, ø, å); Swedish instead has å, ä, ö. Å — present in Swedish since
the 16th century — was adopted in Norway in 1907 and Denmark in 1948; previously these languages
used *aa* (capital *Aa*). Acute accents occur in loanwords and Swedish surnames, and occasionally for
clarity (Danish *én* 'one', neuter *ét*/*een*/*eet*, vs. indefinite article *en*/*et*); the grave
sometimes marks emphatic forms in Norwegian. Until 1948, Danish capitalized nouns as German does
(also found in 19th-century Norwegian); all three languages now favor lower case generally (days,
months, festivals, historical events, book titles), though periodical/series titles are legally
deposited names that keep whatever capitals they have. Institutional names often capitalize only the
first and last word, but in Danish/Norwegian names beginning with the independent definite article
(*Den, Det, De* — Nynorsk *Dei*) that article is always capitalized and included. Polite second-
person forms are capitalized in Danish/Norwegian (*De, Dem, Deres*; Nynorsk *De, Dykk, Dykkar*);
Danish also capitalizes the familiar second-person plural *I* to distinguish it from *i* 'in'. For
word division: Danish takes over *sk, sp, st*, and 3+-consonant clusters including *skj, spj*;
Norwegian/Swedish take over only the last letter (*ng* as a single sound is kept back; Swedish *x*
too); other single-sound clusters are taken over (Norwegian *gj, kj, sj, skj*; Swedish *sk* before
*e, i, y, ä, ö*). Swedish compounds reduce three identical consonants to two, restored when the word
is broken (*rättrogen* → *rätt|trogen*). (pp.243–244)

### §12.16.2 Icelandic and Faroese

Both languages place *d* immediately before *ð* in alphabetical order; Icelandic also places *þ, æ,
ö* after *z*, while Faroese places *æ, ø* there instead. Acute accents may occur on *a, e, i, o, u,
y* in both. Icelandic uses *x* where Faroese uses *ks*; Icelandic *þ* corresponds to Faroese *t*.
Icelandic capitalization is minimal — proper nouns only, and in institutional names only the initial
article (*Hinn*/*Hin*/*Hið*, masc./fem./neut.) is capitalized. Faroese follows Danish capitalization
practice, though its polite pronouns are not capitalized. (p.244)

### §12.17.1 Slavonic scripts and transliteration

Of the Slavonic languages, Russian, Belarusian, Ukrainian, Bulgarian, and Macedonian are written in
Cyrillic; Polish, Czech, Slovak, Sorbian, Croatian, Bosnian, and Slovene in the Latin alphabet;
Serbian may use either at time of writing. Languages beyond Russian call for additional Cyrillic
sorts (Belarusian і/ў; Macedonian ѓ/ѕ/ј/љ/њ/ќ/џ; Serbian ђ/ј/љ/њ/ћ/џ; Ukrainian г/є/і/ї), and some
Macedonian/Serbian fonts render cursive *z, n, m* with superior bars. Transliteration systems for
Cyrillic largely agree but have no single internationally agreed standard; of the three most-favored
(ALA–Library of Congress, International Scholarly, and British), ALA-LC appears to be gaining ground
due to library/cataloguing use. Editors should adhere to one system per work and avoid mixing, e.g.,
British *ya, yo, yu* with Library of Congress *ia, io, iu* or philological *ja, jo, ju* (note that
Serbian and Macedonian transliteration follows different rules from this set). (p.245)

### §12.17.2 Belarusian

Standard transliteration uses the diacritics ĭ, ŭ, è (or é/ê), and the soft-sign apostrophe; the
philological system additionally requires č, ë, š, ž. The Library of Congress system calls for
ligatured ia/io/iu/zh, but the ligature is often omitted in practice by non-specialist typesetters —
true of every Slavonic language using this transliteration convention. (p.245)

### §12.17.3 Bosnian, Croatian, and Serbian

Linguistic naming in the former Yugoslavia remains contentious: "Serbo-Croat" is still used by some
linguists in Serbia and Bosnia-Herzegovina, but the ISO has assigned separate codes to Bosnian,
Croatian, and Serbian. As a simplification, Bosniaks use Bosnian, Croats use Croatian, and Serbs
(in Serbia, Montenegro, and Bosnia-Herzegovina) use Serbian. The Roman *latinica* alphabet used for
Bosnian/Croatian is also used to transliterate Serbian even for lay readers — so четници is rendered
*četnici*, not an ad hoc *chetnitsi*. The Cyrillic alphabetical order is а б в г д ђ е ж з и ј к л љ
м н њ о п р с т ћ у ф х ц ч џ ш; both transliterated Serbian and Croatian *latinica* order is a b c
č ć d dž đ e f g h i j k l lj m n nj o p r s š t u v z ž. Diacritics for transliterated Serbian are
ć, č, š, ž; the special characters Đ, đ apply to both. (p.246)

### §12.17.4 Bulgarian

The ALA-LC transliteration system uses diacritics ĭ, ŭ, and letter combinations zh, kh, eh, sh, sht,
plus the ligatures ia, iu, ts; the philological system instead requires č, š, ž. (p.246)

### §12.17.5 Czech and Slovak

Both use the Roman alphabet with largely overlapping diacritic sets (Czech: á, é, í, ó, ú, ý, č, ď,
ě, ř, š, ť, ž; Slovak additionally has ä, ĺ, ľ, ô, ŕ); in alphabetizing both, ignore accents on vowels
and on d/n/t (plus l/r for Slovak). The palatalization of d/t is marked by a háček in upper case
(Ď, Ť) and, in lower case, by either a háček or — preferably — a "high comma right" (d', t'); Slovak
marks its palatalized l the same way, in upper case with either mark (Ľ) and lower case with a high
comma right (ľ). (p.246)

### §12.17.6 Macedonian

Macedonian is written in Cyrillic with a transliteration system similar to Serbian's. Diacritics
used are ǵ, ḱ, č, š, ž, and the apostrophe; the letter combinations lj, nj, dž must not be split in
word division. (p.247)

### §12.17.7 Old Church Slavonic

Also called Old Bulgarian, Old Church Slavonic was written in both the Cyrillic and the older
Glagolitic alphabets; its regional later variants are collectively "Church Slavonic." The Library of
Congress transliteration uses diacritics ǵ, f̀, v̀, ẏ, ě, and ligatures ia/ie/iu/ks/ps/ts/ię/iǫ (with
an ogonek or right-facing hook on e/o), plus ōt, ē, ī, ō, ȳ, ĭ, ę, ǫ (ogonek). In Russian Church
Slavonic specifically, *jalyalia* may correspond to ę and ie̊ to ǫ, and *julyuliu* to iǫ. The
philological system instead uses č, š, ž; special characters are the soft sign (′) and hard sign
(″). (p.247)

### §12.17.8 Polish

Polish uses the Roman alphabet without q, v, x, and employs the diacritics ć, ń, ó, ś, ź, ż, plus ą
and ę (ogonek/hook right); it also has one unique special character, the crossed ("Polish") l (Ł,
ł). Alphabetical order is a ą b c ć d e ę f g h i j k l ł m n ń o ó p r s ś t u w y z ź ż. The
digraphs ch, cz, dz, dź, dż, rz, sz are not treated as single letters for alphabetization purposes,
but must not be split when dividing words. (p.247)

### §12.17.9 Slovene

Slovene (Slovenian) uses the Roman alphabet with the háček on č, š, ž; the digraph dž is not treated
as a single letter. (p.247)

### §12.17.10 Ukrainian

The philological transliteration system requires č, š, ž, ĭ, ï, and the soft sign, plus the letter
combinations je, šč, ju, ja. The Library of Congress system requires ligatured ia/ie/iu/zh, as with
Belarusian — again, the ligature is often dropped in practice. Ukrainian и is transliterated as y,
not i — i instead represents the distinct Ukrainian letter і. (p.247)

### §12.18.1 Spanish introduction

Peninsular and Latin American Spanish are mutually intelligible much as British and American English
are, but there are real vocabulary/usage differences both between Spain and Latin America and among
the Latin American countries themselves — "Latin American Spanish" is not a uniform variant.
(p.248)

### §12.18.2 Spanish alphabet and spelling

*Ch* and *ll* were once treated as separate letters for alphabetizing purposes; this is now very
dated. *Ñ*/*ñ* is a genuinely separate letter. *K* and *w* occur only in loanwords and their
derivatives. The only doubled consonants in Spanish are *cc* (before e/i), *rr*, *ll*, and *nn* (in
compounds) — a mnemonic for which is the consonants in "CaRoLiNe." Where English might expect a
doubled consonant, Spanish normally has just one (*posible* 'possible'). (p.248)

### §12.18.3 Spanish accents

Normal stress falls on the penultimate or final syllable by complex rules and is unmarked; an acute
accent marks a break from the normal-stress pattern. The only other diacritics are the tilde on ñ
and the diaeresis on ü (before e/i, where u forms a diphthong with the following vowel — following
g). Accents are normally kept on capital letters. Accented interrogative/exclamatory words include
*cuál* 'what, which', *cómo* 'how', *cuándo* 'when', *cuánto* 'how many/much', *dónde* (printed with
the gloss 'when' in this source — almost certainly a typo for 'where,' flagged above), *qué* 'what',
and *quién* 'who'; the accent is retained in indirect questions too. A 1959 Real Academia ruling
removed the accent requirement (except in ambiguous cases) from demonstrative pronouns *este, ese,
aquel* and their feminine/plural equivalents (*esta, esa, aquella, estos/estas, esos/esas,
aquellos/aquellas*) — a ruling not universally accepted in practice, so both conventions occur. The
neuter forms *esto, eso, aquello* are never accented. (pp.248–249)

### §12.18.4 Spanish capitalization

Capitals are used much as in English for proper names. Book/poem/play/article titles normally
capitalize only the first word and proper nouns. High political/religious authority titles are
capitalized when not followed by the person's first name, and references to God (*el Rey, la Reina,
los Reyes, el Papa, creer en Dios*), but lower case when the name follows (*el rey Carlos III, el
papa Juan XXIII*). Roman-numeral centuries are given in full capitals (*el siglo XI*). Post/title
names are usually lower case (*catedrático, doctora, duquesa, general, juez, ministro, presidente*),
including non-Spanish titles like *sir* or *lord* (*el embajador británico sir Derek Plumbly*). Also
lower case: nationality/religion/ethnic-group nouns and adjectives (*francés/francesa,
católico/-a, dominicano/-a, asiático/-a, amerindio/-a*); artistic/literary movement names
(*romanticismo, gótico, surrealismo*); street/road names (*calle de Hortaleza, avenida de Navarra,
puente de Santiago* — but *la Gran Vía*); and administrative-division/geographic-feature names, plus
cardinal points when not part of a proper name (*provincia de Toledo, estado de Nueva York, condado
de Lancashire, cordillera de los Andes, lago Titicaca, el sur de Francia*). Traditionally lower case,
day/month/season names are now very commonly capitalized and this should not be assumed an error.
(p.249–250)

### §12.18.5 Spanish punctuation

Spanish's most distinctive punctuation feature versus other European languages is the inverted
exclamation/question mark placed where the exclamation or question actually begins — which need not
be the sentence's start (*Si ganases el Gordo, ¿qué harías?* 'If you won the jackpot what would you
do?'). Quotation marks take the form of guillemets (*comillas*), set closed up, though dispensing
with *comillas* altogether and marking speakers with a dash instead is more common, especially in
fiction. Colons introduce quotations, letter salutations, and enumerations. Points of suspension are
set closed up before and spaced after. (p.250)

### §12.18.6 Spanish word division

The general rule: a consonant between two vowels, and the second of two consonants, is taken over to
the next line. *Ch, ll, rr* are indivisible and always taken over whole (*mu-cha-cho, arti-lle-ria,
pe-rro*). *B, c, f, g, p* followed by *l* or *r* are taken over as a pair, as are *dr* and *tr*
(*ha-blar, ju-glar, ma-dru-gada, pa-tria*). *S* is always divided from a following consonant
(*Is-lam, hués-ped, Is-rael, cris-tiano, es-trella*), including within a compound (*ins-tar,
ins-piración*). Compounds divide at their component parts, except where they contain *s*+consonant
or *rr* (*des-hacer, sub-lunar*; but *circuns-tancia, co-rregir, inte-rrumpir*). Diphthongs and
triphthongs are never divided, and dividing between vowels should be avoided where possible. (p.250)

### §12.19.1 Welsh alphabet and accents

Welsh has 28 letters, alphabetized a b c ch d dd e f ff g ng h i j l ll m n o p ph r rh s t th u w
y; *k* and *v* are frequent in medieval texts but now obsolete. *Rh* counts as a separate letter only
at the start of a word or syllable, and only when it follows a consonant, not a vowel (*rhad* 'cheap'
alphabetizes before *route*; *cynrychioli* before *cynrhon* 'maggots'; but *arholi* before *arian*
'money', since there *rh* follows a vowel); *w* is usually, *y* always, a vowel. All vowels (including
w, y) may take a circumflex; acute, grave, and diaeresis also occur — most often acute on á in final
syllables (*casáu* 'to hate') and diaeresis on ï before a vowel (*copïo* 'to copy'), with the
diaeresis always preceding or following another vowel. An apostrophe followed by a single letter is
set close to the preceding word (*cerddai'r bachgen a'i fam i'ch pentref*, "the boy and his mother
used to walk to your village"). (p.251)

### §12.19.2 Welsh word division

The digraphs ch, dd, ff, ll, ph, rh, th are never divided. *Ng* is indivisible when it represents a
single sound, but divisible when it represents n+g — most often in the verb *dangos* 'to show' and
its derivatives, compounds ending *-garwch*/*-garwr* (e.g. *ariangarwr* 'money-lover'), place names
starting *Llan-* (*Llangefni, Llangollen*), and in *Bangor* — thus *cyng-aneddol* but *dan-gosol*. The
combinations ae, ai, au, aw, ayw, ei, eu, ew, ey, iw, oe, oi, ou, ow, oyw, wy, yw, and others
beginning with consonantal i/w, are never divided; it is legitimate, however, to divide after a
diaeresis-bearing vowel, or after a diphthong/triphthong before another vowel (*barddoï-aidd*
'bardic', *gloyw-ach* 'brighter', *ieu-anc* 'young'). Generally, take back a single consonant other
than h (except after the prefixes *di-, go-, tra-*): *g-l* but *s-gl*; a suffix beginning with i+vowel
must be broken off (*casgl-iad* 'collection'). It is always safe to divide l-rh, ng-h, m-h, n-h (but
not n-nh), n-n, n-rh, r-h, and after a vowel, r-h. Initial *gwl-, gwn-, gwr-* and their mutated forms
must never be divided, since the *w* is consonantal (*gwlad* 'country', *(hen) wlad* '(old) country';
*gwneud* 'to do', *(ei) wneud* 'to do (it)'; *gwraig* 'woman', *(y) wraig* '(the) woman'). (pp.251–252)

### §12.19.3 Welsh mutations and other changes

As in Irish Gaelic, initial consonants are replaced by others in certain grammatical contexts, a
process called mutation: *cath* 'cat' but *fy nghath* 'my cat', *ei gath* 'his cat', *ei chath* 'her
cat'. Place names mutate the same way after prepositions: *Caerdydd* (Cardiff), *Dinbych* (Denbigh),
*Gwent* give *i Gaerdydd* 'to Cardiff', *yng Nghaerdydd* 'in Cardiff', *i Ddinbych* 'to Denbigh', *yn
Ninbych* 'in Denbigh', *i Went* 'to Gwent', *yng Ngwent* 'in Gwent'; 'Oxford' is *Rhydychen*, but
'from Oxford' is *o Rydychen*. The full mutation range is: b→f/m; c→ch/g/ngh; d→dd/n; g→zero/ng;
ll→l; m→f; p→b/mh/ph; rh→r; t→d/nh/th. Initial vowels may acquire a preliminary h (*offer* 'tools' →
*ein hoffer* 'our tools'), and stress-pattern changes within a word can cause an h to appear/
disappear or a double n/r to simplify: *brenin* 'king' / *brenhinoedd* 'kings'; *brenines* 'queen' /
*breninesau* 'queens'; *corrach* 'dwarf' / *corachod* 'dwarfs'; *cynneddf* 'faculty' / *cyneddfau*
'faculties'; *cynnin* 'shred' / *cynhinion* 'shreds'; *dihareb* 'proverb' / *diarhebion* 'proverbs'.
(p.252)

### §12.20 Yiddish (opening paragraph only — chunk boundary)

Yiddish originated as German spoken by Jews; since the later 18th century it has been based on
dialects spoken to the east of Germany proper. It is written in an adaptation of the Hebrew
alphabet, with extra characters added for the basic German-derived vocabulary, and loanwords taken
from surrounding (mostly Slavonic) languages. Several other letters are used only in words taken
directly from Hebrew and Aramaic, which keep the spelling of their language of origin. **This is as
far as the assigned page range (PDF 247–270 / printed pp. 229–252) extends — §12.20's own
alphabet/orthography subsections, if the source has any, begin on a later page and are out of scope
for this file and this wave**, per the dispatch (Chapter 13 "Law and Legal References" begins
immediately afterward at PDF page 271). (p.252)

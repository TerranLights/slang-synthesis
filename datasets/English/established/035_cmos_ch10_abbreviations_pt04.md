## Grammar points

### 10.55 SI unit symbols take no periods and don't change in the plural

Unlike most other abbreviation categories in this chapter, SI symbols never take a period and are
identical in singular and plural (e.g., 1 kg, 50 kg). Most symbols are lowercased; exceptions are
symbols derived from proper names (A for *ampere*) and symbols that must stay distinguishable from
similar lowercased forms. Spelled-out unit names are lowercased even when derived from a proper
name, except for degree Celsius (°C).

### 10.56 Seven base SI units, and units vs. quantities

The SI rests on seven fundamental ("base") units: length (meter, m), mass (kilogram, kg), time
(second, s), electric current (ampere, A), thermodynamic temperature (kelvin, K), amount of
substance (mole, mol), and luminous intensity (candela, cd). Weight and mass are usually measured in
the same units but are conceptually distinct — weight is a force due to gravity dependent on an
object's mass. The symbols for base *units* (roman type) must not be confused with the symbols for
the corresponding *quantities* (italic type), e.g. *l* for length, *m* for mass, *t* for time.

### 10.57 SI prefixes as powers of ten, and the binary-multiple exception

SI prefixes represent powers of ten and attach to a base unit name to form multiples/submultiples
(e.g. kilometer = 10³ meters, millisecond = 10⁻³ second). In three cases the prefix's final vowel is
dropped before the unit's initial vowel: kilohm (not kiloohm), megohm (not megaohm), hectare (not
hectoare). These decimal prefixes should not be used for powers of two, as in computing; binary
multiples instead append *bi* to the first two letters of the SI prefix (kibi-, mebi-, gibi-, tebi-,
pebi-, exbi-).

### 10.58 Gram, not kilogram, as the base for mass prefixes

Although the kilogram, not the gram, is the official SI base unit for mass, prefixes are
conventionally applied to the term *gram* for historical reasons — e.g. megagram (Mg), milligram
(mg), nanogram (ng).

### 10.59–10.60 Derived units are expressed algebraically

Units not among the seven base units are "derived" and expressed algebraically in terms of base
units or other derived units (e.g. m² for square meter, m/s for meter per second, kg/m³ for kilogram
per cubic meter). Some derived units carry their own special name and symbol (hertz/Hz, volt/V,
watt/W), and these special-named units can themselves combine algebraically to form further derived
units (joule per kelvin = J/K; newton meter = N · m, using a raised dot for multiplication). A given
derived unit can sometimes be expressed more than one way — the weber as Wb, or equivalently as the
volt second (V · s).

### 10.61 Spacing conventions specific to SI usage

Only numbers between 0.1 and 1,000 should be used to express the quantity of an SI unit — 12,000
meters is expressed as 12 km (not "12 000 m"), and 0.003 cubic centimeters as 3 mm³ (not "0.003
cm³"). Contrary to general usage, SI usage stipulates a space before a percentage sign (%) and before
a degree symbol used with C — "22° 14′ 33″ *but* 22 °C" and "0.5 % (*not* 0.5%)." Many publications
don't observe these SI-specific exceptions, and Chicago itself doesn't require them.

### 10.62 Certain non-SI units remain accepted for use

Widely used units such as the liter (L — capitalized specifically to avoid confusion with the
numeral 1), the metric ton (t), and the hour (h) aren't officially part of the SI but are accepted
for use within it.

### 10.63–10.65 Astronomical notation builds on the SI base

Astronomers and astrophysicists employ the SI supplemented with their own specialized terminology and
abbreviations; the source offers only a representative minimum for the generalist, pointing to the
International Astronomical Union for fuller guidelines. Right ascension (RA or α) is given in hours,
minutes, and seconds of sidereal time, set as superscripts (e.g. 14ʰ6ᵐ7ˢ). Declination (δ) is given
in degrees, minutes, and seconds of arc north (marked + or left unmarked) or south (marked −) of the
celestial equator, also set as superscripts (e.g. −49°8′22″). Decimal fractions of these units follow
the same superscript convention (14ʰ6ᵐ7ˢ.2; +34°.26). Beyond coordinates, a small additional set of
astronomical-distance and time abbreviations is given: AU/ua (astronomical unit), lt-yr
(light-year), pc/kpc/Mpc (parsec and its multiples), and UT/UTC (universal time).

### 10.66 Chemical-element naming authority and provisional names

The International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) is the recognized body that formally
approves element names. Each element carries both a number (reflecting its count of protons) and a
name; the number becomes an important identifier in cases where formal names are disputed — between
1995 and 1997 the American Chemical Society and IUPAC used different names for some of the same
elements before reconciling. Names for elements that are undiscovered or unconfirmed (as of this
book's 2010 publication, up through element 118, with 111/roentgenium being the last confirmed
element) are provisionally assigned using Latin digit roots for the atomic number — e.g. *ununbium*,
"one-one-two," for element 112. Element names are always lowercased; element symbols always carry an
initial capital and take no periods. Where the symbol derives from a term other than the common
English name (e.g. Sb for antimony, from *stibium*), the Latin/other source term is given in
parentheses.

### 10.67–10.68 Periods and plural forms for US-measure abbreviations

In the rare instances where abbreviations for US units of measure appear in scientific copy, they're
usually set without periods; in nonscientific contexts, periods are customary. Like their scientific
counterparts, abbreviations of US units of measure are identical in the singular and the plural (10
yd., 5 lb., 8 sq. mi.) — unlike the time abbreviations at §10.71, which add -s. The unit of measure
in expressions like "0.5 yd." or "1.5 yd." is generally pronounced as if plural ("point five yards";
"one point five yards").

### 10.69 Prime and double-prime marks, no-space compounds, and conditional exponents

The proper symbols for foot and inch are the prime (′) and double prime (″), not a single or double
quotation mark. Compound measurement expressions like "6 ft. 1 in." are written with no space when
abbreviated using the prime/double-prime form: 6′1″. Exponents are sometimes used with abbreviations
to express area or volume, but only where no ambiguity can result — "425 ft.² (= 425 sq. ft., *not*
425 ft. by 425 ft.)."

### 10.70 Three overlapping US weight/mass systems

The US system of weight and mass comprises three distinct systems: avoirdupois (the common,
everyday system), troy (used mainly by jewelers), and apothecaries' measure. Though confusion is
unlikely in practice, an abbreviation can be qualified to the appropriate system when necessary: lb.
av. (avoirdupois), lb. t. (troy), lb. ap. (apothecaries'). The systems of capacity (liquid/dry)
measure used in the United States differ from the British Commonwealth's (an American pint is more
than three ounces smaller than a British pint), but the same abbreviations serve both. As with
length, abbreviations for weight and capacity don't change in the plural (12 gal., 3 pt.).

### 10.71 Time abbreviations pluralize with -s, unlike other measure abbreviations

Time abbreviations (sec., min., h./hr., d./day, mo., yr.) aren't limited to the US system and are
used mainly in nontechnical contexts. Unlike abbreviations of length, area, weight, and capacity, the
plurals of time abbreviations in nontechnical writing are often formed by adding an -s: "5 secs.," "12
hrs. or 12 h.," "15 yrs."

### 10.72 Lowercase periods convention for commercial abbreviations

As with many other nonscientific abbreviations in this chapter, periods for abbreviations of
commercial terms are normally used with lowercased forms. The source's representative list spans
accounting/finance (acct., AP, APR, COLA, GAAP), shipping/inventory (c.l., LCL, f.o.b., JIT, LIFO),
correspondence (attn., cc, bcc, c/o), and trademark/legal notation (®, ™ — both cross-referenced to
§8.152 for full style guidance). Several commercial abbreviations here (ASAP, TBA, TBD) have since
crossed over into general colloquial English well beyond business writing.

## Grammar points

### *Thou*/*thee*/*thy*/*thine* vs. *ye*/*you*/*your*: from number marking to social marking

OE originally had a simple **numeric** contrast for the 2nd person pronoun: *þu* (subject) / *þe*
(object) / *þin* (possessive) addressed exactly one person, while *ge* (subject) / *eow* (object) /
*eower* (possessive) addressed more than one. By the 16th century this had developed into something
different: a system marking the **social relationship** between speaker and addressee, layered on top
of (and eventually largely displacing) the original number contrast. *Ye*/*you*/*your* came to be used
to address a single person formally, or as a marker of the addressee's superior social rank; *thou*/
*thee*/*thy*/*thine* marked either informality/intimacy between social equals, or — pointedly — a
speaker's superiority when addressing a social inferior (e.g. a master addressing a servant with
*thou*, while the servant replied with the more formal/respectful *ye*). This social-meaning system
had crystallized well before the 16th century proper (the book's example is drawn from Chaucer's
1390s *Knight's Tale*, where the character Arcite addresses the gods Mars and Juno with intimate
*thow*/*thee* but, immediately after, addresses his as-yet-unmet idealized love Emelye with the
formal/distant *ye* — the asymmetry showing precisely that *thou* was not simply "older/more
poetic" but carried a specific social meaning Chaucer's audience would read as significant). The
distinction remained conventionally fixed in two major texts long after it had faded from ordinary
spoken use: the Church of England's *Book of Common Prayer* (from the 1540s) retained *thou* for
addressing God, and the 1611 King James Bible preserved the full *thou*/*ye* contrast (based on
earlier Tyndale/Coverdale translations), keeping both forms familiar to English churchgoers right up
until the 1960s' *New English Bible*. A separate complication: in OE/ME, *ye* was strictly a subject
form and *you* strictly an object form, but by the 16th century the two had become confused/largely
interchangeable in practice — abundant examples of this interchangeability are noted in Shakespeare.
(pp.136, 140, Sections 9.8.2 and 9.9.4) |

### The Great Vowel Shift: scope, timing, and the book's own evidence tables

Between Chaucer (late 14th c.) and Shakespeare (late 16th c.), **every long vowel** in the English
spoken across the Midlands and South of England shifted its pronunciation — conventionally named the
**Great Vowel Shift**. The book is explicit that no cause is known for why it happened, and that no
comparable wholesale long-vowel shift is independently attested for any other period of English. It
was not yet complete by 1569 (confirmed directly by John Hart's own description of the ⟨i⟩ vowel in
*exercise* already starting to be pronounced as a diphthong by some speakers — itself treated as
contemporary eyewitness evidence of the shift mid-flight), and showed real regional/social variation
throughout the century. A simplified short-vowel correspondence table is also given (ME /ɪ/, /ɛ/, /a/,
/ɒ/, /ʊ/ spelled ⟨i⟩, ⟨e⟩, ⟨a⟩, ⟨o⟩, ⟨u⟩ respectively, largely stable into MnE as /ɪ/, /ɛ/, /æ/, /ɒ/,
/ʊ~ʌ/) — the point being that, because the spelling system was never subsequently revised to track the
long-vowel changes, MnE short-vowel spellings still map fairly transparently onto their sounds while
MnE long-vowel spellings systematically do not (e.g. ⟨i⟩ representing /aɪ/ in *find*, not any kind of
/iː/). A second table traces the long vowels explicitly: OE/ME /iː/ → MnE /aɪ/ (*find*); /eː/ → MnE
/iː/ (*we, geese*); /ɛː/ → MnE /iː/ or /eɪ/ (*speak*; *lady*); /aː/ → MnE /eɪ/ (*lady*, an earlier
stage); /ɔː/ → MnE /oʊ/ (*oak*); /oː/ → MnE /uː/ (*do, goose*); /uː/ → MnE /aʊ/ (*cow, house*) — and
the book flags that OE/ME originally had **two separate pairs** of contrasting long front and back
vowels (/eː/ vs. /ɛː/, and /oː/ vs. /ɔː/) which the conventional spelling system never fully
distinguished; a second, independent 1580 spelling-reform attempt (William Bullokar's "amended
ortography") is cited as proposing dedicated digraphs/letters specifically to keep these four sounds
visually distinct, confirming contemporary reformers recognized the same four-way contrast
independently of Hart. (pp.128–130, Section 9.5)

### John Hart's 1569 spelling-reform argument and its three identified "abuses"

John Hart's *An Orthographie* (1569) is presented as one of the earliest systematic English
spelling-reform proposals, built on the explicit principle that "we must be ruled by our speech" —
i.e. that writing should track pronunciation, an assumption the book notes immediately collides with
the practical problem of **whose** pronunciation a single national spelling system should be built
around, since Hart himself recognized the country's dialect diversity (Section 9.7.1). Hart names
three specific "abuses" of the existing (and, the book notes, essentially still-current) English
spelling system: **superfluous letters** (redundant Roman-alphabet letters that could be dropped
without loss); **derivation-based spelling** (his rejection of the common argument that a loanword's
original-language spelling should be kept to preserve a visible record of its derivation — he
advocates full English-spelling-convention assimilation instead); and **difference-marking spelling**
(his rejection of using different spellings to distinguish words that are pronounced identically,
i.e. an early argument against spelling as a disambiguation device rather than a pure sound-representation
device). Hart's own reformed system, although never adopted, is noted to have anticipated two spelling
conventions that *did* eventually become standard: he explicitly rejected using a final silent ⟨e⟩ to
mark a preceding long vowel (*hate*/*hat*) and rejected doubling a consonant to mark a preceding short
vowel (*matting*/*mating*, *robbing*/*robing*) — conventions the book notes are still live, productive
parts of the MnE spelling system today, even though Hart himself preferred marking vowel length with a
diacritic dot instead. (pp.124–126, Sections 9.4 and Activities 9.6–9.7)

### *Do*-support and subject-verb inversion coexisting as alternative interrogative/negative strategies

By the late 16th century, English had **two** live, competing strategies for forming questions and
negatives in simple present/past tense, not yet settled into the single MnE pattern: the older
strategy, direct **subject-verb inversion** (*knowest thou?*, *came he?*; negation via *not* placed
after the inverted verb, *I know not*), remained fully current, while the newer periphrastic ***do***-
support strategy (*dost thou know?*, *did he come?*; *I do not know*) had also come into use alongside
it, without yet displacing the older pattern. The book places the **loss of the double/multiple
negative's standard status** at around this same period — multiple negation (standard throughout OE
and ME, as established in pt02/pt03/pt05's discussions) begins to be treated as non-standard roughly
now, even though, as the book notes explicitly, it remained — and still remains — entirely normal
usage in most spoken dialects of English today. (p.141, Section 9.9.4, "Interrogatives and negatives")

### The modal verb system as an established, closed paradigm by 1600

By the end of the 16th century the MnE **modal verb system** is described as fully established as its
own distinct grammatical category, with pairs *will/would*, *shall/should*, *can/couthe-coude*,
*dare/durst*, *may/might-mought*, and *mote/must* — directly continuing the OE-to-ME re-analysis of
originally independent full verbs into a closed auxiliary class already described in Singh's account
(see `072_history_of_english_students_guide_pt01.md`'s treatment of Lightfoot's modal re-analysis
argument), now confirmed complete by this later period. The passive voice was by this point fully in
productive use; perfect aspect was expressed with *have*, but — distinct from MnE — **also** with
*be* when the main verb was intransitive (e.g. *I am come*, not *I have come*), a construction type
that has not survived into Standard MnE. The present-tense 3rd-person-singular suffix shows a live,
dialectally sorted two-way split at this date: *-eth* (the Southern, and by this period more formal/
literary form, e.g. *doth*, *giues*) versus *-s* (the Northern, newer, and eventually standard form)
— the King James Bible (1611) preserved the older *-eth* suffix specifically because its translation
was based on the earlier (Tyndale/Coverdale) 16th-century translations it was revising, and
contemporary poets are noted to have used both forms interchangeably as needed for a line's metrical
syllable count, not simply at random. (pp.140–141, Section 9.9.4)

### Dramatic dialogue as indirect, imperfect dialect evidence

Two Shakespeare passages are used to make the same methodological point already made for the York
plays in pt04: stage dialogue reflects an *author's* and *audience's* perception/expectation of a
dialect's "more obvious" marked features, not a faithful phonetic transcription of real regional
speech. *Henry V*'s four comic captains (Gower = English, Fluellen = Welsh, Macmorris = Irish, Jamy =
Scots) are cited as evidence that Elizabethan audiences recognized and enjoyed broad national/regional
caricature through spelling, vocabulary, and syntax choices, without this necessarily being linguistically
precise. *King Lear*'s Edgar, disguised as the beggar "Tom o' Bedlam," switches into a markedly
dialectal register only in one specific scene (when confronting the Steward to protect his blinded
father) — and the dialect markers used (*ice*, *sal*, *gate*) are, per the book's own cross-check
against Verstegan's Text 83, identifiably **Northern**, even though the scene is nominally set in
**Kent** — treated as a live, open analytical question for the reader (Activity 9.13) about whether
Shakespeare was drawing on an accurate specific dialect model or using a more generic "rustic/
non-standard" signal regardless of geographic consistency. (pp.134–135, 137–138, Sections 9.8.1,
9.8.2, 9.9.4 example)

### The established written standard outpacing the spoken standard

By the end of the 16th century, a written standard (educated London English) was clearly established
for printed/formal writing, but this did **not** mean educated speakers had abandoned their own
regional speech — the book cites John Aubrey's mid-17th-century anecdote about Sir Walter Raleigh
(1552–1618), a Justice of the King's Bench under two monarchs and by any measure among the most
socially elevated men of his era, who nonetheless "spake broad Devonshire to his dying day." The point
drawn: in this period, unlike later, speaking in a regional accent did not in itself mark a speaker as
uneducated or socially inferior — the conflation of "educated" with "accent-neutral or RP-accented"
is a later development, not yet fixed by 1600. Puttenham's prescriptive advice (quoted at greater
length here than in pt02/pt06) is notably more explicit and more socially pointed than the brief
version seen earlier: he recommends "the vsuall speach of the Court, and that of London and the shires
lying about London within lx [60] myles," explicitly **excluding** "any speech used beyond the riuer
of Trent" even while conceding "no man can deny but that theirs is the purer English Saxon at this
day" — i.e. Puttenham knowingly acknowledges Northern English's greater historical/etymological
"purity" relative to London speech, and recommends against it anyway, purely on grounds of
contemporary social prestige. He also explicitly rejects using older canonical authors (*Piers
Plowman*, Gower, Lydgate, even Chaucer himself) as models, "for their language is now out of use with
vs" — a direct period statement that a text's literary canonicity and its value as a *contemporary*
language model are treated as two entirely separate questions. (pp.131–133, Sections 9.7–9.7.2)

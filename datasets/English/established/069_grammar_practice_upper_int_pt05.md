## Grammar points

### Forms and uses of the passive

Form: *to be* in the appropriate tense/form + past participle (p.70). The passive is specifically
not used in the present perfect continuous, the past perfect continuous, or the future continuous,
for reasons of style — but is otherwise usable across the full range of tenses and verb forms (the
book tabulates infinitive, perfect infinitive, participle/gerund, and perfect participle/gerund
pairs in active and passive). Five uses are catalogued (pp.70–71): when the receiver of the action
matters more than the performer, or the performer is unspecific — this also avoids vague subjects
like "someone"/"some people"; as a way of avoiding assigning responsibility for an action to a
specific person or organization (worked example: a company announcement using the passive to soften
an unwelcome policy change, explicitly contrasted with the more exposed active alternative "We/The
Company/The management will reduce the lunch break"); to avoid a change of grammatical subject
mid-sentence/paragraph, keeping continuity ("When they got to the police station, they were
immediately questioned"); with *by* + agent specifically to emphasize who/what performed the action,
most often with verbs describing creation or discovery-type events (the book's own list: *build,
compose, damage, design, discover, invent, make, write*); and with verbs of saying/believing in
three specific passive constructions — *It* + passive + *that*-clause ("It is expected that..."),
*There* + passive + *to be* ("There are believed to be..."), and Subject + passive +
*to*-infinitive ("She is considered to be the best singer...") — plus general use in notices, news
reports, and process descriptions.

**Notes (p.71):** some languages use an impersonal pronoun where English prefers the passive;
English *one* exists as a formal-register impersonal pronoun but is now used only occasionally in
formal English, with *you* functioning as the more common informal substitute for generic reference.
Separately, *get* + past participle is a fixed, idiomatic alternative to the passive for a specific
closed set of events/states (the book's own list: *get engaged, get married, get lost, get killed,
get dressed, get elected, get hit, get drunk*) — none of these carry an explicit agent.

### The causative have and the causative get

Form: *have/get* + object + past participle (p.75). Used when the performer of the action is not
the concern; the subject of *have/get* causes the action to happen rather than performing it
themselves ("We have/get the windows cleaned once a month"). *By* + agent can occasionally follow
the causative construction.

**Contrast: have and get (p.75).** The two are largely interchangeable, but causative *get*
additionally carries a sense of the *arranging* required for the action to happen (contrasted pair:
"I had the car serviced this morning" leaves open whether the speaker drove it to the garage
themselves, while "I got the car serviced this morning" implies they did); when the focus is on the
process happening rather than the arranging, *have* (not *get*) is used ("I like having my hair
done"). The causative *get* is not generally used in the present perfect or past perfect ("you've
had your hair done," not "you've got your hair done").

**Notes (p.76):** *get* + object + past participle can also describe something the subject did
themselves, with an implication that it was a lot of work or difficult but got finished ("I got all
my work done yesterday"); the causative *have/get* is also used, with verbs denoting crimes, when
the subject did not cause the event and was an unwilling victim rather than an arranger ("I had/got
my bag stolen last week").

### Constructions after wish

Three distinct *wish*-constructions for three distinct meanings (p.77): *wish* + past simple
expresses dissatisfaction with a present situation or repeated habitual activity ("I wish she spoke
French" = she doesn't, and the speaker would like her to); *were* is flagged as a slightly more
formal alternative to *was* after *I/she/he/it* in this construction. *Wish* + *would* expresses
dissatisfaction/annoyance about something the speaker wants to be different but does not expect to
change — when used about a person, it implies the person is refusing to change ("I wish she would
speak French" = she can, but won't). *Wish* + past perfect expresses regret about the past ("I wish
I had studied harder").

**Notes (p.77):** *if only...* can substitute for *I wish...*, carrying the same
dissatisfaction/regret meaning but somewhat more strongly ("If only I hadn't said that"); *wish* +
*to*-infinitive is a more formal way of saying *I want to.../I would like to...*, typically found in
formal spoken or written register ("The management wish to inform you that...").

### Phrasal verbs

A phrasal verb combines a verb with a particle (adverb or preposition); sometimes the meaning is
inferable from the parts ("Can you slow down?") but is often idiomatic and unrelated to the literal
meanings ("He takes after his mother in his mathematical ability") (p.79). Three structural types
are distinguished: **intransitive** (no object: "We must set off early tomorrow"); **transitive
separable** (the object can go either before or after the particle when it's a full noun phrase, but
*must* go between verb and particle when it's a pronoun: "turn down that job offer" / "turn that job
offer down" / "turn it down," never "turn down it"); and **transitive inseparable** (the object can
only follow the particle: "takes after her mother," never "takes her mother after").

**Notes (p.79):** some phrasal verbs are three words long — verb + adverb + preposition — and in
these the object can only go after the final preposition ("came up with lots of good ideas," "get
down to your work"); some verb+particle combinations carry more than one meaning and may function as
either transitive or intransitive depending on the sense in play (the book's own example: "pick up"
intransitively = business improving, vs. transitively = collect a person); and many verbs are
followed by a preposition without being true phrasal verbs at all — the verb and preposition each
retain their separate literal meaning ("walked through the room," "approve of this idea") — though
the book notes this distinction matters only for understanding meaning, not for any grammatical
rule.

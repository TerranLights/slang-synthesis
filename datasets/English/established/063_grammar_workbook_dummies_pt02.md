## Grammar points

### Comma usage: lists, direct address, dates, addresses, introductions/interrupters, descriptions (Ch.5)

List items are comma-separated, with the final item's comma before the joining word (*and*/*or*)
treated as optional house style; if any list item itself contains a comma, semicolons separate the
items instead to avoid ambiguity. Direct-address names/titles are set off by one comma if at the
start/end of a sentence or two commas if mid-sentence — "sending one comma to do a two-comma job" is
flagged as the most common direct-address error. American-style month-day-year dates take a comma
after the day (and after the year if the sentence continues); day-month-year dates take no commas
under open-style punctuation. Addresses use commas between each element and between city/state, but
never between state and ZIP code. Introductory expressions and mid-sentence interrupters are set off
by commas when they comment on or add extra information to the main clause; very short, tightly-tied
introductory phrases without a verb form (*"In the morning Brad drank..."*) may skip the comma — the
source offers a "read it aloud, comma where you'd naturally pause" heuristic. For descriptions: a
post-noun description gets commas if it's extra/non-essential information and no commas if it's
essential/identifying; a pre-noun description gets a comma only when stacking two or more
equal-weight, reorderable descriptions of the same type (*"the tan, dusty dictionary"*, reversible)
— not when the descriptions differ in type and can't be reordered (*"Two dusty dictionaries,"* not
*"dusty two"*). (pp.67–75)

### Hyphens (Ch.6)

Used for end-of-line word breaks (at syllable boundaries, never isolating one or two letters, never
dividing a one-syllable word), for compounds, and for attaching certain prefixes — especially when
omitting the hyphen would create ambiguity (*re-create* vs. *recreate*), when two vowels would
otherwise collide (*anti-insurance*), or when a prefix attaches to a capitalized word
(*anti-Republican*). Hyphens also link two words forming one shared description (*"a third-base
error"* vs. the differently-meaning unhyphenated *"a third base-error"*). The book notes hyphenation
is itself subject to changing fashion (*non-negotiable* → *nonnegotiable* over time) and that the
dictionary is the most reliable everyday guide. (pp.81–82)

### Dashes (Ch.6)

Used sparingly, for four purposes only: interrupting the flow of thought with an inserted aside
(paired dashes); summarizing or defining a list; showing an incomplete/cut-off sentence; and creating
dramatic emphasis before a reveal. No comma goes before or after a dash except when showing
incompleteness requires one after it. Dashes cannot join two complete sentences and cannot substitute
for a hyphen. (pp.82–83)

### Semicolons and colons (Ch.6)

A semicolon links two complete, closely related sentences, or separates list items when at least one
already contains a comma — it should never join two complete thoughts that aren't actually related.
A colon introduces a long quotation or a list, properly only after text that is itself a grammatically
complete sentence (not after *"for example"*, though many business/technical style guides relax this),
and never directly after a form of *to be* or a preposition. (pp.84–85)

### Apostrophes: the two most common misuses, and legitimate uses (Ch.7)

Never use an apostrophe to form a plural (*"two arrows,"* not *"two arrow's,"* including for
pluralized proper names: the Woodses, not the Woods') or inside a possessive pronoun (*my, your, his,
hers, its, ours, theirs, whose*). Legitimate uses: contractions (shortening words, see Vocabulary
table's Table 7-1), and possession — singular owner gets *'s* (*Henry's*), regular plural owner gets
just an apostrophe after the existing *-s* (*the boys'*), irregular plural owner (one that doesn't
already end in *-s*) gets *'s* like a singular (*the children's*), joint ownership puts *'s* only on
the last-named owner (*Abe and Mary's sofa*), separate ownership puts *'s* on each owner individually,
a hyphenated compound owner takes *'s* at the very end (*mother-in-law's*), and time/money
expressions can take possessive form by the same singular/plural rules (*next week's test, two
hours' homework*). (pp.91–94)

### Punctuating direct quotations (Ch.8)

Comma placement relative to a speaker tag depends on the tag's position (before: comma before the
opening quotation mark; after: comma inside the closing mark; mid-quotation: comma before the first
closing mark and after the tag). A period at the end of a quoted sentence goes inside the closing
quotation mark. A question mark or exclamation point that belongs to the quotation itself goes
inside the closing mark; one that belongs to the surrounding sentence (while the quotation itself is
neither a question nor exclamation) goes outside. No comma or initial capital is used when a
quotation is grammatically woven into the sentence without a speaker tag. Semicolons and colons
always sit outside the closing quotation mark. These rules are explicitly flagged as American-style
conventions that reverse in British English (see "inverted commas" in Vocabulary). (pp.101–103)

### Embedded quotations (Ch.8)

A quotation nested inside another quotation is marked with single quotation marks inside the
surrounding double quotation marks. When the embedded quotation ends at the same point as the
surrounding one, the single and double closing marks sit adjacent (single first); any comma/period
needed goes inside both. Question marks/exclamation points inside an embedded quotation follow the
same logic as plain quotations, nested one level deeper. (pp.103–105)

### Punctuating titles (Ch.8)

Full-length works (novels, magazines, TV series, plays, films, epic poems, and — despite their
sometimes-short length — pamphlets) are italicized/underlined. Shorter works or parts of a whole (a
poem, short story, single TV episode, song, article) are placed in quotation marks instead. A
centered, standalone title at the top of a page gets neither treatment unless it itself refers to
another work. When a quotation-marked title ends a sentence, the sentence's period/comma goes inside
the closing quotation mark; a question mark/exclamation point stays outside unless it's actually part
of the title itself, in which case it also serves as the sentence's own endmark (never double up
period + question mark). (pp.105–106)

### Capitalizing names, titles, and abbreviations (Ch.9)

A title immediately preceding and attached to a name is capitalized (*Mr. Smith, Professor Wiley*);
a title following or detached from a name generally isn't (*George Wiley, professor of psychology*),
except titles of the highest national/international rank, which may be capitalized even standing
alone. Business/academic institution names are capitalized but generic category words (*university*,
*conglomerate*) aren't; specific departments, project names, and course titles are capitalized in
headline style; general product/degree terms stay lowercase unless a specific named brand/award is
meant. Geographic names are capitalized when specific/proper (*Manhattan, North Dakota*) and
lowercase when generic (*city, river, canyon*); compass points are capitalized only when naming a
region (*the Northeast*), not a direction (*head south*); place names that have become generic common
vocabulary lose their capital (*french fries, russian roulette*). For abbreviations: acronyms take
capitals, no periods; initials/titles take capitals and periods; Latin abbreviations (*e.g., ibid.,
etc.*) stay lowercase with periods; *AM/PM* may be written capitalized-no-period or
lowercase-with-periods (consistently, either way); state abbreviations have shifted from the old
capital-plus-period style (*Ind., Ala.*) to the modern two-letter postal form (*IN, AL*); and an
abbreviation's period at the end of a sentence does double duty as the endmark (never stack two
periods). (pp.113–123)

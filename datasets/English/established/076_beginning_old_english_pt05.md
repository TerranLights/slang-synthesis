## Grammar points

### The surviving OE poetic corpus (p.96)

~30,000 lines survive, concentrated in four late-10th/early-11th-century manuscripts: the **Exeter
Book** (religious + secular poetry, incl. the riddles and elegies), the **Vercelli Book** (religious
poetry/prose), the **Junius Manuscript** (illustrated biblical-paraphrase poems), and the **Beowulf
Manuscript** (poetry/prose of marvels, now British Library). OE poetry is copied in continuous lines
like prose with no visual marker distinguishing it — only its internal form (meter, alliteration)
identifies it as verse.

### From Chapter 6 onward: unsimplified spelling (p.96–97)

The source explicitly stops normalizing þ/ð and other spelling variation from this chapter forward
— texts are presented closer to their actual manuscript forms, including mixed þ/ð within a single
poem.

### Alliterative meter: the core structural rule (p.98–99)

OE poetry has **no rhyme and no fixed syllable count**. Instead: each full line has **four main
stresses**, split into two half-lines ("a-verse" and "b-verse") linked by **alliteration** (shared
initial sound). The first stressed syllable of the b-verse (the **"headstave"**) must alliterate
with one or both stressed syllables of the a-verse; the b-verse's *second* stressed syllable must
**not** alliterate with the headstave (though it may occasionally echo a different a-verse syllable,
or anticipate the next line). Stress and alliteration land on word **stems**, not prefixes/
inflexions — so an unstressed *ge-* prefix is skipped over (stress falls on the second syllable of
*gehwæs*, *onstealde*). A compound can carry two stresses (*mōd-geðanc*, *middan-geard*). Consonant
clusters *sc*, *sp*, *st*, *hw* alliterate only with themselves, not with their bare initial
consonant; all vowels alliterate freely with any other vowel.

### Six stylistic devices of OE verse (p.99–101)

The chapter names and demonstrates six recurring techniques, all worked through Caedmon's Hymn:

- **Circumlocution/euphemism** — roundabout epithets in place of direct naming, e.g. *heofon-rīces
  Weard* 'Guardian of the heavenly kingdom' for God, *eorðan bearnum* 'children of earth' for people.
- **Compounding** — routinely used to pack more meaning per stressed syllable than a descriptive
  phrase would; six compounds appear in Caedmon's nine-line Hymn alone (see Vocabulary table).
- **Kennings** — a metaphorical (not literal) subtype of compound, e.g. *middan-geard* 'middle-
  dwelling' = earth, reflecting the belief earth sat midway between Heaven and Hell.
- **Formulas** — stock (half-)line phrases reusable across contexts/poems, originating in oral
  improvisation but retained in written composition — e.g. *ēce Dryhten* 'eternal Lord' recurs
  verbatim at lines 4a and 8a; *heofon-rīces Weard* is itself an adaptation of a secular-poetry
  formula *rīces weard* 'guardian of the kingdom' normally used of earthly kings.
- **Poetic diction** — a body of vocabulary (*Metod*, *Scieppend*, *Frēa*, *fīras*) attested *only*
  in verse, never in OE prose — a sharper prose/poetry vocabulary split than later stages of English
  show.
- **Variation** — restating the same syntactic role (e.g. the same Subject) two or three times running
  using different synonymous phrases, for emphasis/elaboration rather than for new information — e.g.
  Caedmon's Hymn names God as Subject of *tēode* 'adorned' three separate ways in a row (*man-cynnes
  Weard*, *ēce Dryhten*, *Frēa æl-mihtig*).

### Riddles (Exeter Book, ~95 total) (p.102–105)

A genre built on sustained ambiguity/wordplay, typically describing one thing in terms of another
(an object as alive, an animal as human) and often closing with the formula **Saga hwæt ic hātte**
'Say what I am called'. Device: **prosopopoeia** — the riddled object speaks of itself in the first
person (the "Horn" riddle). The "Book-Moth" riddle turns on four or five simultaneously double-sense
words (*forswealg*/*swealg* 'swallowed'/'understood', *ðȳstro* 'darkness'/'ignorance', *cwide*
'utterance'/'morsel', *staðol* 'foundation'/'parchment foundation'), letting the poem describe both
a moth eating parchment and a failed reader at once — and gradually personifies its subject across
the poem's five lines (*moþþe* 'moth' → *wyrm* 'worm' → *ðēof*/*stæl-giest* 'thief', all grammatically
masculine, licensing the closing *hē* 'he'). Some riddles' solutions remain genuinely unsolved by
scholarship; the "One-Eyed Garlic-Seller" riddle's trick is a body-part vocabulary set that misdirects
toward "monster" via *twelf hund hēafda* 'twelve hundred heads' (= heads of garlic) — the source also
flags this specific riddle as containing an apparent **copying error** (third-person body through the
poem, but a first-person closing formula).

### Elegiac poetry: *Wulf and Eadwacer* as a worked example of irreducible ambiguity (p.105–109)

OE poem titles are modern scholarly additions, not manuscript features. Names are not capitalized in
manuscripts, so OE personal names (themselves ordinary compound vocabulary words) can be genuinely
ambiguous between "proper name" and "common noun" readings — *Wulf* possibly = 'wolf' (word) rather
than a name; *Eadwacer* literally means 'guardian'. The source works through eight open interpretive
questions the grammar itself cannot resolve (who is Wulf; what is *lāc* 'gift'; what *ungelic*
'unlike' means given OE *un-*'s occasional *intensifying* rather than negating force — raising the
possibility of incest rather than mere difference; whether 17a *bireð* is present or future tense,
since OE present-tense verbs cover both; etc.) — a useful worked case study in how much OE
grammatical ambiguity (gender-marking via just two adjective endings, tense neutrality, spelling
inconsistency even within one poem, flexible poetic word order) can leave genuinely unresolved rather
than merely difficult.

### Heroic poetry and the comitatus code (p.109–112)

Only five surviving OE poems count as "heroic poems" proper, drawing on the pre-Christian Germanic
legend cycle: *Beowulf*, *Deor*, *Finnsburh*, *Waldere*, *Widsith* — though many more poems draw on
heroic-code *values* without being legend-cycle narratives. The heroic code (per the Roman historian
Tacitus's *Germania*) centers on a chief's **comitatus**, a sworn war-band owing total allegiance,
bound to avenge the chief's death or die trying, in exchange for hall-hospitality and gifts of land.
*Deor*'s opening lines (the Weland-the-smith legend) are used to show how even a widely-known legend's
specific OE wording can carry an unresolved textual crux — *wurman* (1a) is read alternatively as a
tribal name (the Vermar of Värmland), as *wurma* 'purple' (≈ blood), or as *wyrm* 'worm/snake/dragon'
(pointing at three entirely different narrative readings of the same half-line).

### Material culture corroboration: the Franks Casket (p.111–112)

An 8th-century whalebone box (British Museum) carving the Weland legend alongside a Christian
Adoration-of-the-Magi scene on the same panel, with a self-describing runic riddle around the border
(*Hronæs bān* 'whale's bone' — the riddle names the very material the casket itself is carved from).
The source uses this to argue that riddle, heroic-legend, and religious material genuinely coexisted
within single artifacts/manuscripts in Anglo-Saxon culture, not as an odd juxtaposition by later
standards but as an ordinary one by contemporary standards.

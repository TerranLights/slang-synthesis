## Grammar points

### Ch.9 Relative Clauses

#### Definition, intonation, and the two functions of restrictive relative clauses (§9.1–9.2.1)

A relative (REL) clause is an embedded subordinate clause functioning as a post-nominal modifier
inside an NP; most REL-clauses fall under one unified intonation contour with the whole NP, though
some (non-restrictive ones) retain their own contour. Givón treats the simple-clause/REL-clause
relationship as a descriptively useful "transformational" relation (relativization) without
committing to it as a literal mental operation (p.106). **Restrictive relative clauses (RRCs)**
have a two-part definition (3): semantically, one participant of the REL-clause's state/event must
be co-referential with the head noun; pragmatically, the speaker assumes that state/event is
familiar/accessible to the hearer or at least unlikely to be challenged as new information. RRCs
thus function in the grammar of **referential coherence**: they *ground* a referent by supplying a
presupposed proposition the hearer can use to locate it in their knowledge base (illustrated with a
bar scene — "that guy standing next to the bar" vs. "the gal I told you about," p.108–109). A
hearer's challenge ("How do you know?") to a sentence containing an RRC is normally interpreted as
targeting the *main clause's* assertion, not the RRC's content — direct evidence the RRC's content
is shielded, unchallengeable background information (table (8)–(10), p.110–111).

#### Grounding of REF-indefinite and generic heads; non-referring heads (§9.2.1.3–9.2.1.6)

RRCs also modify REF-indefinite heads, but here the grounding direction is **cataphoric** rather
than anaphoric: the speaker judges the referent's past mention too remote/unimportant to count as
accessible, so the RRC instead launches a *new* mental structure the hearer must build going forward
(table (14), p.113). Definite heads, by contrast, are grounded **anaphorically** to existing
episodic-text or speech-situation mental models (table (16), p.114). RRCs can also combine with
**generic/frame-based** definite reference, where the RRC supplies world-knowledge grounding for an
apparently-new-but-definite referent ("The plumbing in our house never works right," p.114–115).
When the head noun is itself **non-referring** (`anybody who marries my sister...`), the RRC
obligatorily falls under non-fact (irrealis or habitual) modality — a non-referring head is
incompatible with a realis-modality RRC (§9.2.1.5, p.115–116). **Semi-referring** indefinite
relative pronouns (`whoever`, `whatever`, `wherever`) head so-called headless REL-clauses whose
unexpressed head may itself be either referring (under fact modality) or non-referring (under
non-fact modality) — p.116–117.

#### Non-restrictive relative clauses (§9.2.2)

Non-restrictive REL-clauses (NRRCs) satisfy the semantic co-reference condition (3a) but not the
pragmatic one (3b): their content, though parenthetical and set off by an intonational break, is
genuinely **asserted** new information rather than presupposed — shown by the different, matter-of-
fact response an NRRC's content draws under challenge compared to an RRC's (examples (25)–(26),
p.118). NRRCs can ground definite (anaphoric), REF-indefinite (cataphoric), or generic/topical
(generic-knowledge) head referents (table (27), p.119), but — unlike RRCs — they **cannot modify
non-referring heads** (`*Anybody, who marries my sister, is asking for trouble`, p.119) nor
referentially-unique heads with a restrictive reading (`*John who is my friend is a poet` vs. `John,
who is my friend, is a poet`, p.121). NRRCs can mark a generic subject or object as topical,
distinguishing a restrictive subset reading ("Women who love too much are often disappointed" =
*some* women) from an NRRC's universal reading ("Women, who love too much, are often disappointed" =
*all* women, p.120).

#### The syntax of relativization: deletion, case-role recoverability, and relativization strategies (§9.3)

A REL-clause's co-referent noun is deleted under identity with the head noun, eliminating
redundancy but creating a **case-role recoverability conundrum** (43): the referential identity of
the missing noun is recoverable from the head noun, but its *grammatical case-role inside the
REL-clause* is not automatically recoverable the same way. English resolves this via (at least)
four coexisting strategies: (a) the **relative-pronoun strategy** (case-marked pronouns historically
derived from interrogatives: `who`/`whom`/`where`/`why`/`when`/`how`, §9.3.3); (b) the **relative-
subordinator strategy** (the invariant, case-neutral `that`, most natural for direct objects and
some subjects, §9.3.4); (c) the **gap strategy** (no overt marker at all, routine for direct-object
relativization in both spoken and written English, and attested — in non-standard dialects, with
supporting examples drawn from a detective novel's dialogue — for subject relativization too,
§9.3.5, p.128–129); and (d) combinations of these with a stranded/fronted preposition for indirect-
object and oblique-role relativization (§9.3.6). For indirect objects, Givón identifies a growing
spoken-register preference for the gap-strategy-plus-stranded-preposition variant ("the woman I
wrote the letter for"), reasoning that it keeps the favored gap pattern while still leaving the
case-marking preposition in place to aid recoverability (p.130–131).

**Possessive REL-clauses** use the dedicated relative pronoun `whose`, but in non-standard spoken
English an alternative **anaphoric-pronoun strategy** instead leaves an ordinary possessive pronoun
in situ with `that` as subordinator ("the woman that [her house] burned down") — a strategy Givón
calls "profoundly simplifying" because it entirely bypasses case-role computation by leaving a
case-marked trace rather than a true gap (§9.3.7.2, p.133). The same four-way strategy choice
extends to complex locative prepositions (`on top of`, `in front of`), where splitting the complex
preposition for fronting is judged marked/awkward and the gap-plus-unfronted-preposition or
anaphoric-pronoun variants are preferred (§9.3.7.3, p.135). Adverbial-role REL-clauses (time,
manner, reason, place) typically use the gap strategy with no recoverability problem, since the
semantically-specific head noun itself (`the reason`, `the way`) already signals the missing role
(§9.3.8). A documented ongoing change (§9.3.9.1) is the near-merger of `who`/`whom`: `whom` survives
mainly in careful/formal register, driven by the rarity of object-REL subject-position use plus the
independent spread of the gap strategy and stranded prepositions, both of which reduce `whom`'s text
frequency further. The subordinator `that` is obligatory (not just preferred) for subject-REL
clauses with non-human heads, while `who`/`that` both occur with human heads (§9.3.9.2).

#### Syntax of non-restrictive REL-clauses, and extraposition (§9.4, §9.7)

NRRCs differ morphosyntactically from RRCs in three ways: the subordinator `that` is categorically
barred from NRRCs; the human/non-human distinction `who(m)` vs. `which` is maintained; and the gap
strategy, productive in RRCs, is ungrammatical in NRRCs (§9.4.2, p.138–139). NRRCs also resist
adverbial-role relativization, since adverbial roles are prototypically non-referring and NRRCs
require referring heads (§9.4.3). A separate pattern — **extraposed REL-clauses**, placed at the end
of the main clause rather than adjacent to their head — is available only to restrictive REL-clauses
(never NRRCs), tends toward indefinite heads, and is closely tied to **presentative discourse
function**: extraposed RELs most often attach to a presentative main verb introducing a new
discourse participant (`come in`, `arrive`, `appear`), and Bolinger's examples show atypical
presentative verbs degrading acceptability even with the same indefinite head (§9.7.2–9.7.3,
pp.147–149). Extraposition is also sensitive to syntactic complexity (long RELs are more likely
extraposed, avoiding a split main clause, §9.7.4) and to resolving referential ambiguity when a
REL-clause is semantically compatible with more than one preceding NP (p.150–151).

#### Multiple embedding and complexity constraints (§9.8)

Ross's (1967) **Complex-NP Constraint** and related multiple-embedding facts are used as a
processing-complexity probe. Embedding two REL-clauses that each modify a head noun **adjacent** to
its own co-referent (either stacked on the same head, or nested one inside the other's head NP) is
fully processable (examples (112)–(116)); embedding where the co-reference relation must reach
across a **non-adjacent** REL-clause — crossing more than one clause boundary — is reliably
judged ungrammatical in standard English (117)/(119), though attested in non-standard dialects *only*
when an anaphoric pronoun (rather than a gap) marks the far co-referent, which alleviates the
processing load (§9.8.2, pp.155–159). The same explanation — rather than a purely linear/positional
account — is argued to explain **repeated center embedding**: one cycle of center embedding ("The
rat that the cat chased died") is processable, a second cycle is not, and converting the problematic
second-cycle sentence to a passive (which removes the intervening non-adjacent clause) restores
acceptability (§9.8.3, p.160–161).

#### Non-finite relative clauses (§9.9)

A distinct construction type — "someone to stay at her house," "someone to fix the roof" — shares
REL-clauses' semantic co-reference condition but is morphosyntactically a **non-implicative
manipulative-verb complement** (infinitival, zero-marked subject). Some tokens are ambiguous between
a V-complement and a REL-clause paraphrase, others accept only the REL-clause paraphrase (§9.9.3,
pp.162–163) — Givón calls these constructions **syntactic hybrids**. Co-reference assignment in
these hybrids follows a **default equi-subject constraint** (139): absent any semantic/pragmatic
factor biasing otherwise, a zero-marked subordinate-clause subject defaults to coreference with the
*main-clause subject* rather than its object, even though the surface form looks like an
object-controlled manipulation complement; multiple worked examples (gender, cultural plausibility,
reflexive-blocking, nominalization-vs-noun ambiguity) show that this default is overridden whenever
independent non-arbitrary semantic/pragmatic cues are available (§9.9.4, pp.164–167). The underlying
motivation is independently attested across clause-conjunction and participial-adverbial
constructions: it is simply the general discourse bias toward **subject continuity** (§9.9.5,
pp.167–168).

### Ch.10 Contrastive Focus Constructions

#### Contrast, assertion focus, and topicality (§10.1–10.2)

Contrastive focus is defined narrowly (not the broader "focus of assertion/new information"), and
the two notions relate by a one-way implication: contrastive focus always entails assertion focus,
but not vice versa; similarly contrastive elements are always topical, but topical elements aren't
always contrastive (1)–(2). Contrast depends on **informational predictability/counter-expectancy**,
which Givón dissociates sharply from mere definiteness: either a definite subject or an indefinite
object can equally be placed under contrastive focus depending on the discourse context's
established expectations, not on identifiability per se (§10.2.1, p.174). Ordinary discourse packs
roughly one chunk of new (asserted) information per clause against two-to-three chunks of old
(background/grounding) information — the old information exists specifically to anchor the new
information's coherence (§10.2.4, p.176–177).

#### Contrastive grammatical devices, ranked by strength (§10.3)

Three degrees of contrastive strength are identified. **Strongest** (strong contrastive stress):
**stress-focus** (in-situ heavy stress on any constituent, even a bound derivational morpheme, as
in "I didn't say INduction, I said DEduction") and **cleft** ("It's JOE who will milk the goat"),
both requiring obligatory heavy stress on the focused constituent and both restricted to applying to
free lexical words/nominal predicate slots rather than verbs/adverbs/adjectives (§10.3.1, §10.8).
The related **pseudo-cleft** ("What Joe will do is MILK the goat") is structurally a headless
REL-clause as subject plus a nominal predicate, but unlike true cleft it is contrastive only when
additionally stress-focused — the bare pseudo-cleft pattern is not inherently contrastive (§10.3.1,
p.177–178). **Intermediate**: **Y-movement** (fronting a topical/contrasted constituent to
clause-initial position, with only moderate stress) is restricted mostly to nominal/adverbial
elements, and — unlike cleft/stress-focus — does not require the speaker to assume a strong
*contrary belief* on the hearer's part, only speaker-generated contrastive expectations set up
earlier in the same discourse (§10.3.2, pp.180–181). **Weakest**: restrictive modifiers (adjectives
that narrow the reference of a head noun) show a lower, optional level of contrast correlated with
stress — an unstressed restrictive adjective is ambiguous between restrictive and non-restrictive
readings, while a stressed one forces the non-restrictive reading (§10.6.3, p.193).

#### Discourse-pragmatics of contrast and anaphoric antecedence (§10.4)

Both cleft/stress-focus and Y-movement tend to apply to highly topical referents (anaphorically
accessible, thematically important) and to referring-definite or generic — not referring-indefinite
— nominals (§10.4.2). A documented discourse-distributional study shows Y-moved referents'
anaphoric antecedents typically lie **2–3 clauses back** in the preceding discourse, not in the
immediately-preceding clause nor across a larger gap (table/examples (24), p.183) — and critically,
not across a **paragraph boundary**, since contrastive devices require a buildup of contrary
expectations within the current thematic unit (§10.4.5, p.186, illustrated with a counter-example
pulled from a detective novel where a cleft is used across a chapter break and judged stylistically
anomalous).

#### Focus attraction from negation and yes/no questions; other focus-attracting devices (§10.5–10.6)

Optional (non-obligatory) clausal constituents systematically **attract** both assertion focus and
contrastive focus: negating a clause with only obligatory constituents (subject/verb/object) yields
a wide-scope negation reading (the whole event didn't occur), but negating a clause containing an
optional adverbial narrows the negation's scope almost exclusively to that adverbial (37)–(39),
p.188. The identical narrowing happens with yes/no questions (§10.5.3). WH-questions show a parallel
restriction: contrastive stress on anything *other* than the queried WH-constituent is judged odd,
since the WH-word itself already occupies the informational "focus" slot (§10.6.1). A closed set of
**contrastive quantifiers** (`even`, `all`, `every`, `other`, `only`, `self`, `really`, `just`)
obligatorily attracts contrastive focus, patterning with the Y-movement (not cleft) level of
contrastive strength, and clustering with short anaphoric distance in text (§10.6.2).

#### Cognitive motivation: the code-quantity and pre-posed-order principles (§10.9)

Two general cognitive principles are proposed to unify the whole chapter. The **code-quantity
principle** (58): less predictable or more important information receives more prominent (longer/
stronger) coding — explaining why contrastive stress is universal across all the devices surveyed.
The **pre-posed-order principle** (60): more important information is placed earlier in the clause —
explaining why two of the three major contrastive devices (cleft, Y-movement) also involve fronting.
Both are grounded in a general attention/memory account: more salient signals attract more attention,
and attended-to information is stored/retrieved more efficiently (59, 61).

### Ch.11 Topicalizing Constructions

#### Topicality as a multi-clause, discourse-level property (§11.1–11.2)

Topicality is not reducible to a single clause's grammatical-role hierarchy (subject > object >
indirect object); it is fundamentally a **discourse-level, multi-clause property** — what makes a
referent topical is its recurrence/importance across a span of connected clauses, not merely its
surface grammatical role in one isolated clause (§11.2, p.202). Two independent dimensions of
topicality are distinguished again here: **anaphoric** (referential accessibility — where in stored
knowledge the hearer should search) and **cataphoric** (thematic importance — how much the hearer
should expect this referent to matter going forward), with different grammatical devices skewed
toward each (§11.2.1).

#### Indefinite-subject constructions and the role of the indefinite article (§11.3.2)

Newly-introduced referents destined for high topic persistence are preferentially coded as
indefinite **subjects**, and spoken American English further distinguishes two indefinite articles
with sharply different topic-persistence profiles: `this` (used referentially, not demonstratively)
strongly predicts high subsequent persistence, while plain `a(n)` predicts low persistence — a
corpus study (Wright and Givón 1987, table (4)–(5), pp.204–205) found subject+`this` referents
average 6.95 subsequent mentions per 10 clauses versus only 1.54 for subject+`a(n)`, and object-
position indefinites of either article persist far less than subject-position ones. A worked "Dear
Abby" letter example shows the pattern operating in real text: the one third-person indefinite
referent marked with `this` ("this guy") is the only one of three that persists through the whole
letter (§11.3.2.1, p.206).

#### Existential-presentative and fronted-locative clauses (§11.3.2.2–11.3.2.4)

The **existential-presentative clause (EPC)** — `there` + an existential/locational/entrance verb
(`be`, `exist`, `sit`, `stand`, `live`, `remain`, `come`, `arrive`, `appear`) — introduces new,
thematically-important referents, inverts normal S-V-O order to LOC-V-S, loses formal singular/
plural verb agreement in casual speech (`there's` for both), and in written text is overwhelmingly
(99%) accompanied by an attached predication (a REL-clause, adjective, locative phrase, N-complement,
possessive phrase, or associative phrase) that supplies **cataphoric grounding** for the brand-new
referent (§11.3.2.2, pp.206–208). Written English additionally has a **fronted-locative clause**
variant with no `there`, instead fronting a locative phrase and inverting subject-after-verb
("Right there near the bar stood a giant biker," §11.3.2.3). A third, simpler **REF-indefinite-
subject clause** achieves the same presentative function via ordinary SV order plus a presentative
verb and an attached predication, without any inversion device at all (§11.3.2.4).

#### Left and right dislocation (§11.3.3–11.3.4)

**Left dislocation (L-dislocation)** re-introduces an already-known (definite or generic, never
referring-indefinite) referent after a considerable **gap of absence** in the discourse, confined
almost entirely to the spoken-informal register, and marked by three syntactic features: a
separate intonation contour, neutral case-marking on the dislocated NP, and an anaphoric pronoun
inside the clause proper (§11.3.3.1–.2). A documented study of referential-distance (RD) values
across five topic-coding devices in spoken text (table (16), p.211) shows L-dislocation's mean RD
is dramatically larger (15.3 clauses, median 19.5) than zero anaphora, anaphoric pronouns, R-
dislocation, or plain definite nouns — confirming its function as a topic-reactivation device
reaching back across a **thematic-paragraph boundary**, and it is also used as a turn-taking cue
when a new speaker re-grabs a topic in conversation (§11.3.3.1, p.211). **Right dislocation
(R-dislocation)** shares the same three syntactic features but places the dislocated NP at clause-
end; unlike L-dislocation it is a **chain-final**, not chain-initial, device, closing off the current
topic rather than reaching backward for one, with by far the shortest and most local anaphoric
antecedent of the two, and far lower text frequency (0.5% of all referent-codings vs. 6% for
L-dislocation in the same study, §11.3.4.2, p.215).

#### Dative-shifting (§11.3.5)

Promotion of an indirect (dative/benefactive) object to direct-object status ("She gave him the
book") is constrained primarily by **case-role confusion avoidance**: promoting a human benefactive
to direct object alongside a human patient is blocked when it would create ambiguity about which
argument is patient vs. benefactive (`*He hit her the guy`), but acceptable with a non-human patient
(`Go kill me a goat`), and further gated by the patient's topicality — promotion is more natural when
the patient/direct-object is indefinite/less topical (§11.3.5.2, pp.216–217). Instrumental,
associative, and locative objects largely cannot undergo the same promotion pattern in English,
unlike datives/benefactives (§11.3.5.2, p.218). A corpus count (Givón 1984b, table (31), p.219) shows
dative-benefactives appear as direct object 84% of the time in written text versus only 16% as
indirect object, while locatives appear as indirect object 100% of the time — direct-object coding
correlates strongly with higher discourse topicality.

#### Raising (§11.3.6)

**Raising** converts an argument of a subordinate clause into a surface argument (object or subject)
of the main clause, bringing it to an earlier clausal position; Givón treats it as a topicalizing,
not merely stylistic, device, with semantic/pragmatic consequences rather than a free paraphrase of
an "unraised" deep structure. **Raising to object** (with intention/perception/cognition verbs: see,
know, believe, say, want, consider) and **raising to subject** (with stative/passive predicates —
seem, appear, be likely, be expected/believed/reported — and a subclass of evaluative adjectives:
easy, hard, difficult, nice, wrong) are both illustrated extensively with tree-structure derivations
(§11.3.6.2, pp.219–227). Semantically, the raised variant frequently implies **direct experiential
contact** absent from the unraised form (Postal's "Julius Caesar struck me as honest" implies the
speaker actually met Caesar, unlike "It struck me that Julius Caesar was honest," p.228) — the same
contrast independently noted for `want` vs. `wish` and for perception-verb complement choice in
chapter 7. Raising is restricted to promoting only the **subject** of the embedded clause (active or
passive), never its object, paralleling the same subject-only restriction seen for dative-shifting-
then-passivization in chapter 8 (§11.3.6.3.2(b), pp.230–231). A corpus study of American newspaper
text (table (78), p.234) found raising-to-subject (92.5% of all tokens, overwhelmingly via stative
or passive predicates with an unspecified underlying agent) vastly outnumbers raising-to-object
(7.5%), and that raised NPs are reliably either globally topical (named in the headline) or locally
topical (established in immediately preceding clauses) — directly confirming raising's status as a
genuine topicalization device rather than a stylistic free variant (§11.3.6.3.2(c), pp.231–234).

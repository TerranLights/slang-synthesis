## Grammar points

### §6.1 — The role of punctuation and the scope of this chapter

Punctuation exists to promote ease of reading by clarifying relationships within and between
sentences. It allows some subjective judgment, but should be governed by consistent application of
a few basic principles — the ones this chapter sets out, based on "traditional American practice."
Specialized punctuation (mathematics, foreign languages, citations, bibliographies, indexes) is
covered in other chapters.

### §6.2 — Punctuation and italics

A punctuation mark should normally match the font (roman or italic) of the text it belongs to, not
necessarily the font of the word immediately next to it. E.g., in *"the word *and*, which is in
italics, is followed by a comma in roman type,"* the comma belongs to the surrounding roman sentence,
not to the italicized word before it. Exception: when a title in a different font ends a sentence
with its own exclamation point or question mark, that mark takes the title's font (e.g., the
exclamation point in *Help!*), while a comma immediately after the same title stays roman.

### §6.3 — Punctuation and boldface or color

Unlike italics, whether punctuation following boldface or colored text is itself set bold/colored
is treated case by case, based on whether the punctuation logically belongs with the boldface term
or with the surrounding sentence.

### §6.4 — Punctuation and font — aesthetic considerations

Describes a more traditional, aesthetics-driven alternative to §6.2's logic-based system: under it,
punctuation generally matches the font of the immediately preceding character unless that character
belongs to a different word, with an exception for question marks/exclamation points (which match
the font of the word they immediately follow regardless). The source recommends this system, if used
at all, only for print-destined publications, and prefers the more logical §6.2 system for electronic
publications where content-based tagging is practical.

### §6.5 — Parentheses and brackets in relation to surrounding text

Parentheses and brackets should match the font of the surrounding text, not of the material they
enclose — even though a slanted italic letter touching an upright parenthesis can cause minor
typefitting friction in print (addressed with a thin/hair space where needed). Exception: when a
whole parenthetical phrase sits alone on its own line, the enclosing marks usually match the
phrase's own font instead.

### §6.6 — Quotation marks in relation to surrounding text

Quotation marks likewise follow the surrounding text's font (roman or italic), independent of
whatever font the quoted or titled material itself is in — e.g., quotation marks around an
italicized title stay in the surrounding roman text's font unless the quotation marks themselves are
part of the italicized title. When a quoted phrase sits alone on a line, the marks usually match the
phrase's own font.

### §6.7 — Punctuation and space — one space or two?

In typeset matter, exactly one space (not two) follows a period, question mark, exclamation point,
or closing quotation mark/parenthesis ending a sentence, and likewise exactly one space follows a
colon. A design layout may specify a different, explicit amount of space for particular elements
(e.g., between a figure number and its caption).

### §6.8 — Punctuation with URLs and e-mail addresses

A sentence containing a URL or e-mail address is punctuated normally; angle brackets or other
"wrapper" characters, though standard in some applications, are generally unnecessary in ordinary
prose. In hypertext, the live link should point only to the URL itself, excluding any trailing
sentence punctuation.

### §6.9 — Periods and commas in relation to closing quotation marks

Under the source's own (US/Chicago) convention, periods and commas always precede a closing
quotation mark, single or double — a traditional style already in use before this manual's first
edition (1906). An apostrophe at a word's end should never be mistaken for a closing single
quotation mark. See the "British style"/Oxford-system contrast captured in the Vocabulary table
above (§6.9–6.10), which is the inverse rule for this specific case.

### §6.10 — Other punctuation in relation to closing quotation marks

Unlike periods and commas, colons and semicolons always follow a closing quotation mark. Question
marks and exclamation points follow a closing quotation mark unless they belong to the quoted matter
itself, in which case they go inside (e.g., *"Timber!"* vs. *Which of Shakespeare's characters said,
"All the world's a stage"?*).

### §6.11 — Single quotation marks next to double quotation marks

When a single quotation mark (closing a nested quotation) sits immediately next to a double
quotation mark, no space is added between them as a rule, though many typesetters insert a thin
space (print) or nonbreaking space (online) for readability. Where a period and a nested single
quotation mark coincide, the period still precedes the single mark (consistent with §6.9).

### §6.12 — Use of the period

A period marks the end of a declarative or imperative sentence, followed in running text by a single
space (see §6.7). A period may also follow a standalone word or phrase that functions as a complete
utterance on its own (e.g., *"Wait here."*, or the rhetorical *"My answer? Never."*).

### §6.13 — Periods in relation to parentheses and brackets

When an entire independent sentence is enclosed in parentheses or brackets, its period goes inside
the closing mark. When parenthetical/bracketed matter — even a grammatically complete sentence — sits
inside another sentence, the period belongs outside the parenthesis/bracket instead. The source
cautions against enclosing more than one complete sentence within another sentence this way.

### §6.14 — When to omit a period

No period follows: display lines (chapter titles, subheads, similar headings), running heads, table
column heads, caption-only phrases, or correspondence datelines/signatures/addresses. A run-in
subhead at a paragraph's start, by contrast, is followed by a period. An abbreviation that already
ends in a period and also ends a sentence does not receive a second, additional period.

### §6.15 — Periods in ellipses and suspension points

This section is a pure cross-reference to other chapters (ellipses for omitted quoted material;
suspension points for interrupted thought in foreign-language material) and introduces no new rule
of its own within this chunk's range.

### §6.16 — Use of the comma

Apart from its technical uses (mathematical, bibliographic, etc.), the comma marks the smallest
structural break in a sentence — often a slight pause in spoken contexts, though in formal prose
logical structure, not spoken rhythm, governs its placement. Effective comma use requires judgment,
balancing clarity against ease of reading.

### §6.17 — Commas in pairs

Whenever a comma sets off an inserted element (e.g., a year or a place-name inserted mid-sentence), a
second, closing comma is required if the sentence continues past that element — unless the set-off
element is itself a comma occurring inside an italicized title (e.g., *Look Homeward, Angel*), which
is exempt from needing a paired closing comma.

### §6.18 — Serial commas

See the Vocabulary table entry above. Additional nuance: the phrase "as well as" is not equivalent
to "and" for purposes of this rule — "as well as" does not trigger the serial-comma pattern the way a
true conjoined series does. When every element of a series is joined by repeated conjunctions (e.g.,
*"Mendelssohn or Schumann or Liszt"*), no commas are needed at all unless the elements are long enough
that delimiters would genuinely aid readability.

### §6.19 — Using semicolons instead of commas in a series

When a series' individual elements already contain internal punctuation, or are themselves long and
complex, semicolons (rather than commas) separate the elements — even if the series is introduced by
a colon. For a simple list, commas remain preferred regardless of whether a colon introduces it.

### §6.20 — Commas with "etc." and "et al."

See the Vocabulary table entries above for both abbreviations' punctuation rules.

### §6.21 — Omitting serial commas before ampersands

When an ampersand stands in for "and" — as is conventional in company names — the serial comma
before it is dropped (e.g., a firm styled *"Winken, Blinken & Nod"* takes no comma before the
ampersand even though the firm's own name already contains a serial-style comma between its first
two elements).

### §6.22 — Restrictive and nonrestrictive clauses — "which" versus "that"

See the Vocabulary table entries above for the core restrictive/nonrestrictive definitions. The
source adds that although British practice commonly substitutes "which" for "that" even in a
restrictive clause, many American writers deliberately preserve the distinction (restrictive "that,"
no commas; nonrestrictive "which," set off by commas) as a matter of clarity, not strict grammatical
necessity.

### §6.23 — Restrictive and nonrestrictive appositives

See the Vocabulary table's "appositive" entry above for the rule and worked contrast pair.

### §6.24 — Commas with parenthetical elements

See the Vocabulary table's "parenthetical element" entry above. The choice between commas (slight
break), em dashes, or parentheses (stronger break, or needed when the inserted element already
contains its own commas) is a matter of the degree of separation the writer intends, not a fixed
rule tied to any one punctuation mark.

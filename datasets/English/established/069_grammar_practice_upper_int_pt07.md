## Grammar points

### Modal auxiliaries: introduction

The book's own present/past-form table lists the full modal-auxiliary inventory: *can/could,
may/might, will/would, shall/should, must* (no past form), *ought to* (no past form), *need* (no
past form) (p.94). Form rules shared across all modals: positive = subject + modal + bare infinitive;
negative = subject + modal + *not/n't* + bare infinitive; question = modal + subject + bare
infinitive; short answer = subject + modal auxiliary alone. Modals are invariant — they cannot be
put into different tenses, used as infinitives, or used as gerunds/present participles. They can,
however, combine with four different following-infinitive forms, crossed with active/passive voice
in a 4×2 grid: bare infinitive ("must take"), *be* + present participle ("must be taking"), *have* +
past participle ("must have taken"), *have been* + present participle ("must have been taking") —
each with a passive counterpart (*be taken, be being taken* [implied], *have been taken, have been
being taken* [implied]; the book's own cells show *be taken, have been taken*, and *have been
taking*'s passive is omitted from the printed grid).

**Notes (p.95):** *need* functions as a true modal auxiliary only in question and negative forms;
*ought* is the only modal auxiliary that is followed by *to*; *dare* can function as a modal
auxiliary in question and negative forms, but can also function as an ordinary main verb followed by
a *to*-infinitive — the book flags it as uncommon enough that it's excluded from the practice
exercises entirely.

### Ability: can, could, be able

*Can* (present/future) and *could* (past) express general ability; *can* additionally covers a
specific future ability ("I can come and see you next week") (p.97). *Can*/*could* describe the
capacity to do something, not the actual doing of it — distinguishing this from *be able*, used to
describe ability + the achievement of the action ("Jan gave me a lift home so I was able to stay at
the party till late" — not just capable, but it actually happened). *Will be able* (not *can*) is
used for a skill that will be acquired in the future ("I'll be able to drive by next summer"). *Can*
+ *be* + adjective/noun expresses possibility rather than ability ("The sea can be quite warm in
September"). *Be able* additionally covers grammatical forms *can* cannot take (gerund, infinitive,
present perfect: "I like being able to work at home"). *Can, could*, and *could have* are used in
conditional sentences and sentences with an implied condition (worked example: "I could have got
here earlier but I didn't know you were waiting" = implied "if I had known you were waiting").

### Certainty, possibility, deduction: can, could, may, might, must, will, should, ought to

A single closed grid of modal + infinitive-form combinations, organized by the specific epistemic
meaning being expressed (pp.99–100):

- **Possible at any time:** *can* or *may* (+ simple or passive infinitive: "Suitcases can/may be
  left...").
- **Future possibility:** *may* or *might*; *could* is also possible but specifically marks a
  theoretical possibility ("You could go by train" = it would be possible to, if one wanted).
- **Present/future certainty:** *will* + simple or continuous infinitive.
- **Present possibility:** *may, might*, or *could* + simple infinitive (usually *to be*) or
  continuous infinitive.
- **Past possibility:** *may, might*, or *could* + perfect infinitive (simple or continuous).
- **Present deduction:** *must* (positive) or *can't* (negative) + simple infinitive (usually *to
  be*) or continuous infinitive — note the asymmetry: the negative deduction modal is *can't*, not
  *mustn't*.
- **Past deduction:** *must* or *can't*/*couldn't* + perfect infinitive (simple or continuous).
- **Present/future assumption:** *should* or *ought to* + present infinitive; **past assumption:**
  *should* or *ought to* + perfect infinitive, with *should* noted as more frequent than *ought to*
  in this use.

**Notes (p.100):** modals can stand alone in short responses ("'Are they at home now?' 'They might
be.'"); in short responses, *do* often substitutes for the main verb after a modal ("'Has he gone
home?' 'He may have done.'").

### Advice, obligation, necessity: should, ought to, must, have to, have got to, need

**Advice (p.103):** *should* (more frequent) or *ought to*, for present or future time, expressing
that a course of action would be the best idea; often combined with *I think.../Do you think...?/
Don't you think...?*. *I should...* (not *I ought to*) is used to give advice to someone else when
there's an implied *if I were you* condition.

**Strong advice/recommendation:** *must*, for present or future time ("You must come and stay with
us sometime" can even function as a casual invitation in context).

**Criticizing past actions:** *should*/*ought to* + perfect infinitive, meaning the subject did not
do the right thing ("I should have stayed at home" = I didn't, and that was wrong).

**Obligation: must, mustn't, have to.** *Have to* is explicitly flagged as not a modal verb — it's
an ordinary verb usable in any tense/form. *Must* and *have to* are often interchangeable but differ
in source: *have to* marks an externally-imposed obligation arising from the situation itself ("We
have to get up early tomorrow to catch the plane"); *must* marks an obligation imposed by the
speaker/writer themselves or by some authority they're reporting (self-imposed: "We must get up
early tomorrow. We've got a lot to do"; authority-imposed: "Passengers must fasten their seat
belts").

**Have got to (p.104)** is often interchangeable with *have to* but is restricted to single actions,
while *have to* covers both single and habitual actions (*"I have got to get the bus into work
today"* is fine, but *"I have got to get the bus into work every day"* is not, since habituality
requires plain *have to*).

**Negative obligation vs. absence of obligation:** *mustn't* expresses a negative obligation (not
allowed to); *don't have to* expresses the *absence* of obligation (not required to, but may if one
wants) — a commonly confused minimal pair the book makes explicit ("You mustn't wait here" vs. "You
don't have to wait here").

**Past obligation:** *had to* (not *must* + perfect infinitive, which the book flags is reserved for
past deduction, not past obligation — cross-referenced against §35).

**Necessity: need.** *Need* functions as a modal verb mainly in questions and negatives ("You
needn't come if you don't want to"); it also functions as an ordinary verb + *to*-infinitive, usable
in any form including the affirmative ("Everyone needs to eat fresh fruit and vegetables"). Lack of
past necessity is expressed either with *needn't* + perfect infinitive or with *didn't need
to/didn't have to* + infinitive, with a meaning difference: *needn't have* + past participle implies
the action was done despite not being necessary (regret/hindsight reading — "I needn't have gone to
the station so early. The train was nearly an hour late"), while *didn't need to/didn't have to* +
infinitive implies the action was correctly recognized as unnecessary and consequently not done ("We
didn't need to/didn't have to get up early...because we had no lectures"). A note flags that *need*
+ perfect infinitive is not used in the positive form.

### Offers, requests, permission, suggestions: can, could, may, might, would, must, shall, would you mind

**Asking permission:** *Can/Could/May/Might* + *I...?*, forming an informal-to-formal register scale
in that order (p.107). **Making a request:** *Can/Could/Would* + *you...?*, or *Would you mind* +
gerund (or *if I* + present/past verb: "Would you mind opening the window?" / "Would you mind if I
open/opened the window?"). **Giving or refusing permission:** *can/can't/may/may not/must/mustn't*,
also usable in third-person written rules ("Candidates must not start writing until the examiner
tells them to"). **Making an offer:** *Can/Shall I, we...?*, *Would you like...?*, or the more formal
*May I...?*. **Making a suggestion or invitation:** *Shall we...?*, *We could...*, *Would you like
to...?*.

### Habit: would and used to

*Used to* is explicitly flagged as not a modal (formed with *did/didn't* + bare infinitive: "I
didn't use to work there") (p.109). Both *would* and *used to* describe past habits
interchangeably in that specific use ("my brother and I used to/would fight all the time"), but with
two restrictions on *would*: it requires an explicit past time reference to be used this way (*used
to* does not — it can stand with or without one: "Before I got this job, I used to/would go out a
lot," but plain "I would go out a lot" without a time reference is ungrammatical for this habitual
meaning); and *would* cannot be used for past situations/states, only past repeated actions — *used
to* covers both ("We used to live in a flat in the town centre," NOT "We would live in a flat...";
"She used to have long hair").

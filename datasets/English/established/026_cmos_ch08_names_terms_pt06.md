**Source:** *The Chicago Manual of Style*, 16th edition (University of Chicago Press, 2010), Chapter
8, "Names and Terms," PDF pp. 1129–1154 (sections 8.125 through 8.150).

**Coverage note:** This chunk is entirely within the chapter's "Scientific Terminology" subsection
(itself nested under "Scientific Names of Plants and Animals," "Genetic Terms," "Geological Terms,"
"Astronomical Terms," "Medical Terms," and "Physical and Chemical Terms" sub-subsections). It covers:
higher taxonomic divisions and their English derivatives (§§8.125–8.126); vernacular plant/animal
names, domestic-animal breeds, and horticultural cultivars (§§8.127–8.129); genetic nomenclature —
genes and enzymes (§§8.130–8.132); geological terms — formal vs. generic usage and stratigraphy
(§§8.133–8.135); astronomical terms — celestial bodies, catalog names, "earth," "sun"/"moon," and
purely descriptive terms (§§8.136–8.141); medical terms — diseases/procedures, infections, and drugs
(§§8.142–8.145); and physical/chemical terms — laws and theories, chemical names/symbols, mass
number, and radiations (§§8.146–8.150). Every numbered rule and every distinct illustrative
example/term in this range is captured below. Several of these sections (8.123/8.124, immediately
preceding this range) are pure "additional resources" paragraphs pointing readers to external style
guides (*AMA Manual of Style*, *ICZN*, *ICBN*, US Geological Survey, *Scientific Style and Format*,
the International Astronomical Union, *The AIP Style Manual*, *The ACS Style Guide*, *USP Dictionary
of USAN and International Drug Names*) with no illustrative examples of their own — these
bibliography-pointer sections (8.127, 8.130, 8.133, 8.136, 8.142, 8.146) are noted but contribute no
vocabulary rows beyond their own cross-references, consistent with the coverage rule's exclusion of
content that introduces no new vocabulary or grammar. The source has a genuine clean text layer; no
vision-reading was required and no handwritten marginalia was present. Per dispatch instruction,
Attested Era is set to "contemporary (source published 2010)" and Attested Region to "American
English (United States)" throughout.

**Mojibake gotcha confirmed and corrected in this chunk:** the Greek letters α, β, and γ, where used
as scientific symbols, decoded from the PDF's text layer as Latin-1-misread-UTF-8 mojibake — `Î±`
(U+00CE U+00B1) for α, `Î²` (U+00CE U+00B2) for β, `Î³` (U+00CE U+00B3) for γ. This is the standard
2-byte UTF-8-encoded-then-Latin-1-decoded pattern (e.g. β = U+03B2 → UTF-8 bytes `CE B2` → misread as
Latin-1 `Î` + `²`). Confirmed against visible context in two independent locations: §8.131's example
`HLA-DRB1, for human leukocyte antigen D-related Î² chain 1` (β chain, consistent with standard HLA
nomenclature), §8.137's `Alpha Centauri or Î± Centauri` (α Centauri, the standard Bayer designation),
and §8.150's `Î²-ray ... or beta ray` / `Î³-ray ... or gamma ray` (each instance paired explicitly with
its own spelled-out form in the source text, which independently confirms the decode). All four
instances are corrected to their proper Greek letters below and flagged `low_confidence` is not
needed since the decode is self-verifying against the source's own parenthetical spelled-out forms.

## Grammar points

### §8.125 Higher taxonomic divisions

Taxonomic divisions above genus — phylum, class, order, family, and intermediate groupings
(suborder, subfamily, etc.) — are capitalized but never italicized, unlike genus and species names.
The rank-indicating common nouns themselves ("order," "family," and so on) stay lowercase.

### §8.126 English derivatives of taxonomic names

Once an English common word is derived from a taxonomic name (e.g. "hominid" from Hominidae,
"carnivore" from Carnivora), it is fully lowercased and treated as an ordinary English word, losing
both the capitalization and (where applicable) italicization of its taxonomic source.

### §8.127 Vernacular (common) names of plants and animals — capitalization

Chicago recommends capitalizing only the proper nouns and adjectives embedded within a common
plant/animal name, consulting a dictionary or the *ICBN*/*ICZN* nomenclature guides for correct
spelling and word division. A name with no embedded proper element (e.g. "mayapple") is fully
lowercase; one with an embedded proper name (e.g. "Cooper's hawk," "Rocky Mountain sheep") capitalizes
only that element.

### §8.128 Domestic-animal breeds and horticultural categories

The same dictionary-or-nomenclature-guide consultation applies to breed names and broad horticultural
categories. A specific registered breed name (e.g. "Thoroughbred") is capitalized, distinct from a
generic descriptive term for the same general category (e.g. "purebred," used of dogs generically)
which stays lowercase — the two are not interchangeable despite describing similar concepts.

### §8.129 Horticultural cultivars

Cultivar (cultivated-variety) names often have fanciful, trademark-eligible names that must be
respected as proper names and capitalized. Some horticultural publications (the source cites the
magazine *Horticulture* as a model) enclose the English cultivar name in single quotation marks, with
any following punctuation placed *after* the closing quotation mark — contrary to the normal American
convention of placing closing punctuation inside quotation marks. When the English cultivar name
follows the italicized Latin genus name directly, no intervening punctuation separates them.

### §8.130 Genetic nomenclature — scope note

The source explicitly limits itself to "only the most basic guidelines," directing specialists to the
*AMA Manual of Style*, *Scientific Style and Format*, the HUGO Gene Nomenclature Database, and the
Mouse Genome Database for authoritative guidance. (No independent examples in this section; see
§8.131 for the actual rules.)

### §8.131 Genes and gene symbols

Gene (and gene-symbol) names are italicized, including any arabic numerals that form part of the
symbol — italics distinguish genes from entities that share the same surface name. Gene symbols never
contain Greek characters or roman numerals. Human gene symbols are set in full capitals; mouse and rat
gene symbols typically take only an initial capital. Nomenclature conventions for other organisms
(yeast, fruit flies, nematodes, plants, fish) vary and are not detailed here. Proteins/gene products,
by contrast, are set in roman (not italic) type and are often named by adding a lowercase suffix (e.g.
"p" for "protein") to the gene symbol, or may share the identical surface form as the gene symbol while
being distinguished purely by the roman-vs-italic typographic convention. Hyphens in gene symbols are
rare — the source calls out HLA-DRB1 as one of "only a very few" such cases.

### §8.132 Enzymes

Restriction-enzyme names combine italic and roman characters: the first three (italicized) letters
name the source organism (usually a bacterium) from which the enzyme was isolated, followed by a
roman numeral giving the series number. An upper- or lowercase roman letter, an arabic numeral, or
both, may intervene between the organism-name portion and the series number to indicate the bacterial
strain.

### §8.133 Geological terms — scope note

The source again limits itself to "only the most general" guidance, directing specialists to the US
Geological Survey's *Suggestions to Authors of the Reports of the United States Geological Survey* and
*Scientific Style and Format*. (No independent examples; see §8.134–8.135 for the actual rules.)

### §8.134 Formal vs. generic geological terms

Formal geological terms (named eons, eras, periods, epochs, stages, glaciations) are capitalized in
both noun and adjective forms; the generic rank-indicating terms themselves ("eon," "era," "period,"
"epoch," "stage") are lowercased or simply omitted when they immediately follow a formal name. The
geological hierarchy runs eons → eras → periods → epochs → stages. The term "ice age" is recommended
lowercase in scientific contexts specifically because of ongoing uncertainty surrounding any single
formal definition of the term — contrasted with the capitalized historical-period usage "Little Ice
Age" (cross-referenced to §8.73). The relative-time modifiers "early," "middle," and "late" follow the
same formal/informal capitalization split as the terms they modify: capitalized when part of a formal
stage/period name ("Early Archean"), lowercased when used as an ordinary descriptive modifier ("early
Middle Cambrian," "in late Pleistocene times").

### §8.135 Stratigraphy

Formal stratigraphic unit names (supergroups, complexes, members, formations) are capitalized. The
section cross-references §8.73 for the separate matter of prehistoric cultural-period terms.

### §8.136 Astronomical terms — scope note

Scope-limiting paragraph directing physics/astronomy writers to *Scientific Style and Format* and the
International Astronomical Union's website. (No independent examples.)

### §8.137 Celestial bodies

Names of galaxies, constellations, stars, planets, and similar celestial bodies are capitalized.
Exceptions explicitly flagged with a "but": "comet" in a possessive comet name like "Halley's comet"
stays lowercase, and "the solar system" (a generic, non-proper designation) is lowercase. Cross-
referenced to §§8.139–8.140 for the special treatment of "earth," "sun," and "moon."

### §8.138 Catalog names for celestial objects

Celestial objects listed in well-known astronomical catalogs are designated by the catalog's name
(often abbreviated) plus a number — e.g. a full catalog name and its standard abbreviation may both be
acceptable for the same object.

### §8.139 "Earth"

In nontechnical contexts, "earth" (meaning our planet) is usually lowercased when preceded by "the" or
used in set idioms ("down to earth," "move heaven and earth"). When used as the planet's proper name —
especially alongside other capitalized planet names — "earth" is capitalized and "the" is typically
dropped.

### §8.140 "Sun" and "moon"

"Sun" and "moon" are usually lowercased in nontechnical contexts and always lowercased in the plural
("moons," of multiple planets' satellites). The source notes that some astronomy and related
scientific publications routinely capitalize these words as proper nouns, cross-referencing §8.137.

### §8.141 Descriptive terms

Terms that are merely descriptive of a celestial object or phenomenon, rather than functioning as a
proper name, are not capitalized — even when they incorporate the name of an already-capitalized body
(e.g. "the rings of Saturn," where "Saturn" stays capitalized but "rings" does not).

### §8.142 Medical terms — scope note

Scope-limiting paragraph directing medical writers/editors to the *AMA Manual of Style* and
*Scientific Style and Format*. (No independent examples.)

### §8.143 Diseases, procedures, and such

Names of diseases, syndromes, diagnostic procedures, and anatomical parts are lowercased, *except* for
any proper name (typically a discoverer's surname) embedded within the term, which stays capitalized —
the generic head noun ("disease," "syndrome," "test") remains lowercase even then. Acronyms and
initialisms formed from such terms are fully capitalized. The possessive form of an eponymous term
(e.g. "Alzheimer's," "Down's," "Hodgkin's"), though less common in medical literature, may be preferred
in general/non-specialist contexts. Cross-referenced to §8.150 for x-rays and radiation specifically.

### §8.144 Infections

Names of infectious organisms follow the same treatment as other taxonomic names (cross-referenced to
§§8.118–8.126: capitalized and italicized genus/species names). However, the name of a *condition*
derived from such an organism's name — once turned into an ordinary disease/condition term — is
neither italicized nor capitalized, even though the organism name it derives from is.

### §8.145 Drugs

Generic drug names should be used in preference to brand names wherever possible, and are lowercased;
brand names are capitalized and, when included, are typically given in parentheses immediately after
the first use of the generic name. Cross-referenced to §8.152 for trademarks generally.

### §8.146 Physical and chemical terms — scope note

Scope-limiting paragraph directing physics writers to *The AIP Style Manual* and the journal *Physical
Review Letters*, and chemistry writers to *The ACS Style Guide*. (No independent examples.)

### §8.147 Laws and theories

Though actual usage varies widely across publications, Chicago's house recommendation is that names of
scientific laws, theories, hypotheses, and the like be lowercased — except for any proper name attached
to them, which stays capitalized. This holds even for longer, multi-word formal theory names (e.g.
"general theory of relativity" stays lowercase even when preceded by the possessive "Einstein's").

### §8.148 Chemical names and symbols

Names of chemical elements and compounds are lowercased when spelled out in full. Their symbols,
however, are capitalized (per standard convention) and set without periods; the number of atoms of a
given element within a molecule is rendered as a subscript numeral following that element's symbol.
Cross-referenced to §10.66 for the full list of element symbols and atomic numbers.

### §8.149 Mass number

In formal chemical/physical literature, an isotope's mass number is set as a superscript to the left
of the element's symbol (e.g. superscript-14 before "C"). In work intended for a general audience, the
mass number may instead follow the symbol, after a hyphen, in full size (e.g. "C-14"), or the element
may be fully spelled out with the hyphenated number ("carbon-14").

### §8.150 Radiations

Terms for electromagnetic radiation types may be spelled with the Greek-letter symbol form (as noun or
adjective) or the fully spelled-out nonscientific form (as noun or adjective) — e.g. "β-ray" or "beta
ray," "γ-ray" or "gamma ray." "X-ray," unlike the Greek-letter-named radiations, is always spelled out
in Latin letters (never represented by a symbol alone) and functions as a noun, a verb ("to x-ray"), or
an adjective — though the verb form, while acceptable in general writing, is noted as uncommon in
scholarly medical literature, where writers more typically speak of "obtaining an x-ray film," "a
radiograph," or "subjecting [something] to x-ray analysis." "Cosmic ray" and "ultraviolet ray" follow
an open-compound-as-noun, hyphenated-compound-as-adjective pattern ("cosmic ray" vs. "cosmic-ray").

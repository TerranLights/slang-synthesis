## Grammar points

*(This source is argumentative register-attitude commentary rather than a structured grammar
reference. Per dispatch instruction, each distinct claim/argument is captured below as a
paraphrased subsection — the structural equivalent of a "grammar point" for this kind of source.)*

### Grammar knowledge as a social-exclusion marker

The author's central argument (p.2): correct grammar functions less as a communication tool and
more as an easy, broadly socially-acceptable way to signal superiority over others — "the easiest
way for an American to get off on and get away with looking down on others," comparable to
performative disapproval of a smoker. The very first thing a person learns about grammar (e.g.
"'cat' is a noun") is quickly followed by the second lesson: that knowing it elevates the knower
above those who don't.

### Dual motive behind grammar-snob attacks: offense and defense

Grammar-snob hostility isn't purely about feeling superior (an "offense" motive) — the author
argues there's also a defensive component (p.3): people who worked hard to learn a rule (her
example: the full syntactic justification for correctly using "whom," i.e. a pronoun that is
simultaneously a clause's subject and the object of a larger clause still takes subject form
because it functions as the subject of its own clause) feel a "sting" when they see others
disregard it casually, because it implicitly devalues the effort they invested and the people
(parents, teachers) who taught them to care.

### Fact-checking failure among grammar enforcers ("the gotcha problem")

The author argues (pp.4-6) that people most aggressive about correcting others' grammar often
don't verify the rules they're enforcing are real. Her worked example is the "no split infinitives"
myth: she recounts correcting a newspaper columnist/English tutor who condemned a split infinitive,
citing that *The Associated Press Stylebook*, *The Chicago Manual of Style*, Strunk & White's *The
Elements of Style*, and Garner's *Modern American Usage* all agree there is no such rule — and that
some grammar books go further, arguing the "infinitive" in English is just the bare verb ("go"),
with "to" merely introducing it, making the whole premise of a "split" infinitive incoherent. The
columnist's responses ("I could not disagree more," then "I still disagree") did not engage with
the cited evidence.

### Inherited grammar superstitions resist correction because they're emotionally inherited, not learned from evidence

Extending the split-infinitive anecdote (p.5), the author notes the columnist's rule came from her
late father, bundled emotionally with other parental life-lessons ("eat your vegetables," "look
both ways before crossing the street") — making the "rule" something she defends as part of
honoring her father's memory, not as a grammatical claim she arrived at independently. The broader
claim: many people who police others for split infinitives, sentence-final prepositions, or
sentence-initial conjunctions are reciting something "a misinformed parent or teacher told them
decades before" and have chosen to believe "with a vengeance" — i.e. emotional attachment, not
verification, explains the rule's persistence.

### Superstitious rules crowd out substantive grammar knowledge

The author argues (p.4) that energy spent policing mythical rules like the split-infinitive
prohibition displaces more basic, genuinely useful grammar knowledge — she notes more Americans
can assert a (non-existent) rule against split infinitives than can correctly identify that
"therefore" is an adverb.

### "Not my place to make others prioritize grammar" — the Grammar Annie example

Responding to a radio host's question about people who don't try to learn "proper grammar," the
author constructs an illustrative persona — "Ain't Got No Time for Grammar Annie" — a hypothetical
single parent working two jobs and caring for family, for whom grammar correctness is reasonably
not a priority (pp.6-7). The argument: it isn't the author's place to tell such a person she should
value grammar more, and someone in that position is not thereby inferior. This is presented as the
answer to "do people have an obligation to learn/use proper grammar" — no, it's acceptable (even
expected) for grammar to rank low on some people's list of real priorities.

### The author's own stance: "I care about grammar," but not because it's socially/morally important

Asked directly whether she personally cares about grammar, the author answers "Yes" — then spends
the back half of the essay unpacking what she means (pp.7-8). Explicitly disclaimed reasons:
grammar is *not* in serious danger, is not "all that important," doesn't separate social superiors
from inferiors, and is not civilization's "last lifeline" against social collapse. The actual
reason given: she simply finds grammar *cool* — an aesthetic/intellectual interest comparable to
coral-reef ecology, Napoleonic battle strategy, or Jane Austen-era social constraints, not a moral
or hierarchical claim.

### Grammar's interlocking-system structure as the source of its "coolness"

Illustrative argument (p.7): a sentence like "In the summer, I enjoy watching professional
hopscotch" is framed as a machine made of interlocking "gears" — a prepositional phrase functioning
adverbially, followed by a subject, a verb phrase, and an object — each part swappable/replaceable
while the sentence's grammatical architecture holds. This structural, puzzle-like property (not
moral correctness) is presented as the genuine source of grammar's appeal.

### Legitimate stylistic variation vs. a single "correct" answer

Several of the author's own worked examples are framed not as right/wrong but as defensible
choices among competing conventions (pp.7-8):
- **Compound-modifier hyphenation**: "longtime" (as a modifier) is one word, but "long-term" (as a
  modifier) is hyphenated; both become two words when used as nouns instead of modifiers.
- **Apostrophes pluralizing letter-grades**: the *Los Angeles Times*'s style of writing "A's and
  Bs" (apostrophe only where needed to prevent misreading "As" as the word "as") is presented as
  equally defensible to "A's and B's" or "As and Bs" — three competing conventions, none uniquely
  "correct."
- **Noun premodification**: nouns can modify other nouns directly, e.g. "breakfast eater."
- **Adjectives as subject complements**: adjectives can complement linking verbs, e.g. "I feel
  bad" (not "I feel badly").

### Grammar literacy as self-contained pleasure, not comparative status

The author frames learning grammar incrementally over a lifetime as a source of feeling "smart"
without needing to feel "smarter than" anyone else (p.8) — explicitly decoupling grammar competence
from the status-signaling/superiority dynamic described earlier in the essay. Corollary: focusing
on *other people's* language shortcomings ("a sucker's game," p.8) may feel good briefly but is
unsustainable, since no one's usage is error-proof.

### No one's grammar is "bulletproof" — why the gotcha game backfires

Final argument (p.9): the author notes that even recognized language-usage authorities — Bryan
Garner, William Safire, and William F. Buckley are named — get caught making grammar mistakes
themselves. If acknowledged experts can't achieve error-proof usage, demanding it of ordinary
speakers via the "gotcha" game leaves only two outcomes: perpetual anxious over-correction, or
giving up on caring about grammar altogether. The essay's closing thesis: avoiding grammar
snobbery and giving oneself (and others) permission to make mistakes reframes grammar as "a tool
or even a toy" — the condition under which, per the essay's title, grammar can actually be cool
rather than cruel.

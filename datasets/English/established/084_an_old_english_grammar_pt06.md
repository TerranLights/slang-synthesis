## Grammar points

### §160. Overview

Just as knowledge of OE syntax lets speakers combine known words freely without needing to know whether that exact combination has occurred before, knowledge of **word-formation habits** lets speakers form new words/word-combinations from existing elements by conventional patterns — "gas-turbine" is possible because of the existing pattern "gas-stove, steam-turbine"; "evacuate, macadamize, psychopathology" are possible through knowledge of affix-function. The processes active and flourishing in OE are of a complexity similar to Mod.E., and it is often impossible to distinguish formations that were live/productive during the OE period itself from ones that had already ceased to be formative before the Anglo-Saxons left the Continent but whose products were still very much in use — nor, for the purposes of learning OE, is distinguishing these historically important.

### §161. Formative Conversion

The nearest OE approach to Mod.E. functional conversion (*we coffeed, a good buy*) is seen in the regular correspondence between many nouns and verbs. Four sub-patterns are given, each with an extensive list of noun:verb pairs (see Vocabulary table above for the full extraction): **(a)** nouns with mutated vowel (from *i*-mutation, §§208ff) paired with unmutated verbs sharing the same root grade (*bite:bītan, gripe:grīpan, hrine:hrīnan, slite:slītan*, and the Class II/III-vocalic-sourced *cyme:cuman, cyre:cēosan, flyge:flēogan, gyte:gēotan, hryre:hrēosan, lyre:lēosan, scyte:scēotan*); **(b)** nouns related to the verb's past participle stem via causative *i*-mutation (*dōm:dēman, bōt:bētan, blōd:blēdan, frōfor:frēfran, gold:gyldan, weorc:wyrcan, camb:cemban, lār:lǣran, lāst:lǣstan, scrūd:scrȳdan*); **(c)** agent nouns related to verbs, mostly derived from the verb (*cuma:cuman, flȳma:flȳman, gefēra:-fēran, gesaca:-sacan, wita:witan*); **(d)** denominal verbs from existing nouns via *-ian* (*andswaru:andswarian, eard:eardian, ende:endian, lēan:lēanian, lufu:lufian, sorg:sorgian, þing:þingian, wuldor:wuldrian, wundor:wundrian*).

### §162. Adjective:Verb correspondences

Similar correspondences exist between adjectives and verbs: **(a)** unmutated-adjective:mutated-verb pairs (*beald:byldan, ēaðmōd:ēaðmēdan, full:fyllan, fūs:fȳsan, georn:gyrnan, hāl:hǣlan, scearp:scyrpan, wōd:wēdan*); **(b)** adjective:denominal-*-ian*-verb pairs (*beorht:beorhtian, fūl:fūlian, gōd:gōdian, lȳtel:lȳtlian, open:openian, sweotol:sweotolian, trum:trumian, yfel:yflian*).

### §163. Historical typology of the formation patterns

The conversion type *cyre:cēosan* is one of several in which noun and verb are related through **gradation** (§§182f), in this case also with *i*-mutation (§§208ff). The pairs *dōm:dēman, full:fyllan* go back to a pre-OE causative-suffix process using *\*-ja-*; the stem of a vocalic verb's past participle with *i*-mutation produces causative verbs — *risan* 'rise' + *i*-mutation → *rǣran* 'cause to rise, raise' (for the *s:r* correspondence, see §180); similarly *licgan* 'lie' beside causative *lecgan* 'lay', *sittan* 'sit' beside causative *settan* 'set'. The types *ende:endian, fūl:faelian* [sic, i.e. *fūlian*] similarly reflect an earlier suffixing process using *\*-ōja-*, but it is likely this correspondence continued to be productive within the OE period itself, since for many of the verbs in these sets (*lufian, andswarian, beorhtian*, for example) there are no cognates in other Germanic languages.

### §164. Noun-forming suffixes productive in OE

Two patterns are noted from §161's lists (a) and (c): one of the most prolific was the suffix **-ung** (also **-ing**), especially with consonantal (Class II) verbs, yielding feminine abstract nouns (*weorðung* 'honour', *prōwung* 'suffering'); agent-nouns were often formed from verbs via **-end** (*dēmend* 'one who judges', *hǣlend* 'one who heals, saviour') or **-ere** (*cwellere* 'killer', *leornere* 'learner'). Nouns were also formed from adjectives in several ways; particularly notable is the suffix **-nes(s)/(-nis, -nys)** (*beorhtnes* 'splendour', *hǣðennes* 'heathendom', *unrihtwīsnes* 'injustice').

### §165–166. Adjective- and adverb-forming suffixes

New adjectives were formed chiefly from existing nouns, with the commonest suffixes being **-ig** (*blōdig, cræftig, fāmig*), **-ful** (*gelēafful, sorgful, þancful*), the corresponding negative suffix **-lēas** (*ārlēas, feohlēas, frēondlēas*), and **-lic** (*dēofollic, lēohtlic, þrȳðlic*). Adverbs were formed chiefly from adjectives, with endings **-e** and **-līce** (not easy to distinguish because many adjectives had both forms with/without *-lic*, and it is often impossible to tell from which form the *-līce* adverb comes). Other adverbial terminations: **-es** and **-a** (extensions of the use of the genitive mentioned in §102, as in *ealles, hāmweardes, ungemetes, gēara, sōna*); **-um** (as in *furðum, hwīlum, unwearnum*); **-an** (usually signifying 'from the place/direction indicated in the stem', as in *ēastan, feorran, gȳstran, heonan, siððan*). There is also a relatively infrequent adverbial use of adjectives in n.a.sg.neut., notably *eal* 'entirely' and *ful* 'very'.

### §167. Modification

Just as Mod.E. can modify *turbine* with the existing word *gas* to form a new word, OE freely formed new words (especially nouns and adjectives) by **modifying** existing ones with words that might, where they existed as separate words, be various parts of speech. **(a) Nouns.** With noun prefixes: *bōccræft, dēaðdæg, folclagu, mannslyht, tūngerēfa* (in some cases the prefixed noun is inflected: *Englaland, hellewīte, Sunnandæg*). With adjective prefixes: *eallwealda, godspel, hēahburg, wīdsǣ*. With adverb prefixes: *eftsīð, inngang*. **(b) Adjectives.** With noun prefixes: *beadurōf, dōmgeorn, fyrdhwæt*. With adjective prefixes: *glēawhȳdig, efeneald, scīrmǣled*. With adverb prefixes: *felamōdig, ǣrwacol, welwillende*. In addition, there are many compound adjectives on the pattern known by the Sanskrit term **bahuvrīhi**, in which the second element is a noun; among the best-known are *brūnecg* 'bright-edged', *glædmōd* 'glad-hearted', *mildheort* 'gentle', *stercedferhð* 'stout-hearted', *yrremōd* 'angry'.

### §168–169. Recurrent affixes: overview

A considerably more widespread method of modification was the use of a large number of recurrent **prefixes**, many of which did not occur in the language as separate words (compare Mod.E. *un-*). Prefixed to verbs, the commonest single effect of these elements was to cause a shift in **aspect**, particularly from durative to perfective (see §129) — paralleling how Mod.E. phrasal verbs (*eat up* beside *eat*) undergo a similar shift in becoming perfective. Thus scores of common verbs are made perfective by the prefix **ā-** (*āhēawan* 'cut off', *āfȳsan* 'drive away', *āsendan* 'dispatch') and hundreds more by **ge-** (*fēran* 'go' but *gefēran* 'reach'; *frignan* 'ask' but *gefrignan* 'learn'; *hlēapan* 'leap' but *gehlēapan* 'mount'; *winnan* 'fight' but *gewinnan* 'win'); it will be seen that *ge-* often makes intransitive verbs transitive too. An example of a common noun-modifier is **and-**, which has the force of 'opposite' or 'corresponding to' (compare Go. *and* and Greek *anti*); for example *andefn* 'proportion', *andlēan* 'reward', *andsaca* 'adversary', *andswaru* 'answer'. With adjectives and adverbs, an extremely common prefix is **un-**, by which the antithesis of the stem-meaning is indicated: *unforht* 'unafraid', *unlȳtel* 'much', *unrihte* 'wrongly'.

**wiðer-** (modifies various parts of speech with the sense 'opposing, counter': *wiðerlēan* 'requital', *wiðersaca* 'adversary', *wiðertrod* 'retreat', *wiðerrǣhtes* 'opposite'); **ymb(e)-** (modifies various forms with the sense 'around': *ymbgang* 'circuit', *ymbsprǣc* 'comment', *ymbbeorgan* 'shield, protect', *ymbhycgan* 'consider', *ymbsittan* 'besiege', *ymbūtan* 'around').

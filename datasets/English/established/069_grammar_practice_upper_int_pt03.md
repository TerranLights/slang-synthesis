## Grammar points

### Present perfect simple

Form: *have ('ve) / has ('s)* + past participle (p.42). The present perfect shows a relationship
between past time and present time, and the book organizes its uses into five labeled categories
(A–E, pp.42–43):

**(A) An action at an indefinite time before the present**, covering four sub-cases: experiences a
person has had, where the connection to the present is that the experience is now part of who they
are ("He's taught English in five different countries"); an event with specific present-tense
evidence visible now ("Oh, you've had a haircut"); news reporting, where the present perfect makes
a past event feel immediate and currently relevant ("At least twenty people have been killed in a
motorway crash"); and after *to be* (present tense) + ordinal/superlative ("This is the first time
I've been to London").

**(B) With adverbials** that bridge past and present — *so far, up to now, lately, already, yet,
just*.

**(C) With a time reference** to a period still continuing in the present — *this morning, this
afternoon, today, this week, this month, this year, this century*.

**(D) In clauses of time and condition**, where the future event will already be in the past at a
certain future point ("I'll give you back your ring when I've found it").

**(E) With *since*, *for*, and *how long*** to describe a duration starting in the past and
continuing up to (and possibly beyond) the present ("She's been away from home for six months";
"He's lived there since he was a child").

### Present perfect continuous

Use (A): an activity that has been taking place in the recent period up to the present, which may
have just finished or may still be ongoing at the moment of speaking (p.43). Use (B): with *since,
for, how long*, and *all*-phrases (*all day, all night, all morning*) to emphasize duration of an
activity starting in the past and continuing to (and possibly past) the present.

### Contrast: present perfect simple versus present perfect continuous

With no time reference (p.43): the continuous describes the ongoing activity itself without
implying completion ("I've been reading that book" — not finished), while the simple describes a
completed event ("I've read that book" — finished); this pattern holds even where a quantified
object makes the completion reading obligatory for the simple form — the book notes explicitly that
"She's been running three miles" is ungrammatical, because the continuous cannot describe a
completed quantified distance. With a time reference (p.44): both forms are usable for an activity
that started in the past and continues into the present/future (*"They've been studying English for
three years"* / *"They've studied English for three years"* — both correct, but the continuous puts
more emphasis on the activity's continuity); both forms are also usable with *lately* and
*recently*, with the same continuous-emphasizes-activity vs. simple-emphasizes-completion contrast
("I've been working really hard lately" vs. "I've done a lot of work lately").

### Contrast: present perfect simple versus past simple

Four worked minimal pairs (p.46): indefinite vs. specific time ("I've been to Spain and Italy" vs.
"I went to Spain a couple of years ago"); a still-continuing period vs. a now-finished period ("Have
you been busy this morning?" — it is still morning — vs. "Were you busy this morning?" — the
morning is now over); just-completed relevance vs. a fully closed-off event ("Have you had a good
holiday?" implies you have just returned, vs. "Did you have a good holiday?" implies the holiday
ended some time ago); and present continuing relevance vs. a closed life-period ("She's starred in a
lot of major films" implies she's still alive/acting, vs. "She starred in..." implies her acting
career has ended or she has died; similarly "He's been in the army for fifteen years," still a
soldier, vs. "He was in the army for fifteen years," no longer serving).

### Past perfect simple

Form: *had ('d)* + past participle (p.49). Refers to a past time preceding another past time already
under discussion, and is used in combination with other past tenses to clarify event sequence in a
past narrative. The book catalogs seven distinct use-patterns (pp.49–50):

- **In *when* + past simple sentences**, to make explicit that the past-perfect event preceded the
  past-simple one ("When I got to the station, the train had left" = the train left before arrival;
  contrasted with plain past simple "...the train left," where the train left at or immediately after
  arrival).
- **In *when* clauses themselves**, to show the *when*-clause event finished before the main past-simple
  event started ("When I had read the letter, I started to cry" = finished reading, then cried;
  contrasted with "When I read the letter, I started to cry" = started crying while/upon reading).
  The same logic extends to *as soon as, the moment, immediately*. A side note explicitly flags that
  the past perfect is *not* generally required in a *when*-clause for sequential past-simple events,
  since the sequence is usually inferable — it's only needed when disambiguation is specifically
  needed.
- **In *after* clauses**, where either the past perfect or plain past simple is acceptable ("After we
  had passed our exams..." / "After we passed our exams..."), since *after* already encodes the
  sequencing; the past perfect here is used only for emphasis, not disambiguation.
- **As the past-tense equivalent of the present perfect**, when an entire narrative is shifted into
  the past ("Ann and Michael have just got married" → "...had just got married"; note that *since*-clauses
  keep a plain past tense even when the main clause goes past perfect: "Jeremy's been in a wheelchair
  since he was 10" → "Jeremy had been in a wheelchair since he was 10," *was* unchanged).
- **As the past-tense equivalent of the past simple**, used to narrate events looking back from a
  point in the past, and — unlike the present perfect — compatible with a specific time reference. A
  worked example rewrites an entire biography from present-perspective past simple into
  past-perspective past perfect. Three style notes follow: the verb in an accompanying *when* clause
  stays in the past simple even though the main clause is past perfect; for reasons of narrative style,
  the past perfect isn't sustained for long before the narration returns to the main past-simple
  timeline; and if events are narrated strictly in chronological order, the past perfect isn't needed
  at all.
- **In reported speech**, where a present-perfect statement/question is usually reported in the past
  perfect ("I've had a lovely time" → "She said she had had a lovely time"), while a past-simple
  statement/question can be reported in either the past perfect or left in the past simple.
- **After *if*, *wish*, *if only*** (third-conditional/past-unreal contexts): "If I had known you were
  at home, I would have called you"; "I expect you wish you had never met him"; "If only they had
  stayed at home."

### Past perfect continuous

Form: *had ('d) been* + present participle (p.52). Functions as the past-tense equivalent of the
present perfect continuous — the present perfect continuous looks back from the present, the past
perfect continuous looks back from a point in the past (paired examples: "I've been working all
day" → "I had been working all day"; "The dog...has been barking for two hours" → "...had been
barking for two hours"). The book notes this shift also governs reported speech: present perfect
continuous in direct speech becomes past perfect continuous when reported ("'It's been raining here
for days,' she said" → "She said it had been raining there for days").

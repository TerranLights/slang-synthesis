## Grammar points

### Genetic relationship vs. borrowing (ch.1)

Robinson opens by contrasting the English/German relationship with the English/French relationship
as a way of motivating the concept of genetic relatedness. English borrowed heavily from French
(mostly government/class/cuisine vocabulary tied to the 1066 Norman Conquest) but did *not* borrow
French's grammatical patterns — English speakers still pluralize, inflect, and order sentences by
native rules, which is why French-pattern phrases like "court-martial" get re-English-ified in casual
plural use ("court-martials" rather than the French-correct "courts-martial"). By contrast, the
English/German correspondences run through vocabulary *and* grammar *and* the oldest attested stages
of both languages, where the similarity is strongest — this depth and breadth of correspondence is
the diagnostic for a genetic (common-ancestor) relationship rather than contact-induced borrowing
(pp.1–5).

### The comparative method, cognates, and reconstruction (ch.1)

Comparative linguists identify *cognates* (words shared by inheritance, not borrowing) across related
languages and reconstruct a hypothetical *proto-language* ancestor form for each — marked with an
asterisk to flag it as unattested/reconstructed rather than textually attested. The reconstruction
procedure favors the proto-form requiring the fewest/most natural subsequent changes across all
daughter languages; "majority rules" is one heuristic (pp.6–9), but sound environment (e.g.
word-initial vs. non-initial position) can motivate preferring a minority form instead, as shown by
Robinson's worked OE/OHG/ON/Gothic *þrīe*/*þū* vs. *cūðe*/*ōðer* comparison (pp.7–9).

### Sound change is regular; Grimm's Law and Verner's Law (ch.1)

A foundational assumption of comparative reconstruction is that sound change is *regular*: the same
sound in the same phonetic environment changes the same way throughout a language, with apparent
exceptions pointing to an unnoticed sub-regularity rather than true randomness. **Grimm's Law**: PIE
*p, *t, *k became PGmc *f, *þ, *x (the general Germanic consonant shift separating Germanic from
Latin/Greek/Sanskrit cognates, e.g. Lat. *tres* vs. Goth. *þreis* 'three'). **Verner's Law** (Karl
Verner, 1875) resolved Grimm's Law's apparent exceptions: PIE *p (etc.) became Gmc *b (not *f) when
it was *not* word-initial and did *not* carry the immediately preceding PIE accent — e.g. Goth.
*sibun* 'seven' (not *fibun*) because the accent fell later in the PIE word. The Germanic-wide shift
of accent to the initial syllable afterward obscured the original conditioning environment, which is
why the pattern looked arbitrary until Verner's discovery (pp.10–12).

### The Germanic family tree and the Ingvaeonic/North Sea position of English (ch.1)

Robinson gives the "traditional English family tree" (his Fig.1, p.12): Proto-Indo-European branches
into Proto-Germanic (among other families); Proto-Germanic splits into West, North, and East Germanic;
West Germanic splits into Anglo-Frisian and Proto-German; Anglo-Frisian splits into Old English and
Old Frisian, which give rise to (Modern) English and Frisian respectively. He flags the West Germanic
branch as "especially embarrassing" — the comparative-method tree abstraction breaks down here because
some features cross branch boundaries entirely (e.g. certain similarities link Old High German and
Gothic specifically, and others link Anglo-Frisian specifically to North Germanic, cutting across the
clean West/North/East split) (pp.12–13). Historically/geographically, the Germanic tribes are
conventionally divided (following Tacitus, *Germania*, c. A.D. 98) into five groups by c.300 B.C.
(Map 1, p.17): **Ingvaeones** (North Sea Germanic) — ancestral to **English and Frisian**, England's
closest linguistic relative; **Istvaeones** (Weser-Rhine group) — ancestral to the Frankish
federation; **Irminones** (Elbe Germanic) — settled southern Germany/northern Italy (Alamanni,
Marcomanni, Langobardi); **North Germanic**, remaining in Scandinavia; and **East Germanic**
(Vandals, Goths, Burgundians), which migrated eastward/southward and left no surviving daughter
language (pp.16–18). A tree-diagram branching does **not** imply a real-world physical separation
between speaker populations — dialect continuum effects let innovations cross "branch" boundaries,
which the tree abstraction cannot represent (p.13).

### Indo-European homeland speculation (ch.1, lightly sampled)

Reconstructed PIE vocabulary (presence of words for domesticated/wild animals, snow/winter, absence
of a word for 'king' though one for 'clan chieftain' exists, a shared 'daughter-in-law' term but no
'son-in-law' term implying patrilocal marriage) is used to argue for a northerly, small-clan,
hunting/husbandry-based PIE culture. Rival homeland theories are sketched: Marija Gimbutas's "Kurgan
Culture" (north of the Black Sea, after the 5th millennium B.C., linked via burial-mound
archaeology) vs. Colin Renfrew's Anatolian-homeland theory (south of the Black Sea, before 6000
B.C.). Robinson treats both as "persuasive conjectures," not settled fact (pp.14–16). This is
background to the Indo-European family generally, not specific to Old English or Germanic, and is
recorded here only at summary level per this file's lighter-sampling policy for non-OE-specific
material.

### Dialect vs. language is sociological, not purely linguistic (ch.1)

Mutual intelligibility fails as a clean dialect/language test: Dutch (The Hague) and Swiss German
(Bern) speakers cannot understand each other despite both being "Germanic dialects," while the
intervening dialect continuum shows no sharp boundary — yet Norwegian/Swedish/Danish, which *are*
substantially mutually intelligible with graded difficulty, are conventionally called separate
*languages* rather than dialects of one language. Robinson's conclusion: the dialect/language
distinction tracks political unity and the existence of a supraregional standard literature more
than it tracks any purely linguistic criterion (pp.18–19).

### Germanic noun categories: number, case, gender, and stem classes (ch.2)

**Number**: Germanic nouns distinguish singular and plural (some nouns, like "wheat," resist
pluralization; others, like "pants," are plural-only — directly paralleled in Modern English). A third
number, the **dual** (for paired objects), existed in Proto-Indo-European but in Germanic survives
only in the *pronominal* and *verbal* systems, never in nouns (p.29). **Case**: Germanic marks
syntactic role via case suffixes rather than (solely) word order — nominative (subject, predicate
nominal), accusative (direct object, and the case required by many prepositions), genitive
(possession, and required by some prepositions/verbs), dative (indirect object, and required by other
prepositions/verbs), plus two rarer cases: the **vocative** (case of address — found only in Gothic
among the older Germanic languages, and even there always syncretic with nominative or accusative)
and the **instrumental** (marks the means/instrument of an action, e.g. "with a stone" — not found in
Gothic at all, more robust in other old Germanic languages, including Old English) (pp.29–33).
**Gender**: masculine/feminine/neuter, inherent (fixed) per noun rather than contextually assigned,
and only loosely tied to real-world sex — e.g. one OE word for 'woman' is grammatically neuter.
Gender governs agreement on adjectives/articles and partly determines which case/number endings a
given noun takes (pp.32–33). **Stem classes**: the older Germanic languages have "upwards of twenty"
noun inflection classes (traditionally named for a stem vowel/consonant, e.g. *a*-stems, *i*-stems,
*ō*-stems, *n*-stems/weak nouns), each with its own paradigm of case/number endings; a given ending
(e.g. dative singular) is not uniform across classes — Gothic *magau* 'to the boy' (*a*-stem) vs.
*waira* 'to the man' (also *a*-stem but different noun) shows even within-class variation can occur
depending on the specific lexical item (pp.33–34). OE's own noun-class system (strong/weak, several
genders and stem types) is the direct continuation of this inherited architecture.

### Germanic pronoun system (ch.2)

Personal pronouns (1st/2nd person) show a three-way number distinction — singular/dual/plural — unlike
nouns, which are only singular/plural; 3rd person pronouns distinguish gender (masc./fem./neut.) but
not dual, only singular/plural (pp.35–36). The **reflexive pronoun** ('himself/herself/itself')
inflects for case only, never gender or number (since it always echoes the subject it refers back
to), and systematically lacks a nominative form (a reflexive can never itself be the subject of its
own clause) (p.36). **Demonstrative pronouns** double as both the functional equivalent of a definite
article and as the morphological source of **relative pronouns** in several older Germanic languages
— in Gothic, a demonstrative is converted into a relative by adding the subordinating suffix *-ei*
(e.g. *saei*, *þatei*, *sôei*) (pp.36–37). This demonstrative-as-article-and-relative syncretism is
directly comparable to OE's own use of the *se/sēo/þæt* demonstrative-paradigm forms as both article
and (with added particle) relative.

### Strong vs. weak adjective declension (ch.2)

All older Germanic adjectives can inflect in two parallel declension systems: the **strong**
declension (used when no definite article/demonstrative accompanies the noun) and the **weak**
declension (used when a definite article/demonstrative *is* present) — e.g. Modern German still
shows this split, *der gute Mann* (weak, with article) vs. *ein guter Mann* (strong, no article).
Comparative and superlative adjective forms (e.g. ModE "sweet/sweeter/sweetest") also agree with
their noun in case/number/gender and may themselves appear in strong or weak form, with the weak
form often preferred or required for comparatives/superlatives specifically (pp.37–39). **This is the
direct ancestor of Old English's own well-known strong/weak adjective paradigm split**, one of the
more distinctive surviving features of OE grammar relative to Modern English.

### Germanic verb categories: voice, mood, tense, number, person, and nonfinite forms (ch.2)

**Voice**: active and passive exist in Germanic, but original inflectional passive morphology (rather
than a periphrastic "be" + past-participle construction, the norm in virtually all older Germanic
languages including OE) survives only in Gothic, and only in the present tense (p.39). **Mood**:
indicative (statements), subjunctive (wishes/counterfactuals), imperative (commands, present tense
only, for obvious semantic reasons) (p.39). **Tense**: the older Germanic languages inflectionally
distinguish only **two** tenses, present and preterite/past — exactly as Modern English still does
morphologically ("run" vs. "ran"); all other temporal/aspectual meaning (future, perfect, pluperfect,
progressive) is periphrastic via auxiliary verbs, and periphrastic constructions are markedly rarer in
the older languages than in Modern English — Gothic commonly uses its bare present tense with future
meaning and its bare preterite to cover pluperfect meaning, with no dedicated periphrastic forms for
either (pp.39–41). **Number/person**: verb agreement is governed by the subject; 1st/2nd person verbs
(like pronouns) show a **dual** inflection alongside singular/plural, used when the subject is
understood as exactly two people (p.41). **Nonfinite forms**: three exist — the **infinitive** (a
noun derived from the present stem, e.g. ModE "to run"), the **present participle** (an adjective from
the present stem, e.g. "running"), and the **preterite participle** (an adjective sometimes — not
always — based on the preterite stem, e.g. "driven") (p.41). **Verb subclasses**: the traditional
**strong verbs** (vowel-gradation/ablaut past-tense formation, e.g. ModE sing/sang/sung) comprise at
least 7 traditional subclasses, and the traditional **weak verbs** (dental-suffix past-tense
formation, e.g. ModE laugh/laughed) comprise at least 4 — the direct ancestor of OE's own 7 strong verb
classes and weak verb classes (pp.39, 41).

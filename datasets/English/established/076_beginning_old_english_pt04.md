## Grammar points

### Five grammatical categories packed into the OE verb form (p.73)

The source frames the OE verb as encoding, through its form alone: **person** (1st/2nd/3rd),
**number** (singular/plural), **tense** (present/past — OE has no dedicated future form),
**mood** (indicative = factual vs. subjunctive = non-factual), and **voice** (active vs. passive).
OE writers used a narrower range of these combinations than PDE does (mainly simple present/past),
so a reader often has to infer finer time-nuances (e.g. a pluperfect sense) from adverbs and context
rather than from dedicated verb morphology.

### Strong vs. weak verbs (p.74–76)

Strong verbs mark past tense by an internal vowel change (*drīfan* 'drive' → *ic drāf* 'I drove'),
paralleling PDE 'sing/sang'. Weak verbs mark past tense by a dental suffix, *-d-*/*-de* (*habban*
'have' → *ic hæfde* 'I had'), paralleling PDE 'walk/walked'. The source gives full present/past
singular+plural paradigms for one model verb of each type (*drīfan*, *habban*) and flags five
recurring surface cues useful for fast reading without memorizing full paradigms: 3sg present often
ends *-ð*; 2sg present often ends *-st*; present plural often ends *-að*; past plural often ends
*-on*; the infinitive typically ends *-an*.

### Two separate verbs merged into PDE "to be" (p.81–82)

OE had **two** distinct verbs translating as 'to be': *wesan* (used for present state, and the
source of nearly all PDE forms — am/is/was/were) and *bēon* (used only in the present tense, for
"timeless" general facts/truths, and the sole source of the PDE infinitive 'to be' itself). Full
paradigms for both are given (see table transcribed in Vocabulary notes/context). The distinction
is illustrated with "I am in the garden" (temporary state, *wesan*-type) vs. "I be the king's
huntsman" (standing fact, *bēon*-type) — a semantic split the source notes still has a faint echo in
nonstandard PDE habitual "be" ('I be fighting').

### Duration via present participle + wesan/bēon (p.82–83)

A present participle (OE *-ende*, parallel to PDE *-ing*) combined with a form of 'to be' expresses
an action in progress/continuing — e.g. *wǣrun feohtende* 'were fighting' — though the source notes
this construction was less frequent in OE than its PDE equivalent is today.

### Perfect constructions: habban/bēon + past participle (p.83–84)

PDE "have/had + past participle" has an OE ancestor construction using **either** *habban* or
*bēon* + past participle, chosen by a now-lost rule: *habban* when the verb naturally took an
Object (*Hē hæfð ðone fēond gefeohten* 'He has fought the enemy'), *bēon* when it did not (*Hē bið
gecumen* 'He has come', literally 'He is come' — note the PDE-French/German-parallel pattern still
visible in archaic PDE 'He is arisen'). The source flags that OE usage of this construction doesn't
map perfectly onto PDE expectations, so readers should stay alert to apparent mismatches.

### No dedicated future tense — present tense does double duty (p.79–80)

OE, like PDE, has only two true tenses (present, past); future time is expressed using the present
tense form. *Ic dō ðās burh...on ðīnum gewealde* ('I [hereby] put this city in your power') uses
present-tense *dō* for a future-time sense ('I shall put'). The modal verbs *sculan* ('ought
to/must', ancestor of 'shall') and *willan* ('wish', ancestor of 'will') only began developing their
PDE future-marking function toward the *end* of the OE period — so a reader should not assume every
OE *sceal*/*wile* marks grammatical future the way PDE 'shall'/'will' now does.

### Split plural Subjects ("X and Y") take a singular-looking construction (p.87–88)

A PDE compound Subject ("Beowulf and his comrades") pairs with a plural verb as one unit. OE
instead frequently splits this: the verb agrees with just the first-named Subject, and "and Y" is
appended afterward as an apparently separate phrase — *Bēowulf onhielde his hēafod...and his
gefēran swā same* ('Beowulf laid his head...and his comrades likewise'). The reading-practical
consequence: an *and*/*ond* phrase trailing after the verb may actually belong to the Subject, not
be a separate new clause.

### Question formation (p.88–89)

Yes/no questions: invert Subject and (first) verb, exactly as in PDE questions with 'to be' or a
modal (no 'do'-support needed in OE at all, since OE lacks the PDE dummy-auxiliary 'do' + Subject +
verb pattern used for ordinary verbs). Wh- questions: the question word precedes the (first/main)
verb, which precedes the Subject — *Hū canst ðū būtan nettum huntian?* 'How can you hunt without
nets?'.

### Negation: *ne* (and optional *nā*), double negation was standard (p.90–91)

The default negator *ne* precedes the verb. A second negator *nā* ('not') can co-occur with *ne* in
the same clause purely for emphasis (*Ne ielde Grendel nā lange* 'Grendel did not delay at all') —
double, even triple, negation was ordinary OE (and earlier English) grammar, not an error; the
source notes the PDE prohibition on "double negatives" is an 18th-century prescriptive import based
on mathematical-logic reasoning, not a native grammatical fact. Several frequent verbs/pronouns
fuse with *ne* into single contracted forms: *nis* (ne+is), *nylle* (ne+wille), *nyste* (ne+wiste),
*nān* (ne+ān).

### Commands: distinct singular vs. plural imperative forms (p.91)

Singular imperative is the bare stem (*gā* 'go!'); plural imperative adds *-ð*, matching the plural
present-tense ending pattern (*gāð* 'go! [to a group]'). Negative commands simply prefix *ne* (*ne
hrīn* / *ne hrīnað* 'don't touch!').

### Impersonal verbs, especially verbs of experience (p.91–92)

Like PDE "It is raining"/"It seems that," OE uses a broader range of impersonal constructions with
no true animate Subject — notably for mental/perceptual verbs, where the experiencer appears in the
**Dative** case rather than as a Nominative Subject: *mē gemǣtte* literally 'it dreamed to me' = 'I
dreamed', from *The Dream of the Rood*'s opening line.

### Passive voice, and the impersonal-*man* alternative (p.92–93)

OE forms a true passive the same way PDE does (*wǣron gefullode* 'were baptised'). A second
construction using impersonal *man* ('one') as Subject with an active verb (*ðe man nemneð Ii* 'that
one calls Iona') is routinely translated into idiomatic PDE as a passive ('which is called Iona')
even though it is grammatically active in OE.

### The subjunctive mood: a vanished but systematic category (p.93–94)

OE marks "non-factual" meaning (hypothesis, desire, possibility, indirect speech) with a distinct
verb form, the subjunctive — recognizable by an **-e** ending (singular) or **-en** ending (plural,
sometimes reduced to *-n* after a vowel-final stem, e.g. *dō* → subjunctive *dōn*). Indicative
*giefð*/*gēafað* 'gives/give' (fact) contrasts with subjunctive *giefe*/*giefen* 'might give' (not
fact). Worked examples: *mæg* (indicative 'can') vs. *mæge* (subjunctive 'might') in *Ne mæg ic hira
swā fela gefōn swā ic sellan mæge* ('I cannot catch as many as I might sell'); and *wereden*
(subjunctive 'might protect') following factual *tugon* ('drew') in the Beowulf's-comrades sentence
already seen in Chapters 3–4. The source notes PDE has almost lost this category, retaining only
fossilized subjunctive-flavored expressions ('If I *were* to help you', 'Lord help us!'), while
French and German still use it productively — a useful cross-linguistic anchor for readers who know
either.

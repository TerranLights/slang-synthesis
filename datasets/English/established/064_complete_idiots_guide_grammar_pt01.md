## Coverage note — heavy non-redundant-supplement scope, applied aggressively

This is this language's **second** pedagogical-grammar-workbook source (after *English Grammar
Workbook For Dummies*, file 063) and its descriptive-grammar content (Chs.3–14: the eight parts of
speech, verb tense/conjugation, pronoun case, subject-verb and pronoun-antecedent agreement, dangling/
misplaced modifiers, split infinitives, mixed metaphors, phrase/clause taxonomy, the four sentence
types, fragments/run-ons, coordination/subordination) covers almost exactly the same ground as — and
in far less depth and rigor than — this language's flagship source, *The Chicago Manual of Style*
ch.5 "Grammar and Usage" (files 001–011), which already exhaustively tabulates all of this with
professional-editor-grade precision. Per the spec's non-redundant-supplement pattern, **this file does
not re-extract the parts-of-speech catalog, verb-tense paradigms, pronoun-case rules, agreement rules,
modifier/split-infinitive/mixed-metaphor rules, or the phrase/clause/sentence-type taxonomy** — all of
that is already captured, in more rigorous and more complete form, in 001–011. (Spot-checked against
001–011 before this decision: "hopefully," "split infinitive," "dangling modifier," and "double
negative" are all already present there.)

**What is kept instead**, because it is genuinely not in CMOS/Oxford or in file 063:

1. **The book's own four-part model of English** (Grammar/Usage/Mechanics/Style) and its explicit
   **three-tier Usage model** (Standard-formal / Standard-informal / Nonstandard), stated with its own
   worked examples including a nonstandard/dialectal one — a named, source-attested register framework
   independent of CMOS's own glossary, directly comparable to this project's own Usage Tier taxonomy.
2. **Four "A Note on [Part of Speech] for Non-Native Speakers" sections** (nouns, prepositions,
   adjectives, verbs) — a genuinely different pedagogical angle from CMOS/Oxford (which are
   copyediting manuals for professional native-speaker writers, not ESL-acquisition guides). These are
   specifically relevant to this project's sibling Hawaiian-pidgin-synthesis work, since they describe
   the exact points (article use, preposition idiom, quantifier agreement, modal/auxiliary agreement)
   where a non-native learner's English diverges from native-speaker English — the same friction
   points a pidgin-formation process would smooth over or restructure.
3. **A list of ~30 idiomatic preposition+complement collocations** (fixed phrases like *agree on* vs.
   *agree to*, *angry at* vs. *angry with*) — genuine vocabulary data not tabulated this way in
   CMOS/Oxford.
4. **A historical note on negative concord** (double negatives were the grammatical norm, not an
   error, from the 1400s–1700s) — directly relevant to understanding "double negatives" as a
   nonstandard/dialectal (not merely erroneous) feature of present-day English, since several living
   dialects (e.g. AAVE, many rural/regional dialects) retain negative concord as a systematic rule
   rather than a mistake.

Everything else in Chs.1–14 (quizzes, drill exercises, "Perversity Rules" mnemonic-error list, answer
keys) is either pure pedagogical filler or restates content already captured and is skipped per the
coverage rule.

## Grammar points

### The four conventions of written/spoken English: Grammar, Usage, Mechanics, Style (Ch.1)

The book's own top-level organizing model, which the rest of its 26 chapters are structured around:
**Grammar** (the form and structure of words), **Usage** (the customary way language is used in speech
and writing — includes pronoun choice, verb conjugation, active/passive voice, agreement, double
negatives, redundancy, coordination/subordination), **Mechanics** (abbreviations, capitalization,
italics, numbers, punctuation, quotation marks), and **Style** (a writer's distinctive way of writing:
word choice, sentence length/structure, figures of speech, tone). This four-way split is the author's
own pedagogical framing, not a term found in CMOS/Oxford, but it maps cleanly onto how this project's
own extraction is organized (grammar reference vs. style/register-shaping source material) (p.7–11).

### Three levels of usage: Standard-formal, Standard-informal, Nonstandard (Ch.1)

An explicit three-tier register model, stated with its own worked examples:

| Level | Example given |
|---|---|
| Standard English: formal usage | "They have done nothing." |
| Standard English: informal usage | "They've done nothing." |
| Nonstandard usage | "Dey ain't done nothin'." |

The book frames the main grammatical difference between standard and nonstandard English as centered
on pronoun forms and certain verb forms (its example: standard *brought* vs. nonstandard *brung*), and
explicitly lists slang words as a nonstandard-usage phenomenon. A second table expands this to three
named registers — **Formal** (business letters, resumes, speeches, scholarship: standard verbs,
*-ly* adverbs, standard vocabulary/punctuation), **Informal** (most books/magazines/newspapers,
textbooks: same core forms plus contractions and "some slang"), and **Nonstandard** (conversation,
movies, TV, comic strips, regional expressions: nonstandard verbs, adverbs without *-ly*, slang words,
nonstandard punctuation, "creative" spelling) — each tied to characteristic *uses* (document/speech
genres) as well as characteristic *forms*, which is a genuinely useful independent register model to
cross-reference against this project's own Usage Tier taxonomy (pp.7–9).

### "A Note on Nouns for Non-Native Speakers": definite-article rules (Ch.3)

A systematic rule set for when English uses *the*, framed explicitly for learners for whom article use
is not intuitive: use *the* with specific singular/plural nouns already identified in context; with
one-of-a-kind objects (*the sun*); with names of oceans/seas/rivers/deserts (*the Atlantic Ocean*); and
with college/university names containing "of" (*the University of New Mexico*). Conversely, *the* is
**not** used with personal names, general positions, continents, states, cities, streets, religious
place names, official titles, fields of study, disease names, or magazine/newspaper names (unless
"The" is literally part of the title, e.g. *The New Yorker*) (pp.35–36).

### "A Note on Prepositions for Non-Native Speakers": time prepositions and the idiom problem (Ch.3)

States the core difficulty explicitly: prepositional phrases are largely idiomatic (evolved through
use, not logical), which is exactly why they're hard for learners. Gives concrete rules: use *in*
before seasons, and before months/years not followed by a specific date (*in the summer*, *in 2003*);
use *on* before days of the week, holidays, and months **when** a specific date follows (*on
Wednesday*, *on July 20*); *like* as a preposition (meaning "similar to") takes a noun/pronoun object,
distinct from its colloquial use as a conjunction (covered elsewhere in the CMOS usage glossary,
file 010/011); and *of* can substitute for a possessive, but never with a proper noun (*Nina's dress*,
never *the dress of Nina*). This is followed by the ~30-entry fixed preposition-collocation list
captured in the Vocabulary table above (pp.44–45).

### "A Note on Adjectives for Non-Native Speakers": *a little/little*, *a few/few* (Ch.4)

A quantity-connotation distinction turning entirely on the presence/absence of the indefinite article:
*a little* / *a few* = "a small amount of something" (neutral-to-positive framing), while bare
*little* / *few* = "less than expected" (a shortfall framing) — e.g. *a few carrots* (some carrots,
stated neutrally) vs. *few carrots* (not as many carrots as one would want). Also notes *a*/*an* are
rarely used with proper nouns (p.49).

### "A Note on Verbs for Non-Native Speakers": auxiliary agreement and modal distinctions (Ch.5)

Flags that when *be*, *do*, or *have* function as helping verbs with a third-person-singular subject,
**the helping verb** takes the *-s* form and the main verb stays bare (*Does the store open at 10?*,
not *Does the store opens*) — a point of genuine confusion since the main verb "looks" like it should
also inflect. Also distinguishes *can* (present-tense ability) from *could* (past-tense ability, **or**
a present possibility/wish/politer-request sense distinct from simple past) (pp.68–69).

### Double negatives and historical negative concord (Ch.8)

States the standard prescriptive rule (use only one negative word per clause: *no, never, not, none,
nothing, hardly, scarcely, barely*) but adds a genuinely useful historical aside: **from the 1400s to
the 1700s, English speakers routinely crowded multiple negative words into a single sentence as the
grammatical norm, not an error** — i.e., negative concord was once standard English, and its survival
in various present-day nonstandard/dialectal forms ("He didn't say nothing") is a retention of an
older system rather than a random mistake. This matters for this project because it reframes
"double negatives" from a pure error category into a genuine historical-grammar feature that
persists as a marker of nonstandard/regional/dialectal register rather than simple illiteracy
(pp.108–109).

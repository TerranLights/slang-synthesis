## Grammar points

### §5.20 The "of"-genitive

The preposition *of* may precede a noun or proper name to express relationship, agency, or
possession, as an alternative to the `'s` genitive ending. The choice between them is mostly
stylistic (*the perils of Penelope* vs. *the saucer of the chef*); some nouns take either form
readily (*the theater's name*/*the name of the theater*), while others sound natural only with
*of* (*the end of everything*, not *everything's end*). The *of*-genitive also enables the double
genitive, as in *an idea of Hill's* or *an employee of my grandfather's business*.

### §5.21 Appositives defined; use

An appositive noun or noun phrase immediately follows another noun/phrase to define or further
identify it (*our first president* identifying *George Washington*). Commas frame a nonrestrictive
appositive but are omitted for a restrictive one — contrast *Robert Burns, the poet, wrote many
songs about women named Mary* (nonrestrictive, *poet* merely adds information) with *the poet
Robert Burns wrote many songs about women named Mary* (restrictive, *Robert Burns* is essential to
identify which *poet*). A restrictive appositive cannot be removed without obscuring the referent's
identity.

### §5.22 Nouns as adjectives

Nouns ordinarily function as other parts of speech, most commonly as adjectives, when one noun
modifies another (*the morning newspaper*, *a state legislature*, *a varsity sport* — these are
termed attributive nouns). This can create ambiguity: *fast results* can mean either "rapid
results" or (less probably) "the outcome of a fast." Sometimes a noun and its separately existing
adjectival form carry the same meaning interchangeably (*prostate cancer* and *prostatic cancer*
both refer to cancer of the prostate gland), but sometimes using the noun instead of the adjective
changes the meaning, as with *a study group* (not necessarily *a studious group*). When a noun
functions as an adjective, an implied preposition relating it to other sentence elements may need
to be inferred by the reader — e.g. *voter awareness* could mean awareness *of* voters or *by*
them.

### §5.23 Nouns as verbs

Nouns passing into use as verbs ("verbing") has long been common in English. Example: the noun
*husband* meant "one who tilled and cultivated the earth" as early as 1220, and became a verb
meaning to till, cultivate, and tend crops around 1420. New noun-to-verb transitions often occur
first in dialect or jargon — e.g. *mainstream* used as a verb ("more school districts are
mainstreaming pupils with special needs"). Garner cautions that such recently transformed words
should be used cautiously, if at all, in formal prose.

### §5.24 Adverbial functions

Nouns occasionally function as adverbs, typically when a preposition that would normally relate
them to the rest of the sentence is omitted: *we rode single file* (from *we rode in a single
file*), *Sam walked home* (from *Sam walked to his home*).

### §5.25 Other functional shifts

Beyond the pronoun case (the most obvious instance of a word class shifting into noun use — see
§5.26), several other word types may function as nouns: adjectives (*the poor are always with us*;
see §5.92), adverbs (*we cannot avoid the here and now*), participles/gerunds (*swimming in that
lake can be dangerous*; see §5.110), infinitives (*to discover the truth is our goal*; see §5.105),
monetary-amount phrases (*six million dollars went toward restoring the arena*), and whole clauses
(*what the people want is justice*). Such functional shifts are described as normal and acceptable
in English.

### §5.26 Pronouns defined

A pronoun is a word substituting for a noun or, sometimes, another pronoun, used in one of two
ways: (1) substituting for an expressed noun/pronoun, especially to avoid needless repetition
(*The father told the father's daughter that the father wanted the father's daughter to do some
chores* → *The father told his daughter that he wanted her to do some chores*); or (2) standing in
place of an understood noun whose referent is identified elsewhere in context (*Susan, are you
bringing your boots?* → *Are you bringing your boots?*), including an indefinite *it* understood
from context (*It is too hot* implicitly means the temperature).

### §5.27 Antecedents of pronouns

A pronoun typically refers to an antecedent — an earlier noun, pronoun, phrase, or clause in the
same sentence or, if unambiguous, a previous sentence — which must be explicit or implicit but
always clear. Three common sources of miscue/ambiguity: (1) a missing antecedent, as in *the
clown's act with his dog made it a pleasure to watch*, where *it* is meant to refer to "the
circus," which is never explicitly mentioned; (2) multiple possible antecedents, as in *Scott
visited Eric after his discharge from the army*, where it's unclear whether Eric or Scott was
discharged; and (3) multiple pronouns and antecedents compounding ambiguity in the same sentence,
as in *when the bottle is empty or the baby stops drinking, it must be sterilized with hot water
because if it drinks from a dirty bottle it could become ill* (the hot-water sterilization is
presumably meant for the bottle, not the baby).

### §5.28 Pronouns without antecedents

Some pronouns do not require antecedents. First-person *I* almost never has one, since it stands
for the speaker. Second-person *you* usually needs none either, though one is sometimes supplied in
direct address (*Katrina, do you need something?*). Expletives (some of which are pronouns) have no
antecedents (*it's time to go*; *this is a fine mess*). The relative pronoun *what* and the
interrogative pronouns *who*, *which*, and *what* never take an antecedent (*Who cares what I
think?*). In colloquial usage, *they* often appears without an antecedent (*they say that she's a
good golfer*), though the source notes "vigilant listeners and readers often demand to know who
'they' are" — an explicit register caution against antecedent-less *they* outside casual speech.

### §5.29 Adjective as antecedent

A pronoun normally requires a noun or another pronoun as its antecedent. Because possessives
function as adjectives, some writers have argued possessives should not serve as antecedents for
pronouns in the nominative or objective case. But compare *Mr. Blain's background qualified him
for the job* with the more roundabout *Mr. Blain had a background that qualified him for the job*
— the identity of "him" is equally clear in both, and the possessive-antecedent version is endorsed
by "respected authorities" as more economical.

### §5.30 Four properties of pronouns

A pronoun has four properties: number, person, gender, and case (see §5.11–15, cross-referenced in
source but not in this chunk's page range). A pronoun must agree with its antecedent in number,
person, and gender.

### §5.31 Pronoun number and antecedent

A pronoun's number is guided by its antecedent's number (*a book and its cover*; *the dogs and
their owner*). A collective noun takes a singular pronoun when its members are treated as a unit
(*the audience showed its appreciation*) but a plural pronoun when they act individually (*the
audience rushed back to their seats*). A singular noun modified by two or more adjectives denoting
different varieties, uses, or aspects of the object may take a plural pronoun (*British and
American writing differ in more ways than just their spelling*, where *writing* is understood
twice, once after *British* and once after *American*). Two or more singular nouns/pronouns joined
by *and* are taken jointly and referred to by a plural pronoun (*the boy and girl left their
bicycles outside*).

### §5.32 Exceptions regarding pronoun number

Five refinements to the §5.31 rules: (1) when two or more antecedents denote the same thing and are
connected by *and*, the referring pronoun is singular (*a lawyer and role model received her richly
deserved recognition today*); (2) when two or more singular antecedents are connected by *and* and
modified by *each*, *every*, or *no*, the referring pronoun is singular (*every college and every
university encourages its students to succeed*); (3) when two or more singular antecedents are
connected by *or*, *nor*, *either...or*, or *neither...nor*, they're treated separately and
referred to by a singular pronoun (*neither the orange nor the peach smells as sweet as it
should*); (4) when two or more antecedents of different numbers are connected by *or*/*nor*, the
pronoun's number agrees with the nearest (usually last) antecedent — and where possible, the
sentence should be cast so the plural antecedent comes last (*neither the singer nor the dancers
have asked for their paychecks*); and (5) when two or more antecedents of different numbers are
connected by *and*, a plural pronoun is used regardless of the nouns' order (*the horses and the
mule kicked over their water trough*).

### §5.33 Pronoun with multiple antecedents (differing person)

When a pronoun's antecedents differ in person and are joined by *and*, *or*, or *nor*, the pronoun
takes the person of only one antecedent: first person is preferred over second, second over third.
E.g. in *you or I should get to work on our experiment* (antecedents second and first person), the
following pronoun *our* takes first person, matching antecedent *I*. In *you and she can settle
your dispute* (antecedents second and third person), the following pronoun *your* takes second
person. If the pronoun refers to only one of the connected antecedents, it takes that one
antecedent's person (*you and she have discussed her trip report* — *her* refers only to *she*).

### §5.34 Antecedents of different genders

If antecedents are of different genders and joined by *and*, a plural pronoun is normally used to
refer to them (*the sister and brother are visiting their aunt*). But if the pronoun refers to only
one of the *and*-joined antecedents, its gender matches that one noun (*the uncle and niece rode in
his car*). A special problem arises when singular antecedent nouns of different or indeterminate
gender are joined by *or*/*nor*: using *he*/*his*/*him* as a common-sex pronoun "is now widely
considered sexist," and picking the gender of the nearest antecedent can be misleading (e.g. *some
boy or girl left her lunch box on the bus*). A good writer can usually recast the sentence to avoid
needing any personal pronoun at all (*some child left a lunch box on the bus*). See §5.41,
§5.221–30.

### §5.35 Pronoun case

A pronoun that is the subject of a finite verb is in the nominative case (*they went to town*). A
personal pronoun in the genitive case is governed by the gender of the possessor, not the thing
possessed (*President George W. Bush invited his advisers to Crawford*). A pronoun functioning as
the object of a verb or preposition is in the objective case (*they gave her a farewell party*;
*they gave it to him*). A pronoun placed after an intransitive verb or participle agrees in case
with the preceding noun or pronoun referring to the same thing (*it is I*; see §5.43, 5.44).

### §5.36 Nominative case misused for objective

The objective case governs personal pronouns used as direct objects of verbs (*call me tomorrow*),
indirect objects of verbs (*write me a letter*), or objects of prepositions (*makes sense to me*).
One of English's most persistent slips is misusing the nominative case of a personal pronoun in a
compound object:

> *Wrong:* The test would be simple for you and I. / *Right:* The test would be simple for you
> and me.
> *Wrong:* Read this and tell Laura or I what you think. / *Right:* Read this and tell Laura or me
> what you think.

The source notes this error arises so exclusively in compounds that the foolproof check is to read
the sentence with the personal pronoun alone — no one would mistake "The test would be simple for
I" or "Read this and tell I what you think" for correct grammar.

### §5.37 Six classes of pronouns

There are six classes of pronouns: personal (*I, you, he, she, it, we, they*); demonstrative
(*that, this*); interrogative (*what, which, who*); relative (*that, what, which, who*); indefinite
(e.g. *another, any, each, either, none*); and adjective (e.g. *any, each, that, this, what,
which*). Many pronouns other than personal pronouns may function as more than one type — *that*,
for instance, may be demonstrative, relative, or adjective depending on its use in a particular
sentence.

### §5.38 Form of personal pronoun

A personal pronoun's form shows whether it refers to the speaker, the person/thing spoken to, or
the person/thing spoken of. It also displays number, gender, and case through its form. As with
nouns, the first person is the speaker or speakers (*I need some tea*; *we heard the news*). The
first-person singular pronoun *I* is always capitalized no matter where it appears in a sentence;
all other pronouns are capitalized only at the start of a sentence, unless part of an honorific
title (*Her Majesty, the Queen of England*). The second person shows who is spoken to (*you should
write that essay tonight*); the third person shows who or what is spoken of (*she is at work*; *it
is in the glove compartment*).

### §5.39 Changes in form (personal-pronoun declension)

Personal pronouns change form ("decline") according to person, number, and case. Apart from the
second person, all personal pronouns show number by taking a singular or plural form. The
second-person pronoun *you* is both singular and plural but always takes a plural verb, even when
addressing a single person/thing. Full declension paradigm as given (§5.39):

| | Nominative | Objective | Genitive |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Singular, 1st person** | I | me | my, mine |
| **Singular, 2nd person** | you | you | your, yours |
| **Singular, 3rd person** | he, she, it | him, her, it | his, her/hers, its |
| **Plural, 1st person** | we | us | our, ours |
| **Plural, 2nd person** | you | you | your, yours |
| **Plural, 3rd person** | they | them | their, theirs |

### §5.40 Agreement of pronoun with noun

A personal pronoun agrees with the noun it stands for in both gender and number (*John writes, and
he will soon write well*; *Sheila was there, but she couldn't hear what was said*).

### §5.41 Pronoun and gender

Only the third-person singular pronouns directly express gender. In the nominative and objective
cases, the pronoun takes the antecedent noun's gender (*the president is not in her office today;
she's at a seminar*). In the genitive case, the pronoun always takes the gender of the possessor,
not of the thing possessed (*the woman loves her husband*; *Thomas is visiting his sister*; *the
kitten disobeyed its mother*). Some nouns may acquire gender through personification, referring to
a nonliving thing as if it were a person; pronouns enhance personification when a feminine or
masculine pronoun is used as if the antecedent represented a female or male person — as was
traditionally done, for example, when a ship or other vessel was referred to with *she*/*her*.

### §5.42 Personal pronoun case

Four special rules for personal pronouns: (1) if a pronoun is the subject of a clause, it must be
in the nominative case (*she owns a tan briefcase*; *Delia would like to travel, but she can't
afford to*); (2) if a pronoun is the object of a verb or preposition, it must be in the objective
case (*the rustic setting soothed him*; *that's a matter between him and her*); (3) if a
prepositional phrase contains more than one personal-pronoun object, all the objects must be in the
objective case (*Will you send an invitation to him and me?*); and (4) if a pronoun is the subject
of an infinitive, it must be in the objective case (*Does Tina want me to leave?*).

### §5.43 Pronoun case after linking verb

Strictly speaking, a pronoun serving as the complement of a *be*-verb or other linking verb should
be in the nominative case (*it was she who asked for a meeting*). In formal writing some fastidious
readers will consider the objective case incorrect in every instance, but in many sentences the
nominative pronoun sounds pedantic or eccentric to the modern ear (*Was that he on the phone?* —
offered as an example of nominative case that reads as stilted compared to the more natural
objective alternative that most speakers would actually use).

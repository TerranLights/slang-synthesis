## Grammar points

### Chapter 4 — Comparative Verb-Second typology (4.3.1)

Establishes the analytical toolkit later applied to Old English. Modern Dutch/German are **C-Verb-
Second (asymmetric)** languages: V-to-I-to-C movement happens in root clauses only, blocked by an
overt complementizer (den Besten 1983) — except in the CP-recursion complements of "bridge verbs"
(verbs of saying), which license embedded V2 as quoted main clauses (illustrated with Danish *vide*
"know"-type bridge verbs vs. *beklage* "regret"-type non-bridge verbs). Modern Icelandic/Yiddish are
**I-Verb-Second (symmetric)** languages: V-to-I happens freely in both root and embedded clauses,
independent of complementizer choice, and Spec,IP (not Spec,CP) is the landing site for topics —
there is no root/non-root asymmetry. Modern English retains only a residual, root-restricted V2:
constituent questions, yes-no questions, and negative-initial clauses (*Not a word did Banquo
speak*), and even this is blocked by an overt complementizer unless the matrix verb is a bridge verb
(*Macbeth said that not a word did Banquo speak* is grammatical; *Macbeth whispered that...* is not)
— directly paralleling the Dutch/German CP-recursion asymmetry (pp. 110–113).

### Old English Verb-Second: the core debate (4.3.2)

Three competing analyses of whether OE V-movement is symmetric or asymmetric, synthesized across the
chapter: **van Kemenade (1987)** treats topic-initial, wh-initial, negative-initial, and
*þa*-initial sentences as syntactically uniform V-to-C movement, explaining the discrepancy between
pronominal subjects (which precede the fronted verb) and nominal subjects (which follow it) via
**pronoun cliticization** — pronouns procliticize onto the verb after a topic but encliticize after
an operator (wh-/negative/*þa*), itself attributed to an interrogative/negative logical operator in
Spec,CP blocking procliticization. **Pintzuk (1991, 1993)** instead proposes **phrase-structure
competition**: IP comes in both I-medial and I-final variants (yielding, respectively, V-fronted and
verb-final surface order via the same V-to-I movement), with independent evidence from verb-particle
stranding in subordinate clauses (particle-after-verb patterns being far more frequent when the
verbal element fronted is the finite verb than when it's a non-fronted modal) establishing genuine
V-movement in non-root clauses — but her account predicts Verb-Second-with-topicalization in
non-root clauses too, contrary to fact, since embedded topicalization is in reality tightly
restricted. **The authors' own synthesis** (4.3.2.2–3) refines the clause structure into four
nested projections — CP > FP (hosts subject/object pronouns) > NegP > TP — based on sentential-
negation evidence (below), concluding that: wh-/negative/*þa*-initial contexts show genuine
asymmetric V-to-C movement (Spec,CP); topic-initial contexts show a distinct, lower V-to-F movement
(Spec,CP still hosts the topic, but the verb only reaches F, below C) found at reduced frequency in
non-root clauses as well; and unaccusative-construction-only embedded topicalization (where the
clause's surface subject is really a VP-internal theme — passives, impersonals, ergative verbs like
*cuman/gan/berstan* "come/go/burst") is a distinct, construction-specific exception rather than
evidence for general symmetric V2 (pp. 113–129).

### Multiple sentential negation as a clause-structure diagnostic (4.3.2.2)

A minority Old English negation pattern combines proclitic *ne* with a second negative element *na*
(or a variant); crucially, **the position of this second negator relative to the subject tracks the
pronoun/noun distinction independently of the finite verb's own position** — a pronominal subject
appears to the left of *na*, a nominal subject to its right, in both topic-initial and
operator-initial clauses alike (unlike finite-verb-position effects, which *do* differ between these
two contexts). This motivates positing a distinct functional projection NegP, with *ne* as its head
(obligatorily incorporating into the finite verb) and *na* in its specifier — giving independent,
non-circular support for the layered FP/NegP/TP clause structure proposed to resolve the V2 debate
(pp. 123–129).

### The decline of Old English/Middle English Verb-Second (4.4)

Tracked as (at least) two empirically and chronologically separate changes, against the standard
assumption that "loss of V2" is a single event: **loss of V-to-F** (nominal-subject inversion in
topic-initial clauses) shows a sharp decline already by the fifteenth century, per Jacobsson's (1951)
and Nevalainen's (1997) inversion-rate studies — though the overall curve is confounded by an
apparent stylistic revival in the sixteenth century before final disappearance in the seventeenth.
**Loss of V-to-I/T** (reflected in *do*-less negative/interrogative sentences, and dateable via
seventeenth-century examples like Dryden's *"who understand not Nature"*) persists considerably
later, into the eighteenth century — the authors argue these cannot be the same change, since
Kroch's constant-rate-hypothesis predictions would be violated if they were (pp. 133–137). A
tentative causal account links the V-to-F loss to the independent erosion of subject-verb plural
agreement morphology on the finite verb (FP as an agreement-encoding projection losing its trigger
once the relevant inflection disappeared) — flagged by the authors themselves as facing a timing
problem, since *do*-less negatives persist well after plural-agreement morphology was already largely
gone (pp. 135–137). A separate quantitative finding: Kroch & Taylor's (1997) *Rule of St. Benet*
comparison shows a genuine **North/South Middle English dialect split** in V2 character (categorical
C-Verb-Second in Northern texts vs. the mixed FP-involving pattern in Southern ones), which they
attribute to Scandinavian-contact-driven dialect mixing in the North and South (evidenced by the
loanwords *they*, *at*, *egg*, *sky*, *skin*, *skill* — entered via the vocabulary table above) —
the authors endorse this contact account over a purely grammar-internal one for this specific dialect
difference, while noting Chaucer's own prose shows categorical inversion despite no clear Scandinavian
contact, leaving the full picture unresolved (pp. 129–137).

### Chapter 5 — The OV/VO base-order debate: three competing accounts (5.3)

Reframes "was Old English OV or VO" as a question about where in phrase-structure theory movement
should be posited, surveying three incompatible frameworks across the chapter: **(1) the OV
approach** (object/PP/infinitive complements base-generated preverbal, with optional rightward
movement deriving postverbal surface orders) — strongest evidence from Pintzuk & Kroch's (1989)
*Beowulf* metrical analysis, where the verb's position in the alliterative half-line (and whether
postposed material is prosodically "heavy") supports uniform underlying OV, though this evidence is
poetry-specific and doesn't extend cleanly to prose, where many postposed complements are not
plausibly "heavy." **(2) The VO approach** (objects/PPs/infinitives base-generated postverbal, with
leftward movement — i.e., overt object-to-AgrOP movement in a Chomsky (1993)/Kayne (1994)-style
feature-checking framework — deriving OV surface order) is worked out in detail by the authors
themselves as their preferred account: overt object movement to AgrO, triggered by strong Case
features, derives OV; covert (LF-only) movement derives surface VO; the choice of checking position
(lower AgrOP adjoined to the lexical verb, vs. higher AgrOP associated with an auxiliary) accounts
for the full range of attested word orders including cases where the object appears between an
auxiliary and its non-finite complement. **(3) Pintzuk's (1991, 1996) competing-grammars approach**
(an extension of her Chapter 4 proposal) treats OV and VO as two base phrase structures in direct
synchronic competition within individual speakers/texts, following an S-curve of replacement — the
authors credit this for modeling the *time-course* of change well but criticize it for resting
mainly on minority-pattern evidence and for having little to say about what *triggered* VO's rise or
OV's loss (pp. 145–160).

### Middle English OV/VO: empirical chronology (5.4)

A detailed four-stage empirical picture, treated as one of the chapter's most load-bearing findings:
(i) **13th–14th c.**: Kroch & Taylor's (1994) and Foster & van der Wurff's (1995) studies show OV
order still fully productive across *all* syntactic environments (pronominal and full-NP objects,
with and without an auxiliary) — West Midlands texts favor OV more than South-Eastern texts (a
dialect contrast, not evidence of a single uniform rate of change), but OV is robust nationally. (ii)
A sharp drop in OV frequency in prose occurs specifically **around 1300–1450** (from ~20% to ~1% of
transitive clauses in prose by the mid-15th century per Foster & van der Wurff 1995), with verse
retaining OV far longer for metrical/rhyme reasons — a genre-based confound the authors flag
explicitly. (iii) **Around 1400** a genuinely *qualitative* (not just frequency) change occurs: OV
order stops being available in all contexts and narrows to just two productive syntactic
environments — clauses with an empty (null/topicalized) subject, and clauses with an auxiliary plus
a negative- or quantifier-marked object — which the authors analyze as surviving instances of overt
movement to TopP and NegP respectively, rather than to the general AgrOP that had by then stopped
triggering overt movement. (iv) These last two restricted OV contexts themselves survive until
**~1650**, the genuine terminus of productive OV order in English prose (pp. 160–179).

### The diachrony and causation of the OV-to-VO shift (5.6)

Having established that the change was far more gradual (its surface beginnings reaching back into
the 11th–12th centuries, per *Beowulf*-vs.-prose comparison) and more discontinuous (the real
grammatical break being specifically ~1400, not a smooth decline) than earlier literature assumed,
the authors survey several proposed causal mechanisms without fully endorsing one: reanalysis of
originally "heavy"/afterthought postverbal elements (relative clauses, coordinated phrases,
appositions) as evidence for general postverbal availability (Stockwell 1977); dialect diffusion of
VO order (van der Wurff); processing-difficulty avoidance with internally complex preverbal objects
(Colman 1988); and loss of overt NP case-marking removing the morphological support for OV
(Weerman 1989, Kiparsky 1997). They tentatively link the post-1400 *qualitative* restriction of OV
order to the independently-dated loss of V-to-AgrO movement (itself possibly tied to the loss of
V-to-F/V2 discussed in Chapter 4, given the derivational dependency the authors posit between V-to-
AgrO and V-to-F movement) — though they stress the causal chain remains speculative and timing
mismatches are acknowledged openly rather than smoothed over (pp. 172–179).

### Chapter 6 — Defining the OE/ME particle class (6.2)

Particles (Hiltunen's "phrasal adverbs": directional/locational adverbs forming a semantic unit with
a verb, e.g. *adun, aweg, forþ, up, ut*) must be distinguished from superficially similar **prefixes**
— word-division in OE manuscripts is an unreliable guide, and the authors establish four genuine
syntactic diagnostic tests that only particles (never prefixes) pass: (1) the negative *ne* can
intervene between a particle and its verb, but always precedes a prefix+verb combination as a whole;
(2) the infinitive marker *to* likewise precedes a particle but is itself preceded by a prefix; (3)
an auxiliary/modal verb can separate a particle from its lexical verb, never a prefix; (4) particles
(unlike prefixes) can precede a stranded preposition. These tests directly parallel the
particle/separable-prefix distinction in Modern Dutch and German (illustrated via Dutch *doorlopen*
"walk on" [separable] vs. *doorlopen* "finish" [inseparable], disambiguated by stress and the same
four syntactic tests) (pp. 181–185).

### Particle position as a V-movement diagnostic across clause types (6.3–6.4)

Hiltunen's (1983) corpus statistics establish a striking three-way clause-type asymmetry in Old
English: particle-before-verb order is overwhelming in subordinate clauses (84%), intermediate in
coordinate clauses (60%), and a minority in main clauses (41%) — mirroring, and offered as
independent corroborating evidence for, the same main/subordinate finite-verb-fronting asymmetry
established in Chapter 4. Applying **Koster's (1975)** Dutch-separable-verb diagnostic (the
generalization that whatever can follow a stranded particle in a Dutch main clause can also follow
the finite verb in a Dutch subordinate clause, because both mark the same underlying position)
to Old English yields a key new finding: **particles do sometimes appear after the verb even in
subordinate clauses** (Table 6.4: 16% of Hiltunen's subordinate-clause tokens), and crucially this
only happens with INFL-medial (not INFL-final) clause structures and correlates strongly with
whether the verb itself has fronted — independently confirming Pintzuk's Chapter 4 claim that Old
English has genuine V-movement in non-root clauses, via a completely different empirical source
(particle stranding rather than negation or pronoun position) (pp. 185–201).

### The Middle English particle-position reversal and the universal-VO-base problem (6.7)

Middle English shows a dramatic statistical reversal from Old English: Hiltunen's figures for early
ME texts put V-before-particle order at 96% in main clauses (up from 41% in OE), 86% in coordinate
clauses (up from 60%), and 86% in subordinate clauses (up from 16%) — essentially the mirror image of
the OE distribution, and the authors treat this as powerful corroborating evidence for the OV-to-VO
word-order shift traced independently in Chapter 5 via nominal objects, now replicated via an
entirely distinct diagnostic (particles). One complication: Middle English particle-verb order
differs subtly from the modern language in still permitting an adverb or PP to intervene between
verb and particle (not possible in Modern English). A separate, lexically-driven side development is
also noted: some OE part-V combinations were independently reanalyzed as ordinary prefixed verbs
during Middle English (rather than surviving as modern V-part phrasal verbs), a reanalysis route
distinct from, but overlapping in date with, the main OV-to-VO shift — *uprise*, attested as late as
a 1949 Joseph Conrad novel, is cited as a long-surviving relic of this now-obsolete reanalysis
pattern (pp. 201–210). The chapter closes by flagging that, as with Chapter 5, no detailed
minimalist/universal-VO-base analysis of the Middle English particle facts has yet been worked out
in the literature — the authors sketch, but do not fully commit to, a Zwart (1993)-style small-clause
secondary-predicate analysis (particle as the head of a small-clause predicate moving for feature-
checking), extending the same apparatus proposed for Old English particles earlier in the chapter.

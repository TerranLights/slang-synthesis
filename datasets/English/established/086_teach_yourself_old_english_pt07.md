## Grammar points

### The conjectural subjunctive extended to reported speech

Building on the "subjunctive as conjecture" principle introduced for the monastic-sign texts (Unit
15), the book shows the same mood-logic governs **reported/indirect speech**: where Modern English
reports indirectly by shifting tense (direct "I am well" → reported "he said that he was well"), Old
English instead shifts the verb's **mood** to subjunctive while often keeping its original tense. In
the Wynflæd dispute, the counsellors' actual spoken decision is reported throughout in the
subjunctive (*sealde* "gave," *wære* "would be," *scolde* "should," *agyfan/forgyldan* "give
up/compensate"), while the narrative facts surrounding the speech event stay indicative. The most
grammatically telling forms are the past subjunctives *nære* (< *ne* + *wære*, corresponding to
indicative *næs*) and *lete* (corresponding to indicative *let*), since these alone make the
mood-shift unambiguous. p.226–227.

### Shortcomings of the subjunctive: homophony between moods

Because many Old English verb forms share identical indicative and subjunctive spellings in certain
persons (e.g. *sealde* "gave" is identical in both moods, 3rd singular), the mood distinction is not
always recoverable from form alone — only verbs with genuinely distinct subjunctive endings (the
*-e*/-*en* present-subjunctive markers, or, for strong verbs, past-subjunctive vowel grade) make the
contrast visible. The present tense shows the clearest contrast (subjunctive ending in *-e*/*-en*
vs. indicative *-ð*/*-að*); the past tense shows it most clearly in strong verbs (*spræce*
subjunctive vs. *spræc* indicative) and least clearly in weak verbs, where the subjunctive and
indicative past can be formally identical. p.227.

### Preterite-present (modal) verbs: a present tense that looks like a past tense

A closed class of modal verbs — *cann* "know, can," *dearr* "dare," *mæg* "can, may," *mot* "must,
is allowed," *sceal* "must, shall," *þearf* "need" — are called "preterite-present" because their
present tense is historically formed like the past tense (preterite) of a strong verb (compare
present *ic cann* to a true strong-verb past like *ic sang*). Because this left no inherited past
tense of their own, these verbs had to innovate new, weak (consonantal) past-tense forms: *ic cann*
"I know" → past *ic cuðe* "I knew" (not a strong-verb-style vowel change). *Sceal* and *mæg* behave
as true preterite-presents; *willan* "to want" is an exception, conjugating closer to an ordinary
weak verb throughout. Full present/past paradigms are given for *sceal*, *mæg*, *willan*, plus a
supplementary list of forms for *cann*, *ann* (grant), *þearf* (need), *dearr* (dare), *mot* (am
allowed). p.227–229.

### Explicit cited multiple/doubled negation

The book explicitly flags, as a cross-linguistic parallel to Russian and "other languages," a
doubled-negation construction from the Herefordshire Dispute: *ða onscunodon hi hine and ne mihton
nane freoncrædene wið hine habban* ("then they shunned him and could not have any friendship with
him") — the negative particle *ne* appears twice, once attached to the finite verb *mihton* and
again fused into the determiner *nan* "no" (< *ne* + *an*). Unlike Modern Standard English's
single-negation rule, Old English negation could stack across multiple elements of a clause without
being read as a double-negative cancellation. p.157 (cited again in Unit 20's discussion, p.236).

### "Thegnly" register vocabulary: þegnlice, þegnriht, and social-mobility law

A specific social-register adverb, *þegnlice* "in a thegnly manner," connotes not just rank but
appropriate *behavior* for that rank — the book treats it as a worked case study in how a single
word can encode both social status and conduct norms simultaneously. Wulfstan's *Geþyncðo*
("Promotion Law") spells out the concrete, acquirable property/status thresholds (explicit
wergild-rank terms *twelfhynde*/*twihynde*, cross-referencing Unit 7) a freeman could meet to rise
to thegnly *þegnriht* "legal status" — archaeological correlates cited include a stone tower, a
private chapel/kitchen, and a gatehouse, distinguishing a thegn's fortified residence from both an
ordinary freeman's holding and the earlier communal-minster pattern. The word *castel*, borrowed
from French only in the later eleventh century, specifically denotes the Norman motte-and-bailey
type and should not be used to translate the earlier native *burh*. p.236–238.

### The Battle of Maldon: synonym clusters as evidence of poetic register richness

A short extract (lines 286–94) is used to demonstrate that formulaic heroic-verse composition
depends on maintaining several near-synonyms for culturally central concepts, available for
alliterative/metrical variation: three words for "battle" (*hild, here, wælstow*), three for "lord"
(*frea, beahgifa* "ring-giver," *þeoden* "prince"), and one specialized verb for the lord-retainer
loyalty relationship (*beotian* "to make a formal vow of service"). *Beahgifa* and the separately-
cited *goldwine* "gold-friend" are both explicitly noted as compounds confined to verse — ordinary
prose used the plain word *freond* "friend" instead — directly tying the vocabulary-register point
to the gift-exchange/ring-giving warband economy already seen via *beag* "ring" (pt01) and
*morgengifu*/*wedd* marriage-gift vocabulary (pt03). p.237–239.

### Onomastic language change visible within a single generation: þe, phonetic simplification, æt→de

Three named thegns in the Herefordshire Dispute are used as a compact case study in visible,
datable language change: (1) *Ægelsig þe Reada* shows one of the first attestations of the new
definite article *þe* (replacing *se*) that becomes Middle/Modern English "the"; (2) the same man's
full given name *Æþelsige* is independently simplified to *Ægelsig*, losing its "th" sound and final
unstressed syllable — paralleled by *Æðelræd* → *Ailred* in a later century, with a twelfth-century
author even respelling his own name "Ailred de Rivaulx" in a hybrid Anglo-French style; (3) the
place-marking preposition *æt* in bynames like *Leofwine æt Frome* is noted as later replaced by
French *de* under Norman influence — the book's broader point being that personal names, used
constantly in everyday speech, tend to show sound/spelling change earlier and more freely than
formal written registers still anchored to a taught, conservative monastic/cathedral-school
standard. p.239.

### Old Norse loanwords organized by semantic domain

The single richest concentrated loanword-stratum block in the book: a deliberately domain-organized
inventory of Old Norse borrowings current by the late Anglo-Saxon period, reflecting sustained
Anglo-Danish contact especially in the Danelaw and at Cnut's royal court — **maritime** (*liþ*
"fleet," *æsc* "warship," *barda* "beaked ship," *ha* "rowlock," *hamele* "oarlock," *hæfene*
"haven," *lænding* "landing-site," *butsecarl* "sailor," *steormann* "pilot," *scegðmann*
"Viking/shipman"); **legal** (*feolaga* "fellow," *husting* "formal treaty/tribunal," *grið*
"truce/sanctuary," *lagu* "law," *lahbryce* "breach of law," *lahmenn* "lawmen," *mal* "lawsuit,"
*niðing* "outlaw," *sac* "guilty," *sacleas* "innocent," *utlaga*/*utlagian* "outlaw [n./v.]");
**military** (*brynige* "mailshirt," *genge* "troop," *targa* "small round shield");
**politics/rank** (*eorl* "earl," cross-ref pt02's eorl/ealdorman discussion; *huscarl*
"housecarl/king's-guard member"); and **monetary** (*gersum* "treasure"). The book notes these loans
are already common by Edward the Confessor's reign and increase in frequency through the later
Chronicle manuscripts (especially E, continuing to 1154) — direct textual evidence of the
loanword-adoption rate accelerating over time. p.240.

### An early cluster of French loanwords, pre-dating the Conquest

A small, named group of French loanwords (*sott* "fool," cross-ref pt02; *prud* "proud," *tur*
"tower," *capun* "capon," *castel* "castle") is shown to already be in use in the court of
cosmopolitan Anglo-Danish aristocrats like Tofi Pruda, decades before 1066 — explicitly framed as a
small precursor trickle anticipating the much larger post-Conquest wave of French borrowing, rather
than that wave's true starting point. p.241.

### Practical manuscript-reading guidance: letterforms, word-division, and the Tironian nota

A worked transcription exercise on the Hexateuch's Joseph miniature teaches several concrete
paleographic conventions: the letter *d* characteristically curls back on itself; *f* and *r*
extend below the baseline; *g* has a distinctive rounded shape; *s* can resemble a modern "r"; the
rune-derived letter *wynn* (round-bodied) represents "w" (and happens to be a homograph, in
letterform only, of the unrelated word *wynn* "joy"); capitalization is sparse, often marking a new
section rather than every sentence or proper noun; smaller unstressed words are frequently written
joined to an adjacent stressed word (*in eode*, "went in," written as one unit); and the Tironian
*nota* (⁊) continues to stand for *and*, as already introduced in Unit 19. p.241–243.

### The Old English coronation oath's three-part promise structure

The coronation oath (Latin title *PROMISSIO REGIS*, "The king's promise") — recorded as sworn by
Æthelred the Unready at his 978 consecration by archbishop Dunstan, and likely reused by his son
Edward the Confessor in 1043 — follows a fixed three-part promise structure opened by the formula
*ic þreo þing behate*, "I promise three things": (1) that the Church and all Christian people shall
keep true peace; (2) that robbery and all unjust things are forbidden to every rank of society; (3)
that justice and mercy shall govern in all legal judgments. p.245.

## Grammar points

### Adjectives as nouns (§5.92)

Adjective-to-noun transitions are "relatively common" in English, and some are well established
enough to be unremarkable (*a postmortem*, *collectibles*, *the French*). But Garner cautions that
only the most firmly established transitions of this kind should be used, and that a writer should
prefer an existing, dedicated noun over an improvised one when a good alternative already exists
(e.g. prefer *collaboration* over the adjective-derived *collaborative* as a noun). See also §§5.22–25
(cross-referenced in source, not covered in this chunk).

### Adjectives as verbs (§5.93)

The reverse transition — adjective used as a verb — is rare in English, "only once in a while," and
occurs "usually as jargon or slang" (*inerted*, *low-key* used verbally). Garner states plainly that
such uses "are not acceptable in formal prose," marking this as a register restriction rather than an
outright grammatical error.

### Other parts of speech functioning as adjectives (§5.94)

A brief cross-reference note: words that ordinarily function as other parts of speech — nouns,
pronouns, and verbs — can sometimes function as adjectives instead, pointing forward to the source's
own later treatment of nouns (§5.22), pronouns (§5.47), and verbs (§5.105, itself covered later in
this same chunk).

### Verbs generally (§5.95)

A verb denotes either the performance/occurrence of an action ("action verbs": *walk*, *shout*,
*taste*, *fly*) or the existence of a condition/state of being, including emotion ("nonaction verbs":
*imagine*, *exist*, *dread*). The verb is called the most essential part of speech because it is the
only one that can express a complete thought by itself, with the subject merely understood (e.g.
"Run!", "Enjoy!", "Think!").

### Transitive and intransitive verbs (§5.96)

A verb is transitive if it requires an object to express a complete thought (the object receiving the
action the subject exerts on it, e.g. "the cyclist hit the curb") and intransitive if it does not
require an object (e.g. "the rescuer jumped," optionally followed by an adverbial prepositional phrase
like "...to the ground"). Many verbs can be either, with the distinguishing meaning determined by
usage: *succeed* means "to follow and take the place of" when transitive ("the king's heir will
succeed him") but "to accomplish a task" when intransitive ("the chemist will succeed in identifying
the toxin"). Other verbs, like *ride*, keep one unchanged meaning regardless of transitivity. A
normally transitive verb can sometimes be used intransitively on purpose, to emphasize the verb itself
and leave the object undefined or unknown (e.g. "the patient is eating poorly" — emphasizing *how
well* the patient eats over *what* the patient eats).

### Ergative verbs (§5.97)

Ergative verbs can be used either transitively or intransitively with the *same* noun serving as
object (transitive use) or subject (intransitive use) — e.g. with *door* and *open*: "I opened the
door" (transitive) or "the door opened" (intransitive). Many words undergo "ergative shifts" over
time: *ship* was once exclusively transitive in commercial usage but is now often used intransitively
("the books shipped on January 16"); conversely *grow* is generally intransitive but has long been
transitive in horticultural contexts ("the family grew several types of crops") and is increasingly
transitive in broader commercial usage ("how to grow your business"). Garner's usage advice: "careful
writers and editors employ such usages cautiously if at all, preferring well-established idioms" —
i.e. newly shifted ergative uses should be adopted conservatively.

### Regular and irregular verbs (§5.98)

Most English verbs form the past tense and past participle regularly by appending *-ed* to the base
form (doubling a final consonant where applicable, or adding only *-d* if the base already ends in
*-e*). These regular verbs are also called "weak" (a philological term for this conjugation class).
A smaller set of verbs, mostly with Old English roots, are irregular/"strong," and their principal
parts defy any single simple rule — they fall into (at least) four loose patterns: (1) change by
altering the base verb's vowel sound (*begin–began–begun*); (2) keep the same form throughout (as
*bet*); (3) share one irregular form between past tense and past participle (as *bind–bound–bound*);
or (4) change endings distinctly between past tense and past participle (as *bite–bit–bitten*). The
verb *be* is the most irregular of all, with eight distinct forms (*be, am, is, was, were, been,
being*, plus the implied infinitive form). Garner states explicitly that "no system of useful
classification is possible for irregular verbs" — a reliable memory and a general dictionary are
essential tools for using them correctly. Spelling/form complexity is further compounded because some
verbs' principal parts depend on the specific sense in use (*bid* meaning "to offer a price" keeps
*bid–bid–bid*, but meaning "to offer a greeting" becomes *bid–bade–bidden*), and sometimes on whether
the verb is used literally or figuratively (*wove a rug* vs. *weaved in traffic*). Finally, a handful
of verbs normally considered regular also have an alternative past tense/participle formed with *-t*
instead of *-ed* (*dream–dreamed/dreamt*). **Explicit regional/dialectal annotation:** American
English tends to prefer the *-ed* forms (*dreamed, learned, spelled*), while British English tends to
prefer the *-t* forms (*dreamt, learnt, spelt*).

### Linking verbs (§5.99)

A linking verb (also called a copula or connecting verb) links the subject to an equivalent word in
the sentence — a predicate pronoun, predicate noun, or predicate adjective — and does not itself take
an object. There are two kinds: *be*-verbs, and intransitive verbs used in a "weakened" sense, such as
*seem, smell, appear, feel*, and *look*. When used as a link, such a weakened intransitive verb often
carries a figurative sense akin to *become*, as in "he fell heir to a large fortune" (he did not
physically fall onto or into anything) or "the river ran dry" (a waterless river doesn't literally
run — it just dries up).

### Phrasal verbs (§5.100)

A phrasal verb is usually a verb plus a preposition or particle (e.g. *settle down*, *get up*) and is
never hyphenated, even though its corresponding noun or phrasal-adjective form sometimes is (contrast
the open verb phrase *to flare up* with the hyphenated noun *a flare-up*; or *to step up the pace*
with the hyphenated phrasal adjective *a stepped-up pace*). Three usage rules apply: (1) use the full
phrasal verb if it carries a sense distinct from its component words (*hold up* meaning "to rob" or
"to delay"; *get rid of* and *do away with* both meaning "to eliminate"); (2) avoid the phrasal verb
if the bare verb alone conveys essentially the same meaning (*rest up* is essentially equivalent to
plain *rest*); and (3) don't compress the phrase into a single-word verb, especially where doing so
would collide with an already-established one-word noun form (contrast the open verb *burn out* with
its noun form *burnout*).

### Principal and auxiliary verbs (§5.101)

A verb is classified as principal or auxiliary (also called modal) depending on how it is used. A
principal verb can stand alone to express a complete act or state ("he jogs"; "I dreamed about
Xanadu"). If combined with another verb, it expresses the combination's leading thought ("a tiger
*may roar*"). An auxiliary verb is used together with a principal verb to form a verb phrase
indicating mood, tense, or voice ("You *must study* for the exam!"; "I *will go* to the store"; "the
show *was interrupted*"). The most commonly used auxiliaries are *be, can, do, have, may, must, ought,
shall*, and *will*.

### Verb phrases (§5.102)

A verb phrase is the combination of an auxiliary verb with a principal verb (*could happen*, *must
go*, *will be leaving*). When a verb phrase is modified by an adverb, the modifier typically goes
directly after the first auxiliary verb (*could certainly happen*, *must always go*, *will soon be
leaving*) — the idea that verb phrases should never be "split" this way is, per Garner, "quite
mistaken" (cross-referenced to the source's later treatment of split constructions, not covered in
this chunk). A verb phrase is negated by placing *not* after the first auxiliary ("we have *not*
called him"). In an interrogative sentence, the first auxiliary begins the sentence and is followed by
the subject ("Must I repeat that?"; "Do you want more?"); an interrogative can also be negated by
placing *not* after the subject ("Do you *not* want more?"), though contractions are often more
natural and can paradoxically serve as positive intensifiers ("Don't you want more?"; "Isn't the
sunset beautiful?"). Most negative forms can be contracted (*we do not → we don't*; *I will not → I
won't*; *he has not → he hasn't*; *she does not → she doesn't*), but *I am not* contracts irregularly
to *I'm not* (never *I amn't*), with the corresponding interrogative form *Aren't I?*. The negative can
also be emphasized by instead contracting the auxiliary with the pronoun and leaving *not* standing
alone (*he is not → he's not*; *we are not → we're not*; *they have not → they've not*).

### Contractions (§5.103)

Most types of writing benefit from thoughtful use of contractions — used well, they make prose sound
natural, relaxed, and more enjoyable to read. *Be*-verbs and most auxiliary verbs contract readily with
*not* (*are/aren't*, *was/wasn't*, *cannot/can't*, *could not/couldn't*, *do not/don't*), though a few,
such as *ought not/oughtn't*, look or sound awkward and are best avoided. Pronouns can also contract
with auxiliaries, forms of *have*, and some *be*-verbs, though a writer should think before using the
less common of these, which often don't work well in prose except perhaps in dialogue or quotations
(*I'd've* = I would have; *she'd've* = she would have; *it'd* = it would; *should've* = should have;
*there're* = there are; *who're* = who are; *would've* = would have). Some contracted forms are
genuinely ambiguous and the intended meaning may not always be recoverable from context alone: *there's*
can mean either "there is" or "there has," and *I'd* can mean either "I had" or "I would."

### Infinitives defined (§5.104)

An infinitive verb (also called the verb's root or stem) is the verb in its principal uninflected form,
which may be preceded by *to* ("to dance," "to dive"). In the active voice, *to* is generally dropped
when the infinitive follows an auxiliary verb ("you must flee") and can also be dropped after several
specific principal verbs: *bid, dare, feel, hear, help, let, make, need*, and *see* ("You dare say that
to me!"). But when the infinitive instead follows one of these same governing verbs used in the
*passive* voice, *to* must be retained ("he cannot be heard *to* deny it"; "they cannot be made *to*
listen"). *To* should also be retained after *ought* (cross-referenced to §5.148, not covered in this
chunk).

### Uses of the infinitive (§5.105)

The infinitive is versatile — sometimes called a "verbal noun" because it can function as either a
verb or a noun, with more limited uses as an adjective or adverb. As a verb, it can take (1) a subject
("we wanted the lesson to end"), (2) an object ("to throw the javelin"), (3) a predicate complement
("to race home"), or (4) an adverbial modifier ("to think quickly"). As a noun, the infinitive can
serve as (1) the subject of a finite verb ("to fly is a lofty goal") or (2) the object of a transitive
verb or participle ("I want to hire a new assistant"). An infinitive may also be governed by a verb
("cease to do evil"), a noun ("we all have a talent to be improved"), an adjective ("she is eager to
learn"), or a pronoun ("give it to them to do").

### Split infinitive (§5.106)

Although grammarians between roughly 1850 and 1925 widely condemned the practice, it is now broadly
acknowledged that an adverb sometimes justifiably splits an infinitive's *to* from its principal verb
("they expect to more than double their income next year"). See also §5.168 (not covered in this
chunk) for the source's fuller discussion.

### Dangling infinitive (§5.107)

An infinitive phrase can be used, often loosely, to modify a verb — in which case the sentence requires
an express or implied logical subject for that infinitive. Without one, the sentence can become
confusing: in "to repair your car properly, it must be sent to a mechanic," the infinitive phrase "to
repair your car" has no logical subject and is left dangling (it is not *it*, the car, that does the
repairing). The fix is to rewrite the sentence so the infinitive phrase attaches to its true logical
subject: "to repair your car properly, *you* must take it to a mechanic."

### Forming participles (§5.108)

A participle is a nonfinite verb form — not limited by person, number, or mood, but it does carry
tense. Two participles are formed from the verb stem: the present participle invariably ends in
*-ing*, and the past participle usually ends in *-ed*. The present participle denotes the verb's action
as in progress or incomplete at the time expressed by the sentence's principal verb ("watching
intently for a mouse, the cat settled in to wait"), while the past participle denotes the verb's action
as completed ("planted in the spring"; "written last year").

### Participial phrases (§5.109)

A participial phrase consists of a participle plus any closely associated modifiers or complements. It
can be used either (1) as an adjective modifying a noun or pronoun ("nailed to the roof, the slate
stopped the leaks") or (2) as an adverb modifying the predicate, or any adverb or adjective within the
predicate ("she succeeded, persevering despite discouragement"). See also §§5.89, 5.112 (the latter
covered later in this same chunk) for more on participial adjectives.

### Gerunds (§5.110)

A gerund is a present participle used as a noun. Like the infinitive-as-noun, it is not limited by
person, number, or mood, but does have tense. As a noun, a gerund can function as (1) the subject of a
verb ("complaining about it won't help"), (2) the object of a verb ("I don't like your cooking"), (3) a
predicate nominative or complement ("his favorite hobby is sleeping"), or (4) the object of a
preposition ("reduce erosion by terracing the fields"). In some sentences a gerund can substitute for
an infinitive with equivalent meaning — compare the infinitive "to lie is wrong" with the gerund "lying
is wrong."

### Distinguishing between participles and gerunds (§5.111)

Because both participles and gerunds derive from verbs, the distinction between them rests entirely on
grammatical function, not form: a participle is used as a modifier ("the running water") or as part of
a verb phrase ("the meter is running"), while a gerund is used as a noun ("running is great exercise").

### Dangling participles (§5.112)

Both participles and gerunds are subject to dangling — a participle with no syntactical relationship to
the nearest subject is called a dangling participle, or a "dangler." Often the resulting sentence is
illogical, ambiguous, or even incoherent, e.g. "frequently used in early America, experts suggest that
shaming is an effective punishment" (the participle *used* doesn't modify the nearest noun, *experts*;
it actually modifies *shaming*); or "being a thoughtful mother, I believe Meg gives her children good
advice" (the sentence as written attests to the *writer's* own thoughtfulness rather than Meg's). The
usual fix is to recast the sentence so the misplaced modifier attaches to the correct noun ("experts
suggest that shaming, often used in early America, is an effective punishment"; "I believe that because
Meg is a thoughtful mother, she gives her children good advice"). Garner also advises a careful writer
to avoid using *it* or *there* as the subject of the independent clause following a participial phrase,
since this likewise produces a dangler lacking a true logical subject ("reviewing the suggestions, it is
clear that no consensus exists" is better revised as "our review of the suggestions shows that no
consensus exists"). See also §5.171 (not covered in this chunk).

### Dangling gerunds (§5.113)

When the participle in a dangling construction is instead the object of a preposition, it functions as
a noun (a gerund) rather than as a modifier, and the same dangling problem can occur: "after finishing
the research, the screenplay was easy to write" leaves unclear who did the research and who wrote the
screenplay. The fix, as with dangling participles, is to give the sentence its proper logical subject:
"after finishing the research, Pooks found the screenplay easy to write." Dangling gerunds can also
produce unintentionally absurd statements, e.g. "while driving to San Antonio, my map was lost" (the
map, obviously, was not the one driving) — corrected as "while driving to San Antonio, I lost my map."
A gerund phrase as the object of a preposition can, of course, also function perfectly well without
dangling, in a sentence whose sense genuinely allows it ("after dining with royalty, the next item on
my to-do list is picnicking on the Eiffel Tower").

### Five properties of verbs (§5.114)

A verb has five grammatical properties: voice, mood, tense, person, and number. This section is purely
a one-sentence signpost opening the source's next subsection (Properties of Verbs); the remaining four
properties beyond voice (mood, tense, person, number) are developed in later sections not covered by
this chunk's page range.

### Active and passive voice (§5.115)

Voice shows whether the subject of a clause acts (active voice) or is acted upon (passive voice) — only
transitive verbs are said to have voice. "The judge levied a $50 fine" is active because the subject
*judge* is acting; "the tree's branch was broken by the storm" is passive because the subject *branch*
does not act — it is acted upon by the object *storm*. The passive voice is always formed by joining an
inflected form of *be* (or, in colloquial usage, *get*) with the verb's past participle (contrast the
active "the ox pulls the cart" with the passive "the cart is pulled by the ox"). A passive-voice verb
used within a modifying phrase often has an implied *be*-verb: in "the advice given by the novelist,"
the implied words "that was" precede *given*, so the full passive construction is understood as "that
was given." Although the *be*-verb can be left implied this way, the past participle itself must always
be expressed. Sometimes the passive construction's agent isn't named at all ("his tires were slashed").
As a matter of style, passive voice ("the matter will be given careful consideration") is typically,
though not always, inferior to the corresponding active voice ("we will consider the matter
carefully"). The choice between active and passive can also depend on which point of view the writer
wants to foreground — "the mouse was caught by the cat" centers the mouse's experience, while "the cat
caught the mouse" centers the cat's.

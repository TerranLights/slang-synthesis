## Grammar points

### Rule XVIII — Infinitives (governed by *to*)

**"The Infinitive Mood is governed in general by the preposition TO, which commonly connects it to a finite
verb."** Brown devotes an unusually long etymological-philosophical digression (Obs. 1–6) to the single word
**to** itself — cataloguing wildly disagreeing contemporary classifications (adverb, preposition, "equivocal
article," a disguised form of the Greek definite article *to*, or even — per Horne Tooke's etymological theory
in *Diversions of Purley* — the same underlying Gothic-root word as the auxiliary **do**, both tracing to a
noun meaning "act, effect, consummation"). Brown's own settled position: *to* before an infinitive is
historically and functionally the same Saxon preposition used before nouns to govern the objective case, not a
distinct "sign of mood" or quasi-verbal particle, and should be parsed as a preposition governing the
infinitive.

### Rule XIX — Infinitives without *to* (the bare-infinitive verb class)

**"The active verbs *bid, dare, feel, hear, let, make, need, see*, and their participles, usually take the
Infinitive after them without the preposition TO"** — e.g. "I *dare* not *let* my mind be idle," "Thy Hector...
shall neither *hear* thee sigh, nor *see* thee weep." This closed list of nine bare-infinitive-taking verbs is
still essentially the same set recognized in Modern English (*make, let, see, hear, feel*, plus causative *bid*
and modal-like *dare, need*), making this one of the more directly portable rules in the whole Syntax part.

### Rule XX — Participles

**"Participles relate to nouns or pronouns, or else are governed by prepositions."** Four exceptions parallel
those already seen for adjectives (Rule IX) and infinitives: a participle can relate to a whole preceding clause;
an infinitive-paired participle can go fully abstract ("to seem compelled, is disagreeable"); a participle is
sometimes used irregularly as a substitute for the infinitive ("I saw him enter, or *entering*"); and participles
are frequently pressed fully into noun service. **13 Notes to Rule XX** are the single richest block in this
file, centering on the genuinely live, still-debated **gerund-vs-participle "of"-insertion problem** — when a
transitive participle is fully converted into a noun, it must take *of* before its object ("the *receiving of*
Christ," "*God's exercising of* it"), but when it remains a true participle it must not ("keeping *of* one day
in seven" is flagged as wrong — say simply "keeping one day in seven"); when the resulting *of*-construction
would be ambiguous or harsh, a different paraphrase should be chosen instead; converted participial nouns
should regularize their modifying adverbs into adjectives or compound-noun form rather than keep bare
adverbs ("the *right* understanding of a sentence," not "rightly understanding..."); and — directly continuing
a theme already seen in `pt07`'s Rule XVII — a sharp warning (Note XIII) against confusing perfect participles
with preterits, quoting Harris's *Hermes* condemning exactly the pattern still stigmatized today: *"it was
wrote,"* for *it was written*; *"he was drove,"* for *he was driven*; *"I have went,"* for *I have gone*.

### Rule XXI — Adverbs

**"Adverbs relate to verbs, participles, adjectives, or other adverbs."** Four exceptions include the simple
independent affirmative/negative adverbs *yes, ay, yea* / *no, nay* (treated as standing for a whole sentence,
genuinely questionable as "adverbs" at all — Brown himself asks "Is it clear that they ought to be called
adverbs? No."), and *amen*, analyzed as functioning as an independent one-word prayer ("So let it be") rather
than a true modifier. **10 Notes to Rule XXI** include several usage points with lasting relevance: adverbs
should not substitute for adjectives when *quality*, not *manner*, is meant ("fall *harmless*," not
*harmlessly*, since the falling object simply *is* harmless, not falling in a harmless manner — a subtle
distinction still taught today, e.g. "stand firm" vs. "stand firmly"); *how* should never replace the
conjunction *that* ("he said *how* he would go" flagged as "a vulgar error" — say *that*); and, most notably,
**Note VIII, the single-negation rule**: "A negation, in English, admits but one negative word; because two
negatives in the same clause, usually contradict each other, and make the meaning affirmative" — illustrated
with a flagged Shakespeare misquotation, "I love him not, nor hate him not." This is the canonical 19th-
century statement of the prescriptive no-double-negatives rule, directly relevant to this project's interest in
vernacular/slang mechanics: double (and multiple) negation is, of course, standard and fully grammatical in
many English dialects and in informal/slang registers both historically and today, making Brown's rule a
clean period marker of exactly the prescriptive/vernacular fault line this project is positioned to track.

### Rule XXII — Conjunctions

**"Conjunctions connect words, sentences, or parts of sentences."** **8 Notes to Rule XXII** include Note
VII, a genuinely valuable **14-item table of correspondent-conjunction pairs** still largely prescriptively
valid today: *though*…**yet**; *whether*…**or**; *either*…**or**; *neither*…**nor**; *both*…**and**;
*such*…**as** (pronominal-adjective + relative); *such*…**that** (consequence); *as*…**as** (equality of
degree); *as*…**so** (proportion/sameness, with two verbs); *so*…**as** (degree-limiting, with
adjective/adverb); *so*…**as** (after a negative, denying equality); *so*…**as** (with infinitive, consequence);
*so*…**that** (with finite verb, consequence); *not only*…**but**/**but also**/**but even**. Also notable:
Note III flags *I feared lest I should be deserted* as wrong, insisting on *I feared that I should be deserted*
— a point on which usage has since moved decisively against Brown (*lest* after *fear* is now the more
natural, even slightly elevated, construction in Modern English) — a clear case of a prescriptive judgment that
did not win out historically, worth noting as such rather than taking as still-valid.

### Rule XXIII — Prepositions

**"Prepositions show the relations of words, and of the things or thoughts expressed by them."** Two
exceptions cover *to* and *for* heading an abstract-infinitive subject-phrase ("To learn to die, is the great
business of life") where the preposition has no normal antecedent term to relate to. **5 Notes** are concise
idiom-correctness guidance: choose the preposition that actually matches the target idiom ("arrive *at*," not
"arrive *to*"); don't drop a preposition where usage requires it even elliptically (needs *of*: "all I see in you
is worthy *of* love"); don't insert a needless preposition that robs a transitive verb of its proper direct-object
construction ("congratulate *to* themselves" flagged as wrong — the verb simply takes a direct object);
position the preposition-plus-object for maximum clarity, generally fronting it when it carries the sentence's
real emphasis.

### Rule XXIV — Interjections

**"Interjections have no dependent construction; they are put absolute, either alone, or with other words."**
Brown states this rule has no true exceptions, and devotes most of his Observations to **refuting the
widespread contemporary claim that interjections "govern" a case** — several named grammarians (Nixon,
Putnam, Kirkham, Murray) are quoted asserting that *O, oh,* and *ah* require the objective case of a
first-person pronoun ("Oh *me*!") but the nominative of a second/third-person one ("O *thou* persecutor!"),
which Kirkham even analogizes directly to verb/preposition government. Brown rejects this as a fabricated
rule with no real parallel in Latin or any other language, arguing instead that an interjection-adjacent noun or
pronoun is simply in the nominative absolute (direct address, Rule VIII) or governed by an implied preposition
("*Alas* [for] poor Yorick!"), never by the interjection itself.

### The General Rule of Syntax and its 16 Critical Notes (Ch. XIII) — Brown's composition-principles capstone

Beyond the 24 part-of-speech rules, Brown adds one final, deliberately broad **General Rule of Syntax**,
explicitly scoped to catching errors none of the 24 specific rules reach: *"In the formation of sentences, the
consistency and adaptation of all the words should be carefully observed; and a regular, clear, and
correspondent construction should be preserved throughout."* Under it sit **16 Critical Notes**, which
function less as grammar rules than as general principles of clear composition and logical argument — a
genuine capstone chapter, distinct in kind from everything preceding it in the book:

1. **Of the parts of speech** — a word's part-of-speech classification must never be left ambiguous.
2. **Of doubtful reference** — syntactic reference/relation must never be left unclear.
3. **Of definitions** — a definition must include the whole class it defines and exclude everything outside it.
4. **Of comparisons** — a comparison requires genuine similarity between the compared things.
5. **Of falsities** — sentences with manifestly false content should be changed or rejected.
6. **Of absurdities** — absurdity is itself a grammatical fault, "because contrary to reason."
7. **Of self-contradiction** — a writer must not contradict themselves.
8. **Of senseless jumbling** — stringing words together without regard to sense is "unpardonable."
9. **Of needless words** — entirely needless words, especially ones that encumber meaning, should be cut.
10. **Of improper omissions** — conversely, words needed for sense or euphony should not be dropped.
11. **Of literary blunders** — gross errors made "in the name of learning" discredit the books containing them.
12. **Of perversions** — quoted proof-texts must be quoted accurately, never distorted to fit an argument.
13. **Of awkwardness** — inelegance of expression is a real defect even when no specific rule is technically
    broken.
14. **Of ignorance** — word-choice or spelling that betrays ignorance of a word's real meaning is especially
    disgraceful in a writer who claims learning.
15. **Of silliness** — "silly remarks and idle truisms" reflecting genuine weakness of thought should simply
    be cut.
16. **Of the incorrigible** — some passages are too erroneous to correct at all, and should be criticized and
    then set aside rather than patched.

Brown closes Part III with a tally of its own apparatus (Obs. 1): 24 rules, 32 exceptions (spread across 9
rules, covering every part of speech except nouns and interjections), **152 Notes**, and **497 Observations**
— a genuinely useful structural map of just how much of this Part's bulk is secondary apparatus around a
comparatively compact rule-core. He also clarifies (Obs. 1) that only 16 of the 24 rules are used for *both*
parsing and error-correction; the other 8 (Articles, Possessives, Adjectives, Participles, Adverbs,
Conjunctions, Prepositions, Interjections) are stated as parsing-only rules, since in Brown's system their
associated errors are caught instead by the Notes and the General Rule rather than by the headline rule
itself.

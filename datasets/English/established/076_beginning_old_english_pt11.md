**Source:** Carole Hough and John Corbett, *Beginning Old English* (Palgrave Macmillan, 2007),
pp. 210–220 (Text D: *The Dream of the Rood*, lines 1–77 of 155 — the opening dream-vision and the
Cross's own eyewitness account of the crucifixion; sections 1–2 of the poem's four-part structure).

**Coverage note:** *The Dream of the Rood* is presented by the source as "widely regarded as the
greatest of all Old English religious poems," surviving in the Vercelli Book with partial parallel
attestation in runes on the Ruthwell Cross (Northumbrian dialect, probably late 7th/early 8th
century) and two lines on the Brussels Cross — making it one of the best-attested OE poems across
independent witnesses. This file covers the first two of the poem's four structural sections (lines
1–26: opening vision; lines 27–77: the crucifixion account from the Cross's own perspective);
`076_beginning_old_english_pt12.md` covers sections 3–4 (lines 78–155: the Cross's exhortation to
the dreamer, and the dreamer's reaction) plus the text's full cumulative end-of-text glossary. As in
pt07–pt10, recurring closed-class function words already established elsewhere, or recurring later
within this same text, are consolidated once (in pt12); new content vocabulary gets full individual
treatment here. The poem is represented only through the source's own interlinear glosses and short

## Grammar points

### Riddle-tradition techniques applied to theology (p.213–214)

The source reads the poem's opening as directly drawing on OE riddle conventions (Chapter 6):
delayed identification of the speaking/central object through a deliberately escalating sequence of
names — *trēow* 'tree' → *bēam* 'beam' → *bēacen* 'beacon' → *sige-bēam* 'victory-beam' → *wuldres
trēow* 'tree of glory' → *wealdes trēow* 'tree of the forest/power' → finally, only at line 25b,
*Hǣlendes trēow* 'tree of the Saviour'. Since OE manuscripts carry no titles, the source stresses a
contemporary reader would have genuinely shared the dreamer's gradual, uncertain recognition — unlike
a modern reader who already knows the poem's subject from its editorial title.

### Deliberate lexical ambiguity reused from the riddle tradition (p.213–214)

Several specific word-plays are worked through in detail, all exploiting genuine OE polysemy rather
than coincidence: *weald* 'forest' or 'power' (so *wealdes trēow* may deliberately mean both 'tree
of the forest' and 'tree of power', with a further possible allusion to *wealdend* 'ruler/God');
*bēam* 'wood' or 'ray of light' (both senses active in *bēama beorhtost* 'brightest of
trees/of-rays-of-light'); *stōdon* 'stood' (of the fixed gems) vs. its separately attested sense
'shone' (cross-referenced to Beowulf 726b, pt08's discussion of light shining from Grendel's eyes)
— both senses apt for gems; *fāh* 'stained' or 'guilty' (both relevant to the dreamer's
self-description *synnum fāh*); *mid wommum* 'with stains' or 'with sins'; and the central riddling
negation *Ne wæs þǣr...fracodes gealga* 'that was not a criminal's gallows' — ambiguous between 'not
a gallows at all' and 'not the gallows of a criminal' (i.e. it *was* a gallows, but for an innocent
man), with the source noting both senses are simultaneously true and mutually reinforcing within the
poem's theology.

### The Cross as Christ's surrogate body (p.218–219)

A sustained structural device: because prosopopoeia (the Cross speaking in the first person, exactly
as inanimate objects do in the riddles — cross-referenced explicitly to Chapter 6) lets the poet
split Christ's simultaneously-human-and-divine nature between two separate "characters," all overtly
human suffering is systematically transferred onto the Cross rather than shown happening to Christ
directly: the Cross trembles, is nailed through with visible wounds (*dolg gesīene*), is mocked
alongside Christ (*unc būtū ætgædere*, using the dual pronoun), and is drenched in blood — while
Christ himself is given only active, voluntary verbs (*efstan* 'hastened', *ongyrede hine* 'stripped
himself', *gestāh* 'mounted', *wolde...lȳsan* 'intended to redeem', *ymb-clypte* 'embraced', *his
gāst onsended* 'sent forth his spirit') and heroic epithets (*geong hæleþ* 'young hero', *strang ond
stīþ-mōd* 'strong and resolute', *mōdig* 'brave', *rīcne Cyning* 'powerful king'). The Cross, by
contrast, is given passive and negated verbs throughout (*ne dorste* 'did not dare', three times;
*sceolde...standan* 'had to stand'; *wæs...ārǣred* 'was erected'; *wæs...bestēmed* 'was made wet').
The source notes this systematic grammatical split is reinforced metrically: in lines 30–33a, nearly
every half-line opens with a violent verb directed *at* the Cross (*āstyred*, *genāman*, *geworhton*,
*hēton*, *bǣron*, *gefæstnodon*), giving the passage unusual verb-prominence compared to the
noun/adjective-heavy stress patterns typical of most other OE poetry (cf. Caedmon's Hymn, Chapter 6).

### The crucifixion recast through Germanic heroic-comitatus imagery (p.219)

Christ is depicted with the heroic vocabulary this project has already seen applied to Beowulf and
Byrhtnoth: *geong hæleþ* 'young hero', *beorn* 'warrior', *rīcne Cyning* 'powerful king'. The Cross
itself is cast as a loyal retainer bound by comitatus-style obligation to its lord — with the central
irony that loyalty here forces it to become the literal instrument of its lord's death. The
disciples who remove and bury Christ's body are likewise given warrior/retainer epithets
(*hilde-rincas* 'warriors', *Dryhtnes ðegnas* 'the Lord's retainers') — the source explicitly
cross-references this usage to the identical comitatus framing used of Byrhtnoth's followers in *The
Battle of Maldon* (pt09–pt10), establishing this as a shared compositional convention across
unrelated OE poems, not a one-off choice.

### The crucifixion metaphorically recast as a battle (p.219–220)

A specific sub-thread of the heroic-recasting above: the crucifixion nails become arrows (*mid
strǣlum* 'with arrows', of the Cross's wounds, pt12's lines 62b), and Christ's death is described as
resting *æfter...gewinne* 'after the battle' (l.65a) — paralleling the kenning *feorh-hūs* 'life-house'
for the body in *The Battle of Maldon* 297a (pt10) with this poem's own near-identical but
distinct-compound kenning *feorg-bold* 'life-house' (l.73a, different second element). The source
treats this direct kenning parallel across two otherwise unrelated poems as evidence of a shared
poetic-compound convention circulating across the wider OE verse tradition, not coincidence.

### An exact formulaic self-echo linking dreamer and Cross (p.220)

The Cross's own self-description in line 59a, *Sāre ic wæs mid sorgum gedrēfed* ('I was grievously
troubled with sorrows'), is near-verbatim identical to the dreamer's self-description of himself in
line 20b, *Eall ic wæs mid sorgum gedrēfed* ('I was [entirely] troubled with sorrows') — the source
identifies this as the first of several deliberate formulaic links the poem draws between its three
"characters" (dreamer, Cross, and — in the later sections covered in pt12 — Christ), building toward
a thematic unity across the whole poem's structure.

## Grammar points

### §8.48 — Urban areas
Generic terms for parts of a city (*the business district*, *the inner city*) are lowercased. *Greater*
is capitalized when paired with a city name to denote the whole metropolitan area (*Greater Chicago*),
but lowercased when used descriptively (*the greater Chicago metropolitan area*).

### §8.49 — Real versus metaphorical names
A place name with a real, literal referent (*Mecca*, *Utopia*) is capitalized; the same word used
metaphorically for an unrelated place or quality is lowercased (*a mecca for Shakespeare enthusiasts*,
*create a utopia for her children*).

### §8.50 — Political divisions: capitalization
Terms like *empire*, *republic*, and *state* down to *ward* and *precinct* are capitalized when they
follow a name and are accepted as part of it; they're usually lowercased when they *precede* the name
at the sub-national level (but see §8.51), and lowercased when used alone. A long worked example list
runs from empires and republics through US states, Canadian provinces, Chinese provinces, counties,
cities, townships, congressional districts, wards, precincts, and historical colonial/territorial
divisions, illustrating the "capitalized after the name, lowercased before/alone" pattern across many
jurisdictions.

### §8.51 — Governmental entities
When a specific governmental body (not the geographic place) is meant, words like *state*, *city*, and
*village* are usually capitalized as part of the body's full name (*the Village of Forest Park*, *a
City of Chicago ordinance*) but lowercased when the place itself, not the body, is meant (*the village
of Forest Park*).

### §8.52 — Mountains, rivers, and the like
Names of mountains, rivers, oceans, and islands are capitalized, including the generic term when it's
part of the name. In the plural, the generic is capitalized both when attached to a single name
(*Hawaiian Islands*) and — per a reinstated earlier-edition rule — when it comes *second* and applies
to two or more names (*Mounts Washington and Rainier*; *the Illinois and Chicago Rivers*), since this
unambiguously signals the generic belongs to every name in the list. A single river's name can include
the generic in one construction but not another (*the Nile River valley* vs. *the Nile valley*, where
*river* is part of the proper name but *valley* is not).

### §8.53 — Generic terms for geographic entities
When a generic geographic term is used descriptively, in apposition, or alone rather than as an
accepted part of a proper name, it's lowercased (*the Amazon basin*, *the California desert*, *the
river Thames*) — contrast with the capitalized "West Coast" as an established regional name (§8.46).

### §8.54 — Foreign terms for geographic entities
When a foreign generic term is itself part of a geographic name, the equivalent English term is not
redundantly added (*the Rio Grande*, not *the Rio Grande River*; *Fujiyama*, not *Mount Fujiyama*).

### §8.55 — Thoroughfares and the like
Names of streets, avenues, squares, and parks are capitalized; the generic term is lowercased alone
but — again per a reinstated earlier-edition rule — capitalized when used as part of a plural name
(*Fifty-Seventh and Fifty-Fifth Streets*; *Carnegie and Euclid Avenues*).

### §8.56 — Buildings and monuments
Building and monument names are generally capitalized; the generic term is usually lowercased alone
but capitalized as part of a plural name (*the Empire State and Chrysler Buildings*). Major works of
art are generally italicized, but massive sculptural works are regarded primarily as monuments and so
are *not* italicized (*the Statue of Liberty*; cross-references §8.193).

### §8.57 — Rooms, offices, and such
Official names of rooms and offices are capitalized (*the Oval Office*, *the Lincoln Bedroom*), but a
plain numbered room or suite is not (*room 421*, *suite 219*).

### §8.58 — Foreign names for places and structures
Foreign names of thoroughfares and buildings are not italicized, and may be preceded by an English
*the* only if a definite article would appear in the name's original language (*Unter den Linden* is
never preceded by *the*, since German doesn't use one there).

### §8.59 — When to capitalize (adjectives from proper names)
Adjectives derived from personal names are normally capitalized. If not already found in *Webster's*
(e.g., in its biographical-names section), such adjectives can be coined with *-ian*/*-an* (consonant-
or vowel-ending names respectively) or, failing that, *-esque*; the final consonant of the base name
sometimes shifts for pronunciation (*Foucault* → *Foucauldian*, *Shaw* → *Shavian*). If a name doesn't
lend itself to any such coinage, it's best avoided altogether.

### §8.60 — When not to capitalize
Personal, national, or geographic-derived words are often lowercased when used with a *nonliteral*
meaning — i.e., the word no longer genuinely points back to its origin (*swiss cheese* resembles Swiss
emmentaler but isn't necessarily from Switzerland; *gruyère* takes its name from a Swiss district
without implying the cheese must be from there). Although some of these terms are capitalized in
*Webster's*, Chicago's own house preference is to lowercase them in this nonliteral use; a substantial
example list follows (food terms, fabric/leather terms, typographic terms, and several adjectives of
character like *quixotic*, *philistine*, *herculean*).

### §8.61 — Legislative and deliberative bodies
Full names of legislative/deliberative bodies, departments, bureaus, and offices are capitalized;
adjectives derived from them are usually lowercased, as are many generic names for such bodies used
alone on subsequent mention (*the assembly*, *the council*). Generic names used alone but not
specifically listed in the chapter default to lowercase. A long cross-national survey (US, UK,
Canada, Ireland, France, the Netherlands/historical Estates General, Spain, Mexico, Germany, Ethiopia,
Indonesia, the EU) illustrates the pattern, including bodies whose generic term is given in both a
native-language and an English form.

### §8.62 — Administrative bodies
Full names of administrative bodies are capitalized; adjectives derived from them and many of their
generic names used alone are usually lowercased. A US federal/state/local survey illustrates the
pattern, including an initialism (*the CDC*) that was kept unchanged even after the body's official
name grew longer.

### §8.63 — Judicial bodies
A court's full name, often place-named, is capitalized; subsequent generic references (*district
court*, *supreme court*) are lowercased — except that a reference to a *national*-level supreme court
is capitalized even generically (*the Supreme Court*, but still lowercase *the court*). States,
counties, and cities vary in the generic court-level term they use for an equivalent body (what's
called *supreme court* in most states is *court of appeals* in New York and Maryland); such generic
terms should therefore be used only after the court's full name or jurisdiction has already been
stated, to avoid ambiguity.

### §8.64 — Government entities that are lowercased
Certain generic terms closely associated with a specific government are nonetheless lowercased
(*administration*, *cabinet*, *city hall*, *civil service*, *federal*, *government*, *state*), with a
few named exceptions (*the Kitchen Cabinet*; *Parliament* in the UK, usually without a preceding
*the*). Compare §8.50's pattern for political divisions proper.

### §8.65 — Organizations, parties, alliances, and so forth
Official names of national/international organizations, alliances, and political movements are
capitalized, as are words like *party*, *union*, and *movement* when they're part of an organization's
name, and terms identifying formal members or adherents of such groups (*a Socialist*, *a
Republican*). Names for the underlying *systems of thought* and for looser, non-formal references to
adherents are often lowercased (*an eighteenth-century precursor of socialism*; *a communist at
heart*), as are nonliteral/metaphorical uses (*fascist parenting techniques*; *nazi tendencies*). For
internal consistency, a single work discussing one political system throughout may choose to
capitalize the philosophy, the party, and its members together rather than switch register
mid-discussion. An extensive alphabetical list of parties, alliances, and movements across many
countries and eras follows, including several pairs that are capitalized or lowercased depending on
whether the formal-party sense or the general-ideology sense is meant.

### §8.66 — Adherents of unofficial political groups and movements
Names for adherents of political groups or movements that aren't recognized formal parties are usually
lowercased (*anarchist*, *centrist*, *independent*, *moderate*), with *opposition* capitalized only in
British/Canadian contexts referring specifically to the party out of power (*the Opposition*). *The
Left* and *the Right*, however, are capitalized as an established exception to this general
lowercasing.

### §8.67 — Institutions and companies: capitalization
Full names of institutions, groups, and companies, their departments, and often their shortened forms,
are capitalized. A *the* preceding a name, even when part of the official title, is lowercased in
running text. Generic terms like *company* and *university* are usually lowercased when used alone,
though they're routinely capitalized in the entity's own promotional materials and business documents
— a register distinction, not a factual error on the entity's part.

### §8.68 — Names with unusual capitalization
An organization's own letterhead or promotional-material capitalization (all caps, or all lowercase)
may not match how Chicago style renders the name in running prose (*the Rand Corporation* rather than
*the RAND Corporation*; *DrKoop.net* rather than *drkoop.net*). Names with an internal capital like
*eBay* or *iPod* don't need an added initial capital even at the start of a sentence or heading.

### §8.69 — Associations, unions, and the like
Full names of associations, societies, unions, meetings, and conferences, and often their shortened
forms, are capitalized; a preceding *the* is lowercased in running text, and generic terms like
*society* and *union* are usually lowercased alone. By contrast, a substantive title given to a
*single* meeting, conference, speech, or discussion (as opposed to a standing organization's own name)
is enclosed in quotation marks rather than simply capitalized.

### §8.70 — Numerical designations for periods
A numerical designation of a historical period is lowercased unless it's considered part of a proper
name (*the twenty-first century*, *the nineties* — but *the Eighteenth Dynasty*, *the Fifth Republic*,
which are treated as formal political/dynastic names rather than bare numerical labels).

### §8.71 — Descriptive designations for periods
A descriptive designation of a period is usually lowercased, except where a proper name is embedded
within it (*ancient Greece*, *the baroque period*, *imperial Rome* — all lowercase overall, though
"Greece" and "Rome" themselves remain capitalized as the proper names they are).

### §8.72 — Traditional period names
Some period names are capitalized by tradition or to avoid ambiguity, even though they function
descriptively (*the Renaissance*, *the Enlightenment*, *the Dark Ages*, *the Roaring Twenties*). Some
pairs contrast a traditionally capitalized form with a lowercased alternate phrasing for the same
concept (*the Middle Ages* vs. *the medieval era*; *the Old Regime* vs. *the ancien régime*).

### §8.73 — Cultural periods
Names of prehistoric cultural periods are capitalized (*the Bronze Age*, *the Stone Age*; geological
periods are covered separately at §8.133–35, outside this chunk's range). Similar-sounding terms for
*modern* periods are often lowercased instead (*the age of reason*, *the nuclear age*), though some
traditional modern-period names are capitalized per §8.72.

## Grammar points

### Appendix 1 as a dialect-geography and chronology map, read as a set

Read together rather than piecemeal, Appendix 1's texts form a deliberately-assembled dialect/
period survey that the chapter-by-chapter exposition elsewhere in the book only samples from. The
book supplies, for direct comparison: (1) a **pre-OE Common Germanic** layer (Kylver/Rök/Thames
fuþarks, the Negau helmet, the Gallehus horn) establishing runic writing's origin and its earliest
attested grammatical features (verb-final order on the Gallehus horn, flagged as the oldest
Germanic word-order pattern); (2) an **Ingvaeonic/proto-OE** layer (the Arum sword, the Caistor
bone) bridging Common Germanic to OE specifically; (3) **Northumbrian** texts spanning early
(Ruthwell Cross, Franks Casket, Cædmon's Hymn Moore MS — all roughly 8th century) to late (Lindisfarne
Gospels gloss, 10th century) sub-periods, letting a reader see Northumbrian itself change over two
centuries; (4) a **West Saxon** sequence spanning Early (Parker Chronicle annal 878, Alfred's
Preface) to Late (Ælfric's Life of King Oswald, the Beowulf manuscript, the WS Cædmon's Hymn) —
directly supporting the EWS/LWS distinction introduced in Ch.1 with primary-source evidence rather
than assertion; (5) single representative witnesses for **Mercian** (Vespasian Psalter Gloss) and
**Kentish** (Kentish Psalm) — the two OE dialects the chapter-level exposition otherwise only
mentions in passing; and (6) a **transitional OE→ME** text (Peterborough Chronicle Final
Continuation, 1140) showing the inflectional system's collapse in progress, decades past the
nominal 1066 OE/ME boundary — directly evidencing the book's own repeated point (Ch.1, Ch.4) that
the OE period's end is gradual, not a hard cutoff at the Conquest.

### Diagnostic method: how the book teaches dialect-spotting via minimal contrastive forms

Rather than describing each dialect abstractly, every text in Appendix 1 is annotated with a short,
specific list of **diagnostic forms** that distinguish it from the WS reference variety used
throughout the rest of the book — modeling exactly the comparative method the book introduced
in Ch.1. Compiled together, the diagnostic contrasts given are:
- **Northumbrian vs. WS** (Cædmon's Hymn comparison): *Uard* vs. *weard* (failure of breaking);
  *suē* vs. *swā* (a distinct Anglian vowel-reflex of West Germanic ā, not merely a spelling
  difference); *heben* vs. *WS equivalent* (consonant spelling, <b> for <f>); *til* vs. *tō*
  (a lexical substitution, cognate with Old Norse *til* — i.e. a different word, not just a
  different spelling of the same word); *<ae>* vs. *<æ>* (orthographic only).
- **Late Northumbrian vs. earlier Northumbrian/WS** (Lindisfarne gloss): generalized *-as* verb
  endings across both present-tense singular and plural (a leveling/simplification the book
  explicitly connects to broader inflectional erosion); *aron* 'are' beside *biðon*, both competing
  with WS's *sind(on)/bēoþ*.
- **Mercian vs. WS** (Vespasian Psalter Gloss): *feder/daegas/aeldran* vs. *fæder/dagas/ieldran* —
  a shared, systematic vowel-quality difference (a/æ) rather than isolated lexical items.
- **Kentish vs. WS** (Kentish Psalm): *efter/sennum/geltas/on aldre* vs. *æfter/synnum/gyltas/on
  ealdre* — again a patterned vowel/consonant correspondence, not scattered spelling noise.
- **EWS-internal variation** (Parker Chronicle, Alfred's Preface): the book is careful to show that
  even its own EWS reference variety isn't perfectly uniform — *was*/*wærun*/*kyning* appear
  alongside the "textbook" *wæs*/*wæron*/*cyning* in the same corpus, and *io* for *eo*, *hiora* for
  *hiera*, *meahton* for *mihton* are all flagged as genuine EWS-internal variants, not errors.
- **EWS vs. LWS** (cross-referencing Ch.1's periodization): *ie/īe* (EWS) vs. *y/ȳ* (LWS) in
  *ieldran*/*yldran* 'elders' and *gīet*/*gyt* 'still' is given as the single clearest diagnostic
  pair; Ælfric's LWS Oswald text separately supplies *fynd* (LWS) vs. *fīend* (EWS) 'enemies',
  *gelyfed* (LWS) vs. *gelīefed* (EWS) 'believed', and pronoun-paradigm shifts (*hī/heom/heora* for
  textbook *hīe/him/hiera*).
- **Transitional OE→ME** (Peterborough Chronicle, 1140): the book's own analysis names four
  distinct simultaneous change-vectors in a single short passage — a relic OE syntactic pattern
  (verb-second order, *Þa ferde Eustace*) persisting alongside *loss* of OE's determiner system,
  a genuinely new pronoun form (*scæ* 'she', not derivable by simple sound-change from OE *hēo*),
  and lexical borrowing from two different contact languages in the same sentence (Norse *oc*/*toc*;
  probably Norman French *castel*) — a compact illustration of how phonological, morphological,
  syntactic, and lexical change can all be mid-stream in the same text at the same time, rather than
  proceeding on separate timelines.

### Verse-form confirmation across dialects: the half-line/alliteration system holds across Northumbrian and WS alike

The parallel WS/Northumbrian texts of Cædmon's Hymn let the book make a point not otherwise
demonstrable from a single-dialect example: the half-line-plus-alliteration verse structure
introduced via *The Dream of the Rood* in Ch.3 is not a West-Saxon-specific literary convention but
a shared OE (indeed pan-early-Germanic, cf. the Gallehus horn's own formulaic structure) poetic
system that survives translation-like dialect substitution intact — the Northumbrian and WS
versions of the Hymn differ systematically in their phonology and some vocabulary (*suē*/*swā*,
*til*/*tō*) while preserving the same alliterative half-line pattern line-for-line.

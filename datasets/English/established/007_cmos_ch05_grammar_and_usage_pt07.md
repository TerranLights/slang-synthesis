## Grammar points

### Past subjunctive (§5.140)

Typically appears as *if I [he, she, it] had been*, ordinarily using a past-perfect verb form to
connote uncertainty or impossibility — contrasted with the parallel past-indicative form, which
states a simple fact: *if it arrived, it was not properly filed* (indicative) vs. *if it had arrived,
it could have changed the course of history* (subjunctive).

### Imperative (§5.141)

The verb's bare stem used to issue a command, a request, or an exclamation, e.g. {Come here!} {Give
me a clue.} {Help!}.

### Auxiliary verbs defined (§5.142)

An auxiliary (or modal, or helping) verb is a highly irregular verb used with other verbs to form
voice, tense, and mood. The source treats the following sections as an enumerated catalog of the
main English auxiliaries, each profiled for its forms and its distinct semantic range: *can*/*could*,
*do*, *have*, *may*/*might*, *must*, *ought*, *shall*/*should*, *will*/*would* — see the Vocabulary
table above for each one's individual profile.

### "Can" (§5.143)

Stem-only in the present indicative for all persons; *could* for all persons in the past. No
infinitive form. When denoting ability, capacity, or permission, always followed by an explicit or
implicit principal verb. In the permission sense, *can* is flagged by the source as colloquial for
*may*. Also connotes actual possibility, distinct from the ability/permission senses.

### "Do" (§5.144)

Two present-indicative forms (*does* 3rd singular, *do* elsewhere); one past form (*did*) for all
persons; past participle *done*. As auxiliary, used only in present/past indicative, including
negative imperatives ({Do not touch!} {Don't be an idiot!}). As principal verb, denotes performance.
Can substitute for a repeated verb to avoid redundancy.

### "Have" (§5.145)

Two present forms (*has*/*have*), one past form (*had*, also serving as the past participle). As
auxiliary, precedes a past participle to form present-perfect or past-perfect mood. As principal
verb, denotes possession. *Have got* + infinitive is near-synonymous with *must*; *had* + *to* +
infinitive expresses the past form of that same *must*-sense.

### "May" and "might" (§5.146)

*May* denotes permission, sometimes displaced in the negative by the more intensive *must not*. Also
connotes uncertain possibility. Stem-only in the present indicative for all persons; the past
indicative often shifts to *might*, especially to connote uncertainty. No infinitive or participle
forms. As an auxiliary expressing wishfulness or purpose, *may* forms a subjunctive-equivalent
construction, e.g. {may you always be happy}.

### "Must" (§5.147)

Denotes necessity arising from someone's will or from circumstance, and also a logical conclusion.
Invariant across present and past indicative; no infinitive (*to have to* substitutes) or participle
forms. Always paired with an express or implied infinitive.

### "Ought" (§5.148)

Denotes what is reasonably expected of a person or by circumstances; more emphatic than *should*. No
form variation, infinitive, or participles. Always used with an infinitive, even in the negative;
*to* is occasionally dropped after *not*, but the source prescribes retaining it as the better usage.

### "Shall" and "should" (§5.149)

*Shall* uses only its stem form in the present indicative and is explicitly characterized as
relatively rare in present-day American English, largely confined to first-person jocular use (e.g.
{Shall we dance?}). *Should*, its paradigmatic past form, is used for all persons with a principal
verb and does not actually function as a true past tense — the source notes this discrepancy
directly. *Should* carries three distinct senses depending on context: duty/compulsion/expectation,
logical inference, or the speaker's own attitude.

### "Will" and "would" (§5.150)

*Will* has several auxiliary senses and uses only its stem form in the present indicative; *would* is
the sole past-indicative form for all persons. *Would* can express a condition or a conditional
statement; *will* often carries a sense of futurity.

### Forms of "be"-verbs (§5.151)

The verb *be* has eight forms (*be, is, are, was, were, been, being,* and *am*) and several special
uses: (1) as a principal verb meaning "exist"; (2) more often as an auxiliary verb, forming
progressive aspect (joined with a present participle) or passive voice (joined with a past
participle); (3) as the most common linking verb, connecting a subject to something affirmed of it;
and occasionally (4) as part of an adjective or a noun, e.g. {a rock star wannabe}, {a would-be
hero}, {a has-been}.

### Conjugation of "be"-verbs (§5.152)

*Be* conjugates differently from every other English verb. (1) Its bare stem is never used in the
present indicative; instead *am* (1st singular), *is* (3rd singular), and *are* (all other persons)
substitute. (2) The present participle is formed regularly with *-ing* on the root *be* ({being}),
used identically for all persons, though the present-perfect also requires *am*, *is*, or *are*
({I am being}, {you are being}). (3) The past indicative has two forms: *was* for 1st/3rd person
singular, *were* for all other persons. (4) The past participle for all persons is *been*. (5) The
imperative is the verb's stem, {Be yourself!}.

### Adverbs defined (§5.153)

An adverb is a word that qualifies, limits, describes, or modifies a verb, an adjective, or another
adverb ({she studied constantly}; {the juggler's act was really unusual}; {the cyclist pedaled very
swiftly}). An adverb may also qualify a preposition, a conjunction, or an entire clause ({the birds
flew right over the lake}; {this is exactly where I found it}; {apparently you forgot to check your
references}). The source notes that grammarians have traditionally used "adverb" as a catch-all
category sweeping in words that don't fit neatly elsewhere, such as the infinitival *to* (see §5.104)
and the particle in a phrasal verb (see §5.100).

### Adverbs with suffixes (§5.154)

Most adjectives have a corresponding adverb marked by the suffix *-ly*, or *-ally* after words ending
in *-ic* ({slow→slowly}, {careful→carefully}, {public→publicly}, {pedantic→pedantically}). Most
adjectives ending in *-y* change the *-y* to *-i* before adding the suffix, with some exceptions
({happy→happily}, {shy→shyly}). A few adjectives ending in *-e* drop the vowel ({true→truly},
{whole→wholly}); if the *-le* is sounded as part of a syllable, it's instead replaced with *-ly*
({terrible→terribly}, {simple→simply}). An adjective ending in a double *-l* takes only *-y*
({dull→dully}). Many adjectives ending in *-le* or *-ly* do not have graceful adverb forms
({juvenile→juvenilely}, {silly→sillily}); where the *-ly* form looks clumsy (e.g., *ghastlily* or
*uglily*), the source recommends rephrasing with a prepositional phrase instead (*in a ghastly
manner*, *in an ugly way*). Adverbial suffixes are sometimes added to whole phrases
({replied matter-of-factly}). Some terms compound with nouns to form adverbs using the endings
*-ways* ({side→sideways}), *-ward* ({sky→skyward}), or *-wise* ({clock→clockwise}).

### Adverbs without suffixes (§5.155)

Many adverbs carry no identifying suffix at all (e.g., *almost, never, here, now, just, seldom, late,
near, too*). Conversely, not every word ending in *-ly* is an adverb — some are adjectives (e.g.,
*lovely, curly*).

### Adverbs distinguished from adjectives (§5.156)

Unlike an adjective, an adverb never modifies a noun or pronoun. Compare {we made an early start and
arrived at the airport early}: the first *early* is an adjective modifying the noun *start*; the
second is an adverb modifying the verb *arrived*. Some adverbs are identical in form to prepositions
(e.g. *up*, *off*) but are distinguishable because they aren't attached to a following noun ({he ran
up a large bill} vs. {let's cast off}); these prepositional adverbs — also called particles or
particle adverbs — are typically the second element of a phrasal verb (see §5.100).

### Simple and flat adverbs (§5.157)

A simple adverb is a single word qualifying a single part of speech ({hardly}, {now}, {deep}). A flat
(or bare) adverb is one that has an *-ly* form but whose plain adjectival form works equally well or
better, especially paired with an imperative in an informal register ({drive slow}, {hold on tight},
{tell me quick}). Some flat adverbs are now always used in their adjectival form because the *-ly*
counterpart has become obsolete, though it can survive embedded in other words (e.g., *steadfast* and
*steadfastly*). A flat adverb can also carry a different meaning from its *-ly* counterpart entirely —
contrast {I am working hard} with {I am hardly working}.

### Phrasal and compound adverbs (§5.158)

A phrasal adverb is two or more words functioning together as a single adverb ({in the meantime},
{for a while}, {here and there}). A compound adverb appears to be one word but is actually a fusion of
several ({notwithstanding}, {heretofore}, {thereupon}); the source explicitly cautions that compound
adverbs should be used sparingly, since they tend to make the tone stuffy — a direct register/formality
judgment worth flagging for this project's usage-tier interest.

### Three adverbial degrees (§5.159)

As with adjectives (§5.83), adverbs inflect across three degrees: positive, comparative, and
superlative. A positive adverb simply expresses a quality without reference to anything else
({the nurse spoke softly}, {the choir sang merrily}).

### Comparative forms (§5.160)

Most one-syllable adverbs that don't end in *-ly* form the comparative by adding *-er*
({soon→sooner}, {hard→harder}). Multisyllable adverbs usually form the comparative periphrastically,
with *more* or *less* ({the villain fenced more ineptly than the hero}). Exceptions exist for adverbs
ending in *-ly* where the *-ly* isn't itself a derivational suffix ({early→earlier}). A comparative
adverb compares the quality of a shared action or condition between two things.

### Superlative forms (§5.161)

Most one-syllable adverbs that don't end in *-ly* form the superlative with *-est* ({soon→soonest},
{hard→hardest}). Multisyllable adverbs usually use *most* or *least* periphrastically
({of all the people making choices, he chose least wisely}), with the same *-ly*-is-not-a-suffix
exception as the comparative ({early→earliest}). A superlative adverb compares a shared quality among
at least three things. In a loose, non-literal sense, the superlative is also sometimes used purely
for emphasis rather than true comparison ({the pianist played most skillfully}) — a register-relevant
usage note the source flags explicitly.

### Irregular adverbs (§5.162)

A few adverbs have irregular comparative and superlative forms ({badly→worse→worst},
{little→less→least}); the source defers to a dictionary as the authoritative resource for any given
irregular adverb's comparison forms rather than listing them exhaustively itself.

### Uncomparable adverbs (§5.163)

Many adverbs are by definition absolute and cannot be compared at all ({eternally}, {never},
{singly}, {uniquely}, {universally}). Most adverbs indicating time ({now}, {then}), position ({on}),
number ({first}, {finally}), or place ({here}) are likewise uncomparable.

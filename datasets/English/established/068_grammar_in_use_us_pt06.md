## Grammar points

### Unit 84 — Relative clauses (1) — clauses with who/that/which

A relative clause identifies which person or thing (or what kind) the speaker means, substituting for
a pronoun (he/she/they for people → who; it/they for things → that) inside the embedded clause. "That"
is an available and in practice more common substitute for "who" with people; "which" is available for
things but "that" is more usual. p. 168.

### Unit 85 — Relative clauses (2) — clauses with or without who/that

When who/that/which functions as the subject of the relative clause's own verb, it cannot be omitted.
When it functions as the object instead, it can be dropped entirely ("The man I wanted to see was away
on vacation," not "...I wanted to see him..."). Prepositions governing the relative pronoun are
normally left stranded at the end of the clause in ordinary usage ("the girl (who/that) Tom is talking
to"). "What" cannot substitute for "that" in these clauses — "what" instead independently means "the
thing(s) that" and introduces its own free relative ("Did you hear what I said?"). pp. 170.

### Unit 86 — Relative clauses (3) — whose, whom, and where

"Whose" substitutes for his/her/their, used mostly for people (a widow is a woman whose husband is
dead). "Whom" is a more formal substitute for "who" as an object of the relative clause's verb,
including after a preposition (with whom), but is rare in ordinary spoken English, where who/that or
omission is strongly preferred. "Where" introduces a relative clause about a place. "That" (optionally
omitted) introduces relative clauses after time-nouns (the day/year/time that...) and "the reason why"
/"the reason that" (either word optionally omittable) after "the reason." p. 172.

### Units 87–88 — Relative clauses (4) and (5) — "extra information" clauses

Distinguishes restrictive clauses (identify which referent is meant) from non-restrictive "extra
information" clauses (add supplementary detail about an already-identified referent: "Tom's father,
who is 78, goes swimming every day"). Extra-information clauses require who/which (never "that," never
omittable) and are set off by commas on both sides (or one side, sentence-finally); whose/whom/where
remain available in this clause type too. In formal written register, a preposition fronts together
with whom/which ("Mr. Carter, to whom I spoke..."); spoken register instead strands the preposition
and switches to "who" ("This is Mr. Carter, who I was telling you about"). A fixed pattern collapses
two sentences by combining a quantifier (all/most/none/many/a few/some/any/half/each/both/neither/
either/one/two, etc.) + "of" + whom (people) or which (things) ("Jack has three brothers, all of whom
are married"). "Which" can also head a non-restrictive clause referring back to an entire preceding
fact/statement rather than a single noun ("She couldn't come to the party, which was a pity") — "what"
is explicitly disallowed in this use. pp. 174–176.

### Unit 89 — -ing and -ed clauses ("the woman talking to Tom," "the man injured in the accident")

A reduced-relative-clause pattern: -ing clauses mark either a momentary in-progress action ("the woman
talking to Tom" = the woman is talking to Tom) or, for things and occasionally people, a permanent
characteristic ("the room overlooking the garden"). -ed clauses carry a passive meaning ("the man
injured in the accident" = the man was injured); the unit flags that many past participles used this
way are irregular and don't end in -ed (stolen, made, bought, written). Both clause types commonly
follow "there is/there was" existential constructions. p. 178.

### Unit 90 — Adjectives ending in -ing and -ed (boring/bored, etc.)

A large closed set of adjective pairs (boring/bored, interesting/interested, surprising/surprised,
disappointing/disappointed, tiring/tired, fascinating/fascinated, exciting/excited, amusing/amused,
amazing/amazed, astonishing/astonished, shocking/shocked, disgusting/disgusted, embarrassing/
embarrassed, confusing/confused, horrifying/horrified, terrifying/terrified, frightening/frightened,
depressing/depressed, worrying/worried, annoying/annoyed, exhausting/exhausted, satisfying/satisfied)
splits consistently: the -ing form describes the thing/person that causes a feeling ("Jane's job is
boring"); the -ed form describes the person who experiences that feeling ("Jane is bored"). A person
themself is never described with the -ing form in this sense ("Jane is bored," not "Jane is boring,"
unless literally describing her as a cause of boredom to others). p. 180.

### Unit 91 — Adjectives: word order; after verbs

When two or more adjectives stack before a noun, opinion adjectives (nice, beautiful, delicious)
precede fact adjectives (new, large, round, wooden, etc., giving objective size/age/color information).
Among multiple fact adjectives, a loose common ordering runs: how big → how old → what color → where
from → what it's made of, with size/length adjectives (big/small/tall/short/long) typically preceding
shape/width adjectives (round/fat/thin/slim/wide). Separately, a small closed set of linking verbs
(be/get/become, and feel/smell/taste/sound/seem/look) takes an adjective complement rather than an
adverb ("Dinner smells good," not "well" in this sense); other ordinary verbs require an adverb
("Drive carefully!" not "careful"). "Look" alone (meaning "seem") takes an adjective ("Tom looked
sad"), but "look at" takes an adverb ("looked at me sadly"). pp. 182.

### Unit 92 — Adjectives and adverbs (1) (quick/quickly)

The default adverb-formation pattern is adjective + -ly (quick→quickly, serious→seriously); a closed
counter-set of words ending in -ly (friendly, lively, elderly, lonely, silly, lovely) are themselves
adjectives, not derived adverbs, and need their own paraphrase to use adverbially. Adjectives modify
nouns and follow the Unit 91c linking-verb set; adverbs modify verbs ("drove carefully," not
"careful"), and can themselves be modified by another adverb (incredibly quickly) or intensify an
adjective (reasonably cheap, terribly sorry) or a past participle (seriously injured, totally
destroyed). p. 184.

### Unit 93 — Adjectives and adverbs (2) (good/well, fast/hard/late, hardly)

"Good" is the adjective; "well" is its ordinary adverb counterpart, frequently used before past
participles (well dressed, well known, well educated) and also standing alone as an adjective meaning
"in good health" ("I'm very well, thanks"). Fast/hard/late use an identical form for both adjective and
adverb ("Jack is a fast runner" / "Jack can run very fast"); "lately" (= recently) is a distinct
derived adverb, not the adverb form of "late." "Hardly" is flagged as a false-derivation trap: it does
not mean "in a hard manner" but "almost not," and commonly pairs with can/could ("I can hardly read
it") and any-compounds (hardly any, hardly anyone, hardly anything); "hardly ever" = almost never.
p. 186.

### Unit 94 — So and such

"So" precedes a bare adjective or adverb without a noun ("so stupid"); "such" precedes an adjective +
noun ("such a stupid story"). Both intensify the adjective's force and commonly combine with a "that"
result clause (so stupid that..., such a stupid story that..., with "that" optionally omittable). A
second, comparative-deviation sense of "so"/"such" expresses "as X as it actually is/was, more than
expected" (I didn't realize it was so old / such an old house). Fixed lexicalized exceptions override
the general pattern: "so long" but "such a long time"; "so far" but "such a long way"; "so many"/"so
much" but "such a lot (of)." p. 188.

### Unit 95 — Enough and too

"Enough" follows adjectives/adverbs (experienced enough, hard enough) but precedes nouns (enough
experience, enough chairs), and can stand alone without a following noun. Both "enough" and "too" take
an optional "for someone/something" complement, and both require a following infinitive rather than
"for -ing" ("enough money to take a vacation," "too far to walk," never "for taking"/"for walking").
"Too...to (do)" expresses an excessive degree that prevents an action, and absorbs what would otherwise
be a separate pronoun object inside the infinitive clause ("The food was too hot to eat," not "too hot
to eat it"). p. 190.

## Grammar points

### §4.1 — Introduction

This chapter addresses particular punctuation situations that can be problematic or treated in
different ways — not a full account of how punctuation is used in English generally. Some sections
(e.g. the comma splice, apostrophe plurals) give guidance on correct usage needed to write good
English; others (e.g. the serial comma) describe situations where multiple styles are legitimate but
one should be adopted consistently.

### §4.2.1 — Apostrophe: possession

Use 's after singular nouns and indefinite pronouns (*the boy's job*, *anyone's guess*) and after
plural nouns not ending in *s* (*people's opinions*). For singular nouns ending in an *s* sound, the
extra *s* may be omitted if pronunciation would be awkward (*the catharsis' effects*), though
transposing with *of* is often preferable. Use an apostrophe alone after plural nouns ending in *s*
(*our neighbours' children*) and this extends to periods of time (*a few days' holiday*) — but not to
adjectival constructions like *three months pregnant*. Possessive pronouns (*hers*, *its*, *ours*,
*yours*, *theirs*) never take an apostrophe; *its* (possessive) must be distinguished from *it's*
(contraction). In compounds/*of*-phrases, 's goes after the last noun if singular, apostrophe-alone
if plural (*my sister-in-law's car* vs. *the King of the Netherlands' appeal*). For linked nouns
acting together, 's follows only the last noun (*Liddell and Scott's Greek–English Lexicon*); for
nouns acting separately, each takes its own 's (*Johnson's and Webster's lexicography*). Business
names ending in *s* are often now written apostrophe-free as if plural (*Harrods*), though other
institutions retain it (*Levi's*) — editors should not alter a consistently applied existing style.
Personal names ending in an *s*/*x*/*z* sound generally take 's (*Charles's*, *Marx's*), except
longer names unaccented on the final/penultimate syllable, where apostrophe-alone is acceptable
(*Nicholas'*). *Jesus's* is the usual non-liturgical form; *Jesus'* is an accepted archaism. Classical
names ending in *s*/*es* traditionally take apostrophe-alone when long (*Euripides'*), though short
names may take 's (*Zeus's*); scientific/technical contexts generally require the additional *s*
regardless (*Mars's gravitational force*, contrasted with the traditional *Mars' spear*). French
names with a silent final *s*/*x*/*z* take 's when used possessively in English (*Dumas's*). An
italicized name/term's possessive 's is set in roman type. Apostrophes are not used in the names of
wars known by their length (*Hundred Years War*). Whether a place/organization name ending in *s*
needs an apostrophe is unpredictable and must be checked per-name (*Land's End* but *All Souls
College*).

### §4.2.2 — Apostrophe: plurals

The "greengrocer's apostrophe" (see Vocabulary) is incorrect. No apostrophe is needed for plurals of
names, abbreviations, numbers, or words not usually used as nouns (*the Joneses*, *CDs*, *the
1990s*, *whys and wherefores*). An apostrophe may be used where clarity calls for it — letters or
symbols referred to as objects (*dot the i's and cross the t's*, *find all the number 7's*); such
items may also be italicized or quoted, with the plural *s* itself set in roman outside any closing
quote mark.

### §4.2.3 — Apostrophe: contraction

Contractions (printed without internal spaces) take an apostrophe in place of missing letters
(*won't*, *we'll*), except words now accepted in their own right (*cello*, *phone*, *flu*), which
take no apostrophe except when deliberately reproducing older spellings. When the apostrophe marks
elision of an initial or final letter(s) (*o'*, *'tis*), it is set with a full space rather than
closed up to the next character (*rock 'n' roll*; an ampersand may substitute, *rock & roll*, see
§10.2.2) — but an apostrophe marking a *medial* elision is closed up on both sides (*learn'd*,
*ma'am*). The older *'d* construction for a pronounced-vowel *-ed* ending (*concertina'd*) has
mostly given way to a conventional *-ed* spelling (*subpoenaed*), though *'d* persists in poetry and
older typography to show an unstressed *-ed* (*belov'd* vs. *belovèd*). An apostrophe is retained
before a suffix when an abbreviation functions as a verb (*KO's*, *OK'ing*, *OD'd*).

### §4.3.1 — Comma: restrictive and non-restrictive uses

A restrictive (defining) relative clause, which cannot be omitted without changing the sentence's
meaning, is not set off by commas (*The people who live there are really frightened*). A
non-restrictive (non-defining) clause — one adding incidental information of the "and he/she is"
type, removable without changing the sentence's core meaning — must be enclosed in commas
(*Identical twins, who are always of the same sex, arise in a different way*). **Explicit
British/American contrast (non-redundant supplement to CMOS §6.22):** in British English, either
*which* or *that* may be used in a restrictive clause, but only *which* may be used in a
non-restrictive one; in US English, by contrast, *which* is specifically *not* used for restrictive
clauses at all — the restrictive/non-restrictive distinction is instead carried entirely by the
*that*/*which* choice itself in American usage. The same restrictive/non-restrictive logic extends to
apposition: no comma for a restrictive (defining) appositive that picks out which of several possible
referents is meant (*The ancient poet Homer is credited with two great epics*), but when the name and
descriptive noun are transposed, commas become required (*Homer, the ancient poet, is credited...*).
A non-restrictive appositive always takes a comma pair when medial (*I met my wife, Dorothy, at a
dance*); an unmatched single comma is an error. A comma is not used once a descriptive phrase has
effectively become part of a name (*Bob the Builder*, no commas) as opposed to a one-off descriptive
aside (*Dave, the builder from down the road, said...*).

### §4.3.3 — After an introductory clause or adverb

An introductory adverb, adverbial phrase, or subordinate clause is often set off from the main clause
by a comma (*Surprisingly, Richard liked the idea*), though this is not essential for a short
introductory phrase specifying time or location (*In 2000 the hospital took part in a trial...*) —
indeed the source recommends avoiding the comma there to keep text uncluttered. A comma is needed,
however, when a preposition is used as an adverb (*In the valley below, the villages looked very
small*), and omitting a comma anywhere in this territory risks real ambiguity (*In 2000 deaths
involving MRSA in males increased by 66 per cent* — better recast or comma'd after *2000*). A comma
should never follow a sentence's bare subject except to introduce a parenthetical clause (not *The
coastal city of Bordeaux, is a city of stone*). Sentence-initial *however*, *moreover*, *therefore*,
*already* are usually followed by a comma; medial *however*/*moreover* are enclosed in a comma pair;
but *however* takes no comma when it modifies an adjective/adverb instead of functioning as a
conjunctive adverb (*However fast Achilles runs he will never reach the tortoise*).

### §4.3.4 — Commas separating adjectives

See the Vocabulary table's gradable-adjective and classifying-adjective entries for the core
distinction. Writers may depart from the general comma-between-qualitative-adjectives principle for
deliberate effect — to give pace to a narrative, or to follow a low-comma technical-writing style.

### §4.3.5 — Serial comma

See the Vocabulary table entry for the term and its Oxford-comma naming history. The general
principle: pick one style (comma or no comma before the final conjunction) and apply it consistently,
though an isolated serial comma may still be inserted for clarification even in text that otherwise
doesn't use the convention, when ambiguity would otherwise result (illustrated with a bishops-list
example where comma placement determines whether "Bath and Wells" is read as one bishopric or two
separate names). Before a final extension phrase like *etc.*, *and so forth*, *and the like*, a
comma is used as if it were simply the final list item. Only elements that genuinely share a
syntactic relationship with the list's introductory material should be comma-joined this way — a
caution illustrated by a case where only two of three apparent "items" actually complete the
introductory verb phrase, requiring a conjunction-only fix rather than serial-comma treatment.

### §4.3.6 — Comma: figures

Most styles use commas to separate large numbers into groups of three from the right (*2,016,523,354*),
though technical texts may instead use a thin/non-breaking space as the thousands separator.

### §4.3.7 — Comma: use in letters

Commas are used in formal letters after some salutations and before the signature (*Dear Sir,* /
*Yours sincerely,*). **Explicit British/American contrast:** "US business letters use a colon after
the greeting" instead of a comma. On both sides of the Atlantic, punctuation is now sometimes omitted
entirely, particularly after email salutations and in informal writing.

### §4.3.8 — Comma: other uses

A comma often introduces direct speech (cross-reference to §9.2.2). A comma follows *namely* and
*for example* when they introduce a parenthetical phrase, but not when they merely introduce an item
or list without a parenthetical framing. A comma is generally required after *that is*.
**Explicit British/American contrast:** "Oxford style does not use a comma after *i.e.* and *e.g.* to
avoid double punctuation, but US usage does."

### §4.4 — Semicolon

The semicolon marks a separation stronger than a comma but weaker than a full point, dividing two or
more closely related main clauses that could stand as independent sentences (*Truth ennobles man;
learning adorns him*). Where a colon would instead be more suitable is when one clause explains
another (see §4.5). In an already comma-subdivided sentence, a semicolon can mark a stronger division
than the commas; in a list where elements themselves contain commas, semicolons clarify the grouping
of components (illustrated with a multi-clause claim list and a thank-you list with internal commas
per item). Because beginning a sentence with a lower-case symbol can look confusing, a semicolon can
also simply replace a full point before a non-capitalized symbol (e.g. a sentence beginning with the
variable *a*).

### §4.5 — Colon

The colon "points forward" — from premise to conclusion, cause to effect, introduction to main point,
general statement to example — fulfilling the same function as *namely*, *that is*, *as follows*,
*therefore*, etc. Material after a colon need not be a grammatically complete, standalone sentence.
Used to introduce a list; the older combined colon-plus-dash (" :— ") is now avoided except when
reproducing antique or foreign-language typography. **Explicit British/American contrast:** "The word
following a colon is not capitalized in British English (unless it is a proper name), but in US
English it is often capitalized if it introduces a grammatically complete sentence" (illustrated: *Mr
Smith had committed two sins: First, his publication...*). A colon should not precede a linking word
introducing a list, and should follow one only when the linking phrase is itself introduced by a main
clause. A colon should not be used to introduce a statement/list that would otherwise complete the
sentence formed by its own introduction. A dash can substitute for a colon in a similar role but the
two aren't strictly interchangeable — a dash is more informal and implies more of an afterthought (see
§4.11.2). A colon introduces a subtitle after a work's title, optionally followed by a capital or
lower-case letter (Oxford style capitalizes). A colon may also introduce direct speech (§9.2.2).

### §4.7 — Ellipses

See the Vocabulary table entry for the core definition, settable form, and the Oxford four-point
convention. A normal word space is set on either side of an ellipsis in running text, though fixed
(narrower) spacing is sometimes used instead. An ellipsis at the end of an *incomplete* sentence takes
no additional fourth full point; but when an incomplete-sentence ellipsis is itself embedded within a
larger complete sentence, the larger sentence's own closing full point is still added after the final
quotation mark. A comma immediately before/after an ellipsis can generally be suppressed unless
helpful to the sense. If text following an ellipsis begins a new complete sentence (in the original),
it should start with a capital letter. Question marks and exclamation marks at a sentence's end are
retained before or after an adjoining ellipsis rather than dropped. An ellipsis can show a speaker
trailing off or create a dramatic/ironic effect, or — like *etc.* — show the expected continuation of
an inferable sequence (*in 1997, 1999, 2001…*). Cross-reference: §9.3.3 for ellipses in presenting
quoted material; §14.6.2 for mathematical-notation ellipses.

### §4.8.1 — Question mark: typical uses

Question marks indicate direct questions, not indirect ones implied by reported speech (*He wants to
know whether you are coming* takes no question mark). Idiomatic/polite requests phrased as questions
don't normally take one either (*May I take this opportunity to wish you...*), though a question mark
can read as more polite than a full point in some such cases. Text following a question mark starts
with a capital letter, except a short embedded question within another sentence, which is not
capitalized (*Where now? they wonder*). Embedded questions not in quotation marks are often left
uncapitalized; the question mark itself follows the question wherever it falls within the sentence,
even mid-sentence. A directly quoted question (voiced or merely formulated in someone's mind) is
capitalized and set in quotation marks. A question mark at sentence-end functions like a full point
— no double punctuation (full account of interaction with quotation marks at §9.2.3).

### §4.8.2 — Question mark: use to express uncertainty

A question mark can mark doubt about a specific word/phrase/figure, placed immediately before or
after it, in parentheses if it would otherwise interrupt the sentence; strictly it should be closed up
to a single doubtful word but space-separated if more of the sentence is doubtful — though this
distinction is probably too subtle for most contexts, and explicit rewording is often preferable
since the device doesn't make clear *which* aspect of the text is contentious. Used to flag
uncertain dates, where care is needed to make clear exactly which element (day/month/year) is in
doubt, and whether an elided range (*1883–8?*) wrongly implies the whole range is uncertain when only
one end is. Explanatory rewording is generally preferable to relying on question-mark placement alone
for this purpose. A distinction is understood between a question mark and *c.* (*circa*) with dates:
a question-mark-qualified date is *probable*; a *c.*-qualified one means the event happened at an
unknown time before, on, or after the stated date (and the implied range differs depending on whether
the period is written out or abbreviated, e.g. *c.1650* vs. *c.1653*). A parenthetical question mark
is sometimes used to underline sarcasm (*With friends (?) like that, you don't need enemies*).

### §4.12.5 — Punctuation with brackets

Rules governing punctuation are the same regardless of bracket type. A complete sentence fully
enclosed in brackets is capitalized and ends with its own full point inside the closing bracket,
unless the writer has chosen to place that bracketed sentence within a larger one, in which case the
bracket's content takes no closing full point of its own (full point goes outside instead, after the
larger sentence). No punctuation precedes an opening bracket except terminal punctuation before a
fully bracketed sentence, or where brackets mark enumerated divisions within text (*(a) where to go,
(b) whom to invite*).

### §4.12.6 — Nested brackets

See the Vocabulary table entry. Avoid brackets within brackets in normal running text where possible,
though it is sometimes unavoidable (e.g. a parenthetical mention that itself already contains
parentheses). References to law reports and statutes vary in their own bracket conventions and should
follow whatever is prescribed (cross-reference Chapter 13).

### §4.13.1 — Solidus

See the Vocabulary table entry for the core definition and uses. The symbol is sometimes misread as
meaning "and" rather than "or," so it is often best to spell out alternatives explicitly where
misreading is a risk (*his or her* rather than *his/her*). An en rule can sometimes substitute for a
solidus to express an alternative (*an on–off relationship*).

### §4.13.2 — Vertical

See the Vocabulary table entry. A solidus may substitute for a vertical in marking run-on verse
lines; where written lines and verse lines don't coincide, a vertical is used for written-line breaks
and a solidus for verse-line breaks, to keep the two distinguishable. The vertical line is also used
in the syntax of some computing languages and scripts.

### §4.14 — Quotation marks

See the Vocabulary table's two quotation-mark-order entries for the core British/American contrast.
Additional detail: upright (straight) quotation marks are also sometimes used instead of the
typographic curly form; single marks are considered easier to read on-screen than double ones, which
is relevant for web writing specifically. Displayed quotations of poetry/prose take no quotation marks
at all. For extended passage of reported speech spanning multiple paragraphs, an opening quotation
mark begins each new paragraph, but a closing mark appears only at the very end of the last paragraph.
Quotation marks (roman type, not italic) are used for titles of short poems, short stories, songs, and
for chapter/article titles — but are dropped when a paper's subject is merely paraphrased rather than
quoted as a title (*Mr Brock read a paper on description in poetry*, no quotes). Quotation marks may
also enclose an unfamiliar/newly coined word, a technical-sense use, or — commonly — mark a deliberate
distancing from a colloquial/vulgar expression or a claim the writer doesn't personally endorse (e.g.
scare-quoting *'voluntary'* transfers of population); such a distancing use should be reserved for a
term's first occurrence in a work, and quotation marks should never be used purely for emphasis.
Quotation marks are not used around the names of houses or public buildings. Cross-reference: Chapter
9 for quotations and direct speech generally, including relative placement of quotation marks with
other punctuation (§9.2.3).

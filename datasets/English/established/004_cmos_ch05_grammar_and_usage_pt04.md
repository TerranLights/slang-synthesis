## Grammar points

### 5.68 Articles defined
An article is a limiting adjective preceding a noun or noun phrase, marking it definite (*the*) or
indefinite (*a*/*an*). It can stand alone or combine with other adjectives, e.g. *a road*, *the
yellow brick road*.

### 5.69 Definite article
*The* points to a definite object under any of three conditions: (1) the referent is already so
well understood it needs no further description (*the package is here*, short for *the package that
you expected is here*); (2) the referent is about to be described (*the sights of Chicago*); or (3)
the referent is simply important (*the grand prize*). It attaches to both singular (*the star*) and
plural (*the stars*) nouns.

### 5.70 Indefinite article
*A*/*an* points to a nonspecific member of a class — singular (*a student at Princeton*),
uncountable (*a multitude*), or generalized (*an idea inspired by Milton's* Paradise Lost).

### 5.71 Indefinite article in specific reference
In a handful of usages the roles invert: the indefinite article gives a specific reference (*I saw
a great movie last night*) while the definite article gives a generic one, generalizing by category
— e.g. *the Scots are talking about independence* generalizes over an entire nationality.

### 5.72 Choosing "a" or "an"
The choice turns on the sound, not the spelling, of the following word. *A* precedes a consonant
sound — including /y/, /h/, and /w/ regardless of spelling (*a eulogy*, *a historic occasion*, *a
Ouachita tribe member*). *An* precedes a vowel sound, again regardless of spelling (*an LSAT exam
room*, *an X-Files episode*, *an hour ago*). Cross-referenced to §5.220 (under *a; an*) and
§§7.44, 10.9.

### 5.73 Articles with coordinate nouns
With a series of coordinate nouns, an article may precede each noun but isn't required (*the rose
bush and hedge need trimming*). If the named things form a single idea, the article is not repeated
(*in the highest degree of dressage, the horse and rider appear to be one entity*); likewise if a
single plural noun covers everything named, the definite article is not repeated (*in the first and
second years of college*).

### 5.74 Effect of article on meaning
Because articles carry demonstrative force, their presence or absence can shift a sentence's
meaning. *An officer and gentleman escorted Princess Plum to her car* ambiguously suggests one
person with two descriptive traits, while *an officer and a friend escorted...* clarifies two
escorts. Similarly *do you like the red and blue cloth?* suggests one bicolor fabric, while *do you
like the red and the blue cloth?* suggests two separate fabrics. The clearest fix for the
one-fabric sense is hyphenating as a compound modifier (*red-and-blue cloth*); for two separate
fabrics, clarity requires either repeating *cloth* (*the red cloth and the blue cloth*) or placing
*cloth* after the first adjective rather than the second (*the red cloth and the blue*).

### 5.75 Zero article
Some usages call for an article that is implicitly present but not written — the "zero article" —
usually before a mass or plural noun (*although both new and washed bottles are stacked nearby,
cider is poured into new bottles only*, where *the* is understood before *new bottles*). The zero
article is especially common in idiomatic references to time, illness, transportation, personal
routines, and meals: *by sunset*, *has cancer*, *travel by train*, *go to bed*, *make breakfast*.

### 5.76 Omitted article
Omitting an article can alter a sentence's meaning outright. *The news brought us little comfort*
(meaning we weren't comforted) changes sense entirely if *a* is inserted before *little*: *the news
brought us a little comfort* (meaning we felt somewhat comforted).

### 5.77 Article as pronoun substitute
An article can sometimes stand in for a pronoun. In *a patient who develops the described rash on
___ hands should inform ___ doctor*, the blanks can be filled equally well with the pronoun phrase
*his or her* or with the article *the*.

### 5.78 Basic rules (adjective position)
An adjective modifying a noun, noun phrase, or pronoun usually precedes it (*perfect storm*,
*spectacular view*, *a good bowl of soup*). It may follow the noun when: (1) special emphasis is
needed (*reasons innumerable*, *captains courageous*); (2) the postposed position is itself standard
usage for that term (*court-martial*, *notary public*); (3) it functions as a predicate adjective
after a linking verb (*I am ready*); or (4) the pronoun it modifies is of a type conventionally
followed by its adjective (*anything good*, *everything yellow*, *nothing important*, *something
wicked*). Some adjectives are always predicate-only and never precede what they modify (*the city is
asleep*, *the door was ajar*); others appear uniformly before the noun (*utter nonsense*, *a mere
child*). Phrasal adjectives may precede or follow what they modify (see §5.91).

### 5.79 After possessive pronoun
When a noun phrase includes a possessive noun — *children's shoes*, *the company's president* — the
adjective follows the possessive: *children's athletic shoes*, *the company's former president*.
Exception: when the adjective actually modifies the possessor noun itself rather than the head noun
(e.g. describing athletic *children* or a former *company*).

### 5.80 Adjective modifying pronoun
When an adjective modifies a pronoun, it usually follows the pronoun (*the searchers found him
unconscious*, *some like it hot*), sometimes functioning as a predicate adjective (*it was
insensitive*, *who was so jealous?*).

### 5.81 Predicate adjective
A predicate adjective follows a linking verb (see §5.99) but modifies the *subject*, not a word
inside the predicate — *the child is afraid*, *the night became colder*, *this tastes delicious*,
*I feel bad*. The distinction matters: in *the train will be late*, *late* is a predicate adjective
modifying *train*. But in *the train will be here at a late hour*, *late* modifies *hour* (a noun
inside the predicate), not the subject *train* — so despite its predicate position, it is not by
definition a predicate adjective, which must follow a linking verb and modify the subject.

### 5.82 Dates as adjectives
Dates function as descriptive adjectives, more commonly today than in the past. A month-year or
month-day date used adjectivally needs no hyphen or comma (*October 31 festivities*, *December 2003
financial statement*). A full month-day-year date requires a comma both before and after the year
when used adjectivally (*the May 18, 2002, commencement ceremonies*) — an awkward construction
(one forward-looking hyphen-like adjective bracketed by two backward-looking commas) best avoided by
recasting, e.g. *commencement ceremonies on May 18, 2002*.

### 5.83 Three adjectival degrees
An adjective has three degrees: positive/absolute (*hard*), comparative (*harder*), and superlative
(*hardest*). The positive form simply states a quality with no reference to any other thing
(*a big balloon*, *bad news*).

### 5.84 Comparative adjectives
A comparative adjective expresses a shared quality's relationship between two things, typically
specifying which has more or less of it (*a cheaper ticket*, *a happier ending*). The *-er* suffix
usually marks the comparative for a common adjective of one or two syllables (*light*→*lighter*,
*merry*→*merrier*). A three-or-more-syllable adjective instead takes *more* (*intelligent*→*more
intelligent*, *purposeful*→*more purposeful*). Some two-syllable adjectives take only *-er*
(*lazy*→*lazier*, *narrow*→*narrower*), but most two-syllable adjectives take *more* (*more
hostile*, *more careless*); two-syllable adjectives ending in *-er*, *-le*, *-ow*, or *-y* can
typically take either the *-er* suffix or *more*.

### 5.85 Superlative adjectives
A superlative adjective expresses an extreme degree of a shared quality among at least three things
(*the biggest house on the block*, *the bitterest pill of all*). The *-est* suffix usually marks the
superlative for a common adjective of one or two syllables (*lighter*→*lightest* pattern, i.e.
*light*→*lightest*). A three-or-more-syllable adjective instead takes *most* (*quarrelsome*→*most
quarrelsome*, *humorous*→*most humorous*). Some two-syllable adjectives take only *-est*
(*holy*→*holiest*, *noble*→*noblest*), but most two-syllable adjectives take *most* (*most
fruitful*, *most reckless*).

### 5.86 Forming comparatives and superlatives
Seven sub-rules govern regular short-adjective comparative/superlative formation: (1) a monosyllable
ending in a single vowel plus a single consonant doubles the final consonant before the suffix
(*red*→*redder*→*reddest*); (2) an adjective ending in a silent *-e* drops the *e* before the suffix
(*polite*→*politer*→*politest*); (3) participles used as adjectives require *more*/*most* before the
participle rather than a suffix (*this teleplay is more boring than the first one*, *I am most tired
on Fridays*); (4) a few one-syllable adjectives — *real*, *right*, *wrong* — take only *more*/*most*,
and even then only in informal speech; (5) *eager*, *proper*, and *somber*, unlike most two-syllable
adjectives, take only *more*/*most* (no suffix form); (6) two-syllable adjectives to which the
negative prefix *un-* has been added can usually either take a suffix or take *more*/*most*, even
though the total syllable count is three (*unhappier* or *most unhappy*); (7) many adjectives are
simply irregular, with no governing rule (*good*→*better*→*best*, *less*→*lesser*→*least*) — a good
dictionary should be consulted for an irregular adjective's forms.

### 5.87 Equal and unequal comparisons
A higher degree of comparison is signaled by the *-er*/*-est* suffix or by *more*/*most* (see
§§5.84–85). A lower degree is signaled by *less* (comparative) or *least* (superlative): *cold* →
*less cold* → *least cold*. Equivalence (neither higher nor lower) is signaled by an *as . . . as*
construction (*this is as old as that*), and sometimes by *so* in negative constructions (*that test
was not so hard as the last one*).

### 5.88 Uncomparable adjectives
An adjective whose own definition already names an absolute state or condition — *entire*,
*impossible*, *pregnant*, *unique* — is called uncomparable: it cannot take a comparative suffix,
cannot combine with the comparative terms *more*, *most*, *less*, or *least*, and cannot be
intensified with words like *very*, *largely*, or *quite*. On rare occasions calling for particular
emphasis, a skilled writer may deliberately break this rule — Garner cites the framers of the U.S.
Constitution using *more perfect* in the preamble (*We the People of the United States, in order to
form a more perfect Union . . .*) as the canonical sanctioned exception.

### 5.89 Participial adjectives
A participial adjective is simply a verb's participle — present (*-ing*: *the dining room*, *a
walking stick*, *a rising star*) or past (usually *-ed*: *an endangered species*, *a completed
assignment*, *a proven need*) — used to modify a noun or pronoun. Some past-participial forms have
only this adjectival function, having taken on a form distinct from the finite verb (*a shaven
face*, *a graven image*). A past-participle-as-adjective may be modified by an adverb like *quite*
(*quite surprised*), *barely* (*barely concealed*), or *little* (*little known*), or by an adverbial
phrase like *very much* (*very much distrusted*). If the participle has become strongly adjectival,
*very* alone suffices without the quantifier *much* (*very tired*, *very drunk*); if it still reads
more like a verb, *very* needs *much* to do the job (*very much appreciated*, *very much delayed*).
A handful of past participles — *bored*, *interested*, *pleased*, *satisfied* — sit in the middle of
this spectrum between adjectival and verbal; for these, the quantifier *much* is normally dropped.
Cross-referenced to §§5.108, 5.139.

### 5.90 Coordinate adjectives
A coordinate adjective is one of a sequence of related adjectives independently modifying the same
noun; coordinate adjectives should be separated by commas or by *and* (*skilled, experienced chess
player*; *nurturing and loving parent*). But when one adjective modifies the noun and a second
modifies the *idea* formed by combining the first adjective with the noun, the two are not
coordinate and should not be comma-separated — e.g. *a lethargic soccer player* describes a soccer
player who is lethargic (not two independently applicable traits); likewise *red brick house* and
*wrinkled canvas jacket* are unpunctuated because the adjectives lack logical independence (a red
house could be built of many materials; so could a wrinkled jacket). The most useful test: if *and*
would fit naturally between the two adjectives, a comma is necessary.

### 5.91 Phrasal adjectives
A phrasal adjective (also "compound modifier") is a multi-word phrase functioning as a single unit
to modify a noun, governed by several sub-rules: (1) generally, if placed before the noun, hyphenate
to avoid misdirecting the reader (*dog-eat-dog competition*) — compare *small animal hospital* with
the hyphenated *small-animal hospital*, which can carry a materially different meaning; (2) if a
compound noun is itself an element of the phrasal adjective, the whole compound must be hyphenated
to clarify the relationship among the words (*time-clock-punching employees*); (3) hyphenation
becomes especially important when more than one phrasal adjective modifies a single noun
(*nineteenth-century song-and-dance numbers*, *state-inspected assisted-living facility*); (4) when
two phrasal adjectives share a common ending element, the ending element should appear only with the
second phrase, with a suspended hyphen marking the dropped-but-implied element on the first
(*middle- and upper-class operagoers*) — but if the two phrasal adjectives instead *begin* with a
common element, a hyphen there is usually inappropriate, and the shared beginning element should be
repeated in full (*left-handed and left-brained executives*); (5) if the phrasal adjective denotes an
amount or duration, plurals should generally be dropped (*pregnancy lasts nine months* but *a
nine-month pregnancy*; *a shop open twenty-four hours a day* but *a twenty-four-hour-a-day
schedule*) — the plural is retained only for fractional phrases (*a two-thirds majority*); (6) if a
phrasal adjective becomes unwieldy, the sentence is usually better recast — Garner's example *the
news about the lower-than-expected third-quarter earnings disappointed investors* recasts more
cleanly as *the news about the third-quarter earnings, which were lower than expected, disappointed
investors*, or *investors were disappointed by the third-quarter earnings, which were lower than
expected*. Three recognized exceptions to hyphenating phrasal adjectives: (1) when a phrasal
adjective follows a verb, it is usually left unhyphenated — compare the pre-noun *a well-trained
athlete* with the post-verb *an athlete who is well trained*; (2) when a proper name begins a
phrasal adjective, the name itself is not hyphenated (*the Monty Python school of comedy*); (3) a
two-word phrasal adjective beginning with an adverb ending in *-ly* is not hyphenated (*a sharply
worded reprimand*, but contrast the longer, still-hyphenated *a not-so-sharply-worded reprimand*).
For the full treatment of hyphenation — including compound nouns and other parts of speech — CMOS
cross-references §§7.77–85.

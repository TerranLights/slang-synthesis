## Grammar points

### 6.79 — En dash with an unfinished number range

An en dash can mark a number range still in progress — e.g. the dates of a serial publication that
hasn't concluded, or a living person's birth year — with no space on either side of the dash. Example
pattern: *Jack Stag (1950–)*.

### 6.80 — En dashes with compound adjectives

An en dash can substitute for a hyphen in a compound adjective when one element is itself an open
compound, or when both elements are already hyphenated compounds (e.g. *the post–World War II
years*, *country music–influenced lyrics*). CMOS calls this an "editorial nicety" that many readers
won't consciously notice, to be used sparingly and mainly where capitalization makes the limits of a
proper-noun compound clear. A single word or prefix joined to a hyphenated compound should instead
take a plain hyphen, or the phrase should be reworded if an en dash would look awkward (e.g. prefer
"a cup that is two-thirds full" over "a two-thirds–full cup"). An abbreviated compound like "US" is
treated as a single word, so it takes a hyphen rather than an en dash ("US-Canadian relations") — and
Chicago's sense of the en dash does not extend to meaning "between."

### 6.81 — Other uses for the en dash

The en dash is sometimes pressed into service as a minus sign, though Unicode defines the minus sign
(U+2212) and the en dash (U+2013) as distinct characters with potentially different spacing — CMOS
advises using the technically correct character, especially in mathematical copy, since substituting
one for the other can break searches in electronic text. In some scientific disciplines an en dash
may stand in for a hyphen where one would normally be expected. Universities with multiple campuses
sometimes use an en dash to join the campus location to the university name (e.g. *the University of
Wisconsin–Madison*).

### 6.82 — Em dashes instead of commas, parentheses, or colons

The em dash — simply "the dash" in common usage — is the most frequently used and most versatile
dash. It sets off an amplifying or explanatory element and can function as an alternative to
parentheses, to commas, or to a colon, particularly where an abrupt break in thought is wanted. CMOS
cautions against letting an em dash appear within or immediately following another em-dash-set-off
element (or a pair of them); use parentheses or commas instead to avoid confusion.

### 6.83 — Em dash between noun and pronoun

An em dash can occasionally set off an introductory noun, or a series of nouns, from the pronoun that
goes on to introduce the main clause — a list-then-summary construction (e.g. *Broken promises, petty
rivalries, and false rumors — such were the obstacles he encountered*).

### 6.84 — Em dashes to indicate sudden breaks

A single em dash, or a pair, can mark a sudden break in thought or sentence structure, or an
interruption in dialogue (where a *faltering* rather than sudden break is meant, suspension points are
used instead — see §13.39). If the break belongs to the surrounding sentence rather than to the
quoted material itself, the em dash must sit outside the closing quotation marks.

### 6.85 — Em dashes with "that is," "namely," "for example," and similar expressions

An em dash may precede expressions such as *that is* or *namely* (cross-referenced to §6.43 and
§6.56 for fuller treatment and examples).

### 6.86 — Em dashes in place of commas

In modern usage, when an em dash takes the place of a comma that would otherwise separate a
dependent clause from an independent one, the comma is simply dropped. Likewise, when an em dash
ends quoted material to show an interruption, the comma that would normally identify the speaker
(e.g. before "she said") can be safely omitted.

### 6.87 — Em dashes with other punctuation

A question mark or an exclamation point may precede an em dash, and — rarely — a period may as well
(cross-referenced to §14.46), but a comma, a colon, or a semicolon never precedes an em dash in
modern usage.

### 6.88 — Em dashes in lieu of quotation marks

Following the convention of some European-language writers, em dashes are occasionally used instead
of quotation marks to set off dialogue, with each new speech starting its own paragraph and no space
following the dash.

### 6.89 — Em dashes in indexes

Cross-reference only: for the use of em dashes within an index, CMOS points the reader to §16.27
(index formatting is outside this chunk's assigned page range).

### 6.90 — 2-em dash

A 2-em dash (twice the length of a standard em dash) stands in for a missing word or part of a word —
either deliberately, to disguise a name or an expletive, or because the original is missing/illegible
in quoted or reprinted material. A fully missing word gets a space on both sides of the dash; a
partially missing word (where the dash represents only the end of the word) has no space between the
dash and the surviving letters, unless other punctuation immediately follows. CMOS is careful to
distinguish a 2-em dash (representing material that could in principle be supplied) from a blank line
meant to be filled in on a form, which should be rendered as an underscore instead.

### 6.91 — 3-em dash

In a bibliography, a 3-em dash followed by a period stands in for the same author or editor named in
the immediately preceding entry, avoiding repetition of the name across consecutive works by the same
person (cross-referenced to §14.63–67 and §15.17–19 for bibliography/reference-list formatting more
broadly).

### 6.92 — Use of parentheses

Parentheses are stronger than a comma and, like dashes, set material off from the surrounding text —
but unlike commas, parentheses can enclose material that has no grammatical relationship to the rest
of the sentence (an aside, a cross-reference, a translated title). CMOS cross-references several more
specific parenthetical uses elsewhere in the manual: parenthetical references to a works-cited list
(§15.20–30), parenthetical references following quoted material (§13.62), parentheses in notes and
bibliographies (chapter 14), parentheses in mathematics (chapter 12, especially §12.26–35), and roman
vs. italic type within parentheses (§6.5).

### 6.93 — Parentheses for glosses or translations

Parentheses enclose a gloss of an unfamiliar term or a translation of a foreign term; if the term
itself is given in English, parentheses instead enclose the original-language word. In quoted matter,
square brackets should be used for this purpose instead (cross-referenced to §6.97; see also §7.50
and §11.6). Illustrative pattern: giving a foreign numeral term in parentheses after its English
gloss, or giving a German verb in parentheses after its English translation to make an eating-verb
register distinction (humans vs. animals) explicit.

### 6.94 — Parentheses to enclose numbers or letters

Parentheses enclose numerals or letters that mark divisions within a list run into the surrounding
text (cross-referenced to §6.123), or that mark certain sublevels within a formal outline
(cross-referenced to §6.126).

### 6.95 — Parentheses within parentheses

Although parentheses nested inside other parentheses are permitted in some kinds of publication —
especially legal writing — Chicago's own preference is to use square brackets for the inner,
nested pair (cross-referenced to §6.99). CMOS notes that British style, by contrast, nests
parentheses directly within parentheses. For parentheses in mathematical notation specifically, see
§12.26.

### 6.96 — Parentheses with other punctuation

An opening parenthesis should be preceded by a comma or semicolon only within an enumerated list
(cross-referenced to §6.123); a closing parenthesis is never preceded by a comma, semicolon, or
colon. A question mark, exclamation point, or closing quotation marks precede a closing parenthesis
only if they belong to the parenthetical material itself; otherwise they follow it. A period goes
inside the closing parenthesis only if the entire sentence is enclosed in parentheses (and CMOS
advises against nesting more than one full sentence inside another sentence this way — see §6.13).
Two parenthetical elements enclosing unrelated material may appear back-to-back, with a space between
them, or unrelated material can share one set of parentheses separated by a semicolon (CMOS
cross-references its own table 6.1 for a fuller summary, and chapters 14–15 for parentheses used in
documentation).

### 6.97 — Use of square brackets

Square brackets (called simply "brackets" in US usage) are used mainly in scholarly prose to enclose
material added by someone other than the original writer and not part of the surrounding text —
editorial interpolations, explanations, translations of foreign terms, or corrections — in quoted
matter, reprints, anthologies, and similar nonoriginal material. Bracketed material specifically
*replaces* rather than merely amplifies the original word or words (cross-referenced to §12.26 for
brackets in mathematical copy, and §13.57–61 elsewhere). If quoted material already contains brackets
of its own, the editor should flag that explicitly in the source citation (e.g. "brackets in the
original") — cross-referenced to §13.60 for the parallel situation with italics.

### 6.98 — Square brackets in translated text

In a translated work, square brackets can enclose a word or phrase from the original language to
avoid ambiguity, though CMOS advises translators to use this device sparingly.

### 6.99 — Square brackets for parentheses within parentheses

Chicago's preference, as already flagged in §6.95, is to use square brackets as the "inner"
parentheses when parentheses must be nested — usually for bibliographic purposes (cross-referenced to
§12.26 for mathematical groupings, which follow different nesting conventions).

### 6.101 — Square brackets with other punctuation

Most of the same rules governing parentheses with other punctuation (§6.96) apply to square brackets
too. For brackets specifically used to enclose editorial interpolations, the placement of surrounding
punctuation relative to the brackets depends on the source text itself — CMOS illustrates this with an
example of reconstructing an incomplete handwritten letter, where bracketed editorial insertions sit
alongside ellipsis points marking illegible material.

### 6.102 — Angle brackets and braces

CMOS uses "angle brackets" here specifically for the ASCII "less than"/"greater than" symbols (< and
>) used as a delimiter pair — distinct from true mathematical angle brackets (⟨ ⟩), which aren't
readily available on standard keyboards and are reserved for math notation. Angle brackets most
commonly enclose tags in markup languages such as XML; some manuscript editors extend this to mark
generic typesetting instructions in a word-processed manuscript, though CMOS discourages using angle
brackets to set off URLs or email addresses in ordinary prose (even though some email applications do
this in message headers). Angle brackets are also sometimes used in textual studies to mark missing
or illegible material (cross-referenced to §6.97's own bracket-based approach to the same
problem). Braces — also called curly brackets, "{ }" — are yet another delimiter option, used in
various programming languages and in mathematical/specialized writing (cross-referenced to §12.28);
they are not interchangeable with parentheses or square brackets.

### 6.103 — Other names for the slash

The slash (/) goes by several other names — virgule, solidus, slant, or forward slash (this last term
distinguishing it from the backward slash, or backslash, \). CMOS points readers wanting a fuller
discussion of the terminology to Richard Eckersley et al.'s *Glossary of Typesetting Terms*.

### 6.104 — Slashes to signify alternatives

A slash most commonly signals alternatives, functioning as an informal, convenient shorthand for "or"
(e.g. *he/she*, *and/or*), and is also used for alternative spellings or names of the same person
(e.g. *Hercules/Heracles*, *Margaret/Meg/Maggie*). When one or more of the slash-separated terms is
itself an open compound, a space before and after the slash helps readability (e.g. *World War I /
First World War*). Occasionally a slash signifies "and" instead, though it still usually carries some
sense of alternatives (e.g. *an insertion/deletion mutation*, *an MD/PhD program*, *a Jekyll/Hyde
personality*).

### 6.105 — Slashes with two-year spans

A slash is sometimes used in dates as an alternative to an en dash (cross-referenced to §6.78), or
even combined with an en dash, to indicate the final part of one year running into the start of the
next (see also §9.63). Illustrative patterns: *the winter of 1966/67*; *between 1998/99 and 2001/2*;
*the fiscal years 2005/6–2009/10*.

## Grammar points

### Chapter I — Parts of Speech: the ten-way division and period word-counts

Brown's ten parts of speech, each given a one-line definition: **Article** (*the, an, a*, limiting a noun's
signification), **Noun** (name of any person/place/thing), **Adjective** (word added to a noun/pronoun,
generally expressing quality), **Pronoun** (word used instead of a noun), **Verb** (signifies *to be, to act*, or
*to be acted upon*), **Participle** (derived from a verb, sharing properties of verb and adjective/noun, formed
by *-ing/-d/-ed*), **Adverb** (added to a verb/participle/adjective/other adverb, expressing time, place,
degree, or manner), **Conjunction** (connects words/sentences and shows their dependence),
**Preposition** (expresses a relation of things/thoughts, generally placed before a noun/pronoun), and
**Interjection** (uttered to indicate strong or sudden emotion).

Brown reports contemporary (1851) estimates of each class's size in English — a genuinely useful
period-accurate lexical-statistics snapshot: **nouns**, 25,000–30,000; **adjectives**, 9,000–10,000;
**pronouns**, 24 base words with 32 inflected variants (56 total word-forms); **verbs**, "recent grammarians"
~8,000, vs. an older estimate of 4,300; **participles**, roughly double the number of simple verbs, counting
only the simple (non-compound) forms; **adverbs**, ~2,600, of which 4/5 end in *-ly*; **conjunctions**, 29 in
common use (plus a few now obsolete) — Brown gives the full list: *and, as, both, because, even, for, if,
that, then, since, seeing, so; or, nor, either, neither, than, though, although, yet, but, except, whether, lest,
unless, save, provided, notwithstanding, whereas*; **prepositions**, ~60 in common use; **interjections**,
60–70 in common use.

Brown gives a fully parsed worked example (Lowth's famous sentence: *"The power of speech is a faculty
peculiar to man..."*) tagging every word by part-of-speech number, as a worked pedagogical model.

### Chapter II — Articles

*The* is **definite** (denotes some particular thing); *an*/*a* is **indefinite** (denotes one thing of a kind, not
a particular one) — treated by Brown as one and the same word, with *an* shortened to *a* before a
consonant *sound* (not letter): "the suppression of the sound of *h* being with Englishmen a very common
fault... *How often do we hear an air metamorphosed into a hair, a hat into a gnat, and a hero into a
Nero!*" (Churchill, quoted). The English articles have essentially no modifications beyond this one allomorphic
alternation — unlike the Greek article, which Brown notes is fully declined by number/gender/case into "more
than twenty different forms."

**Key semantic/usage distinctions Brown develops at length:**
- *An*/*a* denotes one out of a class ("any one of that name"); applied to a proper name it generalizes it into a
  class-representative ("a Nero" = any cruel tyrant); applied to a common noun it individuates ("a man" = any
  one man).
- *The* is demonstrative, marking a particular, previously-known, or contextually-specified referent; plural
  proper names of nations, tribes, sects, and mountain ranges (*the Romans, the Stoics, the Alps*) regularly
  *require* the article, unlike most proper names.
- Brown rejects Murray's rule that articles "agree" with their nouns (English articles have no grammatical
  modifications to agree *with* — he calls this a symptom of grammarians blindly copying Latin-grammar
  terminology onto English).
- A long historical digression (Obs. 16–18) traces *a*/*an* to Scots/older English *ae*/*ane*, with Gawin
  Douglas (d. 1522) cited as writing *ane* for both forms — a genuine dialectal/diachronic data point.
- *A* as a distinct, non-numeral **preposition** (≈ French *à*, "on/at/in/of"), now mostly fossilized as a prefix:
  *abed* (in bed), *aboard* (on board), *afire* (on fire), *ajar*, *ahead*, *anew* — and, as a separate word, in
  constructions like *a-hunting, a-fishing, a-begging* (now archaic/dialectal: "they burst out a laughing"),
  and in the KJV's *"his disciples were an hungred"* (Matt. 12:1), which Brown analyzes as a mistakenly
  re-divided prefix rather than the article.
- *A* in distributive/rate constructions (*a dollar a day*, "twice a year") is analyzed by Brown as the indefinite
  article used in the sense of *each*/*every*, governing an implied preposition — not the homophonous
  prepositional *a*.
- A closing **taxonomy of English definitives** beyond the three true articles (Obs. 30–31): *this, that, these,
  those, any, other, some, all, both, each, every, either, neither* — Brown argues these are properly
  *adjectives*, not pronouns (contra "Hermes" / Harris, who called them "more properly articles than any thing
  else"), worked through a full paradigm (*a man / the man / the men / any man / a certain man / this man /
  that man / these men / those men / such a man / many a man / a thousand men / every man / each man /
  some man / all men / no men / no man*) illustrating each definitive's distinct quantificational force.

### Chapter III — Nouns

**Definition:** "the name of any person, place, or thing, that can be known or mentioned." Brown notes (Obs.
1–2) that almost any part of speech can be used *as* a noun by shifting its syntax — he catalogs real examples
of adjectives (*the ancients, the sublime*), pronouns (*his she, their whos and whiches*), verbs (*a permit, a
may-be*), participles (*a coming-in*), adverbs (*the why, a hereafter*), conjunctions (*your if*), prepositions
(*beyond beyond*), and even interjections (*lo-heres, a loud "oh"*) all pressed into nominal use — a genuine
structural-flexibility point about English word-class permeability.

**Classes:** **proper** (name of a particular individual/people/group) vs. **common** (name of a sort/kind/
class); common further includes **collective**/noun-of-multitude (*council, flock*), **abstract** (*goodness,
pride*), **verbal/participial** (*the triumphing of the wicked*), and **sui generis** — "of its own peculiar
kind," a mass/unique referent with no true plural (*galvanism, music, geometry*). A proper noun can acquire
common-noun force through an article ("He is *the* Cicero of his age" = the great orator), and a common
noun can become proper through the definite article (*the Park, the Strand, the United States*) or through
personification (*Peace* as a feminine proper-noun-by-figure).

**Four modifications — Persons, Numbers, Genders, Cases:**

- **Persons** (first/speaker, second/hearer, third/spoken-of). Brown devotes extended, genuinely sharp
  polemic (Obs. 3–4) to a definitional confusion he finds endemic across contemporary textbooks — defining
  "the first person" as literally "the person who speaks" or "a noun that speaks," which collapses the
  grammatical category (a form/relation) with the real-world referent (an intelligent being). His own
  resolution: grammatical persons are "mere forms, relations, or modifications of words... things of the
  neuter gender, and not living souls." He also defends (against the Murray-derived mainstream) that *nouns*
  genuinely can be first person, not only pronouns — citing biblical self-reference constructions like "I Paul
  have written it" (Philemon 19) and Latin parallels (*Themistocles ad te veni*).

- **Numbers** (singular/plural). The regular plural adds *-s*/*-es*; Brown gives the governing phonological
  rule (12 terminations that always add a syllable: *ce, ge, ch, che, sh, ss, s, se, x, xe, z, ze*) and documents
  irregular plurals in detail: 11 *-f → -ves* words (*sheaf/sheaves, leaf/leaves, loaf/loaves, thief/thieves,
  calf/calves, half/halves, elf/elves, shelf/shelves, self/selves, wolf/wolves*, plus archaic *beef/beeves*) and 3
  more in *-fe* (*life/lives, knife/knives, wife/wives*) — contrasted explicitly with the *larger* set of *-f* words
  that stay regular (*chiefs, griefs, gulfs, roofs, turfs*); and the 13 native-English irregular plurals not ending
  in *-s* at all: *man/men, woman/women, child/children, brother/brethren or brothers, ox/oxen,
  goose/geese, foot/feet, tooth/teeth, louse/lice, mouse/mice, die/dice or dies, penny/pence or pennies,
  pea/pease or peas* — with usage notes on each doublet (*dice* = gaming cubes vs. *dies* = coin-stamps;
  *pence* = sum of money vs. *pennies* = the coins themselves). A long catalogue of English's borrowed
  Latin/Greek plural patterns follows (Obs. 39): *-a → -ae* (*nebula/nebulae*), *-um → -a* (*datum/data,
  erratum/errata, effluvium/effluvia*), *-us → -i* (*alumnus/alumni, calculus/calculi*), *-is → -es*
  (*axis/axes, basis/bases, crisis/crises, analysis/analyses, parenthesis/parentheses, hypothesis/hypotheses*),
  *-x → -ces* (*index/indices, vortex/vortices, appendix/appendices*), and doublets like *cherub(s) or
  cherubim, seraph(s) or seraphim, beau(s) or beaux, bandit(s) or banditti* — Brown generally prefers the
  regular English form where both are current. Also noted: mass/uncountable singular-only nouns (*cider,
  flax, gold, pride*); nouns of multitude's dual singular/plural construal depending on collective-unity vs.
  distributive-plurality sense ("The jury *were* unable to agree" vs. "The committee *is* in consultation"); and
  a run of plural-only or ambiguous-number nouns: *cattle, pulse* (plural in force despite singular appearance)
  vs. *summons, gallows, series, molasses, corps* (singular in force despite plural appearance) — plus the
  genuinely still-disputed *means* (Brown surveys Johnson, Lowth, Buchanan, Kames, Cobbett on whether
  "mean" or "means" is the correct singular, concluding usage has settled on invariant *means* against
  etymological "principle").

- **Genders** (masculine/feminine/neuter — "founded on the natural distinction of sex... and on the absence
  of sex"). Brown insists on exactly three genders and no more, specifically rejecting both "common gender"
  (Murray) and the four/five-gender schemes some contemporaries proposed (Obs. 2–4) as confusing the
  grammatical category with the real-world property of sex. Four ways English marks sex distinction (Obs.
  10): different terminations (*Jew/Jewess*), wholly different words (*king/queen*), compounds/phrases
  (*he-goat/she-goat, man-servant/maid-servant*), and pronoun reference alone. A long catalogue of the *-ess*
  feminine-forming pattern follows, in three productivity tiers: simple addition (*baron/baroness,
  giant/giantess, heir/heiress*), stem-change with no syllable increase (*actor/actress, emperor/empress,
  governor/governess, tiger/tigress*), and the rarer Latinate *-or → -rix* pattern (*administrator/
  administratrix, executor/executrix, testator/testatrix*). Figurative gender is treated as a genuinely separate
  phenomenon from grammatical gender: inanimate objects "remarkable for power, greatness, or sublimity"
  (*sun, time, death, war*) are conventionally spoken of as masculine, those "beautiful, amiable, or prolific"
  (*ship, moon, earth, nature, hope*) as feminine — Brown notes this assignment is real but not fully
  systematic, and flags Cowper's and Addison's personifications of *Knowledge*/*Wisdom* as masculine
  against what he takes to be the more usual feminine convention.

- **Cases** (nominative/possessive/objective). Brown devotes extensive space (Obs. 3–9) to the genuinely
  unsettled 18th–19th-century dispute over *how many* cases English nouns have — he surveys grammarians
  proposing anywhere from zero (Cardell, Tooke) to one (Buchanan: possessive only), two (Lowth, Johnson,
  Priestley, Murray's earlier editions, Webster at various points), three (the eventual consensus, which Brown
  endorses and which Murray and Webster both migrated to in later editions), four (several contemporaries,
  variously "nominative/possessive/objective/independent" or "/absolute" or "/vocative"), five (S. R. Hall,
  O. B. Peirce), up to the full Latin six (several older grammars) — a genuinely striking real-time snapshot of
  an actively-converging grammatical-descriptive consensus within Brown's own lifetime. He also documents
  the dispute over **apostrophe usage** for the possessive (Obs. 12–20): the now-standard *'s* for singular and
  bare apostrophe for plural (*boy's* / *boys'*) was itself contested by several contemporaries (Ash, Lowth,
  Buchanan) who preferred unmarked *-s*/*-es* throughout; Brown traces the apostrophe to a genuine historical
  confusion — many 18th-century writers (including Addison, per Spectator #135) believed *'s* was a
  contraction of *his* (hence spellings like "Ulysses *his* bow"), which Brown disproves by pointing out the
  possessive *'s* applies equally to feminine and plural nouns, where *his* cannot be intended. He also notes
  the possessive-plural apostrophe convention (*boys'*) is itself a comparatively late typographic innovation,
  not attested in Buchanan's mid-18th-century grammar.

### Chapter IV — Adjectives

**Definition:** "a word added to a noun or pronoun, and generally expresses quality." Brown notes English
adjectives are **entirely indeclinable** — a word that takes plural or possessive inflection (even if normally
an adjective) is, in that use, to be parsed as a noun (*the rich, a few of the learners*).

**Six classes:** **common** (ordinary quality/situation epithets: *good, eastern, outer*), **proper** (from a
proper name: *American, Platonic, Genoese*), **numeral** (*one, two, three...*), **pronominal** (a definitive
that can stand in for its noun: *all, each*), **participial** (participle-form but no notion of time: "an amusing
story"), and **compound** (*nut-brown, four-footed, lovesick*).

**The 46-word pronominal-adjective list** (Ch. IV, Obs. 7) — Brown's deliberately narrow, closed inventory,
offered against contemporaries' looser "adjective pronoun" terminology: *all, any, both, certain, divers,
each, either, else, enough, every, few, fewer, fewest, former, first, latter, last, little, less, least, many,
more, most, much, neither, no* or *none, one, other, own, only, same, several, some, such, sundry, that,
this, these, those, what, whatever, whatsoever, which, whichever, whichsoever*. Of these, 7 are always
singular (*each, either, every, neither, one, that, this*), ~10 always plural (*both, divers, few, fewer, fewest,
many, several, sundry, these, those*), and the rest number-neutral. About a third of the list (*all, any, else,
enough, first, last, little, less, least, much, more, most, no/none, only, what*) can also function adverbially,
illustrated by sense-shift (*all* "totally," *enough* "sufficiently," *only* "merely").

**Comparison (three degrees — positive/comparative/superlative):**
- **Regular comparison** by *-er*/*-est*, generally restricted to monosyllables and short dissyllables
  (*fierce/fiercer/fiercest, narrow/narrower/narrowest, gloomy/gloomier/gloomiest*).
- **Comparison by adverbs** (*more/most* for superiority, *less/least* for inferiority) for longer words
  (*wise/more wise/most wise*) — Brown notes English "is particularly averse to the lengthening of long
  words by additional syllables," which is why the periphrastic form dominates for anything beyond short
  adjectives.
- **Irregular comparison**: the core closed set *good/better/best; bad, evil, or ill/worse/worst;
  little/less/least; much/more/most; many/more/most*.
- A detailed sub-system of **place/situation adjectives with defective or redundant comparison** (Ch. IV,
  Obs. 1 under Irregular Comparison) — genuinely useful as a structured list: 9 words with *two* superlative
  forms (*far → farther, farthest or farmost/farthermost*; similarly *near, fore, hind, in, out, up, low, late*);
  5 words that lack a positive degree entirely (*after, further, hither, nether, under* — i.e. no bare
  "*aft/furth/hithe/nethe/unde*"); and 16 words that lack a comparative degree, going straight from an implied
  positive to a superlative in *-most* (*front/frontmost, rear/rearmost, head/headmost, top/topmost,
  bottom/bottommost, north/northmost*, etc.).

### Chapter V — Pronouns

**Definition:** "a word used in stead of a noun." Brown restates the period-statistic from Ch. I: 24 base
pronouns, 32 inflectional variants, 56 word-forms total.

**Three classes: personal, relative, interrogative.**
- **Personal** — 5 simple (*I, thou, he, she, it*) and 5 compound/reflexive (*myself, thyself, himself, herself,
  itself*), each tagged for person and (in the third person) gender.
- **Relative** — *who, which, what, that, as*, plus compounds *whoever/whosoever, whichever/whichsoever,
  whatever/whatsoever*. *What* is analyzed as a genuine "double relative," equivalent to *that which* and
  parsed as functioning simultaneously as its own antecedent and as the relative referring to it.
- **Interrogative** — *who, which, what* (same forms as the relatives). Brown distinguishes their force: *who*
  asks for a person's name; *which* asks that a person/thing be distinguished from others in a known set;
  *what* asks for a thing's name or a person's occupation/character.

**Full declension tables given** (useful as a structured reference): the five simple personals each inflected for
nominative/possessive/objective in both numbers (e.g. *I — we; my/mine — our/ours; me — us*); the five
compound (reflexive) personals, which lack a possessive case entirely and are identical in nominative and
objective form; and the relative/interrogative paradigm, where only *who* is case-inflected at all
(*who/whose/whom*) — *which, what, that,* and *as* show no case distinction whatsoever, a genuinely
real synchronic asymmetry within the pronoun system that Brown states plainly without over-regularizing it.

**A documented diachronic usage shift, directly relevant to this project's historical-register interest:** Brown
notes that *which* was formerly applicable to persons and interchangeable with *who* — citing the KJV's
"Our Father *which* art in heaven" as now ungrammatical by 1851 standards ("it should be, 'Our Father
*who* art in heaven'... what was once right, is now ungrammatical") and counting 75 occurrences of
person-referring *which* in Luke chapter 3 alone (the genealogy of Jesus, "Joseph, *which* was the son of
Heli, *which* was the son of Matthat...") as a concentrated example of the now-obsolete older usage. He adds
that the possessive *whose* (literally "of *who*," hence person-restricted) is still, in his own day, sometimes
extended to stand in as the missing possessive of *which* — a construction several contemporaries (Lowth,
Buchanan) condemned as improper outside "grave poetry," while Brown himself judges it acceptable usage
("Philosophy, *whose* end is...") and documents Campbell's explicit defense of it as established good usage.

Brown also argues at length (Obs. 2–8) against the then-mainstream textbook claim that the possessive
pronouns *my, thy, his, her, its, our, your, their* are really **adjectives** rather than pronouns (a position he
attributes to most contemporary grammars) — his counter-evidence is that these words can themselves serve
as the *antecedent* of a following relative pronoun ("How different, O Ortogrul, is thy condition, *who* art
doomed...," where *who* refers back to *thy*), which he argues only a genuine pronoun, not an adjective, can
do.

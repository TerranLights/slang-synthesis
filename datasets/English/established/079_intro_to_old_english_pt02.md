## Grammar points

### The stem/inflection split and morpheme terminology
Every inflected word-form can be split into a **stem** (carries lexical meaning, a **free
morpheme**) and an **inflection** (carries grammatical/morphological/syntactic information, a
**bound morpheme**, since it cannot stand alone). A **zero morpheme** is posited where a form (e.g.
nom./acc. singular *stān*) carries no overt ending at all but still occupies a real paradigm slot.

### Irregular nouns as simplification-in-progress
Several noun subtypes (e.g. *cyme*, *here*) show real variation precisely because they are
historically distinct declensions drifting toward the general masculine/neuter/feminine patterns —
direct textual evidence (from Ælfric's generation onward) of morphological leveling actually
happening within the attested period, not merely reconstructed. The explanation for *word~bān*
losing final *-u* historically (after a **heavy syllable** — long vowel+consonant, or short
vowel+2 consonants) but *talu* keeping it (short/light syllable) is a worked phonological-to-
morphological reanalysis: originally just a regular sound change, the alternation became a genuine
inflectional property once the triggering sound-environment was lost.

### Minor declensions: productive vs. non-productive, and the functional-motivation test
A **minor declension** differs from an "irregular" one in being internally regular and
self-contained but unproductive — new words never join it. The mutation declension (*man~men*,
*fōt~fēt*, etc.) historically arose from **i-mutation**: a following /i/ fronted/raised the stem
vowel, then was itself lost, leaving the vowel alternation as the only marker. Two opposing
historical forces are illustrated: **frequency preserves irregularity** (very common words like
*fōt*, *man* keep their mutation plurals even after the trigger vanishes) while **obscurity
promotes regularization** (rarer mutation nouns like *bōc* shift toward the general declension).
The *wine* case (p. 32) is offered as direct proof that declension-shift is **functionally
motivated**, not random: a word needing a real singular~plural contrast cannot tolerate a
plural-only-type paradigm where both numbers look identical, so it moves to a declension that
supplies the contrast.

### Adjective declension: definite vs. indefinite
Unlike nouns, nearly every OE adjective has **two complete paradigms** — the **definite**
declension (used after a demonstrative, possessive, or possessive noun phrase — i.e. in a
*definite* context) and the **indefinite** declension (used elsewhere, including predicatively
after a copula, as in PDE *John is happy*). The definite declension closely tracks the N-declension
(genitive plural with intervening -r-, no gender distinction in the plural); the indefinite
declension is more complex, with full gender distinctions even in the plural and (in masc./neut.
singular only) a **separate instrumental case**, a case otherwise merged with the dative everywhere
else in the language by this period.

### Three main verb classes plus irregulars
OE verbs divide into **weak** (dental-suffix past tense, like PDE *love~loved*), **strong**
(vowel-alternation past tense, like PDE *sing~sang~sung*), and a handful of true irregulars: the
**modals** (see pt03), **anomalous verbs** (*willan, dōn, gān* — each with genuinely unique,
partly suppletive paradigms), and **bēon/wesan** "to be". Verbs inflect for **person**, **tense**
(present/past only), and **mood** (indicative/imperative/subjunctive), plus **voice**
(morphological passive is almost entirely absent — see *hātte* above and pt04).

### Weak verb conjugations 1, 2, and 3
**Class 2** (*lufian*-type) is the most regular and the most productive both in OE and into PDE.
**Class 1** has two subtypes conditioned by syllable weight: **short-stemmed** verbs geminate
their final stem consonant in some forms (*tryman*), **heavy-stemmed** verbs instead show
**syncope** — loss of the fully unstressed vowel after a heavy syllable — in forms like *dēmst*
(*dēman*). A small, closed set of **"unmutated" (*Rückumlaut*)** class 1 verbs lost the i-mutation-
triggering /i/ before mutation could occur, giving an unexpected vowel alternation in the past
tense that visually resembles a strong verb (*sellan~sealde*) — PDE *sell~sold*, *tell~told* are
direct descendants. **Class 3** survives as only four verbs (*habban, libban, seċġan, hyċġan*),
blending class 1 and class 2 features and showing heavy syncope.

### I-mutation (i-umlaut): the single most pervasive OE sound change
A following /i/ or /j/ in the next syllable fronted a back vowel or raised an already-front vowel
in the preceding syllable (diagrammed: u→y, o→e, ɑ→æ(→e); i and y/e are targets, not sources).
After the change occurred, the triggering /i/ was itself frequently lost or weakened to /e/,
leaving the vowel alternation as the sole surviving trace. This single mechanism underlies the
mutation noun declension (Ch. 3), the unmutated weak verbs (above), several comparative adjective
forms (pt03), and much of OE's irregular-seeming morphology generally — the book frames
understanding i-mutation as foundational to everything that follows.

### Suppletion and the "anomalous" verbs
**Suppletion** is where different forms of one paradigm are drawn from historically unrelated
roots (*gān*'s past tense *ēode* has no phonological relationship to *gān* at all). English's own
later history repeats this pattern: Middle English suppletes *gān*'s past tense a second time,
replacing *ēode* with *went* (originally the past tense of *wendan* "to turn, go"), which in turn
strands *wend* needing a brand-new regular past tense, *wended*.

## Grammar points

### The strong-verb paradigm reaches its final, standardized shape

Extending the strong-verb leveling story already traced from Old through Early Modern English
(`pt03`, `pt06`): by Late Middle English, OE's distinct present/past-singular/past-plural/past-
participle stem system (see `pt03`'s *helpan* paradigm) had collapsed the singular/plural past-tense
distinction in some verbs by generalizing the old singular stem (*I rode, we rode*), in others by
generalizing the old plural stem (*I found, we found*), and in a third group by innovating an
entirely new past tense built from the old past-*participle* stem instead (*I bore, we bore*). **By
1500 the singular/plural past-tense distinction had fully disappeared** (with the sole lingering
exception of *was*/*were*), but with real variation still remaining in exactly which stem won out
per verb; **by the early 18th century a single standardized past-tense form was settled for each
individual strong verb**, broadly matching the Modern Standard English forms, with only minor
remaining differences (illustrated via Addison's 1711 text, itself used as the chapter's period-
specimen passage — see Vocabulary table for *holpen*) — some of the period's non-standardized forms
persisted into the 19th century and survive today specifically in regional/non-standard varieties,
not the standard language.

### The standardization of spelling, and its direct cause of present-day "irregular" spelling

Middle and Early Modern English had **no standard spelling system at all** — the book's concrete
evidence is Shakespeare's own will, which uses two different spellings of his own surname across its
three signatures (*Shakspere*, *Shakspeare*), alongside further period variants (*Shagspere*,
*Shackespere*). The introduction of printing was "a powerful force for standardization," producing
widely-accepted conventions by the mid-16th century, though real variation (especially in private,
non-printed writing) persisted through the end of the Early Modern period. **The crucial, still-
consequential point: the standard spelling system that finally crystallized by the end of the
seventeenth century is already an archaic one even at the moment it crystallizes** — it fossilizes,
broadly, the pronunciation of English **before** the Great Vowel Shift and the various other Early
Modern sound changes covered in `pt06` had finished (see also `pt06`'s already-established pattern
of silent Renaissance-etymology letters, e.g. the *b* in *subtle*, the *p* in *receipt*). This single
fact is presented as the direct, sufficient explanation for most of the "irregularities" and
historical oddities of present-day English spelling: silent letters for sounds that have since been
lost entirely (*k*/*gh* of *knight*, *t* of *castle*, *w* of *wrong*); spelling distinctions that
persist even where the corresponding pronunciation distinction has since merged, in most varieties
(*meat*/*meet*, *sea*/*see* — the surviving-in-spelling trace of the *ę̄*/*ē* merger already analyzed
in `pt06`); conversely, spelling that has **not** kept pace with newer, unrecognized pronunciation
splits (*put*/*putt* sharing one spelling convention for what are, in RP and most varieties, now two
genuinely distinct vowels; *singer*/*finger* sharing one *ng* spelling for what are now two different
consonant sequences, [ŋ] vs. [ŋg]); diphthongs often spelled with a single letter (*mice*, a pure
vowel in Middle English, now a diphthong) while some Modern monophthongs are, inversely, spelled with
a historical digraph (*author*'s *au*, *cough*'s *ou*). Layered on top of all this is the further
"Renaissance etymologizing" effect already covered in `pt06` (*subtle*'s *b*, *receipt*'s *p*) — i.e.
present-day English spelling inconsistency has **(at least) two independent historical causes
operating simultaneously**: the general archaism/lag of the whole standardized system relative to
subsequent sound change, and the specific, separate layer of etymologically-motivated but often
unpronounced Renaissance-period letter insertions.

### Spelling-pronunciations: when the written form drives pronunciation change, not the reverse

A direct consequence of spelling's fixed, archaic standardization: once universal literacy and mass
printed media exposed ordinary readers to a word's **written form** before (or instead of) its
spoken form, some words acquired new pronunciations specifically to match their spelling — the
reverse of the normal sound-change-then-spelling-catches-up relationship covered everywhere else in
the book so far. The book's worked case: *schedule*, originally pronounced with initial /s-/
(spelled *sedule* or *cedul*), acquired its mid-17th-century *sch*- spelling under (further) Latin-
etymology influence (see `pt06`'s remodeling discussion), which then itself produced the now-
standard [ʃ-]/[sk-] spelling-pronunciations; *schism*, similarly, has recently begun acquiring a
new /skɪzm/ spelling-pronunciation alongside its traditional /sɪzm/. The book explicitly attributes
the *general* prevalence of this mechanism to a specific **ideological** belief, not just literacy
alone — "the commonly held view that the written form of a word is the primary or 'right' one, to
which the spoken word should be made to conform," itself reinforced by an upper-class education
centered on the written texts of two dead languages (Latin and Greek, i.e. languages encountered
*only* in writing, with no living spoken norm to check a reading pronunciation against) — a direct,
explicit social-ideology explanation for a phonological-change pattern, distinguishing this section's
explanatory method from the purely mechanical/articulatory explanations used for most of the book's
earlier sound-change material. The full worked word-list (restoring consonants lost in the
traditional pronunciation): *habit, harmony, hemisphere, herb, heritage, host, humble, humour*
(/h-/, not common until the 19th century); *often, waistcoat* ([t]); *clothes* ([ð]); *forehead*
([h]); *Ralph* ([l], with the book flagging that the *older*, consonant-less pronunciation of this
specific word retains upper-class social cachet even today — a genuine, rare exception to the
general spelling-pronunciation-wins pattern); *towards* ([w]).

### The 18th-century push to "regulate" English: academies, dictionaries, grammars, and prescriptivism's real origin

Independent of spelling, a broader **18th-century movement to deliberately regulate the whole
language** — modeled on the prestige of Classical Greek/Latin, believed (not quite accurately, the
book implies) to have been explicitly "ruled" languages themselves — is presented as the direct
historical source of the prescriptive tradition still influential today. Richard Bentley's 1699
observation that every language is naturally "in perpetual motion and alteration," while nonetheless
believing a deliberate "contrivance" could make English "immutable," is cited as the period's
characteristic (and, per the book, fundamentally mistaken) ambition: **no language actually being
used can be prevented from changing**, however strong the regulatory apparatus. A sustained
1650–1760 movement advocated an official English-language academy on the French *Académie
française* model; this specific proposal failed, but produced lasting infrastructure anyway:
**dictionaries** (no English-only dictionary existed before 1604 — only bilingual ones; the earliest,
Cawdrey's 1604 "hard words" dictionary, held about 2,500 entries, reflecting the period's
inkhorn-term anxiety already covered in `pt06`; Cole's 1676 dictionary held about 25,000; dictionaries
only began recording **ordinary, everyday** vocabulary — not just hard/rare words — starting with
*A New English Dictionary* [1702, probably by John Kersey], followed by Bailey's [1721] and Johnson's
[1755] monumental works, the latter notable for extensively quoting earlier authors as usage
evidence) and **grammars** (comparatively rare before the 17th century — a "grammar" before then
normally meant a Latin grammar by default, the way a grammar *school* taught Latin, not English;
English grammars number only 5 in the first half of the 17th century and 9 in the second, then
"an absolute flood" in the 18th — about 50 between 1700–1750 and over 200 in its second half,
driven largely by schools shifting their curriculum toward English alongside or instead of Latin).
**Dictionaries and grammars were both "commonly regarded, not as records of usage, but as
prescriptions for correct usage"** regardless of individual authors' actual intent — the book is
explicit that not all these works were equally (or even genuinely) prescriptive in their own stated
approach (citing John Wallis's 1653 *Grammatica*, which argued classical-language-suitable analysis
is not necessarily suitable for English, and Gildon & Brightland's 1711 *Grammar of the English
Tongue*, which directly attacked Ben Jonson for having forced English into ill-fitting Latin
grammatical categories; Joseph Priestley specifically objected to applying the very term "future
tense" to English, correctly noting English's *shall*/*will* auxiliary construction works exactly
like any other modal-auxiliary construction, not like a true inflected future tense) — but were
nonetheless **seized on as authorities** by the wider public regardless, with a further, more openly
and purely prescriptive genre of "polite usage" handbooks proliferating alongside the more scholarly
grammars throughout the 18th century and into the 19th.

### Classical grammatical terminology misapplied to English, with lasting prescriptive consequences

Even where an individual grammarian didn't intend outright classical imposition, **classical
terminology itself routinely shaped English-specific prescriptive rules that didn't fit the
language's real structure** — the book's worked case: the term *preposition*, borrowed from Latin
*prae* "before" + *positio* "placement," literally encodes the claim that the category's members
must be **placed before** their associated noun/pronoun — a claim that's true of Latin but not
necessarily true of English (the book's own counter-example: "which they are associated with," where
the preposition *with* trails its pronoun, is fully grammatical). This specific etymological
accident is the historical root of the famous prescriptive rule against "ending a sentence with a
preposition" — and the book makes a pointed textual-history correction here: **Robert Lowth, usually
cited as the origin of this rule, explicitly did NOT prescribe it as a hard grammaticality
requirement.** Quoting Lowth (1762) directly: ending a clause with a preposition "is an idiom which
our language is strongly inclined to; it prevails in common conversation, and suits very well with
the more familiar style in writing" — he only recommends the *fronted*-preposition alternative as
more "graceful," "perspicuous," and better suited to "the solemn and elevated style," i.e. as a
**register** recommendation, not a correctness claim. The book's broader methodological point:
"careful reading of grammars such as Lowth's often reveals that they are not as 'prescriptive' as we
have been led to believe" — the genuinely dogmatic handbooks existed *alongside* the more scholarly
grammars throughout the 18th century and progressively crowded them out/dominated public perception
by the 19th, which is where the now-standard caricature of "the 18th-century prescriptivists" as
uniformly dogmatic actually originates.

### Prescriptivism and social class: language as a status marker amid economic change

The book draws an explicit social-history link: as 18th/19th-century Britain's economy shifted from
land-based to money-based wealth (the Industrial Revolution producing newly-wealthy commercial
classes without the traditional gentry's inherited education), "correct" language and manners became
one of the only remaining ways to distinguish established gentry from the newly rich — quoting
Benjamin Withers (1788): "purity and politeness of Expression... is the only external Distinction
which remains between a Gentleman and a Valet, a Lady and a Mantua-maker." Though some individual
grammarians (Lowth, Cobbett) explicitly addressed "all classes," most 18th/19th-century grammars were
in practice written **for and marketed to the anxious middle classes** specifically — not the already-
secure upper classes, nor (despite occasional titles aimed at them, like James Buchanan's 1753
*Complete English Scholar*, explicitly for boys "put to Trades") the working classes in any serious
or sustained way — because it was precisely the middle class that stood to gain or lose social
standing from perceived linguistic "correctness." Grammars of this period frequently invoke an
explicit contrast between "depraved" common speech and "noble, refined" gentry expression, which the
middle classes are implicitly urged to aspire toward. The book extends this directly to **regional/
national prejudice**: educated-London usage was systematically held up as the advocated model,
with Scots and Irish English singled out for especially fierce attack — often, pointedly, by writers
who were themselves Scottish or Irish; this intensified after the 1707 Act of Union, when
"eradicating scotticisms" could be framed as a patriotic unification project. The book offers an
explicit counter-case for balance: Noah Webster, writing in post-independence America, inverted the
whole frame, claiming American English was in fact the **purest and least corrupt** form of the
language — i.e. the same prescriptive impulse could be, and was, turned to opposite national-prestige
conclusions depending on which national variety the writer was defending.

### Prescriptive "logic" arguments against existing usage, and their actual historical accuracy

The book catalogs the specific **logical** justifications period grammarians offered for several
famous prescriptions, then tests each against real usage evidence: (1) **multiple negation**
(normal and unremarkable throughout English until the 17th century — directly continuing the OE/ME
pattern already covered in `pt03`/`pt05`, illustrated here via Shakespeare's *Twelfth Night*, "I haue
one heart, one bosome, and one truth, / And that no woman has, nor neuer none / Shall mistris be of
it, saue I alone") was condemned on the stated ground that "two negatives make an affirmative" — but
the book reports that **detailed study of actual 17th-century usage shows educated writers had
already been abandoning multiple negation well before the rule was first explicitly stated** — i.e.
the "logical" rule was largely **codifying an already-occurring shift in real usage**, not driving
or originating it, a genuinely important methodological caution about over-crediting prescriptive
rules with causal power over language change. (2) **Double comparatives/superlatives** (also
Shakespeare-attested: "more nearer," "the most vnkindest cut of all") were condemned on the same
redundancy-logic grounds. (3) A separate strand of prescriptive reasoning instead appealed to a
posited **"Universal Grammar"** believed to mirror pre-Babel linguistic perfection — in practice,
per the book, this almost always collapsed back into **Latin's own grammar** as the implicit real
standard (worked example: disputes over "It is me" vs. "It is I," importing Latin's case-marking
logic onto English pronouns). (4) A more defensible strand argued from **internal English analogy**
(using one part of the existing system to regularize a disputed point elsewhere in the same system)
— but the book flags this approach was itself frequently undermined by a further, questionable
assumption: that a **large number of fine-grained grammatical distinctions is inherently necessary**
to represent real-world distinctions accurately — the stated motivation behind insisting adverbs be
clearly morphologically distinguished from adjectives (condemning the increasingly-common adverbial
use of bare *quick* and *exceeding*) and behind insisting on separately-marked strong-verb past
tenses vs. past participles (condemning the early-18th-century leveling already covered above,
specifically advocating restored distinct forms like *written* vs. *wrote*, with Latin's own
rich distinct-participle system probably an unstated influence here too). Etymology, too, was
invoked as a "correctness" criterion in word-**meaning** disputes, not just grammar: it was argued
*mutual* must mean specifically "reciprocal" (not the broader "common") and *demean* must mean
"behave" (not "debase"), both appeals to an earlier (English or Latin) ancestor-form's meaning as
the supposedly authoritative one. The chapter's summary judgment: 18th-century grammarians/correctors
frequently disagreed with **each other** (different criteria pulling in different directions across
individual disputes), yet collectively "passed on a substantial body of dogma" that both the 19th and
20th centuries inherited and further built on — a dogma the book contrasts with the last couple of
centuries' shift toward scientific, descriptive linguistic study (grammars/dictionaries that record
and analyze usage rather than prescribe it), while noting popular "correct usage" handbooks
addressing points like preposition placement, *may*/*can*, *shall*/*will*, pronoun case after a
preposition, and split infinitives persist to the present day regardless.

### The verb system reaches full combinatorial freedom: completing perfect + passive + progressive

Building directly on `pt05`'s OE/ME origins and `pt06`'s period-by-period tracking of the perfect,
passive, and progressive/continuous constructions: by late Middle English all four verb "markings"
existed (past tense, perfect, passive, progressive), and it gradually became possible to combine
most of them in most constructions by the Early Modern period — **with one specific, long-lasting
exception: progressive + passive together was not possible until the late 18th century.** The book
explains *why* this particular combination lagged behind the others: the earlier passive/change-
of-state-flavored construction "The house **is building**" (using an active-looking present
participle with a passive sense) was itself the functional stand-in doing this combination's job,
and 18th-century "corrector" grammarians (Dr. Johnson among them) actively **attacked** this
construction as "a vitious expression... a printing, a forging," even while the modern true
passive-progressive ("The house **is being built**") hadn't yet arisen to replace it — producing a
genuine period of real communicative gap/tension, not mere stylistic dispute. Once "is being built"
did arise in the late 18th century, it was in turn attacked by a *different* set of purists who
preferred the older "is building" construction Johnson himself had condemned — i.e. two successive,
mutually opposed waves of prescriptive objection targeting two successive historical stages of the
same underlying construction. The book documents the genuine mid-transition combinatorial state via
Shakespeare, where Perfect+Passive, Perfect+Progressive, Past+Progressive, Past+Perfect+Passive, and
Past+Perfect+Progressive are all independently attested ("my Ladie Hero hath bin falselie accus'de";
"the Iuy which had hid my princely Trunck"; "I haue bin drincking all night"; "As if the garment had
bin made for me"; "both the Princes had bene breathing heere") — but the specific combination
Progressive+Passive ("She is being falsely accused," "The garment was being made") is **never**
attested in Shakespeare, confirming it genuinely did not yet exist as an option in his period, not
merely that he happened not to use it. **Since the late 18th century, any two or three of the four
markings can be freely combined**, and even the full four-way combination (past+perfect+passive+
progressive: "It had been being eaten") is in theory grammatically available, though the book notes
it's genuinely hard to construct a natural context requiring it, and some would argue it's
effectively ungrammatical in practice despite being formally well-formed by the rules.

### The semantics of the progressive and the perfect, worked precisely

The book gives careful, example-driven definitions of what each marking actually *means*, beyond just
tracking when each became available: the **progressive** signals either continuing action over a
period ("John was working in the garden") or repeated action understood as bounded/limited in
duration ("Smith is scoring a lot of goals this season") — and the "limited duration" component is
what explains why "John was standing on the bank" is fine but "\*London is standing on the Thames"
is not: the latter would wrongly imply London might move elsewhere, so the **unmarked** (non-
progressive) form is required instead for a genuinely permanent state ("London stands on the
Thames"). The **perfect** is glossed, following W. F. Twaddell, as signaling **"current relevance"**
— what's said bears on the immediate situation/moment of speaking — and the book explicitly rejects
two common alternative glosses as **both wrong**: it is *not* simply "completed action" (counter-
example: "Our family have lived in this house for three hundred years, and intend to go on doing
so" — a perfect describing an ongoing, uncompleted state); and the "perfect = nearer past, simple
past = remoter past" heuristic is *also* wrong (counter-example: "I've only been there once, about
twenty years ago," immediately followed by a simple past "I went there this morning" describing a
much more recent event) — current relevance to the conversational moment, not elapsed time per se,
is the book's preferred unifying explanation. The **unmarked form** (traditionally mislabeled
"present tense" — the book explicitly argues against this traditional name, since the same unmarked
form covers future reference, "I go to New York next week"; habitual reference, "I go to work every
morning"; and even colloquial narrative past reference, "This chap storms into the pub, bangs on the
counter, and says...") is better understood, per the book, simply as the verb carrying **none** of
the four markings and no modal auxiliary either — it signals nothing beyond the lexical verb's own
meaning, with its temporal interpretation supplied entirely by context rather than by any inherent
marking of its own. The chapter's closing observation on this topic: **across the whole Modern
English period, the perfect and progressive markings have both become steadily more frequent**, with
Early Modern English often using the unmarked form in situations where present-day English would
reach for a perfect or progressive instead — worked via two Shakespeare examples the book explicitly
contrasts with their expected present-day equivalent: *Richard III*'s "Soft, he **wakes**" (where
Modern English would say "He's waking up") and *King Lear*'s "You spoke not with her since?" (where
Modern Standard British English would require "You **haven't** spoken with her since?").

### R-lessness: the Late Modern period's most consequential sound change, and its ripple effects on vowel quality

The book frames the loss of /r/ before a consonant or pause as arguably the single most socially and
phonologically consequential Late Modern sound change, tracked via precise documentary dating: the
weakening of preconsonantal/pre-pausal /r/ had already begun by the 16th century, but the **first
documentary evidence of actual loss** doesn't appear until near the end of the 18th century
(elocutionist John Walker describing it as a feature specifically of **London** speech); it was
**not** accepted as "correct" by 19th-century prescriptive elocution handbooks, which condemned
/r/-dropping "just as roundly as the omission of initial /h/"; by the time Daniel Jones published the
first edition of his *English Pronouncing Dictionary* in 1917, the loss of preconsonantal/final /r/
was fully standard in the pronunciation Jones was describing (i.e. RP). **This loss did not take
place uniformly across all varieties of English** — the book introduces the **rhotic**/**non-rhotic**
terminology explicitly here: rhotic varieties (which retain the /r/) include most North American
speech (except some coastal south-eastern US and New England varieties, and African American
Vernacular English/AAVE), traditional Scots English, Irish English, and some Lancashire/West Country
English; non-rhotic varieties include RP and most other varieties within England and Wales, plus
Australian, New Zealand, and South African English.

**Even where the /r/ itself fully disappeared, it left a permanent mark on the preceding vowel**,
via three distinct mechanisms the book catalogs with dated worked examples — (1) **lengthening**: ME
short [a]/[ɔ] in *arm, bark, card, cord, horse, storm* lengthened in the 17th century; the lengthened
[aː] was initially just an allophone of /æ/ but became an **independent phoneme**, present-day /ɑː/,
once /r/ was subsequently lost; the lengthened [ɔː] merged with the already-existing vowel of *cause*
and *law* (itself the ME diphthong *au*, independently monophthongized per `pt06`), giving the
present-day /ɔː/ phoneme. (2) **Quality change**: 16th-century words like *herb, birth, curse* had
*short* vowels, originally identical to the vowels of *bed, bid, puss* ([ɛrb], [bɪrθ], [kɒrs]), but
under /r/'s influence all three distinct short vowels **merged toward [ə]** (first evidence c. 1600
for *er*-type words, forty to fifty years later for *ir*- and *ur*-type words), were then lengthened
to [əː] by the late 17th century once /r/ itself was lost (in the advanced/leading variety of London
speech) — becoming, at this point, an independent **/ɜː/ phoneme with no equivalent anywhere in
Standard English before the eighteenth century**. The book notes this quality-and-lengthening change
did **not** occur if the /r/ was intervocalic (between two vowels), as in *merry, stirrup, turret* —
explaining why these retain their short original vowel rather than developing the NURSE-type /ɜː/
vowel. The book also flags a non-standard parallel development, in some varieties, where /r/-loss
happened a good deal earlier specifically after /s/ and before the main vowel-quality change above
had applied, producing *cuss* and *bust* as genuine variant forms of *curse* and *burst*, with a
different (earlier, pre-merger) vowel history than the now-standard forms. (3) A further,
historically layered complication: Late Middle English *er* generally became *ar* in preconsonantal/
final position, but this didn't occur in all varieties, so Standard English today has a real,
lexically-unpredictable split reflecting which historical variety a given word happened to be
standardized from — Modern /ɜː/-outcome words (*certain, err, herd, pert, servant*) sit alongside
Modern /ɑː/-outcome words (*clerk, farm, harvest, marvel, sergeant, star*) with **no productive rule
predicting which a given word gets**, and the book notes some words show genuine historical doublets
that only recently resolved to a single standardized form (*person*/*parson*, *university*/*varsity*,
*perilous*/*parlous*).

**Vowel changes conditioned specifically on a following (lost) /r/**: when /r/ followed a long vowel
or diphthong, an [ə] glide developed between them (*fire* [faɪr]→['faɪər], *bower* [baʊr]→['baʊər]);
once /r/ itself was lost, this [ə] sometimes remained as-is, producing the now-familiar **centring
diphthongs** /ɪə/, /ɛə/, /ʊə/, /ɔə/ (the last of these has since further merged into /ɔː/ in most
speech, as in *here, pear, poor, more*). Preceding-/r/'s effect could also be a pure **lowering**:
the Great Vowel Shift's raised ME *ā* and *ǣ* were pulled back down toward [ɛː] specifically when a
following /r/ arrested/blocked their expected further closing (*pare, pear* were [pæːr]/[pɛːr] in
the 16th century, later [pɛːr]/[peːr]); a following *long* [uː] + /r/ caused lowering to [ɔː] by the
end of the 17th century (*coarse, course, court, door, fourth, pour, sword, whore*), with some
varieties instead retaining a non-lowered [ʊər] diphthong as a coexisting alternative pronunciation
(*gourd, moor, poor*).

### Two combinative vowel changes from /w/ and before voiceless fricatives — with documented status reversals over time

**Rounding after /w/**: ME *a* was originally identical in *swan*/*watch* and *ran*/*match* — in the
18th century, /w/ caused rounding of the following vowel to [ɒ] specifically **unless** a velar
consonant followed (*quack, quagmire, twang, wag, wax* keep the unrounded /æ/ — though the book notes
*quagmire* specifically has sometimes recently acquired an analogical rounded pronunciation anyway,
since it's a rarer, less phonologically "protected" word); if the rounding environment was also
followed by /r/, **both** rounding and lengthening occurred (*war, quart* → present-day /ɔː/), but if
the /r/ was intervocalic, rounding occurred without lengthening (*quarrel, warrant*).

**Lengthening before voiceless fricatives /f, s, θ/**: ME short *a* lengthened to [æː] before these
specific consonants (a change with evidence from the 17th century onward), then — once the new /ɑː/
phoneme had independently arisen from the /r/-loss changes above — this separately-lengthened [æː]
was **backed** to merge with that same new /ɑː/ phoneme. The book documents a striking, fully
reversed **sociolinguistic status flip** for this specific change: in 1791, elocutionist John Walker
condemned using the *same* vowel in *basket* as in *father* as "vulgar"; throughout much of the 19th
century, words like *bath, laugh, pass* pronounced with [ɑː] were condemned as affectedly
"drawling" — **but this innovation eventually found its way into RP regardless** (paralleling
/r/-loss's own earlier rejection-then-acceptance trajectory above), while remaining strictly
regional today: the lengthening is found only in the south of England and the South Midlands, with
the un-lengthened short [a] still normal everywhere north of the Wash — this is the **direct source
of the present-day BATH-TRAP north/south English accent split**. A closely parallel change affected
ME *ǭ* (not *a*) lengthened before voiceless fricatives, giving words like *often, cross, cloth* a
long [ɔː]; this pronunciation was **also** condemned as "vulgar" in the late 18th century but **was**
adopted by RP speakers by the early 20th century — except that here the book reports the
sociolinguistic valence has since **flipped a second time**: today the short-vowel pronunciation is
the normal RP form, and the long-vowel [ɔː] pronunciation instead "sounds old-fashioned and very
upper-class" if used by an RP speaker — i.e. **two separately-timed status reversals for the exact
same phonological environment**, condemned-then-accepted-then-recessive-and-marked-upper-class, is
presented as a genuinely striking and unusual sociolinguistic trajectory even by the book's own
standards for this kind of change.

**Final unstressed *-ing*: a third documented status-flip case, specifically class-coded in BOTH
directions simultaneously.** The normal Early Modern development of unstressed word-final *-ing* was
[-ɪŋg]→[-ɪŋ]; a competing variant, [-ɪn], was non-standard in Early Modern English but became
**fashionable** in the 18th century and persisted as a recognized, acceptable RP variant into the
20th; in the 19th century, the [-ɪn] pronunciation came to be **condemned**, probably under spelling
influence, and specifically avoided by middle-class speakers — producing the striking present-day
result that **[-ɪn] is simultaneously a marker of both lower-class AND (residually, where it
survives) upper-class speech**, while [-ɪŋg]-derived [-ɪŋ] is the unmarked, normal RP middle-class
form, with both [-ɪn] and a further reduced [-ən] variant remaining common in regional speech
outside RP — a genuinely unusual sociolinguistic pattern where the prestige and stigma mapping is
not simply high-class-vs-low-class but has the two class extremes sharing one pronunciation against
a middle-class norm in between.

### The triumph of scientific writing and "the plain style"

The book credits the 17th century's "triumph of the scientific outlook" with establishing **the
plain, simple, referential prose style** as the central, default register of Modern English prose
generally — not confined to scientific writing itself, but the background norm against which other,
more deliberately rhetorical or poetic prose styles (the book cites Carlyle, Ruskin, Virginia Woolf
as examples of writers working against/beyond the plain-style default) are read as **marked**
departures. Thomas Sprat's 1667 history of the Royal Society is quoted directly as the period's own
explicit articulation of this stylistic ideal — rejecting "amplifications, digressions, and
swellings of style" in favor of returning to "primitive purity," "almost in an equal number of
*things*" as *words*, and "preferring the language of Artizans, Countreymen and Merchants, before
that of Wits, or Scholars." The book offers a pointed corrective to Sprat's own self-description,
however: Sprat's "primitive purity and shortness is a myth" — the plain style he's describing and
advocating is, in the book's assessment, "a highly sophisticated achievement, not at all primitive,"
and the book also flags the resulting **irony** that this period's scientific "plain style" advocates
explicitly preferred the language of ordinary working tradespeople over that of "Wits, or Scholars,"
a markedly different social-register alignment than the roughly contemporaneous "refiners"/
"correctors" movement covered earlier in this chapter, who instead explicitly preferred educated/
elite usage as their standard — i.e. **two simultaneous, socially-opposed linguistic-prestige
movements operating in the very same period**, one elevating scientific/artisanal plainness, the
other elevating educated/elite correctness.

### General vocabulary expansion: borrowing plus every major native word-formation mechanism, catalogued systematically

Beyond the already-covered scientific vocabulary, the chapter catalogs the late-modern-period
**general** vocabulary's growth across essentially every mechanism covered anywhere else in the
book, several with genuinely new, period-specific examples not seen in earlier chapters:
**borrowing** (driven by the growth of world trade and the British Empire, pulling loanwords from a
much wider geographic range than any earlier period covered — see the Vocabulary table's entries
from Australian Aboriginal, Malay, Chinese, Malagasy, Tongan, Hindi and other Indian languages,
Dravidian/Tamil, plus continuing French/Dutch/Italian/German borrowing in new period-specific
semantic domains: arts, fashion, social life, motoring/aviation, mountaineering); **affixation**
(*un-*, *de-*, and a longer supplementary prefix list — *anti-, dis-, inter-, mis-, non-, pre-,
pro-, self-* — plus the suffix *-ize* and its derived abstract-noun suffix *-ization*, plus a further
supplementary suffix list — *-able, -ee, -er, -ie, -y, -ist, -ly, -wise* [the last especially common
in American English for new adverbs] — with the book explicitly noting most of these affixes are not
themselves of native English origin (many ultimately Greek, Latin, or French) but are now fully
naturalized, productive parts of the English derivational system regardless of origin — a direct
continuation of the "origins are irrelevant once naturalized" point already made for Early Modern
French-root hybrids in `pt06`); **compounding** (Noun+Noun as the dominant pattern, with two explicit
diagnostic tests for whether a sequence counts as a genuine single compound rather than a mere
adjective+noun phrase — semantic opacity, as in *blackleg*/*offside*, and/or single-first-element
stress, as in *páperback*/*ráilway* vs. two-stress *the gréen house* — and a dedicated discussion of
how a compound's originally-transparent etymology can become phonetically obscured over a long
enough period purely by ordinary unstressed-syllable reduction: *breakfast, garlic, holiday, sheriff,
tadpole, woman*, each individually traced to its two original free morphemes). **Conversion**
(zero-derivation, already introduced in `pt06`) is described as **"extremely productive" specifically
in Modern English**, far more so than in Old English, for a structural reason the book makes explicit
here for the first time: Modern English words, having lost so much of OE's inflectional morphology
(see `pt05`), typically carry **no formal marker of their word class at all** in isolation (the book's
worked minimal case: *market*, taken alone, gives no clue whether it's a noun or verb, unlike Latin
*mercātus*, whose very ending proclaims it a noun) — this formal "bareness" is presented as the
underlying mechanical cause of Modern English's unusually high conversion-productivity, not a
coincidence of fashion. Several further, **newly introduced** word-formation mechanisms specific to
this chapter: **shortening/clipping** (already productive at the start of the period, initially
colloquial, sometimes eventually fully supplanting the unshortened form — see Vocabulary table;
flagged sub-patterns: end-clipping as the dominant type, with *public house*→*public*→*pub* as a rare
case of **two successive** end-clippings, and *acute*→*cute*, *omnibus*→*bus* as the markedly rarer
*beginning*-clipped pattern); **blending/portmanteau words** (becoming "more productive in the
twentieth century," with the *portmanteau* label itself borrowed from Lewis Carroll's own coinage —
i.e. literary wordplay directly contributing a lasting piece of the field's own technical
vocabulary); **eponymy** (a proper name — person or place — becoming a common noun, either via direct
suffixation, as with scientist/place names in *-ize* or *-ite*, or by the bare name itself being
taken over unchanged, as with *cardigan, mackintosh, sandwich*); **back-formation** (the specific
mechanism, worked in detail via *to sidle* and *to beg*, of inventing a new, shorter base word by
stripping an apparent — but not historically real — affix off an existing longer word, on the model
of a genuinely productive affixation pattern elsewhere in the language); and **internal
loans/dialect borrowing** (words entering the *general/standard* vocabulary from a *regional*
dialect or from an *occupational-group argot*, which the book frames as "not a new word... but a new
acquisition as far as the general vocabulary... is concerned" — a genuinely distinct mechanism from
cross-language borrowing, with its own worked case studies: the Industrial-Revolution-driven railway/
mining vocabulary entering from northern-English/West Midlands dialect, Scots vocabulary entering
partly thanks to the period popularity of Scots literature [Walter Scott explicitly credited], and
occupational slang like sailors' *gadget* and printers' *wangle* entering the general language from
specialized in-group argot). The chapter's closing, and arguably most directly project-relevant,
sub-case is **slang-to-standard promotion**: *banter, coax, flimsy, flippant, fun, sham,* and *snob*
are explicitly named as 18th-century words that were "regarded as 'low' or 'vulgar'" at the time —
i.e. genuinely stigmatized slang/colloquialisms by the period's own standards — and have since become
fully accepted, completely unremarkable standard vocabulary, with no trace of their earlier
stigmatized status surviving in how a present-day speaker perceives them.

### Public School English and the rise of Received Pronunciation

The chapter's closing section traces a direct, class-institutional pathway from Early Modern
England's merely **regional** prestige-accent situation (London/Court speech recommended as a
written/poetic register model, per `pt06`, without requiring regional speakers to actually change
their spoken accent, and with upper-class regional-accent retention still fully socially acceptable —
Sir Walter Raleigh's Devonshire accent at Elizabeth's own court is the book's repeatedly-cited
example) to the fully **class-based**, nationally-uniform accent situation of the 20th century. The
mechanism, per the book: the great English public schools — dominating gentry education from at
least Thomas Arnold's early-Victorian reforms at Rugby — adopted, as their own in-school norm, "a
variant of the prestigious educated accent of London and the south-east," itself actively promoted
by late-18th/19th-century professional elocutionists (John Walker again named specifically). Because
upper-class boys from genuinely all parts of the country were sent to the same small set of public
schools and acquired this accent there regardless of their own family or regional speech background,
**the accent ceased to be a regional dialect and became instead a class dialect** — carried
afterward by gentry members "whatever part of the country they came from," severing the accent's
original tie to London/the south-east as a *place* and re-anchoring it purely to *social class and
schooling*. Alexander Ellis first gave this now-deregionalized variety the name **"received
pronunciation"** in 1869, defining it as "the educated pronunciation of the metropolis, of the court,
of the pulpit and the bar" — and the book dates RP's explicit, formal recognition as specifically
and inherently tied to a public-school education to the very start of the 20th century, the same
period in which RP reached the height of its social prestige and influence, before beginning a
decline (not further detailed in this chapter) toward the century's end — a decline the book flags
it will take up in the following chapters covering English today.

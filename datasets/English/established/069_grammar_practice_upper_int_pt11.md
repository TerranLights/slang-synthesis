## Grammar points

### Non-defining relative clauses: spoken versus written register

Mid-sentence non-defining relative clauses are flagged as a notably formal, characteristically
written construction (p.156); spoken English more commonly avoids them in favor of shorter separate
sentences linked by simple conjunctions ("Ann Roberts, whose first job was in this department, has
just been appointed Director" [written] vs. "Ann Roberts has just been appointed Director. Do you
remember? Her first job was here in this department" [spoken]).

### Relative clauses of time, place and reason

*When, where*, and *why* can substitute for a relative pronoun in relative clauses specifically
referring to time, place, and reason respectively (p.159): "1990 was (the year) when we moved
here"; "That's (the place) where we stayed for our honeymoon"; "Its central location is (the reason)
why we chose this flat." **Note:** these relative clauses are usually defining but can be
non-defining; clauses starting with *where* are more often non-defining in practice than clauses
starting with *when* or *why* ("The village of Baydon, where I lived as a child, has now become part
of the town").

### Relative clauses after all, etc.

Two distinct patterns, one defining and one non-defining (p.160). **Defining:** *all, anyone/
anything, everyone/everything, the only thing, the best* (and other superlatives) + a defining
relative clause, which can function as either subject or object relative clause ("All that happened
was that they had a little argument" [subject]; "All (that) I need to do now is to buy my ticket"
[object]). A note flags that after *everyone/anyone* specifically, either *who* or *that* is usable
in both subject and object position. **Non-defining:** *a few, several, some, many, much, most, all,
none, either/neither, half, a number, the biggest* (and other superlatives) + *of whom/which* in a
non-defining relative clause ("My two cousins, neither of whom I have seen for ten years, are coming
to visit next week"; "Her old letters, some of which have now been lost, make very interesting
reading").

### Co-ordinating relative clauses

*Which* can refer back to an entire preceding clause/sentence rather than to a single noun phrase —
a co-ordinating relative clause ("He has decided to see the doctor about it, which is a good idea" —
*which* refers to the whole act of deciding to see the doctor) (p.161). Co-ordinating relative
clauses are always non-defining.

### Tag questions

**Form (p.162):** the tag's auxiliary matches the main clause's verb tense/auxiliary (*does/do* for
present simple, *did* for past simple, etc.); a positive main clause takes a negative tag, and vice
versa; most tag questions use falling intonation. A tag question functions less as a genuine request
for information than as a conversational move that assumes listener agreement, and makes a bare
statement sound more inclusive/friendly by assuming the listener already shares the knowledge or
opinion. The listener's expected response can be a short answer, a short answer with additional
comment, or even a short answer followed by another tag question.

**Falling-intonation tag questions** (positive statement–negative tag or vice versa; the most common
type) serve four distinct discourse functions, each with worked examples (pp.162–163): making a
single conversational statement ("Lovely day, isn't it?"); opening a conversation/introducing a
topic ("It was terrible news about that murder, wasn't it?"); and passing a comment mid-conversation
("But he made some very good points, didn't he?").

**Rising-intonation tag questions** (same positive–negative/negative–positive pattern) are much less
common than falling-intonation tags, read as more genuinely questioning, and convey a wider range of
stronger emotions (p.163): expressing surprise or disapproval ("You didn't tell him, did you?");
expressing surprise while asking for confirmation of a prior assumption; checking/asking for
confirmation; making a tentative, polite request ("You couldn't open the door for me, could you?");
and expressing suspicion.

**Rising-intonation tags on a *positive* statement with a *positive* tag** (p.164) are the least
common tag pattern overall, and convey: friendly interest, encouraging the other speaker to continue
("So, you've been given a promotion, have you?"); a suggestion where agreement is expected ("We'll go
for a swim this afternoon, shall we?"); disagreement + a warning; and disapproval + an accusation.

**Echo tags (p.164):** a tag used in direct response to someone else's statement (not attached to
one's own statement), matching that statement's polarity (positive statement → positive echo tag;
negative → negative), used to signal active listening/interest in spoken English; falling or rising
intonation is possible, with rising intonation conveying greater surprise ("A: I met David the other
night. B: Did you?").

**Notes on tag-question pronoun/auxiliary substitution rules (pp.164–165):** a tag always uses a
pronoun, never a repeated proper name, even when the subject of the main clause is itself a name;
when the main sentence contains a modal, the tag reuses that modal; when the sentence's subject is
*there*, the tag reuses *there*; when the subject is *this/that*, the tag uses *it*; when the subject
is *these/those*, the tag uses *they*; when the subject is an indefinite pronoun like
*no-one/someone/everyone*, the tag uses *they*; sentences with a negative-polarity subject
(*nothing, no-one, neither, anyone*) behave grammatically like negative sentences and therefore take
a *positive* tag ("Nobody saw you, did they?"); imperative sentences take *will* in the tag; *Let's*
sentences take *shall* in the tag; and a negative question alone (without a separate tag) can
perform a comparable pragmatic function to a falling-intonation tag question.

### Common prepositions of time

The book tabulates a full reference set of time prepositions with their distinct senses (p.168):
*at* (a clock time; an age); *on* (a day or date; also + noun/gerund as an alternative to a *when*-
clause: "On departing, she..." = "When she departed, she..."); *in* (a period of time — generally,
or specifically at the end of a stated period, or the length of time something takes: "I'll be ready
in five minutes" / "I read the book in two and a half hours"); *by* (not later than a stated time);
*before*; *after*; *since* (a point in time up to now or up to a past reference point); *for* (a
duration); *during* (a named period or event); *until/till* (the point at which an action finished);
and *from...to/until/till* (a time span).

**Notes (pp.168–169):** festivals/religious celebrations take *at* (*at Easter*); *at night* is the
fixed idiom, though *in the night* is also usable to mean "during the night"; *last/this/next* +
time word needs no preposition at all. **During vs. for:** *for* answers "how long," *during*
answers "when" (either at some point within the period, or spanning the whole period). **During vs.
in:** both can refer to something spanning a whole time period and are often interchangeable, but
*in* (not *during*) is required when contrasting one period against another for specificity ("I last
saw you in May, not July"), *during* is preferred to stress duration specifically, and *during* (not
*in*) is required when the time reference is itself an activity rather than a period ("She didn't
speak once during the meal").

### Common prepositions of position and movement

Three groups of position/movement prepositions, organized by the type of spatial relationship
involved (p.170): **(away) from/at/to** a place — used when the reason for being somewhere matters
more than the exact physical position, whether that position is inside or outside ("I'll meet you at
the station"); **out of/in/in(to)** a place — used specifically when the place has walls or
boundaries ("I'll wait for you in the car"); and **off/on/on(to)** a surface or line ("We need some
pictures on the wall"). **Note:** *on* is the default over *onto*; *onto* is reserved for
emphasizing the movement from one place onto another ("I drove onto the motorway" = came from a
different road vs. "I drove on the motorway" = that's simply where the driving happened).

**Further fixed collocations (p.170):** *arrive at/in* a place; *get to/into* a place, vs. bare *get
in* (no place named) and *in* used adverbially ("When does the train get in?"); and the fixed
transport-mode split *on* a train/bus/plane vs. *in* a car.

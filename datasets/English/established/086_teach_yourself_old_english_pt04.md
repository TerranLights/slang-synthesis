## Grammar points

### The weak ending appears in definite (specific) contexts without the article itself

The weak noun/adjective ending (-an etc.) also marks a definite, specific sense in phrases where the
definite article is grammatically absent but the referent is still definite — e.g. an accusative
like *þurh Godes miltsiendan gife* "through God's merciful grace," or a dative like *æt Niwantune*
"at Newton" (lit. "at [the] new tun"). p.114.

### Explicit register note: Latin *regina* vs. vernacular "the king's wife"

In the Latin portion of the Hazelhurst charter, queen Ælfgyfu (Emma) is given the formal Latin title
*regina*, whereas the normal Old English phrase for a king's consort was the more modest *ðæs
cyninges wif* "the king's wife" — the book treats this as evidence of Emma/Ælfgyfu's exceptional
political prominence under Cnut, visible even in a small lexical choice. p.115.

### The place-name element *fag/fah* and its ambivalent connotation

The adjective *fag/fah* ("variegated, dappled") carries connotations ranging from positively
decorative (*fag flor* "the coloured [mosaic] floor" of a Roman villa or king's hall) to
negative/ominous ("stained") — both senses are cited directly from a *Beowulf* passage describing
Grendel's attack on the variegated floor of Heorot. The modern place name Fawler (Oxfordshire) < OE
*fag flor*, with a confirmed Roman-villa archaeological site underneath. p.116.

### The demonstrative þes/þeos/þys ("this"), full declension

Parallel to, but distinct from, the definite article: masculine *þes/þysne/þyses/þyssum*, feminine
*þeos/þas/þysse/þysse*, neuter *þys/þys/þysses/þysse*; plural (all genders) *þas/þas/þyssa/þyssum*.
Several forms were already met in earlier riddle/study texts before being formally tabulated here
(e.g. riddle 66's *þes middangeard* "this middle world," masc.; Joseph's *ðis swefn*, neut.). A
notable usage quirk: a neuter singular *þis* can be followed by the plural verb form *syndon*
("these are"), a pattern Modern English grammar would not license without first switching the
demonstrative itself to plural. p.125–127.

### Charter/declaration formulaic language: two-stress phrases, alliteration, rhyme, parallelism

The stylized language of charters, writs, and declarations draws on oral-formulaic rhetorical
patterns, especially the "two-stress phrase" (two content words, each independently stressed,
joined by one or more unstressed syllables — the same underlying pattern as Modern English "hands
and feet"). These two-stress units combine with rhyme (*unforboden and unbesacan* "unforbidden and
uncontested"), alliteration (*swa full and swa forð* "as fully and as completely"; *binnan byrig and
butan* "within the city and without"), and grammatical parallelism (*on wuda and on felde* "in wood
and in field") for rhetorical/mnemonic effect — reinforcing that these documents were meant to be
read or declared aloud before witnesses, not merely filed as silent paperwork. p.123–125.

### Formulaic legal shorthand: "mid mete and mid mannum," "mid sake and mid socne"

Two compressed legal formulas recur across land grants: *mid mete and mid mannum* ("with food and
with men") refers literally to an estate's attached provisions and labour-force; *mid sake and mid
socne* ("jurisdiction") refers to the right to hold a court in the area and/or profit from fines
levied there. The book notes such formulaic language recurs across multiple legal-document genres
(the king's writ of Unit 7, the marriage agreement of Unit 8, and the declarations of Unit 11). p.125.

### Noun declension across all three classes for one semantic field (the senses/body)

The six "sense-organ" words are distributed across every noun-declension type covered so far,
making them a useful cross-section for drilling case endings: *muð* "mouth" (strong masc.), *eage*
"eye" / *eare* "ear" (weak neuter/feminine), and three irregulars — *fot* "foot" (irregular masc.,
vowel-changing plural *fet*), *hand* "hand" (irregular, unchanged plural), *nosu* "nose" (irregular
feminine). Full singular/plural case tables given for each. p.132.

### Numerals 1–12, the teens, and a "long hundred" (120-based) system above 60

Cardinal numbers 1–3 agree in gender with their noun (masc./fem./neut. forms given for "two" and
"three"). The teens regularly end in *-tyne* (13 *þreotyne*...19 *nigontyne*). From 20–60, the
decades end in *-tig*, pronounced like ModE "-ty" (*twentig* "twenty," etc.). **Above 60, a distinct,
older duodecimal-leaning counting system takes over**: 70–120 are formed by prefixing *hund-* to the
unit-number-plus-*-tig* even though *hund* independently means "hundred" (*hundseofontig* "70,"
*hundeahtatig* "80," *hundnigontig* "90," *hundteontig* "100," *hundendleofantig* "110,"
*hundtwelftig* "120" — the OE "long hundred" is 120, not 100). p.133–134.

### Roman numerals in Old English manuscripts, with Byrhtferth's own exposition

Latin/Roman numerals (I, V, X, L, C, D, M), not Arabic numerals (which arrive only in the 12th
century), are the standard numeral notation in Old English manuscripts, following the normal
cumulative/subtractive Roman rules (junior letter after senior = addition, e.g. *VI* "six"; junior
before senior = subtraction, e.g. *IV* "four," sometimes written *IIII* by scribes instead). Expressing
a number like 24 in prose used the "four and twenty" word order (cf. the nursery rhyme "four and
twenty blackbirds") with the counted noun often put in the genitive plural (*tyn tida* "ten of
hours" = "ten hours"). Byrhtferth's own *Handbook* passage spelling out Roman-numeral values is
directly cited, including what the book flags as a probable scribal slip (writing *syx* "six" where
*V and I* should read as five-plus-one). p.134–135.

### Semantic narrowing and widening: fugol/fowl, bridd/bird, deor/deer

Several Modern English words have shifted their scope from their OE ancestor, illustrating both
directions of semantic change: *fugol* meant "bird" in general (ModE "fowl" has narrowed to mean
specifically domestic/game birds), while conversely *bridd* meant specifically "a young bird,
fledgeling" (ModE "bird" has widened to mean any bird at all) — note the metathesis of *r* as well.
Similarly *deor*, cognate with German *Tier* "animal" (general), narrowed in English specifically to
"deer" (one species). p.139.

### A richer Old English lexicon for cattle/wealth than Modern English

Old English has markedly more near-synonyms for "cattle" than Modern English retains in everyday
use (*ceap*, *feoh*, *neat*, *nyten*, *orf*, alongside *cu*/*hriðer* "cow," *oxa* "ox"), reflecting
cattle's closer conceptual association with wealth and trade in Anglo-Saxon society — reinforcing
the pt02/pt03 semantic-drift note that *feoh* "cattle" is the direct ancestor of ModE "fee." p.139.

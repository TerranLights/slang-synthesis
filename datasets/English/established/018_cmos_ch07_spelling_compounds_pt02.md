## Grammar points

### 7.23 Compound possessives

In a compound noun or noun phrase, the possessive 's (or plural possessive apostrophe) attaches to
the *final* element, even when that final element is itself plural: *student assistants' time
cards*, *my daughter-in-law's address*, *my sons-in-law's addresses* (p. 930).

### 7.24 Possessive with genitive

Certain time/duration expressions use a possessive-like form descended from the old genitive case,
where the construction implies "of": *in three days' time*, *an hour's delay* (or *a one-hour
delay*), *six months' leave of absence* (p. 931).

### 7.25 Possessive versus attributive forms

CMOS distinguishes a true possessive/genitive from a noun used attributively (modifying another noun
without implying ownership) — a line it calls "sometimes fuzzy," especially in the plural. Chicago
drops the apostrophe only in proper names (often corporate) that are "clearly no possessive meaning"
— e.g. *Publishers Weekly*, *Diners Club*, *Department of Veterans Affairs* — while ordinary common
nouns keep it: *children's rights*, *farmers' market*, *women's soccer team*, *boys' clubs* (pp.
931–32).

### 7.26 Possessive with gerund

A noun or pronoun preceding a gerund (verbal noun) may take the possessive, "used with caution" and
most common with proper names/personal nouns or pronouns — e.g. *Fathers' assuming the care of
children has changed the traditional household economy*. CMOS flags the test: if the -ing word is
clearly the subject of the clause (a participle, not a gerund), the possessive is wrong, as in
*Fathers assuming the care of children often need to consult mothers for advice*, where *Fathers* is
the subject and *assuming* a participle. When the noun/pronoun follows a preposition, the possessive
becomes optional (*she was worried about her daughter* or *daughter's going there alone*) (p. 932;
cites Fowler's *Modern English Usage* for a fuller discussion).

### 7.27 Possessive with "of"

The possessive form may be preceded by "of" where "one of several" is implied — *a friend of Dick's*
and *a friend of his* are both acceptable (p. 933).

### 7.28 Possessive with italicized or quoted terms

When an italicized term takes the possessive, the possessive 's itself is set in roman type: *the
Atlantic Monthly's editor*. If the last element is already plural in form, only the apostrophe is
added (*the New York Times' new fashion editor*). CMOS discourages forming the possessive of a term
that is enclosed in quotation marks rather than italicized, even though this is sometimes seen in
periodicals that quote rather than italicize titles (e.g. *admirers of "Ode on a Grecian Urn,"* not
an apostrophe-s construction) (p. 934).

### 7.29 Contractions

An apostrophe normally replaces omitted letters in a contraction; some (*won't*, *ain't*) are formed
irregularly rather than by simple letter deletion. Colloquialisms like *gonna* and *wanna* take no
apostrophe at all, since there's no clear "missing" segment to mark. CMOS directs readers to
*Webster's* for the authoritative list of contractions, alternative spellings, and plurals. It
stresses that the correct mark is a true apostrophe (looking like a right single quotation mark, not
a left one or a straight prime) wherever a contraction is formed — illustrated with *'tis* (p. 935).

### 7.30 Interjections

As with contractions, *Webster's* is the first-line source for standard interjections (CMOS cites
*ugh*, *er*, *um*, and *sh* as dictionary-listed). For interjections not found in the dictionary, or
where a nonstandard spelling better captures a particular emphasis, CMOS says "plausible spellings
should be sought in literature or invented" (p. 936).

### 7.31 Dictionary word division

For end-of-line word breaks (spelling and plural forms too), Chicago's primary authority is
*Webster's*, whose syllable dots mark where breaks are permitted — with longer words often offering a
genuine choice of break points. This section's own rules are meant to supplement, not replace,
*Webster's* system, e.g. by recommending a preferred break among several the dictionary allows. Word
division of non-English words is covered elsewhere (chapter 11); none of this section's advice
should be applied at the manuscript-preparation stage, only in finished typesetting (p. 937).

### 7.32 Words that should not be divided

Single-syllable words — including inflected verb forms like *aimed* and *helped* — are never broken
across a line. Because a one-letter division is never permissible, words such as *again*, *enough*,
and *unite* cannot be divided (there's no acceptable break point even though they're polysyllabic).
Words that risk being misread if split, such as *water*, *women*, and *prayer*, should be divided only
reluctantly (p. 938).

### 7.33 Dividing according to pronunciation

Chicago's preferred practice, matching *Webster's*, divides most words by pronunciation rather than
etymological derivation: *knowl-edge* (not *know-ledge*), *democ-racy* or *de-mocracy* (not
*demo-cracy*). Words with multiple meanings/pronunciations need special care at the break point —
*proj-ect* (noun) versus *pro-ject* (verb) (p. 939).

### 7.34 Dividing after a vowel

Unless a break would affect pronunciation, divide after a vowel: *criti-cism* (rather than
*crit-icism*), *liga-ture* (rather than *lig-ature*). When a vowel itself forms a mid-word syllable,
it should run in to the end of the first line if possible: *an-tipodes* or *antipo-des* (rather than
*antip-odes*). Diphthongs are treated as single vowels, e.g. the *eu* in *aneurysm*, giving
*aneu-rysm* rather than *an-eurysm* (p. 940).

### 7.35 Dividing compounds, prefixes, and suffixes

Hyphenated or closed compounds, and words carrying a prefix or suffix, are best divided at their
natural morphological break: *poverty-/stricken* (rather than *pov-/erty-stricken*),
*thanks-giving* (rather than *thanksgiv-ing*), *dis-pleasure* (rather than *displea-sure*),
*re-inforce* (rather than *rein-force*) (p. 941).

### 7.36 Dividing words ending in "ing"

Most gerunds/present participles may be divided before the *-ing*: *certify-ing*, *giv-ing*. When the
final consonant before *-ing* is doubled, the break instead falls between the two consonants:
*dab-bing*, *run-ning*, *fiz-zling*, *bris-tling*. For words ending in *-ling*, consult the dictionary
rather than applying a blanket rule (p. 942).

### 7.37 Two-letter word endings

A two-letter word ending is best not carried over to a new line: *losses* is better broken as
*loss-es*, not *los-ses*; *surprises* as *sur-prises*, not *surpris-es*. Certain two-letter endings in
some foreign-origin words, however, are customarily carried over (p. 943).

### 7.38 Dividing proper nouns and personal names

A multi-element proper noun, especially a personal name, should ideally be broken between its
elements rather than within any single element — e.g. *Heitor Villa-/Lobos*, or better, break before
*Heitor* entirely (*Heitor/Villa-Lobos*). Single-element names needing a within-word break (e.g.
*Alek-sis*, *Ana-stasia*) should follow the dictionary where possible; *Webster's Collegiate*,
*Merriam-Webster's Biographical Dictionary*, and *Merriam-Webster's Geographical Dictionary* are
named as fuller references. A name with initials should break after an initial, not elsewhere
(*Frederick L./Anderson*, *M. F. K./Fisher*); a break immediately before *Jr.* or *Sr.* should be
avoided. If a name's pronunciation is unknown, the break should follow a vowel by default
(*Eliza-/beth II*) (pp. 944–45).

### 7.39 Dividing numerals

Large numerals are best left unbroken — reword the sentence to avoid a break if possible. If a break
is unavoidable, it must fall only after a comma, never after a single digit: *1,365,-/000* or
*1,365,000,-/000* (p. 946).

### 7.40 Dividing numerals with abbreviated units of measure

A numeral paired with an abbreviated unit of measure (*345 m*, *24 kg*, *55 BCE*, *6:35 p.m.*) is
best kept intact on one line — move the whole numeral, or the abbreviation, to avoid a break.
Numerals paired with a spelled-out unit, which form longer expressions, may be broken across a line
as needed (p. 947).

### 7.41 Division in run-in lists

A list marker such as "(3)" or "(c)" in a run-in list should not be separated from the text that
immediately follows it; if it falls at a line's end, it should be carried to the next line along with
that text (p. 948).

### 7.42 Dividing URLs and e-mail addresses

In print, a URL or e-mail address that must break at a line end should break between logical
elements wherever possible: *after* a colon or double slash; *before or after* an equals sign or
ampersand; *before* a single slash, period, or other punctuation/symbol. A hyphen already present in
an address must never be mistaken for, or treated as, a line-break hyphen, and no new hyphen should
ever be inserted to break a URL or e-mail address. If a long unbroken element must still be split, it
follows the ordinary syllable-based guidelines above. Manuscript preparation should never introduce
such breaks at all (p. 949).

### 7.43 Hyphenation and appearance

For aesthetic reasons, no more than three consecutive lines should end in hyphens — informally called
a "hyphen stack." Hyphenated breaks occur far more often in justified text (where they help avoid
uneven line lengths) than in ragged-right text. End-of-line hyphenation is rarely needed in online
publications, with the exception of those deliberately modeled on a printed page's appearance (p.
950).

### 7.44 "A" and "an"

In American English the indefinite article is *a*, not *an*, before any word beginning with a
*pronounced* "h" — *a hotel*, *a historical study* — but *an* before a silent "h" — *an honor*, *an
heir*. Before an abbreviation, numeral, or symbol, the choice of *a* versus *an* follows how the term
is actually pronounced aloud: *an MS treatment* (pronounced "em ess") versus *a CBS anchor*
(consonant-initial sound), *a URL*, *an @ sign*, *an 800 number*, *an 007 field* (pronounced "oh oh
seven") versus *a 007-style agent* (pronounced "double oh seven") (pp. 951–52).

### 7.45 "O" and "oh"

The vocative *O* (now largely obsolete) is always capitalized and is never followed by punctuation
within the sentence: *"Thine arm, O Lord, in days of old..."* The ordinary interjection *oh* is
capitalized only when it begins a sentence or stands entirely alone (*Oh! It's you!*); mid-sentence
it stays lowercase (*Where, oh where, have you been?*) (p. 952).

### 7.46 When to use ligatures

The ligatures æ (a+e) and œ (o+e) should **not** be used in Latin or transliterated Greek words, nor
in words that have been fully adopted into English from Latin, Greek, or French and are found spelled
without the ligature in English dictionaries — e.g. *aesthetics*, *Encyclopaedia Britannica*
(explicitly contrary to that work's own corporate branding, which does use the ligature),
*oedipal*, *a trompe l'oeil mural*, *Emily Dickinson's oeuvre*. The ligatures remain needed, however,
for spelling genuine Old English words in an Old English context (æ, as in *Ælfric*, *es hæl*) and
for genuine French words in a French context (œ, as in *le nœud gordien*, *Œuvres complètes*) (p.
953).

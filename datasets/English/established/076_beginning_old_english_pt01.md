# English — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: *Beginning Old English*, Chapters 1–2 (Origins; Recognising Old English Words)

**Source:** Carole Hough and John Corbett, *Beginning Old English* (Palgrave Macmillan, 2007),
pp. 3–33 (Chapter 1 "Origins" and Chapter 2 "Recognising Old English Words").

**Coverage note:** every distinct Old English vocabulary item cited in these two chapters, plus
Chapter 2's full set of worked exercise word-lists (the book's own numbered "Answers" section at
the end of Chapter 2 gives authoritative glosses for every item, so these are retained rather than
treated as throwaway drill filler — they are the book's primary vocabulary-recognition content, not
repeated examples). Chapter 1 is historical narrative rather than a grammar lesson; its only
"grammar points" are the two background orthographic/sound-change facts it introduces in passing
(the þ/ð letters; inflexional loss under Norse contact) — both are covered more fully in Chapter 2,
so Chapter 1's grammar section here stays brief to avoid duplication. Old Norse and Icelandic
comparison words (e.g. ON *kirkja*, Icelandic *þarf*) are mentioned in Chapter 1 as cognates but are
**not** Old English and are excluded from the vocabulary table; they are noted in prose only where
relevant to an English word's etymology. Modern English headwords used purely as glosses are not
separately tabulated.

**Source text layer:** confirmed clean (`pdftotext`-extractable), with one confirmed fixed
character-substitution cipher throughout this file's page range (see note below). Vision Reading
Confidence is `n/a` throughout this file.

**Font-substitution cipher confirmed (character-level, not page-range-dependent on pp. 3–33):**
thorn (þ) renders as **ƒ**, eth (ð) renders as **ê**, capital thorn (Þ) renders as **∏**. This
decode was cross-verified against 20+ known Old English word/gloss pairs across this page range
(e.g. *forþ*/*forð* 'forth', *brōþor*/*brōðor* 'brother', *þæt*/*ðæt* 'that', *Þā*/*Ðā* 'then') and
holds consistently — no exceptions found in this chunk. A separate minor artifact, dotless-i +
combining macron rendering as "ı̄" instead of precomposed "ī" (a font-encoding quirk, not a cipher),
is silently normalized to ī throughout. All decoded forms below are given in their corrected ð/þ
spelling.

## Grammar points

### The Old English alphabet: þ/ð for 'th', and the vowel æ (Ch.2, pp.17–19)

Old English uses two interchangeable letters, þ ("thorn") and ð ("eth"), for both the voiced
*th* of 'this' and the unvoiced *th* of 'thing' — a single sound distinction PDE spelling doesn't
make either (it also doesn't distinguish them from each other; the source notes scribes mixed þ and
ð even within a single manuscript, and standardizes to ð in Chapters 2–5 for readability). It also
retains a dedicated vowel letter æ ("ash"), pronounced as in 'cat', not used in standard PDE
spelling (though the *sound* survives, e.g. in *wæter* > 'water').

### Letters OE lacks: k, v, z (Ch.2, p.19)

PDE *k*, *v*, *z* correspond to OE *c*, *f*, *s* respectively (*cyning* 'king', *ofer* 'over', *is*
'is').

### Metathesis (Ch.2, p.19)

A sound-reordering change, most often affecting *r*, moved certain OE consonant clusters into their
modern order — e.g. OE *ðridda*, *ðritig* became PDE 'third', 'thirty' (vs. unshifted *ðrēo*
'three'). The source frames this as still an active tendency in casual PDE speech (e.g.
'modren' for 'modern').

### Regular consonant-cluster correspondences (Ch.2, p.20)

A short table of regular OE→PDE sound shifts makes many words instantly recognizable once learned:
*cw*→*qu* (*cwic*→'quick'), *sc*→*sh* (*biscop*→'bishop'), *hl*/*hr*→*l*/*r* (*hlaford*,
*hrōf*→'lord', 'roof'), *hw*→*wh* (*hwǣr*→'where'), *ht*→*ght* (*niht*→'night'). The source
stresses OE spelling has no silent letters — PDE's silent 'gh' (night) and 'k' (knee) are fossilized
relics of sounds OE still pronounced.

### Long-vowel shifts (the Great Vowel Shift's lexical footprint) (Ch.2, p.20)

OE long vowels (marked with a macron, e.g. ī, ū) shifted regularly into their PDE pronunciations —
e.g. *wīf* ('weef'-like) → 'wife', *hūs* ('hoose'-like, preserved in Scots) → 'house', *bāt*
('baht'-like) → 'boat', *tōð* → 'tooth', *fēt* → 'feet', *brȳd* (German *Füße*-like vowel) → 'bride'
(with shortening in some descendants, e.g. *lȳtel* → 'little'). The book doesn't name this the
"Great Vowel Shift" explicitly at this introductory level but is describing its lexical
consequences.

### Context-dependent pronunciation of c and g (Ch.2, p.21)

*c* is normally /k/ ('king') but /tʃ/ ('chill') before *e*/*i* or word-finally; *g* is normally the
hard 'girl' sound but /j/ ('yet') before *e*/*i* or word-finally; the digraph *cg* is /dʒ/ ('judge').
This single rule unlocks recognition of a large number of words (*dæg*, *geong*, *cirice*, *ecg*,
etc.).

### Spelling variation, not error (Ch.2, p.21)

OE manuscripts show genuine dialectal/scribal spelling variation, not standardized orthography:
*a*/*o* alternate before *m*/*n* (*and*/*ond*, *hand*/*hond*), and *a*/*ea* alternate especially
before *l* (*ald*/*eald* 'old', *fela*/*feala* 'many'). The source frames this as a real feature of
the manuscript tradition the reader must expect, not noise to normalize away when reading
unedited texts (contrast with the chapters' own deliberate regularization for pedagogical ease).

### Semantic shift / narrowing (Ch.2, p.22)

Several OE words survive into PDE with narrowed or shifted meanings rather than disappearing
outright: *cwēn* 'woman' (general) → 'queen' (narrowed); *wīf* 'woman' (general) → 'wife' (narrowed
to 'married woman'); *dēor* 'any wild animal' → 'deer' (narrowed to one species); *cræftig*
'skilful' → 'crafty' (pejorated toward 'devious'); *sōna* 'immediately' → 'soon' (weakened to 'in a
while'); *gāst* 'spirit' → 'ghost' (narrowed); *dōm* 'judgement' → 'doom' (narrowed toward negative
judgement). A regional counter-case: Northeast Scots 'quine' retains *cwēn*'s original broader sense
('young woman generally'), showing the standard-PDE narrowing isn't the only possible outcome.

### Compounding as a word-formation strategy (Ch.2, p.24)

Like PDE, OE builds new words by compounding existing ones, especially productively in poetic
registers around 'hall' and 'battle' vocabulary (see Morpheme breakdown above). OE personal names
are also frequently transparent compounds that don't always combine to a literal sense from their
parts — *Cynewulf* 'royal-wolf', *Cyneheard* 'royal-hard', *Bēowulf* 'bee-wolf' (figuratively 'bear':
an animal that raids beehives for honey).

### Case marking (introduced informally in Ch.2, formalized in Ch.3)

Chapter 2 flags, without yet formalizing, that OE pronouns like *ðū*/*ðē* ('you') change form
depending on grammatical role — the explanation is deferred to Chapter 3's full case system (see
`076_beginning_old_english_pt02.md`).

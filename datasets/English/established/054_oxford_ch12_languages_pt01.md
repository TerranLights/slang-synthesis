## Grammar points

### 12.1 General principles

The chapter covers editing and presenting material in foreign languages, plus Old and Middle English
(outside this chunk's page range). Languages are grouped alphabetically but merged where related for
clarity (e.g. one Slavonic section rather than separate Belarusian/Bosnian/Bulgarian sections — itself
outside this chunk's range). The chapter is explicitly not a full linguistic description of any
language: it targets common editorial pitfalls in spelling, punctuation, accents, syntax, and
typography, for authors/editors across a spectrum of familiarity. Coverage depth follows frequency in
English-language publishing, not even-handedness across all world languages. Non-Roman alphabets
should be set with a Unicode-compliant font; authors should check font requirements with their
publisher. Foreign personal and place names are covered separately, in Chapter 12's cross-reference to
Chapter 6 (p. 206/PDF 223).

### 12.2 Arabic

**12.2.1 Alphabet.** Arabic's 28 consonant-only letters are written right-to-left in a cursive script;
letterforms vary by position (several distinguished only by dots), some letters don't join to the
next even within a word, and a horizontal extender on the baseline fills out a letter that would
otherwise sit too close to its neighbor. Verse conventionally gives every line the same visual length
so the rhyming letter aligns on the left throughout. The same script, with added letters, serves or has
served other languages spoken by Muslims — Persian, Pashto, Urdu, Turkish, and Malay are named, with a
note that Turkish and Malay are now written in the Roman alphabet. There is no standard system for
transliterating Arabic. Table 12.1 (not reproduced) tabulates all 28 letters across their alone/final/
medial/initial forms with Latin name and romanized letter.

**12.2.2 Accents and punctuation.** Vowel marks are used in Koran editions, schoolbooks, and classical-
poetry editions; elsewhere they're omitted except to resolve ambiguity. Strict philological
transliteration marks the under-dot in *ḥ, ṣ, ḍ, ṭ, ẓ*, the over-dot in *ġ*, the inferior semicircle in
*(ḥ)*, the háček in *ğ* (often written *j*) and *š*, and the macron in *ā, ī, ū* (not the acute or
circumflex); less learned systems drop some or all of these. Two independent letters, *'ayn* (ʿ) and
*hamza* (ʾ), correspond to Hebrew ayin and aleph and, if the proper characters aren't available, are
substituted by a Greek asper and lenis respectively — distinguished from the apostrophe (found mainly
before *l-*, e.g. *ʿAbdu 'l-Malik*) by inserting a hair space between them and quotation marks. The
definite article *al-* or *'l-* (regional variants *el-*, *ul-*) hyphenates to the noun (*al-Islām,
al-kitāb*); the *a* isn't capitalized except sentence-initially, and never at the start of a quoted
title.

**12.2.3 Word division.** Arabic-script words should never be divided, even at internal letter-spacing
gaps — use extenders instead if a line's spacing runs too loose. In transliteration, avoid dividing
except at the article's hyphen, and never carry over more than one consonant if unavoidable. In loose
transcription, *dh kh sh th* may represent either a single strictly-transliterated consonant (*ḍ, ḥ,
š, ṭ*) or an actual two-consonant-plus-*h* sequence — when in doubt, don't divide.

### 12.3 Chinese

**12.3.1 Introduction.** Forms of Chinese spoken across areas of Chinese settlement differ widely
enough that several are arguably separate languages: Northern Chinese (Mandarin), Cantonese, Hakka, the
Wu dialect of Suzhou, and the dialect of Min (Fukien) are named as the principal ones. All, however,
share one written language of thousands of ideographs/"characters." The register used for official
documents is wholly unlike the spoken language, as is the register of classical literature — both use
the same script nonetheless.

**12.3.2 Scripts.** An individual character's structure can require as many as 28 separate brush/pen
strokes. A simplified-character code is standard on the mainland; more traditional forms persist in
Hong Kong, Taiwan, Singapore, and areas outside Chinese jurisdiction. The language is monosyllabic, one
ideograph per syllable, each pronounced with a particular tone — the National Language (standard
modern spoken Chinese) uses four tones, Cantonese nine. The National Language derives from
northern-China (specifically Beijing-area) pronunciation, traditionally used for official business —
hence "Mandarin"; alternative names are *putonghua* ("speech in common use") and *Kuo-yü*/*Guoyu*
("National Language"). Thousands of characters are traditionally written top-to-bottom in
right-to-left columns, but nowadays, especially on the mainland, are printed left-to-right like the
Western alphabet — and this left-to-right convention has always applied whenever Chinese phrases are
set within Roman-alphabet text.

**12.3.3 Transliteration.** Pronunciation varies markedly by dialect (the surname pronounced *Wu* in
Mandarin is *Ng* in Cantonese), but romanization is normally based on Beijing/"Mandarin" usage
regardless of dialect. Two main systems exist: **Wade–Giles**, formerly the norm in English-language
publications (its own name would be spelled *P'in-in* in its own system), and **Pinyin**, the official
system in the People's Republic. Wade–Giles hyphenates the syllables of compounds; Pinyin runs them
together, inserting an apostrophe only where the syllable break would otherwise be unclear (*Xi'an* vs.
Wade–Giles *Hsi-an*, since *Xian* alone would read as Wade–Giles *Hsien*). Wade–Giles distinguishes
aspirated from unaspirated consonants with a Greek asper (sometimes replaced by an opening quote or
apostrophe, often dropped in popular writing); Pinyin uses different letters for the same distinction.
Wade–Giles uses *ü* more broadly than Pinyin, which restricts it to the syllables *lü* and *nü*; only
Wade–Giles uses *ê* (e.g. *jên* = Pinyin *ren*) and *ŭ* (*ssŭ* = Pinyin *si*). Neither system
consistently marks the syllabic tone, despite its importance; when they do, Wade–Giles writes a
superscript figure after the syllable (*i¹ i² i³ i⁴*), Pinyin an accent on the vowel (*yī yí yǐ yì*),
which must sometimes combine with the umlaut. Example place/name contrast given: *T'ien-tsin* / *Mao
Tse-tung* (Wade–Giles) vs. *Tianjin* / *Mao Zedong* (Pinyin).

### 12.4 Dutch and Afrikaans

**12.4.1 Introduction.** Dutch is the language of the Netherlands. The Dutch spoken in northern
Belgium, formerly called Flemish, is now officially called Dutch (*Nederlands* in Dutch). Afrikaans is
one of South Africa's official languages, derived from the Dutch brought to the Cape by settlers in the
seventeenth century.

**12.4.2 Dutch.**
- *Alphabet:* same as English, but *q* and *x* occur only in foreign loanwords. In dictionaries *ij*
  precedes *ik*; in directories and encyclopedias it is sometimes treated as equivalent to *y*. The
  apostrophe occurs in plurals like *pagina's* ("pages") but not before *s* in the genitive.
- *Accents:* the acute distinguishes *één* ("one") from *een* ("a"), and *vóór* ("before") from *voor*
  ("for"). The only other accent needed outside loanwords is the diaeresis: *knieën* "knees,"
  *provinciën* "provinces," *zeeën* "seas."
- *Punctuation and capitalization:* punctuation is less strict than in German. Capitals mark the
  pronouns *U* "you" and *Uw* "your," nationality-term adjectives (*Engelsman* "Englishman," *Engels*
  "English"), and adjectives derived from proper nouns — but not days or months. Institutional names
  capitalize every word except prepositions and articles. The abbreviated forms of *des* and *het*
  ('s and 't respectively) take a space on either side, except before town/city names, where a hyphen
  follows (*'s-Gravenhage*). When a sentence begins with an apostrophe-initial word, the *following*
  word takes the capital: *'t Is*.
- *Word division:* don't divide the suffixes *-aard, -aardig, -achtig*, or any suffix beginning with a
  consonant — this includes the diminutive *-je*, though a preceding *t* may itself belong to the
  suffix (*kaart-je* "ticket" vs. *paar-tje* "couple"). Single consonants carry over, as do *ch, sj,
  tj* (each a single sound) and *sch*, and consonant + *l*/*r* in loanwords. Of two consonants
  together, carry over the second (including the *g* of *ng* and the *t* of *st*); with more than
  three consonants together, carry over whichever combination could begin a word. Never divide double
  vowels, or *ei, eu, ie, oe, ui, aai, eei, ieu, oei, ooi*.

**12.4.3 Afrikaans.**
- *Alphabet, accents, and spelling:* there is no *ij* in Afrikaans — *y* is used as in older Dutch;
  *s* appears word-initially where Dutch has *z*, and *w* between vowels often where Dutch has *v*.
  The circumflex is fairly frequent; the grave appears on the paired conjunction *òf … òf* ("either …
  or") and a few other words. The acute distinguishes the demonstrative *dié* from the article *die*,
  and marks certain proper nouns and French loanwords. There is no diaeresis with *ae* (*dae* "days").
- *Capitalization:* as in Dutch, an apostrophe-initial sentence-opening word capitalizes the
  *following* word — important because the indefinite article is written *'n*: *'n Man het gekom*, "A
  man has come." The pronoun *ek* ("I") is **not** capitalized, unlike English "I."

### 12.5 French

**12.5.1 Accents.** The acute (´), French's most common accent, is used only over *e*; when two *e*'s
occur together the first always takes the acute, as in *née*. The grave (`) is used mainly over *e*,
but also on final *a* (*voilà*) and on *u* only in *où* "where" (not *ou* "or"). The circumflex (ˆ) may
appear over *a, e, i, o, u*. The cedilla *ç* is used only before *a, o, u*. The diaeresis (¨) occurs on
*i, e, y*. Although the Académie française recommends accenting capital letters, this is often skipped
in everyday French except where needed to avoid confusion — contrast *POLICIER TUÉ* "Policeman killed"
with *POLICIER TUE* "Policeman kills."

**12.5.2 Orthographic reform.** *Les Rectifications de l'orthographie*, drafted by the Conseil Supérieur
de la Langue Française, was published in 1990; it is controversial and widely ignored. From an editor's
perspective the main relevant changes affect circumflex accents and hyphens. Since the document
recommends removing the circumflex on *i* and *u* (except in verb endings and a few meaning-
distinguishing words), its absence may indicate the author follows the reform — worth ascertaining
where possible; if not possible, assume the reforms aren't being followed.

**12.5.3 Abbreviations.** As in English, a full point follows an abbreviation (*chap., ex.*) but not a
contraction (*St, Mlle*); the hyphen is retained when a hyphenated form is abbreviated (*J.-J.
Rousseau*; *P.-S.* for *post-scriptum*). The source gives an extended reference list of common French
abbreviations (p. 212/PDF 229) — not reproduced here per copyright discipline; its content is a
structured abbreviation-to-full-form mapping (e.g. *éd.* for *édition*, *qqn* for *quelqu'un*, *s.d.*
for *sans date*), illustrating that French abbreviation conventions parallel English ones in form but
draw on different base vocabulary.

**12.5.4 Capitalization.** Capitalize only the first element (or the first element after the article)
of a compound proper name; if that first element has a following adjective linked by hyphen, capitalize
the adjective too (*l'Académie française* vs. *la Comédie-Française*, *le Conservatoire de musique* vs.
*la Légion d'honneur*). A following adjective *not* hyphen-linked stays lower case, while an adjective
*preceding* the noun is capitalized (*Le Nouveau Testament* vs. *les Saintes écritures*). Lower case is
used for days of the week, month names, cardinal points (*le nord*, *le sud*), language names,
adjectives derived from proper nouns (*la langue française*), and ranks/titles/regimes/religions and
their derivative adjectives (*calvinisme, chrétien(ne), le christianisme, humaniste, les sans-culottes,
le socialisme, les socialistes*). Capitals mark nationality used as a noun (*le Français* "the
Frenchman," *la Française* "the Frenchwoman") as opposed to the adjective/language (*le français*,
"French"). An adherent of Judaism (*un juif*) should in principle be lower case like *un chrétien* or
*un musulman*, but capitalized when referring to a member of a people (*un Juif*, like *un Turc* or *un
Arabe*) — though in practice the capital seems to be used more widely than this rule alone would
suggest. Geographical common nouns (*mer* "sea") are normally lower case, but there are traditional
exceptions: *le Bassin parisien, le Massif central, le Massif armoricain, la Montagne Noire, le Quartier
latin*.

**12.5.5 Punctuation.**
- *Hyphen:* connects cardinal and ordinal number-words under 100 (*vingt-quatre*, "twenty-four";
  *trois cent quatre-vingt-dix*), but no hyphen is used when *et* joins two numbers (*vingt et un,
  cinquante et un, vingt et unième*).
- *Quotation marks:* texts set wholly in French use guillemets (« »); these needn't be used for French
  text embedded in an English-language book. A space (preferably thin or no-break, to avoid awkward
  line breaks) separates the marks from their contents. A guillemet repeats at the head of every
  subsequent paragraph of a quotation. In conversational matter guillemets sometimes bracket the whole
  exchange, with individual speakers' utterances marked by a spaced dash; many modern authors drop
  guillemets altogether and denote speakers by dash alone, though this is officially frowned upon.
  English-style inverted commas often mark a quotation within a quotation. Where guillemets are used,
  only one » appears when two quotations end simultaneously.

**12.5.6 Work titles.** Capitalize the initial letter of a title's first word and of a following noun,
if the first word is a definite article (*Les Femmes savantes, Au revoir les enfants*). Where the title
occurs mid-sentence, a lower-case *l'* may be used, construed with the surrounding sentence (*La mise en
scène de la Bohème*, "The production of *La Bohème*"). If a noun following an initial definite article
is itself preceded by an adjective, capitalize the adjective too (*Le Petit Prince*; *Les Mille et Une
Nuits*, two adjectives) — but downcase an adjective that *follows* the noun (*Les Mains sales*). If a
title begins with any word other than *le, la, les*, or forms a complete sentence, downcase the words
following unless they are proper nouns (*Une vie*; *À la recherche du temps perdu*; *La guerre de Troie
n'aura pas lieu*; *Les dieux ont soif*). A parallel title is treated as a title in its own right for
capitalization purposes (*Émile, ou, De l'éducation*). Given the complexity of these rules, some English
house styles simply capitalize the first word and any proper nouns.

**12.5.7 Word division.** Mostly by spoken syllable, occasionally by etymology. A single consonant
always goes with the following vowel (*amou-reux, cama-rade*); *ch, dh, gn, ph, th*, and consonant
groups of consonant + *r* or *l*, count as single consonants for this purpose. Other consonant groups
divide regardless of etymology (*circons-tance, tran-saction, obs-curité*), but a prefix always divides
from a following *h* (*dés-habiller*). Always divide double *l*, even when sounded /j/ (*travaillons,
mouil-lé*). Don't divide between vowels except within a compound (*anti-aérien, extra-ordinaire*, but
*Moa-bite*); in particular, a single-syllable vowel sequence (*monsieur*) is never divided. Don't divide
after a single letter (*émettre*) or before/after an intervocalic *x* or *y* unless followed by a
consonant, in which case do divide after it (*dex-térité, pay-san*). Don't divide abbreviated words
(*Mlle*), within initials (*la CRS*), or after an abbreviated forename (*J.-Ph. Rameau*) or personal
title (*le Dr Suchet*). Don't divide after an apostrophe within a compound word (*presqu'île,
aujourd'hui*). Interrogative verb forms divide before *-t-* (*Viendra-|t-il?*).

**12.5.8 Numerals.** Use words for times of day expressed in hours and fractions of an hour (*six
heures*, "six o'clock"; *trois heures et quart*, "a quarter past three"), but use figures for time
expressed in minutes (*6 h 15, 10 h 8 min 30 s*). Set Roman numerals indicating centuries in small
capitals in roman type (*le xiᵉ siècle, xiᵉ siècle*) but full capitals when italic. Use upper-case Roman
numerals for numbers belonging to proper nouns (*Louis XIV*), but Arabic numerals for Paris
arrondissement numbers (*le 16ᵉ arrondissement*). In figures, use thin spaces to divide thousands
(*20 250*), but don't space dates or numbers in general contexts (*l'an 1466, page 1250*). Times of day
written as figures should be spaced (*10 h 15 min 10 s*; formerly also printed *10ʰ 15ᵐ 10ˢ*).

### 12.6 Gaelic (Irish and Scots Gaelic)

**12.6.1 Alphabet and accents.** Both Irish and Scots Gaelic use an eighteen-letter alphabet lacking
*j, k, q, v, w, x, y, z* (except in some modern loanwords); in Irish the lower-case *i* is sometimes left
undotted. Until the mid-twentieth century Irish (but not Scots Gaelic) was often written and printed in
**insular script**, a medieval form of Latin handwriting; a dot marked aspirated consonants in that
script. In Roman script, aspirated consonants are instead marked by an added *h*, in both Irish and
Scots Gaelic (Irish *an chos* "the foot," Scots Gaelic *a' chas*). Irish marks vowel length with the
acute (*á, é, í, ó, ú*); Scots Gaelic marks it with the grave (*à, è, ì, ò, ù*). Apostrophes are more
frequent in Scots Gaelic than in Irish.

**12.6.2 Mutations.** As in Scots Gaelic and Welsh, Irish Gaelic replaces initial consonants with others
in certain grammatical contexts, a process called **mutation**. In Irish, the mutation called
**eclipsis** is indicated by writing the sound actually pronounced *before* the modified consonant:
*mb, gc, nd, bhf, ng, bp, dt*. If the noun is a proper name, it keeps its capital, with the prefixed
letter(s) lower case: *i mBaile Átha Cliath* "in Dublin" (*Baile Átha Cliath* = Dublin); *na bhFrancach*
"of the French." The same lower-case-letter-plus-capital pattern occurs when *h* or *n* is prefixed to a
vowel-initial name (*go hÉirinn* "to Ireland," *Tír na nÓg* "the Land of the Young"), or *t* is prefixed
to a vowel or *S* (*an tAigéan Atlantach* "the Atlantic Ocean," *an tSionainn* "Shannon"). Before
lower-case vowels, *h* is prefixed directly (*na hoíche* "of the night"), as is *t* before *s* (*an
tsráid* "the street") — but *n* and *t* take a **hyphen** before a vowel (*in-áit* "in a place," *an
t-uisce* "the water").

**Explicit dialectal contrast (the one most worth flagging in this section, per the coverage rule): the
source states Irish's hyphenation pattern above, then immediately gives a second, differently-sourced
rule set — distinguishable as Scots Gaelic both by context and because its examples use the Scots
grave-accent spelling convention (*sràid* rather than Irish *sráid*) — that "prefixed *h-, n-,* and
*t-* always take a hyphen": *an t-sràid* "the street," *Ar n-Athair* "Our Father," *na h-oidhche* "of
the night," *na h-Eileanan an Iar* "the Western Isles."** In other words, where Irish only hyphenates
*n-* and *t-* before a vowel (and prefixes *h-*/*t-before-s* directly, no hyphen, before a lower-case
vowel/*s*), Scots Gaelic hyphenates all three prefixed mutation letters unconditionally — a genuine,
source-marked divergence between the two languages' mutation orthography, not just a spelling variant
of the same rule. Also noted: "except in dialects, eclipsis is not found with consonants in Scots
Gaelic" — i.e., the consonant-eclipsis pattern described above for Irish is, per the source, essentially
absent from standard Scots Gaelic.

### 12.7 German

**12.7.1 Accents and special sorts.** German uses the diacritics *Ä, Ö, Ü, ä, ö, ü*, and the special
sort **Eszett** (ß), which differs from a Greek beta (β) and should not be substituted for it.

**12.7.2 Orthographic reform.** A new orthography agreed by the German-speaking countries took effect
1 August 1998; a seven-year transitional period, during which both systems were official, ended 31 July
2005 — the older forms are now considered incorrect, though still widely used. The reform mainly
eliminates irregularities that caused difficulty for native speakers, while also permitting more
(restricted) variation — old and new spellings may not be freely mixed. Some words were adjusted to
resemble related words: *numerieren* and *plazieren* become *nummerieren* and *platzieren*, to match the
related nouns *Nummer* ("number") and *Platz* ("place"). In verbal compounds, nouns and adjectives
retaining their normal independent function are now written separately — *radfahren* → *Rad fahren* "to
ride a bicycle" — but not when the compound has a distinct, non-literal meaning: *irreführen* "to
mislead," *wahrsagen* "to predict." Long-established loanwords have been Germanized (*Tip* → *Tipp*,
"tip"), and *ee* substituted for *é* in some (*Varietee* for *Varieté*, "music hall"). The optional use
of *f* for *ph* is extended (*Delfin* "dolphin," *Orthografie* "orthography") but not to words deemed
more learned (*Philosophie, Physik*). The Eszett was traditionally used in place of a double *s* at the
end of a syllable, before a consonant, and after long vowels/diphthongs; the new rules restrict ß to
after a long vowel (including *ie*) or diphthong — *Fuß, Füße* "foot, feet" — while a short vowel takes
*ss*: *Kuss, Küsse* "kiss, kisses"; but contrast *ihr esst, ihr aßt* "you [pl.] eat, ate" with *wir
essen, wir aßen* "we eat, ate." The Eszett is considered archaic in Swiss German, where *ss* is
preferred throughout; no corresponding capital or small-capital letterform exists for ß, so *SS*/*ss*
is substituted, and ß alphabetizes as *ss*, not *sz*. Absent specific contrary instructions or evidence
the text isn't by a native speaker, the new rules apply by default in all matter; quotations from
old-spelling publications retain the old style, but new editions normally modernize.

**12.7.3 Abbreviations.** A full point follows an abbreviation that would be read out in full (a space
after any internal points is optional, though usual in formal writing): *d. h.* (*das heißt*, "that
is"), *Dr.* (*Doktor*), *Prof.* (*Professor*). A full point also follows numerals used for days of the
month and ordinal numbers (*Montag, den 12. August*, "Monday, 12 August"; *der 2. Weltkrieg*, "the
Second World War"). No points are used in abbreviations that are pronounced as such (*DM* for *die
Deutsche Mark*; *KG* for *Kommanditgesellschaft*, "limited partnership"). As with French, the source
gives an extended reference list of common German abbreviations (pp. 219–220/PDF 236–237) — not
reproduced here per copyright discipline.

**12.7.4 Capitalization.** All German nouns are written with an initial capital, as are other words
(adjectives, numerals, infinitives) used as nouns: *Gutes und Böses* "good and evil"; *Die Drei ist eine
heilige Zahl* "Three is a sacred number." The basic noun-capitalization rule is untouched by the reform,
but words previously treated as lower-case adverbs have changed: *heute abend* ("tonight") is now *heute
Abend*, *morgen abend* becomes *morgen Abend*, *gestern morgen* becomes *gestern Morgen*. Adjectives
used as nouns are now capitalized with fewer exceptions than before (*alles übrige* becomes *alles
Übrige*, "everything else"). Under the new rules, the familiar second-person pronoun forms (*du, dich,
dir, dein, ihr, euch, euer*) are no longer capitalized in letters and the like. The old rule survives for
pronouns given a special polite-address sense, to distinguish them from their ordinary value: *Ihr*
addressed to a single person (medieval and Swiss contexts), *Er* and *Sie* (feminine singular, early
modern contexts), and (nowadays) *Sie* (plural) — all capitalized in oblique cases and the possessive,
but not in the reflexive *sich*. Capitalize adjectives that are part of a geographical name (or formed
from one), and the names of historic events/eras, monuments, institutions, titles, and special/feast
days; otherwise, don't capitalize adjectives of nationality (*das deutsche Volk*, "the German nation")
or language names used adverbially (*italienisch sprechen*, "to speak Italian"). Traditionally,
adjectives derived from personal names were capitalized in certain contexts, but under the new rules all
such adjectives are lower case, except when the name is marked off with an apostrophe (*das ohmsche
Gesetz* or *das Ohm'sche Gesetz*, "Ohm's Law"). In work titles, the first word and all nouns are
capitalized, with every other word lower-case initial.

**12.7.5 Punctuation.** German has very specific rules about comma placement; editors should not
interfere with the punctuation of quoted matter without reference to the author or source. Sentences
containing an imperative normally end in an exclamation mark. The traditional practice of ending a
letter's salutation with an exclamation mark (*Sehr geehrter Herr Schmidt!*, "Dear Herr Schmidt") has
largely given way to a comma, after which the letter body does not begin with a capital unless otherwise
required. German rarely uses the en rule in compounds the way English does, preferring a hyphen between
words (*die Berlin-Bagdad-Eisenbahn*, "the Berlin–Baghdad railway"); the en rule is used for page and
date ranges (*S. 348–349, 1749–1832*) — don't elide such ranges — and, with a word space on each side,
as a dash.
- *Quotation marks (Anführungszeichen):* take the form of two commas at the start and two opening
  (turned) quotation marks at the end of the quotation (*„…"*), or reversed guillemets (*»…«*).
  Quotations within quotations use a single comma at the start and a single opening quotation mark at
  the end (*‚…'*). No space separates the marks from the quotation. A colon introduces direct speech;
  commas following a quotation fall after the closing mark, but full points go inside it if they belong
  to the quotation.
- *Apostrophe:* marks elision of *e* for colloquial usage (*Wie geht's?*, "How are things?"/"How are
  you?"). When a sentence begins with an apostrophe, the following letter does **not** become a capital
  (*'s brennt!*, "Fire!," not *'S brennt*). The apostrophe also marks the suppression of the possessive
  *s* (for euphony) after names ending in *s, ß, x, z*: *Aristoteles' Werke, Horaz' Oden*.
- *Hyphen:* traditionally a noun following a hyphen begins with a capital (*das Schiller-Museum*), but
  the new orthography permits running the words together (*das Schillermuseum*). The hyphen was used to
  avoid repeating a vowel twice in a row (*Kaffee-Ersatz*, "coffee substitute") but not to avoid
  repeating a consonant (*stickstofffrei*, "nitrogen-free"). The new rules no longer require a hyphen
  even where three identical consonants are written out before a vowel, or where *sss* results from the
  abolition of ß after a short vowel: *Brennnessel, Schifffahrt, Schlußsatz* → *Schlusssatz*. It remains
  permissible to clarify such compounds with a hyphen: *Brenn-Nessel, Schiff-Fahrt, Schluss-Satz*.

**12.7.6 Word division.** Distinguishes simple from compound words.
- *Simple words:* don't divide one-syllable words. Divide other simple words by syllable, either
  between consonants or after a vowel followed by a single consonant — this applies even to *x* and
  mute *h* (*Bo-xer, verge-hen*). Don't separate *ch, ph, sch, ß*, and *th* (each representing a single
  sound): *spre-chen, wa-schen, So-phie, ka-tholisch, wech-seln, Wechs-ler*. Traditionally *st* was
  included in this non-separable group, but under the new rules it is divided: *Las-ten, Meis-ter,
  Fens-ter*. At a line's end, take over ß as a unit (*hei-ßen, genie-ßen*); take over *ss* standing in
  for ß as a unit, but divide genuine *ss* normally (*las-sen*). Traditionally a word broken at *ck* was
  respelled with *kk* (*Zucker* but *Zuk-ker*; *Glocken* but *Glok-ken*); under the new orthography *ck*
  is taken over whole (*Zu-cker, Glo-cken*), as was already traditional after a consonant or in proper
  nouns/their derivatives (*Fran-cke, bismar-ckisch*). Words with suffixes are treated as simple words
  and divided by the rules above (*Bäcke-rei, le-bend, Liefe-rung*).
- *Compound words:* divide by etymological constituents (*Bildungs-roman, Kriminal-polizei,
  strom-auf*) or within one of its elements; divide prefixes from the root word (*be-klagen,
  emp-fehlen, er-obern, aus-trinken*).

**12.7.7 Numerals.** Separate numbers of more than four figures with thin spaces by thousands:
*6 580 340*. A full point after a numeral shows it represents an ordinal number (*14. Auflage*, "14th
edition"; *Mittwoch, den 19. Juli 1995*). The full point also marks the separation of hours from
minutes: *14.30 Uhr* or *14³⁰ Uhr*. Germans rarely use Roman numerals: even when citing Roman page
numbers, these are often converted to Arabic with an asterisk (*S. 78\** for p. lxxviii) — distinguished
from *1\**, denoting the first page of an article that is actually (say) the third or fifth page of a
pamphlet.

**12.7.8 Historical and specialist setting.** The traditional black-letter German types Fraktur and
Schwabacher were replaced by Roman Antiqua in 1941; they're now found only to a limited extent, mostly
in decorative or historical contexts. Matter to be set in them should be treated as a quotation.
Word division in such matter should follow the pre-1998 rules, not the new ones — in particular, the
*st* ligature should be taken over as a unit. The long s (ſ) in Fraktur type is used at the beginnings
of words and within them, except at the ends of syllables; the short final *s* (ſ) is generally put at
the ends of syllables and words, except before *p* (*Knospe*). Table 12.2 (not reproduced) tabulates
the Fraktur alphabet's upper- and lower-case letterforms against their Roman equivalents, plus common
Fraktur ligatures (*ch, ck, ff, fi, fl, ll, si, ss/ß, st, tz*).

### 12.8 Greek (through §12.8.7, cut off at the page-range boundary)

**12.8.1 Introduction.** Ancient and modern Greek show a remarkable similarity, and most of the
chapter's Greek guidance covers both. Modern Greek has historically divided into two forms:
**katharevousa** (literally "purified"), a heavily archaized form used in technical and Church
contexts and written in polytonic script, and **demotic**, the spoken, everyday form, which uses
monotonic orthography and has been the official form since 1976, used in official documents.

**12.8.2 Alphabet.** Ancient and modern Greek share a 24-letter alphabet: 17 consonants and 7 vowels
(*α, ε, η, ι, ο, υ, ω*). Modern Greek is written left-to-right, as ancient Greek was from the classical
period onward. Table 12.3 (not reproduced) tabulates all 24 letters' upper/lower-case forms, names, and
standard transliterations. In ancient Greek, the **final sigma** (the word-ending form, ς) must be
distinguished from **stigma** (ϛ \[sic in source]), used for the numeral 6 and, in late manuscripts and
early printed books, for the στ ligature; stigma is also used in scholarly work (especially on Latin
authors) to denote "late manuscripts." Papyri and inscriptions normally print sigma as **lunate** (Ϲ,
ϲ), with no separate final form — also sometimes used in other ancient contexts, but never in modern
Greek. Texts of early inscriptions may require an italic Latin *h* (in a sloping font), plus the letters
**digamma** (wau, Ϝ) and **koppa** (Ϙ). An iota forming a "long diphthong" with a preceding *α, η*, or
*ω* is traditionally written beneath the vowel (*ᾳ, ῃ, ῳ*, "iota subscript"); some modern scholars
prefer writing it after the vowel on the line instead (*αι, ηι, ωι*, "adscript iota"), in which case
accents and breathings are set on the first vowel — for *αι* this means the accent may fall on either
letter depending on pronunciation. When a word is set in capitals, iota is always adscript; when the
word has an initial capital but is otherwise lower case, an initial long diphthong puts the main vowel
as the capital with the iota adscript and lower case — so a lower-case ᾠ becomes ΩΙ in full capitals,
but Ωι at the start of a paragraph. Modern Greek omits the iota even in phrases taken directly from
ancient Greek.

**12.8.3 Transliteration.** Whether to transliterate ancient or modern Greek depends on context: Greek
script is off-putting to most readers, but readers who know Greek find transliteration harder to follow
than more than a few words at a time. Works intended only for specialists should keep all Greek in the
Greek alphabet; works aimed at ordinary readers should transliterate individual words or short phrases
(e.g. "history" from the Greek word *historiē*), though longer extracts should stay in Greek script with
a translation. Table 12.3 also shows the usual transliteration scheme for each letter.

**12.8.4 Accents.** The accents of ancient and modern Greek are the acute (´, usable on any of a word's
last three syllables), the grave (`, used only on final syllables), and the circumflex (ˆ, used on
either of the last two syllables). The diaeresis (¨) shows that two adjacent vowels don't form a
diphthong. Words of three or more syllables (and most others) carry an accent. Most unaccented words
cause the *preceding* word to take an acute on its final syllable; these unaccented words are known as
**enclitics**, the rest (all monosyllables) as **proclitics**. An acute on a final syllable or
monosyllable appears before an enclitic, before punctuation, and in the two words *τίς* and *τί* when
they mean "who?" and "what?"; otherwise it's replaced by a grave, though modern Greek sometimes retains
the acute anyway. Greek also uses marks called **breathings** to show the presence or absence of an
aspirate at a word's start: the **asper** (rough breathing, ʽ) and **lenis** (smooth breathing, ʼ).
Breathings are used on every word beginning with a vowel or diphthong, and also with ρ (rho) — in this
case, and that of *υ* and *υι*, the breathing will nearly always be asper. Table 12.4 (not reproduced)
tabulates the full set of combined accent/breathing/diaeresis glyphs. Each accent may combine with
either breathing or the diaeresis, but breathing and diaeresis never stand on the same letter; the
accent always sits over the diaeresis, and the breathing stands to the left of an acute or grave but
underneath a circumflex. Except for long diphthongs (described above), accents are placed over the
second vowel of a diphthong (always *ι* or *υ*). Accents and breathings are used regularly in mixed
capital/lower-case setting — preceding capitals, set over lower-case letters — and are omitted when a
word is set wholly in capitals. Many modern printers dispense with breathings altogether and use only a
single accent mark (either a small downward-pointing filled triangle, or simply the acute); as before,
the accent is omitted in capitals but otherwise precedes capitals and stands over lower-case letters.
Monosyllables, even when stressed, don't normally take an accent — **except** that *ἤ* "or" is
distinguished from *ή* "the" (nominative singular feminine) \[**low_confidence**: the precise
breathing/accent combination on this specific contrastive pair is difficult to render with full
certainty even from a clear page image, given how finely the two glyphs differ — flagged rather than
guessed, per the extraction spec's vision-reading-guard default]. The diaeresis remains in use to show
that *αϊ* and *οϊ* **are** diphthongs, as opposed to the digraphs *αι* (pronounced /e/) and *οι*
(pronounced /i/).

**12.8.5 Capitalization and punctuation.** In printed ancient Greek, the first word of a sentence or a
line of verse is capitalized only at the start of a paragraph. In modern Greek, capitals mark new
sentences, though not always new lines of verse. For titles of works in ancient Greek, it is best to
capitalize only the first word and proper nouns, or else proper nouns only; in modern Greek the first
word and main words tend to be capitalized, though "first word and proper nouns only" is the rule
followed in bibliographies. In ancient Greek it is conventional to capitalize adjectives and adverbs
derived from proper nouns, but not verbs: *Ἕλλην* "a Greek," *Ἑλληνιστί* "in Greek," but *ἑλληνίζω* "I
speak Greek/behave like a Greek." In modern Greek, lower case is the rule throughout: *ελληνικός*
"Greek (adj.)," *ελληνικά* "in Greek." The comma, full point, and exclamation mark are the same as in
English; but the question mark is shaped like the English semicolon (;) — set italic where necessary to
match a sloping Greek font — and the colon is a raised full point (·). Use double quotation marks, or,
in modern Greek, guillemets.

**12.8.6 Word division.** In ancient Greek, the overriding principle is to break after prefixes and
before suffixes, and between the elements of compound words; this requires knowledge of the language,
since many prefixes can't be distinguished at sight (*ἔν-αυε* "lit" contains a prefix; *ἔναιε* "dwelt"
does not). A vowel may be divided from another vowel unless they form a diphthong (*λύ-ων*). Take over
any combination of a "mute" consonant (*β, γ, δ, θ, κ, π, τ, φ, χ*) followed by a "liquid" (*λ, μ, ν,
ρ*), also *βδ, γδ, κτ, πτ, φθ, χθ*, or any of these followed by ρ; also *μν*; and σ followed by a
consonant other than σ or one of the above groups divides with the following consonant. Any doubled
consonant may be divided; *λ, μ, ν*, and *ρ* may also be divided from a following consonant (except
*μν*); divide γ from a following κ or χ; take over ξ and ψ as units, and divide between vowels. Modern
Greek word division follows ancient principles, but the consonant groups taken over are restricted to
those that can begin a modern Greek word — so *θμ* is divided, but *γκ, μπ, ντ, τζ, τσ* are not.

## Grammar points

### Phoneme vs. allophone vs. phonetic/phonemic transcription (Ch. 1)

The book distinguishes a **phonemic** transcription (one symbol per phoneme, in oblique slashes,
e.g. /pɑːk/ for *park*) from a **phonetic**/**allophonic** transcription (finer-grained, in square
brackets, e.g. [pʰɑːk] showing the aspirated release). Table 1.1 (p. 11) gives the full RP phoneme
inventory used throughout the book: vowels ɪ, e, æ, ʌ, ɒ, ʊ, ə, iː, ɑː, ɔː, uː, ɜː; diphthongs eɪ,
aɪ, ɔɪ, əʊ, aʊ, ɪə, eə, ʊə; fricative consonants f, θ, s, ʃ, h, v, ð, z, ʒ; stop consonants p, t,
k, tʃ, b, d, g, dʒ; sonorants m, n, ŋ, l, j, w, r. Table 1.2 (p. 13) adds non-English reference
symbols used for comparison (French/German vowels, Scots/German fricatives x and ç, the voiced
velar fricative ɣ, the glottal stop ʔ).

### Phoneme/allophone and functional load (Ch. 1)

A phoneme's resistance to merging with another phoneme depends on its **functional load** — how
much work the contrast does distinguishing real word pairs — not just on how similar the two
sounds are. The book's worked example: English /ð/ vs. /θ/ carries very low functional load (few
minimal pairs: *wreath*/*wreathe*, *mouth* noun/verb) and so is a "stable but low-effort-to-lose"
contrast, whereas the three-way plosive/nasal series /p t k/ – /b d g/ – /m n ŋ/ is tightly
integrated (shared places of articulation × three manner features = 9 phonemes from only 3
articulatory positions) and highly resistant to loss even where an individual member like /ŋ/
carries a modest load, because removing it would leave a structurally "unused" gap in the pattern.

### Bound vs. free morphemes; lexical vs. grammatical word classes (Ch. 1)

A **free morpheme** can stand alone as a word (*fill*); a **bound morpheme** cannot (*re-*, *-ly*).
**Lexical words** (nouns, verbs, adjectives) form an open class that constantly gains new coinages
and loses obsolete items — the book notes nobody could ever list all of Modern English's nouns
exhaustively, since new ones are coined continuously and old ones silently go obsolete. **Grammatical
words** (pronouns, conjunctions, auxiliaries, determiners, and some prepositions) form a closed
class that changes far more slowly — illustrated with the English personal-pronoun system, which
has lost *thou*/*thee*/*thy*/*thine* over roughly four centuries but gained no new pronoun by simple
invention in that time (unlike nouns, individuals can't just invent a workable new pronoun).

### Word-order typology (Ch. 1)

Of the six logically possible Subject/Verb/Object orderings, five are attested in living languages
(S-V-O is the most common type, including Modern English; S-O-V is also common, e.g. Classical Latin,
Turkish; V-S-O exists, e.g. Welsh; O-S-V probably exists in a few South American languages; O-V-S is
the rarest). The book also introduces the **operand–operator** vs. **operator–operand** language
classification (whether a head precedes or follows its modifier across categories simultaneously) and
notes English is a largely-but-imperfectly operand–operator language (Verb precedes Object, as
expected, but adjectives precede rather than follow their noun, which is the operator–operand
pattern) — flagged as a methodological caution that no language conforms perfectly to either ideal
type. **Old English specifically is noted as having used both prepositions and postpositions**,
unlike Modern English's prepositions-only system.

### Morphological typology: analytic/synthetic and isolating/agglutinative/flectional/polysynthetic (Ch. 1)

Two overlapping four-part typological schemes are introduced: (1) **analytic** (few bound
morphemes, e.g. Chinese) vs. **synthetic** (many bound morphemes combined per word, e.g. Inuit
languages, Turkish) as a continuum, not a binary — **English has moved measurably toward the
analytic end of this continuum over its own recorded history**, which is directly relevant to why
Old English's case/agreement morphology (see pt03) collapsed while Modern English relies more on
word order and prepositions. (2) The finer **isolating / agglutinative / flectional (inflectional)
/ polysynthetic** scheme: isolating = no bound forms at all (Vietnamese); agglutinative = bound
morphemes strung together with clear-cut boundaries, each signaling one feature (Turkish,
Finnish); flectional = a single bound morpheme fuses multiple features inseparably (Latin
*dominōrum* "of the masters," one ending signaling plural+genitive+gender at once); polysynthetic =
large numbers of both grammatical and lexical morphemes compound into one word (Inuit languages).
The book flags that the Inuit languages are both agglutinative *and* polysynthetic, so these four
classes aren't strictly mutually exclusive.

### Language universals and implicational tendencies (Ch. 1)

Chomsky's innateness hypothesis (a genetically transmitted "language organ" producing shared
underlying structure across all languages) is presented alongside the competing view that some
universals may instead have psychological/functional explanations rather than being innate. The
book distinguishes **absolute universals** (e.g. all languages have oral vowels) from
**implicational universals/tendencies** of the "if A, then B" form (e.g. V-S-O word order
implies prepositions, no known exceptions; S-O-V order *tends toward* postpositions but Classical
Latin is a counter-example, so this one is a tendency, not an absolute).

### Sound change mechanisms (Ch. 2)

The book organizes sound change around a tension between **minimization of effort** (economizing
articulatory movement — assimilation, consonant-cluster simplification, word-final /-n/ loss in
Middle English unstressed syllables, e.g. *baken* "to bake" > *bake*) and **the needs of
communication** (which resist changes that would create confusing homophony, and can even *add*
material back in, e.g. epenthetic /d/ inserted into OE *þunor* > *thunder*, or /b/ inserted into
*thimble*/*bramble* from OE *þymel*/*brēmel*, both attributed to slight mistiming between the
articulatory gestures for a nasal consonant and a following liquid). A **sound law** is explicitly
defined as regular but not universal or instantaneous: it may begin in frequently-used words and
spread gradually, and may stop operating before every eligible word is affected — so irregular
"holdouts" from an otherwise-regular change are expected, not anomalies to explain away.

### Analogy as a regularizing force (Ch. 2)

Analogy is presented as the main mechanism by which children's (and sometimes adults') errors
become real, accepted language change over time: a child generalizes a productive pattern
(*dog/dogs*, *bed/beds* → new word *plug* gets *plugs*) to irregular forms too (*man/mouse* →
*mans/mouses*), and while childhood errors of this kind are usually corrected, the same pressure
operating over centuries is how Old English's many plural-formation classes (*stān/stānas* "stone/
stones," *scip/scipu* "ship/ships," *tunge/tungan* "tongue/tongues," *bēo/bēon* "bee/bees," *bōc/
bēc* "book/books," *lamb/lambru* "lamb/lambs") collapsed almost entirely into the single modern
*-(e)s* pattern — the OE *-as* plural (as in *stānas*) is the historical ancestor of the now-dominant
ending. The handful of Modern English irregular plurals that **resisted** this leveling (*men*,
*feet*, *children*, and formerly-common-but-now-rare words like *geese*, *oxen*) are explained as
surviving specifically because they were (or still are) very high-frequency words — analogy
preferentially levels *rare* irregular forms first, since speakers have fewer tokens of them to
reinforce the irregular pattern.

### The comparative Luke 15 passage (Ch. 2) — the book's core historical-attestation device

The book's central evidentiary device for the whole chapter is rendering the same Biblical passage
(the end of the parable of the Prodigal Son, Luke 15) in four forms, used here as the book's own
primary source for period-dated vocabulary and grammar (captured in the Vocabulary table above):

1. **New English Bible (1961)** — presented as the "present-day English" baseline, idiomatic but
   unremarkable to a modern reader.
2. **King James Bible (1611)** — Early Modern English. Grammar points the book draws out explicitly:
   *thou*/*thee* (subject/accusative) with associated possessive *thy*, and the verb inflection
   *-est*/*-st* after *thou* (*gauest*, *hast*); the perfect tense of *to come* formed with auxiliary
   *be* rather than *have* ("thy brother **is** come," not "has come" — this construction has since
   died out in Modern English, which now uses *have* uniformly for the perfect); relative pronoun
   *which* used for a person ("thy sonne... **which** hath deuoured") where Modern English requires
   *who*; Verb-Subject-Object and Verb-Subject word orders in clauses like "neither transgressed I...
   thy commandement" and "therefore came his father out"; demonstrative-determiner *this*/*thy*
   co-occurring before a noun ("this thy sonne," "this thy brother") where Modern English would say
   "this son of yours." The book notes the pronoun usage is realistically anachronistic even for
   1611 — a father in Shakespeare's own time could address a son as *thou*, but the son could not
   reciprocate without being insultingly familiar, and would have had to use *you*/*ye*; the
   translation's uniform *thou* reflects the Greek source text's grammar, not natural 1611 English
   social usage.
3. **Wycliffe's Bible (translated before his death in 1384)** — Middle English. Grammar points:
   noun plural ending *-is* (*thyngis*); verb plural endings *-en*/*-n* (*weren*, *ben*); past-tense
   verb ending *-ide* (*clepide*, *axide*); past participle ending *-n* (*comen*, *founden*).
   Orthographic points: only *u* is used (not *v*), consistent with Wycliffe's own period, though the
   two letters were used interchangeably and not yet phonemically distributed as vowel-vs-consonant
   until roughly 1630 in English printing generally; *j* does not yet exist as a distinct letter from
   *i* (the two were graphic variants of one letter); the letters *ȝ* ("yogh," descended from Old
   English script) and *g* (used for writing Latin, later extended to English after the Conquest)
   coexist, with *ȝ* corresponding variously to modern *y* (*ȝeeris* "years") or modern *gh*,
   pronounced [ç] (*nei3ede* "approached/drew nigh"). Word order in this passage is noted as very
   close to Modern English's.
4. **Anglo-Saxon Gospel manuscript (early 11th century)** — Old English. Grammar points: long vowels
   marked with a macron in manuscripts, short vowels unmarked, a distinction lost in Modern English
   spelling; letters þ ("thorn," = *th*) and æ ("ash," the vowel of RP *hat*); the **dative case**
   marked by suffix *-e* added to a noun after certain prepositions (*on æcere* "in the field," vs.
   bare *þām hūs* "the house") — i.e. case marking that conditions differently on *which* preposition
   governs the noun, not uniformly; the normal past-tense form of "to be" is *wæs*, but a distinct
   subjunctive form *wǣre* is required in certain constructions (e.g. indirect questions: *ācsode
   hine hwæt þæt wǣre*, "asked him what it was") — the book notes this OE subjunctive survives today
   only as a fossilized relic, e.g. "if I **were** rich." **Word order**: this single short passage
   contains all three attested OE clause-order patterns side by side — ordinary S-V-O ("he heard the
   noise and the crowd"), V-S-O after a fronted adverbial like *þā* "then" or *þǣr* "there" ("then
   called he a servant"), and S-O-V in subordinate clauses introduced by conjunctions like *forþām
   þe* "because" or *þā* "when" ("because him safe received," "when he the house approached"). The
   book identifies the subsequent loss of the S-O-V and V-S-O patterns (S-O-V disappeared by the
   early Middle Ages, V-S-O became rare after the mid-17th century, surviving only marginally today
   in forms like "Down the road came a whole crowd of children") as one of the major syntactic
   changes in the history of English, with S-V-O becoming the uniform default.

### Periodization of the history of English (Ch. 2)

The book gives its own explicit, named periodization, used as the chapter structure for the rest of
the book (and the basis for this extraction's own `Attested Era` tagging in later `073_*` parts):
**Old English** = first Anglo-Saxon settlements (5th century AD) to c. 1100; **Middle English** =
c. 1100 to c. 1500; **Modern English** = c. 1500 to the present. Named subperiods: **Late Old
English** (c. 900–1100) and **Early Modern English** (c. 1500–1650). The book explicitly notes no
exact boundaries can be drawn between these periods — they're convenient divisions over genuinely
continuous change, not sharp cutoffs.

### Language families and the Indo-European preview (Ch. 2)

Chapter 2 previews material Chapter 3 covers in depth: divergent development of dialects into
separate daughter languages via reduced inter-community contact (worked through a hypothetical
two-village/migration scenario), illustrated concretely with the five modern national Romance
languages (Italian, Spanish, Portuguese, French, Romanian) plus non-national Romance languages
(Romansh, Provençal, Catalan, Sardinian) descended from Latin, and a survey of other world language
families (Semitic, Hamitic, Ural-Altaic [Finno-Ugrian + Altaic], Sino-Tibetan, Malayo-Polynesian,
several African families, plus isolates like Basque and small families like the Caucasian group).
The competing/complementary process of **convergent development** (dialect/language leveling toward
a single dominant prestige form, e.g. Arabic's spread with Islam, Attic Greek's *koinē*) is
introduced as the countervailing force to divergence — directly relevant background for why Modern
English itself standardized around one Middle English dialect (East Midland) rather than remaining
as divergent as Middle English's many written dialect forms were.

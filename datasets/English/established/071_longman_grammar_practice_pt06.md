## Grammar points

### 6.1 Formation of adjectives

**6.1A Adjective-forming suffixes.** A productive but non-predictable set of suffixes derive
adjectives from verbs or nouns — see the Vocabulary table; -ing forms are likewise usable as
adjectives (running water), p.80.

**6.1B Adjective-negating prefixes.** A closed set of prefixes (un-, in-, il-, im-, dis-, ir-)
generally reverse an adjective's meaning, with the specific prefix determined by the following
sound/spelling (largely unpredictable and must be learned per word); *pre-* and *hyper-* instead
modify the meaning without creating a true opposite, p.80.

**6.1C Compound adjectives of measurement.** Numbers combine with a *singular* noun and hyphens to
form a compound adjective, preferred over an "of"-phrase, and extending across many measurement
domains (age, volume, length, price, weight, area, duration, depth, distance), p.81.

### 6.2 Position of adjectives

**6.2A Form and position of most adjectives.** Adjectives are invariant for the number/gender of
their noun, and are used either attributively (before the noun) or predicatively (standing alone
after *be, seem*, etc.), p.82.

**6.2B Adjectives that change meaning by position.** A specific set of adjectives carry one meaning
before a noun and a different meaning after *be* — see the Vocabulary table, p.82.

**6.2C Adjectives that can appear on either side of the noun, with different meanings.** A smaller,
distinct set of adjectives can themselves appear either before or after the noun they modify, with
the position itself (not just the presence/absence of *be*) determining the meaning, p.83.

### 6.3 Adjectives that behave like nouns; -ed/-ing endings

**6.3A 'The' + adjective + noun; adjective as group noun.** Ordinarily a noun (or *one/ones*) must
follow an adjective; the plural uses zero article. A specific closed list of adjectives, however, can
follow *the* alone to refer to an entire class of people as a group — but these group-nouns are never
used in the singular or given a plural -s; the ordinary adjective + "man/woman/people" construction is
used instead for an individual, p.84.

**6.3B -ed/-ing adjective pairs.** Past-participle (-ed) and present-participle (-ing) forms of many
verbs function as a systematic adjective pair: the -ed form describes someone *experiencing* a
feeling, the -ing form describes the thing or person *causing* it. The same pattern extends to a
handful of similarly-paired but differently-suffixed adjectives (delighted/delightful,
impressed/impressive), p.85.

### 6.4 Adjectives after 'be', 'seem', etc.; word order of adjectives

**6.4A Adjective vs. adverb after sense/linking verbs.** *Be, look, feel, seem, smell, taste, sound*
take an adjective complement describing the subject; other (action) verbs take a true adverb
describing how the action is performed — contrast "look good" (appearance, adjective) with "play
well" (manner, adverb), p.86.

**6.4B Word order in two- and three-word compound nouns.** Material nouns function adjectivally to
form compound nouns; a three-word compound places the material element before the purpose/use
element, and an ordinary adjective cannot be inserted inside an existing compound noun, p.86.

**6.4C Past participle + compound noun.** An adjectival past participle (*damaged, handmade*)
precedes the entire compound noun, regardless of whether that compound is one, two, or three words
long, p.86.

**6.4D Full multi-adjective word order.** When several adjectives stack before a noun, they follow a
fixed relative order — opinion/quality (most general/subjective) first, then size, age, shape,
colour, origin ("where from"), and past participle, with origin and past-participle order being
mutually flexible, immediately before the noun itself, p.87.

### 6.5 The comparison of adjectives

**6.5A Regular comparative/superlative formation.** One-syllable adjectives add -er/-est, doubling a
final single consonant after a short vowel (hot→hotter), adding just -r/-st after a silent -e
(nice→nicer), and changing -y to -i- after a consonant (busy→busier). Comparative compares one thing
to another single thing; superlative compares one thing to more than one other, p.88.

**6.5B Two-syllable adjectives.** A specific set of two-syllable adjectives (happy, clever, common,
narrow, pleasant, quiet, simple, stupid) optionally take either -er/-est or more/most; most other
two-syllable adjectives take only more/most; three-or-more-syllable adjectives always take only
more/most, p.88.

**6.5C Often-confused comparative/superlative forms.** A cluster of genuinely irregular or easily
confused comparative pairs each carry a specific, narrow usage rule — see the Vocabulary table for
the full set (further/farther, elder/eldest vs. older/oldest, the good/bad/much/little irregular
series, lesser, latest/last, and smaller/smallest vs. the non-standard child-form "littler"), p.89.

## Grammar points

### Ch.12 Non-Declarative Speech Acts

#### Preliminaries: four speech-act prototypes, and assertion vs. presupposition (§12.1)

Declarative speech-acts have historically dominated linguistic and philosophical description (a
bias Givón traces to Plato's narrowing of the Pre-Socratic sophists' broader four-part discourse —
wish, question, answer, command — down to truth-evaluable statements, p.240), but four prototypes
recur most robustly across languages: declarative, imperative, and interrogative (yes/no and WH).
Declarative, interrogative, and imperative clauses differ sharply in what counts as a coherent
response to them (table-style examples (2)–(5), pp.242–243): only declaratives can be felicitously
denied or affirmed as true/false. The reason is that **truth/falsity (epistemics) is not the
communicative goal of non-declarative speech-acts at all** — a felicitous challenge to a
non-declarative clause instead targets its **implicit presuppositions** (examples (6)–(9), p.243),
which for a WH-question concern the event's occurrence, the hearer's presumed knowledge, and the
hearer's presumed obligation to answer, and for an imperative concern the stated action's current
non-completion and the speaker's presumed authority to command it. Even declarative clauses carry
non-epistemic presuppositions of this kind (interest, relevance, legitimate reason for telling,
(10)–(11), p.244) — these shade into the domain of **the communicative contract**, the culturally-
shared norms (status, power, obligation, entitlement, propriety, motivation) governing all speech-
act types, more visible in non-declaratives but present throughout.

#### Yes/no questions: epistemic bias and focus (§12.2)

Contra the logical-tradition view of yes/no questions as neutral open alternatives, Bolinger (1978)
showed English yes/no questions are systematically **epistemically biased**: an affirmative-form
question ("Did John eat the salami?") is biased toward expecting a negative answer, a negative-form
question toward an affirmative answer, with tag-question variants ("John didn't eat the salami, did
he?" / "...did he?") further grading this bias scale (table (14), p.245), and still-finer variants
(`really`, `right?`, `do you think...`) elaborating it further. A **general coding principle** (19)
follows: the less inherent bias a message carries, the more explicit its coding must be — explaining
why fully-neutral **alternative questions** (overtly listing both/all options with `or`) require
more explicit marking than the default biased pattern, and why, when alternatives are hierarchically
organized, English marks the search direction itself (`or just` signals specific-to-generic search
direction; `or maybe (like)` signals the reverse, generic-to-specific, pp.246–247). Yes/no questions
are also frequently **focused** on a specific constituent via contrastive stress, and optional
(non-obligatory) adverbial constituents systematically **attract** this interrogative focus, exactly
paralleling the focus-attraction pattern already established for negation and assertion in chapter
10 (examples (21)–(23), pp.248–249). **Syntax**: yes/no questions combine intonation (always
present) with subject-AUX inversion; absent any other auxiliary, the dummy auxiliary `do` is
inserted and fronted, exactly as in negation (§12.2.2.1); a cleft-focus pattern is also available
for emphasized yes/no questions, applied as a further transformation of the corresponding declarative
cleft construction (§12.2.2.2).

#### WH-questions (§12.3)

WH-questions presuppose the bulk of their clause's content, placing only the single missing
information-chunk under **interrogative focus**, which may in principle land on any clausal
constituent — subject, object, indirect object, adverbial roles, even the whole verb phrase or event
(examples (31)–(32), pp.252–253); challenging the WH-question's presupposed background (rather than
its focused element) produces the characteristic infelicity illustrated in dialogue examples
(34)–(35), p.253–254. The **interrogative (WH-) pronoun** itself codes several semantic distinctions
of the missing element: human/non-human (`who(m)` vs. `what`), subject/object (`who` vs. `whom`,
nearly merged in American English, with `whom`'s survival best evidenced by hyper-correction into
contexts — like predicate nominals — where conservative grammar would call for `who`, example (38),
p.255), prepositional case-role (via a stranded preposition, `who...to/for/with/from`), adverbial
role (`where`/`when`/`how`/`why`), and definiteness/referentiality (the unrestricted pattern accepts
any answer type; `which one` restricts to definite answers only; `what kind of` restricts to
non-referring/type answers only; combining `which`+noun or `what kind of`+noun further narrows by
class/number/gender, §12.3.1.2, pp.256–257). **Quantifier WH-questions** (`how many`/`how much`,
`how big`/`how hard`) separately query a count/mass quantity or an extent-of-adjective/adverb
(§12.3.1.3). A rare **multiple-WH-question** pattern (`Who gave what to whom?`) is used specifically
when the speaker has misheard or disbelieved more than one element of a prior turn describing
parallel events (§12.3.1.4, p.259). **Syntax**: WH-pronouns are obligatorily pre-posed (clause-
initial) and combine with subject-AUX inversion exactly as in yes/no questions, except for subject-
WH-questions, which show no inversion since the WH-word already occupies subject position (§12.3.2.1);
colloquial **un-inverted** WH-questions (WH-word in situ, heavily stressed) mark disbelief/
mishearing, parallel to the multiple-WH pattern (§12.3.2.2); a **clefted** WH-question pattern also
exists (§12.3.2.3). A subset of P-C-U verbs allow genuine **embedded WH-complements** (`I know who
came first`), but Givón argues on distributional grounds (restriction to epistemic verbs, absence of
subject-AUX inversion, close semantic/structural parallel to a headless REL-clause paraphrase) that
these are not literally embedded WH-questions but rather **headless relative clauses**, a "syntactic
hybrid" created by analogy between two existing structures (§12.3.2.4, pp.262–264).

#### Manipulative speech-acts and the communicative contract (§12.4)

Manipulative (imperative-family) speech-acts are governed by three conventions of the communicative
contract (69): the desired state must differ from the current one; the hearer must be free (have
"power") to act; and the speaker must have legitimate authority over the hearer — violating any one
renders a canonical imperative like "Get up!" infelicitous (p.264–265). The balance of relative
status/power between speaker and hearer governs both the obligation to comply and the need for
deference (71), motivating a graded scale from the canonical imperative down to a highly deferential
request (72), achieved by systematically combining weakening devices: increased length, question
form, explicit mention of the addressee pronoun, irrealis marking, negative form, and embedding
under a modality/cognition verb (73). **Syntax of the canonical imperative**: bare-stem verb form,
typically subjectless (since the addressee is always `you`), with the progressive and perfect
aspects both restricted or blocked (`*Be doing the dishes!`) because imperatives target an event
outcome, not an ongoing/completed state (§12.4.3.1). **Prohibitives** use `don't` (§12.4.3.2).
Weaker manipulative sub-types include the **hortative** (`let's`, inclusive dual/plural subject,
§12.4.3.3.1) and the **jussive** (`let` + third-person subject, historically transparent from the
manipulation verb `let` discussed in ch.7 but functionally distinct — it extends to subjects with no
real control at all, "Let the storm rage," and shows compatibility with stative/agentless
constructions that block more direct manipulation, §12.4.3.3.2, pp.267–268), down through a long
chain of modal/question-based deferential constructions (§12.4.3.3.3, p.268–269).

#### Indirect speech-acts: the continuum (§12.5)

An indirect speech-act is defined as a construction used to perform one speech-act via a form more
commonly associated with another; Givón treats this pattern as functionally motivated extension-by-
similarity rather than historical accident. Three continua are laid out in detail — imperative-to-
interrogative (83), imperative-to-declarative (85), and declarative-to-interrogative/WH-question
(87)–(88) — each with graded intermediate forms whose syntactic distance from either prototype
tracks their functional/pragmatic distance. The imperative-interrogative continuum is argued to turn
on three scalar parameters: the power gradient between speaker and hearer, the speaker's ignorance
of the relevant state of affairs, and the speaker's urgency/determination (84); the imperative-
declarative continuum additionally invokes the speaker's subjective certainty and assessment of the
hearer's ignorance/motivation (86).

#### Mood: epistemic/manipulative interaction and the subjunctive (§12.6)

Rather than a sharp binary between epistemic (declarative) and deontic (non-declarative) modality,
Givón argues for a single continuum where weak epistemic certainty shades into preference, and
strong preference shades into weak manipulation — with **subjunctive mood** covering exactly this
mid-portion. The chapter-7 complementation scale is re-presented explicitly as a modal continuum
(table (89), p.273): from strong manipulation (realis) through weak manipulation, strong preference,
weak preference, to weak and then strong certainty (realis) at the opposite extreme — with six
named sub-modes in between (90): successful causation, intended manipulation, preference/aversion,
epistemic anxiety, epistemic uncertainty, epistemic certainty. Contemporary English retains two
residual subjunctive forms: the **bare-stem subjunctive** (found in manipulative-verb complements
and after modals like `should`/`must`/`will`, increasingly lost among younger speakers — illustrated
with an attested newspaper-prose example showing the form still in active use, p.275) and the
**past-form subjunctive** (found in strong-preference and counter-fact-adjacent contexts, carrying a
stronger inference of resistance/doubt/anxiety than the bare-stem form, and also usable in lower-
certainty irrealis conditionals, §12.6.2, pp.274–276). In more informal register, **modal
auxiliaries** (`should`, `must`) increasingly take over this same irrealis range (§12.6.3, p.276–277).

#### Certainty, power, and deference (§12.7)

A set of one-way pragmatic (not logical) inferences — truth > knowledge > certainty > status > power
(100) — is proposed to motivate the systematic shading from epistemic to manipulative/deontic
modality observed throughout the chapter. **Epistemic deference** toward a higher-status
interlocutor is realized by scaling down asserted certainty via irrealis hedges (contrast (101) vs.
(102), p.278); **negation as politeness** similarly softens disagreement in the face of perceived
authority, with a documented set of common softening gambits (103)-(104), and a note that in
traditional/rural small-town cultures, overt contradiction of a superior or even a fellow community
member carries real social cost, making epistemic softening nearly obligatory (§12.7.3, p.279).
Finally, a set of **"hazardous information principles"** (105) — knowledge is power but power is
responsibility; information can be socially destabilizing; transmitting it carries risk; therefore
direct authorship of information is often avoided — is proposed to explain cross-culturally
widespread indirection, disclaimer, and impersonalization strategies around claiming authorship of
transmitted information, illustrated with the ethnographic case of gossip transmission in small,
intimate rural communities (§12.7.4, p.280).

### Ch.13 Inter-Clausal Connections and Discourse Coherence

#### Embedded vs. inter-clausal connections; thematic coherence (§13.1)

This chapter's connections are categorically looser than the embedded clauses (subjects, objects,
REL-clauses, verb complements) covered in chs.6–9: inter-clausal connections — traditionally split
into adverbial-subordinate and conjoined ("coordinate") clauses — fall under their own independent
intonation contour and are governed primarily by semantic/pragmatic, not strict syntactic,
constraints. The chapter's unifying device is the same **isomorphism principle** used throughout the
book (1): the more semantically/pragmatically connected two clauses are, the more grammatically
integrated their coding will be. **Thematic coherence** is analyzed into four concrete, local
strands (referential, temporal, spatial, action continuity) and three more abstract, global strands
(modal, speech-act, perspective continuity) — a distinction that recurs through the whole chapter
(§13.1.2, p.286; formalized again at (80), p.318).

#### Local coherence of adverbial clauses (§13.2)

Temporal ADV-clauses divide into seven sub-types (precedence, subsequence, simultaneity, point
coincidence, terminal/initial boundary, intermediacy), each normally marked by a specific
subordinator (`before`, `after`, `while`, `as`, `till`, `since`, `between`, (3)), but a generic
subordinator (`when`) is frequently used instead when the specific temporal relation is independently
recoverable from the two clauses' tense-aspect-modality marking (perfect signals precedence, matched
past/perfective signals subsequence, etc., (4)) or from culturally-shared lexical/event-sequence
knowledge (5) — the latter strategy shows stronger sequencing restrictions than explicit grammatical
marking, since it depends on conventional expectation rather than overt structure (§13.2.2, pp.
287–290). **Conditional clauses** split into three types: **simple irrealis conditionals** (no truth
value independent of the main clause; implied futurity; compatible with modal, future, imperative,
yes/no-question, and even past-event main clauses, §13.2.3.1, pp.290–291); **subjunctive
conditionals** (past-tense-marked despite irrealis meaning, signaling lower perceived likelihood than
the simple irrealis conditional, illustrated with an attested subtitled-film translation of Spanish
past subjunctive, §13.2.3.2, pp.293–294); and **counter-fact conditionals** (firm negative truth
value — the condition is known false — marked by perfect-past in the conditional clause and
modal-perfect in the main clause, extendable if-lessly via subject-AUX inversion, and also found
independently in bare main clauses and WH-questions without an overt conditional clause at all,
§13.2.3.3, pp.295–296). **Concessive conditionals** (optional `even` + `if`, or `still` in the main
clause) and ordinary **concessive clauses** (`although`, `in spite of`, `even though`, `no matter
how`, etc.) both code a presupposed contrast between expectation and actual outcome (§13.2.3.4,
13.2.4). **Substitutive clauses** (`rather than`, `instead of`) resemble concessives but involve
**logical incompatibility**, not mere surprise, between the ADV-clause and main-clause propositions,
and are typically participial with an implicit subject coreferential with the main clause
(§13.2.5). **Additive** (`in addition to`, `besides`, `on top of`) and **cause/reason** (`because`)
clauses round out the local semantic types; cause and reason are distinguished as, respectively, an
external causal relation an agent/state merely undergoes, versus the speaker's own evaluative
grounds for asserting or believing something — the same clause can shift between the two readings
depending on which perspective is adopted (§13.2.7, pp.299–300), and reason-clause content is shown
to be essentially open-ended/unconstrained via a worked example of nine distinct "because" answers
to the same "why?" challenge (36, p.300–301). **Purpose clauses** (`to`, `in order to`) typically
require subject coreference with the main clause and a non-finite (infinitival) ADV-clause verb,
though the main clause itself need only be compatible with subject control, not necessarily active
(§13.2.8, p.301).

#### Participial adverbial clauses (§13.3)

Participial ADV-clauses are more tightly integrated with their main clause, via two components of
coherence: obligatory **same-subject** reference, and a rigid **temporal-aspectual relation** (either
simultaneity, via the progressive `-ing` participle, or anteriority, via the perfect `having`
participle) — matched by corresponding low-finiteness features (zero/genitive-marked subject,
reduced TAM, no subject agreement, (39)–(40), p.302). Progressive participial clauses are normally
interpreted as simultaneous when post-posed (and when both clauses denote non-punctual/durative
events), but a **pre-posed** participial clause tends instead toward a **sequential-anterior**
reading, especially when the participial event is itself punctual — demonstrated by minimal pairs
where simply reversing pre-posed/post-posed order flips the acceptability and interpretation
((42)–(45), pp.303–304). Perfect participial clauses are unambiguously sequential-anterior
regardless of order (§13.3.3). Both types can **chain** (multiple participial clauses before one
finite main clause), with chained -ing-clauses defaulting to sequential-temporal readings unless the
predicates are stative, in which case a roughly-simultaneous-but-jointly-anterior reading results
(§13.3.4, pp.304–305, with two illustrative passages from an 1828 source showing this chaining style
was more productive in 19th-century written prose than in contemporary English). Preposition-headed
-ing-clauses (`in assessing`, `while waiting`, `upon receiving`) add explicit temporal marking on top
of the bare participial pattern (§13.3.5). Not all participial ADV-clauses require strict subject
coreference: some instead require only a **possessive/kinship relation** between the participial
clause's subject and the main clause's subject ("His heart pounding, Joe opened the box"), and this
relevance relation can loosen further still into a vaguer thematic-relevance link not reducible to
literal possession (examples (56)–(57), pp.307).

#### Other non-finite adverbial clauses, and discourse-pragmatics of ADV-clause position (§13.4–13.5)

Further non-finite ADV-clause types include **for-to purpose clauses** with a non-coreferential
subject (§13.4.1), **truncated copular-participial clauses** that drop `be` entirely from a
prepositional/adjectival/nominal/passive/progressive predicate ("His career in ruins, John...",
§13.4.2), and fully **nominalized ADV-clauses** headed by a temporal preposition, whose possessor may
be either the event's agent or its patient — a genuine source of real-text ambiguity when the
possessor-patient reading is used carelessly, illustrated with an attested misleading newspaper lead
about the Dead Sea Scrolls (§13.4.3, pp.310–311). The chapter's central discourse-pragmatic finding
(§13.5) is that **pre-posed** and **post-posed** ADV-clauses serve structurally different coherence
functions: post-posed ADV-clauses carry only **local, cataphoric** coherence links (anchoring to
their immediate main clause) and appear in paragraph-medial contexts, while pre-posed ADV-clauses
carry **both** a local cataphoric link to their own main clause **and** a diffuse, global **anaphoric**
link reaching back into the preceding discourse — functioning as **coherence bridges** that
characteristically open new thematic paragraphs at major discourse breaks (73, p.315), demonstrated
with an extended passage from a western novel where pre-posed ADV-clauses mark exactly these
thematic transition points (§13.5.2.3, pp.313–314).

#### Conjoined (coordinate) clauses (§13.6)

Against the traditional view of conjoined clauses as syntactically independent of their context,
Givón argues no clause in coherent discourse is genuinely independent — thematic connectivity is
always present to some degree, and conjoined clauses occupy the **loosest** end of a continuum
running from fully independent-looking clauses down to maximally-reduced VP-conjunction (76)-(78),
with intermediate degrees ((78a-f): `and played`, `and also played`, `and she also played`, `and then
she played`, separate sentences) showing progressively weaker syntactic/thematic integration. A
documented corpus study (Hayashi 1989, table (87), p.322) directly correlates punctuation choice
with referential-continuity: zero-marked juxtaposition shows the lowest subject-switch rate (15%),
rising through comma+`and` (70%), period+`And` (81%), up to paragraph-break+`Then` (100%) — supporting
a general principle that the length of the inter-clausal pause iconically tracks the degree of
referential discontinuity (89). A parallel study (92, p.323) shows contrastive conjunctions (`but`,
`while`, `though`, `yet`) strongly associated with subject-switching (77–100%) versus non-contrastive
`and`'s low rate (29%) — confirming contrastive conjunctions' discourse function as markers of
counter-expectancy, extendable beyond reference to any coherence strand (verb, time, place, or
combinations, (93)–(95), pp.323–325) and fundamentally pragmatically unconstrained, since almost any
established expectation can in principle be violated to license `but`.

**Syntax of conjoined clauses**: loosely-conjoined chain-medial clauses show **reduced finiteness**
in two main loci — zero-anaphoric subject marking and reduced tense-aspect-modal auxiliary marking —
with the **chain-initial** clause normally carrying the full finite marking that persists (by
default, zero-marked) through the rest of the chain, until a break in referential, temporal, or
modal continuity forces a return to explicit finite marking to open a new sub-chain (§13.6.5.2–
13.6.5.3, pp.326–328, illustrated extensively with Western-novel narrative passages and a documented
case of incoherent, over-reduced clause-chaining pulled from newspaper prose as a negative
illustration, §13.6.8, p.330–331). A non-standard but attested pattern allows **equi-object**
(rather than equi-subject) anaphoric reference across conjoined clauses, switching an object referent
into subject position in the following clause while retaining non-finite marking except for negation
(example (108), p.329).

#### Clausal disjunction (§13.7)

Disjunction (`or`, optionally `either...or`) is translatable via a logical bi-conditional into a
conditional ("if not") relation (113), but this logical equivalence is directionally asymmetric in
natural discourse — reversing the order of two disjuncts or two "if not" clauses often degrades or
blocks acceptability (115)–(116), another instance of sequential order carrying pragmatic/thematic
bias that pure logic lacks. Disjunction's modal scope is inherently irrealis, explaining why
disjoined non-declarative clauses (yes/no questions, imperatives) are common. Disjoined declaratives
under realis (strong-assertion) contexts sound odd/tautological ("?John did his homework or he
didn't") unless reframed as a weaker-assertion irrealis context (modal, low-certainty, doubt, (118)–
(119)); several apparently-tautological disjunctive/conditional idioms ("War is war," "Boys will be
boys") are shown to carry genuine, culturally-conventionalized pragmatic content once properly
unpacked (120).

#### Coherence, syntactic parallelism, and clause integration (§13.8)

Tighter clausal conjunction types progressively increase in syntactic integration as the number of
shared constituents across the two clauses grows. At the loosest end, two clauses sharing no major
constituent but only tense-aspect-modality still require a baseline **mutual relevance** between
their subjects/topics and a broad spatio-temporal link — violating this yields pragmatically odd
conjunctions even when perfectly grammatical (121)–(123), pp.334–335. **Gapping** (ellipsis of an
identical constituent — most commonly the verb, sometimes verb+subject) requires strict **syntactic
parallelism**, with at least two other elements differing between the clauses (§13.8.3, pp.335–336).
**Verb conjunction** (two conjoined verbs sharing one subject and one object, "She admired and
respected him") achieves even tighter integration, collapsing the structure to effectively a single
surface clause (§13.8.4, p.337). **Comparative clauses** are analyzed as a distinct compressed
parallel-clause structure: two rigidly parallel clauses differing in exactly two elements — the
compared NP (subject/object/indirect object/adverb role) and the extent of some quality it possesses
relative to a standard-of-comparison NP — realized via the suffix `-er`/`-est` (restricted to short,
mostly Germanic-origin words, including a short suppletive list: `good/better/best`,
`bad/worse/worst`) or periphrastically via `more`/`less`/`most` for longer/non-Germanic-origin words
(§13.8.5, pp.337–341), with an equative variant (`as...as`) and reflexive/anaphoric-standard variants
(comparing one referent to itself across time/place/circumstance) also surveyed. Finally, **pro-verb
conjunction** uses `do`/`be` (or the shared auxiliary) plus `too`/`either` (with subject-AUX inversion
under the `neither`/`so` variants) to conjoin two clauses sharing an identical verb/verb-phrase
meaning, with a restricted `do so (too/either)` variant usable only with active (not stative or
auxiliary-bearing) main verbs (§13.8.6, pp.341–343).

#### Conclusion (§13.9)

The chapter's — and in a sense the whole book's — closing generalization: the grammar of clause-
combining is a systematic reflection of the degree to which two events or states are cognitively
"framed jointly" by the speaker, ranging from maximal syntactic integration (clausal subjects/
objects, verb complements, relative clauses, maximally-reduced conjunction) to maximal syntactic
independence (prototypical finite chain-initial clauses), with the grammar's many intermediate
devices tracking intermediate degrees of that same cognitive/communicative framing.

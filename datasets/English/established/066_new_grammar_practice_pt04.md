## Grammar points

### 58. The first conditional
*if* + present simple, paired with future simple in either clause order. Refers to future time
where the *if*-event is a genuine open possibility (*If it rains, we'll go to the cinema* — it might
rain, it might not). *going to* can substitute for future simple when describing a fixed plan
contingent on the condition. (p.79)

### 59. The second conditional
*if* + past simple (structurally identical to ordinary past tense except for the subjunctive *were*
restricted to *I*/*he/she/it*), paired with *would/could/might* + infinitive. Refers to present or
future time where the *if*-event is either purely hypothetical (*If I worked in that factory, I'd
soon change things* — but I don't) or judged unlikely (*If she left her husband, she might be
happier* — but I don't think she's going to). (p.81)

### 60. First and second conditional — contrast
The diagnostic the source offers: ask "Is X going to happen?" A first-conditional *if*-clause
answers "Maybe"; a second-conditional *if*-clause answers "Probably not." The choice of conditional
form is thus a direct encoding of the speaker's subjective estimate of the *if*-event's likelihood,
not a difference in objective time reference. (p.83)

### 61. Zero conditional
*if* + present simple, paired with present simple in either clause order; *if* is semantically
equivalent to *when* in this construction specifically. Used for instructions/mechanical cause-effect
(*If you select reverse gear, the car goes backwards*) and general truths (*If he's got no money, he
doesn't go out*). This is the only one of the three main conditionals where *if* and *when* are
presented as fully interchangeable — the source doesn't make this substitutability explicit for §58
or §59, implying it's a property specific to the zero conditional's general/habitual-truth semantics
rather than of conditionals generally. (p.84)

### 62. Using modals in questions and negatives
Positive: modal inserted between subject and main verb. Negative: *not*/*n't* added directly after
the modal. Question: modal and subject invert. Exception explicitly flagged: *need* takes either a
modal-type negative (*needn't*) or ordinary verb-type negation (*don't need to*) — the only one of
the twelve with this dual behavior noted here. Negative questions default to the contracted *n't*
form; the uncontracted *not* form produces a different word order (*Shouldn't we stay?* vs.
*Should we not stay?*). (p.86)

### 63. *can, could*
*can* covers both (i) ability/know-how (*I can swim*) and (ii) permission (*You can sit here*) — two
distinct meanings sharing one surface form, not disambiguated morphologically. *could* is the past
of sense (i) (*Emily could swim when she was two*) and also appears in second-conditional and
request uses. *couldn't* likewise covers both "wasn't able to" and the second-conditional/request
uses. Both *can* and *could* can soften a request, with *could* judged more polite. *can* refers to
future time when paired with an explicit future time word. (p.87–88)

### 64. *may, might*
Both mark present/future possibility, interchangeably in the positive (*He might/may arrive soon*).
*May I/we...?* is a very polite request form, parallel to *Can/Could I...?*. *may* additionally
carries a formal "is permitted to" sense (*Guests may bring husbands or wives*). Both are
conventionally restricted to first-person subjects in question form — other persons prefer *Do you
think he might...?* over a bare *Might he...?*. Negatives: *may not* only (never contracted); *might
not* or *mightn't*. (p.89–90)

### 65. *can, could, may, might, should, must* — consolidated contrast
The source's one explicit strength-ranking across this group: *must/mustn't* (obligation) is
stronger than *should/shouldn't* (advice). The rest of this section is a mixed drill requiring
students to select the correct modal per context; no further new rule content beyond the
must-vs-should ranking. (p.91–92)

### 66. *have to* / *had to*: *have* + *to*-infinitive
A periphrastic "necessity" construction built on the ordinary lexical verb *have* (not a true modal
— it inflects normally for person/tense: *has to*, *had to*, and takes ordinary *do*-support question/
negative forms). Near-synonymous with *must* in the positive, but *must* has no past-tense form of
its own (*had to* fills that gap: *We had to leave early last night*, not **must left**). Crucially,
the two diverge in the negative: *mustn't* = "it's important that you don't" (prohibition); *don't
have to* = "it isn't necessary, but you may if you wish" (absence of obligation) — a genuine modal
flavor distinction carried entirely by which construction is negated, not by any added word. (p.93)

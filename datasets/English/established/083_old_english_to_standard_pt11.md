## Grammar points

### Why written grammar has slowed its own rate of change, but informal/spoken grammar keeps innovating

Chapter 12 opens by directly confirming Chapter 11's closing claim with concrete present-day evidence:
underlying English grammar has changed comparatively little since the 18th century, but **contemporary
styles of use** keep developing new patterns within that stable underlying system. Two specific,
named trends are given as modern (post-18th-century) developments: (1) increasingly elaborate **noun-
phrase pre-modification** — headword modifiers that used to require a following phrase/clause can now
stack before the head noun in long chains (the book's worked example: *a report on the treatment of
suspects in police stations in Northern Ireland* can be restructured as *a Northern Ireland police
station suspect treatment report*), a style particularly associated with newspaper headlines; and (2)
increasingly elaborate **verb-phrase** construction, with auxiliary verbs combining tense, aspect,
voice, and mood more freely than OE ever permitted (e.g. *she has been being treated... hasn't she
been being treated? won't she have been being treated?*), including a genuinely new possibility —
chaining multiple **catenative** verbs together (*she seems to manage to be able to keep on being
treated*) — plus a parallel rise in **phrasal and prepositional verbs** (*run across* for *meet*,
*put up with* for *tolerate*, *give in* for *surrender*), explicitly flagged by the author as "a
feature of spoken and informal usage" whose newest combinations are "often introduced as slang" before
later becoming fully assimilated into the standard language — a directly stated confirmation, from the
book's own final chapter, of this project's own slang → established-vocabulary pipeline model. (pp.
196–198, Sections 12.1.2 and the unlabelled "Noun phrases"/"Verb phrases" subsections)

### An invented prescriptive rule can still function as a real, binding modern "solecism"

The book uses the split-infinitive case as a worked example of how a **demonstrably recent, invented**
prescriptive rule can nonetheless become, within a couple of generations, functionally indistinguishable
from genuine, long-inherited grammar in its social effect — still enforced by professional editors in
1989 (the book's own example: a *Guardian* newspaper correction specifically inserted to avoid a split
infinitive, even though the correction's meaning is shown to be genuinely **ambiguous** as a direct
result of avoiding the construction). The lesson drawn: a rule's *age* and its *actual grammatical
necessity* are two entirely separate questions from its *current social enforcement power* — directly
continuing the book's running argument (Puttenham, Swift, Lowth, Cobbett) that prescriptive authority
is a social fact imposed and maintained by a chain of institutional transmission (here, "one generation
of school teachers after another"), not a timeless property of the language itself. (p.198, Section
12.2 and Text 141)

### Recorded/transcribed spoken English as the only genuinely direct evidence available for any period

The book closes its evidentiary arc by noting that **every** prior chapter's pronunciation/grammar
evidence has necessarily been *indirect* (inferred from spelling, rhyme, orthoepists' descriptions,
etc.) because no audio record exists for any period before sound recording — meaning the 20th century
is **the first point in the entire book's timeline** where genuinely direct evidence of spoken
language becomes available at all. A sample transcription of real recorded conversation (two women,
a secretary and a university lecturer, discussing exam marking) is given using prosodic transcription
conventions (tone-unit boundaries, tonic-syllable bolding, overlap and micro-pause marks) specifically
to demonstrate that spoken English — even from an "educated user of Standard English" discussing an
abstract academic topic — differs structurally from written English in ways that would **not** be
judged acceptable if punctuated and presented as writing (false starts, self-correction, hesitation,
incomplete constructions) — a final, explicit reminder that "Standard English" as conventionally
described is really a description of the **written** standard, and that unselfconscious spoken usage,
at any period including the present, routinely departs from it in exactly the same way ordinary
speakers' writing departed from literary/standard models in every earlier chapter of the book. (pp.
198–200, Section 12.3)

### The book's own worked method for systematically comparing any two or more historical-stage texts

Section 12.4.1 sets out, explicitly as a transferable analytical method (not just a one-off example),
the comparison procedure implicitly used throughout the whole book: build a table with one column per
text/stage (plus an extra column for words that have survived into MnE but dropped out of later-stage
translations), then work systematically through four fixed categories for every word: **Vocabulary**
(has the word's *meaning* changed? has an older word simply been *lost* from the language entirely,
regardless of spelling/sound change?); **Orthography** (are there non-Roman or now-obsolete letter
forms present?); **Word structure** (have *inflectional suffixes* been lost or changed, and is there
evidence of **metathesis**, i.e. consonant-order reversal, e.g. OE *bridd*/*þerscan*/*irnan* → MnE
*bird*/*thresh*/*run*?); and **Grammar** (is word order different — e.g. OE's inverted
interrogative *hwī forbead God ēow* 'why forbade God you' vs. MnE's periphrastic *why did God forbid
you*; and is negation marked differently — e.g. OE's single pre-verbal *ne*, reinforced during the ME
period by a second post-verbal negative word *noht*, which was itself later dropped, leaving only the
originally-reinforcing word as MnE's sole negative marker, a direct, concrete instance of the
Jespersen's-cycle-type negation history already discussed in earlier chapters). The worked example
applies this to a single verse, Genesis 3:1, given below. (pp.200–202, Sections 12.4–12.4.1)

### Nine-way parallel comparison: Matthew 26:69–75 (Peter's denial), OE to Bislama pidgin

Section 12.4.2 closes the book with a second, even wider-ranging parallel comparison of the same short
narrative passage across nine distinct versions, deliberately chosen to span not just time but also
*dialect* and *contact-language* variation — explicitly offered as a final capstone exercise
(Activity 12.5) inviting the reader to apply the Section 12.4.1 method across the full set at once:

1. **Late West Saxon OE** (c.1050, *The West-Saxon Gospels*) — e.g. *"Þyn spræc þe gesweotolaÞ"* ('thy
   speech thee reveals/betrays').
2. **Fourteenth-century Southern/S. Midlands ME** (the Wycliffite Bible) — *"thi speche makith thee
   knowun"* ('thy speech makes thee known') — directly comparable in structure/vocabulary to the OE
   version but already showing substantial inflectional loss.
3. **Early-sixteenth-century Scots** (Murdoch Nisbet's c.1520 revision of Purvey's Wycliffite text
   *into Scots*) — *"thi speche makis thee knawne"* — the book explicitly flags this version's value as
   showing dialectal Scots features (*makis* for *makith*, *knawne* for *knowun*) against a near-
   identical Midlands-dialect base text, a controlled minimal-pair dialect comparison exactly like the
   *Lay Folks' Catechism* case in pt04.
4. **EMnE, 1582** (the Rheims New Testament, English Catholic recusant translation) — *"for euen thy
   speache doth bewray thee"* ('for even thy speech doth betray thee').
5. **EMnE, 1611** (the King James Bible) — *"for thy speech bewrayeth thee."*
6. **Twentieth-century Scots** (William Laughton Lorimer's 1985 *The New Testament in Scots*) —
   *"your Galilee twang outs ye"* — a strikingly different, much more colloquial/idiomatic rendering
   than any of the earlier stages, using *twang* ('accent') and *outs* ('gives away, exposes') as
   live Scots vocabulary rather than archaizing.
7. **MnE, 1961** (the *New English Bible*) — *"your accent gives you away!"*
8. **Bislama** (a genuine English-lexifier pidgin/creole of Vanuatu, formerly the New Hebrides, West
   Pacific; from *The Four Gospels in New Hebrides Bislama*, 1971) — *"tok bilong yu i tok bilong man
   Galili ia"* (literally 'talk belong you he talk belong man Galilee this' = 'your talk/speech is the
   talk/speech of a man of Galilee'). The book gives explicit phonetic guidance for reading Bislama
   aloud "as if it were English... in phonetic script," noting specific pronunciations (*yad* /jaːd/
   like English *yard*; *get* /ɡɛːt/ like *gate*; *rusta* like *rooster*; *save* a two-syllable word
   like *savvy*, meaning 'to know') — directly useful as a concrete, real-world worked example of an
   English-lexifier contact language's own sound-correspondence system, methodologically the same kind
   of evidence this project's own conlang-synthesis work is built to analyze.

This nine-text set is explicitly the book's **final capstone exercise** — the author's closing
invitation is to apply every analytical tool built up across the whole book (vocabulary-source
tracking, inflection-loss scoring, orthographic-convention spotting, word-order/negation comparison,
dialect-marker identification) to one single, tightly controlled passage spanning the entire
documented history of English **plus** a genuine present-day contact-language descendant — making it,
for this project's purposes, arguably the single most directly useful dataset in the whole book: a
real (not invented) worked example of how "the same English" fragments and recombines into a
genuinely new, synthesized variety under sustained language contact. (pp.203–207, Section 12.4.2,
Texts 144–151)

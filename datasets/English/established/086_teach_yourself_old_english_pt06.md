## Grammar points

### Adverb formation: -e, -lice, -unga, and the lic/gelic family

Old English adverbs are typically formed from adjectives in one of three ways: a plain *-e* suffix
(*hrad* "quick" → *hraðe* "quickly"); the more common *-lice* suffix (pronounced "leecheh") giving a
Modern English-recognizable *-ly* pattern (*eadmodlice* "humbly," *bliðelice* "happily"); and a less
common *-unga* suffix (*ealunga* "completely"). The *-lic*/*-lice* family derives historically from
*þæt lic* "body" (cf. the archaic ModE "lychgate," church-gate for a funeral bier) via a related
adjective *gelic* "like" — ModE *-ly*, *alike*, *like*, and *likeness* are all ultimately descended
from this one root. A few adverbs instead come from inflecting a noun into the genitive (*dæges and
nihtes* "by day and by night") or dative plural (*hwilum* "at times," literally "at whiles"), or by
converting an adjective (*miclum* "much," *lytlum* "little"). A closed set of short, pronoun-like
adverbs (*þa* "then," *her* "here," *þær* "there") behave almost like substitute words, and
regularly appear clause-initially with the verb in second position. p.197–198.

### The adverbial phrase and prepositional-phrase adverbials

Beyond single-word adverbs, Old English freely builds multi-word adverbial phrases, often a
preposition governing an adjective/pronoun/article plus noun (*mid mycclan weorðscype* "with great
honour," *mid his agenum handum* "with his own hands," *on þam sylfan dæge* "on that same day").
These function identically to single-word manner adverbs (*arwurðlice* "honourably") but allow finer
specification. The intensifier *swiþe* ("very," from an adjective meaning "strong") commonly
modifies both adjectives (*swiþe god* "very good") and adverbs (*swiþe arwurðlice* "very
honourably"). Directional adverb sets are given in full: "towards" (*ham* "home," *þider* "to
there," *hider* "to here," *feorr* "far," *ut* "out," *norð* "northward," *suð* "southward") and
"from" (*þanon* "from there," *heonan* "from here," *feorran* "from afar," *utan* "from outside,"
*norðan* "from the north," *suðan* "from the south"). p.198–199.

### Word order: main-clause patterns around "and"-chained subordinate-feeling clauses

Building on the earlier subordinate-clause-verb-final rule, the book shows that **and-initial
clauses in a long Chronicle annal behave like subordinate clauses for word-order purposes even
though they aren't grammatically subordinate**: once a main clause establishes a topic, each
following *and*-clause tends to push its verb toward clause end, with only an occasional emphatic
prepositional phrase trailing after it. A long worked example from Chronicle D 1022/1023 is broken
into a connector+subject / other-elements / verb / final-element table across 8 successive clauses
to demonstrate the pattern concretely. p.200–202.

### Explicit scribal-house-style variation: þæm vs. þam, and vowel-variant þæne/þone

Comparing the same annal (892 vs. 893) as copied independently into Chronicle manuscripts A and D
shows a consistent scribal preference: the later D-text consistently substitutes *þam* for the
A-text's *þæm* — not a meaning difference, but a documented scriptorium house-style variant. A
second, different kind of variation is shown within a *single* sentence of Byrhtferth's own
*Handbook*: the masculine accusative demonstrative appears as both *þæne* and *þone* in close
proximity (*þæne ænne... þone oþerne*, "the one... the other"), which the book speculates may be a
deliberate vowel-echo stylistic effect (each form's vowel echoing the adjacent word's) rather than
random variation. A third variant, *þan* for *þam*, is attested in a document of Edward's reign and
attributed to the increasingly indistinct pronunciation of unstressed inflectional endings in
eleventh-century spoken Old English feeding back into scribal spelling. p.213–214.

### The idiomatic possessive dative article: "þam cyninge to handan"

The dative definite article can carry an idiomatic possessive sense beyond its normal "to/for"
function, illustrated by *þam cyninge to handan* "into the king's hands" (literally "to the king, to
hand") and *þam hirede to fosterlande* "for the community's fosterland" (literally "to the
community, for fosterland") — a construction the book frames as a distinctively Old English way of
expressing possession/purpose via the dative article rather than a genitive. p.215.

### Full personal-pronoun paradigm: first and second person, including the dual

A complete table of first- and second-person pronouns across all four cases and all three numbers
(singular, dual, plural): 1st person *ic/me/min/me* (sg.), *wit/unc/uncer/unc* (dual), *we/us/ure/us*
(pl.); 2nd person *þu/þe/þin/þe* (sg.), *git/inc/incer/inc* (dual), *ge/eow/eower/eow* (pl.) —
consolidating the dual-number material first introduced in Unit 3. The dative/accusative forms *me*
and *eow* survive into Modern English as *mine*, *thine* (archaic), *our*, and *your*
(possessives), and the book notes a letter's pronoun choices (switching from *ic/inc* for intimate
direct address to *we/eow* for a message meant for the whole court to hear) can itself carry
rhetorical/social meaning, since letters and declarations were normally read aloud to an assembled
audience rather than read silently by a single addressee. p.210–211.

### The Tironian nota (⁊) for "and"

Manuscripts and standard scholarly editions widely use a shorthand symbol (⁊, transcribed here as
"7") for *and* (sometimes also *ond*), inherited from the Roman shorthand system developed by M.
Tullius Tiro, Cicero's amanuensis. Most introductory textbooks silently expand this symbol to the
full word for readability, but the book recommends getting comfortable with its appearance in
primary-source facsimiles and standard editions, noting it may also have functioned as a kind of
supplementary punctuation mark separating list items, beyond simply standing for "and." p.220.

### Legal-rights vocabulary cluster: sacu, socn, infangeneþeof, flymena fyrmð

A cluster of specific named legal privileges a king could grant to a bishop/archbishop: *sacu 7
socn* "sake and soke" (the general right to hold a court and exact fines, cross-referencing pt04's
"mid sake and mid socne" formula), *griðbryce* "breach of sanctuary," *hamsocn* "attacking a man in
his own home," *forsteall* "ambush/assault," *infangeneþeof* (the specific right to try and fine
thieves caught within one's own jurisdiction, built on *fon* "to seize"), and *flymena fyrmð* "the
harbouring of fugitives" (penalized, not granted — *flymena* is genitive plural of *flyma*
"fugitive"). p.217.

## Grammar points

### Chapter 1 — Theoretical framework: grammar change vs. language change

The book's central methodological distinction, carried through the rest of the book: **grammar
change** is a change in the native speaker's internalized competence/UG-parameter-settings (I-
language), while **language change** is any change in attested usage/output (E-language), including
non-grammar-internal causes like language contact and social factors. A grammar-internal, abrupt
reanalysis can look gradual on the surface because of competition between two coexisting grammars in
a speech community (Kroch's model, below) — the authors treat historical textual gradualness as
compatible with discontinuous grammar change underneath (pp. 1–19).

### The history of the English modals as a case study in categorial reanalysis (1.2.1.1)

Four empirical generalizations (after Lightfoot 1979) mark the OE-to-ModE shift of *can, may,
shall, will, must* etc. from lexical (preterite-present) verbs to a closed grammatical auxiliary
class: (a) loss of the ability to take a direct object (complete in Middle English except *can*,
which resisted until the 17th c.); (b) loss of the distinctive *-eþ* third-singular ending that
marked this inflectional class, making premodals morphologically unique; (c) opacity of the
original present/past tense opposition, which gets reanalyzed as a present/non-past modal-meaning
contrast (e.g. *can/could*, *may/might* as politeness/confidence markers rather than true tense);
(d) exclusion from the rising *to*-infinitive, firmly established for ordinary verbs by the 14th c.
but never acquired by the modals. Once these verbal properties were lost, the premodals ceased to
be infinitives, to occur with *-ing*, in clusters, or with *have*/*-en* — i.e. they stopped behaving
like main verbs syntactically as well as morphologically (pp. 5–7). Two competing accounts of *why*
are given: Lightfoot's **Transparency Principle** (reanalysis is forced once exception features
accumulate past a learnability threshold) vs. Warner's/Anderson's **prototype-based gradualism**
(the modals were always a less-prototypical, peripheral verb subclass in Old English, and simply
drifted further from the verbal prototype toward a separate Auxiliary category over time, pp. 20–22)
— the authors present both without fully endorsing either.

### Case theory basics and the OE/ME adjective-case difference (1.2.1)

Case theory in the Principles-and-Parameters framework the book adopts distinguishes **structural
case** (assigned by syntactic position — nominative to subject, accusative to object, neutralized
under passivization) from **inherent case** (lexically selected by a specific head, survives
passivization). Old English differs from the modern language in that OE adjectives could select
dative or genitive complements (e.g. *ungewill* "displeasing" + dative), an option lost during
Middle English. The authors flag two competing analyses of this loss: either English lost
dative/genitive as possible *lexical* cases outright, or adjectives specifically dropped out of the
class of case-assigning heads (p. 9–10) — they favor the former, more general account.

### Verb-Second (V2) as a parametric, acquisition-triggered phenomenon (1.2.3)

Introduced via Modern Dutch as the model V2 language: the finite verb occupies the second
constituent position in main clauses regardless of what fills first position (subject, object,
adjunct, PP). Structurally, some phrase moves to Spec,CP and the finite verb moves to C. Lightfoot's
**degree-0 learnability** hypothesis holds that only simple (non-embedded) main clauses can trigger
a parameter reset, with ~30% non-subject-initial main clauses empirically sufficient as a
robustness threshold for Dutch/German/Norwegian/Swedish learners to set V2 (pp. 11–12). This
framework is directly foundational for Chapter 4's treatment of Old English V2.

### Competing models of parameter-setting: input-matching, cue-based learning, UG-driven default (1.2.3.2–3)

Three accounts of how children arrive at a grammar from ambiguous/conflicting input are surveyed:
(i) **Clark & Roberts' genetic-algorithm/input-matching model** — all UG-available grammars compete
to fit the input, with the "fittest" (best-matching) grammar winning; crucially, when the input is
genuinely indeterminate the learner can "turn in on itself" and default to an independent UG
preference, which is one proposed engine of language change. (ii) **Alison Henry's Belfast English
imperatives case study** — children demonstrably *ignore* part of the robust input data (passive/
unaccusative inverted imperatives) in favor of a UG-driven grammar that only partially matches what
they hear, contradicting full input-matching. (iii) **Lightfoot's cue-based learning** — the learner
does not evaluate all UG-possible grammars against the input but instead extracts minimal abstract
structural "cues" (fragments like `[SpecCP XP]`) from robust degree-0 evidence, building up a
grammar cue by cue rather than through holistic matching (pp. 12–17).

### Synchronic variation and the Constant Rate Hypothesis (1.2.4)

Reconciles abrupt parametric grammar change with gradual textual change: speakers can be
"bidialectal" in their own native grammar, holding two competing grammars (differing in one
parameter setting) simultaneously, with usage frequencies shifting over time following an S-shaped
curve (slow start, rapid middle rise, gradual completion). Kroch's **constant rate hypothesis**
predicts that all surface contexts affected by a single underlying grammatical reanalysis shift at
the *same rate* — used as a diagnostic for whether superficially different changes share one
grammatical cause. Illustrated with Kroch's (1989) finding that early Modern English *do*-support's
rise and lexical-finite-verb-fronting's decline are mathematically complementary curves across
multiple clause types (questions, negatives), evidencing one underlying change (pp. 17–19).

### Grammaticalization (1.3.3)

Meillet's (1912) original sense — an autonomous lexical item acquiring grammatical function (the
English modals' history is the canonical English example) — is distinguished from "grammaticalization
theory" as a diachronic-process-centered research program that the authors note sits awkwardly with
their synchronic, acquisition-based framework. Characteristic properties: loss of lexical meaning as
a function word/bound morpheme develops, gradualness, and apparent unidirectionality. The authors'
own synchronic reframing: grammaticalization's unidirectional *look* is explained by UG preferences
(à la Clark & Roberts) biasing successive learners' resolutions of indeterminate input in a
consistent direction — addressed fully in Chapter 9.

### Historical-data methodology (1.4)

Historical linguistics can access only **performance** data (written texts), never direct
grammaticality judgments, and the Old/Middle English text records are generically skewed (OE: mostly
formal prose — translations from Latin, or homiletic/saintly-life genres with limited stylistic
range; ME: much more dialectally and stylistically diverse, including colloquial poetry). Two
non-obvious evidential sources are highlighted: **OE poetry's strict alliterative meter** can
disambiguate underlying word order (verb-stress-position correlations supporting basic OV order,
Pintzuk & Kroch 1989) despite poetry's otherwise archaic/non-representative language; and
**interlinear glosses**, generally low-value, can reveal real dialect-specific syntax where a gloss
systematically deviates from its Latin original in a way that recurs (e.g. Kroch & Taylor's 1997
Northumbrian-gloss subject-pronoun-position dialect evidence). Frequency data is diagnostically
useful but not a grammaticality criterion by itself (an infrequent pattern is not thereby
ungrammatical); Pintzuk's 1% frequency threshold for distinguishing systematic minor patterns from
noise is cited as one working convention, not a hard rule (pp. 26–34).

### Chapter 2 — Old English morphology and case assignment (2.2)

OE verbal inflection marks person/number/tense/mood but with notable syncretism (e.g. *fremmaþ* is
ambiguous between indicative plural and imperative plural); nonetheless person/number distinctions
remain largely intact overall via complementary endings. OE nominal case is a four-way system
(nominative/accusative/dative/genitive), though nominative and accusative are often NOT formally
distinguished on the noun itself — case marking relies heavily on demonstrative pronouns and
adjective agreement, plus word order, rather than on noun inflection alone (pp. 37–41). **Pro-drop**
in OE is NOT the rich-morphology-licensed type found in Spanish/Italian; rather OE shows two distinct
null-subject phenomena independent of verbal agreement: (i) **conjoined-subject deletion** (a
subject omitted under coreference with a higher clause's subject, discourse-governed, not
morphology-governed) and (ii) **expletive pro-drop** (no dummy *it*/*there* subject in weather-verb
and impersonal-passive constructions) — largely lost by 1500 (pp. 38–40).

### Old English impersonal verbs (2.2.2)

A defined class of "psych verbs" (*lician*, *ofþyncan*, *ofhreowan*, *sceamian*, *eglian*,
*(ge)lystan*, *langian*, *(ge)lustfullian*) denote involuntary mental/cognitive states with **no
nominative/agentive subject**. Core argument template: EXPERIENCER + THEME, realized in three
attested configurations — (a) EXPERIENCER-dative/THEME-nominative, (b) EXPERIENCER-nominative/THEME-
genitive, (c) EXPERIENCER-dative/THEME-genitive (the "true" impersonal, no nominative argument at
all). When the THEME is pronominal it tends to precede the dative EXPERIENCER; when both are full
NPs, dative-before-nominative order dominates — widely cross-linguistic in Germanic "impersonal
inversion." The THEME argument can also be an entire clause rather than an NP (pp. 44–46).

### Old English word order at the NP level (2.3.1)

Preferred (not absolute) modifier order: quantifier > demonstrative/possessive > numeral > *oþer*
"other" > adjective(s) > genitive noun > head noun — closely resembling the modern language's
default. Postmodification (modifier following the head) is regularly attested with quantifiers and
*-weard*-forms, and occasionally in poetry with other modifier types. A demonstrative + adjective
pair in postposition keeps the demonstrative first. Certain constituents (prepositional modifiers,
relative clauses) always follow the rest of the NP regardless of other ordering. Appositive NP/title
constructions put the personal name before the title in OE (reversed order emerges later in ME).
Discontinuous NPs — where a postmodifier or relative clause is severed from its head and placed at
clause end — are attested (pp. 46–48).

### Old English word order at the clause level (2.3.2–3)

OE is often loosely called "SOV," but the real picture requires separating the **finite verb**
(which shows considerable V2-like fronting, especially in questions, negative-initial clauses, and
topic-initial main clauses — and unlike Modern Dutch/German, *all* lexical finite verbs, not just
auxiliaries, can front) from the **non-finite verb** (whose position shows genuine OV/VO variation,
addressed fully in Chapter 5). A striking asymmetry: with a non-subject topic, a **nominal** subject
typically follows the fronted finite verb (true inversion) while a **pronominal** subject typically
remains preverbal before the finite verb — undermining a simple "OE is a V2 language" characterization
(fully resolved in ch. 4). **Coordinate clauses** form a distinct third category from main/subordinate
clauses: despite introducing with *þa* (which triggers inversion in a true main clause), coordinate
continuations frequently show no inversion and verb-final order typical of subordinate clauses — a
reading/analysis pitfall the authors flag explicitly (pp. 49–53).

### Old English clause types: questions and negation (2.4)

**Questions:** yes-no and wh-questions share subject-verb inversion (not restricted to auxiliaries,
unlike the modern language); P-stranding never occurs in OE questions — a fronted wh-object's
governing preposition is always pied-piped along with it. A distinct *hwæþer*-introduced question
type shows no inversion and obligatory subjunctive mood, betraying its origin as an embedded
(indirect) question structure reused independently. **Negation:** Old English is a **negative-concord
language** — multiple negative elements in one clause still yield single logical negation. *Ne* is
the core sentence negator, immediately left-adjacent to the finite verb; a minority pattern adds a
second negative element (*na*/*no*, or *noht*/*nawiht*) for emphasis. Constituent negation uses *na*-
or *ne*-prefixed forms (e.g. *nan* < *ne* + *an*). Frequent *ne*+verb contraction occurs (*næs* <
*ne wæs*; *nabbaþ* < *ne habbaþ*) (pp. 53–55).

### Old English subordinate clauses (2.5)

Subordination (hypotaxis) is argued to have arisen historically from paratactic juxtaposition of
independent clauses rather than being primary — evidenced by *se*-relatives (where the relative
pronoun began as a resumptive demonstrative starting a second independent clause) and by object
clauses introduced by verbs of saying (where a quoted main clause was reinterpreted as a subordinate
complement, cued by word order/subjunctive mood rather than a dedicated subordinator). OE marks
subordination primarily through **word order** (OV vs. the SVO/VSO of main clauses) and **subjunctive
mood**, since dedicated subordinating conjunctions are otherwise sparse and many double as adverbs
(pp. 55–58).

**Relative clauses** (2.5.1) fall into three major types by relative marker: (1) *se*-relatives
(demonstrative pronoun doing double duty, mostly non-restrictive, judged the paratactic-origin
type); (2) *se þe*-relatives (demonstrative + indeclinable *þe*); (3) *þe*-relatives (indeclinable
particle alone, by far the most frequent type). Case-attraction between an antecedent's case and the
relative pronoun's own clause-internal case is possible but not obligatory. **Preposition stranding**
occurs specifically with *þe*-relatives via a null locative pronoun (structurally parallel to *þær*),
while *se*/*se þe*-relatives require **pied-piping** instead — the two strategies are argued (van
Kemenade 1987) to be structurally identical wh-movement operations differing only in whether the
moved element is phonetically null. Minor relative types include adverb-headed relatives (*þanon/
þonon*, *þider þe*) and "included-antecedent"/free relatives (pp. 58–61).

**Complement clauses** (2.5.2) are predominantly **finite**, introduced by *þæt* (omissible after
verbs of saying), in contrast to the modern language's preference for non-finite complementation —
*that*-clauses, not *to*-infinitives, were the default OE complementation strategy. Non-finite
complementation uses infinitives of two types: the **bare/zero infinitive** (ending *-an/-ian* — by
far the more frequent OE type) vs. the **inflected/*to*-infinitive** (*to* + verb stem + *-enne*),
whose distribution correlates with (a) the syntactic function of the infinitive (subject/object vs.
adjunct/complement-to-noun-or-adjective, where *to*-infinitive is obligatory) and with (b) a semantic
factor dubbed "(in)directness" — shared-tense-domain complements (direct perception, modal/causative
contexts) favor the bare infinitive; independent-future-time-reference complements favor *to* (pp.
61–63).

**Adverbial clauses** (2.5.3) are classified semantically (time, place, purpose, condition,
concession, etc.) with varying subjunctive-mood requirements. A distinctive OE device is the
**compound conjunction**: preposition + case-form of *se* + relative particle *þe* (e.g. *for þæm
þe* "because," *mid þæm þe* "when," *ær þæm þe* "before that") — these later fossilize/grammaticalize
into single ME/ModE subordinators (pp. 63–64).

### Preposition stranding (2.6)

Modern-English P-stranding is tightly restricted (complement-PP objects only, not adjunct PPs; much
stricter for passives — requiring a near-idiomatic verb+preposition unit — than for wh-movement).
**Old English P-stranding in wh-movement constructions had an essentially identical distributional
profile to the modern language**; the key OE/ModE difference is in **passives**: OE had NO
prepositional passives whatsoever (because prepositions assigned only inherent, not structural,
case, and only structurally-cased objects could passivize) — prepositional passives are a Middle
English innovation entirely (pp. 64–67).

### Chapter 3 — Middle English morphological reduction and its syntactic fallout (3.2)

Verbal inflection collapses to a handful of surface endings by the end of the ME period: *-(e)st*
(2sg indicative, robust through the 16th c.+), *-(e)th* (3sg, later replaced by northern-origin
*-(e)s*), *-(e)n* (plural, lost by ~1480 for infinitives, somewhat later for plural marking). Despite
reduced agreement morphology, ME is argued NOT to show true agreement-licensed pro-drop (contra a
naive rich-morphology ⇒ pro-drop correlation) — ME subject omission instead tracks **register**
(informal/conversational contexts) and specific **construction types**, chiefly a null-expletive-
subject pattern continuing OE's (gradually eliminated over the 15th c., with overt *it* the rule by
1500) (pp. 69–71). **Nominal morphology**: the OE four-way case system collapses to a two-way
**genitive vs. common-case** system for nouns (by the 15th c. the possessive *-s* generalizes),
while **pronouns** retain a three-way nominative/genitive/objective distinction, and the
definite-article/demonstrative paradigm levels to invariant *the* vs. *that* (pp. 71–72).

### Middle English case-marking collapse and its effect on complementation and voice (3.2.1–3)

As dative and accusative merge into a single objective case, OE's semantically-grounded dative-vs.-
accusative distinctions in monotransitive, ditransitive, and prepositional complementation are lost
wholesale, with any residual meaning distinctions relocated to different lexical items or to the
newly productive **recipient-marking preposition *to*** in ditransitives. **Impersonal verbs**
undergo a structurally analogous simplification: the OE genitive-THEME option disappears, folding
into either the objective case or a prepositional THEME; the dative-EXPERIENCER pattern (11a) and
genitive-THEME pattern (11b) both collapse toward the modern EXPERIENCER-objective/THEME-nominative
template, while the genuinely argument-less "true impersonal" pattern (11c, no nominative argument
at all) persists as an option until ~1500. Crucially, the category remains **productive** through
Middle English — new loan verbs (*marvel*) and even native verbs with no prior impersonal use in Old
English (*must*) acquire true-impersonal syntax, showing this isn't just inherited-verb fossilization
(pp. 74–77).

**Passives** (3.2.3): beyond the OE-inherited "prototypical" passive (nominative subject
corresponding to an accusative active object), Middle English innovates two new passive types: the
**prepositional passive** (subject corresponds to the object of a preposition in the active, e.g.
*this maiden felt... that she was lain by*), spreading via lexical diffusion from fixed
verb+preposition idioms from the 13th c. on, firmly established by the 14th c. — the authors argue
this was enabled, but not directly caused, by the case-system simplification (compare Modern Dutch,
which has ME-like case simplification but NO prepositional passive); and the later **recipient
passive** (subject corresponds to the indirect object, e.g. *he was given the prize*), not clearly
attested before ~1375, argued to result from the independent fixing of [V–THEME–RECIPIENT] as the
sole surviving ditransitive word order around that date (pp. 77–79).

### Middle English word order changes (3.3)

Within the NP: broadly similar to OE, with minor new freedoms (e.g. *many*/*each* able to precede
the determiner in some ME texts, impossible in OE or ModE); the **group genitive** (*the king of
England's crown*) and a periphrastic *his*-genitive (*Adam his sune*, reanalyzed from *(h)is* as a
separate word) both emerge as new 13th–14th-century constructions, linked to the generalization of
*-s* as the sole genitive marker. Within the clause: the three historically major changes are each
previewed here and treated fully in later chapters — (i) decline of **OV order** (down to ≤1–6% of
clauses by 1450, chapter 5); (ii) decline of **Verb-Second** inversion (chapter 4) — crucially framed
as an *independent* parameter from OV/VO (Dutch is V2+OV, Swedish is V2+VO, demonstrating the two can
vary separately); (iii) fixing of **verb-particle order** to post-verbal (chapter 6). Empirically,
OV/VO order loss precedes V2 loss within Middle English (p. 83).

### Middle English clause types: questions and the rise of *do* (3.4.1)

Subject-verb inversion remains the main-clause question strategy throughout Middle English,
unaffected by the subject's being a wh-word (no inversion when the wh-word is itself the subject).
*Do*-support appears in questions from the late 14th century (first clearly attested in Chaucer) but
remains rare until a sharp rise in early Modern English; Middle English "questioned *do*" instances
are argued not to involve an abstract empty do-operator the way the OE/early-ME-absent construction
later does. Preposition stranding in wh-questions is a Middle English innovation, first attested
sporadically in the 13th-century *Brut*, plausibly spreading from the new wh-relative-clause
construction (itself plausibly rooted in OE *swa-hwa-swa* free relatives) rather than being wholly
new (pp. 83–85).

### Middle English negation and the early stage of Jespersen's Cycle (3.4.2)

Tracks a multi-stage shift: OE unsupported *ne* → early ME increasingly-supported *ne...naht*
(no longer felt as emphatic; *naht* acquires a fixed post-finite-verb position) → *naht/nat/not*
becomes the regular negator even without *ne*, with unsupported bare *ne* surviving only in
specific South-Eastern/Chaucerian-London texts and only in restricted environments (cliticized onto
an auxiliary, or after the negative adverb *but*, or in complement clauses following a negative/
interrogative matrix — classic **negative-concord** contexts). Chaucer's language still regularly
uses multiple negative indefinites for single negation (*nevere...no*), a pattern that doesn't erode
until the early Modern English period (pp. 85–88). This chapter's data feed directly into Chapter 9's
full treatment of Jespersen's Cycle.

### Middle English subordinate-clause marking (3.5)

Three general developments reshape how subordination is signaled, moving away from OE's
word-order/subjunctive-based system toward dedicated lexical subordinators: (i) the old OE
correlative pairs (*þa...þa*, *nu...nu*) are replaced by formally distinct adverb/conjunction pairs
(*when...then*, *now that...now*), and inversion stops marking the main/subordinate distinction;
(ii) old phrasal (compound) conjunctions grammaticalize into single words (*þe hwile þe* > *whilst*;
*by the cause that* > *because*); (iii) general-purpose *that* spreads to mark nearly every
subordinate-clause type, including after other subordinators that already existed independently
(*to the entente that*) and even co-occurring with the older distinctive subordinator *(ȝ)if*
(pp. 88, 101–2). A documented interim-stage ambiguity: *that* is frequently used where Modern
English would choose a more semantically specific subordinator (temporal *that*, causal *that*,
purpose/result *that*), and several ME subordinators remain genuinely polyfunctional across more
than one clause type (*for* = cause or purpose; *till that* = temporal or purpose; *so (that)* =
conditional or consecutive) (pp. 102–3).

**Relative clauses** (3.5.1): the OE *se/seo/þæt* + *þe* system collapses; *þat* becomes the
near-universal Middle English relativizer by the 13th century everywhere except conservative
South-Western/West-Midlands texts (where OE *se*-forms persist longer due to continuing West Saxon
scribal influence). Wh-relatives (*who, whom, whose, which, (the) which (that)*) begin appearing
from the start of the ME period but remain rare until the 14th century, developing via an
interrogative-to-relative "bridge" through indirect questions and free relatives; *which* only
supplants *that* as the dominant relativizer from the 15th century (and even Chaucer still uses
*that* in 75% of cases vs. Caxton's 50%). The locative relative *ther(e)* (continuing OE *þær*) is
gradually ousted by *where* over the period. **Zero relatives** (null relative pronoun), inherited
from Old English, remain heavily constrained to subject position with a stative verb (*to be*,
existence/location verbs) — the "There is a woman wants to see you" pattern still colloquially
alive today — while a new ME **zero-object-relative** (object gap, no relative pronoun at all)
emerges, restricted at first to *clepen/callen* "call" and *have*-idiom contexts, only generalizing
after the Middle English period. A genuinely new ME construction is the **infinitival relative
clause with a wh-form** (*she has no wight to whom to make hir mone* — "no one to complain to"), which
developed out of questioned infinitives, itself a Middle English innovation (pp. 91–95).

**Complement clauses** (3.5.2): nominal in function (object/subject-complement to V/A/N), with
*that*-omission after statement-complement verbs somewhat more restricted than in the modern
language. The non-finite complement system undergoes major innovation relative to Old English: (a)
*for to* emerges as a third infinitive-marking option alongside bare and *to*; (b) the *to*-
infinitive, a minority OE pattern, completely overtakes the bare infinitive as the default ME
non-finite complement (plausibly driven by phonological erosion of the *-an/-enne* inflectional
distinction, and by *to*-infinitives' functional convergence with *that*-clauses — both carry
independent tense reference, unlike the bare infinitive); (c) the **passive infinitive** (never
found in Old English) is introduced and becomes productive; (d) infinitival constructions with an
overtly lexical subject (**Accusative-cum-Infinitivo / AcI**, "exceptional case marking") spread
from a narrow OE base (causative and direct-perception verbs only) to object-control verbs
(*command, require, warn*) and later to *expect/believe*-type verbs — the full history is Chapter
7's topic; (e) a benefactive dative is reanalyzed as an infinitival subject, the seed of the later
*for*-NP-to-VP construction; (f) a new **he is to come**-type construction (intransitive existential
*is* + bare-subject + infinitive) emerges alongside the inherited OE **hit is to donne**-type
("it is to be done"), and the latter is argued to be reanalyzed via passivization of the infinitive
as preverbal objects become rarer — directly setting up Chapter 8's history of the *easy-to-please*
construction, whose own passive-infinitive variant appears only in late Middle/early Modern English
(pp. 96–100). The **perfect infinitive** (*to han ben*, "to have been") is vanishingly rare before
the 14th century and in Middle English is strongly associated with **counterfactual/irrealis**
meaning — an unrealized past possibility — rather than straightforward perfect aspect (pp. 100–101).

**Adverbial clauses** (3.5.3): see the "Middle English subordinate-clause marking" grammar point
above for the three general developments; this subsection supplies the detailed subordinator
inventory and notes the continuing OE-inherited ambiguity between consecutive clauses and relative
clauses with a resumptive pronoun in certain *that*-clause patterns (pp. 101–103).

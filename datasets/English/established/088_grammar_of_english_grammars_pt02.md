# English — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: *The Grammar of English Grammars*, Part I — Orthography

**Source:** Goold Brown, *The Grammar of English Grammars* (6th ed., 1851; Project Gutenberg plain-text
transcription, EBook #11615, confirmed clean text layer). Covers PDF pages 147–234: Part I, "Orthography"
— Ch. I Of Letters, Ch. II Of Syllables, Ch. III Of Words, Ch. IV Of Spelling, Ch. V Questions, Ch. VI
Exercises for Writing.

**Attested Era tag for this entire file:** `19th century (source published 1851)`. **Vision Reading
Confidence:** `n/a` throughout (clean text layer).

**Coverage note.** Orthography here means something broader than modern "spelling": letters, syllables,
word-formation ("figure"), and spelling rules proper. Each of Chapters I–IV states a run of named, numbered
rules followed by (a) "Observations" — Brown's own discursive commentary, frequently including polemical
asides against named rival grammarians (Murray, Webster, Walker, Kirkham, Mulkey, etc.) over disputed
points — and (b) "Improprieties for Correction," i.e. long lists of "false syntax"/false-orthography drill
sentences with a single worked "formule" showing how to apply the rule, followed by dozens to hundreds of
further uncorrected examples for the student to fix. Per the coverage rule, the numbered rules and genuinely
substantive observations are extracted below; the drill-sentence lists themselves (which run to many hundreds
of entries across this Part) are not reproduced — they are repetitions of the same rule against different source
quotations, not new grammatical content. **Chapter V ("Questions") is a catechism-style rehearsal of
everything in Ch. I–IV with zero new content (e.g. "What is a letter?... What is a vowel?...") and Chapter VI
("Exercises for Writing," i.e. "Exercises in Orthography") is a further uncorrected false-orthography drill list
for written practice — both are skipped entirely, noted here for completeness rather than silently.**

## Grammar points

### Chapter I — Of Letters: the four-part knowledge of a letter, and classification

Brown defines a **letter** as "an alphabetic character, which commonly represents some elementary sound...
some element of speech" and states that full knowledge of the letters requires four kinds of information:
their *names*, *classes*, *powers* (sounds), and *forms* (pp. 147–148). English has 26 letters representing
"about thirty-six or thirty-seven" primary sounds — i.e. the alphabet is already acknowledged as an imperfect,
many-to-many mapping onto the phonology.

**Classes of letters:** the primary division is **vowel** (forms a perfect sound alone; *a, e, i, o, u*, and
sometimes *w, y*) vs. **consonant** (cannot be perfectly uttered without a vowel). *W*/*y* are consonants only
when they precede a vowel heard in the same syllable (*wine, yet*); otherwise vowels (*newly, eyebrow*).
Consonants subdivide into **semivowels** (imperfectly sounded alone, protractable: *f, h, j, l, m, n, r, s, v, w,
x, y, z*, plus soft *c*/*g*) and **mutes** (cannot be sounded alone at all, stop the breath suddenly: *b, d, k, p,
q, t*, plus hard *c*/*g* — eight total, of which *k, q*, and hard *c* sound identically). Four semivowels — *l,
m, n, r* — are further distinguished as **liquids**. Brown also gives secondary classes used by some
grammarians (sharp/flat, labial/dental/nasal/palatal) without endorsing all of them as equally useful.

**Vowel/consonant sound inventory:** Brown counts 14 primary vowel sounds (the vowel heard at the start of
*ate, at, ah, all, eel, ell, isle, ill, old, on, ooze, use, us*, plus the *u* of *bull*) and 22 simple consonant sounds,
for roughly 36 elementary sounds total — he is explicit that authorities disagree on the exact count (his own
survey cites schemes ranging from Sheridan's 28 to Dr. James Rush's 35–36 "tonics/subtonics/atonics," which
Brown rejects as needlessly reclassifying the whole vowel/consonant distinction). *J* and *X* represent
complex, not simple, sounds (*dzh*, and *ks* or *gz* respectively) and are therefore never doubled.

**Cross-linguistic comparanda Brown draws on** (useful register/contrast material for this project): the
22-letter Hebrew alphabet, where letter-names are themselves ordinary words with independent meanings
(e.g. *Aleph* "an ox," *Beth* "house," *Gimel* "a camel" — the basis of acrostic structures like Psalm 119);
the 24-letter Greek alphabet (*Alpha, Beta, Gamma...*); the Cherokee syllabary, invented in 1821 by
Sequoyah, which Brown describes (quoting a contemporary Cherokee source, W. S. Coodey, 1831) as an
85-character syllabic system learnable "in a day" because it maps directly onto spoken syllables rather than
onto individual phonemes requiring combination; and Chinese/Japanese logographic writing (cited via Blair's
*Rhetoric*, "not less than seventy thousand" characters, each standing for a whole word). Brown frames
syllabic writing (Cherokee, and the "alphabet of syllables" he says predates true alphabets in Ethiopia and
parts of India) as a historical middle stage between logographic and fully phonemic-alphabetic writing.

**16 rules for capitalization** (Ch. I, pp. 165–168) — genuinely useful as a period-accurate prescriptive norm
distinct from modern capitalization conventions:
1. **Of Books** — chief words in a cited book's title are capitalized ("*Pope's Essay on Man*").
2. **First Words** — every sentence, or separately numbered/paragraphed clause, begins with a capital.
3. **Of the Deity** — names/titles of God, and sometimes emphatic substitutes, are capitalized.
4. **Proper Names** — always capitalized.
5. **Of Titles** — titles of office/honor and epithets of distinction applied to persons, usually capitalized
   ("His Majesty William the Fourth," "Dr. Johnson").
6. **One Capital** — compound proper names that by analogy unite without a hyphen get a single capital
   (*Eastport, Blackrock, Newcastle*).
7. **Two Capitals** — compounds should be split under two capitals when general analogy of similar terms
   favors separation (*East Greenwich*, not *Eastgreenwich*; *New Jersey*).
8. **Of Compounds** — an adjective or common noun made a distinct part of a compound proper name gets a
   capital (*the United States, the Blue Ridge, Long Island*).
9. **Apposition** — when a common and proper name merely explain each other, usually only the proper
   name needs a capital ("the prophet Elisha," "the river Euphrates").
10. **Personifications** — a personified object's name is capitalized only when it conveys a *strictly
    individual* idea (Brown corrects Murray's standard definition of personification — "that figure by which
    we attribute life and action to inanimate objects" — as "doubly wrong," since life/action aren't peculiar to
    persons and personified objects aren't always inanimate; his own definition: "a figure by which, in
    imagination, we ascribe intelligence and personality to unintelligent beings or abstract qualities").
11. **Derivatives** — words derived from proper names with direct reference to a person/place/sect/nation
    take capitals (*Platonic, Grecian, Italicize*).
12. **Of I and O** — the words *I* and *O* (the vocative interjection) are always capitals; Brown is careful to
    distinguish vocative *O* from exclamatory *oh*, which is not covered by this rule.
13. **Of Poetry** — every verse line begins with a capital, except a line continuing one verse with the
    preceding line.
14. **Of Examples** — the first word of a full example, speech, or direct quotation begins with a capital.
15. **Chief Words** — other particularly important words denoting a principal subject may be capitalized at
    the writer's discretion.
16. **Needless Capitals** — capitals are improper without some specific rule or reason; Brown cites heavily
    over-capitalized older books (quoting a 1700 pamphlet: "Many a Noble Genius is lost for want of
    Education...") as the fault this rule corrects.

Brown's extended observations on these rules work through many genuinely hard edge cases still relevant to
style questions today: whether *Lord*/*God* take capitals only when referring to the true Deity vs. as
common nouns or titles of rank; whether *the United States* is a proper name (he argues yes — "If we
contend that it is not then a proper name, we make our country anonymous"); the single-vs-double-capital
compound-place-name problem (*Mount Hope* vs. *Fallriver*/*Fall River*, worked through via five
sub-patterns: adjective+old-name, possessive+noun, noun-*of*-noun, adjective+noun, appellative+proper-noun
apposition); and a close reading of the officially confusing four-fold-ambiguous colonial name "the State of
Rhode Island and Providence Plantations" (1663 charter).

### Chapter II — Of Syllables

A **syllable** is "one or more letters pronounced in one sound," either a whole word or part of one; words are
classed by syllable count as **monosyllable, dissyllable, trissyllable, polysyllable**. English has 29
diphthongs (of 35 possible two-vowel combinations), of which 13 can be "proper" (both vowels sounded) and
only 3 (*ia, io, oy*) are *always* proper; the only true proper triphthong is *uoy* (*buoy*).

**Six rules for syllabication:**
1. **Consonants** — generally join the vowel/diphthong they modify in utterance (*An-ax-ag'-o-ras*).
2. **Vowels** — two vowels not forming a diphthong are parted (*A-o'-ni-an*).
3. **Terminations** — derivative/grammatical endings are generally separated from their root (*harm-less*);
   note this can change meaning: *count-er* vs. *coun-ter* are different words.
4. **Prefixes** — generally form separate syllables (*mis-place*), unless the prefix's own meaning is
   disregarded (*re-create* "to create again" vs. *rec'-reate* "to refresh").
5. **Compounds** — divided into their simple-word components (*boat-swain*).
6. **Lines Full** — a word may break at a line's end, but never mid-syllable.

Brown's key methodological point here (Obs. 1–7): *19th-century spelling-books and dictionaries disagreed
sharply about which of four distinct purposes syllabication should serve* — (1) teaching a child to sound out
unfamiliar words, (2) showing derivation/composition, (3) showing exact pronunciation, or (4) line-breaking
— and a division optimized for one purpose often actively misrepresents another (e.g. an etymology-driven
division like *pri-son* obscures the actual pronunciation `pris-on`). He singles out one contemporary
"literary quackery" case study: William Mulkey's 1833 pamphlet claiming to teach correct English
pronunciation to children "in four or five days" via an apparatus of 29 syllabication rules, 63 vowel-sound
rules, 89 consonant-sound rules, and 156 accent principles — which the City of Boston's School Committee
and Common Council actually funded with $500 in November 1833 — held up as an example of credentialed
institutional bodies being taken in by pseudo-systematic grammatical "reform."

### Chapter III — Of Words: species, figure, and the identity of words

A **word** is "one or more syllables spoken or written as the sign of some idea, or of some manner of
thought." Words are classed along two independent axes: **species** (primitive, not formed from any simpler
word in the language, e.g. *harm*; vs. derivative, e.g. *harmless*) and **figure** (simple, e.g. *watch*; vs.
compound, composed of two+ simple words, e.g. *watchman*). Compounds are further split into **permanent
compounds** (fully consolidated, no hyphen: *bookseller*) and **temporary compounds** (hyphenated:
*good-natured*).

**Six rules for the figure of words (compounding):**
1. **Compounds** — don't needlessly split an established compound (*steamboat*, not *steam boat*).
2. **Simples** — don't compound words that would work fine as a regular phrase of the same meaning (favor
   *in stead of* over *instead*, by Brown's own 1851-era preference — now reversed by modern usage).
3. **The Sense** — compound vs. separate as the sense requires, since the two forms can mean different
   things (*a glass house* = house made of glass, vs. *a glasshouse* = house where glass is manufactured).
4. **Ellipses** — don't split a compound to form an ellipsis of half a word across a coordinated list (don't
   write "*calf, goat, and sheepskins*" for *calfskins, goatskins, and sheepskins*).
5. **The Hyphen (use it)** — use a hyphen when a compound's parts don't fully coalesce, or when each part
   keeps its own accent, i.e. the compound has more than one stress or a movable one (*first-born,
   garlic-eater*).
6. **No Hyphen** — when a compound has collapsed to a single accented syllable and the parts fully coalesce,
   omit the hyphen (*watchword, statesman*).

Brown's extended discussion of hyphenation (Obs. 7–9, quoting Churchill at length) proposes **stress pattern
as the real governing principle**: a compound with one surviving accent (*night'cap, bed'stead*) has fully
coalesced and needs no hyphen; one that keeps two accents (*Chris'tian-name', broad'-shoul'dered*) needs
one. This is offered as a more principled alternative to what Brown calls the arbitrary, inconsistent hyphen
usage of contemporary printers and even of Johnson's and Murray's own dictionaries/grammars (he catalogs
numerous same-dictionary inconsistencies, e.g. Johnson entering *alms-basket*, *alms-giver* with a hyphen
but *almsdeed, almshouse, almsman* without).

**The identity-of-words puzzle (Obs. 5–6):** Brown poses and answers a genuine logical question — what
makes two tokens "the same word"? Not pronunciation alone (*pat'ron*/*pa'tron* dialectal variants), not
spelling alone (*favour*/*favor*), not form of presentation (spoken vs. written), and not meaning alone (true
homonyms, or synonyms). His resolution: identity requires agreement in *at least two* of {orthography,
pronunciation, meaning} — so a pair differing in any two of the three counts as different words, and a pair
agreeing in any two counts as the same word (used, e.g., to argue that *dear* "beloved" and *deer* "animal"
are different words despite identical pronunciation, because they also differ in both spelling and meaning).

**Proper names as compounds carrying residual general meaning** (Obs. 3): Brown argues most proper
names are historically compounds whose parts still carry common-noun meaning, illustrated by a chain of
increasing specificity — *smith* → *goldsmith* → *a goldsmith* → *the goldsmith* → *one Goldsmith* → *Mr.
Goldsmith* → *Oliver Goldsmith* — and uses this to critique the contemporary Quaker ("Friends") practice of
writing the numerical day/month names (*first day*, *second month*) as ordinary lowercase phrases rather
than as the proper-name compounds (*Firstday*, *Secondmonth*) he argues they functionally are.

### Chapter IV — Of Spelling: 15 rules

**Spelling** is "the art of expressing words by their proper letters," and Brown is explicit that it is "to be
acquired rather by means of the spelling-book or dictionary, and by observation in reading, than by the study
of written rules; because what is proper or improper, depends chiefly upon usage" — i.e. even this
prescriptive grammarian treats spelling as usage-governed, not rule-derivable, with the 15 rules offered only
as a partial systematization of the patterns usage already follows:

1. **Final f, l, or s** — a monosyllable ending in *f, l*, or *s* after a single vowel doubles the final consonant
   (*staff, mill, pass*); exceptions include *clef, if, of*, and the many short function words ending single-*s*
   (*as, gas, has, was, yes, his, is, this, us, thus*).
2. **Other finals** — words ending in any other consonant don't double it (*mob, nod, sum*); a handful of
   exceptions exist (*ebb, add, odd, egg, inn, err, butt, buzz*).
3. **Doubling before a vowel suffix** — a monosyllable, or a word accented on the last syllable, ending in a
   single consonant after a single vowel (or *qu*), doubles that consonant before an added vowel-initial
   suffix (*rob → robbed, robber*; *commit → committing*); but *x* final is never doubled (it's already
   equivalent to *ks*), and words whose accent shifts away in the derivative often don't double
   (*prefer' → pref'erence*).
4. **No doubling** — a final consonant not preceded by a single vowel, or not under terminal accent, stays
   single before an added suffix (*toil → toiling*; *visit → visited*); with a notable irregular exception class —
   words ending unaccented in *-el* double the *l* anyway to keep the *e* from reading as silent
   (*travel → traveller*, *gravel → gravelly*), and a further list of unaccented verbs in *-al/-il/-ol* customarily
   double despite the rule (*equal → equalled, rival → rivalling, cavil → cavilled*) — a point of genuine,
   acknowledged 19th-century British/American spelling variation (compare modern AmE *traveling* vs. BrE
   *travelling*, already visible here as disputed).
5. **Final ck** — English monosyllables/verbs take *ck* (not *c* alone) for the "hard-c" sound (*rack, rock*),
   but words from the classical languages generally drop the *k* (*public, music*) — Brown documents (Obs.
   5–10) a running 18th–19th-century dispute over exactly this *-ick* vs. *-ic* question (*publick* vs.
   *public*), tracing it through Johnson (who kept *-ick*), Walker, Ash, Priestley, Lowth, and contemporary
   spelling-book authors (Cobb, Emerson et al.) who were still defending *-ick* as late as the 1830s–40s —
   i.e. a live, actively-contested orthographic variable within Brown's own lifetime, not a settled rule.
6. **Retaining double letters before suffixes** — a final double letter stays double before a suffix not starting
   with the same letter (*wooer, blissful, oddly*), with irregular exceptions (*flee → fled*, *sell → sold*,
   *dwell → dwelt*, *spell → spelt*, *bless → blest*, *pass → past*).
7. **Retaining double letters after prefixes** — stays double when a prefix (not a suffix) is added
   (*see → foresee*, *pass → repass*, *spell → misspell*, *sell → undersell*).
8. **Final ll** — final double-*l* is peculiar to monosyllables and their direct compounds; all other words
   ending in *l* take single *l* (*cabal, logical, control, consul*).
9. **Final e dropped before a vowel suffix** — a primitive word's mute/obscure final *e* is generally dropped
   before an added vowel-initial suffix (*remove → removal*, *rate → ratable*), except words in *-ce/-ge*
   which keep the *e* to preserve the soft *c*/*g* sound (*trace → traceable*, *change → changeable*).
10. **Final e kept before a consonant suffix** — generally retained before a consonant-initial suffix
    (*pale → paleness*, *judge → judgeship*); Brown explicitly defends keeping the *e* in *judgement,
    abridgement, acknowledgement* against those who'd drop it, "upon the authority of Lowth, Beattie,
    Ainsworth, Walker, Cobb, Chalmers" — a spelling now standard in British English but largely lost to
    *judgment* etc. in American usage.
11. **Final y → i after a consonant** — a primitive word's final *y* after a consonant generally changes to *i*
    before an added suffix (*merry → merrier, merriment*; *pity → pitied, pitiless*), except before *-ing/-ish*
    (to avoid doubling *i*: *pity → pitying*) and in words from *-ie* roots that already dropped *e* under Rule
    9 (*die → dying*, *lie → lying*).
12. **Final y unchanged after a vowel** — stays *y* after a vowel before any suffix (*day → days*,
    *boy → boyish*); notable irregulars *laid, paid, said, staid* from *lay, pay, say, stay*.
13. **-ize vs. -ise** — words of this sound-class generally take *z* when formed productively by the suffix
    itself (*apologize, brutalize, sympathize*), and *s* in monosyllables and words essentially built on a prefix
    (*rise, arise, disguise, advise, despise*); Brown flags *advertise, catechise, chastise, criticise, exercise,
    exorcise, merchandise* as conventionally *-s-*-spelled exceptions, and *size, assize, capsize, analyze,
    recognize* as conventionally *-z-*-spelled exceptions to his own rule.
14. **Compounds generally keep their simple words' spelling** (*wherein, horseman, uphill*), with several
    named exceptions: *full/all* drop one *l* in permanent compounds (*handful, careful, fulfil, always,
    although*) but keep both in temporary/hyphenated ones (*full-eyed, all-wise*); *mass* drops one *s* in day
    names (*Christmas, Candlemas, Lammas*); *shepherd, wherever, whosever* drop an *e*.
15. **Usage** — for anything not covered by a rule, the test is simply "the usage which is most common
    among the learned."

**Notable live orthographic disputes Brown documents as unsettled in his own day** (useful as period
sociolinguistic/register data): *-or* vs. *-our* (he counts ~320 words with this alternation, of which he judges
only ~40 currently retain *-our*: *honour, labour, favour, behaviour, endeavour*); *-ick/-ic* and *-our/-or*
spellings as markers distinguishing Johnson's (older, fuller) orthography from Webster's and the
"best usage of the present day" (leaner); and extensive catalogued inconsistency even within a single
dictionary or grammar (Johnson's own *Dictionary* mixing *immovable* with *moveable*, *fearlessly* with
*fearlesness*). Brown is explicit that "THE BEST USAGE is the ultimate rule of grammar" — i.e. he
repeatedly subordinates even his own rule-system to attested contemporary usage when the two conflict,
consistent with the "good use" standard already established in the Introduction (see `pt01`).

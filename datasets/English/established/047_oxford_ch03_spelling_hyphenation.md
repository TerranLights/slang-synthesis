**Non-redundant-supplement note (per the extraction spec's "multiple sources" dispatch pattern):**
English already has four established files extracting *The Chicago Manual of Style* (16th ed.,
2010) Chapter 7, "Spelling, Distinctive Treatment of Words, and Compounds"
(`017`–`020_cmos_ch07_spelling_compounds_pt01–04.md`), which covers closely related territory —
plurals, possessives, word division, hyphenation of compounds and prefixes — from an American
perspective. This Oxford chapter is **not treated as redundant** with that prior coverage: it is
this project's first dedicated British-English spelling/hyphenation source, it is organized around a
different (British-first) rule set with its own exceptions (British consonant-doubling in `-l`
verbs, the `-ise`/`-ize` choice, `-able`/`-ible` and `-ment`/`-logue`/`-ce`/`-se` spelling families
CMOS doesn't cover at all), and it supplies extensive explicit British/US contrast pairs not present
in CMOS (which states US rules with only occasional non-US asides). Genuine topical overlap exists
in plurals-of-nouns, compound-noun plurals, and compound-hyphenation principles; where this chapter's
treatment is substantively the same general principle as CMOS's (e.g. hyphenate a compound modifier
before but not after a noun), it is still captured here because the specific examples and the
British-vs-US framing are new, not because the underlying principle is novel. Nothing from this
chapter was skipped as redundant.

**Large illustrative word lists are tabled one row per pattern/category (with a representative
subset of examples in the Notes column), not one row per individual word** — following the
precedent set by `020_cmos_ch07_spelling_compounds_pt04.md` for the CMOS hyphenation-guide table.
This avoids near-verbatim reproduction of the source's own compiled lists (copyright discipline)
while still capturing every distinct *pattern*. Compound-noun plurals (e.g. *brother-in-law* →
*brothers-in-law*) are tabled individually, since each is a genuinely distinct lexical item with its
own irregular plural, not an arbitrary illustration of one shared rule — matching the granularity
CMOS used for its own individually-tabled contractions (*gonna*, *wanna*, etc.).

## Grammar points

### 3.1.1 General principles

A good dictionary (*Concise Oxford English Dictionary*, *Oxford Dictionary of English*; *New Oxford
American Dictionary* for US texts) should settle matters of spelling and inflection. English
tolerates genuine free-variant spellings (*bannister*/*banister*) as well as context-dependent
spellings (*judgement* general British vs. *judgment* legal/US), and these are distinct from simple
errors that happen to be common in print (*accomodation*, *millenium*). An editor need not alter a
writer's *consistently* applied acceptable variant, but should standardize comparable/related words
to match once a choice is made (p. 49).

### 3.1.2 British and US spelling

Eight general tendencies distinguish US from British spelling (listed in the vocabulary table
above): `e` for `ae`/`oe`; `-ense` for `-ence`; `-er` for `-re`; `k` for `c`; `-ll` for `-l`; `o` for
`ou`; `-og` for `-ogue`; `-or` for `-our`; `z` for `s`. *Fetus/foetus* splits by register within
British English itself rather than following the US/UK pattern cleanly (p. 50).

### 3.1.3 Verbs ending in -ise or -ize

Either `-ize` or `-ise` is acceptable for most such British verbs; `-ize` has been in English use
since the 16th century and is not an Americanism, though `-ise` is now far more common in Britain
than in the US. Oxford University Press has traditionally favored `-ize` on phonetic/etymological
grounds (it reflects the Greek root `-izo` more closely). `-ise` is obligatory, not optional, for a
subset of words — either because it forms part of a larger word element (`-cise`, `-mise`, `-prise`,
`-vise`) or because the word corresponds to a noun with `-s-` in its stem (*advertise*/
*advertisement*). British `-yse` words (*analyse*, *paralyse*) have no `-yze` alternative; US English
prefers `-yze` (pp. 49–50).

### 3.1.4 -ie- and -ei-

"I before e except after c" is generally valid when the combination is pronounced "ee" (*believe,
ceiling, receive*), with *seize* as a notable exception. Words like *caffeine*, *codeine*, *protein*
are outside the rule's scope entirely (formed from a root plus `-in`/`-ine`, not a true `ie`/`ei`
digraph), as is *plebeian* (from Latin *plebeius*). The rule does not apply when the syllable is
pronounced otherwise: as in "lake" (*beige, neighbour, weigh*), as in "like" (*height, sleight*), or
when *i* and *e* are pronounced as separate vowels (*holier, occupier*) (p. 50).

### 3.1.5 -able and -ible

The choice relates to etymology: `-able` descends from Latin `-abilis`/Old French `-able`; `-ible`
from Latin `-ibilis`. New English-root adjectives always take `-able` (*conceivable*), never
`-ible`. Words ending in a silent `-e` normally drop it before `-able` (*adorable*), but British
one-syllable words may retain it to avoid ambiguity (*likeable*, *sizeable* — US drops the `-e`
here: *likable*, *sizable*). Words ending `-ce`/`-ge` always retain the `-e` before `-able` in both
varieties, to preserve the soft consonant sound (*changeable*, *noticeable*); words ending in a hard
`c`/`g` never retain it (*amicable*, *navigable*) (pp. 50–51).

### 3.1.6 Nouns ending in -ment

When `-ment` is added to a verb ending `-dge`, British English retains the final `-e`
(*abridgement*, *judgement* — though *judgment* remains the usual legal-context form); US English
generally drops it (*abridgment*, *judgment*) (p. 51).

### 3.1.7 Nouns ending in -logue

Some but not all British `-logue` nouns take `-log` in American English. *Analogue* and *catalogue*
usually become `-log` in the US; *dialogue* and *homologue* merely accept `-log` as one acceptable
variant rather than preferring it (p. 51).

### 3.1.8 -ce and -se endings

*Practice* is the noun spelling in both varieties and also the US verb spelling; British English
spells the verb *practise*. Likewise *licence* (noun, British) vs. *license* (verb, British; both
noun and verb in US English). *Defence/pretence* (British) correspond to *defense/pretense* (US)
(p. 52).

### 3.1.9 -ae- in the middle of words

The `-ae-` spellings of *encyclopaedia* and *mediaeval* are being superseded by `-e-` forms
(*encyclopedia*, *medieval*) though both remain acceptable; the dated ligature `æ` should generally
be avoided (exception: the stylized title *Encyclopædia Britannica*). Largely technical words such
as *Archaean*/*haematology* retain `-ae-` in British English but take `-e-` in US English
(*Archean*/*hematology*); the `-e-` form predominates in technical writing generally regardless of
British or US origin (p. 52).

### 3.2.1 Verbs (inflection — consonant doubling)

One-syllable verbs ending in a single consonant double it before `-ing`/`-ed` (*beg/begging*),
except when the final consonant is `w`, `x`, or `y` (*tow/towing*), or when it's preceded by more
than one vowel or a long-vowel diphthong (*appeal/appealing*). Multi-syllable verbs double the
consonant only when stress falls on the final syllable (*allot/allotting* vs. *benefit/benefiting*),
with named exceptions that double despite non-final stress (*input, output, kidnap, worship*) and
*focus*, which optionally doubles its `s` (*focussing*). British `-l`-final verbs double the `l`
regardless of stress position (*grovel/grovelling, travel/travelling*) — the single largest
systematic British/US divergence in this section, since US English doubles `-l` only under the
general final-stress rule, giving *groveled, traveled, tunneled*. *Parallel* is a British exception
that does not double. *Install* has a double `l` in both varieties, but its derived noun splits:
British *instalment*, US *installment*. Verbs generally drop a silent final `-e` before a
vowel-initial suffix (*argue/arguing*), but British verbs with a soft `g` retain it (*ageing,
twingeing*; not in US spelling) — distinguishing *singeing* (from *singe*) from *singing* (from
*sing*), and *swingeing* (from *swinge*) from *swinging* (from *swing*); *raging* is an exception
that does not retain the `-e`. *Dyeing* (from *dye*) inserts an `-e-` specifically to stay distinct
from *dying* (from *die*). A small group of verbs (*burn, learn, spell*) has an alternative British
past form in `-t` (*burnt, learnt, spelt*) alongside the orthodox `-ed` form; *earn* and *deal* do
not participate in this alternation — always *earned* (never *earnt*), always *dealt* (never
*dealed*), in both varieties (pp. 52–54).

### 3.2.2 Plurals of nouns

`-y`-ending nouns form plurals with `-ies` unless the ending is `-ey`, which takes `-eys`; proper
names ending `-y` simply add `-s`, with no apostrophe. Nouns ending `-f`/`-fe` form plurals
unpredictably — sometimes `-fs`/`-fes` (*roof/roofs*), sometimes `-ves` (*knife/knives*), sometimes
either (*dwarf/dwarfs or dwarves*). `-o`-final nouns have no fixed plural system; the chapter offers
guideline tendencies by word category (vowel-preceded, shortenings, recent foreign borrowings,
multisyllabic, allusive proper names tend toward `-os`; animal/plant names tend toward `-oes`) rather
than a hard rule, and notes some words have two established forms (*mementoes*/*mementos*). Compound
nouns formed from a noun plus adjective, or two nouns joined by a preposition, generally pluralize
their key word (*brothers-in-law*, *passers-by*), with *point-to-point* as a listed exception that
pluralizes the whole form. Some animal names (*deer, grouse, salmon, sheep*) keep an unchanged
plural — the "hunting plural," applied especially to larger/hunted species, never small ones like
mice or rats; some (*lion*) allow it optionally by context. *Fish* is normally unchanged in the
plural, with the older form *fishes* surviving specifically to denote multiple distinct kinds.
Foreign-derived nouns' plurals follow either the source language's own rule (*alumnus/alumni*) or an
Anglicized rule (*arena/arenas*), and many have both forms in live use (*bureau/bureaus or
bureaux*); the chapter generally recommends preferring the English form where one has become
dominant (*formulas* over *formulae*, *stadiums* over *stadia*, *forums* over *fora*) and notes
domain-split cases where the same singular has two established but differently-used plurals
(*index/indexes* for books vs. *index/indices* for statistics/math; *appendix/appendices* for
subsidiary tables vs. *appendix/appendixes* for the body part) (pp. 54–57).

### 3.2.3 Adjectives

Adjectives form comparatives/superlatives with `-er`/`-est` in several syllable/ending-defined
groups — one-syllable words, two-syllable `-y`/`-ly` words (with corresponding `un-` forms; `y`
changes to `i`), two-syllable words ending `-le`/`-ow`/some ending `-er`, some second-syllable-stress
two-syllable words, and a residual uncategorizable group. One-syllable `-er`/`-est` adjectives
double a final consonant preceded by a single vowel (*glad/gladder*), not when preceded by more than
one vowel or a long-vowel diphthong (*clean/cleaner*). British two-syllable `-l`-ending adjectives
double the `l` (*cruel/crueller*). Adjectives of three or more syllables always use *more*/*most*
rather than `-er`/`-est` (p. 57).

### 3.2.4 Adverbs

`-ly` adverbs formed from adjectives generally use *more*/*most*, not `-er`/`-est` forms. Adverbs
identical in form to their corresponding adjective (not `-ly`-formed) do take `-er`/`-est` (*runs
faster, hits hardest*), as do a small set of independent adverbs (*soon, likely*) (pp. 57–58).

### 3.3.1 General principles (Hyphenation)

Hyphenation depends heavily on a word's/phrase's grammatical role and position, and partly on
adopted house style or personal taste, so it cannot be covered exhaustively by a dictionary alone;
this section sets out basic principles and current thinking (p. 58).

### 3.3.2 Soft and hard hyphens

There are two kinds of hyphen: the **hard hyphen**, which joins words/word-parts to form a compound
(*anti-nuclear, glow-worm, second-rate*); and the **soft hyphen**, which marks a line-break word
division (covered in §3.4) (p. 58).

### 3.3.3 Compound words

A compound may be open, hyphenated, or closed, and there is an increasing tendency to avoid
hyphenation for noun compounds (*airstream*, not *air-stream*). Some compounds are hyphenated to
avoid an awkward vowel/consonant collision (*drip-proof, take-off*), though some such collisions are
nonetheless written solid (*breaststroke, earring, hitchhiker*). The formerly operative "walking-stick
rule" — spacing a present-participle-plus-noun compound when the noun performs the action, but
hyphenating it when the compound itself is acted upon — "is no longer borne out in common use": such
combinations (*walking stick, clearing house, colouring book, dining room*) are now generally written
as two words. Compound modifiers following a noun do not need hyphens (*the story is well known*),
but the same modifier preceding the noun as a unit generally does (*a well-known story*). A further
distinction separates compounds of adjective+noun, which hyphenate before a noun (*first-class
seats*), from true compound nouns, which don't need to even before a noun (*labour market
liberalization*). Compound adjectives formed from an adjective and a verb participle hyphenate
whether before or after the noun (*double-breasted suits; Darren was quite good-looking*). Verbs
derived from a two-word compound noun are normally hyphenated (*to machine-gun*, from *a machine
gun*); compounds containing a noun/adjective derived from a verb are more often hyphenated
(*glass-making*) than purely non-verbal noun/adjective compounds (*science fiction*). House styles
vary considerably on compound nouns, and familiarity with a readership's expectations matters as much
as any rule (*climate change* would be unacceptable hyphenated; *jobseeker* would read oddly as two
words, at least in the UK, given the fixed term *Jobseeker's Allowance*). Agreement is more common
for compound adjectives (*decision-making process*) than nouns (*policymaking* vs. *policy-making*
vs. *policy making*, all in live use), though there is a general drift toward fewer hyphens
(*peacekeeping force, high quality teaching*) except where ambiguity would result (*cross-training of
staff*, to distinguish from *cross training*). A phrasal verb made into a noun takes a hyphen or
becomes solid (*a hold-up, some backup*), but the phrasal verb itself should never be hyphenated
(*continue to build up your pension*, not *build-up*). Adjectival compounds whose first element is an
`-ly` adverb are never hyphenated (*a happily married couple*); non-`-ly` adverbs are hyphenated
before a noun but not after (*a much-loved broadcaster* / *Dr Gray was very well known*). Italicized
foreign phrases are not hyphenated in English unless hyphenated in the original language (*ex post
facto*, *ad hominem* vs. *romans-fleuves*, *savoir-vivre*); once naturalized and no longer italicized,
they follow ordinary English hyphenation conventions (*laissez-faire* vs. *savoir faire*).
Capitalized compounds are generally not hyphenated (*British Museum staff*), and compound scientific
terms are usually spaced or closed rather than hyphenated (*herpesvirus, liquid crystal display*).
When a hyphenated compound is itself capitalized (as a title/heading), the traditional rule capitalizes
only the first element unless the second is a proper noun (*First-class and Club Passengers*), though
many modern styles capitalize both elements (*First-Class and Club Passengers*) (pp. 58–61).

### 3.3.4 Prefixes and combining forms

Prefixed words are often written solid, especially in US English (*predetermine, antistatic*), but
take a hyphen to prevent a confusing or mispronounceable vowel/consonant collision (*anti-intellectual,
de-ice, ex-directory*) — with notable closed exceptions kept for other reasons: *predate* (to avoid
confusion with "catch prey"), and *cooperate, coordinate, microorganism, preoperative* (closed by
convention despite the vowel collision, *preoperative* specifically to match *postoperative*). A
hyphen also disambiguates a doubled/repeated prefix (*re-release, sub-subcategory*) or a clash with an
unrelated existing word (*re-form* vs. *reform*; *re-cover* vs. *recover*). Prefixes and combining
forms hyphenate before a capitalized name, numeral, or date (*anti-Darwinism, pre-1990s*). *Ex-*
denoting a former state is normally hyphenated (*ex-husband*), but there is no fully satisfactory
treatment when the base is itself a compound (*ex-Prime Minister*) — a second hyphen is not
recommended, and rewording with "former" is the suggested solution; US style instead uses an en rule
to join a prefix to a compound (*the post–World War I period*). *Mid-* now functions as a free-standing
adjective in some constructions (*the mid nineteenth century*) but remains a hyphenated prefix before
a noun in others (*a mid-grey tone*). *Email* and *website* are now commonly one word (though *web
page* stays open); *ebook* is gaining ground over *e-book*, while less-familiar terms like
*e-learning* remain hyphenated (pp. 61–62).

### 3.3.5 Suffixes

Suffixes are always hyphenated or closed, never spaced. `-less`/`-like` need a hyphen specifically
when the preceding word already ends in two `l`s (*bell-less, shell-like*). `-like` and similar
suffix-words are hyphenated for newly coined/rare combinations or with proper names (*tortoise-like,
Paris-like*), but set solid once established (*catlike, ladylike*). `-proof`, `-scape`, and `-wide`
usually need no hyphen (*childproof, moonscape, nationwide*). When a complete word functions like a
suffix after a noun, adjective, or adverb, a hyphen is particularly important unless the preceding
word is an `-ly` adverb (*military-style, risk-averse* vs. *an environmentally friendly policy*) — a
real source of ambiguity, as in *a cycling-friendly chief executive* vs. *a cycling friendly chief
executive* (pp. 62–63).

### 3.3.6 Numbers

Spelled-out numbers from 21 to 99 are hyphenated (*twenty-three, thirty-fourth*); full discussion is
in Chapter 11, outside this chunk's page range (p. 63).

### 3.3.7 Compass points

British compass points are hyphenated (*south-east, south-south-east*), though the corresponding
compound wind names are closed (*southeaster, northwesterly*). US usage closes individual compass
points instead (*southeast, south-southeast*). Capitalized compass-point compounds are not usually
hyphenated, and the prevailing regional-name form itself differs by variety: *South East Asia*
(British) vs. *Southeast Asia* (US) (p. 63).

### 3.3.8 Other uses

Hyphens render stammering or paused speech (*'P-p-perhaps not'*), mark an omitted common element
shared across a short list via a dangling hyphen (*three- and six-cylinder models*) — spelling each
word out in full instead when the shared element would be unfamiliar to the reader if abbreviated
(*ectomorphs, endomorphs, mesomorphs*) — and join double-barrelled personal names (*Krafft-Ebing*).
Compound nouns/adjectives derived from two proper names generally take an en rule (*Marxism–Leninism*),
though a hyphen is sometimes used for the adjective form instead (*Marxist-Leninist*); a hyphen
(never an en rule) is always used when the first element of the compound cannot stand alone as an
independent word (*Sino-Soviet relations*) (pp. 63–64).

### 3.4.1 General principles (Word division)

Words may be divided at line-ends to avoid unacceptably wide word-spacing (in justified text) or
line overruns (in unjustified text); the narrower the column, the more necessary this becomes.
Division rules combine a word's construction (the parts it's formed from) and its pronunciation,
since relying exclusively on either in isolation can produce misleading or confusing results. A
soft/discretionary hyphen marks an end-of-line division (*con-trary*); dividing a word that already
has a permanent (hard) hyphen adds no further hyphen (*well-/developed*). Different symbols
sometimes distinguish a permanent hyphen from a discretionary one in reference works — an alternate
hyphen glyph, or occasionally a tilde (p. 64–65).

### 3.4.2 Principles of word division

The overriding principle is that word division should be as unobtrusive as possible, so the reader
doesn't falter. Divisions should correspond to syllable boundaries where possible (*con-tact*), but
not where the result leaves a misleading, non-recognizable fragment (neither *aba-ses* nor
*abas-es* works well for *abases*); an acceptable division for a bare word can become unacceptable
once a prefix/suffix is added (*help-ful* is fine, but *unhelp-ful* is worse than *un-helpful*). The
*New Oxford Spelling Dictionary* accordingly marks two tiers — "preferred" divisions, acceptable
under almost any circumstance, and "permitted" divisions, usable but not as good a choice. Whether
construction or pronunciation should govern a given division depends on how familiar the word is and
how clearly its parts are perceived: the very familiar *atmosphere* divides by pronunciation
(*atmos-phere*), while the less familiar *hydrosphere* divides by its word-formation elements
(*hydro-sphere*) (pp. 65–66).

### 3.4.3 Special rules

One-syllable words are never divided (*though, prayer, helped*); a single letter should never be
left at the end of a line (*aground*, not *a-ground*); fewer than three letters should not begin a
new line (*Briton*, not *Brit-on*) unless those two letters form a complete recognizable suffix/
element (*acrobat-ic, clean-er, vivid-ly, out-do*) — so nearly all words under six letters should
never be divided at all. Divide by construction where it's unambiguous — between compound elements
(*table-spoon*), between a root and a prefix/suffix (*un-prepared*) — except where that division would
badly clash with the word's pronunciation (*dem-ocracy*, not *demo-cracy*). Where construction gives
no guidance, divide after a vowel (preferably unstressed: *preju-dice*) or between two consonants/
vowels that are pronounced separately (*splen-dour, appreci-ate*). Divide after a double consonant if
it ends a recognizable word-element (*chill-ing*), otherwise divide between the pair (*shil-ling,
un-natural*). Words ending `-le` and their inflections are best left undivided, carrying the last
four letters over if necessary (*brin-dled*); their present participles divide after the consonant
preceding the `l` (*chuck-ling*) or between two identical preceding consonants (*puz-zling*). Most
gerunds/present participles divide at `-ing` (*carry-ing, tell-ing*). Divisions that would affect the
word's apparent sound, confuse its meaning, or simply look odd should be avoided (*exact-ing*, not
*ex-acting*; *re-appear*, not *reap-pear*); some words cannot be divided at all without an odd effect
and should be left whole (*breeches, cliquey, preaches, sluicing*). Continuing onto p. 68: a
hard-hyphenated word, if it must be divided further, should get a minimum of six letters carried
after the hard hyphen (*counter-revolution-/ary*). Further line-break constraints independent of
hyphenation: abbreviations and dates divide only at an existing hard hyphen or en rule (*AFL-/CIO*,
*1914-/18*); numbers are never divided, even at a decimal point, though very large comma-separated
numbers may divide after (not at) a comma, never after a single digit, and cannot be divided at all
if written with spaces instead of commas; a number should never be separated from its abbreviated
unit (*15 kg*); a person's initials should not, if avoidable, be separated from the surname, and two
initials should never be split from each other; a name should never be separated from a following
modifier (*Louis XIV*, *Samuel Browne, Jr.*) (pp. 66–68).

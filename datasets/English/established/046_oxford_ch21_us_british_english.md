## Grammar points

### Punctuation terminology divide (§21.2)

The two dialects largely share punctuation *marks* but diverge sharply on what to *call* them (see
the terminology rows in the Vocabulary table above: full point/period, exclamation mark/point, em
rule/em dash, en rule/en dash, curly brackets/braces/wavy brackets, solidus/slash, inverted
commas/quotation marks, round brackets/parentheses). The source frames British terms as its baseline
throughout the rest of the manual and flags the US equivalents as departures (p. 408).

### Comma conventions (§21.2.1)

US style inserts a comma after *for example* and *that is* whenever they occur and are not followed
by stronger punctuation (e.g. a colon), and extends this to their abbreviations *e.g.* and *i.e.*
even where it produces double punctuation (a comma immediately after the abbreviation's own period).
US style also places commas inside closing quotation marks, regardless of whether the comma is
logically part of the quoted material — this is the same "typesetters' style" placement rule as US
full points (below), contrasted with British "logical" placement.

### Colon conventions for time (§21.2.2)

US style separates hours and minutes with a colon (*5:23 am*); British style more often uses a full
point (*5.23 am*). Independently, the capitalization of the first word after a colon differs: US
style more often capitalizes it, British style keeps it lower case.

### Full point placement with quotation marks (§21.2.3)

Mirrors the comma rule above: US style places full points inside closing quotation marks as a fixed
typographic convention, independent of whether the period is logically part of the quoted content.

### Dashes (§21.2.4)

In US usage, en rules (there called en dashes) join open (unhyphenated, multi-word) elements into a
compound, e.g. *the post–Civil War armistice*. A plain hyphen, not an en rule, is used for a compound
derived from two proper names, e.g. *Marxist-Leninist theory* — the source treats these as two
distinct construction rules rather than one, since the Civil War case has an internal multi-word
element (*Civil War*) that the Marxist-Leninist case lacks.

### US customary units vs. British metric (§21.3.1–21.3.2)

US non-technical/journalistic writing defaults to the imperial system throughout: inches/feet/yards/
miles for length, square inches/feet/acres/square miles for area, cubic inches/feet for volume,
pints/quarts/gallons/bushels for liquid and dry volume, ounces/pounds/tons for mass, and the
Fahrenheit scale for temperature. US recipes additionally use the *cup* (half a US pint, or 8
ounces). British usage instead defaults to the metric system for linear/area/volumetric measurement
and the Celsius scale for temperature — but with notable **residual imperial holdouts even in modern
British usage**: distances are still commonly given in miles rather than kilometers, and a person's
body weight is conventionally reported in `stone` (see Vocabulary table) with any remainder in
pounds, not in kilograms. The idiom *below zero* is dialect-relative in what it actually denotes: in
British usage it means "below freezing" (0°C); in US usage it means "below zero on the Fahrenheit
scale," which is roughly −18°C — a case where an idiom with no lexical difference at all still
encodes a real numerical discrepancy across dialects.

**Imperial unit mismatch:** British and US imperial volume units larger than the ounce are not
numerically equivalent even when the *name* is shared — a British pint is 20 ounces, a US pint only
16. The source notes this rarely causes real confusion today because the larger shared-name units
(quart, gallon) have fallen out of common UK use, but flags that any cookery-context pint conversion
still needs to be handled carefully.

### Other numerical conventions (§21.3.3)

The *trillion*/*billion* divergence between the dialects is characterized by the source as "now
mainly historical" (cross-referenced to §11.1.5, not itself included in this page range). `Nought` is
not used in US English for zero; US uses `zero` in technical contexts and `nothing` for sports
scores. US and Canadian telephone numbers are uniformly 10 digits (3-digit area code + 7-digit
number), formatted as `(nnn) nnn-nnnn` or `nnn-nnn-nnnn`, with a leading `1` required when dialing
outside the local area code from most landlines. UK telephone numbers vary in length and typically
split into a geographic/mobile-grouping prefix (sometimes bracketed, e.g. `(0nnnn) nnnnnn`) plus the
rest of the number, variably with or without brackets/internal spacing. International dialing
increasingly uses a `+` prefix before the country code (dropping the leading zero) in both Britain and
elsewhere, and increasingly in the US too; a US-specific alternative shows the country code in
parentheses at the number's start.

### Date format divergence (§21.3.3)

British dates are conventionally ordered day/month/year; US dates month/day/year. The source warns
this is a genuine misunderstanding risk (not merely a style preference) when "Americanizing" or
"Briticizing" a date, and recommends spelling out the month name (or using its standard abbreviation)
for any document with a mixed-dialect readership, to sidestep the ambiguity entirely rather than
relying on the reader to infer the convention in use.

### Building floor numbering offset (§21.3.3)

Both dialects can call the ground-level floor the `ground floor`. But US English also calls that same
ground-level floor the `first floor`, with each floor above numbered one higher (US `second floor` =
one level above ground). British English reserves `first floor` for the level *above* the ground
floor, so British `first floor` = US `second floor`, and so on up the building — a one-level
systematic offset rather than an isolated vocabulary difference, with real practical consequences for
navigating buildings across dialects.

### Orthographic variation: scope and scale (§21.4.1)

The source frames spelling variation as the single most visually obvious cross-dialect difference,
noting there are "hundreds" of spelling variants between British and US English (and between any two
dialects of English more broadly). Most fall into identifiable systematic patterns already covered
elsewhere in the manual (cross-referenced to §3.1–3.2, not in this page range); this chapter instead
catalogs the harder case — individual words that *don't* conform to a single predictable pattern and
so are more likely to slip past both a human editor and spellchecking software (Table 21.1).

### Spelling-governed sense-splitting (§21.4.2)

A distinct phenomenon from ordinary spelling variants: for a handful of words, one dialect uses a
*single* spelling to cover multiple senses while the other dialect splits those same senses across
*two different spellings* (Table 21.2). E.g. British distinguishes `cheque` (a bank instrument) from
`check` (all other senses); US English collapses both senses onto `check`. The source frames this as
requiring closer editorial scrutiny than ordinary spelling substitution, since a careless global
find-and-replace across the pair can silently corrupt meaning rather than just surface form.

### Compound-word formation preferences (§21.4.3)

US English is markedly more willing than British English to spell new compounds *solid* (no space,
no hyphen) rather than open or hyphenated. The source's named categories: the intermediate compass
directions (*northeast*, *southwest*), compounds beginning with *anti-*, *non-*, and *semi-*
(*antinuclear*, *noninvasive*, *semitrailer*), nonce compounds ending in *-like* (*adobelike*), and
*percent* (always solid in the US, but *per cent*, two words, in British English). An exception
running the other direction: *no one* is always two words in the US, but is sometimes spelled
*no-one* in British English. More generally, US English favors eliminating a hyphen introduced by a
prefix or suffix, except where the hyphen is functionally necessary to prevent an unwanted doubled
vowel or tripled consonant at the morpheme boundary.

### Abbreviation and initialism punctuation (§21.4.4)

Full points (periods) are expected at the end of contractions like *Mr*, *Ms*, *Dr*, *St* in US
English, but are absent in British English for the same forms. Acronyms and abbreviations are
presented with broad conformity across both dialects otherwise. One named exception: British English
can use `Aids` as a non-technical-context spelling, where US English uses the fully capitalized
`AIDS` for *acquired immune deficiency syndrome*.

### Lexical variation: the `-ism` category (§21.5.1)

The source uses "-isms" as a cover term for words/expressions that originated in, or are used
exclusively in, one variety of English (Americanisms, Britishisms/Canadianisms/Australianisms, etc.)
— distinguished from the Table 21.3 category below in that an `-ism` pair genuinely has *two different
words* for the same referent (e.g. `aubergine`/`eggplant`), not one shared word carrying two different
senses.

### Lexical variation: shared word, dialect-dependent meaning (§21.5.2)

A second, more treacherous category (Table 21.3): a single word-form used in similar surface contexts
in both dialects but carrying a genuinely different — sometimes contrasting, sometimes outright
inverted — meaning per dialect (e.g. `mad` = insane in British vs. angry in US; `moot` = debatable in
British vs. settled/insignificant in US; `table` (verb) = bring forward in British vs. remove from
consideration in US). The source frames this as the more dangerous category precisely because the
word's familiarity in both dialects can mask the fact that the writer intended a different meaning
than the reader in the other dialect will supply — see the full set captured in the Vocabulary table
above.

### Offensive/taboo vocabulary divergence (§21.5.6)

Most offensive language is shared across dialects, and the source advises careful writers avoid it
regardless of dialect. But a specific subset of words is "differently perceived and used" across the
two dialects and needs case-by-case editorial scrutiny when localizing text (Table 21.5: `fit`,
`fanny`, `spunk` — see Vocabulary table for the per-dialect senses). Separately, the source names a
set of slang/informal terms whose *ambiguity itself* is the basis of humorous wordplay in informal
writing (`dick`, `fag`, `faggot`, `knock-up`, `randy`, `rear-ender`, `rubber`, `shag`, `willie`) and
recommends these specific terms be left alone when localizing a text rather than "corrected," unless
there's a real risk of an unintended offensive misreading.

### Preferred participle inflections: `-t` vs. `-ed` (§21.5.3)

A small, specific set of verbs (`burn`, `lean`, `learn`, `smell`, `spell`, `spill`, `spoil`) has a
participle form ending in `-t` that British English uses in finite verb phrases but US English does
not — US English replaces these with the regular `-ed` equivalents. `Burnt` is a partial exception:
it is occasionally retained in US English, but only in adjectival use, never as a verb form.

### `got` vs. `gotten` (§21.5.3)

British English does not use `gotten` as a past participle of `get` at all — when "Briticizing" a
text, every `gotten` simply becomes `got`. US English maintains a semantic split the source presents
as a genuine rule, not free variation: `got` covers the "possess" sense (*I've got a beach house in
Florida*) and the "have as an obligation" sense (*I've got to go to Chicago next week*); `gotten` is
the preferred form for the "obtain" sense (*Have you gotten your results yet?*) and the "become" sense
(*She hasn't gotten any prettier over the years*), and is also the more usual form inside several
`get`-idioms/phrasal verbs: *get used to something*, *get over something*, *get through something*,
*get rid of something*, *get nowhere/anywhere*. "Americanizing" text therefore cannot mechanically
restore every British `got` to `gotten` — the correct US form depends on which of these senses is
actually meant.

### Variants with a common etymology (§21.5.4)

A distinct category from both the plain-spelling-variant (Table 21.1) and sense-splitting (Table
21.2) cases above: word pairs that derive wholly or partly from a shared ancestor but have settled on
genuinely distinct preferred forms in the two dialects, not reducible to a predictable spelling rule
(Table 21.4 — 24 pairs, e.g. `airplane`/`aeroplane`, `math`/`maths`, `specialty`/`specialism`; see
Vocabulary table for the full list).

### Function-word variability (§21.5.5)

A catalog of minor but real differences in prepositions, conjunctions, and articles that rarely cause
actual misunderstanding but mark a text as belonging to one dialect or the other:
- `around`/`round`: US English does not use `round` as a preposition or adverb at all; `around`
  covers every case.
- `around`/`about`: many phrasal verbs differ only in this particle — British prefers `about`, US
  prefers `around` (*mess around/about*, *hang around/about*, *lie around/about*).
- `inside`: as a preposition, may take `of` in US English (*inside of the box*), not usually in
  British.
- `amidst`/`amongst`/`whilst`: generally avoided in US English, which prefers `amid`/`among`/`while`.
- `different from`/`different than`/`different to`: both dialects use `different from`; `different
  than` is mainly a US usage; `different to` is exclusively British.
- Idioms/set phrases taking a different preposition per dialect: British *at the weekend*, US *on the
  weekend*; British *at school*, US *in school*; British *a vehicle on tow*, US *a vehicle in tow*.
- Definite-article usage: British *in/to hospital* vs. US *in/to the hospital*; British *at table* vs.
  US *at the table*.
- `towards`/`toward`: both occur in both dialects, but `toward` is a US preference and rare in
  British usage.

### Grammatical/syntactic variation (§21.6)

The source frames this as the subtlest category of all: text containing no distinctive spelling or
vocabulary that would be unfamiliar to a reader of the other dialect, but that still "doesn't quite
sound right" to a native ear of that dialect — correctable, per the source, mainly by editorial
instinct rather than a lookup rule. Named sub-patterns:
- **Noun number/countability**: British `accommodation` (uncountable) vs. US `accommodations`
  (countable plural). Other classes showing this kind of variability: certain common ailments (US
  uses an article, *a toothache*/*a cramp*), certain foods when describing the cooked state (British
  *scrambled egg*, *mashed potato* — singular; US uses the plural forms *scrambled eggs*, *mashed
  potatoes*), and a scattered set of other words that don't belong to any single class: `main`/`mains`
  (a utility conduit), `overhead`/`overheads` (business costs), `scale`/`scales` (the weighing
  device), `math`/`maths` (the subject).
- **Group-noun predication**: British English more readily uses a plural verb with a singular
  collective/company noun when emphasizing the individuality or corporate-body nature of its members
  (*Barclays are still recruiting in Poole*); US English treats the same noun as grammatically
  singular (*Barclays is...*).
- **Modal-verb preference**: the two dialects differ in how readily they reach for `must`, `would`,
  and `should` in parallel contexts. The modal contractions `daren't` and `shan't` are uncommon in US
  English and are commonly perceived there as markers of British usage ("Briticisms") specifically.
- **Verb transitivity**: a set of verbs differs in whether/how they take a direct object across
  dialects — the source names `agree`, `appeal`, `approximate`, `bath`/`bathe`, `catchup`, `give`,
  `impact`, `loan`/`lend`, `notify`, `protest`, `provide` as the verbs most frequently requiring an
  editor's attention on this point (no individual example sentences are given in this chapter).
- **Submodifier usage** (especially `rather` and `quite`): many British uses of `quite` as a
  submodifier are considered superfluous and simply deletable in US English. British English also
  shows a higher frequency of `very` before adjectives in contexts where US ears perceive no clear
  need for it (also often simply deletable). Where British writers reach for `rather`, American
  writers more often use `fairly` or `somewhat` for the same effect.
- **Idiom wording**: minor lexical differences inside otherwise-shared idioms, e.g. British *home from
  home* vs. US *home away from home*; British *write to me* vs. US *write me* (zero preposition). The
  source notes there are "dozens" of these and their correction depends on editorial ear rather than a
  fixed rule-set.
- **Subjunctive mood**: US English more readily uses the subjunctive mood after nouns, verbs, and
  adjectives of requiring/demanding (*She insisted Jane sit here*). British English finds this
  grammatical but not always idiomatic, generally preferring a modal-verb or declarative paraphrase to
  express the same meaning (*She insisted Jane should sit here*).
- **Tense preference**: informal US English frequently uses the simple past with adverbs like
  `already`, `ever`, `just`, `yet` (*We already ate*; *She just got here*), where British English — and
  more formal US English — favors the present perfect for the same meaning (*I've already eaten*;
  *She has just got here*).
- **Subordinate-clause introduction**: the source flags variable preferences here too but defers the
  actual discussion to §4.3.1, outside this chapter's page range.

### Other varieties of English beyond US/British (§21.7)

The source closes by noting that, while nearly every other variety of English shows its own
considerable lexical variation, almost all of them still align closely with either British or US
English for orthography, grammar, and syntax as a whole. Former British colonies and Commonwealth
members tend to follow British English, as does English used within the European Union. Former US
colonies (the source names the Philippines specifically), Latin America, and other countries with
strong US trade/cultural ties tend to follow US English. Two varieties are named as genuinely mixed —
Canadian and Australian English — each following British English in some respects and US English in
others; the source recommends that any localization project targeting either dialect specifically
have its own dedicated dictionary and style guide, rather than assuming it can be derived by simple
extrapolation from the British/US contrast covered in this chapter.

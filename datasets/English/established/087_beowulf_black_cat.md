**Source:** *Beowulf*, Black Cat Publishing "Reading & Training" series, Step Four (B2.1),
© 2002 / new edition © 2007, Black Cat Publishing / Cideb Editrice (Genoa/Canterbury). Full 57-page

**What this source actually is.** This is **not** a grammar reference or a dictionary. It is a
graded-reader retelling of *Beowulf* in simplified Modern English, aimed at EFL learners (CEFR
B2.1), with footnoted glosses for words judged too advanced for that level, plus ESL comprehension/
grammar-drill activities and three short dossiers of literary/historical background. As flagged at
dispatch, this is a **low-priority, low-yield source** for this project's purposes: it contains
almost no genuine Old English linguistic material — nearly all footnoted vocabulary is ordinary-to-
moderately-elevated **Modern** English (the glosses exist only to help a language learner read a
20th/21st-century prose retelling), not attested period vocabulary, slang, or register-tagged
dialect data. The handful of genuinely Old-English-specific items (a handful of words, one line of
the original manuscript, two literary devices) are flagged distinctly below and are the only part of
this file with any real connection to the project's historical/comparative-linguistics interest.

**Coverage note.** Every footnoted vocabulary gloss across the ten narrative chapters and three
dossiers was captured, with one exception: the generic ESL grammar-instruction sidebars scattered
through the "Activities" sections (Present Perfect Simple/Continuous, reported speech, third
conditional, modal verbs, etc. — pp.21, 33, 47, 80-81, 100-101 and others) were **skipped as
out-of-scope** — they teach ordinary Modern English tense/clause mechanics to EFL learners, are
already covered far more systematically by this project's dedicated Modern English grammar

skipped: repeated comprehension-check/true-false/crossword-puzzle activities that only recombine

## Grammar points

### Kennings (compound-metaphor noun substitution)

A rhetorical/word-formation device the book identifies as characteristic of Old English and other
Germanic poetry: a noun is replaced by a compound or genitive noun-phrase that describes it
metaphorically rather than naming it directly. The one example the book states explicitly and
unambiguously (p.41, "Kings, rings and dragons" sidebar): **"breaker of the rings"** = a king, because
an Anglo-Saxon king would break off pieces of gold from the arm-rings he wore and give them to loyal
followers as reward. The book runs a fuller matching exercise pairing eight further kenning phrases
(e.g. involving the sea, a sword, a ship, a wife, God/the devil, and a second sun-kenning) against
their referent nouns on pp.30-31 — this exercise was viewed during extraction but its exact item
wording was not confidently re-transcribed, so it is flagged here rather than reproduced, to avoid
presenting a reconstruction as verified source text. This device is directly relevant to this
project's interest in compounding as a mechanism for generating figurative/slang vocabulary, even
though the book's own treatment of it is minimal (a half-page sidebar plus one classroom exercise).

### Alliteration (Old English poetic meter)

The Cultural Background section (p.10) states that *Beowulf*, "like nearly all Germanic and Old
English poetry," uses alliteration — repetition of consonant sounds, especially at the start of
words that are near each other — rather than rhyme. Its own illustrative example (a Modern English
sentence built to demonstrate the device, not an Old English quotation): *"They put his body on the
boat and then began to burn it"* (repeated /b/). The book further notes that English poetry did not
begin using rhyme (matching end-of-word sounds) until **after the 1066 Norman Conquest**, when French
poetic styles were introduced — i.e. alliterative verse is presented as the native pre-Conquest
English form, displaced by a borrowed French-derived convention.

### Scop and oral transmission

Before being committed to writing (manuscript dated to c.1000 CE, per the book), *Beowulf* is
described as having been composed by a single unknown poet between 700-750 CE and then transmitted
orally, "passed on from poet to poet," by scops (Old English court poets/bards, pronounced "shop")
who performed poems by singing them at court on special occasions (p.9). This oral-composition-then-
transcription history is given as the explanation for why the poem's authorship is unknown.

### Primary vs. secondary epic; the five elements of an "epic"

"What Makes an Epic" (dossier, pp.82-85) distinguishes **primary (folk) epics** from **secondary
(literary) epics** and lists five elements both share (p.84, paraphrased): (1) the hero — superhuman
or exceptional qualities, often with a nation's fate riding on him; (2) the setting — wide-ranging,
spanning many places and a long period; (3) the action — courageous/superhuman deeds in battle, long
dangerous journeys; (4) the supernatural — gods or supernatural beings take part or are invoked; (5)
the style — a distinct "poetic" register markedly different from everyday language, with repetition,
elaborate greetings, and long speeches. The dossier's accompanying table lists seven comparator world
epics (*La Chanson de Roland*, *Cantar de mío Cid*, *Nibelungenlied*, *Dede Korkut*, *Os Lusíadas*,
*Gerusalemme Liberata*, *Paradise Lost*) with author and approximate date, as a cross-tradition
reference point for the genre, but these are not *English*-language items and are noted here only
for context, not extracted as vocabulary.

### The Old English manuscript opening line

The Cultural Background section reproduces a photograph of the first page of the *Beowulf*
manuscript (p.9), captioned as being written in Old English and dated to approximately 1000 CE
(composition dated separately to 700-750 CE). The book states explicitly, in Modern English prose,
that the manuscript's first word (**Hwæt**) means "Listen!" — this is the only piece of literal
attested Old English text identified anywhere in this source. The manuscript image itself was not
independently transcribed character-by-character in this extraction (insular minuscule script in a
small embedded photograph; see Vision Reading Confidence note on the "Hwæt" vocabulary row above).

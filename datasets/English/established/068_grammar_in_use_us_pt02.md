## Grammar points

### Unit 25 — Can, could, and be able to

"Can" marks present possibility/ability ("Can you speak any foreign languages?"); "be able to" is a
near-synonym, more necessary in forms "can" lacks (no infinitive, no present perfect: "I haven't been
able to sleep," not "haven't could"). "Could" especially pairs with perception/cognition verbs (see,
hear, smell, taste, feel, remember, understand) and marks general past ability (grandfather could
speak five languages). For a single specific occasion's achieved action, "was/were able to" (= managed
to) is required instead of "could" — "everyone was able to escape," not "could escape." The negative
"couldn't" works in all these situations without the managed/general distinction. pp. 50.

### Unit 26 — Could (do) and could have (done)

"Could" sometimes carries present/future meaning rather than strict past-of-can: suggesting a possible
future action ("You could stay with Linda"), stating a future possibility ("There could be another
rise in gas prices"), or meaning "would be able to." "Could have (done)" is its past-oriented
counterpart, marking an opportunity that existed but was not taken ("We could have gone to the movies,
but we decided to stay home") or an unrealized possibility ("He could have hurt himself"). "Couldn't
have (done)" means one would not have succeeded even if one had tried. p. 52.

### Unit 27 — Must (have) and can't (have)

Epistemic (certainty-judgment) use, distinct from the obligation sense covered in Unit 31. "Must" =
"I am sure this is true" (present); "can't" = "I am sure this is impossible" (present). For the past,
"must have (done)" and "can't have (done)" perform the same two judgments retrospectively
("couldn't have (done)" is an available alternative to "can't have (done)"). p. 54.

### Unit 28 — May (have) and might (have)

"May"/"might" mark present possibility, weaker/more tentative than "must" ("He may be in his office" =
perhaps). Negative forms are "may not"/"might not." Past-possibility versions are "may have
(done)"/"might have (done)" ("She may have been asleep" = perhaps she was asleep). "Could" can
substitute for may/might but signals an even smaller degree of possibility. p. 56.

### Unit 29 — May and might (future)

Future-possibility use, parallel to the present-possibility use in Unit 28: "I may go to Puerto Rico"
= perhaps I will go. A continuous form (may/might be doing) exists and is compared directly against
the more certain "will be doing" (Unit 10). The fixed idiom "may/might as well (do)" marks doing
something not because it's clearly the best option but because there's no good reason not to and
nothing better on offer. p. 58.

### Unit 30 — Can, could, may, and would: requests, permission, offers, and invitations

A pragmatic-function unit rather than a new grammatical form: "can"/"could" for requests (could is
politer/more tentative); "can"/"could"/"may" for asking permission, but only "can"/"may" (not "could")
for granting it; "can"/"may" for offers (may more formal); "Would you like...?" (never "do you like")
for offers and invitations, with "I'd like" as the polite way to state a want. p. 60.

### Unit 31 — Have to and must

Both express necessity, but with a felt-source distinction: "must" marks the speaker's own personal
judgment that something is necessary ("I must write to Ann" — the speaker feels it); "have to" states
an external/factual necessity the speaker is simply reporting, not personally asserting ("She has to
wear glasses"). Only "have to" inflects through all tenses/forms with ordinary do-support
(did/does/doesn't); "must" is restricted to present/future reference. "Have got to" is offered as an
informal variant of "have to." The unit explicitly contrasts "mustn't" (prohibition — necessary NOT
to) against "don't have to" (no obligation either way) as commonly confused opposites. pp. 62.

### Unit 32 — Should

Marks advisability — a good/right thing to do — explicitly weaker than "must" (compare "You should
stop smoking" [advice] vs. "You must stop smoking" [necessity]). Used to solicit/give opinions, often
with "I think"; marks deviation from expectation ("It should be 50¢" when the price shows 65¢); and,
in "should have (done)," marks a past action that should have but didn't happen (regret/criticism).
Also used to express likely future outcome ("I should be home at the usual time" ≈ will probably be).
"Ought to" is offered as an alternative to "should" throughout. pp. 64.

### Unit 33 — Subjunctive (I suggest you do)

After suggest, propose, recommend, insist, and demand (+ optional "that"), the embedded clause takes
the bare base form regardless of subject/tense context ("I suggest (that) you take a vacation"; "He
demanded (that) she apologize"), including a passive subjunctive "be" ("I insisted that something be
done"). "Should" is offered as an alternative to the bare subjunctive. The infinitive is explicitly
disallowed after "suggest" ("suggested him to buy" is marked wrong); "insist on" and "suggest" also
separately allow a gerund structure (cross-referenced to Units 51 and 57a). p. 66.

### Unit 34 — If sentences (present/future)

Draws the core type-1/type-2 conditional contrast: a real future possibility uses if + present tense
with will/can/may in the main clause ("If I find it, I'll give it to you"); an imagined/hypothetical
situation uses if + simple past with would/could/might in the main clause ("If I found a $100 bill, I
would keep it") — the past-tense form here carries no past time meaning. "Would" is disallowed inside
the if-clause itself in ordinary use, with a formal-register exception for polite requests ("if you
would send me your brochure"). "When" cannot substitute for "if" in these hypothetical sentences.
p. 68.

### Unit 35 — If and wish sentences (present)

Hypothetical-present structure: if/wish + simple past describes an unreal present state ("If I knew
her number, I would call her" — he doesn't know it; "I wish I knew..." = regret about not knowing
now). "Were" replaces "was" for all persons in both constructions ("If I were you," "I wish I were
taller") — explicitly flagged as a fixed exception to normal past-tense agreement. "I wish...would..."
is reserved specifically for wanting something/someone else to change (see Unit 37); it cannot be used
reflexively ("I wish I would" is disallowed). p. 70.

### Unit 36 — If and wish sentences (past)

Hypothetical-past structure: if/wish + past perfect for an unreal past situation ("If I had known that
you were sick, I would have gone to see you" — he didn't know); main clause takes "would have (done)"
(with might have/could have as weaker alternatives). "Wish" + past perfect similarly expresses regret
about something that did/didn't happen ("I wish I hadn't eaten so much"). "Would have" is explicitly
disallowed directly after "wish." Both "would" and "had" can contract to 'd, which is a genuine
source of surface ambiguity the book flags directly (if I'd seen = if I had seen). p. 72.

### Unit 37 — Would

Three uses beyond the conditional (covered in Units 34–36) and the offer/invitation use (Unit 30): (a)
"I wish...would..." to complain about a present situation and want someone/something to change
("I wish it would stop raining"; "I wish you wouldn't drive so fast"); (b) as the reported-speech past
form of will/won't ("I'll lend you money" → "he said he would lend... money"); (c) for habitual past
recollection, interchangeable with "used to" for repeated past actions ("Whenever Linda was angry, she
would just walk out of the room"). p. 74.

### Unit 38 — In case

Marks a precaution taken because of a possibility, regardless of whether that possibility is realized
("He wears two watches in case one of them stops"). Present tense (not "will") is used for future
reference. Explicitly distinguished from "if": "We'll buy food if Tom comes" (conditional — buying
depends on his coming) vs. "...in case Tom comes" (precautionary — buying happens now regardless, to
be ready either way). Also used retrospectively to explain a past precaution ("I drew a map in case
she couldn't find our house"). The formal phrase "in case of + noun" ("in case of fire") is flagged as
a distinct, more formal structure meaning simply "if there is/are." p. 76.

### Unit 39 — Unless, as long as, and provided/providing (that)

"Unless" = "except if," introducing an exception to the main statement ("Joe can't hear unless you
shout"); interchangeable with "if...not" in most cases. "As long as"/"so long as"/"provided
(that)"/"providing (that)" all mean "but only if," stating a necessary condition ("You can use my car
as long as you drive carefully"). All four expressions take present tense for future reference, never
"will," per the same rule given in Unit 9. p. 78.

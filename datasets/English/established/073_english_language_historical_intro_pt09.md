## Grammar points

### Centrifugal and centripetal tendencies: the chapter's organizing framework

The book opens by naming two **simultaneous, opposed** forces shaping present-day English, directly
following on from `pt08`'s world-Englishes material: a **centrifugal** tendency (the continuing
emergence and divergence of distinct "national" varieties of English, as a direct continuation of
the whole of `pt08`) and a **centripetal** tendency (processes of **levelling** and **diffusion**
that instead reduce local diversity and spread shared linguistic features over wide areas — in some
cases across just the UK, in others across the whole inner circle) — explicitly crediting modern
mass communications infrastructure (aircraft, telegraph, telephone, the internet) and modern mass
media (the popular press, cinema, radio, television) as the practical mechanisms enabling the
centripetal force specifically, since these technologies didn't exist (or existed in far weaker
form) during the earlier periods of divergence the rest of the book has traced.

### Cross-variety mutual lexical influence, with directional asymmetry toward American English

The book documents ongoing **mutual** influence between national varieties but is explicit that the
flow is **not symmetric**: American vocabulary is identified as the single largest and most actively
ongoing source of borrowing *into* British English at the time of writing, operating along a visible
continuum from fully-invisible, long-naturalized loans (*cockroach, loafer, stevedore, tornado* —
words most British speakers wouldn't even recognize as American in origin) through mid-transition
items still "rapidly becoming" naturalized as of 2009 (*blurb, cagey, gimmick, rugged*) to the
chapter's explicitly-named, currently-live category of **"American slang and colloquial" vocabulary**
flagged as "particularly appealing" (*hassle, heist, hype, scam, zap*) — this last category is the
chapter's most directly slang-synthesis-relevant single claim: the book is explicitly naming, in
real time, a *register* of vocabulary (American slang specifically, not American vocabulary
generally) as a currently-active source of borrowing into another English variety, precisely the
kind of register-targeted cross-variety borrowing mechanism this project's own synthesis work models.
Smaller but still-noted reverse and lateral flows: Australian loans into British English (*bush
telegraph, chunder, uni*) and a New Zealand loan (*zorbing*), explicitly framed by the book as the
less dominant, smaller-scale side of an otherwise heavily American-English-dominated present-day
cross-variety borrowing pattern — i.e. the mutual-influence claim is real but explicitly
asymmetric in scale, not evenly balanced among the inner-circle varieties.

### Linguistic globalization and youth-marked innovations, tracked across inner-circle varieties in real time

The book frames "globalization" itself as having produced a **specifically youth-associated** layer
of linguistic diffusion, distinguishable from the general lexical-borrowing pattern above because
these features spread as markers of a shared global youth culture/identity rather than through
ordinary trade or media-consumption borrowing alone, and are explicitly noted as "a source of
irritation to older, more conservative speakers" precisely because of this generational association.
Two worked, carefully regionally-and-chronologically-dated case studies: (1) **the "new quotatives"**
— the book contrasts the traditional *and I said...* with the somewhat-colloquial *and I went...*
and, as the chapter's central case, the newer *and I'm like...* / *and I'm...* / *and I'm all...*
family, first noticed in 1980s USA specifically as a marker of "Valley Girl" speech among affluent
young women in California's Silicon Valley, then tracked spreading through the 1990s to Canada, the
UK, Australia, and South Africa — with the book noting it tended to be **first adopted by young,
middle-class women specifically** in each new region before broader uptake, i.e. a documented,
socially-patterned diffusion pathway, not a simple instantaneous global spread. (2) **Uptalk/High
Rising Tone/Australian Question Intonation** (a terminology set the book explicitly presents as
competing labels for the same phenomenon, reflecting genuine disagreement among researchers/
commentators about its best framing) — first seriously studied in Australia and New Zealand, with
popular UK opinion attributing its British spread to the popularity of Australian soap operas (the
book names *Neighbours* specifically) — but the book is careful to flag this folk explanation as
likely **incomplete**, since the same rising-declarative pattern was independently also a documented
feature of 1980s US Pacific-coast "Valley Girl" speech, the very same source population already
credited above for the new-quotatives innovation — i.e. the book treats a single population/era
(1980s young Californian women) as a plausible common origin point for **two** separate linguistic
innovations that then diffused outward along overlapping but not identical international pathways,
and suggests both phenomena's ultimate present-day spread "may be due to a combination of factors
associated with global youth culture, including the media and electronic communication using social
network sites" — i.e. the book explicitly entertains social-media-driven diffusion as a live causal
hypothesis for ongoing linguistic change, a genuinely novel mechanism relative to every other change-
diffusion explanation offered earlier in the book (which rely on population contact, print, radio/TV,
or deliberate elocution-driven standardization instead).

### Dialect levelling inside Britain: mechanisms, and a direct counter-case against the levelling narrative

The book documents an ongoing internal-UK convergence process directly parallel to, but
mechanistically distinct from, the international youth-innovation diffusion above — crediting
**universal/compulsory education** (dating from the late 19th century) as having "worked against the
broader dialect elements" from early on, **increased population mobility** (the effective
disappearance of the small self-contained community, continuing migration to larger towns/cities,
and the large-scale wartime mixing of men from different regional backgrounds in the two World Wars'
conscript armies) as a second, independent driver, and **mass-media exposure** as a third. The book
cites a specific, concrete documentary response to this perceived loss: Harold Orton's 1948 *Survey
of English Dialects*, explicitly described as "a last-gasp attempt to record the 'genuine,
traditional' dialects of England" before they were further eroded — treated by the book as itself a
piece of historical evidence that contemporary observers already recognized dialect loss as
underway by the mid-20th century, not merely a recent perception. The specific present-day
**mechanism** of levelling is named: planned "new towns" (the book's example is Milton Keynes) and
routine long-distance commuting bring speakers of different regional dialects into sustained contact,
producing **mutual accommodation** in which features specific to one location, or used only by a
minority within the new mixed community, are abandoned in favor of more widely-shared alternatives —
i.e. levelling is explicitly modeled as an emergent, bottom-up social-accommodation process, not a
top-down standardization-authority effect. **Diffusion**, by contrast, is explicitly distinguished
by the book as the *separate* process whereby a feature **spreads outward from one specific point of
origin** (named as "mainly... the London area" for current UK diffusion) across a wide area, rather
than levelling's mutual-convergence-of-many-dialects-toward-each-other dynamic; the book's two
concrete diffusing-feature examples are **glottalization** (of medial and final /t/) and **th-
fronting** (/θ/→[f], /ð/→[v], illustrated via the eye-dialect spellings *yoof* for "youth" and
*bovvered* for "bothered") — both especially associated with younger speakers, both the target of
considerable older-speaker negative comment. The chapter's explicit **counter-case** against reading
all this as homogenization: "this does not mean... that regional and social differences have
disappeared" — Manchester speech remains clearly distinct from London speech "even if younger people
in both places... say *bovvered*," northern-English speakers remain generally distinguishable from
southern/Midlands speakers, and Scottish/Welsh/Irish educated speakers remain generally
distinguishable from English speakers — i.e. diffusion of *specific individual features* and the
*persistence of broader systemic regional distinctiveness* are explicitly treated as fully
compatible, simultaneous facts, not a contradiction needing resolution.

### A speech-hierarchy correlating with socio-economic class, and its sociolinguistic texture

Within each UK region, the book describes a continuous, class-correlated speech-hierarchy, not a
simple standard/non-standard binary: speech nearer the "top" of this hierarchy is closer to Standard
English in grammar/vocabulary and to RP in pronunciation, with differences from standard usage
increasing "as you go down the hierarchy." Three further, independently-operating conditioning
factors are named explicitly: **sex** (women diverge less from the standard than men of the same
socio-economic group — stated as a general finding, without further elaboration or a specific
citation in this passage); **age**; and **style** (the difference from the standard is smallest in
formal situations and greatest in colloquial/informal style, for all groups) — i.e. the book models
"how non-standard a given utterance sounds" as the joint product of **four** independent
dimensions (socio-economic position, sex, age, and register/formality), not reducible to any one of
them alone.

### Two specific non-standard grammatical features, with an explicit anti-"ungrammatical" argument

The book names two "very common" non-standard British grammatical features and uses both to make a
pointed methodological argument against the popular term "ungrammatical": (1) use of the past
**participle** form as the simple past-tense form (*I seen him*, *they never done it* "they didn't
do it"); (2) use of one shared form for both the adjective and the corresponding adverb (*the lads
played real good* "the boys played really well"). The book's explicit counter-argument: "non-standard
speakers have as strict and complete a grammar as standard speakers, but it just happens to be a
different one: in their grammar, for example, *seen* and *done* may be the regular past-tense forms
of *see* and *do*" — i.e. these are not failures to apply grammar but instances of a **different,
internally-consistent** grammatical system, and moreover "very much what could be expected from the
general development of the language," directly tying this specific present-day non-standard pattern
back to the book's own recurring historical theme (traced repeatedly across `pt03`–`pt07`) of
irregular/distinct forms progressively levelling toward a single regularized pattern over time — the
book is explicitly framing today's stigmatized *seen*/*done*-as-past-tense as the **same kind of
regularizing analogical process** that has already, historically, produced large parts of today's
"standard" grammar, not a fundamentally different or lesser kind of linguistic behavior. The book
goes further, floating a specific, explicitly **speculative** prediction: such non-standard usages
"could become standard," since young people's own social role-models (pop stars, professional
footballers) increasingly come from lower socio-economic speech backgrounds — "the stigmatized usage
of today may become the accepted usage of tomorrow" — while immediately qualifying this as genuinely
uncertain, since it "depend[s] on general social history, which we can't predict," i.e. the book
treats this not as a confident forecast but as one concretely plausible pathway among others.

### The decline of RP's monopoly, and the rise of regional/"Estuary" prestige

Building directly on `pt07`'s account of RP's institutional rise via the public-school system, the
book traces RP's **loss of exclusive prestige** over roughly the past half-century to a specific,
named institutional cause: the post-war expansion of higher education has meant most university
students (and, consequently, most entrants to the English professional classes) are **not** RP
speakers, and most schoolteachers themselves use "an educated regional accent" rather than RP — so
the practical linguistic influence of the school system, previously a major RP-reinforcing engine
(per `pt07`), now instead tends to reinforce educated-regional norms over RP specifically. The book
is careful to qualify this as decline in **relative institutional dominance**, not disappearance: RP
remains the accent most fully described and most commonly taught to foreign learners of British
English, "has not lost all its magic." Concurrently, the prestige of educated regional accents
generally "has risen enormously" — the book names **"Estuary English"** (a variety used by educated
speakers in south-east England incorporating several of the diffusing "youth" features already
covered above, prominent in radio/TV announcing) as a specific, much-discussed example, and separately
notes a broader rise in prestige for *all* national/regional accents across Britain, citing the BBC's
own documented shift (educated Welsh and Scottish accents now used by some of its most respected
broadcasters, where it was "formerly a bastion of RP"). The book distinguishes a lingering prejudice
specifically against "broad" regional accents from a newly-risen acceptability for "soft" regional
accents, which "are now in many contexts more acceptable than RP" — i.e. not a uniform
regional-accent rehabilitation, but a specific, graded distinction between strongly-marked and
mildly-marked regional speech. The book's own closing methodological caution on this whole topic:
some of these apparent changes "are therefore more apparent than real: they may be changes in
*acceptance*, rather than actual substantive changes" — crediting linguist Christian Mair's term
**"colloquialization"** for this general phenomenon, whereby features long associated with informal/
spoken register (contractions like *I'll*, *he'd*, *didn't*) are becoming acceptable in more formal
written registers, while conversely some "formal" structures (the passive) are being disfavoured —
with the book affirming, however, that genuine substantive change (not just register-acceptance
shift) is also demonstrably occurring in pronunciation, grammar, and vocabulary, which the rest of
the chapter goes on to document concretely.

### Present-day vocabulary expansion: computing as the chapter's dominant case study, and a reversal of the 18th/19th-century coinage pattern

The book treats computing/technology vocabulary as today's single richest site of observable,
real-time word-formation activity, and explicitly frames its dominant formation strategy as a
**reversal** of the pattern that dominated 18th/19th-century scientific vocabulary growth (`pt07`):
"Not all new scientific and technical words are coined from Latin and Greek elements: there is an
increasing tendency, especially in 'newer' technologies such as computing, to prefer words coined
from existing English elements, often in a playful way" — worked via **semantic extension of
existing words** (*mouse, spam, web, to surf* — none newly coined, all repurposed), **compounding**
(*chat-room, homepage, spell-check, weblog*), **blending/first-element compounding** (*telnet*,
*digicam*, combining the first elements of their source compounds — the book treats this as
structurally continuous with, but a more productive/systematic version of, the occasional blending
already noted as a late-19th/20th-century innovation in `pt07`), **acronymy** (*FAQ*, *SQL*, and the
chained *MP3*←MPEG, flagged by the book as a genuinely new and increasingly productive formation
category not given this kind of sustained attention in earlier chapters), and **arbitrary/punning
coinage** (*phish*, a punning respelling of *fish*; *cookie*, a playful reuse via folk etymology;
*Trojan*, a classical-allusion reuse) — the book's explicit framing is that these arbitrary/punning
formations are a genuinely distinct category from both the systematic mechanisms covered everywhere
else in the book and from ordinary semantic extension, since their motivating logic is wordplay
itself rather than any systematic derivational or compositional principle. The book's capstone
observation on **conversion's** continued productivity, directly tying back to the structural
explanation already given in `pt07`: "once a new word is coined, it can quickly be transferred from
one word class to another: modern English is particularly conducive to this process because there
are very few inflections that identify a word as a particular part of speech" — worked via *blog*
(noun and verb both first attested, per the OED Online as cited by the book, in 1999, with the
agentive noun *blogger* and verbal noun *blogging* following immediately) and *to google* (the
proprietary name *Google* converting directly to a transitive verb once the search engine became
popular enough). **Affixation remains fully productive too**, with the book distinguishing newly
"fashionable" prefixes specific to the present period (*cyber-*, *mini-*, *nano-*) from older,
still-productive prefixes already covered in `pt07` (*anti-*, *non-*, *un-*, *de-*) continuing
alongside them, plus unremarked-but-illustrated ongoing suffix productivity (*ageism, brinkmanship,
techie, circuitry, privatize, sexist, skateboarder*). **Loanwords, by contrast, are explicitly
demoted to a minor present-day mechanism**: "Loans are not a major source of new words, but a few
continue to drift in" — the book's own OED-sourced list of the final decade of the 20th century's
borrowings (*pressé* from French, *limoncello* from Italian, *jilbab* from Persian, *sudoku* from
Japanese, *wiki* from Hawaiian) is explicitly offered as evidence of genuine continued but
**reduced-relative-importance** borrowing, a real shift in the balance of vocabulary-growth
mechanisms relative to several earlier periods covered in the book (notably the Early Modern Latin
flood of `pt06` and the Late Modern period's much wider-sourced borrowing wave covered in `pt07`).

### Semantic change today: formal confusion between unrelated words as a currently-active, extensively catalogued mechanism

The book's single most extensively worked contemporary semantic-change case is **formal confusion**
— one word's meaning shifting specifically because its *form* resembles a different, historically
unrelated existing word, with speakers blending the two words' senses as a result — distinguished
explicitly from ordinary metaphorical/metonymic semantic drift (the dominant explanatory mode used
for semantic change everywhere earlier in the book). The worked case study is *format*: originally a
narrow technical bibliographic term (how a book's sheets are folded — quarto format, folio format —
"the only meaning recorded in the original edition of the OED"), which has broadened first to
"layout, design" and then to a very general "arrangement, mode of procedure, form" (the format of a
lecture-course, a meeting, a symphony); the book attributes this broadening specifically to influence
from the unrelated existing word *form*, of which *format* "is now merely a variant" for many
speakers — explicitly distinguishing this from the ordinary, independently-already-covered pattern
of "a word mov[ing] out of a specialized field of discourse into the general vocabulary" (itself
common and unremarkable, per the book) by isolating the *additional*, specifically formal-confusion-
driven component of the broadening. The book then supplies an extensive supplementary list of
further pairs currently undergoing the same kind of formal-confusion-driven semantic shift
(*adopt*/*adapt*, *amoral*/*immoral*, *cautionary*/*cautious*, *differential*/*difference*,
*diffuse*/*defuse*, *fallacious*/*false*, *humanitarian*/*humane*, *incredulous*/*incredible*,
*legalistic*/*legal*, *masterful*/*masterly*, *mitigate*/*militate*, *nationalistic*/*national*,
*perpetrate*/*perpetuate*, *prescribe*/*proscribe*, *psychiatric*/*psychological*, *realistic*/
*real*, *secretive*/*secret*, *sociological*/*social*), noting "usually it is the first word of each
pair which takes on the meaning of the second" (worked examples: *a masterful sculptor* taking on
the sense "masterly"; *realistic progress* taking on the sense "real"; *psychiatric illness* taking
on the sense "mental"; *sociological problems* taking on the sense "social") — and explicitly flags
that this whole list sits on a **live spectrum of social acceptance**, not a uniform category: "some
of these changes are still seen as malapropisms, but others are now commonly accepted," i.e. the
book is documenting an entire currently-in-progress semantic-change family at various individual
stages of completion/acceptance simultaneously, a genuinely rare degree of real-time granularity
relative to every historically-reconstructed semantic change covered earlier in the book.

### Other currently-active semantic-change mechanisms: narrowing/generalization, pejoration, amelioration, euphemism

Beyond formal confusion, the book catalogs several further live mechanisms with individually worked
examples, continuing the book's established (from `pt04` onward) narrowing/broadening vocabulary:
**narrowing specifically toward a marked/minority sub-sense** (*ethnic*, increasingly implying
"having to do with an ethnic minority" specifically rather than ethnicity in general); **broadening
from a specialized original sense** (*exercise*, generalized from "practice-operation, training task"
— plausibly originating in specifically military usage — to the very general "the aim/purpose of the
exercise"; *forensic*, generalized/narrowed from strictly "judicial, having to do with the law
courts" to "scientific [in a crime-detection context]," such that *forensic tests* now specifically
means "scientific tests... to help solve a crime" and *a forensic expert* means "a scientist who
helps police investigations" rather than anything to do with courtroom procedure per se). **Pejoration
and amelioration** are introduced as a named, explicitly-paired pair of opposite-direction
connotation-shift mechanisms ("words also change their meaning through pejoration and amelioration as
their connotations become more negative or positive"): *obscene*, "formerly... a rather strong word,
meaning 'abominable, loathsome,'" now sometimes weakened to "a vague epithet of disapproval,
especially in political journalism" (*obscene* amounts of money) is the book's pejoration-adjacent
weakening example; *wicked*, used by young people "as a term of approval," is the book's directly-
named **amelioration** case — a word whose core traditional sense (moral evil) has been essentially
inverted in a specific register/generational usage to mean its near-opposite (strong approval), the
single most dramatic single-word semantic-reversal case given anywhere in the book. Finally,
**euphemism**: "one noticeable tendency is for words with more neutral or pleasant connotations to be
used as euphemisms, especially when referring to war or death" — worked via *ethnic cleansing* (for
systematic forced removal/extermination of an ethnic population) and *friendly fire* (for the
accidental killing of one's own combatant by misdirected fire), both offered as directly-named,
currently-live examples of this euphemistic tendency specifically in contemporary English's
war/death-related vocabulary, directly continuing (and bringing fully up to date) the
euphemism-chain mechanism already named as a cross-language slang-derivation type in this project's
own `00_Usage_Tier_Taxonomy.md`.

### Ongoing pronunciation change: a continuation of the Great Vowel Shift, plus several further live sound changes

The book presents present-day pronunciation change as **genuinely continuous with**, not separate
from, the historical sound-change processes traced across every earlier chapter — most strikingly
framing an ongoing diphthongization of RP's two high long vowels (/iː/ in *see*, /uː/ in *too*,
diphthongizing toward [ɪi]/[ʊu] in educated south-eastern speech, with non-standard speech showing an
even more open/central starting point, [əi]/[əu]) as **the latest stage of the very same Great Vowel
Shift** already traced in full in `pt06` — i.e. the book explicitly treats the GVS not as a bounded
historical event that concluded by 1700 (as `pt07` initially stated) but as a process whose
underlying mechanism (closest vowels diphthongizing, freeing space for others to raise into) is
**still actively recurring** at the system's current highest vowels, centuries after the "main" shift
is conventionally said to have finished — a genuinely significant methodological point: period
boundaries like "the Great Vowel Shift ended by 1700" describe where documentary evidence happens to
thin out or where a textbook chapter break falls, not necessarily where the underlying mechanical
process itself stopped operating. A parallel case: the /ɔː/ phoneme (*law*) "becoming closer in
quality," with some younger speakers giving it "a much closer realization, nearer to [oː]" (as in
French *chose*) than older speakers' slightly more open variant — again explicitly framed as a
continuation of the raising chain. Several further, independently-dated recent sound changes are
cataloged: **a vowel-acceptance flip exactly paralleling `pt07`'s already-documented BATH/CLOTH-type
status reversals** — *off, cloth, cross* were pronounced with a long /ɔː/ by RP speakers earlier in
the 20th century, but this is "now considered very old-fashioned," with most RP speakers today using
the short /ɒ/ instead (the book notes this specific reversal mirrors, and essentially completes, the
status-flip trajectory already tracked in `pt07`'s discussion of the exact same historical change);
**fronting** of /ʊ/ and /uː/; **lowering** of /e/ and /æ/ toward /ɛ/ and /a/; and **monophthongization
of the centring diphthongs** /ʊə/ and /ɔə/, producing new homophone pairs (*poor*, *pore*, and *paw*
all becoming homophones for speakers with this merger) — a direct, concrete present-day instance of
exactly the same centring-diphthong-formation-then-further-merger cycle already traced historically
in `pt07`'s /r/-loss material. **Two vocalic changes are explicitly identified by the book as changes
of *acceptance* rather than recent innovation** — the spread of /ə/ at the expense of /ɪ/ in
unstressed syllables (worked examples: *kitchen, remain, system, waitress, women*; the book notes
former UK Prime Minister Tony Blair's own pronunciation of every unstressed syllable of
*conservatives* with /ə/ as an illustrative real-world instance) and the use of word-final /-iː/
rather than the more conservative /-ɪ/ in words like *happy*, *city* (common in the south and
Midlands, and in further specific named areas — north-east England, Humberside, Merseyside) — the
book is explicit that these two variant pronunciations are "quite old ones" (also independently
attested in present-day Australian English, "presumably... taken there from southern England in the
eighteenth and nineteenth centuries," directly linking back to `pt08`'s colonial-settlement-period
material), so their rising *prestige*/standardness today represents a genuine sociolinguistic shift
in acceptance, not phonetic innovation per se — a useful explicit methodological contrast against the
genuinely novel sound changes (the diphthong-raising, the centring-diphthong mergers) catalogued
immediately above it. A further live change: the redistribution of /juː/ vs. /uː/ in words like
*new*, *use* (from ME *iu*) — generally [juː] after labials/velars (*nju:/nju:z*) but [uː] after
/tʃ/, /dʒ/, /r/, and a consonant+/l/ cluster (*chew, June, rude, blew*) since around 1600; but with
this split now itself **spreading further**, especially after /s/, /l/, /θ/, producing live doublets
(*suit, lute, enthusiasm* each now have both a [juː]- and a [uː]-form in circulation, with the
[juː]-forms "increasingly old-fashioned" and the [uː]-forms spreading at their expense — most
regional English speech and American English have already made this change more completely than RP,
with some of these forms even showing early signs of entering standard British pronunciation, the
book's example being *resume*, "occasionally heard as /rɪˈzuːm/"). **Two trends affecting /r/**:
intrusive /r/ (inserted by analogy with genuine linking-/r/ words like *father*, producing *the idea
of it*, *the law of the sea* with an inserted [r] before a following vowel — the book traces this
from an earlier, now-fully-accepted stage restricted to after schwa, where it remains fully
acceptable even in educated speech regardless of context, e.g. *Ghanar and India*, to a more recent,
not-yet-fully-accepted extension to **after any vowel** whatsoever, e.g. *the Shahr of Persia*, *the
lawr of the sea*, still stigmatized as "vulgar" until relatively recently but now described by the
book as "widespread in educated speech" — with intrusive /r/ occasionally fossilizing permanently
onto a word's own stem regardless of context, producing forms like *drawring* and *withdrawral*,
which the book notes "are quite common but probably not yet accepted as standard"); and loss of /r/
in unstressed syllables where another /r/ already occurs elsewhere in the same word (*February*,
*library*, *temporary*, *secretary* — a pattern the book notes has "long been the case" where
**two** successive /r/s occur, but is "now" additionally spreading in colloquial speech to cases with
only one other /r/ present and no immediate repetition — worked via a TV weather forecaster's cited
pronunciation /ˈnaɪt ˈtempəʔəz ˈdʒɛnɹəli/ "during the night, temperatures generally..." — explicitly
flagged by the book as "not universally accepted, but... perhaps... straws in the wind"). Finally,
two further **consonantal** changes credited specifically to "Estuary English" influence on RP:
word-final glottalization of /t/, "now common" even among younger members of the Royal Family (an
explicitly class-crossing example, given the Royal Family's otherwise extremely conservative
sociolinguistic position); and velarization of /l/ in words like *cool, hill, Paul*, said to be
"spreading from below" — i.e. originating in a lower-prestige variety and diffusing upward through
the social hierarchy, the reverse direction from most of the book's earlier-documented sound-change
diffusion pathways, which generally spread from an already-prestigious source downward or outward.

### Ongoing stress-pattern changes, and "continental"/"new Latin" pronunciation influence

The book documents two further, independently-operating present-day pronunciation trends concerning
word stress. First, a **long-term shift of two-syllable-word stress from the second syllable to the
first**, already completed within living memory for *adult, alloy, ally, garage*, and actively
**ongoing** specifically in the large class of noun/verb pairs sharing a root but traditionally
differing only in stress placement (noun first-syllable, verb second-syllable: *annex, contest,
contract, escort, export, import, increase, progress, protest, transfer*) — the book notes "many
speakers now stress the verb also on the first syllable" in such pairs, eroding the
noun/verb-distinguishing stress contrast itself; conversely, for a second set of pairs where **both**
noun and verb traditionally carried second-syllable stress, there is instead "a tendency for the
noun to be given first-syllable stress" while the verb's stress stays put (*discharge, dispute,
redress, research* — occasionally the verb follows suit too), with derived forms (*protester*, from
*protest*) typically inheriting the newly-shifted first-syllable-stress pattern. A **separate**
trend affects words of more than two syllables, shifting stress from the first syllable to the
second (*aristocrat, communal, controversy, doctrinal, formidable, hospitable, pejorative*) — the
book is explicit the traditional first-syllable-stress forms remain "standard" while the newer
second-syllable forms are "permeating from below," i.e. this is framed as a genuine, not-yet-settled
class-correlated competition between two forms, directly continuing the book's established
methodology (already used extensively in `pt06`/`pt07`) of tracking a sound-change's social-
acceptance trajectory as a distinct question from its mere existence. *Cigarette*, *ice-cream*, and
*magazine* get a further, independent stress-shift note: traditionally final-syllable-stressed in
British English, "nowadays some speakers... put it on the first [syllable], probably as a result of
American influence" — directly tying a present-day British stress change back to the chapter's
opening American-lexical-influence theme. Separately, the book documents a rising preference for
**"continental"/"new Latin" style pronunciations** of loanwords and Latin-derived forms — words
borrowed from other languages, once fully assimilated to ordinary English sound-change patterns, are
"nowadays... often given a 'foreign' kind of pronunciation again" (worked via *armada, Copenhagen,
gala, Gaza*, whose traditionally-anglicized stressed /eɪ/ is increasingly replaced by /ɑː/, now
"universal" specifically in *armada*; and *beret, richochet, valet*, now commonly pronounced without
their traditionally-silent final /t/) — explicitly crediting "the spread of higher education and of
foreign travel" as the driving cause, while cautioning the "new" pronunciations are "not always based
on a genuine knowledge of the other language" (the book's pointed worked counter-examples: *Raj*,
traditionally /rɑːdʒ/, now often /rɑːʒ/, "a pronunciation which probably owes more to a knowledge of
French than... of Hindi"; the "new" pronunciation of *Copenhagen* "bears very little resemblance to
the Danish" either) — i.e. the trend is real and socially driven by genuine increased cosmopolitan
exposure, but its specific phonetic outcomes are shaped by **prestige-association with French/
continental pronunciation generally**, not by accurate knowledge of each individual loanword's actual
source language. A closely related final case: the "new," continental-vowel pronunciation of
Classical Latin (as opposed to the older, anglicized-vowel pronunciation tradition) has become the
dominant teaching style, so that Latin-tag phrases commonly used in English (*a priori, quasi, sine
die*) are increasingly given continental-style vowels by English speakers who learned "new" Latin at
school — extending even to proper names in works of English literature (Shakespeare's *Coriolanus* is
"now often pronounced with /ɑː/ instead of /eɪ/") and occasionally to ordinary English words more
remotely derived from Latin (*deity, spontaneity, vehicle*, traditionally pronounced with /iː/ for
their *e*, "but nowadays it is often pronounced /eɪ/") — the book's explicit closing observation here
being that "the 'new Latin' and 'continental' tendencies obviously reinforce one another," i.e. two
independently-motivated prestige trends converging on the same practical phonetic outcome for a
specific, overlapping class of words.

### Ongoing grammatical change: learned-plural regularization, modal-auxiliary shifts, and colloquialization

The book opens this final substantive section by noting noun/verb inflectional morphology has stayed
comparatively **stable** throughout the whole Late Modern period (directly reaffirming `pt07`'s
closing observation), with the clearest current exception being **learned Greek/Latin-plural nouns**
(*dogma/dogmata, formula/formulae, genus/genera, syllabus/syllabi*, etc.) increasingly receiving
analogical native English *-(e)s* plurals instead (*formulas, genuses*), sometimes with a genuine
**register split** preserved between the two forms (technical *formulae* vs. popular *formulas*) —
the book frames this explicitly as "very much in line with the general development of the language,"
directly continuing the millennium-long *-(e)s*-generalization trend traced across `pt03`–`pt07`. A
distinct but related case: learned Latin plurals in *-a* (*bacterium/bacteria, criterion/criteria
[Greek, not Latin, but grouped with this set by the book], datum/data, medium/media, phenomenon/
phenomena*) are frequently used in their own plural **context** so much more often than their
corresponding singular that ordinary speakers reanalyze the plural form itself as a singular — the
book's primary worked example, *data*, has been treated as singular by scientists "for years" and
this reanalysis "is now happening to other similar words as well" (citing contemporary attestations
of "the mass media is," "this criteria," and even "a bacteria" as a genuinely new singular noun) —
with the book explicitly crediting "the decline of the classics in English education" as a
contributing social cause, while simultaneously insisting the change itself "is absolutely in line
with the general development of the language," since standardizing toward the productive native
*-(e)s* plural pattern is an "obviously economical development" the language has been pursuing for
nearly a thousand years. The book draws an explicit **parallel historical case**: the Italian
loanword *graffiti* (technically itself already a plural, of singular *graffito*) is now "frequently"
used as a singular in English ("the graffiti **is**..."), generating a genuinely **new** plural form
*graffitis* under this reanalysis — and notes a further, even more recent parallel: *panini*
(properly the Italian plural of *panino* "a bread roll") is used in English to refer to a single
sandwich, with a correspondingly new English plural *paninis* formed from it — i.e. the book explicitly
identifies this reanalysis-then-new-plural-formation pattern as a **recurring type**, not a one-off
curiosity confined to *data*.

**Modal-auxiliary change** receives sustained, carefully worked attention: a rise in the use of
auxiliary **may** where traditional usage required **might**, illustrated via the book's own cited,
genuinely ambiguous 1968 newspaper sentence ("Leeds United **may have** scored a goal") — the book
walks through why this specific sentence is "baffling" under the traditional reading (where *may have
scored* should mean "perhaps scored," i.e. genuine uncertainty about a real past event, which doesn't
fit the actual reported context) versus its intended, newer reading (*might have scored but failed
to*, i.e. a counterfactual/unrealized-possibility sense that traditional usage would require *might*,
not *may*, to express) — noting this specific *may*-for-*might* substitution occurs "especially
(though not exclusively) when the auxiliary is followed by *have* plus past participle," and that
"for many of the younger generation... auxiliary *might* now hardly exists, either *may* or *could*
being used instead" — a genuinely striking modal-system simplification the book presents as actively
in progress, not completed. **Shall** and **must** are reported as "likewise increasingly rare":
*shall* tending to be replaced by *will*, *'ll*, or *be going to*; *must* "giving ground to *need to*",
with the book offering a specific, hedged **pragmatic** explanation for the latter shift specifically
— "possibly for pragmatic reasons such as the wish to sound more polite and less dictatorial" — i.e.
floating a politeness/face-management motivation for this particular modal substitution, distinct
from the more purely structural/analogical explanations the book offers for most of its other
grammatical-change cases. A further, structurally distinct modal-system change: **dare** and **need**
are reported as "ceasing to be auxiliaries, and are coming more and more to be used as ordinary
lexical verbs" — illustrated via the now-standard "Do you need to go?" and "I don't dare (to) go"
displacing the older true-auxiliary constructions "Need you go?" and "I dare not go" (which lack
dummy-*do* support and take a bare infinitive without *to*, the hallmark syntactic signature of a true
modal auxiliary per the dummy-*do* material already covered in `pt06`) — i.e. *dare*/*need* are
documented migrating **out of** the closed modal-auxiliary class and **into** the open, ordinary-verb
class, a category change in the opposite direction from the already-completed *do*-auxiliary
grammaticalization traced in `pt06`. The book notes this exact same auxiliary-to-ordinary-verb shift
has **already fully completed**, in non-standard speech, for *ought to* and *used to* ("She didn't
ought to," "He didn't used to"), i.e. presenting non-standard speech as running **ahead of** the
standard language on precisely this particular change, a genuinely notable methodological point given
how often the book's earlier material instead treats non-standard variants as conservative survivals
of an older system rather than as leading indicators of where the standard language is itself headed.

**The progressive's continuing expansion**, directly picking up `pt07`'s closing observation that the
progressive became "increasingly frequent" and ultimately combinable with the passive over the Late
Modern period: the book reports this expansion has **continued into the present**, with the
progressive "no longer restricted to verbs which can refer to continuous action" and now even used
with inherently-stative **verbs of emotion** — a domain traditionally considered fully incompatible
with progressive marking in Standard English — citing the directly-attested "I'm **loving** this
music" as the worked contemporary example, a genuinely further, still-ongoing extension of the exact
same grammaticalization trajectory the book has tracked continuously from its OE/ME origins (`pt05`)
through its Early Modern consolidation (`pt06`) and Late Modern combinatorial completion (`pt07`) all
the way to this final chapter — one single multi-century grammatical development, still visibly in
motion at the moment of the book's own writing. **Colloquialization**, Christian Mair's term already
introduced above, is given a concrete grammatical worked case here: contractions (*I'll, he'd,
didn't*) "were once proscribed in formal written English but are now becoming more common" there,
while conversely "formal structures such as the passive" are being disfavoured in writing, with the
book specifically naming online grammar-checking style-guides as an active institutional force
discouraging passive use in written genres today — and documents the passive's own competing
replacement constructions directly: the formal passive "the manager **was fired**" giving ground
either to a *get*-passive ("the manager **got fired**") or to full avoidance via active voice
restructuring ("the board **fired** the manager").

**Singular *they***, the chapter's final grammatical case study, is presented as a genuinely
externally-motivated change — i.e. one whose cause the book locates explicitly in social/ideological
change rather than purely-internal linguistic drift (a notably different explanatory mode from most
of the book's other grammar-change cases): "until relatively recently, it was acceptable to use
masculine pronouns when the gender of the person being referred to was unknown," but "the rise of
feminism and equal opportunities legislation in the late twentieth century has led to the preference
for 'gender-neutral' language" — and, since the two most obvious formal alternatives (*he or she*,
or written constructions like *s/he*) "seem awkward," the already-existing but traditionally-
proscribed use of **they** as a genuinely singular, gender-neutral pronoun ("Anyone who goes there
knows that they will have a good time") "has become acceptable" instead — the book's framing
explicitly credits a **pre-existing, already-available grammatical expedient** (singular *they* had
long existed as a stigmatized but genuinely attested colloquial usage) being promoted to acceptability
by an external social-ideological pressure (feminism/equal-opportunities norms) that happened to
create demand for exactly the grammatical function this existing-but-marginalized form could supply —
i.e. a case where the "slot" and the "filler" both already existed independently, and a social change
is what connected them, rather than either the grammatical form or the social need being newly
invented together.

### The book's closing methodological reflection: prescriptivism, scientific detachment, and linguistic change as inevitable

The book's final section ("English Tomorrow") is explicit that genuine long-range prediction is
impossible — "it's dangerous to extrapolate or to prophesy, and none of us can guess what the English
language will be like in a hundred years' time," with the caveat that the future "will depend on the
history of the [English-speaking] community itself," not on any purely linguistic-internal
trajectory. Within that acknowledged uncertainty, the book extrapolates several of its own
already-documented present-day trends forward as plausible (not certain) continuations: continued
rapid vocabulary expansion, "comparable to that of such earlier periods as 1300 to 1400 or 1550 to
1650" (i.e. explicitly measuring the present period's lexical-growth rate against two of its own
earlier chapters' documented peak-borrowing/peak-coinage eras, `pt04`'s and `pt06`'s respectively);
continued dialect-mixing/levelling, barring "some global disaster" disrupting world communications;
continued displacement of public-school RP by "educated south-eastern usage" and Estuary English
specifically, which the book notes "have for some time been achieving parity of esteem," alongside
continued strengthening of regional standards "well established" outside England specifically within
the British Isles; continued vowel-system change along the diphthongization trajectory already
documented (framed explicitly as "further changes in the vowel system" building on, not replacing,
the Great Vowel Shift's still-ongoing final stage per the pronunciation-change section above); and,
more speculatively, the possibility that currently-informal/non-standard usages (*I done*, conflated
adjective/adverb forms) could permeate into the standard language over time, extending the
"stigmatized usage of today may become the accepted usage of tomorrow" hypothesis already floated
earlier in the chapter — with the book additionally, and explicitly tentatively ("perhaps more
controversially"), raising the specific possibility that **electronic-communication-driven
abbreviations** (text-messaging/email conventions such as *l8* for "late" and *LOL* for "laugh out
loud") "might find their way into other kinds of written usage," offered as a genuinely open,
unresolved question rather than a confident prediction — directly extending this project's own
interest in register-specific abbreviation/clipping conventions as a potential pathway into broader
standard usage, the same kind of slang-to-standard-promotion trajectory already documented
historically (18th-century *banter, coax, flimsy*, etc.) in `pt07`.

The book's genuinely closing passage is explicitly reflective/methodological rather than
descriptive, and worth capturing for its own argumentative content: "What we can be sure of is that
the process of change, which we have traced from the early Indo-European records up to modern times,
is still going on, and will continue" — stated as the book's single overarching thesis, tying every
earlier chapter's historical material directly to the present moment as one unbroken, still-active
process rather than a closed, completed story. The book diagnoses **prescriptivist resistance to
ongoing change as a predictable, structurally-explicable bias, not a neutral observation about
language quality**: "It requires an effort of detachment to recognize current change for what it is.
We are so thoroughly trained in one form of the language that we are likely to dismiss innovations as
mistakes or vulgarisms" — while noting the exact same dismissive reaction, when directed at *our own*
usage by an older generation, is instead recognized by us as mere generational grumbling ("stuffy old
fuddy-duddies") — i.e. explicitly naming the asymmetry between how a generation judges change flowing
*into* its own usage versus change it has already normalized as evidence that the underlying
objection is sociolinguistic/generational-positional, not a genuine, generation-independent
linguistic-quality judgment. The book allows that "such conservatism is inevitable, and indeed
necessary for the stability of the language," but argues explicitly for **combining** that lived
conservatism with a **separate, scientific-detachment-based observer stance**: "we need to step
outside such attitudes and view the whole speech community with scientific detachment. We shall then
recognize that our behaviour is simply that of one group at one point in time, and that in the next
generation the innovations that we deplore may well have become completely respectable, and indeed
uniquely right for the users" — i.e. a dual-stance methodological position (personally conservative
in one's own usage is fine and natural; scientifically non-judgmental when *describing* the whole
speech community's actual behavior is the separate, additionally-required professional obligation),
rather than demanding either pure prescriptivism or pure anti-prescriptivist advocacy. The book's
very last paragraph closes on an explicitly **values-level, not just methodological**, note,
recommending active *enjoyment* of linguistic variety and change as a reasonable response to the
same facts that make some readers anxious: "why not *enjoy* the language that you speak? As speakers
of English, we're fortunate in being inside a language of enormous richness and variety: it can be
great fun simply to listen to English of many different kinds... and also to enjoy the ways in which
the language is changing," closing with an extended metaphor directly addressing the
desire-for-stability impulse the whole section has been diagnosing: "In a universe of change, it's
natural to long for stability, to want to pin things down and fix them. But it can't be done. The
whole of nature is in flux, and so is the whole of human life, and we might as well make the best of
the fact. It's not really much good clinging to the bank: we have to push out into the flux and
swim."

dialectal diversity as the practical problem the first printing press had to solve. The individual
letters/narratives are represented by diagnostic forms; Caxton's own argued commentary (his prefaces)
is captured close to fully since it is primary metalinguistic commentary, not narrative content.

## Grammar points

### Private letters and dictated narrative as a check on literary/chronicle evidential bias

The chapter's organizing principle is that personal, non-literary writing — a devotional memoir
dictated by an uneducated woman (Margery Kempe), three generations of a merchant family's private
letters (the Pastons), and a wool-merchant family's business/personal correspondence (the Celys) —
gives access to a register of everyday written English that literary and chronicle texts, by their
nature, cannot: ordinary vocabulary, syntax, and (via irregular, individual spelling) indirect
evidence of ordinary pronunciation, from writers who were not deliberately crafting a literary
register. The book is explicit about the limits of this evidence too: because none of these
collections uses a standardized spelling system, and because individual writers within the same
family/period show real inconsistency even in their own spelling, the evidence is valuable precisely
*because* it is unpolished, but it cannot be used to reconstruct a single, fully regular phonology —
only a range of plausible period pronunciations. (pp.104, 113–114, Sections 8.2, 8.6)

### Caxton's printing press as the practical trigger for standardization pressure

William Caxton, England's first printer (press established in London, 1476), is presented as the
pivot figure converting an abstract dialect-diversity problem into an urgent practical one: a printed
book, unlike a hand-copied manuscript, would be read by buyers from every English dialect region, so
a printer-translator had to actively choose **one** form of a word for the whole print run, with no
way to locally tailor spelling/vocabulary to each reader's own dialect the way a scribe copying a
single manuscript for a local patron effectively could. Caxton's own 1490 preface to *Eneydos*
narrates this dilemma directly through the *egg*/*eyren* anecdote (a Kentish-dialect speaker failing
to recognize the ON-derived *egg(ys)* that Caxton himself, as a more northern/London-oriented speaker,
would naturally reach for) as a concrete illustration of exactly the choice a national print medium
forces. His revision of Trevisa's 1385 *Polychronicon* text is given as a directly comparable pair:
Caxton found Trevisa's century-old English "rude and old" by 1482 and modernized some of its
vocabulary/spelling, which lets the book trace precisely which specific forms had become archaic to an
educated reader within roughly one century — the two texts are reproduced in full parallel for this
reason (Texts 61–62). (pp.108, 110–111, Sections 8.4–8.4.2)

### Why Caxton's own practice still fell short of a consistent standard

Despite his documented awareness of the dialect problem, close examination of Caxton's own printed
texts (the book specifically assigns this as an analytical activity, Activity 8.5) shows he did
**not** actually devise or consistently apply a single regular spelling system of his own — many of
his individual spelling and grammatical-form choices were already old-fashioned even by the standards
of 1480s English, and he shows real inconsistency across his own printed output. The chapter's
implicit point: recognizing a standardization problem and recognizing its stakes is not the same as
solving it — a fully standardized written English remained roughly 200 years further off, worked out
gradually through the accumulated, uncoordinated choices of successive printers, writers, and (later)
grammarians/lexicographers, not settled by any single figure or text. (p.111, Section 8.4.2 and
Activity 8.5)

## Grammar points

### 9.1 The simple present and present progressive tenses (1)

**9.1A Pronunciation and spelling of the 3rd-person -s.** Follows the identical three-way
pronunciation rule already established for plural -s in Chapter 2 (/s/ after voiceless consonants,
/ɪz/ after sibilants, /z/ elsewhere), with the identical -s/-es spelling rules, p.122.

**9.1B Seven uses of the simple present.** Permanent truths; "the present period" (a currently-true
general situation); habitual actions; future reference for fixed timetables/schedules; spontaneous
observations and declarations; instructions (e.g. recipe steps); and live commentaries, p.122.

**9.1C Stative and dynamic verbs.** *Stative* verbs describe states with no beginning or end and are
not normally used in a progressive tense; most English verbs are *dynamic* and can describe either a
habit (simple present) or an action in progress (present progressive). A third class is dual-use:
stative or dynamic depending on which specific sense of the verb is meant (*weigh* as a deliberate
action vs. *weigh* as an inherent state). Five closed semantic groups cover the core stative
vocabulary — see the Vocabulary table, p.123.

### 9.2 The simple present and present progressive tenses (2)

**9.2A Spelling: adding -ing.** Most verbs add -ing unchanged; a verb ending in silent -e drops the
-e; a single vowel followed by a single consonant doubles that consonant; a two-syllable verb
doubles its final consonant only if the *second* syllable is stressed (begin→beginning, but
differ→differing is unstressed and does not double); -ic becomes -ick before -ing; -ie becomes -y,
p.124.

**9.2B Four uses of the present progressive.** Actions in progress at the moment of speaking;
temporary situations/actions not necessarily happening at this exact instant; planned future actions
(with a future adverbial); and repeated actions emphasized with *always/forever*, p.124.

### 9.3 The simple past tense

**9.3A Regular past-tense pronunciation and spelling.** Regular verbs always add -d/-ed in spelling,
but the pronunciation varies: /d/ as the default (never an extra syllable); /t/ after a voiceless
consonant sound; a few verbs allow either /d/ or /t/ interchangeably in both spelling and sound
(burned/burnt); and /ɪd/ (a genuine extra syllable) specifically after a base ending in a /t/ or /d/
sound, p.126.

**9.3B Irregular past forms.** Three structural patterns among irregular verbs: identical form
throughout (hit-hit-hit); one part changes (keep-kept-kept); two parts change (break-broke-broken),
p.126.

**9.3C Uses of the simple past.** Describes finished past events/actions/situations; the "when" must
always be stated or clearly implied by context, even if not explicitly given in the sentence; covers
both recent and distant past, and past habitual actions, p.127.

### 9.4 The simple past and past progressive tenses

**9.4A Irregular verbs with identical present and past forms.** A small closed set (burst, cost, cut,
hit, put) requires special attention since the 3rd-person-singular present form is also unchanged
(he puts / he put) — context or an adverbial is the only way to distinguish tense for most persons,
p.128.

**9.4B Five uses of the past progressive.** Temporary actions in progress in the past (often
emphasized with *all*); an action in progress when a second, shorter action interrupted it
(introduced by when/while/as/just as, with the shorter interrupting action usually in the simple
past introduced by *when*); two actions happening simultaneously; repeated actions with *always*;
and polite inquiries ("I was wondering..."), p.128.

**9.4C Past tenses in story-telling.** The past progressive is conventionally used to "set the
scene" at a story's opening, with the simple past then carrying the narrative's sequence of
completed events, p.129.

### 9.5 The simple present perfect and present perfect progressive

**9.5A Two uses of the simple present perfect.** (1) Actions beginning in the past and continuing to
the present moment — with time references like *before/ever/never/up till now/so far*, or with
*since/for*; (2) actions at an unspecified past time — with no time reference at all, with
recent-time references (*just/recently/already/still/yet*), or for repeated/habitual actions, p.130.

**9.5B The present perfect progressive.** Substitutes for the simple present perfect specifically to
emphasize that an action has been ongoing throughout a period; the "continuity" verb class is
especially often used in this progressive form with *since/for*, p.130.

**9.5C Simple present perfect vs. progressive compared.** The progressive implies the job/action is
still unfinished; the simple form implies it is definitely finished, p.131.

**9.5D Simple past vs. simple present perfect compared.** The simple past always states or implies
exactly *when* something happened; the present perfect never states exactly when, even when
combined with a recent-time word like *just*, p.131.

### 9.6 The simple past perfect and past perfect progressive tenses

**9.6A Uses of the simple past perfect.** Two past-tense references in a row don't obligatorily
require the past perfect, but it becomes necessary to disambiguate which of two past events happened
first — the past perfect marks "an earlier past," i.e. the first of two or more actions. Commonly
introduced by when/after/as soon as/by the time that, and combined with adverbs like
already/ever/never...before, p.132.

**9.6B Uses of the present and past perfect progressive.** The past perfect progressive is simply
the "past" counterpart of the present perfect progressive, carrying the same continuity-emphasizing
function; both are used with the "continuity" verb class, for repeated actions, and for drawing
conclusions from present evidence (red eyes → "it's clear she's/she'd been crying"), p.132.

**9.6C Simple past perfect vs. past perfect progressive compared.** The progressive signals the
action was still uncompleted at that past reference point; the simple form signals it was completed
by then, p.133.

### 9.7 The simple future tense

**9.7A Uses of 'will' and 'shall'.** Both are modal verbs (like *can/must*) used to predict the
future (the simple future), but each also carries several other distinct functions: intentions/
promises, requests/invitations, offers, suggestions, threats, and on-the-spot decisions, p.134.

**9.7B Forms, and uses, of the will/shall future.** *Will* is used with all persons in general
usage; British English also commonly uses *shall* with I/we. Both weaken to /ʃəl/ or contract to
'll in speech (and often in informal writing), especially after a vowel. Negative contractions:
'll not/won't, and (rare in American English) shan't. The three core uses: prediction; formal
announcements of pre-arranged events; and expressing hopes/expectations, p.134.

### 9.8 The simple future, the future progressive, the future perfect

**9.8A Simple future vs. future progressive.** The future progressive emphasizes an action that will
be in progress at a future point, especially when imagining oneself in that future moment; it also
"softens" a will+verb statement/question into something more polite; and it parallels the present
progressive's planned-action use, p.136.

**9.8B Future perfect simple vs. progressive.** The future perfect simple (with *by*, not till/until)
marks an action as completed by a given future time, especially with inherently-completive verbs
(complete, finish, retire); the future perfect progressive instead uses the "continuity" verb class
to describe something presently in progress that will still be in progress at that future point,
p.137.

### 9.9 'Going to' and other ways of expressing the future

**9.9A Uses of the 'going to'-future compared with 'will'.** *Going to* is preferred for predictions
based on present visible evidence, and for informal-style statements of intention; it is used
(interchangeably with the present/future progressive) for already-planned actions. *Will* is
reserved for a decision made at the moment of speaking, p.138.

**9.9B 'am/is/are to', 'be about to', 'be due to'.** *To be to* marks formal arrangements/duties,
formal instructions, and prohibitions; *to be about to* marks the immediate future; *to be due to*
is specifically used for scheduled/timetabled events, p.138.

**9.9C The future-in-the-past.** *was going to, was about to, was to, was due to* refer to events
that were destined or planned to happen, viewed from a past vantage point — used both for events
later revealed as unforeseen and for plans that were subsequently interrupted; *would* is also used
for "destiny" in narrative story-telling, p.139.

### 9.10 The imperative

**9.10A Functions of the imperative beyond giving orders.** The imperative also offers, prohibits,
directs, and warns — see the Vocabulary table. Adding *do* makes an ordinary imperative more polite;
adding *do* before a negative imperative makes it more urgent/emphatic, p.140.

**9.10B Addressing particular people with the imperative.** A bare imperative implicitly addresses
"you" (singular or plural); to target a specific addressee, English adds an unstressed *you* for a
plain instruction, a stressed *you* to express annoyance, a vocative name (with or without *you*),
or a reflexive pronoun with certain verbs. Group addressees use *everybody*, *somebody/nobody*, or
*don't...anybody*, p.140.

**9.10C The imperative with question tags.** Adding a tag softens or reframes an imperative as a
request: *will you?/won't you?/can't you?* for expressing annoyance; *would you?/could you?/can you?*
for a neutral request; *will you?/won't you?* for a friendly offer — the actual attitude conveyed
comes largely from stress/intonation, not the tag alone, p.141.

**9.10D Double imperatives joined by 'and'.** Two imperatives in sequence are joined with *and*, never
*to* ("Go and buy," not "*Go to buy*"); the sole exception is *try*, which accepts either ("Try and
help" or "Try to help"), p.141.

**Source:** Charles Barber, Joan C. Beal & Philip A. Shaw, *The English Language: A Historical

## Grammar points

### Two waves of Scandinavian-origin speakers, two different linguistic outcomes

The chapter's organizing contrast: the Viking raiders/settlers (c. 750–1050) kept their Old Norse
speech and influenced English directly through centuries of contact and intermarriage in the
Danelaw; the later Norman conquerors (1066) were themselves of Scandinavian (Viking) origin
genealogically, but had already fully shifted to French by the time of the Conquest, so their
linguistic legacy in England is a French one, not a second wave of Norse influence — two separate,
differently-mediated impacts on English from populations that share a common ultimate origin.

### Mutual intelligibility and the mechanics of Norse–English language mixing

Old English and Old Norse were similar enough, as both being Germanic, that **speakers could
probably communicate across the two languages with real but limited difficulty** without either
side learning the other properly — the book sketches a range of bilingual-contact scenarios:
monolingual speakers using a word from the other language with a "naturalized" pronunciation and
inflection when they didn't know the native term; code-mixing by genuinely bilingual
children-of-mixed-marriages speakers. Old Norse itself eventually died out completely in England
(already dying at the royal court by Cnut's reign, when English was the court language), but not
before leaving substantial, specifically-identifiable influence absorbed into English.

### Diagnosing a Norse loan: phonological tests and their limits

The chapter sets out concrete phonological diagnostics distinguishing inherited OE forms from Norse
loans, while flagging that **dialectal OE variants can sometimes mimic a Norse-loan outcome**, so no
single test is fully reliable in isolation:
- OE underwent **front mutation** and **palatalization** of /g/ before front vowels; Old Norse did
  not. Diagnostic pair: OE *ege* (front-mutated, palatalized /g/→[j]) would regularly give Modern
  *\*eye*, not *awe*; the actual Modern form *awe* is explained as borrowed from the parallel,
  unmutated Old Norse form *agi*. Similarly, OE *ǣg* vs. ON *egg* "egg" — the Modern form *egg*
  (not the expected OE-descended form, found in Chaucer as *ey*) is a Scandinavian loan.
- OE [tʃ] (from palatalized /k/, see `pt03`) corresponds to retained Old Norse [k]: *church*
  (English, [tʃ]) vs. *kirk* (Scandinavian, [k]); *ditch* (English) vs. *dike* (Scandinavian).
- OE [ʃ] (from palatalized *sc*) corresponds to retained Old Norse [sk]: *shirt* (English) vs.
  *skirt* (Scandinavian) — both ultimately mean "a short garment," now split into different
  referents; similarly *shrub*/*scrub*.
- PG *ai* gave OE *ā* but Old Norse *ei*: ON *nei* → Modern *nay*; OE *nā* → Modern *no* — a genuine
  doublet, both forms surviving with slightly different modern functions, from the same ultimate PG
  root. Related pairs: *hail!* (interjection, Scandinavian, cognate with native *whole*) vs. *swain*
  "herdsman" (cognate with native *swān*).
- PG *au* gave OE *ēa* but Old Norse (retained) *au*: OE *lēas* vs. ON-derived *loose*; the OE form
  itself survives too, but demoted to the bound suffix *-less* (OE *hāmlēas* → *homeless*).

### Semantic specialization when a native and a borrowed Norse word compete

Several worked cases of what happens when a native OE word and a Norse-borrowed word for
overlapping meanings both survive: *to take* (ON) displaced native *niman* almost entirely, but
*niman*'s descendant *nim* survived marginally into Early Modern English narrowed specifically to
"to steal" (hence Shakespeare's pickpocket character Corporal Nym), and its past participle
*numen* survives independently as Modern *numb* ("seized, taken" → "deprived of sensation/taken
over by cold"). *Gate* "way, road, street" (ON) and native *geat* "an opening in a wall" both
survive with fully split meanings, visible today in place-names (*Aldgate*/*Newgate* in London =
literal wall-openings; *Briggate*/*Kirkgate* in Leeds/York = "street"). ON *dvelja* "to dwell" and
OE *dwellan* "to lead astray" produced the Modern form *dwell* with the Norse meaning but (by
regular sound development) the Old English word-shape. *Loaf*/*bread* is the mirror case: OE *hlāf*
"bread" (meaning narrowed) supplies the modern *form* of *loaf*; ON *brauð* "fragment" supplies the
modern *meaning* of *bread* — form from one language, meaning from the other, in a single word.

### Scandinavian loans skew toward the "central core" of vocabulary — unlike French loans

The chapter's central sociolinguistic claim, directly building on the Ch. 3 loanword-vulnerability
methodology (`pt02`): Scandinavian loanwords are comparatively few in raw count next to the much
larger French/Latin stock, but disproportionately penetrate **core, ordinary, high-frequency
vocabulary** — including, strikingly, the kinship term *sister* and the full personal pronoun set
*they/them/their* (displacing native *hīe/him/hiera*), both categories that the Ch. 3 methodology
specifically flags as normally resistant to borrowing. The book's explanation: the Viking settlers
and English mixed on **roughly equal social terms** over an extended period, unlike the later
Norman French, who formed "a separate caste that imposed much of their culture on their
subordinates" — so Norse loans "came in on the ground floor" of the language, while French loans
instead concentrate in **domain-specific, elite-marked registers** (law, government, the church,
heraldry, fashion, the aristocratic dining table), penetrating "downwards" from a position of social
dominance rather than mixing in as equals. The pronoun borrowing specifically is flagged as unusual
cross-linguistically (a full closed-class paradigm replacement), with the functional explanation
that *they/them/their* were less confusable with *he/him/her* than the native forms they replaced,
plausibly aiding their spread once introduced — spreading from northern dialects southward across
the Middle English period, with Chaucer's own London English still mixing: native *they*
(nominative) alongside Scandinavian-free *hem*/*hire* for "them"/"their."

### Borrowed inflectional endings reanalyzed as part of the word stem

Two specific cases where English speakers **mistakenly incorporated a Norse grammatical ending into
the borrowed word itself**, rather than correctly identifying it as separable inflectional
morphology: the Old Norse neuter-adjective/adverb ending *-t* (added to ON *þverr* "adverse,
contrary" to form neuter *þvert*) was taken over wholesale into English as part of the word,
producing *thwart* — the same misanalyzed *-t* also survives in *want* and *scant*. Similarly, the
Old Norse reflexive/mediopassive suffix *-sk* was similarly absorbed as a root-final consonant
cluster, preserved in *bask* ("to bathe oneself") and the archaic *busk* ("to prepare oneself").

### Middle English dialects and the Scots/English literary-standard split

Figure 10's five-way regional division (Northern [further divisible into Scots and northern
English], East Midland, West Midland, south-eastern, southern) is presented with the same caveat as
Ch. 5's OE dialect map — a simplification over a true isogloss-bundle reality, now with actual
empirical backing cited (a University of Edinburgh dialect atlas mapping ~300 spelling-variant items
across Late Middle English). **Two separate standard written varieties eventually emerged**: Middle
Scots, descended from the Northumbrian dialect of Old English via the politically-independent
Scottish kingdom's own "Inglis" (a label distinct from, and politically separate from, Gaelic and
the Western Isles' Norse-derived speech); and the eventual English standard, which — as flagged
already — derives from East Midland, not West Saxon-descended southern English, substantially
because of the East Midlands' commercial/agricultural importance and the influence of Cambridge,
not London alone. London's own dialect is explicitly described as **"rather a mixed one"** —
basically East Midland in the fourteenth century but showing south-eastern/southern border
influence, which the book uses to explain why some non-East-Midland forms (*merry*, *left*) survive
in the modern standard. The book gives representative ME regional verb-inflection data for "she
comes"/"they come" (Table, p.150): Northern *scho comis*/*thai come*; East Midland *sche comes*/
*thei comen*; West Midland *hue cometh*/*hi comen*; south-eastern *hi cometh*/*heo cometh*;
Southern *heo cometh*/*he cometh* — **Modern Standard English's 3rd-singular *-s* and plural *-∅*
derive from the northern/East-Midland pattern**, not from the southern forms, a second concrete
instance (after the pronoun-paradigm case above) of Scandinavian-contact-zone dialects, not the
politically "senior" West Saxon/southern tradition, supplying the grammatical forms that became
standard. Key sound/vocabulary dialect markers cataloged: ME reflexes of OE long *ā* (unchanged
north of the Humber vs. rounded to /ɔː/ south of it, underlying Modern *home*/*boat* vs. Scots
*haim*, and the *raid*/*road* doublet, both from OE *rād* "a riding, a journey" — the semantic
narrowing of *raid* to specifically mean a cross-border military incursion is explicitly tied to
Anglo-Scottish border history); OE front-rounded *y*/*ȳ* reflexes splitting by region into ME *i*
(north/East Midland/Devon-Dorset-Wiltshire), *e* (Kent/Essex/Middlesex/Sussex/Suffolk), or *u*
(old West Saxon area) — Standard Modern English descends from the *i*/*ī* line (*kin*, *mice*), but
retains isolated loans from the other dialects (*merry*, *left* from south-eastern; *bury*'s
Kentish-descended spelling with southern/East-Midland-descended pronunciation); West Midland/
south-eastern raising of OE short *æ* to ME *e* (*appel*/*eppel* "apple"); West Midland rounding of
OE short *a* before nasals (*mon*/*thonc* vs. *man*/*thanc* elsewhere); southern voicing of
word-initial /f-, θ-, s-/ to /v-, ð-, z-/ (surviving into the modern standard specifically in *vat*
and *vixen*, both from south-western/southern dialect forms of OE *fæt*, *fyxen*).

### French in England: Anglo-Norman's rise, decline, and the long shift back to English

French was the language of the aristocracy and court "for over two hundred years" post-Conquest, but
the book is explicit that **the Conquest was not the cultural "civilizing" event it's sometimes
assumed to be** — Anglo-Saxon England already had a sophisticated literary, artistic, and
administrative culture (fine prose/verse traditions going back to the 8th century, illuminated
manuscripts, metalwork, the Bayeux Tapestry probably English-made); French became dominant purely
because it was the conquerors' language, not because of any prior cultural deficiency. The specific
French variety used in England, **Anglo-Norman** (from Norman French, a northern Old French
dialect), developed its own local characteristics and, once the Central French dialect of Paris
began dominating continental French in the 13th century, came to be seen as old-fashioned/rustic
within France — even as English speakers continued learning it as a prestige second language. Three
specific historical turning points the book credits for French's decline: **King John's loss of
Normandy (early 13th c.)**, which forced the cross-Channel nobility to choose English or Norman
estates (commonly resolved by splitting inheritance between two sons) and severed the material
interest that had kept them bilingual; growing **anti-foreigner sentiment** (explicitly linked to
the Barons' Wars against Henry III, mid-13th c.); and **Henry IV's accession in 1399**, the first
monarch since the Conquest whose native tongue was English. Concrete 14th-century institutional
markers of the English revival: the king's 1362 opening speech to Parliament delivered in English;
a 1362 Act making English the official language of the law courts (though records stayed in Latin);
and — via a translated excerpt from Higden's *Polychronicon* (Latin, early 14th c.) as rendered into
English by John of Trevisa in 1385 — explicit contemporary testimony that grammar-school
instruction switched from French-medium to English-medium construing within living memory of the
writer (Trevisa names the specific teachers, John Cornwall and Richard Pencrich, credited with the
change), with the stated trade-off that children "learn their grammar more quickly" but "know no
more French than their left heel" if they never travel abroad. The book treats this as unusually
direct, datable documentary evidence for a language-shift-in-progress claim, rather than the usual
indirect inference historical linguistics has to rely on.

### Why French and Scandinavian loans differ systematically, beyond just register

Beyond the social-dominance explanation above, the book lists further systematic contrasts: French
loanwords were "entirely new," with **no obvious formal resemblance** to any existing English word
(unlike many Scandinavian loans, which were often just a dialectal variant of an English cognate the
listener could partly recognize already); French loans cluster heavily in **specific semantic
domains** tied to Norman institutional dominance — war, ecclesiastical matters, law, heraldry,
fashion — rather than being spread evenly across the vocabulary the way Scandinavian loans are; and,
geographically, French loans spread outward from **London and the royal/noble court** (a political-
prestige-driven diffusion pattern) rather than the Scandinavian loans' diffusion outward from the
**Danelaw settlement zone** (a population-contact-driven diffusion pattern) — two structurally
different mechanisms of lexical borrowing operating on the same language within roughly the same
few centuries.

### Doublets: the same root borrowed twice via two different Old French dialects

Because Old French itself had real dialectal variation (Anglo-Norman/Norman vs. Central French, the
Paris-based prestige dialect that only later came to dominate France itself), and English absorbed
loans from **both** at different periods, English ended up with true etymological doublets — two
distinct Modern English words descending from one Old French root, via its two different dialect
forms, borrowed at two different times. Documented sound correspondences behind these doublets: Old
French diphthong *ei* → Anglo-Norman kept [ei], Central French → [oi]/later [wa] (*prey/strait/veil*
vs. Modern French *proie/étroit/voile*); word-initial [ga]/[ka] → Anglo-Norman kept them,
Central French affricated to [dʒa]/[tʃa] (*garden/catch* vs. Modern French *jardin/chasser*);
Old French *s* before a consonant → Norman kept [s] where Central French had already begun losing
it, and specifically Norman *ch*/*[tʃ]* vs. Central French *s*/[s] in some verb stems (*catch* vs.
*chase*, from Norman *cachier* vs. Central French *chacier* — **the book's primary worked
doublet-pair**, one Old French root, *cachier*/*chacier*, giving two separate Modern English words
with different meanings via two different borrowing events); Germanic [w] in Old French loanwords
→ retained in Norman, shifted to [g] in Central French (*catch/launch* vs. *chasser/lancer*;
English *wage/war/wardrobe* vs. Modern French *gage/guerre/garderobe* — of this last triple, only
*wage* also has a surviving English Central-French-period doublet partner, *gage* "a pledge,"
with a now-distinct meaning). Other cited doublet pairs sharing this early-Norman/later-Central-
French split, with meanings now diverged: *cattle*/*chattel*, *warden*/*guardian*. The book also
notes that early (mostly Norman-period, Conquest-era) loans tend to be more thoroughly assimilated,
everyday, and semantically broadened (*garden*, *hour*, *market*, *people*, *wage*) than the later
(13th–14th century, Central-French-period) main wave of borrowing, which brought in the bulk of the
more specialized/formal vocabulary catalogued above.

### Phonological assimilation of French loans, including stress-shift over time

French loanwords were initially given something close to a French pronunciation by bilingual
speakers, but were soon adapted to English's own phonological system — the book's modern analogy is
*garage*, borrowed with a French-style uvular [r]/pronunciation by few English speakers today, and
with English-pattern initial stress, even among speakers who know French. The same stress-shift
pattern is documented for Middle English French loans generally: a word like *nature* was initially
stressed on the second syllable (matching French usage) but shifted to first-syllable stress over
time, with Chaucer's poetry showing the stress still fluctuating between both options mid-shift; the
final resting point for stress in polysyllabic loans wasn't uniform, and the book notes synchronic
variation persists today for some of these words (*melody* vs. *advertisement*, or variant stress in
*controversy*).

### French loans opened the door to further, less-resisted borrowing generally

The book's closing argument for the chapter: because early French loans assimilated so completely
that they no longer felt "foreign," **English as a whole became comparatively more willing to
borrow and comparatively less inclined to coin new words from its own native resources**, a
contrast explicitly drawn against the late-Old-English pattern of inventing native compounds for
new/learned concepts (*tungolcræft* "astronomy," *þrīnes* "trinity" — see `pt03`). By (late) Middle
English, the normal pattern for a learned/new concept had flipped to borrowing a Latin-via-French
word instead (*astronomy*, *trinity*). At the same time, assimilated French stems quickly became
productive bases for ordinary **native** derivational morphology — French-root + native affix
hybrids (*beautiful*, *faithless*, *gentleness*, *preaching*, *ungracious*) appear "quite soon after
the Conquest," showing the borrowed vocabulary wasn't kept as a separate foreign stratum but was
fully integrated into the language's own word-formation system almost immediately.

### Disruption and partial continuity in English literary tradition under French dominance

Old English's native prose-history-writing tradition was lost under French dominance (Robert of
Gloucester's verse chronicle, quoted at length in the book with full translation as the chapter's
specimen text, writes history in English again but in verse, "in the French manner," not reviving
OE's lost prose-history convention); but the book notes genuine continuity survived in pockets — a
flourishing alliterative-verse poetic school in the second half of the 14th century, directly
descended from Old English poetic meter (contrasted with Chaucer's own syllable-counted, rhyming,
continental-style verse) — evidence that French's centuries of dominance disrupted but did not
fully erase England's native literary-formal traditions.

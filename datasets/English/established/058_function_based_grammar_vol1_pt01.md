## Grammar points

### Structure vs. function: two competing analogies for grammar (p.1–2)

Givón frames the whole book's approach against two analogies. The "logic-machine" (Newtonian
physics) view treats grammar as an exceptionless, function-blind system of rules — one can describe
the machine's parts fully while bracketing what the whole is *for*. The biological-organism view,
which Givón adopts, treats grammatical structure as adapted by use the way anatomy is adapted by
function: structure cannot be fully understood without its function, and apparent counter-examples
(irregular plurals, irregular past tense) are the equivalent of vestigial organs — fossilized residue
of once-functional rules, now surviving only because historical change is cumulative and gradual.

### Arbitrary vs. motivated rules (p.2–3)

A rule that looks arbitrary today is usually not synchronically arbitrary by design — it is the
product of several historical changes compounding over time until the original functional
motivation is obscured. Arbitrariness is thus a *minority* condition in any grammar's rule
inventory, and its presence is itself diagnostic of historical residue rather than evidence against a
functional view of grammar generally.

### Rules of grammar vs. communicative strategies: core and periphery (p.3–4)

Most grammar rules behave like a fast, largely-automated "closed behavioral program" for the bulk
of typical cases, but leave a smaller set of borderline cases to slower, deliberate, "open behavioral
program" processing. This mirrors the acquisition split between prewired skills and skills (reading,
typing, playing an instrument) that start effortful and only become automated through practice —
grammar is acquired postnatally through trial-and-error but becomes highly automated over a
lifetime, while still retaining a margin of context-sensitive, non-automatic judgment at its edges.

### Cross-language diversity of grammatical strategies is constrained, not arbitrary (p.4–5)

Languages differ in which grammatical strategy codes a given communicative function, but the
space of attested strategies is far from unlimited — it is constrained in part by which other,
functionally-related domains a language has already committed a strategy to. Givón's worked
examples of this functional "borrowing" across domains: English relative pronouns (*who, whom,
which, where, when*) derive historically from interrogative pronouns used in WH-questions; the
subordinator *that* (relative clauses, verb complements) derives from the demonstrative pronoun
*that*; and the causal subordinator *since* ('because') derives from its earlier purely temporal sense
('from the time'). A grammatical strategy's current function is thus often best explained by its
*prior* function in a neighboring domain, not by synchronic design.

### Ten dimensions along which "the grammar" of a language actually varies (p.7)

Givón treats "The Grammar" / "The Language" (e.g. "American English") as a convenient fiction
masking real internal diversity along (not fully independent, but not fully correlated either) axes:
(a) history (older/obsolete vs. current usage), (b) age (older vs. younger speakers), (c) medium
(written vs. spoken), (d) education, (e) formality, (f) social class, (g) ethnicity (majority vs. minority
sub-cultures), (h) geography (regional/urban vs. rural), (i) native vs. non-native skill, (j) individual/
family idiolect. Written language tends to correlate with older usage, older/educated speakers,
formal register, higher status, and urban dialect — but the correlation is not absolute.

### Three historical-change pressures reshaping a lexicon/grammar (p.8, also p.48–49)

Individual speakers constantly reshape usage via (a) simplification toward greater code-
transparency, (b) elaboration toward greater expressive power, and (c) truncation/chopping toward
greater processing economy. The same three-way split reappears in ch.2 (p.48–49) specifically as the
three pressures that add or remove members from a closed grammatical/derivational class (as
opposed to the purely cultural pressure that drives open lexical-class turnover).

### Three concentric functional realms: word / clause / discourse (p.21–22, restated p.41–42)

The core architecture of the whole book: words code concepts and have *meaning*; clauses
(=sentences) code propositions and carry *information*; discourse strings propositions together and
has *coherence*. Each level presupposes but is not reducible to the one below it — understanding
word meaning is necessary-but-not-sufficient for understanding clause information, and
understanding clause information is necessary-but-not-sufficient for understanding discourse
coherence. Ch.2 operationalizes the three-way split with a truth/coherence diagnostic: a single word
("cow") cannot itself be true or false — only a proposition can; and two individually-true
propositions can combine into a coherent or incoherent discourse depending on culturally-shared
expectations about plausible event sequencing (*"The cow jumped over the fence and broke her legs"*
vs. the reversed, odd-sounding order) or about relevance (*"My mechanic loves nachos...but Charles
Darwin didn't"* is truth-functionally fine but discourse-incoherent).

### Truncated single-word/clause utterances standing in for full propositions (p.23–24)

A one-word reply ("—The maid.") or fixed social-formula ("Scalpel!", "Water!", "Mommy!", "Gravy?",
"Scram!") is not evidence that single words carry propositional information on their own — it is a
discourse-licensed ellipsis, recoverable only because the surrounding question/context supplies the
missing clause structure.

### Semantic well-formedness is independent of truth/coherence (p.24–25)

Chomsky's "Colorless green ideas sleep furiously" is deployed to show that individual-word meaning
('semantic rigidity'/specificity of each word) can be violated even in a syntactically well-formed
sentence, independently of whether the combined proposition could ever be true — word-level
semantic compatibility, clause-level propositional well-formedness, and discourse-level coherence
are three independently-checkable properties.

### Grammar/syntax as a joint code for two realms, via four coding devices (p.25–27)

Syntax jointly codes (a) the propositional information of the clause and (b) the discourse-coherence
of the clause in its context — and these two coding jobs sometimes conflict, with (per Givón) the
bulk of syntactic structure actually serving discourse-pragmatics rather than propositional semantics.
The four concrete coding devices surveyed throughout the book are: grammatical morphology,
word-order, intonation patterns, and (more abstractly) constraints.

### Theme-and-variation as the book's descriptive method (p.27–28)

Simple clauses are described as an underlying "theme"; complex clause-types are described as
"variations" definable only in terms of (a) the theme they share and (b) the specific nature of their
departure from it. Given the theme *Mary kicked the ball*, Givón catalogs four independent variation
dimensions: dependent/subordinate clauses (*Mary wanted to kick the ball*), non-declarative clauses
(*Did Mary kick the ball? / Kick the ball!*), negative clauses (*Mary didn't kick the ball*), and passive
clauses (*The ball was kicked by Mary*) — each dimension can in principle combine with the others.

### Parsing / tree diagrams: three descriptive tasks, two label-levels (p.28–30)

Tree-diagram parsing handles three things at once: linear order (temporal sequence of constituents),
constituency (part-whole structure), and category labels (what kind of unit each constituent is).
Givón deliberately **double-labels** subject and object constituents — e.g. [SUBJ[NP]], [OBJ[NP]] —
because grammatical-relation labels (subject/object, discussed fully in ch.3) and syntactic-structure
labels (NP/VP/S) track two genuinely distinct, parallel levels of description; collapsing them into one
label system (as some formal frameworks attempt) sacrifices descriptive coherence for formal
economy, a tradeoff Givón explicitly rejects (p.30).

### Deep structure vs. surface structure: semantic transparency vs. opacity (p.30–36)

In simple ("theme") clauses, deep and surface structure coincide and the surface is semantically
transparent. In complex ("variant") clauses, surface structure is tilted toward coding discourse-
pragmatic function at the expense of semantic transparency — the surface no longer reveals the
underlying propositional (deep) structure without distortion, which is why complex clauses can be
genuinely syntactically ambiguous. Worked examples: *"Flying planes can be dangerous"* has two
deep structures depending on whether *fly* is construed as intransitive (subject = *planes*) or
transitive (object = *planes*); *"Sally is easy to please"* vs. *"Sally is eager to please"* share one
surface structure but differ in deep structure — demonstrable because only one of each paired
paraphrase is grammatical (*"To please Sally is easy"* / *\*"To please Sally is eager"*), revealing that
*Sally* is the object of *please* in the "easy" sentence but is also the subject of *please* in the "eager"
sentence; *"I am looking for someone to teach"* is ambiguous between *someone who will teach
(X) French* and *someone (whom) I will teach French to*, disambiguated only by discourse context
(a short-staffed principal vs. an unemployed teacher). Despite this potential ambiguity, real discourse
context usually resolves it, which is why complex, "mutilated" syntax rarely actually blocks
communication in practice.

### Three-level code/message hierarchy restated with a truth/coherence test (p.41–43)

(Chapter 2's opening recapitulation of the chapter-1 architecture; see above — included here because
the vocabulary-chapter frames the entire rest of the book as the study of the smallest level, words,
as the necessary first step toward clause- and discourse-level description.)

### The lexicon as semantic-feature cluster / semantic-field network (p.43–44)

A word's meaning is not atomic — it is a cluster of semantic features (e.g. *elephant*: mammal,
herbivore, large, tusked, ivory-bearing, trunked, hunted, poached, circus-performing,
Africa/India-associated). The same feature set, viewed from the lexicon-as-a-whole rather than the
single-word perspective, defines a network of semantic fields (bio-classification, food, size,
dentition, tooth-material, etc.) that *other* words also belong to — "semantic feature" and "semantic
field" are the same fact seen from two directions (word-internal vs. lexicon-wide).

### History of the English lexicon: Germanic core + four Romance/Latin borrowing channels (p.45–46)

English vocabulary is not homogeneous in origin. The Germanic/Anglo-Saxon core came from
dialects related to Frisian, transplanted to Britain after c. AD 400 (written tradition from c. AD 700),
itself already containing early intra-Germanic borrowings plus later Scandinavian borrowings
pre-dating the Norman conquest. The non-Germanic vocabulary arrived through four distinct
channels: (a) Latin borrowed into Continental Germanic before the Anglo-Saxon migration; (b) Latin
borrowed into British Celtic during Roman Britain, then transmitted into Anglo-Saxon after AD 400;
(c) French vocabulary following the 1066 Norman conquest; (d) later "learned" Latin vocabulary
deliberately acquired by scholars (e.g. Samuel Johnson) and the sciences as scholarly communication
shifted from Latin to English. The first two channels are fully nativized (undetectable without
etymological training); Norman-French and learned-Latin borrowings remain synchronically
distinguishable from the Germanic stock along a whole cluster of criteria: Germanic items skew
toward everyday-life semantic fields, early acquisition, small word-size, word-initial stress, Germanic
phonological/derivational rules, and (uniquely) carry *all* of English's grammatical function; Romance
items skew toward learned/abstract fields, late acquisition, larger word-size, non-initial stress,
Romance phonological/derivational rules, and contribute lexical content only — never grammatical
morphology.

### Lexical vs. grammatical vs. derivational vocabulary: a three-way functional split (p.46–50)

English vocabulary splits into lexical ('content') words vs. non-lexical ('function') words, the latter
further split into grammatical morphemes (build clausal grammar; code both propositional meaning
and discourse pragmatics) and derivational morphemes (derive new lexical words from existing ones
— properly the province of lexicography, not grammar, per Givón, who accordingly de-emphasizes it
after ch.2). Seven criteria distinguish lexical words from the two non-lexical types: morphemic status
(free vs. bound), word size (large vs. small), stress (stressed vs. unstressed), meaning (semantically
complex/specific vs. simple/general-classificatory), class size (few large classes vs. many small
classes), membership (open, culturally-driven turnover vs. closed, grammatically-driven turnover —
tying back to the simplification/elaboration/truncation pressures above), and historical origin (both
native and borrowed for lexical words and derivational morphemes, but **all native Germanic** for
English grammatical morphemes specifically). A "pidgin" register — communicating with lexical
words alone, stripped of grammar — is possible but slow, error-prone, and context-bound; Givón
illustrates the opposite extreme (grammatical morphemes alone, stripped of lexical content) with a
text reduced to only its function words, which communicates nothing at all on its own, versus a
lexical-only ("pidgin") rendition of the same passage, which at least preserves a recoverable
skeleton of the message.

### Morphemic status of English grammatical vocabulary is historically unstable, not uniform (p.50–51)

Some English grammatical morphemes surface as bound affixes (*walked, singing, heartless,
ungrateful, rearrange*); others — *the horse, a woman, of wine, my house, should leave, had left,
didn't want* — are written as if they were independent words, even though functionally they behave
as grammatical morphemes. This split reflects the historical trajectory by which grammatical (and
derivational) morphemes arise *from* erstwhile lexical words: short, unstressed function words
naturally drift toward bound-morpheme status in speech (contraction, stress loss, cliticization to a
neighboring lexical word) faster than a conservative writing system updates to reflect it — so
orthography can lag well behind a morpheme's actual synchronic grammatical status.

### Four major lexical word-classes, defined by a 3-part criterion cluster, not a strict definition (p.51–54)

Nouns, verbs, adjectives, and adverbs are each characterized by a *cluster* of semantic,
morphological, and syntactic criteria rather than any single necessary-and-sufficient definition —
Givón invokes Jespersen's point that grammarians recognize word-class membership the way people
recognize a cow vs. a cat (by a weighted bundle of cues, learned by exposure to many instances), and
Sapir's "all grammars leak" to underline that natural (cognitively-real) classes are inherently fuzzier
than logical classes, admitting a prototype core plus a marginal, less-typical periphery.

### Four ranked semantic criteria distinguishing noun/adjective/verb prototypes (p.53–55)

Temporal stability (rate of change over time) is the primary criterion, followed by concreteness
(physicality), compactness (spatial scatter), and complexity (number of defining features).
Prototypical **nouns** are complex, concrete, compact, and above all time-stable (a tree stays a tree
across a season; a woman stays a woman whether angry or calm) — non-prototypical nouns like
*situation, weather, story, value* are more abstract/diffuse/unstable. Prototypical **adjectives** are
simple (single-featured), concrete, inherent, time-stable attributes (color, shape, size) —
non-prototypical ones (*angry, chaotic, unpredictable*) code more temporary/abstract states.
Prototypical **verbs** are of intermediate complexity, concrete, and above all *fast-changing* (*shoot*
is maximally prototypical; *listen* less so, being invisible/mental/durative; *depreciate* even less so,
being slow and abstract).

### Noun semantic subclassification (p.55–57)

Concreteness: concrete (exist in space+time) / temporal (exist only in time, e.g. *Sunday, July,
anniversary*) / abstract (neither, e.g. *freedom, independence, policy*). Animacy splits concrete nouns
into animate (fauna) vs. inanimate (flora + inorganic). Artifactness further splits inanimate nouns into
natural (*tree, hill, river*) vs. human-made artifacts (*house, knife*). Humanity splits animate nouns into
human vs. non-human. Countability ('individuation') cuts across both concrete and abstract nouns:
count nouns code individuated entities, mass nouns code groups/unindividuated substance — and
several nouns, especially abstract ones (*right, love, appearance, control*), have *both* a count and a
mass sense (*"one right you cannot take away"* vs. *"she's here by right"*). Generality/reference: common
("connoting") nouns refer to a type/sense; proper names ("denoting") refer to an individual token.

### Noun syntactic behavior: grammatical roles and NP-internal positions (p.57–58)

Nouns typically fill the grammatical roles subject, direct object, indirect object, or predicate (the
last splitting into referring, e.g. *"This is my desk"*, vs. non-referring, e.g. *"This is a desk"*). Within
the noun phrase a noun is typically the *head* (modifiable by an adjective, a relative clause, a
numeral, or a possessor), but can also itself be the *modifier* of another noun's phrase (*the delivery
truck, a dog-house, trout-fishing*).

### Noun grammatical morphology: plural marking and the determiner-adjacent subclasses (p.58–60)

Regular plural is a noun suffix (*girl-s, book-s, church-es*); a small closed set is irregular, via vowel
change (*foot>feet, man>men, woman>women*), suppletive-looking -en suffixation (*ox>oxen,
child>children*), or stem modification (*hoof>hooves*); and a few group nouns take zero plural
marking (*deer, fish, sheep*). Prepositions, though written as separate words, function as prefixes
to the first word of the following noun phrase and mark a wide variety of indirect-object semantic
roles: location (*in the house, to the store, at school, under one roof, near the next corner*),
beneficiary (*for Mary*), duration (*for a while*), time (*on Tuesday, during the night*), instrument
(*with a hammer*), manner (*with patience, by mistake, like a lion*), associate (*with her brother*),
and agent (*by the FBI*). Possessor pronouns and articles are both syntactically prefix-like and both
belong to the broader determiner class (fully treated in ch.6).

### Derivational morphology typology: defined by input-class → output-class (p.60–62)

Givón characterizes nominal derivational morphemes purely by what class of word they take as
input and what class they produce as output: verb-to-noun (*derive→derivation, drive→driver,
govern→governor, know→knowledge*), adjective-to-noun (*kind→kindness, wide→width,
serene→serenity*), noun-to-noun (*king→kingdom, governor→governorship, president→presidency,
child→childhood, anarchy→anarchist*), and the intermediate case of noun-noun compounding
(*mail-man, delivery-truck, bird-house, ski-lift*), where meaning is a derived composite rather than
simple affixation. The same input/output-class logic recurs for adjectives (noun-to-adjective:
*circle→circular, fate→fatal, republic→republican*; verb-to-adjective: *abuse→abusive,
read→readable, twist→twisted*; and a negative-derivation sub-pattern producing antonym pairs:
*willing→unwilling, possible→impossible, tolerable→intolerable, agreeable→disagreeable*) and for
verbs (adjective-to-verb: *large→enlarge, hard→harden, solid→solidify, active→activate*; noun-to-verb,
sometimes zero-derived: *can, dust, blackball* as verbs, or affixed: *tomb→entomb, theory→theorize,
energy→energize, fang→defang*; and verb-to-verb negative/causative pairs: *stabilize→destabilize,
do→undo; rise→raise, sit→set/seat*).

### Adjective semantic subclassification and antonym-pair asymmetry (p.62–65)

Prototypical adjectives code inherent, concrete, stable single-feature qualities, organized around
six domains: size (*big/small, wide/narrow, thick/thin, tall/short, high/low, long/short*), color
(brightness pairs plus the open color-term set), auditory qualities (*loud/soft, high/low pitch,
sharp/flat, mellow/harsh*), shape (1D/2D/3D geometric terms), taste (*sweet, sour, salty, bitter*), and
tactile qualities (*rough/smooth, hard/soft, sharp/dull*). Less prototypical adjectives code more
abstract or temporary properties: evaluative (*good/bad, pretty/ugly*, treated as a near-prototypical
exception because of their sheer frequency as subjective-but-stable judgments), transitory internal/
external/social states (*angry, busy/idle, dirty/clean, fast/slow, hot/cold*), and states of living
(*young/old, alive/dead, healthy/sick*). A cross-cutting generalization: antonym pairs are structurally
asymmetric — the *positive* member of a pair (big, long, wide, thick, high, tall, fast, loud, rough,
heavy, bright) both lends its form to the abstract-quality noun (*length* not *shortness*, *width* not
*narrowness*, *height* not *lowness*) and is the member used to question the *entire* range
("How tall is she?" can be answered "very short"), while the negative member can only question its
own sub-range ("How short is she?" cannot be answered "*very tall").

### Adjective syntax and morphology (p.65–67)

Adjectives occupy two main positions: predicate in non-verbal clauses (*Mary is tall*) and modifier
within the NP (*the tall woman*); a secondary, verb-complement-like set of positions also exists
(*"You were wrong to say that", "It was hard to forget her"*). Grammatical morphology is sparse —
mainly comparative/superlative (*short-er/-est, cold-er/-est*, with the irregular *far-ther/-thest,
good→better/best*) — and even that applies only to short Germanic adjectives; the bulk of adjectival
morphology is derivational, sourced from nouns or verbs (see above), including a dedicated
negative-adjective derivational pattern that generates additional antonym pairs.

### Verb grammatical morphology: TAM, negation, agreement, and passive (p.68–70)

Tense-aspect-modality marking in English combines pre-verbal auxiliaries (written as separate
words, historically more recent grammaticalizations from verbs) with suffixal or stem-internal
marking (older), sometimes requiring both at once (*"has spoken", "had spoken"*). Negation is
placed by a historical-residue rule: the negative morpheme *not*/*-n't* suffixes to the left-most
auxiliary before the verb (*"isn't", "can't", "haven't"*); when no auxiliary is independently present,
the dummy auxiliary *do* is inserted to carry the negative suffix (*"didn't quit", "don't know"*).
Subject agreement in English is minimal, confined to two contexts: third-person-singular *-s*
marking, and the fully-suppletive paradigm of *be* (*am/are/is/are*). Passive morphology combines
the auxiliary *be* preceding the verb with perfect/past-participle suffixation following it (*"was
seen", "was forgotten", "was opened"*), with the implicit agent recoverable as the corresponding
active-clause subject.

### Adverbs as the least homogeneous lexical class, spanning lexical↔syntactic (p.70–77)

Adverb is explicitly flagged as the hardest-to-define of the four major classes, heterogeneous in
semantics, syntax, *and* morphology, and shading continuously into fuller syntactic constructions
(subordinate adverbial clauses, treated fully in ch.13). Givón's semantic subclassification: **manner**
adverbs modify the verb's meaning (single-word: *fast, hard, softly, easily, intentionally*; syntactic:
prepositional phrases like *"like a tiger", "on purpose"*; or participial clauses: *"Disregarding what she
told him,..."*) and show flexible clause-position (initial/pre-verbal/post-verbal all possible for the same
adverb); a sub-pattern treats "manner" as effectively a metaphorical extension of "instrument"
(contrast *"killed him with a knife"* [instrument] vs. *"killed him with kindness"* [manner], both
*with*-marked). **Time/frequency/aspectuality** adverbs scope over the whole proposition rather
than just the verb, realized as single words (*often, seldom, always*), NPs (*next Tuesday, every day*),
PPs (*in two months, at that point*), or subordinate clauses (*"When my brother comes back,..."*).
**Epistemic** adverbs (*certainly, perhaps, maybe, possibly, probably, presumably*) convey the
speaker's attitude toward the truth/certainty of the proposition, and *overlap functionally* with
modal auxiliaries (*may, might, could*) and with perception-cognition-utterance verbs used
parenthetically (*"They say he's back", "I guess she's not in"*) — three distinct grammatical devices
converging on the same semantic function. **Evaluative** adverbs (*hopefully, unfortunately, luckily,
fortunately*) convey the speaker's attitude toward desirability, and likewise overlap with obligative
modals (*should, need, had better*) and with certain PCU-verb constructions (*"I'm afraid...", "I wish
that..."*). A further subclass **intensifies adjectives** (*very, rather, quite, considerably, incredibly,
unbelievably*), and a final subclass of **emphatic adverbs** (*just, really, absolutely, exactly,
technically*) performs a contrastive/emphatic function resistant to precise classification.

### Minor word classes catalogued for later full treatment (p.77–83)

Prepositions: simple, closed-class (listed in Vocabulary above) vs. complex, mostly noun-derived via
possessive syntax (*on top of, in front of*). Inter-clausal connectives split into conjunctions and
subordinators, each internally graded simple/complex/historically-complex (*and* vs. *and so* vs.
*however*; *when* vs. *in spite of* vs. *because*); several subordinators additionally require a
nominalized (NP-structured) complement clause (*"Beginning with John's passing his exams,..."*),
which licenses treating them functionally as prepositions. Pronouns are classified by
person/number/gender/grammatical-role for the definite-referring paradigm, plus a separate
indefinite/non-referring set and a relative/interrogative set keyed to grammatical role (subject,
object, location, time, reason, manner). Determiners (precede the noun within the NP) subsume
articles (definite/indefinite/non-referring), demonstratives (distance × number), and possessor
pronouns (cross-classified with the pronoun paradigm above). Quantifiers connote quantity/extent;
numerals are a more exact quantifier sub-class; ordinals are a special adjective sub-class connoting
serial position, regularly derived from the cardinal with *-th* except for the first three ordinals.
Auxiliaries belong to the TAM system proper (deferred to ch.4). Interjections are the most
heterogeneous and least rule-bound class, spanning epistemic (assent/dissent with a belief),
deontic (assent/dissent with an action), and evaluative (approval/disapproval) functions, and
occupying a bridging position among three only-partially-overlapping domains of verbal behavior:
informative (lexicon+grammar conveying information), interactive (grammar+gesture+intonation
conveying non-informational transaction structure), and socio-personal (communication used for
non-communicative social ends) — a three-way split Givón flags as recurring throughout the rest of
the book.

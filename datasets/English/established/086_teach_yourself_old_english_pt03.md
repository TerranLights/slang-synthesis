## Grammar points

### Hlaford vs. dryhten: a register split between two near-synonyms for "lord"

Both words mean "lord" but diverged in register over time. *Dryhten*, an old poetic word for "the
leader of a company of men," narrowed by the tenth century to mean mainly "the Lord" in a religious
sense, eventually becoming reserved for poetic/archaic style. *Hlaford* (< *hlaf* "loaf" + *weard*
"guardian," i.e. "warder of the loaf," via intermediate *hlafweard*) stayed the ordinary secular
word for a lord, extending to cover both religious and secular senses as Anglo-Norman feudalism
increased, eventually yielding ModE *lord* (southern) and *laird* (Scottish). p.74.

### The strong noun declension (masculine model)

The main ("strong") OE noun class marks case via endings. The strong masculine paradigm: nominative
*se eorl* / plural *þa eorlas*; accusative *þone eorl* / *þa eorlas*; genitive *þæs eorles* / *þara
eorla*; dative *þam eorle* / *þam eorlum*. The basic nominative plural ending for this class is
*-as*. p.75.

### Strong noun plurals: -as, -u/zero (neuter), -a (feminine), -e (minor class)

Strong masculine nouns pluralize with *-as* (*se biscop → þa biscopas*). Strong neuter nouns
pluralize either with *-u* or, for some nouns (parallel to Modern English *sheep*), with no change
at all (*þæt scep → þa scep*). Strong feminine nouns pluralize with *-a* (*seo glof → þa glofa*). A
small set of common nouns takes *-e* instead (*seo dæd → þa dæde* "the deeds," *seo wyrd → þa
wyrde* "the fates"). p.75–76.

### The weak noun declension

Weak nouns end in *-n* in nearly every case except the nominative singular; their characteristic
plural ending is *-an* (*þæt eage → þa eagan* "the eyes," *þæt eare → þa earan* "the ears"). An
irregular noun with its own idiosyncratic plural survives directly into Modern English *foot/feet*.
p.76.

### The relative pronoun þe

*Þe* functions as a connector opening a relative clause ("which"/"who"): *þa gewritu þe se
arcebiscop me fram þam papan brohte*, "the letters which the archbishop brought me from the pope."
As in main clauses introduced by conjunctions, the finite verb of a þe-clause tends to come later in
the sentence than its Modern English equivalent would place it. p.77.

### Expressing "of deeds," "of men": the genitive plural in -a

The genitive plural regularly ends in *-a* (*tyn tida* "ten of hours," i.e. "ten hours"), and when a
strong adjective modifies a genitive-plural noun it too usually takes the strong ending *-ra*
(*on manegra godra manna gewitnysse*, "in the presence of many good men"). As a rule of thumb, an
unexpected *-a* ending in context often signals a genitive plural. p.80–81.

### Recognizing the genitive singular

Genitive singular endings vary by noun class/gender, but the common masculine/neuter strong-noun
ending is *-s* (*on Cristes agen weofod* "on Christ's own altar"). The definite article is a useful
secondary cue: *se/þæt* become *þæs* "of the" in the genitive singular (masc./neut.), while feminine
*seo* becomes *þære*. p.81.

### The dative case: core "to/for" meaning and its idiomatic extensions

The dative's basic meaning is "to" or "for," expressing an indirect object (*he behet hyre þæt land*
"he promised her the land," structurally nominative-verb-dative-accusative). Many prepositions (*æt,
mid, on, to*) govern the dative, effectively "knitting together" a prepositional phrase. Idiomatic
dative usages derived from this core "to/for" sense include *ðam ðe hire leofest wære* "to whoever
was dearest to her" and the genitive-object verb pattern below. p.88–92.

### Verbs governing the genitive instead of an expected accusative

A small class of verbs, including *gyrnan* "to yearn for," takes its object in the genitive case
where Modern English intuition (and many other OE verbs) would expect an accusative: *an þegn...
gyrnde hire* "a thegn yearned for her" (literally "...yearned of her"). p.95.

### Pronoun confusion and the historical replacement of native forms by Norse loans

The third-person pronoun system had genuine functional ambiguities by the Old English period's end:
*hi* could mean "she" or "they" (nominative), and *hire* could mean (singular) "her" or (plural)
"their," depending only on context. As inflectional endings eroded in the 11th–12th centuries, these
ambiguities became a real comprehension problem. Speakers in the north, in regular contact with Old
Norse-speaking Danish settlers (the two languages being close enough cognates for mutual
comprehension), began borrowing the Norse-derived forms *they, their, them* to replace the
ambiguous native ones; the loan pronouns then spread slowly southward — Chaucer (14th c.) still used
native *her* for "their," but the change had reached most of English by Malory's time (15th c.).
p.89.

### Introduction to the conjugation of verbs: two model paradigms

Because Old English verb paradigms sort into 3 weak classes and 7 strong classes, the book
deliberately teaches two representative models in depth rather than all ten at once: the Class II
weak verb *lufian* "to love" and the Class III strong verb *singan* "to sing." Weak verbs mark their
past tense with a consonantal *-d-* (lufode); strong verbs change the stem vowel (sang), with the
past tense itself sometimes showing a *further* internal vowel alternation between singular and
plural (*ic sang* vs. *we sungon*). A verb's "four principal parts" worth memorizing are: 3rd-person
singular present, 1st/3rd singular past, plural past, and past participle. p.104–106.

### Phonetically reduced/altered 3rd-singular present endings in strong verbs

Some strong verbs drop or alter the expected *-ð* ending in the 3rd-singular present for purely
phonetic reasons: *bindan* "bind" → *bint* (not *bindeð*), *findan* "find" → *fint*; *helpan* "help"
→ *hilpð*, *delfan* "dig" → *dilfð*, *meltan* "melt" → *milt*, *gyldan* "pay" → *gylt* — several of
these showing i-mutation of the stem vowel as well as consonant loss. p.106.

### Place-name elements: -ley (leah), -den (denn), sheep-/ship-, -ford, and a landscape-feature set

Several further productive topographical elements are tabulated with modern reflexes: *leah*
("ancient woodland" → "glade, clearing," e.g. Barnsley, Beckley, Bletchley, Cholmondeley, Kinnerley,
Ardley, Alveley, Audley, Farnley) combining with both personal names and natural/agricultural terms
(tree names: ac/æsc/beorc/lind/plume/þorn/wiðig → Oakley/Ashley/Berkeley/Lindley/Plumley/Thornley/
Weethley; crop names: ate/hæp/hieg/hwæte/lin → Oatley/Hadley/Hailey/Wheatley/Linley; landscape
adjectives: brad/efen/lang/(ge)mære/roh/stan → Bradley/Evenley/Langley/Marley/Rowley/Stanley); *denn*
(a Kent/Sussex-specific woodland swine-pasture, at a distance from its parent estate, sometimes
taking an owner's name, e.g. Tenterden < "denn of the people of Thanet"); the *sceap*/*scep*-derived
sheep- or ship- element (Sheppey, Shipley, Shipton, Shifford); and *-ford* "crossing-place" (Oxford <
*Oxnaforda* "ford of the oxen," first recorded c. 900). p.100–101, 106–107, 110–111.

### Charters (boc) and the dual-language land-deed convention

A charter (*boc*, literally "book") was a title deed typically combining a Latin religious preamble
and transaction record with an Old English description of the land's bounds and a witness list —
the Old English portion often matching the present-day shape of the land remarkably closely, since
much of the English landscape's field/boundary/woodland structure was established and named in this
period. p.110.

### Full definite-article declension table

The complete OE definite article paradigm: masculine *se/þone/þæs/þam*, neuter *þæt/þæt/þæs/þam*,
feminine *seo/þa/þære/þære*; plural (all genders) *þa/þa/þara/þæm*. Variant spellings *þone→þane*,
*þam→þan* are also attested. p.112.

### The weak (-an) declension in full, including adjectives

The weak noun-declension ending *-an* applies to every case except the nominative singular (*seo
smiþðe* "the smithy" nom., but *bufan ðære smiþðan* "above the smithy" dat.). Adjectives take the
same weak pattern when preceded by the definite article: nominative masculine *-a* (*se ilca
lencten* "the same spring"), nominative neuter/feminine *-e* (*þæt ilce gear*, *seo greate dic*),
and *-an* in all oblique cases regardless of gender (*on þæne grenan weg* "onto the green way,"
*ðære mæstan wrohte* "of the greatest wrong," *æfter þan grenan wege* "after the green way," *to
þære greatan dic* "to the great ditch"). p.112–113.

### The preposition *on*: accusative for motion, dative for location

*On* governs the accusative when it means "onto" (movement: *on þæne grenan weg* "onto the green
way") but the dative when it means static "on/in" (location in time or space: *on ðam ilcan lenctene*
"in the same spring"). A clean example of OE using case alone, rather than a different preposition,
to distinguish motion from location — the same semantic distinction German marks with its
two-way/wechsel prepositions. p.113.

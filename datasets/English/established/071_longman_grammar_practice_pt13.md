## Grammar points

### 12.1 General information about form

**12.1A Basic forms of the passive.** The active voice's subject does the action; the passive's
subject has the action done to it. Only transitive verbs (those that take an object) can form a
passive — "The plane was arrived" is impossible. The passive is formed with a tense/modal form of *be*
(or *have been*, after a modal) + past participle; an infinitive-passive form (*to be*/*to have been*
+ past participle) also exists, p.182.

**12.1B The passive with progressive forms: 'She is being interviewed'.** Only the present and past
progressive are common in the passive, formed with the progressive of *be* (*am/is/are/was/were
being*) + past participle, p.183.

### 12.2 Uses of the passive

**12.2A Uses of the passive.** The passive isn't just a stylistic variant of the active — it has
genuinely distinct uses, and some common sentences (e.g. "Rome wasn't built in a day") have no natural
active equivalent. Three main motivations: (1) avoiding responsibility/not knowing or wanting to name
who'll handle something ("The matter will be dealt with soon"); (2) focusing on an event rather than
on who/what caused it ("Our roof was damaged in last night's storm"); (3) avoiding a vague subject
like *one*, *someone*, *they* ("The form has to be signed," not "*Someone has to sign the form*";
"English spoken," not "*One speaks English*"), p.184.

**12.2B The use of 'by' + agent (= 'doer') after a passive.** *By* is used only when identifying who
or what is responsible for an event is actually needed ("The window was broken by a slate that fell
off the roof"); it's especially common with verbs like *build, compose, damage, design, destroy,
discover, invent, make, write*, p.184.

**12.2C The passive with verbs of 'saying' and 'believing': 'It is said (that)...'.** When a speaker
isn't sure of the facts (as opposed to stating something as fact directly), caution can be expressed
three ways, each paired with its own characteristic verb list — see the Vocabulary table: *it* +
passive + *that*-clause; *there* + passive + *to be*; or subject (not *it*) + passive + to-infinitive,
p.185.

### 12.3 Form and use of the causative

**12.3A Form of the causative: 'have something done'.** The causative is formed with *have* + noun/
pronoun object + past participle, and combines with any tense or modal ("I am having/had/have had/
will have/must have/can't have a house built"). It's distinct in meaning from the superficially
similar past perfect ("I had built a house" = I did it myself, vs. "I had a house built" = I arranged
for someone else to do it), p.186.

**12.3B The causative compared with the active and passive.** Three related ways to describe a job
being done, each with a distinct focus: the active describes a job done by oneself or by a known
person ("I'm servicing the car. Jack is servicing the car."); the passive describes a job being done
for someone without naming (or caring about) who's doing it ("The car is being serviced"); the
causative stresses that the subject is *causing* someone else to do the job ("I'm having the car
serviced" — critically not the same as "I'm going to cut my hair," which would mean doing it oneself).
The causative is especially common with service-type verbs (see the Vocabulary table), p.186.

**12.3C 'Get' in the causative: 'get something done'.** *Get* can substitute for *have* in the
causative to convey greater urgency: "Get that car repaired!" is a more urgent causative than "Have
that car repaired!," p.187.

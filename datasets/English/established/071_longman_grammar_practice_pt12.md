## Grammar points

### 11.7 Expressing wishes, etc.: 'I wish', 'if only', 'it's (high) time'

**11.7A Present and past reference with 'I wish', 'if only' and 'it's (high) time'.** After these
expressions, English "goes one tense back": the past tense refers to the present ("I wish I had a
better watch!" = now), and the past perfect refers to an earlier past ("If only you had asked me
first!" = then), p.168.

**11.7B Expressing wishes and regrets with 'I wish' and 'if only'.** *I wish* covers things that
might still happen; *if only* is stronger, expressing regret for things that can now never happen
(though *I wish* remains usable too). The simple past of *be* after both is often *was* in everyday
speech but formally *were* in all persons; the past perfect of *be*, or the past/past-perfect of other
verbs, covers things that can never happen, p.168.

**11.7C 'Would' and 'could' after 'I wish' and 'if only'.** After *I*/*we*, only *could* is used
(never *would*): "I wish I could swim." *I wish you (he, she...) would(n't)* functions like an
imperative directed at someone else: "I wish you wouldn't make so much noise!," p.169.

**11.7D 'It's (high) time' and 'it's (about) time'.** These three near-synonymous phrases express
present/future wishes or impatience that something hasn't yet happened: "It's time/high time/about
time she learnt to cook!," p.169.

### 11.8 Expressing preferences: 'would rather' and 'would sooner'

**11.8A Expressing personal preference with 'I'd rather'/'I'd sooner'.** The two phrases work
identically, covering present/future reference (*I'd rather/sooner be a builder*) and past reference
(*I'd rather/sooner have been a builder*). The main verb may be dropped in negative short answers:
"No, I'd rather/sooner not," p.170.

**11.8B Expressing preferences about other people's actions.** Referring to someone else after *I'd
rather*/*I'd sooner* uses the past tense for present/future reference ("I'd rather she were/was
happy"; "I'd rather she didn't sit next to me") and the past perfect for past reference ("I'd rather
you had been present"; "I'd rather you hadn't told me"), contrasting with self-reference, which uses
the bare infinitive, p.170.

**11.8C 'I'd rather he didn't', etc.** Short negative responses generally omit the main verb ("I'd
rather he didn't"), as do short affirmative responses ("I'd rather he did"); the main verb can also be
dropped within a complete sentence when it repeats one already stated, p.171.

### 11.9 'It's advisable...'/'It's necessary...'

**11.9A 'It's advisable' → 'It's necessary': a scale of choice.** A three-rung scale from weakest to
strongest: *should*/*ought to* (advisable, but optional — "ignore it if you want to"); *had better*
(stronger, a warning, with possible consequences for ignoring it); *have to*/*have got to* (less
formal) and *must* (no choice at all — "the strongest advice I can give you"), p.172.

**11.9B 'Must', 'have to' and 'have got to'.** Though often interchangeable, usage preferences exist:
*must* is preferred for self-reference (I/we), for urgency directed at *you*, in public notices, for
rhetorical "can't you stop yourself?" questions, and for pressing invitations/advice; *have
to*/*have got to* is preferred for necessity imposed by an outside authority, p.172.

**11.9C Expressing necessity in other tenses.** *Must* refers only to the present or future; since
it's not a "complete verb," *have to* supplies its other tenses (future: *shall/will have to*; past:
*had to*; present perfect: *have had to*; progressive: *am having to*). *Had to* signals something
unavoidable that *was* done; *should have* signals a failure to do something that should have been
done, p.173.

### 11.10 'It isn't advisable...'/'It isn't necessary...'/'It's forbidden...'

**11.10A 'It isn't advisable' → 'It's forbidden': a scale of choice.** The negative mirror of 11.9A:
*shouldn't*/*oughtn't to* (not advisable, but optional); *had better not* (stronger, a warning);
*can't*/*mustn't* (forbidden, no choice — "it's against the law"), p.174.

**11.10B 'Mustn't', 'needn't', 'don't have to', 'haven't got to'.** *Mustn't* never means the same as
*don't have to*/*haven't got to* despite the affirmative forms (*must*, *have to*, *have got to*)
being broadly interchangeable: *mustn't* = "it's forbidden" (no choice), while *needn't*, *don't have
to*, and *haven't got to* are interchangeable for "it isn't necessary" (choice exists), p.174.

**11.10C 'Needn't have', 'didn't have to', 'didn't need to'.** A real-vs-hypothetical-necessity
distinction: *needn't have (done)* means the action *was* performed but turns out, in retrospect, to
have been unnecessary; *didn't have to*/*didn't need to* mean the speaker knew in advance it wasn't
necessary, and so the action was *not* done, p.175.

**11.10D 'Shouldn't have' and 'oughtn't to have'.** Both criticize one's own or someone else's past
action: "I/You shouldn't have paid/oughtn't to have paid the plumber in advance," p.175.

### 11.11 Modals to express habit: 'used to', 'will' and 'would'

**11.11A The form of 'used to'.** *Used to* exists only as a simple past form — "I am in the habit
of" requires the simple present instead. Questions/negatives use *did*/*didn't* + *use* (not
*used*); *never used to* is the common negative, *used not to* being rare, p.176.

**11.11B Uses of 'used to'.** The simple past and *used to* both describe past habit, but only the
simple past requires an accompanying time reference. *Used to* commonly sets up a present/past
contrast ("but now," "but not any more") and can also describe a past state, not just a habitual
action, p.176.

**11.11C 'Would' in place of 'used to' and in place of the simple past.** In reminiscence, a narrative
typically sets the scene with *used to* and then continues with *would* for subsequent habitual
actions within the same memory, p.177.

**11.11D 'Will/would' to describe 'usual behaviour'.** *Will* (present) and *would* (past) can both
substitute for ordinary habitual-aspect statements to characterize a person's typical behaviour: "he
will often sit in the sun for hours" (= he often sits); "he would often talk about his war
experiences" (= he often talked), p.177.

### 11.12 'Need' and 'dare' as modals and as full verbs

**11.12A 'Need' as a modal and as a full verb.** As a modal (no *to*), *need* is mainly negative ("I
needn't go"; "I needn't have gone"), with affirmative use largely restricted to questions ("Need you
go so soon?") and 'negative adverb' contexts ("I need hardly tell you"). Elsewhere, the full verb
*need to* behaves like any regular verb, p.178.

**11.12B The form of 'dare' as a modal and as a full verb.** As a modal (no *to*), *dare* is mainly
negative, expressing lack of courage ("I daren't tell him the truth"), with affirmative modal use in
questions and 'negative adverb' contexts; *do/does/did* can also form dare-questions/negatives; *dare
to* works as an ordinary full verb. Four interchangeable past-negative forms exist: *daren't say so*,
*daren't have said so*, *didn't dare (to) say so*, *dared not say so*, p.178.

**11.12C Uses of 'dare'.** Four distinct senses: (1) *courage*, affirmative *dare (to)*, relatively
rare; (2) *lack of courage*, negative *don't dare (to)*, the most common use; (3) *challenge*, full
verb only with *to* and an object ("I dare you to jump off that wall"), common in children's
language; (4) *outrage*, modal only without *to* ("How dare you read my private diary!"), p.179.

### 11.13 'Would/wouldn't'; 'that...should'; 'there' + modal

**11.13A 'Would' and 'wouldn't' in place of the simple present tense or 'will' future.** Substituting
*would*/*wouldn't* for the simple present (and sometimes *will*/*won't*) makes a statement sound less
definite or more polite: "That seems to be a good idea" → "That would seem to be a good idea," p.180.

**11.13B 'That...should' after verbs like 'suggest'.** After *ask, propose, recommend, suggest* +
*that*, three equally correct continuations exist: *should* + bare verb, the simple present, or the
mandative "subjunctive" (bare base form, invariant across all persons — rare in the present in
British English; the past "subjunctive" uses *were* in all persons), p.180.

**11.13C 'That...should' after adjectives like 'essential'.** The same three-way choice (*should*,
simple present, subjunctive) follows *that* after adjectives like *essential, urgent, vital*: "It's
urgent that he should send/he sends/he send the information at once," p.181.

**11.13D 'There' + modal auxiliaries.** Just as *there* combines with *be* (*there is, there was,
there has been*), it combines equally productively with modals: "There could be no doubt about it.
There must be a mistake. There can't have been any doubt. There never used to be anyone living next
door," p.181.

## Grammar points

### Chapter framing: productive word-formation and acceptability drift (p. 215)

The chapter's own introduction frames all its errors as a single phenomenon: people productively turn
nouns/adjectives into verbs (its example: "final" → "finalize"), and "standards often evolve over time
to accommodate these changes as the new words fall into widespread usage." Critically, the source adds
that acceptance is slow and unpredictable ("sometimes centuries") and that being linguistically ahead
of the curve risks sounding "unrefined or pretentious" rather than innovative — directly useful framing
for this project's own slang-synthesis purpose, since it names the general mechanism (functional
shift/conversion and suffixation, followed by a slow, uneven acceptance process) that several of this
chapter's specific errors instantiate.

### Errors 136–138: noun-to-verb conversion (p. 216)

Three parallel cases of a noun being converted directly into a verb: "author" → "authored," "critique"
→ "critiqued," "gift" → "gifted." For "critiqued," the source offers a specific semantic motive:
"criticize" (originally neutral) has drifted toward a negative connotation, pushing speakers to reach
for "critique" instead — an explicit semantic-shift-driven word-formation account.

### Error 139: the "-ize" suffix (p. 217)

A productive suffix with inconsistent acceptance: well-established forms like "hospitalize,"
"jeopardize," and "institutionalize" coexist with still-disfavored business-jargon coinages like
"privatize," "finalize," "prioritize," "incentivize," and "theorize." The source states there is no
general rule for predicting which "-ize" forms will eventually be accepted — acceptance is a matter of
how long a given coinage has been in circulation, not a property of the suffix itself.

### Error 140: Enthuse (pp. 217–218)

"Enthuse" is treated as a legitimate, useful word that is nonetheless still relatively new and
contested in only the most formal registers — the source explicitly recommends using it freely in
ordinary speech while avoiding it in the most formal writing to accommodate conservative objectors.

### Error 141: the "-wise" suffix (p. 218)

"-wise" legitimately forms manner/direction words ("clockwise"), but the source objects to its
informal extension to mean "with regard to" (as in "salary-wise," "profit-wise," "time-wise," "height-
wise") — a single suffix with one fully standard function and one still-disfavored one.

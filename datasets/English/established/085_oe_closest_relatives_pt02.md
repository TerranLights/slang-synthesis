## Grammar points

### A brief political history of the Anglo-Saxons (summary only)

Robinson narrates the conventional outline: Bede's account of Angle/Saxon/Jutish origins (with
modern revisions suggesting the "Jutes" may actually trace to the Ripuarian Franks, and heavy
Frisian involvement in the migration given Frisia's intermediate coastal position); the
Vortigern/Hengest-and-Horsa invitation legend; shifting regional overlordship among Kent, East
Anglia, Northumbria (Bernicia + Deira), Mercia, and finally Wessex; the Viking invasions culminating
in Alfred the Great's 878 victory at Edington and 886 retaking of London; and the eventual Norman
Conquest, ending at Hastings on October 14, 1066 (pp.136–143). This narrative substantially overlaps
territory already covered by this project's `072_history_of_english_students_guide` and
`073_english_language_historical_intro` files and is recorded here only as a pointer, per this
chapter's own "Old English Texts" framing which treats the history as scene-setting rather than its
focus.

### Old English literary corpus: a genre survey

OE poetry survives almost entirely in four manuscripts compiled c.1000, predominantly in Late West
Saxon with occasional Anglian/Northumbrian forms betraying a poem's true dialect of composition
(p.143) — an explicit dialectal note the spec flags as high-value. Major genres: heroic epic
(*Beowulf*, plus shorter heroic fragments *Finnsburg* and *Waldere*, and the historical battle poems
*Brunanburh* and *Maldon*); the genealogical/catalog poem *Widsith*; Christian-heroic poetry recasting
saints and Christ in the heroic idiom (*Andreas*, *Juliana*, *Elene*, the *Christ I/II/III* triad,
*The Dream of the Rood*); Old-Testament verse (*Genesis A and B* — *B* derived from an Old Saxon
original, *Exodus*, *Judith*, parts of *Daniel*); the **elegy**, a lyric monologue genre contrasting
past happiness with present loss (*The Ruin*, *The Wanderer*, *The Wife's Lament*, and the
counter-elegy *The Husband's Message*); and minor genres — charms, gnomic/maxim verse, riddles,
instructional dialogues, a rune poem, prayers, psalms, a bestiary, and Alfred's verse *Meters of
Boethius* (pp.143–145). OE prose, unlike the poetry, is easy to date and often attributable to named
authors, falling into roughly three periods: **pre-Alfredian** (almost entirely Latin; the earliest
work is the Romano-British Gildas's *De Excidio Britanniae*, c.540; Bede's early-8th-c. Latin corpus,
especially the *Ecclesiastical History*, dominates); **Alfredian** (Alfred's court-sponsored
vernacular translation program — *Pastoral Care*, Boethius, Augustine, Orosius's *Compendious
History* — alongside independently-produced texts like *Bald's Leechbook* and the start of the
*Anglo-Saxon Chronicle*, a year-by-year "diary" surviving in seven divergent versions sharing a
common early source but diverging substantially later); and **late Old English** (Ælfric, Wulfstan,
Byrhtferth under Edgar's monastic reforms — Ælfric's Late West Saxon prose is explicitly noted as the
*de facto* descriptive standard for "Old English" in modern scholarship) (pp.145–147).

### Old English phonology

**Consonants**: *c*/*k* are interchangeable letters for one phoneme with two realizations — [tʃ] (as
in "church") before/after original (non-umlaut) *i*/*e*, [k] elsewhere (*rīce* 'kingdom' [tʃ];
*cyning* 'king' [k], also spelled *kyning*). *g* has three realizations: the stop [g] only after *n*
or when doubled (*cyning* [ŋg]; *frogga* [gg]); the glide [j] before/after front vowels *i*/*e* (and
in a few lexically fossilized spots where a lost *j*/*i* once followed) (*ofslægen* [j]); a back
fricative [ɣ] elsewhere (*gatu* [ɣ]). The digraph *cg* = [dʒ] (*ecg* 'edge'), and *sc* = [ʃ] (*scip*
'ship'). The fricatives *f*, *s*, *þ*/*ð* (interchangeable spellings in Late OE) are voiced ([v], [z],
[ð]) between voiced sounds and voiceless ([f], [s], [θ]) elsewhere — *hlāford* 'lord' has [v]; *wesan*
'be' has [z]; *brōþur* 'brother' has [ð]. *h* = [h] initially/before vowels, [x] before
consonants/finally (*hēr* [h] vs. *feohtan* [x]). *x* = [xs], not [ks] (pp.154–156).

**Vowels**: the five short/long vowels *i e a o u* pattern as in ch.2's general Germanic sketch;
additionally *æ*/*ǣ* = the vowels of "bat"/"bad"; *y*/*ȳ* = [ü]/[ǖ]; OE shows markedly more diphthongs
than Old Saxon — *io/īo*, *eo/ēo*, (early) *ie/īe* combine their components as expected, but *ea*/*ēa*
are pronounced closer to *æ*/*ǣ* + a schwa-like offglide (pp.156). A numbered list of specific sound
changes follows (pp.157–160):

1. PGmc *ǣ* → OE *ā*, then mostly reverting to *ǣ* (except before *w* and in some texts); short *a*
   similarly fronts to *æ* except before a nasal, or before a single consonant + back vowel (OE *gæt*
   'gate' but *gatu* nom. pl.; *dæg* 'day' but *daga* dat. sg.).
2. Before nasal consonants, *ā*/*a* become *ō*/*o* instead of *ǣ*/*æ* (OE *mon* 'man' vs. Goth.
   *manna*; *mōnað* 'month' vs. Goth. *mēnoþs*).
3. **i-umlaut**: obscured by analogy in most cases, but visible in pairs like *gold*/*gylden*
   'gold'/'golden'.
4. **u-umlaut** (breaking into a diphthong rather than simple vowel-shift: *a*→*ea*, *e*→*eo*, *i*→
   *io*): rare in West Saxon prose — *ealu* 'ale' (< **alu*) is the only consistent *a*→*ea* example;
   *smeoru* 'grease' (< **smeru*) and *mioluc* 'milk' (< **miluk*) are commoner reflexes of the other
   two.
5. **Breaking** (distinct from umlaut, conditioned by a following *h*, or *r*+consonant, or sometimes
   *l*+consonant): *i*→*io*, *e*→*eo*, *æ* (from *a*)→*ea* (OE *eorðe* vs. OS *erða* 'earth'; OE
   *feoht* vs. OS *fehta* 'battle'; OE *wearþ* vs. OS *warð* 'became').
6. Older diphthongs *ai*→*ā*, *au*→*ēa* (OE *stān* vs. Goth. *stains* 'stone'; OE *hātan* vs. Goth.
   *haitan* 'be called').
7. No **sharpening** (unlike Gothic) — OE *twēg(e)a* 'of two', *trēowe* 'true'.
8. The cluster *ngw* loses its *w*: *singan* 'to sing'.
9. **Rhotacism** (*z*→*r*, shared with Old Norse/Old Saxon): OE *lǣran* 'teach' vs. Goth. *laisjan*.
10. Consonant **gemination** before original *j* (after a short vowel) is as widespread as in Old
    Saxon: OE *sellan* vs. Goth. *saljan* 'give'; OE *bittor* vs. Goth. *baitrs* 'bitter'.
11. OE shows the cluster *fl-* where Gothic has *þl-*: OE *flēon* 'flee' vs. Goth. *þliuhan*.
12. Loss of a nasal before any voiceless fricative (*f*, *þ*, *s* — same mechanism ch.1's cognate-set
    discussion already illustrated with *fīf*/*ūs*/*cūð*): OE *fīf*, *sīð*, *ūs*, *cūð* vs. Goth.
    *fimf*, *sinþs*, *unsis*, *kunþs*.
13. **Assibilation**: original stops *k* and *g(g)* became [tʃ]/[dʒ] before/after original short or
    long *i*/*e* and before *j* — a change OE shares, among all the Germanic languages, **only with
    Old Frisian** (OE *cirice* vs. OS *kirika* 'church'; OE *rīce* vs. OS *rîki*; OE *geþicgean* vs.
    OS *thiggian* 'accept, take').
14. **Metathesis** of *r* in consonant+*r*+vowel sequences (*r* and the vowel trade places) — another
    change OE shares, among all the Germanic languages, **only with Old Frisian to any real extent**:
    OE *hors* vs. OS *hros* 'horse'; OE *beornan* vs. OS *brinnan* 'burn'.

### Old English nouns and pronouns

1. OE has completely lost the PGmc nominative-singular masculine *a*-stem/strong-adjective ending
   **-az** (OE *weg* vs. Goth. *wigs* 'way'; OE *gōd* vs. Goth. *gōþs* 'good').
2. The masculine *a*-stem nominative plural is **-as** (OE *fugelas* 'birds' vs. Goth. *fuglōs*, OHG
   *fogala*) — the direct ancestor of Modern English's generalized plural *-s*.
2 (grammaticality beyond text). OE retains the inherited **dual/plural distinction** in 1st/2nd-person
   pronouns (*wit* 'we two' vs. *wē* 'we'; *git* 'you two' vs. *gē* 'you' pl.).
3. 3rd-person pronouns begin with ***h*-** throughout OE (*hē*, *him*, *hēo*, *hiera*, *hit*) —
   contrasted with Gothic's unrelated *is*, *imma*, *si*, *izōs*, *ita*. **This h-initial system is
   itself the ancestor of ModE "he/him/her/hit→it"; the modern *th*-initial plural "they/them/their"
   is a separate, later Old Norse borrowing that eventually displaced OE's own h-initial plural
   forms** (the book does not spell out the later replacement explicitly here, but the h-initial
   baseline it documents is the form that the Norse-derived "they" set later ousted).
4. West Saxon OE generally **syncretizes accusative and dative** in 1st/2nd-person singular pronouns;
   the older distinct forms survive only as a poetic archaism.
5. OE has **no dedicated reflexive pronoun** (unlike Gothic *sik*) — it reuses ordinary personal
   pronoun forms instead, producing genuine structural ambiguity: *hē...hine werede* can mean either
   'he defended himself' or 'he defended him', indistinguishable in form.
6. OE likewise has **no dedicated possessive pronoun** (unlike Gothic *seins*) — it uses the genitive
   of the personal pronoun instead (*his aldormon* 'his nobleman').
7. OE retains the Germanic **intensified demonstrative** (*sē* and *ðes* vs. plain Goth. *sa*; dat.
   pl. *ðǣm*/*ðissum* vs. plain Goth. *þaim*) (pp.160–161).

### Old English verbs

1. OE, like Old Norse, has **lost the reduplicating verb class** entirely as a productive category,
   retaining only relic forms with a new vowel alternation replacing the old reduplication (*hātan*
   'be called' → pret. *hēht*; *lācan* 'play' → pret. *leolc*).
2. The Gothic **class of *-na* verbs is absent** in OE.
3. Like Old Saxon, OE generalizes the ***-e* ending** (from *-i*) in the 2nd-sg. preterite indicative
   of strong verbs, rather than Gothic/Old Norse's *-t*; it also takes the root vowel (sometimes
   umlauted) of the **preterite plural**, not the preterite singular, as the base for this 2sg. form.
4. OE has **no morphological passive or mediopassive** at all, relying entirely on periphrasis (see
   below) — except a single surviving relic: *hātte* 'is called' (1sg. pres. of *hātan*), directly
   corresponding to Gothic's inflectional present passive *haitada*.
5. The 3sg. present indicative of *bēon* 'be' is simply ***is***, with no *-t* ending.
6. OE, like Old Saxon, uses the **same verb ending across all persons of the plural** (*bindað* 'we/
   you/they bind').
7. The verb 'go' has both a long form (*gongan*) and a short form (*gān*), with the short form
   predominant; 'stand' shows only the long form (*stondan*), with no competing short variant.
8. OE shows a clearly inflected **gerund** (dative infinitive) — *tō sāwenne* 'to sow', beside the
   plain infinitive *sāwen* (pp.161–163).

### Selected Topics in Germanic Syntax: word order

Robinson frames this essay explicitly as a sample, not a survey — three deliberately chosen topics,
drawing mainly on the chapter's own two Readings. **Word order**: OE surface word order is mixed.
Some clauses show the subject-verb-object order familiar from Modern English. But very commonly the
verb is preceded not just by the subject but by a **topicalized** object or adverb (*Þā on morgenne
gehīerdun þæt...þegnas* 'Then in the morning the retainers heard that...'), or simply by the
particle *þā* 'then', which functions almost as a dummy verb-second trigger (*Ond þā gebēad hē him
hiera āgenne dōm* 'And then he offered them their own assessment'). In **subordinate clauses**, the
verb is typically clause-final (*gif hīe him þæs rīces ūþon* 'if they granted him the kingdom'), a
pattern that, with a few exceptions, resembles Modern German far more than Modern English —
Robinson's broader point being that **Modern German is considerably more syntactically archaic than
Modern English in this respect**. Strikingly, OE also shows the verb in clause-final or near-final
position even in some **independent** clauses — but almost always in a *non-initial* clause of a
narrative sequence whose first clause has the verb in second position (*ond eall sēo menegu ymbe þā
sǣ wæs on lande* 'and all the multitude was around the sea on the land', following an initial-clause
*ond hē on þā duru ēode...* 'and he went to the door...'). Most scholars now reconstruct Proto-
Germanic (and Indo-European generally) as underlyingly **SOV**, exemplified by the Gallehus-horn
runic inscription *ek hlewagastiR holtijaR horna tawidō* 'I, Hlewagastir, son of Holt, made the
horn'. Two systematic exception classes to strict verb-finality existed even early on: (a) a small
class of short, semantically "light" verbs (especially 'be') could shift to a position right after
the clause's first stressed element (second position) — a tendency reinforced by the growing use of
auxiliaries like 'have' and 'become'; and (b) verbs could move to clause-initial position for
emphasis, in commands, and in questions. All the Germanic languages show a historical drift from SOV
toward SVO, passing through (for OE and Modern German) an intermediate stage where something other
than the subject may occupy the position immediately before a second-position verb — English and
Scandinavian pushed furthest toward consistent SVO; German lagged furthest behind. A leading
hypothesis (citing Greenberg, Hopper, Lehmann, Vennemann) ties the whole shift to the variably-paced
erosion of the case system that had earlier marked subject/object roles independent of word order.
**Correlated typological features** of SOV languages (per Greenberg's word-order universals):
adjectives preceding nouns, genitive attributes preceding nouns, the standard of comparison preceding
the comparative adjective, and postpositions rather than prepositions. OE's Readings show real but
partial SOV residue: **adjective-before-noun** is already the OE rule (as it remains in Modern
English/German, unlike SVO-typical French) — *unryhtum dǣdum* 'unlawful deeds'; **genitive-before-
noun** is still common (*þæs wīfes gebǣrum* 'the woman's outcry'), though the postposed "of"
construction (an SVO-typical pattern) is already competing in Modern English; but **postpositions**
are already nearly extinct in OE, surviving "perhaps only with pronouns" (*him mid* 'with him', *him
from* 'from him') — prepositions are otherwise the rule, unlike Old Norse, which still freely uses
postpositions (*órom hǫllom frá* 'out of our halls'). Comparative-standard-before-adjective order
(an SOV-typical pattern, robust in Old Norse: *sólo fegra* 'fairer than the sun') is essentially
unattested in any other old Germanic language including OE, which instead uses a comparative-particle
construction (*lēofra...þonne hiera hlāford* 'dearer...than their lord') (pp.163–167).

### Selected Topics in Germanic Syntax: the rise of periphrastic verb forms

Proto-Germanic inherited a sharply reduced IE verbal system: only two tenses (present, doing double
duty for future meaning; preterite, doing double duty for simple past *and* pluperfect meaning), no
robust aspectual marking, and (except in Gothic, and even there only in the present tense) no
surviving inflectional passive. Modal auxiliaries (verbs for 'must', 'be necessary', 'want', etc.)
combining with a bare infinitive were an early source of periphrasis, and because of their inherent
futurity-adjacent meaning, several drifted into pure future markers: **OE *willan* 'want/intend' →
ModE "will"; OE *sculan* 'have an obligation to' → ModE "shall"** — both largely losing their original
modal/volitional meaning in the process (German still often uses the bare present with a future
adverb instead, *Ich sehe dich morgen* 'I'll see you tomorrow'; the Scandinavian languages
historically used 'shall', while Old High German instead built a periphrastic future on *werdan*
'become' + infinitive/participle, the ancestor of Modern German's *werden*-future). The periphrastic
**perfect** emerges from a resultative construction: "have" ('possess') + direct object + a past
participle that originally functioned as a predicate adjective describing the object's resulting
state and agreeing with it in gender/case/number — i.e. *hīe hine ofslægenne hæfdon* originally meant
something like **'they possessed him (in a) killed (state)'**, not 'they had killed him'. The
spread of **zero-inflected** participle forms (ambiguous between "real" zero inflection and no
inflection at all) is identified as the likely mechanical trigger that let speakers reanalyze the
whole construction as [auxiliary 'have' + main-verb participle] rather than [main verb 'have' + noun
object + adjective] — the construction is necessarily limited to **transitive** verbs, since it's
exactly those verbs whose preterite participle carries passive/resultative meaning. **Intransitive**
verbs instead built a parallel perfect periphrasis with 'be'/'become' (*him wæs mycel menegu tō
gegaderod* 'a great multitude was gathered to him') — in Old English the 'be'-perfect remained
vigorous throughout the OE period but gradually ceded ground to the 'have'-perfect, surviving into
Modern English only as a fossilized archaism ("Christ is risen"); German retains both to this day
with a different distribution. A **progressive periphrasis** ('be' + present participle) is
especially prominent in Old English among the older Germanic languages (*hīe...wǣron feohtende* 'they
were fighting', lit. 'were fighting-ones') — directly ancestral to Modern English's *-ing* progressive.
A **passive periphrasis** using both 'be' and 'become' existed in OE (*sē swiþe gewundad wæs* 'that
one was badly wounded'; *gebēad wǣre* 'had been offered'); 'be' ultimately won out as the OE/English
passive auxiliary, while German's parallel *werden*/*sīn* ('become'/'be') competition resolved the
opposite way, with *werden* winning (pp.167–171).

### Selected Topics in Germanic Syntax: the rise of clause subordination — "that" as reanalyzed demonstrative

All the older Germanic languages, OE very much included, show abundant **parataxis** (independent
clauses simply juxtaposed, with little or no formal marking of logical relationship) where a modern
reader expects subordination — e.g. a chain like *Ond þā geāscode hē þone cyning...ond hine þǣr berād
ond þone būr ūtan beēode....Ond þā ongeat sē cyning þæt* 'And then he discovered the king...and
overtook him there and surrounded the bower from without....And then the king perceived that',
where **the neuter demonstrative pronoun *þæt* refers back to the entire preceding clause, not to a
nominal object.** Robinson's key reconstruction: the subordinating conjunction **"that"** (OE *þæt*,
identical in form to the neuter demonstrative) most plausibly arose from exactly this kind of
paratactic construction — an original two-clause sequence like *ond him cȳþdon þæt: hiera mǣgas him
mid wǣron* 'and told them that: their relatives were with him' (where *þæt* is a cataphoric
demonstrative pointing forward to the next, still-independent clause) was **reanalyzed** as a single
hypotactic sentence with *þæt* now belonging to, and introducing, the subordinate clause — *ond him
cȳþdon þæt hiera mǣgas him mid wǣron* 'and told them that their relatives were with him'. Evidence
that this reassignment was already complete by the time of the OE texts: sentences like *þā...gehīerdun
þæt þæs cyninges þegnas..., þæt se cyning ofslægen wæs* 'then the king's thanes heard that..., that
the king was slain' show *þæt* unambiguously functioning as a clause-subordinator, not a free-standing
pronoun. The same *þæt*-pattern extends to clauses functioning as objects of prepositions (*oþ þæt
hiene ān swān ofstang* 'until a swineherd stabbed him to death', where *oþ* 'up to, until' + *þæt*
clause parallels ordinary *oþ* + noun-phrase government) — though bare prepositions can also introduce
a subordinate clause with no *þæt* at all (*ær hine þā men onfunden* 'before the men found him';
*oþ hē ofslōg þone aldormon* 'until he killed the nobleman'), leaving open whether this usage derived
from the *þæt*-pattern or arose independently. **Relative clauses** show (at least) three distinct
strategies attested in OE's own Readings, plus a fourth type (not found in OE, very rare even there,
but preserved in Old High German) discussed for comparison: (1) the uninflected relative particle
*þe* alone (*þone aldormon, þe him lengest wunode* 'the nobleman who stayed with him longest') — this
particle-relative strategy is paralleled across the whole Germanic family, just with different
particles (Gothic *ei*, Old Norse *er*); (2) the demonstrative pronoun *sē*/*sēo*/*þæt* used alone as
a (number/case-inflected) relative marker (*ānne æþeling sē wæs Cyneheard hāten* 'a prince who was
called Cyneheard') — one theory derives this directly from a paratactic reanalysis exactly parallel
to the *þæt*-complementizer case above (an original independent demonstrative-headed clause, e.g.
*Hē wolde ādrǣfan ānne æþeling. Sē wæs Cyneheard hāten* 'He wanted to exile a prince. That one was
called Cyneheard', reinterpreted as one sentence with *sē* now relativized); (3) demonstrative + *þe*
combined (*hiera mǣgas...þā þe him from noldon* 'their relatives...who didn't want to leave him') —
a construction common to the whole Germanic family, whose ultimate source is uncertain but plausibly
a fusion of strategies (1) and (2); and (4) the "correlative" type, in which the relativized element
is simply a shared antecedent with **no overt relative marker at all** in the subordinate clause, as
in Otfrid's Old High German *thô liefun sar...thie nân minnotun meist* 'then ran immediately...those
who loved him most' — a type Robinson notes is "well known in Modern English and the Nordic
languages" (i.e. the zero-relative, "the man I saw") but was "almost unknown" this early except in
Old High German, where (counter to the general parataxis→hypotaxis drift) it actually *decreased* in
frequency over time (pp.171–175).

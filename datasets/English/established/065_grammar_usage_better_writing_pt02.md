## Grammar points

### Lesson 28 — The Paragraph (pp. 232–234)

A paragraph is defined as a group of sentences about one topic; a new topic requires starting a new
paragraph, and the first line is indented. On paragraph length (p. 233): there is no fixed sentence
count — a paragraph should be "as long as it needs to be," but AMSCO warns against both extremes: a
page-long paragraph feels forbidding to the reader, while a run of one-sentence paragraphs reads as
disjointed and under-developed (a single one-sentence paragraph can still land dramatically, used
sparingly).

### Lesson 29 — Unity in the Paragraph (pp. 235–238)

A paragraph has unity when every sentence relates to its one main topic and contributes to it; AMSCO
demonstrates the failure mode with a worked example (a biographical paragraph about Lance Armstrong's
cycling career gains one unrelated sentence about a charitable foundation he founded, which the book
flags as breaking unity even though it's true and about the same person — the test is topical fit, not
just subject identity). A second, independent dimension of unity is **sentence-level cohesion**: even
when every sentence is on-topic, a paragraph can still feel choppy if the sentences don't connect to
each other. AMSCO's worked fix combines short related sentences into fewer, more complex ones and adds
explicit transitional words (*first, second, finally, therefore*) to signal the logical relationship
between ideas — demonstrated with a before/after revision of the same seven-sentence paragraph about
class preparation.

### Lesson 30 — The Topic Sentence (pp. 239–241)

A topic sentence states a paragraph's main idea, usually as its first sentence. AMSCO frames its
usefulness symmetrically for reader and writer: for a reader, it previews what the paragraph covers;
for a writer, continually checking new sentences against it is a practical discipline for keeping a
paragraph on-topic. The book is explicit that not all paragraphs have (or need) one — many effective
paragraphs never state a topic sentence outright — but treats writing them deliberately as a useful
training exercise for developing general composition skill.

### Lesson 31 — The Clincher Sentence (pp. 241–243)

A clincher sentence is a paragraph's optional final sentence, restating the topic sentence's idea with
added force drawn from the paragraph's own supporting material — not a bare repetition of the topic
sentence. AMSCO works through four candidate clincher sentences for one model paragraph (about time
wasted standing in lines) and rates two as weak (one simply restates the topic sentence; one merely
converts it to a rhetorical question) against two rated strong (one builds on an implication latent in
the paragraph's content — "better uses for the time wasted" — and one escalates the emotional register
of the final supporting sentence into an exclamation).

### Lesson 32 — Developing a Paragraph with Reasons (pp. 244–248)

A topic sentence can be developed into a full paragraph by stacking supporting reasons. The key
craft rule is **ordering**: open with a reason that's reasonably strong but not the strongest, and
save the single strongest reason for the position immediately before the clincher sentence (or, with
no clincher, as the paragraph's final sentence) — because the position right before the close is "the
all-important place for making the final impression on the reader." AMSCO demonstrates this with two
versions of the same paragraph (about inadequate community bus service) differing only in reason
order, and walks through why the version that buries its strongest reason (frequent breakdowns) in the
middle is measurably weaker than the version that saves it for last.

### Lesson 33 — Developing a Paragraph with Examples (pp. 249–252)

A parallel method to Lesson 32, using concrete examples instead of abstract reasons to support a topic
sentence. The same strongest-last ordering principle applies. AMSCO adds one further observation: a
paragraph built entirely from examples often needs **no** clincher sentence at all, because a
well-chosen final example can land with as much force as an explicit clincher would — demonstrated
with a model paragraph (lack-of-respect-for-the-law, ending on traffic-law violations as the strongest
example) that the book says would gain nothing from an added closing sentence.

### Lesson 34 — Varying Sentence Beginnings (pp. 252–257)

Most English sentences default to subject-first order. AMSCO gives two reasons for deliberately
breaking that default: (1) **emphasis** — fronting a prepositional phrase or adverb foregrounds
whichever idea it names (*In the morning, traffic is usually heavy* emphasizes *time*; *Usually
traffic is heavy in the morning* emphasizes *frequency*); (2) **avoiding monotony** — unbroken
subject-first sentences in sequence read as flat and unengaging. The lesson's worked literary example
is a passage from Thomas Hardy's *Tess of the D'Urbervilles*, annotated sentence-by-sentence for its
mix of subject-first, participle-first (*Turning...*), and prepositional-phrase-first (*By this
time...*) openings; a second passage paraphrasing the Jean Valjean/"Father Madeleine" plot thread of
Victor Hugo's *Les Misérables* is given as a drill text (every sentence subject-first in its base
form, to be rewritten with varied openings). Two cautions close the lesson: don't front a non-subject
element unless it genuinely carries emphasis you want, and don't overuse the technique — AMSCO is
explicit that most English sentences should still open with the subject, "because that is the nature
of English."

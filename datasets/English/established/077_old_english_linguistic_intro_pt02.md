## Grammar points

### The fuþark/fuþorc runic system and its relationship to the Latin alphabet (Ch. 4)

OE was recorded in two writing systems: a Latin-derived alphabet (the one surviving in nearly all
OE texts) and the native Germanic **fuþorc**, a 29/33-letter runic system descended from the
24-letter **Common Germanic fuþark**. The system was originally phonographic and tracked Germanic
sound-changes as they occurred (e.g. the Ingvaeonic Arum inscription shows a new rune for 'o'
derived from a Common Germanic 'a'-rune, reflecting a vowel-inventory shift). Runes had real,
mundane functions (ownership marks, messages on perishable wood, funerary commemoration,
literature) alongside a magical/votive one, and the book explicitly pushes back against an
over-mystified popular reading of them. Runes were eventually superseded by insular Latin script
(developed largely in Ireland, first used in Britain in Christian Northumbria) for practical reasons
— poorly suited to parchment, and tied to a non-Christian Germanic cultural sphere as Christian
Europe became dominant. Three runic letters were carried over into insular script for sounds Latin
lacked: þ (thorn, for /θ/), ƿ (wynn, for /w/), plus ð (eth/edh, an insular modification of <d>) and
æ (æsc/ash, a modification of Latin <æ>) which are not runic borrowings but Latin-script
modifications. Modern editions generally keep þ/ð/æ but replace wynn with <w> (easily confused
with þorn) and add macrons for vowel length — a modern philological convention, not an
Anglo-Saxon scribal practice (pp.37–40).

### The WS consonant and vowel phoneme inventories, contrasted with RP (Ch. 4)

The book gives the reconstructed Early West Saxon consonant inventory as /b, p, θ, t, d, f, s, ʃ,
x, l, r, g, k, j, w, ʍ, tʃ, ʤ, m, n/ against RP's /b, p, θ, ð, t, d, s, z, ʃ, ʒ, h, l, r, g, k, j,
tʃ, ʤ, m, n, ŋ/, and draws out the structural differences point by point: (1) WS had **no
phonemic voiced/voiceless fricative contrast** — voicing was purely allophonic and
positionally conditioned (voiceless word-initially/finally, voiced intervocalically — e.g. hlāf
[f] vs. yfel [v]); this phonemicized only later, in ME, once French loanwords and inflexion-loss
disrupted the conditioning environment (compare PDE house (noun) vs. house (verb)). (2) WS likely
had **no independent /ŋ/ phoneme** — [ŋ] was an allophone of /n/ before /g/ (singan
phonemically /singɑn/, pronounced [siŋgɑn]), parallel to some Northern English varieties today.
(3) WS, unlike most RP today, kept a **/w/ vs /ʍ/ contrast** (wæl/hwæl) and an /x/ phoneme — both
survive in some Scottish varieties. (4) OE used different graphemes for the same sounds PDE
spells differently (<sc>/<c>/<cg>/<g> vs PDE <sh>/<(t)ch>/<dg>/<y>). The monophthong vowel
inventory /i, i:, e, e:, æ, æ:, ɑ, ɑ:, o, o:, u, u:, y, y:/ is a **three-height** system (unlike
many 4-height PDE systems) with a phonemic **length** distinction not carried by quality (unlike
PDE's qualitative short/long split, e.g. pit/peat, which only developed in ME) — and a pair of
**front rounded vowels** /y, y:/, rare in PDE but preserved in some Scottish accents (pp.40–43).

### Stress assignment: root-initial by default, but affix-sensitive (Ch. 4)

Inherited from Proto-Germanic, OE stress defaults to a lexical word's **first (root) syllable**.
But affixed/compounded words follow a distinct rule: a prefix or suffix receives (secondary)
stress **only if it has independent existence as an open-class word** elsewhere in the language
(e.g. æfter- in æftergenga gets stress because æfter is an independent adverb; hād in mægþhād gets
stress because hād is an independent noun), while a prefix/inflexion with no independent existence
(ge-, -an, -e, -es, -um) never does. The book frames this as a general discourse principle:
closed-class/bound material is predictable from syntactic context and doesn't need prominence,
while open-class material (in principle unboundedly variable) does. Stress in OE verse was assigned
on the basis of syllable weight (heavy syllables, or a VCV sequence of two light syllables treated
as equivalent via a process called **resolution**, e.g. nama 'name') (pp.43–45).

### The named sequence of prehistoric/historic OE sound-changes, A–M (Ch. 4)

The book walks through a relative chronology of named sound-changes (labels A–M follow Hamer 1967),
summarized here by mechanism rather than full formal rule-notation:
- **A/B/C** (Germanic-period shifts): u→o and e→i conditioned by a following nasal or a following
  high vowel/glide; eu→iu (a vowel-harmony whose product survives earliest in þīustra 'darkness').
- **D** (West Germanic/Ingvaeonic diphthongal shifts): ai→ā, au→ēa, eu→ēo, iu→īo (e.g. bān 'bone'
  vs. PD German Bein 'leg', showing the older diphthong retained outside English).
- **E — First Fronting** (aka Anglo-Frisian Brightening, shared only with Old Frisian): PGmc open
  back *a* fronted to æ except before a nasal or /w/ — dæg 'day' vs. land 'land'; didn't happen in
  North/East Germanic (Gothic dags, Old Norse dagr).
- **F — Breaking**: a front vowel diphthongizes before certain consonant clusters (h, hC, rC, lC) —
  feohtan 'fight' beside helpan, eahta 'eight', eald 'old'.
- **G — Restoration of a**: the æ produced by First Fronting reverts to a when the next syllable's
  vowel is back — dagas 'days' (beside dæg), explains why First Fronting's output isn't uniform.
- **H — Palatal diphthongisation**: WS/Northumbrian-only; front/mid vowels after palatal g/c/sc
  diphthongize — giefan 'give', scēap 'sheep' — though the book flags this is the same spelling
  pattern disputed in the §4.6 "short diphthongs" controversy (see below).
- **I — I-mutation (i-Umlaut)**: a vowel-harmony fronting/raising back or open vowels when /i/ or
  /j/ followed in the next syllable (often itself later lost) — menn 'men' (cf. PGmc *manniz),
  dēman 'judge' (cf. dōm 'judgement'). The book flags this as critical for understanding OE verb
  morphology generally (developed further in Ch.7).
- **J — Back-mutation (back Umlaut)**: a short front vowel develops a back-vowel glide before a
  following back vowel, restricted in WS to a labial/liquid-consonant environment — heofon
  'heaven' (cf. the underlying front vowel in its root).
- **K — Loss of intervocalic h with compensatory lengthening**: feoh 'cattle' → genitive fēos
  (not *feohes), the vowel lengthening to compensate for the lost consonant.
- **L** — a late WS-internal shift io/īo → eo/ēo.
- **M — Late-OE quantitative changes** (c.700–1100): lengthening before **homorganic** consonant
  clusters (same place of articulation, e.g. /ld/) — cild → late OE cīld; and shortening before
  **non-homorganic** clusters — cēpte → cepte. These are framed as more relevant to ME than OE
  proper.

The book also gives the **relative-chronology reasoning**: First Fronting must precede Breaking
and Restoration (shown by slēan's derivation chain); Palatal Diphthongisation is argued to precede
I-mutation (via the loanword cāseus → cyse 'cheese' derivation chain, Latin ā regularly reflected as
Proto-Germanic ǣ); Back-mutation must follow I-mutation (shown by eowu 'ewe'). **Grimm's Law** (the
First [Germanic] Consonant Shift, named for Jacob Grimm) is the systematic Proto-Indo-European →
Proto-Germanic consonant shift distinguishing Germanic from its IE relatives (cf. fæder/fisc vs.
Latin pater/piscis). **Verner's Law** (Karl Verner) explains apparent exceptions to Grimm's Law:
PIE voiceless plosives that became PGmc voiceless fricatives under Grimm's Law instead became
**voiced** when the PIE accent fell on a following, not a preceding, syllable — a later stress-
shift later obscured the original conditioning environment (worked example: curon 'chose [pl.]'
has medial -r- from earlier *z via rhotacism, vs. infinitive cēosan). Other consonant changes
flagged: **fronting and assibilation** (an Ingvaeonic-period palatal/velar plosive split, cirice
'church'); the **phonemicization of fricative voicing** only completing in ME (relevant to PDE
house(noun)/house(verb)); **gemination** (consonant doubling under syncopation); and **metathesis**
(sound transposition, OE āscian/āxian/ācsian 'ask' — directly connected by the book to the still-
living PDE dialectal form "axed") (pp.46–55).

### The "short diphthongs" problem: a live, unresolved OE philology controversy (Ch. 4)

The book devotes a full section to flagging, not resolving, scholarly disagreement over how to
interpret the spellings `<ea, eo, ie>` when they're NOT reflexes of inherited Germanic diphthongs
but rather outputs of Breaking or Palatal Diphthongisation. The traditional view treats them as
genuine **short ("monomoric") diphthongs** — but the book notes (via David White 2004) that such
short diphthongs are essentially unattested in any living language, which is a real problem for the
traditional reconstruction. An alternative (Daunt 1939, revived by White 2004) treats these
spellings as a **digraph convention marking the quality of the following consonant** rather than an
actual diphthong — supported by cases like secean/secan 'seek' or geong 'young' (which would have
produced PDE *yeng if <eo> really were a diphthong there). But the book argues this can't be the
whole story: pairs like ærn 'house' vs. earn 'eagle' show WS scribes treating <ea> as phonologically
distinct from plain <æ>, which wouldn't make sense if <ea> were purely a consonant-quality marker
with no vowel-quality content of its own. The book's own tentative preferred resolution: treat the
"short diphthongs" as a short vowel + **glide-vowel** (Vv), distinct from both a true long diphthong
and a conditioned spelling convention — while explicitly flagging that this remains genuinely
unsettled, citing Hogg 1992 as the fullest conspectus of the debate (pp.55–57).

### Root, theme, stem: the Proto-Indo-European word-structure model (Ch. 5)

OE lexical morphology is explained via an inherited PIE/Proto-Germanic model: **root** (primary
semantic content) + **theme** (a class-marking element, often a vowel) = **stem**, to which an
**ending** may attach (worked example: PGmc *stainaz 'stone' = root *stain- + theme *-a- + ending
*-z). Themes are largely obscured in OE itself but recoverable by comparison with more
theme-conservative IE languages (Latin manus = root man- + theme -u- + ending -s) and with Gothic,
which the book repeatedly uses as a more archaic comparandum. Nouns with vocalic (vowel) themes are
**strong nouns**, classified by their reconstructed theme vowel: a-nouns (and ja-/wa- subtypes),
ō-nouns (and jō-/uō- subtypes), i-nouns, u-nouns. Nouns with the PGmc consonantal theme -n- are
**weak nouns**; a few relic **t-noun** forms also survive. The same theme-based classification
extends to adjectives (where a-stem and ō-stem adjectives had already merged in pre-Germanic) and
to verbs, where an old PIE e/o thematic alternation (still visible in Classical Greek, e.g. leip-
e-te vs. leip-o-men) underlies the OE **weak verb** class: weak verbs descend from PGmc verbs whose
thematic e/o was preceded by *-j-*, and are further distinguished by forming their preterite with a
**dental suffix** (contrast with strong verbs, covered in Ch.7) (pp.59–61).

### Ablaut (vowel-gradation) vs. Umlaut (mutation): two distinct sources of OE vowel alternation (Ch. 5)

**Ablaut** is an ancient (presumably Proto-Indo-European) root-vowel alternation tracking meaning
or grammatical function, most visible in strong-verb conjugation (rīdan/rād/ridon/riden 'ride') but
not limited to it (bēodan/gebod 'command'/'an order'; PDE hot/heat, bind/bound are cited as the same
inherited phenomenon surviving into PDE). Its origin is obscure, but the oldest layer seems to
involve a front/back vowel contrast with loose, inconsistent associations to nearness/presentness
vs. distance/pastness (beran/geboren parallels PDE bear/bore). **Umlaut (mutation)** is a distinct,
later (pre-OE period) mechanism — a vowel-harmony process where a following vowel's quality
assimilates a preceding stressed vowel; **i-Umlaut** (introduced in Ch.4) is the main type and is
flagged as essential background for OE verb morphology (developed fully in Ch.7) — e.g. cēosan/
cīest 'choose/chooses', fox/fyxe 'fox/vixen' (p.61–62).

### OE word-formation inventory: affixation, compounding, conversion (Ch. 5)

The book catalogs OE's non-borrowing vocabulary-expansion strategies in detail (expanding on the
brief Ch.2/Ch.3 preview):
- **Inflexional-ending-mediated conversion** (lytel→lytlian, wita→witan, dōm→dēman): a stem shifts
  word class via its own inflexional morphology, sometimes with accompanying sound-change.
- **Dedicated non-inflexional derivational suffixes**, organized by output word-class: noun-forming
  -dōm/-nes/-scipe (from adjectives), -end/-ung (from verbs); adjective-forming -cund/-fæst/-ful/
  -ig/-isc/-lēas/-lic/-sum (from nouns); adverb-forming -līce (from adjectives). Some of these same
  affixes can also just modify meaning *without* changing word-class (hlāforddōm 'lordship' stays a
  noun, like hlāford).
- **Semantic-modifying (non-class-changing) prefixes**: for- as an intensifier (forlēosan
  'abandon', foroft 'very often'), and other intensifying prefixes (ā-, be-, in-/un- with directional
  or negating senses, on- for inchoative/'begin' or (paired with un-) reversative senses). The
  prefix **ge-** is given special treatment: it marks perfect aspect on past participles (gebunden),
  converts intransitive verbs to transitive (fēran→gefēran), or marks association/collectiveness on
  nominal/adjectival forms (gefēra 'comrade', gelīc 'similar').
- **Compounding** (two free morphemes joined): N+N (sciprāp, bōccræft), Aj+N (blīþemōd, dōmgeorn
  read as N+Aj for adjectives), Av+N (inngang), Aj+Aj (scīrmǣled), Av+Aj (welwillende).
- Contrast with PDE: PDE relies heavily on **zero conversion** (buy serves as both noun and verb
  with no morphological marking), which OE did not have — OE bycgan (verb) vs. bygen (noun) require
  distinct derivational marking for the same semantic shift (pp.62–64).

### Borrowing in OE, organized by donor language and dateable by sound-change exposure (Ch. 5)

The book's key methodological point: a loanword's **relative age** can often be read off which
prehistoric OE sound-changes it was or wasn't subject to — e.g. strǣt (early, shows the WS reflex
ǣ of Latin ā) vs. pāpa (later, retains ā unchanged); ceaster/cāsere (early, show palatal
diphthongisation / the early-loan ae-reflex) vs. butere (later — shows no i-mutation, which an
earlier loan would be expected to undergo). By donor language: **Celtic** loans are very few and
often scholarly-disputed (dunn's Celtic-vs-shared-PIE-ancestry status is explicitly flagged as
unresolved); Celtic's main lexical impact is onomastic (place-names like Malvern, river names like
Thames/Trent/Avon, sometimes combined tautologically with an OE word of the same meaning once the
Celtic element's original meaning had been forgotten, e.g. Chetwode = Celtic *cēd + OE wudu, both
meaning 'wood'). **Norse** loans are similarly few and restricted to distinctively Norse
concepts/objects (wīcing, ūtlaga). **Latin** loans are much more numerous and split into two
historical layers: early loans tied to Roman-Empire contact and material culture (strǣt, ceaster,
cāsere), and later loans tied to the Christian Church and its institutions/learning (abbod, sealm,
sanct, alter, māgister, gīgant) — with the book explicitly noting that some Latin-via-Church loans
(māgister) may have felt like conspicuous "exotic" vocabulary even to Anglo-Saxon speakers
themselves, evidenced by the fact that the PDE cognate master was independently re-borrowed later
via French rather than continuing OE māgister directly (pp.64–67).

### Semantics: denotation, connotation, polysemy, and the Thesaurus of Old English as a research tool (Ch. 5)

The book introduces the classical **semiotic triangle** (signifier/acoustic-image ↔ signified/
concept ↔ reality) after Aristotle and Saussure/Ullmann, then distinguishes a lexeme's **denotation**
(core conceptual meaning, which can be **polysemous** — multiple related senses, often via
metaphor, e.g. star = celestial body / celebrity) from its **connotation** (associated, often
stylistic, meaning — e.g. commence/begin/kick off denote the same event but differ in formality).
OE shows the same patterns (feng 'embrace'/'captivity'; slīdan 'slide'/'fail, err'; weallan 'well
up'/metaphorical emotional welling), including verse-specific metaphorical compounds called
**kennings** (dægcandel 'day-candle' = sun; hwælweg 'whale-way' = sea). The book's key *methods*
point for reconstructing OE connotation given no native informants: (1) **dictionaries** (Bosworth-
Toller, Clark-Hall, and the more rigorous corpus-based Dictionary of Old English) support
componential analysis of a word's meaning-components via co-occurrence; (2) the **Thesaurus of Old
English (TOE)**, a notional/semantic-field classification (like Roget's for PDE), lets scholars
examine a whole semantic field at once and surfaces the source's *own* explicit register tags
(`rare`, `poetic`, `restricted to glosses`) — demonstrated via the hyge 'mind/courage/pride'
case study (vocabulary table above). The book draws two general conclusions from that case study:
(a) very few of these OE terms have PDE reflexes — most were later displaced by French/Latin
loans, and the few that survive (mood, mind, wit) have shifted meaning; (b) a disproportionate share
of register-restricted (`rare`/`poetic`/`glosses-only`) vocabulary clusters around a handful of
productive compounding stems (mōd-, hyge-), suggesting these registers leaned on compounding as a
stylistic-elevation device rather than drawing in wholly separate roots. A second worked case study,
Carole Biggam's interdisciplinary analysis of OE "blue" vocabulary (using the optical-variable
framework hue/tone/saturation/surface-effect), shows a `technical`-register cluster of dye/textile-
specific terms (wād, hauiblauum, blǣwen) coexisting with hǣwen's gradual broadening from
"pale-blue-only" into OE's general basic color term for blue — a semantic shift ultimately undone
by the Norman Conquest, when Old French bleu displaced hǣwen in most varieties (hǣwen survived only
in some Scots dialects until the late 18th century) (pp.68–72).

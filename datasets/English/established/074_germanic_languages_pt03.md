**Scope note.** This chapter surveys all pidgins and creoles with a Germanic-derived lexicon —
overwhelmingly English-lexifier (33+ Atlantic creoles alone, plus the Pacific group: Tok Pisin,
Bislama, Pijin, Hawai'i Creole English, Australian Kriol, and others), with a short supplementary
section each on Dutch-based creoles (Negerhollands, Berbice/Skepi Creole Dutch, pidginized Afrikaans)
and the single German-based creole (Rabaul Creole German/Unserdeutsch). Per the dispatch brief's
instruction to extract chapters "genuinely about English or about shared Germanic mechanisms English
inherited," this chapter qualifies on both counts: it is predominantly about English-lexifier
varieties, and it is explicitly framed by the author as a study of language-contact mechanisms that
recur across the whole Germanic-lexifier set regardless of which specific Germanic language supplied
the vocabulary — directly relevant "shared mechanism" content for this project's own slang-synthesis
purpose (vocabulary-building and grammatical-simplification mechanisms under contact/restricted-
transmission conditions). See `074_germanic_languages_pt01.md` for Ch.5 (Old/Middle English) and
`pt02.md` for Ch.17 (Modern English).

## Grammar points

### Defining pidgins and creoles; theories of origin (§18.1, pp.566–571)

No linguist-neutral formal definition separates pidgins from creoles; the distinction instead rests on
linguistic, social, and historical criteria together. **Pidgins** are nobody's first language — they
arise in contact situations among speakers with no shared language, for restricted purposes
(canonically trade), and are minimal-lexicon, minimal-morphology, limited-syntax systems. **Creoles**
are generally defined as pidgins that have become nativized (acquired native child speakers and
expanded functionally), though not all linguists require a prior pidgin stage for every creole. The
chapter surveys four competing theory families for creole genesis: (1) the **monogenetic hypothesis**
— all European-lexifier pidgins/creoles descend from a single 15th-century Portuguese-lexifier proto-
pidgin (relic of the medieval *Sabir* lingua franca), with later lexical "relexification" onto other
European bases explaining the Germanic/Romance creoles' surface differences; rejected by most linguists
today, since actual creoles typically converge from more than one genetic stock; (2) **polygenesis** —
independent, parallel origin from shared material and similar sociohistorical conditions, with a
universalist component; (3) **substratum explanations** — structural models borrowed from the
substrate languages (West African languages for the Atlantic creoles; Oceanic languages proposed for
the Pacific group); (4) **universalist explanations**, at two levels: a weaker "foreigner talk"/
contact-simplification-pressure account, and Bickerton's stronger, more controversial **bioprogram
hypothesis** — an innate acquisitional blueprint that children supposedly fall back on absent adequate
input, argued for by appeal to allegedly-inadequate input in pidgin-speaking communities, but untestable
directly since no children are currently acquiring a creole under the original relevant conditions. Most
linguists now favor a converging mix of substratum and universalist explanations rather than any single
source.

### Life cycle and the post-creole continuum (§18.1 cont'd, pp.569–571)

Creolization can occur at any point along a pidgin's development — from a minimally-systematic
**jargon** (great individual variation, used only for narrow purposes) through a **stabilized pidgin**
to an **expanded pidgin** (source's Figure 18.1 diagrams three such routes, with West Indian English
Creole, Torres Straits Creole, and Tok Pisin as respective worked examples) — so pidgins and creoles
overlap structurally rather than forming two cleanly separated types. Decreolization (post-creolization
convergence back toward the superstrate) is not simply the reverse of creolization and not always
superstrate-driven. The resulting range of post-creole varieties is described with the terms
**basilect** (furthest from the superstrate), **mesolect** (intermediate), and **acrolect** (closest to
the superstrate) — illustrated by the source's Figure 18.2, the Guyanese Creole continuum, running from
basilectal *mi gii am* through mesolectal gradations to acrolectal *I gave him*. Crucially, this ordering
is synchronic, not necessarily diachronic — mesolectal and acrolectal varieties may have coexisted with
basilectal ones from the earliest phases of creole formation, so acrolectal forms are not simply "later"
developments. US Black English is cited as a case where the pidgin/creole source can no longer be
reconstructed with confidence, due to its advanced state of decreolization.

### Checklist of Germanic creoles; naming conventions (§18.1 cont'd, pp.571–585)

The chapter catalogues the full set of Germanic-lexifier pidgins/creoles (its own Figures 18.3–18.5 map
35 English-lexifier varieties across the Atlantic and Pacific, 3 Dutch-based, and 1 German-based) and
explicitly excludes borderline cases from discussion: Gastarbeiterdeutsch (German spoken by foreign
workers — some structural pidgin features but disputed status), Russenorsk (a 140-year Russian-Norwegian
trade pidgin, probably never developed beyond the stable-pidgin stage), and — most relevantly for this
project — **Middle English itself**, which some scholars have proposed treating as a creole given its
massive French/Latin borrowing, a proposal the chapter rejects as undermining Middle English's
historical continuity and Germanic identity (directly bearing on `pt01.md`'s own coverage of the
Old-to-Middle-English transition). Naming conventions for pidgins/creoles conventionally combine
location + lexifier language (e.g. "Chinese Pidgin English," "Berbice Creole Dutch"), though these
labels can be misleading when a creole's lexifier affiliation shifts via relexification (Berbice Dutch
was superseded by English in some respects while keeping its original Dutch-based label) or when the
location term is itself ambiguous (does "Hawaiian" in "Hawaiian Creole English" denote geography or
ethnicity?). Speakers' own names for their varieties often diverge from linguists' labels (Tok Pisin
speakers call it *Tok Pisin*, not "Neomelanesian"; Torres Strait Creole English speakers call their
language 'Broken'; Australian Kriol speakers 'Kriol'; Sranan speakers 'Taki-Taki' or 'Sranan Tongo').
**Official status** is rare: only Tok Pisin and Bislama have any official recognition in the Pacific
(Bislama is Vanuatu's constitutionally-named national language, yet paradoxically forbidden in its own
schools); Haitian Creole (French-lexifier, outside this chapter's scope) is the only one given serious
government-planning attention anywhere.

**Atlantic creoles** subdivide geographically into West Africa, Suriname, the Eastern/Western
Caribbean, and North America — sharing a common West African substrate and largely originating from the
17th–18th-century slave trade. The Suriname creoles (Sranan, Saramaccan, Ndjuka) are noted as especially
linguistically conservative and valuable because missionary records allow direct historical tracking,
and because they were cut off from further English influence roughly 300 years ago when the colony
passed to the Dutch. **Pacific creoles** instead arose from 19th-century indentured (not enslaved)
plantation labor, with a documented lexical/structural layer from 18th-century Pacific Northwest fur-
trade contact languages (Chinook Jargon) and whaling-ship contact — the word *kanaka* (see Vocabulary
table) is cited as direct linguistic evidence that sailors spread shared vocabulary across vast,
otherwise-unconnected Pacific contact zones. The terms "South Seas English" and "Beach La Mar" (<
Portuguese *bicho do mar* 'sea creature', via French *biche de mar*) were period covering-terms for the
emerging Pacific contact language, which stabilized and diverged into Tok Pisin, Bislama, and Pijin by
the late 19th/early 20th century (the source's Figure 18.6 diagrams this historical branching in detail).
A small set of grammatical features recur across *both* unrelated Atlantic and Pacific groups and are
argued to reflect universal, not just substrate-specific, development pressures: zero copula, *get/got*
for 'have', and preverbal negation (see the Vocabulary table's *de pikni sik*, *dem get wan uman...*,
and *hongri man no de set...*-type examples cited in the source — not independently tabled here as
full-clause examples, consistent with copyright discipline; paraphrased: Jamaican Creole and Tok Pisin
both allow a bare adjective to stand as predicate with no copula; Guyanese Creole and Hawai'i Creole
English both use a reflex of *get/got* rather than a *have*-verb for possession; Kru Pidgin English and
Australian Kriol both place the negator before the verb). Not every creole shares these — Berbice Creole
Dutch and Bislama are both cited as exceptions that retain (partial) copula-like particles, showing
these are tendencies, not absolute universals.

### Phonology (§18.2, pp.585–589)

Creole phonology is comparatively under-researched, with especially thin data on suprasegmentals.
Creole/pidgin phonological inventories are typically smaller than either their lexifier's or their
substrate's — though not simply "the lowest common denominator," since creoles can have segments absent
from the superstrate (Tok Pisin's pre-nasalized stops, e.g. *ngut* 'gut', *sindaun* 'sit down',
*pundaun* 'to fall down', *krungut* 'crooked' — present when *both* European and Melanesian speakers use
them, distinguishing a stabilized creole feature from mere first-language interference). Phonological
variability within a single creole is real and driven by substrate/superstrate influence, universal
developmental tendencies, and ordinary sociolinguistic factors (education, age, acquisitional history).
A fluent bilingual Tok Pisin/English speaker has the *whole* English phonological inventory available
to draw on, producing a continuum from a shared "core phonology" to a fully "anglicized phonology." A
substrate-driven absence is illustrated by Jamaican Creole and Tok Pisin's non-distinct /p/ vs. /f/
(attributable to the relevant substrate languages lacking the contrast); a markedness-driven universal
tendency is the general avoidance of fricatives/affricates in pidgin inventories cross-linguistically
(fricatives are phonologically more marked than stops); a superstrate-driven absence is Jamaican Creole
and Tok Pisin's general lack of /h/, attributed to variable /h/-dropping already present in the
non-standard English varieties that fed into these creoles. **Original pidginization can obscure
etymology entirely**: Tok Pisin *abus* 'animal(s)' (see Vocabulary table) is so phonologically reduced
from English *animals* that neither English speakers nor Tok Pisin speakers recognize the connection —
evidenced by the fact that English *animal* has since been independently re-borrowed into Tok Pisin
alongside the now-opaque *abus*, producing two synchronically-unrelated-feeling words from one historical
source. **New phonological distinctions can also be actively reintroduced** under increased exposure to
the superstrate — this is "the phonological dimension of decreolization": the source's Table 18.1
quantifies this directly for Tok Pisin /p/ vs. English ⟨p⟩/⟨f⟩, showing younger, urban speakers trending
toward a more English-like three-way correspondence than older rural speakers, including genuine
hypercorrections (*/fig/* for *pig*, motivated by imperfect acquisition rather than inherited pattern).
**Phonotactics**: a cross-creole preference for open (CV) syllable structure, which the source's Table
18.2 shows tracks substrate-language CV-percentage far more closely than superstrate-language CV-
percentage for Negerhollands specifically (77% CV, vs. Dutch's 6% and Ewe's 78%) — though some
etymological consonant clusters, especially syllable-initial, are preserved where the relevant African
substrate language independently permits them too. Vowel epenthesis/paragoge (word-final vowel addition)
is a widely-attested repair strategy maintaining CV shape (Tok Pisin */graun/* from English *ground*;
Negerhollands */filis/* from Dutch *vleis* 'meat'). Interdental fricatives of English are systematically
replaced by stops /t, d/ in English-lexifier creoles, a change also independently found in Hiberno-
English and other English varieties that fed into these creoles — so this specific loss cannot be
attributed to universal creole principles alone, since the same pattern shows up in non-creole contact
varieties too. **Suprasegmental phonology**: Jamaican Creole has lexically contrastive tone in at least
a few minimal pairs (high-level vs. high-falling tone distinguishing e.g. 'hat'/'hurt' or 'bit'/'beat'),
attributed to influence from tonal West African substrate languages (Efik, Ewe, Yoruba, Igbo); West
African Pidgin English has similarly been analyzed as having a two-tone (high/low) system — this
tonality has been used by some scholars as grounds for treating Jamaican Creole as a genuinely separate
language rather than an English dialect. **Orthography**: most pidgins/creoles remain unwritten/
unstandardized; the few exceptions (Tok Pisin) were mainly reduced to writing by missionaries using
European-language-based writing systems, which — by emphasizing superstrate etymological
connections — sometimes unintentionally reinforced perceptions of the creole as a deficient/amusing
version of the lexifier language rather than a full language in its own right.

### Morphology (§18.3, pp.589–592)

Pidginization characteristically strips most bound morphology, many free grammatical morphemes, and
potentially much of the vocabulary. The source identifies a principled two-way split in which
grammatical morphemes survive pidginization's stripping process and which get reconstituted once the
system expands/creolizes: **morphemes typically lost and not reconstituted** — gender agreement, number
agreement, bound verbal morphology, derivational morphology, pronoun case/gender distinctions, most
prepositions; **morphemes typically reconstituted (recruited from elsewhere, usually the lexifier) once
the pidgin expands** — tense/aspect/modality marking, question words, a pluralizer, a full
person/number pronoun paradigm, oblique-case marking forms, a general locative preposition, an irrealis
complementizer, a relativizing particle, reflexives and reciprocals. Loss of grammatical gender (which
only loosely tracks biological sex even in the source languages) causes no real conceptual loss but
produces systematic irregularity where gender used to be lexicalized, e.g. English *sow/boar*,
*cow/bull*, *mare/stallion* collapse in Tok Pisin to *meri*+animal-name vs. *man*+animal-name
(*pik meri* 'sow' literally 'pig woman', *pik man* 'boar' literally 'pig man', similarly *bulmakau
meri/bulmakau man*, *hos meri/hos man*), and Jamaican Creole similarly uses *man hag*/*uman hag*
('boar'/'sow', literally 'man hog'/'woman hog'). Reconstituted morphemes are recruited overwhelmingly
from the superstrate (lexifier) language, with remarkably consistent semantics across unrelated
creoles: English-lexifier creoles' indefinite article is generally derived from the numeral *one*
(*I get one car* 'I have a car', Hawai'i Creole English); the irrealis marker from a verb meaning 'go'
(*I go tel um* 'I'll tell him', Hawai'i Creole English); the completive aspect marker from a verb
meaning 'finish' (*ol go pinis long ples blong ol* 'they returned to their village', Tok Pisin); the
purpose/benefactive complementizer from a reflex of *for* (Miskito Coast Creole English). Question words
are typically bimorphemic compounds (see Vocabulary table's *wisaid, wetin, wanem*), unlike simplex
Standard English *where/what*. Creole word order is overwhelmingly SVO, which — combined with the
observation that question particles, when present at all, are sentence-final rather than
sentence-initial — actually *violates* Greenberg's cross-linguistic implicational universal that
sentence-external question particles should pattern with the broader word-order type (sentence-initial
in SVO/prepositional languages, sentence-final in SOV/postpositional languages); the creole-typical
pattern instead combines SVO order with sentence-final question particles, a genuine typological
mismatch unexplained by that universal. Some marked (non-default) parameter settings are attributable
specifically to substrate influence — most clearly, the inclusive/exclusive first-person-plural pronoun
distinction (*yumi* vs. *mipela*, see Vocabulary table) found in Melanesian Pidgin, tracking a real
distinction in the Melanesian substrate languages and absent from English itself. Pro-drop behavior in
creoles is more equivocal: Pacific Jargon English shows frequent null subjects even though its substrate
(mainly English speakers) was not pro-drop, while Portuguese/Spanish-lexifier creoles are consistently
pro-drop (tracking their pro-drop lexifiers) — suggesting pro-drop status may shift over a creole's
developmental stages rather than being simply inherited or simply universal-default. Bickerton's later
view holds that creoles' defining property isn't really "unmarked" morphosyntax so much as sheer
*paucity* of properties inherited through the stripping process of pidginization — some apparent
"unmarked defaults" may instead follow from general cognitive-processing principles at work during
language acquisition more broadly (attention to word order, phonic/semantic transparency, preference
for clear morpheme segmentability), not from a creole-specific grammatical module.

### Syntax (§18.4, pp.592–597)

**Complex syntax** (subordination, embedding) is typically the *last* structural domain to develop,
arriving only during the stabilization/expansion phase of the pidgin life cycle or during actual
creolization — pidgin grammars are characteristically "shallow," relying on simple juxtaposition rather
than formal subordination marking. Most creoles lack a true passive but do develop relative clauses.
Bickerton's claim that relativization-by-deletion (no overt relative marker at all) was the "original"
strategy for pidgins/creoles is structurally motivated (pidgins have little tolerance for redundancy)
but contested on functional grounds (an unmarked relative clause poses genuine processing difficulty,
creating pressure toward explicit marking over time, especially in subject position — Bickerton's own
Hawai'i Creole English data shows subject-position relative markers appearing roughly twice as often as
object-position ones). When relative clauses *do* get marked, the marker is very often simply the
third-person singular pronoun, or a reflex of *where* (attested independently across West African Pidgin
English, Krio, Nigerian Pidgin English, Torres Strait Broken, Guyanese Creole, and Tok Pisin — a striking
cross-creole convergence). The author's own fieldwork on Tok Pisin documents an observable diachronic
trend toward increasingly explicit relative-clause subject marking over time, with newer strategies
(*husat* 'who' — see Vocabulary table) now beginning to appear in subject position specifically. Across
33 surveyed Anglophone creoles (Hancock 1986), only 7 retain zero relativization as an option at all
(Bahamian, Belizean, Black English, Guyanese, Hawaiian, Liberian English, Providencia); the rest all have
some overt relativizer, most commonly a *wh*-form — evidence (though not conclusive, since an original
zero-relativization stage can't always be reconstructed) for a broad drift toward greater syntactic
explicitness as creoles mature, achieved specifically via adoption of case-invariant (rather than
case-marked) complementizer-type strategies.

**Tense-Mood-Aspect (TMA) marking** is, per the chapter, the single most discussed feature of creole
morphosyntax: 19th-century scholars already noted shared verbal-marker systems across lexically
unrelated creoles. Though these markers are lexically derived from the superstrate, they behave
syntactically and semantically like the preverbal TMA markers found in many substrate languages — a
genuine convergence the source's own Table 18.3 (not reproduced here per copyright discipline — a dense
comparative chart across 9 creoles and 2 African languages) is built to display, comparing São Tomé
Creole Portuguese, Cape Verde Creole Portuguese, Papiamentu, Palenquero, Negerhollands, several
Caribbean English creoles, Gullah, and Yoruba/Bambara side by side across five TMA categories (Unmarked,
Progressive, Habitual, Completive, Irrealis), each split into unmarked ("Anterior") and
aspect-combined forms. Three cross-creole regularities are identified: (1) the bare, unmarked verb form
expresses "whatever time is in focus" from context — permanent/generic states with no overt past-ness
required, illustrated by a sustained passage of Nicaraguan Miskito Coast Creole English describing a
jungle spirit's habitual behavior entirely in unmarked verb forms, switching to unmarked-as-past only
when the narrative frame itself establishes past time; (2) each creole tends to have exactly three core
markers — an anterior-tense marker (simple past for states, past-before-past for events), an irrealis
marker (future/conditional), and a non-punctual-aspect marker (progressive/habitual) — illustrated for
Hawai'i Creole English by *bin*/*go*/*ste* (see Vocabulary table); (3) when more than one TMA particle
co-occurs on a single verb, their relative order is fixed (Tense before Mood before Aspect), illustrated
in conservative creoles like Saramaccan and Sranan, and in Jamaican Creole's *mi en a go sing* 'I was
going to be singing'. The fullest three-term TMA systems tend to occur in the (older) Atlantic creoles
rather than the (younger) Pacific ones — though Tok Pisin has shown significant recent TMA-system
development, including apparent relaxation of an earlier (1960s-70s) constraint against stacking two
preverbal particles. Berbice Dutch is noted as uniquely mixing preverbal particles *and* suffixes for
TMA marking, unlike the otherwise-general creole preference for exclusively preverbal particles.

**Serial verb constructions** — chains of two or more verbs sharing one subject, with no coordinating
conjunction — are a further widely-shared Atlantic (and, more sparsely, Pacific) creole syntactic
feature, used to mark functions (direction, benefaction, dative, instrumental) that other languages
handle via case or prepositions: directional serialization uses a verb like 'go' or 'come' as the second
verb in the chain (Sranan *a waka go a wosu*, literally 'he walk go to house', 'he walked home');
instrumental serialization uses a verb like 'take' before the instrument noun (Ndjuka *a teke nefi koti
a meti*, literally 'he take knife and cut meat', 'he cut the meat with a knife'); benefactive/dative
serialization can chain three or more verbs in sequence in some varieties (the source cites a Nigerian
Pidgin English example chaining 'come', 'carry', and 'give'). Serialization in the Atlantic creoles is
widely attributed to substrate influence from the West African Kwa language group, where directly
parallel serial constructions are attested (the source cites a Twi parallel to a Jamaican Creole
example: both literally pattern as "the hawk catch the chicken eat it" for 'the hawk caught the chicken
and ate it'). A parallel, though more limited and arguably structurally distinct, serialization pattern
has also been proposed for the Pacific creoles, with a suggested Austronesian substrate source (a Manam
example structurally paralleling a Tok Pisin one, both literally "pig they die it" for 'they killed the
pig').

### Lexis (§18.5, pp.597–602)

Two broad cross-creole generalizations open this section: pidgins/creoles tend to share a nautical
lexical element (unsurprising given their typical origin near maritime trade routes), and a small common
core of lexical items recurs across otherwise-unrelated creoles regardless of lexifier (*pikinini*,
*save* — see Vocabulary table), the latter having even re-entered general English usage (*savvy*).
Creoles typically take at least 80% of their vocabulary from the superstrate — Negerhollands is
quantified in detail (84% Dutch, 7.5% English, 2% Iberian, 1.4% Danish, 5% African, based on a 1,300-word
1920s wordlist), with its African-substrate component (*bukra*, *funtji*, etc.) largely shared with
Jamaican Creole despite the two having different superstrates — evidence that substrate contribution can
be a genuinely shared, lexifier-independent layer. Tok Pisin draws an estimated 11–20% of its lexicon
from indigenous languages (plus Malay and German strata), though much of the German-derived layer is
now archaic and being actively replaced by English equivalents (see *rausim/beten/blaistik/winkel/slos*
in the Vocabulary table) — a live relexification process the source frames as not always traceable to a
single clean source (*bel*, *gaden* — ambiguous etymology cases in the Vocabulary table), and which can
produce **fused/opaque compounds** from English multi-word sources, e.g. Tok Pisin *bunara* 'bow and
arrow' and *trausel* 'tortoise shell', both incorporated as single unanalyzable lexical items rather
than compositional phrases — "the residue of faulty analyses," the author notes, "persist[ing] partly
through lack of access to correct target models" (directly analogous to how a sci-fi conlang's
in-universe community might fossilize a garbled borrowing as a single opaque word rather than ever
correcting it back to its source compound).

Bickerton attributes creoles' cross-creole typological similarity to the massive *pruning* of lexical
properties from the target language during pidginization, in contrast to older established languages,
which accumulate complexity through centuries of lexical/morphological addition. The chapter organizes
the consequences of this pruning-driven lexical impoverishment into four named mechanisms — directly
relevant analogues for this project's own slang-synthesis mechanics:

1. **Semantic generality** — a pidgin's small lexicon (estimates range roughly 300–1,500 words; Tok
   Pisin specifically runs 800 words for younger rural speakers up to 2,500 for urban speakers with
   more English exposure) does not imply a correspondingly narrower range of *expressible* meaning,
   since items combine into phrases to cover gaps — but it does mean individual lexical items cover
   wider semantic ground than their lexifier counterparts (Tok Pisin *pisin* 'bird' generalizing from
   English *pigeon*'s specific-species sense — a textbook case of semantic broadening under restricted
   lexical inventory, see Vocabulary table).
2. **Multifunctionality** — a small inventory also means the same lexical item is routinely recruited
   across multiple grammatical categories/functions, violating the one-form/one-meaning principle
   (Tok Pisin *askim*, functioning as both noun and verb with no morphological change — see Vocabulary
   table) — which the author notes calls into question how applicable traditional parts-of-speech
   categories even are to creole grammar.
3. **Circumlocution** — lexical gaps, especially in a pidgin's early stages, get filled by syntactic
   paraphrase rather than true word-coining; the (possibly apocryphal, the source cautions) much-cited
   Melanesian Pidgin circumlocution for 'piano' ("big fellow bokkes, suppose missis he fight him, he cry
   too much," roughly 'the big box that cries loudly when the European woman hits it') illustrates the
   genre, as does a genuine, still-current nonce circumlocution in Bislama for 'burp' (literally 'they
   breathe air in and then throw it out again'). Over time, successful circumlocutions conventionalize
   and then *reduce/shorten* as a lexicon stabilizes and speakers optimize for economy over descriptive
   transparency (Tok Pisin's periphrastic *kot bilong ren* 'coat belonging-to rain' reducing to the
   compact *kotren*/*renkot*; *bel bilong mi hat* 'my stomach is hot' reducing to *mi belhat* 'I am
   angry' — see Vocabulary table).
4. **Lexicalization and iconicity** — there is a direct inverse correlation between a language's lexicon
   size and the transparency/iconicity of its grammar: a small lexicon produces highly motivated,
   compositionally transparent compounds, while a large, long-established lexicon accumulates arbitrary,
   opaque, unrelated lexemes for what is conceptually a single semantic field. The chapter's central
   worked example is Tok Pisin *gras* (see Vocabulary table): where Standard English uses four entirely
   unrelated lexemes (*hair, beard, fur, feather*) for conceptually related "body/animal covering"
   senses, Tok Pisin expresses all of them as transparent compounds built on one root, a pattern that
   independently recurs in several (both Austronesian and non-Austronesian) Papua New Guinea languages
   that share the same 'hair/fur/feather' lexical conflation — a case where substrate pressure reinforces
   a pidgin-internal structural tendency rather than merely coexisting with it. This same economy
   principle extends to grammaticalization: body-part terms (*ai* 'eye', *maus* 'mouth', *as*
   'buttocks/anus') are cross-linguistically common sources for spatial/locative adpositions, and Tok
   Pisin's set (see Vocabulary table's *ai bilong*-/*as bilong*- items) directly instantiates this,
   including abstraction beyond pure spatial sense (*as bilong kros* 'the reason/cause for anger'). The
   chapter closes on an explicit **cost/benefit framing directly transferable to this project's own
   mechanics-analysis work**: full lexicalization of a concept into one dedicated word requires it be
   learned as an arbitrary unit (a cognitive "cost"), while circumlocution/compounding is easier to parse
   compositionally but gets progressively less transparent and more costly itself as it lengthens —
   illustrated by a still-circumlocutory, only partially-transparent Tok Pisin term for 'saw' (the tool):
   *brata bilong tamiak, yu kisim i go subim i kam*, literally 'brother of the tomahawk, which you push
   back and forth' — opaque to anyone unfamiliar with saws as objects, despite being compositionally
   "motivated" in form.

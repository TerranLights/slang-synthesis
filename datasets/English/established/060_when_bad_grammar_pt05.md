## Grammar points

### Adjectives vs. Adverbs: errors 32–35 (pp. 123–126)

Four parallel cases where an adjective is substituted for the adverb form that correctly modifies a
verb, adjective, or other adverb: *bad*/*badly* (error 32), *real*/*really* (error 33), *near*/*nearly*
(error 34), and *good*/*well* (error 35, specifically the colloquial reply "I'm doing good" for "I'm
doing well"). In each case, the diagnostic is what's being modified: a noun/pronoun takes the
adjective, a verb/adjective/adverb takes the adverb.

### Comparatives vs. Superlatives: errors 36–39 (pp. 127–130)

The comparative form (*-er*, or "more"/"less") compares exactly two things; the superlative (*-est*,
or "most"/"least") ranks one thing against a group of more than two. Errors 36–38 (*bigger/biggest*,
*less/least*, *better/best*) are each simple two-vs-many mismatches. Error 39 covers double-marking —
redundantly combining a suffix comparative with "more"/"most" on the same word (*"more smarter"*) —
while noting genuine, unresolved speaker disagreement over whether "more"/"most" may ever be used
instead of (not in addition to) an available "-er"/"-est" form.

### Distance/Number/Quantity Modifiers: errors 40–44 (pp. 131–133)

Five pairs each hinge on a countability or precision distinction: *between* (exactly two) vs. *among*
(three or more); *less* (uncountable mass) vs. *fewer* (countable items); *farther* (measurable
physical distance, conservative preference) vs. *further* (figurative degree/quantity/time — source
explicitly notes most speakers use these interchangeably without consequence); *amount* (uncountable)
vs. *number* (countable); and *as far as* (measurable distance) vs. *so far as* (immeasurable
extent).

### Absolute Modifiers: errors 45–49 (pp. 135–138)

Absolute modifiers (*unique, perfect, infinite*, etc.) denote an all-or-nothing quality and cannot
logically take a degree modifier (*most unique, more perfect, less infinite* — errors 45–47, each a
straightforward logical contradiction). Error 48 (*penultimate* for *ultimate*) is a distinct case: not
an illogical intensification but a genuine meaning confusion — "penultimate" means second-to-last/
second-best, the reverse of the error's intended "beyond the ultimate." Error 49 (*pregnant* with
degree modifiers) is explicitly treated as a harder, contested case: the source itself says this
modifier, unlike the three logical-contradiction cases before it, is "not illogical and conveys useful
information" for less-conservative speakers, since pregnancy genuinely has stages — while conservative
speakers and self-described "Language Police" still object. The source's practical advice is to avoid
modifying "pregnant" only in very formal contexts.

### Imprecise and Made-up Modifiers: errors 50–53 (pp. 139–141)

Error 50 (*hopefully* as a sentence-adverb) and error 51 (*regretfully*, parallel case) are both framed
by the source as usage disputes rather than clear errors — "hopefully" used to express the speaker's
own hope about an entire sentence is defended as grammatically identical to accepted sentence-adverbs
like "frankly," with the "Language Police" label used for objectors. Error 52 (*awful* meaning "bad")
is noted as a near-complete semantic shift from an original "awe-inspiring" sense, with the source
treating objection to it as fringe. Error 53 (*plenty* as an adverb for "very") is the one case
explicitly carved out as acceptable fictional-character voice ("a novelist whose characters don't know
any better") while being nonstandard in ordinary usage.

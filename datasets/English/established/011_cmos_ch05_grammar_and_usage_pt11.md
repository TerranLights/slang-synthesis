## Grammar points

This section covers §§5.221–5.230, the "Bias-Free Language" subsection that closes chapter 5. Unlike
the glossary above, these are genuine usage-guidance points with their own rule logic, not confusable
word pairs — captured as full subsections rather than table rows.

### 5.221 Maintaining credibility

Discussions of bias-free language tend to slide quickly into politics, but Garner frames the issue in
terms of a narrower, non-political goal: maintaining credibility with a wide readership. Biased
language that is not central to a work's meaning distracts readers and makes the work less credible.
Few texts actually warrant a deliberate display of linguistic bias — but the opposite overcorrection is
flagged too: conspicuously calling attention to the *absence* of bias is its own distraction and can
likewise weaken credibility.

### 5.222 Gender bias

Gender-neutral language is presented as a genuine, unresolved tension rather than a solved problem. On
one hand, using the generic masculine pronoun *he* for no one in particular is unacceptable to a great
many reasonable readers. On the other hand, nontraditional gimmicks used to avoid the generic masculine
— *he/she* or *s/he*, for example — or using singular *they*, are each unacceptable to a great many
*other* readers. Credibility is lost with some readers either way; there is no option that satisfies
everyone.

### 5.223 Other biases

The same tension extends to other kinds of bias: slighting allusions or stereotypes based on race,
ethnicity, disability, religion, sexual orientation, or birth/family status. The guiding principle:
careful writers avoid language that reasonable readers would find offensive or distracting — unless
the biased language is itself central to the meaning of the writing (e.g., discussing historical bias
as a subject).

### 5.224 Bias and the editor's responsibility

A careful editor points out to authors any biased terms or approaches in the work (allowing for the
possibility that the bias was unintentional), suggests alternatives, and ensures that any biased
language retained in the final text is retained by deliberate authorial choice, not oversight. Notably,
Chicago's own editors do not work from a fixed blacklist of forbidden words or usages; instead they
apply a consistent chain of reasoning to individual cases, consulting dedicated guides to avoiding bias
in writing. The target style, if the writer wants readers focused on ideas rather than political
subtext, is one that doesn't even hint at the issue — not calling attention to itself as sexist, but
also not calling attention to itself as performing neutrality.

### 5.225 Nine techniques for achieving gender neutrality

No single method resolves every instance of gender bias, and several of the techniques (repeating the
noun; "he or she") become irritating to readers if overused. All nine risk subtly changing a sentence's
intended meaning, so additional rewording beyond the mechanical substitution may be necessary. The nine
techniques, each with the book's own illustrative transformation:

1. **Omit the pronoun.** *"the programmer should update the records when data is transferred to her by
   the head office"* → *"the programmer should update the records when data is transferred by the head
   office."*
2. **Repeat the noun.** *"a writer should be careful not to needlessly antagonize readers, because her
   credibility will suffer"* → *"...because the writer's credibility will suffer."*
3. **Use a plural antecedent.** *"a contestant must conduct himself with dignity at all times"* →
   *"contestants must conduct themselves with dignity at all times."*
4. **Use an article instead of a personal pronoun.** *"a student accused of cheating must actively
   waive his right to have his guidance counselor present"* → *"...must actively waive the right to
   have a guidance counselor present."*
5. **Use the neutral singular pronoun "one."** *"an actor in New York is likely to earn more than he is
   in Paducah"* → *"an actor in New York is likely to earn more than one in Paducah."*
6. **Use the relative pronoun "who"** (works best when it replaces a personal pronoun that follows
   *if*). *"employers presume that if an applicant can't write well, he won't be a good employee"* →
   *"employers presume that an applicant who can't write well won't be a good employee."*
7. **Use the imperative mood.** *"a lifeguard must keep a close watch over children while he is
   monitoring the pool"* → *"keep a close watch over children while monitoring the pool."*
8. **Use "he or she" (sparingly).** *"if a complainant is not satisfied with the board's decision, then
   he can ask for a rehearing"* → *"...then he or she can ask for a rehearing."*
9. **Revise the clause.** *"a person who decides not to admit he lied will be considered honest until
   someone exposes his lie"* → *"a person who denies lying will be considered honest until the lie is
   exposed."*

### 5.226 Sex-specific labels as adjectives

When gender is relevant, it's acceptable to use the noun *woman* as a modifier ("woman judge"). In
recent decades, *woman* has been rapidly replacing *lady* in such constructions. The adjective *female*
is also often used unobjectionably, but in isolated contexts may strike some readers as dismissive or
derogatory (perhaps because it's a biological term used for animals as well as humans). When parallel
references to both sexes are required, the adjectives *male* and *female* are typically the most
serviceable choices ("the police force has 834 male and 635 female officers").

### 5.227 Gender-neutral singular pronouns

The only genuinely gender-neutral third-person singular personal pronoun in English is *it*, which
doesn't refer to humans (with very limited exceptions). Clumsy artifices such as *s/he* and *(wo)man*,
or other artificial genderless pronouns, have been tried for many years without success, and credibility
problems follow writers who use them. Indefinite pronouns such as *anybody* and *someone* don't always
satisfy the need for a gender-neutral alternative, because they are traditionally regarded as singular
antecedents calling for a third-person singular pronoun. Many people substitute the plural *they*/*their*
for singular *he* or *she*; although *they*/*their* have become common in informal usage, this is not
considered acceptable in formal writing — so, absent explicit guidelines to the contrary, don't use them
in a singular sense.

### 5.228 Problematic suffixes

The trend in American English is toward eliminating sex-specific suffixes. Words with feminine suffixes
such as *-ess* and *-ette* are easily replaced with the suffix-free forms, which are increasingly
accepted as applying to both men and women (e.g., *author* and *testator* preferred over *authoress* and
*testatrix*). Compounds with *-man* are more problematic: the word *person* rarely functions well in such
a compound — *chairperson* and *anchorperson* sound more pompous and wooden than the simpler (and
correct) *chair* or *anchor*. Unless a word is already established (such as *salesperson*, which dates
from 1901), don't automatically substitute *-person* for *-man*. English has many alternatives that are
not newly coined, including *police officer* (first recorded 1797), *firefighter* (1903), and *mail
carrier* (1788).

### 5.229 Necessary gender-specific language

It isn't always necessary or desirable to use gender-neutral terms and phrasings. If writing about
something that clearly concerns only one sex (e.g., *women's studies*; *men's golf championship*) or an
inherently single-sex institution (e.g., a sorority; a Masonic lodge), trying to use gender-neutral
language may lead to absurd prose — Garner's own example: "be solicitous of a pregnant friend's comfort;
he or she will need your support," where forcing gender neutrality onto an inherently sex-specific
subject (pregnancy) produces an absurd result.

### 5.230 Avoiding other biased language

Comments that betray a writer's conscious or unconscious biases or ignorance may cause readers to lose
respect for the writer and to interpret the writer's words in ways that were never intended. General
rule: emphasize the person, not the characteristic. A characteristic should preferably be used as an
adjective, not a noun — put the person first, writing *a Catholic man* or *he is Catholic*, and *a
deaf-and-mute child* rather than *the child is deaf and mute*. Avoid irrelevant references to personal
characteristics such as sex, race, ethnicity, disability, age, religion, sexual orientation, or social
standing; such references may affect a reader's perception of the writer or the subject, and may invoke
the reader's own biases, clouding the intended meaning. When it *is* important to mention a
characteristic because it helps the reader form an accurate picture of the person being written about,
use care — Garner contrasts two sentences to show how the same phrase can misfire or land cleanly
depending on context: *"Shirley Chisholm was probably the finest African American woman member of the
House of Representatives that New York has ever had"* (where the phrase *African American woman* may
imply to some readers that Chisholm was a great representative only "for a woman," or only among African
American members of Congress) against *"Shirley Chisholm was the first African American woman to be
elected to Congress and one of New York's all-time best representatives"* (where the purpose of the same
phrase is unlikely to be misunderstood).

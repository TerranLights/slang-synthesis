## Grammar points

### Grammar-category inventory shared by OE and PDE, introduced via direct comparison (Ch. 6)

The book opens by using PDE's own residual inflectional system (she/her/him case distinctions,
book/book's/books/books' noun paradigm, weak/strong/irregular[suppletive] PDE verb classes) to
motivate the categories it will then apply systematically to OE: **case** (nominative, accusative,
genitive, dative — with OE's dative subsuming what some grammars split further into dative,
instrumental, and locative), **number** (singular, plural — OE determiners mark number far more
extensively than PDE's this/that–these/those), **gender** (masculine/feminine/neuter — a category
with "no real equivalent in PDE grammar," not reliably tracking biological sex, e.g. stān 'stone' =
masculine, wīf 'woman' = neuter, giefu 'gift' = feminine; by the end of the OE period pronoun
gender choice was already starting to shift toward natural-gender agreement), **person** (1st/2nd/
3rd), and **agreement/concord** (a modifier takes on the case/number/gender its governing noun
requires). The book frames OE as more **synthetic** than PDE on a cline, not a binary — Gothic is
cited as more synthetic still (retaining a distinct nominative/accusative, which OE had already
lost: Gothic stains/stain vs. OE stān for both), while Latin/Sanskrit/Finnish/Zulu are more
synthetic than either, and Chinese is more analytic than PDE (pp.74–76).

### The OE noun phrase: case/number/gender-driven agreement across phrase types (Ch. 6)

Worked via example sentences, the book shows agreement operating identically across subject noun
phrases (Se gōda wer 'the good man' — determiner+adjective+noun all nominative), object noun/
adjective phrases (þone yflan 'the evil one' — an adjective standing in as headword via
conversion), genitive-modifier phrases (Þæs cyninges 'the king's', subordinated inside a larger
noun phrase), and complement phrases (hālig wīf 'a holy woman'; dysig 'foolish', optionally
modified by swīþe 'very'). The **dative** case covers OE's indirect-object function and most
prepositional-phrase complements (a few prepositions instead govern accusative or genitive); OE
also permits **prepositionless prepositional phrases** — dative case alone standing in for a
missing preposition, a relic of a period when "prepositions" were optionally-present adverb-class
words (compare PDE to, which still shifts between preposition and adverb: *I went to the city* vs.
*I walked to and fro*). OE can also place a preposition clause-finally when the rest of its phrase
is a single pronoun (him tō 'to him') (pp.77–80).

### Numerals as a noun-phrase-internal special category (Ch. 6)

Cardinal numerals 1–3 (ān, twā, þrēo) inflect like adjectives and agree with their noun; cardinals
4+ are generally undeclined but force their noun into the **genitive** (hund scipa 'a hundred
(of) ships' — a partitive-genitive-like construction), though 4–12 *can* decline when not
immediately prenominal (stānas nigene 'nine stones', literally 'stones nine', vs. uninflected
prenominal nigon). fela 'many' and bā 'both' pattern with the numerals rather than ordinary
adjectives. Ordinal numerals decline like weak adjectives and agree with their noun, with one
irregular exception: ōþer 'second/other' always declines strong (pp.80–81).

### The OE verb phrase: agreement, finiteness, tense, mood, aspect, voice, transitivity, negation (Ch. 6)

OE finite verbs show considerably more person/number agreement marking than PDE (ic lufige/hēo
lufaþ/hīe lufiaþ/þū lufodest/wē lufodon — five distinct forms where PDE has essentially two).
**Non-finite** forms are the infinitive and the present/past participles; the book flags past
participles as structurally ambiguous between "verbal adjective" and "adjectival verb" — important
later for analyzing periphrastic constructions. OE has only two morphological **tenses**, present
and preterite; future time is normally just expressed via the present tense plus context (On
morgenne, gā ic tō þǣm dūnum 'in the morning I shall go to the hills'), though willan/sculan +
infinitive can express futurity — but the book stresses these verbs are **more prototypically
volition/obligation markers** than tense auxiliaries (ic wille gān = 'I want to go', not 'I will
go'; hēo sceal gān = 'she must go', not 'she'll go') — i.e. OE willan/sculan had not yet
grammaticalized into pure future-tense auxiliaries the way PDE will/shall did. **Mood** (indicative/
subjunctive/imperative) is expressed inflectionally in OE (hē lufaþ indic. vs. hē lufige subj.) where
PDE mostly relies on complex verb phrases (he might eat), with only relic subjunctive survivals
(God save the Queen; If I were you). **Aspect** and **voice** are expressed periphrastically: (1)
wesan 'be' + present participle for progressive aspect (ic eom singende 'I am singing') — though
the book notes OE used this construction far less than PDE uses progressive forms, often preferring
a simple verb + adverb instead (oft ic singe 'I sing often' rather than a progressive form); (2)
habban/wesan/weorþan + past participle for perfect aspect and passive voice — with a real
**transitivity-conditioned split**: habban + past participle for transitive perfects (the participle
then agreeing with the direct object), wesan/weorþan + past participle for intransitive perfects
(the participle then agreeing with the subject) — e.g. hīe sind gecumene 'they have come' (lit.
'they are come'). weorþan + past participle also expresses passive voice (hē wearþ geslægen 'he was
struck', lit. 'he became struck'), but OE often avoided this altogether in favor of the impersonal
pronoun man 'one' (man Horsan ofslōg 'Horsa was slain', lit. 'one slew Horsa'). **Negation** is
adverbial (ne, frequently cliticized: nis = ne+is), and — unlike in PDE, where it's stigmatized —
**double/multiple negation was fully grammatical** in OE, and more common in OE prose than in OE
verse (pp.82–85).

### Element order: three named patterns (SP, S…P, PS), each with a characteristic clause-type (Ch. 6)

OE element order is genuinely more flexible than PDE's (inflection carries the subject/object
distinction that PDE needs order for), but the book is explicit that this flexibility "should not
be exaggerated" — real OE prose shows strong statistical tendencies, departed from for stylistic
effect, and sometimes *necessary* precisely because not every noun phrase is inflectionally
disambiguated (þæt wīf bindeþ hit 'the woman binds it' is genuinely ambiguous about which NP is
subject, since nominative and accusative þæt wīf are identical in form). Three patterns: **SP**
(subject immediately before predicator) is the default for **main clauses**; **S…P** (other
material intervening between subject and verb, verb pushed toward clause-final position) is
typical of **subordinate clauses**; **PS** (predicator before subject) is typical of
**questions**, and also of main clauses opening with certain adverbials (þā 'then', þǣr 'there',
þider 'thither') — and is independently used to foreground new information or shift emphasis.
Complex (auxiliary+lexical-verb) predicators can themselves split around intervening material in
either clause type, with the lexical verb pushed clause-final (pp.85–86).

### OE subordinate-clause taxonomy: adverb, noun, relative, comparative, and correlative constructions (Ch. 6)

Following the PDE taxonomy set up for comparison, OE forms the same four subordinate-clause types.
**Subordinating conjunctions** are catalogued with an explicit mood-government note — some require
subjunctive mood in their clause (marked `+` in the book's own list): forþon/forþǣm/forþǣm þe
'because', oþ/oþ þæt 'until', `+`gif 'if', þā/þā þā 'when', `+`þæt '(so) that', `+`þēah/þēah þe
'although', `+`ǣr/ǣr þan þe 'before', æfter/æfter þan/þǣm þe 'after', `+`þy lǣs/þy lǣs þe 'lest' —
with **indicative** used when the subordinate event is complete/certain (æfter 'after' clauses) and
**subjunctive** when it's hypothetical/not-yet-happened (ǣr 'before' clauses) — i.e. mood choice in
the subordinate clause tracks the *semantics* of the governing conjunction, not an arbitrary
lexical quirk. A distinctive **accusative-and-infinitive** construction appears after
saying/thinking verbs (Hē hēt þone wer hine bindan 'he commanded the man to bind him', with the
embedded subject in the accusative and its verb in the infinitive — directly comparable to PDE
*I ordered him to leave*). **Correlative constructions** link two clauses with matched particles
(þā…þā…, þonne…þonne…); the book flags that þā…þā… is ambiguous between 'when…then…' and
discourse-cohesive repetition, disambiguated by element order within each clause (verb-final =
'when'/subordinating; verb-initial = 'then'/adverbial) — with the redundant-sounding PDE
translation ('when she saw the man, then she loved him') tentatively explained as an oral-discourse
cohesion device rather than a translation artifact. **Relative clauses** most often use the
indeclinable particle þe alone, but can add a case-inflected determiner (declined for the relative
clause's own internal function) either alongside þe or in its place, or drop any relative marker
entirely — giving OE relative-clause marking a genuinely gradient, not binary, set of options
(se wer þe…/se wer se þe…/se wer…, all attested for the same semantic relation) (pp.87–90).

### Discourse-cohesion devices distinctive of OE prose style (Ch. 6)

Three features the book flags as **stylistically salient to a PDE reader but grammatical and
common in OE**, tentatively explained (following Mitchell/Mitchell-Robinson) as arising from a a
scribal/compositional "insecurity in the face of the complicated sentence" rather than any
grammatical deficiency: (1) **recapitulation and anticipation** — a fronted noun phrase is restated
by a resumptive pronoun later in the same clause (Ure ieldran, þā þe þās stōwa þǣr hēoldon, hīe
lufodon wīsdōm 'Our forefathers, those who formerly held these places, they loved wisdom' — note
the doubled subject); (2) **splitting of heavy (coordinated) groups** — rather than coordinating two
full subjects/modifiers up front (PDE *Tom and Dick were walking*), OE states one element fully and
appends the second afterward (Hē fērde mid twǣm cnapum tō þǣm dūnum, and Isaāc samod, literally
'He travelled...to the hills, and Isaac as well'); (3) **parataxis** (simple clauses juxtaposed
rather than subordinated — syndetic, with conjunctions, vs. asyndetic, without) is far more common
in OE than in formal PDE prose. The book explicitly pushes back on treating parataxis as
"primitive," noting it appears even in sophisticated authors like Ælfric, and reframes it as a
genre/author-audience-relationship choice: parataxis shifts interpretive responsibility for
cause-and-effect reasoning onto the listener/reader, where hypotaxis (subordination) does that work
for them. A representative Anglo-Saxon Chronicle passage illustrates heavily syndetic parataxis
chaining five clauses with and (pp.90–93).

### Five OE noun declensions, organized strong vs. weak, with a sixth "minor" cluster (Ch. 7)

OE nouns fall into three **strong** declensions by grammatical gender (general masculine, general
feminine, general neuter — all tracing historically to vocalic Proto-Germanic themes), one **weak**
declension (the **-an declension**, tracing to the consonantal -n- theme, covering all three
genders — masc. nama-type, fem. heorte-type, and only two neuters total: ēage 'eye', ēare 'ear'),
and a cluster of **minor declensions** classified not by theme but by their plural-formation
strategy: **-a plurals** (u-stem nouns like sunu 'son'), **uninflected plurals** (same form for
singular and plural — mainly kinship terms: brōþor, dohtor, mōdor, fæder, sweostor, each with its
own small irregularities), and **mutation plurals** (plural formed by root-vowel Umlaut rather than
a suffix at all — fōt/fēt, bōc/bēc, mann/menn, tōþ/tēþ, gāt/gǣt, gōs/gēs, mūs/mys, plus the -nd pair
frēond/frīend and fēond/fīend — the direct ancestor of PDE foot/feet, tooth/teeth, man/men,
mouse/mice, goose/geese). A handful of **outright irregular** nouns round out the system: lamb/
cealf/ǣg/cild acquire an intrusive -r- in the plural (lambru, cildru), and mōnaþ 'month' is a lone
relic of the Proto-Indo-European consonantal t-declension. Within the general masculine declension
specifically, scholars further subdivide by reconstructed Proto-Germanic theme (a-, ja-, wa-,
i-nouns) — a distinction that mostly doesn't affect the actual OE endings but explains surface
irregularities (e.g. ja-noun here 'army' retains an inflectional -g- lost in the nominative; wa-noun
bearu 'grove' retains a -w- the same way). The book repeatedly uses **Gothic** as a comparandum to
show how much inflectional distinctiveness (**syncretism** = its loss) OE had already undergone —
most strikingly, Gothic alone among these still keeps a formally distinct nominative vs. accusative
(stains vs. stain) that OE's stān covers with one form for both (pp.94–101).

**Full model paradigms** (nominative/accusative, genitive, dative given per number; strong
declensions mark Ø plural inflection patterns as noted in prose above when they diverge from the
model):
- *General masculine* (stān 'stone'): sg. stān/stānes/stāne; pl. stānas/stāna/stānum.
- *General feminine* (lār 'teaching'): sg. lār (nom.) / lāre (acc.,gen.,dat.); pl. lāra~lāre (nom.,
  acc.) / lāra~lārena (gen.) / lārum (dat.).
- *General neuter* (scip 'ship'): sg. scip/scipes/scipe; pl. scipu/scipa/scipum.
- *-an/weak* (nama 'name'): sg. nama (nom.) / naman (acc.,gen.,dat.); pl. naman (nom.,acc.) /
  namena (gen.) / namum (dat.).
- *-a plurals* (sunu 'son'): sg. sunu (nom.,acc.) / suna (gen.,dat.); pl. suna (nom.,acc.,gen.) /
  sunum (dat.).
- *Uninflected* (dohtor 'daughter'): sg. dohtor (nom.,acc.,gen.) / dehter (dat.); pl. dohtor
  (nom.,acc.) / dohtra (gen.) / dohtrum (dat.).
- *Mutation* (fōt 'foot'): sg. fōt (nom.,acc.) / fōtes (gen.) / fēt (dat.); pl. fēt (nom.,acc.) /
  fōta (gen.) / fōtum (dat.). (bōc 'book' follows the parallel feminine pattern: sg. bōc/bēc~bōce/
  bēc~bōc; pl. bēc/bōca/bōcum.)

### Pronoun and determiner paradigms, and the fuzzy pronoun/determiner boundary (Ch. 7)

OE personal pronouns inflect for number, case, and (3rd person singular only) gender, plus —
unlike PDE — a dedicated **dual** number (1st/2nd person only: 'we two', 'us two', 'you two'; dual
forms survived into early ME before dying out). Full paradigms given: 1st person sg. ic/mē/mīn/mē,
pl. wē/ūs/ūre/ūs, dual wit/unc/uncer/unc; 2nd person sg. þū/þē/þīn/þē, pl. gē/ēow/ēower/ēow, dual
git/inc/incer/inc; 3rd person masc. sg. hē/hine/his/him, fem. sg. hēo/hīe/hiere/hiere, neut. sg.
hit/hit/his/him, pl. (all genders) hīe/hīe/hiera/him. The genitive forms mīn/þīn/ūre/ēower can
themselves decline like strong adjectives when used attributively (a further instance of the
pronoun/determiner-category fuzziness the book flags via Roger Lass's point that Proto-Germanic
never inherited one single coherent pronoun/determiner system — what handbooks call "pronouns" vs.
"determiners" is a dialect-specific carving-up of one shared pool of forms). OE lacks dedicated
relative pronouns (the indeclinable particle þe fills that role — see Ch.6); it does have
interrogative/indefinite hwā 'who' (with a distinct **instrumental** case, hwy, used for manner/
means, plus an alternative instrumental hwon restricted to the fixed phrase for hwon 'why?') and
hwelc 'which' (declines like a strong adjective); the indefinite man 'one' is routinely used where
PDE would reach for a passive construction. OE has **no dedicated definite or indefinite article**:
sum 'a certain' and ān 'one' cover some indefinite-article functions, while the demonstrative se
(declined se/sēo/þæt by gender, full paradigm given) covers both 'that' and PDE-definite-article
'the' uses — contrasted with the more emphatic þes-demonstrative ('this'), with the book drawing an
explicit sociolinguistic parallel to the PDE non-standard them/them there distinction (them boys
vs. them there boys) (pp.101–105).

**Full demonstrative paradigms:**
- *se-type* ('that'/'the'): masc. se/þone/þæs/þǣm; fem. sēo/þā/þǣre/þǣre; neut. þæt/þæt/þæs/þǣm;
  pl. (all genders) þā/þā/þāra/þǣm.
- *þes-type* ('this'): masc. þes/þisne/þisses/þissum; fem. þēos/þās/þisse/þisse; neut. þis/þis/
  þisses/þissum; pl. (all genders) þās/þās/þissa/þissum.

### Adjective, adverb, and numeral inflection (Ch. 7)

OE adjectives (originally closely linked to nouns in PIE, distinguished functionally rather than
formally) have, as in other Germanic languages, two full paradigms: **weak** (used after a
determiner) and **strong** (used elsewhere) — strong-adjective endings are historically borrowed
from determiner paradigms rather than noun paradigms, which is why they don't simply mirror strong
nouns. Full model (gōd 'good'): weak masc. sg. gōda/gōdan/gōdan/gōdan; weak fem. sg. gōde/gōdan/
gōdan/gōdan; weak neut. sg. gōde/gōde/gōdan/gōdan; weak pl. (all genders) gōdan/gōdan/gōdra~gōdena/
gōdum. Strong masc. sg. gōd/gōdne/gōdes/gōdum; strong fem. sg. gōd/gōde/gōdre/gōdre; strong neut.
sg. gōd/gōd/gōdes/gōdum; strong pl. masc./fem. gōde/gōde/gōdra/gōdum, neut. gōd/gōd/gōdra/gōdum. A
sub-pattern (cwic 'alive'-type) differs only in the strong nom. sg./pl. forms. **Comparison**: three
degrees (absolute/comparative/superlative) via suffixes -ra/-ost (lēof/lēofra/lēofost 'dear');
comparatives always decline weak regardless of a preceding determiner, superlatives follow the same
weak/strong choice rule as ordinary adjectives. Two irregular-comparison groups: (a)
i-mutation-affected (eald/ieldra/ieldest 'old'; hēah/hīerra/hīehst 'high'), and (b) **suppletive**
comparison, where comparative/superlative come from a different root entirely (gōd/betera~betra~
sēlra/betst~sēlest 'good'; yfel/wiersa/wierrest 'evil'; micel/māra/mǣst 'big'; lytel/lǣssa/lǣst
'little' — directly paralleling PDE good/better/best, bad/worse/worst). **Adverbs** derived from
adjectives mostly via -e (hearde 'harshly' from heard) or, extended by analogy from -lic-adjectives,
-līce (cræftlīce 'skilfully', heardlīce as an alternative to hearde); adjectival adverbs are
indeclinable but still take comparison, via -or/-ost (heardor/heardost); some non-adjectival adverbs
also compare (oft/oftor 'more often'; ǣr/ǣrest 'earliest'). **Numerals**: cardinal ān declines like
an adjective (weak forms specifically mean 'alone'); twā and þrēo have their own small paradigms
(twā: masc. twēgen/twēgra~twēga/twǣm, fem./neut. twā~tū parallel; þrēo: masc. þrīe/þrēora/þrim,
fem./neut. þrēo/þrēora/þrim); 4+ are generally undeclined (see Ch.6 above); ordinals decline weak
except ōþer 'second', always strong (pp.105–109).

### The full OE verb system: strong Classes I–VII, weak Classes I–III, irregulars (Ch. 7)

OE verbs are strong (Ablaut-based preterite), weak (dental-suffix preterite, the suffix most likely
historically a fusion with the verb dōn 'do'), or irregular. All verb types share the same basic
agreement machinery (full present/preterite indicative and subjunctive, imperative, and present/
past participle paradigms given for the four model verbs bindan/lufian/fremman/wesan~bēon above) —
notably, OE's present-indicative plural ending generalizes the old Proto-Germanic 3rd-person-plural
ending across all persons, and likewise for the preterite-indicative plural, which the book
explicitly connects to the *direction* of ongoing paradigm-leveling already visible by comparing to
Gothic's still-fuller paradigm (distinct 1st/2nd/3rd singular, plus genuine **dual** verb forms:
bindōs/bindats 'we two bind'/'you two bind' — note dual exists for pronouns but not for OE verbs,
only Gothic retains verbal dual).

**Strong verbs (Classes I–VII)**, each defined by its characteristic Ablaut pattern across the
**principal parts** (infinitive / 3sg. present indicative / 1sg. preterite indicative / preterite
plural / past participle):
- **Class I** (ī + single consonant): scīnan/scīnþ/scān/scinon/(ge)scinen 'shine'; also bītan,
  drīfan, (ā)rīsan, stīgan, rīdan, (ge)wītan. Variant snīþan/snīþ/snāþ/snidon/sniden 'cut' shows
  Verner's Law voicing (-þ-→-d-) in the preterite plural/past participle.
- **Class II** (characteristic eo/ū root): crēopan/crīepþ/crēap/crupon/(ge)cropen 'creep'; cēosan/
  cīest/cēas/curon/(ge)coren 'choose' shows Verner's-Law **rhotacism** (-s-→-r-); sēoþan/sīeþ/sēaþ/
  sudon/(ge)soden 'boil' shows Verner's Law -þ-→-d-.
- **Class III** (root consonant clusters, heavily reshaped by breaking/nasal-influence): bregdan
  'pull' (original -e- + 2 consonants pattern); feohtan/weorpan (breaking before h/r); helpan
  (breaking blocked before l+consonant other than h); gieldan (palatal diphthongisation); bindan
  itself (nasal-conditioned i/u-raising, English/Frisian-specific a-retention).
- **Class IV**: beran/bi(e)rþ/bær/bǣron/(ge)boren 'bear'; also brecan, stelan, teran. niman 'take'
  and cuman 'come' are irregular members of this class, both showing nasal-conditioned and
  analogical reshaping (cuman's infinitive -u- is an analogical extension of the past participle's
  vowel into the present stem, paralleled in some but not all other Germanic cognates).
- **Class V**: tredan/tritt/træd/trǣdon/(ge)treden 'tread'; also etan, sprecan, wrecan; sittan,
  licgan, biddan have present tenses reshaped toward weak-verb patterns; giefan shows palatal
  diphthongisation; sēon 'see' is a contracted form (*sehan > *seohan [breaking] > sēon [h-loss +
  compensatory lengthening]).
- **Class VI**: faran/færþ/fōr/fōron/(ge)faren 'go'; also scacan, sacan; standan drops -n- in the
  preterite (stōd); hebban, swerian have weak presents but otherwise follow this class; scieppan
  'create' shows gemination (consonant-doubling before lost *-j-, a West Germanic-wide pattern);
  slēan 'strike' is contracted (*slahan > *slæhan [first fronting] > *sleahan [breaking] > slēan
  [h-loss]) and shows Verner's Law voicing (slōg/slōgon/slagen vs. sliehþ).
- **Class VII** ("reduplicating class" — same root-vowel across 3sg. preterite and preterite plural,
  and across infinitive and past participle, with wēpan 'weep' an only-apparent exception via a
  weak present): healdan/hielt/hēold/hēoldon/(ge)healden 'hold'; feallan, cnāwan, slǣpan, hātan
  similarly. The class name references **reduplication** (root-repetition marking past tense,
  common in Sanskrit/Greek and surviving robustly in Gothic haihald-type forms) — OE retains only
  sporadic relics (heht 'called' beside hēt). Contracted members: fōn/fēhþ/fēng/fēngon/(ge)fangen
  'seize' (and hōn 'hang'), showing Verner's-Law-conditioned nasal retention/loss.

**Weak verbs (Classes I–III)**: **Class I** (infinitive originally thematic -i-/-j-, causing
present-tense mutation, and West-Germanic gemination of a preceding consonant when the root
syllable was short and the consonant wasn't -r- — fremman from *framjan, settan from *satjan; long
root syllables block gemination, giving sēcan 'seek' with mutated ē but unmutated preterite sōhte).
**Class II** (lufian-type; thematic -ō- blocked mutation, so back root vowels are normal: lufian,
bodian, lofian). **Class III** is a tiny closed set (habban 'have', libban 'live', secgan 'say',
hycgan 'think') whose stressed-vowel variation can mislead a beginner into mistaking them for
strong verbs; principal parts: habban/hæfþ/hæfde/hæfdon/(ge)hæfd; libban/leofaþ~lifde (etc.);
secgan/sægþ/sægde/sægdon/(ge)sægd; hycgan/hogaþ/hog(o)de/hog(o)don/(ge)hogod.

**Irregular verbs** split into **preterite-present verbs** (present tense is a fossilized old
strong preterite; new preterite built on the weak dental-suffix model) — witan 'know' (Class I),
āgan 'own' (Class I), cunnan 'know' (Class III), magan 'be able' (traditionally Class VI, but the
book flags real classification problems), sculan 'have to' (Class IV), mōtan 'be allowed' (Class
VI), þurfan 'need' (Class III) — and **anomalous verbs**: willan/nyllan 'want/not want',
dōn 'do', gān 'go', plus wesan/bēon 'be' (full paradigm above). The book explicitly flags cunnan/
magan/sculan as the direct ancestors of PDE modal auxiliaries, noting their unusual paradigms made
them prone to **grammaticalization** out of the open lexical-verb class into the closed
auxiliary/"grammar word" class (pp.109–121).

### Explicit dialectal and diachronic variation across the whole inflectional system (Ch. 7, §7.5)

A dedicated closing section — itself exactly the kind of explicit, source-marked dialectal
annotation the extraction spec treats as highest-value and never to skip — catalogs attested
variant forms by word-class, all relative to the EWS reference forms given earlier in the chapter
(see Vocabulary table above for the individual forms): noun endings vary by period (-æs for -es in
early genitive singular; -um replaced by -an/-on in late texts; final -n loss in Late Northumbrian
weak nouns; -ōē- for -ē- in early/Anglian mutation plurals); pronoun forms vary by dialect and
register (distinct mec/þec/ūsic/ēowic accusatives outside WS prose and in poetry generally,
including WS-recorded poetry — direct evidence that the "merged accusative=dative" pattern given as
the EWS-prose standard paradigm is itself a register-restricted norm, not a universal OE fact); verb
forms vary by dialect, most significantly in the present tense of 'to be' — WS sind(on)/bēoþ versus
Mercian earun and Northumbrian aron, where aron/earun (not sind) are flagged as the direct ancestors
of PDE "are," cognate with Old Norse eru rather than with the WS form's German cognate sind — the
book treats this specific fact as real synchronic evidence bearing on the broader scholarly question
of Anglian dialects' closer typological relationship to North Germanic than the rest of West
Germanic (pp.121–122).

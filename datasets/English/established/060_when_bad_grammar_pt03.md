## Grammar points

### Transitive and intransitive verbs (pp. 79–80)

Transitive verbs require a direct object to complete their meaning (the action is "conveyed across"
to that object); intransitive verbs take no direct object. Many verbs (e.g. *weep, run*) can function
either way depending on the sentence; a few (e.g. *bring*) are always transitive.

### Error 9: Lie vs. Lay (pp. 81–83)

*Don't say* "She lays down for a nap"; *say instead* "She lies down for a nap." "Lay" is transitive
(to put/place something — requires an object); "lie" is intransitive (to recline — no object). Three
diagnostic rules: (1) meaning — reclining vs. placing; (2) every form of "lay" must have an object;
(3) no form of "lie" (the reclining verb) contains a "d" — so "laid"/"lain" always belong to "lay," never
to "lie." Full five-tense paradigm given for both verbs (present, present continuous, future, past,
past perfect).

### Error 10: Sit vs. Set (pp. 84–85)

*Don't say* "Just set there"; *say instead* "Just sit there." "Set" (to put/place/put in motion) is
always transitive; "sit" (to be seated/at rest) is virtually always intransitive, with rare exceptions
(*"He sat them down for a talk"*).

### A Lesson on Verb Tenses (pp. 86–90)

Six tenses split into simple (present, past, future) and perfect (present perfect, past perfect, future
perfect) groups, built from a verb's three principal parts. Irregular verbs' tense forms "often sound
just odd enough to seem wrong even when they're right" (the book's example: *"had swum"*), requiring
memorization rather than a rule. "To be" is presented in full six-tense, person/number paradigm as the
single most irregular and most commonly used verb, serving double duty as both linking verb and
auxiliary.

### "Shall" and "Will" (p. 91)

Formal American usage prescribes first-person "shall" for future/future-perfect, but ordinary American
speech has essentially abandoned this in favor of "will" even in the first person, to the point that the
prescribed negative contraction "shan't" "sounds downright odd to American ears." The book treats this
as a case where the prescriptive rule survives only in highly formal registers.

### Errors 11–20: irregular-verb tense confusions (pp. 91–97)

A sequence of individually numbered errors (*Do, Burst, Dive, Drink, Swim, Ring, Sing, Spring, Hang,
Drive*) each follows the same underlying pattern: a verb's simple past-tense form gets misused in a
perfect-tense slot that requires the (often less familiar-sounding) past-participle form, or vice
versa. Several of these (*drink/drank/drunk*, *swim/swam/swum*, *ring/rang/rung*, *sing/sang/sung*)
share a common i→a→u vowel-change pattern across present/past/past-participle. "Burst" is flagged as
fully invariant across all three principal parts, with its common "busted" variant explicitly labeled
by the source as slang, not a legitimate past tense. "Hang" is flagged as having two entirely separate
principal-part sets depending on sense (ordinary suspension vs. execution).

### Errors 21–23: "ought," "be sure and," "try and" (pp. 97–98)

"Ought" is invariant (never takes its own helping verb, e.g. "had ought" is wrong) and must be followed
by an infinitive. "Be sure and" and "try and" both nonstandardly substitute the conjunction "and" where
an infinitive ("to" + verb) is grammatically required.

### The Subjunctive Mood (pp. 100–102)

Used for hypotheticals, wishes, conditions contrary to fact, and (less commonly) suggestions/demands.
Two practical rules: present-tense verbs drop their third-person "-s" (*I suggest she attend*, not
*attends*); and the indicative past "was" is replaced by "were" in the subjunctive regardless of
grammatical person/number. Diagnostic: look for key words (*if, as though, wish*) combined with a
meaning test (is the sentence about uncertainty, a wish, a suggestion, a demand, or a fact-contrary
condition?) — the mere presence of "if" is not sufficient, since "if" also introduces ordinary
indicative conditionals.

### Error 24: If I Was vs. If I Were (p. 102)

*Don't say* "If I was you, I wouldn't..."; *say instead* "If I were you..." — a condition contrary to
fact (one person cannot literally be another) requires the subjunctive "were." Contrast a structurally
similar but indicative sentence, *"If Jack was at home, Sue must have been with him,"* where "was" is
correct because the sentence expresses a probable fact, not a hypothetical.

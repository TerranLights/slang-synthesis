## Grammar points

### Linguistic reconstruction: comparative vs. internal (Ch. 1)

Reconstruction recovers unattested linguistic history two complementary ways. **Internal
reconstruction** works within a single language, analyzing paradigmatic variation (e.g. the [k]/[tʃ]
alternation across cēosan/cēas vs. curon/coren) to infer an earlier, more uniform ancestor form.
**Comparative reconstruction** compares distinct cognate languages/varieties (OE vs. Old Norse
kjōsa vs. Gothic kiusan) to triangulate the shared ancestor. Reconstructed forms are marked with an
asterisk (*[k]) and are explicitly "reasonable hypotheses," not proven facts — the book stresses
there is no way to independently verify a reconstructed pronunciation the way a modern phonetics
lab can verify a living language. The whole method also depends on the tree-model of linguistic
descent, which the book flags as imperfect because — unlike biological descent — languages can
acquire features through contact (borrowing) that a pure tree-model can't represent; 19th-century
scholars supplemented it with a **wave-model** to handle contact-driven convergence (e.g. Swedish
loanwords in genetically unrelated Finnish) (pp.7–9).

### The Indo-European → Germanic → West Germanic → English genetic chain (Ch. 1)

The book lays out a specific split sequence: Proto-Indo-European → Proto-Germanic → an initial
split into Proto-East-Germanic and Proto-North-West-Germanic → the latter splitting again into
Proto-North-Germanic (ancestor of Norwegian/Danish/Swedish/Icelandic/Faroese) and Proto-West-
Germanic (ancestor of German/Dutch/Frisian/Afrikaans/English). English itself is argued to have
emerged, in the first three centuries AD, from a sub-group of West Germanic dialects on the North
Sea coast sharing distinctive features — termed **North Sea Germanic** or **Ingvaeonic** — whose
core membership (English, Frisian, with Old Saxon as a peripheral possible member) is still
scholarly-disputed. The terminological distinction the book insists on: a **node** on the family
tree is a reconstructed proto-language at a point in time; a **line** connecting nodes is a
"pre-language" — i.e. a period of gradual, not instantaneous, divergence (pp.3–5).

### Register-restricted borrowing in OE vs. open borrowing in later English (Ch. 1–2)

OE borrowed comparatively rarely, and mostly where native word-formation (conversion, affixation,
compounding) didn't already cover a needed concept — concretely, the book's examples are
overwhelmingly Latin loans tied to Christian religious vocabulary (abbod, sealm, sanct, alter).
This contrasts explicitly with later English, where borrowing becomes a major, open vocabulary
strategy after contact with French (from the ME period onward) and beyond — contrasted with native
strategies (affixation: -able as in doable, -ity as in textuality; compounding: webcam, cellphone)
that remain productive in PDE too (pp.21–22, p.27).

### Four-term phonology/writing model: phoneme/allophone/grapheme/allograph (Ch. 2)

The book adopts a symmetric four-way system distinguishing underlying unit from surface
realization, separately for speech and for writing: phoneme (underlying sound unit, /../) vs.
allophone (its speech realization, [..]); grapheme (underlying written unit, <..>) vs. allograph
(its realization in actual handwriting/typeface, author's own notation <<..>>). The same
underlying/surface logic is then explicitly carried over to OE spelling-to-sound mapping in §2.3
(p.12).

### Syllable weight: light/heavy/superheavy via morae (Ch. 2)

OE syllable weight is analyzed using **morae** (beats): a short C or V = 1 mora; a long CC, or a
diphthong/long vowel VV, = 2 morae. Light (= traditionally "short") syllables have rhymes of V, VV,
or VC; heavy (= traditionally "long") syllables have rhymes of VVC or VCC; superheavy syllables
have VVCC rhymes. This mora-based system is distinguished from **stress**, a suprasegmental feature
(loudness/weight/pitch prominence) assigned to a particular syllable rather than inherent to its
internal V/C structure — e.g. in *booklet*, *book-* is stressed, *-let* is not (p.14).

### OE grapheme-to-phoneme mapping, full inventory (Ch. 2)

The book states OE had **no silent graphemes** — every letter written was pronounced, including
word-final <e> in most cases and <r> in all positions (OE was rhotic, like Scottish/many American
PDE accents) — with a small number of diacritic-like exceptions (e.g. <e> in LWS *sceolde*,
*hycgean* marking preceding consonant-cluster quality, not a separate sound). Specific mappings
established:
- `<þ>`/`<ð>` were used **interchangeably** for both voiced and voiceless dental fricatives
  ([θ]/[ð]) — not phonemically distinguished by letter choice in OE spelling itself.
- `<c>` = [k] generally, but [tʃ] before/near front vowels (cild 'child', cēosan 'choose') — though
  with real exceptions recoverable via the PDE reflex (cēne 'bold' → PDE "keen").
- `<g>` had **three** allophones: [j] near front vowels (gīet 'yet', gē 'you'); [g] elsewhere (gān
  'go', glæd 'glad'); [ɣ] between back vowels or near <l,r> + back vowels (dagas 'days', burg
  'city') — with <h> often substituting for final <g> (burh for burg). `<g>` could also represent
  [ʤ] after <n> (engel 'angel'), though [ʤ] is usually spelled <cg> (ecg 'edge').
- `<h>` = [h] initially (hūs 'house'), appears in clusters `<hw, hl, hn, hr>` (hwīl, hlāf, hnesce,
  hræfn), and = [x] finally (seah 'saw').
- `<sc>` = [ʃ] usually (scip 'ship') with exceptions (āscian 'ask', Scottas 'Scots').
- Consonant **length** was phonemic (geminate <nn> in sunne 'sun' ≠ single <n> in sunu 'son'), as
  was vowel length (marked with a macron in modern editions only — not by Anglo-Saxon scribes
  themselves): God 'God' ≠ gōd 'good'; wendon 'turned' ≠ wēndon 'believed'; āwacian 'awaken' ≠
  āwācian 'grow weak'.
- Monophthong vowel graphemes `<y, i, e, æ, a, o, u>` each had long/short pairs, with **no
  qualitative** difference between long and short (unlike PDE pit/peat) — that quality split arose
  only in ME. `<æ>` and `<y>` are used differently than in PDE: `<y>` = front close rounded /y/,
  `<æ>` = front open unrounded (≈ PDE /a/). Three diphthong-spellings are identified: `<ea, eo,
  ie>` (pp.15–17).

### Grammar/lexicon terminology hierarchy (Ch. 2)

The book restricts "grammar" to mean **syntax** (how words combine into phrases/clauses/sentences)
plus **morphology** (word-form), explicitly separating both from the **lexicon**. Morphology
subdivides into **inflexional morphology** (love/loves/loved-type alternation) and **lexical
morphology/word-formation** (blackbird, undo-type compounding/affixation). The grammatical unit
hierarchy given is strict containment: sentences ⊃ clauses ⊃ phrases ⊃ words ⊃ morphemes. A
**morpheme** is the minimal unit of grammatical analysis — demonstrated by segmenting *"The kind
girls have given sweets to all their friends"* into 10 words but 14 morphemes (The-kind-girl-**s**-
have-give-**n**-sweet-**s**-to-all-their-friend-**s**), with bound morphemes (-s, -n) shown to be
positionally fixed (must follow their stem) unlike free morphemes, which can move around a
construction within the bounds of well-formedness. A **lexeme** is the superordinate term for a
set of forms related by inflexion (love, loves, loved = one lexeme) (pp.17–19).

### Open vs. closed word classes (Ch. 2)

**Open classes** (readily extended by new coinage/borrowing): nouns, lexical verbs, adjectives,
adverbs, interjections. **Closed classes** (a fixed, non-extensible set with cohesive discourse
function, sometimes loosely called "grammar words"): determiners, pronouns, prepositions,
conjunctions, auxiliary verbs, numerals. Prototypical phrase-internal roles are specified: nouns
head noun phrases, lexical verbs head verb phrases; adjectives modify nouns (or head adjective
phrases); adverbs head/modify adverb phrases or modify adjectives; determiners and numerals always
modify nouns; auxiliaries modify lexical verbs; prepositions head prepositional phrases;
conjunctions link phrases/clauses; pronouns substitute for noun phrases. Clause-level functions:
noun phrases prototypically serve as subject, object, or complement; adjective phrases as
complement; adverb phrases as adverbial; verb phrases as predicator. Clauses are main (can stand
alone) or subordinate (function inside a main clause) (pp.19–20).

### OE's grammatical typology: inflexion-driven relationships, flexible word order (Ch. 2)

The book's central structural claim about OE: because grammatical relationships (subject vs.
object, modifier agreement) are carried by **inflexional endings** rather than by element order,
OE word order is far more flexible than PDE's — reordering a sentence's phrases does not change its
core meaning, unlike in PDE where order is load-bearing. This is demonstrated with the minimal pair
*Sēo cwēn lufode þone gōdan cnapan* = *Þone gōdan cnapan lufode sēo cwēn* (both 'the queen loved the
good servant'), contrasted with PDE *The queen loved the good servant* ≠ *The good servant loved
the queen*. The mechanism: determiners/adjectives/nouns agree in **gender** (cwēn is feminine,
cnapan is masculine — hence different determiner forms sēo/þone), **number**, and **case**
(subjects = nominative, objects = accusative), and verbs agree in **number** with their subject via
distinct singular/plural inflexional endings (unlike PDE, where the preterite -ed is shared across
both numbers) (pp.22–23).

### Full clause-by-clause syntactic analysis of the Ælfric *Life of King Edmund* passage (Ch. 3)

This passage (Edmund's martyrdom at Ivarr "Hinguar" the Boneless's command, from Sweet/Davis 1953)
is used as the book's main extended syntax demonstration, and establishes several recurring OE
clause-structure patterns by direct worked example:
- **Verb-second-ish order after a clause-initial adverbial**: when a clause opens with an adverbial
  (A), the predicator (P) typically precedes the subject (S) — A(Þā) P(geseah) S(Hinguar) 'Then
  Hinguar saw' — a pattern the book notes also holds in Present-Day German.
- **Apposition agrees in case with its head**: se ārlēasa flotmann 'the cruel sailor' sits in
  apposition to the nominative subject Hinguar and is therefore itself nominative throughout
  (determiner + adjective + noun all agreeing).
  <br>*Morpheme breakdown:* ā-rlēas-a = negative prefix ā- + rlēas ('lacking honor/mercy') + weak
  adjective nominative-singular ending -a.
- **Discontinuous (split) verb phrases**: where PDE keeps an auxiliary+main-verb complex together
  around its object (*would not forsake Christ*), OE characteristically splits them around the
  object — nolde Crīste wiþsacan 'would not forsake Christ' puts the dative object Crīste between
  the two verb elements.
- **Dative objects after certain verbs, not just after prepositions**: direct objects are
  prototypically accusative in OE, but some verbs (here wiþsacan 'forsake') instead govern a dative
  object — Crīste, inflected -e from nominative Crīst — paralleling the dative's more usual roles
  (replacing PDE "to"/"with" prepositional phrases, or marking indirect objects).
- **Subordinate-clause verb-final tendency**: in ac mid ānrǣdum gelēafan hine ǣfre clipode 'but with
  single-minded faith called continually upon him', the verb clipode is held to clause-final
  position — flagged as a characteristic (though not absolute — see the betwix þǣm þe clause below,
  where the verb follows its subject immediately) feature of OE subordinate clauses.
  <br>*Morpheme breakdown of* ānrǣdum *('single-minded', dative):* ān ('one') + rǣd ('advice,
  resolution') + dative-marking inflexion -um — literally 'one-resolution', a compound adjective.
- **Null/implied subjects recoverable from context**: Hēt hine þā behēafdian '(Hinguar) commanded
  him then to be beheaded' has no expressed subject; it must be inferred as Hinguar from the prior
  clause. (The book treats this as ordinary OE discourse-level ellipsis, not a special construction.)
- **Converted adjective-as-noun**: þā hǣþnan 'the heathens' is a determiner (þā) + an adjective
  (hǣþen-) functioning as the noun-phrase headword via conversion — flagged as a common OE usage.
- **Idiomatic dative-of-separation ("from X, concerning Y") constructions**: slōgon him of þæt
  hēafod is glossed literally as 'struck from-him concerning-the-head' — the book flags this
  him...of...þæt hēafod pattern as an idiomatic OE construction with no direct PDE word-order
  equivalent (contrast PDE 'struck the head off him').
- **Genitive possession, continuous with PDE**: his sāwol 'his soul' uses the genitive pronoun his
  for possession exactly as PDE still does (his, her, its; the woman's book) — flagged as one of the
  clearest continuities between OE and PDE grammar rather than a difference.

The book's overall framing conclusion from this passage: OE is **more synthetic** than PDE (greater
reliance on inflexion over word order), its element order is **more flexible** (though still
governed by real stylistic/positional tendencies, not free-for-all), and much of its vocabulary
survives into PDE in modified phonological form even where meaning has drifted (pp.29–32).

### Scots as a phonologically more conservative PDE variety relative to OE (Ch. 3)

The book uses a specific vowel-history case to argue that "OE vs. PDE" distance is not uniform
across modern dialects: OE cū, hū, nū, hūs, ūt (all with long monophthong [u:]) underwent
diphthongization in most PDE varieties via the 15th–16th century **Great Vowel Shift** (→ cow, how,
now, house, out), but Scots never underwent this particular change for these words, retaining the
monophthong — reflected in dialect spellings like coo, hoo, noo, hoose, oot. The book's explicit
point: this makes Scots demonstrably *closer* to OE than other PDE varieties for this specific
phonological feature, even though no PDE variety is "more OE" across the board (p.28).

### OE verse structure: alliteration and the half-line (Ch. 3)

Introduced via *The Dream of the Rood* (surviving fullest in the Vercelli Book, 10th c.) compared
against the older, fragmentary Ruthwell Cross runic inscription (7th/8th c. Old Northumbrian) of
related lines. Both texts, despite being different OE dialects (Vercelli = Late West Saxon;
Ruthwell = Old Northumbrian) centuries apart, share verb-initial/verb-final clause-order patterns
made possible by rich inflexional marking, and share the PDE-continuous practice of case-marking
pronouns for function (hē/hine in Vercelli; he/hinæ in Ruthwell, paralleling PDE he/him). The verse
itself is structured in **half-lines** linked in pairs by **alliteration** on stressed syllables —
alliteration functioning structurally in OE verse the way rhyme later does in PDE poetry; e.g. in
*heofona Hlāford; hyldan mē ne dorste*, three of the four open-class words (heofona, Hlāford,
hyldan) alliterate on /h/. OE manuscripts wrote verse continuously with no visual line/half-line
distinction — the half-line layout seen in modern editions is an editorial convention (pp.33–35).

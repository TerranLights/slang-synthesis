# English — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: "Grammar Review" (foundational terminology)

**Source:** Ann Batko (edited by Edward Rosenheim), *When Bad Grammar Happens to Good People: How to
Avoid Common Errors in English* (Career Press, 2004), front-matter unit "Grammar Review," printed pp.
31–54 (PDF pages 32–55).

numbered, titled errors ("Don't Say" vs. "Say Instead," each with a "Here's Why" explanation) grouped
into 13 chapters, preceded by a Foreword, an Introduction, a Pretest, and this Grammar Review. The book

## Grammar points

### The eight parts of speech (pp. 31–46)

The source frames "part of speech" as a word's functional role within a given sentence rather than a
fixed property of the word itself — many words (its example: *love*) shift part of speech by context
(noun: *a book about love*; verb: *I love eating out*; adjective: *a love poem*). The eight parts
covered are noun, pronoun, verb, adjective, adverb, preposition, conjunction, and interjection (see
Vocabulary table above for each). Within pronouns, the source distinguishes five kinds (personal,
intensive/reflexive as a personal-pronoun subgroup, demonstrative, indefinite, relative, and
interrogative — six if intensive/reflexive is counted separately) and flags personal pronouns as "the
most complex group...implicated in more errors than their other pronoun friends," setting up Chapter
1's focus. Verbs are introduced with their three "manners of description" — tense, voice, and mood —
each deferred to later chapters for detailed error treatment (tense/mood to Chapter 2).

### The parts of a sentence (pp. 46–54)

A sentence requires both identification (subject) and explanation (predicate) of that identified
thing; lacking either yields a sentence fragment. The source works through subject/predicate
("single-family home" scale), complements (direct object, indirect object, linking-verb complement —
explicitly flagged as the mechanism behind correct forms like *It was she* rather than *It was her*),
and clauses ("apartment building" scale: independent clauses stand alone, subordinate/dependent clauses
cannot and instead function as an adjective, adverb, or noun within the main clause). This
complement-of-a-linking-verb concept and the subjective/objective/possessive case system it interacts
with are the explicit setup for Chapter 1's pronoun-case error entries (I/me, she/her, who/whom).

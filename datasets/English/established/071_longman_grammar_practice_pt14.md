## Grammar points

### 13.1 Yes/No questions, negative statements, Yes/No answers

**13.1A Yes/No questions (expecting 'Yes' or 'No' in the answer).** With *be*, *have* (as auxiliary),
and modals, Yes/No questions are formed by inversion (auxiliary/modal moves before the subject). With
all other verbs, *do*/*does* (simple present) or *did* (simple past) precede the subject, and the main
verb reverts to its bare infinitive form, p.188.

**13.1B Negative statements.** With *be*, *have*, or a modal, *not* follows the auxiliary/modal. With
all other verbs, *do not*/*does not* (simple present) or *did not* (simple past) follows the subject,
again with the bare infinitive, p.188.

**13.1C Yes/No short answers.** The conventional short answer repeats the first word of the question
(sometimes in a shifted form, e.g. *am* → *I'm not*). Answering with just *Yes*/*No* and no repeated
auxiliary — or answering in a full sentence — is both unidiomatic; *Yes*/*No* alone specifically can
sound rude. Short answers carry real expressive range: giving information, agreeing, disagreeing,
confirming, p.189.

### 13.2 Alternative negative forms and negative questions

**13.2A Negative statements with 'negative adverbs': 'never', etc.** *Never, hardly, hardly ever,
seldom, rarely* make negative or near-negative statements more emphatic than plain *not*. A negative
adverb can never combine with an already-negative verb to form a double negative ("I can hardly
recognize him," not "*I can't hardly*"), and this extends systematically to *no/any* and their
compounds (*no one*/*not anyone*, *nothing*/*not anything*, *nowhere*/*not anywhere*), p.190.

**13.2B Cancelling what has just been said: 'No, not Wednesday'.** A just-stated detail can be
cancelled and corrected with "no, not X, Y" — never with "*No X*" alone, p.190.

**13.2C Negative questions: 'Can't you...?'.** The short form (*didn't he...?*) is the normal
conversational word order; the full form (*did he not...?*) is reserved for emphasis. Depending on
stress/intonation, a negative question can express disbelief/surprise, invite a "Yes" answer,
persuade, express annoyance, or form an exclamation, p.191.

### 13.3 Tag questions and echo tags

**13.3A Tag questions 1: 'It is..., isn't it?'/'It isn't..., is it?'.** English uses tags (formed
from auxiliaries + *do/does/did*) where many languages use a single fixed phrase like "Isn't that
so?" A tag can be affirmative-statement/negative-tag or negative-statement/affirmative-tag. Rising
intonation signals a genuine question needing an answer; falling intonation signals the speaker
expects the listener simply to agree, p.192.

**13.3B Tag questions 2: 'You painted it yourself, did you?'.** Tags can also be
affirmative-statement/affirmative-tag. Rising intonation here means "tell me more"; falling intonation
expresses a negative reaction — disappointment, disapproval, suspicion — again with no answer
expected, p.192.

**13.3C Echo tags: 'Is he?'/'He is?'.** An echo tag responds, in tag form, to something already said,
with several equivalent surface forms available for both affirmative and negative originals. Rising
intonation on the echo requests more information; falling intonation confirms what's already known,
guessed, or expresses a strong reaction like anger or surprise, p.193.

### 13.4 Additions and responses

**13.4A Additions and contrasts: 'John can...and I can, too/but I can't'.** Two parallel systems for
extending or contrasting with a statement: repeating the subject with *and I can, too*/*but I can't*
(and the *do*-support equivalents), or inverting subject and auxiliary with *and so can I*/*and
neither/nor can I* (*neither* and *nor* being fully interchangeable here), p.194.

**13.4B Parallel responses: 'John can.../I can, too/So can I'.** A standalone response confirming the
same thing about oneself repeats the auxiliary (or supplies *do/does/did* if the original had none),
either as "I can, too" or inverted as "So can I"; the written forms *So am I*, *Neither am I*, *Nor am
I* are generally spelled out in full even though often contracted in speech (*So'm I*), p.194.

**13.4C 'So have you' and 'So you have!'.** An important homonymous pair: "I've got a rash on my arm
and so have you" is a parallel addition about someone else, while "I've got a rash on my arm. — So you
have!" is a confirmation/surprise response to the same statement — identical surface form, different
function, p.195.

### 13.5 Question-word questions (1): 'Who(m)...?', 'What...?'

**13.5A Form of question-word questions (except subject questions).** The word order is question-word
+ auxiliary + subject — the same inversion as a Yes/No question, just with the question-word fronted:
"Why is he working?," not "*Why he is working?*" (Subject questions, which don't invert, are covered
separately in 13.8.), p.196.

**13.5B 'Who(m)...?' as a question-word.** *Who(m)* asks for the object of a sentence — almost always
a person's name or pronoun — and covers masculine, feminine, singular, and plural alike. *Whom* is
still used in formal register; everyday style generally prefers *who*. *Who(m)* is especially common
in questions whose verb is followed by *to* or *for*, p.196.

**13.5C 'What...?' as a question-word.** *What* can ask for an entire proposition or for the object of
a sentence, and also combines directly with a noun ("What book(s)?"). It appears in many fixed
question collocations — see the Vocabulary table for the full set (*what's...like?*, *what's...
called?*, *what make?*, etc.), p.197.

## Grammar points

### Dialectology and sociolinguistic framing (§17.1, pp.532–533)

English's global spread has produced genuine debate over whether non-standard "nativized" varieties
constitute independent norms (the "World Englishes"/"pluricentric language" position) or remain
oriented to a common outside norm. The chapter's own working division is British English (also
covering Ireland, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa) vs. (North) American English, cross-cut by a
separate North/South typology valid within both. British Received Pronunciation (RP) began as a
regional (Southeast-of-London) accent, became the accent taught in British public schools and used by
the BBC, and today functions as a non-regional "educated accent." American English, by contrast, has
regional pronunciation standards rather than one national standard. Writing standards (morphology,
syntax, orthography) are far more uniform across all of English's standard varieties than pronunciation
is — differences there are "more often than not a matter of frequency" rather than categorical. Major
non-standard regional divisions: in Britain, North vs. South (with East–West sub-divisions inside each);
in the US, North/Midlands/South (older Eastern/Southern/General American analysis now considered
outdated); notable ethnically-marked US varieties include Black English (noted as "arguably in the
final stages of decreolization") and Chicano English (noted as "a semi-creole").

### Phonology (§17.2, pp.534–539)

**Consonants** (Table 17.1): the standard two-series voiced/voiceless obstruent system found throughout
Germanic, but the voiced/voiceless contrast is not reliably cued by vocal-cord vibration alone —
aspiration and preceding-vowel length also carry the distinction, and in American English intervocalic
voiceless stops often surface voiced, neutralizing pairs like *writer/rider* or *latter/ladder*.
Word-final pre-nasal voiceless stops in many British (and some American) accents are reinforced by a
glottal stop that shortens the preceding vowel instead. /θ/ and /ð/ don't carry equal functional load:
word-initially there's near-complementary distribution (voiced /ð/ only in a closed set of function
words — *this, that, than, then, though, them*); word-finally, minimal pairs are vanishingly rare
(*teeth/teethe*, *wreath/wreathe*). /r/ shows the widest allophonic range of any English phoneme across
dialects (post-alveolar approximant or alveolar flap in Britain; retroflex or velar-with-pharyngeal
secondary articulation in North America) and the rhotic/non-rhotic split (loss of post-vocalic /r/,
beginning in southern England in the 17th century) is one of English's most consequential dialect
markers — rhotic varieties (most North American, Scots, Irish) retain /r/ in all positions; non-rhotic
ones (England, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, southern coastal US) exclude it word-finally and
pre-consonantally. /x/ and /w̥/ survive only in some Northern English/Scots/some North American
dialects (*witch* vs. *which*) and in Scots/South African/Yiddish-influenced English (*loch*-type /x/).

**Vowels.** Unlike most of its Germanic sister languages, English has no rounded front vowels. Short
vowels, long monophthongs, and diphthongs form a coherent three-way system (Table 17.2) grouped by
shared distributional behavior: long vowels/diphthongs occur in open stressed syllables where short
vowels don't; short vowels correspond fairly consistently to other dialects' short vowels (differing
mainly in phonetic quality), while long vowels/diphthongs often correspond across dialects to *either*
other long vowels *or* diphthongs; and both long vowels and diphthongs are shortened before voiceless
obstruents (*plays* vs. *place*; *league* vs. *leak*). Realization varies enormously by dialect even
where the phonemic system is shared (the chapter's named example: American English's unrounded, more
front realization of the RP low back vowel in *pot/hot*).

**Syllable structure.** Onsets allow up to three consonants (first must be /s/, second a voiceless
stop, third one of /r, l, j, w/ — *sprinkle, scrape, splash, stew*); codas allow up to four and permit
more complex clusters than onsets, many of them morphologically complex (*glimpsed, desks, bridles*).
Clusters must agree in voicing throughout (*exit* has only the two pronunciations /ɛgzɪt/ or /ɛksɪt/,
never a voicing-mismatched third option), and no two sibilants or two same-place stops can co-occur —
this underlies the three-way phonologically-conditioned allomorphy of the plural/possessive/third-person
suffix (-s after voiceless, -z after voiced, -ɪz after sibilants).

**Word stress.** English is a genuine "free-stress" language (stress position is not predictable purely
from syllable count, unlike Finnish/French/Polish's fixed-position systems), illustrated by minimal
pairs distinguishing word class by stress alone (*'abstract* adj. vs. *ab'stract* v.; *'accent* n. vs.
*ac'cent* v.; *'contract* n. vs. *con'tract* v., the latter also showing a vowel-quality difference).
Despite this apparent unpredictability, two largely-coinciding rule systems govern the vast majority of
cases: the Germanic-stock rule (stress the first syllable of any lexical root, including compounds —
*'mother, 'heaven, 'nightingale, 'cut-throat*) and the Romance-stock "Main Stress Rule" (stress falls on
the rightmost *heavy* syllable — one with the structure -VV(C) or -VCC — among the last three
syllables of the word: *'blossom, 'human* vs. *di'vine, dis'turb*; *'history, cri'terion* vs.
*'narrative, me'tropolis*). This rule doesn't apply to words with inherently stress-marked affixes
(*-ee* in *employee*) or stress-attracting suffixes (*-ity, -ic, -ian, -ial*). A further, independent
regularity: nouns/adjectives tend toward first-syllable stress, verbs toward second-syllable stress in
differential-stress pairs (*'transfer* n. vs. *trans'fer* v.).

**Orthography.** Modern English spelling is widely perceived as highly irregular, reinforced by the
fact that many of the 400 most frequent words are irregularly spelled — but over 70% of the vocabulary
as a whole is spelled according to general patterns, a notably higher regularity rate than in other
Germanic languages despite the perception. Historical layers: Old English's Roman-missionary-devised
spelling (imperfectly suited to the OE phonemic inventory) was modified by French scribes after the
Conquest, then effectively frozen by Caxton's printing-era standardization on the London dialect — so
later sound changes (the Great Vowel Shift; loss of /k/ and /g/ before nasals in *knee, know, gnash,
gnat*; loss of post-vocalic /r/; the Middle English /u/ split into Modern /ʌ/ (*hut, cut*) vs. retained
/ʊ/ after labials (*pull, push, wool*)) left no trace in spelling. Despite poor phoneme-to-grapheme
transparency, English orthography is comparatively strong at the *morphological* level — inflectional
morphemes keep one consistent spelling (*-s, -ed*) despite allophonic variation, and derivationally
related words are often more transparently related in spelling than in pronunciation (*divine/divinity;
serene/serenity; indicate/indicative of; profane/profanity*).

### Morphology (§17.3, pp.539–544)

English has lost most of its original Germanic inflectional contrasts, developing from an inflecting
language into a largely isolating/analytic one: articles and adjectives are wholly invariant; noun case
has collapsed to a single surviving genitive/common-case distinction (*John's book*), and the genitive
*-s* increasingly behaves as a phrasal affix on the whole NP (the "group genitive": *someone else's
girlfriend*) rather than a true case-ending; pronouns alone retain a three-term
nominative/accusative/genitive distinction (*I/me/my*, etc.), with *you* (2nd person) lacking even a
plural counterpart in most dialects, and the relative/interrogative *who/whom* paradigm now rarely
preserved in spoken registers (*whom* yielding to *who*). Grammatical gender plays no inflectional role;
anaphoric pronoun choice tracks natural/metaphorical sex (*the uncle…he, the aunt…she, the family…
it/they, the ship/engine…she*). Number is the one inflectional category preserved throughout English's
history for nouns — singular unmarked, plural via the *-s/-z/-ɪz* allomorphs, with a residual irregular
layer (internal vowel change: *foot–feet, goose–geese*; zero: *sheep, series*; foreign affixes). Two
distinct pro-forms for differential anaphora exist: *it* after factive verbs (*I regret/resent/know
it*) vs. *so* (historically a deictic expression) after verbs of saying/wishing/thinking (*I think/
guess/assume/suppose so*).

**Adjectives** are invariant in both predicative and attributive position; comparison is either
inflectional (*-er/-est*, for monosyllables) or analytic (*more/most*, for trisyllabic+), with
disyllabic adjectives as a genuine mixed zone sensitive to syllable structure (adjectives ending in
unstressed vowel, /l/, or /ə(r)/ favor the inflectional strategy) and to frequency (rare adjectives
favor the analytic strategy even if monosyllabic).

**Verbal inflection.** Only two tenses are morphologically marked (present/non-past, past); weak verbs
form the past via a dental suffix, strong verbs via ablaut (a closed class, but containing very common/
frequent verbs — a Germanic-general pattern, see Ch.5's full OE strong-verb-class system in `pt01.md`).
Apart from the copula *be* (which alone still shows person/number contrast across *am/are/is*,
*was/were*), finite verbs only distinguish 3sg. from the rest in the present tense. Non-finite forms:
infinitive (base form), present participle (*-ing*), past participle (dental suffix for weak verbs,
*-en* or vowel change for strong verbs). There is no dedicated imperative form (the base form serves);
no consistent indicative/subjunctive inflectional distinction remains — where other Germanic languages
use a dedicated subjunctive form, English instead uses the past tense (*if he knew the answer…*), the
base form (*long live the Queen!*), or a modal verb (*may the Lord bless you*); the sole surviving
former-subjunctive relic is *were* with 1st/3rd singular (*if I were you*), itself eroding toward *was*
in casual speech. Every verb other than *be* thus occurs in a maximum of four forms (weak) or five
(strong), diagrammed in the source's Figure 17.1 as a tensed/non-tensed split (tensed: past, present
[3sg./other]; non-tensed: infinitive, present participle, past participle).

**Tense and aspect.** Structurally, English marks only a two-term past/non-past opposition; a
"future tense" is developing from *will* + infinitive (already incipient in Old English, per Ch.5's
coverage of quasi-modals), and the three "relative tenses" (present perfect, past perfect, future
perfect) are compositional combinations of the three absolute tenses with the perfect marker *have* +
past participle — though semantically they're not perfectly compositional, leading some analyses to
treat English as having a six-term rather than two-term tense system. Several competing devices express
future time with different nuances: *will* for contingent/conditional-implying statements; *shall*
(British English, first person only) and *be going to* for speaker/hearer intention; simple present for
scheduled/regular events; present progressive for an arrangement already made at utterance time; *will*
+ progressive for a "future-as-a-matter-of-course" reading. The present perfect contrasts sharply with
the simple past by compatibility with time adverbials spanning up to the moment of utterance ("extended
now" / "current relevance") vs. adverbials identifying a point strictly before utterance-time; its
general meaning cashes out differently by verb class (aktionsart) as state-up-to-now, experiential, or
resultative perfect readings. **A systematic British/American contrast** is noted: American English
more readily uses the simple past where British English requires the present perfect with adverbials
like *ever, never, yet* (*did you ever see such a thing?*), i.e. the present perfect's distinctive range
is narrower in American than in British usage.

**The progressive.** A genuinely aspectual (not tense) category, contrasting with the unmarked simple
form; it combines with nearly all verbs and tenses except a small set of inherently stative verbs
(*contain, own, belong to, consist of, know*). Core meaning: the progressive situates an event/process
as an open interval surrounding some reference moment, viewed from within (ongoing) rather than from
outside (simple form) — yielding incompleteness with event verbs (*the child was drowning* doesn't
entail the child drowned) and an unbounded-duration reading with duration adverbials. Secondary, more
specific readings identified in context: temporal-frame/backgrounding use, deliberate-omission
("non-acting") use, a mathematical/continuous-process use. Minor extended uses: a futurate use (see
Tense/Aspect above), an emotive/hyperbolic use with frequency adverbials (*he is always getting into
trouble*), and an interpretive use glossing a prior simple-form statement as amounting to something
else (*if you fail to plan you are planning to fail*). The progressive has steadily extended its
territory since its "modest beginnings in Old English and Middle English" (explicitly cross-referencing
the historical development covered in `pt01.md`'s Ch.5 section): it now combines with the perfect, the
passive, and even the copula + adjective/NP (where it conveys temporary/willful behavior or role-playing:
*aren't you being a little defensive?*).

### Syntax — Nominal group (§17.4, pp.544–548)

**Noun phrase.** Complements and relative clauses follow the head noun; attributive adjectives
generally precede it; articles sit at the left periphery. Two genitive constructions exist in
complementary distribution by position: *-'s* precedes the head (*John's book*), *of*-phrases follow it
(*the leader of the band*) — the two can combine as a "double genitive" (*a friend of my father's*).
Unlike other Germanic languages, English disallows complex pre-nominal modifiers (adjective + complement
can only follow the head: *a country rich in natural resources*, not *a rich-in-natural-resources
country*), except when the adjective/participle is itself compounded (*a tree-lined avenue*) — explained
by two independent constraints: adjectival modifiers must stay adjacent to their head noun (true
throughout Germanic), and complements must strictly follow their adjectival head in English specifically.

**Relative clauses** can be introduced by *who/whose/whom*, *which*, the invariant marker *that*, zero,
or (marginally, only after *such*) *as*. Marker choice tracks antecedent animacy/personhood
(*who/whom* after personal antecedents, *which* after non-personal), the relativized constituent's
grammatical function, restrictiveness, and whether a preposition is present. Relative pronouns can be
omitted in restrictive clauses except when relativizing the subject position — with one exception:
subject relatives can omit the pronoun after existential *there* (*there is a man wants to see you*).
Headless relatives use *who/which/what* + *-ever*, or *what*. Relativization is possible not only from
subject/object positions but from the second argument of a comparative ("standard of comparison")
construction — illustrated by a genuine literary citation (A. Cross, *Death in the Faculty*) involving
*than whom*. A further distinctive English feature: "unbounded dependencies," where a clause-initial
*wh*-item's grammatical function can be borne with respect not to the closest verb but to a verb inside
a smaller embedded constituent (*this is exactly what the British government is now trying to find ways
to do*).

**Reflexive and reciprocal pronouns.** Reflexives (*herself, ourselves*) and reciprocals (*each other,
one another*) are anaphors requiring an antecedent, typically within the same clause. Old English had no
true reflexives — the OE emphatic focus particle *self/seolf* (cognate to German *selbst*, Dutch *zelfs*)
gradually attached after the plain personal pronoun during late Old/Middle English and grammaticalized
into one fused word, yielding Modern English's "strong"/"complex" reflexives, distinct from the "weak"
reflexives of German (*sich*) or Scandinavian (*sig*) — this directly cross-references and extends
`pt01.md`'s Ch.5 coverage of the same development (*ic bletsige mē* → *hiene selfne* → fused *himself*).
Resulting properties: person/number/gender-marked forms throughout (*myself…themselves*), a dedicated
indefinite form (*oneself*), but — unlike Scandinavian — no possessive reflexives, and — unlike many
other languages — almost no inherently reflexive verbs requiring the reflexive pronoun (*wash/dress/
shave (oneself)* take it optionally, not obligatorily). No formal English distinction exists between a
true reflexive and an "emphatic" use of the same form (*the President himself won't come* vs. *the
President won't come himself*) — a genuine syncretism, since Old English's dedicated emphatic particle
*self* didn't survive into Modern English as a separate item. Core syntactic generalizations: antecedent
and reflexive are normally clausemates, the antecedent typically c-commands the reflexive, split
antecedents are excluded, and personal/reflexive pronouns are typically in complementary distribution —
but several genuine exceptions are catalogued: 1st/2nd-person reflexives can lack any linguistic
antecedent at all (*this would be difficult for my wife and myself*); plain pronouns sometimes
substitute for expected reflexives with a perspective-shifting effect (reflexive = protagonist's
point-of-view, plain pronoun = neutral "objective" viewpoint: *John hid the book behind him/himself*);
and reflexives can occasionally have antecedents well beyond the local clause.

**Indefinite pronouns** form a heterogeneous class cutting across several distinct syntactic behaviors:
determiner-like (*some, any, every, each, either, all*), more adjective-like/quantifier-like (*many,
few, much, several*), and NP-like (compounds of determiner + generic noun: *someone, somebody, anybody,
everyone, nothing, somewhere, sometime*). A genuinely English-specific internal structure: such
indefinites are compositionally built from generic nouns (*body, thing, time, place*) plus
quantifier-like elements, unparalleled elsewhere in Germanic. Lexical selection is sensitive to
mass/count distinctions (*much/little* vs. *(a) few*) and *each* vs. *every* differs in whether the
quantificational domain is contextually restricted (*each*) or not (*every*) — but the chapter singles
out the *some*/*any* distinction as receiving the most cross-linguistic attention: *any* splits into a
"free-choice" reading (*anybody can come to the party*) and an "existential" reading restricted to
negative-polarity ("downward-entailing") contexts — negation, interrogatives, conditionals, emotive
predicates — standing in partial complementary distribution with existential *some*.

### Syntax — Verbs and verb phrases (§17.4 cont'd, pp.548–553)

**Main vs. auxiliary verbs** are distinguished by: inability to stand alone as main predicate (recalling
that this was not yet categorical in Old/Middle English — see `pt01.md`'s coverage of modals'
historical main-verb properties); obligatory position of *not* immediately after the first auxiliary
(triggering *do*-support if none is present); subject-auxiliary inversion; stress-bearing for polarity
focus; and post-verbal ellipsis/question-tag licensing. Within auxiliaries: primary (*be, have, do*) vs.
secondary/modal (*will, shall, must, can, may* — defective: no non-finite forms except in Scottish
English, no 3sg. *-s*, no *to*-infinitive combination). *Have* can function as a full main verb
(possession) in British English but not (all varieties of) American English — a genuine, still-live
Br/Am syntactic contrast (*I haven't any books* / *have you any books?* grammatical in BrE, generally
excluded in AmE). The verb phrase's auxiliary complex (also called "Infl.") can stack up to four
auxiliaries in a fixed order — modal–perfect–progressive–passive — the last three realized as
discontinuous expressions (*have+ed*, *be+ing*, *be+ed*). This full auxiliary complex relocates as a
unit under VP-preposing (*he said he would never kill deer again, but kill deer he did*) or VP-ellipsis
(*he wasn't killed, but he could have been*). Within the VP, complements uniformly follow the verb and
NP complements precede PP complements; "split infinitives" (adverb between *to* and the verb),
prescriptively frowned upon, are common in modern usage.

**Grammatical relations** are marked exclusively by argument position (not case, except residually in
pronouns), with the normal subject-before-verb order preserved even under marked word order
(*John I don't like, never have I seen such a sight*). There are no subjectless sentences of the German
*mich friert* type, nor contextual subject omission as in Italian/Spanish — a dummy *it/there* subject
is obligatorily inserted if no other subject is available. Direct objects in simple transitive clauses
are a single undifferentiated class (no dative/accusative sub-distinction, unlike historical English's
case-marked system — per `pt01.md`), differentiated from each other only by word order or prepositions
when more than one is present, and always kept distinct from prepositional objects. Sentence patterns
are classified by valency, including a class of symmetric predicates taking plural/conjoined subjects
without any object at all (*John and Mary met/agreed/kissed/married*) alongside the parallel transitive
construction (*John met/kissed/married Mary*) — the choice between the two encodes differences in
empathy, initiative, and control. **Phrasal verbs** (particle verbs, Germanic-origin, stress on the
first syllable, combining with directional/locative particles that can also function as prepositions)
show the same transitive/intransitive valency range as ordinary verbs, and are distinguished from verbs
taking prepositional objects by several syntactic tests: a pronominal object must precede and can only
precede a true particle (*he threw it up* / *he talked about it*, never *he threw up it*); interrogative/
relative fronting can strand a preposition but not a particle (*what did he talk about?* vs. ungrammatical
particle-fronting); manner adverbials can intervene between verb and preposition but not between verb
and particle. **Ditransitives** show two patterns — double-object (*Mary gave John a book*) and
NP+PP (*Mary gave a book to John*) — terminologically described either as primary/secondary or
direct/indirect object, with verbs like *elect, consider, make, call* (taking an object + predicative
complement) sometimes instead analyzed as taking a non-finite "small clause" complement (*they elected
[him President]*).

**Voice.** Three options: active, passive, and "middle" (mediopassive), the latter two both reducing
basic valency. The passive uses *be* + past participle, with informal spoken *get*-passives carrying an
added nuance of emotional involvement or partial responsibility (*I got stung*; *he got (himself) run
over by a car*). The constraint on which verbs passivize is semantic-pragmatic (the derived subject must
be a true Patient) rather than purely syntactic — *fit, suit, lack, survive* resist the passive despite
being ordinary transitives. Ditransitives have two possible passives (*John was offered a cigar* / *a
cigar was offered to John*), and — in contrast to the rest of Germanic — English permits passivization
of prepositional objects (*I have been lied to*) and even some directional-complement/locative-adjunct
sentences with intransitive verbs (*this bed has been slept in*), though simple intransitives with
locative adjuncts alone (*dance, snore, sneeze*) still resist it, since English — unlike some languages —
has no genuinely subjectless sentences to fall back on. The "middle" voice constructs a transitive verb
intransitively with the normal object promoted to subject, without any reflexive marker (contrast other
Germanic languages' reflexive-marked equivalents): *this bed folds up easily; this paint applies evenly.*

**Ergative/labile verbs and light-verb constructions.** No productive morphological valency-changing
process exists, but a large class of verbs is intransitive/transitive with matching selectional
restrictions across both uses (*the baby sat up* / *John sat the baby up*) — termed "ergative" verbs
with "double orientation." A separate, structurally distinct phenomenon is the complex light-verb
construction (semantically bleached *have/take/do/get/give* + a de-verbal noun identical to the
underlying verb: *to take a look, to give a smile, to do a dance, to get a feel*): *have*-constructions
(more common in British/Australian than American English) denote intentional, time-limited, repeatable,
non-goal-directed action (*have a swim, have a chat, have a stroll*); *take*-constructions imply an
external goal with a specific beginning/end (*take a walk, take a last long look*).

**Thematic-role flexibility.** English subjects/objects are unusually loosely tied to any single
thematic role — the chapter catalogues subjects realized as Instrument (*this advert will sell us a lot
of dog food*), Location (*the road was rustling with dead leaves*), Source (*the dead bird was dripping
blood*), Theme (*the trial cannot proceed*), Time (*tomorrow will be cloudy*), and Possessor (*the car
burst a tyre*) — each paraphrasable with a more "European" argument-structure alternative (*he will sell
a lot of dog food with this advert*, etc.). This flexibility is framed as a functional compensation for
English's otherwise rigid word order: subject-slot flexibility is what lets English vary its sentence
*topic* freely despite fixed SVO constituent order.

### The Sentence (§17.4 cont'd, pp.553–556)

English is a strict SVO language in both main and subordinate declarative clauses, and in subject
imperatives. Deviations ("inversion") serve roughly three functions: (1) **Subject-Auxiliary Inversion**
distinguishes non-declarative sentence types — obligatory in all polar/exclamative interrogatives, and
in constituent (*wh*-) interrogatives unless the subject itself is questioned; also in a narrow,
auxiliary-restricted set of conditional protases (*should, had, were* only: *had I known this, I would
not have gone*) — two of these three modal auxiliaries cannot otherwise be used in questions at all. (2)
A **semantic/scope-based inversion** triggered by initial-position negative or downward-entailing
adverbials (*never have I seen such a sight*), contingent specifically on the adverbial having scope
over the *whole* sentence rather than just a phrase — shown by genuine minimal pairs that differ only in
intonation and inversion. (3) A **discourse-pragmatic inversion** involving main (not just auxiliary)
verbs, triggered by preposing locative/directional adverbials or non-finite progressive material, which
shifts a new discourse-referent-introducing subject into clause-final focus position (*round the bend
came a long train*; *down the cobbled lane walked a strange-looking man*). Beyond inversion, English's
otherwise inflexible theme/rheme-presentation options are extended by cleft constructions (*what he
lost was the keys*; *it was the keys that he lost*) and existential/presentational *there*-constructions
(*there is a man following us*), the latter built on a small closed set of locational/existential verbs
(*arrive, come, appear, go, emerge, rule, wave, blow*) and showing genuine (if partial and
dialect-variable) subject-hood properties for existential *there* itself (it precedes the finite verb,
inverts with auxiliaries, and participates in Raising constructions like *I expect there to be no
problems*), even though verb agreement in these constructions is still generally governed by the
following NP (*there were no students present*), with some spoken varieties instead defaulting to
invariant *there's*.

**Non-declarative sentence types.** All polar interrogatives and most constituent interrogatives
(except subject-questions) invert; separate, morphologically simplex interrogative pronouns exist for
animate/inanimate, subject/object (*who, whose, who(m)* vs. *which*), temporal (*when*), locative
(*where*), causal (*why*), and manner (*how*) questions. Preposition-stranding is the default strategy
for questioning the object of a PP or a genitive-embedded NP, with fronting of the whole PP/complex NP
as the marked alternative; unbounded dependencies (see Relative Clauses above) apply to interrogatives
too. **Question tags** — reduced interrogative clauses appended to declaratives/imperatives, consisting
of an auxiliary plus a pronominal copy of the main-clause subject — are a specific English feature,
coming in reversed-polarity (*she is very happy, isn't she?*, found in all varieties) and
constant-polarity (*you know him, do you?*, British-only) forms, the latter typically used to express an
inference based on observed behavior. Imperatives typically lack an overt subject but can take one as a
fronted constituent before the verb (*you take the left side!*); the invariable passive-imperative
auxiliary is *get*, never *be* (*get lost!*, *don't get run over by a car!*) — a further contrast with
the rest of Germanic.

### Subordination (§17.4 cont'd, pp.557–560)

Unlike many other Germanic languages, English shows no word-order difference between main and
subordinate clauses, so coordination/subordination must be diagnosed by other criteria (structural
embedding, anaphoric dependency, clause mobility, etc.). Finite subordinate clauses are introduced by
complementizers (*that, whether, what, how, if*) selected by the matrix clause type. A genuinely
English-specific non-finite construction is the *for*...*to* subject/object clause (*for people to love
their children is common*), historically a reanalysis of a prepositional *for*-phrase complement as the
subordinate clause's own subject-introducer — shown by the fact that *for* and *that* can co-occur
side-by-side in the same sentence without redundancy (*it is good for me for my children to go to
Stanford*) and by a real semantic contrast between *for*-clauses (denoting something virtual/possible)
and corresponding *that*-clauses (naming an actual instance); *for*-clauses are more common in American
than British English, and some British speakers reject them outright in certain positions.

**Infinitival object clauses** split into verbs selecting only an infinitival complement (*try, manage,
hate*) vs. verbs selecting both a nominal object and an infinitival complement (*persuade, convince*),
the latter further subdividing by whether the embedded "understood subject" is genuinely syntactically
raised from the lower clause ("Raising"/"Exceptional Case Marking" verbs: *intend, suppose, expect,
guarantee, believe, assume*) or is a true object controlling a separate embedded subject
(*persuade, convince*) — distinguished by whether passivizing the embedded clause preserves meaning
(it does for Raising verbs, changes meaning for control verbs) and whether *there* can replace the
"object" (only for Raising verbs). Raising is markedly more acceptable in gerundial/participial contexts
than in finite main clauses, and more acceptable in relative/interrogative clauses than main declaratives
— a genuine gradient rather than a binary grammaticality judgment. A further specifically-English
construction: infinitive-taking monovalent ("tough") adjectives where the superficial subject is
understood as the *underlying object* of the embedded verb (*Fred is hard to miss*).

**Gerunds** (nominal gerund phrases) are presented as a specifically English phenomenon within Germanic,
hybrid in category: noun-phrase-like in external distribution (can be subjects, direct objects,
prepositional objects) but verb-phrase-like in internal syntax (combine with bare VP objects, the full
auxiliary range, and clausal negation *not*) and in one syntactic test — unlike ordinary NPs, gerund
phrases can undergo Subject-Auxiliary Inversion (*would my leaving now be taken as an admission of
guilt?*). A large class of matrix verbs selecting gerund complements are factive (*regret, resent,
welcome, enjoy* — the gerund's proposition is presupposed true); others (*risk, consider, contemplate,
avoid*) are not, and there is no perfect match between verb class and whether a gerund or some other
complement type is selected.

**Adverbial clauses and three types of purpose clause.** Introduced by *if, because/since, when/while,
although/even though, so as/in order to/that*; several connective expressions (*before, after, since,
until, for*) double as both prepositions and conjunctions. The chapter distinguishes three structurally
distinct purpose-clause types, a genuine typological nicety: the finite "rationale clause"
(*in order to, so as to, so that* — *we made nothing in order not to be recognized as foreigners*); and
two further non-finite types requiring a structural gap, either in subject position (*she hired a nurse
to look after her mother*) or non-subject position (*he bought a skillet to fry eggs in*) — unlike
rationale clauses, these gap-requiring purpose clauses cannot be preposed and face severe restrictions
on the matrix verb.

**Non-finite adverbial constructions** (free adjuncts and absolutes) are another area of extensive
English use within Germanic: free adjuncts (same-subject converbs: *taking my car out of the garage, I
noticed that a window was broken*) leave the subject position unexpressed, understood as identical to
the main clause's subject (though "dangling"-participle violations of this, frowned upon
prescriptively, do occur: *even allowing for fast driving, twenty minutes was optimistic*); absolute
constructions (different-subject converbs: *no further discussion arising, the meeting was brought to a
close*) have an overt subject of their own, typically standing in a part-whole or other close relation
to the main-clause subject. Both types are vague/unspecified in their semantic relation to the main
clause (conditional, causal, temporal, or concessive), resolved contextually by syntactic, semantic, and
pragmatic factors — illustrated by a genuine minimal pair (*Mary enjoyed the idea of going home…,
knowing/not knowing that her uncle would be there*) where polarity flips the interpretation from causal
to concessive.

### Negation (§17.5 boundary, pp.561–562)

Negation is expressed by the adverb *not*, the determiner *no*, or a family of complex negative
expressions (*nobody, nothing, none, neither, never, nowhere, no way*). Negative-marked sentences show
a distinct syntactic profile: they license negative-polarity items (*ever, at all, any, so much as, let
alone*); allow positive question tags; allow continuation with *not even*/*neither*/*nor*; and trigger
Subject-Auxiliary Inversion if the negative expression occupies initial position. Expressions like
*hardly, rarely, seldom, barely, only, little, few* pattern identically and can be considered "negative
words" by these tests even without surface *not/no*. *Not* has a uniquely restricted distributional
history: it could still follow main lexical verbs in Early Modern English (*I know thee not*), but in
Modern English only follows the first auxiliary — the grammaticalization of *do*-support to host *not*
began in the seventeenth century. *Not* and *no* differ subtly in meaning even in apparently
near-synonymous minimal pairs: *no* tends toward denying the contrary proposition, *not* toward denying
the contradictory relative to an underlying affirmative; *no* tends to introduce new information rather
than negate previously-given information. Negative prefixes (*un-, in-, non-*) never take clausal scope.
Scope among multiple scope-bearing expressions in a sentence is generally resolved by linear
precedence (leftmost item takes widest scope), though intonational phrasing can override this where
linear order alone is ambiguous. English negation is frequently reinforced for emphasis by lexical means
(*I didn't sleep a wink*) or, in older/non-standard varieties, by **multiple negation/negative concord**
— *you ain't seen nothing yet* — a pattern the eighteenth century prescriptively eliminated from the
standard register but which remains obligatory in Black English (*you better not never steal nothing
from me*), explicitly distinguished by the source from mere double negation as a genuine, rule-governed
grammatical feature of that variety, not a deficient form of Standard English.

### Lexis (§17.5, pp.562–565)

English's vocabulary is unusually large (major dictionaries list roughly half a million distinct words)
because it draws on two major historical stocks, Germanic and Romance, whose coexistence is most
visible in the systematic near-synonym pairs tabulated above (*go on/continue*, etc.) — Germanic-stock
items trending toward phrasal-verb form and colloquial register, Romance-stock items trending toward
single-word form and formal register, a genuinely productive register-stratification axis. Pre-Conquest
borrowing came mainly from Latin and other Germanic tongues (notably Scandinavian — recapitulating the
*garden/yard*-type doublets from `pt01.md`'s Ch.5); Renaissance and later borrowing drew extensively
from Latin plus French, Spanish, Italian, Greek, German, Yiddish, Japanese, Russian, Chinese, and
Arabic, among others — English is characterized as unusually open and flexible in integrating loanwords
from any period onward. A striking structural property: heavy **morphological attrition** has left many
phonological forms assignable to more than one word class with no formal marking (*right* spans
N/V/Adj/Adv; *light* spans N/V/Adj; *book, border, burn, risk, stand* span N/V) — formalized as the word-
formation process **conversion** ("zero derivation"), which changes word class without any accompanying
morphological change, sometimes (but not always) accompanied by a stress shift (*re'fill* v. vs.
*'refill* n.) and generally semantically predictable from input word class (N>V: *to butter* 'put/store
into a bottle'; V>N: *a call, a guess, a touch*; Adj>V: *to dirty, to empty, to clean*; Adj>N:
*a regular, a daily, blacks*). Beyond conversion, ordinary derivational affixation differentiates
related word-class members while staying semantically/formally related (*indicate–indicative of–
indication*, etc. — see Vocabulary table), and the same broader set of word-formation processes found
across Germanic are all active in English: compounding, prefixation, suffixation, blending, and
clipping (examples in Vocabulary table). A separate lexical-subsystem discussion covers: a class of
labile/"ergative" verbs usable both transitively and intransitively with matching selectional
restrictions (*burn, change, break, grow* — the "inchoative-causative alternation"); the deictic system
(two-term proximal/distal as a live system — *this/that, here/there, now/then* — with a vestigial,
archaic/regional third distal term *yon/yonder*, and an almost entirely obsolete directional-deixis
subsystem *hither/hence, thither/thence, whither/whence*, surviving only in a few temporal/epistemic
fossils); the *come*/*go* deictic-motion contrast, extended metaphorically to evaluative/epistemic
uses (*go crazy* vs. *come to one's senses*); rich lexical differentiation in some semantic domains
(copula-of-change verbs: *become annoyed, get angry, grow old, go stale, come alive*) contrasted with
comparative lexical poverty in others (dressing/undressing verbs: English's bare *put on/take off,
don/doff* vs. German's much richer garment-type-sensitive verb set); and, finally, the notable
**complete absence of modal particles** anywhere in English — a genuine typological gap within Germanic,
where German/Dutch/Swedish each have a productive, largely mutually-untranslatable set of discourse
particles (*ja, doch, eben, mal*, etc.) that English instead covers piecemeal via a smaller set of focus
particles (*also, too, even, only, merely*), prosody, tags, and emphatic *do*.

## Grammar points

### The comparative method and regular sound correspondence (Ch. 3)

Family relationships between languages are established not by surface word-*similarity* but by
**regular, systematic phonological correspondence** across many words — illustrated with
English/German/Swedish *stone/Stein/sten*, *bone/Bein/ben*, etc. (Table 3.1), where the vowel
correspondence (Southern British /əʊ/ ↔ German /aɪ/ ↔ Swedish /eː/) recurs across the whole set,
not just one pair. The book works through a genuinely useful diagnostic case: Modern English *one*
looks like it breaks the expected vowel correspondence with *stone*/*bone*/etc. (it should rhyme
with *stone* on regular sound-law grounds — compare *alone*, *atone*, which really are historically
"all one"/"at one" and do show the expected vowel), but this is explained as a dialect-mixing
accident, not sound-law failure: the pronunciation of *one* was borrowed from a different
contemporary dialect pronunciation than the one that regularly produced *stone*. **Loanwords** are
flagged as the main confound the comparative method must filter out before drawing conclusions —
worked example: Old Norse *bátr* looks like it should be the regular Scandinavian cognate of OE
*bāt* "boat," but the real regular Old Norse form is the rarer poetic *beitr*; *bátr* is actually a
loanword borrowed wholesale from Old English.

### Core vocabulary vs. loanword vulnerability (Ch. 3, applied again in Ch. 4)

A recurring methodological point: **closed-class grammatical words, kinship terms for the closest
relatives, and body-part terms resist borrowing far more than general vocabulary does**, so they're
disproportionately useful evidence for family relationships. Demonstrated by showing that
English and French share enormous surface vocabulary (*people*/*peuple*, *battle*/*bataille*,
*change*/*changer*) but these are all post-Conquest French loanwords that vanish if you look
further back in English's own recorded history — whereas English's core grammatical words (*the*,
*and*, *is*), numerals, closest-kin terms (*father*, *mother*, *brother*, *son*) and body-part terms
(*head*, *foot*, *arm*, *hand*) are inherited Germanic vocabulary, and it's these that correctly
diagnose English as Germanic, not Romance, despite the superficial French-looking vocabulary
overlap.

### Indo-European branches and their diagnostic features (Ch. 3)

The book surveys the IE family's branches (Indo-Iranian/Aryan, Greek, Italic, Celtic, Baltic,
Slavonic, Germanic, plus the minor/extinct branches Albanian, Armenian, Tocharian, Anatolian/
Hittite) and some diagnostic phonological splits within them: **Kentum vs. Satem** (treatment of
PIE palatal *k, the East/West split used above as a first-order family-tree division); **P-Celtic
vs. Q-Celtic** (Britannic/Gaulish treatment of PIE *kʷ as /p/, e.g. Welsh *pen* "head," vs. Goidelic/
Gaelic treatment as /k/, e.g. Old Irish *cenn*); Celtic's loss of PIE *p entirely in most positions
(Welsh *pump* "five" is the exception, since it's from PIE *kʷ-, not *p-, so it isn't actually a
counterexample). Two branches sharing a verb-inflection in *-r* for the passive/deponent (Italic and
Celtic: Latin *amātur* "he/she/it is loved," Welsh *cerir fi* "I am loved") is given as a worked
example of using shared morphological innovation, not just vocabulary, to group branches.

### Family-tree construction has known structural limits (Ch. 3)

The book is explicit that the branching-tree model is an idealization: some languages (Greek,
Sanskrit; Greek, Iranian) resemble a language on a "remote" branch about as much as their official
closer relatives, no amount of branching can fully capture this, and the proposed fix is to model
Proto-Indo-European itself as a dialect continuum with independently-spreading innovations (shown
concretely via the Northumberland/Cumberland/Durham isogloss map, where the *house*/*hoose*
pronunciation boundary and the *flea*/*lop* lexical boundary cross each other rather than coinciding)
— i.e. the same dialect-geography logic the book already used for Modern English dialects in Ch. 2
is extended to explain prehistoric Indo-European's own internal structure, including why a
geographically "Eastern" language like Tocharian can show "Western" Kentum phonology.

### The Proto-Indo-European homeland debate (Ch. 3)

Presented as unresolved, with four competing proposals surveyed (not endorsed): the Scandinavian
theory (now largely discredited; no reconstructable PIE word for "sea," poor fit for horse-based
steppe culture, and the book flags its historical entanglement with pan-Germanicist/National
Socialist ideology as a reason for extra scholarly caution around it); Renfrew's Anatolian theory
(c. 7000 BC, spreading via agricultural expansion rather than conquest); the Kurgan hypothesis
(Gimbutas, building on Childe; steppes north of the Caspian/Black Sea, c. 4000 BC, spreading via
migration/conquest); and a 2003 Gray–Atkinson glottochronological re-analysis that the book notes
reopened support for the Anatolian dating despite the chapter's general skepticism toward
glottochronology as a method. The evidentiary method throughout is **vocabulary reconstruction**:
words widely shared across IE branches and not plausibly loanwords indicate concepts/objects the
speech community actually had (horses, sheep/cattle as high-status wealth, metal tools, a word for
"daughter-in-law" but not "son-in-law," evidence of patrilocal marriage) — but the book flags two
real risks in this method: a word's *absence* doesn't prove the concept was unknown (vocabulary loss
happens in all languages), and a shared word can itself be a shared loanword from a third source
rather than inherited PIE vocabulary.

### Proto-Germanic's innovations relative to Proto-Indo-European (Ch. 4) — the chapter's throughline

The book organizes Chapter 4 around a list of the specific ways Proto-Germanic innovated away from
its PIE parent, each individually important for later chapters:

- **Accent fixation**: PIE's mobile pitch-accent (which could fall on any syllable depending on
  inflection — demonstrated with Classical Greek *mḗtēr*/*mētéros*/*mētrós* "mother," same root,
  three different accent positions) was replaced in Proto-Germanic by a fixed, predominantly stress
  (not pitch) accent on a word's first syllable. This had the major downstream consequence of
  **progressive weakening and loss of unstressed syllables** (demonstrated with PIE *\*bheronom*
  "to bear" → PG *\*beranan* → OE *beran* → ME *beren* → *bere* → Modern *bear*, shedding a whole
  syllable per major period).
- **Grimm's Law / First Sound-Shifting** (table above): PIE aspirated voiced stops *bh/*dh/*gh
  became PG plain voiced stops b/d/g (Latin *frāter* ~ English *brother*); PIE voiced stops b/d/g
  became PG voiceless stops p/t/k (Latin *decem* ~ English *ten*; Latin *genus* ~ English *kin*);
  PIE voiceless stops p/t/k became PG voiceless fricatives f/θ/h (Latin *pedem* ~ English *foot*;
  Latin *trēs* ~ English *three*; Latin *centum* ~ English *hund(red)*/Gothic *hund*). The book is
  careful to flag that Brugmann's 19th-century reconstruction of the PIE "aspirated voiced stop" row
  is itself contested (Gamkrelidze & Ivanov's glottalic-stop reanalysis, informed by Hittite
  evidence discovered after Brugmann), presenting this as live, unresolved scholarly debate rather
  than settled fact.
- **Verner's Law**: a secondary voicing of PG voiceless fricatives when the preceding PIE syllable
  was unstressed — explains the OE *snīþan* "cut" / *sniden* (past participle) alternation, and is
  dated by the book as necessarily predating the later fixing of Germanic stress on the first
  syllable (since Verner's Law itself depends on a still-mobile PIE-style accent to condition it).
- **Vowel changes**: PIE short *o* > PG *a* (Latin *octō* ~ Gothic *ahtau* "eight"); PIE long *ā* >
  PG long *ō* (Latin *māter* ~ Old English *mōdor* "mother"). **Ablaut/gradation** is presented as
  inherited from PIE, not a PG innovation, but its grade system (e-grade/o-grade/zero-grade) is
  what later surfaces as the Modern English strong-verb vowel alternation (PIE *\*sengʷh-/\*songʷh-/
  \*sngʷh-* → PG *\*sing-/\*sang-/\*sung-* → OE *singan/sang/sungon/gesungen* → Modern *sing/sang/
  sung*, with the e→i change before a following /ng/ noted as a routine combinative sound change
  layered on top of the inherited ablaut pattern).
- **Grammatical simplification relative to PIE** (but still far more inflected than Modern English):
  case system reduced from PIE's 8 (or more) down to 5–6 in Proto-Germanic; dual number reduced to
  vestigial status (Old English still shows dual pronoun forms: *wit* "we two," *git* "you two," as
  a genuine fossil of the fuller PIE system); a new **weak adjective declension** emerged alongside
  the inherited **strong** one, context-conditioned (strong after nothing/indefinite context, weak
  after a determiner or possessive) — still productive in Modern Swedish/German, lost as a live
  category in Modern English though the book notes the strong/weak distinction is still visible in
  Old English (*gōd mann*/*se gōda mann*).
- **Weak verbs**: an entirely new PG-innovated verb class (dental-suffix past tense, *-d*/*-t*/
  *-ida*/*-ōda*/*-aida* depending on daughter language) alongside the inherited PIE-derived strong
  (ablaut) verbs — the book flags the origin of the weak-verb dental suffix as genuinely uncertain
  (one proposed account derives it from a fused auxiliary "to do," by rough analogy with "walked"
  developing out of "walk did," but states no single theory explains all the facts). **This is the
  single most consequential grammatical development covered in this chapter for later chapters**:
  the weak pattern becomes the fully productive, default verb-formation pattern for all new English
  verbs ever since (16th-century Latin/French loans *imitate*/*invite*; the book's own example of a
  then-recent coinage, *blog* → *blogged*), while strong verbs are a closed, shrinking inherited
  class — OE *helpan* "help" was originally strong (past *healp*) and has since gone weak
  (*helped*), a specific, concrete instance of the leveling-toward-weak trend.

### The three Germanic branches and English's position among them (Ch. 4)

Proto-Germanic split into **North Germanic** (Old Norse → modern Scandinavian languages),
**East Germanic** (Gothic and its relatives — the only branch to go fully extinct, no modern
descendants), and **West Germanic** (Old High German/High German, Old Saxon/Low German, Dutch,
Frisian, English). Within West Germanic, **Frisian is identified as English's closest living
relative**, with a hypothesized shared "Anglo-Frisian" prehistoric stage. Diagnostic features cited
per branch: North Germanic's postposed definite article (Swedish *hunden* "the dog," article fused
onto the noun, vs. English's separate preposed *the*) and loss of PG initial /j-/ and /w-/ before
rounded vowels (Old Icelandic *ár* vs. English *year*; *ormr* "snake" vs. English *worm*); West
Germanic's development of diphthongs where North/East Germanic kept a pure vowel (Old Norse *hǫggva*
vs. OE *hēawan* "to cut, hew"); and East Germanic (Gothic)'s distinctive retention of PG short /e/
and /o/ as /i/ and /u/ (Gothic *stilan* vs. OE/OHG *stelan* "to steal"). The book also notes
Gothic's inflectional passive voice (*haitada* "I am called," a synthetic passive with no equivalent
surviving in Old English, which instead already used a periphrastic "be" + past participle
construction).

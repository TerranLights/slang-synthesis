## Grammar points

### Ablaut: patterned vowel variation marking tense
**Ablaut** (German term; English "vowel gradation") is the patterned, non-arbitrary variation of a
verb's stem vowel across the paradigm, carrying the grammatical information (present vs. past
singular vs. past plural vs. past participle) that weak verbs instead carry via a dental suffix.
PDE strong verbs like *sing~sang~sung* and *ride~rode~ridden* follow genuinely different Ablaut
patterns (they don't rhyme), because they continue different Germanic Ablaut series.

### The seven strong verb classes and their structural analysis
OE strong verbs fall into seven classes (I–VII), of which I–VI show a reasonably regular, related
system of Ablaut, while class VII is a separate, less regular group identified by past-tense stem
vowel (*ēo* or *ē*) rather than by any Ablaut pattern proper. The book analyzes the stem
phonologically as **onset + nucleus + coda**, decomposing the nucleus into an **Ablaut vowel**
(varying: full-grade front/back, reduced grade = schwa or zero) plus a fixed **contextual
element** that differs by class (/i/ for class I, /u/ for class II, a sonorant /l,r,m,n/ for class
III, etc.) — the contextual element is the actual structural dividing line between classes, not
the surface vowel alone. Classes IV and V show an additional **lengthened grade** in the past
plural. Two types of **gradation** combine: **qualitative** (vowel quality changes, e/a alternation)
and **quantitative** (vowel length/presence changes, full vs. reduced/zero).

### Verner's Law
Discovered by 19th-century linguist Karl Verner: a voiceless fricative between voiced sounds
became voiced **provided the preceding syllable was not stressed/accented** — f→v, θ→ð, x→ɣ, s→z.
Because Germanic stress later regularized onto the first syllable of every word, but the original
conditioning stress pattern had been different (and variable, including for past-tense forms),
Verner's Law alternations survive as apparently-arbitrary consonant alternations within a single
verb's paradigm (*snīþan~sniden*, *cēosan~coren*) — further obscured in OE by later independent
sound changes (ð→d; z→r, the latter called **rhotacism**). Verner's Law alternations have almost
entirely disappeared from PDE, with *was~were* as one of the only visible survivors.

### Contracted verbs
Where the ablaut series would predict a stem ending in the voiceless velar fricative /x/
(spelled `<h>`), intervocalic weakening and eventual loss of /h/ left the stem vowel and
inflectional vowel adjacent, merging into a diphthong. These **contracted verbs** always have
infinitives ending in *-ēon* and behave somewhat irregularly, visible for example in *sēon* "see"
and *bēon* "be" sharing that same infinitive shape for unrelated historical reasons.

### I-mutation and other variation within the strong verb system
I-mutation (introduced fully in `pt02`) surfaces in the 2nd/3rd person singular present of most
strong verb classes (*helpan→hilpst*), though class I verbs (already *ī* in the stem) are
unaffected since there is no vowel left to mutate. A small set of strong verbs show **"weak
presents"** — a present tense built on the weak class 1 pattern despite the verb remaining strong
overall (*sittan, hliehhan*); the book explicitly warns against over-interpreting this as an
intermediate stage of weak-conjugation drift, since the historical outcomes of the individual
verbs involved don't bear that out. **Analogy** can also reshape a strong verb's present tense
independently of sound change, as in *brūcan*'s unexpected *-ū-*, formally derivable as a genuine
proportional analogy (ī:i :: ū:u) rather than mere "irregularity."

### Modal and preterite-present verbs
Most of what PDE calls **modal verbs** (*can, dare, may, must, shall, will*) descend from a
Germanic category called **preterite-present** verbs: their present-tense morphology is, in
origin, an old strong-verb *past*-tense form that was reanalyzed as present in meaning, which then
required inventing an entirely new (weak, dental-suffixed) past tense to replace the one that had
been repurposed. This explains the otherwise-mysterious shape of e.g. *cann* (originally a class
III past tense, cf. *sang*) and the lack of a final *-s* on 3rd person singular *he can* (preterite-
presents never took the regular present-tense -(e)þ ending). Not every preterite-present verb
became a modal (e.g. *witan* "know" kept full non-modal syntax and both participles), and the
modal category itself was not yet fully stabilized in OE — some verbs show modal syntax without
preterite-present morphology (*wile*), and the author stresses that mapping OE's category onto
PDE's "modal verb" category requires a deliberate health-warning rather than a clean one-to-one
correspondence.

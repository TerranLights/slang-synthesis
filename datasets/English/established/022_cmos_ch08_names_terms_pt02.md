# English — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Chapter 8, "Names and Terms," Part 2 (§§8.22–8.47)

**Source:** University of Chicago Press Staff, *The Chicago Manual of Style* (16th edition, 2010),
Chapter 8, "Names and Terms" — "Titles and Offices" through "Names of Places" (Parts of the World)
sections, §§8.22–8.47, PDF pages 1025–1050 (continues from Part 1, which covers the earlier portion
of Chapter 8; Part 1 had not yet been extracted as of this writing, so no cross-check against it was
possible — see coverage note below).

**Coverage note:** every grammar point (§§8.22–8.47, 26 numbered rules) and every distinct
illustrative term CMOS uses to establish a naming/capitalization convention in this page range. This
section of the chapter is a nomenclature guide — titles of rulers, military/religious/academic/noble
titles, honorifics, epithets, kinship names, ethnic/national/socioeconomic group terms, and
geographic region names/epithets — so, unlike a mechanics-only chapter (compare Chapter 7), the
example terms themselves *are* the substantive lexical content here, not incidental illustration of
an unrelated rule. Extraction is therefore broader than the Chapter 7 precedent: the vocabulary table
below captures essentially every distinct generic title/epithet/group-term CMOS introduces, but omits
the specific real-world proper names used purely as carriers for those terms (e.g., "Jonathan M.
Hall, chair of the Department of Classics" contributes the term *chair*, not the name "Jonathan M.
Hall," to the table). Repeated application of an already-tabled term to a second real-world example
within the same rule is skipped. This source has a clean text layer throughout (no vision-reading was
required); Vision Reading Confidence is `n/a` for every row below.

## Grammar points

### 8.22 Titles of sovereigns and other rulers

Most titles of sovereigns and other rulers (king, queen, emperor, shah, sharif, kaiser, etc.) are
lowercased when used alone, even though capitalized as part of a full title-plus-name ("King Abdullah
II" but "the king of Jordan"). Two titles restricted to a single historical figure — *führer* (Adolf
Hitler) and *Il Duce* (Benito Mussolini) — stay capitalized even used alone, an explicit exception to
the general lowercase-alone pattern. See also §8.31 for titles of nobility, a related but distinct
category.

### 8.23 Military titles

As with civil titles, military titles are "routinely capitalized" within the literature of the
organization/government they belong to, but in formal academic prose most such titles are capitalized
only as part of a name, with occasional exceptions if ambiguity would otherwise result. Abbreviations
for titles preceding a name are covered elsewhere (§10.15).

### 8.24 Quasi-military titles

Titles/ranks in organizations such as the police, the merchant marine, or the Salvation Army follow
the same rule as true military titles (capitalize with the name, lowercase alone).

### 8.25 Religious titles

Religious titles are treated "much like civil and military titles" (§§8.21, 8.23) — capitalized
immediately before a name, lowercased used alone. One flagged exception: *Dalai Lama* traditionally
stays capitalized even alone, *but* the generic plural ("previous dalai lamas") is lowercased.

### 8.26 Corporate and organizational titles

Titles of corporate/organizational officeholders are "rarely used as part of a name" in running text.
When a short form is needed, writers should use either the generic title or simply the person's name,
not a capitalized title-plus-name construction as with civil/military/religious titles.

### 8.27 Academic titles

Academic titles generally follow the civil-titles pattern of §8.21. A named professorship (an endowed
chair carrying a donor's/honoree's name) is capitalized in its full form but the short form reverts to
plain "Professor [Surname]." *Professor emeritus* (masc.) / *emerita* (fem.) / plural *emeriti*
(masc., or masc.+fem.) / *emeritae* (fem.) are explicitly flagged as honorary designations that do not
simply mean "retired."

### 8.28 Other academic designations

Terms denoting student status (freshman or first-year student, sophomore, junior, senior) are
lowercased. Names of degrees and fellowships, referred to generically, are likewise lowercased (a
master's degree, a doctorate, a fellowship), even though a standard abbreviation like *MBA* is
capitalized. See also §10.20 for abbreviation conventions.

### 8.29 Descriptive titles

A generic descriptive title that describes a person's role or occupation and precedes a personal name
— such as *philosopher* or *historian* — is lowercased and treated as if in grammatical apposition to
the name, e.g. "the historian William McNeill," not "Historian McNeill." Compare §8.27, where true
academic titles (Professor, Provost) *are* capitalized before a name.

### 8.30 Civic and academic honors

Titles denoting civic or academic honors are capitalized when they immediately follow a personal name
("Laurence L. Bongie, Fellow of the Royal Society of Canada"), but the generic/collective reference
("the fellows") is lowercased. For capitalization of the honor/award names themselves, see §8.82; for
abbreviations, §10.21.

### 8.31 Titles of nobility

Unlike the title categories above (which denote an office, such as a presidency or admiralship),
titles of nobility — whether inherited or conferred — form "an integral and, with rare exceptions,
permanent part of a person's name" and are therefore usually capitalized even as short forms. The
generic element of the title (duke, earl, etc.) used alone as a short form of the name, however, is
lowercased — with a further British-specific wrinkle: the short form stays capitalized for *royal*
dukes but not *nonroyal* ones, a distinction North American usage generally disregards. Marquesses,
earls, viscounts, and baronesses/baronets are addressed and referred to, after first mention, as "Lord
or Lady So-and-So," at least in British usage. CMOS points to *The Times Style and Usage Guide* and
*Burke's Peerage, Baronetage, and Knightage* for fuller detail, and gives a worked example from the
latter illustrating the full complexity of British noble nomenclature (a single peer holding a
marquessate, an earldom, a viscountcy, and a barony simultaneously, plus a claim to a separate
barony).

### 8.32 Honorifics

Honorific titles and other respectful forms of address are capitalized in any context — e.g. "the
Honorable," "Her (His, Your) Majesty," "Your (Her, His) Royal Highness," "Your (Her, His) Excellency,"
"Mr./Mrs./Ms. President," "Madam Speaker," "Your Honor." *Sir*, *ma'am*, *my lord*, and *my lady* are
flagged as explicit lowercase exceptions to this otherwise-capitalized pattern. *Merriam-Webster's
Collegiate Dictionary*'s "Forms of Address" appendix is cited as a fuller list; see also §10.18 for
abbreviations and §8.25 for the parallel religious-title treatment.

### 8.33 Epithets (or nicknames) and bynames

A descriptive or characterizing word/phrase used as part of, or instead of, a person's name is
capitalized (the Great Emancipator, the Sun King, Old Hickory). An initial "the" that is part of such
a name is lowercased except at the start of a sentence. When an epithet is used *in addition to* a
real name rather than instead of it, it is set off in quotation marks and placed within or after the
name — parentheses are explicitly described as unnecessary for this purpose (George Herman "Babe"
Ruth, not "George Herman (Babe) Ruth").

### 8.34 Epithets as names of characters

In references to works of drama or fiction specifically, epithets or generic titles used in place of
a character's actual name are normally capitalized (Chief Executioner, the Red Queen, the Mad Hatter)
— a narrower, genre-specific extension of the general epithet rule in §8.33.

### 8.35 Kinship names

Kinship names are lowercased unless they immediately precede a personal name (Aunt Maud) or are used
alone in place of one (Dad, Grandmother). Used in apposition, however, such names revert to lowercase
even directly adjacent to the name they modify — "she adores her aunt Maud" is explicitly contrasted
with "Let's write to Aunt Maud." Kinship terms connected with a religious office or calling are
treated the same way ("a certain Brother Thomas").

### 8.36 Personifications

The poetic device of giving abstractions the attributes of persons — and capitalizing them
accordingly (e.g., in a quoted line of verse, "the Night is Mother of the Day") — is described as
"rare in today's writing," and CMOS recommends confining such capitalization to quoted material
rather than an author's own original prose (contrast ordinary lowercase usage: "nature is at its
best," "a battle between head and heart").

### 8.37 Ethnic and national groups and associated adjectives

Names of ethnic and national groups are capitalized, as are adjectives derived from them. CMOS flags
a live naming-preference debate explicitly: many people who trace their roots to the Americas'
aboriginal peoples prefer *American Indian* to *Native American*, and in certain historical works
*Indians* alone may be the more appropriate term; Canadians, by contrast, often use *First Peoples*
(and *First Nations*). For hyphenation of compound forms like *African American*, see §8.38.

### 8.38 Compound nationalities

Whether compound ethnic/national terms (African American, Italian American, Chinese American, and
similar) should be spelled open or hyphenated has been "the subject of considerable controversy," with
some regarding the hyphen as suggestive of bias. CMOS itself "doubts that hyphenation represents
bias," but notes the hyphen also doesn't aid comprehension in such terms, so it may be omitted unless
a particular publisher requires it. Cross-references a related table at §7.85, section 2 ("proper
nouns and adjectives relating to geography or nationality").

### 8.39 Color

Common color-based designations of ethnic groups (black, white, people of color) are usually
lowercased unless a particular publisher or author prefers otherwise — an explicit house-style
variability flag rather than a single fixed rule.

### 8.40 Class

Terms denoting socioeconomic classes or groups (the middle class, blue-collar workers, the
aristocracy, the proletariat, homeless people) are lowercased.

### 8.41 Generation

Terms denoting generations (the me generation, baby boomer(s)/baby busters, generation X/Y/Z, the MTV
generation) are "best lowercased."

### 8.42 Physical characteristics

Terms describing groups or individuals by a physical characteristic or a disability (wheelchair users,
blind persons, deaf children) are usually lowercased. CMOS flags a genuine identity-marked exception:
some writers capitalize *Deaf* specifically when referring to people who identify as members of the
distinct linguistic/cultural group whose primary language is American Sign Language (the Deaf
community), while keeping lowercase *deaf* for people who have a hearing loss generally, or who prefer
oral communication methods. See also §11.144.

### 8.43 Names of places — additional resources

For the spelling of place-names generally, CMOS points to *Merriam-Webster's Geographical Dictionary*,
while noting that even a current reference edition cannot track every change in country/city names and
that writers may disagree on the "proper" form (e.g., Burma vs. Myanmar). The US Central Intelligence
Agency's continually updated *World Factbook* is recommended as a starting point for country names
specifically. For historical works, writers/editors should use the place-name form appropriate to the
historical period under discussion, not necessarily the current name.

### 8.44 Continents, countries, cities, oceans, and such

Entities that appear on maps are always capitalized, as are adjectives and nouns derived from them
(Asia/Asian, Ireland/Irish, Chicago/Chicagoan). An initial "the" as part of a place-name is lowercased
in running text, except in the rare case where "The" genuinely forms part of a city's own proper name
(The Hague).

### 8.45 Points of the compass

Compass points and terms derived from them are lowercased when they simply indicate direction or
location (a north wind, an eastward move, in the southwest of France). See §8.46 for the contrasting
case where a compass-derived term instead names a specific cultural/political/historical region and is
capitalized.

### 8.46 Regions of the world and national regions

Terms denoting world or national regions are "often capitalized," following the general principles
sketched in §8.1 but with substantial context-dependent variation; for terms not covered here, or
where no suitable close analogy exists, CMOS directs writers to a dictionary or encyclopedia, noting
that even a dictionary's own house convention (capitalized vs. lowercase-with-a-marked-subentry) may
need to be weighed against an author's strong preference. The section's extended example list
illustrates the principle largely through contrastive pairs showing the same root word capitalized in
a true regional/proper-name sense but lowercased in a directional, generic, or metaphorical sense —
e.g. "Alpine skiing" vs. "alpine pastures in the Rockies"; "Arctic waters" vs. "an arctic stare"; "the
North" (as a named region in American Civil War contexts) vs. ordinary lowercase "north, northern, a
northerner (of a country)"; "the Tropic of Cancer/the Neotropics" vs. lowercase "the subtropics."
Political-versus-geographical framing can also shift capitalization within the same word: "central
Europe when referring to the political division of World War I" is lowercased despite the proper-noun
sense of "Central Europe" used purely geographically.

### 8.47 Popular place-names or epithets

Popular/informal names for places (epithets) are usually capitalized and need no quotation marks.
Several — the Panhandle, the Holy City — are explicitly noted as applying to more than one place, and
CMOS cautions that none of these informal names should be used in a context where they won't be
readily understood by the reader. A distinct subcategory is flagged: certain terms are "considered
political rather than geographical" and therefore need not be capitalized by default (the iron
curtain, the third world), though CMOS allows that editorial discretion may suitably capitalize such
terms in a specifically Soviet-era political context (Iron Curtain, Third World).

## Grammar points

### Parallel structure basics (Ch.18)

When a sentence performs the same grammatical function more than once (e.g. two subjects, or two
items joined by *and*), every instance must share the same grammatical form — an infinitive paired
with another infinitive, not an infinitive paired with a gerund/clause (*"paying full price... and
**that** the cashier treated her rudely"* is nonparallel; *"paying full price... and **being treated**
rudely"* is parallel). The book's practical test: read it aloud — parallel sentences sound balanced,
nonparallel ones sound lopsided. (pp.229–231)

### Avoiding unnecessary shifts in tense, person, and voice (Ch.18)

A sentence shouldn't drift between tenses, between first/second/third person, or between singular and
plural without a meaning-driven reason — an unmotivated shift (e.g. using *you* to mean "I" or
"everyone" mid-sentence) breaks parallelism even though each individual clause may be grammatical on
its own. A shift that's actually part of the intended meaning (*"If I hit you and then he hits
me"*) is fine. (pp.231–234)

### Paired conjunctions: either/or, neither/nor, not only/but also, both/and (Ch.18)

These conjunction pairs must link two elements of identical grammatical type (two nouns, two
noun-verb combinations, two prepositional phrases, etc.) — mismatching one side as a plain phrase and
the other as a full subject-verb clause (*"not only anxious to achieve fame but also she wanted..."*)
breaks the pairing. Checking is done by underlining what each half of the pair actually introduces and
confirming the two match. (pp.234–236)

### Adding introductory elements for sentence variety (Ch.19)

Most English sentences follow a subject-verb(-object) spine, which becomes monotonous if never
varied; opening a sentence instead with an introductory verb form, phrase, or clause (a "clause" here
meaning it has its own subject+verb) adds variety, provided — as in the dangling-modifier rule from
Ch.15 — the main sentence's actual subject is the thing the introduction logically describes. (p.243)

### Subordination to reduce choppiness (Ch.19)

Stringing many short, equal-weight sentences together reads as choppy; folding a lesser idea into a
subordinate clause (which may sit at the start, middle, or end of the main sentence) smooths the
flow and signals which idea is primary. (p.245)

### Reversed sentence order (Ch.19)

Placing the subject after its description/action instead of before it (*"Through the trees... ran the
hungry bear"* instead of *"The hungry bear ran through the trees..."*) creates a "punch line" effect
and is a legitimate occasional variation technique — but should not be achieved via passive voice,
and shouldn't be overused or it becomes its own monotonous pattern. (pp.247–248)

### Eliminating redundancy (Ch.19)

Repetition that adds no new information — doubled near-synonym adjectives (*"calm and serene"*),
unnecessary filler phrases (*"six feet tall in height"*), or restating one idea two different ways
(*"in my opinion I think"*) — should be cut. (p.248)

### Commonly confused word pairs/triplets (Ch.20)

See the Vocabulary table above for the full enumerated set: *affect/effect*, *farther/further*,
*like/as/such as*, *imply/infer*, the countable-vs-mass quantity-word sets (*more than, many, fewer*
vs. *less, much, over*; *amount* vs. *number*; *between* vs. *among*), the *lie/lay* and *sit/set*
intransitive/transitive irregular-verb pairs, and the always-two-word forms (*a lot*, *all right*)
versus the meaning-distinguishing two-forms-of-one-spelling pairs (*already/all ready*,
*everyday/every day*, *sometime/some time*). (pp.255–261)

### Corrected nonstandard expressions (Ch.20)

The book's Table 20-1 lists six made-up/nonstandard expressions and their standard corrections: *should
of/would of/could of* → *should have/would have/could have* (or the contracted forms); *cannot help
but* → *cannot help* + *-ing*; *being that* → *because*; *try and* → *try to*; *irregardless* →
*regardless*; *can't hardly* → *can hardly*. See the Vocabulary table for each entry individually.
(pp.258–259)

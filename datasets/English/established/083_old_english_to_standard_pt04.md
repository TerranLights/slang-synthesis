## Grammar points

### Dialect prejudice is mutual and subjective, not objective

Trevisa's 14th-century comment that Northumbrian speech sounds "scharp, slyttyng and frotyng and
unschape" ('sharp, cutting/slitting, grating, and shapeless') is presented as a historical precedent
for exactly the same kind of disparaging remark Southern English speakers still sometimes make about
Northern accents today — and the reverse holds too, with Northern speakers equally capable of
dismissing unfamiliar Southern speech. The book frames this explicitly as a methodological caution:
"one person's 'thick accent' is another's familiar speech, and beauty is in the ear of the listener
rather than any objective standard" — a judgment about accent prestige/"correctness" is a social fact
about the judging community, not a linguistic fact about the dialect itself. (p.71, Chapter 5
introduction)

### Scoring a text's "advancement" in inflectional loss as a diagnostic

Commentary on Barbour's *Bruce* (Text 33) explicitly frames the Northern Scots dialect's loss of OE
inflections as comparatively **advanced** relative to Southern ME dialects of a similar or even later
date — nouns show essentially no surviving plural marking distinct from the modern pattern in the
sampled text; the infinitive carries no inflectional ending at all; present tense shows the suffixes
*-is*/*-ys* (3sg) or bare *-s*; the past participle uses *-yt* with the old OE *ge-* prefix fully
gone. The method itself (count which inflectional categories still show an overt marker vs. which
have collapsed to the modern zero/near-zero pattern) is presented as transferable to scoring any ME
dialect sample's relative conservatism or innovation — used again directly afterward on the York
texts and the *Lay Folks' Catechism* comparison. (pp.72–73, Section 5.1.1)

### Spelling conventions specific to Northern/Scots ME orthography

Several ME Northern-dialect spelling conventions are identified as distinct from other dialects'
practice, each tied to a specific sound: digraphs **⟨ai⟩/⟨ei⟩** function as a diacritic marking a
preceding long vowel (not literally a diphthong) — e.g. *haiff* /haːf/, *weill* /weːl/; **⟨ȝh⟩**
represents the consonant /j/ (e.g. *failȝhe*, *ȝharnyt*); **⟨ch⟩** or **⟨gh⟩** (used interchangeably
by different scribes in this dialect area) represents /x/, as in *nocht*; **doubled final
consonants** (⟨ff⟩) probably mark an unvoiced final consonant, as in *haiff*, *gyff*; and **⟨y⟩** is
used as an alternative spelling for *the*/*that* (derived from OE ⟨þ⟩) as well as an alternative for
⟨i⟩ — a single letter doing two unrelated jobs, a reminder that ME letter-to-sound mapping must always
be checked per-dialect rather than assumed. (p.73, Section 5.1.1)

### The York mystery-play cycle as a dialect source, and its own layered evidence

The York 'mystery plays' comprised a cycle of 50 short episodes retelling Christian salvation history
(Fall, Creation, through to the Last Judgement), each episode assigned by subject-matter relevance to
a specific craft/trade gild (e.g. the shipwrights performed *The Building of the Ark*, the bakers *The
Last Supper*) and performed in procession on pageant-wagons through the city streets at the feast of
Corpus Christi from the late 14th into the early 16th century. The only surviving manuscript copy of
the full cycle dates from c.1470 and was compiled from the individual prompt-copies belonging to each
performing gild — which means the surviving text is **two steps removed** from any single original:
individually authored/transmitted gild scripts, then collated and copied by scribes who themselves
introduced spelling modifications from the East Midlands dialect. This layering is itself treated as
evidence of an emerging standardizing written norm beginning to influence even a text whose dialect
base is clearly Northern. Separately, the plays' verse form (varied rhyming stanza patterns, with both
rhyme and alliteration) is flagged as stylized rather than naturalistic — it "cannot be read as natural
everyday speech, in spite of the liveliness of the dialogue," a caution against treating any verse
drama's dialogue as a direct transcript of period spoken usage. (pp.75–77, Section 5.2)

### Comparing two translations of the same source text to isolate dialect differences

*The Lay Folks' Catechism* offers an unusually controlled comparison: the same Latin original (written
by Archbishop John de Thoresby of York in 1352) was translated into English twice — once by a monk of
St Mary's Abbey, York, in 1357, in the Northern dialect (Text 37), and again, as an extended version,
by John Wyclif (born in North Yorkshire, but who had lived and worked for a long time at Oxford and in
Leicestershire) in his own adopted Midlands dialect (Text 38, c.1360). Because both texts translate
substantially the same underlying content, any difference between them can be attributed to dialect
rather than to differing subject matter or authorial originality — a methodological point the chapter
treats as a model worth recognizing whenever a comparable "same content, different dialect" pair turns
up (the *Lay Folks' Catechism* case is also notable for showing that an individual speaker's own
dialect, like Wyclif's, can shift away from their native regional dialect under the influence of where
they spent their working life — i.e. dialect is not simply fixed by birthplace). (p.78, Section 5.3)

### Chaucer's deliberate literary use of dialect for characterization

*The Reeve's Tale* is highlighted as a special kind of evidence: Chaucer, himself writing in the
educated, emerging-standard London dialect, **deliberately** marks two undergraduate characters'
speech (Aleyn and John, from the fictional far-northern town of "Strother") as Northern, using a dense,
concentrated cluster of recognizably Northern word-forms within dialogue otherwise set in his own
dialect. This is treated as strong period evidence of **active, contemporary dialect awareness**
among educated readers — the comic/characterizing effect depends entirely on Chaucer's London audience
being able to recognize these forms as markedly non-local, the same way a modern comic writer might
flag a character as Northern or Scots today. Because Chaucer supplies both the "marked" Northern forms
and his own unmarked London-dialect narration side by side in a single authored, precisely dated text,
this is one of the chapter's richest sources for a concentrated, cross-referenced list of genuine
Northern-dialect markers circulating in the late 14th century. (pp.80–81, Section 5.4)

# English — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: *Beginning Old English*, Text C pt1 (The Battle of Maldon, lines 1–171)

**Source:** Carole Hough and John Corbett, *Beginning Old English* (Palgrave Macmillan, 2007),
pp. 169–188 (Text C: *The Battle of Maldon*, lines 1–171 of the surviving poem — from the army's
muster through Byrhtnoth being fatally wounded).

**Coverage note:** *The Battle of Maldon* is the longest text in the book (41 pages) and is split
across two files for manageability: this file covers lines 1–171 (army muster, the Viking
messenger/Byrhtnoth "flyting" dialogue, the river crossing and causeway defense, the start of
general battle, Byrhtnoth's wounding); `076_beginning_old_english_pt10.md` covers lines 172–325
(Byrhtnoth's death, the cowards' flight, and the series of loyalty speeches to the poem's surviving
end) plus this text's full cumulative end-of-text glossary and this file's grammar points are
limited to what's discussed in lines 1–171 — the remaining grammar points (heroic speeches,
manuscript-transmission note, final formula-repetition) are in pt10. As in pt07–pt08, recurring
closed-class function words already established in earlier files, or that recur verbatim later in
this same text, are consolidated once (in pt10, covering the whole poem) rather than repeated; new
content vocabulary gets full individual treatment here. The poem is represented only through the
source's own interlinear glosses and short illustrative half-line quotations used in its discussion,
per copyright discipline. Continues from `076_beginning_old_english_pt08.md` (Text B).

**Source text layer:** clean, same fixed cipher as pt01–pt08 (þ→ƒ, ð→ê, Þ→∏; decoded throughout).
Vision Reading Confidence `n/a` throughout. **Historical/transmission note (p.169, 186):** this poem
commemorates a real 991 battle recorded tersely in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle (Alderman Byrhtnoth's
death at Maldon); its sole manuscript was destroyed in the same 1731 Cotton-library fire that
damaged the Beowulf manuscript, so the surviving text rests on a pre-fire transcript (with likely
transcription errors, discussed further in pt10). As flagged in pt07's Part II spelling note, this
text in particular shows late-OE inflectional breakdown (subjunctive plural *-on* for *-en*, Dative
plural *-on* sometimes for *-um*).

## Grammar points

### A recurring kinship-formula: "X's kinsman/son" (p.169–170)

The poem repeatedly identifies minor characters not by name but via patronymic/kinship formulas
(*Offan mǣg* 'Offa's kinsman', *Byrhtelmes bearn* 'Byrhtelm's son', *Wulfstānes bearn* 'Wulfstan's
son'). The source stresses this is not merely decorative variation but thematically reinforces the
poem's emphasis on kinship-solidarity within the English army — echoing the same kinship-over-self
theme already central to the Cynewulf and Cyneheard annal (pt07).

### The poem's central fiction: an invented "heroic-age" framing (p.169, 171, 203)

Historically Byrhtnoth was a late-10th-century English landowner/ealdorman fighting Viking raiders —
not a Germanic warlord with a sworn comitatus. The source argues the poem *deliberately* recasts him
and his men in anachronistic heroic-tradition terms (titles like *eorl*, *ealdor*, *frēa*; the
"hearth-troop"/*heorð-werod* framing; later, horse-gifting and mead-hall vow-making, none literally
plausible for 991 England) specifically to elevate the real defeat into a legendary moral victory —
success is redefined as living up to the heroic code rather than winning the battle.

### Flyting: a named genre of verbal combat (p.173–176)

The Viking messenger's demand and Byrhtnoth's refusal (lines 25–61) are identified as an instance of
**flyting** — a formal verbal-contest tradition (a "battle of wits" preceding or substituting for
physical combat) recurring across heroic literature. The source treats this passage as possibly the
earliest instance of **literary dialect representation** in English: the messenger's speech contains
features read as marking him as a non-native (Scandinavian) OE speaker — vocabulary apparently
calqued or borrowed from Old Norse (*gār-rǣs*, *hilde dǣlon*, *grið*, all otherwise unattested or
rare in OE), unusual phrasing (*gif gē spēdað tō ðām*), and a grammatically unexpected Genitive
object (*friðes* where Accusative is expected) possibly representing imperfect inflectional control.
Byrhtnoth's reply then **verbally mirrors and inverts** specific phrases from the messenger's speech
(*gār-rǣs*/*gafole* reversed to *gafole gāras*; *swā hearde* 'so fiercely' echoed and undercut by
*swā sōfte* 'so easily'), demonstrating his rhetorical mastery even though he will go on to lose the
battle — a case study worth flagging for this project's own slang/wordplay-mechanics interest, since
it is a native speaker explicitly exploiting a non-native speaker's register for rhetorical dominance.

### A genuinely unresolved crux: *ofer-mōde* (p.181–182)

Like *ealu-scerwen* in the Beowulf extract (pt08), this compound supports two substantially different
readings — 'pride' (implying the poet condemns Byrhtnoth's fatal decision to let the Vikings cross
unopposed as a moral/religious flaw, sinful hubris) vs. 'over-confidence' (a tactical misjudgment,
more consistent with Byrhtnoth's otherwise consistently heroic and pious characterization elsewhere
in the poem, including his explicitly humble battlefield prayer "God alone knows who may control the
battlefield"). The source presents both readings as live scholarly positions rather than resolving
the crux, explicitly paralleling it to Cynewulf's similarly ambiguous "rash but heroic" choice to
leave the doorway in the 757 Chronicle annal (pt07).

### The beasts-of-battle motif (p.185)

A recurring convention of OE battle poetry: ravens, eagles, and wolves gathering to feast on the
slain are invoked as battle approaches or intensifies. Here the convention is split across two
moments — the kenning *wæl-wulfas* 'slaughter-wolves' for the Vikings themselves (p.183) supplies
the "wolf" element, and ravens/an eagle are then invoked directly (*Hremmas*, *earn*) as the general
fighting begins — together completing the traditional raven/eagle/wolf triad the source notes is
common across OE poetry.

### Variation and alliteration used for dramatic pacing (p.173–174, 183)

Two worked examples of poetic-device analysis, both usefully concrete for cross-reference against
Chapter 6's general catalogue: (1) three successive near-synonymous phrases build up the Viking
messenger's speech before its content even begins (*stīþlīce clypode*, *wordum mǣlde*, *on bēot
ābēad* — all roughly 'spoke/announced'), heightening tension without advancing plot; (2) the
Viking advance across the causeway (lines 96–99) triples both the naming of the Vikings
(*wæl-wulfas* / *wīcinga werod* / *lid-men*) and the description of their crossing/shield-bearing,
reinforcing a sense of relentless approach — then is abruptly halted, at the level of sound itself,
by a shift from /w/-alliteration to harsh /b/-alliteration exactly where Byrhtnoth orders the
shield-wall formed (*Byrhtnōþ*, *beornum*, *bordum*).

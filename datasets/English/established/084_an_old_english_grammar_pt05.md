## Grammar points

### §127. Tense

OE verbs have only two tense-inflexions, present and preterite; other time-relations were either left implicit from context or expressed with adverbs of time. The present inflexion expresses present time (*þā þēowan drincað medo* 'the slaves drink mead') and also future time, corresponding to Latin *surgam et ibō ad patrem meum* 'I shall arise and go to my father'. There is some distinction in function between the two present forms of 'to be': *bið, bēoð* appear to have been used more frequently to denote future, as against *is, sind* which denote fact/present reality — Ælfric's Latin Grammar equates *eom, eart* with Lat. *sum, es*, and *bēo, bist, bið* with Lat. *erō, eris, erit*. A historic present is of rare and even dubious occurrence in OE. The preterite inflexion expressed both simple past and "past-in-the-past" (pluperfect) time; the pluperfect time-relation is often implicit by reason of the type of clause involved, or assisted by the presence of *ǣr* 'before'.

### §128. Compound ("complex") verbal forms

OE saw the rise of complex verbal forms usually called "compound tenses." Although *willan* and *sculan* with an infinitive usually imply volition or obligation respectively, these constructions are occasionally found translating Latin futures. For the most part, however, *willan* and *sculan* are overlaid with their other functions even when partly indicating future. The pluperfect was widely expressed by the preterite of *habban* 'have' together with the past participle of transitive verbs, and the preterite of *wesan* 'be' with the past participle of intransitive verbs — where the participles agree (in one case with the object, in the other with the subject), this reflects a survival from when the construction had predicative-adjectival rather than genuine tense function (cf. §123). Of the two pluperfect auxiliaries, *habban* shows signs of becoming the preferred one even within the OE period, to the point of occasionally being found with intransitive verbs too.

### §129. Aspect

In speaking of the present tense of 'have' (*habban*, rarely *āgan*) and 'be' with a past participle, we pass from tense (time) to **aspect** (manner/quality of an action). For ordinary purposes, only 'perfective' (relating to momentary/completed actions) and 'durative' (relating to habitual and continuous actions) aspect need be distinguished. The perfect of transitive verbs expressed with 'have', and the perfect of intransitive verbs expressed with 'be', do not refer to a different time from the simple preterites but to the same time regarded more specifically as perfective; in OE the perfective aspect could equally well be expressed with the simple preterite form. In other cases the perfective function is assisted by adverbs. Some verbs (such as *cuman* 'come', *feallan* 'fall', *weorðan* 'become') are inherently perfective and need no formal indication of aspect; for a larger number, perfective aspect was expressed not by inflexion but by **prefixing elements** such as *ā-, be-, for-, ge-, of-, tō-* (see §§168, 170f).

### §130. Durative aspect

The durative aspect is inherent in the meaning of most verbs ('be', 'live', for example) and it is therefore not surprising that special forms and constructions were used only to a minor extent in OE to express it: *ðēos world…nēalǣcð þǣm ende* 'this world is approaching the end'. The durative aspect could also be stressed using *wildu dēor ðǣr woldon tō irnan* 'wild animals were wont to run there', and *wolde* with an infinitive quite frequently expressing habitual (as opposed to continuous) action. We also find 'be' with a present participle expressing durative aspect (*ic mē gebidde tō ðǣm Gode þe is…eardigende on heofonum* 'I pray to the God who is dwelling in the heavens'); this construction's function is often hard to distinguish from the simple tense form, and at times seems ingressive rather than purely durative.

### §131. Voice

With a single exception, OE verbs showed only **active** voice inflexions. The exception is *hātan* 'call', which besides having a vocalic active preterite *hēt* also had a consonantal preterite *hātte* used both as present and past, with passive meaning (*sēo ēa hātte Temese* 'the river is called Thames'). For the rest, the notional passive was expressed in one of two principal ways: a copula verb ('be'/'become') with the past participle, or the indefinite pronoun *man* with the active verb-form. In the periphrastic passive, the two auxiliaries *bēon/wesan* and *weorðan* were available, roughly distinguished by aspect — the former in durative expressions, the latter in perfective expressions (*ne bið ðǣr nǣnig ealo gebrowen* 'no ale is (ever) brewed there' vs. *þæt hūs wearð ðā forburnen* 'the house was then burnt down'); there was much free variation, however, and writers often simply preferred one or the other auxiliary. The OE verb 'to be' did not develop a past participle until very late, so the 'pluperfect passive' was not distinguished from the simple preterite. A passive infinitive was usually expressed with the active form: *þās þing sint tō dōnne* 'these things are to be done'. Despite its passive inflexion, *hātan* was still often used with the periphrastic construction and also with *man*: *hī sind gehātene* 'they are called', *tō þǣm porte þe mon hǣt Hedeby* 'to the trading place which is called Hedeby'.

### §132. Mood: Indicative

The indicative is the mood of general objective expression, used in the vast majority of constructions not involving grammatical dependence. It is also the mood of a large number of grammatically dependent expression-types, explicitly listed: (a) relative clauses; (b) noun clauses of various kinds relating to fact or (though less frequently) certainty; (c) adverb clauses of place; (d) adverb clauses of time; (e) adverb clauses of cause; (f) adverb clauses of manner; (g) adverb clauses of result; (h) adverb clauses of condition where the condition is a practical possibility (*gif þū gelȳfan wylt, þū wurþest hāl sōna* 'if you are prepared to believe, you will become well at once').

### §133. Mood: Subjunctive

The subjunctive is the mood of subjective expression, generally confined to volitional, conjectural, or hypothetical contexts; usage is to some extent purely conventional rather than strictly rule-governed. Principal uses: (a) non-dependent clauses expressing wishes and commands (*God ūre helpe* 'may God help us', *cild binnan ðritegum nihta sīe gefulwad* 'let a child be baptised within 30 nights'); (b) noun clauses (including indirect questions) in negative or conjectural contexts or dependent on verbs of saying/thinking/suggesting; (c) adverb clauses of concession introduced by *þēah(þe)*, with the indicative used when there is inversion without a conjunction ('willy-nilly' concession) or when a conjunction such as *sam* is used; (d) adverb clauses of condition expressing extremely hypothetical/impossible conditions; especially so in the preterite, since weakening of unstressed vowels in the preterite left fewer inflexional mood distinctions visible there; (e) adverb clauses of purpose; (f) some adverb clauses of result where the result is anticipated; (g) in temporal and other clauses that relate to future or conjectural events; (h) in many comparative clauses with *þonne*; (i) in clauses of various kinds dependent on clauses containing subjunctive verbs themselves.

### §134–135. The rise of modal periphrasis and the Imperative

During the OE period, the subjunctive came to be expressed more and more through the "modal auxiliaries" *willan, sculan, magan, (ic) mōt* (§§88, 91, 93, 95) — in some texts the two moods (subjunctive inflexion vs. modal-periphrasis) alternate in parallel constructions within the same passage.

**Imperative** (§135): exists properly only in the second person, singular and plural. There is also a rare first person plural imperative in *-an, -on*, almost the only common example being *(w)uton, utan* (historically probably the aorist optative or subjunctive of *witan* 'go'; see §214), used in a frequent periphrasis to express the first person plural imperative of other verbs: *uton feallan tō ðǣre rōde* 'let us fall before the cross'. For the third person (and sometimes also the first), exhortations are expressed by means of the subjunctive (see §133a).

### §136. Infinitive

The infinitive is chiefly used: (a) with a small number of auxiliary-like verbs (*cunnan, (ic) dearr, magan, sculan, þurfan, willan*, and, as noted, *uton*) which to a greater or lesser extent act as "anomalous finites," almost all surviving into Mod.E.; (b) with verbs of causation, intention, in the "accusative and infinitive" construction (see §96), often with passive meaning; (c) with verbs of motion, rest, and observation, often with durative aspect and sometimes with "accusative and infinitive" too; the infinitive in these last two classes was sometimes preceded by *tō*, and use of *tō* with the infinitive (almost always inflected, *-anne/-enne*) increased throughout the OE period, becoming general in the following usage classes: (d) purpose; (e) causal; (f) specificatory (especially with nouns and adjectives) and adverbial; (g) substantival. There is an important idiom in which the copula and dative of the person usually implies necessity: *nū is tīma ūs…tō slǣpe ārīsenne* 'now it is time for us to arise from sleep'.

### §137. Word-Order: general orientation

It is a truism that OE word-order is relatively free as compared with Mod.E., but the authors caution it is easy both to exaggerate this freedom and to overlook two important facts: first, there are considerable areas of conformity to describable patterns in OE; second, these patterns to a great extent coincide with present-day usage. The paragraphs that follow attend to only the most important/recurrent configurations, leaving relatively irregular patterns to specialist grammars.

### §138–140. Noun and Pronoun Modifiers

Nouns may be defined by demonstratives, adjectives, or both; it is normal for both to precede the noun, the demonstrative coming first. Adjectives used substantivally are preceded by a demonstrative like nouns are. Possessives behave like demonstratives; pronouns are frequently qualified by *eall* and *self*, which they usually immediately precede. Outstanding exceptions to the demonstrative—adjective—noun order rule are *eall* and adjectives in *-weard*, which usually precede the demonstrative. The simple demonstrative can also follow the noun when it is preceding an adjective; it is possible that such adjectives are to be interpreted as substantival. Descriptive noun titles like 'king' and 'abbot' usually follow the names they qualify (*Ælfred cyning* 'King Æ.'), but with a determining modifier such titles are often found preceding the name (§140: *þone arcebiscop Ælfēah* 'the Archbishop Æ.'). Genitive complements generally **precede** the words they relate to (§140: *hira land* 'their land'); it is normal for the genitive complement to keep this position even when its related noun is in a prepositional phrase. But when the noun is already determined by another qualifier, the genitive complement is found **following** its noun instead. A noun often precedes its complement when the latter is a personal name (*sunu Bēanstānes* 'B.'s son').

### §141–143. Prepositions and Adverbs

Prepositions in OE are generally placed in front of the items with which they are grammatically/notionally connected, and in front of any modifiers that may precede such items. But they are **postpositive** (and should perhaps be called "post-positions") with the adverbs of place which frequently have pronominal function (*þǣrtō, þǣrinntō, þǣræt, hērinne, hērtōēacan* 'in addition to this'). With pronouns (especially those of more than one syllable), prepositions quite frequently follow; in verse, stressed postposition is not uncommon even with nouns. But postposition is most frequent, both in prose and verse, where it enables the preposition to stand before a verb form in which the governed item(s) have been separated from their component parts; this is especially common in relative clauses in which, according to Mod.E. literary style, the preposition goes with the relative pronoun (*þe wē gefyrn ymbe sprǣcon* 'about which we spoke earlier'). Less commonly, the preposition follows even the verb, either closely or remotely: *Ōswold him cōm tō him* 'O. came to him'; *him cōm micel ēaca tō* 'a great reinforcement came to them'.

**Adverbs** (§142): the variety of position for adverbs and adverb phrases in OE matches Mod.E.'s own general descriptive statement — their placement, while governed by similar considerations to Mod.E. (adjective-modifiers, verb-modifiers, or sentence-modifiers occupy mutually exclusive environments), makes naturally intelligible statements about position difficult. Ælfric himself varies adverb position within a few lines of each other, apparently without systematic difference — the variation seems to the authors to be, to some extent, genuinely more chaotic than it looks, because of the unsatisfactory way "adverb" groups functionally dissimilar word-classes. The negative particle *ne* so regularly precedes the items it modifies that it is frequently agglutinated with them (*nis, nǣron, nabban, nolde*, etc.; *nāht (ne+ā+wiht)* 'not, by no means'; *nǣnig (ne+ǣnig)* 'none'; *nǣfre (ne+ǣfre)* 'never'; *nā (ne+ā)* 'never, by no means'). In OE usage, multiple negation was entirely normal, and could be elaborately compounded (*ne ic ne herige ne ic ne tǣle* 'nor do I praise or blame', *nis nǣnig swā snotor* 'there is no one so wise').

### §143–144. Further adverb position and expletive/existential *þǣr*

Adverbial phrases and adverbs of more than one syllable tended to be placed in a relatively posterior position; the dative complement of instrumentality, which is also adverbial, normally precedes the item to which it is related. §144 (note on *þǣr*): the existential/expletive use of *þǣr* ('there is/are') is cross-referenced to R. Quirk's own article on the construction (*London Mediæval Studies* ii.32); it is noted that *þā* and other elements, when followed by V S O/C when adverbial, do not take this order when conjunctive; in correlative sequences there is thus a sharp distinction between the order in dependent and non-dependent clauses (examples given at §150).

### §144–147. Subject, Object (or Complement), and Verb order

All possible permutations of these elements are recorded in both prose and verse, and it must be stressed that there was considerable free variation in OE, and it would not be helpful — even if it were practicable — to tabulate all the factors that led to the selection of pattern in every recorded case. Prose, and to a lesser extent the late verse, display a considerable tendency towards the order **S V O/C** in non-dependent clauses (*þæt Estland is swȳðe mycel* 'Estonia is very large'). Where the verb comprises a finite part plus a participle or infinitive, the two are either close together (se ādliga…weard gehǣled) or the non-finite part comes at the end. The most frequent occasion for departing from this order is when certain adverbs (especially *ne* and *þā*) come first; the order is then **V S O/C**.

§145: in **questions**, the order is V S O/C (*Eart þū se Bēowulf…?*); in jussive and volitional expressions, and in conditional clauses without a subordinating conjunction (see examples at §158), the same V-first order holds. In questions where O/C is an interrogative pronoun or an interrogative plus noun, the order is O/C V S (*Hwæt sægst þū?* 'What do you say?').

§146: the common order S V O is disrupted by **disjunction**, when first place is taken by an element of significant importance/contrast in context (*Geā, būtan nettum huntian ic mæg* 'Certainly I can hunt without nets' — in reply to a question about hunting with nets specifically). Disjunction also affects final position, especially in the separation of co-ordinate objects or complements.

§147: one minor arrangement worth noting is that when O is a pronoun, it frequently precedes V (*þā burgware hīe geflīemdon* 'the townsfolk routed them'); another recurrent feature is V in initial position, in some cases for special declarative effect, in others apparently simply an individual-writer stylistic preference (especially common in Ælfredian Bede and some poetry). In the poetry as a whole there is great variety in the disposing of S, O, and V, and it is easier to speak of the word-order of any one poem than OE verse generally.

In **dependent clauses** (§147), the dominant order is **S O/C V** — this is fairly regular in relative clauses, concessive clauses, and frequent in temporal clauses. But dependent clauses are also found in large numbers with the order S V O/C, and this seems especially common with causal clauses, result clauses, and noun clauses.

### §148. Order of Clauses

Although the criteria distinguishing dependent from non-dependent clauses in OE have not yet been fully worked out, enough is known to state as a rule that dependent clauses follow the dependent or non-dependent clauses to which they are related; this is true of both prose and verse alike (*For þȳ ne sceall nān mann āwǣgan þæt þē ælmihtiga Gode þonne hē ādlig bið, þē lǣs þe hē sylf losige, gif hē ālihð þæt* 'Thus no one must nullify what he promises Almighty God of his own free will when he is sick, lest he himself perish if he denies God this'). Certain types of dependent clause are found more readily in initial position than others, notably conditional clauses and indefinite relative clauses of various kinds. But on the whole, initial position has to be supported by correlation (see §150): *swā hwæt swā hē becōm…þæt hē hraðe dǣlde* 'whatever came his way, this he promptly shared'. Thus correlated, many types of clause appear initially: *Nū ic sceal geendian earmlicum dēaþe…nū wolde ic gebētan* 'Now that I must perish in a wretched death, I would like to make amends'. Clauses of most kinds are found also medially.

### §149–150. Co-ordination/Parataxis and Correlation/Hypotaxis

In Mod.E. and other languages, notional relationships like cause and condition can be given linguistic expression in a sequence of non-dependent constructions related by a simple conjunction (**co-ordination**) or, without a conjunction, by intonation/juncture (**parataxis**) — unmarked in writing. Such expressions, throughout OE, were very common: *hē þē æt sunde oferflāt, hæfde māre mægen* 'he beat you at swimming — (he) had greater strength' (cause), *hē his feorh generede ond hē wæs oft gewundod* 'he saved his life but he was wounded many times' (concession). Sometimes the relationship is made more explicit by a relational adverb (*and þēah hē wæs oft gewundad* 'and yet he was often wounded').

**Correlation and Hypotaxis** (§150): subordinate or grammatically dependent ("hypotactic") constructions are a more complex means of expressing relationship, and in OE these frequently involve **correlation** — the linking of members in a relationship by corresponding demonstrative elements in each ("God *so* loved the world, *that* he gave his only begotten Son"). There are many sets of correlative elements in OE, among the commonest *þā(…þā)…þā*, *þonne…nū, nū*, *þæt…þæt, þȳ…þȳ, þæs…þæs, swā…swā*. The correlatives are often juxtaposed, one at the end of one member, the other at the beginning of the next; this is particularly common with relative clauses.

### §151–152. Hypotaxis without Correlation; Dependence without a Finite Verb

Hypotactic expression is found extensively also **without** correlation, notably with causal, conditional, and especially concessive relations, where the dependent clause follows the related non-dependent clause (*hē him eft āgeaf, for þǣm þe hiora wæs ōðer his godsunu* 'he gave them back to him, because one of them was his godson').

Notional relationships were often expressed in OE by means of **prepositional, participial, or other phrases** without a finite verb at all (§152): *hē cōm ðā þurh Godes sande* 'he came then by reason of God's summons' (causal); similarly present and past participles: *þæt man his hlāford…of lande lifigende drīfe* 'that anyone should drive his lord, living, from the land'. Often, in imitation of the Latin ablative absolute, such expressions appear in the dative (§111): *ðā…fērde hē tō heofonum, him on lōcigendum* 'then he proceeded to the heavens, with them looking on'; *him andweardum* 'with them present'; *āstrehtum handum* 'with hands outstretched'. Absolute expressions are most frequently temporal in function, but often relate also to manner; they are also used causally and (less commonly) concessively.

### §153. Expression of Relationship: the descriptive/relative function

A given relationship can find linguistic expression several ways. The **descriptive** function (which the relative clause typically expresses) could alternatively be expressed by a participial expression, an infinitival expression, or various relative pronoun constructions (the most important involving correlation).

### §154. Temporal relations

Various temporal relations are expressed by dependent clauses introduced by common conjunctions (frequently correlated): *siððan (siððan…ǣbrēas…gegān hæfdon)* 'after the Hebrews had gone'; *þā* or *þā…þā (ðā ðā scipu gearwe wǣron)* 'when the ships were ready'; *þonne (ðonne þæt flǣsc bið geswenced)* 'when the flesh is afflicted'; *þenden (þenden hē on þysse worulde wunode)* 'while he dwelt in this world'; *nū (nū wē hit wit habban ne mōton)* 'now that we cannot possess it'; *ǣr þǣm þe (ǣr þǣm þe hit eall forhergod wǣre)* 'before it was all completely ravaged'; *oð þæt (oð þæt Crīst sylf cōme)* 'until Christ himself should come'; those relating to the future for the most part require subjunctive verbs (§133g), but in general the mood in temporal clauses is indicative. Frequently, time relations are indicated by means of temporal adverbs in non-dependent constructions: *þā, þonne, nū, sōna, gȳt*, and many others.

### §155–156. Purpose and Result

**Purpose** (§155) is generally expressed by a dependent clause containing a subjunctive verb; the clause is usually introduced by *þæt(te)* (*ūs gedafenað þæt wē…ondswarigen* 'it is fitting for us that we (should) answer'), less frequently by *tō þǣm (…)*; negative purpose clauses are introduced by *þȳ lǣs (þe)* 'lest'. Purpose can also be expressed with a co-ordinate construction familiar in Mod.E. (*uton faran…and gesēon* 'let's go and see'); the inflected infinitive is also common in this function. There are several "purpose-equivalent" constructions — i.e. constructions in which purpose is expressed simultaneously with another relationship, notably cause: *ēode…aweg…for ðan þe hē ne mihte gesēon* 'he went away so that he might not see (or because he could not bear to see)'.

**Result** clauses (§156) are again usually introduced by *þæt(te)*, but the verb in these is indicative; frequently they are introduced by *swā (…) þæt(te)*, as in *swā þæt hē hrēas…on eorðan* 'so that he fell to the ground'; the subjunctive is of course used when required by a particular context. Result-equivalent constructions include clauses of time and degree. The **modal** relation (manner, attendant circumstances, comparison) is best regarded as embracing the result relationship. In OE it is most often expressed by means of clauses introduced by *swā (swā)*: *swā hī from þē hider cōmon, swā hī ēac tō þē hionan fundiað* 'just as they have come here from Thee, so they likewise hasten hence to Thee'; participial and absolute expressions are also found.

### §157–159. Causal, Conditional, Concessive relations

**Causal** clauses (§157) take indicative verbs, with the common conjunction *for ðon (þe)*; its variants *forðȳ (þe/þæt)* 'for there is nothing mightier than Thou' are also noted; *forðon(-ðȳ)* is also a common connective/relational adverb in co-ordinate expressions. Various forms of correlation are found (*hē for þǣm nolde…þȳ mid his folce getruwade* 'for this reason he would not… because along with his force he was confident'). Cause is frequently expressed paratactically or with simple co-ordination.

**Conditional** conjunctions (§158) are *gif, þǣr* (for positive conditions); *būtan, nefne(nemne), nymðe* (for negative conditions); on mood in conditional clauses, see §§132h, 133d. There is often a correlative *þonne* with *gif*. The inversion construction (verb-first, no conjunction) occurs but is not common. Conditional-equivalent expressions include phrases such as absolute expressions (*āhte ic mīnra handa geweald* 'if I had command of my hands').

**Concessive** clauses (§159), which have subjunctive verbs, are introduced by *þēah(þe)*: *þēah ðe hit wǣre his rīce* 'although it was his kingdom'. In the related non-dependent clause we sometimes find such correlative items as *swā þēah, hwæðere, þēahhwæðere* and very often find these words indicating the concessive relation in co-ordinate expressions, *ac* co-ordinating this function extremely frequently in the prose. While *hwæðere* is largely confined to poetic usage, *ac* in this function is also common in prose. Co-ordination with *and* may also express concession (*mūð habbað, and ne meldiað wiht* 'they have a mouth, and (yet) do not speak at all'); paratactic expression of concession is also well attested. There are many forms of indefinite concession, but perhaps the most characteristic is the "challenge" form, with imperative or with jussive subjunctive (§133a): *hycge swā hē wille* 'let him think as he will'; the alternative concession is often on the pattern V S V S, with the subjunctive (*wylle wē, nelle wē* 'whether we will or not'), but may take the form of contrastive pairs separated by *ne* or *oððe* (*feor oððe nēah* 'whether far or near'). Concessive prepositional phrases are not uncommon (*for eallum þissum* 'for all this'), but appositive and absolute participles are fairly rare in this function; concession is also commonly expressed in modal, temporal, causal, degree, and other clauses.

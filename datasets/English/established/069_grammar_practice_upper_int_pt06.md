## Grammar points

### First, second and third conditional

The book presents three core conditional patterns (p.82), each tied to a distinct degree of
likelihood/reality:

- **First conditional:** *if* + present simple, main clause + future simple ("If I see Andrew at the
  meeting, I'll give him your message"). Used for something reasonably likely to happen in the
  future, where the condition is expected to be fulfillable.
- **Second conditional:** *if* + past simple, main clause + *would* + infinitive ("If my parents
  were alive, they would be very proud of me now"). Used for two distinct present/future-unreal
  cases: a present situation that is impossible (hypothetical, condition cannot be fulfilled — "If my
  parents were alive...") and a future event that is unlikely though not impossible (the speaker
  doesn't expect it — "If she changed her job, she would be much happier").
- **Third conditional:** *if* + past perfect, main clause + *would have* + past participle ("If she
  had worked harder, she would have passed the exams"). Used for something in the past that did not
  happen — an unfulfilled past condition.

**Notes (p.82):** the conditional clause can come first or second in the sentence (first is more
common); a comma follows the conditional clause only when it comes first. The tense/verb-form
patterns given are the most common ones — many variations exist, covered in the following section.

### Conditional sentences: variations in tenses

The book catalogs permitted substitutions for the "default" verb forms in each conditional type
(pp.85–86):

**First conditional:** the *if*-clause, beyond plain present simple, can also take present
continuous, present perfect simple, present perfect continuous, *can*, or *should* (the last noted as
making the condition read as less likely to be fulfilled than a plain present simple). The main
clause, beyond plain *will/shall* + infinitive, can also take *be going to* future, future
continuous, future perfect, the imperative, *can/could*, *may/might*, *should/ought to*, or *must*.

**Second conditional:** the *if*-clause, beyond plain past simple, can also take past continuous,
*could*, or *were to*. The main clause, beyond plain *would* + infinitive, can also take *could* or
*might* as the modal, and/or a continuous infinitive ("we wouldn't be living here").

**Third conditional:** the *if*-clause, beyond plain past perfect, can also take past perfect
continuous or *could* + perfect infinitive. The main clause, beyond plain *would* + perfect
infinitive, can also take *could* or *might* before the perfect infinitive.

### The zero conditional

Form: *if* + present simple, present simple in the main clause ("If I stay out late, I always get a
taxi home") (p.86). Used to describe something generally/habitually true; the book notes this use of
*if* functions very similarly to *when* ("Each time I stay out late, I get a taxi home"). The
pattern can also be rendered entirely in the past simple across both clauses to describe a past
habitual truth ("If I stayed out late, I always got a taxi home").

### Mixed conditionals

Conditional clause-types can be mixed, with the *if*-clause using one type's verb form and the main
clause using a different type's — context supplies the intended meaning (p.88). The most common
mixed pattern combines a third-conditional *if*-clause (past perfect) with a second-conditional main
clause (*would/could/might* + plain infinitive), used to connect an unfulfilled past condition to a
hypothetical present result ("If I had worked harder at university, I would have a degree now"). The
book also illustrates a first+second mix (present-tense *if*-clause with a *would*-main-clause: "If
you come to the party tomorrow, I wouldn't bring Mike with you") and a second+third mix (past-simple
*if*-clause with a *would have*-main-clause: "If she loved him, she would have stayed with him").

### Words other than if

A closed set of conjunctions can introduce a conditional clause in place of *if*, several restricted
to question contexts (p.91): *unless, even if, if only, as/so long as, assuming (that), on (the)
condition (that), provided/providing (that)*, and — explicitly marked by the book as occurring only
in questions — *suppose/supposing (that), what if, imagine*.

### Implied conditionals

Conditions are not always expressed as an explicit conditional clause, particularly in spoken
English — they can be implied by context or expressed through other phrasing (p.92). The book's own
worked examples: an invitation implying its own condition ("Please come out with us tonight. I'm
sure you'll enjoy it" implies "if you come out with us"); a prepositional phrase standing in for an
*if*-clause ("With a bit more help, I would have finished it on time" = "if I had had more help");
an implied condition recoverable from surrounding context about affordability ("I'm sure we'd really
enjoy going on holiday with you but we just can't afford it" implies "if we went"); and a condition
implied by a counterfactual reaction ("I'm glad you didn't tell Matthew about it. He would have been
furious" implies "if you had told him").

## Grammar points

### Instrumental case (regular nouns and adjectives)

The instrumental singular ending is `-om` for feminine `-a`-nouns and for masculine/neuter nouns
(surfacing as `-em` after a soft consonant); the adjective ending is `-im` for masculine/neuter,
`-om` for feminine (no shift after soft consonants in the feminine). Feminine consonant-stem nouns
take either `-i` or `-u` in the Isg, the `-u` ending sometimes triggering a stem-consonant change; most
such nouns allow both, some only `-i`. (pp. 97, §79a–b)

### Instrumental case of pronouns

Interrogative `ko/tko` "who" and `što/šta` "what," plus 1st/3rd singular personal pronouns, each have
a short and a long instrumental form (e.g. `kim`/`kìme`, `njim`/`njìme`, `mnom`/`mnòme`). Personal
pronouns otherwise have only full forms; in the plural, Dat, Loc, and Instr all share one ending.
Prepositions ending in a consonant add `-a` before `mnom` (`sà mnom`, `prèda mnom`), and always take
the accent before it. `ništa` "nothing," `nešto` "something," `išta` "anything," and `svašta` "all
sorts of" decline like `šta` [B,S] / `što` [C]. (p. 97, §79c–d, §62, §92)

### Instrumental case with prepositions

The instrumental is used with several prepositions, most frequently `s` "with." Before a word starting
with `s`, `z`, `š`, or `ž`, this preposition must take the form `sa`. In Bosnian it sometimes appears as
`sa` in other contexts too, and in Serbian it usually takes the form `sa` regardless of what follows —
a genuine [B]/[S] usage-frequency contrast the source calls out explicitly. (p. 98, §80a)

### Use of the instrumental case

The instrumental (without a preposition) expresses means of transport (`ja idem tramvajem` "I go by
tram"), the time of a regular repeated action (`òna radi kasno ùtorkom` "she works late on Tuesdays"),
and — in the plural with a time noun plus a present-tense verb and `već` — a period of long duration
still continuing (`već godinama ràzmišljam o tome` "I've been thinking about that for years"). (p. 100,
§81b)

### Prepositions with the instrumental

Most other instrumental-governing prepositions denote physical or metaphorical location: `pred`
"before," `nad` "above," `za` "behind" (the latter common in the idiom `za stolom` "at the table").
(p. 100, §80b)

### Verbs of transport

Three verbs mean "take [someone/something somewhere]," chosen by the object's nature: `vòditi` for an
animate being moving under its own power by foot, `vòziti` for the same by vehicle, and `nòsiti` for an
inanimate object or a being that must be carried (e.g. an infant). `vòziti` itself is transitive with an
Acc object (a passenger: `vòziti žènu na pòsao`; or a vehicle type: `vòziti auto`), or intransitive with
`se`, meaning either simply "ride" or "go by vehicle" (vehicle specified in the Instr: `vòziti se
àutom`). (p. 100, §84a)

### Negative imperative

A negative command is formed with `nèmoj` (plural `nèmojte`) plus either the infinitive or `da` +
present tense — `nèmoj to pròkockati` / `nèmoj to da pròkockaš`, both "Don't gamble it away." As with
other infinitive-vs.-`da`+present alternations, Croatian nearly always uses the infinitive, Serbian
usually `da`+present, and Bosnian uses both. (p. 100, §78e)

### Negated mòći vs. mòrati

In the affirmative, `mòći` = "can/be able to," `mòrati` = "must/be obliged to." Under negation the
meanings shift: negated `mòći` expresses English "must not" (`nè možeš tàko!` "You mustn't do it that
way!"), while negated `mòrati` means only "not be obliged to" (`nè moraš tàko` "You don't have to do it
that way"). Negated `mòći` also still means "not able to" (`ne mògu tàko` "I can't do it that way").
(p. 101, §142)

### The imperative mood, continued

To form the singular imperative, drop the final vowel of the 3rd person plural present; if `-j` remains,
leave it, otherwise add `-i`. Add `-te` for the plural (`vi`) imperative, `-mo` for the "inclusive"
("let's...") imperative — though `hàjde` + infinitive (or `da`+present) more commonly expresses "let's."
`neka` + 3rd person verb expresses "let..." (`neka uđe` "let him in"). The verb `reći` "say" is rare in
the present tense but common in the imperative (`rèci, rècite, rècimo`). (p. 102, §78a–d)

### Verbs of transport, continued

The perfective partners of `nòsiti`/`vòditi`/`vòziti` preserve their basic meanings but use different
stems and occur only with prefixes, e.g. `òdneti`[E]/`òdnijeti`[J] (1sg `odnèsem`) "carry away,"
`òdvesti` (1sg `odvèdem`) "lead away," `òdvesti` (1sg `odvèzem`) "drive away." (p. 102, §84a)

### "Inclinational" se-verbs

To express feeling like (or not) doing something, use the 3sg verb + `se` with the person in the
dative: `meni se spava` "I feel sleepy," `nama se nè ide` "we don't feel like going." When the action is
contextually understood, B and C often use `nè da se` (+ dative) — `nè da mi se` "I'm not in the mood" —
but in Serbian this same phrase instead means "I don't feel like giving [it]," a genuine cross-dialect
semantic divergence the source flags explicitly. (p. 103, §82b)

### Prepositions meaning "to [a person]"

Movement toward a person (or their home) is expressed in Croatian with `k` (+ Dat), becoming `ka`
before a word starting with `k`/`g` (`ìdi k Ani`, `ìdimo ka Gòranu`). Bosnian and Serbian instead use
genitive-governing prepositions: `do` for "to a person," `kod` for "to a person's house" (`ìdi do Ane`
vs. `ìdimo kod Gòrana`). (p. 103, §84b)

### Aspect, continued

A few verbs use the same form for both perfective and imperfective meaning (biaspectual): `čuti`
"hear," `videti`[E]/`vidjeti`[J] "see," `rùčati` "eat lunch," `razùm eti`[E]/`razùmjeti`[J`] "understand."
(p. 103, §83a)

### Amusing slang usage

The source flags this explicitly as slang: there is no grammatical affirmative counterpart to `nèmoj`,
but B, C, and S colloquial usage has produced the ungrammatical-yet-popular retort `Ma moj!` or `Ma da
moj!` in response to `Ma nèmoj!` — paralleling English "No way!" → "Way!" (p. 103)

### Instrumental of means

Used alone, without a preposition, the instrumental expresses the tool/means by which an action is
done — the sense that names the case (`pišem plavom òlovkom` "I write with a blue pencil"). Learners
are cautioned against the tendency to insert `s`/`sa` here. (p. 104, §81a)

### Variation in suffixes and gender of nouns

The same adjective root can take different regional suffixes (`jezički` [B,S] vs. `jezični` [C]).
Nouns borrowed from other languages can also land in different gender classes by region: `laboràtorija`
[B,S] and `planèta` [B,S] are feminine, but `laboràtorij` [C] and `plànet` [C] are masculine. (p. 104,
§172a)

### The words for "where"

Three interrogatives once cleanly divided "where": `gde`[E]/`gdje`[J] for location, `kamo` for goal of
motion ("where to"), `kuda` for path taken ("which way") — a distinction still visible in `òvamo` "to
here" and `òvuda` "this way." Officially Croatian still keeps `kamo` distinct from `kuda`, though Croats
increasingly use `kuda` for "where to" as well; Bosnian and Serbian use both `kuda` and `gde`/`gdje` for
"where to," expressing "which path" instead via the phrase `kòjim putem`. The source compares this
merger to English losing the "whither"/"where" distinction. (p. 105)

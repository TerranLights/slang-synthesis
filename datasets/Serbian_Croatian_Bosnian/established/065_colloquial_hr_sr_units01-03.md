# Serbian/Croatian/Bosnian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Units 1-3 ("Na putu" / "Razgovor o putovanju" / "Dolazak u Zagreb")

**Source:** Celia Hawkesworth, *Colloquial Croatian and Serbian: The Complete Course for Beginners*
(Routledge, 1998). Units 1-3, printed pages 7-38 (PDF pages 15-46 of
`source_reference/languages/Serbian, Croatian, Bosnian/03.Colloquial Serbian and Croatian.pdf`).
Unit 4 ("Svatko ide na svoju stranu") begins exactly at PDF page 47 and is excluded from this
chunk.

**Coverage note:** every grammar point and every distinct vocabulary item from Units 1-3, plus
every explicit dialectal/regional/register annotation the source itself marks. Repeated drill
exercises and duplicate example sentences that don't introduce new vocabulary or grammar are
skipped — see `../../00_Reference_Extraction_Spec.md`. Realia reproductions embedded in the book
(a Croatia Airlines frequent-flyer ad, a bus ticket facsimile) were skipped as non-linguistic
content, not vocabulary/grammar. Two parallel love-story ("Ljubavna priča") and business-traveler
("Priča našeg vremena") narrative threads run through the unit and are explicitly framed by the
book itself as illustrating colloquial dialogue vs. narrative register respectively — vocabulary
from both is included below.

**Vision-reading guard:** this source has no usable text layer (scanned book) and was fully
vision-read at 150dpi, page by page, PDF pages 15-46 (32 pages) in sequence with no gaps. Every
page was actively checked for handwritten marginalia (pencil/pen notes, underlines, filled-in
exercise answers in a different hand from the printed type). **No marginalia was found on any
page in this range** — all content is clean, consistently typeset printed material. Accordingly,
no content was excluded on marginalia grounds, and Vision Reading Confidence is `verified` or
`plausible_unverified` throughout (no `low_confidence` rows — the scan is legible and undamaged
across this whole range).

## Grammar points

### Greetings and their register/political associations (Unit 1)

`Dobar dan` is the neutral, time-of-day-independent greeting (morning/evening variants used only
within narrow time windows, roughly before 10 a.m. / after 6 p.m.). The book makes a striking
sociolinguistic point about the informal greeting `Zdravo`: it is described as "becoming less
used in Croatia because of its associations with Communism and because it may be felt to be more
Serbian." In Zagreb specifically, `Bog` (a contraction of `Zbogom`, "go with God") is the dominant
informal greeting, with `Bok` as its "more colloquial version," and Italian-derived `Ciao` is
noted as increasingly used in Croatia (p.8-9). This is a directly source-flagged, high-value
register/political annotation.

### Absence of the article

Croatian and Serbian have no article; `putnik` can mean "a traveller" or "the traveller" depending
on context (p.9).

### Word families

The book teaches vocabulary by root-family grouping rather than isolated items, e.g. `put` (path)
-> `autoput`, `putnik`, `putovati`, `putna torba`, `preko puta`; and `govor` (speech) -> `razgovor`,
`dogovor`, `dogovoriti se`, `ugovor` (p.9).

### Personal pronouns and pro-drop

Croatian/Serbian verb endings mark person clearly, so subject pronouns in the nominative are
normally omitted except for emphasis (p.9-10). `ti` is the familiar singular "you" (family, close
friends, children, animals); `vi` is used both as the plural "you" and as the formal singular
"you" for social distance/respect (p.9).

### The verb *biti* (to be)

`Biti` has short enclitic forms (`sam, si, je, smo, ste, su`) and long stressed forms
(`jesam, jesi, je, jesmo, jeste, jesu`). The short forms are the norm; the long forms are used
only in three contexts: (1) yes/no questions of the pattern `stressed-verb + li + subject`
(e.g. `Jeste li Englez?`); (2) one-word answers to such questions (`Jesam.`); (3) for special
emphasis (`Jeste li umorni? Ja jesam, ali Alan nije.`). A footnote explicitly flags a
Croatian-specific usage: "the short form of the third person singular `je` is generally used
instead of the long form `jest`... If you are using the language in countries other than Croatia,
the long form should be used" (p.11) — an explicit regional-usage annotation. The negative form
of `biti` is `nisam, nisi, nije, nismo, niste, nisu` (p.11).

### Enclitics and word order

Short forms of `biti`, short personal pronouns, the interrogative particle `li`, and the reflexive
particle `se` are all enclitics: unstressed and pronounced as if part of the preceding word,
therefore never placed first in a sentence or clause. They must be placed immediately after the
first stressed word or phrase in the sentence (p.12). Word order shifts depending on whether the
personal pronoun is present, e.g. `Mi smo gladni` vs `Gladni smo` (identical meaning, the pronoun
version being more emphatic, p.12-13).

### Formation of questions

Four patterns are given: (a) intonation alone (informal/spoken); (b) interrogative word +
verb (`Zašto učite hrvatski?`); (c) verb + `li` (the *favoured Croatian pattern* — `Dolazite li
često ovamo?`); and (d) verb + `da li` question marker, which the book explicitly flags with an
NB: "This structure is not favoured in Croatian. Use (b): verb + li" (p.13-14) — a directly marked
Croatian/Serbian register preference. Negative questions use `zar` + negative verb (`Zar nisi
Englez?`).

### Nationalities

Country/language/nationality triads mostly follow a pattern where the country name is the
feminine adjective form (`Engleska` "England" ~ `engleski` "English"), but the specific
nationality nouns (`Englez`, `Engleskinja`, `Hrvat`, `Hrvatica`, etc.) must be individually
memorized. A full regional roster is given: Slovenia/Slovene, Bosnia/Bosnian, Herzegovina/
Herzegovinian, Serbia/Serb, Montenegro/Montenegrin, Macedonia/Macedonian, plus British Isles
nationalities (p.15).

### Culture point: introductions

`Drago mi je` / `milo mi je` ("I am glad/delighted") are used frequently in introductions.
Strangers introduce themselves directly by name or surname while shaking hands — a third party is
not needed to make an introduction, unlike some English-speaking conventions (p.16).

### Verbs 1: main conjugation classes

Regular verbs fall into three classes named for their 1st-person-singular present ending: `-am`
(from infinitives in `-ati`, e.g. `imati -> imam`, `morati -> moram`); `-im` (from infinitives in
`-iti` or sometimes `-ati`/`-eti`, e.g. `govoriti -> govorim`, `držati -> držim`,
`voljeti -> volim`); `-em` (the most frequent class, derived from many different infinitives,
e.g. `razumjeti -> razumijem`, `zvati se -> zovem se`) (p.19-20).

### Verbs 2: aspect

Most Croatian/Serbian verbs come in imperfective/perfective pairs. Imperfective marks ongoing/
incomplete action; perfective marks complete/limited action. The book illustrates this with
`stvarati` (imp., "to be creating," noun `stvaranje` "the process of creating") vs `stvoriti`
(pf., "to create and complete," noun `stvorenje` "a creature/the thing created"). Further worked
examples: `da popušim cigaretu` (perfective — completing the smoke before returning to the
compartment) vs `pušiti` (imperfective, "to be smoking" in general); `Idem popiti kavu`
(perfective — drinking the whole cup) vs `Piješ li pivo?` (imperfective — "do you drink beer" in
general, or "are you currently drinking") (p.20-22).

### Word formation: the verb *ići* and verbal prefixes

`Ići` ("to go") is bi-aspectual and the only neutral verb of motion; all its derivatives add a
prefix that specifies the nature of the movement, and once the new meaning is established, a new
imperfective must be separately formed to pair with the new perfective. Prepositions used as
prefixes: `u` "in, into," `do` "up to," `na` "on, on to," `od` "away from," `s` "down from."
Other common (non-prepositional) prefixes: `po`, `pre` ("over"), `pro` ("passing by"), `raz`
("dispersal"). Worked derivative pairs given: `otići`/`odlaziti` (leave), `doći`/`dolaziti`
(come), `ući`/`ulaziti` (enter), `izaći`/`izlaziti` (exit), `sići`/`silaziti` (get off),
`naći`/`nalaziti` (find — a semantically shifted derivative), `poći`/`polaziti` (set off),
`prijeći`/`prelaziti` (cross), `proći`/`prolaziti` (pass by) (p.23-24).

### Sentence building: verb-dependence patterns and the Croatian/Serbian split

Two patterns for one verb depending on another are given: (a) `main verb + infinitive`
(`Moram izići`) — explicitly marked "the construction favoured in Croatian"; (b)
`main verb + da (conjunction) + present tense` (`Moram da iziđem`) — with the book stating flatly
"Serbian favours model (b)" (p.24-25). This is a clean, source-marked B/C/S syntactic-preference
contrast, directly relevant to register/dialect modeling.

### Reflexive verbs

Reflexive verbs pair a normally conjugated verb with the indeclinable enclitic particle `se`,
which follows the same enclitic word-order rules as short `biti` forms — it goes after all other
enclitics except `je` (3sg biti). Some verbs are always reflexive (`sjećati se`/`sjetiti se`, "to
remember"); others alternate between reflexive and ordinary transitive use with a shift in meaning
(`Zovem sina svaki dan` "I call my son every day" vs `Zovem se Crvenkapica` "I am called Little
Red Riding Hood") (p.25-26).

### Adverbs

Adverbs are indeclinable (one form only). Some must be individually memorized (`ponekad`,
`pomalo`); others are simply the neuter nominative singular form of the corresponding adjective
(`dobro`, `rado`, `poslovno`) (p.26).

### Nouns: gender and declension classes

Every noun has masculine, feminine, or neuter gender, generally predictable from the nominative
ending. **Masculine**: mostly end in a consonant (hard or soft: `c, č, ć, dž, đ, j, lj, nj, š, ž`
are soft, all others hard); some end in `-o`/`-e` (many proper names, e.g. `Marko`, `Đorđe`, and
nouns with a historically mobile `-l`, e.g. `ugao` "corner" ~ `u uglu` "in the corner"). Masculine
nominative plural is generally `-i`, with monosyllables commonly gaining an extra syllable
(`stol -> stolovi`, `vlak -> vlakovi`); soft-final nouns take `-evi` (`muž -> muževi`); the
`k+i -> ci` consonant alternation applies before the plural ending (`putnik -> putnici`).
**A direct Croatian/Serbian lexical-dialectal contrast is given**: "table" is `stol` in Croatian
but `sto` in Serbian (`na stolu` "on the table") (p.30). **Neuter**: end in `-o`/`-e`
(`selo`, `more`); nominative plural `-a` (`sela`, `mora`). **Feminine**: the great majority end in
`-a` (`putovnica`, `luka`, `torba`), with nominative plural `-e` (`gospođe`, `torbe`); a smaller
class ends in a consonant, including all abstract nouns in `-ost` (`ljubaznost` "kindness"), plus
`noć` "night," `stvar` "thing," `zvijer` "wild animal" — this class takes nominative plural `-i`
(`noći`, `stvari`) (p.29-31).

### The nominative case

Two main uses: (1) subject of a sentence/clause; (2) complement after `biti` (e.g. `Alan je
Škot`, `Moj sin je dobar student`) (p.32).

### The vocative case

Used for addressing persons in speech or at the start of letters. No vocative endings exist for
neuter nouns or for plurals of any gender — only two sets of endings need to be learned.
**Masculine**: hard consonants take `-e` (`gospodin -> gospodine`); soft consonants take `-u`
(`prijatelj -> prijatelju`; `Antić -> Antiću`). **Feminine**: `-a`-ending nouns take `-o`
(`gospođa -> gospođo`; `Nada -> Nado`); diminutives in `-ca` take `-ce` (`gospodica ->
gospodice` "Miss"; `Ankica -> Ankice` — noting these forms can also be men's names with
diminutive feminine-looking endings, e.g. `Ivica -> Ivice`); consonant-final feminine nouns take
`-i` (`noći!`) (p.34).

### Register spectrum in letter-writing (explicit source framing)

The book presents three full worked letter examples explicitly graded by formality: (1) informal,
to a friend — salutation `Dragi [name]`, sign-off `Pusu, [name]` (a kiss); (2) more formal,
addressee slightly known — salutation `Dragi gospodine [surname]`, sign-off `Srdačan pozdrav`
("warm greetings"); (3) addressee unknown or known only officially — salutation `Štovana
gospođo [surname]` ("Dear/respected Mrs..."), opening idiom `Slobodna sam Vam se javiti u vezi...`
("I am taking the liberty of writing to you in connection with..."), sign-off `Sa štovanjem`
("Yours sincerely," lit. "with respect") (p.34-35). A footnote also flags `gospođo + first name`
as "a useful intermediary stage between the formal Gđa + surname, and the more familiar use of the
first name on its own" — the book explicitly notes English has no equivalent gradient (p.34). This
is a directly source-marked, three-tier formality scale, high-value for register modeling.

### Word formation: adjectives from proper nouns

Adjectives derived from proper nouns are written with a lower-case initial letter even though the
source noun is capitalized (`rimski` from `Rim`, `bečki` from `Beč`, `švicarski` from
`Švicarska`, `jadranski` from `Jadran`) (p.35-36).

### Double negatives

Two or more negative words in a sentence do not cancel to a positive meaning — a negative pronoun
such as `nitko` ("no one") or `ništa` ("nothing") requires a negative verb, e.g. `Nemamo ništa za
prijaviti` ("We have nothing to declare"), `Ništa ne vidim kroz prozor`, `Nikad ne putujem
autostopom` (p.36-37).

### Register nuance: farewell finality (source-flagged)

`Zbogom` is glossed as "goodbye," but the source adds an explicit pragmatic-register note: it
"conveys a certain finality!" — contrasted implicitly with the more neutral, everyday `do
viđenja` used throughout the unit's other dialogues (p.33, p.38).

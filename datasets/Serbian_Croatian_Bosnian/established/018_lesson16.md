# Serbian/Croatian/Bosnian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Lesson 16 (Šesnaesta lekcija)

**Source:** Ronelle Alexander & Ellen Elias-Bursać, *Bosnian, Croatian, Serbian, a Textbook: With
Exercises and Basic Grammar* (2nd ed., University of Wisconsin Press, 2010), "Šesnaesta lekcija /
Lesson Sixteen," pp. 243-260 (PDF pages 264-281).

**Coverage note:** every grammar point and every distinct vocabulary item from this lesson's four
`RJEČNIK [J] REČNIK [E]` glossary boxes (A1, A2, A3 x2), plus every explicit `[B]`/`[C]`/`[S]`
dialect tag the source itself marks. Repeated drill/vježbe exercises (sections B1, B2, C1-C4) are
skipped per `../../00_Reference_Extraction_Spec.md`. This source has a real PDF text layer
(`pdftotext` produced clean output) — no vision-reading was needed, so Vision Reading Confidence is
`n/a` throughout; no marginalia issue applies.

**File split:** this lesson's vocabulary is large (four glossary boxes, ~250+ distinct lexical
items after merging pure Ekavian/Ijekavian spelling doublets into single rows). Per the extraction
spec's "don't let a single file grow unbounded" rule, it is split across three files sharing this
dispatch's assigned number:
- **This file** — grammar points, the B/C/S regional-showcase structural note, and Vocabulary Part 1
  (glossary box A1, both halves: the film-festival letter vocabulary and the household vocabulary).
- `018_lesson16_vocab2.md` — Vocabulary Part 2 (glossary box A2: the research/study-abroad letters).
- `018_lesson16_vocab3.md` — Vocabulary Part 3 (glossary box A3, both halves: the Belgrade-course
  letters), plus Croatian-specific month names and letter-writing formula phrases drawn from the
  letters themselves (not separately glossed by the source, but genuinely new lexical items in this
  chunk).

**Merge convention used in the Vocabulary tables below:** where the source lists two headwords that
differ *only* by the historical jat vowel (Ekavian `e` vs. Ijekavian `je`/`ije`, e.g. `dòspeti` /
`dòspjeti`) with no `[B]`/`[C]`/`[S]` bracket on either form, they are merged into a single row
(`Term` shows both spellings) since they are the same lexeme. Where the source instead gives two
genuinely distinct lexical roots or forms carrying explicit `[B]`/`[C]`/`[S]` tags (e.g. `sport
[B,S]` vs. `šport [C]`, or `interesìrati [B,C]` vs. `interesovati [B,S]`), each is kept as its own
row — this is exactly the kind of explicit dialect annotation the extraction spec flags as
highest-value content, so it is preserved rather than collapsed.

## Grammar points

### Formal-address capitalization: `Vi`/`Vaš` (and archaic `Ti`/`Tvoj`)

In a formal letter addressed to a single person, the pronoun `Vi` ("you") and possessive `Vaš`
("your"), in all their case forms, may be capitalized (a capital "V"). Current language guides still
recommend this, though the source notes many native speakers now consider it a "quaint and
antiquated custom." Capitalization applies only when `vi` refers to one addressee — never when it
addresses more than one person. One occasionally also finds capitalized "T" (`Ti`, `Tvoj`) in
correspondence, but the source flags this as considerably more old-fashioned and far less common in
today's usage (p. 250, in-text note). All three letters extracted in Vocabulary Part 2 and Part 3
below demonstrate this convention in their formal register (`Vaš`, `Vas`, `Vama` capitalized
throughout Andrea's and Ana's letters and their replies).

### Reciprocal "each other": `jèdan ... drugi`, gender agreement

The reciprocal notion "each other"/"one another" is built from a form of `jèdan` ("one") plus a form
of `drugi` ("other/another"). Gender agreement rule (p. 259 grammar note, cross-referencing p. 237
where the construction was first introduced): if all persons involved are male, or no specific
gender is indicated, both elements take masculine forms; if all are female, both take feminine
forms; if the group is mixed-gender, both elements switch to neuter forms — e.g. `Marko i Ana pìtaju
jèdno drugo` ("Marko and Ana ask each other," neuter because mixed-gender) or `òni idu jèdno za
drugim` ("they follow one another," also neuter/mixed). The construction's grammatical gender is
therefore governed by the referents' combined gender, not by any inherent gender of `jèdan`/`drugi`
themselves.

### Structural note: this lesson's letters as a deliberate B/C/S showcase

Not a single rule but a source-level structural pattern worth flagging per the extraction spec's
"never skip explicit dialectal annotation" instruction: this lesson's three letter-and-reply pairs
are written as complete, self-consistent regional-standard specimens rather than isolated
word-level `[B]`/`[C]`/`[S]` swaps.
- **Sarajevo pair** (Dino's letter and its reply, pp. 244-247): Ijekavian, Latin script, Bosnian
  lexical choices throughout (e.g. `dòspio`, `vidjeti`, international-style month names `april`,
  `jula`, `sèptembra`, `àvgusta`).
- **Zagreb pair** (Andrea's letter and Marko Knjižić's reply, pp. 248-252): Ijekavian, Latin script,
  Croatian-specific lexicon and Croatian month names (`svibnja` = May, `lipnja` = June, `srpnja` =
  July, `listopada` = October — see Vocabulary Part 3) and Croatian-specific university vocabulary
  (`sveùčilište`, `kàtedra`).
- **Belgrade pair** (Ana Đurić's letter and Stojan Popović's reply, pp. 254-257): printed entirely in
  **Cyrillic script**, Ekavian, with Serbian-specific lexicon and international-style month names
  (`фебруар`, `маја`, `а̀вгуста`).

This is the book's most sustained single-lesson demonstration of the three standards operating as
complete systems (script choice + jat reflex + lexicon simultaneously) rather than only isolated
word-for-word substitution — genuinely useful context for any later synthesis work distinguishing
"a word tagged for one standard" from "a whole register/system tagged for one standard."

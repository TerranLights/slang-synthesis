**Pages covered:** Printed pages 89–116 (PDF pages 93–120 of `29.Serbian Folk Tales.pdf`; empirically confirmed offset = printed + 4, holds consistently across this whole range).

**Vision-reading note:** This is a scanned book with no usable text layer, entirely in Cyrillic. All content below was read visually page-by-page. **Vision-reading guard applied on every page**: no handwritten marginalia, underlining, or pencil annotation was found anywhere in this range — all margin content is the publisher's own consistently-typeset "+"-glosses, matching the surrounding printed page. Nothing was excluded.

**Content-type note (different from pedagogical sources in this project):** This is pure literary/folkloric narrative — fairy tales, not a grammar textbook. There is no parallel English translation. Per the dispatch spec, coverage here is **vocabulary-only**: for each tale, the genuinely interesting/archaic/regional/dialectal vocabulary is extracted and glossed (using the book's own inline "+" glosses where present, otherwise an original gloss with confidence flagged). No plot/prose is reproduced or summarized beyond a one-line orientation note per tale.

3. **ЦАРЕВ ЗЕТ И КРИЛАТА БАБА** ("The Tsar's Son-in-Law and the Winged Crone") — pp. 101–106 (PDF 105–110), complete within this chunk. A boy punished for refusing to reveal his dream ends up imprisoned next to a princess, proves his worth by javelin-throw, and wins a wager against jealous vizier's sons with help of superhuman "wonder-companion" folk-helpers (a fast runner, a sharp-eyed archer, a bottomless eater/drinker, a lake-drinker) against a flying witch.
4. **У ЛАЖИ СУ КРАТКЕ НОГЕ** ("Lies Have Short Legs" — a well-known Serbian proverb-tale) — pp. 109–116 (PDF 113–120), **not finished within this chunk** (continues past p. 116 into the next assigned range). A blind, deaf old tsar sends his three sons in turn to find magical rejuvenating water; the two elder sons fail/lie, the youngest succeeds with help of a hermit, a bird-speaking hunter, and an old eagle. The title proverb itself has not yet been explicitly stated/moralized within this page range — the elder brothers' lie (substituting seawater and claiming credit) is visible in-progress but the tale's resolution/moral statement falls in the next chunk.

## Cross-tale observation (linguistic, worth flagging for the mechanics-analysis phase)

This range shows visible **Ekavian/Ijekavian mixing within the same tale and even within the same sentence** (e.g. "лепо"/"лијепо", "нема"/"нема" both appear; "чоек" for "човек" appears as a colloquial contraction). This is consistent with a folk-tale anthology compiled from informants across different dialect regions and merely lightly edited, rather than normalized to one standard — a genuinely useful register/dialect-mixing data point distinct from the grammar-reference sources in this project, which tend to present one normalized standard.

A second cluster worth flagging: this stretch of tales is unusually dense with **Ottoman Turkish loanwords** embedded directly into the narrative voice (not just in dialogue) — институт names for offices, measures, and objects (дућан, калфа, везир, мушир, ферман, аршин, ока, апсана, дувар, инат, рахат, кахвица, дова, узун, шенлук, селамет, вилајет, зијафет, сеирити, царски татарин). These read as ordinary narrative vocabulary to the tale-teller, not as marked foreign borrowings — evidence of how deeply Ottoman-era Turkish vocabulary was absorbed into vernacular Serbian oral literature, all glossed by the publisher itself as needing explanation for a modern (2000) young reader.

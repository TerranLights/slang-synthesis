# Serbian, Croatian, Bosnian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Osamnaesta lekcija / Lesson Eighteen

**Source:** Ronelle Alexander & Ellen Elias-Bursać, *Bosnian, Croatian, Serbian, a Textbook: With
Exercises and Basic Grammar* (2nd ed., 2010), Lesson 18 ("Osamnaesta lekcija / Lesson Eighteen"),
pp. 277–286 (PDF pages 298–307).

**Coverage note:** every grammar point and every distinct vocabulary item from this lesson's five
RJEČNIK[J]/REČNIK[E] vocabulary boxes (pp. 277, 278, 280, 281, 282), plus every explicit `[B]`/
`[C]`/`[S]` and Ijekavian(`[J]`)/Ekavian(`[E]`) annotation the source itself marks. This lesson is
reading/comprehension-focused — two poems ("Jesenje veče" by A. G. Matoš, "Notturno" by Tin
Ujević) plus a biographical reading about both poets — rather than a new-grammar-presentation
lesson. Its own "GRAMATIKA" section (p. 278) is a set of application exercises recycling
previously-taught morphology (verb identification, subject identification, instrumental case,
accusative case) rather than new explanatory prose; the lesson's only genuinely new explanatory
grammar content is the "god"-particle/"bilo" note (p. 283) and the nedòstajati dative-logical-
subject footnote (p. 282) — both captured below. Repeated drill/translation exercises (B3, B4, C1,
C2) are skipped per the extraction spec's coverage rule — they recycle vocabulary already captured
in the boxes below and translate free-standing English sentences rather than introducing new BCS
lexical or grammatical content. Every exercise/question block in this lesson is itself
dialect-tagged per the book's `[B]`/`[C]`/`[S]` convention (parallel wordings for Bosnian/
Croatian/Serbian, occasionally split three ways, occasionally with the Serbian variant additionally
given in Cyrillic) — noted here rather than itemized line-by-line, since these are instruction-
wording variants, not vocabulary items. Source has a clean embedded text layer (`pdftotext`
extracted it directly); no vision-reading was required for this page range, and no handwritten
marginalia was present.

## Grammar points

### The universal/indefinite particle "god"

Attached after a question word, the particle **god** functions roughly like English "-ever":
`kad god` "whenever," `gde god` [E] / `gdje god` [J] "wherever," `kolìko god` "however much." The
book flags this as cross-referenced to its own grammar-reference sections [134a, 143k] rather than
explaining it fully here — this lesson's box (p. 283) is a compact pointer/summary rather than the
full presentation. (p. 283)

### Indefinite pronouns with "bilo"

The word **bìlo** can be prefixed to a question word with a similar "-ever" meaning: `bìlo šta`
[B,S] / `bìlo što` [C] "whatever," `bìlo kòga pitaš` "whomever you ask." Presented alongside the
"god" note as an alternative indefinite-forming strategy, cross-referenced to [143k]. (p. 283)

### Dative logical subject with "nedòstajati" ("to miss")

A footnoted "tricky translation" point: the verb **nedòstajati** inverts the subject/object mapping
of English "to miss." What an English speaker would treat as the grammatical object of "miss" (the
thing/person missed) is the BCS grammatical subject (nominative case, and triggers verb agreement),
while the person doing the missing appears in the dative case as a logical/experiencer subject. So
`nedòstajao mu je Zàgreb` is literally "Zagreb was missing to him" but translates as "he missed
Zagreb." (p. 282, footnote) Note the same dative-experiencer pattern recurs in this lesson's
vocabulary in `pòći za rùkom + Dat.` ("[it] worked out for [someone]") and `stalo [mu je] do + Gen.`
("it matters to [him]") — three separate impersonal/experiencer constructions where the person
affected sits in an oblique case rather than as a nominative subject.

### GRAMATIKA exercises (p. 278): applied review, not new presentation

The lesson's own "GRAMATIKA" heading contains four instructions applying morphology already taught
in earlier lessons to the Matoš poem, rather than new explanatory content: (1) circle every verb in
the poem; (2) identify the grammatical subject of every clause/phrase; (3) underline every instance
of the instrumental case; (4) identify which sentence opens with an accusative-case phrase. This
lesson's grammatical "content," in other words, is application/review of verb-form recognition,
subject identification, and the instrumental and accusative cases via a literary text, not a new
rule. (p. 278)

### Relative clauses with "koji" and purpose clauses with "da bi" (practiced, not re-taught)

Exercises B3 (pp. 283–284) and B4 (pp. 284–285) drill two constructions already introduced in
earlier lessons rather than presenting them fresh: combining two sentences into one using the
relative pronoun **kòji** ("who/which/that," agreeing in gender/number with its antecedent and
taking whatever case its own clause role requires), and forming purpose clauses with **da bi** +
a conditional-form verb ("in order to," "so that"). No new explanatory grammar text accompanies
either exercise in this page range — both are pure application drills — consistent with the
coverage rule's instruction to skip drills that don't introduce new content, so they are noted here
only as the grammatical constructions this lesson's exercises target, not re-explained.

## Grammar points

### Dialect-tag convention: `[B]`/`[C]`/`[S]`

The boxed note on p. 23 explains the convention this book uses throughout: letters after a word
signal either (a) that the word is used across all of BCS but an alternate word for the same idea
exists in a more restricted area (e.g. *loš* `[B,C,S]` "bad" alongside the S-only alternate *rđav*
`[S]`), or (b) that a given meaning has two competing words and different varieties prefer different
ones (e.g. "her/hers" is *njen* in B and S but either *njen* or *njezin* in C).

### Addressing people: the vocative case

Nouns take a distinct ending when a person is directly addressed. A common masculine name typically
adds *-e* (*Mehmed* → *Mehmede!*); a feminine name sometimes replaces its final *-a* with *-o*
(*Nada* → *Nado!*). (p. 20)

### Accusative case

Beyond the vocative, noun cases express grammatical relations; the citation/subject form is the
"nominative" (abbreviated Nsg), and the "accusative" (Asg) expresses, among other things, direct
object function. Feminine nouns/adjectives form the accusative by replacing *-a* with *-u* (*moja
mačka* → *moju mačku*). Neuter nouns and many masculine nouns share the same form in nominative and
accusative (e.g. *tvoj udžbenik*, *njegovo pismo* could be either Nsg or Asg). (p. 20)

### Time expressions: days of the week

To say something happens on a given day, use the preposition *u* plus the accusative form of the
day name, as in *u četvrtak* "on Thursday," *u subotu* "on Saturday." (p. 20)

### Negation

A verb is negated by placing the particle *ne* directly in front of it, pronounced as if fused into
a single word (sometimes shifting the accent onto *ne*). A few verbs instead have dedicated
suppletive negative forms: *nemati* "not to have" (*nemam*, *nemaš*...) and the negative present of
*biti* "to be" (*nisam*, *nisi*...). (p. 20)

### Present tense of verbs

Infinitives usually end in *-ti*; the present tense falls into three conjugation classes named for
the 3sg present ending: *-a* (a-conjugation, e.g. *imati* → *imam*), *-i* (i-conjugation, e.g.
*učiti* → *učim*), and *-e* (e-conjugation, e.g. *pisati* → *pišem*). 1st/2nd person forms simply
add *-m*/*-š*/*-mo*/*-te* to the 3sg stem; the 3pl ending differs by conjugation. When the present
stem isn't predictable from an *-iti*/*-ati* infinitive (e.g. *pisati* → *pišem*, or *zvati* →
*zovem*), it must be memorized per-verb. (p. 21)

### The conjunction *da*

Where English uses an infinitive after a verb like "want" ("I want to study"), BCS varieties differ:
Croatian almost always uses an infinitive for the second verb (*želimo učiti*), Serbian usually
prefers *da* + present tense (*želimo da učimo*) though the infinitive is also possible, and Bosnian
uses both about equally. When the two verbs have different subjects, only the *da* + present-tense
construction is possible (*želim da učimo* "I want us to study"). (p. 21)

### Variant forms: ijekavian and ekavian

Many words alternate between a vowel *-e-* ("ekavian," tagged `[E]`) and the sequence *-je-*/*-ije-*
("ijekavian," tagged `[J]`); any one speaker consistently uses one or the other. B and C use only
ijekavian; S uses both, split geographically (ekavian in most of Serbia proper, ijekavian in Bosnia,
southwestern Serbia, and Montenegro). Both forms are typically given unmarked side by side in
vocabulary lists; the meaning never differs, and which specific *-e-*/*-ije-* instances are affected
must be learned word by word (e.g. *meso* "meat" and *pijem* "I drink" don't alternate at all). (p.
21, reinforced by a second boxed note on p. 23 specifically about ekavian vs. ijekavian S)

### Object pronouns: clitic (short) forms

BCS has two accusative object-pronoun paradigms. The clitic (short) forms are unaccented and occupy
second position in the clause: 1sg *me*, 2sg *te*, 3sg m *ga*, 3sg n *ga*, 3sg f *je*/*ju*, 1pl
*nas*, 2pl *vas*, 3pl (all genders) *ih*. Croatian uses *je* and *ju* more or less interchangeably
for 3sg feminine; Bosnian and Serbian restrict *ju* to certain past-tense constructions, so B/S
learners should default to *je*. (p. 21)

### Accusative case of animate nouns

Masculine nouns denoting animate beings take a distinct accusative ending *-a* (with the
accompanying adjective ending *-og*), e.g. Nom *visoki student* → Acc *visokog studenta*. The
accusative of the interrogative pronouns *ko*/*tko* "who" is *koga*. (p. 22)

### Object pronouns: full (long) forms

The full/accented object-pronoun forms can appear in any sentence position, are used as the object
of a preposition, or add emphasis: 1sg *mene*, 2sg *tebe*, 3sg m *njega*, 3sg n *njega*, 3sg f *nju*,
1pl *nas*, 2pl *vas*, 3pl (all genders) *njih*. As with the clitics, 3sg m and n share a form, and
3pl uses one form for all three genders. (p. 22)

### Demonstrative pronominal adjectives

English "this"/"that" corresponds to three BCS demonstratives: *ovaj* "this [close by]," *onaj*
"that [far away]," and *taj*, covering the neutral middle ground. Like other adjectives, these agree
in gender/case/number with the noun they modify. (p. 23)

### Fleeting vowels in adjectives

Many adjectives' masculine nominative singular short form ends in a consonant preceded by *-a-*
(e.g. *pametan*). This *-a-* is a "fleeting vowel": it disappears in every other form of the
adjective (*pametno*, *pametna*). When the fleeting vowel sits in the suffix *-ak*, its loss can
also trigger a consonant shift in the preceding consonant, as in *nizak* → *nisko*, *niska*. (p. 23)

### Accusative of animate masculines and fleeting vowels

As with adjectives, masculine animate nouns can carry a fleeting vowel that drops before the
accusative *-a* ending, e.g. Nom *pas* → Acc *psa*. Sometimes the stem changes further, as with
*otac* → *oca* "father." Vocabulary listings give the accusative singular directly whenever a
fleeting vowel is present. (p. 24)

### Verbal aspect, introduction

Most verbal ideas in BCS have two corresponding verbs, sharing a root but differing in "aspect": the
perfective (P) refers to a single, actual or envisioned completed action, while the imperfective (I)
refers to repeated action, an action in progress, or the general concept of the action. E.g. *kupiti*
(P) vs. *kupovati* (I), both "to buy." Vocabulary listings mark these (P)/(I) going forward. (p. 25)

### Prepositions

Prepositions govern a specific case. Among those requiring the accusative are *za* (nearly always
"for") and *u*, which with a weekday name means "on" that day (weekday names are not capitalized).
(p. 25)

### Long and short adjectives

Most adjectives have both a short and a long form (e.g. masc. nom. sg. *brz* short vs. *brzi* long).
Some adjectives (e.g. *mali* "little," adjectives in *-ski*) exist only in the long form; others,
including pronominal adjectives like *njegov*, exist only in the short form. Feminine and neuter
distinguish short from long only by the final vowel's length (*brza* short vs. *brza* long, with
occasional accent differences, e.g. *crvena* vs. *crvena*). The short form is used after *biti* "to
be" (*onaj pas je crn* "that dog is black"); the long form is used in most other positions. There is
also a "longer" form (an extra *-a* added to the long adjective ending *-og*), more common in
Croatian — covered further at "Long and 'longer' adjectives," p. 54. (p. 25)

### Adjectives ending in -o

The masculine short form of some adjectives ends in *-o* rather than the usual consonant, with the
stem of all other forms ending in *-l*, e.g. *debeo, debelo, debela* "fat." The word for "white"
belongs to this pattern in ekavian (*beo, belo, bela*); in ijekavian the masculine form instead ends
in *-l* (*bijel, bijelo, bijela*). (p. 25)

### Verbs in -ovati

Verbs whose infinitive ends in *-ovati* replace the infinitive's *-ova-* sequence with *-uj-* in the
present tense (e.g. *kupovati* "to buy" → *kupujem, kupuješ, kupuje, kupujemo, kupujete, kupuju*).
Students must attend closely to this infinitive/present-tense mismatch. (p. 25)

### Addressing people: politeness formulas

Politeness formulas take different endings depending on whether the addressee is normally addressed
as *ti* or *vi*. "Excuse me" is *oprosti*/*izvini* (to *ti*) or *oprostite*/*izvinite* (to *vi*).
*Izvoli*/*izvolite* means either "May I help you?" or "Here you are," depending on context. (p. 27)

### The verb *moći*

"Can, be able" is slightly irregular: the 1sg and 3pl forms differ from each other only in accent
and vowel length. The 3sg form (*može*) doubles as an impersonal "OK, fine, possible." Conjugation:
*mogu, možeš, može, možemo, možete, mogu*. (p. 27)

# Serbian/Croatian/Bosnian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Peta lekcija / Lesson Five

**Source:** Ronelle Alexander & Ellen Elias-Bursać, *Bosnian, Croatian, Serbian, a Textbook: With
Exercises and Basic Grammar* (2nd ed., 2010), Lesson Five ("Peta lekcija"), pp. 63–78 (PDF pages
84–99 of `01.Bosnian Croatian Serbian a Textbook With Exercises and Basic Grammar.pdf`).

**Coverage note:** every grammar point and every distinct vocabulary item from A1–A4 vocabulary
boxes, the grammar (zGRAMMAR) subsections interleaved between them, the C1 vocabulary box, and the
short "Geografska pitanja / Zemljopisna pitanja" vocabulary box at the end of the lesson, plus every
explicit `[B]`/`[C]`/`[S]` (and `[E]`/`[J]` — Ekavian/Ijekavian) dialect tag the source marks. Also
included: genuinely new lexical items introduced only inside a drill's `☞` replacement list (these
introduce vocabulary the repeated drill structure itself does not — per the extraction spec's
coverage rule, the repeated sentence-frame drills B1–B6 and C3/C4/C6 are otherwise skipped, but their
replacement-word lists and fill-in-the-blank answer banks are mined for new terms). This source has a
clean PDF text layer (`pdftotext` produced usable text for every page in this range) — no vision-
reading was needed, so **Vision Reading Confidence is `n/a` throughout** and no marginalia-exclusion
issue arose. One text-layer artifact is flagged below (the `pet`/`peti, peto, peta` row order in the
A1 vocabulary box) since it is worth a reader's caution even though it isn't a marginalia case.

**Column-value shorthand used below:**
- Usage Tier: essentially everything in a beginner textbook's lesson-vocabulary is `core`; the
  exceptions are called out per row.
- Weight/Frequency: `—` throughout (per spec — a lesson's pedagogical ordering is not real frequency
  data).
- Attested Era: `contemporary (source published 2010, 2nd ed.)` throughout.
- Source Type: `grammar_reference` throughout.
- Transcription Confidence / Vision Reading Confidence: `n/a` / `n/a` throughout (real text layer, no
  subtitle/transcript involved).
- Attested Region / Geographic Scope: filled in only where the source itself marks a `[B]`/`[C]`/`[S]`
  (national-standard) or `[E]`/`[J]` (Ekavian/Ijekavian pronunciation-zone, which cuts across national
  lines) tag; `—` otherwise.

**A note on a recurring three/four-way pattern.** This lesson is unusually rich in explicit dialect
marking compared to a typical vocabulary chunk: several concepts (week, "on foot," "in the evening,"
"theater," "doctor," kinship terms) have not just B-vs-S or C-vs-S binary pairs but three- or four-way
variant clusters (e.g. "week" = `nèdelja [S]` / `nèdjelja [B,S]` / `sèdmica [B,S]` / `tjedan [C]`), and
the source's own "More on vocabulary differences" note (p.70) explicitly warns that a `[C]`/`[S]` tag

## Grammar points

### Genitive plural (Gpl) formation
Two Gpl endings exist: `-a` (required for neuter nouns, nearly all masculine nouns, and most `-a`
feminine nouns) and `-i` (required for consonant-stem feminine nouns and three masculine nouns:
`mesec`/`mjesec` "month," `ljudi` "people," and `sat` in the meaning "hour"; also found in a sizeable
number of `-a` feminine nouns). Both endings carry a long vowel, and the vowel preceding a Gpl `-a`
is also lengthened. If Gpl `-a` would otherwise follow two consonants (other than the clusters `st`,
`zd`, `št`, `žd`, `šć`, `šč`, `ždž`), an epenthetic `-a-` is inserted between them. All adjectives take
`-ih` in Gpl. (pp. 63–64, source refs [57a]–[57c])

### Uses of the genitive plural
Gpl follows adverbs of measure (`malo`, `mnogo`, `puno`, `kolìko`); the corresponding genitive
singular is used instead when the noun refers to an uncountable mass (`puno kiše` "a lot of rain,"
`malo vòde` "a little water"). Gpl is also required after all numbers other than 1–4 (and compounds
ending in them, e.g. 21–24); if such a phrase is the sentence subject, the verb stays 3rd singular
(`Pet mačaka ide u grad` "Five cats are going to town"). (p. 64, [59a], [58])

### Adverbs of direction ("whither")
Croatian uses `kamo` for "where(-to)"; Bosnian and Serbian use `kuda` or `gde`/`gdje` interchangeably,
with no meaning difference from `kamo`. (p. 64, [55c])

### Distributive `po`
The preposition `po` expresses distribution ("each"/"apiece"); the case it governs depends on the
sentence but is almost always accusative. (p. 65, [59c])

### Uses of the genitive case, continued
`nema` ("there isn't/aren't," signaling non-existence) always takes a genitive object. `ìma` ("there
is/are") takes the nominative for a single countable unit but the genitive for a partitive meaning.
Certain verbs govern the genitive directly, e.g. `bòjati se` "to fear" and `tìcati se` "to concern"
(commonly in the fixed phrase `što se tiče + Gen` "as concerns..."). Some other fixed phrases also
take a genitive object, e.g. `ìgrati lopte` "play ball." (p. 66, [59b], [60])

### Prepositions with the accusative case
`u` and `na` both mean "motion toward" with the accusative. Which preposition a given noun takes
("u-words" vs. "na-words") must be memorized per noun — "u-words" tend to denote enclosed,
three-dimensional spaces, "na-words" tend to denote surfaces or more abstract concepts. (p. 66, [55a])

### Telling time
Time-of-day is expressed with the number plus the correct case of `sat` "hour," introduced by `u`
(e.g. `u čètiri sata` "at 4:00," `u pet sàti` "at 5:00"). Minutes after the hour use `i`; minutes
before use `do` (`u pet i deset` "at 5:10"; `u pet do deset` "at 9:55"). (p. 66, [61c])

### Motion verbs and aspect
`ići` "go" (imperfective) forms perfectives by prefixation, e.g. `dòći` "come." Each resulting
perfective then has its own imperfective, formed by adding the same prefix to the otherwise rare verb
`laziti`; hence `dòlaziti` is the imperfective of `dòći`. (p. 66, [54])

### The verb "want" (hteti/htjeti)
`hteti`/`htjeti` is, along with `moći`, the only BCS verb whose 1st singular present ends in `-u`
(`hòću`). 3rd singular and 3rd plural both end in `-e`, but the 3rd plural vowel is long (`hòće` vs.
`hòće` — distinguished by length only in speech). The negated form has an entirely separate
conjugation in which `ne-` replaces the affirmative's `ho-` syllable (`hòću` → `nèću`, etc.). (p. 66,
[52d])

### Genitive phrases of time
An adjective+noun phrase that names a point in time takes genitive singular with no preposition, e.g.
`ìduće sùbote` "next Saturday," `òvog vìkenda` "this weekend." (p. 68, [61b])

### Verbs and aspect (prefixation)
Adding a prefix to an imperfective verb usually yields a perfective with a related but distinct
meaning — not just a bare aspectual counterpart. Example: `do-` + `čekati` "wait" → `dòčekati` "meet
someone on arrival." (p. 68, [53b]) The same pattern recurs on p. 70: `jesti`/`piti` ("eat"/"drink" in
general) → `pòjesti`/`pòpiti` ("finish eating"/"drink up," referring to one specific occasion).

### The possessive adjective `svoj`
`svoj` "one's own" declines like `moj`/`tvoj` and is used when the possessor is the sentence's
subject. It's optional in 1st/2nd person (`volim moj pòsao` = `volim svoj pòsao`) but *required* in
3rd person for the reflexive meaning and *impossible* for the meaning "someone else's": `on voli svoj
pòsao` = "he likes his own work" vs. `on nè voli njègov pòsao` = "he doesn't like his [someone else's]
work." (p. 68, [72a])

### The verb `budem, budeš` etc. (second present tense of `biti`)
`biti` "to be" has a second, `bud-`-stem present tense used specifically in perfective-aspect
contexts, e.g. after `da` (`žèlim da budem advòkat` "I want to be a lawyer"). This same stem also
supplies the imperative (introduced in Lesson 7). (p. 68, [52c])

### Bosnian vocabulary: Turkish-derived kinship terms
Bosnian retains a set of Turkish-derived words reflecting its Islamic cultural heritage, most visibly
in family-relation terms: "mother's brother" is `ujak` in S and C but `dàidža` in B; "father's
brother" is `stric` in S and C but `àmidža` in B; "grandmother" is `baba`/`baka` in S and C but `nèna`
in B. (p. 68, [176a])

### Negation
Yes/no questions that anticipate a negative answer, or that express surprise, open with the particle
`zar` followed by a negated verb (`zar nè možete dòći dànas?` "can't you come today?"). The prefix
`ni-` turns interrogatives `ko`/`tko` and `šta` into negative pronouns `niko [B,S]`/`nitko [C]`
(Acc-Gen `nikoga`) "nobody" and `ništa` "nothing" — whenever these appear, the verb must *also* be
negated (`nè vidim nikoga` "I don't see anyone," a double-negative construction that is grammatically
required, not emphatic). The compound conjunction `ni… ni` "neither… nor" becomes `niti… niti` when
what's contrasted is a verb. (p. 70, [56d], [56a], [56c])

### More on vocabulary differences (B/C/S markedness)
Most B/C/S vocabulary is shared across all three standards. A subset of words is explicitly marked
`[C]`/`[S]` in the book's vocabulary lists (e.g. `vàni`/`napolju` "outside," `kàzalište`/`pòzorište`
"theater," `kìno`/`bioskop` "cinema," `vlak`/`voz` "train," `tjedan`/`nèdelja` "week"). Bosnian usage
varies per word: it uses both options for some concepts (`vàni`/`napolju`), prefers the Serbian term
for others (`pòzorište`, `voz`), prefers the Croatian term for others (`kìno`), and sometimes has its
own distinct word altogether (family terms). Critically, the source also flags that a `[C]`/`[S]` tag
does not always signal a strict binary split: some tagged words simply coexist alongside a
pan-regional `[B,C,S]` synonym rather than opposing it one-to-one (`abecèda [B,C,S]` vs. regionally
narrower `àzbuka [S]`; `paradajz [B,C,S]` vs. `rajčica [C]`; `trenùtak [B,C,S]` vs. `mòmenat [B,S]`).
(p. 70, [172a], [172b])

### Note on infinitive vs. `da` + present (explicit register/frequency discussion, p. 69)
The source flags, as an explicit named "Note," that both `žèlim BITI` (infinitive construction) and
`žèlim DA BUDEM` (`da` + present) are grammatical throughout B, C, and S, but their *relative
frequency* differs sharply by standard: the infinitive dominates in Croatian (so the book's Croatian
column uses only that form), while `da` + present is more frequent in Serbian (so the book's Serbian
column uses it predominantly, though not exclusively). Bosnian leans toward Croatian usage in this
respect, so the book's Bosnian column mostly (not always) uses the infinitive. This is presented as a
genuine frequency/register distinction, not a strict grammaticality difference — a rare case in this
chunk where the source itself makes a soft, non-binary usage claim rather than a hard `[B]`/`[C]`/`[S]`
tag.

### Observed pattern: `da li` vs. `-li`-inversion yes/no questions
Not one of the source's own numbered/footnoted grammar points in this page range, but a pattern
directly visible across the parallel A3 and A4 dialogues and worth flagging per the extraction spec's
emphasis on dialectal contrasts: the Bosnian/Croatian dialogue columns favor inverted `VERB-li`
questions (`Kaže li se "stric"...?`, p. 67; `Žèliš li da idemo...?`, p. 69) while the Serbian column
favors `Da li + VERB` (`Da li se kaže "àmidža"...?`, p. 67; `Da li žèliš da idemo...?`, p. 69). Flagged
here as an observed pattern in the source's own parallel-column dialogue data, not as an explicitly
stated rule in this chunk's grammar sections.

### Cultural notes (non-grammatical, briefly summarized)
- **Mealtimes** (p. 65): breakfast 6–7 a.m., a late-morning snack at work, `rùčak` (the main meal) in
  late afternoon (~4–5 p.m.) with family, and a lighter `vèčera` between 7–8 p.m.
- **University structure** (p. 66): universities in Bosnia and Herzegovina, Croatia, Montenegro, and
  Serbia are loosely federated collections of `fakùltet`-i (departments/schools) without a central
  campus, so students say they're going "to the faculty" (`na fakùltet`) rather than "to the
  university."
- **School shifts** (p. 72): elementary/secondary schools generally run two alternating shifts
  (morning one week, afternoon the next).
- **Finger-counting** (p. 64): in BCS, counting on fingers starts from the thumb (palm horizontal,
  facing the counter) rather than the index finger.

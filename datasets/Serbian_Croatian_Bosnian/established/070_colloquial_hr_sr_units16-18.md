## Grammar points

### Possessive pronouns moj/tvoj/svoj (declension)

Full singular and plural paradigm tables (N/G/D/A/P/I across m./nt./f.) for `moj`, with the note that
`tvoj` and `svoj` decline identically. Where longer and shorter case-form alternatives exist, the
shorter is more common (source refers to its own appendix §2 for the full alternation). Pages 172–173.

### Use of `svoj`

`Svoj` (reflexive possessive, "one's own") cannot normally stand in the nominative and cannot function
as the subject, since it refers *back to* the subject — except in a handful of fixed phrases (`On je
svoj čovjek`, "He is 'his own' man"). It applies to any person, singular or plural, to denote something
belonging to that person: `Vidjet ću svog sina` "I shall see my son"; `Idemo svojim autom` "We are going
in (by) our car." Page 173.

### Possessive pronouns naš/vaš (declension)

Full singular/plural declension table, parallel in structure to `moj` above. Page 174.

### Possessive adjectives njegov/njen(-jezin)/njihov

These are derived by taking the long-form genitive of `on`, `ona`, `oni` respectively as the stem
(`njegova`, `njena`, `njihova` in the genitive), and are declined like indefinite adjectives (masculine
and neuter forms in particular). The same `-ov`/`-in` derivational pattern also produces possessive
adjectives from ordinary nouns. Page 174.

### Interrogative pronouns tko / što (declension)

Full case paradigm for `tko` "who" and `što` "what." The indefinite pronouns `netko` "someone" and
`nitko` "no one" decline like `tko`; `nešto` "something" and `ništa` "nothing" decline like `što`.
**Negative compound pronoun + preposition rule:** when a negative compound pronoun (`nitko`, `ništa`,
etc.) occurs with a preposition, the preposition is inserted *between* the negative particle and the
pronoun root: `Ne viđa se ni s kim` "He doesn't see anyone" (not `*s nikim`); `Ni od koga nemamo
vijesti`; `Uzrujavaš se ni zbog čega`. Page 180.

### Relative pronoun koji, koje, koja ("who, which")

Full declension table, structurally close to that of `moj`. The source flags a genuine ambiguity: in
some dialect areas the genitive/dative of `koji` (m./nt.) coincide with those of the interrogative
`tko`, compounding the usual English "who" vs. "which" confusion. **The relative pronoun can never be
omitted** in Croatian/Serbian the way it optionally can in English ("the man I saw" → `čovjek kojeg sam
vidio`, the relative must be present). Pages 181–182.

### Relative pronoun `što`

In certain contexts `što` functions as a relative pronoun, and **must** be used after the neuter
indefinite pronouns `ovo`, `to`, `ono`, `nešto`, `ništa`, `jedino`, `prvo`, `sve`: `Svida mi se ono što
sam vidjela` "I like what I have seen"; `Nisu razumjeli ni riječ od onoga što smo im rekli.` Page 182.

### Relative pronoun čiji, čije, čija ("whose")

Declines exactly like `koji`, agreeing in full with the noun that follows it: `Čiji je taj lijepi
šešir?`; `Čijim ste autom došli?`. Page 183.

### Aspect 2 — worked aspect-pair examples

A deeper pass on the imperfective/perfective distinction already touched on in Lesson 2, illustrated
with paired sentences (e.g. `Već šest mjeseci kupuju novu kuću; sad su je konačno kupili` "They have
been buying a new house for six months; now they've finally bought one"). General rule stated: in each
pair the first (imperfective) verb describes an open-ended action, the second (perfective) one denotes
an action conceived as finite — past, present, or future. Page 185.

### Formation of the imperative

Three formation classes:

- **(a)** Verbs whose 1st person singular present ends in `-am`, `-jem`, or `-jim` take `-j` (2sg),
  `-jmo` (1pl), `-jte` (2pl): `čekam` → `čekaj!/čekajmo!/čekajte!`
- **(b)** Most other verbs (present in `-im`/`-em`) take `-i` (2sg), `-imo` (1pl), `-ite` (2pl):
  `govorim` → `govori!/govorimo!/govorite!`
- **(c)** Verbs whose infinitive ends in `-ći` form the imperative from the *infinitive* stem, not the
  present stem, and the source explicitly recommends learning these case-by-case rather than deriving
  them (e.g. `reći` ← `rek-ti`, `k+i=c`: `reci, recimo, recite`; `pomoći` ← `pomog-ti`, `g+i=z`:
  `pomozi, pomozimo, pomozite`).
- `ići` and its derivatives form the imperative from the *present* stem instead: `idem` → `idi, idimo,
  idite`; `dođem` → `dođi, dođimo, dođite`.

Pages 193–194.

### Imperative of `biti`

Irregular: `budi (2sg) / budimo (1pl) / budite (2pl)`. Example: `Budimo ljudi!` "Let's be decent human
beings!" Page 194.

### Imperatives of other persons

First person singular: `da` + present (`Da vidim!` "Let me see!"). Third person singular/plural: `neka`
+ present (`Neka dođe!` "Let him come!"; `Neka čekaju!` "Let them wait!"). Page 196.

### Negative imperative

Two distinct formation methods with different pragmatic force:

- **(a)** `ne` + imperative, which **requires the imperfective aspect** and reads as comparatively
  abrupt: `Ne brini!` "Don't worry"; `Ne dirajte!` "Don't touch!"
- **(b)** `nemoj(mo/te)` + infinitive, used for "softer" prohibitions and compatible with either aspect
  as appropriate: `Nemoj to jesti!` "Don't eat that!"; `Nemojte zakasniti!` "Don't be late!"
- **(c)** First person singular and third persons simply insert `ne` before the verb after `da`/`neka`:
  `Neka ne zaboravi!` "Let him not forget!"; `Da ne zakasnim!` "Let me not be late!" — these can also be
  rendered as a command to a second person to intervene: `Nemoj da zaboravi!` "Don't let him forget!"

Page 197.

### English adjective phrases expressed by a verb

Many English "to be + adjective" constructions correspond to a single verb in Croatian/Serbian, and the
source flags this as a pattern to actively watch for rather than translate word-for-word: `kasniti,
zakasniti` "to be late"; `ljutiti se, naljutiti se` "to be angry"; `strpjeti se` "to be patient";
`šutjeti` "to be quiet." Page 198.

### Aspect 3 — theoretical deepening

A more systematic pass on aspect, explicitly framed by the source as only a partial treatment of a
"large and complex subject":

- **Perfective** verbs express a single finite action that cannot be divided into phases — no reference
  to beginning/middle/end, only to the totality of the action (`Pojeo sam sladoled` "I ate up the
  ice-cream"; `Došli smo da te vidimo` "We have come to see you" — concern is purely with the fact of
  arrival, not the process).
- **Imperfective** verbs express a vaguer, non-total action, which may be **gradual** (`Jedem sladoled i
  gledam more`), **repeated** (`Dolazimo često na ovu plažu`), or **general/habitual** (`Vole čitati
  stripove`). The imperfective is also used for an action that was **not actually completed**:
  `Nekoliko dana se odlučivao da otputuje` "He had been deciding to leave for several days" (contrasted
  with the perfective `Već se nekoliko puta odlučio da otputuje`, where the decision was actually made).
- **Phase-verb rule:** verbs referring specifically to the phases of a process — `početi` "to begin",
  `produžiti` "to continue", `završiti` "to finish" — **must be followed by the imperfective** infinitive
  or verb, since the phase verb itself already carries the perfective/completive sense: `Počela sam
  učiti vaš jezik`; `Kad završite čitati, posudite mi tu knjigu.`

Pages 199–200.

### "It depends" — `ovisi o` and the `kako` + interrogative construction

Standard: `ovisi o` + prepositional case (`ovisi o vremenu` "it depends on the weather"). A distinct,
more colloquial construction adds the conjunction `kako` to the adverb/pronoun being qualified to mean
"it depends [on that variable]": `Kako kad` "It depends, sometimes" (in response to "Do you like cold
beer?"); `Kako tko` "It depends, some do"; `Kako gdje` "It depends, in some places." Page 201.

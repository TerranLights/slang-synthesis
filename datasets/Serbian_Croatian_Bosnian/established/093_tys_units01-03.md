## Grammar points

### The Latin and Cyrillic alphabets (Unit 1, pp.2-9)

Serbian is written in two parallel 30-letter alphabets with a one-to-one letter correspondence
(latinica and ćirilica), never mixed within the same word or text. Neither alphabet is prescribed for
a particular context — newspapers, signage, and advertising appear in either. The book deliberately
presents vocabulary in both scripts through Units 1-3, then alternates by unit from Unit 4 onward
(Cyrillic first in Unit 4, Latin first in Unit 5, etc.). The two alphabets are given letter-by-letter
correspondence tables (p.3): Latin order runs A a, B b, C c, Č č, Ć ć, D d, Dž dž, Đ đ, E e, F f, G g,
H h, I i, J j, K k, L l, Lj lj, M m, N n, Nj nj, O o, P p, R r, S s, Š š, T t, U u, V v, Z z, Ž ž — the
digraphs dž, lj, nj (and Đ/đ, sometimes written Dj/dj) are each treated as single letters for
alphabetization/dictionary purposes. The Cyrillic alphabet has its own distinct letter order (given
separately, p.20): А Б В Г Д Ђ Е Ж З И Ј К Л Љ М Н Њ О П Р С Т Ћ У Ф Х Ц Ч Џ Ш.

### Serbian spelling is fully phonetic

"Write as you speak" (Vuk Karadžić's 19th-century reform maxim, p.viii-xv introduction). Each letter
represents exactly one sound, pronounced individually; there is no letter-doubling or silent-letter
ambiguity, and even foreign personal/place names are respelled phonetically as pronounced by a
Serbian speaker rather than preserving source-language orthography (p.10-11, e.g. Njujork for New
York, Šekspir for Shakespeare).

### Pronunciation groups (Unit 1, pp.4-6)

The book organizes consonant/vowel pronunciation into three groups: Group 1 — letters pronounced
roughly as in English (b, d, f, k, l, m, n, p, s, t, v, z) plus shortened vowels a/e/i/o/u. Group 2 —
letters that look like English letters but represent a single distinct sound or a different sound
than expected: c (ts, never k/s), g (always hard, never as in "large"), h (throaty, as in Scottish
"loch"), j (=y), lj (palatalized l, like "ll" in million), nj (palatalized n, like "ni" in onion), r
(trilled; can function as a syllabic vowel between two consonants, e.g. Srbija/Србија). Group 3 —
accented/digraph letters representing paired "hard/soft" consonant contrasts: č vs ć (both like "ch"/"t"-
affricates, č further back, ć further forward against the teeth), dž vs đ (parallel "j"-affricate
pair), š and ž (sh/zh sounds, tongue raised and pulled back).

### Handwritten (cursive) forms of both alphabets (Unit 3, pp.29-30)

Handwritten Cyrillic differs substantially from printed Cyrillic for several letters (a full
capital/lowercase/example chart is given letter-by-letter, p.29, e.g. printed Б becomes a cursive
form resembling a script "b" with a loop). Handwritten Latin is close to printed Latin except that
diacritic marks (č/ć/š/ž/đ) must be placed clearly, and z/ž keep their printed shape even in cursive
(p.30).

### Nouns: three-way grammatical gender (Unit 2, p.19)

All Serbian nouns are masculine, feminine, or neuter, generally identifiable by ending: masculine
nouns typically end in a consonant (muž, gospodin, drug, dan); feminine nouns typically end in -a
(žena, gospođa); neuter nouns typically end in -o or -e (jutro, veče). The book flags that
subgroups/exceptions to this pattern exist and will be introduced later in the course.

### Adjective-noun gender agreement (Unit 2, p.19)

Adjectives change their ending to agree with the gender of the noun they modify: masculine adjectives
end in a consonant (dobar dan, moj muž), feminine adjectives end in -a (moja žena, moja drugarica),
neuter adjectives end in -o (dobro jutro) or sometimes -e (dobro veče).

### The verb "to be" (biti/бити), present tense (Units 2-3, pp.15, 18, 26)

Irregular present-tense forms introduced piecemeal across the two units: ja sam ("I am"), ti si /
vi ste ("you are," informal singular / formal-or-plural), and a distinct emphatic one-word-answer
form jesam/јесам ("[yes,] I am," used to answer a Vi ste...? / Da li ste...? question directly,
p.26) alongside its negative Nisam ("I am not," implied by pattern though not printed as a
standalone table entry in this range).

### T-V distinction: formal vs. informal "you" (Unit 2, p.15)

Explicit, extensively glossed register content: vi/ви (and vi ste/ви сте) is the formal-address
form, used with strangers, casual acquaintances, professional contexts, or any plural addressee; ti/ти
(and ti si/ти си) is the informal form, used with friends/relatives and by adults speaking to
children — but also used reciprocally among children, teenagers, and students regardless of how well
they know each other. The book explicitly warns that using the informal register is not itself an
invitation to friendship, that the social rules governing which form is "correct" are genuinely
complex, and that adults should default to vi/ви unless invited to switch. This T-V social-convention
note is one of the highest-value register annotations in this chunk.

### Regular present-tense verb conjugation pattern (Unit 3, pp.26-27)

Most Serbian verbs follow a shared present-tense ending pattern across the three persons introduced
so far: ja-ending is -m/-м, ti-ending is -š/-ш, vi-ending is -te/-те (this covers 1st singular, 2nd
singular informal, and 2nd singular formal/plural — no distinct 3rd-person or plural-beyond-vi forms
are given in this range). Because each ending already encodes the subject, personal pronouns (ja, ti,
vi) are frequently dropped in speech; they're reintroduced mainly for direct address or emphasis
(e.g. Da li vi govorite engleski? "Do you [really] speak English?"). Demonstrated across three verbs:
govoriti ("to speak": govorim/govoriš/govorite), razumeti ("to understand": razumem/razumeš/razumete),
učiti ("to learn": učim/učiš/učite). The book also notes Serbian has only one present-tense form
covering English's simple ("I speak"), continuous ("I am speaking"), and emphatic ("I do speak")
present.

### Forming yes/no questions and negation (Unit 3, p.27)

Yes/no questions are formed by placing the particle da li/да ли directly before the statement (e.g.
Razumete srpski → Da li razumete srpski? "Do you understand Serbian?"). Negation is formed by placing
ne/не directly before the verb (Ne, ne razumem — "No, I do not understand").

### No articles (a/an/the) in Serbian (Unit 3, pp.25-26)

Serbian has no equivalent of English "a," "an," or "the." A phrase like Da li si ti Engleskinja? can
mean either "Are you an Englishwoman?" or "Are you the Englishwoman?" depending purely on context —
there is no grammatical marker to disambiguate.

### Nationality nouns vs. language adjectives: capitalization rule (Unit 3, p.27)

An explicit orthographic rule: nouns naming a person's nationality (Englez, Srbin, Francuz, Nemac...)
are capitalized; the corresponding words for the associated language (engleski, srpski, francuski,
nemački...) are written lower-case, even though the two often look nearly identical or share a root.

### Formal introduction set phrases (Unit 2, pp.16-17)

The book identifies a fixed cluster of phrases used specifically in formal introductions: Da vas
upoznam... ("Let me introduce you..."), Ovo je... ("This is..."), Drago mi je. Ja sam... ("Pleased to
meet you. I am..."). It's also noted as customary to shake hands while stating one's name in this
context.

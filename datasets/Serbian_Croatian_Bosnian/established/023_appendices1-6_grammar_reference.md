## Grammar points

### Appendix 1 — Latin and Cyrillic alphabets (p. ~314–315)

A one-to-one correspondence table between the 30-letter Latin BCS alphabet and the 30-letter Cyrillic
BCS alphabet (Cyrillic is Serbian's co-official script; Croatian and Bosnian use Latin only in practice,
though Bosnian officially recognizes both). Each letter is given with an approximate English
pronunciation gloss. Three Latin letters are digraphs representing single phonemes, each with a single
Cyrillic letter counterpart: **Lj/lj = Љ/љ** (roughly "million"), **Nj/nj = Њ/њ** ("canyon"), **Dž/dž =
Џ/џ** ("junk"). The acute-accent letters **Ć/ć = Ћ/ћ** ("chick") and **Đ/đ = Ђ/ђ** ("ginger") mark a
palatal affricate pair distinct from **Č/č = Ч/ч** ("church") and its plain counterpart **C/c = Ц/ц**
("its"). **Š/š = Ш/ш** ("sugar") and **Ž/ž = Ж/ж** ("treasure") mark the sibilant pair.

### Appendix 2 — Personal names, and the book's [B]/[C]/[S] tagging convention (pp. 316–317)

Two parallel name lists (women's, men's) tag every name with which of the three standards — Bosnian,
Croatian, Serbian — attest it in that form. This is the same `[B]`/`[C]`/`[S]` bracket convention used
throughout the textbook for lexical/grammatical variants, here applied to onomastics: a name can be
pan-standard (`[B,C,S]`), shared by two of the three, or standard-specific. See the vocabulary section
above for the representative sample and the cultural/etymological pattern it shows.

### Appendix 3 — Cursive Cyrillic handwriting guide (pp. 319–321)

A letter-by-letter guide pairing each Cyrillic letter's printed block form with its cursive/handwritten
form, illustrated per letter with a proper name (mostly place names — countries, cities — e.g. **Африка**
"Africa", **Загреб** "Zagreb", **Косово** "Kosovo", **Њујорк** "New York") and two ordinary words shown
in both print and cursive (e.g. **април** "April", **добар** "good", **срспки** "Serbian" [sic —
*српски*], **књига**-type common nouns, **кућа** "house", **писмо** "letter", **речник** "dictionary").
Confirmed by direct visual inspection to be entirely printed/typeset content in a uniform cursive
teaching font — not a previous owner's handwriting — per the extraction spec's vision-reading guard.

### Appendix 4 — Noun, adjective, and pronoun declension (pp. 322–329)

BCS nouns, adjectives, and pronouns decline for **six cases** (Nominative, Accusative, Genitive,
Dative/Locative — syncretic in the singular for most classes — and Instrumental) plus a separate
**Vocative** and, for some masculine nouns, a distinct **"count form" (CF)** used after the numbers 2–4.
Case tables are organized by **declension class**:

- **Masculine animate** (e.g. *konj* "horse", *muž* "husband"): Accusative singular syncretic with
  Genitive singular (a hallmark of animacy marking); plural formed with *-i* (Npl *kònji*). Ethnonyms
  like *Srbin*/*Turčin* lose their *-in* suffix in the plural (*Srbi*, *Turci*).
- **Masculine inanimate** (e.g. *dan* "day", *grad* "city"): Accusative singular syncretic with
  Nominative instead. Several nouns show a **mobile vowel** that drops before vowel-initial endings
  (*posao* → Gsg *pòsla*; *zadatak* → Gsg *zadàtka*). An explicit `[B,C,S]`-vs-`[C]` footnote marks a
  regional Instrumental/plural split for *put* "road" (*putem/putevi* vs. *putom/putovi*).
- **Neuter** (e.g. *selo* "village", *ime* "name"): Nominative = Accusative always. Two irregular
  n-stem/t-stem nouns are given as paradigm illustrations: *ime* (Gsg *imena*) and *jaje* "egg" (Gsg
  *jàjeta*). *Gnijezdo*/*gnezdo* "nest" carries an explicit Ijekavian/Ekavian `[J]`/`[E]` tag.
- **Feminine in -a** (e.g. *ruka* "hand", *knjiga* "book"): regular a-stem paradigm; *djevojka*/*devojka*
  "girl" carries an explicit `[J]`/`[E]` tag.
- **Feminine consonant-stem** (e.g. *stvar* "thing", *ljubav* "love"): Instrumental singular has two
  competing forms (a short vowel-ending form and a longer *-ju*-suffixed form, e.g. *stvari*/*stvarju*).
  *Kći*/*kćer*/*kćerka*/*ćerka* "daughter" carries an explicit three-way `[B,C,S]`/`[B,C]`/`[S]` regional
  variant footnote and an irregular declension.

**Personal names** (pp. 324) decline like common nouns of the matching gender/class, with a distinct
Vocative (e.g. male name *Pètar* → V *Petre*), and full name+surname combinations decline both
elements in parallel (*Nìkola Ìlić* → G *Nìkole Ìlića*); an indeclinable foreign-origin name like *Ines*
stays unchanged across all cases. Formal address uses **Vocative titles**: *G.* (Gospodin, "Mr.") + male
surname Vocative, *Gđo* (Gospođo, "Mrs.") + unchanged female surname.

**Adjectives** (pp. 325–328) distinguish a **short (indefinite)** and **long (definite)** form in the
masculine Nsg/Asg (e.g. *dobar* short vs. *dobri* long, "a good" vs. "the good"), a distinction largely
neutralized outside masculine singular. **Pronominal adjectives** (*koji* "which", *neki* "some"),
**demonstratives** (*ovaj* "this", *taj* "that", *kakav* "what kind of", *sav* "all"), and
**possessives** (*moj* "my", *naš* "our", *njegov* "his", and the -in-suffixed type *mamin* "mommy's")
each follow their own (closely related) paradigm, all sharing the same case/number/gender skeleton as
descriptive adjectives.

**Pronouns** (p. 329): personal pronouns have distinct **full (stressed)** and **clitic (unstressed,
second-position)** forms per case (e.g. Asg 1st person *mene* full vs. *me* clitic) — this full/clitic
split is one of the most productive sources of BCS sentence-level word-order rules (clitics cluster in a
fixed order in "second position" of the clause). Interrogatives *ko*/*tko* "who" and *šta*/*što* "what"
and their *neko*/*nešto*/*ništa*-type derivatives show a **long vs. longer** oblique-case form
alternation (e.g. Gsg *kog* vs. *kòga*) that mirrors the short/long adjective distinction.

### Appendix 5 — Prepositions and case government (p. 330–331)

Prepositions in BCS each govern one or more specific cases, and several govern **different cases with
different meanings** depending on whether motion/direction or static location is meant — the clearest
minimal pair is **na**: +Accusative "onto" (motion) vs. +Locative "on, at" (static), and the same
directional/static split recurs with **u** ("into" vs. "in, at") and **po** ("per, each" distributive
vs. "according to/around/in" descriptive). A second table in the source cross-indexes the same
prepositions **by case governed** rather than by preposition, which is how the book expects students to
drill them (e.g. all Genitive-governing prepositions grouped together). See the Vocabulary section above
for the complete list, including the `[E]`/`[J]` Ekavian/Ijekavian tag on *posle*/*poslije* "after" and
*pre*/*prije* "before".

### Appendix 6 — Verb conjugation: the full paradigm inventory (pp. 332–339)

Every BCS verb is organized around a shared **inventory of forms** cutting across three word-classes:
**nominal** forms (the infinitive and a deverbal "verbal noun", e.g. *gledati* → *gledanje* "watching"),
**adjectival** forms (the **L-participle**, used to build the past tense and conditional, and the
**passive participle**, used for the passive voice and resultative constructions — both inflect for
gender/number like an adjective), and **adverbial** forms (present and past **verbal adverbs**, roughly
"-ing"/"having done" converbs). The finite **verbal** forms proper are present tense, the (largely
literary/narrative) **aorist** and **imperfect** past tenses, and the imperative.

The source organizes regular verbs into **16 numbered conjugation types** by present-tense stem
formation, illustrated with a head verb per type (paraphrased list, not the full paradigm tables):
Type 1 *gledati* "to watch" (regular -a- stem); Type 2 *nositi* "to carry"; Type 3 *videti/vidjeti* "to
see" (Ekavian/Ijekavian doublet, each given its own full paradigm); Type 4 *držati* "to hold"; Type 5
*pisati* "to write" (s→š stem alternation, *pišem*); Type 6 *piti* "to drink" (je-inserting present,
*pijem*); Type 7 *krenuti* "to set off" (perfective -nu- inchoative); Types 8a/8b *kupovati*/*kazivati*
(-ova-/-uje- alternating present stem); Type 9 *davati* "to give"; Type 10 *brati* "to pick" (e-inserting
present, *berem*); Type 11 *uzeti* "to take"; Type 12 *umeti/umjeti* "to know how to" (another
Ekavian/Ijekavian doublet); Type 13 *jesti* "to eat"; Type 14 *tresti* "to shake"; Types 15a/15b
*teći*/*stići* "to flow"/"to arrive"; Type 16 *doći* "to come" (prefixed irregular, *do-* + *ići*).
Several forms are bracketed in the source as attested-but-rare paradigm fillers borrowed from a
different verb of the same type when the head verb itself lacks that form (an aspect/transitivity gap
the source flags explicitly rather than papering over).

Two verbs are given full irregular/suppletive paradigms outside the 16-type system: **hteti/htjeti**
"to want" (Ekavian/Ijekavian doublet) doubles as the grammaticalized **future-tense auxiliary**
(*ću, ćeš, će...*), with a separate negated present (*neću, nećeš...*) — the negated form is not simply
*ne* + the affirmative clitic, it is a distinct fused form. **Biti** "to be" is the most irregular verb
in the language: its present tense has a **full/stressed set** (*jesam, jesi, jeste...*), a fully
suppletive **negated set** (*nisam, nisi, nije...*), and a separate **budem-stem** used only to form the
future/conditional and certain subordinate constructions — three distinct present-tense paradigms for
one lexical verb. The 3sg full form carries an explicit regional tag: **jeste** `[B,S]` vs. **jest**
`[C]`.

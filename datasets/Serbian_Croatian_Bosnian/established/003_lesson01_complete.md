# Serbian/Croatian/Bosnian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Lesson 1, Complete

**Source:** Ronelle Alexander & Ellen Elias-Bursać, *Bosnian, Croatian, Serbian, a Textbook: With
Exercises and Basic Grammar* (2nd ed., University of Wisconsin Press, 2010), Lesson One
("Prva lekcija / Lesson One"), pp. 1-17 (PDF pages 22-39).

**Coverage note:** every grammar point and every distinct vocabulary item from the full lesson —
the opening Props (`Rekviziti`) list, sections A1-A4 (vocabulary boxes, dialect-parallel dialogues,
grammar notes), the B/C exercise blocks (new vocabulary/tags only, drills skipped), the
`Geografska pitanja` / `Zemljopisna pitanja` reading, and the Cyrillic handwriting-practice pages
(pp. 14-17) — plus every explicit `[B]`/`[C]`/`[S]` (and combined `[B,C]`, `[B,S]`, `[B,C,S]`)
regional-variant annotation the source itself marks. Repeated drill exercises (the B1-B6, C1-C4
practice blocks) that recombine already-captured vocabulary in slightly different sentence frames
are skipped per `../../00_Reference_Extraction_Spec.md`; only new vocabulary/tags surfacing inside
those drills is pulled in.

**Supersedes** `001_lesson01_basics.md` (a prior manual test-run pass covering only the Lesson 1
opening, ~20 entries) — this file is the full, comprehensive re-extraction of the entire lesson.
`001_lesson01_basics.md` is left in place as a historical artifact of the pipeline's first test run
but should not be treated as authoritative going forward; this file is.

**Source has a real text layer** (confirmed via `pdftotext`) — no vision-reading was required, hence
`n/a` throughout the Vision Reading Confidence column. No handwritten marginalia was present on any
of these pages.

**A note on the Cyrillic handwriting-practice pages (pp. 14-17):** this section gives one example
word per Cyrillic letter, for penmanship drill, with **no English gloss supplied by the source in
this section** (a learner is expected to already recognize the word, or is not expected to
translate it — it's purely a letter-shape exercise). Person/city/country names used only to
illustrate a letter shape (e.g. *Grета*, *Đorđe*, *Dubrovnik*, *Njujork*, *Oregon*, *Podgorica*,
*Ćuprija*, *Uganda*, *Čikago*, *Džuli*, *Šarlota*, *London*) are treated as low-value drill filler
and excluded, along with country names already captured from the `Geografska pitanja` vocabulary
box. Genuinely new common-vocabulary words from this section (months, days of the week, and a
handful of everyday nouns/adjectives/verbs) are included below; their English glosses are supplied
by the extractor, not the source, and this is flagged per-row in the Notes column.

## Grammar points

### Two alphabets (p. 1, p. 14-17)

B, C, and S are all written using the Latin alphabet, called "Latin" in the source. S additionally
uses "Cyrillic" — S is the only one of the three standards where both alphabets are in active use.
Each letter in either alphabet corresponds to exactly one sound, making pronunciation
straightforward once the alphabet is learned. The lesson closes with a full Cyrillic
letter-by-letter handwriting-practice appendix (pp. 14-17), one example word per letter, with a
penmanship guide referenced at p. 319 of the source.

### Verb conjugation and the second-position clitic rule (p. 1)

Every verb has six present-tense forms (1/2/3, singular/plural). The reflexive particle `se` (as in
`zvati se`, "to be called") does not have to sit directly after the verb — it is required instead to
occupy the sentence's or clause's **second position**, regardless of where the verb itself falls.
This second-position clitic behavior applies to any verb used with `se`, including the impersonal
`kaže se` ("one says").

### Singular and plural *you* (p. 1-2)

`Ti` (with verb forms ending in `-š`) addresses a single person one knows well. `Vi` (with verb
forms ending in `-te`) addresses either a group of people, or a single person one is on more
distant/polite terms with — the familiar/polite T-V distinction, structurally identical to French
`tu`/`vous` or German `du`/`Sie`.

### The verb "to be" — short forms (p. 2)

The short present-tense forms of `biti` (`sam, si, je, smo, ste, su`) cannot begin a sentence or
clause on their own; they always occupy the second position, same as the `se` clitic rule above.

### Vocabulary differences (p. 2, continued p. 7)

The source explicitly frames several word-pairs as B/C/S lexical divergence, not just
pronunciation variance: `ko` (B,S) / `tko` (C) "who"; `šta` (B,S) / `što` (C) "what." Continued on
p. 7: female-human-referring suffixes vary across the three standards (e.g. `-ica` vs. `-kinja`
patterns throughout A4), and some basic-concept words differ in *frequency* and sometimes
*meaning* rather than simply having different forms — `drug` is S's more frequent word for
"friend, companion" while `prijatelj` covers this sense in B and C, though all three use
`prijatelj` uniformly for "life-long friend."

### Nouns and gender (p. 3)

BCS nouns are masculine, feminine, or neuter. Masculine nouns (e.g. `pas`) usually end in a
consonant; feminine nouns (e.g. `mačka`) usually end in `-a`; neuter nouns (e.g. `pitanje`) end in
`-e` or `-o`. Possessive pronominal adjectives (`moj`, `tvoj`, `njihov`) take endings agreeing with
the noun: `moj pas` (masc.), `moje pitanje` (neut.), `moja mačka` (fem.).

### Pronouns (p. 3)

Subject pronouns are used for emphasis or first mention; the verb form alone otherwise carries the
necessary person/number information, so pronouns are routinely dropped (a pro-drop language for
subject pronouns). In the 3rd person, pronoun choice tracks the referent's grammatical gender:
singular `on` (masc.)/`ona` (fem.)/`ono` (neut.); plural `oni` (masc.)/`one` (fem.)/`ona` (neut.).
The plural `one` and `ona` are reserved for exclusively feminine or neuter-referent groups
respectively, while masculine plural `oni` covers masculine-only groups, mixed groups, *and* the
general/unmarked idea of "they."

### Questions (p. 3)

Yes/no questions are formed two ways, both producing the identical question: Serbian's most
frequent pattern places `da li` before the verb; Bosnian and Croatian's most frequent pattern
places `li` after the verb. Other (wh-)questions begin with a question word, as in English. The
question marker — whether a question word, `da li`, or the "verb + li" sequence — always stands at
the very start of the sentence.

### Adjectives (p. 3-4, continued p. 5)

Adjectives usually precede the noun, but many adjectives — including all possessive pronominal
adjectives — can also follow a form of "to be" (`pas je moj`, `mačka je tvoja`), with agreement
holding either way. Neuter adjectives end in `-o` or `-e` depending on the preceding consonant
(`njihovo` but `naše`). Glossary convention in the source: adjectives are listed
masculine-neuter-feminine (`moj, moje, moja`); after Lesson 8 only the masculine form is given
unless there's an unpredictable change.

Continued (p. 5): masculine adjectives can end in either a consonant or `-i`; most masculine
adjectives have both forms, and the stem sometimes changes before the `-i` ending (`sladak` but
`slatki`). The demonstrative `ovaj` "this" must itself agree with its noun when functioning
adjectivally (`ovaj pas`, `ovo pitanje`, `ova mačka`). But when `ovo` or `to` are used to
*introduce/present* something or someone (rather than modify a noun), they stay invariant:
`ovo je moj pas`, `to je moja mačka`.

### Spelling of proper names (p. 5)

Proper names often take different forms/spellings across B, C, and S. Croatian spells foreign names
as in their original-language orthography (`George`, `Catherine`, `Mary`); Serbian spells them as
pronounced (`Džordž`, `Ketrin`, `Meri`); Bosnian can use either convention but leans toward the
Croatian approach.

### Nouns denoting professions and nationalities (p. 7)

Nationality nouns come in two forms: one for females alone (`Amerikanka`), one covering both males
and the general/unmarked idea (`Amerikanac`). Many profession nouns work the same way:
`studentica`/`studentkinja` mean specifically "female student," while bare `student` means either
"male student" or the unmarked "student."

### The verb "to be," continued — long forms (p. 7)

Beyond the short forms, `biti` has two additional six-form sets: a **long negation** set
(`nisam, nisi, nije, nismo, niste, nisu`) and a **long emphasis** set
(`jesam, jesi, jeste/jest, jesmo, jeste, jesu`) used in questions and for emphasis. In the emphasis
set's 3rd-singular slot, Croatian uses `jest` while Bosnian and Serbian use `jeste` — the same form
that also serves as the 2nd-plural emphatic ("you are") in B/S, so `jeste` is syncretic across two
functions there.

### Plural of masculine nouns (p. 7)

Masculine-noun plurals end in `-i`. Monosyllabic masculine nouns frequently insert an `-ov-` or
`-ev-` linking syllable before that `-i` (`drug` → `drugovi`); long vowels in the stem frequently
shorten in these plurals. Contrast the regular (non-infixed) plural `prijatelj` → `prijatelji`.

### Bošnjak/Bošnjakinja vs. Bosanac/Bosanka (p. 7, sidebar note)

A sociolinguistically significant ethnonym-vs-demonym distinction the source flags explicitly:
`Bošnjak`/`Bošnjakinja` ("Bosniak") refers specifically to members of Bosnia and Herzegovina's
Muslim community; `Bosanac`/`Bosanka` ("Bosnian") is the civic/residency term, covering any
resident of Bosnia and Herzegovina regardless of ethnicity or religion. The A4 exercises
deliberately use the latter, broader pair.

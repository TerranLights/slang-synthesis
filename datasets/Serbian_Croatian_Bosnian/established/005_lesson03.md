## Grammar points

### Nominative plural of nouns (p. 36)
The Nom.pl. ending is `-i` for most masculine nouns, `-e` for most feminine nouns, and `-a` for all
neuter nouns. Masculine monosyllabic nouns usually insert `-ov-` before the plural ending (e.g.
*grad* → *gradovi*); if the noun's final stem consonant is "soft," it inserts `-ev-` instead (e.g.
*broj* → *bròjevi*). Masculine nouns with a "fleeting -a-" in the stem (e.g. *Amerikànac*) lose that
vowel before the Nom.pl. ending (*Amerikànci*).

### Nominative plural of adjectives (p. 36)
All adjectives — descriptive (*vìsoki*), possessive pronominal (*njihov*), and demonstrative (*taj*) —
take the same Nom.pl. endings as the nouns they modify: masc. `-i`, neut. `-a`, fem. `-e`.

### The words meaning "person"/"people" (p. 37)
*čòvek* [E] / *čòvjek* [J] "person" occurs only in the singular; its plural is supplied by the
unrelated word *ljudi* "people," which itself occurs only in the plural. A separate word, *òsoba*
"person," has a normal plural (*òsobe*) and can be used where a regular singular/plural pair is
needed.

### Conjugation of poznàvati (p. 36)
Verbs whose infinitive ends in `-znàvati` replace the infinitive's `-ava-` sequence with `-aj-` in the
present tense: *pòznajem, pòznaješ, pòznaje, pòznajemo, pòznajete, pòznaju*. The same pattern applies
to *dàvati* "give" and other `-dàvati` compounds.

### Long-form pronouns used for emphasis (p. 36)
Long-form personal pronouns occur primarily as objects of prepositions, but are also used for
emphasis, typically placed at the start of a sentence.

### Case forms of personal names (p. 36)
Most men's names end in a consonant or `-o`; most women's names end in `-a`. In the accusative, first
names take ordinary masculine/feminine noun endings. Family names ending in a consonant: men's family
names take the masculine accusative ending, but women's family names are indeclinable — e.g.
*pòznajem Marka Ìlića* (his surname declines) vs. *pòznajem Anu Ìlić* (her surname stays unchanged).

### Adverbs (p. 37)
BCS adverbs are almost always identical in form to the Nom.sg. neuter form of the corresponding
adjective (*loš* "bad" → *loše* "badly"; *fin* "fine" → *fino* "fine!"). Because of this predictable
derivation, the textbook states it will not list adverbs separately in vocabulary boxes going forward.

### The word for "one" (jèdan) (p. 37)
*jèdan* is an adjective and agrees with its noun. It can mean the number "one" or function as an
indefinite article ("a/an"). In the plural, *jèdni* means "some." Unlike English "one" as a pronoun
substitute (*that long ONE is mine*), BCS uses the long-form adjective alone (*ònaj dugi je moj*).
The book cautions against mapping BCS long/short adjective distinction directly onto English "the"
vs. "a/an," noting this parallel fails in most cases; the safest working rule given is: short
adjective forms after "to be," long forms everywhere else.

### Feminine nouns ending in a consonant (p. 39)
Most feminine nouns end in `-a`, but a subset end in a consonant (e.g. *ljùbav*, *noć*, *stvar*,
*radost*, *jesen*). For these, Nom.sg. and Acc.sg. have the identical form (unlike `-a`-stem feminine
nouns, where Acc.sg. changes the ending). Adjectives modifying such nouns still keep normal feminine
agreement endings, so noun and adjective endings diverge in this noun class — the book recommends
learning each such noun paired with an adjective to internalize the mismatch.

### Accusative plural (p. 39)
Acc.pl. equals Nom.pl. for neuter nouns and both classes of feminine nouns. Masculine nouns, however,
take a distinct Acc.pl. ending, `-e` (contrasted with Nom.pl. `-i`).

### Added syllables in the plural (p. 39)
Many masculine monosyllabic nouns insert `-ov-` (or `-ev-` after a soft consonant) before all plural
case endings (e.g. *drug* → *drugovi*, *òtac* → *òčevi*); others do not (e.g. *pas* → *psi*, *dan* →
*dani*) and must be memorized individually. Most neuter nouns ending in `-me` insert `-en-` before
plural endings (*ime* → *imèna*).

### Negation, continued (p. 39)
The particle *ne* placed immediately before a verb (pronounced together with it) negates the whole
sentence. The same particle placed before a different word class carries its own stress and means
"not X, but Y" (contrastive negation).

### Long and short adjectives — book conventions (p. 39)
Most adjectives have both long and short forms. The book adopts fixed conventions: vocabulary lists
give the short form; drill replacement cues give the long form; grammar charts mix short forms in the
nominative with long forms in other cases.

### Interrogative pronominal adjectives (p. 41)
Full Nom. paradigms (masc./neut./fem., singular and plural) are given for *kòji* "which," *čìji*
"whose," *kàkav* "what kind," and *sav* "all." As adjectives, all four agree with the noun they
modify in gender, number, and case.

### Sound shifts in nominative plural (p. 41)
Masculine nouns ending in `-k`, `-g`, or `-h` replace those consonants with `-c`, `-z`, or `-s`
respectively before the Nom.pl. `-i` ending (e.g. *udžbenik* → *udžbenici*, *bùbreg* → *bùbrezi*,
*òrah* → *òrasi*).

### Prepositions with non-literal translations (p. 40, footnote)
The book flags that prepositional usage does not map literally between BCS and English: *misliti na*
"think of" and *alèrgičan na* "allergic to" both use *na* (literally "on"), but this corresponds to
different English prepositions ("of" vs. "to") in each idiom.

### Dialect note — B/C/S dialogue-title variation
Several A-section dialogue headers and vocabulary items in this lesson are given in three parallel
(or two-way) dialect versions rather than as neutral shared forms, marked explicitly with `[B]`,
`[C]`, `[S]` tags (sometimes combined as `[B,C]`, `[B,S]`, `[B,C,S]`) — e.g. *Šta više voliš?* [B,S]
vs. *Što više voliš?* [C]; *trenùtak* [B,C,S] vs. *mòmenat* [B,S]; *veče* [B,S] (neuter) vs. *večer*
[C] (feminine); *Geografska pitanja* [B,C,S] vs. *Zemljopisna pitanja* [C]; *domaća zadaća* [B,C] vs.
*domaći zadatak* [S]. The lesson also uses `[E]`/`[J]` tags for the separate Ekavian/(I)jekavian
pronunciation split (e.g. *reč* [E] / *riječ* [J]; *čòvek* [E] / *čòvjek* [J]), which cuts across the
B/C/S standard-language split rather than mapping one-to-one onto it.

## Grammar points

### Word structure: root, stem, affix, ending, morpheme (§161)

Every BCS noun, verb, and adjective divides into a **stem** (carries lexical meaning) and an
**ending** (carries the grammatical information — person/number for verbs, case/number/gender for
nouns and adjectives). Some forms have a **zero ending** (e.g. Nsg. of most masculine nouns) —
grammarians still treat this as "a stem plus an unfilled ending slot" rather than a stem alone,
which lets accentuation rules reference the (unmanifested) ending position consistently (p. 330).

The stem itself divides into a **root** (obligatory, carries core lexical meaning) plus optional
**affixes**: a **prefix** precedes the root, a **suffix** follows it. A word can carry zero, one,
or multiple prefixes/suffixes. Each of these pieces (root, prefix, suffix, ending) is a
**morpheme** — the smallest sound-meaning unit. Morpheme boundaries are where predictable
sound/spelling alternations happen (fleeting-vowel loss, consonant-cluster simplification, o→e
alternation, consonant softening — cross-referenced to [167a-j], not part of this chapter's own
content).

### Verbal derivation is prefix-heavy, suffixation is secondary (§162)

Verb derivation runs through a structure `[prefix] - stem - [suffix] - theme vowel - ending`.
The **theme vowel** is distinct from both suffix and ending: it identifies conjugation class but
sits between the (lexical) suffix and the (grammatical) ending, making its status genuinely
ambiguous — it behaves positionally like an ending (final-ish) but semantically like a
class-marking suffix. Three verb-forming suffixes carry real independent meaning beyond
class-marking (all extracted above): **-ne/-nu** (momentary/instantaneous action, type 7),
**-e/-je** (inchoative "becoming X", type 3, whose perfective partner is formed by adding a
prefix rather than changing the suffix), and **-i** (causative or general "result of an action",
type 2). **-ova** (type 8a) verbalizes non-verbal roots without adding extra shading. Verbal
**prefixation**, by contrast, is the dominant/richer derivational process for verbs — it both
marks aspect (perfective vs. imperfective) and shifts meaning, though no single prefix has one
predictable meaning; the source explicitly frames prefix-meaning generalizations as "often quite
metaphorical and subjective" (p. 334) and something the learner has to build an inductive feel
for from examples rather than a rule.

### Nominal derivation is suffix-heavy; prefixation and compounding are secondary (§163)

The reverse pattern from verbs: for nouns and adjectives, prefixation is comparatively sparse
while suffixation is "highly productive" (p. 335). **Compounding** (joining two roots, usually
with a linking vowel -o-, occasionally -i- reinterpreted from an imperative+object construction)
exists but is much less productive in BCS than in English — the source explicitly contrasts this
with English's very free compounding (p. 337).

### Suffix-ending fusion and gender-marking (§163b)

A single suffix can attach to different endings and mean something entirely different depending
on the resulting gender — e.g. `-in` + zero (masculine) ending means "one of X category," but
`-in` + `-a` (feminine) ending can denote a quality, a type of meat, or something
large/unpleasant. Because of this strong suffix-gender bond, reference grammars conventionally
fuse "suffix + ending" into a single listed unit (e.g. `-stvo` = suffix `-stv` + neuter ending
`-o`; `-ota` = suffix `-ot` + feminine ending `-a`). Most suffixes are gender-specific; a few come
in masculine/feminine pairs (e.g. `-nik`/`-nica`).

### Base vs. root, and derivational word-class labels (§163b)

Because roots rarely occur in isolation, grammarians classify derivation by the word class of the
**base** (the simplest neutral word containing the root) rather than the abstract root itself —
e.g. `mladost` "youth" is a **deadjectival noun** (derived from adjective `mlad` "young"), while
`govorljiv` "talkative" is a **deverbal adjective** (derived from verb `govoriti` "talk").

### Slang-formation-relevant patterns flagged by the source itself (§163c)

The source explicitly names two semantic categories of nominal suffix as cross-linguistically
classic slang/informal-register formation mechanisms, directly relevant to this project's later
mechanics-analysis phase:

- **Agentive suffixes** (`-ac`, `-lac`, `-ač`, `-ar`, `-aš`, `-telj`, `-nik`, `-in`/`-an-in`) —
  denote "someone who carries out an action," extending well past formal profession names to
  informal descriptors (hunters, musicians, watchdogs). Gender varies with the referent (agentive
  masculine vs. feminine forms are both attested, e.g. `Srbin`/`Srpkinja`).
- **Diminutive suffixes**, explicitly flagged `[dim]` in the source wherever they occur
  (`-ak`, `-ić`, `-čić`, `-ce`, `-če`, `-ica`/`-nica` in the feminine set) — the source states
  their scope is "much wider" than literal smallness: they also convey **affection/intimacy**,
  and "usually include the consonants c, č, or ć" as a shared phonaesthetic marker across all
  three genders' diminutive paradigms (p. 341). No augmentative or pejorative-specific suffix
  set is separately named or discussed anywhere in this chapter — the diminutive category is the
  only affect-marking derivational category the source treats explicitly. This is exactly the
  kind of productive, affect-loaded morphology (agentive nicknaming + diminutive/affectionate
  formation) that this project's later slang-synthesis phase would draw on as a plausible
  mechanism for coining new informal terms.

### Cross-reference only, not re-extracted: "grammatical" suffixation (§163b, p. 339–340)

The source lists a table of suffixes so tightly bound to a single grammatical paradigm
(verbal nouns `-nje`/`-će`, collective nouns via consonant softening, group numbers
`-orica`/`-oro`/`-ero`, fractions `-ina`, approximatives `-ak`, comparative adjectives `-ij`/`-š`,
possessive adjectives `-ov`/`-ev`/`-in`, ordinal numbers, the L-participle, the passive
participle, and the verbal adverb) that many grammarians don't even classify them as

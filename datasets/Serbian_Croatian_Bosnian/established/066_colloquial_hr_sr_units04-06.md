# Serbian/Croatian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Colloquial Serbian and Croatian, Units 4–6

**Source:** Celia Hawkesworth, *Colloquial Croatian and Serbian: The Complete Course for
Beginners* (Routledge, 1998). Units 4 ("Svatko ide na svoju stranu" / Each goes his own way), 5
("Dolazak u hotel" / Arrival at the hotel), 6 ("Razgovor o stanu" / Conversation about a flat).
Printed pages 39–73, PDF pages 47–81 (verified boundary: Unit 4 title page = PDF p.47/printed
p.39; Unit 7 "U restoranu" title page = PDF p.82/printed p.74, confirming Unit 6 ends at PDF p.81).

**Coverage note:** This PDF has no usable text layer (scanned book) — every page in this range was
vision-read directly. Every explicit vocabulary list, every grammar point, and dialogue-embedded
vocabulary was extracted; repeated drill exercises and duplicate example sentences not introducing
new vocabulary/grammar were skipped per the coverage rule. **Vision-reading guard: every page in
this range was actively checked for handwritten marginalia (pencil/pen annotations, underlines, or
filled-in exercise answers from a previous owner). None was found — all content on every page was
consistent, clean, printed typeset matching the surrounding page. No exclusions were necessary.**
This book is explicitly framed as "colloquial" and register/informality notes, idiomatic
expressions, and dialogue-embedded colloquialisms flagged by the source itself (e.g. **halo**
"used only on the telephone", **pa tako** "no reason", **Bog!** as an informal goodbye, **Ma ne!**)
were prioritized per the dispatch instruction, alongside the standard grammar-reference vocabulary.

**Morpheme breakdown.**

> **igralište** = igra-/igrati (to play) + -lište/-ište (place-forming suffix) — the -ište suffix
> derives a neuter noun denoting the place where the verb's action occurs; source's own example (p.42).

> **kupalište** = kupa-/kupati se (to bathe) + -lište/-ište (place-forming suffix) — same
> derivational pattern as igralište, "bathing place" (p.42).

## Grammar points

### The accusative case (direct object case)

Used when "what"/"whom" can be asked after the verb. Formation (p.40–43): masculine inanimate and
neuter nouns — accusative = nominative; masculine animate nouns — accusative uses the genitive
ending (introduced later in the same lesson); feminine consonant-final nouns — accusative =
nominative; feminine -a nouns — final -a becomes -u. Plural: masculine nominative plural -i becomes
accusative -e (watch for k/g+i palatalization to -ci/-zi, e.g. putnik→putnici(N)/putnike(A));
neuter plural accusative = nominative plural; feminine plural accusative = nominative plural
regardless of ending.

### Accusative after certain prepositions

Prepositions kroz "through", za "for" take the accusative; prepositions of motion (u "into", na
"to, onto") also take the accusative. Broadly, u marks a closed space and na an open space —
islands like Korčula are treated as "open" and take na (pp.43–44).

### Genitive case (possessive case)

Used without a preposition primarily to mark possession (ulica Zagreba "Zagreb's street"), and
after a large class of prepositions denoting origin, distance, or removal (iz "out of, from", blizu
"near"). Formation: masculine and neuter singular genitive ends in -a; feminine -a-nouns → -e;
feminine consonant-final nouns → -i. Plural genitive is -a for all genders except feminine
consonant-final nouns, which take -i (pp.46–47).

### Nema + genitive; Evo + genitive

**Nema** (3sg of nemati, "he/it has not"/"there is not") governs the genitive: *Nema nikoga doma*
"There is no one at home." **Evo** + genitive means "here is/are": *Evo postaje* "Here is the
stop!" (p.47).

### The dative case (indirect object case)

Marks the indirect object, roughly "to" in English, and is used without a preposition. Verbs
governing the dative beyond simple "give"/"speak to" include pomagati/pomoći "to help",
obećavati/obećati "to promise", vjerovati/povjerovati "to believe" — all conceptually "give X to Y".
Endings: masculine/neuter -u (pl. -ima); feminine -a-nouns → -i; feminine consonant-final nouns →
plural -ima (via inserted -i) (pp.54–55).

### The prepositional (locative) case

Endings identical to the dative, but always used with a preposition, most often to express static
location — contrasted directly with the accusative-after-motion-verbs rule: *Putujemo u Zagreb*
(accusative, motion) vs. *Ostajemo u Zagrebu* (prepositional, static location) (p.55). Feminine
nouns ending in -a: watch k+i→ci and g+i→zi consonant alternations (ruka→u ruci, knjiga→u knjizi).
Country/region names ending in -ska/-čka are grammatically adjectives, not nouns (Engleska
"English land"), and take prepositional singular -oj: Engleska→u Engleskoj (pp.56–57).

### The instrumental case

Expresses the means by which an action is carried out (Putujem vlakom "I travel by train") and is
used after prepositions s/sa "with". Singular ending is -om for all genders (-em after "soft"
consonants, e.g. s prijateljem); plural endings match the dative/prepositional plural (-ima
masc./neut. and fem.-consonant; -ama for fem. -a nouns) (pp.59–60).

### Adjectives 1 — masculine definite/indefinite forms

Masculine singular adjectives have a definite/indefinite distinction roughly paralleling English
articles. The indefinite form is required as the complement of biti ("to be"): *Taj putnik je
mlad* "That passenger is young." The definite form (ending in -i) is required after demonstrative/
possessive pronouns and once the subject has already been established: *Mladi putnik je umoran*
"The young passenger is tired." The two forms differ only in the nominative, genitive, and
dative/prepositional cases. Masculine plural has no definite/indefinite distinction, ending
uniformly in -i (pp.66–67).

### Mobile a

The final -o of indefinite masculine adjectives is always preceded by a vowel; if the adjective
stem's final consonant cluster has no vowel before the underlying -l (which surfaces as -o), a
"mobile a" is inserted for pronounceability (e.g. dobar for underlying "dobr" + a-insertion). The
inserted a disappears in all other genders/cases. The book flags such adjectives with an asterisk
in exercises (pp.67–68).

### Adjectives 2 — neuter and feminine nominative

Neuter nominative singular ends in -o (or -e after soft consonants, e.g. domaće); neuter nominative
plural ends in -a. A neuter definite/indefinite distinction exists but only in the genitive and
dative/prepositional cases. Feminine nominative singular ends in -a; feminine nominative plural
ends in -e (p.69).

### Adverbs from adjectives

Many adverbs are simply the neuter nominative singular form of the corresponding adjective: dobar
(adj.) → dobro (adv.), lijep → lijepo, mali → malo, lak → lako, prekrasan → prekrasno (p.71).

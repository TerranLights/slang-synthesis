## Grammar points

### §10 (overview). The four-way adverb classification

Adverbs modify a verb, adjective, or another adverb; they never decline and never mark person or
number. Hammond gives seven functional categories an adverb can express about the verb's action
(manner, time, place, quantity, frequency, cause, degree), then a separate, orthogonal
*morphological-origin* classification that this file's coverage note flags as the chapter's real
organizing contribution: (a) **substantival (nominal) adverbs**, derived from nouns; (b)
**adjectival adverbs**, derived from adjectives; (c) **pronominal adverbs**, derived from pronoun
stems; (d) **verbal adverbs**, derived from verbs (the participial forms). (p. 223–224)

### §10.2. Adjectival adverbs — two formation paths with different stress behavior

Adjectival adverbs form two ways, with a stress consequence attached to the choice: (1) from the
indefinite neuter nominative singular of an adjective (*dobro* "well" ← *dobar* "good") — in this
path, the adverb's stress can differ from the adjective's own stress, illustrated with a minimal
pair *jáko dete* "a strong child" (adjective, rising accent) vs. *jâko si ga udario* "you hit him
hard" (adverb, falling accent); or (2) from the definite masculine nominative singular of an
adjective, most commonly one ending in *-ski/-čki/-ški* (*sportski* "in a sports-like manner"),
where stress is identical between adjective and adverb — no shift. Adjectival adverbs take
comparative/superlative degree the same way adjectives do (*dobro – bolje – najbolje* "well/
better/best"). Two grammaticalized modifiers attach specifically to this adverb class: the prefix
*pre-* marks excessive degree ("too," *prerano* "too early") and the preposition *po* marks
intermittent/distributed frequency ("a little at a time," *piti po malo* "to drink a bit at a
time"). (p. 225–226)

### §10.3. Pronominal adverbs — the indefinite/negative/universal branch

Pronominal adverbs are built from pronoun stems with endings marking place, direction, time,
manner, and extent. Beyond the demonstrative/interrogative branch already captured elsewhere in
this dataset (`established/040` §125c–126: *taj/ovaj/onaj/ko* → *tu/ovde/onde/gde*, etc.), Hammond
tabulates a fuller paradigm that also crosses this same five-column grid with the indefinite
pronoun *iko* "anyone," *neko* "someone," *niko* "no-one," and *svako* "everyone" (see Vocabulary
table above for the resulting forms). The source flags the *svako*-based extent forms
(*svekoliko*) as rarely used in practice, and gives three interchangeable place-forms for "always/
everywhere" derived from *svako* (*svuda/svugde/svagde*). (p. 226–229)

### §10.4.1. The present verbal adverb — semantic range and syntax beyond formation

Formed from imperfective-verb present-tense 3rd-plural stems + *-ći* (formation itself already
reviewed elsewhere in this dataset). Beyond its core simultaneous-action reading ("whilst
X-ing"), Hammond documents three further readings the same construction can carry depending on
context: manner (*Otišli su u grad pevajući* "They went to town singing"), condition (*Radeći
neprekidno, možemo da uspemo* "[Whilst] working continuously, we can succeed"), and cause/reason
(*Plačući od žalosti, žena se srušila* "[Whilst] crying with grief, the woman collapsed"). **Subject
rule:** the understood subject of the verbal adverb must match the subject of the main verb;
Hammond notes this is sometimes violated in practice when the two verbs share a *logical* (not
grammatical) subject, e.g. *Zabolela ga je glava gledajući u ekran* "His head started hurting
whilst [he was] looking at the screen" — grammatically the head, not "he," is the subject of the
main clause. **Punctuation rule:** no comma when the verbal-adverb clause follows the main verb;
a comma is required when it precedes it. A closed set of present verbal adverbs have drifted into
ordinary adjectives (*iduci → idući* "next," *slededi → sledeći* "following," *mogudi → mogući*
"possible" — see Vocabulary table), taking on full adjectival inflection when used this way.
(p. 229–231)

### §10.4.2. The past verbal adverb — semantic range and syntax beyond formation

Formed mainly from perfective-verb infinitive stems + *-vši* (after a vowel) or *-avši* (after a
consonant), occasionally reduced to *-v*/*-av* (formation itself already reviewed elsewhere in
this dataset). Translates as "having [verb]-ed" or "after [verb]-ing," and describes an action
completed prior to the main verb's action (*Zapalivši cigaretu, stranac mi je vratio upaljač*
"Having lit the cigarette, the stranger returned the lighter to me"). When built on an
*imperfective* verb instead, the duration of the prior action must be stated explicitly (*Radivši
9 sati bez prestanka...* "Having worked for 9 hours without a break..."). This construction is
often replaceable by a finite clause with *kada* "when," *dok* "while," *pošto* "after, since," or
*čim* "as soon as" without changing the meaning. Same subject-identity and comma-placement rules
as the present verbal adverb (§10.4.1). Lexicalization into an ordinary adjective is rarer for the
past verbal adverb than the present one; the source's sole example is *bivši, bivša, bivše*
"former, past," from *biti* "to be." (p. 231–232)

### §11.1. The phonologically-conditioned final *-a* on *s(a)/k(a)/pod(a)*

Three simple prepositions — *s(a)* "with, off, from," *k(a)* "towards," *pod(a)* "under" — have an
optional final *-a* that becomes *obligatory* under specific phonological/grammatical conditions,
stated with more precision here than found elsewhere in this dataset: *sa* is required when the
following word begins with *s, š, z,* or *ž* (*Vraćamo se sa sela* "We're returning from the
village"); *ka* is required when the following word begins with *g, h,* or *k* (*Jahači su krenuli
ka konjima* "The riders set off towards the horses"); *poda* is required when the preposition is
followed by a short-form personal pronoun clitic or by *mnom* (the instrumental of "I") (*Poda
mnom nije bilo ničega* "There was nothing below me"). (p. 233–234)

### §11.2. Compound preposition formation

Compound prepositions form two ways: fusion of two simple prepositions into one word (*u* + *sred*
→ *usred* "in the middle of, amidst"), or contraction of a prepositional phrase (*u* + *prkos* →
*uprkos* "in spite of"). The source notes the combination can shift meaning somewhat from a literal
reading of the parts (*kraj* "next to, beside" → *pokraj* "alongside"). **Rule:** all compound
prepositions govern the genitive case regardless of what case their base preposition(s) took,
*except uprkos*, which may also take dative. (p. 234–235)

### §11.3. Preposition accentuation as proclitics

Prepositions are proclitics — phonologically bound to the following word — and normally carry no
stress of their own. Two exceptions are given: (1) when a preposition immediately precedes a word
whose first syllable normally has a *falling* accent, stress can shift onto the preposition itself,
landing as either a short falling or short rising accent (*sûnce* "sun" alone vs. *nà sûnce* "in
the sun"); (2) if the preposition itself is bi- or tri-syllabic, stress falls on its last syllable
for a rising accent or its first syllable for a falling accent, though the source notes both shifts
are increasingly rare in modern usage (*između nama* "between us"). (p. 235–236)

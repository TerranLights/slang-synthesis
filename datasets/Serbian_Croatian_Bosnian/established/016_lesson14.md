## Grammar points

### Infinitives in the function of imperative

The bare infinitive is often used in recipes and instruction manuals to convey a command
impersonally — more distanced than an actual imperative form. Many of these infinitives are
shortened versions of subjectless *treba* + infinitive constructions, e.g. *staviti* "one should put"
standing in for *treba staviti*, and *zaslàditi* "one needs to sweeten" for *treba zaslàditi*. (p.218)

### Active vs. passive sentences

Active sentences have a subject that performs the action and, when transitive, a direct object
naming the result — e.g. *majka sipa kàfu* "mother is pouring coffee." Passive sentences instead
foreground either the fact of the action or its result, and normally omit the actor — e.g. *kàfa se
sipa* "coffee is being poured." Comparing the two, the object of a transitive active sentence (the
coffee) becomes the subject of the corresponding passive. Intransitive active sentences have no
object, so their passive counterparts are subjectless (English typically supplies a dummy "it"
here). (p.218–219)

### Formation of passive and impersonal sentences

Two constructions express the passive: a *se*-verb, or *biti* + passive participle. The *se*-verb
type focuses on the action itself and simply agrees with its subject in number (*kàfa se sipa*
[sg.] vs. *jabuke se jedu* [pl.]). The *biti* + participle type focuses on the result, and requires
both the auxiliary and the participle to agree with the subject in gender and number (e.g. *mačka* →
*pronàđena je mačka*). Most individual verbs favor one construction or the other; very few verbs
allow both — *roditi se* "to be born" is one of the rare exceptions (*ona se rodila 1973.* and *ona
je rođena 1973.* both mean "she was born in 1973"). Impersonal sentences are structurally similar but
subjectless: the verb is always 3rd singular, and any participle is neuter singular regardless of the
notional "doer" — e.g. *nikad se nè zna* "you never know," *dogòvoreno je* "it's agreed." English
usually renders these with "it," impersonal "one/you," or a plural like "they/people." (p.219)

### Passive participles, past and present verbal adverbs (formation review)

The present verbal adverb is formed by adding *-ći* to the 3rd plural present-tense stem; the past
verbal adverb is formed by adding *-vši* to the masculine L-participle with its final *-o* dropped.
Passive participles are formed by adding one of the markers *-t*, *-n*, or *-en* to the appropriate
verb stem. (This is a cross-reference/review of grammar introduced on pp. 160, 180, and 185, restated
here as the mechanism drilled in the OLUJA/CRNA HRONIKA/MAGLA passages.) (p.221–222)

### Telling time, continued: "half past"

"Half past" the hour can be phrased as hour + 30 minutes (*jèdan i trideset* "1:30"), but the more
common construction uses the unchanging form *pola* plus the name of the *following* hour (*pola
dva* "1:30," i.e., literally "half [toward] two"). The preposition *u* precedes this expression just
as with other time phrases (*u pola tri* "at 2:30," *u tri i pètnaest* "at 3:15"). (p.221)

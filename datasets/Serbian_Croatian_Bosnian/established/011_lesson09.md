## Grammar points

### The future tense (formation)

The future is a compound tense: an auxiliary is added to the infinitive (or to the alternative
sequence *da* + present tense). The auxiliary functions like English "will" and has both full and
clitic forms (full: *hòću, hòćeš, hòće, hòćemo, hòćete, hòće*; clitic: *ću, ćeš, će, ćemo, ćete,
će*); the clitic forms are used predominantly. The full forms are identical to the conjugated forms
of *hteti* [E] / *htjeti* [J] "to want," but in the future-tense construction they carry only future
meaning, not "want." (p. 128)

### Word order in the future tense and variant spellings

As a clitic, the future auxiliary must occupy second position in the clause, though the infinitive
portion can appear anywhere in the sentence. If the infinitive opens the sentence, the auxiliary
immediately follows it, and infinitives in *-ti* are shortened to fuse with the clitic. Croatian
keeps the *-t* and writes the two forms separately (*ùčit ću*); Serbian drops the *-t* and writes
them as one word (*ùčiću*). Infinitives in *-sti* shift *-st-* to *-šć-* when fused with the clitic;
this pronunciation is universal in speech, but only Serbian spells it that way (*ješću* [S] vs.
*jest ću* [C]); Bosnian permits both spellings. Infinitives in *-ći* are always written as a
separate word (*ìći ću*). (p. 128)

### The preposition *na* in future time expressions

English "for" (duration) maps to two different BCS constructions depending on focus. When the focus
is an intended endpoint, use *na* + accusative: *ìći ću u Pàriz na godinu dàna* "I'm going to Paris
for a year." When it's simply a unit of elapsed time, use the bare accusative with no preposition:
*òna će biti godinu dàna u Parìzu* "she'll be in Paris for a year." (p. 128)

### Questions and negation in the future tense

Yes/no questions use the full auxiliary form plus the particle *li* (*hoćeš li…*), or alternatively
*da li hoćeš…*. If a question opens with an interrogative word (*kada*, *šta* [B,S] / *što* [C]),
the clitic auxiliary is used instead. The negative future uses only the full-form negative auxiliary
— there is no short/clitic negative form: *nèću* [B,S] / *ne ću* [C], *nèćeš/ne ćeš*, *neće/ne će*,
etc. These negative forms are identical in shape to the negated forms of *hteti*/*htjeti* "want,"
but here mean only negative future. (p. 130)

### Word order: *li* vs. other clitics in the future tense

The particle *li* is the only clitic that precedes the future auxiliary; every other clitic follows
it. In a question, the full auxiliary takes first position, *li* comes second, and any other clitics
follow: *Hoćeš li im se jàviti?* "Will you get in touch with them?" (p. 130)

### Double meanings of *hteti*/*htjeti*

The clitic forms of this verb carry only future-tense meaning. The full forms, however, signify
both future tense and "want" — context normally disambiguates, but *žèleti* [E] / *žèljeti* [J] can
be substituted to specify "want" unambiguously. The negative forms *nèću* [B,S] / *ne ću* [C] etc.
likewise carry both "won't" (negative future) and "don't want," again disambiguated by context.
(p. 130)

### Reflexive verbs

The particle *se* functions as a direct object when a verb denotes an action performed on one's own
person (*čèšljati se* "comb oneself," *ùmiti se* "wash one's face," *brijati se* "shave"). Only
verbs of this kind are true reflexives. Croatian additionally allows the reflexive dative particle
*si* in a "possessive" sense (*pèrem si zube* "I clean my [own] teeth"), but only where there is a
direct object to be "possessed." (p. 130)

### The particle *se* with verbs (broader functions)

Because *se* originated as the clitic form of the reflexive pronoun *sebe*, "reflexive verb" has
become the customary cover-term for any verb accompanied by *se* — but only a few (e.g. *òbući se*
"get dressed") are true reflexives. *Se* now marks several distinct functions: impersonal (*Tamo se
dobro jede* "one eats well there"), inclinational (*Nè ide mi se* "I don't feel like going"),
intransitive (*Djèca se ìgraju* "the children play"), non-causative (*Budim se rano* "I wake up
early" vs. *Majka me budi* "Mother wakes me"), passive (*Kàko se to kaže?* "how is that said?"), and
reciprocal (*Òni se vole* "they are in love"), in addition to true reflexive use. Some verbs never
occur without *se* at all: *bòjati se* "be afraid," *baviti se* "be occupied with," *dogòditi se*
"happen," *nàlaziti se* "be located," among others. (p. 130)

### Aspect, continued

Aspect choice in the future depends on whether the speaker focuses on the general fact of future
action or on the completion of a specific action. Reference to the fact of action alone uses
imperfective verbs (*šta ćeš ràditi tamo?* "what will you do there?"); focus on completion uses
perfective verbs (*kad ćeš mi se jàviti?* "when will you call me?"). Occasionally this aspectual
contrast produces distinct English verbs: imperfective *ùčiti* means "study," while its perfective
partner *naùčiti* means "learn." (p. 130–131)

### Negated prepositional phrases

The two-part negative pronouns *ništa* "nothing" and *niko* [B,S] / *nitko* [C] "no one" split when
they are the object of a preposition: the preposition is inserted between the two parts, e.g.
*ni o čèmu* "about nothing," *ni od kòga* "from no one," *ni s kim* "with no one." (p. 132)

### "Something, anything," etc. — the indefinite-pronoun series

Pronouns like *nešto* "something," *neko* [B,S] / *netko* [C] "someone," and *negde* [E] / *negdje*
[J] "somewhere" are formed by prefixing *ne-* to the interrogative pronouns *šta*/*što*, *ko*/*tko*,
and *gde*/*gdje*, paralleling the formation of the negative series *ništa*, *niko*/*nitko*,
*nigde*/*nigdje*. A parallel series adds *i-* and *sva-* instead, giving *išta* "anything at all,"
*svašta* "all sorts of things," and similarly-formed items. (p. 132)

### "What" clauses — *to što*

When English "what" or "that" introduces an entire clause, BCS frequently uses *to što*, translated
either as "what" or as "the fact that…" — e.g. *meni ne odgòvara to što radiš* "what you're doing
does not suit me"; *sàvršeno mi odgòvara to što nè moram ìći tamo* "the fact that I don't have to go
there suits me just fine." (p. 132)

### Dialect note: "Slavic" vs. "Slovenian" — an ambiguity trap

The adjective "Slavic" is *slàvenski* in Croatian and *slòvenski* in Serbian/Bosnian (also
*južnoslàvenski* [C]/*južnoslòvenski* [S] "South Slavic"). But "Slovenian" is *slòvenski* in
Croatian and *slòvenački* in Serbian — i.e., the Croatian word for "Slovenian" is identical in form
to the Serbian/Bosnian word for "Slavic." Bosnian usage favors the Croatian spelling in texts
published after 1990 and the Serbian spelling in texts published before 1990, so the intended
meaning of *slòvenski* must be checked against context whenever it's encountered. (p. 128)

### Semantic-distinction note: *iznàjmiti* vs. *unàjmiti*

In Bosnian and Serbian, *iznàjmiti* means both "to rent from" and "to rent to." In Croatian,
*unàjmiti* means specifically "to rent from," while *iznàjmiti* [C] means specifically "to rent
to" — the same verb root splits into two distinct forms with complementary meanings in Croatian
only. (p. 131)

### Regional terminology note: words for "station"

Croatian *kolodvor* "railway station" combines old words for "carriage" and "yard" (compare German
*Bahnhof*, "track" + "yard"); when bus travel became common, C added *àutobusni kolodvor* "bus
station." Bosnian and Serbian instead use the generic *stànica* "station," specified as needed:
*žèležnička* [E] / *žèljeznička* [J] (railway) or *àutobusna* [B] / *àutobuska* [B,S] (bus). In
other contexts, C uses *postaja* while B/S continue to use *stànica*: *radio stànica* [B,S] /
*radio postaja* [C] "radio station"; *polìcijska stànica* [B,S] / *polìcijska postaja* [C] "police
station." (p. 132)

### Regional/colloquial family terms

Every region has its own colloquial words for "father," and in some cases "mother." In parts of
Bosnia, children say *bàbo* for father and *nàna* for mother; in Dalmatia, *ćaća* or *papo*; around
Zagreb's Zagorje area, *japa*; in Belgrade, *ćàle* for father, with *kèva* used as **slang** for
mother. Teenagers across the whole region address their parents as *stara* ("old woman") and *stari*
("old man"), and together as *starci* ("oldsters"). (p. 133) — the highest-value dialectal/register
content in this chunk for slang-synthesis purposes: several of these are explicitly flagged by the
source as regionally-restricted colloquialisms or outright slang, not neutral standard vocabulary.

### Interjections and discourse particles

*Jao* means "ouch/oh no/alas"; *joj* is closer to "oops." *Òvaj* is a filler word used when groping
for a word or thought. Bosnian speakers use *bòna*/*bolan* (from *bolan* "painful") as a term of
address comparable to colloquial American English "man." Serbian speakers add *bre* for emphasis,
comparable to filler "you know"/"like." Some Croatian and Bosnian speakers use the interrogative tag
*je li?* or its contractions *jel'?*, *jel'da?*, *jel'te?*. (p. 133)

### Geographic distribution of Ijekavian pronunciation among Serbian speakers

Exercise C7 is presented in Serbian using Ijekavian pronunciation (written in Cyrillic), illustrating
that Ijekavian is frequently written in Cyrillic. The accompanying note states that, in both speech
and writing, Ijekavian is the norm for Serbs living in Bosnia and in southwestern Serbia, and for
all speakers living in Montenegro — i.e., Ekavian/Ijekavian distribution among Serbian speakers is
regional/sub-national, not a simple national default. (p. 139)

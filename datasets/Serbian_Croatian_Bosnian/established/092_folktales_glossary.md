## Coverage note — what this glossary actually is, and how it was handled

**Important structural finding:** unlike a typical annotated glossary, the book's own "lesser-known
words" section is a **bare word list with in-book page-locator numbers only — it contains no
definitions or explanations of any kind.** Each entry is simply `term <page number(s) where it
appears in the tales>`. This differs from what a "glossary" normally implies and from what the
dispatch briefing assumed.

Given this, **the Gloss column below is supplied from general Serbian lexical/dialectal/etymological
knowledge, not sourced from the book itself**, since the book provides no definition to extract or
paraphrase. This is flagged here once rather than in every row to avoid repeating it ~400 times;
per-row **Vision Reading Confidence** still flags specific entries where either the *printed word
itself* was hard to read (faded/ambiguous character) or the *supplied gloss* is a low-confidence
guess at an obscure dialectal/Turkism form. Treat every Gloss in this file as analyst-supplied
context for an authentically source-attested lesser-known-word list — the term list and its page
locators are the actual extracted source content; the glosses are annotation to make that list
useful.

**Marginalia check:** the four glossary pages themselves (PDF 228–231) show no handwritten marginalia
— clean typeset print throughout. A previous owner's handwritten library accession number and a
library stamp were found on an unrelated front-matter page (PDF p.6, blank verso near the title page)
during page-range verification; excluded as not source content and unrelated to the glossary itself.

**Editorial closing note (paraphrased, printed p.227, not quoted at length):** the book's own note
after the word list explains that the tales are printed as Vuk Karadžić originally published them,
linguistically unchanged, in the Ijekavian sub-dialect he spoke and wrote in himself. The editors
note that many of these words are no longer in active use, some meanings have shifted, and the list
skews toward dialectal words unfamiliar to (Belgrade/urban) city children today, plus terms for
obsolete objects/concepts — which is exactly why this project defaults every entry below to
`archaic`/`regional` register per the dispatch instruction. The editors frame this vocabulary as
worth preserving and reviving, not as a burden — a period-authentic editorial attitude worth noting
for the mechanics-analysis phase.

**Attested Era for all entries:** the book's own closing note states the vocabulary reflects "how
people spoke in Serbia more than 150 years before" the 2000 edition — i.e., Vuk Karadžić's
19th-century recordings, reprinted unchanged. Recorded per entry below as `19th c. (Vuk Karadžić
corpus, per book's own note p.227); reprint 2000`.

**Source Type:** `dictionary` (the book's own curated glossary/index of its lesser-known
vocabulary — closest fit among the taxonomy's allowed types; this is not a general-purpose
dictionary but functions as one for this word list).

**Transcription Confidence:** `n/a` for all rows (reference-book extraction, not corpus/subtitle
material).

**Usage Tier default:** `archaic` or `regional` per dispatch instruction, with two genuinely-supported
dot-notation subcategories used throughout: `archaic.turkism` (Ottoman Turkish/Persian/Arabic
administrative, culinary, and material-culture loanwords — extremely dense in this glossary, since
Vuk's 19th-century Serbia was still under heavy Ottoman-era lexical influence) and `literary.folklore`
(mythological/folkloric terms — *aždaja*, *ala*, *vila*, *zmaj*, *usud*, *utva* — that are not
obsolete so much as confined to folk-narrative register). Plain dialectal/archaic Slavic forms use
bare `archaic` or `regional`.

**Copyright discipline:** only the bare headwords and their in-book page locators are reproduced (a
word list of this kind is factual/lexical data, not creative prose); no tale text, dialogue, or
extended passages are reproduced. The one editorial paragraph on p.227 is paraphrased, not quoted at
length.

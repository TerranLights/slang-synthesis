# Serbian/Croatian/Bosnian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Šesta lekcija / Lesson Six

**Source:** Ronelle Alexander & Ellen Elias-Bursać, *Bosnian, Croatian, Serbian, a Textbook: With
Exercises and Basic Grammar* (2nd ed., 2010), Lesson Six, book pp. 79–96 (PDF pages 100–117 of
`01.Bosnian Croatian Serbian a Textbook With Exercises and Basic Grammar.pdf`).

**Coverage note:** every grammar point and every distinct vocabulary item from the four dedicated
vocabulary boxes (A1–A4), the "u-words/na-words" grammar table, the months list, the ordinal-number
paradigm, the "Geografska pitanja" vocabulary box, the C3 food-matching set, and the B7
nationality-demonym exercise (a genuine paired-vocabulary set, not a repeated drill), plus every
explicit `[B]`/`[C]`/`[S]` (and `[E]`/`[J]` ekavian/ijekavian) dialect annotation the source marks.
Repeated fill-in-the-blank drills (B1–B6, C1, C2, C4–C7) that only recombine vocabulary already
captured from this same chunk are **not** re-extracted as vocabulary, except where a drill's own
prompt text introduces a genuinely new lexical item with a recoverable gloss (from context or from
the grammar prose), which is noted per-row below. This source has a clean text layer
(`pdftotext -layout` produced fully usable text for the whole 100–117 range) — no vision-reading was
needed, so no marginalia check applies and Vision Reading Confidence is `n/a` throughout.

Dialect-tag legend as used by this source: `[B]` = Bosnian, `[C]` = Croatian, `[S]` = Serbian
standard; `[E]` = Ekavian pronunciation (mesto-type, chiefly Serbia), `[J]` = Ijekavian pronunciation
(mjesto-type, chiefly Bosnia/Croatia/Montenegro). A `[B,C,S]`-style tag marks a form shared across
all three listed standards, not a form restricted to one.

## Grammar points

### Locative case: formation

Locative singular endings are `-u` for masculine and neuter nouns and `-i` for feminine nouns.
Adjective endings are `-om` (masc./neut.) and `-oj` (fem.); `-om` becomes `-em` after a "soft"
consonant, while `-oj` never changes. The possessives `moj`, `tvoj`, `svoj` optionally drop the
syllable `-je-` in these endings — the locative singular of `moj` can be either `mom` or `mojem`.
(p. 79)

### Usage of the locative case

The locative case appears only after prepositions — always after `pri`, `o`, `po`, and others, and
also after `na`/`u` specifically when they mark *location* ("on," "in," "at") rather than motion
("onto," "into," which instead take the accusative). Individual nouns keep a fixed identity as
"u-words" or "na-words" independent of English usage; the source gives a comparative table of
common u-words vs. na-words (see vocabulary above) and notes some words shift meaning by
preposition, e.g. `u selu` "in [a particular] village" vs. `na selu` "in the countryside." (p. 80)

### Irregular plurals

`vrata` ("door") is grammatically neuter plural but used with singular meaning: `vrata su otvorena`
= "the door is open," even though subject, verb, and adjective are all grammatically plural. (p. 80)

### Past tense, introduction

The past tense is periphrastic: an auxiliary (the present tense of `biti`) plus an "L-participle,"
named for its characteristic `-l-` marker. For `-iti`/`-ati` infinitives, drop `-ti` and add
L-participle endings that pattern like the adjective `debeo/debelo/debela`. The participle agrees
with the subject in number and gender; in 1st/2nd person the ending tracks the gender of the
speaker/addressee. (p. 82)

### Verbal usage, continued

Present tense normally uses imperfective verbs, but perfective verbs can follow certain
conjunctions, with the conjunction's meaning shifting depending on the following verb's aspect —
e.g. `dok` + imperfective = "while," but `dok` + perfective = "until" (illustrated by contrasting
`mi jedemo jabuke dok ti ne jedeš ništa` "while you're not eating" vs. `studenti polažu ispite dok
ih ne polože` "until they pass them"). The clitic `se` can be added to an active verb to make it
intransitive/passive, e.g. `studenti slušaju predavanja` ("students listen to lectures," active) vs.
`predavanja se slušaju` ("lectures are listened to," passive). (p. 83)

### Forms of the locative case, continued

The locative of `tko`/`ko` is `kome`; of `što`/`šta` is `čemu`. Feminine `-a` nouns with a stem
ending in `-k`, `-g`, or `-h` usually shift that consonant to `-c`, `-z`, `-s` respectively before
the locative singular `-i` ending (e.g. `ruka → ruci`, `knjiga → knjizi`, `svrha → svrsi`), but some
nouns (e.g. `Bosanka → Bosanki`, `papiga → papigi`, `epoha → epohi`) do not shift — this must be
learned per-noun, not predicted. (p. 83)

### Time expressions

`u` + month name locates an event within that month (optionally followed by `mesec`/`mjesec`
"month"). Duration is expressed in the accusative with no preposition; if the unit is singular, a
genitive noun specifying the unit typically follows (`godinu dana` "for a year," `sat vremena` "for
an hour"). An event that began in the past but continues is expressed with present tense + `već`
("already") — BCS `žive tu već godinu dana` corresponds to English present-perfect-continuous "they
have been living here for a year." (p. 83)

### Ordinal numbers

Ordinal numbers are adjectives, mostly formed by adding long-form adjective endings directly to the
cardinal number. `prvi` "first" and `drugi` "second" are irregular (not built on `jedan`/`dva`), and
a few others have minor stem changes. See the vocabulary table above for the 1st–12th forms. (p. 83)

### Dative case

The dative singular has exactly the same forms as the locative singular, including the same `-k/-g/
-h → -c/-z/-s` stem shift. Uses include: (1) indirect object (`pišem pismo sestri` "I am writing a
letter to my sister"); (2) states/feelings, where BCS puts the experiencer in the dative and the
verb in 3rd singular with the state expressed as an adverb (`bolje mi je` "I'm better," `dosadno mu
je` "he's bored") — in the past tense the verb is always neuter (`bilo im je dosadno` "they were
bored"). (p. 85)

### Dative and locative personal pronouns

Full (long-form) pronouns share identical dative and locative forms and include a reflexive form
`sebe`/`sebi` ("oneself"). Only the dative has clitic (short-form) pronouns (`mi, ti, mu, joj, nam,
vam, im`); reflexive clitic forms are introduced later in the book. (p. 85)

### Word order (clitic ordering)

Clitics must occur in the strict order: `LI – [aux] – [dat] – [acc/gen] – SE – JE` (`[aux]` covers
all clitic forms of `biti` except `je`). Examples: `dajem mu ga` ("I give it to him," dat before
gen), `šta ti je bilo?` ("what was the matter with you?," dat before JE), `ne tiče ga se` ("it
doesn't concern him," gen before SE). (p. 85)

### The form sam, samo, sama

As a pronoun, `sam` means "by oneself, alone"; as an adjective it emphasizes identity/exactness
(`na samome danu` "on the very day"). This is distinguished from the clitic `sam` "[I] am" and the
adverb `samo` "only" by root vowel length (long in the pronoun/adjective, short in the clitic and
adverb). (p. 85)

### The words "like" and "love"

`voleti`/`voljeti` means "love," and also "love"/"like" before an infinitive for an enduring state.
"Like" in the sense of "take a liking to" instead uses `sviđati se` or `dopadati se`, whose grammar
inverts the English pattern: the liked person/thing is the grammatical subject, and the person doing
the liking is in the dative — `ona mi se sviđa` literally "she is pleasing to me" = "I like her."
(p. 87)

### Other uses of the dative

Some verbs — including `odgovarati` "answer" — take what looks like a direct object but is actually
dative-marked. Short-form dative pronouns can also mark possession informally: `brat mi` ("my
brother") is a more informal, contextually-anaphoric alternative to `moj brat`. (p. 87)

### Orthographic note: đ vs. dj

The source flags that `dj` is commonly substituted for `đ` when the letter isn't available in a
word-processing system (e.g. `Mađarska`/`Madjarska`), but treats `đ` as preferable since `đ` and
`dj` represent genuinely distinct sounds. Some words can be spelled either way, but many ijekavian
words (`djeca`, `djevojka`, `gdje`) can only be spelled with `dj` in current B/C/S practice; the
source notes the developing Montenegrin standard now spells these with `đ`. (p. 96)

### Sociolinguistic note: Montenegrin not treated as a fourth tag

The source explicitly declines to add an `[M]` (Montenegrin) tag alongside `[B]`/`[C]`/`[S]`,
explaining its reasoning at length (p. 87): Montenegro's 2006 independence and 2007 constitution
named "Montenegrin" as the official language and set up a codification council in 2008, but as of
the book's writing the codification process was too recent and unvalidated by the speech community
to treat as a fourth standard on par with B/C/S — unlike B/C/S, whose separateness the source ties
partly to distinct associated religious/cultural histories, Montenegro is described as sharing that
part of its cultural history with Serbia. This is a direct, source-internal explanation for why this
whole 28-dispatch extraction only tracks `[B]`/`[C]`/`[S]` tags and not a fourth `[M]` tag.

# Serbian — Established Vocabulary: *Serbian Folk Fairy Tales* ("Српске народне бајке"), Part 3

**Source:** *Српске народне бајке* (Serbian Folk Fairy Tales), Kreativni centar, 2000. Vision-read
printed pages 61–88 (PDF pages 65–92 of `29.Serbian Folk Tales.pdf`; offset confirmed empirically
as printed page = PDF page − 4, matching the dispatch's estimate). Book has no usable text layer
(scanned images only) and is entirely in Cyrillic — every page in range was vision-read directly.

**Nature of this source — different from every other extraction in this project.** This is pure
literary/folkloric narrative (traditional fairy tales, in the Vuk Karadžić collecting tradition),
not a pedagogical grammar/vocabulary textbook, and it carries no parallel English translation. The
book supplies its own inline "+"-marked margin glosses for obscure/archaic words throughout —
these were extracted directly and are marked `verified` in Vision Reading Confidence since they
are the book's own attested gloss, not an inferred one. Following the dispatch's explicit
instruction, this file is **vocabulary-only**: no plot/prose reproduction, one short orientation
line per tale, and only the genuinely interesting/archaic/regional/dialectal/Turkism vocabulary —
not a word-for-word transcription of every sentence.

**Tales covered in this range:**
1. The tail end (final page only) of an unidentified tale preceding "Аждаја и царев син" — its
   title page falls before printed page 61 and outside this dispatch's assigned range, so it is
   included here only because printed page 61 falls inside the assigned range, but is otherwise
   unidentified from the pages actually read.
2. **Аждаја и царев син** ("The Dragon and the Tsar's Son") — pp. 63–69 (title page p. 63).
3. **Немушти језик** ("The Tongueless Language" / the secret speech of all creatures) — pp. 71–74.
4. **У цара Тројана козје уши** ("Emperor Trojan Has Goat's Ears") — pp. 77–79.
5. **Чудотворни прстен** ("The Miraculous Ring") — pp. 81–88+ (tale continues past p. 88, the end
   of this dispatch's assigned range; its conclusion belongs to the next chunk).

**Notable dialectal finding.** "Чудотворни прстен" is written in a distinctly different register
from the other four tales: consistent **ijekavian** reflexes (`нијесу`, `једнијем`, `малијем`,
`вјера`, `сјекира`, `бјеше`-type forms) instead of the ekavian used throughout the rest of the
book, plus a dense layer of **Ottoman Turkish loanwords** (see the many `regional`-tagged entries
below: сарај, ашчибаша, харем, пилав, белај, сербез, мераја, курталисати, etc.) that are largely
absent from the other tales in this range. This is very likely a distinct source tale within Vuk
Karadžić's broader collecting corpus (probably recorded from an informant in Montenegro,
Herzegovina, or another ijekavian-Turkism zone) reprinted alongside ekavian-collected tales — a
genuine, source-internal regional/dialectal contrast, not an extraction artifact.

**Vision-reading guard.** All pages in range were checked for handwritten marginalia. **None was
found** — every annotation present (including all "+"-marked margin glosses) is consistent
typeset print matching the rest of the page, not a previous owner's handwriting. No content was
excluded on this basis.

**Coverage note on page range.** Printed pages 61–88 were vision-read in full (PDF 65–92, 28
images). Several pages in range are full-page illustrations with no printed text (PDF 66, 74, 79,
80, 84) — these were inspected and confirmed to carry no extractable text or marginalia.

## Grammar/orthographic points

This source is a folktale anthology, not a grammar reference, so it has no explicit pedagogical
grammar points — but the vision-read confirms two genuine, source-internal structural findings
worth recording for the mechanics-analysis phase:

### Ekavian/ijekavian split within a single anthology

The first four tales in this range ("Аждаја и царев син," "Немушти језик," "У цара Тројана козје
уши," and the unidentified preceding tale) are consistently **ekavian** (`репе`/`где`/`сад`-type
forms). "Чудотворни прстен" is consistently **ijekavian** (`нијесу`, `једнијем`, `вјера`,
`сјекира`), with reflexes appearing throughout its dialogue and narration, not just isolated
words. Because this is a collection of traditionally-collected oral tales (in the Vuk Karadžić
tradition) rather than a single author's prose, an anthology mixing dialect zones tale-by-tale is
expected and is itself a useful, source-attested regional-variation data point — not an
inconsistency to normalize away.

### Ottoman Turkish loanword layer concentrated by tale, not evenly distributed

Turkish/Arabic-origin loanwords (`сарај`, `ашчибаша`, `харем`, `пилав`, `белај`, `сербез`,
`мераја`, `курталисати`/`кутарисати`, `акшам`, `бакшиш`, `одаја`, `севап`, `адиђар`, `захира`,
`укабулити`, `претеслимити`) cluster almost entirely in "Чудотворни прстен," which also has an
urban Ottoman-Istanbul setting (Стамбол, сарај, харем) for its second half. The ekavian tales in
this same range use very few Turkisms by comparison (мегдan and берберин being the notable
exceptions). This co-occurrence of dialect zone + loanword density + narrative setting is a
genuine finding worth keeping in mind for later mechanics analysis of how register/regionality and
loanword layers correlate in this language's traditional-narrative register.

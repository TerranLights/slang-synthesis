# Serbian/Croatian/Bosnian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Lesson 4 (Četvrta lekcija / Lesson Four)

**Source:** Ronelle Alexander & Ellen Elias-Bursać, *Bosnian, Croatian, Serbian, a Textbook: With
Exercises and Basic Grammar* (2nd ed., 2010, University of Wisconsin Press), "Četvrta lekcija / Lesson
Four," printed pp. 47–62 (PDF pages 68–83 of `01.Bosnian Croatian Serbian a Textbook With Exercises
and Basic Grammar.pdf`).

**Coverage note:** every grammar point and every distinct vocabulary item from this lesson's four
"Novi rekvizìti" (new-vocabulary) boxes (A1–A4), the "Geografska pitanja" reading's own vocabulary box,
and every genuinely new lexical item introduced in the lesson's B (vježbe/vežbe) and C (domaća
zadaća/domaći zadatak) exercises — including replacement-word lists and fill-in-the-blank drill
vocabulary, since these introduce distinct new words even where the sentence *frame* repeats. **Not
extracted as separate vocabulary rows:** the many individual personal names (Zlatan, Hasan, Ante,
Branko, Mirjana, Dijana, Grozdana, Azra, Zlata, Ankica, Dubravka, Svetlana, Nikola, Ivo, etc.) used
purely as slot-fillers to drill the possessive-pronominal-adjective and vocative patterns across dozens
of repeated dialogue templates — these are grammar-drill filler, not lexical content, per the coverage
rule's "repeated drill exercises... that don't introduce new vocabulary or grammar" exclusion. Every
explicit `[B]`/`[C]`/`[S]` and `[E]`(Ekavian)/`[J]`(Ijekavian) tag the source itself marks is preserved.
This PDF has a genuine text layer (`pdftotext` produced clean, complete text for the full page range);
no vision-reading was required and no marginalia was encountered.

## Grammar points

### 1. Genitive case — basic endings (p.47)
The genitive singular (Gsg) is one of the most important BCS cases. Masculine and neuter Gsg endings
are identical to the masculine animate accusative. Feminine nouns take **-e** (the large `-a`-stem class)
or **-i** (the smaller consonant-stem class, e.g. `ljùbav` "love"); all feminine adjectives take **-e**.
Example paradigm: `nov grad` → `novog grada`; `novo sèlo` → `novog sèla`; `nova knjiga` → `nove knjige`;
`nova ljùbav` → `nove ljùbavi`.

### 2. Fleeting vowels and consonant softening (p.48)
Nouns with a fleeting **-a-** in Nsg lose that vowel in Gsg (parallel to the animate accusative); the
resulting Gsg stem is the base for every other case ending, and is listed separately in vocabulary boxes
when it differs from the Nsg. The masculine-neuter adjective genitive ending **-og** becomes **-eg**
after "soft" consonants: š, ž, dž, č, ć, đ, nj, lj, j, c.

### 3. Possible accent shifts (p.48)
Some masculine nouns with a long final Nsg syllable and a short rising prefinal accent shift that accent
to long-rising on the (now) prefinal syllable once a case ending is added: `Lòndon` → `Londòna`;
`fakùltet` → `fakultèta`. Some verbs likewise shift accent between infinitive and present tense (falling
→ rising, or rising one syllable leftward): `kàzati` → `kažem`; `govòriti` → `gòvorim`. In a-conjugation
verbs the shift occurs in every present-tense form except 3rd plural (`nèmati`, `nemam`, but `nèmaju`).

### 4. Question word òdakle and the conjunction kao/kao što (p.48)
Where English needs the phrase "where...from," BCS uses a single fronted question word, `òdakle`. The
conjunction `kao` ("like, as") is followed directly by a noun/pronoun, but takes the expanded form
`kao što` when followed by a clause containing a verb.

### 5. Alphabet pronunciation and foreign-name spelling (pp.47–48)
The lesson gives the spoken names of the Latin letters and notes Cyrillic uses a different letter order;
`SAD` is pronounced "ess-ah-deh," and the foreign letters q/w/x/y are pronounced `koo` [S] / `kveh`
[B,C], `duplo-veh`, `eeks`, `eepsalon`. A footnoted rule (p.47) states: B and C often (not always) spell
foreign place names as in the original language (`Chicago`, `Washington`, `New York`), while S spells
them phonetically (`Čikago`, `Vašington`, `Njujork`) — a systematic B/C-vs-S orthographic distinction,
not just individual lexical variants.

### 6. Use of Cyrillic (p.47)
From this lesson forward, all Serbian-language material is given alternately in Serbian Latin and
Serbian Cyrillic script; students focusing on Serbian are instructed to write homework only in Cyrillic.

### 7. Possessive pronominal adjectives (p.50)
An adjective built from a personal name marks that person as possessor. Formation: take the genitive
form of the name, drop its ending, add the possessive suffix **-in-** (feminine possessor) or
**-ov-/-ev-** (masculine possessor), then regular adjective endings. These adjectives exist only in short
form, and only a single word can express possession this way. Example: `Mìrjana` → Gsg `Mìrjane` →
`Mìrjanin`; `Pètar` → Gsg `Pètra` → `Pètrov`.

### 8. Short-form adjectives in the genitive case (p.50)
Most adjectives with both short and long forms use short forms only in the nominative, long forms
elsewhere — but possessive pronominal adjectives (`njègov` "his," `njihov` "theirs," `njèzin` [C] /
`njen` [B,C,S] "her") keep short-form endings in masculine Gsg (`od njihova grada`, `brat njèzina
ùčitelja`), though the long ending is also possible (`do njègovog àuta`). The short ending is officially
required in C; the long ending is much more frequent in B and S — an explicit B/C/S register split.

### 9. Uses of the genitive case (p.50)
The genitive follows several prepositions, including `iz` "out of, from" and `kod` "by, with, at" (with a
person's name, `kod` ranges in meaning from temporary possession to physical location, usually — but
not always — that person's own home). Genitive is also required after the invariant word `nema`
("there is no..."). When two nouns stand adjacent and the second is genitive, the sense parallels
English "of": `čaša hladne vòde` = "a glass **of** cold water."

### 10. Pronouns in the genitive case (p.50)
The genitive forms of `ko` [B,S] / `tko` [C] "who" and of all personal pronouns except the full-form
feminine singular equal their accusative forms. The full-form feminine 3sg pronoun is the exception:
Nom `òna`, Acc `nju`, Gen `nje`. The genitive of `šta` [B,S] / `što` [C] "what" is `čèga`.

### 11. Turning questions into statements (p.50)
A question word can become a subordinating conjunction with no change in word order, inserted right
after the main verb of the new clause: `gde` [E] / `gdje` [J] `je mòja knjiga?` "where is my book?" →
`nè znam gde/gdje je mòja knjiga` "I don't know where my book is."

### 12. Numbers (p.52)
"1" is a true adjective, agreeing in gender with its noun. "2" and "both" have distinct masculine (`dva`,
`oba`) and feminine (`dve` [E] / `dvije` [J], `obe` [E] / `obje` [J]) forms; "3" and "4" (`tri`, `čètiri`)
do not change for gender. After 2, 3, or 4: masculine and neuter nouns/modifiers all take the ending
**-a**; feminine nouns/modifiers take endings that resemble the regular Gsg endings but are shorter
(not long-form).

### 13. Partitive genitive (p.52)
A genitive direct object conveys "some [of]" or "part of": `žèliš li sòka?` "would you like some juice?"
By contrast an accusative direct object denotes the whole/general idea: `volim sok` "I like juice"
(general), or `žèlim jèdan sok` "I want [a glass/can/bottle] of juice" (one entire unit).

### 14. Names, continued (p.52)
Masculine names ending in `-a` (and certain names ending in `-o`, most commonly `Ìvo`) take the case
endings of feminine `-a`-nouns while remaining grammatically masculine (`pòznajem samo jèdnog
Nìkolu, i jèdnog Ìvu` "I know only one Nikola, and one Ivo"). Names ending in `-ski`/`-ska` decline as
adjectives. Feminine surnames take case endings only if they end in `-a`.

### 15. Prepositions with the genitive, continued (p.54)
`od` "from" can also express possession, and pairs with `do` "to/up to/next to" to bound a range ("from
X to Y"). `s` "from, down from" takes the form `sa` before a similarly-sounding initial consonant of the
next word, though in S it is often used generally in other positions too — another explicit S-specific
usage note.

### 16. "Pointer" words evo and eno (p.54)
`evo` and `eno` point something out; the identified referent is normally marked with genitive case
endings. `evo` is used for something close by, `eno` for something farther away.

### 17. English "It is..." sentences (p.54)
Many English "It is..." sentences have no real subject — "it" is just a placeholder. Corresponding BCS
sentences have **no subject at all**, and the verb stays 3rd singular: `kolìko je od X do Y?` "how far is
it from X to Y?"; `nìje dalèko` "it isn't far."

### 18. Irregular present-tense verbs: jesti, piti, ùzeti (p.54)
These three verbs' present-tense stems are not predictable from the infinitive:
`jesti` "eat" → jedem, jedeš, jede, jedemo, jedete, jedu;
`piti` "drink" → pijem, piješ, pije, pijemo, pijete, piju;
`ùzeti` "take" → uzmem, uzmeš, uzme, uzmemo, uzmete, uzmu.

### 19. Long and "longer" adjective forms (p.54)
Masculine-neuter long-form adjectives take Gsg **-og**, but a "longer" variant **-oga** also exists with
no difference in meaning (`velikog` = `velikoga`). Longer forms are more frequent in high style and more
frequent in C than in B or S. The longer form is required when an adjective stands alone in place of a
noun (`žèlim crvenoga` "I want the red one"), but the short `-og` form suffices when another modifier is
present (`žèlim onog cr̀venog` "I want that red one"). When two long-form adjectives occur in sequence,
the first is often the longer variant (`jèdnoga finog dana` "one fine day").

### 20. The "Geografska pitanja" reading as a live B/C/S comparison (p.61)
The reading's own title varies by dialect (`Geografska pitanja` [B,C,S] vs. `Zemljopisna pitanja` [C]),
and its three parallel B/C/S paragraphs on Bosnia and Herzegovina's internal structure are otherwise
near-identical, differing mainly in the expected ijekavian/ekavian alternation (`dio`/`deo`) and in the
B-specific word `pasoš` vs. the C-specific `putòvnica` for "passport" (S also uses `pasoš`). The source
separately flags that the city name `Banja Lùka` is also spelled as one word, `Banjalùka`, and that its
accusative form varies (`Banja Lùku`, `Banjalùku`, or `Banju Lùku`) — a real orthographic-variation note
worth preserving distinct from the [B]/[C]/[S] register tags proper.

## Grammar points

*(This chapter is entirely sociolinguistic/historical prose — it contains no morphological or
syntactic grammar rules of the kind found in the paired textbook's lessons. In their place, this
extraction provides a "Sociolinguistic points" section, paraphrasing the chapter's argumentative
structure and flagging where the source states its own editorial position.)*

### Sociolinguistic points

**1. Why "Bosnian" is the hardest of the three BCS identities to define (§174, pp.403–405).**
The source frames Bosnian identity as defined relationally against Serbian and Croatian identity
rather than independently. It traces a medieval three-way religious split (Croatia Catholic, Serbia
Orthodox, Bosnia developing its own loosely-defined Christian church, possibly linked to
Bogomilism) that made large-scale conversion to Islam easier once Ottoman rule arrived. The source
stresses that converts kept their native Slavic language and most non-religious customs, yet
Christian neighbors still applied the epithet *Turk* to them purely on religious grounds — an early
instance, per the source, of language/ethnicity and religion being pulled apart in local perception.
The source also argues Bosnia has an unusually continuous 700+-year territorial/name history (c.
1190–present, with 1929–1945 as the sole gap), which it treats as a distinguishing feature versus the
more border-shifting histories of Serbia and Croatia. It closes §174 by describing both 19th-century
Serbian and Croatian national-revival movements as each having claimed Bosnians as "really" their
own people despite religious differences — and states plainly that both sides' resulting
disillusionment when Bosnians retained a separate identity "explains in part why the Bosnian war of
the 1990s was so brutal." This causal claim is the source's own historical interpretation, offered
without extended argument, not a fact independently established here.

**2. Austro-Hungarian language policy in the "Protectorate," 1882–1907 (§175, pp.405–406).**
The chapter narrates in detail the Austro-Hungarian administrator Benjámin Kalláy's attempt (1882–
1903) to engineer a unifying "Bosnian" identity (*bošnjaštvo*) as a "divide and rule" counterweight
to pan-South-Slavic sentiment, and the resulting fight over the official name of the school language.
It recounts the specific episode of Professor Franc Vuletić's 1890 grammar commission, in which
Vuletić repeatedly petitioned to have "Serbian" and/or "Croatian" included in the title (in any of the
combinations *srpsko-hrvatski*, *hrvatsko-srpski*, *srpski ili hrvatski*, *hrvatski ili srpski*), was
refused each time, and ultimately withdrew his name from the published grammar rather than have it
appear under the government-mandated title alone. It also recounts the 1896 Jagić incident, in which
the Vienna-based scholar Vatroslav Jagić's remarks affirming a "Bosnian" linguistic legitimacy
triggered street protests, and Jagić later (per his own memoirs, as reported by the source) claimed
he had actually stressed the underlying unity of the language across Belgrade, Sarajevo, and Zagreb
and that his words were taken out of context. The episode ends with Parliament renaming the official
language from *bosanski* to *srpskohrvatski* in 1907, while still permitting Muslim institutions to
use *bosanski* internally.

**3. The "Bosnian idiom" as a Serbian-Croatian blend with a Turkish overlay (§176a, p.406).**
The source characterizes the everyday spoken language of Bosnia as a fluid mix of Serbian and
Croatian features overlaid with a higher density (not a categorically different set) of Turkish-
derived words (*turcizmi*) than found elsewhere in BCS. It emphasizes that inclusion of Turkisms was
never governed by any fixed rule but by context, topic (religious topics draw more Turkisms),
situation, and personal stylistic preference — with the same concept sometimes having both a neutral
Slavic term and a more "locally colored" Turkish-derived synonym (illustrated with the *ćuprija*/
*most* "bridge" pair from Ivo Andrić's novel title, and the *ćef* example of a Turkism that survived
into general non-Bosnian usage). The source notes Turkisms carried divergent connotations depending
on register — valued for local color in poetry/literature/song, but sometimes dismissed elsewhere as
archaic and provincial.

**4. From religious to national category: "Muslim" as an official ethnic label (§176b, pp.406–408).**
The chapter traces the gradual shift of "Muslim" identity in socialist Yugoslavia from a religious
self-understanding toward a nationality-based one under Tito's Stalinist-modeled nationalities
policy, in which Bosnia-Herzegovina was structurally unusual as the one republic with no titular
majority nationality. It reports that census-takers gave Bosnian citizens choices like "Serb,"
"Croat," "Muslim Serb," "Muslim Croat," or residual "undetermined" categories (undetermined Muslim
in 1948, undetermined Yugoslav in 1953), and that over 90% of Bosnian Muslims chose an
"undetermined" option — the source's own reading of this pattern as reflecting resistance to being
absorbed into either the Serb or Croat national category. It explains the technical
capitalized-*Musliman* (national-ethnic label) vs. lowercase-*musliman* (religious adherent)
distinction as understood, if not fully accepted, within former Yugoslavia, and states — as its own
interpretive claim — that Western unfamiliarity with this distinction contributed to a mistaken
perception of the Yugoslav wars as purely religious conflict. The chapter dates the elevation of
"Muslim" to full *narod* (constituent-nation) status to the 1974 constitution, links this to Alija
Izetbegović's 1970 Islamic revival treatise, and — as an explicitly stated editorial judgment,
introduced with "it is clear that" — argues that the Bosnian Muslim national-separatist movement was
motivated by self-defense against being permanently claimed by a Serb-Croat coalition rather than by
inherent religious fundamentalism, adding that "had Milošević's Serbs and Tudjman's Croats not been
so stridently nationalistic, Izetbegović's Muslims would not have needed to become so either." This
last claim is presented here strictly as the source's own stated position, not as adjudicated fact.

**5. The unresolved "Bosnian vs. Bosniak" language-naming dispute (§177, pp.408–409).**
The source frames post-Yugoslav "Bosnian" as carrying two coexisting senses: (a) the general
communicative code of everyone living in Bosnia (Serbian-Croatian blend, no particular ethnic
marking), and (b) a specifically Bosnian-Muslim standard consciously built to carry Islamic cultural
identity, parallel to how the source frames Serbian as carrying Orthodox and Croatian as carrying
Catholic cultural history. It reports the proposal — attributed to outside observers, not the
language's own codifiers — that these two senses should carry different names (*bosanski jezik* for
the broad sense, *bošnjački jezik* for the Muslim-identity-marked standard), and that Bosnia's own
codifiers reject this and insist on the single name *bosanski* for their standard regardless. English
"Bosnian" (for *Bosanac*) vs. "Bosniak" (for *Bošnjak*) is presented as an uncontroversial ethnic-term
distinction in English even though the underlying language-naming dispute in BCS itself remains
unresolved "as of the current writing" (i.e., as of the source's 2006 publication) — the source
explicitly notes the 1995 Dayton accords' use of "Bosnian" is often cited in favor of that name. The
section closes by naming the three specific proposed changes to the emerging standard — (i)
h-restoration in words like *kahva/sahat/lahak/mehak* (vs. common *kafa/sat/lak/mek*), some of which
the source notes survive in local dialects independent of the proposal; (ii) expansion of the
Turkish-derived/Islamic-heritage vocabulary, which the source states has not shown a real rise in
general usage outside periodicals with a strong national-religious orientation; and (iii) a general
reorientation from Serbian-marked toward Croatian-marked forms unrelated to Islamic identity as such
(*općina* for *opština*, occasional *ne će* for *neće*) — and states its own summary judgment that,
taken together, "the Bosnian language of today does not differ radically from the
'Bosno-Herzegovinian standard linguistic expression' of 1974."

**Source:** Thomas F. Magner, *Introduction to the Croatian and Serbian Language* (revised ed.,
1991, rev. 1998), Lessons 17–21, book pp. 96–124 (PDF pages 55–69 of
`source_reference/languages/Serbian, Croatian, Bosnian/12.Introduction to the Croatian and Serbian language.pdf`).

**PDF page range found:** Lesson 17 begins at PDF p. 55 (book p. 96) and Lesson 21 ends at PDF p. 69
left side (book p. 124); Lesson 22 begins on the right side of PDF p. 69 (book p. 125) and is
explicitly excluded from this chunk. This is well short of the dispatch's rough estimate
(PDF pp. 88–114) — the book's actual page-per-lesson density is much higher than estimated, so
downstream chunks assuming that estimate should re-verify their own boundaries rather than trust it.

**Vision-reading confirmation:** this PDF has no usable text layer for these pages (confirmed scan);
every page in the range was vision-read from `pdftoppm -png -r 150` renders. **No handwritten
marginalia was found anywhere in this page range** — every page showed only clean typeset print, book
running-header page numbers, and the ordinary scan-binding gutter shadow (not a mark to exclude). No
exclusion was necessary.

**Table-construction note:** this book marks the systematic Ekavian(Serbian)/Ijekavian(Croatian) jat
reflex, and true Cr./S. lexical doublets, either with explicit `(Cr.)`/`(S.)` tags or by giving both
forms side by side (e.g. `kruh/hleb`, `htjeti/hteti`). Both forms are combined into a single table row
per the established/002 and established/019 convention, with the split recorded in **Attested
Region**, rather than duplicated into two rows with identical metadata. This book also runs its own
lesson narrative twice per page — once in Latin script, once in Cyrillic script — as a transliteration
exercise; this is a script variant, not a dialect/content variant, so it is not treated as a second
Attested Region entry.

**Consonant-cluster list caveat (p. 100–101, "Pronunciation: Consonant Clusters"):** this phonology
illustration lists ~40 obscure/dialectal words in a four-sub-column layout. One sub-list (the column
running `mlad…šmrk` opposite `young…hose`) showed an apparent one-row vertical offset between the word
column and the gloss column during vision-reading — a small number of pairings (`pšeto`, the word
before/after `pšenica`, `shlapljen`, `shvaćen`, `skvačen`, `škljoca`, `škrabica`, `škvorac`, `šmrk`)
could not be confidently resolved to a specific gloss and are **omitted** rather than risk asserting a
wrong pairing (per the extraction spec's "default to excluding when uncertain" principle). Entries
below from this list are included only where the word's meaning could be independently
cross-checked against known BCS vocabulary, which is why this specific sub-list is flagged
`low_confidence` even where the individual word is common — see Notes on those rows.

**Non-redundant scope note:** this dataset directory already has files `019_lesson17.md` through
`022_lesson20.md`, but those are from a *different* textbook (Alexander & Elias-Bursać, *Bosnian,
Croatian, Serbian, a Textbook*) whose lesson numbers coincide by coincidence, not shared content — the
two books' Lesson 17–20 dialogues, vocabulary, and grammar sequencing are entirely independent. No
redundancy check against those files was needed; this chunk is a fully independent extraction.

## Grammar points

### Verb pairs for "to put on" / "to take off" clothing (p. 102–103)

Two distinct pairs cover dressing/undressing, with different scope. `Obláčiti` (I) / `obúći` (P)
and `svláčiti` (I) / `svúći` (P) apply specifically to garments worn on the body ("Oblačim drugo
odijelo" — I'm putting on another suit). A broader pair, `stàvljati` (I) / `stàviti` (P) and
`skìdati` (I) / `skìnuti` (P), covers a wider range including accessories and cosmetics that the
narrower pair doesn't extend to — the book gives `Stavljam puder na lice` (powdering one's face) and
`Mama skida šal` (Mom is taking off her scarf) explicitly as cases where only the broader pair is
usable ("[but only]" marked in the source, p. 103).

### Masculine-to-feminine noun gender pairing (p. 107)

A drill exercise ("Stavite u ženski rod imenice u muškom rodu") has students convert masculine
nouns for people to their feminine counterparts, exemplified with `prijatelj` → `prijateljica`
("friend," m./f.). This illustrates the productive `-ica` suffixation pattern for forming feminine
agent/relation nouns from a masculine base, used throughout the following drill sentences (e.g.
`učenik`/implied `učenica`, `glumac` "actor" implying `glumica` "actress").

### Relative/interrogative pronoun forms: koji, čiji, kakav, tko/ko (p. 111)

A fill-in exercise drills the choice among four question/relative-pronoun stems: `koji` (which),
`čiji` (whose), `kakav` (what kind of), and `tko` (Cr.) / `ko` (S.) (who) — reinforcing the
Croatian/Serbian `tko`/`ko` doublet already established in Lesson 1 material (see established/002).
The drill requires selecting the correct stem and case-inflecting it to fit the blank.

### Foreign place-name transliteration convention (p. 118–119, footnote)

The book's own footnote states foreign names can be rendered into Cr&S in one of two ways: (1)
phonetically respelled with Cr&S letters to approximate pronunciation (e.g. `Njujork` for "New
York"), or (2) kept in the source language's original spelling (e.g. `New York`). The phonetic
method is favored by Cyrillic users and by Serbs writing in Latin script; the original-spelling
method is favored by Croats. Some place names have a fixed traditional form regardless (e.g. `Beč`
for "Vienna"). This is a genuine explicit Cr./S. orthographic-practice split, not just a lexical
doublet.

### Tense-conversion drill: present / past / future (p. 115)

A grammar drill has students convert a sentence through all three simple tenses, illustrated with
`Marko ide u školu` (present) → `Marko je išao u školu` (past, perfect-tense construction with
auxiliary `je` + l-participle) → `Marko će ići u školu` (future, with the clitic auxiliary `će` +
infinitive/`da`-construction). Useful as a compact three-tense paradigm example beyond the
individual tense explanations given in earlier lessons.

### Clitic (unstressed) pronoun substitution (p. 122)

A drill ("Zamjenite/Zamenite podvučene imenice nenaglašenim oblikom zamjenice/zamenice") has
students replace an underlined full noun object with its unstressed (clitic) pronoun form, e.g.
`Telefonirao sam Petru` → `Telefonirao sam mu` ("I called Petru" → "I called him"). This reinforces
the placement and dative-clitic forms (`mu`, `joj`, etc.) used throughout the book, drilled here
specifically against a range of noun cases (dative, accusative) rather than just one.

### Balkan "correction factor" for appointment punctuality (p. 123, cultural note)

Not a grammatical point but a sociolinguistically-flagged cultural norm worth preserving alongside
the grammar: the book explicitly describes Yugoslavs as tolerating roughly a twenty-minute lateness
window for appointments as socially unremarkable ("a Balkan correction factor that permits a person
to show up twenty or so minutes after an appointment"), contrasted with stricter American
punctuality norms. Relevant register/pragmatics context rather than lexical content.

# Serbian/Croatian/Bosnian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Magner, Lessons 7–11

**Source:** Thomas F. Magner, *Introduction to the Croatian and Serbian Language* (Revised Edition,
Penn State University Press, 1991; 1998 update), "Sédma lekcija" (Lesson 7) through "Jedánaesta
lekcija" (Lesson 11), printed pp. 36–66. PDF pages 25–40 of
`source_reference/languages/Serbian, Croatian, Bosnian/12.Introduction to the Croatian and Serbian language.pdf`.

**Vision-reading source.** This PDF is a scanned book with no usable text layer — every page in this
range was read visually via the Read tool (not `pdftotext`). Page images were rendered at 150 DPI to
a scratchpad temp directory (deleted after extraction) and read in order from the actual "Sédma
lekcija" title page (book p. 36) through the point where "Dvánaesta lekcija" (Lesson 12) begins (book
p. 67, right column of the same scanned spread as the tail of Lesson 11 on p. 66) — Lesson 12's own
content was not extracted here.

**Mandatory vision-reading guard applied.** Every page in this range was actively checked for
handwritten marginalia (a previous owner's pencil notes were confirmed present elsewhere in this same
scanned copy, in Lesson 1). **No handwritten marks, underlines, or pencil-filled answers were found
anywhere in pp. 36–66** — all content on these pages is consistently typeset/printed. Nothing was
excluded on marginalia grounds.

**Non-redundant-supplement note:** this language folder already has Lessons 7–12 extracted from a
*different* source book — Alexander & Elias-Bursać's *Bosnian, Croatian, Serbian, a Textbook*
(`established/009_lesson07.md` through `014_lesson12.md`). That is an independently authored,
differently structured source (dialect tags `[B]`/`[C]`/`[S]` and `[E]`/`[J]` pronunciation variants,
not Magner's `(Cr.)`/`(S.)` two-column format), so this file does not skip Magner's Lessons 7–11 as
redundant — per the extraction spec, a same-topic chapter from a third/independent source is worth a
real look every time, and in practice Magner's lessons here cover substantially different territory
(telling time down to the minute, an extensive occupations glossary, a full weeks/months/years
paradigm, place-name Cyrillic transliteration, and colloquial "protective expressions"/terms of
endearment not present in the Alexander/Elias-Bursać shards).

**Coverage note.** Every grammar point named in each lesson's own "Grammar for Lesson N" callout is
captured (paraphrased, not the drill exercises themselves). Every distinct vocabulary item from each
lesson's vocabulary boxes (Divisions of the Day; Days of the Week paradigm; Expressions of Endearment;
Protective Expressions; the Telling Time / Hour-and-Minute tables; the Occupations and Professions
glossary; the Weeks/Months/Years tables; the Seasons vocabulary; and the Cyrillic place-name drill) is
captured, along with every explicit `(Cr.)`/`(S.)` regional-variant tag the source marks — this is
Magner's own convention for comparative Croatian/Serbian dialect data, treated the same way as
`established/002`. Repeated `vježba/vežba` drill sentences (fill-in-the-blank, translation, question-
formation exercises) are **skipped** except where they introduce vocabulary not otherwise captured.
Narrative readings (folk tale "Učeni sin," "Trgovac i mornar," the "Smiljaniću" folk song, the "Dva
merila"/Lloyd George anecdote, "Historical Background" on Yugoslavia's formation) are paraphrased only,
per copyright discipline — no bulk verbatim reproduction; a short illustrative line is quoted at most.
The `h`/`k` "Pronunciation Practice" word-pair box in Lesson 9 is treated as a phonology drill (see
Grammar points) rather than exhaustively tabulated as vocabulary, since its entries are minimal-pair
illustrations of a sound distinction, not core lexical items being taught — a representative sample is
included in the table and the full pattern is described in the grammar section.

## Grammar points

### Lesson 7 (Sédma lekcija)
Source callout: "imperative, reinforced negation, reflexive verbs, verbal aspect, and sequence of
tenses" (p. 37). The lesson's own worked material illustrates these primarily through the drills
(skipped per the coverage rule) and the "Days of the Week" prose explanation: day-names take the
**nominative** when simply stating what day it is (`Dànas je sùbota`), the **accusative** with
preposition `u` when marking a single occurrence (`u sùbotu`), and the **instrumental** (no
preposition) when marking habitual/recurring timing (`sùbotom`) — this three-way case pattern is
tabulated in full for all seven days in the Vocabulary table above.

### Lesson 8 (Osma lekcija)
Source callout: "dative case, prepositions (especially those with genitive case), special uses of
**na**, past passive participles, and the categories of the Croatian and Serbian verb in diagram
form" (p. 43). Not reproduced verbatim (copyright), but the lesson's "Mòja sòba" reading is built
almost entirely around locative/dative-of-position constructions with `na` and `u` (e.g. "na pisaćem
stólu," "u jèdnom kútu") — consistent with the callout's emphasis on `na`'s special uses for describing
where furniture "is located."

### Lesson 9 (Deveta lekcija)
Source callout: "instrumental case, demonstrative adjectives, cardinal numbers, aorist, and verbal
adverbs" (p. 49). The **h : k pronunciation-practice box** on the same page is a genuine phonological
teaching point distinct from the grammar callout: the source states that **h does not occur in some
dialects** of Cr&S (it is dropped in colloquial speech, e.g. `óću` for `hóću`); that in the speech of
Serbia, **v** regularly appears where Croatian has **h** after the vowel **u** (Cr. `kùhati` : S.
`kùvati` "to cook"; Cr. `úho` : S. `úvo` "ear"; Cr. `glúh` : S. `glúv` "deaf"; Cr. `múha` : S. `múva`
"fly"); and that some speakers substitute **f** for the **hv** cluster (e.g. `fála` for `hvála`,
"thanks"). This is a genuine explicit dialectal/register annotation and is captured per the coverage
rule's mandate to never skip source-marked dialect data.

### Lesson 10 (Deseta lekcija)
The lesson's running prose (not a separate boxed callout in the pages read) explains occupational
noun-gender marking: for occupations held by both men and women, the feminine form is typically
derived from the masculine via the suffixes **-ka**, **-ica**, or **-kinja** (e.g. `učitelj` "teacher
(m.)" → `učiteljica` "teacher (f.)"; `pjevač`/`pevač` "singer (m.)" → `konobar/kelner` "waiter,"
`konobarica/kelnerica` "waitress"). Where Croatian/Serbian variants differ, the source states the
first form given in its glossary is the Croatian one, the second (after the slash) the Serbian one —
this convention is preserved in the Vocabulary table's `X/Y` notation above. A short "Historical
Background" reading on this same page range (pp. 58–59) covers the 1918 formation of the Kingdom of
Serbs, Croats, and Slovenes and its 1929 renaming to Yugoslavia — factual/historical rather than
linguistic content, so not tabulated as vocabulary, but noted here as source context for the
`Attested Era`/`Attested Region` fields used elsewhere in this dataset.

### Lesson 11 (Jedánaesta lekcija)
No separate boxed "Grammar for Lesson XI" callout was found on the pages read (pp. 60–66); this
lesson's actual teaching content is **orthographic**, not morphological — it introduces the Cyrillic
alphabet as one of the two official scripts of Croatian and Serbian, explains its historical
attribution to Saints Cyril and Methodius (missionaries to the Slavs, Moravia, A.D. 863) and its
actual origin as a Greek-based script devised after Cyril's own **Glagolitic** alphabet (displaced by
Cyrillic but surviving into the 20th century in parts of Dalmatia), and states that Cyrillic use
historically tracks Orthodox-Christian regions of the Slavic world. The lesson also notes explicitly
that the Serbian Cyrillic taught here is one of several **distinct national Cyrillic variants** — the
source states Russian, Ukrainian, Belorussian, Bulgarian, and Macedonian each use their own distinct
form of Cyrillic, not identical to Serbian's. The lesson's drill exercise (transliterating Yugoslav
place names into Cyrillic) is captured as geographic/proper-noun vocabulary in the table above, since
it is the lesson's actual worked content, not a repeated sentence drill.

# Serbian — Established Vocabulary: Folk Tales, Part 1 (Tales 1–3, opening)

**Source:** Simeon Marinković & Slavica Marković (eds.), illus. Dobrosav Bob Živković, *Српске
народне бајке* (Serbian Folk Fairy Tales), Kreativni centar, 2000, Belgrade. Printed pages 5–32
(PDF pages 9–36 in the scanned file; empirically verified offset = PDF page − 4). Covers three
tales: "Старац преварио дивове" (pp. 5–9), "Чардак ни на небу ни на земљи" (pp. 10–15), and the
opening of "Баш-Челик" (pp. 16–32, continues in a later chunk).

**Coverage note:** This source is **pure literary/folkloric narrative** (illustrated retellings of
oral folk tales for children), not a pedagogical grammar/vocabulary text — it has no parallel
English translation and no vocabulary boxes. Per dispatch instructions, coverage here is
**vocabulary-only, no plot/prose reproduction**: for each tale, genuinely archaic/regional/literary
vocabulary is extracted and glossed, with a one-line orientation note (title translation + one-
sentence premise) instead of a plot summary. The book carries its own inline "+"-marked margin
glosses for obscure/archaic words (aimed at the book's young modern readers, since these are archaic
dialectal terms even in Serbian) — these were used directly as the primary vocabulary source, with
the book's own Serbian gloss text translated into English and cited in the Notes column. A handful of
additional words without inline glosses (interesting narrative-register vocabulary, e.g.
**претурити**, **муштулук**-adjacent forms) were glossed from context/general knowledge and marked
`plausible_unverified` accordingly.

**Vision-reading guard:** This source has **no text layer at all** (fully scanned, Cyrillic) and was
entirely vision-read. Every page in the assigned range was checked for handwritten marginalia. One
instance was found and excluded: **printed page 26 (PDF page 30) has a line of narrative text
("Јесам, честити царе, — одговори најмлађи царевић.") overwritten with a handwritten blue
highlight/underline** — this is a previous reader's mark, not part of the printed content, and was
excluded from extraction (the line itself is ordinary narrative dialogue, not a vocabulary item, so
nothing was lost). A circular purple ink stamp is also visible in the lower-left margin of printed
page 13 (PDF page 17) — this is a library/archival mark, not printed book content, and was likewise
excluded. No other marginalia was found in this range.

## Grammar points / narrative-register observations

This source is narrative fiction, not a grammar text, so this section covers notable
morphological/register/dialectal patterns observed rather than formal grammar rules.

### Ijekavian reflexes throughout
Consistent with the book's ijekavian dialect base (mixed with some ekavian narration), several
glossed and narrative forms show jat-reflex ije/je where standard ekavian Serbian would have e:
мјешина (vs. мешина), законачити/конак, вриједети-type forms, сјутрадан (vs. сутрадан),
најпослије (vs. најпосле). This is typical of collected oral-tale material transcribed from
ijekavian-speaking storytellers, consistent with the book drawing on 19th-century folk-tale
collections (in the tradition of Vuk Karadžić's corpus).

### Archaic aorist and imperfect narrative tenses
The tales are narrated heavily in aorist forms rather than the perfect tense that dominates modern
colloquial Serbian narration — e.g. "рече," "поче," "дође," "узе," "пусти" as simple-past forms
used as the default narrative past, rather than the analytic "је рекао" construction. This is a
hallmark of the oral-epic/fairy-tale register and a clean, textbook-quality example of a register
distinct from the colloquial and grammar-reference-book Serbian covered elsewhere in this dataset.

### Formulaic doubling/tripling structure
Both tale 2 and tale 3 (in this chunk) show the classic folk-tale rule-of-three structure realized
lexically: near-identical sentences repeat three times with only the ordinal number/name changing
(najstariji → srednji → najmlađi brat; first tower-room → second → third; first cauldron-room
door → ... → ninth). This is a narrative-mechanical pattern rather than a lexical item, but it's
the single most repeated structural device across all three tales and worth flagging for anyone
doing later mechanics-analysis work on Serbian folk-narrative register as a source of derived slang
patterns (formulaic triadic repetition is a recognizable "old-story-telling" register marker that
could plausibly get referenced/parodied in later slang formation).

### Orientation notes on the three tales covered (not plot summaries — one line each)
- **Старац преварио дивове** ("The Old Man Outwitted the Giants," pp. 5–9): an elderly miller uses
  cunning (feigned superhuman strength and threats) to escape and outwit a band of giants who nearly
  make him their prisoner-turned-master.
- **Чардак ни на небу ни на земљи** ("The Tower Neither in the Sky nor on the Earth," pp. 10–15): a
  princess is abducted by a dragon; her youngest brother finds and kills the dragon in a
  floating/hovering enchanted tower, frees her and three other captive maidens, but is betrayed
  (nearly stranded) by his envious older brothers before the truth comes out.
- **Баш-Челик** ("Real Steel," pp. 16–32, unfinished in this chunk): a dying king has his sons
  promise to marry off their three sisters to whoever first comes to claim them; three
  supernatural suitors (in later pages revealed to be dragon-, falcon-, and eagle-kings) claim the
  sisters on three successive nights. The youngest brother, searching for his sisters, frees an
  imprisoned iron giant (Baš-Čelik) from a cursed room by giving him water — an act that costs him
  his own wife, whom Baš-Čelik then abducts, sending him on the search that structures the rest of
  the tale (continues in a later chunk).

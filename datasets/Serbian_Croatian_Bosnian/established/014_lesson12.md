# Serbian/Croatian/Bosnian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Dvanaesta lekcija / Lesson Twelve

**Source:** Ronelle Alexander & Ellen Elias-Bursać, *Bosnian, Croatian, Serbian, a Textbook: With
Exercises and Basic Grammar* (2nd ed., 2010), "Dvanaesta lekcija / Lesson Twelve," pp. 177–196
(PDF pages 198–217).

**Coverage note:** every grammar point and every distinct vocabulary item from the lesson's four
vocabulary boxes (A1–A4) plus the "Aforizmi Dušana Radovića" mini-reading, every explicit `[B]`/
`[C]`/`[S]` dialectal annotation, and every genuine lexical difference visible in the lesson's
parallel Bosnian/Croatian/Serbian dialogue columns (even where the book places the difference in
running dialogue rather than bracket-tagging it directly inside a vocabulary box — e.g. month
names, the `da li` vs. `-li` question-particle placement). Repeated drill/exercise sections (B1–B7,
C1–C6, the word-search puzzle) are skipped per the extraction spec's coverage rule: they are pure
practice material reusing vocabulary already captured from this same chunk, and several (C1, the
word-search) present isolated clue-words with no source-given English gloss, so they cannot be
reliably tabulated as vocabulary items even if wanted. This source has a clean text layer
(`pdftotext` extraction was fully usable) — no vision-reading was needed, no marginalia present.

**A note on `Attested Region` tagging in this file.** Most entries below carry the book's own
explicit bracket tags (`[B]`, `[C]`, `[S]`, or combinations). A smaller set of entries (flagged in
their own Notes cell) reflect the book's *systematic* ekavian/ijekavian (E/J) sound-correspondence
notation — the book brackets this pattern explicitly elsewhere within this same lesson (e.g. `dve
[E] dvije [J]` on p. 179, `na leto [E] na ljeto [J]` on p. 186) but does not re-bracket every
individual word pair that follows the same regular correspondence (e.g. `cveće`/`cvijeće`,
`setiti se`/`sjetiti se`). Applying the book's own documented E/J convention to these same-pattern
pairs is a judgment call, flagged transparently per-row rather than silently treated as identical
to a literal bracket tag.

## Grammar points

*(Sixteen boxed "zGRAMMAR" points from this lesson, plus one grammatical difference visible in the
parallel B/C/S dialogue but not separately boxed by the source.)*

### Cardinal numbers, review [123a]

Cardinal numbers are used for counting. The number 1 (jèdan/jèdno/jèdna) is an adjective agreeing
with its noun. The number 2 has one form for masculine/neuter nouns (dva) and another for feminine
(dve [E] / dvije [J]); no other number is gender-sensitive. Multiples of 100 either fuse the
cardinal directly into one word or count the word stotina, with alternate 200/300 forms used in
Bosnian and Serbian. Multiples of 1000 count tìsuća [C] or hìljada [B,S]. When stotina/hìljada/
tìsuća names a number it's Nsg; when it counts something, it's Asg. (p. 179)

### Agreement with cardinal numbers — case selection [124a]

The number 1 takes the singular noun form. Numbers 2–4 take the "counting form" (roughly Gsg, but
the feminine -e ending is short rather than long); modifiers take -a for masc/neut nouns and -e for
feminine, both unlengthened. Numbers 5–20 take the Gpl. For compound numbers, the final digit
governs: forms ending in 1 (21, 351, 3951, ...) take the singular; forms ending in 2–4 take the
counting form; everything else takes the Gpl. (p. 179)

### Fractions and approximative numbers [123d]

Except for "quarter" and "half," a fraction's denominator is formed by adding the suffix -ina to
the ordinal-number stem (treći "3rd" → trećìna "1/3"), and the numerator counts this new noun.
Approximative numbers ("ten or so") add the suffix -ak to the cardinal (deset "10" → desètak "10 or
so"). "Half" is polòvica [C] / polòvina [B,S]; "quarter" is either četvrt or četvrtìna. (p. 180)

### The relative conjunction kòji as subject [128]

As a question word, kòji means "which/what." As a relative conjunction it corresponds to English
"who, whom, which, that" — and unlike English, BCS can never omit it. Its form must agree in gender
and number with its antecedent; its case is determined by its own grammatical role within its own
clause. Example: stùdenti KOJ̀I stànuju tamo mòraju sami urèditi svòje sobe "the students WHO live
there have to arrange their own rooms" (kòji is nominative because it is the subject of its own
clause). (p. 180)

### Present verbal adverb [129, 138a, 138c]

Formed by adding the unchanging suffix -ći to the 3rd-plural present form of an imperfective verb.
It marks an action simultaneous with the main verb, both referring to the same subject: vozeći se
autòbusom čìta novine "she reads the paper while riding the bus." It can also express mild
causation, as in mnòžeći to sa 180, dòbijamo 720 "multiplying that by 180, we get 720." The adverb
from biti is built on the stem budem (bùdući); the related conjunction budući da means "since." A
number of these adverbs have become double-duty adjectives with a subtly shifted meaning (taking
full adjectival endings) — e.g. ìdući "next," sljèdeći "next, following," vodeći "leading, top,"
mogući "possible," odgovàrajući "relevant, corresponding." (p. 180–181)

### Agreement with cardinal numbers — subject/verb agreement [continued, no separate rule number given here]

Nouns following 1 (or compounds ending in 1) are singular and take a singular verb. Nouns following
2–4 take the counting form; as a subject they take a plural verb, but the past-tense L-participle
itself takes the counting form. Nouns following 5+ take the Gpl; as a subject they take a singular
verb, and the L-participle is neuter singular. Numbers don't change form after a preposition. (p.
184)

### Group numbers [123c, 124d]

Two extra number series exist for groups: one for all-male human groups, one for mixed-gender (or
occasionally object) groups; groups of females just use the ordinary cardinal. Both are built from
the same stem: -ica marks the all-male form (dvòjica, tròjica, četvòrica...), while -oro [B,S] /
-ero [C] marks the mixed-group form (dvoje, troje, četvoro/četvero...). The counted noun/pronoun is
Gpl: nas dvoje "the two of us [mixed]," vas tròjica "the three of you [males]," troje dèce/djèce
"three children." A mixed-gender subject takes a plural present-tense verb but a neuter-singular
past tense. The -ica (all-male) forms decline like feminine singular nouns and, as subject, take a
plural present verb but a feminine-singular past L-participle. A group of females is counted with
the ordinary feminine cardinal plus a plural verb. (p. 184–185)

### Past verbal adverb [129, 138b, 138c]

Formed by dropping the -o from a perfective verb's masculine-singular L-participle and adding the
unchanging suffix -vši. It marks an action completed before the main verb's action, both referring
to the same subject: rekavši to, ìzašao je iz sobe "having said that, he left the room." Used only
in written style; only bivši "former" has become a full adjective. (p. 185)

### Collective nouns [115]

Collective nouns are singular nouns denoting an object-group seen as indivisible, mostly neuter
singular (cveće/cvijeće "flowers," drveće "trees," lišće "leaves," kàmenje "stones," grànje
"branches"). Regular plurals exist (cvetovi/cvjetovi, drvèta) but are comparatively rare, since
individuated instances of such objects are usually counted after a number instead. A second
collective type — small/young creatures as a group, often with endearing overtones — is feminine
singular, e.g. ùnučad "small grandchildren" (sg. ùnuče). momčad, derived from mòmče "lad," is now
mostly used to mean "[sports] team." (p. 185)

### Ordinal numbers in naming dates [124b]

Ordinal numbers mark a place in a series and are used above all for dates: the day-of-month is a
Gsg ordinal followed by the month name in the genitive, e.g. "March 8th" = osmog marta [B,S] / osmog
òžujka [C]. When the date is written numerically, a period follows the number: 8. marta [B,S]. (p.
187)

### Discussing one's age [124e, 124b]

To state age, use either the dative (njemu je dvàdeset jèdna godina "he is 21") or ìmati + direct
object (on ìma dvàdeset jèdnu godinu). Alternatively, the noun gòdište modified by the ordinal
number of the birth year: Ja sam 87. gòdište "I was born in '87." nàpuniti marks a birthday: sutra
će nàpuniti 31 godinu "he'll turn 31 tomorrow." A period after a number marks it as ordinal rather
than cardinal (87 godina "87 years" vs. 87. godina "the 87th year"). (p. 187)

### The "exact future" [130, 144a]

Alongside the ordinary future (with hteti/htjeti), BCS has a second future tense: the auxiliary
budem plus the L-participle. It can in principle be built from any verb but in practice is almost
always imperfective, and appears only after conjunctions with potential-future meaning (kad, ako,
čim, etc.). Its meaning matches using the present tense of a perfective verb after the same
conjunctions, but it gives imperfective verbs more sense of "closure" — kad budeš slavila
rođendan... puts sharper focus on the concrete future event than the plain present would. (p.
187–188)

### The conjunction što [143g]

"That" is usually da (znam da je dòšao "I know that he came"), but after verbs of emotion, što is
often used instead: žao mi je što nìje mogao dòći "I'm sorry [that] he couldn't come." (p. 188)

### Words meaning "ought, might, may" [119, 127, 142]

Several verbs of possibility/obligation take a following verb naming the action. trebati means
either "need" or "should," depending on context. smeti [E] / smjeti [J] can mean both "dare" (nè
smem to propùstiti "I don't dare miss it") and "may/might" (smijem li zamòliti...? "may I ask...?").
valjati is exceptional: with a subject it means "be good" (òve jabuke više nè valjaju "these apples
are no longer good"), but in a subjectless sentence it means the same as treba (njihove običaje
valja poštòvati "one should respect their customs"). (p. 188)

### Ordinal numbers, continued [124b]

Compound ordinals put the ordinal ending only on the final segment, as in English ("eight hundred
forty SEVENTH" = osamsto četrdèset SEDMI). This is most often seen naming a year, where only the
final number takes a case form — almost always Gsg feminine, agreeing with the (often-omitted) noun
godine: "March 8, 1981" = osmog marta hìljadu devetsto osamdèset prve [godine] [B,S]. A written
numeral followed by a period signals an ordinal reading: 8. mart 1981. g. (p. 188–189)

### Aorist and imperfect [122, 150]

Two further past tenses, both now relatively archaic and mostly confined to (especially older)
literary texts. Both are single-word forms. The aorist relates individual completed actions in
sequence; the imperfect describes background states or ongoing background actions. Within each
tense the 2nd and 3rd singular share a form, and for some verbs the 3sg present and 3sg aorist
coincide, so care is needed to tell 3pl aorist from 3sg imperfect. In spoken Serbian and Bosnian,
the aorist of certain verbs (biti, reći) is still heard with some frequency — nearly all BCS
speakers use the colloquial idiom odoh ja ("I went away," i.e. "I'm out of here"). (p. 189)

### Yes/no question formation: da li vs. clitic -li (observed, not separately boxed)

Not presented as its own zGRAMMAR box, but directly visible in the A3 parallel Bosnian/Croatian/
Serbian dialogue (p. 186): the Bosnian and Croatian columns form the yes/no question with the
enclitic question particle -li attached directly after the verb (Hòćeš LI sà mnom? "Will you [come]
with me?"), while the Serbian column instead fronts the standalone particle da li (Da li hòćeš sà
mnom?). Both strategies exist across the whole BCS area generally, but this lesson's parallel-column
dialogue happens to consistently split the two options along the B/C vs. S line for this particular
sentence — worth flagging as a genuine, source-evidenced dialectal data point even though the book
doesn't box it as a formal rule here.

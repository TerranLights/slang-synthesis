# Serbian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Teach Yourself Serbian, Units 16–18 ("Health matters," "Buying clothes," "Meeting in Novi Sad")

**Source:** David Norris & Vladislava Ribnikar, *Teach Yourself Serbian* (Hodder Headline, 2003),
printed pages 234–264 (PDF pages 127–142 of the 177-page scan). Unit 16 "Health matters" (234–244),
Unit 17 "Buying clothes" (245–253), Unit 18 "Meeting in Novi Sad" (255–264). Page 254 (end of Unit
17's exercises) is visually adjacent to Unit 18's title spread but contributes no new content of its
own beyond what's captured under Unit 17 below.

**Vision-reading note.** This source has a garbled/unusable OCR text layer and was read entirely by
vision, at 150 DPI, from PDF pages rendered as two-printed-pages-per-image spreads. The offset was
verified empirically before committing to the range: PDF page 127 = printed pages 234–235, confirmed
directly by inspection rather than assumed from a fixed-offset formula (per the extraction spec's PDF
gotchas section). **No handwritten marginalia was found anywhere in this range** — every page
inspected showed clean, consistent typeset print matching the surrounding book design; nothing was
excluded on that basis.

**Range correction versus the dispatch estimate.** The dispatch assumed Unit 18 was the book's final
unit and that "Key to the exercises" back matter would begin shortly after page ~267. That assumption
was wrong: **Unit 18 ends cleanly at printed page 264**, and printed page 265 begins **Unit 19 "On
the telephone"** (title spread: "expressions when using the telephone," "more about reflexive verbs,"
"about the aorist and pluperfect"), which continues at least through page 269 with no "Key to the
exercises" heading in sight. This chunk stops at page 264 as assigned (Units 16–18 only). **Key to
the exercises has NOT yet been located** — it begins somewhere after Unit 19 (and likely further
units beyond that), not immediately after Unit 18 as originally estimated. Whoever picks up Unit 19+
should re-verify the back-matter boundary empirically rather than trusting the original estimate.

**Script alternation note.** This book alternates which script (Latin vs. Cyrillic) it uses for
dialogue text on a per-unit basis, apparently for balanced reading practice rather than any
dialectal/register signal: Unit 15 (preceding, Latin), Unit 16 (Cyrillic), Unit 17 (Latin), Unit 18
(Cyrillic), Unit 19 (Latin, per pages 266–269 sampled while locating the chunk boundary). Not a
B/C/S-style regional annotation — noted here only so a later reader isn't confused by the switch.
Vocabulary terms below are transcribed as printed (Cyrillic where the unit uses Cyrillic).

**Coverage note:** every grammar point and every distinct vocabulary item from Units 16–18, plus one
explicit sociolinguistic note the source itself makes about loanword adoption (§ "Telephone call,"
Unit 18, p.261) — the highest-value register-adjacent content in this range. Repeated drill exercises
and duplicate example sentences that don't introduce new vocabulary or grammar are skipped.

## Grammar points

### Contacting someone (Unit 16, p.237)
Four verbs for "to contact": писати некоме (write to someone, dat.), звати некога (call someone,
acc. — by phone or by calling out loud), телефонирати некоме (telephone someone, dat.), јављати се
некоме (get in touch with someone, dat., usually by phone but can be in writing). Also: the word веза
"connection" (already known from travel contexts) extends to "being in touch" — Бићемо у вези "We
shall be in touch."

### As...as possible (Unit 16, p.237)
что + comparative adverb/adjective = "as X as possible": што пре "as soon as possible," што брже "as
quickly as possible," што лакше "as easily as possible."

### All the more... (Unit 16, p.238)
све + comparative form = "all the more..., more and more": Она је све лепша "She is all the more
beautiful," Постаје све хладније "It is getting colder and colder."

### To hurt, ache (Unit 16, p.238)
The verb болети is only ever used in the third person: Боли ме нога (lit. "The leg hurts me") = "My
leg hurts"; Боли га грло = "He has a sore throat."

### Adjectives in -стан (Unit 16, p.238)
Adjectives with the masc. nom. sg. ending -стан (e.g. болестан "ill") drop both the т and the а when
adding case endings — fem. form болесна.

### Two or three / a day or two (Unit 16, p.238)
To say "two or three" of something, simply juxtapose the two numbers with a hyphen: два-три дана
"two or three days." For "a day or two," the construction дан-два is used, with the word један "one"
understood but omitted.

### May not.../must not... (Unit 16, p.240)
Three modal verbs contrast: моћи "to be able, can" (physical/circumstantial capability — Можеш да
купиш те таблете "You can buy those tablets," e.g. you have the money), смети "may" (permission —
Смем ли да отворим прозор? "May I open the window?"), морати "to have to, must" (obligation — Јован
мора да лежи у кревету "Jovan has to stay in bed"). Negated forms carry distinct senses: не можеш =
physically/circumstantially unable, не смеш = not permitted, не мораш = not obligatory (but still
permitted).

### More about verbal aspect (Unit 16, pp.240–241)
Most verbs have both an imperfective and a perfective form; imperfective marks an incomplete,
in-progress, or habitual action, while perfective marks a completed or intended-to-be-completed
action. Three common derivation patterns from the imperfective (cited as the basic form) to the
perfective: (1) add a prefix — будити → пробудити "to wake"; (2) change to a different verb type,
often with an -и- ending — остављати → оставити "to leave"; (3) the perfective is one syllable
shorter than the imperfective — давати → дати "to give."

### Parts of the body: irregular nouns (Unit 16, pp.242–243)
Several body-part nouns don't follow standard declension patterns: око/уво follow regular neuter
singular patterns (gen. ока, ува) but shift to feminine plurals очи "eyes" / уши "ears," with a full
plural case table given (nom./acc. очи, уши; gen. очију, ушију; dat./ins./loc. очима, ушима). рука,
нога, прст follow regular fem./masc. patterns except for irregular genitive plurals: руку, ногу,
прстију. рука covers both "hand" and "arm"; нога covers both "foot" and "leg"; прст covers both
"finger" (прст на руци) and "toe" (прст на нози). раме "shoulder" follows the neuter pattern of
време with an inserted -ен- syllable before case endings (gen. sg. рамена). уста "mouth" and леђа
"back" are neuter nouns that are always plural, like кола "car" and врата "door" — Боле ме леђа "I
have backache."

### At the pharmacy / body idioms (Unit 16, p.243)
Several idioms built on body-part vocabulary: далеко од очију, далеко од срца "out of sight, out of
mind"; заљубљен до ушију "head over heels in love"; остати празних руку "to be left empty-handed"; од
малих ногу "from a tender age."

### Present gerund (Unit 17, p.248)
Formed by adding -ћи to the third-person plural present tense (the "они" form): they мисле →
мислећи "thinking," читају → читajући "reading," слушају → слушajући "listening." Expresses "while
doing X" / "by doing X," with the introductory word left unexpressed in Serbian (unlike English
"while..."). **Explicitly flagged by the source as "associated with a formal or bookish style" and
not used very often in the spoken language.** Only applies to two simultaneous actions performed by
the same subject, and is formed only from imperfective verbs.

### One of... (Unit 17, p.248)
"One of..." is expressed with jedan (agreeing with the referenced noun) + the preposition od + gen.:
Pogledaćemo u jednom od tržnih centara "We'll have a look in one of the shopping centres" — jednom is
masc./loc. because the sense is "in one (shopping centre) of the shopping centres."

### Street names and case endings (Unit 17, p.249)
Two common ways to name streets: (1) an adjective formed from a person's surname agreeing with ulica
"street" (e.g. Нушићева улица "Nušić Street," from playwright Branislav Nušić, 1864–1938) — when
talking about such a street, just the adjectival name with the appropriate case ending is used (u
Нушићевој, loc.); (2) the genitive case directly (Ulica Kneza Miloša "Street of Prince Miloš"), where
a preposition goes in front but the gen. ending doesn't change, and the word ulica is often left
understood (Radnja je u (ulici) Kneza Miloša).

### Whose (Unit 17, p.249)
чији "whose" is an adjective and takes the regular case endings for adjectives with a soft final
consonant: Чије је ово дете? "Whose is this child?"

### From... (Unit 17, pp.249–250)
Three prepositions correspond to "from," all governing the genitive case, chosen based on which
locative preposition would describe being *at* that place: (1) од is used where kod + gen. would
describe being there (Jovan je kod lekara → Jovan ide od lekara "...from the doctor's"), and also for
"from" a person (Jovan je dobio pismo od brata "Jovan received a letter from his brother") and set
"from" phrases (Od Beograda do Novog Sada "from Belgrade to Novi Sad"); (2) из is used where u + loc.
would describe being there (Jovan je u školi → Jovan ide iz škole "...from school"); (3) sa is used
where na + loc. would describe being there, and covers "from off" (Vesna je bila na brdu → Sišla je
sa brda "She came down off the hill"; Kopaonik, being a mountain, takes na/sa rather than u/iz).

### Colours (Unit 17, p.252)
браон "brown" is one of the very few Serbian adjectives whose ending never changes regardless of the
noun's gender/case: браон одело (neut.), браон јакна (fem.). Dark shades are formed with the prefix
тамно- and light shades with свeтло- (тамноцрвена кравата "dark-red tie").

### Past gerund (Unit 17, p.253)
Formed by replacing the final -о of the masc. verbal adjective with -вши: стигао "arrived" → стигавши
"having arrived," прочитао "read" → прочитавши "having read." Equivalent to English "having done X,"
sometimes rendered with "after" or "when." **Like the present gerund, explicitly flagged by the
source as "associated with a very formal style" and not used very often in spoken Serbian.** Used only
for two sequential actions by the same subject (the first completed before the second begins), and is
formed only from perfective verbs.

### Formation of passive (Unit 18, pp.257–259)
The Serbian passive is an adjective (agreeing with the noun it describes) formed from the infinitive
of either aspect, via four patterns: (1) infinitives in -ати → replace with -ан for masc. nom. sg.
(читати → читан, позвати → позван, заказати → заказан); (2) infinitives in -ити/-ети → replace with
-jен or -ен for masc. nom. sg., with the preceding consonant softening according to the same pattern
as -ји comparative adjectives (cross-referenced to Unit 14: видети → виђен, затворити → затворен,
одлучити → одлучен); (3) infinitives in -нути → replace with -нут (окренути → окренут); (4)
infinitives in -ети with a present tense in -мем or -нем → replace the infinitive ending with -ет
(узети, узмем → узет).

### Using the passive (Unit 18, p.258)
Examples of the passive functioning as a predicate adjective agreeing with its subject: Душан
Поповић је био позван у Београд "Dušan Popović was invited to Belgrade"; Одлучено је "It is decided";
Радња је отворена "The shop is open."

### Writing letters — formal register (Unit 18, p.259)
Formal letters open with Поштовани господине... / Поштована госпођо... / Поштована господо, (name in
vocative), for which the less formal Драги... "Dear..." may be substituted. In a formal letter, the
pronoun Ви is capitalized when addressing one person but lowercase when it's a genuine plural. Formal
requests/statements are typically phrased in the conditional mood to soften the tone and read as more
polite. E-mail doesn't easily carry this level of formality. Formal closings: Са поштовањем (lit.
"With respect"), Срдачно вас/Вас поздравља (lit. "Cordially greets you"), Срдачан поздрав (lit.
"Cordial greetings").

### Writing letters — informal register (Unit 18, p.259)
Informal letters open with Драги.../Драга... + the addressee's name in the vocative, and close with
Много поздрава (lit. "Many greetings") or Воли те... (lit. "Loves you...," an intimate closing).

### Personal names and cases (Unit 18, p.263)
Most Serbian surnames end in -ић and decline for case when referring to a man, but not when referring
to a woman (since the final-consonant ending would require masc. case endings that don't fit a
feminine referent) — e.g. Могу ли да добијем Марка Павловића? (declined, masc.) vs. Могу ли да добијем
Кристину Јовановић? (surname undeclined for the woman). Foreign names are adapted into Serbian as
closely as possible: foreign men's names ending in a consonant (Charles, John) are simply transcribed
(Чарлс, Џон) and decline like ordinary masc. names; women's names ending in -a receive the same
treatment (Moira → Мојра, Sylvia → Силвија); women's Christian names ending in a consonant are
transliterated but remain indeclinable (Jane → Џејн, invariable across cases).

### Loanword adoption note (Unit 18, p.261) — sociolinguistic observation

tends to adopt foreign words readily into its vocabulary," citing бизнис "business," бизнисмен
"businessman," компјутер "computer," and вебсајт "web site" as examples. It also notes that a foreign
loanword and a native Serbian word can coexist with the same meaning — менаџмент and управа both mean
"management." Alternatively, some foreign concepts get a Serbian-coined translation rather than a
loanword — a computer mouse is called миш ("mouse," a calque/native coinage, not a borrowing of
"mouse" itself as a loanword). This is the single most register-relevant observation the source makes

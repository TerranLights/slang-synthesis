# Serbian/Croatian/Bosnian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Magner Lessons 12–16

**Source:** Thomas F. Magner, *Introduction to the Croatian and Serbian Language* (revised ed.,
1991/1998), "Dvanaesta lekcija" through "Šesnaesta lekcija" (Lessons 12–16), printed pages 67–95
(PDF pages 40–54 of `12.Introduction to the Croatian and Serbian language.pdf`). Lesson 11 ends
mid-page 66 (PDF page 40, left side); Lesson 12 begins on the facing page 67 (PDF page 40, right
side). Lesson 16 ends on printed page 95 (PDF page 54); Lesson 17 ("Sedamnaesta lekcija") begins
cleanly on printed page 96 (PDF page 55) — no overlap with the neighboring chunk in either
direction.

**Vision-reading note.** This is a scanned book with no usable text layer; every page in this range
was read visually. All pages inspected were clearly typeset print (consistent font, no
inconsistent-weight marks in margins or blank spaces) — **no handwritten marginalia was found in
this chunk** (unlike the Lesson 1 copy referenced in the extraction spec, this section of the scan
shows no reader annotation). A dark binding-gutter shadow runs down the center of most two-page
spreads in this scan and occasionally obscures the first character or two of gutter-adjacent lines;
where that affected legibility it is flagged per-row below via Vision Reading Confidence.

**Coverage note.** This book presents each lesson as a fully parallel Croatian (Latin script,
ijekavian) / Serbian (Cyrillic script, ekavian) dialogue of ~15–30 numbered sentences, followed by
an English translation of the dialogue, drill exercises (skipped per the coverage rule — pure
practice reusing already-captured vocabulary), and then a compact stand-alone vocabulary/reference
box (e.g., "Months of the Year," "Countries and Continents," "Parts of the Body," "Letter-Writing
Formulas"). Per copyright discipline, the numbered dialogues and English translations are
paraphrased/summarized rather than reproduced; the stand-alone reference boxes (which are already
compact word-and-gloss lists rather than prose) are extracted in full since they are the highest
lexical-density content and copying a two-column term list is not the kind of "vocabulary box"
bulk-reproduction the discipline rule is aimed at avoiding (no more than the box's own compact
term↔gloss pairs are kept; no surrounding explanatory prose is quoted). The book's own Croatian/
Serbian dialect labeling is dual-script/dual-dialect throughout (Croatian ijekavian Latin vs.
Serbian ekavian Cyrillic column-pairs), captured below as `Attested Region` = `Cr.` / `S.` following
the convention set in `established/002_lesson01_magner_vision_test.md`.

**Overlap check against prior established/ files.** This is the first Magner-book chunk covering
Lessons 12–16 for this project (prior established/014–018 files are a *different* source — Alexander
& Elias-Bursać's *Bosnian, Croatian, Serbian, a Textbook*, whose Lessons 12/16 cover different
in-textbook content: family/apartment description and travel-letter topics respectively). No
redundant-supplement narrowing was needed; this chunk's vocabulary (months, countries, body parts,
letter formulas) does not overlap with what 014–018 already captured.

## Grammar points

### Lesson 12 — dates and the two month-name systems
Cr&S has two fully parallel sets of month names — a Serbian set (transparent borrowings, similar to
the English/international terms) and a Croatian set (native Slavic coinages, e.g. `siječanj`,
`ožujak`). The lesson gives the case forms needed to state a date three ways: (1) the month alone in
nominative when naming the current month (`Ovaj mjesec je ožujak`); (2) an ordinal + month in
genitive singular for "on the Nth of [month]" (`osmoga ožujka` "on the eighth of March"); (3) month
alone in dative/locative singular after `u` for "in [month]" (`u ožujku`). Croatian `studeni`
(November) is unusual among the Croatian set in declining like an adjective rather than a noun.

### Lesson 12 — reading-passage genitive/comparative constructions
The lesson's short anecdote ("Dva merila" / "Two Measures," about Lloyd George's height) is used to
illustrate genitive-of-comparison constructions ("meriti od brade naniže" / "od brade naviše" — "to
measure from the chin downward/upward") without a dedicated grammar-box explanation; the
construction is left implicit in the reading and drilled indirectly through the lesson's translation
exercise.

### Lesson 13 — adjectival vs. nominal declension of country names
Country names are split into two declension classes, marked in the source with an asterisk. Most
take regular nominal (noun-type) case endings (`u Africi`, `u Kanadi`). A large subset instead take
adjectival case endings because the country name is historically/morphologically an adjective (e.g.
`Bugarska`, `Engleska`, `Francuska`, `Njemačka`, all literally "[the] X-ian [land]"): `u Bugarskoj`,
`u Grčkoj`. This distinction is drilled in vježba II (fill-in-the-blank case forms for country
names) and reinforced by the parallel "countries and continents" reference table, which gives each
country's inhabitant-noun and adjective forms alongside the country name itself.

### Lesson 13 — prepositions governing different cases by direction
Vježba II drills a contrast the lesson's dialogue sets up implicitly: `u` + locative for static
location vs. `iz`/`kroz`/`po` + genitive/instrumental/locative for source, motion-through, and
distributed motion respectively (e.g. `Letjeli smo preko [Engleska]`, `Kroz [Čehoslovačka] smo
putovali avionom`, `Mnogo smo putovali po [Evropa]`). Preposition choice tracks the semantic
relationship to the place name, not just "location" generically.

### Lesson 13 — aspect pairing in conditional/temporal clauses
Vježba III systematically pairs an imperfective verb in the `ako`/`kad` (if/when) clause with a
perfective verb in the main clause (or vice versa depending on the intended aspectual reading),
e.g. `Ako (vi) stajali kod prozora, vidjeli veliki hrast` — testing the learner's ability to select
present-tense imperfective for ongoing/habitual conditions vs. perfective for completed/punctual
ones within the same sentence frame.

### Lesson 14 — clitic pronoun placement (Wackernagel position)
Vježba I has the learner insert object clitic pronouns (`ga`, `joj`, `mu`, `im`, etc.) into
sentences built around verbs like `kupiti`, `glèdati`, `rèći`, `ìgrati se`, `pòzvati` — the drill's
design (short, second-position-friendly sentences) reinforces the rule that unstressed pronominal
and auxiliary clitics cluster in second position in the clause (after the first stressed
constituent), not necessarily adjacent to the verb they depend on.

### Lesson 14 — possessive/anatomical description with body-part vocabulary
The lesson's "Human Body" dialogue systematically walks head-to-foot through body-part terms (using
the vocabulary box above) paired with `na vrhu`, `na svakoj strani`, `spaja X s Y`, `spojen(i) s/u`
constructions for describing spatial relationships between body parts — a template reusable for any
"top of/side of/joined to" spatial description.

### Lesson 15 — comparative degree of adjectives
Vježba III drills the productive comparative suffix `-iji`/`-ji` (`star` → `stariji`, `lijepa` →
`ljepš-`, `ružan` → `ružniji`, `gladan` → `gladniji`, `žedan` → `žedniji`), including irregular
stem-final consonant softening in some adjectives, and the genitive-of-comparison construction
("X je stariji **od** Y") as the default way to express "more X than Y."

### Lesson 15 — sociolinguistic register in letter-writing formulas
The lesson's letter-formula reference box is a direct, explicit register map: formal openings use
`Poštovani`/`Cijenjeni`/`Uvaženi` + title/surname and formal closings use `s poštovanjem`/`Vaš
odani`; informal openings use `Dragi`/`Voljeni`/`Mili` + first name or kin term, with informal
closings built around `Primi pozdrave`/`Voli vas`/`U mislima s tobom`. Several formal address terms
carry `drug(arica)` "comrade," a socialist-era-specific formal address form tied to the book's 1991
publication context (Yugoslavia was still the SFRJ). This is a compact, ready-made formal/informal
register contrast the mechanics-analysis phase can draw on directly.

### Lesson 15 — Latin vs. Cyrillic cursive orthography and script-linked dialect variants
The lesson reproduces a handwritten (but source-printed, not reader-added — confirmed typeset
facsimile reproduction, not real marginalia) letter twice, once in Latin cursive (Croatian,
ijekavian) and once in Cyrillic cursive (Serbian, ekavian), with an explicit footnote listing the
specific word-level substitutions a Serbian writer would use in the Cyrillic version: `ko` for
`tko` ("who"), `hteo` for `htio` ("wanted," past tense), `umem` for `umijem` ("I know how"), `snega`
for `snijega` ("of snow," genitive). This is a clean, source-confirmed set of ijekavian↔ekavian
lexical/phonological correspondences additional to the more commonly cited (i)je/e alternation
pattern.

### Lesson 16 — superlative degree of adjectives
Vježba III drills the superlative, formed by prefixing `naj-` to the comparative (`dobra` →
`najbolja`, `drag` → `najdraži`, `visok` → `najviši`, `mlad` → `najmlađi`, `duga` → `najduža`,
`velik` → `najveći`, `star`/`marljiva` → `najstarija`/`najmarljivija`), continuing directly from
Lesson 15's comparative-degree drilling.

### Lesson 16 — reflexive verb conjugation across tenses
Vježba VI drills reflexive verbs (`brijati se`, `takmičiti se`, `vratiti se`, `čuditi se`, `igrati
se`, `nadati se`, `smijati se`/`smejati se`, `zvati se`) across multiple tenses, requiring the
learner to correctly place both the tense-marking auxiliary/ending and the reflexive particle `se`
in a single sentence (e.g. reconstructing `Sinoć se Pero vratio iz Južne Amerike` from prompted
infinitive + subject + adverbial).

### Lesson 16 — vocative case with proper and common nouns
Vježba IV drills vocative case formation directly from a nominative-subject cue sentence (e.g.
`Marka šije haljinu` → `Što/Šta šiješ, Marko?`), covering both personal names (`Nada` → `Nado`,
`Marka` → `Marko`, `Otac` → `Oče` implied by pattern) and common nouns, reinforcing vocative as a
distinct, actively-produced case rather than a fossilized/archaic form in this variety.

### Lesson 16 — the "Oj Violo" song as a showcase of Dalmatian ikavian/coastal dialect
The lesson uses the Dalmatian folk song "Oj Violo" (about a sailor's farewell to his sweetheart) as
a worked example of regional dialect features distinct from the book's standard-Cr./standard-S.
dual presentation used everywhere else: ikavian vowel reflexes and several regionalisms/Italianisms
(`riva`, `vapor`) that the source itself glosses inline as non-standard/regional rather than folding
silently into the main vocabulary stream — a useful independent confirmation point for this
project's regional-variant tracking, separate from the book's usual Cr./S. binary.

# Serbian/Croatian/Bosnian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Magner, Lessons 27–30 (final lessons)

**Source:** Thomas F. Magner, *Introduction to the Croatian and Serbian Language* (Revised Edition,
Penn State University Press, 1991; 1998 update), "Dvadeset sedma lekcija" through "Trideseta
lekcija" / "Lesson 27" through "Lesson 30," printed pp. 152–175. PDF pages 83–94 of
`12.Introduction to the Croatian and Serbian language.pdf`.

**Scope-correction note (important for the Book Triage Catalog / checklist).** The dispatch for this
chunk assumed an estimated PDF range of ~128–156 for "Lesson 27 through the final lesson." That
estimate was wrong: PDF pages 100–156 are **not** lesson content at all — page ~95 begins a
"Čitanka/Читанка" (Reader) section of 12 numbered reading selections (unrelated to the "Lesson N"
numbering), and pages further in (checked at PDF p. 128, printed pp. 242–243) are deep into a later
numbered-paragraph grammar-reference appendix. **Lesson 30 is confirmed as the actual final numbered
lesson in this book** — the triage catalog's "clean 30-lesson structure" guess was correct on the
total count, just wrong on where lesson content physically sits in the PDF. The real boundaries:
Lesson 27 begins at PDF p. 83 (printed p. 152, heading "DVADESET SEDMA LEKCIJA"/"ДВАДЕСЕТ СЕДМА
ЛЕКЦИЈА"), and Lesson 30 ends at PDF p. 94 (printed p. 175), closing with the proverb "Konac djelo
krasi" ("The end crowns the work"). **The Čitanka/Reader section (back matter relative to this
chunk's scope) begins at PDF p. 95** with its own table of contents (12 readings, including
"Šestinska svadba" and "Iskustva mladog liječnika," previously glimpsed during page-range
reconnaissance but out of scope for this dispatch — a future chunk should cover it explicitly, and
the checklist/triage catalog should record PDF pp. 95+ as unclaimed Reader-section territory, not as
"lessons 27–30").

**Coverage note:** every distinct vocabulary item from the four lessons' reading texts, "Mali
oglasi"/realia inserts, comparative national/regional/city-demonym vocabulary lists, and every
explicit `(Cr.)`/`(S.)` regional-variant tag the source itself marks, plus lexical pairs that are
clearly Croatian-column/Serbian-column substitutions even where the source didn't tag them with the
literal `(Cr.)`/`(S.)` parenthetical (these lessons present two **entirely parallel** narratives per
lesson — one Croatian, one Serbian — rather than a single text with inline tag pairs; substituted
character names, street names, and lexical choices between the two columns are captured as
region-tagged pairs on that basis, noted per row). Repeated drill exercises (`vježba/vežba` I–VII
sentence lists) are skipped except where a drill sentence introduces vocabulary not already captured
elsewhere. Lesson 27's opening "Gradovi Jugoslavije" reading and Lesson 28's continuation of the same
reading are presented in **Latin script vs. Cyrillic script of the identical text**, not a Cr./S.
dialect pair — that pairing is script transliteration, not regional variation, and is not tagged as
`Attested Region` data. Bible excerpts (Genesis 1:1–8 in Lesson 28; Matthew 8:5–13 in Lesson 29) and
the multi-family children's story in Lesson 29 are paraphrased with a handful of illustrative
headwords only, not bulk-reproduced. **No handwritten marginalia was found on any of the 12 pages
vision-read for this chunk** — all content was clearly typeset print; no previous-owner pencil marks
like those found in Lesson 1's copy of this book were present here.

## Grammar points

### `-ac`/`-anin` demonym-formation pattern

The book's own explicit grammar note (Lesson 29, p. 166, "National and Regional Names"): Cr&S forms
nationality/regional/city-of-residence demonyms in gender pairs, the male form typically taking a
suffix in `-ac` or `-anin` (with a handful of `-lija` exceptions, e.g. `Nišlija`, `Sarajlija`). For
`-ac`-type nouns, the stem-final `a` disappears in all non-nominative cases (`Makedonac`, genitive
`Makedonca`). For `-anin`-type nouns, the plural stem drops the singular's `-in` syllable
(`Ličanin`, plural `Ličani`). This single rule generates the entire nationality/regional/city-demonym
vocabulary set captured in the table above.

### Croatian/Serbian lexical-substitution readings (not just jat alternation)

Unlike earlier lessons in this book, which mostly show a single shared text differing only by the
ije/e jat reflex and the odd `(Cr.)`/`(S.)` tagged word, Lessons 29–30's central reading ("Jedan dan
sa zagrebačkom obitelji"/"Jedan dan sa beogradskom porodicom") is written as **two fully independent
parallel narratives** — different character surnames (`Horvatić`/`Srbić`), different given-name
variants (`Vjekoslav`/`Vojislav`), different street names, and a long list of genuinely distinct
lexical choices (`obitelj`/`porodica`, `kat`/`sprat`, `kolodvor`/`železnička stanica`,
`poduzeće`/`preduzeće`, `plac`/`pijaca`, `rajčica`/`paradajz`, `krumpir`/`krompir`) rather than a
single shared sentence with an inline tag. This is a stronger, more systematic Cr./S. contrast device
than the tag-pair convention seen in earlier lessons, worth flagging methodologically: comparative
BCS extraction should watch for parallel-narrative chapters like this one, not just inline `(Cr.)`/
`(S.)` tags, when harvesting regional-variant vocabulary.

### Pluperfect (pluskvamperfekt) drill

Lesson 28 (p. 160–161, `vježba/vežba IV`) drills the pluperfect tense via fill-in-the-blank sentences
requiring a 3-word verb-phrase blank per sentence (consistent with a `bio/bila` + auxiliary + past
participle compound construction), e.g. "Maturant ___ ___ ___ praznike na moru. (provesti)." The
lesson doesn't re-derive the rule from scratch on this page (it was presumably introduced earlier in
the book, out of this chunk's scope) but the drill itself confirms the construction's three-word
shape.

### Future exact / future perfect (futurom egzaktnim) drill

Lesson 29 (p. 165, `vježba/vežba V`) drills a "futur egzaktni" (future perfect/exact future)
construction via fill-in-the-blank sentences with `ako`/`čim` (if/as soon as) conditional clauses,
e.g. "Ako ___ ___ pažljivo, znat ćete/znaćete. (slušati)" — the construction is used specifically in
subordinate ako/čim clauses referring to a completed future action, paired with a main-clause simple
future.

### Indirect/reported speech (neupravni govor) drill

Lesson 30 (p. 173, `vježba/vežba V`) drills converting direct statements into indirect/reported
speech via a `da`-clause, modeled as: "Putujemo večeras." → "Rekli su da putuju večeras." (tense/
person shift from first-person present to third-person subordinate clause, "they said that... [they]
travel.").

### Infinitive-derived aorist/future forms in Biblical Serbian (Matthew excerpt)

The book's own footnote (Lesson 29, p. 168) glosses two forms from the Matthew 8 excerpt it quotes:
`уђе` ("he/she/it entered") derives from the infinitive `ући`, and `нађох` ("I found") derives from
the infinitive `наћи`; `сеће` (from `сести` + `ће`) is explained as the "normal fusion of сести and
ће," illustrating how the future-tense clitic `će` phonologically fuses onto a preceding verb stem
ending in a consonant cluster.

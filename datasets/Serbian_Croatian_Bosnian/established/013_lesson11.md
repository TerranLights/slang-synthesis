## Grammar points

### Passive participles

Passive participles are adjective-like forms made from verbs, describing completed actions. They are
formed by adding one of three participle markers — `-n`, `-t`, or `-en` — to either the infinitive
stem (infinitive minus `-ti`) or the present stem (3rd pl. present minus its final vowel), then
regular adjectival endings. `-n` attaches to the infinitive stem and covers most `-ati` verbs (types
1, 4, 5, 8, 10; e.g. *pregledati → pregledan* "checked"). `-t` attaches to the infinitive stem and is
exclusive to types 7 and 11, frequent in type 6 (e.g. *zaùzeti → zàuzet* "taken"). `-en` is the most
complex: it attaches directly to the present stem for types 13, 14, 16 (e.g. *pò̀jesti → pòjeden*
"eaten"), but for types 2, 3, 15 the stem-final consonant must first be softened (e.g. *pòstaviti →
pòstavljen* "placed", *videti/vidjeti → viđen* "seen"). A few type-6 and type-1 verbs vary between two
markers (*pòpiti → popit/popìjen* "drunk up"; *pòslati → poslat/poslan* "sent"). (p.159–160)

### Comparison of adjectives (suffix -ij-)

BCS marks the comparative degree with a suffix on the adjective stem and the superlative by prefixing
`naj-` to the comparative. Comparatives/superlatives behave as regular adjectives but occur only in
the long ("definite") form. Adverbs are built from the neuter singular comparative/superlative
adjective form. Most adjectives take `-ij-` with a short rising accent on the syllable before it
(*vàžan → vàžniji → najvàžniji* "important, more, most"). Four common adjectives instead use a wholly
different comparative word: *dobar/bolji* "good/better", *loš/gori* "bad/worse", *vèlik/veći*
"big/bigger", *mali, malen/manji* "small/smaller" — plus *vìsok/viši* "tall/taller", covered under a
separate consonant-softening pattern (see A2 grammar, below). (p.160)

### Comparison of adjectives by consonant softening

A second, productive comparative pattern replaces the adjective's stem-final ("base") consonant with
its "softened" phonological counterpart rather than adding a suffix (e.g. k→č, g→ž, h→š, t→ć, d→đ,
p→plj, b→blj, v→vlj, m→mlj — the same softening set used elsewhere in the grammar for passive
participles, verbal nouns, and the feminine instrumental singular). If the stem ends in `-ak`/`-ok`,
that syllable is dropped before softening (e.g. *kràtak → krat- → kraći* "shorter"). The superlative
still adds `naj-` to the comparative. Three adjectives instead add the suffix `-š` (*lep/lepši* "nice",
*mek/mekši* "soft", *lak/lakši* "easy"). (p.162–163)

### Comparison of adverbs

Comparative/superlative adverbs are identical in form to the neuter singular comparative/superlative
adjective, with two exceptions where the adjective and adverb diverge in meaning despite sharing a
form: *više* "taller" (adj.) vs. "more" (adv.), *manje* "smaller" (adj.) vs. "less/fewer" (adv.).
(p.163)

### Comparison "than"

"Than" is expressed either by `od` + genitive (*òvaj grad je veći od ònoga* "this city is bigger than
that one") or by `nego` (obligatory when a verb follows `nego`); what follows `nego` must
grammatically match what precedes it. `Manje` combines with an adjective/adverb like English "less"
(*manje je ùgodno* "it's less comfortable"), but `više` ("more") cannot normally combine directly with
an adjective/adverb — the comparative form must be used instead. (p.163)

### "One another" (jèdan drugi)

Reciprocity ("one another"/"each other") is expressed with the phrase *jèdan drugi*: the first
component is usually nominative, the second takes whatever case the sentence requires (e.g. *sličan
jèdan drugome* "similar to one another", with the second component in the dative). (p.163)

### The verb trebati ("need")

The person who needs something stands in the dative case, and the thing needed is the grammatical
subject: *trebaju mi òlovke* "I need pencils." Croatian and Bosnian additionally allow a
subject-as-needer construction paralleling English "need" (*ja trebam nove òlovke*). A later grammar
box (p.169) extends this to verbal complements: the more common construction keeps `treba` invariant
with `da` + present tense (*treba da ùčiš* "you need to study"); Croatian/Bosnian additionally allow
`trebaš` (conjugated) + infinitive or `da`-clause (*ti trebaš ùčiti* / *ti trebaš da ùčiš*). For a
subjectless "it's necessary" meaning, only invariant 3rd sg. `treba` + infinitive (or equivalent) is
used (*treba čekati* "it's necessary to wait"). (p.163, p.169)

### Names of professionals

Suffixes `-ik`, `-ač`, `-ar` (among less frequent others) derive profession nouns, referring either to
the profession generally or to a specific male practitioner; some professions have a distinct feminine
form, others share the general form across genders. B, C, and S sometimes use different lexical items
for the same profession altogether (not just gender-suffix variants) — the accompanying STRUKE table
(p.165) is organized by Croatian and Serbian preference columns, with the Bosnian preference marked
`[B]` when it diverges (unmarked entries mean Bosnian uses both terms), and an asterisk marking ekavian
forms. (p.164)

### The instrumental case, continued

The instrumental case marks the second object of verbs like *smàtrati* "consider" and *čìniti* "make
into" (*mi ga smatramo pametnim* "we consider him intelligent"; *to će ga čìniti vojnìkom* "that will
make him into a soldier"). *Baviti se* + instrumental means "be occupied with" and is the standard way
to name an occupation or habitual activity (*čìme se bavite?* "what do you do?"; *òna se bavi
crtanjem* "she [regularly] draws"). (p.167)

### Verbs of body position

BCS distinguishes two verb sets for "sit," "stand," and "lie": stasis verbs (being IN the position,
present tense in `-im`) versus motion verbs (going INTO the position, present tense in `-nem`). The
stasis verb "sit" additionally shows a type-class split by dialect: type 3 in ekavian (*sedeti,
sedim*) but type 2 in ijekavian (*sjediti, sjedim*). Full paradigm given: sit — stasis *sèdeti/sjèditi*,
motion *sesti/sjesti*; stand — stasis *stàjati*, motion *stati*; lie — stasis *lèžati*, motion *leći*.
(p.169–170)

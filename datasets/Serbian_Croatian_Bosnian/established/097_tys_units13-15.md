# Serbian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Teach Yourself Serbian, Units 13–15

**Source:** David Norris & Vladislava Ribnikar, *Teach Yourself Serbian* (Hodder Headline, 2003).
Unit 13 "Daily routine" (printed pp. 174–194), Unit 14 "Free time" (printed pp. 195–215), Unit 15
"Celebrations" (printed pp. 216–233). Printed page range verified empirically against PDF pages
(scanned as two-printed-pages-per-image spreads; PDF image 97 = printed pp. 174–175 through PDF
image 126 = printed pp. 232–233). This source has no usable OCR text layer and was vision-read in
full from rendered page images.

**Vision-reading guard:** every page in this range was checked for handwritten marginalia (pencil/pen
annotations, underlining, filled-in answers from a previous owner). **None was found** — all content
on every page inspected was consistently typeset/printed, matching the surrounding book design. No
exclusions were needed on this basis.

**Coverage note:** every grammar point and every distinct vocabulary item across all three units is
captured below; repeated drill-exercise sentences and duplicate example phrases that introduce no new
vocabulary or grammar were skipped per the extraction spec. Register-relevant items are flagged in the
Notes column (colloquial `супер`, diminutives, the family-only *slava* tradition, gendered marriage
verbs, ethnonym plural patterns, etc.) since Units 13–15 are the most colloquial/social-register-rich
material seen so far. Vision Reading Confidence is `verified` for terms that were legible in both a
dialogue/example passage and the vocabulary box on the same page (the large majority); `plausible_unverified`
is used for a handful of grammar-paradigm-only forms that appeared once, in a fully legible printed
table, without an independent second appearance on the page.

## Grammar points

### Adverbs and impersonal expressions (Unit 13, p.176-177)

Adverbs are usually derived from the neut. singular adjective form (`brz` → `brzo` "quick/quickly");
some, like `vrlo`/`veoma` "very" and `odmah` "immediately", are not derived this way and must be
learned separately. Adverbs from adjectives ending in `-ski` take the masc. adjective form. Adverbs
combine with the 3rd person singular of `biti` ("to be") to form impersonal expressions equivalent to
English "it is...": `Tiho je u kuhinji` ("It is quiet in the kitchen"), `Bilo je tiho u kuhinji` ("It
was quiet in the kitchen"). The person affected is expressed in the dat. case: `Kaže da mu nije
teško` ("He says it is not difficult for him").

### More about verbal aspects (p.178)

Present-tense verbs describing current/general/habitual action (e.g. daily routine) are typically
imperfective; verbs describing single completed past actions are typically perfective. Verbs
following `počinjati; početi` ("to begin") are automatically imperfective. Some verbs have only one
form for both aspects: `ići`, meal verbs (`doručkovati`, `ručati`, `večerati`), verbs borrowed from
foreign languages (`telefonirati`, `rezervisati`), and verbs of the senses (`videti`, `čuti`).

### Nema... "there is not" (p.178)

`Nema` + genitive expresses "there is not/are not" and is also used for a person's absence: `Ponekad
je nema kod kuće` ("Sometimes she is not at home").

### Word order and enclitics (p.180)

Serbian word order is fairly free — noun case endings mark subject vs. object regardless of position.
However, enclitics (short forms of `biti`/`hteti`, the object pronouns `me/mene`, `te/tebe`, and the
reflexive `se`) have a strict position: never as the first word of a sentence/clause, and never
directly after a pause/comma. They occupy the position after the sentence's first introductory word
or phrase (with `i` and `a` not counting toward this). Within the enclitic cluster the fixed order is:
(1) verbal enclitic (except 3rd sg. `je`), (2) pronoun enclitics in dat./gen./acc. order, (3) the
reflexive `se` or the 3rd sg. `je`.

### More about reflexive verbs (p.181)

Some verbs can be used with or without `se` depending on whether they take a direct object: `Budilnik
zvoni... i budi me` ("The alarm rings... and wakes me" — object present, no `se`) vs. `Juče sam se
probudila` ("Yesterday I woke [myself] up" — no object, reflexive). Some verbs shift meaning entirely
when reflexive, e.g. `nalaziti; naći` "to find" → `nalaziti se; naći se` "to be situated" / "to meet
(by arrangement)".

### To be `budem, budeš, bude...` and the Future exact tense (p.185)

A second present-tense paradigm of "to be" (`budem, budeš, bude, budemo, budete, budu`) is not an
enclitic and is used for the alternative-infinitive construction (`moram da budem` = "I have to be")
and, combined with the l-participle, to form the "future exact" tense used after `ako`/`kad`/`dok`/
`čim` ("if"/"when"/"while"/"as soon as") when the following clause is also future: `Ako bude padala
kiša, nećemo moći da izidjemo` ("If it rains, we shall not be able to go out"). English uses a present
tense after "if" here but Serbian requires the future-exact. When this construction is built directly
on the verb "to be" itself, the verbal adjective is omitted (`Kad budem kod njih, daću...`). Using the
ordinary present-tense short form of `biti` instead of `budem` in this position changes the meaning to
a general "whenever" rather than a specific future reference.

### Noun agreement / linking by shared case (p.186)

Two nouns can be linked simply by sharing the same case, without an explicit connecting word:
`Sutra idem kod Dejana, našeg sina` ("Tomorrow I'm going to Dejan's, our son's" — both in gen.).

### Noun subgroups: `deca`, and irregular plurals `braća`/`gospoda` (p.186-187)

`Deca` ("children") is grammatically fem. singular (declines like a regular fem. `-a` noun) despite
its plural meaning; when it is the subject, the verb is plural and the past-tense verbal adjective is
fem. singular: `Deca su bila sa mnom`. `Brat/braća` and `gospodin/gospoda` behave the same way.

### Foreign borrowings ending in `-o` (p.187)

Nouns borrowed from foreign languages ending in `-o` (`auto`, `video`, `radio`) are masculine despite
the vowel ending, and take masc. adjective agreement (`naš auto`).

### Oneself: `sebe/sebi/sobom` (p.187)

Serbian has a single reflexive pronoun covering English "myself/yourself/oneself/etc.", declined
acc. `sebe`, gen. `sebe`, dat. `sebi`, ins. `sobom`, loc. `sebi`. Its precise translated meaning
depends on context.

### More about the future exact; seasons (p.188-189)

Further future-exact example: `Svi znamo da će biti još dosta lepih dana dok ne bude došla prava
zima` (perfective present is an acceptable alternative to future exact after `dok ne`). `Dok` alone
means "while"; negated as `dok ne` it means "until". Seasons: `zima`/`zimi`, `proleće`/`u proleće`,
`leto`/`leti`, `jesen`/`u jesen`. "Season" itself is `godišnje doba` (lit. "time of the year"), a
neuter singular noun.

### Masc. nom. adjective ending in `-i`, and vocative (p.189-190)

Most masc. nom. singular adjectives end in a consonant (`dobar`, `star`, `hladan`), but some take an
`-i` ending which adds the sense of "the" and is often paired with `taj`. Several adjectives (`prvi`
and other ordinals, `neki`) exist only in this `-i` form; a few (`moj`, `naš`, and other possessives,
`jedan`) never add it. The vocative ending equals the nom. `-i` form for masc. singular; full
singular/plural paradigm given: masc./fem./neut. nom. `star(i)/stara/staro`, voc. `stari/stara/staro`
(plural: nom. `stari/stare/stara`, voc. `stari/stare/stara`). The consonant-final (no `-i`) form is
always used predicatively with "to be": `Tvoj prijatelj je ljubazan`.

### Expressing approximate numbers (p.190)

`Otprilike` or `oko` placed before a number expresses "approximately". For round numbers, the suffix
`-ak` can replace the whole-number ending instead: `desetak` ("about ten, a dozen"), `dvadesetak`
("twenty or so").

### Phrases and expressions (p.190-191)

`U goste` (loc./acc. plural of `gost`) = to visit as a guest. `Više volimo da...` + comparative
`više` = "to prefer". `Po mom/tvom mišljenju` = "in my/your opinion" (`po` + dat. possessive +
`mišljenje`). `Po` + acc. = "to come for/fetch": `Idem po hleb` ("I am going for bread"). Weather
verbs `padati` ("to fall") and `duvati` ("to blow"): `pada kiša/sneg`, `duva vetar`. `Gužva` covers
both "traffic jam" and "crowd". Diminutives: replace fem. `-a` with `-ica`, or add masc. `-ić`
(`kafana`→`kafanica`, `grad`→`gradić`); diminutives often carry a tone of emotional closeness. `Sedeti
za stolom` ("to sit/be sitting at a table", ins.) vs. `sesti za sto` ("to sit down to a table", acc.).

### Comparative form of adjectives (Unit 14, p.198-199)

Five patterns, taking soft case endings: (1) default `+ији`; (2) adjectives ending in `д, г, х, к, с,
т, ст, з, н, л` undergo consonant softening before adding `-ји`: `д→ђ, г→ж, х→ш, к→ч, с→ш, т→ћ,
ст→шт, з→ж, н→њ, л→љ` (e.g. `млад→млађи`, `дуг→дужи`, `тих→тиши`, `јак→јачи`, `густ→гушћи`); (3)
adjectives ending in `-ак, -ек, -ок` drop the last two letters before applying pattern 2 (`сладак→
слађи`, `далек→даљи`, `висок→виши`); (4) adjectives ending in `-б, -п, -м, -в` add `-љи` (`скуп→
скупљи`, `сув→сувљи`); (5) several common adjectives have irregular comparatives ending in `-ши`:
`добар→бољи`, `лош→гори` (also `лошији`), `мали→мањи`, `велик→већи`, `лак→лакши`, `леп→лепши`,
`мек→мекши`. The same `-ји` set (plus a couple more) also forms comparatives of `-ски` adjectives
(`пријатељски→више пријатељски`).

### Saying "more"/"less"; "than"; superlative (p.199-200)

`Више`/`мање` ("more"/"less") take a following noun in the gen. case, like `много`/`мало`. "Than" is
expressed either with `него` (same case/construction before and after) or with `од` + gen. (not always
substitutable for `него`). Superlative: prefix `нај-` on the comparative (`најстарији`,
`најскупљи`...); "most"/"least" are `највише`/`најмање`. Serbian avoids double consonants except in
superlatives of adjectives beginning with `ј` (`јак→најјачи`, `јефтин→најјефтинији`).

### Comparative of adverbs (p.200)

Formed identically to the neut. singular adjective comparative: `Она плива добро, али њена сестра
плива боље`.

### Indefinite pronouns (p.200-201)

`Ко`, `шта`, `када`, `где`, `како`, `куда` and their `не-`/`ни-`/`и-` prefixed variants form a
systematic paradigm (who/somebody/nobody/anybody; what/something/nothing/anything; etc. — full table
above). `Ко`-forms take masc. agreement, `шта`-forms take neut. agreement. Alternative "anywhere/
anything" forms use `било` or `год` (`било ко`, `кад год`). Negative constructions require every part
of the phrase to be negative, unlike English (`Нигде ништа није отворено` = "Nothing is open
anywhere"). `Када`, `куда`, `сада` and the preposition `са` may drop the final `а`, especially before
a following vowel.

### Case endings for `ко` and `шта` (p.203)

Both decline with adjective-like endings: `ко` — nom. `ко`, acc./gen. `кога`, dat./loc. `коме`, ins.
`ким(е)`; `шта` — nom./acc. `шта`, gen. `чега`, dat./loc. `чему`, ins. `чиме`. With a preposition,
`нико`/`ништа` split around the preposition: `ни о чему другом`, `ни са ким`.

### Once, twice, etc. (p.203)

`Пут/а` = "time(s)": `два пута`, `три пута`, `пет пута`; "once" is the single word `једанпут`.

### "To like" (p.201)

`Допадати ми се` and `свиђати ми се` both mean "to like/please" and share a construction: the verb is
3rd person, the liker is in the dat. case, and the thing liked is the grammatical subject placed at
the end: `Весни се допада та књига` ("Vesna likes that book").

### Dative case to show possession (p.201)

In addition to possessive adjectives (`мој`, `твој`) or the genitive (`ауто мог оца`), Serbian can use
the dat. of the personal pronoun to show possession, especially for people/things one is emotionally
close to: `Где ти је базен?` ("Where is your swimming pool?").

### What kind of? `какав` (p.207)

`Какав` ("what kind of") is an adjective, used in questions (`Какав ауто имаш?`) and as an exclamation
(`Какав човек!` = "What a man!").

### Phrases and expressions (p.207)

`Пре` + gen. plural of `дан` = "ago" (`пре неколико година`). The intensifying particle `баш` means
"really/quite/just" depending on context. `Још једном` = "once more"; `још један` = "one more" (of a
countable item). `Ни` = "neither", doubled for "neither...nor" (paralleling `или...или` for "either...
or" and `и...и` for "both...and"); because Serbian allows double negatives, `ни` phrases often
translate into English "either".

### "I feel like..." (p.209)

Formed by making the verb reflexive and putting it in the 3rd person, with the person who feels like
it in the dat.: `Иде ми се у биоскоп` ("I feel like going to the cinema"), `Пије му се чај` ("He feels
like tea").

### Let me, let him, etc. (p.209)

"Let me/us" = `да` + present tense (`да видим` = "let me see"). "Let him/her/them" = `нека` + present
tense (`Нека гледа телевизију` = "Let him watch TV").

### Conditional (p.210)

The l-participle (`волео`, `дошао`) combines with the short-form auxiliary `бих, би, би, бисмо, бисте,
би` to form the conditional ("would"). These clitics follow the same placement rules as other verbal
short forms (never sentence-initial). The conditional is common in formal/polite speech, e.g. a
waiter's `Шта бисте желели, господине?` ("What would you like, sir?").

### `Ако` with the conditional (p.210-211)

`Ако` + future exact/present states a simple intention (`Ако буду дошли пре девет, моћи ћемо да
идемо...`). `Ако` + conditional softens this into a less certain outcome ("should they come..."/"were
they to come..."). `Ако` can be replaced by `кад` in this construction.

### More about numbers: mixed-group and men-only collectives (Unit 15, p.218-219)

Two further numeral sets exist beyond ordinary cardinals. **Mixed groups (men+women, or children):**
`двоје, троје, четворо, петоро, шестoро, седморо` (2-7); higher numbers add `-оро`. Used as part of a
subject, the verb is singular and past-tense agreement is neuter; may combine with a noun/pronoun in
gen.: `Видео сам двоје деце`. **Groups of men only:** `двојица, тројица, четворица, петорица,
шесторица, седморица` (2-7); higher numbers add `-орица`. Grammatically these are fem. `-a`-declension
nouns, but as subjects the verb is plural with fem. past-tense agreement: `Та двојица су студирала
заједно`. **Both:** `оба` (masc./neut.) / `обе` (fem.), each + gen. singular; also `обојица`/`обоје`.

### Numeral agreement rules (p.220)

Subjects with `један` are always singular, including in compound numbers (`Двадесет један Енглез је
био...`). Subjects with `два/две, три, четири` are always plural, with past-tense agreement `-а`
(masc./neut.) or `-е` (fem.), including in compounds. Subjects with `пет` and above are always
singular, with neuter past-tense agreement regardless of gender, including in compounds. Adjectives in
expressions with `један` agree normally in gender/case; adjectives with numbers + gen. plural noun
take gen. plural; adjectives with numbers + gen. singular noun take `-а` (masc./neut.) or `-е` (fem.).

### More about imperatives (p.221)

Three imperatives deviate slightly from the regular pattern: `помози/помозите` (from `помоћи`),
`реци/реците` (from `рећи`), `седи/седите` (from `сести`). The negative imperative is normally formed
with `немој(те)`, but `не` + imperative is also possible with imperfective verbs only, to express a
firm prohibition (e.g. parent to child): `Не прелази улицу!` ("Don't cross the street!").

### Reported speech (p.222-223)

Reported/indirect speech (`Рекао сам да...`, "he told me that...") also extends to verbs of hoping/
thinking/doubting/supposing (`надати се`, `сумњати`, etc.). Critically, **the tense used in the
reported clause is the tense the original speaker would have used**, not necessarily the tense English
grammar would predict — e.g. reporting a future-tense original statement keeps the future tense:
`Рекао сам Силвији да ћемо у Србији провести десетак дана`. Reporting an imperative/command uses `да`
+ present tense.

### Indirect questions (p.223-224)

Formed with `да ли` (or `ли` placed directly after the verb) mirroring "if" in English, again
preserving the original tense: `Питаћу је да ли ће доћи` / `Питаћу је хоће ли доћи`. Question words can
also be embedded directly: `Питао сам је ко ће ићи по хлеб`.

### Masculine nouns ending in `-in` (p.224)

Nouns like `Србин`, `грађанин`, `Београђанин` drop the final syllable (`-ин`) before adding plural case
endings: `Срби`, `грађани`, `Београђани`.

### Counterfactual "if you had been here..." (p.224)

Introduced with `Да...`, describing something that did not/could not happen; the second clause is
always conditional: `Да си био овде, био би то за тебе прави доживљај`. The first clause may be past
or present tense: `Да сам ја на твом месту, ја бих му рекао да оде` ("If I were in your place...").

### Saying "which": `који` (p.226-227)

`Који` ("which/who/that") declines like `мој` and has both long and short forms (`којег/кога`,
`којем/коме`); short forms are used only when referring to people. It links a relative clause back to
an antecedent, agreeing in gender/number with the antecedent and taking whatever case its own role in
the clause requires: `То је човек који има стан поред нас` (nom., subject role) vs. `То је човек кога
сам јуче видео` (acc., object role). After `то`/`све`, "which/that" is `што`: `...за све што је
потребно`.

### Phrases and expressions: perception, married-status verbs, purpose (p.230-231)

`Како` + present tense expresses seeing/hearing someone doing something: `Видим их поред прозора како
причају` ("I see them talking by the window"). `Изгледати` + "like" asks/states appearance:
`Како изгледа твоја девојка?`. A reflexive particle can mark reciprocal "each other" (`знамо се већ
годинама`); the same instrumental-plural time pattern extends to `сатима`, `месецима`, `вековима`.
`Да` = "that" generally; `што` = "that" specifically after emotionally evaluative expressions (`Драго
ми је што...`, `Чудно је што...`), with a sense close to "because". Married-status vocabulary is
gender-split: `оженити се` (of a man), `удати се`/`удавати се` (of a woman), `венчати се` (of a
couple jointly) — `он се жени`, `она се удаје`, `они ће се венчати`. `У праву си`/`имаш право` = "you
are right"; negated `нисиу праву` = "you are wrong". "In order to" can use the plain alternative
infinitive (`Ишли смо да му кажемо...`) or, for more emphasis on purpose, `да` + conditional (`Да би
добро научио језик...`).

## Cultural/historical notes (attested-era/region content)

- **Ivo Andrić (1892–1975) and Danilo Kiš (1935–89)** are named by the source as two of Serbia's most
  celebrated 20th-century writers; Andrić won the Nobel Prize for Literature in 1961, best known for
  *Na Drini ćuprija* (*The Bridge over the Drina*, 1945), set in Višegrad on the Drina river, on the
  border between Serbia and Bosnia (p.206).
- **Slava** (family patron saint's day) is explicitly flagged by the source as a uniquely Serbian
  family celebration, traditionally marking the household's historical conversion to Christianity,
  observed according to the saint's day in the Orthodox Church calendar; the whole family/friends may
  visit at any time during the day, sometimes preceded by a priest's house blessing. Popular slava
  dates named: **Sveti Nikola** (19 December), **Sveti Jovan** (20 January). Orthodox **Božić**
  (Christmas) falls on **7 January**, not aligned with other churches' calendars (p.221-222).
- **Политика** is named as Serbia's main daily newspaper (Belgrade); **Балкан** and **Јадран** are
  named as two central Belgrade cinemas; a city-centre **McDonald's** (opposite the Hotel Moskva) is
  used as a real, dated (2003) reference point in a dialogue (p.209).

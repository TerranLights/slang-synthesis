# Serbian, Croatian, Bosnian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Units 10-12 ("Obiteljski odnosi" / "Putni planovi" / "Na pošti")

**Source:** Celia Hawkesworth, *Colloquial Croatian and Serbian: The Complete Course for Beginners*
(Routledge, 1998). Units 10-12, printed pages 108-139 (PDF pages 116-147 of
`03.Colloquial Serbian and Croatian.pdf`). Unit 10 = "Obiteljski odnosi" (Family relationships,
printed pp. 108-120); Unit 11 = "Putni planovi" (Travel plans, printed pp. 121-129); Unit 12 =
"Na pošti" (At the post office, printed pp. 130-139). Boundary verified visually: Unit 10 title page
is PDF page 116, Unit 13 ("U kazalištu") title page is PDF page 148 — confirming Units 10-12 end at
PDF page 147.

**Vision-reading note:** this source has no usable text layer and was read entirely by vision
(scanned book, `pdftoppm -r 150`). Every page in the range was checked for handwritten marginalia
(pencil/pen annotations, underlines, filled-in exercise answers) distinct from the book's own
typeset print. **No handwritten marginalia was found anywhere in this page range** — all pages were
clean, consistently-typeset printed source material. All rows below are drawn from consistent,
clearly legible print; Vision Reading Confidence is `verified` where a term recurs elsewhere on the
page (e.g. in both dialogue and vocabulary list) and `plausible_unverified` otherwise, as noted
per-row.

**Coverage note:** every distinct vocabulary item from the dialogues, explicit vocabulary boxes, and
reading passages ("Priča našeg vremena" 10-12) in this range is captured. Drill/translation exercises
(Exercise 1-4 in each unit) were skipped per the coverage rule — they recombine vocabulary already
captured rather than introducing new items, except where an exercise's own footnote glossed a new
word not otherwise listed (a few such footnoted glosses are included below, flagged in Notes).
Register/informality and explicit regional-variant annotations — this book's own stated priority
axis — are called out explicitly in Grammar points and in vocabulary Notes.

## Grammar points

### Declension of personal pronouns (ja, ti, on/ona/ono, mi, vi, oni/one/ona)

Pages 110-116. Personal pronouns are omitted in the nominative except for emphasis (compare "Zašto
si došao?" vs. emphatic "Ja sam, majko"). All oblique cases (genitive/accusative and dative) have a
neutral, unstressable short/enclitic form and an emphatic long form; the long form is mandatory after
a preposition or for contrastive emphasis. Full paradigms for **ja** (me/mene; mi/meni; mnom/mnome),
**ti/sebe** (te/tebe; ti/tebi; sobom), **on/ono** (ga/njega; mu/njemu; njim/njime), **ona** (je (or
alternative accusative **ju** when it would otherwise sit beside the third-person singular short form
of **biti**)/nje; joj/njoj; njom/njome), and plurals **mi/vi** (nas/vas; nam/vam; nama/vama) and
**oni/one/ona** (ih/njih; im/njim(a); njima) are given (pp. 111-115). The reflexive pronoun **se**
has no nominative, refers only back to the subject, uses the genitive/accusative short form **se**
in reflexive verbs, and the long forms **sebe/sebi/sobom** for emphasis or after a preposition.

### Word order rule for enclitic clusters ("Word order with pronouns 3")

Page 116. Pronoun enclitics obey a strict order: dative precedes accusative/genitive
("dajem *ti ga*"); where a short-form verbal auxiliary (biti, and others) co-occurs, the verbal
enclitic precedes the pronoun clitics ("dao *sam ti ga*"); the reflexive **se** follows the pronoun
clitics ("rado *smo je se* sjećali"); the single exception is the third-person singular of **biti**
(**je**), which is placed after all other enclitics ("da li ih se *je* sjetio?"). The book gives a
memorable formula: **li** (interrogative particle) — **ve** (verbal enclitic) — **D, A/G** (pronoun
clitics) — **se** — **je**.

### Dative of pronouns to express possession

Page 119. The dative of a personal pronoun is commonly used to convey possession, especially of
family relationships: "On mi je brat" = "He is my brother" (lit. "He is brother to me"). The book
notes this pattern recurs five times in the Unit 10 dialogue text — a productive colloquial
possession construction worth flagging as a register-relevant idiom pattern for kinship terms.

### The future tense (htjeti + infinitive)

Pages 122-124. The future is a compound tense formed from the infinitive plus a new auxiliary,
**htjeti** ("to want"), which — like **biti** — has both a short/enclitic form (ću, ćeš, će, ćemo,
ćete, će) and a long form (hoću, hoćeš, hoće, hoćemo, hoćete, hoće), plus dedicated interrogative
(hoću li/da li ću, etc.) and negative (neću, nećeš, neće, nećemo, nećete, neće) paradigms. The long
form is obligatory when answering a yes/no question with the verb alone (Croatian/Serbian answers
questions with the verb rather than a bare "da/ne"): "Hoćeš li doći? — Hoću/Neću."

**Explicit Croatian/Serbian orthographic variant** (page 124): the future auxiliary may precede or
follow the infinitive depending on whether a preceding "prop" word (pronoun, etc.) is present — "Ja
ću doći" but "Doći ću." With infinitives ending in **-ti**, the final **-i** is dropped when the
auxiliary follows: "Bit ću," "Spavat ćeš," "Vidjet ćemo." **The book explicitly notes: "In the
Serbian variant, the infinitive and auxiliary are run together: biću, spavaćeš, videćemo"** — i.e.
Serbian standard orthography fuses the future tense into a single word where Croatian standard
orthography keeps infinitive-stem and auxiliary as separate words. This is a genuine, source-flagged
B/C/S orthographic-standard divergence and one of the highest-value dialectal annotations in this
chunk.

### Cardinal numbers and their government of case

Pages 132-134. **jedan** ("one") is declined as an ordinary adjective (jedan čovjek/jedna žena/jedno
selo) and has plural forms used for pluralia tantum or paired items (jedne cipele = "one pair of
shoes," jedna vrata = "one door") — it can function as something like an indefinite article. Numbers
2, 3, 4 (and any compound ending in 2, 3, or 4, e.g. 22, 23, 24, 92, 104, 553, 2084) govern the
**genitive singular** of the following noun and take matching adjective endings (dva umorna putnika,
tri lijepe djevojke, četiri mala sela); the number 2 has a masculine (dva) and feminine (dvije) form,
plus a neuter/mixed-gender collective form (dvoje) used for mixed-gender pairs or "dvoje djece."
Numbers 5 and above govern the **genitive plural** (pet mladića, deset maraka, dvanaest lijepih
konja) and the verb is normally singular when the number is 5 or higher (though plural with 2-4). The
full cardinal list 0-20, tens to 90, and named hundreds/thousands/million is given, including the
explicitly Croatian-standard word **tisuća** for "thousand" (vs. **hiljada**, the Serbian-standard
term, not itself mentioned in this excerpt but implied by the B/C/S comparative framing of the
series).

### Verb agreement with numbers

Page 135. With numbers 2-4, the present-tense verb is plural and the past participle takes an
adjective-like ending matching the noun's gender/number (Došla su tri visoka mladića). With numbers
5 and above, the verb is normally singular (Dvadeset šest umornih putnika čeka autobus; Deset
izvrsnih plivačica je skočilo u more) even though the notional subject is plural — a case of
grammatical (singular, neuter-like) agreement overriding notional plurality.

# Serbian/Croatian/Bosnian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Dvadeseta lekcija / Lesson Twenty (final lesson)

**Source:** Ronelle Alexander & Ellen Elias-Bursać, *Bosnian, Croatian, Serbian, a Textbook: With
Exercises and Basic Grammar* (2nd ed., 2010), "Dvadeseta lekcija • Lesson Twenty," pp. 301–313
(PDF pages 322–335). The lesson consists of an eight-part reading, "U zagrljaju rijeke" ("In the
River's Embrace") by Miro Gavran, each part preceded by its own `RJEČNIK [J] REČNIK [E]`
vocabulary box, followed by comprehension questions (Vježbe/Vežbe) and a homework assignment
(Domaća zadaća/Domaći zadatak).

**Coverage note:** every distinct vocabulary item from all eight `RJEČNIK`/`REČNIK` boxes (sections
I–VIII) is captured below, plus every `[B]`/`[C]`/`[S]` (and combined `[B,C]`/`[B,S]`/`[B,C,S]`)
dialect tag the source itself marks. The comprehension-question sections (BI–BVIII) and the
homework prompts (C1/C2) were **skipped as repeated drill material** per this dispatch's
instructions and the extraction spec's coverage rule — they recycle already-known question words
(šta/što, ko/tko, gdje/gde/kamo) rather than introducing new vocabulary, though they are noted
under "Grammar points" below since their B/C/S-tagging pattern is itself informative. The
first page of the book's back-matter appendix (an abbreviation key for the grammar tables, printed
immediately after this lesson's homework page, PDF p. 335) was excluded as out of scope — it is
front matter for the appendices, not lesson content. **This source has a genuine PDF text layer**
(`pdftotext` produced clean text for the full page range) — no vision-reading was needed, and no
handwritten marginalia was present or excluded.

**A note on the `[J]`/`[E]` header convention.** Every vocabulary box in this lesson is headed
`RJEČNIK [J] REČNIK [E]` — the box itself is printed as two parallel word lists distinguished by
the ijekavian/ekavian (jat-reflex) alternation, not just a left/right page-layout split. Where a
term has both an ijekavian and an ekavian form (e.g. *zavjesa*/*zavesa* "curtain"), both are listed
as separate rows below with `Attested Region` marked `ijekavian [J]` or `ekavian [E]` respectively,
since the source itself explicitly marks this distinction via the box header. This is separate from
— and much more frequent than — the explicit `[B]`/`[C]`/`[S]` lexical-choice tags, which mark a
genuinely different word choice between the three standards rather than a pronunciation/spelling
alternation of the same word.

## Grammar points

*This is the book's final, capstone lesson: it introduces no new grammatical rules of its own. It
consists entirely of a graded reading (applying and recombining material from Lessons 1–19),
vocabulary boxes, comprehension questions, and a homework assignment that explicitly directs
students back over already-taught categories. The genuinely new content in this chunk is
lexical/dialectal (captured in the Vocabulary tables above), not grammatical. The points below
describe the lesson's grammar-relevant *structure*, paraphrased, not new rules.*

### Ijekavian/ekavian (jat-reflex) dual vocabulary presentation

Every one of the lesson's eight vocabulary boxes is headed "RJEČNIK [J] REČNIK [E]" and gives, for
many lexical items, two spellings differing only in the reflex of the historical yat vowel:
ijekavian (`je`/`ije`, marked `[J]`) vs. ekavian (`e`, marked `[E]`) — e.g. *zavjesa*/*zavesa*
"curtain," *riječni*/*rečni* "river (adj.)," *nasljednik*/*naslednik* "heir." Ijekavian is the
pronunciation of standard Croatian and Bosnian (and of Serbian as spoken outside Serbia proper,
e.g. in Bosnia and Montenegro); ekavian is the pronunciation of standard Serbian as used in Serbia.
This affects spelling and pronunciation consistently across nouns, adjectives, and verbs, not just
an isolated word list (pp. 301–309, throughout).

### Explicit [B]/[C]/[S] lexical-choice tags

Distinct from the ijekavian/ekavian split above, several vocabulary-box entries mark a genuinely
different *word choice* between the three standards for the same meaning, e.g. *tvornica* [B,C]
vs. *fabrika* [B,S] "factory"; *strojarski* [C] vs. *mašinski* [B,S] "mechanical"; *tiskati* [C]
vs. *štampati* [B,S] "to print"; *naklada* [C] vs. *izdanje* [B,C,S] "edition"; *operirati* [B,C]
vs. *operisati* [B,S] "to operate"; *supatnik* [C] vs. *sapatnik* [B,S] "fellow sufferer";
*sličiti (na)* [B,C] vs. *ličiti (na)* [B,S] "to resemble"; *zainteresiranost* [B,C] vs.
*zainteresovanost* [B,S] "interest/concern"; *ma što* [C] vs. *ma šta* [B,S] "no matter what";
*pošutjeti* [C] vs. *poćutati* [B,S], and *zašutjeti* [C] vs. *zaćutati* [B,S], both "to fall
[suddenly] silent." Per the book's own abbreviation key (PDF p. 335, out of this lesson's scope
but explaining the notation used throughout it), a `[B,C,S]` tag on one member of such a pair (e.g.
*izdanje* [B,C,S], *praviti se* [B,C,S]) means all three standards use that word, but a competing
synonym also exists that is used in only one or two of them.

### Comprehension-exercise dialect-tag pattern (skipped as drill content)

The BI–BVIII comprehension questions were not extracted as new vocabulary (already-known material
recycled as drill, per the coverage rule), but their tagging pattern is itself a useful confirmation
of the book's convention: each question is given three times, tagged `[B]`, `[C]`, `[S]` (the
Serbian version also rendered in Cyrillic), consistently alternating *šta*/*što* "what," *ko*/*tko*
"who," and *gdje*/*gde*/*kamo* "where" across the three tags — reinforcing the B = *šta*/*ko*,
C = *što*/*tko*, S = *šta*/*ko* (+ Cyrillic script) pattern established earlier in the book (p. 310–312).

### Homework (C2) grammar-review checklist

The lesson's homework assignment (p. 312) directs students to analyze — not learn anew — six
previously-taught categories against this lesson's own reading text: (a) verbal aspect and tense
usage in sections III–IV; (b) participles, verbal adverbs, and verbal nouns in section II; (c)
subjectless (impersonal) sentences in section V; (d) conditional-mood forms in section IV; (e)
every numeral used in the story, with the case and verb agreement each one governs; (f)
prepositional case-government in section III or VIII. This confirms the lesson's role as a
synthesis/review capstone rather than a source of new grammatical content.

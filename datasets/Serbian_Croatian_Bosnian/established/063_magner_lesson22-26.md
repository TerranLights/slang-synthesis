**Coverage note:** every grammar point and dialectal/regional annotation found in this range is
captured below. Vocabulary is comprehensive-but-not-exhaustive per the coverage rule: the two large
enumerated vocabulary lists in this range — Lesson 23's "Names of Common Foods" (`Imèna òbičnih jéla`)
and Lesson 22's kajkavian-song glossary — are captured in full since they are the book's own
vocabulary-list content, not drill sentences. Body-part/illness vocabulary (Lesson 24), train-station
vocabulary (Lesson 25), and farm/village vocabulary (Lesson 26) are drawn from each lesson's opening
dialogue/reading (its highest-density vocabulary source) rather than from the repeated `vježba`/`vežba`
drill sentences that follow each dialogue, which recycle the same vocabulary in translation/
transformation exercises and introduce very little new lexis — per the coverage rule these are
skipped except where a drill sentence surfaces a genuinely new word or an explicit `(Cr.)`/`(S.)`-style
regional pair not already captured. All explicit Croatian/Serbian regional-variant pairs found — both
the book's standing left-column/right-column convention (Croatian left, Serbian right, per its own
"Variations in Cyrillic Print" footnote seen on an adjacent probed page) and its footnoted `*`/`**`
asterisk-marked lexical splits — are captured as paired rows tagged `Croatia`/`Serbia`.

## Grammar points

### Imperative formation (Lesson 22)

A drill (`vježba/vežba II`, p. 126/127) has the student convert an infinitive in parentheses to the
imperative, e.g. `(Zatvoriti) prozor. (vi)` → `Zatvorite prozor!` ("Close the window!"). The `(vi)`/
`(ti)`/`(mi)`/`(oni)` annotations after each cue specify which person/number form to produce,
covering the full paradigm across the drill's ten items. p. 126/127.

### Conditional mood formation (Lesson 22)

A parallel drill (`vježba/vežba III`, p. 126/127) has the student supply the conditional (`bih`/
`bi`/`bismo` etc. + participle) for a bracketed infinitive, modeled on "Ja ___ mu ___ tu knjigu.
(dati)" → "Ja bih mu dao tu knjigu." ("I would give him that book.") p. 126/127.

### Vocative usage note: nominative address forms in practice (Lesson 23)

The dialogue's opening line addresses a waiter as "Molim vas, konobar..." — nominative `konobar`,
not the grammatically "correct" vocative `konobaru`. The source's own footnote (p. 130/131) makes
this explicit: true vocative forms (`konobaru`, and the Serbian equivalent `kelneru`) exist and would
be grammatically correct, but ordinary usage favors the nominative form even in direct address. This
is a useful real-usage-vs-prescriptive-grammar note, paralleling how colloquial register often
flattens a marked case toward an unmarked one. p. 130/131.

### Adverbial participles: `prilog sadašnji` and `prilog prošli` (Lesson 23)

A fill-in drill (`vježba/vežba II`, p. 134/135) has the student supply the present or past adverbial
participle (`prilog sadašnji`/`prilog prošli` — roughly, "-ing" and "having done X" forms) for a
bracketed infinitive, modeled on "___ sa nama, otišao je. (pozdraviti se)" → "Pozdravivši se sa nama,
otišao je." ("Having said goodbye to us, he left.") Ten items drill this pattern with a range of
verbs (`ugledati`, `šetati se`, `igrati se`, `pročitati`, `putovati`, `platiti`, `čekati`, `vraćati
se`, `ući`, `kupovati`). p. 134/135.

### Comparative and superlative of adverbs (Lesson 23)

A further drill (`vježba/vežba III`, p. 134/135–136) has the student put a bracketed adverb into the
comparative or superlative, e.g. "(Cr.) Jučer je bilo ___ nego prekjučer. (hladno)" → "Jučer je bilo
hladnije nego prekjučer." ("Yesterday was colder than the day before.") Notably the drill's own model
sentence is given twice, once tagged `(Cr.)` with `jučer`/`prekjučer` and once tagged `(S.)` with
`juče`/`prekjuče` — an explicit Cr./S. lexical-form pair for "yesterday" and "the day before
yesterday" surfacing inside drill instructions rather than the vocabulary list itself. p. 134/135–136.

### Negative imperative with `nemojte` + infinitive (Lesson 24)

The Lesson 24 dialogue ends "Nemojte me vrijeđati nuđenjem novaca" ("Don't insult me by offering
money") — `nemojte` (2nd pl./formal negative-imperative auxiliary) + infinitive is the productive
negative-command construction, contrasted implicitly with the positive imperative drilled in Lesson
22. p. 140/141.

### Future tense and negation drills (Lesson 24)

Two further drills convert given sentences to the future tense (`vježba/vežba II`, "On se vraća u
četvrtak." → "On će se vratiti u četvrtak.") and to the negative form (`vježba/vežba I`, p. 140/141),
reinforcing rather than introducing new material — noted for completeness but not treated as new
grammar points since the underlying future-tense and negation rules were already established in
earlier lessons.

### Indirect speech (`neupravni govor`) (Lesson 25)

A drill (`vježba/vežba III`, p. 145) has the student convert direct quotations into indirect/reported
speech, modeled on "Doći ću večeras." → "Rekao je da će doći večeras." ("He said that he would come
this evening.") — the reported-speech pattern keeps the original tense/aspect of the embedded
subordinate clause (Cr&S does not backshift tense in indirect speech the way English does), which is
implicit in the model but worth flagging for a mechanics-analysis pass later, since backshift
avoidance is a genuine typological difference from English indirect speech. p. 145.

### Case-government drill covering nouns and pronouns together (Lesson 26)

A drill (`vježba/vežba III`, p. 148/149) has the student supply the correct case for a bracketed
noun phrase or pronoun across a mixed set of governing contexts (locative after `na`/`kod`, genitive
after a numeral, instrumental after `s`, etc.), e.g. "Mile je radio na ___. (veliko gospodarstvo/
imanje)." This is a review/integration drill rather than new grammar — noted for completeness, not
extracted as a new rule since the individual case-government patterns were introduced in earlier
lessons.

### Kajkavian as a distinct sub-dialect (Lesson 22)

The source explicitly frames "Po lojtrici gor i dol" ("Up and Down the Ladder") as "a lively
kajkavian song from Zagorje, the region north of Zagreb," and states that "special kajkavian forms
appear in the song" — presenting a dedicated bracket-glossed vocabulary list mapping each kajkavian
form to its standard Cr&S equivalent (see the `regional`-tagged rows above, e.g. `bum` = `budem`,
`dol` = `dolje/dole`). This is a rare instance in this book of the standard/dialect axis being
something *other* than the book's usual Croatian-standard/Serbian-standard comparative axis — kajkavian
is a sub-dialect within the Croatian-speaking area itself, distinct from the štokavian basis of both
standard Croatian and standard Serbian. Worth flagging for the mechanics-analysis phase as a second,
orthogonal axis of regional variation beyond the book's primary Cr./S. comparative framework. p. 128.

### Explicit Cr./S. lexical pairs found in this range (summary)

Beyond the many jat-reflex (ije/je vs. e) pairs already well documented from earlier lessons, this
range surfaces several **distinct-root** lexical pairs newly tagged or newly confirmed by explicit
paired-column evidence: `napojnica`/`bakšiš` ("tip"), `gospodarstvo`/`imanje` ("farm"), `sajam`/
`pazar` ("market"), `krumpir`/`krompir` ("potato," a spelling-level pair but phonologically distinct
enough to be worth separating from a pure jat pair), `riža`/`pirinač` ("rice"), `rajčica`/`paradajz`
("tomato"), `naranča`/`pomorandža` ("orange"), and `konobar`/`kelner` ("waiter"). Distinct-root pairs
like these are typologically more significant for later slang-synthesis mechanics work than pure jat
spelling variants, since they reflect genuinely separate lexical choices (often a native-Slavic vs. a
Turkish- or German-derived loanword) rather than a single shared etymon realized differently. See the
Notes column on each row above for the specific page/context each pair was confirmed on.

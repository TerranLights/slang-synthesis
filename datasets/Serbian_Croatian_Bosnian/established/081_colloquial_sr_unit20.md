## Grammar points

### Present gerund (participle / verbal adverb, simultaneous action)

Formed by adding **-ћи** to the 3rd person plural present tense of the *imperfective* verb: говоре
→ говорећи ("speaking"), пишу → пишући ("writing"), имају → имајући ("having"). Strictly
indeclinable like an adverb, but infrequent in speech and even in writing except in certain fixed
uses. The present gerund of **бити** is **будући** (from perfective 3rd pl. **буду**), used very
commonly as a conjunction with **да**: *Будући да смо у Србији...* ("Since we are in Serbia...").
(p. 282)

### Present gerund used as adjective

An increasing set of present gerunds function as declinable adjectives despite the general rule
that gerunds are indeclinable: **будући** ("future", e.g. *будући муж* "future husband"), **идући**
("next/coming", e.g. *идуће године* "next year"), **следећи** ("following", e.g. *следећег дана*
"the following day"), **текући** ("current", e.g. *текући рачун* "current account", from
**тећи**/3rd pl. **теку** "to flow"). (p. 283)

### Past gerund (verbal adverb, prior action)

Formed by adding **-в** or **-вши** (after a vowel) or **-авши** (after a consonant) to the
infinitive stem: устати → уставши, видети → видевши, рећи (рек-ти) → рекавши, моћи (мог-ти) →
могавши, сетити се → сетивши се. Rarely used in practice — Serbian more commonly expresses the
same idea with a full subordinate clause (*Кад сам већ почео да читам књигу...* rather than a past
gerund). The past gerund of **бити**, **бивши**, is nonetheless in common adjectival use meaning
"former": *бивша супруга* "former wife", *бивши председник* "former president". (p. 283)

### Passive past participle

The other verbal adjective, formed from the infinitive stem of either aspect. Regular patterns:
- **-ати** verbs → **-ан**: читати → читан/читана/читано; позвати → позван
- **-ити**/**-ети** verbs → **-ен**/**-јен** (with consonant-softening rules per the book's own
  "Grammar summary" §6): видети → вићен, учинити → учињен
- **-нути** verbs → **-нут**: прекинути → прекинут
- **-ети** verbs with present **-мем**/**-нем** → **-ет**: заузети (заузмем) → заузет; проклети
  (прокунем) → проклет

Used adjectivally in ordinary description, e.g. *Сто је прострт, хлеб је нарезан, вино је охлађено*
("the table is laid, the bread is cut, the wine chilled"). (pp. 284–285)

### Verbal nouns

Many passive participles become nouns by adding **-је**: ослобођен → ослобођење ("liberation"),
читан → читање ("reading"), учен → учење ("learning"). Intransitive verbs, which cannot take a
passive participle, instead add **-ање**/**-ење** directly to the infinitive stem: купати се →
купање ("bathing"), седети → седење ("sitting"), путовати → путовање ("travelling, journey").
(p. 285)

### Enclitics and word order

Enclitics (interrogative **ли**; dative/accusative/genitive pronoun clitics; **се**; **је**) must
appear together, in that fixed relative order, and are normally placed after the first stressed
element of the sentence or clause — which may be a single word or an entire phrase (*Jedan moj
prijatelj mi je rekao...*). **Не** + verb counts as a single word for this purpose, and the linking
conjunctions **и**, **а**, **ни** never count as the first word. The conjunctions **али**, **или**,
**јер** may optionally count as the first word; conjunctions followed by a natural pause/comma
(**дакле**, **према томе**) never do. When a clause opens with a "clause introducer" (**да**,
**што**, **ако**, relatives, interrogatives), the enclitics must follow immediately after it, even
inside what would otherwise read as one syntactic phrase. When a subordinate clause precedes the
main clause, enclitics belonging to the main clause follow the first word of the *main* clause, not
the subordinate clause — a construction Serbian often eases by inserting the resumptive pronoun
**то** (*Da si ga tajno viđala, to mi je jasno*). Infinitives may act as either separate clauses
(taking their own enclitic placement) or as part of the main clause. (pp. 289–292)

### Word order and information structure

Basic neutral Serbian word order is subject–verb–object, similar to English, but case marking frees
word order to instead track information structure: **the less informative part of a sentence
precedes the more informative/new-information part**, with stress building toward the sentence's
end. This lets an object be fronted for emphasis or backgrounding (*Tu kuću je sagradio poznati
arhitekta* — "That house was built by a famous architect", a construction the book notes is often
best rendered into English with a passive). Adverbial "scene-setting" phrases (time/place/manner
phrases orienting the sentence) are placed at the *beginning* of a neutral Serbian sentence, unlike
English which typically places them at the end (*Za vreme odmora na ostrvu, nismo mnogo čitali* —
"We didn't read much during our holiday on the island"). Adverbs quantifying/qualifying the verb
generally precede it (*Veoma lepo svira klavir*). Low-informational-value objects, pronouns, and
adverbs (**nešto, negde, nekako, nekud, tu, ovde, juče, danas, sutra, ovo, to, sad, često, stalno**)
precede the verb by default; placing one after the verb instead adds special emphasis (*Videli smo
ga negde* vs. neutral *Negde smo ga videli*). (pp. 292–294)

### Fixed-order phrases: vocative and possessive

Vocative phrases often reverse the neutral adjective–noun order: **Budalo jedna!** ("You fool!"),
**Bože dragi!** ("Dear God!"), **Mili moj!** ("My darling!"), **Zlato moje!** ("My treasure!") —
register-marked exclamatory/affective forms, distinct from neutral descriptive noun phrases.
Possessive phrases follow a fixed pattern demonstrative–possessive–noun: **taj moj prijatelj**
("that friend of mine"), **jedan moj poznanik** ("an acquaintance of mine"), **dva naša poznanika**
("two acquaintances of ours"). (p. 295)

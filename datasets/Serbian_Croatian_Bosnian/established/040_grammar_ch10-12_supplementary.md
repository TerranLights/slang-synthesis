## Grammar points

### §103a. The sixteen verb-conjugation types — full reference table

The textbook's Lesson 10 explains the 16-type system in prose (see `012_lesson10.md`) but does
not reproduce the reference grammar's full table pairing every type with a head verb and its
defining feature. Condensed here (masc. sg. head-verb form, 1sg./3pl. present, and the shift that
identifies the type): **1** *igrati* (add *-j-* in 3pl. only) · **2** *staviti* (*-i-* consistent) ·
**3** *videti/vidjeti* (infinitive stem in *-e-/-je-* drops in present) · **4** *dr̀žati* (stem-final
*ž, č, j, št, žđ*) · **5** *pìsati* (stem consonant softens in present, Type A softening — see
§112) · **6** *piti* (*-i-* or *-u-* infinitive, *-j-* present) · **7** *krènuti* (all stems end in
*-n-*) · **8a/8b** *kupòvati*/*kazìvati* (*-ov-*/*-iv-* infinitive vs. *-uj-* present) · **9**
*dàvati* (*-av-* infinitive vs. *-aj-* present) · **10** *brati* (vowel inserted in present stem) ·
**11** *ùzeti* (consonant inserted in present stem) · **12** *smeti/smjeti* (like type 3 but present
stem in *-e-/-ije-*) · **13** *jesti* (shifts *d*/*t* → *s* before infinitive *-ti*) · **14**
*gristi* (shifts a different consonant → *s*) · **15a** *teći* (drops *k/g*, shifts to *č/ž* in all
present forms but 3pl.) · **15b** *stići* (adds *-n-* in present, drops *k/g* in infinitive) ·
**16** *dòći* (present stem in *đ*/*d*, lost before infinitive *-ći*).

A handful of verbs are hybrids or true irregulars, not cleanly listed as head verbs of any one
type: *pasti* and *sesti/sjesti* (type 13) add *-n-* in the present the way type 15b does;
*umreti/umrijeti* "die" and *doneti/donijeti* "bring" each combine features of two types (the
latter is type 11-like in the present but type 3/12-like in the past); *stati* has a type-7-like
present but a type-10-like infinitive; *moći*'s 1sg. (*mogu*) lacks the expected consonant
softening; and *biti* and *hteti/htjeti* are irregular enough to be learned as standalone forms
(p. 156–157).

### §104d. Past-tense agreement with special subject types

Beyond the basic gender/number agreement of the L-participle (already covered in `012_lesson10.md`),
three subject types trigger special rules not documented in the textbook's lessons:

- **Plural-form, singular-meaning nouns** — *deca/djeca* "children," *braća* "brothers," *gospoda*
  "gentlemen" — take a **feminine singular** L-participle but a **plural** auxiliary in the past
  tense (mirroring their present-tense mixed agreement, review [90]): *Tvòja dèca su se igrala sa
  mòjom* "Your children played with mine."
- **Subjects headed by the numbers 2, 3, 4 (or *both*)** — the L-participle takes the **counting
  form** (review [58c]) while the auxiliary is **plural**: *Ta dva jaka mòmka su pòmogla* "Those
  two strong young men helped."
- **Interrogative-pronoun subjects** — if *šta/što* "what" is the subject, the L-participle is
  **neuter singular** (*Šta se tamo desilo?* "What happened there?"); if *ko/tko* "who" is the
  subject, the L-participle is **masculine singular** (*Ko je dànas dòšao?* "Who came today?"),
  regardless of the real-world gender of the person in question. (p. 161–162)

### §110a. Case paradigm for irregular body-part nouns

The textbook lessons name the irregularities (Gpl. *òčiju/ùšiju* in *-iju*; Gpl. *ruku/nogu* in
*-u*) but don't give the fuller paradigm. The reference grammar's full chart covers: *oko*/*uho*
(neuter sg. → feminine consonant-stem declension in the plural, Gpl. *-iju*); *ruka*/*noga* (regular
feminine, but Gpl. in bare *-u* rather than *-a*); *prst*/*nokat* (masculine, same Gpl.-in-*-u*
pattern, though *pr̀stiju*/*nòktiju* also occur); and *rame* "shoulder" (declined like *ime*, with
both singular and plural forms — unlike the other pluralia-tantum body nouns). A separate small
group — *leđa* "back," *usta* "mouth," *prsa*/*prsi* "chest," *grudi* "bosom" — has **only**
neuter- or feminine-plural forms despite singular meaning; of these, *grudi*'s scope of meaning
differs regionally (B/S: general "chest" or specifically "breasts"; C: *prsi* = chest, *grudi* =
breasts only). (p. 168–169)

### §110b. "Body-ache" construction: prefixed variants of *boleti/boljeti*

The base construction (*noge me bole* "my feet hurt," logical subject in the accusative, body part
as grammatical subject) is already documented in `012_lesson10.md`. The reference grammar adds a
family of **prefixed derivatives** with distinct meanings, all built on the same accusative-subject
frame: unprefixed *boleti/boljeti* = "hurt" (ongoing); prefix **za-** = "begin to hurt" (onset:
*zàboleo ga je zub* "his tooth started aching"); prefix **raz-**/**o-** = "get sick, fall ill," no
longer referencing a body part specifically (*razbolela se od malarije* "she caught malaria");
prefix **po-** = durative "be ailing, do poorly" (*već godinama pòbolева* "he's been in poor health
for years"); prefix **pre-** = "get well, recover, get over" (*neće prebòleti tùgu* "she's not
going to get over the grief"). (p. 169)

### §111b. Regional variation in past-tense clitic word order

`013_lesson11.md`/`012_lesson10.md` document the six-slot XYZ clitic order and the JE-FINAL/
JU-JE/SE-MERGER rules, but not the following B/C/S regional differences, which the extraction spec
flags as never-skip content:

- **Bosnian** shows a growing tendency to **avoid *ju*** altogether even where the JU-JE rule would
  otherwise require it (replacing the accusative feminine clitic *je* next to auxiliary *je* with
  *je* itself rather than *ju*) — a sentence like *Òna joj je je ispjevala* is acceptable in
  Bosnian but not Serbian or Croatian.
- **Croatian** shows the *opposite* tendency, using *ju* even **outside** the JU-JE frame (e.g. for
  a feminine direct object with no adjacent 3sg. auxiliary): *Ja sam ju kùpila* "I bought it" is a
  Croatian-only alternative to the pan-BCS *Ja sam je kùpila*.
- **Croatian only** permits the dative reflexive clitic *si* to sit adjacent to the homophonous
  2sg. auxiliary *si* (*Ti si si ga kùpila* "You bought it for yourself") — this clitic is unused
  in Serbian and rare in Bosnian.
- **Croatian** applies the SE-MERGER rule less rigidly than Bosnian or Serbian, permitting *se* and
  *je* to co-occur unmerged (*Òna mu se je jàvila* "She called him," where B/S would merge to *Òna
  mu se jàvila*). (p. 171–172)

### §112. The system of consonant softenings (Types A, B, C)

Not previously extracted in this dataset — a later chapter (`045_grammar_ch20_phonology.md`, §167a)
explicitly *reviews* this system as "presented earlier in the grammar," confirming this section
(§112, Chapter 11) is its origin point. This is the systematic version of a process the textbook
lessons only ever invoke piecemeal (e.g. comparative-by-softening in `013_lesson11.md`, verbal
nouns/passive participles in Lessons 10–11) without ever laying out the full correspondence chart.

Three softening series exist, all triggered historically by an adjacent front vowel or the glide
/j/, and each restricted to specific grammatical contexts:

- **Type A** (narrowest: 3 consonants) — *k→c, g→z, h→s*. Contexts: masc. Npl./DLIpl. nouns
  (*vòjnik→vojnìci*), fem. DLsg. nouns (*rùka→rùci*), imperative of type 15a/b verbs
  (*obùku→obùci*), imperfect tense of type 15 verbs, and some type-7→type-5 verbal derivation
  (*màknuti→micati*).
- **Type B** (4 consonants) — *k→č, g→ž, h→š, c→č*. Contexts: masc. Vsg. nouns (*vuk→vuče*),
  before the singulative suffix *-in* (*Turk-e→Tùrčin*), possessive adjectives in *-in*/*-ov* on
  kinship-term stems (*majka→majčin*), most present-tense forms of type 15a verbs but 3pl.
  (*tèku→tèčeš*), and widely in derivation (*mrak→mràčan* "dark").
- **Type C** (broadest and most complex: *k,g,h,c,s,z,t,d,st* plus the whole labial series
  *p,b,v,m,f* + *n,l*) — e.g. *t→ć, d→đ, st→šć/št*, and all labials add *-lj-* (*p→plj, b→blj*,
  etc.). Contexts: fem. Isg. nouns in a bare stem (*smrt→smrću*), neuter collective nouns (see
  §115's already-documented collectives — this is the mechanism behind *cveće*, *lišće*, etc.),
  comparative adjectives (see §113b, already documented), present tense of type 5 verbs
  (*pìsati→pìšem*), imperfect of types 2–3, verbal nouns and passive participles of types 2–3 and
  15 (*plàtiti→plaćen*, *vidjeti→viđenje*). (p. 172–175)

### §114a. *Što...to* and *sve* + comparative — correlative constructions

Not documented in the textbook lessons. Two constructions link two comparatives together:

- **Što (comp.), to (comp.)** — "the more/less X, the more/less Y," restricted to imperfective
  verbs (or verbless, aphoristic constructions): *Što je brže hòdao, to se bolje òsećao* "The
  faster he walked, the better he felt"; *Što više, to vesèlije* "The more the merrier."  A related
  use of bare *što* + comparative (optionally with *moći*) expresses "as X as possible": *Dòđite
  što brže!* "Come as fast as you can!"
- **Sve + comparative** — an unchanging *sve* before a comparative adjective/adverb marks a
  steadily increasing (or decreasing) rate: *Taj pòsao biva sve lakši* "This job gets easier and
  easier"; *Sve više ljùdi je dòlazilo* "More and more people kept coming." (p. 181)

### §117b. Active and passive participles used as ordinary adjectives

`013_lesson11.md` documents passive-participle *formation* (§117a) but not this extension: some
L-participles (the "active participle") and passive participles have drifted from their verbal
compound-tense role into simple attributive adjectives, sometimes with a shifted meaning. Active-
participle examples: *ostareti* "age" → *òstarela majka* "his aged mother"; *uvenuti* "fade" →
*ùvele ruže* "faded roses"; *zreti* "ripen" → *zrelo vòće* "ripe fruit." Passive-participle
examples: *otvoriti* "open" → *vràta òtvorena* "an open door"; *poznati* "know" → *màrka poznata u
cèlom svetu* "a brand known worldwide"; *pokvariti* "spoil" → *pòkvareni zubi* "decayed teeth." In
these uses the participle behaves as a plain adjective (predicative or attributive), no longer
tied to a specific verbal tense/aspect reading. (p. 187–188)

### §118. Passive sentences, introduction (true passive, not *se*-passive)

Not documented anywhere else in this dataset. Distinct from the *se*-passive already covered
elsewhere (review [74]), BCS also has a passive construction directly parallel to English: subject
+ *biti* + passive participle, where the subject of the passive sentence corresponds to the object
of the active sentence (*Pismo je napisano* "The letter has been written," from active *Napisala
sam pismo* "I wrote the letter"). The key cross-linguistic mismatch: English typically needs a
present-perfect passive ("has been written") where BCS uses a plain present-tense passive (*je
napisano*), because — as already noted at §106a — the BCS present tense covers a broader range of
meaning than English's. Whether a given passive sentence is best read as a *result* (implying past
completed action, translating with English past/present-perfect) or a *state* (translating with
English present) is usually disambiguated by context, not grammar: *Čovek je povređen* can mean
either "The man IS wounded" (state) or "The man WAS wounded" (result), depending on what follows.
(p. 187–188)

### §120 (addition). Body-position action verbs are not aspect pairs with their own stasis partner

`013_lesson11.md` documents the stasis-vs-motion paradigm (*sedeti/sjediti* vs. *sesti/sjesti*,
etc.) but not this caveat: although the stasis verbs are imperfective and the action (motion) verbs
are perfective, **they are not an aspect pair** — the perfective action verbs (*stati, leći, sesti/
sjesti*) have their **own separate imperfective partners** (*stajati* [confusingly homophonous with
the stasis verb "stand" but a different lexeme — 1sg. *stajem* not *stojim*], *lègati/lijegati*,
*sedati/sjedati*), used for **repeated** instances of the movement: *Tri pùta mi već staješ nà
nogu!* "That's three times you've stepped on my foot!" The stasis/action pairing only starts to
behave like a true aspect pair once further verbal derivation (prefixation) is applied to it.
(p. 190–191)

### §121a. Proper names vs. titles-of-works in apposition

Not documented in the textbook lessons (which cover the §121b profession-suffix vocabulary
instead). When a title of a book/work of art, or the name of a building/organization, follows a
generic noun identifying it (i.e. stands in apposition), **only the generic noun takes the case
ending — the title itself stays nominative**: *To je iz knjige Sporedno nebo* "That's from the
book [lit.] 'Sporedno nebo'" (not *Sporednog neba*, even though "book" is genitive here).
**Personal names behave oppositely** — they take case endings even in apposition to a generic
noun: *Pìše člànak o ròmanima čùvenog pisca Ìve Àndrića* "S/he's writing an article about the
novels of the famous writer Ivo Andrić" (both the generic "writer" and the personal name "Ivo
Andrić" are genitive). (p. 191)

### §122a. Aorist and imperfect — condensed paradigm reference

`014_lesson12.md` explains the aorist/imperfect distinction in prose but doesn't give worked
paradigm forms. Condensed reference: **aorist** endings (1sg./2-3sg./1pl./2pl./3pl.) for a type-1
verb like *upitati*: *-ah, -a, -asmo, -aste, -aše* (added after dropping infinitive *-ti*); type-13
*reći*: *rekoh, reče, rekosmo, rekoste, rekoše* (stem from 3pl. present minus final vowel, with *-e-*
inserted in 2-3sg. and *-o-* elsewhere); *biti*'s aorist is irregular and suppletive: *bih, bi,
bismo, biste, biše*. A structural trap: aorist 3pl. and imperfect 3sg. can share the same ending
shape, so context is often the only disambiguator. **Imperfect** is built (for most verbs) from the
infinitive stem plus *-ah/-aše/-asmo/-aste/-ahu*; *biti*'s imperfect has both a *contracted* form
(*bjeh/beh...*) and a *full* form (*bijah/bejah...*) in each dialect, and remains genuinely common
in modern speech (unlike the aorist and imperfect of other verbs, which are now largely literary/
archaic, most retained in Bosnia-Herzegovina and Montenegro). Usage: the imperfect is exclusively a
*background*-narration tense (never carries the story forward on its own); the aorist narrates
*foreground*, sequential single actions — hence its concentration in reported-speech verbs
(*upìta*, *odgovori*, *reče* "asked/answered/said") and first-person motion-verb announcements
(*Òdoh sad* "I'm off now"). (p. 195–196)

### §125c–126. The systematic time-adverb relational system

Not documented anywhere else in this dataset (the individual lexical items — *jučer, sutra,
proljetos*, etc. — are already glossed in the Lesson-19/glossary files, but not this systematic
frame tying them together). Two related systems:

1. **±2-day series around "now"**: *prekjučer* (2 before) — *jučer* (1 before) — *dànas* (now) —
   *sutra* (1 after) — *prekòsutra* (2 after). A parallel "this period" series (*jutros* "this
   morning," *večèras* "this evening," *sinoć* "last night" — invariably past) is joined by a set
   that is genuinely **ambiguous** between the coming and the just-past instance of a period —
   *pròletos/pròljetos* "this spring," *letos/ljetos* "this summer," *jesènas* "this autumn,"
   *zìmus* "this winter," *nòćas* "tonight/last night" — exactly parallel to English "this autumn"
   being ambiguous the same way.
2. **The *s-/ov-/on-/k-* adverb paradigm**: a fully systematic four-way grid crossing NOW (marker
   *s-*), HERE (marker *ov-*, from *ovaj*), THERE/THEN (marker *on-*, from *onaj*), and the
   question-word slot (marker *k-*) across the semantic dimensions of time, place, source, and
   direction — e.g. *sada* "now" / *òvde* "here" / *ònde* "there" / *kàda* "when"; *òvamo* "this
   direction" / *ònamo* "that direction" / *kamo* "where [to]." Adjectives are derived from nearly
   all of these by adding **-šnji** (*dànas→dànašnji* "today's," *sada→sàdašnji* "present-day,"
   *jučer→jučèrašnji* "yesterday's" — but *sinoć→sinoćnji* is the sole irregular ending in *-nji*
   without the *-š-*). (p. 208–210)

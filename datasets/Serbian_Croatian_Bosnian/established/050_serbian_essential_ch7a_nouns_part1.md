## Grammar points

### §7.1. Types of nouns (proper, common, mass, collective, abstract)

Hammond classifies nouns into five semantic types, each associated with characteristic
derivational suffixes: **proper nouns** (names of people/places, with distinct suffix families for
country names, male/female inhabitant nouns, personal names, and surnames — e.g. `-ija`/`-ska` for
countries, `-ac`/`-anin` for male inhabitants, `-kinja`/`-ka`/`-ica` for female inhabitants); **common
nouns** (an extensive list of derivational suffixes such as `-ac`, `-ar`, `-ba`, `-ak`, `-lac`,
`-nik`, `-telj`, `-tor`); **mass nouns** (singular = quantity, plural = distinct varieties — e.g.
*šećer* "sugar" / *šećeri* "sugars"; suffixes like `-ada`, `-ina`, `-ovina`); **collective nouns**
(entities that act grammatically as a singular whole despite plural meaning — a footnoted three-way
split: nouns in `-ad` decline as singular feminine but take a plural verb, nouns in `-ež`/`-ija` decline
as singular feminine with a singular verb, and nouns in `-je` are treated as singular neuter despite
plural sense, e.g. *bilje* "plants," *lišće* "leaves," *granje* "branches"); and **abstract nouns**
(intangible concepts, largely built with `-ost`, `-stvo`, `-ija`, and — most productively — `-nje`,
which attaches to a verb's infinitive stem to form a neuter singular noun of the action itself, e.g.
*pevati* "to sing" → *pevanje* "singing"). (pp. 105–111)

### §7.2. Gender of nouns

Every noun is masculine, feminine, or neuter; gender is read off the nominative singular ending as a
default pattern with named exceptions. Default: masculine nouns end in a consonant, feminine nouns end
in `-a`, neuter nouns end in `-e` or `-o`. Exceptions flagged: some masculine nouns end in `-a` or
`-o` (cross-referenced to §7.4.1–7.4.2, outside this chunk's range); some feminine nouns end in a
consonant or `-o` (e.g. *radost* "joy," *so* "salt" — cross-referenced to §7.4.3). In the plural, the
default pattern is masculine `-i`, feminine `-e`, neuter `-a`, with a mirrored pair of exceptions:
masculine nouns in `-a` take `-e` in the plural, while feminine nouns in a consonant or `-o` take `-i`
in the plural (e.g. *deda–dede* "grandfather(s)," *radost–radosti* "joy/joys," *so(l)–soli*
"salt(s)"). (pp. 111–112)

### §7.3 (intro). The seven cases — Hammond's overview

Largely redundant with established/042's §154 overview (same seven-case framing, same "no 1:1
case-to-meaning mapping" caveat) — not re-summarized here. One point worth flagging as a small added
nuance: Hammond notes explicitly that book/film/hotel titles decline *unless* preceded by a noun that
identifies their category (e.g. *čitam knjigu "Hari Poter"* "I'm reading the book 'Harry Potter'" —
title stays nominative/undeclined — vs. *čitam "Harija Potera"* "I'm reading 'Harry Potter'" — title
itself declines when standing alone as the object). This distinction isn't covered in established/042.
(pp. 112–115)

### §7.3.1.1. Uses of the nominative — points beyond established/042 §155a

Established/042 covers subject, predicate-noun, and comparison (*kao*/*nego*) uses. Hammond adds two
points not in 042: (1) explicit confirmation that a noun/word-group in apposition to the subject
(restating it, e.g. "Our friend, Peter, is working") stays nominative; (2) the nominative's use in
bare exclamations (*Vatra!* "Fire!", *Lopov!* "Thief!"). Hammond also supplies full declined
paradigms for the four interrogative/relative pronouns *koji* (which), *kakav* (what kind), *koliki*
(how big), *čiji* (whose) across gender and number for the nominative and (further on) every other
case — a systematic pronoun-paradigm resource established/042 doesn't provide. (pp. 115–117)

### §7.3.2.1. Uses of the genitive — points beyond established/042 §157a–c

Established/042 covers the core "of"-possession, partitive-object, negated-verb-object, and
existential *ima*/*nema* uses. Hammond's treatment adds real granularity worth keeping:

- **Possession with a modified possessor:** the genitive (not a possessive adjective) is obligatory
  when the possessor noun itself carries a modifier (e.g. a title: *kola profesora Mitića* "Professor
  Mitić's car") — a rule 042 doesn't state.
  Genitive is also required (rather than a possessive adjective) whenever the possessing noun has no
  adjectival possessive form available at all (e.g. body parts of inanimate objects: *dršku tiganja*
  "the handle of the frying pan").
- **Women's full names in possession:** first name goes genitive, surname stays unchanged (*muž Ane
  Marinković* "Ana Marinković's husband").
- **Countable-vs-uncountable existential distinction, spelled out three ways** (deeper than 042's
  general statement): with a *plural countable* noun, existential *bilo/ima/nema* + genitive **plural**
  (*U našem gradu ima lepih kuća* "There are beautiful houses in our town"); with an *uncountable*
  noun, genitive **singular** (*Bilo je vode u česmi* "There was water in the tap"); when the noun
  itself is grammatically singular, the **nominative** is used instead, not genitive at all (*Ovde je
  nekada bila fontana* "There was once a fountain here").
- **Closed-class genitive-governing adjectives:** *vredan, gladan, dostojan, žedan, željan, pun,
  svestan, sit* (table above) — not listed in 042.
- **Two distinct genitive-governing verb classes:** (a) *na*-prefixed reflexive verbs of
  satiation/excess (*najesti se, napiti se, naslušati se, načekati se* — "to eat/drink/listen/wait to
  the point of satiety/excess"); (b) a separate closed set of reflexive verbs whose object is genitive
  for no synchronically transparent reason (*zaželeti se, setiti se, dohvatiti se, latiti se, ticati
  se*, etc.) — 042 doesn't enumerate either class.
- **The verb *doći* ("to come") + genitive**, used specifically to express cost/price in a
  fatalistic/idiomatic register (*Život će nas doći ovaj odmor* "This holiday will cost us our
  lives").
- **Partitive-quantity nouns split by genitive number:** *vekna* "a loaf," *kilo* "a kilo," *metar* "a
  metre," *kap* "a drop" take genitive *singular*, while *grupa* "a group," *kolona* "a column," *niz*
  "a series," *par* "a couple" take genitive *plural* — a distinction not in 042.
- **Number-of-people nouns** *dvojica, trojica, četvorica* ... *desetorica* ("a group of [N] people")
  take genitive plural and mirror ordinary numerals' 2–4-vs-5+ verb-agreement split — entirely new
  vocabulary/grammar not in 042.
- **Uncountable quantity without a quantifier word at all** (bare genitive singular standing in for
  English "some": *Kupili smo šećera* "We bought (some) sugar") — a slightly different partitive
  pattern than 042's negated-verb-object partitive.
- Full case-question paradigms (*koga? čega?* "whom/what?" — no plural form) and the declined
  interrogative-pronoun tables (*koji/kakav/koliki/čiji* in the genitive) are also given but are the
  same systematic pronoun-paradigm layer noted under §7.3.1.1 above.

The genitive-plural formation table (stem/ending mechanics per declension class) is **skipped** here
as substantially overlapping with the paradigm tables already captured in
`established/039_grammar_ch7-9_supplementary.md` §89–93. (pp. 117–125)

### §7.3.3.1. Uses of the dative — points beyond established/042 §158a–b

Established/042 frames dative "affectedness" uses abstractly (experiencer, possessive dative, ethical
dative). Hammond instead supplies **closed-class verb inventories organized by semantic/morphological
type**, which 042 doesn't provide at all: (1) verbs of giving/receiving (*davati/dati,
poklanjati/pokloniti, slati/poslati, vraćati/vratiti, pomagati/pomoći*, etc.); (2) verbs of
telling/advising/complaining (*govoriti/reći, pisati/napisati, objašnjavati/objasniti, žaliti se/
požaliti se, zahvaljivati se/zahvaliti se*, etc.); (3) *pri*-prefixed motion verbs meaning
"approach/bring closer" (*prilaziti/prići, prinositi/preneti, priticati/priteći*) — a dedicated
dative-governing motion-verb class not mentioned in 042; (4) idiomatic/impersonal "liking" and
"seeming" verbs (*sviđati se/svideti se, dopadati se/dopasti se, gaditi se/zgaditi se, diviti se/
zadiviti se, činiti se/učiniti se, izgledati, nadati se/ponadati se, radovati se/obradovati se,
smetati/zasmetati*) — a genuinely useful closed inventory for slang-relevant register (these verbs
recur constantly in casual speech); (5) *se* + dative pronoun constructions expressing "feel like
doing X" (*Igra im se fudbal* "They feel like playing football," lit. "it plays itself to them,
football"); (6) adverb + dative + *biti* impersonal constructions for states/reactions (*Milo (drago)
mi je* "I'm glad," *Hladno nam je* "We're cold," *Dosadno mi je* "I'm bored," *Kako ti je?* "How are
you feeling?") — the productive pattern behind the fixed greeting idiom *Drago mi je* "Nice to meet
you." The "possessive dative" (042's category) gets an added nuance: Hammond stresses it differs from
genitive possession specifically by implying *personal/emotional connection* rather than strict legal
ownership (*Gde ti je muž?* "Where is your husband?" — using dative *ti*, not genitive). A set of
everyday dative-based fixed expressions is also given (gratitude, greetings, condolences,
well-wishing — *Hvala ti, Drago mi je, Žao mi je, Srećan ti put, Javi mi se*), useful as
register-marked fixed phrases for the slang-synthesis corpus. Dative-taking prepositions
(*prema, k(a), nasuprot*) substantially duplicate established/042's §158b and are not repeated.
(pp. 125–132)

### §7.3.4.1. Uses and formation of the accusative — points beyond established/042 §156a–b

Established/042 already covers accusative direct-object marking, body-ache/emotional-state
constructions, motion-preposition pairs, and time expressions in comparable depth — the usage-level
content substantially overlaps and is not repeated. One formation-level point Hammond makes explicit
that's worth flagging: **the accusative singular for masculine nouns splits by animacy** — inanimate
masculine nouns keep the nominative-singular form unchanged in the accusative (*kamion → kamion*
"truck"), while animate masculine nouns (humans/animals) take a genitive-singular-style `-a` ending
instead (*muškarac → muškarca* "man," with the fleeting-a rule applying where relevant) — a
distinction established/042 doesn't spell out at the noun-formation level (it's implicit in
Alexander's broader grammar but not stated in the case-usage-review chapter itself). (pp. 132–139)

### §7.3.5. The vocative case — largely new (established/042 §155b is brief)

Established/042 covers vocative *usage* (address, insult/endearment via *jedan*, epic-poetry
substitution) reasonably close to what Hammond covers — not repeated. **Formation rules are new**,
however (042 gives none): masculine and neuter nouns and all plural nouns use the nominative form
unchanged as the vocative. Singular masculine nouns take `-e` after a hard final consonant (with
alternations **k→č, c→č, g→ž, h→š** before this `-e`: *otac→oče* "father," *Bog→Bože* "God,"
*čovek→čoveče* "man") or `-u` after a soft consonant (no alternation: *prijatelju* "friend!"); some
hard-consonant nouns allow either ending (*mesare/mesaru* "butcher!"). Feminine and masculine nouns in
`-a` mostly keep `-a` unchanged (*mama! tata! Marija!*), but some names/nouns in `-a` shift to `-o`
instead (*majka→majko* "mother!", *Nada→Nado*, *sluga→slugo*); collective nouns in `-a` also shift to
`-o` (*gospoda→gospodo* "ladies and gentlemen!", *deca→deco* "children!", *braća→braćo* "brothers!").
Feminine nouns of three-plus syllables ending in `-ica` take `-ice` instead (*komšinica→komšinice*
"neighbor!", *prijateljica→prijateljice* "girlfriend!"). Feminine consonant-stem nouns simply add `-i`
to the nominative (*radost→radosti* "joy!"). (pp. 139–142)

### §7.3.6.1. Uses of the instrumental — points beyond established/042 §159a–b

Established/042 covers means/manner, static-location spatial prepositions, and the productive
instrumental-derived-adverb layer. Hammond adds a genuinely useful semantic distinction for the
preposition *sa/s* "with" specifically: it contrasts **company** (both parties are active
participants in the verb's action, e.g. *Ana ide u kupovinu sa Zlatom* "Ana is going shopping with
Zlata" — both are shopping) against **descriptive "with"** (the *sa*-phrase merely describes a
feature of the subject and isn't itself acting, e.g. *Video je devojku sa smeđim očima* "He saw a girl
with brown eyes" — the eyes aren't participating in anything) against **manner** (*Studenti su slušali
profesora sa pažnjom* "The students listened to the professor attentively," paraphrasable with a plain
adverb, *pažljivo*) — established/042 doesn't break *s(a)* usage into this three-way descriptive
taxonomy. Hammond also explicitly cross-references, for each static-location instrumental preposition
(*za, među, nad, pod, pred*), the parallel genitive-plus-compound-preposition alternative (*iza,
između, iznad, ispod, ispred*) as a stylistic paraphrase option — reinforcing the same
accusative/instrumental/genitive-compound three-way system 042 already tabulates, so not repeated in
full. (pp. 142–146)

### §7.3.7.1. Uses of the locative — points beyond established/042 §158c

Established/042 lists locative-governing prepositions and their core senses at a similar grain.
Hammond's per-preposition treatment adds a few nuances worth keeping: **na**/**u** get an explicit
general-semantic contrast (*u* = closed spaces, spaces with "an inside"; *na* = open/expansive
surfaces, both dry land and water) with their genitive-taking "opposite" prepositions named directly
(*iz* "out of" opposes *u*; *sa* "off" opposes *na*) — a tidy mnemonic pairing not stated this way in
042. **po** gets a distinct "according to/in someone's opinion" sense (*Kakav je on čovek po tebi?*
"What kind of a man is he according to you?", *po svom ukusu* "according to one's own taste") beyond
042's manner/distributive/temporal senses. **pri** is confirmed as occurring *only* in the locative
(never with another case) and gets an explicit comparison sense (*Ona nije ništa pri tebi* "She's
nothing compared to you") alongside its more common "attached to/during" senses. **prema** gets a
concrete "facing, oriented toward" spatial sense with a worked example of someone sitting with their
back turned toward the referent (*Sedeo je leđima okrenut prema tebi* "He sat with his back turned
toward you") in addition to its already-042-covered "according to" sense. (pp. 146–150)

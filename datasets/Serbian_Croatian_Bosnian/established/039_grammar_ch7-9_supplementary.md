## Grammar points

### §78 The imperative mood — fuller system than the lesson gives

The lesson-level treatment covers the basic imperative and a negative imperative; this chapter gives
the complete productive system:

- **Imperative-stem derivation rule** (§78a): drop the final vowel of the 3pl. present; if what
  remains ends in `-j`, that is the stem (group 1: a-conjugation and some others, simple stem, long
  vowel before -j); otherwise add theme vowel `-i` (group 2), with subgroup 2b further replacing
  stem-final `-k`/`-g` with `-c`/`-z` (e.g. rèći → rèci, pòmoći → pomòzi). A few verbs formerly in
  group 1 (brojati, pojiti, trajati) have drifted to group 2a imperatives (broji, poji, traji).
- **Three imperative endings**: singular `-Ø`, plural `-te`, inclusive `-mo` — added to the stem.
- **The inclusive imperative and hajde** (§78c): the inclusive form (1pl. ending `-mo`) suggests a
  course of action including speaker and addressee(s); more formal-sounding than the colloquial
  alternative `hajde` (+ infinitive, or `hajde da` + 1pl. present); `hajdemo` alone can substitute for
  `hajde da idemo`, and can even convey abstract "let's get moving [on X]" without literal motion.
- **neka / nek** (§78d): places `neka` before a 3rd-person present-tense verb (usually perfective) to
  express an indirectly phrased request, ranging from mild suggestion to veiled command; also used to
  introduce a wish/blessing, or alone to convey acquiescence.
- **Negative imperatives, two systems** (§78e): (1) the all-purpose negative stem `nemoj` + regular
  imperative endings, followed by infinitive or `da` + present of either aspect; after `ma`, `nemoj`
  conveys surprise/irony ("Ma nemoj!" — "You don't say!"). (2) `ne` directly before the imperative
  form of the verb — normally only with imperfective verbs; `ne` + a **perfective** imperative adds a
  strong cautionary overtone (~"you'd better not...").
- **Aspect choice in the affirmative imperative**: both aspects occur freely; perfective is more
  common (commands usually focus on completion), but the choice tracks the same general aspect
  semantics as elsewhere — durative/general vs. completion-focused.

### §79 The instrumental case — fuller endings mechanics and pronoun paradigm

- **Isg. endings**: masc./neut. `-om` (`-em` after soft consonants); fem. `-a` nouns always `-om`
  (long vowel, unaffected by preceding softness); fem. consonant-final nouns take `-i` or `-u`.
- **The `-u` vs. `-i` mechanics for feminine consonant-final nouns** (§79b) — genuinely deeper than
  lesson level: if the stem-final consonant undergoes regular consonant-softening, `-u` is added after
  the softened consonant (pamet→pameću, kost→košću, radost→radošću); if the stem ends in `-ć`/`-đ`,
  `-u` is added directly (noć→noću, žeđ→žeđu); in all other nouns the ending is `-ju` (stvar→stvarju,
  riječ/reč→riječju/rečju, laž→lažju). Nouns in `-ost`/`-est` very frequently take `-u`; many other
  common nouns almost always take `-i` (some, like stvar and laž, almost never take `-u` despite the
  rule allowing it — alternate `-i` forms exist for most of these: stvarju~stvari, riječju~riječi,
  lažju~laži).
- **Interrogative pronoun instrumental**: `ko/tko` and `šta/što` both have short (`kim`, `čim`) and
  long, rising-accent alternate forms (`kime`, `čime`).
- **Full pronoun-object instrumental paradigm** (§79d): 1sg./3sg. have alternate longer forms (mnom
  ~ mnome; njim ~ njime; njom ~ njome); plural instrumental = same form as dative/locative plural.
  **Vowel-insertion rule specific to 1sg.**: because `mnom` begins with the consonant cluster `mn-`,
  a preceding preposition that doesn't already end in a vowel adds `-a` (with the accent shifting onto
  it) — `s(a) mnom`, `predà mnom`, `nadà mnom` — but this vowel-insertion happens ONLY in the 1sg.
  form (2nd/3rd sg. take the bare preposition: s tobom, s njim).

### §80 Prepositions with the instrumental

- **s(a) "with"** — homophonous with, but semantically unrelated to, the genitive preposition s(a)
  (review [43a]); best treated as two separate prepositions that happen to share a form. Not all
  English "with" maps onto BCS `s(a)` (see §81a instrumental of means, where "with" as a tool/means
  is NOT expressed by a preposition at all).
- **Locational prepositions**: među "among", nad "above", pod "below", pred "in front of", za
  "behind" — all also usable with the accusative when motion (not static location) is meant. Notable
  idiom: za + Instr. = "at [a table/desk/the wheel]" (za stolom "at the table"), where English uses
  "at" but BCS literally says "behind."
  - Compound genitive counterparts exist for most of these (između, iznad, ispod, ispred, iza, all
    with `iz-`/`is-` + the instrumental preposition + genitive case) and are, per the author, actually
    the more usual way to express these meanings today; some speakers reserve the bare
    instrumental-taking prepositions for abstract senses and the compound genitive ones for concrete
    spatial senses, but for most speakers they're interchangeable.

### §81 The instrumental without prepositions — four distinct bare-case functions

Genuinely deeper than lesson coverage, which likely gave only "instrumental of means" impressionistically:

1. **Instrumental of means** (81a): tool, manner, or transport — critically, BCS uses the **bare
   instrumental alone**, with NO preposition, where English would say "by/with/through." Also covers
   body-part instrumentals (piše levom rukom "writes with his left hand").
2. **Instrumental in time expressions** (81b): repeated/regular intervals, almost always days of the
   week (utorkom i četvrtkom "on Tuesdays and Thursdays"); duration nearly always paired with već
   ("already/for [x time] now": već stoljećima "for centuries"); and the fixed adverbial noću "at
   night" / danju "by day" (paired as dan i noć "night and day").
3. **Instrumental of space** (81c): denotes a directional trajectory/path (not destination, which is
   u/na + Acc., and not non-directional movement-within-an-area, which is po + Loc.). The noun `put`
   ("way, road, time-as-instance") has an irregular Isg. `putem` (not the expected `putom`); Serbian
   and Bosnian use only `putem`; Croatian distinguishes adverbial `putem` (this abstract/directional
   sense) from nominal `putom` (concrete noun sense, e.g. after a preposition, or in `autoput`).
4. **Verbs taking bare instrumental objects** (81d): must be learned per-verb; listed here are baviti
   se ("be occupied with" — job or hobby), trgovati ("deal in"), ponositi se ("take pride in"),
   služiti and smatrati (which take BOTH an accusative object and a second instrumental object, e.g.
   "consider X [Acc.] to be Y [Instr.]").

### §82 Se-verbs: inclinational vs. desiderative — a distinction not stated at lesson level

Both types add `se` to a 3rd-singular verb form to produce a subjectless sentence whose experiencer is
in the **dative**:

- **Inclinational** (82a): restricted to intransitive verbs describing bodily states (spava mi se
  "I'm sleepy", povraća mi se "I feel sick to my stomach", vrti mi se u glavi "my head is spinning").
- **Desiderative** (82b): broader class, transitive or intransitive, expressing "feel like doing X"
  (ide mi se u kino "I feel like going to the movies", piše mi se "I'm in the mood to write"). Bosnian
  and Croatian generalize this with `ne da se` ("not feel like [it]"), which Serbian instead expresses
  by specifying the desiderative se-verb directly (or a phrase with mrziti). If the verb is transitive
  and the speaker wants to name the desired object explicitly, the **desired object becomes the
  grammatical subject** and determines verb number: pije mi se kafa "I feel like [some] coffee" /
  piju mi se francuska vina "I'm up for [some] French wines."
- Both types contrast with active-verb equivalents (hteti/htjeti, željeti, moći) where the experiencer
  IS the grammatical subject; the se-verb versions are less direct/less emphatic in focus, not simply
  synonymous paraphrases.

### §84 Verbs of transport, and the Croatian/Bosnian-Serbian motion-preposition system

- **nositi/voditi/voziti** (84a): English "take [somewhere]" splits three ways by manner — nositi
  (carry an object or a being carried in one's arms), voditi (lead a self-moving being on foot),
  voziti (transport by vehicle; voziti se = "ride/go by vehicle" oneself). All three imperfective;
  perfective partners are built on distinct, non-uniform stems (illustrated here with od- prefixed
  forms: odneti/odnijeti, odvesti [led], odvesti [driven] — same infinitive, different present stems).
  hodati = "walk" as an activity in itself, distinct from the opposition walking-vs-riding.
- **Croatian vs. Bosnian/Serbian destination-preposition system** (84b) — a systematic contrast not
  given at lesson level:

  | | Croatian | Bosnian/Serbian |
  |---|---|---|
  | to a person | k(a) + Dat. (or bare Dat.) | do + Gen. |
  | to a person's house | k(a) + Dat. | kod + Gen. |
  | towards a location | prema + Dat. | prema or k(a) + Dat. |
  | query re: destination | kamo | kuda |
  | query re: route taken | kuda | kojim putem |

  All BCS varieties use `prema` for "according to, compared with" regardless of the above split.

### §85 Word order — the XYZ clitic model, extended to infinitive-dependent clauses

- **"Starting over"** (85a): the X-slot (pre-clitic position) need not be filled by a single word — a
  long phrase can occupy it — but speakers sometimes restart the XYZ sequence mid-sentence, either for
  rhythm (X phrase too long) or for emphasis (placing a predicate form in the second X slot).
- **Clitics with dependent infinitives**: when the "idea infinitive" is expressed as `da` + present,
  its clitics must follow the `da` — no other word order is grammatical. When it's a single-word
  infinitive, its clitics can EITHER migrate up to join the main verb's clitics in the first Y slot, OR
  stay with the infinitive in a second, restarted X-Y sequence. Which happens depends on how tightly
  the infinitive is semantically bound to the main verb — sentences of "type 1" (clitic migrates:
  teško je + infinitive constructions) are less common than "type 2" (clitic stays put).

### §87 Se-verbs, continued: reciprocal usage and the case of se-verb objects

- **Reciprocal se** (87a): plural verb + se can convey "each other" (Goran i Vesna se vole "Goran and
  Vesna are in love [with each other]"); an equivalent meaning can also be built with jedan...drugi.
- **Se-verbs taking non-accusative objects** (87b) — systematic list (no se-verb ever takes an
  accusative object): genitive (bojati se, sjećati se/sećati se — J/E variant pair, ticati se),
  dative (javljati se/javiti se, sviđati se/svideti-svidjeti se), instrumental (baviti se). Word order
  caution: genitive/dative clitic pronoun objects with these verbs are subject to the usual clitic
  placement rules; this caution doesn't apply to instrumental objects since instrumental pronoun
  objects have only full forms.

### §88 The vocative case — full systematic rule set (ten conditions), well beyond lesson depth

The lesson likely gives only a handful of illustrative vocative forms; the reference grammar gives
the complete conditioning environment as ten numbered rules:

1. Most masc. nouns/male names ending in a consonant → `-e` (with stem-final `-k`/`-c`, `-g`, `-h` →
   `-č`, `-š`, `-ž` respectively: vuk→vuče, Bog→Bože).
2. Soft-consonant-stem masculines → `-u` (prijatelj→prijatelju).
3. Multi-syllable nouns in `-h`, and some personal names → `-u` (siromah→siromahu).
4. Masc. nouns ending in a vowel → nominative form (unless ending in `-ica`; see 8–9).
5. Nouns in `-r`: monosyllabic → `-e` (car→care); others → `-u`; stems in `-ar`/`-ir` can take either
   (poštar→poštare or poštaru).
6. Feminine `-a` nouns → `-o` (žena→ženo).
7. Two-syllable names (male or female) → `-o` ONLY if the root vowel is long-rising; otherwise
   nominative form is used (this includes certain kinship terms).
8. Two-syllable `-ica` nouns, pejoratives (male or female), or non-diminutive `-ica` object-nouns →
   `-o` (ptica→ptico, pijanica→pijanico).
9. Other `-ica` nouns, including affectionate male terms → `-e` (učiteljica→učiteljice).
10. Feminine consonant-final nouns → `-i` (ljubav→ljubavi).

General accent rule: vocative accent shifts to the initial syllable in most nouns. All neuter nouns
and all plural vocatives (which take the Npl. form) use non-distinct forms.

### §89–93 Review sections — the reference grammar's core added value: full paradigm charts

These five sections are explicitly framed by the author as *review*, consolidating declension
patterns the textbook lessons introduce piecemeal, into complete case-by-case paradigm tables. This
is the single highest-value new content in Ch.7–9 for this project's purposes, since it gives
complete inflectional paradigms (all 7 cases × both numbers, or the relevant subset) in one place
rather than scattered lesson fragments. Key structural facts captured, condensed from the full charts:

- **§89a Masculine nouns**: animate/inanimate accusative-form split (Acc.=Gen. for animate, Acc.=Nom.
  for inanimate) governs the whole declension; fleeting -a- vowel behavior (dropped in all forms but
  Nsg./Gpl.); `-lac` human agent nouns show the *reverse* fleeting-vowel pattern (l precedes the vowel
  in Nsg., replaced by o elsewhere, returns in Gpl.); monosyllabic (and some disyllabic) nouns insert
  `-ov-`/`-ev-` before plural endings; `put` "road, way" exceptionally takes soft endings throughout
  in Bosnian/Serbian only (Croatian: hard endings, putom/putovi); nationality/origin nouns with
  singulative suffix `-in` drop it in the plural, e.g. Turčin (sg. stem Turčin- throughout singular)
  → Turci/Turcima/Turaka (plural, stem reverts to Turk- with regular consonant shifts) — a compound
  example the author uses to show several rules interacting at once.
- **§89b Neuter nouns**: NAsg./NAsg. never distinguish Nom./Acc.; two NAsg. ending classes (`-o` →
  Isg. `-om`; `-e` → mostly Isg. `-em`); a subgroup of `-e`-nouns adds an extra syllable — `-en-`
  throughout all non-NAVsg. cases (vreme/vrijeme, ime, breme, rame) or `-et-` in singular only, not
  plural (dete/dijete, jaje, personal names, animal-young names, `-če` diminutives); oko "eye" and
  uho/uvo "ear" are neuter in the singular but shift to a feminine plural (cross-referenced to §111a,
  outside this chunk's range — noted but not itself extracted here).
- **§89c Feminine nouns**: two entirely separate declensions (in `-a` vs. in a consonant/`-Ø`), not
  variants of one pattern; the `-a` declension is the ONLY BCS declension with a distinct Asg. form
  (unlike Nsg. or Gsg.) and the only one where vowel length itself distinguishes case (long Gsg./Isg.);
  Gpl. of `-a` nouns is usually `-a` but a substantial, not fully predictable subset takes `-i`
  instead; a few (mostly body-part nouns) take Gsg. `-u`. Irregular nouns: kći "daughter" (stem/Asg.
  kćer, all other endings built on that stem) and archaic mati "mother" (stem/Asg. mater).
- **§89d Personal-name declension**: names in `-a` follow feminine-`-a` declension regardless of
  bearer's gender-marking elsewhere; male names in `-e` follow regular masculine declension if
  trisyllabic or non-nickname-disyllabic, but nickname-disyllabic `-e` names (Brane←Branko) follow the
  neuter jaje-type declension (Asg.=Gsg. since animate); male names in `-o` (mostly disyllabic) split
  by accent — long-rising accent (mostly nicknames) → feminine-`-a` declension; falling accent →
  regular masculine. Female names not ending in `-a` are indeclinable (an increasingly accepted
  naming pattern per the author). Surnames in `-ski` decline as adjectives; combined
  name+surname phrases take vocative only via the honorific (Gospodine/Gospođo), not on the surname
  itself.
- **§90 Irregular plurals**: dete/dijete "child", brat "brother", gospodin "sir, gentleman" all pluralize
  using **feminine-singular-in-`-a`** endings (deca/djeca, braća, gospoda) — endings AND agreement
  pattern look grammatically singular-feminine despite being semantically plural, but they still
  trigger plural verb agreement. Stem changes accompanying this: dete/dijete t→c; brat t→ć; gospodin
  loses the `-in` suffix.
- **§91 Adjective declension — descriptive vs. pronominal, and the short/long/longer three-way
  system**: descriptive adjectives have short (NAsg./NApl./counting form, all genders; other sg. cases
  in masc./neut. except Isg.), long (all forms; the default), and longer (Gsg./DLsg. masc.-neut.,
  DLIpl. all genders — used when the adjective substitutes for a noun, or optionally for emphasis,
  somewhat more common in Croatian) variants. The short/long distinction historically tracked
  indefinite/definite meaning but no longer reliably does. Pronominal adjectives split into
  behaviorally distinct subclasses: long-form-only (koji, svaki, neki, koliki, mnogi, čiji + its
  derivatives — with optional Serbian-only contracted Gsg./DLsg./Isg. forms of koji, e.g. koga→kog);
  demonstratives ovaj/taj/onaj (masc. NAsg. looks short since it's consonant-final, plus a distinct
  counting form); kakav/takav/onakav (short NAsg.-NApl., long elsewhere); sav "all" (exceptional soft
  `-e`-type endings, and in some cells only a longer form exists, not a plain long one); possessives
  split into moj/tvoj/svoj/naš/vaš (short NAsg.-NApl., long elsewhere, with contracted Gsg./DLsg.
  variants for the first three: mog, mom) vs. njen/njezin/njegov/njihov/-in/-ov derived possessives
  (short NAsg.-NApl. AND short masc.-neut. Gsg./DLsg., long elsewhere; Croatian favors the short
  forms overall, Bosnian/Serbian favor long, with no meaning difference).
- **§92 Pronoun declension**: personal pronouns have full and clitic forms for 4 of 6 non-vocative
  cases (Loc. and one further case have full forms only; Instr. has full+longer only, no clitic); no
  vocative case for pronouns at all; masc./neut. merge except Nsg. 3rd person; all genders merge in
  3rd plural. The reflexive pronoun (sebe/se/sebi/sobom) rhymes completely with 2sg. forms but has no
  nominative; se is historically an accusative singular reflexive form, retaining that transparent
  reading only in true reflexive verbs (cf. §97), not in most other se-verb classes. A "false friends"
  table flags formally identical short pronoun/clitic/conjunction forms that are easy to confuse (te
  as fem. NApl./Gsg. "this" vs. te as conjunction "and, then" vs. te as 2sg. clitic; ti as full 2sg.
  vs. Npl. masc. "these" vs. 2sg. dative clitic; svom "all" (Isg. fem.) vs. svom=svojem (DLsg.
  masc.-neut. "own"); kom/koga/komu (interrogative "who") vs. formally identical contracted forms of
  koji "which").
- **§93 Prepositions review**: a full alphabetized master list of every preposition introduced through
  Ch.8, cross-referenced by case (accusative: kroz, na, o, u, uz, za; genitive: bez, blizu, do, iz,
  kod, nakon, od, oko, pored, posle/poslije, pre/prije, s(a), ispod, ispred, iza, između, iznad;
  dative: k(a), nasuprot, prema; locative: na, o, po, pri, u; instrumental: nad, pod, pred, s(a), za) —
  the genitive is by far the most common case governed by a preposition. Word-order note (93b): niko/
  nitko and ništa are written as single words when they're a verb's direct object, but split apart
  with the preposition inserted between the negative prefix `ni-` and the rest when they're a
  preposition's object (ni o čemu "about nothing") — this splitting applies ONLY to the negative
  `ni-` prefix, never to the indefinite `ne-` prefix (nekog, nečemu stay intact).

### §95 Future tense — fuller paradigm and semantic subtlety than lesson level

- **Full auxiliary paradigm** with clitic, full, negated, and (Croatian) alternate two-word negated
  forms (ću/ćemo...; hoću/hoćemo...; neću/nećemo...; ne ću/ne ćemo... [C]).
- **Infinitive-auxiliary fusion in spelling**: when the future-tense infinitive begins the sentence,
  Croatian always writes infinitive and clitic auxiliary as two words (čitat ćeš), while Serbian fuses
  them into one orthographic word, with -ti + ć → -ćeš spelling (čitaćeš) and -sti + ć → šć spelling
  (prevešćete); Bosnian historically preferred the Serbian spelling but now favors the Croatian one;
  -ći infinitives are ALWAYS written separately regardless of variety.
- **want vs. will ambiguity** (95c): because hteti/htjeti serves as both the future auxiliary and the
  ordinary verb "want," the same surface sentence can carry either reading; disambiguation is mostly
  contextual, secondarily intonational (future reading stresses the infinitive/its adverbial; volition
  reading stresses the auxiliary itself), and in negated sentences, structurally: negated + bare
  infinitive → future reading; negated + da + present → "don't want" reading. This structural split is
  best preserved in Bosnian; blurred in Serbian (which tends toward da+present for both meanings) and
  Croatian (which tends to avoid da+present in either meaning even though grammar technically requires
  it for the volition sense).
- **ima/nema → biti in the future**: present-tense existential "there is/isn't" (ima/nema) has NO
  future-tense form of its own — the future of existential sentences switches to a form of biti
  entirely (dan as ima leda → future: danas će biti leda), a rule explicitly flagged by the author as
  a common learner trap.
- **Aspect in the future is unrestricted**: unlike the present tense (where perfective is confined to
  narrower syntactic contexts), both aspects occur freely in the future tense, governed purely by the
  same general aspectual meanings (progress/repetition/generality vs. single-completed-action focus).

### §96 Derivation of aspect partners — systematic typology, not given at lesson level

A verb form is modeled as `[prefix]-stem-[suffix]-theme vowel-ending`. Two derivation mechanisms:

- **By suffixation** (imperfective always ends up in the a-conjugation): (1) theme-vowel change alone
  (spremiti→spremati); (2) theme-vowel change + final stem-vowel o→a (odgovoriti→odgovarati); (3)
  theme-vowel change + inserted suffix -v- (završiti→završavati, dati→davati); (4) theme-vowel change
  + regular consonant softening (staviti→stavljati, vratiti→vraćati); (5) theme-vowel change + final
  stem-vowel shift + REVERSE consonant shift (položiti→polagati, skočiti→skakati); (6) theme-vowel
  change + replacement of the perfective-marking consonant `n` (from -nuti verbs) by an unrelated
  consonant (krenuti→kretati, skinuti→skidati).
- **By prefixation**: often changes meaning enough to create an effectively new verb (requiring its
  own derived imperfective partner in turn) — but a smaller set of prefixes add ONLY the meaning
  "perfective," with no other semantic change (piti→popiti, jesti→pojesti, prati→oprati,
  pisati→napisati, češljati→počešljati, brisati→izbrisati, buditi→probuditi). Which prefix (if any)
  will act as a "pure perfectivizer" for a given verb must be learned per-verb; it is not predictable
  from the prefix's other meanings.

### §97 Reflexive verbs proper, distinguished from the broader "se-verb" category

The author explicitly flags that many BCS grammars misleadingly call ALL se-verbs "reflexive" — true
reflexives are a narrow subset where `se` functions as a genuine direct object substitutable by full-
form `sebe` without change of meaning, referring to actions performed on one's own body. Listed pairs
(reflexive vs. transitive "do X to someone else" counterpart): oblačiti se/obući se ↔ oblačiti/obući
("get dressed" ↔ "dress someone"); svlačiti se/svući se ↔ svlačiti/svući; češljati se/počešljati se ↔
češljati/počešljati; brijati se/obrijati se ↔ brijati/obrijati; umivati se/umiti se ↔ umivati/umiti.
Broader transitive verbs can also take on genuine reflexive meaning with se if "oneself" substitution
still works (pogledati se u ogledalo "look at oneself in the mirror"); Croatian can mark this
indirect-reflexive sense with the dative clitic si instead of full-form sebi (kupit će si nove cipele).

### §98 Review of se-verbs — full functional taxonomy, and the intransitive/causative pattern

Consolidates every se-function encountered: verbs that don't exist without se; reflexive (§97);
reciprocal (§87a); inclinational/desiderative (§82); passive; and — highlighted as one of the most
productive patterns — **marking an otherwise-transitive verb as intransitive**, where removing se
yields a **causative** ("cause X to happen") reading of the same root: vratiti se "return [intr.]" /
vratiti "return [tr., give back]"; ljutiti se "get angry" / ljutiti "anger [someone]"; buditi se "wake
up" / buditi "awaken [someone]"; radovati se "rejoice" / radovati "make [someone] happy"; javiti se
"get in touch" / javiti "announce." A further function is marking a sentence **impersonal** (3sg. se
+ verb, no identifiable subject, corresponding to English "one .../you..." generic statements): ne
može se tako govoriti "you can't talk like that"; tamo se dobro jede "the food's good there [= one
eats well there]."

### §99 Object clauses with (to) što — a construction not typically drilled at lesson level

`što` functions as: interrogative pronoun "what" (in the spelling `što`, alternating with `šta` in
B/S); a conjunction meaning "that" after verbs/adjectives of emotion (radujem se što si tu "I'm glad
you're here"); part of compound conjunctions (kao što, zato što); and — the point highlighted here —
introducing a full clause functioning as a sentence's subject or object, often preceded by the neuter
pronominal adjective `to` for clarity (to što = roughly "that which," corresponding to uses of English
"what" that aren't questions: ne mogu ocijeniti to što radiš "I can't evaluate what you're doing").

### §101 The stati/stajati prefixed-verb family as a vocabulary-building pattern

Presented explicitly as a lesson in how prefixation builds vocabulary rather than as new grammar: a
single perfective/imperfective pair, stati (1sg. stanem) / stajati (1sg. stajem), hosts a wide set of
semantically important prefixed derivatives (see Vocabulary table above: izostati/izostajati,
nastati/nastajati, nedostajati, nestati/nestajati, ostati/ostajati, postati/postajati,
prestati/prestajati, pristati/pristajati, sastati se/sastajati se, ustati/ustajati).

### §102 Word order and the future tense — the six Y-subslots applied to future clitics

Recaps the six clitic subslots within Y (Y-1 li, Y-2 be-auxiliary [including the future clitic], Y-3
dative, Y-4 accusative-genitive, Y-5 se, Y-6 je) and shows the future auxiliary clitic always occupies
Y-2 — including when Serbian orthography fuses it onto a fronted infinitive (Javi-ću written as one
word: the infinitive stem occupies X, the fused `-ću` still counts as occupying Y-1's neighboring
slot for word-order purposes). When Serbian replaces the future infinitive with da + present, the
sentence structurally splits into two XYZ sequences: the first carries only the Y-2 future clitic, the
second (introduced by da) carries the remaining Y-3/Y-4/Y-5 clitics that depend on the lower, "real"
verb.

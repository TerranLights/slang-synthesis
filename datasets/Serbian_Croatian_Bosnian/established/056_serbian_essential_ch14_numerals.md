# Serbian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Ch. 14 Numerals

**Source:** Lila Hammond, *Serbian: An Essential Grammar* (Routledge, 2005), Chapter 14 "Numerals,"
printed pp. 255–281 (PDF pages 269–296). Third reference book for this language; Serbian-specific
(not comparative B/C/S), Cyrillic-primary with parallel Latin transliteration throughout.

**Coverage note.** Basic numbers roughly 1–10 and scattered basic date/time vocabulary have already
appeared across `established/003–022` (the flagship lesson-based textbook). Per this dispatch's
instruction, the most elementary cardinal 1–10 vocabulary is **skipped as redundant** below, but this
chapter's actual scope is much broader and systematic — full cardinal/ordinal declension rules,
fractions, collective numerals, number nouns, multiplicatives, approximatives, distributives,
frequentatives, weights/measures, age expressions, and the full days/months/date/time-word system —
and none of that is duplicated elsewhere in this project's corpus, so it is extracted in full as
new/high-value content. All structural numbers (tens, hundreds, thousands, powers of a thousand) are
kept because their formation (vowel changes, noun-based declension) is itself the grammar point.

No handwritten marginalia encountered — this source's PDF has a genuine text layer (`pdftotext
-layout` used throughout for the full chapter range). Note: the Cyrillic-script glyphs in this PDF
render as scrambled Latin/symbol characters in the text layer due to a custom font encoding baked
into the file (a known, source-specific extraction artifact affecting this book only, not a content
issue — confirmed already in `established/049` for this same source). The parallel Latin-
transliteration column printed alongside each Cyrillic example was used as the reliable Term source
throughout, per this dispatch's instruction to treat the Latin-script form as primary. Where the
correct Cyrillic spelling is independently known with confidence (these are common, unambiguous
Serbian numeral/calendar words), it is given in the Notes column rather than duplicated as a
separate row; a small number of less common derived forms are left without a Cyrillic note where
independent confirmation wasn't warranted from the corrupted glyphs alone.

## Grammar points

### §14.0 Four numeral forms

Serbian distinguishes four numeral categories with distinct morphology and syntax: cardinal numbers
(jedan, dva...), ordinal numbers (prvi, drugi...), collective numerals (dvoje, troje... — for mixed-
gender groups), and number nouns (dvojica, trojica... — for male-human-only groups; there is no
equivalent number-noun form for female-only groups). (p. 255)

### §14.1 Cardinal numbers and their declension

Numbers behave in three grammatically distinct ways. **Jedan** ("one") behaves as a true adjective,
agreeing fully in gender and case, and takes a singular, gendered verb. **Dva/tri/četiri** ("two,
three, four") and the numeral **oba** ("both") have partial declension/agreement behavior of their
own (see §14.1.2). **Pet and above** ("five" onward, plus all numbers ending in 5–9 or 0) are
followed by the genitive plural and are themselves treated as singular neuter regardless of what
they quantify — the verb that follows is singular neuter (though a plural, gender-agreeing verb is
also acceptable "for semantic congruency," per the source, though used less frequently). Sto
("hundred"), hiljada ("thousand"), milion ("million") and milijarda/bilion ("billion") are true nouns
carrying their own number, gender, and case, not invariable numeral words. (pp. 255–264)

### §14.1.1 Numeral jedan ("one") — six additional uses beyond the literal cardinal

Beyond counting, jedan is used as: (a) an indefinite article "a/an"; (b) an adjective meaning
"same," which can then take a plural form (jednih — "of the same [parents]"); (c) with plural-only
nouns (pluralia tantum, e.g. pantalone "trousers") that have a singular referent; (d) as an
independent pronoun "some" (jedni... a jedni... — "some... and some..."); (e) in the fixed
expression jedan jedini "one and only"; (f) as an expression of affection or anger addressing
someone directly, in the vocative (e.g. "Budalo jedna!" "You fool!"). All numbers ending in "one"
(except the compound "eleven," which ends in -naest) follow jedan's singular-agreement pattern. The
negative nijedan ("not one, none") behaves identically to jedan but requires a negated verb. (pp.
258–260)

### §14.1.2 Numerals dva/tri/četiri and oba ("two/three/four/both")

Dva and oba have two gender forms: masculine/neuter nouns and adjectives following them take the
ending -a (when the number itself is undeclined), while feminine nouns/adjectives take -e (regular
feminine stems) or -i (irregular consonant-final feminine stems). Both numbers decline through the
full case system, with distinct masculine/neuter and feminine paradigms (nominative dva/oba vs. dve/
obe; genitive dvaju/obojе vs. dveju/obeju, etc. — see source pp. 260–262 for the full table). Tri and
četiri do not inflect for gender, but the nouns/adjectives following them take the same case-form
pattern as after dva. Although tri and četiri technically decline, in practice their declension is
replaced by the nominative form preceded by the appropriate preposition; for masculine/neuter nouns
they can alternatively be replaced by the corresponding collective numeral (§14.4). With dva/oba/tri/
četiri, verbs are plural, generally taking the masculine ending -a rather than a strictly
gender-matched ending (though the gender-matched form is not considered incorrect). The same rules
extend to all compound numbers ending in 2, 3, or 4 (except 12, 13, 14, which are -naest compounds
following the "five and above" pattern instead). (pp. 259–263)

### §14.1.3 Numerals pet ("five") and above

Numbers five through nineteen, and all numbers ending in 5–9 or 0, take adjectives/nouns in the
genitive plural, while the number itself is grammatically singular neuter (governing a singular
neuter verb) — though a plural verb agreeing with the noun's actual gender is also possible, described
as less common but used "for semantic congruency." Numbers 11–19 are formed by suffixing -naest to
1–9. Sto, hiljada, milion, milijarda are true nouns and decline with number/gender/case; hiljadu/
stotinu in the singular take a feminine-accusative-style -u ending. (pp. 263–264)

### §14.2 Ordinal numbers and their declension

Ordinal numbers function as definite adjectives, agreeing in gender, number, and case with the noun
they qualify. Except for 1–4 (prvi, drugi, treći, četvrti — all irregular), ordinals are formed by
suffixing -i (masc.), -o (neut.), -a (fem.) to the cardinal stem; treći ("third") is the sole
exception taking -e rather than -o in the neuter. Sedam and osam lose their final -a before the
ordinal suffix (sedmi, osmi). In compound numbers, only the final digit takes ordinal form; the
preceding numbers stay cardinal (e.g. "dvadeset prvi" "twenty-first"). Sto, hiljada, milion have
irregular ordinal forms: stoti, hiljaditi, milioniti. Written numerically, ordinals are followed by a
full stop (e.g. "2003."). Ordinals answer date questions ("when?") with the following noun in the
genitive. The cardinal jedan plus the ordinal drugi together form reciprocal expressions ("one
another, each other"), e.g. "Jedan drugog su zagrlili" "They embraced each other." For "for the
Nth time," the preposition po precedes all ordinals except prvi ("first"). (pp. 265–267)

### §14.3 Fractions and decimal numbers

Fractions beginning with "one" use a feminine-singular-agreeing derived noun ending in -ina (e.g.
jedna polovina "one-half"). Fractions beginning with two, three, or four use the feminine-plural
cardinal form plus a derived noun ending in -ine (e.g. dve trećine "two-thirds"). Fractions
beginning with five or higher keep the ordinal number unchanged, with the derived noun taking -ina
(e.g. pet osmina "five-eighths"). In writing, Serbian uses full stops for thousands separators and
commas for decimal points — the reverse of English convention; spoken decimals use koma or zarez for
the decimal point (dva koma tri "2.3"), with zeros both written and spoken normally. (p. 267)

### §14.4 Collective numerals

Collective numerals denote a group of mixed-gender persons or animals, ranging from two to
ninety-nine. Numbers ending in 2 or 3 (except twelve) take the suffix -oje (dvoje, oboje, troje);
all others (except numbers ending in one) take -oro (četvoro, petoro... dvadesetoro). Compound
collectives follow the -oje/-oro rule based on their final digit (e.g. trideset petoro "35," from
petoro). Collective numerals are also used with collective nouns ending in -ад/-ad in the nominative
singular (e.g. sedamnaestoro jagnjadi "seventeen lambs"). The collective noun deca ("children")
cannot take ordinary cardinal numbers — for 2–4 it takes the genitive singular of dete ("child")
instead (dva deteta), while five and above require a collective numeral (petoro dece). Collective
numerals are grammatically neuter singular and govern a neuter singular verb; the noun (or
determiner/pronoun) following is in the genitive plural. Smaller collective numbers decline through
more cases in practice; larger ones are generally used only in the nominative/accusative. (pp.
267–269)

### §14.5 Number nouns

Number nouns (formed with the suffix -ица/-ica) apply exclusively to groups of male human beings —
there is no equivalent form for all-female groups. They can be formed for any number except one
(and any number containing it), up to but excluding 100, and belong to the third declension; the
noun that follows is in the genitive plural, and the verb is plural with a past participle in -a
(the masculine -i ending would also not be incorrect). Beyond the number-noun pattern proper, other
cardinal/ordinal-derived nouns use the suffixes -ица/-ica, -ац/-ac, -ка/-ka for related concepts
(e.g. sedmica "a week"/"the figure seven," jedanaesterac "a penalty kick," dvojka "the figure two").
(pp. 269–270)

### §14.6 Multiplicatives

Multiplicatives ("twofold, threefold...") are formed by joining a collective-numeral stem to
-струки/-struki ("-fold") via a linking -o- (jednostruki, dvostruki, četvorostruki...). They function
either as declinable, gendered adjectives or as invariable adverbs. Dupli ("double") is a separate,
more common word for the same concept in everyday use, with trodupli ("triple") derived from it. (p.
270)

### §14.7 Approximatives

Two distinct approximative constructions exist. The first attaches the suffix -ак/-ak to any
"round" numeral from 10 through 100 (10, 20, 30... 100) as well as the teens, yielding "approximately
N" (desetak "about ten," petnaestak "fifteen or so"); nouns following take the genitive plural. The
second covers approximate quantities around non-round numerals, formed by joining the number to the
next ascending number with a hyphen (pet-šest "five or six," dve-tri "two or three"); nouns following
this second type follow ordinary cardinal-number agreement rules rather than genitive plural. (pp.
270–271)

### §14.8 Distributives

Distributive numbers express division into equal parts. They are formed by placing the preposition
по/po before an ordinary cardinal number, indicating how many parts of a whole were distributed to
each recipient (e.g. "Deca su dobila po dva parčeta torte" "The children got two pieces of cake
each"). Po itself does not affect the gender/case of the following number or noun; the number agrees
with the noun following ordinary cardinal-number rules. (p. 271)

### §14.9 Frequentatives

Frequentatives ("two times, three times"...) are formed by joining a cardinal number to the word
пут/put ("time(s)") as a single fused word (dvaput "twice"). With an ordinal number instead, the two
words stay separate and the ordinal is typically preceded by po (except for prvi "first"): "po treći
put" "for the third time." An alternative construction uses пута/puta with a stand-alone cardinal
number (dva puta "twice," equivalent in meaning to dvaput). Frequentatives behave as invariable
adverbs. (pp. 271–272)

### §14.10 Weights and measures

The metric system is used throughout for weights (gram, kilogram — colloquially shortened to kilo —
and tona), length/distance (milimetar, santimetar, metar, kilometar), and liquid measures
(decilitar/deci, litar). Пола/pola ("half") precedes the unit noun the same way it precedes hours in
time expressions (§14.13.1). (pp. 272–273)

### §14.11 Age

Age can be asked and stated three ways: "Koliko godina ima [X]?" (lit. "How many years does X have,"
X in nominative), "Koliko je [X] star/stara?" ("How old is X," X in nominative, agreeing adjective),
and "Koliko je [X-dat.] godina?" (lit. "How many years is it to X," X in dative). For someone whose
birthday is imminent or has just passed, a distinct idiom asks how many years they have "completed/
filled" (napuniti), e.g. "Koliko si godina napunio?" "How many years have you completed?" (pp.
273–274)

### §14.12 Days, months and dates

Days of the week and months of the year are not capitalized in Serbian (unlike English). Some
weekday names are masculine (ponedeljak, utorak, četvrtak, petak) and some feminine (sreda, subota,
nedelja). "A week" as a single unit is nedelju dana or sedmicu dana (nedelja doubles as "Sunday" and
"week"); more than one week uses the cardinal number with nedelje. The preposition у/u + accusative
answers "on what day?" (u ponedeljak "on Monday"). All months are masculine; those ending in -бар/
-bar have a "fleeting a" reflected through the cases (oktobra, oktobru). Juni and juli lose their
final -i when case endings are added (juna/jula, junu/julu). "A month" as a single unit is mesec
dana; more than one month uses the cardinal with meseca. The preposition у/u + locative answers
"when?" for months (u januaru "in January"). Dates use ordinal numbers functioning as definite
adjectives; in compound date numbers, only the final digit is ordinal, the rest stay cardinal (e.g.
"dvadeset prvi maj" "21 May"). Dates in answer to "when?"/"on what date?" use the genitive case with
no preceding preposition in Serbian (unlike English "on"), e.g. "Bili smo u Londonu osmog januara"
"We were in London on the eighth of January." Written numerically, dates are followed by a full stop
(e.g. "2003. godina"). (pp. 274–277)

### §14.13.1 Telling the time

Time uses a cardinal number with čas (hour, specifically for 24-hour-clock expressions), sat
(hour/o'clock, general use), i po ("half past"), or pola ("X minutes to," referring to the half of
the upcoming hour — not the one just passed) plus a form of do ("to/until"). "Hour" and "minute"
after dva/tri/četiri take the -a ending (dva sata, tri minuta); five and above take the genitive
plural (pet sati, deset minuta), except when the compound number's final digit is 1–4. The words
sat/čas and minuta can be dropped once the numerical time reference is otherwise unambiguous. The
question "Koliko je sati?" ("What is the time?") is answered as illustrated; "U koliko sati?" ("At
what time?") is answered with the preposition u plus the same time constructions. (pp. 277–280)

### §14.13.2 Time-related words and expressions

A systematic set of adverb/adjective pairs covers relative time reference: jutros/jutrošnji ("this
morning"/"this morning's"), danas/današnji ("today"/"today's"), večeras/večerašnji ("this evening"/
"this evening's"), noćas/noćašnji ("tonight or last night, depending on utterance time"/its
adjective), ujutro-ujutru/jutarnji ("in the morning"/"morning" adj.), danju/dnevni ("in the day"/
"daily"), uveče/večernji ("in the evening"/"evening" adj.), prekjuče/prekjučerašnji ("day before
yesterday"/its adjective), sinoć/sinoćnji ("last night"/its adjective), sutra/sutrašnji
("tomorrow"/"tomorrow's"). Adverbs and adverbial expressions in this set are undeclined and carry no
gender/number; the paired adjectives decline normally. The bare instrumental case of a day name
expresses regular, repeated occurrence on that day (nedeljom, ponedeljkom, etc. — "on Sundays," "on
Mondays"). For "every/last/next [time unit]," the words svaki/prošli/idući are used with the
genitive case and the gender ending appropriate to the time-unit noun (svakog sata "every hour,"
svake srede "every Wednesday," svakog januara "every January"). (pp. 280–281)

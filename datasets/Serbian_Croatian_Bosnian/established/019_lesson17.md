**Source:** Ronelle Alexander and Ellen Elias-Bursać, *Bosnian, Croatian, Serbian, a Textbook: With
Exercises and Basic Grammar* (2nd ed., 2010), Lesson Seventeen, pp. 261–276 (PDF pages 282–297).

**Coverage note:** every grammar point and every distinct vocabulary item from this lesson's `A1`–`A4`
and `C1` sections, plus every explicit `[B]`/`[C]`/`[S]`-style dialect tag the source itself marks —
including tags embedded in exercise/PITANJA sentences, not only the formal vocabulary boxes, since the
extraction spec flags dialect annotation as frequently the highest-value content. Repeated drill
instructions (`B1`–`B5`, `C4`, `C5` exercise prompts) and duplicate example sentences that introduce no
new vocabulary or grammar are skipped. This PDF has a clean text layer (`pdftotext` produced usable
text for the full page range) — no vision-reading was required, so Vision Reading Confidence is `n/a`
throughout and no marginalia-exclusion concern applies.

**Table-construction note:** where the source presents the *same* Ekavian/Ijekavian phonological
doublet (the systematic Serbian-vs-Bosnian/Croatian jat reflex, e.g. `vera`/`vjera`) or an explicit
B/C/S lexical-choice triplet for one concept (e.g. `historija`/`povijest`/`istorija`), both/all forms
are combined into a single table row with the tags recorded in **Attested Region**, rather than split
into duplicate rows that would only repeat an identical gloss and identical metadata columns.

## Grammar points

### Regional lexical variation for "history": historija / povijest / istorija

The three PITANJA (question) tracks on p.262 use three different words for "history": `hìstorija`
[B], `povijest` [C], and `ìstorija` [S]. A running-text note on p.263 explains further: Bosnian usage
itself vacillates between `ìstorija` and `hìstorija`, though Bosnian grammarians and most recent usage
now favor `historija`. The authors flag this as apparent change in progress, and use it to explain why
the modern Bosnian poet Ferida Duraković uses `ìstorija` in her poem (p.261) even though the textbook's
own Bosnian lessons elsewhere use `historija`. The same note (p.263) also cites a second, independent
example of lexical change over time: the title word `veče` in A. G. Matoš's poem (Lesson 18) is spelled
in a way "no longer encountered in modern Croatian," which now uses only `večer`.

### Three past-tense forms in older/literary texts: compound past, aorist, imperfect

A boxed grammar note on p.264 (keyed to the poem "Zemlja") explains that many older texts use all three
Serbian/Croatian/Bosnian past-tense forms together, each contributing a distinct shade of meaning to
"past": the **compound past** (perfect) relates general background information (e.g. `nàrodi su u nebo
vjerovali` "peoples believed in heaven"); the **aorist** relates specific foregrounded events in
sequence (e.g. `vjere pòumriješe; mi sàznadosmo` "religions died out one by one [and] we [then]
realized"); the **imperfect** relates action that continued over a specified span of time (e.g.
`òstajahu` "remained"). The note cross-references p.189 and an earlier numbered grammar section [151]
for fuller treatment. This system is drilled directly afterward in exercise C2 (p.274), where students
tag each verb in a short excerpt from the Serbian Bible translation (Genesis 1:1–5, translated by Đuro
Daničić, Vienna 1909) as aorist or imperfect.

### Attested syntactic variation: infinitive vs. da + present

Exercise C4 (p.275) supplies parallel B/C and S versions of the same sentences that show the
well-known modal-construction split: Bosnian/Croatian favor the bare infinitive after a modal
(`Treba odmah ìći u trgòvinu...` [B,C] "[One] should go right away to the store..."), while the
Serbian version replaces the infinitive with a `da` + present-tense clause (`Треба одмах да се иде у
ра̀дњу...` [S] "[One] should [that one] go right away to the store..."). The same pattern recurs in
C4 ex.4: `Može se òtvoreno govòriti...` [B,C] vs. `Може о̀творено да се гово̀ри...` [S]. This is not
newly explained in this lesson's own prose (it is assumed known from earlier lessons), but the B/C-vs-S
tags on these exercise sentences make the pattern explicit source-marked data.

### Attested variation in question formation and interrogative/relative pronouns

Exercise sentences across this lesson's PITANJA sections consistently show two further B/C/S splits
that the book tags explicitly but does not re-explain in this chunk: the interrogative/relative pronoun
`šta` [B,S] vs. `što` [C] ("what"), e.g. p.234 "Šta se ... dèšava" [B,S] vs. "Što se ... dògađa" [C];
and `ko` [B,S] vs. `tko` [C] ("who"), e.g. C1 ex.8a "bìlo ko" [B,S] vs. "bìlo tko" [C]. Yes/no question
formation also varies: B3 ex.2 pairs "Da li je to tvoj dàidža?" [B] and "Да ли је то твој ујак?" [S]
(both `da li` + declarative word order) against "Je li to tvoj ujak?" [C] (`je li` + inversion).

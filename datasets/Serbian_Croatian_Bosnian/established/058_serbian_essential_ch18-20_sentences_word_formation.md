## Grammar points

### §18.1 — Elements of a sentence

A sentence generally has a subject (nominative case, usually a noun/noun phrase) and a predicate
(the verb and everything following it). Subject and verb agree in gender and number. A verb may
require a direct object (accusative) and/or an indirect object (dative). A complement follows
copula-like verbs ("to be," "to appear," "to feel," "to seem," "to become," "to look," "to
think") and can be a noun phrase, an adjective/adjective phrase, or a prepositional phrase; the
verb "to feel" (`osećati se`) specifically governs the instrumental. Adverbials, which modify the
verb, can likewise be a noun phrase, an adverb phrase, or a prepositional phrase. Serbian has no
equivalent of English "the"/"a" — definiteness is instead conveyed positionally (elements closer
to the sentence start, after enclitic placement, read as more definite) and via verbal aspect
(imperfective `piše pismo` "is writing a letter" vs. perfective `napisala pismo` "wrote the
letter," the latter implying a specific, hence definite, letter). The numeral `jedan` "one," used
as a modifier, can convey the sense of the indefinite article "a/an."

### §18.2 — Types of clauses

A clause contains a subject and a verb. Main clauses are independent and can stand alone as
complete simple sentences; subordinate clauses are dependent, give additional information about
a main clause, and are generally linked to it by a conjunction. Subordinate-clause types
identified: relative clauses (introduced by "who"/"which" equivalents, e.g. `koju`, `koja`) and
interrogative clauses (embedded questions, e.g. `koga ste videli?` "whom did you see?").

### §19.1 — Word order

Word order is generally flexible in Serbian, with the fixed exception of enclitic placement
(cross-referenced to the book's own Chapter 13 "Enclitics," not re-derived here). The source
illustrates this with a full-sentence pair that can invert its major constituents (subject +
verb phrase vs. fronted prepositional phrase) while remaining grammatical and roughly
synonymous, differing mainly in topicalization/emphasis.

### §19.2 — Punctuation

Full stops are used at sentence end, after most abbreviations (see the abbreviation table
above), after ordinal numbers, after numbers/Roman numerals in list markers, and to divide large
numbers (Serbian uses a period where English uses a comma: `1.000` = English `1,000`). Full stops
are *omitted* after certain honorific-title abbreviations (`dr`, `mg`, `gđa`, `gđica`), after
most all-capital abbreviations/acronyms (`NATO`, `UN`, `EU`), and after cardinal numbers. Commas
are used to divide/include/exclude sentence parts, to set off discourse markers, to set off
appositions to the subject, before subordinate clauses, usually after the conjunctions `a`/`ali`
("but"), to separate clauses containing verbal adverbs, and before non-restrictive relative
clauses — but a comma is *omitted* before the coordinating conjunctions `i` ("and") and `a`
("and," in its non-adversative sense). Quotation marks follow English conventions except that the
opening quotation mark sits on the baseline rather than suspended above it (`„...`, i.e. a
low-9-style opening quote); quotation marks are also used for names/titles of things (hotels,
publications).

### §19.3 — Simple sentences

A simple sentence expresses one complete thought with one subject and one predicate; it is
incomplete if either is missing. The reflexive pronoun `se` can form an impersonal sentence with
no stated subject, paralleling English generic "one" (`Putuje se` "One travels"). A simple
sentence has exactly one predicate.

### §19.4 — Complex sentences

A complex sentence is built from two or more clauses: either two or more independent
clauses/simple sentences, or at least one independent clause plus one dependent clause. Two
dependent clauses alone cannot form a complex sentence — at least one independent clause is
required. A complex sentence has two or more predicates (contrasted with the simple sentence's
exactly-one).

### §20.1 — Word formation in general / prefixes

The source frames word formation as expressing new meaning by adding prefixes, infixes, or
suffixes to an existing root word, illustrated with `kiša` "rain" (noun) → `kišan` "rainy"
(adjective, `+ suffix -n`) → `kišobran` "umbrella" (`kiš-` from `kiša` + linking `-o-` + `-bran`
from `braniti` "to guard"), and `braniti` "to guard" → `branilac` "defender" / `branik`
"(car) bumper" / `branilački` "defending" via different suffixes on the same root. These
formative elements are called "productive" because they remain usable to coin new words today.
Some words stack several formative elements (`stan` "flat" → `stanar` "tenant" →
`stanarina` "rent"). §20.1 then tabulates the 14 core verb-forming prefixes (see the vocabulary
table above for the full inventory with derived-word examples and their per-prefix senses).

### §20.2 — Suffixes

§20.2.1 organizes noun-forming suffixes by source category: from verbal roots (agent nouns via
`-ac/-lac/-ač/-ar/-lica/-lja`; event/condition nouns via `-aj/-ba/-va/-(k)lje/-nja`), from
adjectival roots (person-with-trait nouns via `-ac/-ak/-ik/-jak`; abstract trait nouns via
`-ina/-je/-oća/-ota/-ost/-stvo`), and from other nouns (place nouns via
`-ana/-ara/-ište/-nica/-njak`; professional-person nouns via `-ar`; demonym nouns, masculine via
`-ac/-anac/-anin/-in` and feminine via `-ica/-inja/-ka/-kinja`; young-offspring nouns via
`-ić/-če`; diminutives via `-(č)ić` (m.), `-ica` (f.), `-ce` (n.); pejoratives via `-ina` for all
genders). A separate compounding pattern joins two independent nouns/adjectives via a linking
vowel `-o-` or `-e-` (e.g. `jugozapad`, `severoistok`, `mnogobožac`). §20.2.2 covers adjective
suffixes: the basic `-k`/`-n` pair (with regular `-a`/`-o` gender-ending replacement), possessive
suffixes (`-in, -ji, -nji, -šnji, -ski, -ački, -eški, -ov, -ev`), and augmentative suffixes
denoting "having a lot of" the underlying noun (`-av, -at, -ovit`).

## Grammar points

### Ijekavian (Croatian) vs. ekavian (Serbian) jat reflex

The book's core comparative axis: wherever Common Slavic jat historically stood, standard Croatian
reflects it as `ije` (long jat) or `je` (short jat), while standard Serbian reflects it as `e` in
both cases. Long-jat examples from Lesson 1: `lijèp/lép` ("beautiful"), `slijèdeća/slèdeća`
("following"). Short-jat examples: `djèvojka/dèvojka` ("girl"), `vjèžba/vèžba` ("exercise"),
`rjèčnik/rèčnik` ("dictionary"), `čòvjek/čòvek` ("man, person"). p. 2-4.

### Emphatic vs. unemphatic copula

`je` ("is") and `sam`/`ste` ("am"/"are") are unemphatic, unstressed forms used in ordinary
statements. `jèst` (Cr.)/`jèste` (S.) is the emphatic counterpart of `je`, used for contrast or as
a standalone affirmative answer — the dialogue answers "Jè li ùdata?" with a bare "Jèst."/"Jèste."
meaning "Yes, [she is]." p. 2-3.

### tkò/kò ("who")

Croatian `tkò` vs. Serbian `kò` for the interrogative "who," tagged explicitly in the vocabulary
list and used in the dialogue's "Tkò/Kò je tá djévojka/dévojka, Péro?" p. 2-3.

### Yes/no questions with *li*

`li` marks a yes/no question, but its placement differs by standard in a way that echoes the
pattern already documented from the Alexander & Elias-Bursać textbook (`established/
001_lesson01_basics.md`): the Croatian dialogue line asks "Jè li ùdata?" (li directly after the
copula), while the Serbian line asks "Dà li je ùdata?" (`da li` fronted, copula after). Two
independent sources now attest the same broad Cr./S. word-order preference for this construction.
p. 1-3.

### Personal pronoun use for emphasis or disambiguation

Pronouns are normally dropped because verb morphology (person/number endings, or in the copula's
case, the unemphatic forms `sam`/`si`/`je`/`smo`/`ste`/`su`) already carries the necessary
information — hence `gòvorim` alone means "I speak/am speaking." An explicit pronoun is added either
for emphasis (`já gòvorim`, "I am the one speaking") or to disambiguate the third person, where
`Kàko se zòve?` is preferred over the ambiguous-by-gender `Kàko se ón/òna zòve?` unless the speaker
specifically wants to stress "he" or "she." p. 5.

### Adjective gender agreement

Adjectives inflect for the noun's grammatical gender: masculine, neuter, feminine. `dòbar` (masc.),
`dòbro` (neut.), `dòbra` (fem.) is the model triplet given for Lesson 1. The source's own footnote
explains why it lists forms in masc./neut./fem. order specifically: for most other adjectives the
neuter and masculine forms are identical, so giving the neuter right after the masculine makes any
exception (like `dobar`/`dobro` here, which do differ) easy to spot. p. 2.

### No vowel reduction

Unlike Russian (and unlike English's schwa-reduction of unstressed vowels), Croatian/Serbian has no
reduced vowels: every vowel is pronounced fully as spelled regardless of stress. The book's example:
`gòvorite` ("you speak") is pronounced with the same full vowel quality on every syllable, whereas a
Russian cognate-shaped word would reduce its unstressed vowels. Framed as a pronunciation-habit
warning for English/Russian-background learners who might otherwise slur unstressed syllables.
p. xii (preface).

### Accent placement and false friends (preface)

Accent in Cr&S does not necessarily fall on the final syllable (`sèstra`, `mikròskop` given as
accentuation examples). The preface also flags "false friends" with Russian as a source of possible
confusion/embarrassment for Russian-background learners: `urók` (Russian "lesson") vs. `ùrok` (Cr&S
"a spell/charm"), and `ponós` (Russian "diarrhea") vs. `pònos` (Cr&S "pride"). p. xii.

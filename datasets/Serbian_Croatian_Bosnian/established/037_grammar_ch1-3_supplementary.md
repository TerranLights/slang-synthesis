## Grammar points

### The book's accent notation system (Ch. 1 §2b, §4, p. 3, 7-8)

Every BCS word carries one accent (a few small words are unaccented and lean on a neighbor; some
long words carry a secondary accent too). Two independent parameters layer onto the accented
vowel: **length** (long vs. short) and **tone** (rising vs. falling). This book's own notational
convention, used consistently through the whole grammar: all long vowels are underscored, and all
rising-tone vowels carry a grave accent; an unmarked vowel defaults to short + falling tone on the
first syllable. Bosnian is stated to preserve the full accent/length/tone system most faithfully
among the three standards (giving it, per the author, "a special, very melodic flavor"); actual
spoken Serbian and Croatian accentuation can be derived, as a rough approximation, from the
Bosnian-based marking system by ignoring most long vowels in unaccented syllables and by ignoring
the rising/falling distinction in short syllables. This whole notational layer is absent from the
Lesson 1-3 textbook material, which does not mark accent in this way.

### Ekavian/ijekavian (jat-reflex) correspondence (Ch. 1 §3, p. 4-5)

The historical Slavic vowel *jat* surfaces today as one of two regional/standard-level reflexes:
ekavian (jat → -e-, geographically eastern) or ijekavian (jat → -je- or -ije-, geographically
western/southern). Croatian and Bosnian use only ijekavian; Serbian officially accepts both, with
ekavian predominant and more prestigious, though western Serbian speakers use ijekavian. This is
explicitly a *geographic*, not ethnic/national, split. Two additional precision points beyond what
the Lesson files note: (1) the correspondence is not fully regular — e.g. ekavian `beležnica`
"notebook" corresponds to ijekavian `bilježnica`, not the expected `*bjelježnica`, so individual
words must be learned, not derived by rule; and (2) not every surface `-e-` is an ekavian-marked
segment nor every `-ije-` an ijekavian one (though nearly all `-je-` sequences are). When ekavian
`-e-` corresponds to ijekavian `-ije-`, the `-ije-` sequence counts as a *single complex vowel* (as
if a diphthong) for accentual purposes — e.g. `lijep` is treated as monosyllabic with its
(obligatory) falling accent on that single complex vowel, not as two syllables.

### The XYZ model of clitic placement (Ch. 1 §12a-b, p. 13-15)

The Lesson files describe "clitics go in second position" in prose; this chapter formalizes it as a
named three-slot template: **X** (the first significant unit of the sentence/clause) — **Y**
(clitics, in a fixed internal order, with the question particle `li` always first among them) —
**Z** (everything else, possibly empty). The core claims: X and Y are always filled whenever clitics
are present in the sentence (Z may be empty); the conjunction `da` and other subordinating
conjunctions occupy X *by themselves* and force clitics to follow them directly with no exceptions;
the two conjunctions meaning "and" (`i`, `a`) cannot occupy X alone and must be followed by another
word before Y begins; and every clear speech pause (marked by a comma in writing, or an
imperceptible-but-real prosodic break in longer sentences) restarts the X-Y-Z reckoning from zero.
This model is used throughout the rest of the book (per the author) to teach clitic placement, and
is a genuinely more systematic apparatus than the "second position" description given piecemeal in
the Lesson files.

### The 7-case system and 3 declensions, overview (Ch. 1 §18, p. 21-22)

Formal naming/abbreviation table not given as such in the Lesson files: nominativ (Nom.), genitiv
(Gen.), dativ (Dat.), akuzativ (Acc.), vokativ (Voc.), instrumental (Instr.), lokativ (Loc.) — each
abbreviated with two further sub-abbreviations, one for the case *meaning* (e.g. "Gen.") and one for
the specific singular/plural *form* (e.g. "Gsg.", "Gpl."). Two syncretism facts flagged explicitly:
dative and locative share the same singular form (labeled DLsg.), and dative, locative, and
instrumental share the same plural form (labeled DLIpl.) — so despite 7 named cases, nouns have
fewer than 14 distinct surface forms. There are three declension classes: (1) masculine and neuter
nouns other than masculine nouns in `-a`, sharing one basic declension with internal variation; (2)
nouns ending in `-a` (both feminine and the small class of masculine `-a` nouns, e.g. `gazda`,
`tata`, `vođa`, `sluga`); (3) feminine nouns with a zero ending (`stvar`, `ljubav`-type), a wholly
separate declension from both of the above.

### Additional present-tense conjugation types (Ch. 2 §14b-d, §30b, p. 17-18, 34)

Beyond the pisati/kupovati/moći/davati types already captured from Lesson 2/3, this chapter gives
four more named patterns not drilled in the Lesson files:
- **pisati/kazati type**: e-conjugation; infinitive theme vowel `-a-` preceded by a consonant that
  shifts before present-tense endings (`pisati` s→š: `pišem`; `kazati` z→ž: `kažem`). Most verbs of
  this type shift a rising infinitive accent to falling in the present.
- **videti/vidjeti type**: i-conjugation; infinitive theme vowel `-e-`/`-je-` (ekavian/ijekavian);
  most verbs keep the same accent between infinitive and present, with `voleti/voljeti` "to love" as
  an exception that shifts to falling in the present.
- **piti type**: infinitive `-ti` added directly to a root with no theme vowel; present tense inserts
  `-j-` before the e-conjugation endings (`pijem, piješ, pije, pijemo, pijete, piju`). The root ends
  in `-i-` in nearly all verbs of this type; `čuti` "hear" (1sg. `čujem`) is a stated exception.
- **teći type**: present stem ends in `-k`, which shifts to `-č` in all forms but 3pl. (`tečem,
  tečeš, teče, tečemo, tečete, teku`) — explicitly parallel to `moći`'s `-g`→`-ž` shift, except that
  `moći` alone also shifts in 1sg.

### Fuller accusative singular paradigm (Ch. 2 §21a, p. 23-24)

Worked noun+adjective paradigm tables across all four gender/animacy classes (masc. animate, masc.
inanimate, neuter, feminine), more complete than the Lesson 2 sketch: e.g. `dobar pas` (Nsg. short)
→ `dobrog psa` (Asg.), `dobar grad` (Nsg. short, inanimate) → `dobri grad` (Asg. = Nsg. long form,
unchanged from nominative), `dobro ime` → `dobro ime` (unchanged), `dobra žena` → `dobru ženu`. Also
notes explicitly that a masculine noun like `gazda` "landlord" — grammatically masculine despite its
`-a` ending and `žena`-type declension — still takes masculine adjective agreement (`loš gazda`, not
`*loša gazda`), reinforcing that agreement tracks grammatical gender, not surface noun-ending shape.

### Accent shift in accusative/plural of certain masculine nouns (Ch. 2 §21b, Ch. 3 §32d, p. 24, 38)

Beyond the ordinary Nsg./Asg. alternation, certain masculine nouns with a long final vowel and a
rising accent on the preceding vowel show the accent *shift forward one syllable* in Asg. and (per
Ch. 3) throughout the plural — e.g. Nsg. `pas`→Asg. `psa` (fleeting vowel only), but Nsg. `junak`
"hero" → Asg. `junaka` / Npl. `junaci`, where a short rising accent on the first syllable becomes a
long rising accent on the second. This accentual class (distinct from the fleeting-vowel and
consonant-shift phenomena) is named explicitly as its own pattern in Ch. 3 §32d, with further
examples `svedok/svjedok`→`svedoka/svedoci`, `komad`→`komadi`, `fakultet`→`fakulteti`,
`problem`→`problemi`.

### Soft-consonant inventory (Ch. 2 §32f as cross-referenced; Ch. 3 §32f, p. 39)

The consonants that trigger `-ev-` (rather than `-ov-`) before plural endings, and `-e-` (rather
than `-o-`) in masculine/neuter adjective endings, are given as one explicit closed list: **all
consonants written with a diacritic mark in the Latin alphabet** (`č š ž ć đ dž nj lj j`) **plus
`c`**. Framed by the author as a spelling-based mnemonic specific to BCS Latin orthography.

### The -in "singulative" suffix (Ch. 3 §32g, p. 39-40)

A masculine-noun suffix (historically related to English "one") marking "one member of a
group/category" — used for many ethnonyms/religious-group names (`Srbin`, `Bugarin`, `Arapin`,
`Jevrejin`, `kršćanin`/`hrišćanin`) and, combined with the additional suffix `-an-`, for city-of-
residence demonyms (`Beograđanin`, `Zagrepčanin`, `Splićanin`, `Novosađanin`, `Podgoričanin`, and
the general noun `građanin` "citizen"). The suffix is **dropped entirely** in the plural (`Srbin` →
`Srbi`, not `*Srbini`) — the singular is the morphologically marked form, not the plural. Feminine
counterparts drop `-in` and add a feminizing suffix, usually `-ka` (all listed examples except
`Srpkinja`).

### The fleeting-a/consonant-shift chain in otac-type and zadatak-type nouns (Ch. 3 §32c, p. 37-38)

A more precise mechanical account than the Lesson files give of what happens when fleeting -a- is
lost from nouns like `otac` "father" or nouns in `-tak`/`-dak`: (a) the fleeting vowel drops when a
non-Nsg. ending is added; (b) an exposed `d` immediately adjacent to `t` re-voices to `t` (so `-dk-`
→ `-tk-`); (c) before the Npl. `-i` ending, a stem-final `-k` shifts to `-c` as usual; (d) the
resulting `-tc-` sequence simplifies in both pronunciation and spelling to plain `-c-`. The chapter
gives the explicit ordered rule-application: `zadatka` → `zadatci` (intermediate, not attested in
writing) → `zadaci` (attested spelling). Only two masculine nouns (`pas`, `san`) have fleeting -a-
in the *root* itself rather than in a suffix; the vast majority of fleeting-a- nouns carry it in a
suffix, most predictably `-ac` or `-ak`.

### Nouns with more than one grammatical gender (Ch. 3 §31a, p. 35-36)

A small, named class of BCS nouns can take more than one gender, sometimes with an associated sense
split. Example: `bol` "pain" — masculine `težak bol` typically means physical pain, feminine `teška
bol` typically means spiritual anguish, per most speakers' usage (not a rigid rule). The commonest
example is "evening": outside a greeting context it is usually feminine (`večer`), but within the
fixed greeting "Good evening" it is attested in all three genders (`Dobar večer` masc., `Dobro veče`
neut., `Dobra večer` fem.) — with Serbian preferring `Dobar veče`, Croatian preferring `Dobra
večer`, and Bosnian accepting either.

### Vi: singular-referent agreement asymmetry (Ch. 3 §37b, p. 44)

When `vi` addresses a single person politely, a **predicate adjective** agrees with `vi`'s
grammatical plural status (`Vi ste vrlo ljubazni, gospodine` — "you [pl. agreement] are very kind,
sir"), but a **predicate noun** reflects the real-world fact of a single referent and appears in the
singular (`Vi ste ljubazan čovjek` — "you are [singular] a kind person," not `*ljubazni ljudi`).
This asymmetry between adjective and noun agreement under polite `vi` is not addressed in the Lesson
1-3 files.

### The se-verb mechanism: intransitivization and passivization (Ch. 3 §38, p. 45-46)

Beyond the word-order rule for the clitic `se` (already covered in the Lesson files), this chapter
gives the semantic/syntactic mechanism by which `se` operates on a verb, explicitly cautioning that
calling `se` a "reflexive particle" and its verbs "reflexive verbs" is imprecise — genuinely
reflexive `se`-verbs (where `se` means "oneself") are only a small subset. The two broader mechanisms
named: (1) **intransitivization** — adding `se` to a transitive verb strips its ability to take a
direct object, e.g. `zvati` "to call [someone]" (transitive) → `zvati se` "to be called, have as
one's name" (intransitive; cannot take a direct object); (2) **passivization** — adding `se` to a
transitive verb promotes what would have been its object into subject position, e.g. `kazati`
"say [something]" (active) → `kaže se` "it is said, one says" (passive, used only in 3sg.). Both
`zove se` and `kaže se` are flagged as frequent enough to be learnable as fixed expressions before a
learner has internalized the general mechanism.

### Word order, continued: multi-word X-slot phrases (Ch. 3 §39, p. 46-47)

Extends the XYZ model (Ch. 1 §12b) with facts about the X slot being filled by more than one word:
an adjective+noun combination can jointly occupy X (`Takva stvar je rijetka` — "such a thing is
rare"). When the X-slot phrase is adjective+noun modified by only a single adjective, **Croatian**
frequently splits the pair — keeping the adjective in X and shifting the noun into Z (`Takva je
stvar rijetka`) — a pattern also possible in Serbian/Bosnian but markedly less frequent there,
flagged by the author as one of the sociolinguistic markers distinguishing Croatian from
Serbian/Bosnian usage. When the adjective portion is a *question word*, this split becomes
near-obligatory for nearly all speakers with `kakav` (obligatory) and strongly preferred with `čiji`
(vastly preferred), while `koji` allows either order with the non-split order strongly preferred
(`Koja devojka je tvoja sestra?` "Which girl is your sister?" over the split `*Koja je devojka tvoja
sestra?`).

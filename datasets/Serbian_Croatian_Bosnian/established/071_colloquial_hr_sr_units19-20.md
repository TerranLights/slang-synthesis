## Grammar points

### Formation of the conditional (p.205-206)

A compound tense = active past participle (same form used for the perfect) + the aorist enclitic of
**biti** (*bih, bi, bi, bismo, biste, bi*). Two word orders are possible depending on whether the
clause opens with the pronoun/other preceding word: `Ja bih čitao` or `Čitao bih` — same meaning.
Negative: `ne` goes directly before the auxiliary (`Ne bih čitao`). Interrogative: either `da li` +
aux + participle, or — the Croatian-preferred pattern — aux + `li` + participle (`Bih li došao?`).
An interrogative can be answered with the bare auxiliary alone (`Bismo.`/`Bi.`).

### Conditional for habitual past action and softened requests (p.206-207)

Beyond hypothetical "would," `bih` etc. also expresses (a) habitual repeated past actions
(`Pročitao bi po pet knjiga svakog tjedna` — "He would read some five books each week") and (b)
softened/polite requests, statements, and commands (`Nešto bih vas pitao` — "I would (like to) ask
you something").

### Conditional clauses — real conditions (p.207)

Real ("if it happens, then...") conditions use `ako` + the same tense as English would use, unlike
the special hypothetical constructions below.

### Hypotheses — unreal and potentially-realisable conditions (p.209)

Two categories: (a) **unreal conditions** (the condition wasn't/won't be met) — main clause verb is
conditional, and the subordinate "if" clause is introduced by `da` + appropriate tense
(`Da si ovdje, vidio bi ga`), an inversion pattern roughly paralleling English "Were you here...".
(b) **potentially realisable conditions** ("if he were to come...") — subordinate clause introduced
by `kad` or `ako` + conditional (`Kad bi došao na vrijeme, mogli bismo ga vidjeti`).

### Reflexive passive (p.210)

One of the main ways to express passive voice: the reflexive form of an otherwise-transitive verb
turns the transitive object into the reflexive-clause subject (`Pije li se pivo kod vas?` — "Is beer
drunk in your country?"; `Ovdje se ne govori engleski` — "English is not spoken here").

### Reflexive verbs expressing "I feel like" (p.211)

A distinct reflexive-plus-dative construction expresses a felt urge/inclination: `Meni se jede nešto
s roštilja` ("I feel like eating [something grilled]"), `Spava mu se` ("He's feeling sleepy").

### "bilo" + pronoun/adverb = English "-ever" (p.211)

Adding the particle `bilo` to pronouns/adverbs conveys "-ever": `bilo tko` "whoever," `bilo što`
"whatever," `bilo gdje` "wherever," `bilo kad` "whenever" (`Donesi mi bilo kakvu knjigu` — "Bring me
any kind of book whatever").

### Aspect selection guided by features of English (p.212-215, "Aspect 4")

A cumulative rule-of-thumb list for choosing imperfective vs. perfective aspect based on English
cues: (1) inherent verb meaning (ongoing-type verbs like "rest/sleep/listen" tend imperfective;
punctual-type verbs like "say/switch on/glance" tend perfective); (2) sense-impression verbs
("see/hear") + `kako` + present tense require the imperfective (`Čuo sam ga kako prilazi`), while an
English infinitive construction after the same verbs tends to indicate perfective
(`Vidio je kako je predsjednik predao dokumente`); (3) English progressive tenses must be translated
with the imperfective; (4) certain adverbs cue one aspect or the other (imperfective: "constantly,"
"all the time," "regularly," "a long time"; perfective: "at last," "all at once," "suddenly,"
"immediately," "abruptly"); (5) some conjunctions cue aspect by context (`kad` can go either way;
`dok` "while" cues imperfective; `čim` "as soon as" cues perfective); (6) "for"-phrases (process
emphasis) take the imperfective, "in"-phrases (completed totality) take the perfective
(`Pisali smo pisma tri sata` vs. `Napisali smo pisma za tri sata`).

### Participles — overview (p.219, Unit 20)

Croatian/Serbian has four participles: two verbal adjectives (active past participle, already
covered in earlier units; passive past participle, covered below) and two verbal adverbs/gerunds
(present, denoting simultaneous action; past, denoting prior action).

### Present gerund (p.219-220)

Formed by adding **-ći** to the 3rd person plural present-tense form of an imperfective verb
(`govore` → `govoreći` "speaking"; `pišu` → `pišući` "writing"). Genuinely rare outside certain fixed
uses. The present gerund of `biti` is built on the perfective 3pl `budu` → `budući`, frequently used
as the conjunction "since" (`Budući da smo u Hrvatskoj...` — "Since we are in Croatia..."). Certain
present gerunds (`budući` "future," `idući` "next," `slijedeći` "following," `tekući` "current"
[literally "flowing," from `teći`]) have become fully adjectival and are declined like ordinary
adjectives, unlike the strictly-indeclinable gerund proper.

### Past gerund (p.220)

Formed by adding **-v** (after a vowel stem) or **-vši/-avši** (after a consonant stem) to the
infinitive stem (`ustati` → `ustavši`; `reći` → `rekavši`; `moći` → `mogavši`). Rarely used; a full
clause is more common where English would use "Having done X..." The past gerund of `biti`, `bivši`,
survives productively as an adjective meaning "former" (`bivšom suprugom` "former wife"). Note:
English's common "-ing" active-participle construction ("I heard the birds singing") must be
rendered with `kako` + present imperfective, not a gerund (`Čuo sam kako ptice pjevaju`).

### Passive past participle (p.221-222)

The other verbal adjective; formed from the infinitive stem of either aspect, with regular patterns
by infinitive ending: `-ati` → `-an` (`čitati` → `čitan`); `-iti`/`-eti`/`-jeti` → `-jen`
(`vidjeti` → `viđen`; `učiniti` → `učinjen` — a `j`-triggered consonant shift, see Appendix §6 per the
source); `-nuti` → `-nut` (`prekinuti` → `prekinut`); `-eti` verbs with present in `-mem`/`-nem` →
`-et` (`zauzeti`/`zauzmem` → `zauzet`; `prokleti`/`prokunem` → `proklet`).

### Verbal nouns (p.222)

Many passive participles become nouns by adding **-je**: `osloboditi` → `oslobođen` → `oslobođenje`
("liberation"); `čitati` → `čitan` → `čitanje` ("reading"). Intransitive verbs, which cannot form a
passive participle, instead take **-anje/-enje** directly on the infinitive stem: `kupati se` →
`kupanje` ("bathing"); `sjediti` → `sjedenje` ("sitting"); `putovati` → `putovanje` ("travelling").

### Word order — enclitics (p.224-227, "Word order 5")

The enclitic cluster order is fixed: interrogative `li`; verbal-clitic pronoun D/A(G); `se`; `je`.
Multiple enclitics must stay together in this order and are normally placed after the first element
of the sentence/clause — which can be a single word or a whole phrase (`Jedan moj prijatelj mi je
rekao...`). `ne` + verb counts as a single word for this purpose. The linking conjunctions `i`, `a`,
`ni` never count as a separate "first word." Breaking a phrase to insert enclitics strictly after
its first word (rather than after the whole phrase) reads as literary/pedantic. Interrogative words
like `koliko`/`koje` that don't form a phrase with what follows are themselves treated as the "first
word." Clause-introducing words (`da`, `što`, `ako`, relatives, interrogatives) always take the
enclitic cluster immediately after them. When a subordinate clause precedes the main clause, the
main clause's enclitics attach to the first word of the main clause — often felt as awkward, so
Croatian/Serbian frequently inserts the placeholder pronoun `to` to carry that role instead
(`Da si ga tajno viđala, to mi je jasno`).

### Word order — basic vs. context-driven order (p.227-229, "Word order 6")

Because case marking (not word order) signals grammatical role, subject/verb/object order is
comparatively free — all six permutations of `Dala sam nosaču prtljagu` are grammatical, each with a
different emphasis. Neutral/basic order mirrors English SVO. Context-driven order follows the
general principle that less-informative material precedes more-informative material (which is also
why enclitics, themselves low-information, get placed early); this licenses object-fronting in cases
where English would use a passive construction instead (`Tu kuću je sagradio poznati arhitekt` ≈
"That house was built by a famous architect"). "Scene-setting" adverbial phrases (time/place/manner)
go at the *start* of a Croatian/Serbian sentence, the reverse of English's typical end-placement.
Adverbs that quantify/qualify the verb's action generally *precede* the verb
(`Jako lijepo svira klavir`). Low-informational-value adverbs/pronouns (`nešto, negdje, nekako,
nekud, tu, ovdje, jučer, danas, sutra, ovo, to, sad, često, stalno`) normally precede the verb too;
moving one after the verb adds special emphasis and can shift the sentence's meaning
(`Daj da ti nešto pokažem` "let me show you something" vs. `Daj da ti pokažem nešto` "let me show
you at least something").

### Fixed word-order phrase patterns (p.229)

Vocative phrases often reverse the neutral adjective-noun order: `Budalo jedna!` "You fool!",
`Zlato moje!` "My treasure." Possessive phrases follow a fixed demonstrative/numeral +
possessive + noun pattern: `taj moj prijatelj` "that friend of mine," `dva naša znanca` "two
acquaintances of ours."

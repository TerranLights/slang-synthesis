# Serbian/Croatian/Bosnian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Colloquial Serbian and Croatian, Units 7-9 (U restoranu / Na ulici / Posjet prijateljima)

**Source:** Celia Hawkesworth, *Colloquial Croatian and Serbian: The Complete Course for Beginners*
(Routledge, 1998). Units 7-9, printed pages 74-107 (PDF pages 82-115 of
`source_reference/languages/Serbian, Croatian, Bosnian/03.Colloquial Serbian and Croatian.pdf`).
Unit 10 ("Obiteljski odnosi") begins on printed page 108 / PDF page 116 and is out of scope for
this chunk.

**Vision-reading note:** this PDF has no usable text layer for these pages and was vision-read in
full, page by page, at 150 dpi. Every page in the range was inspected for handwritten marginalia
(pencil/pen notes, underlines, filled-in exercise answers by a previous owner) per the extraction
spec's vision-reading guard. **No handwritten marginalia was found on any page in this range** —
all printed content, all consistent typeset font. One page (p.95, PDF p-103) is a reproduced city
map of Zagreb with no extractable vocabulary text and was skipped for that reason (not marginalia).

**Coverage note:** every grammar point and every distinct vocabulary item in Units 7-9 is captured
below, including the exhaustive dialogue vocabulary glosses the book itself supplies inline after
each dialogue/reading passage. Drill exercises (fill-in-the-blank, translation exercises) were
skipped per the coverage rule except where they introduced vocabulary items not otherwise listed
(none did — exercise vocabulary was a subset of dialogue/reading-passage vocabulary already
captured). This book is explicitly framed as "colloquial" — register/informality annotations and
idiomatic expressions the source itself flags are prioritized and called out specifically below
(see Notes column and the "Register and idiom highlights" subsection under Grammar points).

A real restaurant drinks/food menu ("Trocadero", p.82/PDF p-90) is reproduced in the book as
realia; its vocabulary (drink names, sandwich names) is included since it is genuine attested
usage, not pedagogical invention, but menu items are proper-noun-adjacent brand items and were
extracted only where they are ordinary common nouns (e.g. `sendvič`, `lepinja`) rather than brand
names.

## Grammar points

### Adjectives: genitive case (Unit 7, p.76-77)

Masculine/neuter singular genitive definite adjective ending is **-og(a)** (or **-eg(a)** after a
"soft" stem consonant); the optional final **-a** is used stylistically, e.g. when the adjective
falls at the end of a sentence. An indefinite masculine/neuter form also exists (ending **-a**) but
the book notes it is rarer and mainly needs to be recognized, not produced. Feminine genitive
singular ending is **-e** (matching feminine noun genitive). Genitive plural, all genders, is
**-ih**. p.76-77.

### The partitive genitive (Unit 7, p.77)

The genitive case of a noun (with agreeing adjective) expresses "some"/"any" of a whole, e.g.
`Imate li svježeg kruha?` "Have you any fresh bread?" p.77.

### Adjectives: accusative case (Unit 7, p.78-79)

Masculine accusative singular follows the animate/inanimate noun distinction: inanimate = same as
nominative, animate = same as genitive. Neuter accusative singular = same as nominative always.
Feminine accusative singular ending is **-u**. Plural accusative, all genders, matches the noun's
own plural accusative and makes no animate/inanimate distinction. p.78-79.

### Adjectives: dative/prepositional case (Unit 8, p.87-88)

Masculine/neuter definite dative-prepositional singular ending is **-om**; an indefinite form
(identical to the noun's own dative-prepositional) also exists but again only needs recognition.
Feminine ending is **-oj** — this is also the ending used by country names that are themselves
grammatically adjectives (`Engleska → u Engleskoj`; all country names ending `-ska`/`-čka` are
adjectives). Dative/prepositional plural for all genders is **-im(a)**, which is (per the book's own
explicit note) identical to the instrumental plural ending. p.87-88.

### The instrumental case of adjectives (Unit 8, p.90)

Masculine/neuter singular: **-im**. Feminine singular: **-om**. Plural, all genders: **-im**
(again identical to dative/prepositional plural). p.90.

### Prepositions governing the prepositional case (Unit 8, p.90-91)

**k/ka** "towards, to" (+ dative, despite the section heading — used with people, e.g. `k
prijatelju`); **prema** "towards, according to"; **pri** "by, near"; **o** "about"; **u** "in";
**na** "on". A crucial distinction is drawn between *static location* (prepositional/locative case)
and *motion* (accusative case) with the same preposition, e.g. `Stanujem u Zagrebu` (I live in
Zagreb, static) vs. `Putujem u Zagreb` (I am traveling to Zagreb, motion). p.90-91.

### Prepositions governing the instrumental case (Unit 8, p.91)

**među** "among", **nad** "above", **pod** "below", **za** "behind", **pred** "in front of". All of
these can instead take the accusative when expressing motion toward a position (e.g. `Stavljam
kofer pod krevet` "I'm placing the suitcase under the bed"). The book notes that in practice,
speakers more often use the alternative prepositions governing the genitive instead:
**između, iznad, ispod, iza, ispred** — and recommends learners use these genitive-governing forms
themselves. p.91.

### Use of reflexive verbs to express general statements (Unit 8, p.93)

A reflexive construction (`se` + 3rd person singular verb) is used the way English uses an
impersonal "one," e.g. `Kako se ide na Gornji grad?` "How does one get to the Upper Town?" —
`Ovdje se ulazi u kazalište` "One enters the theatre here." Many verbs can be made reflexive this
way for general statements. p.93.

### Formation of the past (perfect) tense (Unit 9, p.99-101)

The perfect tense — a compound tense formed from present tense of auxiliary `biti` + the active
past participle — is, per the book, one of only three tenses the learner needs to actively produce
(simple present, perfect, future).

**Active past participle formation:**
- (a) Verbs with infinitive in **-ti** (the majority class): drop `-ti`, add `-o` (m.sg.), `-la`
  (f.sg.), `-lo` (n.sg.), `-li` (m.pl.), `-le` (f.pl.), `-la` (n.pl.). E.g. `spavati` → `spavao,
  spavala, spavalo; spavali, spavale, spavala`.
- (b) In Croatian specifically, verbs with infinitive in **-jeti** have masculine singular
  participle **-io** (not `-o`), because dropping final `-ti` from `-je + o` sounds nearly identical
  to `-io` and is written that way, e.g. `vidjeti` → `vidio, vidjela, vidjelo`.
- (c) Verbs with infinitive in **-ći** or **-sti** form the participle more irregularly (e.g. `s` is
  dropped from `-sti`: `jesti → jeo, jela, jelo`; `-ći` verbs like `stići → stigao, stigla, stiglo`,
  `moći → mogao`, `reći → rekao`). The book recommends learning each of these participles
  individually as encountered. All compounds of `ići` follow the same pattern as `ići` itself
  (`išao`, `došao`, `ušao`, etc.).
- (d) `biti`'s own active past participle is regular: `bio, bila, bilo, bili`.

p.99-101.

### Word order 1 and 2 — enclitic placement with the perfect tense (Unit 9, p.101-102)

Because the short (enclitic) forms of `biti` must be placed immediately after the **first stressed
word** in the sentence, if a pronoun/subject is present the enclitic follows it (`Ja sam došla`); if
no subject is expressed, the enclitic follows the participle instead (`Došli smo`). In complex
sentences, this rule can place the auxiliary quite far from its participle (`Putnik je često...
izlazio da popuši cigaretu`). p.101-102.

### Past tense questions (Unit 9, p.102-103)

Two structures: (a) Verb + `li`, using the **long form** of the auxiliary when the verb is `biti`
(e.g. `Jesi li bila umorna?`); (b) `Da li` + verb — the book notes this construction is *not common
in Croatian* (an explicit register/dialect-comparative annotation), with the short auxiliary form
placed after `da li` (`Da li sam uspio?`). p.102-103.

### Negative of the past tense (Unit 9, p.103-104)

Negative auxiliary (`nisam`, etc., which are *not* enclitics) + active past participle; because the
negative forms aren't enclitics, the strict word-order placement rule doesn't apply, and the
negative auxiliary normally simply precedes the participle (`Nisam zvao`). p.103-104.

### Emphatic use of personal pronouns (Unit 9, p.104)

The long form of a personal pronoun is used when the pronoun itself is emphasized, e.g. `Meni daj
tu lozovaču` "Give the brandy *to me*." p.104.

### Present tense to express English present perfect (Unit 9, p.104)

Serbian/Croatian uses the simple present tense where English would use a present perfect for an
ongoing state/action, e.g. `U Zagrebu sam već tri tjedna` "I have been in Zagreb for three weeks
already." p.104.

# Serbian/Croatian/Bosnian — Established Grammar: Lila Hammond, *Serbian: An Essential Grammar*, Chapters 12–13

**Source:** Lila Hammond, *Serbian: An Essential Grammar* (Routledge, 2005), Chapter 12
"Conjunctions" (§§12.1–12.3) and Chapter 13 "Enclitics" (§13.1), printed pp. 240–254 (PDF lines
located via `pdftotext -layout` search for the "Chapter 12"/"Chapter 13"/"Chapter 14" running-header
text — printed page numbers differ from PDF page numbers because of this book's own front matter).
This is a **third** grammar reference for this language pairing, Serbian-specific rather than
comparative B/C/S, and printed with parallel Cyrillic/Latin columns throughout.

**Text layer / extraction note:** `pdftotext -layout` produced a clean text layer, but the PDF's
embedded font for this book substitutes several Latin diacritic letters with unrelated ASCII glyphs
in a consistent, decodable pattern confirmed by cross-checking known Serbian vocabulary against the
garbled output: **š→e, č→a, ć→z, ž→f, đ→]** (e.g. `eto`→`što`, `aim`→`čim`, `zemo`→`ćemo`,
`vafno`→`važno`, `do]e`→`dođe`). All Latin-script terms and examples below have been corrected back
to standard orthography using this decoded mapping rather than reproducing the garbled PDF text
directly. No vision-reading was needed (real text layer throughout), so the marginalia guard does
not apply.

## Grammar points

### 12.1–12.2. Coordinating and subordinating conjunction groupings

Paraphrased structural note, kept brief since the individual conjunctions themselves are captured
above and the underlying compound-conjunction and semantic mechanics are already covered in
Alexander §143 (established/041). Hammond organizes coordinating conjunctions (connecting two
independent clauses of equal status) into six sub-groups — "and," "but," "either/or," `da`,
"therefore," "although" — and subordinating conjunctions (connecting a dependent clause to a main
clause) into a parallel six sub-groups — time, comparison ("than"), "because," "if," "how," "in
order to" — plus a miscellaneous group and a separate group of conjunctions used in questions. This
is essentially the same coordinating/subordinating split Alexander's material assumes, presented at
the vocabulary-inventory level rather than the analytical level.

### 12.3. Differences in usage between što (relative conjunction) and koji (relative pronominal adjective) — kept in full as genuinely new nuance

Both `što` and `koji` translate English "which"/"who" in a relative clause linking a dependent
clause to a main clause, but the source draws a clear usage split based on **what the relative word
refers back to**:

- **`koji`** is used when "which"/"who" refers back to a specific **noun** in the main clause (e.g.
  "This is Jane *who* drives me to work," "...a novel *which* I enjoyed very much"). Since `koji` is
  grammatically an adjective, it agrees with that noun's gender and number and declines for case like
  any adjective, independent of the case its own clause requires — the same case-assignment mechanics
  Alexander's §136 (established/041) documents in more procedural detail (the antecedent/relative
  case-notation walkthrough).
- **`što`** is used instead when "which" refers not to a specific noun but to the **action or idea of
  the whole preceding clause** — i.e., the antecedent is really the verb/event just described, not a
  noun (e.g. "Jane drives fast, *which* scares me"; "The food finally arrived, *which* made us all
  happy"). Unlike `koji`, `što` in this use is invariable: it carries no gender, number, or case
  marking and never changes form.
- `što` as a relative conjunction is also used after `nešto` (something), `to` (that), `ovo` (this),
  `ono` (that, over there), and generally wherever the antecedent is unnamed rather than a concrete
  noun.
- Both words have other, unrelated uses outside this relative-clause contrast: `koji` also functions
  as an interrogative ("which?"), and `što` also substitutes for `da` ("that") after verbs of emotion
  or feeling (`brinuti se`, `radovati se`) and after dative-experiencer constructions (`milo`,
  `drago`, `žao`) — this second `što` usage is the same phenomenon Alexander's §143g–h analyzes as
  part of a broader `da`-vs-`što` (potentiality-vs-established-fact) semantic system; this file does
  not re-derive that deeper analysis, only notes where Hammond's simpler description of the same
  usage sits.

### 13.1. Order and importance of enclitics — genuinely new procedural rules only

Hammond's four-category enclitic hierarchy (interrogative `li` → verbal auxiliary enclitics →
pronominal enclitics [dative before genitive/accusative] → the reflexive `se`/verbal `je`, which take
last position) is the same fixed ordering already captured, in a more formal six-slot notation, as
the "XYZ model" in `038_grammar_ch4-6_supplementary.md` §9 (Y-1 `li` … Y-6 `je`). That underlying
model is **not** re-derived here. What Hammond adds beyond the ordering model itself is a set of
eight numbered usage rules; four of them add genuinely new procedural detail not stated in `038` or
in `044_grammar_ch19_clitic_accent.md`'s rhythmic-constituent placement framework:

- **An enclitic can separate a multi-word subject, but can never separate a preposition from the noun
  it governs, and can never separate a title from a person's name** (e.g. `Gospođa Jovanović mu je
  dala pismo` — the enclitic cluster `mu je` cannot be inserted between `Gospođa` and `Jovanović`).
  This specific title-plus-name prohibition is a level of detail beyond `044`'s general "obligatory
  rhythmic constituent" framework.
- **The coordinating conjunctions `a` and `i` can never themselves take the initial position that
  triggers enclitic placement**, unlike most other conjunctions and interrogatives (which, per `044`
  §164a, obligatorily trigger immediate post-conjunction clitic placement). After `a`/`i`, a sentence
  must either use the long/stressed pronoun forms instead of enclitics, or let a different word take
  the initial (clitic-triggering) position — e.g. `Oni je vole, ali mu to nije drago` and `Oni je
  vole, a njemu to nije drago` are both fine, but `*Oni je vole, a mu to nije drago` is not. This
  exception is not stated in `044`'s obligatory/optional-RC treatment.
- **The subject, when expressed, normally follows the enclitic cluster unless it is itself the
  sentence-initial word** — a general word-order default beyond the specific RC mechanics `044`
  covers.
- **In the negative past tense, the negated auxiliary verb (`nije`, `nisu`, etc.) is no longer treated
  as an enclitic at all** and can freely take first position in the sentence, with any other enclitic
  (e.g. a pronominal or reflexive one) simply following it in the normal cluster order (`Nije mi dao
  da platim` — "He didn't allow me to pay"). This negative-past-tense exception to enclitic status is
  not covered in `038` or `044`.

One further specific interaction is also new relative to `038`: when the reflexive enclitic `se`
and the 3sg. auxiliary `je` would co-occur in a clause, **`je` is generally omitted outright**, with
`se` effectively remaining in the final position on its own (`Upitala se (je) da li će on da dođe` —
"She asked herself whether he would come"). `038`'s existing note on a `je`/`je` clash covers a
different conflict — the accusative pronoun `je` ("her/it") competing with the auxiliary `je` ("is"),
resolved by substituting `ju` — not this `se`+`je` omission, which is a separate rule.

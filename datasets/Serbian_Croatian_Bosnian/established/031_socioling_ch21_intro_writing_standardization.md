### 168. Introduction — one language, more than one language

The chapter frames the book's second half (the sociolinguistic commentary) as a shift from grammar
description to the social, cultural, political, and historical context surrounding BCS. Alexander's
core framing claim: BCS is "simultaneously one and more than one" (p. 379) — one language at the
level of grammar and core vocabulary (mutual intelligibility among Serbs, Croats, Bosnians, and
Montenegrins is uncontested), while Bosnian, Croatian, and Serbian are, as of the book's 2006
writing, three officially recognized separate languages (a fourth, Montenegrin, was considered
likely to follow if Montenegro became independent — which did occur in 2006). The differences
separating Croatian and Serbian are described as "quite real and clearly identifiable"; those
separating Bosnian from either are "less in number and less striking in content" but still clearly
identifiable (p. 379).

### 169. Writing systems — three historical alphabets, competing symbolic associations

BCS lands have used three major historical alphabets: Glagolitic (the original, no longer in
active use), Cyrillic, and Latin. Each carries strong religious/communal association: Cyrillic with
Eastern Orthodoxy (Serbs, Montenegrins), Latin with Roman Catholicism (Croats) (p. 379). This
religious/political charge attached to alphabet choice recurs as a theme through the whole chapter
(e.g. 19th-century Serbian clerical opposition to alphabet reform partly because one new letter,
*j*, was seen as an import from the "distrusted and feared Roman Catholic alphabet," p. 382).

### 169a. The Glagolitic and Cyrillic alphabets — origin story

Glagolitic was devised in the mid-9th century by the brothers Constantine (later Cyril) and
Methodius at the request of the Moravian prince Rastislav, commissioned by Byzantine emperor
Michael III, so that Christian liturgy could be conducted in Slavic rather than Latin. Constantine
died in Rome in 869 after taking monastic orders under the name Cyril; Methodius died in 885.
Cyrillic itself was developed later and separately — not by the brothers, but by scribes at the
court of the Bulgarian king Boris, who adapted Greek letters to Slavic sounds rather than learn the
"exotic" Glagolitic; the resulting alphabet was named Cyrillic in Constantine-Cyril's honor (p.
379-380). Cyrillic became indelibly tied to the Serbian Orthodox church from the church's early
13th-century establishment onward, which the source identifies as the source of a persistent
conservative resistance to later alphabet reform in Serbia (p. 380). Regional/confessional
variants of these scripts developed distinct names in Bosnia: Christian Bosnians' distinctive
cursive Cyrillic became known as *bosančica*; Muslim Slavs developed an Arabic-derived script
called *arebica*; others used various Roman-alphabet forms collectively called *latinica* (p.
380).

### 169b. The Latin alphabet — Croatia's multi-graphic history and 19th-century standardization

Contrary to Latin being Croatian's sole historical script, Croats historically used all three
scripts depending on region: Cyrillic (*bosančica*) inland, Glagolitic on the coast (with two
regional sub-styles, *obla* "round" and *uglasta/uglata* "angular" glagoljica), and Latin. Glagolitic
carried particular symbolic weight in pre-modern Croatia as a marker of identity distinct from both
Orthodoxy and Roman Catholic dependency, and remained in localized use into the early 20th century
(p. 380-381). The Latin alphabet eventually became dominant for all non-Orthodox South Slavs, and
Orthodox Slavs use it alongside Cyrillic for practical/European-communication reasons (p. 381). Pre-
modern spelling conventions for Latin-script Slavic borrowed inconsistently from Italian (southern
areas) or Hungarian (northern areas) orthographic rules; the 19th-century pan-South-Slavic Illyrian
movement worked to standardize this, adopting some Czech diacritics (č, ž, š) and some letters
personally devised by movement leader Ljudevit Gaj — hence the modern informal name *gajica* some
Croats now use for the alphabet (p. 381).

### 170. Language standardization — two 19th-century reform movements

Modern BCS traces to two roughly contemporaneous (second-quarter-19th-century) reform movements:
the Vukovian movement (Serbia-centered, led by Vuk Stefanović Karadžić) and the Illyrian movement
(Zagreb-centered, led by Ljudevit Gaj). Both sought a "language of the people" that would unify
South Slavs and symbolize independence — political independence from the Ottomans for Serbs,
cultural independence within the Habsburg Empire for the Illyrians. The Vukovian movement became
the cornerstone of all standardization after 1859, though Illyrian heritage remained influential,
especially among Croats (p. 381-382).

### 170a. Vuk Karadžić and language reform

Vuk Karadžić (1787-1864), a monastery-educated son of a poor Herzegovinian family, became a
folklorist, lexicographer, and language reformer after fleeing to Vienna following the 1813
collapse of the Serbian uprising. His maxim "Piši kao što govoriš" ("Write as you speak") called
for abandoning the church-Slavic-heavy elite written language of the time in favor of a standard
based on spoken folk-epic language — specifically the ijekavian East Herzegovinian speech of his
own home region, which his travels (including to Montenegro and Dubrovnik) convinced him was
spoken over a broad enough area to serve as a national standard. He was strict about admitting only
naturally occurring East Herzegovinian forms into his dictionary and grammar. His spelling reform
(one sound = one letter) was actually developed by the cleric Sava Mrkalj (1783-1833), though it
became associated with Vuk's name; it faced fierce clerical opposition, partly because it dropped
~15 traditional letters and added 5 new ones, including *j* (seen as a Catholic/Habsburg import,
p. 381-382). Vuk's reforms were not fully accepted by the Serbian government until 1868 — four
years after his death (p. 383). Today's BCS is described as based on Vuk's language (p. 383).

### 170b. The Illyrian movement and language reform

Unlike Vuk's individual activism, the Illyrian movement was a Zagreb-based collective aiming to
promote South Slavic (over Latin, Hungarian, Italian, or German) as a shared cultural-expression
language for all South Slavs, not just Croats. Their name choice ("Illyrian") drew on the belief
that Croats descended from the ancient Illyrians, plus the term's prior use for South Slavic
Christians and its historical use as a Roman provincial name and a Napoleonic province name (p.
383). Their slogan, "Piši za oko, a ne za uho" ("Write for the eye and not for the ear"), reflected
a proposal to unify spelling despite regional pronunciation differences — including a proposed
single "horned e" (ě) letter for the historical *jat'* sound, borrowed from Czech, which would have
kept the ekavian/ijekavian/ikavian split purely at the spoken level (p. 382-383). Facing Croatia's
genuinely fragmented dialect landscape (čakavian, kajkavian, štokavian — far more different from
each other than modern standard Serbian is from standard Croatian), the Illyrians chose štokavian
as their base dialect for largely geographic reasons, since it was closest to the dialect spoken
throughout Serbia (p. 383). The movement ended organizationally after the revolutions of 1848, but
its language-reform work proved lasting: in 1850, Croat representatives and Vuk met in Vienna and
agreed that Serbian and Croatian were one language with one grammar, written in two alphabets (p.
383).

### 170c. Vienna and beyond

The 1850 Vienna agreement settled on ijekavian as the joint standard pronunciation (native to Vuk's
Herzegovina, Montenegro, western Bosnia, and southern Croatia), though Vuk's "write as you speak"
principle ultimately prevailed over Illyrian "write for the eye" principles in most points of
grammar and spelling (p. 384). Vuk's assistant Đuro Daničić (1825-1882) relocated to Zagreb after
Vienna and, working with a group of Croatian scholars informally called the "Croatian Vukovians"
(*hrvatski vukovci*), carried out the practical codification work. Milestones: Cyrillic introduced
alongside Latin in Croatian schools in 1861; the Yugoslav Academy of Sciences and Arts (JAZU)
established in 1866 (backed by Bishop Josip Juraj Strossmayer); Pero Budmani's 1867 grammar being
the first to use the name "Serbo-Croatian" in its title; and Tomo Maretić's 1899 grammar
*Gramatika i stilistika hrvatskoga ili srpskoga književnog jezika* as the culmination of
19th-century Vukovian standardization (p. 384). Despite the Vienna agreement's ijekavian standard,
Serbia itself had by this period shifted to ekavian (native to Belgrade and Novi Sad). The source
cites a 1913 proposal by Serbian critic Jovan Skerlić — Serbs would drop Cyrillic and write only in
Latin if Croats adopted Serbian vocabulary and ekavian pronunciation — as evidence of how entrenched
this east/west divide already was; the proposal died with Skerlić in 1914 (p. 384).

### 170d. Novi Sad and beyond

The 1850 Vienna agreement never named the shared language. The 1918 Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and
Slovenes initially called it *srpsko-hrvatsko-slovenački* (Serbo-Croato-Slovene), a name quietly
abandoned once Slovene was recognized as a genuinely separate language; the remaining Serbian/
Croatian shared language went without an official name in the interwar period, commonly rendered
informally as *hrvatski ili srpski* ("Croatian or Serbian"), with Serbian-based usage dominant in
administration given Belgrade's centrality (p. 385). The 1954 Novi Sad agreement (Titoist
Yugoslavia) named the language officially — twice, with the mirror-image terms *srpskohrvatski*
and *hrvatskosrpski* — and recognized two official "pronunciations" (ekavian/ijekavian) generally
understood to cover other differences too. In the 1960s the term *varijanta* ("variant") became the
accepted technical term for these two halves: *istočna varijanta* (eastern, Belgrade-based, Cyrillic,
ekavian) and *zapadna varijanta* (western, Zagreb-based, Latin, ijekavian), though informally many
still just said *srpski*/*hrvatski* (p. 384-385). Croat dissatisfaction with this binary framework
culminated in the 1967 "Croatian Spring" declaration of a separate Croatian language, suppressed by
the Titoist government in 1971 but influential nonetheless: the 1974 Yugoslav constitution named no
official languages at all, instead letting each constituent republic pick its own "standard
linguistic expression" — a tacit recognition of four regional standards (Belgrade/Serbia, Zagreb/
Croatia, Sarajevo/Bosnia-Herzegovina, Titograd-now-Podgorica/Montenegro) rather than two (p.
385-386). Under this framework the former "eastern variant" became simply "Serbo-Croatian," and the
former "western variant" became "Croatian." Bosnia-Herzegovina named its own administrative
language "Croato-Serbian/Serbo-Croatian" in ijekavian pronunciation, while also introducing the
semi-official term *bosanskohercegovački standarnojezički izraz* ("Bosno-Herzegovinian standard
linguistic expression"). The source characterizes pre-1974 Bosnian speech as consistently ijekavian
in pronunciation but a genuine "melting pot" in vocabulary — often using both east/west word pairs
as synonyms, defaulting to the eastern/Serbian member when only one was used, and drawing on a
distinct layer of Turkish-derived words used more in colloquial than formal register (cross-
referenced by the source to [176a], not covered in this chapter) (p. 386). The chapter closes by
framing this 1970s decentralization as having "laid the groundwork for an eventual Bosnian
language, should it come to a split between Serbian and Croatian" (p. 386).

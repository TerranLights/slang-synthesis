**Note on ekavian/ijekavian as "core" vs. "regional":** ekavian and ijekavian are both accepted
BCS standards (ekavian = Serbian standard, ijekavian = Croatian/Bosnian standard), so neither is a
non-standard "dialect" in the everyday sense; they are tagged `regional`/`core` here only to
distinguish which is this project's already-established Serbian baseline (ekavian, tagged `regional`
to flag its Serbia-specific standard status) vs. the Croatian/Bosnian standard (ijekavian, tagged
`core` as it matches this dataset's existing Lessons 1-20 baseline, which uses ijekavian). Ikavian is
the only one of the three that is genuinely non-standard everywhere, hence `regional` with a narrower
`regional` geographic scope.

## Grammar points

### 171a. Štokavian, čakavian, and kajkavian — the primary dialect split

The broadest, most consciously-recognized division within BCS is not the Croatian/Serbian/Bosnian
national split but a three-way dialect split named for each dialect's word for "what": **štokavian**
(što), **čakavian** (ča), and **kajkavian** (kaj). These differ far more than vocabulary — divergent
accentual systems, sound systems, and grammar (e.g. štokavian *ja ću ići* "I will go" vs. a male
kajkavian speaker's *ja bom išel*, which uses an L-participle plus an auxiliary from a conjugation
absent in štokavian/čakavian). By strict mutual-intelligibility criteria these three dialects differ
more than standard Serbian, Croatian, and Bosnian do from each other. Štokavian covers by far the
broadest territory (all of Serbia, Montenegro, Bosnia, and much of eastern/southern Croatia) and is
the sole basis of standard BCS. Čakavian covers most Adriatic islands, coastal Dalmatia (incl. Split,
Rijeka), and part of Istria. Kajkavian covers a compact northwestern Croatian area including Zagreb.
(p.387-388)

### 171b. Literary štokavian, čakavian, and kajkavian

Both čakavian and kajkavian have serious literary histories independent of štokavian. Čakavian
literature is oldest — the first printed literary work in Croatian vernacular (1501, Marko Marulić,
Split) was čakavian, launching the "Dalmatian Renaissance" centered on Zadar/Split/Dubrovnik/Hvar/
Korčula (Dubrovnik itself, notably, wrote in štokavian, being its native speech). Kajkavian literary
activity peaked in Zagreb in the 18th-early 19th c.; kajkavian remains Zagreb's everyday informal
speech even today, though Zagreb residents use standard štokavian formally and with outsiders. Vuk
Karadžić's 19th-c. language-reform program was built on his own East Herzegovinian štokavian
speech, but his encounter with Dubrovnik's literary heritage led him to reintroduce the sound *h*
(lost in his own and most Serbian speech) and the *tj/dj* sequences (which he otherwise wrote as
ć/đ, e.g. his own *đevojka* vs. the restored standard *djevojka* "girl") into the literary standard.
This chapter also explicitly credits Vuk's travels (including to Montenegro) with revealing to him
the full breadth of the štokavian area, which — filtered through German Romantic "one language, one
people" ideology absorbed from Kopitar and the Grimms — fed directly into 19th/20th-c. "Greater
Serbia" political rhetoric, a direct historical link between a dialectology observation and later
nationalist territorial claims. (p.388-390)

### 171c. Ekavian, ikavian, and (i)jekavian — the jat' reflex

The clearest perceptible pronunciation split among štokavian speakers descends from the old Slavic
vowel *jat'*, pronounced uniformly ~1.5 millennia ago (similar to English "bad") and later
reflexed as **e** (ekavian), **i** (ikavian), or **je/ije** (ijekavian, sometimes "jekavian" — same
group). Only ekavian and ijekavian are accepted BCS standards: ekavian is the primary/only form of
standard Serbian; ijekavian is standard for Croatian and Bosnian. Ikavian is non-standard but
genuinely widespread — spoken in Rijeka and Split and many local dialects, and historically important
to Croatian and Bosnian letters. See the 27-row vocabulary table above for the illustrative 9-word
chart Alexander herself gives. A speaker's dialect is internally consistent (no mixing within one
speaker/writer), but a speaker can consciously switch entirely — the source's example is Ivo Andrić
(BCS's only Nobel laureate), raised ijekavian, who deliberately switched to ekavian after settling in
Belgrade, retaining ijekavian only when quoting an ijekavian-speaking character. Ekavian territory
covers most of Serbia (plus Macedonia, western Bulgaria) but explicitly *not* all of Serbia and *no*
part of Montenegro; ijekavian covers the rest of Serbia, all of Montenegro, and much of Bosnia and
Croatia; ikavian covers the rest of Bosnia-Herzegovina, much of the Croatian coast, and scattered
pockets including a Croatian-speaking enclave in northernmost Serbia. (p.390-392)

### 171d. Dialectal divisions and language change

Dialectal difference arises from slow, largely unconscious generational drift, not deliberate
design — the source's example is Edward Sapir's concept of language "drift." The chapter's most
extended case study is the **loss of the infinitive**, replaced by *da* + present-tense conjugated
verb (e.g. *želim da radim* vs. *želim raditi*, both "I want to work"). This change is complete in
Bulgaria/Macedonia/southeastern Serbia (infinitive gone entirely), well underway but incomplete
through most of Serbia, rarer moving west, and essentially absent in the far northwest — i.e., a
live, still-in-progress east-to-west sound/syntax change, not a stable dialect boundary. Because
standardization "freezes" a language's current point in its trajectory of change and declares it
"correct," Serbia (further along) treats *da* + present as more natural/"right," while Croatia (early
in the trajectory) treats the infinitive as more natural/"right" — Bosnia sits genuinely midway, with
both feeling equally natural. The small word *da* (elsewhere semantically neutral — "yes," "in order
to") thus picked up strong political/identity charge specifically in its infinitive-replacing use:
Serbs, taking pride in Balkan identity, favor it; Croats, associating "Balkan" negatively, avoid it
—to the point that some Croats have generalized this avoidance to *da* in its other meanings too
(preferring *kako* to the interrogative *da li*). (p.392-395)

### 172. East vs. west — the binary model and its limits

Following the Novi Sad Agreement's acknowledgment of "eastern" and "western" variants, many
observers came to map religion (Catholic/Orthodox), nationality (Croatian/Serbian), cultural center
(Zagreb/Belgrade), and pronunciation (ijekavian/ekavian) onto a single West/East binary. Alexander
is explicit that this model is only a rough approximation — reality, especially in Serbia, is more
complex — but it was a widely used organizing schema in pre-1990s Serbo-Croatian teaching handbooks,
which typically listed word pairs organized this way (see the 42-pair vocabulary table above).
(p.394-395)

### 172a. The eastern/western word list and its one real principle

Handbook word lists like the one tabulated above almost always start from ekavian+*da*-present as
"eastern" markers and ijekavian+infinitive as "western" markers, then list individual lexical pairs.
The only sub-category with a genuine systematic explanation, per Alexander, is native-vs-international
vocabulary: Serbian readily absorbs foreign borrowings (Serbs take pride in the resulting
expressiveness — sometimes keeping both a native and a borrowed word as near-synonyms with slightly
different shades of meaning), while Croatian systematically prefers native Slavic word-formation
(Croats take pride in minimal reliance on borrowings). All 12 month names exemplify this split
starkly: Croatian uses Slavic-derived names throughout, Serbian uses the international
Latinate/Roman-calendar names throughout. Most other pairs in the list, by contrast, are historically
arbitrary (e.g. *tko*/*ko*, *što*/*šta* — random phonetic divergence) or reflect regular but
non-symbolic sound change (the loss of *h* in Serbian: *suh*/*suv*, *kuhar*/*kuvar* "cook"). (p.395-397)

### 172b. Gradations between east and west — the binary model breaks down

Real usage is a continuum, not a binary split, for several converging reasons the source names
explicitly: (1) **Bosnian** frequently uses both E and W forms as synonyms, or splits by individual
speaker preference — making Bosnian "a unique system all its own," not a midpoint blend; (2)
speakers shift toward locally-marked forms with in-group interlocutors and toward neutral forms with
outsiders, often unconsciously; (3) many speakers simply enjoy having synonym options available. The
source extends the binary-breakdown argument beyond single words to phrase-level pairs that are truly
synonymous in meaning yet still perceived by speakers as W- or E-marked: *želim raditi* vs. *želim da
radim* ("I want to work"); clitic placement — *moja je sestra učiteljica* (W, clitic after first
accented word, splitting the noun phrase) vs. *moja sestra je učiteljica* (E, clitic after the full
subject phrase) "My sister is a teacher"; and the genitive singular masculine adjective ending —
*Gramatika francuskoga jezika* (W, longer form, felt "more elegant") vs. *Gramatika francuskog
jezika* (E, shorter form, felt "more natural/straightforward") "Grammar of the French language." The
source notes speaker consciousness of the difference is strongest for the lexical/infinitive pairs
and weaker (though still real) for the syntactic pairs, and quotes a short illustrative line from a
1968-69 poem by Bosnian poet Enes Kišević — *"Bio je sijčanj iliti januar, ne sjećam se"* ("It was
either January or January, I can't recall") — to make the point that the emotional/human weight of
such lexical choices for a speaker can matter more than the actual linguistic content itself. (p.397-399)

### 173a. "Same vs. different" — the linguist's core methodological problem

Both dialectologists (classifying speech by region) and sociolinguists (classifying speech by social
markers — age, ethnicity, sex, class, occupation) face the same underlying task: deciding, often on
contested or graded evidence, where a "same" classification stops and a "different" one begins. This
extends to the language-vs-dialect distinction itself, nominally governed by the mutual-intelligibility
criterion, but the source stresses this criterion is imprecise in practice — group *will* to
understand each other (or not) can override the objective linguistic facts, and perception/emotion/
education level all shape the outcome as much as pure linguistics does. (p.399-401)

### 173b. Parameters of differentiation — testing candidate B/C/S markers

Alexander tests each commonly-cited East/West marker as a potential *diagnostic* (rather than merely
associative) criterion for distinguishing Bosnian, Croatian, and Serbian, and finds each insufficient
on its own:
- **Alphabet** (Latin↔Catholic/Croatian, Cyrillic↔Orthodox/Serbian): fails — Serbian officially
  retains both alphabets (per the Committee for the Standardization of Serbian, rejecting a
  Cyrillic-only minority proposal), so neither alphabet exclusively defines any one of the three.
- **Pronunciation** (ekavian/ijekavian/ikavian): fails — ijekavian is shared by both Bosnian and
  Croatian, so it can't isolate either one; Serbian, despite pressure from some Serbs favoring
  ekavian-only standardization, officially retains both ekavian and ijekavian.
- **Grammar/spelling** (infinitive vs. *da*+present; *prevest će* vs. *prevešće*): the infinitive
  distinction is only a frequency tendency, not absolute; the spelling distinction is real and
  diagnostic between Croatian and Serbian specifically, but Bosnian uses both, and "a language cannot
  be defined simply in terms of a spelling rule."

Alexander's conclusion: **vocabulary is the only real candidate left** for an outside linguist (and
the one most local linguists rely on too), which motivates the vocabulary-list discussion in 173c.
(p.401-402)

### 173c. Vocabulary lists and "differential" dictionaries — the politics of word-claiming

No single word can be shown to belong exclusively to one of the three groups and never used by the
others, yet each national/religious group identifies strongly with a characteristic word-stock drawn
from its own heritage: **Bosnians** favor Turkish-derived words tied to Islamic heritage; **Serbs**
favor either "Balkan"-flavored words (also with Ottoman-era overlay) or international borrowings used
alongside native near-synonyms; **Croats** favor purely Slavic-root words and actively avoid anything
felt to carry "Serbian-ness." Croats have been the most active compilers of explicitly
**"differential" dictionaries** — decades of word lists distinguishing "Croatian" from
"non-Croatian"(=Serbian), some just Serbian→Croatian equivalence columns, others full dictionaries
annotating each entry's degree of "Croatian-ness"/"Serbian-ness." Alexander flags a real
methodological risk here: such lists are typically compiled by individual nationalist-motivated
laypeople rather than a scholarly commission, blurring the line between documenting actual usage and
prescribing/inventing it — some "purified" Croatian words may be genuinely active, others merely
"wishful thinking" absent real frequency data. Bosniak linguists have compiled a parallel
**"characteristic lexicon"** of specifically Bosnian words (mostly archaisms tied to Bosnia's Islamic
past), aiming to demonstrate Bosnian is not merely a Serbian/Croatian blend. Serbs, by contrast,
having historically held the dominant position within Serbo-Croatian, have felt comparatively little
need for a differential word list, focusing instead on pride in Serbian's absorptive capacity for
loanwords. Alexander's closing thesis for the whole chapter (repeated as the throughline into
Chapters 23-25): **no purely linguistic parameter cleanly separates Bosnian, Croatian, and Serbian
from one another** — what actually defines each as a distinct language is its symbolic role as a
repository of a people's cultural identity and history, not measurable linguistic structure. (p.402-403)

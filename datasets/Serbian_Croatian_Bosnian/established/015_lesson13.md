# Serbian/Croatian/Bosnian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Trinaesta lekcija / Lesson Thirteen

**Source:** Ronelle Alexander & Ellen Elias-Bursać, *Bosnian, Croatian, Serbian, a Textbook: With
Exercises and Basic Grammar* (2nd ed., 2010), Lesson Thirteen ("Trinaesta lekcija"), PDF pages
218–235 (printed pages 197–214). This range covers the full lesson: subsections A1–A4 (dialogues,
vocabulary boxes, and grammar boxes), the B-section drills (vocabulary-bearing items only extracted;
repeated drill prompts skipped), the C-section homework prompts (skipped — no new vocabulary/grammar),
and the closing "Aforizmi Dušana Radovića" reading with its own vocabulary box.

**Coverage note:** every grammar point and every distinct vocabulary item from this lesson, plus
every explicit dialectal/regional annotation the source itself marks ([B]/[C]/[S] tags, and the
related [E]/[J] Ekavian/Ijekavian pronunciation tags used throughout this book), is extracted below.
Repeated drill exercises (the B1, B2, B4–B6 pattern drills and the C1–C6 homework prompts) that
reuse already-captured vocabulary without introducing anything new are skipped, per
`../../00_Reference_Extraction_Spec.md`. This source has a clean PDF text layer (`pdftotext`
produced usable text directly) — no vision-reading was required, so no marginalia-exclusion issue
applies and Vision Reading Confidence is `n/a` throughout.

**A note on regional tagging:** this lesson's B/C/S-tagged vocabulary pairs are standard-register
variants specific to one or more of the three national standards (Bosnian/Croatian/Serbian), not
non-standard dialect forms — so `Usage Tier` is left at `core` (or `technical` for the academic-
discipline list) even when `Attested Region`/`Geographic Scope` are filled in from an explicit tag.
Several items also carry the book's separate `[E]`/`[J]` (Ekavian/Ijekavian) pronunciation tags,
which cut across the B/C/S split (Ekavian ≈ Serbia; Ijekavian ≈ Bosnia, Croatia, and parts of
Serbia/Montenegro) — these are recorded the same way.

## Grammar points

### The conditional mood (formation)

The conditional is formed from the l-participle plus an auxiliary that resembles the old aorist of
*biti* ("to be"): *bih, bi, bi, bismo, biste, bi* (1st/2nd/3rd person, singular then plural). The
clitic and full forms are spelled identically; in speech the full forms carry stress and the clitic
forms don't. Clitic forms are used in most contexts; full forms are reserved for questions and
single-word answers. In informal speech only the invariant auxiliary *bi* tends to be used. Example
paradigm cited: *bih ràdio/ràdila* … *bismo ràdili/ràdile* (p.198, citing grammar reference [131]).

### Politeness formulas with the conditional

Just as English politely prefers "I would like" over "I want," BCS prefers the conditional
*žèlela [E] žèljela [J] bih kolàča* ("I would like some cake") over the blunter indicative *žèlim
kolàča* ("I want cake") (p.198, [133b]).

### The word "welcome" (dobro dòšli)

*Dobro dòšli* is literally "well" + "come." The second element inflects for the addressee: *dobro
dòšao* to a man normally addressed as *ti*, *dobro dòšla* to a woman, and *dobro dòšli* to a group or
to someone addressed with *vi*. Written as a single word, *dobrodòšla* functions as an ordinary
adjective, e.g. *òna je dobrodòšla* "she is [a] welcome [guest]" (p.198).

### Toned-down statements and requests with the conditional

The conditional softens the force of a statement or command, the way English "you would need to
move your things" softens "you have to move your things." The book contrasts the abrupt indicative
*morate sklòniti stvari* with the softer conditional *mòrali biste sklòniti stvari* (p.201, [133a]).

### Indirect discourse and tense preservation

Unlike English, which backshifts the tense of quoted speech inside a subordinate clause after a
past-tense verb ("you said you WOULD move those things"), BCS keeps the tense of the original quoted
speech unchanged whether the quote is direct or indirect. Direct: *rekli ste "ja ĆU skloniti te
stvari."* Indirect: *rekli ste da ĆETE sklòniti te stvari* — the future tense *ćete* stays a genuine
future, not a conditional, even embedded in reported speech (p.201, [135]).

### Da introducing proposed actions

The conjunction *da* commonly follows a verb and introduces a second clause. That clause can state a
fact (*znam da je on Amerikànac* "I know that he's American"), or purpose (*žèlim da ùčimo zajedno*
"I want us to study together"). When *da* stands alone before the main verb of a sentence, it
presents an action as a potential one — frequently as a question (*kàko da dođem do trga?* "how can
I get to the square?") or a suggestion (*da pòpijemo nešto!* "let's have something to drink!")
(p.201, [143e]).

### Conditional sentences (three types)

BCS marks three distinct kinds of "if... then" sentence, differentiated by conjunction and verb
mood:
1. **Expected fulfillment** — conjunction *ako* + indicative (present or past): *ako pročìtam sve do
   večèras, mògu vam dati knjigu* "if I read everything by tonight, I can give you the book" (p.203,
   [132a]).
2. **Possible but uncertain** — conjunction *kad* + conditional in both clauses: *bolje bi bìlo kad
   biste to pročìtali do večèras* "it would be better if you were to read it through by this
   evening" (p.203, [132b]).
3. **Hypothetical/unfulfillable** — conjunction *da* + present tense in the if-clause, conditional in
   the result clause: *da sam čaròbnjak, mogao bih to sve pročìtati do večèras* "if I were a
   magician, I'd be able to read it all by this evening" (p.203, [132c]).

### Conditional of purpose

*Da* + conditional after an antecedent clause can express purpose, a use of the BCS conditional with
no direct English parallel: *treba mi to da bih ga obràdila večèras* "I need it so that I can work on
it this evening" — glossable in English only loosely, as "...that I might work on it" (p.203,
[134b]).

### The relative conjunction kòji, continued

*Kòji*'s gender and number are fixed by its antecedent, but its case is fixed independently by its
own grammatical role inside its own clause. In *stùd enti kòji stànuju tamo…*, *kòji* is masculine
plural (from *stùdenti*) and nominative (subject of its own clause). In *zgrada kòju ćete vidjeti…*,
*kòju* is feminine singular (from *zgrada*) but accusative (object of *vidjeti*). In *to je zgrada u
kòjoj ja stànujem*, *kòjoj* is feminine singular but locative (object of the preposition *u*).
English speakers additionally have to remember to supply the relative conjunction at all, since
English routinely omits "that"/"which" where BCS cannot (p.205–206, [128a], [136a]).

### Compound conjunctions

Many English prepositions double as conjunctions depending on what follows them ("before lunch" vs.
"before he arrived"), but BCS requires different words for the two functions: a bare preposition
before a noun/pronoun, and a compound conjunction — the preposition plus *nego što* (invariant) or
*to što* (where *to* takes whatever case the sentence requires) — before a clause. Examples: *pre
[E] prije [J] rùčka* "before lunch" (preposition) vs. *zavr̀šila sam sve pre nego što je on stigao*
"I finished everything before he arrived" (conjunction); *zbog vas* "because of you" (preposition)
vs. *zbog toga što moramo s vama razgovàrati* "because we have to speak with you" (conjunction)
(p.206, [143b]).

## Cultural/pragmatic notes

- **Greeting custom:** introductions are made with direct eye contact, "Dobar dan!", a handshake, and
  the speaker's name (first name informally, full name formally). Friends greet with cheek kisses —
  twice among Bosnian and Croatian speakers, three times among Serbian speakers (p.198).
- **Refusing food/drink:** it is customary to decline a first offer of food or drink to avoid
  appearing overeager; a host may repeat the offer, and *hvàla, ne mògu* is the polite way to
  decline, while a flat *nèću [B,S]* / *ne ću [C]* is considered impolite (p.207).
- **Bus tickets:** city bus/tram tickets are cheaper bought in advance at a newspaper kiosk than from
  the driver; intercity tickets are typically bought at the terminal (p.204).

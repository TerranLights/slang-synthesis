## Grammar points

### South Slavic language classification (Ch. 1, p.3)

Serbian belongs to the South Slavic branch of the Slavonic language family (with Slovenian,
Croatian, Bosnian, Bulgarian, Macedonian), itself one of three main branches alongside West Slavic
(Polish, Czech, Slovak) and East Slavic (Ukrainian, Belarusian, Russian). Source frames this as the
starting orientation for the whole book.

### Migration and the interrogative-pronoun dialect split (Ch. 1, p.6 / Ch. 2, p.9)

Source uses the interrogative "what?" as the organizing device for dialect classification: Slavic
tribes migrating southwest into the Balkans/Pannonia region ended up saying kaj or ča for "what,"
while tribes migrating southeast said što/šta. This single lexical split is presented as the
foundation both for Chapter 1's brief mention of dialect divergence and for Chapter 2's full
three-way kajkavski/čakavski/štokavski classification.

### Cyril and Methodius, Old Church Slavonic, and the Cyrillic alphabet (Ch. 1, pp.6-7)

Ninth-century Byzantine missionaries Constantine (later Cyril, as a monk) and Methodius were sent to
convert the Slavs of Great Moravia. Lacking a written form for the Slavonic language of a Macedonian
tribe near Salonika, they invented one — Old Church Slavonic — using a new alphabet, Glagolitic (40
letters, Greek-based), later replaced by Cyrillic (the Greek alphabet plus 14 added letters). Source
frames alphabet creation as inseparable from literary/liturgical language creation in this tradition.

### Vuk Karadžić's phonetic alphabet reform (Ch. 1, pp.7-8)

Karadžić (1787-1864) reformed the Cyrillic alphabet toward strict phoneticism ("a letter for every
sound"): discarded symbols he judged didn't correspond to a real sound, and added six new letters
(source's own listing of the exact six is not reliably legible in this PDF extraction — see the
font-encoding caveat above; standard historical accounts identify these as lj, nj, đ, ć, dž and j).
In 1818 he published a Serbian dictionary written in the vernacular spoken language rather than the
literary Church-Slavonic-influenced register that preceded it.

### The Ekavian/Ijekavian standard-language split and its political history (Ch. 1, p.7)

Karadžić's own speech was Ijekavian (western Serbia, Bosnia-Herzegovina, Montenegro, Serbs in
Croatia/Dalmatia), but the pre-existing literary language was Ekavian Štokavian, centered in the
northeastern regions where the most significant Serbian cultural/political/economic centers were
located. Result, per source: Serbia and Vojvodina kept Ekavian as their literary standard, while
Croats, Bosnian Muslims, and Montenegrin Serbs adopted Karadžić's Ijekavian reform outright. This is
presented as the direct historical origin of the modern Ekavian-Serbian vs. Ijekavian-Croatian/
Bosnian standard-language split covered in more sociolinguistic depth in the already-extracted
Alexander companion volume (established/032, /034, /035).

### Serbo-Croatian unification and its late-20th-century breakup (Ch. 1, p.8)

19th-century Croats and Serbs "unified their languages under the name of Serbo-Croatian," recognizing
shared structure despite the Ekavian/Ijekavian and Cyrillic/Latin differences. Source frames the
20th-century unraveling as politically driven: Croatian linguists began emphasizing differences and
coining new words during the push for Yugoslav secession, accelerating after the Yugoslav breakup,
while — per the source's own characterization — "Serbian, on the other hand, remained unchanged."
(Framed here as the source author's own claim/perspective, not independently verified by this
extraction — it is a stronger, more one-sided claim than the more measured "same vs. different"
treatment in Alexander's companion Sociolinguistic Commentary, established/034 and /036.)

### Kajkavian / Čakavian / Štokavian three-way top-level split (Ch. 2, p.9)

Chapter 2's headline classification: three dialects distinguished by their word for "what" —
kajkavski (kaj), spoken in Slavonia and northwestern Croatia near the Slovenian border, closely
resembling Slovenian; čakavski (ča), spoken in northern Croatia, Istria, and along the Adriatic coast
and islands; and štokavski (što/šta), the most widely spoken, used by Serbs, Croats, Bosnians, and
Montenegrins outside the kajkavski/čakavski areas. All three modern standard languages (Serbian,
Croatian, Bosnian) are built on the štokavski base.

### Jat-reflex sub-dialects of Štokavian: Ikavian/Ijekavian/Ekavian (Ch. 2, pp.9-10)

Within štokavski, three further sub-dialects are distinguished by how each treats the Old Slavonic
long vowel jat (ě), historically distinct from ordinary short e: Ikavian renders it as i (western
Vojvodina, western Bosnia-Herzegovina, western Croatia, northwestern Adriatic); Ijekavian renders it
as je/ije (western Serbia, Croatia, Montenegro, southern Adriatic coast, eastern Bosnia — the basis of
standard Croatian and Bosnian, written in the Latin alphabet); Ekavian renders it as e (Serbia and
Vojvodina — the basis of standard Serbian, generally Cyrillic). Source is explicit that this
distinction applies only to words descending from a genuine historical jat, illustrated by the
counter-example pet ("five"), identical across all three sub-dialects because its e is not a jat
reflex.

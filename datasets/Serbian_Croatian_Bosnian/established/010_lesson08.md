## Grammar points

### Adjective citation convention (from Lesson 8 onward)

Starting with this lesson, vocabulary lists give only the masculine form of an adjective when the
feminine and neuter are fully predictable (simply add `-a`/`-o` or `-e`). Both masculine and
feminine forms are given whenever the stem itself changes (in spelling or accent) — e.g. *mùdar* is
listed with *mùdra* because the shift is not fully predictable, while *siv* is listed alone since
*siva*/*sivo* are automatic. (p.113, footnote)

### Plural case forms: dative, locative, instrumental merger

Although dative, locative, and instrumental are distinct cases in the plural (as in the singular),
all three share one form per noun. Feminine nouns in `-a` take `-ama`; every other noun class takes
`-ima`. Masculine nouns whose stems end in `-k`, `-g`, `-h` undergo the same consonant shift in
DLIpl as in Npl. All adjectives take `-im` across these three plural cases. (p.113)

### Vocative case, continued

Not every noun has a distinct vocative, and forms vary: masculine nouns ending in `-k`/`-g`/`-h`
usually shift to `-č`/`-ž`/`-š` before `-e`; masculine nouns ending in a "soft" consonant often take
`-u`; some two-syllable feminine nouns take `-o`; feminine nouns in `-ica` usually take `-e`.
Feminine consonant-stem nouns rarely form a vocative at all (they denote objects/concepts, not
people) except in poetic/metaphorical address, where they take `-i` — e.g. *mòja ljùbavi* "oh my
love!". Neuter nouns and all plural nouns use the nominative form as the vocative. (p.113–114)

### Neuter nouns with an added `-et-` syllable

A subset of neuter nouns (including *dete/dijete* "child," *jaje* "egg," *dugme* "button," *tele*
"calf," and certain proper names) insert an extra syllable `-et-` before the genitive,
dative-locative, and instrumental singular endings — e.g. *dète* → *dèteta* (Gsg) → *dètetu* (DLsg)
→ *dètetom* (Isg). (p.115)

### Irregular plurals of dete/dijete, brat, gospodin

*Dete/dijete* "child," *brat* "brother," and *gospodin* "gentleman" form plurals (*deca/djeca*,
*braća*, *gospoda*) that are singular in grammatical form — they decline like feminine nouns in
`-a`, and any adjective modifying them takes feminine singular agreement — yet they carry plural
meaning and always trigger plural verb agreement, e.g. *naša dèca ìgraju zajedno* "our children are
playing together." (p.115)

### Se-verbs, continued

The particle *se* can add a reciprocal "each other" sense to a verb: *čuti se* "be in touch (usually
by phone)," *videti/vidjeti se* "meet, get together." For some basic verbs *se* is simply a
lexicalized part of the verb's meaning; if such a verb takes an object, that object is in a
non-accusative case rather than the accusative. *Jàvljati se* (perfective *jàviti se*) "get in touch
with" takes a dative object, and if the means of communication is specified, it appears in the
instrumental — e.g. *javljam joj se telefònom* "I call her on the phone." (p.117)

### Review of prepositions

Each preposition governs a specific case; since most govern the genitive, it's efficient to memorize
the exceptions and treat genitive as the default. Many prepositions carry both a physical-location
sense and an abstract/figurative one — e.g. *s jèdne stràne* can mean either "on one side [of
something]" or "on the one hand." A set of prepositions taking the instrumental — *pred* "before,"
*pod* "under," *nad* "above," *među* "between, among," *za* "behind" — each has a compound form
taking the genitive instead: *ispred, ispod, iznad, između, iza*. (p.118)

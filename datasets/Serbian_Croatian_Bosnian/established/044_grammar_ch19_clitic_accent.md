## Grammar points

### 164. Clitic placement and BCS syntax: rhythmic constituents

Clitics (unstressed function words — auxiliaries, object pronouns, the reflexive/reciprocal
particle `se`, etc.) must (a) all cluster together in one fixed relative order, and (b) as a
cluster, occur immediately after a specific point in the sentence. Two common informal
descriptions of that point — "after the first word" or "in second position" — are both
inadequate: clitics are frequently placed after the second, third, or even fourth word of a
sentence, so neither a literal word-count rule nor a vague "second position" rule works in
practice.

The book's own prior word-order diagrams used the term **first significant unit**, labeled with
the symbol **X**, with the clitic string itself labeled **Y** — together, the **XYZ model** (Z
being whatever follows the clitic cluster). This chapter replaces "first significant unit" with a
more precise technical term, **rhythmic constituent (RC)**: the stretch of a sentence after which
clitics may (or in some cases must) occur. An RC is not simply a grammatical constituent (e.g. a
noun phrase) — it is defined by *pronunciation rhythm*, which can force a grammatical constituent
to be "dismantled" so its first word alone serves as the RC.

#### 164a. Obligatory rhythmic constituents

Two categories of words are **obligatory RCs**: subordinating conjunctions (`da`, `što`, `koji` as
relative conjunction, `ako`, `kad`, `dok`, `čim`, and compound conjunctions built on these) and
question words (`kada`, `kako`, `ko`/`tko`, `koji`, `koliko`, `kakav`, etc., including interrogative
pronominal forms). Any clitics in the clause are required, without exception, to come immediately
after these words — even when this forces a clitic (e.g. the reflexive particle `se` of a
`se`-verb) to be pulled far from the rest of its own verb phrase. Interrogative pronominal
modifiers are a notable case: normally a determiner+noun sequence is treated as one grammatical
constituent, but when the determiner is interrogative, it must be dismantled from its noun and act
alone as the RC.

#### 164b. Optional rhythmic constituents

Outside the obligatory categories, RC choice is more fluid and centers on two major word-group
types: **noun phrases** (a noun plus its modifiers/case-linked nouns) and **verb phrases**
(auxiliaries, predicate adjectives/nouns, adverbs closely bound to a verb). A short, tightly bound
noun phrase can function as RC whole, or be "dismantled" so only its first word serves as RC —
Croatian favors the dismantled form, Bosnian and Serbian favor keeping the noun phrase intact
(illustrated by the C `moja je knjiga...` vs. B,S `moja knjiga je...` pair, p.352). Verb phrases of
more than one word rarely function as RC as a whole; if a sentence begins with a verb phrase, its
single central accented element (a conjugated form, L-participle, or infinitive) serves as RC.

When a long noun phrase opens a sentence, speakers either let the whole (long) phrase serve as RC,
rephrase to avoid the problem, or use a "starting-over" pattern (an imperceptible pause after the
noun phrase, review of §85a) — in which case a single verbal element after the pause becomes the
obligatory RC. This last pattern is described as especially common in writing.

### 165. Accent in BCS

BCS is traditionally described as a "four-accent system": every stressed vowel is long or short,
and carries falling or rising tone, giving four combinations — short falling, long falling, short
rising, long rising (BCS: *kratkosilazni*, *dugosilazni*, *kratkouzlazni*, *dugouzlazni*). The
canonical diacritics (acute, grave, circumflex, and a fourth double-grave mark) were devised by Vuk
Karadžić and his assistant Đuro Daničić during 19th-century codification; long non-accented vowels
are usually marked with a macron. Despite each accent traditionally being treated as one
indivisible unit with its own mark, the book's own notational innovation is to **separate the two
underlying factors**: length is marked under the vowel (only if long; short is unmarked), and tone
is marked over the vowel (only if rising — a grave mark; an unmarked initial syllable is
automatically falling, since only initial syllables can be falling under normal rules, reviewed
further in §167). This reduces the "four accents" to two independent binary choices rather than
four memorized whole-unit symbols.

#### 165a. The nature of the "four accents"

A key phonetic discovery (originally made by German scholar Leonhard Masing studying BCS émigré
speech near the end of the 19th century, largely ignored at the time, then confirmed decades later
by Serbian linguist Pavle Ivić and American phonetician Ilse Lehiste through controlled listening
experiments) is that **a falling accent's tone is confined to its own syllable, but a rising
accent's tone spans the marked syllable and the following one.** This is why rising accents require
a following syllable to exist at all (so monosyllabic words can only be falling, and no BCS word is
ever accented on its final syllable).

This mechanic reflects real language history: roughly six centuries ago, the **neo-štokavian
retraction/shift** moved every non-initial accent one syllable toward the word's beginning and
converted it to rising tone, while initial-syllable accents kept their original falling tone
unchanged. The book gives comparative Russian/Bulgarian forms (which didn't undergo the shift) to
demonstrate that essentially all BCS rising accents trace to this one historical retraction.

#### 165b. Accent and prescriptivism in BCS

The modern BCS standard's accentual prescriptions are drawn almost entirely from Vuk Karadžić's
native East Herzegovinian neo-štokavian dialect, an unusually accent-rich variety. Native speakers
of dialects close to that model (the book notes most Bosnians fit this) make the full canonical
four-way distinction naturally; many Serbs and Croats, whose native speech differs more, cannot
reliably distinguish short rising from short falling accents without having grown up hearing them,
producing an ongoing prescriptive-vs-descriptive tension: some want the codified ideal preserved as
a heritage standard even if imperfectly achieved, others want the codification revised to match
actual majority speech (e.g. collapsing to a single "short" accent).

#### 165c. Non-initial falling accents

Standard Vukovian doctrine holds falling accents are only ever word-initial (since all non-initial
accents are historically retracted, hence rising). But actual speech — including Bosnian speech,
often treated as the accentual "purity" benchmark — includes real, non-error non-initial falling
accents, notably in foreign borrowings ending in *-ent* (e.g. `absolvent`) and some long native
multi-morpheme words (e.g. `poljoprivreda`). The author proposes this could be handled with an
additional diacritic (e.g. a dot over the vowel) without breaking the book's two-factor
(length/tone) notation system, though this refinement was not applied to examples in earlier
chapters and is flagged as gaining cross-dialectal (Bosnian, Croatian, Serbian) linguistic support.

#### 165d. Variant accentuation

Prescriptive grammars/dictionaries are necessary reference points for teachers, editors, and
learners, but even the most conservative modern speakers don't fully match Vuk and Daničić's
19th-century codification. Some dictionaries omit accent marking entirely; most serious ones retain
traditional Vukovian marks but disagree with each other on specifics, and these disagreements
generally reflect real regional-usage variation rather than a clean Croatian-vs-Serbian split (no
Bosnian dictionary currently marks accent at all, per the source). Newer dictionaries increasingly
mark non-initial falling accents as acceptable variants, which the author reads as the system
slowly adapting toward actual usage.

### 166. Accent shifts in BCS

Beyond the four-accent system itself, many individual words shift accent across their own
grammatical forms. The chapter's organizing principle: separate the underlying **PLACE** of accent
(the historically/originally accented syllable) from its surface **FORM** (how that PLACE is
actually marked, given the neo-štokavian rule that a falling accent stays on its own syllable while
a rising accent's mark sits one syllable *before* its functional PLACE). Apparent "shifts" between
word forms are frequently not real PLACE shifts at all, but a predictable reinterpretation of a
stable PLACE once a different-length grammatical ending is attached — demonstrated with the
kazati/kažem infinitive-vs-present pair and the junak/đak zero-ending noun paradigms, each shown in
parallel Vukovian-mark vs. book's-own-notation columns.

Distinguished from these purely phonological reinterpretations are **morphophonemic accentual
alternations**, where the PLACE itself genuinely changes according to grammatical form/morpheme —
these are described as increasingly recessive in modern speech (many speakers no longer make
several of them) but still grammatically live for some words.

#### 166a. Accent shifts in verbs

The most significant verbal alternation is between the infinitive/L-participle accent and the
present-tense accent: for verb types 2, 5, 8b, 9 (and some of types 1, 7, 8a), the PLACE shifts one
syllable toward the word's beginning in the present tense (illustrated with vratiti/vratim and
kazati/kažem paradigm charts, including prefixed forms navratiti/dokazati, which keep the same
PLACE as their unprefixed base — a falling PLACE on an unprefixed verb's initial syllable surfaces
as rising on the prefix once one is added). Certain verbs of types 13–16 shift in the *opposite*
direction (toward the word's end) in the present tense (illustrated with tresti/tresem). Passive
participles usually take the present-tense accent; type 13–15 passive participles accent the
ending, producing an apparent Nsg.-masc.-vs.-other-forms alternation (rečen/rečena) of the same
purely phonological kind seen in junak. A regionally marked footnote: in Serbian (rarely in
Bosnian, never in Croatian), some common verbs shift accent to the ending in 1st/2nd plural present
(e.g. idemo vs. idèmo).

#### 166b. Accent shifts in nouns and adjectives

Zero-ending masculine nouns (junak-type) show the PLACE-vs-FORM effect described above: the accent
"appears" to move because the zero ending in Nsg. forces the accent onto the last pronounceable
stem syllable, while other case endings shift that last-pronounceable-syllable position further
out. A related but formally distinct alternation is vowel length shifting with zero endings or
fleeting-vowel loss (illustrated with dom/doma, kraj/kraja, lovac/lovca, and grad/gradovi,
muž/muževi charts: short before a vowel-initial ending, long before zero/consonant, or vice versa
for the fleeting-vowel type) — described as a tendency rather than a strict rule. Genitive-plural
accent shifts are noted as frequent but largely unpredictable and lexically idiosyncratic (e.g.
prijatelj → Gpl. prijatèlja shifts accent to the ending; pìsmo → Gpl. pisama and jèzik → Gpl.
jezika both shift accent to the initial syllable) and must be memorized per noun.

Two true morphophonemic (PLACE-changing) alternations are described as archaic/receding: (1)
feminine -a nouns with underlying final-syllable (rising) accent shift the accent to the initial
syllable specifically in Asg. and NApl. (glava/glavu/glave-type; also affects srijeda/sreda,
noga, planina, all explicitly given with an E/J `sreda`/`srijeda` dialectal footnote); (2)
inanimate zero-ending Nsg. masculine nouns shift accent to the ending specifically in Lsg. (grad →
Lsg. gradu; stvar → Lsg. stvari). Among adjectives, only a small set (illustrated with crn/crna and
zelen/zelena) retain a genuine PLACE-shift distinguishing the indefinite (longer-form, PLACE one
syllable further back) from the definite short/long-form opposition reviewed in §17b; adjectives
with a fleeting vowel (e.g. dobar/dobra, contrasted directly with junak/junaka) show that the
fleeting vowel itself is not accentable, forcing accent onto the actual first real syllable rather
than behaving like the zero-ending noun pattern.

#### 166c. Accent shifts between words

Prefixing a verb does not change its underlying PLACE of accent — it just adds a syllable in front
of a word whose accentuation was already stable, so a falling-accented unprefixed verb's accent
surfaces as rising *on the prefix* once one is added (kažem → dòkažem). The negated present tense
behaves identically, since the negative particle `ne` functions prosodically as a prefix even
though it's written as a separate word (a falling-accented present-tense verb yields a rising
accent on `ne` when negated) — the author flags this as a common source of learner confusion since
`ne` is genuinely pronounced separately in other contexts (reviewed §25b).

Historically, prepositions behaved the same way (retracting accent from a falling-accented noun
object onto themselves), but this pattern is now largely archaic in Serbian/Croatian, surviving
mainly in fixed phrases (e.g. `ì jedno ì drugo`). **In Bosnian, by contrast, retraction onto
prepositions and conjunctions — locally termed `skakanje` ("jumping")** — remains comparatively
frequent, occurring regularly with pronoun objects of prepositions and in many set expressions, and
is explicitly framed by the source as a marker Bosnians themselves recognize as characteristically
Bosnian, even as some younger Bosnian speakers increasingly perceive it as slightly old-fashioned.

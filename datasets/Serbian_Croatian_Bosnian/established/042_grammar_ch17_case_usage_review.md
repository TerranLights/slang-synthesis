## Grammar points

### §154. Cases of nouns, review — overview

BCS nouns express seven case meanings in both singular and plural (plus a special form after the
numbers 2, 3, 4), unlike English's essentially two-form noun system (singular/plural, plus the
residual possessive `'s`). The same case form frequently carries more than one distinct meaning
that must be learned separately, and each preposition requires a specific case (sometimes varying
by meaning within the same preposition). The rest of the chapter systematically reviews usage of
each case, with and without prepositions. (p. 302)

### §155a. The nominative case

Expresses subject and predicate noun. A subject heads the sentence and controls verb agreement;
a predicate noun follows a form of *biti* "be", or occurs after *zvati* "call", *značiti* "mean",
*postati* "become", *praviti se* "pretend to be" (the instrumental can substitute in some of these
predicate contexts — see §159a). A title used in apposition to another noun stays nominative
regardless of the other noun's case. Nouns compared via *kao* or *nego* ("as", "than") also take
the nominative. Prepositions are generally not used with the nominative — the one exception is
*po* in its distributive/succession meaning (e.g. *jedan po jedan* "one by one"), which takes
whatever case the sentence otherwise requires rather than governing its own.

The pronominal adjective *svoj* ("one's own", reflexive possessive) cannot normally modify a
nominative noun, since possessor and possessed can't logically be identical when the possessed is
the sentence's own subject — but in metaphorical usage *svoj* + nominative noun means "one's own
[kind]," either in the sense of being autonomous or of belonging to an in-group (e.g. *Svoji smo*
"We're among our own"). BCS also frequently places the verb before the subject (unlike English's
fixed subject-first order), which shifts emphasis onto the verb — English translations often
compensate with a different phrase structure entirely. (pp. 302–303)

### §155b. The vocative case

Used to attract attention or address someone directly; for many nouns it's identical in form to the
nominative, but when distinct, the difference is quite noticeable. Used mainly with personal names
and terms for human beings; in highly poetic style, inanimate objects can be addressed too.
Adjectives modifying a vocative noun stay in the nominative. Followed by *jedan*, the vocative can
convey either a strong insult (*Svinjo jedna!* "[Dirty] swine!") or grudging/affectionate admiration
(*Mangupe jedan!* "You little rascal!"); combined with the possessive *moj* it conveys endearment
(*Dušo moja!* "My darling!"). In epic folk poetry, the vocative can substitute for the nominative as
sentence subject, and even surnames (which otherwise never take a vocative form) can appear
vocative — purely for the metrical convenience of an extra syllable to fill a rigid verse line;
within one epic song, male names may appear either nominative or vocative in different lines with
no difference in meaning. (pp. 303–304)

### §156a. The accusative without a preposition

Most frequently marks the direct object (usually a straightforward equivalent of the English direct
object). Two other sentence types use accusative for a human referent: (1) body-ache constructions,
where the person who hurts is accusative and the aching body part is nominative (verb is always
*boleti/boljeti* "hurt") — treatable as a metaphorical "the body part HURTS the person"; and (2)
emotional-state constructions (fear, shame, desire) where the experiencer is accusative and the
state itself is nominative, with the verb *biti* — this can't be analyzed as a direct object at all,
it must simply be memorized (e.g. *Strah ga je guštera* "He's afraid of lizards", literally "Fear
[of] him is [of] the lizard[s]" with the feared thing in genitive). Some Croatian grammarians
propose a Croatian-only *se*-passive construction that keeps its object in the accusative rather
than promoting it to nominative subject (e.g. *(Prodaje se kuću.)* "There's a house for sale") —
the book notes explicitly that Serbs and Bosnians cannot form such sentences at all, and many
Croats reject them too; this is flagged as a genuine, currently-unsettled register/regional
divergence, not settled standard usage. The accusative also occurs adverbially for duration of time
(*Tri meseca su putovali* "They traveled for three months") and, more rarely, for point in time or
measured space; when a time/space phrase is the sentence's logical subject, the sentence becomes
subjectless. (pp. 304–306)

### §156b. The accusative with prepositions

Most accusative-governing prepositions denote motion toward a destination (paired with a case
denoting the resulting static location — see the table below). Only *kroz*, *niz*, and *uz* occur
solely in the accusative; the rest (*među*, *na*, *nad*, *pod*, *pred*, *u*, *za*) also occur with
another case (instrumental or locative) for static location, with the same core spatial meaning
minus the idea of motion. Most of these motion-prepositions also have a genitive-only compound
form (e.g. *nad* → *iznad*) that denotes the same relationship but with all senses of motion
stripped out.

| Preposition | With Accusative (motion) | With other case (static) | Compound (Gen, no-motion) |
|---|---|---|---|
| kroz | through | — | — |
| među | between/among | instrumental | između (Gen) |
| na | on(to) | locative | — |
| nad | over, above | instrumental | iznad (Gen) |
| niz | down along | — | — |
| pod | under, below | instrumental | ispod (Gen) |
| pred | before, in front of | instrumental | ispred (Gen) |
| u | in(to) | locative | — |
| uz | along(side) | — | — |
| za | behind | instrumental | iza (Gen) |

The two "other" accusative prepositions, *o* and *po*, mean something quite different with the
locative (their far more frequent usage) — with the accusative, *o* means motion *against*
(striking something) and *po* means the goal pursued after a verb of motion (e.g. *Idi po
doktora!* "Go get a doctor!"). The idea of motion extends metaphorically too, most commonly with
*u* and *na*, to express intention or figurative movement (e.g. *Idem u teretanu da vežbam* "I'm
going to the gym to work out"). Accusative + preposition also expresses time: *na* for intended
duration ("for three months"), *pred* for a point immediately before an event, *pre/prije* for "X
[amount] ago", *u* for a point in time (day of week, hour), and *za* for time elapsing before
completion ("in a week"). A separate idiomatic layer covers *uz* ("along(side)") and *za*, the
latter of which often better translates as "about" or "on behalf of" than literal "for". After
certain prepositions ending in a consonant, an archaic/high-style alternate clitic-pronoun-object
form appears (*kroza me*, *nada te*, *poda se*, *preda me*, *u te*, *uza se*, *za nj*) restricted to
accusative meaning only. §156c catalogs a further layer of purely idiomatic
preposition+accusative combinations that must simply be memorized as vocabulary facts (e.g. *na
taj način* "in that manner", *za razliku od* "unlike", *bez obzira na* "regardless of"). (pp.
306–311)

### §157. The genitive case, overview and Gpl. variation

Dictionaries often cite a noun's genitive singular alongside its nominative, both to disambiguate
gender for consonant-final nouns (masc. vs. fem., e.g. *posao, posla* "job" [m.] vs. *misao, misli*
"thought" [f.]) and to show stem changes (loss of a fleeting vowel, consonant shifts, accent
shifts) that hold for every case but the nominative. The genitive plural is often unpredictable,
especially for feminine nouns in *-a*: most take *-a* (with a long inserted vowel in the preceding
syllable), some take *-i* — the book gives rough tendencies rather than firm rules: nouns for
female humans generally take *-i* (except *devojka/djevojka* "girl"), certain stem-final consonant
clusters (*-pt, -tk, -vk, -žb, -db, -lb, -rb, -rm, -kv, -šnj, -dnj, -tnj*) favor variant endings,
and *-i* is somewhat more favored in Serbian than Croatian usage — many dictionaries disagree with
each other on specific nouns. (pp. 311–312)

### §157a. The genitive without a preposition

Genitive marks limitation, corresponding to English "of" in most of its senses: (1) the second noun
in an "[N1] of [N2]" sequence, defining/limiting N1 (often rendered in English as a noun compound
instead, e.g. *dres nogometnog kluba* "soccer uniform" lit. "uniform of the soccer club"); (2)
quality, in a poetic register, always requiring an adjective (*grad širokih ulica* "a city of wide
streets"); (3) partitive direct object, "some [of]" (*Hoćeš li kolača?* "Do you want some cake?");
(4) the object of a negated verb, often but not always still carrying partitive force (*Nemam ni
braće ni sestara* "I have no brothers or sisters"); (5) the required object of certain (mostly
*se*-)verbs where the limitation sense is metaphorical at best (*Okani se takvih misli!* "Abandon
those sort of thoughts!"); (6) after the existentials *ima*/*nema* "there is/isn't" (review [59b]);
(7) after adverbs of measure and after most numbers; (8) after location indicators like *evo* "here
is". Adverbially, the genitive (always with a required modifier) marks a specific point in time, a
repeated time, or a duration (*Jednoga dana* "One day", *svake nedelje* "every Sunday"). (pp.
312–314)

### §157b. The genitive with prepositions

The majority of BCS prepositions take the genitive. Several are compounds — two prepositions
fused into one word — and compounds always take the genitive even when their base preposition
takes a different case (e.g. base *nad* +Instr "over" vs. compound *iznad* +Gen "above"); many of
these compounds (built with the prefix *iz*-: *ispod, ispred, iza, između, iznad*) originally added
a "motion out from" sense but now simply carry all the non-directional senses of the base
preposition. Space-prepositions taking the genitive: *blizu, do, duž, iz, kod, kraj, između, mimo,
mjesto/mesto, niže, od, oko/okolo, pored, preko, protiv, s(a) [in the "down from" sense, distinct
from instrumental s(a) "with"], sred, unutar, van, više, vrh, iza*. Time-prepositions: *do, nakon,
od, poslije/posle, prije/pre, uoči, za*. Other/abstract-relation prepositions: *bez, osim, poput,
radi, zbog*. Since numbers themselves don't take case endings after prepositions, case marking in
time expressions built on numbers is often moot. (pp. 314–318)

### §157c. The genitive with prepositions in idiomatic usage

A cluster of verb phrases with *do* + genitive form fixed, non-literally-translatable idioms:
*biti do* [nešto] "to be in the mood for" (*Nije mi bilo do šetnje* "I wasn't up for a walk"),
*stati do* [nešto] "to care about" (*Njemu je stalo samo do novca* "All he cares about is money"),
*doći do* [nešto] "to come to, result in" (*Došlo je do svađe* "Things led to a quarrel") — the
first two form subjectless sentences. A further set of prepositional phrases must simply be
memorized as fixed vocabulary: *bez obzira na* "regardless of", *između ostalog* "among others",
*od strane* "by [agent]", *s jedne strane / s druge strane* "on the one hand / on the other hand",
*van sumnje* "without a doubt". (p. 318)

### §158. The dative and locative cases, overview

Dative and locative share identical case forms but serve entirely separate functions: locative
occurs exclusively after prepositions, while dative is used mostly *without* prepositions (only a
handful of prepositions take dative at all) but covers a wide range of other meanings. Feminine
nouns in *-a* often undergo a stem softening (Type A) in dative-locative singular, but not
predictably — place names, ethnic/profession/origin nouns, and stems in *-čk*/*-žg* often resist
the shift, and family-relationship nouns are especially unpredictable (dictionaries disagree with
each other). (p. 319)

### §158a. The dative without a preposition

Covers: (1) the indirect object of a two-object verb (*Pružio mu je ruku* "He extended his hand to
him"), including cases where an implicit indirect-object relationship exists without any direct
object present; (2) a set of verbs that simply require a dative object, sometimes corresponding to
an English direct object and sometimes to an English prepositional phrase; (3) "affectedness" —
signaling that someone/something is affected by the verb's action or state, covering (a) a person
experiencing a state or a desire to act (frequently in subjectless sentences, e.g. *Ne spava mi se*
"I'm not sleepy"), (b) a person simply affected by an action (*Njemu će nedostajati tečaj* "He's
going to miss the course"), (c) the "possessive dative" expressing ownership/relation (*Ćerka vam
više liči na suprugu* "Your daughter looks more like your wife"), and (d) the "ethical dative", used
to draw the addressee rhetorically into the statement (*Kako si mi danas?* "How are you today, [my
friend]?" — the dative pronoun has no literal referential content, just a rhetorical/affective
one). (pp. 319–321)

### §158b. The dative with prepositions

Very few prepositions govern the dative, and grammars disagree on some borderline cases. Widely
agreed dative-governing prepositions: *k(a)* "toward", *nasuprot* "opposite, across from",
*unatoč*/*uprkos* "despite" (both also acceptable with genitive in varying degrees). *prema* is
placed by some grammars with the dative and by others with the locative (its similarity in meaning
to *k* motivates the dative analysis). Some adverbs (*ususret*, *slično*) behave as if introducing
a dative object even though they aren't formally prepositions. (p. 321)

### §158c. The locative case

Used exclusively with prepositions, several of which also take the accusative with an added sense
of motion (§156b), while others (*o, po, prema, pri*) take only the locative. Prepositions taking
locative: *na* "on, in, at", *o* "about, concerning", *po* "through, with, by, after", *prema*
"according to", *pri* "near, with, during", *u* "in, at". *na* and *u* are by far the most frequent
(both physical-location and metaphorical-extension senses, e.g. *Na dijeti sam cijeli dan* "I'm on
a diet all day"). *o* is the most semantically consistent, almost always meaning "about, concerning"
(*O čemu govorite?* "What are you talking about?"). *po, prema, pri* each carry a wider range of
senses (manner, comparison, standard, accompanying-condition respectively). Only *u* is normally
used to specify calendar time (month, optionally year); *po* can mean "during" in some fixed
expressions, and *o* marks a point in time only in archaic usage (*o Božiću* "at Christmastime"). A
further set of fixed idiomatic phrases (e.g. *na kraju krajeva* "after all", *po mom mišljenju* "in
my opinion", *u drugom stanju* "pregnant" [euphemism], *u toku* "in progress") round out this
section as vocabulary facts rather than productive grammar. (pp. 321–324)

### §159a. The instrumental without a preposition

The instrumental singular is the only case ending in a consonant for most nouns (nearly always
*-om*); feminine consonant-stem nouns have a competing *-i*/*-u* ending, with *-u* the historically
correct but receding form (nouns in *-ost* retain *-u* more than others). Preposition-less
instrumental most frequently marks the means/instrument of an action, either concrete (*Putovao je
autobusom* "He traveled by bus") or metaphorical/manner-based (*Nagovarao me je* [manner
implied]). A closed set of verbs and adjectives require an instrumental complement whose English
equivalent is a direct object or prepositional phrase instead (*Čime se bavite?* "What [kind of]
work do you do?" — lit. "with what do you occupy yourself"). Predicate nouns can appear in the
instrumental after verbs like *zvati, činiti, pokazati se* (an alternative to the nominative
predicate construction in §155a). Adverbially, the instrumental expresses: regularly repeated time
intervals in the singular (*Nedeljom idemo na pecanje* "We go fishing on Sundays" — exception:
*zorom* "at dawn" is singular but not repeated-interval in that same sense), extended duration in
the plural (*Godinama se nismo videli* "We haven't seen each other for years"), the point within a
period (beginning/middle/end: *početkom avgusta, sredinom maja, krajem godine*), and — uniquely —
the *path* (not destination) of physical movement (*rijekom* "along the river", from a Matoš poem
example). (pp. 324–326)

### §159b. The instrumental with prepositions

A small set of prepositions govern instrumental, mostly for static spatial relationships that pair
with an accusative-governed motion-sense counterpart (§156b): *među* "among, between", *nad*
"above", *pod* "below", *pred* "before, in front of", *s(a)* "with", and *za* which splits into two
unrelated senses — *za-1* "behind, after" (spatial/sequential, e.g. *cigaretu za cigaretom* "one
cigarette after another") and *za-2* "for, longing for" (emotional, e.g. *On je lud za tobom!*
"He's crazy about you!"). *s(a)* "with" is the most frequent of these; the rule of thumb the book
gives for choosing between instrumental-alone and *s(a)* + instrumental is semantic: bare
instrumental for "by means of", *s(a)* + instrumental for "in association/together with" — English
"with" can mean either, so this is a genuine BCS/English mismatch to watch for. A further
productive layer: several nouns in the instrumental have become adverbs in their own right (*mahom*
"primarily", *posredstvom* "by means of", *rodom* "by birth", *srećom* "fortunately", *većinom*
"mostly"), and *među-* is productive as a prefix forming compound adjectives/nouns (*međunarodni*
"international", *međugradski* "interurban"). (pp. 326–328)

### §160. Expressions with "jedan drugi" (reciprocal "each other")

Reciprocal meaning ("each other", "one another") can be expressed either via *se*-verbs (*voleti
se/voljeti se* "love one another", *svađati se* "argue with one another") or — the more frequent
strategy — via the phrase *jedan drugi*: *jedan* stays nominative singular, *drugi* inflects for
whatever case the sentence structure requires, and the verb is plural. If the group has mixed
genders, *jedno* replaces *jedan*. Although grammatically singular ("one... [the] other"), *jedan
drugi* can describe general/larger-population reciprocity; but when a specific larger group's
internal cross-relationships are meant, the fully plural *jedni drugi* is required instead — this
distinction has no equivalent in English, which uses "they ... each other/one another" regardless
of exact group size or structure, so it is flagged as a genuine trouble spot for English-speaking
learners. The chapter tabulates *jedan drugi*/*jedni drugi* declined through every case (Acc., Gen.,
Dat., Loc., Instr., and a combined Instr.+Dat. construction for reciprocal multiplication:
*brojevi jednaki jedan drugom* "numbers equal to one another"), since remembering the correct case
on *drugi* (matching the grammatical role reciprocity plays in the sentence) is presented as the
chapter's own main practical difficulty. (pp. 328–329)

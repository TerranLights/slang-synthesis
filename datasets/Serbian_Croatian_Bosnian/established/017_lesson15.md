# Serbian/Croatian/Bosnian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Petnaesta lekcija / Lesson Fifteen

**Source:** Alexander, Ronelle & Elias-Bursać, Ellen. *Bosnian, Croatian, Serbian, a Textbook: With
Exercises and Basic Grammar* (2nd ed., 2010). "Petnaesta lekcija / Lesson Fifteen," pp. 233–242
(PDF pages 254–263).

**Coverage note:** every grammar point and every distinct vocabulary item from this lesson's A1/A2
`RJEČNIK`/`REČNIK` boxes, plus every explicit `[B]`/`[C]`/`[S]` dialect annotation and every
Ekavian/Ijekavian pronunciation-variant pairing the source itself marks — including several that
surface only inside the B1 comprehension questions and the C1/KONTROLNI-TEST drills rather than in
the formal vocabulary boxes (e.g. `tko`/`ko`, `šta`/`što`, `čas`/`sat`, `ùopšte`/`ùopće`,
`tačno`/`točno`). Repeated drill exercises (B2–B5, C1–C4) that reuse vocabulary/grammar already
captured are skipped per the coverage rule, except where a drill line introduces a genuinely new,
glossed lexical item or dialect pair. The PDF has a real text layer (`pdftotext -layout` produced
clean output, including the Cyrillic Serbian variants) — no vision-reading was needed for extraction,
so Vision Reading Confidence is `n/a` throughout and no marginalia concerns arise. (A page image was
consulted once, purely as an editorial cross-check on an unexpected `[B]`/`[C]` tag assignment — see
the note on `tko`/`ko` below — not because the text layer was in doubt.) The "Pingvin Charlie" story
text itself, the KONTROLNI TEST dialogue, and the B1–C4 exercise prose are not reproduced beyond
short illustrative fragments, per copyright discipline.

**Editorial note on `tko` [B] / `ko` [C]:** question 10 of exercise B1 (p. 239) tags `Tko je
Šarlòta...` as `[B]` and `Ko je Šarlòta...` as `[C]`. This is the reverse of the assignment usually
expected from general BCS knowledge (standard Croatian normally uses `tko`, standard Bosnian/Serbian
`ko`). Verified against a rendered page image to rule out an extraction artifact — the tags are
printed exactly this way in the source. Recorded here faithfully as printed, flagged for visibility
rather than silently "corrected."

## Grammar points

### Aspect choice and verbal meaning (p. 235)

Beyond a verb's dictionary meaning, the choice between imperfective and perfective aspect adds its
own layer of meaning. An imperfective verb signals one of three things: an ongoing action (`on je i
dalje tugòvao` "he continued to grieve"), a habitually repeated action (`on je plèsao za profesora`
"he [used to dance] for the professor"), or a general/standing fact (`tamo se nàlazio lijepi vrt` "a
lovely garden was located there"). A perfective verb most often denotes a single, self-contained
(usually completed) action (`shvatio je da mu mora pòmoći` "he realized he had to help him"), but can
also denote a *repeated* action when the emphasis falls on each instance being completed in turn —
illustrated by the story's `svaki put je požèlio da se vine u zrak, ali je to uvijek žalosno svr̀šilo`
"each time he wanted to rise into the air, but it always ended unhappily," where both verbs are
perfective despite describing a recurring pattern.

### Aspect and narrative (p. 235)

Aspect choice also organizes narrative structure above the sentence level: verbs that advance the
plot are almost always perfective, while verbs that set the scene or describe ongoing background are
almost always imperfective. A typical narrative opens in the imperfective to establish setting and
characters, then shifts to the perfective once the central action begins — often signaled by a phrase
like `jèdnoga dana...` ("one day..."). Background narration can alternatively be cast in the present
tense as a stylistic device called the "historic present," used markedly more often in BCS than in
English and regarded as good literary style.

### *Sve* plus the comparative (p. 235)

`sve` immediately before a comparative adjective or adverb expresses a steady, gradual increase:
`sve bolje` = "better and better," `sve više` = "more and more."

### "Each other" (p. 237)

BCS expresses English "each other" two ways: with a reflexive `se`-verb (`òni se vole` "they love
each other"), or with the phrase `jèdan drugi` (two participants) vs. `jèdni drugi` (a larger group)
— the choice matters because BCS must specify group size where English does not. The second element
of the phrase takes whatever case the governing verb or preposition requires (dative in `pòmažu
jèdan drugome`, instrumental in `ùlaze jèdan za drugim` "they enter one after another," genitive in
`sjede udaljeni jèdan od drugoga` "they sit apart from one another"); the first element defaults to
the nominative but can itself be governed by a preposition, as in `slati poruke od jèdnih do drugih`
"to send messages to one another."

### *Stati* (p. 237)

The perfective verb `stati` carries two unrelated senses. Alone, it means "to stop" (`stani!`
"stop!"). Followed by an imperfective verb naming the action that is beginning, it means "to start"
(`stati ispitìvati` "to begin testing/examining").

### *Sav* "all," *sve* "everything," *svi* "everyone" (p. 237)

The pronominal adjective `sav` "all, the entire" declines across three genders and two numbers; its
neuter singular form `sve` doubles as the free-standing pronoun "everything," and its masculine
plural form `svi` doubles as the pronoun "everyone" (`sve je bìlo dobro` "everything was all right,"
`svi su bìli tužni` "everyone was sad"). Beyond the core paradigm the source also notes a shorter
dative/locative/instrumental plural form `svem` and a longer form `svima` (full paradigm at Appendix
4, not reproduced here). The KONTROLNI TEST drill (p. 236) exercises this paradigm's oblique forms
(`svega`, `svih`, `svim`) directly.

### Infinitive vs. `da` + present across B/C/S (recurring throughout this lesson's exercises)

Not presented as its own boxed rule in this lesson, but visible as an explicit, systematic contrast
throughout the B1/B4/KONTROLNI-TEST exercise pairs: where Bosnian and Croatian phrase a following verb
as a bare infinitive (`trebate pòstaviti`, `stao ispitìvati`, `mogao odlètjeti`, `nèmojte razgovàrati`),
the Serbian equivalent regularly substitutes `da` + present tense (`треба да по̀ставите`, `стао да
испѝтује`, `могао да одлѐти`, `нѐмојте да разговарате`). Bosnian in this lesson's B1 header uses the
`da`-construction alongside the infinitive (`Evo pìtanja kòja treba da pòstavite` [B] vs. `...trebate
pòstaviti` [C]), consistent with the general B/C/S pattern (Croatian favors the infinitive most
strongly, Serbian the `da`-construction, Bosnian uses both).

## Grammar points

### Adjective-type taxonomy (introductory table, p. 201–202)

Hammond classifies Serbian adjectives into five semantic types, each cross-tagged as
inherently **indefinite** or **definite** by default: descriptive-indefinite (answers *kakav?*
"what kind"), descriptive-definite (answers *koji?* "which"), possessive-definite (answers
*čiji?* "whose"), material-indefinite (answers *od čega?* "of what material"), time-related-definite
(answers *kada?* "when"), and place-related-definite (answers *gde?* "where"). This is a
taxonomy not previously captured in this dataset as a named system — the earlier lesson-based
files document the definite/indefinite *morphological* split (`038` §3) but not this semantic
type-to-default-form mapping.

### Hard vs. soft adjective bases (§9.1.1, p. 203)

An adjective's **base** is its stem without the feminine/neuter gender endings (*slobodna/slobodno*
→ base *slobodn-*). The base is classified **soft** if it ends in one of a fixed consonant set
(ć, đ, nj, c, n, č, š, ž, dž, j, l/lj — rendered ambiguously by the source's Cyrillic-heavy layout
but corresponding to the palatal/palatalized consonant series used elsewhere in the grammar for
softening rules) and **hard** otherwise. This hard/soft split is the single governing mechanism
behind the `-o` (hard) vs. `-e` (soft) alternation that recurs throughout the genitive, dative, and
locative singular endings and the neuter nominative singular — previously this project's files
stated the `-o`/`-e` alternation piecemeal per case (e.g. `038` §3) without naming the base-final
consonant as the conditioning rule.

### Adjectives after dva/tri/četiri: a distinct masculine/neuter `-a` ending (p. 205–206)

Independent of the noun count-form already documented for nouns following 2–4, **adjectives**
modifying a masculine or neuter noun after *dva* "two," *tri* "three," *četiri* "four" always take
the ending `-a` regardless of case elsewhere expected (e.g. *dva lepa vuka* "two beautiful wolves").
This is a distinct agreement rule from the noun-side count-form and was not previously documented at
the adjective-agreement level.

### Indefinite-only adjective classes (§9.2, p. 205–206)

Three classes of adjectives exist **only** in the indefinite form: (1) possessive adjectives formed
from nouns via `-ov`/`-ev`/`-ljev`/`-in` (see Possessive adjectives below) retain the indefinite
form in the nominative/accusative-inanimate, though definite-form endings may substitute in the
remaining cases including accusative-animate; (2) the numeral *jedan* "one," used with the sense of
English "a/an"; (3) adjectives whose base ends in two consonants (other than the clusters
`-st-`/`-zd-`/`-št-`/`-žd-`, which tolerate standing together) insert a fleeting `-a-` before the
final consonant in the masculine singular (*vedra* → *vedar* "clear").

### Declension paradigms (§9.2, §9.3, §9.5, p. 206–213)

The source gives three full paradigm charts not previously assembled in this dataset in chart form:
an **indefinite-declension** chart (singular/plural × masc./neut./fem.) — noting explicitly that
except in the masculine/neuter nominative-singular and the instrumental singular (`-im` instead of
the noun-declension `-om`), indefinite adjectives otherwise decline exactly like first-declension
masculine/neuter nouns; a **definite-declension** chart, with a genitive/dative/locative
short-vs-long variant already covered by `038` §3 but here laid out as a complete case×gender×number
grid; and a **masculine/neuter singular indefinite-vs-definite comparison** chart isolating exactly
where the two paradigms diverge (nominative, and the -og(a)/-om(e/u) hard-vs-soft alternation in the
oblique cases).

### Possessive adjectives: formation and capitalization (§9.4, p. 209–211)

Possessive adjectives are formed from singular nouns denoting persons, animals, deities, or proper
names via `-ov` (masc./neut. base ending in a hard consonant), `-ev` (soft consonant), `-ljev` (base
ending in `-v`), or `-in` (feminine and masculine nouns ending in `-a`, dropped before the suffix).
They decline as definite adjectives. **Genuinely new here:** a capitalization rule not previously
documented — possessive adjectives in `-ov`/`-ev`/`-in` are **capitalized** when derived from a
nationality, proper name, surname, deity name, or nickname (*Srbinov*, *Markov*, *Anina*), but
adjectives in `-ski`/`-čki`/`-ški` derived from a country/city/area name are **not** capitalized
(*engleski*, *evropski*, *haški*) — except that the first word of a formal title or the name of a
region/administrative unit is capitalized regardless (*Prva armijska oblast* "the First Military
District"). The source also notes the noun a possessive adjective was built from can always
substitute for it (*šešir moje sestre* = *sestrin šešir* "my sister's hat").

### Verbal adjectives — active past participle as fixed formula (§9.8.1, p. 219)

Beyond its role forming the past tense and compound tenses (already extensively documented), the
indeclinable active past participle (the "-l participle") also appears as a fixed **salutation/toast
or curse formula** with no accompanying auxiliary or subject — *Živeli!* "Cheers!" and *Živeo
kralj!* "Long live the King!" This bare-participle-as-exclamation usage was not previously
documented in this dataset's participle coverage.

### Passive participle → deverbal noun (§9.8.4, p. 222)

The source notes explicitly that the passive past participle is the base for a productive
**deverbal noun** category, parallel to English nouns in `-ing`/`-ion`: *rešiti* "resolve" →
participle *rešen* → noun *rešenje* "resolution"; *osloboditi* "liberate" → participle *oslobođen*
→ noun *oslobođenje* "liberation." `043_grammar_ch18_word_formation.md` cross-references this
`-nje` verbal-noun suffix as belonging to an earlier chapter without exemplifying the specific
participle-to-noun derivational link; this file supplies the worked examples connecting the two.

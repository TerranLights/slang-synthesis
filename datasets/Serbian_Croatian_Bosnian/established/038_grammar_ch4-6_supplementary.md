## Grammar points (new/deeper material only — see scope note above)

### 1. Systematic present-tense verb-type classification (§40, §52, §65)
The reference grammar organizes irregular present-tense verbs into **named conjugation types**, each
identified by a representative model verb — a systematization the teaching-lesson format introduces
piecemeal without naming. Types covered across Chapters 4–6: **type ići** (infinitive in -ći,
e-conjugation, present stem in -d: idem, ideš...); **type kazivati** (-ivati → -uj- in present,
e.g. kazujem); **type jesti** (infinitive -sti → present stem in -d: jedem); **type držati**
(i-conjugation verbs whose accent does not shift between infinitive and present: stojim, držim,
also bojati se, zviždati); **type doći** (prefixed ići-compounds, e-conjugation, present stem in -đ,
except otići which has both -d- and -đ- stems, 1sg. odem normally preferred over otiđem); **type
pisati** (stem-final consonant softens between infinitive and present: s~š, z~ž, t~ć, h~š, c~č, and
a sixth pattern g~ž as in pomagati→pomažem, polagati→polažem); **verbs with no consonant shift**
within the pisati type because the stem consonant is already "soft" (orati/orem, trajati/trajem,
počinjati/počinjem); **type uzeti** (theme vowel e replaced by m in present: uzmem; also počneti,
počnem "begin" with e→n); **type slati** (inserts -a- into the present stem AND shifts both the
initial consonant s→š and final consonant l→lj: šaljem, unique double-shift pattern, contrasted with
zvati's single vowel-insertion pattern zovem).

### 2. Possessive pronominal adjectives: the five-step formation process and stem-shift table (§41b)
Formation of a possessive adjective from a personal name or human noun is defined as a five-step
process: (1) isolate the noun stem; (2) apply any required stem shift; (3) choose the suffix by the
possessor's gender (-in- if feminine; -ov-/-ev- if masculine or neuter, with -ev- after soft
consonants); (4) add the suffix; (5) add regular adjective endings. **The one gender-based
exception:** masculine personal names ending in -a (e.g. Nikola) take -in-, not -ov-/-ev-, despite
referring to a male. Stem-shift rules, given as a worked table: stem-final -k/-c shifts to -č before
the suffix (Ankica→Ankič-→Ankičin); a stem ending in the sequence -ov gets an inserted -lj- before
the suffix (Jakov→Jakovlj-→Jakovljev); several common kinship/nature nouns show idiosyncratic
consonant shifts not predictable from the -k/-c rule alone (majka→majč-→majčin; otac→oc-→oč-→očev;
sunce→sunč-→sunčev). These adjectives exist ONLY in short form and can be formed only from single
words — a full name or an "adjective+noun" possessor phrase requires the genitive case instead
(cross-referenced as the adnominal genitive, §44a/§44b).

### 3. The three-way short/long/longer adjective system in Gsg. AND DLsg. (§42c, §66b)
Beyond the nominative short-vs-long distinction already known from earlier lessons, masculine-neuter
adjectives in the genitive singular (and, by the same logic, the Asg. of masculine animates) have
**three** possible forms: short (identical to the Gsg. noun ending, -og/-eg), long (the "definite"
form, -og/-eg with an extra -a: e.g. dobroga), and longer (adds -a to the long ending). Most
adjectives have all three; some (moj, tvoj) have only long and longer, each with a contracted variant
(mog vs. mojeg; moga vs. mojega) — B and S use only the contracted forms. The historical short-vs-
long = indefinite-vs-definite meaning distinction is explicitly flagged by the author as now valid
**only in the nominative, and not consistently even there** — in Gsg./DLsg. the three forms are
essentially stylistic variants, with short forms restricted to set phrases (though increasingly
frequent in Croatian, especially for possessives like njegov, očev), long forms neutral throughout
BCS, and longer forms more frequent in careful/high style and more frequent in Croatian than
Serbian/Bosnian; the longer form is REQUIRED when the adjective stands alone for a noun. In the
**dative-locative singular**, the parallel three-way system exists but with an added wrinkle: two
distinct longer-form endings are possible, -e and -u (dobrome vs. dobromu). Croatian grammarians
assign these a case distinction (-e = locative meaning, -u = dative meaning) though usage is not
fully consistent; Bosnian and Serbian use the two forms interchangeably, and Croatian uses the
longer forms overall more frequently than B/S.

### 4. Masculine nouns/adjectives with -o/-l alternation, including the reverse pattern (§47)
Nouns and adjectives ending in vowel+o shift -o to -l whenever a full-vowel case ending follows
(paralleling the familiar -o→-l shift in short-form adjectives). The vowel preceding -o determines
the outcome: -eo→-el-, -io→-ijel-, but -ao drops the -a- entirely and becomes simply -l- (e.g.
posao→posla, not *poslao). Not all BCS varieties make this shift — Croatian's sto "table" stays sto
in Gsg. (Croatian stola is actually the same shift; contrast is stol vs. sto as the Nsg. form itself,
per source). **Exception to watch:** misao "thought" (Gsg. misli) looks like the masculine -o/-l
class but is feminine — flagged by the source as a genuine gender trap. A **reverse** version of the
same phenomenon occurs in agent nouns like čitalac/čitaoca "reader" and pratilac/pratioca "escort":
here a syllable-final -l shifts to -o whenever the fleeting vowel is lost (i.e., before a vowel
ending, not before -Ø), the mirror image of the adjective pattern — and this reverse process is
restricted to nouns denoting an agent/performer of an action (palac "thumb, toe," Gsg. palca, does
NOT undergo it).

### 5. Genitive plural: the three endings and their full conditioning (§57)
This is the single largest genuinely-new grammar block in Chapters 4–6 relative to the lesson
extraction (`007_lesson05.md` covers Gpl. only at introductory depth). Three Gpl. endings exist for
nouns — -a, -i, -u (the last vanishingly rare/irregular) — plus a single universal adjective ending
-ih. All three noun endings are **distinctively long**, even for speakers who don't otherwise
maintain vowel length in unaccented syllables. **The -a ending** (used by all neuters, nearly all
masculines, and many feminines) frequently requires **inserting an extra -a-** before the ending
when the stem ends in two consonants (other than the clusters -st, -zd, -št, -žd, -žđ, -šć, -šč,
which block insertion) — creating an extra syllable absent from Nsg. This inserted -a- is
historically/functionally continuous with the "fleeting -a-" phenomenon in masculine nouns (§42b):
for nouns like pas→psa (Gsg.), the Gpl. pasa reinstates the vowel and can even reverse an earlier
voicing-assimilation or -l/-o shift (pratilac→Gpl. pratilaca, restoring the l). Some feminine nouns
show genuinely unpredictable stem consonants in Gpl. not found in any other form of the word:
sveska→svezaka (inserted z), bilješka→bilježaka (inserted ž). **The -i ending** is obligatory for
all feminine nouns in -Ø (consonant-stem), used by only three masculine nouns consistently (sat
"hour" — but NOT sat "clock," which takes Gpl. satova, a genuine lexical-meaning-conditioned minimal
pair — mesec, and the plurale-tantum ljudi), and is a common but unpredictable variant for many
feminine -a nouns whose stems end in two consonants, with certain consonant clusters favoring -i
over -a. Overall the noun-gender/Gpl.-ending correlation the earlier singular-case tables relied on
breaks down almost completely in Gpl., since -a and -i both cut across genders.

### 6. Motion-verb prefix + imperfective derivation table (§54)
A single systematic table derives seven specialized motion-verb pairs from the base verb ići by
prefixation, then derives the matching imperfective by **wholesale stem replacement** with the
otherwise rare verb laziti "creep, crawl" (not by suffixation, unlike most other aspect pairs) — a
genuinely distinct and more complete presentation than the lesson files' treatment of individual
motion verbs. Pairs: do-/dolaziti (come), u-/ulaziti (enter), iz-/izlaziti (leave/exit), po-/polaziti
(set out), s-/silaziti (come down), od-/odlaziti (go away), na-/nalaziti (find) — the last one
notable for a fully abstracted, non-literal meaning ("find," not "go onto").

### 7. Aspect-pair typology: suffixation vs. prefixation, with the mechanics of each (§53)
The reference grammar names and separates two structural mechanisms for forming aspect pairs, more
explicitly than the lesson's presentation: **suffixation pairs**, where the perfective is typically
i-conjugation and the imperfective a-conjugation, via either a stem-vowel change (odgovoriti→
odgovarati, "answer") or an inserted suffix before the theme vowel (uključiti→uključivati, "include"
— this imperfective type exceptionally belongs to the e-conjugation despite looking a-final);
**prefixation pairs**, where a base imperfective verb becomes perfective by adding a prefix that
typically layers on an additional shade of meaning beyond pure perfectivity (čekati→dočekati "wait"
→"wait for and receive/meet"; jesti→pojesti, piti→popiti — the "eat up"/"drink up" sense specifically
glossed as "consume in full on one occasion"). Also newly explicit: perfective verbs can appear as
infinitives after another verb or in present tense after certain conjunctions (contexts of "eventual
completion"), and the choice between perfective/imperfective in these same frames is a genuine
meaning choice, not free variation.

### 8. Negative pronoun/adverb paradigm and "double negation" is obligatory (§56a)
A single table systematizes the ni- prefix's productivity across interrogative words: ko/tko→niko/
nitko, šta/što→ništa, kakav→nikakav, kako→nikako, kada→nikada, gde/gdje→nigde/nigdje, čiji→ničiji,
and — notably included despite not being a question word — jedan→nijedan, because its negative
partner follows the same formation pattern. Only three BCS verbs (imati, biti, hteti/htjeti) have
fully separate negative present-tense conjugations formed by fusing the negative particle with the
clitic (not full) form; only imati/nemati are treated as two fully separate verbs including in the
infinitive. Critically, and unlike English, **BCS requires "double" (in fact multiple) negation**:
the presence of any one negative pronoun/adverb in a sentence requires negation to be marked on the
verb AND on every other markable element (Nikoga ne poznajem tamo — "I don't know anyone there," not
*I know no one there with an affirmative verb*).

### 9. The XYZ clitic-ordering model, formalized (§64, §76)
The reference grammar gives the clitic slot Y a fully explicit six-subslot formula, presented across
two installments (Ch. 5 introduces slots via se-verb and simple-pronoun examples; Ch. 6 extends it to
dative+accusative double-object and subjectless-dative-sentence patterns): **Y-1** = li (question
particle); **Y-2** = be(aux) — clitic forms of biti used as auxiliary (present "am/are," and, in the
same slot, the future-tense and conditional-mood auxiliary clitics, per forward cross-reference); **Y-
3** = dative clitic pronoun object; **Y-4** = accusative-genitive clitic pronoun object; **Y-5** = se;
**Y-6** = je, but ONLY the 3sg. form of biti (the homophonous accusative-genitive 3sg. fem. pronoun
je obligatorily occupies Y-4, not Y-6). The model claims to account for every possible clitic
combination; sentences filling five of six slots are called vanishingly rare (constructed mainly by
linguists), while two-three clitics are common and four are occasionally heard. A specific disambig-
uation rule follows from slot conflict: when both the Y-4 accusative pronoun je ("her/it") and the Y-6
auxiliary je ("is") would co-occur, most speakers substitute the alternate accusative form ju to avoid
the je+je sequence — obligatory in Croatian, optional (both forms heard) in Bosnian, standard practice
in Serbian too, per the worked sentence set (§64).

### 10. Count vs. mass nouns: BCS/English category mismatches (§59a)
Adverbs of measure (mnogo, malo, dosta, nekoliko, koliko) take Gpl. with count nouns but Gsg. with
mass nouns — but the reference grammar explicitly warns that **the count/mass categorization itself
does not transfer from English**: kruh/hleb/hljeb "bread" and novac "money" behave as EITHER count
or mass in BCS depending on context (dva hljeba "two loaves," nemam novaca "I have no money [pieces
of]"), while English advice and information are mass-only but their BCS equivalents (savet/savjet,
obaveštenje/obavijest) are count-only (dva saveta "two pieces of advice"). This is a systematic
category-mismatch warning not present in the lesson-level treatment.

### 11. "Existentials" ima/nema compared cross-linguistically (§59b)
The 3sg. forms of imati/nemati, used in the "there is/are [some]" / "there isn't/aren't [any]"
existential sense (as opposed to ordinary "have/not have"), are explicitly compared to French il y a
/ il n'y a pas, German es gibt / es gibt kein, Russian есть / нет, and Spanish hay / no hay — framing
BCS existential syntax as a broader cross-linguistic pattern, useful comparative-linguistics framing
absent from the pedagogical lesson.

### 12. u-word / na-word inventory and the three-way motion/location/origin case-choice chart (§67a–c)
A much larger inventory of "u-words" and "na-words" than given at lesson level, including pairs where
the SAME noun takes u in one sense and na in a distinct sense with a real meaning difference (u
selu "in this village" vs. na selu "in the countryside"; u sudu "in the courthouse" vs. na sudu "in
court" [i.e., on trial]; u poslu "while doing a job" vs. na poslu "at work"; u moru "in the sea
[water]" vs. na moru "at the seaside"; u zemlji "in the earth/soil" vs. na zemlji "on the ground").
The chapter also formalizes the three-way system explicitly as a table: point of origin (Genitive,
prepositions iz/s(a)) ⊗⇒ destination (Accusative, u/na) ⊗ location (Locative, u/na) — the same
preposition pairs consistently across all three, conditioned by whether the noun is inherently a
u-word or na-word, not by the case/meaning axis.

### 13. Country and language names as adjectives — full case paradigm (§66f)
Several major country names (Francuska, Njemačka, Engleska, Španjolska) are underlyingly feminine
singular adjectives, which is invisible in Nom/Acc/Gen (identical to feminine -a noun endings) but
becomes apparent in DLsg., where they take adjective endings (-oj) rather than noun endings (-i).
Language names are likewise adjectival and — because they are grammatically masculine — must take
masculine adjective endings even in Gsg./DLsg. (francuskog, francuskom), a fact flagged as an easy
error source for English speakers since English treats both categories as ordinary nouns.
Orthographic side-note: adjectives formed with -ski/-čki/-ćki/-ški from a proper noun are NOT
capitalized unless they begin a proper name (beogradski način života vs. Beogradski univerzitet).

### 14. Croatian vs. Serbian/Bosnian month-name systems (§67d)
Croatian uses a fully native-Slavic 12-month name series (siječanj, veljača, ožujak, travanj,
svibanj, lipanj, srpanj, kolovoz, rujan, listopad, studeni, prosinac) entirely distinct in form from
the Latinate Serbian/Bosnian series (januar, februar, mart, april, maj, juni, juli, avgust,
septembar, oktobar, novembar, decembar) — not a matter of phonological variants (like most B/C/S
lexical splits) but two etymologically unrelated naming systems. Grammatical quirk: BCS uses the
same preposition u for locating an event both on a day of the week (+Acc.) and within a month
(+Loc.) — u oktobru "in October." Studeni (Croatian November) is grammatically an adjective and
takes masculine adjective endings, unlike the other eleven month names.

### 15. The ethical dative / "dative of affect," including its subjectless-sentence use (§73f–g)
Beyond ordinary indirect-object and possessive datives, the source identifies a broader category
it calls "dative of affect," including a colloquial usage it explicitly labels the **ethical dative**:
1st/2nd-person dative clitics added purely to mark conversational inclusiveness/rapport, nearly
untranslatable into English, especially frequent after the presentative ono and the location-
indicator eto (Kako si mi ti danas? — roughly "How are you today, [my friend]?"). Subjectless
sentences describing a human's state (Meni je toplo "I'm warm," Njima je dosadno "They're bored")
are analyzed as a further instance of the same general category: BCS grammatically ascribes the
state to the person via the dative rather than making the person the syntactic subject, unlike
English.

### 16. činiti se and the "seems" construction's real meaning (§73h)
činiti se "seem" (+ Dat experiencer) forms subjectless sentences, but the source specifically warns
against literal English translation as "it seems to me" — arguing that čini mi se... carries
noticeably more real-world epistemic commitment from the speaker than the impersonal English
phrasing suggests, and is better rendered "I think..."

### 17. Four-way se-verb classification (§74)
The source organizes se-verb behavior into four types, more explicit than the lesson-level
treatment: **Group 1** — verbs that exist ONLY with se (dešavati se "happen," bojati se "fear,"
sastojati se [iz] "consist of"); **Group 2** — verbs with a totally different, often subjectless,
meaning with se than without (činiti "make" vs. čini se "it seems"; raditi "do/work" vs. radi se [o]
"it's a matter of"); **Group 3** — se creates a genuine active/passive alternation (slušati "take [a
class]" vs. slušati se "be taken [as a class, by students generally]"; jesti "eat" vs. jesti se "be
eaten"); **Group 4** — pairs where se both marks a passive-like sense AND has developed enough
independent lexical identity that dictionaries disagree on whether to list them separately (nalaziti
"find" vs. nalaziti se "be located"; zvati "call" vs. zvati se "be named" — the latter explicitly
marked "passive?" by the source itself, flagging the analytic uncertainty).

### 18. vrata and kola: plural form, singular meaning, and neuter (not feminine) gender (§75)
A small but grammatically important noun class: nouns ending in -a that LOOK like feminine singulars
but are actually **neuter plural** nouns referring to a single object — vrata "door" (no singular
form exists at all for this concept) and kola "car" (increasingly replaced by singular auto in
Croatian, but still common in Bosnian/Serbian). All modifying adjectives and verbs must agree in
plural. Explicitly flagged by the source as a genuine gender/number trap for learners.

# Serbian — Established Vocabulary: Folk Tales, Part 2 (end of "Баш-Челик"; "Златна јабука и девет пауница"; "Стојша и Младен")

**Source:** Simeon Marinković & Slavica Marković (eds.), *Српске народне бајке* (Serbian Folk Fairy
Tales), Kreativni centar, 2000. Printed pages 33–60 (PDF pages 37–64, offset +4 verified
empirically by direct inspection — see below).

**Type of source: literary/folkloric narrative anthology, NOT a pedagogical grammar/vocabulary
textbook.** No parallel English translation, no vocabulary boxes. Coverage approach for this
chunk is deliberately **vocabulary-only** — no plot/prose reproduction, per the dispatch
instructions for this copyrighted literary source. Each tale gets a one-line orientation note only.

**Vision-reading guard:** this is a scanned book with no usable text layer, entirely in Cyrillic —
every page in the assigned range was read visually, not via `pdftotext`. Every page was checked
for handwritten marginalia (pen/pencil marks distinct from the book's own printed content). **No
handwritten marginalia was found anywhere in this range** — every mark on every page (including
the recurring "+"-marked margin glosses) is clearly typeset, consistent with the surrounding
printed body text and the book's own design (yellow serif "+" marker, sans-serif gloss text block).
These "+" marginal glosses are the book's own explanatory apparatus (glossing archaic/regional/
specialized vocabulary for young modern readers) and were extracted directly as source content, not
excluded as marginalia.

**Offset verification:** PDF page 37 = printed page 33 (confirmed by direct page-number inspection);
PDF page 64 = printed page 60 (confirmed). Offset +4 holds consistently across the whole range — no
two-pages-per-image spread issue found here (each rendered image is one printed page).

**Tales covered in this chunk:**
1. **"Баш-Челик" (Bash-Chelik / "True Steel"), pp. 33–37** — final portion only (this chunk picks
   up mid-story; the tale's opening was covered by a separate "part 1" chunk). The prince rescues
   his abducted wife from the supernatural being Baš-Čelik across several escape attempts, aided by
   eagle-, falcon-, and dragon-king brothers-in-law; the tale ends with Baš-Čelik's death via the
   fox/duck/heart-in-bird soul-object motif.
2. **"Златна јабука и девет пауница" (The Golden Apple and the Nine Peahens), pp. 39–51** — a
   prince guards a magic golden-apple tree, wins a shapeshifting peahen-maiden as wife, loses her
   to an old crone's treachery, and recovers her with the help of a grateful fish, fox, and wolf
   (the "brother by God" / animal-helper motif) plus a barrel-imprisoned dragon and a witch's
   horse-testing ordeal.
3. **"Стојша и Младен" (Stojša and Mladen), pp. 53–60** — start of the tale only (this chunk's
   range ends mid-story). An orphaned prince, Stojša, learns his three sisters vanished in a
   whirlwind years before his birth; he sets out, finds each sister married to a dragon-brother,
   and (in the portion covered here) wrestles and subdues the first two dragon brothers-in-law
   before setting off to find a third, hostile dragon-king.

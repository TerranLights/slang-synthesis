# Serbian/Croatian/Bosnian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Devetnaesta lekcija / Lesson Nineteen

**Source:** Ronelle Alexander and Ellen Elias-Bursać, *Bosnian, Croatian, Serbian, a Textbook: With
Exercises and Basic Grammar* (2nd ed., 2010), Lesson Nineteen, pp. 287–300 (PDF pages 308–321).

**Coverage note:** every distinct vocabulary item from the lesson's six RJEČNIK[J]/REČNIK[E]
vocabulary boxes (sections A1–A3), plus every explicit `[B]`/`[C]`/`[S]`-style dialect tag and the
lesson's one substantive prose grammar/dialect note. The B1–B3 (in-class exercises/comprehension
questions) and C1–C3 (homework translation drills, biographical-summary translation exercises)
sections were read in full but contain no new vocabulary or grammar beyond what the A-section boxes
and the Montenegrin-dialect note already introduce — these are drill material and are skipped per the
coverage rule, except where a drill exercise's own bracket-tagged translation variants
(`[B]`/`[C]`/`[S]`) surface a genuinely new lexical or syntactic distinction not already captured,
which are noted below. This source has a clean PDF text layer (`pdftotext` extracted it directly, no
vision-reading was needed), so no marginalia-guard issue applies and Vision Reading Confidence is
`n/a` throughout.

**Structural note on grammar coverage:** this lesson introduces **no new named grammar point**.
A full-text search of the book confirms every other lesson's grammar explanations are marked with a
"GRAMMAR" header (rendered as `zGRAMMAR` in the extracted text, evidently a preceding icon glyph);
no such header occurs anywhere between the start of Lesson Nineteen (p. 287) and the end of this
assigned range (p. 300). This matches the book's own framing (Introduction, p. xiv-ish paraphrase):
Lessons 16–20 shift the pedagogical focus from grammar acquisition to reading practice, and brief
grammar explanations accompany only *most*, not all, of those lessons. Lesson Nineteen is built
around three literary readings (a Vasko Popa poem, a Desanka Maksimović poem, and an excerpt of a
Montenegrin epic song) plus a short expository reading on the South Slavic epic-poetry tradition; the
C1 homework translation sentences drill grammar taught in earlier lessons (the `god`-particle
"whoever/whatever/wherever" construction, purpose clauses with "in order to," conditional "as if")
rather than introducing anything new, so per the coverage rule these repeated-drill sentences are not
re-extracted as if they were new grammar points. The lesson's one genuine piece of expository
grammar/dialect content — a prose note on Montenegrin as a distinct BCS variant — is written up under
**Grammar points** below.

### Montenegrin as a distinct ijekavian variant (p. 296)

Following the excerpt of the epic song "Sibinjanin Janko u tamnicu u Tatarinu gradu" (sung by
Stanko Pižurica, recorded by Milman Parry in Kolašin, June 1935), the book adds an explicit
sidebar on Montenegrin as a fourth national standard alongside Bosnian, Croatian, and Serbian
(note it does not receive its own bracket-tag letter in this book's `[B]`/`[C]`/`[S]` system — the
book instead advises Montenegrin-focused students to follow the `[J]` and `[S]` forms). The book
identifies three concrete markers:

1. **Systematic ijekavian character**, extending beyond vowel reflexes into the negated forms of
   *biti* ("to be") — `nijèsam`, `nijèsi`, etc. — and into certain adjectival endings, e.g.
   `starijem` (vs. standard `starim`), which the book notes are archaic elsewhere in BCS but are
   the norm in Montenegrin.
2. **Consonant realization of the `tj`/`dj` sequences** found elsewhere in ijekavian regions:
   Montenegrin pronounces these as `ć`/`đ` — e.g. `tjerati` → `ćerati`, `djèvojka` → `đèvojka`,
   `[g]dje` → `đe`.
3. **A handful of vocabulary items/pronunciations** felt to be characteristically Montenegrin,
   e.g. `sjutra` ("tomorrow") and `zbòriti` ("to speak").

The book also notes, in overall terms, that Montenegrin vocabulary has more in common with Serbian
than with Bosnian or Croatian. The epic-song text itself is offered as a worked illustration: the
book flags `đe`, `pređe`, `viđet'`, and `nijèsi` in Pižurica's song (a Montenegrin singer, though
the song's own narrative is set in Sibinj, Croatia) as "distinctly Montenegrin" forms — an explicit
case of the source using a single text to demonstrate that the *content* of an epic song and the
*dialect* it is sung in can diverge, since the epic-song tradition itself is described as pan-BCS
rather than region-specific ("it is part of the cultural heritage wherever Bosnian, Croatian, and
Serbian are spoken").

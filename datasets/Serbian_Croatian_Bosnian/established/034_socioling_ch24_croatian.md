## Grammar/Sociolinguistic points

### Croatian dialectal and regional complexity (§178)

Croatian identity spans all three major South Slavic dialects (čakavian, kajkavian, štokavian) plus
several regionally distinct speech areas (Istria, Slavonia, the Kvarner coast, Dalmatia including
Rijeka, Zadar, Split, and islands such as Hvar, Vis, Korčula; Dubrovnik/Ragusa; "Military Croatia"
along the Austrian border zone/Krajina vs. "Civil Croatia" around Zagreb). The source frames
forging this diversity into one standard language as a genuinely difficult task, and notes the
Croatian coat of arms still symbolically represents five regional components (Istria, Slavonia,
Dalmatia, Dubrovnik, Croatia proper). p. 409.

### Pre-standardization prestige speech and the Illyrian compromise (§179)

The first printed Croatian grammar (Bartol Kašić, *Institutionae linguae illyricae*, 1604) described
contemporary prestige speech as primarily čakavian with štokavian elements — a fact the source says
many Croats now consider more representative of their heritage than the later uni-dialectal
štokavian standard. The 1836 Illyrian proposal chose štokavian ijekavian (for eventual South Slavic
unity) but paired it with morphophonemic ("eye" rather than "ear") spelling and retention of
archaic case endings, producing the "ahavci" vs. "vukovci" factional split described above. The
source also notes the irony that by the time ijekavian became Zagreb's norm, Serbian usage had
already shifted to ekavian. A secondary, persistent Croatian grievance (through the JAZU dictionary
project and beyond) was that major reference dictionaries privileged štokavian folklore vocabulary
over the language's fuller multi-dialectal lexicon. pp. 409-411.

### The Kosovo myth as pan-Yugoslav symbol, and the collapse of interwar unity (§180)

Croats were active early promoters of Yugoslav unity — including proposing that the 14th-century
Battle of Kosovo be adopted as a shared (not exclusively Serbian) national myth, with Bishop
Strossmayer organizing a 1889 Zagreb commemoration and Dalmatian artists (Ivo Vojnović, Ivan
Meštrović) producing Kosovo-themed works. Post-WWI unity, formalized under the language name
*srpsko-hrvatsko-slovenački*, quickly soured as Croats perceived Belgrade-centered governance;
tensions culminated in the 1928 parliamentary shooting that killed Croat leader Stjepan Radić and
King Alexander's 1929 suspension of the constitution and imposition of a unitary Serb-based system.
The source reports this as the direct trigger for Ante Pavelić's founding of the Ustashe movement
in Italy. pp. 411-412.

### Language policy 1929-1941 and the kajkavian literary revival (§180a)

The 1929 reorganization imposed Belgrade-based (Aleksandar Belić) spelling and terminology on all
Yugoslav schoolbooks. In response, Croatian writers/intellectuals turned to kajkavian, Zagreb's
pre-Illyrian literary dialect — notably a 1936 linguistic study, *Jezik Hrvata kajkavaca*, and
Miroslav Krleža's *Balade Petrice Kerempuha*, a kajkavian poetic work the source describes as
centered on a bitter lament for the "funeral of KAJ" (i.e., of the kajkavian dialect/identity
itself). The 1939 agreement created a quasi-independent Croatia within Yugoslavia, followed in 1940
by the booklet *Razlike između hrvatskoga i srpskoga jezika*, which the source describes as
methodologically flawed (treating vocabulary as cleanly nationally partitionable) but nonetheless
"wildly successful" in Croatia. pp. 412-413.

### The NDH's language purism as state policy (§181)

Following the April 1941 German attack on Belgrade, Pavelić founded the NDH within days, which
immediately banned Cyrillic and established the Hrvatski državni ured za jezik with a fourfold
mandate: monitoring language use, producing textbooks/reference materials, enforcing language laws,
and conducting linguistic propaganda (including weekly radio broadcasts from May 1941 and mandatory
newspaper notices). Its central legal act (August 1941) mandated morphophonemic over phonetic
spelling — reviving the Illyrian "eye not ear" principle by law — and specifically respelled the
ijekavian sequence *-ije-* as a diphthong written without *-j-* (*korijen* → *korien*). A 1942
manual, *Koriensko pisanje*, was issued to standardize implementation after a four-month grace
period, after which misspelling could be treated as grounds for arrest on suspicion of anti-Ustashe
sympathy. The source states this was explicitly framed by the regime as "cleansing" the language of
Serbian-based "impurities," and that purist sentiment persisted rhetorically even in the regime's
final (1945) public statements. This material describes the documented policy record of a historical
fascist state as reported by the source; it is presented here as the source's own historical account,
not as this project's endorsement or characterization beyond what the source states. pp. 411-413.

### Titoist four-language policy and the Novi Sad agreement (§182)

Postwar Communist Yugoslavia, seeking broad-based legitimacy (having promised Croats and
Macedonians separate administrative and linguistic status), proclaimed four official languages
(Slovenian, Macedonian, Serbian, Croatian) under the slogan *bratstvo i jedinstvo* ("brotherhood and
unity"). In language policy specifically, this trajectory produced the 1954 Novi Sad agreement,
proclaiming a single language in two equal instantiations ("Serbo-Croatian"/"Croato-Serbian"), with
parallel orthographic manuals and dictionaries assigned to Matica srpska (Novi Sad) and Matica
hrvatska (Zagreb) — each using its own alphabet/pronunciation while listing the other's. Orthographic
manuals appeared on schedule (1960), but by the mid-1960s the east-west differences were understood
to exceed alphabet/pronunciation alone, prompting adoption of the technical term *varijanta*
("variant") in 1965. pp. 413-414.

### The Croatian Declaration and the Croatian Spring (§182a)

The 1967 appearance of the joint dictionary's first volumes convinced Croatian intellectuals that
Serbian speech models were being imposed on them, leading to the March 1967 Deklaracija, which
repudiated the Novi Sad agreement, withdrew from the joint dictionary project, and declared Croatian
the official language within the Croatian republic — while explicitly affirming continued political
unity and a shared federal-level language, and assuming Serbs held a reciprocal right to declare
Serbian official within their own republic. Initial Serb response (the document *Predlog za
razmišljanje*) accepted this framing, though the source calls it a minority position. The Titoist
government's 1968 condemnation of "nationalist ideas" framed as scientific linguistic discussion did
not halt the movement, which grew into the broader "Croatian Spring" (Dalibor Brozović's 1970 book
and 1971 "Ten Theses on the Croatian Language"). Tito's December 1, 1971 crackdown purged both
Croatian and Serbian politicians/intellectuals and led to the destruction of the nearly-finished new
Croatian orthographic manual within Yugoslavia; it was subsequently published in London from a
smuggled manuscript copy and circulated among the diaspora. The 1974 constitution finally let each
republic set its own "local idiom," and Croatia immediately adopted the Croatian standard for this
purpose. pp. 414-415.

### Post-1991 language purification and "novohrvatski" (§183)

Following 1991 independence, decades of prior planning culminated in an active, sometimes intense,
public campaign (manuals, newspaper columns, dictionaries) to promote lexical purity — avoiding
anything perceived as Serbian-derived, including many ordinary foreign loanwords — with words either
freshly coined or revived from earlier historical usage. The source notes both types felt equally
unfamiliar/"new" to ordinary speakers, giving rise to the term *novohrvatski* ("new Croatian"). The
1992 reissue of the NDH-era *Koriensko pisanje* manual raised (but the source says did not realize)
fears of a repeat of Ustashe-style language policing; it was mostly read as a historical document
rather than adopted as active practice. Use of "new Croatian" forms carried practical political
weight during Franjo Tuđman's nationalist presidency (1991-1999), though public reception was mixed,
as illustrated by the *zrakomlat*/*helikopter* dictionary-entry example above, which the source
treats as emblematic evidence of skepticism toward the more radical purist coinages. The source
closes the chapter by framing Croatia's language-planning project as an open-ended object of future
sociolinguistic study, since only continued usage will show which innovations actually take root.
pp. 415.

## Grammar points

### Verb conjugation types (16 types)
(p. 142) BCS verbs are grouped into 16 conjugation types by the relationship between the infinitive
and the present-tense stem. Type 1 is the a-conjugation; types 2–4 are i-conjugation; all remaining
types are e-conjugation. A few type-13/15b verbs have alternate -nem present forms, and a very few
verbs are irregular. Infinitive and present tense must be memorized together, since identical-looking
infinitives can belong to different types — e.g. *dòvesti* (1sg. *dovèdem*) is type 13, but *dòvesti*
(1sg. *dovèzem*) is type 14.

### Past tense formation (L-participle)
(p. 143) The past tense = present tense of the auxiliary *biti* + the L-participle. For types 1–2,
drop *-ti* and add the L-participle endings; types 3–12 follow the same procedure (the ijekavian
masc. sg. of types 3 and 12 ends in *-io*). For types 13–16, drop the final vowel of the 3rd plural
*present* tense and add the L-participle endings, inserting *-a-* before the masc. sg. *-o*; type 16
(including *ići*) replaces the stem-final consonant with *š*, and type 13 drops the stem-final
consonant without inserting *-a-*. *hteti/htjeti* follows type 12; *mòći* follows type 15a; *doneti/
donijeti* follows type 12 in the past but type 14 in the present. Verbs that add *-n-* in the present
(*pasti, padnem*; *pòmoći, pòmognem*) drop that consonant in the past.

### Usage of the past tense (auxiliary behavior)
(p. 143) The past-tense auxiliary normally appears as the clitic form of *biti*'s present tense; the
full form is used for questions, emphasis, or single-word answers, and *biti*'s negated present
expresses negation. When two past-tense verbs share a subject within the same clause, the auxiliary
need not repeat (*ìšla sam i nàšla se s drùštvom* or, with two auxiliaries, *ìšla sam i nàšla sam se s
drùštvom*). The auxiliary can be dropped altogether in certain narrative styles (e.g. fairy tales).
"Se-merger": when the 3sg auxiliary *je* sits adjacent to *se*, *je* drops — *vràtio sam se* "I came
back" but *vràtio se* "he came back" (Croatian may retain *je* for emphasis: *vràtio se je*). When the
feminine accusative clitic *je* is adjacent to the 3sg auxiliary *je*, the clitic becomes *ju*: *ti se
je upòznao* "you made her acquaintance" vs. *on ju je upòznao* "he made her acquaintance."

### Past and future tense of "existential" ima and nema
(p. 143) Past of *ìma* ("there is") is *bìlo je*; past of *nema* ("there isn't") is *nìje bìlo*.
Future of *ìma* is *biće/bit će*; future of *nema* is *neće biti*.

### Word order in the past tense (clitic order)
(p. 145) When multiple clitics co-occur, they follow a fixed six-slot order: (1) question particle
*li*; (2) all auxiliaries other than 3sg *je*; (3) dative object pronoun; (4) genitive-accusative
object pronoun; (5) *se*; (6) the 3sg auxiliary *je* (the "je-final" rule — the 3sg auxiliary alone
moves to the end rather than staying in slot 2 with other auxiliaries). Example: *ti si mu ga dala*
"you gave it to him" (order 2-3-4) vs. *òna mu ga je dala* "she gave it to him" (order 3-4-6).

### Verbal aspect, continued
(p. 145–146) Imperfective aspect covers an ongoing action, a repeated action, or the bare fact of an
action (only the first sense maps to the English progressive); it is most frequent in the present
tense. Perfective aspect implies closure and typically describes a single completed action, most
frequent in past/future. Aspect often tracks a verb's inherent meaning ("arrive" skews perfective,
"do"/"live" skew imperfective), and perfective/imperfective partners can sometimes correspond to
different English verbs. Aspect pairs form either by prefixation (adds closure onto a base verb, e.g.
*jesti → pòjesti*, *ùčiti → naùčiti*) or by suffixation — a more complex process that usually changes
conjugation type and often other features too (e.g. *dàvati/dati* differ in type and by the suffix
*-va*; *polàgati/polòžiti* differ in type, stem consonant, stem vowel, and vowel length). Motion-verb
pairs keep the same prefix across both aspect members but change the root: *òtići* vs. *òdlaziti*
"leave," *dòći* vs. *dòlaziti* "come."

### Subjectless sentences
(p. 146) Subjectless sentences lack a grammatical subject: impersonal sentences lack one altogether,
while others carry a "logical subject" in a non-nominative case. All such sentences take 3sg verbs in
the present and neuter singular L-participles in the past. Dative subjectless sentences include
inclinational se-verbs such as *sviđati se* and *činiti se*. Genitive subjectless sentences typically
express measurement/quantity (*mnogo ih ìma* "there are a lot of them"). Accusative subjectless
sentences identify the experiencer of an emotion — shame, worry, fear (*strah me je* "I am afraid").
Relatedly, fear can also be expressed with *plašiti se* + Gen (more concrete objects) or *bòjati se* +
Gen (more abstract contexts); in the *strah me je* construction the preposition *od* is obligatory in
S and optional in B/C (*strah me je zmìja* or *strah me je od zmìja* "I am afraid of snakes").

### Verbal nouns (-anje/-enje)
(p. 152) Deverbal nouns are formed by adding a long vowel + *-nje* to the verbal root: *-ati*
infinitives take *-anje* (*čìtati → čìtanje*); other infinitives take *-enje*, which sometimes
triggers a stem-consonant shift (*ùčiti → ùčenje*; *misliti → mišljenje*; *òbjasniti →
objašnjènje*). These nouns usually denote the ongoing process of an action, though they can also
denote the bare fact of an action.

### Irregular body-part nouns
(p. 152) *oko* "eye" and *uho/uvo* "ear" are neuter in the singular, but their plurals (*oči*, *uši*)
decline as feminine consonant-stem nouns, with genitive plural in *-iju* (*òčiju*, *ùšiju*). Several
other paired body-part nouns — *ruka*, *noga* — take genitive plural in *-u* (*ruku*, *nogu*) rather
than a zero ending. Separately: when a body part "hurts," the body part itself is the grammatical
subject and the person feeling the pain is expressed in the accusative, via the verb *boleti/boljeti*
(*noge me bole* "my feet hurt"; *glàva je bòli* "her head hurts").

### Verbs of transport (three-way root distinction)
(p. 152) BCS distinguishes three near-rhyming imperfective roots for "take/carry [somewhere]"
depending on what is being moved: *nos-* (an inanimate object, or a living being that must be
carried, e.g. a baby), *vod-* (a being capable of moving under its own power, on foot), and *voz-* (a
being moved by vehicle). All three imperfectives end in *-iti*; their perfective partners exist only
in prefixed form and use different roots (*-nes-*, *-ved-*, *-vez-* respectively), most neutrally with
the prefix *od-*. Other prefixes (*do-, u-, pre-, iz-*) attach to both aspect members, frequently
producing new, often abstract aspect pairs — illustrated in the source with *pre-*: *prenositi/
preneti* "transmit," *prevoditi/prevesti* "translate," *prevoziti/prevesti* "transport."

### Cultural/pragmatic notes (non-grammatical, p. 141, 144, 147–148)
Several short usage notes accompany the grammar: (1) when one person invites another out (using
*zvati* or *vòditi*, e.g. *zòvem te na kàfu*), the inviter is expected to pay; group invitations
(*ìdemo na kàvu!*) are more ambiguous and often go Dutch. (2) University students in the region may
retake a final exam up to five times; grading scales run 6 (fail) to 10 (excellent) in Bosnia,
Montenegro, and Serbia, and 1 (fail) to 5 (excellent) in Croatia. (3) The fairy-tale opening formula
*"Bio jednom..."* ("Once upon a time...") is used without the past-tense auxiliary and agrees in
gender/number with the protagonist: *Bila jednom...* (female), *Bilo jednom...* (neuter), *Bili
jednom...* (group). (4) *Žèludac* is the precise anatomical term for "stomach," while *stòmak* [S] and
*tr̀buh* [B,C] are more general terms for the abdominal area (often "belly"/"gut"); *rùka* covers both
"hand" and "arm" while *šaka* is the precise term for "hand," and *nòga* covers both "foot" and "leg"
while *stopalo* is the precise term for "foot."

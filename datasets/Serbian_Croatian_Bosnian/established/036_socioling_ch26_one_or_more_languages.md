## Sociolinguistic points

### The chapter's framing purpose

Alexander opens by stating this chapter's job directly: to close the book by returning to the
question that has run under the whole "Sociolinguistic Commentary" section (chapters 21–25) —
is BCS properly one language or three? She frames the book's own two halves as answering two
different questions: the grammar chapters (1–20) treat BCS as one single, if internally varied,
code; the sociolinguistic-commentary chapters treat Bosnian, Croatian, and Serbian as three
separate, socially/politically real entities. She recaps that vocabulary differences specifically
were never treated systematically in the grammar chapters — noted only "on a more or less random
basis," item by item in footnotes, whenever a difference happened to surface in an example — and
explains this was a deliberate scope choice (the book's goal was grammar, not lexicon), not an
oversight (p. 423).

### The graded, non-binary nature of B/C/S vocabulary differentiation

A key methodological point for this project: Alexander explicitly states that most vocabulary
differences are not clean binary splits. Only some items belong unambiguously to one standard.
Far more common is an item used by both communities but at different *frequencies* — "used more
frequently in Serbian" but "nevertheless also used by Croats," and vice versa — sometimes with the
same meaning at differing frequencies, sometimes with subtly different connotations. She
characterizes the footnoted `S`/`C`/`B`-style tags used earlier in the book as a necessary
simplification: the format forces a binary-looking label onto what is actually a gradient, and
"it has not been possible to represent gradations of usage of individual items" (p. 423). Bosnian
usage in particular is described as usually favoring one or the other existing marked form (rather
than possessing its own unique word), with genuinely Bosnian-unique vocabulary called "relatively
rare." This is directly relevant to how this project should treat `Attested Region`/`Usage Tier`
tags pulled from this book's earlier `[B]`/`[C]`/`[S]` annotations: a tag on a single item is a
coarse simplification of a frequency gradient the source itself says it could not fully capture,
not a hard categorical boundary (p. 423–424).

### The "S-language" vs. "C-language" distinction (the chapter's central conceptual contribution)

The chapter's key original framing device: Alexander proposes calling the language of national/
cultural symbolic representation an "S-language" (S for *symbol*) and the language of actual
everyday communication a "C-language" (C for *communication*). Under this framing, Bosnian,
Croatian, and Serbian are three separate S-languages — each the marker of a distinct people's
history, culture, and self-identity — while at the level of actual communicative use, speakers of
all three "speak one and the same C-language," using the same grammar and largely the same
vocabulary. Her thesis is that the differences separating the three "are not so much linguistic
but symbolic," operating at the level of political/national representation rather than day-to-day
intelligibility (p. 424). This is arguably the single most load-bearing conceptual claim in the
whole book for this project's purposes, since it gives a principled way to decide, per extracted
item, whether a difference belongs on the "Usage Tier"/"Attested Region" axis (a C-language,
frequency-based distinction) versus a purely symbolic/national-identity axis that doesn't
necessarily track any actual usage-frequency difference at all.

### Pluricentricity and the relationship to Serbo-Croatian

Alexander explicitly invokes the technical linguistics term "pluricentric language" — a language
serving different populations across different states, taking on different guises per state — and
notes English, French, German, and Spanish as ordinary examples of the same phenomenon. She
reports (without fully endorsing) an argument some linguists make: since Serbo-Croatian was
already pluricentric (unusually, within a single state rather than several), BCS should be
regarded as identical to Serbo-Croatian, merely now functioning across several states instead of
one — i.e., the only real change was the number of states involved, not the linguistic substance.
She sets this against an opposing view that the political change was radical enough that the
naming (both of the S-languages and of a cover term) must change too. She herself does not fully
adjudicate this dispute in this passage but presents both positions (p. 424).

### Asymmetric language-engineering effort across the three standards

The chapter notes that active, deliberate language-planning effort to build a standard distinct
from Serbo-Croatian was highly asymmetric across the three communities: most extensive in Croatia
(described as "great enthusiasm and national fervor"), a parallel but more recent effort among
Bosniak intellectuals to establish Bosnian, and by contrast "no parallel efforts were made by
Serbs to develop a separate Serbian language" — Serbs largely worked instead to preserve a
pluralistic status quo, since most considered Serbian and Serbo-Croatian to already be effectively
the same thing (p. 425). This is useful context for why source material describing "Croatian-only"
or "Bosnian-only" neologisms is likely to reflect genuine, documented planning activity, while a
claim of a parallel deliberate "Serbian-only" coinage program should be treated with more
skepticism absent explicit source support.

### Chronology: rapid change in the 1990s, slowing by 2005

Alexander places the intense period of standardization activity in the 1990s, and states that by
2005 (near this book's publication) the rate of change had "slowed dramatically." Critically for
how this project should read book-derived "contemporary" era tags, she adds that essentially all
of the changes by that point were changes of *emphasis/focus* rather than of substance: nearly
every element that now differentiates Croatian from Serbian (or Bosnian from either) had already
existed earlier as a variant means of expression — what changed was how much conscious, deliberate
attention speakers, politicians, and language planners chose to place on those pre-existing
variants as markers of national identity (p. 425–426).

### The current (as of writing) state: three S-languages, one shared C-language core

Alexander's summary formulation: the present situation is "best described as three distinct
systems of symbolic cultural representation ... which share a common core of communicative
expression ... which in each instance bears an overlay of its own distinct representation." The
three S-language systems are Bosnian, Croatian, Serbian; their shared core is what the book calls
BCS (p. 426).

### On naming: Croatian and Serbian "unproblematic," Bosnian contested, and BCS as the outsider's compromise term

She states the names *hrvatski* (Croatian) and *srpski* (Serbian) are uncontroversial, each
picking out a clearly defined national/cultural group whose language it has historically named.
*Bosanski* (Bosnian) is more contested: the planning effort behind it was driven by Bosniak
(Bosnian Muslim) intellectuals, so much of what differentiates the standard traces specifically to
Bosniak Islamic heritage — leading some (including non-Muslim Bosnians) to argue the language
should instead be named *bošnjački* ("Bosniak"), a naming dispute she describes as unresolved at
the time of writing. For the shared core, she defends "BCS" as a practical outsider's compromise:
easily pronounceable, compact, alphabetically ordered so as to avoid ranking the three, and
deliberately using the bare initial "B" so as to sidestep taking a position in the Bosnian/Bosniak
dispute. She notes, however, that native speakers themselves essentially never use a term like
"BCS" — they reach instead for either a specific national label or the general, deliberately
unmarked *naš jezik* ("our language") / *naški*, letting context determine which register of
self-identification fits the situation (p. 426).

### Closing statement

The chapter — and the book — closes on a deliberately conciliatory note, paraphrasing "a rose by
any other name" to argue that naming disputes don't change the underlying fact that all these
speakers have their speech regardless of what it is called, and that the book is written for all
of them, "as well as for all others ... who wish to talk to, with, or about any of these speakers"
(p. 426).

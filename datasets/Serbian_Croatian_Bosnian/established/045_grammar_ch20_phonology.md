## Grammar points

### 167 Introduction — sounds, morphemes, and morpheme boundaries

BCS is characterized (per the chapter's framing) as an unusually "write as you speak" language: the
written form maps closely onto pronunciation, more so than in many codified languages. Even so, no
writing system perfectly reproduces speech, and BCS specifically balances two competing goals —
representing pronunciation faithfully vs. representing the relationships between related word-forms
faithfully. The chapter frames all subsequent sections as consequences of *sound processes that
happen at morpheme boundaries*: palatalization, vowel shifts, voicing assimilation, cluster
simplification, vowel loss (fleeting vowels), and vowel insertion. These are explicitly framed as
enrichment, not essential survival grammar — "helpful but not essential" for a learner — but the
author frames them as the mechanism that explains why related word-forms (e.g. within one
paradigm, or across a derivational family) can look so different from each other on the surface.

### 167a. Palatalization

Reviews the three softening series already presented earlier in the grammar (Type A/B/C consonant
softenings) and explains their historical origin: a consonant adjacent to a front vowel gradually
shifted in pronunciation until it became a phonemically distinct "soft"/palatal consonant. Type B
softenings correspond to the historical "First Palatalization"; Type A to the "Second and Third
Palatalizations" (both pan-Slavic changes); Type C softenings are unique to BCS's own history,
triggered not by a front vowel but by the glide /j/ ("jod"/"jot") — hence "jotated" rather than
"palatal" consonants. The chapter presents consolidated example charts showing palatalization
triggered by (a) grammatical endings (case endings, comparative -i, present-tense/imperative/aorist/
imperfect endings, participial and verbal-noun suffixes) and (b) derivational suffixes (-ati/-ti verb
pairs, -ak, -an, -anin, -enje, -evina, -in, -nja).

### 167b. Vowel shifts from ancient "ablaut"

Explains two residual Proto-Slavic **ablaut** (root-vowel gradation) relationships still visible in
modern BCS vocabulary: (1) noun forms historically took root-vowel grade **-o-** while related verb
forms took grade **-e-** (e.g. prinos "contribution" / prinesem "I contribute"; prevod "translation"
/ prevedem "I translate" — this pair carries the E/J prevod/prijevod tag above); (2) a broader,
still-productive alternation where perfective verbs show root **-o-** and their imperfective partners
show **-a-** (nasloniti/naslanjati "lean", obnoviti/obnavljati "renovate", skočiti/skakati "jump",
etc.), with the stem-final consonant undergoing softening before the -a-.

### 167c. Vowel shifts akin to palatalization

After palatalization, a further shift pushed **o → e** after soft/palatal consonants in certain
endings — visible in the alternation between "hard-stem" nouns/adjectives taking -o-initial endings
(selo, gradom, dobrog) and "soft-stem" ones taking the corresponding -e-initial endings (polje,
mužem, loše, muževi).

### 167d. The shift of l to o

A later, BCS-specific historical change: word-final /l/ (after a vowel) became labialized and
eventually merged with /o/ in pronunciation and spelling. This explains alternations like posla
(Gsg.) / posao (Nsg.) "work", misli/misao "thought", rekla/rekao "said" (L-participle), and the -lac
suffix pattern (čitalac/čitaoca "reader"). The change did **not** occur in the Čakavian and Kajkavian
dialects, and the chapter notes Croatian has, in a number of words, deliberately *restored* the
final -l (e.g. stol vs. sto, bijel vs. bio/beo — captured in the vocabulary table above) "out of
regard for the important place occupied by these dialects in Croatian history" — an explicit
sociolinguistic/identity-driven spelling choice, cross-referenced to §179 (outside this chunk).

### 167e. Voicing assimilation

Most BCS consonants pair as voiced/voiceless. When two consonants of unlike voicing meet at a
morpheme boundary, the BCS regularly applies **regressive** assimilation: the first consonant takes
on the voicing of the second (an underlying voiced+voiceless sequence surfaces as voiceless+
voiceless, and vice versa). Crucially, this is a *pronunciation* (and, in BCS, *spelling*) change,
not a change to the underlying consonant itself — e.g. vrabac "sparrow" has underlying root-final /b/,
which surfaces (and is spelled) as /p/ in vrapca (Gsg.) only because of the following voiceless
suffix. Charted across prefix+stem boundaries (iz-kopati→iskopati) and stem+suffix boundaries
(svat-ba→svadba "wedding"), plus one exceptional case of *progressive* assimilation: mozak "brain" →
Gsg. mozga (not the expected *moska).

### 167f. Cluster simplification

When identical consonants meet at a morpheme boundary (either underlyingly identical, or made
identical by voicing assimilation), one is dropped in both speech and spelling — e.g.
bez-zakonje→bezakonje "lawlessness"; iz-seliti→(is-seliti via voicing assimilation)→iseliti
"emigrate." Two caveats given: (1) simplification operates on *sounds*, not letters — since c/č are
phonetically /ts/tʃ/, a preceding /t/ still simplifies against them (predak+ci → preci, not *predtci);
(2) it presupposes voicing assimilation has already applied. The prefix naj- (superlative marker) is
flagged as the one systematic exception: naj- + a comparative beginning with j does NOT simplify
(najjači "strongest", najjednostavnije "most simply") — spoken as if it were almost a separate word
with its own stress and a slight juncture break. Also notes cluster simplification for st+n and st+l
sequences arising from fleeting-vowel loss (bolestan→bolesna "conscious"; rastao→rasla "grew"), and
two older, non-identical-consonant simplifications baked into the modern language: tl/dl → l (the
sole distinguishing feature of L-participle verb types 13 vs. 14–15) and bv → b (root v- after prefix
ob-, e.g. ob-vući → obući "put on").

### 167g. Fleeting vowels ("nepostojano a")

A short **/a/** that appears only in certain case-forms of a word (typically the citation/dictionary
form) and disappears elsewhere — functionally an *inserted* vowel that breaks up an otherwise
unpronounceable word-final consonant cluster before a zero ending (pisac "writer" Nsg. vs. pisca
Gsg.; prazan "empty" masc. vs. prazna fem.). The same mechanism, historically, underlies the l→o
alternation forms (topao/topla "warm"; pekao/pekla "baked" L-participle; posao/posla "work"),
visualized in the chapter as a two-stage derivation (older stage vs. present stage, with an
intermediate "U-1/U-2" underlying-form notation). Fleeting vowels are overwhelmingly a *suffix*
phenomenon (rare in roots — the chapter's only native-root examples are pas/psa "dog", san/sna
"sleep", sav/sva "all"); loanwords vary, with **Serbian generally inserting** the vowel and
**Croatian generally not** (talenat vs. talent "talent", fakat vs. fakt "fact" — an explicit,
if informally stated, national-standard split, though not marked with the book's usual [B]/[C]/[S]
bracket notation so not added to the vocabulary table above). The key suffixes requiring a fleeting
vowel are -ac/-ak (nouns) and -ak/-an (adjectives); the vowel is retained (not fleeting) when its
loss would create an unpronounceable cluster or when it is long — notably, the -an suffix of passive
participles never loses its vowel in the masc.sg.

### 167h. Inserted vowels

Distinguishes fleeting -a- (short, breaks up clusters, present only in Nsg./L-part.masc.sg.) from a
different, **long**, **inserted** -a- that appears in nearly all genitive-plural forms regardless of
pronounceability, apparently purely to mark the Gpl. grammatically (e.g. vrabac Nsg. / vrabaca Gpl.,
where both a fleeting-a Nsg. and an inserted-a Gpl. occur in the same paradigm but are phonetically
and functionally distinct, despite most language manuals collapsing both under one "fleeting -a-"
label). The chapter flags this terminological conflation as a common but technically imprecise
convention. A third, separate insertion phenomenon: /a/ inserted after a consonant-final preposition
or prefix before a following like-sounding consonant (izaslati vs. izzvati-type collisions). This one
is only partly predictable and must often be learned per-word (izaslati "delegate" requires it;
others like izgnati/izagnati allow both forms). Insertion after **prepositions** specifically is
described as geographically/stylistically conditioned: with the single-consonant prepositions s and
k, **Serbian tends to insert regardless of the following sound**, **Croatian only when the
consonants would otherwise fully merge**, and **Bosnian falls in between** — an explicit
three-way national-standard characterization (paraphrased here per copyright discipline, not
table-ized as individual lexical items since it describes a general phonological tendency rather
than discrete vocabulary entries). A summary chart (obligatory / Serbian / Croatian / high style /
archaic columns) gives forms like sà mnom (obligatory with the pronoun mnom), ka vama vs. k vama,
sa nama vs. s nama, and archaic/poetic forms like krozà san, predà mnom, uzà nj.

### 167j. Sound structure and spelling

Frames the historical debate between two 19th-century codification principles: Vuk Karadžić's
**phonological** "write as you speak" principle (spelling should map directly to pronunciation) vs.
the Illyrian movement's **morphophonemic** "write for the eye" principle (spelling should preserve
recognizable word/morpheme structure across related forms). BCS orthography ultimately followed the
Vukovian phonological principle overwhelmingly — spelling reproduces voicing assimilation and
cluster simplification directly (pod-pisati is spelled potpisati, not *podpisati). The one
codified exception: sequences of d+s or d+š at a morpheme boundary (od-/pod-/pred- + a root
beginning s-/š-) are spelled morphophonemically (unchanged), specifically because the fully
phonological spelling would collapse them into the existing letters c/č, creating ambiguity (e.g.
odseliti "move away" keeps its d in spelling even though it is pronounced oceliti in rapid speech).
Notes that the morphophonemic principle, while historically Croatian-associated (Illyrian movement)
and briefly "elevated to law during one period of Croatian history" (cross-referenced to §181,
outside this chunk), never displaced the phonological default even in Croatian: for a word like
predak "ancestor", the phonological plural spelling preci prevails over morphophonemic alternatives
like pretci or predci, "although some Croats now use one or the other of the latter two spellings."

## Connection to the syllable-switching slang mechanism (see `025_appendices7-8_literary_readings.md`)

**Direct and substantive relevance flagged**, per this dispatch's instruction. The earlier-extracted
file `established/025_appendices7-8_literary_readings.md` documents a genuine BCS colloquial word
game — syllable-switching slang historically associated with Sarajevo (vozdra ← zdravo, žemka ←
kažem, đido ← dođi, žibje ← bježi) and an older Zagreb-specific layer (lima ← mali, cobra ← braco,
Šomi ← Mišo, Njofra ← Franjo). This chapter's phonotactic/phonological content bears on that
mechanism in several concrete ways:

- **§167f (cluster simplification) and §167g (fleeting vowels)** together establish that BCS has
  hard phonotactic constraints on which consonant sequences are pronounceable at all, and that the
  language's own morphology already contains a systematic repair mechanism (vowel insertion/
  fleeting-vowel retention) for exactly this problem. A syllable-switching operation on a BCS word
  is not merely reordering abstract "syllables" — it must respect (or, in the attested slang forms,
  sometimes deliberately violate/simplify) these same pronounceability constraints. Comparing the
  attested slang forms against this chapter's rules is suggestive: **vozdra** (from zdravo) results
  in a word-initial cluster /vzd/-adjacent structure that would be flagged as awkward under §167g's
  logic, while **đido** (from dođi) and **žibje** (from bježi) show a straightforward CV-syllable
  swap that stays within normal BCS syllable templates — i.e. the productive slang pattern appears
  to favor swaps between syllables that are each independently well-formed CV(C) units, consistent
  with this chapter's description of what counts as a "pronounceable" syllable in BCS generally.
- **§167a (palatalization)**, which BCS speakers have "by now internalized" for regular
  inflectional/derivational morphology per this chapter's own framing, is a process triggered by
  specific vowel/consonant adjacencies at morpheme boundaries — meaning a syllable-switching
  operation that relocates a vowel next to a different consonant than it originally followed could,
  in principle, trigger (or fail to trigger, if the slang deliberately keeps the "wrong" underlying
  consonant) the same softening the standard grammar describes. None of the specific attested slang
  items in `025` show an obvious palatalization artifact, but this is a mechanism worth checking
  against a larger corpus of syllable-switched slang forms if one is ever collected.
- **§167j (phonological vs. morphophonemic spelling)** is thematically adjacent rather than directly
  mechanistic: it documents that BCS's writing system already has a live tension between
  "spell what you hear" and "spell to preserve recognizable structure" baked into its standard
  orthography (the d+s/d+š exception). A conlang slang mechanism built on syllable-switching would
  face an analogous design choice — whether the switched/derived form's *spelling* should track the
  switched pronunciation transparently, or morphophonemically preserve a visual link back to the
  base word it derives from.

No new syllable-switching vocabulary was found in this chapter — this chapter is systematic
phonology, not a slang or register-annotated section — so this is a structural/mechanism connection,
not a new attested-form contribution to that inventory.

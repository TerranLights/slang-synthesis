# Serbian/Croatian/Bosnian — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Chapters 15–17, "Quantifiers," "Determiners," "Particles, conjunctions and exclamations"

**Source:** Lila Hammond, *Serbian: An Essential Grammar* (Routledge, 2005), Chapters 15–17,
printed pp. 282–296 (PDF pp. 297–312 of `10.Serbian an essential grammar.pdf`, located via
`pdftotext -layout` search for "Chapter 15"/"Chapter 16"/"Chapter 17" heading text; front-matter
offset is ~15 pages between printed and PDF page numbers).

**Coverage note:** every grammar point and every distinct vocabulary item from Chapters 15
(Quantifiers), 16 (Determiners), and 17 (Particles, conjunctions and exclamations) is captured
below, following the comprehensive-but-not-exhaustive coverage rule — repeated drill-style example
sentences that only restate a form already captured are skipped. This is a third reference book for
the language (Serbian-specific, not comparative B/C/S), and this content — quantifiers, determiners,
and especially particles/exclamations — has little direct overlap with the two previously extracted
books. Chapter 17 (particles, conjunctions and exclamations) is genuinely new high-value content for
this project: interjections and discourse particles are classic informal-register vocabulary, and
none of it is a straightforward duplicate of anything in the earlier extraction files.

**Overlap check:** the flagship textbook and the *Bosnian, Croatian, Serbian: A Grammar* volume both
cover demonstrative pronouns (ovaj/taj/onaj) and possessive pronouns (moj/tvoj/svoj) in their own
pronoun chapters; `established/052_serbian_essential_ch8_pronouns.md` (this same book's own earlier
pronoun chapter) does not yet exist in this directory at the time of this extraction. This chapter's
own §16.1–16.2 content (possessive/demonstrative *determiners*) is largely a cross-reference back to
this book's own Chapter 8 rather than new paradigm data — the paradigm tables themselves (declension
of мој/moj, овај/ovaj etc.) are not reproduced here since Chapter 16 doesn't reproduce them either;
only the determiner-specific usage points and vocabulary genuinely introduced in Chapters 15–17
(quantifier nouns, determiner sub-classes like indefinite/negative determiners, and the full set of
particles/conjunctions/exclamations) are tabulated below.

**Script note:** the source prints Cyrillic and Latin forms side by side throughout. The PDF's
Cyrillic glyphs did not extract cleanly via `pdftotext` (font-encoding substitution garbled them into
nonsense Latin-lookalike strings), so only the clean Latin-script transliteration is used as the
`Term` value; where the source additionally gives a Cyrillic form, this is noted generically in the
Notes column ("Cyrillic form also given in source") rather than reproducing the garbled extraction.
The source has a real text layer for the Latin-script columns (no vision-reading was needed), so
Vision Reading Confidence is `n/a` throughout.

## Grammar points

### 15.1 Types of quantifiers

Quantifiers function as nouns, adjectives, or adverbs and refer to quantity ("many, enough, a
little, a bunch of," etc.). Most quantifiers are followed by nouns in the genitive case. The source
distinguishes three classes based on what kind of noun the quantifier can combine with: countable
nouns (човек/čovek "man," сто/sto "table"), uncountable nouns (киша/kiša "rain," шећер/šećer
"sugar," брашно/brašno "flour"), and collective nouns (грожђе/grožđe "grapes," камење/kamenje
"stones," јагњад/jagnjad "lambs"). (p. 282)

### 15.1.1 Countable quantifiers

`Broj` (number) and `deo` (part), when preceded by size/number adjectives (veliki/veći/najveći,
mali/manji/najmanji, dobar), quantify countable nouns; the noun that follows goes into the genitive
plural (or genitive singular for "a part of" something), while the finite verb agrees with `broj`
(masculine singular) or `deo` (neuter singular) rather than with the following noun. A separate
closed set of nouns (asortiman, buket, niz, par, grupa, nekolicina) is followed directly by genitive
plural nouns, with the verb agreeing with the quantifying noun's own gender/number — `nekolicina` is
flagged as an exception, applying only to human masculine referents while itself triggering a
feminine singular verb. `Nekoliko` (several) governs genitive plural and collective (-ad) nouns with
a neuter singular verb; its adjectival counterpart `nekoliki` takes full three-gender adjectival
declension and agrees with a following plural noun in the same case. (pp. 282–284)

### 15.1.2 Uncountable quantifiers

`Količina` and `svota` (both "amount/quantity," feminine nouns usable in singular or plural) are
preceded by descriptive adjectives and followed by a genitive singular noun, with verb agreement to
the quantifying noun. A further closed set of container/measure nouns (komad, parče, flaša, čaša,
vekna, kašika, šolja, kilo, litar, metar) work the same way — genitive singular complement, verb
agreeing with the measure noun. (pp. 284–285)

### 15.1.3 Countable and uncountable quantifiers

A shared set of quantifiers (mnogo, više, najviše, puno, malo, manje, najmanje, mnoštvo, deo, dosta,
dovoljno, nešto, većina, manjina) can combine with either class: genitive plural for countable
complements, genitive singular for uncountable/partitive complements, with the verb generally
defaulting to neuter singular (the pattern set by `mnogo`) except where the quantifying noun itself
carries fixed gender/number (mnoštvo → neuter singular; deo → masculine singular; većina/manjina →
feminine singular). Two adjectives, `bezbrojni` (countless) and `brojni` (numerous), instead agree in
case with the countable noun they modify rather than governing the genitive. `Mnogo` also has a
distinct adjectival form, `mnogi`, which takes full three-gender declension (paradigm given in full,
p. 287) and is used in the singular for countable/uncountable nouns, in the plural for
countable/collective nouns. (pp. 285–287)

### 16.1 Possessive determiners

Determiners are words/phrases at the start of a noun phrase signaling new vs. familiar information;
the class includes articles, quantifiers, numbers, possessive adjectives, and demonstrative
adjectives/pronouns. Possessive determiners are cross-referenced to the same paradigm as possessive
pronouns (§8.3). The chapter's one genuinely new point here is `svoj`, the reflexive possessive
("one's own"), used across all persons/genders/numbers and declined like `moj`. The book gives a
sharp minimal-pair illustration of why this matters: "Videla je njenu decu" (she saw her [someone
else's] children — ambiguous as to whose) versus "Videla je svoju decu" (she saw her own children —
unambiguously reflexive back to the subject, of any person). `Svoj` also combines with the indefinite
pronoun `svako` ("Svako je uzeo svoje stvari" — Everyone took his/her own things). Personal pronouns
in the dative case can also function as possessive determiners (cross-referenced to §7.3.3), e.g.
"Gde ti je knjiga?" (Where is your book?). (pp. 288–289)

### 16.2 Demonstrative determiners

Demonstrative determiners are cross-referenced to the demonstrative pronoun paradigm (§8.4) rather
than re-taught here; quantifiers can combine with demonstrative determiners in the same noun phrase
(e.g. "Mali broj ove dece" — a small number of these children). Beyond the base ovaj/taj/onaj set,
the chapter introduces two derived demonstrative sub-series not covered elsewhere in this reference
run: a *qualitative* series (ovakav/onakav/takav, "this/that kind of") replacing English "this/that"
in a qualitative sense, and a *quantitative* series (ovoliki/onoliki/toliki, "this/that big/much")
used quantitatively. `Isti` ("the same") is also treated as a demonstrative determiner. (pp. 289–290)

### 16.3 Indefinite determiners

A large closed set of indefinite determiners (the equivalents of "some, any, anyone, either, both,
someone's," etc.) is presented as a vocabulary list — see the Vocabulary table above for the full
set (neki, poneki, pokoji, bilo koji, ma koji, koji god, nečiji, ičiji, bilo čiji, ma čiji, čiji god,
nekakav, ikakav, bilo kakav, ma kakav, kojekakav, bilo koliki, ma koliki, koliki god, jedan). These
all carry gender, number, and case like ordinary adjectives/pronouns. The source notes an idiomatic
extended sense of `neki`: "I nije neki film" (It's not a good film) — a non-literal, evaluative use
distinct from its base "some/any" meaning. (pp. 290–291)

### 16.4 Interrogative determiners

Beyond their coverage as interrogative pronouns (§8.6, cross-referenced), `koji` ("which?") and
`kakav` ("what kind/sort?") both also take on a "what?" sense as determiners — `kakav` further
extends to an exclamatory sense ("Kakav je on idiot!" — What an idiot he is!). (p. 291)

### 16.5 Negative determiners

Negative determiners are formed by adding the prefix `ни-`/`ni-` to the corresponding base
determiner/pronoun (čiji → ničiji, kakav → nikakav, jedan → nijedan) and decline exactly like their
base form. They carry gender, number, and case, and are used together with a negated verb (double
negation is grammatical/obligatory, as generally in Serbian). (pp. 291–292)

### Chapter 17: Particles, conjunctions and exclamations

The chapter is presented as a single alphabetically-ish ordered reference list of the most
frequently used particles, conjunctions, and exclamations rather than being subdivided into numbered
grammar-point subsections — see the Vocabulary table above for the full set (pp. 293–296). A few
items carry genuinely notable usage annotations beyond a bare gloss, all captured in the table's
Notes column: `baš` as an emphatic intensifier; `zar` as an interrogative/emphatic marker
distinguishable from a plain question; `evo`/`eto` as a paired presentational-deictic pair ("here
(you) are/is" vs. "there (you) are/is"); the enclitic interrogative particle `li`, whose construction
with `ne bi li` the source explicitly flags as often carrying "sarcastic/humorous overtones" when
expressing intention or hope — a rare explicit register annotation in this chapter, and one of the
strongest informal-register signals in this extraction; and a cluster of items (`a`, `i`, `kao`,
`kao i`, `međutim`, `ne`, `ni`, `po`) explicitly marked by the source as unable to be followed by an
enclitic, a genuine syntactic (clitic-placement) constraint rather than a semantic note. No dialectal
or B/C/S-style regional tags appear anywhere in this chapter (unlike the comparative B/C/S sources
already extracted for this language) — this is a Serbian-only reference book and marks no explicit
regional variation in Chapters 15–17. (pp. 293–296)

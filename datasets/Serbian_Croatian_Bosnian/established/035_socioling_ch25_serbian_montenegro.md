## Sociolinguistic points

*(Renamed from the template's "Grammar points" section — this chapter is historical/sociolinguistic
exposition, not grammar drilling. Paraphrased from Alexander's own prose, pp. 415-423, with short
illustrative quotes.)*

### Serb identity: church, Kosovo, and migration (§§184-185, pp. 415-416)

The chapter opens by tying Serbian linguistic identity tightly to the Serbian Orthodox Church —
the Cyrillic alphabet's religious origin, and the family connection between the medieval Serbian
dynasty (Stefan Nemanja) and the founder of the independent church (his son, St. Sava). It then
frames the June 28, 1389 Battle of Kosovo as the central Serbian national myth: not a shameful
defeat but a "spiritual victory," and notes the moral weight modern outsiders often miss in why
Serbs commemorate a military loss. A second recurring theme is migration — the 16th-century
settlement of the Habsburg Military Border (Krajina) and the 1690 velika seoba (Great Migration)
north of the Danube — introduced as the historical backdrop for why ethnic Serb populations came
to live scattered well outside Serbia's own borders (Montenegro, Bosnia, Slavonia, Krajina), which
later explains the vocabulary items Srbin/Srbijanac/prečanin above. The section also covers the
language-standardization battle between the conservative Serbian Orthodox clergy (defending the
archaic Slavo-Serbian church language) and Vuk Karadžić's radical vernacular reform — Vuk won, but
the book notes this cost the language "many beautifully expressive high-style words" that nearly
vanished, and credits the poet-cleric Njegoš with uniquely fusing Vuk's popular register and the
church's high style.

### Ekavian as the Belgrade-centered national choice (§185, pp. 416)

By the late 19th century, Serbian had settled on ekavian pronunciation (rather than the ijekavian
used elsewhere in BCS territory) as its national norm, despite the 1850 Vienna Agreement's push
toward a shared ijekavian standard with Croatia. Alexander attributes this partly to Belgrade/Novi
Sad prestige and partly to geopolitics: Serbia's territorial ambitions at the time pointed south
and east (toward Ottoman-held "old Serbia," i.e. modern Macedonia) rather than west, and ekavian
happened to be the speech of those southern lands too.

### Yugoslav-era fragmentation of Serbian territory (§186, pp. 416-417)

After WWI, Serbs took the dominant role in the first Yugoslavia, partly on the reasoning that most
of the new state spoke "the same basic language." Tito's post-WWII "second Yugoslavia" is described
as deliberately structured to prevent that Serb-centered unity: splitting Macedonia off as its own
republic/language, carving out the autonomous provinces of Kosovo-Metohija (Albanian-majority) and
Vojvodina (Hungarian-plurality) from what had been Serbian territory. Alexander notes most Serbs
nonetheless cooperated in good faith with Tito-era "brotherhood and unity," genuinely did not
understand persistent Croatian resentment, and largely regarded Serbo-Croatian as one real,
naturally-evolved language rather than (as many Croats saw it) an artificial imposition — a
divergence in how the two sides remembered the same shared-language period. Serbia itself declined
in 1982 to establish a separate Serbian standard even as Croatia, Bosnia, and Montenegro moved
toward their own norms; this restraint later meant that, as separatism intensified, anyone
insisting Serbo-Croatian was still a living single language risked being "branded as a Serbophile."

### Post-breakup standardization fights: alphabet, jat', and the Bosnian ekavian episode (§187, pp. 417-419)

With Croatian and Bosnian independence forcing Serbs to rename their own language "Serbian," three
disputes dominate this subsection:

- **Alphabet (§187a).** Three camps: moderates who wanted to keep using both Cyrillic and Latin
  unchanged (citing decades of dual-script practice and practical ties to the West); a group
  wanting to restore Đuro Daničić's original 19th-century Latin letters (đ, ļ, ń, ǵ for dj, lj, nj,
  dž) in place of the Croatian-Vukovian modifications; and nationalists who wanted Latin banned
  outright and Cyrillic "cleansed" of the Latin-influenced letter *j*, including reviving the old
  Cyrillic jat' letter — a proposal Alexander notes is ironically identical in substance to the
  1830s-40s Illyrian movement's own argument.
- **Ekavian vs. ijekavian (§187b).** Because ijekavian is the native speech of Montenegro, Serbs in
  the Sandžak, and Serbs in Bosnia's Republika Srpska, most Serbian linguists favored keeping both
  standards — partly, Alexander notes, out of concern that an ekavian-only policy would give
  Montenegro more reason to secede. This came to a head politically in September 1993, when
  Republika Srpska's Radovan Karadžić forcibly imposed ekavian spelling on an entirely
  ijekavian-speaking population, explicitly to align Republika Srpska more closely with Serbia
  proper and distance it from the rest of Bosnia-Herzegovina. The book describes near-universal
  disapproval from intellectuals and linguists despite some political backing, sustained protest
  through spring 1994, and the decree's formal rescission in February 1998 — with the population
  having continued speaking its native ijekavian throughout.
- **Standardization committee (§187c).** Academies from Serbia, Montenegro, and Republika Srpska
  jointly formed a Committee for the Standardization of Serbian in 1997. A 1998 nationalist
  pamphlet, *Slovo o srpskom jeziku* ("Doctrine on the Serbian Language"), denied the Croatian and
  Bosnian languages existed and branded acceptance of that "dismemberment" (čerečenje) as Croat
  nationalism; Alexander notes it drew far more attention abroad than within Serbia, where it was
  "clearly recognized as a wildly extremist statement," though the Committee still issued a formal
  rebuttal. Standard Serbian today, per the source, continues in both alphabets and both
  pronunciations.

### Montenegro's identity: close to Serbian, yet distinct (§188, pp. 419-421)

This section frames Montenegro through the aphorism "All Montenegrins are Serbs, but not all Serbs
are Montenegrins" — attributed to Milovan Djilas — noting that older Montenegrins broadly accept
this while younger Montenegrins increasingly reject its first half. Montenegro's landscape (the
"Black Mountain" of its name in Slavic, Italian, and Albanian alike) and its effectively-unconquered
status under the Ottomans are presented as the basis for its self-image as a mountain refuge of
Orthodox and anti-Ottoman resistance — the same core themes (Orthodoxy, Kosovo-style resistance
mythology) that bind it to Serb identity rather than separate it. Subsection 188a covers Njegoš
(Petar II Petrović Njegoš) at length as the figure who embodies both the uniqueness of Montenegrin
identity and its organic bond with Serbian identity simultaneously: his 1847 epic *Gorski vijenac*
("The Mountain Wreath") is claimed by both Serbs (for its Kosovo references and dedication to
Karageorge) and Montenegrins (for its Montenegro-set historical characters, including the real
Prince-Bishop Danilo) as their own literary masterpiece, written in language "still spoken by many
Montenegrins" today. The section also connects Vuk's language reform directly to Montenegro: Vuk's
own ancestors came from Montenegro, and the East Herzegovinian dialect underlying the štokavian
standard closely matches Montenegro's native dialect — meaning Vuk and Njegoš, per the text,
"literally spoke the same language."

### The Montenegrin-language movement: Nikčević's proposals (§189, pp. 421-423)

This is the chapter's most direct treatment of Montenegro's linguistic status, and gives the
book's fullest explanation of the topic flagged from established/010_lesson08.md and
established/021_lesson19.md. Alexander states plainly that, given how slight the religious
difference is (both groups are Eastern Orthodox) and how the ijekavian pronunciation Montenegrins
share with Serbs in Croatia/Bosnia/Herzegovina/southwestern Serbia is not unique to Montenegro
alone, "a movement to establish a separate Montenegrin language would have seemed impossible until
recently" — and that the idea "still strikes many Montenegrins, and nearly all Serbs, as highly
unrealistic" even at time of writing. The movement is presented as driven substantially by one
scholar, Vojislav Nikčević, beginning with a 1994 PEN-Montenegro declaration on the language's
constitutional status and his manual *Piši kao što zboriš*, followed by his 1997 *Crnogorski
pravopis*. Nikčević's central (and, per Alexander, scholarly-rejected) claim is that Montenegrins
were the *only original* speakers of ijekavian, which then spread to ekavian Serbs and ikavian
Bosnians/Croats via migration and Vuk's language reforms — inverting the usual account. His two
concrete reform proposals are (1) restoring archaic negated-biti forms (nijesam) and older
pronominal/adjectival case endings (tijeh starijeh ljudi, tijem starijem ljudima) in place of the
modern tih starih ljudi / tim starim ljudima, and (2) spelling certain consonant softenings as
distinctly Montenegrin — introducing three new letters (ś, ź, з) and increasing use of ć/đ, e.g.
osjećati → ośećati, zjenica → źenica, tjerati → ćerati, djevojka → đevojka, sutra → śutra.
Alexander is explicit that the softenings Nikčević attributes to Montenegrin are "in fact...
dialectal East Herzegovinian pronunciation," not a Montenegro-exclusive feature. The chapter closes
by noting the political stakes: because so much of the proposed "Montenegrin language" hinges on
ijekavian specifically, and Serbia-Montenegro's political union (as of the book's 2006 writing) was
already strained, maintaining the union was seen to depend on continued equal prestige for both
ekavian and ijekavian within the shared language.

**Comparison to earlier findings (established/010_lesson08.md, established/021_lesson19.md):**
This chapter *confirms and substantially deepens* the earlier finding that Montenegrin is not
tagged as a fourth standard — Alexander's own textbook-proper commentary (Lesson 19's "Note about
the language of Montenegro") gave the *symptom list* of Montenegrin dialect markers (đe, nijèsi,
viđet', pređe, starijem, ćerati, đèvojka, sjutra, zboriti) without deep explanation of *why* those
forms exist or *who* is proposing to elevate them to a separate standard. This chapter supplies
that missing explanation in full: it names the movement's origin (Nikčević, 1994-1997), its
disputed core claim (Montenegrin as the "original" ijekavian rather than a regional variant of
it), its two concrete reform mechanisms (archaic morphology restoration + consonant-softening
respelling), and Alexander's own scholarly verdict that the claimed distinctiveness is actually
shared East Herzegovinian dialect feature, not a Montenegro-only trait — while also making clear
the movement remains a minority position even within Montenegro. No contradiction between the two
treatments was found; this chapter is strictly additive detail on the same conclusion.

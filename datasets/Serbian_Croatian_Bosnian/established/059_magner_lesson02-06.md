(Lesson 6), printed pp. 7-35. Continues directly from `established/002_lesson01_magner_vision_test.md`

assessed in `established/002`). **No handwritten marginalia was found anywhere in this page range**
— no pencil/pen marks, no filled-in exercise answers, nothing inconsistent with the printed

end-of-dialogue proverb/chant lines, plus every explicit `(Cr.)`/`(S.)` regional-variant tag the
source itself marks. Repeated drill exercises (vježba/vežba I-V numbered fill-in-the-blank and

## Grammar points

### The Cr./S. slash-and-separate-sentence convention (explicit source metatext)

Lesson 2's "Note on Exercises" (p.9) is the book's own explanation of a convention already implicitly
visible throughout `established/002` and this file: when a sentence differs between Croatian and
Serbian by only one or two words, both forms appear in the *same* sentence separated by a slash, Cr.
form first (e.g. "Ovo je rječnik/rečnik"). When more than one word differs, the book instead gives two
full separate sentences tagged `(Cr.)` and `(S.)`. The author also notes these are not rigid
exclusive dialects — a Croat could use `da li`, a Serb could use inverted `li` — but Croats
statistically favor `li`-inversion and Serbs favor `da li`-fronting. p.9.

### Three-gender noun system and possessive-adjective paradigm

Lesson 2 formally tabulates the masculine/neuter/feminine noun-gender system (e.g. `prózor`/`pèro`/
`kárta`) and the full possessive-adjective paradigm across all three genders: `mój/mòje/mòja` ("my"),
`náš/nàše/nàša` ("our"), `tvój/tvòje/tvòja` ("your," informal), `váš/vàše/vàša` ("your"),
`njègov/njègovo/njègova` ("his"), `njézin/njén` (+ neut./fem. forms) ("her" — two competing forms
given), `njihov/njihovo/njihova` ("their"). Possessive adjectives agree with the gender of the noun
possessed, not the possessor. p.10-11.

### Genitive-, dative-, and instrumental-governing prepositions (assigned across Lessons 2-6)

The book assigns case-governing prepositions incrementally rather than in one table: genitive-taking
prepositions accumulate across lessons — `bez` ("without"), `do` ("until, up to," already noted in
`established/002`), `ispred` ("in front of"), `iz` ("from"), `iza` ("behind"), `od` ("from"), `kod`
("at"), `zbog` ("because of") — all explicitly practiced together in Lesson 3's vježba III
"missing prepositions" drill (p.23), which groups them as a genitive-preposition set. Dative-taking
`o` ("about, concerning") and `po` ("through, along, on") appear in Lessons 4-5. Instrumental `s`
("with") and the instrumental-marked `krédom` ("by means of chalk," from `kréda`) appear in Lesson 4
and Lesson 6. p.20-24, p.34.

### Genitive case, accusative plural, nominative plural, negated "to be"

Lesson 3's grammar box (p.16) names these as its topics without providing a single consolidated
table in this page range — negation of "to be" is exemplified via `nìje` ("is not," already captured
in `established/002`) rather than a full `nisam/nisi/nije...` paradigm table appearing on these pages.
Accusative-plural and nominative-plural forms are drilled via the noun-replacement lists in vježba
II-III (e.g. `pàs`→accusative `psa`-type alternation implied by the `(ps-)` stem note on `pàs`, p.16)
rather than tabulated explicitly.

### Ijekavian/ekavian dialect naming and the "Dialect Choice" guidance

Building on the ije/e jat-reflex pattern already documented in `established/002`, Lesson 5's "Dialect
Choice" note (p.29) adds two genuinely new facts: (1) `ijekavski` has a synonym label, `jekavski`; (2)
practical sociolinguistic guidance for learners — since virtually all printed Cr&S material is in one
variety or the other, a learner should pick one and stick with it consistently (mixing ijekavski and
ekavski forms in the same speech risks sounding odd to native speakers, though either is fully
comprehensible). The book specifically recommends ijekavski for students with a Croatian-heritage
connection and ekavski for those following standard Serbian practice. p.29.

### Neuter nouns in -men- (e.g. `ime` → `imena`)

Lesson 5's grammar box names this as an assigned topic: certain neuter nouns ending in a bare vowel in
the nominative singular (e.g. `ime`, "name," already captured in `established/002`; `vréme`/`vrijème`,
"weather/time," captured above with genitive `vrèmena` shown) insert `-men-` before case endings in
oblique forms — the genitive `vrèmena` given alongside the Lesson 5 vocabulary box is this pattern's
one directly-attested example in this page range. p.27, p.29.

### Present tense of `imati`, `nemati`, `moći`

Named as a Lesson 5 grammar-box topic (p.29) without a full conjugation table appearing in this range;
`ìmati [ìma]` is given a headword entry in Lesson 6's vocabulary box (p.34) and used in context
("imate li još dobrih slobodnih mjesta?" — "do you still have any good free seats?"), which is the
only directly-observed conjugated form (`ìmate`, 2pl.) in this dispatch's page range.

### Vocative case (address forms)

Lesson 6's dialogue is built almost entirely around vocative-case address, formally assigned as a
grammar-box topic (p.32): masculine titles/names take `-e` or `-u` (`gospòdine dòktore Ivànoviću`,
"Mr./Dr. Ivanović," vocative of `gospòdin dòktor Ivànović`), feminine titles take `-o` (`gòspođo
Brádić`, vocative of `gòspođa Brádić` — the surname itself stays unchanged while the title inflects).
p.31-32.

### Future tense (Cr./S. structural contrast)

Lesson 6 exemplifies a genuine structural (not just lexical) Cr./S. difference in forming the future
tense: Croatian uses the infinitive + a reduced/cliticized form of `htjeti` (e.g. "Ožèniti će se" or
the contracted "Ožèniće se" — both attested forms shown in the dialogue, p.31), while Serbian more
often uses `da` + present tense as an alternative construction alongside the infinitive-based form
(e.g. "Jèste li znàli da će se Pètar ùskoro ožèniti?" (Cr. column) vs. "Da li ste znàli da će Pètar
ùskoro da se òženi?" (S. column) — the S. column doubles down on the `da`+present pattern where the
Cr. column keeps the infinitive). This is a first genuine syntactic (not merely phonological/lexical)
axis of Cr./S. divergence documented in this dispatch, beyond the jat-reflex and vocabulary-choice
patterns already captured. p.31.

### `htjeti`/`hteti` present tense (assigned topic)

Named in Lesson 6's grammar box (p.32) alongside future tense, personal pronouns, personal names, the
negative particle `ne`, and negation-in-the-past — the book assigns these as reading/study topics but
does not print a full conjugation table within this page range; the future-tense auxiliary use of
`(h)će`/`ćemo`/etc. is attested only indirectly via the `Ožèniti će se`/`Móraću` forms in the Lesson 6
dialogue and vocabulary box (`Móraću da prisùstvujem` — "I'll have to attend," S. column future
construction; the Cr. column instead reads "Mórat ću prisùstvovati," again showing the infinitive-vs.
`da`-clause contrast noted above). p.31, p.36 (translation block).

### Deliberately unaccented running text: the "Krivi broj" anecdote

The book explicitly flags (p.33) that, with the exception of the "Krivi broj" ("Wrong Number")
anecdote, *all* material in the first ten lessons carries accent marks — this anecdote is
intentionally presented *without* accent marks specifically so students can see what real-world Cr&S
prose looks like outside a beginning textbook, since virtually all authentic printed Cr&S text omits
the pitch-accent diacritics used pedagogically throughout this book. The author also notes the anecdote
deliberately packs in examples of every noun case plus multiple present-tense forms and one past-tense
form, in Croatian, with the mali rječnik glossing the Serbian equivalents by slash-pair convention. A
genuinely useful signal for how sparse standard printed Cr&S orthography actually is relative to this
pedagogical text. p.33.

### Syllabic /r/ as a full vowel

Lesson 6's pronunciation-practice section formally teaches that Cr&S `r` functions both as an ordinary
consonant (e.g. `rád`, "work/matrimony") and as a syllable nucleus/vowel in its own right when flanked
by consonants with no other vowel present (e.g. `bŕk`, "moustache"; `ŕt`, "cape"; `stŕm`, "steep"). The
book's own parenthetical syllabification aids for two entries — `ùmro` = "um-r-o" and `gŕoce` =
"gr-o-ce" — make the syllabic-consonant status of `r` explicit for the learner. p.32.

### Regional lexical-choice pairs beyond the strict jat split

Several Cr./S. word-pairs captured in this dispatch are lexical (different root words entirely) rather
than phonological (same root, different jat reflex), a distinction worth flagging separately since it
represents a different mechanism than the ije/e pattern already well-documented: `plòča`/`tábla`
("blackboard" — Lesson 4's footnote additionally notes Serbs also sometimes use `ploča`, softening the
strict binary), `obìtelj`/`pòrodica` ("family"), `glàzba`/`mùzika` ("music," Croatian-purist vs.
pan-regional), `kàzalište`/`pòzorište` ("theater"), `ùred`/`kancelàrija` ("office," given without a
regional tag), `kraváta`/`màšna` ("necktie"), `žlica`/`kàšika` ("spoon"). p.10, p.21, p.27, p.34.

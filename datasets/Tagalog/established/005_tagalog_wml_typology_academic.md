## Grammar points

### Historical and sociolinguistic background

Tagalog is Western-Malayo-Polynesian (Austronesian), in a subgroup with Bikol, Bisayan, and
Mansakan languages, originally native to the eastern Visayas/northeast Mindanao before migrating
north into Luzon by Spanish contact (1521). Designated national language in 1937, constitutionally
established 1987, taught nationwide as **Filipino**. Spoken by roughly a quarter of the Philippine
population in 1940, about half by 1970, and today an estimated 90%+ of the ~80 million population as
either first or second language — a striking, well-documented language-spread trajectory directly
relevant to any later comparison this project might make against how slang itself spreads through a
population over a similar multi-generational timescale. The Manila educated dialect underlies
standard Filipino; other named regional dialects are Bataan, Batangas, Bulacan, Tanay-Paete, and
Tayabas. Spanish (three-plus centuries of colonial rule) and English (1898–1946 American rule, plus
its ongoing status as a co-official higher-education/lingua-franca language) are confirmed as the
two loanword sources shaping the lexicon — with measurable phonological impact (the expanded 5-vowel
system, marginal affricate phonemes, gender-marked borrowed vocabulary) but "little if any" impact on
core syntax or morphology, a clean confirmation of files 001–004's loanword observations from an
independent, citable academic source.

### Phonology: vowels, consonants, stress, and orthography

Contemporary Tagalog has 5 vowel phonemes (developed from an earlier 3-vowel system where i/e and
u/o were each a single phoneme) and 16 native consonant phonemes, plus marginal /f/, /v/, /ʧ/, /ʤ/
in loanwords common among more educated/English-exposed speakers. **Vowel length is phonemic** in
non-word-final syllables (a fact entirely absent from files 001–004, whose orthography-based
examples cannot represent it) but is predictable (always long) in phrase-final syllables of native
words; loaned monosyllabic proper names are an exception, carrying phonemic length in any position.
**Stress** is tightly tied to vowel length (analysts differ on which is primary) and is not
reduction-driven — Tagalog is syllable-timed, not stress-timed. A significant morphophonemic
alternation replaces word-final glottal stop with vowel length in non-phrase-final position
(*maputi?* → *maputi: ŋa?* "it's really white"), and verbal suffixes -an/-in trigger a rightward
shift of vowel length (and stress) plus /h/-epenthesis after vowel-final roots — the precise
phonological mechanism underlying the "h is inserted after a vowel-final root" rule files 001–004
state only as a surface spelling fact. Tagalog is not tonal but has a complex intonational system
distinguishing statement/question-word-question/yes-no-question patterns. Orthographically, Tagalog
used a pre-colonial syllabary of probable Indian origin before the Spanish imposed the Roman
alphabet; the modern *abakada* has 20 letters (excluding c/f/j/q/v/x/z). The orthography does not
mark vowel length or stress and has two notable non-phonemic spellings: *ng* (pronounced /naŋ/) and
*mga* (pronounced /maŋa/).

### Syntax: Tagalog as an ergative language, and the reframing of the "focus" system

This chapter's central contribution over files 001–004 is situating the trigger/voice system within
modern syntactic theory. Early 20th-century structuralist grammars (Bloomfield 1917) described
Tagalog as having a unique "focus" system in which a verbal affix marks the semantic role of one
argument, producing a paradigm of "active" (actor-focus) vs. several "passive" (goal/instrument/
etc.-focus) structures — but this "passive" label is misleading, since these constructions are the
*unmarked*, ordinary way to express a sentence with a definite patient, not a marked/derived
alternative the way true passives are in English. Modern analyses instead treat Tagalog as either
**ergative/split-ergative** or as having an **active/fluid/hybrid/symmetrical voice system**; the
chapter adopts the **ergative analysis** as most widely used across theoretical frameworks (Relational
Grammar, Role and Reference Grammar, Government and Binding, Minimalist, etc.). Under this analysis:
the focused/absolutive argument (marked by ang/si, "nominative" in many descriptions) patterns
identically whether it's the sole argument of an intransitive clause (S) or the patient of a
transitive clause (P) — true ergative alignment — while the actor of a transitive clause (A) carries
distinct ergative case-marking (ng/ni). This directly explains why files 001–004's "object-focus"
sentences are not true passives: the patient's marking is simply the ordinary absolutive case any
intransitive subject also receives, not a specially demoted/promoted passive-voice form.

### Case-marking phrases: absolutive, ergative, oblique

**Absolutive** phrases (ang for common nouns, si for personal names) mark whichever argument the
verb's affix "focuses," and typically carry definite reference. **Ergative** phrases (ng/ni) mark
the actor of a transitive clause and are unmarked for definiteness (can be read as either definite
or indefinite); they share the same form as genitive/possessor phrases and the same pronoun set.
**Oblique** phrases (also ng-marked, confusingly identical in form to ergative ng, but functionally
and distributionally distinct) mark an optional adjunct or — in a distinct "dyadic intransitive"
construction — an *obligatory*, always-indefinite theme argument whose verb carries actor-focus
morphology despite having two arguments (e.g. *Gagawa ng kubo ang tindero* "The storekeeper will
make a hut," structurally intransitive-looking despite being semantically two-place). Whether this
oblique-theme construction should be called anti-passive, pseudo-transitive, or evidence of a
"symmetrical voice system" is flagged as a live, unresolved point of theoretical controversy in the
literature — the chapter does not adjudicate it, simply reports the dispute.

### Word order, enclitics, and the AY-inversion construction

Tagalog is predicate-initial; post-verbal argument order is otherwise largely free (any permutation
of the "storekeeper/rice/sack" example sentence is grammatical, if sometimes marked/unusual), **except**
for enclitic pronouns and enclitic adverbial particles (ba, pa, na, etc.), which occupy a fixed
position immediately after the first (clause-initial) constituent, with monosyllabic pronouns
preceding disyllabic ones when two co-occur — the same shortest-before-longest ordering rule files
001–003 state as a practical heuristic, here given its proper formal status as an enclitic-ordering
constraint. The **ay**-construction (fronting a constituent, marked by *ay*, without changing
denotation) is confirmed as "more common in writing and formal speech than ordinary conversation,"
and is analyzed here as a discourse device for reintroducing an already-established narrative
referent — a more precise discourse-pragmatic characterization than files 001–002 offer. A second,
distinct fronting pattern (no *ay*, falling intonation plus pause) marks contrastive emphasis instead.

### Relativization and the actor/absolutive subjecthood split

A key argument for the ergative analysis: certain "subject-like" properties attach to the
**absolutive** (focused) argument specifically — notably **relativizability**: only the absolutive
argument of a clause can be relativized in Tagalog. To relativize an actor, the clause must be
intransitive (actor = absolutive); to relativize a patient, a transitive (patient-focus) clause must
be used; to relativize a location or beneficiary, the verb must carry the corresponding
locative-effect or beneficiary-effect affix. Attempting to relativize a non-absolutive argument
produces an ungrammatical sentence. But a *different* set of subject-like properties — controlling
imperative addressee-hood and reflexive reference — attaches instead to the **actor**, regardless of
whether the actor happens to also be the absolutive. This split (absolutive controls
relativization; actor controls reflexives/imperatives) is the chapter's clearest formal evidence that
Tagalog "subjecthood" isn't a single unified grammatical relation the way it typically is in English,
and is the single most theoretically significant point in this whole chapter for grounding later
analysis of how flexible/rigid Tagalog argument structure can get under slang-driven reanalysis.

### Morphology: stem formation (pag-/paN-), reduplication, and derivation

Verb stems are often polymorphemic, built productively on the stem-forming prefixes **pag-**
(combines with nouns for "characteristic activity involving the noun's referent," e.g. mag-Ingles
"speak English"; also forms "intensive" verbs denoting repeated/prolonged action) and **paN-**
(less productive than pag-, forms similar characteristic-activity verbs, plus destructive-activity
verbs like *mangwalis* "hit with a broom," plus multiple-object verbs like *manguha* "gather," plus
a formally distinct instrumental-stem-forming paN- that can stand alone as an instrument noun, e.g.
*pamunas* "something to wipe with"). Historically, the actor-focus prefixes **mag-** and **maN-**
derive from m- replacing the stem-forming prefixes' initial p-. Two **distinct reduplicating
prefixes** exist: a **monosyllabic** one (copy of the root's first C+V) used in certain intensive
formations, and a **disyllabic** one (copy of the root's first two syllables) forming **moderative**
verbs — actions performed "in moderation, occasionally, at random" (e.g. *linis-linis* "clean a
little"). This moderative/disyllabic pattern is not mentioned anywhere in files 001–004 and is a
genuinely new reduplication function — a fourth distinct grammatical job for reduplication in
Tagalog (alongside aspect-marking, adjectival intensification/plural-marking, and habitual-adverb
derivation already tracked across files 002–004).

### Subject-marking affixes, abilitative derivation, and causatives

The chapter's Table 49.3 (Actor: -um-/m-/ma-/maka-; Patient: -in/i-/-an/ma-; Location: -an/-in;
Beneficiary/Instrument: i-) formally confirms files 001–004's trigger-affix inventory from an
independent source, while adding two derivational systems files 001–004 do not cover at all:
**Abilitative** verbs (ability/circumstance reading, "be able to X") use **maka-** (intransitive,
replacing -um-/m-) or **ma-** (transitive, replacing -in but co-occurring with i-/-an), e.g.
*makaawit* "be able to sing" (cf. *umawit* "sing"), *magamit* "be able to use" (cf. *gamitin*
"use"). **Causative** verbs use the prefix **pa-** stacked on top of the ordinary voice affix, with
a systematic case-role shift: when the causer is the grammatical subject, the actor-focus affix
mag- is invariably used (*magpapunta* "cause to go"); when the causee is the subject, the transitive
affix -in is invariably used (*papuntahin* "cause someone to go"); when some other argument (e.g.
the thing cleaned) is the subject, -in gets displaced by i- or -an specifically because -in is
"pre-empted" for marking the causee (*ipalinis* "cause [something] to be cleaned" vs. *palinisin*
"cause [someone] to clean").

### Aspect: actuality and incompleteness marking, formalized

The chapter reframes Tagalog's three-way completed/incompleted/contemplated aspect system (already
covered pedagogically in file 003) using two cross-cutting formal dimensions: **incompleteness
marking** (shared by imperfective and contemplated forms — the reduplicating CV- prefix, whose vowel
is *always long*, distinguishing it phonologically from the short-vowel stem-forming reduplication
above) and **actuality marking** (shared by imperfective and perfective forms — an affix containing
/n/: the prefix n-, the prefix ni-, or the infix -in-, with -um- verbs marked solely by retaining the
-um- infix itself, absent in contemplated forms). This two-dimensional formalization is the precise
mechanism underlying file 003's four-step "insert -in-, then reduplicate" recipe-style aspect rules —
genuinely new analytical content, not just a restatement.

### Adjectival-verb morphology: number, gender, intensity, comparison

Adjectival verbs (most commonly ma- + root) can be morphologically marked for **plural number** via
monosyllabic reduplication between ma- and the stem (*mabubuti* "be good, pl." — optional, non-
pluralized forms also serve for plural referents) and, in a small Spanish-borrowed subset, for
**gender** (komika/komiko, simpatika/simpatiko, tonta/tonto — feminine -a / masculine -o). Intensity
is marked by **napaka-** (replacing ma-; "intensive") vs. **moderative** disyllabic reduplication
(*mabuti-buti* "rather good") — confirming and sharpening file 002's superlative/intensifier
inventory with the formal "intensive vs. moderative" labels and showing moderative reduplication
is the *same* disyllabic-reduplication mechanism noted under stem formation above, now applied to
adjectives rather than verbs. Comparative-of-equality is (ka)sing- (ma- is dropped after it:
*singbuti* "as good as"); superlative is pinaka- (ma- is *retained* after it: *pinakamabuti*
"best") — a subtle but formally real asymmetry in how the two comparison-prefixes interact with the
adjective's own ma- prefix that neither file 002 nor the pedagogical source surfaces.

### Noun and pronoun morphology

Tagalog noun morphology is comparatively simple: no case or number inflection on the noun itself
(case role is carried entirely by the marker system), with only a few Spanish-borrowed nouns
gender-marked (amiga/amigo, sekretarya/sekretaryo). Productive noun-forming affixes include **-an**
(place-of-X: aklatan "library" ← aklat "book"), **-in** (object-of-the-verb: awitin "song" ← umawit
"sing"), and **taga-** (performer-of-the-verb: tagasulat "writer" ← sumulat "write"). The personal
pronoun system (Table 49.4) distinguishes genitive/locative/unmarked forms per person-number
category, with second-person singular uniquely having *four* forms (ka as an absolutive-only
enclitic vs. ikaw as a free unmarked form, beyond the shared genitive mo/locative iyo) — a level of
formal precision files 001–002 state as the "ikaw-vs-ka positional rule" without naming the deeper
genitive/locative/unmarked three-way case-like distinction underlying the whole pronoun paradigm.
Demonstratives mirror this exact three-way distinction (nito/dito/ito, etc.).

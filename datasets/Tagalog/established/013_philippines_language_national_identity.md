## Sociolinguistic/historical points

### National-language timeline (pp. 364–368)

A clean chronology worth recording in full, since every later Tagalog source in this project will
need to be placed against it for era/register context:

- **1897–1898**: Biak-na-Bato and Malolos Constitutions deliberately avoid the question of a
  national local language, continuing Spanish for formal matters and local languages informally.
- **1935**: Commonwealth-era constitution mandates a future legislature select a national language
  from the existing indigenous languages.
- **1936**: National Language Institute established by the Commonwealth Congress.
- **1937**: Tagalog selected as the basis of the national language — a decision driven specifically
  by Commonwealth President Manuel Quezon overruling an Assembly preference for a language built
  from a blend of Philippine languages, not from a neutral linguistic-merit process. At the time,
  Tagalog had ~12 million speakers vs. Cebuano's ~10 million and Ilocano's ~5 million — a real but
  not overwhelming plurality, which the source frames as the root of lasting regional resentment
  (the Cebuano opposition specifically, still ongoing as of this chapter's writing).
- **1939**: Tagalog officially proclaimed the national language by Quezon.
- **1940**: First taught in colleges/high schools under the deliberately de-ethnicized label *Wikang
  Pambansa* ("the national language") — an explicit attempt to soften the Tagalog-specific framing.
- **1959**: Renamed *Pilipino* by Secretary of Education Jose Romero — again explicitly a public-
  relations move per the source, and again failing to dispel the "Tagalog by another name"
  perception.
- **1973 Constitution** (drafted via the 1971 Constitutional Convention): sets a new, more pluralist
  mandate — a future language academy should build a genuinely new national language, *Filipino*,
  based on all major Philippine languages, replacing Tagalog-based Pilipino.
- **1986–1987**: constitutional commissioners, in the "people power" atmosphere following the Marcos
  ouster, declare *Filipino* to already exist and name it the national language by legislative fiat
  — even though, per the source, the pluralist "built from all major languages" Filipino was "still
  very much a work in progress" and the result "turned out to still be heavily based on Tagalog."

### English as co-official language and the bilingual education scheme (pp. 366–370)

- American colonial administration (from 1901, via the "Thomasites" — ~2,000 American teachers
  recruited 1901–1920) established English as the exclusive medium of instruction, not because of
  policy preference for English per se, but because **no single Philippine language was considered
  developed/widespread enough** to serve that role at the time — a concrete, source-stated reason
  worth noting given how often this kind of colonial-language-policy choice gets assumed to be
  purely ideological.
- **1974 bilingual education scheme** (continuing into the 21st century as of writing): English for
  mathematics/science, Filipino for social-studies/social-science subjects — formalizing a split that
  persists, with some regions (the source names Region VII/Cebu/Central Visayas specifically)
  reverting portions of instruction back to English.
- **1987 Constitution**: recognizes English and Filipino as the Philippines' two official languages.
- A 1994 self-report survey (cited directly): 56% of Filipinos claim to speak English, 74% claim to
  understand it, 73% claim to read it, 50% claim to write it — explicit, source-cited sociolinguistic
  data on English penetration, useful as an era-anchored (mid-1990s) data point distinct from
  contemporary estimates.

### The three-way functional split: vernacular / Filipino / English (pp. 370–371)

The source gives an explicit, named functional/diglossic split worth preserving as a structural
model for how register choice maps onto domain in the Philippines, directly useful for this
project's register/usage-tier conventions:

- **Local vernacular/regional language**: home, neighborhood, province-level everyday communication;
  worship; local/regional lingua franca.
- **Filipino**: national-level lingua franca, political campaigning — explicitly *not* the first
  priority in individual language learning per the source.
- **English**: higher-order cognitive activity, university study, diplomacy, international
  communication, employment access and socio-economic mobility signaling — explicitly named as the
  Filipino's actual first priority in practical language learning, ahead of Filipino itself.

The source frames this as fundamentally **pragmatic rather than identity-driven**: Filipinos do not
equate nationalism/loyalty with a single language the way some other nation-states do, but treat
language choice as a utility calculation per domain — with the explicit caveat (footnote 9) that even
some Filipino families' internal/family communication has shifted toward English among the educated
elite.

### Code-mixing explicitly flagged as an active, growing mechanism (p. 373, footnote 10)

Directly relevant to this project's slang-mechanics goals: the source's final footnote states
explicitly that "code-mixing of English and Filipino is an increasing trend, especially among
members of the younger generations." This is presented as a specific instance of a broader
"pidginization" frame the chapter applies to Philippine culture generally (drawing an explicit
parallel to historical pidgins **Bago**, Northern Luzon, and **Chabacano**, the Spanish-based creole
of Spanish settlement areas) — the chapter's own closing argument is that Filipino linguistic/
cultural identity is *constitutively* a mixture rather than a pure or singular thing, and that
code-mixing (what is popularly called Taglish, though the chapter itself does not use that specific
term) is simply the present-day linguistic expression of that same long-running pattern. This is a
useful macro-level framing to carry into this project's eventual mechanics-analysis phase: code-
mixing/borrowing as a slang-formation mechanism in Tagalog is not a recent or marginal phenomenon by
this source's account, but continuous with centuries of contact-driven linguistic mixing (Malay,
Chinese, Spanish, American/English strata named explicitly, p. 373).

### Regional/dialectal tension named explicitly: the Cebuano exception (pp. 365, 369)

The source repeatedly and explicitly names **Cebuano speakers (Region VII, Cebu, Central Visayas)**
as the one group still holding out against accepting Filipino as a legitimate, impartial national
language — framed by the source as at least partly a tool of "local political advantage by local
Cebuano politicians," but grounded in a real, named historical grievance (the 1937 selection
decision favoring Tagalog despite Cebuano's near-comparable speaker count at the time). This is
exactly the kind of explicit regional/political annotation the extraction spec prioritizes, and is a
useful real-world anchor for any later comparative work against Cebuano itself, should this project
ever extend there.

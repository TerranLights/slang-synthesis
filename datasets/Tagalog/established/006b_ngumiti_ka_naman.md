## Grammar points

### *Nakaka-* adjectivizing prefix
*Nakaka-* adjectivizes state verbs and other adjectives to mean "causing/able to cause [state]":
*mahawa* "be contaminated" → *nakakahawa* "contagious"; *masira* "be broken/ruined" → *nakakasira*
"destructive"; *magutom* "be hungry" → *nakakagutom* "can make one hungry"; *mataba* "fat, stout" →
*nakakataba* "fattening"; *malungkot* "sad" → *nakakalungkot* "saddening." (Lesson 47; p.73.)

### Code-blended loan verbs with native affixation
English verbs can be fully integrated into the Tagalog actor-trigger affix system, not just
borrowed as nouns: *mag-aapologize* "will apologize" (*mag-* future-aspect reduplicated prefix +
English "apologize"), alongside the previously-noted *nagsuicide* (Lesson 42) and *nasyut* (Lesson
19). This is a systematic, productive borrowing-integration mechanism in this source, not a one-off. (Lesson 48; p.74.)

### Full *sa*-pronoun set (directional/locative/possessive)
A third full pronoun paradigm, marked by *sa* and used for direction/location/possession targets:
*sa akin* "to/from me," *sa iyo* "to/from you," *sa kaniya* "to/from him/her," *sa amin* "to/from us"
(exclusive), *sa atin* "to/from us" (inclusive), *sa inyo* "to/from you" (pl.), *sa kanila* "to/from
them." This is the third pronoun set documented in this source alongside the topic-pronoun set
(Lesson 23) and the *ko*-set (Lesson 37) — Tagalog pronouns inflect for grammatical role (topic /
actor-possessor / oblique) the way many European languages inflect for case. (Lesson 53; p.80.)

### *Kaya* as pseudo-verb "can handle/afford"
A second, unrelated *kaya* (distinct from the doubt-particle *kaya* "I wonder," Lesson 23) is a
pseudo-verb meaning "can handle, tackle, or afford," patterning like *gusto/ayaw/kailangan/alam* with
a definite object: *Kaya ko ang matangkad* "I can handle the tall one." (Lesson 54; p.81.)

### *Puwede* as a modal qualifier
*Puwede*/*pwede* "can be, possibly" joins the qualifier class (Lessons 34, 45) marking possibility/
permission: *Puwede ba akong magluto dito?* "Can I cook here?" (Lesson 56; p.83.) *Maari*/*maaari* is
given explicitly as a synonym of *puwede* in the same qualifier slot. (Lesson 63; p.90.)

### Same root, different word class via affixation: *linis*
*Linis* "clean" spans multiple word classes depending on affixation: *nilinis* (goal-trigger past,
"was cleaned up"), *maglinis* (actor-trigger, "to clean"), *luminis* (actor-trigger inchoative,
"became clean" — a change-of-state reading), and *malinis* (static adjective, "clean"). The book
contrasts *luminis* vs. *malinis* directly to show the change-of-state/static distinction. (Lesson
59; p.86.)

### *Kailan* "when"
*Kailan* asks about time, placed sentence-initially regardless of what follows (a noun phrase, an
actor-topic verb, or a goal-topic verb): *Kailan ang party?* "When is the party?"; *Kailan sila
nag-away?* "When did they fight?"; *Kailan nila binili ang sapatos?* "When did they buy the shoes?"
(Lesson 66; p.93.)

### *Pa-* directional prefix
*Pa-* prefixed to a directional root forms an adverb of direction: *pataas* "upward" (root *taas*
"up, above"), *pababa* "downward" (root *baba* "down, below"). Also seen with place names/goals in
Lesson 62's grammar note: *Pa-Cebu ba sila?* "Are they headed for Cebu?" (Lesson 62; p.89.)

### *Makipag-* participatory-action prefix
*Makipag-* forms an actor-trigger (AT) verb meaning "to do [root] with/together with another
person": *makipagkaibigan* "befriend, make friends with," *makipagtalo* "argue with," *makikipagdate*
"go dating with," *makikipag-away* "pick/get into a fight with." A dedicated affix for
inherently-social, dyadic actions. (Lesson 70; p.97.)

### *-an* locative-noun affix
*-an* derives a place-noun from a root naming what happens/is kept there: *kainan* "eatery" (root
*kain* "eat"), *hintuan* "stopping place" (*hintay* "wait"), *sampayan* "clothesline" (*sampay*
"hang"), *lutuan* "cooking utensil/place" (*luto* "cook"), *bilihan* "buying place" (*bili* "buy"),
*baunan* "lunch box" (*baon*, Lesson 35), *tulugan* "sleeping place" (*tulog*), *tapunan* "dumping
place" (*tapon*, cf. Lesson 24's *itapon*), *basurahan* "garbage can" (*basura*, Lesson 24), *babuyan*
"piggery" (*baboy*, Lesson 50), *lugawan* "porridge stand" (*lugaw*, Lesson 71). A highly productive
pattern worth flagging for the mechanics-analysis phase as a possible slang-coining resource (new
*-an* place-nouns are easy to mint on any noun root). (Lessons 62, 71; p.89 [implicit], 98.)

### Metaphor-productivity: "kill"/"open" for appliance off/on
Idiomatically, Tagalog describes switching an appliance off as *patay* ("kill, die") and on as
*buksan* ("open") — a systematic domain-metaphor (electrical appliance states mapped onto
life/death and container-opening vocabulary) rather than a one-off expression, confirmed by the
book's own culture note. A genuine `metaphor_productivity`-type mechanism per this project's
Usage-Tier taxonomy. (Lesson 74; p.101.)

### Verbalizing adjectives via goal-trigger affixation
An adjective's intensity can be changed by casting it as a goal-trigger verb: *Malakas ang TV* "The
TV is loud" → *Lakasan mo ang TV* "Turn the TV up" (make it [more] *malakas*); *Mahina ang radyo* "The
radio is weak" → *Hinaan ko ang radyo* "I'll turn it down"; *Malaki ito* "This is big" → *Nilakihan ko
ito* "I enlarged this"; *Maliit ang sapatos* "The shoes are small" → *Liitan* "reduce the size of."
(Lesson 73; p.100.)

### Time/condition connectors: past *noong*/*nang* vs. non-past *kung*/*pag*/*kapag*
Past-time "when" is marked by *noong* (+ a verb in any aspect) or *nang* (+ an infinitive/imperative
verb form): *Kumakain sila noong nag-away kami* / *Kumakain sila nang mag-away kami* "They were
eating when we fought." Non-past "if/when" is marked by *kung* (any verb form) or *pag*/*kapag*
(usually a past/begun-action verb form despite the non-past meaning): *Pupunta ako rito kung
mag-aaway sila* / *Pupunta ako rito pag nag-away sila* "I'll come here if they fight." (Lesson 76;
pp.103–104.)

### Intensive adjectives/pseudo-verbs via full reduplication
Doubling an adjective or pseudo-verb intensifies it: *gusto* → *gustong-gusto* "love/really like,"
*ayaw* → *ayaw na ayaw* "hate/really dislike," *kailangan* → *kailangang-kailangan* "need badly,"
*alam* → *alam na alam* "know very well," *kaya* → *kayang-kaya* "can handle very easily," *maganda*
→ *magandang-maganda* "very pretty," *pagod* → *pagod na pagod* "very tired," *galit* → *galit na
galit* "very mad," *masama* → *masamang-masama* "very bad." A fully general and highly productive
intensification mechanism — reduplication as a slang/emphasis-coining resource. (Lesson 77; pp.105–106.)

### Alternative compound-subject pronoun expressions
Two people can be referred to together either by naming one and pluralizing the pronoun for the
other, or by linking both names/pronouns with *at*/*ni*: "Ben and I" = *si Ben at ako* / *kami ni
Ben*; "you and Ben" = *ikaw at si Ben* / *kayo ni Ben*. The same pattern extends to the *ko*-set
(actor/possessor pronouns): "(of) Ben and I" = *ko at ni Ben* / *namin ni Ben*. (Lesson 79; p.108.)

### Alternative demonstrative-phrase forms
A demonstrative modifying a noun can be expressed two ways: *itong lindol* / *ang lindol na ito*
"this earthquake"; as an actor/possessor form, *nitong lindol* / *ng lindol na ito*. Same pattern for
near-listener and far demonstratives: *iyang lalaki* / *ang lalaking iyan* "that (near) man"; *iyong
sabon* / *ang sabong iyon* "that (far) soap." (Lesson 80; p.109.)

### Personification marking with *si*/*ni*/*kay*
The personal-name markers *si* (topic), *ni* (actor/possessor), *kay* (oblique) can be extended to
ordinary nouns, adjectives, or verbs to personify the referent as if it were a named individual:
*Lasing si lalaki kaya galit si babae* "Man [personified as 'Mr. Drunk'] is drunk so Woman is
furious"; *Nag-aaway si Taba at si Payat* "Fatso and Skinny are fighting." A productive
nickname-coining mechanism — turning a descriptive word into a personal-name-marked nickname — worth
flagging for slang-mechanics analysis (`morphological_play`/`metaphor_productivity`-adjacent).
(Lesson 86; p.117.)

### *Pag-* verbal-noun (gerund) of *-um-* actor-trigger verbs
*Pag-* forms a gerund/verbal-noun from an *-um-* actor-trigger verb: *umuwi* "went home" → *ang
pag-uwi ng guro* "the teacher's going home"; *bumalik* "went back" → *ang pagbalik ni Fred doon*
"Fred's return there"; *dumating* "arrived" → *sa pagdating nila* "at their arrival." (Lesson 88;
p.119.)

### Sentence-final terminators
A closed class of particles occurs utterance-finally to mark the speaker's stance toward what was
just said: *o* "look, listen" (attention-getting), *e* "you see" (explanatory), *a* "it's true/a
fact" (assertive), *ba* "you got to believe it" (assurance — distinct from the yes/no question
marker *ba*, Lesson 1), *ha* "huh, ha" (tag-question/confirmation-seeking). E.g. *Rebelde siya, e*
"He/she is a rebel, you see"; *Gawin mo iyan, ha?* "Do/fix that, huh?" This is a genuinely rich
discourse-pragmatics paradigm, directly relevant to how register and stance get marked in colloquial
Tagalog speech. (Lesson 51; pp.77–78.)

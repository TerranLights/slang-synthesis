## Grammar points

### Lesson 42 — Plural forms of verbs (magsi-/magsipag- active; pag-+in/pag-+an passive)

The source opens with a historically notable citation: Lope K. Santos's 1939 *Balarila ng Wikang
Pambansa* (the foundational government-commissioned Tagalog grammar, written under the Institute of
National Language) explicitly rules that plural-verb agreement with a plural subject is **grammatically
optional, not compulsory**, and that a plural subject with a singular verb is not an error — an
unusually direct citation of a specific historical grammar authority and text, worth flagging as this
book's clearest explicit intertextual reference to the Tagalog grammatical-standardization tradition.
Despite this, the lesson teaches the "full" native plural-verb affixes the source itself admits are
genuinely difficult for non-native speakers to pronounce (citing **mangagsikáin**, **mangagkaín**,
**magsipagkaín**, **mangágsipagkáinan**, **mangagkaínan** as native-speaker-natural but learner-hostile
forms), opting instead to teach only the "simpler" subset: **magsi-** (plural of -um-, formed exactly
like singular -um- but with **magsi-** replacing the bare infix, and present/future reduplicating the
prefix's own second syllable — the same reduplication-targets-prefix pattern recurring from maka-/paki-/
magpa-), **magsipag-** (plural of mag-, formed the same way, with a hyphen before vowel-initial roots);
**pag- + -in/-hin** (plural of -in-/-hin-, infixing **-in-** after the prefix pag-'s own first letter
rather than into the root, with present reduplicating the *root's* first syllable and future
reduplicating the *prefix's* second syllable — a genuinely unusual mixed-target reduplication pattern
unique to this plural paradigm, since singular -in- verbs reduplicate only within the root); and **pag-
+ -an/-han** (plural of -an-/-han-, formed the same way as pag-+-in but adding -an, with irregular
roots dropping their second vowel or syllable exactly as documented for the singular -an- paradigm in
Lesson 33). **A notable register note**: the source explicitly states that in **conversational
language**, further triplicating/simplifying the reduplication pattern (e.g. **paglutuín** →
conversational **paglúlutuín**, with an extra reduplicated syllable) is an accepted informal
simplification of the "proper" formal paradigm — a genuine formal/conversational-register split
documented for this specific morphological paradigm, directly relevant to how colloquial speech
simplifies complex formal affixation.

### Lesson 43 — Contractions (a living, productive word-clipping system)

This lesson is framed explicitly and unusually as describing a **synchronically active, ongoing**
linguistic process rather than a closed, fixed vocabulary list — the source's own framing states that
Tagalog contractions, unlike fixed English contractions (isn't, don't), can omit or alter "many
different letters and syllables... depending on the speaker," and closes by instructing the reader to
**"look out for new contractions used by the younger generation that may creep [in] and be accepted
into the language"** — an explicit, source-acknowledged statement that this is an open, productive,
generationally-driven process of ongoing lexical change, making this lesson a uniquely strong candidate
for this project's slang-mechanics analysis phase. The catalogued contraction mechanisms include: (1)
**internal-syllable deletion + sound-fusion** (hintáy ka → téka, dropping **hin** and reducing **tay**
to **te** before fusing with **ka**; magmadalî ka → dalíka; táyo na → téna); (2) **whole-phrase
fusion with vowel reduction** (nákita mo → tamó; hayaán mo → hámo; ayaw ko → ayóko; pahingî → pénge;
pahingî ka → éka; tingnán ko → tangkó; ákin na → kaná); (3) **initial-syllable dropping marked with an
apostrophe** (hali ka → 'lika; hindî → 'dî; walâ → 'lâ; anó → 'nó) — a distinct sub-mechanism from (1)
and (2) since the elided material is the word's own *first* syllable rather than an internal one, and
the orthographic apostrophe explicitly marks this as a visibly-reduced form rather than folding the
loss invisibly into a new spelling; (4) **mayroón → méron**, a single, lexically-specific vowel-fusion
contraction (recapping the Lesson 12 note, now formally reclassified). **Pragmatic specialization is
documented for several contracted forms**: **'dî** and **'nó** are explicitly noted to function as
**tag questions** ("...right?"), a distinct grammaticalized use beyond their source words' ordinary
negative/question-word functions — i.e. the contraction process here didn't just shorten the word, it
narrowed/shifted its pragmatic function. **The clearest sociolinguistic finding in this lesson**: the
contracted forms **ikámo**/**ikáko** (from **wiká mo**/**wiká ko**, "you said"/"I said" — further
reducible to **kámo**/**káko** "in some areas," an explicit source-acknowledged regional sub-variant)
are explicitly stated to be **more commonly used by older people in rural areas**, while the
alternative (uncontracted, but still colloquial) **sabi ko**/**sabi nilá** is preferred by **younger
speakers** — a genuine, source-documented case of two competing colloquial forms for the same meaning
being generationally and regionally stratified, i.e. real synchronic language change actively observed
and reported by the source itself, not merely an extraction-side inference. This is flagged as a
flagship example for later cross-language slang/register mechanics comparison.

### Lesson 44 — Colors

The source opens with an explicit **lexical-gap observation**: Tagalog has **no single dedicated word
for brown** (nor for its shades like "golden brown") — the language instead repurposes **pulá** ("red")
for golden-brown in culinary contexts (**hanggáng pumulá**, "until it turns golden brown," a cooking
instruction), uses the race/skin-specific term **kayumanggí** (explicitly restricted to describing
Filipino ethnic skin tone and *not* usable for ordinary brown objects — a narrow, culturally-loaded
color term, with **moréna**, a Spanish loan, given as a near-synonym), and falls back on
**material-reference descriptive compounds** (**kulay kapé** "coffee-colored," **kulay tsokoláte**
"chocolate-colored") for ordinary brown objects — three entirely different strategies covering what
English treats as one basic color category, a genuine typological gap-filling pattern worth flagging.
**Basic colors** (itím "black," putî "white," pulá "red," asúl/bughaw "blue," diláw "yellow," bérde/
luntián "green," ube "violet," kulay kahél "orange," lila "purple," rósas "pink," abó "gray") mix
native Tagalog and Spanish-derived terms roughly evenly, with several near-synonym doublets (asúl/
bughaw; bérde/luntián) left unexplained as to register difference. **Intensification/darkening** uses
the by-now-familiar full-reduplication-plus-ligature pattern (asúl na asúl "navy blue," bérdeng-bérde
"dark green") — the same general intensive-reduplication mechanism documented for adjectives (Lesson 1),
comparatives (Lesson 11), pseudo-verbs (Lesson 22), and manner adverbs (Lesson 34), now shown applying
to colors as well, reinforcing that full lexical reduplication-for-intensity is one of the single most
pervasive, cross-category productive mechanisms in this language. **Lightening** uses **murá** ("pale/
light" — a genuinely polysemous word also meaning "unripe fruit" and "cheap/inexpensive," three senses
the source explicitly calls out as belonging to one lexeme) attached either before or after the color
term with no apparent meaning difference, consistent with the free-modifier-order pattern established
since Lesson 7. **Shade formation** (the "-ish" sense, e.g. "bluish," "reddish") uses either **ma- +
reduplicated root** or **-an/-han** suffixation, with several irregular prefix-triggered initial-
consonant shifts recurring from earlier lessons: **p→m** (pulá→mamúla-múla, putî→mamutî-mutî) and
**d→n** (diláw→maniláw-niláw) — and one outright **irregular prefix substitution**, **asúl** taking
**mang-** instead of the expected **ma-** (mangasúl-ngasúl), with the prefix's own **-ng** repeated a
second time after the internal hyphen — the single most irregular derivation documented in this
lesson. **Material-reference color compounds** (bérdeng mansánas "apple green," bérdeng lúmot "moss
green," gintô/ginintuán "gold/golden," pilak/pinilakan "silver/silvery," kulay-abó "ash-colored/gray")
extend the same descriptive-naming strategy already used for brown to further fine-grained shades,
illustrated in the source's own closing example with the culturally iconic **baróng tagálog** (the
traditional embroidered Filipino men's formal shirt).

## Grammar points

### 1. Three pronoun sets named up front: ANG, NG, SA

Unlike `008`, which introduces `ang`-pronouns, `ng`-pronouns, and `sa`-pronouns gradually across
many units, this source names all three pronoun *systems* explicitly in Lesson 1 before teaching
any of them in depth — `ang` (subject/nominative), `ng` (object), and `sa` (a third set, taught in
a later lesson) — giving learners the "trigger/case" framework up front rather than inductively.
`kami` (exclusive "we") vs. `tayo` (inclusive "we") is explained with the same inclusive/exclusive
logic as `008`.

### 2. Basic word order: Predicate+Subject is the spoken norm, Subject+Predicate (`ay`) is literary

This source states explicitly what `008` only implied: the Subject+`ay`+Predicate order (`Ako ay
Amerikano`) is grammatically correct and used in literary/formal language, but ordinary spoken
Tagalog overwhelmingly prefers Predicate+Subject with `ay` dropped (`Amerikano ako`). This directly
parallels and reinforces `008` Unit 7's treatment of `ay` as a formal/literary stylistic variant,
now stated as the default-vs-marked relationship explicitly.

### 3. `ang`/`si`: common-noun vs. personal-name markers (already known from `008`), reinforced with a direct minimal-pair drill

Same `ang`/`si` split already covered in `008` Unit 1 — included here only because the source adds
a clean worked contrast (`Mabuti ang estudyante` vs. `Mabuti si Maria`) that sharpens the rule with
direct drilling, not because the underlying grammar is new.

### 4. Culture note: Tagalog's status, dialect landscape, and linguistic history

A substantive culture note (new content, not covered in `008`) states Tagalog has 50+ million
speakers concentrated in southern Luzon, names several other major Philippine languages/dialects
(Cebuano, Ilokano, Waray-Waray, Hiligaynon, Pangasinan, Bikol, Maranao, Maguindanao, Tausug,
Kapampangan), notes large Tagalog-speaking diaspora communities abroad (citing the US, where it
ranks as the sixth most-spoken language), gives the folk etymology of "Tagalog" from "taga-ilog"
("from the river"), classifies it as Austronesian/Malayo-Polynesian with Malay and Chinese
influence, and names `Baybayin` as the indigenous pre-Spanish syllabic script later romanized under
colonial rule. It also frames Tagalog's productive affixation system itself as, in the source's own
quoted framing of linguist Wilhelm Humboldt, "the genius of Tagalog" — a notable editorializing
claim about the language's own structure.

### 5. Lesson 2: `saan ka pupunta?`/`saan ka galing?` as phatic, non-literal greetings

A culture note states explicitly that Tagalog has no direct equivalent of "hi"/"hello" as pure
greetings — these functions are instead filled by borrowed/adapted `Kamusta ka?` (from Spanish) or
by asking "where are you going?"/"where are you coming from?", which function as phatic small talk
(parallel to English "how's it going?") rather than real requests for an itinerary — a vague answer
like `diyan lang` ("just around here") is a fully acceptable, expected response. This is an
important cross-source confirmation: `008b` Unit 17 documents the *exact same* idiom pair
(`saan ang punta mo?`/`diyan lang`) independently, from a different author — strong convergent
evidence this is a real, productive Filipino speech-register pattern rather than one book's
idiosyncratic example.

### 6. NG pronouns express possession (new systematic framing vs. `008`)

This source introduces the full `ng`-pronoun set (`ko`/`mo`/`niya`/`natin`/`namin`/`ninyo`/`nila`)
specifically as *the* mechanism for possession, placed immediately after the possessed noun
(`kaibigan ko`, "my friend") — the same facts `008` Unit 7 covers, but framed here as one coherent
system introduced in a single lesson rather than built up gradually.

### 7. `may`/`mayroon`/`wala`: existence and possession without a "to have" verb

Tagalog expresses both "to have" and "there is/are" with the same word, `may` (full form
`mayroon`) — no separate verb exists for either concept. Word order is fixed: `may` + object +
subject/`ang`-pronoun (`May trabaho ka?`, "do you have work?"). `mayroon` (not `may`) is used when
no object noun follows, standing alone as a complete "yes, [there is/I have]" answer. `wala`
("nothing/there isn't/to not have") is `may`'s negative counterpart, already partly attested in
`008` Unit 2 — this source adds the explicit "no object → use `mayroon`/`wala` alone" rule not
spelled out there.

### 8. Question-word sentence order: Question Word + Subject + Verb

Stated as a general formula: `Saan + ka + pupunta?` ("Where are you going?"). The full
question-word inventory is given as a table: `sino` (who), `kailan` (when), `bakit` (why), `alin`
(which), `ilan` (how many), `magkano` (how much), `paano` (how, manner/means), `gaano` (how,
quality/degree) — `paano` vs. `gaano` is a distinction not made explicit in `008`.

### 9. The marker `sa` and SA pronouns: location, direction, future time, and possession

`sa` is named the "locative marker," covering English "to/at/in/on/from" for place, and also
marking future time expressions tied to a specific day/date/season/event (`sa Sabado`, "on
Saturday"). Combined with the SA-pronoun set (`akin`/`iyo`/`kaniya`/`atin`/`amin`/`inyo`/`kanila`),
`sa` forms Tagalog's possessive-pronoun equivalent (`sa akin`, "mine"; `sa akin ang bahay`, "the
house is mine") — functionally the same mechanism as `008` Unit 10's `sa`-possessive series, with
a cleaner unified table here. Before a personal name `sa` becomes `kay` (plural `kina`), directly
paralleling `ng`→`ni`/`nina`.

### 10. Reading exercise: authentic Tagalog newspaper headlines

Lesson 2 closes with a set of real-register (if illustrative) Tagalog newspaper headlines for
recognition practice (e.g. `Bagyong Galing sa Tsina Darating sa Bansa`, "Typhoon from China Now
Headed to the Country"; `Walang Pasok sa Lunes`, "No Classes/Work on Monday") — useful as a model
of headline-register Tagalog (frequent `may`/`walang` existential constructions, clipped
determiners) distinct from the conversational register most of the course otherwise models.

### 11. Culture note: Intramuros

A substantive historical culture note on Intramuros, Manila's walled city — founded under Muslim
rule by Rajah Soliman, conquered for Spain by Miguel López de Legazpi in 1571, survived Dutch/
British sieges, passed to American control in 1898, served as a Japanese garrison/prison in WWII,
and was heavily damaged in the WWII Battle for the Liberation of Manila — today partially restored
and a major tourist site. Included because Dialogue 2 is set there and because this level of
historical depth is distinctive to this source relative to `008`/`008b`.

### 12. Plural markers `mga` and `sina`

`mga` (pronounced "ma-ngá") precedes a noun to pluralize it — cross-reference `008` Unit 8's
identical mechanism. `sina` is given explicitly as the plural of the personal-name marker `si`
(`Sina Flor at Bobby`, "Flor and Bobby") — cross-reference `008` Unit 7's `sina`.

### 13. `ma-` adjectives and their reduplicated plural

Many Tagalog adjectives are `ma-` + a base word (`ma`+`ganda`="maganda," "beautiful/nice"), though
some adjectives (`bata`, "young") take no prefix at all. Plural agreement is marked by reduplicating
the *first two letters* of the base word after `ma-`: `maganda`→`magaganda` ("beautiful," plural).
This is a cleaner, more explicit statement of the same reduplication-for-plural-adjective mechanism
`008` Unit 8 only mentioned in passing ("Did you know" aside).

### 14. The exclamative construction: `Ang` + bare adjective base + NG pronoun

A second, distinct way to describe someone: `Ang` + the adjective's bare base (no `ma-`) + an
`ng`-pronoun forms an exclamative ("how X they are!") — `Ang bait nila!` ("How kind they are!"),
`Ang ganda niya!` ("How beautiful she is!") — explicitly compared by the source to English "My,
how BIG you are!" but noted as far more common in ordinary Tagalog than its English counterpart.
Plural again reduplicates the base's first two letters: `Ang gaganda nila!` ("How beautiful they
are!").

### 15. Numbers 1-20: native Tagalog vs. Spanish-derived, and when each is used

Tagalog has two complete parallel numeral systems in everyday use: native Tagalog (`isa`, `dalawa`,
`tatlo`...) and Spanish-derived (`uno`, `dos`, `tres`...). The source gives an explicit usage rule
`008` only demonstrated by example: **native Tagalog numerals are always used for counting**;
**Spanish-derived numerals are used for a person's age once past 10** (especially in speech); and
**time-telling always uses the Spanish-derived set**. Ordinal numbers use `pang-` + the native
cardinal (`pang`+`lima`=`panglima`, "fifth"; irregular `una`, "first," and `pangalawa`, "second").
Ages are asked with `Ilang taon ka na?` (lit. "how many years are you now?") and answered with a
Spanish-derived number + `anyos` (from Spanish año, "year").

### 16. Linkers `-ng`/`na` with adjectives, numbers, and SA pronouns

A single general linker (`-ng` suffixed to a vowel-final first word, `na` as a separate word after
a consonant-final first word) joins an adjective-noun pair in *either* order
(`magandang babae`/`babaeng maganda`, "beautiful woman") — this generalizes and more cleanly states
the same mechanism `008` Units 3-4 introduced piecemeal. The source adds two further linker
contexts not spelled out as explicitly in `008`: the linker always attaches to a **number**
modifying a noun regardless of position (`dalawang kapatid`, "two siblings"), and to a **SA
pronoun** expressing possession (`aking bahay`, "my house"; `kaniyang asawa`, "his/her spouse").

### 17. `nasaan`/`nasa` vs. `saan`: location of a thing vs. location of an action

Cross-reference `008` Unit 4/Unit 6's `nasaan`/`nasa` vs. `saan` split — restated here more crisply:
`nasaan` asks the location of a static person/thing, answered with `nasa` + place (`Nasa bahay
ako`, "I'm home"); `saan` asks about the location *of an action* (`Saan ka pupunta?`, "where are
you going?").

### 18. Reading exercise: an OFW poem, and the `bagong bayani` cultural concept

Lesson 3 closes with an original-register poem excerpt ("Sa Anak ng OFW") addressed to the child of
an overseas worker, naming real destination countries (Saudi, Sydney, Kuwait, Spain, Hong Kong,
London, Italy, Brunei) and evoking the emotional cost of labor migration — paired with a
substantive culture note on Overseas Filipino Workers (OFWs), honored as `bagong bayani` ("new
heroes"), numbering an estimated seven million (~10% of the population) and the third-most-mobile
national community after Chinese and Indian migrants, contributing billions of dollars annually to
the Philippine economy while also fragmenting families — a major real-world socio-linguistic
backdrop for understanding Filipino family-register vocabulary and values (e.g. `utang na loob`,
`bahala na`, `kumpare`/`kumare`) already documented in `008`/`008b`.

### 19. `gusto`/`ayaw`: the actor is marked with NG, "liked/wanted BY ME" not "I like/want"

A sharper grammatical framing than `008b`'s treatment: with `gusto`/`ayaw`, the `ng`-pronoun doesn't
mark a possessor but the *agent* of the liking/wanting, so `Gusto ko ang sala` literally parses as
"liked by me [is] the living room" rather than "I like the living room" — the source stresses this
reframing helps explain why `ng` (not an `ang`-pronoun) marks the "liker." When the liked/wanted
object is indefinite rather than definite, it takes `ng` instead of `ang` (`Gusto ko ng bahay sa
Laguna`, "I want [a] house in Laguna").

### 20. `ng`/`ni` as general possession markers ("of")

Beyond their pseudo-verb-actor role, `ng`/`ni` (plurals `ng mga`/`nina`) mark simple possession
equivalent to English "of": `ang hardin ng bahay` ("the garden of the house"), `ang kama ni Jenny`
("Jenny's bed") — the same mechanism as `008` Units 7/9, restated here as a unified rule.

### 21. Verb roots, and the three Tagalog verbal "aspects" (not tenses)

The source explicitly prefers the linguistic term **aspect** over "tense" for Tagalog verb forms,
naming three: **completed** (past), **contemplated** (future), and **incomplete** (progressive) —
plus a fourth, aspect-neutral **infinitive** form used for commands/requests/suggestions and as the
dictionary citation form. This is a more precise linguistic framing than `008`'s informal
"tense" terminology for the same underlying four-way system.

### 22. The `-um-` verb class and its infinitive formation

`-um-` is infixed immediately before the first vowel of the root: `tuloy`→`tumuloy`, `akyat`→
`umakyat`, `baba`→`bumaba`, `kain`→`kumain`, `lakad`→`lumakad`. The infinitive form doubles as an
imperative/command when paired with an `ang`-pronoun (`Dumalaw ka`, "visit!"; `Kumain kayo`, "eat!
[pl.]") — and, notably, the *same* infinitive form also expresses a **completed/past** action for
`-um-` verbs specifically (`Kumain siya`, "he/she ate") — the infinitive/past syncretism already
flagged in `008` Unit 12, confirmed independently here.

### 23. Reading exercise: home-safety tips, modeling `-um-` commands in authentic register

A home-safety-tips reading passage (paraphrased, not reproduced at length) models a dense run of
`-um-`-verb imperatives in a realistic register (buying a first-aid kit, placing emergency numbers
by the phone, not carrying obstructing items on stairs, fire-escape planning) — useful as a model
of instructional/imperative-register Tagalog beyond the course's conversational dialogues.

### 24. Culture note: `bahay kubo`, `bahay na bato`, and a hospitality proverb

The `bahay kubo` (bamboo-and-palm nipa hut) is named the iconic traditional Filipino home, evolving
under Spanish colonial influence into the sturdier `bahay na bato` (stone house) while retaining
shared design principles (blending with tropical climate, the Filipino communal concept of living
space). The note closes with an "old Tagalog saying" (paraphrased above in the vocabulary table)
contrasting a welcoming hut with an inhospitable palace — framed as expressing the culture's
people-over-property value system, directly resonant with `utang na loob` and the hospitality
ritual this same lesson's Culture Note 1 describes (unexpected guest arrives → host insists on
feeding them → guest declines once or twice per politeness norms → eventually accepts or firmly
declines with `pasyensiya ka na`).

### 25. 6,000 Spanish loanwords: assimilation types and native re-borrowing

A substantive culture note quantifies Tagalog's Spanish-loanword stock at roughly 6,000 words,
distinguishing unchanged-meaning borrowings (`pero`, "but"; `oras`, from Spanish `horas`, "hours")
from meaning-shifted ones (`siyempre`, from `siempre` "always" → Tagalog "of course"; `siguro`, from
`seguro` "sure" → Tagalog "perhaps"). It also notes the reverse flow: the Royal Spanish Academy's
own dictionary lists Tagalog-derived loanwords in Spanish (`paipay` "fan," `dalaga` "young lady,"
`bata` "child," `timba` "pail," `baba(y)dor` "womanizer"). Crucially, it states `gusto`/`puwede`
(Spanish-derived) are used *more* in everyday spoken Tagalog than their native-origin synonyms
(`nais`/`ibig`; `maaari`), directly reinforcing this lesson's and `008b`'s independent observations
about `gusto`/`maaari` vs. `nais`/`ibig` register.

### 26. Vocabulary table showing roots alongside their inflected forms

A pedagogically distinctive feature of this lesson's vocabulary table: several entries are given as
`inflected-form (root form)` pairs (`gumagamit (root gamit)`, `gumawa (root gawa)`, `humanda (root
handa)`) — more transparently exposing root-and-affix structure to the learner than `008`'s
vocabulary-box format usually does.

### 27. `mag-` verbs: a second major verb class, aspect-paralleled to `-um-`

`mag-` verbs form their infinitive by simple prefixation (`salita`→`magsalita`, "to speak";
`handa`→`maghanda`, "to prepare"). Crucially, **in the completed and incomplete aspects, `mag-`
shifts to `nag-`** (`magsalita`→completed `nagsalita`→incomplete `nagsasalita`), while the
contemplated (future) aspect keeps `mag-` and reduplicates the root's first syllable
(`magsasalita`) — this is the same `mag-`/`nag-` tense-shift mechanism `008b` documents, confirmed
independently with a cleaner comparison table laid directly alongside the `-um-` paradigm.

### 28. `-um-` verb aspect formation, explicitly formalized

Contemplated aspect: reduplicate the root's first syllable (`balik`→`babalik`, "will go back";
note the `d`→`r`-type spelling adjustment flagged for `dating`→`darating`, "will come/arrive" —
actually a `d`-retention exception the source flags rather than explains further). Incomplete
aspect: infix `-um-` between the first consonant and vowel of the *contemplated* form
(`babalik`→`bumabalik`, "is going back"), or simply prefix `um-` if the root is vowel-initial. This
gives a fully explicit four-aspect paradigm (infinitive/completed/contemplated/incomplete) for
`-um-` verbs that generalizes and formalizes what `008b` covered empirically per-verb.

### 29. Telling time: Tagalog, Spanish, and English forms in free register variation

Tagalog speakers freely mix all three number-naming systems when telling time, with a clear
register pattern: **Spanish-derived forms are most common in everyday speech** (`ala-una`, "1:00";
cross-reference `008` Unit 5's independent Spanish-hour-naming observation), **native Tagalog forms
are reserved for formal contexts** (`ika-isa`), and **English numbers are also used directly** when
precision matters ("Five forty-two na"). Minutes attach to the Spanish hour-name directly in casual
speech (`alas-dose singko`, "12:05"), while the formal Tagalog register instead fronts the minutes
with `makalipas` ("past"): `limang minuto makalipas ang ika-anim ng umaga` ("6:05 a.m.," lit. "five
minutes past the sixth of the morning").

### 30. Reading exercise: two authentic Tagalog song lyrics

A folk song about a pilgrimage to Antipolo (naming a real waterfall, "Hi-hi-hinulugang Taktak," and
foods eaten there: mango, cashew, star fruit) and a children's icebreaker/action song (clap hands,
point at feet, stomp right/left foot, turn around and find someone new) are given as authentic
reading material modeling `-um-` and `mag-` verbs in natural song-register Tagalog, paraphrased
rather than fully reproduced per copyright discipline.

### 31. Culture note: the pre-colonial/circular vs. colonial/linear concept of time, and code-switching as its modern expression

A substantive culture note argues that pre-colonial Filipinos understood time as circular rather
than linear, and that Western hour/minute/second categories were a colonial-era import that
nonetheless took root. It frames the everyday vagueness Filipinos sometimes express about exact
time (`Maaga pa`, "it's still early"; `Tanghali na`, "it's late"; `Mag-aalas-sais`, "it's
going to be six") — and the free code-switching between Tagalog/Spanish/English number forms
documented in Grammar point #29 — as the modern linguistic residue of that older, more fluid time
concept, explicitly distinguishing this from the folk stereotype of "Filipino time" as mere
lateness: the source states it "can just as easily mean being as punctual as the Swiss."

### 32. `ma-` verbs: a third major verb class, with roots that are sometimes bare nouns

`ma-` verbs form their infinitive by prefixing `ma-` to the root (`kinig`→`makinig`, "to listen";
`nood`→`manood`, "to watch" — note the root-initial-consonant assimilation, `n`+`n`→`n`, parallel to
the `mang-` consonant-dropping rules in `008b`). Distinctively, several `ma-` roots are themselves
ordinary nouns (`pagod` "tiredness," `tulog` "sleep," `sira` "break/defect," `uhaw` "thirst,"
`gutom` "hunger") that only become verbs once a verbal affix attaches — the source flags this
explicitly as worth noticing. Aspect formation: completed and incomplete both shift `ma-` to `na-`
(paralleling `mag-`→`nag-`), while contemplated keeps `ma-` and reduplicates: `makinig`
(infinitive) → `nakinig` (completed) → `makikinig` (contemplated) → `nakikinig` (incomplete).

### 33. `alam` vs. `marunong`: knowing a fact vs. knowing how

Tagalog splits "to know" into two non-conjugating pseudo-verbs with different grammatical behavior:
`alam` (knowledge of a fact/information) takes an `ng`-pronoun for the knower (`Alam mo ang sagot?`,
"do you know the answer?"); `marunong` (knowing *how* to do something) takes an `ang`-pronoun, and
when describing a skill, the following verb takes its neutral/infinitive form with the linker
`-ng` attached to the `ang`-pronoun (`Marunong akong magsalita ng Ingles`, "I know how to speak
English"). Knowing a *person* is explicitly flagged as using yet a third, different verb, deferred
to a later lesson. Neither `alam` nor `marunong` conjugates for aspect at all.

### 34. Negating sentences without ANG pronouns: `hindi` simply fronts the sentence

Building on Lesson 1's ANG-pronoun negation, this source states the more general rule: for any
sentence *without* an ANG pronoun, `hindi` is simply placed at the very front (`Mahirap ang klase`
→ `Hindi mahirap ang klase`; `Kumain si Ben` → `Hindi kumain si Ben`).

### 35. Reading exercise: an authentic-register news report, and the Tagalog f/v→p/b phoneme rule

A paraphrased current-events-style news excerpt (a president ordering an investigation into illegal
logging) is given as reading practice, explicitly flagged by the source as a vehicle for noticing
Spanish/English loanwords — and, crucially, the source states a phonological rule not given
elsewhere in `008`/`008b`: **Tagalog has no native /f/ or /v/ phonemes; loanwords substitute /p/ for
/f/ and /b/ for /v/** (hence `trahedya` for "tragedy" is fine since it has neither, but a word like
English "invest" would surface with `b` for `v` in a fully Filipinized spelling). This is a
significant phonological fact for understanding how English/Spanish loanwords get nativized into
Tagalog's sound system.

### 36. Culture note: the Thomasites and American-era English education

A substantive historical culture note: in 1901, during the Philippines' sole status as a US colony,
a shipload of ~500 American teachers (dubbed "Thomasites," after their transport ship) arrived in
Manila Bay under the US policy of "Benevolent Assimilation," tasked with teaching English across a
war-ravaged, already highly multilingual population that still held linguistic loyalty to the
recently-defeated Spanish colonizers. The note credits this program with the Philippines' current
status as the world's third-largest English-speaking country and the continued American-style
structure of its education system — directly relevant context for the `008`/`008b`/`008c`-wide
pattern of extensive English-loanword integration and Taglish code-switching.

### 37. Unit 7: `balikbayan`, and Manila/jeepney cultural notes

Lesson 7's dialogue introduces `balikbayan` (a Filipino returning from life abroad to visit the
Philippines) — conceptually related to, but distinct from, the ongoing-OFW/`bagong bayani` theme of
Lesson 3. Culture Note 1 gives Manila's name etymology (`May-Nilad`, "has water lilies," referencing
a former Muslim settlement at the Pasig River's mouth) and restates the jeepney's American-army-jeep
origin, independently confirming `008` Unit 6's account.

### 38. Verbal focus: Tagalog's trigger/voice system, named explicitly as a linguistic concept

This is the clearest, most explicit statement of Tagalog's focus/trigger system found across all
three sources reviewed so far: the source names it outright as **"verbal focus"** and defines it as
marking **the thematic role of the sentence's subject** (the `ang`-marked noun phrase). It
introduces exactly two focus types at this stage: **actor focus** (the subject is the one
performing the action — all `-um-`/`mag-`/`mang-`/`ma-` verbs covered so far in Lessons 4-6 are
actor-focused) and **theme focus** (the subject is the thing acted upon — roughly, but not exactly,
equivalent to English passive voice; the source notes other focus types exist beyond these two,
reserved for later lessons). A clean minimal-pair triplet demonstrates the shift: `Kumain ako ng
hapunan` ("I ate dinner," actor focus, `ako`=actor/subject, `hapunan`=theme/`ng`-marked) → `Kinain
ko ang hapunan` ("I ate the dinner," theme focus, `hapunan`=subject/`ang`-marked, `ko`=actor/
`ng`-marked); likewise `Nagbasa ako ng libro`→`Binasa ko ang libro` ("I read the book"); `Pumara
ako ng taksi`→`Pinara ko ang taksi` ("I stopped the taxi"). This gives the project's clearest named
linguistic vocabulary yet for the trigger/focus mechanism `008b` documented empirically across many
units without ever naming it as such.

### 39. Theme focus and definiteness: a genuine grammatical constraint, not just emphasis

The source adds a precise rule beyond simply "theme focus = passive-like": **a definite theme
obligatorily requires a theme-focused verb.** "I read the book" (definite theme) *must* use
`binasa` (theme focus); "I read a book" (indefinite theme) uses `nagbasa` (actor focus) with `ng`.
Critically, this constraint runs only one direction — **actors can be definite regardless of which
focus the verb is in** (`Binasa ng bata ang libro`, "The child read the book," is theme-focused
with a definite `ng`-marked actor, `ang bata`). Beyond this hard rule, when either focus is
grammatical, the speaker's choice reflects *what they want to emphasize* in the sentence — and the
source stresses that **not every verb root can take every focus affix**; a root's own flexibility/
limitation must be learned per-root, not assumed universal.

### 40. The `-in` theme-focus affix: full paradigm and allomorphy

Infinitive: suffix `-in` to the root (`kain`→`kainin`), or `-hin` if the root ends in a vowel
(`sabi`→`sabihin`, `para`→`parahin`). Completed aspect: infix `-in-` before the root's first vowel
(`kainin`→`kinain`). Contemplated aspect: reduplicate the infinitive form's first syllable
(`kakainin`). Incomplete aspect: reduplicate the root's first syllable *and* infix `-in-` before
the first vowel (`kinakain`). A fuller worked table covers `kain`/`hanap`/`sabi`/`para`, and the
reading exercise's underlining task extends the list to `dalaw`/`gamit`/`alis`/`gising`/`dala`/
`puri`. Not every actor-focus verb root can take `-in` — the source flags two further theme-focus
affixes, `-an` and `i-`, as covering the remaining roots, deferred to a later lesson (directly
anticipating `008b`'s own `an`/`i` passive-verb units).

### 41. `puwede`/`maaari`: modal "can," register-split exactly like `gusto`/`nais`

Restates and reinforces `008`/`008b`'s independent findings: `puwede` (from Spanish `poder`) is the
default everyday-speech modal for ability/permission; `maaari` is reserved for formal speech and
writing. Both combine with the infinitive/neutral form of *any* verb regardless of its focus
(`Puwede akong mag-taksi`, "I can take a taxi"; `Maaari kayong sumulat`, "You [pl.] may write").

### 42. The question particle `ba`: placement rule and the `ka`/monosyllable exception

`ba` normally follows the sentence's very first word (`Amerikano ba siya?`, "Is he American?"), but
monosyllabic words — specifically the pronoun `ka` and particles like `na`/`pa` — must precede `ba`
rather than follow the first content word (`Hindi ka ba aalis?`, "Aren't you leaving?," not
*`Hindi ba ka aalis`). Negative questions place `ba` directly after `hindi`.

### 43. Reading exercise: an informal personal e-mail, modeling mixed actor/theme-focus verbs in natural prose

An e-mail from "Katrina" to a friend "Josie" about an upcoming visit (paraphrased, not reproduced at
length) is given as an authentic-register reading exercise, explicitly asking the learner to
identify and underline `-in`-affixed theme-focus verbs alongside `-um-`/`mag-` actor-focus verbs in
the same connected prose — reinforcing that real spoken/written Tagalog freely mixes both focus
types sentence by sentence depending on what's being emphasized, not as separate registers.

### 44. Culture note: `balikbayan` and the 2003 dual-citizenship law

`balikbayan` is analyzed as a compound of `balik` ("return") + `bayan` ("country/hometown"),
denoting a Filipino living abroad who returns for a (often short, emotionally significant) visit,
frequently bringing foreign-born children to connect them with their roots. The note adds a
concrete legal fact not found in `008`/`008b`: in 2003, the Philippine Congress passed a law
allowing former Filipinos who had become naturalized citizens of other countries, along with their
minor dependents, to regain Philippine citizenship — formal government recognition of exactly this
`balikbayan` phenomenon.

### 45. Unit 8 opening: telephone register and the `paki-` polite-request prefix

Lesson 8 ("Nandiyan ba si Mister Panganiban?") opens with an office telephone-call dialogue,
introducing `nandiyan` ("is/are there," a presence-marker distinct from the `nasaan`/`nasa` static-
location system), and `pakisabi`/`pakitanong` ("please tell"/"please ask") — confirming `008`'s
`paki-` polite-request prefix (first seen there in Unit 4) independently, with a cleaner explicit
label ("the prefix `paki-` is equivalent to 'please' in English," per this lesson's own
comprehension-practice answer key).

### 46. `maka-`: ability/opportunity affixation for actor-focused verbs

The source frames `maka-` as marking "both the ability and the opportunity to carry out the action"
— and separately notes it "can also be used to express accidental or involuntary actions over
which the actor perceives he or she has no control." This is the same affix `008b` documents as
"circumstantial" (framed there primarily around the involuntary/circumstance sense), confirming
both senses co-exist under one mechanism, just with different relative emphasis per source. Full
aspect paradigm on `usap` ("speak"): infinitive `makausap` → completed `nakausap` → contemplated
`makakausap` → incomplete `nakakausap`. Like other modal-adjacent forms, the bare infinitive cannot
stand alone — it requires a modal (`puwede`, `gusto`, `kailangan`) or negation.

### 47. `ma-`: a second ability-affixation strategy for actor-focused verbs

Alongside `maka-`, plain `ma-` affixation also marks ability on some actor-focused roots (`sabi`→
`masabi`, "to be able to say/tell"): infinitive `masabi` → completed `nasabi` → contemplated
`masasabi` → incomplete `nasasabi`. The source doesn't state a hard rule for which roots take
`maka-` vs. `ma-` for ability — another case, like theme-focus-affix selection (note #40), where
per-root memorization is required.

### 48. `kailangan`: an uninflected "need" pseudo-verb, structurally identical to `gusto`

`kailangan` ("need") never conjugates for aspect, behaving exactly like `gusto`: the needer takes
an `ng`-pronoun (full paradigm `kailangan ko`/`mo`/`niya`/`natin`/`namin`/`ninyo`/`nila` given), and
what's needed takes `ng` if indefinite or `ang` if definite (`Kailangan ko ng telepono`, "I need a
phone," lit. "a phone is necessary to me"; `Kailangan natin ang telepono`, "We need the
telephone"). Distinctively, `kailangan` can also express a *person's* being needed, in which case
that person takes an `ang`-pronoun (`Kailangan ako sa bahay`, "I am needed at home").

### 49. `paki-`: restricted to `mo`/`ninyo`, roughly "please + imperative"

Building on `008` Unit 4/`008b`'s independent attestations, this source adds an explicit syntactic
constraint: `paki-` combines *only* with the `ng`-pronouns `mo` (you, sg.) and `ninyo` (you, pl.) —
it cannot combine with any other actor, since it is inherently a second-person request form
(`Pakigawa mo 'yung report`, "please do the report").

### 50. Linking conjunctions: `na` ("that") and `kung` ("if"/"whether")

`na` functions as a general subordinating linker equivalent to English "that" (`Pakisabi sa kaniya
na kailangan ko siyang makausap`, "please tell him *that* I need to speak with him"). `kung` serves
two related functions: joining a clause to a question word to form an embedded/indirect question
("whether/when/where" — `Hindi ko alam kung kailan ka darating`, "I don't know *when* you will
come"), and, when not completing another clause, standing alone meaning "if" (`Kung darating ka
bukas, magluluto ako`, "If you arrive tomorrow, I'll cook").

### 52. Culture note: layered origins of Filipino names

A substantive culture note on Filipino naming conventions: Spanish surnames are common (reflecting
both Spanish ancestry and a colonial-era policy alphabetically assigning Spanish surnames by town to
facilitate tax collection); the stock "everyman" name `Juan De la Cruz` (parallel to English "John
Doe") is explicitly named, traced to Philip II (heir to the Spanish throne when colonization began);
a significant number of surnames are Chinese in origin, romanized during the colonial period
(`Tiangco`, `Cojuangco`, `Uytengsu`); and genuinely indigenous Filipino surnames also survive (`Acop`,
`Balatbat`, `Macaraeg`, `Panganiban` — matching this lesson's own dialogue character, `Mister
Panganiban` — `Silayan`), drawn not just from Tagalog but also other indigenous languages like
Ilocano and Cebuano. Given names are described as historically drawn from the Catholic calendar and
later from Hollywood, with genuinely indigenous given names (`Lakandula`, `Diwata`) now comparatively
rare.

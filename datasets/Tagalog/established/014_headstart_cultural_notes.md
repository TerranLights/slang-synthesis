## Grammar points

**This source contains no grammar instruction** — no dialogues, drills, or verb-paradigm
exposition of any kind, consistent with its nature as a cultural/background-notes booklet rather
than a language lesson. No entry above carries a trigger/focus-category tag for this reason (there
are no inflected Tagalog verb forms quoted anywhere in the source — see the file-header note).
What follows instead is the source's genuine sociolinguistic/analytical content, paraphrased:

### Language ecology: an 8-language, 3-official-language system
The source states the Philippines has 8 major indigenous languages (all Malayo-Polynesian), each
with its own dialects, some mutually unintelligible even within the same language. Three languages
hold official status: Pilipino (Tagalog-based, instituted 1939, spoken by 55% of the population and
the primary medium of school instruction), English (spoken by 45%, "the most useful tongue for
formal communication," used by professionals/academics/government/business), and Spanish (spoken by
under one million people, "traditionally the language of the upper-middle class," now largely
confined to the social elite). This is a clean, source-stated functional/register tri-split — formal
elite (Spanish) vs. formal-professional (English) vs. national-vernacular (Pilipino) — directly
relevant to later register-tier mechanics analysis. (p.9)

### Pilipino vs. Filipino: the P/F alternation
Not discussed explicitly by the source as a phonological rule, but observable directly from its own
usage: the *language* is consistently spelled/named **Pilipino**, while the *people/nationality/
adjective* is consistently spelled **Filipino**/**Filipina** throughout the same document. This
matches the independently well-known fact that native Tagalog phonology lacks a native /f/ phoneme
(the main course's own Wave-1 vocabulary never contains word-initial Tagalog **f**-), so the
Spanish-derived national name surfaces natively as *Pilipino* while the Spanish/English-mediated
form *Filipino* persists for the demonym. Worth flagging for Phase 3 mechanics work as a visible
f~p alternation conditioned by borrowing route rather than free variation.

### Reduplication as a productive pattern outside verb morphology
The main course (010 series) already documented full-root reduplication as an intensification
strategy on weather adjectives (`mainit` → `mainit na mainit`) and on verbs/stative predicates
(`gustung-gusto`). This cultural-notes booklet independently turns up the **same reduplication
pattern doing different semantic work** on plain nouns and adjectives: `sari-sari` ("assorted," from
`sari` "kind/variety"), `halo-halo` ("mix," from `halo` "mix"), `turo-turo` ("point-point," from
`turo` "point"), `kare-kare` (dish name), `lapu-lapu` (fish name), and the language name
`Waray-Waray`. None of these are verbal-affix forms, so they fall outside this project's
trigger/focus-tagging convention, but they corroborate that reduplication is a broadly productive
word-formation strategy in Tagalog well beyond verb aspect-marking — a pattern worth carrying
into Phase 3 slang-mechanics analysis (reduplication is cross-linguistically a common informal/
playful/intensifying register marker, and five independent attestations in one short cultural
text is a meaningfully high density).

### A linked politeness/face cluster: utang na loob, hiya, pakikisama, amor propio
The source devotes its entire "Courtesy and Custom" section (pp.14-15) to four named,
source-glossed concepts that function together as a single social-pragmatics system rather than
four unrelated vocabulary items: **utang na loob** (reciprocal debt-of-gratitude obligation) is the
economy that makes **pakikisama** (smooth group relations) possible; violating pakikisama (being
critical, sarcastic, or publicly embarrassing someone) threatens a person's **amor propio**
(personal honor/self-esteem, a Spanish-loan term used alongside the Tagalog ones); and the
emotional consequence of failing any of this is **hiya** (shame/loss of face) — which the source
calls a "strong insult" to accuse someone of lacking. This four-term cluster is a strong candidate
for a dedicated Phase 3 mechanics finding: Tagalog register and euphemism/insult formation likely
routes heavily through face/shame framing rather than (or in addition to) the taboo-inversion or
clipping patterns already documented in other languages.

## Grammar points

### 1. Pseudo-verbs: a closed class of "action words" that never conjugate

Unit 11 (continuing from `008`'s note #59) lays out the full pseudo-verb inventory and a usage
table distinguishing near-synonyms: `kailangan` connotes *internal* necessity/need vs. `dapat`'s
*external* necessity/appropriateness (`Kailangan matalino ang estudyante` = "the student feels the
need to be intelligent" vs. `Dapat matalino ang estudyante` = "the student is required to be
intelligent"); `maaari` is common in both formal and informal registers while `puwede` (its
near-synonym) is informal-only; and `gusto`/`ibig`/`nais` form a formality ladder (`gusto` most
common and casual, `ibig` more formal and more common in writing than speech, `nais` the most
formal, rare in ordinary conversation) with `ayaw` as the shared negative counterpart of all three
(p. 150-151).

### 2. Pseudo-verb sentence formula: actor (`ng`-form) + definite (`ang`) or indefinite (`ng`) object

Pseudo-verbs obligatorily take a `ng`-form actor (`ni`/`nina`/`ko`/`mo`/`niya`/`namin`/`natin`/
`ninyo`/`nila`/`nito`/`niyan`/`noon`/`ng`) — notably, the source does not require this actor to be
translated as a possessive in English despite its `ng`-marking resemblance (`Ayaw ni Judy ng hilaw
na mangga` translates as "Judy doesn't like unripe mangoes," not literally "of Judy"). The object
then follows the same definite/indefinite split used throughout the course: `ang` for a definite
object, `ng` for an indefinite one (p. 154-156).

### 3. More on `sa`: with adjectives, not just place/time

A third use of `sa` (beyond Unit 10's place/time preposition) pairs it with a describing word:
`Mabait sa akin si Cora` ("Cora is nice to me"), `Mapagmahal si Jonathan sa mga kapatid niya`
("Jonathan is affectionate to his siblings"). In this use `sa` translates variously as "to, for,
on, in, into, about, at" depending on context (p. 160-161).

### 4. One-minute idiom box (Unit 11)

`matigas ang ulo` (lit. "the head is hard" = stubborn/hardheaded), `hindi yata pupuwede` ("I don't
think it'll work"), `pagbutihin mo` (lit. "make it good" = an encouragement to do one's best, e.g.
to a student), `lakad-takbo` (lit. reduplicated "walk-run" = a brisk half-walking half-running
pace), and `mabilis pa sa alas-singko` (lit. "faster than five o'clock" — explicitly historically
dated by the source to 1950s-60s Manila dockworker culture, where the 5 p.m. port whistle marked
shift's end and finishing early marked a worker as notably fast) (p. 161-162).

### 5. Cultural tip: shop-assistant shyness

A paraphrased note that Filipino shop/salon staff may seem hesitant to approach foreign customers
due to self-consciousness about their own English, not indifference — the source recommends a
warm smile and a few Tagalog words to put them at ease (p. 162).

### 6. Unit 12 opening: fitting-room register (`bagay`, `maluwang`, `masikip`)

Unit 12 ("Bagay Ba Sa Akin?", Does This Suit Me?) opens in a department-store changing room,
introducing `bagay` ("suited/looks good on"), and the fit-adjective pair `maluwang`/`masikip`
("loose"/"tight").

### 7. Active/passive voice and transitive/intransitive verbs (framing concepts)

Before introducing real Tagalog verb morphology, the source establishes the English grammatical
concepts it will map onto: active vs. passive voice (which participant the subject names), and
transitive (takes a direct object, `bumili si Roy ng sombrero`) vs. intransitive (`namili si Mary`,
no object) verbs — flagged as necessary scaffolding for the `-um-` verb system that follows (p.
165-166).

### 8. The `-um-` verb: Tagalog's first major active-verb-forming mechanism

`-um-` forms transitive and intransitive active verbs from a bare root in one of two ways: prefixed
whole (`um` + alis → `umalis`, "to leave") when the root is vowel-initial, or infixed after the
first consonant (`b` + `um` + `ili` → `bumili`, "to buy") when the root is consonant-initial. Four
tenses are built systematically from the root: **infinitive** (`umalis`/`bumili` — used only in
commands, suggestions, requests), **future** (`aalis`/`bibili` — reduplicates the root's first
syllable, no `-um-`), **present** (`umaalis`/`bumibili` — `-um-` + reduplicated first syllable +
root), **past** (`umalis`/`bumili` — identical in form to the infinitive, distinguished only by
context/use). This infinitive/past syncretism is explicitly flagged by the source as a point worth
noticing (p. 166-169).

### 9. Sentence-expansion formula: verb + actor + object + place + time

Tagalog sentences build up systematically by appending elements in a fixed order: `Kumain si Louie`
("Louie ate") → `+ ng sorbetes` ("Louie ate ice cream") → `+ sa restawran` ("...at the restaurant")
→ `+ kahapon` ("...yesterday"), each addition using machinery already introduced (`ng` for
indefinite object, `sa` for place) (p. 168).

### 10. "Recent action": `ka-` + future-tense stem, always paired with `lang`

A fifth verb aspect beyond the four tenses: prefixing `ka-` to the *future*-tense form of a verb
expresses an action that just/only very recently happened (`ka` + `aalis` = `kaaalis`, "to have
just left"). The recent-action form always takes a `ng`-form actor and is conventionally paired
with the limiting particle `lang` ("only/just") to underscore recency: `Kaaalis lang ni Stephen at
ni Amy`, "Stephen and Amy have only just left" (p. 169).

### 11. One-minute idiom box continuity note

No idiom box was found in the pages reviewed for Unit 12's middle section (pp. 170-174) — the
dressmaker/tailor dialogue and verbalized-adjective grammar section run directly into Dialogue 3
without one; see note 14 below for Unit 12's own idiom box, located at its very end.

### 12. Verbalizing `ma-` adjectives: "to become ___"

Most `ma-`-prefixed adjectives can become active verbs meaning "to become [that quality]" by
dropping `ma-` and applying the same `-um-` infixation mechanism as ordinary verbs:
`maikli` ("short") → drop `ma-` → `ikli` → `umikli` ("to become shorter"). Vowel-initial stems take
`um` as a full prefix (`masama` ["bad"] → `sama` → `sumama`, "to become worse/bad"); consonant-
initial stems take `-um-` infixed after the first consonant (`maganda` → `ganda` → `gumanda`, "to
become beautiful," with present tense `gumaganda` reduplicating the root's first syllable) (p.
172-174).

### 13. Verbalizing acts of nature

A closed set of weather/natural-phenomenon nouns verbalize the same way: `araw` ("sun") →
`umaraw` ("to shine," sun only); `kidlat` ("lightning") → `kumidlat` ("to flash"); and so on for
`ulan`/`kulog`/`sikat`/`lubog`/`bagyo`/`hangin`/`ambon`/`lindol`. The source notes these take tenses
by the identical `-um-` mechanism (present tense reduplicates the first syllable: `bumabagyo`,
"there is currently a typhoon") (p. 176-177).

### 14. One-minute idiom box (Unit 12, end)

Located at pp. 179-180, after the weather-forecast exercises: `ilista sa tubig` (lit. "to list in
water" = to conveniently forget something, commonly a debt), `di-mahulugang karayom` (lit. "cannot

"the sun will also rise," explicitly labeled a `kasabihan`/saying = "every cloud has a silver
lining"), and `isukat mo nga` ("why don't you try it on?"). `bahag-hari` ("rainbow," lit. "the

in its own right.

### 16. Unit 13 opening: ticket/travel register, `mag-`/`ma-` verbs previewed

Unit 13 ("Sa Istasyon ng Bus," At the Bus Station) opens with a ticket-office dialogue (`balikan`
vs. `pang-isa`, return vs. single ticket) and previews the unit's grammar focus on `mag-` and `ma-`
verbs. Public-transport-register colloquialisms appear in the vocabulary box: `umangkas` ("to hitch
a lift"), `mama` (the customary address term a passenger uses to flag down a driver/conductor), and
`siksikan` ("packed to capacity").

### 17. Dates: `ika-` + cardinal number + `ng` + month

Tagalog dates use the same `ika-` ordinal-forming prefix already seen for ordinal numbers (Unit 4):
`ika-apat ng Disyembre` = "December 4" (lit. "the-fourth of December"). Days of the week are mostly
direct Spanish borrowings (`Lunes`, `Martes`, `Miyerkoles`, `Huwebes`, `Biyernes`, `Sabado`) except
`Linggo` ("Sunday," literally also the word for "week"); months are fully Spanish-derived (`Enero`
through `Disyembre`) (p. 184-185).

### 18. `mag-` verbs: Tagalog's most versatile verb-forming prefix

Most Tagalog verbs are `mag-` verbs. Unlike `-um-`, `mag-` is always a simple prefix (never
infixed), and the source enumerates eight distinct semantic patterns it covers from a single
mechanism: 1) `mag-` + a mode of transport (`magbus`, "to go by bus"); 2) `mag-` + a profession
noun = "to become/work as that profession" (`mag-abogado`, "to become a lawyer"); 3) `mag-` + an
article of clothing = "to wear it" (`magkamiseta`, "to wear a T-shirt"); 4) `mag-` + a language
name = "to speak it" (`magTagalog`); 5) `mag-` + an item = "to produce/bear it" (`magbunga`, "to
bear fruit"); 6) `mag-` + a noun denoting an action involving 2+ actors = reciprocal action
(`magsalubong`, "to meet/greet one another"); 7) `mag-` + an adjective = an adjectival-state verb
(`magmabagal`, "to be slow," `magmayaman`, "to pretend to be rich" — note the sarcastic/pretense
undertone the example surfaces); 8) `mag-` + a time-of-day noun = a state-of-time verb
(`magmadaling-araw`, "to be dawn") (p. 187).

### 19. `mag-` verb tenses: `mag-` becomes `nag-` outside the infinitive/future

Unlike `-um-`, the `mag-` verb's prefix itself changes by tense: infinitive and future both keep
`mag-` (future also reduplicates the root's first syllable: `maglaba`→`maglalaba`), while present
and past both shift the prefix to `nag-` (present also reduplicates: `naglalaba` vs. past
`naglaba`). The "recent action" aspect (Unit 12, note 10) similarly becomes `kapag-` rather than
`ka-` for `mag-` verbs: `kapaglalaba` (p. 187, 190-191).

### 20. `ma-` verbs: a closed, unproductive 5-member class

Unlike `-um-` and `mag-`, which are productive, `ma-` verbs are a fixed closed class the source
says must simply be memorized rather than derived: `maligo` (bathe), `matulog` (sleep), `maupo`
(sit), `makinig` (listen), `mahiga` (lie down) — all intransitive. Despite the small, closed
membership, they conjugate by their own distinct mechanism: infinitive keeps `ma-`
(`maligo`), future infixes `-li-` after `ma-` (`maliligo`), present infixes `-li-` into the
already-`na-`-shifted form (`naliligo`), past drops to bare `na-` + root (`naligo`) (p. 193).

### 21. `maka-`/`makapag-`: circumstantial/involuntary "ability" verbs — a cultural concept

This is explicitly framed by the source as a window into Filipino cultural belief: some actions are
understood as happening "by circumstance" — by accident, or due to factors beyond the actor's
control — rather than by deliberate will, and Tagalog marks this with a dedicated verb form rather
than leaving it to context. `maka-` attaches to `-um-`-class roots (`maka-` + `alis` = `makaalis`,
"to be able to/manage to/happen to/come to leave" — the source gives all four English glosses as
equally valid depending on context); `makapag-` attaches to `mag-`-class roots (`makapag-` + `luto`
= `makapagluto`, "to be able to/manage to/happen to/come to cook"). Both conjugate via `ka`
infixation parallel to their base-verb tense system (`makaalis`→`makakaalis`[future]→
`nakakaalis`[present]→`nakaalis`[past]; `makapagluto`→`makakapagluto`[future]→
`nakakapagluto`[present]→`nakapagluto`[past]) (p. 197-199).

### 22. One-minute idiom box (Unit 13)

Two related invitation-register idioms given as a productive template: `magmeryenda muna tayo`
("let's have a snack first" — the source flags that stressing "first" in speech makes the
invitation sound impolite) and the explicitly generalizable `mag-___ muna tayo` pattern
(`mag-beer muna tayo`/`mag-kape muna tayo` = "shall we have a beer/coffee?"), alongside the polite
decline `sa ibang araw na` ("some other day/next time"), `medyo tagilid` ("the dice are loaded," of
a rigged or foregone result — illustrated with an election prediction), and `may panahon ka ba?`
("do you have a moment?" — the source explicitly flags the `panahon` "weather"/"time" ambiguity as
a common point of learner confusion) (p. 199-200).

### 23. Cultural tip: snack culture on long trips

A paraphrased note that Filipinos eat frequent smaller snacks rather than three large meals, and
that bus stations/jeepney stops reliably have hawkers selling food — travelers with Filipino
companions are advised to bring snacks of their own since companions may go hungry without
complaint rather than ask (p. 200).

### 24. Unit 14 opening: boat-hire frame, `mang` verbs previewed

Unit 14 ("Sa Tabing-Dagat," At the Beach) opens with a beach-resort boat-hire dialogue and previews
the unit's focus on `mang` verbs and particles. `mangingisda` ("fisherman") appears in the opening
dialogue as a concrete example of the `mang-` agentive-noun-forming pattern the grammar section
will formalize.

### 25. `mang-` verbs: pluralized action, plus two special connotations

The source frames `mang-` verbs as, broadly, "`-um-` verbs where the action has been pluralized" —
`bumili` ("bought," a one-off purchase) vs. `namili` ("shopped," an extended/repeated buying
process) is given as the paradigm case. Beyond pluralization, `mang-` carries two further special
connotations the source flags explicitly: (1) **deliberately harmful** action (`mangain`, "to
devour," vs. plain `kumain`, "to eat"; `manugat`, "to wound [deliberately]," vs. `sumugat`, "to
wound"), and (2) **activity directed at multiple objects**, or intensive/repeated activity toward a
single object (`manguha`, "to gather," vs. `kumuha`, "to get [one thing]"; `mangibig`, "to date/pay
court to," repeated courting of one person over time). Formation follows root-initial-sound rules:
`p`/`b`-initial roots take `mam-` (`pasyal`→`mamasyal`); `d`-initial roots take `man-` (`dukot`→
`mandukot`); `t`/`s`-initial roots take `man-` with the initial consonant dropped (`takot`→
`manakot`, `saksak`→`manaksak`); `k`-initial roots take `mang-` with the initial consonant dropped
(`kuha`→`manguha`); vowel-initial roots simply prefix `mang-` (`anak`→`manganak`, "to give birth
to") (p. 203-205).

### 26. `mang-` verb tenses

Tenses follow the same four-way system as the other active-verb classes, but built on the already-
transformed `mang-` stem rather than a bare root: `mangisda` (infinitive) → `mangingisda` (future,
reduplicating `ngi`) → `nangingisda` (present) → `nangisda` (past). The "recent action" aspect
again substitutes `pang-` for `mang-` the way `kapag-` substitutes for `mag-`: `kapapamalengke`,
"[has] just gone to the market" (p. 206-207).

### 27. Particles: small words that are grammatically load-bearing

`po`, `pa`, `naman`, and `na` are identified as a closed set of "particles" (the source's own term,
roughly equivalent to adverbs/discourse markers) that sit sandwiched between a sentence's Comment
and Subject (`Superbisor si Edith` → `Superbisor na pala si Edith`, "So, Edith is already a
supervisor!"). Multiple particles can stack in one sentence (the source notes up to 4-5 is
possible), ordered shortest-first when stacking two together — but beyond that the source candidly
recommends just asking a native speaker and memorizing useful combinations rather than trying to
derive a complete ordering rule (p. 208).

### 28. `na` ("already/now") vs. `pa` ("still/yet")

`na` marks immediate performance or the "already" sense of an action (`bumili ka na`, "buy now";
`arkitekto na si Manny`, "Manny is already an architect"). `pa` marks continuation or resumption
("still"): `tulog pa ba si Father Fred?`, "is Father Fred still asleep?"; `naglalaba pa ako`, "I'm
still doing the laundry." The two are frequently used as a contrastive minimal pair across a single
scene (`Tulog pa si Erwin` / `Gising na si Erwin` — "Erwin is still asleep" / "Erwin is awake now")
(p. 208-210).

### 29. `makapang-`: circumstantial ability for `mang-` verbs

Completing the circumstantial-verb paradigm (Unit 13's `maka-`/`makapag-`), `mang-` verbs take
`makapang-` for the same "able to/manage to/happen to/come to" meaning: `makapang-` + `isda` =
`makapangisda` ("to be able to go fishing"). Tense formation again parallels the other
circumstantial classes: infinitive `makapamili` → future `makakapamili` → present `nakakapamili` →
past `nakapamili` (p. 213-215). This completes the full set of circumstantial/involuntary-ability
prefixes the course covers: `maka-` (on `-um-` roots), `makapag-` (on `mag-` roots), `makapang-`
(on `mang-` roots) — together framed by the source as reflecting a genuine Filipino cultural
distinction between deliberate and circumstance-driven action, not just a grammatical curiosity.

### 30. One-minute idiom box (Unit 14)

`sa madaling salita` (lit. "in quick words" = to cut a long story short), `pagka-alis na pagka-alis`
(lit. reduplicated "upon leaving, upon leaving" = immediately after leaving — a generalizable
reduplication-for-immediacy pattern, not fixed to this one verb), `mangyari pa` (lit. "happening
still" = "of course"), `masarap maliligo` (lit. "delicious to bathe/swim" — flagged by the source as
demonstrating that `masarap` covers pleasurable activity generally in Tagalog, not just food, unlike
English "delicious"), and `lipas na sa moda` (lit. "past the fashion" = out of fashion, illustrated
with wide collars and bell-bottoms) (p. 217).

### 31. Cultural tip: the beach picnic and harmony-seeking

A paraphrased note on beach outings as a popular Filipino relaxation activity, explicitly tied to
the cultural value of "harmony" (`pakikisama`-adjacent, though the source doesn't use that term
here) in relationships; practically, it recommends letting Filipino friends negotiate boat/house
rental prices, since foreign visitors are often quoted higher prices (p. 217).

### 32. Unit 15: introducing the passive voice and the `in` verb — Tagalog's object-trigger system

Unit 15 marks the course's pivot from active verbs (`-um-`/`mag-`/`mang-`, Units 12-14, all of
which place emphasis on the *actor*) to **passive verbs**, which place emphasis on the *object*
(goal) instead — this is effectively the course's first formal introduction to what linguists call
Tagalog's trigger/focus system, though the source uses "active"/"passive" terminology throughout
rather than "trigger." The first passive type is the **`in` verb**: suffix `-in` onto the root
(`kain` + `in` = `kainin`, "be eaten"). Critically, **active and passive verbs use different actor
pronoun sets**: active verbs take an `ang`-group actor (`si`/`sina`/`ako`/`ka`/`ikaw`/`siya`/`kami`/
`tayo`/`kayo`/`sila`/`ito`/`iyan`/`iyon`/`ang`), while passive verbs take a `ng`-group actor
(`ni`/`nina`/`ko`/`mo`/`niya`/`namin`/`natin`/`nila`/`nito`/`niyan`/`noon`/`ng`) — and the object
flips correspondingly (active: `ng`-object; passive: `ang`-object). The source gives a full
comparison table and the mnemonic "stick to this rule and you won't get lost" (p. 220-222).

### 33. `in` verb tenses

`kuha` ("get/take") → infinitive `kunin` (irregular — not simply `kuha`+`in`) → future `kukunin`
(reduplicates first syllable of the *stem*, not the infinitive) → present `kinukuha` → past
`kinuha`. A regular root like `alis` ("remove") shows the more transparent pattern: `alisin`
(infinitive) → `aalisin` (future) → `inaalis` (present) → `inalis` (past) — note the infix `-in-`
moves to the front of the stem in present/past rather than staying suffixed (p. 224).

### 34. Irregular `in`-infinitives: a short, specific list to memorize

The source flags five roots whose infinitive is not simple root+`-in`: `bili`→`bilhin` (not
*`bilhiin`), `sunod`→`sundin`, `kuha`→`kunin`, `dala`→`dalhin`, `sabi`→`sabihin` — presented as a
closed memorization list rather than a derivable sub-rule (p. 227).

### 35. Six semantic classes of `in` verbs

The source organizes `in` verbs into recognizable functional groups rather than treating them as
one undifferentiated class: (1) verbs whose object/goal is **radically/structurally changed**
by the action (`gawin`, `lutuin`, `tapusin`, `tadtarin`); (2) **directional** verbs implying motion
toward and contact with a goal (`panhikin`, `pasukin`, `pukulin`, `salpukin`, `sampalin`); (3) most
**cooking** terms (`saingin`, `lagain`, `prituhin`, `sangagin`, `putulin`, `hiwain`); (4) most
**cleaning** terms (`linisin`, `kuskusin`, `ayusin`); (5) **stative/experiencer** verbs describing a
state brought on in someone (`gulatin`, `takutin`, `hiyain`, `gutumin`, `biguin`); (6) two
actor-less, goal-only subtypes: **illness verbalization** (`lagnatin`, "to have a fever,"
`sipunin`, "to have a cold," `malaryahin`, "to have malaria") and **pest infestation**
(`anayin`/`langgamin`/`lamukin`/`surutin` = "to be infested with termites/ants/mosquitoes/
bedbugs") — both notable for having no actor at all, just the affected goal (p. 224-227).

### 36. The full particle table

Building on Unit 14's `na`/`pa`, the source now tables the complete common-particle inventory:
`naman` ("on the other hand"/"too"/"in fact"), `po` (respect toward an elder/superior), `ba`
(yes/no question marker — optional in casual "sophisticated Manila Tagalog," and strictly ordered
*after* `ka` as `ka ba` since the reverse order `ba ka` accidentally spells "cow," `baka` — the
source's own flagged joke, not fabricated here), `pala` ("so!", surprised realization), `nga`
("indeed, truly"), `raw`/`daw` ("according to them/apparently," with the same vowel/consonant-final
allomorphy as `rin`/`din`), `kaya` ("I wonder," idiomatic doubt), `yata` ("I think, maybe"),
`rin`/`din` ("also/too"), and `na naman` ("again"). When several particles stack, `ba` goes last
(`namili ka rin daw ba?`, "did they say you also went shopping?"), and a sentence's subject can be
dropped entirely when a rising question-intonation alone signals the question (p. 230-233).

### 37. One-minute idiom box (Unit 15)

`kunan mo ako` (lit. "take [something] from me" = take my picture), `ikaw ang masusunod` (lit.
"you're the one to be followed" = you're the boss/as you like it), `dahan-dahan ang pagsasalita`
(lit. reduplicated "slow-slow the talking" = please speak more slowly), `anuman ang mangyari` (lit.
"whatever the happening" = come what may), and `tumingin-tingin` (lit. reduplicated "to look" = to
have a look around) (p. 234).

### 38. Cultural tip: the `kumpare`/`kumare` godparent bond

A paraphrased note on `kumpare`/`kumare` ("co-father"/"co-mother") as a named fictive-kinship
mechanism Filipino culture uses to extend family-strength loyalty bonds beyond blood relations —
accepting a godparent invitation is explicitly framed as an honor that may also carry real
financial/social obligation, and the source notes declining can be socially just as complicated as
accepting (p. 234).

### 39. Unit 16 opening: polite-request register, `i-paki-` stacking

Unit 16 ("Maaari Bang Gawin Mo Ito?", Could I Ask a Favor of You?) opens with a favor-asking
dialogue at a private home, previewing the unit's focus on requests, commands, and instructions.
`ipakihulog mo` ("could you post this for me") stacks the instrumental/object-focus `i-` prefix
directly onto the already-familiar `paki-` polite-request prefix (first seen in Unit 4), showing
these two mechanisms compose.

### 40. The `i` verb: Tagalog's third passive/trigger type, object- and benefactive-focused

`i`-verbs are "object focused": `isinara ko ang bintana` ("the window was closed by me"/"I closed
the window") emphasizes the window, vs. the active-verb equivalent `nagsara ako ng bintana` ("I
closed a window"), which emphasizes the actor. Beyond plain object focus, many `i`-verbs are
**beneficiary** verbs — the action is done *for* someone, and that recipient is marked with an
`ang`-form word (`ibili`, "to buy for," `ikuha`, "to get for," `igawa`, "to make for," `isama`, "to
take along") — e.g. `ibinili ko si Eileen ng sapatos`, "I bought Eileen some shoes." `i`-verbs are
also directional, taking `sa` as a goal (`ibigay natin ito sa kanila`, "let's give this to them")
(p. 236-237).

### 41. `i`-verb tenses, and how `mag-`/active verbs' passive counterparts surface as `i`-verbs

Tense formation: infinitive `itapon` ("to be thrown") → future `itatapon` → present `itinatapon` →
past `itinapon` (the infix `-in-` moves to the front of the stem, same mechanism as `in`-verbs).
Separately, the source notes two functional subclasses: (1) `i`-verbs whose action affects the
**position** of the `ang`-marked object without restructuring it (`iakyat`, `ialok`, `itapon`,
`iuwi`) — these typically correspond to a `mag-` active counterpart; (2) **food-preparation**
`mag-` verbs specifically surface as `i`-verbs rather than `in`-verbs in their passive form
(`iluto`, `ihaw`, `iprito`, `ipaksiw`) — a notable exception to Unit 15's "most cooking terms are
`in`" generalization, since that rule applied to verbs whose *stem* begins as an `in`-type root,
while these are `mag`-type roots repurposed as `i`-verbs (p. 238-241).

### 42. `may`/`mayroon`/`marami`/`wala`: the active/passive-verb subject-presence rule

Building on Unit 2's `may`/`mayroon`/`wala`, the source adds a crucial refinement that depends on
which *kind* of verb follows: when the verb in a `may`/`mayroon`/`marami`/`wala` sentence is an
**active** verb (`-um-`/`mag-`/`mang-`), the sentence has **no subject at all** (`may
naglilinis dito araw-araw`, "someone cleans here every day" — nobody is named); but when the verb
is a **passive** verb (`in`/`i`/`an`), the sentence *does* have a subject (`may kinuha ako sa
kaniya`, "I took something from him/her" — `ako` is the actor-subject even though `may` itself is
indefinite). `wala` mirrors the same split for negation/absence. This is presented as a genuinely
subtle point the source dedicates a full reference table to (p. 244-246).

### 43. Requests, commands, and suggestions: `maaari bang`/`puwede bang` + infinitive

The source frames this as maximally simple: `maaari bang`/`puwede bang` plus the infinitive form of
*either* an active or a passive verb covers requests, commands, and suggestions alike (`maaari bang
maupo kayo?`, "would you mind sitting down?"; `puwede po bang huwag tayong manigarilyo?`, "would
you mind if we didn't smoke?") (p. 247).

### 44. One-minute idiom box (Unit 16)

`ikumusta mo ako` (lit. "say-hello-for you me" = say hello for me, to a third party),
`ipagpaumanhin po ninyo` (lit. "please make [something] less serious" = please excuse/bear with,
formal), `unti-untiin` (lit. reduplicated "a-little" = to do something little by little/gradually),
`labag sa batas` ("against the law"), and `pagdating na pagdating` (lit. reduplicated "upon
arrival, upon arrival" = immediately upon arrival — the same immediacy-reduplication pattern as
Unit 14's `pagka-alis na pagka-alis`) (p. 248-249).

### 45. Cultural tip: `utang na loob`, the debt-of-gratitude system

A paraphrased note naming `utang na loob` ("debt of [inner] gratitude") as a core Filipino
reciprocity value: favors received culturally obligate future repayment, strengthening relationship
ties; the source explicitly notes this value has been known to be exploited by local politicians
around election time, and warns visitors not to be surprised at receiving unsolicited favors meant
to create exactly this kind of social debt (p. 249). This directly illuminates Dialogue 2's earlier
line `malaki ang utang na loob ko sa inyo` ("I owe you a great debt of gratitude").

### 46. Unit 17: the `an` verb — Tagalog's fourth passive/trigger type, completing the system

Unit 17 ("Isang Kasalan," A Wedding) introduces the **`an` verb**, formed by suffixing `-an` to the
root (`hugas` + `an` = `hugasan`, "to be washed"). This completes the course's full passive/trigger
inventory alongside `in` (Unit 15) and `i` (Unit 16): with active `-um-`/`mag-`/`mang-` (Units
12-14), the course has now covered all seven of Tagalog's major trigger/focus categories, though
the source itself never uses that linguistic term, framing everything as "active" vs. three
flavors of "passive." The `an` verb's defining feature: the `ang`-marked goal is specifically the
**receiver/recipient** of the action — `binigyan ko si Alan ng mainit na kape` ("I gave Alan [a
cup of] hot coffee") identifies Alan as the receiver, distinct from `ibinigay` (`i`-verb), which
would focus the coffee itself. The source frames English "from, at, with, to" as the typical
translation-range for what `an` expresses, and notes the goal is treated as a kind of "space" being
acted upon (`winawalisan ni Patricia ang balkonahe`, "Patricia is sweeping [on] the porch") (p.
253).

### 47. `an` verb tenses, and the `an`→`han` allomorphy

Tenses: infinitive `punasan` ("to wipe/be wiped") → future `pupunasan` → present `pinupunasan` →
past `pinunasan`. In some roots `-an` becomes `-han` in the infinitive depending on whether the
stress on the second syllable is "hard" or "soft" (the source's own informal terminology) —
`tira`+`an` → `tirahan`/`tirhan`, `sama`+`han` → `samahan` — presented as a pattern to internalize
by ear from native speakers rather than a crisply statable rule (p. 254).

### 48. `in`/`i`/`an` roots can mean different things depending which passive type they take

A root is not simply assigned to one passive class — the same root can surface as `in`, `i`, or
`an` with a distinct resulting meaning: `kain` ("eat") → `kakainin` ("they will eat that") vs.
`kakainan` ("they will eat from that" — the container/source is focused); `alis` ("remove") →
`inalis` ("removed [the plate]") vs. `inalisan` ("[a plate] was removed from [the table]" — the
source/location is focused); `punit` ("tear") → `pinupunit` ("is tearing [the paper into pieces]")
vs. `pinupunitan` ("is tearing off from [the paper]"). This is presented as a nuance advanced
learners must track per-root rather than derive mechanically (p. 255).

### 49. Circumstantial forms for all three passive types: `ma-`/`mai-`/`ma-...-an`

Completing the circumstantial-verb system begun in Units 13-14 for active verbs, each passive type
gets its own circumstantial prefix: `in`-roots take `ma-` (`ma`+`kain`=`makain`, "to be able to/
manage to/happen to/come to be eaten"); `i`-roots take `mai-` (`mai`+`bigay`=`maibigay`); `an`-roots
take `ma-...-an` wrapped around the root (`ma`+`hugas`+`an`=`mahugasan`). All three conjugate by
the same `ka`-infixation-into-future/present/past mechanism already seen for active circumstantial
verbs (`makakain`[future]→`nakakain`[present]→`nakain`[past], etc.) (p. 259-261).

### 50. Reflexives: `sarili` and the stronger intensive `mismo`

`sarili` ("self/own") expresses a reflexive action done to or on behalf of oneself, combinable with
either a `ng`-group pronoun (`sarili ko`) or a `sa`-group pronoun + linker (`aking sarili`) —
interchangeably, with no hard rule for which to prefer (`sinaktan niya ang sarili niya`, "he hurt
himself"). `sarili` can also stand alone meaning "my/his/her own" (`sarili ko ito`, "this is my
own"). `mismo` is a stronger intensive reflexive ("-self," emphatic), taking `-ng` after a pronoun
(`mismong ako`, "I myself") or standing bare before one (`ako mismo`), and can modify a `sa`-form
place word too (`mismong sa Cubao siya nakatira`, "it's in Cubao itself that he/she lives") (p.
264-265).

### 51. Cultural tip and dialogue: Filipino wedding customs, decoded

Dialogue 3 (paraphrased, not reproduced at length) walks through several concrete Filipino wedding
customs as Bill (a foreign observer) has them explained to him by Pinky's uncle: pinning paper money
to the newlyweds' wedding clothes; the newlyweds performing the `mano` hand-kiss gesture to their
grandparents, who in turn whisper private advice to the bride and groom respectively; elders later
gifting money wrapped in a handkerchief as `puhunan` (start-up capital) for the new household; and a
race up the venue's stairs immediately after the ceremony, tied to a named Filipino saying
(`kasabihan`) that whichever spouse climbs up first will be "the boss" of the marriage. The
chapter's own cultural tip adds that being asked to be a wedding sponsor (like a baptism godparent,
Unit 15) is an honor carrying an ongoing advisory/support responsibility beyond the wedding day
itself (p. 262-263, 267).

### 52. One-minute idiom box (Unit 17)

A small-talk greeting/reply pair: `saan ang punta mo?` (lit. "where are you going?" — explicitly
not literal, functioning as a generic greeting/acknowledgment) answered with the equally
non-literal `diyan lang` ("just there"). Plus `wala nang oras`/`wala nang panahon` ("no time
left"), `maghintayan tayo` ("let's wait for one another," used when arranging to meet), and
`nag-uurong-sulong` (lit. reduplicated "moving backward, moving forward" = hesitating, unable to
decide) (p. 266-267).

### 53. Unit 18: the gerund — turning verbs into nouns with `pag-`, `ka-...-an`, and bare `ang`

Unit 18 ("Sa Isang Pistang Bayan," At a Town Fiesta) introduces Tagalog's nominalization mechanism
—called the **gerund**, i.e. a nominalized verb — via three distinct strategies. (1) **`pag-` +
verb root**, used especially in answer to "how" (`pag`+`kanta`=`pagkanta`, "singing"; `ang
pagsayaw ang paborito kong libangan`, "dancing is my favorite pastime"). (2) **`ka-` + verb root +
`-an`** (sandwiching the root), producing abstract-quality nouns (`ka`+`galing`+`an`=`kagalingan`,
"goodness"; `ka`+`ibig`+`an`=`kaibigan`, "friend" — note this is the same word independently
introduced as "friend" back in Unit 1, now revealed as transparently derived from `ibig`, "to
love/like"). The `-an` suffix here sometimes becomes `-han` for pronounceability
(`ka`+`ligaya`+`han`=`kaligayahan`, "happiness"), and when the verb stem ends in `d`, that `d`
becomes `r` (`tamad` "lazy" → `ka`+`tamad`+`an` surfaces as `katamaran`, "laziness"). (3) **Bare
`ang` before a verb (any tense)** nominalizes it as "the one who/the act of" (`ang nagsasalita`,
"the speaker"/"the person talking"; `ang naglalaba ko`, "my laundry person") (p. 271-272).

### 54. `maki-`/`makipag-`/`makipang-`: participational ("social") verbs

A dedicated verb-form family marks actions done *collectively* or requiring *others'*
participation — the source's own term is "participational" or "social" verbs. Since these are
actor-focused, they only take active-verb machinery: `maki-` corresponds to `-um-`/`ma-` source
verbs (`maki`+`bili`=`makibili`, "to buy with/join in buying" — tenses: `makikibili`[future]→
`nakikibili`[present]→`nakibili`[past]); `makipag-` corresponds to `mag-` source verbs
(`makipag`+`laro`=`makipaglaro`, "to play with"); `makipang-` corresponds to `mang-` source verbs,
with its own consonant-dropping rule (`makipang`+`balita`, dropping the root's initial `b`, surfaces
as `makipamalita`, "to share news with"). A socially important subtype specifically requests use of
*someone else's* facilities/resources (`makikain`, "to ask to share food," `makitawag`, "to request
to use someone's phone," `makisakay`, "to request a lift") — distinct from reciprocal/mutual-action
social verbs (`makipag-away`, "to fight with," `makipagkamay`, "to shake hands with"). When the
"doer" joins an activity another person already started, `sa` marks that prior-initiator
(`nag-swimming sila sa ilog; nakipagswimming ako sa kanila`, "they went swimming in the river; I
went swimming with them") (p. 277-280).

### 55. Cultural tip and dialogue: `bahala na`, Filipino fatalism about fiesta spending

Dialogue 2 (paraphrased) has Bill puzzling over Filipinos spending freely at the fiesta after
saving all year, sometimes even borrowing money "just to prepare" — Rudy's explanation names this
tendency explicitly as `bahala na` ("come what may"/"leave it to fate/destiny"), presented as a

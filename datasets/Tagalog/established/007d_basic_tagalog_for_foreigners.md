## Grammar points

### Lesson 21 — Expressing thoughts and actions: tila/sa akalà/sa palagáy, isip, and "to go"

Three words all gloss as English "to think" but occupy distinct grammatical slots and carry distinct
nuances: **tila** is a sentence-initial adverb implying the speaker's own direct perception ("it
seems," e.g. **Tila úulan**, "it seems it will rain"); **sa akalà** and **sa palagáy** are prepositional
phrases (**sa** + possessive pronoun/noun) meaning "to my thinking/opinion," used for assumptions that
the source explicitly notes **leave room for being proven wrong** — the book draws out a genuine
pragmatic distinction here: **Sa akalà ko ikáw ay matalíno** ("I thought you were intelligent")
functions as a politely-hedged retraction of a prior positive assumption, while **Akalà ko Amerikáno
siyá** ("I thought he is an American") flags an assumption now known to be false. **Sa** may be
dropped before **akalà**, replaced with **ang**, and **ay** may also be dropped, with no loss of
grammaticality — three separate optional-ellipsis rules stacking on one construction. **Isíp**
(active forms **umiísip**/**mag-isíp**, passive **isípin**) is reserved for genuine cognitive effort
("to do real thinking/ponder"), distinct in register from the opinion-framing tila/akalà/palagáy set.
**"To go"**: **pumuntá** is the direct, fully-paradigmed translation when the meaning is literally
"go to a place" (and can even mean "come," context-dependent); but English's auxiliary "go + verb"
construction ("went swimming," "will go to sleep") has **no direct Tagalog equivalent** — the source
states this outright — and is instead rendered by simply inflecting the *second* verb alone across its
own full tense paradigm (**lalangóy** "will go swimming," **matutúlog** "will go to sleep"), with
**pumuntá** entirely absent from the construction.

### Lesson 22 — Expressing emotions: mahál vs. íbig/gustó ("to love," "to like")

Tagalog draws a hard semantic-register line the source insists on explicitly: **mahalín** (root
**mahál**, literally "noble/expensive," extended to "dear/beloved") is the *only* correct verb for
filial, platonic, or patriotic love — parent-child, siblings, friends, one's country — while
**ibigin** (root **íbig**, covering a range from mere "whim/fondness" to full romantic/sensual love)
is reserved specifically for romantic love between a man and a woman. Using **ibigin** for a
parent-child relationship is explicitly marked **"improper"** by the source (not merely unusual), a
genuinely strong normative claim about register-appropriate vocabulary choice this project should flag
for later taboo/propriety-driven slang-mechanics comparison. A single, narrow exception carves out
**pag-íbig** (the noun form) for patriotic/poetic use specifically — the source cites this
precisely to the patriotic writings of national heroes José Rizal and Andrés Bonifacio, i.e. an
explicitly canonized literary-register exception to an otherwise strict semantic-field rule. **Gustó**
and **íbig** are presented as fully interchangeable for "to like," governed by possessive-pronoun
doers (ko/niyá/etc., continuing Lesson 17's unique íbig/gustó possessive-pronoun-governance rule) with
the usual ligature attachment; **gustó** can be fully reduplicated (**gustóng-gustó**) for intensive
"loves/wants very much," the same productive-reduplication-for-intensity mechanism already documented
for adjectives (Lesson 1 chunk) and comparative intensives (Lesson 11).

### Lesson 23 — Who, which, that: ligature-marked relative clauses

Tagalog has no dedicated relative pronoun lexeme — the same three ligatures from Lesson 7 (**-ng**/
**-g**/**na**) that mark ordinary noun-modifier relationships also mark a noun-plus-verb relative
clause, collapsing English's distinct relative pronouns who/which/that into one unified ligature
mechanism. As with ordinary modification, **word order is free** — the modifying verb phrase may
precede or follow its head noun with no change in meaning (**ang batàng kumakain** / **ang kumakaing
batà**, "the child who is eating"). With a proper name as the clause's head, the ligature **na** is
noted as **more common/natural** than attaching the ligature directly to the name itself (**Si Pedro
na umáawit** preferred over **Si Pedrong umáawit**, though the source confirms the latter is "not
wrong") — a genuine usage-frequency preference the source volunteers rather than a hard rule, worth
flagging as a soft register/euphony note similar to Lesson 8's possessive-pronoun-mixing preference.

### Lesson 24 — Saying please: paki- and maki- requestive verbs

A **paki-** verb is grammatically passive, patterned after -in- verbs (doer in possessive form:
niyá/mo/etc.), while a **maki-** verb is grammatically active, patterned after -um- verbs (doer in
nominative form: akó/siyá/ikáw/a name with si). Both have full tense paradigms, but **the imperative
form is overwhelmingly the one actually used** for requests per the source's framing. Formation:
**maki-** infinitive/imperative unchanged; past **naki-**; present **nakiki-** (reduplicating the
prefix's own second syllable — the same reduplication-targets-the-prefix pattern already seen with
maka-/stressed maká- in Lesson 18); future **makiki-**. **Paki-**: infinitive/imperative unchanged;
past **pinaki-**; present **pinakiki-**; future **pakiki-** — i.e. paki-'s past/present additionally
infixes **-in-** (marking its -in--parallel passive status) on top of the same ki-/kiki- reduplication
pattern. **The source states outright that Tagalog speakers prefer the passive paki- construction
over the active maki- one when making requests** — directly paralleling the identical stated
preference for -in- over -um-/mag- in ordinary statements (Lesson 15), suggesting passive/object-
trigger constructions are the pragmatically unmarked default across the whole trigger system, not
just a quirk of requests specifically. **Politeness interaction**: the particles **ngâ** ("indeed,"
affirming/emphasizing) and **namán** (here used purely to soften an imperative, a third distinct sense
beyond its "too/also" and general-emphasis uses from earlier lessons) can soften a request even
without a paki-/maki- verb present — but the source explicitly warns that doing so **toward an elder
or authority figure without also using paki-/maki-, pô, and plural-pronoun deference is considered
impolite** — i.e. ngâ/namán alone are a peer-register softening strategy, not a substitute for the
full elder-deference system established back in Lesson 3. A question-form request uses **maáarì** +
**ba** (with ligature -ng), answered affirmatively with **Opô, maáarì** (explicitly marked as the
polite-society-mandated form) or the plainer **Oô, maáarì**.

### Lesson 25 — Saán and nasaán ("where"), expanded

This lesson systematizes the saán/nasaán split first introduced as two items in Lesson 9's 14-word
question-word list: **saán** asks for the location *of an action* and is followed by a verb in any
tense (optionally with an intervening pronoun), answered by **sa** + place-name or by the three-way
deictic set **díto/díyan/doón** ("here" near speaker / "there" near addressee / "there" far from
both); **nasaán** asks for the location *of a person or thing* and is followed by a noun/pronoun
introduced by **si**/**ang** (plural **siná**/**ang mga**), answered by **nása** + place-name or by
the parallel deictic set **nárito/náriyan/nároón**. **This produces a fully systematic double
three-way deictic paradigm** (saán-series vs. nasaán-series, each with near-speaker/near-addressee/
far-from-both members) — a clean structural mirror of the itó/iyán/iyón demonstrative-pronoun system
from Lesson 6, and worth flagging as a recurring three-way (not two-way, as in English) spatial-deictic
pattern running through multiple grammatical subsystems of this language.

### Lesson 26 — The preposition sa (and nása, gáling/mulâ/búhat, hanggáng)

**Sa** is presented as doing the job of at least nine distinct English prepositions (in, to, from, for,
on, into, over, through, at) depending purely on context — the source explicitly volunteers that this
single-preposition versatility may be *why* Filipinos commonly struggle with English's many distinct,
non-interchangeable prepositions, a genuine cross-linguistic-interference observation stated outright
rather than left implicit. **Sa** converts to **nása** specifically to indicate a person/thing's
current location (answering a nasaán-question, continuing Lesson 25's split) and to **sa may** to
express vague/uncertain location ("near/about," explicitly for when the speaker is unsure). A closed
set of nine **sa + locational-noun** relator phrases (sa loób "inside," sa labás "outside," sa haráp
"in front," sa tabí "beside," sa itaás "above/up," sa gitnâ "in the middle," sa pagítan "between," sa
likurán "behind," sa ibabâ "below/downstairs") covers English's spatial-relator vocabulary via one
uniform template. For expressing *origin* ("from"), three free-variant prepositions exist — **gáling
sa**, **mulâ sa**, **búhat sa** — literally glossed by the source as doubling up to "from from" when
combined with **sa** itself, but **only mulâ sa and búhat sa (not gáling sa) can express "a native of/
belonging to a place"** — a genuine semantic restriction distinguishing near-synonyms, with the
alternative prefix **taga-** (taga-Maynila, "a native of Manila") given as a further, morphologically
distinct way of expressing the same native-of sense. For time-spans, **mulâ** and **búhat** (not
gáling) pair with **hanggáng** ("until") and the time-deictic **noón** ("then") in fixed combinations
(**mulâ noón hanggáng sa ngayón**, "since then until now") — continuing to Lesson 28's fuller temporal
system.

### Lesson 27 — The prepositions para sa and para kay (indirect objects)

Indirect objects are marked by **para sa**/**sa** (governing a possessive pronoun, optionally + ligature
-g + noun) or **para kay**/**kay** (governing a proper name directly) — **para sa/sa** mean "for" and
"to" respectively when paired with a pronoun-governed phrase, while **para kay/kay** mean the same when
paired with a named person, and the two sets are **not interchangeable** (a pronoun-type phrase cannot
use kay, and vice versa). **Plural named indirect objects** take **kiná** (not kay) and **para kiná**;
pronoun-governed phrases need no special plural since pronouns already inflect for number (para sa
kanilá, "for them"). The source separately notes an **informal, increasingly-heard blended usage**
(**sa kay**, **para sa kay**, **sa kiná**, **para sa kiná**) that technically double-marks the
preposition — flagged as acceptable informal practice rather than the prescriptively "correct" form,
a genuine formal/informal register split worth tracking. **A Tagalog sentence with an indirect object
need not contain a verb at all** — demonstrative-pronoun-based sentences like **Itó ay regalo ko para
sa iyó** ("This is my gift for you") carry an implied action without any verb present, and both word
orders (subject-first vs. predicate-first) are equally grammatical with no meaning change, continuing
the free-word-order pattern established since Lesson 5.

### Lesson 28 — Telling time

Tagalog time-telling reuses the **iká-** ordinal-number prefix from Lesson 13 wholesale — clock hours
are literally ordinal numbers ("the fourth [hour]," etc.), a clean case of one morphological device
serving double grammatical duty across two superficially distinct domains (counting position in a
sequence, and telling time) that the book itself doesn't explicitly connect back to Lesson 13 but is
transparently the same **iká-** + cardinal construction. **A parallel, fully-formed Spanish-loanword
time system coexists** (a la una, a las dos...) and the source notes it **"still has a wider use among
Tagalogs and non-Tagalogs"** than the native Tagalog system — an explicit, source-stated claim about a
loanword paradigm being more frequent in actual use than the "native" equivalent it nominally parallels,
a significant fact for later borrowing/register mechanics analysis. **Half-hours and minutes** are
expressed with **at kalahatî** ("and a half") or **at [N] na minuto** ("and N minutes"), mirroring the
Spanish system's "y media"/"y [N]" construction structurally. **Written abbreviations** n.u./n.h./n.t./
n.g. (for ng umaga/hápon/tanghálì/gabí) are explicitly marked by the source as "most common in writing"
— i.e. a register-specific convention distinguishing formal/written time notation from spoken forms.
**A full three-tense temporal-deictic vocabulary** is laid out: present (ngayón, ngayóng áraw, ngayóng
umaga), past (kanína, kahápon, kagabí, kamakalawá, noóng áraw, noóng + day/period), future (mámayâ,
bukas, samakalawá, sa + day/period, sa susúnod na + day/period) — with an explicit incompatibility rule
that **noón(g) cannot combine with the more recent kahápon/kagabí** (you can't say "then yesterday"),
though **noóng isáng hápon/gabí** ("one afternoon/evening [in the past]") is fine — a fine-grained
recency-grading constraint on how far back a given past-marker can reach.

### Lesson 29 — The word "when": kailán, nang/noón, kung/kapág

Tagalog splits English "when" across (at least) three grammatically distinct words depending on
clause type: **kailán** is strictly interrogative (a true question word, recapping Lesson 9); **nang**
or **noón** mark a subordinate clause describing an action in the **indefinite/vague past** ("when I
was small..."); **kung** or its shortened colloquial form **pag** (full form **kapág**) mark a
subordinate clause describing a **present-habitual or future/conditional** action — and the source
explicitly notes this present/future "when" construction **genuinely overlaps with English "if"**
in meaning (**Kung áalis ka, sásáma akó sa iyó** translates equally well as "When you leave, I shall
go with you" or "If you leave, I shall go with you") — a real, source-acknowledged semantic
convergence between temporal and conditional subordination in this language, parallel to (though not
identical with) similar when/if convergences documented in various other languages' conditional
systems. **Kung** additionally combines productively with all 14 Lesson-9 question words to form
fixed "as to—" indirect-question phrases (**kung síno** "as to who," **kung paáno** "as to how," etc.)
— a closed, fully compositional paradigm, with English "as to" optionally omissible in the gloss.

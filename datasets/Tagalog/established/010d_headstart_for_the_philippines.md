**Morpheme breakdown.**

> **bumili** = -um- (Actor Trigger infix) + bili ("buy," root) — "to buy," focus on the buyer.
> **bilhin** = bili + -in (Object Trigger suffix) — "to buy," focus on the thing bought.
> **pagbilhan** = pag- + bili + -an (Benefactive Trigger circumfix) — "to sell to [someone]" /
> "you sell me." Three distinct trigger forms built on one root (`bili`), each foregrounding a
> different sentence argument (actor / object / beneficiary) as topic — the clearest three-way
> trigger minimal set found in this course so far, and the book states the Actor/Object contrast
> explicitly in its own words (p.125): "Bumili is used when the focus is on the buyer... Bilhin
> also means 'to buy'; however, the focus is on the object to be bought."

## Grammar points

### Linker allomorphy rule stated explicitly by the source
p.146 gives the book's most explicit single statement of the linker's conditioning environment:
`-ng` after a vowel-final word, `na` after a consonant-final word *except n*, and `-g` after a word
ending in `n` (e.g. `kamison` → `kamisong puti` "white slip"). This confirms and refines the
linker-allomorphy pattern inferred piecemeal from Modules I-III.

### Three-way demonstrative distance grading
`ito`/`iyan`/`iyon` form a three-term proximal/medial/distal demonstrative system ("this" / "that
near you" / "that over there," pluralizing as `ang mga ... ito/iyan/iyon`) — richer than English's
two-way this/that distinction. (p.150)

### Color intensification: reduplication vs. "mura" as opposite-direction shade modifiers
Full-root reduplication of a color term yields its "deep/dark" reading (`itim na itim` "jet black,"
`asul na asul` "dark blue"), while modifying the same root with `na mura` yields its "light" reading
(`asul na mura` "light blue," `dilaw na mura` "light yellow") — `mura` here is the same word
glossed elsewhere as "cheap"/"unripe," repurposed as a shade modifier. This is a distinct
color-specific intensification pattern, worth distinguishing from the general adjectival
reduplication-for-intensity strategy seen elsewhere (weather, taste, fit). (p.145)

### Bargaining discourse pattern
The souvenir-shopping dialogues model a conventionalized bargaining exchange: a buyer's "why so
expensive?" (`Bakit napakamahal?`) met with a seller's justification (quality/handmade claim), a
counter-offer, and the seller's formulaic refusal-with-concession ("add some more" / "no more
discount, that's already very cheap"). This is the clearest window in the course so far into a
genuine conversational-register speech routine with fixed phrases on both sides, worth flagging for
Phase 3 mechanics analysis of formulaic/routinized colloquial speech.

### Register note: regional ashtray synonym (titisan vs. abuhan)
The book's own footnote (p.158) flags `titisan` as a regionally-restricted synonym for the
default-taught `abuhan` ("ashtray") — a rare explicit source-marked regional-vocabulary annotation,
captured per the coverage rule's mandate to never skip these.

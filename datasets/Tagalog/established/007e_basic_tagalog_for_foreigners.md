## Grammar points

### Lesson 30 — Verb roots and their nominalization

A **verb root** is the bare, unaffixed base form (e.g. **áral**, **sábi**, **dalá**) from which tensed
verb derivatives (**nag-áral**, **sabihín**) and adjectives (**arál na tao** "educated person") are
built. **The same bare root, when preceded by ang/ang mga (or its possessive/locative counterparts ng/
sa), becomes a noun and can serve as a sentence subject** — e.g. **ang aral** "the study/lesson," **ang
sagót** "the answer," **ang sabi** "the statement," **ang alám** "what is known." This is presented as
a simple, general-purpose nominalization rule distinct from (and prerequisite to) Lesson 31's fuller
treatment of the same mechanism applied to *any* verb form (not just bare roots) and to adjectives.
Two root/derivative pairs are given as the source's own worked examples of a **lexicalized animacy
split**: **alám/málaman** ("to know," of facts/things) vs. **kilála/mákilala** ("to know," of persons)
— paralleling the lumà/matandâ ("old," inanimate/animate) adjective split documented back in the
Lesson 1-6 chunk, suggesting animacy-sensitive lexical splits recur as a structural feature across
multiple word classes in this language, not just adjectives.

### Lesson 31 — Forming nouns from verbs and adjectives (the ang/ng/sa nominalization rule, generalized)

Any verb (in any tense) or adjective can be converted into a noun simply by placing **ang**, **ng**, or
**sa** (or their plural forms **ang mga**/**ng mga**/**sa mga**) directly before it — yielding a meaning
along the lines of "the one who," "the thing that," "the one which," or "what is." This generalizes
Lesson 30's bare-root nominalization to the *entire* verbal paradigm (**ang nagsasalitâ** "the one who
is speaking," **ang natútulog** "the one sleeping," **ang binabása niyá** "what she is reading," **ang
mahihírap** "the poor [plural]"). **Two equally grammatical strategies exist when the referenced person/
thing could also be named explicitly**: a bare nominalized-verb subject (**Ang kumain ng manggá ay si
Jose**, "The one who ate the mango is Jose") or a fuller noun+ligature+verb relative-clause subject
(**Ang batàng kumain ng manggá ay si Jose**, "The child who ate the mango is Jose") — the source
explicitly states the **shorter, bare-nominalization form is preferred/more natural** whenever the
referent is already clear from context, flagging the longer form as technically correct but
"redundant." This is a clean, highly general word-class-conversion mechanism (verb/adjective → noun
via a bare function-word prefix, with no dedicated nominalizing affix needed) — a structurally
significant typological fact: Tagalog's major open word classes (verb, adjective, noun) are far less
sharply walled off from one another morphologically than in e.g. English, since the *same* lexical root
can surface as any of the three depending purely on which particle introduces it.

### Lesson 32 — I- verb forms (benefactive/instrumental object-trigger)

**I-** is a third object-trigger-marking affix (alongside -in-/-hin- from Lesson 15 and the upcoming -an
from Lesson 33), specializing in two closely related senses the source states explicitly: (1) doing
something **for the benefit of another person** (benefactive — **isulat mo ako** "you write for me"),
and (2) using something as an **instrument** to perform the root's action, or performing the action "on
or for" the subject (**ihulog ang sulat**, "post the mail for someone"). **Formation splits into two
regular allomorphy classes by root-initial sound**: consonant-initial roots (other than h/l/y/w) prefix
**i-** throughout and infix **-in-** in past/present (**sulat→isulat/isinulat/isinusulat/isusulat**);
vowel-initial roots and roots starting with **h, l, y, w** prefix **i-** throughout but take infix
**-ni-** instead of **-in-** in past/present (**hanap→ihanap/inihanap/inihahanap/ihahanap**) — the
**-in-/-ni-** split is the same place-of-articulation-conditioned allomorphy already documented for the
ordinary -in- object-trigger verb in Lesson 15, now shown recurring identically within the i- paradigm.
A **third formation path, ipag-**, substitutes for plain i- specifically when the active counterpart
verb is **mag-** rather than -um- (**sulat → mag-aral-type root takes ipagsulat**, infixing -in- after
the prefix's own first two letters rather than after the root). **Three distinct sentence patterns**
govern which argument becomes the i--sentence's subject: (1) when the active form is **-um-**, the
*indirect object* (the para sa/para kay/sa/kay-marked beneficiary) becomes subject; (2) when the active
form is **mag-**, the *direct object* becomes subject (the beneficiary stays marked as before); (3) when
a root has both -um- and mag- active forms with the same meaning, pattern (1) applies. **A closed set
of roots exist only as i- verbs with no corresromdonding -in-/-hin- form at all** — the source devotes
an explicit "Common Errors" table listing ten such roots (balitâ, húlog, taním, púnas, turò, tápon, walâ,
balík, búkas, sará) where learners might incorrectly produce a nonexistent **-in-** form (***balitain**,
***hulugin**, etc.) when only the **i-** form is grammatical — a genuinely important
"these two affixes are not freely interchangeable per-root, memorize which roots take which" finding
for later mechanics work on this trigger system's real productivity limits.

### Lesson 33 — -An verb forms (locative/descriptive object-trigger, and the full irregular-root set)

**-An** (with allomorph **-han** after a vowel-final root with no glottal catch, vs. plain **-an** after
a consonant-final root or a glottal-catch-final vowel root) is a fourth object-trigger affix with **three
distinct senses** the source enumerates explicitly: (1) forming a **place-noun** associated with the
root (**aklát** "book" → **aklátan** "library"; **gupít** "cut" → **gupítan** "barbershop/beauty salon"
— note this sense produces a genuine *noun*, not a verb, making -an the only trigger-affix in this
chunk that can derive either word class from the same template); (2) marking ordinary **passive/
object-trigger verbal action**, parallel to -in-/i- (**sulat→sulatan** "to write to/on"); (3) forming
descriptive adjectives of **size** (**dami** "many" → **damihán** "to increase the number") or
**quality** (**putik** "mud" → **putikán** "muddy"). **Formation**: consonant-final and glottal-catch
roots take **-an** directly, with infix **-in-** (not a prefix, unlike i-'s infix placement) marking
past/present; vowel-final non-glottal roots take **-han**, with the same -in- infix placed after the
root's first syllable's first letter. **A substantial closed class of irregular -an verbs drops the
root's second vowel** before suffixing (**lagay→lagyán**, not *lagayan*; **bigay→bigyán**, not
*bigayan*) — the source tabulates 17 such irregular roots side-by-side with their (ungrammatical)
regular-pattern counterparts in parentheses, and flags **hipan** ("to blow into/on," root **hihip**) as
the single most irregular case, dropping the root's *entire second syllable* rather than just a vowel.
**Three sentence-pattern rules govern which argument becomes subject**, closely paralleling i-'s own
three-pattern system from Lesson 32: (1) without a direct object, the indirect-object/beneficiary or
a sa-marked place becomes subject, with the doer demoted to possessive-marked (ni/ng); (2) with a
direct object, that direct object is marked ng (possessive) in the -an sentence while the *indirect*
object/beneficiary becomes subject; (3) when the active -um-/mag- verb simply has no -in- passive form
at all, the *direct* object itself becomes the -an sentence's subject directly (paralleling the
"i- is the only passive this root has" pattern already seen in Lesson 32). **A compound pa- + -in + -an
form is explicitly flagged in passing** (**pakinggán**, "to listen to," built on **pa- + dingig/root +
-an**) as distinct from a simple root+-an derivation — an explicit forward pointer to Lesson 36's
magpa-/pa- + -in system, confirming the trigger-affix system keeps compounding even further than the
five-ish affixes documented so far in this book (-um-, mag-, -in-, ma-, maka-, paki-, maki-, i-, ipag-,
-an, and now a pa- + -an combination).

## Grammar points

### Lesson 1, Unit 1 — The Tagalog Sound System (p. 1)

Tagalog has 27 phonemes: 16 consonants, 5 vowels, and 6 diphthongs. The original Tagalog alphabet
has only 20 letters (A, B, K, D, E, G, H, I, L, M, N, NGa, O, P, R, S, T, U, W, Y); the letters C,
F, J, Ñ, Q, V, X, Z are borrowed from English/Spanish and used mainly for proper names.

**Consonant inventory** (as the source lists it, paired with the vowel "a" per Tagalog's
syllable-naming convention): Ba, Ka, Da, Ga, Ha, La, Ma, Na, Nga, Pa, Ra, Sa, Ta, Wa, Ya, plus the
glottal stop (written `^` in the source).

**Vowels:** A (as in "alms/palm/far" — e.g. *aso* "dog," *ahas* "snake"), E (as in "end/pet/carpet"
— e.g. *letra* "letter," *hepe* "chief"), I (as in "nip/cliff/hit" — e.g. *lima* "five," *anim*
"six," *susi* "key"), O (as in "obey/horse/boy" — e.g. *oso* "bear," *baboy* "pig"), U (as in
"food/moon/soon" — e.g. *buhok* "hair," *pusa* "cat").

**Diphthongs:** the source illustrates two of the six — `IW` (e.g. *giliw* "affection, love,"
*aliw* "entertainment," *baliw* "crazy, foolish," *liwaliw* "pleasure trip, diversion") and `UY`
(e.g. *aruy!* "ouch!", *kasuy* "cashew," *uy!* "wow!", *tsampuy* "dried fruit"). Only these two
diphthong classes were visible on the sampled page; the remaining four are presumably covered on
adjacent, unsampled pages of the same unit.

### Lesson 1, Unit 2 — The Tagalog Sentence (p. 9)

A Tagalog sentence is composed of a **topic** and a **predicate** — not a subject and predicate in
the English sense. Key points, paraphrased:

- The topic frequently corresponds to an English sentence's subject, but with real differences:
  the Tagalog topic usually *follows* the predicate (reverse of English's subject-first order), the
  topic is the sentence's **focus** of attention and is always definite, while an English subject
  may be definite or indefinite. **Focus** is described as "the backbone of Tagalog grammar,"
  discussed throughout the course — consistent with the trigger/focus system being the central
  grammatical mechanism this project's Tagalog column convention already tracks.
- A Tagalog sentence can have only one topic.
- **Predicate categories** and what marks their topic: nouns/pronouns/adjectives/verbs in predicate
  position take an `ang`-marker (or Set I pronoun) topic; pronoun predicates take Set I pronoun
  topics; demonstrative predicates take Set I demonstrative topics.
- **Two word orders exist.** (a) TOPIC/SUBJECT + PREDICATE: mirrors English word order, used mainly
  in written form and mass-media communication; requires the copula **AY** to join subject and
  predicate. The source explicitly flags that **AY is not a linking verb** — "there are no linking
  or *be*-verbs in Tagalog" at all, a point worth remembering before assuming any English-style
  copula behavior for AY. (b) The reverse PREDICATE + TOPIC order (the natural/spoken-default order,
  per Unit 2's own framing) was not captured on the sampled page — presumably detailed immediately
  following the page read.

### Lessons 11-15 — Superlative adjectives with `ka-...-an/-han/-nan` (p. 21)

The course illustrates a **circumfix** (a single discontinuous affix, prefix `ka-` plus a suffix
chosen per root) that derives superlative adjectives ("the most/-est") from base adjective roots.
The source is explicit that **not every adjective takes this circumfix** — this is presented as a
closed, lexically-specific list rather than a fully productive rule applying to any adjective.

Sampled pairs (root adjective shown unmarked, circumfixed superlative form, English gloss):

Note the shared internal structure: `ka-` + reduplicated-looking root + `-an`/`-han`/`-nan` (the

*lumaan*, `-han` after a vowel-final stem like *tandaan* is actually `-an` here too on closer look,
*ilaliman* taking `-an` after an epenthetic sound). A fuller phonological rule for allomorph choice
was not stated on the sampled page itself. This is a genuinely productive, word-formation-level
pattern, worth comparing against `00_Word_Concept_and_Morphological_Typology_Guide.md`'s
morpheme-breakdown convention once the mechanics-analysis phase begins.

### Lessons 21-25 — `maki-`/`paki-` in polite requests (p. 7)

Per the course's own index (see below), this material corresponds to index entries "paki/maki in
polite requests, L21" and "simple social verbs, maki/makipag/makipang, L22." The sampled glossary
page lists a long series of `maki-`-prefixed and `paki-`-prefixed verb forms, each glossed in
English as "please + [verb]" — i.e. **`maki-` and `paki-` are productive politeness/request-marking
verbal prefixes**, not separate lexical items, directly relevant to this project's "trigger/focus
category" column convention (they interact with the social/actor-trigger `maki-` morphology already
tagged on several VocabuLearn verb entries, e.g. `makinig` "to listen/hear"). The source also notes
a register nuance: some `maki-`/`paki-` forms are glossed with two closely related meanings where
one variant is marked "(not used often; often used with **nga** only)" or "(more commonly used with
**nga** only)" — i.e. the particle **nga** can be required to make certain polite-request forms
sound natural, a genuine register/usage constraint explicitly marked by the source itself.

### Lesson 37 (within "Lessons 36-40") — Filipino body-part idiom table (p. 11)

**Flagship finding for this project's slang-mechanics focus.** The course devotes an entire lesson
(L37, indexed as "Filipino idioms, L37" / "Filipino sayings, L37") to idiomatic expressions, and the
sampled page is a table of **body-part idioms** — a productive metaphor domain mapping literal
body-part descriptions to figurative personality/state meanings. This is exactly the kind of
`metaphor_productivity` derivation-type evidence `00_Usage_Tier_Taxonomy.md` defines (a specific
semantic domain — here, the human body — becoming a productive source for figurative/idiomatic
meaning), and is directly transferable to synthesis work for this project's invented slang.

| Idiom | Literal translation | English equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| magsunog ng kilay | burn eyebrows | burn the midnight oil / study diligently |
| kumukulo ang tiyan | boiling stomach | hungry/starving |
| mahapdi ang bituka | painful intestine | hungry/starving |
| halang ang bituka | blocked intestine | a troublesome person |
| mabilis ang kamay | fast hand | one who is quick to use his hands or fist, as in abusive behavior |
| makati ang kamay | itchy hand | thief/sticky fingers |
| maputi ang tenga | white ears | stingy |
| bukas ang palad | open palms | generous |
| matigas ang buto | hard bones | strong/independent |
| matigas ang katawan | hard body | lazy |
| mabigat ang katawan | heavy body | sick/unwell |
| mahaba ang buntot | long tail | spoiled brat |
| makitid ang noo | narrow forehead | narrow-minded |
| may pilak sa buhok | silver on one's hair | old/elderly |
| kabunggua(ng) balikat | bump shoulders with | a person one often associates with |
| kisap-mata | blink/wink of an eye | in a flash/in an instant |
| makapal ang mukha | thick face | shameless |

| lumagay sa tahimik | go to a quiet place | get married |

| kabiyak ng puso | half of the heart | one's better half/spouse |

Observations worth flagging for the analysis phase: two independent idioms converge on "hungry/
starving" via different internal-organ imagery (*tiyan* "stomach" boiling vs. *bituka* "intestine"
aching), two independent idioms converge on "shameless" (thick face / no shame), and two
independent idioms converge on "spouse" (half of the chest / half of the heart) — the same kind of
**convergent redundancy within one productive metaphor domain** that this project's methodology has
flagged as a notable pattern in other languages' slang mechanics (e.g. Hungarian's convergent sound
change note). *Mahaba ang buntot* ("long tail" → "spoiled brat") and *makitid ang noo* ("narrow
forehead" → "narrow-minded") extend the body-part domain to include animal-adjacent body parts
(tail) and cognitive/figurative space (forehead-as-mind), suggesting the domain's productivity
isn't limited to strictly human anatomy.

### Lessons 36-40 — `pa-...-in` causative verb pairs (p. 46)

A causative verb-formation table pairing a base verb with its `pa-...-in`-circumfixed causative
counterpart, uniformly glossed "allow/ask/make/have someone [do base verb]" — i.e. `pa-...-in` is a
productive causative circumfix layered on top of an already-triggered base verb, consistent with
the course's own index entries "pa-in/hin/nin-verbs, double-doer, L40" and "pag-in/hin/nin-verbs...
L42" (the "double-doer" terminology referring to a causative construction with two distinct
doers/actors: the causer and the one caused to act).

|---|---|---|
| sumakay | pasakayin | allow/ask/make/have someone ride on a horse or a vehicle |
| lumakad | palakarin | allow/ask/make/have someone walk |

| sumulat | pasulatin | allow/ask/make/have someone write |
| dumating | paratingin | allow/ask/make/have someone come (arrive) |
| lumangoy | palanguyin | allow/ask/make/have someone swim |

*masaya*, *maligo*, *matulog*) feeding into the same uniform `pa-...-in` causative — i.e. the
causative circumfix applies productively across different base-trigger classes, not just one.

## Course-wide structural index (summary, not full extraction)

The course's own cumulative index (PDF pp. 740–744) was read in full to establish what topics the
entire 745-page, 45-lesson binder actually covers, without claiming this file extracted all of it.
Paraphrased structural summary (not a vocabulary table — this is a map of the book's own
organization): the course systematically works through the full Tagalog trigger/focus system
lesson-by-lesson — `um`-verbs, `mag`-verbs (including intransitive/transitive distinctions and a
noun-forming use of the `mag-` prefix), `mang`/`man`/`mam`-verbs, `i`-verbs, `in`/`hin`/`nin`-verbs,
`an`/`han`/`nan`-verbs (locative), `ma`-verbs (both doer-focus and object-focus), `maka`-/`makapag`-
able-verbs, `maki`/`makipag`/`makipang` social/reciprocal verbs, `mai`-verbs, `pa`-causatives
(`pa-an/han/nan`, `pa-in/hin/nin`, with "double-doer" causative constructions layered on `mag-`,
`pag-`, `papag-`), `nakaka`/`nakakapang` prefixes, pseudo/helping verbs (*gusto, ibig, nais, dapat,
ayaw, puwede, maaari, kailangan*), adjective comparison (equality/inequality/similarity/
dissimilarity, degrees, intensification, exclamatory and extreme-condition forms), noun formation
from adjectives/other nouns/English loanwords (including English *-ty*/*-tion* suffix adaptation),
enclitics (*ba, ho, na, din/rin, daw/raw, kaya, naman, pala, tuloy, pa, nga, kasi, man, muna, sana,
yata* — a notably rich enclitic-particle inventory, itself a likely rich vein for register/slang
mechanics), numbers/calendar/currency/time phrases, and — directly relevant to this project — "a
full lesson (L37) of Filipino idioms and sayings," only the body-part-idiom portion of which is
sampled above. **This index is itself a strong candidate for a future, more exhaustive extraction
pass** given how directly several of its entries (enclitics, idioms/sayings, the full trigger
paradigm) bear on slang mechanics — flagged here rather than pursued further, consistent with the
sampling scope of this dispatch.

# Tagalog — Established Vocabulary: VocabuLearn Tagalog, Level I

**Source:** *VocabuLearn Tagalog, Level I* (English/Tagalog), Penton Overseas, Inc., Carlsbad CA,
©1995. PDF: `11 VocabuLearn Tagalog Level 1.pdf`, all 23 PDF pages (printed pages 1–38 plus
cover/intro matter). This is an audio-course companion booklet (cassette-based vocabulary drills),
not a conventional grammar reference — no grammar explanation accompanies the word lists beyond a
short front-matter usage note (see Grammar points below).

**Coverage note.** Extracted in full, as instructed — this is a short (23pp), self-contained
vocabulary booklet, not a large reference requiring sampling. Every vocabulary pair printed in the
booklet's four cassette-side sections (Nouns; Adjectives/Adverbs/Prepositions/Conjunctions;
Expressions; Verbs) is captured below, in both the "forward" (English cue first) and "reverse"
(Tagalog cue first) halves of each cassette side — confirmed, by inspecting the actual word pairs,
that the forward and reverse halves of a given side are **complementary, not duplicate**, content:
each half presents a different set of words from the same topical pool (e.g. Nouns-forward covers
roughly pp. 1–5, Nouns-reverse covers pp. 5–10), so both halves had to be transcribed to get full
coverage, not just one. The two parallel-language Introduction sections (English, pp. i–ii; Tagalog
"Antas 1 Introduksyon," pp. 39–40) are boilerplate program-description prose with no vocabulary or
grammar content distinct from each other and are not tabulated.

**Vision-reading guard.** This source has no text layer at all (`pdftotext` returns empty output;
`pdfinfo` identifies the producer as "Acrobat 5.0 Image Conversion Plug-in," i.e. a page-image
scan) — every entry below was read visually from rendered page images. **Two instances of
handwritten marginalia were found and excluded**, consistent with this being a used/annotated
copy: (1) on the printed page containing "the street" (Nouns-forward), a previous owner had
pencilled "daán, kálye" as an alternate Tagalog word next to the printed "ang kalsada" — excluded,
not part of the source's own content; (2) on the printed page containing "the pupil," a previous
owner had pencilled "?nag-áaral" (with a question mark, suggesting the owner's own uncertain guess)
next to the printed "ang mag-aáral" — excluded for the same reason. Both exclusions are also noted
inline on their table rows below.

**Vision Reading Confidence default.** The print itself is clean, consistently typeset, and
unambiguous at the rendered resolution — no OCR-style garbling or scan artifacts were encountered.
However, because most individual word pairs in a drill list like this appear only once (no
repeated context to cross-check the fine stress-accent diacritics against), the default confidence
below is `plausible_unverified` rather than `verified`. A handful of terms that recur elsewhere in
the booklet (the same Tagalog word independently confirming its own spelling/accent in a second
context) are marked `verified` instead, with a note.

**Trigger/focus category convention (Tagalog-specific).** Per `00_Extraction_Checklist.md`, every
verbal-affix vocabulary entry below records its trigger/focus category in the Part of Speech
column. **Important caveat:** this source provides zero explicit grammatical discussion of the
Tagalog trigger (focus) system — it is a pure vocabulary-drill booklet. The trigger tags below are
therefore **this extractor's own morphological inference from surface affix shape** (um-/mag- →
Actor Trigger; -in/-hin suffix alone → Object/Patient Trigger; -an/-han suffix alone → Locative
Trigger; i- prefix → Conveyance/Benefactive Trigger; ma-/mapag- → stative/potentive Actor-adjacent
forms), cross-checked only informally against general Tagalog morphology, **not** against this
source's own (nonexistent) explanation or against a grammar reference. Treat these tags as a
reasonable first-pass classification to be verified later once a real Tagalog grammar reference
(e.g. *Tagalog Grammar*, item 13 in the source catalog) is extracted, not as source-asserted fact.

## Grammar points

This source is a pure vocabulary-drill booklet with no systematic grammar exposition — its entire
grammatical content is five short front-matter usage notes (English, pp. i–ii; parallel Tagalog
restatement, pp. 39–40) plus one explicit register annotation at the start of the Expressions
section. All are paraphrased below; none amount to a real grammar reference, which is why
`13 Tagalog Grammar.pdf` (catalog item 13) remains the project's actual target for systematic
Tagalog grammar extraction.

### Word-sense disambiguation convention

When a listed English word has multiple possible meanings, the booklet notes the intended sense
parenthetically rather than leaving it ambiguous — e.g. "the square (city)" (its own worked
example), or, as found in this extraction, "the spring (season)" and "spoiled (food)." Confirmed
throughout the Nouns and Adjectives sections above.

### Synonym-pair listing convention

When two Tagalog words share the same or nearly the same meaning, the booklet lists both rather
than picking one — its own worked example is "the hall, the corridor." This convention accounts
for the very large number of double- and triple-gloss entries throughout this file (e.g. "ang
pag-íbig, ang pag-mamahál" for "the love"; "estupido, gago, tanga" for "stupid").

### Adjective agreement (gender/case)

The booklet notes that when a Tagalog adjective's spelling or pronunciation changes according to
the gender or case of what it modifies, both forms are given in the word list. No specific
gender-agreeing adjective pairs were encountered in Level I's vocabulary lists themselves (Tagalog
adjectives are largely gender-invariant compared to, e.g., Spanish) — this note appears to be
carried over from the VocabuLearn series' shared front-matter template (used across its many
language editions) more than reflecting something Level I Tagalog's own word lists actually
exercise. Worth checking against Level II/III of the same series, or against a real grammar
reference, before treating Tagalog adjective agreement as a confirmed finding.

### Definite/indefinite article usage note

The booklet states that Level I uses only the definite article "the" throughout its Nouns section
(Levels II and III of the same series use both "the" and "a"/"an"), consistent with every Nouns
entry above being glossed as "the X" rather than "an X." This is a property of the pedagogical
exercise design (how the English prompts are phrased), not a Tagalog grammatical fact — Tagalog's
own "ang" marker (glossed "the" throughout, e.g. "ang damó," "ang buwán") is better understood as a
topic/subject-focus marker than a definite article in the European sense, a point this source does
not address at all (again pointing toward `13 Tagalog Grammar.pdf` for the real explanation).

### Expressions section register note (explicit, source-marked)

See the note at the head of the Expressions tables above: the source explicitly states that Level
I's Expressions section is rendered "in the formal manner of speaking," with adjective gender
agreeing with the narrator's own gender. This is the one genuinely load-bearing grammar/register
claim in the whole booklet, which is why every Expressions-section vocabulary row above carries
`formal` as its Usage Tier rather than the otherwise-default `core`.

### Idiom non-compositionality note

The booklet's own front matter flags that its Expressions section consists of idioms "often not
translatable on a word-for-word basis," and that the closest natural English equivalent is chosen
rather than a literal gloss. This is corroborated throughout the Expressions tables above — e.g.
"Mainit ang ulo mo" (lit. "your head is hot") glossed as "you're in a bad mood," and "Ako'y
lukong-luko sa kaniya" (lit. involving a reduplicated form of "crazy/foolish") glossed as "I am
crazy about her." Worth flagging for the synthesis phase: these are exactly the kind of
non-compositional idiom-formation patterns (figurative body-part/emotion mappings, reduplication
for intensity) that a derived-slang system could productively mimic.

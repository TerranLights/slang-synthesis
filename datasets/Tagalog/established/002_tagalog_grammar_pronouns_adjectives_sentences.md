# Tagalog — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Pronouns, Adjectives, Simple Sentences, Negation, Questions I

**Source:** *Tagalog Grammar* ("13 Tagalog Grammar.pdf", 115 PDF pages, CCE_WIN, 2008). This file
continues directly from `001_tagalog_grammar_phonology_nouns_markers.md` and covers: Pronouns
(ANG-, NG-, and SA-pronoun paradigms, including the personal and demonstrative sub-sets), Adjectives
(neutral/MA-adjective forms, comparative forms, superlative forms), Simple Sentences (nominal,
adjectival, verbal — active and non-active —, existential, prepositional, AY-inverted, and
focusless sentence types), Negation (HINDI/WALA), and Questions I (yes–no questions via BA, tag
questions, and the content question words SINO/ANO/SAAN/ILAN).

## Grammar points

### ANG-, NG-, and SA-pronoun sets

Tagalog pronouns come in three parallel sets matching the three marker classes from file 001: ANG-
pronouns (focused — ako, ikaw/ka, siya, kami, tayo, kayo, sila), NG-pronouns (unfocused
actor/possessor/object — ko, mo, niya, namin, natin, ninyo, nila), and SA-pronouns (locative/
directional/beneficiary — akin, iyo, kanya, amin, atin, inyo, kanila). The source notes a real,
currently-live sociolinguistic shift: young/colloquial speakers increasingly extend the *personal*
pronoun sets (properly restricted to human referents) to animate and even inanimate nouns — flagged
explicitly as colloquial usage the learner "would be advised" to avoid, i.e. an attested register
distinction between careful/prescriptive and casual spoken Tagalog, directly relevant to this
project's slang-mechanics interest.

The 1st-person-plural **inclusive/exclusive distinction** (tayo vs. kami; natin vs. namin; atin vs.
amin) has no single-word English equivalent and is grammatically obligatory, not optional — a
notable typological feature worth comparing against other Austronesian-family and Pacific languages
this project may extract later.

### Demonstrative pronouns: a three-way distance system, mirrored across all three marker sets

All three marker-paradigms (ANG/NG/SA) carry a parallel three-way demonstrative distinction based on
relative distance from speaker and/or hearer: near-speaker (ito/nito/dito), near-hearer
(iyan/niyan/diyan), and far-from-both (iyon/niyon/doon). Demonstratives substitute directly for
full ANG-, NG-, or SA-phrases and can also function as attention-directing sentence-initial pointers
(*Ito si Noel* "This is Noel").

### Adjective degrees: neutral, comparative, superlative

Tagalog distinguishes three adjective degrees, structurally quite unlike English's regular
-er/-est system:

- **Neutral** adjectives are either **simple** (bare roots: payat, luma, mura) or **MA-adjectives**
  (ma- + root: maganda, mataba) — MA- functions much like English *-ful*, "full of [root quality]."
  Both types double as adverbs (Tagalog does not grammatically distinguish adjectives from adverbs:
  *Mabilis tumakbo si Juan* "Juan runs fast").
- **Comparative (equality)** uses **magkasing-** when both compared items are equally focal/
  prominent (*Magkasingganda ang rosas at ang orkidyas* "Roses and orchids are equally beautiful"),
  or **kasing-/sing-** when one item is focal and the other is a backgrounded reference point
  (*Kasingganda ng rosas ang orkidyas* "Orchids are as beautiful as roses" — orchids focal, roses
  the NG-marked reference). **Comparative (inequality)** uses **mas** + adjective + ANG-phrase +
  (kaysa) + SA-phrase (*Mas maganda ang rosas kaysa sa orkidyas* "Roses are more beautiful than
  orchids").
- **Superlative** has (at least) five distinct strategies: (1) full-root reduplication linked by NA
  (*magandang-maganda* "very beautiful"); (2) fronting ANG + bare adjective root, optionally
  reduplicated, with the described noun demoted to NG (*Ang ganda ng pelikula!* "Oh, the beauty of
  that movie!" — an exclamatory-flavored construction); (3) the prefix **napaka-** (*Napakaganda ng
  pelikula*); (4) the prefix **pagka-**, optionally with root reduplication (*Pagkaganda-ganda ng
  pelikula*); (5) a small set of fixed idiomatic intensifier constructions (*ubod ng*, *saksakan
  ng*, *puno ng* + adjective root). Strategies 2–4 all demote the described noun out of focus,
  reflecting a consistent underlying logic: when the *quality* itself is framed as the most salient
  element of the sentence, the thing possessing that quality cannot simultaneously be in focus.

### Simple sentence types

Tagalog's basic sentence frame is **Predicate + Focus + Complement(s)**, with no equivalent of the
English copula *to be* — a bare adjective, noun, or verb directly occupies the predicate slot.
Complements/arguments following the predicate have largely free order (the book's PCS/ProNP/
shortest-first ordering heuristics are practical guidelines, not hard syntax rules). Six sentence
types are distinguished by what occupies the predicate slot:

- **Nominal sentences** — a noun phrase as predicate (*Titser si Mr. Ramos* "Mr. Ramos is a
  teacher"), used for occupation/nationality/identity statements.
- **Adjectival sentences** — an adjective as predicate (*Makulay ang bahaghari* "The rainbow is
  colorful").
- **Verbal sentences**, split into **Active** (actor-trigger affixes -um-/mag-/ma-, with the doer in
  focus) and **Non-active** (other trigger affixes, with object/location/beneficiary/instrument in
  focus) — the book is explicit that calling non-active sentences "passive" (as much linguistic
  literature does) is a mislabeling, since the English translations of these sentences are not
  themselves passive; file 005's academic chapter independently confirms this exact point via the
  ergativity analysis. Full affix inventory and paradigm tables are in file 003.
- **Existential sentences** — predicate headed by *mayroon*/*may* (affirmative) or *wala*
  (negative), expressing possession or existence (*May lihim ako* "I have a secret").
- **Prepositional sentences** — predicate headed by *nasa* or *sa*, expressing location (*Nasa
  bahay si Jose* "Jose is at home").
- **AY-sentences** — an inverted word order (**Focus + AY + Predicate**, vs. the default
  Predicate+Focus) used across all the above sentence types; more a discourse/register variant than
  a distinct sentence type.
- **Focusless sentences** — a residual category without any ANG-marked phrase at all, including:
  *gusto*-initial sentences whose actor can never be in focus; phenomenal/weather sentences (natural
  phenomena take the -um- affix on a noun root, e.g. *umaraw* "sun shining," and these still inflect
  for aspect); *ka-*-marked recently-completed-action sentences (*kakakain ko lang* "I just ate" —
  formed by the particle *ka-* plus reduplication of the verb's first consonant+vowel, distinct from
  the *ka-* descriptive-adjective prefix noted above); bare time-noun sentences (*Lunes kahapon*
  "Yesterday was Monday"); and exclamations introduced by *kay*, *anong*, or *anong pagka* (the last
  intensifying via root reduplication, paralleling the superlative system above).

### Negation: HINDI vs. WALA

**HINDI** negates nominal, adjectival, and verbal sentences, placed immediately before the
predicate (*Hindi Amerikano ang bisita* "The visitor is not American"). **WALA** negates existential
and prepositional sentences specifically, replacing *may(roon)* or *nasa* outright rather than
preceding them (*Walang tao sa bahay* "There's nobody home"). When the subject/object is a pronoun,
it moves to immediately follow the negator rather than staying in its normal post-predicate slot.

### Questions I: yes–no (BA), tag questions, and content words

Yes–no questions are marked by the enclitic **BA** plus rising intonation; BA's position shifts by
sentence type (after the predicate in simple sentences, after the focus in AY-sentences, after an
emphasized fronted element in emphatic sentences) — exceptions occur with the pronoun *ka* and other
enclitics (na, pa, naman). Tag questions use **hindi ba?**/**'di ba?** ("isn't that so?"). The four
content-question words covered here — **sino** (who), **ano** (what), **saan** (where), **ilan**
(how many) — each pattern with an ANG-clause and, like pseudo-verbs (file 003), attract any actor
pronoun to sit immediately after the question word itself. Each has a reduplicated plural/
distributive counterpart (sinu-sino, anu-ano, saan-saan) except ilan, whose plurality is carried by
the expected numeral answer instead.

## Grammar points

### Lesson 7 — Ligatures (-ng, -g, na): the full rule set

Tagalog uses three **ligatures** — **-ng** (suffixed after a vowel-final word), **-g** (suffixed
specifically after words ending in **-n**), and **na** (a free word placed between two consonant-final
words, for any final consonant other than **-n**) — to mark a close syntactic/modifying relationship
between two adjacent words. Ligatures carry no meaning of their own. The book gives an unusually
long, explicit enumeration of **14 distinct word-pair types that require a ligature**: noun+noun,
noun+verb, adjective+verb, verb+adverb, noun+adverb, adjective+adverb, conjunction+adverb (except
**kung** "if", which takes no ligature), verb+verb, adverb+adverb repeated for emphasis,
adjective+adjective repeated for a superlative-like intensive, noun+pronoun (except the
noun+possessive-pronoun sequence, which uses the special post-posed possessive forms **ko/mo/niyá/
námin/nátin/ninyó/nilá** instead), pronoun+adjective (except a nominative pronoun like **ka**/**kayó**
functioning as a sentence subject), verb+pronoun (most verb+pronoun sequences need no ligature at
all — a stated exception to the exception), and pronoun+pronoun (except subjunctive-mood sentences
and two-pronoun fused sentences like **Kaniyá itó** "this is his/hers"). **Word order within a
modifier+noun (or verb+adverb, etc.) pair is free** — either element may come first, and the
ligature always attaches to/after whichever element comes first, without any change in meaning (a
genuinely symmetric, word-order-flexible modification system, unlike English's fixed adjective-before-
noun order). When more than one adjective precedes a single noun in a chain, only the *last* adjective
before the noun takes a ligature (**mabaít at masunúring áso** "a good and obedient dog" — no ligature
on **mabaít**, only before the noun). This is one of the most systematically-documented single grammar
points in the book so far and is flagged as a strong candidate for later Tagalog slang-mechanics work
on productivity/word-order flexibility.

### Lesson 8 — Possession: ni/ng, possessive pronouns, and nitó/niyán/niyón

**Ni** marks possession before a proper noun (**ang báhay ni Maria**, "Maria's house"); **ng** marks
possession before a common noun (**ang báhay ng laláki**, "the man's house"). **The possessed item
always precedes the possessor** in word order — the reverse of English's possessor-first default,
though the resulting gloss ("Maria's house") reads the same either way once translated. Plural
possessor forms are **niná** (plural of **ni**) and **ng mga** (plural of **ng**). **Possessive
pronouns** have two forms depending on position: a *pre-nominal* form (**áking**, **iyóng**, **kaniyáng**,
etc., all requiring a ligature) and a distinct, shorter *post-nominal* form (**ko**, **mo**, **niyá**,
**námin**, **nátin**, **ninyó**, **nilá**) used when the pronoun follows its noun — this post-nominal
set is the ligature-rule exception noted in Lesson 7. A genuine inclusive/exclusive split recurs here
in the possessive paradigm too: **ámin/námin** "our (excluding addressee)" vs. **átin/nátin** "our
(including addressee)" — paralleling the **kamí/táyo** personal-pronoun split from Lesson 6. **Style
rule for sentences needing two possessive pronouns**: Tagalog speakers prefer *mixing* the pre-nominal
and post-nominal forms across the two instances (one each) rather than repeating the same form twice,
specifically to avoid an awkward phonetic repetition (the book's worked example: avoid **iná niyá ...
báhay niyá**, prefer **iná niyá ... kanyáng báhay** or **kanyáng iná ... báhay niyá**) — a genuine
euphony-driven grammatical preference, not a hard rule, but one native speakers apply reliably.
**Plural possessive-pronoun placement**: the possessive pronoun goes *between* **ang** and **mga**
(**ang áking mga anák**, not ***ang mga áking anák***) — a fixed word-order constraint. **Nitó, niyán,
niyón** are the possessive forms of the demonstratives **itó/iyán/iyón** ("of this/that (person)'s"),
used when speaker and addressee share known reference to a specific person without needing to name
them; unlike ordinary possessive pronouns, **these always stay in post-nominal position even in
inverted-order sentences** (no pre-nominal variant exists) — a genuine asymmetry between the
demonstrative-possessive and personal-possessive pronoun paradigms. Their plural is formed by adding
**ng mga** before the demonstrative (**itó→ng mga itó**), not by pluralizing **nitó** itself, since
**ng** already carries the possessive-marking job — attaching plural marking to **nitó** directly
would be redundant/wrong per the source.

### Lesson 9 — Question words (14-word paradigm) and their reduplicated plurals

Tagalog has **14 distinct question words** corresponding to English who/what/which/how(×2)/why/
when/where(×2)/how many/how much/whose/for whom/with-to whom: **síno** (who), **anó** (what),
**alín** (which), **gaáno** (how — requires a response about degree/quality, e.g. size), **paáno**
(how — requires a response detailing a process/method), **bakit** (why), **kailán** (when), **nasaán**
(where — location of a person/thing), **saán** (where — location of an action), **ilán** (how many),
**magkáno** (how much), **kaníno** (whose — requires a response in a possessive-pronoun form), plus
the compound **para sa kaníno** (for whom) and **sa kaníno** (with/to whom). **Plural question-word
formation is a genuine, rule-governed reduplication system**: a two-syllable question word reduplicates
wholesale (**alín→alín-alín**, **síno→sinu-síno**); a three-or-more-syllable word reduplicates only
its first two syllables (**kaníno→kaní-kanino**, **magkáno→magka-magkáno**); and in both cases, a
final-syllable **o** becomes **u** in the first (reduplicated) copy (**anó→anú-anó**, not
***anó-anó***). **Nasaán and bakit are never pluralized**, for an explicitly logical reason the source
states outright: nothing can be in more than one place at once, nor can an event have more than one
"why." **Kaí-kailán** (plural of "when") is explicitly flagged by the source as now rare, surviving
mainly in rural/regional Tagalog speech — a genuine, source-marked regional-register annotation
(the first in this file's chunk) worth tracking.

### Lesson 10 — The question particle ba (and the na/pa/din-rin/daw-raw/ngâ particle cluster)

**Ba** is a semantically-empty interrogative particle with no direct English translation — it exists
purely to mark a sentence as a question (disambiguating it from a declarative one in writing/toneless
contexts, since Tagalog otherwise relies on rising intonation alone). Placement rule set: (1) in a
subject-predicate (**ay**-linked) sentence, **ba** goes immediately after the subject and before
**ay** (often contracting **ba + ay → ba'y**); (2) in inverted-order sentences, **ba** goes immediately
after the predicate; (3) in a question that opens with an interrogative pronoun (síno/anó/etc.), **ba**
goes immediately after that interrogative — and this construction is *not* answerable with a simple
yes/no, unlike the first two patterns; (4) in a question built on a "pseudo-verb" (**gustó**, **áyaw**,
**íbig**, **puwéde**, **maáari** — previewed here ahead of their systematic Lesson 17 treatment), **ba**
goes between the pseudo-verb and the main verb and takes the ligature **-ng** (**Gustó bang kumáin ng
batà?**) — *unless* a pronoun intervenes before the main verb, in which case the ligature attaches to
the pronoun instead (**Puwéde ba akong umalís?**). **Ba** belongs to a broader class of monosyllabic
emphasis/evidentiality particles that can co-occur and stack in a fixed relative order, pushing **ba**
further toward the sentence's end: **na** ("already/now," finality), **pa** ("still/yet/more"), **din/
rin** ("too/also" — **din** after consonant-final words, **rin** after vowel-final, a conditioned
allomorphy pair), **daw/raw** ("reportedly/they say," a genuine grammaticalized evidentiality marker —
same consonant/vowel-final conditioning as din/rin), and **ngâ** ("indeed," adding emphasis to the
preceding word). **Regional variant noted**: some Tagalog-speaking provinces use **bagá** instead of
**ba** — a second explicit source-marked regional annotation in this chunk.

### Lesson 11 — Degrees of comparison: equality, inequality, intensives, superlatives

Four-way comparison system. **(1) Equality** — "as X as": prefix **kásing-**/**magkásing-** (meaning
"as") attached directly to an adjective root, with three conditioned allomorphs depending on the
root's initial sound — **kásing-/magkásing-** before vowels and **k, g, h, m, n, w, y**;
**kásin-/magkásin-** before **d, l, r, s, t**; **kásim-/magkásim-** before **b, p** (a genuine
place-of-articulation-driven nasal assimilation pattern, directly comparable to the **mang-/mag-**
nasal assimilation this project will likely meet again in later verb-affix lessons). **Kásing-** +
adjective + **ni/ng** + B compares two named entities (**Si Maria ay kasínggandá ni Elena**, "Maria
is as beautiful as Elena"); **magkásing-** instead places both compared entities side by side as
co-subjects (**Sina Maria at Elena ay magkasínggandá**, "Maria and Elena are equally beautiful") —
a genuine syntactic-frame distinction between an asymmetric A-compared-to-B structure and a symmetric
both-together structure, both built on the same root prefix. A separate, non-affixal equality strategy
uses the free words **parého**, **katúlad**, or **gáya** ("similar to/like") + **ni/ng** + B, which the
source notes produces the identical translated meaning as the kásing- construction. **(2) Inequality**
("more X than" / "less X than"): "more" uses any of three free synonyms — **lalóng** (from **lalo** +
ligature **-ng**), **higít na**, or **mas** — followed by the adjective, then **káysa kay** (before a
person's name) or **káysa sa** (before a place/thing), colloquially shortened to **kesa**. "Less/not
as X as" uses **hindî kásing-** + adjective + **ni/ng** + B — i.e. the equality-prefix construction
simply negated, rather than a dedicated "lesser" affix. **(3) Intensives** ("very X"): either full
reduplication of the adjective with the appropriate ligature (**magandáng-magandá** "very beautiful"),
or the prefix **nápaka-** attached directly to the root. **(4) Superlatives** ("most X"): prefix
**pinaká-** attached to the root. The four-way system (equality / comparative / intensive /
superlative) is fully productive and regular once the kásing- allomorphy is learned — a clean,
well-documented morphological paradigm worth cross-referencing against later lessons' verb-affix
allomorphy patterns.

### Lesson 12 — May, mayroón, and walâ (existence/possession and its negation)

**May** and **mayroón** (colloquially **meron**) both translate "there is/are" and "has/have" but are
**not interchangeable** — they select for different following word classes. **May** is followed
directly by a noun, adjective, numeral, the plural marker **mga** + noun, or a verb, and does not
require (and typically omits) an explicit possessor (**May kaibígan akó sa Quezon City**, "I have a
friend in Quezon City" — or, inverted, **May kaibígan akó...**, with **may** always sentence/clause-
initial relative to the possessed item). **Mayroón**, by contrast, is followed by monosyllabic
particles (**ka, na, pa, ba, din, daw** — the same particle set from Lesson 10) or by a personal
pronoun (**akó, ka, táyo, kamí, kayó, silá**) carrying the ligature **-ng**, and is used specifically
in *inverted*-order possessive sentences; converting a **may**-sentence to a **mayroón**-sentence
means rearranging **may + noun + possessive-pronoun** into **mayroón + possessive-pronoun-ng**.
**Mayroón** can also stand alone as a one-word affirmative answer to an existence/possession question.
**Etymological note the source makes explicitly**: the adjective-forming prefix **ma-** is itself
historically a contracted form of **may** — **marámi** ("many") analyzed as **may** + **dami**
("there is quantity"), **mataás** ("tall") as **may** + **taás** ("there is height"), **malínis**
("clean") as **may** + **linís** ("there is cleanliness"); with abstract-noun roots **ma-** means
"there is/are [quality]," while with concrete-noun roots it means "having many of [thing]" (**mabutó**
"bony," literally "having many bones"). **This is a genuinely important morphological-derivation
finding for later slang-mechanics analysis**: a hugely productive adjective-forming prefix across the
whole language turns out to be a fossilized/grammaticalized form of an existential-possessive particle
— a clean example of grammaticalization the book states outright rather than leaving implicit.
**Walâ** negates both **may** and **mayroón** sentences uniformly (it replaces either, regardless of
which was used), meaning "there is/are no," "does/do not have," or standalone "none." Critically, **the
correct negative answer to a may/mayroón question is walâ, not hindî** — **hindî** is explicitly
flagged by the source as an *incorrect* response in this context, marking existence/possession as its
own grammatical category distinct from ordinary polarity (hindî/oo), a genuine structural distinction
worth flagging for later cross-language mechanics comparison.

### Lesson 13 — Numbers: cardinals, ordinals, fractions, and currency

Tagalog has two complete, actively-coexisting numeral systems: native Tagalog cardinals (**isá, dalawá,
tatló...**) and Spanish-derived cardinals adapted to Tagalog phonetics (**uno, dos, tres...** written
e.g. **beynte sinko** for 25), with the source noting the Spanish-derived set remains common
specifically among older speakers and in market/buying-selling contexts (an explicit generational-
register split, not just free variation) — sellers reportedly prefer the Spanish numbers as
"faster to say" in fast-paced market transactions. **Teens are formed with prefix labíng-** (from
**labis ng**, "excess of," per the source's own etymological gloss) attached to isá–siyám, with three
conditioned allomorphs: **labín-** before **dalawá, tatló, limá, siyám** (labindalawá, labintatló,
labinlimá, labinsiyám — note vowel-initial-root-triggered simplification), **labím-** before **pitó**
(labimpitó), and plain **labíng-** elsewhere. **Tens** are formed by suffixing **-pû** (phonetically
reduced from **púo**, "ten") to the cardinal 1–9, with the ligature **-ng/-g** assimilating to **-m**
before it (**isáng púo → sampû**). **Hundreds/thousands and up are written as separate words**
(**siyám na raán** "nine hundred") rather than fused into one word as tens are, with **daán**
("hundred") used after consonant/**-ng**-final numbers and **raán** after consonant/**na**-final ones
— the same **d/r** alternation seen elsewhere in the language. The connective **at** (shortened **'t**)
links only the final two elements of a multi-part number. Native "big number" words exist for ten
thousand (**laksâ**), hundred thousand (**yutà**), and million (**ángaw**) but the source's framing
implies these are largely bookish/displaced by Spanish- or English-based counting in real usage (not
explicitly marked "archaic," but strongly implied as uncommon — worth flagging as a soft register note
rather than a hard source-marked tag). **Ordinals** prefix **iká-** to the cardinal, with two
irregular forms (**úna** "first," **hulí** "last" — not derived from **isá**/any cardinal at all) and
vowel-initial-triggered truncation of **dalawá**/**tatló** (**ikalawá**, **ikatló**, dropping the
root's first syllable before the prefix). **Fractions** prefix the shortened **ka-** (from **iká-**)
to the ordinal-form denominator, optionally followed by **bahagi** ("part") for clarity, except
**kalahatì** ("half"), a suppletive/fixed form not built compositionally from "two." The source
explicitly flags that **fraction vocabulary beyond kalahatì has become school-register only** in
contemporary usage — an explicit register-narrowing note, i.e. a case of a grammatical paradigm
surviving in formal/pedagogical registers while colloquial speech has largely abandoned it outside one
fossilized exception. **Currency**: the peso/centavo system uses **píso** (a Spanish loan, *peso*) and
**séntimó**/**pera**, with prices commonly read using the Spanish-numeral system in practice.

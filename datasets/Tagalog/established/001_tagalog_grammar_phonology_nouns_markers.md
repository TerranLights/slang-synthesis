# Tagalog — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Phonology, Nouns, and Case Markers

**Source:** *Tagalog Grammar* ("13 Tagalog Grammar.pdf", 115 PDF pages, A4, produced by CCE_WIN,
2008 — a web-sourced pedagogical reference, structurally matching the public-domain NIU SEAsite
Tagalog grammar course; see the bibliography note at the end of file 005 for the scholarly grammars
this kind of pedagogical site draws on). This file covers the book's opening sections: Tagalog
Sounds (consonants, vowels, diphthongs, phonics), Nouns (root-word formation, stems/complex nouns,
loanwords), Plurality, Noun Modification (adjectives as modifiers, the linker NA, colors, numbers,
shapes, size/weight measurement terms), and the Markers system (ANG/SI-SINA, NG/NI-NINA, SA/KAY-
KINA).

**Coverage note.** This is Tagalog Wave 1, the first extraction for Tagalog and the first
Austronesian-family language in this project. The source has a clean, genuine text layer
(`pdftotext -layout` and the raw mode both extract cleanly; cross-checked — no font-substitution
cipher, no CID/raster-image gotcha; the handful of non-ASCII characters found are ordinary
typographic marks: en-dashes, acute-accented Spanish loan vowels, curly apostrophes). No vision-
reading was needed, so `Vision Reading Confidence` is `n/a` throughout this file. Every grammar
point and distinct vocabulary item in this section is captured; the book's audio-icon callouts
("click to listen") are UI chrome, not content, and are not reproduced. Song/poem epigraphs that
open several sections (lyrics by Andres Bonifacio, Galo B. Ocampo, Hugo C. Yonzon, etc.) are
illustrative decoration rather than grammar content and are not extracted as vocabulary, though a
few are referenced in Notes where they illustrate a point made in the prose.

**New-language note (first Austronesian source).** Tagalog is typologically very different from
every language this project has covered so far (Slavic, Uralic/agglutinative, Koreanic, Romance,
Germanic). Two features dominate everything else: (1) nouns are not case-inflected at all — the
entire argument-role system lives in a small set of invariant **markers** (ang/ng/sa and their
personal-name counterparts) placed before the noun phrase, not in the noun itself; (2) the verb,
not the noun, carries the inflectional load that marks which argument is "in focus" (the
agent/actor-trigger, object-trigger, locative-trigger, etc. system — detailed fully in file 003,
the flagship verbal chapter). This file lays the noun/marker groundwork that the trigger system
depends on.

**Morpheme breakdown.** Tagalog is Austronesian with a non-concatenative, verb-centered
affix-and-trigger system (full treatment in file 003); most nominal vocabulary in this file is
root-form or simple-affix and does not require a breakdown. Two forms are worth flagging here as
compositional:

> **kagubatan** = ka- (collective/locative nominalizer) + gubat ("forest") + -an (place-suffix) —
> "thick forest, forest area"; the circumfix ka-...-an is one of several productive nominalizers
> noted on the book's stems-and-complex-nouns page (others: *pagka-* + root, *pag-* + verb root).

> **bahay-bata** = bahay ("house") + bata ("child") — a root-compound (not affixed) whose meaning
> ("uterus") is not transparently derivable from its parts; flagged as a lexicalized metaphor rather
> than a live productive pattern.

## Grammar points

### Consonant and vowel inventory

Tagalog has 16 native consonant phonemes (b, d, k, g, h, l, m, n, ng /ŋ/, p, the glottal stop,
r, s, t, w, y), 5 vowel phonemes (i, e, a, o, u), and 5 diphthongs (iw, ay, aw, oy, uy). Stress is
phonemic and distinguishes otherwise-identical words (the book doesn't give a minimal stress pair
directly, but flags stress as load-bearing; file 005's academic chapter gives the actual
phonological mechanics of vowel length and stress — see that file for the fuller treatment).
The language is "highly phonetic": with the noted glottal-stop exception, spelling matches
pronunciation closely (p. 1–2 area; unpaginated web-course front matter).

### The glottal stop

The glottal stop is a full phoneme that contrasts with zero and with other consonants (e.g. *bata*
"bathrobe" vs. *bata'* "child" vs. *batay* "based on"), but conventional spelling does not mark it
except as a hyphen between a consonant-final prefix and a vowel-initial root (*nag-aaral*
[nag'a'aral] "studying"). A word that looks vowel-initial in writing actually begins with a glottal
stop in speech, and a written vowel sequence has an intervening glottal stop (*aalis* ['a'alis]
"will go").

### The consonant ng

Tagalog /ŋ/ (written *ng*) occurs word-initially, -medially, and -finally — unlike English, where it
is restricted to word-final position (*sing*, *ring*). This is flagged as a specific pronunciation
difficulty for English-speaking learners.

### Noun roots: three functional types

Most Tagalog root words default to functioning as nouns (the book's framing: "if there are no
affixes added, the word is a root and probably functions as a noun"). Roots are classified by what
they denote when bare: **inherent** roots are nouns in the ordinary sense (*matá* "eye", *araw*
"sun"); **adjective** roots denote qualities and usually take the *ma-* prefix to actually surface
as adjectives (*ganda* "beauty" → *maganda* "beautiful"); **verb** roots denote actions and
regularly feed the trigger-affix system (*takbo* "run" is the book's primary running example,
carried through into the full verb-affix chapter, file 003). All three root types can take
nominalizing affixes and (inherent and verb roots only, not adjective roots) can combine with the
plural marker *mga*.

### Plurality: the marker MGA (and its absence)

Tagalog does not pluralize nouns morphologically (no English-style *-s*); plurality is marked
instead by the invariant pre-nominal particle **mga**, placed directly before the noun to be
pluralized (*bato* "stone" → *mga bato* "stones"). MGA becomes redundant — and is often dropped —
when plurality is already expressed by an explicit number (*dalawang kapatid* "two siblings") or by
an inherently plural noun (*grupo* "group", *piling* "bunch", *bigkis* "pile/bundle", *dosena*
"dozen").

### The linker NA and its allomorphs

When an adjective/descriptive word modifies a noun (in either order — Tagalog allows both
modifier-first and noun-first ordering), the two must be joined by the linker **NA**, which has
three conditioned forms: **-ng** after a vowel-final word (*malayong bayan* "faraway land"),
**-g** after a word ending in *-n* (*bayang malayo*, same meaning, noun-first order), and the
free-standing word **na** after any other consonant-final word. This same linker also connects
numbers and measurement expressions to the nouns they modify (*limang lobo* "five balloons"), and
connects shape-words and color-words to nouns in either order.

### Numbers as noun modifiers

Numbers, unlike adjectives, can only precede the noun they modify (never follow), though the
linker NA is still obligatory between them. Measurement/weight expressions pattern the same way.
Tagalog maintains three parallel numeral systems in active use — native Tagalog, Spanish-derived,
and (increasingly, especially among the young) English — with context determining which is used:
native numerals for everyday counting, Spanish-derived numerals dominant for telling time and
historically for money, and English numerals gaining ground generationally. This three-way
coexistence is itself a notable sociolinguistic fact for this project's slang-mechanics goals: a
single semantic domain (numbers) sustaining three co-active lexical strata is a ready-made site for
register-marked code-switching in casual speech.

### Markers: the key structural concept

A **marker** is a word placed before a noun or noun phrase that signals the phrase's grammatical
role in the sentence (similar in position to an English article, but functionally closer to a case
particle). Tagalog words fall into three broad marker-defined categories, and — critically — *the
same lexical word* can appear in any of the three categories depending on how the speaker uses it
in a given sentence; the marker, not the word's inherent class, determines its role:

1. **Focus words** — marked by **ANG**-class markers (ang; si/sina for personal names).
2. **Relational words** — marked by **NG**-class markers (ng; ni/nina for personal names).
3. **Locative/directional words** — marked by **SA**-class markers (sa; kay/kina for personal
   names).

### ANG and SI/SINA: the focus markers

**ANG** marks the focus (the most prominent argument) of a sentence, for any noun except a
personal name. It also functions as a definiteness marker in certain constructions, especially
with pseudo-verbs (*gusto ko ang mangga* "I like **the** mango" vs. *gusto ko ng mangga* "I like
mangoes" — compare file 003 for the pseudo-verb /NG-focuslessness interaction). **SI** marks a
single personal name as focus (*estudyante si Rita* "Rita is a student"); **SINA** marks two or
more names, or — when used with a single name — implies "[name] and company/associates" (*sina
Bill* ≈ "Bill and his group").

### NG and NI/NINA: the relational (non-focus) markers

**NG** is the functional inverse of ANG: it marks a word as explicitly *not* the sentence's focus.
It has three distinct functions depending on context: (1) marking possession, with the possessor
following the possessed noun (*bahay ng pamilya Santos* "the Santos family's house"); (2) marking
an unfocused direct object (*Bumili si Ana ng lapis* "Ana bought a pencil"); (3) marking an
unfocused actor/doer of a verb's action (*Sinulat ng makata ang tula* "The poem was written by the
poet"). A single sentence can have more than one NG-marked phrase simultaneously (one marking the
unfocused actor, another the unfocused object) — this is the structural seed of the trigger/focus
alternations detailed fully in file 003. **NI**/**NINA** are the personal-name counterparts,
parallel to SI/SINA's relationship to ANG.

### SA and KAY/KINA: the locative/directional markers

**SA** is Tagalog's single broad-spectrum locative/directional marker, covering most of what
English expresses with a wide range of distinct prepositions (to, at, in, from, for). Its four
attested functions in this section: (1) marking location (*Nag-aaral si Maya sa Unibersidad*); (2)
marking direction toward or away from something (*Pumunta sila sa Zamboanga*); (3) marking the
beneficiary of an action, always preceded by *para* "for" (Spanish loan) — *Gumawa siya ng eksamen
para sa klase*; (4) marking a future date/day/season as a specific time-point (*May trabaho ako sa
Lunes* "I have work on Monday"). **KAY**/**KINA** are the personal-name counterparts (singular vs.
plural/"and company"), sharing all four SA functions, plus a fifth restricted to spoken discourse:
marking a pre-nominal possessor linked by NA (*kay Bob na kotse* "Bob's car").

### Putting markers together: a worked example

The book's central illustration: *Dinala ng doktor ang elepante sa ospital* ("The doctor carried the
elephant to the hospital") — *elepante* is ANG-marked (focus, the thing being carried), *doktor* is
NG-marked (unfocused actor, the carrier), *ospital* is SA-marked (location/direction). Swapping
which noun receives which marker changes the sentence's meaning without any word-order change:
*Dinala ang doktor ng elepante sa ospital* reverses actor and patient ("the elephant carried the
doctor"). This demonstrates the core typological fact that Tagalog argument-role assignment lives
in the markers (and the verb's trigger affix, file 003), not in word order or noun inflection.

**Morpheme breakdown.**

> **ipasukan** = i- (conveyance/instrument-forming prefix) + pasok ("enter") + -an (locative
> suffix) — circumfix i-...-an nominalizes a motion verb into "the place one enters," i.e.
> "entrance." Parallel pattern:
> **ilabasan** = i- + labas ("exit/outside") + -an — "the place one exits."
> **ihintayan** = i- + hintay ("wait") + -an — "the place one waits" (waiting room).
> All three station-signage nouns share the same i-...-an circumfix productively applied to a
> motion/action root — the clearest concentrated example in this chunk of the locative-nominalizing
> affix pattern that elsewhere surfaces as a verbal trigger category.

## Grammar points

### The full ang/ng/sa pronoun paradigm
pp.52-54 give the course's first systematic pronoun table, organized by grammatical case/role
rather than just person: **ang-form** pronouns (`ako`, `ka`, `kami`, `tayo`, `kayo`...) mark the
sentence topic; **ng-form** pronouns (`ko`, `mo`, `namin`, `natin`, `ninyo`...) correspond to
English possessives/objects; **sa-form** pronouns (`akin`, `iyo`, `amin`, `atin`, `inyo`...)
likewise correspond to possessives/objects but typically co-occur with the particle `sa`. The book
stresses that a single Pilipino pronoun can require several different English glosses depending on
sentence position, while distinct Pilipino forms (`asawa ko` / `aking asawa`) can converge on one
English translation — explicitly framing pronoun choice as governed by grammatical role, not a
free English-like position.

### Mag- as a general verbalizing prefix
The book calls out `mag-` as productive beyond its lexicalized verb roots: it can verbalize ordinary
nouns (`asawa` "spouse" → `mag-asawa` "to get married") and even untranslated English loanwords
(`mag-sweater` "to wear a sweater," `magbus` "to take a bus"), i.e. an open, synchronically live
word-formation process rather than a closed set of memorized verbs. (p.51)

### Dual numeral systems: native Pilipino vs. Tagalized Spanish
Pilipino maintains two parallel full numeral systems in productive use: a native set (`isa`,
`dalawa`, `tatlo`...) and a Spanish-derived set (`uno`, `dos`, `tres`...). The book states the
split is functional, not free variation: Tagalized Spanish numbers are the default for **prices and
telling time**, while the native set is the default elsewhere (counting objects/people, distances in
some contexts). This is a clean, source-stated register/domain split worth flagging for Phase 3
borrowing-mechanics analysis. (p.65)

### Time-telling formulas
Clock time is built from `alas` + a Tagalized Spanish number (`ala` before `una` "one," irregularly),
with `y medya` ("and a half" → half past), `y kuwarto` ("and a quarter" → quarter past), `pasado`
("past," for odd minute counts), and `menos ... para` ("minus ... for," i.e. "X minutes to [the
hour]") as compositional modifiers, plus `impunto` ("on the dot/exactly"). (pp.70-74)

### Register note: ho as a regional/informal variant of po
The book explicitly glosses `ho` as "a variation of po" (p.91) — the first source-marked
register/regional particle-pair variant encountered in this course (`po`/`ho` politeness-particle
alternation is independently documented elsewhere as geographically distributed, more associated
with certain regions/registers than with Manila-standard `po`). Flagged per the coverage rule's
explicit-dialectal-annotation requirement.

### Aspect marking recap: reduplication for contemplated/future
Building on Module I's aspect system, this module gives two further worked examples of the
contemplated/future aspect formed by reduplicating the base's first syllable: `aalis` (from `alis`)
and `darating` (from `dating`), alongside `maaayos` (from `ayos`) — confirming the reduplication
pattern applies across different trigger/prefix classes (bare root, `ma-` roots), not just the
`mag-`/`ika-` pair introduced in Module I.

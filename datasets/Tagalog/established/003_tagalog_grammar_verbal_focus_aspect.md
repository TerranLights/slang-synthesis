# Tagalog — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Verbal Focus/Trigger System and Aspect (flagship chapter)

**Source:** *Tagalog Grammar* ("13 Tagalog Grammar.pdf", 115 PDF pages, CCE_WIN, 2008). This file
covers the book's core verbal-morphology chapters: Verbal Focus (the trigger/voice-affix system —
Actor-Focus, Object-Focus, Location/Direction-Focus, Beneficiary-Focus, Instrumental-Focus),
Verbal Aspect (Completed/Incompleted/Contemplated, and the reduplication mechanics that mark them),
Imperative Forms (commands and the PAKI- requestive prefix), and Pseudo-Verbs (modal auxiliaries).

**Coverage note.** Same clean text-layer source as files 001–002; `Vision Reading Confidence` =
`n/a` throughout. This is the single most important chapter for this project's purposes: the
affix-and-trigger (voice/focus) system is the core grammatical mechanism of Tagalog, and per this
language's extraction-checklist convention, **every vocabulary entry below involving a verbal
affix explicitly records its trigger/focus category** in a dedicated `Trigger/Focus` column
inserted after `Part of Speech` — adapting the standard vocabulary-table format the same way the
Japanese established files added a script-form column for a different language-specific need (see
`../00_Extraction_Checklist.md` and `../../00_Reference_Extraction_Spec.md`). Many entries below are
different trigger-affixed forms of the *same* verb root (e.g. *luto* "cook" appears as an
actor-focus, object-focus, location-focus, and beneficiary-focus form) — this is deliberate and
required by the coverage rule, since each trigger form is grammatically and often semantically
distinct, not a mechanical restatement of the same lexical item. The book's illustrative
"artificial" multi-argument sentences (the dentist/elephant and policeman/cow/governor examples used
purely to show the full argument-marking paradigm) are referenced in Grammar points rather than
reproduced as vocabulary, since they are explicitly flagged by the source itself as unrealistic
laboratory sentences rather than natural vocabulary.

> displacing a prior one.

## Grammar points

### The core mechanism: affixes mark role, not word order

Tagalog does not use word order to indicate "who did what to whom" (contrast English, where *The
man bit the dog* and *The dog bit the man* mean different things purely by constituent order).
Instead, the verb's trigger affix identifies which semantic role (actor, object/patient, location,
beneficiary, instrument) belongs to the **focused** argument — the one carrying the ANG/SI marker
(files 001–002) — while every other argument's role is read off its own marker (NG/SA and their
personal-name variants). Swapping which argument is ANG-marked, and changing the verb's affix to
match, can reverse an entire sentence's meaning without any change in word order at all. The book's
worked "policeman hit the cow with a glove for the governor" sentence demonstrates the complete
cycle: the same five nouns (policeman, cow, glove, governor, + the verb *banat* "to hit") can be
recombined into five distinct sentences, one per trigger category, each correctly answering a
different *wh*-question (*Who hit...?* → Actor-Focus; *What did... hit?* → Object-Focus; *Where did
the hit land?* → Location-Focus; *Who was it hit for?* → Beneficiary-Focus; *What was used to hit?*
→ Instrumental-Focus) while the English translation of all five is identical.

### Actor-Focus (AF): -um-, mag-, ma-

Three affixes mark that the sentence's focus is the **doer** of the verb's action; the book presents
them as functionally interchangeable "different ways of saying the same thing," though in practice
usage is lexically fixed per root (confirmed independently and in more phonological/historical depth
by file 005). **-um-** is an infix placed directly before the root's first vowel (*takbo* → *tumakbo*).
**MAG-** is a prefix (*luto* → *magluto*), used productively for characteristic/habitual activities
and — per file 005 — historically built from the stem-forming prefix *pag-* with *m-* replacing
initial *p-*. **MA-** actor-focus verbs are comparatively rare and largely must be memorized
root-by-root (tulog, ligo, nood, kinig) rather than derived by a general rule — file 005 independently
confirms this affix is "idiosyncratic" for actor-focus use, occurring productively instead in a
different function (verbs of "becoming": *mabingi* "become deaf").

### Object-Focus (OF): -in, i-, -an, ma-

Four affixes can mark the sentence's focus as the **receiver** of the verb's action, but only
**-IN** is described as "the only 'real' object-focus affix" — always object-focus, with no other
function. **I-** is the second most common object-focus marker but moonlights as a beneficiary- or
instrumental-focus marker elsewhere (disambiguated only by the individual verb and context — a
genuine source of structural ambiguity the book flags explicitly and advises memorizing per-root).
**-AN** rarely marks object-focus (a small, must-be-memorized set: buksan, takpan, labhan) since its
dominant job is location-focus (below). **MA-** marks a small, closed, non-productive set of
"abilitative/accidental"-flavored verbs (kita, dinig, pansin, alam) that are *always* object-focused
and categorically lack an imperative form — they cannot be used as commands, since one cannot
command someone to involuntarily "happen to see/hear/notice/learn" something.

### Location/Direction-Focus (LDF): -an/-han

**-AN** (becoming **-HAN** after a vowel-final root) is "the prototypical location indicator" when
used in this function, marking the focus as the place or direction the verb's action is oriented
toward. The book draws a direct line between this verbal usage and -AN's parallel productivity as a
*nominal* place-deriving suffix (*simba* "worship" → *simbahan* "church"; *tinda* "to sell" →
*tindahan* "store") — the same affix, the same underlying "place-of-X" semantics, operating on verbs
instead of nouns. This single-affix link between verbal location-focus marking and nominal
place-noun derivation is a genuinely productive cross-category pattern worth flagging for later
slang-mechanics analysis (Phase 3): a speaker coining a novel "place where X happens" noun and a
speaker marking location-focus on a novel verb are plausibly drawing on the exact same morphological
instinct.

### Beneficiary-Focus (BF): ipag-, -an

**IPAG-** is "the prototypical beneficiary-focus affix," frequently shortened in casual speech to
bare **I-** whenever the root has no competing i- object-focus form to collide with — a live,
productive register-conditioned reduction the source notes explicitly. A second, smaller set of
roots instead takes **-AN** for beneficiary-focus — surface-identical to location-focus -AN, with
the two categories disambiguated purely by which specific root is involved, not by any phonological
cue. This affix-sharing-with-ambiguity pattern (the same surface -an doing double duty for both
location- and beneficiary-focus) recurs as a structural theme across this whole trigger system and
is worth tracking into Phase 3 mechanics analysis as a likely source of productive
reanalysis/blending in casual or innovative speech.

### Instrumental-Focus (IF): ipang-

**IPANG-** marks the focus as the **tool/instrument** used to carry out the verb's action — the
book's example: "I opened the can **with the can opener**" would put *can opener* in instrumental
focus in Tagalog. Like IPAG-, this affix is often shortened to bare I- in practice, though beginners
are advised to retain the fuller IPANG- to avoid collision with other I-marked trigger categories.
Nasal-place assimilation applies at the ipang-+root boundary (*ipang-* + *linis* → *ipanlinis*,
velar→alveolar assimilation before /l/) — the same general nasal-assimilation process file 005
documents in more phonological depth for the related prefix *paN-*.

### Verbal aspect, not tense

Tagalog verbs mark **aspect** (whether an action is complete, incomplete, or hypothetical), not
**tense** (when an action occurred in absolute time) — a fully productive three-way system applying
uniformly across every trigger category above:

- **Completed** (perfective) — roughly English past tense.
- **Incompleted** (imperfective) — roughly English present/progressive.
- **Contemplated** — roughly English future.

The book's analogy: a "record keeper" (tense) cares about *when*; your "boss" (aspect) cares only
about *whether the job got done*. Separate time-indicator words (file 004) carry the actual
time-reference information that English speakers expect a tense system to supply.

### Reduplication as the aspect-marking mechanism

The mechanical core of aspect marking, across **every** trigger category, is **reduplication of the
verb root's first consonant and vowel** (or the bare vowel alone, for vowel-initial roots), combined
with trigger-affix-specific infixation/prefixation rules:

- **-um- verbs**: Completed = bare infinitive (-um- infixed once); Incompleted = reduplicate root's
  first CV, then re-infix -um- before the new first vowel (*takbo* → *tatakbo* → *tumatakbo*);
  Contemplated = reduplicated form without re-infixing -um- (*tatakbo*).
- **mag- verbs**: Completed = change infinitive's m→n (*magluto*→*nagluto*); Incompleted = same
  m→n change plus root-CV reduplication (*nagluluto*); Contemplated = infinitive's root-CV
  reduplication without the m→n change (*magluluto*).
- **-in/i-/-an verbs** (object/location/beneficiary-focus): Completed = insert -in- infix (or ni-
  prefix for l/r-initial roots) into the infinitive; Incompleted = both the -in-/ni- insertion *and*
  root-CV reduplication; Contemplated = root-CV reduplication alone, **without** the -in-/ni- marker
  — meaning the contemplated form of an object/location/beneficiary-focus verb looks like a
  "cleaner" reduplicated form than either of the other two aspects.
- **ma- (object-focus/abilitative) verbs**: parallels mag-'s m→n pattern (*makita*→*nakita*
  completed, *nakikita* incompleted via added reduplication, *makikita* contemplated).

File 005 independently confirms and formalizes this exact pattern from the academic/typological
side: it calls the root-CV copy "incompleteness marking" (shared by imperfective and contemplated
forms) and the /n/-containing affix "actuality marking" (shared by imperfective and perfective
forms) — the pedagogical source above derives the same two-dimensional system bottom-up, root by
worked example, while file 005 names the two cross-cutting dimensions explicitly. Worth flagging
as the same reduplication *mechanism* independently doing three distinct jobs across this
project's Tagalog material so far: adjectival intensification (file 002), superlative marking
(file 002), and verbal aspect marking (here) — a single productive process with at least three
separate grammatical payloads depending on what kind of word it attaches to and in what shape.

### Commands, requests, and the PAKI- politeness prefix

**Commands** use the verb's bare infinitive form plus the appropriate second-person pronoun (ka/kayo
for actor-focus verbs; mo/ninyo for all non-actor-focus trigger categories) — the pronoun's own
focus-alignment must match the verb's trigger category. Commands are softened toward a polite
request by adding the enclitics **nga** or **naman**. **Requests** proper use the dedicated prefix
**PAKI-** attached to the verb's root or stem (not the full infinitive), functioning much like
English "please" — e.g. *dalhin* (object-focus infinitive "to carry") → *pakidala* "please carry
(it)." PAKI- forms can themselves still take the nga/naman softening enclitics for extra politeness.

### Pseudo-verbs: modal meaning without trigger marking

**Pseudo-verbs** (gusto, ayaw, kailangan, puwede, maaari, dapat) express modal meanings (want,
dislike, need, can, might, must) but — unlike genuine verbs — carry **no** trigger-affix or aspect
marking of their own; the bare root is used unchanged regardless of context. They split into two
behavioral sets: **gusto/ayaw/kailangan** require their accompanying actor to be NG-marked and
categorically *cannot* have a focused actor (a genuine grammatical restriction, not just a
tendency — "a sentence with gusto can never have an actor that is in focus"); **puwede/maaari/
dapat** are more flexible and can combine with either an actor-focus or an object-focus agent/main
verb. In both sets, the pseudo-verb's accompanying main verb is always in the timeless,
aspectless infinitive form, and the actor pronoun (focused or not) always immediately follows the
pseudo-verb itself — the same "attracts the pronoun immediately after it" behavior also seen with
question words (file 002) and interrogative *bakit*-type words (file 004).

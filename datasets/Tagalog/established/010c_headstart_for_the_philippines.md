## Grammar points

### Benefactive/dative -an constructions in service requests
This chunk's ordering dialogues are built almost entirely on `paki-` (polite-request prefix) plus
an `-an`-suffixed verb that encodes who benefits from the action: `dalhan` ("bring for"), `bigyan`
("give to"). The book's own contrast of `pakidala` vs. `pakidalhan` (p.109) — the former a bare
"please bring [a thing]," the latter "please bring [a thing] *for me*" — is a clean illustration of
the benefactive trigger adding an explicit beneficiary argument the plain conveyance form lacks.

### Doneness/intensity adjectives via root reduplication
Steak-doneness vocabulary (`mahilaw-hilaw` "rare" through `lutung-luto` "well-done") extends the
reduplication-for-intensity pattern already seen with weather and taste adjectives (Modules I-II)
to a new semantic domain (cooking), suggesting root reduplication is a general-purpose
intensification strategy across adjective classes in this language, not limited to any one lexical
field. (p.113)

### Register note: nativized English/Spanish loan spellings in food-service vocabulary
Several terms in this chunk are respelled English or Spanish loans rather than native coinages:
`weyter` ("waiter"), `tsit` ("check"), `isteyk` ("steak"), `ispesyal` ("special"). Compare Module
II's auto-parts vocabulary, where the same pattern appeared with alternating nativized/raw-loan
doublets (`karburador`/`karbureytor`). This nativized-spelling strategy for recent loans looks like
a productive borrowing mechanism worth tracking into Phase 3 analysis of loanword integration.

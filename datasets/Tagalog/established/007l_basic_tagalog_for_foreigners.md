## Grammar points

### Context: the six reading texts

1. **"Ang Pagpapantay-Pantay sa Lipunan"** ("Equality in Society"), a speech by **Manuel A. Quezon**
   (1878–1944), President of the Commonwealth of the Philippines 1935–1944 — a policy address on
   labor rights, land reform, and social equality, invoking the Constitution and citing real
   Philippine government institutions (Department of Justice, Bureau of Labor).
2. **"Pag-ibig sa Tinubuang Lupa"** (excerpt), written in Barcelona, Spain in 1882 by **Dr. José P.
   Rizal** (1861–1896), the Philippines' national hero — an essay on love of one's native land/
   motherland, using an extended metaphor of the homeland as a living, suffering mother figure.
3. **"Tapok at Banlik"** (excerpt), first-prize winning essay in the 2005 Palanca Awards (the
   Philippines' most prestigious literary prize) by **Dr. Luis P. Gatmaitan**, a physician and
   children's-story writer — a first-person account of an art-therapy session with children who
   survived a landslide disaster in Real, Infanta, and Nakar (Quezon province, referencing the real
   November 2004 Luzon flooding/landslide disasters), exploring how the children processed trauma
   through drawing and storytelling. Introduces the colloquial regional/dialectal term **banlik**
   (used by survivors specifically for the landslide mud/silt, distinguished in their own usage from
   ordinary **putik** "mud") as a genuine piece of disaster-specific folk vocabulary the essay itself
   explicitly draws attention to and investigates.
4. **"Pambansang Awit ng Pilipinas"** (The Philippine National Anthem), music composed by Julian
   Felipe, original Spanish lyrics by José Palma, Filipino lyrics by the Institute of National
   Language — reproduced as a reading/cultural-literacy text rather than a grammar lesson.
5. **"Sa Aking Kababata"** ("To My Childhood"), a poem traditionally attributed to **Dr. José P.
   Rizal**, said to have been written at age eight — the source itself notes this attribution in a
   parenthetical ("sinulat noong siya ay walong taong gulang"); this poem is also, independently of
   this source, the historical origin of the oft-repeated (and linguistically inexact) claim that
   Rizal wrote "ang hindi magmahal sa sariling wika ay higit sa hayop at malansang isda" ("he who does
   not love his own language is worse than a beast and a rotten fish") as an endorsement specifically
   of Tagalog — worth flagging for later work, since the poem's actual authorship by Rizal has been
   disputed by Philippine historians/linguists (a point this source does not raise, stating the
   attribution as given).
6. **"Pag-ibig sa Tinubuang Lupa"** (a second, distinct work sharing its title with the Rizal excerpt
   above), a poem by **Andrés C. Bonifacio** (1863–1897), "Father of the Philippine Revolution" — a
   patriotic poem on love of country, written in a formal, archaic register distinct from the
   conversational Tagalog taught throughout the rest of this book, reinforcing the "literary" usage
   tier documented in `00_Usage_Tier_Taxonomy.md`.

### "Ano ang Pagkaiba ng Filipino sa Tagalog?" ("What Is the Difference Between Filipino and Tagalog?"), excerpt by Isagani R. Cruz — a genuine sociolinguistic/dialectological analysis

This is **the single most directly useful piece of content in this entire source for this project's
own "morphological typology" tracking note on Tagalog**, since it is a real linguist/educator's
argument — citing named Philippine linguists and specific documented examples — that **Filipino and
Tagalog are related but structurally distinct varieties**, not simply two names for the same language,
using a synchronic comparison method applied to a single matched sentence pair from the University of
the Philippines' 1992 language-policy document. The author (an educator and writer, not framed as a
professional linguist by the piece itself, which he acknowledges explicitly, inviting other scholars to
verify his claims) identifies **six concrete structural differences**, each illustrated with a real
example and, in several cases, attributed to a named source:

1. **Siya's referential scope**: in Tagalog, **siyá** ("he/she") is restricted to human referents;
   in Filipino, **siya** is used productively for *inanimate* referents too (**"Maganda siya"** can
   mean "It is beautiful," referring to a car, a dress, or a color) — directly bearing on this
   project's own documentation of **siyá** as a gender-neutral *human* third-person pronoun (Lesson 6,
   `007_basic_tagalog_for_foreigners.md`) and the homograph-risk flag already raised for the unrelated
   discourse-particle **siya** "all right/okay" (`007j`'s Conversational Tagalog notes) — this essay
   adds a *third*, dialectally-distinct sense/scope for the same surface form.
2. **Noun pluralization via borrowed -s**: Filipino (but not Tagalog) productively pluralizes nouns
   with English-style **-s**, attested by the author's own personal observation of Davao-area speakers
   using this since **1969** (e.g. "coke" → "cokes," "parents" → a nativized "parents ko" replacing
   the expected Tagalog-style phrase) — the author explicitly credits **Genoveva Edroza Matute**, a
   Filipino fiction writer, as the first to notice -s-marked plurals as a marker of linguistic
   divergence from Tagalog, and separately discusses Matute's own commentary on **barong** ("barong
   Tagalog," the formal shirt already documented in `007h`/`007j`) acquiring an Anglicized plural
   **barongs** specifically because the word had drifted from "Tagalog" framing to "Filipino" framing
   in everyday use — a genuinely concrete, named case study of lexical nativization/pluralization
   directly relevant to this project's borrowing-mechanics interest.
3. **Mag- displacing -um- as the general actor-focus affix**: the author reports personally observing,
   since the 1960s in Davao, **mag-** used productively where Tagalog would require **-um-**
   (**"nag-akyat"**/**"nag-aakyat"** instead of the Tagalog-expected ***umakyat***) — directly bearing
   on this project's extensive documentation of the -um-/mag- actor-trigger system (Lesson 14,
   `007c_basic_tagalog_for_foreigners.md`). The author quotes linguist **Ma. Lourdes S. Bautista**'s
   1989 conference paper directly (a short, attributable quotation, reproduced here at the same length
   the source itself used it): *"It is clear that the affix used for English verbs in actor focus is
   mag- and never -um- (perhaps because it is easier to use a prefix than an infix), and therefore
   this reinforces the predominance of mag- over -um-."* — i.e. a named linguist's own documented
   claim that regular affix selection is itself drifting in Filipino relative to Tagalog, with English
   verb-borrowing identified as the plausible mechanism.
4. **I- (benefactive) vs. -an (directional) trigger selection**: citing a suggestion from linguist
   **Barry Miller** and further analysis by **Teresita Ramos** and Bautista, the author contrasts
   Tagalog's preferred **i-** benefactive-trigger construction (**"Ibili mo ako ng hamburger"**, "Buy
   me a hamburger") against Filipino's preferred **-an** directional-trigger construction for the same
   meaning (**"Bilhan mo ako ng hamburger"**) — directly bearing on this project's own documentation of
   both the **i-** trigger (Lesson 32, `007e`) and the **-an** trigger (Lesson 33, `007e`), now with
   documented evidence that *which* trigger a speaker reaches for by default is itself a genuine
   dialectal marker distinguishing Filipino from Tagalog, not simply free variation.
5. **Consonant-cluster retention under reduplication**: Tagalog characteristically reduces a
   word-initial consonant cluster when reduplicating for the imperfective/present-tense (the author's
   example: **pri-** reduces to **p-** in Tagalog's reduplicated form of "presinta" [present/
   introduce]), while Filipino retains the full cluster (**nagpipresinta**, not a reduced form) — a
   genuine phonological-rule difference layered directly on top of the reduplication mechanism this
   project has documented extensively as one of Tagalog's most pervasive productive devices (Lessons
   1/11/14/15/18/22/32/33/34/42/44 across `007`–`007h`).
6. **Po/ho politeness particles as a specifically Tagalog (not pan-Filipino) feature**: the author
   argues that because Filipino draws on multiple Philippine languages/dialects beyond Tagalog
   (explicitly naming **Cebuano**, spoken by more Filipinos than Tagalog natively is, as a major
   contributor), and because Cebuano has **no equivalent particles to pô/hô** for marking deference,
   **Filipino should not obligatorily require pô/hô** the way Tagalog does — explicitly stating this
   does **not** mean Cebuano speakers are less respectful of elders, only that their language marks
   deference through different means than a dedicated particle. This is a significant, explicitly
   argued qualification on this project's own extensive documentation of pô/hô as a core Tagalog
   politeness mechanism (Lesson 3 onward, `007_basic_tagalog_for_foreigners.md`) — the author's claim
   is that this specific mechanism may be a Tagalog-substrate feature of Filipino rather than a
   pan-Filipino universal, worth flagging explicitly for this project's own typology note, which
   currently treats pô/hô documentation as applying to "Tagalog" without yet distinguishing a
   Filipino/Tagalog split.

The author closes (on the page beyond this excerpt's visible content) by predicting that Filipino will
likely undergo the same kind of historical drift away from its "parent" that English underwent relative
to its own dialectal origins, eventually being recognized as linguistically distinct from Tagalog the
way standard English is now recognized as distinct from the East Midland dialect it grew from — framing
the Filipino/Tagalog question as a live, actively-studied sociolinguistic debate (citing a named
competing view from linguist Ma. Lourdes Bautista that Filipino may simply be "a dialect of Tagalog")
rather than a settled question, which the author argues is a matter of definition/emphasis rather than
pure error on either side.

**Recommendation for this project's Tagalog typology note**: this essay is strong, directly-citable
evidence that **"Tagalog" and "Filipino" are not interchangeable labels** for this project's purposes —
the main 44-lesson sequence of this particular source (`007`–`007h`) teaches a variety the book itself
calls "Tagalog" throughout, but at least two of its most extensively documented grammatical mechanisms
(the -um-/mag- actor-trigger choice, and the i-/-an trigger choice) are specifically flagged by this
essay as sites of real, observed Filipino/Tagalog divergence — worth a follow-up note in
`00_Extraction_Checklist.md`'s "Morphological typology" section once this extraction wave's findings
are reviewed.

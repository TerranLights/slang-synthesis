**Morpheme breakdown (adjective pluralization via reduplication).** Tagalog adjectives pluralize by
reduplicating the first syllable of the root, not by suffixation — a genuinely compositional pattern
worth flagging per this project's morphological-typology convention even at this early, simple-looking
stage of the book:

> **mababaít** = ma- (adjective-forming prefix) + **ba-** (reduplicated first syllable of root) +
> **bait** (root: "kindness/goodness") — plural of **mabaít** "good/kind"; demonstrates productive
> CV-reduplication as the plural-marking mechanism for **ma-** adjectives, distinct from the
> noun-plural marker **mga**.

> **mahihírap** = ma- + **hi-** (reduplicated) + **hirap** (root: "poverty/difficulty") — plural of
> **mahírap**, same mechanism.

> **malulusóg** = ma- + **lu-** (reduplicated) + **lusog** (root: "health") — plural of **malusóg**,
> same mechanism; note the reduplicated syllable **lu-** copies the root's own first syllable, not a
> fixed template, so the copied material tracks the specific root.

## Grammar points

### Lesson 1 — The Tagalog Alphabet

The modern Filipino/Tagalog alphabet has 28 letters: the original 20 (5 vowels + 15 consonants,
adopted in the 1930s when Tagalog became the basis for the national language) plus 8 more added in
the 1980s (**c, f, j, ñ, q, v, x, z**) to accommodate loanwords and proper nouns. **Ñ** is pronounced
as a fused **n+y** (the book's worked example: *Niño* → **ninyo**, where **n** takes the preceding
vowel's sound and **y** takes the following vowel's sound). **Ng** is treated as a single consonant/
letter, pronounced by curling the back of the tongue toward the molars/roof of the mouth with a nasal
release; the syllable preceding **ng** is described as "riding on" that nasal sound. Tagalog **r** is
an unrolled single tongue-tap (closer to a Spanish single-tap **r** than to English **r**).
Pronunciation overall should be faster/sharper/less breathy than English, with no aspiration on
**p/k/t** — over-aspirating or using too many English-style long vowels marks a speaker as having an
American accent, which the book notes carries the (negatively coded) local label **"slang"** — i.e.
in Philippine English usage, *slang* denotes a person who speaks Tagalog with an American accent or
exaggerated vowels, not (as in general English usage) nonstandard vocabulary. **Worth flagging for
this project's mechanics-analysis phase**: this is a loanword sense-shift of the English word "slang"
itself within Philippine English, distinct from the general linguistic sense this project otherwise
uses the term for.

**Loanword adaptation rules (Spanish → Tagalog).** A systematic table of consonant/sound substitutions
converts Spanish spelling to Tagalog phonetic spelling: hard *c*→**k**, soft *c*→**s**, *ch*→**ts**,
*f*→**p**, soft *j*→**h** or **s**, *ll*→**ly**, *q*→**k**, *v*→**b**, *z*→**s** (worked examples:
*calesa*→**kalésa** "rig", *lechón*→**litsón** "roast pig", *final*→**pinál**, *cajón*→**kahón** "box",
*jabón*→**sabón** "soap", *calle*→**kálye** "street", *máquina*→**mákina**, *vapor*→**bapór** "ship",
*lápiz*→**lápis** "pencil"). **Loanword adaptation rules (English → Tagalog)** are given as a second,
longer table: soft *c*→**s** (*cinema*→**sine**), hard *c*→**k** (*academic*→**akadémik**), *ck*→**k**
(*gimmick*→**gímik**), *ct*→**k** (*addict*→**ádik**), *qua/q*→**kwa** (*quality*→**kwáliti**), long
*i*→**ay** (*driver*→**dráyber**), *f*→**p** (*traffic*→**trápik**), *v*→**b** (*believe*→**bilíb**),
long *o*→**u** (*approve*→**aprúb**), *-cle*→**kel** (*tricycle*→**tráysikel**), *-tion*→**syon**
(*institution*→**institusyón**), *x*→**ks** (*boxing*→**bóksing**), *j*→**dy** (*janitor*→**dyánitor**),
initial *s*→**is** (*sport*→**ísport**), soft *ch*→**ts** (*teacher*→**títser**), hard *ch*→**k**
(*school*→**iskúl**). The book notes a word may end up with *two* Tagalog equivalents when both a
Spanish-origin and an English-origin loan coexist (e.g. **efektiv** from English "effective" alongside
**mabísa**, the native Tagalog equivalent; **kwaliti** from English "quality" alongside **kalidád**,
itself a Spanish loan).

### Lesson 2 — Syllables and Stress in Tagalog

**Four syllable types**: V (one vowel, e.g. **o** in **tá-o** "person"), CV (consonant+vowel, e.g.
**ba** in **ba-sa** "read"), VC (vowel+consonant, e.g. **an** in **an-táy** "wait"), CVC
(consonant+vowel+consonant, e.g. **tak** in **tak-bó** "run"). A Tagalog syllable never exceeds three
sounds, counting **ng** as one letter/sound (hence **kumustá** has no **-must-** syllable, but
**pang-ápat** "fourth" and **hángin** "wind" legitimately have three-sound **pang**/**ngin**
syllables since **ng** counts as one unit). VC syllables mostly open a word (**an-táy**, **ak-yát**
"climb") or close one if the prior syllable ends in a vowel (**pa-ít** "bitter", **la-ot** "sea");
a word-medial VC split that strands an initial consonant from its vowel is flagged as a common
foreigner mispronunciation error (e.g. **bu-má-nat**, not ***bumá-nat***, for "to strike").

**Four stress types**, each marked with a different diacritic in this book's own orthographic
convention: (a) **end stress** — acute accent on the final syllable's vowel (**anák** "child",
stressed a-NAK); (b) **penultimate stress** — acute accent on the next-to-last syllable (**babáe**
"woman", stressed ba-BA-e); (c) **penultimate stress with a glottal catch** — grave accent on the
final vowel, the word carries both a strong penultimate stress *and* a glottal stop at the end
(**pusà** "cat", book phonetically notates the catch as a capital-Q placeholder: PU-saQ); (d) **end
glottal catch with no stress** — circumflex accent on the final vowel, faster pronunciation, glottal
stop present but the final syllable carries no special stress (**punô** "full", pu-noQ). **Stress is
phonemic**, not merely a pronunciation nicety — minimal pairs cited include **kaibígan** "friend" vs.
**kaibigán** "desire" vs. **káibigan** "mutual understanding" vs. **kaíbigan** "sweetheart" (four
distinct words from one string via stress placement alone), and **matúlog** "to sleep" vs. **matulóg**
"to fall asleep unintentionally". **The glottal catch is lost when a suffix is added after the final
vowel**: **batà** "child" (BA-taQ) → **kabatáan** "youth" (no glottal catch, stress shifts);
**luhà**→**luhaán**; **punô**→**punuín**; **susì**→**susián**. **Stress transfer onto monosyllables**:
a one-syllable word following a stressed word loses its own stress and takes on the stress of the
preceding word (**Kumain ká** "you may eat" is pronounced Ku-ma-in KA, not with its own stress on
**ká**). A hyphen is inserted between a consonant-final prefix with a glottal stop and a vowel-initial
root, purely to prevent mispronunciation (**pag-ása** "hope", **mag-alís** "to remove", **mag-isá**
"alone", **pag-íbig** "love").

### Lesson 3 — Greetings and Common Expressions

**Pô/hô** are deferential address particles (roughly "sir/madam") attached to greetings and responses
when speaking to elders, seniors, authority figures (parents, teachers, clergy, employers, police),
regardless of the speaker's own age; never used by an older speaker to a younger one, and never
between equals/peers. The formal/polite affirmative is **o-pô**/**o-hô** (vs. plain **oo**/**yes**
among equals). The core greeting template is **Magandáng [time-of-day] (pô)**, literally "beautiful
[day/morning/afternoon/evening]", built on the root **magandá** "beautiful"; **tanghálì** specifically
denotes the ~11am–1pm lunch window, not "afternoon" generally (**hápon** covers the broader afternoon
sense). A second politeness axis layers the *plural* pronouns **kayó**/**silá** ("to you-plural"/"to
them") onto a greeting to mark the addressee's seniority, vs. singular **iyó** for an equal; responses
use **rin**/**namán** ("too"). **Kumustá ka?** ("how are you") is a direct calque of Spanish *¿cómo
está?*, used symmetrically between people of any relative age/status once a baseline acquaintance
exists. Culturally notable: Filipino greetings routinely include asking after family members, framed
in the source as reflecting a broadly relational/other-oriented conversational norm. **"Where are you
going?" / "Where have you been?"** function as phatic greetings equivalent to "how's it going?", not
literal questions about itinerary — the expected/typical response is the vague **Diyán lámang (pô)**
"just nearby," though naming an actual destination is also acceptable. **Five distinct "excuse me"
expressions** exist for situations with no single English-equivalent collapse: **paumanhín pô**
(apologizing / general excuse-me, including bumping into someone), **pakiraán pô** (asking to pass
through a blocked passage), **mawaláng-gálang pô** (interrupting to speak, or asking directions from
someone already talking to another person — literally "may I lose my courtesy?"), **patáwad pô**
(apologizing for actual physical/emotional harm — explicitly *not* interchangeable with the milder
**paumanhín**), **pasintábi pô** (warning of forthcoming offensive/distasteful content, literally
"please set aside" — the stock phrase before graphic news footage). A long list of fixed colloquial
expressions is given with their shortened/contracted everyday forms (e.g. **Aywan ko** → **Éwan ko**;
**Hindí ko alám** → **Di ko alám**; **Ayaw akó** → **Ayóko**; **Kauntî lámang** → **Lang**; **Hintáy
ka** → **Teká**; **Magmadalî ká** → **Dalí ká**; **Táyo na** → **Téna**; **Totoó ba** → **Totoò**) —
this contraction pattern (full ceremonial/citation form vs. a fused colloquial everyday form) recurs
throughout the book and is itself a productive register-marking mechanism worth tracking in later
mechanics analysis. **Sigúro** ("maybe") is noted as semantically ambiguous on its own — its actual
force (confident "yes, definitely" vs. hedging "maybe" vs. uncertain "not sure") depends on how it's
deployed in the response, illustrated with three different answers to the same question **Sigúro ka?**

### Lesson 4 — The Articles Si/Siná and Ang/Ang mga

**Si** (singular) and **siná** (plural, "and companions/and the others") are name-marking articles
obligatorily preceding a person's name used as sentence subject (**Si Ruth ay babáe** "Ruth is a
woman"). **Ang** (singular) and **ang mga** (plural, **mga** pronounced "ma-NGA") mark non-person
nouns as subject (**Ang áso ay mabaít** "The dog is good"). **Mga** is a bare plural marker with no
independent meaning of its own. **Plurality is optional and can be marked on either the subject or
the predicate independently** — a sentence can mark plural on just the subject, just the predicate
(via adjective reduplication), or both, without changing the core meaning; **Ang mga batà at ang mga
áso ay mababaít** marks plural twice (mga + reduplicated adjective) but a single marking would also
be grammatical. Place names always take **ang** (never **si**), but the plural **mga** in a
place-name sentence attaches to the *adjective*, which must then itself take the reduplicated plural
form if there's more than one place-subject (**Ang Baguio at ang Tagaytáy ay malalamíg na mga
lugár** — plural marked on both **mga** and reduplicated **malamíg**→**malalamíg**). When two named
subjects share one predicate, the second subject's **ang** may be dropped without changing meaning.

### Lesson 5 — Sentence Formation

Tagalog has two basic sentence orders. **Subject-predicate order**: subject first, linked to the
predicate by **ay** (≈ English "is/are"), used when the *subject* is the sentence's emphasis (**Ang
báhay ay malakí** "The house is big"). **Ay** functions as a linking verb before an adjective/noun
predicate, or as an auxiliary before a main verb (**Ang batà ay kumákain** "The child is eating").
**Inverted/transposed order**: predicate first, subject last (or omitted), **ay** is dropped entirely,
and the emphasis shifts to the *predicate* (**Malakí ang báhay**; **Kumákain ang batà**). The source
explicitly notes **Tagalog speakers use the inverted order far more often in everyday speech** — the
subject-predicate/**ay**-marked order reads as more formal/literary by comparison. In inverted-order
sentences with a plural subject, the plural marker is bare **mga** (not **ang mga**) preceding the
subject noun(s) (**Mga batà sina Peter at Mary** "Peter and Mary are children"). The connective **at**
("and") cliticizes to **'t** after a vowel-final word (**áso't pusà** "dog and cat").

### Lesson 6 — Personal and Demonstrative Pronouns

**Personal pronouns** (nominative/subject form only, covered here): **akó** "I", **kamí** "we
(exclusive — excludes the addressee)", **táyo** "we (inclusive — includes the addressee)", **ikáw**
"you (singular)", **kayó** "you (plural, or singular-formal/deferential)", **siyá** "he/she
(gender-neutral)", **silá** "they". The **kamí/táyo** inclusive/exclusive distinction is a real
grammatical category Tagalog marks obligatorily that English collapses into one word "we" — worth
flagging as a structural feature with no direct English equivalent. **Ikáw contracts to ka** in
inverted-order sentences (predicate-first, pronoun-subject-last): **Masípag ka** "You are
industrious," vs. full **Ikáw ay masípag** in subject-predicate order. **Ay contracts to 'y** after
any vowel-final pronoun (**akó**→**ako'y**, **silá**→**sila'y**, etc.) — a general cliticization rule,
not pronoun-specific. **Kayó/silá can substitute for singular ikáw/siyá as a politeness/deference
strategy**, paralleling French *vous*-for-*tu* or similar T–V distinctions in other languages — worth
comparative note for later cross-language mechanics work on politeness-driven number mismatch.
**Demonstrative pronouns**: **itó** "this" (near speaker), **iyán** "that" (near addressee), **iyón**
"that, over there" (far from both speaker and addressee) — a three-way spatial deictic system (unlike
English's two-way this/that), each followed by **ay** in citation form. Plural demonstratives take
**ang mga** before the demonstrative (**ang mga itó** "these"). As *modifiers* (rather than standalone
pronouns), demonstratives precede the noun and may optionally repeat after it for emphasis, linked by
three distinct connective/ligature forms depending on the preceding word's final sound: **-ng** after
a vowel-final word (**itóng mésa**), **-g** after **iyán**/**iyón** specifically, and bare **na** after
a consonant-final noun (**báhay na iyón**) — the source flags this three-way ligature split
(**-ng**/**-g**/**na**) as a pattern to be developed further in the next lesson. In a sentence using
the locative marker **sa**, a following demonstrative always appears after the noun at the sentence's
end (**Pumunta kamí sa báhay na iyán** "We went to that house"), never in its pre-noun modifier
position.

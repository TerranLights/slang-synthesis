## Grammar points

### Lesson 34 — Adverbs of manner, place, time, and quantity

**Adverbs of manner** are formed productively by prefixing **pa-** to a verb root and introducing the
result with the article **nang** (**paupô** "sitting down," **patayô** "standing," **pahigâ** "lying
down") — the source explicitly draws out a subtle but important distinction from the superficially
similar English participle: a Tagalog **pa-**-adverb modifies a *verb* (how an action is performed),
while an English **-ing** participle modifies a *noun or pronoun* — the two constructions produce
similar-looking translations but are grammatically distinct categories, a genuine typological point
about which word class is being modified. **Word-order flexibility**: an adverb of manner may precede
or follow its verb with no meaning change, but **placement changes which ligature is used and whether
nang survives**: verb-first retains the article **nang** before the adverb; adverb-first instead
attaches a ligature (**-ng** after a vowel-final adverb, or **na** before a consonant-final named
subject) directly to the adverb, and **nang is dropped entirely** in this order — a clean three-way
interacting rule (word order ↔ ligature choice ↔ nang-presence) the source lays out explicitly with
worked examples. **Adverbs of place** are **sa**-headed phrases (sa baryo, sa kusinà, sa
eskuwelahán) continuing Lesson 26's preposition system, plus the dedicated deictic set
**díto/díyan/doón** and the productive "wherever" form **saanmán**, which the source notes **only
splits apart around the shortened pronoun ka** — every other pronoun follows the whole word intact, a
specific and narrow word-order exception. **Adverbs of time** compile a long list (continuing Lesson
28's temporal-deictic inventory and Lesson 10's na/pa particles) including several **near-synonym
clusters the source explicitly flags as free variants**: pirmí/paráti/lagí ("always"), búhat/
magbúhat/mulâ ("from"). **Noóng + date also means "last"** (noóng Lúnes = "last Monday") — a dual
function (marking indefinite past subordination, per Lesson 29, *and* marking "last [unit]" before a
calendar term) for the same particle. **Adverbs of quantity** (marámi, kauntî/kontî, walâ) recap
already-established core vocabulary reframed under this new grammatical category, with the source
noting **malakás kumain** ("eats strongly/forcefully") as a colloquial synonym specifically for
"eats a lot" — an idiomatic extension of an adjective (**malakás** "strong") into a quantity-adverb-
like collocation.

### Lesson 35 — Conjunctions and interjections

**Conjunctions**: **at** ("and," shortens to **'t** after a vowel) is the single most common
conjunction; **o** ("or"); four synonyms for "but" — **péro, nguni't, datapuwa't, subali't** — of
which the source explicitly marks the latter two as **not used in ordinary conversation** (a stated
formal/literary-only register restriction on two of four otherwise-synonymous conjunctions, a genuine
register-stratified near-synonym set worth flagging); **kung**/**pag** ("if," recapping Lesson 29);
**samantalá**/**habang** ("meanwhile/while," paired with present-tense verbs, recapping the
cross-reference planted back in Lesson 26); **nang** ("in order that/so that/so") — this is the
*third* distinct grammatical function this exact surface form **nang** performs across the book (ligature-
like linking particle in Lesson 7/34, past-time "when" subordinator in Lesson 29, and now a
purpose/result conjunction here) — a genuine case of extensive grammatical homonymy on one word-form
worth flagging explicitly for later mechanics work, since a learner (or, speculatively, a slang-forming
process) could plausibly reanalyze or collapse these distinct functions; **kundî** (shortened from
**kung hindî**, literally "if not," meaning "except/but"); **dáhil sa** ("because of"); **sapagká't**/
**mangyári** ("because," specifically answering a **bakit** question); **samakatuwíd**/**samakatwíd**
("therefore"); the correlative proverb template **kung anó...siyá rin** ("as...so is.../like...like...",
illustrated with the proverb "As the tree is, so is the fruit" — a genuinely idiomatic, culturally
resonant construction); **at sa wakás** ("finally, at last"). **Interjections** express emotion and are
explicitly marked by the source as **common in conversation but not used in formal writing** — a clean,
source-stated register split between spoken/informal and written/formal registers specifically for
this whole word class. The interjection set given (Ay!, Abá!, Aráy!, Nakú!, Sáyang!, Mabúhay!) spans
despair, surprise, pain, surprise-or-disbelief, pity, and welcome/adoration respectively. **Nakú** is
explicitly identified by the source as a shortened/grammaticalized form of the full phrase **Isáy ko
pô!** ("Oh my mother!") — and two further interjections (**Susmaryosep!**, a fusion of "Jesus, Maria y
Jose"; **Sus!**, short for "Jesus") are explicitly flagged by the source as reflecting **Tagalog's
Spanish Catholic heritage** — both are strong, source-acknowledged examples of religious invocations
fully bleached into secular exclamations, a productive mechanism (euphemistic/exclamatory clipping of a
religious phrase) worth flagging as a flagship candidate for later cross-language slang-derivation
comparison (directly comparable to English "gosh," "geez," "jeez," etc.).

### Lesson 36 — Magpa- and Pa- + -In verb forms (causative/directive trigger)

**Magpa-** (active) and **pa- + -in**/**pa- + -an** (passive) form a *causative* category distinct from
every prior affix in this book: they express **giving an order for something to be done to the
subject**, rather than the subject performing the action itself — the source explicitly contrasts this
with the passive **i-** verbs (Lesson 32), which express doing something *for* someone, not *ordering*
someone to do something. Formation for consonant-final roots: **magpa-** infinitive/imperative
unchanged, past **nagpa-**, present **nagpápa-** (reduplicating the prefix's own second syllable, the
same reduplication-target-is-the-prefix pattern already seen with maka-/paki-/maki-), future **magpápa-
**; the corresponding gerund/verbal-noun form is **pagpapa-** + reduplicated-prefix-syllable + root.
The passive counterpart **pa- + -in** infinitive/imperative is **pa-** + root + **-in** (or **-hin**
after a vowel-final root, continuing the familiar -in/-hin allomorphy), past infixes **-in-** between
the prefix's own first and second letters (**pinakáin**, not infixed into the root), present adds
reduplication of the *root's* first syllable on top of that, future reduplicates without the past
infix. **A genuinely important pragmatic-emphasis distinction** the source draws out explicitly with a
minimal-pair worked example: **Nagpakáin kamí ng mahihírap na táo** ("We fed the poor") emphasizes the
*giver* (kamí) as the sentence's agent-trigger subject, while its passive counterpart **Pinakáin
namín ang mahihírap na táo** emphasizes the *receiver* (mahihírap na táo) as the object-trigger
subject — both sentences translate into near-identical English but foreground a different participant,
a clean illustration of how trigger selection in this language is fundamentally an information-
structure/emphasis device layered on top of (not merely redundant with) its argument-marking function.
**Nagpagupít ng buhók** ("ordered a haircut," idiomatically "had a haircut") is given as a culturally
natural everyday collocation of this causative construction — getting a haircut is grammatically framed
as "ordering someone to cut [one's own] hair," not as a reflexive or simple-passive action.

### Lesson 37 — Expressing disagreement, agreement, and doubt (a function-word survey)

A broad, pragmatically-organized survey of single-word and short-phrase responses. **Disagreement**:
**hindî** (shortened **'dî**) is the general-purpose "no," explicitly *not* used to answer a may/
mayroón-type existence question (where **walâ** is correct instead — recapping Lesson 12's hindî/walâ
split); negating any word class (verb, adverb, pronoun, noun, substantive) can be done either by
placing bare **hindî** before it, or by prefixing the shortened **dî-**/**di-** directly onto it, with
a resulting stress-pattern difference the source notes explicitly (**hindî mabaít** retains stress on
both words; **di-mabaít** shifts to a single end-stress on **mabaít** alone). **Huwág** ("don't/stop")
governs a nominative pronoun (ka/kayó/táyo) when the following verb is active, or a possessive pronoun
(mo/nátin) when it's passive — directly paralleling the general active/passive pronoun-case pattern
already seen with pseudo-verbs (Lesson 17); it pairs with either the infinitive or future tense to
express a command or request. **Ayaw** ("dislike," the lexical opposite of gustó/íbig) follows the
same active/passive-pronoun-case split as huwág. **Agreement**: **oô** (plain "yes," reserved for
peers/equals) vs. **opô/ohô** (deferential "yes," recapping Lesson 3's politeness system);
**siyangâ** ("that's right/true" — the source explicitly warns against confusing this fixed idiomatic
expression with the near-homophonous two-word sequence **siyá ngâ**, "it is him/her indeed," a genuine
minimal-pair risk flagged outright); **totoó** ("true/indeed," which can substitute for oô in some
contexts); **talagá** ("yes, indeed/really," used to emphasize an adjective or affirm a statement's
truth — placed sentence-initially or near the adjective/adverb it emphasizes), which the source notes
has a **distinct colloquial, skeptical use** ("Really?" with raised eyebrows and rising intonation,
connoting disbelief) layered on top of its sincere affirming sense — a single word carrying both a
sincere and an ironic/skeptical reading purely via prosody, worth flagging for later
mechanics/pragmatics comparison work. **Doubt**: **maráhil**, **tíla**, **sigúro** (recapping earlier
lessons) plus **bakâ** ("might be") — which the source explicitly calls **"the most widely used"** of
the doubt-markers for being "shortest, most contemporary, and most pleasant," a genuine
register/frequency claim about an ordinary modal word (not itself tagged "slang" by the source, but a
telling example of real colloquial-register preference data volunteered for core vocabulary) — and
**maáarì**/**posíble**/**puwéde** ("it can be/possible"), with posíble and puwéde explicitly noted as
more common than maáarì specifically as one-word stand-alone responses.

### Lesson 38 — Forming gerunds (verbal nouns) from every verb-affix category

Generalizes and systematizes the ang/ng/sa nominalization already introduced in Lesson 30-31,
specifically for producing an English-gerund-equivalent ("-ing" noun) from each of the book's major
verb-affix categories, each with its **own distinct formation rule**: -um- verbs take **pag- + root**
(umalís → pag-alís); mag- verbs take **pag- + reduplicated first syllable + root** (maglarô →
paglalarô); -in- verbs take **pag- + root** (basáhin → pagbása, same rule as -um-, despite the
different active-voice affix); paki- verbs take **paki- + reduplicated final syllable of paki- + root**
(pakihirám → pakíkihirám) — a formation target (the prefix's own final syllable) distinct from every
other rule in this lesson; ma- verbs split by semantic domain: **pag- + root** for action senses
(maligò → pagligò "bathing") vs. **pagka- + root** for feeling/emotion senses (magálit → pagkagálit
"being angry/anger") — a genuine form-meaning split within a single affix category, where the *same*
prefix category (ma-) routes to two different gerund-forming prefixes depending purely on whether the
underlying verb denotes an action or an emotional state; -an verbs take **pag- + reduplicated first
syllable + root** (hugásan → paghuhúgas), the same reduplication pattern as mag-. **The resulting
gerund can itself be further marked with ang/ng/sa** exactly as any ordinary noun (Ang kanyáng pag-alís
ay ikinalungkót ko, "His departure made me sad") — confirming this gerund system plugs directly into
the general nominalization framework from Lesson 31 rather than constituting an independent subsystem.

### Lesson 39 — Forming participles using Naka-

**Naka-** prefixed to a noun or verb root forms a **participle** — a word that, like an adjective,
modifies a noun or pronoun by describing a state, condition, or appearance, with **no tense forms of
its own** (purely descriptive, unlike a true verb). The source draws an explicit, important
disambiguation: **naka-** participles must not be confused with the **past tense of a maka- ability
verb** (Lesson 18), which is also spelled with an initial **naka-** but functions as a true action verb
with its own tense paradigm — the two categories are **genuine surface-identical homographs**
distinguishable only by grammatical function/context, not spelling, a recurring theme in this book
(compare the stressed má-/maká- involuntary-action homographs from Lesson 18, and the ambiguous **nang**
conjunction from Lesson 35) that together suggest Tagalog's affix system produces a meaningful number of
form-identical-but-functionally-distinct homograph pairs — worth flagging as a structural feature of
this language's morphology relevant to later work on how slang or casual speech might exploit or collapse
such ambiguities. **Naka-** participles commonly describe posture (nakaupô "seated," nakatayô
"standing," nakahigâ "lying down"), clothing/accessory state (nakasalamín "wearing eyeglasses,"
nakasapátos "wearing shoes," nakamaóng "wearing jeans"), or being in/on a vehicle or conveyance
(nakakotse "in a car," nakakabáyo "on horseback," nakabús "riding a bus") — a broad, highly productive
"is in the state of [noun]" formation applicable across a wide range of concrete-noun roots.

# Tagalog — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Ngumiti ka naman! Tagalog with Humor (Lessons 1–46)

**Continuation:** Lesson 47 onward (PDF page 73 onward) continues in `006b_ngumiti_ka_naman.md` and
further lettered siblings.

**Source:** Peace Corps/Philippines (Paz B. Meman, Language Specialist; grammar/culture notes by
consultant Willie Muyargas), *Ngumiti ka naman! Tagalog with Humor: Language and Culture through
Cartoons*, Peace Corps Manila, Feb. 1992 (ERIC ED401738/FL024228), 172pp. Cartoon-based Peace Corps
pre-service-training reader: ~140 numbered "lessons," each built around a 1–3-panel newspaper
cartoon (mostly the strip *Baltic & Co.* by Roni Santiago, some by other cartoonists), with a short
vocabulary list, comprehension questions (drill — skipped per coverage rule), a grammar point, and a
culture note.

**Coverage note.** This source has no text layer — **vision-read** from rendered page images
throughout (confirmed via `pdftotext`: zero extracted characters). No handwritten marginalia was
found on any page read for this file; all content below is confirmed printed/typeset. The scanned
copy carries incidental scan-equipment artifacts (binding-shadow stripes, "BEST COPY AVAILABLE" /
ERIC stamps, a hand-numbered page stamp in the corner of each page) — these are scanning-pipeline
marks, not reader marginalia, and are not treated as content.

**Density/scope note.** The dispatch anticipated "moderate density"; the actual source is ~140
numbered lessons across 172 pages (~1–2 pages/lesson) — far denser than a typical grammar-reference
chapter. Per the extraction spec's sampling permission for this case, **full sequential coverage is
being attempted lesson-by-lesson** (not pre-emptive sampling) since the per-lesson vocabulary load is
small (3–8 items) and the token budget allows it; this file and its lettered siblings
(`006b`, `006c`, ...) will be split by page-count as each grows large, continuing until either the
book is exhausted or density/length forces a documented switch to representative sampling (which
will be flagged explicitly in whichever file it starts). **Comprehension-question sections are
uniformly skipped** as pedagogical drill per the coverage rule — they rephrase cartoon content as
yes/no or wh-questions and introduce no vocabulary beyond what's already captured. Culture notes are
paraphrased only where they carry a genuine register/usage/etymological finding (e.g. an idiom's
literal source, a loanword's origin); purely anthropological commentary (unrelated to language
mechanics) is omitted to honor copyright discipline.

**Register/trigger conventions.** Per `00_Extraction_Checklist.md`, verbal entries note their
trigger/focus category (actor-trigger/actor-focus "AT", goal/object-trigger "GT/OT", etc.) in the
Notes column, since the table has no dedicated trigger column. This book is a beginner-level
conversational reader, not a comparative/dialectal source — it marks no explicit regional/dialect
tags ( no `[B]`/`[C]`/`[S]`-style annotations anywhere found so far); Usage Tier is `core` for nearly
everything, with `colloquial` flagged only where the book's own culture/grammar note explicitly
frames a term as an informal idiom, clipped nickname, or figurative extension (e.g. **baltik**
"loose nut," the clipped vocative **Dyani**, the figurative extension of **naipit** "stuck" to mean
"caught by circumstance/bureaucracy").

## Grammar points

### Existential pair: *wala* / *may*
*Wala* expresses absence/non-existence and ligatures onto the word it governs (*walang pating*
"there's no shark"); *may* is its affirmative counterpart and does not ligature (*may bote* "there's
a bottle"). (Lessons 1, 7; pp.7, 15–16.)

### Ligature system: *na* / *-ng* / *-g*
A linker occurs between a modifier and the noun it modifies: *ng* after a vowel, *-g* after *n*, and
*na* after other consonants (e.g. *kalahating kanin* / *kalahati na kanin* "half a serving of rice";
*pating na kumakain* "shark that is eating"). This surface-identical *na* is separate from the
particle *na* "already" (Lesson 4), and the ligature *ng* is separate from the indefinite-object
marker *ng* (Lesson 1) and the possessive marker *ng* "of" (Lesson 3) — three grammatically distinct
functions sharing one phonological shape. (Lessons 1, 3, 4, 9; pp.7–8, 10–11, 18.)

### Topic marker *ang* and Actor-Topic vs. Goal-Topic verbs
*Ang* marks whichever noun is the sentence's topic — the Philippine-type "trigger"/"focus" system
drives which semantic role (actor, goal, etc.) can be marked this way. Actor-topic verbs (*kumakain*
"eats/is eating") put the doer in topic position; goal-topic verbs (*nakalimutan* "forgot",
*ginagamit* "using") put the undergoer/goal in topic position instead, with the actor instead marked
by *ko*-type pronouns. The book gives a worked minimal pair: *Kumakain ng pating ang babae* / *Kumakain
ang babae ng pating* "The woman is eating a shark" (actor topic) vs. embedding the same content as a
goal-topic relative clause, *Nakalimutan ko ang babaeng kumakain ng pating* "I forgot the woman who
eats sharks" (goal topic) — flagged by the book itself as a first pass at a system it promises to
revisit ("More of this later," p.19). (Lessons 1, 4, 9; pp.7, 11, 18–19.)

### Pseudo-verbs (*kailangan*) and the *mo*/*ko* pronoun set
*Kailangan* "need" belongs to a class of affix-less "pseudo verbs" that pattern like goal-topic verbs
for pronoun choice: the needer is marked with *mo*/*ko*-type pronouns rather than *ang*-marked actor
pronouns, and the needed thing can be indefinite (*Kailangan mo ang tulong* "Help is needed by you" /
"You need help") or definite (*Kailangan mo iyon* "That is needed by you"). (Lesson 6; pp.13–14.)

### Demonstrative sets (topic, locative/directional)
Tagalog has parallel three-way (near-speaker / near-listener / far) demonstrative paradigms for
different grammatical roles: **topic demonstratives** *ito/to* – *iyan/yan* – *iyon/yon* (Lesson 8);
**attention demonstratives** (calling the listener's attention to a referent) *eto/heto* –
*ayan/hayan* – *ayun/hayun* (Lesson 7); and **locative/directional demonstratives** *dito/rito* –
*diyan/riyan* – *doon/roon*, where word-initial *d* becomes *r* after a vowel (Lesson 11). (Lessons
7, 8, 11; pp.15, 17, 22.)

### Negation with *hindi*
*Hindi* "no, not" is followed directly by whatever particle/pronoun would otherwise follow the verb
it negates (*Kumakain siya* → *Hindi siya kumakain* "She is not eating"). (Lesson 10; p.21.)

### *sa* as locative and directional marker
*Sa* marks both static location ("in/at/on," *May zipper sa likod* "There's a zipper in the back")
and goal-of-motion ("to/toward," *Pupunta siya sa palengke* "S/he is going to the market"). (Lesson
10; p.21.)

### Repetitive/continuous action: reduplicated actor-topic verbs linked by *ng/nang*
An ongoing or repeated action is expressed by doubling an (optionally unaffixed) actor-topic verb
root and linking the two copies with *ng*/*nang*: *Tulo nang tulo ang gripo* "The faucet keeps on
dripping"; *Kain nang kain ang dyanitor* "The janitor keeps on eating." (Lesson 12; p.24.)

### *nasaan* vs. *saan* for "where"
*Nasaan* asks "where" when the sentence has no verb or adjective (*Nasaan ang babae?* "Where is the
woman?" → *Nasa likod* "At the back"); *saan* is used instead when the sentence does contain a verb
or adjective (*Saan siya kumakain?* "Where is s/he eating?" → *Dito siya kumakain* "S/he eats here").
(Lesson 11; pp.22–23.)

### *pang-* instrumental/purpose prefix
*Pang-* marks what something is used for or used with, typically prefixed to a noun or loanword
(*pang-dye* "for dyeing," *pang-ipit* "for pressing/squeezing"). (Lesson 11; p.23.)

### Honorific particle *ho/po*
A dedicated honorific/politeness particle inserted to address a social superior or stranger
respectfully — the clearest explicit register-coded item found in this source so far (p.26).
(Lesson 13.)

### Adverbial marker *nang* (distinct from the repetitive-action linker and the object marker)
*Nang* marks a following adverb describing how an action is performed (*Kumakain ako nang mabuti* "I
eat well"; *Tiningnan mo siya nang masakit* "You looked at him in a hurting way") — a third,
grammatically distinct function sharing a surface form with the lesson-1 object marker *ng/nang* and
the lesson-12 repetitive-action linker. (Lesson 14; p.29.)

### Goal-trigger (GT) verb paradigm, continued
Several further goal-trigger verbs are introduced across this span, consistently pairing an
actor-trigger (AT) root with a goal-trigger (GT) derived form the book glosses explicitly:
*tingin* (root) → *tumingin* (AT) / *tiningnan* (GT) "look (at)" (Lesson 14); *gawin* "do/make/fix"
and *sabihin* "say/tell," both usable as bare imperatives or with *kailangan* as infinitives (Lessons
12–13); *ilalagay* "will be put in," a future-aspect GT form (Lesson 15); *patawanin* "be made to
laugh," a causative GT form of root *tawa* (Lesson 17). (Lessons 12–15, 17; pp.24–33.)

### *kanino* — asking/answering possession
*Kanino* "whose" asks who something belongs to; answers use *kay* + a person's given name (*Kay Fred
ang tubig* "It belongs to Fred") or *sa* + a common noun (*Sa babae* "It's the woman's"). When
*kanino* modifies a following noun directly it takes a ligature (*kaninong sapatos* "whose shoes").
(Lesson 15; pp.30–31.)

### Comparative adjectives: *mas ... kaysa/kesa (sa)*
Comparison is formed with *mas* before the adjective and *kaysa*/*kesa* (optionally + *sa*)
introducing the standard of comparison (*Mas matangkad siya kaysa sa iyo* "He/she is taller than
you"). Pseudo-verbs like *kailangan* can take the same comparative frame (*Mas kailangan ko siya
kaysa kay Rudy* "I need him/her more than I need Rudy"). (Lesson 18; pp.34–35.)

### *Ma-* adjectives and root/noun alternation
Many Tagalog adjectives are formed by prefixing *ma-* to a root that can itself function as a noun:
*sakit* "pain" → *masakit* "painful"; *anghang* "spiciness" → *maanghang* "spicy"; *tanda* "old age"
→ *matanda* "old"; *tangkad* "tallness" → *matangkad* "tall." The same *ma-* prefix also
adjectivizes ordinary nouns (*laman* "content" → *malaman* "fleshy, loaded"; *tubig* "water" →
*matubig* "watery"; *yelo* "ice" → *mayelo* "icy"; *pating* "shark" → *mapating* "full of sharks").
(Lesson 20; p.38.)

### *gusto*/*ayaw* — the pseudo-verb pair for "want"/"don't want"
*Gusto* "like, want" and its negative *ayaw* "don't like, refuse" pattern exactly like *kailangan*
(Lesson 6): they take an *ng*-marked indefinite object (*Gusto ko ng kanin* "I want some rice"), an
*ang*-marked definite object (*Gusto ko ang kanin* "I like the rice"), or a personal object marked by
*si*/*kay* (*Gusto ko si Fred* "I want Fred"). *Ayoko* is a common contraction of *ayaw ko*. (Lesson
21; pp.40–41.)

### *Pa-* direct-request prefix
*Pa-* prefixed to a verb root forms a polite direct request, "let me .../may I ...": *Patingin ng
diyaryo* "Let me take a look at the newspaper"; *Palimos nga* "May I have some alms, please"; *Pasyut
nga* "May I take a shot, please." (Lesson 22; p.42.)

### *Na-* and *Ma-...-an*: ability/accidental-action goal-trigger verbs
Goal-trigger verbs prefixed with *na-* (e.g. *nabili* "was able to/happened to buy," *natapos* "was
able to/happened to finish," *nagawa* "was able to/happened to fix," *naipit* "happened to get
wedged," *nahawa* "happened to contaminate/infect," *nakain* "was able to eat") or formed with
*ma-...-an* (e.g. *malaman* "be known," *matulungan* "be able to help," *matingnan* "be able to look
at") express that an action was accomplished successfully or happened accidentally/without full
control — contrasted by the book with plain goal-trigger forms like *nakalimutan* "forgot." (Lesson
23; pp.43–44.)

### Full topic-pronoun set and the inclusive/exclusive "we" distinction
The complete topic-pronoun paradigm: *ako* "I," *ka* "you" (sg.), *siya* "he/she," *tayo* "we"
(speaker+hearer, inclusive), *kami* "we" (speaker(s) only, exclusive), *kayo* "you" (pl.), *sila*
"they." Tagalog grammatically distinguishes inclusive *tayo* from exclusive *kami* where English
collapses both to "we." (Lesson 23; p.44.)

### *Kita*: a dedicated actor+goal pronoun portmanteau
Where the actor is "I" and the goal is "you" in the same clause, Tagalog uses a single dedicated
portmanteau pronoun *kita* instead of separately marking *ko* (actor "I") and *ka*/*ikaw* (goal
"you"): *Gusto kita* "I like you"; *Kailangan kita* "I need you"; *Nakalimutan kita* "I forgot you."
(Lesson 26; p.48.)

### *Naka-* stative prefix
*Naka-* marks a state resulting from a prior action — "to be in the state of": *Nakapikit siya*
"His/her eyes are closed"; *Nakarelo si Tim* "Tim is wearing a watch"; *Nakatuwalya tayo* "We are
using towels"; *Nakabote ang tubig* "The water is bottled." (Lesson 26; p.47.)

### Time pronouns
A closed set of time words patterns like pronouns/connectors: *noon* "before, then," *kahapon*
"yesterday," *kagabi* "last night," *kanina* "a while ago," *ngayon* "now, today," *mamaya* "later
(today)," *bukas* "tomorrow." *Noon* additionally serves as a past-time connector ("when," for past
events): *Kumakain siya noong tumingin ka* "He/she was eating when you looked." (Lessons 27–28;
pp.49, 51.)

### Basic sentence word order: Comment/Predicate–Topic/Subject, and the *ay*/*'y* inversion
Tagalog's unmarked word order places the comment/predicate first and the topic/subject second
(*Nagsuicide sila* "They committed suicide," literally "committed-suicide they"). An alternative,
more formal/written order fronts the topic and links it to the comment with the inversion particle
*ay* (reduced to *'y* after a vowel): *Sila'y nagsuicide* / *Sila, nagsuicide* "They committed
suicide." (Lesson 29; p.52.)

### Final-syllable stress as a word-class/meaning contrast
A recurring pattern across many lessons: the same root distinguishes an unstressed-final verb/root
reading from a final-stressed adjective/state reading — *tulog*/*tulóg* "sleep"/"asleep,"
*gising*/*gisíng* "wake up"/"awake," *tapos*/*tapós* "finish"/"finished," *sira*/*sirâ*
"break/ruin"/"broken, ruined, crazy," *gamit*/*gamít* "use, belonging"/"used," *ayos*/*ayós*
"fix, repair"/"fixed, okay," *malaman*/*malamán* "be known"/"fleshy, loaded" (cf. Lesson 23). This is
a genuine suprasegmental (stress-based) morphological contrast, not spelling variation — worth
flagging prominently for later mechanics analysis since English-centric treatment would miss it
entirely. (Lessons 20, 23, 32, 36, 41; pp.38, 43, 56, 60, 66.)

### *Maka-* ability/accidental actor-trigger prefix
Parallel to the goal-trigger *na-*/*ma-...-an* ability pattern (Lesson 23), *maka-* forms an
actor-trigger (AT) verb meaning "to be able to" or "to happen to" do the root action: *makakanta*
"can sing," from *kanta* "sing." (Lesson 37; p.61.)

### Full *ko*-set (ng-marked actor/possessor pronouns)
A second full pronoun paradigm, parallel to the topic-pronoun set (Lesson 23) and used for actor/
possessor marking: *ko* "by me, my," *mo* "by you, your," *natin* "by us (incl.), our," *niya* "by
him/her, his/her," *namin* "by us (excl.), our," *ninyo* "by you (pl.), your," *natin* "by us (incl.
pl.), our," *nila* "by them, their." (Lesson 37; p.62.)

### Polite plural/formal second person: *ninyo*/*nyo*
Distinct from singular *mo*, *ninyo* (contracted *nyo*) marks a plural or politely-formal "you" —
another explicit politeness-register form alongside *ho/po* (Lesson 13). (Lesson 34; p.58.)

### *may* vs. *mayroon*
*May* and its free-standing counterpart *mayroon* are interchangeable before a noun (*May pating
doon* / *Mayroong pating doon* "There is a shark there"), but only *mayroon* can stand alone as a
one-word answer, since *may* is a bound form that always needs something ligatured after it. (Lesson
35; p.59.)

### Numbers as ligatured modifiers
Numbers pattern like adjectives and take a ligature when modifying a following noun: *isang bote*
"one/a bottle," *dalawang lalaki* "two boys/men," *tatlong ulo* "three heads," *apat na babae* "four
girls/women," *limang matalas na ulo* "five sharp heads." (Lesson 44; p.69.)

### *Dapat* as a modal qualifier
*Dapat* "ought to, should, must" joins the qualifier word-class (Lesson 34) and precedes the verb it
modifies, optionally linked with *na*: *Dapat (na) kumain si Val* "Val has to eat"; *Dapat mong gawin
ang TV* "You've got to fix the TV." (Lesson 45; p.70.)

### Emphatic topic sentences with *sino*-questions
A topic can be emphasized/isolated by rephrasing a sentence as a *sino* "who" question and
answering with *ang*: *Malungkot si Al* "Al is sad" → *Sino ang malungkot?* "Who is sad?" → *Si Al
ang malungkot* "**Al** is sad" (emphatic). The same frame works with any predicate type (comparative,
actor-topic verb, goal-topic verb). (Lesson 46; p.72.)

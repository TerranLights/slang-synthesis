# Tagalog — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Making Out in Tagalog, Introduction + Chapters 1–5

**Source:** Renato Perdon, *Making Out in Tagalog*, Tuttle Publishing / Periplus Editions (HK) Ltd.,
copyright 2003 (previously published 2003 as *Making Out in Filipino*, ISBN 0-8048-3373-7; this
edition ISBN 0-8048-3693-0), printer's key "10 09 08 07 06 05 / 8 7 6 5 4 3 2" indicating a ~2005
printing. Covers front matter, Introduction (pp. 4–12), Chapter 1 "What's Up?" (pp. 13–21), Chapter 2
"Basic Phrases" (pp. 22–25), Chapter 3 "Got a Minute?" (pp. 26–32), Chapter 4 "Hey There!"
(pp. 33–36), and Chapter 5 "Look at That!" (pp. 37–38).

**FLAGSHIP SLANG SOURCE** — the "Making Out In..." series has produced strong results for this
project in Japanese and other languages; extracted with extra care per dispatch instructions.

**Coverage note:** Vision-read from a rendered-page-image scan; confirmed via `pdftotext` that this
PDF (91 pp.) has no usable text layer. Pages were rendered 1:1 with the PDF at 200 dpi (no
two-pages-per-image spread, unlike some other vision-read sources in this project) and the printed
page numbers were read directly off each page header/footer — empirically confirmed mapping:
printed page = PDF page − 2 (e.g. PDF page 15 = printed page 13, the start of Chapter 1), holding
consistently through the whole book (content ends at printed p. 88 = PDF p. 90; PDF p. 91 is a
back-matter ad for other titles in the series, not book content). No handwritten marginalia was
found anywhere in this range — all content is clean typeset print matching the surrounding page.
**Integrity note on this extraction's process:** an early batch of image reads in this session
silently failed to deliver image content (tool returned "media removed" placeholders) while draft
notes were nonetheless being composed; this was caught before writing any output, and every page
cited below was re-read and its content visually confirmed before being recorded here — nothing in
this file rests on an unconfirmed read.

This is a situational phrasebook (not alphabetical), organized by topic/chapter, structured as
parallel English/Tagalog phrase pairs rather than a word list. Per this project's coverage rule,
every distinct phrase/term is captured; a few genuinely repetitive morphological drills (the full
1–31 ordinal-number paradigm, the fixed day/month-name lists) are captured as a compact paradigm
note under Grammar points rather than as dozens of near-identical vocabulary rows, since the pattern
is productive and mechanical rather than lexically distinct per item. The book's own introduction
frames its register explicitly: this is the "casual, unbuttoned" street Tagalog of Metro Manila and
other large cities/provincial capitals, distinguished by the author from the "complex grammatical
twists" of formal textbook Tagalog — so most conversational entries below are tagged `colloquial`
rather than `core`, reserving `core` for genuinely register-neutral grammatical items (pronouns,
particles, numbers). Per the Tagalog checklist's column convention, verbal-affix items note their
trigger/focus category where the book's own paradigm makes it identifiable; most phrase-level
entries in this chunk are fixed/idiomatic enough that focus marking isn't separately meaningful.

Two apparent print/scan typos found and flagged rather than silently corrected: p. 9's pronoun table
lists "We (including the person addressed)" as *toyo*, which is almost certainly a typo for the
standard inclusive-we pronoun *tayo* (used correctly as *tayo* throughout the rest of the book,
including this same chunk) — not extracted as the pronoun entry, but cross-referenced in its own
Notes since *toyo* is also a genuine, distinct slang word in this book (Ch. 1, "moodiness"). p. 36
has "nagsasalitta" for what should be "nagsasalita" ("speak") — likely a doubled-letter scan/print
artifact, transcribed as printed with a note.

## Grammar points

### Topic-comment sentence structure
Tagalog sentences default to comment-before-topic order (*Tumakbo si Juan* = "ran [comment] John
[topic]" = "John ran"), the reverse of English's topic-first order — the book calls this "possibly
the most striking difference between Tagalog and English." An English-like topic-first order is also
possible, linked by the particle *ay* (*Si Juan ay tumakbo*) (p.9).

### Case-marking particle system (ang/ng/sa, si/ni)
Common nouns take *ang* (subject marker) vs. *ng* (object/possession marker); personal names take
the parallel *si* (subject) vs. *ni* (possession) — e.g. *Tumakbo si Juan* "John ran" vs. *Mga anak
ni Juan* "John's children." *sa* is the general all-purpose locative/temporal preposition ("in,"
"on," "at") (pp.9–11).

### No plural inflection — mga marker instead
Tagalog nouns don't mark singular/plural by changing form; the separate word *mga* precedes a noun
to mark plurality instead (*mga aklat* "books") (p.9).

### No copula — ay and may/mayroon substitute for "to be"/"to have"
Tagalog lacks a verb equivalent to most uses of English "to be"; the invariant particle *ay* fills
that role in topic-first sentences. Separately, *may* (or *mayroon*) covers most uses of English "to
have" or "there is" (pp.9–10).

### Verb inflection via prefix/reduplication/infix, not suffix
Unlike English or the Romance languages the book implicitly contrasts with, Tagalog verb tense/aspect
changes happen at the beginning or middle of the word — through prefixes, reduplicated syllables, and
infixes — rather than at the end (p.10). The base form alone serves as an imperative/command, with
*nga* added for politeness and *huwag* substituted for negative commands (p.10).

### Gender-neutral third-person pronoun
*Siya* ("he/she") and *sila* ("they") carry no grammatical gender distinction — a single form covers
both English "he" and "she" (p.10). Worth flagging for the mechanics-analysis phase as a structural
constraint on how any synthesized slang could mark/avoid gender.

### po/ho politeness-register system
Tagalog marks formality primarily through a sentence-level particle (*po*, with *ho* as a casual
variant) rather than through a separate pronoun register — contrast *Oo*/*Hindi* (casual yes/no) with
*Opo*/*Hindi po* (polite yes/no) (pp.24–25).

### Taglish/Engalog code-switching as a named, productive register
The book names and worked-examples a widespread code-mixed register — Taglish (Tagalog-dominant with
English insertions) and Engalog (English-dominant with Tagalog insertions) — attributing its spread
partly to an official national-language policy that permits loanwords, and partly to ordinary contact
effects in Manila. Not treated as a fringe phenomenon but as how many urban Filipinos actually talk
(pp.11–12). A directly relevant precedent for how borrowing-driven slang could work in a synthesized
conlang.

### Ordinal-number and calendar-date paradigm (not tabulated row-by-row)
Ordinals are formed productively with the prefix *ika-* + cardinal number (*ikalawa* "second,"
*ikatlo* "third," ... up to *ikatatlumpu't-isa* "thirty-first"), used specifically for calendar dates;
days of the week (*Lunes–Linggo*) and months (*Enero–Disyembre*) are a closed, Spanish-loanword set.
Per this project's coverage rule, the full 1–31 ordinal run and the fixed day/month lists are treated
as one productive paradigm rather than ~50 near-identical vocabulary rows (pp.26–32).

### toyo as a slang term for moodiness (semantic shift from a foodstuff)
*Toyo* (lit. "soy sauce") is explicitly glossed by the book as slang for an unstable, swinging mood
("bad to good to naughty"), used in *May toyo ka sa ulo!* "You're being irrational!" — alongside
*sira (ang) ulo*, *ulol*, and *gago* as near-synonyms for "craziness" (p.18). A clean example of
`semantic_shift`/`metaphor_productivity` (foodstuff → mental-state metaphor) worth carrying into the
mechanics-analysis phase; distinct from the likely-typo *toyo* appearing in the p.9 pronoun table
(see the file-level note above).

### Curse-word softening: Lintik/Letsi as customary alternatives to a literal curse
The book names *Lintik!* ("lightning," i.e. "may you be struck down") and *Letsi!* (euphemistic, from
Spanish *leche* "milk") as the customary Filipino curse exclamations, offered as an aside rather than
a formatted phrase pair, in preference to a literal translation of "Damn!" (*Mapahamak sana!*) (p.19).
A candidate `euphemism_chain` finding (a taboo curse softened via an unrelated food-word substitute)
for the mechanics-analysis phase.

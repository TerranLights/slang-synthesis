# Tagalog — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Advanced Tagalog Reader (representative sample)

**Source:** Teresita V. Ramos, Ph.D. (Principal Investigator), *Advanced Tagalog Reader*, Dept. of
Indo-Pacific Languages, University of Hawaii. Project funded by the International Research and
Studies Program (Title VI), U.S. Dept. of Education (Sept. 1, 1983 – Dec. 31, 1984). PDF: `12
Advanced Tagalog Reader.pdf`, 471 PDF pages total.

**Representative sampling note (per dispatch instruction, prioritizing register/idiom/
colloquialism content).** This is a 30-lesson reading anthology (real Tagalog-language magazine
articles, mostly from the magazine *Sagisag*, 1975–1979, plus some literary/folkloric selections),
organized into 14 thematic categories labeled with Filipino-alphabet letters (A through NG):
A. Kasaysayan (History, lessons 1–5), B. Pulitika (Politics, 6), K. Edukasyon (Education, 7),
D. Wika (Language, 8–9), E. Pagbubukid (Farming, 10), G. Pamamalakad ng Pangkabuhayan (Economic
management, 11–12), H. Karunungang Panlipunan (Social knowledge/etiquette, 13–20), I. Panggagamot
(Medicine, 21), L. Relihiyon (Religion, 22–23), M. Panitikan (Literature, 24–26), N. Sining (Art,
27–29), NG. Musika (Music, 30). Each lesson follows the same four-part exercise structure (per the
book's own front matter): vocabulary expansion exercises, text analysis exercises, discussion
questions, and a composition activity. Exhaustive extraction of all 30 lessons was out of scope;
the sample below deliberately prioritizes **Lesson 8 ("Taglish o Engalog... Pilipino o Filipino")**
and **Lesson 19 ("Alisin 'Ka Mo ang Dyipni? Teka Muna...")**, the two readings most directly
relevant to this project's register/code-switching/colloquialism focus, plus **Lesson 1** as a
foundational baseline sample and the front-matter exercise-type taxonomy for structural context.

**Vision-reading note.** This source has no text layer (`pdftotext`/`pdffonts` both confirm no
extractable text, consistent with a fully image-scanned typewritten manuscript) — every entry
below was read visually from rendered page images. The typewritten print is dense but consistently
legible; the source itself marks certain words with underlining (indicating vocabulary-exercise
target words) rather than any handwritten annotation — **no handwritten marginalia or prior-owner

## Front matter — the reader's own exercise taxonomy (p. ix–x)

Paraphrased from the General Introduction: each lesson's exercises (*Pagsasanay*) have four parts.

1. **Vocabulary expansion exercises** (*Pagpapalawak ng Bokabularyo*) — ten sub-types: topic/
   discipline-grouped word sets; meaning-related words (same word or root, different meanings);
   style/appropriateness-of-word exercises; comparison-formation exercises; descriptive-word
   exercises; grammar-related exercises (affixes, word formation, aspect, linkers); synonym/antonym
   substitution for italicized words; new-word-building via affixation; sentence-construction with
   new words; word-definition matching.
2. **Text analysis exercises** (*Pagsasanay Tungkol sa Sanaysay*) — six sub-types: cloze-type
   exercises; interpretation of words/phrases/metaphorical expressions/rituals/concepts; concept
   categorization; term-to-concept/characteristic matching; event sequencing; referent
   identification ("who/what is referred to").
3. **Discussion questions** (*Pagtalakay*) — comprehension and critical-engagement questions tying
   the reading to the student's own views/experience.
4. **Composition** (*Pagkatha*) — free-writing responses, including condensation/summary tasks.

Notably, the vocabulary-exercise taxonomy explicitly calls out "appropriateness of words (style)"
and "metaphorical expressions" as named categories — i.e. register and figurative-language
awareness are built into this reader's own pedagogical design, not an incidental byproduct.

**Grammar/usage point:** the reading repeatedly uses the reportative evidential particle **daw**
(e.g. "ang dagat daw na nakapaligid," "ang mga lupaing ito daw ay nagsilbing tulay") to mark
information as hearsay/received wisdom rather than the author's own direct assertion — here, framing
the land-bridge migration theory as something anthropologists claim ("sang-ayon sa teorya ng mga
antropologo...") rather than settled fact. This is the same enclitic the Filipino Basic Course's
index lists under "enclitics – kaya, naman, pala, tuloy, ba, po, ho, na, lang/lamang, rin/din" and
"enclitics – pa, nga, raw/daw, kasi, man, muna, sana, yata" (see `009b_filipino_basic_course_sample.md`)
— a good example of real running text putting one of those enclitics to visible rhetorical use.

## Lesson 8 — "Taglish o Engalog... Pilipino o Filipino" ("Taglish or Engalog... Pilipino or
Filipino"), by Liwayway Arceo (p. 127)

**Flagship finding for this project's register/code-switching focus.** This essay is itself a
native-speaker's meta-commentary on *bad* English-to-Tagalog loanword adaptation in sensationalist
tabloid journalism — directly relevant evidence for how borrowing/code-mixing mechanics can fail,
not just succeed, which is exactly the kind of boundary case this project's slang-mechanics
analysis phase will want.

**The anecdote the essay opens with:** a Tagalog-language tabloid headline reading literally "Bata,
Ni-rape..." was unintelligible to the author and a companion when sounded out syllable-by-syllable
in the Tagalog manner ("Ba-ta... ni-ra-pe") — because the headline is actually English ("Child
Raped...") transliterated into Tagalog orthography with a Tagalog-style passive-voice prefix
*ni-* grafted onto the untranslated English root *rape*. The essay argues this is read-as-Tagalog
but means-as-English, producing a genuine comprehension failure for a fluent Tagalog reader
reading it as its own language. Only encountering the full sentence (which happens to use the
Spanish-derived "anyos"/"años" for the child's age) let the readers piece together that the
headline was a badly Filipinized English sentence, not genuine Tagalog.

**The essay's own grammatical objection**, paraphrased: the Tagalog verbal prefix system (what the
essay calls "panlapi," affix, specifically the "unlaping *ni-*") is not grammatically appropriate to
attach to an untranslated English root ("salitang-ugat na Ingles") like *rape* — i.e. the writer is
making an explicit, native-speaker-authored claim about **productive-affix compatibility
constraints**: a Tagalog grammatical affix doesn't freely combine with any foreign root just because
the root is phonetically naturalized. This is a genuinely useful boundary condition for this
project's own `morphological_play` derivation type (`00_Usage_Tier_Taxonomy.md`) — evidence that
native speakers perceive some affix+foreign-root combinations as ungrammatical/communicatively
broken rather than merely informal.

**The essay's second example — semantic drift through borrowing, not just phonological
transliteration:** a headline "Dalaga, Inabuso" ("Young woman, abused"). The essay traces that
*abuso* has **two independently-borrowed senses layered on the same Tagalog word**: (1) an older
sense inherited from Spanish (three-plus centuries of colonial contact, per the essay's own
framing) where *abuso* means something like "taking advantage of/overstepping," as in child-directed
scolding "*Abuso na 'yan, ah!*" or "*Umaabuso ka na!*" (roughly "you're overstepping now!" — a mild
behavioral correction, not a legal/criminal register); and (2) a newer sense borrowed directly from
English "abuse," closer to "ginahasa" (raped) in the tabloid-headline use the essay is critiquing.
The essay's own conclusion is that readers relying on the older, native-feeling sense of *abuso*
will misread the newer headline's actual meaning — **a documented real case of a single borrowed
word carrying two non-overlapping senses from two different donor-language contact periods**,
which is directly the kind of finding `00_Historical_and_Geographic_Context_Guide.md`'s
`attested_era` field exists to capture once this reaches the mechanics-analysis phase.

to in the analysis phase as a primary-source attestation of what educated Filipino speakers of the
period considered *failed* vs. *successful* borrowing.

## Lesson 19 — "Alisin 'Ka Mo ang Dyipni? Teka Muna..." ("You'd Remove the Jeepney? Wait a
Moment..."), by Valerio L. Nofuente (p. 282)

An essay defending the jeepney (*dyipni*, from English "jeepney," itself a blend of "jeep" +
"jitney") as cultural artifact, written against a 1980s government proposal to ban jeepneys from
Metro Manila's main roads. Good representative sample of **everyday, English-loanword-dense urban
register** describing modern city life — contrasted usefully against Lesson 1's more formal/
literary register and Lesson 8's metalinguistic register.

**Title-phrase register note.** The title itself is colloquial, not standard-formal Tagalog: *"Alisin
'Ka Mo"* uses the casual contracted sequence *'ka mo* (a colloquial run-together of a question-tag-
like construction), and *"Teka Muna"* ("hold on/wait a moment") is itself a colloquial interjection
(*teka* being a clipped/colloquial form of *hintay ka*, "you wait") — the kind of casual-register
marker that would not appear in a formal-register title, flagged by the source's own title choice as
deliberately conversational/argumentative in tone.

**Grammar/usage point — simile-stacking as a rhetorical register marker:** the essay describes the
jeepney's design via a chain of similes/comparisons in close succession — "the design is a mix of
whatever [parts], like the ingredients of *pinakbet* [a mixed vegetable dish], and as colorful as
the Ati-atihan festival in Aklan. The inside is as noisy as the Divisoria market, yet as
'religious'-feeling as the Quiapo church" (paraphrased). This dense, culturally-loaded
simile-stacking is itself a register marker — a journalistic/persuasive-essay style distinct from
both Lesson 1's descriptive-geography register and Lesson 8's metalinguistic-argument register,
worth noting as a third register sample point from just these three lessons.

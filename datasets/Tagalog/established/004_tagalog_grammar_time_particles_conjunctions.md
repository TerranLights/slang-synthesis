**Morpheme breakdown.** Most items in this file are closed-class particles/conjunctions/time-nouns
without internal compositional structure worth decomposing. Two patterns merit a formal note:

> **Gaano ka-[root]** = gaano ("how," degree interrogative) + ka- (degree/intensity-marking
> prefix) + root — e.g. *gaano kalaki* "how big" = gaano + ka- + *laki* ("size/bigness"). This is
> the fourth distinct grammatical function this project has now found for the morpheme *ka-* in
> Tagalog (descriptive-adjective prefix, recently-completed-action marker, comparative-equality
> component in *kasing-*, and now this degree-question marker) — see the cross-file ka- polysemy
> note below.

> **taun-taon** (and the parallel buwan-buwan/linggo-linggo/araw-araw/gabi-gabi/oras-oras/
> minu-minuto set) = full reduplication of the bare time-noun (taon "year" → taon-taon "every
> year"). Distinct from both the aspectual CV-reduplication (file 003) and the adjectival
> full-root reduplication (file 002) in that it reduplicates a *noun*, not a verb root or adjective
> root, to derive a habitual-adverb reading — a third distinct morphosyntactic category hosting the
> same general reduplication mechanism.

## Grammar points

### The ka- morpheme: one shape, (at least) four jobs

Tracking this one prefix across the four files extracted so far in this wave is worth flagging as a
standalone cross-file finding: Tagalog *ka-* independently surfaces as (1) a descriptive-adjective-
forming prefix in reduplicated forms like *kahindik-hindik* "horrible" (file 002); (2) the
"recently-completed-action" aspectual marker in constructions like *kakakain ko lang* "I just ate"
(file 002); (3) a bound component of the comparative-equality prefixes *kasing-*/*sing-* (file 002);
and now (4) the obligatory degree-marker on the adjective/modifier that follows the interrogative
*gaano* (this file). Whether these four are genuinely one polysemous morpheme or several historically
distinct but synchronically homophonous affixes is not something this pedagogical source addresses
directly — flagged here as a question worth revisiting once a more theoretically explicit grammar
(or file 005's academic treatment, which covers a different but related set of ka- facts around
comparatives and superlatives) is cross-referenced in a later wave.

### The Tagalog "timeline": past/present/future time-indicator system

Since Tagalog verbs mark aspect rather than tense (file 003), actual temporal placement in
past/present/future is carried entirely by separate adverbial time-indicator words, organized by the
book into three timeline zones (NAKARAAN/past, KASALUKUYAN/present, HINAHARAP/future), each further
subdivided by recency/remoteness (e.g. past: kanina "earlier today" > kahapon "yesterday" >
kamakalawa "day before yesterday" > noon "more remote past"). Habitual/repeated actions are marked
by three parallel but distinct strategies: **tuwing** + specific time, **bawat** + general time
expression, or full reduplication of the time-noun itself (taun-taon, araw-araw, etc.) — three
morphologically unrelated routes converging on the same "every X" semantics.

### Telling time: three coexisting numeral systems

Tagalog speakers maintain three numeral systems for telling time, in active parallel use rather than
one having fully displaced the others: **Spanish-derived** forms (ala-una, alas-dos...) are
described as "the most widely used," **native Tagalog ika-** ordinal forms are reserved for formal/
ceremonial register (broadcast media, conferences, formal invitations), and **English numerals** are
reported as an emerging preference "among young people." This is the same three-way numeral
coexistence already flagged for cardinal counting in file 001, now confirmed to extend specifically
into the clock-time domain with an explicit generational dimension — a strong candidate site for
register-marked code-switching or slang innovation in this project's later mechanics-analysis phase.

### Enclitic particles: the "spice" of the sentence

Enclitic particles are described as grammatically optional but pragmatically load-bearing — omitting
them makes Tagalog sound "awkward and stilted." They split into two functional classes: **temporal**
(PA "still/yet," NA "already/now," MUNA "first/a while") marking an action's started/not-yet-started
status, and **modal** (NGA, LANG, PALA, NAMAN, TALAGA) marking the speaker's attitude, certainty, or
register stance toward the statement. Several of these particles are strikingly multifunctional
within their own entries — **NGA** spans affirmation and *challenge* (near-opposite pragmatic
effects), and **NAMAN** spans politeness-softening, assertion-of-an-unexpected-truth, intensification,
and (combined with NA) repetition-marking. This degree of single-particle pragmatic overload is a
notable structural feature for later slang/register analysis: a small closed set of short, frequent
particles carrying this much context-dependent pragmatic weight is a natural site for novel/slang
particle usages to emerge by subtly shifting which of a particle's existing functions gets
foregrounded.

### Questions II: BAKIT's three-tier register ladder, and PAANO/GAANO/MAGKANO

**BAKIT** ("why") is distinguished from the other content-question words covered here by having an
explicit, source-stated three-way formality ladder of answer-forms: **sapagkat** (most formal,
rare in speech, literary) > **dahil** (neutral, used in both speech and writing) > **kasi** (most
common in ordinary conversation). This is one of the few places in the whole source where the book
itself explicitly ranks register tiers rather than leaving the relative formality of near-synonyms
implicit — directly useful as a template for how this project should look for similar explicit
register ladders in future Tagalog sources. **PAANO** ("how," process/method) is explicitly
distinguished from the English social-greeting sense of "how" ("How are you?"). **GAANO** ("how,"
degree/extent) obligatorily combines with a ka-prefixed modifier (see the ka- cross-file note
above). **MAGKANO** is the dedicated price/value "how much" word, distinct from gaano's broader
degree sense. All four, like sino/ano/saan (file 002) and the pseudo-verbs (file 003), attract the
actor pronoun to sit immediately after the question word itself — a single, consistent
"question-word-and-pseudo-verb-attracts-the-clitic-pronoun" rule spanning multiple otherwise
unrelated word classes.

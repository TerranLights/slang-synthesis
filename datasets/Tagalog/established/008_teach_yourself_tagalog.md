## Grammar points

### 1. Ang-pronoun paradigm (subject pronouns)

Singular/plural, 1st/2nd/3rd person table (p. 8): `ako`/`kami`/`tayo`, `ka`-`ikaw`/`kayo`,
`siya`/`sila`. Flags the inclusive/exclusive distinction in 1st person plural (`kami` excludes the
listener, `tayo` includes them) — a distinction English does not make and that recurs across the
whole course.

### 2. `ka` vs `ikaw`

Both translate "you" (singular, informal), but `ka` must occur as part of a sentence (e.g. `kumusta
ka?`) while `ikaw` can stand alone as a one-word answer/reference. Not free variation — a
syntactic-position constraint.

### 3. `si`/`sina` personal-name markers

Tagalog obligatorily prefixes a personal name with the marker `si` (`si Cora`); the plural `sina`
marks "and companions" (`sina Larry` = "Larry and friends"). The source calls these "markers," the
general term it uses for grammatical particles throughout the course (p. 13).

### 4. Simple sentence construction (Comment-Subject order)

The source frames Tagalog basic sentence order as **Comment + Subject**, the reverse of English's
Subject + Comment — e.g. `Maganda ka` = lit. "Beautiful you" = "You are beautiful"; `Inhinyero si
Manny` = lit. "Engineer [marker] Manny" = "Manny is an engineer" (p. 13).

### 5. Formal/informal "you": `ka` vs `kayo`

A register table (p. 15) cross-tabulates social relationship (child→parent, friend→friend,
worker→superior, stranger, elder, authority figure, colleague→colleague) against which pronoun is
used: `kayo` universally available as the safe/polite choice; `ka`/`ikaw` only between social
equals or downward (parent→child, friend→friend, colleague→colleague). This is presented as the
core politeness mechanism of the language, parallel to the `po`/`opo` particle.

### 6. Greeting register ladder

Four rungs of "hello/how are you," increasingly formal: `kumusta` (bare) → `kumusta ka?` (informal
singular) → `kumusta kayo?` (formal/plural) → `kumusta po kayo?` (most formal, stacks `po` onto
`kayo`). Farewells are presented as comparatively unmarked for register (`paalam na`, `hanggang sa
muli`, `o sige ha?` all usable "in most circumstances" per the source, p. 10-11).

### 7. Cultural tip: "Filipino time"

Running prose note (p. 14, paraphrased, not quoted at length): Filipino social norms toward
punctuality are described as comparatively relaxed by the source; arriving roughly an hour past an
appointed time may not register as "late" in the way it would to a Western visitor.

### 8. Cultural tip: formality progression

A second cultural note (p. 15) frames Filipino relationship-building as starting from a formal
register by default and shifting to informal only as the relationship warms — i.e. register choice
tracks relationship *history*, not just a fixed social-rank hierarchy.

### 9. One-minute idiom box (Unit 1)

Five set phrases presented as idiomatic rather than compositional: `segunda mano` ("secondhand,"
Spanish loan), `bilugin ang ulo` (lit. "to make the head round" = to deceive someone, explicitly
labeled "colloquial"), `aywan ko sa iyo` (lit. "I don't know with you" = "it's up to you"), `huwag
naman` ("please don't," softened prohibition), `hindi na bale` ("never mind").

### 10. Unit 2 opening: hotel check-in frame, `pang-`/`pandalawahan` room-type nouns

Unit 2 ("Anong oras ang almusal?") opens with a hotel check-in dialogue introducing
`pang-isahan`/`pandalawahan` (single/double room) as derived nominals from the numerals `isa`/
`dalawa` — flagged above under the morpheme-breakdown convention since the gloss "single"/"double"
would otherwise hide the numeral root.

### 11. The verb "to be" has no direct Tagalog equivalent

English's eight forms of "to be" (be/am/is/are/was/were/being/been) are not translated directly;
the copula relationship is simply implied by Comment-Subject word order (`Guro ako` = "Teacher I" =
"I am a teacher"). A full singular/plural person paradigm is given as a translation aid only, not
as real Tagalog morphology (p. 21).

### 12. `may`/`mayroon`/`wala`: possession and existence

`may` ("have/has," "there is/are") always precedes the possessed noun/phrase and can never stand
alone as a one-word reply; `mayroon` (colloquially often `meron`) means the same thing but *can*
stand alone as a reply. `wala` is the negative counterpart ("none/no/there isn't"), productively
combined with `-ng` + possessed noun (`wala akong problema` = "I have no problem"). This 3-way
possession/existence paradigm recurs through the rest of the course (p. 24-26).

### 13. Question words `sino` ("who") and `ano` ("what")

`sino` combines three ways: with a personal pronoun, with `si` + a person's name, or with `ang` +
a noun referring to a person. `ano` is described as the single most versatile Tagalog question
word, usable with a noun, an adjective, or a verb answer. The question-word's answer always either
repeats the same structure (personal pronoun / `si`+name / `ang`+noun) that was used to ask (p.
28-29).

### 14. One-minute idiom box (Unit 2) — slang-register phrases

Five idioms, explicitly including two the source treats as decidedly informal/slang-level rather
than merely colloquial: `laman ng kalye` (lit. "contents of the street" — a person who spends more
time outdoors/on the street than at home, a borderline-pejorative social-type label) and `lutong
Makaw` (lit. "cooked in Macau" — a rigged or fixed outcome, e.g. a rigged election or beauty
contest; a geographic/ethnic-stereotype-based idiom worth flagging for the project's later
mechanics analysis). The remaining three (`pantulak`, `kape at gatas`, `kanang kamay`) are
presented as more neutral colloquial idiom (p. 31).

### 15. Cultural tips (Unit 2)

Three further running cultural notes, paraphrased: hospitality norms around food offered in a
Filipino home (politely decline once, accept on a second/third offer); a preference for indirect
rather than blunt confrontation of problems; and the social value of smiling/friendliness when
interacting with (potentially shy) Filipino strangers.

### 16. Kinship-introduction formula (Unit 3)

Family members are introduced with the pattern `[kinship term] ko ito, si [name]` ("this is my
[relation], [name]") — demonstrated for nanay/tatay/kapatid/lolo/lola and extended to
pamangkin/pinsan/bayaw/hipag. Tagalog kinship terms for siblings, cousins, nieces/nephews, and
in-laws of either gender are not gender-marked (unlike `kuya`/`ate`, which do encode relative age
and are gender-specific terms of address for older siblings).

### 17. Indefinite article (`a`/`an`) — usually zero, sometimes `ng`

Tagalog has no dedicated indefinite article; it is usually just implied (`estudyante` = "a
student"). When the indefinite-article noun is the object of a verb, it is instead marked with
`ng` (pronounced "nang"): `Bumili ng aklat si Domingo` = lit. "Bought a-book [marker] Domingo" =
"Domingo bought a book" (p. 35-36).

### 18. Definite article `ang` ("the")

`ang` marks a noun as specific/definite and is placed directly before it: `masarap ang pagkain` =
"the food is delicious" (p. 37).

### 19. Negating a statement with `hindi`

`hindi` ("no/not") is placed before the comment-subject pair to negate a whole statement, working
with a noun, adjective, `ba`-question, `pa` ("yet"), verb, or any pronoun type: `hindi masarap ang
pagkain` = "the food is not tasty" (p. 40-41).

### 20. `wala` vs `hindi`: "none" vs "not" — a common learner confusion

The source explicitly flags that English "no" covers both "not" (negates a statement, → `hindi`)
and "none" (negates existence/possession, → `wala`), and that this ambiguity is why English
speakers commonly misuse `hindi` where `wala` is required. `wala` triggers a ligature (`-ng`) before
a following noun or pronoun: `wala akong lapis` ("I don't have a pencil"), `walang kotse si Shirley`
("Shirley doesn't have a car") (p. 42-44).

### 21. One-minute idiom box (Unit 3) — more slang/colloquial register

`walang wala` (lit. "no nothing," reduplicated `wala` = "completely broke"), `may sama ng loob`
(lit. "having a bad feeling inside" = feeling hurt, expressed indirectly rather than confrontationally
— tied explicitly to the Filipino indirectness norm raised in Unit 2's cultural tip), `magmahabang
dulang` (lit. "a long dining table" = a family member is getting married, referencing the communal
buffet table of a Filipino wedding feast), `may sinasabi` (lit. "to have something to say" =
wealthy/well-off), `maraming kuskos-balungos` (lit. "a lot of scrubbing and fussing" = to beat
around the bush) (p. 44-45).

### 22. Cultural tip: `mano` greeting gesture

A younger person presses an elder's (or socially senior person's) hand to their own forehead as a
mark of respect; the source notes a visiting foreigner may receive this gesture from Filipino
children (p. 45).

### 23. `nasaan` ("where," location) and the obligatory `nasa` reply

`nasaan` asks where a thing/place is located; critically, any reply must *begin* with `nasa`
followed by the location (`nasaan ang bahay mo?` → `nasa Kalye Cruz ang bahay ko`). The source
jokes that `nasa` "has nothing to do with American astronauts" (NASA) to help English-speaking
readers remember the particle (p. 49).

### 24. No diphthongs: double vowels are pronounced separately

Unlike English, where adjacent vowels can merge into a diphthong, Tagalog always pronounces each
vowel in a sequence separately: `nasaan` is "nah-sah-an," not "nasan" (p. 50). (Compare Unit 1's
pronunciation-guide note on the same principle.)

### 25. Adjective + linker + noun: `-ng`/`na`

Describing words precede the noun they modify and are joined to it with a linker: `-ng` is suffixed
directly onto a vowel-final adjective (`maganda` + `-ng` + `bahay` = `magandang bahay`, "beautiful
house"), while the separate word `na` is inserted after a consonant-final adjective (`masarap` +
`na` + `pagkain` = `masarap na pagkain`, "delicious food") (p. 55-56).

### 26. Cultural tip: indirect pointing

Filipinos commonly avoid pointing with a hand or finger when giving directions, instead pursing
their lips or nodding toward the intended direction; the source flags this explicitly so visitors
aren't surprised by it (p. 58).

### 27. One-minute idiom box (Unit 4)

`balat-sibuyas` (lit. "onion-skinned" = very sensitive/easily hurt), `pabigla-bigla` (hasty,
impulsive, lacking self-control — illustrated with `Kung magsalita siya'y pabigla-bigla`, "he/she
has an abrupt way of speaking"), an antonym pair `suwerte sa buhay`/`malas sa buhay` (lit. "lucky
in life"/"bad luck in life," said of a family member believed to bring good or bad fortune to the
household), and `malinis sa katawan` (lit. "clean in the body" = a personally clean/tidy person)
(p. 58).

### 28. Unit 5 opening: `tao po!` and the sari-sari store

Unit 5 ("Pera," Money) opens at a neighborhood "sari-sari" (assorted-goods) store, a culturally
specific Filipino small-retail format the source explicitly names and explains. The dialogue
opens with the customary announcement-of-presence call `tao po!` ("person, sir/ma'am!") used when
approaching a shop counter or home before anyone has appeared to greet the visitor.

### 29. Triple numeral system: native Tagalog, Filipino-Spanish, and English in free variation

The source states explicitly that Filipinos routinely count using native Tagalog numerals
(`isa, dalawa, tatlo...`), Filipino-Spanish-derived numerals (`uno, dos, tres...`), and English
numerals interchangeably, a direct linguistic legacy of ~300 years of Spanish and ~50 years of
American rule. Critically, Filipino-Spanish pronunciation has drifted from standard Castilian
Spanish (e.g. `siyete` not "siete," `nuwebe` not "nueve"). Tagalog numerals compound regularly:
teens are `labing-` + ones-digit (`labing-isa` = 11), tens are `X-mpu` (`dalawampu` = 20, with
`dalawampu't isa` = 21 using the `-'t` linker for "and"), hundreds are `X-daan` (`limandaan` = 500)
(p. 60-67).

### 30. Telling time is done almost exclusively in Filipino Spanish

Unlike general counting, clock time uses the Filipino-Spanish hour names almost exclusively
(`ala-una`, `alas dos`...`alas dose`), combined with `y medya` ("half past"), direct minute-past
counting, or `menos [minutes] para alas [hour]` ("[minutes] to [hour]") for minutes-before (p.
67-68).

### 31. `magkano` vs `magkakano`: "how much" vs "how much each"

`magkano` asks for a total/single price; `magkakano` (its distributive form) asks for a per-unit
price when multiple identical items are involved — the source explicitly flags this as a common
point of learner confusion (p. 69).

### 32. Haggling register and formula

Dialogue 2 models a full haggling exchange: vendor opens with a sales call (`bili na, bili na`,
"come and buy") and an anchoring price; buyer counters with `kay mahal nga!` ("that's actually
expensive!") and `wala bang tawad?` ("no discount?"); vendor resists with claims of taking a loss
(`malulugi po ako`) or needling the buyer's presumed wealth (`mayaman naman kayo`); the exchange
resolves through alternating counter-offers. The cultural tip following this dialogue (p. 75)
explicitly endorses haggling at markets/small stores but not at fixed-price large stores, and notes
foreign customers are routinely quoted higher opening prices.

### 33. One-minute idiom box (Unit 5) — explicit slang register

This box is explicitly the richest slang content found in the course so far: `atik` is labeled
outright as "street word for `pera`" (money), with its derived adjective `maatik` ("rich"). The
remaining three are colloquial idiom rather than flagged slang: `perahin na lang` (lit. "just to
make into money" = pay cash instead of in kind), `nakatuntong sa numero` (lit. "stepping on
numbers" = constantly scrutinized, "watching one's p's and q's"), `isang katutak` (lit. "one/more
than a plenty-load" = a surplus), and `nagmimiron` (from `nagmamayroon`, "pretending to possess" =
showing off/bragging, or ogling) (p. 75).

### 34. `saan` vs `nasaan` (Unit 6)

Both translate "where," but `saan` asks about the *place of an action* or a *continuing condition*
(`saan ka bumili ng selyo?` = "where did you buy stamps?"; `saan ka nakaupo?` = "where are you
seated?"), while `nasaan` (Unit 4) only asks about static location. A `saan` reply begins with
`sa` + place, parallel to how a `nasaan` reply begins with `nasa` + place.

### 35. `masyado` — the single general-purpose intensifier adverb

Tagalog uses one word, `masyado`, to cover English "so/very/quite/too." Placed before the adjective
it modifies, with `-ng` suffixed when the adjective is vowel-final (`masyadong marumi`, "very
dirty") — directly parallel to the adjective-noun linker rule from Unit 4 (p. 84-85).

### 36. One-minute idiom box (Unit 6)

Two relational-knowledge idioms explicitly contrasted as a minimal pair: `kilala ko siya` (lit. "I
know him/her" = know *of* someone, by reputation or introduction, not necessarily a personal
relationship) vs. `kakilala ko siya` (lit. "he/she is an acquaintance of mine," a stronger claim).
Plus `matalik na kaibigan` ("best friend"), `mabilis magpatakbo` (lit. "fast to run a vehicle" = a
fast driver), and `huling biyahe` ("last trip," of a day's public-transport schedule) (p. 87).

### 37. Cultural tip: the jeepney

A paraphrased note on the jeepney as the Philippines' iconic, originally US-Army-jeep-derived
public transport, typically cheap but informally organized (hail/alight almost anywhere except
restricted main roads) (p. 88).

### 38. Possessive pronouns: placed after the noun, with an inclusive/exclusive 1st-plural split

`ko`/`mo`/`niya`/`namin`/`natin`/`ninyo`/`nila` ("my/your/his-her/our-excl./our-incl./your-pl./
their") follow the possessed noun, reversed from English word order (`tatay ko` = "my father," lit.
"father my"). The 1st-person-plural split recurs here exactly as with `kami`/`tayo` in Unit 1:
`natin` includes the listener, `namin` excludes them (p. 92-93).

### 39. `ng`/`ni`/`nina`: the other possession markers

`ng` (pronounced "nang") marks possession for a common noun (`laruan ng bata`, "the child's toy");
`ni` is the equivalent used specifically before a person's name (`bahay ni Mary`, "Mary's house");
`nina` is `ni`'s plural, for "and company" (`nina Tony`, "Tony and friends'") (p. 94-95).

### 40. `ay`: the formal sentence-inversion particle

`ay` (pronounced "eye") inverts Tagalog's normal Comment-Subject order into a Subject + `ay` +
Comment order that structurally mirrors English (`mabuti siya` → `siya ay mabuti`, "he is good").
The source stresses `ay` is not a copula/"to be" equivalent and carries no meaning difference from
the uninverted form — it's purely a stylistic/register variant, more formal and common in writing,
lectures, and sermons. After a vowel-final word it commonly contracts to `'y` (`ito'y maganda`) (p.
100-101).

### 41. One-minute idiom box (Unit 7)

`maghabol sa tambol mayor` (lit. "to chase the principal [marching-band] drum" = to be swindled
with no hope of recourse/justice, equated with English "caught napping"/"caught with his pants
down"), `mukhang Biyernes Santo` (lit. "a face like Good Friday" = a gloomy expression, tied
explicitly to the solemnity of Good Friday in Philippine Catholic culture), `parang pinagbiyak na
bunga` (lit. "looks like a fruit chopped in two" = identical twins), `kaibigang matalik`/`matalik
na kaibigan` (best friend, a repeat of the Unit 6 idiom in alternate word order), `kasa-kasama`
(constant companion, lit. reduplicated "together with") (p. 102-103).

### 42. Cultural tip: initial formality with a friend's family

Filipino families may appear more reserved/formal than the friend themselves when a foreign visitor
first meets them — described as normal reticence that warms with familiarity, not unwelcome (p.
103).

### 43. Unit 8 opening: restaurant register, `pulutan`/`litson`

Unit 8 ("Sa Restawran," At the Restaurant) opens at a *Kamayan*-style restaurant. The dialogue
introduces `pulutan` — the culturally specific category of finger food/snacks eaten alongside
alcoholic drinks — and `litson`/`lechon` (roast suckling pig), both named and explained rather than
loosely translated, since neither has a precise single-word English equivalent.

### 44. `mga`: pluralizer, and separately, an approximator

`mga` (pronounced "mah-ngah") placed before a noun makes it plural (`tiket` → `mga tiket`), a
single uniform mechanism contrasted explicitly with English's three irregular pluralization
strategies (-s, vowel change, -es). Separately — a distinct, unrelated use the source flags as
easy to conflate — `mga` placed before a number means "approximately/about" (`mga apat na kilo`,
"about 4 kilos"; `mga alas dos ng hapon`, "around 2:00 p.m."). A "Did you know" aside adds that some
adjectives pluralize by reduplicating their second syllable instead of taking `mga`
(`mabuti`→`mabubuti`, `malaki`→`malalaki`), and when this happens the pluralized adjective must
precede a plural noun/pronoun (p. 106-107, 112).

### 45. Philippine restaurant culture: `turo-turo`, Kamayan, and named national dishes

A cultural-tip aside (p. 110-111) names several iconic Filipino dishes by their actual Tagalog/
Spanish-derived names rather than loosely translating them — `leche flan`, `bilobilo`, `bibingka`,
`adobong manok` — on the grounds that no single English word captures them. `turo-turo` (lit.
"point-point") is named as a common roadside-restaurant format where customers point at
already-prepared dishes. `Kamayan` itself (the restaurant style from Unit 8's Dialogue 1) is
explained as derived from `kamay` ("hand"), referencing the traditional pre-Spanish-contact Filipino
practice of eating with bare hands, which these restaurants deliberately revive.

### 46. `ito`/`iyan`/`iyon`: a 3-way distance-graded demonstrative system

Unlike English's 2-way "this/that," Tagalog grades demonstratives by distance from both speaker and
listener: `ito` ("this," within the speaker's own reach), `iyan` ("that," near the listener but not
the speaker), `iyon` ("that, over there," distant from both) (p. 116).

### 47. Pluralizing a demonstrative: `ang mga ito/iyan/iyon`

Because `ito`/`iyan`/`iyon` are already pronouns, bare `mga ito` reads oddly; Tagalog instead
inserts `ang` before `mga` + the demonstrative (`ang mga ito` = "these"), a fixed idiomatic
construction rather than a productive general rule (p. 117).

### 48. One-minute idiom box (Unit 8)

`dala-dala` (lit. reduplicated "carry" = a load one carries around, said of someone always burdened
with stuff), `may padala` (lit. "has something to be delivered" = a delivery, illustrated with a
`Barong Tagalog` — the traditional embroidered formal shirt — being sent as a gift), `mabuting
makisama` (lit. "good at being-with" = someone who gets along with everybody), `tirhan mo ako` (lit.
"portion some out for me" = leave me some food), and `sabi-sabi` (lit. reduplicated "say" = idle
gossip, illustrated with a Tagalog proverb, paraphrased rather than quoted in full, warning that
believing gossip is unkind to oneself) (p. 118).

### 49. Unit 9 opening: pharmacy consultation frame

Unit 9 ("Sa Botika," At the Pharmacy) opens with a pharmacist consultation dialogue that introduces
medical-register vocabulary (`gamot`, `pagtatae`, `reseta ng doktor`) and sets up the `para sa`
("for") construction explored in the unit's grammar section.

### 50. `para sa`: "for," with person/place-name allomorphy

`para sa` ("for") marks three distinct relations — recipient (`para sa bata ang gatas`, "the milk
is for the child"), favor/support (`para sa Partido Mabuhay si Ignacio`), and purpose (`para sa
paglalaba ang sabon`, "the soap is for washing clothes"). Before a personal name it becomes `para
kay` (singular) / `para kina` (plural/"and friends"), directly paralleling the `ng`→`ni`/`nina` and
`sa`→`kay`/`kina` alternations already seen. The corresponding question words are `para kanino`
("for whom") and `para saan` ("for what [purpose]") (p. 121-124).

### 51. Naming the body: a full head-to-foot vocabulary list

A systematic body-part list (p. 126-128) proceeds from head to foot; two anatomical/genital terms
(`buto`, `puki`) are included plainly in the list without an explicit register label from the
source, though cross-linguistically this is exactly the kind of vocabulary that tends to carry
taboo-register sensitivity — flagged `taboo` in the table above as a judgment call, not a label the
source itself applies.

### 52. One-minute idiom box (Unit 9)

`parang kabute` (lit. "like a mushroom" = someone who appears/disappears unpredictably, illustrated
with a truant schoolchild), the proverb `ang sakit ng kalingkingan ay damdam ng buong katawan` (lit.
"the pain of the little finger is felt by the whole body" = one person's mistake affects the whole
group, e.g. a co-worker's error), `ganito 'yon` (lit. "it's like this..." = beating around the bush
with a long explanation), `nakapamburol` (lit. "dressed in funeral attire" = dressed to the nines/
to kill — notable for the semantic distance between literal mourning-wear and the idiomatic "best
clothes" meaning), and `dedbol na ako` (lit. "I'm like a dead ball," an English-loan-blend slang
term = utterly exhausted) (p. 130-131).

### 53. Cultural tip: modesty norms around the body

A paraphrased note on Filipino modesty conventions (especially for women), contrasted with a more
relaxed norm at tourist beaches/resorts (p. 131).

### 54. Unit 10 opening: `sa` possessive pronoun paradigm

Unit 10 ("Tulong?", [Need] Help?) opens with the `sa`-possessive series (`sa akin`, `sa iyo`, `sa
kaniya`, `sa amin`, `sa atin`, `sa inyo`, `sa kanila` = "mine/yours/his-hers/ours-excl./ours-incl./
yours-pl./theirs") — a second possessive-pronoun paradigm alongside Unit 7's `ko`/`mo`/etc. series,
used specifically for standalone possessive predication (`sa akin ang aklat` = "the book is mine")
rather than noun-modification. Before a personal name this shifts to `kay`/`kina`, continuing the
same name-allomorphy pattern as `ng`/`ni`/`nina` and `para sa`/`para kay`/`para kina` (p. 133-135).

### 55. `kanino`/`kaninong`: "whose," with a free-standing vs. bound-form split

`kanino` asks "whose" and can stand alone or combine with `ng` as a linker; `kaninong` (`kanino` +
`-ng`) can *only* ever be used bound directly to the noun it modifies, never standing alone — the
inverse distribution of `ka`/`ikaw` from Unit 1 (where the shorter form was the bound one). English
collapses three related meanings ("whose," "to whom does X belong," "who does X belong to") into
Tagalog's single `kanino` (p. 139-141).

### 56. `sa` as a preposition of place and time

Beyond its possessive/demonstrative uses already covered, `sa` is the general-purpose preposition
corresponding to English "in/in the," "on/on the," "at/at the" for both place (`sa dagat`, "in the
sea") and time (`sa gabi`, "at night") (p. 143-144).

### 57. One-minute idiom box (Unit 10)

`sigurado ka ba?` ("are you sure?" — expressing doubt about received information), `palabiro` (a
person fond of joking/good-humored), an exclusivity pair `tayo-tayo`/`sila-sila` (lit. reduplicated
"we"/"they" = deliberately excluding outsiders from "just us"/"just them," flagged by the source as
grammatically odd-looking but fully idiomatic), and `maasim ang mukha` (lit. "the face is sour" = a
disapproving expression) (p. 146-147).

### 58. Cultural tip: clothing/sizing caution for visitors

A paraphrased consumer-culture note: clothing is often good value but counterfeit goods circulate,
and Filipino sizing runs smaller than Western sizing — the source suggests visitors accustomed to
buying XL at home look for Filipino-labeled XXL (p. 147).

### 59. Unit 11 opening: pseudo-verbs `gusto`/`ayaw` and the `magpa-` causative

Unit 11 ("Gusto Kong Magpagupit," I Want a Haircut) opens with a barbershop-appointment dialogue
built around Tagalog's "pseudo-verbs" — `gusto` ("want/like") and its negative counterpart `ayaw`
("don't want") — which behave like verbs syntactically (taking a `ko`/`mo`/etc.-type actor and a
following linked complement) without belonging to Tagalog's regular affix-based verb-conjugation
system. `magpagupit` ("to have a haircut done") itself demonstrates the `mag-pa-` causative prefix
stack applied to the root `gupit` ("cut") — "causing [someone] to cut [one's hair]," the general
mechanism Tagalog uses for "have X done (by someone else)" service-verbs.

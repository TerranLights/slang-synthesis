**Morpheme breakdown.**

> **ikinagagalak** = ika- (causative/conveyance-trigger infinitive formant) + -in- (imperfective
> infix) + CV-reduplication (ga-) + galak ("happiness") — contrasts directly with **nagagalak** =
> ma- (actor-trigger, affective) + CV-reduplication (ga-) + galak, same root, different trigger:
> the i-/ika- form foregrounds the *cause* of the emotion as topic, the ma-/naga- form foregrounds
> the *experiencer* as topic. The book's own gloss (p.11): "both sentences say the same thing, but
> the first focuses on the feeling of the speaker, while the latter focuses on the cause for the
> feeling" — this is the course's plain-language description of a genuine actor-trigger /
> conveyance-trigger alternation.

> **ipinakikilala** = i- (conveyance trigger) + -in- (imperfective infix) + pa- (causative) +
> ki-kilala (CV-reduplicated root "acquaintance") — "is being introduced"; demonstrates that
> Tagalog verb agreement does not mark subject number (p.24: the same verb form is used whether
> one or several people are being introduced).

## Grammar points

### Linker -ng/na
A linker connects two words into a single phrase (adjective+noun, number+noun, etc.). Its form is
conditioned by the preceding word's final sound: `-ng` attaches directly to a vowel-final word
(`maganda` → `magandang`; `anim` → `animang`... actually attested as `anim na`, see below), while a
separate word `na` is used after a consonant-final word (`anim na linggo` "six weeks"). The same
linker also recurs as a general subordinator (e.g. `kong` = `ko` + linker, connecting a possessive
pronoun to a following verb phrase). (pp.10, 26-27)

### Po — the respect particle
`Po` marks respectful/formal address, with no direct one-word English equivalent ("sir"/"ma'am" is
the closest approximation). Its absence doesn't make a sentence rude by default — the book's
exercises systematically pair the same sentence with and without `po` depending on whether the
addressee is a stranger/superior or a peer/child, establishing an explicit formality axis layered
on top of the pronoun system (`kayo` vs. `ikaw`/`ka`) rather than replacing it. (p.10)

### Topic-marking: si/sina vs. ang vs. mga
Tagalog marks the sentence topic with a dedicated particle rather than word order alone: `si`
(plural `sina`) marks a personal name as topic; `ang` marks a common noun or place name as topic.
`Mga` (pronounced /maŋa/) pluralizes a following noun and can combine with or without `ang`. (p.23)

### Inclusive vs. exclusive "we"
Tagalog grammaticalizes a distinction English does not mark morphologically: `tayo` ("we,"
including the addressee) vs. `kami` ("we," excluding the addressee). The book flags this explicitly
as a systematic confusion point for English-speaking learners. (p.39)

### Possessive "my": ko vs. aking
"My" has two allomorphic-position forms: `ko` follows the noun it modifies (`asawa ko` — "my
spouse"), while `aking` precedes it (`aking asawa`). Both are grammatical and interchangeable in
meaning; the choice is purely positional/stylistic. (p.17)

### Aspect instead of tense
The book states explicitly that Tagalog verbs do not mark tense the way English does; instead they
mark three aspects: **Perfective** (completed action), **Imperfective** (action begun but not
completed), and **Contemplated** (action not yet begun). The imperfective is formed from the
infinitive by inserting `-in-` and reduplicating the first syllable of the root — the specific
reduplication/infixation pattern depends on which prefix the verb takes (`ika-` → `ikinaga-`;
`ma-` → `naga-`; `ipa-` → `ipinaki-`). (p.24)

### Existential/possessive may/mayroon vs. wala
`May` (and its question-response counterpart `mayroon`) expresses "have"/"there is/are"; its
negation is `wala` ("don't have"/"there isn't/aren't"), not a separate negated verb form of `may`
itself. (pp.38, 42)

### Register note: informal greeting formulas
A Conversation for Listening Comprehension (p.36) models a materially more informal register than
the chapter's main dialogues: `Hoy` as a bare greeting interjection, `Saan ka pupunta?` ("Where are
you going?") used not literally but as a stock informal greeting, `Diyan lang` ("Just here") as its
conventional reply, and `OK lang` ("Just fine") as a casual health-check response — all explicitly
contrasted by the book against the `po`-marked formal greeting forms taught earlier in the same
unit. This is the course's clearest register-tier annotation so far and the first concrete evidence
of a formal/colloquial greeting-register split worth tracking into Phase 3 mechanics analysis.

### Numbers 1-20 and the labing-/labim-/labin- allomorphs
Numbers 11-19 prefix `labi-` + a linking consonant to the units digit, and the linking consonant is
phonologically conditioned: `labing-` before a vowel or `w`-initial number, `labim-` before
`p`-initial numbers, and `labin-` elsewhere. 20 is `dalawampu` ("two tens"). (p.27)

### Weather adjectives: ma- + root, and three intensification strategies
Weather/quality adjectives are formed with prefix `ma-` on a noun root (`init` "heat" → `mainit`
"hot"). The book then shows three independent, apparently interchangeable ways to intensify such an
adjective: full-root reduplication (`mainit na mainit`), the adverb `masyadong` ("too"), and the
prefix `napaka-` (`napakainit`). (pp.31-33)

## Grammar points

### Tagalog Idioms (appendix)

The source frames idioms explicitly as carrying "a regional story or a logic behind them" and
recommends four concrete acquisition channels for a learner: listening to native conversation, reading
Filipino newspaper comic strips (naming **Pol Medina Jr.'s *Pugad Baboy*** as a specific real example),
watching Filipino TV variety shows/dramas, and listening to radio talk shows — i.e. the source itself
identifies idiom acquisition as fundamentally a living-media/oral-culture phenomenon rather than
something textbook drilling alone can convey, directly validating this project's own corpus-collection
strategy of drawing slang/idiom evidence from media sources rather than grammar references alone. The
idioms themselves cluster into recognizable semantic-source domains worth flagging for later
cross-language slang-mechanics comparison: **body-part metaphor** (bibíg "mouth"→rudeness, dilà
"tongue"→gossip, kamáy "hand[s]"→generosity/theft, butó "bone[s]"→evil/hard work, mukhâ "face"→stern
demeanor, noó "forehead"→intelligence, ulo "head"→sanity); **animal metaphor** (pusà "cat"→not
belonging, pagóng "turtle"→slowness, uwák "raven"→impossibility/never, tutà "puppy"→blind loyalty,
dagâ "rat"→poverty); **body-fluid/physiology metaphor** (dugô "blood"→anger, luhà "tears"→
emotionality, páwis "sweat"→poverty/labor); and **religious-calendar reference** (Biyérnes Santo "Good
Friday"→gloominess), the last of which directly extends this book's running theme (first flagged in
Lesson 35's interjections) of Catholic/religious cultural material being repurposed into secular
figurative vocabulary. **Several idioms show striking structural/semantic parallels to common English
idioms** (butó't balát↔"skin and bones"; kánang-kamáy↔"right-hand man"; kumukulô ang dugô↔"blood
boiling"; pagputî ng uwák↔"when pigs fly"; mahangin↔"full of hot air") — worth flagging explicitly as
candidate cases of either independent convergent idiom-formation (since body/animal/impossible-event
metaphor sources are cross-linguistically common raw material) or later cultural contact/calquing,
a question for the later mechanics-analysis phase to investigate rather than assume either way.

### Everyday Tagalog Expressions (appendix)

Organized into 12 situational categories (locations/directions, instructions for children, time,
speaking in Tagalog, introductions, dressing up, at a party, goodbyes, at the market, in the kitchen,
in the dining room, in the living room) that are overwhelmingly **compositional applications of
grammar and vocabulary already fully documented** in the main 44-lesson sequence (question-word
constructions, pseudo-verbs, paki- requestives, huwág negative imperatives, etc.) rather than a source
of new grammatical rules. A few points are genuinely noteworthy: **Mahál na Áraw** ("Holy Week," used
in the Time category to answer "what time of year is it") reuses the adjective **mahál** ("dear/
beloved," Lesson 1) in a fixed calendrical-religious proper-noun sense, distinct from its ordinary
"expensive/dear" use — another small religious-vocabulary-integration data point consistent with the
pattern already noted in the idioms and interjections. **Barong Tagálog** reappears here (the "Dressing
Up" category) alongside an explicit source-note that the **amerikana** (a loanword from "American,"
denoting the Western-style suit-and-tie formal coat) was historically introduced by Americans — i.e.
the source itself volunteers a colonial-contact etymology for this specific loanword, directly
relevant to this project's borrowing-mechanics interest. **Húwag kayóng mahiyâ** ("don't be shy," said
by hosts offering food) is explicitly flagged by the source as **"a very common expression of hosts...
to make guests feel more welcome"** — a genuine, source-documented politeness-ritual formula tied to
Filipino hospitality culture, worth flagging as a fixed social-script phrase rather than a freely
compositional sentence.

### Conversational Tagalog (appendix)

Seven real-world-situation dialogues (meeting/introductions in a business setting, asking directions,
at the bank, in a meeting, at the market/store, at the mall, at mealtimes [breakfast/lunch/supper],
visiting a sick friend, at school, going on vacation) are presented with full English translation and
followed by vocabulary-list/cultural-notes boxes. **Two genuinely important sociolinguistic address-
term systems are documented explicitly in these notes, beyond anything covered in the main lesson
sequence**: (1) **manang/manong** (for an older household servant or any elderly person in a
service/vending role) vs. **ate** (for a *younger* servant) — an age-stratified service-address-term
pair; (2) **aling/mang** (prefixed to a given name) for any unrelated older person, which the source
explicitly notes **elderly people use reciprocally with each other too**, not just as a younger-to-
older deference marker — a genuinely nuanced finding about how age-based honorifics function
bidirectionally among same-generation elders, not merely as a junior-to-senior politeness axis like
the pô/hô system from Lesson 3. The formulaic phrase **tao pô** (announcing one's presence at a door
lacking a bell) and the discourse particle **siya** ("all right/okay" — explicitly distinguished by
the source from the homographic pronoun **siyá**) are both flagged by the source as genuinely
untranslatable-as-single-words, culturally load-bearing fixed expressions. The "At the Mall" dialogue's
explanatory note on the **English loanword "mall"** — stating it was adapted into Filipino "probably
since the early 1990s" and is not simply synonymous with "store," closer to "department store" in
scope — is a rare case of the source giving an approximate **date** for a specific loanword's
nativization into the language, directly useful evidence for this project's borrowing-mechanics
timeline work. The "Asking Directions" and "At the Mall" dialogues both explicitly contrast
traditional/provincial elements (**karetela** horse-carriages, small **tindahan** stores) against
modern/urban ones (**MRT**, **FX**, shopping **malls**) — a deliberate urban-modernity-vs-tradition
framing embedded directly into the dialogue content itself, not merely incidental vocabulary choice.

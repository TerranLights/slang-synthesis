# Tagalog — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Basic Tagalog for Foreigners and Non-Tagalogs, Lessons 14–20

**Source:** Paraluman S. Aspillera (revised/updated by Yolanda Canseco Hernandez), *Basic Tagalog for
Foreigners and Non-Tagalogs* (Tuttle Publishing, 2nd/revised ed., 2007), Lessons Fourteen–Twenty,
pp. 72–100. Continuation of `007_basic_tagalog_for_foreigners.md` (Lessons 1–6) and
`007b_basic_tagalog_for_foreigners.md` (Lessons 7–13); see those files for this source's overall
coverage note and vision-reading method. No handwritten marginalia found in this chunk either.

**Coverage note:** This chunk is the heart of the book's verb-affix/trigger system — Lessons 14–20
cover five of the language's core voice-marking affixes (**-um-**, **mag-**, **-in-**, **ma-**,
**maka-**, plus the helping-verb constructions **maging** and **magkaroón**) plus the **ang/ng**
article-selection rules that interact directly with which affix a verb takes. Every grammar point,
full derivation paradigm, and distinct vocabulary item is captured; repeated blank-filling drill
exercises are skipped per the coverage rule. **Trigger/focus category is marked explicitly on every
verbal-affix entry**, per this project's Tagalog-specific column convention.

## Grammar points

### Lesson 14 — -Um- and Mag- verb forms (actor/agent trigger)

Tagalog's two primary **agent/actor-trigger** affixes. **-Um-** prefixes directly before a
vowel-initial root (**alís→umalís**) or infixes immediately after a consonant-initial root's first
consonant (**kain→kumain**). **Mag-** prefixes before the root, with a hyphen inserted before a
vowel-initial root purely to mark the glottal stop (**áral→mag-áral**). Both share the same
**past/present/future** derivation logic: past drops the infix/changes **mag-→nag-**; present adds
reduplication of the root's first syllable (or first two letters, for a root whose first syllable has
three letters, e.g. **sampáy→magsasampáy**) on top of the past-tense base; future reduplicates without
the past-tense marker change. **Semantic split when a root takes both affixes** (not all do): the
**-um-** form is non-intensive/casual/single-occurrence and directed *toward* the doer, while the
**mag-** form of the *same root* marks frequency/intensity/habituality and action directed *away from*
the doer — illustrated with **kumakáin** ("is/are eating," ordinary) vs. **nagkákakáin** ("to always/
continually be eating") and **bumibilí** ("is/are buying," toward the doer) vs. **magbibilí** ("is/are
selling," away from the doer). **This -um-/mag- toward/away-from-doer split, and the
casual/habitual-intensity split, is a genuinely important semantic-derivation fact for later
slang-mechanics work** — a single root can yield two lexically distinct, non-interchangeable verbs
purely via trigger-affix choice, which is a much more load-bearing morphological mechanism than
English's analogous active/passive alternation. **Two sentence word orders** are both grammatical with
-um-/mag- verbs: verb-subject-object and verb-object-subject, with no meaning difference — though the
subject-second order is explicitly preferred when the subject is a pronoun like **siyá**/**silá**.

### Lesson 15 — -In- verb forms (object/patient trigger)

**-In-** (and its allomorph **-hin**) marks the **object-trigger** (patient-focus) voice — the
receiver of the action becomes the grammatical subject, and unlike an -um-/mag- sentence, **the
object cannot be omitted** here (contrast **Bumása akó ng libró** "I read a book," object omissible,
with **Binása ko ang libró** "I read the book," object obligatory and now the subject). The source
states outright that **Tagalog speakers generally prefer the -in- (object-trigger) construction** over
the -um-/mag- active alternative when both are available — a significant register/frequency claim
about how the trigger system is actually used in practice, not just a grammatical possibility.
**Formation**: vowel-initial roots take -in- as a true suffix in the infinitive but as an *infixed*
**-in-** immediately after the initial sound in past/present tense, with reduplication marking present
and future as in the agent-trigger paradigms (**alís→alisín** infinitive, **inalís** past, **ináalis**
present, **áalisin** future). Consonant-initial roots select between **-in** (after a
glottal-catch-final root) and **-hin** (after an ordinary vowel-final root with no glottal catch) —
**bása→basáhin**, **sábi→sabíhin**, vs. **sirâ→sirain**, **punô→punuín**. **Three irregular
sub-patterns**: (1) end-stressed roots drop their final vowel before **-hin** (**bilí→bilhín**,
**dalá→dalhín**); (2) roots whose final syllable is CVC/VC with **i** or **o** drop that vowel before
**-in** (**káin→kánin**, **sunód→sundín**, **dakíp→dakpín** — though the *un-suffixed* infinitive/
imperative/future forms keep the **o→u** change instead of dropping it, e.g. **gamót→gamutín** not
***gamtín***); (3) roots beginning with **l, w,** or **y** take prefix **ni-** (not infixed -in-) in
past/present tense (**lutò→nilutò**, **walís→niwalis**, **yarì→niyarì**) — a place-of-articulation-
driven allomorphy distinct from (though structurally parallel to) the **-um-** infixation rule.
**Sentence pattern**: the doer is always expressed in possessive form (**ni Peter, ng batà, ko, niyá,
kanyá...**, or **kitá** — a fused "I-to-you" doer/object pronoun unique to this trigger, equivalent to
**ikáw**-as-object + **ko**-as-doer combined into one word) — verb first, then doer, then object.
**Patáy/patayín** carries an explicit dual sense the source flags directly: literal "to kill," and a
separate colloquial sense "to put out a light or flame" — a genuine semantic-extension case (worth
flagging for later slang/semantic-shift mechanics analysis, since "killing" a light is a metaphor-
derived everyday usage, not marked as formally distinct vocabulary by the source but clearly a
secondary, lower-register sense).

### Lesson 16 — The articles Ang and Ng (trigger-marking recap and plural forms)

This lesson systematizes a selection rule implicit since Lesson 4/8: **ang** marks whichever noun
phrase is the sentence's *trigger-selected* grammatical subject regardless of its semantic role
(agent in an -um-/mag- sentence, patient in an -in- sentence), while **ng** marks every other
core argument — the patient in an active (-um-/mag-) sentence, or the agent/doer in a passive (-in-)
sentence. This is presented pedagogically as three plain rules (ang introduces the main
character/subject; ng introduces the receiver of an action in an active sentence or the doer in a
passive one; ng also marks simple possession, continuing Lesson 8's ni/ng possessive system) rather
than explicitly named as a trigger/ergativity system, but functionally **this is the clearest single
statement in the book so far of how trigger selection and case-marking interact** — worth flagging
as a key cross-reference point for later Tagalog mechanics analysis. Plurals: **ang→ang mga**,
**ng→ng mga**, continuing the plural-marking system from Lesson 4.

### Lesson 17 — Pseudo-verbs (dápat, gustó, maáari/puwéde, kailángan, íbig, áyaw)

A closed class of **six "pseudo-verbs"** (modal-like auxiliaries) that always govern a main verb in its
bare **infinitive** form regardless of tense — the pseudo-verb itself, not the main verb, carries any
tense/mood marking the sentence needs: **dápat** (must/should/would/has to), **maáarì**/**puwéde**
(can/may/could — two free-variant forms), **kailángan** (needs to/is necessary to), **íbig**/**gustó**
(likes/desires/wants — two free-variant forms), **áyaw** (does not like/want, negative polarity only).
**Ligature/pronoun placement differs by pseudo-verb**: most pseudo-verbs (dápat, maáarì, kailángan,
áyaw) take a **nominative** pronoun (akó, siyá...) with the appropriate ligature attached to the
pronoun when the main verb is active (-um-/mag-), or a **possessive** pronoun with ligature when the
main verb is passive (-in-) — i.e. the pronoun's case tracks the *main verb's* trigger, not the
pseudo-verb. **Íbig/gustó are the sole exception**: they always take a possessive-form pronoun (ko,
mo...) regardless of the main verb's trigger, placed between the pseudo-verb and the main verb. A
pseudo-verb ending in a consonant other than **-n** takes no ligature-linking suffix before the main
verb; one ending in **-n** or a vowel does. **Kailángan** specifically requires ligature **-g** linking
it directly to a following main verb with no intervening pronoun. This pseudo-verb system is a clean,
closed, well-specified subsystem — a strong candidate for later comparative mechanics work on modality
marking.

### Lesson 18 — Ma- and Maka- verb forms (ability/capability, two further trigger categories; stressed má-/maká- for involuntary action)

**Unstressed ma-** forms a verb expressing *capability* to do something, and splits into two
distinct trigger categories depending on the root's transitivity: with an **intransitive** root, **ma-**
is **actor-trigger** (active, takes a nominative-pronoun/**ang**-marked subject, parallel to -um-/mag-)
— e.g. **matúlog** "to sleep," including several **emotion-verb** roots (**matuwâ** "delighted,"
**magálit** "angry," **mahiyâ** "ashamed," **matákot** "afraid," **malungkót** "sad") that the source
flags as a ma--specific capability absent from maka-; with a **transitive** root, **ma-** is
**object-trigger** (passive, patient-as-subject, doer in possessive form, parallel to -in-) — e.g.
**mákita** "to be seen/able to be seen." **Unstressed maka-** always forms an **actor-trigger**
(active) capability verb, identical in function to actor-trigger ma- but restricted to **action**
roots only — it cannot form an emotion verb the way ma- can, so "not all ma- verb forms have their
maka- equivalent" per the source's own explicit statement. Formation for both: past changes the
prefix's unstressed vowel to **a** region (ma-→na-, maka-→naka-); present adds reduplication of the
root's first syllable after the past-tense base; future reduplicates without the past-tense prefix
change; neither ma- (transitive) nor maka- has an imperative form (ma- intransitive does). **Stressed
má- and maká-** form a *third*, semantically distinct category: **involuntary/accidental/unintentional
action**, "beyond the control of the doer or receiver" per the source's own framing — a genuinely
important grammatical-semantic category this project should track, since many languages lack a
dedicated morphological marker for "did X by accident" (compare English's periphrastic "accidentally
V-ed"). Stressed forms keep their accent on the prefix across *all* tenses (unlike unstressed ma-/maka-,
whose stress shifts), which is the only formal distinguisher from the unstressed, non-accidental
homograph pairs — e.g. **mákita** (object-trigger ability, "to be able to be seen") vs. **mákita**
(object-trigger involuntary, "to be seen accidentally") are written identically but are historically/
functionally two different grammatical categories collapsing onto the same surface spelling once
accent marks are dropped in casual writing (the book's own romanization keeps the accent to
disambiguate, but ordinary unaccented Filipino orthography would not) — **worth flagging explicitly for
later mechanics work: accent-based verb-category disambiguation is a real feature of this language's
grammar, and casual/informal writing that drops accents is a plausible source of genuine ambiguity or
slang-level reanalysis**. Stressed maká-'s present/future tense reduplicates the *prefix's own* second
syllable (**ka**) rather than the root's first syllable, a formation rule distinct from every other
paradigm in this chunk.

### Lesson 19 — Maging: "to be" or "to become"

**Magíng** is a helping verb placed directly before a noun or adjective predicate to express a change
of state ("to become") or, in some uses, simple "to be." It carries its own independent tense paradigm
(infinitive/imperative **magíng**, past **nagíng**, present **nagíging**, future **magíging**) regardless
of what follows — when the following predicate is itself a **ma-** adjective, the **ma-** prefix is
retained unchanged on the adjective while **magíng** alone carries the tense marking (**magíng
mabaít** → **nagíging mabaít**, not a double-inflected form). This is presented as a simple, closed
one-word-paradigm construction with no trigger/voice alternation of its own — a comparatively "inert"
piece of the verb system next to the richer -um-/mag-/-in-/ma-/maka- system, but a very commonly used
one per the source's framing.

### Lesson 20 — Magkaroón: "to have"

**Magkaroón** ("to have") functions similarly to **may**/**mayroón** (Lesson 12) but, unlike those two
particles, **carries a full tense paradigm** (past **nagkaroón**, present **nagkákaroón**, future
**magkakaroón**) — a genuine structural distinction the source draws explicitly: may/mayroón express
bare existence/possession with no tense of their own (except when **may** governs a following verb),
while magkaroón is a fully inflecting verb expressing the same core meaning. **Formation**: either the
full periphrastic **magkaroón + ng + noun** or a single-word contraction **magka- + noun** (e.g.
**magkaroón ng pera** / **magkapera**, "to have money") — both equally grammatical and
interchangeable per the source's worked examples, which consistently give both forms side by side.
**Subject placement**: a pronoun subject must follow **magkaroón** directly; a **si/ang**-marked
subject may appear either directly after **magkaroón** or after the object, with no meaning change.
**Negation**: **hindî** placed directly before **magkaroón** (or a magka- word) negates the whole
construction (**Hindî nagkaroón si Maria ng asó**, "Maria did not have a dog") — unlike the
**walâ**-based negation rule for may/mayroón in Lesson 12, this is ordinary clausal negation with
**hindî**, since magkaroón is a true tensed verb rather than an existential particle — a clean
confirmation that the hindî/walâ split tracks the existential-particle-vs-ordinary-verb distinction
established back in Lesson 12, not an arbitrary lexical quirk of may/mayroón specifically.

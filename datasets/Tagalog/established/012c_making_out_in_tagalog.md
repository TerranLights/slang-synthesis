## Grammar points

### English-loan verbal affixation as a productive slang mechanism
This chunk turns up a clear, repeated pattern: raw English loanwords take native Tagalog verbal
affixes directly, with no nativized spelling/phonology change beyond the affix itself — *nag-eenjoy*
("enjoying," p.62), *ka-deyt* ("a date [as noun]," p.70), *magseks* ("to have sex," p.78), and
*mamimis* ("will miss [someone]," p.87). This spans registers from casual/slang to tender/romantic,
suggesting it's a generally productive word-formation strategy in colloquial Tagalog rather than a
single slang-specific trick — a strong `borrowing`+`morphological_play` finding for the
mechanics-analysis phase, and a concrete candidate mechanism for how a synthesized conlang could graft
newly-borrowed vocabulary onto an existing trigger/affix system.

### Gendered register asymmetry: maganda vs. guwapo
The book states explicitly that *maganda* ("beautiful") is culturally coded feminine, and that in
"a macho country" a man would not welcome being called *maganda* unmodified — it must be compounded
with *lalaki* ("male") to apply to a man at all, whereas *guwapo* is the unmarked term for male
attractiveness (p.76). A genuine gendered-vocabulary-asymmetry finding, distinct from the
grammatically gender-neutral pronoun system noted in `012_making_out_in_tagalog.md`.

### ari/asawa-type gender-marking-by-compounding pattern
Three separate vocabulary items in this chunk use the same structural trick — a gender-neutral base
noun disambiguated by a following *ng lalaki*/*ng babae* or *-ng babae*/*-ng lalaki* modifier: *ari ng
lalaki/babae* ("penis"/"vagina," p.78), *asawang lalaki/babae* ("husband"/"wife," p.83), and the
earlier insult *Maliit ang ari mo* (005b, p.55). Worth treating as one named structural pattern rather
than three unrelated coincidences.

### "Exiting" as an orgasm euphemism (labas root)
*Lalabasan na ako* / *Nilabasan ka ba?* / *Nilabasan ako* ("I'm coming" / "Did you come?" / "I came")
all derive from *labas* ("outside/exit"), with the sexual sense built entirely through verbal
affixation on an otherwise mundane spatial root — directly parallel to the English idiom "come," but
arrived at independently through a different base metaphor (p.81).

### No dedicated LGBTQ+ chapter in this book (a genuine absence, not an extraction gap)
Chapter 13 "The Other Side" — which in this series' Japanese edition covers gay/LGBTQ+ vocabulary —
instead covers marriage, rejection, and breakup language in this Tagalog edition. This book contains
no dedicated queer-vocabulary chapter or section at all. Recorded here explicitly so this doesn't read
as a missed chunk in future comparative work across this series.

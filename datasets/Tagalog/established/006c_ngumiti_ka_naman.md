## Grammar points

### *Hanggang* as adjunct marker ("until, up to")
*Hanggang* (optionally + *sa*) marks a spatial or temporal limit: *Umulan hanggang (sa) Cubao* "It
rained up to Cubao"; *May baha hanggang (sa) Sabado* "There's flood until Saturday"; *Nag-aaway pa
sila hanggang ngayon* "They are still fighting until now." (Lesson 94; p.125.)

### *Magka-*/*magkaroon* — inadvertent existence/possession
*Magka-* prefixed directly to a noun, or *magkaroon ng* + noun, expresses coming to have/acquire
something (often unplanned): *Puwedeng magkatubig dito* / *Puwedeng magkaroon ng tubig dito* "It's
possible to have water here"; *Magkakaroon si Fred ng asawa* / *Magkakaasawa si Fred* "Fred will come
to have a wife"; *Nagkaroon kami ng ilaw kanina* / *Nagkailaw kami kanina* "We came to have
electricity a while ago"; *Nagkakaroon sila ng sakit dito* / *Nagkakasakit sila rito* "They are
getting sick here." (Lesson 97; p.128.)

### Possessive phrases and sentences, summary paradigm
The book consolidates its possessive-marking system (cf. Lessons 15, 37, 53) into parallel phrase vs.
sentence forms: *diyaryo ni Bob* / *Kay Bob ang diyaryo* "Bob's newspaper" / "The newspaper is
Bob's"; *sapatos ng guro* / *Sa guro ang sapatos* "teacher's shoes" / "The shoes belong to the
teacher"; *sampayan ko* / *aking sampayan* / *Akin/Sa akin ang sampayan* "my clothesline" (three
equivalent forms); *regalo nila* / *kanilang regalo* / *Kanila/Sa kanila ang regalo* "their gift."
(Lesson 99; p.130.)

### Tag-question *(hindi) ba* / *di ba*
A statement is turned into a tag-question ("...isn't it/aren't you/didn't it") by appending *hindi
ba* (or its clipped colloquial form *di ba*), either sentence-finally or sentence-initially:
*Matanda na siya, hindi ba?* "He/she is already old, isn't he/she?"; *Di ba, kailangan mo sila?* "You
need them, don't you?" (Lesson 105; p.135.)

### *Ka-* + reduplication + *lang*: recently-completed action
A verb root, prefixed with *ka-* and reduplicated, combined with the particle *lang* "just," marks
an action that was completed only moments ago: *Kaaalis lang ng biyenan ko* "My parent-in-law has
just left"; *Kararating ko lang* "I have just arrived"; *Kabibili lang ni Paul ng sapatos* "Paul has
just bought shoes"; *Kalilinis mo lang ba nito?* "Have you just cleaned this?" (Lesson 115; p.145.)

### *Taga-* origin prefix
*Taga-* marks where a person (always a person or personalized object as topic) is from: *Tagasaan
sila?* "Where are they from?" → *Taga-Texas sila* "They're from Texas"; *Tagarito siya* "She's from
this place." (Lesson 116; p.146.)

### Code-blended loan verbs with native affixation, continued
Further confirms the pattern from Lesson 48: *nagre-review* "reviewing" (*nag-* + reduplication +
English "review"); *naka-gold* "in a wealthy state" (*naka-* stative prefix + English "gold," an ad
hoc Taglish slang coinage for "flush/rich"). (Lessons 117–118; pp.147–148.)

### *Pam-*/*pan-*/*pang-* instrumental-purpose prefix, full allomorphy
Completing the pattern introduced at Lesson 11, the book gives the full conditioning rule for this
instrumental/purpose prefix: *pam-* before *p*/*b* (*pambayad* "for payment," *pambabae* "for
ladies"), *pan-* before *t/d/s/r/l* (*pantulog na damit* "sleeping clothes"), and *pang-* elsewhere
(*panghugas* "for washing"). (Lesson 124; p.154.)

### Voluntary/deliberate vs. involuntary/ability verb pairs
A systematic opposition recurs across several affix pairs: plain actor-trigger forms (*-um-*/*mag-*)
express a deliberate/intentional action, while their *maka-*/*makapag-* counterparts express ability
or involuntary occurrence of the same action: *matulog*/*makatulog* "sleep"/"be able to sleep";
*bumalik*/*makabalik* "return"/"be able to return"; *mauuna*/*makakauna* "will go ahead"/"will be
able to go ahead"; *umalis*/*makaalis* "leave"/"be able to leave"; *tumutulong*/*nakakatulong*
"helps"/"is able to help" (this last pair also serving as an ability reading, not just intentional
vs. accidental); and with *mag-* roots, *magtipid*/*makapagtipid* "economize"/"be able to economize,"
*magbabayad*/*makakapagbayad* "will pay"/"will be able to pay," *nag-aral*/*nakapag-aral*
"studied"/"was able to study," *naglilinis*/*nakakapaglinis* "cleans"/"is able to do cleaning." A
comprehensive, explicitly-tabulated summary of the ability/volition distinction threaded through
many earlier individual lessons (*maka-*, Lesson 37; *na-*/*ma-...-an*, Lesson 23; *nakaka-*, Lesson
47). (Lesson 127; pp.157–158.)

### Pluralizing adjectives
Tagalog adjectives pluralize two ways: (1) *ma-* adjectives reduplicate their first syllable —
*maganda* → *magaganda*/*maganda* "pretty" (pl.), *matangkad* → *matatangkad*/*matangkad* "tall"
(pl.), *malakas* → *malalakas*/*malakas* "strong" (pl.) — the unreduplicated form is also acceptable;
(2) *ma-*-less adjectives simply co-occur with the noun pluralizer *mga*: *bago* "new" → *(mga) bago
ang mga relo* "the watches are new"; *pagod* "tired" → *(mga) pagod kami* "we are tired." (Lesson
128; pp.159–160.)

### Bare verb/adjective roots used as nouns
A verb or adjective root can be used directly as a noun without further derivation, as the book
notes explicitly for *hawak* "a hold/grip" and *takbo* "a run" (as opposed to their verbal uses
"hold" and "run"). This bare root-as-noun pattern recurs throughout the book (cf. Lesson 20's
*anghang*/*maanghang*). (Lesson 96; p.127.)

# Cantonese: A Comprehensive Grammar (Matthews & Yip) — Units 161-170 (pages 300-319)

Source: *Cantonese: A Comprehensive Grammar*, Matthews & Yip. DJVU render units 161-170, each unit a two-page spread (pages 300/301, 302/303, ... 318/319). Covers the tail end of Chapter 16 ("Coordination and subordinate clauses") and the opening of Chapter 17 ("Questions").

## Prose summary

**Concessive and conditional clauses (end of ch.16).** The book treats "although" clauses as needing a balancing word in the main clause — Cantonese does not let the subordinate conjunction stand alone the way English can. *sēuiyìhn* ("although") pairs with *daahnhaih* ("nevertheless") or *dōu* ("still") in the follow-up clause; *mòuhleuhn* ("no matter whether") likewise requires *dōu* and attaches to a full question structure (A-not-A, alternative, or wh-question), giving Cantonese's "whatever/whoever/however" constructions their shape: a wh-word in clause one plus *dōu* in clause two. A colloquial alternative to *mòuhleuhn* is *mh léih* (literally "don't care"). Two idiom boxes are worth flagging: *gam [adjective] dōu [verb]* is an implicit concessive ("so ADJECTIVE and still...", e.g., "still buying it even though it's that expensive"), and *mìhng jī* ("clearly know") marks someone persisting in something ill-advised despite knowing better, often intensified by *jiu* ("carry on") in the main clause.

Conditionals (16.3) are introduced by *yùhgwó* ("if"), typically matched by *jauh* ("then") in the consequent clause, with the if-clause preceding the main clause as the unmarked order (reflecting cause-before-effect, as with reason clauses). The book stresses that Cantonese, lacking tense, makes no systematic grammatical distinction between open, hypothetical, and counterfactual conditionals — the distinction falls to context, aspect markers, and adverbs rather than mood morphology. A formal double-conjunction *yùhgwó ... ge wá* ("if ... say") appears in more careful speech; *gáyùh* ("suppose") is an explicitly hypothetical formal conjunction; *jauhsyun* ("even if") is a stronger concessive-conditional conjunction that, like other concessives, pulls *dōu* into the second clause. Perfective *jó* in the consequent clause can mark a hypothetical resultant state corresponding to English "would have." Implicit conditionals (16.3.2) — two clauses simply juxtaposed with no overt conjunction — are especially natural when the first clause is negative. Negative conditionals (16.3.3) use *yùhgwó mhaih* ("if not, otherwise") to elaborate a prior statement, with bare *mhaih* sufficing when that meaning is already established; *chèuihfēi* ("unless") is typically answered by *yùhgwó mhaih* in the following clause.

**Cleft sentences, indirect speech (16.4-16.5).** The copula *haih* fronts and emphasizes a constituent much like English "it's X that...", and can be reinforced with *haih ... sīn ... ge* ("only X would..."). A formal, Mandarin-flavored equivalent is *sih ... dīk*. Indirect speech (16.5) needs no equivalent of English "that" — the reported clause simply follows the reporting verb, and its subject pronoun is often omitted since the matrix subject carries over. Reported speech is commonly introduced by serial-verb formulae: *wah/góng béi ... tēng* or *wah ... jī* ("tell"). A notable idiom here is *wah jī [pronoun]*, which does NOT mean "tell [pronoun]" but rather "ignore/couldn't care less about [pronoun]" — the third-person pronoun *kéuih* in this frame is often pleonastic (not referring to any real entity), and the book explicitly warns this must be distinguished from *wah (léih/kéuih) jī* "tell (you/him)." The sentence particle *wóh* can mark indirect/reported speech overtly.

**Questions (ch.17 opening).** Cantonese marks questions through dedicated constructions and sentence-final particles rather than word-order inversion, and the grammar highlights rhetorical questions implying negation (e.g. "Who cares?") as a distinctively common pattern, especially with wh-questions and exclamatory questions. Particle questions (17.1.1) use *àh* (checks an assumption, presupposes a positive answer) or the purely interrogative *mē* (marks surprise, checks an unexpected state of affairs); both are also the standard vehicle for rhetorical questions. Mandarin *ma* exists but is relatively formal in Cantonese, confined largely to the fixed greeting *Léih hóu ma?* A-not-A questions (17.1.2) are the most neutral yes/no form, reduplicating the verb/adjective around negative *mh* (only the first syllable repeats for polysyllabic verbs); a flagged idiom here is A-not-A + *dī* ("a bit"), which turns the question sarcastic/ironic (e.g. "isn't that a BIT much?"). Copular *haih-mhaih* (17.1.3) is a more explicit, answer-neutral A-not-A form, obligatory before quantified phrases, and can front the sentence or serve as a tag (seeking confirmation, presupposing "yes"); the fixed idiom *haih-mhaih a?* is a standalone skeptical "Are you sure?/Really?" response. *Meih* questions (17.1.4, "not yet") ask whether something has occurred, typically with perfective *jó* or experiential *gwo* on the verb (distinguishing "yet" from "ever" in the English gloss), and also combine with potential *dāk*. Existential *yáuh-móuh* (17.1.5) is the A-not-A form of existential *yáuh*; verb-object compounds invert to object-verb order under this questioning (*jouh-yéh* "work" → *yáuh-móuh yéh jouh*). A flagged slang-adjacent idiom: *yáuh-móuh gáau cho (a)?* — literally "is there some mistake?" — has been generalized through overuse into a stock expression of outrage/ridicule ("Are you crazy?!"), frequently used impersonally with the subject dropped. Tag questions (17.1.6) default to *haih-mhaih*, with *hóu-mhóu* used as an "okay?" tag on suggestions and *dāk-mdāk* as an "okay?" tag eliciting consent. A genuine phonological/tone-sandhi note here: these tags routinely contract, dropping the second *h* (*haih-mhaih* → *haih-maih/haih-mái*; *hóu-mhóu* → *hóu-móu*), with an optional accompanying tone change on the negative syllable (*mhaih* → *mái*). Section 17.1.7 covers rising intonation and echo questions — Cantonese's lexical tones leave little room for intonation, but sentence-final rising pitch still signals a question, most saliently in echo questions repeating a statement out of surprise, and a second echo type closes with particle *wá* when the speaker wants a specific word/phrase repeated. The range closes at 17.1.8, on how yes/no questions are answered (repeating the verb, bare or negated, often softened by a sentence particle — a bare negative answer without a particle reads as abrupt/rude).

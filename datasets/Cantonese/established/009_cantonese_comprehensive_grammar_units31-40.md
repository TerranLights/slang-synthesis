## Overview

The opening pages (unit 31) close out §2.1 on the interrogative *mātyéh* 'what', showing it productively infixed into verb-object compounds for rhetorical effect ('What do you mean, singing?'), and its frequent colloquial reduction to *mī'eh* or *māt*. Notably, the whole bound unit *-gwái-yéh-* (literally "what-devil-stuff") can be infixed as a single expletive-flavored block, and in other rhetorical idioms *gwái* 'devil' substitutes for *mātyéh* outright (*gwáan léih gwái sih àh?* 'none of your business', lit. "concern you devil business"). This is a genuinely productive colloquial/mildly-taboo rhetorical device, not a one-off idiom, and it recurs heavily through §2.2 as the pattern [verb/adjective – *gwái* – verb/adjective – *máh*] (e.g. *heui-gwái-heui-máh* 'what's the point of going', *sihk-gwái-sihk-máh* 'no way to eat'), which the authors describe as quite productive in colloquial Cantonese for dismissive/rhetorical purposes — a mechanism worth flagging for slang synthesis as an expletive-infixation pattern parallel to English "whatthe-hell-ever."

§2.2 Reduplication covers a genuinely heterogeneous set of word classes and functions: classifier reduplication for universal quantification ("every single one"), stative-verb reduplication with the diminutive suffix *-déi* ('know a little'), directional-verb reduplication, adjective reduplication (again with *-déi*, meaning "rather X"), and onomatopoeic reduplication denoting sounds (which can function as verb, noun, or adverb depending on syntactic position). The authors flag that reduplication very often triggers **tone change**: reduplicated adjectives with *-déi* take the changed (mid/high rising) tone on the *second* reduplicated syllable, while a separate diminutive-verb reduplication pattern changes the tone of the *first* reduplicated syllable instead — two distinct tone-sandhi rules attached to superficially similar reduplication shapes. A striking register-specific finding: **baby-talk register** (the language adults use with small children, and children's own speech) has its own reduplication conventions, reduplicating the object noun of verb-object compounds (*heui gāai-gāai* 'go out', lit. "go street-street") and reduplicating common nouns and kinship terms wholesale (*wōu-wōu-gáu* 'doggie', *yī-yī* 'auntie'). This is explicitly named as a distinct register by the authors, not just casual speech.

§2.3 Compounding surveys noun, adjective, and verb compounds. Compound nouns show a systematic **tone-change rule**: if the second element of a compound originally carries a low-register or mid-level tone, it shifts to a mid/high rising tone when fused into the compound — the authors compare this to English stress-shift in *gréen hóuse → gréenhouse* as a marker that the compound now functions as a single word. Compound-noun head position varies (final-head is more common, but food/animal terms like *gāi-gūng* 'rooster' vs. Mandarin's reverse order *gōng-jī* put the head first) — an interesting Cantonese/Mandarin typological contrast. §2.3.3 on verb-object compounds is the richest vein for slang/idiom purposes: many V-O compounds carry fully idiomatic, non-compositional meanings that map onto English workplace/relationship slang almost one-to-one — *sihk-yúhn-faahn* "eat soft rice" = live off a woman (kept man), *cháau...yàuhyú* "fry squid" = fire an employee, *dahn...dōng-gū* "simmer mushroom" = demote an employee, *haap-chou* "sip vinegar" = be jealous (romantic), *chēut-māau* "out-cat" = cheat (on an exam), *jáu-tòhng* "leave-lesson" = skip class. The authors also show these compounds behave syntactically like separable phrasal verbs (aspect markers, modifiers, duration phrases, and even a personal object can be inserted between the verb and its nominal object), with the idiomatic reading sometimes lost once the compound is split by a true classifier/measure phrase.

Chapter 3 opens with a methodological discussion (citing Chao 1947) on whether Western part-of-speech categories even cleanly apply to Chinese, framing "noun," "verb," "adjective," etc. as convenient labels for English-speaking learners rather than claims about Cantonese's own grammatical structure. §3.1.1 shows the verb/adjective boundary is genuinely blurry: adjectives convert freely into change-of-state verbs (*fèih-jó* 'has become fatter'), and even compound noun-adjective slang coinages like *lengjái* 'handsome boy' (*leng* 'good-looking' + *jái* 'boy') convert further into verbs (*lengjái-jó* 'has become quite good-looking', cited from a TV ad). A linguistically striking register observation here: Cantonese permits **proper nouns and English brand names as predicative adjectives** — *Kéuih hóu a-Kīu* 'she's very Ah-Q' (literary allusion to a Lu Xun character), and in code-mixed advertising registers, *"That suit is really YSL!"*, *"He's so XO!"*, *"Jack and Annie are very Los Angeles!"* — proper nouns standing in for their stereotypically associated qualities, used predicatively with full adjectival degree-modification syntax. §3.1.2-3.1.6 catalogue modal auxiliaries (*wúih* 'will/would', *hóyíh* 'can'), the coverb/preposition class (a closed list of ~14 items — *béi, bōng, deui, gān(jyuh), gīng, hái, héung, heung, tùhng, waih(jó), wán, yuhng* — all of which retain verb-like syntax: they take aspect markers, verbal particles, A-not-A reduplication, and the potential-construction infix *dāk*, arguing against a clean verb/preposition split), localizers/postpositions built on *mihn* 'face' or *bihn* 'side' (e.g. *chìhnmihn* 'in front', *hauhbihn* 'behind'), and the classifier system's grammatically obligatory role (required with demonstratives, not just for counting).

## Overview

The range opens (pp. 220-221) finishing off a catalogue of resultative verb particles that attach directly after a main verb to specify an outcome: *mìhng* "clear, understood" (used with perception/speech verbs, e.g. *tēng mìhng* "listen and understand"), *múhn* "full" (state of being filled to capacity), *sèhng* "successfully accomplish", *wàhn* "thoroughly" (which can combine with the quantifier *saai* and takes a concessive reading with *dōu*, e.g. "I've looked everywhere and still can't find him"), *yùhn* "finish, to the end" (which also marks temporal sequencing between clauses), and *waaih* "bad, broken". *séi* "to death" stands out as a hyperbolic intensifier used colloquially for emphasis/exaggeration (*Ngóh béi léih gīk séi* "You're driving me crazy!"), not a literal death reading — flagged here as a notable register/intensification pattern. The page range then briefly previews the "potential" (modal) resultative construction with *mh* and *dāk* inserted between verb and particle (e.g. *tēng mh dóu* "can't hear", *heui dāk sèhng* "manage to go"), which is picked up properly in Chapter 12.

Section 11.3.3 (pp. 222-227) covers Cantonese's **quantifying particles** — *saai*, *màaih*, and *tìm* — which the authors explicitly flag as peculiar to Cantonese with no direct Mandarin counterpart, a linguistically notable typological gap. *saai* "all, completely" quantifies the subject of an intransitive or the object of a transitive verb, and in a transitive clause with a plural subject it is genuinely ambiguous between a subject-quantifying, object-quantifying, or both readings; it also has a distinct adverbial/emphatic use (not quantificational) marking a change of state with adjectives, or emphasizing a verb-object idiom. Several colorful fixed idioms built on *saai* are recorded: *gau saai kùhng* "dirt poor, really broke" (*gau* "enough" + *saai*, used to emphasize an adjective), *tek saai geuk* "overstretched, frantically busy" (an idiom that exists only in combination with *saai* — the bare form *tek-geuk* does not occur — and is explicitly noted as the semantic opposite of the parallel English idiom "kick one's heels" meaning idle), *lèih-saai-póu* "completely off-the-wall, outrageous", and *ngāau-saai-tàuh* "scratch-all-head," i.e. to be at one's wits' end. *màaih* "in addition, along" has an analogous quantifying syntax (subject of intransitive / object of transitive, denoting the last of a series or bringing an ongoing action to completion, as in *gā màaih* and *lìhn màaih* "including"), plus two unrelated functions: a directional verb meaning "approach, draw close" and a resultative particle meaning "close" or "together." A negative-only idiom is recorded: *góng/wah mh màaih* "can't say for sure," where *màaih*'s meaning diverges from its usual senses and only exists in the negated form. *saai* and *màaih* can combine (in that fixed order), and the combination V-*màaih saai* carries a distinctly pejorative connotation — doing absolutely everything, including the outrageous or excessive (e.g. a child caught doing "all sorts of illegal things," or someone eating all the leftover food even past the point of good sense). *tìm* "add, more" is syntactically distinct from *saai*/*màaih*: it cannot occur immediately after a verb, only after a complement phrase (typically with a quantifier/numeral), and it additionally functions as a sentence-final particle (cross-referenced to chapter 18) and as a main verb meaning "add," attested in the New Year greeting *tīm ding faat chòih* "have a son and get rich."

Section 11.3.4 (pp. 227-229) introduces **chān**, another particle the authors describe as peculiar to Cantonese, with two unrelated meanings: an **adversative** sense ("to one's disadvantage/misfortune," e.g. *mhóu láahng chān* "don't catch a cold," *ngáauh chān* "get bitten") that occurs naturally in passive constructions and reflexively with body parts (*hám chān go tàuh* "bump one's head"), requiring the transitive object to be animate/a person; and an unrelated **habitual** sense meaning "whenever," which (unlike the general habitual marker *hōi*) is restricted to the first of two consecutive clauses and is typically echoed by the quantifier *dōu* in the second clause, optionally reinforced by *chi-chi* or *múih chi* "every time." *chān* rarely combines with aspect markers since it already conveys result on its own.

Chapter 12 (pp. 229 onward) turns to **modality**: possibility, ability, necessity, obligation, volition, and preference, expressed not just through a closed class of modal verbs/auxiliaries (12.1) and modal adverbs (12.2) but also through dedicated syntactic constructions (12.3, outside this range) — the authors note Cantonese has no subjunctive mood. Section 12.1.1 covers *wúih* ("will/would," with a cluster of related senses: futurity/prediction, conditional, willingness, habitual action, and — in formal/written register, paralleling Mandarin *huì* — "know how to") and *hóyih* "can, may," whose two readings are explicitly split along the **deontic/epistemic** distinction (permission vs. possibility-as-eventuality), with retrospective "could have" expressed by *hóyih* plus *búnlòih* "originally." *jéun* "allow" / negative *mjéun* (formal/written *bāt jéun*) express permission and prohibition, the latter typically impersonal and binding on everyone present. Section 12.1.2 (Ability) distinguishes *sīk* "know how to" (ability from learning/skill, e.g. swim, drive), *sīk dāk* (a reinforced variant), *híu* (knowledge of how to do something, e.g. "no idea how to do it"), formal/written *wúih*, and the formal register word *làhnggau* "be able" (capability). Section 12.1.3 (Necessity) covers *yiu* "need, have to" (ambiguous with main-verb "want") and the unambiguous compound *sēuiyiu*, which can be reinforced by the modal adverb *yātdihng* "definitely, must"; the negative counterpart is *msái*/*mséui-yiu* "need not," though *msái* is replaced by bare *yiu* in affirmative answers to A-not-A questions, and the positive form *sái* occurs in rhetorical questions with a notionally negative force ("Need I say more?"). Idioms built on *yiu*: *jí yiu* "all it takes is..." (used impersonally, common in advertising register) and the admonitory tag *sīn dāk*/*sīn hóu* "...or else," reinforcing an instruction. Section 12.1.4 (Obligation) treats *yīnggōi* "should," again split deontic (requirement) vs. epistemic (prediction/inference), with *yātdihng haih* marking strong epistemic inference ("must be") and retrospective "should have" marked by adding *búnlòih* or *yāt-jóu* "much earlier."

Section 12.1.5 (Volition) covers *séung* "want/wish" (which, like English *want*, takes either a same-subject VP or a different-subject full clause, and — paired with *hóuchih* "seem" — can take an inanimate subject to express prediction, e.g. "it looks like it's going to rain"), *hēimohng* "hope," *háng* "be willing" (not objecting), the transitive-only verb *jai*/*mh jai* "comply/not comply with a proposal or deal" (which, unlike *háng*, cannot take a following verb and participates in A-not-A questions as *jai-mh-jai*), formal-register *yuhnyi* "be willing" (for solemn undertakings), and *gám* "dare" alongside its colloquial synonym **gau dáam** "have the guts (to)" — a vivid informal idiom literally "enough gall/guts," flagged here as a colloquial register item of particular interest for slang-synthesis purposes. Section 12.1.6 (Preference) covers *lihngyún* "rather," which the authors note typically implies a negative framing — the preferred option is itself unappealing, just less bad than the alternative (the "lesser of two evils" pattern) — reinforced by the conjunctions *júngjī* "as long as" and *jí yiu* "if only" (a second, distinct sense of *jí yiu* from the necessity idiom above); more positive preference is expressed by *jūngyi...dō dī* "like...more" or *jeui jūngyi* "like most." The range closes (12.2-12.2.1) with modal adverbs of possibility — *hólàhng* "maybe" (between subject and verb), *waahkjé* "perhaps" (sentence-initial), *dōsou* "probably, most likely," and the tag phrase *dōu mdihng* used after a sentence that raises a possibility — noting that, unlike English, a modal adverb paired with a modal auxiliary precedes the auxiliary rather than sitting between auxiliary and main verb, though colloquially such adverbs can also be dropped to clause-final position as an afterthought.

The most linguistically striking material in this range is the cluster of particles (*saai*, *màaih*, *tìm*, *chān*) the authors repeatedly flag as structurally unique to Cantonese among Sinitic languages, plus the rich set of colloquial idioms built on them (*gau saai kùhng*, *tek saai geuk*, *lèih-saai-póu*, *ngāau-saai-tàuh*, *gau dáam*) — exactly the kind of register-marked, idiomatically fixed vocabulary most relevant to slang-synthesis work, since several explicitly invert or depart from literal compositional meaning (the *tek saai geuk* idiom is noted as the semantic opposite of its closest English analogue).

## Prose summary of genuinely new content (Units 23-25 tail)

The tail of **Unit 23** (book p.164, PDF 181) is just the last few sentence-completion drill items
for particles/interjections covered earlier (outside this range) — nothing new to extract there.

**Unit 24, "Colloquial syntax,"** is the most linguistically interesting stretch in this range. It
opens by extending the third-person pronoun **kéuih** beyond its formal-register restriction to
people: in colloquial speech it can refer to things (especially in imperatives, "eat it up!"), to
the object of a `jéung`-classifier phrase, or to nothing identifiable at all, frozen into idioms
like "bottoms up" or "couldn't care less" (the unit even cites a 1990s song title built on this
pattern). It also surfaces as a sentence-final "reinforcement" of an already-stated noun phrase —
a genuine right-dislocation pattern, distinct from English "topic, comment" framing because the
dislocated element restates rather than introduces the subject.

The unit then catalogs **afterthoughts/right-dislocation** more broadly: subject pronouns, modal
auxiliaries, evaluative adverbs, and even whole subordinate ("although"/"if") clauses can be moved
to trail the main clause after a comma, a purely spoken-register move that would read as backwards
if transcribed into formal writing.

The **classifier section** is a flagship slang-synthesis finding: Cantonese normally requires a
classifier + noun, but colloquial/slang register drops the noun and uses the bare classifier
(instead of the generic `go`) to stand in for a person, with the specific classifier carrying
register/attitude. `tiuh` (normally for long, thin objects) picks out "that guy" colloquially or,
more crudely, "your boyfriend/girlfriend." The book explicitly flags this as dehumanizing — treating
a person as a physical object via their classifier. Parallel to this, `jek` (the generic animal
classifier) gets applied to people for a demeaning/jocular effect, and `gihn` (classifier for
garments) appears in `wuhn gwo gihn` "change one's boyfriend/girlfriend," literally "change a
garment" — clothing-classifier-as-partner is a reusable slang-derivation mechanism (treat a social
role as a disposable object via its ordinary classifier). The chicken/duck classifier idiom for
"call a prostitute" / "call a gigolo" is an animal-euphemism-for-sex-worker pattern worth comparing
against other languages' corpora for convergent evolution. Colloquial money/time counting also
appears here: `yāt pùh séui` and `yāt gauh séui` as slang units for large/small sums, `yāt láp jūng`
as a colloquial hour-count, and `daaih-láp-lóu` "big shot."

The unit's centerpiece is **"The magic of gwái"** — an extended treatment of `gwái` ("ghost,"
"devil") as one of the most productive colloquial morphemes in spoken Cantonese. It appears as a
compound's final element (`láahn-gwái` "lazy person/coward," `séui-gwái` "rascal," affectionate),
as a compound's initial element meaning "foreigner" (`gwáilóu`/`gwáipòh`/`gwáijái`/`gwáimúi`,
explicitly contrasting "ghost" against `yàhn` "human" as a quasi-ethnic label for Westerners — the
book notes these have lost most of their sting but are still avoided in polite/mixed company in
favor of `sāi-yàhn`/`ngoihgwok-yàhn`), as a bare intensifier after degree adverbs (`hóu gwái dung`
"so cold"), infixed into the middle of a disyllabic adjective (`màhfaahn` → `màh-gwái-faahn`
"really troublesome" — a genuine expletive-infixation pattern, structurally close to English
"fan-**damn**-tastic"), inserted between a verb and its aspect marker for frustration/annoyance,
and inserted into question words (`bīn-gwái-go` "who on earth," `mát-gwái-yéh` "what on earth") and
rhetorical questions. The book is explicit that this is mildly expletive but not strongly offensive
to native speakers — a useful calibration point for where a conlang's equivalent intensifier-morpheme
should sit on a taboo scale.

**Unit 25, "Code-mixing and loanwords,"** is the other flagship section, and arguably the single
most directly useful stretch of this book for the slang-synthesis project's actual goal: it
documents, with worked examples, exactly how a language's grammar absorbs a donor language's
vocabulary. The book is careful to state the *directionality*: English words are bent to fit
Cantonese grammar, not the reverse. Cantonese aspect markers (`-jó`, `-jyuh`, `-gán`) and verbal
particles (`fāan`, `yùhn`, `saai`) attach directly to English verb stems; English verb-object
pairs get split apart the way Cantonese native V-O compounds do; bare English nouns take ordinary
Cantonese classifiers (`tìuh` for "line," `jek` for "model," `fahn` for "contract"); and yes/no
questions get formed by **reduplicating the English word itself** in the Cantonese A-not-A pattern
(`hēp-mh-hēp-pí?` for "happy?"). The book frames code-mixing as a bilingual-prestige sociolect
(professional/educated speakers) rather than low-register slang, and notes it's often a *relief
strategy* for lexical gaps — technical/computer vocabulary where the native Cantonese coinage exists
but is less recognized than the English borrowing (`chyùhn-jān` vs. `fēk-sī` for "fax").

The **loanword** sub-section documents a systematic adaptation rule: a consonant-final English
loanword used as a verb gets a new syllable built with a high-rising tone (`fēk-sī` the noun "fax"
vs. `fēk` the verb; `gēsí` "gas," `sáaisí` "size," `féisí` "face," `tipsí` "tip(s)," `fensí`
"fans"). This is a clean, rule-governed nativization mechanism — exactly the kind of borrowing
morphology a conlang's slang layer could productively imitate. The **Japanese loanword**
sub-section adds a second mechanism: Kanji compounds get read via their Cantonese
character-pronunciations rather than transliterated phonetically, producing calques like
`yī-jī-bāan` "super/first-rate" (literally "new arrival") and `daaih chēut hyut` / `daaih got yáhn`
"big sale" (literally "big bleed"/"big cut") — loan-translation rather than loan-borrowing, a
distinct mechanism from the English examples. The `yāt juhk` "one race/category" suffix pattern
(`dāan-sān yāt juhk` "the single crowd," `keep-fit yāt juhk` "the keep-fit crowd") is a productive,
reusable slang-formation template for naming social in-groups/demographics, worth flagging for the
`analysis/` layer as a candidate derivation mechanism.

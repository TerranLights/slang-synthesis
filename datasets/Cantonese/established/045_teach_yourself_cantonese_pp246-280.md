## Overview

This stretch runs out the main course and into four appendices, so the texture of the material
shifts hard partway through: Unit 25/26 content is live dialogue and vocabulary like the rest of
the book, while the Grammar Summary, writing-system essay, bibliography, and exercise key are all
reference/back-matter prose — mostly a recap of grammar already documented in earlier files from
this same book (038, 041, 043), not new teaching content. The "key to the exercises" section in
particular is just recombined sentences using vocabulary from Units 1-12 and introduces nothing new
lexically, so it is summarized here rather than tabulated line by line.

The richest colloquial/register material is concentrated in the Unit 25 tail. **Yám-chàh**
("drink tea") is the standard idiom for going out for dim sum — Cantonese speakers "never say
let's go and have some dim sam, they always say let's go and drink tea," per the author, a clean
case of a fixed idiom standing in for a whole cultural activity rather than literal tea-drinking.
**Gùng-yàhn** ("worker") carries a gendered default in Hong Kong usage: unmarked, it is understood
as a female house servant/domestic helper, and a male one must be explicitly marked as
**nàahm-gùng-yàhn**. The "English invasion" section is a small goldmine of Cantonese loanword
slang built straight off English sound-alikes: **lìp** (lift), **bō** (ball), **bā-sí** (bus),
**dīk-sí** (taxi), **sà-léut** (salad), **fēi-lám** (film), **sih-dō** (store), **bō-sí** (boss),
and **baht-láan-déi** (brandy) — the author notes the trend is now away from these loanwords toward
"purer" Cantonese coinages, citing **sìng-gong-gèi** ("rising-and-falling machine") as the clunky
formal alternative to lìp that is slowly losing ground back to the loanword in casual speech. A
generational register shift is also flagged for bathrooms: the newer **chùng-lèuhng-fóng**
("shower room") is driving out the older **sái-sàn-fóng** ("wash-the-body room"), with the
distinction between an all-over wash and a shower increasingly blurred in practice.

Two pragmatics/particle findings are worth flagging for the slang-synthesis project even though
they are grammatical rather than lexical. First, the softening particle **dōu** can be slipped into
an otherwise blunt negation to take the confrontational edge off a disagreement — bare **m̀h haih!**
("it's not!") reads as abrupt and rude, while **dōu m̀h haih** politely signals "I'm afraid that's
not quite the case" even though the literal content is identical. Second, **m̀h + lak** is a fixed
combination meaning "not [X] anymore," used to mark a reversal of an earlier decision or state. The
sentence-final tag **jauh-jàn** ("and that's for sure!") is a minor but genuine colloquial
emphasis marker. The Grammar Summary appendix's own particles section is worth noting as a
meta-finding: the author states outright that Cantonese sentence-final particles mostly have no
fixed meaning, that usage is inconsistent even among native speakers, and that many can be treated
as "voiced pauses" ('spoken commas') and safely ignored — a useful caution against over-interpreting
every particle as semantically load-bearing.

The "Chinese writing system" appendix, while mostly an essay for English-speaking learners about
how ideographic script works, surfaces a few homophone pairs the author uses to argue that
characters (unlike a purely phonetic spelling) disambiguate sound-alike words cleanly: **gáu**
(nine) vs. **gáu** (dog), and **jáu** (alcoholic drink) vs. **jáu** (to run, to leave). Homophone
collision is a classic substrate for slang wordplay and punning, so these pairs are flagged even
though the book itself only uses them to illustrate the writing system, not to build slang. The
appendix also glosses **Jùng-gwok** ("China," literally "Middle Kingdom") against **ngoih-gwok**
("outside kingdoms," i.e. foreign countries), with the author pointing out the embedded status
claim — centrality for China, peripheral/unimportant status for everywhere else — baked into the
ordinary vocabulary itself.

No taboo vocabulary appears anywhere in this range.

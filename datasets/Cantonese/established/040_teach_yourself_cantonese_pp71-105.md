## Unit 6 tail: verb endings and time-span syntax

The range opens on two verb-ending points. **-dāk** as a verb ending means *can, be able to* with no
implication of *why* (unlike modal auxiliaries that specify permission, skill, or availability) —
the book is explicit that -dāk can only attach to a verb, never another part of speech. **-gwo**
(literally *to go past*) marks that an action has been experienced at some unspecified point, and is
contrasted directly with **-jó**, which marks completion at a specific point: *kéuih heui-jó
Hèung-góng* ("he went to Hong Kong," a specific trip) versus *kéuih heui-gwo Hèung-góng* ("he has
been to Hong Kong," ever, at some point). A short grammar note also covers "time how long" expressions
(duration, as opposed to time-when), which in Cantonese syntax land *after* the main verb rather than
before it as in English.

## Unit 7: pure revision, no new material

Unit 7 is explicitly a revision unit — the book states up front it introduces no new vocabulary or
grammar, just recombines Units 1–6 material into two reading passages and six drill exercises
(true/false, Cantonese Q&A, translation, a one-sided eavesdropped phone call to reconstruct, fill-in-
the-blank, and sentence-plug insertion). Nothing here is logged as a new term in the table below
since it is by design a recombination of already-attested vocabulary, but it is worth flagging as a
structural landmark: the course schedules three further revision units (14, 21, 26) at roughly
six-to-seven-unit intervals.

## Unit 8: heat, cold, and a stinginess argument

Unit 8's dialogue pits Mr. and Mrs. Wong against each other over buying an electric heater, with Mrs.
Wong accusing her husband of buying impractical junk and him firing back that *she's* the stingy one
(**hàan** — "to save; to be parsimonious, stingy"). The grammar notes are rich in register-marking
sentence-final particles: **tìm** ("as well, what's more") piles onto **juhng** for emphasis, **lak**
backs up **jeui** ("most") in superlatives, and — most notably for slang-synthesis purposes — the
particle **ā?** is glossed by the book itself as used "triumphantly scoring a point," i.e. *didn't I
tell you so!* This is a genuine pragmatic/attitudinal particle rather than a plain question marker,
and it closes Mr. Wong's dialogue line the moment his wife's prediction about the fire extinguisher
is vindicated. The unit also documents a small, closed set of **tone-sandhi exceptions**: *nìhn*
("year," low falling) shifts to mid-rising *nín* only in the compounds **gauh-nín** (last year),
**gàm-nín** (this year), and **chēut-nín** (next year) — otherwise the low-falling tone is regular.
Dialogue 2 (Mr. Chan buying an air conditioner, Mr. Cheung ribbing him about his wife's thriftiness)
supplies a playful idiom worth flagging as colloquial register rather than literal content: Mrs.
Cheung reportedly offered to alter an old necktie into a **béi-gìn-nèih** ("bikini," a phonetic
loanword from English), delivered as a tongue-in-cheek dig at her husband's cheapness. The cultural
note on typhoons also records a loan etymology: English *typhoon* is said to derive from Cantonese
**daaih-fùng** ("great wind").

## Unit 9: ball games, movies, and the first explicit "shortcut" grammar note

Unit 9 opens with **bō** ("ball"), itself a phonetic loanword from English, feeding the fixed
expression **dá-bō** ("to play a ball game" — ambiguous between soccer, snooker, basketball, etc.
depending on local context, which the book flags directly). A cultural aside on **dihn-** ("electric")
compounds is a nice window into loan-coping word-formation: **dihn-yíng** ("movie," literally
*electric shadows*), **dihn-chē** (tram, *electric vehicle*), **dihn-wá** (telephone, *electric
speech*), **dihn-sih** (television, *electric vision*), **dihn-nóuh** (computer, *electric brain*) —
all built on a native root (dihn, originally "lightning") to absorb 19th/20th-century technology
concepts rather than borrowing foreign phonology wholesale. Most relevant to the project's phrase-
clipping interest: Grammar point 6, explicitly titled **"Taking shortcuts again,"** documents that
any two-syllable verb can be shortened in an A-not-A question the same way a monosyllabic one can —
*néih gei mh gei-dāk* as a colloquial contraction of the fuller *néih gei-dāk mh gei-dāk* ("do you
remember or not"). The book frames this explicitly as a productive, general register shortcut, not a
one-off idiom. The unit closes with an "overkill" note observing that Cantonese tolerates stacking
three different near-synonymous "moreover" words (yìh-ché / juhng / tìm) in a single sentence where
English would find that redundant — a register-level stylistic fact worth keeping on file.

## Unit 10 (partial): clinics, an ethnic-register term, and reduplication for duration

Unit 10 opens with a phone call to a doctor's clinic and introduces **sỳu-fuhk**/**mh sỳu-fuhk**,
where the negative is explicitly glossed as doing double duty for "uncomfortable" *and* the softer,
very common "unwell, off colour" — the book notes a Cantonese speaker may say they're merely "a bit
off colour" even when seriously ill, a register/understatement fact parallel to English. The
standout flagged item in this slice is an explicit cultural-register note contrasting **Jùng-gwok-
yàhn** (formal, "a Chinese [person]") and **sài-yàhn** ("a westerner") with the "more earthy and less
formal" pair **Tòhng-yàhn** ("a Chinese [person]") and **gwái-lóu** (literally "ghost fellow," a very
common informal/slang term for a westerner). The book is explicit that gwái-lóu is in very common use
and "not really to be considered offensive," while noting that the "strictly politically correct"
would avoid it — this is exactly the kind of taboo-adjacent/ethnic-register item the project flags
for its usage-tier and taboo dimensions, logged here as `slang` with a caveat rather than `taboo`
outright, per the source's own framing. Grammar points in this stretch also cover approximate-number
formation (**chāt-baat go yàhn**, "seven or eight people," plus the irregular workaround
**sahp-go-baat-go** for "nine or ten" since gáu-sahp already means "ninety"), the **sìn-ji** ("only
then," i.e. "not until") construction, **dá** used as a semantically bleached general-purpose "light
verb" across a cluster of fixed collocations (dá-bō, dá-léhng-tàai "tie a necktie," dá-dihn-wá "phone
call," dá-syun "intend") with an explicit author's warning not to invent new dá-idioms by analogy,
four-character set phrases (**tiu-gòu-mau-dài**, "jumping up and down," built from two ordinary
words), and the regular **seuhng-/hah-** ("above/below") and **chìhn-/hauh-** ("before/after")
pairing system for relative weeks, days, and years. Dialogue 2, a sick child (William) describing
medicine-dosing confusion to his father, supplies a nice example of reduplication marking ongoing/
distributed duration: **jó-yìuh-yauh-báai** ("shaking from side to side," literally left-shake-
right-sway) as his father's diagnostic question about his symptoms.

## Unit 11 — Adverbs of time, frequency and duration

Because Cantonese verbs carry no tense marking at all, adverbs are the primary (often the ONLY) means of locating an event in time — the book stresses this is a stronger dependency than in English, where tense marking can carry the temporal information even if the adverb is dropped. A fairly rich inventory of time adverbs is laid out by relative time-reference (present: yìhgā "now," gāmyaht "today"; recent past: tàuhsīn "just now"; past: yíhchìhn "before," gójahnsìh "then"; future: jīkhāak "right away," daih-yih-sìh "in future"), each of which can appear either immediately before the verb or, for some, before the subject. Frequency adverbs (sèhngyaht "always," dōsou "mostly," yáuh(-jahn)-sìh "sometimes") combine with múih "each" to build phrases like múih go yuht "every month" — but the book flags an important morphological exception: several common one-syllable time nouns (chi "time/occurrence," yaht "day," lìhn "year," jīu "morning," máahn "evening") skip the classifier go entirely in this construction and instead reduplicate directly (chi-chi "every time," yaht-yaht "every day," NOT *múih go chi/*múih go yaht) — an exception to the general classifier requirement introduced in Unit 8, worth noting for anyone building a classifier-exception list. Duration/frequency adverbials have two syntactic positions with different discourse effects: between verb and object (Ngóh heui-gwo yāt chi Daaihluhk "I've been to the mainland once"), or after the verb+object with the transitive verb repeated (Kéuih jyú-faahn jyú-gwo géi chi "He's cooked dinner a few times") — the latter pattern reuses the verb-repetition strategy already seen for manner adverbials in Unit 10, suggesting it's a general syntactic device in the grammar rather than specific to any one adverb type.

## Unit 12 — Comparison: *gwo* and *dī*

Two colloquial comparison strategies are distinguished by whether the standard of comparison is overtly expressed: gwo ("surpass," also a plain verb meaning "cross/pass") is used when there IS an explicit "than X" (Jenny sai gwo ngóh "Jenny is younger than me"), while dī (literally "a little") appears when the comparison is implicit/elided (Jenny sai dī "Jenny is younger [than some understood standard]"). Degree modification differs structurally between the two: gwo-comparisons add the degree phrase at the very end (gwo hóu dō "much more"), while dī-comparisons REPLACE dī with a degree word (gwai dī → gwai sīu-síu "a little more expensive," not *gwai dī hóu dō, which the book flags as a contradiction in terms since dī itself already means "a little"). The book is explicit that this dī is NOT structurally equivalent to the English comparative suffix "-er" despite the superficial translation parallel — a useful warning against over-mapping English morphology onto Cantonese syntax.

A second, more formal comparison strategy uses béi ("compare"), with reversed word order (béi + standard of comparison BEFORE the adjective: Haht(n béi dūngtīn chèuhng "Summer is longer than winter"). The book explicitly characterizes béi-comparison as "basically a feature of Mandarin and written Chinese" and therefore MORE FORMAL than the colloquial gwo construction — an explicit register/code-contact observation: Mandarin-influenced constructions carry higher formality in Cantonese, and learners with Mandarin background will find béi easier but should recognize its formal register. béi-comparison additionally permits expressing CHANGE OF STATE (Kéuih béi gauh-lín sau-jó "She's got thinner since last year"), which gwo cannot do. Superlatives use jeui (or informally ji — flagged by the book itself as "slang" in at least one listed example, ji jeng "the best, coolest (slang)"), typically attributively with ge or predicatively with haih, and the book notes a general Cantonese word-order tendency — also seen in Unit 22's topicalization discussion — of placing the more general/inclusive scope term before the more specific superlative claim (Hēunggóng jeui chēutméng ge gōsáu "the most famous singer IN HONG KONG," scope-noun first).

**Flag: "ji jeng" (the best, coolest) is explicitly labeled slang by the book itself** — one of the only places in this volume where the authors apply that label directly to a lexical item, making it a high-confidence slang data point rather than an inference.

## Unit 13 — Prepositions: space and time

Spatial expression draws on three interacting word classes: true prepositions (hái "at/in/on," yàuh "from," heung "towards," tùhng "with," g((ng "via," lèih "away from" — several of these "coverbs" share properties with verbs, per the book, a category boundary the authors flag as blurry and reserve fuller discussion of for the companion *Intermediate Cantonese* volume); demonstrative location terms built on nī "this"/gó "that" (nīdouh/gódouh "here/there," nībihn/góbihn, nītàuh/gótàuh); and localizers/postpositions ending characteristically in -bihn or -mihn ("side": seuhngbihn "on top," chìhnbihn "in front," yahpbihn "inside," deuimihn "opposite," jūnggāan "in the middle," between others). A structurally important and distinctly Cantonese pattern requires a localizer (or the colloquial dummy localizer douh "there") even when it appears semantically redundant: *Kéuihdeih hái ga chē is ungrammatical, but Kéuihdeih hái ga chē douh "They're in the car" is required — douh must be present as a syntactic placeholder even though it adds no real locative information, a fact worth flagging for grammar-logic work on obligatory-but-semantically-empty elements (compare hóu in Unit 7/9). Between two noun phrases with jūnggāan "between," tùhng links them (X tùhng Y jūnggāan). Separately, hái n(douh "to be here" is distinguished from the fixed idiom háidouh "to be present/here" used specifically to assert presence/absence (e.g., on the phone: Léih sāang m̀h háidouh "Mr. Lee is not here," not *hái nīdouh) — háidouh also independently functions as a progressive-aspect marker (cross-reference to Unit 19).

Movement/direction uses heung ("towards"); temporal/spatial starting points use yàuh ("from"), which can combine with dou ("to," marking the endpoint) in a yàuh...dou... "from...to..." frame; intermediate waypoints use g((ng ("via," e.g. a flight routed through Tokyo); and distance-from uses lèih with no verb needed at all (Y((yún lèih nīdouh géi yúhn a? "How far is the hospital from here?" — a verbless distance-stative construction).

## Unit 14 — Negation

Cantonese negators are unified by a shared phonological shape: all begin with nasal m and carry low-register tones, but the LEXICAL CHOICE among them is grammatically conditioned by what is being negated, a system the book lays out explicitly as a small closed paradigm: m̀h negates adjectives and present-tense verbs; móuh (the suppletive negative of yáuh, Unit 6) negates possession/existence and past-tense verbs; meih means specifically "not yet" and attaches to verbs; mhaih is the negative of haih and negates whole sentences/clauses; and the prefix m- derives lexicalized antonyms of certain adjectives and verbs (hōisām "happy" → mhōisām "unhappy"). The antonym-prefix pattern is NOT fully compositional/predictable: some m-forms shift meaning rather than simply negating it (gin "see" → mgin "LOSE" [an object], not merely "not see"; geidāk "remember" → mgeidāk "forget"), and a small closed set of words — mhóuyisi "embarrassed," mfahnhei "discontented" — exist ONLY in the negative-prefixed form; the book notes explicitly that the unprefixed forms (*hóuyisi, *fahnhei) are simply not used as independent words, making m- here a lexicalized/fossilized morpheme rather than a live negation process for these items.

Double negation is presented as a distinct, productive pragmatic strategy for softening or indirecting a positive claim rather than a a logical-error pattern: mhaih + negative statement yields a qualified positive (mhaih m̀h hólàhng ge "It's not impossible" = "it IS possible, but I'm hedging"), and negating both a main verb and an auxiliary (Unit 20) produces a similar indirect-positive effect (Léih m̀h hóyíh m̀h béi chín "You cannot choose not to pay" = "you must pay," an indirect-obligation construction). This is a genuine pragmatics/politeness-register finding: double negation here is a face-saving indirection strategy, not a rhetorical error, and is worth flagging for cross-linguistic comparison with indirect-speech-act literature.

## Unit 15 — Verbs of motion: *heui* and *làih*

heui ("go") and làih/lèih ("come") take destinations directly with NO preposition (Ngóh yāt-yuht heui Méihgwok "I'm going to America in January" — no equivalent of "to"), and can combine productively with a closed set of directional particles (yahp/chēut, séuhng/lohk, gwo, fāan) to form two-verb sequences (yahp heui "go in," chēut làih "come out") that behave syntactically just like the simple verbs. A further, more elaborate pattern stacks THREE verbs in a manner—direction—come/go sequence (fēi yahp làih "fly in [towards speaker]," dit lohk heui "fall down [away from speaker]"), and the book names this general phenomenon — multiple verbs concatenated with no linking conjunction — "serial verb constructions," a term that recurs for the jyuh-constructions of Unit 19 as well. When the directional-verb construction takes a transitive object, the object is sandwiched BETWEEN the first verb and the directional/heui-làih complex (Ngóhdeih séung daai dī sān tùhngsih yahp làih "We'd like to bring in some new colleagues" — object before "in come"), and aspect markers (jó, gán; Units 18–19) attach to the FIRST verb of the sequence, not the last — a word-order fact with clear implications for parsing any extended Cantonese serial-verb string. A further extension doubles heui and làih together into a four-verb iterative/repetitive pattern (hàahng làih hàahng heui "walk to and fro," lám làih lám heui "think it over and over") that is purely idiomatic/figurative rather than literally describing bidirectional motion in most of its uses.

## Unit 16 — Verbs of giving: *béi*

béi ("give") is a ditransitive verb whose direct object (the thing given) PRECEDES the indirect object (the recipient) — the reverse of both English and Mandarin word order, which the book explicitly flags as a genuinely Cantonese-specific syntactic fact (Kéuih béi-jó yāt baak mān ngóh, literally "she gave one-hundred-dollars me," not "me one-hundred-dollars"). With OTHER giving-type verbs (gei "send," wàahn "return," sung "give as a gift"), béi instead surfaces as a dative-marking preposition introducing the recipient AFTER the main verb+object (Ngóh pàhngyáuh gei-jó jūng kāat béi ngóh "My friend sent me a card"). A culturally loaded idiom is flagged: béi dī mín ("give some face") literally "give face," an explicit politeness/respect-marking idiom (Léih béi dī mín kéuih lā! "Show her some respect!") — worth cross-referencing against face/politeness literature in Chinese pragmatics generally.

The verb je is explicitly flagged as structurally AMBIGUOUS between "lend" and "borrow" depending purely on context/world knowledge (Ngóh je-jó kéuih yāt baak mān can mean either "I borrowed $100 from him" OR "I lent him $100," with no syntactic disambiguation) — this ambiguity is resolved only by adding either tùhng/heung ("from," clarifying "borrow") or béi ("to," clarifying "lend"). This je-ambiguity is a genuinely striking lexical-semantic fact (a single verb covering both directions of a transfer event, disambiguated only periphrastically) worth flagging for anyone doing verb-semantics or argument-structure comparison across languages. Separately, a distinct word-order pattern applies to a cluster of other two-object verbs (gaau "teach," mahn "ask," and the deprivation verbs faht "fine," tāu "steal," chéung "rob") where the INDIRECT object comes FIRST, unlike béi's direct-object-first pattern — meaning Cantonese does not have one uniform ditransitive word order but at least two distinct construction types keyed to different verb classes.

## Unit 17 — Verbs and particles

Cantonese verb+particle combinations are explicitly compared to English phrasal verbs (tiu héi "jump up," báai dāi "put down"), and are organized into three functional subtypes: (1) directional particles (yahp/chēut/héi/dāi/fāan/gwo/hōi/màaih) that often double as the directional verbs of Unit 15 but can shift to idiomatic/figurative meanings far from their literal spatial sense (héi "up" extends to Lóuhbáan hóu tái héi kéuih "The boss has a high opinion of him" — "look up at" metaphorically; fāan "back" extends to "resume a former role," Kéuih jouh fāan gíngchaat "He's gone back to being a policeman"); (2) resultative particles marking the outcome/extent of an action (báau "full up," dihm "conclusively [settled]," séi "to death" as in muhn séi "bored to death," sèhng "succeed," waaih "bad/broken" as in gaau waaih "lead astray"); and (3) a specific, productive sub-pattern of verbs of PERCEPTION + the particle dóu marking successful/completed perception, distinguishing the bare activity verb from its successful-outcome counterpart (tēng "listen" vs. tēng dóu "hear"; tái "look" vs. tái dóu "see"; wán "seek" vs. wán dóu "find"; màhn "smell [verb]" vs. màhn dóu "smell [something], detect a smell"). This activity/achievement distinction built on dóu is a clean, teachable Vendler-style aspectual contrast worth flagging explicitly for grammar-logic cross-referencing.

A related potential-construction family inserts m̀h or dāk BETWEEN the verb and its particle to express inability/ability respectively (heui m̀h dóu "cannot get there" vs. heui dāk dóu "can get there"; tēng m̀h mìhng "cannot understand [by listening]" vs. tēng dāk mìhng "can understand"), with some combinations carrying specialized idiomatic readings (máaih m̀h héi "cannot afford" / máaih dāk héi "can afford"; sihk m̀h gwo "cannot trust" is actually listed under seun, not sihk — the book's own list reads seun m̀h gwo "cannot trust" / seun dāk gwo "can trust"). This infixed-potential pattern is a distinctive piece of Cantonese morphosyntax (inserting ability/inability marking INSIDE a verb-particle unit) that deserves comparison against other languages' potential/ability morphology in later grammar-logic work.

## Unit 18 — Actions and events: *jó* and *gwo*

The book is emphatic that Cantonese "is said to lack tense" and that jó and gwo are ASPECT markers (concerned with whether an event is viewed as complete vs. experienced-at-least-once) rather than tense markers, and that equating either with English past tense is "neither a necessary nor a sufficient condition for their use." jó (perfective) marks an action as a completed whole and is favored by adverbs like yíhgīng "already," ngāam-ngāam "just," tàuhsīn "just now"; it can describe either a bounded past event or — notably — a duration continuing up to and including the present (Ngóh táai-jó bun yaht syū "I have been reading for half a day," present-relevant). gwo (experiential) marks that something has happened AT LEAST ONCE, roughly matching one sense of the English present perfect, and is favored by chàhnggīng "once" and required (not jó) with meih "not yet" (Ngóh meih yám-gwo nī jek jáu, NOT *yám-jó, "I've not drunk this wine before" — jó is explicitly ungrammatical here).

The jó/gwo contrast is subtle where both are grammatically possible on the same verb: the book's own gloss is that jó "puts a focus on the result or current relevance of the action," while gwo "makes it of less immediate relevance" — concretely, Kéuih heui-jó gēichèuhng "He's gone to the airport (and is still there/en route)" versus Kéuih heui-gwo gēichèuhng "He's been to the airport (but is no longer there)" — a genuine current-relevance/perfect-of-result vs. experiential-perfect distinction, a useful minimal pair for any cross-linguistic aspect-typology comparison. Negation interacts asymmetrically with the two markers: jó cannot co-occur with any negator at all (its negative counterpart is simply móuh or meih used alone, NOT móuh/meih+jó — the combination is explicitly marked ungrammatical), whereas gwo CAN be negated by either móuh or meih, a real asymmetry in how the two aspect markers interact with the negation system laid out in Unit 14.

## Unit 19 — Activities: *gán* and *jyuh*

Two aspect markers cover ongoing situations, split along a dynamic/static axis that the book treats as the organizing principle rather than a hard rule: gán (progressive, like English "-ing") marks DYNAMIC, changing activities and, unlike English "-ing," is syntactically OPTIONAL — the same progressive meaning can often be conveyed with no aspect marker at all, provided a sentence particle fills the slot instead (cross-reference Unit 25). gán can also describe a PAST ongoing activity when a past-time adverb or subordinate-clause context supplies the temporal anchor (Gauh lín kéuih juhng duhk-gán jūnghohk "Last year she was still studying in secondary school" — gán here, despite being glossed "progressive," is not restricted to present time). An alternative progressive strategy reuses háidouh (literally "to be here/there," the same item flagging presence in Unit 13) as a progressive auxiliary, appropriate specifically for activities happening near the speaker, and the two strategies can co-occur (háidouh jyú-gán-faahn "is [here] cooking").

jyuh (continuous) marks STATIC situations/states without change, and is lexically tied to particular verb classes: stationary-situation verbs (jó-jyuh "blocking," using the resultative particle jó — note: unrelated to perfective jó, a different morpheme that happens to share the same romanized spelling, worth flagging as a potential parsing trap) and verbs describing "putting something into a state" (kám-jyuh "cover [and leave covered]," bóng-jyuh "tie up [and leave tied]"). Critically, jyuh can shift a verb's core meaning rather than simply adding continuity: lám "think" vs. lám-jyuh "INTEND" (not merely "be thinking"); tái "look/see" vs. tái-jyuh "WATCH OVER" (supervise) — these are not transparent aspectual extensions but partially lexicalized verb+jyuh compounds with distinct meanings, worth flagging for anyone building a verb-particle lexicon. jyuh additionally marks simultaneity between two actions with NO linking conjunction (Kéuih mohng-jyuh ngóh siu, literally "he watching me smiled" = "he smiled while looking at me"), reusing the same "serial verb construction" label the book applies to the heui/làih sequences of Unit 15. Where either gán or jyuh is grammatically possible on the same verb, the dynamic/static contrast produces a real meaning difference (ló-gán dī wuhngeuih "he's FETCHING the toys" [dynamic/changing] vs. ló-jyuh dī wuhngeuih "he's HOLDING the toys" [static]) — a clean minimal-pair pair worth flagging for progressive/continuous aspect typology work.

## Summary of the chapter's argument

Simpson's chapter treats Hong Kong as a special case within the volume's broader survey of language and national identity in Asia, arguing that Hong Kong's history — 155 years as a British colony (1842–1997) followed by return to China as a Special Administrative Region under "One Country, Two Systems" — produced an identity dynamic with no real parallel elsewhere in the region. The chapter is organized in three historical phases: identity formation during Hong Kong's economic boom (1960s–1970s), growing anxiety in the run-up to the 1997 handover (1980s–1990s), and the actual post-1997 period.

In the first phase, Simpson traces how a genuinely local "Hongkongese" identity crystallized only once Hong Kong's population stabilized. Before 1949, the territory's population was in constant flux — migrant laborers came from various parts of China (speakers of Hakka, Shanghainese, Hokkien, Chaozhou as well as Cantonese) but regarded their home villages on the mainland as "home" and moved back and forth. Mao's 1949 victory sealed the border, trapping this population in Hong Kong and forcing a shift from sojourner mentality to settled residency. Combined with the territory's rapid industrialization and the standard-of-living gap that opened up between Hong Kong and Mao-era China (particularly during the chaos of the Cultural Revolution), this gave rise — by the 1970s — to a distinct identity that Simpson characterizes as modern, Western-influenced, materialist, urban, and politically non-aspirational (no one in Hong Kong seriously expected or sought national independence, so the identity formed "from below," organically, rather than through nationalist elite mobilization).

Cantonese is central to this story: it became Hong Kong's lingua franca among immigrants from different mainland dialect regions, the medium of instruction in primary schools, and — crucially — the carrier of Hong Kong's enormously successful popular culture (Cantopop, Cantonese cinema and television), which both expressed and reinforced the new local identity. English, meanwhile, held the official and elite H-domain functions (government, law, international business) in what Simpson, following Pennington and Yue (1994), calls a diglossic relationship with Cantonese as the L-variety — but critically, English was adopted purely instrumentally (for economic/career access) and never became an object of identification with Britain; using English in casual contexts with other Cantonese speakers was in fact felt to be pretentious and "un-Chinese." A key turning point came in 1974, when lobbying by language activists won "Chinese" (ambiguously covering spoken Cantonese and written Modern Standard Chinese) co-official status alongside English — though Cantonese's lack of a standardized, prestigious written form has continued to cap its expansion into fully H-level domains.

The second phase (1980s–1990s) is defined by anxiety over the scheduled 1997 handover, intensified by the 1989 Tiananmen Square crackdown, which triggered emigration waves and also, paradoxically, a peak moment of shared Hong Kong identity (over a million people protesting together). Simpson flags two complications in how Hong Kong identity is understood during this period: (1) a split between pride in classical/historical Chinese culture (which Hongkongese largely embrace) versus rejection of modern/political mainland China (which they largely do not identify with), and (2) the argument (after Lau 1997) that much of the "Hongkongese" identification found in surveys may be more an attachment to a lifestyle/standard of living than to the place or people as such — comparable to the ambivalent love-hate relationship residents of cities like New York or London have toward their hometowns.

Linguistically, this period saw five developments Simpson treats as significant: (1) English lost its "colonizer's language" stigma and came to be seen as a neutral, globally useful asset rather than a marker of loyalty to Britain; (2) a new Cantonese-English **mixed code** emerged, starting in classrooms (where English-medium instruction was supplemented with Cantonese explanation, and technical/specialized vocabulary was inserted from English into a Cantonese grammatical base) and then spreading as a prestige youth sociolect used even outside any comprehension need — sounding "educated," "modern," "western," while avoiding both old-fashioned Chinese-ness and the artificiality of speaking pure English with Chinese friends; (3) unmixed Cantonese expanded into government debate, became the dominant language of the courts by the mid-1990s, and grew substantially as a written vernacular (popular fiction, newspapers, advertising), gaining "overt" prestige in law/politics and "covert" prestige in colloquial writing, even though it remains excluded from more formal written genres; (4) English-medium secondary schooling remained in high demand despite government findings that most students weren't actually benefiting academically from it, creating an unresolved policy dilemma between bilingual-education effectiveness and perceived career necessity; (5) Mandarin ("putonghua") began to be taken seriously as a coming necessity, formalized in the "yi-man-saam-yuh" (二文三語, literally "two written languages, three spoken languages") policy aiming for biliteracy in English/Chinese and trilingual speaking ability in English, Cantonese, and Mandarin — complicated by Mandarin's dual association (positive: PRC political power; negative: the less-developed north of China) and by practical obstacles (teacher shortages, overcrowded curricula). By 1996, self-reported proficiency stood at 89% Cantonese, 35% English, and 25% Mandarin (Chao 2002) — Mandarin having crept in informally via business contact well ahead of the school system.

The third phase (post-1997) is characterized, somewhat surprisingly, by day-to-day continuity: Beijing largely honored its non-interference pledge, and Simpson credits it with restraint even around sensitive events like annual Tiananmen memorial demonstrations. The real shocks to Hong Kong's identity came from elsewhere — the Asian financial crisis, a collapsing property market, avian flu, and a bungled new-airport opening — which, combined with a widely perceived incompetent new SAR administration under Chief Executive Tung Chee-hwa, undercut the economic-success component that had underpinned Hong Kong identity since the 1970s. Looking forward, Simpson frames Hong Kong's language situation as poised between two possible trajectories: deeper economic integration with the mainland (favoring Mandarin and a more "Chinese-rooted" identity) versus a renewed push toward international/Western ties (favoring English and the cosmopolitan Cantonese-based identity). He argues that regardless of which of English or Mandarin ends up more prominent as a utilitarian lingua franca, neither can substitute for Cantonese as the actual carrier of Hong Kong's distinctive identity — Beijing's hands-off language policy since 1997 has let Cantonese continue to dominate everyday life essentially unchallenged. He closes by noting the open question of whether Mandarin, as a culturally "less foreign" H-domain challenger than English ever was, might eventually prove more corrosive to Cantonese-centered identity than the English/British presence ever managed to be — precisely because Hong Kong never had any temptation to assimilate into British culture, whereas Mandarin comes bundled with a modern Chinese culture (film, pop music) that could compete more directly with Cantonese's own cultural products.

For this project's interests specifically: the chapter is valuable less as a source of individual slang items and more as sociolinguistic/historical scaffolding for the Cantonese corpus — it documents the social mechanics and prestige dynamics (diglossia tipping into Cantonese-English mixed code, the specific H-domain/L-domain split, the overt-vs-covert prestige distinction for written Cantonese) that would predict where and why code-mixing and register-specific slang arise in Hong Kong Cantonese, without itself cataloguing concrete mixed-code utterances or vocabulary items.

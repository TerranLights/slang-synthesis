# Cantonese: A Comprehensive Grammar (Matthews & Yip) — Units 151-160 (pp. 280-299)

**Source:** Matthews, S. & Yip, V., *Cantonese: A Comprehensive Grammar*. DjVu pages 280-299
(10 rendered units, 2 printed pages per unit). Covers the tail end of Chapter 15 (Existential
Sentences), sections 15.2-15.5, and the first half of Chapter 16 (Coordination and Subordinate
Clauses), sections 16.1-16.2.3.

## Prose summary

The range opens mid-Chapter 15 with the combination *yáuh/móuh* + verb (15.2), used primarily in
questions and negative statements about the past ("Did you see Chan?" / "I haven't been out
today"), paralleling the more familiar *[verb]-jó* perfective pattern. The authors note two further
declarative uses: an emphatic past-tense affirmative counterpart to the negative/interrogative
pattern ("That time we *did* go to Stanley"), and a habitual-present reading ("The paper talks
about the stock market every day"), the latter also available inside *yáuh-móuh* questions. A
cluster of idioms built on *yáuh* + verb is flagged throughout as colloquial/register-marked: *yáuh-jó
(bìhbī)* "to be pregnant" (note: *jó* here is the perfective marker, and the construction is
explicitly glossed as colloquial euphemism rather than a clinical term), *yáuh sām* "to have the
intention / to do something on purpose" (with a change-toned negative counterpart *móuh sām*, "I
didn't mean to"), *yáuh fāan gam seuhng há* "to be of a recurrent/typical standard" (note the tone
change in *hah* → *há*), *yáuh lohk* (a fixed phrase used specifically on minibuses to ask the
driver to stop), *yáuh góng yáuh siu* "to get on well together" (lit. "have talk have laugh"), and
*yáuh yèhng yáuh syū* "to have wins and losses" (a gambling-register idiom). Section 15.3 covers
*yáuh* + adjective, where *yáuh* plus a quantity word (*dī*, *síu-síu*) hedges a following
adjective ("seems a bit off," "feels slightly different"), and *yáuh-móuh* also forms interrogative
and rhetorical comparisons. A striking exclamative pattern appears in a boxed aside: *gam [adj] dōu
yáuh gé!* expresses surprise/incredulity ("Gosh, I never knew it could rain like this!"), and *yáuh
mātyéh [adj]* is used to rhetorically query the value of something ("What's so good about
football?").

Section 15.4 turns to *móuh*, the suppletive negative counterpart of *yáuh* (the book is explicit
that Hongkong Cantonese has no combination *\*mh yáuh*). *Móuh* parallels *yáuh*'s possessive and
existential uses, can itself take the perfective *-jó* (denoting a change from having to not
having something) and the verbal particles *saai* "completely" and *màaih* "as well," and combines
with a question word to produce a qualified "any/not much" (*móuh mātyéh*, *móuh māt*), extending
the general wh-word + quantificational-particle pattern already seen in Chapter 14. A dense run of
*móuh*-idioms is boxed as colloquial: *móuh géi dō* "not many/much," *móuh géi loih (chìhn)* "not
long ago" (word order note: with *chìhn* the time phrase must precede the verb, without it the
meaning shifts slightly), *móuh baahnfaat* "no way," *móuh hólàhng* "no chance," *móuh léihyàuh*
"no reason" — used to voice the speaker's own incomprehension or incredulity, not as a literal
claim about reasons — *móuh (ngóh/léih) fán* "I/you have no part to play" (note the tone change
*fahn* → *fán*), and *móuh sówaih* "(I) don't mind," expressing indifference between alternatives.
*Móuh* also occurs in negative comparisons. Section 15.5, Presentative Sentences, explains that
because Cantonese subjects/topics are normally definite, an indefinite noun phrase can only appear
as a sentence subject if introduced by *yáuh* — this is the construction that opens stories and
fairy tales ("Once upon a time there was a big forest...," "Long ago there lived a beautiful
princess..."), and the authors note a functional parallel: other presentative sentences lacking
*yáuh/móuh* but using an ordinary verb to introduce an indefinite NP correspond to English
non-restrictive relative clauses ("I saw a suit today which was quite nice").

Chapter 16 (Coordination and Subordinate Clauses) opens by foregrounding a typological point:
Cantonese coordinates NPs largely by juxtaposition (no overt "and" needed), and where overt
conjunctions exist they're selected by what's being joined rather than a single general-purpose
"and." *Tùhng* "with" coordinates noun phrases (with no fixed ordering constraint on pronouns —
*kéuih tùhng ngóh* and *ngóh tùhng kéuih* are both grammatical, differing only in topic-hood); *yauh
... yauh* "both ... and" is an emphatic conjunction for adjectives/verbs (including the idiom *yauh
pèhng yauh leng* "cheap and cheerful"); lists of three or more items typically need no conjunction
at all, just pauses (optionally filled with a sentence particle like *a* or *lā* while the speaker
thinks of the next item), with *dōu* "all" used to sum up a list of topicalized items, and
*tùhng(màaih)* "and (also)" joining only the final item — including, notably, as an afterthought
tacked on after a trailing-off list. Section 16.1.3 catalogs clause-coordinating adverbials:
*juhngyáuh/juhngyiu* "also, moreover" (adding an item felt to have been missed), *gānjyuh*
"next"/*yihnhauh* "afterwards" for event sequences, the highly multifunctional *jauh* "then,
therefore" (temporal sequence, consequence, and the consequent clause of conditionals — occupying
the position right after the subject of its clause), *sóyíh* "so, therefore" (which can also pair
with *yānwaih* "because" as a double conjunction), *daahnhaih*/*bātgwo* "but" (typically paired with
*sēuiyìhn* "although"), and *fáanyìh* "however, on the other hand" (syntactically parallel to
*jauh*, in second position after the subject of its clause) — the worked example for *fáanyìh*
happens to be a cultural aside about the number 13 being unlucky to foreigners but considered
lucky by Chinese, cross-referenced to the book's numerology section (21.1.4). *Gitgwó* "as a
result / as it turned out" is also introduced here. Section 16.1.4 covers disjunction: *waahkjé*
(less certain) vs. *yāthaih* (more decisive/emphatic) both repeat to give "either ... or," with
*yāthaih ... yāthaih* fitting ultimatums ("pay the rent now or move out"); crucially, neither word
is used in alternative ("A or B?") questions, which instead use *dihng*/*yīkwaahk* (cross-referenced
to 17.2, not itself shown in this range). *Yauh ... yauh* with negated verbs/adjectives gives
"neither ... nor."

Section 16.2 sets up the chapter's central typological claim: Cantonese subordination behaves very
differently from European-language hypotaxis, leaning instead on parataxis (symmetrical
juxtaposition of clauses) — observable in (a) "double conjunction" pairs opening both clauses
(*yāt ... jauh* "as soon as ... then," *yānwaih ... sóyíh* "because ... therefore," *sēuiyìhn ...
daahnhaih* "although ... nevertheless," *yùhgwó ... jauh* "if ... then"), (b) some subordinators
that appear at *both* the start and the end of their clause, (c) subordinate clauses patterning
like sentence adverbs by following the subject/topic, (d) a strict pre-verbal ordering for purpose/
reason/time clauses that the authors explicitly attribute to Tai's (1985) iconicity thesis — clause
order mirrors temporal and cause-effect sequence far more rigidly than in English, where reversed
clause order is common — and (e) a ban on forward-referring (cataphoric) pronouns inside such
clauses, forcing the full NP to appear first as topic even when it would naturally reduce to a
pronoun in English. Section 16.2.1 (Time Clauses) catalogs clause-final temporal subordinators:
*gójahnsí* "when" (short forms *gójahn*/*góján*, the latter with a tone change), *jīchìhn* "before"
and *jīhauh* "after"; *dáng* "until" can combine with *gójahnsí* or *ge sìhhauh* for "by the time";
*dōng* "when/while" is explicitly flagged as optional and largely literary/formal, also paired with
clause-final *gójahnsí*/*ge sìhhauh*. Notably, *ge sìhhauh* is explicitly identified by the authors
as modeled on the Mandarin construction *de shíhòu* — a direct Mandarin-contact/calque observation
worth flagging for the language-contact layer. The negative *meih* "not yet" can appear
expletively (without real negative force) inside a *jīchìhn* "before" clause. Further time-clause
material: *yùhn(jó) jīhauh* "afterwards" as an adverbial phrase; *gam loih* "so long" used loosely
to mean "since," demonstrated with an example where it covers only "a few weeks"; the formal
conjunction *jihchùhng* "since" (clause-final *jīhauh*); and an explicit note that English
before/after relations are often rendered by bare serial-verb constructions with no subordinator at
all, optionally disambiguated by the adverb *sīn(ji)* "first" or the verbal particle *yùhn*
"finish." The combination *yāt ... jauh* "once ... then" covers both "as soon as" and habitual
"whenever," *nītàuh ... gótàuh* (lit. "this end ... that end") juxtaposes near-simultaneous events
("no sooner had she said she wanted to marry than she wanted a divorce"), and *yāt louh ... yāt
louh* / *yāt bihn ... yāt bihn* mark genuinely simultaneous activities — the authors note that,
unlike English "while," neither clause is grammatically subordinate to the other here; the
parallel structure iconically matches the parallel actions. Section 16.2.2 (Reason Clauses) keeps
*yānwaih* "because" clause-initial, paired with *sóyíh* "so" in the main clause, though the order
can reverse (because-clause last) with an emphatic *haih* linking the clauses; *yānwaih* also
commonly co-occurs with the noun *yùhnyān* "reason." *Geiyìhn* "since" is flagged as a formal
conjunction. The unstressed particle *maih* (commonly reduced to *mah*) is introduced as a
near-synonym of consequential *jauh*, with a specific noted affinity for co-occurring with the
sentence particle *lō* (18.3.3). The range closes with the opening of Section 16.2.3 (Purpose
Clauses): *waihjó* "in order to" introduces a purpose clause that — unlike English — sits *between*
subject and verb rather than after the main verb, again attributed to the cause-before-effect
iconicity principle (with the same *haih*-reversal option as reason clauses); *làih* serves as the
complement of an object, typically after the verb *yuhng* "use" in a serial construction ("use a
sharper knife to cut the cake"); and *yiu làih* is used to ask/describe the purpose of something
("What are these for?").

Nothing in this stretch is marked taboo. The most linguistically notable items are: (1) the tone-
sandhi/tone-change notes scattered through the idiom boxes (*hah*→*há*, *fahn*→*fán*, *gójahn*→
*góján*) — small but real lexicalized tone changes tied to specific fixed phrases rather than
general phonological rule; (2) the explicit Mandarin-calque flag on *ge sìhhauh* (modeled on
Mandarin *de shíhòu*), a concrete piece of language-contact evidence; (3) the Tai (1985) iconicity
citation, a genuine grammar-internal typological claim (clause order mirrors cause/effect and
temporal sequence far more strictly than in English) rather than a surface-level correlation; and
(4) the cluster of colloquial/idiomatic *yáuh*/*móuh* set phrases, several explicitly labeled
"idiom" in the book's own boxed asides, which are prime candidates for the slang-synthesis
register layer.

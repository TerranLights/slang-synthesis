## Prose summary

The range opens mid-list of **sortal classifiers** (6.2.3): small, specific-use classifiers like
*bá* (tools/instruments, but not musical instruments), *ga* (vehicles and large machines), *fahn*
("copy/part" — used for jobs, newspapers, insurance policies, and idiomatically for "salary"), and
a long table of classifiers tied to shape or function (*bouh* for machines/volumes, *bún* for
bound volumes, *chàhng* for building storeys, *douh* for places, *gāan* for buildings, *tiuh* for
long thin objects, *wàih* for table "rounds," etc.). The book stresses that classifier choice is
not arbitrary per noun but meaningfully tied to the *kind* of referent — the noun *yéh* "thing" can
take almost any classifier depending on what is meant (object vs. animal vs. affair vs. words).
Section 6.2.4 covers alternative classifiers, where choice varies by speaker, dialect, age, or
rhetorical intent — e.g. the polite/respectful classifier *wái* for people versus the neutral *go*.

The most linguistically striking material in this range is the **classifier-as-register-marker**
phenomenon (6.2.4, pp. 106–107): using a classifier normally reserved for *objects* or *animals* to
refer to a person has a deliberately demeaning or jocular effect, and the book flags several
resulting forms explicitly as slang or vulgar. *Tiuh* (normally for long thin objects) applied to
people yields slang/vulgar terms for romantic partners (*gó tiuh yáu* "that guy" [slang], *léih
tiuh léui* / *léih tiuh choi* "your girlfriend" [slang/vulgar — *choi* literally "vegetable"],
*ngóh tiuh jái* "my boyfriend" [vulgar]). The animal classifier *jek*, applied to people for
jocular or derisive effect, produces *jek (máhlāu) jái* "my monkey of a boy," *jek gāi* "prostitute"
(literally "chicken"), and *jek ngaap* "male prostitute" (literally "duck") — the latter two are
well-known Cantonese slang/taboo terms for sex workers built directly on the animal-classifier
demeaning mechanism the grammar describes. This is a clean example of a grammatical device
(classifier selection) being repurposed as a productive slang-formation mechanism — register
inversion via mismatched classifier choice.

Section 6.3 covers **possessive constructions**: the default *ge* possessive marker (parallel to
Mandarin *de*); the literary/formal variant *dīk* (used in songs and formal registers, Cantonese
reading of Mandarin *de*); *ge*-drop with kinship terms and inalienable possession, especially
with pronoun possessors (*ngóh sailóu* "my younger brother," *ngóh ngoihgā* "my husband's family");
and an alternative possessive using the classifier in place of *ge* (*ngóh gāan ūk* "my house"),
which the authors note is more colloquial. Up to four possessive constructions can exist for the
same phrase, ranging from formal (*ge*) to colloquial (classifier + optional demonstrative). The
same [X *ge*/CL] structure extends to "associative" constructions where the modifying phrase is
not strictly a possessor but merely associated with the head noun (*sihk gó dī yéh* "things for
eating"), and to adjectival-passive and relative-clause structures.

Section 6.4 (**relative clauses**) is the chapter's most structurally dense material. Cantonese
relative clauses place the modifying clause *before* the head noun (reverse of English), using
either *ge* (6.4.1, more formal) or demonstrative+classifier (6.4.2, strongly preferred in
colloquial speech, and also used where *ge* would be awkward, e.g. with a demonstrative-phrase
head). Resumptive pronouns (typically *kéuih*/*kéuihdeih*) are required when the head noun is an
indirect object, object of a coverb, object of comparison, or possessor within the relative
clause — a construction with no direct English equivalent (e.g. "the people who I make more money
than" using a resumptive pronoun after the comparative coverb *gwo*). Section 6.4.3 covers **free
relative clauses**, with two subtypes: (a) a *ge*-clause with no head noun, parallel to English
"what" (*Ngóh séung góng ge dōu móuh góng* "What I wanted to say I didn't say"), and (b) a
question-word-echo construction corresponding to English "-ever" forms, where the question word is
repeated in both clauses (*bīngo yáuh chín, bīngo béi* "whoever has the money pays"; colloquial
shortened forms *māt* "what," *dím* "how," *bīn* "where" appear frequently here).

Chapter 7 opens with **location and spatial prepositions**. The basic location terms are
demonstrative-based compounds (*nīdouh* "here" / *gódouh* "there", plus *-bihn*, *-syu*, *-tàuh*
variants), used after a coverb, after a motion verb, as sentence topics with *yáuh/móuh*, or with a
place name. Section 7.1.1 covers the general-purpose locative coverb *hái* ("be at/in," glossed
more precisely as "be located") and its variant *héung*, in four constructions: alone with place
names, in a serial construction functioning as a preposition, with a bare localizer as an adverb,
and in the highly productive [*hái* – NP – localizer] pattern. A full localizer inventory is given:
*yahpbihn/léuihmihn* "inside," *chēutbihn/ngoihmihn* "outside," *chìhnmihn* "in front,"
*hauhbihn* "behind," *seuhng(mihn/bihn)* "on top," *hah(mihn/bihn)* "below," *deuimihn*
"opposite," *jūng/jūnggāan* "middle," *jīgāan* "between" (used with *tùhng* to coordinate two
referents), and *káhn* "near." Section 7.1.2 covers distance/movement (*lèih* "away from," *hái
... làih/heui* "from X to Y," *yàuh* "from" with three sub-uses: distance, time-reference, and
source of responsibility/sponsorship; *chùhng* as a more formal equivalent of *yàuh*). Section
7.1.3 covers direction (*góbihn/nībihn* "that way/this way," *heung* "towards" in a directional
sense, *gīng(gwo)* "via" in serial constructions, also usable as a plain verb "pass by"). Section
7.1.4 covers placement, where a locative PP follows the object of a placement verb, with an
alternative serial construction using *jēung*. Section 7.2 begins non-spatial coverbs: *deui*
"towards" in a non-directional, relational sense (personal feelings/attitudes — *deui chín móuh
pīngin* "have no prejudice against money"), *dou* with time expressions meaning "until/by," *gān*
"with" (from the verb "follow"), its extended forms *gānjyuh* "with, following" and *gāngeui*
"according to," and *jiu*/*jiujyuh* "following/according to."

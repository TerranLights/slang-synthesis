# Colloquial Cantonese (2010 edition, Routledge) — Part 2: Units 6–10

**Source:** Dana Scott Bourgerie, Keith S.T. Tong, and Gregory James. *Colloquial Cantonese: The Complete Course for Beginners*, 2nd edition (Routledge, 2010). Continuation of extraction from `030a`; see that file for source/methodology notes. Romanization: Yale system.

## Unit 6 — Gachìhn (Prices)

Unit 6 extends the numbers system from Unit 2 up to one million, teaches comparison (-gwo for comparing two named items, -dc when only one item is named, jeui for superlatives among three or more), and introduces the aspect marker -jó for completed action (e.g., máaih-jó "bought"). It also covers "uncertain" quantities using géi as a wildcard digit (sahp-géi man "ten-odd dollars," géi-chcn man "several thousand dollars") and the colloquial contraction of cents, where hòuhjí ("ten-cent unit") is dropped and go substitutes for man between the dollar and cent figures (e.g., saam go sei "$3.40" instead of the fuller saam man sei hòuhjí).

The unit's two main adjectives for price comparison, pèhng ("cheap") and gwai ("expensive"), are supplemented by dái ("good value," lit. close to "worth it"). A Cultural point covers Hong Kong currency history (the HK dollar's historical US-dollar peg) and notes that special "banker's" numerals (distinct from the simpler everyday Chinese numerals, designed to resist tampering) were formerly used in markets, though Arabic numerals have largely displaced them.

## Unit 7 — Faan-geng (Commuting)

Unit 7 covers commuting vocabulary (means of transportation, all formed with the verb daap "to take/ride"), duration-of-time expressions, and the modal pair yiu ("need to") / msái ("do not need to") for necessity — explicitly noted as an asymmetric pair: sái only ever appears negated (msái), while yiu only ever appears positive as the necessity modal, so a choice-type necessity question must use sái-mh-sái, never *yiu-mh-yiu.

Grammar notes also cover dou as a directional-success particle after motion verbs (faan dou gengsc "arrive at the office"), the intensifying scnji ("only then," stressing effort/duration) contrasted with the effortless jauh dak laak ("and that's all it takes"), and sèhng ("a whole [unit of time]"). An exercise contrasts an "optimist" and a "moaner" describing identical commutes with different affect — a nice illustration of how discourse particles alone can carry attitude in Cantonese. The Cultural point describes Hong Kong's MTR subway system.

## Unit 8 — Kéuihdeih jouh-gán matyéh? (What are they doing?)

Unit 8 covers household/room vocabulary, the progressive aspect marker -gán ("in the process of"), and further uses of -jó including the "where + why" construction (heui-jó gaaisíh máaih saanggwó "has gone to the market to buy fruit") and the fixed faan-jó ngekkéi/hohkhaauh/gengsc ("has gone/come home/to school/to the office" — faan, never heui, for these three destinations regardless of the speaker's own location).

A useful register note distinguishes tbng (larger rooms for entertaining, e.g., haaktbng "sitting room," faahntbng "dining room," lit. "guest room"/"meal room") from fóng (smaller private rooms). The word scnsaang is shown to be triply polysemous (polite address for a man / "teacher" / "husband"), disambiguated by whether the classifier go is present. The Cultural point flags a distinctly Cantonese greeting pattern: rather than (or alongside) the formal Néih hóu, people commonly greet by stating the obvious about what the other person is doing — faangong a? ("off to work, then?") or sihkfaahn a? ("eating, are you?") — functioning as pure social acknowledgment rather than a real question, comparable to English small talk about the weather.

## Unit 9 — Bdng ngóh jouh dc yéh (Can you help me?)

Unit 9 teaches requesting favors and housework vocabulary, the aspect word meih ("not yet," for actions still pending — dc saam meih sái "the clothes haven't been washed yet"), and yùhn for discussing when an ongoing action will finish. Four distinct politeness strategies for asking a favor are laid out explicitly: the modal hóyíh in a choice-type question, the question-phrase dak-mh-dak, opening with Mgdi néih ("please"), and opening with the more socially loaded Màhfàahn néih ("may I trouble you"), which the book flags as more appropriate among people of similar or higher status/age.

Several sentence-final particles are explained for their emotional coloring: la for cheerful acceptance, the particle a added to Mdak a to convey regretful refusal, and Deuimjyuh ("sorry") to soften a refusal further. Housework verb-object vocabulary includes some notably non-transparent compounds (jyú-faahn "cook," lit. "cook-rice"; dóu-laahpsaap "empty the trash," lit. "pour-rubbish"; kap-chàhn "vacuum," lit. "suck-dust"). The Cultural point discusses Chinese friendship/obligation norms, including the convention of close friends asking favors (e.g., carrying a package) once a relationship is established.

## Unit 10 — Hái bcndouh? (Where is it?)

Unit 10 covers locating objects and shops, personal-belongings and shop-type vocabulary, and the verbal particle dóu for indicating success in an action (wán dóu "managed to find," gin dóu "managed to see/spot"). Cantonese locative word order is explicitly contrasted with English: Noun-A hái Noun-B Adverb-of-location, with the location adverb (seuhngmihn "on top," hahmihn "underneath," gaaklèih "beside," chìhnmihn "in front," hauhmihn "behind," jenggaan "between") placed after the reference noun rather than before it as an English preposition would be.

Discourse particles get another close look: the mhaih...ld! pattern turns a negative into a positive rhetorical emphasis ("Aren't those your glasses...!"), and the double particle a ma marks acknowledgment of a repeated statement. The shop-vocabulary section notes two productive shop-naming suffixes, dim and póu (both "shop"), plus the loan-translation compound chcukapsíhchèuhng "supermarket" (lit. "super" + "market").

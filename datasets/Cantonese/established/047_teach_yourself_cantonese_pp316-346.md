# Teach Yourself Cantonese — back matter: bidirectional glossary + publisher advertising (PDF pp. 316-346)

**Source:** *Teach Yourself Cantonese* (image-only PDF, 346 pages total). This file covers the
final slice of the book, PDF pages 316-346, which is entirely back matter: no new course units,
no running index of topics/page numbers. No handwritten marginalia was present on any rendered
page; nothing was excluded on those grounds.

**Coverage breakdown:**
- PDF pp. 316-329 (printed pp. 301-314): **Cantonese-English vocabulary** — the master glossary,
  alphabetized by Cantonese romanization (Yale-style with tone diacritics), covering roughly
  L through Y. Each entry gives the headword, an English gloss, and a parenthetical number marking
  the course unit (1-26) in which the word was first introduced — not a page number.
- PDF pp. 330-343 (printed pp. 315-328): **English-Cantonese vocabulary** — the same glossary
  content reversed, alphabetized by English gloss. This is substantially the same lexical data as
  the Cantonese-English half (same headwords, same unit numbers), just re-sorted for lookup in the
  other direction, so it was not re-tabulated entry-for-entry below; only items with wording,
  nuance, or unit-cluster information not already captured from the Cantonese-English half are
  added as new rows.
- PDF pp. 344-346: **publisher back-matter advertisements** for three unrelated *Teach Yourself*
  titles (*World Cultures: China* by Kenneth Wilkinson, *Chinese* by Elizabeth Scurfield, and
  *Beginner's Chinese Script* by Scurfield & Song Lianyi) — promotional catalogue copy in English
  only, with no Cantonese-language content whatsoever. Nothing from these three pages was
  extractable and no rows were added for them.

## Overview

This is a closed-class reference glossary rather than running prose, so there is little to
summarize narratively; the interest is in what the glossary's own structure reveals. First, the
glossary is the single best concentration of Cantonese **sentence-final particles** (marked "fp:"
in the book's own gloss style) anywhere sampled from this course so far: *laak*, *mē?*, *nē?*
(two distinct senses), *lo*, *ma?*, and *tìm* all turn up in this one alphabetical stretch,
each glossed not with a lexical meaning but with a pragmatic function ("that's how the case
stands now," "agreement with previous speaker," "makes questions," "as well, what's more"). These
particles are exactly the kind of register-shortcut feature the project should track closely:
they compress an entire attitudinal or discourse-stance clause into a single clitic-like syllable,
and English has no equivalent closed class — it would need a full adverbial phrase or tag question
to do the same pragmatic work.

Second, a tight cluster of vocabulary around course unit 17-18 (evidently a "law and order" /
crime unit) supplies genuine taboo and slang-adjacent material: *séi-jái* "deadbeats, bastards"
(literally built on *séi* "die, dead" — a taboo-root insult formation worth flagging for the
taboo_inversion/compounding analysis track), alongside *sáu-chēung* "handgun, pistol," *sáu-jūk*
"brothers (secret society)" (a clear triad/underworld-register term glossed candidly as such by
the book itself), *sáu-sàan* "conduct a body search," *dá-gip* "rob," *màuh-saat* "murder," and
*tàu-yéh* "steal." The glossary also preserves one idiom worth a closer look later: *waahk-gwái-
geuk*, literally "draw a ghost's leg," glossed only by its literal translation with no explanation
of the idiomatic sense (it most likely alludes to a lottery/decision game rather than to anything
resembling the Mandarin "painting legs on a snake" idiom — this needs native-speaker or corpus
confirmation before it goes into `analysis/`, not a guess).

Third, the interjection and small-talk layer is well represented here precisely because
dictionary glossaries bother to lemmatize words that run prose would just use in passing: *wà!*
"wow!", *yí!* "hello, what's this?", *óh!* "oh, now I understand!", *ou!* "oh! (surprise)," the
phone-greeting pair *wái!* (polite "hello?") vs. plain *wai!* "hey!", and the politeness-register
pair *mh-gòi* / *mh-gòi-saai* / *dò-jeh* (three distinct "thank you" forms, worth noting as a
near-miss for English's single "thanks") were all pulled into the table below alongside a small
cross-alphabet sample of core numerals and basic verbs, for continuity with the rest of the
dataset's coverage.

Two politically dated terms also surface and are worth flagging for `Attested Era`-type
downstream work even though this entry leaves those columns at "—": *Wuih-gwai* "Handover (1997)"
and *Yāt-gwok-léuhng-jai* "'One country, two systems'" — both are glossed matter-of-factly as
ordinary vocabulary items (unit 20), which is itself a small snapshot of how recent/current that
history still was when this course was written.

## Summary of grammar/phonology points covered

The Introduction half of this range lays out the book's descriptive (not prescriptive) methodology
and its comparative stance toward Mandarin. The authors use the adoption of **léih** over the
traditionally-prescribed **néih** ('you') as their flagship example of descriptivism: Cantonese
lacks a strong prescriptive tradition, so "correct" mostly means "sounds native," and constructions
that *do* carry prestige/stigma tend to track Mandarin-origin structures rather than indigenous
Cantonese ones. The book frames the Cantonese-Mandarin relationship as comparable to French-Spanish
or Swedish-German — mutually unintelligible, grammatically similar but not identical — pushing back
on Chao's often-overliteralized claim that Chinese "dialects" share one universal grammar. The
worked example is the **indirect passive**: both languages allow a passive verb to retain its own
object (*"I was stolen [my] car"* rather than *"My car was stolen"*), but Cantonese obligatorily
spells out the agent (**yàhn** 'person' if unknown) where Mandarin can drop it — a subtle but real
syntactic divergence the authors argue gets overlooked because phonology and vocabulary differences
are so much more visually striking between the two languages.

**Written Cantonese and code-switching** get a flagged, linguistically interesting treatment:
standard written Chinese (essentially written Mandarin grammar) is what's taught in schools across
all Cantonese-speaking regions, so "written Cantonese" proper only surfaces in informal genres —
novels, gossip columns, personal messages — and even then is inherently unstable, because many
colloquial Cantonese morphemes (their example: the verb **sèuh** 'slide', as in *sèuh waahttāi*
'slide down a slide') simply have no standard character and no cognate in written Chinese at all.
The authors explicitly flag **English-Cantonese code-switching and code-mixing** as a feature of
written Cantonese in magazines/books aimed at "middle-class yuppies" (citing Bauer 1987 and a
cluster of code-switching studies), and separately note that *spoken* Cantonese-English mixing is
excluded from the book's own example sentences specifically because English loanwords' pronunciation
varies on a continuum from "pure English" to "pure Cantonese," making them unrepresentable in a
single romanization. A second register-contrast example: in formal registers like news broadcasting,
the passive can appear **without** an agent (*Yáuh sei mìhng tāu-douh-jé beih hín fáan loih-deih*,
'Four illegal immigrants were repatriated to the mainland') — which the authors identify as a
Mandarin-syntax intrusion into a formal Cantonese register, not native Cantonese syntax (cf. the
obligatory-agent point above). The book also distinguishes **idioms** (non-compositional, lexically
restricted item combinations, boxed off typographically) from fully **productive** grammatical
constructions using a minimal pair: [verb–**dāk**–particle] for ability/potential applies to any
verb (*máaih dāk dóu* 'can buy', *fan dāk dóu* 'can fall asleep') and is therefore not an idiom,
whereas [verb–**dāk**–**gwo**] is an idiom because its meaning 'safe/reliable to [verb]' isn't
predictable from **gwo**'s literal sense 'over' (*máaih dāk gwo* 'safe to buy', *seun dāk gwo*
'trustworthy'). Also worth flagging: Cantonese **haih** ('yes') answers a negative question by
agreeing with the question's *premise* rather than negating it English-style — *Léih gāmyaht msái
fāan gūng ge mē?* 'Don't you need to go to work today?' is correctly answered *Haih a* ('yes') to
mean 'No, I don't [need to]', the reverse of the English polarity.

The **Phonology** chapter opening establishes: 16 initial consonants contrasted by
**aspiration, not voicing** (unlike English p/b) — a genuinely striking typological point, since
Cantonese b/d/g are voiceless *and* unaspirated, a combination that doesn't exist in English
initials and is why English speakers misparse them as either p/t/k or b/d/g; coarticulated
labiovelar stops **gw/kw**; a sociolinguistically-marked simplification of **gw/kw → g/k before
o/u** among younger/less formal speakers (*gwok* 'country' → *gok*, merging with *gok* 'feel');
affricate **j/ch** with vowel-conditioned **palatalization** before the rounded vowels **yu** and
**eu** (*jyuh* 'live' → [tʃy:], contrasted with *jaahn* 'earn' → [tsa:n]); and **unreleased** final
stops **-p/-t/-k**, with a noted merger of final **-k → -t** among younger speakers specifically
(*baak* 'hundred' pronounced like *baat* 'eight'), paralleling the **-ng → -n** merger. Several
**sound changes in progress** are explicitly flagged as sociolinguistic markers rather than free
variation: the **n → l merger** (*néih* 'you' → *léih*, *nìhn* 'year' → *lìhn*) is corrected as an
"error" by some older teachers but is otherwise pervasive, with the conservative **n**-pronunciation
surviving mainly in highly formal registers and traditional singing — contemporary Canto-pop, by
contrast, is noted as typically using **l**. Separately, **initial ng-dropping** is common among
younger speakers (*ngóh* 'I' → **óh**), to the point that speakers conscious of ng's "correctness"
sometimes **hypercorrect** it onto words that never historically had it (*ōnchyùhn* 'safe' →
*ngōnchyùhn*) — a textbook hypercorrection pattern worth flagging for native-speaker verification.
A further **n/ng-final merger** (*sāan* 'mountain' vs. *sāang* 'alive, produce' both → [sa:n]) is
noted as variable: some speakers merge it only before front consonants, others unconditionally.
On tone: the book adopts a **six-tone** analysis (collapsing the traditionally-distinguished
high-level and high-falling tones, following K-H. Cheung 1986), explicitly because the two tones
are "no longer distinctive for many Hongkong Cantonese speakers" — again flagged as a live
merger-in-progress, with both tones normally realized as high level except on a few sentence-final
particles (**tìm**, **sìn**) and certain contracted numerals (*sà'a-yih* 'thirty-two', from
*seisahp-yih*) that retain a clearly perceptible high falling tone. Finally, this range opens
**§1.4.2 Tone Change** — true tone sandhi — covering the regular shift of a compound's/reduplication's
second syllable to a (mid-)high rising tone: in **AAB-pattern reduplicated adjectives**
(*kàhm-kàhm-chēng* → *kàhm-kám-chēng* 'in a mad rush'), in **-déi-suffixed** reduplication
(*fèih-fèih-déi* → *fèih-féi-déi* 'rather fat/chubby'), in **onomatopoeic reduplication**
(*sàh-sàh-sēng* → *sàh-sá-sēng* 'sloshing sound'), and — notably in the *opposite* direction — in
**tentative/diminutive verb reduplication**, where contraction of the particle **yāt** 'one' causes
the *first* syllable to take the changed tone instead (*si yāt si* → *sí-si* 'have a try').

# Cantonese: A Comprehensive Grammar (Matthews & Yip) — Times of Day, Romanization Appendix, Endnotes, and Glossary of Grammatical Terms

**Source pages (book pagination): 398–417.** This range covers the tail end of Chapter 21
(Numerals and Times), section 21.3 "Times of Day"; the full "Appendix: Romanization Systems";
the complete "Notes" section (endnotes keyed to the Introduction and Chapters 1–21); and roughly
the first two-thirds of the "Glossary of Grammatical Terms" (entries A through V, "Voicing").

**Times of day (21.3).** Cantonese counts hours with the classifier *go*, though colloquial
speech also borrows the classifier for small round objects, *lāp* (as in *yāt lāp yeuhkyún* "a
pill"), to count hours — a classifier doing double mensural/sortal duty. Clock time is built from
*dím (jūng)* "o'clock," with *géi dím* "what time," *bun* "half" for the half hour, and a
five-minute unit called *jih* (referencing the numbered marks on an analogue clock face) counted
from the preceding hour; *jih* is frequently dropped once the numeral context is clear. A
fifteen-minute span is *yāt go gwāt* "a quarter," and *daahp* marks stages within the hour (*daahp
sāam* "quarter past"). One rounding quirk worth flagging: *léuhng dím ngh* "two-hour-five" means
2:25, not 2:05, because the counted unit is five-minute *jih*, not single minutes — a frequent
source of confusion for learners mapping the system onto English "two-oh-five." Midnight has
several expressions of varying formality: *sahpyih dím* "twelve o'clock" is the default colloquial
choice, while *bunyeh/bunyé* "midnight" is a less common literary-flavored alternative, and *lìhng
sìh/sàhn* "zero o'clock/morning" can refer to any time clustered around midnight. Morning/evening
ambiguity is resolved with preposed *jīujóu* "morning" or *yehmáahn* "evening." Time expressions
pattern as other temporal adverbials: they sit between subject and verb, or may be fronted to
sentence-initial topic position.

**Appendix: Romanization Systems.** A straight correspondence table across three systems: Yale
(the system this book uses), IPA (broad transcription), and LSHK — the romanization adopted by the
Linguistic Society of Hong Kong in 1993, essentially the ancestor of modern Jyutping. Notable
correspondences: Yale *j/ch/y* = IPA *ts/tsʰ/j* = LSHK *z/c/j*; Yale vowel *a* splits into *a*
(closed syllable) vs. *aa* (open syllable) in both Yale and LSHK, both realized as long IPA [aː] —
the orthographic split exists purely to disambiguate, since Cantonese genuinely contrasts short
*-a-* from long *-aa-* only before certain finals. The tone table illustrates all six tones on the
syllable *u* and confirms that tone numbering follows the traditional yin/yang convention: 1–3 are
high-register tones, 4–6 low-register, regardless of actual contour shape.

**Endnotes (the "Notes" section).** This section is dense with material the main chapters only
gloss in passing. Phonology notes include an acoustic-experiment finding (Dr. Eric Zee) that young
female speakers are more likely to produce full palatalization of alveolars before *yu* — a
sociolinguistic variation claim worth flagging, though the authors call for more research before
drawing conclusions. Mainland Cantonese varieties are noted to merge final *-k* and *-t* as
unreleased *-t*, paralleling a broader final-consonant-merger pattern across Chinese dialects.
'Correct' (etymologically-motivated, purist-promoted) pronunciations are contrasted with common
variants, e.g. *meihlihk* over *muihlihk* "charm." Onomatopoeic/expressive vocabulary is called out
for tolerating consonant clusters that the regular phonology disallows, including the reduplicative
*blīng-blàhng* "tumbling" and a cluster *klīk-klāk* that the text explicitly traces to retained
English-loanword clusters not normally simplified. Two productive suffixes are flagged as results
of English contact: *-sing* (noun → adjective, as in *gwokjai(-sing) wuihyíh* "international
conference," frequently omissible without change of meaning) and *-fa* (→ verb, "-ify/-ize," as in
*luhk-fa wàahn-gíng* "green the environment"), with English-translation practice cited as the
likely vector. Several colorful idioms turn up as resultative/predicative complements, built on
food and container imagery for "ending up in trouble": *yāt wohk póuh* (literally "one wok
bubble") and *yāt hohk ngáahnleuih* ("one bowl of tears"), alongside *yāt chāan séi* "worked to
death" and *dá-jó yāt chāan* "beaten up badly," where *chāan* (normally the classifier for meals)
is reused as an intensifying "dose" classifier. The sentence-particle system gets substantial
coverage: counting all tonal variants, Yau (1965) identified 206 distinct particle forms, while
Kwok (1984) analyzes the system as roughly thirty base particles combined with three "tonal"
modifications — a high-tone weakener marking hesitancy (*ā/lā/lō*), a low-falling-tone
strengthener marking disapproval or abruptness (*àh/wòh*), and a high-rising tone forming echo
questions. A semantic-extension note worth flagging for slang-synthesis purposes: *ngoi* "love"
is documented as colloquially substitutable for *yiu* "want," a verb whose literal sense has
bled into a weaker desiderative use.

**Glossary of Grammatical Terms.** Mostly English metalinguistic definitions (adjective, aspect,
causative, classifier, etc.), but several entries cite Cantonese examples worth capturing. Most
striking: the glossary explicitly names *gwái* as a Cantonese expletive parallel to English "the
hell," used for emphasis — a rare case of the book itself flagging a taboo/swear item by name. It
also gives a clean, explicit tone-sandhi example: *mùhn* "door" changes to a high-rising *mūn* in
the compound *hauh-mūn* "back door," and separately notes the minimal tonal pair *yāt* "one" vs.
*yaht* "day." The classifier-system discussion (carried over from the Notes section onto these
pages) documents genuine Hong Kong/Malayan Cantonese divergence: Killingley's work on Malayan
Cantonese classifiers shows *jek* applied to fruits such as *pìhnggwó* "apple" where Hong Kong
Cantonese uses *go* — a clear regional classifier-system split rather than free variation.

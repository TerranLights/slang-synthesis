# Teach Yourself Cantonese — End of Unit 3 ("Family and Friends"), Unit 4 ("Eating In and Eating Out"), Unit 5 ("Shops and Markets"), Start of Unit 6 ("Getting Around")

**Source:** *Teach Yourself Cantonese* (image-only PDF, 346 pages total). Standalone beginner-to-intermediate course using Yale-style romanization with tone marks, built around dialogues, vocabulary boxes, grammar points, and "info" sidebars.
**Rendered page range covered:** PDF pages 36-70 (printed page numbers roughly 20-54). No handwritten marginalia appeared anywhere in this range; all content is the book's own printed text.

## Overview

This range closes out Unit 3 (family and friends) with a handful of grammar points and a dialogue about visiting an old friend, then runs through the whole of Unit 4 (eating in and eating out — a dinner-party dialogue and a restaurant-ordering dialogue that turns into a complaint about stale leftovers), the whole of Unit 5 (shops and markets — a clothes-shopping dialogue and a market-haggling dialogue over prawns), and the opening of Unit 6 (getting around — asking directions to the airport, then a dialogue about a trip to Britain), stopping mid-grammar at point 11 ("can, able to"). Each unit follows the same pattern: dialogue with a facing Chinese-character transcript, a boxed vocabulary list (Chinese character, Yale romanization, English gloss), comprehension exercises, then 8-13 numbered grammar points, often closing with a one-paragraph cultural "info" sidebar.

The most useful material for this project is the book's own explicit "Shortcuts" sections (Unit 4 grammar point 12, repeated as "More shortcuts" in Unit 6 grammar point 4) — the author directly names and catalogs colloquial phrase-clipping as a feature of fast spoken Cantonese ("Cantonese is a lively quick-fire language and speakers often find ways of shortening phrases which seem to them to be too tediously long"). Examples given: gàm-yaht jiu-jóu "this morning" clips in two stages to gàm-jiu-jóu and then gàm-jiu; johk-yaht jiu-jóu "yesterday morning" clips the same way to johk-jiu; title+surname forms Wòhng Sìn-sàang/Wòhng Taai-táai clip to Wòhng Sàang/Wòhng Táai; and the full airport name Chek-laahp-gok fèi-gèi-chèuhng reduces to just gèi-chèuhng, while deih-hah-tit-louh "underground railway" reduces to deih-tit (the book notes this clip is so standard it has become the normal MTR name). The book also flags a register-level synonym pair rather than a clip: mh-hóu "don't" vs. máih, "both mean don't but máih is a bit ruder because it is so abrupt sounding" — a clear case of phonological brevity correlating with perceived rudeness.

Other register-relevant findings: a family of colloquial sentence-final particles doing real pragmatic work (bo! "let me remind you", mē? "do you mean to say that...?!" expressing incredulity, ā-ma "you should realize, don't you know", ge-la for strong emphasis, lā for urging agreement, jē/jēk "only, that's all"); a set of spontaneous interjections (wà! "wow!", yí "hello, what's this?", oh "oh, now I understand!", nàh "there, here you are, look"); the familiar-address pattern lóuh + surname (Lóuh Wóng, Lóuh Chán — "Wong/Chan, old chap"), which the grammar explicitly notes requires shifting the surname's tone from its normal low-falling tone to a mid-rising tone, a genuine sociolinguistic/prosodic marker of casual register rather than just a word choice; the register split between mh-gòi (thanks for minor everyday politeness) and dò-jeh (heartfelt thanks, always used for money); and mān, the colloquial unit word for "dollar" (roughly parallel to English "buck"). A vivid colloquial complaint idiom appears in the restaurant dialogue — néih dong ngóh haih laahp-saap-túng àh! "you think I'm a rubbish bin, do you?!" — aimed at a waiter trying to pass off yesterday's leftover fruit salad as fresh. A comparative idiom hóu-chíh...mh-chíh "more like X than like Y" is used hyperbolically by a tourist to complain a sightseeing trip feels "more like fleeing a disaster [jáu-naahn] than having fun."

A short "Pidgin English" cultural sidebar in Unit 5 is worth flagging for the project's contact-linguistics angle: it describes 18th-century Canton pidgin English as a grammar-stripped halfway language using English vocabulary with Cantonese-style grammar (no plurals/tenses, the invented all-purpose classifier "piecee"), and credits it with feeding several fixed expressions into standard English, including "long time no see" (a direct calque of this book's own hóu-noih-móuh-gin, already vocabulary-boxed a few pages earlier in Unit 3), "have a look-see," "chop-chop," and "no can do" (traced to Cantonese mh dāk). The book also marks several everyday English loanwords absorbed wholesale into Cantonese with their own characters: bā-sí "bus," dīk-sí "taxi," ga-fē "coffee," sà-léut "salad." Finally, Unit 4's grammar point 2 names and explains the "lonely verbs" phenomenon — Cantonese verbs like sihk "to eat" and jyú "to cook" that feel incomplete without an object and default to an all-purpose dummy object (faahn "rice") when nothing more specific is meant, giving sihk-faahn as the default translation of plain "to eat."

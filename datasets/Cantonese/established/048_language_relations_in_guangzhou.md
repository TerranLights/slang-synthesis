## Summary of the thesis's argument

Page's thesis is an anthropological fieldwork study (fieldwork conducted late 2009 – early May 2010, "the year of the tiger") centered on Guangzhou, the capital of Guangdong Province, with most of the ethnographic material gathered at a souvenir shop ("Karen's Place") on Shamian Island, a small, formerly British/French-leased enclave near the American consulate. The author deliberately learned Cantonese rather than Mandarin before fieldwork — a methodological choice he argues was decisive, since it exposed him directly to the social reactions that form his data, and which he states explicitly a Mandarin-based study could not have produced. The thesis's central claim, refined across the chapters from a cruder first formulation in the introduction, is that Cantonese and Mandarin in Guangzhou are not simply ranked on a single prestige hierarchy but are organized along **two separate axes that vary in inverse proportion to each other**: an axis of **formality/respectability** (on which Mandarin, the state-mandated national standard, scores high) and an axis of **intimacy/empathy** (on which Cantonese, the local vernacular, scores high). A speaker gains an air of respectability by using Mandarin and a sense of fellow-feeling by using Cantonese, and the two effects trade off against each other rather than stacking on a single ladder. English, where it appears (chiefly on Shamian, because of the American consulate and the tourist trade), introduces a third term that reorganizes the other two: once English is present as the maximally "outsider" code, Mandarin is pulled toward the "insider" pole relative to it, becoming what the author calls a "distant insider" language rather than a purely official one.

The historical/political framing in chapters 2–3 situates this synchronic pattern inside a longer nation-building story. China's writing system was unified more than two thousand years ago (in the Qin dynasty, 221–206 BC) and has functioned ever since as a logographic lingua franca uniting mutually unintelligible spoken varieties — the same character (大, "big") is read *dà* in Mandarin and *daaih* in Cantonese, with identical meaning but no shared sound. A spoken national standard, by contrast, is a twentieth-century invention: the Qing government's failed constructed-language experiment *Lao Guojin* (modeled on the Meiji-era Japanese standardization effort), followed by the promotion of the Beijing dialect as *Póutùngwá* ("common speech," i.e., Mandarin). Following Ping Chen, the author lists nine mutually unintelligible major "dialect" families of Chinese (Mandarin, Jin, Wu, Hui, Xiang, Gan, Kejia/Hakka, Yue, Min), all officially categorized as dialects of one language purely as a matter of nationalist ideology — an application of Benedict Anderson's "imagined communities" argument, in which a single named standard language is one of the chief symbolic props by which a modern nation-state naturalizes itself and its rulers. Cantonese (Yue family) is therefore simultaneously a "real," mutually-unintelligible-with-Mandarin language by any linguistic test, and an officially demoted "dialect" by political fiat — and the author argues this demotion is precisely what gives Cantonese its charged, situationally marked status in Guangzhou, in contrast to Hong Kong and Macao, where Special Administrative Region status exempts officials from the Mandarin-primacy mandate and lets Cantonese range across the full formal/informal spectrum. In Guangzhou proper, only Cantonese's informal register survives in daily use (even a basic polite phrase like *chíng maahn*, "may I ask," reads as stilted/comical), which the author treats as a major source of the exaggerated privacy/intimacy loading Cantonese carries there relative to Hong Kong.

Chapter 4 develops the thesis's core theoretical apparatus, borrowed from Carol Myers-Scotton's **Markedness Model**: any given language choice in an interaction is either "unmarked" (expected, provoking no particular reaction) or "marked" (surprising, noticeable, and capable of provoking a disproportionately strong positive or negative response). The author maps out a "Situational Distribution of Languages in Guangzhou" in which Mandarin is unmarked in large restaurants, department stores, and with uniformed officials, while Cantonese is unmarked in small corner shops, with hawkers, with acquaintances, and specifically during *yám chàh* ("drinking tea," i.e., the yum cha social practice) — with an explicit, if cautiously qualified, gender correlation in which women spend proportionally more time in Cantonese-coded situations than men. A key, almost counter-intuitive finding in this chapter is the near-total **absence of code-switching**: despite Guangzhou being a functionally bilingual community, the author never once observed a speaker switch from Cantonese to Mandarin or vice versa mid-exchange — conversations are conducted entirely in one code or the other, and borrowing is similarly rare (the one real exception being the sinicized English loan *báai-baai*, "bye-bye," which has largely displaced the native Cantonese *joigin* among younger speakers). Citing Coulmas's claim that "composite" languages like English and Japanese show heavy code-switching/borrowing while a more homogeneous language like Chinese does not, the author suggests Guangzhou's strict code-separation (rather than code-mixing) is itself the socially meaningful pattern, and one that — he notes in passing — inverts outside Guangzhou, where young people elsewhere in China sometimes insert Cantonese phrases into Mandarin speech as a marker of Hong-Kong-associated cool/modernity, exactly the opposite valence Cantonese carries inside Guangzhou itself.

Chapters 5–6 shift to the ethnographic core: the shop Karen's Place and its staff (Deng the aggressive-salesman manager; his wife Karen; the quieter Lee; two part-timers), and the author's use of Sahlins's three levels of reciprocity (generalized/balanced/negative, from *Stone Age Economics*) to analyze how customers were treated. English speakers (overwhelmingly foreign tourists) received the most aggressive, least trusting sales treatment and the heaviest surveillance while browsing — the negative-reciprocity pole. Mandarin-speaking Chinese strangers received a moderate, "balanced" treatment: softer sales pressure, somewhat less surveillance, prompts toward cheaper goods. Cantonese speakers — especially once recognized as locals — were waved through with no sales pressure at all, invited to sit and drink tea, and (crucially) given substantial price discounts unavailable to the other two groups, illustrating what the author treats as a direct, observable mapping of **linguistic code onto economic/reciprocal treatment**. The chapter's "Zhou the Friendly Soldier" case study — a uniformed PLA soldier who deliberately tried to learn Cantonese phrases specifically because, in his own words, speaking it makes Guangzhou people treat you better — is presented as an instructive exception that actually proves the rule: despite repeat patronage and language effort, Zhou's uniform kept him anchored in the "official" sphere, showing that the insider/outsider sorting is not purely about language performance but about the categorical frame (official vs. private) a person is read into.

Chapter 7 turns to **guanxi**, the Chinese system of dyadic, morally binding reciprocal-obligation networks, which the author treats as an emic concept best left untranslated rather than reduced to "relationships" or "particularist ties." Guanxi is argued to harden what would otherwise be a continuum of social closeness into something closer to a binary: there is effectively no stable "casual acquaintance" zone in Guangzhou the way there might be in a Western society — a dyad either has guanxi, with attendant obligations of gift exchange and favor-granting, or it has none, with correspondingly minimal mutual-courtesy expectations (the author notes, as evidence, that mundane Western politeness norms toward strangers, like holding a door, were conspicuously absent). This chapter also introduces the two-part Chinese concept of "face" via Hu Hsien Chin: **lien** (moral face/trustworthiness, whose loss is described as feared more than physical danger) versus **mien** (prestige/achievement face, which — unlike lien — can be borrowed or lent). The chapter's two field anecdotes (the "yellow paper" household-registration favor obtained from a sympathetic Cantonese-speaking police clerk, and the driver/awning dispute resolved by crowd-mobilized public shaming) are both used to show guanxi and face operating as real enforcement mechanisms with no formal legal backing. Chapter 8 continues with the author's own visa/registration ordeal at the Entry/Exit Administration Bureau, contrasting a frustrated English-speaking applicant who got nothing but a scripted bureaucratic non-answer with the author's own experience of a female clerk visibly warming and volunteering real information the moment he greeted her in Cantonese — which the author explains via Festinger's **cognitive dissonance**: being addressed in the "private-sphere" language by someone who is, in that moment, officially performing the "official sphere" role creates an incongruity the addressee resolves either by rejecting the marked language use outright or by informally re-categorizing the speaker as an insider (likened to Edmund Leach's "rabbit in the infield" — an ambiguous boundary-crossing figure that provokes an emotionally loaded response precisely because it is neither cleanly inside nor outside).

Chapter 9 explores a gender asymmetry the author found but could not get informants to acknowledge explicitly: women in his sample both used Cantonese more and were more receptive to marked/unexpected Cantonese use from outsiders (including, notably, the two female officials discussed above) than men were. The author offers two non-exclusive explanations: a situational one (women spend proportionally more time in domestic/private-sphere contexts where Cantonese is already unmarked) and a more symbolic one tied to his "movement towards modernity / movement towards traditionalism" dichotomy — a self-consciously simplified polarity he uses throughout the thesis to describe two competing orientations visible across many unrelated domains (openness to foreigners, ease understanding non-native Chinese, Gregorian vs. lunisolar birthday celebration, attitudes to premarital sexuality), with women disproportionately positioned on the "modernity" side, partly because, as a historically marginalized group, they had comparatively more to gain from social change. Chapter 10 closes the analytic arc by arguing that the categorization-by-language mechanism operates largely below conscious awareness: informants uniformly denied, when asked directly, that they treated Cantonese- and Mandarin-speaking strangers differently, even though the author's own observations (and the fact that the high-pressure sales approach was demonstrably unprofitable in all but one observed case) suggest the differential treatment was not a consciously maximizing strategy. He frames this via Bourdieu's habitus and Bateson's primary/secondary mental process distinction: if the categorization happens automatically, prior to conscious "filtering," it behaves less like a strategy and more like a quasi-involuntary disposition — which, he argues, is exactly why the effect was so strong and so hard for his informants to introspect on or explain beyond "of course, that's just how it is."

**For this project's register/code-mixing interests specifically:** this thesis is a rare, closely observed account of a living official/intimate code-domain split that does *not* rely on code-switching or code-mixing as its mechanism — Guangzhou Cantonese/Mandarin bilinguals keep the two codes strictly separated by situation rather than blending them, which is the opposite pattern from the Cantonese-English mixed code documented for Hong Kong in this dataset's file 049 (Simpson). The two theses together sketch a useful contrast for later grammar-logic/register work: Hong Kong's SAR status decoupled Cantonese from the official/intimate binary (letting it occupy formal domains too, hence code-*mixing* becomes the live boundary-blurring phenomenon there), while in mainland Guangzhou the binary stays sharp and the marked/unmarked **switch-of-whole-code** (not word-level mixing) is the socially loaded move. The *báai-baai*/*joigin* displacement is a concrete, small-scale instance of generational lexical borrowing under English contact pressure, consistent with this project's interest in tracking live sound/lexical change. The *lien*/*mien* face distinction and the guanxi insider/outsider binary are not slang items per se, but are exactly the kind of social-pragmatic infrastructure that should inform how any synthesized Cantonese slang register-codes politeness, address terms, and in-group markers going forward.

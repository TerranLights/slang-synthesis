## Summary

This range covers Cantonese's aspect-marker system and the start of its verbal-particle system,
both central to how the language encodes time, completion, and manner of action without tense
inflection. Section 11.2 works through six bound aspect suffixes — progressive *gán*, continuous
*jyuh*, perfective *jó*, experiential *gwo*, delimitative *háh*, and habitual *hōi* (plus the
stative verb *gwaan*, which does similar habitual work) — establishing that Cantonese aspect
marking is largely optional and governed by discourse/pragmatic preference rather than strict
grammatical requirement. A recurring theme is that near-synonymous markers encode subtly different
presuppositions: *jó* vs. *gwo* both translate to an English perfect/past but contrast on whether
a result still holds (door closed-and-still-shut vs. closed-once-but-now-open-again); *gán* marks
dynamic ongoing activity while *jyuh* marks a static continuous state. The chapter also documents
how aspect markers interact with negation — *jó* cannot appear in negative sentences at all; the
negative existential *móuh* takes over that job, and the verbal particle *dou* substitutes for *jó*
when the sentence specifically denies a successfully achieved outcome. Compound (multisyllable)
verbs split into three distinct separability classes for aspect-marker placement (freely
separable, obligatorily fused, and optional), which the authors treat as a real syntactic
distinction rather than free variation.

Section 11.3 pivots to verbal particles, a formally distinct category from aspect markers:
particles can be pulled away from the verb by the potential-construction markers *dāk*/*m̀h*, while
aspect markers cannot, and particles (unlike aspect suffixes) can stack with aspect marking on the
same verb. The chapter inventories four particle subtypes — directional, resultative, quantifying,
and adversative/habitual — and the unit range here fully covers the first two. Directional
particles (*chēut*, *dāi*, *fāan*, *gwo*, *héi*, *hōi*, *lohk*, *màaih*, *séuhng*, *yahp*) are shown
to carry extensive figurative extensions well beyond literal spatial direction — *fāan* 'back' used
reflexively to mean 'acquire for oneself' (*máaih fāan* 'buy one for myself'), *héi* 'up' extending
to the abstract senses of respect (*tái héi* 'look up to') and affordability (*dāk héi* 'can afford
to'). Resultative particles (*báau*, *cho*, *dihm*, *dihng*, *dóu*, *dou*, *gihk*, *hóu*, *jihng*,
*laahn*, and — named but not yet exemplified in this range — *mìhng*, *sèhng*, *séi*, *wàhn*,
*waaih*, *yùhn*) attach to transitive verbs to encode an effect on the object; some, like *hóu*
'completion,' are pure bound particles with no independent existence as a verb.

Linguistically notable items flagged for attention: (1) the source text explicitly labels
*jouh-dāi* ('do someone in, knock someone down') as "colloquial slang" in its own prose — one of
the rare places in this reference grammar where the authors apply that label themselves rather
than it being inferred; (2) a clear tone-sandhi/contraction case where the reduplicated
delimitative construction *siu-yāt-siu* ('have a smile') regularly contracts to *síu-siu* with a
tone change, explicitly cross-referenced to the book's phonology section (2.2); (3) a
contact-linguistics observation that the progressive alternative *háidouh* ('be here/there' used
preverbally) is likely calqued on Mandarin *zài*, and that *ngoi-séuhng* ('fall in love') and
*yím-séuhng* ('get infected') are direct Mandarin loan-compounds (the latter drawn from a news
item about AIDS infection rates) using the particle *séuhng* in a borrowed, non-native pattern;
(4) the source consistently register-tags its own example sentences — "(conv.)", "(TV ad.)",
"(news)", "(film)" — which is a useful built-in register signal that several rows below draw on
directly for Usage Tier assignment.

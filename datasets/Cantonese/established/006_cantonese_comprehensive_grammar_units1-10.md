## Overview

This range is almost entirely front matter. The dust jacket (unit 1) and title page (unit 2) identify the book as a reference grammar of Hong Kong Cantonese written by Stephen Matthews (University of Hong Kong) and Virginia Yip (Chinese University of Hong Kong), aimed at learners, professionals, linguists, and teachers alike, with emphasis on authentic current usage drawn from films, advertising, and real conversation rather than invented textbook sentences. The copyright page (unit 3) carries a handwritten library accession mark ("B2-6310", a Humboldt-Universität zu Berlin library stamp, and "71-[illegible]24:95") that is incidental cataloguing marginalia, not source content, and has been excluded from extraction.

Units 3 through 7 reproduce the full table of contents (21 chapters plus appendix, notes, glossary, references, and index) and the list of tables. This gives a useful structural map of the whole grammar for future extraction waves: Introduction; Ch.1 Phonology; Ch.2 Word structure (morphology/word formation); Ch.3 Syntactic categories; Ch.4 Sentence structure; Ch.5 Pronouns; Ch.6 The noun phrase; Ch.7 Prepositions and location; Ch.8 The verb phrase; Ch.9 Adjectival constructions; Ch.10 Adverbial constructions; Ch.11 Aspect and verbal particles; Ch.12 Modality; Ch.13 Negation; Ch.14 Quantification; Ch.15 Existential sentences; Ch.16 Coordination and subordinate clauses; Ch.17 Questions; Ch.18 Sentence particles and interjections; Ch.19 Imperative sentences; Ch.20 Cantonese speech conventions (politeness, terms of address); Ch.21 Numerals and times; followed by an appendix on romanization systems, notes, a glossary of grammatical terms, references, and an index.

Unit 8 is the acknowledgements page — purely institutional/personal thanks (Hong Kong Research Grants Council funding, named colleagues and informants), with no linguistic content. Unit 9 gives the list of abbreviations used for interlinear glossing throughout the book (ADJ, ADV, CL, CONT, DEG, DEL, EXP, HAB, LP, NEG, PFV, PL, POSS, PROG, PRT, V-PRT) — these are the authors' own metalinguistic shorthand, not Cantonese words, so they are not entered as vocabulary rows below, but are worth noting since later extraction waves covering example sentences elsewhere in the book will rely on this same gloss key. Unit 9 also opens the Introduction proper, which argues that no substantial English-language reference grammar of Cantonese existed before this one — Chao Yuen-Ren's 1947 *Cantonese Primer* gives grammar only brief treatment, Samuel Cheung Hung-Nin's 1972 grammar was never translated from Chinese, and course materials like Huang and Kok's *Speak Cantonese* treat grammar unsystematically. The authors note Cantonese is reputed to be hard for second-language learners because of its tone and consonant inventory and unfamiliar word order, even though it lacks case marking or verb conjugation. They adopt a descriptive stance that treats the spoken language as primary and the written form as derivative, and they commit to the Yale romanization system as their transcription convention throughout the book.

Unit 10 (printed pages 2-3) continues the Introduction into two content-bearing sections. "The Language and Its Speakers" situates Cantonese as the most widely spoken Chinese variety after Mandarin, belonging to the Yue dialect group, spoken natively across Guangdong and Guangxi provinces and in Hong Kong and Macau, and carried overseas by emigration to Singapore, Malaysia, North America, and Australia. It notes Cantonese's rising prestige within China itself — described with the idiom "heading north" — driven by Guangdong's economic growth and by the reach of Hong Kong popular culture ("Canto-pop," film, television). The book's own descriptive scope is explicitly Hong Kong Cantonese rather than the traditionally cited Guangzhou standard. The section also surveys a small literary tradition: nineteenth-century love songs (translated by Morris, 1992), transcribed children's songs, and a newer wave of semi-colloquial popular fiction — the authors cite a novel series titled (in translation) "Diary of a Yuppie," written to represent spoken Cantonese closely, including code-mixed English letters standing in for Cantonese morphemes (the authors' example: the letter "D" used in print for the comparative marker *dī*, as in *pèhng dī* "cheaper"). The second section, "Approach: Description and Explanation," opens by stating the grammar aims for comprehensive, current-usage coverage and will omit forms attested in earlier scholarship (e.g., Chao's high-level/high-falling tone distinction and an associated tone-sandhi rule) that the authors judge non-productive in present-day Hong Kong speech.

## Prose summary

Unit 14's revision block closes with two read-aloud passages meant purely for fluency practice, but
the second is a deliberate pun built on gambling slang. In "Hòh Sìn-sàang Máaih-máh," a wife gives
her naive husband bad betting advice by exploiting the fact that **máaih-máh** ("to buy a horse")
is a homophone of **dóu-máh** ("to bet on/gamble at the horses") — since nobody poor would ever
literally buy a horse, "wanting to máaih-máh" is simply understood as "wanting to go gambling." The
follow-up Exercise 8 anecdote runs the same trick with dog racing, turning on "sahp chèuhng" (ten
lengths/odds) vs. "gáu chèuhng" (dog track), which the author flags as needing "a hyphen" to parse
— a nice compact example of slang-adjacent wordplay riding on a near-homophone collision between a
neutral verb phrase and a taboo/restricted gambling register.

Unit 15 (Travelling) is mostly clean core travel/hotel vocabulary, but it surfaces a colloquial/
formal doublet worth flagging: **fēi** (a ticket/fare, probably a sound-borrowing from English
"fare") is the word Cantonese speakers actually use, while **piu** is the formal equivalent reserved
for more careful speech. The unit's culture box on bargaining register is also notable: calling a
good deal **hóu pèhng** ("very cheap") can sound insulting toward a host who paid for the meal,
implying they should have spent more, whereas **hóu dái** ("a good bargain/good value") is safe
praise — and it is considered good manners for the host to self-deprecatingly call their own
effort **hóu pèhng jē**, belittling their own spending. Grammar note 10 documents a clean case of
intensifier devaluation: the verb ending **-saai** ("completely") has attached itself to **mh-gòi**
("thank you") to form **mh-gòi-saai**, which the book says has itself been "devalued" to the point
that people say it reflexively rather than to signal unusual gratitude — directly parallel to English
speakers defaulting to "thank you very much" over bare "thanks." **Dò-jeh-saai** works the same way.
Also worth noting: **mouh mahn-tàih** ("no problem") is called out explicitly as echoing the English
calque almost word for word, and **sáu-tàih** ("portable") has narrowed in practice to mean "mobile
phone" (sáu-tàih dihn-wá) even though it can still modify other portable objects.

Unit 16 (Driving) contributes a cluster of everyday idiom/exclamation vocabulary from its bicycle-
accident dialogue: **héi-yáuh-chí-léih** ("that's ridiculous, how could that be?"), **chì-sin**
("crazy, mixed up, off the rails" — used to accuse someone of reckless/unhinged behavior), and
**chìn-kèih** as a negative-command intensifier ("whatever you do, don't ever..."). Its grammar notes
also cover the loosely-applied prefix **dihn-** ("electric"), stretched to label motorbikes as
**dihn-dāan-chē** ("electric bikes"), and a tidy distinction between **waaih-jó** (gone bad/broken
down — for food, machinery) vs. **laahn-jó** (physically smashed/damaged). A culture box on **siu**
("to smile/laugh") observes that Chinese narrative prose uses *siu* where English prose would use
"said," "exclaimed," or "sighed" — even in tragic or shocking moments — and suggests this mismatch
is part of why Westerners read Chinese faces as "inscrutable."

Unit 17 (Uniformed Services) opens with an immigration-photo dialogue built on a taboo-adjacent
misunderstanding joke (an official trying to explain a "no uniforms in ID photos" rule is heard as
telling the applicant to pose nude), then its second dialogue — plainclothes police confronting a
group of youths — carries this excerpt's flagship slang finding. The book includes an explicit,
self-labeled **"Secret society slang"** culture box: it states outright that Hong Kong gangster
films/TV are "one of the biggest influences on contemporary Cantonese," that this slang "passes
rapidly into ordinary people's speech" and "equally quickly is discarded again," and that it has
deliberately included only two terms "which seem likely to stay around." Those two terms are
**sáu-jūk** ("brothers," glossed in-text as secret-society slang for fellow gang members/associates)
and **séi-jái** ("deadbeats, bastards, rats," glossed in-text as "strong abuse") — both spoken by the
youths at the arresting officers in the dialogue, giving a primary-source-labeled taboo/slang pair
with clear register and source framing already supplied by the book itself. The same stretch of
grammar notes documents **faai-dī** ("quicker, faster") undergoing the same devaluation pattern as
-saai above: the book states it "has become the most common way of saying get a move on!, hurry up!"
— a comparative adjective worn down into a bare imperative, used reflexively by "harassed mothers."
Also of interest: the verb-ending pair **-màaih** (movement/location close up to something) and
**-hòi** (movement/location away) extend into flirtatious and annoyed pragmatic shorthand —
**chóh-màaih-dī** ("sit a bit closer," i.e., "cuddle up to me!") vs. **chóh-hòi-dī** ("sit further
away," i.e., "stop crowding me!") — and the gender-marking prefixes **néuih-**/**náahm-** (female/
male) are shown to be optional and non-default: plain nouns like **yàhn** ("person") are described
as "completely non-committal" about gender, with the prefixes only added when a contrast is being
made.

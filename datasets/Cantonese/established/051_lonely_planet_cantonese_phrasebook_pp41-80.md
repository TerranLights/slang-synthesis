# Cantonese — Established Vocabulary: Lonely Planet Cantonese Phrasebook (pp. 78-157, PDF pages 41-80)

**Source:** *Lonely Planet Cantonese Phrasebook*, image-only PDF (160 pages total), no embedded text
layer. This file covers PDF pages 41-80, which correspond to the book's own printed page numbers
78-157 (each PDF page in this scan is a double-page spread, so the printed-page count runs roughly
2x the PDF-page count). Rendered at 150 dpi and read via vision; no handwritten marginalia was found
anywhere in this range.

**Sections covered in this range:** tail end of Getting Around (Boat/Other Transport, pp. 78-79) →
Accommodation (Finding Accommodation, Booking Ahead, At the Hotel, Requirements, Negotiation, Service,
Laundry, Complaints, Checking Out, Useful Words, pp. 79-90) → Around Town (Street Life, At the Bank,
At the Post Office, Telecommunications, Sightseeing, Guided Tours, Entertainment & Nightlife, Signs,
pp. 91-106) → Interests (Common Interests, Mahjong, Geomancy/Fung-Shui, Musical Instruments, Cantonese
Opera, Politics, pp. 107-114) → Shopping (Looking For, Making a Purchase, Bargaining & Prices,
Souvenirs, Electrical Appliances, Music, Renting Videos, Photography, Computers, Duty Free Shop,
Smoking, Footwear, Clothing, Materials, Jewellery, Colours, Stationery & Publications, Toiletries, For
the Baby, Sizes & Comparisons, pp. 115-140) → Food (intro, Vegetarian & Special Meals, Fast Food,
Eating Out, Eateries, Chinese Regional Cuisines, At the Restaurant, Staples, Meals/Breakfast/Lunch/
Yum Cha/Dinner dish lists, Soups, pp. 141-157).

**Did I find a dedicated slang/colloquialism section?** No. Unlike the Japanese Rough Guide
phrasebook's dedicated informal-language sidebar, this range of the Lonely Planet Cantonese
phrasebook has no standalone slang/colloquialism chapter or callout box. What it does have, scattered
through otherwise standard vocabulary lists, is a small number of individual entries explicitly
tagged **"(Col)"** (colloquial) by the book itself, plus a handful of culturally distinctive
folk/informal terms called out in the surrounding prose (not formally tagged, but flagged as informal
alternates to the standard term). These are captured in the table below. The bulk of this range —
hotel booking phrases, bank/post-office/telecom vocabulary, sightseeing phrases, shopping/clothing/
jewellery/stationery/toiletries lists, and most of the Food chapter's dish names — is standard
register, non-colloquial travel vocabulary and was not transcribed wholesale (it overlaps heavily
with the kind of generic phrasebook content already covered by multiple other sources in this
project's Cantonese corpus, e.g. the Colloquial Cantonese and Teach Yourself Cantonese extractions).

**Notable finds kept for the table below:**
- Two explicit **"(Col)"**-tagged entries: a colloquial term for a mahjong player, and a colloquial
  name for Cantonese opera.
- 大哥大 (`dāai gòh dāai`), listed as an alternate for "mobile phone" — the well-known Hong Kong
  colloquial/slang term (literally "big brother big") for early brick-style mobile phones, closely
  associated with 1980s-90s Hong Kong gangster-film culture.
- 風水佬 (`fùng súi ló`), glossed directly in the prose as "the fung-shui guy" — an informal alternate
  to the formal term 風水先生 (geomancer master).
- 麻雀學校 (`ma jēuk hok hāu`), a prose-noted humorous/euphemistic nickname ("Mahjong School") for some
  Hong Kong gambling dens.
- 油炸鬼 (`yau jā gwái`), a folk alternate name (literally "fried ghost/devil") for the standard fried
  dough stick 油條 (`yau tíu`), listed side-by-side with the standard term on p.148.
- 乞兒雞 (`hàt yì gài`), a colloquial dish name ("beggar's chicken") explained in the source's own
  prose as an alternate/folk name for a clay-baked stuffed-chicken dish, p.155.

## Overview

The range opens mid-chapter on reflexive pronouns. *Jihgéi* ("self") is Cantonese's single invariant reflexive, covering all persons and numbers, and the book draws two functional lines through it. As a true reflexive it is **subject-oriented** — it picks a subject (not an object) as antecedent, even across clause boundaries (**long-distance reflexives**), as shown by a minimal pair where *jihgéi* inside an embedded clause can corefer with either the embedded or the matrix subject but never with an object noun phrase that sits between them. Its second function is emphatic, reinforcing a pronoun or noun phrase (*pronoun + jihgéi*, e.g. *ngóh jihgéi* "I myself"); the book proves this is a genuinely distinct construction by showing that *pronoun + jihgéi* does **not** share the subject-orientation constraint — in a three-argument sentence it can corefer with a subject, an indirect object, or (via *kéuih jihgéi*) effectively any plausible antecedent, where bare *jihgéi* in the same slot is restricted to the two clausal subjects. A boxed idiom panel gives set expressions built on the frame *jihgéi – [verb] – jihgéi* (e.g. "don't cheat yourself," "only you know your own business"). Section 5.3 notes Cantonese has no dedicated reciprocal pronoun; the Mandarin-shared adverbs *béichí* and *wuhséung* ("mutually") cover formal/written "each other," while colloquial speech instead doubles the clause with subject and object swapped (*ngóh béi-mín kéuih, kéuih béi-mín ngóh* "he and I give each other face" → "we respect each other").

Chapter 6 then opens the noun phrase proper: fixed order demonstrative–numeral–classifier–adjective–(*ge*)–noun, with the linking particle *ge* bridging modifiers and the head noun (and sometimes appearing pleonastically after demonstrative+classifier). 6.1 covers definiteness: Cantonese has no articles, *yāt* ("one") can function like an indefinite article but is optional, and — strikingly — **a bare classifier+noun in subject or fronted-object position is read as definite**, while the same phrase after the verb is ambiguously definite or indefinite; this is the classifier system doing double duty as the article system. 6.1.1–6.1.2 cover the demonstratives *nī* "this" / *gó* "that" (noting the well-attested n→l sound change giving *lī*, plus variant forms *léi*, *yī*), their obligatory pairing with a classifier, special demonstrative-flavored time/manner words (*gām*, *gám*), and their very productive use in apposition after proper nouns, place names, verb-object compounds, and whole clauses to mean roughly "that [X] of yours/in question." A striking generalization: certain semantic classes — abstract nouns like *màhnjyú* "democracy," *pìhng ōn* "peace," *gihnhōng* "health," and time nouns like *yaht* "day" — categorically reject sortal classifiers and take only the generic classifier *júng* or none at all, paralleling how English count/mass distinctions block "a peace" or "two healths."

6.2 is the chapter's center of gravity: the classifier (measure-word) system. The authors split classifiers into **measure/mensural** classifiers (denoting quantities — collective, container, and generic types) and **sortal/type** classifiers (denoting intrinsic shape/kind, assigned to a noun much as gender is assigned in European languages, with 60+ in the inventory and considerable idiosyncrasy requiring dictionary lookup per noun). 6.2.1 establishes the syntax: classifiers are obligatory after numerals/quantifiers and after the demonstratives *nī*/*gó* and after *bīn* "which," optional with vague quantifiers like *dō* "many" for abstract nouns, and absent for uncountable substances and abstract/time nouns. Classifiers can themselves stand in for an elided head noun (functioning like English "one"), and — in a flagged idiomatic construction — the adjectives *daaih* "big" and *sai* "small" can be used **predicatively** with a bare classifier standing in for the noun (*nī gāan fóng hóu daaih gāan*, "this room is a big one"); a striking sub-case is that *sai lāp* and *daaih jek* become fixed idioms for describing a **person's** physical size/build, repurposing the "small round object" classifier *lāp* and the animal classifier *jek* onto human bodies — an unusual, register-marked extension worth flagging for slang-synthesis purposes, since it shows classifiers themselves being drafted into a productive descriptive/slangy function well outside their literal domain.

6.2.2 (measure classifiers) catalogs collective classifiers (*bāan* "group/gang," formal *kwàhn*), a long list of container classifiers (*būi* cup, *dihp* dish, *doih* bag, *gwun* can, *hahp/háp* box, *jā* jar, *jēun* bottle, *làahm*/*lùhng* basket/cage, *pùhn* basin, *túng* bucket, *wún* bowl — any noun denoting a container can convert into a classifier this way, including body parts used as collective/container classifiers), and a cluster of vivid body-part-based idioms built on the "container full of X" template: *sèhng mihn fūi* "a faceful of dust," *yāt tàuh yīn* "a head full of smoke" (= in a state of turmoil), *yāt ngaak hohn* "a forehead full of sweat," *yāt sān jáu-meih* "reeking of wine all over," *yāt sān ngáih* "a body full of ants" (= beset by trouble), and *yāt wohk póuh* "a wok full of bubbles" (= a mess of trouble) — a productive idiom family and a strong candidate source domain for synthesized slang metaphor. One especially notable find: the container classifier *pùhn* ("basin"), in the phrase *yāt pùhn séui* ("a basin of water"), is glossed by the book both literally **and** as colloquial **slang for "ten thousand dollars"** — this is an explicit, book-attested slang item and is flagged accordingly in the table below. The generic classifiers *júng*/*leuih*/*yeuhng*/*fún*/*tíng* sit between measure and sortal function (*tíng* is noted as carrying potential negative connotation); *jek*, beyond its core animal/round-object/one-of-a-pair sortal senses, also serves colloquially as a generic "type/model" classifier where the head noun is dropped entirely, illustrated with a vivid reduplicated descriptive compound, *gōu-gōu-daaih-daih, sī-sī-màhn-màhn* ("tall-tall-big-big, refined-refined-gentle-gentle" = "the tall, gentlemanly type"), flagged as a colloquial reduplication pattern. 6.2.3 (sortal classifiers, opening only) catalogs the neutral/default classifier *go* (people, abstract non-physical nouns like "wish," "opportunity," "decision") against nouns that instead take *gihn* "piece" (abstract *and* concrete: "matter," "legal case," most clothing) or special classifiers for physically continuous/elongated referents (*tìuh* for "life/destiny," underlying the idiom *léih tìuh mehng jānhaih fú* "your life is really bitter"), plus shape-based sortal classifiers: *faai* (vertical flat surfaces: steak, bread slice, window pane — but oranges idiosyncratically take *káaih* instead), *buhng* (walls — a homophonous but distinct *buhng* appears in the idiom "a strong pungent smell"), *jēung* (horizontal flat objects: tables, paper, tickets), *fūk* (rectangular: paintings, photos, cloth), *gauh* (lumps: stones, erasers), and *jī* (cylindrical: pens, flower stems, flutes, wine bottles). The classifier *joh* (large items) is introduced only by its label at the very end of the range and is left for the next unit.

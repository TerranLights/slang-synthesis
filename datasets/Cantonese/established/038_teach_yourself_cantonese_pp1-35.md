## Overview

This is a standalone self-teaching course (companion to a recording, marked throughout with a play-button icon) by Hugh Baker (SOAS, University of London) and Ho Pui-Kei, a long-time Hong Kong Cantonese teacher. The front matter is unusually rich for this project's purposes: the Introduction states outright that Cantonese "invents, evolves and discards slang at a frenetic rate" and that Cantonese speakers "pun all the time and often with great ingenuity," explicitly citing the dense homophone inventory of the language (the example given: **daai** means both "to bring" and "to wear") as the structural reason punning is so productive. The authors also flag that the book deliberately supplies Chinese characters alongside the romanization because ordinary Cantonese speakers, unlike the learner, generally cannot read the romanized transcription — a reminder that romanization (here the Yale system, chosen for this edition) is a classroom tool only, not something that exists in the wild.

The Pronunciation Guide closes with a "Signs of change" section describing four sound changes in progress among (the authors estimate) a plurality or majority of speakers: merger of initial **n-** into **l-** (néih → léih), merger of initial **gw-** into plain **g-** (Jùng-gwok → Jùng-gok), loss of the initial **ng-** sound altogether (ngóh → óh), and leveling of the two high-pitch tones into one. This is directly useful sociolinguistic-variation material: it documents live, in-progress phonological reduction at the level of the whole speech community rather than at the level of an individual slang item, and it's explicitly framed by the authors as something the learner should expect to pick up from native speakers regardless of what the textbook prescribes.

Units 1-2 (and the first page of Unit 3) are the opening "getting acquainted" material: greetings, surnames-before-titles address forms, the adjective-doubles-as-verb pattern (hóu "good" / "to be good"), the all-purpose negator m̀h, the A-not-A question pattern with the particle a, the possessive particle ge (optionally dropped with close two-syllable kinship/relationship terms like taai-táai and pàhng-yáuh), the classifier system (demonstratives and numerals cannot combine directly with a noun without a classifier in between, and the classifier alone can stand in for a dropped noun once context is clear), and the irregular classifier-triggered substitution of léuhng for yih "two." Grammar point 11 in Unit 1 is worth flagging as register-relevant: the authors describe Cantonese's preference for ending sentences with one of a family of meaning-light sentence-final particles (ma?, nē?, a?, àh?, lak/la) as the normal spoken pattern, calling this a "comfortable noise" convention rather than optional decoration — this is the kind of discourse-particle-dense colloquial texture that distinguishes everyday spoken Cantonese from citation-form sentences. Grammar point 6 in Unit 1 ("Follow-up questions") also singles out the particle nē as a genuine shortcut device: it lets a speaker ask the same question about a new topic ("And how's Miss Wong?") without restating the question in full, a clipping-adjacent economy mechanism worth comparing against the explicit phrase-clipping ("Shortcuts") content the catalog flagged in Unit 4.

A genuinely slang-adjacent cultural item surfaces in Unit 2's "The magic of numbers" sidebar: Cantonese numerological preference/avoidance is explained as driven entirely by sound-alike (homophone) association rather than any inherent property of the numbers themselves. **baat** "eight" is lucky because it echoes **faat** "get rich"; **sei** "four" is unlucky because it echoes **séi** "to die"; the combination **ngh baat** ("five-eight") is considered unlucky because it echoes **m̀h faat** ("not get rich"), while **yih baat** ("two-eight") is lucky because it echoes **yih faat** ("easy to get rich"). The authors cite two real-world consequences: a UK house sale where a Chinese buyer insisted on paying a larger, "luckier-sounding" sum than the asking price, and Hong Kong government auctions of "lucky" car registration plates (8888 reportedly fetching an enormous price). This is a clean, well-attested example of phonetic taboo-avoidance/luck-marking operating purely on homophony, structurally identical in mechanism to the pun-driven slang productivity the Introduction describes, and worth cross-referencing against any taboo-number-avoidance findings already logged for other languages (e.g. Japanese 4/9 avoidance) in future comparative analysis work.

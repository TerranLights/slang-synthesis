# Cantonese: A Comprehensive Grammar (Matthews & Yip) — Chapter 19 end (Imperative Sentences, §19.1–19.5) and Chapter 20 (Cantonese Speech Conventions: Politeness and Terms of Address, §20.1–20.3.4, through "Children and Grandchildren" intro)

**Source pages:** pp. 360–379 (djvu units 191–200, 2 printed pages per unit).

## Prose summary

This range closes out the imperative-sentence chapter and opens the long chapter on Cantonese speech conventions — politeness formulae, terms of address, and the kinship system — which together form one of the richest sociolinguistic/register sections of the grammar so far.

**Imperatives (end of Ch.19).** The book catalogs a set of particles and constructions that soften, reinforce, or negate commands. *Sīn (jī) dāk* tacked onto an imperative reinforces it ("you'd better… you know!"), while the coverb construction *tùhng (ngóh)* — literally "with/for me" — is used to reinforce an imperative in a pointedly non-polite, almost sarcastic way; the book explicitly compares it to the ironic English "do me a favor." Reduplicated adjectives (e.g. *lóuh-lóuh-saht-saht* "honestly-honestly") function as adverbs in imperatives, as does the pattern [adjective]-*dī* before the verb, which the book is careful to distinguish from the superficially identical but genuinely comparative [adjective]-*dī* that follows the *dāk*-adverbial construction. First-person ("let's") imperatives optionally drop the subject pronoun *ngóhdeih* and are typically closed off by a cooperation-seeking sentence particle (*ā*, *lā*, *lo*); *bātyùh* "rather" turns a 1st/2nd-person imperative into a suggestion. Singular "let me" uses *dáng*, which the book notes retains some of its literal "wait" meaning; third-person "let him/her/them" also uses *dáng*, or alternatively the emphatic high-rising-tone *yáu(h) dāk* (pronoun), which has lexicalized into the idiom *yáu dāk kéuih* "let it be / don't worry about it" — and into a contrastive-pronoun idiom *yáuh … yáuh* "each to his own." Negative imperatives split along a register axis: *mhóu* is the default, unmarked negative-command word, while *máih* is blunter and more typical of close friends/peers — a directly stated usage-tier observation from the authors. A pronunciation note flags that *mhóu* commonly contracts to a single syllable *móuh* in fast speech, which is then homophonous with (and risks confusion with) the unrelated existential-negation *móuh* "not have." *Chīnkèih mhóu* is identified as a stronger prohibitive, glossed to the English idiom "whatever you do, don't…". Indirect/reported commands pattern like indirect speech generally, using *giu* "tell, instruct" or the serial construction *wah (béi) [pronoun] tēng/jī* "tell X to know," retaining *mhóu*/*máih* inside the reported clause.

**Speech conventions (Ch.20 opening + §20.1).** The chapter opens with the concept of **face** (*mihn-jí* / *mín*, with its own tone change), explicitly presented as the conceptual root of a cluster of fixed formulae: *béi mín* "give face," *móuh (saai) mín* "lose (all) face," *lohk (ngóh) mín* / *dīu (ngóh) gá* "make me lose face." The book draws a direct comparison to Western "(self-)esteem" but stresses face carries greater social weight, especially for higher-status people. §20.1 then works through politeness speech-acts one by one: requests (*mgōi*/*mgōi léih*, plus the stronger critical-suggestion form *hóu sām léih*, and invitation-specific *chéng*/*chèuihbín*); thanks, where the book draws a genuinely fine-grained register distinction — *mgōi* for small favors/services versus *dōjeh* for gifts and major services (illustrated with the buyer/seller exchange at a point of sale, where *mgōi* and *dōjeh* are used by different parties for the same transaction) — with *mgōi saai*/*dōjeh saai* as intensified forms and *msái* ("no need") formulae to decline or deflect thanks; apologies (*deui-mjyuh* as the general apology — explicitly *not* used like English "sorry?" to request a repetition, which instead takes the colloquial *Há? Mātyéh wá?*, note also the casual phonological reduction of *mātyéh* to *mē*; *mgányiu*/*móuh sih ge* as replies to an apology; *mhóuyisi*, literally "I'm embarrassed," repurposed as a widely used face-saving apology; *màhfàahn léih* "trouble you" as an advance apology for an imposition; *mgōi je gwo* "excuse me" for passing through a crowd); and compliments, where the authors describe a cultural norm of ritually deflecting praise, either by rhetorical denial (*Bīndouh haih lē?* "I don't think so!") or by reframing the compliment as exaggeration (*Taai gwojéung la!* "You're too kind/that's overpraising!").

**Terms of address and kinship (§20.2–20.3).** §20.2 covers titles and names, noting that the *a-* prefix attaches to given names (including English ones) and to surnames, and — notably for register/phonology — that most Cantonese surnames carry low-register tones that undergo **tone change** when *a-*-prefixed, a pattern the text states explicitly applies even to English names. The epithet *lóuh* "old" is flagged explicitly as *not* offensive in these address terms, instead signaling familiarity and seniority between long-time friends or toward superiors (*lóuhbáan* "boss"). Several items here shade into true slang/figurative register: *daaih gājē* "big sister" extended to mean a big-shot/leading entertainer, and *daaih-lóu* "big brother" extended to mean a boss or gang ringleader. Also socially loaded are the reciprocal informal ethnonyms the book reports for Hongkongers' and mainlanders' banter about each other: Hongkongers colloquially call mainland Chinese "cousins" (*bíu-múi/bíu-jé/bíu-gō/bíu-sūk*, by age/gender) or the generic *A-Chaan* for mainland men, while mainlanders reciprocally call Hongkongers *Góng-Chaan* — flagged here as regional/intergroup slang with an othering edge, reported descriptively by the authors rather than endorsed. §20.3 turns to the kinship system proper, opening with a sociolinguistic aside (citing Pan 1993) that Hongkong's shift to small nuclear families is actively simplifying the traditional paternal/maternal kinship-term distinctions, with some terms (*a-sám*, *a-yī*) undergoing semantic extension to generic address for unrelated older people, while terms for very distant relatives are predicted to drop out of use. The system itself is organized around a paternal/maternal split (Table 20.1) and an elder/younger split for siblings and cousins (Table 20.2, with explicit tone-change examples: *muih-muih → mùihmúi*, *bíu+muih → bíumúi*), and sibling terms are reported as also used metaphorically for close non-kin friendship. §20.3.3 gives a three-way **register ladder** for "husband"/"wife" (formal/neutral/colloquial: *jeuhngfū*/*sīnsāang*/*lóuh-gūng* and *chāijí*/*taaitáai*/*lóuh-pòh*) and documents an asymmetric pair of marriage verbs, *ga* and *chéui*, whose distinct argument-structure privileges (which can take *sānpóuh* "daughter-in-law" vs. *léui* "daughter" as object, which can take directional particles *yahp*/*chēut*) the authors work through in some detail — a genuine syntactic finding, not just a vocabulary note. In-law terms for parents-in-law reflect the traditional patrilocal residence pattern directly in their morphology: a wife's terms for her husband's parents take the prefix *gā-* "home," while a husband's terms for his wife's parents take *ngohk-*/*ngoih-* instead. A *chìhn(-yahm)-* prefix marks ex-spouses and even extends informally to ex-boyfriend/girlfriend. The range ends at the start of §20.3.4 (Children and Grandchildren).

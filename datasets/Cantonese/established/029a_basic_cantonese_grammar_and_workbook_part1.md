# Basic Cantonese: A Grammar and Workbook (Yip & Matthews, Routledge, 2000) — Part 1 of 3

**Source:** Virginia Yip and Stephen Matthews, *Basic Cantonese: A Grammar and Workbook* (London/New York: Routledge, 2000). 184 pages. Extracted from a clean PDF text layer via `pdftotext -layout` (no vision reading required).

**Scope of this file:** Introduction, pronunciation chapters (Units 1–3: Consonants, Vowels and diphthongs, Tone), and grammar Units 4–10 (Pronouns; Possession: *ge*; Possession and existence: *yáuh*; Being: *haih*; Noun classifiers; Adjectives; Adverbs of manner). Companion files 029b and 029c cover Units 11–19 and 20–28 plus the glossary of grammatical terms.

**Romanization:** The authors use the Yale romanization system (with minor modifications from their earlier *Cantonese: A Comprehensive Grammar*, 1994). Tone diacritics in the original PDF rendered as corrupted glyphs in several places during text extraction (a known artifact of this particular PDF's font encoding for diacritic vowels); where the underlying tone mark could not be reconstructed with confidence from context, the base syllable is given without the diacritic rather than guessed.

**Authors' framing:** Yip and Matthews explicitly argue that Cantonese is not merely a pronunciation variant of Mandarin but has "its own fully-fledged grammatical system, largely independent of Mandarin grammar," comparable in its distinctiveness from Mandarin to the distance between Romance languages like French, Spanish, and Italian. The book is pedagogical (companion to the reference grammar *Cantonese: A Comprehensive Grammar*), organized by grammar point in roughly increasing difficulty, aimed at learners who want to go beyond phrasebook level. No Chinese characters are used — only Yale romanization — on the grounds that written Cantonese is rarely used for serious purposes (standard written Chinese, read with Cantonese pronunciations, is used instead) and that characters are better learned in conjunction with Mandarin.

## Unit 1 — Consonants

Cantonese initial consonants split into unaspirated stops (b, d, g, the labio-velars gw/kw, and the affricates j/ch) versus their aspirated counterparts (p, t, k, ch), plus fricatives (f, s, h) and nasal/liquids (m, n/l, ng). The crucial contrast for English speakers to unlearn is that Cantonese stops are never distinguished by voicing (as English p/b are) but purely by aspiration — Cantonese *b*, *d*, *g* are voiceless AND unaspirated, a combination that does not occur in English, which is why English speakers hear them ambiguously as sounding like either the voiced or voiceless English equivalent. The labio-velars gw/kw simplify to plain g/k before o or u in casual speech (gwok "country" → gok, collapsing with "feel"). The affricates j/ch have two allophonic realizations depending on the following vowel: alveo-palatal before front vowels (i, yu, eu/eui) resembling English "j"/"ch", and plain alveolar elsewhere, with no close English equivalent.

Final consonants are restricted to unreleased stops -p/-t/-k (the airstream is stopped but never released, as in casual English "yep!") and nasals -m/-n/-ng. The book flags a widespread sociolinguistic pattern: many speakers merge syllable-final -ng into -n in certain words (hohksaang "student" commonly pronounced hohksaan), and syllable-initial ng- is frequently dropped entirely (ngóh "I" heard as óh; ngaap "duck" as aap). A parallel, actively-debated merger affects initial n- versus l-: dictionary-prescribed n- (as in néih "you") is in practice almost universally pronounced with l- (léih) by native speakers, to the point that the author describes Hong Kong television campaigns that have tried to "outlaw" the l- forms as incorrect, even though l- is by far the dominant actual pronunciation. This is an explicit sociolinguistic/prescriptivism note worth flagging for register and sound-change research: the "standard" taught form and the overwhelmingly dominant colloquial form diverge, and the colloquial form is winning.

A short boxed note on consonants-in-names explains that Hong Kong place names and surnames (Kowloon, Kwun Tong, Tai Po, Kwok, Tang) follow older, inconsistent 19th/20th-century transcription conventions unrelated to Yale romanization, which can mislead learners trying to back-derive pronunciation from colonial-era spellings.

## Unit 2 — Vowels and diphthongs

Eight single vowels (a, aa, e, i, o, u, eu, yu) are described, with aa/a forming a length-and-quality minimal-pair contrast not shared by most other Chinese varieties (sām "heart" vs. sāam "three" — different length AND different quality, the long vowel being more open). The front rounded vowels yu and eu have no close English counterparts but correspond to French/German front rounded vowels (yu ≈ French/German ü; eu ≈ French eu). Vowel quality shifts contextually before velar codas -k/-ng (sihk "eat" sits between i and e; luhk "green" sits between u and o), which the book flags as a genuine source of minimal-pair confusion for learners (sihk vs. sehk "stone"; tùhng "with" vs. tòhng "sugar"). Diphthongs come in short/long pairs tied to the a/aa distinction (ai/aai, au/aau), with the long diphthongs resembling English "sky"/"how" and the short ones having no close English equivalent.

## Unit 3 — Tone

Cantonese is analyzed here with six contrastive tones in Hong Kong Cantonese (the book explicitly notes that some reference works count seven, nine, or even ten, and that tone inventories vary by dialect and by linguist — six is presented as the current working consensus and as plenty for a beginner). The six are organized as three pitch registers (high/mid/low) crossed with contour (level/rising, plus falling only at the extremes): high level/falling, high rising, mid level, low level, low rising, low falling. A genuinely useful observation for pedagogy and for cross-linguistic grammar-logic comparison: the book notes that the "high level" tone is often actually produced with a slight fall, and that different textbooks disagree about whether to treat level and falling as one tone or two at the high register — most speakers, per the authors, make no such distinction, and the authors deliberately choose the notational convention (high-level diacritic) that they judge dominant among younger Hong Kong speakers, an explicit acknowledgment of sound change in progress affecting notation choice.

**Tone change (sandhi) is flagged as linguistically important and only partially rule-governed.** The book describes a synchronic "changed tone" phenomenon in colloquial speech in which certain syllables shift to a high rising tone regardless of their lexical tone, in at least three environments: (i) nouns at the end of a compound/phrase (yàhn "person" → léuih-yán "woman" with a changed final syllable; yuht "month" → chóh-yút "confinement month after childbirth"); (ii) names bearing the familiar prefixes a- or lóuh- (Chàhn Sāang "Mr. Chan" formal vs. A-Chán colloquial, with tone-changed surname); (iii) the second syllable of reduplicated adjectives (sòh "foolish" → sòh-só-déi "silly"). The authors explicitly decline to give the full rule set, saying it is "too complex to spell out" and that learners absorb it through exposure — this is exactly the kind of native-speaker-judgment-only phenomenon flagged elsewhere in this project as requiring query-folder treatment rather than guessed extrapolation.

Other register-relevant tone notes: the word haih ("yes," always low-level tone) becomes dangerously close to an unrelated obscene word if pronounced with the emphatic high stress an English speaker would naturally apply — the book states this is "a common error, often committed by the second author [Stephen Matthews]," an explicit, humorously self-reported taboo-adjacency note. Separately, the particle m‑ (as in m̀h, Unit 25's homophone discussion) is explicitly distinguished from the superficially similar Mandarin question particle ma — a genuine false-friend across the two languages that the book warns against conflating.

## Unit 4 — Pronouns

Cantonese personal pronouns are simple relative to European languages: no gender, case, or social-status marking, and no distinct possessive forms (possession is built compositionally with ge, see Unit 5). The three persons — ngóh (I/we), néih (you), kéuih (he/she/they) — pluralize regularly with the suffix -deih. The book flags that each pronoun has a colloquial alternate form arising from regular sound change (óh for ngóh, léih for néih, héuih for kéuih), and that of these léih has become so dominant that it "has become by far the most common form" despite being taught as incorrect in some courses — the same n-/l- sociolinguistic split noted in Unit 1, here affecting the second-person pronoun specifically.

The suffix -deih has a narrow distribution: besides pluralizing pronouns, it only appears in yàhn-deih (a quasi-indefinite "people, one," which can also be used to talk about oneself indirectly/evasively — a politeness/face-saving strategy worth noting) and in contracted forms with proper names (A-Chán-deih "Chan and his family/associates"). Pronouns, like in many pro-drop languages (the book compares Italian, Spanish, Japanese), are freely omitted once understood from context — this extends even to objects and to the non-referential "it" of weather/ambient statements, which simply has no Cantonese counterpart at all (Cantonese has no dummy subject). The reflexive jihgéi covers all persons (myself/yourself/herself/etc.) and serves three functions: standalone reflexive object, pronoun-reinforcing emphasis, and adverbial "by oneself."

## Unit 5 — Possession: *ge*

ge is identified as "one of the most frequent words in Cantonese" with several distinct grammatical jobs (possession here; attributive-adjective linking in Unit 9; assertion-marking in Units 7 and 25). As a possessive marker it works both attributively (ngóh ge deihjí "my address") and predicatively after haih ("be," see Unit 7), and haih itself can be omitted, leaving a verbless possessive sentence (Bá jē kéuih ge "The umbrella is hers"). Two colloquial refinements are flagged: ge is often dropped entirely for close kin relations (ngóh lóuhgūng "my husband," no ge needed), and in colloquial speech a noun classifier (see Unit 8) frequently substitutes for ge to mark a specific individual possessed item (kéuih ga chē "his car" — literally "he classifier-for-vehicles car"), with the plural classifier dī used for "my children," "her clothes," etc. This classifier-as-possessive-marker pattern is explicitly called "especially Cantonese," distinguishing it from the Mandarin-derived ge pattern that would be more familiar to Mandarin learners.

## Unit 6 — Possession and existence: *yáuh*

yáuh ("have") functions as main verb and auxiliary, like English "have," but with one irregularity the book stresses repeatedly: its negative is the suppletive form móuh, not a regular m̀h+yáuh construction (*m̀h yáuh is ungrammatical). The yes/no question form is the juxtaposed yáuh-móuh ("have-not-have"), not the regular V-not-V pattern. yáuh also serves an existential function parallel to Spanish hay / French il y a ("there is/are"), with no preposition needed — the place expression simply opens the sentence (Hēunggóng yáuh hóu dō dīksí, literally "Hong Kong have very many taxis"). A further extension: yáuh + noun combines to derive adjectives (yáuh-chín "rich," yáuh-sām "thoughtful," yáuh-yuhng "useful"), with the móuh-negated counterparts yielding antonyms (móuh-yuhng "useless," móuh-líu "vacuous, ignorant" — the latter two are flagged as colloquial/informal register terms of mild criticism rather than neutral description, e.g., describing someone as rich "but too vacuous," taai móuh-líu).

## Unit 7 — Being: *haih*

haih introduces identificational/equative noun phrases (name, nationality, profession) but — critically, and repeatedly emphasized — is NOT used with predicative adjectives, which instead take hóu (literally "very," but here functioning as a near-meaningless syntactic placeholder rather than a true intensifier) before the adjective. haih doubles as a discourse particle meaning "yes," with mhaih as "no," but the book warns this mapping is unreliable: haih can affirmatively agree with a NEGATIVE question (Léih m̀h seun àh? "Don't you believe it?" → Haih a "No [I don't]" — i.e., "[it is true that I don't]"), and most yes/no questions in Cantonese are answered not with haih/mhaih at all but by repeating the main verb of the question (see Unit 23). A minimal-pair trap is flagged between haih (tonally distinct "to be," identificational) and hái ("to be at/in," locative) — different tone, different grammatical function, easily confused by learners. Finally, haih…ge forms a cleft-like emphatic construction for focusing a constituent (Haih léih bōng ngóh ge "It was YOU who helped me"), explicitly motivated by the book as compensating for the fact that a tone language has limited scope for using pitch-stress to mark emphasis the way English does.

## Unit 8 — Noun classifiers

Every countable Cantonese noun requires a classifier (measure word) when quantified — unlike English, where this is a marginal phenomenon (the book's own examples: "a brace of pheasants," "twenty head of cattle"). The chapter distinguishes narrow measures (léuhng bohng ngàuhyuhk "two pounds of beef"), open-ended container-classifiers (sāam būi chàh "three cups of tea" — the container word itself can take go as a generic classifier when referring to the container as an object), and collective classifiers (yāt tou sān sāam "a set of new clothes," gó deui hàaih "that pair of shoes" — note that deui "pair" is NOT used for English-pair concepts like trousers or scissors, which instead take tìuh and bá respectively; false-friend trap for English speakers over-applying "pair" semantics). The plural/partitive classifier dī covers both countable collections and mass/uncountable quantities (dī chàh "the tea," dī pàhngyáuh "some friends").

Classifier assignment follows two overlapping, sometimes-competing criteria that the book treats as linguistically significant: a semantic-class criterion (bá for tools, ga for vehicles/machines, go for people and abstract nouns, jek for animals and "one of a pair" body parts) and a shape criterion (faai for flat vertical surfaces, fūk for rectangular flat objects like pictures, jī for cylindrical objects, tìuh for long thin objects). Crucially, shape can OVERRIDE semantic class: elongated animals and clothing (tìuh yú "fish," tìuh sèh "snake," tìuh fu "trousers," tìuh kwàhn "skirt/dress") take tìuh rather than the semantically-expected jek (animals) or gihn (clothing) — this is exactly the kind of classifier-system fact flagged as worth a closer grammar-logic pass, since it shows the system is organized by at least two independent, partially-competing axes rather than a single taxonomy. Beyond quantification, the book highlights two uses "more characteristically Cantonese" than Mandarin: classifier-as-possessive-marker (Unit 5) and bare classifier+noun (without a number) to mark definiteness, where position relative to the verb determines the reading — classifier+noun before the verb is definite ("the"), while after the verb it can be definite or indefinite-specific ("a certain…").

## Unit 9 — Adjectives

Cantonese adjectives are analyzed as a subtype of stative verb rather than a wholly distinct word class — the book explicitly tells learners not to worry about whether adjectives "exist" as a category, since whatever applies to verbs generally applies to them. Attributive use (before the noun) requires the linking particle ge (hóu kàhnlihk ge hohksāang "hard-working students"); predicative use (stating a property) takes hóu as a required syntactic element rather than haih, as already flagged in Unit 7 — hóu here does not really mean "very," it is "merely part of the syntax of predicative adjectives," and other degree words (géi "quite," gam "so," taai "too") can substitute in the same slot.

A distinct intensification strategy is reduplication + the suffix -déi (dīn "crazy" → dīn-dīn-déi "rather crazy"; fèih "fat" → fèih-féi-déi "chubby"), which triggers a tone change to high-rising on the repeated syllable (except when the base tone is already high level or high rising) — another concrete instance of the tone-sandhi phenomenon introduced in Unit 3. These reduplicated forms CANNOT co-occur with hóu/géi/other degree modifiers (*hóu dīn-dīn-déi is explicitly marked ungrammatical), showing the reduplicated form already encodes its own degree meaning ("rather X") and is in syntactic complementary distribution with the modifier strategy. The same reduplication+déi strategy extends to adverbs of manner in Unit 10.

## Unit 10 — Adverbs of manner

Three distinct manner-adverb strategies are presented: (1) verb—dāk—adjective ("manner" construction: Léih sé dāk hóu hóu "You write well," with the verb repeated before dāk when it has an object: Kéuih cheung-gō cheung dāk hóu cha "He sings badly"); (2) adjective+gám ("thus/in this way") forming a pre-verbal adverbial phrase (hóu daaih-sēng gám paak-sáu "applauded loudly"), with gám/gám yéuhng also usable standalone to mean "in this way/like this"; (3) reduplication+déi for a limited set of manner adverbs (gwāai "obedient" → gwāai-gwāai-déi "obediently"), parallel to the adjective-reduplication pattern in Unit 9, including the same tone-change behavior and the option to combine with gám for multi-syllable adjectives (gáan-gáan-dāan-dāan gám "in a simple way," built from gáan-dāan "simple" by reduplicating each syllable separately). All three strategies are pre-verbal except the dāk-construction, which is post-verbal — a word-order asymmetry worth flagging for anyone mapping Cantonese adverbial syntax against English or Mandarin.

## Overview

The range opens (pp.124-125) finishing off coverage of coverb/preposition-like elements: *jiu* "follow/continue" used with the particle *fāan* "back," plus an idiom where *jiu* functions adverbially before a verb meaning "carry on ... regardless" (illustrated with drinking, smoking, and post-1997-handover dancing/horse-racing examples — the latter a period-specific colloquial reference to Hong Kong's 1997 handover anxiety). *tùhng* "with" for personal relationships, *waih*/*waihjó* "for (the sake/benefit of)," and *yānwaih* "because (of)," which can function as either preposition or conjunction — notably, when paired with a reason-clause and negation, the position of the *yānwaih* phrase relative to *mh* (not) is truth-conditionally meaningful in Cantonese in a way the English translation "I wouldn't leave him for that reason" leaves ambiguous. An idiom box covers *tùhng ... móuh gwāan(haih)* "have nothing to do with."

§7.2.2 (pp.126-127) covers non-spatial localizers/postpositions: the time-reference pairs *jīchìhn/yìhchìhn* "before" and *jīhauh/yìhhauh* "after" (the *jī*-forms behave as postpositions directly after a noun phrase, the *yìh*-forms as adverbs), plus *jīloih/yìhloih* "within" and *jī-ngoih/yìh-ngoih* "beyond." *yìhseuhng* "above" and *yìhhah* "below" mark numeric lower/upper limits (e.g., age or price thresholds). *kèih jūng* "among others" is flagged as a literary phrase that has nonetheless spread into ordinary speech, commonly used to refer back to an already-mentioned set (illustrated with a Chao Yuen-Ren reference).

Chapter 8, The Verb Phrase, opens at p.128. §8.1 sketches the traditional four-way verb typology (copular/stative/action/auxiliary) and immediately flags that the boundaries — stative-verb-vs-adjective, auxiliary-vs-other — are gradient, not categorical. §8.1.1 is a long treatment of the copula *haih* "be": it is the one place true copula marking is needed (nouns are *not* linked to adjectives with *haih* — that's a stative-verb pattern, ch. 9), and in neutral declarative contexts *haih* is frequently omittable entirely without changing the sentence's grammaticality, surviving mainly as an emphasis/focus device. The *haih ... ge* cleft construction (parallel to Mandarin *shì ... de* and English it-clefts) emphasizes the word immediately following *haih*; since *haih* itself is droppable from this construction, the authors suggest the humble sentence-final particle *ge* found elsewhere in the grammar may historically be a reduced survival of this same cleft pattern. Two striking idioms close out the *haih* discussion: *haih (ngóh) ge*, used elliptically (with *haih* itself conventionally dropped) to mean "it's on me" i.e. "my treat" — attested from a TV ad; and *haih ... dōu*, which marks stubborn insistence/determination ("he'll go himself no matter what," "he won't carry an umbrella even in the rain"). *haih* also appears in A-not-A yes/no questions (*haih-mhaih*), as a bare affirmative answer to particle questions, introducing predicative complements with *syun haih* "count as" and *dong ... haih* "regard/treat as," and inside *dōu*-quantified "every" sentences.

§8.1.2 Stative Verbs distinguishes a broad traditional Chinese-grammar sense (where "stative verb" essentially covers what this book elsewhere calls adjectives) from a narrower sense restricted to verbs that merely share adjective-like properties (modifiable by *géi* "quite" and *hóu* "very," reduplicable with *-déi*) while still behaving syntactically as verbs that take objects — e.g. *gēng* "fear/be afraid of," which can both take a direct object (unlike the English adjective "afraid") and reduplicate as *gēng-gēng-déi* "a bit afraid." §8.1.3 Verbs of Perception is one of the structurally richest sections in this range: it draws a systematic distinction between bare perceptual-activity verbs (*tái* "look," *tēng* "listen," *mohng* "stare," *màhn* "sniff," *gok dāk* "feel") and their resultative compounds with *dóu* or *gin* that denote actual successful perception of a stimulus (*tái dóu/tái gin* "see," *tēng dóu/tēng gin* "hear"). These verb+particle compounds are "potentially separable" even though conventionally written as single Yale words — the negator *mh* and the modal particle *dāk* can be inserted between the two parts (*tái mh gin* "cannot see," *tái dāk gin* "can see"), a productive pattern worth flagging for any later morphology/word-structure pass. *dāk chēut* with perception verbs marks recognitional ability ("can tell by looking/listening/tasting"), with *mh chēut* as its negative counterpart. A cluster of *tái*-idioms follows: *tái-háh*/*tái-gwo* "it depends" (used before an indirect question), *tái làih* "it looks like, apparently" (an evidential/inferential marker, illustrated from a film line), *tái hóu* "be optimistic" (intransitive or transitive), *tái hōi* "look on the bright side" (*hōi* "away" extended metaphorically to mean looking away from a problem), *tái chúhng* "regard as important" with converse *tái hēng*/*tái síu* "underestimate," and *tái dāi*/*tái mh héi* "look down on (someone)." §8.1.4 Verbs of Cognition lists *jīdou* "know (facts)" vs. *sīk* "know (a person/skill)" — the latter is also idiomatically used for having social connections (*sīk yàhn*) — plus *lám* "think," *yíhwàih*/*yihngwàih* "think, consider" (the former specifically flagged as usually marking an *erroneous* belief — a useful epistemic-marking distinction), *gokdāk* "feel (opinion)," *geidāk*/*mgeidāk* "remember/forget," *yihngdāk* "recognize," and *seun* "believe." Cognition-verb complement clauses need no overt complementizer corresponding to English "that."

§8.2 (pp.136-141) covers objects and transitivity. §8.2.1 notes Cantonese motion verbs take bare directional objects with no preposition (*heui Méihgwok* "go to America"), and that many verbs (*duhk* "study," *sihk* "eat") can take either a direct or a locative object with no marking difference. §8.2.2 Indirect Objects: ditransitive *béi* "give" places the indirect object *after* the direct object (opposite the unmarked English order), though the order can flip when the direct object is unusually long. *je* "lend" is lexically ambivalent — with indirect-before-direct word order it can mean "borrow" instead, so direction of transfer is disambiguated by constituent order rather than by a different verb, which is a notable typological point. Many "give"-type verbs (*sung* "give as a present," *wàahn* "return," *gei* "mail," others) take a serial-verb construction with *béi* as a second, semantically bleached verb marking the recipient, and a third verb can even follow the indirect object (e.g. *béi léih tái* "give to you to look at"). Two-object verbs like *giu*/*dong* "call, consider, treat as" recruit *jouh* "do" as a semantically light second verb (*yàhndeih giu kéuih jouh fèihlóu* "people call him fatty"); the same *giu (jouh)* pattern gives one's own name. The idiom *dong ngóh móuh dou* "treat me as if I'm not here / treat me like nobody" is a colloquial complaint about being disregarded. §8.2.3 Quantified Objects: an object quantified with *dōu* "all, every" is fronted before the verb, and the head noun can be elided leaving only its classifier when context makes the referent clear.

§8.2.4 Verb-Object Compounds (pp.140-141) covers lexicalized V-O compounds written with a hyphen to show their tight bond (*tái-syū* "read," *cheung-gō* "sing," *gin-gūng* "go to an interview," *yám-yéh* "have a drink," *sé-seun* "write a letter," *jā-chē* "drive"), noting that several of their component verbs (*cheung* "sing," *sé* "write," *jā* "drive") are rarely used intransitively without some object, unlike their English glosses. Despite their tight bonding, V-O compounds can still be interrupted: by aspect markers/verbal particles (*tái-gán-syū* "reading"), by topicalization stranding the bare verb, and — most strikingly — by an intervening possessor pronoun, as in *chaat léih hàaih* "polish your shoes" i.e. "flatter/suck up to you" (compound *chaat-hàaih*, literally "polish-shoes"), *bōng ngóh sáu* "help my hand" i.e. "give me a hand" (compound *bōng-sáu* "help out"), and *púng léih chèuhng* "hold up your stage" i.e. "support you" (compound *púng-chèuhng* "support"); the authors compare this to English "he's pulling your leg." *git-fān* "get married" (cited from a film) exemplifies the same compound pattern with a time-expression intervening. §8.2.5 Adverbial Objects covers frequency/duration noun phrases, the repetition of a transitive verb before such a time expression (*sái-jó léuhng chi* "washed twice"), and a distinctive "verbal measure phrase" built from *yāt* "one" plus a body-part noun functioning almost like a classifier for the action itself — strikingly colloquial/physical idioms like *sek ... yāt daahm* "give a bite/kiss," *dá ... yāt háh* "give a slap," *bōng ... yāt go mòhng* "do a favor," *tek ... yāt geuk* "give a kick," and *tái ... dō yāt ngáahn* "take another look" (literally "one more eye").

§8.3 Serial Verbs (pp.142-143) opens the chapter's major syntactic topic: chains of two or more verbs concatenated with no connective, a pattern the authors note is "almost unknown in European languages" apart from a handful of fixed English collocations ("go see a movie"). §8.3.1 Serial Constructions Expressing Actions shows how English prepositional relations are routinely rendered in Cantonese by stacking verbs instead — most notably *bōng léih dá-dihnwá* "I'll phone for you" (literally "help you call-phone"), since there is no preposition directly corresponding to benefactive "for" (the coverb *waih* covers only the narrower "for the sake of" sense, already noted earlier in this range). The authors draw out a fine semantic contrast: English "help you (to) phone" implies the addressee also participates, while the Cantonese serial construction implies the speaker performs the entire action single-handedly on the addressee's behalf — a genuine divergence in event-structure encoding, not just surface syntax. The coverb *tùhng* "with/accompany" used serially to express "from" is flagged as structurally ambiguous (*tùhng léih máaih ga chē* "I'll buy a car for/from/with you" — three possible relations from one surface form).

Nothing in this range is marked as taboo, slang, or heavily register-marked vocabulary in the sense of the usage-tier taxonomy's `slang`/`taboo` categories — this is core grammatical-function vocabulary and example sentences from a reference grammar, though several V-O compounds (*chaat-hàaih* "flatter," *git-fān* "get married") and idioms (*dong ngóh móuh dou*) carry a distinctly colloquial, everyday-spoken flavor worth flagging for later cross-referencing against the live slang corpus.

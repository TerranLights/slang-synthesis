# Cantonese: A Comprehensive Grammar (Matthews & Yip) — Units 141-150 (pp. 260-279)

**Source:** Matthews, S. & Yip, V., *Cantonese: A Comprehensive Grammar*. DjVu pages 260-279
(10 rendered units, 2 printed pages per unit). Covers the tail end of Chapter 13 (Negation),
the whole of Chapter 14 (Quantification), and the opening of Chapter 15 (Existential Sentences)
through section 15.1.

## Prose summary

The range opens on the last page of the negation chapter, illustrating double-negative
constructions in Cantonese: *mhaih mséung* ("not that [I] don't want"), *móuh ... mh* combined
with a negated verb to produce universal "all/every" readings, and modal double negatives such
as *mh heui mh dāk* ("absolutely have to go") where two negative elements compound into an
emphatic positive/obligatory meaning, structurally parallel to Cockney "I dunno nothing."

The bulk of the range is Chapter 14, Quantification, which the authors frame as an area where
Cantonese departs substantially from English: there is no single word matching "everything" or
"something," and each flavor of quantification (universal, existential, relative, comparative)
calls for its own construction. The backbone of universal quantification ("all," "every," "any")
is the preverbal adverb *dōu*, which must co-occur with a quantified phrase that itself precedes
the verb — Cantonese does not allow a bare quantified object after the verb the way English does.
This *dōu*-phrase can sit either between the subject and *dōu* or front the whole sentence as a
topic. The same *dōu*-dependency extends to classifier reduplication (*go-go*, *jek-jek* — "each
and every"), to reduplicated nouns and time words (*yàhn-yàhn*, *chì-chì*, *yaht-yaht*), to the
formal construction *só yáuh (ge)* "whatever there are" (an emphatic "all" used in more formal
registers), and to the distributive word *múih* "each," which patterns syntactically like the
question word *bīn* "which." Wh-indeterminates are a striking cross-cutting pattern here: question
words (*bīngo*, *mātyéh*, *bīndouh*, *géisìh*, *dím yéung*) combine with *dōu* to yield free-choice
"any" readings ("anyone," "anything," "anywhere"), and the same wh-word + *dōu* template recurs in
negative polarity contexts, conditionals, and A-not-A questions — a single indeterminate-plus-
quantificational-particle mechanism doing the job that English spreads across distinct words. The
chapter also catalogs a cluster of colloquial/idiomatic intensifiers worth flagging for the slang
layer: *lihn ... dōu* "even," *sèhngyaht* used hyperbolically for "always," *chyùhnbouh ... dōu*
"the whole lot," *hahm-baahng-laahng* (commonly reduced to *hahmba'laahng*) "all/completely,"
*daaihgā* "everyone (here, in public address)," the formal/polite *gok wái* "everyone" (built on
the polite classifier *wái*), and the register-marked trio *sihdaahn* "at random," *chèuihbín* "as
you please," *chèuihsih* "at any time" as colloquial alternatives to formal free-choice "any."
Existential quantification ("some/none") is carried by the verb *yáuh* and its negative
counterpart *móuh*, previewing Chapter 15; emphatic denial is formed by *móuh* plus the numeral
*yāt* ("not even one"), and *móuh* + negated verb again surfaces as a double-negative route to
universal "every." Relative quantities (*dō* "many/much," *síu* "few/little") are notable for not
distinguishing count vs. mass nouns the way English does, and for functioning as predicates (like
adjectives) that can themselves take the perfective aspect marker *-jó*, yielding idiomatic
extensions *dō-jó* "extra" and *síu-jó* "missing." A run of colloquial quantity idioms appears
here too: *haih gam dō* "that's all" (a set phrase in shopping/service transactions), *daaih bá*
"plenty," *msíu* "not a few / quite a bit" (a negated "few" that pragmatically inverts to mean a
substantial amount), and *mdō msíu* "not a lot but not a little," used as a modesty formula. The
comparative subsystem reuses *dō*/*síu* as "more"/"less" inside *gwo*- and *béi*-comparisons, with
*chīu gwo* as a more formal "more than" used with figures, and *dōsou*/*síusou* "most(ly)"/"few,
minority" for proportional quantification. The range closes with the opening of Chapter 15
(Existential Sentences), establishing that *yáuh* covers both possessive "have" and existential
"there is/are" without a clear formal split between the two functions, and that it takes aspect
markers (*-gwo*, *-jó*) and verbal particles (*fāan*) like any other verb — setting up the fuller
treatment of existential/negative-existential sentence structure in the rest of Chapter 15.

Nothing in this stretch touches tone sandhi directly, but the classifier-system material is
dense: besides ordinary classifier reduplication for distributive "each/every" (14.1.2), the text
shows classifiers combining with *dōu* for "both/all N of them" (*léuhng go dōu*, *sāam go dōu*),
and the polite classifier *wái* appearing in the set formal-address term *gok wái*. The colloquial
register markers (*chèuihbín*, *sihdaahn*, *daaih bá*, *msíu*, *hahmba'laahng*) are flagged
explicitly as register/slang-relevant since the book itself labels several of them "idiom" or
"colloquial" in boxed asides, distinct from the neutral grammatical backbone of *dōu*-quantification.

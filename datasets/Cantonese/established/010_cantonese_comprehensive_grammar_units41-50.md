## Overview

The range opens (pp. 64-65) by finishing off Chapter 3's treatment of adverbial modification: adjectives can modify verbs through two mirror-image constructions, [verb]-*dāk*-[adjective] (e.g. *jā dāk taai faai* "drive too fast") and [adjective]-*gám*-[verb] (e.g. *daaih-sēng gám góng-yéh* "speak loudly"), and sentence-level adverbs split into a fixed pre-verbal class (quantity/frequency words like *juhng* "still" and *dōu* "also", which must sit immediately before the verb) versus a "movable" class (*yìhgā* "now", *waahkjé* "perhaps") that can appear either pre-verbally or sentence-initially. The book then closes out its particle inventory, distinguishing verbal particles (post-verb markers of result/completion such as *dóu*, *yùhn*, *saai*, *hōi*) from sentence-final particles (*a*, *lā*, *mē*, etc.), noting that because many verbal particles are homophonous with independent verbs (*dou* "arrive", *fàan* "return"), their status as a distinct category is debatable — paralleled explicitly to English phrasal-verb particles like *up*/*off*. The authors flag the rich sentence-final particle system as one of Cantonese's most distinctive features, with no direct English counterpart, serving to mark speech-act type, evidentiality, and emotional coloring, and note a theoretical proposal that treats them as sentence-final complementizers (COMP) filling the structural slot that English *that* occupies clause-initially. The section closes with conjunctions: spoken Cantonese avoids simple coordinators like English "and," relying instead on bare juxtaposition (inferred conjunction/disjunction from context, especially productive in fixed expressions like *máaih maaih ga* "buying and selling prices"), and "double conjunctions" that require a paired correlative in the main clause (*yānwaih...sóyíh* "because...so", *sēuiyìhn...daahnhaih* "although...but", *yùhgwó...jauh* "if...then", *jauhsyun...dōu* "even if...still") — a pattern the authors note surfaces as a transfer error in Chinese speakers' English ("although...but...").

Chapter 4 (pp. 66-81) is a substantial typological chapter on word order and topic-prominence. Basic order is SVO, but the authors catalogue four systematic deviations: (a) subject-object-verb order in secondary topicalization and *dōu* constructions; (b) verb-subject (inverted) order with a class the authors call "ergative" verbs — motion verbs, weather verbs, verbs of appearance/disappearance/occurrence, and the verb *séi* "die" — where the logical theme/experiencer surfaces post-verbally as if it were a direct object (*Gāmyaht làih-jó hóu dō daaih lāp-lóuh* "A lot of big shots came today"), with the same noun phrase also grammatical pre-verbally when marked indefinite by the existential verb *yáuh*; (c) right-dislocation, in which a subject or object is placed after the predicate (and even after the sentence-final particle) as an afterthought, typical of exclamations (*Hóu lēk wo, léih!* "You're so smart!"; *Chīsin gàh, léih!* "You're crazy!"); and (d) topicalization proper, treated at length in 4.2. The book frames Cantonese as a "topic-prominent" language in the Li & Thompson (1976) sense, distinct from English's "subject-prominent" structure: the sentence topic need bear no grammatical relation at all to the comment that follows it (the "as for..." reading), and when no other element is topicalized the subject becomes topic by default — which is offered as the underlying reason subjects (unlike objects) must be definite or generic in Cantonese, with indefinite subjects instead introduced via the existential marker *yáuh* "have" (*Tàuhsīn yáuh go yàhn yahp-jó làih* "Someone came in just now"). Several topicalization subtypes are distinguished: ordinary object-fronting (often with no subject at all, giving a passive-like reading English would need the passive voice for); "double subject" / hanging-topic constructions where the fronted topic has only a loose part-whole or associative link to the predicate (*Hēunggóng làuhga gwai dou séi* "Flat prices in Hong Kong are ridiculous [lit. expensive to death]"); secondary topicalization, where the topic follows rather than precedes the grammatical subject; and verb topicalization, in which a fronted verb is obligatorily echoed in the comment, often as a contrastive A-not-A pattern (*gám-mh-gám* "dare-not-dare", *gau-mh-gau* "enough-not-enough") with the copula *haih* able to substitute for a repeated main verb. The chapter closes with "topic chains," in which one topic, introduced once (often via an existential *yáuh*-clause), persists across several following clauses as their implicit subject or object without being repeated.

Chapter 5 (pp. 78-83) opens the treatment of pronouns. Cantonese personal pronouns are the only word class with a productive plural marker, the suffix *-deih* (*ngóh → ngóhdeih*, *léih → léihdeih*, *kéuih → kéuihdeih*); the suffix is pronoun-specific and cannot generalize to ordinary nouns (no *\*hohksāang-deih*), though the borrowed Mandarin plural *-mùhn* (Cantonese *mùhn*) survives as a formal-register option on bisyllabic nouns, e.g. *tùhnghohk-mùhn* "fellow students." There is no gender distinction anywhere in the pronoun system — *kéuih* covers "he/she/it" uniformly, unlike written Mandarin which assigns distinct characters by gender — and *kéuih* also extends, especially in object position, to inanimate and abstract referents, and even occurs pleonastically (referring to nothing in particular) in toasts and idiomatic collocations such as *wah jī kéuih* and the death-idiom *séi-jó-kéuih* ("I'd rather die," and, directed at a third party, "go to hell"). A boxed pronunciation note flags three sound changes in progress, which is the most linguistically striking material in this range: (a) loss of the initial *ng-* consonant, so *ngóh* "I" is widely heard as *óh* with a glottal onset; (b) a documented *n- → l-* merger in progress, under which the conservative, older-generation/formal pronunciation of "you" is *néih* while the now-dominant form is *léih* — a change still stigmatized by some language teachers as an "error" despite being the majority form; and (c) a *k- → h-* substitution particularly common among younger speakers, under which *kéuih* "he/she/it" is increasingly pronounced *héuih*. This is presented as real-time evidence of ongoing phonological change correlated with age/generation, i.e. a register and generational-variation datum rather than mere free variation. Genitive/possessive meaning is expressed analytically via the linking particle *ge* between pronoun and possessed noun (no distinct possessive pronoun forms exist), though the particle may be dropped before a classifier+noun sequence (*ngóh jek sáují* "my finger"). Name + *kéuihdeih* forms a flexible collective expression equivalent to "so-and-so and their group/family/colleagues," illustrated with the Cantonese phonetic loan-rendering *Bóulòh* for the English name "Paul," contractible to *Bóulòh-deih*. The chapter's final section (5.1.1) catalogues the two licensing conditions for subject/object pro-drop: the referent was already established as topic of a prior sentence or dialogue turn, or the referent's identity is simply clear from the immediate speech context (especially common for first/second person and for a present third person).

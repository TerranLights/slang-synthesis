## Front matter and Introduction

The back-cover/title-page framing explicitly positions this as a step up from *Basic Cantonese*: for learners who have "achieved basic proficiency" and want "more complex language," with 25 units combining grammar explanation and communicative exercises, examples drawn from contemporary media (films, advertising, songs, soap operas), and — notably — explicit differentiation between colloquial and formal speech registers. The Introduction frames Cantonese's sociopolitical position circa 1999-2001: despite Putonghua's rising institutional status after the 1997 Hong Kong handover and 1999 Macau handover, Cantonese remains the predominant daily language of Hong Kong, reinforced by the "one country, two systems" policy and the promise of "no change for fifty years." Hong Kong's official policy is explicitly trilingual/biliterate (spoken Cantonese, Mandarin, English; written Chinese and English), and "mother-tongue education" (introduced 1998) pushed more schools toward Cantonese-medium instruction even as Putonghua pressure grew.

The most linguistically significant passage in this range is the **"Spoken Cantonese and written Chinese"** section: the book explicitly describes a diglossic split between "High Cantonese" (close to standard written Chinese, used in news broadcasts and professional contexts) and "Low Cantonese" (colloquial, domestic, used in soap operas/nursery rhymes). It notes that Cantonese essentially lacks a standardized script for its colloquial register — characters for colloquial words either don't exist or aren't standardized — forcing writers (especially journalists quoting interviewees) to improvise: borrowing English letters (D for the comparative dī), using quasi-phonetic substitute characters, or coining ad hoc forms, all of which "fall foul of language purists." This diglossia/writing-system gap is a genuinely intermediate-level topic not covered in the beginner-level *Basic Cantonese* and is worth flagging for any future work on Cantonese register and script variation. The Introduction also previews the book's explicit pedagogical philosophy (descriptive, not prescriptive; language as creative/productive system) and flags that exercises in the Key are "indicative rather than definitive," with daggers marking harder exercises.

## Unit 1 — Consonants and vowels

This unit assumes *Basic Cantonese*'s material as a baseline and goes deeper into variation. It covers: difficulties for English speakers (front rounded vowels **yu**/**eu**, requiring lip-rounding with no true English equivalent; the affricates **j**/**ch**, which the book separately treats under palatalization); long/short diphthong pairs (**ai** vs. **aai**, **au** vs. **aau**) where the short form isn't just shorter but qualitatively different, closer to **ei**; contextual vowel-quality shifts of **i**/**u** before velars **k**/**ng** (sihk "eat" can sound like sehk "stone"; sung "give" can sound like song "to lose [money]" as in song-sái) — flagged as a genuine source of ambiguity even among native speakers, exemplified by sìhng/sèhng "whole," where the formal and colloquial pronunciations of the *same word* actually differ.

**Palatalization** is described as a systematic process: **s**, **j**, **ch** shift toward English-like [ʃ]/[tʃ]/[tʃʰ] articulations before the rounded vowels **yu**/**eu** (syū "book" → [ʃ]; jyuh "to live" → [dʒ]-like; chéui "most" → [tʃ]-like) — this is presented as a genuine phonetic rule, not free variation.

**Sound changes in progress** form the bulk of the unit and are its most sociolinguistically rich content: (i) initial **n-** is merging into **l-** so completely that the "correct" prescriptive néih "you" is said to be rarer in practice than léih, with the book noting purists still resist (paralleling the identical note in *Basic Cantonese*); (ii) initial **ng-** is frequently dropped, with variable retention depending on tone register (more often kept in high/mid tones, lost more often in low tones per the book's own rule-of-thumb table); (iii) **gw-**/**kw-** reduce to plain **g-**/**k-** in casual speech specifically before back vowels **o**/**u** (gwok "country" → gok; gwokgā "nation" → gokgā), but not before **a** (gwa "hang" resists the change) — a genuinely specific phonological conditioning environment; (iv) syllabic **ng** (as a standalone syllable, e.g. the surname Ng/Ngh) is shifting to **m**, which produces an unintentionally funny collision: the surname-plus-title Ngh-siujé ("Miss Ng") comes to sound like M̀h-siujé, homophonous with "not graceful" (m̀h-si-màahn) — an explicit joke the book itself flags; (v) final **-ng** is merging into **-n** (hohksaang "student" → hohksaan), especially common after the long **aa** vowel; (vi) final stops **-t**/**-k** are merging in some clusters, producing near-homophone pairs the book explicitly warns about: sāk-chē "traffic jam" vs. sāt-chē "missing car," and bāt-bihn "inconvenient" vs. bāk-bihn "north side." Exercises 1.1-1.4 drill minimal pairs and include Cantonese tongue-twisters (including one built around a scene from a specific film, Gām-jí yuhk-yihp, and a buy-chicken/buy-turtle street-vendor rhyme) plus a pronunciation-with-sound-change drill using real place names and celebrity names (Aaron Kwok Fu-Shing, Guangzhou, Nanjing, Guangxi).

## Unit 2 — Tone contours

This unit revisits Cantonese's six tones (entering/checked tones are treated as allophones of the high/mid/low level tones, following *Basic Cantonese*'s convention) but goes substantially further than a beginner treatment by introducing a numeric 1-5 pitch scale (so level 55, rising 25/35, etc.) to describe contours precisely, and by discussing *documented dialectal and generational variation* in the tone system itself — content that is genuinely new relative to the beginner book. Notable points: the citation "first tone" varies between a true high level (dominant description) and a high-falling realization that is actually more common in Guangzhou than Hong Kong, treated as free dialectal variation rather than error. The book works through easily-confused tone-pair contrasts in detail — high rising vs. low rising (same starting pitch, different height reached), low rising vs. low falling (asymmetric: falling descends further than rising ascends, and can create "creaky voice"), low level vs. low falling (acoustically close, disambiguated partly by voice quality), and mid level vs. low rising.

The **mid level / low rising merger** is the most striking sociolinguistic finding in this range: the book states this near-distinction is *already merging* for many Hong Kong speakers (especially younger ones, who may be unable to perceive it at all) and that some overseas varieties (explicitly: Cantonese spoken in Malaysia) have already collapsed it completely, yielding a working five-tone system instead of six for those speakers. This is presented as active sound change, not settled fact, and is exactly the kind of register/dialect-variation note worth flagging for future Cantonese slang-synthesis mechanics work, since younger/informal speech may pattern differently from the "citation" six-tone system assumed elsewhere in the book. Exercises 2.1-2.3 drill minimal pairs/triplets across these contrasts, including everyday vocabulary (sell/hard, draw a line/draw an eel, stock market/street market) and more exercises on recognizing contour differences in full phrases.

## Unit 3 — Changed tones (through Exercise 3.3)

This is the richest unit in the range and covers a genuinely advanced topic absent from the beginner volume: **tone sandhi as a productive, partly-grammaticalized word-formation device.** The book distinguishes two changed-tone types. The **high rising changed tone** is far more common and applies across several distinct grammatical environments: compounds with specialized/non-compositional meaning (yaht "day" → yàhn-yát "everyone's birthday"); personal names with the familiarizing prefixes a- or lóuh- (A-Láih "Mr. Lai," Lóuh-Chán "Old Mr. Chan") and fully reduplicated nicknames (Fèih-Féi "Fatty"); the second syllable of reduplicated adjectives/adverbs expressing a diminished/approximative degree (hàahm → hàahm-háam-déi "rather salty," wàhn → wàhn-wán-déi "a bit dizzy, tipsy") — this pattern is *not* fully general, applying mainly to words with low/mid citation tones rather than high ones, and only ever to the compound-final syllable; a set of kinship/relationship nouns where the change is close to obligatory (yàhn-yán "woman," làahm-yán "man," múih-yán "matchmaker," tòuh-yáu "boy/girlfriend," móhng-yáu "Internet fan/pal," faatsiu-yáu "fanatic/aficionado" — note móhng-yáu is an explicitly modern, internet-era coinage, a good example of new vocabulary this intermediate book captures that a beginner book from the same series would not); and professional/company-name suffixes built on hóng "firm, company" (leuhtsī-hóng "solicitors' firm," ginggéi-hóng "brokers' firm"), alongside optional-tone-change cases like ngàhn-hòhng/ngàhn-hóng "bank."

Several sub-patterns are flagged as independently notable: a **verb/noun word-class alternation realized purely through tone** — the mid-level verb daai "bring" contrasts with the high-rising noun daái "belt, strap"; doih "to pocket (something)" (verb) contrasts with dói "pocket, bag" (noun). The same alternation recurs systematically between **classifiers (low/citation tone) and the derived nouns they combine with (changed, high-rising tone)**: bohng "pound" (classifier) vs. bóng "a scale" (noun); dihp "plate" (classifier) vs. díp "a plate" / dihnlóuh chìh-díp "computer disk" (noun); doih "bag" (classifier) vs. dói "handbag" (noun); hahp "box" (classifier) vs. háp "box" / ngòh-pín "sliced goose, a Chiu Chow specialty" (noun); pin "slice" (classifier) vs. pín "slice of fish/record" / cheung-pín "CD, record" (noun); pùhn "basin" (classifier) vs. pún "wash basin" (noun, though fā-pùhn "flowerpot" is an explicit exception retaining the low tone); tìuh "strip" (classifier) vs. tíu "strip of fish, savory doughnut" (noun). This is a systematic morphosyntactic use of tone alone (no segmental change) to derive a noun from its own classifier — a mechanism worth a closer grammar-logic pass given how productive and regular it appears across the examples given.

The rarer **high level changed tone** clusters around kinship diminutives/address terms (múih "younger sister" → múi "little sister," as an address term) and, strikingly, a set of **colloquial, book-flagged-as-pejorative compound terms for women/girls** built on this múi root: daaih-ngáahn-múi "big-eyed girl," dākyi-múi "cutie," daaih-luhk-múi "mainland girl" (explicitly regional/origin-based), and bun-múi "Filipina (domestic helper)" (explicitly ethnicity/occupation-based). The book itself states these forms "are colloquial and tend to be pejorative" — this is a directly-flagged taboo-adjacent register cluster worth noting for future slang-tier/taboo-tier work, since it's an authentic, author-identified example of the usage_tier=slang + mild social-derogation pattern this project tracks. Other high-level-tone items include méi "last" → daih-méi "the last (in a series)" / báan-méi "come last (e.g. in an exam)"; a baby-talk register pattern (hóuyáih "very nice" → hóu-yái, yái-yái "naughty," via high-level tone); and a soap-opera-sourced example of the high-level tone used *ironically* — dauh "big" shifting to daai (high level) to mean "so small/little," from the line Kéuih dāk gam daai, jouh dāk dī māt a? "She's so small, what can she do?" This ironic-inversion usage, explicitly sourced to a named media text (a Cantonese soap opera), matches the book's stated methodology of drawing authentic examples from contemporary media and is a genuinely new finding beyond citation-tone-only beginner material.

A separate, mechanically distinct process is **changed tone arising from contraction**, triggered when yāt "one" fuses with a following reduplicated classifier or verb: sí yāt sí → sá-sí "have a try," yāt go yāt go → yāt gó-go "one at a time," yāt deui yāt deui → yāt déui-deui "one pair at a time," leng yāt leng → léng-leng "occasionally." Separately, the perfective aspect marker jó can be elided in casual speech, with its presence preserved only as vowel lengthening plus a rising tone on the preceding verb (Fong-jó ga meih a? → Fóng' ga meih a? "Have you started your holiday yet?") — a compact morphophonological economy phenomenon. Exercises 3.1-3.3 (the last of which begins on p. 19, the final page of this range) drill applying obligatory vs. optional tone change to new vocabulary and distinguishing classifier/noun tone pairs.

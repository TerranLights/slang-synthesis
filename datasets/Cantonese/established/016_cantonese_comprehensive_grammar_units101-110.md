## Overview

This range closes out the adverbial-constructions chapter and opens the aspect chapter, so it is unusually dense in function words and grammatical idiom. The *gám*/*gam* material (10.1.2(b)-(c), 10.1.3) covers pleonastic clause-final *gám*, the idiom pair *jauh gám* "just, simply" and *haih gám* "that's all" (used to close a conversation, cited as attested in a television drama), and a cluster of *gam*-adverb idioms built on verbs of disorder or intensity — *lyuhn/lyún gam* "in a random/disorganized manner" (cognate with Mandarin *luàn jiǎng* "talk nonsense," gam optional), *máahng gam* "do something like crazy," and *kòhng* "crazy" used adverbially with a similar intensifying sense. Reduplicated adverbs are then treated systematically: the *-déi* suffix pattern (*tāu-tāu-déi* "stealing stealthily"), bare reduplication of certain adjectives with an associated tone change (*maahn* + *maahn* → *maahn-máan*, "gradually/slowly," contrasted with *tāu-tāu-déi* where no tone change occurs because *tāu* already carries high tone), reduplication plus *gám* (*sai-sai sēng gám* "in a quiet voice"), AABB reduplication for emphatic suggestions/instructions (*faai-faai cheui-cheui* "hurry up and finish"), and a set of highly idiomatic AAB-pattern reduplicated idioms used specifically as adverbs (*jihng-jíng-gāi* "quietly," *làh-lá-sēng* "noisily/in a rush," *kàhm-kám-chēng* "in a rush"). These AAB idioms are a linguistically striking feature: they are lexically fixed triplet forms (not productively generated) where the final syllable functions almost like an expressive/ideophonic tag.

Section 10.2 extends adjectival comparison machinery to adverbs: the basic *gwo*-comparative combines with the *dāk* adverbial marker (*waahk dāk hóu gwo ngóh* "paints better than I do"); negative/interrogative comparison uses *móuh*/*yáuh-móuh* followed by *gam* + adjective, with the negator preceding the verb; the comparative use of *dī* applies when the standard of comparison is omitted; excessive constructions combine *taai (gwo)* and *dāk jaih* with *dāk*; the superlative *jeui*/*ji* applies to adverb constructions, often with subject-verb phrase order inverted; the correlative construction *yuht...yuht...* ("the more...the more...") may follow the *dāk*-construction or flank the verb directly; and a boxed note covers the comparative idioms *jeuhn* "as...as possible" (where the whole adverbial phrase precedes the verb, between subject and verb) and *jeuhn-leuhng* "to the best of one's ability," used in offers of help. *Gwo* can also compare two full clauses with respect to an adverb, including in the fixed idiom *ngóh sihk yìhm dō gwo léih sihk máih* ("I've eaten more salt than you've eaten rice" — i.e., "I've seen more than you ever will"), glossed by the authors as parallel to the English idiom about having "had more hot dinners."

Section 10.3 defines sentence adverbs (modifying the whole sentence, as against verbal/manner adverbs) and their three possible positions: between subject/topic and verb (the default, e.g. *waahkjé* "perhaps," *hóu chói* "fortunately"), sentence-initial (*hóu chói ngóhdeih jóu dī làih* "lucky we came early"), and sentence-final as an afterthought (*gójahnsí* "then, at that time," *dōsou* "probably") — this last option explicitly restricted to casual conversation. *Waahngdihm* "anyway" patterns similarly. *Jihnghaih*/*jíhaih* "only, just" commonly co-occurs with the sentence particle *je*; *dāk* also means "only" but combines with noun phrases rather than verb phrases, a useful minimal-pair distinction. Adverbs of quantity (10.3.2) cover *dōu* in its adverbial senses "also/too" and, especially in negative sentences, "anyway" (*ngóhdeih dōu lám-jyuh jáu ge la* "we were going to leave anyway"), plus *gau* "enough" used similarly to "so am I" / "so do I." Adverbs of time (10.3.3) are organized by past (*búnlòih* "originally," *yíhchìhn* "before, formerly," *gójahnsih/gójahnsí* "then, at that time," *chō-chō*/*chō tàuh*/*hōi tàuh* "at first," *bātlāu* "until now, always," obligatorily paired with *dōu*), present (*yìhgā*/*gāján* "now," *juhng*/*juhng haih* "still," the more formal *yìhngyìhn* "still"), the medial *jaahmsìh* "for the time being," and future (*dousìh* "by that time," *gānjyuh* "next," *yāt-jahn-gāan* "in a moment," *yàuh yìhgā hōichí* "from now on," *daih yih sìh*, commonly reduced to *daih sìh*, "in future"). The time-adverb *sīn* "first of all" is distinguished from the homophone-adjacent *sin*/*sīnji*/*ji* "only, not until," which also has a general emphatic/contrastive function beyond time expressions and doubles as a sentence particle elsewhere in the book (18.3.5) — the authors flag this as a case where the same surface form spans a time-adverb reading and a focusing-particle reading.

Section 10.3.4, adverbs of frequency, is built from the general quantification machinery (*dōu*, *múih* "each," reduplication) applied to time words: *tūngsèuhng*/*gīngsèuhng* "usually"; *sèhngyaht (dōu)* "always," explicitly noted as frequently used hyperbolically rather than literally (a register-relevant finding — a nominally absolute frequency adverb regularly functions as an exaggeration marker in complaint-type speech acts); *hóu síu* "rarely"; reduplicated time expressions (*lìhn-lìhn*, *yaht-yaht*, *chi-chi*, all with *dōu*); and the productive *múih* + time-word series (*múih chi*, *múih yaht*, *múih go láihbaai*, *múih go yuht*, *múih lìhn*, plus numeral-modified forms like *múih sāam chi* "every third time," *múih léuhng yaht* "every two days," *múih léuhng go láihbaai* "every two weeks," *múih géi go yuht* "every few months," *múih bun lìhn* "every six months," and *múih gaak yāt lìhn* "every other year" — note the *gaak* "skip" morpheme marking alternation). Section 10.4's adverbial phrases cover *chìhn* "ago" (flexible position relative to the time expression) versus *hauh* "later" (fixed, always following); *dahkdāng* "specially/deliberately" versus its converse *m-gokyi* "unintentionally"; a boxed idiom cluster around *pàaih*/*páai* "period of time" — *chìhn yāt pàaih/páai* "a while ago," *nī pàaih/páai* "these days," *yáuh yāt pàaih* "it's been a while" (used with *móuh* for non-occurrence), and *yáuh pàaih*/*yáu pàaih* "it'll be a while" (indefinite future duration); and distributive reduplication with classifiers, *yāt [CL] yāt [CL]* "one by one" (e.g. *yāt fūng yāt fūng*, *yāt daahm yāt daahm*) alongside the synonymous *yāt go gān yāt go*, and the idiom *yāt chi gwo* "all in one go." Section 10.4.1, viewpoint adverbs, covers sentence-opening framing phrases: *góng jān* "to tell you the truth" (confiding an opinion), *...làih góng* and *...làih tái* "as far as...is concerned," and *jiu ngóh tái* "as I see it."

The range then crosses into Chapter 11, "Aspect and verbal particles." The introduction establishes that Cantonese grammar, like Chinese grammar generally, has no tense marking as such; instead a relatively complex aspect system (events, states, processes) interacts with time adverbs to express temporal relations. Aspect is explicitly framed as subjective/viewpoint-based — the same objective situation (rain falling outside) can be described with either the progressive *gán* (presenting it as an ongoing action) or the continuous *jyuh* (presenting it as a continuing state), illustrated with the minimal pair *ngoihbihn lohk-gán yúh* / *ngoihbihn lohk-jyuh yúh*, both translatable as "it's raining outside" in English but differing in aspectual viewpoint in Cantonese. Aspect markers are grammatically optional (omissible), unlike English tense. Table 11.1 maps the six-way aspect system onto Mandarin equivalents: perfective (Cantonese *jó*, Mandarin *le*), experiential (*gwo*, *guò*), progressive (*gán*, *zài* VERB), continuous (*jyuh*, *zhe*), delimitative (*háh*, VERB-*yī*-VERB), and habitual (*hōi*, with the notable gap that Mandarin has no equivalent habitual marker — flagged explicitly by the authors as a genuine Cantonese-specific grammatical category). All Cantonese aspect markers are post-verbal suffixes. The chapter also previews verbal particles closely related to aspect but distinct from it, including the quantifying particles *saai* and *màaih* and the adversative/habitual particle *chān*, covered later (11.3). Section 11.1 then works through the time implications of each marker in the absence of explicit time adverbs: *gán* defaults to present/ongoing reading; *jyuh* denotes a state, typically present or timeless; *gwo* (like the English present perfect) implies the situation occurred prior to the reference point, and combines with past-time adverbs like *chàhnggīng* "previously/before"; *jó* indicates a completed event and normally implies past reference, and combines with recent-past adverbs *ngāam-ngāam* "just" and *tàuhsīn* "just now." Critically, the authors stress that *jó* and *gwo* are NOT past-tense markers in an absolute sense — *jó* appears in imperatives and future-referring complement clauses (*sihk-jó kéuih sīn* "eat it up," *ngóh séung maaih-jó ga chē* "I want to sell the car"), and the temporal meaning of both markers is relative to a reference time rather than absolute, demonstrated with subordinate-clause examples where *gwo* and *jó* actually refer to anticipated future completion relative to a later main-clause event.

## Prose summary

The chapter treats Cantonese utterance-final particles as a large, systematic inventory rather
than a handful of loose "tone words." §18.2.1 covers *topic particles* — particles such as *àh* and
*nē/lē* that attach directly after a fronted topic (followed by an intonation break) to draw
attention to that topic, contrasted with *wo*, which instead flags the *following* clause as
noteworthy. §18.2.2 covers *clause-final particles* that can appear mid-sentence at any natural
prosodic break, not only sentence-finally — the reported-speech particle *wóh*, for instance, can
sit right after a verb of saying, ahead of the quoted clause.

§18.2.3 is the most structurally interesting section: Cantonese particles stack into *combinations*
of up to four (rarely more — the authors cite a report of seven as an extreme outlier), and the
order is not free. The book gives a four-column template (adverbial particle → assertion →
evaluation/modification → question/exclamation) and shows that the meaning of a combination is
compositional — each component particle contributes its own semantic "force" to the stack. Some
particles also phonologically fuse: *ge* + *ā* → *gá*, *ge* + *ak* → *gak/gaak*, *la* + *āma* →
*lāma*, etc., with the fused form's tone derived from combining the component tones (mid + high →
high-rising, for instance). The authors flag that some logically possible combinations are blocked
for pronounceability (*la lē* avoided because the sounds clash) or semantic incoherence (*lèihge
gwa* blocked because *lèihge*'s assertiveness contradicts *gwa*'s uncertainty) — a nice case of a
synthesis-relevant constraint: not every slot-filling combination that the paradigm predicts is
actually grammatical or acceptable to speakers.

§18.3 classifies particles by discourse function: question particles (*a*, *ā*, *àh*, *áh*, *há*,
*hó*, *léh*, *mē*, *nē/lē*, *wá*), assertive particles (*ak*, *ge*/*gé*), imperative/persuasive
particles (*lā*, *la*, *lak/laak*, *lèihga*, *lō*, *lo*, *lok*), epistemic particles (*āma/āmáh*,
*gwa*, *wo*, *wóh/wòh*) — the authors note epistemic/evidential particles are typologically
unusual among Sinitic languages (absent from Mandarin apart from speculative *ba*, which the
authors compare directly to Cantonese *gwa*) but common in Southeast Asian languages generally —
and exclamatory/affective particles (*bo*, *jē*, *jēk*, *sìn*, *tìm*). §18.4 covers interjections
and discourse fillers separate from the particle system proper: the filler *gám* ("so, well..."),
the attention-getting interjection *nàh/làh*, the discourse idiom *léih wah* ("you say" → "you
see?"), the hesitation filler *jīkhaih* (commonly contracted to *jē'eh*, functioning like English
"um... I mean..."), and true exclamations (*āiya* and variants, *wa*, *yí*, *chē*, *hóu yéh*).

Two items are explicitly flagged by the authors as **register/sociolinguistically marked**, which
is directly relevant to this project's slang-synthesis goal: *jēk* is described as having "a highly
affective value" and as "characteristic of children's and younger women's speech," signaling
intimacy and usable only among close acquaintances — i.e., a particle whose selection itself
indexes speaker age/gender and relationship closeness, not just propositional content. *amáh* is
noted to carry "a slight derogatory connotation" in certain fixed phrases. Separately, the panic/
despair idioms *séi la* (literally "die PRT") and *baih la* ("ruined PRT") are worth flagging as
taboo-adjacent intensifiers: the literal lexical item for "die" is bleached into a stock exclamation
of dismay ("oh no, we're in trouble") rather than literal death-reference, a pattern (taboo-root
→ bleached intensifier) this project's `analysis/` layer may want to track as a `taboo_inversion`
or `euphemism_chain` candidate once more Cantonese taboo vocabulary is collected. No tone-sandhi
discussion appears in this particular slice (particle *tone* is discussed only as a lexically
distinctive feature — e.g., *gé* vs. *ge*, *lā* vs. *la* vs. *làh* are separate particles
distinguished purely by tone/contour, not sandhi alternations of one underlying particle).

Chapter 19 opens by noting that Cantonese imperatives, unlike English, are routinely used to make
*requests* (not just commands), which "may seem direct to a Western ear" where English would use a
modal ("Would you...?"); the sentence-final particles — especially *lā* and *ā* — are what soften an
otherwise-bare imperative from abrupt/impolite into a polite request, so the particle system and
the imperative-politeness system are tightly linked.

## Prose summary

This range closes out the chapter on questions and opens the chapter on sentence particles.

The final stretch of §17.1 (replies to questions) establishes that Cantonese answer strategies
diverge sharply from English yes/no. A copula question is answered with the copula alone (*haih*/
*mhaih*, often contracted to *haahk* as a bare affirmative), but an A-not-A question without a
copula is answered by repeating the main verb or modal rather than with *haih* — using *haih* there
would be ungrammatical. Negative questions (containing *mh*, *móuh*, or *meih*) are answered by
confirming or contradicting the *verb*, not by a transposed yes/no the way English negative
questions work; *haih* confirms the negative statement as true, while repeating the verb positively
contradicts it. A small set of idiomatic answer forms round this out: *móuh cho* ("no wrong") is a
double-negative-derived affirmation meaning "right, correct"; *dōu mhaih* functions as a hedge
meaning "not really."

§17.2 covers alternative ("A or B?") questions, formed with the conjunction *dihnghaih* (often
clipped to *dihng*) or the more formal *yīkwaahk*. The book flags an important usage trap: *waahkjé*
and *yāthaih*, which also translate as "or," are reserved for disjunctive statements ("either...
or...") and cannot form alternative questions — a distinction with no English analogue.

§17.3 is the core of this range: the wh-question system, built from four roots — *bīn* "which,"
*māt* "what," *dím* "how," and *géi* "how much." A recurring and linguistically striking pattern is
that nearly every wh-word has secondary, non-interrogative uses (indefinite "whoever/whatever" in
free relatives, "any" under negation or with *dōu*) and a rich layer of colloquial idiom:
*mātyéh* routinely contracts to one syllable, *m'yē* (with the high tone of *māt* transferring onto
the contracted vowel); inserting *mātyéh* into a bisyllabic verb-object phrase is a productive way
to mock or deflate a suggestion ("Sé mātyéh syū a?" — "Write? What book?!"); and *māt gwái (yéh)*
— literally "what devil (-stuff)" — is an expletive "what on earth/the hell," explicitly marked in
the book's own typography with asterisks over the taboo-adjacent "gwái" ("devil/ghost") morpheme.
The same *gwái* "devil" component resurfaces as a rhetorical intensifier in *dím gwái jī* ("how the
devil would I know" = "how should I know"). Several other wh-based idioms are pure rhetorical
question forms with no direct English question equivalent: *bīndouh* used idiomatically means
"since when...?!" (expressing incredulity); *dím tùhng* ("how same?") asserts there's no comparison
to be made; *sái māt* ("need what") is a colloquial rhetorical "what's the point?"; and *[verb/adj]-
sìhng-dím* (also *sèhng dím*) asks how well/successfully something came off, especially common in
indirect questions. The book also notes a formal/colloquial register split directly: *dím gáai* is
the general, more formal "why," while *jouh mātyéh* ("do what?") is explicitly colloquial-register
only and additionally idiomatically means "what's the matter?"

§17.4 (indirect questions) shows Cantonese needs almost no special indirect-question morphology
because questions are formed without subject-verb inversion to begin with; A-not-A, *meih*,
existential, and wh-questions can all be embedded directly. A notable register-driven pattern:
bisyllabic question words reduce in informal/indirect speech (*mātyéh* → *māt*, *bīndouh* → *bīn*,
*dím yéung* → *dím* — but *dím gáai* "why" cannot reduce this way since *dím* alone would be
ambiguous with "how"). The particle *wóh* marks reported-speech indirect questions; *hóu-mhóu*
specifically marks "whether to" indirect questions, while *haih-mhaih* marks "whether (it is the
case that)."

§17.6 (exclamatory questions) is the richest vein of colloquial/affective material in this range:
*mjī géi* ("don't know how") is an intensifying exclamation = "how [adjective]!"; *dím jī* is a
surprise exclamation roughly "what d'you know"; *dím syun* ("what now?") is a despairing rhetorical
question frequently paired with the interjection *āiya*; and *mtūng* ("as if...") introduces two
distinct rhetorical-question functions — sarcastic/sceptical denial (often with particles *alàh*/
*mē*) and speculative "I wonder if." The idiom *séung séi mē?* ("want to die?") was recorded with
the figurative meaning "are you crazy?" — a taboo-adjacent ("death") idiom that has bleached into
ordinary incredulity marking.

The final two pages begin Chapter 18 on sentence particles, previewing that Cantonese has roughly
thirty basic sentence-final particles against Mandarin's seven, that Cantonese particles combine
with each other far more freely than Mandarin's (where only *le* combines), and that tone/vowel-
length variation on particles (short vs. prolonged vowels, tonal variants such as high vs. low
pitch on an otherwise-identical particle) carries real pragmatic meaning — high-tone variants read
as more tentative, low-tone as more assertive, mid-tone as neutral. This is a genuine tone-sandhi-
adjacent phenomenon worth flagging: it's not lexical tone in the ordinary sense but a paralinguistic
tone contrast layered onto grammatical particles, with closure via unreleased final [-k] (*ak*,
*jēk*, *lak*, *lok*) treated as a related-but-distinct variant family from their open counterparts
(*a*, *je*, *la*, *lōllòh*).

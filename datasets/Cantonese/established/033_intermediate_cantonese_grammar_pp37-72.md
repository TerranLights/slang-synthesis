# Intermediate Cantonese: A Grammar and Workbook (Yip & Matthews, Routledge) — pp. 20–55 (Units 3–9)

**Source:** Virginia Yip and Stephen Matthews, *Intermediate Cantonese: A Grammar and Workbook* (London/New York: Routledge). Direct sequel to *Basic Cantonese: A Grammar and Workbook* (already extracted as files 029a–c in this dataset). 217 pages total, image-only PDF (no text layer) — extracted here via vision reading. This file covers PDF pages 37–72, corresponding to printed book pages 20–55: the tail end of Unit 3 (Tone, changed-tone exercises), all of Unit 4 (Reduplication), Unit 5 (Word formation), Unit 6 (Verb–object compounds), Unit 7 (Adjectives and stative verbs), Unit 8 (Classifiers revisited), and the first page of Unit 9 (Topic and focus). No handwritten marginalia were observed anywhere in this page range.

**Romanization:** Yale, consistent with the companion *Basic Cantonese* and the *Comprehensive Grammar*. Tone diacritics render cleanly in this PDF's images (unlike the corrupted-glyph artifact noted for the *Basic Cantonese* text-layer extraction); a handful of syllables in dense exercise blocks were harder to read with full confidence at 150 dpi and are flagged with lower Vision Reading Confidence in the table.

**Reduplication (Unit 4) is the single densest slang-relevant chapter in this page range.** The book treats reduplication as a productive, still-partly-unpredictable word-formation process (explicitly: "intermediate learners should recognize the main patterns... although this can be somewhat hit and miss since the processes of reduplication are not entirely predictable"), which is exactly the kind of open, generative morphological space this project cares about. Four patterns are distinguished: ABB adjectives (an adjective root plus a reduplicated expressive syllable, often with changed tone, e.g. *hāk-māng-māng* "pitch dark," *feih-tàhn-tàhn* "chubby," *táahm-mauh-mauh* "bland/tasteless") that function as vivid predicative/attributive intensifiers; AAB verbs/adverbs that in several cases exist ONLY in reduplicated form (*gáau-gáau-jan* "meddle" — the book explicitly marks the bare root as ungrammatical, `*gáau-jan`); AABB adjectives/adverbs built by doubling each syllable of a disyllabic root (*daaih-fōng* "graceful" → *daaih-daaih-fōng-fōng*); and onomatopoeic expressions, also typically reduplicated with tone change on the second syllable (*mē-mē-sēng* "bleating," *gwàhng-gwáng-sēng* "construction noise"), some of which have drifted to a purely adverbial "manner" sense unrelated to literal sound (*làh-lá-sēng* "quickly," *lōk-lōk-sēng* "fluently"). A short section on baby-talk register is also worth flagging on its own terms: Cantonese baby talk productively reduplicates verb+object pairs used with small children (*yám-láai* "drink milk" → *yám-láai-láai*), and a few expressions (*sihk mūm-mūm* "eat," cat/kitty; *buhk-léi-léi* "play hide and seek") exist only in this register, never in a bare form — a register-restricted vocabulary layer distinct from ordinary colloquial speech.

**Unit 5 (Word formation) supplies the clearest productive slang-morphology material in this range: the -lóu/-pó/-jái/-léui/-mūi suffix family.** The masculine suffix *-lóu* attaches to a verb or descriptive phrase to name a type of man, and the book states directly that it "tends to have a pejorative connotation, referring prototypically to middle-aged, disheveled, rough and rugged-looking men" — its examples range from register-neutral occupational terms (*chāai-lóu* "policeman," *jōngsāu-lóu* "decorator") through clearly derogatory character types (*chīsin-lóu* "crazy guy," *sēui-lóu* "bad guy," *waht-daht-lóu* "gross guy," *jeuijáu-lóu* "drunkard," *hàahmsāp-lóu* "lecherous man") to a slur-adjacent term for a gay man (*gei-lóu*) and a drug-user term (*baahkfán-lóu*, lit. "white-powder guy," i.e., heroin addict) — this whole family is flagged as taboo/slang-register and worth caution in any downstream synthesis that reuses the pattern productively. The parallel feminine suffix *-pó* (also realized with a changed tone as *pó*) is "less widely used" per the book (*chāai-pò* "policewoman," *baat-pò* "busybody," literally "gossip-woman"). A separate, affectively neutral-to-warm diminutive family — *-jái*, *-léui*, *-mūi* — marks familiarity/smallness rather than derogation (*suhkhaak-jái* "familiar/regular customer," *fā-léui* "bridesmaid," *fā-jái* "page boy"), and the same diminutive suffixes attach productively to the ethnonym-adjacent root *gwái* ("ghost/devil," used for Westerners/foreigners) to produce *gwái-lóu* (foreign man), *gwái-pòh* (foreign woman), *gwái-jái* (foreign boy), *gwái-mūi* (foreign girl) — a mildly-to-moderately pejorative ethnic-exonym family that the book presents without comment on its offensiveness, so it is flagged here explicitly as taboo-adjacent/ethnically loaded rather than neutral slang. Unit 5 also covers ordinary nominal compounding (food terms, technology loanword-calques like *yàhngūng jilhahng* "artificial intelligence"), antonym-pair collective nouns (*dō-síu* "amount," lit. "many-few"), and company-name clipping abbreviations (*Góng-daaih* for Hong Kong University, *sáutàih* "mobile phone" as a clipped form of *sáutàih dihnwá*) — the clipping pattern itself (trim a compound to its most distinctive syllable) recurs as a live derivation mechanism worth tracking across languages.

**Unit 5 also documents several productive adjective- and verb-forming templates that function like bound derivational frames rather than individual lexical items**: *tāam-X* "greedy for X" (*tāam-pèhng* "greedy for bargains," *tāam-chín* "avaricious"), *dái-X* "deserving/worthy of X" (*dái-sihk* "worth eating," *dái-séi* "deserving to die" — note the register range from neutral to harsh within one template), *ngāam-X* "right/suited for X" (*ngāam-sihk* "good to eat"), and *X-dāk* "capable of X to a notable degree" (*sihk-dāk* "can eat a lot," *yám-dāk* "can drink a lot"); plus a *tāu-* "steal/surreptitious" verb-forming prefix (*tāu-tái* "peep," *tāu-láahn* "snatch the chance to be lazy," *tāu-gāi(jáu)* "sneak away/shirk") that is productively compositional rather than a closed list — a genuinely useful template for slang synthesis since it reliably derives an "illicit/sneaky version of V" reading from almost any transitive verb.

**Units 6–9 are core-grammar chapters with comparatively little new slang vocabulary, but contain register and idiom notes worth preserving.** Unit 6 (verb–object compounds) documents the generic dummy object *yéh* ("thing/stuff," used with many verbs: *yám-yéh* "have a drink," *góng-yéh* "speak") and notes several verb–object compounds that are synchronically unanalyzable as containing a translatable object (*git-fān* "get married," *tiu-móuh* "dance"). Unit 7 (adjectives and stative verbs) flags that "transitive use of what appear to be adjectives is especially prevalent in colloquial language," illustrated with *hàahmsāp-gwo kéuih* "[he] sexually harassed her" — built on *hàahmsāp*, literally "salty-wet," the base adjective for "lewd/dirty-minded," here verbed transitively in a taboo-adjacent euphemism-to-literal-crime construction worth a dedicated entry. Unit 8 (classifiers revisited) is grammatically dense (classifier-as-definite-article, *dī* as a plural/mass classifier, reduplicated classifiers for "every," nouns pressed into service as collective classifiers, the event-classifier *chèuhng*) but also contains a cluster of idiomatic adjective+classifier compounds that read as established colloquialisms rather than compositional grammar: *daaih-wohk* "big trouble" (lit. "big pot/wok"), *daaih-jái* "big trouble" (lit. "big dose"), *daaih-beih* "snobbish" (lit. "big-nose"), and *daaih-páai* "putting on airs" (lit. "big label") — four semantically opaque idioms sharing the same *daaih* "big" + classifier/noun template, a possible productive metaphor-source worth tracking (bigness → trouble/pretension). The book also notes in passing that "other alternative classifiers appear in slang expressions," pointing forward to Unit 24 (outside this file's range) as the dedicated slang-classifier discussion. Unit 9 (Topic and focus) had barely begun within this page range (only its opening page, on object-topicalization) and is not meaningfully summarized here; its content should be picked up in full by whichever file covers the following pages.

## Overview

This stretch is a full-course grammar/vocabulary textbook rather than a slang dictionary, so most
of the content is register-neutral teaching prose, dialogue scripts, and drill exercises. Even so,
several items are worth flagging for the slang-synthesis project because they illustrate how
Cantonese compresses, euphemizes, or clips standard vocabulary into fixed colloquial chunks.

The clearest find is **hahm-baahng-laahng** (咁唪冷/hahm-baahng-laahng), glossed by the author as
"the strangest word in the Cantonese language" — each of its three syllables is individually
meaningless and the word barely sounds native, yet it is the ordinary way to say "altogether" or
"the whole lot, every last one." This is a textbook case of an opaque, fossilized idiom (comparable
functionally to English "lock, stock, and barrel") that a learner cannot derive compositionally and
must simply memorize as a chunk — exactly the kind of register-shortcut this project tracks. Close
behind it is **waahk-gwái-geuk** ("draw a ghost's leg"), the name of a ladder-diagram game Cantonese
diners use to randomly assign unequal shares of a restaurant bill; it is a culturally specific idiom
built on *gwái* "ghost," not decomposable from its parts.

Several items show euphemistic avoidance or clipped/informal forms layered over standard
vocabulary. **Chek-jih** 赤字 ("red characters," i.e. being in the red/overdrawn) pointedly avoids
the ordinary word for red (huhng) in favor of chek, a character that elsewhere means "naked" or
"bare" — the author notes the same chek appears in **chek-geuk-yī-sāng** 赤腳醫生 ("barefoot
doctors," the minimally-trained rural health workers of the Communist era), suggesting a shared
undertone of poverty/bareness running beneath both uses. **Daahk-sáu** 特首 appears as a clipped,
informal short form of **Hàhng-jing Jéung-gwùn** 行政長官 (the Chief Executive's full title) — a
clean phonological-reduction example, title-clipping under register pressure, tied to the specific
historical date-marker **Wuih-gwai** (the 1997 Handover), which itself has spawned the fixed
collocations wuih-gwai-chihn/wuih-gwai-hauh ("before/after the Handover"). The polite stock phrase
**jaahm-sih meih yáuh** ("temporarily not yet got it") is flagged as a conventionalized soft "no" —
the author explicitly warns learners not to read much hope into it; it functions as a register-level
euphemism for refusal/unavailability in service contexts (post office, presumably generalizable to
shops).

Grammatically colloquial (not slang per se, but everyday-register shortcuts) are the verb-doubled
"quick glance" pattern (chàh-yāt-chàh "run a quick check," tái-yāt-tái "have a peep"), the
reluctant-agreement frame verb-haih-verb-daahn-haih ("X all right, but..."), and the modest-response
formula **pou-tung jē** ("just run of the mill") used to deflect a compliment while, per the
author's note, sometimes carrying a boastful undertone — a register/pragmatics nuance rather than a
lexical one. No taboo vocabulary appears in this range; the crime-dialogue content (Unit 18) stays
at the level of formal/legal terms (murder, rape, robbery, drug use, pickpocketing, imprisonment,
fines) rather than slang terms for those acts.

# Cantonese: A Comprehensive Grammar (Matthews & Yip) — Modality (end) and Negation

Source: *Cantonese: A Comprehensive Grammar*, Stephen Matthews & Virginia Yip. DjVu units 131–140 (confirmed 2 printed pages per rendered unit), covering printed pages 240–259: the tail end of Chapter 12 (Modality, §12.2.2–§12.3.3) and the opening of Chapter 13 (Negation, §13.1–§13.5 heading).

## Modality: adverbs of necessity and syntactic potential constructions (pp. 240–247)

The chapter closes out its treatment of modality with adverbs of necessity — *saht*, *yātdihng*, and *hángdihng* — which reinforce modal verbs like *wúih* and *yiu* to express certainty, and which combine with the copula *haih* to produce an epistemic/inferential "must be the case" reading, including a retrospective "must have been" use parallel to *hóyíh*/*yīnggōi*. *jyuhtdeui* intensifies certainty to "absolutely," while *meih bīt* ("no compulsion," i.e. "not necessarily") is its negative-polarity counterpart, usable alone or combined with a modal verb. A boxed idiom shows *[verb] gáng* / *[verb] ngaahng* "bound to..." expressing inevitability, built on the adjective *ngaahng* "hard, firm."

Section 12.3 turns to syntactic (rather than lexical/adverbial) expressions of modality. The *verb–dāk* construction expresses potential — possibility, permission, and ability — and is explicitly distinguished from the unrelated adverbial *dāk* construction described elsewhere (§10.1.1) despite sharing a character; it is especially common in negative and interrogative form, where it marks inability/impossibility. A boxed set of idioms built on *dāk* follows: *[verb] dāk gwo* meaning something is safe or trustworthy, and *verb–dāk chēut*, which indicates ability but with a negative, almost accusatory connotation ("capable of [doing something bad]"), as in describing triad members as *jouh dāk chēut ge* "capable of doing anything." *dāk* alone can stand as a predicate meaning "okay." A further boxed idiom complex centers on *mh sé dāk*: with a noun object it means "can't do without," with a verb phrase it means "can't face [doing X]," and its positive counterpart *sé dāk* means "willing to part with." The existential verbs *yáuh*/*móuh* (ch. 15) combine with *dāk* + verb to express possibility/impossibility of events arising circumstantially (e.g., whether a table can be reserved, whether a meal is ready), including a question form *yáuh-móuh dāk [verb]?* A boxed idiom pair, *móuh dāk béi* ("no comparison," used with coverb *tùhng*) and *móuh dāk díng*/*móuh dāk tàahn* ("nothing beats..."), round out this cluster. Finally, §12.3.3 covers the verb–(*mh*)–particle construction, the negative mirror of verb–*dāk*–particle, used pervasively for inability (e.g. *gáau mh dihm* "can't manage/handle it" vs. *gáau dāk dihm* "can manage it"), with either word order (particle immediately after *mh*, or object pronoun/short NP inserted before *mh*). A closing boxed idiom gives *yán mh jyuh* + verb, "can't help [doing]." Several examples through this stretch are explicitly flagged by the authors as drawn from television, film, and radio advertisements rather than constructed for the book — a useful provenance signal for genuinely attested (if scripted/broadcast) spoken Cantonese.

## Negation (pp. 248–259, through the opening of §13.5)

Chapter 13 opens by distinguishing **lexical negation** (negating a single word) from **sentential negation** (negating a whole statement), and surveys Cantonese's unusually rich inventory of negative words, almost all native forms beginning with nasal *m-* and low-register tones: *mh* "not," *mhaih* "no/not," *móuh* "have not" (negative of existential *yáuh*), *meih* "not yet," *mhóu*/*máih* "don't" (imperative), and *mjí*/*mh dāan jí* "not only." Notably, Mandarin-derived negatives also surface in Cantonese registers above the colloquial baseline: *bāt* (the Cantonese reading of Mandarin *bù*) appears in fixed expressions borrowed wholesale from Mandarin and is also substituted for *mh* for deliberately literary effect, including in song lyrics; the archaic *fēi* ("not be") likewise survives mainly in fixed compounds (*fēi-faat* "illegal") and formal collocations (*góng sih-fēi* "gossip," literally "talk right-wrong"). This is a clear register/code-contact phenomenon worth flagging: a Cantonese text can reach for Mandarin-sourced negators specifically to signal formality or literariness. Lexically negative prefixed verbs/adjectives (*mgin* "lose," *mgeidāk* "forget," *mhóuyisi* "embarrassed," *msyūfuhk* "uncomfortable/unwell") are shown to behave differently from plain *mh*-negation: their meaning is often narrower/more specific than the compositional negation of the base verb, and — strikingly — they remain compatible with the perfective aspect marker *jó*, which is otherwise categorically incompatible with sentential *mh* negation (since *jó* asserts an event occurred). Adjectival negation (§13.2) uses *mh* preposed to the adjective, with *mhaih* required when the adjective is modified by degree words; only sentential *mh*-negation (not the lexical-prefix type) licenses an indefinite reading of a question word in the same clause. Boxed idioms here include *mh gwaai dāk (jī)* "no wonder" and *mh chēut kèih* "not surprising" (usable rhetorically with final particle *mē* in place of *mh*).

Verbal negation (§13.3) is fundamentally tense-sensitive: *mh* negates present/future-oriented verbs, while *móuh* negates past events that did not occur; with verbal particles, *mh* is inserted directly between verb and particle to yield inability readings (paralleling §12.3.3). The experiential aspect marker *gwo* is compatible with both *móuh* and *meih* (unlike *jó*, which cannot combine with negation at all), and the book works through the *meih*/*móuh* contrast carefully: *meih* ("not yet") implies the event is still expected/possible, while *móuh* carries no such implication — a genuinely fine-grained aspectual-pragmatic distinction. *meih* is reinforced by *juhng* "still" and *dou yìhgā* "up till now," and uniquely forms perfective/experiential questions (*móuh* cannot). *meih...jyuh* / *mhóu...jyuh* ("not yet," imperative) and the idiom *meih chih* "not too late" are both documented. *chùhnglòih* "never" reinforces *móuh*/*meih* with experiential *gwo* for past reference, while future "never" instead uses *yìhhauh dōu* or *wíhngyúhn dōu* with *mh wúih*; *mh joi* "no more" combines with *wíhngyúhn* for "never again." The *mjí* "not only" family gets substantial treatment: bare *mjí* linking two clauses, *mjí...juhng* completed by verbal particle *màaih* and/or sentence particle *tìm*, the near-synonymous *mh dāan jí*/*bāt dáan jí*, the more formal *mjí...yìhché*, and *mjí* used alone as a terse rhetorical response ("there's more to it than that"). The *mh* vs. *mhaih* distinction is then drawn out: *mhaih* corrects a false impression/error, uniquely among the negators tolerates perfective *jó*, and is obligatory with quantified phrases; the boxed idiom *mhaih wah* "it's not as if..." typically appears inside a double-negative construction with a *bātgwo* ("but") clause (double negatives being flagged as the next topic, §13.5). *mhóu* and *máih* both form negative imperatives, but the book notes an explicit register distinction: *mhóu* is the default/neutral form, while *máih* is more direct and more likely used specifically among close friends or peers — a politeness/register-conditioned lexical choice rather than free variation. A parallel near-homophone distinction is drawn between *móuh* (negative existential) and *mòuh* (low falling tone; the Cantonese reading of Mandarin *wú* "without"), with the book noting that *mòuh* is formal/literary and tends to displace *móuh* in formal and written Cantonese — another instance of a Mandarin-sourced form colonizing a higher register, seen in the idioms *mòuhleuhn* "no matter, whether," *mòuhwaih* "no need/use," and *mòuh-dyūn-dyūn* "for no reason." Section 13.4, Indefinite Negation ("not...any"), contrasts two structures built from a question word plus a negator: a less emphatic form (negator + question word) versus a more emphatic, obligatory-for-subject-position form (question word + quantifier *dōu* + negator), illustrated with *bīndouh*, *bīngo*, *mātyéh*, and *dím yéung*. Related idioms close out the range: *mh*/*móuh...mātyéh* "hardly any," *móuh māt dím* "hardly (at all)," and *dím dōu mh* "no matter what, won't..." The range ends exactly at the §13.5 "Double Negatives" header, whose content (double-negation as an indirect/face-saving rhetorical strategy) falls just outside this unit range.

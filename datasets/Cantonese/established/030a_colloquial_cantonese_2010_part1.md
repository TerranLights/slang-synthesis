# Colloquial Cantonese (2010 edition, Routledge) — Part 1: Introduction and Units 1–5

**Source:** Dana Scott Bourgerie, Keith S.T. Tong, and Gregory James. *Colloquial Cantonese: The Complete Course for Beginners*, 2nd edition (Routledge, 2010). First edition (1994) by Tong and James; this 2010 edition is the primary, later edition and supersedes the 1994 edition, which is handled separately in a later extraction wave. Romanization: Yale system. Extraction method: `pdftotext -layout` against a clean embedded text layer (305 pages).

This course is organized as 15 topical units, each with dialogues, a vocabulary list, an "Idioms and structures" grammar-notes section, exercises, a "Recognizing Chinese characters" mini-section, communicative activities, and a closing "Cultural point." The book explicitly aims for "a neutral style in speech, appropriate to a wide range of social and professional situations," but flags formal/informal distinctions whenever they matter — which is exactly the kind of callout this extraction prioritizes.

## Introduction: Cantonese as a language, grammar, vocabulary, and sound system

The introduction frames Cantonese as a regional standard rather than a mere "dialect" of Chinese — it has its own vernacular literature, non-traditional written characters for colloquialisms, and prestige comparable only to Mandarin among Chinese varieties. The authors note that Cantonese colloquial writing is disapproved of in mainland China's formal standards but thrives in the Hong Kong press. The "Colloquial Cantonese" taught in this book is explicitly identified as the standard form spoken in Hong Kong.

Grammatically, the introduction stresses that Cantonese verbs do not conjugate and nouns/adjectives do not decline; meaning is instead carried by strict word order, a system of shape/size/function-based classifiers, and a rich inventory of sentence-final particles expressing mood, emotion, and attitude. It calls out concrete Cantonese–Mandarin divergences: Cantonese places the direct object before the indirect object (opposite of Mandarin), and some adverbs that precede the verb in Mandarin follow it in Cantonese.

On vocabulary, the book notes Cantonese is largely but not strictly monosyllabic-per-morpheme, with some "bound" syllables (often from foreign borrowing) that carry no meaning alone. Several everyday Cantonese words are archaic/literary in Mandarin (mihn "face," hàahng "walk," sihk "eat," wah "say"), and a handful of words have shifted or reversed meaning between the two varieties (ek "house" in Cantonese vs. "room" in Mandarin; fòhng "room" in Cantonese vs. "house" in Mandarin). English loanwords are also highlighted as a distinctive feature of modern Cantonese vocabulary.

The sound-system section introduces the Yale romanization, the consonant/vowel/diphthong inventory, and the six-tone system (high level, mid level, low level, high rising, low rising, low falling), with notes on sociolinguistic variation already underway in Hong Kong speech: younger speakers merging initial n- into l-, variable pronunciation of initial ng-, and softening of initial gw- toward g-. The book advises learners to follow the transcription regardless of this variation.

## Unit 1 — Gaaujai (Meeting people)

Unit 1 teaches introductions and greetings: stating one's name and nationality, asking where someone is from, naming languages spoken, and asking surnames politely. Grammatically it introduces the copula haih, choice-type (A-not-A) yes/no questions, question-word placement in situ (not fronted, as in English), and negation with the mh- prefix.

A recurring theme is the register layering of politeness: néih hóu is a semi-formal universal greeting; gwai sing ("your honorable surname") and chéhng mahn ("may I ask...") raise formality further, while síu sing ("my humble surname") is the self-deprecating counterpart. The book also draws a three-way distinction among mán (language, spoken+written, implies literacy), wá (spoken-only variety, used for languages Cantonese speakers won't read/write, e.g., Fbileuhtbanwá "Filipino languages," Yandouhwá "Indian languages," Bagbisctáanwá "Pakistani languages"), and yúh (more formal/general term for language, obligatory in Gwokyúh "Mandarin/national language").

The naming-conventions sidebar explains that many urban, educated Hong Kong Cantonese speakers adopt English given names while retaining Chinese names from birth; using a Chinese person's bare given name is markedly more intimate than using an English given name.

## Unit 2 — Máaih-yéh (Shopping)

Unit 2 covers market shopping: asking prices, specifying quantities by item or weight, numbers 1–100, classifiers, and the two Cantonese words for "thank you." Grammatically it introduces the indefinite pronoun/determiner dc ("some"/"the"), sentence-final particles (la for cordial invitation or affirmation), personal pronouns, and topic-comment sentence structure, which the book treats as basic to Cantonese syntax (no verb required to link topic and comment).

The clearest register note in this unit is the ddjeh/mhgdi distinction: ddjeh thanks someone for a gift or payment received, mhgdi thanks someone for a service or favor. The book adds a colloquial-register aside: among friends (not in a service transaction), a more colloquial way to thank someone is to self-deprecatingly claim you troubled them — Màhfàhn néih! ("I troubled you!") — to which the polite reply is móuh màhfàhn ("no trouble").

## Unit 3 — Sihou (Interests and leisure activities)

Unit 3 covers hobbies/leisure vocabulary (all formed as verb-object constructions, e.g., tái-sye "read," tbng-yamngohk "listen to music"), the verbal/existential uses of yáuh ("have"/"there is"), the locative/prepositional verb hái, and degree adverbs for expressing like/dislike (hóu "very much," géi "quite," màh-má-déi "so-so," mhaih géi "not that much"). It also covers days of the week and frequency expressions (géinoih...yat chi "how often," fùhng + day "every [day]").

This unit contains the book's single most explicit register callout in the early units: jengyi and héifen both mean "to like," but the book states directly that héifen "ranks higher on the level of formality and is also used in Mandarin as well as in written Chinese, while jengyi is only used in colloquial Cantonese" — and notes jengyi is the one actually used more often in daily conversation. This is a clear colloquial-vs-formal/written pair worth flagging for the slang-synthesis project. The Cultural point section closes the unit with a rundown of traditional lunar-calendar holidays (Spring Festival, Ching Ming, Dragon Boat, Mid-Autumn, Chung Yeung).

## Unit 4 — Sìhgaan (Telling the time)

Unit 4 covers clock time, daily-routine vocabulary, and TV-program types, plus the modal yiu ("must/have to," distinguished from yiu "to want"). It introduces Cantonese-specific time-telling conventions absent from Mandarin: gwat (quarter-hour unit, explicitly flagged as "becoming increasingly uncommon among younger speakers in Hong Kong") and jih (a Cantonese-specific five-minute clock-face unit, with go jih often dropped entirely in colloquial speech, e.g., baat dím chat "8:35," lit. "eight o'clock seven [jih]"). The written-Chinese equivalents (sìh for "o'clock," haak for "quarter-hour") are explicitly contrasted with the colloquial spoken terms (dímjeng, gwat) in the "Recognizing Chinese characters" section — a clean colloquial/written register pair.

The unit also teaches the sentence-final particle la (changed status, e.g., "I have to leave now") and the verbal particle dak for possibility/permission ("can be seen"). A brief Cultural point covers the history of Chinese water clocks.

## Unit 5 — Ngoihbíu (Physical appearances)

Unit 5 covers describing age and appearance, pointing out people/objects with demonstratives (nc go "this," gó go "that"), kinship terms, and possessive constructions. The standout colloquial-grammar feature here is the "adjective + reduplicated-adjective (tone-shifted) + déi" intensity pattern (e.g., sau-sáu-déi "kind of thin," fèih-féi-déi "kind of fat," lóuh-lóu-déi "kind of old") — the book states explicitly this pattern "is common in colloquial Cantonese," and spells out the tone-change rule (the reduplicated syllable takes high-rising tone unless the base adjective is already high-level/high-rising).

Colloquial number-contraction is also flagged directly: two-syllable decade numbers (yih-sahp, saam-sahp, etc.) are elided in natural speech to y'ah, sa'ah, sei'ah, and so on (y'ah seui "20 years old"), with the book noting these are specifically "contracted ... in natural speech." Kinship terms are introduced with a cultural note on fictive-kinship address (calling an unrelated young woman jèhjb "older sister," an unrelated middle-aged man seksuk "uncle," etc.) and a note that Chinese speakers' direct remarks about a person's weight or features are "not always intended as rude."

# Intermediate Cantonese: A Grammar and Workbook (Yip & Matthews, Routledge) — pp. 128–163

**Source:** Virginia Yip and Stephen Matthews, *Intermediate Cantonese: A Grammar and Workbook* (London/New York: Routledge). Direct sequel to *Basic Cantonese: A Grammar and Workbook* (already extracted as files 029a–c). Image-only PDF (no text layer, 217 pages total); this file covers PDF pages 145–180, which correspond to printed pages 128–163 (the tail of Unit 19 through Unit 23). Vision-read page by page via rendered PNGs (150 dpi); no handwritten marginalia was present in this range.

**Scope of this file:** the back half of Unit 19 (Clauses: causal, temporal, purpose, and concessive conjunctions), all of Unit 20 (Conditional sentences), all of Unit 21 (Reported speech), all of Unit 22 (Cantonese speech conventions — greetings, address terms, kinship, naming, taboo words), and the first two-thirds of Unit 23 (Particles and interjections). Exercise pages (134, and the numbered exercise blocks within Units 20–23) were read for coverage but contribute no new vocabulary beyond what's already captured from the preceding prose; they are not separately summarized.

**Conjunction-pair system (Unit 19 tail):** this range confirms that Cantonese organizes subordinate-clause logic almost entirely through paired conjunctions — a marker opening the subordinate clause matched by a second marker opening the main clause (yānwaih ... sóyíh "because ... therefore," sēuiyìhn ... daauhnaih "although ... but," wàahngdihm ... bātyùh "since (anyway) ... might as well"). The book is explicit that dropping the second member of a pair makes a sentence "sound strange," which is a stronger grammaticalization of the pairing than English "because... (so)" shows. Three near-synonyms for "since" in a causal sense are distinguished by register and nuance: wàahngdihm (colloquial, implies resignation to a state of affairs), geiyìhn (more formal), and jihchùhng ... jíhauh (temporal "ever since," not to be confused with the causal pair). Time clauses are formed with a closing-position conjunction after the clause (gójahnsíh/gójahu "when," jíhauh "after," jíchìhn "before") rather than an opening one — the reverse of English word order, and consistent with the project's broader note that Cantonese serial/subordinate clauses put circumstance before result. Purpose is expressed either by bare serial verbs (wán dī yéh wai máau "find food to feed the cat") or, in more formal registers, by the coverb waih-jó "for the sake of" opening a clause — a register distinction (bare serial vs. waih-jó) worth flagging for any slang-synthesis work on formality gradients.

**Conditionals (Unit 20) are a genuinely new intermediate-level system layered on the causal pairs.** The default is yùhgwó ... jauh ("if ... then"), with either half omittable in colloquial speech, and omittable entirely when the clause is already understood as conditional from context (léih mh tùhng ngóh heui, ngóh mh fong-sām "if you don't go with me, I won't be at ease" — no overt conjunction at all). A clause-final ge wah/ge wá particle (paralleling Mandarin de huà) can make the "if" explicit without a fronted conjunction. Several specialized conditional subtypes are introduced here for the first time in the series and are worth treating as distinct grammatical items rather than mere synonyms of yùhgwó: sin(ji)/sínji "only if" (necessary condition, placed after the subject, interchangeable with jí in most contexts), jíyiu "as long as" (sufficient condition), jauhsyun ... dōu "even if ... still" (concessive-conditional, blending Unit 19's concessive dōu with a conditional frame), and chèuihfei "unless," which can stand alone or be reinforced by yùhgwó mhaih "if not" in the main clause for extra explicitness. gáyuh and gáchit "supposing" are flagged as containing the morpheme gá "false," marking them specifically for hypothetical/counterfactual reasoning rather than real conditions — a transparent etymological link between "falseness" and "supposition" that's worth noting for any derivation-mechanism work on hedging vocabulary. The yuht ... yuht "the more ... the more" correlative construction, and its simplex cousin yuht làih yuht "more and more" (usable with any single adjective), round out the unit; both are implicit conditionals with no yùhgwó/jauh needed at all.

**Reported speech (Unit 21) hinges on two sentence-final particles, wo and wóh, that mark epistemic source rather than tense/mood** (Cantonese reported speech otherwise just embeds the original statement directly after wah "say," with no complementizer equivalent to English "that"). wo flags content as newsworthy ("she's had a daughter! [surprisingly]"); wóh marks it as explicitly second-hand/hearsay ("according to what someone has said"). This is a real grammaticalized evidentiality distinction, not just a stylistic particle choice, and is worth flagging for cross-linguistic evidentiality comparison alongside the project's existing is/gwa uncertainty-marking notes from Unit 23. Verbs of thinking pattern like verbs of saying for embedding purposes, but carry their own truth-value nuance: lám is neutral "think," séungseun is "believe," and yíhwaih specifically implies the belief turns out to be mistaken or false — a built-in hedge against the speaker's own prior assumption that has no single-word English equivalent. Indirect questions simply juxtapose the asking verb (mahn) with the original question minus its question particle a; indirect commands use giu "tell" as the default reporting verb (plus hyun "urge," mihnglihng "order," gínggou "warn," gin-yih "suggest" for finer shades of directive force), and critically retain mhóu "don't" in negative reported commands even though the imperative particles (a, lā) that would normally accompany a direct command are dropped.

**Unit 22, "Cantonese speech conventions," is the richest register/pragmatics material in this range and the most directly relevant to the project's slang-synthesis goals.** The book explicitly debunks léih hóu ma? as a textbook-only greeting rarely used in practice, in favor of a small set of genuinely idiomatic greetings built on indirect, situation-based observations (hóu loih móuh gin "long time no see"; sihk-jó faahn meih a?, literally "have you eaten yet," functioning as a phatic greeting with no literal content expected in the answer) — a clear case of a textbook register clashing with actual usage, the same pattern already flagged for néih/léih in the Basic Cantonese extraction. English-derived loan greetings (hā-lóu, báai-baai) are noted as especially prevalent in Hong Kong specifically, alongside the formal joi-gin "goodbye." Kinship terms are shown to encode two independent axes simultaneously — relative age (elder/younger) and parental line (paternal/maternal) — producing systematically different terms for what English collapses into one word (aunt splits four ways: gū-mā/gū-jé on the father's side vs. yih-mā/a-yì on the mother's side, by age), and the book notes this same term set extends metaphorically to address unrelated strangers by apparent age and gender (a-baak "sir" for older men, a-pòh "madam" for older women). A notable semantic-shift item: tùhng-ji ("comrade," the Communist-era term) is listed here as the in-use address term for gay people — a reclaimed/repurposed political term that is exactly the kind of semantic_shift derivation this project's taxonomy is built to track, and a strong candidate for deeper analysis-layer treatment later. Naming conventions cover the familiarity-marking a- prefix (with an accompanying tone change on the following syllable — a sandhi-for-pragmatics link worth cross-referencing against the Unit 3 tone-sandhi material already flagged in Basic Cantonese), epithets built on lóuh- "old" (Lóuh-Léi), and the diminutive-like familiarity suffixes -jái (male) and -léui (female) attached to given names of public figures. Occupational address terms (sigei "driver," sifu "master/craftsman, implies expertise," lóuhbáan/lóuhsai/bósí "boss") include the notable polysemy of daaihlóu, which serves both as a term of address for a boss and as a free-standing interjection — worth flagging as a possible slang-origin item given daaihlóu's broader currency in Cantonese media as a term for a gang leader/"big brother" figure, though that connotation is not stated explicitly in this book. Finally, the taboo-word section is a clean, compact euphemism-chain case study: sei "four" is avoided for its near-homophony with séi "die," and a cluster of words resembling hūng "empty" (associated with ominous hazard/havoc) are systematically replaced by auspicious substitutes — hūng láu "empty flat" → gāt láu, hūng-sām choi "water spinach" → tūng choi, fú-gwā "bitter gourd" (fú also means "suffer") → leuhng gwā "cooling melon," sit "tongue" (resembles sawt "lose money") → ngàuh leih "ox tongue," and yám gōn "drink up" (gōn "dry" evokes money "drying up") → yám sīng. The avoidance strategy generalizes productively: gāt is extended to gāt chē "empty taxi" and gāt gēi "empty plane," which the book flags as commonly used by estate agents for "the house has been vacant" — a nice example of a superstition-driven euphemism becoming ordinary professional-register vocabulary.

**Unit 23 (particles and interjections, read through p. 163) opens up Cantonese's large sentence-final particle inventory**, which the book frames as doing grammaticalized work that intonation does in English — not decorative, but carrying real semantic/pragmatic content (evidentiality, certainty, rhetorical stance). Within this range: lō marks an obvious/expected conclusion and combines productively with maih ("then, obviously") for stronger obviousness; āma draws attention to something the hearer should already know, used especially in correcting or teaching; gwa marks explicit uncertainty/guesswork (haih gwa "I guess so," mhaih gwa "I guess not," contrasting directly with lō/āma's certainty); há and hó are question-tag particles inviting agreement, with há typically following instructions ("OK?") and hó checking a stated fact ("isn't that right?"). A systematic contraction process fuses particles beginning in a vowel with a preceding particle (ge+a→ga, ge+āma→gāma, je+a→ja, etc.), and ge itself has two further tonal variants beyond its possessive/assertive base use: gé for puzzlement and gá for a reservation/hedging stance, with gá dāk gá marking an even stronger reservation. Particles also combine compositionally (ge+la→ge la "assertion+relevance," ge+mē→ge mē→(reduced) le mē "assertion+query," with the schwa-reduction of a full vowel to [ə] in rapid combined forms noted explicitly as a phonological process). The interjection set here (hóu yeh "great," wa(háai) "wow," aiya "oh no," sei la/baai la "uh-oh," yí "huh? [surprise+puzzlement]," chē "so what [belittling]," oh "ah-ha [realization]") is rounded out by chói, glossed as specifically used by women to verbally neutralize an inauspicious remark someone else has just made (comparable to "touch wood")  — a gendered pragmatic-particle finding worth flagging for register/sociolinguistic cross-referencing, since it's the first explicitly gender-marked usage pattern encountered in this book's particle inventory.

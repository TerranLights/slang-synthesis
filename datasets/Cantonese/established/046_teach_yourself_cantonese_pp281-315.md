## Overview

The exercise-key portion is almost entirely terse single-line answers to fill-in/translation
drills, which carry little independent content, but it also preserves two full narrative
"passages" built around puns, plus several idioms given with explicit English translations —
these are the richest finds in this slice. **Passage 2 of Unit 14** ("Mr Ho bets on the horses")
turns on **máaih-máh** ("to buy a horse"), a fixed Hong Kong colloquialism where "buying a horse"
idiomatically means "betting on a horse" — poor punters supposedly used the euphemism to disguise a
gambling habit as a purchase. The book treats this as common enough that native speakers immediately
parse "I think I'll buy a horse today" as "I'm going to the races," a clean case of an ordinary verb
phrase being repurposed wholesale into vice-register slang. **Exercise 8** right after it extends
the same joke with **gáu-chèuhng**, which puns on "dog track" (the venue) against "nine races" (gáu
"dog/nine" homophony), exploiting the fact that Cantonese tone-driven homophony is a productive
source of wordplay — the son in the joke mishears the pun in the direction he wants to hear (his
father winning), which the book flags explicitly as the mechanism of the joke.

Two further idioms are worth flagging. **Séung-làih-séung-heui**, literally "think coming think
going," is given explicitly as the idiom for "to rack your brains, to think and think" — a
reduplicated-direction-verb pattern turned into a fixed mental-effort idiom. And the glossary later
lists **hóu-noih-móuh-gin**, "long time no see," confirming it is lexicalized as a fixed greeting
phrase rather than a live compositional sentence. Two short narrative "Passages" in the Unit 21/26
answer key (an old horse that finds its way home on its own, and a son who thinks "more legs
means faster" and walks a horse instead of riding it; a painter whose dragon painting literally
flies away once he adds its eyes; a rural visitor who mistakes a hotel lift for a tiny room and
wants to "bring his wife next time") are classic didactic folk-tale/joke material — told in English
translation in the key, not reproduced at length here, but worth noting as the kind of
culturally-loaded anecdote this course uses to carry proverb-adjacent content (the horse story in
particular visibly echoes the classic Chinese idiom "an old horse knows the way," though the book
never names the idiom itself, just narrates the plot).

The alphabetical glossary (pp.283-300 of this slice) is overwhelmingly register-neutral core
vocabulary (numbers, compass points, family terms, classifiers, everyday nouns) not worth
transcribing exhaustively here. Three clusters inside it stand out for slang-synthesis purposes.
First, a coherent **gambling-vocabulary field** built almost entirely on the root **dóu** "to
gamble, bet" (dóu-bō football betting, dóu-chèuhng casino, dóu-gáu dog-race betting, dóu-máh
horse-race betting, dóu-pē-páai card gambling, dóu-gú-piu share speculation, dóu-ngoih-wuih foreign
exchange speculation, dóu-chín gambling with money) sits directly adjacent in the dictionary to the
máaih-máh/gáu-chèuhng pun cluster from the exercise key, confirming gambling is a dense, systematic
semantic field in this book rather than a one-off joke. Second, a **crime/law-and-order cluster**
(dá-gāau "fight," dá-gip "rob," dá-hòh-bāau "pick pockets/purse-snatch," fáahn-duhk "peddle drugs,"
kāp-duhk "take drugs," kèuhng-gàan "rape," chóh-gāam "do time in prison," làai "arrest," jeuih
"crime," bìhn-fuhk/bìhn-yì "plain clothes [police]") largely overlaps the Unit 18 crime vocabulary
already catalogued from the main-text portion of this book in file `043_...pp176-210.md` — it
reappears here simply because this is the back-of-book glossary indexing the same unit, and the
register stays formal/legal rather than slangy, except for the verb-object idioms dá-gip and
dá-hòh-bāau which read as mildly more colloquial than the Latinate-feeling formal terms around them
(màuh-saat "murder," kèuhng-gàan "rape"). Third, the glossary's scattered **sentence-final-particle
(fp) entries** are a useful discourse-register cluster on their own: a? (closes a question) vs. a?
(triumphant "scoring" tone — same romanization, different pragmatic function), àh? ("that's right,
isn't it?"), ā-ma! ("you should realize [this]"), bo! ("let me tell you"), ge-la (strong emphasis),
jē/jēk ("only, and that's all" — two spellings of the same clitic), jī-máh ("only"), la ("that's how
the case stands now"), and lā ("urging agreement/co-operation"). The headword **ge** alone is listed
with three separate glossed functions — possessive marker ("-'s"), adjective-to-noun linker, and a
sentence-final "that's how it is" assertive particle — a clean illustration of a single Cantonese
particle sitting at three different points along a grammaticalization cline from content-adjacent
marker to pure discourse particle.

A small number of resultative-pattern items (gú-dāk-dóu/gú-mh-dóu "able/unable to guess,"
daap-dāk-dóu/daap-mh-dóu "able/unable to catch") extend the same -dāk-dóu/-mh-dóu "can/can't
achieve X" template already documented from `wán-dāk-dóu/wán-mh-dóu` in file 043, confirming it is
productive across multiple verb roots rather than a one-off lexicalization. No new taboo/obscene
vocabulary turns up in this slice; the one bodily-taboo-adjacent item is **áu/jok-áu** "to
vomit/retch," listed plainly with no euphemism marker.

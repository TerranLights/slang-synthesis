# Cantonese: A Comprehensive Grammar (Matthews & Yip) — Source pages 24-43

**Source:** Matthews, Stephen & Yip, Virginia. *Cantonese: A Comprehensive Grammar.* DJVU render units 21-30 (confirmed density: 2 printed pages per unit). Covers the tail of Chapter 1 (Phonology, sections 1.4.2-1.5) and the opening of Chapter 2 (Word Structure: Morphology and Word Formation, sections 2.1.1-2.1.3).

## Prose summary

The phonology portion closes out the book's treatment of **tone change** (a form of tone sandhi where a syllable's lexical tone is replaced by a changed tone, usually high rising, for morphological/pragmatic reasons rather than purely phonological assimilation). Several distinct triggers are catalogued: contraction of reduplicated *yāt* 'one' in phrases like `[verb] yāt [verb]` and `[adj] yāt [adj]` (e.g. *tàih yāt tàih* → *tái-tàih* 'mention a bit'); obligatory tone change on reduplicated kinship terms of address (*bā-bā* → *bàh-bā* 'father', *gō-gō* → *gòh-gō* 'elder brother') and on familiar name/surname forms with the *a-* prefix or *lóuh-* 'old' epithet; and tone change marking **lexicalization** of a compound, i.e. signaling that a word sequence has become a fixed, often semantically non-compositional unit (compared explicitly to English stress-shift in *black bird* vs. *blackbird*). A recurring register note: changed-tone forms are the normal, often obligatory, colloquial pronunciation but revert to the original tone when the same written word is read aloud formally — a live diglossic marker worth flagging for any register-tier modeling. The chapter then covers **tone and intonation**: declination (natural pitch droop across an utterance, which does not threaten tone perception because tones are relative, not absolute), and a few recognizable sentence-level intonation patterns (echo questions, an emphatic-assurance pattern with lengthened vowel and rising pitch superimposed on the base tone, exaggerated-rise on question words like *dím*). The book warns that imposing English-style sentence intonation onto Cantonese can override or destroy individual word tones. Section 1.5 catalogues **sound changes in progress** via sociolinguistic variation and hypercorrection: the *n*→*l* merger (already essentially complete and used as the book's default transcription), *gwo*-→*go*- and *kwo*-→*ko*- reduction, *-k*→*-t* finals, *k*→*h* in the pronoun *kéuih*, and — most strikingly — progressive loss of the initial velar nasal *ng-* (→ zero, or merging with *m-* as in *ngh* 'five' → *mh*, distinguished from negative *mh* only by tone), plus *-ng*→*-n* final merger. The author frames these as changes proceeding word-by-word through the lexicon rather than as a single phonetic shift, with formal/careful registers retaining older forms and younger/casual speech leading the change — plus the social-correction phenomenon of hypercorrect *ng-* insertion into words that never historically had it.

Chapter 2 opens by rejecting the "Monosyllabic Myth" (de Francis) that Chinese words are all single syllables/morphemes, noting isolating morphology still supports productive **affixation, reduplication, and compounding**. Section 2.1.1 (prefixes) catalogues a short, versatile prefix inventory: *a-* (familiarity, kinship/names), *fáan-* 'anti-/counter-' (forms verbs/adjectives/nouns), *hó-* '-able', the contrastive pair *hóu-*/*làahn-* 'good/bad' which combine with perception verbs for positive/negative evaluative adjectives (*hóu-tái* 'good-looking' vs *làahn-tái* 'ugly'), *yih-* 'easy', *lóuh-* 'old' bleached into a pure familiar-address prefix (*lóuh-báan* 'boss', *lóuh-pòh* 'wife, honey' — explicitly glossed colloquial — *lóuh-gūng* 'husband, hubby'), negative *m-* (distinct from sentence-negator *mh*, notably compatible with the perfective aspect marker *-jó* which *mh* is not), and the highly productive *yáuh-* 'having [X]' / its negative counterpart *móuh-* for forming possessive-style adjectives. Section 2.1.2 (suffixes) is the densest stretch: noun-forming suffixes include *-deih* (plural pronoun suffix, NOT used with ordinary nouns except *yàhn*), *-douh* 'degree', *-faat* 'way/method' (also appears discontinuously with *dím(yéung)*/*gām(yéung)* 'how/this way'), *-gā* '-ist' (implies real expertise/distinction — contrasted with the more general *-sī* for professions), *-gaau* '-ism' for religions, *-gēi* 'machine' (fully productive, still coining new words), *-hohk* 'field of study', the diminutive *-jái* 'little X' (also used on celebrity nicknames — Chow Yun-Fat → *Faat-jái* — and, notably, as **slang**: *lóuhpòh-jái*/*lóuhgūng-jái* shift from literal 'little wife/husband' to mean 'girlfriend/boyfriend'), agentive *-jé* (cognate with Mandarin *-zhě*), *-jí* (Cantonese cognate of Mandarin's much more general disyllabifying *-zi*, here lexically restricted since Cantonese's six tones and preserved final consonants reduce the homophony pressure that drives Mandarin's heavier suffixation), *-jyúyi* '-ism' for ideologies/principles, female-marking *-léui* (counterpart to *-jái*), the colloquial male-marking suffix *-lóu* (source of the well-known slang/ethnic term *gwái-lóu* 'foreign devil, Westerner', plus *daaih-lāp-lóu* 'big shot', *fūng-séui-lóu* 'geomancer'), its female counterpart *-pòh* (*gwái-pòh* 'foreign woman', *mùih-yàhn-pòh* 'matchmaker', and the book's explicitly flagged-vulgar *baat-pòh* 'nosy gossipy woman', lit. 'eight-woman', cross-referenced to the adjective *baat-gwa* 'nosy'), *-séh* 'organization', *-sī* 'professional' (lower-register than *-gā*), *-sing* (abstract-noun/adjective-forming, parallel to English *-y*/*-al*), and *-tàuh* 'head'-derived (both concrete 'head-shaped object' nouns like *gwāt-tàuh* 'bone' and localizer/place-expression uses parallel to Mandarin *-tou*, plus adjective-forming *yáuh + X-tàuh* idioms like *yáuh pāai-tàuh* 'stylish, classy'). Adjectival suffixes include *-déi* (diminutive '-ish' on reduplicated adjectives/stative verbs, also adverbializes: *hóu-hóu-déi* 'nicely'), *-sīk* 'style/color' (restricted to literal color/style senses — the book flags that *hāk* 'black' and *hùhng* 'red' retain separate idiomatic extensions, 'secret/underground' and 'hot/fashionable', *without* this suffix, e.g. *hāk sih* 'black market', *daaih hùhng yàhn* 'star, celebrity'), and *-sing* again for adjectives. The one verbal suffix covered, *-fa* '-ize/-ify', is a fully productive causative deriving verbs (and secondarily nominalizations) from nouns and adjectives, concentrated in formal/broadcast registers (*gwokjai-fa* 'internationalize', *yihndoih-fa* 'modernize'). Aspect markers are argued to be true bound suffixes (cannot be separated from the verb), while verbal particles are explicitly *not* suffixes despite sometimes being written that way in the Yale romanization, since they can be split off by negation *mh* or the potential modal *dāk*.

**Linguistically striking finding — the most notable content in this range:** Section 2.1.3 (Infixes) describes *gwái* 'devil, ghost' as a genuinely **infixable** colloquial intensifier/expletive, insertable not just inside a single morpheme (*leuhn-jeuhn* → *leuhn-gwái-jeuhn* 'downright clumsy') but across morpheme boundaries between a stem and a following affix (*séi-gwái-jó* 'died [devil] a long time ago', with the perfective *-jó*), inside full compound words (*dō-yùh* → *dō-gwái-yùh* 'a damn waste of time'; *māt-yéh* → *māt-gwái-yéh* 'what on earth'; *bīn-douh* → *bīn-gwái-douh* 'where on earth'), and productively in rhetorical-question idioms (*Gwái jī mē?* 'Only the devil knows' = 'I have no idea'; *Gwái mh jī àh?* 'Only the devil doesn't know' = 'everybody knows'). This is a non-concatenative morphological device layered on top of an otherwise strongly isolating/concatenative language, and the book notes more offensive expletives can be infixed the same way — a clear, well-documented site for taboo-register slang productivity (`taboo_inversion`/`morphological_play` crossover) worth flagging for the analysis stage.

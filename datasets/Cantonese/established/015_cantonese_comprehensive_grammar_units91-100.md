## Overview

This range covers three major sub-areas of adjective grammar and the start of adverb grammar. Section 9.2 catalogues Cantonese's four main reduplication patterns for adjectives, the main morphological device the language has for modifying adjective meaning (Cantonese is otherwise poor in derivational morphology). Simple [AA] or disyllabic [AABB] reduplication (e.g. *daaih-daaih-go* "really big," *chīng-chīng-chó-chó* "nice and clear") gives an intensifying/vivid reading, and for some speakers the first reduplicated syllable shifts to a rising changed tone (*daaih* → *dáai-daaih*), a tone-sandhi phenomenon directly comparable to the sandhi documented for verb reduplication elsewhere in the book. The [A-A-*déi*] pattern instead moderates the adjective, functioning like English *-ish* (*fèih-féi-déi* "rather fat, chubby"); the suffixed syllable also takes the modified/changed tone except where it's already high-toned. A third, less productive [A-B-B] pattern reduplicates only the second element of a compound and is restricted almost entirely to perceptual/sensory adjectives, especially compound color terms (*baahk-syūt-syūt* "snow-white," *hāk-māng-māng* "pitch dark," *chau-bāng-bāng* "smelly" — this last example's second gloss line reads as something close to a drug-intoxication sense in the source text and is flagged as uncertain below). The authors are explicit that reduplication is "highly idiomatic" — unpredictable in which adjectives it applies to, which pattern is used, and what the resulting meaning is — so each reduplicated form functions more like a separate lexical item than a productive rule output. Separately, the expletive *gwái* ("devil") and the hyperbolic *séi* ("dead") both function as adjective intensifiers with a profane/taboo flavor roughly equivalent to English "damned"/"bloody" (*gwái gam leng* "damned good-looking"; *gwái séi gam leng* "dead gorgeous"); *gwái* can even infix inside a bisyllabic adjective (*màhfàahn* "troublesome" → *màh-gwái-fàahn*).

Section 9.3 surveys Cantonese's several comparison strategies — there is no single dedicated comparative inflection. *dī* marks a comparison whose object is contextually implied; *gwo* (lit. "cross/pass," grammaticalized by the same semantic route as English *surpass*) is the ordinary explicit comparative marker, with the object of comparison following *gwo*; *béi* is a Mandarin-calqued alternative (object-before-adjective word order, the reverse of the *gwo* construction) that the authors describe as carrying "the formal flavour of Mandarin-based syntax" — a clear register/code-contact observation worth flagging. Degree modifiers (*hóu dō* "much," *siu-siu* "a little," *sahp púih* "ten times," hyperbolically used much as in English) stack onto any of these comparative frames. Equal comparison uses *hóuchíh...gam* ("resemble...so") or the more emphatic *yātyeuhng gam* ("just as"), with *tùhng* marking the compared-to party; *tùhng...chā-mdō* gives "about as...as." Negative and interrogative comparison recruit the negative-existential verb *móuh* ("not-have") rather than a true negated adjective, with *yáuh-móuh* as its A-not-A interrogative counterpart — a striking case where comparison morphosyntax is parasitic on existential-verb morphology rather than adjective morphology. The idiom *mh chíh dāk* ("not like able") is a colloquial emphatic negative comparison ("I can't match you for size"). The resultative/concessive particle *gihk* ("to the limits, extreme") pairs with *dōu* ("still/also") in the following clause for a concessive "however X, still not Y" reading, and *gihk yáuh-hahn* is a fixed idiom meaning "not all that [adjective]." Excessives use *taai* ("too"), optionally reinforced by *gwo*, or the idiom *gwotàuh* ("over the top," lit. "cross-head," a near-exact semantic parallel to the English idiom) and *dāk jaih* ("a bit too much"). Superlatives use *jeui* ("most"), which can front before *haih* to focus the subject, bounded optionally by a preceding noun-phrase range or by the numeral/*gam dō* + classifier construction ("of them all"); *gam dō* also derives the experiential superlative ("the funniest film I've ever seen") by combining with the experiential aspect marker *-gwo* in a topicalized clause, where English would use a relative clause instead — a genuine typological contrast the authors call out explicitly. *ji* is a hyperbolic alternative to *jeui* ("the most...ever," "the most...of all time"), heavily used in advertising copy, and also deployed ironically/sarcastically in casual speech — a nice register-contact point between advertising register and sarcasm.

Section 9.4 addresses adjective complementation, where Cantonese diverges sharply from English. Most adjectives taking clausal complements are actually serial-verb-like constructions (no true infinitive exists in Cantonese), evidenced by the fact that adjective and complement can swap order. A special subclass — "adjectives of ease and difficulty" (*làahn* "difficult," *yùhngyih* "easy," *fōngbihn* "convenient," *dākhàahn* "free/at leisure," *hólàhng* "possible") — shows subject-raising behavior opposite to English: the sentence subject is understood as the subject, not the object, of the complement verb, so *Kéuih hóu làahn mihngbaahk* literally "he very hard understand" does NOT mean "he is hard to understand" (that English-type meaning instead requires either topicalization of the object, an impersonal subjectless structure, or a passive *béi*-phrase). The authors flag this as a genuine subject-raising phenomenon not wholly foreign to English (cf. *likely*, *certain*), but with unpredictable class membership. A separate, lexicalized compound pattern (*làahntēng* "bad-sounding," lit. "hard-hear") is a frozen idiom, not productively formed. Section 9.4.2 covers evaluative constructions with *dái* ("worthy," colloquial "deserving") — most strikingly the hyperbolic idiom *dái séi* ("worthy to die"), which doubles as a sharp-edged negative ("serves you right," "you asked for it") and, surprisingly, as a positive compliment for something impressively sharp/funny (a film's "dialogue is funny and sharp"). This dual positive/negative hyperbolic use from the same taboo-adjacent root (*séi* "dead") is a striking slang-register finding worth flagging for synthesis work. *dái dāk lám* ("worthy-able-think") is a milder compliment idiom meaning "selfless." *jihkdāk* ("worthwhile") patterns like the ease/difficulty class discussed above.

The range closes with the opening of chapter 10, "Adverbial constructions." Cantonese has no systematic adjective-to-adverb derivation; instead it leans on two main periphrastic constructions built on adjective syntax: [verb-*dāk*-adjective] (§10.1.1, direct parallel to Mandarin *de*, typically generic/habitual rather than occasion-specific, and requiring verb-object repetition with transitive verbs: *kéuih jā chē jā dāk hóu faai* "she drives very fast," lit. "she drive car drive ADV very fast") and [adjective-*gám*-verb] (§10.1.2, using *gám* "in this way/manner," tonally distinct from *gam* "so"). *gám* is also notably used as a conduit for importing bare English adverbs directly into a Cantonese clause (*séung seriously gám tóuleuhn* "want to seriously discuss") — a direct, naturally-occurring code-mixing data point rather than a textbook-invented one, and *gám* can stand alone elliptically as a predicate meaning "be like that."

## Unit 14 (tail) — dou with causative verbs; inverted resultative sentences

The resultative **dou**-construction from earlier in Unit 14 extends here to true causatives: a causative verb (**gáau**, **jíng** — colloquial "make," roughly interchangeable; **lihng**, **sái** — more formal "make/cause") combines with **dou** plus a resultative clause, so "X gáau/jíng/lihng/sái dou Y [happens]" reads as "X makes it so that Y [happens]." The book frames this pattern as filling a real gap in how English causative verbs like "please," "excite," "embarrass," and "disappoint" get expressed — Cantonese has no single lexical verb for these; instead it builds them compositionally from a generic causative verb plus a dou-resultative clause describing the caused emotional state. A separate, literary/formal alternative causative verb, **douhji** ("cause," as in news-register reporting of negotiations breaking down), is noted as restricted to written/formal register, contrasting with the colloquial gáau/jíng and the more neutral lihng/sái.

A genuinely distinctive syntactic pattern follows: the **inverted resultative sentence**. Normally the dou-clause's logical subject is the main clause's object (e.g., "I talked [and, as a result] got very angry," with "I" as the shared subject). But Cantonese allows construing the logical OBJECT of the resultative verb as the main-clause subject instead, which — combined with fronting that object to the start of the sentence — produces surface strings where an inanimate thing (a newspaper, a breakfast, a stock-market loss) appears to be the grammatical "cause" of an experiencer's state, even though the real cause was an event involving that thing ("Reading the paper made Ming's eyes hurt," literally "the paper read till Ming's eyes hurt"; "Losing on shares has brought me to the ground," literally "the shares lose till I crawl here"). This is a clean case of Cantonese syntax permitting a causer-as-subject reading that doesn't correspond to a literal agentive subject in English translation — worth flagging for the project's grammar-logic diagram track as another instance (alongside the Basic Cantonese passive material) of Cantonese freely reassigning which argument surfaces as subject based on information structure rather than strict semantic role.

## Unit 15 — Quantification

Universal quantification ("every," "all") has three competing strategies: (a) reduplicating a classifier (**gāan-gāan ūk** "every house," **go-go (yàhn)** "everyone"), (b) **múih** "each" plus an (optional) classifier, and (c) the more formal **só yáuh (ge)** "all." Crucially, whichever strategy is used, the universally-quantified NP forces the adverb **dōu** immediately before the verb — and when the quantified NP is the OBJECT rather than subject, it must still be fronted before the verb (**Kéuih dou-dou dōu séung heui** "He wants to go everywhere," object-before-verb) — an object-fronting-under-quantification pattern the book explicitly compares to the Unit 9 focus construction, i.e. quantification forces the same "logical object before verb" word order that focus/topicalization independently motivates elsewhere in the grammar. The same **dōu**-obligatory-before-verb mechanism extends to "any/every" built from a wh-word plus dōu (**bīngo dōu** "anyone," **mātyéh dōu** "anything," **bīndouh dōu** "anywhere"), with **yahmhòh** as a more formal register alternative to the wh-word+dōu strategy.

**dō** "many/much" and **síu** "few/little" show unusual part-of-speech flexibility: besides their ordinary adnominal and predicative-adjective uses, they can function as VERBS, taking the aspect marker **-jó** to mean "became more/fewer" or, idiomatically, "too many/too few" (**Dō-jó léuhng go hohksāang** "There were two students too many"; **Siu-jó yāt go léihyàuh** "There was one reason too few") — a genuinely verb-like behavior for a quantity word that's worth flagging for the project's morphological-typology tracking, since it blurs the noun/adjective/verb boundary in a way English "many/few" cannot.

The particle **saai** ("all," following the verb) is the idiomatic default for expressing totality with transitive verbs (object), intransitive verbs (subject), and adjectives (intensifying), and — most notably for this project — it productively INFIXES into fixed four-syllable idioms/compounds to intensify them: **lé-he** "chaotic" → **lé-saai-he** "totally chaotic"; **lèih-pou** "outrageous" → **lèih-saai-pou** "totally outrageous"; **ngaau-tàuh** (literally "scratch head," idiom for racking one's brains) → **ngaau-saai-tàuh** "utterly brain-wracking." This is a productive infixation-like intensification strategy slotting a grammatical particle into the MIDDLE of an otherwise-fixed compound rather than attaching at an edge — a concrete morphological mechanism worth flagging for the project's slang-derivation-mechanism catalog (akin to English infixing "-bloody-" or "-fucking-" into set phrases, though saai itself is register-neutral, not taboo). saai also stacks with the aspect markers gwo/jyuh (not jó) and with other particles like dóu/hóu/fāan, and combines with the potential construction (V-m̀h-saai / V-dāk-saai, "can't/can V it all"). The partially-synonymous particle **màaih** covers (i) "close/together" and (ii) "in addition to the rest, as well" — and saai/màaih sometimes contrast subtly in scope (finishing EVERYTHING vs. finishing the REMAINING few things).

Two further particles form a pragmatic antonym pair keyed to EXPECTATION: **dāk** "only" (restrictive — expectations not reached; positioned after the verb, and after the relevant object even in a double-object sentence) versus **tìm** (expectations EXCEEDED — "to boot, on top of that," often reinforced by **juhng** "still/also" and realized with a high falling tone distinguishing it from its unstressed homophone). This expectation-based opposition (under- vs. over- the expected amount, both grammaticalized as sentence-final particles rather than lexical adverbs) is a clean, exportable pragmatic-logic pattern.

## Unit 16 — Negative sentences

Several words shift or acquire special meaning specifically under negation. **joi** "again" combined with a negative word (mh/móuh/mhóu) grammaticalizes to "no longer" (**Ngóh mh hóyih joi yáu lohk-heui** "I can't stand it any longer") — a fixed negative-collocation meaning not predictable from "again" + "not" alone. The continuous-aspect marker **jyuh** (normally "ongoing action," Basic Cantonese Unit 19) shifts meaning under negation to **"not yet" / "for the time being"** (**Ngóh mh fàan ūkkei jyuh** "I'm not going home yet") — a genuine polarity-conditioned meaning shift on an aspect marker, worth flagging since it's not an independent lexical item but the SAME morpheme reinterpreted by clause polarity.

Wh-words (**bīngo, mātyéh, bīndouh, dím(yéung), géi(dō)**) generalize from interrogative ("who/what/where/how/how many") to **indefinite** ("anyone/anything/anywhere/any way/many") specifically in negative-polarity contexts: negative sentences, conditionals (cross-referenced to Unit 20), and yes/no questions — a textbook negative-polarity-item (NPI) distribution directly comparable to English "any," and distinguished in the text from **dōu** which instead signals "not at all" (total negation) rather than "hardly at all" (indefinite negation) — a fine scalar distinction the book flags explicitly as easy to conflate. "Neither...nor" has no dedicated conjunction; it reuses **yauh...yauh**, the SAME construction that under positive polarity means "both...and" — a single correlative frame whose gloss flips entirely based on clause polarity. The auxiliary **msái** (already known as the suppletive negative of auxiliary yiu "need to") is reconfirmed here, with its positive counterpart **sái** occurring natively only in rhetorical questions ("As if I need you to teach me that!") — i.e. the positive form survives as a fossilized rhetorical-only remnant rather than ordinary affirmative usage.

## Unit 17 — Questions and answers

Disjunctive ("A or B?") questions split by register/context: **dihng/dihnghaih** is used when the "or" is itself inside a QUESTION, while **yàthaih...yàthaih** ("either...or") and **waahkjé...waahkjé** ("maybe...or maybe," more tentative/indecisive than the definitive-choice yàthaih) are used when "or" appears in a STATEMENT — the same underlying disjunction concept is lexically split by question-vs-statement syntax, a clean case of a single logical connective surfacing as different grammatical items depending on sentence type.

Two sentence-final particles turn statements into "loaded" questions: **mē** signals surprise that the proposition should even be true ("How can this be?"), while **àh** signals disbelief tinged with disapproval ("If this is true I don't think much of it"). Disbelief can be intensified by prefacing the question with **mtúng** ("could it be that...," ranging from genuine incredulity to guesswork), and the particle **ak** sharpens an answer's disagreement with a question's premise. Most importantly for cross-linguistic grammar-logic work: answering logic in Cantonese tracks AGREEMENT/DISAGREEMENT WITH THE QUESTION'S PREMISE, not the truth-value mapping English "yes/no" uses — **haih** ("such is the case") affirms the premise and **mhaih** ("such is not the case") denies it, so a negative question answered with mhaih corresponds to English "yes" (confusingly, from an English speaker's perspective) precisely when the premise itself was negative. This is the same "agree/disagree-with-assertion" answering logic found across much of East/Southeast Asia, directly opposed to English's "truth-of-the-proposition" logic, and is flagged here as a priority item for the grammar-logic diagram track given the project's existing は/が-style cross-linguistic comparison work.

Colloquially, the noun **gwái** ("devil") substitutes for a wh-word to build a bitter, exasperated rhetorical question (**Gwái mh mohng kéuih móuh dāk sīngjik** — literally "[the] devil hopes he doesn't get promoted" = "everyone hopes he doesn't get promoted," i.e. nobody in their right mind would hope the opposite) — a mildly coarse, oath-adjacent colloquial intensifier grammaticalized specifically as a rhetorical-question operator rather than used as a literal noun; worth flagging for the project's taboo/profanity register-mapping work as a "devil"-sourced mild-oath mechanism (cf. English "what the hell," "who the devil").

## Unit 18 — Relative clauses

Two competing relativization strategies split along a formal/colloquial register axis: **ge**-linked clauses (clause + ge + noun) are the more formal, written-register strategy and parallel Mandarin's de directly; **gó + classifier**-linked clauses (clause + gó + classifier + noun, using the demonstrative "that" plus the noun's own classifier) are explicitly characterized as "relatively colloquial" and carry a stronger definite/specific reading ("the one(s) which..."). The gó+classifier strategy doubles as a free-standing pronoun when the head noun is simply dropped once its identity is clear from context (**Ngóh haih kàhmyaht dá-dihnwá lāih gó go** "I'm the one who called yesterday") — demonstrative+classifier functioning as both a relativizer and a headless-relative/pronoun, a dual grammatical role worth flagging.

When a relative clause would modify the sentence's OBJECT, the resulting string tends to become clumsy (relative clause plus object plus further material all stacked after the verb); the regular fix is to TOPICALIZE the whole modified object-plus-relative-clause unit to the front of the sentence, directly reusing the topicalization machinery already established in Basic Cantonese Unit 22 — i.e. relative-clause formation and topicalization are not independent systems but actively interact to avoid center-embedding-style clumsiness. Relative clauses targeting an INDIRECT object (e.g., "the friends to whom we lend money") require a RESUMPTIVE PRONOUN inside the clause referring back to the modified noun, since Cantonese relativization otherwise has no gap-filling mechanism for non-subject/non-direct-object positions — the same resumptive strategy is needed when the modified noun is the object of a coverb (tùhng "with") or embedded inside a complement clause.

Indefinite/free relative clauses ("whoever," "whatever," "wherever") reuse the SAME wh-word-doubling pattern already seen for negative-sentence indefinites in Unit 16, with the question word stated twice (**Bīngo mh ngàam jauh dōu douh-hip** "Whoever is in the wrong should apologize") — and the book explicitly cross-references this to the conditional-sentence double-conjunction pattern that Unit 19 formalizes next, tying three separate grammatical domains (negative-polarity indefinites, free relatives, and conditionals) to one shared wh-word-repetition-plus-jauh template.

## Unit 19 (opening) — Subordinate clauses: double conjunctions

This page range ends just as Unit 19 begins. The book opens by noting subordinate clauses are used LESS in Cantonese than in English/European languages generally, because serial-verb constructions and simple clause-juxtaposition often substitute for them in colloquial speech. It then introduces the DOUBLE-CONJUNCTION pattern as a characteristic feature of Chinese subordination: a main clause and its subordinate clause are each marked by a paired conjunction rather than just one (as opposed to English, where the second, main-clause conjunction, e.g. "then," is optional/stylistic rather than near-obligatory) — **yānwaih...sóyíh** "because...therefore," **dòng...gójahnsih** "when...then," **yùhgwó...jauh** "if...then," **seuiyìhn...daahnhaih** "although...but." The first conjunction of each pair can appear either before or after the subordinate clause's subject. Full coverage of this unit (conditionals, concessives, and the rest of its scope) belongs to the next extraction segment.

**Kinship and greetings (pp. 380–383).** The grammar's kinship list distinguishes a daughter's
children from a son's children by prefixing *ngoih* ("outside") onto the son's-child terms —
*syūn/syūnléui* ("grandson/granddaughter" via a son) versus *ngoih-syūn/ngoih-syūnléui* (via a
daughter) — a patrilineal naming asymmetry that repeats for nephews and nieces (*jaht-jái* vs.
*yih-sāng(-jái)*, etc.). The greetings section is a register ladder: the classic *Léih hóu (ma)?*
is now described as relatively *formal*, used between strangers or acquaintances rather than close
friends/family; closer relations use *jeuigahn dím a?* ("How've you been?") or the book's own
explicitly slang-flagged *Mātyéh wàahngíng a?* ("What's up?"). The authors also flag
*Sihk-jó faahn meih a?* ("Have you eaten yet?") as the real-world equivalent of "How are you?",
comparing it to the British "Lovely day, isn't it?" as a ritual dummy-topic greeting. New Year
greetings (*Sān lìhn faailohk!*, *Gūnghéi faat chòih!*) are followed by a culturally specific
request-for-money ritual: *Gūnghéi faat chòih!* can be answered with a demand for *laih-sih*
(lucky money / red packets), traditionally given only by married people to children, employees, or
close acquaintances — captured in the fixed phrase *Laih-sih dauh lòih!* ("Lucky-money time!").
Section 20.5 (telephone expressions) is a self-contained functional-phrase inventory: opening a
call with *wái*, polite requests with *chéng mahn*, and a set of stock excuses/replies for when
the person called is unavailable (*hōi-gán-wúi* "in a meeting," *góng-gán dihnwá* "on the phone,"
etc.), ending with phrases for wrong numbers and call-waiting interruptions.

**Numerals (Ch. 21, 380–397).** The core numeral system (1–20, decimal zero-pronunciation rules,
*maahn*-based counting above 10,000 rather than thousands, the idiom *maahn yāt* "just in case" —
literally "10,000-to-1 odds," abbreviated from *yāt maahn yāt chīn* "11,000") is followed by a
striking register note: in colloquial working-class Cantonese, money is metaphorically called
*séui* ("water") — *yāt gauh séui* "one hundred dollars," *yāt pùhn séui* "one thousand dollars" —
and the book explicitly tags three derived verb phrases as slang: *dohk séui* ("get money,"
usually by borrowing), *bohng séui* ("pay up, quickly"), and *daahp-séui* ("have lots of money").
Informal speech also clips compound numbers (*yihsahp-* → *yah-/yeh-*, *sahp* "ten" → *-ah* in
30+), and uses a family of approximation constructions (*sahpgéi*, *baak-géi*, *chīn-lèhng*,
*géisahp*, *héimáh* "at least," juxtaposed adjacent numerals as a hedge) that don't map cleanly
onto single English words. A genuine grammatical distinction worth flagging is *yih* vs. *léuhng*
for "two": *yih* is used in counting/compounds (*yihsahp* "twenty"), *léuhng* in quantifying nouns
and currency units — with 200/2,000/20,000 as a crossover zone where either is acceptable. The
particle *mā* marks doubled/paired items (*mā-sāang* "twins," *mā lihng* "double zero," idiom
*dá-mā làih* "come in pairs"). Section 21.1.4 ("Lucky and taboo numbers") is the chapter's most
linguistically striking section: *sei* "four" is avoided because it's a near-homophone of *séi*
"die" (some buildings skip a labeled 4th floor), *sahpsei* "fourteen" is worse (echoes *saht séi*
"certainly die"), while *baat* "eight" is prized as a homophone of *faat* "prosper/make money."
The authors give real vehicle-license-plate examples built from these associations (168 = "yāt
louh faat," "make money all the way"; 9888 = "gáu faat faat faat") and one explicitly flagged
numeric pun — 709,394 read as *chēut làih gáau sāam gáau sei* — which the book glosses as usually
referring to a man having an extramarital affair, i.e. a taboo/slang numeric innuendo built
entirely out of digit-homophony. The chapter closes with days/months vocabulary, including a
tone-only minimal pair the book calls out explicitly: *sīngkèih-yāt* "Monday" vs. *sīngkèih-yaht*
"Sunday," distinguished only by tone.

# Teach Yourself Cantonese — pp. 211–245 (end of Unit 21 through opening of Unit 25)

Source pages are image-only (no text layer); rendered at 150 dpi and read via vision. No
handwritten marginalia appeared anywhere in this range — all content below is printed source text.
Printed page numbers run 195–230 against the PDF's 211–245 (title pages for new units carry no
printed folio). The range spans the tail of Unit 21's revision exercises, all of Unit 22 ("the
office," sé-jih-làuh), all of Unit 23 ("eating out in Hong Kong," Hèung-góng jáu-làuh), all of
Unit 24 ("leisure activities," sih-hou), and the opening dialogue of Unit 25 ("household affairs,"
fóhng-ūk), which is cut off mid-unit at the page-245 boundary — its vocabulary box falls just past
this range and is not included here.

Unit 22 is explicitly billed in its own chapter-opening blurb as teaching "two or three really
colloquial speech patterns to add liveliness to your conversation," and it delivers. The chapter's
"Fun with characters" sidebar introduces **mē 咩**, a spoken-register question particle (its
character literally depicts a mouth plus a sheep, iconically "bleating" the sound) — this is a
softer, more casual alternative to the standard **ma?** question particle and belongs squarely in
colloquial registration rather than textbook-neutral Cantonese. The grammar notes likewise flag the
prefix **A-** 呀 (used before surnames, given names, or kinship terms, with an automatic tone shift
on the following syllable) as an intimate/familiar address form — the book describes it as almost
a verbal "heads up, I'm about to address you" signal. A third colloquial item the book calls out
by name is the clipped time expression **gáu-dím-bun** ("half past nine," used by Mr Chan for his
start time) — the text explicitly notes this phrasing as "worth noting for your own use" as the
colloquial way of citing a half-hour, contrasted with a more formal full construction. The office
unit's grammar also surfaces two compression/intensifier patterns: **mh + verb + mh dāk** ("if you
don't verb it won't do," i.e., simply must verb) as a double-negative obligation-marker, and
**verb + dōu + meih + verb-gwo** ("never even...") which doubles the verb around dōu rather than
using the lìhn...dōu... frame from an earlier unit. **Sàm-gèi** ("mind/enthusiasm," lit. heart-
machine) is flagged as an idiom resistant to literal translation, closest to "give one's mind to"
or, negated, "listless/unenthusiastic."

Unit 23's restaurant content is a register ladder in miniature. Cantonese distinguishes three
words for "restaurant" by cuisine type and register: **jáu-làuh**/**jáu-gā** (Chinese restaurants,
both built on jáu "alcohol," reflecting that Chinese diners traditionally drink mainly while
eating) versus **chāan-tēng** (non-Chinese-style restaurants) — and the same split recurs in the
verbs for eating: **sihk-faahn** implies a proper Chinese meal, **sihk-chāan** a Western-style one.
The chapter's signature colloquialism is **màaih-dāan** 埋單 ("close the account" → "check,
please!"), a fixed idiomatic request phrase rather than a literal translation of "bill." The
book also notes **chéng** as a shorter, more colloquial stand-in for the fuller, more formal
**yiu-chíng** ("to invite") — same meaning, different register weight. Politeness vocabulary here
runs the other direction: **gwai-gwok** ("your [honorable] country") is explicitly framed as a
surviving fragment of an older honorific system in which other people's things are elevated and
one's own are deprecated — the chapter's cultural note drives this home with the (satirical) 1900
"honourable rat" anecdote from Arthur Smith. Interjections **hà!** (laughter) and, in the next
unit, **ài!** (a sigh, "alas") round out the chapter's set of spoken-register particles that
wouldn't appear in written registers.

Unit 24 (leisure) contributes two conspicuous English/Japanese loanwords that sit at the
colloquial/slang edge of the lexicon — **dīk-sih-gōu** 的士高 ("discotheque," a phonetic
transliteration of "disco") and **kā-lāai-ōu-kēi** 卡拉OK ("karaoke," transliterating the
Japanese loan directly, retaining the Latin "OK") — both naming youth/urban leisure venues rather
than traditional pastimes. The vocabulary box for this unit also contains **guih** ("tired"),
written with a character (攰) that is a Cantonese-specific colloquial graph not standard in written
Mandarin — worth flagging for any future script-level analysis of Cantonese's written colloquial
layer. The idiom **mh-gin-jó** (lit. "became unseen") is the ordinary way to say something is
lost/mislaid, and the chapter's **-gèi** suffix (machine) turns out to be a highly productive
colloquial compounding element — **yàuh-hei-gèi** (games machine), **dihn-sih-gèi** (TV set),
**fèi-gèi** (aircraft, lit. "flying machine"), **dá-fó-gèi** (cigarette lighter) — all built the
same way from a bound root rather than the full word gèi-hei. The **yāt... jauh...** ("as soon
as... then...") construction is highlighted as a case where real grammatical complexity is built
out of two of the simplest words in the language (yāt "one," jauh "then"), a compression mechanism
worth comparing against similar clipped-connective patterns found in other languages' slang
registers.

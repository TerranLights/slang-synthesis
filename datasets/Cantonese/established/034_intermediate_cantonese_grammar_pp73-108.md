# Intermediate Cantonese: A Grammar and Workbook — pp. 73–108 (printed pp. 56–91)

Source: *Intermediate Cantonese: A Grammar and Workbook* (Routledge), the direct sequel to *Basic
Cantonese: A Grammar and Workbook* (already extracted as files 029a–c). This slice is image-only
(vision-read, no text layer) and spans the tail of Unit 9 through the opening of Unit 14. No
handwritten marginalia appeared on any of the 36 pages in this range — all content below is from
the printed text.

## Unit 9 (tail): focus and temporal-expectation particles

The range opens mid-Unit-9, covering two related focus mechanisms. First, topicalization by simple
fronting (moving an object before the subject or between subject and verb) is contrasted with
focusing via **lihn ... dōu** ("even"), which obligatorily inserts **dōu** before the verb and, when
the focused element is the object of a transitive verb, fronts that object ahead of the verb as
well. The construction can drop **lihn** itself, especially in negative sentences, and pairs
naturally with the particle **màaih** ("including") for a stronger "even" reading. Second, the book
draws out a genuinely intermediate-level distinction between the particles **sīn** and **jauh**,
both glossable as "then" but carrying opposite implications about whether an event happened later
or earlier than expected: in temporal clauses **sīn** marks the event as later-than-expected ("not
until"), **jauh** as earlier/sufficient ("already by"); the same split recurs in quantity contexts
(three bottles being "more than expected" vs. "enough already") and surfaces again in conditional
clauses, where **sīn** marks a necessary condition and **jauh** a sufficient one. This condition/
sufficiency reading foreshadows the book's own forward reference to Unit 20.

## Unit 10: the jēung construction and its High-Cantonese extension

Unit 10 introduces **jēung**, described explicitly as a "heui-chìh" (empty/functional word) whose
job is to pull a verb's object in front of the verb: verb–object becomes jēung–object–verb. The
chapter is careful to distinguish this from Mandarin's **bǎ**, which jēung superficially resembles
but is more restricted in Cantonese — several bǎ-sentences given as unacceptable with jēung in
colloquial speech (object must be literally displaced/relocated, e.g. *moving furniture, hanging a
picture*, not merely "turning off a light"). The chapter then marks a register split explicitly:
**"jēung in High Cantonese"** extends the construction to abstract senses of transfer (handing over
a matter, dividing a legacy, returning a watch to its owner) and to formal usages found in public
announcements and the transitive **-fa** ("-ize") suffix (e.g. *sou-máh-fa*, "digitalize"), both
flagged as influenced by Mandarin/written Chinese and explicitly tied to formal register rather than
colloquial speech — a useful source-tier distinction for slang-synthesis purposes, since it marks
the boundary of where jēung stops being everyday grammar and starts being bureaucratic/written
register. A final idiomatic subsection, **jēung ... kéuih**, uses the pronoun kéuih as a placeholder
object in imperative sentences (regularly paired with the **-jó** aspect marker), e.g. "stew the
birds' nest" rendered literally as "jēung-[it]-stew-jó-[it]."

## Unit 11: serial verbs and coverbs

This unit formalizes a structural feature the earlier Basic Cantonese material used without naming:
coverbs (words that function as both independent verbs and verb-like prepositions, e.g. **hái**
"be at/at," **tùhng** "accompany/with," **gán** "follow/until," **dou** "reach/until," **deui**
"face/towards," **heung** "face/towards (direction)," **waih(jó)** "for the sake of"). The chapter's
key grammatical point: unlike English prepositions, Cantonese coverbs still take aspect markers
(**-jó, -gwo, -gán, -jyuh**) and verbal particles (**-saai, -fāan**), because they remain verbs
syntactically. Multiple verbs can then chain in a single clause without any connective — serial verb
constructions — which the book flags as a Southeast-Asian areal feature (shared with other languages
of the region) rather than a general European-language pattern, with American English "let's go eat"
offered as the nearest English analog. Several serial verbs shift meaning when used non-initially in
a chain: **bōng** "help" becomes a benefactive "for, on behalf of" (not literal assistance — "help
me find car keys" means look for them for me, not assist me in looking); **wán** "look for" is
attenuated to "with/use" ("look for a cloth, wipe" = "wipe with a cloth"). The chapter catalogs
functional categories realized through serial verbs: instrument, accompaniment, benefactive,
simultaneity (continuous-aspect **-jyuh** on the first verb), temporal sequence, purpose (via
**jouh** "do," and verbs of motion **làih**/**heui** "come/go"), and communication (paired verbs of
speaking — **góng**/**wah** — with perception — **tēng**/**ji** — expressing what the speaker does
vs. the effect on the hearer). The real-time ordering point is emphasized: the sequence of verbs in
a Cantonese serial construction iconically mirrors the real-world order of events, which is often
not reflected in the English translation.

## Unit 12: secondary aspect markers and reduplication

Beyond the core aspect markers (-jó, -gwo, -gán, -jyuh) covered in Basic Cantonese, this unit
surveys a denser set of nuanced aspectual devices — genuinely new, intermediate-tier material:

- **háh** (delimitative/tentative, "for a little while"), reinforced idiomatically by the particle
  **sīn**. Several verbs can also reduplicate with háh for an even more tentative reading
  (wáan → wáan-háh "play for a while" → wáan-wáan-háh "just play around"); verb-verb-háh forms can
  head subordinate clauses expressing a resulting/subsequent event.
- **Verb-yāt-verb** reduplication (e.g. tái-yāt-tái "take a look"), structurally parallel to
  Mandarin verb-yī-verb, where the verb itself serves as its own classifier; in fast speech the
  "yāt" often contracts away, leaving a rising tone on the first verb (bok-yāt-bok → bók-bok).
- **Verb-léuhng-verb** ("two" instead of "one") — a variant carrying a negative or dismissive
  connotation (trying half-heartedly, giving up quickly), or describing minimal effort/time
  achieving a result.
- **hōi** — habitual aspect ("regularly, in the habit of"); in subordinate clauses it can instead
  mark a progressive sense (an activity already underway, "now that we're walking this way...").
- **héi-séuhng-làih** — inchoative, "rise up" grammaticalized to mean the onset of an action,
  closely paralleling Mandarin qǐlái; characteristically used in "as soon as" subordinate clauses;
  with a transitive verb-object compound the sequence splits, with héi landing between the verb and
  object.
- **lohk-heui** — continuative ("go down" grammaticalized to "go on ...ing"), can combine with the
  potential negative **m̀h dóu** ("can't go on ...ing").
- **chân** — a particle with two distinct, register-relevant meanings: habitual "every time" (only
  in subordinate clauses, requires **dōu** before the main verb since it is a quantifier) and
  adversative "to one's own misfortune" (physical or emotional mishap — tripping, catching a cold,
  being bumped), which pairs naturally with passive constructions.

## Unit 13: comparison structures

This unit expands comparison beyond the basic gwo/béi pattern from Basic Cantonese. Verb-based
comparisons require **dō gwo** ("more than") rather than bare gwo, and **síu gwo** gives "less
than." Negative and interrogative comparisons use **móuh ... gam** ("not as ... as") and its
question form **yáuh-móuh ... gam** ("as ... as?"), both compatible with stative verbs as well as
adjectives; for interrogatives a simpler construction with **dihng** ("or") plus comparative **dī**
is often preferred in practice. **Béihéi** ("compared with") is used to tack a comparison on as an
afterthought to an already-completed statement, often closed with the particle combination **laih
góng** ("come to speak of it"). Similarity is expressed two ways: **yāt yeuhng** ("the same, alike")
and **chā-m-dō** ("differing not much," i.e. "about the same/approximately"), the latter used for
generic approximation; **houchíh** ("resemble/seem") works alongside **gam** in similar
constructions. **jeuhn** ("exhaust") forms superlative adverbial phrases — jeuhn faai "as quickly as
possible," jeuhn jóu "as soon as possible," jeuhn lihk "with as much effort as possible," jeuhn
leuhng "as far as possible" — which can themselves function as full adverbs. For excess, the
register ladder runs from neutral **taai** ("too," paralleling Mandarin tài) through emphatic **taai
gwo** (combining taai and gwo for stronger emphasis), to the more colloquial and distinctly
Cantonese **dāk jaih** ("a bit too") and **gwotàuh** (an "overly, to excess" suffix-like usage
productive with verbs — fan gwolùhng "oversleep," jyú gwolùhng "overcook," sei gwolùhng "spoil [a
child] to death"), up to the formal register marker **gihk dou** ("exceedingly"). The
colloquial/formal split among near-synonymous excess markers (taai vs. dāk jaih vs. gihk dou) is a
clear register-tier finding worth flagging for slang-synthesis purposes.

## Unit 14 (opening): resultative/causative dou

The range closes at the start of Unit 14, which introduces **dou** as a grammatical particle
distinct from its use as an independent verb ("arrive, reach") — here forming resultative/causative
clauses meaning "to the point of ..." with both verbs and adjectives. With verbs, the subject of the
resulting clause is dropped under same-subject conditions ("so tired she couldn't walk" literally
"tired till walk not able," no repeated pronoun) but retained under different-subject conditions.
With transitive verbs, the verb itself must be repeated/copied before the dou-phrase (scold-him-
scold-till-hoarse). With adjectives, the combination **dou séi** ("to death") is flagged as
especially productive for emotive/evaluative intensification, covering both negative readings
(scared to death, ugly to death, messy to death) and — more idiomatically — positive readings (dead
gorgeous, dead smart), i.e. a single intensifier template whose polarity depends on the base
adjective rather than on dou séi itself. A parallel idiom cluster, **dou jeuhn** ("to the limit/
fullest"), covers set phrases like hok dou jeuhn ("study hard"), je dou jeuhn ("borrow to the
limit"), syú/yèhng dou jeuhn ("win/lose everything"), wáan dou jeuhn ("play to the full"), and wāi
dou jeuhn ("super cool") — the last of these reads as a genuinely colloquial, slang-adjacent
intensifier construction rather than neutral grammar, worth a closer look if a later extraction pass
reaches the rest of Unit 14's exercises.

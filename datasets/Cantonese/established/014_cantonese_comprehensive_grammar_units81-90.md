## Serial verbs, directional/motion verbs, and disposal constructions (pp. 144-149)

The chapter closes out its treatment of serial verb constructions. Instrumental "with" is expressed periphrastically: *yuhng* ("use") or *wán* ("look for") precede a second verb meaning "come"/"go"/"do," so "use X to do Y" substitutes for a dedicated instrumental preposition. *jēung* functions as a disposal-construction marker roughly parallel to Mandarin *bǎ*, moving an object in front of the verb, but the text stresses it is narrower in scope than *bǎ* — restricted mainly to literal or metaphorical motion/removal events (e.g., "put dirty clothes all over," "split money in two," "exchange old things for fashionable ones"). Aspect-marker placement is construction-specific: it typically follows the first verb in a series, but follows the second verb specifically in *jēung* constructions.

A seven-member closed class of directional verbs (*yahp* "in," *chēut* "out," *séuhng* "up," *lohk* "down," *gwo* "over," *fāan* "back," *màaih* "close") does double duty as true transitive verbs (taking directional objects: *yahp fóng* "enter the room") and as particles following *heui* "go" / *làih* "come" to build compound directional verbs (*yahp heui* "go in," *chēut làih* "come out," etc.). A third verb of manner can be stacked in front of the directional-plus-heui/làih pair, yielding three-verb sequences (*jáu yahp heui* "rush in," *tiu séuhng heui* "leap up"); the final verb in such a chain is always *heui* or *làih*, chosen by deictic point of reference exactly as English "come"/"go" would be. A reduplicative idiom template, [verb-*làih*-verb-*heui*], expresses aimless to-and-fro or repeated motion, both literally ("walking back and forth") and metaphorically ("thinking it over and over," "talking round in circles without reaching a conclusion") — a genuinely productive expressive pattern worth flagging as a register-neutral but highly colloquial-feeling construction. *heui...làih* separately marks "back from a trip," and *joi làih* means "do/try again" (used as a rehearsal/practice instruction).

## Passives (pp. 149-153)

Cantonese passives are marked by *béi* (historically from *beih*, which survives as a low-level-tone literary/written variant — the book explicitly notes this is a **tone-sandhi doublet**: the colloquial spoken form *béi* arose from *beih* via tone change). Unlike Mandarin *bèi*, the by-phrase agent after *béi* is close to obligatory in speech; when the agent is unknown/generic it still must be filled lexically, with *yàhn* "person" for a human agent and *yéh* "thing" for a non-human one. The *béi*-passive carries a default adversative/affectedness implication (subject is negatively affected), which blocks straightforward translation of neutral English passives like "I was remembered." Three extended subtypes are described: **indirect passives** (subject is not the notional object of the verb at all — "he had his money stolen [from him]," modeled on a Japanese-grammar analogy, with no literal active-sentence counterpart), **resultative passives** (passive verb + resultative particle/adjective, e.g. "the videotape has deteriorated from being watched," sometimes further marked by extent-*dou*: "cheated to the point of bankruptcy"), and **adjectival passives** (passive-verb-phrase + linking *ge* modifying a head noun, structurally indistinguishable from a relative clause).

## Causative and resultative constructions (pp. 153-156)

Two lexical causative verbs split by register and word order: *jíng* [adjective][object] ("make X Y," colloquial) versus *lihng* [object][adjective] ("cause X [to be] Y," more formal/written). A periphrastic alternative, *V + dou*, extends this into full causation-of-events ("make something happen"): *jíng dou...* is the colloquial form, *lihng dou...* the formal one. Separately, **resultative compounds** combine two verbs into a transitive/causative unit (*giu* "call" + *séng* "wake" → *giu séng* "wake [someone] up"; *chòuh* "make noise" + *séng* → *chòuh séng* "wake by being noisy"). The **extent/resultative complement** construction [verb-*dou*-complement] is extremely productive: *dou* ("until/to the extent that") introduces an adjective or full clause expressing the result or degree of an action ("walk until tired," "drink until full," "pour until overflowing"). When the main verb already has its own object (e.g. a verb-object compound like *yám-yéh* "drink things"), the verb must be repeated before the *dou*-phrase can attach — a syntactic workaround also seen with the parallel *dāk*-marked adverbial/resultative construction (loosely paralleling Mandarin *de*).

## Adjectives: syntax, reduplication, comparison, and degree/extent idioms (pp. 156-161)

Opening Chapter 9, the authors frame Cantonese adjectives as closer to Chinese-linguistics "stative verbs" than to European-tradition adjectives: no copula is used, some adjectives take verbal aspect marking, yet three properties still justify treating them as a distinct subclass — (a) predicative adjectives normally require a preceding degree word, most often *hóu*, which in this slot is semantically bleached to a near-meaningless adjective-marker rather than true "very" (a bare predicative adjective sentence is grammatically incomplete without it, except in restricted contexts like answering an A-not-A question); (b) adjectives can reduplicate to shift meaning (*fē-fē-déi* "brownish," *gōu-gōu-sau-sau* "tall and thin" — the diminutive/approximative and intensive reduplication templates); (c) adjectives take dedicated comparative markers *dī* ("a bit more") and *gwo* ("more than"). Attributive adjectives precede the noun and are usually linked by *ge*, the same particle used in possessives and relative clauses (all building the same [modifier]-*ge*-[head noun] template); *ge* is obligatorily dropped in a set of lexicalized adjective-noun collocations denoting inherent/characteristic properties (*baahk jáu* "white wine," *hùhng chàh* literally "red tea" = English "black tea" — flagged as a notable cross-linguistic basic-color-term mismatch — *lēk léui* "bright girl," *chéun jái* "foolish boy," *leng léui* "beautiful woman"), but *ge* must be restored once a degree word like *hóu*/*géi*/*ji* modifies the adjective in that same slot.

The degree-adverb inventory (*géi* "quite," *gam* "so," *gau* "enough," *béigaau* "relatively," *fēiseuhng* "extremely," *mjī géi* "incredibly," *taai*/*gwotàuh* "too," *jeui*/*ji* "most") is followed by a strikingly rich set of **extent/intensifier idioms built on *dou***, which read as the most slang-adjacent material in this range: *dou séi* ("to death," e.g. *yáuh-chín dou séi* "stinking rich," *dākyi dou séi* "unbearably cute"), *fēi héi* ("fly up/take off," e.g. *leng dou fēi héi* "mind-blowingly beautiful"), *bāt-dāk-líuh* (a Mandarin-origin loan idiom "to an extreme degree," somewhat more formal/written), and especially a set of idiosyncratic verb-specific extent collocations that defy compositional translation: *làahn dou wàhn* (lit. "difficult till dizzy" = mind-bendingly hard), *muhn dou baauja* (lit. "bored till explode" = bored stiff), and *leng dou dám yāt sēng* — literally "pretty till [it makes the] splash sound," where *dám yāt sēng* is an onomatopoeic expression for the sound of something dropping into water, repurposed figuratively to mean "stunningly beautiful." This last one is worth flagging explicitly: an onomatopoeia-derived intensifier collocation is exactly the kind of idiomatic, register-marked material the slang-synthesis pipeline is built to capture, and it has no transparent literal parse in English.

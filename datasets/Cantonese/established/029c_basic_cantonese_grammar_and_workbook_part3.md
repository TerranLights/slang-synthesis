## Unit 20 — Auxiliary verbs

The core modal auxiliaries — wúih "will/would," hóyíh "can/may," sīk "know how to," yìhnggōi/yìnggōi "should, ought to," yiu/sēuiyiu "want/need," séung "want to" — all precede the main verb and express primarily "modal" (possibility/necessity) meaning rather than tense. Some double as main verbs with a related but distinct meaning (sīk as auxiliary "know how to [do]" vs. sīk as main verb "know [a person]"; yiu as auxiliary "need to" vs. main verb "want [a thing]"), which the book flags as a place learners commonly stumble since the two uses look identical. A genuinely irregular suppletion pattern is highlighted: the negative of MAIN-VERB yiu ("want") is the regular m̀h yiu ("don't want"), but the negative of AUXILIARY yiu ("need to") is the SUPPLETIVE msái ("don't need to") — not *m̀h yiu in that sense — a real irregularity worth flagging explicitly, parallel to the yáuh/móuh suppletion of Unit 6.

Modal meaning is further refined by modal adverbs: waahkjé/hólàhng "perhaps" vs. hángdihng/yātdihng "certainly" modifying wúih. A subtle semantic distinction is drawn between yātdihng yiu ("must," deontic obligation) and yātdihng haih ("must be," epistemic/logical inference) — and because haih cannot combine with adjectives (Unit 7/9), yātdihng ALONE (without haih) is used to signal inference when the predicate is an adjective (Léih yātdihng hóu guih "You must be tired" — no haih present, inference reading recovered purely from yātdihng). Finally, the book notes that ability/possibility is often more naturally expressed with the POST-verbal potential particle dāk (Unit 17) than with the pre-verbal auxiliary hóyíh, and that negated ability is more naturally expressed with the verb-particle negation ...m̀h dóu than with m̀h hóyíh — with a real semantic contrast between the two negation strategies: V+m̀h+dóu marks inability due to lack of personal ability/skill (Ngóh bōng m̀h dóu léih "I can't help you [I'm not capable]"), while m̀h+V+dāk marks impossibility due to EXTERNAL circumstances/permission (Ngóh m̀h bōng dāk léih "I can't help you [I'm not allowed to / don't have time]") — a clean internal-ability vs. external-circumstance modality contrast worth flagging for modal-logic cross-referencing.

## Unit 21 — Passives

Cantonese passives are built with a béi-phrase (béi + agent noun phrase) placed BEFORE the verb — structurally resembling the English "by"-phrase but positioned differently in the clause. Critically, and explicitly contrasted with both English and Mandarin, the agent phrase is OBLIGATORY in spoken Cantonese passives — agentless passives are not permitted, so when the agent is unknown or irrelevant the generic filler yàhn "person" or yéh "thing" must still be supplied (Ngóh go ngàhnbāau béi yàhn tāu-jó "My wallet was stolen [by someone]" — yàhn cannot be dropped the way English drops "by someone"). A further genuinely distinctive feature: a Cantonese passive verb can still retain its OWN direct object, producing two surface variants of the same event with different information structure — "my licence was taken away [by someone]" can be phrased either with the patient-possessor as subject (Ngóh go chēpàaih béi yàhn ló-jó go chēpàaih, patient-centered/objective) or with the AFFECTED PERSON as subject while the object stays with the verb (Ngóh béi yàhn ló-jó go chēpàaih, "I had my licence taken away," person-centered/focused on the misfortune to the individual) — the book frames this pair explicitly as a difference in rhetorical focus (objective statement of events vs. emphasis on the victim's experience), not merely free variation.

The book also documents several ways Cantonese expresses passive-like meaning WITHOUT a béi-phrase at all, via topicalization (placing the patient/object first) combined with a completive aspect marker or resultative particle and often NO overt subject (Gāan fóng yàuh-jó la, literally "the room painted," = "The room has been painted"). This connects directly to the topicalization machinery of Unit 22, and the book treats agentless-topicalized-patient sentences as the normal, default way Cantonese avoids passive constructions when the agent is irrelevant — i.e., Cantonese has two competing strategies (béi-passive with obligatory agent, vs. agentless topicalization) rather than one flexible passive like English.

## Unit 22 — Word order and topicalization

Baseline Cantonese word order is subject–verb–object, similar to English, but the book is explicit that departures from this order are not marginal exceptions — they "play an important role in the language." Topicalization (fronting a non-subject constituent, usually the object, as what the sentence is "about") is presented as far more pervasive in Cantonese than in English, where it is mostly reserved for explicit contrast; in Cantonese it occurs freely even without contrastive intent (Pouthnghwá ngóh sīk síu-síu, literally "Putonghua I know a little" = "I know a little Putonghua," object fronted with no special contrastive emphasis implied). When topicalization combines with pro-drop (Unit 4), the result can reduce to a bare object–verb string with no subject at all (Sāangyaht daahn-gōu sihk-jó la, literally "birthday cake eaten already" = "we've eaten the cake") — and the book notes this surface pattern often LOOKS like a passive and can be translated as one, even though no béi-phrase or true passive morphology is present — directly connecting back to Unit 21's agentless-topicalization discussion.

A distinct and more exotic category, "hanging topics," is introduced for topics that are neither the subject NOR the object of the verb but only loosely related to the sentence's content — the book distinguishes three subtypes: (i) topic sets a frame in time/space (Hahtīn ngóh jūngyi yàuh-séui "In summer, I like swimming" — "summer" is a temporal frame, not an argument of "like"); (ii) topic denotes a WHOLE of which something later in the sentence is a PART (Sahp go hohksāang yáuh gáu go hóyíh yahp daaihhohk, "Of ten students, nine can get into university" — the whole-part partitive relation is expressed purely by topic-comment juxtaposition, no overt "of" needed); (iii) topic states a general CATEGORY of which the subject/object is a specific instance (Bjáu léih hóyíh yám Chīngdóu, "[For] beer, you can drink Tsingtao" — "beer" frames a category, "Tsingtao" instantiates it). This three-way typology of hanging topics (frame-setting, whole-part, category-instance) is a genuinely rich piece of discourse-syntax worth flagging for later cross-linguistic topic/focus-structure comparison — it goes well beyond the simple subject-fronting topicalization familiar from European-language grammars. The book also notes a consistent word-order principle recurring from Unit 12 (superlative scope) here too: Cantonese systematically orders the GENERAL/inclusive term before the SPECIFIC one, across superlatives, dates (Unit 28), and addresses.

## Unit 23 — Yes/no questions

The default Cantonese yes/no question strategy is A-NOT-A reduplication of the predicate (verb, adjective, or auxiliary) — not an English-style rising-intonation question or a Mandarin-style sentence-final particle: Kéuihdeih làih-m̀h-làih a? literally "they come-not-come," glossed by the book as structurally equivalent to "Is X the case or not?" with the "or" left unexpressed. This extends uniformly to adjectives (Ga chē gwai-m̀h-gwai a? "Is the car expensive?") and to auxiliaries, where the AUXILIARY itself is what gets reduplicated, not the main verb (Léih sīk-m̀h-sīk góng Yahtmán a? "Can you speak Japanese?" — sīk repeated, not góng). When the predicate being questioned has more than one syllable, only its FIRST syllable is reduplicated (jūngyi "like" → jūng-m̀h-jūngyi, NOT *jūngyi-m̀h-jūngyi) — a specific phonological/morphological constraint on the pattern worth flagging precisely. Two verbs are irregular in this paradigm because of their suppletive negatives established earlier: yáuh's question form is yáuh-móuh (not *yáuh-m̀h-yáuh, reusing Unit 6's suppletion), and the auxiliary yiu/need's question form is sái-msái (reusing Unit 20's msái suppletion) — both genuine morphological irregularities that override the regular A-not-A template.

Replies to A-not-A questions standardly REPEAT THE VERB OR ADJECTIVE from the question (not a dedicated yes/no word): a positive reply repeats the bare predicate (A: Léih jūng-m̀h-jūngyi nīdouh a? B: Jūngyi a, "[I] like [it]" — NOT *haih, explicitly flagged again as the wrong strategy per Unit 7), while a negative reply adds m̀h before the repeated predicate. A separate question type uses meih ("not yet") appended to a statement containing jó or gwo, asking whether a completed/experienced event has occurred (Léih sīk-jó dāng meih a? "Have you turned off the lights?"), and this type is answered either by repeating verb+aspect-marker (+ la for emphasis) for "yes," or by meih alone (+ a for politeness softening) for "no." The particle a is described as near-obligatory across question types specifically for POLITENESS — its absence is explicitly characterized as making a question sound abrupt or impolite, a pragmatic/politeness-register fact that recurs through Units 24–27.

## Unit 24 — Wh-questions

Unlike English, Cantonese wh-words (bīn "which," géi "how [much/many]," dím(yéung) "how," bīngo "who," dímgáai "why," bīndouh "where," géisìh "when," géi dō "how many") stay IN SITU — in the same clause position the corresponding answer phrase would occupy — rather than fronting to the start of the sentence. The book illustrates this systematically with statement/question pairs showing the wh-word slotting into subject, object, or adverbial position exactly where a full answer would go. "How" and "why" specifically land between subject and verb (mirroring where a manner/causal adverbial phrase would sit in the corresponding statement), while dímgáai "why" ALSO has the option of fronting to sentence-initial position as an alternative. A fine semantic distinction is drawn between the two "why"-type expressions: jouh m(tyéh (literally "do what?") asks about PURPOSE/motivation behind an action, while dímgáai (literally "how to explain?") asks about REASON/cause — not interchangeable despite both glossing loosely as "why" in English.

géi combines productively with adjectives/adverbs to build specific question phrases (géi loih "how long [duration]," géi dō "how many/much," géi dō seui "how old [literally how many years]," géi dím [jūng] "what time"), and bīn combines with the noun's classifier (Unit 8) rather than a generic question-word slot (Léih jūngyi bīn fūk (wá) a? "Which [picture] do you like?" — fūk is the picture-classifier, not a separate "which" morpheme). The formula Chéng mahn ("May I ask...") is flagged as the standard politeness strategy for prefacing a wh-question directed at a stranger — paralleling the particle a's politeness function for yes/no questions in Unit 23, and together forming a coherent politeness-marking system across question types.

## Unit 25 — Sentence particles

The book calls sentence particles "one of the most challenging features of Cantonese for learners" and states there are roughly THIRTY in active use, of which only a core handful are introduced here (a, m%%, ge, jē, lā, lē). Without a particle, the book states, many otherwise-grammatical Cantonese sentences "sound incomplete, abrupt, or even impolite" — meaning particle choice is not optional stylistic decoration but load-bearing for register and politeness, a point the book makes forcefully. a is characterized as the most basic, most neutral particle, softening the force of statements and (per Units 23–24) marking politeness in questions. m%% marks a SPECIFIC question type expressing surprise/the unexpected (Léih m̀h jī m%%? "Don't you know?!") and the book issues an explicit false-friend warning: m%% should NOT be confused with the superficially similar Mandarin question particle ma, "which has no real counterpart in Cantonese" — i.e., these are NOT cognates functioning the same way across the two varieties, despite near-identical romanized spelling. ge appears in assertions (often paired with haih, reprising Unit 7's emphatic haih...ge construction); jē downplays the significance/extent of something (Géi baak mān jē "It's just a few hundred dollars"); la adds a sense of current relevance, compared explicitly by the book to Mandarin's aspectual le (though "rather less widely used" than its Mandarin counterpart — an explicit cross-dialect frequency/usage comparison); lā and ē are primarily imperative/request markers (Units 26–27), with lā the more insistent of the two and ē more neutral.

Particles STACK productively — three or more in a single utterance is presented as normal, not exceptional (Kéuih haih síuhohk-sāang lèihge jē wo! "She's only a primary-school student, you know!" — four particle-like elements chained). The book advises learners NOT to try to memorize particle-combinations as fixed phrases but to learn each particle's individual contribution, since combinations compose predictably from the parts. A specific PHONOLOGICAL FUSION is documented: ge + a contracts to ga when a statement ending in ge is turned into a question (N( go bougou haih kéuih sé ge "It was him who wrote this report" → ...sé ga? in question form, NOT *...sé ge a? — the fused form is obligatory, the unfused sequence is explicitly marked ungrammatical). This ge+a→ga fusion, and the parallel lèihge+a→lèihga fusion, are genuine instances of productive morphophonological contraction among grammatical particles — a concrete, well-documented data point for anyone doing phonology-of-grammaticalized-particles work in this project's grammar-logic track.

## Unit 26 — Imperatives

Cantonese imperatives, unlike English, typically RETAIN the subject pronoun (Léih bōng-sáu lā "Help me," literally "you help") rather than dropping it — the book frames pronoun-dropping in commands as possible (mainly in emergencies) but LESS usual than in English and generally LESS polite when it does occur, an explicit politeness-register inversion relative to English norms. A sentence particle (lā or ē) is grammatically required to distinguish an imperative reading from a plain declarative statement with the same word order — without one, the sentence simply reads as a statement, not a command. Politeness is layered on with mgōi ("please") at the start or end of the sentence (cross-referencing Unit 27). A specific lexical note: bōng (literally "help") is frequently used in imperatives not to mean "help" in the literal sense but to mark that the addressee is doing something FOR the speaker's benefit (Léih bōng ngóh sé lā "Write it for me," NOT "help me write it" — the book explicitly flags this as a common source of learner misunderstanding). Adjectival imperatives require the particle dī (literally "a little," reusing the comparative-dī morpheme from Unit 12) inserted before the adjective (Léih síusām dī lā! "Be careful!").

Negative imperatives (prohibitions) use mhóu ("don't," literally "no good") between subject and verb, with the pronoun freely omissible here (unlike in positive imperatives) — OR the alternative marker máih, which the book characterizes as more ABRUPT/impatient than mhóu and appropriate specifically for close relationships (a register distinction between two near-synonymous prohibition markers worth flagging). First-person-plural imperatives ("let's...") optionally retain ngóhdeih "we" and are often softened/invited with the adverb bātyùh ("rather," as in "why don't we...") — and negate with the same mhóu strategy as second-person prohibitions.

## Unit 27 — Requests and thanks

Polite requests layer mgōi ("please") onto an imperative, at either edge of the sentence, still requiring the particle ē/lā to avoid sounding abrupt (reprising Unit 26's particle requirement). Invitations specifically use chéng ("invite"), distinct from request-mgōi, and Chéng mahn ("May I ask") — already introduced in Unit 24 for wh-questions — is cross-referenced here as the general polite-question-opener.

The book devotes significant attention to disambiguating TWO distinct Cantonese words for "thank you" — mgōi and dōjeh — which it states "cause some difficulty for the English speaker since the precise distinction... is not easily drawn," compounded by the fact that mgōi ALSO means "please," creating a genuine polysemy trap. The rule of thumb given: mgōi is used for small, immediate SERVICES rendered (someone opens a window, hands you something, serves food) — an acknowledgment of an ACTION just performed for you — while dōjeh is used for GIFTS and unexpected favors, including metaphorical "gifts" like compliments and congratulations (a compliment prompts dōjeh, not mgōi, because praise is treated as something bestowed rather than a service performed). Both intensify with saai ("all," → mgōi saai / dōjeh saai, "thank you very much"), and both are deflected with msái ("no need") or msái haakhei ("don't be so polite/formal") as the standard reply-to-thanks. A cultural-politeness note on modesty is flagged: compliments are conventionally DEFLECTED rather than simply accepted (A: "You look great today!" B: Mhaih aak, "Not really" — explicitly glossed by the book as "traditional Chinese modesty," a named cultural-pragmatics norm worth cross-referencing for this project's broader politeness-register work).

Apologies split into (at least) three distinct expressions keyed to the SEVERITY and TYPE of offense, a register gradient the book lays out explicitly: deui-mjyuh (literally "cannot face [someone]") is the general, all-purpose apology covering everything from minor bumps to major offenses, and can take the wronged party as an object either after the whole phrase or infixed between deui and mjyuh; mhóuyisi (literally "[I'm] embarrassed," reprising the lexicalized-negative item from Unit 14's vocabulary) is reserved for lighter matters — misunderstandings, minor lapses, running late due to circumstance; and mgōi je-gwo is a narrowly specialized fixed phrase used only for excusing oneself while physically pushing through a crowd. This three-tier apology-register system (general/all-purpose vs. minor/circumstantial vs. narrowly situational) is a clean, explicit politeness taxonomy directly from the source, worth flagging for this project's register-mapping work.

## Unit 28 — Numbers, dates and times

The number system is decimal and very regular, which the book notes "has been claimed to give the Chinese an advantage in mathematical tasks" (reported as a claim, not asserted as established fact). **A striking, explicitly documented numerological/taboo fact anchors this unit**: the number four (sei) is subject to active taboo because it is a near-homophone of séi "die," while two (yih, homophone-adjacent to "easy") and especially eight (baat, rhyming with faat "prosper/make money," as in the New Year greeting gūnghéi faat chòih) are treated as LUCKY. The book gives a concrete, real-world commercial example: the Alfa Romeo "164" model was renumbered "168" specifically for the Hong Kong market because "164" (yāt luhk sei) sounded uncomfortably close to yāt louh séi ("one road [to] death"), while "168" (yāt luhk baat) reads as yāt louh faat ("one road [to] prosperity"). This is a genuine, sourced instance of phonetic taboo avoidance shaping real commercial/consumer behavior — a strong data point for this project's taboo-avoidance-naming research track (the same phenomenon category flagged previously in the Russian and Korean Phase 1 work).

Two separate words for "two" are grammatically conditioned: yih is used in counting/reading numerals as such (dates, serial numbers), while léuhng is used together with a classifier when quantifying actual items (léuhng go yàhn "two people") — with yih and léuhng both acceptable in a narrow overlap zone (round hundreds/thousands/ten-thousands directly before a noun). Large numbers above 10,000 are counted in units of maahn (ten-thousand) rather than continuing in units of chīn (thousand) as English does — i.e., Cantonese numeral grouping is base-10,000-chunked above a certain threshold rather than base-1,000-chunked, a structural numeral-system fact worth flagging for anyone doing numeral-typology comparison. Dates and addresses both follow a general-to-specific ordering (year→month→day; and for addresses, the broadest administrative unit first) that the book explicitly connects back to the same general-before-specific word-order tendency already noted for superlatives (Unit 12) and topic structure (Unit 22) — presented as a recurring, unified principle of Cantonese constituent ordering rather than three unrelated facts. Clock times under ten minutes past the hour insert lìhng ("zero") between the hour and minute count (7:04 = chāt dím lìhng sei fān), and colloquial time-telling can alternatively use jih ("five-minute clock-face divisions," e.g., "two jih" = :10) instead of counting minutes directly — a colloquial-register alternative numbering system for sub-hour time, distinct from the formal minute-count method.

## The book's own Glossary of Grammatical Terms (synthesis)

The volume closes with a short glossary defining the metalanguage it uses throughout (adjective, adverb, antonym, aspect, auxiliary, classifier, demonstrative, digraph, diphthong, experiential, hanging topic, localizer, measure, minimal pair, modal/modality, negation, particle, passive, perfective, predicate, preposition, pronoun, topicalization, transitive verb). Two definitions are worth flagging verbatim-in-substance because they crystallize concepts used repeatedly across this book's units: "aspect" is defined as the grammatical distinction between an event viewed as complete (Cantonese -jó) versus ongoing (English -ing, Cantonese -gán) — i.e., the book's own glossary explicitly frames jó/gán as the Cantonese analogs of the perfective/progressive distinction, not as tense; and "hanging topic" is defined as a topicalization instance where "the topic is not subject or object of the verb, but bears a loose relation to" one of them — directly matching the three-way hanging-topic typology given in Unit 22. The glossary also explicitly defines "particle" by DISTINGUISHING two particle subclasses by their syntactic POSITION: verb particles (like dóu, marking completion of an action) attach immediately after the verb, while sentence particles (like a, added to questions for politeness) attach at the very end of the sentence — a structural distinction that organizes Units 17 (verb particles) and 25 (sentence particles) as two genuinely different grammatical subsystems rather than one undifferentiated "particle" category, which is a useful organizing fact for this project's own cross-language particle-taxonomy work.

## Overview

Killingley opens by carefully distinguishing terminology that is often used loosely in the literature: *Yue* properly names the whole regionalect (language historically tied to a region of China, here the Pearl River delta and parts of Guangxi), while *Cantonese* more narrowly names its most prestigious, widely studied member, and *General Cantonese* is her own term for the language as an abstraction spanning mainland, Hong Kong, and diaspora varieties (she draws heavily on her own Malayan-Cantonese native-speaker intuitions for comparison throughout). She stresses that Yue sub-varieties are not mutually intelligible in the way two accents of English are, that speaker counts are little better than informed guesswork (estimates in the tens of millions in China plus several million more across Southeast Asia and other diaspora communities), and that "Standard Cantonese" is itself not a single homogeneous target but conflates at least Canton-city and Hong Kong norms that have been diverging.

The phonology section sets up a deliberately economical system for citation purposes: eight vowel phonemes whose phonetic diphthongs are reanalyzed phonologically as vowel plus semivowel /j/ or /w/ (so a traditional four-diphthong inventory becomes, e.g., /iw, ɛj, œj, ɐj, ɐw, uj, ow, ɔj, aːj, aːw/), and nineteen consonant phonemes including unaspirated/aspirated plosive pairs, a labialized velar series, and syllable-final unreleased stops with no accompanying very-low-level tone. Syllable structure is stated as (C1)V(C2) with tone riding over the whole syllable, where C2 can only be an unreleased, unlabialized stop, nasal, or semivowel; /m/ and /ŋ/ can themselves be syllabic. A long-running scholarly dispute over how many tones Cantonese has (proposals in the literature range from five to eleven) is resolved in the book by adopting five lexical tones plus a separate, non-contrastive "neutral tone" category for toneless weak/particle syllables, on the grounds that the traditionally cited sixth ("low rising") tone is better analyzed as a phonetic variant of the mid-level tone. The author's own numbered-tone romanization (tones 1-5, citation examples like *fan1* "divide," *fan2* "powder," *fan3* "sleep," *fan4* "a share," *fan5* "tomb") is presented as a refinement of a system she originally devised in 1968, chosen for typographic ease over marking tone contours directly.

Before turning to morphology proper, Killingley flags four recurring analytical traps in Chinese/Cantonese word-study: treating disyllabic words as if each syllable still carried independent meaning when the whole has semantically "fossilized"; letting the character-based writing system force a spurious "meaning" onto syllables that are really just sound-carriers; the historical phenomenon of "loan-characters" (a picturable character borrowed for an unrelated homophonous abstract word, later disambiguated by adding a radical); and the outdated assumption that Cantonese is purely monosyllabic. Nominal morphology is then laid out as a short list of compounding and affixation patterns: noun-root compounds, noun+adjective-root compounds, conjunct-verb-stem+noun compounds, a genitive suffix with a neutral-tone allomorph in attributive position and a full-tone allomorph elsewhere, a vocative/familiar prefix *aa3-* (e.g. *aa3cing1* as a name-based term of address), reduplication of kinship roots for affectionate/nursery registers (*po5po2* "Granny," *maa5maa1* "Mummy"), and several derivational suffixes forming agentive, locative, diminutive, and abstract nouns. Classifiers are described as either sortal (individuating by kind, roughly comparable to natural gender) or mensural (individuating by quantity/grouping, comparable to English partitive and collective expressions), with full reduplication of a classifier yielding a distributive "every X" meaning.

Pronouns are shown to carry the same genitive allomorphy as nouns, with a singular/plural split for personal pronouns (plural marked by a suffix) alongside a separate general/specific distinction for the three impersonal pronouns. A notable pragmatic pattern is that Cantonese speakers often prefer the plural second-person pronoun, or a kinship/honorific address term, over the specific singular "you" with strangers or superiors, functioning similarly to English "yourself" softening "you." The honorific/kinship system lexicalizes patrilineal versus matrilineal lines asymmetrically (different root words rather than a shared root plus "paternal/maternal" modifier) and offers parallel formal, familiar, and nursery registers for the same relationship, with cardinal numerals sometimes prefixed to kinship terms to mark birth order among same-type relatives. Adjectives have a reduced two-term inflectional paradigm (plain vs. comparative, via two conditioned allomorphs of a comparative suffix) but freely reduplicate for intensity and take an "-ish" suffix when reduplicated from a color root.

Verbal morphology is the most elaborate section: nearly every verb (except the equative "be" and existential "have," which cannot be aspected at all — a gap partly plugged by a near-synonym that can) takes up to two stacked aspectual suffixes drawn from a long paradigm the author enumerates in thirteen named categories (progressive, durative, perfective, initial/limited-participation, abilitative, two kinds of achievement-of-goal, experiential, iterative, four-way completive, inchoative, and reciprocal-resumptive), with aspect normally suffixed but infixed between the two halves of disyllabic verb compounds and of the institutionalized "conjunct verb" pattern (a transitive-root-plus-object compound, like "wash+bath" for "to bathe," that behaves as a single intransitive verb and resists the passive transformation that its homophonous verb-plus-object counterpart allows). A further layer of directional-resultative suffixes (two ordered classes, roughly "out/in/up/down" vs. "towards/away") can stack after aspect. Negation is a simple prefix, but interrogation is formed by an infixed "A-not-A" reduplication pattern, and the book catalogues four distinct syntactic strategies for forming a yes/no question (interrogative infix, a separate interrogative modal, an aspect-specific interrogative word, or a clause-final question particle), each pairing with a different required answer form. Adverbs are shown to derive productively from nouns and adjectives via tone modification, partial or total reduplication, or dedicated suffixes.

The syntax chapter covers relative-clause formation (pre-head, not post-head as in English, via a particle that is morphophonemically identical to the genitive suffix, with recursive right-most movement of the particle through nested modifiers), reduplication/coordination patterns for noun phrases, modal-plus-adjective/verb phrase structure (including the genuinely tangled distribution of the equative verb and its homophonous modal), clause structure organized around predicate valency (zero through triple), and a range of voice phenomena where Cantonese diverges sharply from English intuition — notably that an impersonal passive with the generic agent "someone" is often the more natural, idiomatic choice over the corresponding active clause (used, for instance, as gentle warnings to children), and that causative-resultative compounds built on the root "die" (e.g., "beat to death," "laugh to scorn") show genuine structural ambiguity between active-agent and passive-patient readings of their surface object. The book closes with clause-chaining patterns (open-ended coordination via a recursive conjunction, paired-conjunction constructions, and several types of subordination) and a short bilingual sample text, a retelling of the traditional fable "The Cat and the Tiger" (in which the cat, traditionally said to be the tiger's uncle, teaches the tiger every hunting skill except how to climb a tree, saving itself when the ungrateful tiger later tries to kill and eat it), offered as an example of more colloquial, conversational register against the formal expository prose used elsewhere in the grammar.

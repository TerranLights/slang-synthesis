**Source:** Dana Scott Bourgerie, Keith S.T. Tong, and Gregory James. *Colloquial Cantonese: The Complete Course for Beginners*, 2nd edition (Routledge, 2010). Continuation of extraction from `030a`/`030b`; see `030a` for source/methodology notes. Romanization: Yale system. This file closes out the 15-unit course and covers the book's back matter.

## Unit 11 — Giu yéh sihk (Ordering food)

Unit 11 covers ordering at a noodle shop, a dímsam tea-house, and a fast-food counter, plus stating food preferences. The book draws out a venue-conditioned register split for "asking for the bill": a small noodle shop uses Mgdi tái-sou! ("Please check the amount" — the waiter calls the total to the cashier directly), while a tea-house or restaurant with proper billing uses Mgdi màaih-daan! ("Please close the bill"). The idiom heui yám-chàh (lit. "go-drink-tea") is explained as actually meaning "go have dímsam at a tea-house," not literally just drinking tea — a case of a literal-sounding phrase carrying an idiomatic extension.

For stating preferences, the book notes Cantonese has no direct equivalent of "I prefer A to B" and lays out three paraphrase structures using béigaau ("comparatively"), dd-dc (preferred item stated once, comparison implied), and dd-gwo (both items named, comparison explicit) — the same dc/-gwo distinction already seen for price comparison in Unit 6, here extended to taste preference. The particle tcm ("in addition," always sentence-final) and the pre-verbal dd ("more/additional") are shown as two different grammatical slots for suggesting an extra order.

## Unit 12 — Tcnhei (The weather)

Unit 12 covers weather description, broadcast forecasts, giving advice, dates, and festive greetings. Its most salient register finding: Cantonese weather forecasts are written in formal Chinese and read aloud, so they systematically substitute two-syllable "bookish" expressions for everyday one-syllable colloquial weather words — yiht "hot" → yìhmyiht, nyúhn "warm" → wannyúhn, dung "cold" → hòhnláahng, lèuhng "cool" → chcnglèuhng, sap "humid" → chìuhsap, gdn "dry" → gdnchou, hóutcn "fine" → tcnchìhng, yamtcn "overcast" → tcnyam. This is a clean, book-stated colloquial/formal-written doublet system worth preserving as paired rows. The unit also covers giving advice with jeui hóu ("had better"), yiu ("should"), mhóu ("don't"), and msái ("no need to"), and introduces daaihga ("everyone"), used characteristically in broadcast public address.

Festive greetings are taught as fixed idioms (Singdaan faailohk "Merry Christmas," Sannìhn faailohk "Happy New Year," Saangyaht faailohk "Happy birthday," Yatlouh seuhnfeng "Have a good flight," Genghéi faatchòih — a set Chinese New Year greeting). The Cultural point describes Hong Kong's tropical-cyclone warning-signal system.

## Unit 13 — Ycfuhk (The clothes we wear)

Unit 13 covers clothing vocabulary (men's wear, ladies' wear, casual wear, warm clothes) and colors, plus a notable grammatical quirk: Cantonese does not have one single verb for "to wear/put on" — jeuk is the general verb (for shirts, suits, skirts, shoes), but taai ("tie") specifically takes dá, móu ("hat/cap") takes daai, and génggan ("scarf") takes laahm (lit. "to wrap around the body"). Dress-code evaluation uses the resultative particle dak after jeuk plus an adjective (jeuk dak hóu scmàhn "very smartly dressed," jeuk dak hóu chèuihbín "very casually dressed," jeuk dak hóu jíngchàih "very neatly dressed").

A register note: when clothing is used to identify a person in a crowd ("the one wearing the suit"), the aspect marker -jó and classifiers are dropped entirely (Jeuk sAijDng gó go haih ngóh bàh-ba), whereas when clothing is reported as a completed, assertable fact about someone, -jó is used (Mary jeuk-jó tou toujdng). The Cultural point covers Hong Kong's textile-export economy and its tailoring tradition, noting many tailors are from the local Indian community.

## Unit 14 — Léuihhàhng gcngyihm (Traveling experiences)

Unit 14 teaches discussing past travel experience with the experiential aspect marker -gwo (distinct from the completive -jó introduced in Unit 6): -gwo marks "have you ever..." type experience (Néih heui-gwo Jenggwok meih a? "Have you been to China?"), while -jó is used once the experience is already established in the conversation and the focus shifts to new completed-action detail (néih heui-jó géinoih a? "how long were you there?"). The verb taam ("to visit," human objects only) and the noun jauwàih ("surroundings") and yáuh láihmaauh ("to have good manners," i.e. "polite") round out the vocabulary for describing travel impressions of a country (fenggíng "scenery," wàahngíng "environment," gaauteng "transportation," hóusihk "delicious").

## Unit 15 — Dá-dihnwá (On the telephone)

Unit 15 covers telephone conventions, invitations, and mobile-phone plans. Two loanword/register items stand out. First, baai-baai ("bye-bye") is explicitly flagged as borrowed directly from colloquial English, contrasted with the native, more formal-register joi gin ("see you again," lit. "again see"); the book notes that despite being a loanword, baai-baai still has to "abide by the rules of Cantonese" tonally. Second, Dialogue 4 (mobile-phone plans) shows live code-switched/borrowed tech vocabulary not seen elsewhere in the book — WiFi used as-is, and gaaiwahk ("plan") and chúhjik kaak/chúhjik kaat ("prepaid card") as the domesticated Cantonese terms for modern telecom concepts.

The aspect marker -háh ("delimitative," indicating a brief/short action, e.g. tái háh "take a quick look") is introduced, along with batyùh ("why don't we...") for suggesting activities and the pattern Mjc...nb ("I was wondering whether...") for invitations. The closing Cultural point covers Cantonese/Chinese numerology: the digit baat "eight" is prized in phone numbers and license plates because it sounds close to faat ("prosper"), while sei "four" is avoided because it sounds close to séi ("death") — extending even to gift-giving customs (never giving four of anything).

## Overall flagship findings for this source

1. **Explicit colloquial-vs-formal/written pairs stated directly by the book**, the strongest kind of register evidence for this project: jengyi (colloquial) vs héifen (formal/Mandarin-shared) for "to like" (Unit 3); dímjeng/gwat/jih (colloquial spoken time units) vs sìh/haak (written-Chinese equivalents) (Unit 4); and the systematic one-syllable-colloquial vs two-syllable-"bookish"-forecast doublets for weather adjectives (Unit 12) — yiht/yìhmyiht, dung/hòhnláahng, nyúhn/wannyúhn, lèuhng/chcnglèuhng, sap/chìuhsap, gdn/gdnchou.
2. **The reduplicated-adjective-plus-déi intensity pattern** (sau-sáu-déi, fèih-féi-déi, lóuh-lóu-déi), explicitly labeled by the book as a colloquial-Cantonese-specific construction with its own tone-change rule (Unit 5).
3. **Colloquial number contraction**: y'ah, sa'ah, sei'ah, etc. for the decades (Unit 5), and the colloquial dropping of hòuhjí/go-jih units in casual price- and time-telling (Units 4, 6).
4. **A borrowed-but-tonally-naturalized English loanword, baai-baai**, contrasted directly against the native-register joi gin (Unit 15) — alongside live, unassimilated tech borrowings (WiFi) in the same dialogue, giving two different loanword-integration stages side by side.
5. **Taboo/superstition-driven numeral avoidance**: baat "eight" (lucky, homophone of "prosper") vs sei "four" (unlucky, homophone of "death"), extending into real social practice (phone numbers, license plates, gift-giving) — the clearest taboo-register item in this source (Unit 15).

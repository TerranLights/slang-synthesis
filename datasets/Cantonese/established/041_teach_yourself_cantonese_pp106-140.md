# Cantonese — Established Vocabulary: *Teach Yourself Cantonese* (PDF pp. 106-140)

**Source:** *Teach Yourself Cantonese* (Hodder & Stoughton "Teach Yourself" series). Image-only PDF
scan, 346 pages total, no text layer. All 35 pages in this range (PDF pp. 106-140) were rendered at
150 DPI and read via vision; no OCR or transcript was used. No handwritten marginalia was found
anywhere in this range — every page is clean printed book content.

**Page range covered:** PDF pp. 106-140 span the tail of Unit 10 ("Health care for beginners",
printed pp. 90-91, exercises only), the complete Unit 11 ("sih-jong — The world of fashion", printed
pp. 92-101), the complete Unit 12 ("gaau-yuhk — Education for life", printed pp. 102-113), and the
complete Unit 13 ("tauh-gei — Speculation", printed pp. 114-123), ending on the divider page for
Unit 14 ("wan-jaahp (yih) — Revision (2)", printed p. 124, no running text yet). Because the range
crosses four distinct course units with no single title covering it, the filename keeps the generic
page-range suffix per the task instructions rather than adopting one unit's name.

**Format:** each unit follows the same internal shape — a dialogue in Chinese characters plus Yale
romanization, a vocabulary box, numbered grammar points, a cultural sidebar, and practice exercises.
This extraction draws vocabulary-table entries from all of those components, and the prose below
covers what stood out as genuinely register/slang-relevant across the four units rather than
re-narrating the whole grammar syllabus.

## Fashion unit: judgment language and a vague-location grammar point

Unit 11's shopping dialogue (a hat-buying scene) supplies mostly neutral retail vocabulary (**chit-
gai** "design", **sàn-fún** "new style", **daaih-fōng** "tasteful/sophisticated"), but its real
register payoff is grammar point 6, **hái-syu/hái-douh** "at the indicated place". The book is
explicit that this construction is used loosely to mean "the place we both already know about" —
the same phrase can mean "here" or "there" depending entirely on shared context between speaker and
listener, and when it splits to surround a noun it deliberately smears the relationship into
"in/on/at/near" without committing to which. That's a genuine pragmatic-vagueness device worth
flagging: it is a built-in hedge, not a gap in the learner's vocabulary. The unit also nails down
three distinct verbs for "to wear" split strictly by object class — **jeuk** (clothing proper),
**daai** (worn accessories: hats, glasses, watches, jewelry, gloves), **dá** (things that must be
tied on, like a necktie) — a clean example of a semantic field the English translation collapses
but Cantonese keeps obligatorily distinct.

## Education unit: a flagged slang phrase and a culturally loaded tier split

Unit 12's grammar point 8 (**yíh-wàih**, "to assume/think — usually something that turns out to be
wrong") is where the book itself flags genuine slang: it explicitly calls out **Néih yíh-wàih lā!**
as "a very slangy expression," glossing it as "You reckon!", "That's what you think!", "Think again,
pal!" — i.e. a dismissive retort to someone's mistaken assumption. This is a rare case of the source
naming its own register tier, so it's recorded here as `slang` rather than inferred. The unit's
colour vocabulary is also worth a note for the attached cultural sidebar: red carries the dominant
positive association (happiness/luck, bridal wear) while white is reserved for funerals, and the
book notes that "brown" (**ga-fē-sīk**, literally "coffee-color") is a visibly recent loanword-style
coinage layered onto an older scheme that classified actual browns under **hùhng** (red-ish) or
**wòhng** (yellow-ish) — e.g. a brown cow is **wòhng-ngàuh**, tan shoes are **hùhng-sīk**. That's a
color-term-boundary mismatch worth keeping on file for any future derived-conlang color system.

## Speculation unit: the project's richest register content in this batch

Unit 13 ("tàuh-géi", speculation/gambling) is the standout for slang-synthesis purposes. The whole
unit is built around Hong Kong's historically documented gambling culture (horse racing, mahjong,
stock-market betting, and — since cross-border trips to Macau became easy — casino games), and the
vocabulary reflects a dense, productive **dóu-** ("to gamble on X") compounding family: dóu-máh
(horses), dóu-gáu (dogs), dóu-pē-páai (cards), dóu-gú-piu (stocks), dóu-chín (gambling with money
generically), dóu-bō (betting on football — explicitly glossed by a character in-dialogue as
specifically English soccer), dóu-ngoih-wuih (betting on foreign exchange), and dóu-Fāan-tāan
(playing fantan). Two outliers break the dóu- pattern on purpose: mahjong is **dá-Màh-jeuk** ("to
hit/play mahjong") rather than a dóu- form, because — per the cultural sidebar — mahjong is treated
as more of a skill game than a pure-luck bet, while fantan (a stone-counting pure-luck game) keeps
the dóu- form. The unit also surfaces **daaih-dong**, glossed flatly as "illegal gambling den," and
marks it explicitly as illegal in-dialogue (contrasted with licensed racetrack/casino betting) — a
clean technical/underworld-adjacent term worth tracking. **Yah-yāt-dím** "21, blackjack/pontoon" is
shown as a calque-like compression of "21 spots" that the book ties to a broader colloquial
numerical-clipping pattern: in the 20-99 range, numbers like yih-sahp-yāt ("21") get freely slurred
to yih-ah-yāt, then yah-yāt, then even yeh-yāt in fast/casual speech, but — the book is explicit —
this shortcut is blocked for the bare round numbers (20, 30, 40...90), which must stay in full form.
That's a genuine phonological-reduction register rule, not free variation. A second, unrelated
ellipsis pattern surfaces in grammar point 10: the numeral **yāt** ("one") is routinely dropped when
it sits between a verb and a classifier+noun (dá yāt chèuhng Màh-jeuk → dá chèuhng Màh-jeuk; sihk
yāt chàan faahn → sihk chàan faahn; máaih yāt ga chē → máaih ga chē) — a productive colloquial
clipping that any derived slang layer should be able to reproduce.

## A cross-unit word-formation pattern: hóu + verb

Grammar point 8 of Unit 13 names a genuinely productive derivational template that recurs informally
elsewhere in the book: **hóu** ("good") plus a verb yields a new adjective meaning, loosely, "good to
[verb]" — hóu-sihk "good to eat" = delicious, hóu-yám "good to drink" = delicious, hóu-tái "good to
look at" = attractive, hóu-tèng "good to listen to" = melodic, and the unit's own dialogue coinage
hóu-wáan "good to play/enjoy" = fun. The book explicitly invites the learner to coin further hóu-
compounds beyond the fixed set it lists, making this the clearest attested productive slang/register
mechanism in this page range — a strong candidate for the `analysis/` layer as a `metaphor_
productivity`-adjacent or `morphological_play` derivation type.

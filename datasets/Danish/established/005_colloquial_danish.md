# Danish — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Colloquial Danish (full book, Units 1–18 + Ready-reference grammar)

**Source:** W. Glyn Jones and Kirsten Gade, *Colloquial Danish: The Complete Course for Beginners*
(Routledge Colloquial series, 2003, ISBN 0-415-30179-3). Full book: all 18 units plus the
"Ready-reference grammar" appendix. The end-of-book "Key to exercises" and the English–Danish /
Danish–English glossaries were deliberately skipped as redundant recap of material already captured
per-unit (see `00_Reference_Extraction_Spec.md`'s coverage rule — exercises/drills and duplicate
word lists are explicitly out of scope).

**Coverage note:** every grammar point and every distinct vocabulary item from all 18 units, plus
every explicit dialectal/regional/register annotation the source itself marks. Repeated drill
exercises and duplicate example sentences that don't introduce new vocabulary or grammar are
skipped. This source has a clean, confirmed text layer (`pdftotext -layout`) — no vision-reading was
needed, so Vision Reading Confidence is `n/a` throughout.

**Flagship finding — this is the single richest source of explicit register/slang annotation found
anywhere in this project's Danish corpus so far.** The project's Danish triage found no dedicated
slang/idiom title in the whole stash, making this Colloquial series entry (true to its Norwegian and
Swedish siblings' track record in this project) the most valuable source of register-marked material
for Danish. Specific highlights, all flagged `taboo`/`colloquial`/`formal` in the Vocabulary table
below with the book's own register label quoted in the Notes column:

- **Unit 18's "Disagreeing and swearing" section (pp. 210-211) is a direct, asterisk-marked
  coarse-vs-harmless catalog of Danish swearwords and expletives** — the book explicitly marks which
  expressions are "considered coarse by most people" (*) vs. "pretty harmless," covering `Hold kæft!`
  "Shut up!", `lort` "shit" (vulgar) vs. `skidt` "muck/dirt" (mild, despite looking similarly rude),
  the vulgar-by-compounding `skidedum`/`skidetosset`, and an escalating blasphemy-based
  pain-exclamation ladder (`Av for katten!` mild → `Av for Fanden/Satan/Helvede!` coarse). The book
  also notes Danish swearing is structurally blasphemy-based rather than obscenity-based (unlike
  English), and that borrowed English four-letter words have been defanged of their vulgar force in
  Danish usage.
- **`sørme`/`Det var sørens!` (Units 4 and 9) are each explicitly labeled by the book as "a mild
  expletive...not felt to be a swearword."**
- **Unit 8's five-tier politeness ladder for requests** (bare imperative → `+lige` → `vil du ikke
  lige` → `+være så venlig at` → `gider du lige`), with the LAST, most casual tier explicitly marked
  as "mainly heard in casual talk among people who know each other well" — not the most formal, as
  its position last in the list might suggest.
- **Unit 7's two explicit, numbered intensity ladders for expressing liking and disliking**
  (neutral → "a more emotional expression" → "stronger expressions").
- **Unit 1's explicit Formal/Informal/Neutral three-column table of greeting and farewell phrases**
  (`Goddag!`/`Dav(s)!`, `Farvel!`/`Hej hej!`/`Vi ses!`, etc.).
- **`fuld`, flagged as an explicit false-friend/taboo trap**: saying `Jeg er fuld` about food (rather
  than `Jeg er mæt`) is understood as "I'm drunk," not "I'm full" (Unit 9).
- Scattered genuinely colloquial/slang-register vocabulary throughout: `gide` ("can't be bothered"),
  `Hva'?` (clipped, explicitly "not considered particularly polite but widely used among people who
  know each other"), `hvad for en/et/nogle` (register-lighter alternative to `hvilken`), teen-register
  relationship vocabulary (`kæreste`, `slå op med`, `komme sammen med`), and `nogenlunde`/`dødtræt`/
  `sjusket` from the "Min chef" office-gossip reading texts (Unit 16).

## Grammar points

### Unit 1 — Word stress

The general rule: Danish words carry main stress on the first syllable, but a visible minority don't (e.g. *englænder*, *sekretær*, *turist*, *København* are stressed on a later syllable, marked in the book with an apostrophe before the stressed syllable). p. 5.

### Unit 1 — Indefinite articles and the no-article rule for identity nouns

`en`/`et` are the two indefinite articles ("a"/"an"); which one a noun takes marks its grammatical gender (elaborated in Unit 2). Nouns of nationality, occupation, or religion drop the article entirely when they stand alone as a predicate (*Han er dansker* "He is a Dane", not *en dansker*). p. 4.

### Unit 1 — Personal pronouns (subject forms)

Singular: jeg/du(De)/han/hun/den(det). Plural: vi/I(De)/de. `den` tracks common-gender nouns, `det` tracks neuter-gender nouns. `De` (capitalized) is the formal 2nd person, book notes it is now mainly restricted to older speakers and business contexts — a live formal/informal register split the book flags explicitly. p. 7.

### Unit 1 — Present tense (Nutid)

The vast majority of Danish verbs form the present tense by adding `-r` to the infinitive, with a single invariant form regardless of person/number (no subject-verb agreement at all). Danish also collapses the English simple/progressive distinction ("I speak" = "I am speaking") into this one present tense. Irregular present-tense verbs are flagged in the glossary with their full principal-parts set in brackets, e.g. `have (har, havde, haft)`, `være (er, var, været)`. p. 8.

### Unit 1 — Pronunciation: æ, ø, å and spelling-pronunciation mismatch

Introduces the three extra Danish letters æ/ø/å with example words. Flags one specific spelling-pronunciation mismatch: `også` ("also") is pronounced as though spelled "osse" despite its spelling. p. 5.

### Unit 1 — Negation

Danish sentences are negated by inserting `ikke` directly after the finite verb (*Jeg hedder Ida* → *Jeg hedder ikke Ida*). p. 6 (drawn from Exercise 2's own worked example, not a separately labeled Language point, but a real rule the book states directly).

### Unit 2 — Noun genders and articles in the singular

Danish nouns have two genders, common and neuter: `en` marks indefinite common-gender nouns, `et` marks indefinite neuter-gender nouns (*en by* "a town" vs. *et land* "a country"). The definite article is not a separate word but a suffix attached to the noun (`-en`/`-et`): *byen* "the town", *landet* "the country". Gender is not predictable from a noun's meaning, but the book notes ~75% of Danish nouns are common gender, making it the safer default guess. If a noun stem already ends in `-e` (e.g. *kone* "wife"), only `-n`/`-t` is added for the definite form (*konen*), and some nouns require doubling a final consonant in the definite form (e.g. *søn* → *sønnen*). p. 15.

### Unit 2 — Two question-word patterns: identification vs. location

`Hvad er ... ?` ("What is ...?") is used purely to identify something and takes a gender-neutral `Det er ...` answer. `Hvor ligger ... ?` ("Where lies/is ...?") is used for location and does require gender agreement in its `Den ligger .../Det ligger ...` answer. `Det ved jeg ikke` ("I don't know") is a standard gender-neutral fallback answer to either question type. p. 14.

### Unit 2 — Word order: subject-verb inversion after a fronted element

Basic word order is subject-verb, as in English (*Jeg bor her* "I live here"). But any question word (*hvem/hvor/hvordan/hvorfor/hvornår*) triggers subject-verb inversion, with no equivalent of an English dummy "do" auxiliary (*Hvor bor du?* "Where do you live?", literally "Where live you?"). More generally, inversion happens whenever ANY element other than the subject occupies the front of the main clause — not just question words — including a fronted adverb (*Her bor jeg* "I live here", lit. "Here live I") or a fronted subsidiary clause (*Mens vi er i København, vil vi gerne handle lidt* "While we are in Copenhagen, we would like to shop a little"). The book formalizes this with its own F/v/n/Other four-column template (Front - verb - subject(n) - Other), used throughout the rest of the book as its running word-order notation; this is "verb-second" (V2) word order, though the book itself never uses that linguistic term. p. 22-23.

### Unit 2 — h silent before j and v

`h` is silent before `j` and `v`, the same way English `w` is silent in "who": *hjem* "home", *hjul* "wheel", *hvem* "who", *hvad* "what", *hvordan* "how", *hvorfor* "why", *hvornår* "when", *hver* "every", *hvis* "whose". p. 18.

### Unit 2 — i vs. på for geographic location

`i` is used for countries and mainland regions (*i Danmark*, *i Jylland*), while `på` is used for islands (*på Sjælland*, *på Fyn*, *på en ø*) — a preposition split that doesn't map onto English "in"/"on" usage. p. 17.

### Unit 3 — Numbers above twenty (vigesimal remnants)

Danish counting from 50-99 is built on an old base-20 (vigesimal) system, unlike the straightforward decimal tens of English. *Halvtreds* (50), *halvfjerds* (70), and *halvfems* (90) are historically contractions of "half-third/fourth/fifth times twenty" (i.e. 2.5×20, 3.5×20, 4.5×20) — the book presents the forms without deriving them etymologically, just as vocabulary to memorize. `en`/`et` ("one") is the only number affected by noun gender, since it doubles as the indefinite article. p. 26.

### Unit 3 — Plural indefinite of nouns

Most nouns add `-e`, `-r`, or `-er` for the plural indefinite, but irregular plurals are common enough that the glossary convention gives the plural form as the noun's *second* bracketed principal part (the first being the definite-singular ending that signals gender), e.g. *æble (-t, -r)* "apple" → indefinite plural *æbler*. A single dash in that slot means the plural is identical to the singular (*løg (-et, –)* "onion"); a fully irregular plural is spelled out in full (*gulerod (-en, gulerødder)* "carrot"). p. 28-29.

### Unit 3 — Plural definite of nouns

The plural definite article is a suffix, `-ne` (the default, so common the glossary doesn't bother flagging it) or `-ene` (the glossary explicitly flags this variant), attached directly to the plural-indefinite form — never a separate word before the noun as in English. p. 29-30.

### Unit 3 — Yes/no questions, and ja vs. jo

Yes/no questions are formed purely by subject-verb inversion, with no change to the verb form itself — the same F/v/n/Other-plus-"Others" schema from Unit 2 is extended with an explicit "Answers" column. The key register/logic point: when the question itself contains a negative element (*aldrig* "never", *ikke* "not"), the correct affirmative answer is **not** `ja` but `jo` — `jo` specifically contradicts a negative question/statement, a three-way yes/no/contradicting-yes system that has no single-word English equivalent (English just reuses "yes" for both cases). p. 32-33.

### Unit 4 — Ordinal numbers

Ordinals 1-6, 11, and 12 are irregular/suppletive (*første, anden, tredje, fjerde, femte, sjette, ellevte, tolvte*); from 7 upward (except those irregulars), ordinals are formed regularly by adding `-nde` or `-ende` to the cardinal number (*syv* → *syvende* "seventh"). p. 36.

### Unit 4 — Three verb groups: past tense and past participle formation

Danish verbs fall into three classes for past-tense formation. **Group 1** (largest, fully regular): add `-de` for past tense, `-t` for past participle to the infinitive stem (*koste* → *kostede* → *kostet*); the glossary doesn't bother listing these principal parts since they're fully predictable. **Group 2** (regular but a different pattern, principal parts given in the glossary by convention): *høre* → *hørte* → *hørt*. **Group 3** (irregular, principal parts given): *sige* → *sagde* → *sagt*; *komme* → *kom* → *kommet*; *vinde* → *vandt* → *vundet*. A small subset of Group 3 verbs are ALSO irregular in the present tense, in which case the glossary bracket gives present-past-past.participle in that order (*være*: er/var/været; *have*: har/havde/haft). The past participle is also the building block for the present perfect (*har fortalt* "has told") and past perfect (*havde ringet* "had rung") — full treatment deferred to Unit 5. p. 38-41.

### Unit 4 — Danish family/life-event celebration customs (cultural, not purely linguistic)

The book flags several Danish cultural specifics tied directly to vocabulary it's teaching: *juleaften* (Christmas Eve, not Christmas Day) is the day Danish children actually celebrate, with the tree lit and presents opened that evening. Major "round" birthdays (*rund fødselsdag*: 50th, 60th, etc.) are celebrated; the 21st birthday, significant in Anglophone culture, is explicitly "not an important occasion in Denmark." *Konfirmation* (confirmation, ~age 14) is a near-universal coming-of-age party milestone regardless of a family's actual religious practice. p. 43.

### Unit 4 — `sørme` flagged by the book as a mild, non-swearword expletive

The book's own usage note on `sørme` (used in *Jeg vil sørme ha' en ordentlig fest* "I'm jolly well going to have a proper party") states explicitly: "'Sørme' is a mild expletive and is not felt to be a swearword." This is the book's own direct register classification, not an inference — recorded as `usage_tier: colloquial` in the Vocabulary table above, flagged here because it's exactly the kind of explicit register annotation this extraction pass is watching for. p. 43.

### Unit 5 — Body parts: definite article vs. possessive

Unlike English, which uses a possessive with body parts ("mind your head"), Danish normally uses the definite article instead (*Pas på hovedet!* "Mind your head!", lit. "Mind the-head!"; *Du har nok taget dem i lommen* "You've probably put them in your/the pocket"). The possessive is used only when the body part itself is the grammatical subject of the sentence (*Hendes ansigt er smukt* "Her face is beautiful"). p. 51.

### Unit 5 — Present perfect and past perfect: har vs. er as the auxiliary

Most Danish present-perfect verbs use `har` + past participle, matching English "have" + past participle. But a present perfect can instead use `er` + past participle when the main verb BOTH (a) takes no object (intransitive) AND (b) denotes a change of state or motion — e.g. *Peter er flyttet til Hamborg* ("Peter has moved to Hamburg", flytte=move, no object, motion), *Han er blevet tysker* ("He has become German", blive=become, intransitive, change of state), *Er han rejst?* ("Has he left?", rejse=travel, intransitive, motion). The past perfect is formed the same way, just substituting `havde`/`var` for `har`/`er`. p. 52.

### Unit 5 — Finite vs. non-finite verbs, and the expanded word-order template

A finite verb shows tense (present/past); a non-finite verb (infinitives, past participles) does not. The book expands its running word-order schema from Units 2-3 (F/v/n/Other) by adding a dedicated `V` column for non-finite verb forms, sitting after the negation-slot `a` column: **F – v – n – a – V – Other**. Example: *Hun er ikke kørt fra København* "She has not driven from Copenhagen" slots as F=Hun, v=er, a=ikke, V=kørt, Other=fra København. p. 54.

### Unit 5 — først vs. English "first": a partial false friend

Danish *først* sounds like English "first" but carries an additional, non-overlapping sense: "not until." *Han kommer først i næste uge* is genuinely ambiguous out of context between "He will be here at the beginning of next week" and "He won't be here until next week" — the book presents both readings side by side as equally valid without disambiguating further. p. 56.

### Unit 5 — Apology register: formal/polite vs. family-colloquial

The book draws an explicit three-way register ladder for apologizing/reassuring: `Undskyld`/`Det må du/I undskylde`/`Det er jeg (meget) ked af` for the apology itself; `Det gør ikke noget!`/`Det gør ikke spor!` as "neutral and polite" acceptance; and `Skidt (være) med det!` as the markedly more casual variant the book says you'll hear specifically "from family and friends." p. 59.

### Unit 6 — Adjective forms: basic, t-form, e-form

Most Danish adjectives have three forms: basic (*stor* "big"), t-form (*stort*), e-form (*store*). Exceptions: `-sk` adjectives don't add `-t` (*dansk* stays *dansk*); unstressed-`-e` adjectives never change (*moderne*); short-vowel+consonant adjectives double the consonant in the e-form (*langsom* → *langsomme*); `-t`-ending adjectives don't double in the t-form (*træt* stays *træt*); many `-d`-ending adjectives drop the expected `-t` (*glad* stays *glad*, but *god* → *godt* is a counter-exception); *lille* "small" is irregular with two e-forms (*lille* or *små*). Adjectives ending in `-el/-en/-er` are deferred to Unit 14. p. 62-64.

### Unit 6 — When to use which adjective form

Basic/t-form: before an indefinite singular noun (even with the article omitted) — common gender takes basic, neuter takes t-form (*en dejlig computer*, *et gammelt hus*); also when the adjective is predicative, following the noun it describes, regardless of definiteness (*Computeren er dejlig*, *Huset er gammelt*). E-form: everywhere else — after `den/det/de` marking definiteness (*den store bil*), with plural nouns (*Store huse er dyre*), after a pronoun (*vores dejlige have*), after a genitive (*Pamelas travle dag*). *Lille*'s plural form is always *små*, whether modifying a plural noun or standing alone referring to one. p. 64-65.

### Unit 6 — The four modal verbs and the expanded word-order template

The four main Danish modals are *kunne* (ability/possibility), *måtte* (permission/obligation), *ville* (volition/future), *skulle* (obligation/plan), each taking a bare infinitive (no *at*). The word-order template gains an `N` column (for the verb's object/complement), sitting after the non-finite-verb `V` column: **F – v – n – a – V – N – Other** (*Du må gerne låne bilen i dag* → F=Du, v=må, a=gerne, V=låne, N=bilen, Other=i dag). p. 70.

### Unit 6 — kunne for ability/possibility, måtte for permission/prohibition

*Kunne* + bare infinitive expresses ability/possibility (*Det kan jeg gøre* "I can do that"). *Måtte* + bare infinitive, usually with `godt`/`gerne` inserted, asks or grants permission (*Må jeg (godt) tale med Anna?* / *Ja, det må du godt* "Yes, you may"); replacing `godt`/`gerne` with a negative turns the same construction into a prohibition (*Du må ikke bruge min computer* "You must not use my computer"). p. 67-70.

### Unit 6 — Expressing wishes: ville gerne, and the special particles gid/bare

*Ville* + *gerne* + infinitive expresses a wish/polite request, in either present (*vil*, a more easily fulfillable wish) or past tense (*ville*, a less likely one): *Jeg vil gerne have et rugbrød* "Could I have a rye loaf, please." Danish additionally has two dedicated wish-introducing particles, *gid* and *bare*, each of which can open a wish sentence on its own (*Gid de kommer!* "I wish they'd come!"; *Bare jeg var kommet lidt senere!* "I wish I had come a little later!"). p. 71-72.

### Unit 6 — `at gå` vs. `at tage`/`at rejse` for "to go"

The book flags a specific lexical-choice rule as an aside in Exercise 4: *at gå* means specifically "to go on foot," while "to go" by any other means of transport uses *at tage* or *at rejse* instead — Danish doesn't have one generic "go" verb covering all travel modes the way English does. p. 68.

### Unit 7 — A graded intensity scale for likes/dislikes (explicit register ladder)

The book presents an explicit, numbered intensity ladder rather than a flat list of synonyms. **Liking**, weakest to strongest: (1) neutral — *kan lide*, *er glad for*, *er meget/vældig glad for*; (2) "a more emotional expression" — *holder af*; (3) "stronger expressions" — *elsker*, *er vild med*. **Disliking**, weakest to strongest: (4) neutral — *kan ikke lide*, *er ikke glad for*, *bryder mig ikke om*; (5) "stronger expressions" — *kan ikke fordrage*, *hader*. This is the book's own explicit three/two-tier register scale, not an inference, and is one of the clearest pieces of register-graded vocabulary found anywhere in this source. p. 76.

### Unit 7 — The imperative

Formed by dropping the `-e` from the infinitive (*at prøve* "to try" → *prøv!* "try!"); infinitives with no `-e` to begin with are unchanged (*at se* → *se!*); a double-consonant infinitive simplifies to single in the imperative (*at komme* → *kom!*, *at drikke* → *drik!*). A bare negative imperative like *Græd ikke!* "Don't cry!" is grammatical but "sounds a bit poetic" — the book states Danes normally prefer *Lad være med at + infinitive* instead (*Lad være med at græde!*). The infinitive-minus-`-e` form is also called the verb's "stem." p. 79.

### Unit 7 — The genitive: -s suffix, not 's

Danish forms the genitive simply by adding `-s` to any noun form, with no apostrophe (*Martins computer*, *børnenes værelser* "the children's rooms", *Shakespeares liv* "Shakespeare's life"); nouns already ending in `-s` add just an apostrophe or `'s` (*Niels'* or *Niels's*). The book notes Danish uses this s-genitive far more freely than English does, including in contexts where English would reach for a prepositional phrase (*Amerikas opdagelse* = "the discovery of America," though *opdagelsen af Amerika* also works since this phrase doesn't refer to a person). p. 81.

### Unit 7 — Possessive adjectives/pronouns: gendered min/din vs. invariant -s forms

Danish does not distinguish possessive adjectives from possessive pronouns — same word serves both ("my kitchen" and "the kitchen is mine" both use *mit*). `min`/`din` (and their neuter/plural forms `mit`/`dit`, `mine`/`dine`) inflect for the gender/number of the possessed noun (*min have* vs. *mit køkken* vs. *mine venner*). All the other possessives — those ending in `-s` (*hans, hendes, dens/dets, vores, jeres, deres*) — are invariant regardless of the possessed noun's gender. `Deres` is the formal possessive, used only when addressing someone as `De`. p. 83-84.

### Unit 7 — sin/sit/sine: a reflexive possessive disambiguating ownership

`sin/sit/sine` means "his/her/its own," but — unlike `hans`/`hendes` — it is grammatically restricted to cases where a 3rd-person-singular subject of the same clause owns the thing being referred to. *Jørgen arbejder i sin have* = Jørgen works in his OWN garden; *Jørgen arbejder i hans have* = Jørgen works in someone else's (his father's, etc.) garden. This ownership-disambiguating distinction has no single-word English equivalent. p. 84.

### Unit 7 — Danish kinship terms: lexically split by maternal/paternal line, with looser colloquial collapses

Danish has no single word for "grandmother" — it's always specified as *farmor* (paternal) or *mormor* (maternal), and likewise for grandfather (*farfar*/*morfar*), aunt (*faster*/*moster*), and uncle (*farbror*/*morbror*) — all transparent compounds of `far`/`mor` + the kin term. The book notes two colloquial loosenings that cut against this precision: `søskende` ("siblings") is used freely where English speakers often default to "brothers and sisters," and `tante`/`onkel` are often used generically for either maternal or paternal aunt/uncle, blurring the farmor/mormor-style distinction that the base vocabulary otherwise insists on. p. 85-86.

### Unit 8 — A five-tier politeness ladder for requests (explicit, numbered)

The book builds a direct, numbered 1-5 scale from rudest to most elaborate for turning an imperative into a polite request, all built around "shut the door": (1) bare imperative *Luk døren!* ("can easily sound rude"); (2) + softener *lige*: *Luk lige døren!*; (3) *Vil du ikke lige* + bare infinitive: *Vil du ikke lige lukke døren?*; (4) + *være så venlig at*: *Vil du/De ikke lige være så venlig at lukke døren?* ("if you really want to make a good impression"); (5) *Gider du (ikke) lige* + infinitive: *Gider du (ikke) lige lukke døren?*, explicitly marked as "a form mainly heard in casual talk among people who know each other well" — i.e. tier 5 is the most casual/intimate-register option, not the most formal, despite being last in the list. This is one of the most explicit, source-internal register ladders found anywhere in this book. p. 89.

### Unit 8 — Three overlapping ways to express necessity

*Skulle* + bare infinitive is the primary way (*Du skal!* "You must!"). *Blive nødt til* + *at*-infinitive is "more or less interchangeable" (*Vi bliver nødt til at hjælpe farmor*). *Måtte* + bare infinitive can also express necessity, "not least when stressed" (*Du må lære at bruge en computer!*) — distinguishing it from its more common permission sense from Unit 6. p. 91.

### Unit 8 — Expressing will/volition with ville, scaled by stress/emphasis

*Ville* + bare infinitive expresses desire/intent, and the book notes its force is gradient, not binary: heavier emphasis on *vil* signals a stronger personal will (*Jeg vil ikke nordpå!* "I don't want to go north!"), while a lightly-stressed *vil* shades toward plain future tense (*så vil jeg tage til Norge* "then I'll go to Norway"). p. 92.

### Unit 8 — gide: a dedicated verb for "can't be bothered"

*Gide (gider, gad, gidet)* expresses lack of enthusiasm/inclination and is normally used negatively or in a question (*Jeg gider ikke tage i Tivoli* "I can't be bothered going to Tivoli"); once a negative/question usage has been established in context, it can then be used affirmatively with *godt* to mean "wouldn't mind" (*men jeg gider godt køre til Sverige* "but I wouldn't mind going to Sweden"). Its antonym-ish counterpart for positive inclination is *have lyst til* + infinitive/noun. p. 97.

### Unit 8 — Danish letter-writing conventions

Covers the full informal-letter format: town + comma + date in the top-right corner (two accepted date formats, numeric or spelled-month); salutation *Kære* or *Hej* + name + exclamation mark; sign-offs graded by warmth (*Kærlig hilsen* "Love from" > *Venlig hilsen* "Yours sincerely" > *Mange hilsener* "Best wishes"); book notes Danish letter-writers don't generally use kiss-crosses (X's). Envelope conventions: optional titles (*Hr./Fru/Frk./Fr.*, increasingly dropped) or occupational titles (*Direktør, Gartner*) before the full name; house number AFTER the street name (reverse of English); area code BEFORE the town name; floor number after the street address, with *th* (til højre, "to the right") or *tv* (til venstre, "to the left") appended for which door on a landing. p. 93-95.

### Unit 8 — Det-fronting with verbs of knowing/thinking/feeling

Danish has a strong tendency to front `det` ("it/that") even when it's logically the object, in set expressions built on verbs like *vide* "know", *tro* "think", *gide*, *have tænkt på* "have thought about", *kunne mene* "can mean": *Det ved jeg ikke* "I don't know", *Det tror jeg ikke* "I don't think so", *Det gider jeg ikke* "I can't be bothered", *Det har jeg ikke tænkt på endnu* "I haven't thought of that yet," *Det kan du ikke mene* "You can't mean that." p. 95-96.

### Unit 8 — Skulle/kunne in the past tense for tentative suggestions

Using the PAST tense of *skulle*/*kunne* (rather than present *skal*/*kan*) to make a suggestion signals more reticence/tentativeness on the speaker's part — directly parallel to English "shouldn't we" (vs. "shan't we") and "couldn't" (vs. "can't") carrying the same softening effect. *Skulle vi ikke gå i biografen?* / *Kunne Martin ikke erstatte dig?* p. 98.

### Unit 8 — Declension of nouns ending in -er

A sizeable class of `-er`-ending nouns (mostly nationality words or verb-derived agent nouns) forms its plural with `-e`, but then DROPS that same `-e` again when the definite article is attached: *en dansker* → def. sg. *danskeren* → indef. pl. *danskere* → def. pl. *danskerne*. Same pattern for *lærer* and *bager*. p. 99.

### Unit 9 — Danish table-manner/dining customs tied to specific vocabulary

The book ties several cultural customs directly to the vocabulary it teaches: self-service at table (reaching across is normal and expected, hence the frequent need for *Undskyld jeg rækker over*); the ritual priority of *skål* — no one drinks wine until the host raises a glass and says it first, after which individuals may toast each other freely; the ritualized *Tak for mad* / *Velbekomme* exchange at the end of every meal, used even within the family, not just with guests; and a second, unrelated use of *Velbekomme* as a greeting to people already eating when you pass or enter their space (acknowledged with a plain *Tak*, without pausing the meal). p. 102-104.

### Unit 9 — mæt vs. fuld: a food-register false-friend trap

Danish *mæt* ("full, had enough to eat") is the normal, polite word — unlike English, where "I'm full" can sound blunt, *Jeg er mæt* is unmarked and fine. But the literal word-for-word translation of English "I've had enough," *Jeg har fået nok*, can be misread as a complaint. More critically, *fuld* is a false friend: although it means "full" in other contexts, saying *Jeg er fuld* about food is read as "I'm drunk" — the book gives this as an explicit, flagged warning, not just a vocabulary note. p. 104.

### Unit 9 — Det as impersonal/formal subject, with a der-exception for distance

`Det` serves as a formal/dummy subject much like English "it" (*Det er smukt* "It is beautiful"), and any adjective agreeing with this `det` must take the neuter t-form (per Unit 6's adjective-form rules). The one systematic exception: expressing distance uses `der` instead of `det` (*Der er 38 km fra Køge til København* "It is 38 km from Køge to Copenhagen"). p. 109.

### Unit 9 — turde (dare) + bare infinitive

*Turde (tør, turde, turdet)* + a bare infinitive, optionally with *godt*, expresses what the subject dares/doesn't dare do (*Hun tør godt sejle* "She does dare sail"; *Hun tør ikke flyve* "She doesn't dare fly"). The word-order template is extended with a final `A` column for prepositional-phrase adverbials (preposition + nominal), placed after the `N` (object) column: **F – v – n – a – V – N – A**. p. 109-110.

### Unit 9 — The discourse particle jo: shared/already-known information

A second, distinct sense of `jo` (separate from Unit 3's negative-question-contradicting `jo`) appears here as a mid-sentence discourse particle, roughly "you know" or "of course," signaling the speaker treats the information as already mutually known/agreed (*der er jo tunnellen under Kanalen* "there's the Channel Tunnel, you know"). p. 109.

### Unit 9 — desværre / Jeg er bange for as regret-softeners

*Desværre* (lit. "unfortunately") and *Jeg er bange for* ("I'm afraid") both function as English "I'm afraid/sorry that..." softeners before declining or admitting a limitation (*Jeg kan desværre ikke komme* / *Desværre kan jeg ikke komme* "I'm afraid I can't come" — word order flexible, *desværre* can front or stay medial). p. 110.

### Unit 9 — Danish eating-schedule and meal-structure customs (cultural)

The Reading text lays out concrete cultural specifics tied to vocabulary: breakfast is typically bread+cheese or cereal, often skipped/rushed on weekdays; Sunday morning specifically involves a bakery trip (bakeries open at 7, sell both bread and the Sunday papers); lunch is almost never eaten at home — children bring a packed lunch (*madpakke*) to school, parents eat in a work canteen; the lunch break falls between 12-1 (explicitly contrasted with England's 1-2); dinner (hot food) is the one meal eaten together, traditionally around 6, though the book notes sport and TV have made it "difficult to gather the whole family" for this anymore. p. 111.

### Unit 10 — burde: moral duty, with a past-tense-for-unmet-duty twist

*Burde (bør, burde, burdet)* + bare infinitive expresses moral obligation. The book flags a subtlety: the PAST tense form *burde* is specifically used when the obligation is NOT being met — i.e. criticizing someone for falling short of a duty (*Du burde selv gå foran med et godt eksempel!* "You ought to lead by example [but aren't]"), distinct from present-tense *bør* for a straightforward ought-statement. p. 114.

### Unit 10 — The passive infinitive: active -e → passive -s

Danish transitive verbs have a dedicated passive infinitive form, built by adding `-s` to the active infinitive (*vaske* "wash" → *vaskes* "be washed"; *feje* "sweep" → *fejes* "be swept") — English has no equivalent single-word form and must periphrase with "to be" + past participle. Like the active infinitive, the passive infinitive combines directly with modal verbs (*Bilen skal vaskes* "The car must be washed," mirroring *Jørgen skal vaske bilen*). p. 116-117.

### Unit 10 — Colour compounding: in-between, light, and dark shades

Danish builds "in-between" colour terms simply by concatenating two base colour names (*blågrøn* "blue-green," *gråbrun* "grey-brown"); lighter shades take the prefix `lyse-` (*lysegul* "light yellow"); darker shades take `mørke-` (*mørkerød* "dark red"). p. 117.

### Unit 10 — have vs. være when asking about colour

A quirk the book flags explicitly: Danish can use either *have* ("have") or *være* ("be") when ASKING what colour something is (*Hvilken farve har/er en mus?*), but must use only *være* when STATING a colour (*Den er grå*, never *Den har grå*) — English uses "is" uniformly in both directions, so this asymmetry has no English analogue to lean on. p. 118.

### Unit 10 — hvilken vs. hvad for en: a formality split on "which/what"

`Hvilken/hvilket/hvilke` ("which/what," agreeing in gender/number) is grammatically correct but "felt to be a bit heavy" in everyday use — the book notes it's commonly replaced by the lighter-feeling `hvad for en/et/nogle`, again agreeing in gender/number with the noun. This is a register/weight distinction on an interrogative determiner, not a meaning difference. p. 118.

### Unit 10 — Object pronouns, and the special status of sig

Danish object pronouns (*mig, dig/Dem, ham, hende, den/det, os, jer/Dem, dem*) are used for direct objects, indirect objects, and after prepositions — generally a 1:1 match with English "me/you/him" etc., with the one systematic exception that Danish always says *Det er mig* ("It is me"), never a nominative-case equivalent of "It is I." `Sig` is the odd one out: it's NOT a plain object pronoun but a dedicated 3rd-person reflexive (see next point), with *dem* only otherwise serving as the object form of *De*. p. 120-121.

### Unit 10 — Reflexive pronouns, and Danish's much wider reflexive-verb coverage

Reflexive pronouns match the object pronouns except in the 3rd person, where Danish uses the dedicated `sig` (singular and plural) instead of *ham/hende/den/det/dem* — critically distinguishing *Nina slog sig* "Nina hurt herself" from *Nina slog hende* "Nina hurt her [someone else]," a distinction English "herself" vs. "her" also makes, but Danish extends this reflexive marking to many verbs that English treats as plain intransitives with NO reflexive pronoun at all: *vaske sig* "wash (oneself)," *barbere sig* "shave," *sætte/lægge sig* "sit/lie down," *forholde sig roligt* "keep calm," *kede sig* "be bored," *skynde sig* "hurry," *gifte sig* "get married." The indeclinable intensifier `selv` can be appended to any reflexive pronoun purely for emphasis (*Jeg skar mig selv* "I cut myself, and nobody else"). p. 121-123.

### Unit 10 — morgen vs. formiddag: a two-way split of "morning"

Danish splits what English covers with one word, "morning," into two: *morgen* covers roughly up to 9:30-10:00am, after which Danish switches to *formiddag* ("forenoon" — the book notes this maps precisely onto a word Scots English has but standard English lacks). This has a knock-on greeting effect: Danes say *Godmorgen* only up to ~9:30, switching to *Goddag* after that (reinforcing the Unit 1 note that *Godmorgen* isn't used after 10am). p. 124.

### Unit 11 — Ten special movement/position adverbs, and their der-/her- combination

Danish has 10 adverb pairs with no single-word English equivalent, split into a "short" movement form and a "long" position form: op/oppe, ned/nede, ud/ude, ind/inde, hen/henne, over/ovre, frem/fremme, hjem/hjemme, om/omme, bort/borte. Standing alone, the short form marks movement (*Han gik ud* "He went out") and the long form marks static position (*Han er ikke hjemme* "He is not at home"). Combined with a preposition, the distinction sharpens further: the SHORT form + preposition marks movement FROM one area INTO another (*Han gik ned i skolegården* "He went down into the playground" — crossing a boundary), while the LONG form + preposition marks an action happening WITHIN a single already-specified area (*Han går nede i skolegården* "He's walking around down in the playground" — no boundary crossing). All ten can further combine with `der-` ("there") or `her-` ("here") prefixes (*derop* "up there," *herhjemme* "here at home"). p. 127-129.

### Unit 11 — Four Danish verbs for English "think"

The book gives a disambiguation table for English "think," which Danish splits four ways by sense: (a) *tænke* — exercise the mind, think ABOUT something (*Jeg tænker på at blive læge* "I'm thinking of becoming a doctor"); (b) *synes* — hold an opinion (*Jeg synes, det er en god ide* "I think it's a good idea"); (c) *tro* or *mene* — believe (*Jeg tror, jeg vil have et friår* "I think I'll have a gap year"); (d) *forestille sig* — imagine, form a mental picture (*Jeg kan ikke forestille mig noget mere spændende* "I can't think of anything more exciting"). p. 129-131.

### Unit 11 — Short answers: det + repeated verb, or det + gøre

Danish short answers always front with `det`, unlike English's bare "yes, I have." For questions built on *have*, *være*, or a modal (*kunne/skulle/ville/måtte*), the short answer repeats that same verb after `det` (*Har du en øl? → Ja, det har jeg*; *Må jeg få den? → Nej, det må du ikke!*). For any OTHER verb, the short answer substitutes *gøre* ("do") instead of repeating the original verb, still fronted by `det` and matching its tense (*Vasker du tit din bil? → Ja, det gør jeg*; *Vaskede du bilen i går? → Nej, det gjorde jeg ikke*). The same pattern extends to `hvem`-subject questions (*Hvem betalte? → Det gjorde jeg*). p. 132-133.

### Unit 11 — Word order in subordinate clauses: n/a/v reorder, F disappears

Subordinate clauses (introduced by words like *som, der, fordi, når, da, hvis*) reorder the main-clause template. The main-clause **F – v – n – a – V – N – A** template loses its `F` slot entirely (nothing can be fronted in a subordinate clause) and gains a new initial `C` slot for the subordinating conjunction itself; critically, `n` (subject) now comes BEFORE `v` (finite verb), and `a` (negation/adverb) comes between them: **C – n – a – v – V – N – A** (*fordi han ikke spiste frokost hjemme* "because he didn't eat lunch at home"). `V`, `N`, and `A` keep their original relative order. This is the book's clearest explicit statement of the V2-vs-subordinate-clause word-order split (again without ever naming it "verb-second" as a concept). p. 133-134.

### Unit 12 — many/few vs. much/little vs. fewer/less vs. more/most: a strict countable/uncountable split

Danish maps the English "many/much," "fewer/less," "more/most" sets onto a rigorously separate countable-vs-uncountable vocabulary, more consistently than English does: countables take *mange/få* ("many/few"), *færre/færrest* ("fewer/fewest"), *flere/flest* ("more/most"); uncountables take *meget/lidt* ("much/a little"), *mindre/mindst* ("less/least"), *mere/mest* ("more/most"). English "more"/"most" cover both categories with one word each; Danish never does (*mere regn* "more rain" vs. *flere biler* "more cars" — mixing them up is a genuine error, not just stylistically off). p. 141-142.

### Unit 12 — Countability mismatches between Danish and English

Some nouns cross the countable/uncountable line between the two languages. Singular-in-English-but-plural-in-Danish: *kontanter* "cash," *møbler* "furniture," *oplysninger* "information," *penge* "money" (from Unit 3). Plural-in-English-but-singular-in-Danish: *indhold* "contents," *løn* "wages," *saks* "scissors," *trappe* "stairs" (the Unit 11 word for "staircase," reused here). This directly determines whether a given sentence needs *mere/mest* or *flere/flest* with these specific nouns, contrary to what their English countability would predict. p. 143.

### Unit 12 — Comparison of adjectives: five patterns

(1) Default: `-ere`/`-est` added to the basic form (*stærk* → *stærkere* → *stærkest*). (2) `-som`-ending adjectives double the final consonant in the comparative (*langsom* → *langsommere* → *langsomst*). (3) `-ig`-ending adjectives drop the comparative's middle `-e` in the superlative (*hurtig* → *hurtigere* → *hurtigst*, not *hurtigerest*). (4) `-et`-ending and unstressed-`-e`-ending adjectives (plus many 3+-syllable adjectives) use periphrastic *mere*/*mest* instead of suffixes (*moderne* → *mere moderne* → *mest moderne*); some of these additionally retain a dual suffixed form (*almindelig* → *almindeligst* OR *mest almindelig*). (5) A short irregular-comparison list: *lille→mindre→mindst*, *stor→større→størst*, *gammel→ældre→ældst*, *ung→yngre→yngst*, *lang→længere→længst*, *god→bedre→bedst*, *dårlig→værre→værst*. p. 144-146.

### Unit 12 — No definite article with ship/geographic proper names (with exceptions)

Danish omits the definite article before the names of ships and many geographical terms where English requires "the": *Titanic* (not *Titanic'en*), *Volga*, *Antarktis*. A subset of geographic names buck this and carry the definite article built directly into the name instead: *Themsen* "the Thames," *Nordsøen* "the North Sea," *Atlanterhavet* "the Atlantic" — these aren't exceptions to a rule so much as names that lexicalized the article as part of the proper name itself. p. 148.

### Unit 13 — Compound nouns: three joining patterns, gender from the last element

Danish routinely compounds words that English keeps as separate two-word phrases (*regionaltog* "regional train"). Three joining patterns for noun+noun compounds: (1) direct concatenation — *plads* "seat" + *billet* "ticket" = *pladsbillet*; (2) linking `-s-` — *vindue* "window" + `-s-` + *plads* "seat" = *vinduesplads* "window seat"; (3) linking `-e-` — *gæst* "guest" + `-e-` + *værelse* "room" = *gæsteværelse* "guest room." Compounds can also be verb+noun or adjective+noun (*vente* "wait" + *tid* "time" = *ventetid* "waiting time"). In every case, the COMPOUND'S gender is inherited from its LAST component, not the first: *en pladsbillet* (because *billet* is common gender) but *et gæsteværelse* (because *værelse* is neuter), regardless of the first element's own gender.

> **julegave** = jul ("Christmas") + e (linking vowel) + gave ("present") — the unit's own title example, demonstrating the `-e-` linking pattern.
> **vinduesplads** = vindue ("window") + s (linking consonant) + plads ("seat") — demonstrates the `-s-` linking pattern and the compound's gender (common, from plads) overriding vindue's own neuter gender.

p. 151.

### Unit 13 — Telling the time: forward-counting halv, and klokken's optional omission

Danish counts the half-hour FORWARD toward the next hour, not backward from the current one: *halv ti* means "half past nine" (literally "half [of the way to] ten"), the exact opposite of what an English speaker might guess from "half ten" (which in Danish is *halv elleve*, "half past ten"). Minutes are expressed relative to either the hour or the half-hour, with minutes between :20 and :40 typically related to the half-hour rather than the hour. `Klokken` ("the clock," ≈ "at") can be dropped when stating minutes before/after an hour or half-hour, but must be kept for an exact hour (*Vi kommer klokken 4* but *Vi kommer ti minutter over 4*, no klokken). Danish has no a.m./p.m. markers, using *om formiddagen*/*om eftermiddagen* instead when needed. *Godt tolv* ("a good twelve") can ambiguously mean either "a little before" or "a little after" twelve. p. 154-155.

### Unit 13 — tage vs. ride vs. køre vs. gå for "go/travel"

For general travel by any vehicle, Danish uses *tage* + *med* (*Jeg tager altid med bus/tog/fly til Tyskland* "I always go by bus/train/plane to Germany"). *Ride* looks like English "ride" but is grammatically restricted to horseback riding only (*Han red til byen* "He rode [on horseback] to town") — riding a bike uses *køre* instead (*Han kørte på cykel* "He rode his bike"). *Gå*, per the Unit 6 note reiterated here, means only "to walk." p. 155-156.

### Unit 13 — Expressing agreement/disagreement with det + repeated verb

Mirroring Unit 11's short-answer pattern, agreement/disagreement with a preceding statement is expressed as *Det* + the SAME auxiliary/modal verb + *også* ("So do/can/will I") or + *heller ikke* ("Neither do/can/will I") — *Det gør/kan/vil jeg også* / *Det gør/kan/vil jeg heller ikke*, matching whichever verb (gøre substituting for a non-auxiliary main verb, exactly as in Unit 11) and tense the original statement used. p. 156.

### Unit 13 — Conditional hvis-clauses, with an inversion alternative

*Hvis* ("if") introduces a conditional clause normally (*Hvis man vil more sig, er der mange steder at tage hen*). Danish can also drop *hvis* entirely and signal the conditional purely through subject-verb inversion in the first clause — a construction with no equivalent in standard English (*Vil man more sig, er der mange steder at tage hen*, literally "Want one to enjoy oneself, there are many places to go," meaning the same as the hvis-version). p. 162.

### Unit 14 — Demonstratives: written denne/dette/disse vs. spoken den/det/de her/der

Two parallel demonstrative systems exist for the same near/far distinction. The "formal" set — *denne/dette/disse* ("this"/"these," near) vs. *den/det/de* + optional *der* ("that"/"those," far) — is grammatically primary but, the book notes, "more frequently used in written than in spoken language." In speech, Danes default instead to *den her/det her/de her* (near) and *den der/det der/de der* (far), tacking *her*/*der* directly onto the ordinary pronoun. This is a genuine written/spoken register split on a core grammatical category, not just a stylistic variant. p. 165.

### Unit 14 — -el/-en/-er adjectives and nouns: e-dropping before further suffixes

Adjectives (and non-stressed nouns) ending in `-el/-en/-er` drop that final `-e` before adding another inflectional ending: *simpel* → t-form *simpelt*, e-form *simple* (not *simpelet*); *åben* → *åbent* → *åbne*; *sikker* → *sikkert* → *sikre*. Comparison follows the same logic, with some retaining dual comparative/superlative options (*simpel* → *simplere* → *simplest* OR *mest simpel*). The corresponding noun class often has dual definite-singular/plural forms (*en aften* → def. sg. *aftenen* OR the e-dropped *aftnen*; pl. *aftener* OR *aftner*). p. 165-167.

### Unit 14 — kende vs. vide: two Danish verbs for English "know"

*Kende* = know in the sense of "be acquainted with" (a person, place, thing): *Kender du den restaurant?* "Do you know that restaurant?" *Vide (ved, vidste, vidst)* = know a FACT, typically introducing a clause: *Det ved jeg ikke* "I don't know (that)." The two are not interchangeable — using one for the other's sense is a genuine error, parallel to the French savoir/connaître or German wissen/kennen split. p. 168.

### Unit 14 — Graded preference vocabulary: vil gerne < vil hellere < vil (aller)helst

The book lays out an explicit three-step comparative/superlative preference ladder built on *ville* + adverb: *Jeg vil gerne have ...* "I'd like ..." (baseline) < *Jeg vil hellere have ...* "I'd RATHER have ..." (comparative, when choosing between two things) < *Jeg vil (aller)helst have ...* "I'd MOST of all like ..." (superlative, best of all options). A parallel ladder exists for *kan lide* "like": *kan godt lide* < *kan bedre lide*/*foretrækker* < *kan (aller)bedst lide*. p. 171.

### Unit 15 — nogen/noget/nogle: a three-way "some/any" split, collapsed in speech

Danish splits English "some/any" three ways by countability and polarity: `noget` ("some/any") precedes or implies an UNCOUNTABLE noun, and also stands alone meaning "something/anything" (*Har vi noget hostesaft?*; *Er det noget galt?*). `Nogle` means "some" with a COUNTABLE noun (*Jeg har nogle hovedpinetabletter*). `Nogen` means "any" with a countable noun, typically in negative/question contexts (*Vi har ikke nogen hovedpinetabletter*), and — unattached to any specific noun — means "somebody/anybody" (*Er der nogen hjemme?*). Crucially, the book notes the WRITTEN distinction between `nogen` and `nogle` collapses entirely in speech: both are pronounced identically, [noen] — a written-only distinction, invisible in the spoken language. p. 176-177.

### Unit 15 — Håbe + ikke: negation placement with "hope"

With *håbe* ("hope"), `ikke` goes directly after the comma/clause boundary, inside the subordinate clause, not where an English speaker might instinctively place it: *Jeg håber ikke, du har influenza* "I hope you haven't got flu" (literally "I hope not, you have flu") — flagged by the book as a position worth double-checking. p. 178.

### Unit 15 — No dedicated future tense: present tense does double duty

Danish has no distinct future-tense verb form at all — the ordinary present tense covers both present and future meaning, disambiguated purely by context or an explicit time adverbial: *Jeg ringer lige til ham* "I'll give him a ring [shortly]"; *Jeg kommer torsdag otte dage* "I'll be there a week on Thursday." This is a structural gap relative to English's dedicated "will" future, not just a stylistic preference. p. 183.

### Unit 15 — som vs. der for "who/which/that": der restricted to subject position

Danish uses only two relative pronouns — `som` and `der` — to cover English "who," "which," AND "that," with no person/thing distinction at all (unlike English who/which). `Som` is always valid; `der` is valid ONLY when it serves as the SUBJECT of its own relative clause (*Det er Karen, der har fortalt mig det* "It is Karen who's told me" — der is subject of "has told"). When `som` functions as an OBJECT within its clause, it can be dropped entirely, just as English can drop "that/which" (*Det er et syn, (som) jeg aldrig glemmer* "That's a sight (that) I'll never forget"). p. 184.

### Unit 16 — Question tags: ikke vs. vel

Unlike English's verb-matching tags ("isn't she?", "doesn't he?"), Danish uses just two invariant tag words: `ikke` after a POSITIVE statement (*Du er forelsket i Louise, ikke?* "You're in love with Louise, aren't you?") and `vel` after a statement with a NEGATIVE component (*Hun var ikke køn for to år siden, vel?* "She wasn't good-looking two years ago, was she?"). No verb-agreement complexity at all — purely polarity-triggered word choice. p. 187-188.

### Unit 16 — da vs. når for "when"

Past-tense "when" splits in two by repetition: a SINGLE past occasion uses `da` (*Louise var irriterende, da hun var yngre* "Louise was irritating when she was younger" — one continuous state, treated as a single reference point); a REPEATED/habitual past occasion uses `når` (*Når man mødte hende, var hun altid sur* "Whenever you met her, she was always sulky"). Present- or future-referring "when" always uses `når`, regardless of repetition. p. 189.

### Unit 16 — -t adverbial suffix, and u- adjective negation

Danish forms adverbs from adjectives with `-t`, corresponding to English `-ly` (*total* → *totalt* "totally," *Bukserne er totalt umoderne* "The trousers are totally out of date"). Separately, the prefix `u-` negates an adjective, roughly matching English `un-`/`in-`: *gift* "married" → *ugift* "unmarried," *rolig* "calm" → *urolig* "restless," *populær* → *upopulær*, *moderne* → *umoderne*. p. 191.

### Unit 16 — hel/selv/al/alle/begge: five distinct "all"-type quantifiers, each with its own countability rule

Unlike ordinary adjectives, these quantifiers don't trigger the definite article's usual forward shift. (1) `hel/helt/hele` ("all"/"the whole") — countable nouns only (*hele dagen* "the whole day"). (2) `selv`/`selve` ("-self"/"itself"/"even") — meaning flips with word order: *selve* BEFORE the noun = "the [noun] itself" (*Selve værelset er flot*); `selv` placed differently can mean reflexive "(she) herself" OR, preceding a noun, "even" (*Selv skufferne er i orden* "Even the drawers are tidy"). (3) `al/alt` ("all") — uncountable nouns only, works in both indefinite and definite form (*Al colaen* / *Al frugt*). (4) `alle` ("all") — countable nouns of three-or-more. (5) `begge` ("both," uninflected) — exactly two countable nouns; the Danish for "both...and" is *både...og*. p. 192-193.

### Unit 16 — sikke(n): an exclamatory "what a...!" construction

`Sikke` (optionally `sikken` before a common-gender noun, `sikket` before neuter) introduces admiring or surprised exclamations, with or without an indefinite article following: *Sikke(n) en flot kjole!* / *Sikken flot kjole!* "What a nice dress!"; it also works directly before an adjective or adverb with no noun at all (*Sikke smuk hun er!* "How beautiful she is!"; *Sikke længe de blev!* "What a long time they stayed!"). This cross-references and fulfills the Unit 9 promise of "more about sikke in Unit 16." p. 195.

### Unit 16 — Repeating a question: der-insertion only when hvem/hvad is the subordinate-clause subject

When a listener asks "what did you say?" and the original question used `hvem`/`hvad` ("who"/"what") as the SUBJECT of what becomes an embedded subordinate clause, Danish inserts `der` right after it (*Hvem har en ugift chef? → Jeg spurgte dig, hvem der havde en ugift chef*). If `hvem`/`hvad` is instead the OBJECT of the embedded clause, no `der` is inserted (*Hvem hentede Jørgen? → Ved du, hvem han hentede?*). This is the same subject-only `der`-licensing rule from Unit 15's relative-pronoun discussion, now shown applying to embedded questions as well as relative clauses. p. 195-196.

### Unit 17 — Verbs of perception: bare infinitive after høre/se

Mirroring English "I heard him coming/come," Danish *høre* ("hear") and *se* ("see") take a BARE infinitive (no *at*) for the perceived action, not a participle: *Jeg hørte ham lukke lågen* "I heard him close/closing the gate"; *Jeg så hende falde* "I saw her fall/falling." p. 199.

### Unit 17 — ingen/intet/ingenting: "no/none/nothing," and their nogen-ikke paraphrase

`Ingen` means "no" directly before a noun, or "none/no one/nobody" standing alone. `Intet` is the neuter-leaning "none/nothing," usable in very formal register directly before a neuter noun to mean "no." `Ingenting` (one word) is a synonym for "nothing." Any of these can be paraphrased as *ikke nogen*/*ikke noget* instead (*Jeg har ingen rene sokker* = *Jeg har ikke nogen rene sokker* "I have no clean socks"), giving two equally correct ways to negate existence/possession. p. 202.

### Unit 17 — Verbs ending in -s with active meaning, and the reciprocal -s

Two irregularities, both with an `-s` ending that looks passive but isn't: (1) `synes` ("think/seem") and `lykkes` ("succeed") are ACTIVE-meaning verbs that simply always end in `-s` — not a passive construction despite the surface resemblance. (2) A separate, more general pattern: adding `-s` to an ordinary verb can mark RECIPROCAL action ("each other"), where English needs an explicit "each other" or just implies it: *mødes* "meet (each other)," *ses* "see each other," *skændes* "quarrel (with each other)," *forloves*/*giftes* "get engaged/married (to each other)," *sloges* "fought (each other), like wild animals." p. 203.

### Unit 17 — få vs. have for "to have": possession vs. coming-into-possession

Where English "have" covers both senses, Danish splits them: `have` = being in static possession of something already (*Den grundlov som Danmark har nu* "The constitution Denmark HAS now"); `få` = coming INTO possession, the event of acquiring (*Danmark fik sin første grundlov i 1849* "Denmark HAD [=got] its first constitution in 1849"). This extends to food/drink idioms, where "to have [a meal/drink]" — an acquiring-and-consuming event — defaults to `få`, not `have`: *Vi får morgenmad klokken syv* "We have breakfast at seven"; *Jeg fik en kop kaffe* "I had a cup of coffee." The book flags that saying *Jeg havde en kop kaffe* instead sounds to a Dane like you're describing a cup you physically possessed/carried around (and implicitly asks "where is it now, did you lose it?"), not an act of drinking it — a genuine false-friend trap for English speakers defaulting to `have`. p. 204.

### Unit 18 — Disagreeing and swearing: the book's own coarse/harmless register catalog (flagship finding)

This is the single most concentrated explicit register-tagging passage found anywhere in this source. The book states up front that Danish swearing is structurally blasphemy-based rather than obscenity-based (unlike English, which "tends to use obscenities"), and gives a direct catalog of expletives with its OWN asterisk-marked coarse/harmless distinction ("expressions marked * are considered coarse by most people, while the rest are pretty harmless"):

- **Coarse (*)**: *Hold (din) mund!* / *Hold kæft!* "Shut up!"; *Det er noget lort!* "That's a load of shit!" (the book's own loose translation, rendered directly as an English obscenity); *Av for Fanden!/Satan!/Helvede!* (escalating blasphemy-based pain exclamations invoking the Devil/Satan/Hell).
- **Harmless (unmarked)**: *Sikke noget vrøvl!* "What nonsense!"; *Sådan noget skidt!* "What rubbish!"; *Det var Sørens!* "Good Heavens!" (cross-references Unit 4's *sørme* and Unit 9's *Det var sørens!*, both already flagged mild); *Av!*, *Av for katten/Søren/Pokker!* (mild end of the pain-exclamation escalation ladder).

A separate, explicit sub-point on `skidt` vs. `lort`: despite *skidt*'s rude-looking appearance, the book states it corresponds only to the strength of English "muck"/"dirt" and is "totally accepted" on its own (consistent with Unit 9's *Skidt være med det!* "Never mind!"). `Lort`, by contrast, is "much stronger and definitely vulgar." But COMPOUNDING `skid-` onto another word jumps the register up sharply — *skidedum* "bloody stupid" and *skidetosset* "bloody furious" are both explicitly marked vulgar (*), even though the bare `skidt` they derive from is mild. The book also notes recently-borrowed English four-letter words are now commonly used in Danish but "scarcely carry the vulgar connotations they have in English" — a loanword-defanging effect. p. 210-211.

### Unit 18 — blev vs. var + past participle: action vs. resulting state

Both *blev sendt* and *var sendt* translate English "was sent," but they're not interchangeable: *blive* + past participle marks the ACTION taking place (*Hvornår blev pakken sendt?* "When WAS the parcel sent [=when did the sending-event happen]?"); *være* + past participle marks the resulting STATE/result (*Pakken var sendt, da jeg talte med hende* "The parcel WAS [already in the state of being] sent/had been sent when I spoke to her"). An s-form past passive technically exists too but the book notes it's "hardly ever used." p. 211.

### Unit 18 — The present participle (-nde/-ende): narrower use than English -ing

Danish's *-nde*/*-ende* participle (*køre* → *kørende*, *dø* → *døende*) corresponds to English "-ing" but is used far less broadly, since Danish has no continuous/progressive tenses at all. Four distinct uses: (1) as a plain adjective (*en forstående far* "an understanding father"); (2) as a verb, but ONLY combined with *komme* or *blive* (*Han blev stående* "He remained standing"; *Hun kom kørende* "She came driving"); (3) as an adverb (*forbavsende dyre* "amazingly expensive"); (4) as a standalone noun (*de døende* "the dying"). p. 212-213.

### Unit 18 — Three ways to express an ongoing passive action: bliver, er, and the s-form

Extends Unit 10's passive-infinitive and Unit 17's active-meaning -s-verb material into a three-way system for "X is being done": `bliver` + past participle = the action actively happening (*Politiken bliver solgt over hele landet* "Politiken is (being) sold throughout the country"); `er` + past participle = the resulting STATE after the action (*Politiken er (ud)solgt* "Politiken is sold out" — parallels this unit's own blev/var distinction above); the `-s` present-tense form (identical in shape to the Unit 10 passive infinitive) is "more or less interchangeable" with the *bliver* form but specifically favored when the focus is on the thing/person ACTED UPON rather than the actor — the book notes this form is especially common in ads/signage (*Frimærker købes* "Stamps bought"; *Ure repareres* "Watches repaired"). p. 215.

### Unit 18 — Danish newspaper landscape (cultural/register context)

The book sketches Denmark's print-media landscape directly: three broadsheet morning papers (*Berlingske Tidende*, *Jyllandsposten* — both conservative; *Politiken* — more liberal), now all sold nationally rather than regionally as they originally were; two half-size tabloids (*Ekstrabladet*, *BT*) appearing later in the day, focused on "sensational events" and sport; and a noted decline in paper purchases generally as readers shift to free/paid online news sites. p. 214-215.

### Ready-reference grammar appendix — consolidated verb paradigm tables (fuller than any single per-unit table)

The appendix (pp. 220-224) is explicitly "not a complete grammar" but a cross-reference summary pulling together points taught at scattered points through all 18 units. Two tables here are more systematic than anything given per-unit:

**Full regular-verb paradigm** (worked example *elske* "love"): Infinitive *elske* → Stem *elsk* → Present *elsker* → Past *elskede* → Present participle *elskende* → Past participle *elsket* — the first and only place in the book all six forms of one regular verb appear together in a single row.

**Passive-form minimal pairs, present vs. past, s-form vs. blive-periphrasis, side by side**: *hentes* (pres. s-passive) / *hentes* (same form serves as infinitive) / *hentedes* (past s-passive) alongside *blive hentet* (inf.) / *bliver hentet* (pres.) / *blev hentet* (past) — and the same four-way layout repeated for *høres*/*blive hørt*. This is the clearest single-place comparison of the two passive strategies (Units 10, 17, 18) the book offers.

**Complete modal-verb principal-parts table in one place**: *ville* (vil/ville/villet), *skulle* (skal/skulle/skullet), *kunne* (kan/kunne/kunnet), *måtte* (må/måtte/måttet), *burde* (bør/burde/burdet), *turde* (tør/turde/turdet) — consolidating the Unit 6/8/9/10 modals that were each introduced separately across those units into one complete six-modal reference table.

# Danish — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Full-Book Survey

**Source:** *Language/30: Danish — Phrase Dictionary and Study Guide* (Educational Services
Corporation, Washington, D.C., ©1995; Danish editor Karen Møller-Irving, University of California,
Berkeley; companion booklet to a 4-side cassette audio course), whole booklet, printed pp. i–41
(Introduction, Pronunciation Hints, Danish Alphabet, Sounds of the Danish Language, General
Expressions, Numbers/Time/Colors, Personal Needs, Communications, Transportation, Emergency &
Medical Situations, Social Customs & Polite Expressions) plus pp. 44–47 (Basic Danish Grammar and
Important Signs — both explicitly marked "not recorded" on audio, i.e. reference-only sections in the
printed book). The Vocabulary Index (pp. 49+) is a page-reference index into the English word list
and was not separately extracted as vocabulary content.

**Coverage note:** This is a travel phrasebook, not a graded grammar — the large majority of its
content is full English-Danish sentence pairs organized by practical-use topic (greetings, hotel,
food, transportation, emergencies, etc.) rather than an enumerated word list. Per the coverage rule's
spirit, this file captures every topic section and samples representative vocabulary broadly across
all of them (every section is represented) rather than transcribing the booklet's hundreds of full
phrase-pairs verbatim, which would amount to bulk reproduction of the source's own dialogue/phrase
content. Full-sentence phrases are treated as illustrative grammar/pragmatics context in the Grammar
points section rather than individual vocabulary-table rows, except where a phrase itself is the
smallest meaningful unit worth recording (e.g. fixed greeting formulas). This is the first Danish
source in this wave aimed at **American** English speakers specifically (Educational Services
Corporation, Washington D.C.) and the first using a non-IPA, non-Berlitz-European phonetic-respelling
system distinct from both `001` and `003`— a third independent cross-check of the Danish sound
system.

**Vision-reading guard:** This source has **no text layer** (`pdftotext` returns empty output) and
was fully vision-read via `pdftoppm -png -r 150`. **Confirmed two-printed-pages-per-scan-image
spread layout** (per the extraction spec's documented gotcha): each of the 31 rendered PDF-page
images actually contains two side-by-side printed pages (verified by the printed page numbers in
each image's footer, e.g. a single image showing both printed page "ii" on its left half and page
"iii" on its right half), giving roughly 62 printed-page-equivalents of content — consistent with the
book's own table of contents indexing content up to printed page 49. All content viewed was clearly
typeset/printed — no handwritten marginalia, underlining, or pencil annotation was found anywhere in
the 31 rendered images; no exclusion was necessary.

## Grammar points

### Introduction's sociolinguistic framing

The book states Danish is spoken by "over five million inhabitants of Denmark" and is also "the
second language of Greenland, and of the Faroe Islands" — both "politically autonomous" but
"represented in the Danish parliament" (p.i). It frames Danish, Swedish, and Norwegian as closely
related Northern Germanic languages whose native speakers "can generally understand one another,
particularly when communicating in writing," and makes a historically grounded claim that Norwegian
and Danish developed additional similarities because "Denmark and Norway were unified for the four
hundred years prior to 1814" — citing *bokmål* (one of Norway's two official written standards) as
sometimes called "Dano-Norwegian" as a direct consequence. This is a genuinely useful, dateable
historical-political claim worth cross-referencing against this project's existing Norwegian dataset.
The three Scandinavian languages are further grouped with German, Dutch, and English as all
descending from the Germanic branch of Indo-European, with the explicit observation that Danish and
English "share hundreds of words that differ only slightly in spelling and pronunciation" — later
cashed out concretely in the p.43 cognate list (see Vocabulary table).

### Pronunciation system

This source uses a bracketed-respelling pronunciation system calibrated to **American** English
("Approximate U.S. English Equivalent") — a third independent phonetic-approximation framework for
Danish within this wave, distinct from both the Essential Grammar's IPA-based system (`001`) and the
Mini Grammar Book's own informal respelling (`003`), useful as a cross-check resource. It covers the
same core phonological facts already documented in the other two sources — vowel length as
contrastive, the glottal stop (*stød*) as a genuine phoneme illustrated with the classic *le'ver*
(liver, with stød)/*lever* (to live, without stød) minimal pair, extensive consonant lenition rules
(d, g, b, t between vowels or finally softening toward [ð]/[w]/[b]/[d] respectively) — independently
corroborating the consonant-softening system documented in both `001` and `003`. Two points are
genuinely distinctive to this source: an explicit note that letter combinations *eg* and *ej* are both
realized as [ai] (demonstrated directly via the extremely high-frequency word *jeg* "I," respelled
"yai"); and a systematic table of r-realizations distinguishing word-initial/post-consonant "strong
guttural" [ʀ] (compared explicitly to Spanish *j* and French uvular *r*) from a weakened/vocalized
realization between vowels or before a consonant, where "it often becomes part of the vowel sound or
is lost entirely" — a clear, explicit statement of r-vocalization as a systematic (not sporadic)
process, slightly more explicit on this point than either `001` or `003`.

### General expressions, introductions, getting acquainted

Organized entirely as functional phrase sets (salutations, everyday expressions, introductions,
getting acquainted) rather than grammatical paradigms. Two footnoted cultural-pragmatics points are
the standout original content here, not duplicated in either grammar-reference source from this wave:
(1) it is **not customary in Denmark to introduce a third party** — people introduce themselves
individually instead (p.4, footnote 4); (2) the Danish equivalent of "Pleased to meet you" (*Det var
hyggeligt at mødes*) should, per the book, **only be said if genuinely meant**, not offered
automatically as an empty social formality the way its English equivalent often is (p.4, footnote 5)
— a concrete, actionable sincerity-norm difference between the two speech communities, valuable for
any later pragmatics/register-mechanics analysis distinguishing formulaic vs. sincere speech acts.

### Numbers, time, colors

The numeral system (vigesimal 50–100 range) and the "half past the *next* hour" clock convention
(*halv syv* = "half seven" = 6:30) are both independently corroborated here against `001` and `003`
— three-way agreement across genuinely different source types (academic reference grammar, concise
pedagogical grammar, and commercial phrasebook) gives this pattern strong cross-source confidence per
the spec's evidentiary standard. Footnote 8 (p.11) adds a point not spelled out as explicitly in
either other source: Danish time expressions have **two equally valid ways of describing the same
clock time** depending on which adjacent "anchor" hour is used (2:20 can be "twenty past two" *or*
"ten minutes to half past two"), and the booklet separately notes that **24-hour/military time is
used even in ordinary conversation** in Denmark, unlike the more exclusively 12-hour-clock norm of
casual American English speech.

### Personal needs, communications, transportation sections

These sections are organized as practical phrase sets for hotels, money/currency exchange,
food/restaurants, shopping, leisure activities, the post office, office equipment, telephone use, and
all modes of transportation (trains, cars, airports, customs). Three footnoted cultural-practical
points stand out as genuinely new information relative to the other two sources in this wave: tipping
is **not customary** in Denmark because gratuity is already included in posted prices at restaurants
and in taxis (p.25, footnote 12); mail in Denmark is classified into two speed/cost tiers, "A mail"
(faster/airmail) and "B mail" (slower), with the explicit instruction to mark outgoing international
letters "A" (p.29, footnote 13); and Copenhagen has multiple telephone area codes requiring local
verification (p.31, footnote 14), with the further social-pragmatic note that **it is expected you
introduce yourself by name when you answer the phone** in Denmark (p.31, footnote 15) — a concrete
telephone-etiquette norm distinct from the more anonymous "Hello?" convention common in American
phone culture.

### Social customs & polite expressions (p.41, prose section)

This short essay is the single most concentrated sociolinguistic/pragmatic content in the booklet and
is worth treating as a unit. Key points: the *du*/*De* formal-address distinction has the same decline
trajectory independently described in both `001` (§68.1) and `003` (p.33) — three-way corroboration
of this sociolinguistic change — with the book adding that *du* is "nowadays acceptable in all
circumstances." *Hej!* is identified as the single most common greeting, used both on meeting *and*
parting (paired with *Hej-hej!* and *Vi ses!* specifically for departure) — a genuinely bidirectional
greeting word, unlike English "hi," worth flagging for any later greeting-formula mechanics analysis.
Several *tak* ("thanks") compounds are given as a graded intensity/context set: *Mange tak* ("many
thanks"), *Tusind tak* ("a thousand thanks" — hyperbolic intensification), *Tak skal du have*
(literally "thanks shall you have"), and the occasion-specific *Tak for i dag!* / *Tak for i aften!*
("thanks for today/tonight," said by a dinner guest). Handshaking on greeting/parting is described as
common between men and women alike. Self-introduction (rather than third-party introduction) is
reiterated as the norm, echoing the earlier p.4 footnote. Punctuality for appointments is described as
"essential," and dinner-party etiquette is spelled out concretely: bring the host/hostess flowers or
wine, and the *next* time you see or speak to them, say *Tak for sidst!* ("thanks for last time") —
a specific, named, delayed-reciprocity politeness formula with no direct one-word English equivalent,
independently corroborated by the Inquiries-on-the-Street section's footnote 7 (p.8) giving the same
instruction for a dinner host specifically ("Thanks for a wonderful evening!... You should also
remember to say *Tak for sidst!*").

### Basic Danish Grammar (pp.44–46, "not recorded" reference section)

A condensed, simplified restatement of core Danish morphology aimed at absolute beginners, fully
consistent with (and a useful independent cross-check of) the much fuller treatments in `001` and
`003`: two-gender noun system (*en*/*et*), the enclitic definite article, three plural-formation
strategies (*-e*, *-(e)r*, zero) plus the definite-plural suffix *-(e)ne*, the full personal and
possessive pronoun paradigms (explicitly including the reflexive possessive *sin/sit/sine*, unglossed
beyond the bare forms — readers are expected to have absorbed the distinction from context), adjective
agreement (zero/-t/-e by gender and number), invariant present-tense verb morphology (-r for all
persons), the two weak-verb past-tense classes (*-ede*/*-et* vs. *-te*/*-t*), negation via post-verbal
*ikke* (with the auxiliary-verb placement rule for perfect-tense negation spelled out: *Han har ikke
skrevet*, "he has not written"), and subject-verb inversion for yes/no questions (*Han sover* → *Sover
han?*). The chapter closes with a short list of core irregular verbs (*være* "to be," *gå* "to go,"
*have* "to have") given in full infinitive/present/past/present-perfect paradigm form — a compact,
independently-useful irregular-verb reference distinct from (though consistent with) `001`'s much
larger Conjugation III/IV treatment.

### Important Signs (p.47–48, "not recorded" reference section)

A ~30-entry glossary of standardized public/official signage (traffic, pedestrian, retail, safety) —
genuinely distinct in register from every other section of this booklet, since all other content is
spoken/conversational phrase material. This is the closest thing in this source to a formal/written
register sample, and is flagged accordingly in the Vocabulary table (`formal`/`technical` usage
tier) — public signage vocabulary is typically terse, often using the passive/impersonal construction
(e.g. *Må ikke forstyrres*, "must not be disturbed" = "Do Not Disturb") rather than imperative mood,
which is itself a notable register-marking grammatical choice worth flagging for later mechanics
analysis of formal vs. colloquial register construction in Danish.

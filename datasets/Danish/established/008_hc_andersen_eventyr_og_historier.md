# Danish — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: H.C. Andersen, *Eventyr og Historier*

**Source:** H.C. Andersen, *Eventyr og Historier*, introduction by Johannes Møllehave, illustrated
by Vilhelm Pedersen and Lorenz Frølich (Forlaget Sesam, 2004/2005, 3rd printing 2005,
ISBN 87-11-22269-7). 912-page omnibus edition of Andersen's own fairy tales ("Eventyr og
Historier"), DJVU file `20 H.C. Andersen - Eventyr og historier.djvu`. This is Wave 3 (native
literature) of the Danish extraction pass.

**Coverage note — representative sample, not exhaustive (per this project's 700+-page
precedent).** The DJVU has a genuine, clean text layer (`djvutxt` extraction, ~305,000 words
total for the whole 912-page book) — not a scan requiring vision-reading for the bulk of the
work. **Marginalia check performed first:** several pages were rendered to image (pp. 67, 125,
144, 179, 400, 600, 850) and visually inspected — this is a pristine modern (2004/2005) trade
edition, not a used/scanned library copy, and no handwritten marginalia of any kind was found
anywhere sampled. No marginalia exclusion was needed.

Sampled:
- **The introduction** by Johannes Møllehave (pp. 9–65, ~21,000 words) — read via the full text
  extraction; the biographical/critical sections were read start-to-finish through its first
  ~55 of 57 pages (Andersen's childhood, flight to Copenhagen, theatrical failures, the 1835
  breakthrough and the four-eventyr first "hefte," the evolution from *krostuehistorie* to
  Andersen's own voice, the etymology of *eventyr*, the "store eksistenseventyr" of the 1840s);
  the final ~2 pages (closing remarks on time-as-theme and a closing anecdote) were scanned by
  section header rather than read in full line-by-line — this is a 57-page literary essay, and
  copyright discipline (paraphrase, never bulk-reproduce) makes exhaustive verbatim capture both
  unnecessary and against the project's own rules regardless.
- **Five famous tales, read in full**, selected across the book's actual print-order chronology
  (distinct from the omnibus's own front-matter index, which is alphabetized by title, not
  page order — confirmed by cross-referencing the Indhold's page numbers against actual print
  sequence):
  - **Fyrtøjet** ("The Tinderbox," pp. 67–73) — first tale in the whole collection, first tale
    of the first 1835 "eventyrhefte."
  - **Prinsessen på ærten** ("The Princess and the Pea," pp. 85–86) — shortest tale sampled.
  - **Den lille havfrue** ("The Little Mermaid," pp. 125–143) — Andersen's single most famous
    tale, from the 1837 third heft of the first collection.
  - **Kejserens nye klæder** ("The Emperor's New Clothes," pp. 144–147) — same 1837 heft.
  - **Den standhaftige tinsoldat** ("The Steadfast Tin Soldier," pp. 179–182) — 1838, second
    collection's first heft.

**Gotcha found and flagged: localized DJVU text-layer corruption around in-text illustrations.**
Confirmed on two independent pages (144 and 72): `djvutxt`'s extraction is excellent on plain-text
pages but becomes locally scrambled on pages where a wood-engraving illustration sits directly
above or beside the body-text block — e.g. extracted page 144 read "...af fmukTe nye klæder...
Han bL sig ikke om sine soldater, bred sig ej om komedie eller om at [missing: køre i skoven]..."
where the rendered page image clearly shows the correct printed text "...af smukke, nye klæder...
Han brød sig ikke om sine soldater, brød sig ej om komedie eller om at køre i skoven, uden alene
for at vise sine nye klæder." Similarly on page 72, "og sifyrtøj havde han glemt...en skoma- ff
dreng Ld skødskmd" decoded on inspection to the correct "og sit fyrtøj havde han glemt...en
skomagerdreng med skødskind." **This is not a font-substitution cipher** (the surrounding
plain-text pages extract perfectly and the garbling isn't a consistent 1:1 character map) — it
looks like an OCR/text-layer artifact specific to pages where illustration and body text share
vertical space. All quotes used below were either drawn from confirmed-clean pages or
cross-verified against the rendered page image where the extracted text looked suspect; none of
the corrupted raw text is reproduced here. Worth checking for on any future DJVU source with
in-text (not full-page) illustrations.

## Grammar points

### Formulaic opening and the "in medias res" shift (1830 → 1835)

Møllehave's introduction makes an explicit, well-evidenced grammatical/stylistic argument: Andersen's
breakthrough between 1830 and 1835 wasn't primarily plot invention but a change in sentence-opening
strategy. He quotes Andersen's own 1830 tale *Dødningen* ("The Dead Man") opening with a scene-setting,
geographically-anchored sentence ("Omtrent en mil fra Bogense finder man på marken...") and compares it
directly to the 1835 rewrite of the same story as *Rejsekammeraten*, which instead opens mid-scene with
two named characters already in a dramatic situation ("Den stakkels Johannes var så bedrøvet, for hans
fader var meget syg og kunne ikke leve..."). The grammatical marker of the shift is the subordinating
causal clause placed immediately after the subject-plus-predicate rather than after extended scene-setting
— Andersen's mature style front-loads a named person's emotional state, then immediately supplies the
*for*-clause reason, rather than building up an impersonal establishing shot first.

### *for* as light causal connector in child-directed prose

Møllehave specifically flags Andersen's habit of tacking a *for*-clause reason onto a factual statement as
a deliberately child-pleasing rhetorical device, distinct from ordinary causal subordination — he cites
"...forlangte de allerbedste værelser og mad, som han holdt af, for nu var han rig, da han jo havde så
mange penge" (*Fyrtøjet*) and comments that the sentence is simultaneously an explanation and a tease: it
states "he was rich" as if it needed justifying, then justifies it with the near-tautological "because he
had so much money." This is presented as a named narrative-register device (unnamed in standard Danish
grammar terminology, but explicitly identified here as a recurring Andersen technique), not merely
ordinary subordinate-clause syntax.

### *så* as a child-register intensifier/hedge, used in triplicate

Citing *Tommelise*, Møllehave notes Andersen's practice of repeating the intensifier *så* up to three
times in a single sentence to imitate how children talk ("hun var så fornøjet, for nu kunne skrubtudsen
ikke nå hende og der var så dejligt, hvor hun sejlede") — paraphrased explicitly as children's own
characteristic hedge-without-referent ("Den er så stor" — "so big," without ever specifying how big).
Grammatically this is just the ordinary degree adverb *så* ("so"), but its *stacking* within one sentence
is flagged as a deliberate register marker rather than incidental repetition.

### Direct-address narrator asides ("vi" framing)

All five sampled tales use first-person-plural narrator intrusions that address the listening child
directly, distinct from the otherwise third-person narration: *Prinsessen på ærten* closes with "Se, det
var en rigtig historie!" ("See, now that was a real story!"); *Fyrtøjet*'s witch-cave passage is framed by
the narrator's own aside "...og — ja nu skal vi få at høre!" ("and — well, now we shall hear!"); *Lille
Claus og Store Claus* (cited in the introduction, not sampled directly here) opens "Nu skulle vi høre,
hvorledes de to havde det" ("Now we shall hear how the two of them fared"). Møllehave explicitly contrasts
this with the anonymous, impersonal voice of genuine oral folk tales — the recurring first-person-plural
"vi" ("we/us") is presented as the single clearest grammatical fingerprint of Andersen's own authorial
voice layered on top of borrowed folk material, distinguishing *Fyrtøjet*/*Lille Claus og Store Claus*/
*Prinsessen på ærten* (folk-tale retellings, still carrying this device) from *Den lille Idas blomster*
(Andersen's first wholly original plot, discussed at length in the introduction as the moment "the fairy
tale loses its innocence").

### Formal address: *De*/*Deres* vs. plain imperative command

*Kejserens nye klæder* contrasts two address registers within a few lines: the swindlers and the
ceremony-master use formal *Deres Majestæt*/*De* toward the emperor ("Vil Deres kejserlige Majestæt
allernådigst behage at tage Deres klæder af!"), while the emperor's own interior monologue and the
crowd's eventual chorus use plain declaratives/exclamations with no address pronoun at all ("Han har jo
ikke noget på!"). The grammatical shift in address formality tracks the social distance collapsing as the
crowd's collective voice (led by the child) breaks the spell — worth noting as a register-contrast example
for any mechanics-analysis work on Danish formal/informal address.

### Superlative stacking as a satirical device

*Kejserens nye klæder* repeatedly stacks superlative and elative adjective forms for satirical effect:
"det dejligste tøj," "det prægtigste guld," "allerkæreste," "allerhøjeste bifald," "allernådigst." The
swindlers' and courtiers' dialogue is grammatically marked by a conspicuously higher density of *aller-*
prefixed absolute superlatives than the surrounding narration — a stylistic/grammatical tell used to mark
empty flattery, worth flagging for any later analysis of how Danish intensification strategies get
weaponized for irony.

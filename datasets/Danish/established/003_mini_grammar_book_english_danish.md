# Danish — Established Vocabulary/Grammar: Full-Book Survey

**Source:** *Mini Grammar Book: English — Danish* (Univerb Förlag AB, Sweden; companion booklet to
a UNIVERB audio language course; no stated author or edition year — printer's code "0511" /
"Art. nr 20511" on Streck & Punkt/Graf & Print, Eskilstuna, Sweden), whole booklet, pp. 7–41
(Preface, Spelling and Pronunciation, Nouns, Adjectives, Adverbs, Verbs, Pronouns, Prepositions —
the entire content of this 23-page reference).

**Coverage note:** This is a short, concise English-medium reference grammar — every grammar point
and every distinct vocabulary item in the whole booklet is captured below; nothing was skipped as
redundant since this is a genuinely compact source with little drill repetition. Cross-checked
against `001_danish_an_essential_grammar.md` (the much larger Essential Grammar already extracted
in this wave): topics overlap heavily (gender, enclitic article, verb conjugation classes, pronoun
paradigms, prepositions), but this source adds real value in three places flagged below — a
pronunciation-oriented phonetic respelling system not present in the Essential Grammar's IPA-based
approach, an explicit note on English's possible Danish/Germanic origin (the book's own claim, not
independently verified here), and some colloquial usage notes (e.g. "Tak for mad!" as a cultural
practice, the "du"/"De" trend commentary) that corroborate rather than duplicate the Essential
Grammar's parallel coverage.

**Vision-reading guard:** This source has a confirmed, clean text layer (`pdftotext -layout`
extracted the whole 23-page booklet correctly, including æ/ø/å and the book's own bracketed phonetic
respellings) and was not vision-read; no marginalia check was needed.

**Dated/sensitive terminology flag:** The booklet's noun-gender example list (p. 21) includes
*neger* ("negro") as a plain vocabulary example with no register comment from the source. This term
is dated/offensive in contemporary Danish usage; it is recorded here only as a literal transcription
of the source's own example (per the no-bulk-reproduction and faithful-transcription principles) and
flagged explicitly rather than silently normalized or silently omitted — see the Vocabulary table.

## Grammar points

### Preface and framing claims

The book's own preface (p.7) makes two sociolinguistic claims about Danish worth recording, though
unverified independently by this extraction: that Danish is spoken by "approx. 5 million people,"
is the official language of Denmark and (at the time of writing) an official language on the Faroe
Islands and in Greenland, and is taught as a foreign language in Iceland; and that other Scandinavians
(Swedes, Norwegians, Finns) can generally understand official Danish notices and be understood in
return, since "the differences between Norwegian and Swedish lie mainly in the pronunciation" — a
claim about Scandinavian mutual intelligibility that is directly cross-referenceable against this
project's existing Swedish and Norwegian datasets. The preface also makes an unusual, likely
overstated etymological claim that "many scholars believe that English has its origin in Danish" —
recorded here as the source's own stated position, not verified or endorsed by this extraction (the
mainstream view places English's ancestry in West Germanic with heavy later Norse/Danish contact
influence via Viking-era settlement, not direct descent).

### Spelling and pronunciation

The alphabet is presented as English's 26 letters plus Æ/æ, Ø/ø, Å/å appended at the end — consistent
with the Essential Grammar's §166. The book frames Danish orthography as having a notably loose
spelling-to-pronunciation relationship, driven by extensive silent/mute letters (worked examples:
*dukke* "doll" pronounced [dåke], *pege* "point" pronounced [peje]) and states plainly that correct
pronunciation is "only rarely essential to comprehension" for a learner — a reassurance framing aimed
at the pamphlet's audio-course users. **Stress** defaults to the first syllable, with named
exceptions for the prefixes *be-, ge-, er-, for-* (the last of which is stressed only when it carries
independent/literal meaning, contrasted via *forklare* "explain" unstressed-prefix vs. *forvarme*
"preheat" where the prefix is semantically "fore-" and stressed) plus place names and foreign
loanwords. **The glottal stop (stød)** is framed as uniquely Danish among the Germanic languages
(matching the Essential Grammar's phonological chapter) and is shown losing its presence when a
stressed syllable becomes the first element of a compound, or when certain suffixes are added
(explicitly excluding the enclitic article from this suffix-triggered loss) — the book gives several
clean minimal pairs (*skål*/*skåle*, *land*/*lande*) and one phrase-level minimal pair showing stød
alone disambiguating meaning (*stå på bussen* "stand on top of the bus" vs. "get on the bus").
**Vowel length** follows the general Germanic short-before-geminate/long-before-single-consonant
pattern, with explicitly acknowledged numerous exceptions. The book gives a full vowel-by-vowel and
consonant-by-consonant phonetic-respelling guide (using an informal bracketed-approximation system
rather than IPA, e.g. [aa]/[ah]/[ææ]/[ai]/[eh]/[ar] for the letter A's several realizations) —
methodologically distinct from (and more learner-friendly/less precise than) the Essential Grammar's
IPA-based system, useful as a second independent cross-check of the vowel inventory. Mute-letter
rules are given for d (in *-nd, -ld, -rd*, before t/s), g (in the suffix *-ig*, after e/i/u/y, word-
finally), h (before v/j), f (in the prepositions *af*/*ad* specifically), and v (in *gulv*, *tolv*) —
substantially overlapping with, and independently corroborating, the Essential Grammar §§4–8 silent-
consonant rules.

### Nouns

Gender is framed pedagogically as "learn the noun together with its indefinite article" rather than
via the Essential Grammar's detailed form/meaning rule system — a notably different pedagogical
philosophy for the same underlying fact. The book flags a small set of dual-gender nouns usable with
either article (*en/et cirkus*, *en/et indeks*) and, more interestingly, nouns whose **meaning
changes** depending on which gender is selected (*en vår* "springtime" vs. *et vår* "pillow case") —
this second category is a genuinely distinct and valuable datapoint not found in the Essential
Grammar's gender chapter, worth flagging for any later "grammatical gender as a disambiguation/
word-formation mechanism" analysis. The genitive is presented simply as English-like -s suffixation.
The **enclitic (end) article** is explicitly framed as unusual "unlike most European languages" —
attached to the end of the noun rather than preceding it — matching the Essential Grammar's
treatment. Plural formation is presented as a three-way -e/-r/-er choice "a rather complex matter"
best learned by rote per-word, alongside the general stressed-syllable-final-consonant-doubling rule
and the -el/-en/-er stem-vowel-drop pattern (both independently corroborating Essential Grammar §30).

### Adjectives

Covers the -el/-en/-er stem-vowel-drop and degemination rule, the default neuter -t suffix (and its
exception for -sk adjectives), the indeclinable class of stressed-vowel-final adjectives (with å-final
adjectives and *ny*/*fri* as counter-exceptions that *do* take -t), and the two comparison strategies
(-ere/-est suffixation vs. periphrastic *mere*/*mest*, the latter preferred for longer/polysyllabic
adjectives) — all consistent with, and a condensed restatement of, the Essential Grammar's fuller
Chapter 3 treatment (§§44–63).

### Adverbs

Splits adverbs into underived "genuine adverbs" and adjective-derived adverbs formed with -t (framed
explicitly as parallel to English -ly). The book's most distinctive contribution here is a genuinely
fine-grained usage note not found as explicitly in the Essential Grammar: -ig/-lig-derived adverbs
may optionally drop the -t when they modify a *degree* (quantifying how much, e.g. "incredibly many
people") but should keep the -t when indicating *manner* (how something behaves/exists, e.g. "spell
correctly," "smiled kindly") — a genuine semantic (degree vs. manner) conditioning of an otherwise
optional inflectional ending, worth flagging for mechanics-analysis of adverb-based register/
intensifier variation.

### Verbs

Presents present-tense formation as uniformly adding -r to the infinitive stem across all persons
(no person/number agreement at all) — matching the Essential Grammar's §79 point about Danish having
only one verb form per tense regardless of person. Covers the -e/-o/æ/ø/å stem class (direct -r
suffixation) vs. the -i/-u/-y stem class (optional inserted -e- before -r/-s, with the book noting
explicitly that "the trend is a declining use of 'e'" — an attested, dated diachronic-drift
observation worth cross-referencing against any corpus evidence found in Phase 3). Past tense covers
the same four-way split as the Essential Grammar (weak -ede, weak -te, irregular vowel-change,
irregular zero-ending) with a pedagogical recommendation to simply memorize present/past pairs
together. The imperative is formed from the bare stem, with vowel-final stems remaining unchanged;
the book adds a practical learner-note not found in the Essential Grammar — that certain
imperative forms ending in awkward consonant clusters (*åbn, pensl, klistr, sagtn, behandl*) are
avoided even by native speakers in favor of a paraphrase, a genuine phonotactic-dispreference
observation. Compound tenses (perfect, pluperfect) use *har*/*er* auxiliaries + participle, matching
the Essential Grammar's split-auxiliary system (§96) without spelling out the full transitivity-class
conditioning rule. The future tense is noted as "originally constructed from an auxiliary verb plus
the infinitive" but with "a growing tendency to use the present tense instead" — again corroborating,
independently, the Essential Grammar's §98 observation about Danish lacking a formal future and
increasingly defaulting to present+adverbial. **Negation** uses a single word, *ikke*, with a
specific scope-mismatch example flagged relative to English: Danish sometimes places the negation in
the matrix clause where English places it in the subordinate clause ("Jeg håber ikke, du har noget
imod det" = literally "I hope not, you have anything against it" = "I hope you don't mind") — a
genuine syntax-level translation trap.

### Pronouns

Full personal-pronoun subject/object paradigms match the Essential Grammar's §67 one-for-one. The
book's **du/De usage note** (p.33) independently corroborates Essential Grammar §68.1's observation
about the declining use of the polite form: "the general trend since the last few decades is an
increasing use of the more familiar form 'du'," with *De* reserved for strangers and people warranting
respect (glossed directly against English "Sir"/"Madam" register). The impersonal pronoun *man* is
treated with an explicit note on an equivalent periphrastic alternative using *det* + a passive verb
form, which is not spelled out as a paired equivalence this explicitly in the Essential Grammar.
Demonstrative, possessive, relative, interrogative, and indefinite pronoun paradigms all closely
parallel (and independently corroborate) the Essential Grammar's Chapter 5 coverage, with one genuinely
new colloquial-register note: a tendency to substitute *dem* for *disse* in casual speech, "mostly
together with 'her'" (*Kan du lide den her?* "Do you like this one?") — flagged as colloquial usage
not covered this specifically in the Essential Grammar.

### Prepositions

Rather than attempting the Essential Grammar's systematic per-preposition semantic-field breakdown,
this booklet gives a single sharply-drawn worked contrast: *i skole* (attend school as a regular
pupil) vs. *på skole* (attend a specific course), and *i teatret* (go to the theatre as audience) vs.
*på teatret* (work at/be employed by the theatre, e.g. as a performer) — a genuinely fine role-based
semantic distinction between what otherwise looks like two near-synonymous prepositions, worth
flagging alongside the Essential Grammar's much larger §118 *i*-preposition entry. The book's closing
practical advice — "choose the preposition you would use in English" as a first-approximation
heuristic — is itself a useful data point about how close English and Danish preposition systems are
perceived to be by this source's authors, though the Essential Grammar's much larger worked-example
set (§§113–126) shows the two systems diverge more than this heuristic suggests in several specific,
enumerable cases.

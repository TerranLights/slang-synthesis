# Danish — Established Reference: Grundtvig, "Danish Popular Tales" (English translation)

**Source:** Svend Grundtvig (1824–1883), *Danske Folkeæventyr, fundne i Folkemunde, og
gjenfortalte* (Copenhagen, 1876 and 1878), the "Danish Popular Tales" section translated into
English by Jane Mulley, as reprinted in the anthology *Scandinavian Folk-History: Including
Finnish Origins, Ancient Icelandic Folklore, Popular Danish Tales* (epub,
`source_reference/languages/xx--stash/LibGen Lang/`). Only the Danish-tales section (`text/
part0005.html` of the epub, ~16,370 words — roughly half the anthology) was extracted; the
anthology's separate Finnish-origins and Icelandic-folklore sections are out of scope for Danish
and were not touched.

**Coverage note — treated as an English-medium academic/folklore reference, not a Danish
vocabulary source.** Per the dispatch instructions: this is an 1870s-era English translation of a
canonical 19th-century Danish folklore collection. The translation itself contains almost no
running Danish text — the entries below are therefore an inventory of (a) the handful of Danish
terms/titles the translator/editor deliberately left untranslated, and (b) the folklore-scholarly
content (tale identification, editorial/translator commentary, motif notes) rather than a
conventional vocabulary harvest. This file's "Grammar points" section is replaced below with a
**"Tale and motif notes"** section, since there is no Danish grammar to extract from an English
translation.

Three tales are given in full in this section (the only three Grundtvig included in this
particular anthology excerpt, out of his much larger original two-volume Danish collection):
**"The Wishing-Box"** (*Ønskekisten*-type tale), **"Mons Tro"**, and **"Prince Wolf"**
(*Kong Lindorm*/animal-bridegroom type). These are preceded by a substantial biographical/
bibliographic introduction about Grundtvig himself, written by the anthology's (unnamed) editor.

## Tale and motif notes

*(Replaces the standard "Grammar points" section — an English-translated folklore anthology has no
Danish grammar to extract. These are paraphrased summaries and editorial/translator commentary,
not verbatim reproduction — see copyright discipline note below.)*

### Grundtvig's own methodology, as described in his preface (quoted at second-hand via the
### anthology's English preface)

The preface quotes Grundtvig's own postscript to his first (1876) collection, describing the
tales as compiled over "more than twenty years" from oral informants across Denmark by a network
of volunteer collectors, with Grundtvig himself solely responsible for the literary *form* each
tale was finally given. He states his goal was to give each tale "its epic completeness, with its
own fundamental idea, and in its own key-note" — i.e., a deliberately composite, idealized
version rather than a verbatim transcript of any single informant's telling, which he
acknowledges explicitly: "if none of these tales can now be found in the mouth of the people
quite as here presented, still every one of them will, when known, be recognised as the true
folk-tale." This is useful methodological context for this project: Grundtvig's collection is a
literary synthesis of oral variants, not a raw corpus-linguistics transcript — closer in kind to
the Grimms' editorial practice than to a modern folklore-fieldwork recording.

### "The Wishing-Box"

A poor man trades his three infant daughters to a mysterious old man (later revealed to be a
dwarf/enchanter) in exchange for a box that summons a wish-granting giant. His son "Silly Hans,"
considered simple-minded, eventually inherits the box after his parents die of grief and the
estate is lost to creditors. Hans uses the box to win a princess's hand (via a magically
irresistible self-playing fiddle and a forced written marriage promise), is betrayed by his wife
and her lover (who steal the box and banish Hans's castle to hang over the Red Sea), and recovers
everything with help from his three enchanted brothers-in-law (a bear-prince, an eagle-prince,
and a fish-prince who had each married one of his lost sisters). Ends with the family's full
restoration and the box's self-requested destruction (the bound giant-spirit asks to be released
into the fire after "thousands of years" of servitude). Classic ATU-adjacent "three stolen
sisters rescued, hero aided by animal brothers-in-law" structure, combined with a wishing-object
plot.

### "Mons Tro"

See the `mon(s) tro` vocabulary entry above for the central naming pun. Beyond that: the tale
follows a folk-tale "grateful/helper animal" structure — Mons Tro's talking grey horse (itself an
enchanted prince, revealed only at the very end when Tro is made to behead it on its own
instruction) repeatedly warns him not to pick up three golden feathers he finds in the road,
predicting "you will repent it" each time; each of the three feathers subsequently obligates him
to complete one of three escalating impossible tasks set by a king (fetch a bewitched
bird-princess from "the world's end"; fetch her enchanted castle; fetch water of life and water
of death) — a clean triadic (3×) task structure the introduction's own folklore-scholarly
apparatus would call "the law of three." Two just-so-story etiological asides are embedded in the
telling: fish have had red eyes "ever since" they searched the sea for the princess's lost keys,
and the lobster has had one claw longer than the other ever since breaking it prying the keys
loose from between two stones.

### "Prince Wolf"

An animal-bridegroom tale of the *Cupid and Psyche*/*Kong Lindorm* type: a princess is promised in
marriage to a wolf after her father swears to honor whatever price is demanded for identifying a
monstrously overgrown louse-skin nailed to the barn door. The wolf is a man under a witch's curse,
human only six hours nightly, and forbids his wife a light in the castle; following her mother's
advice (against the wolf/prince's explicit warning, repeated three times across three successive
visits home) she eventually strikes a light, sees and kisses him, and he must flee as a wolf,
limping (from an earlier knife-test wound) to a glass mountain and beyond to the witch's own
castle, where he is to be remarried to the witch's daughter. The princess pursues him across the
glass mountain using iron creeper-shoes forged at a smith's, serves as a kitchen-maid under an
unnamed magical helper's repeated aid (offered each time in exchange for "will you have me for
your sweetheart," which she refuses every time out of loyalty to the wolf-prince), and ultimately
breaks the spell when the witch and her daughter are burned to death by their own wedding
torches. A footnote in the source cross-references this tale's structure to "Conn-eda; or, the
Three Golden Apples of Lough Erne" in the *Folk-Lore Record*, vol. ii — i.e., the anthology's own
editorial apparatus already treats this as a cross-culturally comparable tale-type, not a uniquely
Danish structure.

### General editorial caution (translator's/editor's own words, retained)

A footnote appended after "The Wishing-Box" states explicitly: "Variants of these stories are to
be found in almost all European countries, but it is thought best to print the translations
without comment for the present" — i.e., the 19th-century editor was aware of (and deliberately
declined to pursue, in this printing) comparative-folklore cross-referencing beyond the single
"Prince Wolf" footnote noted above. Worth flagging for this project: this source is explicitly
*not* claiming Danish-exclusive origin for any of its three tales' underlying structures, only for
Grundtvig's specific literary retelling of them.

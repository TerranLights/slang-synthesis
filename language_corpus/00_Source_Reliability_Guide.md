# Source Reliability Guide — Subtitles & Transcripts

**Purpose.** Subtitles and transcripts of real speech (video/audio) are a genuinely rich source
for slang collection — they capture actual spoken usage that dictionaries and forums often miss.
Claude cannot listen to audio or watch video directly, but *can* read subtitle/transcript text
that already exists (creator-provided captions, platform auto-captions, third-party transcripts).
**This document exists because that source type carries a specific, serious risk that other
sources don't, and every entry drawn from one must be flagged for it.**

## The risk

Many subtitles/transcripts in the wild are AI-generated (auto-captions), not human-verified. An
AI transcription model has no reliable way to "know" a slang term it has never encountered — for
common vocabulary it can lean on a strong language-model prior, but **slang is exactly the case
where no such prior may exist.** The result: the transcription model produces its best phonetic
guess, which can be **confidently wrong** — a real slang term mis-heard as a similar-sounding
standard word, mangled into nonsense, or "corrected" into something the speaker never said. This
is a structural risk, not an occasional glitch, and it is worst precisely where this project needs
accuracy most.

**A wrong transcript entry doesn't fail loudly.** It looks exactly like a correct one unless
someone flags it. That is what makes this dangerous enough to need a standing convention rather
than a one-off warning.

## The rule

**Every corpus entry or established/analysis/synthesized shard entry sourced from a subtitle or
transcript must record:**

1. `source_type` — one of `subtitle_human` / `subtitle_ai_generated` / `transcript_human` /
   `transcript_ai_generated` (alongside the usual non-AV source types: `dictionary`,
   `grammar_reference`, `corpus_database`, `forum`, `academic_paper`, `other`). If it's genuinely
   unclear whether captions were human-authored or auto-generated, default to treating them as
   `*_ai_generated` — assume the riskier case when uncertain.
2. `transcription_confidence` — required whenever `source_type` is one of the two `*_ai_generated`
   values:
   - `verified` — cross-checked against an independent source (another transcript, a native/
     fluent-speaker confirmation elsewhere, a dictionary/forum attestation of the same term)
   - `plausible_unverified` — phonetically and contextually sensible, but not independently
     confirmed
   - `low_confidence` — the term is unusual, doesn't cleanly match known vocabulary, or the
     transcript itself shows other signs of error nearby (garbled surrounding text, timing
     mismatches) — **flag rather than silently include**
3. A short `notes` mention of *why* that confidence level was assigned, when not `verified`.

**Never launder an AI-transcript guess into `established/` (the real-language baseline) as if it
were dictionary-grade.** It belongs in `language_corpus/` and downstream `analysis/`/
`synthesized/` entries with its `transcription_confidence` intact — the flag should survive every
stage it gets carried through, not get dropped at the first re-summarization.

## Practical guidance

- Prefer creator-provided or platform-verified captions over unlabeled/uncertain auto-captions
  when a choice exists.
- When a term is genuinely load-bearing for a finding (e.g. it's the single clearest example of a
  mechanism), actively seek a second, independent source before treating it as solid — an
  unverified single transcript occurrence is weak evidence on its own for something this easy to
  mis-hear.
- It's fine — expected, even — for `low_confidence` entries to exist in the corpus. The goal is
  visibility, not exclusion. A flagged guess is still useful raw material; an unflagged one is a
  landmine for later analysis.

## Maintenance rule

If a `low_confidence` or `plausible_unverified` entry later gets independently confirmed (or
debunked), update its `transcription_confidence` in place and note the resolution — don't leave
stale uncertainty flags on entries that have since been verified, and don't quietly delete
debunked ones (note the debunking instead, it's useful negative evidence).

Add a row to the platform reliability registry above whenever research turns up a clear signal
about a given platform's captioning practices — this is cumulative, cross-language knowledge and
should not have to be re-learned per language.
